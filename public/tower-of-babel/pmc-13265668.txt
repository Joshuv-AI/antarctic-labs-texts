Rethinking the “D-” and “ L-” prefix paradigm for transposition
Authors: Sarv Priya, Prashant Nagpal, Robert H Anderson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe prefixes “D-” and “L-” are widely used as shorthand descriptors for the segmental connections in transposition of the great arteries (TGA), yet their original intent was solely to indicate the spatial position of the aortic root relative to the pulmonary trunk at the level of the semilunar valves and not the underlying atrioventricular (AV) or ventriculo-arterial (VA) connections. Yet this usage persists widely in clinical and imaging practice. We present an illustrative case of TGA with concordant AV and discordant VA connections in which the aortic root is levoposed, a configuration that highlights how prefix-only labeling generates inaccurate descriptions and scope for miscommunication among professionals. The case highlights that aortic position is not the defining feature of the segmental connections in the setting of transposition. Full segmental description is necessary to provide the complete picture.Keywords: Transposition of great arteries, D-TGA, L-TGA, Levoposed aorta, Segmental notation

Introduction

It is widely accepted that the term transposition denotes the combination of concordant atrioventricular and discordant ventriculo-arterial connections, with the aorta typically positioned anterior and to the right of the pulmonary trunk. Conversely, when the aorta is anterior and to the left, this configuration is usually associated with congenitally corrected transposition, characterized by discordant atrioventricular and ventriculo-arterial connections. A commonly misunderstood but important distinction is that the term “levoposed aorta” refers only to the position of the aortic root in relation to the pulmonary trunk at the level of the semilunar valves. It does not refer to the sidedness of the aortic arch, which remains left-sided in most patients irrespective of the underlying cardiac connections [1]. We describe a case of transposition with usual atrial arrangement and concordant atrioventricular connections, but with a levoposed aorta, illustrating that the spatial position of the great arteries may vary independently of segmental connections. Hence, the prefixes “D” and “L” should not be used in isolation to define the segmental connections. Instead, description always requires complete information regarding the atrioventricular and ventriculo-arterial connections.

Illustrative case

A 38-year-old male with a complex congenital cardiac history presented with regular transposition treated using the Mustard atrial switch procedure. He presented after experiencing a shock from his implantable cardioverter-defibrillator, which was inserted because of rhythm problems after the operative procedures. He reported a 1-day history of lightheadedness and near-syncope while showering, culminating in a single discharge from his defibrillator. Device interrogation revealed sustained episodes of monomorphic ventricular tachycardia, which were successfully terminated by a 35-joule shock. A cardiac magnetic resonance investigation was ordered for further evaluation.

The findings were consistent with the known diagnosis of regular transposition corrected using the Mustard operation. Thus, the pulmonary trunk was seen arising from the morphologically left ventricle, with the aorta arising from the right ventricle (Fig. 1). The morphologically right ventricle was located on the right and the morphologically left ventricle on the left side, as expected for D-looped ventricles, or right-handed ventricular topology (Fig. 2). The aorta, however, was positioned anterior and to the left of the pulmonary trunk as seen on axial cross-sectional imaging (Fig. 3). The systemic and pulmonary venous pathways created by the Mustard baffle were patent (Fig 2). The patient was managed for heart failure with preserved ejection fraction and pulmonary hypertension, with follow-up arrangements made through the electrophysiology team.

Discussion

The prefixes ``D-'' and ``L-'', when used for description of the discordantly connected aorta in the setting of transposition, were originally used to denote the spatial position of the aorta in relation to the pulmonary trunk. The ``D-'' prefix accounted for the arrangement in which the aorta is positioned anterior and to the right of the pulmonary trunk, while the ``L-'' prefix denoted a levoposed aorta, located anteriorly and leftward (Fig. 4) [1]. When first introduced by Van Praagh, however, the labels were introduced as part of the overall segmental description, which included information regarding not only the ventriculo-arterial connection, which was implied by the recognition of transposition, but also the type of atrioventricular connections present. The latter information was provided based on the codification of atrial arrangement and ventricular looping. These descriptors were to be used as part of the overall segmental notation to describe spatial relationship of the aortic root and pulmonary trunk and not as diagnostic labels for the overall segmental anatomy. It was not his idea to use "D" and "L" as the prefixes for "regular" and congenitally corrected transposition. Thus, the regular variant was described as transposition {S, D, D}, whereas the usual congenitally corrected variant was coded as transposition {S, L, L} [2].

Spatial Meaning of the “D” and “L” Prefixes. Diagrammatic axial semilunar valve level cross-sections demonstrating the original spatial meaning of the “D” and “L” prefixes. “D” denotes a dextroposed aortic root positioned to the right of the pulmonary trunk, whereas “L” denotes a levoposed aortic root positioned to the left. These prefixes describe only the relative arterial root position and do not define segmental connections or overall physiology.

In current practice, however, the "D" and "L" prefixes are often used as descriptors for the overall segmental combinations. For example, "D-TGA" is now commonly used to describe all those patients having concordant atrioventricular and discordant ventriculo-arterial connections. The majority of such patients will indeed have their aortic roots in an anterior and right-sided position. However, the aorta may also be levoposed (left-sided) when there is the usual atrial arrangement, or arranged side-by-side, or even be posterior in relation to the pulmonary trunk [3,4]. Accounting for examples of such atypical configurations as "D-TGA with levoposed aorta" introduces inconsistency, since the prefix no longer aligns with its original positional meaning. Simply describing such a case as L-TGA would be correct in terms of the aortic position, but potentially confusing if used in isolation, since this term is commonly used, to describe congenitally corrected transposition (Fig. 5).

This issue becomes even more complex when considering the arrangement in which transposition is found in mirror-image variants. In patients having mirror-imaged atrial arrangement with concordant atrioventricular connections, it is the rule to find left-handed ventricular topology, of L-looping. The aortic root in this setting is expected to be left-sided, but the segmental combinations are those of regular, rather than congenitally corrected transposition. In such situations, a simple description of the arrangement as "L-TGA" would be grossly misleading, since this could well convey the impression that the transposition was, indeed, congenitally corrected [4,5]. In patients with mirror-imaged atrial arrangement and discordant atrioventricular connections, the segmental anatomy when the aorta is itself discordantly connected is usually {I, D, D}. Referring to this combination simply as "D-TGA" is accurate in terms of the aortic position, but again confusing, since the hemodynamic arrangement is that of congenitally corrected rather than regular transposition (Fig. 6).

Illustration of mirror-image transposition variants demonstrating reversal of spatial arterial relationships while preserving the underlying segmental arrangement pattern. The figure highlights that mirror-image configurations may produce opposite arterial root positions despite analogous physiologic and segmental relationships, further emphasizing the limitations of relying solely on “D” and “L” prefixes for classification.

Even with the current understanding of ventriculo-arterial relationships and the availability of well-defined anatomical classifications, terms such as “DORV with D-TGA” or “DORV with transposition” are still commonly encountered in imaging reports and clinical documentation. This confusion is often seen in patients with double outlet right ventricle and anteriorly positioned aorta. The term transposition refers to discordant ventriculo-arterial connections, where the great arteries arise from different ventricles. In contrast, a double outlet right ventricle describes both arterial trunks arising predominantly from the morphologic right ventricle. As such, "double outlet with transposition" is inaccurate terminology. The appropriate descriptor in such cases would be "double outlet with anterior aorta."

The distinctions discussed above are not merely semantic but have important practical implications for imaging interpretation and segmental analysis in congenital heart disease. From a radiologic standpoint, accurate interpretation of transposition and related ventriculo-arterial malpositions require clear separation of segmental connections from the spatial relationship of the great arteries. Imaging reports should therefore avoid using isolated positional prefixes such as “D-TGA” or “L-TGA” without explicit clarification of the atrioventricular and ventriculo-arterial connections.

A structured segmental approach is recommended for cross-sectional imaging interpretation. Reports should sequentially document: (1) atrial arrangement, (2) ventricular topology and atrioventricular connections, (3) ventriculo-arterial connections, and (4) the spatial relationship of the great arteries. The position of the aortic root relative to the pulmonary trunk should be described independently as rightward, leftward (levoposed), anterior, posterior, or side-by-side [6]. This assessment should be performed using sequential axial cross-sectional images at the level of the semilunar valves. The sidedness of the aortic arch should also be documented separately from the relationship of the arterial roots, since these represent distinct anatomic features [2]. Adopting precise and anatomically accurate terminology facilitates more transparent communication and serves to remove ambiguity from the terminology of complex congenital heart disease.
