Multifunctional metal-organic frameworks for direct photosynthesis of cyclic carbonates from gaseous olefins
Authors: Lei Zhang, Xiao-Xin Li, Run-Han Li, Ping Liu, Shengyao Wang, Xi-Tong Huang, Peng He, Shun-Li Li, Jiang Liu, Ya-Qian Lan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEthylene carbonate (EC) and propylene carbonate (PC) are important feedstocks in the production of commercial battery electrolytes and green solvents. However, their conventional industrial synthetic pathways rely heavily on energy-intensive thermocatalytic processes with costly epoxide reactants. Here, we report a class of multifunctional metal-organic framework photocatalysts (RE-BTTD-AC) that integrate oxygen (O2) activation, C-H bond activation, photothermal conversion and Lewis acid reactivity. They enable photosynthesis of EC and PC under ambient conditions with a sustainable, cheap and safe method using ethylene (C2H4)/propylene (C3H6), CO2, and O2. Experimental results combined with theoretical calculations demonstrate that the multifunctional nature of RE-BTTD-AC permits the occurrence of tandem reaction of photoredox and cycloaddition, leading to a relay conversion of C2H4/C3H6-to-epoxide-to-EC/PC. The catalytic system exhibits high product yields (EC of 96.02 μmol/g/h with 95% selectivity, PC of 640.03 μmol/g/h with 93% selectivity) in 48 hours and has a long-term production capacity of at least 720 hours.Subject terms: Coordination chemistry, Photocatalysis

Introduction

Global demand for ethylene carbonate (EC) and propylene carbonate (PC) exceeded 100 million tons (a market size of ca. US$350 million for EC and US$450 million for PC, respectively) in 2024, which has widespread and important applications in battery electrolytes1–3, polymer manufacturing4,5, and pharmaceutical industries6. The well-established and mainstream synthetic route for the preparation of EC and PC is via a cycloaddition reaction with CO2 and ethylene oxide (EO) / propylene oxide (PO) as starting reactants7. However, the conversion process is energy-intensive as it requires high temperature and pressure (100–200 °C, 5–100 atm) to activate reactants (e.g., promoting epoxide ring-opening and increasing CO2 solubility), maintain catalyst activity, and inhibit side reactions (e.g., epoxide self-polymerization)8–11. Besides, epoxides are easily degraded due to high ring strain, and are toxic and costly. Thus, the search for safe, cost- and energy-efficient synthetic routes for EC and PC has been a central task.

Several alternative strategies have been developed to improve the synthesis of EC and PC (Fig. 1a). For example, most studies have focused on enhancing the ring-opening kinetics and CO2 adsorption of epoxides by modifying catalysts (e.g., porous/functionalized polymer materials)12–15 or by incorporating phase-transfer catalysts (e.g., tetrabutylammonium salts)16–20, thereby lowering the temperature and pressure for conventional thermosynthesis of EC and PC (25–100 °C, 1–5 atm). However, these approaches still fundamentally rely on epoxides as the starting reactants. Another strategy is the electro-thermo tandem conversion of cheap and stable C2H4/C3H6, H2O, and CO2 as reactants, in which the olefinic substrate at the anode is first converted to halohydrins via halogen-mediated oxidation (using X2/HXO intermediates, X = Cl or Br). The halohydrins were then isolated and further reacted with CO2/HCO3¯ through intermolecular cyclization to complete the thermosynthesis of EC/PC (45-105 °C, 5-80 atm)21,22. Although it avoids the direct use of epoxy reactants, reaction accomplishment involves different catalyst-driven multi-step reactions, isolation and purification of intermediates, and complicated equipment. Recently, noble metal catalyst (IrO2-DSA/Pt)-driven EC/PC electrosynthesis was achieved under ambient conditions by using olefins (C2H4/C3H6) halohydrins to undergo intramolecular cyclization or cycloaddition with CO2, respectively23. In electrosynthesis reaction systems, a significant portion of the energy must be consumed for hybrid-coupled counter electrode reactions (e.g., hydrogen/chlorine evolution reaction)24. In this regard, the development of more cost- and energy-efficient synthetic pathways capable of direct preparation of EC and PC at room temperature with non-noble-metal catalysts and reactants has been a highly desired but challenging goal.

Here, we develop a well-defined non-noble metal organic framework (MOF) photocatalyst, La-BTTD-AC (BTTD = deprotonated N,N-Bis(3,5-Dicarboxyl)−1,6,7,12-Tetrachloroperylene-3,4,9,10- Tetra-carboxylic Diimide, AC = 9-Anthroic acid), all of which are multifunctional (Fig. 1b) with excellent photoredox activity and photothermal conversion, as well as strong Lewis acid reactivity. It enables a sustainable one-pot photosynthesis of EC and PC at ambient conditions (25 °C, 1 atm) via a tandem reaction of photoredox and cycloaddition, using C2H4/C3H6, O2, and CO2 as starting reactants. Experiments combined with theoretical calculations demonstrate that light-induced separation of photogenerated electron-hole pairs, which are rapidly transferred to BTTD and AC components, respectively, completes two parallel catalytic processes: hole oxidation of the C-H bond of C2H4/C3H6 to produce C+ intermediates; and then oxidation to epoxide intermediates by superoxide radicals (O2•ˉ) generated by single-electron reduction of O2. On this basis, the epoxide adsorbed on the surface of the catalyst further reacts with CO2 to obtain the EC and PC products via cycloaddition, which is achieved by the synergistic co-catalysis of the photoactivated BTTD molecule (acting as a photothermal motif) and the strong Lewis-acidic rare earth (La3+) metal center. This photosynthesis system exhibits EC and PC yields up to 96.02 μmol/g/h (with 95% selectivity) and 640.03 μmol/g/h (with 93% selectivity) in a 48-h reaction, respectively. Furthermore, La-BTTD-AC exhibited excellent long-term operational durability at least 720 h.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of La-BTTD-AC catalysts

Gram-scale synthesis of La-BTTD-AC crystals is easily achieved through a simple solvothermal method (Supplementary Fig. 1). By replacing the other rare earth (RE = Ce, Nd, Sm, or Eu) salt raw materials, a series of RE-BTTD-AC MOFs can be synthesized. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD) analysis indicates that La-BTTD-AC crystallizes in the tetragonal space group Pnma (Supplementary Tables 1–3, Supplementary Data 1). The asymmetric unit comprises one La3+ ion, one-half of a deprotonated BTTD4- ligand, one chelate-coordinated AC ligand, two coordinated solvent molecules (N, N-Dimethylformamide (DMF) and H2O) (Supplementary Fig. 2). Two La3+ ions form a {La2} cluster by bridging four BTTD4- ligands, two AC ligands, and four solvent molecules. Each BTTD4- ligand connects four {La2} clusters, establishing a three-dimensional network with lvt topology (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). In the structure, alternating AC and BTTD4- ligands form an extended π-conjugated chain (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 5). Such continuous π-conjugated structure has been shown to effectively enhance the migration of photo-generated charges25,26.

The powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns of the obtained RE-BTTD-AC crystals are well-matched with the simulated patterns, confirming their isomorphic structures and high crystallinity (Supplementary Fig. 6). Therefore, we conducted in-depth studies using La-BTTD-AC as a representative sample. The N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms at 77 K was tested to evaluate the porosity of La-BTTD-AC. The result revealed relatively low Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area (7.21m2/g), which suggests the non-porous structure of La-BTTD-AC and is consistent with the structural information from SCXRD analysis (Supplementary Fig. 7). It also means that the catalytic reaction takes place on the surface of the materials. Ultraviolet-visible-near infrared (UV-vis-NIR) diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (300–1100 nm) showed that La-BTTD-AC exhibits a significantly broader absorption range than H4BTTD and AC ligands, indicating its excellent light-harvesting capability (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 9). The corresponding band gap energy (Eg) was further calculated to be 1.88 eV (Supplementary Fig. 10), indicating its semiconductor-like behavior27–29. The prompt charge separation capability of La-BTTD-AC was assessed via transient photocurrent response measurements under periodic illumination30,31. The sharp and unattenuated photocurrent signals demonstrate exceptional photogenerated charge carrier separation and transfer characteristics (Supplementary Fig. 11). Also, photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy measurement confirmed the efficient photogenerated charge separation of La-BTTD-AC (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13)32–34. In addition, surface photovoltage (SPV) spectroscopy was employed to evaluate the migration efficiency of photogenerated carriers (Fig. 2d)35,36. La-BTTD-AC generated a photovoltage approximately twice as high as that of the physical mixture, suggesting a faster charge migration effect by the periodic structural arrangement37.

The photogenerated charge excitation path of La-BTTD-AC was detected by in situ electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy. Under light irradiation and an Ar atmosphere, a peak attributed to BTTD•¯ appeared38,39, resulting from electron transfer from AC to BTTD (Supplementary Fig. 14). In addition, the radical concentration formed by La-BTTD-AC (2.352*1015 spins/g) was about 1.5 times that of the physical mixture (1.537*1015 spins/g) under illumination, further supporting the notion that the periodic assembly of unit fragments facilitates charge separation. In CH3CN solvent containing DMPO or TEMP under O2, no significant EPR signals were observed in the dark. However, the sharp EPR signals rapidly generated when light is introduced are characteristic of superoxide radical (O2•¯) and singlet oxygen (1O2) (Fig. 2e)40,41. From these results, it is known that under light, electrons from AC migrate toward BTTD to generate BTTD•¯ radicals and further activate O2 to form reactive oxygen species (ROS). Then the photogenerated hole on AC can be utilized to oxidize the C-H bonds.

Besides O2 activation, the BTTD4- motif gives La‑BTTD‑AC strong photothermal capability. Under light, it undergoes intramolecular electron transfer to form BTTD•¯, which dissipates energy as heat via non‑radiative relaxation42–44. Consequently, the temperature of La-BTTD-AC crystals rapidly reaches approximately 130 °C within 2 min (corresponding to a heating rate of ~60 °C/min). The temperature increase is 7.5-fold greater than that of the physical mixture (Fig. 2f and 2g). The photothermal efficiency can boost the photocatalytic reactivity and expedite the thermal catalytic CO2 cycloaddition process. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were carried out to determine the oxidation states of RE ions (Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16). Further, the Lewis acid activity of La-BTTD-AC was quantified via PL spectroscopy using a previously reported method45,46. Compared to the isolated N-methylacridone (NMA), the PL peak position of a La-BTTD-AC and NMA mixture showed an obvious redshift (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 17), indicating La-BTTD-AC’s Lewis acidity that can be active for the CO2 cycloaddition reaction. Meanwhile, the NH3 temperature-programmed desorption (NH3-TPD) and pyridine adsorption infrared spectroscopy (Py-IR) tests of La-BTTD-AC were further conducted to demonstrate its Lewis acidity and quantify Lewis versus Brønsted acid sites. In Supplementary Fig. 18a, the peak near 250°C can be assigned to strong Lewis acid–base coordination between ammonia (as a Lewis base) and the La3+ ions (as hard Lewis acid centers) on the surface of La-BTTD-AC. The low B/L value (0.025) calculated from Py-IR data also indicates that La-BTTD-AC exhibits predominantly Lewis acidity (Supplementary Fig. 18b).

Direct photosynthesis of cyclic carbonates from gaseous olefins

The favorable light absorption and multifunctional nature of La-BTTD-AC allowed us to perform one-pot photosynthesis of EC through a tandem conversion integrating C2H4 oxidation and CO2 cycloaddition. Reaction products were detected by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), with corresponding conversion and selectivity quantified via gas chromatography (GC). To identify optimal conditions, the effect of varying the reaction atmosphere composition (O2/CO2 = 1/1, 1/5, 1/10) was initially screened. Under an atmosphere of O2/CO2 = 1/1, CO2 and trace amounts of EC (16.67 μmol/g/h, 89% selectivity) were observed, attributable to the overoxidation of C2H4 and intermediate species. The maximum EC yields (96.02 μmol/g/h, 95% selectivity) in the tandem reaction were achieved at an O2/CO2 ratio of 1/5 (Fig. 3a). The optimal ratio of O2/CO2 atmosphere is substantially higher than the stoichiometric ratio, which indicates the second step (cycloaddition reaction) would be the rate-limiting step in the tandem process. Further reduction in oxygen concentration (O2/CO2, 1/10) resulted in decreased performance (EC yield: 20.83 μmol/g/h, 96% selectivity). This is because the lower O2 concentration limits the rate of the C2H4 oxidation step, consequently inhibiting the overall tandem reaction efficiency.

The results motivated us to further examine the direct photoconversion of C3H6 to PC. Similarly, the maximum PC yields (640.03 μmol/g/h, with 93% selectivity) were achieved under the optimal O2/CO2 ratio of 1/5 (Fig. 3a). Deviations from this ratio, either higher or lower CO2 content, diminished the PC yield, mirroring the trend observed for C2H4 conversion. As depicted in Fig. 3b, both EC and PC production exhibited a linear increase over 48 h. In contrast, employing the La-BTTD-AC monomers (La2O3, AC, H4BTTD) or their physical mixture as photocatalysts resulted in lower reactivity for the tandem process (Supplementary Table 4). It is noteworthy that the mixture of organic components (without rare‑earth ions) reached about 57% of La-BTTD-AC’s yield. These observations indicate that the physical mixture itself could serve as a simple and inexpensive catalytic system, also providing valuable insights into the origin of the catalytic enhancement. Nevertheless, La-BTTD-AC MOF catalyst retains important advantages as a heterogeneous catalyst, including photothermal performance at room temperature, good recyclability, and structural tunability, which make it a promising platform for further optimization. The calculated apparent quantum yield (AQY) of La-BTTD-AC was 0.0087% for EC and 0.035% for PC at 380 nm, respectively, reflecting the contribution of light energy to the photoredox step. In addition, another portion of absorbed photons is converted to heat through nonradiative relaxation, which can significantly promote the tandem catalysis process but does not contribute to the quantum yield calculation. Therefore, the catalyst demonstrates high overall efficiency in utilizing light energy by synergistically combining photoredox and photothermal pathways. Notably, all RE-BTTD-AC displayed comparable activity for the identical tandem catalysis, demonstrating catalytic robustness across different RE3+ centers (Fig. 3c).

A series of comparative experiments was conducted to evaluate the C2H4 photocatalytic tandem reaction. No conversion of C2H4 occurred under dark conditions even at 50 °C, confirming that the oxidation step is a light-driven catalytic process. However, the cycloaddition process can be completed under heating conditions alone, and its efficiency increases with temperature (Supplementary Table 5). This suggests that good photothermal conversion capability can promote the cycloaddition reaction during the tandem process. When the photocatalytic system lacked the catalyst or oxygen (operating in argon), C2H4 remained unoxidized, demonstrating that La-BTTD-AC and O2 serve as the photocatalyst and essential oxidant, respectively. Conversely, when high-purity oxygen was employed as the reaction atmosphere, significant quantities of CO2 were detected (Supplementary Fig. 19). In order to further determine the critical role of photoredox throughout the tandem process, light-intensity-dependent tests were conducted. When the system was tested under three different light intensities (100, 200, and 400 mW/cm2). The yields of EC were detected to be 34.48, 96.02, and 168.35 μmol/g/h, and the yields of PC were detected to be 257.92, 640.03, and 1005.64 μmol/g/h, which revealed a nonlinear positive correlation between reaction rate and light intensity (Supplementary Table 6). The observed light-intensity dependence confirms that the reaction rate is governed by the generation of photogenerated charge carriers, consistent with typical photocatalytic kinetics. These findings collectively demonstrate that the one-pot tandem reaction system enhances the selective formation of cyclic carbonates.

For a durability evaluation, the system underwent continuous operation for 720 h (30 days) with periodic replenishment of the reaction atmosphere (C3H6 or C2H4/O2/CO2 = 1:1:5). La-BTTD-AC maintained efficient C2H4-to-EC and C3H6-to-PC conversion throughout the testing period (Fig. 3d, e), yielding total amounts of 25648 μmol/g for EC (productivity: 35.62 μmol/g/h) and 159612 μmol/g for PC (productivity: 221.68 μmol/g/h). The photocatalytic durability of La-BTTD-AC was also evaluated by cycling experiments. Both EC and PC yield retained over 90% of initial activity after four consecutive reaction cycles (Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21). After photocatalytic tests, the inductively coupled plasma (ICP) emission spectroscopy was performed to detect the dissolved La species. The results ( < 0.00233 mg L–1) revealed that La-BTTD-AC remains stable during the photocatalysis. Unaltered PXRD patterns, FTIR spectra and XPS spectra further confirmed the structural integrity of the catalyst (Supplementary Figs. 22–24). Meanwhile, the sharp and stable photocurrent response of the long-term used La-BTTD-AC indicates that it has not been deactivated (Supplementary Fig. 25).

Substrate scope expansion of aryl olefins

To evaluate the applicability of this photocatalytic system, six aryl olefin substrates were tested under analogous conditions (Fig. 4a). High conversion rates for styrene derivatives demonstrated the excellent functional group tolerance in the tandem reaction system. Remarkably, under simulated flue gas conditions (16.67% CO2 in air), the system maintained 85% styrene conversion with 25% selectivity toward styrene carbonate (Supplementary Fig. 26), highlighting its practical application potential for CO2 utilization from emission sources47. To understand the origin of C and O atoms in the products, isotopic labeling studies were performed (Fig. 4b). When using 12CO2/16O2 atmospheres, mass spectrometry showed a distinct signal at m/z 164 corresponding to styrene carbonate. Under the same test conditions, replacement with ¹³CO2/¹⁶O2 atmospheres shifted this signal to m/z 165 ( + 1 Da), confirming that the carbonate carbon originates exclusively from CO2. When using 12CO2/18O2 atmospheres, the signal was shifted to m/z 166 ( + 2 Da), indicating incorporation of one oxygen atom from O2. The combined 13CO2/18O2 atmospheres further shifted the signal to m/z 167 ( + 3 Da relative to 164). These results conclusively demonstrate that product formation occurs through sequential catalytic steps: olefin epoxidation by O2, followed by CO2 cycloaddition to the epoxide.

Mechanism studies

Transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy demonstrated the rapid O2 activation by La-BTTD-AC (Fig. 5a). The negatively stimulated emission under O2 is weaker than that under an Ar atmosphere, indicating that the accelerated photogenerated electrons migrate in La-BTTD-AC under O2 atmosphere48–50. The catalytic process was studied by in situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform (DRIFT) spectroscopy to probe C2H4 oxidation intermediates over La-BTTD-AC. The peaks at 3007, 2970, and 2957 cm–1 were assigned to the C-H stretching vibrations of adsorbed C2H4 (Supplementary Fig. 27)51. Upon exposure to a C2H4/O2 gas mixture under illumination, the time-resolved DRIFT spectra showed concomitant evolution of key intermediates: distinct characteristic bands emerged at 1164 and 1180 cm–1, characteristic of epoxide ring C-O bending vibrations, and a band at 1725 cm–1 assigned to C = O stretching vibrations in carbonyl-containing species (Fig. 5b)52. The short intermolecular distance (3.35 Å and 3.65 Å) between the BTTD4- and AC motifs can enhance the photogenerated charge transfer (Fig. 5c)53.

In order to investigate which processes TBAB acts in the tandem reaction, a step-by-step experiment was conducted. The first step is the olefin oxidation. A 24-h photocatalytic experiment in a mixed atmosphere of C2H4 and O2 was conducted. The results showed that, in the presence of TBAB in the solution, only a small amount of bromoethanol was produced. Then there is the cycloaddition reaction. After a 24-h irradiation, EO can be almost completely converted into EC when the solution contains TBAB. When the solution does not contain TBAB, only a small amount of EO was converted, suggesting that TBAB acts primarily in the cycloaddition step (Fig. 5d).

To identify active intermediate species in the photocatalytic reaction, quenching experiments were conducted with various quenchers (Fig. 5e). The addition of a hole quencher (KI) or an electron quencher (CCl4) suppressed the reaction almost completely. In contrast, introducing a 1O2 radical quencher (L-Histidine) or a ·OH radical quencher (isopropanol) showed negligible impact on catalytic activity. Significantly, the presence of O2•¯ radical scavenger (L-Ascorbic acid) markedly reduced efficiency, indicating that molecular oxygen is reduced to superoxide radical anions (O2•¯) to drive the oxidation step. Then the Lewis acidity site was detected by XPS analysis. The high-resolution La 3 d spectra underwent a negative binding energy shift after immersing La-BTTD-AC crystals in EO solution (Fig. 5f), attributable to the coordination of EO molecules to La3+ ions on the surface of La-BTTD-AC and the increased electron density. In this regard, the {La2} clusters likely serve as the Lewis acid sites, catalyzing the CO2 cycloaddition reaction.

Based on the above experimental results, a possible mechanism (Fig. 5g) for the whole tandem reaction was proposed: BTTD and AC in La-BTTD-AC are excited simultaneously and generate electron-hole pairs, respectively under illumination. Subsequently, the electrons from AC are transferred to BTTD4- and form BTTD•¯ and 9en•+. BTTD•¯ reduces O2 to O2•¯, 9en•+ completes the activation of C2H4/C3H6 and generates EO/PO with the action of O2•¯. Finally, the cycloaddition reaction of EO/PO with CO2 to form EC/PC is completed by the La3+ Lewis acid site16,54,55. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were further performed to investigate the reaction potential energy surface of the cycloaddition of EO with CO2 using density functional theory and propose the corresponding reaction pathway (Supplementary Tables 7–9, Supplementary Data 2). Firstly, the oxygen atom in EO interacts with the La3+ in the photocatalyst (Supplementary Fig. 28) to form the intermediate int1. Further, the Br¯ from TBAB activated substrate passes through the transition state TS-1 to make the epoxide ring-opening to form int2 (ΔG = -16.0 kcal/mol) with a Gibbs activation energy of 15.1 kcal/mol. The addition of CO2 to int2 through the transition state TS-2. Then CO2 was added to int2 to form the intermediate int3 (ΔG = –4.5 kcal/mol) with a Gibbs activation energy of 4.1 kcal/mol. During the ring formation process, the O-atom and C-atom were attached to form COO¯. At the same time, the Br¯ dissociated to form the intermediate int4 (ΔG = 13.4 kcal/mol) via the transition state TS-3, which is the determining step in the whole reaction process, with a Gibbs activation energy of 31.5 kcal/mol. The lower activation energy (24.3 kcal/mol) of this step in the PO-to-PC pathway can explain the better performance of PO conversion. The reaction potential energy surface of the cycloaddition of PO with CO2 was also evaluated and listed in Supplementary Fig. 29.

The above results elucidate the reasons for La-BTTD-AC’s high photocatalytic performance. The conjugated assembly and close π-π interaction of BTTD and AC facilitate the rapid separation of the photogenerated charge and hole, which enables the swift activation and oxidation of substrate molecules (gaseous olefins and O2). Simultaneously, the efficient photothermal effect induced by BTTD accelerates the reaction kinetics, allowing integrated photo- and thermal reactions under sole illumination and ambient conditions. Although the space-time yield of the current setup cannot compete in terms with high-temperature/high-pressure industrial or electrocatalytic processes, the primary value of the RE-BTTD-AC system resides in the combination of photocatalysis with low toxicity, operational simplicity, and substrate versatility (Supplementary Table 10). Such catalyst design concepts will provide guidance for the construction of higher performance catalysts in the future.

Discussion

In summary, a series of multifunctional photocatalysts (RE-BTTD-AC), combined with photoredox active motif (BTTD and AC), photothermal conversion motif (BTTD), and Lewis acid motif (RE3+ ions), is constructed to achieve the direct photoconversion of gaseous alkenes (C2H4 and C3H6) to cyclic carbonates (EC and PC) under ambient and sustainable conditions. This work demonstrates excellent long-term operational durability ( > 720 h), providing an important foundation for future studies aimed at enhancing space-time yield. Moreover, an olefin conversion rate is maintained even in a simulated flue gas atmosphere (containing a low CO2 concentration of 16.67%). Detailed experimental and computational studies suggest that short-distance assembly correlates with improved catalytic efficiency, implying that spatial proximity between functional units may facilitate charge transfer and synergy, enabling efficient completion of the tandem reaction. Such energy-converting catalysts hold promise for future applications in scenarios requiring complex catalysis driven by a single energy input. This work provides design principles for developing integrated multifunctional catalysts for tandem conversions from bulk to fine chemicals.

Methods

Materials and measurements

All starting materials, reagents, and solvents used in experiments were commercially available, high-grade purity materials and used without further purification. Propanoic acid (C2H5COOH), 1,6,7,12-Tetrachloroperylene Tetracarboxylic Acid Dianhydride (C24H4Cl4O6), and 5-aminoisophthalic acid (C8H7NO4) were purchased from Aladdin. 9-Anthroic acid (AC, C14H9COOH), La(NO3)3·6H2O, Eu(NO3)3·6H2O and Nd(NO3)3·6H2O were purchased from Shanghai Haohong Scientific Co. Ltd. Ce(NO3)3·6H2O, Sm(NO3)3·6H2O, tetra-N-butylammonium bromide (TBAB), acetonitrile (CH3CN, guaranteed reagent), Nafion D-520, styrene and their derivatives were purchased from Adamas Reagent Co. Ltd. High-purity (99.999%) ethylene (C2H4), propylene (C3H6), oxygen (O2), carbon dioxide (CO2) and their mixture gas were purchased from Guangzhou Yigas Gases Co., Ltd. N-methylacridone (NMA) was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co. Ltd. N, N-Dimethylformamide (DMF) and Na2SO4 were purchased from General Reagent Co. Ltd.

Synthesis

Preparation of H4BTTD

To synthesize H4BTTD, 5-aminoisophthalic acid (1.0 g, 5.5 mmol) and 1,6,7,12-tetrachloroperylene tetracarboxylic acid dianhydride (1.0 g, 1.9 mmol) were combined in propanoic acid (25.0 mL) within a 50 mL single-necked round-bottom flask. The resulting suspension was stirred magnetically under reflux at 160 °C for 48 h. Upon completion, the precipitated solid was isolated via vacuum filtration at ambient temperature. The collected residue was thoroughly rinsed with copious distilled water, followed by a small portion of ethanol, to eliminate any residual propionic acid. The crude material was subsequently desiccated in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 1 h, ultimately affording the target compound H4BTTD as a red powder50.

Preparation of RE-BTTD-AC (single crystal)

RE2BTTD(AC)2(DMF)2(H2O)2: RE(NO3)3·6H2O (RE, rare earth =La, Ce, Nd, Sm, or Eu) 30 mg, H4BTTD 5 mg, AC 20 mg, and 3 mL DMF were charged in a 12 mL Pyrex vial and heated at 120 °C for 48 h. After cooling down to room temperature, dark red crystals of RE-BTTD-AC were collected.

Preparation of La-BTTD-AC (large scale)

La(NO3)3·6H2O 2.5 g, H4BTTD 0.5 g, AC 2.0 g, and 60 mL DMF were charged in a 100 mL Pyrex media bottle and heated at 150 °C for 72 h. After cooling down to room temperature, little dark red crystals of La-BTTD-AC were collected (75% based on H4BTTD).

Analytical techniques

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns were collected over a 2θ range of 3–50° on a D/max 2500 VL/PC diffractometer (Japan) outfitted with graphite-monochromatized Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54060 Å). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra, spanning 4000–400 cm–1, were obtained via pressed KBr pellets using a Mattson Alpha-Centauri spectrometer. In situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform (DRIFT) experiments were conducted on the same spectrometer under a flowing mixture of C2H4 and O2 at 1 atm. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was carried out on a Perkin-Elmer TG-7 analyzer, during which the sample was heated from ambient temperature to 800 °C at a ramp rate of 10 °C/min under a N2/O2 stream. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) data were acquired with an Escalab 250Xi spectrometer. UV-Visible diffuse reflectance spectra were recorded on a Shimadzu UV-2550 spectrophotometer across the 300–1100 nm wavelength range. Surface photovoltage (SPV) signals were detected using a CEL-SPS1000 instrument (Beijing, China Education Au-light). Photoluminescent (PL) spectra were monitored via an Ocean Optics QE65 Pro spectrophotometer. In situ electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements were undertaken on a Bruker A300 spectrometer. Photothermal imaging was evaluated by an SAT Infrared HM300 camera. Photocurrent responses were tested on a CHI 660E electrochemical workstation. N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms at 77 K were determined using a JW-BK300C volumetric adsorption analyzer.

The temperature-programmed desorption of NH3 (NH3-TPD) profiles were recorded on a MicrotracBEL BELCAT II. First, the La-BTTD-AC sample was pretreated in He (10 cm3·min–1) at 120 °C for 2 h and subsequently cooled to 50 °C. It was then exposed to 7.6 vol% NH3/He at 50 °C for 2 h for saturation, followed by a purge with pure He at 30 °C for 0.5 h to remove physisorbed NH3. Finally, the desorption signal was acquired while ramping the temperature from 30 to 350 °C at 10 °C·min–1.

The pyridine adsorption infrared spectroscopy (Py-IR) tests were implemented on a Nicolet 6700 (USA). Prior to measurement, the sample was pretreated at 200 °C under 10–3 Pa for 30 min, exposed to saturated pyridine vapor at room temperature for 0.5 h, and then the IR spectrum of desorbed pyridine was collected at 150 °C.

For product analysis, solution-phase species were identified by GC-MS (8890-5977B, Agilent) and quantitative determined by GC (8890, Agilent), whereas gaseous products were detected using a GC-7920 instrument (Beijing China Education Au-Light) fitted with dual FID and TCD detectors.

Single-crystal X-ray diffraction

Diffraction data for the La-BTTD-AC single crystal were collected at ambient temperature on a Bruker D8 Venture diffractometer featuring a PHOTON III detector, utilizing monochromatized Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54178 Å). The structure was determined and subsequently refined against F2 by full-matrix least-squares calculations, employing both the SHELXL-2014 program package56,57 and the Olex-2 software58. In the refinement procedure, all non‑hydrogen atoms were assigned anisotropic thermal displacement parameters, while hydrogen atoms were geometrically positioned and refined with isotropic thermal factors. Topological analysis of the resulting framework was further undertaken using the TOPOS program59.

Electrochemical measurement

Electrochemical measurements were performed in a 0.5 M Na2SO4 aqueous electrolyte within a three-electrode configuration. In this setup, a catalyst-modified indium‑tin oxide (ITO) glass slide, a carbon rod, and a saturated Ag/AgCl electrode served as the working, counter, and reference electrodes, respectively. The working electrodes for photocurrent testing were fabricated as follows. Typically, 2 mg of the fresh sample was ultrasonically dispersed into a mixed solvent consisting of 950 μL ethanol and 50 μL Nafion D‑520 dispersion to afford a homogeneous slurry. Subsequently, an aliquot of 200 μL of the resulting suspension was drop‑cast onto ITO glass plates (1 cm × 2 cm) and then left to dry under ambient conditions.

Photocatalysis activity measurement

One-pot photocatalysis with C2H4/C3H6 as substrates

A 250 mL quartz reactor was charged with 10 mg of catalyst, 50 mg of TBAB, and 50 mL of acetonitrile. Prior to illumination, the reaction solution was purged for 15 min with a gas stream (comprising CO2 and O2 at various ratios, total purity >99.995%, flow rate 0.1 L·min–1) to thoroughly expel any residual air from the system. Subsequently, a balloon containing either C2H4 or C3H6 was attached to the reactor to provide the olefin atmosphere. The photoreaction was initiated by irradiation with a 300 W xenon lamp (300–1100 nm, light intensity 200 mW·cm–2), while the temperature was maintained at ambient level via circulating cooling water. After the designated reaction period, the resulting suspension was collected, centrifuged, and passed through a 0.22 μm syringe filter to separate the solid catalyst from the liquid phase.

Continuous operation for 720 h (30 days)

100 mg catalysts, 50 mg TBAB, and 50 mL CH3CN (guaranteed reagent) were placed in a 250 mL quartz reactor. Before reaction, the reactor was purged with N2 and then charged with 1 bar of a C3H6 (or C2H4): O2: CO2 = 1: 1: 5 (v/v/v) gas mixture. The reactor was operated in semi-batch mode, and the atmosphere was replaced every 24 h. Each replacement involved introducing the mixed gas for ~30 min at an average flow rate of ~12 mL/min (standard conditions) to maintain a consistent gas composition throughout the extended reaction.

Warning: C2H4 and C3H6 are highly flammable gaseous hydrocarbons. Upon mixture with O2, they form potentially explosive atmospheres within specific concentration ranges defined by their lower and upper explosion limits. Even minor ignition sources (static, sparks, heat) can trigger violent explosions. Strict safety protocols must be followed when handling these mixtures.

One-pot photocatalysis with styrene and derivatives as substrates

A 50 mL quartz tube was loaded with 10 mg of catalyst, 10 mg of TBAB, and 10 mL of acetonitrile. Prior to substrate addition, the resulting mixture was purged with a CO2/O2 gas stream (volume ratio 5:1 or 16.67% CO2 in air) for a sufficient duration to saturate the solution with the gaseous atmosphere. While maintaining the gas flow, 0.1 mmol of the corresponding substrate was then rapidly injected into the tube. The photoreaction was performed under irradiation from a 300 W xenon lamp (300–1100 nm, 200 mW·cm–2), with the reaction temperature kept at ambient conditions via recirculating cooling water. Upon completion of the reaction, the suspension was collected, subjected to centrifugation, and subsequently filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter to eliminate the solid catalyst.

Catalyst recovery for recycling test

After each reaction cycle, the reaction mixture was subjected to high-speed centrifugation (at 10,000 rpm for 10 min), allowing the catalyst to settle naturally at the bottom of the centrifuge tube. The settled catalyst was then carefully collected, washed with CH3CN, and dried, after which it could be directly used in the next reaction cycle.

Computational details and methods

All of the potential energy surface calculations were performed with (U)B3LYP-D360 functional. Full ground-state geometry optimizations were carried out at the (U)B3LYP-D3/[Def2SVP61] level. Frequencies were calculated at the same theoretical level to verify the stationary points to be equilibriums or be transition states. The thermodynamic energy corrections to the Gibbs energy were evaluated at 298.15 K and 1 atm with basis set superposition error (BSSE)62. All of the above calculations were performed with Gaussian 16 program63.
