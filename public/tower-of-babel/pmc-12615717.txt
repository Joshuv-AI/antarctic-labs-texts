In-air microfluidic sorting of single cells on multiple paths
Authors: Yuhe Chen, Ziyang Wang, Rui Tong, Congying Liu, Xiaojin Yang, Pengfei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSorting of desired single cells from a cell population is crucial for many applications in biology and biomedicine that require analysis at the cellular level. Microfluidic dielectrophoresis (DEP)-based single-cell sorting method has been demonstrated as a powerful technology to enable high-throughput and accurate sorting of single cells. However, conventional DEP sorting is mainly performed in the oil phase constrained by solid channels, which restricts the capacity and tunability of the sorting path. Here, we describe an approach to sort single cells on multiple paths in air. Our device allows for tunable ejection of droplets containing single cells in air, which are interrogated and sorted by a microfluidic DEP sorter with a cylindrical electrode, showing a sorting accuracy of >99% for all paths with high survival rates. We demonstrate the utility of our device by isolating multiple subpopulations from a cell sample with three types of cells. Our technology holds the potential to perform sorting on numerous paths, making it applicable for multipurpose sorting from complex heterogeneous cell populations. Subject terms: Engineering, Chemistry

Selective isolation of single cells from a heterogeneous cell population is of great importance for numerous applications in biology and biomedicine, such as molecular diagnostics, drug discovery, and personalized therapeutics1–4. The interrogation of isolated subpopulations at the single-cell level has advanced our understanding of biology and disease, elucidating the drawbacks of conventional measurement and analysis of cells in bulk that cannot accurately reflect the information at the cellular level4,5. Single-cell isolation relies on single-cell technologies to detect and isolate individual cells6. Flow cytometers, based on fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) or magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS), are commercially available single-cell sorting instruments and capable of rapidly sorting desired single cells from a heterogeneous cell suspension7,8. However, FACS usually works with a high fluid pressure, which shows a deleterious effect on cell survival rate9. And the sorting of FACS, which relies on the electrostatic charge of a small group of droplets, usually only yields a single-cell sorting efficiency of ~70–90%10,11. MACS operation requires magnetic labeling of single cells and showcases a relatively low cell recovery rate and poor manipulation precision12. Over the past decade, microfluidics has emerged as a powerful alternative to enable high-throughput and accurate isolation of single cells, which is enabled by the core technique of microfluidic sorting13,14.

Microfluidic single-cell sorting relies on the precision manipulation of single cells or droplets on a microfluidic chip. The sorting can be actuated by integrated pneumatic, acoustic, or electrical components15. In general, due to the slow response of pneumatic valves, the throughput of pneumatic-based sorting is relatively low16,17. Acoustics-based sorting method requires a microfluidic device with multiple components comprised of aligned microfluidic channels and piezoelectrics; its fabrication is laborious, and thus it is not widely adopted18,19. Dielectrophoresis (DEP)-based sorting exhibits a sorting throughput of up to 30 kHz with high precision, and the design and fabrication are straightforward20–22. Thus, the microfluidic DEP sorter has been widely used in microfluidics, especially as the sorting approach in droplet microfluidics23. In recent years, sorting of droplets encapsulating single cells has significantly promoted the advances of single-cell analysis24–26. However, single-cell sorting based on a conventional microfluidic DEP sorter is usually performed in pre-fabricated channels. The solid microfluidic channels, in which sorted droplets and single cells flow, restrict the sorting capacity to very limited sorting paths23. Moreover, sorting in these solid channels is performed in the oil phase, and the collection of sorted droplets requires demulsification to release single cells for subsequent analysis, the process of which may damage cells27. One possible way to avoid these limits induced by solid channels is to perform microfluidic sorting of single cells on multiple paths in air, which, however, has yet to be achieved.

In this work, we describe a microfluidic single-cell sorting approach capable of accurately sorting single cells on multiple paths in air. Our technology utilizes the co-flow of two air flows and the cell suspension phase to generate droplets containing single cells, the ejection direction of which can be adjusted by tuning the asymmetry of air pressures. Droplets ejected are interrogated and sorted by a fluorescence-activated droplet sorter with a cylindrical electrode, which enables simultaneous sorting of in-flight droplets over it from different directions, thus achieving sorting of single cells on multiple paths. We observe an accuracy of >99% for all sorting paths with high survival rates. To demonstrate the capability of multipurpose sorting, we sort single cells of defined compositions on three paths using the same cell suspension with three types of cells in a single sorting run. Our technology further extends the capability of microfluidic DEP-based single-cell sorting and will enable new opportunities in single-cell isolation and analysis of heterogeneous cell populations.

Results and discussion

Device design and working principle

Current on-chip single-cell sorters mainly work with a sorting process in a continuous liquid phase constrained by solid microfluidic channels15. These channels provide the guide for fluids with sorted cells or cell-containing droplets; once fabricated, channel structures are difficult to adjust, restricting the device’s sorting flexibility for multiple purposes. To enable the capability of multipurpose sorting, accommodating different applications, we have developed a microfluidic device capable of sorting single cells on tunable paths (Fig. 1a). To enable this technology, two core designs are integrated in the chip. First, we design a co-flow geometry with two air flows and a continuous liquid phase to generate and eject droplets in air. Pressures of these two air flows can be regulated in real time, resulting in a dynamically tunable ejection of droplets (Fig. 1b), overcoming the drawback of fixed droplet flow in a solid microfluidic channel. Second, to enable sorting of droplets coming from different directions, we design a cylindrical electrode, DEP effect of which can deflect flying droplets over it irrespective of their coming directions (Fig. 1c). For a single-cell sorting run on the device, fluorescence-labeling cells are injected into the device and then droplets containing single cells are formed and ejected in a controlled direction in air. Droplets and cells inside are excited by lasers to generate fluorescence signals, which are analyzed by the control system to selectively activate the cylindrical electrode for sorting28,29. Through dynamically tuning the asymmetry of air pressures to change ejection direction, sorting of single cells on multiple paths can be achieved.

Controlled regulation of droplet ejection with air flow asymmetry

For sorting in the liquid phase, the flow direction of droplets or cells is guided by the channel’s geometric design. While in the air, no fixed boundary constrains flying droplets, and the ejection direction is determined by the droplet’s initial generation. For conventional droplet ejection methods, such as ink-jetting and jetting by electrohydrodynamics, a droplet is ejected by mechanical pulse or electrical pulse; the ejection direction of the droplet is on a fixed path30,31. Although sorting or printing into multiple targets may be achieved by adjustment of the orientation of the receiving substrate or printhead, this would increase the complexity of the sorting system. Ejection with a tunable ejection direction can facilitate the sorting on multiple paths. We design a co-flow geometry, utilizing the shear effect of two independent air flows on a continuous liquid phase to eject droplets. Air pressures of these two air flows can be precisely regulated by electropneumatic transducers, so that air flow asymmetry is controlled to adjust droplet ejection direction (Fig. 2a). We define three sorting paths: path 1 with 3 psi vs 7 psi, path 2 with 5 psi vs 5 psi, and path 3 with 7 psi vs 3 psi. Based on observation by a high-speed camera, we can clearly distinguish three ejection directions of droplets (Fig. 2b, Video S1). Droplet generation in the device works in a dripping regime for different paths, resulting in monodispersed droplets. To characterize the monodispersity, we collect ejected droplets and image them using a microscope (Fig. 2c, left). Diameter analysis shows a good uniformity for all ejection directions (Fig. 2c, right).

Ejection direction can be precisely regulated by the asymmetry of air flows. To investigate the range of ejection angle, we record droplet ejection with different combinations of air pressures. We find that the deflection angle increases with the asymmetry of air pressures. When air pressure is smaller than 2 psi, the shear effect from the air flows is not sufficient to generate droplets; when air pressure is larger than 8 psi, the strong shear effect tends to form polydispersed droplets. With air pressure in the range of 2 psi to 8 psi, if the asymmetry is too large, droplets contact the solid wall, resulting in a failure of ejection. Overall, for the device used in this study, an ejection angle range of ~32.8° is observed (Fig. 2d). In this range, ejected droplets are monodispersed and droplet size decreases with increase of the sum of the two air pressures (Fig. 2e).

A reliable droplet sorting requires a good stability of the ejection direction. In our device, air pressures not only shear the liquid phase at the nozzle but also accelerate it after the generation, resulting in an ejection speed roughly varying from 1 m/s to 3 m/s for different combinations of air pressures. With this relatively high flying speed, the ejection direction can be kept. To confirm this, we measure the ejection direction by switching three sorting paths after different cycles. No significance for all three ejection deflection angles is observed after ~5 × 105 cycles of regulation of air pressures (Fig. S1). These results thus demonstrate the feasibility of air pressure regulation for long-term sorting switch on multiple paths.

Sorting on different paths with a cylindrical electrode

DEP-based sorting has been widely used in microfluidic droplet sorting because of its high throughput and good adaptability for diverse geometries of fluid channels. The working principle is based on the non-uniform electric field generated by the electrode to produce the DEP force on droplets or cells. For droplet-based sorting, the difference in permittivity between droplets encapsulating cells and surrounding media is important32,33. To obtain a desired DEP effect, the shape of the electrode is also critical. Although many efforts have been made to optimize the geometry of the electrode to allow for accurate and stable sorting, these electrodes only work for sorting on very limited paths (mostly just one path)20,34. In our case, droplets are ejected within a direction range in air, and a uniform DEP effect is required for all ejection directions, which cannot be generated by a conventional electrode with a curved or round tip. To enable uniform sorting of ejected droplets on all sorting paths, we design a cylindrical DEP electrode long enough to cover all ejection directions. This electrode has a round shape to magnify the working time and the length to adapt to droplets from different directions (Fig. 3a). Because it is the first design of a cylindrical electrode used for microfluidic droplet sorting, we first carry out simulations to investigate its feasibility. We input parameters of the droplet and device into multiphysics simulation software and model the DEP effect as it is in the real device. The results show that the electrode generates a DEP field around it; when a droplet flies over it, the DEP force can deflect it, and deflection of droplets occurs for all sorting paths (Fig. 3b).

When performing sorting on our device, a constant high voltage is first applied to the electrode, such that any coming droplets are deflected into the collection tube. If a droplet containing a desired single cell is detected by the laser-activated sorter, the high voltage is interrupted to allow for an uninfluenced ejection, leading to a successful sorting event (Fig. 3c, Video S2). The collection tube integrated in the device is used to collect deflected droplets, i.e., waste droplets. A stable collection requires deflected droplets to be introduced into the tube for all sorting paths. Because the tube is fixed in the device, deflection for all paths needs to be accommodated for its position. We measure the deflection distance at a fixed plane away from the ejection nozzle, and find that droplets show similar deflection distances irrespective of ejection angles (Fig. 3d). These results demonstrate that a collection tube integrated at the proper position can efficiently collect waste droplets on all sorting paths.

Single-cell sorting on multiple paths

The combination of tunable droplet ejection and cylindrical electrode enables our device the capability to sort droplets on different sorting paths. To characterize its accuracy for sorting of single cells, we inject a cell suspension with Calcein Green-stained cells into the microfluidic chip and perform single-cell sorting on multiple paths. Ejected droplets are collected in the oil phase, so that droplets and their contents can be imaged and analyzed. We find that, without sorting, most ejected droplets are empty (Fig. 4a, top). When sorting of green cells is applied, only droplets containing green single cells are sorted (Fig. 4a, bottom). Based on independent sorting on path 1, path 2, and path 3, we observe a sorting accuracy of >99% for all sorting paths (Fig. 4b). This sorting accuracy represents a significant improvement compared with FACS. Further improvement of it may be achieved by improving the quality of optics and electronics to increase the stability and sensitivity of the sorting system. The survival rate after sorting is critical if sorted cells are subsequently used for culture. After sorting single cells into well plates, we stain them with live/dead dyes of Calcein Green, AM (live, green) and Ethidium Homodimer-1 (dead, red) (Fig. 4c, left). We observe a cell viability of >92% for all sorting paths. These results demonstrate that our device can accurately sort desired single cells irrespective of sorting paths, and the sorting process is gentle, leading to a high cell survival rate.

Isolation of a subpopulation from heterogeneous cells is of great importance for many biological and biomedical applications. When a cell sample contains several desired types of cells, sorting of multiple subpopulations is required. Sorting on multiple paths provides our device the capability to meet this need. To demonstrate it, we perform a proof-of-concept experiment by sorting three defined subpopulations from a cell sample containing three types of cells. Cell suspension with green, red, and pink labeled cells at the ratio of 33.4%:32.7:33.9% is injected into the device. On sorting path 1, only green cells are sorted; on sorting path 2, green cells and red cells are sorted at the ratio of 1:1; on path 3, only red cells are sorted (Figs. 5a and S2a). In each sorting run, a total of 1000 cells are sorted into the well plate. The sorted cells are imaged using fluorescence microscope (Figs. 5b and S2b–d), and cell numbers are subsequently analyzed. We observe a sorting accuracy of ~95.6% and ~94.7% for green cells on sorting path 1 and red cells on sorting path 3, respectively. On path 2, we finally obtain a cell ratio of ~50.1%: ~ 46.8% for green cells and red cells. These results demonstrate that our device can accurately isolate subpopulations by single-cell sorting, which is also demonstrated for cell populations with different cell types (Fig. S3). Because of limitations of optical sensitivity in the sorting system, some cell doublets or dim cells may be incorrectly sorted, thus leading to a slightly reduced sorting accuracy compared with single-cell sorting from one cell type (Fig. 4b). While, with better optics and improvement of the sorting algorithms to avoid sorting of cell doublets or dim cells, an improved sorting accuracy and capacity would be possible.

Conclusions

We have developed a microfluidic device capable of accurately sorting single cells from a heterogeneous cell population on multiple paths in air. Instead of in a continuous liquid phase, we perform sorting of single cells in air by constructing a co-flow geometry with air flows and the liquid phase. Regulating the asymmetry of air pressures enables a tunable ejection direction of droplets, which subsequently can be sorted by a laser-activated sorting system with a cylindrical electrode. The capability of sorting single cells is further demonstrated by a proof-of-concept experiment by isolating subpopulations from a cell sample with three-color cells. Our device thus provides both the accuracy of conventional DEP-based sorting on a microfluidic chip and the in-air cell sorting capability of commercial FACS. Besides, due to the low pressure of air flow, the shear on the cells during droplet generation and sorting is lower compared with FACS, thus leading to a relatively higher cell survival rate. Compared with MACS, our technology presents a precision manipulation at the single-cell level and is capable of sorting cells with higher accuracy.

Although we only carry out sorting on three distinct paths in this study, however, with the cylindrical electrode, the sorting should work on other paths if they are within the range of tunable ejection direction. Current version of device uses three-color laser, limiting the sorting for three types of cells at most; while, the laser numbers can be further broadened as it uses a similar sorting principle as FACS, in which signals across multiple (up to 18) channels simultaneously can be achieved35. Theoretical sorting paths on our device are already numerous; with the integration of more laser channels, our device thus hold the potential for sorting desired cells from more complex cell populations. This would be valuable for the isolation of multiple subpopulations from heterogeneous cell populations, such as the simultaneous isolation of tumor cells and immune cells from the whole blood of cancer patients. Also, our technology can be integrated with image-based sorting components, which will empower our approach to achieve the sorting of samples directly derived from patients. Together, our method presents an advance in sorting technology and will open up new avenues for multipurpose sorting of single cells.

Materials and methods

Device fabrication

Microfluidic devices (Fig. S4) were fabricated using standard soft lithography with poly(dimethylsiloxane) (PDMS). To make a microfluidic device with air flow-assisted droplet ejection, two layers of structured PDMS slabs were bonded. The PDMS slabs were obtained by replicating masters with SU-8 structures based on soft lithography. To fabricate masters, a standard photography process was performed: photoresist (SU-8, MicroChem, USA) was spin-coated on a 3-inch silicon wafer, baked in an oven, exposed with UV, and developed in SU-8 developer. Prepared PDMS with prepolymer and curing agent at a ratio of 10:1 was cast onto the masters, and with degassing in a vacuum chamber and baking in an oven at 65 °C for 24 h, PDMS slabs were then obtained by extraction from the masters. The inlet holes were punched using a 0.75-mm biopsy core. Before bonding, PDMS slabs were treated by oxygen plasma (Technics Plasma etcher) and aligned under a microscope; subsequently, bonding was performed in the oven at 65 °C for 2 days to render the channel hydrophobic.

Two air flows were used in a microfluidic chip to control the ejection direction of droplets. The air flows were tuned by electropneumatic transducers (900×, Control Air Inc., NH), which were controlled by a custom-made LabVIEW interface. The aqueous phase injected into the microfluidic chip was controlled by syringe pumps (New Era, Farmingdale, NY). The cell detection in the chip was based on the flow cytometry principle. Cells were labeled with fluorescence protein, which was activated by embedded continuous-wave lasers with wavelengths of 473, 532, and 640 nm (CNI Lasers, Changchun, China). Lasers were guided into the microfluidic chip with fibers, with a cladding diameter of 225 μm, an optical core diameter of 200 μm, and an NA of 0.22. To enable sorting in different paths, a cylindrical electrode was made using a solder with a diameter of 0.6 mm, and inserted in the corresponding channel in the microfluidic chip.

Sorting operation

Sorting in different paths was automatically performed by a custom-made LabVIEW interface. The air flows were tuned such that the desired droplet ejection direction was enabled. The cylindrical electrode was connected to a high voltage amplifier (690E-6, Trek, Lockport, NY). When sorting was performed, a DC voltage of ~2 kV was applied, and all droplets were collected into open tubes; selective sorting was actuated by triggering an interval electrical signal to turn off the amplifier.

Characterization of ejection and droplets

The droplet ejection and sorting were monitored by a high-speed camera (Phantom Miro M-310, Vision Research). To characterize ejected droplets, they were first ejected onto an oil phase (Novec HFE-7500, 3 M, supplemented with 0.2% wt/wt biocompatible surfactant) layer on a glass slide, and then pipetted into a count slide (Countess, Invitrogen). These droplets were imaged by a fluorescence microscope (EVOS, ThermoFisher) and analyzed by the ImageJ software. Sorted cells were collected into microwells (96-well microplates with flat bottom, Corning) for subsequent analysis.

Cell sorting

NIH 3T3 cells were used in this study for sorting evaluation. NIH 3T3 cells were cultured in 75-cm2 flasks with base medium of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 4.5 g/L glucose, 0.584 g/L L-glutamine, and 3.7 g/L NaHCO3. Complete growth medium was obtained by supplementing DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics. Subculturing was performed when cells were grown to ~75% confluency. Cells were suspended in PBS for staining. Stained cells were resuspended in complete growth medium with a final concentration of ~1 million/mL for sorting. FITC-dextran (average mol wt 10,000, final concentration 5 μM) was added to the final cell suspension to serve as the background fluorescence dye. For cell viability assays, cells were stained with Calcein green, AM (5 μM final concentration). After cell sorting, 0.5 μL of Ethidium Homodimer-1 (EthD-1, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 5 mM final concentration) was added into each well, followed by incubation in the incubator for 15 min. Viability was examined via fluorescence imaging. For the three-color cell sorting assay, cells were stained with Calcein Green, AM, Calcein Red-Orange, AM, and CellTraceTM Far Red (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a final concentration of 5 μM. Cell count was measured by TC20 automated cell counter (BioRad).
