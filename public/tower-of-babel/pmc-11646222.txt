Identification of a distinctive immunogenomic gene signature in stage-matched colorectal cancer
Authors: Pankaj Ahluwalia, Ashis K. Mondal, Ashutosh Vashisht, Harmanpreet Singh, Ahmet Alptekin, Kalyani Ballur, Nivin Omar, Meenakshi Ahluwalia, Kimya Jones, Amanda Barrett, Vamsi Kota, Ravindra Kolhe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Despite advances in diagnosis and treatment, including surgery, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, accurate clinical markers are still lacking. The development of prognostic and predictive indicators, particularly in the context of personalized medicine, could significantly improve CRC patient management.MethodIn this retrospective study, we used FFPE blocks of tissue samples from CRC patients at Augusta University (AU) to quantify a custom 15-gene panel. To differentiate the tumor and adjacent normal regions (NAT), H&E staining was utilized. For the quantification of transcripts, we used the NanoString nCounter platform. Kaplan–Meier and Log-rank tests were used to perform survival analyses. Several independent datasets were explored to validate the gene signature. Orthogonal analyses included single-cell profiling, differential gene expression, immune cell deconvolution, neoantigen prediction, and biological pathway assessment.ResultsA 3-gene signature (GTF3A, PKM, and VEGFA) was found to be associated with overall survival in the AU cohort (HR = 2.26, 95% CI 1.05–4.84, p = 0.02, 93 patients), TCGA cohort (HR = 1.57, 95% CI 1.05–2.35, p < 0.02, 435 patients) and four other GEO datasets. Independent single-cell analysis identified relatively higher expression of the 3-gene signature in the tumor region. Differential analysis revealed dysregulated tissue inflammation, immune dysfunction, and neoantigen load of cell cycle processes among high-risk patients compared to low-risk patients.ConclusionWe developed a 3-gene signature with the potential for prognostic and predictive clinical assessment of CRC patients. This gene-based stratification offers a cost-effective approach to personalized cancer management. Further research using similar methods could identify therapy-specific gene signatures to strengthen the development of personalized medicine for CRC patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00432-024-06034-4.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a malignancy with the third highest incidence rate globally, with an estimated 1.9 million cases in 2020 (Xi and Xu 2021). Although mortality rates have improved in recent years, innovative advances to reduce the disease burden are urgently required. Significant progress has been made in the management of colorectal cancer patients in recent decades due to advances in early diagnosis and treatment. The American Joint Committee on Cancer/Union for International Cancer Control (AJCC/UICC) tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) classification is a primary tool for clinicians to assess the risk and treatment options of patients, but it does not fully capitalize on immunobiological information (Bruni et al. 2020). Additionally, the TNM classification exhibits variability in the survival and clinical prognosis of patients in the same stage or grade, contributing to misclassification (Bruni et al. 2020; Nagtegaal et al. 2011). Recent advancements, especially the introduction of immunotherapy, have significantly increased the therapeutic options available to patients with cancer. The FDA's 2011 approval of the first checkpoint inhibitor (ipilimumab, anti-CTLA-4), paved the way for the application of these treatments to other solid tumors (Graziani et al. 2022). Despite encouraging results, there are still significant barriers to its application for the majority of patients, with more than 85% of patients not showing an effective response rate for several cancers (Pilard et al. 2021). To improve the clinical outcome for the majority of patients, reliable prognostic and predictive indicators must be identified (Biller & Schrag 2021; Golshani & Zhang 2020). By employing these markers, clinicians can better assess tumor responsiveness and select the optimal treatment strategy.

In recent years, the expansion of genomics has led to the emergence of novel approaches that can enhance our understanding of colorectal cancer. Among these, gene expression signatures have shown potential for application in multiple cancers (Lin et al. 2021; Luo et al. 2022; Xie et al. 2019; B. Zhang et al. 2019a, b). In colorectal cancer, these signatures span diverse biological pathways, including extracellular matrix, endoplasmic reticulum stress, metabolism, necroptosis, and DNA repair (Liang et al. 2022; B. Liu et al. 2022; Lv et al. 2022; Nersisyan et al. 2021; Xiao et al. 2022). These gene signature-based models have the potential to identify patients at higher risk or with poor responsiveness to therapies. These insights can be utilized to personalize the application of alternative therapies based on immunological or molecular profiling. Moreover, excluding non-responding patients can help reduce the financial burden of expensive treatments and prevent adverse drug effects (Carrera et al. 2018; Okwundu et al. 2021).

In this study, we have demonstrated a novel way to strengthen the conventional TNM classification system for CRC by incorporating gene expression signatures. We designed a custom gene panel based on the literature review of biologically and clinically relevant expression profiles in tumors and adjacent to tumor tissue. Additional exploration of orthogonal datasets was performed at multidimensional levels to assess the prognostic, predictive, and personalized utility of the gene signature. We identified a 3-gene signature that showed prognostic utility in an internal cohort, The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset, and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets. The 3-gene signature was associated with poorer survival in these datasets. Further, the tumorigenic role of this gene signature was identified using separate analysis using publicly available datasets such as the Oncomine database and single cell analysis. The elevated expression of the gene signature and its validation across multiple datasets reinforces its promising role in CRC prognosis. At the therapeutic level, this signature was associated with a higher neoantigen load, immune dysfunction, disrupted anti-tumor immune cell infiltration and showed a negative correlation with independently validated immunotherapy response in kidney and melanoma datasets. Further validation of this signature could support the design and optimization of therapeutic strategies for high-risk CRC patients. Overall, our study highlights the potential clinical utility of a custom gene panel in delivering prognostic, predictive, and therapeutic insights. This approach could greatly facilitate personalized medicine for CRC patients.

Material and methods

The design of the gene panel and selection of FFPE tissues

To identify genes that may play a role at clinical and therapeutic levels in cancer, we conducted a literature search using PubMed and identified 15 genes for further analysis (Table 1). The overall flowchart of the study is shown in Fig. 1. Briefly, we ensured that the selected genes were relevant and had been previously studied in the context of cancer, though they had not yet been used for tumor-adjacent analysis or gene signature construction. Additionally, we retrieved the cellular localization of the genes from the Genecards database (Stelzer et al. 2016). For clinical relevance, we assessed the differential function of genes in tumor and NAT regions. To quantify the gene expression, we utilized formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) blocks from Augusta University as previously described (Ahluwalia et al. 2022). The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB-HAC # 611,298) of the Medical College of Georgia at Augusta University. To identify tumor and NAT) regions, hematoxylin and eosin staining (H&E) was performed on 250 blocks of 93 colorectal cancer patients. The standard protocols were used for H&E staining and a distinction between tumors and NAT was made by a board-certified pathologist (Snow et al. 2014). Selected FFPE sections were macro-dissected and total RNA was isolated using the miRNEasy FFPE kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer's protocol. The QC of the RNA was further performed using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (NanoDrop ND-1000, NanoDrop Technologies). Additionally, patients with incomplete documentation, failure of QC at RNA isolation, and degraded RNA were excluded from the study. Flowchart of the study

The quantification of mRNA using the NanoString platform

The RNA isolated from the CRC FFPE blocks was utilized for mRNA quantification using the NanoString platform (NanoString Technologies Inc.). nCounter PlexSet technology is a digital system that quantifies specific RNA molecules using a target-specific oligonucleotide probe pair with high sensitivity and specificity. The target sequences (Supplementary Table 1), Probe A (Supplementary Table 2), and Probe B (Supplementary Table 3) were used in this study. The probes for this gene panel were developed by Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc. This technology utilizes a PlexSet panel with a fluorescently coded barcode coupled to reporter tags and a biotinylated universal capture tag. The specificity of the signals is acquired through a uniquely barcoded fingerprint. The universal capture tag fixes particular RNA molecules to the streptavidin-coated lane of the nCounter instrument. In the downstream processes, the count data was collected using the nCounter Digital Analyzer (DA). As previously mentioned, the field of view (FOV) setting for DA was set to 280 (Kulkarni 2011). This analysis was performed using 300 ng of total RNA as the input for the nCounter assay. The raw gene counts were further processed and normalized using the manufacturer’s nCounter software.

Genomic analysis and identification of prognostic gene signature

The gene panel investigated in the AU cohort was also investigated in the TCGA-COAD (Colon Adenocarcinoma) dataset for further validation. We extracted a total of 435 patients from the TCGA-COAD dataset available on cBioportal. (Gao et al. 2013; Mounir et al. 2019). This dataset was used in this study for genomics, transcriptomics, and other clinicopathological analysis. The mutation and copy number variation data were analyzed and visualized as previously described (Ahluwalia et al. 2022). The prognostic significance of individual genes was assessed in the TCGA dataset using univariate analysis, and the results were mapped based on individual p-values and z-scores. To analyze the distribution, we assessed four clinical endpoints: Progression Free Survival (PFS), Overall Survival (OS), DSS (Disease-Specific Survival), and DFS (Disease Free Survival). The top 3 genes, GTF3A, VEGFA, and PKM, were found to have the highest association with survival values and were subsequently selected for signature development. The Cox regression method was employed to calculate hazard ratios and p-values. Further, the gene signature was validated using four independent datasets, GSE14333 (n = 226 patients), GSE17536 (n = 177 patients), GSE28722 (n = 125), and GSE40967 (n = 574).

Comparison of tumors and normal tissue

To determine the relevance of the 3-gene signature in tumorigenesis based on previous analysis, we searched the Oncomine database. It comprises datasets derived from more than 18,000 gene expression microarrays and is used to identify differential expression patterns in diverse tumors (Rhodes et al. 2007). Additionally, we included the GSE41258 dataset, consisting of 82 samples, to provide an additional comparison between normal and tumor tissues in our analysis of GEO-CRC datasets (70 tumors and 12 normal). To further explore the tumorigenic role of our gene signature, we assessed the single-cell distribution to identify the distribution pattern at higher resolution compared to bulk RNA-Seq, Microarray, and NanoString analysis. For single-cell analysis, the GSE178341 CRC dataset was utilized (62 patients) (Pelka et al. 2021). This single-cell dataset included 371,223 single cells (258,359 CRC tumor cells and 112,864 normal cells).

Gene enrichment analysis, immune infiltration, and immunogenomics analysis of the gene signature

The biological variations in the two risk groups were further explored using functional enrichment analysis. To characterize the differential gene expression based on the distribution of gene signature, the DESeq2 package was utilized (Love et al. 2014). To identify the pathways associated with different risk groups, gene set enrichment analysis was performed using GSEA and the Molecular signature database (Liberzon et al. 2011; Subramanian et al. 2007). In this analysis, 100 patients were compared to identify the most significantly perturbed pathways in the TCGA data set. The genes were pre-ranked based on the fold-change output of the DESeq2 algorithm. The number of permutations was set at 1000. The weighted enrichment statistic was employed and the “Signal2Noise” metric was used for ranking genes. The pathway resources used were derived from Hallmarks, Biocarta, KEGG, and Wikipathways. Further, we applied the T cell dysfunction and exclusion prediction (TIDE) algorithm to evaluate the predictive properties of our gene signature (Jiang et al. 2018). TCGA-COAD expression data for patients was analyzed using the TIDE algorithm generating output scores that were quantified and compared between two groups: patients with a higher gene signature associated with higher risk and those with a lower gene signature associated with lower risk. In parallel, this gene signature was tested on two independent solid cancer datasets—kidney and melanoma—due to the unavailability of CRC-specific response data in TIDE. (Jiang et al. 2018; D. Liu et al. 2019; Miao et al. 2018). Within the TIDE framework, these datasets support the broader applicability of the predictive potential of our 3-gene signature markers. The TIDE algorithm incorporates a range of immune-related markers, each uniquely contributing to the assessment of tumor immune dynamics and response to immunotherapy. Briefly, TIDE quantifies immune evasion, where TAM (Tumor-Associated Macrophages) range from M1 (pro-inflammatory) to M2 (anti-inflammatory); CAF (Cancer-Associated Fibroblasts) are stromal cells that support tumor growth; and MDSC (Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cells) inhibit immune response. "Exclusion" refers to immune cell exclusion, preventing immune cells from entering the tumor, while "Dysfunction" indicates T-cell dysfunction, reducing T-cell killing efficiency. CD8 represents CD8 + T cells, which are cytotoxic immune cells targeting tumors. CD274, or Programmed Death-Ligand 1 (PD-L1), is an immune checkpoint protein. Merck18 is Merck’s 18-Gene Signature, MSI denotes microsatellite instability, indicating DNA mismatch repair defects, and IFNG (Interferon Gamma) is a cytokine that promotes immune activation (Jiang et al. 2018). Additionally, tumor neoantigen load was compared between high-risk and low-risk patients using The Cancer Immunome Atlas (TCIA) to identify all putative neoantigens (Charoentong et al. 2017). Genes with more than 10 predicted neoantigens were selected from each patient group. These genes were profiled using g:Profiler, which integrates data from multiple major databases including Gene Ontology, KEGG, Reactome, CORUM, Ensembl, Human Phenotype Ontology, WikiPathways, and TRANSFAC (Kolberg et al. 2023). This analysis identified overrepresented biological pathways and processes. Additionally, GSVA (Gene Set Variation Analysis) was used to quantify the distribution of immune cell infiltration between the high-risk and low-risk groups (Hanzelmann et al. 2013).

Statistical methods

The unprocessed count data was normalized using nCounter software (NanoString Technologies Inc.). In brief, the geometric mean of the negative and positive control samples was utilized to standardize the expression across all samples. Additional normalization was carried out using six internal control genes (ABCF1, GUSB, HPRT1, LDHA, POLR1B, and RPLO). The hierarchal clustering was performed using Ward’s method to visualize the differential expression of genes in the AU cohort. The principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the GEPIA portal (Tang et al. 2017). The p-values of the prognostic values of each gene were derived from cBioportal based on the z-score. Kaplan–Meier analysis and a log-rank test were used to compare the survival distribution in the AU, TCGA, and GEO datasets. The immune cell infiltration was analyzed using the non-parametric Wilcoxon method. All the statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.2, GraphPad Software), R (version 4.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) (http://www.R-project.org/), and SAS-JMP (version 16, SAS Institute). The p values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

The sub-cellular analysis of these genes in the tumor microenvironment revealed that PKM expression is most prevalent in the cytosol, the nucleus, the mitochondria, and the extracellular matrix (Table 1). To compare the genes with other cancers, we performed a mutation analysis. Among the top 10 genes based on distribution across all cancers, a higher frequency of mutations was observed in STAT1, GATA3, and MCM4 in major cancer types (Fig. 2A). In a comparative analysis of these cancers, these genes showed a relatively higher mutation frequency in CRC compared to other cancers (Fig. 2B). The prognostic significance of the gene signature

The survival distribution in the TCGA dataset was used to evaluate the prognostic relationship of individual genes. GTF3A expression showed a higher association with PFS, DSS, and DFS. VEGFA demonstrated a higher correlation with PFS and DFS. PKM showed an association with DFS (p < 0.10) (Fig. 4A). In the AU cohort, the 3-gene signature (GTF3A, PKM, and VEGFA) was found to be linked with overall survival (HR = 2.26, 95% CI 1.05–4.84, p = 0.03, 93 patients) (Fig. 4B). In the TCGA cohort, it was found to be associated with overall survival (HR = 1.57, 95% CI 1.05–2.35, p < 0.02, 435 patients), progression-free survival (HR = 1.56, 95% CI 1.08–2.27, p < 0.01, 435 patients), disease-free survival (HR = 2.52, 95% CI 1.03–6.14, p < 0.03, 180 patients), and disease-specific survival (HR = 1.91, 95% CI 1.03–3.22, p < 0.01, 420 patients) (Fig. 4C,D, and Supplementary Figs. 1A, B, respectively). Also, the higher gene expression was associated with the stage and involvement of lymph node metastasis in the TCGA dataset (Table 2). In the GSE17536 dataset, the gene signature was significantly associated with reduced disease-free survival (HR = 3.67, 95% CI 1.72–7.81, p < 0.01, 177 patients) (Fig. 5H). In the GSE14333 dataset, overall survival showed a significant association with the prognostic gene signature (HR = 2.02, 95% CI 1.12–3.63, p < 0.01, 226 patients) (Fig. 5I). The predictive component of the gene signature

To further investigate the potential role of the 3-gene signature in immune modulation, we utilized the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) and The Cancer Immunome Atlas (TCIA) analysis. Analysis using TCIA revealed distinct neoantigen enrichment patterns between risk groups. High-risk patients showed enrichment for pathways related to ATP-binding, cell cycle processes, and sphingolipid signaling (Fig. 7A, Supplementary Table 4). In contrast, low-risk patients exhibited enrichment in genes associated with protein binding, anatomical structure formation, bile secretion, and extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation (Fig. 7B, Supplementary Table 5). Comparative analysis of top hits between risk groups revealed significant enrichment of cell cycle processes in the high-risk group and extracellular matrix (ECM) genes in the low-risk group (Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 6). Immune GSVA analysis revealed higher gene expression associated with neutrophils in high-risk patients, while expression was negatively correlated with cytotoxic and NK cells (Fig. 7C, Supplementary Table 7). Furthermore, the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm revealed a correlation between high-risk patients and increased immune dysfunction, marked by higher levels of Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cells (MDSCs), as well as elevated Dysfunction and TIDE scores in these patients (Fig. 7D). Low-risk patients, on the other hand, were associated with infiltration of TAM, or tumor-associated macrophages (Fig. 7E). Our 3-gene signature demonstrated superior predictive ability compared to established biomarkers, including mutation count, MSI score, IFNG, and T and B cell clonality, in independent immunotherapy response datasets for renal cancer and metastatic melanoma (Supplementary Figs. 2A and 2C, respectively). Discussion

The advent of genomics has provided an unprecedented opportunity to investigate and evaluate novel gene signatures for prognostic and therapeutic use. Among these, the transcriptomic assessment holds immense clinical promise across a spectrum of conditions, from malignancies to infectious diseases (Gliddon et al. 2018; Kaforou et al. 2022; Narrandes and Xu 2018; Walker et al. 2021). Moreover, comparing gene expression between healthy and diseased states can reveal new predictors of disease susceptibility, progression, and severity. (Porcu et al. 2021). In cancer, the clinical manifestation is influenced by complex parameters that affect both immune infiltration and gene expression in surrounding tissues. Recent evidence suggests that NAT regions exhibit a unique expression state, distinct from both tumor and healthy tissue (Aran et al. 2017; Kulinczak et al. 2022; Russi et al. 2019). This biological heterogeneity, caused by genetic variations among individual patients, has impeded the development of precise prognostic and predictive biomarkers. While several prognostic markers have been identified over the past decade, their clinical utility has been limited (Ahluwalia et al. 2021; Liang et al. 2022; B. Liu et al. 2022; Lv et al. 2022; Nersisyan et al. 2021; Qian et al. 2021; Xiao et al. 2022). To address these gaps, we investigated the relevance of gene expression signatures for clinical utility in colorectal cancer.

In this study, we aimed to identify the prognostic and predictive properties of a gene panel using internal Augusta University samples and multiple publicly available datasets. We assessed our gene signature's clinical utility at multiple resolutions, employing NanoString analysis for our internal dataset. For independent datasets, we utilized bulk RNA-Seq analysis, microarray analysis, and single-cell resolution. Our findings revealed that higher expression of a 3-gene signature (GTF3A, VEGFA, and PKM) was associated with worse survival in both internal and external cohorts. Additionally, this 3-gene signature was significantly associated with pathway perturbations, pro-tumor immune cell infiltration, and dysfunction. Among the biological pathways, TNF signaling was predominantly associated with high-risk patients. Tumor-promoting inflammation has emerged as an important regulator of tumorigenesis and cancer progression (Grivennikov 2013). Indeed, inflammation through the aberrant TNF-α–mediated NF-κB axis has been associated with more than 50% of colorectal cancer cases (Karin and Greten 2005; Li et al. 2021). Increased activity of this pathway has been linked to poor prognosis in CRC, tumor growth, and chemotherapy resistance (Al Obeed et al. 2014; Huang et al. 2022; Kobelt et al. 2020).

The immunogenomic profiling of the risk subgroups based on the gene signature identified promising avenues for future therapies. Differential expression of neoantigens between risk groups revealed distinct biological pathways. Functional enrichment analysis showed that high-risk patients had a significant neoantigen burden in pathways related to cell cycle processes—biological functions often implicated in tumor growth, immune evasion, and metabolic adaptation. Conversely, low-risk patients exhibited enrichment in genes associated with anatomical structures and the extracellular matrix (ECM). This aligns with the literature indicating that aggressive tumors have higher mutational and neoantigen loads compared to non-aggressive tumors (Marques et al. 2024; Meeks et al. 2020). The enrichment of ECM-related neoantigens in low-risk patients suggests a microenvironment where the ECM significantly influences the immune landscape. This finding supports that targeting the ECM could enhance the infiltration of anti-tumor immune cells and improve treatment outcomes in low-risk patients (Gordon-Weeks and Yuzhalin 2020). In addition to the neoantigen variation between high- and low-risk CRC patients, significant immune differences were also observed. The assessment of tumor dysfunction revealed higher infiltration of immunosuppressive MDSCs, along with elevated immune dysfunction and TIDE scores in the high-risk group. MDSCs play a significant role in the tumor microenvironment by suppressing the anti-tumor immune response in various chronic infections and cancers. (Tcyganov et al. 2021). It has been demonstrated that MDSCs negatively impact the prognosis of CRC (Yin, Xia, Rui, Wang, & Wang, 2020). In addition, the presence of MDSCs can drastically reduce the efficacy of checkpoint inhibitor therapy (Awad et al. 2018; Barrueto et al. 2020; Kamran et al. 2017). Comparatively, low-risk tumors were found to be associated with tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). Monocytes in the tumor microenvironment exhibit high plasticity, with roles that can shift contextually to either support or inhibit tumor growth (Jeong et al. 2019; Kim and Zhang 2016). Several of their functions, ranging from the modification of the extracellular matrix to differentiation into macrophages or dendritic cells can directly affect tumor progression (Olingy et al. 2019). Macrophages exist along a spectrum from M1 (pro-inflammatory, anti-tumor) to M2 (anti-inflammatory, pro-tumor) phenotypes, and they can perform both pro- and anti-tumor roles depending on the context. TAMs have been associated with favorable outcomes in CRC (Cavalleri et al. 2022). Furthermore, cross-cancer analysis of the 3-gene signature in kidney and melanoma datasets demonstrates its predictive potential in other solid tumors. It also effectively stratified patients based on survival in both renal and melanoma datasets suggesting that immune evasion mechanisms and the signature's role in modulating tumor dynamics may be conserved.

In this study, we identified and validated a unique gene signature with prognostic utility and explored its potential to predict which patients may benefit from immunotherapies. Following validation in independent external cohorts, we examined the signature's predictive properties for immunotherapy response through five interlinked analyses and observations: First, pathway analysis revealed differences in inflammation and metabolic and homeostatic functions in high-risk patients. Second, neoantigen analysis indicated a higher load of active cellular processes in high-risk patients compared to the homeostatic profile observed in low-risk patients. Third, GSVA analysis identified elevated neutrophil infiltration in high-risk patients and increased cytotoxic and NK cell infiltration in low-risk patients. Fourth, the TIDE algorithm revealed immune dysfunction in the high-risk group. Finally, we extended our assessment by testing the predictive ability of the gene signature in two TIDE datasets (renal and melanoma solid tumors), given the lack of validated colorectal cancer datasets. Our integrated analysis associated this gene signature with high immune dysfunction scores in high-risk patients and elevated TAM scores in low-risk patients, suggesting that the signature can partially predict immune response in CRC patients (Cavalleri et al. 2022; Tcyganov et al. 2021). Coupled with its promising prognostic performance and survival stratification in independent solid tumors, these findings emphasize its clinical potential. However, this approach has limitations, particularly as the TIDE algorithm has not yet been validated for colorectal cancer. Therefore, our findings should be considered preliminary and provide a foundation for further research as noted by others (Farha et al. 2023; Ma et al. 2023; Zheng et al. 2024). It is also important to note that due to the intricate nature of the tumor microenvironment, classical immune cells alone only partially explain the underlying tumor heterogeneity. For instance, a recent study characterized these intricate subsets in colorectal cancer. At single-cell resolution, a distinct population of TAMs, the SPP1 + subset, was found to be associated with pro-angiogenic properties. In contrast, a pro-inflammatory C1QC + subset showed enrichment for genes involved in antigen presentation and phagocytosis, thus demonstrating anti-tumor properties. The C1QC + subset was also identified as a promising candidate for combination with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies. (Zhang et al. 2020). Thus, to fully exploit the prognostic and predictive roles of these immune cells, it is essential to annotate their functions and map their differential gene expression in tumor-specific contexts using emerging technologies (Jia et al. 2022).

In summary, we identified a 3-gene signature that showed prognostic and predictive properties associated with altered biological pathways, pro-tumor cell infiltration, and immune dysfunction. In addition, immune deconvolution analysis of bulk RNA-seq can yield novel insights into the intricate dynamics of dysregulated pathways and perturbed immune infiltration. Additional gene expression-based prognostic research within the framework of prognostic classifiers can expand the number of prognostic and predictive biomarkers that can aid in the development of novel therapies.

Strength and limitations

In this study, we conducted a retrospective analysis of an internal cohort of Colorectal cancer (CRC) and independent datasets to identify a prognostic gene signature. We used highly sensitive and specific NanoString technology to quantify mRNA transcripts and developed a gene signature that is scalable, cost-effective, and has a reduced turnaround time, making it practical and feasible for use in diverse healthcare settings. Furthermore, we validated our findings using independent colorectal cancer datasets, demonstrating the prognostic properties of our gene signature. Although our study has several strengths, we also acknowledge its limitations. First, prospective validation of these results is necessary to optimize the prognostic model for real-world applications. Second, the predictive potential of our gene signature should be interpreted with caution, as the TIDE algorithm has not been validated for colorectal cancer. Additionally, further investigation is needed to address inter- and intra-patient variability, sex disparities, and age-related differences that could affect the model’s accuracy across diverse populations.

Conclusions

Genomics has revolutionized patient care by providing unprecedented access to molecular variables associated with cancer progression. However, its full potential remains unrealized, and accurate markers are needed to guide clinicians in making better therapeutic decisions. Transcriptomics offers a high-resolution view of molecular and immune dynamics in patients, presenting an opportunity to develop novel biomarkers. Our study focused on developing and validating a risk-based gene expression signature for colorectal cancer (CRC) patients. This signature can enhance personalized care and provide cost-effective methods for precision medicine screening. We demonstrate the potential of panel-based gene signatures to improve CRC patient prognosis, potentially leading to more effective risk stratification, better therapeutic approaches, and improved outcomes. Integrating classical approaches like TNM classification and screening methods with emerging technologies such as gene expression signatures, liquid biopsy, circulating tumor DNA, and immunological contexture can achieve a comprehensive and personalized approach to cancer care.
