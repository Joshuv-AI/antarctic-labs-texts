Deciphering the mechanisms of macrophage polarization through comprehensive analysis of cellular and cell-surface protein glycosylation
Authors: Xing Xu, Yue Wu, Kejun Yin, Ronghu Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTargeting tumor-associated macrophages holds great promise for cancer immunotherapy. Surface glycoproteins in macrophages regulate their interactions with tumor cells and represent emerging therapeutic targets. Given the importance of glycoproteins in immunity and cancer, we systematically investigate the total and surface glycoproteomes in polarized macrophages using multiplexed proteomics coupled with selective enrichment. In monocyte-derived macrophages, distinct changes of N-glycoproteins are observed between M1 and M2 phenotypes, with more pronounced alterations in pro-inflammatory M1. In M1, glycoproteins involved in chondroitin sulfate-type glycosaminoglycan (CS-GAG) biosynthesis are upregulated, validated by the elevated CS-GAG levels, and enzymes associated with nucleotide sugar biosynthesis are also upregulated, consistent with the increased hexokinase activity. Furthermore, surface glycoproteomic analysis of primary human macrophages with M1 or M2 phenotype (HM1 and HM2) reveals phenotype-specific remodeling: HM1 display enhanced innate immune signaling, whereas surface glycoproteins related to adhesion and endocytosis are upregulated in HM2. Integrative analyses of cell-surface glycoproteomic, transcriptomic, and tissue-specific datasets enable prioritization of candidate surface proteins associated with M2 phenotype, including LIPA, CD200R1, and CR1, and phenotype-specific changes of several surface glycoproteins are validated by immunofluorescence imaging. Together, this study provides insights into macrophage functional specialization and advances our understanding of cell-surface glycoproteins.Subject terms: Proteomics, Mass spectrometry

Introduction

Macrophages are a type of innate immune cell widely distributed throughout the entire mammalian body, and they play crucial roles in maintaining tissue homeostasis and safeguarding against infection. When stimulated by specific cues from the tumor microenvironment (TME), macrophages are activated and polarized into functionally distinct subpopulations generally classified into two phenotypes: classically activated macrophages (M1) and alternatively activated macrophages (M2)1. Notably, macrophages are one of the most abundant immune cells in the TME, referred to as tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), comprising up to 50% of total cell mass in solid tumors2. Within the TME, TAMs predominantly adopt an M2-like phenotype in response to cytokines such as IL-4. These M2-like TAMs promote cancer cell proliferation, metastasis, and immunosuppression. In contrast, M1 macrophages can interact with other components of the innate and adaptive immune systems, including T lymphocytes and natural killer cells, to enhance antitumor immunity. Thus, suppressing the M2-like phenotype of TAMs has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy in cancer therapy3,4.

Given the importance of macrophages in immunity and cancer, considerable progress has been made in understanding macrophage polarization and discerning differences between M1 and M2 macrophages, thereby advancing the development of strategies for reprogramming TAMs5–7. Metabolic and transcriptional studies showed that M2 macrophages exhibited elevated consumption of glutamine5,8. Consequently, blocking glutamine metabolism by small molecules resulted in decreased tumor growth9,10. Several kinases, such as PI3Kγ/δ and MEK, were reported to promote M2-type polarization, leading to the study of small molecule inhibitors for disrupting these kinases11,12. Additionally, targeting cell-surface proteins on TAMs, including SIGLEC713, SIGLEC1014, SIGLEC1515, SIRPα16, VISTA17, and LILRB family18, exhibited substantial potential in enhancing antitumor responses.

Considering that more than 60% of drugs in DrugBank target cell-surface proteins19, these proteins represent a valuable source for potential disease diagnosis and therapy20. Almost all cell-surface proteins are glycosylated, and protein glycosylation plays pivotal roles in microenvironment sensing, intercellular communication, and immune modulation21–23. Several studies have highlighted abnormal glycosylation as a promising target for immunotherapy24,25. Despite the availability of extensive RNA-seq datasets for both cell lines26 and patient-derived TAM samples27, transcript-level expression may not be correlated well with protein abundance, particularly for surface proteins. Furthermore, RNA-seq data cannot capture alterations in protein glycosylation. Mass spectrometry (MS)-based proteomics is very powerful to globally analyze proteins and their modifications, such as glycosylation28–38. While previous studies examined global proteomic changes among different macrophage phenotypes39–41, systematic profiling of the cell-surface glycoproteome during macrophage polarization, the functional interface between macrophages and their environment, remains to be explored. Moreover, most large-scale proteomic studies rely on immortalized cell lines such as THP-1 monocyte-derived macrophages. Although the THP-1 monocyte-derived macrophage is a well-established model for studying macrophage polarization, its transformed cell-line nature may not fully capture the complexity of primary human macrophages.

In this work, we establish an integrated proteomic framework to systematically characterize the proteome, N-glycoproteome, and cell-surface glycoproteome remodeling during macrophage polarization. In THP-1 monocyte-derived macrophages, we perform integrated analyses of the whole-cell proteome, the N-glycoproteome, and the surfaceome to investigate molecular changes associated with M1 and M2 polarization. These analyses reveal extensive and phenotype-specific glycoproteome remodeling, including increased abundance of glycoproteins involved in chondroitin sulfate-type glycosaminoglycan (CS-GAG) biosynthesis and elevated expression of enzymes associated with nucleotide sugar biosynthesis in M1 macrophages. The results are further verified by increased levels of CS-GAGs and enhanced hexokinase activity in M1 macrophages measured by orthogonal methods. To assess the physiological relevance of macrophage polarization, we subsequently profile the surfaceomes of primary human macrophages representing distinct polarization states (HM0, HM1, and HM2). Comparative analysis of these datasets allows us to evaluate these different macrophage polarization models, delineate conserved and divergent surface features, and identify candidate cell-surface proteins associated with the immunosuppressive M2 phenotype. Furthermore, using an orthogonal (immunofluorescence) method, we validate the abundance changes of several important surface glycoproteins (PTGFRN, ITGA3, and LIPA) in M1 and M2 macrophages. The multi-layered proteomic analyses provide a comprehensive resource for further studying macrophage biology and the roles of cell-surface glycoproteins in macrophage functional specialization.

Results

Systematic profiling of THP-1-derived macrophages reveals the changes in the proteome, glycoproteome and surfaceome

To systematically characterize molecular remodeling during macrophage polarization, we established a multi-omics framework using THP-1 monocyte–derived macrophages (a widely used model for macrophage polarization) (Fig. 1a)42–44. THP-1 cells were treated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) for 24 h to induce differentiation into unpolarized macrophages (M0). Then, M0 macrophages were polarized into M1 macrophages with the IFN-γ/LPS treatment or into M2 macrophages with the IL-4 treatment. These M1- and M2-like macrophages recapitulate the opposing polarization states of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs)45,46.

For each polarization state, aliquots of the same cell population were processed for the whole-cell proteome (WP), N-glycoproteome (NG), and cell-surface glycoproteome (SG) analyses. For the analysis of WP, around 5% of the cells were taken from each of the M0, M1, and M2 macrophage samples. These cells were subsequently lysed, and proteins were digested. Peptides from the samples were labeled with the tandem mass tag (TMT) reagents, respectively. The labeled samples were then pooled and subjected to LC-MS analysis. For the analysis of NG, around 25% of the cells were taken from each of the M0, M1, and M2 macrophage samples. Following cell lysis, protein extraction and digestion, N-glycopeptides were enriched using our previously published method, i.e., the DBA enrichment method47. For the analysis of SG, around 65% of the cells were taken from the M0, M1, and M2 macrophage samples, respectively. Subsequently, we employed an enzymatic tagging approach to separate and enrich N-glycoproteins only on the cell surface48. Briefly, these intact cells were treated with neuraminidase to remove sialic acid, followed by oxidation with galactose oxidase (GAO) in the presence of horseradish peroxidase (HRP), converting the hydroxyl group at the C6 position of the Gal/GalNAc residues to an aldehyde group. The enrichment was achieved using hydrazide beads, and N-glycopeptides were eluted by methoxylamine. Enriched N-glycopeptides were labeled with the TMT reagents, respectively, and then pooled and dried, followed by the treatment with PNGase F in heavy-oxygen water (H218O) to remove N-glycans and, at the same time, to introduce a unique isotopic tag at the glycosylation sites for MS analysis.

With the treatment of IFN-γ/LPS or IL-4, we observed the M1 or M2 phenotypes polarized from THP-1-derived macrophages, and they displayed markedly different cell morphologies (Fig. 1b), as reported previously49,50. PBS-treated macrophages (M0) served as a control. Exposure to IFN-γ/LPS, which stimulated M1-type polarization, led to cellular elongation, whereas the addition of IL-4, which induced M2-type polarization, caused cells to be flattened and rounded with elongated filopodia. Furthermore, we quantified well-established M1 markers (e.g., IDO1, GBP1, and CXCL10) (Fig. 1c) in IFN-γ/LPS-treated cells, and M2 markers (e.g., TGM2, FABP4, and CD209) (Fig. 1d) in IL-4-treated cells to further validate the formation of M1 and M2 macrophages51,52. Gene ontology (GO)-based clustering of upregulated proteins in M1 or M2 with 1.5-fold-change also showed distinct biological functions among M1 and M2 macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 1). In M1 macrophages, proteins related to MHC class II protein complex binding, defense response to virus, and IFN-γ-mediated signaling pathway are highly enriched (Supplementary Fig. 1), whereas in M2 macrophages, proteins related to integrin binding, fatty acid transport, collagen binding, and wound healing are overrepresented (Supplementary Fig. 1). Collectively, these results clearly demonstrate that M1 and M2 macrophages were successfully induced as reported previously52.

In total, we quantified 8994 proteins from biological replicate experiments (WP: 8455 proteins; NG: 1466 glycoproteins; SG: 548 surface glycoproteins; Fig. 2a; Supplementary Data 1, 2, and 3). To assess the changes at the N-glycosylation site level among M1 and M2 macrophages, we quantified 2970 well-localized N-glycosylation sites from 5387 N-glycopeptides. Moreover, we quantified 1336 well-localized N-glycosylation sites from 1884 surface N-glycopeptides (Supplementary Data 4 and 5). To characterize sequence and structural features of protein N-glycosylation, we compared regulated N-glycosylation sites versus all quantified N-glycosylation sites and all Asn residues in the NG in terms of their relative positions in protein sequences, relative accessibility, and disorder scores (Supplementary Fig. 2). We found a preference of either regulated N-glycosylation sites or quantified N-glycosylation sites occurring in the middle region, and these sites were found to be preferentially located in regions with relatively high accessibility and disorder. These findings can be explained by the fact that there is a low occurrence of sequons at both the N- and C-termini, and the regions with high accessibility or disorder exhibit higher N-glycosylation efficiency53. To ensure that quantified N-glycopeptides in the SG are from the cell surface, we employed the surfaceome predictor, SURFY54, and subcellular location information from the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID)55 to analyze these glycoproteins in the SG. As a result, all quantified glycoproteins are either cell-surface-associated or secreted.

To test the reproducibility of the current datasets, we first compared all quantified proteins within the replicates of the same treatments and found high coefficients with a median of 1.00, indicating high reproducibility (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). Next, we performed principal-component analysis (PCA) of all quantified proteins, and the results from M0, M1, and M2 macrophages were clustered separately, indicating that these macrophages have distinct protein signatures (Fig. 2b, c and Supplementary Fig. 5a). Furthermore, we created a matrix of Pearson’s correlation coefficients comparing abundance changes of glycoproteins in M1- and M2-type macrophages either in the NG or SG (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 5b). Generally low correlations (0.2–0.3) were found between M1 and M2 macrophages in both the NG and the SG, indicating substantial differences in their glycoproteomes. Notably, cell-surface glycoproteins exhibited even lower correlation between M1 and M2 macrophages (a median value of correlation coefficients in NG: 0.29; in SG: 0.25), suggesting more pronounced alterations in cell-surface glycoproteins compared to total glycoproteins.

M1 polarization induces more pronounced alterations in N-glycosylation than M2 polarization in THP-1 monocyte-derived macrophages

To investigate the N-glycosylation changes during macrophage polarization, we performed comparative N-glycoproteomic analyses between THP-1-derived M1 and M2 macrophages. The overall distributions of log₂-fold-changes (log2 FC) revealed widespread changes of N-glycoproteins in both phenotypes, with more extensive alterations observed in M1 macrophages (Fig. 3a). To further elucidate these differences of glycoprotein alterations, we first identified glycoproteins whose abundances changed significantly in macrophage polarization, with the fold-changes of >1.5 and adjusted p-value < 0.05. Among 1466 glycoproteins quantified here, 36.4% of glycoproteins were regulated in M1 macrophages (308 upregulated, 225 downregulated), while only 13.7% of glycoproteins were regulated in M2 macrophages (106 upregulated, 95 downregulated). Only 8.3% of glycoproteins were regulated in both M1 and M2 macrophages (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 6), underscoring more pronounced N-glycosylation alterations in M1 macrophages.

To investigate functional differences in regulated glycoproteins, we performed GO analysis among these differentially regulated glycoproteins (Fig. 3c). Immune-related terms such as cytokine-mediated signaling pathway (p = 2 × 10-13), regulation of cell killing (p = 2.8 × 10-7), and regulation of T cell-mediated cytotoxicity (p = 4.5 × 10-6) were strongly enriched among 298 glycoproteins, reflecting key features of M1 macrophages. Importantly, glycoproteins involved in the chondroitin sulfate (CS) glycosaminoglycan biosynthetic process (e.g., CHPF, CHST2, CHST3, CHST11, CHSY1, and DSE; p = 1.7 × 10-13) were upregulated in M1 (Fig. 3d). Proteoglycans have been shown to play important roles in blocking the conversion of pro-inflammatory immune cells to a pro-repair phenotype and stimulating the production of proinflammatory molecules56,57. Interestingly, the abundance of the corresponding parent proteins did not change significantly following macrophage polarization, suggesting that the observed alterations primarily occurred at the glycosylation level. To determine whether these glycoprotein changes were associated with altered CS glycosaminoglycan production, we quantified chondroitin sulfate-type glycosaminoglycans (CS-type GAGs) across different macrophage phenotypes. Consistent with the glycoproteomic results, CS-type GAG levels were significantly elevated in M1 macrophages (Fig. 3e). The upregulation of glycoproteins involved in the chondroitin sulfate glycosaminoglycan biosynthetic process may contribute to the pro-inflammatory phenotype of M1 macrophages.

In contrast, glycoproteins related to cell adhesion (p = 4 × 10-26), animal organ development (p = 1 × 10-6), cation transport (p = 4 × 10-5), and coagulation (p = 7 × 10-5) were overrepresented in M2 macrophages. Consistent with the higher endocytic ability of M2 macrophages58,59, endocytosis-related glycoproteins (p = 2 × 10-7) were also enriched. These glycoproteins exhibited distinct changes in the abundance of their glycoproteoforms. For example, calcitonin gene-related peptide type 1 receptor (CALCRL) has two N-glycosylation sites quantified in the NG dataset, N66 and N123. Its proteoform with the N-glycosylation site at N123 was increased by more than eightfold in M2, while the proteoform with the N-glycosylation site at N66 has no significant change (Fig. 3f). Sortilin (SORT1) has five N-glycosylation sites, with the glycoproteoforms at N162, N274, and N684 increasing in M2 but decreasing in M1, while those at N98 and N406 showed no significant changes in either M1 or M2 (Fig. 3g). Mannose receptor 1 (MRC1), an endocytic receptor and M2 macrophage biomarker, exhibited a dramatic increase in its glycoproteoform with the N-glycosylation site at N1205 in M2 macrophages (Fig. 3h). Furthermore, glycoproteins related to angiogenesis (p = 2 × 10-5) were enriched in M2 macrophages. For example, fibronectin (FN1) has five N-glycosylation sites (N139, N430, N528, N542, and N1291), three of which (N139, N430, and N528) were upregulated in M2 (Fig. 3i). Aminopeptidase N (ANPEP) has seven N-glycosylation sites at N128, N234, N265, N625, N681, N735, and N818. Among them, the glycoproteoforms at N128, N265, N735, and N818 were upregulated in M2 macrophages (Fig. 3j). These upregulated glycoproteoforms may play important roles in modulating M2 macrophage activities.

Protein glycosylation remodeling during macrophage polarization is primarily driven by changes in protein expression and sugar donors

To unravel the mechanisms driving remodeling of protein N-glycosylation during macrophage polarization, we explored correlations in abundance changes between N-glycoproteins and their corresponding parent proteins. Comparison of these abundance changes revealed that 55% of genes in M1 macrophages and 57% of genes in M2 macrophages displayed a moderate or strong correlation between N-glycoproteins and their parent proteins (Pearson’s r > 0.5) (Fig. 4a). This finding suggests that the abundance changes of N-glycoproteins are largely influenced by the expression levels of their parent proteins. Then, we examined N-glycosylation-related enzymes quantified in M1 and M2 macrophages. Notably, the abundance of most N-glycosylation-related enzymes remained unchanged during macrophage polarization, except for keratinocyte-associated protein 2 (KRTCAP2), which displayed a modest increase in M1 macrophages (Fig. 4b). These results suggest that while the N-glycosylation machinery remains relatively stable, specific enzymes may play a role in fine-tuning glycosylation during macrophage polarization.

Besides the enzymes involved in the protein N-glycosylation machinery, the abundance of glycoproteins may also be determined by another important factor, i.e., the local concentrations of nucleotide sugars such as UDP-GlcNAc and UDP-Man60. To investigate this, we assessed the expression of enzymes involved in nucleotide sugar biosynthesis. We found the elevated levels of hexokinases (HK1/2), N-acetylglucosamine kinase (NAGK), and phosphoglucomutase 3 (PGM3) in M1 macrophages, while no significant alterations were detected in M2 macrophages (Fig. 4c–f). Hexokinases catalyze the phosphorylation of glucose to glucose-6-phosphate, a key precursor in nucleotide sugar biosynthesis, thus playing an important role in the protein glycosylation pathway61. Additionally, NAGK and PGM3 are important enzymes in the Hexosamine Biosynthetic Pathway (HBP), which produces UDP-GlcNAc required for N-glycosylation62. To further validate these proteomic results, we measured total hexokinase activity across macrophage phenotypes using an orthogonal enzymatic activity assay. Using M0 macrophages as the baseline reference, M1 macrophages exhibited significantly higher HK activity than M2 macrophages (Fig. 4g). This result is consistent with the increased abundance of enzymes associated with hexosamine metabolism in M1 macrophages. Collectively, these findings indicate that enhanced expression and activity of enzymes involved in nucleotide sugar biosynthesis increased nucleotide sugar availability in M1 macrophages, thereby contributing to the more extensive N-glycoprotein alterations observed in M1 relative to M2 macrophages.

Surface glycoprotein profiling reveals phenotype-specific patterns in THP-1 monocyte-derived macrophages

Given that cell-surface glycoproteins play crucial roles in cell signaling, cell survival, and cell identity, we systematically investigated cell-surface glycoproteins in M1 and M2 macrophages. We first compared the abundance changes in the SG and the WP between M1 and M2 macrophages and observed more pronounced alterations in the cell-surface glycoproteome compared to the whole cell proteome (Fig. 5a). Among 1884 cell-surface glycopeptides quantified from the SG biological triplicate experiments, 682 N-glycosylation sites were regulated in M1 macrophages (320 upregulated, 362 downregulated), while 362 N-glycosylation sites were regulated in M2 macrophages (176 upregulated, 186 downregulated) (Fig. 5b, c). When comparing the proportion of regulated glycoproteins across the WP, NG, and SG datasets, cell-surface glycoproteins exhibited the highest proportion of regulated proteins. Specifically, in the SG dataset, 52.9% and 27.2% of cell-surface glycoproteins were regulated in M1 and M2 macrophages, respectively, while 36.4% and 13.7% of glycoproteins in the NG dataset were regulated in M1 and M2 macrophages, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Comparison of glycoprotein abundance changes between the NG and SG datasets reveals that 51% of glycoproteins in M1 macrophages and 47% of glycoproteins in M2 macrophages displayed a moderate or strong correlation (Pearson’s r > 0.5) (Supplementary Fig. 7b). These findings highlight dramatic remodeling of cell-surface glycoproteins during the macrophage polarization, with more pronounced alterations than those observed in the total N-glycoproteome.

To investigate the differences in the changes of cell-surface glycoproteins caused during the macrophage polarization, we first performed a hierarchical clustering analysis of 548 quantified cell-surface glycoproteins, resulting in three clusters: Cluster I includes 226 cell-surface glycoproteins exhibiting the characteristics of M1 macrophages; Cluster II consists of 215 cell-surface glycoproteins mainly displaying the features of M2 macrophages; and Cluster III has 107 cell-surface glycoproteins with similar regulatory patterns in both M1 and M2 macrophages (Fig. 5d). We next explored the differences among these cell-surface glycoproteins in each cluster. Based on biological process, the distinctive GO terms identified in each cluster are presented in Fig. 5e. Cell-surface glycoproteins in Cluster I were enriched in immune-related pathways, including cytokine-mediated signaling pathway (IL1R1, IL3RA, IL6R, IL10RA, etc.), adaptive immune response (HLA-DRA, HLA-DRB1, LILRB1, LILRB2, CD274, etc.) (Fig. 5f), and cytokine-mediated signaling pathway (IL13RA1, CSF2RB, etc.) (Fig. 5g). Cell-surface glycoproteins in Cluster II were found to be enriched in integrin-mediated signaling pathway (ITGA5, ITGB1, ITGB3, ITGAV, etc.) (Fig. 5h), cell junction organization (TGFB1, ESAM, THSD1, etc.), and tissue remodeling (TIMP1, LIPA, JAG1, etc.). Interestingly, we found GO terms such as inorganic ion homeostasis, cation homeostasis, and cation transport also enriched in Cluster II (Fig. 5i), suggesting that the functions of M2 macrophages may rely more on inorganic ions.

Cell-surface proteins can be categorized into transporters, receptors, and enzymes. To gain deeper insights into the expression changes of these proteins during macrophage polarization, we analyzed 102 glycoforms of 53 transporters, 493 glycoforms of 153 receptors, and 125 glycoforms of 46 enzymes and compared their abundance changes between M1 and M2 macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 8). Distinct regulatory patterns were observed among transporters, receptors, and enzymes. Solute carrier (SLC) transporters regulate many important physiological activities, including nutrient uptake, ion influx/efflux, and waste disposal, and previous research suggested their involvement in regulating macrophage polarization63,64. Thus, we focused on the abundance changes of these SLC families and found that 27 glycoforms from 19 SLC families were regulated in M1, while 9 glycoforms from 7 SLC families were regulated in M2. For example, UDP-sugar transporter protein (SLC35A5) with the N-glycosylation sites at N204 and N218, and glucose transporter type 9 (SLC2A9) with an N-glycosylation site at N90, exhibited increased abundance in M1 but remained unchanged in M2. Glucose transporter (SLC2A1) with the N-glycosylation site at N45 was downregulated in M2 but showed no alteration in M1. Furthermore, zinc transporters (SLC39A6 with the N-glycosylation sites at N67, N241, and N226; SLC39A10 with the N-glycosylation sites at N218 and N339) and sodium bicarbonate cotransporters 3 (SLC4A7) with the N-glycosylation sites at N776, N786, and N796 were downregulated in M1, but exhibited no change or a slight increase in M2 (Supplementary Fig. 9). Considering the metabolic rewiring in macrophage polarization, this finding suggests that metabolite transporters on the cell surface may contribute to defining macrophage phenotypes. Collectively, these results reveal that macrophage polarization triggers extensive and functionally coherent remodeling of the cell-surface glycoproteome. M1 macrophages display enrichment of immune-regulatory and cytokine-signaling receptors, whereas in M2 macrophages, adhesion molecules, ion-transport systems, and tissue-repair mediators are upregulated. While the THP-1 model provides a tractable platform for dissecting these multi-omic dynamics, immortalized cell lines may not fully capture the regulatory complexity of primary human macrophages. To evaluate the physiological relevance of these observations, we next profiled the surface glycoproteome of primary human macrophages.

Surfaceome analysis of primary human macrophages uncovers the physiological context of cell-surface remodeling

Although THP-1-derived macrophages are an excellent and widely used model to study macrophage polarization, they may be different from primary human macrophages and thus lack physiological relevance. Next, we studied primary human macrophages and systematically investigated surface glycoproteins on unpolarized (HM0), classically activated (HM1), and alternatively activated (HM2) macrophages. Approximately 4 × 10⁵ cells from each phenotype were subjected to an enzymatic tagging approach designed to selectively label and enrich cell-surface N-glycoproteins (Fig. 6a). To enhance surface glycoprotein identification and quantification due to the limited number of primary macrophages, an additional ~1 × 10⁵ cells from each phenotype were lysed, and enriched N-glycopeptides from the whole-cell lysates were used as a boosting channel during LC–MS/MS analysis65,66. In total, 320 cell-surface glycoproteins were confidently quantified across three technical replicates per phenotype (Supplementary Data 6). Annotation using SURFY54 and the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID)55 revealed that all quantified proteins were plasma-membrane or secreted proteins, validating the surface specificity of the current experiment. Some well-documented M1 and M2 markers were detected at the expected levels, confirming the polarization fidelity: CD80 was elevated in HM1 macrophages, whereas CD209 and MRC1 were upregulated in HM2 macrophages (Fig. 6b). The results from the replicates for each phenotype showed strong pairwise correlations, and the principal-component analysis demonstrated clear separation among the HM0, HM1, and HM2 samples (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Figs. 10–12), indicating high reproducibility and distinct surfaceome signatures for each phenotype.

We next assessed the surfaceome remodeling across the macrophage phenotypes. Similar to THP-1-derived cells, HM1 macrophages exhibited more extensive changes than HM2 macrophages (Fig. 6d). Among 320 quantified proteins, 51% were significantly altered in HM1 macrophages (86 upregulated, 76 downregulated), whereas 30% were regulated in HM2 macrophages (54 upregulated, 43 downregulated) (Supplementary Fig. 13 and Supplementary Data 6). These results indicate that macrophage polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 state induces more pronounced surfaceome remodeling than M2 polarization. Hierarchical clustering of all quantified cell-surface glycoproteins revealed three major clusters corresponding to distinct patterns (Fig. 6e). Cluster I contains 160 glycoproteins enriched in HM1 macrophages, Cluster II includes 121 glycoproteins elevated in HM2 macrophages, and 39 glycoproteins in Cluster III have similar alterations in both polarized states.

To validate the proteomic results, we selected PTGFRN (also called CD315), a cell-surface glycoprotein highly enriched in HM2 macrophages, for verification using an orthogonal method, i.e., immunofluorescence imaging. This protein has been reported to inhibit the binding of prostaglandin F2-alpha (PGF2-alpha) to its specific FP receptor and has also been implicated in cancer progression67,68. However, it has never been reported to be involved in macrophage polarization. In our work, we found that PTGFRN exhibited markedly increased abundance (5.8-fold) in HM2 compared to HM1 macrophages (Fig. 6f). The results from the immunofluorescence imaging experiment clearly confirmed the elevated expression of PTGFRN on the cell surface of HM2 compared to HM1 macrophages (Fig. 6g).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of surface glycoproteins in Clusters I and II revealed distinct biological processes associated with each polarization state (Fig. 6h). HM1-enriched surface proteins were involved in innate immune response and positive regulation of the canonical NF-κB signaling pathway, consistent with classical activation. Glycoproteins associated with Toll-like receptors (TLR2, TLR4, TLR7, and TLR8) were notably elevated in HM1 macrophages (Fig. 6i), whereas no significant difference was observed in proteins linked to adaptive immune or cytokine-mediated signaling pathways. Conversely, HM2-enriched proteins were significantly associated with positive regulation of angiogenesis, receptor-mediated endocytosis, and leukocyte cell-cell adhesion (Fig. 6h). Among these, the integrin-mediated signaling pathway was prominently upregulated in HM2 macrophages (Fig. 6j), exemplified by ITGA3, which showed a 3.8-fold increase compared with HM1 macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 14a). Immunofluorescence imaging further validated the upregulation of ITGA3 on the cell surface of HM2 macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 14b). Together, these findings indicate that HM1 macrophages display enriched innate immune signaling capacity, whereas in HM2 macrophages, surface glycoproteins related to adhesion, endocytosis, and angiogenesis were upregulated.

Comparative analysis between THP-1 monocyte-derived and primary human macrophages shows similarities and differences

To assess THP-1 monocyte-derived macrophages and primary human macrophages, we directly compared their quantified cell-surface glycoproteomes. As shown in Figs. 5a and 6d, the two systems exhibited extensive remodeling of surface glycoproteins upon polarization, with the most pronounced changes observed in the M1 phenotype. Due to the limited starting materials of primary human macrophages, as expected, fewer surface glycoproteins were quantified. Despite this, 255 glycoproteins were quantified in the two systems, indicating substantial overlap between the two systems (Fig. 7a). However, the correlations of polarization-induced abundance changes were low, with Pearson r values ranging from 0.23 to 0.28 for M1-type polarization and 0.10 to 0.18 for M2-type polarization (Fig. 7b). The low correlations indicate substantial differences between THP-1-derived and primary macrophages. THP-1 monocyte-derived macrophages are a leukemia cell line and exhibit altered basal transcriptional programs and receptor expression levels compared with primary human macrophages69,70. Moreover, the IFN-γ/LPS-induced M1 polarization may trigger broad and dynamic transcriptional and post-translational changes, whereas IL-4–mediated M2 polarization involves a narrower signaling program that may be more stable and reproducible across these two systems. To further visualize these relationships, we rank-ordered the log₂ fold-changes of individual surface glycoproteins. The abundance changes of surface glycoproteins in THP-1-derived M1 macrophages are relatively more consistent with those in primary human M1 macrophages, while weaker correlation and higher variability are observed between THP-1-derived M2 macrophages and primary human M2 macrophages (Fig. 7c, d). Collectively, this comparative analysis underscores that while THP-1-derived macrophages are a useful and experimentally tractable model for studying macrophage polarization, primary human macrophages are more physiologically relevant and may therefore be particularly valuable for helping identify cell-surface proteins associated with distinct macrophage phenotypes.

Prioritization of candidate cell-surface proteins associated with M2-type macrophages

After performing the surfaceome analysis of primary human macrophages, we next leveraged this dataset to prioritize candidate cell-surface proteins associated with the M2 phenotype. Because more than 60% of approved drugs in DrugBank target cell-surface glycoproteins19, we reasoned that our dataset may contain valuable information to develop novel strategies for M2 macrophage inhibition in cancer immunotherapy. We first selected proteins highly enriched in HM2 macrophages from our quantitative cell-surface glycoproteome dataset. These candidates were then cross-referenced with monocyte- and macrophage-specific genes in the Human Protein Atlas to confirm preferential expression in immune cells rather than ubiquitous distribution across tissues71. To ensure their tumor relevance, we further compared our HM2-enriched proteins with the transcriptomic datasets of M2-like tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs)27,72. Finally, to minimize potential off-target effects, we used the GTEx single-nucleus RNA-sequencing (snRNA-seq) database to exclude proteins with high expression in major healthy tissues73, as bulk tissue samples often contain resident macrophages that may confound expression profiles (see “Methods”). Based on these criteria, we generated a composite ranking system integrating immune enrichment, tumor relevance, and tissue specificity to calculate a prioritization index for each candidate surface glycoprotein (Fig. 8a and Supplementary Fig. 15; Supplementary Data 7). Among all candidates, macrophage scavenger receptor 1 (MSR1, also known as CD204) exhibited the highest prioritization index. MSR1 is a known M2-type macrophage marker within the tumor microenvironment74,75, validating the effectiveness of our ranking system to select potential targets. Notably, eight of the top fifteen ranked targets identified by our analysis have been previously explored in clinical contexts, such as ANPEP, CTSC, CYSLTR1, and KIT, or have been recognized as discovery- or tractable-stage targets in the Open Targets Platform (e.g., FCGR1A, SIRPA, ITGAM, LILRA2, and CPM) (Fig. 8b)76.

We next examined high-ranked candidates from our integrative analysis that have not yet been previously implicated in macrophage biology or extensively investigated in the context of tumor-associated macrophages, including those in Open Targets. Among the top fifteen candidates, CPM and ANO6 showed strong expression in HM2 macrophages; however, GTEx data indicated moderate expression in several normal tissues, including breast, esophagus, heart, lung, and skeletal muscle (Supplementary Fig. 15). Thus, we focused on CD200R1, CR1, and LIPA because they are enriched in M2-type macrophages across both our proteomic datasets and the TAM transcriptomic studies, and they exhibit minimal expression in other cell types and normal tissues according to GTEx and the Human Protein Atlas (Supplementary Data 7). Because N-glycosylation critically influences protein trafficking and function at the cell surface77, we next compared site-specific abundance changes for these candidates between HM1 and HM2 macrophages (Supplementary Data 8). CD200R1, CR1, and LIPA displayed increased N-glycosylation in HM2 macrophages (Fig. 8c–e). CD200R1 acts as an inhibitory checkpoint by binding CD20078, and upregulation of N-glycosylation in CD200R1 in HM2 may further suppress phagocytosis of tumor cells. In contrast, enhanced N-glycosylation of CR1 likely reflects increased efferocytosis of apoptotic cells and tumor-derived debris, an immunosuppressive hallmark of TAMs that limits antigen exposure and supports tumor progression79. LIPA (lysosomal acid lipase) is a hydrolase essential for cholesteryl ester and triglyceride degradation, contributing to lipid metabolism and immune cell programming80. Notably, LIPA exhibited markedly elevated surface expression in HM2 macrophages, with N-glycosylation at the site of N72 showing a greater than 12-fold increase compared with HM1 macrophages (Fig. 8e). Enhanced N-glycosylation may promote LIPA trafficking to, or retention at, the cell surface, supporting the lipid-driven metabolic adaptations characteristic of the immunosuppressive M2 phenotype81. Immunofluorescence imaging confirmed that LIPA is more abundant on the surface of HM2 macrophages (Fig. 8f). Together, this integrative analysis delineates a high-confidence list of candidate targets for HM2 macrophage inhibition, combining our quantitative surface glycoproteomic datasets with transcriptomic and tissue expression datasets. Among these, LIPA, CD200R1, and CR1 emerge as promising, previously underexplored cell-surface proteins whose glycosylation status and expression patterns are uniquely associated with the immunosuppressive M2 phenotype. These findings provide a valuable resource for future studies on the functional roles of these proteins in macrophage biology.

Discussion

Low T-cell infiltration remains a major challenge for anticancer therapy, in part due to an M2-like macrophage barrier that restricts T-cell access to tumor cells82. Although these macrophages are immunosuppressive in tumors, macrophages inherently possess antitumor functions as innate immune cells that do not require antigen specificity. Thus, reprogramming or inhibiting M2-like macrophages represents a promising strategy to enhance T-cell infiltration and synergize with existing cancer immunotherapies. Given the pivotal roles of protein N-glycosylation in cellular functions, particularly immune responses, it is critical to systematically investigate it and quantify cell-surface glycoproteins during macrophage polarization. In this work, we established an integrated proteomic framework that combines whole-proteome, N-glycoproteome, and cell-surface glycoproteome analyses in THP-1 monocyte-derived macrophages, together with surfaceome profiling of primary human macrophages. This strategy enabled us to distinguish changes associated with global protein abundance from those related to glycoproteome remodeling and to identify candidate surface glycoproteins potentially involved in macrophage-mediated immune regulation.

In THP-1 monocyte-derived macrophages, our analyses revealed extensive remodeling of proteins and glycoproteins during macrophage polarization, with substantially more pronounced alterations observed in M1 macrophages than in M2 macrophages. Notably, glycoproteins involved in chondroitin sulfate glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis were significantly enriched in M1 macrophages, and orthogonal quantification further confirmed the elevated abundance of CS-type glycosaminoglycans in M1 macrophages. Proteoglycans and CS-type glycosaminoglycans were reported to regulate inflammatory signaling and immune-cell phenotypic transitions56,57, and these findings demonstrated that inflammatory macrophage polarization is strongly related to coordinated remodeling of extracellular glycan-associated pathways in addition to protein-level changes. The increased abundance of enzymes involved in CS-type glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis further supports the interpretation that glycan remodeling represents an important component of macrophage functional specialization.

Previous studies demonstrated that macrophage polarization is accompanied by major metabolic rewiring, including changes in glycolysis and hexosamine biosynthetic pathways5,83. Consistent with these findings, we observed elevated abundance and activity of enzymes involved in nucleotide sugar biosynthesis in M1 macrophages, including HK1, HK2, NAGK, and PGM3. Importantly, orthogonal validation using a hexokinase activity assay further supported enhanced hexosamine-related metabolism in M1 macrophages. Increased nucleotide sugar availability may contribute to more extensive N-glycoprotein changes observed in M1 macrophages. In contrast, most enzymes directly involved in the canonical N-glycosylation machinery remained relatively stable during polarization, indicating that the glycoproteome remodeling is driven primarily by coordinated changes in protein expression and nucleotide sugar rather than enzymes, i.e., glycosyltransferases, responsible for protein N-glycosylation.

Among all proteomic layers analyzed, the cell-surface glycoproteome exhibited the highest degree of regulation during macrophage polarization, highlighting the importance of surface remodeling in macrophage functional specialization. M1 macrophages displayed enrichment of receptors involved in inflammatory and innate immune signaling, whereas M2 macrophages exhibited elevated abundance of glycoproteins associated with adhesion, endocytosis, angiogenesis, and ion transport. Notably, several proteins showed site-specific regulation of N-glycosylation rather than uniform changes across all glycosylation sites, meaning that individual glycoproteoforms are critical in macrophage polarization. These findings further support that protein glycosylation may fine-tune receptor function and cellular communication during macrophage activation.

Our comparative analysis between THP-1 monocyte-derived macrophages and primary human macrophages reveals both the similarities and distinctions between an immortalized cell model and a physiologically relevant system. Cell-surface glycoprotein profiling demonstrated partial overlap between THP-1-derived and primary human macrophages, including shared enrichment of surface glycoproteins related to innate immune signaling pathways in M1 phenotypes and increased expression of adhesion- and endocytosis-related glycoproteins in M2 phenotypes. These shared features suggest that core polarization-associated programs are preserved across the two systems. However, the overall correlations of surface glycoprotein abundance changes remained relatively low, particularly in M2 macrophages, underscoring substantial context dependency in macrophage surface remodeling. These differences likely reflect the distinct biological origins of an immortalized leukemia-derived cell line versus primary human macrophages differentiated from peripheral blood monocytes. Moreover, IFN-γ/LPS-induced M1 polarization involves broad transcriptional and post-translational reprogramming, whereas IL-4-driven M2 polarization follows a more restricted and stable signaling program that is better preserved across different model systems. Thus, although THP-1 macrophages provide an experimentally tractable platform for mechanistic investigation, primary human macrophages more closely reflect physiologically relevant immune states.

M2-type macrophages promote tumor progression by dampening cytotoxic T-cell activity, facilitating angiogenesis, and remodeling the extracellular matrix to support metastasis84,85. Thus, characterizing their cell-surface proteins may provide insights into the mechanisms underlying macrophage functional specialization. Characterizing the surfaceome of primary human macrophages is particularly important for identifying candidate proteins associated with distinct macrophage phenotypes. To systematically prioritize candidate surface proteins, we developed a composite ranking strategy integrating multiple layers of information: (1) quantitative enrichment in HM2 macrophages; (2) immune cell specificity; (3) tumor relevance; and (4) low expression in major healthy tissues. This multi-parameter scoring system yielded a prioritization index for each surface glycoprotein, enabling us to rank candidates by predicted selectivity. Using this approach, several surface proteins, such as LIPA, CD200R1, and CR1, emerged as top candidates enriched in M2 macrophages and tumor-associated macrophage transcriptomes but minimally expressed in normal tissues. Site-specific analysis of N-glycosylation suggests functional modulation of receptor activity, such as CD200R1 N122 and LIPA N72. The combination of surface specificity, low tissue background, and functional glycosylation signatures highlights these proteins as candidates for future mechanistic and functional studies.

Although the present workflow enabled large-scale, site-specific quantification of N-glycoproteins, it did not resolve glycan structural heterogeneity or microheterogeneity at individual glycosylation sites. Future studies integrating intact glycopeptide analysis or glycomics approaches will be valuable for further characterizing glycan structural remodeling during macrophage polarization. Second, the current work focused primarily on N-glycosylation, whereas O-glycosylation also plays important roles in immune regulation and cell-surface biology. Investigation of O-glycosylation changes in polarized macrophages will therefore represent an important direction for future studies. Furthermore, the primary human macrophages used here were independently derived from single donors for each phenotype, and donor-to-donor variability was not systematically assessed. Future studies using macrophages derived from many donors will be important for evaluating the generalizability of the observed glycoproteomic alterations. Additionally, although our prioritization strategy favored targets with relatively low expression in healthy tissues to minimize potential on-target toxicity, this filtering approach may exclude biologically important TAM regulators expressed in tissue-resident macrophages. Additional functional studies, including macrophage–T-cell co-culture systems and in vivo tumor models, will therefore be required to determine the functional significance of these candidates and evaluate their potential relevance in tumor-associated macrophages.

In conclusion, we established an integrated multi-omics framework to systematically characterize the proteome, N-glycoproteome, and cell-surface glycoproteome remodeling during macrophage polarization. Beyond the expression changes at the proteome level, our results demonstrate that macrophage functional specialization is accompanied by extensive remodeling of glycosylation-associated pathways, including the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway (HBP) and CS-GAGs biosynthesis, together with site-specific alterations of cell-surface glycoproteins. These findings establish glycosylation remodeling as an important regulatory feature of macrophage polarization that is closely linked to immune signaling, metabolic adaptation, extracellular matrix interactions, and immunosuppressive functions. Importantly, comparative analyses of THP-1 monocyte-derived macrophages and primary human macrophages revealed both conserved and context-dependent surfaceome features, underscoring the value of primary human macrophages for physiologically relevant investigations of macrophage biology. Integrative analysis of quantitative glycoproteomics with transcriptomic and tissue expression datasets enabled prioritization of surface proteins associated with immunosuppressive macrophage states, including LIPA, CD200R1, and CR1, highlighting them as high-priority targets for future functional investigation. Collectively, this work advances our understanding of glycosylation-mediated immune regulation and highlights the importance of cell-surface glycoproteins in macrophage biology with potential translational implications. The comprehensive datasets and analytical framework presented here provide a foundation for future mechanistic studies of macrophage biology and glycosylation-dependent immune regulation.

Methods

Cell culture and macrophage polarization

THP-1 cells (ATCC) were maintained in RPMI1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Macrophage polarization was introduced as reported previously.44 Once the cell density reached 6 × 105 cells/mL, THP-1 cells were treated with 50 nM phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) for 24 h in T300 flasks. After the PMA treatment, cells were incubated for 2 additional days with either (1) fresh medium (RPMI1640 + 10% FBS + 1% penicillin-streptomycin), (2) fresh medium + interferon-γ (IFN-γ, 20 ng/mL) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 10 ng/mL), or (3) fresh medium + interleukin-4 (IL-4, 20 ng/mL).

Whole-cell proteomics analysis

Cells were washed with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) three times, then scraped and pelleted. Cells were lysed by adding 200 µL lysis buffer containing 50 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 % sodium deoxycholate (SDC), 20 units benzonase (Pierce Universal Nuclease) and 1 protease inhibitor tablet/10 mL at 4 °C for 45 min on an end-over-end rotator. The cell lysate was centrifuged at 25,830 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected.

Proteins were reduced with 5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) at 56 °C for 25 min, and then were alkylated with 14 mM iodoacetamide for 30 min in the dark at room temperature. The alkylation reaction was quenched by incubating with DTT at a concentration of 5 mM in the dark for another 15 min. Then, proteins were purified using the chloroform/methanol precipitation method. Briefly, for 1 mL solution, we added 4 mL methanol, 1 mL chloroform, and 3 mL H2O. The solution was centrifuged at 4600 × g for 10 min, and the top phase was removed. This process was performed again. Purified proteins were digested with sequencing grade modified trypsin in the digestion buffer containing 1.6 M urea and 50 mM HEPES (pH = 8.2) at 37 °C overnight. The digestion was quenched by adding trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) until the pH was ~2. Peptides were purified using a Sep-Pak Vac tC18 cartridge and dried in a lyophilizer overnight for the subsequent tandem mass tag (TMT) labeling.

Enrichment of glycopeptides using DBA

Glycopeptides were enriched using the dendrimer-conjugated boronic acid derivative (DBA) method developed in our lab previously.47 Peptides from M0, M1, and M2 macrophages were dissolved in the binding buffer (DMSO, 0.5% TEA) and incubated for 30 min with the dendrimer-conjugated boronic acid-derivatized magnetic beads at room temperature. After the incubation, the beads were washed with the washing buffer (50% H2O, 50% DMSO, 100 mM NH4OAc, pH = 10) four times. Then, enriched glycopeptides were eluted twice through the incubation with a solution containing ACN: H2O: TFA (50: 49:1) at room temperature for 1 h. The enriched samples were dried in a lyophilizer overnight for the subsequent TMT labeling and the PNGase F treatment.

Enzymatic labeling of cell-surface glycoproteins

Cells were washed with warm PBS three times, then treated with 250 mU/mL neuraminidase (sialidase) in PBS at 37 °C for 30 min. The cells were then scraped and pelleted. Glycans of proteins on the cell surface were oxidized using a solution containing 5% FBS, 50 U/mL galactose oxidase (Innovative Research), and 40 U/mL horseradish peroxidase (Sigma) at 37 °C with end-over-end rotation for 1 h.48 Then cells were pelleted again at 500 × g for 3 min. The cell pellets were treated with digitonin in an ice-cold buffer containing 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM HEPES, 25 µg/mL digitonin (Sigma-Aldrich), and 1 tablet/10 mL protease inhibitor (Sigma-Aldrich) at 4 °C for 10 min on an end-over-end rotator. For each sample, the solution was centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min. The membrane pellets were lysed in the same lysis buffer as the whole cell proteome analysis. Hydrazide beads (Thermo) were washed twice with water and added to each lysate. Aniline was also used as a catalyst at a concentration of 10 mM. The enrichment proceeded at 4 °C with end-over-end rotation for 24 h. Then the beads were washed twice with the washing solution containing 8 M urea, 0.4 M ammonium bicarbonate, and 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS). Proteins were reduced and alkylated as above. The beads were washed with the washing solution four more times. The beads were resuspended in the digestion buffer. After on-bead digestion, the beads were washed with (1) 80% ACN, 0.1% TFA, (2) the washing solution, (3) dimethyl formamide, and (4) 0.1 M ammonium bicarbonate, 5 times each. The enriched peptides were eluted twice using the elution buffer containing 0.2 M methoxylamine hydrochloride, 1.5 M NaCl, and 0.1 M aniline in 0.1 M sodium acetate solution pH = 4.5, at 37 °C for 30 min. The eluants were pooled, desalted using a Sep-Pak cartridge, and dried in a lyophilizer overnight for subsequent TMT labeling and PNGase F treatment.

Measurement of the activity of hexokinases

M0, M1, and M2 macrophages were generated as described above. The hexokinase activity was measured using a colorimetric Hexokinase Assay Kit (ab136957, Abcam) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were lysed by sonication in the assay buffer, and then the clarified lysates were incubated with either the reaction mixture or the background reaction mixture in 96-well plates. Absorbance at 450 nm was monitored kinetically every 1 min for 60 min using a Synergy H4 multi-mode microplate reader (BioTek). The hexokinase activity was calculated based on the NADH standard curve generated according to the manufacturer’s protocol. To account for differences in sample loading, the hexokinase activity was normalized by the total protein amount in each sample.

Quantification of chondroitin sulfate (CS)-type glycosaminoglycans

M0, M1, and M2 macrophages were generated as described above. Chondroitin sulfate-type glycosaminoglycans (CS-GAGs) were quantified using a Human Chondroitin Sulfate (CS) ELISA Kit (EK700410, AFG Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Standards and samples were analyzed in 96-well plates. After incubation with the HRP-conjugate reagent and chromogenic substrates, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Synergy H4 multi-mode microplate reader (BioTek). CS-GAG concentrations were determined from the standard curve and subsequently normalized to total protein.

Analysis of cell-surface glycoproteins in primary human macrophages

Primary human macrophages with three different types of phenotypes (HM0, HM1, and HM2) were purchased from STEMCELL Technologies. According to the manufacturer, each macrophage phenotype was derived independently from peripheral blood monocytes obtained from a single individual donor. These peripheral blood monocytes were isolated by negative immunomagnetic selection and cultured for 5 days in serum-free ImmunoCult™-SF Macrophage Medium supplemented with macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) to generate M0 macrophages. For M1 macrophages, monocytes were cultured under identical conditions and then activated with IFN-γ/LPS for the final 2 days. For M2 macrophages, IL-4 was added during the last 2 days of culture. Frozen cells were thawed and immediately processed to preserve cell-surface integrity. Technical triplicates were performed for each phenotype to assess the reproducibility of measurements. Approximately 4 × 10⁵ cells from each phenotype were subjected to an enzymatic tagging protocol for selective labeling and enrichment of cell-surface N-glycoproteins, following the same procedures used for THP-1–derived macrophages. Briefly, intact cells were sequentially treated with neuraminidase, galactose oxidase, and horseradish peroxidase to oxidize terminal galactose and N-acetylgalactosamine residues, and glycopeptides with the aldehyde group were captured using the hydrazide beads. N-Glycopeptides were released using methoxylamine and then processed for tandem mass tag (TMT) labeling. To enhance the identification of low-abundance surface glycoproteins, approximately 1 × 10⁵ additional cells from each phenotype were lysed and enriched N-glycopeptides using the DBA beads were used as boosting channels during LC–MS/MS analysis.

TMT labeling and PNGase F treatment

Dried peptides or glycopeptides from THP-1-derived macrophages (M0, M1, and M2) were labeled with each channel (M0: 126, 127N, 127C; M1: 128N, 128C, 129N; M2:129C, 130C, 131) of the multiplexed TMT reagents (Thermo), respectively, following the manufacturer’s protocol. For primary human macrophages, TMT labeling was performed using the same procedure, with the following channel assignments: HM0-126, 127N, 127C; HM1-128N, 128C, 129N; HM2-129C, 130N, 130C; Boosting channel-131. The labeled peptide or glycopeptide solutions were mixed and then purified using the tC18 Sep-Pak cartridges and dried overnight. The PNGase F treatment was performed by dissolving completely dried and TMT-labeled peptides in 40 µL of 40 mM ammonium bicarbonate (pH = 9) (Sigma-Aldrich) in heavy-oxygen water (H218O) with 3 µL of 1 unit/uL PNGase F (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37°C for 3 h with shaking86. The reaction was quenched with formic acid (FA) till the pH reached ~2.0. The samples were desalted again with the stage-tip method and dried overnight.

High-pH HPLC fractionation and LC-MS/MS analysis

Samples from THP-1 monocyte-derived macrophages were fractionated using high-pH HPLC with a 40-min gradient of 5–55% ACN in 10 mM ammonium acetate (pH = 10). The sample for the whole proteome (WP) analysis was separated into 24 fractions, while the N-glycoproteomic sample (NG) was separated into 15 fractions, and the surface glycoproteomic sample (SG) into 12 fractions. Each fraction was purified using the stage-tip method and dried again. Then, samples were dissolved in a solution with 5% ACN and 4% FA. Samples from primary human macrophages were fractionated into five fractions using the stage-tip method. For each sample, four microliters of the solution were loaded by a Dionex WPS-3000TPLRS autosampler (UltiMate 3000 thermostatted Rapid Separation Pulled Loop Wellplate Sampler) onto a microcapillary column packed with C18 beads (Magic C18AQ, 1.9 μm, 200 Å, 75 μm × 16 cm, Michrom Bioresources). Peptides were separated using a nanoflow reversed-phase HPLC (Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano, Dionex). The buffer A was constituted with 0.1% formic acid in water, and the buffer B contained 0.1% formic acid in ACN. The microcapillary column was directly coupled to the MS for analysis using a Nanospray Flex ion source. The peptides were analyzed using an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo). For the WP analysis, peptides were separated using a 95-min gradient of 2–26% buffer B at 0.3 µL/min. The full MS spectra were recorded using the following parameters: MS scan range: 350–1600 m/z; resolution: 120,000; Maximum injection time: Auto; AGC target: Standard. The precursor ions were selected for fragmentation by data-dependent acquisition (DDA). The most abundant precursor ions in each full MS scan were selected for fragmentation for 2 s at maximum. The following parameters were used for tandem MS scanning: Isolation width: 0.7 m/z; Isolation specificity: 90%; Resolution: 45,000; Normalized collision energy: 38%; AGC target: Standard; Max injection time: 100 ms. The selected ions were excluded for 45 s. For the NG and SG analysis, deglycopeptides were separated using a 95-min gradient of 5–30% buffer B at 0.3 µL/min. The parameters of full MS and tandem MS scanning are the same as the WP analysis.

Database search and data filtering

The raw files were converted into the mzXML format and searched against the human (Homo sapiens) proteome database from UniProt using the SEQUEST algorithm (version 28)87. The following parameters were used for NG and SG during the search: 10 ppm precursor mass tolerance; 0.025 Da fragment ion tolerance; fully digested with trypsin; up to two missed cleavages; fixed modification: carbamidomethylation of cysteine ( + 57.0214), TMT labeling of the lysine residue ( + 229.1629) and the peptide N-terminus ( + 229.1629); variable modifications: oxidation of methionine ( + 15.9949), 18O tag of asparagine ( + 2.9883, N-glycosylation site). The parameters for WP searching were the same except for the 18O tag of asparagine ( + 2.9883, N-glycosylation site). The false discovery rates (FDR) of peptide and glycopeptide identifications were controlled to less than 1%. The search results were further filtered by using multiple parameters such as XCorr ( > 1.2) and precursor mass error ( < 10 ppm) to better control the quality of the dataset, and the FDR was controlled to <1% at the protein level. Peptides with fewer than six amino acid residues were discarded. For the quantification, the isotopic information provided by Thermo was utilized to calibrate the ion intensities. The signal-to-noise ratio of the TMT reporter ions was used. S/N > 5 is required for the quantification of every peptide.

The confidence of glycosylation site localization was estimated by ModScore88. All sites with ModScore >13 (p-value < 0.05) were considered confidently localized. For site-specific analysis, all glycopeptides were further checked and only the sites with the consensus motif, NXS/T/C (X refers to any amino acid except proline) were kept. For site-specific quantification, only singly glycosylated peptides with well-localized sites were analyzed. For quantification of unique glycopeptides, the ratio was calculated from the sum S/N of all glycopeptides for this unique glycopeptide. For quantification of glycoproteins, the ratio was calculated from the ratios of all unique peptides from the protein. To correct for sample loading variation, the log₂-transformed reporter ion S/N intensities were normalized using the trimmed median intensity of all quantified peptides or glycopeptides, followed by mean-centering across samples89. Differential expression analysis was performed using the limma package in R (version 4.2.2)90. Regulated glycoproteins were defined using a fold-change cutoff of >1.5 together with a Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p-value < 0.05.

Immunofluorescence imaging of primary human macrophages

Primary human macrophages (HM1 and HM2) were seeded onto 8-well chambered coverglasses (Cellvis) pre-coated with poly-D-lysine (Sigma, Cat# P1149) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and cultured for 24 h at 37 °C in complete growth medium. After incubation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min at room temperature (RT), followed by three washes with PBS. Samples were then blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min at RT and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ITGA3 (Abcam, Cat# ab131055), PTGFRN (Thermo Fisher, Cat# PA5-21913), or LIPA (Thermo Fisher, Cat# PA5-85126). After three PBS washes, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor® 647-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher, Cat# A78957) for 30 min at RT, washed, and mounted using ProLong™ Gold Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Thermo Fisher). Confocal images were acquired using a Nikon CSU-W1 spinning disk microscope with NIS-Elements software. Excitation wavelengths of 405 nm and 647 nm were used to image DAPI and Alexa Fluor® 647 signals, respectively.

Bioinformatics analysis

All analyses were performed in Excel and OriginLab 2022 unless mentioned otherwise. Gene ontology (GO)-based clustering was performed using the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID, https://david.ncifcrf.gov/tools.jsp)55. The information for different types of cell-surface proteins was extracted from the UniProt database (http://www.uniprot.org) and the in silico surfaceome database (SURFY)54. Domain analysis was performed by SUPERFAMILY 2.0 (https://supfam.org/)91. The residue solvent accessibility and structure were predicted using NetsurfP (version 3.0)92.

Integrative analysis of potential targets for M2-type macrophage inhibition

For identifying potential targets, the scoring rubric used is included in Supporting Table S7-1. Cell-surface glycoproteins showing at least 1.4-fold higher abundance in HM2 macrophages relative to HM1 were first selected as M2-enriched candidates. To confirm their immune cell specificity, these candidates were cross-referenced with monocyte- and macrophage-specific genes in the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) (https://www.proteinatlas.org/humanproteome/single+cell/single+cell+type). To ensure tumor relevance, transcriptomic datasets of M2-like tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) were used to evaluate expression consistency within the tumor microenvironment27,72. To minimize potential off-target effects, candidates were further filtered using the GTEx snRNA database “GTEx_8_tissues_snRNAseq_atlas_071421.public_obs.h5ad” (https://www.gtexportal.org/home/downloads/adult-gtex/single_cell)73, to exclude proteins with high expression across eight normal human tissues (breast, esophagus mucosa, esophagus muscularis, heart, lung, skeletal muscle, prostate, and skin). A composite ranking system integrating (i) M2 macrophage cell-surface enrichment, (ii) immune cell specificity, (iii) enrichment in tumor-associated macrophages, and (iv) restricted expression in normal tissues was subsequently used to calculate a prioritization index for each candidate. In this study, the prioritization index is a metric that reflects the predicted selectivity of a target for M2-type macrophages relative to normal tissues. Higher prioritization index, therefore, indicates stronger support across these datasets and, therefore, higher priority for future functional investigation.
