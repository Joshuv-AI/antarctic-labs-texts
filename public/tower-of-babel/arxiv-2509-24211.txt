Far-infrared lines hidden in archival deep multi-wavelength surveys: Limits on [CII]-158 μ \mu m at z ∼ 0.3 − 2.9 z\sim 0.3-2.9
Shubh Agrawal ,, James Aguirre, Ryan Keenan

Abstract
Context: Singly-ionized carbon [CII] is theorized to be the brightest emission line feature in star-forming galaxies, and hence an excellent tracer of the evolution of cosmic star formation. Archival maps from far-infrared and sub-millimeter surveys potentially contain the redshifted [CII]-158 μ \mu m, hidden in the much-brighter continuum emission. Aim: We present a search for aggregate [CII]-158 μ \mu m line emission across the predicted peak of star formation history by tomographically stacking a high-completeness galaxy catalog on broadband deep maps of the COSMOS field and constraining residual excess emission after subtracting the continuum spectral energy distribution (SED). Methods: The COSMOS equatorial 2 ​ deg 2 2\deg^{2} patch has been mapped by Spitzer , Herschel , and SCUBA2/JCMT. With the high precision UV-O-IR photometry catalog COSMOS2020, we perform unbiased simultaneous stacking of ∼ 360,000 \sim 360,000 photometric redshifts on these confusion-limited maps to resolve the sub-THz radiation background. By subtracting an continuum SED model with conservative uncertainty estimation and completeness correction through comparison to the COBE /FIRAS monopole spectrum, we obtain tomographic constraints on the sky-averaged [CII]-158 μ \mu m signal within the three SPIRE maps: 11.8 ± 10.2 11.8\pm 10.2 , 11.0 ± 8.7 11.0\pm 8.7 , 9.6 ± 9.8 9.6\pm 9.8 , and 9.2 ± 6.6 9.2\pm 6.6 k k Jy/sr at redshifts z ∼ 0.65 z\sim 0.65 , ∼ 1.3 \sim 1.3 , ∼ 2.1 \sim 2.1 , and ∼ 2.6 \sim 2.6 respectively, corresponding to 1 − 1.4 ​ σ 1-1.4\sigma significance in each bin. Results: Our 3 ​ σ 3\sigma upper limits are in tension with past z ∼ 2.6 z\sim 2.6 results from cross-correlating SDSS-BOSS quasars with high-frequency Planck maps, and indicate a much less dramatic evolution ( ∼ \sim × 7.5 \times 7.5 ) of mean [CII] intensity across the peak of star formation history than collisional excitation models or frameworks calibrated to the tentative Planck xBOSS measurement. We discuss this tension, particularly in the context of in-development surveys (TIM, EXCLAIM) that will map this [CII] at high redshift resolution. Conclusion: Having demonstrated stacking in broadband deep surveys as a complementary methodology to next-generation spectrometers for line intensity mapping, our novel methods can be extended to upcoming galaxy surveys such as Euclid , as well as to place upper limits on fainter atomic and molecular lines.



1
Introduction

Constraining the cosmic history of star formation and the chemical evolution of interstellar medium (ISM) in galaxies is an intriguing and outstanding question in observational cosmology, forming the basis for in-development and planned surveys
(
Vieira et al. 2020
;
Pullen et al. 2023
;
CCAT-Prime Collaboration et al. 2022
;
Keating et al. 2015
;
Keating et al. 2020
)
. Observations from the last decade imply a consistent formalism that star formation rate peaked roughly 3.5 Gyr after the Big Bang (the so-called “cosmic noon”), at
z\sim 2

(
Madau & Dickinson 2014
)
and has decayed exponentially to current day due to decrease in available cold gas as well as stellar wind and active galactic nuclei (AGN). Dust in the ISM absorbs ultraviolet (UV) radiation from star-forming galaxies, re-emitting in the thermal far-infrared (FIR); half of the energy produced as starlight has been absorbed and re-emitted by dust as the cosmic infrared background (CIB)
(
Dole et al. 2006
)
.

Carbon is one of the most abundant metals in the ISM and singly ionizes to [CII] (or C+) at 11.6 eV, a lower excitation energy than hydrogen. The [CII]-158
\mu
m fine structure line is empirically expected to be the primary coolant and brightest feature in star-forming galaxies, emitting as much as 0.5-1% of the total FIR luminosity
(
Crawford et al. 1985
;
Stacey et al. 1991
)
. Charting the redshift evolution of the mean [CII] luminosity is an important diagnostic for star formation rate density (SFRD)
(
Yang et al. 2021
;
Vallini et al. 2015
;
Liang et al. 2024
;
Olsen et al. 2017
)
. As a result, in addition to probing the growth of large-scale structure
(
Karkare et al. 2022
;
Moradinezhad Dizgah & Keating 2019
;
Fonseca et al. 2017
)
, line intensity mapping with atomic lines, such as [CII]-158
\mu
m, aims to constrain the formation and chemical evolution of these galaxies.

The mean [CII] luminosity is at present constrained by local
z\sim 0
measurements of [CII]-158
\mu
m luminosity function (LF) from
Herschel
/PACS observations
(
Hemmati et al. 2017
)
, as well as by
z\sim 4-6
measurements of [CII]-158
\mu
m-LF by the ALPINE-ALMA survey
(
Yan et al. 2020
;
Loiacono, Federica et al. 2021
)
.
There are only a few measurements across cosmic noon when SFR and the ISM evolve dramatically, as the [CII] line is redshifted to
wavelengths that are difficult to observe
from the ground and require sub-orbital or space platforms.
One such example is from the work of
Pullen et al. 2018
;
Yang et al. 2019
(hereafter Y19+P18), who report a tentative detection of the [CII] emission at
z\sim 2.6
in
Planck
Cosmic Infrared Background (CIB) maps, by tomographically cross-correlating with quasars and CMASS galaxies in the Sloan Digital Sky Survey’s (SDSS-III) Baryon Oscillations Spectroscopic Survey (BOSS) DR12 spectroscopic redshift catalog.

Theoretical and semi-analytical models (SAMs) for [CII] emission and its relation to star formation rate (SFR), metallicity, and gas depletion rate are dependent on redshift.
While there is a large scatter due to unconstrained model parameters, collisional excitation models such as
Gong et al. 2012
;
Silva et al. 2015
tend to make higher predictions for [CII]-158
\mu
m luminosity
L_{\nu,\text{[CII]}}
than models based on scaling relations that link
L_{\nu,\text{[CII]}}
to SFR, such as
Serra et al. 2016
;
Yue et al. 2015
, calibrated by local luminosity function or SFRD measurements.
The Y19+P18 measured intensity
\langle I_{\nu,\text{[CII]}}\rangle
is bright, about 20% of the total CIB (as measured by FIRAS/
COBE

(
Fixsen et al. 1998
)
; see Figure
5
) and favors certain parameterizations of the collisional models. Empirical models
(
Padmanabhan 2019
)
have also attempted to calibrate the [CII] luminosity - halo mass function to this measurement and forecast the detection capabilities of current-generation [CII]-158
\mu
m observatories at cosmic noon and reionization.

Such an excess [CII]-158
\mu
m emission would also be present in far-infrared or sub-millimeter observations of the CIB, e.g. from
Herschel
or
Spitzer
. However, while
Planck
constructed all-sky maps in bands up to 857 GHz by observing tens of thousands of square degrees, far-infrared space observations have been limited to deep observations of smaller patches of the sky. This motivates exploring correlation at smaller scales by methods such as
stacking
, where the correlated signal is amplified using an ancillary source catalog. Stacking has been successfully used to constrain the properties of the CIB and FIR sources
(
Dole et al. 2006
;
Devlin et al. 2009
;
Béthermin et al. 2012
;
Wilson et al. 2017
;
Romano et al. 2022
;
Dunne et al. 2024
)
. In highly confused images (such as those produced by smaller-aperture dishes observing in the far-infrared), simultaneous stacking
(
Viero et al. 2013
)
of a catalog, binned into subgroups based on emitter properties, has been effective in overcoming inherent biases and has been employed to understand infrared emission as a function of galaxy properties, particularly redshift
(
Viero et al. 2013
;
Viero et al. 2015
;
Sun et al. 2018
;
Duivenvoorden et al. 2020
;
Viero et al. 2022
)
. Our methods extend
Viero et al. 2022
, who used analogous COSMOS-field data to explore aggregate dust and thermal properties; we further analyze the residuals from a model fit to the continuum of the spectral energy distribution (SED), instead of the regressed model parameters. The residuals after continuum removal constrain spectral line emission.

In this letter, we demonstrate the application of simultaneous stacking with mid-to-far infrared and sub-millimeter maps and a deep photometric survey, in order to place limits on the cosmic line emission from [CII]-158
\mu
m. Section
2
describes the COSMOS deep field, the high-completeness redshift catalog COSMOS2020, and maps from instruments onboard
Spitzer
,
Herschel
, and the JCMT. Section
3
outlines our methods: stacking in the confusion limit with
LinSimStack
, estimating errors in stacked fluxes, constraining [CII] emission as a residual in continuum fitting, and correcting for catalog incompleteness. We report and discuss results in Section
4
, and conclude with Section
5
.

Methods

3.1
Stacking in the Confusion Limit with
LinSimStack

Far-infrared or sub-millimeter observations from single-dish telescopes, such as the maps discussed in Section
2
, are restricted in angular resolution, resulting in the so-called “confusion limit”. Simultaneous stacking has been shown to overcome clustering bias inherent in stacking on maps dominated by confusion noise
(
Viero et al. 2013
;
Viero et al. 2022
)
, unlike conventional postage-stamp stacking (“mean” or “median”) that is prone to wavelength-dependent biases.

We model a broadband map
m_{\lambda}
(in Jy/beam, indexed by the mean band wavelength
\lambda
) as a linear combination of several sub-maps or
layers
, each layer
A^{z,m}
being a convolution of the instrument beam with a hits-map of a portion or
bin
of the galaxy catalog. The binning is user-defined and supposed to be based on properties that determine emission in that band, such that stacking with these homogeneous sources gives a meaningful result. The coefficients
\bar{S}_{z,m}(\lambda)
thus regressed give the average flux density of emitters in that bin/layer:

\displaystyle m_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=A^{z,m}\bar{S}_{z,m}(\lambda)+\epsilon

(1)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\bar{S}_{z,m}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=(A^{T}WA)^{-1}A^{T}Wm_{\lambda},

(2)

where
W
is the weights matrix, given by the inverse per-pixel variance in the broadband maps and
\epsilon
is the corresponding Gaussian noise vector. Note the linearity in the regressed parameters
\bar{S}_{z,m}(\lambda)
can be exploited to write down a closed form for the least-squares minimization problem.

For our application, we developed
LinSimStack

3
3

3

github.com/shubhagrawal30/LinSimStack
as a fork of the previously introduced Python-based
SIMSTACK3

(
Viero et al. 2022
)
. Our version addresses inefficiencies in the
SIMSTACK3
codebase, notably exploiting this linearity by analytically marginalizing over the coefficients, instead of using a Levenberg-Marquardt non-linear solver, resulting in order-of-magnitude gains in speed with
LinSimStack
4
4

4

Other improvements included code profiling and refactoring to improve time/memory complexity bottlenecks, discussion of which is outside the scope of this work; further implementation/details are available on GitHub.
. This also enables us to analytically estimate the statistical covariance (see Section
3.2
).

We also implemented the following additions, some of which have been utilized in previous works. We include an additive flat foreground layer in Eqn.
1
to mitigate biases from mean subtraction of the maps or galactic foreground contamination. We additionally follow
Duivenvoorden et al. 2020
to model leakage of flux from masked areas due to large beams; we add another layer that is the beam convolved with the masked pixels of the galaxy catalog. All redshift and stellar mass bins are simultaneously stacked to reduce any biases due to interlopers
(
Sun et al. 2018
)
. Finally, the linear least-squares fitting is performed twice, with bright outlier pixels removed in the second iteration to reduce positive bias from close interlopers or bad pixels.

3.2
Unbiased error estimation

We estimate errors in the average emission flux
\bar{S}_{z,m}(\lambda)
measured by
LinSimStack
with the following four independent methods, and conservatively combine them to get a final variance in
\bar{S}
.

1.

Linear Formulation:
For the linear least-squares problem, the statistical covariance has the analytical form:
C_{\bar{S}}=(A^{T}WA)^{-1}
.
This encodes a very robust lower bound for the full error on
\bar{S}_{z,m}(\lambda)
, as it encodes the random error limit as a result of the covariance of the map pixels, but does not encode any information about systematics.

2.

Pixel Bootstrapping:
Similar to
Duivenvoorden et al. 2020
, we randomly select
80\%
of the unmasked pixels and perform the least-squares stacking algorithm ten times, to randomly resample the pixels in each map, which correspond to different instrumental noise realizations.

3.

Catalog Bootstrapping:
We follow
(
Viero et al. 2022
)
and run ten iterations with
80\%
:
20\%
split bootstrapping on the catalog, where the number of bins is doubled while still fitting all emitters available in the catalog to prevent negatively biasing the fluxes. Note that galaxy bootstrapping also encodes the noise-realization variations that is encoded by pixel bootstrapping; it is reasonable to expect the variance in
\bar{S}_{z,m}(\lambda)
when randomly sampling over galaxies would be higher than when pixel sampling.

4.

Jack-knifing:
Similar to
Duivenvoorden et al. 2020
, for a given map, we rerun our stacking algorithm four times, with each quartile of the map masked for one run. This encapsulates sample or cosmic variance, at least at the scale of the field (
\lesssim
2 sq. deg.).

Figure
3
shows the
fractional
error estimates in the mean stacked fluxes using these four individual methods. Note that the uncertainty in
\bar{S}_{z,m}(\lambda)
is one of two direct contributors to the constraining power on [CII] emission, the other being the uncertainty in the SED fit; see Sections
3.3
and
3.5
(empirically, the overall uncertainty in our line emission measurement is moderately more dependent on the stacking flux uncertainty than the SED fit uncertainty).
Thus, we conservatively select the quadrature sum of the jackknifing error (which encodes cosmic variance at the scale of the field) and the maximum of the other three error estimates, to get the
\sigma(\bar{S}_{z,m}(\lambda))
for the subsequent analyses.

3.4
Aggregate infrared background as a measure of completeness correction

Within some frequency bandwidths, we measure (with
LinSimStack
) the integrated average emission from galaxies within a redshift range
z
and stellar mass
m
:
\bar{S}_{z,m}(\lambda)
. We can write down the portion of the cosmic infrared background (CIB) that stacking with our catalog has resolved as a sum over our bins:

F(\lambda)=\int dN_{z,m}\;\bar{S}_{z,m}(\lambda)\approx\sum_{z,m}N_{z,m}\bar{S}_{z,m}(\lambda),

(4)

where
N_{z,m}
is the number of emitters in a given bin. Naively, this can be set to the galaxy counts in COSMOS2020 we input to
LinSimStack
, but this would be a significant underestimate due to the galaxy catalog incompleteness. Instead, we use completeness-corrected measurements of the Stellar Mass Function (SMF)
\frac{dN}{dm}(m,z)
, which encode the number density of emitters as a function of stellar mass content, at a given redshift:

\displaystyle F(\lambda)=\int dV(z)n_{z,m}

\displaystyle\bar{S}_{z,m}(\lambda)

(5)

\displaystyle=\iint dV(z)\;dm\frac{dN}{dm}(m,z)

4.3
Other systematics

We briefly discuss other potential systematics in our best estimates and
3\sigma
upper limits.

Photometric Redshift Uncertainty:

The COSMOS2020 photometric galaxy catalog claims sub-percent accuracy for bright (
i<21
) and
<5

25<i<27
) sources. As our broadband filters and corresponding
z

z

\mu
m emission into the neighboring bins.
To quantify the effects of
z
errors, we generate the same catalog used in the analysis above, except sampling the estimated photometric
z
from a skewed Gaussian distribution that replicates the median, lower 68%, and upper 68% of the probability distribution functions (
z
-PDFs) in the COSMOS2020 catalog. We rerun our stacking and SED fitting analysis on this catalog with the modified sampled redshifts. We find that our [CII]-158
\mu
m constraints deviate by no more than 0.09
\sigma
in any of the four
z

Diffuse emission from Intergalactic Medium and unmodeled Extragalactic Background Light:
Our stacking methodology measures [CII]-158
\mu
m emission from physical scales within the
Herschel
beam of the sources in the COSMOS2020 catalog, which is
\sim.2-1

c
Mpc at
z\sim.3-2.9
. We expect this to account for the circumgalactic medium and the diffuse [CII] halo around sources, which are typically within
\lesssim 100

(
Tumlinson et al. 2017
;
Fujimoto et al. 2020
;
Lambert et al. 2023
;
Fujimoto et al. 2019
;
Ikeda et al. 2025

Herrera-Camus et al. 2015

Our results could be negatively biased due to underestimating emission from the large-scale diffuse intergalactic medium (IGM). However, this effect is subdominant as the mean [CII]-158
\mu
m emission from the IGM is expected to be orders of magnitude lower than galaxies
(
Gong et al. 2012
)
.

Past work
(
Duivenvoorden et al. 2020
)
has shown that the emission in the
Herschel

Section

Invalidity of the assumptions underlying these two extrapolations would bias our measurement.
Potentially more complete photo-

(

z\sim 2.6
lower than the 95% C.L. interval.
Also plotted are expected
3\sigma
sensitivity limits of two in-development surveys
(
Pullen et al. 2023
;
Bracks et al. in prep.
)
that will measure C+ at high
R
. We discuss these and this tension further in text (Section
4
).

Examining the final column of Figure

(or Figure 7 from
Pullen et al. 2018
), note that Y19+P18 uses three points, all
>350\penalty\ \mu
m to constrain a similar continuum SED model as our analysis; empirically, using a low number of samples only on the Rayleigh-Jean side of the modified black body model could be prone to fitting and numerical systematics. More data points at
wavelengths away from the bands in which [CII]  emission would appear to
enable an analysis like ours to be robust at constraining the nuisance SED parameters.

4.5
Future Outlook

Figure
7
also plots sensitivity forecasts from two in-development instruments. We include the
3\sigma
noise limits expected from cross-correlating datasets from two C+ spectrometers with a respective ancillary galaxy catalog. The Terahertz Intensity Mapper (TIM;
Vieira et al. 2020
) is a balloon-borne far-infrared spectrometer, with spectral resolution
R\sim 250
, mapping a deep field at GOODS-South for
O(100)
hours. TIM’s field overlaps with Euclid Deep Field Fornax (EDFF); TIM plans to cross-correlate their [CII]-158
\mu
m maps at
z\sim 0.5-1.5
with the deep high number-count
Euclid
spectroscopic galaxy catalog
(
Bracks et al. in prep.
)
. EXCLAIM
(
Pullen et al. 2023
)
will map [CII]-158
\mu
m at
z\sim 2.5-3.5
over an equatorial field over
\sim 10
hours; EXCLAIM plans to cross-correlate with the SDSS Baryon Oscillation Spectroscopic Survey (BOSS) spectroscopic redshift catalog. Figure
7
shows that either experiment reaching its forecasted sensitivity will be a step towards resolving this perceived tension by detecting [CII] at several
\sigma
.

Constraints from our methodology can be improved by increasing either the depth of the far-infrared broadband maps, the number counts in the galaxy catalog, or by addressing systematics with smaller photo-
z
uncertainty or narrower bandpass measurements. We are currently limited to broadband mapping instruments of the past (
Herschel
,
Spitzer
), but wider and/or more complete galaxy catalogs are imminent. Deeper UVOIR measurements of COSMOS, such as with
JWST
/NIRCam or
JWST
/MIRI
(
Casey et al. 2023
;
Shuntov et al. 2025
)
, can improve the far-infrared completeness of our forward model. More promisingly, galaxy catalogs with the same depth but more overlap with the
Herschel
deep fields, e.g.
Euclid
photometry and spectra
(
Mellier et al. 2025
;
Jahnke et al. 2025
)
, would decrease our constraint variance as roughly inversely proportional to the total number counts of emitters with photo-
z
in the coinciding regions.

5
Conclusion

We place the first constraints on [CII]-158
\mu
m emission during and right after the peak of star formation history, at
z\sim 0.6-2
. These imply [CII]-158
\mu
m emission rose by roughly an order of magnitude from
z\sim 5
and then fell by a similar amount to the present day, accounting for no more than a few percent of the cosmic infrared background at its peak. Our measurements, with a total signal-to-noise of 2.42, imply
I^{\text{[CII]}}_{\nu}
of
\sim 10
kJy/sr across
z\sim 0.6-2.6
.

We are unable to replicate measurements at
z\sim 2.6
obtained from cross-correlating
Planck
infrared maps with quasars from the wide spectroscopic survey BOSS. Experiments actively being developed, TIM and EXCLAIM, are forecasted to be sensitive enough to address tension and measure [CII] at several
\sigma
s. Additionally, imminent data releases of the deep field galaxy catalogs from the recently launched
Euclid
, as well as the COSMOS-Web survey, are expected to yield higher-completeness coverage over larger fields overlapping with
Herschel
deep maps. A future extension of our analysis with
Euclid
redshifts can leverage such higher effective number counts to decrease uncertainties on stacked fluxes, and thus
I^{\text{[CII]}}_{\nu}
.

We have demonstrated a search for far-infrared line emission in deep field multi-wavelength surveys, using
LinSimStack
, a faster linearized version of previously introduced stacking utilities. It is straightforward to extend our analysis to obtain complementary constraints from different datasets, e.g.
Euclid
, and fields, e.g. GOODS. It is also possible to use our methodology to constrain the redshift evolution of molecular lines from CO, [OI], [OIII], and N+, as long as these narrow spectral features are expected to be significantly brighter than contaminating interlopers, or maps at higher spectral resolution are available.

Appendix C
Comparing
LinSimStack
to SIMSTACK3 from
Viero et al. 2022

Our stacking methodology is similar to SIMSTACK3 from
Viero et al. 2022
(V22), with a crucial change. While SIMSTACK3 formulates the stacking as a non-linear problem and uses a Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm to iteratively converge to a best-fit solution,
LinSimStack
exploits the linearity of the forward modeling problem to gain an order-of-magnitude speed-up. Notably, our method, unlike SIMSTACK3, does not require a feasible initial guess to converge to the global minima. In Figure
12
, we attempt to replicate stacking results from V+22 (see their Figure 1), by using V+22’s specific choice of binning over COSMOS2020.

Results from
LinSimStack
(blue) typically agree with SIMSTACK3 results (orange); the RMS of
z
-scores is
\sim 1.35
, close to 1. Note that the uncertainties plotted and used in the calculation above are just catalog bootstrapping errors (which is all that V+22 use); if we used our more conservative error estimation (Section
3.2
), our uncertainties will be
\sim\sqrt{2}
larger (see Fig.
3
), resulting in a lower statistic above.
We expect
LinSimStack
is more robust against local extrema in the loss function; we qualitatively note that our stacking estimates trace the continuum better than SIMSTACK3.

Replicating Figure 1 from
Viero et al. 2022
, to compare results from
LinSimStack
(blue) to SIMSTACK3 (orange). Error bars plotted are from catalog bootstrapping alone (see Section
3.2
), to allow a direct comparison with SIMSTACK3/V+22. The RMS of
z
-scores is
\sim 1.35
, close to the expected unity. We expect our methodology to be more robust against local minima in the loss function.
