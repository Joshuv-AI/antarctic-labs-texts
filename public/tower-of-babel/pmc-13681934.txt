Perceived service quality and tourist’ pro-environmental behavior: The mediating role of destination psychological ownership and the moderating role of self-improvement motivation
Authors: Liu Ding, Ruyou Li, Lei Zhu, Zhimeng Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe paramount significance of tourist' pro-environmental behavior (TPEB) in propelling the sustainable evolution of tourist destinations cannot be underscored sufficiently. Despite the ubiquitous acknowledgment of perceived service quality (PSQ) as a pivotal antecedent to nurturing favorable tourist experiences and behaviors, the specific mechanisms underlying its influence on TPEB remains under-explored. This study proposes a moderated mediation model to delve into the intricate interplay between PSQ and TPEB, by examining the mediating influence of destination psychological ownership (DPO) and the moderating effect of self-improvement motivation (SIM) within the context of vacation tourism. Utilizing structural equation modeling (SEM) and the bootstrap method, we analyzed data collected from a sample of 501 tourists. The findings indicate that PSQ exerts a positive effect on TPEB, and this relationship is partially mediated by DPO. Furthermore, SIM emerges as a salient moderator, amplifying both the direct and indirect effects of DPO on TPEB. This study contributes to a deeper comprehension of the underlying mechanisms that shape TPEB and enriches the literature on DPO within the tourism domain. Additionally, the insights garnered have implications for tourism management practices aimed at effectively promoting TPEB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05141-w.Keywords: Perceived service quality, Tourists’ pro- environmental behavior, Destination psychological ownership, Self-improvement motivation

Introduction

The uncivilized behavior exhibited by tourists has engendered substantial adverse consequences on the sustainable tourism development [1]. Notably, the burgeoning popularity of vacation tourism has conspicuously lengthened the duration of tourists' stays, imposing unprecedented strains on the resources of destination [2]. Consequently, environmental concerns including waste accumulation, water contamination, and the degradation of natural vegetation have intensified [3]. In the face of these pressing challenges, the United Nations World Tourism Organization, alongside governments globally, have staunchly advocated for the adoption of sustainable tourism practices over the past two decades [4].

Given the often-observed link between environmental degradation in tourist destinations and various tourist behaviors [5], the mitigation or eradication of environmental harm stemming from tourists' uncivilized conduct, alongside fostering tourist' pro-environmental behavior (TPEB), has emerged as a pressing imperative [2]. Extensive research has been undertaken to delve into the underlying mechanisms driving TPEB [6, 7]. Previous studies have predominantly relied on classical frameworks such as the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) [8, 9], focusing mainly on the influence of cognitive factors like attitudes and subjective norms on behavioral intentions. However, these studies often treat pro-environmental behavior as a general form of civic behavior, failing to emphasize the uniqueness of "tourist citizenship behavior"—that is, tourists' behaviors in non-habitual environments may significantly differ from those in habitual environments due to the uniqueness of the context. Precisely because of this, such complexity may exceed the explanatory scope of classical models [10], suggesting that traditional theories might be inadequate for fully capturing the complex motivations behind pro-environmental behavior in tourism contexts. Tourism is inherently an experiential activity. Consequently, tourists' perceptions at destinations—particularly their evaluations of services—and the resulting psychological states intrinsically shape their subsequent behavioral patterns, making them critical influencing factors.

Service quality stands as a paramount concept in marketing, garnering considerable attention from scholars amidst the backdrop of a fiercely competitive marketplace [11]. Within tourism research, perceived service quality (PSQ) emerges as a significant antecedent of favorable tourist perceptions and subsequent consumption behaviors (encompassing satisfaction, repurchase intentions, and destination loyalty) across diverse contexts, such as cruise tourism [12], Edu-tourism [11], and yoga tourism [13]. However, existing research on PSQ primarily focuses on egoistic outcome variables such as business performance and customer loyalty, with limited exploration of how it guides tourists toward altruistic behaviors (specifically TPEB) that benefit the destination's environmental well-being. Drawing upon social exchange theory (SET), tourists reciprocate with benevolent behaviors when they perceive their destination experience to be favorable [14]. Consequently, the likelihood of tourists engaging in actions conducive to the destination's well-being (e.g., TPEB) intensifies when they perceive the services offered as fulfilling their psychological needs. However, the extent to which PSQ encountered at a destination influences TPEB, along with the underlying mechanisms that govern this relationship, remains an under-examined area, necessitating further scrutiny.

Additionally, amidst today's fiercely competitive tourism market, an escalating trend emerges among service providers, offering diverse avenues for tourists to embody a sense of ownership over their service experiences [15]. The concept of destination psychological ownership (DPO), defined as "a sense of ownership towards the destination" [16], represents a profound cognitive-affective bond between tourists and destinations, harmoniously aligning the welfare aspirations of both parties [17]. Given its essential role in fostering tourists' favorable attitudes and behaviors, DPO has garnered significant scholarly attention in recent years [18]. Consequently, there is a pressing need to delve into the value of DPO within the vacation tourism realm and elucidate the mechanisms through which such experiences elicit altruistic tendencies among tourists [19]. Drawing upon the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S–O-R) paradigm, outside environment (stimuli) lead to individual responses via their effects on cognition and affection [20]. In the context of tourism, these stimuli are often construed as tourists' encounters with destination attributes [21]. Therefore, it is expected that PSQ, acting as a stimulus, has the potential to ignite DPO within tourists (the organism), which subsequently prompts the manifestation of TPEB as a response. By introducing DPO as a key mediating variable, this study aims to construct and validate a theoretical path model of "PSQ → DPO → TPEB", thereby deepening the understanding of the intrinsic psychological processes through which PSQ influences TPEB. This constitutes an important supplement to the existing literature.

Furthermore, while prior endeavors has predominantly focused on the antecedents of TPEB, there remains a notable gap in elucidating the significance of factors that interfere with the emergence of such behaviors. Meanwhile, the boundary conditions under which psychological ownership affects behaviors are being discussed [22, 23]. For instance, while studies have indicated that the influence of DPO on behavior is not always consistent, there remains limited understanding of the specific boundary conditions under which its efficacy fluctuates in particular contexts (such as TPEB). Consequently, it is imperative to delve into the delineation of the boundary conditions specific to TPEB formation, particularly identifying variables that interfere with the effect of DPO on TPEB. It is universally acknowledged that personal traits shape an individual's response to a given scenario [24], either augmenting or attenuating behavioral outcomes. One such trait that merits consideration is self-improvement motivation (SIM), a psychological construct that drives individuals to strive for positive self-perceptions, thereby fostering a sense of progress and growth, has been shown to bolster performance across physical, social, and psychological domains [25]. In the realm of tourism, it is plausible that tourists with a heightened motivation for self-improvement may be more inclined to invest in environmentally sustainable practices that contribute to the destination's development. This, in turn, could foster positive reinforcement and bolster their self-esteem. Hence, SIM emerges as a potential boundary condition underpinning the mechanisms that drive TPEB, a dimension that has yet to be clearly delineated in the extant literature. A key innovation of this study lies in introducing SIM as a moderating variable within the TPEB research framework, systematically examining its amplifying or attenuating effects during the transformation from DPO to TPEB. This approach contrasts with previous studies that predominantly treated TPEB or DPO as universal phenomena, thereby helping to reveal the heterogeneity in the formation mechanisms of TPEB. Consequently, it provides a novel explanatory perspective for understanding why identical levels of psychological ownership may lead to divergent pro-environmental behavioral outcomes.

In response to the aforementioned research gaps, the present study endeavors to delve into the mechanisms underlying how tourists' PSQ stimulates their destination positive outcomes (DPO) and subsequently elicits TPEB, with tourists' SIM functioning as a moderating variable. In summary, the principal contributions of this study are threefold: Theoretically, it transcends the traditional Theory of Planned Behavior framework by integrating the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S–O-R) paradigm and social exchange theory to construct a comprehensive "PSQ → DPO → TPEB" model, which is better aligned with the experiential nature of tourism. Mechanistically, it systematically validates the mediating role of DPO and the moderating effect of SIM, thereby revealing the boundary conditions governing the transformation of psychological ownership into pro-environmental behaviors. Practically, it proposes targeted strategies for fostering TPEB through enhanced service quality and attention to individual motivational differences, offering novel insights for sustainable destination management.

Literature review

Theoretical foundation

To construct a theoretical framework suitable for explaining the formation mechanism of tourists' pro-environmental behaviors in tourism contexts, this study integrates the S–O-R paradigm and SET. The combination of these two theories provides a dynamic and complementary perspective for understanding how external service experiences are transformed into TPEB for the destination through internal psychological processes.

The S–O-R paradigm, originating from environmental psychology, offers a classical framework for analyzing how external environmental cues influence an individual's internal states and ultimately drive behavioral responses [21]. Its core premise posits that external environmental cues, serving as "stimuli," first affect an individual's internal cognitive and affective states—the "organism." This activated internal state then prompts the individual to produce specific behavioral "responses." The paradigm emphasizes that behavior is the outcome of a dynamic interaction between the external environment and internal psychological processes. Its applicability to this study lies in the fact that the tourism experience itself constitutes a continuous process of exposure to a series of external contacts (e.g., services, landscapes, facilities). Specifically, the PSQ encountered by tourists at a destination can be viewed as a crucial environmental stimulus. This stimulus is expected to influence tourists' internal psychological state, which is the focus of this study—DPO (organism)—ultimately leading to the overt TPEB (response). Therefore, the S–O-R paradigm provides a direct and logically rigorous meta-theoretical scaffold for linking the "PSQ → DPO → TPEB" pathway.

SET, from a sociological perspective, posits that human social behavior is based on a series of implicit, reciprocity-governed exchange relationships [14]. Its core principle is that when individuals perceive benefits from an exchange relationship, they develop a sense of obligation to reciprocate, potentially leading to actions favorable to the exchange partner. In the tourism context, an implicit social exchange relationship exists between tourists and the destination: the destination provides resources, services, and experiences, while tourists "pay" through consumption, evaluation, and behavior. The key point of its applicability to this study is that it can explain why tourists, after perceiving high-quality tourism services (a positive "giving" or benefit from the destination), may not only generate self-interest outcomes like satisfaction but also trigger an altruistic willingness to reciprocate that transcends direct transactions—a sense of responsibility for the destination's environmental well-being, manifesting as TPEB. This helps clarify why PSQ can lead to TPEB, an altruistic behavior, addressing the limitation of traditional marketing models that often focus narrowly on commercial returns.

Traditionally, cognitive decision-making models like the TPB have been widely applied to predict pro-environmental behaviors [8, 9]. However, TPB focuses on a static, proactive decision-making process where individuals form behavioral intentions based on rational calculations (attitudes, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control). Its model variables (e.g., attitudes) are often relatively stable traits or pre-formed judgments existing before the behavior occurs. This creates a certain tension with the "experiential nature of tourism." Tourism is inherently a dynamic, situated, and continuous process of physical and mental experience. Tourists' behaviors, especially in non-habitual environments, are profoundly influenced by real-time, on-site experiences rather than merely the execution of pre-formed plans.

In contrast, the S–O-R paradigm is better suited to capture this dynamic experiential process. It views behavior as a sequential chain of causation involving real-time psychological processing of situational stimuli leading to responses. In this study: PSQ as the stimulus: embodies the comprehensive evaluation formed in real-time by tourists during the experiential process at the destination regarding service encounters. DPO as the organism: represents a dynamic psychological state (sense of ownership) activated and constructed during the process of the tourism experience by the aforementioned real-time stimuli, rather than a pre-existing stable trait. TPEB as the response: is the specific behavioral outcome generated within the specific tourism context, based on this dynamic psychological state.

Therefore, integrating the S–O-R paradigm with SET more aptly models tourism as a "stream of experience"—where external service stimuli (PSQ) trigger and shape internal psychological states (DPO, which also embodies reciprocity perceptions based on social exchange) during the experiential process, subsequently driving situated behavioral responses (TPEB). This framework not only aligns better with the dynamic, situated, and affective nature of tourism consumption than the static TPB model but also allows the key variables of this study to be clearly positioned within a coherent, causally progressive logical chain, thereby establishing a more robust and self-consistent theoretical analytical system.

Tourists’ pro-environment behavior (TPEB)

Pro-environmental behavior (PEB), defined as "actions that minimize adverse impacts on the environment or even contribute positively to it," has garnered global attention amidst escalating environmental concerns [26]. Within the context of tourism, TPEB encompasses behaviors aimed at preserving and enhancing destination environments [10]. Initially conceptualized as a unitary construct, TPEB was later refined by Ramkissoon et al. [2] into a two-dimensional framework, distinguishing between low-effort and high-effort TPEB in nature-based tourism settings. Low-effort PEB entails actions that necessitate minimal personal investment, whereas high-effort PEB necessitates a relatively substantial commitment of time and energy. This dual-factor categorization of TPEB has garnered widespread acceptance and application in sustainable tourism literature [27].

A considerable body of research has delved into the driving forces and underlying mechanisms of TPEB, identifying three crucial categories of variables: internal factors, external factors, and demographic characteristics [28]. Among these, internal variables have received the most extensive examination in existing literature [29]. Initially, researchers explored the antecedents of TPEB through various theoretical lenses, highlighting cognitive variables like environmental awareness, personal norms, environmental knowledge, and moral obligation as significant precursors fostering TPEB [30, 31]. However, it became evident that TPEB is a multifaceted phenomenon, and simplistic frameworks fell short in comprehensively capturing its complexity [10]. This shift has led to a surge in attention towards affective variables, including satisfaction, place attachment, as recently evidenced in studies by Cheng and Wu [8] and Xu et al. [17]. In the tourism consumption context, external stimuli, such as destination landscapes, service offerings, and reputation, significantly influence tourists' affective experiences, thereby eliciting corresponding behavioral responses [1]. Hence, this perspective warrants heightened attention in TPEB research endeavors.

Perceived service quality (PSQ)

Service quality stands as a paramount concept in marketing, intricately defined as the gap between customers' preconceived expectations of a service and the actual service experience they encounter [32]. Specifically, a service is deemed low in quality when customers' anticipations surpass their perceptions of the actual service delivered, whereas it is perceived as high when their perceptions exceed their initial expectations [33]. Drawing upon this foundational definition, the PZB group introduced the PSQ scale, known as SEVQUAL, which encapsulates five dimensions: reliability, tangibility, assurance, responsiveness, and empathy [32]. This scale has garnered widespread academic recognition and application. Furthermore, to cater to the nuances of specific industries, researchers have devised several domain-tailored service quality scales within the realm of tourism and hospitality studies [34].

In the fiercely competitive marketplace, service quality has garnered extensive scrutiny, with prior literature highlighting PSQ as a pivotal antecedent to customers' behavioral intentions and satisfaction, ultimately fostering financial performance and profitability [13]. Tourism, an integral segment of the service sector, presents a unique challenge due to its intangible nature, rendering the assessment of tourism service quality inherently subjective and intricate. Within the tourism context, PSQ has emerged as a vital precursor to tourists' favorable perceptions and post-consumption behaviors, encompassing satisfaction, repurchase intentions, destination loyalty, and word-of-mouth recommendations, across diverse tourism activities [13, 35]. Although the impact of service quality on behavioral intentions is well-established, scholars contend that further exploration is necessary to unravel novel outcomes [33]. This study endeavors to address this gap in the literature.

Destination psychological ownership (DPO)

Psychological ownership is defined as "a state in which individuals feel as though the target of ownership (or a piece of that target) is theirs" [36]. It embodies a robust psychological bond with the target object, fostering a profound sense of possessiveness [23]. Fundamentally, psychological ownership arises from the fulfillment of three core needs—self-identity, efficacy, and belongingness—through three primary pathways: acquaintance with the target, control, and investment [37]. Notably, any of these avenues is sufficient to elicit psychological ownership [38]. Over the past few decades, the field of management studies has witnessed a surge in research examining psychological ownership. A myriad of studies have consistently corroborated that psychological ownership elicits favorable attitudinal and behavioral outcomes for organizations, including heightened employee commitment, job engagement, citizenship behaviors, and constructive actions [22, 39].

Recently, psychological ownership has been incorporated into the tourism domain, giving rise to the novel construct of destination psychological ownership (DPO) [16]. DPO pertains to the profound psychological bond tourists forge with destinations [17]. Given the absence of legal ownership, DPO becomes pivotal in satisfying tourists' innate desire for possession and addressing their fundamental needs [15]. Prior research has illuminated that destination identification [16], perceived justice [40], and perceived restorativeness [16] are vital antecedents of DPO. Furthermore, DPO fosters a psychological closeness between tourists and destinations, thereby nurturing pro-social attitudes and behaviors towards them [41]. Li et al. [18] confirmed that DPO positively influences tourists' environmentally responsible behavior, while Qu et al. [42]uncovered its role in promoting civilized tourist behavior. Undeniably, DPO broadens our understanding of the relationship between tourists and tourism-related entities [15]. However, as a nascent tourism concept [18], academic inquiry into DPO remains in its early stages. Hence, this study introduces DPO into the research framework, aiming to enrich the tourism literature in this regard.

Specifically, the introduction of DPO has significantly advanced knowledge in the field of tourism, primarily manifested in three aspects: First, it provides a theoretical perspective that transcends traditional economic transactions or emotional preferences (such as satisfaction and place attachment), revealing the intrinsic motivation behind tourists' protective behaviors toward destinations through the deep-seated driver of "psychological ownership." Second, via core mechanisms like "sense of control," "sense of investment," and "self-identity," DPO systematically connects fragmented service experiences, environmental interactions, and individual need satisfaction, offering a unified theoretical framework for understanding the psychological transformation process of tourists progressing from general consumption to developing a sense of responsibility and even engaging in prosocial behaviors. Third, this construct expands the intervention strategies for sustainable tourism management, suggesting that managers can more effectively inspire spontaneous and enduring environmental protection behaviors by cultivating tourists' psychological ownership, rather than relying solely on information campaigns or facility convenience. Thereby, it achieves a knowledge deepening progression from behavioral description, to motivational explanation, and further to managerial implications.

Summary

In conclusion, while existing studies have extensively explored the cognitive and affective drivers of TPEB, significant gaps remain in integrating how external service context stimuli and internal psychological mechanisms jointly shape TPEB. This study aims to address this gap by introducing perceived service quality as a key external stimulus variable and examining its impact on TPEB through affective and cognitive psychological mechanisms (such as destination psychological ownership). In doing so, it seeks to develop an integrated framework that combines external service attributes with internal psychological processes, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of the formation mechanisms of TPEB.

Hypothesis development

PSQ and TPEB

Social exchange theory serves as a fundamental theoretical framework for elucidating customer behaviors, with its pivotal principle of reciprocity positing that social exchange shape individuals' attitudes and subsequent behaviors [43]. Within this theoretical lens, customers reciprocate positively towards organizations that fulfill their personal needs [44]. In today's fiercely competitive marketing landscape, service quality has emerged as a significant antecedent to diverse customer behaviors [34]. Analogously, tourism research has consistently shown that PSQ fosters tourists' positive emotions and subsequent behavioral intentions, such as satisfaction and destination loyalty, across various tourism contexts [13, 34]. Drawing from social exchange theory, it is plausible to surmise that tourists engage in prosocial actions if they perceive destination service providers as beneficial, thereby maintaining cognitive consistency as proactive behaviors bolster positive self-perceptions [14, 43]. Empirical evidence underscores the pivotal role of on-site experiences in dictating tourists' attitudes and behaviors towards destinations [16]. For instance, Qiu et al. [45] found that tourists' perceived restorative qualities positively influence their environmentally responsible behavior, while Liu et al. [46] demonstrated that high destination service quality can nurture tourist citizenship behavior. In this context, it is conceivable that tourists may be inclined to engage in environmentally beneficial practices when they perceive that the service meets their psychological needs. Consequently, it is anticipated that PSQ effectively promotes TPEB, leading to the following hypothesis:

Mediating role of DPO

PSQ constitutes an individual's subjective appraisal of objective service offerings, with this cognition eliciting emotional responses [47]. In the realm of tourism and hospitality, PSQ has been identified as an essential factor in evoking positive affective states, such as pleasure and identification, among tourists [34, 48]. As a critical psychological bridge between tourists and destinations, DPO is intimately linked to PSQ. During leisure travel, tourists partake in diverse tourism activities, catering to their aspirations for physical rejuvenation and mental rejuvenation, thereby fostering their psychological ownership [49]. Notably, positive interactions with multiple destination elements, coupled with favorable service encounters, strengthen feelings of trust and intimacy, subsequently enhancing feelings of belonging. Research has confirmed that tourists cultivate DPO when they experience a sense of belonging towards a destination [16]. Moreover, PSQ influences tourists' sense of control during their journey. Perceived control encapsulates tourists' belief in their capacity to execute their travel plans as desired [50]. Timely and efficient service undeniably bolsters tourists' self-efficacy, thereby enhancing their overall travel experience. Furthermore, PSQ significantly impacts individuals' identification with service providers, as evidenced by prior studies [34, 51]. When tourists establish a favorable cognitive bond with a service provider, they are more prone to identifying with the destination, as this connection fulfills their need for uniqueness and personal growth [33]. In essence, tourists who receive positive service quality are inclined to perceive the destination as an extension of their self-concept, and the following hypothesis is formulated:

H2. PSQ has a positive impact on DPO.

H2. PSQ has a positive impact on DPO.

Psychological ownership fosters a profound sense of responsibility, rooted in its ability to satisfy fundamental human needs, where the integration of objects into one's self-concept serves as a potent impetus for individual actions [36, 52]. Consequently, individuals nurture, safeguard, and even make personal sacrifices on behalf of these objects [37]. Prior organizational behavior research underscores PO's role in enhancing positive emotions and fostering pro-organizational behaviors among employees [22]. Analogously, in tourism contexts, DPO strengthens the bond between tourists and destinations, fostering intimacy and satisfying tourists' needs for belonging and self-identity [18]. When tourists' essential needs are met through destination experiences, they often reciprocate by engaging in behaviors beneficial to the destination, as such acts are inherently rewarding [53]. Essentially, DPO aligns tourists' personal interests with those of the destination, making its preservation a primary concern [54]. Furthermore, research has affirmed the positive influence of tourists' DPO on stewardship behaviors (e.g., cleaning leisure spaces) [15]. Li et al. [18] specifically demonstrated DPO's positive effect on tourists' environmentally responsible behavior. Psychological ownership, in essence, engenders a sense of accountability towards destination-related issues, motivating tourists to actively participate and contribute to the enhancement of the destination's environmental condition [17]. These favorable actions are often driven not by extrinsic motivations but by the favorable experience associated with self-expansion [55, 56]. Based on this synthesis, the present study posits the following hypothesis:

H3. DPO exerts a positive impact on TPEB.

Within this framework, a tourist encounters a stimulus (S), eliciting a cognitive or affective state (O), which subsequently inspires a behavioral response (R), encompassing psychological outcomes and behavioral intentions [33]. A heightened level of PSQ fosters psychological ownership towards the destination, thereby motivating tourists to engage in more environmentally friendly behaviors. Moderating role of self-improvement motivation (SIM)

Previous studies have primarily focused on the antecedents of TPEB, yet have seldom illuminated the factors that may interfere with this process. Additionally, the boundary conditions under which psychological ownership affects individual’s behavior are an ongoing debate [23]. Consequently, there is a pressing need to delve deeper into the boundary conditions of TPEB formation, particularly the variables that moderate the effects of DPO on TPEB. As previously noted, individual behavior is inherently influenced by personality traits [57], with personal variables capable of either hindering or amplifying behavioral responses. For example, Li et al. [18] discovered that the positive impact of psychological ownership on environmentally responsible behavior is more substantial among tourists who strongly endorse the New Ecological Paradigm.

SIM arises when individuals strive to elevate themselves by acquiring novel knowledge and competencies, essential for social, educational, and cognitive adaptation [56]. This motivation fosters robust behavioral dispositions and enhances performance in domains of personal significance (e.g., social and physical self) [58], ultimately contributing to a favorable self-perception. Prior research has demonstrated that SIM positively influences customer citizenship behavior [59]. In the tourism context, tourists may be further incentivized to engage in environmental conservation due to the intrinsic rewards associated with personal growth. However, the moderating role of SIM in shaping TPEB remains unexplored. Drawing upon the aforementioned analysis and our hypotheses H4 (PSQ positively affects TPEB) and H6 (DPO positively affects TPEB), we posit that tourists with varying degrees of SIM will exhibit PEB to differing extents. Specifically, tourists with heightened SIM are more likely to engage in PEB that reinforce their self-image. Conversely, those with lower levels of motivation may be less inclined to demonstrate TPEB. Accordingly, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H5. SIM positively moderates the relationship between PSQ and TPEB.

H6. SIM positively moderates the relationship between DPO and TPEB.

Furthermore, grounded in motivated information processing theory and the self-regulatory framework, individuals' environmental behavior decisions often follow a "motivation-enhanced cognition-behavior" pathway. Self-improvement motivation (SIM) plays a critical enabling role in this process [56]: individuals with high SIM are more inclined to actively integrate external service contexts (such as the environmental values conveyed by PSQ) into the cognitive schema for self-concept maintenance, thereby strengthening their psychological ownership of tourism resources (DPO) and further translating it into TPEB consistent with their ideal self-image [58]. This mechanism aligns with self-congruity theory, which posits that individuals possess an inherent drive to align their behaviors with their self-concept. When such behaviors simultaneously serve self-improvement functions, the integrated chain effect of motivation-cognition-behavior becomes particularly pronounced.

Specifically, when tourists exhibit higher levels of self-improvement motivation, they demonstrate greater sensitivity to the environmental norms embedded in service interactions and show increased willingness to shape a public self-image as "responsible tourists" through resource conservation behaviors. In such cases, the indirect path from PSQ to TPEB via DPO becomes more robust, as it receives continuous reinforcement from the motivational system [57]. Conversely, under low SIM conditions, tourists exhibit reduced willingness to extract symbolic values from service environments, making it difficult to fully activate psychological ownership. This consequently weakens the mediating efficiency of this cognitive variable in the relationship between PSQ and TPEB [57]. Therefore, the following moderated mediation hypothesis is proposed:

Methods

Construct measurement

The measurement items pertaining to PSQ, TPEB, and SIM were grounded in established scales that have been validated for acceptable reliability and validity within the extant literature. These scales were meticulously adapted to ensure their enhanced fit within the specific context of vacation tourism. Specifically, PSQ was captured using a six-item scale adapted from Su et al. [34] and He et al. [48], encompassing the SERVQUAL dimensions. SIM was assessed with a five-item scale derived from the works of Clary et al. [60] and Allard et al. [25]. Furthermore, TPEB was measured utilizing a six-item scale adapted from Ramkissoon et al. [2] and Wu et al. [27]. To ensure the suitability of the questionnaire, the content validity was rigorously examined by three professors specializing in tourism management. Based on their feedback, refinements were made to the wording of certain statements and the overall layout, tailoring them to the unique characteristics of the tourism context.

The measurement of Destination Psychological Ownership (DPO) in this study diverged from existing scales, instead developing a completely new measurement tool based on profound theoretical reflection and contextual needs. The core rationale for this decision lies in our observation that mainstream scales exhibit significant conceptualization and measurement limitations. As noted by Olckers [61], many measurement approaches centered on the core statement "the target is mine" [36], while capturing the essential "sense of possession" in psychological ownership, may mislead its application to non-property objects (such as tourist destinations) and even raise validity concerns [62]. Particularly within the Chinese cultural context that emphasizes collectivism and public attributes, directly asking tourists whether they feel a destination "belongs to them" may not only elicit insincere responses due to cultural misfit but also fail to capture the deeper, richer emotional and psychological connections between tourists and destinations.

Therefore, in conceptualizing DPO, this study returns to the most classical and fundamental theoretical origin of the construct—the three-dimensional framework proposed by Pierce et al. [36]. This theory explicitly posits that psychological ownership is not a singular, vague "feeling," but rather a composite psychological state driven by three fundamental human motivations: belongingness (the desire to connect with and become part of the target), self-efficacy (demonstrating one's capabilities through control over and understanding of the target), and self-identity (the target becoming an extension and expression of one's self-concept). We argue that this classical framework is particularly well-suited for understanding the psychological connection between tourists and tourism destinations. In the non-routine, high-involvement experience of vacation tourism, what drives tourists to engage in PEB is often not a declarative sense of possession akin to "legal property rights" over the destination. Instead, it is the deep-seated psychological needs fulfilled by the destination during the immersive experience: feeling part of the destination community or environment (belongingness), feeling capable of influencing the state of the destination (self-efficacy), and internalizing the characteristics and values of the destination as part of one's self-image (self-identity). These three dimensions collectively constitute the tourist's experience of psychological ownership toward the destination and ultimately drive responsible behaviors such as TPEB. Our conceptual choice aims precisely to measure this complex, multi-dimensionally motivated internal state, rather than merely capturing a possibly culturally-taboo or ambiguously expressed declaration of "possession."

Although subsequent research, such as that by Avey et al. [63], attempted to incorporate "responsibility" as a new dimension, the prevailing academic consensus maintains that responsibility is more appropriately characterized as a resultant attitude or behavioral tendency derived from psychological ownership [22, 36]. Conflating it with ownership itself blurs the construct boundaries and hinders the clear revelation of its mechanism as an antecedent variable. Based on this theoretical clarification, the new DPO scale developed in this study fundamentally differs from existing scales. Traditional scales predominantly focus on the single dimension of "sense of possession," whereas our scale strictly adheres to the classical theory, constructing a three-dimensional measurement model encompassing destination belongingness, self-identity, and self-efficacy. This signifies a shift in measurement focus from "Do you feel you own it?" to "Does your connection with it make you feel a sense of belonging, does it define who you are, does it make you feel capable?". This adjustment enables the scale to better reflect the complex psychological feelings tourists experience during transient yet profound travel experiences and to better adapt to the relational and harmonious characteristics emphasized in Chinese culture.

To ensure the scientific rigor and robustness of this new scale, we strictly adhered to the scale development procedures recommended by Gerbing and Anderson [64]. The process began with in-depth interviews with 21 tourists who had vacation experiences, extracting initial item pools from genuine travel experiences that reflected the aforementioned three dimensions. Subsequently, through a series of rigorous statistical purification procedures—including homogeneity testing, corrected item-total correlation (CITC) analysis, and exploratory factor analysis (EFA)—we screened and retained 13 items from an initial pool of 20 that best represented the DPO construct. In the final validation phase, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) results confirmed the scale's excellent reliability and validity: Cronbach's alpha values and composite reliability (CR) for all dimensions significantly exceeded the 0.7 threshold; factor loadings and average variance extracted (AVE) met the standards for convergent validity; and the dimensions demonstrated good discriminant validity. Furthermore, we invited experts and scholars in the field to review and optimize the item wording, thereby ensuring the scale's face validity and content validity. Ultimately, we successfully developed a 13-item DPO scale with sound psychometric properties, combining theoretical depth and contextual relevance, providing a reliable measurement foundation for subsequent empirical research.The finalized constructs and measurement items are appended in Appendix A.

This study selected gender, education, age, and travelling time as control variables, grounded on the established evidence that demographic characteristics significantly impact TPEB [17]. Each of the measurement items was evaluated using a 7-point Likert-type scale, with anchor points spanning from 'strongly disagree' (1) to 'strongly agree' (7), ensuring a comprehensive assessment of participant responses.

Pilot survey and data collection

All participants provided informed consent prior to completing a self-administered questionnaire and were made aware of their right to withdraw from the study at any time. To ensure the reliability of the measurement scale, a pilot survey was conducted in September 2021 among vacation tourists visiting Wenzhou City, Zhejiang Province. Respondents were pre-screened to confirm they had resided in the destination for a minimum of two days. A total of 220 questionnaires were administered, resulting in the collection of 193 completed responses. Following the exclusion of 18 invalid responses, 175 valid questionnaires remained, yielding an effective response rate of 79.5%. A reliability analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 to assess the Cronbach's alpha coefficient and corrected item-total correlations (CITC). The findings indicated that all construct scales achieved an acceptable level of reliability, as evidenced by Cronbach's alpha values exceeding 0.80 and CITC values greater than 0.50, consistent with the benchmarks established by Hair et al. [65].

The formal data collection process was executed through a combination of on-site and online surveys, aimed at enhancing sample diversity and strengthening the external validity of the study. The on-site survey was conducted in two prestigious provincial tourism resorts located in Zhejiang Province—Wenzhou Dongtou Banping Mountain Ocean Resort and Taishun Langqiao-Radon Spring Tourism Resort—spanning from April 14th to June 26th, 2022. These destinations are renowned for their distinctive natural landscapes, picturesque vistas, and rich cultural heritage. To ensure the representativeness of the sample, the following criteria were implemented for tourist selection: (1) a minimum stay of two days in the destination, (2) domestic tourists only, and (3) individuals aged 16 or above with adequate reading and comprehension abilities. To maximize diversity and minimize sampling bias, data collection was randomly distributed across various weekdays throughout the survey period, adhering to the methodology advocated by Megeirhi et al. [9]. Ultimately, 400 questionnaires were distributed, with 350 successfully returned, comprising 145 responses from Taishun Langqiao-Radon Spring Tourism Resort and 205 from Dongtou Banping Mountain Ocean Tourism Resort.

The online survey was administered via Sojump, China's foremost professional online survey platform, from June 6th to June 25th, 2022. Data was gathered utilizing a snowball sampling approach through personal social network relationships. This strategy was selected due to the cultural context in China, where leveraging personal social relationships for questionnaire surveys can effectively garner the trust and cooperation of respondents, thereby enhancing the authenticity of the findings [66]. The online survey specifically targeted tourists aged 16 and above who had undertaken vacation tourism experiences within the past six months. Ultimately, 238 online questionnaires were amassed, with IP addresses indicating a diverse respondent pool spanning 15 provinces, encompassing Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Shanghai, Henan, Anhui, Jilin, and Sichuan.

In total, this study collected 588 questionnaires based on a combination of onsite and online surveys. Following a rigorous screening process to eliminate invalid questionnaires, which included those with numerous identical answers or incomplete responses, 501 valid questionnaires were retained for analysis. These comprised 193 online questionnaires and 308 on-site questionnaires. The demographic profiles are shown in Table 1.

Data analysis

This study employed SPSS 25.0 and AMOS 23.0 software to conduct a comprehensive data analysis. Descriptive statistics, assessment of common method variance bias, and demographic profiles were comprehensively analyzed using SPSS 25.0. Subsequently, a measurement model was rigorously tested through CFA and reliability analysis to ensure the validity and reliability of the constructs. A structural equation model (SEM) was then utilized to delve into the intricate relationships between the latent constructs. Furthermore, the bootstrapping method was implemented to perform mediation and moderated mediation analyses.

Results

Measurement invariance test

Given that data were collected through both on-site and online channels, we conducted a measurement invariance test to ensure that the constructs were interpreted consistently across the two subsamples. Using AMOS 23.0, we tested for configural, metric, and scalar invariance for the measurement model. The results indicated that the configural model (unconstrained) fit the data well (χ2/df = 1.897, CFI = 0.941, RMSEA = 0.042). The metric model (factor loadings constrained equal) showed no significant fit deterioration (ΔCFI = −0.008, ΔRMSEA = 0.001), supporting metric invariance. Similarly, the scalar model (intercepts and loadings constrained) also demonstrated acceptable fit (ΔCFI = −0.012, ΔRMSEA = 0.002), supporting scalar invariance. These results suggest that the constructs are measured equivalently across the on-site and online samples, justifying the pooling of data for subsequent analysis.

Common method variance

Given the reliance on self-reported questionnaires for data collection, Harman's single-factor test was administered to assess the severity of common method variance. The outcomes indicated that the first unrotated factor accounted for 38.66% of the total variance, falling below the established critical threshold of 40% [67]. This finding underscores the absence of a significant common method variance issue, thereby mitigating concerns regarding potential bias in the data.

Data normality

Adhering to Kline's recommendations [68], skewness and kurtosis values were utilized to evaluate the normality of the data. The analysis revealed that the absolute skewness values ranged from 0.223 to 1.057, remaining below the threshold of 2, while the absolute kurtosis values ranged from 0.189 to 1.416, also staying within the acceptable limit of 3. Consequently, the study's data can be considered normally distributed, satisfying the prerequisite for subsequent statistical analyses.

Measurement model test

This study conducted CFA to scrutinize the underlying structure of the constructs, yielding an acceptable fit of the proposed measurement model to the empirical data, as evidenced by the following indices: χ2/df = 2.188, RMSEA = 0.049, NFI = 0.927, RFI = 0.919, IFI = 0.959, TLI = 0.954, CFI = 0.959. The reliability and validity of the measurement model were rigorously evaluated using various indicators. Furthermore, the CR values ranged from 0.900 to 0.918, exceeding the recommended benchmark of 0.700, thereby ensuring adequate reliability of the latent constructs. Additionally, the standardized factor loadings of the items were substantial, ranging from 0.749 to 0.895, and statistically significant at the 0.01 level. The AVE values, spanning from 0.644 to 0.747, exceeded the threshold of 0.5 [69], signifying an acceptable level of convergent validity.

Discussions

This study provides a comprehensive investigation into the mechanisms through which tourists' perceived service quality (PSQ) influences their pro-environmental behavior (TPEB) within the vacation tourism context, specifically examining the mediating role of destination psychological ownership (DPO) and the moderating effect of self-improvement motivation (SIM). While prior research has explored the relationship between PSQ and behavioral intentions across various tourism settings [13], studies linking PSQ specifically to TPEB remain limited, particularly those systematically elucidating the underlying psychological pathways. Our empirical findings demonstrate that PSQ not only directly enhances TPEB but also exerts indirect influence through the mediating mechanism of DPO. This discovery extends the impact of service quality from the functional level to the psychological dimension of belonging, thereby deepening our understanding of the drivers behind tourists' environmental behaviors. These results align with the work of He et al. [48] and Qiu et al. [45], further confirming that tourists who perceive high-quality services and restorative environmental conditions are more inclined to respond through environmentally responsible behaviors. Theoretically, this also resonates with social exchange theory, suggesting that when tourists' personal needs are satisfied through quality services, they reciprocate by engaging in pro-environmental behaviors at the destination [44].

Further analysis reveals that DPO plays a partial mediating role between PSQ and TPEB, illuminating the sequential psychological mechanism of "service quality perception → psychological ownership establishment → pro-environmental behavior formation." This pathway not only corroborates findings from Wang et al. [72] and Yao et al. [19] regarding the bridging function of psychological ownership between subjective perceptions and prosocial behaviors, but also expands the theoretical antecedents of the DPO-TPEB relationship established by Li et al. [18] and Xu et al. [17]. From the perspective of psychological ownership theory, tourists develop a sense of control and belonging toward the destination through perceived service quality, subsequently coming to view environmental protection as their personal responsibility. This demonstrates the effectiveness of psychological ownership theory in explaining the profound emotional connection between tourists and destinations.

Furthermore, this study identifies the moderating role of self-improvement motivation (SIM) in the "PSQ → DPO → TPEB" pathway, indicating that individual differences among tourists play a crucial role in behavioral formation mechanisms. Specifically, SIM strengthens the effects of both PSQ and DPO on TPEB, suggesting that tourists pursuing positive self-images are more inclined to translate high-quality service experiences and psychological ownership into actual pro-environmental behaviors. This finding not only supports Angelis et al.'s [59] theoretical proposition regarding the behavior-facilitating effect of self-improvement motivation, but also aligns with the "self-enhancement/self-protection" theory advocated by Alicke et al. [73], further revealing how goal-driven mindsets regulate environmental protection behaviors in tourism contexts. Therefore, by empirically confirming the amplifying effect of SIM and expanding the theoretical boundaries of TPEB formation mechanisms through the lens of personality-situation interaction, this study provides new perspectives for future research integrating individual motivations with psychological mechanisms.

Theoretical contributions

This study makes three significant theoretical contributions through the construction and validation of a moderated mediation model, substantially deepening and expanding the theoretical understanding of the formation mechanisms underlying Tourists' Pro-Environmental Behavior (TPEB).

First, this research systematically elucidates the intrinsic psychological mechanisms through which Perceived Service Quality (PSQ) influences TPEB, thereby transcending the conventional behavioral outcomes typically examined in service quality management research. While existing studies have confirmed PSQ's impact on commercial outcome variables such as customer satisfaction and loyalty [34], theoretical explanations regarding how it induces prosocial environmental behaviors remain inadequate. This study not only confirms the direct positive effect of PSQ on TPEB but, more importantly, identifies the crucial mediating role of Destination Psychological Ownership (DPO) in this relationship. This finding creatively bridges social exchange theory with the SOR framework, elucidating a theoretical pathway of "service experience → psychological ownership → pro-environmental behavior." Specifically, the research reveals that high-quality services fulfill tourists' intrinsic needs for control and belonging, prompting them to incorporate the destination into their cognitive sphere of the "extended self," thereby stimulating pro-environmental behavior driven by a sense of responsibility. This mechanism provides a novel theoretical perspective for understanding the environmental value of service investments.

Second, this study significantly expands the application boundaries and explanatory depth of psychological ownership theory within sustainable tourism research. Although the DPO concept originated in organizational behavior studies [22], this research demonstrates its unique value in explaining tourist-environment relationships by contextualizing it within "tourist-destination" interactions. The findings indicate that DPO not only serves as a key psychological bridge connecting external service stimuli with individual environmental behaviors but that its formation is significantly influenced by service quality—a manageable factor. This understanding extends DPO's theoretical framework from being primarily driven by emotional responses (such as awe and aesthetic appreciation) in previous research to one driven by service experiences, thereby enriching the comprehension of DPO's antecedents [16, 17]. Simultaneously, by situating DPO within the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S–O-R) theoretical framework, this study provides a comprehensive theoretical explanation for understanding how external environmental stimuli in tourism contexts ultimately drive behavioral responses through internal psychological state changes, thereby reinforcing the applicability and explanatory power of the S–O-R framework in sustainable tourism research.

Finally, by introducing Self-Improvement Motivation (SIM) as a key moderating variable, this study provides crucial theoretical insights into the boundary conditions of pro-environmental behavior. The findings reveal that SIM not only strengthens the direct impact of PSQ on TPEB but also significantly enhances the driving effect of DPO on TPEB. This discovery moves beyond previous research that predominantly focused on demographic variables or situational factors as moderators, revealing from a motivational psychology perspective the critical role of individual self-construction needs in environmental behavior decision-making. It indicates that tourists' pro-environmental behavior serves, to some extent, as a self-presentation strategy; the behavioral translation effects of service experiences and psychological ownership are most pronounced when environmental behaviors help reinforce their self-concept as "responsible travelers." This understanding organically integrates self-enhancement theory [59, 73] into the environmental behavior research framework, providing a powerful theoretical explanation for understanding why different tourists exhibit behavioral differences under similar service experiences and psychological states, thereby addressing a significant gap in TPEB formation mechanism research.

Practical implications

This study elucidates the mechanisms linking perceived service quality (PSQ), destination psychological ownership (DPO), and tourists' pro-environmental behavior (TPEB), providing both theoretical foundation and practical guidance for destination managers seeking to design effective strategies for promoting environmental behaviors. Building upon the core pathway of "PSQ → DPO → TPEB" while fully considering the moderating role of self-improvement motivation (SIM), we propose the following systematic management recommendations.

First, a systematic enhancement of service quality should be implemented to solidify the experiential foundation for pro-environmental behaviors. Given that PSQ not only directly positively influences TPEB but also exerts indirect effects through DPO, destinations should establish a multi-level service optimization system. At the personnel service level, frontline staff training should integrate environmental awareness with service skills, enabling them not only to provide friendly, professional standardized services but also to act as conveyors of the destination's environmental philosophy—for instance, proactively introducing local ecological conservation measures during service interactions and guiding tourists to participate in environmental activities. At the facility and service process level, improvements should focus on service supports directly related to pro-environmental behaviors, such as installing clear and comprehensible waste sorting guidelines within scenic areas, providing convenient water refill stations to reduce plastic bottle consumption, and designing digital tour guide systems to replace paper maps. These initiatives not only enhance functional service quality but also seamlessly embed environmental values into the tourist experience, creating both convenient conditions and contextual cues for TPEB occurrence.

Second, focused efforts should be made to cultivate psychological ownership, activating tourists' intrinsic motivation for environmental protection. Addressing DPO's crucial mediating role between PSQ and TPEB, management strategies should aim to transform tourists from "bystanders" into "caretakers." On one hand, DPO can be fostered by enhancing tourists' sense of control and participation. For example, establishing digital "My Travel Footprint" maps that record tourists' environmental behaviors and provide positive feedback; creating "Tourist Think Tank" platforms to solicit opinions and ideas for destination environmental protection, with public acknowledgment and attribution for adopted suggestions. This gives tourists tangible influence over the destination. On the other hand, emotional identification between tourists and the destination should be deepened. Through designing immersive nature education programs, organizing eco-tours led by professional guides, and narrating unique ecological conservation stories of the destination, managers can help tourists understand the fragility and conservation value of the local environment, thereby sparking genuine care and belonging, and internalizing environmental responsibility as a natural behavior of "ownership."

Finally, self-improvement motivation should be strategically leveraged to reinforce the sustainability of pro-environmental behaviors. This study's finding that SIM enhances the promoting effect of DPO on TPEB suggests that managers should position pro-environmental behaviors as valuable "social assets" and "identity symbols." A destination-wide unified "Green Tourist" certification and incentive system could be established. When tourists complete specific environmental tasks (such as maintaining low-carbon transportation throughout their journey, participating in garbage clean-ups, or completing leave-no-trace challenges), the system would award them with tiered electronic badges and shareable certificates of honor, linked to social media displays. For top performers, titles like "Honorary Environmental Supervisor" could be conferred, along with exclusive experiences such as special access rights to ecological reserve areas. This approach aims to transform TPEB from an external moral obligation into a social behavior through which tourists construct and display their ideal identity as "responsible travelers," thereby harnessing their intrinsic self-improvement motivation to ensure the continuity and broadcast effect of environmental engagement.

Limitations and future directions

This study is subject to several limitations. Firstly, the reliance on a self-report methodology in data collection necessitates consideration of potential social desirability biases, which may influence the results. Despite efforts to mitigate common method variance through rigorous data collection procedures and statistical handling, its complete elimination remains elusive. Hence, a mixed-methods approach, integrating both experimental and qualitative methodologies, is advocated to offer a more credible and nuanced understanding of the constructs under investigation. Secondly, the use of cross-sectional data to examine causal relationships, particularly those pertaining to TPEB, introduces uncertainties about the causality of the observed effects. To rectify this limitation, longitudinal or experimental designs that explicitly manipulate the constructs of interest are strongly recommended [23]. Thirdly, the onsite survey was conducted exclusively in two tourist destinations characterized by natural landscapes, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to other tourism contexts. Future research could further examine the boundary conditions and generalizability of the findings by validating them across more diverse destination types (e.g., urban tourism, theme parks) with international tourists from varied cultural backgrounds. Lastly, while this study explores the sense of ownership of a destination from an individual perspective, it acknowledges that psychological ownership can be conceptualized at both individual and collective levels [74]. Given the collective nature of tourist destinations and the prevalent collectivist tendencies in Chinese culture, the sole focus on individual psychological ownership may overlook culturally influences on the emotional bond between tourists and destinations. Consequently, future research is encouraged to delve into tourists' collective DPO, recognizing that collaborative efforts are pivotal in addressing environmental challenges.

Conclusions

This study presents a moderated mediation model to delve into the intricate interplay between PSQ and TPEB within the context of vacation tourism. The findings reveal that PSQ exerts a positive and significant influence on TPEB, with destination psychological ownership (DPO) partially mediating this relationship. Furthermore, SIM emerges as a crucial moderator, enhancing both the direct and indirect effects of DPO on TPEB. This research contributes to a profounder comprehension of the underlying mechanisms that govern TPEB and expands the literature on DPO within the tourism domain. The findings offer novel insights into the sustainable development of tourist destinations, suggesting that destination managers can foster more proactive PEB among tourists by enhancing service quality and fostering their sense of psychological ownership towards the destination.
