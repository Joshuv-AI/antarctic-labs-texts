Work-family conflict and professional identity among Chinese young faculty: examining the action paths and moderating mechanisms
Authors: Chunmei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study examined the relationship between work-family conflict and professional identity among young faculty in Chinese higher education institutions. It also tested the indirect role of mindful teaching and the moderating role of work-family support. The study further explored the different gender patterns of indirect and moderating effects.MethodsData were collected from 505 young university faculty using the Work-Family Conflict Scale, Mindfulness in Teaching Scale, Teacher Professional Identity Scale, and Work-Family Support Scale. Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, and hierarchical regression.Results(1) Work-family conflict exhibited a significant negative association with young faculty’s professional identity. (2) Mindfulness in teaching (intrapersonal and interpersonal mindfulness) played an indirect role in the relationship between work-family conflict and professional identity. (3) Work-family support moderated the relationships between work-family conflict and mindfulness in teaching/professional identity. When the level of work-family support was higher, the negative association between work-family conflict and mindfulness in teaching/professional identity was weaker. (4) Indirect and moderating effects exhibited distinct gender patterns. For indirect effects, subgroup analyses by gender revealed that, in the male teacher sample, the indirect pathway via interpersonal mindfulness was significant. In contrast, in the female teacher sample, the indirect pathway via intrapersonal mindfulness was significant. For moderating effects, descriptive comparisons between gender subgroups showed that the moderating effect of work-family support had a larger coefficient in the female sample than in the male sample. However, this difference was not formally tested for statistical significance.ConclusionsThis study offers a new perspective for understanding the occupational psychological well-being of university faculty. Based on these correlational findings, cautious exploration is warranted regarding possible intervention entry points, such as enhancing teachers’ mindfulness levels and building supportive environments, with potential differential emphases for male and female teachers. Nevertheless, further effectiveness testing is required.Keywords: Young faculty, Work-family conflict, Mindfulness in teaching, Professional identity, Work-family support, Gender differences

Introduction

As of 2022, young faculty under 40 account for 48% of China’s higher education workforce (949400 individuals), serving as the backbone of teaching and research [1]. University teaching is a profession highly dependent on self-identity, requiring stronger vocational self-recognition compared to other fields [2]. The professional identity of faculty members is crucial for talent development and scientific advancement in higher education. Teachers’ professional identity (TPI) refers to the integrated acceptance and affirmation of one’s professional role through dynamic interactions between the individual and their vocation, encompassing positive cognition, affective experiences, and behavioral inclinations [3, 4]. Robust professional identity serves as the psychological foundation for young faculty to effectively fulfill their academic roles [5, 6]. However, recent rapid systemic transformations in higher education have subjected young academics to unprecedented pressures, evidenced by increasing reports of sudden deaths and extreme cases of self-harm or interpersonal violence [7]. This cohort now faces a crisis in professional identity, with empirical studies consistently revealing suboptimal levels of vocational commitment [8, 9]. Investigating the formative pathways and regulatory mechanisms of TPI during this critical developmental phase holds significant practical value for faculty cultivation and institutional advancement.

TPI is an important form of process-role identity. According to the Process-Role Theory, role formation is a dynamic process in which individuals interact with their surrounding environment to construct their roles, with interaction serving as a critical link between role cognition and role enactment. It is evident that an individual’s role identity is shaped by a complex interplay of internal and external factors. A typical example of such factors is role conflict, including intra-role conflict and inter-role conflict [10]. Existing scholarship has predominantly focused on intra-role overload among young faculty, particularly the teaching-research dichotomy and pressures from rigorous performance evaluations [8]. Yet limited attention has been paid to inter-role conflicts (e.g., work-family imbalance) and their impact on TPI development. The limited relevant studies have primarily examined the proximal effects of work-family conflict on short-term work states like job performance and satisfaction [11], with scant research delving into its long-term mechanisms affecting TPI. Accordingly, this study aims to explore the relationship between work-family conflict and TPI among young university faculty, further investigate how this external factor relates to TPI through underlying internal psychological processes, and explore potential correlational factors that may moderate this relationship.

The relationship between WFC and TPI among young faculty

Work-family conflict (WFC) refers to a state of conflict in which incompatible pressures from the work and family domains—in terms of time, emotion, and behavior—make it difficult for individuals to balance their roles across these domains [12, 13]. Early-career university faculty are in a unique phase of life characterized by both career establishment and family formation. During this critical period for TPI development, they face dual pressures and challenges from both work and family domains [14, 15]. Surveys have also found that the level of WFC among early-career university faculty in China is generally high [13, 16].

According to the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) Model, all job characteristics can be categorized into job demands and job resources. If excessively high demands cannot be mitigated through sufficient resources, employees may experience depletion of psychological resources, which in turn impairs their work performance and overall well-being [17]. From this theoretical perspective, WFC can be viewed as a typical spillover of job demands. Early-career faculty frequently navigate the boundary between work and family roles, making them highly susceptible to a state of psychological resource depletion characterized by time scarcity and divided attention. Importantly, the construction of TPI among university faculty has unique characteristics; it depends on faculty having free, autonomous time and ample mental space to engage in highly innovative teaching and research activities [2, 18]. Therefore, when early-career faculty experience continuous psychological resource loss due to WFC, the psychological energy required for deep thinking, emotional investment, and value reflection is compromised, directly threatening their deep-seated acceptance and identity construction of the faculty role. Previous empirical research has also shown that WFC, as a risk factor, can lead to burnout among university faculty and reduce their job performance and job satisfaction [11]. Based on this, the present study proposes that WFC among early-career university faculty is significantly negatively correlated with their TPI.

The indirect role of mindfulness in teaching

To understand how WFC is associated with TPI, it is essential to examine potential psychological mechanisms. Based on the JD-R model, faculty’s psychological resource status plays a critical role in the relationship between WFC and their TPI, and mindfulness, as an active resource management strategy, may play an important indirect role in this process. Mindfulness refers to an individual’s conscious and non-judgmental focus on present-moment experiences [19], which itself requires sustained investment of substantial psychological resources [20]. When individuals cope with WFC, they expend significant resources, which may lead to a depletion of psychological energy and an inability to maintain focus on the present state. Previous research has also found that when individuals experience higher levels of WFC, their mindfulness levels tend to be lower [21].

However, prior studies have mainly focused on individuals’ internal mindfulness, neglecting awareness of others’ consciousness and behavior. The concept of “teaching mindfulness” can address this gap [22, 23]. Mindfulness in teaching refers to the ability of teachers to consciously and receptively perceive internal immediate experiences and external environmental stimuli during instruction, with an attitude of curiosity, acceptance, and non-judgment. It includes intrapersonal mindfulness and interpersonal mindfulness. The former involves teachers maintaining high awareness and acceptance of their own teaching thoughts, feelings, and behaviors, while the latter entails teachers immediately recognizing students’ states and needs during teacher-student interactions, approaching complex teaching situations with openness, acceptance, and a non-judgmental attitude [24, 25]. Mindfulness in teaching serves as a protective factor for teachers’ professional development, helping them effectively recognize their own consciousness and behaviors during teaching, break free from unconscious and automatic emotional patterns, enhance regulation of negative stimuli and emotions, and improve the quality of teacher-student interactions. Moreover, compared to individual components, integrated teaching mindfulness shows stronger predictive power for teachers’ emotional and work states [26]. Previous empirical studies have also shown that teachers’ mindfulness in teaching has a significant positive correlation with their work engagement [27–29], job satisfaction [30, 31], self-efficacy and professional competence [22, 32], and teacher-student relationship quality [33]. while it has a significant negative correlation with negative emotions [23] and burnout [34–36]. Factors such as work engagement, self-efficacy, positive emotions, and teacher-student relationship quality are important elements of TPI. Accordingly, this study proposes that mindfulness in teaching plays an indirect role in the relationship between WFC and TPI among young faculty.

The moderating role of work-family support

The JD-R model also indicates that job resources refer to factors that facilitate the achievement of work goals and mitigate work stress, buffering the negative health effects of job demands and enhancing employees’ work engagement and identification [12]. Work-family support (WFS) is an important external organizational resource for individuals, referring to various forms of support from both work and family domains that benefit employees’ lives during their work processes, playing a protective and enhancing role in individuals’ mental health [37, 38]. The WFS perceived by Chinese enterprise employees includes four dimensions: organizational support, supervisor support, emotional support, and instrumental support. Among these, organizational support and supervisor support mainly refer to support from the work domain, while emotional support and instrumental support primarily refer to support from the family domain [37]. According to the JD-R model, it can be inferred that when early-career faculty in higher education institutions receive higher levels of WFS (e.g., childcare assistance provided by the institution or family members sharing household chores), even if conflicts occur, faculty can replenish resources from external sources, thereby slowing the intensity and speed of psychological resource depletion. This will weaken the direct negative impact of WFC on TPI while compensating for resource losses caused by conflicts through WFS resources, creating a demand-resource balance. This balance makes it easier for faculty to maintain a mindful teaching state, indirectly transforming crises and rebuilding identity. Moreover, compared to general or broad social support, support from work and family domains may be more direct, specific, and effective in mitigating the negative effects of WFC. Previous studies have also confirmed that WFS can reduce the adverse impact of WFC on faculty’s turnover intentions and job burnout [37, 39] and can moderate the negative effects of WFC on faculty’s subjective well-being and job satisfaction [40]. TPI is closely related to their turnover intentions and professional well-being, all of which represent relatively stable and holistic experiences and evaluations of their careers. Therefore, this study proposes that WFS can moderate the negative associations of WFC with both TPI and mindful teaching among young university faculty, such that when the level of WFS is higher, this negative association would be weaker.

Gender-specific patterns of indirect and moderating effects

It is noteworthy that WFC and its coping mechanisms may exhibit different gender-specific patterns. Based on social role theory, society holds different expectations for different genders. Men are generally expected to be primary economic providers (work role), whereas women are expected to be primary family caregivers (family role) [41]. This traditional division of gender roles continues to exert a profound influence in contemporary Chinese society, shaping the dominant cultural norm of “men primarily responsible for work outside the home and women primarily responsible for family care” [42]. However, with socioeconomic development, women’s aspirations for career advancement have intensified, leading them to face more pronounced inter-role conflicts [43]. When men participate in both work and family domains, there is often an enrichment effect between roles; for instance, work itself fulfills the role of “providing for the family”. For women, however, increased investment in the work domain is not accompanied by a corresponding decrease in family role expectations, making them more susceptible to role overload and higher levels of WFC.

More importantly, gender role beliefs not only influence the extent to which individuals perceive conflict but may also profoundly moderate the effectiveness of psychological resource pathways and support systems used to cope with such conflict. According to the theoretical perspective of gender role congruity [44], coping strategies or external resources are likely to be more effective when they align with an individual’s gender-role socialization characteristics. First, concerning mindfulness in teaching as an internal psychological resource, women are often socialized over time to develop higher emotional awareness and relational sensitivity. This may predispose female faculty to be more adept at utilizing mindfulness to buffer the erosion of TPI by WFC. Second, regarding WFS as an external resource, because women bear the primary responsibility for family care, support from family and the organization often serves as crucial “timely assistance” for them, directly counteracting the structural disadvantages they face. For men, such support may function more as a “supplemental bonus”, with relatively lower marginal utility. Based on this theoretical analysis, social role theory provides a macro-level social structural basis for gender-specific patterns, while gender role consistency theory offers a micro-level psychological mechanism explanation. Therefore, for exploratory purposes, this study tests the indirect and moderating path patterns separately in samples of young male and female faculty. Different path patterns are expected to be observed, but no specific hypotheses are made regarding the direction of the differences.

In summary, the current study aims to construct a moderated mediation model (see Fig. 1) to examine the relationship between WFC and TPI among young university faculty members. The following hypotheses are proposed: (1) There is a negative correlation between WFC and TPI among young university faculty; (2) Mindfulness in teaching plays a significant indirect role in the relationship between WFC and TPI; (3) WFS moderates the direct path and the first half of the indirect path; (4) The indirect and moderating effects may exhibit gender-specific patterns.

Methods

Participants

Using the convenience sampling method, 528 married young faculty members aged 28–40 (34.87 ± 3.06) from 13 undergraduate institutions across nine provinces and municipalities in China were recruited as participants. Electronic questionnaires, created via Wenjuanxing (a Chinese online survey platform), were distributed to the participants through social software such as WeChat or QQ. Prior to the survey, informed consent was obtained from all participants, who were also informed that they could terminate or withdraw from the study at any time. This study was approved by the ethics committee of the affiliated institution. A total of 528 questionnaires were distributed, and 505 valid responses were retained after excluding incomplete or patterned responses, yielding an effective response rate of 95.64%. The sample comprised 228 males (45.15%) and 277 females (54.85%). Among them, 175 were married without children (34.65%), and 330 were married with children (65.35%). Teaching experience ranged from 1 to 11 years (3.72 ± 0.75 years), with 213 participants (42.18%) having 1–3 years of experience, 163 (32.28%) 4–6 years, 114 (22.57%) 7–9 years, and 15 (2.97%) 10 or more years. All participants held teaching-research positions, including 331 lecturers (65.54%), 161 associate professors (31.88%), and 13 full professors (2.58%).

Research instruments

Work-Family Conflict Questionnaire

The Work-Family Conflict Questionnaire for teachers, developed by Wu et al. (2009), was employed [45]. This 22-item scale consists of two subscales: Work Interfering with Family (11 items) and Family Interfering with Work (11 items). Each subscale includes three dimensions: emotional affect, psychological resources, and behavioral patterns. Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater conflict. In this study, the overall Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.90. For the Work Interfering with Family subscale and its dimensions, Cronbach’s α ranged from 0.76 to 0.84, and for the Family Interfering with Work subscale and its dimensions, it ranged from 0.74 to 0.85.

Mindfulness in Teaching Scale

The Mindfulness in Teaching Scale developed by Frank et al. (2016) was used [24]. This 16-item scale comprises two dimensions: intrapersonal mindfulness (9 items) and interpersonal mindfulness (7 items). Responses were scored on a 5-point Likert scale, with reverse-scored items converted prior to analysis. Higher scores reflect higher levels of mindful teaching. In this study, Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.81 for the total scale, 0.85 for intrapersonal mindfulness, and 0.79 for interpersonal mindfulness.

Teacher Professional Identity Scale

The Teacher Professional Identity Scale, developed by Li and Yan (2018),was utilized [3]. This 26-item scale includes two subscales: Professional Identity Perception (14 items) and Professional Identity Behavioral Tendency (12 items). The Professional Identity Perception subscale consists of three dimensions: professional values, sense of belonging, and professional efficacy. The Professional Identity Behavioral Tendency subscale includes value-driven behavior tendency, teacher community behavior tendency, and occupational stress coping. Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating stronger professional identity. In this study, the overall Cronbach’s α was 0.91. For the Professional Identity Perception subscale and its dimensions, Cronbach’s α ranged from 0.82 to 0.89, and for the Professional Identity Behavioral Tendency subscale and its dimensions, it ranged from 0.84 to 0.86.

Work-Family Support Scale

The Work-Family Support Scale, developed by Li and Zhao, was adopted [37]. This 30-item scale includes four dimensions: organizational support, leadership support, emotional support, and instrumental support. The first two dimensions reflect workplace support, while the latter two reflect family support. Responses were scored on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating stronger support. In this study, the overall Cronbach’s α was 0.87, and the coefficients for the three dimensions ranged from 0.75 to 0.85.

Procedure and data processing

Questionnaires were distributed online via the Wenjuanxing platform, with participants completing them anonymously. Prior to data collection, participants were informed of confidentiality protocols, provided informed consent, and given clear instructions. The survey took approximately 15 min to complete, and participants received compensation.

We used SPSS 26.0 to conduct descriptive statistics and correlation analyses. Indirect and moderating effects were examined using the SPSS Process macro. In all Process models, variables were entered into the regression model in blocks according to theory-driven hypotheses, rather than using automated stepwise selection. Bias-corrected percentile bootstrap methods with 5,000 resamples were applied to compute 95% confidence intervals.

Results

Confirmatory factor analysis and common method bias test

Given that all variables in this study were collected through self-reports from university faculty, there may be a risk of common method bias. To minimize the potential impact of this bias on the research findings, procedural controls were implemented in advance, including anonymous responses, reverse scoring of certain items, data collection at different time points (administer one scale per week), and random ordering of questions. In terms of statistical analysis, Harman’s single-factor test was first conducted to assess common method bias. The confirmatory factor analysis results indicated that the four-factor model demonstrated a significantly better fit (χ²/df = 2.21, CFI = 0.96, TLI = 0.96, RMSEA = 0.04) compared to other competing models, suggesting good discriminant validity among the core variables. Additionally, the unrotated principal component factor analysis revealed 19 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, with the first common factor accounting for 20.38% of the total variance, which is below the critical threshold of 40%. Although Harman’s single-factor test is considered a relatively rough diagnostic tool due to its limited sensitivity, the strong discriminant validity results, combined with rigorous procedural controls, provide preliminary evidence that common method bias is not a significant concern in this study and does not pose a serious threat to the reliability of the research conclusions.

Discussion

This study finds a significant negative correlation between WFC and TPI among young university faculty, with mindfulness teaching playing a significant indirect role and WFS serving as a conditional moderating factor. By extending the JD-R model to the psychological domain of faculty, this research highlights the importance of resource dynamics and balance in shaping TPI.

The negative correlation between WFC and TPI among young university faculty

Young university faculty are in the early career stage, a critical period for forming professional identity. Previous studies have shown that their TPI remains precarious. Current research revealed a significant negative correlation between WFC and TPI among young university faculty, enriching the understanding of antecedents of TPI in this group. Prior studies indicate that WFC among university faculty negatively affects job performance and satisfaction and is a risk factor for burnout [11]. The findings of the current study suggest that the negative effect of WFC on young university faculty is not limited to short-term job performance and satisfaction; it may also have cumulative effects, thereby establishing a profound negative correlation with their TPI. Contrary to the public perception that university faculty enjoy flexible working hours conducive to balancing work and family, such flexibility often blurs boundaries between these domains [46], exacerbating WFC. In Chinese cultural values, both “establishing a family” and “building a career” are paramount life goals. Young faculty navigating these dual demands experience heightened role permeability and conflict. Teaching and research roles require significant time and energy investments, often necessitating “working from home” during non-office hours. This overlap of work and life spaces increases WFC, leading to psychological strain, reduced job engagement and satisfaction [47], and ultimately hindering the development of TPI.

The indirect role of mindfulness in teaching

This study identified both intrapersonal and interpersonal mindfulness in teaching played an important indirect role in the relationship between WFC and TPI among young faculty. Previous research similarly highlights mindfulness as an indirect factor between faculty’s WFC and negative emotions [23] and between nurses’ WFC and sleep quality [21]. Shapiro et al. (2006) propose that mindfulness operates through “reperceiving”—a decoupling of the self from events—facilitating proactive reappraisal of environmental or internal stressors and disengagement from negative experiences [48]. Conversely, low mindfulness predisposes individuals to automatic entanglement in stress-induced negative emotions and self-evaluations [49]. Integrating the JD-R model, high WFC can lead to emotional exhaustion and distraction among young university faculty, consuming the psychological energy otherwise allocated for teaching reflection and emotional engagement. This makes it difficult for them to maintain a “mindful state” in teaching—such as sustaining comprehensive attention and keen awareness of teaching behaviors and classroom activities, and adopting effective, non-reactive coping and regulatory strategies in the face of conflicts and stress. Thus, WFC, through its negative correlation with the level of mindful teaching, is indirectly negatively associated with the self-evaluation and TPI of young university faculty during the teaching process. In summary, this study elucidates the significant indirect role of mindful teaching in the relationship between WFC and TPI among young university faculty. The findings offer meaningful implications for maintaining and enhancing the TPI of young faculty in higher education.

The moderating role of work-family support

Current research shows that WFS significantly moderates both the direct and indirect pathways between WFC and TPI. Among young faculty with higher levels of WFS, the negative associations of WFC with both TPI and mindful teaching are weaker. This conditional pattern aligns with theoretical expectations from the JD-R model. According to this model, young faculty facing WFC can draw on understanding, support, and assistance from family, colleagues, and supervisors. These act as key external job resources. Abundant job resources fulfill basic psychological needs (e.g., autonomy, competence, relatedness). They are positively associated with intrinsic motivation, work efficacy, work engagement, and job performance. This reflects the direct motivational role of job resources. More importantly, the JD-R model suggests that job resources and demands do not operate in isolation but interact with each other. There is a statistical interaction pattern between job resources and job demands in mitigating the negative effects of demands on performance and well-being. This means that support from family and work is linked to a weaker negative impact of WFC on young faculty [17]. When young faculty perceive support from family and their institution, they show lower psychological resource depletion and a higher demands-resources balance. Under such conditions, they feel more secure in their work environment. They are more likely to reappraise conflict situations and regulate their emotions. In stressful contexts, they maintain mindful awareness and sustained engagement in teaching. They are more self-compassionate and kind toward themselves. They remain open, accepting, and empathic in student-teacher and collegial relationships. They also flexibly shift between different goals. These states are accompanied by a more positive, focused, and grateful approach to work-life challenges. In turn, high levels of mindful teaching are associated with more positive and satisfying evaluations of one’s own work [50]. In summary, high WFS serves as a critical contextual resource. It coincides with a weaker direct negative link between WFC and TPI. It also shows a positive, mutual dependence with the indirect pathway involving the maintenance of mindful teaching and the promotion of TPI.

Gender patterns in indirect and moderating effects

In the subgroup path analysis of the current study, male and female teachers exhibited different patterns in terms of the absolute value and significance of effect sizes. This may suggest that the pathway between WFC and TPI is characterized by gender, and the underlying causes warrant further investigation. The gender patterns observed in this study may reflect the differential shaping of teachers’ behavioral strategies by social gender norms, rather than inherent characteristics of gender itself. Regarding the gender patterns found in the indirect pathways, we speculate that these may be related to gender-specific professional role expectations within the teaching profession, as well as the differing coping strategies individuals develop in response. Within mainstream discourses on teacher professionalization, rationality, instrumentality, and procedurality are highly valued—traits often culturally associated with masculinity [51]. In this context, male faculty may rely more on external performance and a sense of role competence that aligns with these “rational actor” expectations to construct their TPI, thereby achieving gender role consistency within the profession. Conversely, emotional, caring, and relational labor, while crucial in practice, are often marginalized in professional discourse. This is due to their labeling as feminized activities. Therefore, female faculty’s TPI may be more closely tied to their own emotional experiences and intrinsic sense of worth.

The gender pattern regarding the moderating role of WFS can be potentially explained from the perspectives of social role theory and gender role congruity theory. Under traditional gender division of labor, men’s access to instrumental support from the family (e.g., time, resources) may represent a socially expected and normalized form of resource matching. It serves as a direct extension of their role as “breadwinners”. Thus, the marginal gain from such support systems is relatively limited [52, 53]. In contrast, female teachers, as traditional providers of family support, are themselves less legitimized in receiving support [54, 55].

In summary, the gender patterns revealed in this study may not stem from intrinsic differences between males and females. Instead, they likely reflect variations in the psychological adaptation strategies individuals adopt, and the resources they require, when coping with socially constructed gender role expectations and structural constraints. These interpretations offer valuable perspectives for understanding gender, work-family relations, and vocational psychology. However, it must be emphasized that these explanations are theoretical inferences based on statistical trends and social mechanisms. They were not directly tested in this study. Moreover, they do not deny the extensive variations within each gender, nor do they presume any innate advantage of either gender in emotional awareness or relational sensitivity. Furthermore, this study did not test the measurement equivalence of the core constructs across male and female teacher groups. Therefore, it cannot be ruled out that the observed gender patterns may be partly due to systematic differences in how male and female respondents interpreted the scale items, rather than reflecting substantive psychological mechanisms. Future research should first test measurement invariance, at least at the scalar level, in larger samples before comparing indirect and moderating pathways across genders, in order to draw more robust conclusions. Finally, it is important to note that the gender patterns found in this study are based on preliminary observations using separate-sample path analyses. Significance of differences in path coefficients between groups does not equate to a statistically significant moderating effect of gender. Due to limited analytical resources, this study did not formally test these differences using multi-group modeling or a moderated mediation index, which remains one of its limitations.

Research implications

First and foremost, it must be emphasized that this study is based on cross-sectional data. All findings reflect correlational rather than causal relationships. Accordingly, the following discussion does not provide direct operational recommendations. Instead, it offers extensions and hypotheses derived from the current statistical results. These are intended to inform future intervention studies using longitudinal or experimental designs. First, regarding the gender-specific indirect pathways of mindfulness teaching. This study found that in the relationship between WFC and TPI, male teachers exert an indirect effect through interpersonal mindfulness, whereas female teachers exert an indirect effect through intrapersonal mindfulness. Whether this gender-specific pattern is stable under intervention remains to be verified through causal designs. If future research confirms the causal direction of these indirect pathways, the following hypotheses could be tested: interventions for male teachers might emphasize mindfulness-based communication training in interpersonal settings, whereas those for female teachers might prioritize self-compassion and resilience training targeting emotional exhaustion. At present, these speculations should be viewed as intervention hypotheses based on gender-specific patterns, not as empirically supported practical strategies. Second, regarding the moderating role of WFS. The descriptive statistics of this study showed that the moderating effect of WFS had a higher coefficient in the female sample than in the male sample (not based on cross-group statistical comparison). Future research could examine whether enhancing emotional support (e.g., flexible work arrangements, family care assistance) more effectively improves female teachers’ TPI. Until experimental or longitudinal evidence is obtained, we suggest that university administrators treat such measures as exploratory directions rather than confirmed effective strategies.

In summary, this study provides preliminary insights into the gendered mechanisms of TPI among young faculty, based on cross-sectional data. Any practical exploration of differentiated interventions should begin with small-scale pilot studies. These should be combined with qualitative interviews to carefully verify their applicability and boundary conditions.

Limitations and future directions

There are several limitations to this study. First, this study employed a cross-sectional design. Therefore, directional assumptions among variables are derived from theoretical reasoning. These assumptions reflect statistical associations, not causal conclusions. The proposed and tested indirect action model is grounded in the following theoretical rationale: WFC is widely regarded as a stressor in the work environment (antecedent variable), mindful teaching represents a state-like coping process (mediator variable), and TPI is an attitudinal outcome shaped by the regulation of work and family domains (outcome variable). Nevertheless, alternative directional models cannot be ruled out. For instance, teachers with low TPI may perceive higher WFC. Mindful teaching could also serve as a common antecedent of WFC and TPI, rather than a mediator. Future research may adopt longitudinal designs or experience sampling methods to examine cross-lagged effects, thereby clarifying the true temporal sequence among variables.

Second, the data in this study were collected from teachers across 13 universities. Teachers within the same university may share similar organizational climates, management policies, and work-related stressors. Consequently, their observations may exhibit within-group similarities. Although conventional regression methods were used in the analysis, we did not statistically correct for the nested data structure using cluster-robust standard errors or multilevel modeling. Future studies should employ hierarchical linear modeling or include university-level variables (e.g., organizational support climate, workload intensity) to more accurately estimate cross-level and within-level effects.

Third, regarding common method bias, the data in this study were derived solely from self-reports of university faculty. This single-source data collection method may introduce the risk of common method bias. Although efforts were made in procedural controls and statistical tests, and the results indicate that common method bias is within an acceptable range, this limitation should still be viewed critically. Harman’s single-factor test, while commonly used, has inherent shortcomings as a diagnostic technique [56]; it can only detect severe common method bias but cannot completely rule out its presence. Therefore, we acknowledge that the relationships among variables may have been either inflated or attenuated to some extent. Specifically, when participants responded to items concerning WFC, mindful teaching, WFS, and TPI, their underlying emotional states, personality traits, or social desirability motivations might have simultaneously influenced their responses across multiple variables. For instance, young faculty members with higher negative affectivity might report both greater WFC and lower TPI, potentially artificially strengthening the negative association between the two. In this study, this implies that the effect sizes of indirect effects and moderating effects may be overestimated, or that certain path coefficients might be influenced by common source variance. Future research should adopt multi-wave and multi-source data collection methods—such as incorporating spouse reports of WFC, student evaluations of mindful teaching behaviors, or colleague assessments of professional engagement—to further mitigate the confounding effects of common method bias on the conclusions.

Finally, WFC and WFS are both multidimensional constructs. Using total scores in this study may have masked the unique mechanisms of specific dimensions. For example, work-to-family conflict and family-to-work conflict may be associated with TPI through interpersonal mindfulness and intrapersonal mindfulness, respectively. Similarly, work support and family support may play different roles in the moderating pathways. Therefore, although the total score approach used in this study is convenient for presenting overall effects, it fails to reveal the aforementioned potential dimension-specific pathways. Future research should adopt more fine-grained dimensional scales to examine whether different path patterns emerge, and to explore whether gender-specific patterns become more complex at the dimensional level.

Conclusion

Based on a survey of early-career university faculty, this study explored the complex relationships among WFC, mindful teaching, WFS, and TPI. The main findings are as follows: (1) WFC exhibits a significant negative association with TPI, indicating that when faculty perceive difficulties in coordinating their work and family roles, their sense of identification, value, and belonging related to their profession may diminish. (2) Mindful teaching plays an indirect role, revealing the intrinsic mechanism of the relationship between WFC and TPI. This suggests that WFC is not only directly associated with TPI, but also linked to lower levels of TPI through its relation to teachers’ ability to recognize and regulate their internal experiences in teaching practice (intrapersonal mindfulness), as well as their capacity for attention, acceptance, and empathy in interactions with students and colleagues (interpersonal mindfulness). (3) WFS plays a conditional role, serving as an important contextual resource that moderates the negative effects of WFC. Specifically, WFS significantly moderates the relationships between WFC and both mindful teaching and TPI. For faculty perceiving higher levels of WFS, the negative associations between WFC and their levels of mindful teaching and TPI are weaker. In other words, adequate organizational and family support helps faculty maintain a more stable teaching mindset and professional beliefs when facing work-family pressure. (4) Indirect and moderating effects exhibit distinct gender patterns. Regarding the indirect effect, in the male teacher sample, WFC was linked to TPI primarily through its negative association with interpersonal mindfulness. In contrast, in the female teacher sample, WFC was linked to TPI mainly through its negative association with intrapersonal mindfulness. This finding suggests that, under the pressure of WFC, the key point at which psychological resources are impaired may differ between male and female faculty. Regarding the moderating effect, the coefficient for the moderating effect of WFS was descriptively higher in the female teacher sample. This suggests that, for female teachers, higher levels of WFS co-occur with a weaker negative WFC-TPI association, indicating that WFS may be an important related factor. Given these observed associations, one may cautiously explore enhancing teachers’ mindfulness and building supportive environments as potential intervention entry points, while considering possible different priorities for male versus female teachers.
