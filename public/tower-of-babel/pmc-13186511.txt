Delayed blast lung injury following a truck tyre explosion in a civilian
Authors: V Ramkishore, Praveen Dubey, Ajoy K. Behera, Manish Kumar, Ranganath Ganga
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Blast lung injury is classically associated with military or industrial explosions; however, similar pulmonary involvement can occur in civilian settings and can be easily overlooked.[1] Although tyre explosions have been reported in civilian trauma, documented cases of delayed and isolated primary blast lung injury without direct thoracic trauma, followed by a rapid recovery without invasive ventilation, have been rarely reported [Table 1].[1234]

A 67-year-old man was exposed to a close-range truck tyre explosion at an estimated distance of 2 m in a semi-enclosed workshop environment, with direct anterior thoracic exposure to the blast wave. The incident resulted in a comminuted fracture of the right tibia and fibula in the absence of penetrating trauma. On admission, he was hemodynamically stable, had no respiratory symptoms, maintained an oxygen saturation (SPO2) of 98% on room air, with a normal chest radiograph [Figure 1a]. Forty-eight hours later, he developed acute onset dyspnoea with hypoxemia. At the time of respiratory deterioration, arterial blood gas analysis revealed a pH of 7.43, PaCO₂ of 37 mmHg, and PaO₂ of 132 mmHg while receiving FiO₂ 0.60 with an expiratory positive airway pressure (EPAP) of 5 cm H₂O, yielding a PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio of 220, consistent with mild acute respiratory distress syndrome. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated a haemoglobin level of 12.8 g/dL and a platelet count of 2.45 × 10⁵/µL, with no subsequent decline on serial measurements. The white blood cell count was 8600/µL and C-reactive protein was 6 mg/L. Serum lipid profile was within normal limits. Neurological examination remained normal throughout hospitalization, with no evidence of altered sensorium or focal deficits, and fundoscopic examination did not reveal retinal haemorrhages or fat globules. No petechial rash was observed. Two-dimensional echocardiography showed preserved left ventricular systolic function with an ejection fraction of 62%, and cardiac biomarkers were normal (troponin I < 0.01 ng/mL and NT-proBNP within normal range). Repeat chest imaging demonstrated bilateral alveolar opacities with ground-glass changes and minimal pleural effusions [Figures 1b and 2]. Lung ultrasound revealed bilateral, patchy B-lines with pleural line irregularity, small subpleural consolidations, and minimal dependent pleural effusions. Computed tomography pulmonary angiography showed diffuse ground glass opacities with bilateral pleural effusion with no evidence of pulmonary thromboembolism and no focal or dependent lobar consolidation.

The patient was managed in the intensive care unit on non-invasive ventilation using bilevel positive airway pressure, a conservative fluid strategy, and diuretic therapy. The initial EPAP was set at 5 cm H₂O, with inspiratory positive airway pressure titrated according to clinical response. Continuous non-invasive ventilation was required for 24 h, followed by successful weaning over the subsequent 24 h. He showed rapid clinical and radiological recovery within 48 h [Figure 1c]. In view of the temporal relationship between blast exposure and the transient nature of lung injury, a diagnosis of blast lung injury with non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema was considered more appropriate. He subsequently underwent definitive orthopaedic fixation and was discharged on room air without residual respiratory symptoms after an 8-day hospital stay.

In the present case, the injury mechanism was likely attributable to rapid decompression from the ruptured truck tyre, producing a transient high-intensity overpressure wave. Unlike military explosive devices, which often generate combined blast, thermal, and penetrating injuries. The lack of external chest injury and the initially normal chest radiograph suggest early subclinical alveolar–capillary membrane disruption at the time of exposure. Delayed hypoxemia at 48 h could indicate a progressive inflammatory response and increased pulmonary vascular permeability after the initial pressure-wave insult, eventually leading to secondary alveolar oedema. The major diagnostic dilemma was the differentiation of fat embolism syndrome (FES), as there was a long bone fracture. Although FES may present incompletely, the absence of neurological deficits, a petechial rash, progressive thrombocytopenia, anaemia, or fundoscopic abnormalities made this diagnosis unlikely. The rapid clinical and radiological recovery within 48 h was also not consistent with the inflammatory course that is generally more prolonged in FES. Cardiogenic pulmonary oedema was excluded based on normal cardiac biomarkers, the absence of heart failure features, and preserved left ventricular function. Pulmonary contusion was ruled out because of the absence of chest wall injury and a diffuse rather than focal radiographic appearance, whereas aspiration and trauma-induced ARDS lacked clinical or radiographic evidence. In the setting of close-range blast exposure, delayed hypoxemia, typical imaging findings, and rapid reversibility with non-invasive ventilatory support collectively indicate primary blast lung injury as the most likely diagnosis. This case highlights that careful observation for 24–72 h following blast exposure is essential, even when initial evaluation appears reassuring.
