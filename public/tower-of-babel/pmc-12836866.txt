Structural marginalization of older adults within health care settings: a concept analysis
Authors: Anna Garnett, Ruheena Sangrar, Christina Oleynikov, David Gerstle, Denise Connelly
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesAgeing populations present new challenges for society, governments and for health systems. To accommodate the global demographic shift towards older populations, healthcare systems preparedness is of paramount importance. To prepare and equip health systems to best support older adults’ health and well-being over the long-term it is necessary to understand how current systems may unintentionally marginalize older adults. Therefore, the purpose of this review and analysis of the literature was to develop an operational definition of structural marginalization of older adults within healthcare settings.Research design and methodsA concept analysis approach, which is a specialized type of literature review used to guide an in-depth examination of a word(s) to understand its importance, application and meaning, was used to review the literature published between January 2000 and December 2024. Eligible articles included those published in English, in peer-reviewed journals or grey literature, with a focus on how older adults experienced structural marginalization within health care settings. Older adults were defined as study participants who were identified as older adults by the study authors, where the lower age limit for inclusion was 60 years or where the study participants or population of interest had an average age of at least 60 years. Studies with participants who had conditions frequently associated with ageing such as dementia were also eligible for inclusion.ResultsThe following defining attributes of structural marginalization of older adults within health care settings were determined through analysis of the 36 included articles: (a) health system actions bias and disadvantage accessibility to care, (b) insufficient organizational structure, composition, activities, and actions undermine older adults’ health, (c) policies fail to protect or support, (d) care is reduced or compromised, and (e) the social determinants of health influence access and experience with healthcare organizations.Discussion and implicationsThe contributors to structural marginalization of older adults within healthcare are complex and multifaceted. Successful resolution of this widespread concern requires a collaborative approach that includes recognition of the issue, targeted education, financial resources, and action towards resolution across all levels within healthcare systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06572-7.

Background

The perception that ageing populations are an extensive and worldwide challenge to be urgently addressed is ubiquitous and well-supported in the literature [1–5]. For instance, the World Health Organization [2] reports a global demographic shift is taking place, with one in six people expected to be over the age of 60 by 2030. Globally, long-range strategies will be paramount to enable governments, societies and health care systems to plan for and successfully respond to this demographic shift towards increasing older adult populations. However, in doing so, care must be taken to avoid problematizing ageing which could lead to ageist actions and policies that could harm a population that may already have multiple vulnerabilities. Health care systems and their associated programs provide valued support to older adults and their families supporting them to age at home, in their communities, or within retirement or long-term care homes [6–8]. Nonetheless, to effectively inform future health system preparedness, it is necessary to understand the structural and systemic contributors to marginalization of older adults across health settings.

Throughout the life course, ongoing access to and use of health care services has been recognized as essential to maintaining health and well-being [9]. As people age their likelihood of developing chronic health conditions increases often necessitating ongoing engagement with health care providers across a range of settings [10]. In addition to disease-specific increases with advancing age, there is also a greater likelihood of having multimorbidity, that is, the co-occurrence of two or more chronic conditions [11, 12]. While people typically receive the highest proportion of health services in their last six months of life, living with chronic disease can also necessitate substantial ongoing engagement with a range of health services [10, 13, 14]. Notwithstanding the fact that ageing is inevitable and the likelihood of developing conditions that impact health and well-being increases with age, there continues to be wide variability in health and the ageing trajectory of older adults [15, 16]. Older adults who receive timely and regular care fare better over time in managing both disease burden and their overall health [17]. Although, factors such as geography, financial status, social networks, education, and other parameters related to the social determinants of health, have substantial bearing on older adults’ ageing experiences [18].

However, the ageing trajectory can also be hampered by ageist attitudes that exist across society impacting older adults’ life experiences and their ability to receive optimal care [19]. An extensive body of literature suggests that ageism is global, widespread, and has increased with time [20]. Historically older adults were esteemed for their experience, wisdom and knowledge but by the start of the Industrial Revolution which, necessitated strength and youth, attitudes towards older adults changed [21]. Coupled with advances in medicine that extended longevity, society began to see older adults as needing to be managed as opposed to valued contributors [22]. Ageism is defined as the stereotypes, preconceptions and actions which serve to discriminate either against or in favour of a segment of a population due to their age [23, 24].

Notwithstanding that ageism can exist across the age span, most research literature focuses on ageism and its impact on older adults [23]. With time, the term ageism has evolved to include both positive and negative stereotypes of older adults. For example, according to the social psychological stereotype content model, older adults are perceived as lacking competence but are considered to be warm and caring individuals [25]. Ageism can also lead to negative behaviours towards older adults such as avoiding them or using condescending language. Moreover, it can impact many aspects of society such as employment and health systems [22, 26, 27]. Of note, older adults themselves may hold ageist beliefs towards other older adults, particularly when they are perceived to be in poorer health than themselves [28]. Many inequities can result from ageism, be it from individual behaviours or at a societal level whereby the structures, namely organizations or collective actions, discriminate against people based on their age [20].

Formal care settings provide a structural example that may unintentionally stigmatize older adults and perpetuate ageist stereotypes. For example, programs within long-term care may institute rules or activities that undermine older adults’ dignity such as moving them to specific levels of care rather than accommodating multiple abilities in one level or by delivering programming that is preferential to those of certain abilities or disabilities [28]. Structural ageism encompasses the actions and behaviours of those employed by institutions, as well as the direct and indirect policies or operations that take place within those institutions, that leads to the inequitable treatment of older adults [20]. Often unintended and underrecognized, structural ageism perpetuates widespread population level discrimination of older adults and has been implicated in increased rates of violence against older adults [29, 30].

A further consequence of ageism is marginalization, defined as the act of reducing a person or group of people to an unvalued or powerless position within either a group or in society [31]. Structural marginalization ensues when denigratory actions or behaviours are attributed to collective structures, organizations and institutions rather than individual people [32]. These structures can also represent economic, political and ideological interests [33]. As such, older adults experience structural marginalization when ageist policies, procedures and operations undermine their safety, autonomy, position and voice. For instance, during COVID-19, ageist attitudes aimed at protecting those deemed most vulnerable led to policies in long-term care that promoted physical and social isolation [34]. While a variety of exemplars of structural marginalization of older adults across multiple settings exists in the literature, we do not have a comprehensive understanding of how to define structural marginalization of older adults as it pertains to their access and use of health care services. Therefore, the purpose of this article is to review the available literature and to apply the concept analysis method outlined by Walker and Avant [35] to develop a working definition of structural marginalization of older adults within health care settings.

Methods

A concept analysis is a methodological approach focused on conducting a literature review for the purpose of an in-depth exploration of a word(s) to determine its significance, to explicate its meaning, and to determine its applications [36]. In the context of a concept analysis, literature is broadly encompassing peer-reviewed journal articles (primary and secondary studies), grey literature, technical texts and other sources of information. This method calls for a stepwise process that entails comparing similar but potentially different concepts obtained through an extensive review of the literature. The resultant definition derived through the concept analysis is bounded by the available literature and the specific contexts of the included texts. The resultant definition can be used to clarify, operationalize and inform understanding of a concept’s application within a given domain, in this case, older adults within health care settings [35]. Much akin to other types of formal literature review strategies, the Walker and Avant [35] concept analysis method provides clear direction and necessitates following a successive series of steps that is transparent and reproducible.

In accord with the method, the following sequential progression through the stages was completed: (a) selecting a concept; (b) deciding on the purpose of the analysis; (completed within background section above) (c) recalling as many uses of the concept as possible; (d) discerning the defining attributes; (e) determining a model case; (f) determining borderline, related, contrary, invented, and illegitimate cases; (g) determining antecedents and consequences; and the penultimate step (h) determining the empirical referents of the concept before arriving at (i) a formal definition of the concept [35]. The model case incorporates all defining attributes of the concept, thus making it the clearest exemplar to fully comprehend the concept [35]. Antecedents must be found prior to the occurrence of the concept while consequences arise as a direct result of the concept. An empirical referent serves to demonstrate the existence of the concept [35]. Within the current article, a decision was made to focus on using the available literature to define the model case, the contrary case and to identify the defining attributes, empirical referents, antecedents and consequences of the concept. Ultimately, a definition of the concept of structural marginalization of older adults within health care settings was determined and will be described.

Rigour of the concept analysis was ensured through multiple actions including, the participation of an academic librarian throughout the project, through a review of the available literature, which was accessed using multiple databases, through the in-depth analysis that clearly explicated uses of the term and through the inclusion of a wide array of exemplars of the concept. In addition, the team involved in the analysis regularly consulted with one another to ensure consensus was reached on aspects such as inclusion criteria, article selection and refinement of the concept of interest. The resultant conceptual definition provides a new contribution to the knowledge base on structural marginalization with potential for operational application [37, 38].

Eligibility criteria

Literature published between 2000 and 2024, available in full-text and published in English were eligible for inclusion. Types of literature considered for the analysis included articles published in peer reviewed journals or grey literature such as published output from policy makers, government offices, commercial organizations and other entities. To accommodate variability within the literature regarding how to numerically define older adult and to maximize article inclusion, the decision was made to include those articles that: identified their participants as older adults, studies whose samples had conditions commonly associated with older ages such as dementia, and those studies where the lower age limit for inclusion was 60 years of age or the study participants had an average age of 60 or older. Eligible articles were also required to include a focus on how older adults experienced structural marginalization within health care settings.

Articles were excluded if they examined other types of marginalization, considered participants below 60 years of age, focused on informal or unpaid care, considered contexts outside of health care or were written in a language other than English. Research protocols and abstracts were also excluded.

Search strategy and study selection

To begin the analysis, it is necessary to identify the various ways in which the concept has been used. As such a combination of resources, including dictionaries as well as the academic literature were used to inform the initial steps of the analysis [35]. To ensure a comprehensive coverage of the academic literature across different databases, initial ad hoc searches were completed to identify synonyms for the primary search terms of structural marginalization, older adult(s), and health service(s). Synonyms for structural marginalization included but were not limited to the various combinations of the following terms: structural, institutional, organizational, and systemic as well as marginalization, bias, inequity, discrimination, oppression, and injustice. An extensive list of synonyms was used to circumscribe health services because many types of care exist and the settings in which care is provided are diverse. Consequently, synonyms for health services included but were not limited to ambulatory care, primary care, acute care, rehabilitation services, telehealth and nursing home (Appendix A).

Next, a comprehensive search of electronic literature databases was conducted in consultation with the academic librarian using the following terms (and associated synonyms): structural marginalization, older adult, and health services to identify articles published between January 2000 and December 2024 which allowed for a fulsome but manageable selection of literature. The databases searched were comprised of Web of Science, Scopus, PsychINFO, Sociological Abstracts, CINAHL, and MEDLINE. Using Covidence review software (Veritas Health Innovation), the title and abstract of each article located through the database searches were assessed by at least two reviewers (AG, CO, DG). Next, the full texts of potentially relevant articles were located and independently reviewed by at least two reviewers (AG, CO, DG) to determine which articles met all inclusion criteria and compiled the final selection of articles to inform the concept analysis. Any discrepancies on article selection were resolved through meeting and discussion among the reviewers until consensus was reached.

Data extraction

The resultant list of articles including reviews, qualitative and mixed methods research articles and expert opinion pieces were reviewed in detail to ensure a focus on structural marginalization among older adults within health care settings and data was independently extracted by two reviewers (AG, CO). Information extracted included: (1) country where article was authored; (2) study design and methods; (3) participants and demographics; (4) results; (5) implications and next steps. Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias assessments were conducted on all included literature using standardized critical appraisal tools outlined by the Joanna Briggs Institute Manual for Evidence Synthesis [39]. At least one reviewer (AG, CO) completed the risk of bias assessment for each article which was checked by a second reviewer (AG). The findings of the quality assessments are summarized in the results, and the full risk of bias assessments are presented in Appendix B. All articles were used to inform the review regardless of their risk of bias assessments to ensure that the concept analysis was informed by an exhaustive and widespread representation of the literature. The risk of bias assessments allows readers to clearly evaluate the methodological quality of the included literature.

Results

Overview of included literature

Based on the review of the literature a final selection of 36 peer-reviewed articles informed the concept analysis on structural marginalization among older adults within health care settings (Fig. 1, Table 1). No grey literature met the inclusion criteria. Countries represented in the literature included the USA (n = 20 articles), Canada (n = 5 articles), the United Kingdom (n = 3), with singular contributions from Australia, China, Germany, Iran, Jordan, the Netherlands, Portugal, and Senegal. Article types included theoretical reviews, conceptual analyses or scientific statements (n = 14), scoping reviews (n = 3), letters to the editor (n = 1), and primary research consisting of qualitative studies (n = 11), quantitative studies (n = 5), and mixed methods studies (n = 2).

Risk of bias in included studies

The complete risk of bias (quality appraisal) of the 36 included studies is presented in Appendix B. Articles were assessed according to their article type: cross-sectional, case control, case report, expert opinion, qualitative, and systematic review. Conceptual analyses and theoretical articles (qualitative) were evaluated using the criteria for qualitative research. Potential bias in the included cross-sectional studies (5/36) related to a lack of clarity regarding the use of reliable and valid measurement tools and uncertainty regarding confounding factors [40–44]. The lone case control study also lacked clarity regarding confounding factors [45]. Sources of bias among case report studies (3/36) included failure to discuss occurrence of adverse events, unclear description of patient history [46–48]. The four expert opinion articles met all JBI risk of bias criteria [49–52]. Among included qualitative studies (18/36) while the majority met most of the reporting of bias assessment standards, shortcomings included researchers failing to theoretically position themselves in relation to their work or to discuss their potential influence on the research [53–69]. Ten studies lacked clarity on whether they met ethical reporting criteria or whether ethics approval was obtained. Of the five included systematic and scoping reviews, all met most of the risk of bias assessment criteria although shortcomings were noted pertaining to reporting a clear research question, article appraisal, and reporting on likelihood of publication bias [70–74].

Uses of structural marginalization

In accord with Walker and Avant [35] it is necessary to first identify the different ways the concept is used prior to circumscribing its definition and associated parameters. Therefore, examples of how structural marginalization has been used both within and beyond research on older adults were identified and described. A sub-type of marginalization, structural marginalization is a term used to indicate the oppression of individuals through the act of having limited economic, political or social status [75]. Early uses of the term can be found in the literature regarding post-colonization and the oppression of others particularly of colour. With time, the term became less connected with racialized marginalization but nevertheless continued to involve the oppression of groups of people by powerful policies and political practices [76]. For example, migrants across the globe without immigrant status, frequently experience structural marginalization through policies that serve to restrict their access to the local domestic services or opportunities [77].

Structural marginalization has been explored in many other settings and populations. For example, targeted initiatives have been developed among certain populations such as, racialized transgender communities, to address widespread structural marginalization of these populations [78]. The term has also been used in efforts to frame and understand how inner-city children’s reduced access to safe and freely accessible play spaces is shaped by the structures and policies within their communities [79]. Other exemplars of structural marginalization exist within the education literature. Here, the term has been used to describe the experience of substitute or casual relief teachers who are excluded from the school community in their role as private contractors with limited power, authority or protection within the educational sector [80]. In other fields of study, such as violence, research suggests that exploration of structural factors impacting violence are in their infancy particularly when compared with studies using other types of data such as geographic or census data [81].

Within the context of health, structural marginalization has been used to describe what has been historically hierarchical and western dominant approaches to delivering health knowledge and care particularly within Third World countries [82, 83]. Acknowledgement of these hegemonic behaviours has highlighted the need for community-based and participatory approaches to health care that empower and foster capacity building within communities [83]. Other historical examples include the widespread institutionalization and structural marginalization of individuals with mental or physical disabilities which continues to impact individuals despite policies and practices aimed at reducing such marginalization [84]. More recently, the COVID-19 pandemic highlighted examples of structural marginalization within health care, whereby those impacted included employees as well as those receiving care [85]. For example, health care workers, particularly within long-term care, were considered essential workers, mandated to work, and as a result were placed at high risk of contracting COVID-19 [86]. At the same time, ageist policies and practices, that in some cases biased care to younger populations, limited access to care for older adults and failed to consider their health and social well-being [87–89].

Actions within health systems bias and disadvantage accessibility

Actions within the health systems bias and disadvantage older adults’ accessibility and use of healthcare services were identified across 21 articles (Table 3). The combination of age and gender has also been documented to disadvantage health care experience for older adults who are members of sexual and gender diverse communities (i.e., 2SLGBTQ2 +) within Canada and the US. These older adults have reported feeling unsupported, negatively treated and discriminated against because of their age and gender across a range of healthcare contexts in the US [90]. Canadian 2SLGBTQ2 + older adults have also expressed uncertainty, concern and fears of being discriminated against, or potentially receiving inadequate or insensitive care through homecare services or in long-term care settings [59].

Qualitative research in Canada on discharge planning from healthcare services demonstrated that health providers held a common belief that as older adults increased in age, their ability to partake in decision making would inevitably decline [46]. This sentiment was also upheld by family members and older adults themselves [46]. Within the context of American Indian Elders, older adults and health providers alike noted limited existence of home care services and long-term care homes [62]. In addition, while study participants experienced positive interactions with the health providers that they were able to engage with, they experienced many procedural challenges such as long wait times, lack of local service provision, issues having their care re-imbursed or lack of long term continuity in health care providers [44, 62].

Various levels of discrimination within the health system particularly regarding ageism and cognitive status served to reinforce inequities and was related to substantial distress for older adults. Moreover, article findings highlight the ubiquity of ageism and the need for systemic changes, including better education and supports for older adults experiencing intersectionality [41, 47, 49, 50, 60, 61]. Lastly, outcomes related to health systems’ biased actions include the tendency of care and quality initiatives to focus on health status rather than on aspects related to equity such race, ethnicity and sexual orientation [68].

Insufficient organizational structure, activities and actions undermine health

The second defining attribute informed by 23 articles suggests that when organizational structure, activities, and actions are insufficient, they negatively impact health care delivery (Table 3). Organizational structural shortcomings can include the physical structure of a building or its contents, which when insufficient, can impact the ability to safely isolate residents, have adequate bed ratios, provide appropriate living conditions, and make it challenging for older adults to hear. It may also involve the availability of equipment and resources, such as personal protective equipment, all of which when insufficient, have been shown to negatively impact care provision to older adults [57, 60, 67, 69, 92].

Activities and actions within health care organizations marginalize older adults in a range of ways. For example, poorer care provision resulting from staff shortfalls are associated with higher rates of avoidable hospitalizations and deaths [45, 53]. Limited services, insufficient training, staffing, prolonged wait times, inadequate translation services, and a lack of culturally safe care, have all been identified as contributors to older adult marginalization particularly in instances when the older adult is also from a marginalized race, gender, or cultural background [56, 59, 63, 66, 68, 71–74, 90]. In addition, actions such as prioritizing safety over older adult autonomy, and stigmatized attitudes by health providers can overtly or inadvertently serve to negatively bias care, discriminate, or exclude older adults from health services [46, 48, 61]. Lastly, omissions in communication, insufficient or a paucity of health provider training and long-standing negative perceptions of older adults’ abilities also contribute to sub-quality care provision to this population [48, 64, 65].

Policies that fail to protect or support

The third defining attribute identified across 10 articles is the reporting of policies that fail to protect or support older adults (Table 3). For example, uncertain, and in some cases exclusionary, and constantly changing policies on funding for health services in the US leave undocumented older adults at greater risk of negative health outcomes [55]. Research conducted in Senegal has highlighted how wide ranging population-based policies intended to improve older adults’ access to healthcare have not necessarily achieved this goal [65]. Administrative policies can fail to accommodate for sensitive information, such as having Acquired Immunodeficiency Virus (AIDS) [58]. While ambiguous implementation of policies can bias care, exclude older adults or have unintended consequences, such as exacerbating social isolation [47, 49, 50, 60, 71]. Additionally, in as much as policies are necessary, failures in updating and reviewing them in response to changing health contexts (e.g., during the COVID-19 pandemic), further renders policy implementation ineffective, perpetuating marginalization of older adults. [57, 69].

Reduced or compromised health care delivery

Reduced or compromised care delivery to older adults encompasses the ways in which older adults’ care can be impacted when structural marginalization exists and constitutes the fourth defining attribute. A total of 24 articles informed this attribute (Table 3). Key among relevant findings is that older adults may experience inequitable access to treatments or preventative measures, and poorer health outcomes which can be exacerbated by the intersection of their age with factors such as race, gender, immigration status and disease condition [44, 50, 53, 55, 56, 58, 59, 70, 73, 90].

Types of compromised care include underdiagnosis, undertreatment both in inpatient and community settings, and reduced and delayed access to care [42, 47, 48, 52, 62, 73]. Qualitative research conducted in Iran suggests that older adults perceive multiple organizational injustices such as lack of geriatric focused services and guidelines, insufficient equipment or geographical spaces and a scarcity of geriatric professionals as detrimental to older adult care [60]. Moreover, older adult care needs are neglected through systemic and population-level knowledge gaps about services available to older adults according to a scoping review conducted in Canada and primary mixed methods research carried out in Senegal [65, 72]. In addition, fundamental aspects of older adult health such as social well-being and the opportunity for autonomy in care decisions such as, when choosing a long-term care home, are often overlooked within the institutions that provide support to older adults [40, 64]. Furthermore, in the worst scenarios, such as during COVID-19, older adults within long-term care homes in particular, experienced neglect, abuse and heightened mortality according to retrospective research conducted in the United Kingdom that examined over 66,000 long-term care home deaths [67].

The social determinants of health influence access and experience with health care

The last defining attribute apprised by 26 articles was the impact of the social determinants of health on health care access and use by older adults (Table 3). While many articles discussed the influence of multiple determinants of health the most prevalent were a) culture and race (including language), b) gender, and c) income and social status. Older adults who identified as being from a racial minority experienced inequality of care options, biases within research and health systems, poorer health outcomes, and a lack of understanding of their cultural values as they related to their care needs [48, 50, 53, 54, 57, 63]. A lack of culturally appropriate services and language barriers were notable as key barriers to older adults who were accessing care across a range of care settings. As a result, some older adults failed to access or receive any services or they used services on an inconsistent basis, particularly in the case of chronic disease management [55, 72]. Similarities in care experience have also been documented among older adults with disease conditions such as HIV or Alzheimer’s disease [56, 61]. Participants in a US qualitative study on ageing and HIV likened the concomitance of ageing and a specific disease to compounding their negative experience with health care [58].

Du Mont, Kosa [71] highlight the complexity of environment within long-term care calling attention to the connection between a previous history of trauma and its relation to an increased likelihood of poor health and victimization among older adults. Furthermore, long-term care homes were found to potentially replicate socio-economic inequalities and discrimination experienced in community settings [64, 68]. Beyond long-term care, socioeconomic status, social vulnerability, and financial constraints were an oft cited contributor to older adults’ failure to access or receive services as well as to the discrimination they experienced [42–44, 65, 66, 69, 73, 91]. In the case of Indigenous populations in the US, cost of services and lack of clarity on insurance coverage deterred them from seeking care beyond their own indigenous providers, undermining their trust in health systems [62].

Model case of structural marginalization of an older adult within a health care setting

The next stage of the Walker and Avant [35] approach for a concept analysis entails developing a model case of the construct of interest encompassing all defining attributes. For the purposes of this analysis, the model case will describe an older adult experiencing structural marginalization, as per all the defining attributes, within a healthcare setting (Table 4). The older adult, who may be impacted by certain social determinants of health (e.g., low-income, racialized, and/or non-binary) experiences biases (e.g., not meeting the criteria for necessary geriatric services) within the health system that disadvantage their ability to receive appropriate care. Within a structural marginalized position, once an older adult has accessed care, they are faced with a lack of policies to support their position as marginalized (e.g., no interpreters or cultural liaisons) and they experience sub-optimal care when compared with dominant segments of society. A range of exemplars were identified for each construct some of which will be briefly presented. Failure to prioritize older adults included a lack of personnel with age specific training, limited data and resources, and a paucity of culturally diverse services [50, 60, 66]. Actions that devalued older adults consisted of a failure to acknowledge sexuality, stigmatizing behaviour based on gender or disease, physical or psychological abuse, and devaluation of culture and language [48, 59, 71, 72, 74, 90]. Older adults’ negative experiences consisted of feelings of abandonment, discrimination, ageism, and perceptions of victim blaming [41, 64, 66, 71]. Policy exemplars include insufficient guidelines, violation of fundamental rights, failure to recognize best interests of older adults, and inadequate policy implementation [49, 60, 64, 69]. Lastly, inequalities that constrained access to health care services consisted of differences in care based on race, gender, social position, and ethnicity, structural barriers such as cost, transportation, geography, technological proficiency or internet availability, poor coordination of services, and limited-service availability [42, 44, 62, 65, 68, 73, 91].

Antecedents

Antecedents are the necessary events or conditions that must precede the occurrence of the concept itself [35]. As such, the following constructs were identified through the current analysis as indicators that would harbinger the subsequent development of structural marginalization of older adults within health care. The antecedents are presented under six domains (Table 7). They include assumptions made around ageing and older adults, such as regarding the capability of an older adult with a dementia diagnosis [61]. Biases and stigma such as those related to ableism, race, culture, language, or migration status were also identified [49, 52, 54, 74]. Underlying structural challenges were determined to precede incidences of structural marginalization of older adults such as poorer quality care provision, lack of staff, and reduced service hours [44, 45, 53, 64]. Health care provider background and prior experience were determined to be precursors that could contribute to structural marginalization, for example, their own experiences and biases impacted the care they provided and sometimes led them to stigmatize older adults [46, 59]. Older adults’ background, identities and prior experiences with health systems also served as antecedents, for example, parameters related to the social determinants of health such as literacy, race, gender, and financial status could impact subsequent experiences with health care service providers [52, 55, 70]. Consequences

The consequences occur as a result of the concept being actualized [35]. In the current analysis, the consequences indicate what may transpire when older adults experience structural marginalization within health care settings. The consequences are presented under six domains and were represented by a range of exemplars in the included literature (Table 8). The first domain includes the actual or perceived burden being placed on older adults, such as, feeling the need to hide an aspect of their identity in the case of gender nonconforming older adults, or being unable to receive sensitive care tailored to their needs [59, 90]. The second domain was exemplified through health care providers behaving in discriminatory ways such as in their communication style or through neglect of home care residents [52, 60, 67, 74]. The third domain suggests that older adults’ interests and values were often disregarded or omitted because of structural marginalization [47, 57, 91]. The fourth domain was exemplified by older adults’ experiences of trauma through discrimination, racism, or being excluded [48, 68]. The fifth domain was characterized by poor health outcomes in older adults such as mortality, avoidable hospitalization, and increased infections [44, 60, 67, 70]. Definition of structural marginalization of an older adult within health care settings

Using the findings of this concept analysis the following operational definition was determined. Structural marginalization of older adults within health care occurs when one or more of the following ensue: health system actions bias and disadvantage accessibility to care; organizations’ structure, composition, activities, and actions undermine older adults’ health; policies fail to protect or support older adults; care is reduced or compromised; and the social determinants of health influence access and experience with health care organizations (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Antecedents, defining attributes, empirical referents, and consequences of structural marginalization of older adults within health care

Discussion

This literature review was conducted using a concept analysis approach outlined by Walker and Avant to establish a working definition of structural marginalization of older adults within health care settings [35]. The review findings provide an important contribution to the discourse on ageism, structural marginalization and its impacts on older adults within a variety of health care contexts. Notably, structural marginalization of older adults within health care frequently was found to involve a confluence of factors including organizational and action-based shortcomings, inequitable access to services, inadequate or unactualized policies and practices and negative care outcomes associated with the social determinants of health [47, 49, 50, 55, 56, 60]. In addition, intersectionality in conjunction with disease presentation were found to exacerbate experiences of structural marginalization by older adults [59, 62, 66, 72]. The development of a concise, evidence-informed definition of structural marginalization of older adults within healthcare will help to support guiding actions to improve care for this population through the identification of key contributors to this issue and highlighting the complexity and need for action across a range of systems (e.g., education, health, employment) and within society.

Health system actions can stigmatize many older adults by devaluing their perspectives, using discriminatory communication, or viewing them as unable to navigate their own care [52, 59, 61]. As a result, older adults are reluctant to seek care even when it is necessary. In some instances, intersections between age and race or ethnicity compound older adults’ negative experiences with health care [54]. Addressing these disparities will require an intersectional lens within research and practice using strategies that are trauma-informed, inclusive and representative of the diversity that exists at the population level [93].

Efforts to support healthy aging, must prioritize social determinants of health. Factors such as socioeconomic status impact older adults’ ability to engage in health supporting activities like physical exercise, access to healthy food, and living in age-friendly environments such as retirement homes. For instance, research has shown the benefits of targeted prevention programmes in Japan where publicly funded exercise programs are provided to improve strength and function of older adults [94]. Healthy aging emphasizes quality of life, but this does not necessarily need to mean total absence of chronic conditions. Collective action that includes governments, health care systems and wider communities will be key in promoting environments that support ageing well [95]. For example, the World Health Organization (WHO) developed the Global Age-Friendly Cities Project in 2006 to unite cities on a global scale to identify community-based strategies to promote age friendly communities. Building on that initiative, in 2007, 10 communities across Canada used the WHO model as a template and developed a guide for age-friendly communities in remote and rural locations [96].

Policy shortfalls were documented at the organizational level but also at the broader societal levels which then impacted older adults’ experience with health care organizations [55, 60, 71]. Social and immigration policies were found to intersect with health care needs to influence older adults’ ability to access health services [55]. For example, undocumented migrants within the U.S. are unable to access health insurance programs offered by federal programs such as the Affordable Care Act leading to some older migrant’s health conditions remaining untreated until they are so sick they have to access emergent care [55]. This policy shortfall emphasizes the need for an overarching; multiple systems approach to address structural marginalization of all older adults. However, progress is challenging and other strains, such as availability of beds in acute care hospitals, can continue to jeopardize older adult care. For example, the More Beds, Better Care Act implemented in Ontario was intended to make more acute beds available within hospitals [97]. The Act has certain provisions for patients who are deemed by a clinician to require an alternate level of care, many of whom are older adults. In such situations, a placement coordinator may oversee and authorize the admission of such patients to a long-term care home without the patient’s consent [97]. Parallels with literature on other marginalized populations such as those with intellectual disabilities highlight how inadequate policies can place already vulnerable populations at greater risk of compromised health or well-being [20, 80, 84]. Strategies to support older adults should emphasize human rights and include global initiatives to address older adult health and well-being such as those outlined by the World Health Organization [98, 99]. In addition, using co-design approaches that include and engage older adults in the development of care models and policy will be important [100, 101]. Additional results of the review highlight the varied challenges older adults experience when trying to access and use health services. Older adults experienced limited-service availability, biases and discrimination in care, and exclusionary practices which in some case fostered distrust among older adults thereby leading to their further avoidance of health services [46, 56, 64, 66, 72]. These findings corroborate a recent systematic review of 75 reports on older adults’ research priorities that emphasized an overwhelming need to make health system changes including the need for tailored health services, improved home and community care, and better access to services together with system navigation [102].

Implications

Critically, approaches to address structural marginalization among older adults must draw from work in other realms and seek to understand why structures became instruments of marginalization, not just how they marginalize [32]. This could also entail a critical examination of the way policies are exclusionary to some segments of the older adult population but not to others. It will also be important to determine whether inadequate policy implementation or lack of collaboration among key stakeholders influences the long-term success of policies intended to improve care delivery [103].

A recurrent theme of insufficiency in the provision of health care services to older adults across the review findings highlights the need for greater societal emphasis to be placed on older adult care, health and well-being. Many older adults are highly motivated to manage their health as they age at home within their communities [104]. Although population aging in general is associated with increases in comorbidity, polypharmacy and greater use of emergent care, this is not inevitable for all individuals [105]. Use of multi-modal strategies that increase efficiency in care such as remote care provision, improving access to a range of health providers, and disease prevention initiatives can be used to support healthy ageing and foster effective chronic disease management among older adults [95].

At the level of health systems, it will be necessary to recognize and respond to population disparities in older adult’s health trajectories with chronic disease. Immigrant and other minority populations face challenges in accessing services, experience disparities in care provision, and have poorer health outcomes [72, 106]. Interventions to address these shortcomings must be prioritized. Strategies such as increasing outreach to vulnerable populations through community-based health clinics that include culturally sensitive care, and the use of multi-lingual system navigators embedded within communities could streamline care and encourage older adults to receive care earlier to avoid crisis situations [107].

Within health systems, attracting and retaining health care workforces must be prioritized. Initiatives that help to raise awareness, further education and research within geriatrics and gerontology, and provide support to those employed within older adult care settings will continue to be extremely important [108–112]. For example, creating opportunities within education to expand dialogue around aging and foster positive attitudes towards older adults while challenging stereotypes is warranted [113]. Approaches that include educating students on collaborative practice and embedding geriatric care within health-related curriculums will help prepare health providers to better support an aging population [114].

To effect long-term success in reducing structural marginalization within healthcare, efforts to address ageism must continue to be a societal priority [113]. Widespread ageist attitudes towards older adults undermine their health and well-being, exclude them from workforces, reduce their social participation and bias the health workers who support them [115]. Collective actions to address negative societal and economic perspectives regarding aging are necessary, as are initiatives to include and recognize the value that older adults bring to society [116].

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of the Walker and Avant [35] concept analysis method include a straightforward stepwise approach that facilitates identification of the central features of the concept that are then used to circumscribe a working definition of the concept. However, the method provides limited direction on searching the literature and does not consider whether certain types of literature could or should have greater merit in shaping the conceptual definition. As such, to strengthen the current analysis, a rigorous approach was used to conduct the literature review (adhering to PRISMA principles where feasible) including direct involvement of an academic librarian on the study team, the use of six academic databases, the inclusion of academic literature, risk of bias assessments, and the implementation of carefully curated search terms to ensure that the resultant literature was applicable to the analysis. The risk of bias assessments provides a snapshot of the overall merit of the literature used to inform the concept of structural marginalization of older adults within health care settings. This enables the reader to consider whether certain attributes or antecedents should be weighed differently according to the quality of the literature that contributed to the formulation of the overall construct of structural marginalization.

Additional strengths of the current analysis are a detailed explication of the identified literature, including the use of an additional summary table to help elucidate each articles’ contribution to the concept analysis. Furthermore, prior to the analysis, the authors sought to clearly describe how the term structural marginalization has been used both within and beyond the domain of older adults and health care.

A limitation of the analysis is the inclusion of English-only literature thereby potentially excluding articles that could inform the culminating working definition. Also, there is inherent subjectivity and potential bias that the researchers bring to the article selection and the analytic process which could nuance the resultant derived definition and impact the reproducibility of the search. To offset this risk, the authors ensured that the proceedings of this analysis were rigorous, transparent, and involved regular consultation and collaboration to help ensure the study’s veracity and replicability. Additionally, the concept analysis approach does not adhere to the same principles as a systematic review and as such interpretation of the findings cannot be used to answer a specific question and interpretation of the findings can change over time particularly if more data becomes available.

Conclusion

The current concept analysis defines structural marginalization of older adults as that which occurs when health systems’ actions bias and disadvantage their accessibility to care, when organizations’ structure, composition, and actions undermine older adults’ health, when policies fail to protect or support them, when their care is reduced or compromised, and lastly, when the social determinants of health impact older adults’ access and experience with health care organizations. The contributors to structural marginalization of older adults within health settings are diverse. Importantly, population level attitudes and biases have a role to play in addition to systemic factors such as funding and targeted resources within health systems. To successfully address this phenomenon, resources and purposeful attention will be required. Future research, informed by this analysis, should include active measures to assess how, why, and quantify to what extent older adults experience structural marginalization across a range of health settings. These research initiatives should include evaluation of health provider and administrative perspectives, recognizing that they too are influenced by wider systemic biases and actions. Affirmative action such as the development of working groups to address structural marginalization must include older adults as active participants recognizing they are ideally positioned to provide clarity and identify potentially overlooked elements of marginalization.
