Application of microsurgical techniques in severe hand injury management: a retrospective analysis of clinical data
Authors: Jian Liu, Gang Zhao, Zhengfeng Lu, Jiandong Zhou, Fei Zhang, Yuzhou Liu, Haoyu Yang, Fei Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe objective of this study is to investigate the application and outcomes of microsurgical nerve and vascular repair, replantation, and reconstruction in the management of severe hand injury.MethodsA retrospective analysis of clinical data was conducted for 103 patients with severe hand injury admitted to our hospital from July 2018 to December 2021. All patients received treatment using microsurgical techniques. The postoperative surgical time, emergency operation blood loss, length of hospital stay, average number of surgeries, early sensory function (assessed at a minimum of 3 months postoperatively), flap appearance, flap survival rate, scar contracture, hand function, and occurrence of postoperative complications were recorded. The improvement in daily activity capacity before and after treatment was compared.ResultsThe average surgical time was 16.98 ± 3.29 h, often reflecting the complexity of concurrent multi-tissue repairs. The average number of surgeries per patient was 2.8 ± 0.9 (range: 1–5), emergency operation blood loss was (97.38 ± 9.85) mL, and the length of hospital stay was (19.87 ± 0.47) days. The excellent rate of early sensory function was 76.70%. The daily activity capacity score (Barthel Index) improved significantly from 46.98 ± 10.32 preoperatively to 73.76 ± 9.87 postoperatively (p < 0.001). The overall flap survival rate was 97.09% (100/103 primary flaps), with a flap appearance satisfaction rate of 97.09% among surviving flaps, and an incidence rate of persistent flap edema of 2.91%. Scar contracture was mild in 47.57% of cases, moderate in 50.49%, and severe in 1.94%. The hand function score (DASH) was (73.28 ± 18.76). The incidence rate of postoperative complications was 26.21%.ConclusionMicrosurgical Techniques are indispensable in the treatment of patients with severe hand injury, facilitating the restoration of early sensory function, daily activity capacity, achieving high flap survival rates and good flap appearance, managing scar contracture, and improving hand function. Despite the complexity and lengthy operative times, these techniques offer a viable path towards functional limb salvage.

Introduction

The intricate anatomy and vital role of the hand in daily activities make severe hand injuries particularly devastating [1].With the development of society and the advancement of mechanization, numerous mechanical devices have been introduced into labor-intensive industries. As a result, hands are constantly exposed to various objects and mechanical tools without sufficient preventive measures. Often, due to physiological reflexes, hands act to shield the body from external hazards, rendering them highly susceptible to injury [2, 3]. Consequently, hands are among the most vulnerable parts of the human body [4]. Moreover, hand tendon injuries are prevalent due to the thin subcutaneous tissue in the hands, lacking effective buffering against external forces. These injuries often result in complex damage to tissues such as nerves, blood vessels, muscles, and bones [5]. Currently, the main treatment methods in clinical practice involve repair, early immobilization, and postoperative functional exercises for traumatic hand injuries [6, 7]. This technique is widely employed in the treatment of various traumas and diseases, including severe hand injury. Surgical management of hand trauma requires specialized knowledge in microsurgery, involving the repair of blood vessels, tendons, nerves, and the coverage of traumatic defects with flaps [8, 9]. Microsurgery plays a significant role in accurately assessing fracture conditions, preserving local blood supply, promoting fracture healing, and expediting hand function recovery. While numerous reports exist on specific microsurgical procedures, comprehensive analyses detailing the spectrum of techniques applied in a large cohort of diverse severe hand injuries and their collective outcomes are less common [10, 11]. Therefore, this study aims to explore the application and outcomes of various microsurgical techniques in the management of severe hand injury within a single center, providing insights into the challenges and successes of treating these complex cases.

Materials and methods

A retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical data of 103 patients with severe hand injury admitted to our hospital from July 2018 to December 2021. The average follow-up period for the cohort was 2.5 ± 1.1 years (range: 6 months to 4 years). Inclusion criteria were as follows: patients who received treatment at our hospital and voluntarily agreed to follow-up; individuals with clear-cut injuries such as lacerations, incised wounds, crush injuries, contusions, and blunt trauma to the hand, often involving multiple tissue types (bone, tendon, nerve, vessel, skin); confirmed by clinical examination and appropriate imaging (e.g., X-ray for fractures, Doppler ultrasound for vascular assessment) to have soft tissue defects; requiring microsurgical repair with vascular and nerve transplantation, and the defect necessitates skin flap coverage and finger reconstruction; presence of indications for limb amputation; good compliance; patients with normal mental and conscious states. “Severe hand injury” was defined as complex trauma involving damage to at least two of the following: major nerves, major vessels, multiple tendons, significant bone loss or comminution, and/or extensive soft tissue defects requiring flap coverage, often falling under classifications such as Gustilo-Anderson type III open fractures or mangled extremity severity scores indicative of high risk for amputation. Exclusion criteria included: Necrosis after replantation leading to eventual amputation (these patients’ initial procedures and outcomes until amputation were analyzed as part of the cohort if they met inclusion criteria, but their final status reflects a failed salvage) and immediate emergency amputation without reimplantation; patients with malignant tumors; hepatic or renal dysfunction; dropouts during the study period; pre-existing disabilities or deformities prior to the injury; congenital finger abnormalities; pregnant or postpartum women; unwillingness to participate in the study; coagulation disorders; failure to attend regular follow-up visits.

Surgical technique

All emergency cases were treated with the application of upper limb plexus nerve block anesthesia and tourniquet. In cases with extensive soft tissue defects requiring free skin flap reconstruction, the procedure was performed under general anesthesia. The surgical area was disinfected, thoroughly debrided, and fracture reduction was achieved through internal fixation. Muscle and tendon suturing, as well as flap and soft tissue repairs, were performed. Microscopic assistance was utilized for suture repair of severed blood vessels. In cases requiring vascular reconstruction, arborizing vein transplantation was performed. For superficial arch destruction of the palm, a preoperative forearm radial artery perforator flap with accompanying venous network transplantation was employed for the defect. In cases of vascular insufficiency in the fingers, at least one arterial blood supply was restored to each finger, while a portion of adipose tissue was preserved during the harvesting of the transplant vein. The proximal end was anastomosed to the ulnar artery or radial artery, and the transplanted vein was sutured and fixed to prevent arterial tortuosity after congestion. Nerve suturing was performed using an extramural technique under tension-free conditions. Nerve repair methods were selected based on the extent of nerve damage (defect > 2 mm), with autologous sural nerve transplantation used for repairs. Hemostasis, suturing, and other procedures were completed after the repair. For soft tissue defects, the first choice included reverse pedicle island flaps and pedicle flaps from the abdomen to repair the defects and cover the vascular bed. For extensive defects, free flaps were utilized, such as anterolateral thigh (ALT) flaps or radial forearm flaps. For segmental destruction that cannot be replanted in situ, we chose to reconstruct or replant the finger segments to restore partial hand function. For completely destroyed limb segments or tissue blocks that cannot be replanted in an emergency, multi-tissue transplantation repair was performed within 72 h after emergency debridement. After the emergency repair was successfully completed, secondary surgeries were performed for bone grafting, tendon nerve release, flap debulking, scar revision, and hand function reconstruction.

Postoperative management included hand splinting (e.g., functional hand splints or custom thermoplastic splints appropriate to the injury) external fixation, antibiotic prophylaxis against infection, and starting active or passive limb function training for patients after 2 weeks to prevent tendon adhesion. Massage therapy was employed to promote nerve repair. Sensory function training was guided postoperatively based on the type of injury. Sensory function training was initiated in the fourth week for partial nerve lacerations or contusions, and in the sixth week for complete nerve lacerations. The training process involved examining areas of reduced sensation in the hand, assessing sensory function before training, conducting cold and hot sensation training, and engaging in repetitive eye-opening and closing exercises to rebuild the sensory information processing system. Training sessions were conducted 2–3 times daily, each lasting 15 min.

Outcome measures

The following patient information was collected: age, gender, length of hospital stay, cause of injury, intraoperative blood loss, surgical time, length of hospitalization, tendon zone of injury, hand distribution, associated injuries, associated fractures, and complications.

Early sensory function and daily activity ability assessments were conducted, and flap appearance, scar contracture, hand function, and postoperative complications were analyzed.

Early sensory function assessment criteria [12] (assessed at a minimum of 3 months postoperatively): Sensory function recovery in the hand was classified into five grades (S1-S5). S1: No sensation; S2: Deep pain sensation in a single nerve distribution area; S3: Superficial pain and touch sensation in a single nerve distribution area; S4: Superimposed loss of sensation, with both superficial pain and touch sensation present but overlapping; S5: Recovery of two-point discrimination in the distribution area. The sensory function was categorized into four levels: Excellent (graded as S4 or S5), Good (S3), Fair (S2), and Poor (S1). The rate of excellent and good outcomes was calculated.

Daily activity ability assessment: The Barthel Index [13] was used to determine functional status. Scores ranging from 0 to 100 were categorized as follows: ≥60 points indicated basic self-care ability; 40–60 points indicated moderate functional impairment, requiring assistance in daily activities; 20–40 points indicated severe functional impairment; and < 20 points indicated complete disability. Scores were recorded preoperatively and at the final follow-up.

Flap appearance: The color, texture, and edema of the flap were observed, and the incidence of persistent edema was calculated. Flap survival was defined as complete viability of the flap without need for further surgical intervention for necrosis.

Scar contracture [14]: Mild: Scar was normal or barely noticeable, with a small area limited to one anatomical site; Moderate: Scar was more noticeable, with a moderate area involving two anatomical sites; Severe: Scar covered a wide area, involving more than two anatomical sites.

Hand function: Hand function was assessed using the Disability of Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) questionnaire [15]. The questionnaire consisted of 34 items, including activities such as driving, sweeping, opening doors, and writing. The scoring system was as follows: A (items 1–23), B (items 24–30), and C (items 31–34), with scores ranging from 1 to 5. Thumb function was evaluated based on the DASH score. The DASH score was calculated using the formula: DASH score = (A + B − 30) / 1.2, with a total score of 100. A score of 0 indicated normal function, while a score of 100 indicated complete loss of function. The scores were categorized into five levels: 20–30, 40–50, 50–60, 60–70, and > 70.

Complications: The occurrence of postoperative complications including flap-related issues (e.g., partial or total necrosis, venous congestion), infection, tendon adhesion, joint stiffness, and re-rupture of repaired structures was recorded and analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were analyzed using the independent samples t-test for comparisons between groups or paired t-test for pre-post comparisons within the same group. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using appropriate non-parametric tests (e.g., Mann-Whitney U test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test). Categorical data were presented as numbers (n) and percentages (%) and analyzed using the chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for detecting differences.

Clinical data of patients

Machine injuries were the most common cause of injury (n = 61, 59.22%), followed by traffic accidents (n = 35, 33.98%) and explosion injuries (n = 7, 6.80%). Emergency primary reconstruction surgery was performed in 75 patients (72.82%), subacute reconstruction (within 1–3 weeks) in 28 patients (27.18%), and secondary/functional reconstruction surgery in 56 patients (54.37%), with many patients undergoing multiple procedures (average 2.8 ± 0.9 surgeries per patient). Vascular transplants were done in 45 patients (43.69%), and nerve transplants in 18 patients (17.48%). In transplantation, a single tissue type was involved in 21 procedures (20.39% of patients requiring transplantation), two or more tissues in 11 procedures (10.68%), and three or more tissues in 2 procedures (1.94%). Second-stage bone grafting for bone defects not in continuity was done in 15 patients (14.56%). The average surgical time for the primary reconstructive procedure was 16.98 ± 3.29 h, reflecting the complexity of these injuries often involving multiple system repairs (bone, tendon, nerve, vessel, and soft tissue coverage) in a single setting. For instance, cases involving free flap transfer, multiple digital replantations, or extensive neurovascular reconstruction contributed to these longer durations.

Preoperative assessment of 103 patients with limb destruction and tissue separation guided the surgical strategy. Emergency in-situ repair using microsurgical techniques was prioritized. For severely damaged digits where replantation was not feasible, options included using parts as free tissue transfer (e.g., “spare parts” surgery) or combining with flap reconstruction to restore function to other digits. Two patients underwent repositioning of remaining digits onto the wrist, restoring partial gripping function. In cases of severe hand degloving injuries where degloved tissues retained some integrity, microsurgical exploration often identified vessels suitable for anastomosis, permitting replantation attempts. We report one successful replantation in a total hand degloving injury. In-situ replantation was attempted in 12 cases of thumb and 2–5 finger degloving injuries, of which 9 were successful. If primary repair was not feasible, subacute reconstruction using skin flaps or toe-to-hand transfer was considered. Most injuries in this study were crush or twist injuries, with damaged or detached limbs often partially intact. After debridement, microscopic dissection identified repairable blood vessels and nerves in the tissue block, and bone damage was evaluated. Restoration of the hand’s original integrity through bone scaffolding reconstruction was foundational for emergency limb preservation. For vascular injury repair, forearm vein grafts were commonly used. For palmar superficial arch damage, forearm dendritic vein transplantation was employed. In cases of finger vascular insufficiency, at least one arterial supply was restored per finger. During transplant vein harvesting, a portion of adipose tissue was preserved. The proximal end was anastomosed to the ulnar or radial artery, and the transplanted vein was sutured and fixed to prevent kinking. For severe hand soft tissue defects, flap coverage after anastomosis was essential. While free flaps (e.g., anterolateral thigh flap for a palm defect in one emergency case) offer optimal coverage, their demands in terms of technique, manpower, and surgical time led us to more frequently use regional pedicled flaps in emergencies. Forearm perforator pedicled reversed island flaps were a primary choice, with the posterior interosseous artery perforator flap for dorsal defects and the radial artery wrist perforator pedicled flap for palmar ulnar-side defects. These flaps have relatively stable perforators, are easier to harvest, and can be rotated quickly by experienced surgeons, often under brachial plexus anesthesia, which is better tolerated by patients. Vascular transplantation and flap harvesting were generally performed on the affected limb. Sixteen patients presented with total hand crush injuries, characterized by severe multi-tissue damage but without large soft tissue defects. These primarily resulted from crushing or twisting, with severe tissue damage but good integrity. Emergency internal fixation of palm and finger fractures restored bone scaffold stability. One-stage repair of blood vessels, nerves, and tendons was performed, with forearm superficial vein transplantation for vascular injuries. All 16 of these limb salvage attempts were successful. Three patients had non-continuous bone defects requiring second-stage iliac bone grafting; 6 underwent tendon and nerve release, and 2 had thumb-to-palm function reconstruction. The most frequent presentation (25 patients) involved wrist, palm, and finger separation combined with soft tissue defects. Replantation was attempted in all 25; 11 underwent vascular transplantation, and 5 with large soft tissue defects received emergency pedicle flap coverage. Four patients experienced postoperative vascular crises but survived after re-exploration. Eighteen patients underwent second-stage procedures including bone grafting, tendon/nerve release or transplantation, flap trimming, and hand function reconstruction (details in Table 2).

Sensory function, barthel index, flap outcomes, hand function, and complications

Early sensory function (S1-S5 distribution) showed an excellence rate (S4 or S5) of 76.70% (n = 79). The Barthel Index score significantly improved from a preoperative mean of 46.98 ± 10.32 to a postoperative mean of 73.76 ± 9.87 (paired t-test, p < 0.001). The overall primary flap survival rate was 97.09% (100 out of 103 primary flaps). Three primary flaps (2.91%) failed due to vascular compromise leading to total necrosis. Among the surviving flaps, the appearance was rated as good in 97.09% (100/103 patients, as some patients may have had flap success but other issues, or this refers to the 100 successful flaps), with a low incidence of persistent flap bloating or significant edema (2.91%, n = 3). Scar contracture was mild in 47.57% (n = 49), moderate in 50.49% (n = 52), and severe in 1.94% (n = 2). Hand function, measured by the DASH score, averaged 73.28 ± 18.76 (where 0 is no disability and 100 is severe disability). Postoperative complications occurred in 26.21% (n = 27) of patients, detailed in Table 3.

Discussion

Due to the thin skin and minimal subcutaneous tissue in the hand, there is a lack of effective cushioning during injury, resulting in extensive skin defects, skin necrosis, or avulsion [16]. The main characteristics of severe hand injury include complex damage to multiple tissue types such as degloving of the palm, finger, and back skin, multiple fractures in the hand and palm, and contusion of the tendons and small muscles. There may also be tissue rupture or defects in the digital arteries and the arch of the palm, making these injuries challenging to manage and associated with a high clinical incidence [17, 18]. Improper management can lead to a high rate of disability, making hand preservation a challenge for clinicians. Relevant studies have indicated that besides its own functions such as support and pressing, the palm plays a coordinating role in finger function. Therefore, it is crucial to repair palm defects as much as possible after injury.

In cases of severe hand injury, after bone reconstruction, exposed structures like bones, tendons, and neurovascular bundles become a challenge to treat. The key to successful treatment involves covering the soft tissues robustly, utilizing salvageable avulsed tissues where possible, and replanting relatively intact but non-vascularized fingers. Reconstructing the soft tissues of the palm and back of the hand ensures that the preserved or reconstructed fingers receive adequate vascular and neural coverage, promoting the survival and restoration of sensory function in the affected fingers [19]. It has been noted that due to the severe damage to the palm tissues in cases of degloving, palm flap transplantation is necessaryoptions, particularly when free flap expertise or resources are limited. For larger defects on the back of the hand, the reversed island flap of the dorsal interosseous artery should be considered first, while for defects on the palm side, the ulnar artery wrist perforator flap is preferred. The radial forearm perforator flap is a commonly used technique for repairing soft tissue defects. The posterior interosseous artery (PIA) flap in the forearm sends out 5–13 perforators, nourishing the skin on the dorsal side of the forearm. The proximal part of the flap has more perforators, ranging from 3 to 9, with an average of 5.2, which are long and large. The distal part has 2–5 smaller perforators, with an average of 3.8. The PIA flap in the forearm sends out 2–5 intermuscular or muscle-cutaneous branches in the upper-middle part of the forearm, nourishing the skin. The thickest perforator branch is located at a distance of (9.6 ± 3.2) cm from the lateral epicondyle of the humerus and has an outer diameter of (0.6 ± 0.1) cm at the branching point. The advantages of the PIA perforator flap include a long and consistent vascular pedicle, a simple surgical procedure, no damage to the major arteries in the forearm, minimal damage to the donor area, and a texture similar to that of the hand’s skin [20]. It has been widely used for repairing soft tissue defects in the hand. In this study, we also employed various perforator flaps, including the PIA perforator flap for the repair of severe hand injury. Previous studies have shown that using the forearm flap for the repair of deep forearm and hand wounds in elderly patients resulted in a flap operation time of 75 min, minimal surgical trauma, low postoperative complications, and a significant preservation of limb function [21]. The results of our study showed that the surgical time for patients with severe hand injury was 16.98 ± 3.29 h, intraoperative blood loss was (97.38 ± 9.85) mL, and the length of hospital stay was (19.87 ± 0.47) days. The prolonged surgical time in our series is attributable to the severity of the injuries, often requiring multiple concomitant procedures such as complex fracture fixation, multiple tendon repairs, nerve grafting, and intricate vascular anastomoses for replantation or free tissue transfer, rather than isolated flap coverage.

The ulnar artery wrist perforator flap exhibits consistent perforation positioning, originating approximately (3.7 ± 0.6) cm above the pisiform bone from the ulnar artery. With a vascular pedicle length of (1.2 ± 0.2) cm and an initial outer diameter of (1.3 ± 0.1) mm, it represents the thickest among all ulnar artery perforator branches. Few branches are absent or thin, ensuring reliable blood supply to the flap. The pedicled flap procedure is relatively straightforward, requiring no microsurgical vascular anastomosis if used as a pedicled island flap rather than a free flap, thereby reducing surgical complexity and significantly shortening operation time for that specific component of reconstruction. Additionally, due to the thinness of the forearm tissues, secondary thinning procedures are mostly unnecessary postoperatively, resulting in favorable cosmetic outcomes in certain scenarios. The anterolateral thigh flap features relatively consistent anatomy, a large harvesting area, spares the main vascular trunk, and offers a concealed donor site [22]. It can be flexibly designed according to the shape of the recipient site and the type of perforation, providing unique advantages for repairing irregular hand wounds [23, 24]. Severe injuries to the palm primarily result from compression or crushing, leading to extensive damage to bones, joints, blood vessels, nerves, tendons, and soft tissues. The disrupted bone support structure and segmental bone and soft tissue defects pose challenges. Key considerations for preserving limbs in severe injuries include: (1) reconstructing the bone support structure to maintain hand morphology; (2) repairing and restoring blood vessels to reestablish distal finger blood flow; and (3) effectively covering the vascular bed with soft tissues. Reconstructing the bone support structure is fundamental; however, severe bone destruction often accompanies significant soft tissue injuries, precluding limb preservation in some instances. In such cases, partial hand function can sometimes only be restored through techniques such as pollicization, toe-to-hand transfers, or complete replantation of an intact digit or forearm segmentoften correlates with preserving function, emphasizing the importance of maintaining hand function in cases of severe hand injury. Therefore, we advocate avoiding amputation whenever possible and prioritizing restoration of hand function. The repair of severely damaged hands presents a significant challenge, requiring the collaboration of surgeons with extensive microsurgical experience. The successful repair and preservation of hand function are of immense importance, as current technological developments such as bionic and prosthetic hands are not yet capable of fully replacing the sensory and functional capabilities of the human handor different healthcare settings. Thirdly, the study focused on a specific time period and while follow-up was conducted, the heterogeneity in follow-up duration for individual patients may not capture the very long-term outcomes and complications associated with microsurgical techniques in the management of severe hand injury. Additionally, the study lacked a control group for comparison, which could have provided further insights into the effectiveness of the treatment approach against alternative strategies, although establishing such a control for these unique injuries is ethically and practically challenging. Fourthly, detailed data on individual flap success rates beyond overall survival, such as specific rates of partial necrosis for different flap types, were not uniformly available for all patients. Finally, the study primarily relied on clinical data and subjective assessments for some outcomes (e.g., flap appearance, patient satisfaction in typical cases), which may be influenced by individual variations and subjective biases. Furthermore, the definition of “severe hand injury,” while outlined, encompasses a broad spectrum of injuries, and future studies might benefit from more granular sub-categorization. These limitations should be taken into consideration when interpreting the results of this study and future research should aim to address these limitations to further enhance our understanding of the application of microsurgical techniques in the management of severe hand injury.

Conclusion

Microsurgical techniques are indispensable in the contemporary management of severe hand injuries. This study demonstrates their utility in achieving viable limb salvage, restoring early sensory function, improving daily activity capacity, and attaining satisfactory flap outcomes with high survival rates. Despite the inherent complexities, prolonged operative times, and potential for complications, a dedicated microsurgical approach allows for functional reconstruction that can significantly enhance patients’ quality of life. Continued refinement of techniques and patient-specific strategies are crucial for optimizing outcomes in this challenging patient population.
