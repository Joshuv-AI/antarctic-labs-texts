Metaverse and Immersive Technologies: Redefining the Future of Healthcare: A Scoping Review
Authors: Mohamadreza Maleki, Hassan Reyhani Khouzani, Ali Zackery, Salime Ghoharinejhad, Hamzeh Zarei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The rapid emergence of the metaverse, defined as an integrated and persistent three-dimensional virtual ecosystem that incorporates immersive enabling technologies such as virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), and mixed reality (MR) alongside artificial intelligence (AI), blockchain, and the Internet of Medical Things (IoMT), is redefining opportunities in healthcare provision, medical education, and patient engagement. Extending beyond telemedicine, it offers transformative potential for personalized, equitable, and sustainable medical services. This scoping review synthesizes current evidence on applications, benefits, barriers, and strategies for integrating immersive technologies into healthcare.Methods:Following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 guidelines, a comprehensive search was conducted in June 2024 across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, ProQuest, and DOAJ using Medical Subject Headings, Embase Tree terms, and free-text keywords, combined with Boolean operators and manual reference checks. Eligible publications included empirical studies, systematic and scoping reviews, meta-analyses, conceptual frameworks, and implementation reports, published from 1994 onward in English. Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts. Data were extracted using a structured form and thematically synthesized to map applications, benefits, and implementation barriers of metaverse technologies in healthcare.Results:From 3,542 records, 50 studies met the inclusion criteria. Evidence indicates that metaverse technologies can enhance healthcare provision via immersive telepresence consultations, AI-driven decision support, blockchain-enabled secure data handling, and integration with wearable devices. In education, VR, AR, and MR improved anatomical comprehension, procedural accuracy, clinical reasoning, and learner confidence. In patient care and rehabilitation, immersive tools reduced pain, enhanced neurorehabilitation and post-stroke recovery, lowered pre-operative anxiety, and improved chronic disease management and geriatric wellbeing. Barriers to adoption included high infrastructure costs, interoperability gaps, unequal technology access, and ethical issues concerning privacy, consent, and equity. Proposed solutions emphasized open technical standards, infrastructure investment, culturally adaptive design, and integration within hybrid care models.Conclusions:The metaverse is moving from conceptual innovation to practical application, showing measurable value across healthcare delivery, education, and patient care. Sustainable, equitable integration will depend on overcoming technical, economic, and ethical challenges while embedding immersive technologies into existing health systems to complement, rather than replace, physical care.

Introduction

Since the 1990s, innovations such as telemedicine and electronic health records (EHRs) have redefined how healthcare is delivered, recorded, and coordinated, enabling faster diagnostics, broader access, and more efficient care pathways.[1] Yet the next disruptive wave in digital health is emerging with unprecedented speed: the metaverse, a persistent, shared, three-dimensional virtual ecosystem, enriched by virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), mixed reality (MR), artificial intelligence (AI), blockchain, and the Internet of Medical Things (IoMT).[234] This immersive environment is not a mere extension of telemedicine: it constitutes a paradigm shift toward individually tailored, equitable, and sustainable medical services.[56]

The metaverse’s potential in healthcare provision is multifaceted. Immersive platforms allow clinicians to conduct real-time telepresence consultations, integrate patient-generated wearable data, and employ predictive analytics to guide personalized interventions.[78] Implementation cases, such as the Esantem Smart Healthcare System, demonstrate improved operational efficiency, streamlined decision-making, and enhanced patient satisfaction through metaverse-enabled workflows.[9] The secure handling of sensitive health data, frequently cited as a critical barrier, is addressed in experimental integrations of explainable AI and blockchain, which establish transparent decision-making while safeguarding privacy.[1011]

Healthcare education stands to gain equally transformative benefits. Metaverse-based simulations create risk-free, high-fidelity training environments, enabling medical students and professionals to rehearse complex procedures repeatedly without patient risk. While not all included studies investigated fully realized metaverse environments, the reviewed VR, AR, and MR applications represent foundational technological layers upon which contemporary healthcare metaverse systems are being developed.[1213] Systematic reviews confirm that immersive VR and AR improve anatomical understanding, procedural accuracy, clinical confidence, and long-term retention.[141516] These modalities have proven effective not only in surgery,[17] but also in nursing education,[1819] emergency medicine simulations,[20] diagnostic imaging training,[21] and interprofessional collaboration.[13]

For direct patient care, immersive technologies have documented positive impacts in chronic pain management,[22] neurorehabilitation,[23] post-stroke recovery,[2425] anxiety reduction in orthopedic surgery,[26] therapeutic education for chronic diseases,[27] and geriatric wellbeing in long-term care.[28] Beyond specific interventions, metaverse environments offer opportunities for empathetic, continuous clinician–patient engagement[29] and accessible public health outreach capable of bridging geographical and socio-economic divides.[3031]

Yet, despite the breadth of its promise, widespread adoption is constrained by several interlocking barriers. Technical obstacles include latency, lack of interoperability, and limited bandwidth in low-resource settings.[32] Economic hurdles remain, with high costs for VR/AR hardware, maintenance, and specialist training.[33] Ethical and legal challenges-especially around informed consent, equity of access, and data governance-are recurrent concerns in the literature.[34] There are also educational challenges, as inexperienced users may require substantial orientation before engaging effectively in metaverse healthcare scenarios.[35]

Addressing these barriers requires multi-sector strategies: regulatory frameworks ensuring safety and privacy, equitable infrastructure investment, reimbursement models incentivizing adoption, and continuous professional development for clinicians and educators.[3637] Crucially, the metaverse should be positioned as a complement, not a replacement, to traditional care delivery-enhancing, rather than supplanting, existing patient–provider relationships.[34]

The purpose of this scoping review is to systematically map and synthesize the current body of evidence on the application of metaverse and immersive technologies in healthcare. Specifically, the review aims to: (1) examine how metaverse-oriented platforms and immersive technologies enhance healthcare provision; (2) identify practical applications in medical education, clinical training, rehabilitation, and direct patient care; and (3) analyze the key technical, ethical, and organizational barriers to implementation, along with proposed strategies to address them. By addressing these objectives, this review seeks to clarify the scope, maturity, and future potential of metaverse-enabled healthcare ecosystems.

Methods

Study design

This review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines.[38] The complete PRISMA checklist is available in Appendix 1.

Search strategy and information sources

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with established methodological guidance for evidence synthesis in health research and followed the PRISMA 2020 framework. The electronic search was initiated in June 2024 and performed across five major bibliographic databases with complementary coverage of biomedical, clinical, and technological literature: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, ProQuest, and DOAJ. These databases were selected to ensure broad retrieval of peer-reviewed publications addressing both healthcare and immersive digital technologies.

To achieve a comprehensive yet focused search, we employed a structured, two-stage strategy. First, controlled vocabulary terms-Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) in PubMed and EMTREE terms in Scopus-were combined with relevant free-text keywords to capture both indexed and non-indexed records. Second, Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used to systematically link conceptual domains related to the metaverse, immersive technologies, and healthcare applications.

A complete search strategy was developed and pilot-tested in PubMed, combining synonymous and closely related terms as follows:

(“Metaverse” [Title/Abstract] OR “virtual worlds” [Title/Abstract] OR “virtual reality” [MeSH Terms] OR “virtual reality” [Title/Abstract] OR “augmented reality” [MeSH Terms] OR “augmented reality” [Title/Abstract] OR “mixed reality” [Title/Abstract])

AND

(“Health Care” [MeSH Terms] OR healthcare [Title/Abstract] OR health [Title/Abstract] OR medicine [Title/Abstract] OR “medical education” [MeSH Terms] OR rehabilitation [Title/Abstract] OR telemedicine [MeSH Terms])*

Searches were limited to studies published in English between January 1994 and June 2024, reflecting the early adoption and subsequent evolution of immersive technologies in healthcare. No restrictions were applied regarding study design in order to maximize sensitivity, consistent with the exploratory objectives of a scoping review. Equivalent search strategies, adapted to the syntax and indexing systems of each database, were subsequently applied in Scopus, Web of Science, ProQuest, and DOAJ [Appendix 2].

To further enhance completeness, backward reference list screening and selective forward citation tracking were conducted for all included studies. All retrieved records were imported into a reference management system, where duplicates were removed prior to the title- and abstract-screening process.

Although engineering-focused databases such as IEEE Xplore index a substantial body of technical research on IoMT, blockchain, and AI architectures, this scoping review prioritized biomedical and interdisciplinary databases to capture studies with explicit healthcare application, clinical relevance, and implementation context. As a result, highly technical system-level papers without direct clinical, educational, or patient-centered evaluation were considered outside the primary scope of this review.

Inclusion Criteria

Studies were included based on the following predefined criteria:

Publication Date: Articles published from January 1994 onwards, corresponding to the early adoption of digital health technologies, to encompass the full emergence of VR, AR, and MR in healthcare.

Language: Studies written in English or translated into English with an accompanying English abstract.

Topical Relevance: Studies were eligible if they explicitly examined healthcare applications of the metaverse or immersive technologies (VR, AR, MR) that either operationalized metaverse-like features (e.g., persistent virtual environments, multi-user interaction, avatar-based presence) or were conceptually positioned in the literature as foundational or enabling components of metaverse healthcare systems.

Study Type: Eligible designs included original research (quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-methods), systematic or scoping reviews, meta-analyses, conceptual/theoretical frameworks, applied/implementation studies, and feasibility assessments.

Exclusion criteria

Articles were excluded if they:

Lacked healthcare relevance (e.g., metaverse applications for entertainment or gaming unrelated to health).

Did not provide full-text access after reasonable retrieval attempts, including interlibrary requests.

Lacked a transparent methodology or had insufficient description to assess study rigor.

Were non-original publications, such as abstracts, editorials, commentaries without data, clinical case reports without analytical context, conference summaries, or non-peer-reviewed industry reports.

They were written in languages other than English without a translated version or English abstract.

Selection criteria

The selection process followed a multi-stage filtration protocol to ensure methodological transparency:

De-duplication: All records retrieved from the databases were imported into EndNote (Clarivate Analytics) and automatically screened to identify and remove duplicate entries.

Title and Abstract Screening: Two independent reviewers (blinded to each other’s decisions) screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining studies against the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Full-Text Review: Studies deemed potentially eligible proceeded to full-text assessment by both reviewers.

Consensus and Adjudication: Discrepancies in reviewer decisions were resolved through discussion. In cases where consensus could not be reached, a third senior reviewer acted as arbiter.

Final Inclusion: Only studies meeting all inclusion criteria were retained for data extraction and qualitative synthesis.

This rigorous selection process ensured that the final body of evidence comprised exclusively high-quality, thematically relevant studies. The screening process is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (presented in the methods section) and resulted in 50 studies forming the analytic dataset for this review.

Data extraction

The extraction of data was done with the help of an extraction table developed in Excel software. The table presents the main characteristics of the studies included in it and their principal findings. The details of the studies incorporated would include the year of publication, the country in which the study was conducted, the methodology followed, and the principal findings of the studies. This approach enabled the effective and coherent analysis of the metaverse in healthcare.[39]

Assessment of methodological quality

Consistent with the objectives of a scoping review and in accordance with PRISMA guidance, no formal critical appraisal or quality scoring tool was applied. The primary aim of this review was to map the breadth, characteristics, and applications of metaverse and immersive technologies in healthcare, rather than to evaluate intervention effectiveness or economic outcomes. Methodological rigor was instead ensured through transparent eligibility criteria, independent screening by multiple reviewers, structured data extraction, and comprehensive reporting of study characteristics.[40]

The database search identified 3,542 records across PubMed (n = 440), Web of Science (n = 1,240), Scopus (n = 1,247), and ProQuest (n = 615). After removing 1,939 duplicates, 1,603 records remained for title and abstract screening, of which 1,362 were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. Full texts were sought for 241 potentially eligible reports; 166 could not be retrieved despite reasonable attempts. Seventy-five full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 25 were excluded with reasons (scoping reviews, n = 5; systematic reviews, n = 2; narrative or conceptual reviews without original data, n =6; non-peer-reviewed books or chapters, n = 4; letters, editorials, or opinion pieces, n = 3; insufficient methodological transparency, n = 5). After the final screening stage, 50 studies met all inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the qualitative synthesis. These included a diverse range of study designs such as systematic reviews, meta-analyses, experimental interventions, qualitative research, conceptual frameworks, and implementation case studies, providing a broad evidence base for analyzing the applications, benefits, and barriers of the metaverse and immersive technologies in healthcare.

Applications in healthcare provision and systems innovation

Evidence from the included studies indicates that the metaverse is emerging as a transformative layer in healthcare provision, capable of reconfiguring service delivery models, enhancing operational efficiency, and increasing patient engagement across diverse domains and settings. Applied implementation research provides some of the strongest current evidence: for example, Dilibal and Tur (2022) described the deployment of the Esantem Smart Healthcare System, showing that a virtual-environment-enabled service could reduce bottlenecks in clinical workflows, shorten care delivery times, and provide patients with interactive services in real time.[9] Similar technology-integration work from Ali et al. (2023) and Sowan et al. (2025) demonstrated that coupling metaverse platforms with explainable AI and blockchain can simultaneously improve user immersion and address one of the most cited adoption barriers- secure, transparent handling of sensitive health data. Both studies underline that verifiable, auditable decision-making processes and immutable data records are not peripheral benefits but structural requirements for sustainable adoption.[1011]

Narrative and conceptual analyses by Ahuja et al. (2023), Diaconu (2024), and Wang et al. (2022) extend these findings by outlining how immersive telepresence, unified interoperability between clinical systems, AI-linked wearable data streams, and remote monitoring could be configured into predictive, personalized care pathways.[2846] Mittal et al. (2024)[5] went further in conceptualizing multi-layered metaverse hubs that interlink remote diagnostics with synchronous, team-based clinical collaboration. These visions are supported by technology-mapping reviews such as Wearable Patient Monitoring and Metaverse-enabled Healthcare Systems (Pera, 2022) that emphasize the synergistic advantage of combining sensor-rich wearables with immersive platforms.[7]

Despite these opportunities, both consensus-based expert work by Yang et al. (2022) and practical clinical analyses by Namazi et al. (2025)[26] highlight three recurring constraints that stand in the way of scale-up: the lack of interoperable standards across platforms, the prohibitive upfront and maintenance costs of immersive hardware, and limitations in broadband capacity, challenges that are particularly acute in rural and resource-limited environments.[3] Addressing these limitations, conceptual frameworks proposed by Massetti and Chiariello (2023), and reinforced in broader surveys of adoption strategy (Chengoden et al., 2023),[36] position the metaverse not as a wholesale replacement for in-person interactions but as a systematically integrated hybrid environment: a care ecosystem in which immersive spaces handle monitoring, follow-up, and multidisciplinary meetings, while sensitive, high-stakes consultations remain in the physical domain.[4] Across these works, there is notable convergence on the principle that successful integration requires a standards-based infrastructure, robust cybersecurity, and an explicit alignment with existing care pathways.

Medical and health professions education

Evidence from the included studies provides robust support for the transformative role of metaverse and immersive technologies in medical and health professions education. High-quality meta-analytical work by Moro et al. (2017)[15] on immersive VR and AR in anatomy education demonstrated medium to large positive effect sizes on knowledge acquisition and retention. In contrast, the systematic review by Kovoor et al. (2021)[17] confirmed that augmented reality in surgical education improves technical skill acquisition and reduces procedural errors. Nursing education has been a particularly active field for immersive integration: the umbrella review by De Gagne et al. (2023)[18] and the systematic review by Evgenikos et al. (2025) found that mixed reality creates more engaging, hands-on learning environments without exposing patients to risk, while fostering interactivity and simulation fidelity.[19]

Direct patient care, rehabilitation, and therapeutic interventions

The evidence base from the included studies demonstrates that metaverse and immersive technologies are being actively deployed across a range of direct patient care and therapeutic contexts, yielding consistent improvements in clinical outcomes, patient engagement, and quality of life. A meta-analysis of VR interventions for chronic pain management[22] confirmed significant and clinically meaningful reductions in pain intensity, coupled with high patient acceptance, supporting VR as an effective adjunct to conventional analgesic strategies. In neurorehabilitation, a synthesis of published literature[23] emphasized that VR has matured beyond a research novelty, with mounting evidence for its utility in improving motor recovery, cognitive function, and patient motivation in neurological disorders. A systematic review and meta-analysis of virtual, augmented, and mixed reality (VAMR) therapies for stroke rehabilitation[24]found moderate-to-large effect sizes for upper limb functional recovery and activities of daily living, demonstrating strong therapeutic potential when integrated into multidisciplinary rehabilitation programs. Complementary experimental work in elderly post-stroke patients[25] further validated VR as a modality that enhances both motor and cognitive recovery, with participants reporting higher engagement compared to traditional therapy.

Beyond neurological conditions, immersive modalities have been employed to address procedural anxiety and psychological distress. A systematic review of VR and AR for orthopedic patients and providers[26] revealed their capacity to substantially reduce preoperative anxiety and improve overall patient comfort, with ancillary benefits for clinician focus and intraoperative efficiency. In chronic disease self-management, comparative experimental research in therapeutic education for diabetes[27] showed that mixed reality, particularly with color passthrough, produced higher engagement, better knowledge retention, and stronger patient satisfaction than both augmented reality and traditional instructional formats. Geriatric wellbeing applications are also emerging: a systematic review of VR interventions for older adults in long-term care[28] reported improvements in both physical health metrics and psychological wellbeing, underscoring immersive environments’ capacity to enhance quality of life in institutional settings. Furthermore, conceptual reviews highlight the role of the metaverse in enabling sustained clinician–patient interaction and empathetic communication,[29] as well as expanding access to health education and outreach across geographic and socioeconomic barriers.[3031]

Collectively, these high-quality studies, subjected to appraisal via the adapted Drummond checklist, indicate that immersive technologies can deliver measurable benefits in direct patient care when implemented with methodological rigor and in alignment with patient needs. Across domains-from pain management and neurorehabilitation to anxiety reduction, chronic disease education, and geriatric engagement-the most successful interventions combined immersive realism, interactivity, and structured feedback loops. Persistent challenges, however, include ensuring equitable access, addressing cost and infrastructure constraints, and integrating these tools into existing care pathways without disrupting the provider–patient relationship.

Public health applications and community engagement

Evidence from the included studies highlights the potential of the metaverse to enhance public health systems, expand outreach, and promote active community participation. Conceptual analyses such as Harnessing public health with “metaverse” technology[30] emphasize that immersive platforms can deliver health promotion messages, prevention strategies, and educational content in more engaging, interactive formats, reaching geographically dispersed or underserved populations. Similarly, the metaverse, the built environment, and public health: Opportunities and uncertainties[31] explores how virtual environments can simulate healthy urban design, model environmental health interventions, and enable citizen-driven participatory planning, while cautioning against emerging risks such as digital exclusion and health misinformation. Communication-focused perspectives like How the Metaverse is Shaping the Future of Healthcare Communication[29] examine its dual potential-improving transparency, bidirectional dialogue, and patient engagement on one hand, but also introducing possible new barriers to trust and understanding if implemented without accessibility and cultural adaptation measures.

Supporting the scale and scope of this application area, the bibliometric analysis by Wu et al.[39] documents a rapid acceleration in global metaverse-health research output, with distinct publication clusters emerging around public health engagement, rehabilitation, and clinical education. Review studies on broader healthcare applications[3650] further reinforce that, when designed inclusively, metaverse environments can act as extensions of public health infrastructure, enabling real-time community data gathering, gamified health promotion, and equitable service delivery at scale. Collectively, these findings from diverse methodological approaches-conceptual reviews, thematic analyses, and bibliometric mapping-support positioning the metaverse as an adjunct public health tool that can bridge geographic and socio-economic divides, provided that equitable access, privacy protection, and culturally responsive design remain central to its deployment.

Cross-cutting barriers and ethical considerations

Despite the breadth of promise demonstrated across application areas, convergent evidence from the included studies indicates that recurring technical, economic, ethical, and educational obstacles continue to constrain large-scale adoption of metaverse technologies in healthcare. Technical limitations are prominently noted in the expert consensus by Yang et al. (2022)[3] and the thematic analyses of Namazi et al. (2024),[26] who identify device latency, limited interoperability between platforms, and inadequate broadband infrastructure in low-resource or rural settings as consistently disruptive to seamless service delivery. In parallel, A revolution in health: Opportunities and challenges of the Metaverse by Massetti (2023)[4] and system-level reviews such as Exploring the potential of metaverse technology in healthcare[36] underscore the economic burden posed by high acquisition costs for VR/AR hardware, ongoing maintenance expenditures, and the specialist training needed to operate and support these systems.

Ethical and legal concerns emerge as another strong thematic strand. Works such as Massetti and Chiariello (2023)[4] and Marzaleh et al. (2022)[34] highlight the difficulty of ensuring informed consent within fully immersive environments, the heightened risks to patient data privacy given the volume and granularity of behavioral and biometric data collected, and the imperative to promote equity of access so as not to exacerbate existing healthcare disparities. These viewpoints are echoed in broader syntheses, such as “Navigating the Healthcare Metaverse”[37] and “Development of the Metaverse for Intelligent Healthcare,”[46] both of which emphasize the importance of formal governance frameworks, technical safeguards, and culturally sensitive design in sustaining public trust.

Educational and user-adaptation challenges also surface across multiple reports. The two-decade mapping exercise by Popov et al. (2024)[35] and analysis from Exploring augmented reality integration in diagnostic imaging[21] demonstrate that clinicians, educators, and patients unfamiliar with immersive modalities require structured onboarding and continuous support to use these environments effectively, particularly when clinical stakes are high. Without this preparatory investment, even technically sound deployments risk underutilization.

Proposed mitigation strategies in the included literature consistently emphasize multi-sector coordination. Yang et al. (2022)[3] and Massetti and Chiariello (2023)[4] advocate regulatory harmonization and the adoption of open standards to overcome interoperability barriers. Infrastructure enhancement through public–private partnerships is highlighted in Wang et al. (2022)[46] as crucial to building equitable access. Critically, several expert consensus outputs, including Yang et al. (2022),[3] stress that the metaverse must be framed as a complement to - not a replacement for - traditional care models, preserving the provider–patient relationship while enhancing its reach and depth through immersive augmentation.

Interpretation of overall evidence

The synthesis of evidence from the 50 included studies confirms that the metaverse-encompassing VR, AR, MR, and their integration with emerging tools such as AI, blockchain, and wearable IoMT devices, has reached a stage of demonstrable utility across multiple domains of healthcare. Across implementation research, such as the Esantem Smart Healthcare System,[9] and applied technology integrations combining explainable AI and blockchain,[1011] immersive platforms have demonstrated the ability to streamline clinical workflows, enhance operational efficiency, improve patient satisfaction, and safeguard the transparency and security of sensitive health data. Conceptual and technology-mapping reviews[23578] extend these findings, outlining predictive and personalized care pathways that are achievable through immersive telepresence, interoperable clinical systems, and wearable-linked remote monitoring.

The educational evidence base is remarkably mature, with systematic reviews and meta-analyses in anatomy,[1415] surgical training,[17] and nursing education[1819] reporting significant improvements in knowledge retention, procedural accuracy, technical proficiency, and learner engagement compared to traditional pedagogies. Large-scale literature mappings[35] and feasibility studies[16] confirm that these gains are transferable to real-world clinical contexts, while maintaining high learner acceptability.

In direct patient care and rehabilitation, high-quality quantitative syntheses highlight robust clinical benefits. Meta-analyses and systematic reviews demonstrate that VR interventions can significantly reduce chronic pain,[22] improve motor and cognitive recovery in neurorehabilitation,[23] and produce moderate-to-significant functional gains in VAMR-assisted stroke rehabilitation.[2425] Additional applications include reducing procedural anxiety in orthopedic patients,[26] enhancing engagement and knowledge retention in chronic disease education,[27] and improving quality of life for older adults in long-term care.[28] Conceptual works[293031] extend this to the relational domain, emphasizing the potential for continuous, empathetic patient–provider engagement and geographically equitable health education delivery.

Public health applications are emerging as a distinct thematic cluster. Perspectives and conceptual reviews[293031] describe how immersive platforms can deliver health promotion content, simulate healthy environments, and foster citizen-driven planning, while bibliometric analyses[39] confirm rapid research growth and topic clustering around public health engagement, rehabilitation, and education. Systematic reviews on broader healthcare deployment[3650] reinforce the view that, when designed inclusively with attention to cultural and accessibility considerations, metaverse environments can enhance public health infrastructure through scalable, interactive service delivery.

These benefits, however, are moderated by persistent cross-cutting constraints. Technical limitations-including latency, bandwidth deficits in low-resource settings, and lack of interoperability- are highlighted in expert consensus and application-level analyses.[326] Economic barriers, such as hardware acquisition, maintenance costs, and training requirements, are identified in broad thematic reviews.[43436] Ethical challenges, particularly regarding immersive informed consent, privacy, and equity of access, recur across both conceptual and implementation-level literature.[4343746] Educational adaptation issues persist, with evidence from mapping and exploratory studies,[2135] showing that both clinicians and patients require structured onboarding and skill development to maximize benefits.

Across methodological approaches-from controlled interventions and meta-analyses to implementation case studies, conceptual frameworks, and bibliometric mappings-the collective evidence positions the metaverse not as a wholesale replacement for traditional healthcare but as a hybrid complement that can expand reach, augment training, enhance care coordination, and enrich patient engagement. The common success factors identified include immersive realism, interactivity, structured feedback mechanisms, and integration with existing clinical and educational infrastructures. The sustainability of these advances will hinge on addressing the interconnected technical, economic, ethical, and educational barriers through regulatory harmonization,[34] open standards, equitable infrastructure investment,[46] and inclusive design that preserves and strengthens the core provider–patient relationship. Discussion

An important finding of this scoping review is that much of the current empirical evidence labeled under the “healthcare metaverse” originates from studies examining VR, AR, and MR technologies implemented in clinical or educational contexts. Although not all of these studies evaluated fully integrated, persistent metaverse environments, they constitute the technological substrate upon which metaverse healthcare ecosystems are emerging. Accordingly, this review adopts an inclusive, evolutionary interpretation of the metaverse, capturing both mature metaverse platforms and their foundational immersive predecessors.

This scoping review synthesized evidence from 50 peer-reviewed studies to comprehensively map how the metaverse and immersive technologies are being conceptualized, implemented, and evaluated within contemporary healthcare. By systematically addressing three central research questions-(RQ1) the enhancement of healthcare provision, (RQ2) practical application in education and patient care, and (RQ3) barriers and potential solutions-we have identified a coherent but still emergent evidence base that spans experimental interventions, systematic reviews, bibliometric analyses, conceptual modeling, and real-world case studies.

Across diverse methodological designs, the metaverse is consistently portrayed as a transformative infrastructure layer able to reshape healthcare delivery models. Implementation-level research, such as the Esantem Smart Healthcare System,[9] demonstrated measurable efficiencies in workflow and real-time patient engagement. The applied integration of explainable AI and blockchain[1011]enhanced transparency, algorithmic accountability, and secure data stewardship features highlighted as foundational for sustained adoption. Conceptual and narrative syntheses[2457846] converged on the potential of immersive telepresence, interoperable wearable-linked data feeds, and predictive analytics to enable personalized, proactive models of care. This convergence reinforces broader digital transformation literature that positions interoperability and trust as equally critical to clinical safety and patient adoption as the immersive experience itself. However, our mapping also revealed a notable imbalance: while simulated and conceptual depictions of intelligent, hybrid care ecosystems are abundant, empirical evaluations in operational, diverse health settings remain scarce. The few real-world trials demonstrate feasibility and potential impact, but replication in different socio-technical contexts is minimal, limiting generalizability.

In health professions education, systematic reviews and meta-analyses[1415171819] consistently support the superiority of VR/AR/MR simulations over traditional modalities for improving anatomical understanding, procedural precision, clinical decision-making, and learner confidence. Nursing and interprofessional education benefited particularly from mixed reality’s capacity to provide interactive, scenario-rich environments without exposing patients to risk. Intervention studies[131644] reinforce these findings with quantitative data on performance improvements and error reduction, while scoping reviews[2034] demonstrate that such approaches are applicable across specialties, including emergency medicine and diagnostic imaging[21].

In direct patient care and therapeutic interventions, quantitative syntheses provide robust signals of clinical benefit. VR-based treatments demonstrate significant reductions in chronic pain,[22] improved neurorehabilitation outcomes,[23] moderate-to-large functional recovery in stroke rehabilitation,[2425] and reductions in anxiety for surgical patients.[26] More novel applications extend to therapeutic education for chronic diseases[27] and improved quality of life for elderly patients in long-term care.[28] Conceptual perspectives[293031] further highlight the metaverse’s potential for sustained clinician–patient engagement and scalable health education across geographic barriers. Yet, translation from trial to routine practice is impeded by scalability constraints, cost of hardware, and the need for integration into existing workflows without fragmenting the patient–provider relationship. None of the included intervention studies directly compared immersive interventions against high-fidelity non-immersive alternatives across multiple health systems-a gap that future research could address to isolate the added value of immersion itself.

Recurring barriers to adoption were identified across methodologies. Technical constraints-latency, non-standardized platform architectures, and inadequate broadband in low-resource areas-were prominent in expert consensus and applied evaluations.[326] Economic barriers related to acquisition, maintenance, and specialized training were noted in narrative and survey-based analyses.[43436] Ethical challenges, particularly regarding immersive informed consent, equity of access, and the granularity of health data collected, were persistent concerns.[4343746] Educational adaptation issues emerged in mapping exercises,[2135] reinforcing that without structured onboarding and continuous support, even technically robust deployments risk under-utilization. Proposed solutions across the literature emphasized multi-sector cooperation. Recommendations include: regulatory harmonization and open interoperability standards[34]; public–private investment in broadband and hardware access[46]; integration of cultural and accessibility considerations into design[2930313650]; and treating the metaverse as a complement to, not a replacement for, physical care models.[3446] These align with global digital health policy priorities, suggesting that lessons learned here may be transferable beyond the metaverse to other emerging health technologies.

In summary, this scoping review reveals a vibrant but still maturing field. The metaverse is no longer a speculative concept in health; tangible applications are demonstrating measurable value in education, patient care, and public health engagement. However, without parallel investment in interoperability, economic accessibility, ethical safeguards, and user readiness, these advancements risk remaining confined to pilot projects and well-resourced settings. By treating immersive technologies as an integrated, complementary modality within the broader healthcare ecosystem, stakeholders can harness their potential to create more personalized, equitable, and sustainable models of care.

Strengths and limitations of the evidence base

The body of included studies is methodologically diverse, with a healthy proportion of systematic syntheses and controlled interventions that provide higher confidence in reported outcomes. However, variation in study quality, small sample sizes in many experimental designs, and a predominance of short-term evaluations limit our ability to infer long-term effectiveness or cost-efficiency. Conceptual and perspective pieces, while valuable for theory building, often lack empirical substantiation. The geographic distribution is skewed toward high-income settings, leaving uncertainties about feasibility and adoption in low- and middle-income countries.

This review has several limitations. First, although the literature search encompassed major biomedical and interdisciplinary databases, engineering-focused repositories such as IEEE Xplore were not systematically searched. Consequently, some highly technical studies addressing underlying IoMT, blockchain, or AI system architectures may not have been captured. However, the objective of this review was to synthesize evidence on healthcare-relevant applications and implementation-oriented insights rather than low-level system design. Future reviews could benefit from integrating engineering databases to provide a more comprehensive technical perspective.

Implications for policy, practice, and research

For policy-makers, the findings underscore the need to integrate immersive health strategies into broader e-health and telehealth planning, ensuring supporting infrastructure, standards, and reimbursement frameworks. For educators and clinicians, the evidence supports embedding immersive modules into curricula and clinical pathways where risk-free skill acquisition or sustained engagement is a priority. Industry stakeholders can draw on identified enablers-such as blockchain for secure data exchange and adaptive MR interfaces-to design platforms aligned with health system needs.

Future research suggestions

Future research priorities include large-scale, diverse-setting implementation trials; longitudinal impact studies on patient outcomes, professional skills, and health equity; economic evaluations that incorporate societal cost-benefit perspectives; exploration of environmental impacts from hardware production and platform energy use; and trans-cultural adaptation studies to identify and mitigate value-based adoption barriers.

Despite the rapid growth of interest in metaverse-enabled healthcare, several critical research gaps remain. First, future studies should prioritize large-scale, longitudinal implementation trials to assess sustained clinical effectiveness, user adoption, and system integration beyond short-term pilot projects. Second, economic evaluations incorporating cost-effectiveness, cost-utility, and societal return on investment are needed to support evidence-based policy and reimbursement decisions. Third, there is a notable lack of research from low- and middle-income countries, highlighting the need for context-sensitive studies addressing infrastructure constraints and health equity. Fourth, future research should explore interoperability standards, cybersecurity frameworks, and governance models, particularly for integrating AI, blockchain, and IoMT within metaverse healthcare ecosystems. Finally, human factors and behavioral research is required to better understand clinician and patient acceptance, ethical concerns, workload implications, and long-term impacts on the provider–patient relationship.

Conclusions

This scoping review set out to chart, with methodological breadth and depth, how the metaverse and immersive technologies are reshaping healthcare provision, advancing medical and health professions education, and redefining patient engagement. By systematically mapping and synthesizing evidence from 50 high-quality studies spanning implementation research, systematic reviews, quantitative trials, conceptual modeling, and bibliometric analyses, we achieved a comprehensive overview that addresses not only what is currently possible but also what is probable in the near future.

The review demonstrates that immersive platforms-when integrated with enabling technologies such as AI, blockchain, and wearable IoMT devices-are no longer peripheral novelties but are emerging as functional components of healthcare ecosystems. Across education, they provide risk-free, high-fidelity learning environments that measurably improve skill acquisition, confidence, and knowledge retention. In patient care, they enhance chronic disease management, rehabilitation outcomes, procedural preparation, and quality of life. At the same time, in public health, they offer new modalities for outreach, engagement, and participatory planning, often bridging geographical and socio-economic divides.

At the same time, this review acknowledges that the global adoption of such technologies is contingent on overcoming persistent challenges: technical barriers related to interoperability and bandwidth, economic constraints associated with hardware and training, ethical imperatives of privacy and equity, and behavioral obstacles to user adoption. The most promising mitigation strategies-evidenced across the included literature-emphasize interoperable, standards-based infrastructures; multi-stakeholder governance; public–private investment in equitable access; culturally responsive design; and treating the metaverse as a complement to, rather than a replacement for, physical care.

By synthesizing disparate strands of evidence into a coherent map of applications, benefits, barriers, and solutions, this review delivers on its objectives: to inform policy formulation, to guide implementation strategies, and to set an agenda for future research. The implications are clear. If harnessed strategically, the metaverse can be a backbone for more personalized, participatory, and sustainable healthcare systems worldwide. Realizing that potential will require coordinated effort: scaling from controlled trials to health-system integration, conducting longitudinal and cost–benefit evaluations, and continually aligning technological innovation with the ethical and human imperatives of care.

In sum, the metaverse in healthcare is no longer a speculative frontier-it is an active, evolving landscape. The work now is to ensure that its growth is directed, equitable, and grounded in rigorous evidence, so its transformative promise becomes a reality accessible to all.
