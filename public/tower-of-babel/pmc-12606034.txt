Understanding Menopause: Knowledge and Influencing Factors Among Postmenopausal Women in Bench Sheko Zone, Southwest Ethiopia: Cross‐Sectional Study Design
Authors: Nigusie Shifera, Tewodros Yosef, Smegnew Gichew Wondie, Alemseged Aydiko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsMenopause is the cessation of menstruation and reproductive capacity due to decreased ovarian function and hormone production. Menopausal symptoms affect about 70% of women during their menopausal period. Despite the increasing proportion of the population experiencing menopause, only a limited number of studies have been conducted to understand menopause among women, particularly in Ethiopia. Therefore, this study aims to assess knowledge and influencing factors among postmenopausal women in the Bench Sheko Zone, Southwest Ethiopia.MethodsA community‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted among 404 post‐menopausal women in the Bench‐Sheko Zone from August to September 2023. Simple random sampling was used to obtain the required sample size. The data was collected by using structured, pre‐tested, and interviewer‐administered questionnaires. Epi data version 3.1 was used to enter data and then exported to Statistical Package for Social Science version 25 for analysis. The binary Logistic regression model was used to select candidate variables, and only variables with a p‐value < 0.25 in bivariate logistic regression analysis were checked for association in a multivariable logistic regression model, and variables with a p‐value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.ResultThe overall level of knowledge about menopause among post‐menopausal women was 24.8%. Factors associated with knowledge status included the educational status of college and above (AOR = 4.23; 95% CI = 1.74, 10.24), educational level of secondary school (AOR = 3.87; 95% CI = 1.58, 9.48), educational level of primary school (AOR = 3.29; 95% CI = 1.33, 8.13), being a government employee (AOR = 5.56; 95% CI = 2.31, 13.4), and history of contraceptive use (AOR = 2.83; 95% CI = 1.15, 6.94).ConclusionPost‐menopausal women had low knowledge about menopause. Educational status, occupation, and use of contraception were significantly associated with knowledge about menopause. Further menopause education is recommended to empower women to increase health‐seeking behavior and access to information and healthcare services.Keywords: Bench‐Sheko Zone, knowledge, menopause

Introduction

Menopause is the cessation of menstruation and reproductive capacity as a result of the decrease in ovarian function and hormone production mainly, steroids and peptides. It can be a natural physiological process that occurs as a part of a female's normal aging process. Worldwide, the natural age of menopause is 45–55 years on average, being 51 years for developed countries, while it is inconsistent for developing nations [1]. Menopause has two phases. As defined by the Stages of Reproductive Aging Workshop (STRAW) the perimenopause, which literally means about or around the menopause, it begins at the same time as the menopausal transition and ends 1 year after the final menstrual period [2]. during Perimenopause biological, physical, and clinical features of reaching menopause commence, and a woman may experience irregular cycles and hormonal fluctuations with normal fertility [3, 4]. The post‐menopausal period is determined after a time of 12 months of spontaneous amenorrhea has been observed [5].

Women experience different symptoms at menopause because of the menopausal event (absence of ovarian activity) or due to the aging process. These can be manifested as physical, somatic, vasomotor, sexual, or psychological symptoms [1]. The physical manifestations of menopause include tiredness, generalized body pain, internal heat, joint pain, sweating, back pain, restlessness, dryness of skin, and shrinking of the body while psychologically, they will have mood swings, unhappiness, irritability, headache, anxiety, sleep disorders as well as difficulty in concentrating and forgetfulness. Vasomotor symptoms mainly comprise hot flushes and sweating whereas the sexual symptoms include reduced desire, vaginal dryness, decreased pleasure, and painful penetration [1, 6, 7]

Menopausal symptoms affect about 70% of women in their menopausal period, lasting not only the menopausal transition period from the peri‐menopausal to the post‐menopausal period, but may continue for the rest of their lives [8]. These symptoms, beyond causing great distress and disability to the women, bring additional threats to the health resources of the country. Women's quality of life is significantly affected by menopausal‐associated symptoms like hot flashes, sleep problems, mood changes, and overall sense of well‐being. As a result, the physical, mental, emotional, and social well‐being becomes highly affected [9].

It was estimated that worldwide, the number of women in the menopausal period is expected to rise from 467 million to 1200 million by 2030, of which 76% would likely be in developing countries. It is also likely that the nearly 250 million menopausal women will double by the 2020 s, which accounts for about 5%–8% of the total population, and is increasing at a rate of 2%–3.5% in developing countries, which is non‐negligible and a great concern [6, 10]. In Ethiopia, the proportion of menopausal women; with an age range between 30 and 49 years is 16% with the percentage of menopausal women increasing with age, as 6% among the age group of 30–34 years to 49% in women with age 48–49 years where the proportion of post‐menopausal mothers with age above 45 years was about 6.5% [11].

Despite this increase in the proportion of the population in the menopausal period, most women at this age have poor knowledge, and even healthcare providers do not have a great intention to create awareness about menopause and related issues like self‐care. For example, in Ethiopia, only 22% of menopausal women know about menopause [6]. Women have minimal knowledge about menopause due to a lack of information and limited access to self‐care resources. Even those who recognize menopausal changes often don't know how to manage them or the health implications associated with menopause [12, 13]. Additionally, both women and healthcare professionals lack awareness of hormone replacement therapy and self‐care techniques for alleviating symptoms [14].

Although there have been limited studies on women's understanding of menopause [15, 16, 17, 18] Most of them have not been large‐scale, lacking involvement from multiple health facilities and a focus on diverse demographic areas and sociocultural contexts. Therefore, this study aimed to assess women's knowledge about women's menopause and the factors affecting it by addressing the above limitation. Understanding knowledge about menopausal symptoms enables women to improve their lifestyle and health‐seeking behaviors appropriate for minimizing their effects on daily life activities. Moreover, this study will provide valuable information for the planners, administrators, and other stakeholders in designing possible programs and interventions, amending the strategy, and taking corrective measures to improve the knowledge of menopausal symptoms for realizing sustainable development goals.

General Objective

The general objective of this study was to assess knowledge and influencing factors among postmenopausal women in the Bench Sheko Zone, Southwest Ethiopia.

Specific Objective

To assess women's knowledge about menopause in the Bench‐Sheko Zone, Southwest Ethiopia.

To identify factors associated with women's knowledge of menopause in the Bench‐Sheko Zone, Southwest Ethiopia.

Methods

Study Area and Period

The study was conducted from August 1 to September 30, 2023, in the Bench‐Sheko Zone found in Southwest Ethiopia. It is located about 561 km southwest of Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. Bench‐Sheko zone has six districts and two city administrations, with the capital city of Mizan‐Aman. Bench‐Sheko zone has a total population of 609,588, of which 302,356 were males and 307,232 were females, with an expected number of 124,405 households in the zone. The majority of the zonal population resides in rural areas, accounting for approximately 0.49% of Ethiopia's total population.

Study Design

A community‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted.

Study Population

All post‐menopausal women in the Bench‐Sheko Zone who fulfilled the eligibility criteria during the study period.

Study Units

Post‐menopausal women in the selected households of the randomly selected district or city administration in the Bench‐Sheko Zone.

Eligibility Criteria

Women who are above the age of 45 years and were amenorrheic for the last year and menstruated previously. Menopause is a result of medical or surgical interventions or pathological processes. Menopausal women who are unable to communicate, and Women who had never menstruated, were excluded from this study.

Study Variables

Knowledge about menopausal symptoms was the dependent variable. The independent variables included socio‐demographic factors (age, marital status, educational status, occupation, and monthly income), reproductive health‐related factors (age at menarche, parity, contraceptive use, and history of abortion), menopause‐related factors (age at menopause), and health‐seeking behaviors (consulting a healthcare provider and engaging in regular physical exercise).

Operational Definitions

Menopausal women: a woman aged 45 years and in the post‐menopausal period.

Post‐menopausal: A woman after a time of 12 months of spontaneous amenorrhea, provided that other primary amenorrhea and artificial menopause are excluded [19].

Natural menopause: spontaneous stopping of menstrual bleeding for consecutive 12 months due to aging and natural reduction in ovarian function, but not as a result of medical or surgical intervention or other pathological conditions or both [11].

Primary amenorrhea: the absence of menses by age 13 years in the absence of normal growth or secondary sexual development; or the absence of menses by age 15 years in the setting of normal growth and secondary sexual development [6].

Premature menopause: menopause before the age of 40 years, and is mainly caused by secondary causes like hormonal imbalance or even natural causes [7].

Artificial menopause: menopause is brought about by the deliberate removal of any organ that directly affects the estrogen production process and menstrual process, including medical or surgical interventions or both [6, 11].

Knowledge about menopause includes understanding its definition, causes, possible symptoms and manifestations, potential consequences, and measures to alleviate these symptoms. It will be assessed using a set of 19 questions. Each question has a yes/no option, with each correct response awarded one mark. Finally, the level of knowledge will be determined by calculating the mean score of the 19 items. Participants scoring equal to or above the mean will be categorized as having good knowledge, while those scoring below the mean will be categorized as having poor knowledge.

Kebele is the smallest administrative unit in Ethiopia.

Sample Size Determination

The sample size was determined using a single population proportion based on the following assumptions: a 95% confidence interval (CI), a 5% margin of error, and a prevalence of 21% from a study conducted in Adama town, Oromia regional state, Central Ethiopia [20]. After accounting for a 10% nonresponse rate and a design effect (D) of 1.5 (Due to the multistage nature of the study design), the final sample size became 421.

Sampling Procedure

The study area comprised six districts and two town administrations: Sheko, Semen Benchi, Shey Benchi, Gedi Benchi, Gura Ferda, Debub Benchi, Siz Town, and Mizan Aman Town. Two districts and one town administration were selected by lottery (37.5%). A preliminary census was then conducted in the selected areas to identify households with menopausal women. Eligible households were registered using a household listing form and assigned a unique code.

The census identified 1029 menopausal women, of whom 1006 met the eligibility criteria. Thirteen individuals were excluded based on the predefined exclusion criteria. The total sample size was proportionally allocated across the selected sites. Systematic random sampling was used to select study participants, with the first household in each area chosen by lottery. One was randomly selected for data collection in households with multiple eligible women.

Data Collection Tools and Procedure

A structured interviewer‐administered questionnaire was developed from standard tools and adapted from a review of different literature. First, the tool was prepared in English, translated to Amharic, and then retranslated to English. During difficulties created in language, oral translation into the local language was done. Four data collectors and two supervisors were recruited to collect the data. Before the data collection process, 2 days of training were given to all data collectors to fill out the questionnaire appropriately. The questionnaires included information on socio‐demographic characteristics, reproductive health‐related factors, and knowledge‐assessing questions. The respondents’ knowledge was assessed using a 19‐item questionnaire containing questions in which each correct response was given a score of 1 and a wrong response was given a score of 0.

Before the data collection, the questionnaire was pretested on 5% of the total sample size, 21 participants in the Debub Bench district. The data was collected through a home‐to‐home visit with interviewer‐administered questionnaires from postmenopausal women who were identified by a preliminary survey and randomly selected by lottery method after the required sample size was proportionally allocated to the randomly selected kebeles.

Data Quality Control/Management

The Investigators prepared questionnaires in English and then translated them into the local language. Then, the questionnaire was translated back to English to maintain consistency. The instrument was pretested before the actual period of data collection. The data collectors were trained for 2 days before data collection. The investigator checked the filled questionnaires daily for completeness and consistency. During analysis, data was checked and cleaned carefully.

Statistical Analysis

Data was entered into Epi‐data software version 3.1 and then exported to SPSS version 25.0 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were done and summarized by frequency tables. In addition to descriptive statistics, the chi‐square test assessed factors associated with outcome variables after checking assumptions. A binary logistic regression analysis was performed to determine the association of knowledge and self‐care practice with their associated factors. Variables having a p‐value of < 0.25 in bivariate analysis were potential candidates for multivariable logistic analysis [21, 22]. Finally, 95% CI and adjusted odds ratio (AOR) were checked, and the significant variables were taken as associated factors of knowledge and self‐care practice. P‐P‐values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Model fitness was checked by Hosmer and Lemeshow Goodness of fit test, and multi‐collinearly was checked. Finally, the result was presented using tables.

Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

Ethical clearance was obtained from the ethical review committee of the College of Medicine and Health Sciences of Mizan Tepi University with the reference number ERCCMHS/016/2023. A letter of permission was obtained from the College of Health & Medical Sciences. Written informed consent was obtained from each study subject. Information was secured after a detailed explanation of the study's primary purpose. Confidentiality of the information forwarded from the subjects was assured by omitting the names of the study subjects from the questionnaire, and a large effort was made to maintain the privacy of the respondents during the.

Result

Of all participants involved in the study, 167 (41.3%) were found in the age group above 60, and the mean age was 57.77 ± 7.9 years. Of the women interviewed, 174(43.1%) were protestant, followed by 163 (40.3%) Orthodox religion followers. Above seventy‐five percent of the respondents were married. Regarding the educational status of respondents, the majority, 146 (36.1%), had no formal education, 86(21.3%) were in secondary school, and 102 (25.2%) of the respondents had educational backgrounds above college/university (Table 1).

Factors Influencing Menopause Knowledge

In binary logistic regression analysis for factors of knowledge of menopause among study participants, was performed and found that the educational status of the women, age of the respondent, occupational status, monthly income, age at menarche, use of contraceptive, and age at last menstrual period were candidates with p‐value less than 0.25. The variables were included in multiple binary logistic regressions. The educational status of the women, occupational status, and use of contraceptives were significantly associated with the knowledge of the respondents on menopause in the final multivariable logistic regression analysis.

Analysis showed that menopausal women with educational status of college and above were 4.2 times more knowledgeable than those with no formal education (AOR = 4.23; 95% CI = 1.74, 10.24., p < 0.001). while participants with the educational level of secondary school and primary school were 3.8 and3.2 times more knowledgeable than their counterparts with no formal education(AOR = 3.87; 95% CI = 1.58, 9.48, p < 0.001) and (AOR = 3.29; 95% CI = 1.33, 8.13, p ≤ 0.01) respectively. Depending on their occupational status menopausal women who are government employees were 5.5 times more knowledgeable than those who are housewives (AOR = 5.56; 95% CI = 2.31, 13.4, p < 0.001). Study Participants who had a history of contraceptive use were 2.8 times more knowledgeable than those women who had not used contraceptive methods (AOR = 2.83; 95% CI = 1.15, 6.94, p ≤ 0.02) (Table 4).

Discussion

In the study, the overall knowledge status of the respondents was 24.8% [95% CI (20.5, 29.0%)]. This finding aligns with studies conducted in North Western and Central Ethiopia, where 23.3% and 20.9% of participants demonstrated good knowledge of menopause [23]. The similarity may be attributed to comparable socio‐demographic statuses and the timing of the studies. In another study conducted in Botswana, 29% of participants reported good knowledge of menopause, which is consistent with the findings of this study; this may be due to similar sample sizes and the context of developing countries. However, the level of knowledge in this study was higher compared to a study conducted in India, which reported an 18.18% knowledge rate [24]. The discrepancy may stem from socio‐demographic and cultural variations among the study subjects.

However, the level of knowledge of post‐menopausal women in this study was lower than the study conducted in the United Arab Emirates, where 33% of the study participants had good knowledge of menopause [25]. Likewise, the finding of this study is lower when compared to studies conducted in Sri Lanka and Nigeria where the knowledge level of respondents on menopause was above 50% and 59.2%, respectively [19, 26]. The discrepancy might be due to socioeconomic variability, geographic characteristics, and differences in the time of the study. Similarly, a study conducted in India among school teachers also revealed that the level of menopausal knowledge was 92%, which is about three times higher than the current study [27]. This inconsistency might be due to differences in educational status between the study participants.

In this study, the knowledge status of the study participants was associated with educational status, occupation, and history of contraceptive use. Menopausal women with a college education and above were 4.2 times more knowledgeable than those with no formal education. This result is supported by a study conducted in Motta(northwestern Ethiopia), Adama, Alexandria(Egypt), Botswana, and Sri Lanka (17,20,23,24) [19, 23, 28, 29]. A possible explanation could be, that women with better educational status have good health‐seeking behavior and are highly interested

To know about their health status. Besides, women with better literacy have better access to and utilization of health‐related information and healthcare services.

This study's findings revealed that the study participants’ knowledge level is significantly associated with occupational status; the women who are government employees were 5.5 times more knowledgeable than housewives. This result is supported by a study conducted in Alexandria (Egypt) and Botswana [28, 29]. The reason for this might be due to better access to healthcare services and information regarding health‐related issues, including menopause, and these women may have a chance to receive counseling to manage menopausal symptoms. The study also showed that the knowledge level of the study participants is significantly associated with the history of contraceptive use, which is in agreement with a study conducted in Motta (northwestern Ethiopia) [23]. This might be due to contact with health professionals in search of contraceptives, which might create an opportunity to deal with reproductive health issues, encompassing information related to menopause.

Limitation of the Study

Some of the questions assessing the reproductive health of the participants require remembering the time of occurrence of events, which might have recall bias. To minimize the bias, the data collector helps respondents remember those events. Some sexual life modification‐related questions might introduce social desirability bias, and this was minimized by assigning female data collectors from other sub‐cities in the town. The questionnaire used for this study was not the validated tool since there is limited studies conducted and lack of standardized and validated tools.

Conclusion and Recommendation

In conclusion, the finding of this study shows that the knowledge about menopause among post‐menopausal women was low. The educational status of the mother, occupational status, and use of contraceptives were significantly associated with the understanding of the respondents on menopause in the final multivariable logistic regression analysis. Based on the study's findings, the following recommendations are suggested to improve menopausal women's knowledge of menopausal symptoms. Planners, administrators, and other stakeholders have to design programs and interventions, amend strategies, and take corrective measures to improve the status of knowledge of menopausal women. Empower women in education to increase their health‐seeking behavior and access to information and health care services. Provide health education by health professionals and health extension workers emphasizing those women who are uneducated and unemployed. Further studies should be done to see other unexplored associated factors that were not included in the current study by mixing with qualitative study design.
