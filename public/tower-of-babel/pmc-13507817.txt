Detection of local Mycoplasma pneumoniae epidemic: universal PCR screening and sentinel surveillance
Authors: Taito Kitano, Daisuke Kitagawa, Soshi Hachisuka, Naohiro Yamamoto, Hiroki Nishikawa, Masayuki Onaka, Rika Suzuki, Soma Suzuki, Fumihiko Nakamura, Sayaka Yoshida
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

An early detection of a disease outbreak is crucial from a perspective of a rapid response to new epidemic. Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) is an important cause of pneumonia in school-aged children and adolescents. In Japan, cases with MP pneumonia are monitored through sentinel surveillance [1]. The number of tests conducted to detect each pathogen and a type of test (e.g., rapid antigen, antibody and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test) are not collected in the current sentinel surveillance system. A multiplex PCR test to detect respiratory pathogens has recently become more available in Japan. The objective of this study was to evaluate the timing of detecting a local epidemic of MP in 2024 in Nara, Japan.

Nara Prefecture General Medical Center (NGPMC) initiated a universal PCR screening for all symptomatic children and adolescents with acute onset of fever or respiratory symptoms using a multiplex PCR test, FilmArray® respiratory panel (FARP) 2.0 and 2.1 (bioMérieux, Marcy l’Etoile, France), since November 2020 for a purpose of appropriate cohorting. Eligible study participants at NPGMC were those aged 15 years or younger. Conversely, the sentinel surveillance data of MP in Nara prefecture by 5 sentinel facilities, except for NPGMC, were obtained [1]. The weekly number of MP cases between week 1 and week 39 in 2024 by the universal PCR screening at NPGMC and the sentinel surveillance at the 5 facilities in Nara prefecture was evaluated. As a benchmark, the national and Nara prefecture weekly numbers of MP pneumonia per sentinel facility between 2014 and 2023 were extracted [1], with the mean plus 3 standard deviations (5.8 and 7.9 cases per week for national and Nara prefecture benchmarks, respectively) set as upper limits for an outbreak detection [2]. In both surveillance systems, MP cases with clinically diagnosed pneumonia were included. The diagnosis was based on clinical documentation of pneumonia by physicians (certified pediatrician) in electronic health records at NPGMC. The reporting definition in the sentinel surveillance was physician's clinical diagnosis of MP pneumonia. Because the number of detected cases is influenced by the number of tests, we also evaluated the positivity rate in the NPGMC data. Because the national or prefecture surveillance does not have the positivity rate benchmark, we used international data. Japanese data showed that the positivity rate of MP at non-epidemic phases was <5%, and it increased to 11.8% in the epidemic period [3,4]. Therefore, we used 5% and 10% positivity rates with 2 consecutive weeks as a threshold for the outbreak detection.

At NPGMC, from November 13, 2020 to September 30, 2024, 6301 FARP tests were conducted. Of these, 105 (1.67%) were positive for MP. From week 21 in 2024, MP cases were continuously detected, and the number of cases increased, exceeding the upper limits of the national and Nara prefecture benchmarks in week 27 and week 29 in 2024, respectively (Fig. 1). The reported weekly number in the five sentinel facilities in Nara prefecture initiated to surge a few weeks later, reaching to the upper limits of the national and Nara prefecture benchmarks in week 30 and week 39 in 2024, respectively. The results of our study help detect a local epidemic. Following relaxation of COVID-19 mitigation measures, multiple countries have reported unusual resurgences of MP [1,2]. Our study indicated that the PCR-based surveillance may contribute to the detection of re-emergence of infectious diseases in the post-COVID-19 pandemic era. Clinically, MP may also be diagnosed by rapid antigen test or antibody titers. However, compared with PCR testing, these methods are less sensitive and specific [5]. In addition, clinicians usually order tests to detect respiratory pathogens based on a local epidemiology situation, which indicates that physicians in sentinel facilities may also have not started testing to detect MP for cases with pneumonia until they recognize the local outbreak of MP. Conversely, the universal PCR screening was not affected by physician's recognition of local epidemic. While the current sentinel surveillance cannot obtain data of positivity rates, the PCR screening indicated that the positivity rate could detect a local epidemic. However, its cost-effectiveness should be evaluated in future research.

Some limitations exist in this study. Settings of the two surveillance methods were largely different not only in testing modality, but in case definition and testing sites. Therefore, the direct comparison regarding which one is superior should be avoided. This study was conducted in a single center. The reliance on weekly absolute case counts as the trigger for outbreak detection was statistically fragile, and a couple of cases can alter the timing of signal. For example, with one more case, the weeks exceeding the upper limits of the national and Nara prefecture benchmarks would have been week 27 and week 29 for NPGMC and week 30 and week 37 for the sentinel surveillance, respectively. Conversely, with one fewer case, the weeks exceeding the upper limits of the national and Nara prefecture benchmarks would have been week 29 and week 38 for NPGMC and week 37 and week 39 for the sentinel surveillance, respectively. The reason for unchanged crossing weeks with one more case was the sufficiently low case counts in preceding weeks in which a single additional case would not have shifted the crossing weeks earlier. However, relying solely on the absolute number to detect local epidemic raises concerns about false-alarm risk. Evaluating the timing of local outbreak using more than one method, including positivity rate, can strengthen the robustness of signal detection. However, the positivity rate was not collected in the national surveillance system. Developing other indicators not to solely rely on absolute case number may be important for Japanese disease surveillance systems in the future. There was no national positivity rate threshold to define an epidemic in Japan. In addition, the date of positive cases in both methods was counted based on a date of testing, instead of a date of symptom onset. With the basis of symptom onset, the outbreak detection dates could have been earlier. The study only focused on detection timing but did not evaluate clinical outcomes or outbreak control. Therefore, this study does not imply that earlier detection necessarily confers public health benefit. In conclusion, this study indicated that the universal PCR screening could detect a local epidemic of MP. However, the systematic differences between the two strategies require cautious interpretations of the findings.
