Potential health impacts and costs of active case finding guided by Mycobacterium tuberculosis immunoreactivity survey results in Blantyre, Malawi: A mathematical modeling study

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundActive case finding (ACF) for tuberculosis (TB) can reduce transmission, yet efficient targeting requires high-quality surveillance data. We investigated the potential costs and impact of targeted ACF, guided by local estimates of the annual risk of TB infection (ARTI) derived from Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) immunoreactivity survey data in children aged <5 years.Methods and findingsUsing mathematical models parameterized with local data, we compared three case-finding approaches across 33 urban neighborhoods in Blantyre, Malawi: passive case finding (PCF) only; PCF with untargeted ACF; and PCF with ARTI-guided targeted ACF. Health outcomes (life expectancy, disability-adjusted life years [DALYs]) and costs were estimated using a Markov microsimulation model. Costs were assessed from health system and societal perspectives. Results were calculated for different assumptions about the relationship between ARTI and true TB prevalence.Compared to PCF-only, untargeted ACF was estimated to improve life expectancy by 3.3 years (95% credible interval (CrI) [1.7, 5.4]) for individuals with TB disease, but at high cost. Targeted ACF covering half of the study population was estimated to identify 80% of all individuals with TB and achieved a lower cost per DALY averted (US$400, 95% CrI [20,1,000]) than untargeted ACF (US$700, 95% CrI [100, 1,500]). In the main analysis, these cost-effectiveness ratios exceeded available cost-effectiveness thresholds for Malawi, although cost-effectiveness improved under assumptions of higher TB prevalence or greater transmission reduction. The findings are based on mathematical modeling and are subject to several limitations, including assumptions regarding the relationship between ARTI and TB prevalence, which remains uncertain.ConclusionsTargeting ACF using Mtb immunoreactivity survey data could substantially improve health impact and cost-effectiveness compared to untargeted approaches. However, low-cost approaches for collecting these data are needed for wider adoption.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global public health threat, especially in regions with a high burden of HIV [1]. Active case finding (ACF) is recommended by the World Health Organization to identify individuals with untreated TB, and involves offering systematic screening to community members regardless of symptoms. ACF can be effective in reducing the prevalence of undiagnosed infectious TB, and therefore interrupting community transmission [2]. While it holds promise, untargeted implementation across entire populations may be expensive and inefficient. Targeting ACF to high-prevalence areas may increase efficiency; however, data to identify such areas (e.g., subnational TB prevalence surveys) are usually not available due to the cost and large sample sizes required to conduct such surveys [3]. TB case notification data could be used as a proxy for prevalence to guide ACF targeting, but areas with high notification rates may reflect capacity for prompt care-seeking behavior and better access to health services, rather than underlying disease burden. New approaches to prioritizing communities with a high burden of undiagnosed disease are required, especially in the context of declining TB burdens [4].

Malawi is a low-income country in southeastern Africa, with TB incidence of 119 per 100,000 and nearly half of all cases occurring among persons with HIV [1]. In Blantyre, the second most populous city in the country, high TB notification rates are frequently observed in better-resourced neighborhoods with stronger health infrastructure, potentially obscuring areas with high levels of undiagnosed TB [4,5]. Like many high TB burden countries, Malawi’s National Tuberculosis and Leprosy Elimination Program (NTLEP) undertakes ACF, including community-based door-to-door outreach and mobile van-based diagnostic units equipped with Xpert and digital chest X-ray [6]. But determining which communities should be prioritized for ACF is challenging.

Recent findings from a Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) immunoreactivity survey using convenience samples of healthy children under five years of age attending primary care in Blantyre offer an alternative surveillance approach for targeting ACF [7,8]. The survey measured Mtb immune response using interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs), providing neighborhood-level estimates of the annual risk of TB infection (ARTI) [7,9–11]. ARTI estimates in young children may be particularly informative for identifying areas of recent transmission and elevated force of infection [12–14], and thus useful for prioritizing locations where undiagnosed TB disease is more likely to be found.

In the present study, we used mathematical modeling to assess the potential health and economic impact of ARTI-guided targeted ACF in Blantyre to inform how surveillance innovations might contribute to more efficient and impactful TB prevention efforts.

Methods

Study setting and population

Blantyre is Malawi’s second-largest city and the country’s most densely populated urban center, with an estimated population of 800,000 [15]. Adult HIV prevalence is estimated to be 14%, among the highest recorded nationally [16]. In 2019, the prevalence of TB among adults was estimated at 215 per 100,000, based on a prevalence survey conducted as part of a cluster-randomized trial [17,18]. A large proportion of Blantyre’s population resides in informal settlements on underdeveloped land, with limited access to healthcare and other municipal services, despite TB and HIV care being free at the point of delivery [17,19].

The study focused on 33 urban neighborhoods (total estimated population of 266,710), defined by community health worker catchment areas and selected based on population density and proximity to 3 study clinics [20]. Recent neighborhood ARTI estimates were derived from a Mtb immunoreactivity survey, with methods described in detail by Rickman and colleagues (2025) [7]. These ARTI estimates were used to create scenarios for targeted ACF campaigns, as described below.

Intervention scenarios

In collaboration with representatives from Malawi’s NTLEP, we defined three hypothetical intervention scenarios. The first scenario, (‘passive case finding [PCF] only’), served as a counterfactual baseline with no ACF, in which individuals with TB were only diagnosed if they self-presented to a health facility. The second scenario (‘untargeted ACF’) involved PCF in combination with untargeted ACF, implemented uniformly across all clusters regardless of underlying epidemiological differences. The third scenario (‘targeted ACF’) involved PCF in combination with targeted ACF, where clusters were prioritized sequentially from highest to lowest ARTI, as estimated from Mtb immunoreactivity survey data.

In both ACF scenarios, mobile diagnostic units equipped with digital chest X-ray and Xpert MTB/RIF were deployed to communities, accompanied by community health workers. Community members were invited for screening, beginning with a standardized symptom questionnaire. Individuals reporting TB symptoms (prolonged cough), or with any abnormality on chest X-ray, were asked to provide sputum for testing with Xpert MTB-RIF. Individuals with Xpert-confirmed TB were linked to treatment services. For both ACF scenarios, we calculated results for a one-time community-wide ACF intervention based on a previous randomized trial in Blantyre [20]. PCF was assumed to occur through the routine health system, with symptomatic clinic attendees receiving clinical evaluation and bacteriological testing through the Malawi Ministry of Health primary and secondary healthcare systems. As compared to ACF, individuals identified through PCF were assumed to face additional risks of mortality before and during treatment, and elevated rates of post-TB sequelae following treatment completion. Health and cost outcomes

Health outcomes included life expectancy, TB-attributable mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). DALYs were calculated as the sum of years of life lost (YLLs) and years lived with disability (YLDs) attributable to TB or HIV. Cost outcomes included both health system expenditures as well as patient-incurred costs, and were expressed in 2024 dollars (USD). Since TB services are provided free of charge in Malawi, patients were assumed to face no direct medical costs. Direct non-medical costs included transport, food, and accommodation during healthcare visits. Indirect costs reflected income loss or the value of time lost by patients and their households during the TB episode [21]. The incremental health benefits of ACF were calculated based on the consequences for individuals receiving ACF (benefits for individuals receiving true-positive diagnoses and harms for individuals receiving false-positive diagnoses) as well as health benefits resulting from averted secondary TB cases. Incremental costs for ACF scenarios were calculated from the direct costs of the ACF intervention (assumed to cost US$7 per person receiving ACF) and associated TB treatment, cost-savings from averted TB diagnosis and treatment through passive diagnosis, cost-savings resulting from averted secondary cases, and changes in ART costs due to changes in survival among individuals with HIV. We calculated all costs and health outcomes over the lifetime of affected individuals. For the calculation of incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs), costs and health benefits (DALYs averted) were both discounted at a rate of 3% per year.

Modeling approach for individuals receiving ACF or PCF

We used a published Markov simulation model [22] to estimate lifetime health outcomes and TB care costs for individuals with or without TB and/or HIV under each intervention scenario. Using this model a cohort of 10,000 individuals representing the ACF target population was exposed to different TB diagnosis and treatment scenarios and tracked over time to estimate cumulative health outcomes and costs. Individuals were represented in the model by starting age (mean: 34.0, IQR: 26.0–40.0), sex (75.0% male for individuals with TB), HIV and ART status (13.6% HIV–positive, 70.1% of these on ART), CD4 cell count for individuals with HIV (mean: 274 cells/μL, IQR: 68–372 cells/μL), and FEV1 percentage as a measure of TB-associated lung damage (mean: 63%, IQR: 52%–75% for individuals with prevalent TB). Fig A in S1 Appendix provides a schematic of the model structure. In the model, individual survival and health status were updated with a weekly timestep, until each individual in the simulated cohort was dead. HIV-associated immune function was tracked through changes in CD4 cell count, which declined for individuals not receiving ART, and rebounded among individuals receiving ART. Individuals with HIV had greater HIV-associated mortality and disability weights, determined by CD4 cell count and ART receipt. HIV-associated immune function also affected rates of TB progression and probability of self-cure. For individuals with untreated TB, those receiving ART were assumed to have higher rates of diagnostic evaluation compared to individuals not receiving ART. Lung function was tracked through changes in FEV1 percentage, which was assumed to decline for individuals with untreated TB disease, rebound during TB treatment, and continue to improve following successful TB treatment. The extent of lung function recovery following TB cure was determined by the minimum FEV1 value reached before treatment initiation. FEV1 percentage was assumed to continue to decline for individuals receiving a false-negative TB diagnosis, lost to follow-up from treatment before TB cure, or with ongoing disease following treatment completion. TB-associated mortality and disability were determined by TB status and FEV1 percentage. Individuals surviving TB disease were assumed to face ongoing morbidity and mortality risks due to post-TB sequelae. The magnitude of these risks was based on the extent of residual long impairment as measured by FEV1 percentage. As compared to the earlier application of the model [22], this analysis adopted higher values for the FEV1 distribution for individuals with TB, reflecting a milder distribution of TB disease compared to individuals detected through passive case detection. Key model parameters are provided in Table A in S1 Appendix, and the model was programmed in R and C++. Additional details are reported in Can and colleagues, 2025 [22]. Modeled individuals were stratified by TB and HIV status and were simulated under the diagnostic pathways defined in each scenario.

Model inputs were drawn from multiple sources, including cluster-level ARTI estimates [7], a TB case notification dataset (2022) with residential geolocation collected via tablet-based electronic entry [4,23,24], Blantyre TB prevalence survey data (2019) [25], and population estimates from World Pop (2020) [15]. Cost inputs were based on published literature and NTLEP reports, and included unit costs for the ACF intervention, TB diagnosis, treatment, and HIV-related care, as well as patient-incurred costs (Table B in S1 Appendix).

Approach for estimating secondary TB cases averted by ACF

We estimated the number of secondary TB cases averted based on the numbers of individuals with TB diagnosed and treated through ACF and the reproductive number for Blantyre. The reproductive number was estimated from the secular trend in TB incidence (assumed to be proportional to the TB notification rate), and published estimates of the generation time [26–28]. For individuals with TB identified through ACF we assumed transmission would be reduced by 50% (with an uncertainty interval from 25% to 75%) as compared to the PCF scenario. This was based on the rationale that a single cross-sectional ACF round will identify individuals approximately halfway through their infectious period, on expectation. We used the reproductive number to calculate the geometric series of future TB cases averted by ACF. For each averted TB case, incremental health benefits and costs were based on the health and economic outcomes for individuals with versus without TB, calculated using the Markov model. We considered costs and health benefits associated with all future secondary TB cases averted by ACF. In sensitivity analyses, we recalculated costs and health consequences i) assuming ACF would reduce transmission among detected TB cases by 75% (vs. 50% in the main analysis), ii) using a higher TB prevalence estimate (452 per 100,000, based on data for Blantyre extracted from Malawi’s 2013–2014 national TB prevalence survey [29], versus 215 per 100,000 in the main analysis), and iii) excluding transmission benefits entirely.

ACF algorithm performance

Based on input from the Malawi NTLEP and implementing partners, we assumed that ACF would be undertaken with an algorithm in which individuals with any reported cough would be tested with Xpert. Individuals not reporting a cough would receive chest X-ray, and those with any abnormality on this X-ray would also receive Xpert. Individuals testing Xpert positive would be diagnosed with TB. Under the ACF scenarios, we assumed that all residents in a given cluster would be offered ACF, 10.2% of those offered ACF would refuse or be unable to provide sputum [30], 5.0% of those diagnosed with TB would be lost to follow-up before treatment initiation (based on NTLEP input), and individuals initiating treatment would experience treatment outcomes (death, loss to follow-up, failure, and cure) consistent with reported treatment outcomes data [31].

ACF algorithm sensitivity was calculated as (1 − (1 − Senssymp)*(1 − Senscxr))*Sensxpert, where Senssymp, Senscxr, and Sensxpert represent the sensitivity of the symptom screen, chest X-ray, and Xpert, respectively. Algorithm specificity was calculated as 1 − (1 − Specsymp*Speccxr)*(1 − Specxpert), where Specsymp, Speccxr, and Specxpert represent the specificity of the symptom screen, chest X-ray, and Xpert, respectively. Values and sources for these calculations are shown in Table C in S1 Appendix.

Statistical analysis

Simulation outputs were aggregated at the cluster level to estimate TB disease detection, life expectancy, DALYs, and costs under each intervention scenario. These results were used to calculate incremental differences in health outcomes and costs compared to the PCF-only scenario. ICERs comparing ACF scenarios to PCF-only were computed at both the cluster level and across all clusters combined. The analysis was conducted over a lifetime horizon.

To evaluate the efficiency of ARTI-guided targeted ACF, we assumed that cluster TB prevalence was positively associated with ARTI. We operationalized this by assuming that the TB prevalence in cluster i (πi), is equal to k·AiP, where Ai is ARTI in cluster i, P parameterizes the strength of relationship between ARTI and TB prevalence, and k is a normalizing constant calculated to ensure average prevalence across clusters equals 215 per 100,000:πi=k·AiP.

We varied the value of P from 0.0 (no relationship between TB prevalence and ARTI) to 1.0 (TB prevalence linearly proportional to ARTI), in increments of 0.2. The number of individuals with prevalent TB in each cluster was calculated as πi·Ni, where Ni is cluster population.

Using these cluster-specific TB prevalence estimates, we then computed the cumulative number of TB cases identified, DALYs averted, and incremental costs as clusters were sequentially added to the ARTI-guided targeted ACF intervention in order of descending ARTI. To further assess the implications of geographic targeting, we modeled a scenario in which ACF was implemented in clusters comprising half of the total population, starting with clusters with the highest ARTI. Clusters were ranked and included sequentially until reaching 50% population coverage. We compared this targeted approach to an untargeted strategy in which the same population coverage was achieved through random selection of clusters.

We computed 95% credible intervals as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the sample of estimates for each outcome, as generated from 1,000 randomly drawn parameter sets, reflecting uncertainty in input parameters. All analyses were performed in R software (v4.4.3), using the Rcpp package [32]. ChatGPT (OpenAI; GPT-5.5) was used solely to support grammar and language editing and to assist with limited code development and debugging. All Artificial Intelligence (AI)-assisted text was checked and revised by the authors. All AI-assisted code was reviewed, tested, and validated against the intended model specifications and outputs. The authors retain full responsibility for the study, code, and final manuscript.

Ethics statement

This mathematical modeling study used previously collected, published, and anonymized data and involved no new participant recruitment or data collection. The data were fully anonymized and posed no risk of individual identification; additional ethical approval and informed consent were therefore not required.

Health impact for individuals receiving ACF

Under both ACF scenarios, ACF implementation resulted in improved health outcomes for individuals with TB. For HIV–negative individuals with TB, ACF increased life expectancy by an average of 3.3 years (95% credible interval (CrI) [1.7, 5.4]) compared to PCF alone, while HIV–positive individuals gained an average of 2.1 years (95% CrI, [0.8, 3.7]) with ACF (Table 1). False-positive results among individuals without TB, leading to unnecessary TB treatment, were associated with a small decrease in life expectancy of 3.2e−5 years (95% CrI [3.9e−6, 7.0e−5]) among HIV–negative individuals and 1.4e−5 years (95% CrI [0, 4.3e−5]) among people living with HIV.

ACF also led to substantial reductions in DALYs among individuals with TB. Among those who were HIV–negative, the average DALYs averted per person was 5.8 (95% CrI, [3.1, 9.3]), and among HIV–positive individuals, 2.8 DALYs were averted (95% CrI [1.0, 5.0]) (Table 1). The majority of these gains were driven by reductions in years of life lost (YLLs) (Tables E and F in S1 Appendix). Reductions in the share of deaths attributable to TB were also notable: under ACF, the proportion of TB-attributable deaths was 7.2 percentage points lower (95% CrI [3.3, 13.4]) among HIV–negative individuals and 14.2 percentage points lower (95% CrI [7.3, 23.6]) among HIV–positive individuals, compared with PCF (Table G in S1 Appendix).

Secondary TB cases averted by ACF

With ACF, the reduction in transmission resulting from earlier TB identification and treatment through ACF was estimated to avert 0.88 (95% CrI [0.24, 2.29]) downstream TB cases per index case. Accounting for the estimated number of secondary cases averted and the health burden associated with each case, each TB case identified through ACF was estimated to yield an additional 5.08 (95% CrI [1.32, 15.04]) life-years gained and 8.98 (95% CrI [2.28, 26.83]) DALYs averted through reduced transmission.

Cost-effectiveness of ACF by cluster

Cost-effectiveness ratios (comparing ACF to passive detection) varied substantially across clusters, driven by differences in HIV prevalence and estimated TB burden. Under the assumption of perfect predictive power (P = 1), the majority of clusters had ICERs below $1,000 per DALY averted, with a median of $845 (Table 2). As anticipated, the ACF intervention was dominated for clusters with zero ARTI, meaning that for these clusters (with assumed zero TB prevalence) ACF was estimated to incur higher costs and harm health outcomes.

Comparison of targeted versus untargeted ACF

As shown in Table 3, a targeted ACF intervention including half of the total population consistently identified clusters with higher average TB prevalence, ranging from 362 to 269 per 100,000 as predictive power decreased from P = 1 to P = 0.2. This targeting approach captured a larger share of the total TB burden than untargeted ACF. For example, when ARTI was assumed to be perfectly predictive (P = 1), targeting half of the total population through ACF using prioritized clusters identified 81.5% of all prevalent TB cases, compared to only 50% in the untargeted scenario. As predictive power decreased from 1 to 0.2, ICERs increased from $400 (95% CrI [20, 1,000]) to $500 (95% CrI [60, 1,200]) per DALY averted. In contrast, untargeted ACF (P = 0) produced an ICER of $700 (95% CrI [100, 1,500]) per DALY averted.

Sensitivity analyses

When we assumed that ACF reduced transmission among individuals identified with TB by 75% (vs. 50% on the main analysis), ICERs fell by approximately 30% compared to the main analysis, ranging from $300 (95% CrI [dominant, $800]) with P = 1, to $500 (95% CrI [$20, $1,200]) with P = 0 per DALY averted. In a sensitivity analysis that assumed a higher baseline TB prevalence (452 per 100,000, versus 215 per 100,000 in the main analysis), ICERs fell by approximately 50% compared to the main analysis, ranging from $200 (95% CrI [dominant, $500]) (P = 1) to $300 (95% CrI [dominant, $800]) (P = 0) per DALY averted. In a sensitivity analysis that excluded transmission benefits, ICERs were 2.5–3.0 times the values estimated in the main analysis, ranged from $1,200 (95% CrI [$600, $2,100]) (P = 1) to $1,800 (95% CrI [$900, $3,200]) (P = 0) per DALY averted. Additional results for these sensitivity analyses are shown in Table M in S1 Appendix.

Discussion

This study shows that geographically targeted ACF using ARTI estimates derived from a convenience sampling approach for Mtb immunoreactivity in young children can result in greater health gains and efficiency compared to untargeted ACF. In Blantyre, Malawi—a setting with a high burden of TB and HIV—we found that differences in community-level ARTI, as measured through Mtb immunoreactivity surveys, could help prioritize areas for diagnostic interventions more effectively than untargeted approaches. Combining novel approaches to TB surveillance (convenience Mtb immunoreactivity sampling among under 5-year-old children) with targeted ACF therefore offers an approach to increase the effectiveness and efficiency of TB screening in high-burden settings.

Prioritizing where to implement ACF has been a long-standing challenge for TB programs [3]. Although modeling analyses have suggested that targeted ACF could offer substantial efficiency gains in reducing transmission [33], systematic reviews of implementation studies have identified only a small number of studies that evaluated the effectiveness of targeted TB interventions [34,35], likely because of the difficulty in identifying a suitable epidemiological marker to guide targeting. Our findings suggest that ARTI data could fill this gap, offering a feasible and practical epidemiological marker for directing ACF implementation.

For programs planning to implement ACF, how to best deploy ACF services is a central question. In our analysis, compared to PCF alone, ACF improved life expectancy, reduced TB-attributable mortality, and averted 767 DALYs per 100,000 individuals screened. Efficiency gains were observed when comparing targeted to untargeted ACF. For example, providing ACF to approximately half of the population through targeted ACF could find 80% of all TB cases potentially identifiable through ACF, with more favorable ICERs compared to untargeted ACF. These findings highlight the potential value of geographic targeting in enhancing the efficiency of ACF implementation under real-world constraints on population coverage.

While targeted ACF improved efficiency relative to untargeted ACF, the absolute cost-effectiveness of the intervention remains a critical consideration [36,37]. For Malawi, published estimates suggest that cost-effectiveness threshold may lie between 33% and 44% of per-capita GDP (equivalent to $205 to $274 in 2025 USD) [38]. A recent government report proposed a more stringent threshold of $66 per DALY averted [39]. By these benchmarks, the ICERs estimated for both targeted and untargeted ACF—ranging from $400 to $700 per DALY averted—would not meet conventional definitions of cost-effectiveness, and represent a substantial cost relative to available healthcare funding Malawi, where per capita health expenditure was approximately US$41 in 2023. However, for infectious diseases like TB such thresholds may underestimate longer-term population benefits, including transmission reduction, mitigation of future risks of drug-resistant disease, and progress toward elimination goals. Moreover, the cost-effectiveness of ACF could improve under settings with higher TB prevalence or with reduced implementation costs.

These findings suggest that although ACF is a high-cost intervention, its efficiency can be substantially improved through geographic prioritization, potentially offering a higher return on investment in settings where ACF is already being implemented—as is the case in Blantyre and across Malawi. Although this study used ARTI estimates to guide targeted ACF, the modeling framework is data-source agnostic and can be parameterized using alternative proxies for TB burden, including case notification rates, genomic risk predictions, or community prevalence estimates. Each of these other data sources representing alternative possible approaches for targeting ACF.

A key contribution of this study lies in demonstrating the potential of using Mtb immunoreactivity survey data as a basis for targeting ACF. In many high-burden urban settings, including Blantyre, TB notification data may not accurately reflect underlying transmission risk due to variations in health system access, diagnostic capacity, or health-seeking behavior [4]. Historically, ARTI data have been widely used to track TB epidemiology and inform public health policy and planning, including prioritization of interventions such as case finding, preventive therapy, and vaccination strategies [40–44]. These surveys have been conducted less frequently in recent years, with greater priority being given to surveys of TB disease prevalence for tracking changes in TB epidemiology. However, compared to TB prevalence surveys immunoreactivity surveys are considerably less resource-intensive to implement, and can be feasibly integrated into existing surveillance systems, particularly within primary care settings [45]. For these reasons, there have been calls to expand the use of immunoreactivity surveys to supplement other methods for tracking TB burden. For this study, the cost of immunoreactivity survey implementation was excluded from the analysis, as the survey data were collected through a separate research study that was embedded within primary care visits, and not as part of the intervention being evaluated. While providing potentially valuable data, Mtb immunoreactivity surveys can introduce a number of challenges, including the feasibility and acceptability of testing young children, which were not examined in this analysis. Moreover, multiple factors could weaken the relationship between immunoreactivity survey estimates and true TB prevalence, including statistical uncertainty from small sample sizes, differential sampling bias across clusters, imperfect test performance, and uncertainty in the quantitative relationship between immunoreactivity among children and population TB prevalence. We tested the implications of these factors through varying the predictive power of ARTI, but more research on this relationship is needed.

Our model projected that ACF substantially reduced TB-attributable deaths, thereby increasing life expectancy among both people with and without HIV, underscoring its potential value in mitigating excess TB-associated mortality. This contrasts with findings from the TREATS study in Zambia and South Africa, which found no significant reduction in TB prevalence following a community-based intervention combining HIV testing, linkage to care, and TB symptom screening [46]. However, there are several differences between our study and the TREATS model, including differences in the approach for screening (TREATS primarily relied on symptom screening, whereas our ACF strategy also used chest X-ray with computer-aided detection for individuals without symptoms), and targeting (our ARTI-guided approach allowed fine-scale targeting of high-transmission neighborhoods, with potentially greater impact on transmission). Furthermore, ACF may mitigate long-term morbidity associated with post-TB sequelae, which are increasingly recognized as major contributors to TB disease burden [22,47]. These sequelae are often underdiagnosed and underreported yet contribute significantly to the burden of DALYs in high TB-burden settings [22]. By allowing earlier identification and initiation of treatment, ACF may prevent progression to severe TB disease and reduce the likelihood of permanent impairment. These delayed outcomes may not be captured by empirical data on short-term ACF outcomes.

At the cluster level, we observed substantial variation in the economic efficiency of ACF, with high-ARTI clusters generally achieving lower ICERs. These findings support a phased approach to ACF scale-up: initiating ACF in high-ARTI clusters and expanding outward as resources allow. However, interpretation of value for money should consider not only cost per DALY but also health system priorities, budget constraints, and equity considerations, particularly in resource-limited settings [3,37].

This study has several limitations. First, although our simulations assumed a correlation between ARTI and active TB disease prevalence, the strength of this relationship may vary depending on factors such as age, immune status, test performance, and sampling uncertainty. In addition, ARTI estimates themselves are subject to uncertainty, particularly in clusters with zero positives, which may reflect limited sample size rather than true absence of infection. To explore this, we modeled a range of predictive power scenarios; nonetheless, further empirical validation of the link between ARTI and TB prevalence is needed [44,48,49]. Second, we computed the number of TB cases that could be averted by ACF based on an estimate of the reproductive number for Blantyre and an assumption that early diagnosis via ACF would avert approximately half of the transmission associated with each TB case. However, there is substantial uncertainty about the effects of ACF on transmission, and sensitivity analyses that tested optimistic and pessimistic transmission assumptions found that cost-effectiveness ratios were sensitive to the magnitude of transmission effects. Third, we examined a scenario in which immunoreactivity survey data were already available from an earlier study. The cost of collecting immunoreactivity survey data is a factor that could be influential for cost-effectiveness, and so while excluded from this analysis these costs would need to be considered in other settings where these data are not available. For this reason, research is needed on how to best conduct these surveys, how to make them more efficient, and the frequency with which they need to be repeated. Fourth, we only examined one ACF diagnostic algorithm, yet different approaches have been proposed [50], and new technologies may soon be available that lower the cost of testing [51]. Fifth, in this study we examined the impact and cost-effectiveness of a single round of ACF. However, in some settings ACF will be implemented as repeated rounds, with each round generating additional health benefits by identifying further people with TB. For ACF implemented as multiple rounds, the long-term health effects will depend on changes in disease prevalence over time and resulting dynamic effects on TB transmission [52]. We did not explicitly model repeated ACF rounds, and the incremental costs and benefits of such strategies may differ from those presented here. Lastly, this study was conducted in a setting of co-prevalent HIV and TB, and the health impact and cost-effectiveness of ACF will vary according to the prevalence of these conditions.

Evidence describing even modest differences in transmission can inform more strategic and efficient ACF delivery. Building on data sources like convenience-based immunoreactivity surveys, this approach can be embedded in national surveillance systems and adapted to other high-burden settings. While targeted ACF improves efficiency, it raises equity concerns and cost implications—particularly around prioritization and long-term ART provision following extended survival. These downstream costs, though linked to positive outcomes, should be considered in programmatic planning.

In conclusion, geographically targeted TB ACF directed by ARTI data from convenience surveys of Mtb immunoreactivity offers a data-driven strategy to improve the efficacy and effectiveness of community-based TB screening. Even in settings with relatively uniform burden, immunoreactivity data can support more targeted resource allocation and strengthen the case for scaling up ACF. Embedding targeted approaches within existing systems—while considering equity and long-term costs—may improve the effectiveness and sustainability of TB programs.

Comments from the reviewers:

Reviewer #1: The study is clearly and thoroughly analysed and presented. However, I have a number of questions that do not appear to be covered in the description of the intervention and measurement of outcomes (effectiveness).

1.What is the justification for limiting the ACF to one round of screening? Both empirical and modelling studies of ACF have shown that benefits are cumulative and repeated screening is required to demonstrate effectiveness on prevalence, transmission and incidence, and hence on health outcomes (mortality and DALYs).

2.Does the model incorporate the effect of the intervention on transmission and subsequent incidence (which is additional to the benefit for the individuals who are diagnosed by the intervention). Without including these compounding benefits, the effectiveness is substantially underestimated and hence ICERs are substantially over-estimated.

3.Related to the above, what is the time horizon for measuring the outcomes?

4.Results are cited as " improve life expectancy by …. for individuals with TB disease". This is not a relevant outcome. The relevant outcome is improved life expectancy (and reduction in DALYs) for the population as a whole and this includes the effective on incidence of TB prevented over a long time horizon due to reduced transmission (due to reduced prevalence).

5.What are the assumptions about participation rates in screening and rates of successful transition for TB diagnosis to completing TB treatment (in all three arms)?

6.My other comment would be that the cost of screening using the proposed algorithm may be over-estimated based on newer developments. I realise this may not need to be estimated but it should be acknowledged that ICER would be lower if the cost of screening could be lowered, based on evolution of new tools, without losing effectiveness.

Reviewer #2: In their manuscript "Potential health impacts and costs of active case finding guided by Mycobacterium tuberculosis immunoreactivity survey results in Blantyre, Malawi", Kim et al. present a mathematical model assessing the effect and cost-effectiveness of a tuberculosis (TB) active case finding (ACF) intervention. Major Comments

1. Lack of detail regarding the underlying model structure and inputs

The analysis relies heavily on a Markov microsimulation model described as being "developed by Can et al. (2025)" with only a brief reference stating "The model incorporated transitions between health states, including undiagnosed TB, diagnosis and treatment, post-TB sequelae, and death" (page 6, Methods). This creates a significant transparency issue as readers must consult an external publication to understand fundamental aspects of the analysis, like the complete model structure and health states, transition probabilities between states, natural history assumptions, how HIV status affects transitions, age stratification and demographic processes

In light of this, the Methods section should be restructured to first provide a clear, self-contained description of the model architecture before explaining how it was applied to this specific question. Consider adding a diagram showing the complete model structure with all health states and transitions, a table of key transition parameters, clear statement that this analysis applies a previously published and validated model to a new question (ARTI-guided targeting) and an

explicit description of which aspects are novel to this study versus which are from the prior work.

2. Post-TB sequelae modeling

The manuscript mentions that the model includes "post-TB sequelae" as a health state and notes these "are increasingly recognized as major contributors to TB disease burden" (page 14, Discussion). The model appears to account for post-TB morbidity through YLDs and ongoing productivity losses (Appendix Table 2). However, critical details are missing, namely:

What specific post-TB sequelae are included (respiratory impairment, cardiovascular complications, etc.)?

What proportion of treated/untreated TB cases progress to sequelae?

How do sequelae severity and duration differ by HIV status?

What disability weights are applied?

How do post-TB sequelae affect mortality risk?

Given that post-TB sequelae appear to influence the cost-effectiveness estimates (through ongoing costs and DALYs), these assumptions require transparent reporting.

Perhaps add a dedicated subsection or table describing the post-TB sequelae model component, including transition probabilities to sequelae states, disability weights, mortality risks, and costs associated with this state. If these details are only in Can et al. (2025), they should be summarized here given their importance to the current analysis.

3. MTB immunoreactivity surveys as a targeting tool

While the novelty of using Mtb immunoreactivity surveys in children <5 years for ACF targeting is central to this work, there is insufficient critical discussion of this approach's strengths and limitations. The manuscript briefly mentions that "immunoreactivity surveys are considerably less intensive than TB prevalence surveys" (page 14) and that the survey used convenience sampling from primary care, but several important issues receive inadequate attention, like feasibility and acceptability of testing young children; selection bias from convenience sampling (children attending clinics may not represent community burden); Uncertainty in ARTI estimates, particularly in clusters with zero positives (acknowledged briefly but not discussed in depth); The assumption that infection risk in children <5 reflects recent transmission and correlates with prevalent disease (which is central but examined only through sensitivity analysis)

The manuscript acknowledges in limitations that "the strength of this relationship [between ARTI and TB prevalence] may vary" but doesn't adequately discuss the implications of the convenience sampling approach or situate this method among alternatives.

Additional Comments

Minor point on model description: The Methods would benefit from explicitly stating upfront (perhaps in the first paragraph of "Modelling approach") that the analysis applies a previously developed and validated TB natural history model to assess a novel intervention targeting strategy. Currently, this only becomes clear midway through the Methods section.

These comments aim to help the authors improve transparency and strengthen the manuscript's contribution. The work addresses an important question, but clearer presentation of the underlying model and more critical engagement with the surveillance approach would substantially enhance its value to the field.

Comments from the reviewers:

Reviewer #1: Comment #2.

Evaluating the impact of a single round of screening when theoretically (and in previous empirical studies) the benefits are only likely to be evident after multiple rounds of screening is NOT a proof of concept. Furthermore, since the risk of transmission (and hence secondary cases) is related to community prevalence, there is a good reason to expect that, for the incidence outcome, there will not be diminishing returns, but in fact, accentuated returns from multiple rounds of screening. The concept of diminishing returns assumes no benefits from preventing secondary and subsequent cases due to transmission.

While there is a substantial cost in collecting empirical data from multiple rounds of screenings, there is no such cost from doing this in a modelling study. Hence, if the authors truly want to test the proof of concept, they should present the evaluation of multiple rounds of screening - with an endpoint being prevalence reduced to a specified level.

Comment #3.

Good to see this additional analysis. However, it seems to be based on the assumption that ACF will reduce transmission by 50% compared with PCF. Not sure where this estimate comes from. It should be noted that most cases detected by ACF may never have been detected by PCF, since they are asymptomatic. While some may have become symptomatic (and hence be available for PCF) others will not have and will remain infectious until self-healing. In these instances, transmission risk will have been reduced by 100%. In a minority of cases, the individual may have been subsequently diagnosed with PCF, in which case there would be a partial reduction in transmission risk.

Comment #5

I would argue that having a focus on individuals with TB, rather the population at risk of TB, remains an underlying problem with the interpretation of this analysis. This approach is suitable for modelling noncommunicable diseases, but is not, in my opinion, suitable for modelling the impact of interventions on communicable diseases (particularly those with airborne, human-to-human transmission, such as TB, influenza and COVID-19).
