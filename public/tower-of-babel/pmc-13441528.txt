Practical Guide to Clazosentan Administration in Patients with Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage
Authors: Hidenori SUZUKI, Yosuke AKAMATSU, Hiroshi ABE, Satoshi IIHOSHI, Fusao IKAWA, Tsuyoshi IZUMO, Hidenori ENDO, Hiroharu KATAOKA, Shunsuke KAWAMOTO, Kenichiro KIKUTA, Yuichiro KIKKAWA, Yuichi KUBOTA, Hiroki KURITA, Satoshi KURODA, Atsushi SAITO, Makoto SAKAMOTO, Masaaki SHOJIMA, Taku SUGIYAMA, Jun C TAKAHASHI, Ichiro NAKAGAWA, Hirofumi NAKATOMI, Yu HASEGAWA, Morito HAYASHI, Tomohito HISHIKAWA, Miki FUJIMURA, Nobutaka HORIE, Mitsuhito MASE, Tatsushi MUTOH, Motohiro MORIOKA, Atsuo YOSHINO, Masataka YOSHIMURA, on behalf of the SAH/Spasm Symposium

Introduction

The outcome of subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) due to ruptured cerebral aneurysms remains poor, with delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) being an important determinant of outcomes.1) The American Heart Association guidelines recommend only nimodipine as a preventive medication for DCI,1) and nimodipine is widely used in Europe and the United States. However, nimodipine is not approved in Japan. Instead, several drugs are clinically available for DCI only in Japan, and until the endothelin receptor antagonist clazosentan became available, multidrug therapy centered on fasudil hydrochloride (a Rho-kinase inhibitor) was commonly used.2,3)

The endothelin receptor antagonist clazosentan received regulatory approval in Japan in 2022, the first country in the world, for the prevention of cerebral vasospasm (CVS) and associated cerebral infarction and ischemic symptoms in patients with SAH who have undergone surgical or interventional obliteration of a ruptured cerebral aneurysm, and it has been used in clinical practice for over 3 years. During the period, many institutions published reports on their experience with clazosentan, and recent reports showed that it had better treatment outcomes than conventional drugs for the same purpose.4) Meanwhile, the introduction of clazosentan has significantly changed postoperative management for aneurysmal SAH. Simply adding clazosentan to postoperative management methods that many institutions have implemented independently not only prevents patients from fully benefiting from clazosentan but may even result in complications associated with clazosentan administration. In order to improve treatment outcomes with clazosentan compared with conventional treatments, it is necessary to become familiar with how to manage complications associated with clazosentan administration, which is thought to have taken several months to 1 year at many institutions. Therefore, for neurosurgeons or other doctors who are about to start using clazosentan for the first time, we published a “Practical Guide to Clazosentan Administration” in Japanese5) with the aim of avoiding or shortening this “learning period of several months to 1 year.” This article is based on the Japanese version of the guide.

There is no evidence regarding optimal systemic management during clazosentan administration, and the content of this guide represents a current expert consensus (basic approach). However, even among experts, there is no complete consensus. Furthermore, this guide has been written with a practical approach to ensure that even neurosurgeons unfamiliar with administering clazosentan will not encounter major difficulties. At this time, it does not introduce ambitious trials being conducted only at specific facilities. The content of this guide should be considered a practical guide for administering clazosentan. It in no way restricts the discretion of neurosurgeons or physicians who provide medical care tailored to individual patient circumstances. We encourage readers to present their ambitious endeavors at academic conferences and receive evaluation. We hope that this guide will contribute to improving outcomes for patients with SAH and that this guide will be revised as appropriate in the future as knowledge accumulates (Table 1).

Serious complications of clazosentan and their treatment

1) Pulmonary edema/pleural effusion

Frequency: 10%-13%.

Treatment: Oxygen administration, diuretics, positive-pressure ventilation.

Monitoring points: Weight gain, decreased saturation of percutaneous oxygen (SpO2), chest x-ray findings.

2) Cerebral edema and increased intracranial pressure (ICP)

Treatment: Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage, ICP monitoring.

Aim for ICP ≤ 20 mmHg and cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) ≥60 mmHg.

3) Hypotension (shock)

Treatment: Vasopressor (norepinephrine → vasopressin).

If tachycardia is present, increase blood pressure (BP) with anti-tachycardia measures and phenylephrine.

Blood transfusion, albumin preparations (, fluid replacement).

4) Bleeding tendency (retina, gastrointestinal tract, etc.)

Treatment: Use caution with concomitant medications.

5) QT prolongation, arrhythmia

Treatment: Serum electrolyte correction (potassium, magnesium) and discontinuation of QT-prolonging drugs.

In severe cases, continuous electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring.

Special notes

1) Before starting clazosentan administration, confirm absence of pregnancy, severe liver dysfunction, or persistent intracranial hemorrhage.

2) Patients aged 76 years or older and patients with impaired cardiac or renal function are at high risk of complications; therefore, clazosentan administration should be determined carefully.

3) If complications associated with clazosentan administration are severe, consider discontinuing treatment midway (Section “Criteria for discontinuing clazosentan”).

4) It is recommended that institutions create and implement a clazosentan postoperative management protocol.

Checkpoints before Administering Clazosentan

Basic approach

1) Confirm that there are no contraindications to clazosentan administration (history of hypersensitivity to the ingredients of this drug, pregnancy or possibly pregnancy, severe hepatic dysfunction (Child-Pugh classification class C), or ongoing intracranial hemorrhage).

2) Clazosentan can be administered if there are no contraindications.

3) When administering clazosentan, weight change is an indicator of fluid retention; therefore, it is advisable to measure the patient's weight at the time of admission.

4) Complications related to fluid retention caused by clazosentan often occur within 5 days of starting clazosentan administration.6) Therefore, care should be taken to avoid excessive fluid administration, including during surgery or intervention (maintain an in/out balance of ≤ +500 mL/day). If urine output is insufficient, consider administering a diuretic (furosemide 10-20 mg).

5) Patients with concomitant heart failure or chronic kidney disease, patients with catecholamine cardiomyopathy or neurogenic pulmonary edema due to SAH, and patients aged 76 years or older, especially those with impaired cardiac or renal function, are more likely to experience side effects from clazosentan. Therefore, carefully consider whether the facility can manage these side effects.

6) Carefully consider whether to administer clazosentan to patients with QT prolongation on ECG, cerebral edema or increased ICP, pulmonary edema or pleural effusion, or bleeding (vitreous hemorrhage, gastrointestinal bleeding, etc.), as their condition may worsen after initiation of clazosentan administration.

7) In patients with hemodynamic cerebral ischemia due to moyamoya disease, clazosentan should be administered with caution.

8) It is desirable to develop and implement a common postoperative management protocol within the facility.

Commentary

1) Based on the attached document.

2) The following patients were not included in the phase III randomized controlled trial (RCT) conducted in Japan: patients aged 76 years or older, patients with World Federation of Neurological Surgeons (WFNS) grade V, patients whose Fisher computed tomography (CT) classification was other than group 3, patients who started clazosentan treatment more than 48 hours after the onset of SAH, and patients who received other preventive medications for CVS or DCI (fasudil hydrochloride, ozagrel, milrinone, nicardipine, and statins). Therefore, the efficacy of clazosentan has not been established in these patients.6)

3-5) Although there is no evidence supporting any of these opinions, they have been repeatedly reported at academic conferences and other events.

6) Based on the attached document.

7) This is expert opinion based on clinical experience.

8) A multicenter observational study showed that complications associated with clazosentan decreased after protocol implementation.7)

Elderly Patients Aged 76 Years or Older

Basic approach

1) Clazosentan can be administered to patients aged 76 years and over, but the risk of complications such as pulmonary edema and hypotension is generally higher in these patients than in younger patients.

2) Taking into consideration organ disorders such as cardiac and renal dysfunction, administration of clazosentan should be determined based on medical history and biological age, regardless of chronological age.

3) When administering clazosentan, stricter systemic management, such as in/out balance monitoring, is required.

Commentary

1-3) There is no evidence regarding clazosentan administration to patients aged 76 years or older, and it has been repeatedly reported at academic conferences that the risk of complications is higher and that careful systemic management is required for these patients.

3') With appropriate systemic management, clazosentan administration may result in better outcomes at discharge than fasudil hydrochloride, even in patients aged 76 years or older.4)

Patients with WFNS Grade V

Basic approach

1) There is no evidence based on RCTs that clazosentan inhibits CVS or DCI and improves outcomes in patients with WFNS grade V.

2) In clinical practice, clazosentan is widely used even in patients with WFNS grade V.

3) Administration of clazosentan to patients with WFNS grade V may not significantly increase the risk of complications such as pulmonary edema.

Commentary

1) Patients with WFNS grade V are often excluded from RCTs. As a result, there are no drugs with proven efficacy for patients with WFNS grade V, including clazosentan and nimodipine, the latter of which is not approved in Japan.

2) A retrospective multicenter study reported that clazosentan resulted in better discharge outcomes than fasudil hydrochloride, even in patients with WFNS grade V, when appropriate systemic management was performed.4)

3) A retrospective single-center study reported that patients with WFNS grade V did not experience an increased incidence of complications such as pulmonary edema or cerebral edema.8)

Patients Other Than Fisher Group 3

Basic approach

1) In clinical practice, clazosentan is widely used for patients other than those classified as Fisher group 3.

2) In patients other than those classified as Fisher group 3, the side effects of clazosentan are less frequently problematic, and efficacy is good.

Commentary

1-2) In a retrospective multicenter study including 43.5% of patients with SAH who were not classified as Fisher group 3, clazosentan treatment reduced CVS and delayed cerebral infarction compared with fasudil hydrochloride, resulting in significantly better outcomes at discharge.4)

Patients with Widespread Cerebral Infarction

Basic approach

1) In patients with widespread cerebral infarction, there is a risk of worsening cerebral edema; therefore, administration of clazosentan should be carefully considered.

Commentary

1) Clazosentan is a cerebral vasodilator associated with fluid retention and increased vascular permeability and is thought to carry a high risk of worsening cerebral edema (expert opinion). In particular, after SAH or extensive cerebral infarction, cerebral autoregulation is disrupted, and the blood-brain barrier becomes more permeable. Pharmacological vasodilation in this state may exacerbate vasogenic edema.9-11) In fact, when vasodilators such as nicardipine and fasudil are used, side effects such as pulmonary edema, hypotension, and cerebral edema have been reported.12) Caution is required when using clazosentan because it is a drug with a similar mechanism.

Clazosentan Administration Method

Basic administration method

1) Clazosentan should be administered alone through a 0.2 μm filter using a dedicated lumen in a central venous line or a dedicated infusion line.

2) The usual adult dosage is 300 mg (12 mL) of clazosentan added to 500 mL of saline and administered intravenously at a rate of 17 mL/h (10 mg/h of clazosentan) using a volumetric continuous infusion pump. The dosage may be reduced depending on liver function and concomitant medications.

3) Replace the clazosentan solution every 24 hours and discard any remaining solution.

4) Start administration early after obliteration of a ruptured aneurysm (within 48 hours of the onset of SAH) and continue until 15 days after the onset of SAH.

5) When other drugs are used in combination to prevent CVS or DCI, careful attention must be paid. In particular, strict fluid management as described in 8) below is required to prevent worsening of fluid retention.

6) Carefully consider whether to administer clazosentan to patients with moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh class B). If administered, reduce the usual dose by half (5 mg/h of clazosentan). Specifically, add 150 mg (6 mL) of clazosentan to 500 mL of saline and administer intravenously at a rate of 17 mL/h using a volumetric continuous infusion pump until 15 days after the onset of SAH.

7) Avoid coadministration with rifampicin unless unavoidable for therapeutic reasons. If coadministration is required, reduce the usual dose to one-quarter (2.5 mg/h of clazosentan), carefully monitor the patient's condition, and closely observe for side effects. Specifically, add 150 mg (6 mL) of clazosentan to 500 mL of saline and administer intravenously at a rate of 8.5 mL/h using a volumetric continuous infusion pump until 15 days after the onset of SAH.

8) To reduce the risk of pulmonary edema, the goal during clazosentan administration should be to maintain a fluid (in/out) balance of +500 mL/day or less, and loop diuretics (e.g., furosemide) should be introduced and adjusted as necessary. Additionally, maintenance fluid infusion should be limited to approximately 1.0-1.5 mL/kg/h (expert opinion). Furthermore, fluid infusion should primarily consist of isotonic to hypertonic fluids, with albumin preparations used as needed to maintain colloidal osmotic pressure.

Commentary

1-4, 6-7) The administration method for clazosentan should follow the package insert.

4') Administration up to 15 days after the onset of SAH means that, assuming the day of onset is day 0, the last administration of clazosentan solution is started on day 14 and discontinued 24 hours later on day 15.

4'') Some experts believe that initiation of clazosentan therapy in patients considered to be at high risk of pulmonary complications (e.g., high cardiothoracic ratio) should be delayed until urine output increases and cardiac enlargement resolves.13)

4''') Although there is limited experience with administering clazosentan to patients with unknown symptom onset or to patients transported more than 48 hours after symptom onset, administration does not appear to increase the risk of clazosentan's side effects (expert opinion).

4'''') A phase II RCT conducted in Japan (the Japan-Korea joint dose-finding study) found no significant difference in the frequency of adverse events in patients treated >48 hours after onset,14,15) and the REACT trial showed no safety issues even when administered within 96 hours.16) Given these findings, clazosentan administration is acceptable even in patients treated > 48 hours after onset following assessment of their overall condition. However, if clazosentan is administered after 5-7 days from onset, when CVS is severe, the possibility of local or widespread cerebral edema or hemorrhagic complications due to increased vascular permeability and rapid vasodilation cannot be ruled out. In such cases, careful assessment of vascular status (e.g., via CT angiography [CTA] or digital subtraction angiography [DSA]) and evaluation for the presence or absence of cerebral edema are recommended before deciding whether to administer clazosentan, along with frequent subsequent imaging evaluations (expert opinion).

5) Side effects of clazosentan occur more frequently when it is used concomitantly with fasudil hydrochloride, ozagrel, nicardipine, or cilostazol.17,18) On the other hand, complications related to fluid retention associated with clazosentan may be mitigated by appropriate protocol-based fluid management.4,7)

6') In the Child-Pugh class B group, the bioavailability of clazosentan increases approximately twofold. In moderate hepatic impairment, clazosentan clearance is reduced, and overdose at the usual dose may occur; therefore, dose reduction by half is recommended.19)

7') Concomitant use of rifampicin increases the bioavailability of clazosentan by approximately 3.9-fold, increasing the risk of adverse reactions. Therefore, a reduced dose of clazosentan and frequent monitoring are recommended during coadministration with rifampicin.20)

8) In addition to fluid balance, weight measurement is considered useful for systemic management during clazosentan administration.21)

8') It is recommended that hydration be provided enterally (preferably orally or via an enteral feeding tube) to limit the need for increased intravenous fluid intake (expert opinion).

Side Effects of Clazosentan

Basic approach

1) Clazosentan is an endothelin receptor antagonist used to suppress CVS after aneurysmal SAH. However, phase III RCTs, meta-analyses, and real-world clinical data have shown that clazosentan frequently causes side effects such as pulmonary complications (pleural effusion and pulmonary edema), hypotension, and anemia.6,18,22-24)

2) In particular, fluid retention (edema in various parts of the body, pleural effusion, and pulmonary edema) occurs in approximately 10%-40% of patients receiving clazosentan, with a higher risk in elderly patients, patients with renal impairment, and patients receiving concomitant vasodilators such as fasudil hydrochloride.18,25) Although rare, reversible cerebral edema (basal ganglia edema, etc.) has also been reported.26)

3) Clazosentan is effective in preventing CVS and DCI; however, reports regarding improvements in mortality and functional outcomes differ between Japan and Western countries, and the decision to administer clazosentan should be made with careful consideration of the risk of side effects.6,27-29)

Commentary

1) Frequency of side effects: The main side effects are pleural effusion (～13%), pulmonary edema (～11%), hypotension (5%-20%), and anemia (10%-15%).17)

2) Fluid retention often occurs within a few days of initiating clazosentan therapy; therefore, caution is required, particularly during the early stage of treatment (early CVS phase).30) Risk factors include advanced age (>70 years), hypoalbuminemia, an increased cardiothoracic ratio, and renal dysfunction.24) Concomitant use of vasodilators (e.g., fasudil, nicardipine, and cilostazol) tends to exacerbate clazosentan side effects.18,25) Preventive measures against fluid retention include strict fluid management, and early detection and treatment of heart failure are essential. Administration of clazosentan to elderly patients should be carefully considered, with dose adjustment as necessary (expert opinion). If serious adverse reactions occur, prompt action, including discontinuation of clazosentan, is required.31)

3) Meta-analyses showed a trend toward improved outcomes with clazosentan treatment but did not reach statistical significance.27,28,32)

Infusion Management

Basic approach

1) The total volume of fluid infusion in the fasting state should be 1.0-1.5 mL/kg/h, including 0.9% saline for clazosentan administration.6,7)

2) For patients who are unable to take food or fluids orally and who receive enteral nutrition, the target fluid volume should be: (total volume of infusion fluids + enteral nutrition) − (urine volume + CSF drainage volume) ≤500 mL/day. Insensible perspiration is not taken into account.6,21)

3) If patient weight gain exceeds 2-3 kg since admission, fluid overload should be considered, and administration of a diuretic (such as furosemide) should be considered.30)

4) In cases of hypoalbuminemia, consider administering albumin preparations.

5) When clazosentan is discontinued, consider readjusting the volume of administered fluids.

Commentary

1) In a Phase III RCT in Japan, a fluid infusion rate of 1.0-1.5 mL/kg/h was used as the standard, and a policy of avoiding excessive fluid infusion was clearly stated.6) In a study, the infusion protocol for the clazosentan group was reported to be an extracellular fluid rate of 1.0 mL/kg/h, a fluid balance target of 0-500 mL/day, and laboratory targets of sodium 130-150 mEq/L, albumin (Alb) >2.5 g/dL, and hemoglobin (Hb) >8 g/dL.7)

2) As for fluid strategies, emphasis has been placed on maintaining normovolemia while avoiding excessive fluid replacement, and the importance of individualized dynamic management (combining vital signs, weight, laboratory results, and echocardiography) has been highlighted.25) A case report described respiratory failure due to pulmonary edema diagnosed early through hemodynamic monitoring and weight change, which improved with fluid restriction and diuretic administration.33) Dynamic management based on the ROSE (Resuscitation-Optimization-Stabilization-Evacuation) model was reported to be effective.33) Based on the experience of a single institution, it was suggested that pulmonary edema due to excessive fluid infusion may be a problem in elderly patients and that it is important to limit fluid administration and, if necessary, combine it with heart failure treatment.31) As evidence and experience accumulate, continuous refinement of fluid management protocols at each facility is considered key to improving outcomes with clazosentan.3)

3) Fluid adjustment is necessary in cases of excessive fluid infusion, and it has been reported that diuretics should be used in cases of fluid overload >1,500 mL.7) Attempts to prevent excessive fluid infusion should also be made by maintaining fluid (in/out) balance during surgery or intervention, limiting the postoperative fluid infusion rate to 1.0 mL/kg/h or less, and administering a diuretic (furosemide) early.30)

3') It was reported that combined use of Goreisan (a traditional Japanese [Kampo] medicine) significantly reduced symptoms related to mucosal edema, such as diarrhea and nasal mucosal swelling, and suppressed refractory hyponatremia, making it a potential adjunctive therapy for clazosentan-related side effects.34)

4-5) Although there is no evidence supporting either of these, they have been repeatedly reported at academic conferences, etc. Avoid intravascular dehydration and strive to maintain normovolemia.

Nutrition

Basic approach

1) After obliteration of a ruptured cerebral aneurysm, begin oral feeding or tube feeding as soon as possible.

2) Enteral nutrition products should be selected that are high in calories, high in protein, and low in water content.

3) The amount of enteral nutrition should be gradually increased, aiming for a total volume of infusion fluids + enteral nutrition volume ≤2,000 mL/day. Since fluid volume tends to become excessive after starting enteral nutrition, attention should be paid to fluid (in/out) balance. Monitor for diarrhea as enteral nutrition is gradually increased. Diarrhea is more likely to occur at faster administration rates. It is also more likely to occur with high osmolality and high fat content, and lactose intolerance and disruption of the host intestinal flora can also have an effect.

Commentary

1-3) There is no evidence regarding nutrition, and these statements represent expert opinion based on clinical experience.

2') Standard enteral nutrition formulas contain 1.0 kcal/mL, but by selecting a high-concentration enteral nutrition formula containing 1.5-2.0 kcal/mL, sufficient nutrition can be provided while keeping total fluid intake within the target range.

2'') In a study of postoperative management of patients with SAH, ensuring an energy intake of ≥25 kcal/kg/day and protein intake of ≥0.8 g/kg/day from the third postoperative day significantly improved serum Alb levels 25-35 days after hospitalization and was an independent factor associated with favorable outcomes at discharge.35)

2''') It has been reported that in severe SAH, energy expenditure is approximately 1.5 times the basal energy expenditure.36,37) The optimal calorie intake is unknown, but the Japanese Guidelines for Nutritional Therapy for Critically Ill Patients weakly recommend not intentionally reducing energy intake below energy expenditure during the early stage of treatment for critically ill patients.38) Refer to the following.37)

1.5 × basal energy expenditure

Basal energy expenditure (Harris-Benedict equation):

Men: 66.47 + 13.75 × weight (kg) + 5.0 × height (cm) −6.75 × age (years)

Women: 66.51 + 9.56 × weight (kg)+1.85 × height (cm) −4.68 × age (years)

3') When protein is broken down into amino acids, digestion becomes easier, but the osmotic pressure of the nutritional supplement increases. Osmotic pressure increases in the order of semi-digested, digested, and elemental nutrition. Excess lipid content can also easily cause diarrhea, but the frequency of diarrhea decreases when medium-chain fatty acids are used.

BP Management

Basic approach

1) After obliteration of a ruptured cerebral aneurysm, the goal is to maintain normal circulatory blood volume and keep systolic BP at normal to slightly elevated levels (120-160 mmHg). Excessive lowering of BP should be avoided.

2) Clazosentan has a vasodilatory effect and may lower BP. Therefore, antihypertensive drugs are often unnecessary during clazosentan administration, even in patients with coexisting hypertension. Side effects of clazosentan may occur more frequently when used in combination with intravenous antihypertensive drugs such as nicardipine. If BP remains low, diuresis may not be achieved, leading to worsening edema.

3) Anemia and hypoalbuminemia often occur during clazosentan administration. When BP decreased, vasopressors should be administered, and anemia and hypoalbuminemia should be corrected (Hb ≥9 to 10 g/dL and Alb ≥2.5 to 3.0 g/dL, respectively).

4) Norepinephrine (noradrenaline) is the first-choice vasopressor used to increase BP. Dopamine is also acceptable; however, because it requires high doses and may induce arrhythmia, switching to norepinephrine should be considered. If norepinephrine does not sufficiently increase BP, continuous intravenous administration of low-dose vasopressin is recommended.

5) For hypotension accompanied by tachycardia, consider discontinuing medications that can cause tachycardia, such as cilostazol. Next, administration of a beta-blocker (such as bisoprolol patches or continuous intravenous microdoses of landiolol) may normalize heart rate and increase BP. If hypotension persists, consider continuous intravenous microdose administration of phenylephrine, which increases BP without increasing heart rate.

6) If vasopressors are ineffective, consider administering stress-dose of corticosteroids while considereing possible endocrine abnormalities.37)

7) If CPP ≥60 mmHg cannot be maintained despite the above measures, consider discontinuing clazosentan.

Commentary

1) This statement is based on expert opinion derived from clinical experience and the Japan Stroke Society Guideline 2021 for the Treatment of Stroke (Revised version 2025).39)

2) In an unanesthetized dog model, clazosentan administration alone reduced systolic BP by 10-20 mmHg.40)

3) The optimal Alb level is unknown. In RCTs, transfusion strategies maintaining Hb levels of ≥9 g/dL and ≥10 g/dL significantly reduced poor outcomes and CVS, respectively.41,42)

4) Although low-dose dopamine administration showed vasodilation and increased organ blood flow in animal experiments, clinical trials did not confirm these effects.43) In a clinical trial comparing dopamine and norepinephrine as vasopressors during shock,44) the dopamine group showed a higher incidence of arrhythmias; therefore, guidelines recommend norepinephrine as the first choice.45)

5) Phenylephrine is the first choice for postoperative hypotension accompanied by tachycardia, with the aim of increasing BP without further increasing heart rate.46)

7) Failure to maintain CPP, defined as mean BP ([systolic BP − diastolic BP] ÷ 3 + diastolic BP) − ICP < 60 mmHg, has been reported to result in poor outcomes.47)

CSF Drainage

Basic approach

1) It is preferable to insert a CSF drainage system (ventricular, cisternal, or lumbar drainage) to control ICP.

Commentary

1) In the LUMAS trial, in which patients, primarily with mild SAH, were randomized to receive or not receive lumbar drainage (5-10 mL/h) while receiving nimodipine, the incidence of DCI was significantly reduced in the drainage group, but there was no significant difference in the incidence of delayed cerebral infarction or outcome.48)

1') In the EARLYDRAIN trial, patients with primarily severe SAH who were receiving nimodipine and ventricular drainage for ICP monitoring were randomly assigned to receive or not receive lumbar drainage (5 mL/h). Although there was no difference in the incidence of CVS, the drainage group had a significantly lower incidence of delayed cerebral infarction and significantly better outcomes.49) Significantly lower ICP in the drainage group was thought to contribute to these favorable effects.49)

1'') Maintaining ICP ≤ 20 mmHg and CPP, defined as mean BP ([systolic BP − diastolic BP] ÷ 3 + diastolic BP) − ICP, ≥ 60 mmHg, may lead to improved cerebral energy metabolism and outcomes.47)

1''') The revised Japan Stroke Society Guidelines (revised version 2025) add that lumbar drainage is recommended over ventricular drainage in cases of WFNS grade III and coil embolization and also mention the appropriateness of cisternal drainage in surgically treated cases.39)

1'''') A meta-analysis reported that lumbar drainage in patients with aneurysmal SAH was associated with improvement in CVS, secondary cerebral infarction, and mortality without increasing the risk of adverse events.50)

Concomitant Medications

Basic approach

1) From a safety perspective, treatment with clazosentan as a single agent is recommended. When used in combination with other drugs, strict systemic management is required to avoid side effects.

2) Clazosentan can be used in combination with drugs that do not have vasodilatory properties, such as antiplatelet and antiepileptic drugs.

Commentary

1) Patients receiving clazosentan in combination with other preventive medications for CVS or DCI (fasudil hydrochloride, ozagrel, milrinone, nicardipine, and statins) were not included in the phase III RCT conducted in Japan, and the efficacy of clazosentan in combination with these medications has not been established.6)

1') Side effects of clazosentan occur more frequently when it is used in combination with vasodilatory drugs (such as fasudil hydrochloride, ozagrel, nicardipine, and cilostazol).17)

1'') In clinical practice, clazosentan has been used in combination with other preventive medications for CVS and DCI.17) However, when used in combination, stricter systemic management is required to minimize adverse effects of clazosentan. A Japanese multicenter retrospective study conducted at 20 institutions between April 2023 and March 2024 compared 161 patients who underwent coil embolization or surgical clipping within 48 hours of SAH onset between the clazosentan monotherapy group (67 patients) and the clazosentan plus cilostazol combination group (94 patients). Although there was no significant difference in outcomes at discharge, the cilostazol combination group tended to have a lower rate of poor outcomes (11.7% vs. 14.9%, p = 0.095), and the incidence of DCI was significantly lower in the combination group (0.0% vs. 7.5%, p = 0.02).51) Combining clazosentan with other drugs, such as cilostazol, may result in better outcomes than clazosentan alone, but further investigation, including systemic management methods, is needed.

1''') A Japanese multicenter retrospective study found that prophylactic use of clazosentan in combination with fasudil did not reduce the incidence of CVS and suggested an association with pulmonary edema, CVS, DCI, and poor outcomes.25) Evidence supporting the usefulness of prophylactic use of clazosentan in combination with fasudil is currently limited.

1'''') It has been reported that elderly patients (≥75 years) are more likely to discontinue clazosentan treatment because of complications such as fluid retention, hypoxemia, and hypotension than younger patients and that adjustment of concomitant antihypertensive medications, early introduction of diuretics, and intensified cardiopulmonary monitoring are recommended for elderly patients.24,52)

2) There are currently no reports that the frequency of side effects increases significantly when clazosentan is used in combination with drugs that do not have vasodilatory properties, such as aspirin, clopidogrel, or antiepileptic drugs (expert opinion).

Monitoring

Basic approach

1) Neurological monitoring involves regular assessment of level of consciousness and focal neurological symptoms.

2) Direct monitoring of CVS includes magnetic resonance (MR) imaging, 3-dimensional CTA, DSA, cerebral blood flow measurement (single-photon emission CT, etc.), transcranial Doppler, and transcranial color flow imaging, and evaluation is performed by combining these modalities.

3) Monitor BP, urine output, ECG, and SpO2. To monitor fluid retention, calculate fluid (in/out) balance and measure body weight. Perform blood tests and chest radiographs as appropriate. Cardiac ultrasound (and measurement of inferior vena cava diameter) is also useful.

4) Body weight is a useful indicator for preventing complications related to fluid retention caused by clazosentan; therefore, if possible, weight should be measured at admission or before aneurysmal obliteration. Diuretics should be administered intravenously to address weight gain and maintain fluid balance, and if ineffective, continuous administration of diuretics may be necessary.53) If negative fluid balance persists, blood tests should be performed to confirm dehydration or ultrasound examination should be performed to confirm collapse of the inferior vena cava, and extracellular fluid loading should be performed as necessary.

5) In cases with complications such as takotsubo cardiomyopathy or cardiogenic pulmonary edema, or in cases where fluid management is difficult, consultation with the emergency medicine, intensive care, anesthesiology, or cardiology departments is recommended to monitor cardiopulmonary function.

6) If an ICP monitor or CSF drainage is placed, manage the patient to maintain appropriate CPP.47,54) In addition, body temperature can be managed by measuring bladder temperature, rectal temperature, and other core body temperatures.

Commentary

1-6) These statements represent expert opinion based on clinical experience.

Pulmonary Edema

Basic approach

1) Pulmonary edema is a significant complication during clazosentan administration and is particularly prevalent in elderly patients.6,17)

2) Strict fluid (in/out) balance management and aggressive administration of diuretics reduce the incidence of pulmonary edema during clazosentan treatment.55,56)

3) There is no special treatment for pulmonary edema during clazosentan administration. As with typical pulmonary edema, treatment involves administration of oxygen, diuretics, Alb, and positive-pressure ventilation while monitoring oxygenation with SpO2.

4) If symptoms worsen during treatment of pulmonary edema, consider discontinuing clazosentan; however, administration may be continued while managing fluid infusion and weight.

Commentary

1) In a Japanese phase III RCT, pulmonary edema occurred in 11.9% of patients.6) In addition, an interim report of the clazosentan post-marketing surveillance study found that pulmonary edema occurred in 13.5% of patients aged 75 years or older, a higher rate than in those under 75 years (4.9%).17)

3-4) These are expert opinions based on clinical experience.

Treatment for DCI (Delayed Ischemic Symptoms that Appear Regardless of the Presence or Absence of CVS) under Clazosentan Administration

Basic approach

1) This is basically the same as rescue therapy for DCI or CVS.1,39) Clazosentan administration should be continued until day 15 unless adverse events, such as pulmonary complications, interfere with rescue therapy.

2) If DCI is observed, add an anti-CVS drug other than clazosentan (fasudil hydrochloride, ozagrel, cilostazol, etc.).

3) Correct intravascular dehydration. If hypoalbuminemia is present, administer Alb preparations. If not, administer isotonic fluids such as saline. However, maintain euvolemia and avoid hypervolemia.1)

4) Intravascular dehydration should be assessed by monitoring fluid (in/out) balance as well as other preload indices (inferior vena cava diameter, central venous pressure, intravascular blood volume measured by transpulmonary thermodilution, extravascular lung water volume, etc.).33,52) The presence of pulmonary edema and congestion should be confirmed by chest radiography, CT, echocardiography, etc., as appropriate.

5) If intravascular dehydration is caused by pulmonary edema or congestion, efforts should be made to maintain urine output by considering the use of diuretics or vasopressin V2 receptor antagonists. Particularly in elderly patients, attention should be paid to the risk of serious cardiopulmonary complications associated with fluid therapy.24,57)

6) When intentionally increasing BP, aim for an increase of at least 20% over baseline. If necessary, systolic BP may be increased to 160-220 mmHg. Since clazosentan may be associated with vasogenic cerebral edema,26) excessive BP elevation should be avoided. If increasing BP is difficult, hyperdynamic therapy with dobutamine or milrinone58,59) is also a useful method.

7) If CVS is present, selective intra-arterial infusion of a vasodilator (such as fasudil hydrochloride) or percutaneous transluminal angioplasty may be performed.7,16)

Commentary

1-7) These are expert opinions based on clinical experience. Continuing clazosentan administration even after the DCI onset is recommended. It is important to select treatment that minimizes serious side effects of clazosentan. Among the so-called 3H therapies, hypervolemia and hemodilution have been shown to be ineffective against DCI,1,39) and there is concern that they may further increase cardiopulmonary complications during clazosentan administration.24,57) Although evidence is insufficient, rescue therapies such as intentional hypertension, selective intra-arterial infusion of vasodilators, and percutaneous transluminal angioplasty are considered effective.7,16) Meanwhile, further investigation is needed to determine whether additional administration of oral or intravenous anti-CVS drugs (e.g., fasudil hydrochloride, ozagrel, cilostazol) provide benefits that outweigh the risk of side effects.

Management after Day 15 When Clazosentan Administration Is Completed

Basic approach

1) Even if no DCI is detected, it is important to check for the presence or absence of CVS by performing MR angiography or other imaging on days 13-14 before discontinuing clazosentan treatment.7) Assessment of intravascular dehydration using chest radiography, CT scans, echocardiograms, etc., at appropriate times is also advisable.

2) From day 15 onward, dehydration should be avoided until approximately day 21, and mobilization and rehabilitation should be encouraged while carefully monitoring for the appearance of DCI.

3) If asymptomatic CVS is present, avoid antihypertensive drugs and diuretics and maintain high BP.

4) If DCI is detected, continue monitoring fluid (in/out) balance and urine output to avoid intravascular dehydration. In addition to intentional hypertension, administer anti-CVS drugs other than clazosentan (fasudil hydrochloride, ozagrel, cilostazol, etc.) as needed.

Commentary

1-4) These are expert opinions based on clinical experience. The occurrence of CVS or DCI after the vasodilatory effects of clazosentan have resolved has not been adequately investigated. Therefore, it is recommended to check for the presence of CVS and intravascular dehydration through multiple tests before discontinuing clazosentan administration on day 15.7) Generally, the incidence of DCI after day 14 is low at 3.5% and is not thought to affect outcomes.60) Even if DCI occurs, continuing clazosentan administration after day 15 is not covered by health insurance. However, if DCI is observed, treatment similar to that used for DCI up to day 14 should be considered (Section “Treatment for DCI (delayed ischemic symptoms that appear regardless of the presence or absence of CVS) under clazosentan administration” above).

Criteria for Discontinuing Clazosentan (Figure 1)

Flowchart for discontinuing clazosentan administration.

If new intracranial hemorrhage, severe liver dysfunction, severe arrhythmia, or severe allergic reactions occur while receiving clazosentan, consider immediately discontinuing clazosentan. If hypotension, pulmonary edema, or cerebral edema occurs, treat these side effects first, including discontinuing concomitant medications that may increase the side effects, such as fasudil hydrochloride, and then consider discontinuing clazosentan if uncontrolled (Section “Blood pressure management” for hypotension and Section “Pulmonary edema” for pulmonary edema).

Circumstances in which clazosentan discontinuation has been reported

1) Respiratory failure due to fluid retention: When hypoxemia progresses due to pleural effusion or pulmonary edema and does not improve with diuresis or respiratory management.

2) Difficult-to-control hypotension: When it is determined that systolic BP or CPP is difficult to maintain despite administration of vasopressors.

3) Decreased urine output, poor response to diuretics, and worsening pulmonary edema.

4) Cerebral edema worsens, and ICP cannot be controlled.

5) New intracranial hemorrhage: If intracerebral hemorrhage or subdural hematoma occurs, discontinue clazosentan treatment immediately.

6) Other side effects that may be caused by clazosentan:

i) Severe liver damage: Serum levels of aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, or bilirubin are significantly elevated and poorly controlled.

ii) Severe ECG abnormalities: QT prolongation or life-threatening arrhythmia.

iii) Severe allergic reactions, etc.

Commentary

1-4) These are expert opinions based on clinical experience.3,6,17,52) However, according to results of a nationwide questionnaire survey on treatment and management of aneurysmal SAH conducted in 2024 among neurosurgeons certified by the Japanese Society on Surgery for Cerebral Stroke, reasons for discontinuing clazosentan were pulmonary complications such as pulmonary edema in 30 cases (60%), cardiac complications such as heart failure in 16 cases (32%), cerebral edema in 14 cases (28%), and other edema in 4 cases (8%).3)

5-6) The reason for discontinuing clazosentan when intracranial hemorrhage is observed is attributed to concern regarding bleeding progression; however, in a Japanese phase II/III study, the incidence was not higher than in the placebo group (one case each of intracranial hemorrhage and epidural hematoma of unknown cause).61) Serious drug-related side effects have been reported, including liver dysfunction, heart disease, and severe allergic reaction.61)

Addendum) According to an analysis of the Japanese Adverse Drug Event Report database of the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency, older age (>70 years) and concomitant use of fasudil hydrochloride were identified as independent risk factors for fluid retention associated with clazosentan.18) Discontinuation of fasudil hydrochloride should be considered if this side effect develops during concomitant use.18)

Countermeasures for DCI after Discontinuation of Clazosentan

Basic approach

1) First, prioritize treatment of clacosentan side effects, correcting negative fluid balance, hypotension, and dehydration.

2) Avoid intravascular dehydration while maintaining euvolemia and avoiding hypervolemia.

3) If safe, administer conventional medications for CVS and DCI (fasudil hydrochloride, ozagrel, nicardipine, cilostazol, etc.). Rescue therapy for DCI should also be provided as usual.

Commentary

1-3) These statements represent expert opinion based on clinical experience.1,21,52)

2') Hypervolemia increases the risk of pulmonary complications but is considered ineffective for DCI.1)

3') These interventions suggest that the incidence of DCI and final outcomes may not necessarily worsen even when clazosentan is discontinued.6)
