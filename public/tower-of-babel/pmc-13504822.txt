Compensating patient partners on health research teams: a qualitative descriptive study of barriers, facilitators, and implications for equity, diversity, and inclusion in Canadian patient-oriented research
Authors: Mwalu Peeters, Kimberly Strain, Shelley Doucet, Adrienne Gulliver, Anna Samson, Sarah Bridges, Amy Ma, Samantha Fowler, Dawn P Richards, Ravneet Somal, Madeline Levesque, Kathryn Asher, Alison Luke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResearch teams increasingly rely on patient engagement–where patients are engaged as partners in knowledge production–to improve the quality and relevance of health research. An ongoing challenge is how best to compensate patient partners for their time and expertise, given diverse personal, financial, and institutional contexts. This study identifies common barriers and facilitators to patient partner compensation; examines how compensation practices actively shape who can participate in patient-oriented research, enabling or constraining equity, diversity, and inclusion within health research teams; and explores strategies to mitigate barriers to equitable compensation and boost diverse and inclusive patient engagement in health research.MethodsUsing a qualitative descriptive design, and with active involvement from patient partners on our research team, we recruited geographically and socially diverse participants from across the spectrum of patient-oriented research in Canada, including patient partners, research staff, administrative and finance employees, and funding organization staff. Semi-structured interviews (n = 25) and focus groups (10 in number, n = 24), conducted between 2023 and 2025, elicited respondents’ impressions of the interactions between patient partnership, compensation practices, and equity, diversity, and inclusion. We applied inductive thematic analysis to identify shared values and common experiences, and explore solutions to compensation practices that challenge equitable patient engagement.ResultsThematic analysis of interviews and focus groups generated seven themes: Clear expectations and processes, Bureaucratic inertia, Reproduction of privilege, Institutional mistrust, Tax and benefit implications, Funding cycles, and Relational dynamics.ConclusionCompensation is an integral component of effective patient-oriented research and has significant implications for research engagement among structurally marginalized communities. We propose recommendations for advancing equitable and inclusive compensation practices within Canadian patient-oriented research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40900-026-00961-x.Keywords: Patient-oriented research; Patient and public involvement; Research engagement; Compensation; Payment; Honoraria; Equity, diversity, and inclusion; Relationality; Power dynamics; Relationship building

Background

Health research in Canada has increasingly embraced the principles of patient engagement and patient-oriented research (POR), recognizing that individuals with lived and living experience of health conditions and the healthcare system bring invaluable perspectives to the research process. The Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR) defines POR as “a continuum of research that engages patients as partners, focuses on patient-identified priorities, and improves patient outcomes” [1]. POR is most often conducted by multidisciplinary teams in collaboration with relevant interest holders, with the goal of applying knowledge generated through these partnerships to improve healthcare systems, services, and practices [1].

This shift toward POR reflects a broader international movement emphasizing research conducted not only for patients but also with and by them [1, 2]. By embedding patient voices throughout all stages of research, from priority setting and study design to data interpretation and knowledge translation, research teams can produce findings that are more relevant, applicable, and impactful to the communities they aim to serve. The inclusion of patients as partners also enhances accountability, transparency, and integrity, aligning research more closely with societal needs and health system priorities [3, 4].

In Canada as elsewhere, POR takes the social science traditions of participatory action and community-based research as a starting point [5]. Active involvement of and leadership by the individuals and communities affected by research is fundamental to these approaches, and these principles were integral to the vision for Canada’s Strategy for Patient-Oriented Research (SPOR) [6]. SPOR was launched by the CIHR in 2011, and, as such, marked an official shift by the country’s leading funder of health research in favour of translational approaches that move beyond basic biomedicine towards the synthesis, dissemination, and integration of evidence into the healthcare system and clinical practice [6, 7]. Crucially, this shift has emphasized the need for active and meaningful involvement of patients in all aspects of the research endeavour -- governance, priority-setting, data collection and analysis, and knowledge translation – in order to make the knowledge production process more ethical, democratic, transparent, informed by, and accountable to the populations it is meant to serve [1, 6, 8]. A recent report noted a palpable shift in national health research culture since 2011, with clear increases in the frequency and perceived value of patient and public involvement [9]. However, challenges persist. The increased awareness and valuation of patient-oriented research have not been consistent across all levels of the healthcare system, with senior leadership in some provinces and territories yet to buy in [9]. While patient-oriented research outputs and citations have increased, the evidence linking these outputs to improvements in patient experiences and outcomes, or to system level outcomes and efficiencies, remains scant [9, 10]. Patient engagement has increasingly become accepted practice in health research [8, 11], but this increase in acceptance has also occasioned tokenistic engagement, with patient or public participation viewed as a box to tick rather than a meaningful partnering process [9, 12]. As described by Turcotte et al. [13], the patient-oriented discourse can function as a “…dominant (royal) science which appropriates, colonizes, and ultimately subjugates more marginal (nomadic) forms of participatory research” (p. 701). Critics also point to a tendency for patient-oriented approaches to focus too closely on individual circumstances within a reductive framing of what constitutes patient identity, neglecting the multiple and overlapping social locations, differences in power and privilege, and complex web of social, environmental, economic, and policy conditions that determine care experiences, health outcomes, and health equity [12, 14]. Related to this, a persistent shortcoming in patient-oriented research has been the inability to consistently engage patient research partners from diverse communities. A recent survey of Canadian patient research partners describes a high degree of sociodemographic homogeneity, with engagement concentrated among the urban-dwelling, White, Canadian-born, and well-educated [11].

Achieving meaningful and equitable patient engagement requires more than simply inviting diverse patients to join the research team. Fully realized patient research partnerships necessitate careful attention to the structural, relational, and procedural factors that shape how patient partners are engaged and valued [15]. Among these considerations, compensation for patient partners has emerged as a critical yet complex issue. As defined by SPOR [16], compensation encompasses fair recognition of a patient partner for their research engagement, and should be commensurate with and reflective of the value of their time and effort. It may be cash, cash-equivalent, or some other form valued by and agreed to by the receiving patient partner. Compensation acknowledges the expertise that patient partners contribute, while also signaling respect for their role as equal collaborators in the research enterprise [17]. Conversely, the absence of clear, consistent, and equitable approaches to compensation risks reinforcing power imbalances and limiting the authenticity and sustainability of patient engagement [3, 18].

Challenges related to compensation in patient-oriented research have been highlighted in several studies. In Canada, compensation practices remain largely unstandardized [19], contributing to a range of barriers including variability in compensation rates, distinctions between honoraria and other forms of compensation, delays in disbursement, and administrative complexities such as contracts and tax considerations. Other reported concerns include potential impacts on government benefits, the need to differentiate between compensation and reimbursement for expenses (while also ensuring that patient partners’ out-of-pocket costs are covered), liability coverage, and the requirement to provide personal financial information, such as Social Insurance Numbers (SINs) or banking details. Institutional barriers also persist, as research budgets, grant structures, and authorship policies are often misaligned with the principles of patient engagement. Collectively, these challenges contribute to frustration, uncertainty, and inequity among all members of the research team [1, 3, 4, 20–23].

Despite growing recognition of these issues, the knowledge base describing current practice in patient research compensation remains limited. Much of the existing evidence is anecdotal, relying on case studies or informal feedback rather than systematic investigation. Moreover, scholarship on patient partner compensation has tended to emphasize barriers rather than facilitators, leaving key questions unanswered about what strategies enable equitable, sustainable, and meaningful engagement [4, 23–26]. Encouragingly, some emerging guidelines now provide direction for research teams on best practices for patient partner compensation [16, 27–29], but their application remains inconsistent across Canadian contexts.

Significantly, the intersection between compensation practices and principles of equity, diversity, and inclusion (EDI) remains insufficiently explored [30–33]. Compensation processes such as requirements for banking information, restrictions on reimbursement eligibility, or payment structures that affect social benefits can unintentionally exclude or disadvantage structurally marginalized groups [19, 25]. These groups include individuals living with low income, persons with disabilities, those without stable immigration status, and members of Indigenous and racialized communities. Addressing these gaps is crucial to ensuring that patient engagement fulfills its promise of democratizing health research rather than reproducing inequities and entrenched power dynamics [18, 22, 33, 34].

The present study

The present study contributes to this evolving field by examining patient partner compensation through the dual lenses of practical implementation and equity considerations. Specifically, it seeks to identify barriers and facilitators to compensation, explore their implications for EDI, and generate insights to inform more inclusive and sustainable compensation practices within POR by exploring the following research questions:

What compensation-related barriers and facilitators shape patient partner engagement in Canadian POR?

How do these practices affect equity, diversity, and inclusion for structurally marginalized groups?

What strategies can be applied to mitigate barriers to equitable compensation and boost diverse and inclusive patient engagement on health research teams?

We examined perspectives of patient partners, researchers, institutional finance/administrative staff, and research funders. Our findings describe current practices that effectively support patient partner compensation, identify persistent challenges, and highlight potential solutions to overcome these barriers. Additionally, the results detail whether and how compensation practices can either limit or promote inclusion of structurally marginalized groups, including but not limited to First Nations, Métis, Inuit, and other Indigenous Peoples; gender-diverse individuals; persons facing housing insecurity or homelessness; those living on low income; members of the 2SLGBTQIA+ community; racialized groups; newcomers to Canada; persons with disabilities; and women, as well as other demographic groups that are underrepresented in POR (e.g., younger adults and men) [11]. Ultimately, this research aims to advance understanding of patient partner compensation as a mechanism for achieving more equitable and inclusive POR. The findings will contribute to improving compensation practices and inclusion efforts, thereby strengthening the overall quality and impact of health research in Canada.

Methods

Research team

Our research team was comprised of a group of patient partners (AS, AM, KS), a research engagement facilitator with lived experience as a patient partner (DPR), and academic and research professionals from the University of New Brunswick (UNB) (AL, KA, MP, SD) and Maritime SPOR SUPPORT (Support for People and Patient-Oriented Research and Trials) Unit (MSSU) (AG, ML, RS, SB, SF). Team composition encompassed variation across sociodemographic characteristics including disability, race/ethnicity, gender identity, sexual orientation, and rurality. Patient partners were contracted as university employees, paid an hourly wage corresponding to the SPOR recommendations current at that time [16], and engaged as full collaborators as per the IAP2 Spectrum of Public Participation [1, 35]. Collectively, research team members held a shared goal of making engagement in POR open to individuals and community members with diverse backgrounds, circumstances and social identities, including but not limited to disability, racial or ethnic background, Indigeneity, socio-economic status, gender, sexuality, and age.

Design

A qualitative descriptive design was employed to explore experiences, perceptions, and practices related to patient partner compensation and equity, diversity, and inclusion in POR. This data-driven approach was appropriate for the nature and intent of our study. Qualitative descriptive methodologies typically follow an inductive process, moving from literal description of the phenomenon under study to interpretation of the meaning of the described experiences, without necessarily relying on or providing evidence for existing theoretical constructs [36]. Qualitative description has shown its utility in health research in that it remains close to the data without excessive theoretical abstraction, producing results that are directly relevant to informant experiences and clinical practice [36–38]. In keeping with this tendency, we chose inductive thematic analysis as our analytic method, with Braun and Clarke’s six step process serving as a framework for generating codes and themes derived from our reading of the experiences and perspectives related by our participants [39–41].

Participants

Participants included individuals from four key groups: (1) Patient partners: Individuals with personal experience of a health issue, or informal caregivers of those individuals such as family and friends, who have drawn on their lived and living experiences to inform health research through a process of meaningful and active engagement on health research teams [1, 28]; (2) Researchers: Individuals with experience conducting patient-oriented health research in Canada, including, but not limited to, professors, clinicians, research associates, postdoctoral fellows, graduate students, and research assistants; (3) Administration and finance staff from interest-holding organizations: Support staff from Canadian universities or other organizations (e.g., health sector agencies, charitable or governmental organizations, provincial/territorial health authorities, and advocacy groups) working in a capacity that supports POR; and (4) Research funding organization employees: Staff from national, provincial, regional, or local funding bodies with experience supporting POR initiatives.

Eligibility criteria included residence in Canada, age 19 years or older, and, for participating patient partners, membership in one or more structurally marginalized or underrepresented groups identified in a pre-screening questionnaire (administered in Qualtrics, see Supplementary file 1). To prevent real or perceived conflicts of interest, individuals affiliated with the University of New Brunswick, where members of the research team are based, were excluded from participation.

Recruitment

Participants were recruited from networks associated with Canada’s Strategy for Patient-Oriented Research (SPOR), such as the MSSU, other provincial SPOR SUPPORT Units, the New Brunswick Primary Care Research Network (PCRN), the SPOR Evidence Alliance, and the SPOR Chronic Disease Networks. In some cases, publicly available contact information from Canadian university and health research organization websites was used to identify eligible participants. Recruitment efforts used snowball sampling and outreach through established research and patient engagement networks, such as UNB’s Centre for Research in Integrated Care (CRIC). These networks, their social media platforms (Facebook, Instagram, X, LinkedIn), and those of partner organizations and patient advisory groups were leveraged to share recruitment materials, such as posters, social media posts, third-party emails, and website notices. Interested participants contacted the research team by email or phone. Informed consent was obtained via email, over the phone, or via postal service (Canada Post) if required. A research team member coordinated scheduling for all participants to take part in either a virtual interview or focus group.

Sampling

A maximum variation sampling strategy was used to facilitate recruitment of a cohort reflecting diversity across a number of characteristics. Prospective patient partner participants completed a pre-screening questionnaire asking about their age, province of residence, experience as a patient partner in academic health research, and self-identification across demographic characteristics including gender, sexual identity, disability, Indigeneity, racialization, newcomer status (having relocated to Canada within the past 5 years), employment, income, and housing security. Similarly, researchers, university and other interest-holding organization staff, and research funding body employees completed a pre-screening questionnaire identifying their province of residence, organizational role, and familiarity or experience with patient-oriented research and patient partner compensation (see Supplementary file 2). Aggregate sample characteristics were reviewed as participants were onboarded, and purposive sampling was applied, prioritizing underrepresented communities and demographic groups based on missing or lacking characteristics.

Honoraria

All participants were offered an honorarium in recognition of their time and contributions. Patient partner participants received a $25 gift card from one of several categories (i.e., grocery, gas, coffee shop, pharmacy, restaurant, Amazon, or pre-paid Visa/Mastercard) and could select their preferred retailer. Researchers, administration and finance staff, and funding agency employees were entered into a draw for a $50 Amazon gift card, with separate draws held for each participant group.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted between December 2023 and July 2025. Semi-structured interview and focus group guides were developed for each category of respondent (Supplementary files 3–6). Initial drafts were based on a review of current literature and subsequently pilot tested and revised in consultation with patient partners and other members of our research team. All participants were invited to complete a brief demographic questionnaire (approximately 5–10 min) via Qualtrics (see Supplementary file 7). Patient partner participants were offered a choice between a virtual focus group or interview; all opted for interviews. Researchers, administration/finance staff, and funding body employees primarily participated in focus groups, with interviews offered when focus group participation was not feasible. Focus groups were conducted separately for each participant category and included two to three participants per group. Although initial focus group targets were set at around eight participants per group, lower interest and scheduling issues resulted in smaller numbers per focus group. A total of 31 individual interviews and 10 focus groups (involving 26 participants) were conducted. For a breakdown of the number of sessions per participant group, see Table 1.

All interviews and focus groups were conducted using Zoom (with phone dial-in available) and lasted 25–95 min. Wherever possible, these sessions were co-facilitated by two research team members: one patient research partner and one research professional. To begin, all participants were reminded that participation was voluntary, that they could withdraw at any time, and that debriefing resources were available if needed. Participants were also encouraged to share any accessibility accommodations that could support their participation.

Data security and confidentiality

Participant information (i.e., name, contact details, professional role, sociodemographic data) was collected solely for study purposes. Identifiers were used only for communication and were not linked to the demographic questionnaire, interview, or focus group responses. Each participant was assigned a unique alphanumeric code comprised of a letter (P for Patients, R for Researchers, S for administration and finance Staff from interest-holding organizations, and F for research Funding body employees) and number. All identifiable information was removed from focus group and interview transcripts. Data files were stored securely on encrypted researcher laptops and uploaded to the project’s SharePoint repository for online collaboration.

Data analysis

Quantitative data from demographic questionnaires was analyzed using SPSS v. 27 [42] to produce descriptive statistics summarizing participant characteristics. Qualitative data from focus groups and interviews were transcribed verbatim using automated transcription software with manual verification and analyzed via inductive thematic analysis assisted by NVivo Version 14 [40, 43, 44]. Two research team members independently coded the transcripts, meeting periodically to discuss and refine themes. As part of our inductive process, we first identified overarching concepts that encapsulated the most salient concerns expressed in the interview and focus group transcripts. Within these conceptual groupings, we identified initial themes. Each of these were categorized as either barriers or facilitators, with accompanying examples of effective strategies to either amplify facilitating practices or mitigate barriers. Eventually, these candidate themes began to seem like mirror images of one another. In order to avoid this redundancy, barrier and facilitator categories were dispensed with. Themes were instead conceptualized as situated along continua, with respondents’ approaches to or experiences with the different concerns reflecting, in different instances, positive or negative impacts on compensation for patient partner’s research engagement, and the resulting implications for EDI. As such, themes should not be read as solely representing barriers or facilitators, but instead as presenting a salient concern expressed in our data set, along with examples of our participants’ experiences of these and ways in which they have been or could be negotiated in practice. Once identified, themes were reviewed in collaboration with a data analysis sub-committee and fixed definitions achieved through a process of discussion and consensus.

Data from demographic questionnaires describe substantial diversity in our study sample, with a majority of participants across categories identifying as having a disability (67%) and/or belonging to a group racialized as non-White (58%). Our sample also included representation from 2SLGBTQ2IA+ communities and people facing homelessness or housing insecurity (19% and 12%, respectively, of participants who completed a demographic survey). Following data review, five interviews and two focus group participants were excluded from analysis because their reported research experiences did not align with the study’s inclusion criteria, while one interviewee requested their data be withdrawn. The final dataset comprised 49 participants, including 25 who participated in individual interviews and 24 who participated across 10 focus groups. Working through an iterative process of inductive thematic analysis, we generated seven themes that organize and describe our data: Clear expectations and processes, Bureaucratic inertia, Reproduction of privilege, Institutional mistrust, Tax and benefit implications, Funding cycles, and Relational dynamics. Direct quotes from the interviews and focus groups are attributed to their source using the corresponding code.

Clear expectations and processes - “Communicating right from the start”

The value of clear expectations and processes was present as a theme across all participant groups, impacting procedural and relational experiences of patient partner compensation. Establishing and maintaining clear expectations among all research team members about patient partner roles and their associated compensation was viewed as essential, and was significantly influenced by the presence or absence of clear policy guidance.

Patient partners placed high value on full and transparent disclosure of compensation details, including amounts, steps for reporting hours, disbursement procedures and timelines, and a clear delineation of patient partner roles and expectations. Having fulsome conversations about compensation early in the research engagement process—“Communicating right from the start and being extremely clear when you’re welcoming someone to a project” (P3)—was thought to also facilitate coherence around role expectations. Researchers, administration and finance staff, and research funding body employees also described the importance of having early conversations to help set expectations and clarify “what is set in stone and what we can flex on” (R2) to build consensus around compensation strategies that are best suited to both patient partners’ and the research project’s needs.

While participants highlighted the need for effective onboarding processes, they also drew attention to the need for communication to be ongoing and bi-directional for the duration of the research engagement. Participants described the importance of continuous transparency when expectations around compensation changed, such as shifting or delayed timelines, and of providing avenues for two-way communication such as regular emails or virtual or in-person meetings.

Clear policy guidance was identified as an important feature that can support clarity of expectations around patient partner compensation. Such guidance was seen as a complementary, institutional analogue to the more interpersonal communication practices described above, facilitating intra-organizational and interdepartmental cooperation around patient partner compensation. Established compensation guidelines, when present, were valued as “clear point[s] of reference” (R15) that could serve to “guide conversations” (S5) between researchers, patient partners, funders, and university and healthcare organization finance departments. As one staff member from a research funding body expressed, “There’s definitely a need across the organization for some guidance around compensation” (F1). For many participants, a lack of clear policies complicated patient engagement by producing uncertainty around accepted compensation practices. One administration and finance staff member noted “a hesitancy to give people cash payments” (S7), even though their institution permitted this with the appropriate documentation. In other cases, researchers found that the lack of internal policy guidance meant there were no formalized procedures for adding patient partners to their institutions’ payroll system: “(W)e don’t have any kind of space in terms of like our paperwork and policies about how to, I don’t know what the word is, employ, work with or compensate a patient partner” (R6).

Sometimes variation in compensation practices over time and across programs and institutions added to the confusion. “(T)he policies and regulations keep changing kind of moment to moment under our feet” (R17). This “lack of consistency” (R5) carried over to patient partners’ experiences, with a particular impact on those working with multiple health research teams. A respondent with experience as both a patient partner and a finance office employee noted, “I’m also a patient partner with another university, and their systems are vastly different and so much easier” (S6).

In some situations, the lack of clear policies had negative consequences for morale. Several researchers described feeling like they were in an adversarial or “combative” relationship with their own institutions. “(I)t’s very confusing when it’s like, this is my institution, we’re supposed to be on the same page. So why do I feel like I have to be fighting you?” (R1). On the other hand, informal networks and alliances within researchers’ home institutions were seen as valuable resources when navigating uncertainty around compensation practices. As one researcher put it, ”Sometimes bumping up against the red tape feels challenging, especially if you’re at it alone. But I feel fortunate I’ve found a bit of a community and people who share those values and so we can advocate together” (R5).

Bureaucratic inertia - “The institution moves much more slowly sometimes than folks need it to move”

Participants from all four groups cited bureaucracy, administrative burden, and the slow pace of institutional processes as concerns, but also noted these could be mitigated by flexibility and responsiveness to patient partners’ needs. Patient partners described the administrative processes required to be compensated for their research engagement, such as onboarding into human resource systems; tracking, reporting, and reconciling work hours; and waiting for payments to be disbursed; as “exhausting” (P8) and “a real test of patience” (P1). Finance office staff at different institutions identified the administrative burden associated with patient partner compensation as a “big barrier” (S4). One researcher summarized these concerns succinctly: “(T)he institution moves much more slowly sometimes than folks need it to move” (R11).

Participants across several respondent groups described this bureaucratic lag as a significant concern, as delays in compensation were thought to be felt more acutely by patient partners from structurally marginalized communities. “I think, especially if you come from, you know, economically denied communities, getting paid in a really efficient manner is important. Sometimes people don’t want to wait one month, two months…” (S8). Workarounds to expedite payment processes or facilitate cash payment were seen as valuable elements of patient engagement. “I think the idea of being able to walk out that day with cash was a bit of a– I think it makes the patient partner feel valued for their time” (S3).

Flexibility in compensation processes emerged as a key strategy for mitigating the impacts of bureaucratic barriers on patient partner engagement. One participant described offering both electronic and paper forms to less technologically inclined patient partners as an example of flexible adaptation. Another described their organization’s compensation policies and practices as continuously evolving. “I take feedback openly all the time. So, people share with me like, ‘Oh, that was really a lot of paperwork,’ or ‘Can’t you offer this?’ We’re always floating ideas back to finance” (F6).

Adaptation of reimbursement policies and practices was also discussed as an adjunct to patient partner compensation. Reimbursement for out-of-pocket costs, though distinct from compensation, can impact patient partner experiences and, as with the timeliness of payments, influence diversity and inclusion in POR. Researchers described the particular importance of being able to pay up-front for accessible transport costs when partnering with a patient living with disability, or of finding workarounds to ensure that they could reimburse childcare expenses for a patient partner traveling from a remote community. In contrast, another participant related being unable to pay up-front for respite care to enable a patient partner to participate in workshops and meetings. “He would have to find it and arrange it and pay for it himself, then we might be able to reimburse him. And for [someone with] lower income that’s just not a possibility” (S11).

Reproduction of privilege - “It’s always the same type of people”

Compensation practices were seen to interact with wider social processes in ways that both enable and inhibit patient engagement, disrupting or reinforcing patterns of power and privilege within health research. Respondents from all four participant groups voiced concern about a lack of diversity in patient engagement. As one patient partner stated, “Folks that get involved in or that have the time, the space and the energy to contribute to research, they are– what’s the word? White, older, middle class and all those pieces that represent power and privilege” (P1). These patterns reinforce existing challenges when trying to engage patient partners from structurally marginalized communities. As the following participant noted:

(I)t’s always the same type of people, because the meetings are during work time. People are like, ‘Well I have school,’ or ‘I have work, I can’t take off an hour to join this.’ And then maybe you’re not compensating them for that time. (S2)

Appropriate compensation was seen as helping to disrupt these processes. One patient partner described accepting compensation “(a)s a matter of principle.”

I will accept compensation all the time. Because how are you going to get variety in research if you’ve got all these goody-two-shoes people saying, ‘Oh, I’m doing it as a volunteer. … I don’t wanna spoil the experience. You don’t even have to pay my expenses.’ It’s like, no. You can’t do that. … How are you gonna get people like me when I was 20? Living in a cheap rental, with kids and with a chronic disease. How are you going to get people like me to come out and be part of your research, when I am so clearly in the demographic that would be useful? (P4)

Similarly, an administration and finance staffer stated, “We feel very strongly that, for equity, we need to compensate people. Otherwise, you are restricting participation to the people who can afford to participate” (S11).

Participants identified demographic factors and aspects of identity, such as disability, housing instability, and living with stigmatized health conditions, that might interact with social hierarchies and systemic inequalities to produce vulnerability as important to consider in the compensation process. Several patient partners noted that, when reporting on experiences with stigmatized health conditions such as substance use or mental health concerns, some might prefer forms of compensation that maintained their anonymity, precluding recognition as co-authors or open participation in things like conferences and presentations. Respondents also discussed the need to consult and adapt compensation practices to meet the needs of unhoused patient research partners. One patient partner remarked, “If somebody doesn’t have a fixed address, then getting compensation to them would be difficult. … I think it’s really important that they have a voice in things” (P9). One researcher described a situation where

…we are paying patient partners who do not have bank accounts, and so they require cash. And we wanted to follow best practice in mental health substance use research by paying cash at the end of every meeting. And again, with the policies, that’s not really clearly detailed. So we’ve had to find some loopholes and some workarounds. (R16)

Another researcher described the added complexity inherent in equitably compensating patient partners living with disability. “(E)ven the hourly rate is quite complex because everybody will take a different amount of time to be able to complete a task. And even an individual themselves can take a different amount of time depending on where they’re at” (R8).

Institutional mistrust - “Some people just don’t want any record of things”

Participants described how historical, lived, and living experiences of discrimination, harm, or inadequate service contributed to mistrust of academic, healthcare, and government institutions among structurally marginalized groups. This mistrust was seen to complicate research engagement and compensation processes. Several respondents described the need to approach compensation in ways that mitigated the impacts of institutional mistrust on research engagement among members of equity-denied communities. Researchers who worked with newcomers to Canada, people who use drugs, unhoused people, and Black and other racialized communities described challenges in engaging members of these groups due to the documentation required to compensate them. “Some people just don’t want any record of things. And so, while we want to have a lot of diversity in our patient partners, that’s a problem when it comes to compensation” (R13). In some cases, people’s participation in a research project was contingent on the level of documentation required. Other researchers described the moral discomfort inherent in requiring documentation for honoraria or other forms of compensation purported to be offered as gifts. “They’re documenting their name, right? But this is about the issue about truly giving a gift. And if I gave a gift to you, [Interviewer], then I wouldn’t expect to collect your name” (R14).

Concerns around institutional mistrust were also voiced by participants working with Indigenous communities. As one person explained,

…often I had to ask Elders to open meetings and do different things. And you know, they [the academic or research institution] always would say to me, you know, ‘We’re gonna pay them this amount of money, but I need their social insurance number.’ I’m like, ‘I’m not asking them. I am not asking an Elder. That is disrespectful and I will not do it.’ I will pay them myself. (P10)

One researcher described how policies at their institution had been adapted to address these concerns. “And so our Indigenous honorarium forms don’t require a SIN [social insurance] number anymore, which is one of the things that folks really didn’t want to give” (R11).

Tax and benefit implications - “The biggest barrier that I find is the taxation piece”

Many participants discussed the challenges and impact of tax reporting requirements on patient partner compensation. In particular, the obligation to report income earned through research engagement affected lower income individuals and those receiving social assistance or disability benefits, as these additional earnings could negatively impact their eligibility for federal and provincial supports. Participants described how these requirements could impact individuals’ willingness or ability to engage in patient-oriented research. Participants frequently voiced sentiments like, “I have to be careful as to what sources of income I earn, otherwise the government is gonna take back whatever benefits that I receive” (P1), and, “If you pull in too much extra money, you could be denied your long-term disability benefits” (P7). Researchers, administration, and finance staff, and funding body employees aired similar concerns, going as far in one case as identifying “the taxation piece” as the “biggest barrier” to equitable compensation (F6). Participants also expressed concern about the impact of income reporting on equity, diversity, and inclusion in POR. As one research funding organization employee phrased it,

(W)e cannot engage people and patients who are the most marginalized because we don’t have an adequate way to compensate them. They deserve compensation and they would accept it if we could give it to them in a way that doesn’t compromise their benefits. (F1)

Income reporting requirements were seen to vary over time and across institutions. Many patient partners described previously being able to receive gift cards without it impacting their tax status or benefit eligibility. However, this less stringent approach to reporting was changing, and participants described research and healthcare institutions trending towards stricter requirements for all forms of compensation, including honoraria and gift cards. Apart from contexts where gift cards were still considered acceptable modes of compensation with no reporting requirements, a variety of strategies were used to circumvent the tax and benefit implications of patient partner compensation. These included breaking payments up across calendar years to avoid exceeding annual thresholds, and in-kind offerings of goods or services such as meals, software, equipment, training opportunities, and university courses.

While working to incorporate innovative solutions to the challenges posed by income reporting requirements, several respondents also described their personal ethical concerns that these policies not only have a chilling effect on research engagement, but also limit economic opportunities for social benefit recipients.

(T)o me it feels like we’re trapping people with disabilities in poverty because we’re not encouraging people to find opportunities that can help them thrive, right? We say, you know, ‘If you make past X amount of money then we’re going to take it all back.’ … And that doesn’t incentivize people to pursue these kinds of opportunities, right? (S11)

Funding cycles - “It means that I tend to engage the same partners repeatedly, which is an equity issue”

The cyclical nature of research funding, and the resulting gaps in finance that can occur between projects, was identified as another compensation-related structural barrier limiting full patient participation in the research process. In particular, participants described how the frequent absence of funding in the early, pre-approval stages of a project made it difficult to compensate patient partners for engagement in the design, planning, funding application, and ethical review stages of research. One researcher explained that, for them, finding ways to compensate patient partners for early engagement often meant “working with people that I’m already funding through some existing thing” (R17). They framed this as an equity issue, as it meant “engaging with the same partners repeatedly”, as opposed to bringing in new, perhaps underrepresented, voices.

On the other hand, administration and finance staff described the benefit of grant application guidelines that increasingly require some consideration of patient and public involvement, which increased the likelihood of funds being allocated for patient partner compensation.

Relational dynamics - “It’s all about relationships, right?”

The relational aspects of research engagement were significant across respondent categories. Compensation was considered an important factor in reducing power disparities and fostering reciprocity and trust among research team members. Relationship building and the social components of research participation were highly valued. As one patient partner put it, “I think the human connection is really important in health research” (P3). This was reiterated by the researchers in our study. “Engagement for community–the people we work with like this–usually it’s beyond the compensation, it’s more about the relationship and feeling like they’re part of the process and the study” (R1). That said, patient partners reported often feeling a power imbalance when engaging in research activities. “I always felt that there is a barrier. There is a barrier between the patient and the researcher. Like, it’s unwritten, undiscussed, unacknowledged” (P2). This disparity was often exacerbated when compensation was lacking or inequitable. “Quite often when I was in these large meetings as a patient partner, I was the only one not being paid to be there” (P10). These sentiments were echoed in the researcher focus groups:

There’s an inherent power imbalance in research that often doesn’t get explicitly called out. Namely, the researcher has more power than the patients… And I think that one way to restructure some of that power imbalance is to look at how we compensate patients who are participating in that research. (R11)

Beyond pay equity, participants emphasized the importance of a values-based approach that “…align(s) what our team or organization or whatever is doing with what people actually want and value” (R17). In this regard, non-monetary aspects of compensation, such as inclusion in team-building activities and being informed about the impacts of their involvement—“Knowing what you did, how it was used, like the value of your contribution” (P6)—were experienced as meaningful adjuncts to compensation that boosted patient partner’s satisfaction and feelings of belonging as members of the research team.

In addition to the social and empathic dimension, building personal relationships was instrumental to compensation, as the trust that developed allowed patient partners to feel more comfortable sharing banking information or divulging details about their financial circumstances. Collectively, building relationships grounded in trust and reciprocity allowed patient-oriented researchers to develop ongoing partnerships with communities that traversed individual funding opportunities. According to one researcher,

It’s all about relationships, right? Even with patient engagement, it’s about relationships and who we have relationships with and how do we work within those relationships? So sometimes, we’re able to offer things. Other times we can’t necessarily, but because we have those relationships, we’re able to continue working with those communities. (R11)

Discussion

This study explored the barriers to and facilitators of patient partner compensation in Canadian health research, with a particular focus on how practices intersect with principles of equity, diversity, and inclusion. Through analysis of perspectives gathered from patient partners, health researchers, and individuals representing institutions with an interest in patient-oriented research, seven interconnected themes emerged: Clear expectations and processes, Bureaucratic inertia, Reproduction of privilege, Institutional mistrust, Tax and benefit implications, Funding cycles, and Relational dynamics. Different aspects of these themes were prevalent in our analyses of data from all four participant groups, with points of emphasis at times shifting within this convergence in accordance with the perspectives being expressed. When discussing Clear expectations, for example, the interpersonal, affective components of communication were more salient to our patient partner participants, whereas respondents from other categories were more apt to discuss communication’s procedural or utilitarian dimensions. Together, our findings demonstrate that compensation practices are shaped by multiple layers of influence, including local and national level policies, research team and institutional cultures, interpersonal relationships and larger social processes, all of which collectively determine how well principles of equity are enacted in patient-oriented health research.

The themes generated through our analysis reinforce growing calls for clear and transparent compensation frameworks that promote equity and trust between patient partners and research teams. Consistent with previous work [25, 30, 34, 45, 46], participants emphasized that early expectation-setting and ongoing, clear, and bi-directional communication are foundational to meaningful engagement. This theme reinforces other findings that describe how a lack of policy consistency across institutions and projects contributes to confusion, frustration, and inequity [22, 26, 45].

The persistence of bureaucratic inertia mirrors challenges identified in previous publications noting that rigid administrative systems often impede timely or fair compensation [3, 18, 22, 31]. Similar to the findings in reviews by Novak-Pavlic et al. [24] and Fox et al. [27], participants in our study described the need for adaptive and flexible processes to overcome administrative barriers. The notion that delays in compensation or untimely or restrictive reimbursement practices can have a disproportionate negative impact on research engagement by people from structurally marginalized communities echoes findings by Becu and Allan [30] and Richards et al. [17]. Crichlow et al. [21] describe a similar concern in their discussion of ‘job security’, and the need to provide consistent, predictable compensation for patient research partners from equity-denied groups, whose lived and living experiences may include social marginalization and economic precarity.

Our analysis identified patient partner compensation as a key inflection point where practices can either disrupt or reproduce societal patterns of privilege. Much scholarship has discussed the ongoing lack of diversity in patient-oriented research [10, 47–50]. A recent survey of Canadian patient research partners [11] found the vast majority of partners identified as urban-dwelling (89.4%), White (84%), Canadian-born (82.9%), female (76.6%), and university educated (70.2%). Similar to other recent publications [17, 19, 27, 34], our study reinforces the idea that while inadequate compensation represents a significant barrier, constraining research engagement to a small, non-representative subset of the larger population, in contrast, adaptive compensation practices can support diversity and facilitate inclusion of patient partners whose social positions may render them more vulnerable.

Further, our findings support the need to take an intersectional approach. Participants voiced their recognition that multiple axes of discrimination [51], based on race, class, gender, disability, citizenship, and other vectors of privilege, condition individual and collective capacity for research engagement. As highlighted by Shimmin et al. [32], patient partners should be understood as a heterogenous group representing diverse economic, social, cultural, subjective and experiential realities. To engage partners from structurally marginalized groups and avoid reifying patient engagement as the domain of whiter, wealthier, and more educated social strata, patient-oriented research teams must acknowledge and try to contend with the ways in which patient partners’ (and other team members’) multiple, overlapping, and mutually constitutive social positions condition their relative privilege and oppression both within and outside of the research team. Our findings are in keeping with Shimmin et al. [32], who advocate for a trauma-informed intersectional approach, and Sayani et al. [50] and Sayani and Maybee [52], who espouse equity-oriented, critical POR. These ways of framing research recognize that health disparities, as well as underrepresentation in research engagement, are undergirded by colonialism, racism, classism, ableism, sexism, heterosexism, and other processes that reinforce social hierarchies and a gradient in health outcomes and experiences. As such, compensation practices that are attuned to the needs of partners from structurally marginalized groups, particularly those inhabiting multiple systemically oppressed social positions, can serve as a valuable fulcrum for disrupting privilege and fostering inclusion. As described by our participants and other researchers, these practices would take into account the simultaneous, multiplicative effects of patient partners’ various social positions and economic circumstances, and strive to provide compensation in ways that are “structurally competent” [52] and commensurate and responsive to patient partners’ living and working conditions, care-giving responsibilities, housing and citizenship status, environmental or geographic barriers, varying capacities to contribute, experiences of trauma, etc.

Previous research supports adapting or waiving compensation-related documentation requirements in order to reduce the negative impacts of institutional mistrust and facilitate research engagement among individuals and communities with historical, lived, and living experiences of trauma, or where such documentation is culturally inappropriate. Guidance from different academic institutions and research networks, for example, discourage requiring Indigenous Elders or knowledge holders to provide SIN numbers, banking information, or signatures, as these kinds of transactional interactions imply the commodification or “sale” of cultural knowledge [53, 54]. Similarly, substantive research exploring strategies to boost equitable participation in POR emphasizes the need to build trust within structurally marginalized communities and develop “psychologically safe” spaces for research engagement [12, 32, 34, 50, 55–57], suggesting that concessions around documentation for communities who may experience stigma, discrimination, or mistrust within academic, healthcare, and government institutions should be considered.

Findings describing the potential impacts of compensation on patient partners’ tax status and benefit eligibility illustrate how institutional structures can perpetuate inequity and limit the inclusivity of POR, particularly for individuals receiving social benefits. These findings corroborate previous research insights. Analyses of and commentaries on effective research engagement emphasize the need to discuss the potential impacts of compensation on income tax and social benefit status with prospective patient partners and offer an informed choice of alternatives that can accommodate individual financial circumstances without jeopardizing income supports [17, 19, 26, 30, 46]. In addition to disincentivizing POR engagement by people receiving disability benefits, policies that explicitly limit people with disabilities’ earning capacity are a form of structural ableism [58–60] that, as our participants describe, institutionalizes poverty.

Our participants’ descriptions of the challenges posed by funding cycles that effectively restrict patient partner compensation for engagement in the planning and application stages of a project are well illustrated in the literature. While studies of POR compensation support our finding that cyclical gaps in research funding can foreclose patient partner involvement in project design and early leadership roles [21, 22, 24, 26], the observation that these practices may also have a homogenizing effect, limiting diversity by restricting patient partner participation in planning and governance roles to those with existing relationships with the research team or institution, is a novel one.

Relational and power dynamics were evident in participants’ accounts, aligning with current discourse on the role of compensation [17, 19, 26] and broader discussions of participatory research ethics and power sharing [12, 21, 25, 34, 52, 57, 61]. Participants espoused the value of a relational ethos in patient-oriented research, which Tynan [62] positions as the inverse of an extractive model of research practice. Consistent with previous research [17, 21, 26, 46, 50], participants described how perceived investment by research teams in partnering communities—via monetary and in-kind payments, capacity building, and the development of long-term, mutually-supportive relationships—contributed to feelings of reciprocity, respect, and belonging. Further, the relational dynamics identified in this study highlight how equitable compensation practices such as offering choice, ensuring pay equity with professional researchers, aligning compensation with partners’ values, and effectively communicating the value of partners’ contributions, can foster connection, trust, and accountability [24, 34, 46, 55]. Building on Shimmin et al. [32], Rao et al. [10], and Sayani and Maybee [52], these findings suggest that reflexivity at the individual and institutional level is vital. Research teams must continually examine how entrenched power structures shape their interactions and actively renegotiate these dynamics to enable transformative collaboration.

Practice recommendations

Our study underscores clear opportunities for advancing more equitable and inclusive compensation practices within POR. Opportunities for change exist at multiple levels: federal and provincial, territorial, or other regional governments; research and other interest-holding institutions; and within research teams. Some of these opportunities are specific to the Canadian regulatory and research environment, while others occur more broadly. Our recommendations for acting on these opportunities are described below and summarized in Supplementary file 8. We recognize that these suggestions should only serve as a starting point. More formal recommendations should be developed and refined via a transparent consultation process that uses a systems perspective and takes direction from patient partners, researchers, research institution and funding body representatives, and other interest holders.

Federal and provincial/territorial governments

Increase income thresholds

Increase income thresholds

Our findings and previous research consistently point to the federal revenue agency requirement to issue a tax form (T4A slip) declaring non-employment income for amounts over $500, coupled with the low income thresholds afforded disability and other social benefit recipients across provincial and territorial jurisdictions, as significant barriers to equitable patient partner compensation. These have the knock-on effect of constraining research engagement by people receiving disability benefits or other means-tested government transfers. Patient partners who want to maintain their eligibility for such benefits must either forgo equitable compensation or reduce the scope of their participation to stay within the designated limits. Our findings suggest that increasing these limits or creating an exemption for research and related activities to allow for more sustained POR participation by people who are receiving social benefits should be considered. Such a change would have the dual benefit of increasing individuals’ earning capacity and facilitating inclusion of their lived and living experiences to help inform, direct, interpret, and enact academic health research. Incorporating first-hand knowledge of the impacts of disability and/or economic marginalization on other social determinants, healthcare service delivery, symptom and treatment burden, and other health experiences and outcomes are key elements of effective POR, and inclusion of these perspectives should be supported.

Promote awareness and clarity around tax regulations

Promote awareness and clarity around tax regulations

Further downstream, increasing awareness of government-mandated income thresholds and regulations around taxable and non-taxable gifts and honoraria among patient-oriented researchers and team members would enable the provision of acceptable alternatives. Clarity around the rules governing these different forms of compensation would alleviate concerns about unintended financial consequences for participants receiving government benefits or living with low income.

Universities, research funding bodies, and other interest-holding organizations.

Integrate national standards into institutional policies

Integrate national standards into institutional policies

On a broad scale, integrating national standards for equitable patient research partner compensation, such as those recently produced by the SPOR Evidence Alliance (28), into local policies would enhance consistency and transparency across regions, research disciplines, and institutions. Adopting a coordinated national approach that allows for regular review and continuous adaptation in response to local conditions would provide a consensus baseline for regional actors to work from, solidifying patient partnerships and advancing Canada’s leadership in ethical, inclusive, and impactful POR.

Simplify administrative processes and provide clear, institution-specific guidelines

Simplify administrative processes and provide clear, institution-specific guidelines

At the level of individual organizations, many participants described confusion around what forms of payment were permissible and how to navigate bureaucratic processes involving contracts, taxes, and reimbursements. Simplifying administrative procedures and developing clear, institution-specific policies that reflect national guidance outlining compensation options, eligibility, and timelines, while allowing for contextual flexibility, would reduce uncertainty and administrative burden for researchers employed by these institutions and their patient partners.

Allow for flexible and responsive disbursement practices

Allow for flexible and responsive disbursement practices

With regard to contextual flexibility, institutions could offer a range of disbursement methods, such as electronic transfers, gift cards, pre-paid cards, cash, direct reimbursements, or in-kind payments, to accommodate patient partners’ varied social and financial realities. Where necessary, documentation requirements for patient partner compensation, such as collection of SINs or signatures, could be waived or adapted, such as by having a witness from the research team sign in their stead. These measures would demonstrate organizational commitment to inclusion and put the principles of accessibility and respect embedded within POR into practice.

Develop bridge funds

Develop bridge funds

To mitigate the impacts of cyclical funding gaps, research institutions engaged in POR could establish “bridge funds” allotted to support compensation for patient partner contributions to research projects that have yet to receive financial support. In doing so, research organizations would reinforce the message that equitable compensation is a core component of ethical and effective POR, not an afterthought. Such changes could facilitate more diverse participation in planning and governance roles by allowing researchers to recruit and engage patient partners with whom they have no pre-existing relationship. Enabling these new connections could open up opportunities to partner with people from structurally marginalized, less frequently engaged communities.

Within research teams

Prioritize communication and relationship-building

Prioritize communication and relationship-building

Research teams play a central role in fostering transparent, reflexive, and equitable compensation practices at the project level. Establishing open, bi-directional conversations about compensation expectations from the start of the collaboration and through the duration of the project helps to build and maintain mutual understanding between team members.

Co-develop compensation plans

Co-develop compensation plans

Compensation plans should be co-developed with patient partners and reviewed and updated collaboratively on an ongoing basis. This approach promotes accountability and supports shared decision-making, intentional power sharing, and genuine, respectful partnership.

Work reflexively; intentionally and actively addressing power dynamics and structural barriers

Work reflexively; intentionally and actively addressing power dynamics and structural barriers

As part of this process, researchers should invest time and energy, paying careful attention and being responsive to the needs of partners from structurally marginalized and equity-denied communities in order to build trust, mitigate the impacts of historical and lived traumas, and enact compensation practices that acknowledge and function to remove or minimize structural barriers. Reflexivity and a commitment to identifying and actively reshaping power dynamics are critical. In this regard, equitable compensation is more than a financial transaction between researchers and patient partners: it is an ethical commitment that recognizes and prioritizes lived experience as a form of expertise equal to academic or professional knowledge.

Collectively, these recommendations can work in concert to help align compensation practices with the core principles of the Strategy for Patient-Oriented Research and emerging institutional frameworks across Canada (28). By embedding reflexivity, flexibility, and an intersectional approach into compensation practices, researchers and their institutions and funders can foster environments where all contributors feel valued, safe, empowered to fully participate, and recognized for their efforts. Such shifts are crucial for realizing the transformative potential of POR, moving beyond symbolic inclusion and toward genuine equity and partnership.

Strengths, limitations & future considerations

A key strength of this study lies in its inclusion of both patient partners and institutional interest holders, enabling a multidimensional understanding of compensation practices. The participatory design and collaborative data collection, analysis, and knowledge translation processes of this study, which prioritized the involvement of patient partners throughout, modeled the equitable engagement principles that we sought to examine (see the attached GRIPP2-SF checklist, Supplementary file 9) [63]. Our use of maximum variation sampling among patient partners was also effective in some regards, as our cohort included more diverse representation across demographic characteristics and dimensions of social identity, including race, Indigeneity, disability, and housing status, than is typical in patient-oriented research.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. While, as described above, our patient partner cohort included significant diversity, we failed to recruit any patient partner participants who identified as newcomers to Canada, nor any residing in the provinces of Quebec, Saskatchewan, Newfoundland and Labrador, or Prince Edward Island, nor Nunavut or the Yukon or Northwest Territories.

When gathering demographic data, we essentially treated race as a dichotomous variable, asking participants whether they identified as a member of a racialized group or not, with Whiteness as an implied default or normative category. This approach not only represents a missed opportunity to gather more precise data about the racial diversity in our sample, but also inadvertently contributes to racial hegemony by positing Whiteness as the ‘normal’ category, dominant in its invisibility, with Black, Indigenous, people of colour, and other ‘racialized as Other’ categories positioned as deviant [64]. In hindsight, better practice would have been to apply a more granular data collection standard, such as that recommended by the Canadian Institute for Health Information [65], or the ‘visible minority’ or ‘population group’ variables used in the Canadian census [66].

We used purposive and snowball sampling, exclusively recruiting participants who were familiar with and supportive of patient research partnership. Future studies could seek to incorporate perspectives from participants who are less familiar with or invested in POR, possibly generating broader insight into potential barriers to equitable compensation. Additionally, comparative and longitudinal studies could examine how evolving compensation policies influence the diversity and sustainability of patient engagement over time.

Some participants in our study demonstrated uncertainty about the distinctions between terms such as patient partner and research participant. This ambiguity was apparent across all participant groups, including researchers, funders, and patient partners themselves, from early in our recruitment efforts. While we attempted to make adjustments, including using consistent phrasing such as ‘patient research partner’ and ‘patient partners who have experience in academic health research’ in recruitment materials and revising our pre-screening survey to include our definition of patient partner (see Supplementary file 10), the persistent confusion underscores the need for continued dialogue to develop shared understanding of what meaningful patient engagement entails in practice. Future studies might investigate how conceptual clarity can be strengthened through shared frameworks, training, and co-developed definitions that resonate across research, institutional, and funding contexts.

Similarly, future research could further explore the ethical dimensions of patient partner compensation, especially as it relates to power dynamics. The concept of patient partner independence was prominent in one interview, where the patient partner participant voiced their concerns that compensation could represent a conflict of interest, potentially biasing research partners in favour of the project funder’s perspective. Though not directly applicable, it may be worth noting that guidance on research incentives for research participants has emphasized the need to balance the size, value, or attractiveness of compensation with the voluntary nature of engagement, such that prospective participants are not likely to disregard risks in favour of research participation [67, 68]. If one extrapolates this thinking to patient research partners, particularly those coming from economically marginalized communities, this concern is potentially magnified. However, patient-oriented researchers increasingly view compensation less as a potentially coercive factor and more as a necessary tool to help mitigate existing structural inequities in power and resources in order to facilitate more equitable research engagement. Studies exploring compensation for research engagement among partners from structurally marginalized communities emphasize that such partners’ relative lack of access to economic power and financial resources make their time extremely valuable [30, 57]. As a result, approaches to compensation that prioritize pay equity and favour consistency and security over precarity represent a “philosophical and practical orientation towards equity during the research process” [21] p. 1) indicative of the larger paradigmatic shift toward patient-oriented and patient-led frameworks occurring in health research [17]. As patient partners on our research team put it, no one would question the ethics of compensating a research assistant working in a similar capacity to a patient research partner; it would in fact be unethical to not offer some form of compensation for such research engagement. Advancing this agenda will not only enhance equity in compensation but also deepen the authenticity and impact of patient partnership in Canadian health research.

Finally, our themes and the suggestions for action derived from them represent a potentially generative starting point for the development of more fully realized policy and practice recommendations. Validation and refinement of these suggestions in collaboration with experienced and prospective patient research partners, patient-oriented researchers, supporting institutions, clinicians, and health research funders is well warranted.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the growing body of evidence demonstrating that patient partner compensation is an essential practice research teams must consider to fully embody POR principles of equity, inclusion, and respect. By examining the barriers and facilitators to patient partner compensation through the lenses of both patient partners and institutional interest holders, this research highlights how structural, procedural, and cultural factors interact to shape the accessibility and fairness of patient engagement.

Our findings underscore that compensation may be complex, but it is reflective of the relational and structural commitments that underpin truly equitable collaboration in patient-oriented research. Equitable compensation practices that are transparent, flexible, and co-developed serve as tangible examples of valuing lived experience and fostering mutual accountability within research partnerships. Advancing clear, inclusive, and intersectionality-informed compensation practices and policies will be essential for realizing the transformative potential of POR across Canada, and is a critical step toward building research systems that genuinely share power, recognition, and impact.
