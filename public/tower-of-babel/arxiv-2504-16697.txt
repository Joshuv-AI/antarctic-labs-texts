On deciding transcendence of power series
Alin Bostan, Bruno Salvy, Michael F. Singer

Abstract
. It is well known that algebraic power series are differentially finite (D-finite): they satisfy linear differential equations with polynomial coefficients. The converse problem—determining whether a given D-finite power series is algebraic or transcendental—is notoriously difficult. We prove that this problem is decidable: we give two theoretical algorithms and a transcendence test that results in an efficient Maple implementation.

Introduction

1.1.
Stanley’s problem and our contribution

A power series
f\in{\mathbb{Q}}[[z]]
is called

equation
P(z,f(z))=0

P\in\mathbb{Q}[x,y]\setminus\{0\}

A classical result, known to Abel (1827) and possibly much older, states that any algebraic power series
f\in{\mathbb{Q}}[[z]]
is

(or,

)—that is, it satisfies a linear differential equation with coefficients in
{\mathbb{Q}}[z]
. Moreover,
the minimal-order nontrivial homogeneous linear differential equation satisfied by
f
has order at most
\deg_{y}(P)
and coefficients in
{\mathbb{Q}}[z]
of degree at most
4\deg_{x}(P)\deg_{y}(P)^{2}

[
13
]
.

Conversely, not every D-finite power series is algebraic; for
instance,
\exp(z)
and
\log(1-z)
are both D-finite and transcendental. Several methods are available to prove the transcendence of
\exp(z)
and
\log(1-z)
(see e.g.,
[
12
]
), but in general it is notoriously difficult to decide if a given D-finite function is algebraic or transcendental. This is the topic of our article.

In his seminal article on D-finite
functions
[
103

, Richard Stanley asked the following question:

“Given the
differential equation

(1)

{L}(f(z))=a_{r}(z)f^{(r)}(z)+\dots+a_{0}(z)f(z)=0

with polynomial coefficients

together with suitable (finitely many) initial conditions, satisfied by a D-finite power series

, give an algorithm suitable for computer implementation for deciding whether

is algebraic.”

For instance,

is a D-finite power series represented by the second-order differential equation
(z-1)f^{\prime\prime}(z)+f^{\prime}(z)=0
and initial conditions
f(0)=0,f^{\prime}(0)=1
,
and such an algorithm would have to recognize the transcendence of

Deciding transcendence of formal power series has many motivations and applications. For
instance, in number theory a first step towards proving the transcendence of a complex number
consists in proving the transcendence of some suitable power series attached to it. Other examples
come from combinatorics, where the nature of generating functions may reveal strong
underlying structures
[
15
]
, and from computer science, where a natural
question is whether algebraic power series are easier to manipulate than transcendental
ones
[
9
]
.

Although Stanley insisted on the practical aspect of the targeted algorithm for deciding
algebraicity of D-finite functions, not even a theoretical one has appeared so far. In this
work, we make progress on Stanley’s question: we give two algorithms that prove the
decidability of the algebraic or transcendental nature of D-finite power series in
{\mathbb{Q}}[[z]]

and we give an (incomplete) transcendence test whose implementation is efficient in practice.

The first theoretical algorithm (
Algorithm

[

]

1.4.
Sufficient transcendence criteria

There exist several criteria that can be used to prove that a given power series
f\in{\mathbb{Q}}[[z]]
is transcendental. They are simply built on
properties of algebraic power series. We recall below two important ones,
Eisenstein’s arithmetic criterion and Flajolet’s analytic criterion.
Although very useful in practice, none of these sufficient criteria is also necessary,
and there are concrete examples that escape them
(see
Example

6
). This observation brings additional motivation to Stanley’s problem
(S)
.

1.4.1.
Eisenstein’s criterion

Definition 2
.

A power series
f=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}
in
{\mathbb{Q}}[[z]]

globally bounded

if it has a non-zero and finite radius of convergence, and if there exists

A famous criterion, stated (and sketchily proved) in 1852
by Gotthold Eisenstein
[

and fully proved one year later by Eduard Heine
[

]
, asserts that if
a power series

is algebraic, then only a finite number of prime numbers can divide the denominators of the coefficient sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}

This is also a consequence of a theorem
5
5

5

Heine’s 1854 result is what is commonly called “Eisenstein’s criterion” in the literature. Hence, strictly speaking there are two distinct criteria, one due to Eisenstein and the other one due to Heine.
It might well be that they coincide for D-finite functions; this is
related to Siegel’s conjecture that the classes of “broad

[

stating that any algebraic power series

This arithmetic criterion immediately implies that the power series

However, when

is not given in closed form, but rather like in

1.4.2.
Flajolet’s criterion

In many situations,
the given D-finite power series

already has integer coefficients, therefore Eisenstein’s criterion is useless.
This is systematically the case in combinatorics when
f(z)
is the generating function of a sequence whose

Much more useful is

One form of the criterion asserts that

For more general and more refined versions on the asymptotic behavior of the coefficients of algebraic power series, we refer to
[

[

This criterion is the most commonly used for transcendence

It allows for instance to show

For instance, the Beukers-Heckman criterion immediately implies that the power series

Fürnsinn and Yurkevich

1.8.
Other approaches to problem
(F)

Another way of seeing that Fuchs’ problem

is decidable, in a

between having a full basis of algebraic solutions and having a finite differential Galois group.
Indeed, since Hrushovski’s seminal article
[

known that one can fully determine algorithmically the Galois group of

[
58
]
, it was only known how to compute the Galois group of

[
28
]
, a condition that can itself be algorithmically tested
[
99
]

The complexity of Hrushovski’s algorithm is not yet fully understood,
and its simplification is the object of ongoing works, e.g.,
[
37
,
105
,
2
]
.
An approach to understanding the Galois group of

by computing
the Lie algebra of its identity component is given in
[
6
,
7
]

and
[
33
]
.
In a different direction, van der Hoeven proposed
in
[
55
]
a symbolic-numeric approach for computing

but for the moment its potential has not been exploited further.

To our knowledge, none of the algorithms mentioned in

Sections

1.7
and
1.8
has been implemented yet, nor are they expected to provide good practical behavior (except perhaps for very moderate orders, as in e.g.,
[

).

Let us finally mention a very recent sufficient (algorithmic)

{\bf(F)}

Assume that the linear differential equation

admits a basis

2.
Solving problem
(S)

For a D-finite power

{\mathbb{Q}}(z)\langle\partial_{z}\rangle

that annihilates

in
{\mathbb{Q}}(z)

The

[
18

starting from any linear differential equation satisfied by

Our solution to Stanley’s problem

2.1.
Properties of
L^{\mathrm{min}}_{f}

In simple terms, the minimal differential operator
L^{\mathrm{min}}_{f}
is a differential analogue for D-finite functions of the more classical notion of minimal polynomial for algebraic power series.
Indeed, any
L\in{\mathbb{Q}}[z]\langle\partial_{z}\rangle

is a left multiple of
L^{\mathrm{min}}_{f}
in the ring
{\mathbb{Q}}(z)\langle\partial_{z}\rangle

L^{\mathrm{min}}_{f}
is an annihilator for
f

But there are two main differences between these similar notions.
One is that, for such an
L

L^{\mathrm{min}}_{f}
, it is in general not the case that the maximal degree of the coefficients of
L
is an upper bound on the maximal degree of the coefficients of

L^{\mathrm{min}}_{f}

[
18
]
for more details.
Another difference important to note is that

does not need to be irreducible in
{\mathbb{Q}}(z)\langle\partial_{z}\rangle

Reducibility can already occur when

is transcendental,
as illustrated by

The same also holds when

is algebraic.
For instance, for the algebraic power series of degree 2

we have that

=\left(\left(1-2z\right)\left(1-4z\right)\partial_{z}+4z-6+\frac{1}{z}\right)\cdot\left(\partial_{z}-\frac{1}{z}\right).

Moreover, this is actually the “generic behavior” for an algebraic power series

4.
Other algorithmic approaches to algebraicity and
transcendence

4.1.
Using the Grothendieck-Katz
p
-curvature conjecture

In the 1970s, Alexander Grothendieck proposed a conjectural “arithmetic” characterization for linear differential operators with coefficients in
{\mathbb{Q}}(z)
: such an operator admits a full basis of algebraic solutions if and only if the same holds for its reductions modulo
p
for almost all primes
p
.
Grothendieck’s conjecture can be seen as a differential generalization
of a particular case, due to Kronecker, of Chebotarev’s theorem: the
roots of a polynomial in
{\mathbb{Q}}[z]
are all rational if and only if the roots of its reductions modulo
p
are in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
for almost all primes
p
.
(See
[
12
,
43
]
, and the references therein.)

Although it remains largely open in the general case, the
p
-curvature conjecture has been proven in several important cases. It was first studied in depth and popularized by Nicholas Katz
[
61
]
, who proved it for the Gauss hypergeometric equations, a result later extended to the generalized hypergeometric equations by Beukers and Heckman
[
8
]
.
More generally, it was proved in
[
61
]
and
[
4
, §III]
for Picard-Fuchs differential operators (satisfied by the periods of families of smooth algebraic varieties) and for certain of their direct factors and subquotients (sometimes called “differential equations coming from geometry”).
In a different direction, the
p
-curvature conjecture was proved by the Chudnovsky brothers
[
26
]
(see also
[
4
, §6]
) for linear differential operators of first order with algebraic function coefficients in
\overline{{\mathbb{Q}}(z)}
.
(This last arithmetic approach was used to prove the algebraicity of
some power series e.g., in
[
64
,
31
,
10
]
.)

Consider now problem
(F)
for any linear differential operator
L
for which the Grothendieck-Katz conjecture is known to hold.
For any fixed prime
p
,
the linear differential operator
L\bmod p
, with
coefficients in
\mathbb{F}_{p}(z)
, admits a basis of algebraic
solutions if and only if it admits a basis of rational
solutions
[
21
]
, and this is verified by a very simple algorithm,
based on checking the nullity of the
p
-curvature of
L
.

In practice, computing
p
-curvatures for a few primes
p
provides a very efficient heuristic: if
L
has several zero
p
-curvatures, then
it very likely admits a full basis of algebraic solutions.
In particular, for problem
(S)
, if the Grothendieck-Katz
conjecture is known to hold for
L^{\mathrm{min}}_{f}
and if
L^{\mathrm{min}}_{f}
has several
zero
p
-curvatures, then
f(z)
is very likely algebraic, otherwise
it is very likely transcendental.
To turn this heuristic into a decision algorithm, one would need an
upper bound on the number of primes
p
for which the
p
-curvature is to be tested. In other words, one needs an
effective
Grothendieck-Katz theorem, similar to effective versions of Chebotarev’s theorem (e.g.,
[
86
]
and the references therein).
For Picard-Fuchs operators, the existence of such results
is alluded to by André
[
4
, §16.3.1]
.
A first complete effective result was recently obtained, for first-order linear operators with rational function coefficients, by Fürnsinn and Pannier
[
43
]
.
Much remains to be done in this direction, for more general classes of linear differential operators.

4.2.
Guess-and-prove

To prove algebraicity of a given power series
f(z)
, a very popular method in computer algebra is to first guess a polynomial
P(z,u)\in{\mathbb{Q}}[z,u]
such that
P(z,f(z))=O(z^{\sigma})
for some value
\sigma
, and then to certify that
P(z,f(z))=0
by manipulating the roots of
P
via the differential operator
L_{P}
whose solution space is generated by these roots.
This strategy is explained for instance in the proof of
[
16
, Corollary 2]
. The guessing part is based on (structured) linear algebra (over
{\mathbb{Q}}
) in size
\sigma

and the most efficient algorithms use Hermite-Padé approximants (which reduce the problem to linear algebra over
{\mathbb{Q}}(z)
in a size much smaller than
\sigma
).
The proving part is based on the computation of
L_{P}
, which
ultimately also relies on linear algebra
[
13
]
, and
sufficiently many initial conditions.
A delicate issue is the choice of
\sigma
; usually one keeps doubling
its value and degree bounds on
P
until a candidate
P
is found.
If
f(z)
is algebraic, then the procedure will eventually discover it (modulo computational difficulties related to the size of
\sigma
that can be huge).

This approach applies to problem
(S)
when
f
is D-finite.
Its strength is that it is able to recognize and prove
algebraicity of
f(z)
even in situations where
f(z)
is not
a
priori
known to be D-finite (e.g., it is given not by an ODE, but by
a different kind of functional equation), see e.g.,
[
16
]
.
The weak point of the approach is that it cannot prove that
f(z)
is transcendental. This is why it is usually used in conjunction with other heuristics such as computations of
p
-curvatures, or numerical computations of local monodromies.

4.3.
Factoring

If the input
L
is checked to be irreducible, then its solution
f
is algebraic if and only if all solutions of
L
are algebraic (by Proposition 2.5 in
[
96
]
), and this can be tested using Singer’s 1979 algorithm
[
96
]
.
But if
L
is reducible,
then it is
a priori
not clear how to combine factoring algorithms
[
57
,
46
]
and Singer’s algorithm
[
96
]
in order to solve Stanley’s problem
(S)
.
One practical way is: factor the input
L=AB
for irreducible
A
of order less than the order of
L
and check if
A
has a basis of algebraic solutions.
If yes, then we cannot conclude.
(See the examples at the end of
Section

5.2
.)
If no, then
A
has no nontrivial algebraic solutions, and there are two possible cases.
Either
g\coloneq B(f)
is nonzero, in which case it is transcendental (as it is a nontrivial solution of
A
), hence
f
itself is transcendental (otherwise
g
would be algebraic).
Or,
g=0
, and we can repeat the whole procedure on
B
.
This procedure is guaranteed to succeed in the particular case when
L
factors as a product of irreducible operators each of them having no algebraic solutions.

By looking closely at the indicial polynomials
I_{p,q}(z)
, we made a few additional observations.
First, the degree of
I_{p,q}(z)
, that is the order of
L^{\mathrm{min}}_{f_{p,q}}
, is equal to
\lfloor(p+q)^{2}/4\rfloor
except when
p
and
q
are both even and equal, in which case it equals
p^{2}-1
.
In particular, the order of
L^{\mathrm{min}}_{f_{p,q}}
is equal to that of
L^{\mathrm{min}}_{f_{q,p}}
in the 45 cases.
Second, if
p
is odd or if
p>q
, then
I_{p,q}(z)
factors

z^{p+q-1}(z-1)^{p+q-3}\cdots(z-r)^{p+q-2r-1}
,
where
r=\lfloor(p+q)/2\rfloor-1
; when
p
and
q
are
both even and equal, then
I_{p,q}(z)
equals
z^{2p-1}\cdot(z-1)^{2p-3}\cdots(z-(p-2))^{3}
.
In the remaining case (
p<q
with even
p
), the previous pattern may break; for instance,

I_{2,4}(z)=z^{5}(z-1)^{3}(z-\frac{1}{2})
.
We leave the proof of these experimentally observed formulas, and
their extension to arbitrary
p,q
, as open problems.
(Similar considerations for the exponential generating versions of
{f_{p,q}}
appear in
[
18
, Question 5.2]
.)

Appendix A
Generalized Apéry series

A.1.
A multiple binomial sum

A direct application of Flajolet’s criterion proves the
transcendence of the following
family of power series, extending the one in
Section

5.3
.

Proposition 18
.

Let
(p_{0},\dots,p_{m})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{m+1}
with
p_{0}\geq 1
. Then the
power series

f_{\mathbf{p}}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}^{p_{0}}\binom{n+k}{k}^{p_{1}}\binom{n+2k}{k}^{p_{2}}\dotsm\binom{n+mk}{k}^{p_{m}}\right)z^{n}

is transcendental if and only if
p:=p_{0}+\dots+p_{m}>2
.

Proof.

The case
p>2
is covered by Flajolet’s criterion. Asymptotically, the

n
th coefficient
S_{\mathbf{p}}(n)
of
f_{\mathbf{p}}(z)
behaves like

(4)

S_{\mathbf{p}}(n)\sim\gamma(\pi n)^{\frac{1-p}{2}}\beta^{n},\quad n\rightarrow\infty

with
\gamma
and
\beta
algebraic
[
78
]
.
If
p>2
is odd, the exponent of
n
is a negative integer making
f_{\mathbf{p}}(z)
transcendental; if
p
is even,
\Gamma((3-p)/2)
is
a rational multiple of
\sqrt{\pi}
and thus
\gamma\Gamma((3-p)/2)\pi^{(1-p)/2}
is an algebraic multiple of
\pi^{1-p/2}
,
which is not algebraic for
p>2
.

For
p=1
, the series
f_{(1,0,\dots,0)}
is the rational
1/(1-2z)
.

Finally, for
p=2
, either
p_{0}=2
and all the other
p_{i}
are 0, in
which case
f_{\mathbf{p}}=(1-4z)^{-1/2}
, or
p_{0}=1
,
p_{j}=1
for
one
j\neq 0
and all other
p_{i}
are 0. In that case, the power series
can be seen to be algebraic as the diagonal of a bivariate rational
function. Here, the rational
function

\frac{1}{1-z(1+y)(y+(1+y)^{j})}=\sum_{n\geq 0}z^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}y^{n-k}(1+y)^{n+jk},

has a Taylor expansion whose coefficient of
z^{n}y^{n}
is the desired

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\binom{n+jk}{k}.\qed

A.2.
Generic diagonals in more than 2 variables are
transcendental

It is well known that the Apéry power series
f_{2,2}
can be written as the diagonal of a rational power series in 4 variables, e.g., as the diagonal of

1/((1-x-y)(1-t-z)-xyzt)

[
104
, Thm. 1.1]

or of

1/(((1-z)(1-t)-x)(1-y)-xyzt)
(see
Section

5.3
).
From such a diagonal representation, the approach of analytic
combinatorics in several variables (ACSV) allows to deduce the
asymptotics of the
n
th Apéry number
A_{n}
as in
Example

5
, see e.g.,
[
79
, Example 1]
.
More generally, the generalized Apéry power series
f_{p,q}
can be written as the diagonal of a rational power series in
m=p+q
variables
with nonnegative coefficients
, e.g., via (
3
).
Now,
[
79
, Result 1]
states that under certain assumptions that hold generically,
the
n
th coefficient of the diagonal of a rational power series in
m
variables with nonnegative coefficients grows asymptotically like
\gamma\beta^{n}n^{r}
where

r=(1-m)/2
and
\gamma/(2\pi)^{r}\in{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}
.
(This is coherent with the asymptotics (
4
) in the
proof of
Proposition

18
.)
Combined with Flajolet’s criterion, the formula of
[
79
, Result 1]
implies that diagonals of “generic” rational functions with
nonnegative coefficients are transcendental if
m>2
; conversely, the
bivariate case is algebraic by the result of Pólya and
Furstenberg.
Note that the genericity assumption is essential, as shown by the example of the diagonal of

1/(1-x-y-yz-xyz)
, equal to
f_{1,1}(z)
which is algebraic by

Proposition

18
, or by the algebraic examples of
Section

5.2
.
