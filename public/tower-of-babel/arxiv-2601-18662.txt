A Unique Inverse Decomposition of Positive Definite Matrices under Linear Constraints Thanks: Submitted to SIMAX.
Yan Dolinsky, Or Zuk

Abstract
We study a nonlinear decomposition of a positive definite matrix into two components: the inverse of another positive definite matrix and a symmetric matrix constrained to lie in a prescribed linear subspace. Equivalently, the inverse component is required to belong to the orthogonal complement of that subspace with respect to the trace inner product. Under a sharp nondegeneracy condition on the subspace, we show that every positive definite matrix admits a unique decomposition of this form. This decomposition admits a variational characterization as the unique minimizer of a strictly convex log-determinant optimization problem, which in turn yields a natural dual formulation that can be efficiently exploited computationally. We derive several properties, including the stability of the decomposition. We further develop feasibility-preserving Newton-type algorithms with provable convergence guarantees and analyze their per-iteration complexity in terms of algebraic properties of the decomposed matrix and the underlying subspace. Finally, we show that the proposed decomposition arises naturally in exponential utility maximization, a central problem in mathematical finance.



keywords

positive definite matrices, inverse-based matrix decomposition, log-determinant optimization, Newton-CG, exponential utility maximization

Introduction

We study a nonlinear decomposition of a symmetric positive definite (SPD) matrix
A\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}
of the form
A=(B^{*})^{-1}+C^{*}
, where the components are constrained such that
C^{*}\in S
and
B^{*}\in S^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}
for a prescribed linear subspace
S\subset\mathbb{S}^{n}
. Our main contribution is a global existence and uniqueness theorem for this decomposition, valid for any subspace
S
satisfying the sharp nondegeneracy condition
S\cap\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}=\{0\}
. By intertwining matrix inversion with these dual linear constraints, the result provides a canonical algebraic decomposition that serves as a structured, non-Euclidean analogue to the classical orthogonal decomposition of symmetric matrices.

Our proof proceeds by characterizing the decomposition as the unique stationary point of a strictly convex log-determinant functional. Log-determinant objectives are classical and arise throughout convex analysis, matrix theory, information geometry, and statistics (see, e.g.,
[
3
,
5
,
1
,
24
]
); furthermore, nonlinear projections under Bregman divergences were studied in
[
8
]
. Here, however, the optimization problem serves as a
tool
rather than the primary object of study. The resulting theorem is an exact algebraic statement about matrix structure: for every admissible subspace
S
, the decomposition exists globally, is unique, and depends smoothly on
A
. The feasibility condition on
S
is shown to be both natural and essentially necessary.

Beyond existence and uniqueness, we establish several structural properties of the decomposition.
First, we show that it is compatible with symmetry:
if a finite orthogonal group acts by conjugation on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
,
and both
A
and the constraint subspace
S
are invariant under this action,
then the resulting matrices
B^{*}
and
C^{*}
inherit the same invariance.
This yields automatic reductions in dimension and computational complexity
in the presence of symmetry.
Second, we derive algebraic consequences including determinant inequalities
and a precise relationship between the decomposition of
A

and that of its inverse
A^{-1}
. We also prove that the decomposition is stable.
These properties are not generic features of convex optimization problems,
but instead reflect the specific geometric structure of the decomposition.

Gaussian maximum-likelihood estimation under structural constraints on the
inverse covariance (precision) matrix has been studied extensively in the
statistics literature, beginning with classical covariance selection

[
7
]
and extending to models with more general linear
constraints on the concentration matrix

[
22
,
23
,
27
]
.
In this setting, the constrained Gaussian maximum-likelihood estimation problem
corresponds to the dual optimization problem associated with our decomposition.
The present paper takes a different perspective, focusing on a deterministic
inverse-based decomposition of an arbitrary positive definite matrix.
We establish global existence, uniqueness, stability, symmetry inheritance, and
develop structure-exploiting Newton-type algorithms for computing the
decomposition, hence the results in the current paper go beyond the scope of the covariance
selection literature.

From an algorithmic perspective, the variational characterization
leads naturally to Newton-type methods with feasibility-preserving line search.
We provide explicit expressions for gradients and Hessians,
prove global convergence with local quadratic rates,
and give detailed per-iteration complexity estimates.
The framework accommodates structured settings such as banded,
Toeplitz, and group-invariant matrices,
where the cost of each iteration can be reduced substantially
using structure-exploiting linear algebra
(see, e.g.,
[
13
,
2
]
).

Finally, we illustrate the theory with applications.
In particular, we show that the decomposition arises naturally
in a Gaussian exponential-utility maximization problems in mathematical finance,
where
B^{*}
and
C^{*}
admit clear economic interpretations
related to information constraints and hedging structure.
These applications demonstrate that the decomposition
is not merely an abstract construction,
but a flexible tool that appears across several domains.
Possible directions for future research include applications to structured covariance estimation and precision-matrix modeling.

2
Decomposition Theorem

Let
\mathbb{S}^{n}
denote the space of symmetric real
n\times n
matrices. Let

\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n}
be the (open) cone of positive-definite matrices, and let

\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n}
be the (closed) cone of positive semidefinite matrices.

We arrive at the main result of the paper.

Theorem 1
(General structured decomposition).

Let
A\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}
. Let
S\subset\mathbb{S}^{n}
be a linear subspace, and let

S^{\perp}:=\{\,X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}:\ \tr(XY)=0\ \ \forall\,Y\in S\,\},

the orthogonal complement with respect to the trace inner product

\langle X,Y\rangle:=\tr(XY)
.

Assume that
S
contains no nonzero positive semidefinite matrix, that is,

S\cap\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}=\{0\}.

(1)

Then there exists a
unique
pair
(B^{\ast}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}\cap S^{\perp},C^{\ast}\in S)
such that

A={B^{\ast}}^{-1}+C^{\ast}.

(2)

The proof of Theorem
1
will follow two major stages. First, in Lemma
2
we show that the set of matrices
C
yielding a positive definite matrix
A-C
is bounded.

Then, we frame the decomposition problem as a log determinant maximization convex problem, guaranteeing a unique solution.

Lemma 2
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{n}
and let
S\subset\mathbb{S}^{n}
be a linear subspace satisfying (
1
).
Define the feasible set

\Omega_{C}:=\{\,C\in S:A-C\succ 0\,\}.

(3)

Then
\Omega_{C}
is a bounded subset of
S
. In particular, its closure
\overline{\Omega_{C}}
is compact.

Proof.

Assume by contradiction that
\Omega_{C}
is unbounded. Then there exists a
sequence
\{C^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset\Omega_{C}
with
\|C^{(k)}\|_{F}\underset{k\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\infty
, where
||\cdot||_{F}
is the Frobenius norm. Define the normalized matrices

H^{(k)}:=\frac{C^{(k)}}{\|C^{(k)}\|_{F}}\in S.

(4)

Since
\|H^{(k)}\|_{F}=1
, the sequence
\{H^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
lies in a compact set, and therefore it has a converging subsequence
\{H^{(k_{j})}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
with

H^{(k_{j})}\underset{j\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}H
for some
H\in S
with
\|H\|_{F}=1
.
Take the corresponding subsequence
\{C^{(k_{j})}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
of
\{C^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
. The condition
A-C^{(k_{j})}\succ 0
yields for every fixed nonzero vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\frac{v^{\top}C^{(k_{j})}v}{\|C^{(k_{j})}\|_{F}}<\frac{v^{\top}Av}{\|C^{(k_{j})}\|_{F}}\underset{j\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0,

(5)

where the limit on the right-hand side follows from the assumption

\|C^{(k)}\|_{F}\underset{k\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\infty
. But continuity of quadratic forms yields

\frac{v^{\top}C^{(k_{j})}v}{\|C^{(k_{j})}\|_{F}}\;\underset{j\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\;v^{\top}Hv.

(6)

Hence
v^{\top}Hv\leq 0
for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus,
H\preceq 0
. But
H\neq 0
and
H\in S
, and so
-H\in S
satisfies
-H\succeq 0
, in
contradiction to
S\cap\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}=\{0\}
.
Therefore
\Omega_{C}
must be bounded.

We next prove the main theorem by casting a log-det optimization problem and showing that the trace conditions for
B
are optimality conditions for the problem.

Proof.

Step 1: Reduction of the decomposition problem to a stationarity condition.

Let
D_{1},...,D_{m}\in S
be a basis for
S
.
Define the linear bijection

C(x):=\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{k}D_{k},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

(7)

and the corresponding feasible parameter set

\Omega_{x}:=\{\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:A-C(x)\succ 0\,\}.

(8)

Hence the feasible set of matrices
\Omega_{C}
defined in eq. (
3
) satisfies

\Omega_{C}=C(\Omega_{x})
.

For
x\in\Omega_{x}
let

M(x):=A-C(x),\qquad B(x):=M(x)^{-1},\qquad\Phi(x):=-\log|M(x)|.

(9)

Using the chain rule for
M
and
-log|\cdot|
we obtain

\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x_{k}}(x)=\tr\big(B(x)D_{k}\big),\qquad k=1,\dots,m.

(10)

Thus a pair
(B,C)\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}\times S
with
A=B^{-1}+C
and
\tr(BD_{k})=0
for all
k
corresponds
precisely to a parameter
x
such that
C=C(x)
and

\nabla\Phi(x)=0.

(11)

Step 2: Existence and uniqueness of a minimizer.

The map
M(x)
is affine and injective, and
-\log|\cdot|
is strictly convex on

\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}
, hence
\Phi
is strictly convex on the convex set
\Omega_{x}
.
If a sequence
\{x^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
approaches
\partial\Omega_{x}
, then
|M(x^{(k)})|\to 0
and
\Phi(x^{(k)})\to+\infty
as
k\to\infty
.

Since
A\succ 0
and
C(0)=0
, we have
0\in\Omega_{x}
and
\Omega_{x}
is nonempty.
By Lemma
2
, the set
\Omega_{C}
is bounded.
Thus
\Omega_{x}=C^{-1}(\Omega_{C})
is bounded as well, and
\overline{\Omega_{x}}

is compact.

Extend
\Phi
to
\overline{\Omega_{x}}
by setting
\Phi=+\infty
on

\partial\Omega_{x}
.
Lower semicontinuity of
\Phi
and compactness of
\overline{\Omega_{x}}
yield
a minimizer
x^{\ast}\in\Omega_{x}
, and strict convexity implies that

x^{\ast}
is unique. Since
\Phi
is differentiable and strictly convex on the open set
\Omega_{x}
, any stationary point is the global minimizer. Thus the unique
minimizer
x^{\ast}
is also the unique point in
\Omega_{x}
satisfying

\nabla\Phi(x^{\ast})=0
.

Define
C^{\ast}:=C(x^{\ast})
and
B^{\ast}:=(A-C^{\ast})^{-1}
.
Then the pair
(B^{\ast},C^{\ast})\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}\times S
satisfies (
2
).
Uniqueness of
x^{\ast}
and the equivalence in Step 1 imply the uniqueness of the decomposition
(
2
).

Remark 3
.

A sufficient condition for (
1
) is the existence of a
matrix
\hat{B}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}
such that

\tr(\hat{B}D)=0,\ \forall D\in S.

Conversely, the condition (
1
) is necessary for the existence of a minimizer of (
14
). Suppose there exists a matrix
H\neq 0
satisfying
H\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}\cap S
. Define
C_{t}=-tH
. Then
A-C_{t}=A+tH\in\Omega_{C}
for all
t\geq 0
, hence the feasible set
\Omega_{C}
is unbounded. Therefore,
|A-C_{t}|\underset{t\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\infty
hence the objective
-\log|A-C_{t}|
is unbounded below.

2.1
A dual variational formulation

The decomposition of Theorem
1
admits a natural
primal-dual interpretation. This viewpoint clarifies the variational structure
of the problem and suggests alternative algorithmic approaches. The dual formulation below coincides formally with the Gaussian maximum-likelihood objective under linear constraints on the precision matrix, but is used here in the context of a deterministic decomposition.

Primal formulation.

Recall the feasible set

\Omega_{C}:=\{\,C\in S:A-C\succ 0\,\},

(12)

and the strictly convex objective

\Phi_{A}(C):=-\log|A-C|,\qquad C\in\Omega_{C}.

(13)

The matrix
C^{\ast}
in the decomposition is the unique minimizer of

\min_{C\in S,\;A-C\succ 0}\;-\log|A-C|.

(14)

Dual formulation.

The primal problem (
14
) can be written as minimizing
-\log|M|
over
M\succ 0
subject to the linear constraint
A-M\in S
, where
M=A-C
is an auxiliary variable.
The associated Lagrangian involves a dual variable
B\in S^{\perp}
.
Eliminating
M
via the first-order optimality condition
M^{-1}=B
yields the
dual problem stated below:

Let
(B^{\ast}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}\cap S^{\perp},\,C^{\ast}\in S)
be the unique pair satisfying
(
2
). For any
B\in S^{\perp}
and
C\in S
we have
\tr(BC)=0
hence
\tr\big((A-C)B\big)=\tr(BA)
.
The function

\Psi_{A}(B):=\log|B|-\tr(BA),\qquad B\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}\cap S^{\perp},

is strictly concave and satisfies the first-order optimality condition

\nabla\Psi_{A}(B^{\ast})={B^{\ast}}^{-1}-A\quad;\quad\tr\!\big(({B^{\ast}}^{-1}-A)H\big)=0\>,\>\forall H\in S^{\perp}.

Moreover, using the decomposition
A={B^{\ast}}^{-1}+C^{\ast}
with
C^{\ast}\in S
, we have for every
H\in S^{\perp}
,

\tr\big(({B^{\ast}}^{-1}-A)H\big)=-\tr(C^{\ast}H)=0.

Hence
B^{\ast}
satisfies the first-order optimality conditions for
\Psi_{A}
.
Since
\log|B|
is strictly concave on
\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}\cap S^{\perp}
, we conclude that
B^{\ast}
is the unique maximizer of the dual problem

\max_{B\succ 0,\;B\in S^{\perp}}\log|B|-\tr(BA).

(15)

Since the primal problem (
14
) is convex and strictly feasible,
strong duality holds, and the primal and dual optimal values coincide. Accordingly, the dual problem (
15
) provides an alternative computational
route to the decomposition.
One may optimize directly over
B\in S^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}
using standard iterative methods, including Newton-type schemes with feasibility-preserving backtracking, in complete
analogy with the primal algorithms of Section
3
.
The primal formulation is preferable when
m=\dim S
is small, while the dual
formulation may be advantageous when
\dim(S^{\perp})
is comparatively small or when the
constraint structure is naturally expressed in terms of
B
.

2.2
Properties of the decomposition

In this subsection we collect several properties of the
variational characterization of the decomposition
(
2
).

1.

Action by conjugation.

Let
O(n)
denote the set of all
n\times n
orthogonal matrices.
For
P\in O(n)
, consider the action on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
by conjugation,

D\mapsto PDP^{\top}
(
P^{\top}
denotes transposition).
From the equality

\tr\!\left(PB^{\ast}P^{\top}\,PDP^{\top}\right)=\tr(B^{\ast}D)=0\>,\qquad\forall D\in S,

we conclude that

PAP^{\top}=(PB^{\ast}P^{\top})^{-1}+PC^{\ast}P^{\top}

is the unique decomposition (
2
) of
PAP^{\top}

associated with the subspace

\tilde{S}:=PSP^{\top}.

Next, we describe the following inheritance of group symmetry.
Let
G\subset O(n)
act on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
by conjugation,

D\mapsto PDP^{\top}.

Assume that
A
is
G
-fixed, i.e.,

PAP^{\top}=A\quad\forall P\in G,

and that
S
is
G
-invariant, i.e.,

PSP^{\top}=S\quad\forall P\in G.

Then the decomposition is
G
-fixed:

PB^{\ast}P^{\top}=B^{\ast},\qquad PC^{\ast}P^{\top}=C^{\ast},\quad\forall P\in G.

(16)

This follows from the above analysis and the uniqueness of the
decomposition (
2
).

2.

Determinant inequality.

From the primal representation,

-\log|A|=-\log|A-0|\;\geq\;-\log|A-C^{\ast}|,

where the inequality follows from optimality of
C^{\ast}
.
Hence

-\log|A|\geq-\log|{B^{\ast}}^{-1}|,

which implies

|{B^{\ast}}^{-1}|\;\geq\;|A|,\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad|B^{\ast}|\leq|A^{-1}|.

(17)

3.

Decomposition of the inverse.

Define the transformed subspace

\hat{S}:=A^{-1}SA^{-1}=\{\,A^{-1}DA^{-1}:D\in S\,\}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n},

and set

\hat{B}:=AB^{\ast}A,\qquad\hat{C}:=A^{-1}C^{\ast}A^{-1}.

(18)

Then, for all
D\in S
,

\tr(\hat{B}\,A^{-1}DA^{-1})=\tr(B^{\ast}D)=0.

Consequently,

\hat{B}\in\hat{S}^{\perp},\quad\hat{B}\succ 0,\quad\hat{C}\in\hat{S},\quad A^{-1}=\hat{B}^{-1}+\hat{C}.

By uniqueness of the decomposition (
2
)
(and since
\hat{S}
satisfies (
1
)),

(\hat{B},\hat{C})\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}\times\hat{S}

is the unique decomposition of
A^{-1}
associated with
\hat{S}
.

4.

Stability of the decomposition.

Assume that
\Theta\subset\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}
is an open set and that

\min_{D\in\Theta,\;C\in S}\|D-C\|_{F}>0.

Consider the map

F:\Theta\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

defined by

F\big(D,(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\big):=\Bigl(\tr\bigl((D-\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{k}D_{k})^{-1}D_{1}\bigr),\ldots,\tr\bigl((D-\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{k}D_{k})^{-1}D_{m}\bigr)\Bigr).

Observe that the Jacobian of
F
with respect to
x
is the Gram matrix

\left[\frac{\partial F(D,x)}{\partial x}\right]_{ij}=\tr\bigl((D-\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{k}D_{k})^{-1}D_{i}(D-\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{k}D_{k})^{-1}D_{j}\bigr).

Moreover, the Fréchet derivative of

F(D,x)=(F_{1}(D,x),\ldots,F_{m}(D,x))

with respect to
D
is given by

\frac{\partial F_{i}(D,x)}{\partial D}(\Upsilon)=-\tr\bigl((D-\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{k}D_{k})^{-1}\Upsilon(D-\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{k}D_{k})^{-1}D_{i}\bigr).

Next, let
x=x(D)
,
D\in\Theta
denote the unique solution of the equation

F(D,x)=0.

From the proof of Theorem
1
, it follows that

C^{*}:=C^{*}(A)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}{x}_{k}(A)D_{k}.

By the implicit function theorem,

\frac{\partial x}{\partial D}(\Upsilon)=-\left(\frac{\partial F(D,x)}{\partial x}\right)^{-1}_{x=x(D)}\frac{\partial F(D,x(D))}{\partial D}(\Upsilon).

Consequently, the map

A\mapsto C^{*}(A)

is Lipschitz continuous on
\Theta
, with a Lipschitz constant depending on
\min_{D\in\Theta,\;C\in S}\|D-C\|_{F}
and on the base
D_{1},...,D_{m}
. Since

B^{*}=(A-C^{*})^{-1}
, the same stability property holds for
B^{*}
.
Equivalently, the Lipschitz constant is governed by the conditioning of the
Jacobian
U\mapsto\proj_{S}(B^{*}UB^{*})
on
S
and may deteriorate near
degenerate instances.

3
Algorithms for Computing the Decomposition

In this section we discuss numerical methods for computing the unique pair

(B^{\ast},C^{\ast})\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}\times S
from
Theorem
1
.
Writing

C(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{k}D_{k}

and

\Phi(x):=-\log|A-C(x)|
,
the decomposition is obtained by solving the convex optimization problem

x^{\ast}\;\in\;\argmin_{x\in\Omega_{x}}\;\Phi(x),

(19)

and then setting
C^{\ast}=C(x^{\ast})
and
B^{\ast}=M(x^{\ast})^{-1}
.

This formulation yields a smooth, strictly convex optimization problem over an
open convex domain.
Our focus here is not on advocating a particular optimization algorithm, but on
understanding how
algebraic structure
in the data matrix
A
and in the
constraint subspace
S
affects the computational complexity of solving this
problem.

Throughout, we use a
Newton-CG
method as the default algorithm.
This choice avoids explicit formation of the Hessian and replaces the Newton
system solve by a matrix-free conjugate-gradient iteration.
Exact Newton methods, based on forming and factorizing the full Hessian, are
used only when
m=\dim S
is sufficiently small that the associated
m^{3}
cost
is negligible.
Newton-CG is particularly convenient in this setting because all derivatives
of
\Phi
admit closed-form expressions in terms of linear solves with

M(x)=A-C(x)
, allowing the impact of algebraic structure (such as sparsity,
bandedness, Toeplitz structure, or symmetry) to be tracked explicitly.

A reference Python implementation of all algorithms described in this section,
including structure-exploiting variants, is freely available at

github.com/orzuk/ConstrainedDecomposition
.

A detailed demonstration of our implementations on illustrative structural examples is available in Appendix
A
.

3.1
Derivatives

Recall the parametrization
M(x)=A-C(x)
and
B(x)=M(x)^{-1}
, and the gradient
elements
\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x_{k}}(x)=\tr\big(B(x)D_{k}\big)
given in
(
10
). Differentiating once more yields the Hessian

\frac{\partial^{2}\Phi}{\partial x_{k}\partial x_{\ell}}(x)=\tr\big(B(x)D_{k}B(x)D_{\ell}\big),\qquad k,\ell=1,\dots,m.

(20)

Under the standing assumption
S\cap\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}=\{0\}
, the function
\Phi
is strictly
convex on
\Omega_{x}
, and hence
H(x)\succ 0
for all
x\in\Omega_{x}
.

All derivatives of
\Phi
are computed using explicit analytic expressions and
require only applications of
M(x)^{-1}
, implemented through a factorization
or structure-exploiting solver for
M(x)
. The inverse
B(x)
is never formed
explicitly, and no numerical differentiation is used.

Newton-CG requires only Hessian-vector products. Given
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, define

D(v):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}v_{\ell}D_{\ell}.

(21)

Then

\big(H(x)v\big)_{k}=\tr\big(B(x)D_{k}B(x)D(v)\big),\qquad k=1,\dots,m,

(22)

which can be evaluated using the same linear solves with
M(x)
. This matrix-free
access to second-order information is analogous in spirit to reverse-mode
automatic differentiation, but here all derivatives are available in closed
form.

3.2
Newton-CG with feasibility-preserving backtracking

We compute the minimizer of
\Phi
using a Newton-CG method combined with
feasibility-preserving Armijo backtracking.

At a current iterate
x\in\Omega_{x}
, let
g=\nabla\Phi(x)
and
H=\nabla^{2}\Phi(x)
.
The Newton direction
d
is defined implicitly as the solution of

H(x)d=-g(x),

(23)

which is computed using conjugate gradients with Hessian-vector products

p\mapsto H(x)p
given by (
22
).
Step sizes are chosen by backtracking to ensure
M(x+td)\succ 0
and sufficient decrease of
\Phi
.

Algorithmic details, including evaluation of
\Phi
, its gradient, and
Hessian-vector products, are given in Appendix
B
.

Convergence.

The objective
\Phi(x)=-\log|A-C(x)|
is strictly convex and self-concordant on

\Omega_{x}
, as it is the composition of the self-concordant log-determinant barrier with an affine map.
By Theorem
1
and Lemma
2
,
the sublevel set
\{x:\Phi(x)\leq\Phi(0)\}
is nonempty, compact, and bounded away from
\partial\Omega_{x}
.
Consequently, Newton-CG with feasibility-preserving backtracking is globally convergent (provided the CG residual is driven below a standard relative tolerance) from any feasible initialization.
Once the iterates enter a neighborhood of the unique minimizer
x^{\ast}
, full Newton steps are accepted and the method exhibits a local quadratic convergence rate; see, e.g.,

[
18
,
19
]
.

3.3
Per-iteration cost and algorithmic considerations

We summarize the per-iteration computational cost of Newton-type methods for the
decomposition problem, focusing on the dependence on the ambient matrix dimension

n
and the constraint dimension
m=\dim S
.
All costs are stated per outer iteration and refer to asymptotic worst-case
scaling, ignoring constant factors and lower-order terms.

The dominant operations in each iteration are:
(i) construction of a solver for
M(x)=A-C(x)
,
(ii) evaluation of gradient traces
\tr\big(M(x)^{-1}D_{k}\big)
,
and (iii) computation of a second-order search direction via CG.

Table
1
summarizes the resulting costs in several
representative structural settings.
Exact Newton methods are included only as a baseline for very small

The dense bounds assume unstructured basis matrices

As shown in Section

4
Exponential Utility Maximization

The utility maximization problem is a central theme in mathematical finance, providing a rigorous framework for optimal decision-making under uncertainty and for linking investors’ preferences to market dynamics. A particularly important specification is exponential utility, which exhibits constant absolute risk aversion.
For background and details see
[
17
]
, Chapter 3 of
[
14
]
,
[
21
]
, Chapter 2 of
[
11
]

and Chapter 10 in
[
10
]
.

We consider a discrete-time market model with normally distributed asset increments and a general information and hedging structure. Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
denote the time horizon. At each time
i=1,\dots,N
, suppose that
p_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
risky assets are available for trading, with increments

X^{i}_{1},\dots,X^{i}_{p_{i}}.

In addition, we allow for further random variables

X^{i}_{p_{i}+1},\dots,X^{i}_{q_{i}}
,

q_{i}\geq p_{i},

which represent increments of other risky factors or assets that may influence the market but are not tradable at time
i
.

The information available for trading at time
i
is generated by the random vector

Y^{i}=(Y^{i}_{1},\dots,Y^{i}_{m_{i}})=\bigl(X^{1}_{1},\dots,X^{1}_{q_{1}},\;\dots,\;X^{i-1}_{1},\dots,X^{i-1}_{q_{i-1}}\bigr)A^{i},

where
m_{i}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}q_{j}
and
A^{i}
is a deterministic matrix. Thus, the investor’s information at time
i
consists of linear combinations of past market increments.

Let
n=\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}
, and assume that the random vector

Z=(Z_{1},...,Z_{n}):=\bigl(X^{1}_{1},\dots,X^{1}_{q_{1}},\;\dots,\;X^{N}_{1},\dots,X^{N}_{q_{N}}\bigr)

has a non-degenerate multivariate normal distribution with mean zero (for simplicity) and covariance matrix
\Sigma\in M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. Denote by
{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}=\Sigma^{-1}
the corresponding precision matrix.

A trading strategy is a random vector

\gamma=\bigl(\gamma^{1}_{1},\dots,\gamma^{1}_{p_{1}},\;\dots,\;\gamma^{N}_{1},\dots,\gamma^{N}_{p_{N}}\bigr),

where
\gamma^{i}_{j}
denotes the number of shares held in the risky asset with increment
X^{i}_{j}
. The position
\gamma^{i}_{j}
is required to be
Y^{i}
-measurable, that is,

\gamma^{i}_{j}=g^{i}_{j}(Y^{i})\ \text{for some measurable function}\ g^{i}_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}\to\mathbb{R},\ \ i=1,...,N,\ \ j=1,...,p_{i}.

(32)

The corresponding terminal portfolio value is given by

V^{\gamma}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{p_{i}}\gamma^{i}_{j}X^{i}_{j}.

(33)

Denote by
\mathcal{A}
the set of all trading strategies.

The investor’s preferences are described by the exponential utility function

u(x)=-\exp(-\alpha x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},

with absolute risk aversion parameter
\alpha>0
. The optimization problem is therefore

\max_{\gamma\in\mathcal{A}}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\!\left[-\exp\!\left(-\alpha V^{\gamma}\right)\right],

(34)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}
denotes expectation under the physical probability measure
\mathbb{P}
.
Note that for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{A}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\lambda\gamma\in\mathcal{A}
and

V^{\lambda\gamma}=\lambda V^{\gamma}
. Hence, without loss of generality, we set
\alpha:=1
.

Next, we introduce some notation.
Let
\mathcal{A}_{l}\subset\mathcal{A}
be the set of trading strategies corresponding to linear functions
g^{i}_{j}
in (
32
) (
g^{i}_{j}(0)=0
).
For any
i=1,\ldots,N
,
j=1,\ldots,p_{i}
, and
k=1,\ldots,m_{i}
, the product
Y^{i}_{k}X^{i}_{j}
can be represented as,

Y^{i}_{k}X^{i}_{j}=-\frac{1}{2}\,ZD^{i,j,k}Z^{\top},

(35)

where
D^{i,j,k}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is the unique symmetric matrix representing this quadratic form. Since the random vector
Y^{i}
is
determined by

X^{r}_{j}
,
r<i
,
j=1,...,p_{r}
we obtain that
D^{i,j,k}

is zero-diagonal matrix.
Let
{\mathcal{S}}\subset M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
denote the linear span of all matrices of the form
D^{i,j,k}
.

Notice that
\gamma\in\mathcal{A}_{l}
if and only if there exists

D\in{\mathcal{S}}
such that

V^{\gamma}=-\frac{1}{2}ZDZ^{\top}.

(36)

The following solves the exponential utility maximization problem (
34
).

Proposition 5
.

There exists a unique decomposition

{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}=\hat{Q}^{-1}+\hat{\Gamma},\ \ \hat{Q}\succ 0,\ \ \hat{Q}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\perp},\ \ \hat{\Gamma}\in{\mathcal{S}}.

(37)

The maximizer
\hat{\gamma}=(\hat{\gamma}_{1},...,\hat{\gamma}_{n})
for the optimization problem (
34
) is unique (and lies in
\mathcal{A}_{l}
) with terminal wealth given by

V^{\hat{\gamma}}=-\frac{1}{2}Z\hat{\Gamma}Z^{\top}.

(38)

The corresponding value is given by

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[-\exp\left(-V^{\hat{\gamma}}\right)\right]=-\sqrt{\frac{|\hat{Q}|}{|\Sigma|}}.

(39)

Remark 6
.

The framework of Proposition
5
shows that, in the Gaussian setting with exponential utility,
the optimal portfolio is linear and can be characterized through a specific matrix decomposition.
Our model is deliberately general and accommodates several realistic market features, including partial information, the presence of non-traded assets, and the ability to trade indices rather than individual securities. The latter corresponds to situations in which an investor can trade a portfolio (index) formed as a linear combination of underlying risky assets that are not individually tradable.
Furthermore, the information structure we consider is not necessarily a filtration: information may be lost over time, reflecting practical limitations such as delayed observations, aggregation of data, or reporting constraints.

Proof of Proposition
5
.

Proof.

Recall that the elements in
S
are zero-diagonal matrices, and so
(
1
) holds true. Hence, from Theorem
1
we obtain the uniqueness and the existence of (
37
).

Define a probability measure
\hat{\mathbb{Q}}
such that
the distribution of
(Z_{1},...,Z_{n})
is multivariate normal with mean zero and covariance matrix
\hat{Q}
. From (
35
)
it follows that for any
i=1,\ldots,N
,
j=1,\ldots,p_{i}
, and
k=1,\ldots,m_{i}

\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}}\left[Y^{i}_{k}X^{i}_{j}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}}\left[ZD^{i,j,k}Z^{\top}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\tr\left(D^{i,j,k}\hat{Q}\right)=0.

Hence, since
(X^{i},Y^{i})
is jointly Gaussian and the above cross-covariances vanish, the random vectors
X^{i}
and
Y^{i}
are independent under
\hat{\mathbb{Q}}
for any
i\leq N
.
Thus, from the tower property of conditional expectation we conclude

\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}}\left[V^{\gamma}\right]=0,\ \ \forall\gamma\in\mathcal{A}.

(40)

Next, choose
\gamma\in\mathcal{A}
.
For any
z\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exp\left(-V^{\gamma}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exp\left(-V^{\gamma}\right)+z\frac{d{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}}}{d\mathbb{P}}V^{\gamma}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[z\frac{d{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}}}{d\mathbb{P}}\left(1-\log\left(z\frac{d{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}}}{d\mathbb{P}}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=z-z\log z-z\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}}\left[\log\left(\frac{d{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}}}{d\mathbb{P}}\right)\right].

The first equality is due to (
40
).
The inequality follows from the Legendre-Fenchel duality inequality
xy\leq e^{x}+y(\log y-1)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R},y>0
by setting
x=-V^{\gamma}
and

y=z\frac{d\hat{\mathbb{Q}}}{d\mathbb{P}}
, and rearranging terms.
The last equality is straightforward.
From simple calculus it follows that the concave function

z\rightarrow z-z\log z-z\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}}\left[\log\left(\frac{d{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}}}{d\mathbb{P}}\right)\right]
,
z>0
attains its maximum at
z^{*}:=\exp\left(-\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}}\left[\log\left(\frac{d{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}}}{d\mathbb{P}}\right)\right]\right)
and the corresponding maximal value
is also
z^{*}
. Thus,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exp\left(-V^{\gamma}\right)\right]\geq\exp\left(-\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}}\left[\log\left(\frac{d{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}}}{d\mathbb{P}}\right)\right]\right),\ \ \forall\gamma\in\mathcal{A}.

(41)

Finally, let
\hat{\gamma}\in\mathcal{A}_{l}
be the (unique) maximizer of (
34
), such that (
38
) holds true.
It is well known that for multivariate normal distributions the corresponding Radon-Nykodim derivative is given by

\frac{d{{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}}}}{d\mathbb{P}}=\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}Z\hat{Q}^{-1}Z^{\top}\right)/|\hat{Q}|^{1/2}}{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}Z{\rm Phys.~Rev.~E}Z^{\top}\right)/|\Sigma|^{1/2}}.

(42)

Thus, from (
37
),(
38
) and (
42
) we obtain

V^{\hat{\gamma}}+\log\left(\frac{d{{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}}}}{d\mathbb{P}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\log|\Sigma|-\log|\hat{Q}|\right).

Hence, from (
40
)

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exp\left(-V^{\hat{\gamma}}\right)\right]=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\log|\Sigma|-\log|\hat{Q}|\right)\right)=\exp\left(-\mathbb{E}_{{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}}}\left[\log\left(\frac{d{\hat{\mathbb{Q}}}}{d\mathbb{P}}\right)\right]\right).

(43)

From (
41
) and (
43
) it follows that
\hat{\gamma}
is optimal portfolio and the value is given by the right hand side of (
39
).
The uniqueness of the optimal portfolio is due to the strict concavity of the exponential utility.

4.1
Numerical Computations for Mixed Fractional Brownian Motion

A fractional Brownian motion (fBm)
[
16
]
with Hurst parameter

{\mathcal{H}}\in(0,1)
is a centered Gaussian process

\{B^{{\mathcal{H}}}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
with covariance function

\Cov\!\left(B^{\mathcal{H}}_{s},B^{\mathcal{H}}_{t}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(t^{2{\mathcal{H}}}+s^{2{\mathcal{H}}}-|t-s|^{2{\mathcal{H}}}\right).

(44)

The process
(B^{{\mathcal{H}}}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is
self-similar
with index
{\mathcal{H}}
, meaning that for any
a>0
,

B^{{\mathcal{H}}}(at)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\;a^{H}B^{{\mathcal{H}}}(t),

and it has
stationary increments
. Moreover, the successive increments of
B^{{\mathcal{H}}}
are

positively correlated
for
{\mathcal{H}}>\tfrac{1}{2}
,
negatively correlated
for

{\mathcal{H}}<\tfrac{1}{2}
, and
uncorrelated
for
{\mathcal{H}}=\tfrac{1}{2}
, in this case
the process reduces to standard Brownian motion,
which has independent increments.
Asset price models driven by fBm have long attracted interest due to their
ability to capture long-range dependence; see, for instance,

[
15
,
25
]
.
We focus on the positive correlation case
{\mathcal{H}}>\frac{1}{2}
.

Consider a financial market with two independent risky assets. The first asset
is driven by a standard Brownian motion
\{W_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
, while the second
asset is driven by a fractional Brownian motion
\{B_{t}^{{\mathcal{H}}}\}_{t\geq 0}
. The assets
cannot be traded individually; instead, trading is permitted only in a single
index defined by

S_{t}=W_{t}+\alpha B_{t}^{{\mathcal{H}}},\qquad t\geq 0,

where
\alpha>0
is a fixed constant.

We now introduce a discretized version of this model. Let
\Delta t>0
be a
fixed time step and set the terminal time
T=N\Delta t
for some

N\in\mathbb{N}
. Trading is allowed only at the discrete times

0,\ \Delta t,\ 2\Delta t,\ \ldots,\ N\Delta t=T.

We analyze two different information structures, which are described below.

Full-information strategies

In the first setup, the investor observes both processes
W
and

B^{{\mathcal{H}}}
at all trading dates. Formally, for all
i=1,\ldots,N
we set

p_{i}=1
,
q_{i}=2
, and
m_{i}=2(i-1)
, and define

\displaystyle X^{i}_{1}

\displaystyle=W_{i\Delta t}-W_{(i-1)\Delta t}+\alpha\!\left(B^{{\mathcal{H}}}_{i\Delta t}-B^{{\mathcal{H}}}_{(i-1)\Delta t}\right),

\displaystyle X^{i}_{2}

\displaystyle=W_{i\Delta t}-W_{(i-1)\Delta t},

\displaystyle Y^{i}

\displaystyle=\left(X^{1}_{1},X^{1}_{2},\ldots,X^{i-1}_{1},X^{i-1}_{2}\right).

The covariance matrix of the vector

(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{2N}):=(X^{1}_{1},X^{1}_{2},\ldots,X^{N}_{1},X^{N}_{2})

is given by

\Sigma_{ij}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2^{1+2{\mathcal{H}}}}\Delta t^{2{\mathcal{H}}}\begin{aligned} \Big(&|i-j+2|^{2{\mathcal{H}}}+|i-j-2|^{2{\mathcal{H}}}\\
&-|i-j|^{2{\mathcal{H}}}\Big),\end{aligned}&\text{if $i,j$ are odd},\\[4.30554pt]
\Delta t,&\text{if $j$ is even and ($i=j$ or $i=j-1$)},\\
\Delta t,&\text{if $j$ is odd and $i=j+1$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The subspace
{\mathcal{S}}
is spanned by matrices

D^{k,l}\in M_{2N}(\mathbb{R})
,
k=1,\ldots,N
,
l\leq 2k-2
, of the form

D^{k,l}_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $i=l$ and $j=2k-1$},\\
1,&\text{if $i=2k-1$ and $j=l$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Markovian strategies

In the second setup, the investor determines her strategy using only the
current asset prices at the decision time, rather than the entire price
history. This restriction is motivated by practical considerations, as it
leads to strategies that are easier and faster to implement.

Formally, for all
i=1,\ldots,N
we again set
p_{i}=1
,
q_{i}=2
, and

m_{i}=2(i-1)
, and define

\displaystyle X^{i}_{1}

\displaystyle=W_{i\Delta t}-W_{(i-1)\Delta t}+\alpha\!\left(B^{{\mathcal{H}}}_{i\Delta t}-B^{{\mathcal{H}}}_{(i-1)\Delta t}\right),

\displaystyle X^{i}_{2}

\displaystyle=W_{i\Delta t}-W_{(i-1)\Delta t},

\displaystyle Y^{i}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}X^{j}_{1},\;\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}X^{j}_{2}\right).

The distribution of

(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{2N}):=(X^{1}_{1},X^{1}_{2},\ldots,X^{N}_{1},X^{N}_{2})

coincides with that of the first setup. However, the subspace
{\mathcal{S}}

is different.
It is spanned by matrices
D^{k,p}\in M_{2N}(\mathbb{R})
,

k=1,\ldots,N
,
p\in\{1,2\}
, defined by

D^{k,p}_{ij}:=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }j=2k-1,\ i\neq j,\ \text{and }i\equiv p\pmod{2},\\
1,&\text{if }i=2k-1,\ j\neq i,\ \text{and }j\equiv p\pmod{2},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
