Consensus recommendations for the management of Dato-DXd-associated adverse events in patients with advanced/metastatic NSCLC: insights from a multiregional steering committee
Authors: William Nassib William, Daniel Brungs, Emerson Fernandes de Sousa e Castro, Herbert H. Loong, James C. H. Yang, Kyung-Ah Park, Mehmet ali Nahit Sendur, Mohsen Mokhtar, Se-Hoon Lee, Senthil Rajappa, Stephanie P. L. Saw, Tsung-Ying Yang, Tuncay Göksel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Datopotamab deruxtecan (Dato-DXd) is a novel antibody-drug conjugate currently under investigation in clinical trials of various solid tumors. In this manuscript, an international steering committee formed by medical oncologists, ophthalmologists, and pulmonologists reached consensus to provide multidisciplinary healthcare professionals (HCPs) with practical recommendations for Dato-DXd adverse event (AE) prevention, monitoring, and management in patients with advanced/metastatic non-small cell lung cancer. Experts’ recommendations focused on the following AEs: oral mucositis/stomatitis, interstitial lung disease/pneumonitis, ocular surface events, nausea, and vomiting. Based on available guidelines, clinical trials data, and real-world experience, 55 statements were developed and, as per Delphi methodology, all reached consensus with more than 75% of experts’ agreement. The key recommendations include preventive measures, such as proactive communication between patients and caretakers, regular clinical examinations, collaboration with multidisciplinary HCPs; and monitoring measures, such as treatment dosage and care planning decisions based on the level of severity of each AE. Experts pointed out real-world challenges and local differences on AEs management and gave insight into implementation of regional strategies with a multidisciplinary collaboration approach. Dato-DXd AEs awareness, understanding, and proper management via a well-established multidisciplinary collaboration is crucial to guarantee patients’ quality of life. This international consensus aims to provide comprehensive recommendations for Dato-DXd AEs management, considering the regional and institutional differences present in the healthcare system, such as access to examinations and treatment possibilities.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the primary cause of cancer-associated incidence and mortality worldwide, and around 85% of the cases are related to the non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) subtype. 1 The NSCLC treatment landscape is constantly changing, with different therapeutic options for different stages emerging in the last decades, including targeted therapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), bispecific antibodies, and antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs). 2 Anti-trophoblast cell surface antigen 2 (TROP2)-ADCs, such as sacituzumab govitecan and datopotamab deruxtecan (Dato-DXd) have shown clinically meaningful survival benefits and improved quality of life with tolerable safety profiles in advanced NSCLC3,4 with increasing therapeutic potential of Dato-DXd. 5

Dato-DXd is an ADC that consists of a humanized TROP2-targeted monoclonal antibody covalently linked to deruxtecan (a topoisomerase I inhibitor cytotoxic payload) via a tetrapeptide-based cleavable linker, which is plasma-stable and tumor-selective. Dato-DXd binds to TROP2 in the target cells and then it is internalized, leading to the deruxtecan release, inducing DNA damage and apoptosis. In the tumor microenvironment, deruxtecan diffusion into neighboring cells rises cytotoxicity via a bystander antitumor effect. 6

Dato-DXd was first evaluated in a phase I clinical trial (TROPION-PanTumor01, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03401385) in patients with advanced/metastatic solid tumors to determine the maximum dose tolerated. Based on this ongoing study, a dose of 6 mg/kg Dato-DXd was identified as the most effective and tolerable by patients with NSCLC. 7 Treatment with Dato-DXd at this dose demonstrated a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 6.9 months in phase II studies in pre-treated NSCLC patients.8 –10

Dato-DXd is being evaluated in several clinical studies of patients with NSCLC (ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers: NCT05460273, NCT06357533, NCT06564844, NCT06350097, NCT06417814, NCT05687266),7,11 –15 breast cancer (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05460273),7,16 –18 and other solid tumors (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05489211), either as monotherapy or in combination with targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and/or chemotherapy. Primary results have shown that Dato-DXd PFS was superior to chemotherapy in patients with previously treated advanced/metastatic NSCLC (TROPION-Lung01, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04656652) 15 and previously treated inoperable or metastatic hormone receptor (HR)-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative breast cancer (HR+/HER2– BC; TROPION-Breast01, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05104866). 18 The results from the TROPION-Breast01 trial have been granted for Dato-DXd approval for adult patients with unresectable or metastatic HR+/HER2− BC who have received prior endocrine-based therapy and chemotherapy for unresectable or metastatic disease. 19 The results of the TROPION-Lung05 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04484142) 10 and TROPION-Lung01 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04656652) 15 trials allowed the FDA to grant accelerated approval in the United States to Dato-DXd for adults with locally advanced or metastatic epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mutated NSCLC who have received prior EGFR-directed therapy and platinum-based chemotherapy back in June 2025. 20 The use of Dato-DXd in real-world clinical practice will allow healthcare professionals (HCPs) to obtain more data in the near future on both the efficacy and the side effects of its use.

The most clinically impactful adverse events (AEs) associated with Dato-DXd in different clinical trials are oral mucositis (OM)/stomatitis, ocular surface events (OSEs), interstitial lung disease (ILD)/pneumonitis, nausea, and vomiting (Supplemental Table 1).7 –15,18,21 –31 These AEs have been described in multiple tumor scenarios in the phase I TROPION-PanTumor01. 7 In 2024, a review article on recommendations for Dato-DXd main associated AEs prevention and management strategies among patients with HR+/HER2− BC, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), and NSCLC reported in TROPION-PanTumor01 was published. 22 Nevertheless, regional differences in healthcare systems, such as therapies availability, access to specific treatments or the possibility to implement multidisciplinary team (MDT) collaborations, remain to be taken into consideration.

Here, an international Steering Committee comprised of experts in oncology, ophthalmology, and pulmonology discussed and gained practical implementable strategies in advanced/metastatic NSCLC management. The experts identified the most common and challenging AEs from the diverse regions’ clinical settings; highlighted regional differences in AE experiences and management approaches, considering factors such as access to specialists, available resources, and patient perceptions; and described practical strategies for the prevention and management of such AEs, providing consensus-based recommendations on how different HCPs can effectively collaborate. In this work, we summarize this practical set of recommendations to improve treatment strategies and overall quality of life of patients with NSCLC treated with Dato-DXd across different regions, which can be readily implemented by MDT members in the clinical setting.

Methods

This manuscript was developed based on the recommendations of an international steering committee (SC) on the AEs management in advanced/metastatic NSCLC patients treated with Dato-DXd in a virtual meeting setting and an asynchronous survey based on a set of statements and open-ended questions developed with information gathered from literature review on clinical trials, real-world data, and guidelines available for AEs management.

The SC was selected based on expertise in lung cancer and active management of specific AEs, recent publications in peer-reviewed journals, and different geographic location of their clinical practice with varying healthcare systems. The SC was composed of 13 experts, including 9 medical oncologists with experience in management of Dato-DXd-derived AEs from Brazil, Australia, Egypt, Hong Kong, India, Singapore, South Korea, Taiwan, and Turkey; 2 ophthalmologists with experience in OSEs management from Brazil and South Korea; and 2 pulmonologists with experience in ILD/pneumonitis management from Taiwan and Turkey.

The SC discussed in a virtual meeting the most common and challenging AEs associated with Dato-DXd in advanced/metastatic NSCLC from the diverse regions’ clinical settings, as well as practical strategies for the prevention and management of those AEs, based on real-world experiences. Based on the experts’ insights gathered from the virtual meeting discussion and information from a literature search in PubMed centered on clinical trials, reviews, real-world experience, and guidelines on AEs management associated with Dato-DXd in NSCLC from the last 10 years, a set of 55 statements were drawn to establish a consensus among the SC members. The Consensus Statements (CS) are listed in Tables 1–5 of the main text, which have been divided into different sections: (1) general principles on AEs management (2) OM/stomatitis management; (3) OSEs management; (4) ILD/pneumonitis management; and (5) nausea and vomiting management.

These statements were reviewed by the SC in an asynchronous discussion taking place through the MphaR virtual platform. The SC voted on each CS indicating their level of agreement (strongly agree, agree, disagree, or strongly disagree) in accordance with the Delphi methodology. 32 A statement was considered to have reached consensus if 75% or more of SC selected strongly agree or agree. In addition to the voting options, the SC could add comments, which were taken into consideration for the rounds of review and the development of the manuscript. There were 2 Delphi rounds, in which 54 statements reached consensus in the first round of voting. The remaining statement had reached 72.73% agreement, was modified based on the SC comments, and reached consensus in the second round of voting (CS4.12, Table 4).

The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation system 33 was used to evaluate the quality of evidence of the CS, and it is incorporated in Tables 1–5. The level of evidence from publications, clinical trials, and real-world experience was taken into consideration. The quality of evidence is graded as “High” if further research is very unlikely to change the confidence in the estimate of effect; “Moderate” if further research is likely to have an important impact on the confidence in the estimate of effect and may change the estimate; and “Low” if further research is very likely to have an important impact on the confidence in the estimate of the effect and is likely to change the estimate.

In addition to the CS voting and comments, the experts answered a set of 12 open questions during the asynchronous discussion, to gain knowledge on the challenges in real-world management of the different AEs, preventive measures, monitoring, and supportive care. The collected answers were synthesized and, together with the development of the CS and the background information, were used to write this manuscript.

Study limitations

The consensus-based design might have limitations such as the panel size and number of experts required to vote for each section. All 13 experts voted for the first section on general principles on AEs management. Only medical oncologists (nine) were required to vote for section 2 on OM/stomatitis management, and section 5 on nausea and vomiting management. In addition to all medical oncologists (nine), all ophthalmologists (two) were required to vote for section 3 on OSEs management; while all pulmonologists (two) were required to vote for section 4 on ILD/pneumonitis management. At the time of voting, one of the pulmonologists voted for all the CS. In the only cases in which there was lack of voting, due to lack of knowledge or their experience, the comment of the expert was taken into consideration and reflected in the respective table containing the CS and the main text.

The recommendations contained in this study are based on expert consensus, which should not be taken as an alternative to clinical evidence and guidelines, but as complementary information. Future validation and real-world data might change the evidence of such recommendations.

Key AEs and management strategies

General overview of the key AEs and management strategies with the CSs on general principles

Guidelines on how to manage Dato-DXd-associated AEs are depicted in Figure 1 and specifics for each AE management are further described in the following sections.

Guidelines on prevention and management of Dato-DXd associated AEs.

AEs, adverse event; Dato-DXd, datopotamab deruxtecan.

Experts agreed that before and during Dato-DXd treatment, the medical team and NSCLC patients should have a good understanding of AEs detection and management, with early implementation of appropriate prophylactic measures, to prevent or reduce AE’s severity, and allow a better quality of life for patients. It is important to conduct a targeted history and examination to establish a clinical baseline prior to starting Dato-DXd with a view to potential AEs which may emerge with treatment. During treatment with Dato-DXd, AEs monitoring should be performed during regular clinical consultations and, if the clinicians suspect the onset of an AE, immediate action should be taken. If necessary, clinicians should consider early referral to other specialists for specific assessments to confirm the suspected AEs and plan a more effective treatment. In case of AE confirmation, clinicians should consider adjusting Dato-DXd dosage and its treatment plan depending on the AE grade of severity and perform the measures needed to control the specific AE. This treatment plan should be reviewed in each regular visit and reconsider if changes are necessary, until recovery.

All 13 experts voted for the CSs on general principles for Dato-DXd AEs management, giving rise to an agreement on four main recommendations (Table 1). Experts agreed that there is a need to establish MDT collaboration between oncologists, ophthalmologists, pulmonologists, nurses, dental oncologists, and pharmacists for a good AE understanding and proper management. Likewise, it is important to educate patients and encourage them for regular reporting of symptoms, to allow appropriate interventions and to incorporate real-world data for future guidelines.

Good patient education implementation could be achieved effectively by providing concise handouts, in either paper or electronic format, during medical consultation, summarizing the risks of AEs and their management, highlighting the importance of early symptom reporting, as well as adhering to prophylactic medication.

Oral mucositis/stomatitis

Clinical relevance and incidence of OM/stomatitis

Oral mucositis/stomatitis, hereafter referred to as stomatitis, a severe inflammation/ulceration of the oral mucosa, is a common AE in cancer patients who are undergoing chemotherapy, head and neck radiation therapy, hematopoietic cell transplantation, and select targeted agents.34,35

Stomatitis is often the most impactful AE affecting quality of life in patients with advanced/metastatic NSCLC receiving Dato-DXd. In the TROPION-Lung01 clinical trial, 15 an incidence of 55.2% (164/297 patients) stomatitis cases was reported, most of them being mild to moderate in severity (27.6%, 82/297 grade 1 and 20.9%, 62/297 grade 2 events). The rate of Dato-DXd discontinuation by this AE was low (0.7%).

Protocols for Dato-DXd-related stomatitis prevention and management were first shown in the TROPION-Lung0115 and TROPION-Breast0118 clinical trials, 22 and we describe them further in Figure 1 and in the following sections.

Challenges in real-world management of OM/stomatitis

In daily practice, clinicians generally recommend dentist consultation during screening before treatment with Dato-DXd, acknowledging this is not required per Patient Safety Recommendations and might not be feasible in all healthcare settings given challenges in accessing dental services. Patient education on prevention and symptom management, and compliance to such strategies are strongly encouraged.

Multidisciplinary consensus recommendations for OM/stomatitis management

Preventive measures before treatment

All nine experts consulted achieved more than 75% agreement on preventive stomatitis measures before Dato-DXd treatment (Table 2). They recommend starting a well-organized oral care plan, emphasizing the importance of patients’ education on early identification and reporting of symptoms. Likewise, it is recommended to encourage patients to take care of their oral hygiene (tooth brushing, flossing, hydration, and lubrication of the oral mucosa) before starting Dato-DXd treatment to prevent or reduce the severity of stomatitis.15,18

Oncologists should consider consultation with dental specialists for dental examinations before treatment, if their healthcare system allows it.

When planning the oral care strategy, it is recommended to use prophylactic steroid-containing mouthwash. 18 Ongoing clinical trials are analyzing the use of dexamethasone mouthwash and there is evidence supporting its use for stomatitis prevention in chemotherapy-treated breast cancer patients, which indirectly led to recommendation of its use in patients treated with Dato-DXd. 26 Dexamethasone is unavailable or costly in some regions, therefore, alternatives to it are recommended, such as daily use of an inert, bland non-alcoholic, and/or bicarbonate-containing mouthwash, to prevent stomatitis. 26

Measures during treatment

Monitoring and supportive care during treatment

CSs on monitoring and supportive stomatitis measures during Dato-DXd treatment are listed in Table 2. Experts recommend close surveillance of patient symptoms in addition to physical examination, as stomatitis might be unnoticeable in some cases.

Cryotherapy (ice sucking) can be considered during infusion of Dato-DXd and several times daily thereafter, to reduce the incidence and severity of stomatitis. 18 Although not widely available, photobiomodulation (low-level laser) therapy has been shown to minimize or prevent stomatitis during oncological treatments. 36 Photobiomodulation remains to be studied as a preventative and/or therapeutic strategy for Dato-DXd-induced stomatitis. Up to the date, there is evidence of this approach in hematopoietic malignancies. 37 According to some experts, it could be used in refractory NSCLC cases to relieve symptoms. 37

Experts recommend starting or intensifying steroid-containing mouth rinses (up to four times per day) if signs of pain, erythema, or ulceration appear. Additionally, some experts suggested that if there is overt ulceration, patients probably need topical steroid-containing ointments rather than mouth rinses. In case of secondary fungal infection, consider oral nystatin suspension or other topical antifungals.

Treatment adjustment based on severity grade

CSs on Dato-DXd treatment adjustments based on stomatitis severity grade are listed in the last part of Table 2. In case of grade 1 stomatitis, Dato-DXd treatment should be kept without dose change and proceed with supportive care measures. For grade 2 stomatitis, Dato-DXd should be delayed or reduce its dose if clinically indicated and optimize the supportive care measures. In case of grade 3, Dato-DXd should be delayed until resolution to grade ⩽1 or baseline, followed by optimization of supportive care and resumption at the same dose. If grade 3 stomatitis recurs, drug discontinuation might happen in cases with suboptimal patient education or less experienced clinicians. In case of grade 4 development, Dato-DXd treatment should be permanently discontinued.

Open discussion and future directions

Experts recommend preventive and monitoring strategies, explained in the sections above, which are both practical and scalable in routine clinical settings.

Considering the variability in patient response, real-world data collection on alternative interventions (e.g., laser therapy, herbal rinses) should be prioritized to inform future guidelines and should be evaluated in upcoming formal clinical trials. Collecting data on Dato-DXd-related OM from real-world clinical practice on a systematic basis will benefit the AEs management strategy.

Ocular surface events

Clinical relevance and incidence of OSEs

OSEs are characterized by toxicities in the surface of the eye, which may appear in cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy, 38 immunotherapy, 39 and targeted therapy with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). 40

In the TROPION-Lung01 clinical trial, 15 OSEs were reported in patients with NSCLC treated with Dato-DXd with an incidence of 21.5% (64/297 patients) of any-grade, most of them being mild in severity: 11.4% (34/297 patients) grade 1, 7.7% (23/297 patients) grade 2, and only 2% (6/297 patients) grade 3 events. The most common OSEs were lacrimation with an incidence of 7.7% (23/297 patients), dry eye with an incidence of 7.1% (21/297 patients), conjunctivitis with an incidence of 5.1% (15/297 patients), and keratitis with an incidence of 4% (12/297 patients).

Protocols for Dato-DXd-derived OSEs prevention and management were first implemented in the TROPION-Lung0115 and TROPION-Breast0118 clinical trials, 22 and we describe them further in Figure 1 and in the following sections.

Challenges in real-world management of OSEs

Experts agreed that the most challenging issue in the real-world management of OSEs is the difficulty of having a routine ophthalmological screening due to its limited availability. There are differences across diverse healthcare systems in local resources and infrastructure.

In clinical practice, general terms like “dry eyes” are commonly used to describe OSEs symptoms, but they do not reveal underlying causes like gland dysfunction or inflammation. Specific diagnostics, such as tear osmolarity and ocular imaging, are required to accurately determine OSEs pathophysiology. 41

Multidisciplinary consensus recommendations for OSEs

Preventive measures before or at treatment initiation

All experts consulted achieved more than 75% agreement on preventive OSE measures before Dato-DXd treatment (Table 3) in NSCLC. Patients’ education concerning the risk of OSEs and the value of early symptom reports would help early prevention. Experts suggested advising patients to consider not using contact lenses while being on Dato-DXd treatment, although there is minimal evidence of this and more studies are needed. Other suggested preventive measures by the experts are the use of artificial tears and avoiding topical dexamethasone or antibiotics routinely, although more evidence is needed. A routine ophthalmological screening before starting Dato-DXd treatment can be a beneficial strategy for OSE prevention. 15 However, in different healthcare settings, this pre-treatment approach is not established, and in those cases, experts suggest that patients should be referred to an ophthalmologist for consultation at the first sign or symptom of an OSE.

Measures during treatment

Monitoring and supportive care during treatment

CSs on monitoring and supportive OSE measures during Dato-DXd treatment in NSCLC patients are listed in Table 3. Experts agreed that MDT collaboration between oncologists and ophthalmologists is essential for effective OSEs management, with defined responsibilities and agreements on intervention organization.

The establishment of guidelines and referral pathways would provide efficient and selective ophthalmology recommendations in situations with limited resources.

Treatment adjustment based on severity grade

CSs on Dato-DXd treatment adjustments based on OSEs’ severity grade are listed in the last part of Table 3. For grades 1–3 an ophthalmological evaluation should be considered to accurately grade OSEs’ severity and reinforce regular use of eye care. In case of grade 2/3, Dato-DXd should be delayed until resolution to grade ⩽1 and then resume at the same dose (for grade 2 patients) or at a reduced dose level (for grade 3 patients). Finally, for patients with grade 4 OSEs, an urgent ophthalmological assessment is needed and Dato-DXd treatment should be permanently discontinued.

Open discussion and future directions

In settings with limited access to ophthalmology, alternative measures, following NCCN, 42 ASCO, 43 and ESMO 44 guidelines, could empower oncologists to identify and triage OSEs effectively. A combination of educational strategies can be implemented to improve patient awareness and adherence to prophylactic measures and early reporting of signs and symptoms of OSEs. One strategy would be to provide patients with adequate informative materials, like structured questionnaires addressing detailed ocular symptoms. Experts suggested combining this strategy with appropriate verbal counseling to patients, by the physician and/or nurse specialist, with emphasis on the necessity of prophylactic measures during consultation. Furthermore, experts recommend having pathways or flowcharts to guide clinicians on appropriate referrals and early management for an effective medical team education. Although simple visual acuity testing would help to notice decline in vision, early check-ups by ophthalmologists are indispensable to properly grade OSEs, due to the difficulty of early detection of ocular toxicities by symptomatology.

ILD/pneumonitis

Clinical relevance and incidence of ILD/pneumonitis

Interstitial (or diffuse parenchymal) lung diseases (ILDs) are alterations of the pulmonary interstitium, alveolar structures, and small airways, such as inflammation or fibrosis,45,46 and may appear in cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or targeted therapies like ADCs.47,48 It is important to highlight that ILD caused by ADCs manifests differently from ILD induced by TKIs or ICIs, in terms of mechanism and clinical profiles. 49

The most common risk factors for this AE (in the setting of most anticancer therapies) are pre-existing or suspected ILD or other lung disease, clinically severe pulmonary reduced lung function, prior thoracic radiotherapy or chemotherapy, and prior anticancer therapies, poor overall health, smoking, advanced age, male sex.45,48,50 –52

In the TROPION-Lung01 clinical trial, 15 ILD was reported in patients with NSCLC treated with Dato-DXd with an 8.8% incidence (26/297 patients) of any-grade, 0.7% (2/297 patients) were reported as grade 1, 4.4% (13/297 patients) grade 2, and 3.7% (11/297 patients) grade ⩾3 events. Seven patients (2.4%) in the Dato-DXd regimen experienced grade 5 ILD events, 4 of which were due to disease progression.

Protocols for Dato-DXd-derived ILD prevention and management were first implemented 22 in the TROPION-Lung0115 and TROPION-Breast0118 clinical trials, and we describe them further in Figure 1 and in the following sections.

Challenges in real-world management of ILD/pneumonitis

ILD real-world practice encounters several challenges, such as early diagnosis and prompt treatment due to non-specific symptoms and the need to exclude other diagnoses. Additionally, there are inequalities across different income-level countries in access to resources for ILD detection, differential diagnoses, and management, which affect the lower income groups and minorities. All regions reflected in this manuscript, despite their income level, face delays in healthcare access and therefore late ILD diagnosis. Lastly, there is limited research on this topic, which leads to scarcity of evidence on ILD treatment strategies. MDTs should collaborate to improve the availability of these resources and reach a better understanding and effective management of ILD. 53

Multidisciplinary consensus recommendations for ILD/pneumonitis management

Early detection and monitoring

All experts consulted achieved more than 75% agreement on preventive ILD/pneumonitis measures before Dato-DXd treatment (Table 4). Experts agreed that education of patients and healthcare providers on respiratory symptoms and reporting is essential for early detection and effective management of ILD/pneumonitis. Patients monitoring should be performed even if they are asymptomatic. Any signs of ILD need to be investigated urgently and, if suspected, Dato-DXd treatment should not be performed, to reduce the risk of severe complications and improve patient outcomes.

Experts agreed that in routine practice, regular monitoring of ILD symptoms is the most effective way for early detection. Baseline pulmonary function tests, high-resolution CT imaging, chest X-rays, and pulse oximetry are recommended by experts.

Measures during treatment

Monitoring and supportive care during treatment

CSs on monitoring ILD/pneumonitis measures during Dato-DXd treatment are listed in Table 4. If ILD is suspected, experts recommend performing a full diagnostic workup, including pulmonologist consultation, high-resolution chest computed tomography (HRCT), and bronchoscopy, although not always required. If clinically indicated, differential diagnosis should contain a microbiological test, including bacterial, fungal, or viral panels, to exclude infections. In patients with increasing respiratory symptoms, additional assessment of oxygen saturation (SpO2) with pulse oximetry should be monitored. If suspicion of ILD increases, a HRCT should be performed immediately and the patient referred for pulmonology consultation.

Treatment adjustment based on severity grade

CSs on Dato-DXd treatment adjustments based on ILD/pneumonitis severity grade are listed in the last part of Table 4.

Grade 1

Depending on severity of clinical signs and radiology results, regular monitoring for symptom progression and oxygen saturation should be done, according to the experts. For grade 1 cases, experts recommend considering starting systemic corticosteroid treatment and, if symptomatic or the condition worsens, Dato-DXd should be permanently discontinued. If grade 1 resolves, Dato-DXd may be resumed at the same dose (if it resolves within 28 days), or at a reduced dose (if it resolves at >28 days).

Grade 2

In patients with grade 2 ILD/pneumonitis, Dato-DXd should be permanently discontinued. Experts recommend immediately starting systemic corticosteroids and if no clinical improvement is observed within 5 days, consider increasing the steroid dose, re-evaluate for alternative diagnoses, and increase care as needed.

Grades 3 and 4

In grade 3 or 4 cases, patients should be hospitalized, and Dato-DXd should be permanently discontinued. It is recommended to start high-dose IV methylprednisolone, followed by oral prednisone until resolution and then tapered gradually. If no improvement is observed, reconsider alternative diagnoses and consider additional immunosuppressive therapies or further escalation of care by a specialist pulmonologist. Some experts recommend early interval imaging for these severe cases, as clinically indicated.

Open discussion and future directions

Experts agreed that HCPs (clinicians, radiologists, oncologists, and nurses in cooperation with pulmonologists and emergency department clinicians) education is the most effective way to develop and implement a training plan for ILD/pneumonitis early detection and monitoring to reduce the risk of severe complications and improve patient outcomes. To refine ILD differential diagnosis and optimize management pathways, it is important to have a well-established MDT collaboration. Additionally, there should be clear guidelines to help clinicians in management decisions that allow a better development of care pathways for clinicians. Furthermore, establishing close patient monitoring, real-time use of patient-reported outcomes in daily clinical practice, differential diagnosis, and increasing patient awareness can accelerate early diagnosis.

Nausea and vomiting

Clinical relevance and incidence of nausea and vomiting

Nausea and vomiting are two of the most common AEs of cancer patients who are undergoing chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and ADCs treatment and their prevention and management are essential for patients’ quality of life.54 –56 Recommendations on their prevention and management were also established for Dato-DXd-treated HR+/HER2− BC, TNBC, and NSCLC patients. 22

In the TROPION-Lung01 clinical trial, 15 nausea was reported in patients with NSCLC treated with Dato-DXd with an incidence of 34% (101/297 patients) of any-grade nausea. Most of the events were mild or moderate and only 2.4% (7/297) patients had grade ⩾3 nausea.

Dato-DXd is considered moderate to high emetic risk and prophylactic and treatment strategies recommended in various guidelines (from ASCO, ESMO, or NCCN) should be implemented according to these criteria. Protocols for Dato-DXd-derived nausea prevention and management were first shown 22 in the TROPION-Lung0115 and TROPION-Breast0118 clinical trials, and we describe them further in Figure 1 and in the following sections.

Challenges in real-world management of nausea and vomiting

Nausea and vomiting present several management challenges, such as their persistence, unsolved breakthrough, refractory, and anticipatory emesis. In the case of advanced cancer, nausea and vomiting are multifactorial, which often requires tailored approaches. 56 Patients treated with ADCs present differences in the severity and duration of nausea and vomiting, leading to difficulties on the development of tailored antiemetic therapies. 54 Furthermore, there is a lack of antiemetic trials for antineoplastic agents, making difficult to establish guidelines on emetogenic risk and management. 56

Multidisciplinary consensus recommendations for nausea and vomiting management

Preventive measures before treatment initiation

All experts consulted achieved 100% agreement on preventive nausea and vomiting measures before Dato-DXd treatment on NSCLC patients (Table 5). Patient training on these AEs risk and symptom management should be provided before and during treatment. Before Dato-DXd infusion, prophylactic antiemetic agents should be administered as clinically needed.

Measures during treatment

Monitoring and supportive care during treatment

CSs on monitoring nausea and vomiting measures during Dato-DXd treatment are listed in Table 5. Although Dato-DXd was previously considered to be of low emetogenic risk, 22 the treatment management guidelines for Dato-DXd AEs included more conservative anti-emetic prophylactic recommendations. 57 Experts in this SC considered Dato-DXd of moderate-high emetic risk. Clinicians of patients with breakthrough nausea or vomiting should re-evaluate the emetic risk, disease status, concurrent illnesses, and co-medications to determine whether the best treatment is being administered. According to NCCN 57 , and MASCC/ESMO 58 guidelines, antiemetics such as 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT3) antagonists and corticosteroids are recommended for patients with these AEs. 22 If nausea or vomiting persist, treatment interruptions may be considered, and medications should also be assessed.

Treatment adjustment based on severity grade

CSs on Dato-DXd treatment adjustments in NSCLC patients based on nausea/vomiting severity grade are listed in the last part of Table 5. In grade 1 or 2 cases, Dato-DXd dose should be kept without modification, although some experts stated that Dato-DXd dosage could be modified if clinically indicated for patients who could potentially benefit from it. If grade 2 nausea/vomiting persists despite optimum supportive care management, some experts proposed reducing the dose of Dato-DXd or dose interruption. In case of grade 3 nausea/vomiting, Dato-DXd should be delayed until resolution to grade ⩽1 or baseline, followed by optimization of supportive care and resumption at the same dose. Based on their experience, some experts suggested starting at a lower dose and then escalate after optimization, rather than taking the risk of grade 3 occurrence leading to further dose decrease or discontinuation. If grade 3 occurs regardless of optimized prophylactic/supportive care, Dato-DXd should be delayed until resolution to grade ⩽1 or baseline and then resumed at a reduced dose. If grade 4 occurs despite optimal measures, the experts agreed on permanently discontinuing Dato-DXd.

Future directions

Established antiemetic measures 57 developed for chemotherapy and other anticancer therapies, including pharmacologic regimens with agents like ondansetron, dexamethasone, aprepitant, and olanzapine, as well as non-pharmacologic interventions such as acupressure, dietary modifications, and relaxation techniques, should be evaluated for their effectiveness and tolerability in patients receiving Dato-DXd, through observational and interventional studies. 56

Anticipating patient emetic risk and tailoring the onset and duration of nausea and vomiting would be needed in future clinical studies to optimize treatment strategies. Personalized biomarker-based approaches are expected to be developed, as there are data suggesting certain cytokines and peptides as potential biomarkers. 54

Conclusion

The unique AE profile of Dato-DXd requires special attention in clinical practice, which poses challenges in the real world including timely access to relevant subspecialty care. Experts agreed that the key to gaining a better understanding of AEs and improving management efficiency is the education of patients and healthcare providers. Furthermore, there is a need to establish a proactive MDT collaboration between oncologists, ophthalmologists, pulmonologists, nurses, dental oncologists, and pharmacists to achieve a correct comprehension of Dato-DXd AEs and their appropriate management.

Here, the panel of 13 experts agreed on a set of CSs on specific measures for Dato-DXd associated AEs in patients with advanced/metastatic NSCLC (Tables 1–5) and provided recommendations (summarized in Figure 1) based on available guidelines from NCCN,42,57 ASCO,43,55 and ESMO,44,56,58,59 and MASCC58,60; clinical trials data7 –15,18,21 –31; and real-world experience. As a summary, preventive measures such as proactive communication between patients and caretakers, regular clinical assessments, collaboration with multidisciplinary HCPs, and prophylactic measures are highly recommended. When AEs occur, treatment dose modifications and care-planning decisions should be guided by the severity of each AE. Finally, clinicians and researchers should incorporate real-world data into clinical studies and future guidelines, which will enable a better understanding of these AEs’ management and therefore a more efficient therapeutic plan leading to improvements in patient quality of life.
