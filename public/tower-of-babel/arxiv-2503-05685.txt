Isogenies of CM Elliptic Curves
Edgar Assing, Yingkun Li, Tian Wang, Jiacheng Xia

Abstract
. Given two CM elliptic curves over a number field and a natural number m m , we establish a polynomial lower bound (in terms of m m ) for the number of rational primes p p such that the reductions of these elliptic curves modulo a prime above p p are m m -isogenous. The proof relies on higher Green functions and theorems of Gross-Zagier and Gross-Kohnen-Zagier. A crucial observation is that the Fourier coefficients of incoherent Eisenstein series can be approximated by those of coherent Eisenstein series of increasing level. Another key ingredient is an explicit upper bound for the Petersson norm of an arbitrary elliptic modular form in terms of finitely many of its Fourier coefficients at the cusp infinity, which is a result of independent interest.

1.
Introduction

In the study of the arithmetic of elliptic curves, it is of great interest to understand isogenies of elliptic curves in analogy to understanding their torsion points. Let
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
be two elliptic curves defined over a number field
K

\mathfrak{p}
be a prime ideal of the ring of integers

and

have good reductions at

m
-isogeny between

and

m
-isogeny between
E_{1,\mathfrak{p}}
and
E_{2,\mathfrak{p}}

E_{1,\mathfrak{p}}
and

are
m
-isogenous for sufficiently many primes

-isogenous? For a rational prime

[
Kat81
]

[

,
BC14
,
Sut12
,
Vog20
]
. In general, addressing this local-global principle is closely related to the broader question of understanding the image of the mod
\ell

There is a significant distinction between cyclic isogenies in CM and non-CM cases. For instance, for any two non-CM elliptic curves over a number field, there is at most one number that can appear as degrees of cyclic isogenies between them. In contrast, there are infinitely many different degrees of cyclic isogenies between any two rationally isogenous CM elliptic curves. In terms of the local-global principle for cyclic
m
-isogenies, we want to understand how many primes are “sufficiently many” in the CM case, which leads us to the following natural question.

Suppose
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are two CM elliptic curves over
K
which are not
m
-isogenous. For a fixed positive integer
m
, how many prime ideals in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
are there such that the reductions

and
E_{2,\mathfrak{p}}

\mathfrak{p}

-isogenous? Note that, without fixing the degree

, similar questions have already been addressed in previous works. For instance, an upper bound of such primes with bounded norm was established in
[
CHW24
,
Won22
]
for non-isogenous non-CM elliptic curves using analytic tools such as effective Chebotarev density theorem and effective joint Sato-Tate distribution. For non-isogenous CM elliptic curves, an asymptotic formula can be easily derived using Deuring’s criterion (see Remark
1.3
). For both CM and non-CM elliptic curves, Charles
[
Cha18
]
applied Arakelov intersection theory to prove the existence of infinitely many primes
\mathfrak{p}
for which
E_{1,\mathfrak{p}}
and
E_{2,\mathfrak{p}}
are isogenous. Nonetheless, as far as we know, there is no such results in the literature when we fix the degree of cyclic isogenies. In this note, we establish an polynomial lower bound, in terms of
m
, for the number of primes where the reductions of two CM elliptic curves become
m
-isogenous.

The size of

We now briefly outline the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
The proof proceeds along a similar line of argument in
[

]

The main innovation is to control the valuation of

After this, we still need a bound of such Fourier coefficients in terms of the level.
This is achieved in Theorem

\infty

Define a function

For

We now state the formula relating the value of

in (

Theorem 2.2
.

Let

odd and vanishing constant term if

3.
A uniform bound for the Petersson norm

Let
f\in S_{\kappa}(N)
be a cusp form for
\Gamma_{0}(N)
(i.e. of level
N
) and weight
\kappa\geq 2
. We put

(3.1)

d=\dim S_{\kappa}(N)\ll\kappa N\log\log(N),

where the upper bound is taken from
[
Mar05
, Theorem 6]
.
The Fourier expansion of
f
at the cusp
\infty
is written as

f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{f}(n)e(nz).

Finally, the Petersson norm of
f
is defined by

\|f\|^{2}=\frac{1}{[\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\colon\Gamma_{0}(N)]}\int_{\mathcal{F}_{N}}|f(x+iy)|^{2}y^{{\kappa}-2}dxdy.

Here
\mathcal{F}_{N}
is a fundamental domain for
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, which we assume to be contained in the strip

\mathcal{P}=\{x+iy\in\mathbb{H}\colon 0\leq x\leq 1\}.

We recall the bound

(3.2)

|a_{f}(n)|\ll_{\epsilon}d^{\frac{1}{2}}N^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}n^{\frac{\kappa-1}{2}+\epsilon}\|f\|

from
[
SPY18
, Theorem 12]
. This bound holds for general
f
. It is a natural question to ask for suitable estimate for the the Petersson norm
\|f\|
in terms of the Fourier coefficients of
f
(at infinity). For small levels this has been considered in
[
JR11
,
JP15
,
CI18
]
. In Theorem
3.5
below we give a general answer.

Remark 3.1
.

A similar problem occurs in the classical literature concerning quadratic forms. Here one naturally encounters the problem of uniformly estimating the Petersson norm of the cuspidal part
f
of certain theta functions. See
[
Wai22
, Section 3]
and the references within for further discussion.

The standard approach is to cover the fundamental domain
\mathcal{F}_{N}
by a suitable collection of Siegel sets. Executing the integrals over these sets yields an upper bound for the Petersson norm of
f
in terms of sums of Fourier coefficients. Typically this estimate involves the Fourier coefficients of
f
at all cusps. In the case where
f
arises from a theta function, one has sufficient control on all Fourier coefficients to obtain the desired estimate for the Petersson norm of
f
.

However, for general
f\in S_{\kappa}(N)
, the Fourier coefficients at cusps different from
\infty
can be hard to access. To circumvent this issue we modify the approach described above by using different Siegel sets.

We introduce the sup-norm

\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{H}}\Im(z)^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}|f(z)|.

For a general form
f\in S_{\kappa}(N)
one has the bound

(3.3)

\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}\ll[\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\colon\Gamma_{0}(N)]\cdot\kappa^{\frac{3}{2}}\cdot\|f\|^{2}.

This is a direct consequence of general bounds for the Bergman kernel as given in
[
kramer
]
for example.

Remark 3.2
.

The value of the estimates (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) lies in their generality. Indeed, they do not require any assumption on the form
f\in S_{\kappa}(N)
. The price paid for this is that they are not sharp in general.

Let
Y>0
be a small parameter. We define the Siegel set

\mathcal{P}_{Y}=\{x+iy\in\mathbb{H}\colon 0\leq x\leq 1\text{ and }y>Y\}.

A standard computation shows that

I_{f}(Y)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|a_{f}(n)|^{2}\int_{Y}^{\infty}e^{-4\pi ny}y^{\kappa-2}dy.\\

The remaining
y
-integral decays exponentially for sufficiently large
n
. This allows us to truncate the
n
-sum as follows.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
f\in S_{\kappa}(N)
arbitrary. For
\epsilon,\delta>0
there is a (small) constant
C=C(\epsilon,\delta,\kappa)>0
so that for
Y\leq C
we have

(3.4)

I_{f}(Y)\ll_{\kappa,\epsilon}S_{f}(Y^{-1-\delta})+YN^{2+\epsilon}\|f\|^{2},

where

S_{f}(X)=\sum_{n\leq X}|a_{f}(n)|^{2}n^{1-\kappa}.

Proof.

For a large parameter
K>0
we use (
3.2
) to estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{n>K}|a_{f}(n)|^{2}\cdot\int_{Y}^{\infty}e^{-4\pi ny}y^{\kappa-2}dy

\displaystyle\qquad\ll_{\epsilon}dN^{1+\epsilon}\|f\|^{2}\sum_{n>K}n^{\epsilon}\int_{Y}^{\infty}e^{-4\pi ny}(ny)^{\kappa-1}\frac{dy}{y}

\displaystyle\qquad\ll_{\epsilon}dN^{1+\epsilon}\|f\|^{2}\sum_{n>K}n^{\epsilon}\int_{nY}^{\infty}e^{-4\pi y}y^{\kappa-2}dy

\displaystyle\qquad\ll_{\epsilon}dN^{1+\epsilon}\|f\|^{2}\int_{KY}^{\infty}e^{-4\pi y}y^{\kappa-2}\sum_{K\leq n<\frac{y}{Y}}n^{\epsilon}dy

\displaystyle\qquad\ll_{\epsilon}\frac{dN^{1+\epsilon}}{Y^{1+\epsilon}}\|f\|^{2}\int_{KY}^{\infty}e^{-4\pi y}y^{\kappa-1+\epsilon}dy

\displaystyle\qquad\ll_{\epsilon}N^{2+\epsilon}\kappa^{1+\epsilon}\|f\|^{2}\cdot\frac{e^{-(4\pi-1)KY}}{Y^{1+\epsilon}}\cdot\int_{KY}^{\infty}e^{-y}y^{\kappa-1+\epsilon}dy.

If we assume that
YK>1
, which is a reasonable assumption, then we can estimate the remaining integral using repeated partial integration. We end up with

\sum_{n>K}|a_{f}(n)|^{2}\cdot\int_{Y}^{\infty}e^{-4\pi ny}y^{\kappa-2}dy\ll_{\epsilon}N^{2+\epsilon}\kappa^{1+\epsilon}\cdot\frac{e^{-4\pi KY}(KY)^{\kappa-1+\epsilon}}{Y^{1+\epsilon}}\cdot\|f\|^{2}.

On the other hand we compute

\sum_{n\leq K}|a_{f}(n)|^{2}\cdot\int_{Y}^{\infty}e^{-4\pi ny}y^{\kappa-2}dy\leq\int_{Y}^{\infty}y^{\kappa-2}e^{-4\pi y}dy\sum_{n\leq K}|a_{f}(n)|^{2}n^{1-\kappa}\ll\frac{(\kappa-2)!}{(4\pi)^{\kappa-1}}\cdot S_{f}(K).

For fixed
\kappa
we choose
K=Y^{-1-\delta}
and obtain

I_{f}(Y)\ll_{\kappa,\epsilon}S_{f}(Y^{-1-\delta})+N^{2+\epsilon}\cdot e^{-4\pi Y^{-\delta}}Y^{-\delta(\kappa-1+\epsilon)-1-\epsilon}\cdot\|f\|^{2}

Note that for
x\gg_{\epsilon,\delta,\kappa}1
we have

(3.5)

e^{-4\pi x}x^{\kappa-1+\epsilon}\leq x^{-\frac{2+\epsilon}{\delta}}.

We take
C=C(\epsilon,\delta,\kappa)>0
so that for
Y\leq C
the inequality (
3.5
) holds for
x=Y^{-\delta}
. The claimed statement follows directly.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

In principle one can make the estimate (
3.4
) explicit in
\kappa
. Since this is not required for our application, we do not pursue this here.

Next we compute the hyperbolic volume of
\mathcal{F}_{N}\cap\mathcal{P}^{c}_{Y}
. Recall that
Y>0
is a small parameter, so that this volume should be tiny.

The set of cusps of
\Gamma_{0}(N)
can be parametrized by

\mathfrak{C}_{N}=\{\frac{u}{v}\colon v\mid N,\,(u,v)=1\text{ and }u\text{ mod }(v,\frac{N}{v})\}.

Note that
1/N
is equivalent to the cusp
\infty
, which plays a special role for us. We can choose the fundamental domain
\mathcal{F}_{N}
, so that it has cuspidal vertices at the representatives in
\mathfrak{C}_{N}\setminus\{\frac{1}{N}\}
and
\infty
.

The width of a cusp
\mathfrak{a}=\frac{u}{v}\in\mathfrak{C}_{N}
is given by

w_{\mathfrak{a}}=\frac{N}{(N,v^{2})}.

Furthermore, we choose a matrix

\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}=\left(\begin{matrix}u&\ast\\
v&\ast\end{matrix}\right)\in\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}).

In particular,
\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}\infty=\mathfrak{a}
and

\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}^{-1}\Gamma_{\mathfrak{a}}\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}=\left\{\pm\left(\begin{matrix}1&w_{\mathfrak{a}}m\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right)\colon m\in\mathbb{Z}\right\},

where
\Gamma_{\mathfrak{a}}\subseteq\Gamma_{0}(N)
is the stabilizer of the cusp in question.

Let
K
be a parameter, which we imagine large. We define the horocycle

L(K)=\{x+iK\colon x\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Given a matrix

\sigma=\left(\begin{matrix}u&\ast\\
v&\ast\end{matrix}\right)\in\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}),

we observe that
\sigma L(K)
is mapped to the circle tangent to
u/v
of diameter
(v^{2}K)^{-1}
. In particular,
\sigma
maps the set
\mathcal{P}_{K}
to the interior of this circle. It is easy compute that

\textrm{Vol}(\sigma\mathcal{P}_{K})=\textrm{Vol}(\mathcal{P}_{K})=\frac{1}{K}.

Using these observations we can estimate

\displaystyle\textrm{Vol}(\mathcal{F}_{N}\cap\mathcal{P}^{c}_{Y})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\frac{1}{N}\neq\mathfrak{a}=u/v\in\mathfrak{C}_{N}}w_{\mathfrak{a}}\textrm{Vol}(\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}\overline{\mathcal{P}_{(2v^{2}Y)^{-1}}})

\displaystyle=2Y\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\mid N,\\
v\neq N\end{subarray}}\frac{Nv^{2}}{(v^{2},N)}\cdot\varphi((v,\frac{N}{v})).

The remaining sum is multiplicative in
N
and is easily evaluated on prime powers. Indeed, if we call

\psi(N)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\mid N\end{subarray}}\frac{Nv^{2}}{(v^{2},N)}\cdot\varphi((v,\frac{N}{v})),

then we compute

\psi(N)=N^{2}\cdot\prod_{p\mid N}(1+\frac{1}{p})\ll_{\epsilon}N^{2+\epsilon}.

We conclude that

(3.6)

\textrm{Vol}(\mathcal{F}_{N}\cap\mathcal{P}^{c}_{Y})\ll_{\epsilon}N^{2+\epsilon}Y.

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
f\in S_{\kappa}(N)
be an arbitrary non-trivial (i.e.
f\neq 0
) form. For a fixed
\epsilon>0
, there are constants
c_{1}=c_{1}(\epsilon,\kappa)
and
c_{2}(\epsilon,\kappa)
such that

\|f\|^{2}\leq\frac{c_{1}}{[\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\colon\Gamma_{0}(N)]}\cdot\sum_{n\leq c_{2}N^{2+\epsilon}}|a_{f}(n)|^{2}n^{1-\kappa}.

Therefore the
m
-th Fourier coefficient
a_{f}(m)
is bounded by

(3.7)

|a_{f}(m)|\ll_{\epsilon,\kappa}N^{1/2+\epsilon}\left(\sum_{n\leq c_{2}N^{2+\epsilon}}|a_{f}(n)|^{2}n^{1-\kappa}\right)^{1/2}m^{\frac{\kappa-1}{2}+\epsilon}

for all
m
.

Proof.

Let
Y>0
be a small parameter chosen later. We start with the estimate

\displaystyle\|f\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{[\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\colon\Gamma_{0}(N)]}\int_{\mathcal{F}_{N}}|f(x+iy)|^{2}y^{\kappa-2}dxdy

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{[\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\colon\Gamma_{0}(N)]}\cdot I_{f}(Y)+\frac{1}{[\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\colon\Gamma_{0}(N)]}\int_{\mathcal{F}_{N}\cap\mathcal{P}^{c}_{Y}}|f(x+iy)|^{2}y^{\kappa-2}dxdy

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{[\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\colon\Gamma_{0}(N)]}\cdot I_{f}(Y)+\frac{\textrm{Vol}(\mathcal{F}_{N}\cap\mathcal{P}^{c}_{Y})}{[\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\colon\Gamma_{0}(N)]}\cdot\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}.

According to (
3.3
) and (
3.6
) we obtain

\frac{\textrm{Vol}(\mathcal{F}_{N}\cap\mathcal{P}^{c}_{Y})}{[\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\colon\Gamma_{0}(N)]}\cdot\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}\ll_{\kappa,\epsilon}YN^{2+\epsilon}\|f\|^{2}.

Let us call the implicit constant in this bound
C_{2}=C_{2}(\epsilon,\kappa)
. Similarly we write

\frac{1}{[\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\colon\Gamma_{0}(N)]}\cdot I_{f}(Y)\leq\frac{C_{1}}{[\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\colon\Gamma_{0}(N)]}S_{f}(Y^{-1-\epsilon})+C_{1}YN^{1+\epsilon}\|f\|^{2},

for a constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(\epsilon,\kappa)
. This leaves us with

\|f\|^{2}\leq\frac{C_{1}}{[\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\colon\Gamma_{0}(N)]}\cdot\left(1-C_{2}YN^{2+\epsilon}-C_{1}YN^{1+\epsilon}\right)^{-1}\cdot\sum_{n\leq Y^{-(1+\epsilon)}}|a_{f}(n)|^{2}n^{1-\kappa}.

Choosing
Y\asymp N^{-2-\epsilon}
yields

\|f\|^{2}\ll_{\kappa,\epsilon}\frac{1}{[\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\colon\Gamma_{0}(N)]}\cdot\sum_{n\ll_{\kappa,\epsilon}N^{2+\epsilon}}|a_{f}(n)|^{2}n^{1-\kappa}.

This gives the desired bound on the Petersson norm. Substituting this into (
3.2
) finishes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

This argument is quite flexible and works for more general congruence subgroups (i.e.
\Gamma_{1}(N)
) and more general weights (i.e.
\kappa
half integral).

Remark 3.7
.

Considering that the dimension
d
of
S_{\kappa}(N)
satisfies
N^{1-\epsilon}\ll_{\kappa,\epsilon}d\ll_{\kappa,\epsilon}N^{1+\epsilon}
, the best bound for the Petersson norm of a general element
f\in S_{\kappa}(N)
one can expect is of the form

\|f\|^{2}\leq\frac{c_{1}}{[\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\colon\Gamma_{0}(N)]}\cdot\sum_{n\leq c_{2}N^{1+\epsilon}}|a_{f}(n)|^{2}n^{1-\kappa}.

However, such a bound seems to be out of reach of our method.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section, we will prove Theorem
1.1
.
To do this, we record two lemmas concerning the sizes of the Fourier coefficients of
g_{k,\mathfrak{p},r}
defined in (
2.26
).

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
g_{k,p,r}\in S_{2k}(N)
be the modular form defined in (
2.26
) with
k>1,N=p^{1+2r},r\geq 0
and
p\in\mathbb{N}
a prime.
Then

(4.1)

|c_{k}(m;N)|\ll_{k,\epsilon}\frac{m^{k-\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}}{N\sqrt{D}}\min\left(m^{1/2},N^{5/2+\epsilon}\right)

for any
m\geq 1
and
\epsilon>0
.
In particular, the implied constant is independent of
N
and
m
.

Proof.

We only need to consider those primes
p
such that there exists a prime
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
of
F
that is inert in
K
, otherwise
g_{k,p,r}
is trivial.
In that case any prime
\tilde{\mathfrak{p}}\mid p
will be non-split in
K
.
For such
p
, putting together (
2.23
) and (
2.27
) then gives us

c_{k}(n;p^{2r+1})=n^{k-1}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\mid p}\sum_{a\in 2\mathbb{Z}+nD,|a|<n\sqrt{D}}P_{k-1}\left(\frac{a}{n\sqrt{D}}\right)\rho_{K/F}\left(\frac{n\sqrt{D}+a}{2}/\mathfrak{p}^{2r+1}\right),

where we have written
t=\frac{n\sqrt{D}+a}{2\sqrt{D}}
.
For totally positive

t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathfrak{p}^{2r+1}
with

t_{i}=\frac{m\sqrt{D}+a_{i}}{2\sqrt{D}}\in\mathfrak{d}^{-1}
, we have
a_{1}-a_{2}\in\mathfrak{p}^{2r+1}\sqrt{D}\cap\mathbb{Z}=p^{2r+1}D\mathbb{Z}
.
So for each
r
, any non-zero
a
appearing in the summand is bounded above by
n/(p^{2r+1}\sqrt{D})
. Moreover, when
|a|<n\sqrt{D}
, we have
P_{k-1}\left(\frac{a}{n\sqrt{D}}\right)<1
. Finally, the ideal counting function
\rho_{K/F}(\mathfrak{a})
is bounded by
\log{\mathrm{Nm}}(\mathfrak{a})
for any integral ideal
\mathfrak{a}
. Putting these together gives us

(4.2)

|c_{k}(n;p^{2r+1})|\ll\frac{n^{k}\log n}{p^{2r+1}\sqrt{D}}.

Putting this into (
3.7
) gives us

n^{1-\kappa}
in the sum

(4.3)

|c_{k}(m;p^{2r+1})|\ll_{\epsilon}N^{3/2+\epsilon}D^{-1/2}m^{k-1/2+\epsilon}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By equidistribution of Hecke correspondence on product of modular curves
[
COU01
, Theorem 1.1]
and negativity of higher Green function
G_{k}
, we have

|G^{m}_{k}(z_{1},z_{2})|\gg_{z_{1},z_{2},k}m

as
m\to\infty
.
Applying this to (
2.25
) with
k=3,5,7
and combining with Remarks
2.8
and Corollary
2.7
gives us

(4.4)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p,r\\
p^{2r+1}\leq\frac{m^{2}}{4}\end{subarray}}\log p\cdot|c_{k}(m;p^{2r+1})|=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\pi(m),r\\
p^{2r+1}\leq\frac{m^{2}}{4}\end{subarray}}\log p\cdot|c_{k}(m;p^{2r+1})|\gg_{D_{1},D_{2}}m^{k}.

The size of the summation above is then bounded by
|\pi(m)|\cdot\log m
.
Denote
\delta=1/5
.
On the one hand, the second bound in (
4.1
) gives us

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\pi(m),r\\
p^{2r+1}\leq m^{\delta}\end{subarray}}\log p\cdot|c_{k}(m;p^{2r+1})|

\displaystyle\ll_{\epsilon}|\pi(m)|\cdot\log m\cdot m^{k-1/2+\epsilon}(m^{\delta})^{3/2+\epsilon}/\sqrt{D}.

On the other hand, the first bound in (
4.1
) yields

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\pi(m),r\\
m^{\delta}<p^{2r+1}\leq m^{2}/4\end{subarray}}\log p\cdot|c_{k}(m;p^{2r+1})|

\displaystyle\ll_{\epsilon}|\pi(m)|\cdot\frac{m^{k+\epsilon}}{m^{\delta}\sqrt{D}}.

Adding them together and putting it into (
4.4
)
completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The quality of the lower bound in Theorem
1.1
directly depends on the strength of the upper bound in (
4.1
). Indeed, running our argument with a general bound of the form

|c_{k}(m;N)|\ll_{k,\epsilon}\frac{m^{k-\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}}{N\sqrt{D}}\min\left(m^{1/2},N^{\alpha+\epsilon}\right)\text{ for }\alpha\geq 1,

would yield

\#\pi(m)\gg_{E_{1},E_{2},\epsilon}m^{\frac{1}{2\alpha}-\epsilon}.

In particular, the bound stated in Remark
3.7
would allow us to use
\alpha=\frac{3}{2}
and thus produce the lower bound
\#\pi(m)\gg_{E_{1},E_{2},\epsilon}m^{\frac{1}{3}-\epsilon}
. We conclude that, using our method, any improvement upon the exponent
\frac{1}{3}
would rely on a finer understanding of the Petersson norms of the cusp forms
g_{k,p,r}
.

5.
Relation with minimal isogeny degree bound

We keep the notation from the introduction. Recall that for each
i=1,2
,
E_{i}/H
is a CM elliptic curve with CM by different orders
\mathcal{O}_{D_{i}}
. According to our discussion in the introduction and by Deuring’s criterion, there is a density 1/4 subset of the rational primes
p
satisfying that, for any
\mathfrak{p}
in
H
above
p
that is also a prime of good reduction, one has
E_{1,\mathfrak{p}}\sim E_{2,\mathfrak{p}}
over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
. For any such prime
\mathfrak{p}
in
H
, we define

m_{D_{1},D_{2}}(\mathfrak{p}):=\min\{\deg\phi:\phi:E_{1,\mathfrak{p}}\to E_{2,\mathfrak{p}}\text{ is a non-isomorphic isogeny over $\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}$}\},

which represents the minimal isogeny degree between
E_{1,\mathfrak{p}}
and
E_{2,\mathfrak{p}}
. Note that
m_{D_{1},D_{2}}(\mathfrak{p})
is independent of the choice of
\mathfrak{p}
, so we can also write it as
m_{D_{1},D_{2}}(p)
. For primes
\mathfrak{p}
where no isogeny exists between
E_{1,\mathfrak{p}}
and
E_{2,\mathfrak{p}}
, we set
m_{D_{1},D_{2}}(p)=0
.

It is known that for supersingular elliptic curves, the minimal cyclic isogeny degree is at most
\lfloor\frac{1}{2}p^{2/3}+\frac{1}{4}\rfloor

[
Elkies1987
, Section 4]
, and the exponent
2/3
is optimal
[
Yang2008
, Proposition 1.4, p. 1319]
. From the discussion before Theorem
1.1
, for each prime
\mathfrak{p}
in
H
, if
E_{1,\mathfrak{p}}
and
E_{2,\mathfrak{p}}
are isogenous, then they must be supersingular elliptic curves. In particular, the minimal isogeny bound applies to
E_{1,\mathfrak{p}}
and
E_{2,\mathfrak{p}}
. The following result shows that either we can find better upper bounds of
m_{D_{1},D_{2}}(p)
for plenty of primes
p
, or better upper bounds of
\#\pi(m)
for almost all integers
m
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
be coprime fundamental discriminants and set
D:=D_{1}D_{2}
.
Let
x
be a positive real number. For any
C>0
,
0<\delta<\frac{2}{3}
, and
0<\eta<\frac{1}{2}
, as
x\to\infty
, we have either

(1)

\#\{p\leq x:m_{D_{1},D_{2}}(p)\leq p^{2/3-\delta}\}\gg_{E_{1},E_{2}}x^{1/3+\delta}
or

(2)

\#\{m\leq x:\#\pi(m)\geq Cm^{1-\eta}\}\ll_{E_{1},E_{2},\eta,C}x^{\frac{5/6+\delta}{1-\eta}}
.

In particular, for any sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, we have either

(1)

\#\{p\leq\frac{x^{2}D}{2}:m_{D_{1},D_{2}}(p)\leq p^{2/3-\epsilon}\}\gg_{E_{1},E_{2},\epsilon}x^{2/3+2\epsilon}
or

(2)

\#\{m\leq x:\#\pi(m)\geq Cm^{\frac{5}{6}+2\epsilon}\}\ll_{E_{1},E_{2},\epsilon,C}x^{1-\epsilon}
.

Proof.

First, we observe that

(5.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{m\leq x}\#\pi(m)=\sum_{m\leq x}\#\{p\leq\frac{m^{2}D}{2}:\exists\;\mathfrak{p}\text{ of $H$ such that }\mathfrak{p}\mid p,\mathfrak{p}\nmid N_{E_{1}}N_{E_{2}},E_{1,\mathfrak{p}}\sim_{m}E_{2,\mathfrak{p}}\},

where
N_{E_{1}}
and
N_{E_{2}}
are conductors of
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
, respectively.

On the other hand, by Deuring’s correspondence, the equivalence class of a degree
m
isogeny in
\mathrm{Hom}(E_{1,\mathfrak{p}},E_{2,\mathfrak{p}})
corresponds to a left ideal class in the endomorphism ring
\mathrm{End}_{\overline{\mathbb{F}}{p}}(E_{1,\mathfrak{p}})
whose reduced norm is
m
. Therefore, the degree of any isogeny in
\mathrm{Hom}(E_{1,\mathfrak{p}},E_{2,\mathfrak{p}})
is of the form
km^{\prime}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
m^{\prime}\geq m_{D_{1},D_{2}}(p)
. Hence, for any fixed
0<\delta<2/3
, we have

(5.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{m\leq x}\#\{p\leq\frac{m^{2}D}{2}:\exists\;\mathfrak{p}\text{ of $H$ such that }\mathfrak{p}\mid p,\mathfrak{p}\nmid N_{E_{1}}N_{E_{2}},E_{1,\mathfrak{p}}\sim_{m}E_{2,\mathfrak{p}}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\leq\frac{x^{2}D}{2}}\#\left\{\sqrt{\frac{2p}{D}}\leq m\leq x:\exists\;\mathfrak{p}\text{ of $H$ such that }\mathfrak{p}\mid p,\mathfrak{p}\nmid N_{E_{1}}N_{E_{2}},E_{1,\mathfrak{p}}\sim_{m}E_{2,\mathfrak{p}}\right\}

\displaystyle\ll_{E_{1},E_{2}}\sum_{p\leq\frac{x^{2}D}{2}}\frac{x}{m_{D_{1},D_{2}}(p)}

\displaystyle\ll_{E_{1},E_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq\frac{x^{2}D}{2}\\
p^{2/3-\delta}\leq m_{D_{1},D_{2}}(p)\leq p^{2/3}\end{subarray}}\frac{x}{m_{D_{1},D_{2}}(p)}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq\frac{x^{2}D}{2}\\
m_{D_{1},D_{2}}(p)\leq p^{2/3-\delta}\end{subarray}}\frac{x}{m_{D_{1},D_{2}}(p)}

\displaystyle\ll_{E_{1},E_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq\frac{x^{2}D}{2}\\
p^{2/3-\delta}\leq m_{D_{1},D_{2}}(p)\leq p^{2/3}\end{subarray}}\frac{x}{p^{2/3-\delta}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq\frac{x^{2}D}{2}\\
m_{D_{1},D_{2}}(p)\leq p^{2/3-\delta}\end{subarray}}x

\displaystyle\ll_{E_{1},E_{2}}x^{5/3+2\delta}+x\cdot\#\{p\leq\frac{x^{2}D}{2}:m_{D_{1},D_{2}}(p)\leq p^{2/3-\delta}\}

We assume
\#\{p\leq\frac{x^{2}D}{2}:m_{D_{1},D_{2}}(p)\leq p^{2/3-\delta}\}\ll_{E_{1},E_{2}}x^{2/3+2\delta}
.
For any
0<\eta<1/2
and
C>0
set

M_{\eta}(x):=\#\{m\leq x:\#\pi(m)\geq Cm^{1-\eta}\}

Combining with (
5.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle x^{5/3+2\delta}\gg_{E_{1},E_{2}}\sum_{m\leq x}\#\pi(m)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x\\
\#\pi(m)\geq Cm^{1-\eta}\end{subarray}}\#\pi(m)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x\\
\#\pi(m)<Cm^{1-\eta}\end{subarray}}\#\pi(m)

\displaystyle\gg_{E_{1},E_{2}}

\displaystyle\sum_{m\leq M_{\eta}(x)}Cm^{1-\eta}\gg_{E_{1},E_{2}}M_{\eta}(x)^{2-2\eta}.

Hence,

\#\{m\leq x:\#\pi(m)\geq Cm^{1-\eta}\}\ll_{E_{1},E_{2},\eta,C}x^{\frac{5/6+\delta}{1-\eta}}.

The first statement follows.

Now, for any sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, taking
\delta=\epsilon
,
\eta=1/6-2\epsilon
,
as
x\to\infty
,
we get either

\#\{p\leq\frac{x^{2}D}{2}:m_{D_{1},D_{2}}(p)\leq p^{2/3-\epsilon}\}\gg_{E_{1},E_{2},\epsilon}x^{2/3+2\epsilon}

or

\#\{m\leq x:\#\pi(m)\geq Cm^{\frac{5}{6}+2\epsilon}\}\ll_{E_{1},E_{2},\epsilon,C}x^{1-\epsilon}.

The second result follows as well.

∎
