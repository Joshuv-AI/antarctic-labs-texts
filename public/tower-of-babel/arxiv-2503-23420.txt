Solving Indefinite Quadratic Programs by Dynamical Systems: Preliminary Investigations
Massimo Pappalardo, Nguyen Nang Thieu, Nguyen Dong Yen

Abstract
Preliminary results of our investigations on solving indefinite quadratic programs by dynamical systems are given. First, dynamical systems corresponding to two fundamental DC programming algorithms to deal with indefinite quadratic programs are considered. Second, the existence and the uniqueness of the global solution of the dynamical system are proved by using some theorems from nonsmooth analysis and the theory of ordinary differential equations. Third, the strong pseudomonotonicity of the restriction of an affine operator on a closed convex set is analyzed in a special case. Finally, for a parametric indefinite quadratic program related to that special case, convergence of the trajectories of the dynamical system to the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker points is established. The elementary direct proofs in the third and fourth topics would be useful for understanding the meaning and significance of several open problems proposed in this paper.

Trajectories of the Dynamical Systems

The following result on the existence and uniqueness of a global solution of the dynamical system (
5
), which is an initial value problem for differential equations (see, e.g., the IVP given by formula (2.12) in
(
30
, p. 26)

Theorem 2.1

For any
x^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\rho>0
, and
\eta>0
, there exists a unique
C^{1}
function
x:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying the differential equation and the initial condition in (
5
).

Proof

Let
F(x)=\dfrac{1}{\eta}\left[P_{C}\left(x-\dfrac{1}{\rho}\big(Qx+q\big)\right)-x\right]
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since the operator
P_{C}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to C
is nonexpansive
(
15
, Chapter I, Corollary 2.4)
, one has for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the estimates

\begin{array}[]{rcl}&&\|F(y)-F(x)\|\\
&=&\big\|\dfrac{1}{\eta}\left[P_{C}\left(y-\dfrac{1}{\rho}\big(Qy+q\big)\right)-y\right]-\dfrac{1}{\eta}\left[P_{C}\left(x-\dfrac{1}{\rho}\big(Qx+q\big)\right)-x\right]\big\|\\
&\leq&\dfrac{1}{\eta}\left[\big\|P_{C}\left(y-\dfrac{1}{\rho}\big(Qy+q\big)\right)-P_{C}\left(x-\dfrac{1}{\rho}\big(Qx+q\big)\right)\big\|+\|y-x\|\right]\\
&\leq&\dfrac{1}{\eta}\left[\big\|\left(y-\dfrac{1}{\rho}\big(Qy+q\big)\right)-\left(x-\dfrac{1}{\rho}\big(Qx+q\big)\right)\big\|+\|y-x\|\right]\\
&\leq&\dfrac{1}{\eta}\left(\dfrac{1}{\rho}\|Q\|+2\right)\|y-x\|.\end{array}

Thus, the mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is globally Lipschitz with the constant
\dfrac{1}{\eta}\left(\dfrac{1}{\rho}\|Q\|+2\right)
. Rewrite (
5
) as

\dot{x}=F(x),\quad x(0)=x^{0}.

(10)

Applying the Picard-Lindelöf Theorem (see, e.g.,
(
30
, Chapter 2, Theorem 2.3)
), we can assert that the initial value problem (
10
) has a locally unique solution.

Now, to show that all solutions of the IVP in (
10
) are defined for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by using the result in
(
30
, Chapter 2, Theorem 2.12)
, it suffices to prove that there are constants
M
and
L
such that

\|F(x)\|\leq M+L\|x\|\quad\;\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(11)

To do so, select any point
\bar{x}\in C
and note that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\|F(x)\|&=&\big\|\dfrac{1}{\eta}\Big[P_{C}\left(x-\dfrac{1}{\rho}\big(Qx+q\big)\right)-x\Big]\big\|\\
&=&\dfrac{1}{\eta}\big\|\Big[P_{C}\left(x-\dfrac{1}{\rho}\big(Qx+q\big)\right)-x\Big]\big\|\\
&=&\dfrac{1}{\eta}\big\|\Big[\left\{P_{C}\left(x-\dfrac{1}{\rho}\big(Qx+q\big)\right)-\left(x-\dfrac{1}{\rho}\big(Qx+q\big)\right)\right\}-\dfrac{1}{\rho}\big(Qx+q\big)\Big]\big\|\\
&\leq&\dfrac{1}{\eta}\Big[\big\|\bar{x}-\left(x-\dfrac{1}{\rho}\big(Qx+q)\right)\|+\dfrac{1}{\rho}\|Qx+q\|\Big]\\
&\leq&\dfrac{1}{\eta}\Big[\left(\|\bar{x}\|+\dfrac{2}{\rho}\|q\|\right)+\left(\dfrac{2}{\rho}\|Q\|+1\right)\|x\|\Big].\end{array}

Therefore, the growth condition (
11
) is fulfilled with

M:=\dfrac{1}{\eta}\left(\|\bar{x}-q\|+\dfrac{1}{\rho}\|q\|\right),\quad\;L:=\dfrac{1}{\eta}\left(\dfrac{2}{\rho}\|Q\|+1\right).

We have proved that there is a unique
C^{1}
function
x(\cdot)
defined on the whole real line satisfying the conditions in (
5
).

\hfill\Box

Theorem 2.2

For any
x^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\rho>0
, and
\eta>0
, the dynamical system (
9
) has a unique
C^{1}
trajectory
x(\cdot)
, which is defined on the whole real line.

Proof

Let
\ell
be a positive constant satisfying the condition (
7
). For every vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, put
G(x)=\dfrac{1}{\eta}\left[F_{C}(x)-x\right]
. By (
7
), we have for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the following

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\|G(y)-G(x)\|&=&\big\|\dfrac{1}{\eta}\big[(F_{C}(y)-y)-(F_{C}(x)-x)\big]\big\|\\
&\leq&\dfrac{1}{\eta}\left[\big\|F_{C}(y)-F_{C}(x)\big\|+\|y-x\|\right]\\[6.45831pt]
&\leq&\dfrac{1}{\eta}(\ell+1)\|y-x\|.\end{array}

So, the mapping
G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is globally Lipschitz with the constant
\dfrac{1}{\eta}(\ell+1)
. Rewrite the system (
9
) as

\dot{x}=G(x),\quad x(0)=x^{0}.

(12)

This IVP has a locally unique solution by the Picard-Lindelöf Theorem.

There are constants
M_{1}
and
L_{1}
such that

\|G(x)\|\leq M_{1}+L_{1}\|x\|\quad\;\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(13)

Indeed, fixing a point
\bar{x}\in C
, we have for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the estimates

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\|G(x)\|=\big\|\dfrac{1}{\eta}\left[F_{C}(\bar{x})-x\right]\big\|&=&\dfrac{1}{\eta}\big\|\left(F_{C}(x)-F_{C}(\bar{x})\right)-\left(F_{C}(\bar{x})-x\right)\big\|\\[6.45831pt]
&\leq&\dfrac{1}{\eta}\Big[\ell\|x-\bar{x}\|+\|F_{C}(\bar{x})-x\|\Big]\\[6.45831pt]
&\leq&\dfrac{1}{\eta}\Big[\ell\|\bar{x}\|+\|F_{C}(\bar{x})\|+(1+\ell)\|x\|\Big]\end{array}

Therefore, the growth condition (
13
) is satisfied if we choose

M_{1}=\dfrac{1}{\eta}\left(\ell\|\bar{x}\|+\|F_{C}(\bar{x})\|\right),\quad\;L_{1}=\dfrac{1+\ell}{\eta}.

Thanks to (
13
), by
(
30

we can assert all solutions of the IVP in (
12
) are defined for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We have seen that the system (
9
) possesses a unique
C^{1}
trajectory
x(\cdot)
, which is defined on the whole real line.
\hfill\Box

is a convex set, the Clarke tangent cone
T_{C}(\bar{x})
to
C
at
\bar{x}
can be computed by (
15
). This together with the inclusions in (
16
) implies that
G(\bar{x})\in T_{C}(\bar{x})
. Furthermore, as shown in the proof of Theorem
2.2
,
G
is Lipschitz continuous. Since
\bar{x}\in C
was chosen arbitrarily, we deduce from
(
6
, Theorem 4.4)
that
C
is flow-invariant for
G
. Consequently,
x(t)\in C
for all
t\geq 0
.

\hfill\Box

and

, do not rely on the assumption that

3
Which Affine Operators are Strongly Pseudomonotone?

As in
(
10
, Definition 2.3.1)
and
(
13
, Definition 2.1)
, an operator
F:C\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
C
is a convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, is said to be

(a)
monotone
if
\langle F(y)-F(x),y-x\rangle\geq 0
for all
x,y\in C
;

(b)
pseudomonotone
if for all
x,y\in C
it holds

\langle F(x),y-x\rangle\geq 0\ \;\Longrightarrow\ \;\langle F(y),y-x\rangle\geq 0;

(c)
strongly monotone
if there exists a constant
\gamma>0
such that

\langle F(y)-F(x),y-x\rangle\geq\gamma\|y-x\|^{2}

for all
x,y\in C
;

(d)
strongly pseudomonotone
if there exists a constant
\gamma>0
such that

\langle F(x),y-x\rangle\geq 0\ \;\Longrightarrow\ \;\langle F(y),y-x\rangle\geq\gamma\|y-x\|^{2}

(17)

for all
x,y\in C
.

If the property (
17
) holds for all
x,y\in C
, then one says that
F
is

\gamma
-strongly pseudomonotone on
C
.

Remark 3

If
F:C\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a monotone (resp., pseudomonotone, strongly monotone, strongly pseudomonotone) operator and
\lambda
is a positive real number, then the operator
\lambda F:C\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by setting
(\lambda F)(x)=\lambda F(x)
for all
x\in C
is also monotone (resp., pseudomonotone, strongly monotone, strongly pseudomonotone). Thus, the set of monotone (resp., pseudomonotone, strongly monotone, strongly pseudomonotone) operators from
C
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a cone.

As it has been noted in Section
1
, strong pseudomonotonicity of the operator defining a variational inequality is an important property, and many existing results rely on the latter. In an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space setting, Khanh and Vuong
(
13
, Example 4.1)
gave some examples of strongly pseudomonotone operators, which are neither strongly monotone nor monotone. Given by the formula
F_{\beta}(x)=(\beta-\|x\|)x
on the set
C_{r}:=\{x\mid\|x\|\leq r\}
, where the constants
\beta
and
r
satisfy the conditions
\beta>r>\dfrac{\beta}{2}>0
, these operators are non-affine. In
[
13
]
, it is proved that
F_{\beta}
is strongly pseudomonotone on
C_{r}
with the coefficient
\alpha:=\beta-r>0
.

Focusing on the restrictions of affine operators on convex sets (called
affine operators on convex sets
for brevity), we have the following natural question.

Question 5:

Whether there is a strongly pseudomonotone affine operator on a convex set, which is not strongly monotone on that set?

The next example gives an answer in the affirmative to Question 5.

Example 1

Let
A:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by
A(x)=-x+1
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
and let
C=\left[0,\frac{1}{2}\right]
. For any
x,y\in C
, if

(-x+1)(y-x)\geq 0,

(18)

then
y-x\geq 0
. Since
x,y\in C
, we also have
1\geq 2y-x
or, equivalently,
1-y\geq y-x
. As
y-x\geq 0
, we can multiply both sides of the last inequality by
(y-x)
to get

(-y+1)(y-x)\geq(y-x)^{2}.

(19)

Therefore, we have shown that if (
18
) holds, i.e.,
\langle A(x),y-x\rangle\geq 0,
then (
19
) is valid, i.e.,
\langle A(y),y-x\rangle\geq|y-x|^{2}.

So,
A
is
\gamma
-strongly pseudomonotone on
C
with the constant
\gamma:=1
.

The affine function
A
is non-monotone on
C
. Indeed, for any
x,y\in C
with
x\neq y
we have

\langle A(y)-A(x),y-x\rangle=\big[(-y+1)-(-x+1)\big](y-x)=-(y-x)^{2}<0.

Thus, we have seen that the affine function
A
is strongly pseudomonotone on
C
, but not monotone on
C
.

Example
1
shows that for affine operators on convex sets, strong pseudomonotonicity is a genuine relaxed version of strong monotonicity. In addition, the example demonstrates that strongly pseudomonotone affine operators on convex sets on convex sets may be non-monotone as well.

The subset
C=\left[0,\frac{1}{2}\right]
of
\mathbb{R}
in Example
1
is non-symmetric with respect to 0. So, it is of interest to examine the effects produced by the example for affine operators on convex sets which are symmetric w.r.t. the origin of the space under consideration. Note that the closed unit ball in
\mathbb{R}
is exactly the segment
[-1,1]
, which is also a compact polyhedral convex set with nonempty interior. Hence, the next question is of interest as it seeks for a
complete characterization of strong monotonicity for affine operators defined on the closed unit ball
of a special Euclidean space.

Question 6:

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by
F(x)=\alpha x+\beta
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. Under which conditions on
(\alpha,\beta)
is
F
strongly pseudomonotone on
C:=[-1,1]
?

Theorem 3.1

Let
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by
F(x)=\alpha x+\beta
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
with
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, and
C:=[-1,1]
. Then,
F
is strongly pseudomonotone if and only if
(\alpha,\beta)
belongs to the cone

\mathcal{A}:=\big\{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid\alpha+\beta>0\;\;\text{\rm or}\;\;\alpha-\beta>0\big\}.

Figure 1:
The cone
\mathcal{A}
is non-closed

Proof

Depending on the position of the parameter pair
(\alpha,\beta)
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have the following six cases.

Case 1:

\alpha=\beta=0
. We have
F(x)\equiv 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus,
F
is not strongly pseudomonotone on
C
.

Case 2:

\alpha=0
and
\beta\neq 0
. In this case,
F(x)=\beta
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. If
x,y\in C
are such that
\beta(y-x)\geq 0
, then we have

\beta(y-x)=|\beta||y-x|=\dfrac{|\beta|}{2}(2|y-x|)\geq\dfrac{|\beta|}{2}(y-x)^{2},

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
|y-x|\leq 2
for any
x,y\in C
. So,
F
is
\gamma
-strongly pseudomonotone on
C
with
\gamma=\dfrac{|\beta|}{2}
.

Case 3:

\alpha>0
. Then, for all
x,y\in C
and for all
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, one has

\langle F(y)-F(x),y-x\rangle=\big[(\alpha y+\beta)-(\alpha x+\beta)\big](y-x)=\alpha(y-x)^{2}.

Therefore,
F
is
\gamma
-strongly monotone on
C
for
\gamma:=\alpha
.

Case 4:

\alpha<0
and
\beta=0
. Letting
x=-1
and
y=1
, we have

\langle F(x),y-x\rangle=\alpha x(y-x)=-2\alpha>0,

while

\langle F(y),y-x\rangle=\alpha y(y-x)=2\alpha<0.

Hence,
F
is not pseudomonotone on
C
.

Case 5:

\alpha<0
and
\beta>0
. We will consider the 3 following subcases.

Subcase 5a:

\alpha<-\beta
, i.e.,
\alpha+\beta<0
. We have
\dfrac{-\beta}{\alpha}\in(0,1)
. Choose
x=\dfrac{-\beta}{\alpha}
and
y=1
. Then, on one hand it holds that

\langle F(x),y-x\rangle=(\alpha x+\beta)(y-x)=\left(\alpha\dfrac{-\beta}{\alpha}+\beta\right)\left(1-\dfrac{-\beta}{\alpha}\right)=0.

On the other hand, we have

\langle F(y),y-x\rangle=(\alpha y+\beta)(y-x)=(\alpha+\beta)\left(1-\dfrac{-\beta}{\alpha}\right)<0.

Therefore,
F
is not pseudomonotone on
C
.

Subcase 5b:

\alpha=-\beta
, i.e.,
\alpha+\beta=0
. Taking
x=1
and
y=-1
, we have

\langle F(x),y-x\rangle=(\alpha x+\beta)(y-x)=-2(\alpha+\beta)=0,

and

\langle F(y),y-x\rangle=(\alpha y+\beta)(y-x)=-2(-\alpha+\beta)=-2\beta<0.

This implies that
F
is not pseudomonotone on
C
.

Subcase 5c:

\alpha>-\beta
, i.e.,
\alpha+\beta>0
. Since
x\leq 1
for all
x\in C
, it follows that

-\alpha x\leq-\alpha<\beta,

which implies
\alpha x+\beta>0
for all
x\in C
. Let
x,y\in C
be such that

\langle F(x),y-x\rangle=(\alpha x+\beta)(y-x)\geq 0.

This condition is equivalent to
y-x\geq 0
. As
\alpha<0
and
x\leq 1
, we have

\alpha x+\beta\geq\alpha+\beta=\dfrac{\alpha+\beta}{2}-\dfrac{\alpha+\beta}{2}(-1).

Moreover, since
\alpha+\beta>0
,
y\leq 1
and
x\geq-1
, this yields

\alpha x+\beta\geq\dfrac{\alpha+\beta}{2}y-\dfrac{\alpha+\beta}{2}x.

Multiplying both sides of the last inequality by
(y-x)
, we obtain

\langle F(y),y-x\rangle=(\alpha y+\beta)(y-x)\geq\dfrac{\alpha+\beta}{2}(y-x)^{2}.

Thus,
F
is
\gamma
-strongly pseudomonotone on
C
with
\gamma=\dfrac{\alpha+\beta}{2}
.

Case 6:

\alpha<0
and
\beta<0
. In this case,
\dfrac{-\beta}{\alpha}<0
.

Subcase 6a:

\alpha\leq\beta
. Then,
\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\leq 1
or, equivalently,
-1\leq\dfrac{-\beta}{\alpha}
. Choose
x=\dfrac{-\beta}{\alpha}
,
y=1
, and observe that
x,y\in C
. Clearly,

\langle F(x),y-x\rangle=(\alpha x+\beta)(y-x)=0.

Since
\alpha+\beta<0
and
\dfrac{-\beta}{\alpha}<0
, we have

\langle F(y),y-x\rangle=(\alpha y+\beta)(y-x)=(\alpha+\beta)\left(1-\dfrac{-\beta}{\alpha}\right)<0.

Hence,
F
is not pseudomonotone on
C
.

Subcase 6b:

\alpha>\beta
. This implies that
\alpha-\beta>0
and
\dfrac{-\beta}{\alpha}<-1
. For any
x\in C
, one has
x\geq-1>\dfrac{-\beta}{\alpha}
. Thus,
\alpha x+\beta<0
for all
x\in C
. Let
x,y\in C
be such that

\langle F(x),y-x\rangle=(\alpha x+\beta)(y-x)\geq 0.

Since
\alpha x+\beta<0
for every
x\in C
, we have
y-x\leq 0
. In addition, as
y\geq-1
and
\alpha<0
, we have

\alpha y+\beta\leq-\alpha+\beta=-\dfrac{\alpha-\beta}{2}-\dfrac{\alpha-\beta}{2}=\dfrac{\alpha-\beta}{2}(-1)-\dfrac{\alpha-\beta}{2}.

Combining this with the fact that
x,y\in[-1,1]
gives

\alpha y+\beta\leq\dfrac{\alpha-\beta}{2}y-\dfrac{\alpha-\beta}{2}x.

Multiplying both sides of the last inequality by
(y-x)
yields

\langle F(y),y-x\rangle=(\alpha y+\beta)(y-x)\geq\dfrac{\alpha-\beta}{2}(y-x)^{2}.

Therefore,
F
is
\gamma
-strongly pseudomonotone on
C
with
\gamma=\dfrac{\alpha-\beta}{2}
.

In conclusion,
F
is strongly pseudomonotone on
C
if and only if
(\alpha,\beta)
is such that one of the Cases 2 and 3, or one of the Subcases 5c and 6b, occurs. Equivalently,
F
is strongly pseudomonotone on
C
if and only if
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{A}
.
\hfill\Box

Remark 4

The cone
\mathcal{A}
is nonconvex. Indeed, taking any
(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})
and
(\alpha_{2},\beta_{2})
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\alpha_{1}<0
,
\alpha_{2}<0
,
\beta_{1}>-\alpha_{1}
and
\beta_{2}<\alpha_{2}
, we see that the line segment joining
(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})
with
(\alpha_{2},\beta_{2})
does not lie entirely in
\mathcal{A}
. If one requires in addition that
\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}=0
, then the midpoint of that segment does not belong to
\mathcal{A}
. Since the latter is a cone,
(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})+(\alpha_{2},\beta_{2})\notin\mathcal{A}
. This means that the sum of two strongly pseudomonotone affine operators on a compact polyhedral convex set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
may not be strongly pseudomonotone on the set. To have another example of this type, take any
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{A}
with
\alpha<0
and note that
(-\alpha,-\beta)\in\mathcal{A}
, but the sum
(\alpha,\beta)+(-\alpha,-\beta)=(0,0)
does not belong to
\mathcal{A}
.

4
When the Trajectories of the Dynamical System Converge to the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Points?

As in Theorem
3.1
, let
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by
F(x)=\alpha x+\beta
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
with
\alpha,\beta
being real constants, and let
C=[-1,1]
. Consider the (possibly nonconvex) quadratic optimization problem

\min\left\{f(x):=\dfrac{1}{2}\alpha x^{2}+\beta x\mid x\in C\right\}.

(20)

According to the generalized Fermat’s rule (see, for example,
(
15
, p. 2)
and
(
16
, Theorem 3.1)
), if
\bar{x}\in C
is a local solution of (
20
), then

\nabla f(\bar{x})(y-\bar{x})\geq 0,\quad\mbox{\rm for all}\;\;y\in C,

or, equivalently,

(\alpha\bar{x}+\beta)(y-\bar{x})\geq 0,\quad\mbox{\rm for all}\;\;y\in C.

(21)

In agreement with the definition recalled in Section
1
,
\bar{x}\in C
is said to be a Karush-Kuhn-Tucker point (a KKT point) of (
20
) if the condition (
21
) is satisfied.

For some
x^{0}\in C
, let
x(\cdot)
be the solution of the following dynamical system

\begin{cases}\dot{x}(t)=\dfrac{1}{\eta}\left[P_{C}\left(x(t)-\dfrac{1}{\rho}\big(\alpha x(t)+\beta\big)\right)-x(t)\right],\quad\mbox{\rm for all}\;\;t>0,\\
x(0)=x^{0}.\end{cases}

(22)

where
\eta
and
\rho
are fixed positive constants.

Question 7:

Does
x(t)
converge to a KKT point of (
20
) as
t\to\infty
for any
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
and for any
x^{0}\in[-1,1]
, or not?

A partial answer to this question is given in the next proposition.

Proposition 1

For any
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\alpha<0
and for any
x^{0}\in[-1,1]
, the unique solution
x(t)
of (
22
) converges to a KKT point of (
20
) as
t\to\infty
.

Proof

Let
\alpha<0
and
x^{0}\in[-1,1]
be given arbitrarily. Let
x(t)
be the unique solution of the initial value problem (
22
). To study the behavior of
x(t)
, note that

P_{C}(u)=\begin{cases}-1\qquad&\mbox{\rm for}\;\;u<-1,\\
1&\mbox{\rm for}\;\;u>1,\\
u&\mbox{\rm for}\;\;-1\leq u\leq 1.\end{cases}

(23)

Furthermore, for our convenience, we put
\mu_{1}=\dfrac{\beta-\rho}{\rho-\alpha}
,
\mu_{2}=\dfrac{\beta+\rho}{\rho-\alpha}
, and observe from the inequalities
\rho-\alpha>0
and
\rho>0
that
\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}
. For every
x\in\mathbb{R}
, let
u(x)=x-\dfrac{1}{\rho}(\alpha x+\beta)
. Then, the following assertions hold:

(i)

u(x)<-1
if and only if
x<\mu_{1}
;

(ii)

u(x)>1
if and only if
x>\mu_{2}
;

(iii)

-1\leq u(x)\leq 1
if and only if
\mu_{1}\leq x\leq\mu_{2}
.

Therefore, the differential relation in (
22
) means that

\dot{x}(t)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{\eta}\big(-1-x(t)\big)\qquad&\mbox{\rm if}\;\;x(t)<\mu_{1},\\[8.61108pt]
\dfrac{1}{\eta}\big(1-x(t)\big)&\mbox{\rm if}\;\;x(t)>\mu_{2},\\[8.61108pt]
-\dfrac{1}{\eta\rho}\big(\alpha x(t)+\beta\big)&\mbox{\rm if}\;\;\mu_{1}\leq x(t)\leq\mu_{2}.\end{cases}

(24)

Based on the relationships between the initial point
x^{0}
and the segment
[\mu_{1},\mu_{2}]\subset\mathbb{R}
, we distinguish the next cases.

Case 1:

x^{0}<\mu_{1}
. In this case, we have
-1\leq x^{0}<\mu_{1}=\dfrac{\beta-\rho}{\rho-\alpha}
. It follows that
\alpha-\rho<\beta-\rho
; hence
\beta>\alpha
. So,
\bar{x}=-1
satisfies the condition (
21
), i.e.,
\bar{x}=-1
is a KKT point of (
20
). Since
x^{0}<\mu_{1}
, by the continuity of
x(\cdot)
we can find some
\tau>0
such that

x(t)<\mu_{1}\quad\mbox{for all}\;\;t\in[0,\tau].

Hence, using (
24
), we have the following initial value problem for a first-order linear ordinary differential equation with constant coefficients

\begin{cases}\dot{x}(t)=-\dfrac{1}{\eta}x(t)-\dfrac{1}{\eta},\quad\mbox{\rm for all}\;\;t\in[0,\tau],\\
x(0)=x^{0}.\end{cases}

So, from the result in
(
30
, Formula (3.43), p. 53)
it follows that

x(t)=\left(x^{0}+1\right)e^{-\frac{1}{\eta}t}-1

(25)

for all
t\in[0,\tau]
. Since
e^{-\frac{1}{\eta}t}>0
for all
t
and
x^{0}<\mu_{1}
, one has

x(t)=\left(x^{0}+1\right)e^{-\frac{1}{\eta}t}-1<\left(\mu_{1}+1\right)e^{-\frac{1}{\eta}t}-1,

for all
t\in[0,\tau]
.
Moreover, as
\mu_{1}>-1
, this implies that

x(t)<\left(\mu_{1}+1\right)-1=\mu_{1},\quad\mbox{\rm for all}\;\;t\in[0,\tau].

In particular,
x(\tau)<\mu_{1}
.

Claim 1:

The solution (
25
) can be extended to the whole half-line
[0,\infty)
.

Indeed, letting
\tau
play the role of the previous initial time
t=0
and
x(\tau)
play the role of the former
x^{0}
, we can deduce from the previous passage of proof that
x(t)
is of the form (
25
) for all
t\in[\tau,2\tau]
. Then, letting
2\tau
play the role of the initial time and
x(2\tau)
play the role of the initial value, we see that
x(t)
is of the form (
25
) for all
t\in[2\tau,3\tau]
. Therefore, by induction we can extend the formula (
25
) for
x(t)
to
[0,\infty)
.

According to Claim 1, we have
x(t)=\left(x^{0}+1\right)e^{-\frac{1}{\eta}t}-1
for all
t\geq 0
. It follows that
\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}x(t)=-1
. In other words, under the assumption made in this case,
x(t)
converges to the KKT point
\bar{x}=-1
of (
20
) as
t\to\infty
.

Case 2:

x^{0}=\mu_{1}
. Since
-1\leq x^{0}=\mu_{1}=\dfrac{\beta-\rho}{\rho-\alpha}
, one has
\alpha\leq\beta
. Therefore,

\mu_{1}+\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}=\dfrac{\beta-\rho}{\rho-\alpha}+\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}=\dfrac{\rho(\beta-\alpha)}{\alpha(\rho-\alpha)}\leq 0.

(26)

Setting
\bar{x}=-1
, we deduce from the relation
\beta-\alpha\geq 0
and (
21
) that
\bar{x}
is a KKT point of (
20
).

If
\alpha=\beta
, then we have
x^{0}=\dfrac{\beta-\rho}{\rho-\alpha}=-1
. As
x(t)\in C
for all
t\geq 0
, it holds that

x(t)\geq-1=\mu_{1}\quad\mbox{\rm for all}\;\;t\geq 0.

Since
\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}
, by the continuity of
x(\cdot)
we can find a number
\tau>0
such that
\mu_{1}\leq x(t)\leq\mu_{2}
for all
t\in[0,\tau]
. Combining this with (
24
) yields

\begin{cases}\dot{x}(t)=-\dfrac{\alpha}{\eta\rho}x(t)-\dfrac{\beta}{\eta\rho},\quad\mbox{\rm for all}\;\;t\in[0,\tau],\\
x(0)=x^{0}.\end{cases}

Hence, by
(
30
, Formula (3.43), p. 53)
we get

x(t)=\left(x^{0}+\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)e^{\frac{-\alpha}{\eta\rho}t}-\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha},

(27)

for all
t\in[0,\tau]
.
Recalling that
\alpha=\beta
and
x^{0}=-1
, from this we can infer that
x(t)=-1
for all
t\in[0,\tau]
. In particular,
x(\tau)=-1
. Arguing similarly as in the proof of Claim 1, we get
x(t)=-1
for every
t\in[0,\infty)
. So, the assertion of the proposition is valid.

Now, assume that
\alpha<\beta
. We have
x(0)=x^{0}=\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}
. If there exists
\bar{t}>0
such that
x(\bar{t})<\mu_{1}
then, arguing similarly as in Case 1, we can show that

x(t)=\left(x(\bar{t})+1\right)e^{-\frac{1}{\eta}(t-\bar{t})}-1

for every

. It follows that
\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}x(t)=-1
, i.e.,
x(t)
tends to the KKT point
\bar{x}=-1
of (
20
) as
t\to\infty
. If
x(t)\geq\mu_{1}
for all
t\geq 0
then, by the continuity of
x(\cdot)
, there exists
\tau>0
such that
\mu_{1}\leq x(t)\leq\mu_{2}
for all
t\in[0,\tau]
. So, applying (
24
) and
(
30
, Formula (3.43), p. 53)
, we can infer that
x(t)
is of the form (
27
) for all
t\in[0,\tau]
. Furthermore, since
\alpha<\beta
, the last inequality in (

) is strict; hence,
\mu_{1}+\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}<0
. Besides, as
\alpha<0
, we have
e^{\frac{-\alpha}{\eta\rho}t}>1
for all

. Therefore, for every

, it holds that

x(t)=\left(x^{0}+\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)e^{\frac{-\alpha}{\eta\rho}t}-\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}=\left(\mu_{1}+\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)e^{\frac{-\alpha}{\eta\rho}t}-\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}<\left(\mu_{1}+\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)-\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}=\mu_{1}.

We have arrived at a contradiction. Thus, the situation
x(t)\geq\mu_{1}
for all

Case 3:

Using

It follows that

.
For

, or
\alpha<-\beta

\mu_{1}<x^{0}<\mu_{2}

Another partial answer to Question 7 is provided by the next proposition, which deals with convex quadratic programs and the corresponding monotone affine variational inequalities.

Proposition 2

For any
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\alpha\geq 0
and for any
x^{0}\in[-1,1]
, the unique solution
x(t)
of (
22
) converges to a KKT point of (
20
) as
t\to\infty
, provided that
\rho-\alpha\geq 0
.

Proof

Let
x^{0}\in[-1,1]
be given arbitrarily and let
x(t)
be the unique solution of (
22
). According to Theorems
2.1
and
2.3
, the trajectory
x(t)
is defined on the whole real line and
x(t)\in C
for all
t\geq 0
.

By the assumptions of the proposition,
\alpha\geq 0
. Hence, either
\alpha=0
or
\alpha>0
. If
(\alpha,\beta)=(0,0)
, then one has
C_{*}=[-1,1]
, where
C_{*}
denotes the KKT point of (
20
). For every
t\in[0,\infty)
, since
x(t)-\dfrac{1}{\rho}\big(\alpha x(t)+\beta\big)=x(t)
and
x(t)\in C
, the differential equation in (
22
) becomes
\dot{x}(t)=0
for all
t>0
. Thus, by (
22
) we have
x(t)=x^{0}
for all
t\geq 0
. As
x^{0}\in C_{*}
, the assertion saying that
x(t)
converges to a KKT point of (
20
) as
t\to\infty
is valid. To prove this fact in the general case, we cannot apply many arguments of the above proof, because the number
(\rho-\alpha)
can be negative or null, as well. However, a modified version of that proof scheme can be used.

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}
, put
u(x)=x-\dfrac{1}{\rho}(\alpha x+\beta)
and observe that

(i)

u(x)<-1
if and only if
(\rho-\alpha)x<\beta-\rho
;

(ii)

u(x)>1
if and only if
(\rho-\alpha)x>\beta+\rho
;

(iii)

-1\leq u(x)\leq 1
if and only if
\beta-\rho\leq(\rho-\alpha)x\leq\beta+\rho
.

Therefore, thanks to (
23
) we can equivalently rewrite the differential relation in (
22
) as follows

\dot{x}(t)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{\eta}\big(-1-x(t)\big)\qquad&\mbox{\rm if}\;\;(\rho-\alpha)x(t)<\beta-\rho,\\[8.61108pt]
\dfrac{1}{\eta}\big(1-x(t)\big)&\mbox{\rm if}\;\;(\rho-\alpha)x(t)>\beta+\rho,\\[8.61108pt]
-\dfrac{1}{\eta\rho}\big(\alpha x(t)+\beta\big)&\mbox{\rm if}\;\;\beta-\rho\leq(\rho-\alpha)x(t)\leq\beta+\rho.\end{cases}

(30)

By our assumption,
\rho-\alpha\geq 0
. The assertion will be proved by analyzing the following 3 cases. Observe that, in some sense, the second case is symmetric to the first one. In addition, note that if none of the situations in the first two cases occurs, then the third case must happen.

Case 1:

There exists
\bar{t}\geq 0
such that

(\rho-\alpha)x(\bar{t})<\beta-\rho.

(31)

Claim 1:

The unique solution
x(t)
of (
22
) converges to
\bar{x}:=-1
.

Indeed, by (
31
) and the continuity of
x(\cdot)
we can find some
\tau>0
such that

(\rho-\alpha)x(t)<\beta-\rho\quad\mbox{for all}\;\;t\in[\bar{t},\bar{t}+\tau].

(32)

Hence, from (
30
) we get the following initial value problem for a first-order linear ordinary differential equation with constant coefficients

\begin{cases}\dot{x}(t)=-\dfrac{1}{\eta}x(t)-\dfrac{1}{\eta},\quad\mbox{\rm for all}\;\;t\in[\bar{t},\bar{t}+\tau],\\
x(\bar{t})=\theta\end{cases}

with
\theta:=x(\bar{t})
. So, from the result in
(
30
, Formula (3.43), p. 53)
it follows that

x(t)=\left(\theta+1\right)e^{-\frac{1}{\eta}(t-\bar{t})}-1

(33)

for all
t\in[\bar{t},\bar{t}+\tau]
. As
\theta+1\geq 0
, applied for all
t\in[\bar{t},\infty)
, the formula

y(t)=\left(\theta+1\right)e^{-\frac{1}{\eta}(t-\bar{t})}-1

(34)

defines a non-increasing function
y:[\bar{t},\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
. From (
34
) it follows that

\begin{cases}\dot{y}(t)=-\dfrac{1}{\eta}y(t)-\dfrac{1}{\eta},\quad\mbox{\rm for all}\;\;t\in[\bar{t},\infty),\\
y(\bar{t})=\theta.\end{cases}

(35)

Moreover,

(\rho-\alpha)y(t)\leq(\rho-\alpha)y(\bar{t})=(\rho-\alpha)x(\bar{t})<\beta-\rho\quad\mbox{for all}\;\;t\in[\bar{t},\infty).

Hence, for every
t\in[\bar{t},\infty)
, one has
u(y(t))<-1
. It follows that

\dfrac{1}{\eta}\left[P_{C}(u(y(t)))-y(t)\right]=-\dfrac{1}{\eta}y(t)-\dfrac{1}{\eta}.

Combining this with (
35
), we can assert that the condition

\dot{y}(t)=\dfrac{1}{\eta}\left[P_{C}\left(y(t)-\dfrac{1}{\rho}\big(\alpha y(t)+\beta\big)\right)-y(t)\right]

is satisfied for all
t\in[\bar{t},\infty)
. Therefore, recalling that
y(\bar{t})=\theta=x(\bar{t})
and the trajectory
x(t)
under our consideration is unique, we get
x(t)=y(t)
for all
t\in[\bar{t},\infty)
. Hence, from (
35
) we obtain
\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}x(t)=-1
.

Claim 2:

Condition (
31
) implies that
\bar{x}=-1
is a KKT point of (
20
).

Indeed, from (
31
) we can deduce that
(\rho-\alpha)(x(\bar{t})+1)<\beta-\alpha
. Since
\rho-\alpha\geq 0
and
x(\bar{t})+1\geq 0
, this yields
\beta-\alpha>0
. So,
\bar{x}=-1
satisfies the condition (
21
), i.e.,
\bar{x}
is a KKT point of (
20
).

From Claims 1 and 2 it follows that the unique solution
x(t)
of (
22
) converges to a KKT point of (
20
) as
t\to\infty
.

Case 2:

There exists
\bar{t}\geq 0
such that

(\rho-\alpha)x(\bar{t})>\beta+\rho.

(36)

Claim 3:

The unique solution
x(t)
of (
22
) converges to
\bar{x}:=1
.

Indeed, using (
36
) and the continuity of
x(\cdot)
we can find some
\tau>0
such that

(\rho-\alpha)x(t)>\beta-\rho\quad\mbox{for all}\;\;t\in[\bar{t},\bar{t}+\tau].

Hence, from (
30
) we get the following initial value problem for a differential equation

\begin{cases}\dot{x}(t)=-\dfrac{1}{\eta}x(t)+\dfrac{1}{\eta},\quad\mbox{\rm for all}\;\;t\in[\bar{t},\bar{t}+\tau],\\
x(\bar{t})=\theta\end{cases}

with
\theta:=x(\bar{t})
. Applying
(
30
, Formula (3.43), p. 53)
yields

x(t)=\left(\theta-1\right)e^{-\frac{1}{\eta}(t-\bar{t})}+1

(37)

for all
t\in[\bar{t},\bar{t}+\tau]
. Since

, the function
y:[\bar{t},\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
defined by the formula

y(t)=\left(\theta-1\right)e^{-\frac{1}{\eta}(t-\bar{t})}+1

(38)

for
t\in[\bar{t},\infty)
is non-decreasing . Clearly, (
38

\begin{cases}\dot{y}(t)=-\dfrac{1}{\eta}y(t)+\dfrac{1}{\eta},\quad\mbox{\rm for all}\;\;t\in[\bar{t},\infty),\\

(39)

Moreover, by (

) we have

(\rho-\alpha)y(t)\geq(\rho-\alpha)y(\bar{t})=(\rho-\alpha)x(\bar{t})>\beta-\rho\quad\mbox{for all}\;\;t\in[\bar{t},\infty).

Then, the inequality
u(y(t))>1
holds for every

. Therefore,

\dfrac{1}{\eta}\left[P_{C}(u(y(t)))-y(t)\right]=-\dfrac{1}{\eta}y(t)+\dfrac{1}{\eta}.

\dot{y}(t)=\dfrac{1}{\eta}\left[P_{C}\left(y(t)-\dfrac{1}{\rho}\big(\alpha y(t)+\beta\big)\right)-y(t)\right]

is fulfilled for all

. Hence, the uniqueness of the trajectory

imply that

for all

. Hence, from (
38

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}x(t)=\bar{x}
.

Claim 4:

Condition (
36
) implies that
\bar{x}:=1
is a KKT point of (
20
).

Indeed, from (
36
) we can deduce that
(\rho-\alpha)(x(\bar{t})-1)>\beta+\alpha
. Since

and

.

From Claims 3 and 4 we get the desired conclusion on the asymptotic behavior of the trajectory
x(t)
of (

).

Case 3:

For any
t\geq 0
, one has

\beta-\rho\leq(\rho-\alpha)x(t)\leq\beta+\rho.

(40)

Thanks to (
30
), we can use condition (
40
) to equivalently rewrite the system

\begin{cases}\dot{x}(t)=-\dfrac{1}{\eta\rho}\big(\alpha x(t)+\beta\big),\quad\mbox{\rm for all}\;\;t>0,\\

(41)

If

(42)

If

x(t)=\left(x^{0}+\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)e^{\frac{-\alpha}{\eta\rho}t}-\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha},\quad\mbox{\rm for all}\;\;t\geq 0.

(43)

Claim 5:

If

, then the point

Indeed, if

-1\leq-\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\leq 1
. Now, suppose that
\rho\neq\alpha
. Since
\rho-\alpha\geq 0
, we must have
\rho-\alpha>0
. Then, combining (
40
) with (
43
) gives

\dfrac{\beta-\rho}{\rho-\alpha}\leq\left(x^{0}+\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)e^{\frac{-\alpha}{\eta\rho}t}-\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\leq\dfrac{\beta+\rho}{\rho-\alpha}

(44)

for all
t\geq 0
. Passing the relations (
44
) to limits as
t\to\infty
, we get

\dfrac{\beta-\rho}{\rho-\alpha}\leq-\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\leq\dfrac{\beta+\rho}{\rho-\alpha},

which implies that
-1\leq-\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\leq 1
. Thus,
\bar{x}\in C
.

Since
f(x)=\dfrac{1}{2}\alpha x^{2}+\beta x
, the number
\bar{x}=-\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}
is a solution of the equation
\nabla f(x)=0
. As
\bar{x}\in C
, this implies that
\bar{x}
is a KKT point of (
20
). Finally, from (
43
) we can deduce that
\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}x(t)=\bar{x}
.

The proof is complete.
\hfill\Box

Remark 5

The condition
\rho-\alpha\geq 0
is not essential for the analysis of Case 3 in the above proof, hence it can be dropped in Case 3. Indeed, if
\rho-\alpha<0
, then from (
40
) and (
43
) we have

\dfrac{\beta-\rho}{\rho-\alpha}\geq\left(x^{0}+\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)e^{\frac{-\alpha}{\eta\rho}t}-\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\geq\dfrac{\beta+\rho}{\rho-\alpha}

(45)

for all
t\geq 0
. Letting
t\to\infty
, from (
45
) we obtain

\dfrac{\beta-\rho}{\rho-\alpha}\geq-\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\geq\dfrac{\beta+\rho}{\rho-\alpha},

which implies that
-1\leq-\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\leq 1
.

Remark 6

The condition

is essential for the validity of Claims 1–4 in the proof of Proposition (
