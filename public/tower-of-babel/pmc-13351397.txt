Seymour Fractures Revisited: Recognition and Management of an Open Physeal Distal Phalanx Injury
Authors: Shane J. Ross, Sarah Lu, Andrew H. Engel, Jane S. Doyle, Stuart Ferguson, Shevaun M. Doyle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Seymour fractures are pediatric distal phalanx physeal injuries of the fingers and toes associated with disruption of the nail bed. Despite increasing recognition, they are still frequently misdiagnosed, leading to delayed treatment and preventable morbidity. This review synthesizes contemporary literature on the definition, clinical and radiographic diagnosis, differential diagnoses, management, and outcomes of Seymour fractures. Diagnosis relies on maintaining a high index of suspicion in skeletally immature patients presenting with distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint flexion, bleeding at the nail fold, or proximal nail plate displacement superficial to the eponychial fold. Anteroposterior (AP) and lateral radiographs may demonstrate physeal separation; however, equivocal imaging findings should not override clinical evidence of nail-unit violation. In acute presentations, management should follow open fracture principles, including early antibiotic administration, irrigation and debridement, removal of interposed germinal matrix or nail bed tissue from the fracture site, anatomic reduction, and selective stabilization when reduction is unstable; however, management of delayed presentations without clinical infection may require a more individualized approach. Contemporary literature does not support routine pinning of every fracture, but delayed presentation, instability, and infection lower the threshold for operative management (eg, percutaneous pinning and/or irrigation and debridement). Available evidence is derived largely from retrospective series and case reports; therefore, recommendations regarding fixation strategy and antibiotic selection should be interpreted in this context. Across studies, early recognition and timely treatment are associated with favorable outcomes, whereas delayed or incomplete management increases the risk of infection, osteomyelitis, malunion, nail deformity, and physeal disturbance. Introduction

Phalangeal fractures are among the most common pediatric skeletal injuries, with an estimated incidence of 185 per 100,000 children aged 5-14 years [1]. Most distal phalanx fractures of the fingers and toes are treated nonoperatively, but Seymour fractures should be recognized as open distal phalanx physeal injuries that require urgent treatment according to open-fracture principles, with operative exploration and fixation used selectively based on instability, soft-tissue interposition, contamination, delayed presentation, or infection [2]. The Seymour fracture, first defined by Neville Seymour in 1966, is characterized by proximal displacement of the nail plate superficial to the eponychial fold in skeletally immature individuals [3]. Although the original description referenced the associated soft-tissue injury in the context of “more common open crushing injuries,” the clinical implications of nail bed disruption were not fully articulated [4]. More recent literature has clarified that Seymour fractures are, by definition, open physeal injuries of the distal phalanx. Seymour fractures most frequently correspond to Salter–Harris type I or II injuries of the distal phalanx [5]. The proximity between the distal phalanx physis and the germinal matrix predisposes these fractures to nail bed laceration, soft-tissue interposition, and bacterial contamination [4,[6], [7], [8], [9]].

Although uncommon, Seymour fractures can result in severe complications, including osteomyelitis, malunion, and paronychia, if not managed promptly [10]. Despite greater recognition, Seymour fractures are frequently misdiagnosed as benign fingertip trauma [11]. This can lead to delayed or inappropriate management, increasing the risk of infection, physeal arrest, and permanent deformity [1,4,12,13]. Given these diagnostic challenges and management implications, this review aims to clarify the conceptual understanding of Seymour fractures as open physeal injuries of the distal phalanx while synthesizing current evidence on their diagnosis, treatment, outcomes, and associated complications.

Diagnosis of Seymour fractures

History and physical examination

In skeletally immature patients, fingertip or toe trauma caused by a crush or direct axial load mechanism should raise concern for a Seymour fracture [13,14]. These injuries occur most frequently in the third digit of the hand in children and typically manifest with persistent pain and a resting flexion deformity at the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint [4,10,11,15,16]. An open injury, ecchymosis, and swelling are common findings on physical examination [1,4,10,16]. Findings such as bleeding at the nail fold, subungual hematoma, elongation of the lunula, proximal nail plate avulsion superficial to the eponychial fold, or a minor nail bed laceration may appear minor and can be easily overlooked (Fig. 1) [10,11]. Clinical examination should document nail plate position, integrity of the eponychial fold, degree of contamination, and any suggestive signs of infection [1,4,16]. Because these injuries are frequently mistaken for mallet finger or simple nail bed trauma, a mallet-type posture in a child with blood at the nail fold, subungual hematoma, or proximal nail plate displacement should be considered a Seymour fracture until proven otherwise [10,11,15,16].Figure 1Clinical features suspicious for Seymour fracture. Imaging

Further evaluation with radiographs is needed to confirm the diagnosis of a distal phalanx physeal fracture [7,11]. Anteroposterior (AP) and dedicated lateral radiographs centered on the involved DIP joint are obtained (Fig. 2). A dedicated lateral view of the involved digit is particularly important because it may demonstrate physeal separation, angular deformity, or displacement between the epiphysis and metaphysis [17,18]. Inadequate lateral imaging of the hand or foot may obscure these findings, particularly in toe injuries where overlap from adjacent digits can limit visualization [19,20]. These radiographic abnormalities may be subtle, especially when displacement is minimal or attention is directed toward the associated soft-tissue injury. Radiographic findings also help distinguish Seymour fractures from bony mallet injuries, in which the fracture is intraarticular rather than transphyseal [11,20,21]. Equivocal imaging should not exclude the diagnosis in the presence of clinical signs of nail-unit violation [10,11,16].Figure 2Radiographic features of Seymour fracture. In delayed presentations, radiographs should also be scrutinized for evidence of osteomyelitis, including periosteal reaction, sclerosis, and bony erosion [15]. Advanced imaging such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is generally reserved for cases of diagnostic uncertainty, worsening infection, suspected osteomyelitis, or suspected abscess formation [15]. When the clinical examination is strongly suggestive despite subtle or atypical radiographic findings, the diagnosis of Seymour fracture should still be strongly considered [22].

Differential diagnoses

Seymour fractures are frequently mistaken for more benign distal phalangeal injuries of the fingers and toes as they often present with DIP flexion and subtle soft-tissue findings (Fig. 3A) [10,21,23]. Failure to recognize the associated nail bed disruption and physeal injury may lead to inadequate nonoperative management [[24], [25], [26]]. Differential diagnoses include injuries resulting from crush mechanisms, such as isolated nail bed injuries and distal phalanx tuft fractures occurring in both fingers and toes, as well as injuries specific to the fingers, such as mallet finger. Although fingertip amputations may also present with exposed bone, nail disruption, and an open wound, they generally represent a clinically distinct injury pattern rather than a close diagnostic mimic of Seymour fracture because they are characterized by distal soft-tissue and osseous loss rather than a reducible transphyseal physeal injury with germinal matrix or nail bed interposition [1,2]. In skeletally immature patients, however, partial fingertip amputations occurring near the distal phalanx physis may still warrant careful radiographic assessment to exclude an associated physeal injury [16,27]. Management principles also differ substantially as treatment of fingertip amputations is guided by the level of tissue loss, vascular status, patient age, and soft-tissue availability, with options ranging from conservative wound care to revision amputation, flap reconstruction, or replantation rather than reduction and treatment according to open physeal fracture principles [[28], [29], [30]].Figure 3Schematic comparison of Seymour fracture and common differential diagnoses. Mallet finger

Mallet finger is the most relevant differential diagnosis for Seymour fracture. Both injuries present with flexion at the DIP [10,16,23]. Mallet finger results from either an avulsion of the terminal extensor tendon or a fracture at the dorsal base of the distal phalanx (Figs. 3B and 3C). In skeletally immature patients, mallet finger may also involve a Salter-Harris type III or IV injury of the distal phalanx [31]. Seymour fractures do not primarily represent tendon disruption; rather, a transphyseal fracture of the distal phalanx creates an imbalance between the flexor and extensor mechanisms, resulting in a mallet-like deformity. They are associated with nail bed or germinal matrix disruption and usually correspond to a Salter-Harris type I or II injury [16,31]. Unlike mallet injuries, Seymour fractures are extraarticular transphyseal injuries rather than intraarticular avulsion injuries [21,31]. Mallet finger is generally treated nonoperatively with splint immobilization, whereas Seymour fractures require treatment according to open-fracture principles [16]. Antibiotics are not routinely prescribed for a closed mallet finger but are recommended for Seymour fractures [15].

Isolated nail bed injury

Usually caused by crush mechanisms, isolated nail bed injuries of the fingers or toes represent the second-most relevant differential diagnosis for Seymour fractures [32]. The three main categories of nail bed injuries are subungual hematomas, nail bed laceration, or nail plate avulsion (Fig. 3D). All may present with nail plate disruption, subungual hematoma, or laceration of the sterile or germinal matrix on initial physical examination [12,33]. However, these injuries primarily involve soft-tissue disruption without an associated physeal fracture [3,4,32]. Misclassification may lead to inadequate treatment and an increased risk of infection, growth disturbance, or nail deformity [[1], [2], [3]].

Tuft fracture

Tuft fractures are distal phalanx injuries of the fingers and toes typically resulting from crush mechanisms and may be associated with soft-tissue and nail bed injury (Fig. 3E) [16]. Both tuft and Seymour fractures occur in children, with tuft fractures being the most common in toddlers and preschool-aged patients [27]. The key distinction is that Seymour fractures involve the distal phalanx physis and occur more proximally, whereas tuft fractures are confined to the distal tip; radiographs can help differentiate between these two entities.

Management

Seymour fractures are open fractures and should not be managed as simple fingertip injuries [1,2,10,11,34]. Current literature supports debridement within 48 h and antibiotics within 24 h because infection risk increases substantially thereafter [1,8,34]. In the acute setting, initial management should therefore include irrigation and debridement, antibiotic therapy, and tetanus prophylaxis as indicated by vaccination history and wound contamination [7,15]. A sterile field should be maintained in the emergency department and outpatient setting [6,35]. Subsequent management should be guided by time from injury, fracture stability, soft-tissue interposition, contamination, and the presence or absence of infection [10]. In general, early presentation without signs of infection warrants prompt nail plate removal or elevation, irrigation and debridement, removal of interposed tissue, fracture reduction, and selective fixation when instability persists [2]. Management of delayed presentation is more nuanced and is addressed separately in the following.

Acute presentation (<24 h)

In acute Seymour fractures presenting within 24 h, management should include early antibiotic therapy and prompt irrigation and debridement of the fracture site [9,36]. Removal or gentle elevation of the nail plate with a freer elevator and/or hemostat may be required to allow direct visualization of the germinal matrix and physis [2,4,9,10]. Interposed germinal matrix or nail bed tissue should be removed from the fracture site to permit anatomic reduction as soft-tissue interposition commonly blocks reduction [4,15,16].

Nail bed lacerations should be repaired when present as retained soft tissue may prevent reduction and contribute to infection, nail deformity, and growth disturbance [4,9,10]. This is commonly done with 6-0 or 7-0 absorbable sutures [2,37]. Longitudinal incisions in the eponychial fold may be required to adequately visualize and repair the proximal extent of the germinal matrix [2]. The native nail, when available, or a sterile nonadherent substitute may then be placed beneath the eponychial fold to maintain the nail fold space and prevent adherence of the eponychial fold to the underlying germinal matrix during healing and the wound dressed with nonadherent gauze [38]. Nail matrix transfer may be required if there is extensive damage to the germinal nail matrix [2,39]. These procedures are commonly performed with local anesthetic and a digital tourniquet, although the anesthetic setting may vary by patient age, cooperation, and treatment location [2].

If, after debridement and reduction, the fracture is stable and the soft-tissue injury is limited, definitive treatment in the emergency department with splint immobilization and close follow-up may be sufficient (Fig. 4) [9,11,36]. Operative treatment is favored when reduction is unsuccessful, unstable, or blocked by soft-tissue interposition [7]. Obvious proximal nail-fold violation, severe soft-tissue injury, or substantial contamination may also lower the threshold for operative exploration and repair [4,[9], [10], [11],36]. Retrograde percutaneous Kirschner-wire fixation across the distal phalanx and DIP joint remains the most reported method of stabilization [9,10,40]. Published antibiotic regimens vary but the literature consistently supports early prophylactic treatment [[9], [10], [11],34]. Commonly used antibiotics include oral cephalexin, intravenous cefazolin, or clindamycin if patients have a cephalosporin or penicillin allergy [15]. In contaminated wounds, broader oral coverage such as amoxicillin-clavulanate may be considered [[9], [10], [11],15]. What appears most important across studies is not a single preferred agent but timely administration of an antibiotic regimen as part of a standardized protocol that also includes debridement and reduction [8,10,11,34].

Delayed presentation (>24 h) without clinical deep infection

In delayed presentations without deep infection, management should be individualized rather than reflexively applying the acute open-fracture algorithm [9,15,34]. In the largest delayed-presentation series, delayed presentation was defined as more than 24 h after injury, the median time to presentation was 7 days, and deep infection at presentation—not delay alone—was associated with operative intervention [15]. Cha et al. also reported good outcomes after debridement, open reduction, nail bed repair, and antibiotics without routine K-wire fixation in children who presented 28 to 44 h after injury, suggesting that delayed presentation alone does not mandate fixation when a stable reduction can be achieved [41]. Accordingly, in the absence of clinical infection, persistent malalignment, soft-tissue interposition, or residual instability after reduction, the authors’ preference is not to treat a substantially delayed Seymour fracture as an acute open fracture requiring operative treatment solely on principle, even when presentation is beyond 72 h [41].

Antibiotic selection in delayed presentations should account for the greater possibility of occult bony contamination or early osteomyelitis [15]. In the same delayed-presentation series, antibiotic administration was associated with better fracture-healing outcomes, clindamycin had fewer treatment failures than cephalexin, and time to antibiotic initiation was not independently protective against delayed union or nonunion [15]. The benefit of starting prophylactic antibiotics several days after injury in an otherwise well-appearing patient without clinical evidence of infection therefore remains uncertain [9]. In that circumstance, the authors’ preference is close clinical follow-up rather than delayed prophylactic antibiotics given solely because the injury originally met open-fracture criteria.

Delayed presentation with suspected or established infection

Evidence of infection at presentation should heighten the concern for a more complicated course and lower the threshold for operative management [11,15]. Deep infection should be suspected in the setting of worsening pain, swelling, erythema, warmth, hyperemic granulation tissue, purulent drainage communicating with the fracture site, persistent mallet-type deformity after prior treatment, or radiographic evidence of osteomyelitis [11,15]. These findings should prompt operative irrigation and debridement. Blood and wound cultures should be collected [7,17]. In delayed Seymour fractures, deep infection at presentation is a strong predictor of the need for operative intervention [15]. Management typically includes nail plate removal or elevation, extraction of interposed or devitalized tissue, copious irrigation, open reduction, and nail bed repair, with structural stabilization added if instability persists after debridement [2,13,42]. Suture-based stabilization has also been described in select infected or subacute cases when prolonged transphyseal hardware is undesirable in an infected field [13,42].

When available, culture and sensitivity data should guide antibiotic therapy, which is often prolonged in this setting [43]. Seymour-specific reports describe treatment courses of approximately 4 weeks for delayed infections and up to 6 weeks when osteomyelitis is established [15,42]. MRI is not part of routine initial management but may be useful when the diagnosis remains uncertain, infection progresses despite treatment, or abscess formation is suspected [15].

Posttreatment care

Posttreatment management after Seymour fracture remains incompletely standardized. Seymour fracture-specific evidence supports prompt antibiotics and irrigation/debridement but does not define an optimal postoperative antibiotic duration. A systematic review found that debridement within 48 h and antibiotics within 24 h significantly reduced infection risk, and Rask et al. reported infection in 6.9% of patients treated with antibiotics within 24 h versus 76.5% when antibiotics were delayed [34]. Reported regimens vary, including perioperative IV antibiotics followed by 4 days of oral antibiotics, 3 days of IV cephalosporins, 4-10 days of oral antibiotics, or longer/culture-directed therapy for delayed or infected presentations [15]. Immobilization duration is also based on retrospective protocols rather than comparative trials; reported Seymour-specific protocols commonly use protective splinting or pin retention for approximately 2-4 weeks depending on age, stability, and fixation [4].

Complications and outcomes

When Seymour fractures are recognized early and treated as open physeal injuries, outcomes are generally favorable. However, available outcomes data are derived predominantly from retrospective series and case reports, so reported complication rates and comparisons between fixation strategies should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Most series report high patient satisfaction and near-full motion at follow-up [4,[9], [10], [11]]. Complications occur disproportionately in delayed, partially treated, or initially missed injuries [1,22,34].

Infection and osteomyelitis are the most consequential preventable complications [1,8,34]. Infection risk increases markedly with treatment delay. Reyes and Ho reported infection rates reaching 45% in delayed presentations and as high as 76.5% when antibiotics were initiated beyond 24 h, compared with 6.9% when antibiotics were administered early [1,8]. Rask et al. found that antibiotics administered more than 24 h after injury were associated with a markedly higher infection rate compared with earlier treatment [8]. Kiely et al. demonstrated that prophylactic antibiotics administered within 24 h significantly reduced infection risk, while debridement within 48 h reduced both infection and malunion [34].

Nail dystrophy and physeal disturbance may occur despite otherwise successful treatment, particularly when diagnosis is delayed, the germinal matrix is not adequately reduced, or infection develops [2,10,11]. In the long-term series by Krusche-Mandl et al., 21% of patients had minor growth disturbance of the distal phalanx and nail, and 25% of patients had solitary nail dystrophy [4]. However, most of these irregularities were not cosmetically important to patients [4]. Osteomyelitis-related physeal arrest can also produce progressive deformity over time [11,15]. Malunion, redisplacement, flexion deformity, and delayed union or nonunion are additional concerns when reduction is incomplete or unstable [2,15,40]. Available series suggest that K-wire fixation may reduce redisplacement and recurrent flexion deformity in select unstable injuries, although any apparent benefit must be weighed against the risk of physeal disturbance and interpreted in light of the retrospective nature of the evidence [40].

Summary

Seymour fractures are distinct pediatric distal phalangeal injuries of the fingers and toes in which physeal disruption and nail bed violation render the injury an open fracture rather than a benign fingertip injury. The available literature, composed largely of retrospective series and case reports, suggests that outcomes depend less on routine fixation and more on early recognition and timely application of open-fracture principles, including antibiotic administration, irrigation and debridement, removal of interposed tissue, anatomic reduction, and maintenance of reduction. Delayed or incomplete treatment is associated with higher rates of infection, osteomyelitis, growth disturbance, nail deformity, and malunion, whereas prompt treatment substantially reduces preventable morbidity. In children, a mallet-type fingertip deformity with nail-unit violation should prompt a presumptive diagnosis of Seymour fracture until proven otherwise.
