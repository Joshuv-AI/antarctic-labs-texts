How Global Calibration Strengthens Multiaccuracy
Sílvia Casacuberta, Parikshit Gopalan, Varun Kanade, Omer Reingold

Abstract
Multiaccuracy and multicalibration are multigroup fairness notions for prediction that have found numerous applications in learning and computational complexity [ 24 ] . They can be achieved from a single learning primitive: weak agnostic learning. A line of work starting from [ 17 ] has shown that multicalibration implies a very strong form of learning. Here we investigate the power of multiaccuracy as a learning primitive, both with and without the additional assumption of calibration. We find that multiaccuracy in itself is rather weak, but that the addition of global calibration (this notion is called calibrated multiaccuracy ) boosts its power substantially, enough to recover implications that were previously known only assuming the stronger notion of multicalibration. We give evidence that multiaccuracy might not be as powerful as standard weak agnostic learning, by showing that there is no way to post-process a multiaccurate predictor to get a weak learner, even assuming the best hypothesis has correlation 1 / 2 1/2 . Rather, we show that it yields a restricted form of weak agnostic learning, which requires some concept in the class to have correlation greater than 1 / 2 1/2 with the labels. However, by also requiring the predictor to be calibrated, we recover not just weak, but strong agnostic learning. A similar picture emerges when we consider the derivation of hardcore measures from predictors satisfying multigroup fairness notions [ 46 , 8 ] . On the one hand, while multiaccuracy only yields hardcore measures of density half the optimal, we show that (a weighted version of) calibrated multiaccuracy achieves optimal density. Our results yield new insights into the complementary roles played by multiaccuracy and calibration in each setting. They shed light on why multiaccuracy and global calibration, although not particularly powerful by themselves, together yield considerably stronger notions.

Introduction

A major line of research within the algorithmic fairness community focuses on the notion of
multicalibration,
first introduced by Hébert-Johnson, Kim, Reingold, and Rothblum
[
24
]
. This concept centers on risk predictors that estimate individual probabilities for outcomes such as: what is the likelihood that an individual will repay a loan, successfully complete their studies, or be hired after an interview? These predictive tasks often inform high-stakes decisions—granting loans, admitting applicants to universities, or extending job interview invitations—and thus raise critical fairness concerns.
Multicalibration requires that predictions be
calibrated
across a rich and potentially intersecting collection of subpopulations. A predictor is said to be calibrated on a subpopulation if, for individuals to whom it assigns a probability
v
of a given outcome, approximately a
v
fraction of them actually experience that outcome. In other words, the predictions should “mean what they say” not just globally, but also within each relevant subpopulation.

While multicalibration gained widespread acceptance as an appealing fairness criterion, this paper focuses on its interaction with concepts from other domains, specifically complexity theory and computational learning theory. Multicalibration draws from both fields. It is naturally viewed as a complexity-theoretic perspective on fairness, as it asks for predictors to be able to pass a rich class of computationally-defined tests. This connection has been sharpened through the lens of outcome indistinguishability
[
10
]
. From the standpoint of learning theory, multicalibration is only meaningful as a fairness notion if it is computationally attainable. The algorithms used to learn multicalibrated predictors rely on foundational concepts from learning theory, such as weak agnostic learning and agnostic boosting
[
7
,
38
,
34
,
30
,
13
]
.

A large body of work has shown deep connections in the other direction; namely, exploring the impact of multicalibration and its relaxations to complexity theory and learning. Most relevant to this paper, multicalibration implies a strong form of loss minimization called
omniprediction

[
17
,
16
,
18
,
41
]
and constructions of hardcore distributions in computational complexity
[
8
]
. Some of the other implications are the notion of domain adaptation
[
31
]
, and additional complexity-theoretic applications
[
11
,
25
,
8
,
39
]
.
In the application of multicalibration to computational complexity and learning theory, it is natural to ask whether multicalibration is truly necessary and what the minimal underlying assumptions are. Beyond potential efficiency improvements, our goal is to offer new insights into the fundamental concepts of agnostic learning and hardcore distributions.

Calibrated Multiaccuracy.

Multiaccuracy
[
46
,
24
,
29
]
is a weaker notion of multigroup fairness. Like multicalibration, it is parameterized by a family of subpopulations
\mathcal{C}
. For the purposes of this discussion, we can assume that each
c\in\mathcal{C}
is a Boolean function, that is, the characteristic function of a set.
1
1

1

The formal definition allows for real-valued functions which can be interpreted as encoding fuzzy set membership.
In multiaccuracy, we require that for each
c\in\mathcal{C}
, the expected prediction over individuals in the set defined by
c
is approximately equal to the fraction of those individuals who experience a positive outcome. Hence, such predictors are accurate on average for each such
c
.
In this work, we explore the power of multiaccuracy as a primitive in computational learning and in the context of hardcore distributions. In both settings, a common theme arises: multiaccuracy alone is insufficient to achieve our desired guarantees.

However, when we supplement multiaccuracy (or a weighted variant thereof) with
global
calibration—that is, calibration over the entire population rather than all subgroups in
\mathcal{C}
—we recover many of the appealing consequences of multicalibration.
This strengthened notion, referred to as
calibrated multiaccuracy
, has already demonstrated its utility in prior work on omnipredictors via outcome indistinguishability
[
16
]
. In
[
16
]
, it is also argued that calibrated multiaccuracy can be more efficient to achieve than full multicalibration, and costs about the same as multiaccuracy. For this reason, our results give efficiency improvements compared with basing the applications we consider on multicalibration instead. Furthermore, by clarifying the distinct but complementary contributions of multiaccuracy and global calibration in each application, this work offers new insights on these fundamental notions.

Computational learning.

Multicalibrated predictors with respect to a family
\mathcal{C}
can be learned from a sample of individuals and outcome pairs via a learning primitive known as
weak agnostic learning
for
\mathcal{C}

[
24
]
. Weak agnostic learning allows learning a hypothesis that is somewhat correlated with the labels as long as some hypothesis in
\mathcal{C}
is sufficiently correlated
[
7
,
34
,
30
,
13
]
. Quantitatively, assuming the existence of a hypothesis in
\mathcal{C}
that achieves correlation
\alpha>0
, an
(\alpha,\beta)
weak learner returns a hypothesis with correlation at least
\beta
. Typically, one hopes to get
\beta=\poly(\alpha)
even when
\alpha
approaches
0
. This should be contrasted with (strong) agnostic learning, where a hypothesis can be found that is almost as correlated with the data as the best hypothesis in the class
\mathcal{C}
is; in other words
\beta\geq\alpha-\varepsilon
.

A line of work
[
17
,
16
,
18
,
41
]
shows that multicalibration implies a strong form of learning called
omniprediction
that allows for the simultaneous minimization of all convex, Lipschitz loss functions. All one needs to do is to post-process the predictions with a best-response function. This a very broad guarantee. Specializing to the case of correlation loss tells us that multicalibration implies a strong agnostic learning algorithm, where the post-processing function here corresponds to
\mathrm{sign}(p-1/2)
. Thus, agnostic learning is both necessary and sufficient for multicalibration.

Can the same be said about multiaccuracy? Obviously, weak agnostic learning is sufficient for multiaccuracy (which is a weaker notion than multicalibration), and the original multiaccuracy algorithm from
[
24
]
builds a multiaccurate predictor by repeatedly calling a weak agnostic learner. But, is it necessary?
While it seems intuitive that the answer should be yes, this paper paints a much more subtle picture:

•

Perhaps surprisingly, we give evidence that multiaccuracy in itself need not imply weak agnostic learning in the standard sense. We prove the existence of a class
\mathcal{C}
, a distribution on labels, and a
\mathcal{C}
-multiaccurate predictor
p
such that even though there is hypothesis in
\mathcal{C}
that gives correlation
1/2
, no post-processing of the predictor has any non-zero correlation with the labels.

•

We complement this lower bound by showing that multiaccuracy does imply weak learning once the correlation
\alpha
exceeds
1/2
: If there is a hypothesis in
\mathcal{C}
with correlation
\alpha
, then
\mathrm{sign}(p-1/2)
where
p
is
(\mathcal{C},0)
-multiaccurate achieves correlation
\beta=2\alpha-1
, a guarantee that is only non-trivial when
\alpha>1/2
. We call this
restricted weak agnostic learning
, to contrast it with the standard notion of weak agnostic learning where
\alpha
approaches
0
.

•

In contrast, we show that if the predictor
p
is both
\mathcal{C}
-multiaccurate and calibrated, then
\mathrm{sign}(p-1/2)
is a strong agnostic learner for
\mathcal{C}
, whose correlation is at least that of the best hypothesis in
\mathcal{C}
. Thus, the addition of calibration considerably increases the power of the predictor.

•

Given the agnostic boosting results of
[
30
,
13
]
that show that
(\alpha,\beta)
-weak agnostic learning can be boosted to
(\gamma,\gamma-\alpha)
-weak learning for any
\gamma>\alpha
, one might wonder, if boosting in the opposite direction is possible, i.e., given an
(\alpha,\beta)
-weak learner, can we design an
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
-weak learner for
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
? We answer this question in the negative under standard cryptographic assumptions.

•

Lastly, the result of
[
24
]
shows that
(\alpha,\beta)
-weak agnostic learning achieves a multiaccuracy parameter of
\alpha
; we show that in general, given an such a weak learner, it is not possible to achieve multiaccuracy parameter better than
\alpha/2
.

Hardcore set constructions.

Impagliazzo’s hardcore lemma is a fundamental result in computational complexity that relates a function
g
being (mildly)
\delta
-hard to predict under a distribution
\mathcal{D}
for distinguishers in a class
\mathcal{C}_{q}

to the existence of a hardcore measure
\mu
of density
\Omega(\delta)
in
\mathcal{D}
, such that
g
is
1/2-\varepsilon
-hard to predict
(i.e., cannot improve over random guessing) when sampling from the measure to distinguishers from a family
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{C}_{q}
. The parameter
q
measures the number of
\mathcal{C}
-gates required for functions in
\mathcal{C}_{q}
. The larger
q
is, the stronger the hardness assumption we make to show the existence of a hardcore distribution for
\mathcal{C}
.

The work of Trevisan, Tulsiani, and Vadhan
[
46
]
, which preceded the literature on multigroup fairness, gave an elegant connection to multiaccuracy, showing how to get a simple and explicit hardcore measure from a
\mathcal{C}
-multiaccurate predictor.
2
2

2

As pointed out in
[
11
,
8
]
, the notion of
(\mathcal{C},\epsilon)
-indistinguishability in complexity theory, as used in
[
46
]
, is exactly equivalent to the notion of
(\mathcal{C},\epsilon)
-multiaccuracy as defined in the multigroup fairness literature.
Their construction, however, achieves a hardcore measure of density
\delta
, while the optimal density is
2\delta

[
26
]
.
Recently, Casacuberta, Dwork, and Vadhan
[
8
]
showed that multicalibration implies a stronger form of the hardcore lemma, which they call ICHL
++
. From this they derive the original hardcore lemma by Impagliazzo, this time with a hardcore measure of optimal density
2\delta
. However, while
[
46
]
achieve
q=O(1/\varepsilon^{2}\delta^{2})
,
[
8
]
get
q=O(1/(\varepsilon^{12}\delta^{6}))
.
3
3

3

The stronger bound of
O(1/(\varepsilon^{2}\delta^{2}))
stated in
[
8
]
appears not to be correct. We refer the reader to a detailed discussion at the end of Section
5.3
.

This increased complexity comes in part because achieving multicalibration requires more calls to a (strong) agnostic learner for
\mathcal{C}
, in contrast to multiaccuracy.

We show how to construct hardcore distributions with optimal density
2\delta
as in
[
8
]
but with the lower complexity bound of
q=O(1/(\varepsilon^{2}\delta^{2}))
for
\mathcal{C}_{q}
as in
[
46
]
. Indeed, we give a simple and explicit form for such a measure, starting from a predictor satisfying calibration and a weighted variant of multiaccuracy that we call
weighted multiaccuracy
. Recall that in multiaccuracy we ask that the expected prediction on every set is accurate. In weighted multiaccuracy we instead consider a weighted average (modified by a fixed function of the predictions where we weigh individuals with prediction value closer to half more). Both calibration and weighted multiaccuracy are implied by multicalibration, so this gives an alternate proof of the optimal density result of
[
8
]
. But crucially, the cost of obtaining both these conditions together (by many measures, including oracle calls to the weak learner) is not much more than that of multiaccuracy, which yields a considerably better bound on the parameter
q
.

The three tiers of multigroup fairness.

As shown in Figure
1
, our work together with the results of
[
46
,
8

18
]
paints a clear picture of the relative power of various multigroup fairness notions in the context of learning and hardcore measure constructions. Arrows represent implications that either follow by definition or through post-processing the underlying object. When more powerful oracle reductions are allowed, all these notions are achievable using (weak) agnostic learning.

Figure 1:
Three tiers of multigroup fairness notions.

The tiers are anchored by multiaccuracy, calibrated multiaccuracy and multicalibration respectively. For simplicity, we ignore the differences between swap multicalibration and standard multicalibration, and weighted multiaccuracy and standard multiaccuracy, since the computational costs of these notions are roughly the same.

•

Multicalibration: the top tier.
Multicalibration implies the strongest known form of the Impagliazzo hardcore lemma, namely the IHCL
++

[
8
]
. On the learning front, it implies several strong forms of learning: swap agnostic learning and swap omniprediction (we refer the readers to
[
17
,
18
]
for the definitions of these notions). It however comes at the cost of higher complexity of learning, more complex predictors (in terms of circuit size) and higher sample complexity.

•

Calibrated Multiaccuracy: the Goldilocks zone.
On the learning front, calibrated multiaccuracy implies strong agnostic learning and loss OI for generalized linear models (see
[
16
]
for definitions). On the hardcore lemma front, it gives hardcore sets of optimal density
2\delta
, but with relatively low blowup in
q
. The computational, and representational, costs are much lower than multicalibration, and comparable to multiaccuracy. But it is not as powerful as full-blown multicalibration, and does not imply IHCL
++
or swap agnostic learning (see
[
18
]
for the latter separation).

•

Multiaccuracy: the lowest rung.
Multiaccuracy implies hardcore sets of density
\delta
by
[
46
]
. On the learning front, it does not give weak agnostic learning for
\alpha\leq 1/2
, using post-processing. It does give restricted weak agnostic learning for
\alpha>1/2
. The key argument in its favor is that it is cheap and easy to achieve, both in theory and practice: minimizing squared loss or running a GLM over a base class
\mathcal{C}
is guaranteed to yield multiaccurate predictors
[
16
]
.

Independent of our work, Pranay Tankala has observed that getting optimal density
2\delta
in the IHCL was possible under weaker multigroup fairness conditions than multicalibration
[
44
]
.

The case for calibrated multiaccuracy.

The power of multicalibration
[
24
,
10
,
17
]
illustrates the potency of combining calibration and multiaccuracy. But this great power comes at a high computational price. Our results show that in two important settings, agnostic learning and hardcore set constructions, global calibration together with multiaccuracy is a cheaper alternative to multicalibration, adding weight to a thesis that was first put forth by
[
16
]
. Our proofs highlight the complementary roles that multiaccuracy and calibration play, and give clear intuition for why their combination succeeds even when they each fail in isolation. A deep insight in theoretical computer science has been that hardness and learning are two sides of the same coin
[
27
,
35
,
46
,
13
]
; our work provides further evidence of this phenomenon.

We believe that these theoretical results also offer some insights to practice. As pointed out in the work of
[
16
]
, some form of multiaccuracy is achieved by commonly used machine learning models that perform empirical risk minimization with a proper loss. Such a model will not likely be calibrated as is; see the discussion in
[
4
]
. But calibrated multiaccuracy is not much harder to achieve, and it can be easily implemented using standard libraries for calibration and regression, in contrast to full-blown multicalibration which is not easily implemented via standard libraries.
4
4

4

While implementations do exist
[
42
,
15
,
23
]
, experiments have found that its sample complexity is fairly large in contrast with multiaccuracy
[
21
]
.
The argument for adding calibration is typically that calibration is a nice property to have, and it does not harm the loss (as long as it is proper). Our work gives a more compelling argument; namely, that global calibration significantly bolsters the power of a predictive model beyond multiaccuracy, without incurring much additional cost.

Overview of our Results

In this section, we describe our main results in more detail and explain the key ideas behind them. For this, we quickly recap some notation and definitions, leaving a more formal treatment to Section
3
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
denote a distribution over pairs
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
where
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
denotes a point and
\mathbf{y}\in\{0,1\}
its label. All expectations unless mentioned otherwise refer to this distribution. A predictor is a function
p:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
while a hypothesis class
\mathcal{C}=\{c:\mathcal{X}\to[-1,1]\}
is a collection of bounded functions, which we assume is closed under negation and contains the constant
\mathbf{1}
function. The Bayes optimal predictor is
p^{*}(\mathbf{x})=\E[\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}]
. We will think of functions from
\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as vectors with the inner product
\langle p,q\rangle=\E[p(\mathbf{x})q(\mathbf{x})]
.

Multigroup fairness notions.

The notion of
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multiaccuracy as defined in
[
24
]
requires that

\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\Bigl\lvert\E_{\mathcal{D}}[c(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{y}-p(\mathbf{x}))]\Bigr\rvert\leq\tau.

For intuition, let
\tau=0
. In this case multiaccuracy requires that for every
c\in\mathcal{C}
, we have

\E[c(\mathbf{x})(p^{*}(\mathbf{x})-p(\mathbf{x}))]=0,

or equivalently that every
c\in\mathcal{C}
is orthogonal to
p-p^{*}
. Hence
p
and
p^{*}
both have the same projection onto the subspace spanned by
\mathcal{C}
.

Calibration is a classic notion from the forecasting literature
[
9
]
. Perfect calibration asks that
\E[\mathbf{y}|p(\mathbf{x})]=p(\mathbf{x})
, in other words, conditioned on any prediction value
v
, the expectation of the label is exactly
v
. We say that the predictor is
\tau
-calibrated if

\E[|\E[\mathbf{y}|p(\mathbf{x})]-p(\mathbf{x}))|]\leq\tau.

Calibrated multiaccuracy introduced in
[
16
]
, asks for predictors that are both multiaccurate and calibrated, as the name suggests. It is a stronger requirement than just multiaccuracy, although of comparable sample and computational complexity
[
16
, Section 7]
.

Multicalibration
[
24
]
is a further strengthening of calibrated multiaccuracy. We say that
p
is
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-
multicalibrated
if

\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\E[|c(\mathbf{x})(\E[\mathbf{y}|p(\mathbf{x})]-p(\mathbf{x}))|]\leq\tau.

If we take
c=\mathrm{1}
, we recover calibration, and if we ignore the absolute values in the outer expectation, we recover multiaccuracy.

Agnostic learning.

We define weak and strong agnostic learning following
[
7
,
36
,
34
,
30
,
13
]
. The correlation between a function
c:\mathcal{X}\to[-1,1]
and the labels is defined as
\mathrm{cor}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{y},c)=\E[c(\mathbf{x})(2\mathbf{y}-1)]\in[-1,1]
, where the map
\mathbf{y}\mapsto 2\mathbf{y}-1
maps labels in
\{0,1\}
to
\{\pm 1\}
. A
\beta
-weak learner is a function
h:\mathcal{X}\to[-1,1]
such that
\mathrm{cor}(\mathbf{y},h)\geq\beta
. Taking
h=0
which gives
0
correlation as our baseline, we would like
\beta\in(0,1]
to be as large as possible. For a predictor
p:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
, we will often use the transformation
p\mapsto 2p-1
. This maps the
p=1/2
predictor to
h=0
, corresponding to the fact that guessing randomly at every point gives
0
correlation.

For
\alpha\geq\beta\in[0,1]
, an
(\alpha,\beta)
weak agnostic learner for
\mathcal{C}
is an algorithm that has access to a distribution
\mathcal{D}
(with a known marginal
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
over
\mathcal{X}
). If
\mathcal{D}
is such that if there exists a hypothesis
c
such that
\mathrm{cor}(\mathbf{y},c)\geq\alpha
, the algorithm is guaranteed to return a
\beta
-weak learner. It helps to think of
\beta
as smaller than but polynomially related to
\alpha
. In contrast, a strong agnostic learner must return a hypothesis that achieves correlation
\mathrm{Opt}(\mathcal{C})-\varepsilon
where
\mathrm{Opt}(\mathcal{C})=\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{cor}(\mathbf{y},c)
for every
\varepsilon>0
. The agnostic boosting results of
[
30
,
13
]
imply that an
(\alpha,\beta)
weak learner for
\mathcal{C}
can be boosted to a hypothesis that gives correlation at least
\mathrm{Opt}(\mathcal{C})-\alpha-\varepsilon
. Thus, given a weak learner that can handle arbitrarily small correlation
\alpha
, we can achieve strong agnostic learning.
5
5

5

The parameter
\beta
only affects the number of iterations of boosting, hence it is less important than
\alpha
.

Multigroup fairness and agnostic learning.

Results of
[
24
,
16
]
show that all of multiaccuracy, calibrated multiaccuracy, and multicalibration with error
\tau
can be achieved using an
(\tau,\beta)
weak agnostic learner as an oracle. The algorithms will involve multiple calls to the weak agnostic learner. The number of calls grows as
O(1/\beta^{2})
for multiaccuracy and calibrated multiaccuracy, and
O(1/\tau^{2}\beta^{4})
for multicalibration. The added complexity in multicalibration comes from having to ensure multiaccuracy holds for prediction values that have fairly small probability, see the detailed discussion in
[
16
, Section 7.3]
.

Our goal is to understand the relative strengths of the various multigroup fairness notions for agnostic learning. More precisely, given a
\mathcal{C}
-multiaccurate predictor
p
, when can we post-process its predictions (by some function
k:[0,1]\to[-1,1]
) to get a weak agnostic learner? Here is what was known before:

•

When
p
is
multicalibrated
, then
\mathrm{sign}(p-1/2)
is a strong agnostic learner. This is an implication of a general result proved in
[
17
]
, that a
\mathcal{C}
-multicalibrated
p
is an omnipredictor for all convex Lipschitz loss functions. This means that for every such loss
\ell
, there is a post-processing function
k_{\ell}
so that
k_{\ell}(p)
suffers a loss comparable to the best hypothesis in
\mathcal{C}
. The implication for agnostic learning comes from considering the (negation of) correlation as a loss.

•

When
\mathcal{C}=\{c:\mathcal{X}\to\{\pm 1\}\}
is a family of Boolean rather than bounded functions, then correlation coincides with the
0
-
1
loss. In this setting, the work of
[
16
]
shows that if
p
satisfies calibrated multiaccuracy, then it is an omnipredictor, using the technique of loss outcome indistinguishability. This implies that the hypothesis
\mathrm{sign}(p-1/2)
is a strong agnostic learner.

We are unaware of work that considered multiaccuracy as a learning primitive. In light of the above results, it would be natural to guess that multiaccuracy ought to imply (at least) weak agnostic learning. This would tightly characterize weak agnostic learning as being necessary and sufficient for all the multigroup fairness notion above. Perhaps surprisingly, the answer is more nuanced.

2.1
Agnostic Learning and Multiaccuracy: With and Without Calibration

We start with the observation that weak learning is essentially equivalent to non-trivial square loss minimization, that beats random guessing. This observation is not entirely new, but will help elucidate several of our results.

Assume that
h
is a
\beta
-weak learner. Then, as observed by
[
24
]
, taking a standard gradient step, the predictor
p(\mathbf{x})=(1+\beta h(\mathbf{x}))/2
satisfies

\displaystyle\E[(\mathbf{y}-p(\mathbf{x}))^{2}]\leq\E\left[\left(\mathbf{y}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\right]-\frac{3\beta^{2}}{4}.

In other words, weak agnostic learning enables non-trivial squared loss minimization, when compared to random guessing.

In the other direction, we show that any function
h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
which beats random guessing in squared error gives a weak agnostic learner. Theorem
3.5
shows that if
h:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
satisfies

\displaystyle\E[(\mathbf{y}-h(\mathbf{x}))^{2}]\leq\E\left[\left(\mathbf{y}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\right]-\gamma.

then
2h(x)-1
is a
2\gamma
weak learner. A similar result is proved in the work of
[
2
]
.
With this characterization in hand, we can rephrase our question to ask when a multiaccurate predictor improves upon random guessing in terms of squared loss.
More generally, for what values of
\alpha,\beta
can one get an
(\alpha,\beta)
-weak learner for
\mathcal{C}
by post-processing a
\mathcal{C}
-multiaccurate predictor?

When multiaccuracy does not imply agnostic learning.

Motivated by Theorem
3.5
, let us first focus on the hypothesis
q(x)=2p(x)-1
and understand when this fails as a weak learner. To frame this as a geometric question, let
q^{*}(x)=2p^{*}(x)-1
. Assume there exists
c\in\mathcal{C}
so that
\langle q^{*},c\rangle=\mathrm{cor}(\mathbf{y},c)\geq\alpha
. By multiaccuracy, this also means that
\langle q,c\rangle\geq\alpha
, since multiaccuracy preserves the projection onto
\mathcal{C}
. For what values of
\alpha
is it possible that
\langle q^{*},q\rangle=0
, so that
q
is not a
\beta
-weak learner for
\mathcal{C}
?

We will show that the answer is
\alpha\leq 1/2
. Take
q,q^{*}
to be vectors in
\{\pm 1\}^{N}
, so that
\langle q,q^{*}\rangle=0
. Let
d_{H}
denote the normalized Hamming distance in this space. Let
c\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
be the mid-point of
q,q^{*}
in the Hamming metric. Since
d_{H}(q,c)=d_{H}(q^{*},c)=1/4
, we have
\langle q^{*},c\rangle=\langle q,c\rangle=1/2
. Thus we have a triple of vectors
q^{*},q,c\in\{\pm 1\}^{N}
such that
\langle q^{*},c\rangle=\langle q,c\rangle=1/2
, whereas
\langle q^{*},q\rangle=0
. Translating this geometric example to a learning setting requires some care, but it conveys the intuition for why
1/2
arises as the right threshold.

Theorem
4.1
builds on this to show that there exists a distribution
\mathcal{D}
, a predictor
p
and a hypothesis class
\mathcal{C}
such that

•

\mathrm{Opt}(\mathcal{C})\geq 1/2
,

•

p
is
(\mathcal{C},0)
-multiaccurate,

•

\mathrm{cor}(\mathbf{y},k(p))=0
for any
k:[0,1]\to[-1,1]
.

The key challenge is ruling out all post-processing functions
k(p)
, not just
2p-1
. Our construction achieves this by ensuring that for every predicted value
v
,
\E[\mathbf{y}|p(\mathbf{x})=v]=1/2
. Thus the predictor is
anti-calibrated
in the sense that even conditioned on its predictions, the bit
\mathbf{y}
is uniformly random. This property ensures that no function
k(p)
can beat random guessing.

When multiaccuracy implies (restricted) weak agnostic learning.

Complementing this lower bound, Theorem
4.2
shows that
p
being
\mathcal{C}
-multiaccurate implies that
2p-1
is an
(\alpha,2\alpha-1)
weak agnostic learner. Note that
\beta>0
only when
\alpha>1/2
. This is in contrast with the standard usage of weak learning, where on typically thinks of
\alpha
being close to
0
, hence we refer to this notion as restricted weak agnostic learning. In the realizable setting where
\alpha=1
, we get
\beta=1
.

Intuition for why this is the right bound on
\beta
can again be obtained from the geometric setting, where
q^{*},q,c
are vectors in
\{\pm 1\}^{N}
. The condition
\langle q,c\rangle\geq\alpha
translates to
d_{H}(q,c)\leq(1-\alpha)/2
, and similarly for
d_{H}(q^{*},c)
. By the triangle inequality, we must have

d_{H}(q,q^{*})\leq d_{H}(q,c)+d_{H}(q^{*},c)\leq 1-\alpha.

But this means that the correlation is lower bounded by

\langle q,q^{*}\rangle\geq 1-2(1-\alpha)=2\alpha-1.

While translating this intuition from the Boolean to the continuous setting is by no means immediate, we show that
2p-1
is indeed a
\beta=2\alpha-1
-weak learner.
The key step is to show the following upper bound on the correlation obtainable using any hypothesis in
\mathcal{C}
: if
p
is
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multiaccurate, then

\mathrm{Opt}(\mathcal{C})\leq\E_{\mathcal{D}}[p(\mathbf{x})p^{*}(\mathbf{x})+(1-p(\mathbf{x}))(1-p^{*}(\mathbf{x}))]+\tau.

Calibrated multiaccuracy gives strong agnostic learning.

We have seen that
\mathcal{C}
-multiaccuracy alone might not give a weak learner. Similarly, calibration by itself need not give a weak learner, since predicting the constant
1/2
might be calibrated (if
\mathbf{y}
is balanced). This is a well-known problem with calibration as a standalone notion: its predictions might not be informative.

But we observe that this is the only way that it can fail.
In Lemma
4.3
, we show that for a calibrated predictor
p
,

\mathrm{cor}(\mathbf{y},\mathrm{sign}(2p-1))\geq 2\E\left[\left|p(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2}\right|\right]-2\tau.

Thus a calibrated predictor that deviates noticeably from random guessing (saying
1/2
all the time) gives rise to an agnostic learner.
But if we add the assumption that
p
is
\mathcal{C}
-multiaccurate, and that there exists
c\in\mathcal{C}
so that
\mathrm{cor}(\mathbf{y},c\mathbf{x})\geq\alpha
, then
p
can no longer predict
1/2
all the time. This is proved in Lemma
4.4
which shows that for every
c\in\mathcal{C}
,

\E\left[\left|p(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2}\right|\right]\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{cor}(\mathbf{y},c(\mathbf{x}))-\tau.

Intuitively, a hypothesis
c
that is correlated with
\mathbf{y}
provides a certificate that
\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}
is not uniformly random. A
\mathcal{C}
-multiaccurate predictor has to capture the correlations with
c
accurately, which forces it to deviate from random guessing. Putting these insights together, Theorem
4.5
shows that if
p
is both calibrated and
\mathcal{C}
-multiaccurate, then
\mathrm{sign}(2p-1)
is a strong agnostic learner.

We note that this result can also be shown using the loss OI technique of
[
16
]
, where it is applied to show agnostic learning for Boolean function classes
\mathcal{C}
(where correlation coincides with
0
-
1
loss). But we find the proof above
more intuitive as it highlights how multiaccuracy and calibration complement each other.

2.2
Hardcore Sets of Optimal Density

In the context of hardcore sets, we will restrict
\mathcal{C}=\{c:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}\}
to be a class of Boolean functions, rather than bounded functions. We will assume that it contains the constant functions
\mathrm{0},\mathrm{1}
and is closed under complement. We will use
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
to denote a distribution over
\mathcal{X}
. Given a class of functions
\mathcal{C}
, we are interested in studying how well the functions
c\in\mathcal{C}
can approximate a target
hard
Boolean function
g
. For
\delta>0
, we say that
g
is
(\mathcal{C},\delta)
-
hard
on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
if for all
c\in\mathcal{C}
,
\Pr_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[c(\mathbf{x})=g(\mathbf{x})]\leq 1-\delta
. We say a distribution is hardcore when
\delta
becomes arbitrarily close to
1/2
, since this means that it is hard to predict the values of
g
much better than random guessing.

A
measure
on
\mathcal{X}
is a function
\mu:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]
, which is not identically
0
. It defines a probability distribution
\bar{\mu}
by re-weighting
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
by
\mu
. A hardcore measure
\mu
is one which induces a hardcore distribution
\bar{\mu}
.
Given a measure
\mu:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]
and a base distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
on
\mathcal{X}
, the
density
of
\mu
in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
is given by

\mathsf{dns}(\mu)=\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[\mu(\mathbf{x})].

The existence of hardcore measure
\mu
immediately implies that
g
is
\delta=\mathsf{dns}(\mu)/2
hard for
\mathcal{C}
. Impagliazzo’s seminal Hardcore Lemma (IHCL) proves a surprising converse to this statement, provided we assume that
g
is
\delta
-hard not just for
\mathcal{C}
, but for a larger class
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\mathcal{C}_{t,q}
containing
q

oracle gates
from
\mathcal{C}
and a total of
t
logical gates. Since
q
is the more important parameter for us (as
\mathcal{C}
grows in complexity), in this informal discussion we will focus on

and ignore

.

Theorem 2.1

(IHCL informal statement,
[
27
,
26
]
)
.

There exists

(\mathcal{C}_{q},\delta)
-hard on

, then there is a measure

•

Hardcore-ness:

is
(\mathcal{C},1/2-O(\epsilon))
-hard on
\bar{\mu}
.

•

Optimal density:

\mathsf{dns}(\mu)=2\delta-o(1)
.

The density parameter of
2\delta
is optimal, since even a function that computes

perfectly on the complement of

will incur error
\delta
from
\mu
. Impagliazzo’s original proof gives density
\delta

26
]

[

IHCL with density
\delta
from multiaccuracy.

Trevisan, Tulsiani, and Vadhan showed that one can derive Impagliazzo’s Hardcore Lemma from the existence of (what we now call) multiaccurate predictors. We consider the labelled distribution
\mathcal{D}^{g}=(\mathbf{x},g(\mathbf{x}))
and let
p
be a
(\mathcal{C},\epsilon\delta)
-multiaccurate predictor

for

. Their hardcore measure defined as
\mu_{\mathsf{TTV}}(x)=|g(x)-p(x)|
is simple and explicit. Their analysis shows
\mathsf{dns}(\mu_{\mathsf{TTV}})\geq\delta
, and indeed (while we give a slightly sharper density analysis), one can construct instances where the
\mathsf{dns}(\mu_{\mathsf{TTV}})=\delta+o(1)
.
The parameter
q=O(1/(\varepsilon^{2}\delta^{2}))
is governed by the number of calls to a (proper) agnostic learner for
\mathcal{C}
needed to achieve multiaccuracy with error
\tau=\varepsilon\delta
.

IHCL
++
from multicalibration.

Recently, Casacuberta, Dwork and Vadhan
[
8
]
showed that starting from a multicalibrated predictor, one can derive a stronger and more general version of the IHCL, which they call IHCL
++
.
Specifically, they show that given a multicalibrated partition, we can construct hardcore distributions of locally optimal density on each (large enough) piece of the partition.
As expected, given that multicalibration is stronger than multiaccuracy, one can recover the original IHCL from IHCL

by “gluing” these local hardcore distributions.
This glued distribution is a global

-dense hardcore measure.

While this result recovers the optimal hardcore density, the complexity of the class

is now considerably larger than in
[
46
]

Our result: IHCL from calibration and weighted multiaccuracy.

We show how to construct hardcore distributions with optimal density
2\delta
as in
[

but with the lower complexity bound of

for

as in
[
46

To formally state our result, we define weighted multiaccuracy. We will consider weight functions
w:[0,1]\to[1,2]
, which assign weights to predictions, as is standard in the calibration literature
[
19

3
]

is more standard, this choice of range is appropriate for hardcore measures. The weight function that will be important for us is

w_{\mathsf{Max}}(p)=\frac{1}{\max(p,1-p)}.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a distribution on
\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}
whose marginal on
\mathcal{X}
is
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}

\mathsf{MA}_{\mathcal{D}}(w,\mathcal{C},p)=\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\Big|\E_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}}[c(\mathbf{x})w(p(\mathbf{x}))(\mathbf{y}-p(\mathbf{x}))]\Big|.

Let us contrast weighted multiaccuracy to the notions of multiaccuracy and multicalibration. Multiaccuracy corresponds to the case where

, so it is a special case of weighted multiaccuracy. Weighted multiaccuracy specializes the definition of weighted multicalibration from
[
19
]
to the case when the weight family consists of a single weight function. Having to focus on a single weight function rather than a family translates to the complexity of computing a predictor satisfying weighted multiaccuracy being similar to that needed for multiaccuracy.

With this definition in place, we can informally state our main result.
Recall the distribution
\mathcal{D}^{g}=(\mathbf{x},g(\mathbf{x}))
for

.

Theorem 2.2

(IHCL from calibration and weighted multiaccuracy)
.

Let
\varepsilon,\delta>0

q=O(1/(\varepsilon^{2}\delta^{2}))
. Assume that

Then:

•

Hardcore-ness from weighted multiaccuracy:
If
\mathsf{MA}_{\mathcal{D}^{g}}(w_{\mathsf{Max}},\mathcal{C},p)\leq\varepsilon\mathsf{dns}(\mu_{\mathsf{Max}})

•

Optimal density from calibration:
If

.

Along the way, our argument fixes a gap in the gluing argument used to derive the IHCL with density

Improving the density.

We explain at an intuitive level how
\mu_{\mathsf{Max}}
improves over

and gets optimal density. Consider a level set
S_{v}=\{x:p(x)=v\}
of the predictor

based on whether

is

\mu_{\mathsf{TTV}}
assigns weight

and

\E[\mu_{\mathsf{TTV}}(\mathbf{x})|\mathbf{x}\in S_{v}]=v\Pr[g(\mathbf{x})=0|\mathbf{x}\in S_{v}]+(1-v)\Pr[g(\mathbf{x})=1|\mathbf{x}\in S_{v}]=2v(1-v).

Crucially, the measure gives equal weight

Now consider the measure

Why is this a good weighting strategy? Consider the majority predictor

The analysis of the density follows that of

2.3
Multiaccuracy and Restricted Weak Agnostic Learning

As discussed earlier and shown in Theorem

, any

can be viewed as a
(2\alpha-1)
-weak learner when
\alpha>1/2
. Thus multiaccuracy by itself only yields a
restricted

(\alpha,\beta)
-weak agnostic learning, giving non-trivial learners only for
\alpha>1/2
. Syntactically, the task of
(\alpha,\beta)
-weak learning gets harder as
\alpha
decreases, since this corresponds to detecting lower correlation, and as
\beta
increases, since this corresponds to learning a better hypothesis.

The agnostic boosting results of
[
30
,
13
]
tell us that increasing
\beta
does not necessarily result in harder learning problems, provided weak learners exist for arbitrarily small values of
\alpha
. They show that an
(\alpha,\beta)
weak learner implies a

weak learner for all
\gamma>\alpha
. This will improve on the trivial
(\gamma,\beta)
weak learner provided
\gamma>\alpha+\beta
. In particular, this result shows that if weak agnostic learning is possible for arbitrarily small values of
\alpha
, then strong agnostic learning is possible.

The following natural questions arise:

•

Boosting the
\alpha
parameter
: Given an
(\alpha,\beta)
-weak learner, can one construct an
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
-weak learner for
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
? If this is possible, it would give a boosting result for the
\alpha

•

Multiaccuracy from Restricted Weak Agnostic Learning
: Given an
(\alpha,\beta)
-weak agnostic learner for
\mathcal{C}
, can one get a
(\mathcal{C},\alpha^{\prime})
-multiaccuracy for
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
?
[
24
]
already show how to get
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha
.

The questions are related since if we can boost the

The
\alpha
-parameter for Restricted Weak Agnostic Learning cannot be boosted.

We make a standard cryptographic assumption that

Assuming the existence of a

We introduce the notation and background that is used throughout the paper in this section. Further notation and background is introduced in sections as required.
We consider the setting of supervised learning where

Boldface typically refers to random variables.

3.1

We consider a hypothesis class

Definition 3.1
.

\{\pm 1\}

,

Definition 3.2

(Weak agnostic learner)
.

For

,

10

As the algorithm has access to random examples, for any

.

\mathrm{cor}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{y},h(\mathbf{x}))\geq\beta.

We further say that

Weak agnostic learning is a broad definition that covers a wide range of learners.

•

A weak agnostic learner gets more powerful as

The most powerful weak agnostic learner is one that can handle any positive

11

11

We would expect the sample complexity and computational complexity to grow as an (ideally polynomial) function of

•

Intuitively, weak agnostic learning gets easier as
\alpha

Definition 3.3

(Strong agnostic learner)
.

For a hypothesis class

Weak Learning as Squared Loss Minimization

We observe that weak learning is essentially equivalent to non-trivial square loss minimization, that beats random guessing. This observation is not entirely new, but will help elucidate several of our results.

Assume that we have a function
h:\mathcal{X}\to[-1,1]
so that
\mathrm{cor}(\mathbf{y},h(\mathbf{x}))=\E[h(\mathbf{x})(2\mathbf{y}-1)]\geq\beta
. For instance,
h
could be the output of an
(\alpha,\beta)
-weak agnostic learner. Then
[
24
]
shows that the update

p(\mathbf{x})=(1+\beta h(\mathbf{x}))/2
satisfies

\displaystyle\E[(\mathbf{y}-p(\mathbf{x}))^{2}]\leq\E\left[\left(\mathbf{y}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\right]-\frac{3\beta^{2}}{4}.

In other words, weak agnostic learning enables non-trivial squared loss minimization, when compared to random guessing.

In the other direction, we show that any function
h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
which beats random guessing in squared error gives a weak agnostic learner. A similar result is proved in the work of
[
2
]
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy

\displaystyle\E[(\mathbf{y}-h(\mathbf{x}))^{2}]\leq\E\left[\left(\mathbf{y}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\right]-\gamma.

Define the function
\bar{h}
which truncates
h
to
[0,1]
, and let
g(x)=2\bar{h}(x)-1\in[-1,1]
. Then
\mathrm{cor}(\mathbf{y},g(\mathbf{x}))\geq 2\gamma
.

Proof.

Since truncation to the range
[0,1]
only reduces the squared loss, we have

\displaystyle\E[(\mathbf{y}-\bar{h}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}]\leq\E\left[\left(\mathbf{y}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\right]-\gamma.

(1)

We can write

\displaystyle\E[(2\mathbf{y}-2\bar{h}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}]

\displaystyle=\E[(2\mathbf{y}-1-g(x))^{2}]

\displaystyle=\E[(2\mathbf{y}-1)^{2}]-2\E[g(\mathbf{x})(2\mathbf{y}-1)]+\E[g(\mathbf{x})^{2}].

Rearranging terms, we get

\displaystyle\E[g(\mathbf{x})(2\mathbf{y}-1)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\E[(2\mathbf{y}-1)^{2}]-\E[(2\mathbf{y}-2\bar{h}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}]+\E[g(\mathbf{x})^{2}]\right)

\displaystyle\geq 2\left(\E\left[\left(\mathbf{y}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\right]-\E[(\mathbf{y}-\bar{h}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}]\right)

\displaystyle\geq 2\gamma.

where we use Equation (
1
). The claim follows since the LHS is equal to
\mathrm{cor}(\mathbf{y},g(\mathbf{x}))
.
∎

In essence, these results tell us that weak agnostic learning is equivalent to learning a predictor whose squared loss beats random guessing. This is reminiscent of the result of
[
32
]
who show that weak agnostic learning for a class
\mathcal{C}
is equivalent to distinguishing labelled distributions where
\mathrm{cor}(\mathbf{y},c(\mathbf{x}))\geq\alpha
for some hypothesis
c\in\mathcal{C}
from the distribution where
\mathbf{y}
consists of random bits (since in the latter case, random guessing is in fact optimal).

3.2
Multigroup Fairness Notions

We start by recalling the basic notions of accuracy in expectation and calibration.

Accuracy in expectation and calibration.

A predictor
p
is (perfectly) accurate in expectation if
\E[\mathbf{y}]=\E[p(\mathbf{x})]
. Accordingly, we define the

expected accuracy error
of a predictor as

\displaystyle\mathsf{EAE}(p)=\Bigl\lvert\E[\mathbf{y}]-\E[p(\mathbf{x})]\Bigr\rvert.

(2)

A predictor
p
is calibrated if
\E[\mathbf{y}|p(\mathbf{x})]=p(\mathbf{x})
(we assume
p(\mathbf{x})
has a discrete range for simplicity). Accordingly, we define the

expected calibration error
of a predictor as

\displaystyle\mathsf{ECE}(p)=\E\left[\Bigl\lvert\E[\mathbf{y}|p(\mathbf{x})]-p(\mathbf{x})\Bigr\rvert\right].

(3)

We say
p
is
\tau
-calibrated under
\mathcal{D}
if
\mathsf{ECE}(p,\mathcal{D})\leq\tau
.
The following characterization of
\mathsf{ECE}
in terms of bounded auditing functions is standard (see e.g
[
19
]
).

\displaystyle\mathsf{ECE}(p,\mathcal{D})=\max_{v:[0,1]\to[-1,1]}\E_{\mathcal{D}}[v(p(x))(y-p(x))].

(4)

We now define the notions of multiaccuracy
[
46
,
24
]
, calibration
[
9
]
, calibrated multiaccuracy
[
16
]
and multicalibration
[
24
]
. In this subsection, we assume that
\mathcal{C}
is a class of functions,
c:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
; this is more natural as these functions are viewed as group membership.

Definition 3.6
.

We say that a predictor
p
is
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-
multiaccurate
for a distribution
\mathcal{D}
if

\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\Bigl\lvert\E_{\mathcal{D}}[c(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{y}-p(\mathbf{x}))]\Bigr\rvert\leq\tau.

We say the predictor is
\tau
-
calibrated
for
\mathcal{D}
if

\E[|\E[\mathbf{y}|p(\mathbf{x})]-p(\mathbf{x})]|]\leq\tau.

We say that
p
is
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-
multiaccurate and calibrated
if it is both
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multiaccurate and
\tau
-calibrated.
Further, we say that
p
is
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-
multicalibrated
if

\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\E[|c(\mathbf{x})(\E[\mathbf{y}|p(\mathbf{x})]-p(\mathbf{x}))|]\leq\tau.

We will assume that the class
\mathcal{C}
always contains the constant
\mathbf{1}
function; note that this is simply requiring that (approximate) accuracy in expectation hold. For a function
c:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
, let
\bar{c}:X\to[-1,1]
denote the function,
\bar{c}(\mathbf{x})=2c(\mathbf{x})-1
. Then if
p
satisfies
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multiaccuracy for distribution
\mathcal{D}
, we have

\displaystyle\Bigl\lvert\E_{\mathcal{D}}[\bar{c}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{y}-p(\mathbf{x}))]\Bigr\rvert

\displaystyle=\Bigl\lvert\E_{\mathcal{D}}[(2c(\mathbf{x})-1)(\mathbf{y}-p^{*}(\mathbf{x}))]\Bigr\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 2\Bigl\lvert\E_{\mathcal{D}}[c(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{y}-p^{*}(\mathbf{x})]\Bigr\rvert+\Bigl\lvert\E_{\mathcal{D}}[1\cdot(\mathbf{y}-p^{*}(\mathbf{x}))]\Bigr\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 3\tau.

Thus, if
\bar{\mathcal{C}}
is the hypothesis class representing the
\{\pm 1\}
versions of the functions in
\mathcal{C}
, then
p
is
(\bar{\mathcal{C}},3\tau)
-multiaccurate with respect to
\mathcal{D}
as per Definition
3.6
. As a result, we will move freely between the
\{0,1\}
and
\{\pm 1\}
versions of functions.

Calibrated multiaccuracy is an intermediate notion between multiaccuracy and multicalibration; see
[
16
]
for more details. For any
\tau>0
, it is known that having an
(\tau,\poly(\tau))
-weak agnostic learner for
\mathcal{C}
is sufficient to learn a predictor that
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multicalibrated
[
24
,
17
]
. Indeed, if one only wants
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multiaccuracy, then there is a simple algorithm which performs least-squares regression over
\mathcal{C}
via gradient descent, using the weak agnostic learner at each stage to find a gradient update
[
16
]
. One could ask if the reverse connection holds: is having an algorithm that learns a
\mathcal{C}
-multiaccurate predictor sufficient to get a weak agnostic learner for
\mathcal{C}
? We provide some answers to this question in Section

.

4
From Multiaccuracy to Agnostic Learning

As discussed in Section
3
, it is known that having a
(\tau,\poly(\tau))
-weak agnostic learner for
\mathcal{C}
is sufficient learn a predictor that is
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multicalibrated with
\poly(1/\tau)
oracle calls to the weak learner
[
24
,
17
]
. Indeed, if one only wants
(\mathcal{C},\poly(\tau))
-multiaccuracy, there is a simpler algorithm which performs least-squares regression over
\mathcal{C}
via gradient descent, using the weak agnostic learner at each stage to find a gradient update
[

]

-multiaccurate predictor yield some form of agnostic learning?

4.1
Multiaccuracy and Weak Agnostic Learning

Our first theorem shows that a multiaccurate predictor
p
by itself cannot be transformed into even a weak agnostic learner, even when there exists a function in
\mathcal{C}
that has
\mathrm{cor}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{y},c)=1/2
.

Theorem 4.1
.

There exists a class
\mathcal{C}
, a distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}
, and a predictor
p:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
such that

•

p
is
(\mathcal{C},0)
-multiaccurate.

•

For any
k:[0,1]\to[-1,1]
,
k(p)
is not a
(1/2,\beta)
weak agnostic learner for
\mathcal{C}
for any
\beta>0
.

Proof.

We consider the uniform distribution on the domain
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
. Let
\E[\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}]=p^{*}(\mathbf{x})
for
p^{*}:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to[0,1]
. Every such function specifies a joint distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
\{\pm 1\}^{n}\times\{0,1\}
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\{c:\{\pm 1\}^{n-1}\to[-1,1]\}
be the set of all functions of the first
n-1
bits. Now the the predictor
p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=p^{*}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1},-x_{n})
. If we write the multilinear expansion of
p
as

\displaystyle p^{*}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=p^{*}_{0}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})+x_{n}p^{*}_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})

then we have

\displaystyle p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=p^{*}_{0}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})-x_{n}p^{*}_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})

\displaystyle\text{hence}\ p^{*}(x)-p(x)

\displaystyle=2x_{n}p^{*}_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})

It follows that for any function
c(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})
of the first
n-1
bits,

\displaystyle\E[c(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{y}-p(\mathbf{x}))]=\E[c(\mathbf{x})(p^{*}(\mathbf{x})-p(\mathbf{x}))]=\E[c(\mathbf{x})2\mathbf{x}_{n}p^{*}_{1}(\mathbf{x})]=0

since both
c
and
p^{*}_{1}
only depend on the first
n-1
bits. So
p
is
(\mathcal{C},0)
-multiaccurate for
\mathcal{D}
.

We now construct examples of distributions where
k(p)
does not give non-trivial correlation for any
k:[0,1]\to[-1,1]
. Define
\mathrm{MAJ}:\{\pm 1\}^{3}\to\{0,1\}
as

\mathrm{MAJ}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\begin{cases}1&x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}>0\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

For any pair of distinct indices
i,j\in[n-1]
let us denote the distribution
\mathcal{D}_{ij}
where the marginal over
\mathbf{x}
is unfiorm and the conditional label distribution
\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}
is defined by the Bayes optimal predictor

p^{*}_{ij}(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{MAJ}(x_{i},x_{j},x_{n}).

Then consider the predictor

p_{ij}(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{MAJ}(x_{i},x_{j},-x_{n}).

The key observation is that while
p_{ij}
is multiaccurate, its predictions do not tell us much about
p^{*}_{ij}
.
Indeed we have

\Pr[p^{*}_{ij}(\mathbf{x})=p_{ij}(\mathbf{x})]=\Pr[\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{j}]=\frac{1}{2}.

\Pr[p^{*}_{ij}(\mathbf{x})\neq p_{ij}(\mathbf{x})]=\Pr[\mathbf{x}_{i}\neq\mathbf{x}_{j}]=\frac{1}{2}.

A simple analysis shows that

\displaystyle\E[(2\mathbf{y}-1)|p_{ij}(\mathbf{x})=0]

\displaystyle=\E[(2\mathbf{y}-1)|p_{ij}(\mathbf{x})=1]=0.

(5)

Hence for any
k:[0,1]\to[-1,1]
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{cor}_{\mathcal{D}_{ij}}(\mathbf{y},k\circ p_{ij}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=\frac{k(0)}{2}\E[(2\mathbf{y}-1)|p_{ij}(\mathbf{x})=0]+\frac{k(1)}{2}\E[(2\mathbf{y}-1)|p_{ij}(\mathbf{x})=1]=0.

Although no function
k\circ p_{ij}
has non-zero correlation with
p^{*}_{ij}
, the dictator function
c_{i}\in\mathcal{C}
defined as
c_{i}(\mathbf{x})=x_{i}
has correlation
1/2
since

\displaystyle\E[(2\mathbf{y}-1)x_{i}|x_{i}=1]

\displaystyle=\E[(2\mathbf{y}-1)x_{i}|x_{i}=-1]=\frac{3}{4}-\frac{1}{4}=\frac{1}{2}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathrm{cor}_{\mathcal{D}_{ij}}(\mathbf{y},c_{i}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\E[(2\mathbf{y}-1)x_{i}|x_{i}=1]+\frac{1}{2}\E[(2\mathbf{y}-1)x_{i}|x_{i}=-1]=\frac{1}{2}.

This proves that a black-box reduction from
(C,0)
-multiaccuracy cannot give a
(1/2,\beta)
weak agnostic learner for any
\beta>0
.
∎

We observe that Eqn.
5
in the proof above shows that
p
is
maximally
anti-calibrated
, in the sense that
\E[\mathbf{y}|p(\mathbf{x})=v]=1/2
for any
v
in the range of
p
. Such a predictor is clearly no better than random guessing in terms of squared loss. This construction also provides a counterpoint to Lemma
4.3
which shows that any non-trivial

calibration
enforces some correlation with the target
\mathbf{y}
.

The following theorem shows that Theorem
4.1
is tight in the sense that whenever the optimal correlation is noticeably greater than
1/2
, then a simple transformation
p\mapsto 2p-1
, does indeed provide a weak agnostic learning guarantee.

Theorem 4.2
.

For any
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}
, on can post-process a
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multiaccurate predictor to obtain a
(\alpha,2\alpha-1-2\tau)
-weak agnostic learning for
\mathcal{C}
under the distribution
\mathcal{D}
. In particular, any
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multiaccurate predictor
p
for
\mathcal{D}
satisfies

\mathrm{cor}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{y},2p(\mathbf{x})-1)\geq 2\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{cor}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{y},c(\mathbf{x}))-1-2\tau.

Proof.

Let
p
be a
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multiaccurate predictor with respect to
\mathcal{D}
. We have the following for any
c\in\mathcal{C}
.

\displaystyle\mathrm{cor}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{y},c)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{cor}_{\mathcal{D}}(p^{*},c)

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathcal{D}}[(2p^{*}(\mathbf{x})-1)c(\mathbf{x})]

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathcal{D}}[c(\mathbf{x})(p^{*}(\mathbf{x})-(1-p^{*}(\mathbf{x})))(p(\mathbf{x})+(1-p(\mathbf{x})))]

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathcal{D}}[c(x)(p^{*}(\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x})-(1-p^{*}(\mathbf{x}))(1-p(\mathbf{x}))]+\E_{\mathcal{D}}[c(\mathbf{x})(p^{*}(\mathbf{x})-p(\mathbf{x}))]

\displaystyle\leq\E_{\mathcal{D}}[|c(\mathbf{x})||p^{*}(\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x})-(1-p^{*}(\mathbf{x}))(1-p(\mathbf{x}))|]+\tau

\displaystyle\leq\E_{\mathcal{D}}[p^{*}(\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x})+(1-p^{*}(\mathbf{x}))(1-p(\mathbf{x}))]+\tau.

Above, in the penultimate inequality, we used the fact that
p
satisfies
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multiaccuracy with respect to
\mathcal{D}
, and in the final inequality, we use the fact that
|c(\mathbf{x})|\leq 1
, and that

|p^{*}(\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x})-(1-p^{*}(\mathbf{x}))(1-p(\mathbf{x}))|\leq p^{*}(\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x})+(1-p^{*}(\mathbf{x}))(1-p(\mathbf{x})),

since both
p(\mathbf{x}),p^{*}(\mathbf{x})\in[0,1]
.

On the other hand, one can easily check the following identity for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
:

(2a-1)(2b-1)=2(ab+(1-a)(1-b))-1.

Using this we write

\displaystyle\mathrm{cor}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{y},2p-1)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{cor}_{\mathcal{D}}(p^{*},2p-1)

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathcal{D}}[(2p^{*}(\mathbf{x})-1)(2p(\mathbf{x})-1)]

\displaystyle=2\E_{\mathcal{D}}[p^{*}(\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x})+(1-p^{*}(\mathbf{x}))(1-p(\mathbf{x}))]-1.

Combining the two inequalities, we get that,

\displaystyle\mathrm{cor}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{y},2p-1)

\displaystyle\geq 2\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{cor}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{y},c)-1-2\tau,

which proves the claim.
∎

4.2
Strong Agnostic Learning from Calibrated Multiaccuracy

In this section, we show that provided the predictor
p
that is
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multiaccurate is also well-calibrated, then
\mathrm{sign}(2p-1)
in fact is a
strong
agnostic learner. Interestingly, our proof decomposes the roles of
multi-accuracy
and
calibration
neatly and highlights the contribution that both these properties make in order to obtain a strong agnostic learning guarantee. We will assume in this section that the hypothesis class
C
contains functions from
\mathcal{X}\to[-1,1]
; as discussed in Section
3.2
, this is essentially without loss of any generality.

Assume that
p
is well-calibrated. It is intuitive that
p
should do much better than random guessing, since when it predicts
v\in[0,1]
, the expected value of the label is indeed
v
. The only exception to this is if the predictor always predicts values
v
close to
1/2
. We can formalize this as follows:

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
p
be
\tau
-calibrated for some distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}
. Then

\mathrm{cor}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{y},\mathrm{sign}(2p(\mathbf{x})-1))\geq 2\E\left[\left|p(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2}\right|\right]-2\tau.

Proof.

We can write

\displaystyle\mathrm{cor}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{y},\mathrm{sign}(2p(\mathbf{x})-1))

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathcal{D}}[\mathrm{sign}(2p(\mathbf{x})-1)(2\mathbf{y}-1)]

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathcal{D}}[\mathrm{sign}(2p(\mathbf{x})-1)(2\E[\mathbf{y}|p(\mathbf{x})]-1)]

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathcal{D}}[\mathrm{sign}(2p(\mathbf{x})-1)(2p(\mathbf{x})-1)]+2\E_{\mathcal{D}}[\mathrm{sign}(2p(x)-1)(\E[\mathbf{y}|p(\mathbf{x})]-p(\mathbf{x}))]

\displaystyle\geq\E_{\mathcal{D}}[|2p(\mathbf{x})-1|]-2\E[|\E[\mathbf{y}|p(\mathbf{x})]-p(\mathbf{x})|]

\displaystyle\geq 2\E_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\left|p(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2}\right|\right]-2\tau.

The last inequality follows from the definition of
\tau
-calibration.
∎

Next we show that if
\mathcal{C}
contains a hypothesis that correlates well with the labels, and if
p
is
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multiaccurate, then
p
cannot always predict values close to
1/2
.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
p
is
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multiaccurate for the distribution
\mathcal{D}
, then for every
c\in\mathcal{C}
,

\E_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\left|p(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2}\right|\right]\geq\frac{\mathrm{cor}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{y},c(\mathbf{x}))}{2}-\tau.

Proof.

Fix any
c\in C
. Since
p
is
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multiaccurate, we have

\displaystyle\E_{\mathcal{D}}[c(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{y}-p(\mathbf{x}))]

\displaystyle\leq\tau.

(6)

We can also write

\displaystyle\E_{\mathcal{D}}\left[c(\mathbf{x})\left(\mathbf{y}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{cor}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{y},c(\mathbf{x}))}{2}.

(7)

Subtracting Equation (
6
) from (
7
) gives

\displaystyle\E_{\mathcal{D}}\left[c(\mathbf{x})\left(p(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mathrm{cor}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{y},c(\mathbf{x}))}{2}-\tau.

The claim now follows by Hölder’s inequality, since

\displaystyle\E_{\mathcal{D}}\left[c(\mathbf{x})\left(p(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\mathbf{x}}|c(\mathbf{x})|\E\left[\left|p(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2}\right|\right]\leq\E\left[\left|p(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2}\right|\right],

given that
|c(\mathbf{x})|\leq 1
for all
c\in\mathcal{C}
.
∎

The proof of Theorem
4.5
below follows immediately from
Lemmas
4.3
(which applies to
\tau
-calibrated predictors) and
4.4
(which applies to
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multiaccurate predictors). This shows provided that for
any
\tau>0
it is possible to construct a predictor
p
that is
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multiaccurate for
\mathcal{D}
such that
p
is also
\tau
-calibrated, then

\mathrm{sign}(2p-1)
yields a strong agnostic learning guarantee for the class
\mathcal{C}

under
\mathcal{D}
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a hypothesis class and
\tau>0
. Suppose
p
is a predictor that is
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multiaccurate and
\tau
-calibrated with respect to
\mathcal{D}
. Then,
\mathrm{sign}(2p-1)
satisfies,

\mathrm{cor}(\mathbf{y},\mathrm{sign}(2p(\mathbf{x})-1))]\geq\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{cor}(\mathbf{y},c(\mathbf{x}))-4\tau.

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathrm{cor}(\mathbf{y},\mathrm{sign}(2p(\mathbf{x})-1))

\displaystyle\geq 2\E\left[\left|p(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2}\right|\right]-2\tau

p
is
\tau
-calibrated (Lemma
4.3
)

\displaystyle\geq 2\cdot\Bigg(\frac{\mathrm{cor}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{y},c(\mathbf{x}))}{2}-\tau\Bigg)-2\tau

p

(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multiaccurate (Lemma

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{cor}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{y},c(\mathbf{x}))-4\tau.

∎

5
Hardcore Measures with Optimal Density

In this section we consider a domain
\mathcal{X}
endowed with a base distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
. We encourage the reader to think of
\mathcal{X}=\{0,1\}^{n}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
to be the uniform distribution. A
measure
on
\mathcal{X}
is a function
\mu:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]
, which is not identically
0
.
A measure
\mu
defines a probability distribution
\bar{\mu}
by setting

\bar{\mu}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\Pr_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[\mathbf{x}=x]\mu(\mathbf{x})}{\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[\mu(\mathbf{x})]}.

Given a measure
\mu:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]
and a base distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
on
\mathcal{X}
, the
density
of
\mu
in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
is given by

\mathsf{dns}(\mu)=\E_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[\mu(x)].

We drop
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
from the density notation, but note that this is density with respect to the base distribution.
Density measures the min-entropy of a distribution since

\displaystyle\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{\bar{\mu}(x)}{\Pr_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[\mathbf{x}=x]}=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{\mu(x)}{\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[\mu(\mathbf{x})]}=\frac{\max_{x}\mu(x)}{\mathsf{dns}(\mu)}\leq\frac{1}{\mathsf{dns}(\mu)}

(8)

When considering hardcore measures, we will restrict
\mathcal{C}=\{c:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}\}
to be a class of Boolean functions, rather than bounded functions. We will assume that it contains the constant functions
\mathrm{0},\mathrm{1}
and is closed under complement. In complexity theory, given a class of distinguisher functions
\mathcal{C}
, we are interested in studying how well the functions
c\in\mathcal{C}
can approximate the outputs of a given boolean function
g
.
We quantify the
hardness
of
g
with respect to a class of functions
\mathcal{C}
and a distribution
\mathcal{D}
on
\mathcal{X}
as follows:

Definition 5.1

(Hardness of a function)
.

Given a class
\mathcal{C}=\{c:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{0,1\}\}
, a function
g:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
on
\mathcal{X}
, and
\delta>0
, we say that
g
is
(\mathcal{C},\delta)
-
hard
on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
if for all
c\in\mathcal{C}
,

\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[c(\mathbf{x})=g(\mathbf{x})]\leq 1-\delta.

We say a distribution is hardcore when
\delta
becomes arbitrarily close to
1/2
, since this means that it is hard to predict the values of
g
much better than random guessing. A hardcore measure
\mu
is one which induces a hardcore distribution
\bar{\mu}
.

Definition 5.2

(Relative complexity of a function and a function class
[
28
, Definition 6]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a family of functions
f\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]
. A function
h
has
complexity
(t,q)
relative to
\mathcal{C}
if it can be computed by an oracle-aided circuit of size
t
with
q
oracle gates, where each oracle gate is instantiated with a function from
\mathcal{C}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{C}_{t,q}
the class of functions that have complexity at most
(t,q)
relative to
\mathcal{C}
.

Impagliazzo’s hardcore lemma
[
27
]
studies boolean functions
g
that are somewhat hard to predict, and it shows that for these functions we can always find a hardcore measure
\mu
(called a
hardcore measure
) such that, under the distribution
\bar{\mu}
, the function
g
is very hard to compute with respect to
\mathcal{C}
, one cannot improve much over random guessing. Formally:

Theorem 5.3

(IHCL,
[
27
,
26
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\{c:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{0,1\}\}
be a family of functions, let
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
be a probability distribution over
\mathcal{X}
, and let
\epsilon,\delta>0
.
There exist
t=\mathrm{poly}(\log|\mathcal{X}|,1/\epsilon,1/\delta)

and
q=\emph{poly}(1/\epsilon,1/\delta)

such that the following holds: If
g:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
is
(\mathcal{C}_{t,q},\delta)
-hard on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
, then there is a measure
\mu
satisfying:

•

Hardness:
g
is
(\mathcal{C},1/2-\epsilon)
-hard on
\bar{\mu}
.

•

Optimal density:
\mathsf{dns}(\mu)=2\delta
.

Impagliazzo’s original proof gives density
\delta

[
27
]
, the improvement to the optimal
2\delta
is due to Holenstein
[
26
]
.

We now define the set of balanced under calibration measures, which play a key role in our result.

Definition 5.4
.

Define the weight function
w_{\mathsf{Max}}:[0,1]\to[1,2]
as
w_{\mathsf{Max}}(p)=1/\max(p,1-p)
and the weight function family

W=\{w:[0,1]\to[1,2]\ s.t.\ w(p)\in[1,w_{\mathsf{Max}}(p)]\}.

Given
g:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
and
p:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]
, we define the set
M(p,g)
of balanced under calibration measures to be
\{\mu_{w}\}_{w\in W}
where

\mu_{w}(x)=w(p(x))\cdot|g(x)-p(x)|\ \text{for}\ w\in W.

We define the the maximal balanced under calibration measure
\mu_{\mathsf{Max}}\in M(p,g)
as

\mu_{\mathsf{Max}}(x)=w_{\mathsf{Max}}(p(x))\cdot|g(x)-p(x)|

while the minimal balanced under calibration measure is
\mu_{\mathsf{TTV}}(x)=|g(x)-p(x)|
, corresponding to
w=1
.

The condition
\mu(x)\in[0,1]
requires the constraint
w(p(x))\leq w_{\mathsf{Max}}(p(x))\leq 2
.
We also impose the condition
w(p)\geq 1
since the goal is to find large/dense measures, so there is no point in choosing weights less than
1
.
Intuitively, this ensures that, across the level sets
S_{v}
, the measure
\mu_{\mathsf{Max}}
assigns higher probability measure to level sets with
v
value closer to
1/2
, which increases the hardness of
g
when sampling according to the measure.
We note following chain of inequalities:

\mu_{\mathsf{TTV}}(x)\leq\mu(x)\leq\mu_{\mathsf{Max}}(x)\leq 2\mu_{\mathsf{TTV}}(x).

Some comments on the density and hardness of distributions in
M(p,g)
:

•

As
w_{\mu}
increases from
1
to
w_{\mathsf{Max}}(p)
,
\mu(x)
and hence
\mathsf{dns}(\mu)=\E_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[\mu(\mathbf{x})]
increases. However, it is less clear how the hardness of the distribution
\bar{\mu}
changes, since this changes the balance of weight between the different level sets. Indeed, as we shall see, all these distributions are hard, under suitable assumptions on
p
(
p
being multicalibrated will suffice).

•

In contrast with
\mu_{\mathsf{TTV}}
,
\mu_{\mathsf{Max}}
gives (nearly) twice as much weight to the level sets where
p(x)\approx 1/2
, which intuitively is the region where
g
is hardest for the predictors in
\mathcal{C}
. So one might hope that (under suitable assumptions about the predictor
p
)
\mu_{\mathsf{Max}}
is indeed hardcore, and has density
2\delta
.

•

For
p
close to
\{0,1\}
,
w_{\mathsf{Max}}(p)\approx 1
and
\mu_{\mathsf{Max}}
and
\mu_{\mathsf{TTV}}
are almost equal. For distributions where
p
takes such values with reasonable probability, if
\mu_{\mathsf{Max}}
is indeed
2\delta
dense, it suggests that the density of
\mu_{\mathsf{TTV}}
is in fact greater than
\delta
.

5.1
Weighted Multiaccuracy and Hardcore Measures

We define the notion of weighted multiaccuracy, which applies to distributions on
\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}
.

Definition 5.5

(Weighted multiaccuracy)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a distribution on
\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}
, let
\mathcal{C}=\{c:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{0,1\}\}
be a class of functions, let
p:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]
be predictor, and
w:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
a weight function. We define the
(w,\mathcal{C})
-
multiaccuracy error
of
p
under the distribution
\mathcal{D}
as

\mathsf{MA}_{\mathcal{D}}(w,\mathcal{C},p)=\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\Big|\E_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}}[c(\mathbf{x})w(p(\mathbf{x}))(\mathbf{y}-p(\mathbf{x}))]\Big|.

We say that the predictor
p
is
(w,\mathcal{C},\varepsilon)
-
multiaccurate
for
\mathcal{D}
if
\mathsf{MA}_{\mathcal{D}}(w,\mathcal{C},p)\leq\epsilon
.

Given a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
on
\mathcal{X}
and
g:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
, define the distribution
\mathcal{D}^{g}
on
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}
where
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathbf{y}=g(\mathbf{x})
. We relate the hardness of predicting
g
using functions from
\mathcal{C}
under
\bar{\mu}_{w}
to the
w
-weighted multiaccuracy error of
p
for the class
\mathcal{C}
under distribution
\mathcal{D}^{g}
.

Lemma 5.6
.

If
\mathsf{MA}_{\mathcal{D}^{g}}(w,\mathcal{C},p)\leq\varepsilon
, then
g
is
\big(1/2-3\varepsilon/(2\mathsf{dns}(\mu_{w}))\big)
-hard for
\mathcal{C}
under
\bar{\mu}_{w}
.

Before we prove Lemma
5.6
, we show some intermediate results. Let
\bar{\mu}
denote a distribution on
\mathcal{X}
.
Let
\eta=\E_{\bar{\mu}}[g(x)]
.
The function class
\mathcal{C}
can trivially approximate
g
to accuracy
\max(\eta,1-\eta)
under the distribution
\bar{\mu}
by using a constant function. To characterize how much better
\mathcal{C}
can do, we define

\displaystyle\mathrm{cor}_{\bar{\mu}}(g,\mathcal{C})=\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}|\mathrm{cor}_{\bar{\mu}}(g,c)|=\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\left|\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\bar{\mu}}[(2g(\mathbf{x})-1)c(\mathbf{x})]\right|.

The next lemma shows that this captures the hardness of
g
for the class
\mathcal{C}
, under the distribution
\mathcal{D}
.

Lemma 5.7
.

We have

\displaystyle\min(\eta,1-\eta)+\mathrm{cor}_{\bar{\mu}}(g,\mathcal{C})\leq\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\Pr_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[c(\mathbf{x})=g(\mathbf{x})]\leq\max(\eta,1-\eta)+\mathrm{cor}_{\bar{\mu}}(g,\mathcal{C}).

(9)

Proof.

We can write

\displaystyle\Pr_{\bar{\mu}}[c(\mathbf{x})=g(\mathbf{x})]

\displaystyle=\E_{\bar{\mu}}\left[\frac{1}{2}(1+(2c(\mathbf{x})-1)(2g(\mathbf{x})-1))\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{\bar{\mu}}[c(\mathbf{x})(2g(\mathbf{x})-1)+(1-g(\mathbf{x}))]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{cor}_{\bar{\mu}}(g,c)+1-\eta.

Recall that
\bar{c}=1-c
and that we are assuming that
\mathcal{C}
is closed under complemntation. So we have

\displaystyle\Pr_{\bar{\mu}}[\bar{c}(\mathbf{x})=g(\mathbf{x})]

\displaystyle=1-\Pr_{\bar{\mu}}[c(\mathbf{x})=g(\mathbf{x})]

\displaystyle=\E_{\bar{\mu}}[g(\mathbf{x})]-\E_{\bar{\mu}}[c(\mathbf{x})(2g(\mathbf{x})-1)]]

\displaystyle=\eta-\mathrm{cor}_{\bar{\mu}}(g,c).

The upper bound of
\max(\eta,1-\eta)+|\mathrm{cor}_{\bar{\mu}}(g,c)|
holds in both cases. The upper bound follows by maximizing over all
c\in\mathcal{C}
.

To prove the lower bound, in order predict
g
with accuracy
\min(\eta,1-\eta)+|\mathrm{cor}_{\bar{\mu}}(g,c)|
, we use
c
if
\mathrm{cor}_{\bar{\mu}}(g,c)\geq 0
; else we use
\bar{c}
, which also lies in
\mathcal{C}
by assumption. Maximizing over
c\in\mathcal{C}
gives the claimed bound.
∎

Equation (
9
) gives tight bounds on the prediction advantage of
\mathcal{C}
for balanced functions where
\eta\approx 1/2
. In general, it characterizes the advantage to within an additive
|1-2\eta|
, since

\max(\eta,1-\eta)-\min(\eta,1-\eta)=|1-2\eta|.

Lemma
5.7
can be seen as a generalization of Yao’s Lemma relating pseudorandomness and unpredictability (see Lemma 2.14 in
[
8
]
for a formal statement).

For balanced under calibration measures, we can relate correlation of
\mathcal{C}
with
g
under the distribution
\bar{\mu}_{w}
to the
(w,\mathcal{C})
-weighted multiaccuracy error for the labeled distribution
\mathcal{D}^{g}
on
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
where
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}
and
\mathbf{y}=g(\mathbf{x})
.

Lemma 5.8
.

Consider a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
on
\mathcal{X}
, a labeling function
g:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
, a predictor
p
, a weight function
w\in W
and the corresponding measure
\mu_{w}\in M(p,g)
. Then

\mathsf{MA}_{\mathcal{D}^{g}}(w,\mathcal{C},p)=\mathrm{cor}_{\bar{\mu}_{w}}(\mathcal{C},g)\cdot\mathsf{dns}(\mu_{w}).

Proof.

For
y\in\{0,1\}
and
t\in[0,1]
we have the identity
|y-t|\cdot(2y-1)=y-t
.
Applying this with
\mathbf{y}=g(\mathbf{x})
and
t=p(\mathbf{x})
, we can write

\displaystyle\E_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}^{g}}[c(\mathbf{x})w(p(\mathbf{x}))(\mathbf{y}-p(\mathbf{x}))]

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[c(\mathbf{x})w(p(\mathbf{x}))(g(\mathbf{x})-p(\mathbf{x}))]

\displaystyle=\E_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[c(\mathbf{x})w(p(\mathbf{x}))\cdot|g(\mathbf{x})-p(\mathbf{x})|\cdot(2g(\mathbf{x})-1)]

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[\mu_{w}(\mathbf{x})c(\mathbf{x})(2g(\mathbf{x})-1)]

\displaystyle=\frac{\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[\mu_{w}(\mathbf{x})c(\mathbf{x})(2g(\mathbf{x})-1)]}{\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[\mu_{w}(\mathbf{x})]}\cdot\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[\mu_{w}(\mathbf{x})]

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\bar{\mu}_{w}}[c(\mathbf{x})(2g(\mathbf{x})-1)]\cdot\mathsf{dns}(\mu_{w})

(10)

Two steps above, we used the fact that
\mu_{w}(\mathbf{x})=w(p(\mathbf{x}))\cdot|g(\mathbf{x})-p(\mathbf{x})|
.
Taking the absolute value on either side and maximizing over all
c\in\mathcal{C}
gives the claimed result.
∎

We use this to prove that weighted multiaccuracy of
p
under
\mathcal{D}^{g}
implies
g
is hard for
\mathcal{C}
under
\bar{\mu}_{w}
.

Proof of Lemma
5.6
.

We first show that multiaccuracy implies
\eta=\E_{\bar{\mu}_{w}}[g(x)]
is close to
1/2
. Since
\mathbf{1}\in\mathcal{C}
, we can set
c=\mathbf{1}
in Equation (

) to get

\displaystyle\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[w(p(\mathbf{x}))(g(\mathbf{x})-p(\mathbf{x}))]|=\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\bar{\mu}_{w}}[(2g(\mathbf{x})-1)]\cdot\mathsf{dns}(\mu)=(2\eta-1)\cdot\mathsf{dns}(\mu).

Since this is bounded in absolute value by
\varepsilon
, given that
\mathsf{MA}_{\mathcal{D}^{g}}(w,\mathcal{C},p)\leq\varepsilon
, we conclude that

\left|\eta-\frac{1}{2}\right|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2\mathsf{dns}(\mu)}.

By Lemma
5.8
, we can use multiaccuracy to bound

\mathrm{cor}_{\bar{\mu}_{w}}(\mathcal{C},g)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathsf{dns}(\mu)}.

We plug these bounds into Lemma
5.7
to get

\displaystyle\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\Pr_{\mathbf{x}\sim\bar{\mu}_{w}}[c(\mathbf{x})=g(\mathbf{x})]

\displaystyle\leq\max(\eta,1-\eta)+\mathrm{cor}_{\bar{\mu}_{w}}(\mathcal{C},g)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2\mathsf{dns}(\mu)}+\mathrm{cor}_{\bar{\mu}_{w}}(\mathcal{C},g)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3\varepsilon}{2\mathsf{dns}(\mu)}

which implies the desired hardness.
∎

Optimal Density Analysis Using Calibration

that

, which is expressive enough to compute

Restricted Weak Agnostic Learning and Multiacccuracy

Theorem

proves a reduction to multiaccuracy from
(\alpha,\beta)
-weak agnostic learning for
\alpha>1/2

We begin by the observation that given any (restricted)

-weak agnostic learner for

, can we efficiently construct a

)-multiaccurate predictor by the original multiaccuracy algorithm

24
]
. The following result is essentially proved in their paper and follows from the observation that if there is a multiaccuracy violation, then the weak agnostic learner can be used to provide a gradient update that reduces the potential function.

Theorem 6.1

(
[
24
]
)

For any concept class
\mathcal{C}
, distribution
\mathcal{D}
and parameters

(\alpha,\beta)

, one can produce a
(\mathcal{C},\alpha+\varepsilon)
-multiaccurate predictor for

using

The remainder of the section is devoted to proving Theorem
6.5
below, which shows that (a) in general,
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta)
-weak agnostic learning cannot be reduced to
(\alpha,\beta)
-agnostic learning for
\alpha>\alpha^{\prime}
, (b) in general, we cannot obtain a predictor that is
(\mathcal{C},\alpha/2-\varepsilon)
-multiaccurate given access to an
(\alpha,\beta)
-weak agnostic learner. The latter result has a gap of a factor of two when viewed in conjunction with Theorem

. While closing this is an interesting question, we observe that this is not possible for all values of
\alpha
, e.g. when
\alpha=1
, i.e., we only have
realizable
weak learning, it is still always possible to achieve
(\mathcal{C},1/2)
-multiaccuracy, by using the constant predictor

.

Note that the task of
(\alpha,\beta)
weak learning gets harder as

•

\alpha
decreases, since this corresponds to detecting lower correlation.

•

\beta
increases, since this corresponds to learning a better hypothesis.

The agnostic boosting results of
[
13
,
30
]
tell us that increasing
\beta
does not necessarily result in harder learning problems. They show that an
(\alpha,\beta)
weak learner implies a
(\gamma,\gamma-\alpha)
weak learner for all
\gamma>\alpha
. This result is typically interpreted to say that if weak agnostic learning is possible for
\alpha

, then strong agnostic learning is possible.

It is natural to ask if there might be a similar boosting statement for
\alpha
: that having an
(\alpha,\beta)
weak learner might imply an
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
weak learner for some values of
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
. We will show that such a result is unlikely, assuming the existence of standard cryptographic primitives.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon>0
, we will show that there exist classes that are agnostically learnable in polynomial time given correlation
\alpha+\varepsilon
with the labels, but are hard to learn in polynomial time if the correlation is bounded by
\alpha
. Further, in the former case, the learner only requires random samples and is a proper learner, but for the latter case, the weak learner is allowed query access, and is allowed to be improper. This result shows that weak learning indeed becomes easier as
\alpha
increases, supporting our claim that multiaccuracy might be weaker as a learning primitive than weak agnostic learning.

Our construction of classes that are hard to learn assumes the existence of pseudorandom families of functions, and on the construction of binary error-correcting codes that can be (list)decoded from error rates approaching
1/2

[
22
]
. Decoding from error rate

is information-theoretically impossible, since a random string would be at distance
1/2
from a codeword. The gap between distance

and

can be viewed as a gap of
0
versus
2\varepsilon
in the correlation between the received word and codeword. Our result uses pseudorandom functions to translate this to a computational gap in the learnability of a suitably defined concept class with correlation
\alpha
versus
\alpha+\varepsilon

[
12

which he uses to show that membership queries add power to agnostic learning.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

We have shown in this paper that multiaccuracy, together with global calibration, is a fairly powerful notion that has implications for agnostic learning and (optimal) hardcore set constructions. However, our work leaves open several open questions.

An immediate one is closing the gap in Theorem
6.5
which only shows that an
(\alpha,\beta)
-weak agnostic learner does not yield anything better than
(\mathcal{C},\alpha/2)
-multiaccuracy; however, Theorem
6.1
only shows that it is possible to achive
(\mathcal{C},\alpha)
-multiaccuracy using access to an
(\alpha,\beta)
-weak agnostic learner.

Another natural question is whether more general reductions to
multiaccuracy
can yield a weak agnostic learner. The result in Theorem
4.1
only rules out learners obtained by post-processing a predictor satisfying certain multigroup guarantees. A stronger model would be to consider an algorithm that has oracle access to a learner that for multiaccurate predictors, which it can call multiple times. In Appendix
A
, we show how a more
general
post-processing involving points
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
in addition to just the value
p(\mathbf{x})
can in some cases yield agnostic learners.

Lastly, by the equivalence between the Regularity Lemma by
[
46
]
and the multiaccuracy theorem, multicalibration implies stronger and more general versions of other well-known theorems in complexity theory besides the hardcore lemma, such as the Dense Model Theorem and characterizations of pseudo-average min-entropy
[
43
,
47
,
8
]
, and Yao’s XOR lemma
[
39
]
.
Similar to our result for Impagliazzo’s Hardcore Lemma,
it is natural to ask if versions of these theorems can be proved under weaker assumptions like calibrated multiaccuracy.

p
. Then,

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle\leq\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[\tilde{p}(\mathbf{x})^{2}]-\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[(\tilde{p}(\mathbf{x})-q(\mathbf{x}))^{2}]

\displaystyle=2\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[\tilde{p}(\mathbf{x})q(\mathbf{x})]-\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[q(\mathbf{x})^{2}]

\displaystyle=2\E_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}}[(2\mathbf{y}-1)q(\mathbf{x})]-\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[q(\mathbf{x})^{2}]+2\E_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}}[q(\mathbf{x})(\tilde{p}(\mathbf{x})-(2\mathbf{y}-1))]

\displaystyle=2\E_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}}[(2\mathbf{y}-1)q(\mathbf{x})]-\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[q(\mathbf{x})^{2}]+4\E_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}}[q(\mathbf{x})(p(\mathbf{x})-y(\mathbf{x}))]

\displaystyle=2\E_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}}[(2\mathbf{y}-1)q(\mathbf{x})]-\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[q(\mathbf{x})^{2}]+4\sum_{s}\lambda_{s}\E_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}}[c_{s}(\mathbf{x})(p(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{y})]

\displaystyle\leq\E_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}}[(2\mathbf{y}-1)^{2}]-\E_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}}[((2\mathbf{y}-1)-q(\mathbf{x}))^{2}]+4\tau\sum_{s}|\lambda_{s}|

\displaystyle\leq 1-\E_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}}[((2\mathbf{y}-1)-h(\mathbf{x}))^{2}]+4\tau\sum_{s}|\lambda_{s}|.

Above in the second last inequality, we used the fact that
p
is
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multiaccurate for
\mathcal{D}
and that
c_{s}\in\mathcal{C}
for all

, and in the last inequality we use that

q

only reduces the squared error with respect to

∎

Let

Then,

Conversely, it follows immediately that if we have a

, such that

q=\alpha c/\|c\|
, satisfies,

\displaystyle\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[((2p(\mathbf{x})-1)-q(\mathbf{x}))^{2}]

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[(2p(\mathbf{x})-1)^{2}]-2\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[(2p(\mathbf{x})-1)q(\mathbf{x})]+\E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[q(\mathbf{x})^{2}]

A.1
Multiaccuracy as a Query Oracle

A natural question is whether there are any cases where such
\ell_{1}
-sparse projections are possible. One advantage of having access to the predictor
p
rather than just examples
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
drawn from
\mathcal{D}
is that we may query the predictor
p
at any point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
of our choice. As a result, if we have a
proper
learning algorithm for a class
\mathcal{C}
that uses membership queries, then we can learn such a class only with random examples and access to a multiaccurate predictor
p
.

Theorem A.3
.

Suppose the class
\mathcal{C}
(that contains the constant function
1
) is efficiently
properly
agnostically learnable with access to random examples from
\mathcal{D}
and query access to the target function to accuracy
\varepsilon
, then provided we have access to a
(C,\tau)
-multiaccurate predictor
p
for
\mathcal{D}
,
\mathcal{C}
is efficiently
properly
agnostically learnable using only access to random examples from
\mathcal{D}
and (using the predictor
p
) to accuracy
\varepsilon+3\tau
.

Proof.

In this proof, we will assume that the target function
f
takes values in
\{0,1\}
and also that
\mathcal{C}
is a class of functions from
\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be the algorithm for efficiently agnostically learning
\mathcal{C}
using the random examples from
\mathcal{D}
and query access to the target function and
p
be the
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multiaccurate predictor for
\mathcal{D}
. Since
p
may not be Boolean, we denote by
\mathbf{Z}_{p(\mathbf{x})}
a (freshly independently drawn each time) Bernoulli random variable with parameter
p(\mathbf{x})
. When the algorithm draws a random example, we replace
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, by
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Z}_{p(\mathbf{x})})
and we simulate queries by returning
\mathbf{Z}_{p(\mathbf{x})}
when queried with point
\mathbf{x}
. Then, we are guaranteed that
\mathcal{A}
returns
c_{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathcal{C}
, such that with probability at least
0.99
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}(c_{\mathcal{A}};\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\leq\min_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\E[|c(\mathbf{x})-p(\mathbf{x})|]+\varepsilon

Let
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
denote the target, i.e. we get
(\mathbf{x},f(\mathbf{x}))
from
\mathcal{D}
. Then, we have the following,

\displaystyle\Pr[c(\mathbf{x})\neq f(\mathbf{x})]

\displaystyle=\E[c(\mathbf{x})(1-f(\mathbf{x}))+(1-c(\mathbf{x}))f(\mathbf{x})]

\displaystyle=\E[c(\mathbf{x})+f(\mathbf{x})]-2\E[c(\mathbf{x})f(\mathbf{x})]

\displaystyle\leq\E[c(\mathbf{x})+p(\mathbf{x})]-2\E[c(\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x})]+3\tau

\displaystyle=\E[c(\mathbf{x})(1-p(\mathbf{x}))+(1-c(\mathbf{x}))p(\mathbf{x})]+3\tau

\displaystyle=\E[|c(\mathbf{x})-p(\mathbf{x})|]+3\tau.

Similarly, we may show that

\displaystyle\Pr[c(\mathbf{x})\neq f(\mathbf{x})]

\displaystyle\geq\E[|c(\mathbf{x})-p(\mathbf{x})|]-3\tau.

Above we have used that
p
is
(\mathcal{C},\tau)
-multiaccurate for
\mathcal{D}
as well as assumed that the constant
\mathbf{1}
function is in
\mathcal{C}
.

Thus, we get that,

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}(c_{\mathcal{A}};\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\leq\min_{c\in C}\mathrm{err}(c;\mathcal{D})+\varepsilon+3\delta.

∎

When considering learning functions defined over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
with respect to the uniform (or in some cases a product) distribution over the domain, a classical result of Goldreich and Levin shows that with query access to the target function, we can identify all the
heavy
Fourier coefficients. Essentially this algorithm allows us to find projections as required by Theorem
A.1
; this also gives a proper agnostic learning algorithm for the class of parities. We briefly the Goldreich-Levin algorithm below.

The Goldreich-Levin algorithm.

By the Fourier expansion theorem we know that every function
f:\{-1,1\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
can be uniquely expressed as a multilinear polynomial:

f(x)=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}(S)\chi_{S},

where
\chi_{S}(x)=\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}
for
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\widehat{f}(S)=\E_{x\sim\{-1,1\}^{n}}[f(x)\chi_{S}]

denotes the Fourier coefficient of
f
on
S
.
Again defining the inner product
\langle f,g\rangle
between two functions
f,g:\{-1,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as
\E_{x\sim\{-1,1\}^{n}}[f(x)g(x)]
we see that the
2^{n}
parity functions
\chi_{S}:\{-1,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{-1,1\}
form an orthonormal basis for the vector space of functions
\{-1,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

[
40
, Thm. 1.5]
.
The Goldreich-Levin algorithm finds the largest Fourier coefficients of a function given membership access to it:

Theorem A.4

(Goldreich-Levin
[
20
]
)
.

Given query access to
f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to[-1,1]
, and given
\gamma,\delta>0
,
there is a
\text{poly}(n,\frac{1}{\gamma},\log\frac{1}{\delta})
-time algorithm that outputs a list

L=\{S_{1},\dots,S_{m}\}
such that:

1.

If
\widehat{f}(S)\geq\gamma
, then
S\in L
, and

2.

If
S\in L
, then
\widehat{f}(S)\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}
holds with probability
1-\delta
.
