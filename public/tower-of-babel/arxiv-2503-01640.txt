Integral closure of 1-dimensional rings
Mohsen Asgharzadeh

Abstract
. We study certain properties of modules over 1-dimensional local integral domains. First, we examine the order of the conductor ideal and its expected relationship with multiplicity. Next, we investigate the reflexivity of certain colength-two ideals. Finally, we consider the freeness problem of the absolute integral closure of a DVR \operatorname{DVR} , and connect this to the reflexivity problem of R 1 p n R^{\frac{1}{p^{n}}} .

2.
When is order of conductor 2?

In this section we study the following question:

Question 2.1
.

Let
R=k[[H]]
be a numerical semigroup ring with
e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)-\mu(\mathfrak{m})=1
. Then is
\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})=2
?

The
trace
of an
R
-module
M
, denoted
\operatorname{tr}_{R}(M)
, is defined to be

\operatorname{tr}_{R}(M)=\sum_{f\in M^{*}}\operatorname{im}(f),
with the convenience that
M^{\ast}:=\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(M,R)
. Recall from
[
9
]
that
a ring
R
is
far-flung Gorenstein
provided

\mathfrak{C}=\operatorname{tr}(\omega_{R}),

where
\omega_{R}
is the canonical module.

Lemma 2.2
.

(See
[
9
, Corollary 6.3]
). Let
a\geq 3
and d be coprime nonnegative integers and
H=\langle a,a+d,\cdots,a+(a-1)d\rangle
. Then
R:=k[[H]]
is a far-flung Gorenstein ring if and only if
d=1
.

Recall from
[
8
]
that
a ring
R
is
nearly Gorenstein
provided

\mathfrak{m}\subseteq\operatorname{tr}(\omega_{R}).

Also,

R
is called
almost Gorenstein
if there exists an exact sequence

0\to R\to\omega_{R}\to C\to 0
with

e_{\mathfrak{m}}(C)=\mu(C)
. For more details,
see
[
7
]
. Also, we cite
[
2
]
as a reference monograph on totally reflexive modules.

Lemma 2.3
.

(See
[
12
, Corollary 3.7]
). Let
R=k[[t^{4},t^{5},t^{7}]]
. Then
R
is an almost Gorenstein local ring of dimension
one. Also,
R
is nearly Gorenstein and any totally reflexive module is free.

We cite
[
2
]
to see definition and basic properties of totally reflexive modules. Now, we are ready to solve the first question:

Example 2.4
.

Let
R:=k[[t^{4},t^{5},t^{7}]]
. Then
e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)-\mu(\mathfrak{m})=1
but
\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})=1
.

Proof.

We know from
[
11
, Ex. 11.9]
that

e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)=\min\{4,5,7\}

and also that
\mu(\mathfrak{m})=3
. Thus,

e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)-\mu(\mathfrak{m})=1.

Recall that

H=\langle a,a+d,\dots,a+(a-1)d\rangle

with
a:=4
and
d=1
.
Thanks to Lemma
2.2
, the ring
R
is far-flung Gorenstein. Hence,

\mathfrak{C}=\operatorname{tr}(\omega_{R})
.
In view of Lemma
2.3
,
R
is nearly Gorenstein. Then, we have

\mathfrak{m}\subseteq\operatorname{tr}(\omega_{R}).

However,
R
is non-regular but totally reflexive module is free. It follows that
R
is not Gorenstein, and consequently,

\operatorname{tr}(\omega_{R})\neq R.

Therefore,

\mathfrak{m}=\operatorname{tr}(\omega_{R})=\mathfrak{C}.

By definition,

\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})=1.

∎

Let

We know

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be any complete domain with
\mathfrak{C}+xR=\mathfrak{m}
for some
x\in\mathfrak{m}
. Then
\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})=1.

Proof.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
\mathfrak{C}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{2}
. Since
\mathfrak{C}+xR=\mathfrak{m}
and
\mathfrak{C}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{2}
, it follows that

\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{C}=(\mathfrak{C}+xR)/\mathfrak{C}

is cyclic. The natural surjection

\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{C}\to\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^{2}\to 0

implies that
\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^{2}
is cyclic as well. Consequently, we obtain
\mu(\mathfrak{m})=1
, which in turn implies that
R
is a discrete valuation ring (
\operatorname{DVR}
).
Since
R
is a
\operatorname{DVR}
, we must have
\mathfrak{C}=R
. However, this leads to a contradiction because

R=\mathfrak{C}+xR=\mathfrak{m},

whereas
\mathfrak{m}
is a proper ideal of
R
. Thus, our assumption that
\mathfrak{C}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{2}
must be false.
We have therefore shown that
\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})\leq 1
. Moreover, note that
\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})=0
if and only if
\mathfrak{C}=R
, which we have already ruled out. Hence, it follows that

\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})=1.

∎

However, Question 2.1 is true for
1
-dimensional hypersurfaces.

Let

Since

3.
The order of conductor looking for multiplicity

The results of this section are in support of:

Question 3.1
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a complete hypersurface domain of dimension
1
. Is
\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})=e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)-1
?

Observation 3.2
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{C})
be a complete local domain of dimension
1
and multiplicity 2. Then
\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})=e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)-1
.

3.1.
Presenting a bound

Since the above ring is Cohen-Macaulay
and of multiplicity 2, it should be hypersurface. So, it is the special case of:

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m},\mathbb{C})
be a complete local hypersurface domain of dimension
1
and multiplicity
\leq 3
. Then
\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})=e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)-1
.

Proof.

Note that
\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})=0
if and only if
\mathfrak{C}=R
, if and only if
R
is normal, if and only if
R
is regular, if and only if
e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)=1
.

Now, assume
e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)=2
.
Since
R
is analytically unramified, it follows from a theorem of Buchweitz-Greuel-Schreyer that
R
is a simple singularity. Moreover, by a theorem of Greuel-Knörrer, since
R
is a hypersurface, it must have an
A_{n}
-singularity. In other words,

R:=\mathbb{C}[[X,Y]]/(X^{2}+Y^{n}).

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
n=2m
is even. Then,

0=X^{2}+Y^{n}=(X+iY^{m})(X-iY^{m}),

where
i\in\mathbb{C}
is the imaginary unit satisfying
i^{2}=-1
. This contradicts the assumption that
R
is a domain. Hence,
n
must be odd. From this, we conclude

R\cong\mathbb{C}[[t^{2},t^{n}]].

Since
\gcd(2,n)=1
, it follows from
[
5
, 5.5]
that

\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})=e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)-1.

Next, assume
e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)=3
. By
[
22
, Theorem 8.5]
, we see that
R
must be one of the following:

\big\{\mathbb{C}[[X,Y]]/(X^{3}+Y^{4}),\mathbb{C}[[X,Y]]/(X^{3}+Y^{5}),\mathbb{C}[[X,Y]]/(X^{3}+XY^{3})\big\}
.

In the third case, we derive a contradiction, as the ring contains a zero-divisor. Thus, we must have either

R\cong\mathbb{C}[[t^{3},t^{4}]]
or
R\cong\mathbb{C}[[t^{3},t^{5}]].

Since
\gcd(3,4)=1=\gcd(3,5)
, it follows from
[
5
, 5.5]
, or even directly
*
*

*

t^{8}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R_{1}}\setminus\mathfrak{m}^{3}
and
t^{7}\in\mathfrak{C}_{R_{2}}\setminus\mathfrak{m}^{3}
. Thus,
\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})=2
.
, that

\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})=e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)-1.

∎

For any
f\in R
, we define
\operatorname{ord}(f):=\operatorname{ord}(fR)
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Suppose
R
is an affine hypersurface over a field of zero characteristic and of dimension
1
. Then
\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})\leq e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)-1
.

Proof.

Suppose
R=\mathbb{C}[X,Y]/(f)
for some
f\in\mathbb{C}[X,Y]
. Recall that

e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)=\operatorname{ord}(f)

(see
[
11
, Example 11.2.8]
).
Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that

\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})>e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)-1.

Then we have

\mathfrak{C}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)}.

It is known from
[
21
]
, with a contribution to E. Noether, that

\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial X},\frac{\partial f}{\partial Y}\right)\subseteq\mathfrak{C}.

By symmetry, we may assume that
\frac{\partial f}{\partial X}
is nonzero and has order exactly
\operatorname{ord}(f)-1
. Combining these facts, we conclude that

\frac{\partial f}{\partial X}\in\mathfrak{m}^{\operatorname{ord}(f)}.

This leads to a contradiction.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a complete hypersurfacee local domain of dimension
1
and
\mathfrak{C}=\mathfrak{m}^{i}
with
i\leq 3
, then
\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})\geq e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)-1
.

Proof.

We present the case for
i=3
. By the exact sequence

0\to\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^{2}\to R/\mathfrak{m}^{2}\to R/\mathfrak{m}\to 0,

we know that

\ell(R/\mathfrak{m}^{2})=\mu(\mathfrak{m})+1=3.

Since
\mu(\mathfrak{m})=2
, we deduce that
\mu(\mathfrak{m}^{2})\leq 3
. Similarly,

\ell(R/\mathfrak{m}^{3})=\mu(\mathfrak{m}^{2})+\ell(R/\mathfrak{m}^{2})\leq 3+\ell(R/\mathfrak{m}^{2})=6.

Recall from
[
11
, Corollary 12.2.4]
that

12\geq 2\ell(R/\mathfrak{m}^{3})=2\ell(R/\mathfrak{C})=\ell(\overline{R}/\mathfrak{C})=:\ell,

where
\overline{R}
is the integral closure of
R
. Since
R
is complete-local,
\overline{R}
is also local and, moreover, regular. Thus, its maximal ideal is principal, and we denote its uniformizer by
t
. Then

\mathfrak{m}^{3}\overline{R}=t^{\ell}\overline{R}.

Since the extension of ideals is multiplicative, we deduce that

\mathfrak{m}^{3n}\overline{R}=t^{n\ell}\overline{R}.

Now, recall that

e_{\mathfrak{m}}(\overline{R})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\ell(\overline{R}/\mathfrak{m}^{3n}\overline{R})}{3n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\ell(\overline{R}/t^{n\ell}\overline{R})}{3n}=\frac{n\ell}{3n}\leq\frac{12}{3}=4.

Consequently,

e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)=\frac{e_{\mathfrak{m}}(\overline{R})}{\operatorname{rank}\overline{R}}\leq 4=\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{m}^{3n})+1=\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})+1.

∎

The previous remark illustrates a more general phenomena:

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a hypersurfacee local domain of dimension
1
and
\mathfrak{C}=\mathfrak{m}^{n-1}
. Suppose

\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})\leq e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)-1
.
Then
\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})=e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)-1
.

Proof.

We will use Matlis’ step lemma:

\mu(\mathfrak{m}^{i})=\begin{cases}i+1&\text{if }i\leq e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)-1,\\
e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(see
[
13
, Theorem 13.7]
).
By the exact sequence

0\to\mathfrak{m}^{n-2}/\mathfrak{m}^{n-1}\to R/\mathfrak{m}^{n-1}\to R/\mathfrak{m}^{n-2}\to 0,
we know that
\ell(R/\mathfrak{m}^{n-1})=\mu(\mathfrak{m}^{n-2})+\ell(R/\mathfrak{m}^{n-2}).

Thanks to induction, we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\ell(R/\mathfrak{m}^{n-1})&=\mu(\mathfrak{m}^{n-2})+\mu(\mathfrak{m}^{n-3})+\cdots+\mu(\mathfrak{m})+\ell(R/\mathfrak{m})\\
&=(n-1)+(n-2)+\cdots+2+1\\
&=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}.\end{array}

It turns out from the Gorenstein property that

\ell(\overline{R}/\mathfrak{m}^{n-1}\overline{R})=2\ell(R/\mathfrak{m}^{n-1})=n(n-1),
and so

\mathfrak{m}^{k(n-1)}\overline{R}=t^{kn(n-1)}\overline{R}
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}.
Consequently,

e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)=e_{\mathfrak{m}}(\overline{R})=\frac{kn(n-1)}{k(n-1)}=n

and the claim follows.∎

3.2.
A length argument for the first blow-up

We first recall the following fact:

Lemma 3.8
.

(Matlis)
Let
(A,\mathfrak{n},k)
be Artinian local, and let
D(-)=\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(-,E_{A}(k))
.
On finite modules,
D
is exact, preserves length, and the evaluation

N\to D(D(N))
is an isomorphism. Moreover,

\ell(E_{A}(k))=\ell(A),\qquad D(E_{A}(k))\cong A.

If
\dim_{k}\operatorname{soc}(A)=1
, then
E_{A}(k)\cong A
. Consequently, for every
ideal
L\subseteq A
,

(\ast):\quad\ell(A/\operatorname{Ann}_{A}L)=\ell(L),\qquad\operatorname{Ann}_{A}\operatorname{Ann}_{A}L=L.

Proof.

Injectivity of

gives exactness. Its socle is

, so

; a composition series then proves preservation of length and finiteness.
In particular
D(A)=E_{A}(k)
has length
\ell(A)
.
Maps to

separate points of a finite module.
If

, then

, and its image in

is nonzero by Nakayama’s lemma. A map from this quotient to

Thus evaluation is injective, and equality of lengths makes it an
isomorphism. Taking

gives
D(E_{A}(k))\cong A
.
Every nonzero submodule of an Artinian module meets its socle. When

\operatorname{soc}(A)\cong k
, extend an isomorphism of this socle to the socle of

to a map
A\to E_{A}(k)
. Its kernel is zero, and equal lengths
make it an isomorphism. Hence

may be identified with
\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(-,A)
.
Now
D(A/L)=\operatorname{Ann}_{A}L

. Applying it twice and using

∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a one-dimensional Gorenstein local domain with finite
normalization. Suppose a nonzero

and an integer
r\geq 1

(\ast)\quad\mathfrak{m}^{r+1}=x\mathfrak{m}^{r},\qquad 2\ell(R/\mathfrak{m}^{r})=\ell(R/x^{r}R).

Set
T:=x^{-r}\mathfrak{m}^{r}\subseteq\operatorname{Q}(R)
. Then
T
is a finite birational
ring, and

R:T=\mathfrak{m}^{r}=x^{r}T.

Writing
\mathfrak{D}=T:\overline{R}
, one has

\mathfrak{C}=x^{r}\mathfrak{D}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{r},

and
\mathfrak{C}\not\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{r+1}.

In particular
\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})=r
.

Proof.

Induction from the first hypothesis gives

\mathfrak{m}^{r+j}=x^{j}\mathfrak{m}^{r}
for every
j\geq 0
. In particular

\mathfrak{m}^{2r}=x^{r}\mathfrak{m}^{r}
. Since
x^{r}\in\mathfrak{m}^{r}
, the finite module

T=x^{-r}\mathfrak{m}^{r}
contains
R
and is closed under multiplication.
It is finite and birational, hence integral over
R
and contained
in
\overline{R}
.
Let us denote
J=R:T=(x^{r}R):_{R}\mathfrak{m}^{r}
. The equality of powers shows

\mathfrak{m}^{r}\subseteq J
. The Artinian ring
A=R/x^{r}R
is Gorenstein.
Let
L=\mathfrak{m}^{r}/x^{r}R
. Then
J/x^{r}R=\operatorname{Ann}_{A}L
, so
Lemma
3.8
gives

\ell(R/J)=\ell(L)=\ell(R/x^{r}R)-\ell(R/\mathfrak{m}^{r})=\ell(R/\mathfrak{m}^{r}).

Together with
\mathfrak{m}^{r}\subseteq J
, equality of colengths proves

J=\mathfrak{m}^{r}=x^{r}T
.
Recall that
\mathfrak{C}=R:\overline{R}
. If
c\in\mathfrak{C}
and
b\in\overline{R}
, then

cbT\subseteq c\overline{R}\subseteq R
, hence
cb\in J=x^{r}T
.
Thus
(c/x^{r})\overline{R}\subseteq T
, or

.
Conversely, if

, then

x^{r}d\overline{R}\subseteq x^{r}T=J\subseteq R
. This proves

\mathfrak{C}=x^{r}\mathfrak{D}
and
\mathfrak{C}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{r}
.
Suppose
\mathfrak{C}\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{r+1}=x^{r+1}T
. Cancelling

gives

\mathfrak{D}\subseteq xT
. The conductor
\mathfrak{D}
is an ideal of
\overline{R}
,
so

is an
\overline{R}
-stable submodule contained in

.
Every such submodule is contained in

. To see this,
if

\mathfrak{D}\subseteq x\mathfrak{D}

is automatic, so
\mathfrak{D}=x\mathfrak{D}
. The nonzero conductor

is finite over

; Nakayama’s lemma gives the contradiction

∎

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m},k)
be a complete hypersurface domain of dimension
one. Then

\operatorname{ord}(\mathfrak{C})=e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)-1.

Proof.

A complete local domain is excellent, so its normalization is finite;
this is the standard finiteness-of-normalization consequence of
excellence. If
R
is regular, it is a discrete valuation ring, its
conductor is
R
, and the formula is
0=1-1
. Assume henceforth
that
R
is singular. Its hypersurface presentation is

R=S/(f)
, where
(S,\mathfrak{n},k)
is regular local of dimension two,

0\neq f\in\mathfrak{n}^{2}
, and
R
is Gorenstein.

First suppose
k
is infinite. With a regular system of parameters
of
S
, its associated graded ring is
k[X,Y]
. Write

e=\operatorname{ord}_{\mathfrak{n}}(f)
. Since the associated graded ring of
S
is a
domain, orders of products add, and the initial ideal of
(f)
is
generated by the initial form
f_{e}
. Hence

(\ast):\quad\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}R\cong k[X,Y]/(f_{e}),\qquad\dim_{k}\mathfrak{m}^{j}/\mathfrak{m}^{j+1}=\min\{j+1,e\}.

In particular
e=e_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)
.
Choose a linear form
l
not dividing
f_{e}
and lift it to
x\in\mathfrak{m}
.
Such a form exists over an infinite field. Multiplication by
l
is
injective on
k[X,Y]/(f_{e})
, so
x
is a nonzerodivisor and its
initial form is regular. Furthermore

\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}R/(l)\cong k[Z]/(Z^{e}).

Indeed restricting
f_{e}
to the line
l=0
gives a nonzero scalar
multiple of
Z^{e}
. Regularity of the initial form makes the

x
-adic quotient filtration strict: if
xu\in\mathfrak{m}^{j}
then

u\in\mathfrak{m}^{j-1}
. Thus the displayed quotient is the associated
graded ring of
R/xR
, and
\ell(R/xR)=e
.
In degree
e
, the same graded quotient vanishes, yielding

\mathfrak{m}^{e}=x\mathfrak{m}^{e-1}+\mathfrak{m}^{e+1}
. Applying Nakayama to

\mathfrak{m}^{e}/x\mathfrak{m}^{e-1}
gives
\mathfrak{m}^{e}=x\mathfrak{m}^{e-1}
.
Set
r=e-1
. Equation
(\ast)
gives

\ell(R/\mathfrak{m}^{r})=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}(j+1)=\frac{e(e-1)}{2}.

Since
x
is regular, successive quotients of
R/x^{r}R
have
length
\ell(R/xR)=e
, so

\ell(R/x^{r}R)=re=e(e-1)
. Both hypotheses of
Lemma
3.9
hold, proving the theorem for infinite
k
.
Nothing in this argument uses a coefficient field or derivatives.
To remove the infinitude assumption, form the faithfully flat local
extension

R^{\prime}=R[Z]_{\mathfrak{m}R[Z]}
and
k^{\prime}=k(Z).

It is again a one-dimensional local hypersurface domain, with
regular ambient ring
S[Z]_{\mathfrak{n}S[Z]}
, and its residue field is
infinite. Its associated graded ring is the scalar extension of

(\ast)
, so its multiplicity is unchanged.
If
B=\overline{R}
, then
B^{\prime}=B\otimes_{R}R^{\prime}
is finite, birational,
and normal: it is a localization of the normal polynomial ring

B[Z]
. Thus it is the normalization of
R^{\prime}
.

For a finite birational extension,
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}(B,R)
identifies with

R:B
by multiplication in the fraction field. Hom from a finitely
presented module commutes with flat base change. Hence

(R:B)R^{\prime}=R^{\prime}:B^{\prime}.

Faithful flatness detects containment in every
\mathfrak{m}^{j}
, since

\mathfrak{m}^{\prime j}=\mathfrak{m}^{j}R^{\prime}
. Apply the already proved infinite-field argument
to
R^{\prime}
and descend its two conductor containments to
R
.
∎

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a
1
-dimensional complete almost Gorenstein domain, of minimal multiplicity. Suppose

is not hypersurface. Then

Proof.

By the argument of Observation

is not Gorenstein.
In particular,

is not reflexive. We apply duality to the exact sequence

and obtain the exact sequence

Recall from the work of Eilenberg-MacLane that biduality is a functor. This yields the following commutative diagram

\begin{CD}\quad 0\\
\quad @A{}A{}A\\

\quad\quad @A{}A{}A\quad @A{}A{}A\\
\quad\quad 0\quad 0\end{CD}

Now, using the
