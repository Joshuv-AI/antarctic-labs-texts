Early physiological response of Pinus yunnanensis Franch. seedlings to simulated karst rocky desertification stress
Authors: Zhuangyue Lu, Puyan Fu, Yufan Hou, Honglin Wang, Yuji Zhou, Chengxiang Gao, Zilin Cao, Xiaoli Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRocky desertification in the karst regions of southwestern China is characterized by shallow soils, nutrient depletion, and ionic imbalance, which severely restrict vegetation recovery and ecological restoration. Pinus yunnanensis Franch., a native conifer adapted to nutrient-poor karst habitats, has been widely used for ecological restoration. However, its early physiological responses to rocky desertification stress remain poorly understood, particularly the coordination among growth, carbon metabolism, nutrient dynamics, and ion homeostasis. In this study, one-year-old P. yunnanensis seedlings were grown for 60 days in soils collected from Yunnan Province, China, representing a non-karst rocky desertification control (CK) and three rocky desertification levels: light (LRD), moderate (MRD), and severe (SRD). Growth, non-structural carbohydrates (NSC), nutrient stoichiometry, and ion profiles were evaluated. The results indicated that P. yunnanensis responds to rocky desertification stress via coordinated physiological adjustments. Seedling growth was maintained under CK and LRD but was progressively suppressed under MRD and SRD. NSC analysis showed that LRD promoted soluble sugar accumulation and increased the sugar-to-starch ratio, which may enhance metabolic flexibility and stress tolerance. Organ-specific N: P ratios were 2.42 in roots, 0.73 in stems, and 1.04 in needles, with an overall mean of 1.40, indicating nitrogen limitation and pronounced organ-specific nutrient allocation. Under SRD, K⁺ content declined significantly, whereas Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺ accumulated in stems and needles, suggesting dynamic ion regulation under severe stress. Na⁺ content was markedly reduced, indicating restricted ion uptake and transport under degraded substrate conditions. Overall, P. yunnanensis exhibited ecological adaptability under LRD, while MRD and SRD increasingly restricted growth and metabolic coordination. These findings highlight the integrative role of the carbon–nutrient–ion network in early stress adaptation and provide a physiological basis for karst ecosystem restoration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09002-x.Keywords: Pinus yunnanensis Franch., Non-structural carbohydrates, Mineral ions, Rocky desertification, Stoichiometric characteristics

Introduction

Rocky desertification is a widespread form of land degradation in arid and semi-arid karst regions worldwide. It is characterized by intensified soil erosion, increased bedrock exposure, and a significant reduction in vegetation cover [1–3]. This process is typically driven by both natural factors and human disturbances, resulting in the transformation of previously soil- and vegetation-covered karst landscapes into barren rocky terrain [3, 4]. In China, rocky desertification primarily occurs on the Yunnan–Guizhou Plateau and adjacent areas, impacting several provinces, including Yunnan, Guizhou, and Chongqing. The total affected area exceeds 120,000 km², and these regions are ecologically highly vulnerable [3, 5]. Yunnan is characterized by highly developed karst topography and contains one of the largest karst areas in China [2]. Rocky desertification compromises not only the structural and functional integrity of regional ecosystems but also the long-term stability of ecological restoration efforts [6–8].

In karst rocky desertification (KRD) regions, vegetation is exposed to severe edaphic and hydrological constraints. Shallow soils, poor water retention, low organic matter, and ionic imbalances create persistent barriers to plant establishment and growth [9, 10]. These environmental pressures require native species to develop complex physiological adaptations to survive under multiple concurrent stressors [10, 11]. One important adaptive strategy involves the regulation of non-structural carbohydrates (NSC). Functioning as readily mobilizable carbon and energy pools, NSC help maintain metabolic activity during drought or photosynthetic limitation, while also contributing to osmotic regulation, tissue recovery, and the stabilization of cellular energy supply [12, 13]. In rocky desertification environments, NSC mobilization supports short-term physiological resilience and may also contribute to long-term survival. Ecological stoichiometry, particularly the balance of carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and phosphorus (P), provides important insights into plant adaptation at the biochemical level [14–16]. The C: N:P ratio reflects internal nutrient allocation and links plant metabolism with environmental nutrient availability. Ecological stoichiometry has generated important theoretical advances, including the “limiting element stability hypothesis” and the use of plant N: P ratios to identify nutrient limitation [15, 17]. Previous studies have extensively examined nutrient contents and ecological stoichiometry in soils and plants across different ecosystems. In non-karst areas, such as the Loess Plateau [18, 19] and degraded alpine grasslands [20, 21], research has mainly focused on soil and plant C, N, and P stoichiometry, as well as the associated nutrient limitations. In karst regions of Southwest China, where nutrient limitation is severe, understanding stoichiometric regulation is essential for evaluating plant resource-use efficiency and stress tolerance [22, 23]. Mineral ions such as potassium (K⁺), calcium (Ca²⁺), sodium (Na⁺), and magnesium (Mg²⁺) are also critical for osmotic regulation and enzymatic activity. Karst soils typically exhibit low bioavailability of essential ions and high Na⁺ levels, which disrupt ionic homeostasis and impair physiological function [24, 25]. In karst regions undergoing rocky desertification, plants often rely on selective ion uptake, transporter regulation, and inter-organ redistribution to maintain mineral ion homeostasis, thereby enhancing nutrient-use efficiency under nutrient-poor conditions [26]. However, under severe rocky desertification stress, these regulatory mechanisms may be substantially impaired, particularly the uptake efficiency of K⁺ and Ca²⁺, which become critical limiting factors for plant growth and stress resistance [22]. Despite advances in understanding these mechanisms individually [12, 24, 27], an integrative view of how carbon allocation, nutrient stoichiometry, and ion regulation interact under combined environmental stress remains lacking. Therefore, this study employed a three-dimensional analytical framework to investigate the physiological adaptation mechanisms of seedlings under simulated KRD conditions. This approach was intended to reveal synergistic strategies by which seedlings optimize internal resource use and maintain physiological function under extreme ecological stress.

Pinus yunnanensis Franch., an evergreen conifer in the family Pinaceae, is native to the Yunnan–Guizhou Plateau and is mainly distributed in Yunnan Province, western Guizhou, and southwestern Sichuan, where the climate is strongly influenced by the southwestern monsoon [28–30]. This species shows strong ecological adaptability and tolerance to drought and nutrient-poor soils. For decades, it has been widely used as both a pioneer species and a major timber tree for afforestation on barren land in the karst regions of southwestern China [31]. In the karst landscapes of Yunnan Province, P. yunnanensis functions as a dominant conifer species with strong natural regenerative capacity and substantial importance for forest restoration and vegetation reestablishment [32, 33]. Despite its broad cultivation and considerable restoration potential in rocky desertification areas, systematic studies on the early physiological responses of P. yunnanensis seedlings to different degrees of rocky desertification stress remain limited. Previous studies have mainly focused on provenance comparisons [34, 35] and silvicultural management [28, 36], whereas the coordinated regulation of growth, carbon metabolism, nutrient balance, and ion homeostasis during early seedling development under rocky desertification stress remains poorly understood. In particular, under the dual constraints of habitat degradation and resource limitation, the mechanisms by which P. yunnanensis seedlings maintain stress adaptation through carbon–nutrient–ion coordination remain unclear. Therefore, this study investigated how increasing KRD intensity affects the early growth and physiological regulation of P. yunnanensis seedlings. Specifically, we examined seedling growth, C:N: P stoichiometry, NSC accumulation, and mineral ion profiles during early development. We hypothesized that: (1) increasing rocky desertification intensity would progressively suppress seedling growth and disturb physiological homeostasis; (2) P. yunnanensis seedlings would respond to stress through coordinated adjustments in carbon reserves, nutrient stoichiometry, and ion balance; and (3) seedlings exposed to LRD would maintain relative physiological stability, whereas those under MRD and SRD would exhibit stronger metabolic disturbance and growth inhibition. This study aimed to clarify the early physiological responses of P. yunnanensis seedlings to simulated KRD stress and to provide a theoretical basis for native species selection, ecological restoration, and the development of stress-resilient germplasm in karst ecosystems.

Materials and methods

Study area

The experiment was conducted in a greenhouse at Southwest Forestry University, located in Kunming, Yunnan Province, China (102°45′41″ E, 25°04′00″ N), at an elevation of approximately 1,945 m above sea level. The site is characterized by a subtropical plateau monsoon climate. The mean annual temperature is 14.7 °C, with an absolute maximum of 32.5 °C and an absolute minimum of -9.0 °C. The average annual relative humidity is 68.2%, and annual precipitation ranges from 700 to 1,100 mm, with the most rainfall occurring from May to October. The maximum monthly rainfall is 208.3 mm, and the maximum daily rainfall is 153.3 mm. The region receives an average of 2,445.6 h of sunshine annually, with a sunshine percentage of 56%. The annual total solar radiation is approximately 543.4 MJ·m⁻², and the mean annual evaporation is 1,856.4 mm [31].

Soil sample collection

Soil samples were collected from Lingjiao Township, Zhanyi District, Qujing City, Yunnan Province, China (103°29′08″ E, 25°31′16″ N), at an elevation of approximately 2,000 m above sea level. The region has a mean annual temperature of 14.5 °C and annual precipitation ranging from 800 to 1,300 mm. The soil type was classified as red soil [37]. Rocky desertification in karst areas is characterized by progressive loss of soil and vegetation cover and increasing bedrock exposure, which collectively reduce habitat quality [38–40]. In the present study, rocky desertification severity was classified using three key indicators: slope, bare rock fraction, and fractional vegetation cover [41, 42]. The criteria used to classify the non-karst rocky desertification control (CK), light rocky desertification (LRD), moderate rocky desertification (MRD), and severe rocky desertification (SRD) treatments are listed in Table 1.

In May 2023, three fixed plots (10 m × 10 m) were established at representative bare-land sites for each of the four rocky desertification levels, namely CK, LRD, MRD, and SRD [3, 43]. In each plot, approximately 3 cm of surface soil was removed before soil samples were collected in layers from the 0–30 cm profile. The collected soils were subsequently used as growth substrates in the pot experiment to preserve the edaphic characteristics associated with different rocky desertification intensities while reducing interference from uncontrolled field factors. This approach allowed the early physiological responses of P. yunnanensis seedlings to be evaluated under relatively uniform conditions. For each rocky desertification level, samples from the three plots were combined, thoroughly homogenized, and transported to the laboratory for the potted seedling experiments using P. yunnanensis. Undisturbed soil samples were collected using a ring cutter (internal diameter: 5.04 cm; height: 5.00 cm) and analyzed for physicochemical properties of the soils (Table 2).

Plant material source

The experimental materials consisted of one-year-old P. yunnanensis seedlings with uniform growth that had been previously cultivated by the research team. The seeds were sourced from Leye County, Guangxi Province, China (accession number: LY2-1). All samples were collected with the approval of the local government. Species identification was performed by Prof. Mou Fengjuan, a plant taxonomist at Southwest Forestry University, based on the morphological classification of P. yunnanensis. All specimens were deposited in the Herbarium of Southwest Forestry University (acronym: SWFU), pending formal accession numbering. In May 2023, equal volumes of soil representing four rocky desertification levels (CK, LRD, MRD, and SRD) were placed into plastic containers (top diameter: 20.60 cm; bottom diameter: 15.00 cm; height: 17.50 cm). 60 containers were prepared for each treatment. A total of 240 healthy P. yunnanensis seedlings of uniform height were individually transplanted into the containers, each fitted with a tray. During the initial transplanting phase, soil moisture was manually adjusted to maintain field capacity and ensure successful seedling establishment. After seedling establishment, soil moisture was regulated within different field capacity (FC) ranges to simulate rocky desertification stress: 15–20% for CK, 10–15% for LRD, 5–10% for MRD, and 0–5% for SRD. These treatments were consistent with established drought-simulation protocols and reflected the typical water-limited conditions of karst regions [44, 45]. Soil moisture was monitored regularly using a soil moisture meter in combination with the gravimetric method. To minimize the potential influence of sampling disturbance on seedling growth, seedlings used for physiological, nutrient, and mineral-ion analyses at each sampling time were independent destructive samples and were not used for subsequent growth monitoring. Seedlings selected for dynamic growth measurements were monitored non-destructively for height and basal diameter throughout the experiment. This sampling design allowed temporal physiological and stoichiometric responses to be evaluated while avoiding cumulative damage to the same individuals. The experiment was conducted for 60 days, from July to September 2023. Greenhouse temperature (18.5–45.0 °C) and relative humidity (22.3–48.0%) were allowed to fluctuate naturally with day–night variation. This design enabled an assessment of seedling responses to different rocky desertification stress intensities under controlled pot conditions.

Morphological trait assessment

In July 2023, baseline morphological measurements were taken for all P. yunnanensis seedlings included in the experiment. Seedling height was measured using a ruler (precision: 1.0 mm), and basal diameter was measured using a digital caliper (precision: 0.02 mm). These values were recorded as baseline data for the experiment. After the simulated rocky desertification experiment began, seedling height and basal diameter were measured on days 15, 30, 45, and 60. Once soil moisture in each treatment reached the target range corresponding to its rocky desertification level, seedling height and basal diameter were measured every 15 days to ensure data continuity and accuracy.

Determination of non-structural carbohydrates, nutrient elements, and mineral ions

At each sampling time point (15, 30, 45, and 60 days after treatment), four seedlings per treatment were destructively harvested and separated into roots, stems, and needles for physiological and chemical analyses. Organ samples were heat-inactivated, oven-dried, ground into powder, passed through a 40-mesh sieve, sealed, and stored until analysis. Four biological replicates were included for each treatment at each sampling stage, resulting in a total of 64 destructively sampled seedlings across the four sampling stages. For each organ and treatment, 0.100 g of dry powder was used to determine NSC content using the phenol–sulfuric acid colorimetric method [46]. NSC content was defined as the sum of soluble sugar and starch contents. For nutrient-element analysis, 0.020 g of dry powder was used to determine C, N, and P concent using heated potassium dichromate redox titration [47], Nessler’s reagent colorimetry [48], and the vanadomolybdate yellow colorimetric method [49], respectively. For mineral-ion analysis, 0.020 g of dry tissue powder was used to determine K⁺, Ca²⁺, Na⁺, and Mg²⁺ concent by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) according to the Chinese National Standard GB/T 26416.3–2022.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to assess differences in the growth parameters of P. yunnanensis seedlings, followed by Duncan’s multiple range test to determine significant differences among treatments (p < 0.05). Duncan’s multiple range test was also used to evaluate significant differences in NSC, nutrient element, and mineral ion concentrations of P. yunnanensis seedlings across rocky desertification levels. Principal component analysis (PCA) and Pearson correlation analysis were used to evaluate coordinated relationships among growth, physiological, stoichiometric, and mineral ion traits. In this study, structural equation modeling (SEM) was performed using the partial least squares (PLS) method. Figures were generated using Microsoft Excel 2021 and Origin 2021. All data were presented as mean ± standard error (SE).

Results

Effects of rocky desertification intensity on the growth characteristics of P. yunnanensis seedlings

Rocky desertification intensity significantly affected the growth of P. yunnanensis seedlings (Fig. 1, p < 0.05). Seedling height increased over time under all treatments, with the greatest increase observed under CK and LRD (Fig. 1A). At 60 days, seedling height increased by 6.72 cm under CK and 7.59 cm under LRD, both of which were significantly greater than the increases observed under MRD (3.47 cm) and SRD (0.99 cm). The persistently low height increment under SRD indicates that severe rocky desertification significantly inhibits vertical growth. Basal diameter showed a similar response pattern (Fig. 1B). At 60 days, basal diameter increments reached 3.14 mm under CK and 4.31 mm under LRD. These values were significantly higher than those recorded under MRD (1.30 mm) and SRD (0.26 mm). Although early differences in basal diameter between LRD and MRD were limited, LRD showed a clear growth advantage at later stages.

Effects of rocky desertification intensity on non-structural carbohydrate content in P. yunnanensis seedlings

Rocky desertification intensity, stress duration, and their interaction significantly affected most NSC-related traits in the roots, stems, and needles of P. yunnanensis seedlings (Table S1, p < 0.05). Soluble sugar content showed the strongest response to stress duration in roots (F = 57.00), followed by needles. Starch content was also significantly affected, with marked variation in roots and stems, whereas needles showed greater sensitivity to stress intensity. Total NSC content showed a strong response to stress duration in roots (F = 52.12), while needles showed a significant response to the interaction between stress duration and stress intensity. The sugar-to-starch ratio also showed significant variation in needles (F = 45.07).

Different KRD intensities significantly affected soluble sugar, starch, total NSC content, and sugar-to-starch ratio (Fig. 2, p < 0.05). Soluble sugar content showed an initial increase followed by a decline across all treatments, with greater accumulation in roots, stems, and needles under LRD. At 45 days, soluble sugar content under LRD reached peak values in stems (118.34 mg·g⁻¹) and needles (123.50 mg·g⁻¹), both of which were significantly higher than those in roots, indicating preferential accumulation of soluble sugars in aboveground organs (Fig. 2A). Under SRD and MRD, starch content initially increased and then declined, with significant decrease during the later stage of stress (45–60 days). In contrast, starch levels under CK and LRD fluctuated only slightly, and needle starch content remained within 56.37–82.02 mg·g⁻¹, indicating that mild stress caused only limited disturbance to carbon reserves (Fig. 2B). Under LRD and MRD, NSC contents in stems and needles remained relatively high, with needle NSC reaching a peak at 60 days (197.85 mg·g⁻¹). In contrast, NSC content under SRD was generally lower than that under the other treatments, reflecting a reduced capacity to maintain carbon reserves under severe rocky desertification. Root NSC content showed a transient increase at 30 days and was significantly higher than at the other time points, suggesting that NSC regulation may exhibit organ-specific patterns (Fig. 2C). The sugar-to-starch ratio was generally higher under stress treatments than under CK, and the highest values under CK and LRD were observed at 45 days. At most time points, the sugar-to-starch ratio under LRD was higher than that under the other treatments, indicating that carbon allocation under mild rocky desertification stress was biased toward the soluble sugar form. In contrast, the sugar-to-starch ratio under SRD showed an initial decline followed by an increase and reached its maximum at 60 days, indicating that the interconversion between soluble sugars and starch was dynamically adjusted under severe stress (Fig. 2D). P. yunnanensis seedlings exhibited organ-specific patterns of carbohydrate response under different stress conditions.

Effects of rocky desertification intensity on C:N:P stoichiometric characteristics of P. yunnanensis seedlings

C:N:P stoichiometric responses were evaluated by comparing nutrient-element concent and stoichiometric ratios in the roots, stems, and needles of P. yunnanensis seedlings under different KRD intensities (Fig. 3). Rocky desertification intensity significantly influenced the C: N:P stoichiometric traits of P. yunnanensis seedlings (p < 0.05). Throughout the treatment period, carbon content remained relatively high in all organs, with roots showing greater sensitivity to stress intensity. From 30 to 60 days, root carbon concent were significantly higher under LRD and MRD than under CK and SRD. At 60 days, carbon contents in roots and stems under LRD exceeded 360 mg·g⁻¹, whereas those under SRD declined significantly to below 300 mg·g⁻¹ (Fig. 3A). Differences in nitrogen content among organs were relatively small, but nitrogen concentration was significantly affected by rocky desertification stress. Nitrogen concent were generally higher under CK and LRD, whereas SRD significantly reduced nitrogen concent in all organs by 60 days (Fig. 3B). Phosphorus content showed clear organ-specific differences and were generally higher in needles and stems than in roots. Needle phosphorus concent remained relatively high under CK and LRD. In contrast, SRD markedly reduced needle phosphorus concent (Fig. 3C). In terms of stoichiometric ratios, stem C: N was significantly higher than that in roots and needles. The C: N ratio under SRD and MRD increased significantly and showed greater variation during the later treatment stage, whereas that under CK and LRD remained relatively stable and generally lower (Fig. 3D). Root C: P was significantly higher than that in stems and needles, and under most treatments, stem and needle C: P ratios reached peak values at 15 days (approximately 37.2–63.5) (Fig. 3E). Root N: P was consistently higher than stem and needle N: P across treatments. Under stress treatments, root N: P generally peaked at 30 days. Needle N: P peaked during the early treatment stage and was higher under MRD and SRD than under CK and LRD. Stem N: P remained consistently low throughout the experiment (Fig. 3F). Across the entire experiment, the mean N: P ratios were 2.42 in roots, 0.73 in stems, and 1.04 in needles, with an overall mean of 1.40, indicating pronounced organ-specific differences in nutrient allocation and suggesting a tendency toward nitrogen limitation in P. yunnanensis seedlings.

Effects of rocky desertification intensity on mineral ion content in P. yunnanensis seedlings

Mineral ion responses were evaluated by comparing K⁺, Ca²⁺, Na⁺, and Mg²⁺ concent in roots, stems, and needles under different KRD intensities (Fig. 4). Different degrees of rocky desertification had significant effects on mineral ion contents in P. yunnanensis seedlings (p < 0.05). For K⁺, roots under CK and LRD maintained relatively high and stable contents, remaining above approximately 4.1 mg·g⁻¹ at 60 days, which were significantly higher than those under MRD and SRD. Root K⁺ content under MRD and SRD decreased markedly over time, with a sharper decline after 30 days, and had fallen to approximately 50% of the CK level by 60 days. Stem K⁺ content generally declined over time, and the differences among treatments became more pronounced as the experiment progressed. Needle K⁺ content was generally high at 15 days but declined markedly under LRD and SRD from 30 to 45 days. By 60 days, needle K⁺ content under CK and MRD had recovered to 8.00 and 7.84 mg·g⁻¹, respectively, whereas that under LRD and SRD remained markedly lower (Fig. 4A). Ca²⁺ concent showed clear organ-specific and temporal variation. In roots, Ca²⁺ content was generally higher under CK and LRD than under MRD and SRD. Under LRD, it peaked at 30 days (2.87 mg·g⁻¹) and remained relatively high during the later stage of treatment. Root Ca²⁺ concent was lower and more variable under MRD and SRD. Needle Ca²⁺ concent changed more markedly with increasing stress duration. Under SRD, needle Ca²⁺ concent increased markedly by 60 days (Fig. 4B). Root Na⁺ concent remained higher under CK but decreased under rocky desertification stress, particularly under SRD by 60 days. Stem Na⁺ content varied only slightly, with no significant differences among treatments. Under CK, needle Na⁺ content gradually increased over time. In contrast, needle Na⁺ concent remained lower under SRD than under CK during the middle treatment stage. These results indicate that Na⁺ accumulation in roots and needles was inhibited under rocky desertification stress, which may be associated with the regulation of ion uptake and transport (Fig. 4C). Mg²⁺ concent showed clear temporal and treatment-dependent variation across organs. Root Mg²⁺ concent tended to increase under MRD by the end of the experiment. Stem Mg²⁺ concent increased under MRD and remained relatively high under SRD during the middle-to-late treatment stages. Needle Mg²⁺ concent increased under SRD during the early stage and peaked under LRD and MRD during the middle-to-late stages. By 60 days, Mg²⁺ content had become relatively stable. Overall, Mg²⁺ showed clear temporal responses and treatment differences, suggesting that it may be redistributed among organs under stress to maintain metabolism-related ionic supply (Fig. 4D).

Relationships among growth, nutrient status, non-structural carbohydrates, and mineral ion traits in P. yunnanensis seedlings

Principal component analysis showed treatment-related separation for NSC components, C, N, and P contents and stoichiometric ratios, and mineral ion traits in the two-dimensional principal component space (Fig. 5A–C). In the PCA of NSC components, the first two principal components explained 74.0% of the total variance (PC1 = 46.8% and PC2 = 27.2%). The separation among treatments was mainly associated with organ-specific variation in soluble sugars, starch, total NSC, and the sugar-to-starch ratio. These variables showed high loadings on the principal component axes, indicating that carbon storage and soluble sugar–starch conversion contributed strongly to treatment separation (Fig. 5A). In the PCA of stoichiometric traits, the first two principal components explained 44.1% of the total variance (PC1 = 28.1% and PC2 = 16.0%). Treatment separation was mainly driven by organ C, N, and P concent and their stoichiometric ratios. In particular, C:N, C:P, and N: P ratios contributed to the differentiation of nutrient-balance patterns among treatments (Fig. 5B). In the PCA of mineral ions, the first two principal components explained 44.9% of the total variance (PC1 = 28.7% and PC2 = 16.2%). CK and LRD were clearly separated from SRD, indicating distinct mineral ion regulation patterns between mild and severe rocky desertification stress. K⁺- and Na⁺-related variables contributed more strongly to PC1, whereas Ca²⁺- and Mg²⁺-related variables contributed more prominently to PC2, suggesting that K⁺/Na⁺ balance and divalent-cation regulation were the main drivers of separation in the mineral ion PCA (Fig. 5C).

Pearson correlation heatmaps and network analysis further showed coordinated relationships among the measured variables (Fig. 6). Within the NSC module, soluble sugars, starch, and NSC contents in different organs were generally positively correlated at highly significant levels. In the stoichiometric module, significant correlations were observed among N and P contents and stoichiometric ratios. In the mineral ion module, K⁺ in roots, stems, and needles was predominantly negatively correlated with soluble sugar and starch contents and their related ratios. Mg²⁺ concent also showed negative correlations with NSC-related traits. Ca²⁺ concent were negatively correlated with N- and P-related variables. Na⁺ concent were positively correlated with N and P concent. The network structure further showed that, with increasing rocky desertification intensity from CK to SRD, positive correlations within the system became stronger, and a dense positive correlation network formed, particularly under SRD. This suggests that under stress conditions, different functional traits within seedlings may help maintain system stability through coordinated regulatory mechanisms.

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to clarify the pathways through which treatment-driven changes in soil properties were associated with seedling physiological regulation and growth (Fig. 7). The model showed an acceptable fit, with a goodness of fit (GOF) value of 0.49. The explained variance of the composite variable modules showed that mineral ions (R² = 0.80) and plant growth (R² = 0.72) had relatively high explanatory power, followed by stoichiometric properties (R² = 0.62), whereas soil properties (R² = 0.10) and non-structural carbohydrates (R² = 0.10) had lower explanatory power. In terms of path coefficients, soil properties showed a strong positive association with mineral ion traits (β = 0.90, p < 0.001) and a significant negative association with stoichiometric properties (β = −0.55, p < 0.01). Stoichiometric properties were negatively associated with NSCs (β = −0.50, p < 0.05), whereas NSCs showed the strongest positive direct association with plant growth (β = 0.65, p < 0.001). These results indicate that treatment-driven changes in soil conditions were associated with seedling growth through integrated mineral ion, stoichiometric, and carbon-regulatory pathways (Fig. 7A). Effect decomposition further showed that both soil properties and non-structural carbohydrates had positive direct effects on plant growth, with soil properties showing the stronger promotive effect. By contrast, stoichiometric properties and mineral ions showed negative direct effects on plant growth, with the inhibitory effect of stoichiometric properties being more pronounced. Meanwhile, soil properties and mineral ions had positive indirect effects on plant growth, indicating that both could further promote plant growth by regulating other mediating variables. In contrast, stoichiometric properties showed a negative indirect effect, whereas the indirect effect of non-structural carbohydrates was relatively weak, suggesting that the latter influenced plant growth mainly through direct effects (Fig. 7B). P. yunnanensis seedlings exhibited a highly coordinated physiological response to simulated KRD stress.

Discussion

Morphological adaptation strategies of P. yunnanensis to rocky desertification stress

Rocky desertification represents an extreme form of ecological degradation in karst regions because it simultaneously alters soil structure, reduces water-holding capacity, depletes organic matter, and constrains nutrient availability, thereby weakening the environmental basis required for plant establishment and forest recovery [50]. Under such conditions, seedling height and basal diameter provide integrative indicators of early structural development and stress response. In the present study, P. yunnanensis maintained relatively stable increases in seedling height and basal diameter under CK and LRD, whereas growth was progressively restricted under MRD and SRD. This pattern suggests that mild rocky desertification remained within the physiological buffering range of the species, whereas moderate and severe rocky desertification exceeded this range and constrained structural development. The relatively favorable growth under LRD indicates that P. yunnanensis seedlings have a degree of early-stage plasticity in response to habitat deterioration. Such a response may reflect a mild-stress compensation effect, in which plants maintain or even transiently enhance growth by optimizing internal resource allocation and preserving metabolic activity under sublethal stress [51, 52]. Basal diameter differed little among treatments during the early stage, but treatment differences became more pronounced as stress duration increased. After 45 days, seedlings under LRD exhibited significantly greater basal diameter than those under MRD and SRD. This delayed divergence may be related to carbon reallocation, hormonal regulation, and reinforcement of mechanical tissues under mild to moderate stress, which can support structural integrity and water transport during later development [47, 53–55]. In contrast, SRD markedly suppressed both height and basal diameter increments. Under severe rocky desertification, limited soil moisture and nutrient availability, together with ionic imbalance associated with Na⁺ accumulation, may hinder cell division and expansion in seedlings [56, 57]. Previous studies have demonstrated that plants often experience a trade-off between growth and survival under stress conditions [10]. Under CK and LRD, sustained seedling height and basal diameter growth may have been supported by relatively stable carbon reserves and physiological regulation, enabling seedlings to respond to environmental variation [58]. In contrast, growth inhibition under MRD and SRD suggests that seedling performance may decline once KRD stress exceeds a physiological tolerance threshold [57]. P. yunnanensis appears to exhibit substantial phenotypic plasticity under light to moderate rocky desertification conditions, making it a promising candidate species for afforestation in these environments. However, its morphological development is significantly constrained under severely degraded conditions [58]. Therefore, future afforestation efforts should consider the effects of stress intensity on seedling morphogenesis in order to optimize site selection and implement appropriate silvicultural strategies.

Effects of different rocky desertification levels on non-structural carbohydrate contents in P. yunnanensis seedlings

Non-structural carbohydrates, mainly soluble sugars and starch, are important carbon reserves and metabolic buffers under abiotic stress [13, 59, 60]. By supporting respiration, osmotic adjustment, and rapid metabolic regulation, NSC are pivotal for preserving physiological stability when external resource supply becomes limited [10, 13]. In KRD ecosystems, where soils are nutrient-poor and water availability is limited, plants must adopt flexible carbon allocation strategies to maintain metabolic homeostasis and improve environmental adaptability [61]. Therefore, investigating the dynamic accumulation and allocation of NSC can provide important insights into the physiological mechanisms underlying plant adaptation to rocky desertification stress [61]. In the present study, P. yunnanensis seedlings exhibited pronounced organ-specific NSC responses under different levels of rocky desertification, indicating that carbon allocation was actively adjusted according to stress intensity. Under LRD, soluble sugars increased and peaked in stems and needles at 45 days, suggesting a shift toward soluble carbon forms that may support osmotic adjustment and stress signaling. This pattern is consistent with previous findings showing that moderate stress promotes the allocation of NSC to rapidly mobilizable forms, such as soluble sugars, thereby facilitating short-term physiological adaptation [60, 62]. Starch content remained relatively stable under CK and LRD but declined significantly during the late-stage of MRD and SRD, suggesting that high-intensity stress may inhibit starch biosynthesis or accelerate starch hydrolysis, thereby intensifying carbon depletion. Under SRD, NSC levels declined markedly across multiple organs, indicating that prolonged and severe rocky desertification stress substantially impaired carbon assimilation, ultimately limiting energy reserves and constraining seedling growth potential [10]. Variations in the sugar-to-starch ratio further highlight the dynamic regulation of carbon allocation strategies [63]. Under LRD, the sugar-to-starch ratio peaked during the mid-stress phase, indicating that light stress promoted a shift in carbon allocation toward soluble sugars. This shift may enhance metabolic flexibility and improve stress resilience in seedlings. In contrast, under SRD, the sugar-to-starch ratio initially declined and then increased, indicating restricted soluble sugar accumulation during the early stage of stress. The subsequent rebound may reflect a compensatory mechanism that restores carbon reserves and sustains minimal metabolic activity under prolonged stress. As the primary photosynthetic organ, needles showed high sensitivity to NSC dynamics under stress. The early increase in the sugar-to-starch ratio, together with reduced starch content, suggests that needles may participate in early carbon adjustment under stress [10]. Root NSC also fluctuated markedly, indicating that roots were important sites of carbon regulation related to water and nutrient acquisition. Given their direct exposure to substrate deterioration, NSC accumulation and redistribution in roots are likely essential for maintaining physiological activity in P. yunnanensis under adverse conditions [64]. Similar root-centered NSC responses under stress have been reported in woody plants, in which carbohydrate depletion or redistribution in roots is closely associated with drought resistance and survival processes [64]. In addition, previous studies in woody plants have shown that NSC regulation under environmental stress involves source–sink reconfiguration across organs, resource reallocation, and the activation of sugar metabolic pathways, with these responses being strongly dependent on stress intensity [65]. In summary, P. yunnanensis seedlings exhibited a multilayered regulatory strategy of NSC in response to different intensities of rocky desertification. Light stress enhanced tolerance by promoting the accumulation of soluble sugars and elevating the sugar-to-starch ratio, whereas severe stress markedly impaired carbon assimilation and energy distribution, thereby restricting seedling growth and survival. The dynamic shifts in NSC levels and the functional divergence among organs underscore the strategic physiological adaptation of this species to complex environmental stress [64, 65]. These findings help explain how carbon-reserve regulation contributes to early seedling adaptation in degraded karst habitats.

Nutrient accumulation and C: N:P stoichiometric responses of P. yunnanensis seedlings under different levels of rocky desertification

C: N:P stoichiometric characteristics are important indicators of plant physiological status, nutrient acquisition strategies, and ecological adaptability [66]. In KRD habitats, limited water and nutrient availability impose strong constraints on seedling growth and nutrient regulation [67]. Therefore, elucidating the C: N:P stoichiometric characteristics across different desertification intensities is essential for understanding plant nutrient regulation mechanisms and guiding regional ecological restoration strategies. In the present study, C, N, and P concent and their stoichiometric ratios varied markedly among roots, stems, and needles across KRD intensities, indicating stress-dependent nutrient regulation. Carbon content remained relatively high in all organs, but greater accumulation in roots and stems under LRD and MRD than under CK and SRD suggests that moderate stress may promote carbon retention to maintain metabolic activity and structural stability [68]. C content was significantly reduced under SRD, likely because of impaired photosynthetic carbon assimilation, which limited both the accumulation and translocation of carbon resources [69]. Similarly, N content varied significantly among treatments. Seedlings under LRD maintained relatively high N levels across organs, suggesting that light stress did not substantially impair nitrogen uptake at the root-soil interface. In contrast, N content in roots, stems, and needles declined markedly under SRD, indicating that increasing stress intensity inhibited nitrogen absorption and translocation. Similar stoichiometric responses have been reported in plantation species growing in karst ecosystems, where nutrient limitation becomes increasingly pronounced with habitat degradation [70]. Phosphorus showed more pronounced inter-organ variation. Its concentration was generally higher in needles and stems than in roots, reflecting functional differentiation among organs. Under SRD, P content declined significantly, suggesting that severe rocky desertification may reduce soil P availability or impair root uptake efficiency [71].

The C: N and C: P ratios serve as key ecological indicators of carbon–nutrient balance, and their dynamic variation reflects the metabolic adaptation strategies of seedlings under nutrient stress [72]. In the later stages under SRD and MRD, the C: N ratio increased significantly, indicating relative carbon accumulation and nitrogen deficiency, which together formed a “high-carbon, low-nitrogen” resource profile. This metabolic imbalance may hinder the synthesis of enzymatic proteins and structural compounds, ultimately restricting plant growth [73]. The C: P ratio peaked during the early stage of treatment and subsequently stabilized, suggesting that plants are highly sensitive to phosphorus during initial stress and respond rapidly. Dynamic changes in the N: P ratio further reflect the coordination between nitrogen and phosphorus uptake. The highest root N: P ratio was observed under LRD, indicating enhanced nitrogen uptake under light stress, which contributes to nutrient balance. In contrast, the N: P ratio decreased significantly under SRD, implying restricted phosphorus uptake, which may impair energy metabolism and nucleic acid synthesis [16]. This study found that the needle N: P ratio fluctuated markedly across treatments, with a peak observed under SRD as early as day 15. This suggests that needles exhibit greater sensitivity to stress during the early stage, with nitrogen being preferentially allocated to support photosynthetic processes. This is consistent with the shoot-priority response hypothesis, which proposes that plants under early stress preferentially maintain photosynthetic structures and metabolic activity [74]. It is generally accepted that when N: P < 14, N is the primary limiting element for plant growth; when N: P > 16, P is the primary limiting element; and when 14 ≤ N: P ≤ 16, both N and P are limiting factors for plant growth [75]. In this study, the overall mean N: P ratio was 1.40, indicating that the growth of P. yunnanensis was primarily limited by nitrogen, with a more pronounced limitation than that reported in previous studies [31, 76]. The observed variations in elemental concentrations across rocky desertification levels indicate significant differences in nutrient distribution among plant organs, potentially reflecting organ-specific nutrient allocation strategies [31]. Comprehensive analysis revealed that P. yunnanensis exhibits distinct organ-specific nutrient responses under different levels of rocky desertification stress. Under LRD, seedlings maintained relatively stable C: N:P balance, supporting metabolic stability under mild stress. In contrast, MRD and SRD disrupted carbon–nutrient coordination, resulting in stoichiometric imbalance and growth limitation.

Mineral ion accumulation and ionic homeostasis in P. yunnanensis under rocky desertification stress

In KRD habitats, nutrient-poor soils, uneven precipitation, and limited ion exchange capacity severely constrain the uptake and regulation of mineral elements in plants, thereby disrupting growth and metabolic homeostasis [57, 77]. Plants may maintain physiological function under stress by adjusting ion accumulation across organs and preserving ionic homeostasis [78, 79]. In the present study, mineral ion responses of P. yunnanensis seedlings differed among organs and KRD intensities. K⁺, a key ion for osmotic regulation and stomatal control, remained stable under LRD but declined significantly under SRD, particularly in roots and stems. These results suggest that severe stress may inhibit K⁺ uptake at the root–soil interface or disrupt its transmembrane transport, thereby impairing osmotic adjustment and associated carbon metabolism [79, 80]. In contrast, under MRD, K⁺ levels remained relatively high at certain stages, potentially due to the activation of compensatory uptake mechanisms or specific ion transporters [57]. Ca²⁺ is essential for cellular signal transduction, membrane stabilization, and the regulation of stress responses [81, 82]. Under SRD, Ca²⁺ concent increased significantly in the roots and needles, with needle Ca²⁺ levels peaking during the later stages of stress. This pattern may indicate the involvement of Ca²⁺-mediated signaling in the response to severe rocky desertification stress [81, 82]. Under MRD, Ca²⁺ homeostasis was effectively maintained, suggesting that moderate stress may promote Ca²⁺ uptake and redistribution, thereby enhancing stress tolerance. Although Na⁺ is a non-essential element, it must be tightly regulated in plants to prevent potential cytotoxicity. Under SRD, Na⁺ accumulation was significantly inhibited in all organs, with the greatest reduction observed in roots. This indicates that severe stress may impair Na⁺ transmembrane transport or selective uptake at the cellular level, helping to mitigate ion toxicity [57]. In addition, Na⁺ content in needles gradually increased over time under CK, while remaining consistently low under SRD. This indicates that P. yunnanensis may actively restrict Na⁺ translocation to leaves in order to protect photosynthetic tissues. Mg²⁺, as a core component of chlorophyll, is essential for photosynthesis and energy transfer [83]. In this study, Mg²⁺ concent in stems and needles increased under MRD and SRD, with relatively high levels maintained under SRD during the later treatment stages. These findings indicate that enhanced Mg²⁺ accumulation may sustain photosynthetic activity and cellular metabolism under prolonged stress [84, 85]. Moreover, root Mg²⁺ concent gradually increased under MRD, indicating a stress-induced compensatory regulatory mechanism. This adjustment may stabilize root metabolism and improve resource uptake efficiency [85]. Overall, P. yunnanensis exhibited distinct organ-specific strategies for regulating mineral ions under different levels of rocky desertification stress. Under moderate stress, seedlings maintained relatively stable K⁺, Mg²⁺, and Ca²⁺ regulation. In contrast, severe rocky desertification disrupted K⁺, Na⁺, and Ca²⁺ balance, which may have weakened ionic homeostasis and growth maintenance.

Effects of rocky desertification intensity on the interrelationships among physiological traits in P. yunnanensis seedlings

Early seedling adaptation to KRD stress depends on coordinated regulation of carbon metabolism, nutrient balance, and ion homeostasis rather than on a single physiological trait [86]. Previous studies have shown that the coupling between NSC and nutrient elements is an important component of plant stress responses by influencing osmotic regulation, energy balance, and resource allocation strategies [61, 87]. In the present study, multivariate analyses further supported this integrative view. PCA revealed clear treatment-related shifts in the distribution of NSC-, stoichiometric-, and ion-related traits, indicating that rocky desertification intensity altered the overall physiological organization of P. yunnanensis seedlings rather than affecting isolated variables alone. In the NSC module, the association of SRD samples with needle NSC and soluble sugars suggests that severe stress altered carbon allocation toward metabolically active organs [88]. By contrast, CK samples were closely associated with needle starch and a higher sugar-to-starch ratio, indicating that carbon storage is favored under non-stress conditions. Under LRD, the marked accumulation of root NSC and stem starch suggests that mild stress may promote carbon redistribution to belowground organs, thereby enhancing resource acquisition and transport capacity [61]. Similar inter-organ adjustments in carbon allocation have been reported in other woody species under environmental stress, in which source–sink reconfiguration improves resource-use efficiency and buffering capacity [88, 89]. These results suggest that carbon redistribution among organs is an important component of the seedling response to different KRD intensities.

From a nutrient perspective, rocky desertification stress significantly altered C, N, and P contents and their stoichiometric relationships in P. yunnanensis seedlings. Under MRD, the C: N and C: P ratios in seedling organs increased significantly, suggesting that seedlings may enhance stress tolerance by increasing carbon reserves to compensate for limitations in N and P [90]. By contrast, the relative stability of N and P contents and their associated ratios under CK and LRD indicates stronger internal coordination of nutrient acquisition and utilization under non-stress and light stress conditions [91].

The organ-specific pattern of C: N:P ratios further suggests that P. yunnanensis maintains internal homeostasis through differential nutrient allocation among roots, stems, and needles. SEM further showed that soil properties were negatively associated with C: N:P stoichiometric traits, which were then negatively associated with NSCs. This pathway suggests that substrate deterioration may disrupt nutrient balance and subsequently constrain carbon-reserve regulation. Thus, stoichiometric imbalance may represent an important link between soil degradation and carbon-reserve limitation during early seedling establishment.

Mineral ions responses further highlighted the role of ionic regulation in the integrated stress response. PCA showed that K⁺- and Na⁺-related traits contributed strongly to treatment separation, indicating that ion homeostasis was highly sensitive to substrate degradation. Under severe stress, the separation of SRD samples from K⁺- and Na⁺-associated variables suggests that both nutrient-ion retention and ionic balance were altered as stress intensified. In addition, Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ appeared to contribute more strongly under MRD and SRD, implying that these ions may play compensatory roles in stress buffering, possibly through membrane stabilization, osmotic adjustment, and signal transduction [91, 92]. SEM showed a strong pathway from soil properties to mineral ions, supporting the role of mineral ions regulation as an intermediate process linking soil conditions with seedling growth. Effect decomposition indicated that mineral ions contributed to growth through both direct and indirect pathways, suggesting that ion regulation involved both homeostatic adjustment and stress-related costs. Appropriate ion uptake and distribution may sustain metabolism, whereas ionic imbalance may increase physiological burden.

Pearson correlation analysis and the network structure provided additional evidence for strong cross-module coordination among carbon, nutrient, and ion traits. Significant positive correlations among NSC components, and between NSC indices and stoichiometric ratios such as C: N and C: P, indicate that P. yunnanensis seedlings maintain metabolic stability through coordinated regulation of carbon storage and nutrient balance [93]. At the same time, the negative correlations of K⁺ and Mg²⁺ with soluble sugars, starch, and related indices, together with the negative association of Ca²⁺ with N- and P-related traits and the positive association of Na⁺ with N and P contents, suggest that ionic variation is tightly coupled with both carbon metabolism and elemental allocation under stress [92, 94]. Notably, as rocky desertification intensified, the network became progressively denser and more positively connected, especially under SRD. This pattern suggests that severe stress increased the degree of coupling among functional modules within seedlings, likely because the system stability could only be maintained through tighter coordination among multiple physiological pathways. Overall, P. yunnanensis seedlings exposed to different rocky desertification intensities exhibited a coordinated carbon–nutrient–ion regulatory network in which NSC allocation formed the metabolic core, while nutrient balance and ion homeostasis acted as critical supporting mechanisms. Combined with the SEM pathway of “soil–ions–stoichiometry–NSC–growth,” these findings indicate that early adaptation to rocky desertification stress depends on whole-system physiological integration rather than the adjustment of individual traits in isolation. This integrated strategy may help seedlings maintain short-term stability under degraded karst conditions, but it likely becomes increasingly costly under severe stress, where stronger coupling reflects tighter physiological constraint rather than greater functional efficiency. Although this study evaluated dynamic changes in seedling height, basal diameter, physiological traits, C:N: P stoichiometry, and mineral ion regulation, biomass allocation traits were not measured. Therefore, the present study could not directly quantify aboveground–belowground biomass partitioning, root-to-shoot ratio, or carbon investment among organs. Future studies should incorporate final biomass harvests and root–shoot allocation analyses to further clarify how KRD stress affects biomass partitioning and growth-allocation strategies in P. yunnanensis seedlings.

Conclusion

In summary, the early responses of P. yunnanensis seedlings to simulated KRD stress depended on the coordinated regulation of carbon allocation, nutrient balance, and ion homeostasis. Seedlings under LRD retained relatively stable growth and metabolic flexibility, indicating that light stress was maintained within their physiological buffering capacity. By contrast, MRD and SRD progressively disrupted carbon reserves, stoichiometric balance, and ionic regulation, ultimately constraining growth and weakening overall metabolic coordination. These patterns suggest that P. yunnanensis seedlings adopt different adaptive strategies depending on stress intensity: under LRD, they maintain growth through physiological adjustment, whereas under SRD, they shift toward a more conservative survival-oriented strategy that prioritizes maintenance over biomass accumulation. Although this strategy may improve short-term persistence in degraded karst habitats, it likely does so at the cost of growth potential and developmental performance. Overall, this study clarifies the physiological basis of early seedling adaptation to rocky desertification stress and provides a useful basis for native species selection and vegetation restoration in fragile karst ecosystems.
