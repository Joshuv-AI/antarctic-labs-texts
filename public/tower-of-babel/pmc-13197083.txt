Lung Cancer Yield by Baseline Screening Lung CT Screening Reporting and Data System (Lung-RADS) Assignment and Patient Factors
Authors: Andrea N Burnett-Hartman, Nikki M Carroll, Kris F Wain, Brian P Hixon, Anil Vachani, Robert T Greenlee, Stacey A Honda, Roger Y Kim, Christine Neslund-Dudas, Caryn ES Oshiro, Katharine A Rendle, Michael Simoff, Debra P Ritzwoller

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung cancer yield from lung cancer screening (LCS) via low-dose CT (LDCT) imaging may differ in community-based settings compared with clinical trials.Research QuestionWhat proportion of those undergoing baseline LCS LDCT imaging in 5 health care systems in the United States received a diagnosis of lung cancer within 12 months overall and by LCS findings and patient characteristics?Study Design and MethodsStudy participants were eligible for LCS and received care and underwent baseline LCS LDCT imaging within health care systems in the Population-Based Research to Optimize the Screening Process Lung (PROSPR-Lung) Consortium from 2014 through 2020. We collected data on LCS use, results, patient characteristics, and smoking history via electronic health records. Baseline LCS LDCT imaging findings were categorized using the Lung CT Screening Reporting and Data System (Lung-RADS), and lung cancer diagnoses were ascertained via cancer registries. Differences may be attributable to higher smoking intensity, increased comorbidities, and an average older age at baseline scan in community settings compared with clinical trial settings.Key Words: cancer detection rate, cancer yield, lung cancer, Lung-RADS, screening

Take-Home Points

Study Question: What proportion of those undergoing baseline LCS LDCT imaging in 5 health care systems in the United States received a diagnosis of lung cancer within 12 months overall and by LCS findings and patient characteristics?

Results: Overall 12-month lung cancer yield across 5 health care systems in the United States was 2.3%, and those at older ages, who currently smoked, with more pack-years of smoking, greater comorbidity burden, or lower BMI showed higher lung cancer yields (P < .05).

Interpretation: Our results suggest that lung cancer screening programs within US clinical settings likely will observe higher 12-month lung cancer yields than the yield observed in LCS clinical trials because of higher smoking intensity, increased comorbidities, and an average older age at baseline scan in community settings compared with clinical trial settings.

Based on the finding of a 20% relative reduction in lung cancer mortality in the National Lung Screening Trial (NLST),1 lung cancer screening (LCS) via low-dose CT (LDCT) imaging for those with a high risk of lung cancer based on age and smoking history has been recommended in the United States for > 1 decade.2,3 However, LCS performance outcome measures in terms of lung cancer yield, also known as lung cancer detection rate, in community-based settings relative to estimates from clinical trials remain unclear. Lung cancer yield is calculated as the proportion of screened individuals with a cancer diagnosis within a specified amount of time after screening.4

Evidence from community settings has demonstrated that LCS metrics, including participation, primary adherence after an order for baseline LCS LDCT imaging, and adherence to follow-up care after baseline screening, are significantly lower compared with those reported from LCS clinical trials.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 Additionally, the comorbidity burden, the proportion of people who currently smoke, and the number of procedures and complications after positive baseline screening results may be higher than reports from clinical trials.11, 12, 13, 14 Although implementation of LCS in community settings has demonstrated a favorable shift to a higher proportion of stage I lung cancer with an associated decline in stage IV disease,15 little is known about the variation in lung cancer yield among those undergoing LCS outside of clinical trial settings. One resource developed to collect information on LCS across clinical settings in the United States is the American College of Radiology LCS registry, but this registry has had challenges with underreporting of outcomes associated with LCS, including lung cancer yield.16

Cancer yield from screening is used to calculate the number needed to screen to detect 1 cancer case and is an important metric used by health policy decision-makers and health care system stakeholders.17 Although not yet broadly reported as an LCS outcome in community settings, lung cancer yield is a commonly reported metric for breast cancer and other cancer screening methods.18, 19, 20, 21 Ideally, the numerator for cancer yield would include only cancer cases that were diagnosed because of the cancer screening process. However, in practice and outside of clinical trial settings, a time window that is short enough to capture primarily cancer cases that were prevalent at the time of the screening, rather than incident after the screening, but long enough to account for the diagnostic process, generally is applied.4 Also, separating those who receive a diagnosis after screening into those who received a diagnosis after positive screening results vs after negative screening results allows estimation of positive predictive value and negative predictive value. This also provides insight into how well different result categories risk stratify patients into having a low risk vs a high risk of having a prevalent cancer. For example, breast cancer yield derived from screening mammography, ultrasound, or MRI often is reported by negative vs positive Breast Imaging-Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) scores18,20,22; similarly, reporting lung cancer yield by Lung CT Screening Reporting and Data System (Lung-RADS) assignment may represent an important LCS metric.23,24

To date, studies of lung cancer yield in the context of LCS are derived primarily from randomized clinical trials, where estimates of lung cancer yield within 12 months of baseline LDCT imaging are about 1%.1,25,26 Prior analyses of clinical trials also suggest that cancer yield may differ based on sex and age. To address gaps in knowledge regarding LCS outcomes in community-based settings, we used a longitudinal cohort of patients undergoing LCS across 5 diverse US health care systems to estimate overall lung cancer yield from baseline LCS LDCT imaging and yield stratified by Lung-RADS category and certain demographic and clinical factors.27, 28, 29

Study Design and Methods

Study Setting and Data Sources

Data for this study were derived from the Population-Based Research to Optimize the Screening Process Lung (PROSPR-Lung) Consortium Common Data Model (CDM), which contains standardized electronic health record (EHR) data associated with individuals who receive health care services at Henry Ford Health, Kaiser Permanente Colorado, Kaiser Permanente Hawaii, Marshfield Clinic Health System, or the University of Pennsylvania Health System.29 The PROSPR-Lung Consortium CDM contains individual-level EHR data including patient demographics, smoking status, family history of cancer, pharmacy, diagnoses, and procedures.6,11,30 Cancer registry data were collected in a manner consistent with the North American Association of Central Cancer Registries standards.31 These standards include abstraction for cancer diagnoses by certified tumor registrars using standard cancer case definitions and processes resulting in strong sensitivity and specificity for capture of cancer diagnoses. Death data were captured from cancer registries, health plan membership data, and state-level death data sets. Baseline LDCT scans and Lung-RADS assignments were identified from radiology reports within the EHR using procedure codes (S8032, G0297, and 71271).32 In our prior research,33 we validated site-specific text-mining scripts to extract nodule characteristics and Lung-RADS assignment for each baseline LDCT scan. Lung-RADS assignment was obtained via text mining in radiology reports using these previously developed scripts. If Lung-RADS assignment was missing according to the text-mining approach, manual abstraction was used to verify that it was truly missing or to capture the Lung-RADS assignment.

Study Population

Patients eligible for LCS using 2013 US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) criteria who underwent baseline LDCT imaging between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2020, were identified from the PROSPR-Lung Consortium CDM. Specifically, we identified patients 55 to 80 years of age who had at least a 30-pack-year smoking history and currently smoked or quit smoking within the past 15 years. To ensure ascertainment of cancer outcomes occurring within 12 months of a baseline LDCT imaging, patients from integrated care health plan enrollment-based health care systems were required to have 12 months of continuous enrollment or a diagnosis of lung cancer within 12 months of baseline LDCT imaging. Patients from primary care-based engagement in health care systems without health plan enrollment were required to have at least 1 visit to a primary care provider, subsequent LDCT imaging, or a diagnosis of lung cancer within 12 months of the baseline LDCT imaging. Patients with a diagnosis of lung cancer before baseline LDCT imaging were excluded.

Age at the time of the patient’s baseline LDCT scan, sex, self-reported race and ethnicity, family history of lung cancer, and lung cancer diagnoses were derived from data within the PROSPR-Lung Consortium CDM. Those identifying as a member of a racial group that comprised < 1% of the study population and those with unknown race or multiple races were combined into a category labeled Unknown/Another race/Multiple races. Cigarette smoking status, pack-years of smoking history, and BMI (in kilograms per square meter) were assessed at the closest informative recorded value before the baseline LDCT scan. The Deyo adaptation of the Charlson Comorbidity Index was used to assess comorbid burden during the 12 months before baseline LDCT imaging.34 Patients with COPD were identified using the Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease diagnostic category from the Charlson Comorbidity Index.

Statistical Analysis

The primary outcome was lung cancer yield defined as the proportion of patients with baseline LDCT imaging who had received a lung cancer diagnosis within 12 months after baseline LDCT imaging. To evaluate variations in lung cancer yield, we reported yield estimates stratified by Lung-RADS category, sex, age (55-59 years, 60-64 years, 65-69 years, and ≥ 70 years), race and ethnicity, COPD status, smoking status (former vs current), pack-years of smoking (30-39.99 and ≥ 40), and family history of lung cancer (yes or no). Differences in cancer yield between categories within each stratification variable were tested using the χ2 test or Fisher exact test. Positive predictive value was defined as the proportion of patients with a positive Lung-RADS score (3, 4-NOS [not otherwise specified], 4A, 4B, or 4X) who received a diagnosis of lung cancer, and negative predictive value was defined as the proportion of patients with negative Lung-RADS scores (1 or 2) who did not receive a diagnosis of lung cancer. We calculated 95% CIs using a close-form formula. We estimated the number needed to screen to detect 1 case of lung cancer using the formula 1 / yield. We also estimated the cumulative incidence of lung cancer diagnoses to obtain the probability of a lung cancer diagnosis while controlling for loss to follow-up over time resulting from death or lack of engagement with the health system. For the cumulative incidence estimates, patients were followed up from baseline LDCT imaging to the first of lung cancer diagnosis, death, or the date of each patient’s last documented encounter within the health care system.

All analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4M6 (SAS Institute, Inc.). Study protocols were approved with a waiver of consent owing to the use of only retrospective EHR data by the Kaiser Permanente Interregional Institutional Review Board (Identifier: CO-17-2466).

Results

Cohort Demographics

Between 2014 and 2020, 13,448 patients with documentation of LCS eligibility underwent baseline LCS via LDCT imaging (e-Fig 1). A total of 2,408 baseline LDCT scans (18%) showed positive findings, including 1,420 scans (11%) with Lung-RADS category 3 and 988 scans (7%) with Lung-RADS category 4+, inclusive of 4-NOS, 4A, 4B, and 4X. A total of 11,025 baseline scans (82%) showed negative findings (Lung-RADS category 1 or 2), and 15 scans were missing Lung-RADS assignment (data not shown). The overall racial and ethnic distribution was 74% White, 14% Black, 4% Hispanic, 3% Asian, and 1% Native Hawaiian or Pacific, and the cohort was 53% male, 28% were 65 to 69 years of age, 58% currently smoked cigarettes, and 39% had a diagnosis of COPD (Table 1).

Lung Cancer Diagnoses Within 12 Months of Baseline LDCT Imaging

Within 12 months of baseline LDCT imaging, 304 individuals (2.3%) received a diagnosis of lung cancer (Fig 1). This resulted in the number needed to screen to detect 1 case of lung cancer of 44 individuals who met USPSTF 2013 criteria. The lung cancer yield from baseline LDCT imaging was lower in younger patients compared with older patients (1.6% for those < 65 years vs 2.3% for those 65-69 years vs 3.5% for those ≥ 70 years; P < .01). A larger proportion of patients who currently smoke received a diagnosis of lung cancer relative to patients who formerly smoked (2.6% vs 1.9%, respectively; P < .01). Patients with a smoking history of ≥ 40 pack-years were more likely to receive a diagnosis of lung cancer relative to patients with a 30- to 39.99-pack-year history (2.6% vs 1.5%, respectively; P < .01). Those with a BMI of < 25 kg/m2 showed a higher lung cancer yield than those with a BMI of 25 to 29.99 kg/m2 and those with a BMI of ≥ 30 kg/m2 (3.4%, 2,0%, and 1.6%, respectively; P < .01). Lung cancer yield from baseline LDCT imaging did not differ significantly by sex, race or ethnicity, or COPD diagnosis (Fig 1).

Bar graph showing 12-month lung cancer yield, stratified by demographics and risk factors. LDCT = low-dose CT.

Lung-RADS assignment was associated strongly with the proportion receiving a lung cancer diagnosis within 12 months of baseline LDCT imaging (Fig 2). Of those with a baseline Lung-RADS assignment of 1 or 2, < 0.2% received a lung cancer diagnosis within 12 months of baseline LDCT imaging (negative predictive value, 99.8% [95% CI, 99.7%-99.9%], data not shown), whereas 11.9% of those with positive findings received a lung cancer diagnosis within 12 months (positive predictive value, 11.9% [95% CI, 10.6%-13.2%], data not shown). Among those with Lung-RADS category 3 at baseline (n = 1,420), 24 people (1.7%) received a diagnosis of lung cancer. For those with baseline Lung-RADS category of 4-NOS (not otherwise specified; n = 39), 15 people (38.5%) received a lung cancer diagnosis. Among those with Lung-RADS category 4A (n = 548), 59 people (10.8%) received a lung cancer diagnosis. For those with Lung-RADS category 4B (n = 294) and category 4X (n = 107), 114 people (38.8%) and 74 people (69.2%), respectively, received a diagnosis of lung cancer (Fig 2). Among those missing a Lung-RADS assignment (n = 15), 1 individual had a lung cancer diagnosis.

Cumulative Incidence

For cumulative incidence estimates, the cohort had a median follow-up time of 37.0 months and a maximum follow-up time of 93.9 months. Accounting for loss to follow-up because of death or disenrollment, the cumulative incidence of lung cancer diagnoses at 12 months after baseline LDCT imaging was 2.3% and increased to 3.2% at 24 months and 6.1% at 60 months (e-Fig 2).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the largest study of LCS within US community-based health care settings to estimate the lung cancer yield of baseline LDCT imaging, overall and by demographic characteristics, risk factors, and baseline Lung-RADS category. Our primary outcome, lung cancer yield, was 2.3% within 12 months after a baseline screen. Compared with the NLST and other randomized clinical trials, our estimate of the proportion of those with a diagnosis of lung cancer within 12 months of baseline LCS LDCT imaging within the PROSPR-Lung Consortium cohort was twice as high as those reported in randomized clinical trials.1,25,26 For example, estimates of the proportion of those undergoing LCS who received a lung cancer diagnosis within 12 months of the baseline screening in the NLST, the Nederlands-Leuvens Lonkanker Screenings Onderzoek, and the German Lung Cancer Screening Intervention randomized trials were 1.1%, 0.9%, and 1.2%.35

Consistent with our previous study that used PROSPR-Lung Consortium baseline screening results through 2018,11 we observed that the use of Lung-RADS categorization in community settings appropriately stratifies patients into those with a low risk vs high risk of prevalent lung cancer. As anticipated, 12-month lung cancer yield varied by Lung-RADS category, with Lung-RADS 1 and 2 categories having a very low percent of people receiving a diagnosis of lung cancer within 12 months, and Lung RADS category 4X having the highest lung cancer yield. Furthermore, 86.2% (n = 262/304) of the lung cancers detected in the first 12 months after baseline LDCT imaging were in patients with a Lung-RADS assignment of 4A or higher. This estimate is higher than the estimate from a retrospective analysis that applied Lung-RADS criteria to the NLST, which found that 70.2% were mapped to Lung-RADS categories 4A, 4B, or 4X.1,24, 25, 26

Relative to findings associated with individuals undergoing LCS in community settings, Zhang et al,36 in a single-site evaluation of LCS of 4,176 patients, found an overall lung cancer detection rate of 3.8% over a median follow time of 3.3 years, with two-thirds of lung cancer cases (n = 106) detected after 1 round of screening, resulting in a 2.5% 12-month lung cancer yield after baseline LDCT imaging. These findings, higher than the estimates reported by NLST, are consistent with the 12-month lung cancer yield and lung cancer cumulative incidence estimates from our study. Our results stratified by demographic characteristics and risk factors also are consistent with a prior study from an urban academic medical center that reported that lung cancer yield was higher in those in older age groups who currently smoke.37 We also reported that those with lower BMI (< 25 kg/m2) showed a higher lung cancer yield. Given that the association between BMI and lung cancer risk is unclear, this may be related partially to higher smoking intensity associated with lower BMI.38 Taken together, our findings, coupled with those of Zhang et al suggest that it is plausible that differences in lung cancer yield estimates between clinical trial findings and those derived from community-based settings may be attributable to a higher risk population undergoing screening in community-based settings. This includes populations with an older age distribution, higher smoking intensity, and a larger proportion of Black individuals undergoing LCS in community settings compared with clinical trial study populations.39,40 Thus, community-based settings providing LCS to patients in the United States generally should anticipate higher lung cancer yield resulting from baseline LCS LDCT imaging than has been reported in clinical trials.

In addition to studies from the United States, LCS has been evaluated in community settings in other parts of the world. The Ontario LCS Pilot in Canada included 4,451 individuals who were screened successfully after LCS eligibility assessment based on a lung cancer risk prediction model developed by Tammemägi et al,41,42 using data from the Prostate, Lung, Colorectal, and Ovarian Cancer Screening Trial (PLCO), known as the PLCOm2012 model. This pilot reported a 12-month lung cancer yield of 2.2%, which is similar to the 12-month lung cancer yield found in the present study. The Ontario LCS Pilot applied different eligibility criteria for LCS than in the present study, and differences existed in the racial distribution of individuals in the Ontario LCS pilot compared with the PROSPR-Lung Consortium cohort, which has a larger proportion of Black people who smoke. Another study, the Taiwan Lung Cancer Screening in Never-Smoker Trial estimated lung cancer yield for LCS in 12,011 individuals in Taiwan who had never smoked or had a light history of smoking, but who had other risk factors for lung cancer (eg, family history or cooking-related exposures).43 The Taiwan Lung Cancer Screening in Never-Smoker Trial reported a similar invasive lung cancer yield (2.1%) as the present study, but the Taiwan Lung Cancer Screening in Never-Smoker Trial results may not be generalizable to nonsmoking US populations because of higher rates of lung cancer among those who have never smoked in Taiwan than in the United States.44

Despite consistency between our results and results reported from other US community settings, our findings stand in stark contrast to results published using data from the American College of Radiology (ACR) LCS registry.45 In a cohort of > 1 million individuals who underwent LCS from 2017 through 2019 in community practices across the United States, the ACR registry data estimated that the 12-month lung cancer yield after baseline LDCT imaging was only 0.56%. Underascertainment of lung cancer cases was a possible issue noted by the study investigators, because the ACR registry estimates were much lower than other community estimates and those of the NLST. Recent efforts to link the ACR LCS registry to national cancer registry data are aimed at improving ascertainment of lung cancer diagnoses and other outcomes within the ACR LCS registry, and future updated analyses within the ACR LCS registry may provide better estimates of LCS lung cancer yield.16 Despite underreporting of lung cancer diagnoses in the ACR LCS registry, it does provide insight into the potential for Lung-RADS classification to risk stratify individuals, and similar to our results, findings from the ACR registry data indicated a strong association between lung cancer yield and Lung-RADS assignment.

Our study is 1 of the first studies to report lung cancer yield within a large and heterogenous community-based screening population across multiple regions in the United States. The strengths include that our data were linked to gold standard tumor registry data and that Lung-RADS assignment at the baseline scan was captured via manual chart abstraction, validated text string algorithms, or both. However, our results should be interpreted in consideration of the following limitations. The study populations included patients who receive care within a US health care system who met USPSTF LCS screening eligibility criteria, so our results may not be generalizable to or comparable with populations or studies outside of the United States or to populations that do not meet USPSTF LCS eligibility criteria, such as recent population-based studies out of East Asia.46, 47, 48, 49 Because of the inability to obtain spirometry data across all PROSPR-Lung Consortium health care systems, the proportion of patients identified as having COPD may have been overestimated as a result of using diagnostic codes alone. Although we were able to assess health care system enrollment or engagement, we did not capture Lung-RADS assignment fully in LDCT imaging after baseline. In addition, for the primary care-based engagement health care system populations, it is possible that we did not identify all of the post-baseline lung cancer diagnoses (eg, patient received cancer care at other tertiary care institutions). However, underascertainment of lung cancers detected in other health care systems or tumor registries would bias our estimates (that exceeded estimates from clinical trials) downward.

Interpretation

Our results have implications for estimating the impacts of LCS as implemented in community-based clinical practice settings in the United States. Higher lung cancer yield in community-based settings could result in increased diagnoses and treatment of lung cancer in populations undergoing LCS. At the same time, higher lung cancer yield, if it is followed by a long-term reduction in the incidence of late-stage lung cancer diagnoses and reduced lung cancer mortality, could result in higher benefits of LCS in community-based settings than were observed in clinical trial settings. Additional research that includes community-based parameter estimates of lung cancer yield along with other community-based parameter estimates is needed to model the harms, costs, and benefits of LCS in clinical practice settings within the United States.
