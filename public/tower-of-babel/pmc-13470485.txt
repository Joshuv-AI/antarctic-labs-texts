MuseRAG++: a deep retrieval-augmented generation framework for semantic interaction and multi-modal reasoning in virtual museums
Authors: Yifan Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVirtual museums offer new opportunities for cultural heritage engagement by enabling interactive and personalized experiences beyond physical constraints. However, existing dialogue systems struggle to provide semantically adaptive, factually grounded, and multimodal interactions, often suffering from shallow user intent understanding, limited retrieval capabilities, and unverifiable response generation. To address these challenges, we propose MuseRAG++, a unified retrieval-augmented framework that integrates deep user intent modeling, a hybrid sparse-dense retrieval pipeline spanning text, images, and structured metadata, and a provenance-aware generation module that explicitly grounds responses in retrieved evidence. Unlike traditional methods relying on benchmark datasets, we evaluate MuseRAG++ through a qualitative user study within a functional virtual museum prototype, focusing on engagement, usability, and trustworthiness. Experimental results demonstrate substantial improvements in retrieval and generation metrics compared to strong baselines, while user evaluations confirm enhanced factual accuracy and interpretability.Keywords: Virtual museums, Cultural heritage, Retrieval-augmented framework, User intent modeling, Provenance-aware generationSubject terms: Information systems and information technology, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The digitization of cultural heritage1 and the increasing accessibility of immersive technologies2 have catalyzed the development of virtual museums, offering users a novel paradigm for interacting with historical artifacts and cultural narratives. Compared to traditional physical exhibitions, virtual museums offer asynchronous, location-independent access to curated content while also enabling novel forms of engagement through interactive interfaces3. As societal interest in cultural learning expands beyond institutional settings, there is a growing demand for intelligent systems that not only present digital replicas of artifacts but also support context-aware explanation, provenance reasoning, and personalized exploration. In this context, effective virtual museum interaction requires systems to understand what visitors are asking, identify reliable cultural evidence, and present explanations that users can verify. This positions the virtual museum as both a technological and educational frontier requiring deeper integration of human–AI interaction capabilities.

Prior research on virtual museums has explored a wide spectrum of interaction modalities and content presentation strategies. Early systems focused primarily on graphical reconstruction and spatial navigation4, using 3D modeling5, panoramic views6, or VR-based walkthroughs7 to simulate the physical visiting experience. More recent work has introduced rule-based or retrieval-based conversational agents8, enabling users to query exhibit-related information through pre-scripted dialogues or keyword matching. Other approaches have leveraged recommender systems to personalize content delivery based on user profiles or browsing behavior9. Despite these advances, traditional virtual museum systems remain limited in semantic understanding and contextual adaptability. They often rely on static metadata or curated descriptions10, which restricts their ability to accommodate open-domain queries or support in-depth, conversational engagement with cultural content. Consequently, many systems can present cultural objects effectively, but remain less capable of sustaining evidence-grounded dialogue that connects visitor questions with diverse museum resources.

With the advent of large language models (LLMs), virtual museum interaction has evolved toward more natural, adaptive, and knowledge-rich paradigms. LLMs, pre-trained on large-scale corpora encompassing both general and domain-specific knowledge, have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in open-domain question answering, contextual reasoning, and multi-turn dialogue generation11–14. These advances have catalyzed a new generation of virtual museum systems in which LLMs serve as intelligent cultural guides, enabling users to engage with exhibits through natural language interactions. Current LLM-based approaches to virtual museum dialogue typically adopt one of three architectural paradigms. The first relies on prompt-based generation15, where instruction-tuned LLMs directly produce descriptions and responses based on user queries, offering flexibility but often lacking factual grounding. The second approach integrates retrieval-augmented generation16, which augments language models with external evidence retrieval to enhance accuracy and contextual relevance. The third follows a modular or agent design17, separating the interaction pipeline into discrete components for intent recognition, knowledge retrieval, and response generation, thereby improving interpretability and component-wise optimization. These architectures represent a significant advancement over rule-based or scripted systems, enabling more semantically enriched and conversationally fluid user experiences. However, museum dialogue differs from generic open-domain question answering because visitors often ask context-dependent questions, while reliable answers must be grounded in heterogeneous sources and remain traceable to cultural evidence.

Several key challenges persist in effectively applying LLMs to cultural heritage interaction. A central limitation lies in the lack of robust mechanisms for modeling user intent, which is essential for generating responses aligned with nuanced informational needs, ranging from historical interpretation to stylistic analysis and provenance tracing. While retrieval-augmented methods have improved factual grounding, many implementations rely on shallow or unimodal retrieval strategies that fail to capture the rich heterogeneity of museum data, such as textual descriptions, visual representations, and structured metadata. Furthermore, current generation models often lack explicit provenance awareness, producing outputs that are fluent but difficult to verify. This lack of traceability undermines trust, particularly in cultural and educational contexts where accuracy and transparency of source are critical. These limitations point to the need for a virtual museum dialogue framework that can follow the visitor’s evolving intent, retrieve appropriate evidence from heterogeneous museum resources, and generate explanations whose factual content can be traced back to supporting sources.

To address these challenges, we propose MuseRAG++, a retrieval-augmented interaction framework for semantically adaptive and provenance-aware dialogue in virtual museum environments. MuseRAG++ is designed to support cultural heritage exploration by connecting three processes that are jointly required in museum dialogue: understanding the visitor’s evolving intent, retrieving relevant evidence from heterogeneous museum resources, and generating responses that can be traced back to supporting sources. Specifically, the framework integrates context-aware user intent modeling, hybrid sparse-dense semantic retrieval, multimodal evidence fusion, and provenance-aware response generation. This design enables the system to move beyond generic question answering and provide artifact explanations that are contextually relevant, evidence-grounded, and interpretable. Extensive Experimental results demonstrates that MuseRAG++ outperforms retrieval-augmented and LLM-only baselines across retrieval and generation metrics, achieving the best Recall@10, Precision, Accuracy, and F1. Human evaluation further shows that MuseRAG++ receives higher scores in factuality, evidentiality, usefulness, and interpretability, indicating that provenance-aware generation improves both response reliability and user-perceived trustworthiness. Results

Dataset

The effectiveness of the proposed provenance-aware response generation framework is evaluated using a dedicated dataset centered on the Changxin Palace Lantern, a renowned artifact in Chinese cultural heritage. The dataset comprises 188 scholarly articles encompassing archaeological interpretations, artistic analyses, and technological investigations related to the lantern. In addition to textual materials, five high-quality documentary videos are incorporated, offering multimodal perspectives and expert commentary that contextualize the artifact’s historical and cultural significance.

All data sources undergo systematic preprocessing to remove typographical noise, duplicated content, and irrelevant metadata. Textual materials are segmented into coherent passages ranging from 100 to 300 words to ensure semantic completeness and retrieval granularity, and all video transcripts are aligned with textual metadata for cross-modal indexing. The resulting corpus is subsequently indexed to support structured and efficient evidence retrieval.

To prevent potential data leakage, corpus splitting is performed at the article level with a 6:2:2 ratio for training, validation, and testing. Each article and its associated video transcript are assigned exclusively to one split, ensuring that no textual or visual fragments appear across subsets. Cross-split redundancy is further mitigated through MinHash-based near-duplicate filtering (Jaccard \ge 0.9), followed by manual verification. The dataset is available at https://github.com/Alkaid-Hu/MuseRAG-.git.

Implementation details

Our framework is implemented in PyTorch using HuggingFace Transformers and the Accelerate library for distributed training. The retriever adopts a hybrid strategy that combines BM25 with a fine-tuned BGE-base embedding model, retrieving the top-10 passages per query. Sparse and dense retrieval scores are normalized before fusion, and the fusion weight is selected on the validation set. The response generator is built upon Qwen3-8B11, while the intent encoder employs the first twelve transformer layers of the same backbone for supervised contrastive fine-tuning. The lower six layers of the intent encoder are frozen, while the upper six layers are fine-tuned for cultural heritage dialogue. During contrastive fine-tuning, the positive-to-negative sampling ratio is set to 1:5, and negative samples with cosine similarity above 0.4 are filtered out. The temperature parameter is selected from [0.05, 0.15], with the best value set to 0.07.

For training the provenance-aware generation component, we use the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of , a batch size of 4, and a maximum decoding length of 256 tokens. The balancing coefficient λ is empirically set to 0.4. Claim-level grounding labels are obtained by matching generated factual claims with expert-verified supporting passages. During inference, the generator is conditioned on the user query, retrieved evidence, and provenance-aware response plan, with the decoding temperature set to 0.2 and top-p set to 0.9. All experiments are conducted on an NVIDIA DGX station equipped with four A100 GPUs (40 GB each) and Intel Xeon Platinum 8358 CPUs. During inference, MuseRAG++ operates efficiently on a single A100 using mixed-precision (fp16) computation. The average end-to-end latency per query is approximately 10 seconds. Memory usage during inference remains below 32 GB.

Evaluation metrics

A comprehensive evaluation of the proposed provenance-aware response generation framework involves both retrieval quality and factual integrity of generated responses. Retrieval performance is measured using Recall@k, which reflects the system’s ability to identify ground-truth supporting passages among the top-k retrieved candidates. In the Changxin Palace Lantern corpus, these supporting passages are established through a hybrid annotation process that combines automatic matching and expert validation. Initially, candidate passages are aligned with queries via rule-based entity and keyword matching, capturing artifact names, materials, dynasties, and stylistic terms. Subsequently, two domain scholars manually review and refine the pairs, labeling each passage as supporting or irrelevant according to its factual correspondence with the query.

Generation quality is evaluated at two complementary levels. Precision is computed at the fact level, representing the proportion of factual statements within generated responses that are both accurate and explicitly supported by retrieved evidence. Recall is also defined at the fact level and measures the proportion of gold-standard facts (manually annotated by domain experts for each query) that are successfully covered by the model’s output. Accuracy is measured at the response level, assessing whether the overall answer maintains semantic coherence and factual consistency with the underlying knowledge base. The F1-score is then derived as the harmonic mean of fact-level precision and recall, summarizing the model’s ability to balance factual completeness with evidential correctness under provenance-aware constraints.

Baselines

We evaluated MuseRAG++ against five representative baseline configurations covering both retrieval-augmented and prompt-based paradigms. The BM25 + ChatGLM4-9B-Chat18 adopts classical sparse retrieval, concatenating the top-10 passages returned by BM25 with the user query for generation. The Dense-RAG + ChatGLM4-9B-Chat replaces sparse retrieval with a dense semantic retriever based on BGE m3-embeddings19. The Citation Prompting baseline employs ChatGLM4-9B-Chat directly prompted to include source identifiers in its responses without performing any explicit retrieval or provenance alignment. In addition, Qwen3-8B11 and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B14 serve as LLM-only prompt-based baselines that generate responses purely from internal knowledge without any retrieval augmentation.

The hybrid retriever jointly optimizes semantic relevance and lexical precision, enabling accurate retrieval of both surface and conceptual information in domain-specific corpora where knowledge is limited and terminology complex. The structured evidence planning mechanism further organizes the retrieved content into semantically aligned segments, ensuring that each generated statement is explicitly grounded in verified evidence. User study

To comprehensively evaluate human-perceived quality, a 50-participant user study was conducted to assess the interpretability, factual grounding, and overall credibility of generated responses. Participants were drawn from three groups: (i) NLP and information retrieval researchers (24), (ii) museum and art history professionals (18), and (3) graduate students in digital humanities (iii). All participants had prior experience in evaluating text generation or cultural content quality, ensuring both linguistic and domain expertise.

Each participant was presented with outputs from MuseRAG++ and baseline models under double-blind, within-subject conditions, where model identities were concealed and output order randomized. Each query instance contained three candidate responses generated from different systems, covering factual descriptions, stylistic interpretations, and historical reasoning tasks. A Latin-square assignment was used to balance presentation order and reduce potential bias. Participants were instructed to evaluate the responses independently, without discussion, using a web-based annotation interface that displayed model outputs along with corresponding retrieval snippets for reference. As shown in Table 2, responses were evaluated along 5 key dimensions reflecting the objectives of provenance-aware generation, including Factuality, Fluency, Evidentiality, Usefulness, and Interpretability. Each dimension was rated on a five-point Likert scale (1–5), where higher scores indicate stronger performance. Detailed examples and rating guidelines were provided during a training session to align evaluators’ understanding. To reduce halo effects, each participant rated a maximum of 30 responses per session, and responses were mixed across query categories. To verify inter-rater consistency, two independent evaluators scored each output, and disagreements were adjudicated by a third rater for 10% of randomly selected cases. The inter-rater reliability, computed using the two-way mixed-effects model ICC(3,k), was 0.962 with a 95% confidence interval of [0.941, 0.978], F = 25.91 (, , p < 0.0001). These values indicate excellent agreement across all evaluation dimensions.

The ablation results reveal that each module contributes meaningfully to overall system performance. Removing user intent modeling leads to the largest drop in Recall@10 and F1, reflecting its critical role in aligning retrieval with evolving conversational goals. Without the hybrid retrieval mechanism, the model’s ability to identify lexically sparse yet semantically relevant evidence decreases, demonstrating the importance of jointly optimizing sparse and dense signals.

The multimodal gating mechanism also provides a measurable benefit, improving factual precision by dynamically weighting textual and visual features based on contextual relevance. Real visitor study

To complement the original user study with domain experts, we invited realistic interactions with five representative museum visitors differing in background and curiosity patterns, as shown in Fig. 3. Each participant interacted freely with MuseRAG++ through natural language dialogue to explore the Changxin Palace Lantern exhibition. The conversations revealed distinct inquiry trajectories: some visitors pursued factual and structural details of the artifact’s design, while others focused on symbolic meaning, historical provenance, or conservation techniques. MuseRAG++ was able to dynamically adjust its retrieval and reasoning strategies to accommodate these heterogeneous intents, synthesizing multimodal evidence and providing traceable, contextually coherent explanations. Participants consistently reported that the system enhanced their understanding and engagement compared with static digital displays, noting the clarity of provenance links and the balance between educational content and conversational fluency.

Discussion

Failure analysis

To complement the case-level observations, we conducted a quantitative failure analysis on the test set. Among 120 test queries, MuseRAG++ produced 31 failed responses, corresponding to an overall failure rate of 25.8%. Each failed response was assigned to one dominant error source by jointly examining the retrieved top-10 evidence, the expert-annotated supporting passages, the gold-standard facts, and the generated answer. As shown in Table 5, the most frequent error type was partial evidence retrieval, accounting for 29.0% of all failed cases. This indicates that the system often retrieved evidence related to the query topic but failed to collect all supporting information required for complete factual coverage.

Retrieval-related failures formed the largest group of errors. Retrieval miss, partial evidence retrieval, and ranking error together accounted for 58.0% of all failed responses. Among these, retrieval misses occurred in 12.9% of failed cases, suggesting that complete failure to retrieve relevant evidence was relatively limited. In contrast, partial evidence retrieval and ranking error accounted for 29.0% and 16.1%, respectively. This pattern suggests that the main retrieval limitation of MuseRAG++ lies not in locating the broad topic but in retrieving and ranking fine-grained evidence necessary for detailed cultural interpretation. Generation-related failures were less frequent but still observable. Unsupported generation accounted for 12.9% of failed responses. These cases occurred when the generated answer contained factual claims that could not be verified by the retrieved evidence. Although the provenance-aware decoding mechanism reduces unsupported generation, it does not fully eliminate factual over-generalization when the retrieved evidence is incomplete or semantically ambiguous.

Temporal or attributional reasoning errors accounted for 19.4% of failed responses. In these cases, relevant evidence was retrieved, but the model failed to synthesize chronology, ownership, later use, or artifact attribution correctly. This type of failure is particularly important in cultural heritage scenarios, because historical interpretation often requires connecting scattered evidence across multiple sources rather than extracting a single factual statement. Tone or usability mismatch accounted for 9.7% of failed responses, indicating that a small proportion of answers were factually acceptable but perceived as overly academic, verbose, or insufficiently visitor-friendly.

Table 6 further shows that failure rates varied across query types. Factual description and functional reasoning queries had relatively low overall failure rates of 16.0% and 13.6%, respectively. This suggests that MuseRAG++ performs more reliably when the answer is supported by explicit artifact descriptions or clearly stated technical evidence. Conservation knowledge and stylistic interpretation showed moderate failure rates of 23.5% and 27.8%, respectively, indicating that these questions require more contextual synthesis but are still partly supported by available evidence. The highest failure rates were observed for provenance or ownership queries and temporal reasoning queries, reaching 40.0% and 38.9%, respectively. These two categories also showed higher reasoning-related failure rates, particularly in temporal reasoning, where such failures reached 22.2%. This result is consistent with the error distribution in Table 4 and suggests that MuseRAG++ remains less robust when the answer requires reconstructing ownership chains, interpreting inscriptional evidence, or synthesizing historical sequences from dispersed sources.

Overall, the quantitative failure analysis shows that MuseRAG++ is more reliable for factual description and functional explanation than for historical reconstruction and attributional reasoning. The main limitation does not arise from a single module, but from the interaction between incomplete evidence coverage, ranking limitations, and the difficulty of synthesizing temporally distributed cultural knowledge.

Case study

Based on the quantitative failure distribution above, we further examine representative successful and unsuccessful cases to illustrate how different modules affect the final response. These cases are not used as standalone evidence of effectiveness, but as qualitative explanations of the observed statistical patterns.

The first case represents functional reasoning queries, where the required evidence is explicit and can be supported by both textual descriptions and documentary transcripts. When the system was asked, “How did the Changxin Palace Lantern’s design reduce smoke during use?”, MuseRAG++ accurately retrieved technical descriptions of the hollow sleeve and internal air-duct system within the kneeling maidservant lamp. Sparse lexical cues such as “sleeve,” “vent,” and “water” were aligned with semantically related expressions such as “airflow channel” and “smoke absorption” in archaeological analyses. The multimodal gating mechanism further incorporated documentary transcript evidence referring to the same structure. During generation, the provenance-aware decoder linked explanatory clauses to supporting passages, producing a response that was both fluent and verifiable. This case explains why functional reasoning queries show a relatively low failure rate in the quantitative analysis.

The second case corresponds to the temporal and attributional reasoning errors identified in Table 5. When asked, “What do the inscriptions on the Changxin Palace Lantern reveal about its ownership and later use?”, MuseRAG++ retrieved partial evidence describing the nine inscriptions and sixty-five engraved characters. However, the retrieved evidence did not adequately address the distribution of inscriptions, ownership transfers, and chronological implications. The dense retriever favored general discussions of bronze craftsmanship, while the sparse retriever over-emphasized high-frequency but context-independent terms. As a result, the generated answer was coherent but incomplete, omitting key details required to reconstruct the historical progression of ownership and later use. This failure suggests that provenance-aware generation cannot compensate for incomplete evidence coverage when the answer requires multi-source temporal synthesis.

These cases show that the interaction among intent modeling, hybrid retrieval, and provenance-aware decoding is highly dependent on the structure of available evidence. When the evidence is explicit and semantically concentrated, MuseRAG++ can effectively align user intent, retrieve relevant support, and generate verifiable explanations. When the evidence is dispersed, low-frequency, or historically implicit, the system may retrieve only partial support and generate answers that are fluent but insufficiently complete.

Cross-artifact generalization analysis

We extended the evaluation to three additional cross-artifact datasets covering different types of cultural heritage objects. As summarized in Tables 7 and 8, BronzeCraft focuses on mechanical and bronze artifacts, including the Sanxingdui bronzes, the Simuwu Ding, and the Qin bronze chariots. AcousticRelic focuses on acoustic and structurally complex artifacts, including the Jiahu bone flute, the Marquis Yi of Zeng bells, and the Shizhaishan bronze drum. VisualNarrative focuses on paintings and dense visual narratives, including Along the River During the Qingming Festival, Nymph of the Luo River, and Night Revels of Han Xizai. Each artifact corpus contains approximately 100–150 scholarly documents. The three extended datasets are designed to test different aspects of provenance-aware cultural heritage dialogue. Mechanical and bronze artifacts require the system to retrieve evidence related to material composition, casting techniques, mechanical structure, ritual function, and archaeological interpretation. Acoustic and structurally complex artifacts require reasoning over structure, sound production, craftsmanship, and historical function. Painting-related artifacts place greater emphasis on dense visual details, scene understanding, figure relations, stylistic description, and narrative interpretation. These settings allow us to evaluate whether MuseRAG++ remains effective when the evidence structure, visual density, and reasoning requirements vary across artifact types.

Computational efficiency analysis

We further report its computational efficiency during inference. All runtime results are measured on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU with mixed-precision computation. The retrieval corpus is pre-indexed before inference, and each query retrieves the top-10 evidence passages. The maximum decoding length is set to 256 tokens.

As shown in Table 10, the average end-to-end latency of MuseRAG++ is 9.8 s per query. The response generation stage accounts for the largest proportion of runtime, mainly due to autoregressive decoding with provenance-aware conditioning. The hybrid retrieval and evidence planning stages introduce additional computational cost, but their latency remains relatively small compared with generation. The peak GPU memory usage is 31.6 GB, indicating that the system can be deployed on a single high-memory GPU for practical virtual museum interaction. These results suggest that MuseRAG++ can support interactive museum dialogue with acceptable latency. Although the framework introduces additional steps for intent modeling, hybrid retrieval, and provenance-aware planning, the overall runtime remains within a practical range for virtual museum applications. Future deployment can further reduce latency through model compression, retrieval caching, smaller generators, and streaming response generation.

Limitations and future work

This study still has several limitations that warrant further investigation. Although MuseRAG++ has been extended to multiple categories of cultural artifacts, its current performance still depends on the quality, granularity, and completeness of the underlying museum knowledge base. When historical evidence is sparse, inconsistently described, or distributed across multiple sources, the system may retrieve only partial support and produce answers that are factually plausible but incomplete. The failure analysis further shows that temporal reasoning, ownership reconstruction, inscription interpretation, and later-use attribution remain challenging because these tasks require synthesizing low-frequency evidence across long historical contexts. In addition, the current provenance-aware generation strategy improves traceability at the passage level, but it does not yet provide fine-grained claim-level verification for every generated statement. The system also needs stronger adaptation to different visitor profiles, since expert users and general visitors may require different levels of detail, terminology, and narrative style. Future work will therefore focus on finer-grained evidence indexing, temporal and attributional reasoning, claim-level provenance verification, and adaptive response generation for diverse museum audiences.

Related work

Virtual museums and cultural heritage interaction

Virtual museums have become important platforms for broadening access to cultural heritage by presenting artifacts through digital, spatially organized, and visually immersive environments. Early virtual museum systems mainly aimed to reproduce the experience of physical exhibitions through 3D modeling20, panoramic imaging21, and interactive web interfaces22. With the development of immersive technologies, many platforms further incorporated virtual and augmented reality to strengthen spatial presence and visitor engagement23. These systems have improved access to museum collections and allowed users to explore cultural objects beyond physical constraints.

Beyond visual reconstruction, recent virtual museum research has increasingly emphasized content enrichment and user participation. Functions including thematic browsing, multilingual annotation, interactive timelines, recommendation systems, and cross-collection search have been introduced to support more flexible exploration24. Metadata-based navigation and structured cultural databases also allow artifacts to be presented within broader historical, stylistic, and geographical contexts. In parallel, interaction modes have moved from static point-and-click interfaces toward more adaptive forms, including conversational interfaces, voice control, and guided tours25. These advances have improved accessibility and engagement, but many systems still rely on predefined dialogue flows, static metadata, or rule-based logic. As a result, they remain limited in interpreting open-ended visitor questions, synthesizing curatorial knowledge, and explaining the provenance or historical development of cultural objects. This creates a gap between visual immersion and deeper semantic understanding.

Large language models for interactive cultural dialogue

Large language models have changed the design of interactive systems by enabling more natural, flexible, and context-aware communication26. Because they are trained on large-scale corpora, LLMs show strong abilities in question answering, contextual reasoning, and multi-turn dialogue generation27–29. They have been adopted in education30, healthcare31, and cultural computing32, where users often require explanations that go beyond simple factual retrieval. Compared with rule-based or intent-matching dialogue systems, LLM-based systems can better interpret varied user expressions, maintain discourse coherence, and generate informative responses across multiple turns33.

These capabilities have encouraged the use of LLMs in intelligent tutoring systems, virtual museum guides, and assistants for scientific or historical exploration32–35. In virtual museum scenarios, LLMs can serve as cultural guides by answering visitor questions, explaining artifact functions, and connecting objects with historical context. However, directly using LLMs for museum dialogue also introduces important risks. Since museum knowledge often requires factual accuracy, source transparency, and careful historical interpretation, purely generative responses may produce fluent but unverifiable content. This is particularly problematic when users ask about artifact provenance, ownership, inscriptions, or historical functions, where inaccurate explanations may reduce trust in the system.

Retrieval-augmented generation and provenance-aware interaction

Retrieval-augmented generation has emerged as an effective strategy for improving the factual reliability of LLM-based systems36. By retrieving external evidence before generation, RAG frameworks can reduce hallucination and provide responses that are more closely connected to domain-specific knowledge sources35,37–39. Modular pipelines further divide the interaction process into intent recognition, evidence retrieval, and response generation, which can improve transparency and make each component easier to optimize. These properties are especially relevant to virtual museums, where answers should be grounded in curated descriptions, scholarly documents, visual materials, and structured metadata.

Despite these advances, existing RAG and agent-based dialogue systems still face several challenges in cultural heritage interaction. First, user intent is often treated as a fixed classification label, although museum visitors frequently ask follow-up questions whose meanings depend on previous dialogue. Second, many retrieval pipelines focus mainly on textual corpora and do not sufficiently use visual evidence, documentary transcripts, or structured metadata, all of which are important for understanding cultural objects. Third, generation modules often provide answers without explicit provenance constraints, making it difficult for users to verify where a claim comes from. These limitations reduce the reliability and interpretability of LLM-based museum dialogue systems.

Taken together, existing studies provide important foundations for immersive presentation, natural-language interaction, and retrieval-augmented generation. However, they do not fully address the problem of provenance-aware cultural heritage dialogue, where a system must jointly understand evolving visitor intent, retrieve evidence from heterogeneous museum resources, and generate traceable responses in multi-turn interaction. This gap motivates the design of MuseRAG++, which formulates virtual museum dialogue as an evidence-grounded and source-traceable generation problem rather than as generic question answering.
