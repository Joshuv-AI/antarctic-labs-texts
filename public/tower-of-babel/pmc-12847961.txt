Neuroprotective effects of α-lipoic acid on ischemia/reperfusion injury in global cerebral ischemia mouse model
Authors: Soyoung Kwak, Ji Sun Lim, Shin Kim, Ye Jin Kwon, Mi-Hee Yu, Mee-Na Park, Dong Kyu Choi, Jeong-Ho Hong, Hae Won Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIschemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury, resulting from transient or permanent cerebral vessel occlusion, triggers oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption, leading to progressive neuronal damage and cognitive decline. The hippocampus, due to its high metabolic demand and susceptibility to oxidative stress, is particularly vulnerable to I/R-induced injury. This study evaluated the neuroprotective effects of α-lipoic acid (α-LA), a potent antioxidant, using bilateral common carotid arteries occlusion/reperfusion (BCCAO/R) mouse model and an oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygenation in vitro model. In BCCAO/R mice, α-LA improved spatial memory without affecting motor activity and restored hippocampal tight junction proteins (Claudin-5 and Occludin) and antioxidant enzyme expression, indicating BBB stabilization and oxidative stress reduction. Although synaptic proteins (BDNF and PSD-95) were not restored, cognitive improvements suggest alternative protective mechanisms. In HT22 cells, α-LA decreased intracellular reactive oxygen species levels, enhanced viability, and inhibited apoptosis via decreased PARP cleavage and caspase-3 activation. These protective effects were linked to the activation of the Nrf2/ARE signaling pathway and the upregulation of its downstream antioxidant targets. Overall, α-LA demonstrated marked neuroprotective effects in ischemic models by reducing oxidative stress, preserving BBB integrity, and restoring hippocampal function, positioning it as a promising therapeutic candidate for ischemic brain injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33565-8.Keywords: Α-lipoic acid, Ischemia/reperfusion injury, Cognitive function, Blood-brain barrier integrity, Oxidative stress, NQO1Subject terms: Molecular biology, Diseases, Neurology

Introduction

Ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury occurs when blood flow is restored following a temporary or permanent occlusion of cerebral blood vessels, and it represents a major cause of neuronal damage and cognitive impairment1. Although reperfusion is essential to salvage ischemic brain tissue, it can paradoxically exacerbate injury by generating excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to oxidative stress that overwhelms the body’s endogenous antioxidant defense mechanisms2. This oxidative stress burden initiates a cascade of secondary pathological events, including neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption, ultimately resulting in neuronal death3–5. These processes collectively exacerbate brain injury and are closely linked to the onset of cognitive deficits following ischemic insults.

The hippocampus is particularly vulnerable to I/R-induced oxidative damage due to its high metabolic demands and limited antioxidant defense capacity6. This vulnerability is evident in synaptic proteolysis, neuronal loss, and impaired neuroplasticity, all of which contribute directly to deficits in spatial memory and cognitive function7. NMDA receptor–driven Ca²⁺ influx, premature mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS overload, limited Ca²⁺ buffering and antioxidant stores, heightened glucocorticoid responsiveness, and neurovascular/BBB vulnerability are more prominent in hippocampal CA1 than in neocortex8–10. Notably, decreased expression of synaptic markers such as PSD-95 and alterations in neurotrophic factors such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) have been observed following ischemic injury, highlighting the critical role of synaptic integrity in post-ischemic cognitive outcomes11.

Although significant advancements have been made in reperfusion therapies such as thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy, effective neuroprotective strategies that target oxidative stress and preserve hippocampal function are essentially lacking. This gap underscores the growing need to explore therapeutic agents capable of mitigating ROS-mediated neuronal damage and stabilizing the BBB following ischemic events. α-Lipoic acid (α-LA) is a naturally occurring dithiol compound known for its potent antioxidant properties and role as a metabolic cofactor12. As a low–molecular-weight amphipathic molecule, α-LA readily crosses the blood–brain barrier, enabling central nervous system exposure13,14. It can directly scavenge free radicals, regenerate key endogenous antioxidants such as glutathione and vitamins C and E, and modulate redox-sensitive transcription factors, including the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)15. Activation of the Nrf2/antioxidant response element (ARE) pathway triggers the upregulation of cytoprotective genes16. Emerging evidence suggests that α-LA may offer neuroprotective effects in models of neurodegenerative diseases and stroke by reducing oxidative stress, inhibiting neuroinflammation, and preserving BBB integrity17. However, its specific effects in global cerebral ischemia models, particularly those examining hippocampus-specific oxidative damage and cognitive recovery, remain inadequately explored. In this study, we employed a bilateral common carotid artery occlusion/reperfusion (BCCAO/R) mouse model to induce mild ischemic injury without infarction, selectively impairing hippocampal function. We examined whether intervention with α-LA could ameliorate oxidative stress in the hippocampus, restore BBB integrity by upregulating tight junction proteins, and improve cognitive performance through activation of the Nrf2/ARE antioxidant pathway. This study aims to provide new insights into the therapeutic potential of α-LA in managing I/R-induced brain injury and associated cognitive deficits.

Materials and methods

Materials

α-LA (racemic form; Cat# L0207) was purchased from TCI America, Inc. (Portland, OR, USA). Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin-streptomycin, and glucose were obtained from Welgene (Gyeongsan, South Korea) or Gibco/Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). TTC (2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride; T8877), H2DCFDA, and trypsin-EDTA were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich and Welgene, respectively. Protein extraction reagents, Bradford assay kits, and luciferase assay reagents were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific, Roche Applied Science, Bio-Rad Laboratories, and Promega. All primary and secondary antibodies used for Western blotting were procured from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, TX, USA), Abcam (Cambridge, UK), Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA), or Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA), as detailed in the Western blot section. The ARE-reporter vector (pGL4.37[luc2P/ARE/Hyg]) was purchased from Promega, and transfections were carried out using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen).

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old, weighing 15–20 g) were obtained from Daehan Biolink (Eumseong, Korea). The mice were housed in standard laboratory cages under controlled conditions, including a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 08:00), a temperature maintained at 22–24℃, and a relative humidity of 55–60%. They had ad libitum access to chow food pellets and water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Keimyung University (Approval Number KM-2023-15).

For surgical procedures, mice were anesthetized with 2.0% isoflurane in N₂O/O₂ (70:30). Animal sacrifice was performed under deep anesthesia (2.5% isoflurane) by CO₂ inhalation delivered at 20% of the chamber volume per minute.

Establishment of the BCCAO/R mouse model

A total of 45 mice were used in the study, including 14 mice that underwent sham surgery and 31 mice that underwent surgical induction of global cerebral ischemia using the BCCAO/R model. Anesthesia was induced with 5% isoflurane and maintained with 2% isoflurane in a 70:30 mixture of N2O/O2, administered via a face mask throughout the procedure. Bilateral common carotid arteries were exposed through a midline cervical incision and freed from their sheaths. Arterial occlusion was induced by applying microvascular clamps to the CCAs for 20 min, followed by reperfusion upon clamp removal. Rectal temperature was continuously monitored and maintained at 37 ℃ using a heating pad. Relative cerebral blood flow (CBF) was assessed before, during, and after the procedure using Laser Doppler flowmetry (Omega flow FLO-C1 BV, Omegawave, Tokyo, Japan). Mice that exhibited a CBF reduction of less than 80% intraoperatively were excluded from the study.

To assess ischemic injury, 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining was conducted 24 h after BCCAO/R to verify infarct formation following transient global ischemia. Cognitive function was examined on Day 14 using the Y-maze test. On Day 15, mice were sacrificed, and brain tissues were collected for biochemical analyses to explore the mechanisms underlying the effects of α-LA. All in vivo procedures preceded in vitro experiments; in vivo results are shown in Figs. 1, 2 and 3 and informed the subsequent cell‑based assays presented in Figs. 4 and 5.

TTC staining

TTC staining was performed 24 h after BCCAO/R surgery. A subset of three mice per group were euthanized, and their brains were collected and sectioned into five 2-mm-thick coronal slices. The brain slices were incubated in 2% TTC solution (T8877, Sigma Aldrich) prepared in saline for 15 min at 37℃, then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight. Viable brain tissue was stained red, while infarcted areas remained white, indicating regions of cerebral injury.

Behavioral assessments

The Y-maze test was performed 14 days after BCCAO/R surgery to evaluate spatial working memory. The Y-maze apparatus comprised three arms (500 mm long, 150 mm high, 100 mm wide) labeled A, B, and C, arranged at 120° angles. Each mouse was placed at the end of arm C to begin the test, and their arm entries were recorded for 5 min. Spontaneous alternations were defined as consecutive entries into three different arms. Both the sequence and total number of arm entries were recorded with a video tracking system (SMART v3.0, Panlab). The percentage of spontaneous alternations was calculated using the formula: Spontaneous alternation (%) = (number of alternations/[total arm entries – 2]) × 100.

Cell culture and in vitro OGD/R model

Mouse HT22 hippocampal neurons were generously provided by Dr. Jong-Sang Kim (Kyungpook National University, Daegu, South Korea). HT22 hippocampal neuronal cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; LM001-05, Welgene, Gyeongsan, South Korea) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; #160004, Gibco/Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (#15140122, Invitrogen/Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were maintained at 37℃ in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ and subcultured using 0.05% trypsin-EDTA (LS015-01, Welgene, South Korea) when approximately 80% confluent.

For in vitro I/R studies, HT22 cells were seeded at 4 × 10⁵ cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured under standard conditions for 24 h. Cells assigned to the oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R) protocol were incubated in serum- and glucose-free DMEM under hypoxic conditions (0.5% O₂, 5% CO₂) for 4 h to simulate ischemia. After this hypoxic phase, glucose was added to a final concentration of 11 mM (A2494001, Gibco/Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the cells were reoxygenated by incubating them at 37℃ in a 5% CO₂ environment for an additional 24 h to mimic reperfusion. In treatment groups, α-LA was administered at concentrations of 50 or 100 µM immediately after the hypoxic phase to assess its neuroprotective and antioxidant effects under OGD/R conditions.

Western blot analysis

Brain tissues were homogenized using T-PER™ Tissue Protein Extraction Reagent (78510; Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (cOmplete Mini, EDTA-free; Roche Applied Science, Germany) and PhosSTOP EASY (Roche Applied Science). BV2 and HT22 cells were lysed with radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (EBA-1149, ELPIS BIOTECH, Daejeon, South Korea). Samples were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 30 min at 4℃. Protein concentrations were measured using the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were loaded onto 10–15% SDS-PAGE gels for electrophoresis and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. Immunoreactive bands were detected using the SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (34096, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and band intensities were analyzed using Scion Image software and the FUSIONSOLO5 imaging system (KOREA BIOMICS, Korea). The following primary antibodies were used: anti-postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD95; sc-32290, Santa Cruz), anti-brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF; ab108319, Abcam), anti-occludin (sc-133256, Santa Cruz), anti-claudin 5 (35–2500, Invitrogen), anti-ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba-1; ab118847, Abcam), anti-poly ADP ribose polymerase (PARP; 9542 S, cell signaling), anti-cleaved caspase-3 9664 S, Cell signaling), anti-nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2; ab62352, Abcam), anti-heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1; ab13243, Abcam), anti-NAD(P)H quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1; sc-32793, Santa Cruz), and anti-β-actin (2118, Cell Signaling Technology). Secondary antibodies included HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (1:1000, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000, Santa Cruz Biotechnology).

Brain tissue preparation

Brain tissues were collected 15 days after BCCAO/R surgery, following the completion of cognitive assessments. Mice were anesthetized and perfused transcardially with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for 4 h at 4 ℃. The brains were washed three times with PBS, immersed in 30% sucrose solution for 24 h at 4 ℃ for cryoprotection. Tissues were then embedded in Leica Surgipath FCS 22 Clear frozen section compound (#3801480, Leica Biosystems, Germany) and stored at − 80 ℃ until sectioning. Coronal brain Sect. (20 μm thickness) were prepared using a cryostat (Leica CM1950, Germany) and mounted on glass slides for immunofluorescence analysis.

Immunofluorescence staining

Coronal brain Sect. (20 μm) were air-dried and rinsed with PBS. Sections were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin in PBS for 24 h at 25 ℃. The samples were incubated for 24 h at 4 ℃ with the following primary antibodies: NeuN (Millipore, #MAB377; 1:200) and Cleaved caspase-3 (Cell Signaling Technology, #9664; 1:200). After washing, slides were incubated for 1 h at 25 ℃ with secondary antibodies: Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 488 (Invitrogen, #A11001), and Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 568 (Invitrogen, #A11011). The slides were coverslipped with antifade mounting medium. Images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica Stellaris 5, Leica Microsystems, Germany) at 10× or 40× magnification. Fluorescence intensity and co-localization were analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Antioxidant response element (ARE) luciferase assay

HT22 cells were transfected with the pGL4.37[luc2P/ARE/Hyg] vector (E3641, Promega, Madison, WI, USA) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Stable HT22-ARE cell lines were established by selecting transfected cells with 0.8 mM hygromycin after three passages. For the luciferase assay, HT22-ARE cells (4 × 10⁵ cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, cells were treated with α-LA for an additional 24 h. Post-treatment, cells were lysed using Reporter Lysis Buffer (Promega), and the lysates were mixed with luciferase assay substrate (Promega). Luminescence was measured using a white 96-well plate and a microplate reader (Tecan, Infinite M200 Pro Hombrechtikon, Switzerland). Luciferase activity was normalized to total protein content as determined by the Bradford assay (#5000006; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

Cell viability assay

Cell viability following H/R-induced neurotoxicity was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; CK-04–13, Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan). Cells exposed to hypoxia conditions, with or without α-LA treatment, were incubated with the CCK-8 reagent, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Sunrise, Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland).

Intracellular ROS measurement

Intracellular oxidative stress was assessed using 2′,7′-dichlorofuorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA; Sigma Aldrich), a non-fluorescent probe that, once hydrolyzed intracellularly, is oxidized by ROS to generate the fluorescent product 2′, 7′-dichlorofuorescein. Cells were seeded in a black-bottom 96-well plate (Nunc, Rochester, NY, USA) at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with α-LA (100 µM) for an additional 24 h. Following treatment, cells exposed to the OGD/R protocol were incubated with H2DCFDA (20 µM) for 30 min. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader (Infinite 200; Tecan, Grodig, Austria) with excitation and emission wavelengths set at 485 nm and 535 nm, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Group comparisons were assessed using a one-way analysis of variance, followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant and indicated by an asterisk (*).

Results

Infarct size and the effects of α-LA on BBB integrity in the BCCAO/R mouse model

The α-LA treatment group received daily intraperitoneal injections of α-LA for 2 weeks following BCCAO/R surgery, while the control group received vehicle injections (Fig. 1A). TTC staining revealed that BCCAO/R produced small infarcts. Although α-LA-treated mice showed a trend toward smaller infarct sizes compared to untreated BCCAO/R mice, the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 1B, C).

To assess whether α-LA helps maintain the integrity of the BBB, we evaluated the expression levels of the tight junction proteins occludin and claudin-5 in the cortex and hippocampus (Fig. 1D–I). BCCAO/R significantly decreased the expression of occludin and claudin-5 in the hippocampus. In contrast, the decrease in claudin-5 expression in the cortex was not statistically significant. Notably, α-LA treatment markedly restored the expression of occludin and claudin-5 in the hippocampus. These findings indicate that α-LA promotes region-specific restoration of BBB integrity.

Effects of α-LA on cognitive function and synaptic plasticity in the BCCAO/R mouse model

To further evaluate cognitive function and exploratory behavior, the Y-maze test was performed following BCCAO/R surgery (Fig. 5A–C). BCCAO/R led to a significant decline in spatial memory, demonstrated by a reduced percentage of spontaneous alternations. However, treatment with α-LA significantly increased the percentage of alternation triplets compared to the untreated group, indicating improved cognitive performance. Notably, α-LA had no significant effect on the total distance traveled. During the Y-maze test, anxiety-related behaviors such as rearing, circling, and freezing showed no significant differences between groups. Similarly, locomotor activity, measured as total distance, did not differ significantly, indicating comparable overall activity levels. Therefore, the observed improvement in spontaneous alternation is unlikely to be attributable to differences in anxiety or general activity. In the hippocampus, BCCAO/R significantly reduced PSD-95 expression without affecting BDNF levels (Fig. 5G-I). In contrast, no significant changes in PSD-95 or BDNF expression were observed in the cortex, and α-LA treatment did not restore these markers (Fig. 5D-F). These findings indicate that the cognitive improvements conferred by α-LA are not mediated by changes in synaptic plasticity-related proteins.

Effects of α-LA on neuronal survival and apoptosis in the hippocampus following BCCAO/R

Immunofluorescence staining for NeuN and cleaved caspase-3 was performed in the hippocampal CA1 region to evaluate neuronal survival and apoptosis (Fig. 5A). The NeuN antibody specifically recognizes the DNA-binding, neuron-specific protein NeuN, a well-established marker of mature neurons, whereas cleaved caspase-3 serves as a key indicator of apoptotic activation. No significant differences in NeuN immunoreactivity were observed among the groups (graph not shown). In contrast, cleaved caspase-3 fluorescence intensity was markedly increased in the BCCAO/R group compared with the sham group, indicating enhanced apoptotic activity. α-LA treatment restored NeuN-positive neuronal density and markedly reduced cleaved caspase-3 expression, demonstrating its neuroprotective and anti-apoptotic effects. Quantitative fluorescence analysis further confirmed that cleaved caspase-3 intensity was significantly higher in the BCCAO/R group than in the sham group and was significantly decreased following α-LA administration (Fig. 5B). Data are presented as fold change relative to the BCCAO/R group (mean ± SD, n = 3 per group).

Effects of α-LA on HO-1 and NQO1 production in the BCCAO/R animal model

In the BCCAO/R model, the expression of NQO1 was markedly reduced compared to the sham group. Treatment with α-LA significantly restored the expression of NQO1 (Fig. 5A–F). Region-specific analysis revealed that these changes were prominent in the hippocampus, while no significant alterations were observed in the cortex. These findings suggest that α-LA exerts its antioxidant protective effect primarily by activating the Nrf2 pathway and upregulating the expression of NQO1, particularly in the hippocampal region.

Antioxidant effect of α-LA in the OGD/R cell model

To evaluate the antioxidant effects of α-LA in HT-22 hippocampal neuronal cells, cells were treated with α-LA under both normal and OGD/R conditions. Cells were treated with either 50 or 100 µM α-LA for 24 h according to previously reported protocols for neuroprotective or antioxidant assays18–21. Under normal conditions, α-LA at treatment concentrations (6.25–100 µM) did not significantly affect cell viability, indicating an absence of cytotoxicity (Fig. 5A). In contrast, OGD/R exposure significantly reduced cell viability, which was partially restored by α-LA in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5B). Oxidative stress was assessed via DCFDA fluorescence staining to measure ROS production. OGD/R markedly increased ROS levels, while α-LA treatment (100 µM) markedly reduced ROS levels, confirming its antioxidant effects (Fig. 5C). Additionally, OGD/R induced the expression of cleaved caspase-3 and PARP, markers of apoptosis, which were attenuated by α-LA treatment, indicating anti-apoptotic effects (Fig. 5D–F).

Mechanism of the antioxidant effect of α-LA in the OGD/R cell model

To further explore the antioxidant response mediated by Nrf2, ARE-luciferase activity was assessed following α-LA treatment. α-LA significantly increased ARE activity (Fig. 6A). Western blot analysis revealed that α-LA treatment increased nuclear Nrf2 levels, as confirmed using nuclear extract fractions (Fig. 6B, C). Under OGD/R conditions, the expression of HO-1 and NQO1 remained unchanged compared to normal conditions; however, α-LA treatment led to a statistically significant upregulation of both proteins (Fig. 6D-F). These findings suggest that α-LA exerts its antioxidant protective effects by activating the Nrf2/ARE signaling pathway and promoting the expression of HO-1 and NQO1.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that α-LA treatment mitigates cognitive decline after global cerebral ischemia in the BCCAO/R model and reduces oxidative stress in the OGD/R model, primarily by activating the Nrf2/ARE signaling pathway and upregulating HO-1 and NQO1 expression. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of α-LA as a neuroprotective agent for brain I/R injury by enhancing endogenous antioxidant defense mechanisms.

The BCCAO/R surgical model used in this study involved transient occlusion of the CCA followed by reperfusion, which led to cognitive decline without causing overt infarction. In the Y-maze test, α-LA treatment significantly improved spatial memory performance without affecting general exploratory behavior. This finding suggests that the effects of α-LA treatment are specific to cognitive function rather than reflecting broad behavioral changes. Since the cerebral cortex primarily mediates general exploratory activity, whereas spatial memory is highly dependent on hippocampal function, these results indicate that α-LA specifically preserved hippocampal integrity following ischemic injury.

Consistent with these behavioral findings, our immunofluorescence analysis revealed that α-LA exerted a direct neuroprotective effect in the hippocampal CA1 region. NeuN staining showed preservation of neuronal integrity, whereas cleaved caspase-3, a key executioner of apoptosis, was markedly increased following BCCAO/R and significantly reduced by α-LA treatment. These results indicate that α-LA effectively attenuates ischemia-induced neuronal apoptosis and contributes to the maintenance of hippocampal neuronal viability. This finding supports the hypothesis that the cognitive improvement observed after α-LA administration is mediated, at least in part, by its anti-apoptotic activity in vulnerable hippocampal neurons.

To determine whether the Nrf2-dependent antioxidant and BBB-stabilizing effects of α-LA are region-selective, we analyzed the prefrontal cortex as a lower-vulnerability comparator in the mild BCCAO/R model. In this model, even with brief CCA occlusion, PSD-95 expression was significantly reduced in the hippocampus but not in the cortex, suggesting a selective vulnerability of the hippocampus22. Notably, α-LA treatment did not significantly restore the expression of synaptic plasticity markers such as PSD-95 or BDNF in either region, suggesting that its cognitive benefits are not directly mediated by enhancing synaptic plasticity-related proteins. Instead, the improvement in cognitive function appears to be driven by the potent antioxidant properties of α-LA. The marked increase in cleaved caspase-3 immunoreactivity in the hippocampal CA1 region after BCCAO/R suggests activation of the apoptotic cascade in vulnerable neurons. Caspase-3 is a key executioner protease that mediates the proteolytic degradation of cytoskeletal and structural proteins during apoptosis23. Notably, NeuN staining revealed no significant differences in neuronal density among the three groups, indicating that overt neuronal loss had not yet occurred and that neurons were likely undergoing functional impairment or were in the early stages of apoptotic signaling24. Thus, the increase in cleaved caspase-3 reflects the initiation of apoptosis prior to detectable structural damage25. α-LA administration markedly reduced cleaved caspase-3 activation, indicating suppression of apoptotic progression. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have shown that α-LA attenuates MPP⁺-induced apoptosis in PC12 cells by reducing intracellular ROS levels26. Moreover, another study demonstrated that α-LA increases mitochondrial membrane potential, enhances ATP production, and decreases ROS accumulation in SH-SY5Y cells overexpressing the APP695 gene27. Therefore, our results suggest that α-LA provides neuroprotective effects by modulating the apoptotic process, thereby supporting hippocampal neuronal viability. Mechanistically, α-LA activates the Nrf2/ARE signaling pathway, upregulating downstream antioxidant enzymes, including HO-1 and NQO1. This activation appears to protect hippocampal neurons from oxidative damage and preserve mitochondrial function. Given the essential role of ROS in disrupting ion channel function during I/R injury, α-LA may also have contributed to cognitive protection by stabilizing ion channels such as voltage-dependent Ca²⁺ channels, K⁺ channels, and NMDA receptors28,29.

Additionally, α-LA treatment restored the expression of tight junction proteins, including claudin-5 and occludin, which were disrupted by I/R injury. These proteins are crucial components of tight junctions that regulate paracellular permeability and maintain the selective barrier function of the BBB30. Their dysfunction or loss during ischemic events increases vascular permeability, promotes neuroinflammation, and contributes to secondary neuronal damage31. By preserving the integrity of tight junctions, α-LA may also exert anti-inflammatory effects, potentially limiting peripheral immune cell infiltration and reducing the progression of secondary inflammatory cascades. Overall, the findings of this study underscore the therapeutic potential of α-LA in alleviating I/R-induced brain damage and cognitive decline. By reducing oxidative stress, preserving mitochondrial and BBB integrity, and stabilizing hippocampal function, α-LA demonstrates multifaceted antioxidant properties. These attributes position it as a promising candidate for targeting the complex pathophysiological mechanisms underlying global cerebral ischemia.

This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, we focused on the late neuroprotective effects assessed at 7 and 14 days after α-LA treatment in the BCCAO/R model, which may not fully capture early-phase injury mechanisms. Simultaneously, the observed decrease in PSD-95 could reflect neuronal loss or synaptic scaffold/spine remodeling. Because cognition improved even without restoration of PSD-95/BDNF, the causal relationship remains unclear. Functional synaptic plasticity, postsynaptic receptor phosphorylation (GluA1, NR2A/NR2B), and dendritic spine structure were not assessed. Future studies will integrate early-stage analyses of apoptotic signaling pathways together with electrophysiological analyses to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the cognitive improvements. Second, we did not directly assess hippocampal cell death at day 14. In future studies, we will evaluate neuronal integrity at 14 days post-reperfusion using Nissl staining and quantify apoptotic cell death using the TUNEL assay, thereby providing a more comprehensive evaluation of delayed tissue injury and the potential recovery induced by α-LA. Third, although α‑LA improved cognitive function and partially restored tight‑junction proteins (occludin, claudin‑5) after BCCAO/R, the mechanistic basis remains unresolved, including whether the observed improvement in cognitive function reflects enhanced neuronal survival, neurotransmitter regulation, or other neuroprotective pathways beyond antioxidant activity. These questions will be addressed in future mechanistic studies of BBB‑regulatory signaling.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that α-LA offers neuroprotective effects in I/R injury associated with global cerebral ischemia. α-LA enhanced cognitive function and preserved BBB integrity, potentially through its antioxidant properties. Notably, α-LA upregulated NQO1 expression in the hippocampus, helping to reduce oxidative stress while also inhibiting microglial activation and NF-κB signaling pathways. Although these findings support the therapeutic potential of α-LA for ischemic brain injury, further studies are needed to clarify its underlying mechanisms, assess acute and long-term effects, and validate its efficacy in clinically relevant models. Overall, α-LA represents a promising candidate for treating ischemic brain injury and merits further investigation for future clinical application.
