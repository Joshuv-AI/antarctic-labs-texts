Occupational injury among firefighters in Canada: a trends analysis of fatality and time-loss injury claims (2007–2021)
Authors: Samar Al-Hajj, Larry Thomas, Len Garis, Khalil El-Asmar, Farah El-Hajj, Ian Pike
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOccupational injury accounts for the highest rate of morbidity and mortality among firefighters, surpassing other occupations. Canada is particularly susceptible to fires and consequently increased health burden. This study examines trends in firefighters’ injury and death claims across Canada, focusing on claim characteristics, mechanisms of injury and disease, and geographic distribution across provinces and territories.MethodsData on accepted fatality and time-loss injury claims were collected from the Association of Workers’ Compensation Boards of Canada (AWCBC) and WorkSafeBC over 15 years (2007–2021). Linear regression models were used to assess trends in time-loss injury and fatality claims. Chi-square tests were conducted to analyze the distribution of fatalities and injuries across age groups.ResultsA total of 29,499 claims were analyzed, including 1,509 fatalities and 27,990 time-loss injuries, with males representing 92.2% of injury and 99.4% of fatality claims. Cancer was the leading cause of fatalities, accounting for 84.6% of claims with a fatality rate of 993.7 per 100,000 firefighters per year. Traumatic injuries were the primary cause of time-loss claims, comprising 80.6% of cases with a rate of 17,859.1 per 100,000 firefighters per year. Mental health-related claims saw a significant 231.2% increase. The “65 and over” age group had the highest fatality claims (n = 826, 54.7%), with statistically significant differences observed in the distribution of fatalities and injuries between age groups (p = 0.002 < 0.05). Ontario reported the most fatalities (n = 713, 47.3%) and time-loss claims (n = 8591, 30.7%), followed by Quebec, British Columbia, and Alberta.ConclusionThis study highlights the increasing burden of cancer among Canadian firefighters and the sharp rise in mental health-related claims, emphasizing the need for further research into the occupational health risks faced by firefighters to improve their long-term well-being.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40621-025-00639-0.Keywords: Firefighters, Occupational injuries, Non-communicable disease, Epidemiology

Introduction

Occupational injuries account for the highest rate of morbidity and mortality among firefighters, surpassing other occupations [1–3]. Firefighting is widely recognized as a hazardous profession, exposing firefighters to continuous physical and psychological stress as frontline responders in various emergencies, including explosions, natural disasters, wildfires, and traffic collisions [4]. Globally, the mortality rate for firefighters exceeds that of other occupations as firefighters face numerous challenges that extend beyond the immediate dangers of exposure to severe temperatures, hazardous chemicals, and fumes [1–3].

Further to the persistent risk of occupational injuries, firefighters suffer from the real threat of Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs) that pose a long-term concern to their health and well-being, with an increased risk for developing cancer, cardiovascular diseases (CVD), and mental health disorders [5]. Work, fatigue, and sleep disorders, coupled with long shifts and low fitness levels, have been identified as major contributors to the increasing prevalence of injuries and disorders sustained by firefighters [6–9]. These injuries and associated disorders often result in chronic pain and inflammation, negatively impacting the musculoskeletal health and overall occupational performance of firefighters [10]. Transportation to and from emergency scenes poses an additional risk of sustaining injuries among firefighters, emerging as the second most frequent cause of on-duty firefighter fatalities in the USA in 2022 [9, 11, 12]. Previous research has associated the burden of transportation-related injuries among emergency first responders with fatigue, speeding, and non-compliance with occupant restraints [8, 13].

Prior research has extensively documented a significant correlation between firefighters’ exposure to toxins and the prevalence of NCDs [14–17]. Prolonged inhalation of toxic chemicals present in fires, smoke, or combustion, as well as those found in firefighting gear, including flame retardants, are linked to the heightened incidence of cancer and CVD, including sudden cardiac arrests, among firefighters [14–20]. Chronic stress and other stressors further complicate the long-term health burden on firefighters. Significant concern arises regarding the heightened susceptibility of public service workers and emergency first responders, particularly firefighters, to cardiovascular diseases (CVD) [21–26]. Despite these major health concerns, low screening rates for physical diseases align with the low mental health screenings and follow-ups among public service and emergency responders, including firefighters [21–26]. Studies consistently highlight the significantly higher rates of mental illnesses within this group compared to the general population, mainly associated with sleep disorders, depression, burnout, substance abuse, post-traumatic stress injury, and suicidal ideation, planning, and attempts [21–26].

Canada, with 9% of the world’s forests, together with the added burden of structural fires, is particularly susceptible to fires and consequently increased occupational morbidity and mortality among its firefighters [27, 28]. While current data indicates a relatively low burden of occupational injuries and NCDs among Canadian firefighters compared to other countries, a limited number of studies compare and assess their health status using consistent health metrics. Earlier studies examined the increased prevalence of traumatic injuries among Canadian firefighters [27–29]. The current study aims to describe national and provincial trends, plus age- and sex-specific distributions, of accepted time-loss and fatality claims among Canadian firefighters from 1997 to 2011. Findings from this study will provide insights into developing comprehensive approaches and strategies to address the short- and long-term health and safety needs of firefighters, to improve their longer-term health and well–being.

Materials and methods

Study design

This is a secondary data analysis of de-identified injury- and fatality-related claims data accepted over a period of 15 years spanning from 2007 to 2021. Data were retrieved from 2 sources: The Association of Workers’ Compensation Boards of Canada (AWCBC) and WorkSafeBC – for additional provincial data from British Columbia (BC). The compiled dataset encompasses national data from the twelve provinces and territories across Canada. Due to smaller numbers, data from the Northwest Territories and Nunavut are combined into one dataset. The retrieved data consisted of three cohorts covering different time periods: 2007–2017, 2007–2018, and 2018–2021. Each time period included two datasets, one database for accepted fatality claims and the other for accepted time-loss injury claims. Duplicate values were removed across all datasets to avoid redundancy due to overlapping years.

Data management and analysis

The compiled data were converted in Excel into columns that represented the province, year, age, sex, and the particular nature of injury that resulted in a fatality or time-loss injury claim. The time-loss claims refer only to accepted claims for time-loss submitted by firefighters and not all claims related to injuries or fatalities occurring at the workplace. Claims data comprise firefighters’ demographic information (i.e., age and gender), claim year, claim type (time loss, fatality), claim geographic sources (i.e., province), injury event, nature of injury, and body part injured.

The initial databases included ‘x’ as a replacement for all values 1, 2, or 3 injuries or fatalities in an attempt to safeguard firefighters’ data privacy and confidentiality. Following the random-rounding approach recommended by Statistics Canada for small-cell suppression each ‘x’ was randomly replaced with an integer between 1 and 3 using STATA to minimise information loss while protecting privacy [Statistics Canada, 2021]. For the third cohort (2018–2021), the subcategories of the nature of injuries were aggregated into an upper main category based on the first two digits displayed in their codes (Additional file 1) to ensure standardization and to facilitate the merging of the two databases. After removing duplicates for the year 2018, the merged dataset was analyzed to identify the trend over time of accepted fatality and injury claims. The data were combined by province, year, age, or nature of injury, and exported to Excel to generate the tables and graphs representing patterns and trends. It’s worth noting that the compensability criteria and claim adjucation processes used in Workers’ compensation system vary across Canadian provinces.

Because the workers’ compensation database is claim based and lacks unique personal identifiers, the 27 990 accepted time-loss claims may include more than one claim from the same firefighter. Counts should therefore be interpreted as injury events rather than unique individuals.

Linear regression models were developed to determine the significance of linear trends in time-loss injury and fatality claims over the 15-year period. We modelled yearly claim counts with a simple linear regression, where is the number of accepted claims in year t and Year is coded as a continuous variable with 1997 = 0. No other predictors were entered, so the model is univariate and serves only to describe the linear trend across the observation window. To evaluate the fit for the linear regression model, we report the R-squared statistic, which quantifies the proportion of variance in claim counts explained by the model.

Alpha was set at p ≤ 0.05 level of significance. Additionally, Chi-square tests were conducted for departure from trend to assess if there is a statistically significant difference in the distribution of fatalities and injuries across age groups (Alpha: p ≤ 0.05 level of significance).

Rate calculations

Fatality and time-loss injury rates were calculated for the years 2007–2021. Annual counts of firefighter fatalities and injuries across all Canadian provinces were summed to obtain total numbers for the study period. The denominator used was the total number of firefighters in Canada (126,300)[https://cafc.ca]. Rates were expressed as annualized rates per 100,000 firefighters per year by dividing the total number of fatalities or injuries by the total number of firefighters, dividing by the number of years in the study period (2007–2021), and multiplying by 100,000. These rates therefore represent the average annual number of fatalities or time-loss injuries per 100,000 firefighters per year, rather than cumulative rates.

Results

Sample profile

Between 2007 and 2021, Canadian firefighters reported a total of 29,499 claims with 1,509 fatalities and 27,990 time-loss claims. The majority of the claims were submitted by males, with 99.40% of the fatality claims and 92.24% of the injury claims resulting from male firefighters. Regional differences were observed, with Ontario reporting the highest number of claims, accounting for 47.25% of the fatality claims and 30.69% of the injury claims. In terms of age distribution, fatality claims were more common among firefighters aged 65 and over, while injury claims were most prevalent among those aged 30 to 49 (Fig. 1) (Table 1).

Fatality claims

During the 15-year period, cancer was the leading cause of fatalities among firefighters, accounting for 84.6% of claims and a fatality rate of 993.7 per 100,000 firefighters per year (Fig. 2) (Additional Table S1). The data shows a clear increase in fatality claims with increasing age among Canadian firefighters. Figure 1 visually underscores the mid-life peak of claims, which is lower in the younger and older age groups, reflecting a possible combination of reduced workforce numbers in those age brackets and potentially different coping mechanisms or support systems as firefighters age.

The highest number of fatality claims, 826 deaths representing 54.7% of all deaths, occurred in the “65 and over” age group. The test for departure from the trendline showed a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05) in fatality distribution between age groups.

Time-loss injury claims

Traumatic injuries were the most common, making up over 80% of claims at a rate of 17,859.1 per 100,000 firefighters per year. Mental health-related issues accounted for nearly 6% of claims. Additional Table S2 shows the breakdown of time-loss claims by Canadian firefighters based on the nature of injury from 2007 to 2021.

The percentage of total time-loss injury claims among Canadian firefighters from 2007 to 2021 follows a bell-shaped curve, peaking in the mid-life age groups (30 to 49 years) (Fig. 1). We notice a decrease in both the younger and older age groups. A chi-square test for departure indicates no statistically significant difference in injury distribution between age groups (p > 0.05).

Mental health

In 2007, there were 26 injury claims related to mental health, and the number fluctuated every year until a significant increase emerged in 2016, reaching 411 claims in 2021. This data indicates a substantial positive linear trend in time-loss injury claims related to mental disorders among Canadian firefighters during this period (Fig. 3).

Looking at the distribution across Canadian provinces, the number of mental health cases reported in each province was relatively low during the initial years of the study (2007–2010) and increased only slightly. However, starting in 2011, there was a more noticeable upward trend in all provinces, which increased significantly in 2016, with Ontario showing a particularly sharp rise. illustrates a significant positive linear trend in time-loss injury claims from mental disorders across Canadian provinces during this time period.

Discussion

This study assessed the burden of injuries and NCDs among firefighters in Canada. While expanding on previous research work, this study underscores the high prevalence of injury and disease mortality and morbidity in the firefighters’ population and highlights the longer-term impacts on their lives and well-being.

Despite rigorous training and the utilization of personal protective equipment (PPE), there remains a significant probability of sustaining occupational injury among firefighters [30]. This study confirms the high risk associated with the occupation of firefighting on physical and psychological well-being. Traumatic injury was the most common type, making up over 80% of claims while mental health-related issues accounted for nearly 6% of claims, a clear increase from previous years [1].

Consistent with findings from previous literature, this study indicates the prevalence of musculoskeletal system injuries sustained by firefighters. Similar trends are observed globally, with overexertion, followed by slips, trips, and falls emerging as the predominant causes of fatal and non-fatal injuries among firefighters [9, 31–33]. Strains and sprains, primarily affecting the back and extremities, are the most frequently reported injuries, with a French-based study revealing that 98% of its participants suffered strains [7, 9, 32, 34, 35].

Several studies have documented that firefighters are not only directly exposed to fire and explosive hazards that may lead to burns, but also face numerous physical challenges on the job. These include carrying heavy equipment, navigating slippery and uneven surfaces, encountering fall hazards, and working in conditions with poor visibility. Firefighters are also frequently required to adopt stress-inducing postures such as lifting and bending [34, 36–39]. These factors collectively contribute to their heightened vulnerability to musculoskeletal injuries compared to individuals in other occupations [38, 40]. This susceptibility is further pronounced in older, obese, and inactive firefighters, with full-time firefighters in the USA being nearly five times more likely to suffer musculoskeletal injuries compared to other full-time jobs [6, 7, 9, 41–43].

This study suggests that cancer is the primary cause of death among firefighters. This finding confirms previous evidence indicating that firefighting as an occupation is not only associated with an increased incidence of cancer but also with a higher mortality rate due to the disease [44]. A study found that firefighters with 15 or more years of service had almost twice the odds of developing cancer, irrespective of age, knowing that only 14% of the study cohort underwent cancer screening in the previous three years of the study [45]. This low screening rate underscores the significant impact of inadequate routine health follow-ups on the health burden faced by firefighters, resulting in more frequent time-loss claims and poorer health outcomes [46].

This study reported high rates of CVDs among firefighters. Recent investigations underscore that firefighters face the highest mortality rate attributed to sudden cardiac arrests among emergency services workers [6]. This elevated prevalence is influenced by a combination of occupational factors, underlying health conditions, lifestyle, and family history [47]. Existing literature established a correlation between prevalent health conditions among firefighters, such as obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and musculoskeletal disorders, and an increased risk of CVD [7, 10, 48, 49]. One study further revealed that 68% of firefighters had two or more risk factors for CVD [48]. A US study reported that 77% of cardiac fatalities occurred during the on-duty responses of firefighters, in contrast to non-emergency duties, and compared to a documented 51% average among all on-duty public service workers [6, 50]. Another US study revealed that firefighters have been reported to be 12.1 to 136 times more prone to death from CVD during emergency response compared to non-emergency duties [51]. The heightened risk during on-duty responses is linked to increased susceptibility to extreme heat, increased annual fire exposure, career durations, as well as stress and overexertion [52, 53]. An extensive investigation of the cases over the years 2002 to 2012 reported that 98% of cardiac fatalities among US firefighters were attributed to stress and overexertion [54]. Chronic stress is also exacerbated by extended working hours and shift work with altered circadian rhythms [44, 55, 56]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), long working hours pose an independent risk for heart disease and stroke [57]. Firefighters, often work 24-hour shifts with a total of over 50 weekly working hours, surpassing the 40-hour weekly average of other occupations [58]. Moreover, age emerges as a significant risk factor. Studies reveal that firefighters aged over 40–45 years are particularly prone to CVD [49, 50, 59]. Notably, the average age of firefighters in Australia is 44 years, 38.6 years in the USA, and 41 years in the UK, reflecting the age-related vulnerability of this occupation in these three high-income countries [11, 60, 61].

This study documented the increased incidence of mental health among firefighters by 231.20% in time-loss claims. Aligning with existing literature, this study indicated a substantial positive linear trend in time-loss injury claims related to mental disorders among Canadian firefighters over the 15-year period from 2007 to 2021. Several studies underscore the link between depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), sleep issues, and mental health [60–62], revealing that firefighters with sleep problems are almost twice as likely to develop depression, anxiety, and other mental health issues compared to those without sleep issues [62]. Another study found that firefighters predisposed to PTSD and alcohol abuse were more than 12 and 5 times more likely to develop depression, respectively [63]. Evidence reported a burden of depression among firefighters, reaching 23% among Chinese firefighters and 27% among USA firefighters [64, 65]. PTSD is a recurring concern across studies due to the trauma firefighters are exposed to throughout their careers, indicating increased odds of developing multiple associated mental illnesses, including suicidal ideation, planning, and attempts [21, 23, 66, 67]. Emergency Medical Technicians, including firefighters and paramedics, face 1.39 times higher odds of suicidal mortality in the USA compared to the general population [68].

This study has strengths and limitations. A key strength of this study is the large cohort that spans over 15 years and encompasses all Canadian provinces and territories. This provides an in-depth view and a greater understanding of the current burden of injuries and NCDs within the firefighter community in Canada. Including such a comprehensive database enhances the generalizability of the study findings and implications to similar populations and occupational settings. The main limitation of the study is the fact that the databases included only accepted firefighters’ occupational injury and mortality claims. Injury claims that are not reported, claimed, or rejected are not included in this study. A small number of claims reported in recent years that are still open and being adjudicated are not included in this study. Excluding rejected claims may underestimate the actual incidence of specific health issues and underestimate firefighters’ health burden. A second important limitation is the absence of reliable denominators. National files do not record the yearly number of active firefighters, full-time equivalents, or hours worked. As a result, we report counts of accepted claims rather than rates. The figures therefore reflect the burden of compensated events, not individual risk. If the size or composition of the workforce changed during the study years, our trend estimates could be biased upward or downward. Third, the study reports raw counts rather than rates and does not adjust for changes in workforce size, exposure, or demographic shifts over time, which may overstate the magnitude of observed trends. Also, the absence of comparative data from other occupations limits the ability to contextualize whether the observed patterns are unique to firefighters or part of broader labor market trends. Fourth, the trend analyses were conducted using univariate regression models with year as the sole explanatory variable. While statistical significance was found across outcomes, the practical significance and implications for prioritizing interventions remain unclear. The current data limit the ability to calculate age adjusted rates by occupations. There is a need for age-specific injury and fatality rate, rather than counts to ensure rigourous analysis. We propose the use of workforce-adjusted denominators in future analyses and to benefit from advanced modeling of injury and fatality rates using relevant covariates including gender, age groups and occupational categories to enable a detailed assessement of the unique risk factors associated with the occupation of firefighting. Finally, this study measures the burden of accepted injury claims, not the incidence of injured firefighters. Individual firefighters could file multiple accepted claims during the 15-year period, which may over-represent those with recurrent injuries. While this does not affect the observed linear trends in claim counts, it prevents estimation of person-level risk and may bias age- or sex-specific distributions toward groups with higher recurrence. These methodological constraints underscore the need for cautious interpretation of the findings. Another limitation, the high prevalence of occupational cancer and mental health claims might be related to the increased reporting of these claims with the recent Canadian presumptive policies. These presumptive policies play a signifant role in firefighters claims by simplifying the adjucation process and defering claims approvals to the Workers Compensation Board (WCB); they largely shifted the work related proof burden from the workers to the WCB, which may have led to an increase in reporting and acceptance rates for claims related to occupational cancer, infectious diseases and mental health conditions.
