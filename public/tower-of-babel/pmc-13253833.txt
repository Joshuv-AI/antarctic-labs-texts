Sparseness facilitates image encoding across visuo-frontal networks in freely moving macaque
Authors: Arun Parajuli, Melissa Franch, Valentin Dragoi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSparseness is a quantitative measure of coding efficiency that characterizes the extent to which information is represented by the selective activation of a small subset of neurons. However, although highly influential, this general principle of sensory coding has only been tested under restrictive experimental conditions whereby stimuli were presented on a computer screen while animals were physically restrained. Here, we wirelessly recorded the spiking activity of populations of neurons in visual (area V4) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) in conjunction with oculomotor events while freely moving male macaques inspected their environment. A convolutional autoencoder was trained to reduce the dimensionality of fixated natural stimuli to help examine the structure of population activity in each cortical area and its contribution to stimulus encoding in different states of wakefulness. We found a sparseness-based connectivity rule between neurons in different cortical areas whereby V4 and dlPFC neurons with high degrees of sparseness are more strongly coupled. The highly sparse neurons in both areas had a higher contribution to stimulus encoding during active relative to passive wakefulness. Our results indicate that sparsification constitutes a general principle underlying population coding across sensory and executive cortical circuits during unrestrained environmental exploration.Subject terms: Visual system, Neural circuits

Introduction

It has long been proposed that the brain optimizes the efficiency of information processing by using sparse representations of natural stimuli, with lifetime sparseness describing the selective firing of individual neurons and population sparseness describing the small fraction of neurons active for a given stimulus1–3. However, most experimental evidence of sparse coding has originated from studies examining the responses of individual neurons, primarily in sensory areas, exposed to a large set of stimuli presented on a computer screen under physically restrained conditions. Furthermore, previous sparseness studies have focused on individual cell responses, but whether populations of neurons in multiple brain areas exhibit sparseness has not been explored. Indeed, finding that the responses of single neurons are sparse does not guarantee sparseness in a population of cells4. Studies investigating population sparseness have relied on two-photon imaging studies in rodent4 and monkey5 visual cortex. However, while calcium imaging does provide access to a large number of cells simultaneously, spiking activity is reported only indirectly, resulting in a low-pass filtered, delayed, and transformed version of the neural response. Calcium imaging also has lower signal-to-noise ratio for detecting spikes and limited dynamic range6, which altogether may complicate our understanding of the relationship between neural activity and sparseness.

Here, we developed a novel approach that combines behavioral and wireless eye tracking and neural monitoring to record populations of neurons in visual (area V4) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) while monkeys are freely moving to visually inspect their natural environment. A Convolutional Autoencoder (CA) was trained to represent the scene camera video frames in a low-dimensional vector space, and then identify fixation clusters with similar visual content around a selected “seed” fixation frame. This allowed us to examine the degree of population sparseness and visual information encoded by neural populations during quiet and active wakefulness. We found a sparseness-based connectivity rule between neurons in different cortical areas, allowing highly sparse neurons in both areas to contribute most to stimulus encoding during active relative to passive wakefulness.

Results

We recorded the electrical activity of populations of neurons in mid-level visual cortex (area V4) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (area dlPFC) while monkeys were moving freely and inspecting their environment (see “Methods”). Each monkey (n = 2) had a 96-channel multielectrode dual array implanted in V4 and dlPFC (n = 25 sessions, N = 3442 single and multi-units, see “Methods”; Fig. 1c and Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). A head-mounted integrated wireless system allowed us to simultaneously monitor animal’s gaze and pupil diameter with an eye camera, record the visual field using a scene camera, observe behavior with an overhead camera, and conduct large-scale electrophysiology (recording sessions lasted 1.48 ± 0.09 h, mean ± s.e.m., see “Methods”; Figs. 1c, d and 2a–c, and Supplementary Figs. 1, 3 and 4). Oculomotor and scene-camera information were wirelessly transmitted to a dedicated eye tracking computer (Supplementary Fig. 3). The overhead camera monitored monkey’s movements at 30 frames/s and transmitted it to a digital video recorder.

Dimensionality reduction using convolutional autoencoders

Visual fixations during free viewing were identified using the eye position and velocity data captured by the eye camera in the epochs when the animal was stationary (see “Methods,” Fig. 2a, b). To extract fixations, we used movement-threshold identification to determine the conservative speed threshold that best separated the bimodal distribution of eye speed in a session. A fixation was defined as a minimum 100 ms period when the eye speed remained below this threshold. For each fixation interval, we extracted the corresponding scene camera frames, referred to as fixation frames within neurons’ receptive fields (Fig. 2b and “Methods”). We trained a convolutional autoencoder (CA) using the image content of video frames captured by the scene camera (10656 ± 648 frames per session, mean ± s.e.m., 2 frames per second; Fig. 1b, and Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6). The CA had a 128-dimensional hidden layer acting as a bottleneck to capture the most salient features of the input image and develop a compact representation7,8 (see “Methods”). To further reduce dimensionality, we applied principal component analysis (PCA), resulting in 16-dimensional CA vector representations of the video frames (Fig. 1e). To represent each fixation interval in the CA vector space, we used the centroid of the CA vector representations of its corresponding fixation frames. We quantified the similarity of visual content between two fixation frames by calculating the Euclidean distance in the CA vector space (Fig. 2d). To efficiently search for fixations with most similar visual content to any given fixation, a space-partitioning data structure, called a k-d tree, organized the CA vector representations of all fixation frames (Fig. 1e) allowing a very fast search of nearest neighbors of any given frame9.

We found a strong correlation between the distance in CA vector space and distance in the 65536-dimensional pixel-based vector space (corresponding to 256×256 px dimensional fixation frames; r = 0.85, p < 1e-15; Fig. 2e). Nonetheless, the representation of visual stimuli in the CA vector space had significant benefits over pixel-based vector representations in terms of speed and accuracy (see “Methods”). We used Separability Index (SI; “Methods”)—the ratio of inter- to intra-fixation distances—to quantify the degree of separation between individual fixations in CA vector space versus pixel-based vector space. The SI measure in the CA vector space representation was nearly five times greater than that in the pixel-based vector space representation (Fig. 2f), indicating a greater separation between fixations in the CA vector representation. The superior robustness of the CA vector space representation likely arises from the autoencoder’s inherent tolerance to salt-and-pepper noise—caused by thermal fluctuations in the imaging sensor—and to minor image shifts induced by vibrations of the head-mounted scene camera.

Natural image population coding

A k-nearest neighbor (k-NN) search was used to form clusters of similar fixations around a selected seed fixation (see “Methods”). Pairs of seed fixations were then randomly chosen to generate fixation clusters (Figs. 1f, h and 2g). The separation between cluster pairs was quantified using the Mahalanobis distance (see “Methods”; n = 200,000 repetitions to ensure sampling across a wide range of distances). We quantified the similarity of neural responses evoked by pairs of fixation clusters by computing the Mahalanobis distance between their population response vectors in the multidimensional population space (dimensionality equal to the number of neurons). Population responses to clusters that were farther apart in CA space were more distinct (V4: r = 0.59 ± 0.03; dlPFC: r = 0.45 ± 0.05; mean ± s.e.m., n = 25 sessions; Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 7), further validating the CA representation of fixation content. To assess the information encoded in the population activity of V4 and dlPFC neurons, we trained linear support vector machine (SVM; Fig. 1f–i; see “Methods”) decoders to identify each fixation’s cluster membership from the neural responses it evoked. Linear SVM decoders performed significantly above chance in both areas (V4: 61.27 ± 5.38%, p = 0.019; dlPFC: 76.52 ± 6.74%, p < 1e-5; mean ± s.d., example session, bootstrap with 200,000 iterations; Fig. 2i). Decoder performance increased with the Mahalanobis distance between clusters, and this relationship was consistent across animals (Fig. 2j, k). Finally, we compared the decoding performance of V4 and dlPFC neurons by sampling an equal number of cells from each area to decode fixation classes across 200,000 random cluster pairs. As expected for a predominantly visual area, V4 outperformed dlPFC by 5.6 ± 1.3% (mean ± s.e.m., two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p = 8.9e-4, n = 25 sessions; Fig. 2l).

Sparse coding of visual information

We examined each neuron’s lifetime sparseness, or stimulus specificity10. Most neurons responded strongly to only a small subset of fixated images from the entire stimulus set (Fig. 3a; see “Methods”). At the population level, we assessed population sparseness—the fraction of neurons activated by a given stimulus—and found that each stimulus evoked strong responses from only a small subset of neurons (Fig. 3b; see “Methods”).

Stimulus encoding in both V4 and dlPFC revealed sparse coding—lifetime sparseness in V4 and dlPFC was 0.497 ± 0.006 and 0.505 ± 0.015, respectively, while population sparseness was 0.503 ± 0.001 (V4) and 0.525 ± 0.005 (dlPFC, mean ± s.e.m.). Sparseness levels were indistinguishable between the two areas (Supplementary Fig. 9). Notably, sparseness measured in freely moving conditions was significantly higher than that measured when the head and body were restrained, even when the same stimuli encountered during free viewing were replayed on a computer screen during fixation in both areas (Fig. 3c, d). Similar results were found even when sparseness was calculated using the fixations associated with pupil sizes in the lowest 10th percentile—sparseness during free movement remained significantly higher in both areas compared to restrained conditions, indicating that the increased sparseness of neural responses cannot be attributed to elevated motivational or attentional levels (Supplementary Fig. 10). Further, to rule out the influence of gaze position on sparseness, the animal’s gaze space was divided into four quadrants with approximately equal fixations (Supplementary Fig. 11a). However, no significant differences in lifetime or population sparseness were observed across quadrants in V4 or dlPFC (Supplementary Fig. 11b, c), indicating that gaze position does not influence sparseness.

Although, in principle, lifetime and population sparseness could be independent of each other, we found these measures to be correlated (Fig. 3e). From a theoretical standpoint, the mean firing rate of neurons and sparseness could be independent. However, given that these measures could in fact be correlated due to the relationship between stimulus selectivity and sparseness5,10, across cells we found a strong negative correlation between these two variables (Fig. 3f). That is, lifetime sparseness decreased as the average firing rates of neurons increased; the rate of decrease was greater in dlPFC compared to V4 (Fig. 3g). This suggests that the relationship between firing rate and sparseness may differ across brain regions, and that sparsity in dlPFC may be more strongly influenced by overall firing rates compared to V4.

Sparseness-based inter-areal coupling

Could individual neurons in visual and prefrontal cortex be functionally connected based on their sparsity level? We analyzed pairs of cells (one from each area) exhibiting significant cross-correlogram (CCG) peaks (see “Methods”) at both positive (V4 leading dlPFC, indicating feedforward coupling) and negative lags (dlPFC leading V4, indicating feedback coupling; Fig. 3h). Further, we assigned neurons in V4 and dlPFC to low (L) and high (H) sparseness subpopulations based on their lifetime sparseness values using the median in each session. We then calculated the percentage of significant CCG pairs between similarly sparse neuron subpopulations in V4 and dlPFC (either both high or both low) and dissimilarly sparse subpopulations (one high and one low). Surprisingly, similarly sparse neuron subpopulations across the two areas were more likely to exhibit significant correlations in their spike timing relative to dissimilarly sparse subpopulations (Monkey M: 63.87 vs. 36.13%, p = 3.9e-3, n = 9 sessions; Monkey R: 68.88 vs. 31.12%, p = 4.4e-4, n = 16 sessions; two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test; Fig. 3i).

Further examining feedforward sparseness-based coupling, we found that highly sparse V4 cells are more likely to be connected to highly sparse dlPFC cells (H-H vs. H-L: 72.7 vs. 27.3%, p = 4.5e-4, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, n = 25 sessions; Fig. 3j). Conversely, weakly sparse V4 cells were equally likely to be connected to either H or L cells in dlPFC (L-H vs. L-L: 53.5 vs. 46.5%, p = 0.68, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, n = 25 sessions; Fig. 3j). This sparseness-based selective coupling scheme is beneficial for information coding as H cells (highly-informative) feeding to L cells (weakly-informative) would be detrimental, but H cells could still make use of the input from L cells. Similarly, examining feedback coupling revealed that highly sparse dlPFC neurons are more likely coupled to highly sparse V4 neurons (H-H vs. H-L: 73.4 vs. 26.6%, p = 3.03e-5, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, n = 25 sessions; Fig. 3k). However, weakly sparse dlPFC neurons are equally likely to be coupled to V4 neurons of either sparsity level (L-H vs. L-L: 44.3 vs. 55.7%, p = 0.38, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, n = 25 sessions; Fig. 3k). Notably, despite the fact that highly sparse neurons fire at lower rates than weakly sparse neurons (Fig. 3f, g), H-H neurons were more strongly correlated in their spike timing compared to L-L neurons (Fig. 3l). This sparseness-based coupling rule is highly beneficial for information coding as highly sparse neurons across both areas have superior decoding performance compared to heterogeneously sparse neurons, which is an issue that we examined next.

We also examined whether this sparseness-based functional coupling occurs within individual areas. Neurons with similar sparseness (either both high or both low) exhibited more significant CCG coupling than those with dissimilar sparseness (one high and one low) in both V4 and dlPFC (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). This indicates that the principles of sparseness-based coupling also apply within a single cortical area. However, we observed a key difference: in both V4 and dlPFC, significant CCG peaks were more frequent between L–L neuron pairs than between H–H pairs (V4: 35.42 ± 1.8% vs. 20.59 ± 1.14%, p = 8.05e-5; dlPFC: 61.98 ± 1.90% vs. 6.86 ± 0.61%, p = 1.23e-5; mean ± s.e.m., two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, n = 25 sessions; Supplementary Fig. 12c, d). Since low-sparseness neurons generally exhibit higher firing rates (and vice versa, Fig. 3f, g), this indicates that coupling occurs predominantly among high-firing neurons. Thus, intra-areal coupling is primarily governed by firing-rate-based interactions, in contrast to inter-areal connectivity, which is driven by sparseness-based coupling (Fig. 3l).

Sparse neurons facilitate stimulus coding

A linear support vector machine (SVM) was used to investigate the relationship between neurons’ lifetime sparseness and their ability to encode natural images. The greater the neuron’s decoder weight, the more significant its impact to stimulus coding. In both V4 and dlPFC, neurons with higher lifetime sparseness exhibited greater weights in the trained decoder (Fig. 4a, b), indicating their more significant contribution to stimulus coding. Because neurons with high lifetime sparseness typically exhibit low firing rates (Fig. 3f, g), we conducted additional analyses to confirm that their larger weights in the trained decoder were not simply a consequence of their lower firing rates. First, we shifted the response distribution of all neurons so that they have the same mean firing rate (Fig. 4c). This did not alter their sparseness as the shape of their response distribution was unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 13). Next, we trained a linear SVM decoder using the shifted firing rates and examined the relationship between each neuron’s lifetime sparseness and the magnitude of its decoder weight. We found a similar relationship between a neuron’s sparseness and its contribution to stimulus coding as the one in the original data (Fig. 4b, d, and Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15). This indicates that the increased weights of highly sparse neurons originate from their significant contribution to stimulus coding rather than their low firing rates. Importantly, decoder performance was unchanged by shifting neural responses (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 16).

Finally, we found a significant positive correlation between the average lifetime and population sparseness and decoder performance (Fig. 4f, g and Supplementary Fig. 17) in both areas V4 and dlPFC, indicating that ensembles of neurons can enhance decoding accuracy by increasing average lifetime and population sparseness. Further, we divided the neurons in each brain area into high- and low-sparse groups based on median sparseness value in each area. Pooling the highly sparse neurons in both cortical areas (H-H groups) yielded a 22.03% increase in decoder performance relative to poorly sparse (L-L) neurons across the two areas (p = 1.2e-5, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, n = 25 sessions; Fig. 4h) (neurons with higher individual contributions to stimulus encoding also have greater contributions to decoder accuracy when considered within the context of the entire population; Supplementary Fig. 18). Altogether, these results indicate a functional significance for the sparseness-based connectivity rule described in Fig. 3 whereby highly sparse neurons across both areas communicate information more efficiently (Fig. 3j–l) to facilitate stimulus encoding in their population activity.

Active wakefulness increases decoder performance

Finally, we examined the relationship between sparseness and population coding during quiet and active wakefulness. The animal’s overall movement was quantified while freely roaming inside its environment. To detect epochs of quiet and active wakefulness, overhead and scene camera videos were analyzed by computing the frame-by-frame change in image content using correlation-based algorithms (Fig. 5a, b; see “Methods”). Across visual fixations, the average pupil diameter, which is correlated with arousal11–13, was significantly correlated with the degree of movement during the pre-fixation period (r = 0.26 ± 0.02, mean ± s.e.m.; Fig. 5c–f), and hence we used it as a proxy for quiet and active wakefulness. We further divided all fixations into low- and high- pupil-diameter groups (low- and high-PD, respectively) using the median value. Both lifetime and population sparseness increased in V4 during high-pupil-diameter (PD) relative to low-PD epochs (lifetime: 0.468 ± 0.006 vs 0.514 ± 0.006, p < 1e-15, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, n = 25 sessions, 825 units; population: 0.482 ± 0.002 vs 0.524 ± 0.003, p < 1e-15, two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test, n = 25 sessions, 3250 fixations; mean ± s.e.m.; Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 20). The increased sparseness in V4 during active wakefulness resulted in a higher decoder performance (Fig. 5h, 18.43 ± 5.81% increase, mean ± s.e.m., p = 0.008, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, n = 25 sessions). As lifetime sparseness of a neuron is negatively correlated with firing rate, the mean responses of V4 neurons were lower during active wakefulness (normalized firing rate: 0.171 ± 0.002 vs. 0.150 ± 0.005, mean ± s.e.m., p < 1e-15, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, n = 25 sessions, 825 units; Supplementary Fig. 19). Thus, the increased decoder performance in V4 can be attributed to increased sparseness, not firing rate changes.

These state-dependent effects on decoder performance were similar in dlPFC: performance improved for high-PD compared with low-PD fixations (43.02 ± 8.21% increase, mean ± s.e.m., p = 4.6e-5, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, n = 25 sessions, 1100 units; Fig. 5h). Nonetheless, the increase in lifetime sparseness in the high-PD condition was only minimal, and there was no increase in population sparseness (lifetime: 0.503 ± 0.005 vs. 0.507 ± 0.005, p = 4.8e-5, two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, n = 25 sessions, 1100 units; population: 0.527 ± 0.002 vs. 0.526 ± 0.002, p = 0.065, two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test, n = 25 sessions, 3250 fixations; mean ± s.e.m.; Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 20). In contrast to V4, firing rates of dlPFC neurons were significantly higher for the high-PD fixations (7.99% increase, p < 1e-15, two-sided Wilcoxon sign-rank test, n = 25 sessions, 1100 units; Supplementary Fig. 19). Hence, the improved decoder performance in dlPFC can be attributed to elevated firing rates while preserving a high degree of response sparsity. Finally, given that neurons in visual and prefrontal cortical areas decode stimuli better during active wakefulness, we examined the lifetime sparseness of the neurons exhibiting increased decoder weight due to elevated arousal. Notably, in both areas, the neurons showing an increase in decoder weight, and hence contributing most to stimulus encoding during active wakefulness, were those with greater lifetime sparseness (Fig. 5i, j). This provides additional evidence that sparseness is critically involved in stimulus encoding during natural viewing, even when firing rates are reduced.

Discussion

Our study provides the first examination of the contribution of sparseness to stimulus encoding by neural populations in different states of wakefulness during free environment exploration. Previous studies in rodents, non-human primates2,5,14,15, and humans16, measured sparseness, mainly in primary visual cortex, under restrictive experimental conditions whereby stimuli were presented on a computer screen, and animals were physically restrained. Importantly, we found significantly higher sparseness during free environment exploration compared to restrained conditions in both cortical areas, even when natural stimuli were replayed on a computer display while monkey’s head and body were fixed. Surprisingly, we revealed a sparseness-based connectivity rule across cortical networks whereby V4 and dlPFC neurons with high degrees of sparseness are more strongly coupled. This connectivity rule was functionally relevant as highly sparse neurons in visual and prefrontal cortex were found to contribute the most to the accurate encoding of stimuli during natural viewing. Thus, sparsification constitutes a general principle underlying population coding across sensory and executive cortical circuits during unrestrained environmental exploration.

Acquiring visual information during free movement consists of random alternations between periods of active and passive exploration. Our results reveal that during these periods, the neural representation of natural images is state-dependent, although state dependency was manifested differently in visual and prefrontal areas. Indeed, whereas active wakefulness increased sparseness in visual cortex while decreasing neural responses, sparseness was only mildly changed in prefrontal cortical neurons while evoked responses to natural images were significantly elevated. Nonetheless, these different mechanisms, the increase in sparseness in V4 and increase in firing rates in dlPFC, both contributed to increasing visual performance during active wakefulness in each cortical area. Notably, the highly sparse neurons in both areas had a higher contribution to stimulus encoding during active relative to passive wakefulness. This fundamentally differs from the effects of arousal in sensory cortical areas in rodents, whereby both neural responses and sparseness were found to be greatly elevated4. This indicates species-specific general principles of image encoding across neocortical circuits during naturalistic viewing.

Future studies will examine whether the sparseness-based connectivity rule revealed here is ubiquitous across large cortical areas beyond visuo-frontal networks. Importantly, these questions are now possible to answer by allowing animals to move freely during environment exploration, which allows the study of the neural underpinnings of natural vision in free-roaming settings. Indeed, recent advances in low-power, high-throughput electrophysiological devices coupled with wireless behavioral monitoring and large-scale computing13,17,18, such as those used here, render this research feasible now. A continued shift toward more naturalistic behavior in which multi-sensory information is recorded wirelessly in conjunction with large-scale population recordings will be essential for understanding the neural mechanisms of active vision and, more broadly, animal cognition19,20.

Methods

Ethics statement and animals

All experiments were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Animal Welfare Committee (AWC) and the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) for the University of Texas at Houston (UTHealth). Two adult male rhesus monkeys (Macaca mulatta; M: 10 kg, 11 years old; R: 12 kg, 10 years old) were used in the experiments.

Surgical procedures

Titanium headposts (Crist Instruments) were implanted medially on two 9-year-old male rhesus monkeys (Macaca mulatta) with anchor screws. Following a recovery period exceeding 6 weeks, animals were acclimatized to the experimental cage for at least 4 days per week for over 4 weeks. After acclimatization, animals were implanted with two Utah arrays (64 channels each, Blackrock Microsystems) in the left visual cortical area V4 and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) and pedestal on the caudal skull using either bone cement or bone screws and dental acrylic. Two wires to act as reference during the recording were passed through the craniotomy under and above the dura mater. The stereotaxic coordinates were chosen to make the craniotomies based on MRI and brain atlases17,21,22. Visual identification of arcuate and principal sulci was used to guide precise implantation of arrays into V4 and dlPFC during the surgery. Following array implantation, animals had a 3-week recovery period before recording from the array.

Chronic implantation of Utah arrays

A titanium headpost was implanted medially with anchor screws on each monkey. After acclimatization, animals were implanted with a 64-channel dual Utah array in the left hemisphere dlPFC (anterior of the arcuate sulcus and dorsal of the principal sulcus), left V4 (ventrally anterior to lunate sulcus and posterior to superior temporal sulcus), and a pedestal on the caudal skull (Blackrock Microsystems). We used Brainsight, a neuronavigational system, and animal’s MRIs to determine the location for V4 and dlPFC craniotomies (Rogue Research). During surgery, visual identification of arcuate and principal sulci guided precise implantation of arrays into the dlPFC, and visual identification of the lunate and superior temporal sulci supported array placement in area V4. The dura was sutured over each array, and two reference wires were placed above the dura mater and under the bone flap. The bone flaps were secured over the arrays using titanium bridges and screws, and the electrical contacts on the pedestal were protected using a plastic cap except during the experiment. Following the implantation, the monkeys had a 3-week recovery period before recording from the arrays.

Wireless electrophysiology

To record the activity of neurons while minimizing interference with animal’s behavior, we used a lightweight, rechargeable battery-powered device (Cereplex-W, Blackrock Microsystems) that communicates wirelessly with a central amplifier and digital processor (Cerebus Neural Signal Processor, Blackrock Microsystems). First, the monkey was head-fixed, the protective cap of the array’s pedestal was removed, and the wireless transmitter was screwed to the pedestal. Neural activity was recorded in the head-fixed position for 10 min to ensure the quality of the signal before releasing the monkey in the experimental arena. The arena was surrounded by eight antennas. Spikes from each brain area were recorded simultaneously at 30 kHz and detected online (Cerebus neural signal processor, Blackrock Microsystems) using a manually selected upper and lower threshold on the amplitude of the recorded signal in each channel, which was helpful to eliminate noise from the animal chewing, or by using the software’s automatic thresholding which was ±6.25 times the standard deviation of the raw signal. The on-site digitization in the wireless device showed lower noise than common wired head-stages. The remaining noise from the animal’s movements and muscle activity was removed offline using the automatic algorithms in Offline Sorter (Plexon Inc.). Briefly, this was done by removing the outliers (outlier threshold = 4–5 standard deviations) in a 3-dimensional space that was formed by the first three principal components of the spike waveforms. Then, the principal components were used to sort single units using the k-means clustering algorithm. Each signal was then automatically evaluated and manually checked as multi- or single-unit using several criteria: consistent spike waveforms, waveform shape (slope, amplitude, trough-to-peak), and exponentially decaying ISI histogram with no ISI shorter than the refractory period (1 ms). In this study, we recorded 493 single units (SU) and 1053 multi units (MU) from monkey M, and 720 SUs and 1176 MUs from monkey R.

Experimental conditions

Freely moving recordings: Neural recordings were made with the wireless system while the monkey freely explored its surroundings inside an arena measuring 7 × 4 × 3 ft (length × width × height) constructed using polyvinyl chloride piping, plastic, and plexiglass. The animal was allowed to move freely, with food pellets intermittently delivered via a feeding tray to encourage exploration. Transparent plexiglass walls allowed the monkey to view the entire room. All the analyses except parts of Fig. 3c,d were performed using data collected from freely moving animals. Head-fixed recordings: (a) Animals were trained to fixate on a small point (0.2°) within a small rectangular 1° window at the center of a cathode ray tube (CRT) monitor while remaining head-fixed and movement restrained in a polycarbonate primate chair (Crist Instruments Co. Inc.). If at any point during the trial, eye position exceeded 0.25° outside the boundaries of the rectangular box, then the trial was automatically aborted. Animals were rewarded with juice at the end of each trial in which fixation was successfully maintained for the entire duration of stimulus presentation. Eye movements were monitored throughout the recording session using an infrared eye tracking system (EyeLink II, SR Research) at a 1-kHz sampling rate. Stimulus presentation was recorded and synchronized with the neural data using a programmable Experiment Control Module device (FHC). Frames from the scene-camera video recorded during free-moving sessions were used as stimuli. Five fixation frames that were maximally separated in the CA vector space were used. Stimuli were presented for 100 ms each with an interstimulus interval of 75 ms. This data set was used only for comparison of sparseness during free-moving and head-fixed conditions (parts of Fig. 3c, d). (b) Same as (a), but 30 natural stimuli were used. This data set was used only for comparison of sparseness during free-moving and head-fixed conditions (parts of Fig. 3c, d).

Receptive field mapping of neurons

We identified receptive fields (RFs) of recorded neurons in a head-fixed task where the animal was trained to maintain fixation during stimulus presentation on a monitor. Neural activity was recorded and thresholded using a wired head-stage and recording system, alike the methods described above in wireless electrophysiology (Cerebus Neural signal processor, Blackrock Microsystems). We divided the right visual field into a 3 × 3 grid consisting of nine squares, with each square covering 8° × 8° of visual space. The entire grid covered 24° × 24° of visual space. Each of the nine squares was further subdivided into a 6 × 6 grid. In each trial, one of the nine squares was randomly chosen, and the RF mapping stimuli were presented at each of the 36 locations in a random order. The RF mapping stimuli consisted of a reverse correlation movie with red, blue, green, and white patches (~1.33° each). A complete RF session is composed of 10 presentations of the RF mapping stimuli in each of the nine squares forming the 3 × 3 grid. We averaged the responses over multiple presentations to generate RF heatmaps and corresponding receptive field plots (as shown in Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 4a). Since most recorded units remained stable across days (>70% of units), RF mapping was done prior to the first session, and performed once every month during recordings for each monkey.

Movement quantification during free roaming

We quantified the overall movement of the animal by analyzing the overhead camera and scene camera video frames and computing total movement in each of the frames. Specifically, to extract the total movement in a video frame of interest, we used the “BlockMatcher” method available in the MATLAB Vision Toolbox to calculate how much the content of a video frame has moved relative to its position in the preceding frame using spatial correlation algorithms. The total movement during a time window of interest was computed by summing the movement values of all the video frames belonging to that particular time window.

Wireless eye tracking and fixation identification

We used a custom wireless eye tracker (ISCAN, Inc.) to measure pupil position and diameter from monkey during experiments. The portable wireless eye tracker mounted dorsally, right above the animal’s eye, consisted of an eye mirror, eye camera for detecting pupil size and eye position, and scene camera, situated above the eye camera, that records animal’s field of view (Fig. 1c, d). All data was recorded at 30 Hz. To train animals to wear the device without damaging it, its 3D geometry was modeled (Sketchup Pro), and dummies were 3D printed and fitted with eye mirrors. To properly position the eye tracker and dummies relative to the eye, custom adapters were designed and 3D printed to attach directly to the animal’s headpost and serve as an anchor point for the eye tracker. These adapters were designed to interface with the headpost, without touching the animal directly, to minimize discomfort and reduce the likelihood of the device being tampered with. These dummy eye trackers were worn by animals for several mock recording sessions to adjust them to wearing the device. Once the animals grew accustomed to wearing the dummy and stopped touching it altogether, the real device was used. Before each experiment, the eye tracker was secured on the animal, and we performed a calibration procedure (“point-of-regard” calibration) while the animal was head-fixed, which mapped the eye position data to the matrix of the head-mounted scene camera. Animals were trained to view 5 calibration points within the field of view of the scene camera (640 × 480 pixel space of a scene camera frame maps to 35° × 28°, LxH), including a center calibration point and 4 outer points, positioned at ±8 to ±10° with respect to the center (we chose the distance between the animal’s eye and calibration monitor based on the approximate range of eye-stimulus distances during free viewing, which was 70 cm). As the animal viewed each point, the eye calibration software synchronizes the eye movement data with the image frames recorded by the scene camera. With the help of the calibration, the system is able to display the animal’s center of gaze on each scene camera frame in real time as a crosshair (Supplementary Fig. 3). If the animal looks outside the field of view of the scene camera frames, gaze location is not detected, and the crosshair will not appear on the scene camera frames. When this occurs, eye position data is reflected as zero. Therefore, only scene camera frames that included a crosshair were used in analysis, which mostly occurred (65–88%). We used the horizontal and vertical coordinates of the pupil to compute eye velocity. To extract fixations, we used velocity-threshold identification to determine the conservative velocity threshold that best separated the bimodal distribution of eye velocities in a session23. A fixation was defined as a minimum 100 ms period when the eye velocity remained below this threshold.

Unlike traditional eye tracking recording systems, where the optics tracking the eye gaze is mounted to a structure external to the monkey’s body, our wireless eye tracking system for free roaming monkey uses eye camera that is mounted to the monkey’s head itself. Hence, the camera moves with the monkey’s head. Because of this, there are instances when the monkey’s eye gaze direction is constant, but his head is moving. Hence, during these moments, the cross-hair in the scene camera video will be fixed at the same coordinate, but the content of the video will be moving. These moments will be incorrectly identified as fixations by the fixation identification algorithm, just because the eye gaze cross-hair remains fixed at the same coordinate in the scene camera video, and registering low eye velocity. We estimated the motion of the scene content between two consecutive frames (“pixel-flow”) of the scene camera video using the “BlockMatcher” method available in the MATLAB Vision Toolbox. Pixel-flow is a measure that estimates how much the content of a frame has moved relative to its position in the reference frame by moving a block of pixels over a search region and computing their similarities. We quantified the pixel-flow present in each frame of the scene camera video and computed the average pixel-flow present in each identified fixation by averaging the pixel-flow of each frame within the fixation. We discarded all the fixations during which the monkey was moving its head, as determined by the average pixel-flow during those fixations. This approach ensures that only true fixations are included in the analysis.

Autoencoder

An autoencoder is a feedforward artificial neural network (Supplementary Fig. 5) that uses unsupervised learning method to perform the identity mapping, where the network inputs are reproduced at the output layer with a hidden layer in between that describes a code used to represent the input7,8,24. The hidden layer is constrained to have smaller dimension than the input and output layers—a bottleneck—which forces the autoencoder to develop a compact representation of the input data by capturing its most salient features7,8. The first half of the autoencoder (Supplementary Fig. 5: pink segment), which compresses input data to encoded representation, is called “Encoder”, and the second half (Supplementary Fig. 5: aqua segment), which takes the encoded representation and tries to reconstruct the original input data as close as possible, is called “Decoder”. Autoencoder analysis is similar to the well-known method of principal component analysis (PCA): it is used to identify and remove correlations among problem variables as an aid to dimensionality reduction, visualization, and exploratory data analysis7. While PCA identifies only linear correlations between variables, autoencoder analysis uncovers both linear and nonlinear correlations, without restriction on the character of the nonlinearities present in the data7.

Convolutional autoencoder (CA)

Nearby pixel intensities in natural images are highly correlated. Therefore, autoencoder designed to process image data is typically constructed using convolutional layers that preserve the local spatial relationships of the input pixels. The resulting autoencoder is called Convolutional Autoencoder (CA) (Fig. 1b). Convolution layers minimize the computation overhead of the network by inherently utilizing parameter sharing among connections. Using simple fully connected layers instead of convolutional layers would result in a very large neural network that is prohibitively slow to compute. The convolutional layers further allow CAs to filter out noise and create robust and stable compressed representations of the image data25–27. The encoder segment of CA is constructed using convolutional and max-pooling layers, and the decoder segment is constructed using convolutional and up-sampling layers (Fig. 1b). The max-pooling layers in the encoder segment gradually reduce the input data size and add tolerance to small shift variance (vibrations) in the data28. CAs have been widely used for dimensionality reduction of image data28–34.

We trained a convolutional autoencoder (CA) (Fig. 1b) using images from frames of video captured using a scene camera mounted on monkey’s head. We made sure that we were not using overly complex model and overfitting it to the data by looking at the traces of training and validation loss as a function of epoch (Supplementary Fig. 6). We used 300 epochs for training because the validation loss started to decrease negligibly after that. The autoencoder was then used to compress visual input to the monkey (frames of the scene camera video) to 128-dimensional encoded vector representation. We applied PCA to the 128-D vector representations to further reduce their dimensionality to 16 to arrive at 16-D CA vector representations of the video frames. The Euclidean distance between encoded vectors of two frames was then used to quantify the similarity of salient features present in those two frames. Space-partitioning data structure for organizing points in a multi-dimensional space (k-d tree) was used to store the encoded vector representations of all the visual input. This enabled very fast searching of nearest neighbors of any given frame9. All the fixation frames were encoded using the trained convolutional autoencoder to represent them in a CA vector space.

Creating cluster of similar fixation frames in CA vector space

Cluster of “k” fixation vectors in the CA vector space around any seed vector was created by finding “k” nearest (Euclidean distance) neighbors to the seed vector. All the fixations belonging to a cluster had similar visual content. While creating a cluster pair around two seeds, if the k-nearest neighbor search around them returned some overlapping (same) fixations, they were randomly assigned to only one of the two clusters.

Comparison of convolutional autoencoder and pixel-based representations of fixation frames

Each scene-camera frame was encoded using a convolutional autoencoder (CA) into a 16-dimensional latent vector. Visual similarity between frames was quantified as the Euclidean distance between their CA vectors, with smaller distances indicating greater similarity. For each session, fixation frames were embedded in CA space and organized using a k-d tree to enable efficient nearest-neighbor searches. Two seed fixations were randomly selected, and clusters were constructed from their nearest neighbors in CA space. Linear classifiers were then trained to distinguish cluster membership based on neural population responses. Classification accuracy was well above chance and increased with the Mahalanobis distance between cluster centroids. Because the autoencoder was trained using a pixel-wise reconstruction loss, we compared CA-based representations with raw pixel vectors (256 × 256 pixels; 65536 dimensions). Pairwise distances in CA and pixel spaces were strongly correlated (r = 0.85; Fig. 2e), indicating that the low-dimensional embedding preserved the geometric structure of the original image space. CA vector provided substantial advantages over pixel vector in both computational efficiency and robustness. Construction of the k-d tree and nearest-neighbor searches were approximately 1400-fold faster in CA space than in pixel space. Moreover, CA vector was tolerant to small pixel-level translations arising from minor head-mounted camera movements/vibrations. In contrast, pixel vector distances can be highly sensitive to small image shifts, leading to large variability even within the same fixation. This was supported by the observation that the fixation-image classification accuracy was higher using CA than pixel vector representations (Supplementary Fig. 8). Higher separability index (SI; Eq. 1) values indicate better discrimination between fixations. Hence, we used SI to compare CA and pixel-based vector representations and found that the CA representation produced an SI approximately fivefold greater than the pixel representation (Fig. 2f), indicating superior representational fidelity. In addition, the CA representation is also robust to salt-and-pepper noise arising from sensor or transmission artifacts.

Sparseness

Firing rate of each neuron during the fixation period was used to compute the sparseness values. Sparseness is a function of the shape of the response distribution and is a measure of its peakedness. Responses with high sparseness have values that are disproportionately at the tails of the response distribution (heavy-tailed) relative to a Gaussian distribution, and those with low sparseness have a response distribution with thin tails2,10,14,35. To compute sparseness, we applied the method used by Vinje and Gallant2, which is a slight modification of the method first proposed by Treves and Rolls14.
