Association of micronutrient levels with dementia risk in adults in Telangana, India: a cross-sectional study (2023–2024)
Authors: Vineela Vepakomma, Karthikeyan Ramanujam, Kiran K Angadi, Shalini Tattari, Francesca Mangialasche, Miia Kivipelto, Vidyani Suryadevara, G Bhanuprakash Reddy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundDementia develops from a complex interplay of genetic and environmental factors, and nearly half of dementia globally are attributable to modifiable risk factors, including nutrition. We aim to examine the relationship between nutritional status and dementia risk factor burden, as quantified by the context-specific CAIDE score, for its applicability in India.MethodsThis community-based cross-sectional study involved middle-aged and older-adults residing in Telangana, India. The Indian-specific CAIDE score was calculated using the variables: age, gender, education, BMI, physical activity, systolic blood pressure, and total cholesterol. Dietary intake and blood vitamin levels were estimated and correlated with the dementia risk factor burden.FindingsAmong 556 study participants (mean age: 63 years), 39% had a predicted risk of dementia (≥9 CAIDE score). The risk was higher in rural areas (60% vs 27%) and in females (55% vs 45%). Vitamin B2 deficiency was most prevalent (64%), followed by vitamin D (42%), B6 (34%), active B12 (17%), B9 (8%), and B1 (3%). The prevalence of deficiencies in vitamin D, B2, B6, and B9 was significantly higher in the high-risk group (HRG) than in the low-risk group (LRG). Dietary diversity was lower in the HRG than in the LRG. Saturated fat intake was higher, whereas unsaturated fat intake was lower in HRG than in LRG. Vitamin deficiencies (D, B2, B6, B9, B12) were significantly higher among rural participants than among urban participants.InterpretationThis study applies a context-specific CAIDE score for dementia risk assessment in adults from India and reports its association with nutritional markers.FundingThis work was supported by grants from the Department of Biotechnology (BT/PR36689/PFN/20/1524/2020) and the Indian Council of Medical Research, Government of India.Keywords: Dementia, CAIDE score, Dietary intake, Micronutrients, Vitamins, India

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Previous research has reported that direct adaptation of dementia risk prediction models from high-income countries (HICs) to low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) is often inconsistent. In particular, the Cardiovascular risk factors, ageing, and dementia (CAIDE) model's applicability and the possible need for adaptation have not been examined across diverse LMIC populations. Given the growing emphasis on dementia prevention in LMICs, it becomes imperative to develop valid and culturally appropriate early risk assessment tools for the successful prediction of dementia risk and ensure targeted interventions. Our group has developed and reported CAIDE-related risk factors and is part of the World-Wide FINGERS (WW-FINGERS) initiative for dementia risk reduction via multidomain interventions. Yet, the association of micronutrients with dementia risk has not been examined in India or elsewhere. We searched PubMed and Google Scholar until December 2025, using the terms “CAIDE”, “context specific”, “dementia risk”, “vitamin levels”, “micronutrients”, “cognitive dysfunction”, and “India”. To date, no published studies have investigated the association between objectively measured vitamin biomarkers and the CAIDE-based prediction of dementia risk, particularly in India.

Added value of this study

The present study leveraged country-specific estimates of dementia risk in India by adapting context and culturally specific cut-offs, an approach not previously demonstrated in LMICs. While one Indian study reported CAIDE scores for rural and urban cohorts, it did not apply context-specific thresholds for variables such as total cholesterol, education, and BMI, which are much needed to reduce cross-cultural variability and misclassification. In addition to the CAIDE model variables, we evaluated potential modifiable factors that can be incorporated into multidomain lifestyle interventions to achieve accurate and comprehensive outcomes in dementia risk prevention. Furthermore, we integrated information on blood vitamin levels, dietary intakes, and their associations with CAIDE dementia risk scores. We highlighted the correlation of nutritional status with increased dementia risk in LMIC settings. In addition, we demonstrate the contribution of rural-specific risk factors and higher vitamin deficiencies that may disproportionately increase the risk of dementia.

Implications of all the available evidence

Due to an increase in the aged population, dementia is expected to rise in India by 2050. Considering the absence of widely available disease-modifying therapies for Alzheimer's disease, it would be appropriate to address known modifiable risk factors. Evidence from multimodal interventions encompassing nutrition, lifestyle, and cardiometabolic components has shown benefits for cognition in at-risk older adults. Early risk prediction and intervention strategies in India, would have a significant impact on reducing socioeconomic burdens. Essentially, policy reforms are urgently needed to allocate healthcare resource budgets for the early screening of those at risk of dementia at the population level, predominantly in India.

Introduction

The global population is experiencing a rapid increase in the proportion of older adults due to increased life expectancy, including in India,1 with the proportion of older adults being 10.5% in 2022 and projected to rise to 20.8% by 2050.2 This demographic transition has been accompanied by a concurrent rise in the prevalence of chronic age-associated non-communicable diseases (NCDs) such as dementia. Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), like India, account for approximately 60% of the global dementia burden,3 with 8.8 million people living with dementia in India in 2023 alone.4,5 The associated care costs pose considerable strain on the country's healthcare system.

Dementia develops from a complex interplay of both genetic and environmental factors.6 Evidence suggests that nearly 45% of dementia globally may be attributable to modifiable risk factors,7 including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, obesity, physical inactivity, social isolation, smoking, depression, and low literacy, all of which are highly prevalent in India.8 Cardiovascular risk is significant, with nearly 25% of Indian elderly people having a ≥10% risk of a cardiovascular event within 10 years.9 Moreover, neuropathological changes are known to precede clinical symptoms by up to two decades.10 Thereby, proactive risk assessment facilitates early prediction of dementia risk, enabling appropriate interventions.

Although prior studies have reported the role of individual risk factors for dementia, real-world scenarios often involve co-occurring risks.11 These factors tend to cluster together, increasing the likelihood of disease onset.12 There are numerous models developed for dementia risk assessment, given that each risk factor contributes differently to overall risk, the CAIDE (Cardiovascular Risk Factors, Ageing, and Incidence of Dementia) score was developed, incorporating multiple non-modifiable (age, sex, education) and modifiable risk factors (systolic blood pressure, body mass index, blood total cholesterol, level of physical activity) to estimate dementia risk.13 This tool has been validated for predicting mid-life risk of dementia in different populations.14,15 The CAIDE score has been used to select participants and monitor intervention effects in randomised controlled trials (RCTs) testing multidomain lifestyle-based interventions for dementia risk reduction.16, 17, 18, 19 However, significantly fewer studies from LMICs limited the CAIDE score's applicability and geographic representation across diverse populations.

One modifiable risk factor crucial for dementia is nutrition,20 particularly micronutrient status. Substantial evidence indicates an association between adherence to dietary patterns, such as the Mediterranean–DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay (MIND) diets, and better cognitive performance.21,22 Further, malnutrition contributes to cognitive impairment indirectly by affecting the cardiometabolic risk factors, which are established dementia risk factors. Despite numerous studies, the independent role of nutrition in cognitive health remains uncertain, with intervention trials yielding mixed findings.23,24 Moreover, no studies have investigated the association between nutritional biomarkers and validated dementia risk prediction models, such as CAIDE scores. As the Indian socio-demographic landscape serves as a good representation of South Asian diversity, there is a need to derive context-specific risk assessment tools and facilitate early interventions for dementia prevention in India. Thus, we aim to address this critical gap by exploring the relationship between circulatory micronutrient status and dementia risk factor burden, as quantified by the context-specific CAIDE score.

Methods

We conducted a community-based cross-sectional study across four geographical regions (east, west, north, and south) of Telangana encompassing both urban and rural areas using a multistage community-based sampling approach. To maximise participants engagement, approximately 14–16 health outreach camps per region were organised at randomly selected locations from May 2023–May 2024. On average, 20–25 adults, both men and women, aged 40–80 years were initially screened from each camp, and 8–10 apparently healthy individuals were enrolled into the study. The sample size was calculated to estimate the proportion of individuals at risk of dementia.25 Assuming that 50% of the population has the outcome of interest, with a desired margin of error of 5% and a 95% confidence level, the required sample size was estimated to be 385. To account for an anticipated 30% non-response rate for blood sample collection and dietary assessment, the final target sample size was increased to 550 participants. Written informed consent was collected from the subjects.

A self-designed questionnaire documented sociodemographic information from participants, including age, gender, residence, literacy status, current alcohol intake and smoking behaviour, food habits, physical activity, and cohabitation. Food habits were grouped as vegetarian (those who never consumed animal foods, including eggs) and the mixed group, which consumed both animal and plant foods.

To determine body mass index (BMI), body weight and height of the subjects were recorded using a calibrated SECA 813 digital scale (Hamburg, Germany) and a SECA 213 portable stadiometer, respectively. Waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) was calculated from waist circumference (WC) and hip circumference (HC) measured with a SECA 201 non-stretchable measuring tape. Blood pressure was measured three times using a digital oscillometric device (HEM-7111, Omron Healthcare Co., Ltd.), and the average was calculated.

Following an overnight fast, venous blood samples were drawn and transported to the laboratory on ice. Upon arrival, the required volumes of whole blood were aliquoted, and remaining samples were centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to separate plasma within 4–6 h. All samples were stored at −80 °C for further investigation.

From the whole blood sample, fasting blood glucose (FBG) and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) were measured using a glucometer (Accu-Chek® Active Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) and an Affinion AS100 Analyser (Abbott, USA), respectively. Total haemoglobin (Hb) was quantified by the cyanmethemoglobin method using a Shimadzu UV-2600 spectrophotometer. Through enzymatic methods, plasma lipid profiles were assessed using the LDX Analyser. Renal function was evaluated by measuring plasma creatinine levels using the modified kinetic Jaffe method with a Biosystems kit (Barcelona, Spain).

To predict the risk of dementia, several risk factors were determined from the population-based CAIDE study in 2006.13 We have adapted this scoring model by incorporating Indian-specific cut-offs for variables such as education,26 total cholesterol,27 and physical activity,28 taking into account population characteristics and geographical variations. The score for these variables ranges from 0 to 15. The modified CAIDE scoring model is described in Supplementary Table S1. Participants who scored ≥9 were considered as the High-Risk Group (HRG), whereas subjects with a score of <9 were as the Low-Risk Group (LRG).13

Plasma levels of vitamins A and D were determined using reverse-phase HPLC coupled with ultraviolet detection.29 The active coenzyme forms of vitamins B1 (thiamine pyrophosphate) and B2 (flavin adenine dinucleotide) were measured in whole blood, while the active form of vitamin B6 (pyridoxal-5′-phosphate) was quantified in plasma by utilising commercially available HPLC kits with fluorescence detection (Recipe Chemicals + Instruments GmbH, Germany).29 Plasma folate and B12 levels were estimated using an immunoanalyser (Beckman Coulter Unicel DXI 800, version 7.1.0, USA). Additionally, active B12, the biologically available fraction bound to transcobalamin II, was quantified using Abbott Architect i1000SR. The prevalence of subclinical micronutrient deficiencies was estimated by using well-defined cut-offs.29

Dietary intake was assessed individually in a randomly selected subset of samples (n = 247). A 24-h dietary recall was conducted on three days (two non-consecutive weekdays and one weekend) to account for daily and individual variability. The person involved in household meal preparation was interviewed to report food consumption over the previous 24 h, excluding festival days, special events, or fasting days. Standardised visual aids, including 12 cups and two spoons, were used to estimate portion sizes.30 The quantities of raw food consumed by each participant were calculated based on their cooked food intake. Furthermore, nutrient values of the raw food items were determined using the Indian Food Consumption Table (IFCT). Additionally, the USDA-Food and Nutrient Database was approached for items not included in IFCT. Total daily food and nutrient intakes were calculated by averaging values from the three dietary recall days, using in-house software for computation.

To evaluate the diversity of food group consumption, dietary diversity scores (DDS) were calculated for each dietary recall using a median cut-off of seven. Individuals consuming greater than seven food groups were classified as a high diversity group, and those consuming fewer than seven food groups as a low diversity group.

Statistical analysis

Data processing and statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The normality of the data, as well as its skewness and kurtosis, were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Values were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for clinical parameters, whereas blood vitamin levels, dietary food groups, and nutrient intake were reported using median values (interquartile range). For group comparisons, the student's t-test and the Mann–Whitney U-test were performed depending on the distribution. The Chi-square test was used to test the association between CAIDE variables and sociodemographic characteristics of the population. The Spearman rank correlation coefficient was computed to examine correlations between vitamin levels and CAIDE score as continuous variables, on a scale of −1 to +1. A multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to examine the association between vitamin deficiency and CAIDE-defined high-risk status, adjusting for conventional risk factors. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethics statement

The Institutional Ethics Committee for human studies research at the ICMR-National Institute of Nutrition, Hyderabad, India, approved the study procedures (protocol number: IEC # 01/I/2022; date of approval: February 07, 2022). Written informed consent was obtained from the subjects. The study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines outlined in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Role of funding source

The funders had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, interpretation, and writing of the manuscript.

Results

The detailed process of subject recruitment is demonstrated in Supplementary Figure S1. Initially, a total of 1250 subjects, 660 urban and 590 rural, were approached and screened, accounting for anticipated declines in participation. Finally, we obtained a sample of 556 subjects, comprising 201 rural and 355 urban participants.

Table 1 presents the differences in socio-demographic characteristics of the study groups. The cohort of 556 subjects comprised 52% men and 48% women. Alcohol consumption was reported by 38% of the participants, whereas 62% abstained. 11% of the subjects are currently smoking, while 89% were non-smokers. Furthermore, 94% of participants lived with family, and only 6.3% lived alone. Among participants, 26% reported adhering to a vegetarian diet, and 74% consumed a mixed diet.

Specific modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors from the CAIDE model are described in Supplementary Table S1. Scores ranged from 0 to 13 in the study population. Based on this classification, 39% of participants were identified as being at high-risk group (HRG), while 61% were classified as low-risk group (LRG).

Further, anthropometrics, and clinical profiles for LRG and HRG were presented in Table 1. Notably, in addition to CAIDE model variables, frequency of smokers (p = 0.03), the mean diastolic blood pressure (p = <0.01), plasma LDL (p = 0.04), and creatinine levels (p = 0.03) were significantly higher in the HRG compared to the LRG, while triglyceride levels (p = 0.14) were also elevated. The waist-to-hip ratio, haemoglobin concentration, and HbA1c levels did not differ significantly between the study groups.

Table 2 describes the distribution of CAIDE variables across rural and urban cohorts. The mean age was significantly higher in the rural cohort compared to the urban cohort (p < 0.001). Also, it is strikingly evident from the educational status that only 2% of rural subjects have more than 12 years of education, whereas 68% in the urban group (p < 0.001). Hypertension was significantly higher in the rural cohort, whereas total cholesterol levels and BMI were significantly higher in the urban cohort (p < 0.001). Furthermore, physical inactivity was substantially more prevalent in the urban group compared to the rural cohort (p = 0.05). Overall, the mean CAIDE score was significantly higher in the rural cohort (8.75 ± 1.6) compared to the urban cohort (6.86 ± 2.4) (p < 0.001), and a greater proportion of rural subjects (60%) were classified as high-risk for dementia than urban subjects (27%).

Plasma vitamin levels between LRG and HRG were reported as medians with IQRs. The overall prevalence of subclinical vitamin deficiencies is detailed in Table 3. Vitamin B2 deficiency was most prevalent (64%), followed by total B12 (47%), D (42%), B6 (34%), active B12 (17%), B9 (7.7%), B1 (3%), and A (1%). Though not statistically different, the prevalence of vitamin D, B1, B9, and B12 (total and active) deficiencies was higher in HRG than in LRG. Multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed that vitamin D deficiency remained independently associated with high dementia risk factor burden in the adjusted model. However, these are associative findings, considering the cross-sectional study design. Interestingly, deficiencies of vitamins D, B2, B6, B9, and active B12 were significantly higher in the rural cohort than in the urban cohort, as shown in Supplementary Table S2.

The median intake of food groups and nutrients is presented in Table 4. The median intake levels of dairy products, nuts and seeds, and other vegetables were significantly lower in the HRG compared to the LRG. Discussion

This study assesses the risk of dementia using the context-specific CAIDE score and examine associations with nutritional intakes and sub-clinical vitamin levels in Indian adults from rural and urban settings. As highlighted by the results, 39% of the study population was classified as at high risk of developing dementia at a later stage using the CAIDE risk model. To substantiate the utility of the modified CAIDE score, we have assessed cognitive performance in a subset of samples (n = 140) using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), a globally standardised tool, and found that the mean MoCA scores were significantly lower in the HRG compared to LRG (p < 0.001) (data not shown). Nevertheless, further validation studies using other cognitive assessment tools on large populations are needed to confirm these findings. Moreover, individuals in HRG exhibited significantly elevated levels of LDL, triglycerides, and plasma creatinine, consistent with the Lancet Standing Commission and WHO dementia prevention guidelines, which emphasise managing midlife dyslipidemia to reduce dementia risk.7

Conducting dementia research in India presents unique challenges, particularly the adaptation of dementia risk assessment tools to the sociocultural context, as most prior studies were designed for literate, Western populations.31 Another concern while adapting international risk assessment tools is the need to incorporate region-specific cut-offs. While a previous study from South India utilised CAIDE thresholds as per the original model,25 for example TC (>6.5 mmol/L or 251 mg/dL), these may not be specific to the Indian population in the current scenario. In this context, our study addressed this limitation by adapting to the Indian education model and cholesterol cut-offs to better reflect Indian guidelines. Furthermore, by applying the WHO Asian BMI cut-offs for grade 1 (25–30 kg/m2) obesity, the predicted risk increased from 39% to 56%, underscoring the need for further attention. Through this research, we took the initiative to develop region-specific cut-offs, and demonstrated the relevance of the modified risk score in both urban and rural settings.

A greater risk measured in our sample, within rural areas compared to urban areas, may be attributed to mean higher age (66.2 vs 62.4 years), a higher proportion of women (59% vs 42%), higher prevalence of hypertension (53% vs 34%), and markedly lower educational attainment (83% vs 11.5%) in our rural cohort, despite lower prevalence of obesity (9.4% vs 24.2%), hypercholesterolaemia (7.4% vs 20%), and higher physical activity (75% vs 66%) compared to urban cohort. Our results corroborate a South Indian rural-urban comparison study that used the CAIDE scale to assess dementia risk.25 However, despite lower obesity and hypercholesterolaemia, and higher physical activity (75%) in rural areas, these protective factors did not offset the dementia risks and call for an adjustment in the model considering low education. Additionally, we found a higher prevalence of hypertension in the rural areas, reflecting national trends. This variation can be understood as in tune with the demographic transition; the urban-rural gap in hypertension prevalence has narrowed and even reversed in developed LMICs, due to a rapid increase of hypertension in rural areas.32 Another reason could be due to higher rates of undiagnosed hypertension in rural populations with low education and a lack of health awareness.33 Predominantly, subclinical vitamin deficiencies were significantly more prevalent among the rural population, emphasising the need for further investigation into their independent association with dementia risk and consideration in future nutritional interventions.

We extended our analysis to assess nutritional correlates of dementia risk factor burden. DDS was notably higher in the LRG, indicating greater nutritional awareness and consumption of more varied diets. These findings align with existing literature showing that diets rich in micronutrients - especially fruits and vegetables are associated with lower dementia risk. HRG participants also consumed more carbohydrates, while protein intake remained consistent across groups. Intake of unsaturated fats, including monounsaturated (MUFA) and polyunsaturated fats (PUFA), was significantly higher in the LRG. These fats are considered neuroprotective, although evidence from observational studies remains mixed,20 and a few studies have reported a link between high saturated fat intake and dementia.34

HRG group showed significantly lower levels of major B-vitamins and vitamin D. These vitamins play critical roles in homocysteine metabolism, neuronal function, and glucose metabolism, and their deficiencies have been linked to cognitive decline and dementia.35 Weak inverse correlations with CAIDE scores were observed for vitamins B2, D, B6, and active B12. Vitamin D, in particular, has been associated with reduced neuroinflammation and amyloid-β clearance, and levels up to 25 ng/ml appear protective.36 Most prior studies focused on individual nutrients; however, our study adapts a cumulative approach, exploring how multiple micronutrients together influence dementia risk factor burden. These findings highlight the potential role of vitamin status as a modifiable determinant of dementia risk factor burden. This integrative analysis provides new insights into which vitamin deficiencies correlate with dementia risk factor burden in the rural-urban Indian context and could inform targeted nutritional interventions.

While studies have addressed many key predictors of dementia risk, such as family history of dementia, waist-hip ratio, diabetes status, triglycerides, LDL, and C-reactive protein, these factors were not included in the CAIDE model and may be considered in future adaptations.13 Another point of discussion is the weighting assigned to females in the CAIDE model. Although the original model allotted a score of zero for women, in this study, female participants exhibited a higher risk for dementia. Also, previous studies suggested that females may be more affected by vascular risk factors, more likely to develop mid-life hypertension, which could increase their risk of developing later-life dementia.37 This highlights the need to reconsider and possibly modify the gender scoring in light of contemporary data. Recent evidence confirms that risk can be modified even in people with increased genetic risk of dementia through modifiable risk factors.7 Thereby, preventive actions should focus on early interventions and lifelong maintenance of favourable risk profiles.

This study reported the dementia risk factor burden using the CAIDE tool in the Indian population, incorporating regional cut-offs to enhance relevance and feasibility, and considering its applicability within the World-Wide-FINGERS network. Furthermore, extensive nutrient evaluation was conducted by assessing dietary intakes and subclinical nutritional status, which have been shown to be associated with a higher dementia risk. The study recruited volunteers from a specific state, which limits the generalisability of findings to the entire nation. A substantial proportion of exclusions at early recruitment stages results in the unavailability of comparable baseline data, limiting the nonresponse analysis. As the study is descriptive, causal inference cannot be interpreted from the study findings. Context-specific adaptation of this CAIDE model requires validation using biomarkers of dementia. While we examined the link between dementia risk factor burden and critical B-vitamins and vitamin D, the relationship with other micronutrients needs investigation for a better understanding of the role of nutrition and dementia. Further, cross-sectional design of this study limits its ability to infer casual relationships among the variables.

In conclusion, this study applies a context-specific CAIDE score for assessing dementia risk and its association with vitamin status in Indian adults which revealed that those with higher dementia risk showed significantly lower levels of major B-vitamins and vitamin D. It was found that 39% of participants were at high risk. Furthermore, rural populations and women are at a greater risk, with suboptimal intake of micronutrients and a higher prevalence of vitamin deficiencies. The different risk profiles between rural and urban may be considered to design tailored interventions. Also, establishing population-specific cut-offs for scores relevant to the Indian context can be planned in a future study. These findings underscore the importance of adapting nutrition-focused dementia prevention strategies relevant to the Indian context by integrating nutritional biomarkers and lifestyle variables. Given that CAIDE tool is being used in multidomain lifestyle-based RCTs for dementia risk reduction,14,38 this study further showed that people with increased dementia risk are also likely to have additional modifiable factors (smoking, LDL levels, dietary intake, specific subclinical micronutrient profile) which could be considered while planning preventive interventions.
