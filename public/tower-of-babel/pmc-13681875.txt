Current status of anti-obesity medications and performance, an EHR based survey
Authors: Xiaoyang Ruan, Rui Li, Liwei Wang, Shuyu Lu, Andrew Wen, Murali Sameer, Hongfang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile randomized clinical trials (RCTs) establish the efficacy of anti-obesity medications (AOMs), their real-world effectiveness, inter-individual variability, and weight regain post discontinuation remain poorly characterized across diverse, large scale populations.MethodsElectronic health records with pharmacy dispensation of obesity or overweight individuals were retrieved from local UT-Physician and nationwide EPIC COSMOS databases. AOMs were analyzed for short to long-term effectiveness on obesity relevant metrics.ResultsFrom the two cohorts, 71,318 (11·7%) and 1 M (27·9%) individuals documented AOM exposures, respectively, with about 70% individuals experiencing more than one treatment session. Tirzepatide and Semaglutide outperformed other AOMs and were associated with up to 9·6[IQR:11]% and 6·2[IQR:11]% weight loss, respectively, and both improved blood pressure, HbA1c, and lipid profile. The median exposure durations were approximately 4·6 months for the two GLP-1/GIP AOMs, far short of the 72-week duration typically required for peak therapeutic benefit in RCTs. All AOMs showed substantial inter-individual variability in responses, regardless of exposure length. Substantial differences were found between RCT and real-world performance. For example, while Tirzepatide led both cohorts, nearly 50% and 25% of individuals didn’t achieve the 10% and 5% weight loss target respectively, after a minimum 72 weeks exposure, highlighting a notable effectiveness gap. Critically, we identified that Metformin co-therapy acted as a modifier of the ‘rebound effect,’ significantly attenuating (median 1·9 − 2·6%, p < 0.0001) weight regain compared to semaglutide or tirzepatide monotherapy.ConclusionOur findings confirm real-world AOM effectiveness and reveal challenges of the ‘one-size-fits-all’ approach of current pharmacotherapy. The marked inter-individual variability suggests that stratified, personalized pharmacotherapy is essential to transform transient weight loss into sustainable metabolic health.Clinical trialNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12902-026-02423-7.Keywords: Anti-obesity medication, Body weight, BMI, EHR

Background

Obesity is a multifactorial disease that affects over a third of the world’s population [1], and ~ 34% of adults and 15–20% of children/adolescents in the USA [2]. Over 85% of adults are projected to be overweight or obesity by 2030 in the USA [3]. Obesity greatly increases the risk of a wide spectrum of chronic diseases [4], and causes significant social economic burden [5].

During the past decades, various therapies have emerged for obesity treatment, such as bariatric surgeries, anti-obesity medicines (AOMs), hydrogels, vagal nerve blockade, along with dietary control and exercise. However, no single or combination of therapies perform consistently across individuals, either in short or long term. For example, meta-analysis indicates bariatric surgeries patients have wide inter-individual response during longer-term follow-up [6]. A prospective study of 6000 Roux-en-Y gastric bypass patients showed 17·1% with inadequate weight loss 1 year after surgery, and 23·1% as non-responders 5 years after surgery [7]. In contrast, the majority of AOMs reduce average Body Weight (BW) by less than 10% at tolerable doses, with remarkable inter-individual variability [8]. To illustrate, in an Orlistat phase 3 trial [9], 59% participants failed to achieve 10% weight loss in 1 year. In Phentermine phase 3 trial [10], 30% and 52% participants in the high dose (15 mg) intervention group failed to achieve 5% and 10% weight loss in 1 year. While GLP-1 drug Semaglutide claims to achieve an average of > 10% weight loss [11], this was seen in only 69% of 1306 participants [12]. Even with additional intensive behavioral therapy, 10% weight loss was achieved in < 75% participants [12]. The latestly approved GLP-1/GIP drug Tirzepatide [13] achieved 10% weight loss in only 68·5% participants at a relatively safe dose level (5 mg).

Notably, most of the weight loss results came from randomized clinical trials (RCTs), which have rigorous exclusion criteria (e.g. comorbidities), study regulations, limited study duration [14], and potential conflicts of interest stemming from pharmaceutical industry sponsorship. Whereas existing real-world studies are often limited by strict inclusion criteria that yield small cohort sizes [15], or exclude individuals with type 2 diabetes [16]. Others are subject to selection and attrition bias [17], or focus primarily on diabetes incidence rates rather than broader effectiveness [18]. In view of the shortcomings, here we conducted a comprehensive walk through of the UT-Physician EHR systems on individuals’ exposures and responses to various AOMs, and cross-validated the findings in a nationwide cohort curated from the EPIC COSMOS database. We aim to provide a landscape view of the prevalence and effectiveness of AOMs in the general population, along with the complexity of treatment journey experienced by obesity individuals, and highlight the desiderata of next generation phenotyping for precision medicine.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted in Houston, Texas, USA, using two hospital-based EHR data sources, hereafter referred to as the UT-Physician Cohort and the COSMOS Cohort.

UT-physician cohort

This includes 626,078 individuals with either obesity (n = 365,091) or overweight (n = 260,987) diagnosis from 2015/01/01 to 2025/06/30 in the UT-Physician EHR system. The system was served by Allscripts before 2020 and transitioned to EPIC thereafter, which started capturing pharmacy dispensing data from more than 50,000 pharmacies nationwide. All records were mapped to the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) common data model [19]. Obesity was defined using six OMOP diagnosis concepts representing common clinical obesity subtypes (Supp Text 1). For overweight we considered individuals with any comorbidities (Supp Text 2) within 1 year prior to a BMI measurement ≥ 27. After excluding those with obesity-related surgery, finally 610,372 (97.5%) individuals left, among which 71,318 (11·7%) reported AOM exposure (Supp Fig. 1a).

COSMOS cohort

COSMOS is an EPIC-supported, Caboodle data model based platform providing de-identified data from over 300 million individuals (as of Nov 2025) from more than 1,800 hospitals across the United States and Lebanon. COSMOS has drug dispensing information recorded since Apr. 2007. For this cohort, we selected 29,793,115 individuals with ICD-9 and ICD-10 based obesity diagnosis (Supp Text 3) from 2018/01/01 to 2024/12/31. After excluding those with obesity-related surgeries (Supp Text 4), 29,075,858 (97·6%) individuals left. Among which 10,794,237 (37%) reported AOM exposure. Due to limited computation capacity, we randomly selected 1,002,225 (~ 10%) individuals for downstream analysis (Supp Fig. 1b).

FDA approved AOMs (F-AOMs) and off-label AOMs (O-AOMs)

We categorized AOMs into F-AOMs and O-AOMs. F-AOMs include bupropion-naltrexone, orlistat, phentermine w/wo topiramate, and two GLP-1/GIP agonists. O-AOMs include medications that are not FDA-approved for obesity treatment but are commonly prescribed for other approved indications and have recognized weight-reducing effects (e.g., metformin and SGLT2 inhibitors) (Supp Table 1). O-AOM exposure under 30 days without a neighboring record was excluded to remove non-weight-related prescriptions. For the UT-Physician cohort, ingredients and all aliases were mapped via RxNav [20] and OHDSI Athena [21] concept IDs; For the COSMOS cohort, ingredients were identified by regular expression matching.

AOM exposure

For both cohorts, data documented in the pharmacy dispense table was used as a proxy for drug exposure. Specifically, the drug dispense datetime was used as the exposure start date, and the start date plus the days supply was used as the exposure end date.

Drug dose

Drug dose was extracted for the UT-Physician cohort from the descriptive text associated with each dispensing record. The daily dose was obtained by multiplying the milligram-standardized dose amount with the dispensation unit count and then dividing by the days supplied. Dose was not analyzed for the COSMOS cohort due to only minimum dose was documented when dose is a range.

Treatment session

We defined a “treatment session” as a continuous period of exposure to generally the same ingredient(s). Specifically, compared to an existing exposure record RECA, a new exposure record RECB belongs to the same session (as RECA) if it (1) has exactly the same ingredient(s) as RECA and has ≤ 40 days gap, or (2) has fewer ingredients than RECA, and has both exposure length and gap to RECA ≤40 days, or (3) has new ingredient(s) added within 40 days of initiating RECA and has exposure length ≤ 40 days. For all other circumstances, we consider RECB as a new treatment session.

Measurements

We retrieved measurements for BW, body mass index (BMI), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), triglycerides, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and HbA1C level from EHR database. Specifically, as an internal consistency check, BW and BMI were analyzed independently due to discordant measurements in 1% of records. Multiple measurements on a single day were resolved by median value, giving < 20% difference between the largest and the smallest value, or excluded. For each treatment session we considered four prioritized scenarios of valid measurements (Supp Table 3, Supp Fig. 2), and enforce a minimum of 30 days difference between two measurements to allow for meaningful treatment effect. Furthermore, with slight modification [22], we adopted the Eq. (90 * exposure days / 365 days) to calculate customized allowable flanking range, which was further hard-clamped to between 30 days and 180 days to avoid overly stringent or loose requirements. Importantly, a measurement is disqualified if there are other AOM exposures between the measurement date and the date of exposure to the AOM of interest (Supp Fig. 2, scenario 3,4).

Calculation of weight change after AOM discontinuation

For each exposure record, the reference weight was determined by using the median BW recorded from 30 days before to 30 days after the exposure end date. This reference weight was then compared with the median weights from six non-overlapping periods – 30–90 days, 91–180 days, 181 days-1 year, 1–1·5 years, 1·5 − 2 years, and over 2 years – to calculate the percentage changes in weight. For a post-exposure measurement to be valid, individuals must have remained AOM-free from the exposure end until the measurement date.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are summarized as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages. For percentage changes of measurements before and after treatment, the values beyond outer fence (Q3 + 3*IQR, Q1–3*IQR) were removed.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study cohorts

The baseline characteristics of the two study cohorts are shown in Table 1. AOM exposure was recorded in 11·7% of the UT-Physician cohort and 27·9% in the COSMOS cohort, reflecting differences in observation periods (2020–2025 vs. 2018–2024) and population coverage (regional Houston-based vs. nationwide).

AOMs exposure

Analysis of UT-Physician prescription data indicates F-AOMs were rarely prescribed before 2015 (Supp Fig. 3a). From 2015 to 2020, the F-AOMs prescription were dominated by liraglutide, phentermine, and bupropion naltrexone. Significant rise in prescription (Supp Fig. 3a and b) and dispensing (Supp Fig. 3c and d) of both F-AOMs and O-AOMs was observed after 2020, mainly attributable to semaglutide and metformin series, with 29% and 68% prevalence, respectively. Tirzepatide stepped into the market after mid-2022, and became as popular as semaglutide after mid-2024. Similar dispensing trends were observed in the COSMOS cohort (Supp Fig. 3e and f). For the UT-Physician cohort with AOM exposure, 48% received a single AOM, 52% exposed to two or more AOMs; in extreme cases, 15 individuals were exposed to ten or more AOMs. Focusing on F-AOMs, 27% of individuals received one and 10% received two or more F-AOMs.

Number and length of treatment sessions

A treatment session reflects a period of continuous exposure to given ingredient(s). Transitioning to a new session often entails physical, financial, and psychological burdens for individuals and typically reflects suboptimal response to the preceding session. Across both cohorts, roughly 70% of individuals engaged in two or more treatment sessions. Conversely, single-session therapy was documented for only 28% of the UT-Physician and 33% of the COSMOS cohort. Subgroup analyses consistently indicate that individuals with overweight had fewer treatment sessions, while those with morbid obesity had more sessions than other diagnostic categories (Supp Fig. 4).

Across all AOMs, short-term sessions (≤ 112 days) represent the predominant exposure type (Supp Fig. 5), with median session lengths of around 3 months for both cohorts. In the UT-Physician cohort, when ignoring interruptions from different AOM ingredients (Supp Fig. 6), semaglutide and tirzepatide showed nearly identical median treatment session lengths: 4·6[IQR:7·6] months (up to 64 months) and 4·7[8·1] months (up to 39 months), respectively.

Responses of obesity relevant metabolic metrics to AOMs

We investigated the effect of different exposure durations (short-term: 30 to 112 days; medium-term: 113 to 365 days; long-term: >365 days) [22] to F-AOMs (Fig. 1) and O-AOMs (Supp Figs. 7 and 8). The obesity relevant metabolic metrics, including BW, BMI, HDL, LDL, SBP, DBP, HbA1c, and triglyceride levels were analyzed on their percentage changes after AOM exposure. In the following report, we employ both visual judgment and statistical significance tests to (1) prevent overlooking large effect sizes with small sample sizes, and (2) avoid misinterpreting high statistical significance purely due to large sample sizes but without meaningful effect sizes. We summarize the major observations as follows.

Response to F-AOMs (Fig. 1)

BW and BMI

Both tirzepatide and semaglutide were associated with substantial reductions in BW, with median long-term weight losses of 8·5 [10]–9·6 [11]% and 4·8 [10]–6·2 [11]%, respectively. The weight loss improved with longer exposures and reached statistical significance (p < 0·001), with effect sizes substantially outperform other F-AOMs on medium- to long-term exposure. In contrast, under short-term exposure, semaglutide demonstrated comparable effectiveness to phentermine-topiramate combination (< 2%), which conferred better performance than phentermine monotherapy on long-term exposure. Across both cohorts, liraglutide was associated with only modest weight loss of (0·66 [6]-0·89[5·2]%) across all exposure lengths. Analyzing BMI independently yields consistent results.

Lipid levels

All F-AOMs were associated with triglyceride reduction across all exposure durations, with tirzepatide (13 [40]-14 [42]%), semaglutide (10 [43]-12 [41]%), and phentermine-topiramate (8[45]%) demonstrating top long-term benefits. In contrast, reductions in LDL cholesterol were observed only for tirzepatide and semaglutide during short- (tirz: 6·6 [32]-11 [24]%; sema: 6·7 [33]-11 [30]%) to medium-term (tirz: 2·9 [29]-6 [29]%; sema: 3·6 [28]-5·8 [28]%) exposure, with these effects attenuating at longer exposure durations. Conversely, increases in HDL were mainly observed with long-term F-AOMs, and were generally below 10%.

Blood pressure and HbA1c levels

Across both cohorts, only tirzepatide and semaglutide were associated with significant reductions in HbA1c levels, with larger effect size on longer exposure duration and achieved 9 [14]-9·5 [14]% and 5·2 [13]-6·3 [14]% reduction on long-term exposure, respectively. Both AOMs also demonstrated modest < 3% reductions in SBP and DBP, with more reduction on longer exposure. Liraglutide exhibited only minimal HbA1c-lowering effects (< 2·7%) and no consistent impact on blood pressure. In contrast, other F-AOMs showed no appreciable effects on either HbA1c or blood pressure.

Response to O-AOMs (Supp Figs. 7 and 8)

BW and BMI

Among O-AOMs, only long-term exposure to metformin-based regimens and SGLT2 inhibitors (liflozin series) was associated with modest BW reduction (< 5%) across both cohorts, substantially less effective than tirzepatide and semaglutide. Most other O-AOMs did not demonstrate meaningful weight-loss benefits. Notably, long-term exposure to lisdexamfetamine was consistently associated with median weight gain across both cohorts. Combining metformin with tirzepatide or semaglutide did not confer additional weight-loss benefits compared with tirzepatide or semaglutide monotherapy. Analyzing BMI independently yields consistent results.

Lipid levels

Across both cohorts, all O-AOMs were associated with modest reductions in triglycerides, generally below 10%. Notably, the magnitude was consistently greater with short-term exposure than with medium- or long-term exposure. A similar pattern was observed for LDL, with most O-AOMs (except zonisamide and lisdexamfetamine) showing < 10% LDL reductions predominantly during short-term use. In contrast, effects on HDL were modest and inconsistent; when present, HDL increases were largely seen on long-term exposures and generally below 5%.

Blood pressure and HbA1c levels

None of the O-AOMs showed a meaningful effect on either SBP or DBP. The metformin series and SGLT2 inhibitors modestly reduced HbA1c, primarily with short-term exposure, with effect sizes substantially smaller than those observed for F-AOMs.

Variations in BW responses to AOMs

Individual BW responses varied substantially for all AOMs. Among F-AOMs, even for tirzepatide – the most effective AOM in this study – only 42–48% of individuals achieved ≥ 10% weight loss with long-term use, whereas 29–35% failed to reach 5% weight loss and 8–12% experienced net weight gain by the end of exposure. Extending exposure to ≥ 72 weeks yielded only marginal additional benefit (Table 2). In comparison, long-term exposure to semaglutide was associated with weight gain in 21–27% of cases, with only a marginal reduction to 20–26% at ≥ 72 weeks (Table 2). Among F-AOMs, excluding orlistat due to insufficient sample, liraglutide showed the poorest performance, associated with weight gain in ≥ 40% of cases (Table 2). Among O-AOMs, bupropion, lisdexamfetamine, and zonisamide exhibited the least favorable outcomes, with weight gain observed in ≥ 50% of cases on various exposure durations across both cohorts (Supp Tables 1 and 2). While long-term metformin was associated with significant reduction in BW (1·5[7·1]-1·6[7·7]%, Supp Fig. 8), 72–74% of individuals failed to achieve 5% weight loss target, and 39–40% experienced weight gain (Supp Tables 1 and 2).

Scatterplots of exposure records for each F-AOM showed that, for liraglutide and phentermine, the association between exposure duration and BW change was generally weak beyond six months (Fig. 2). For semaglutide, longer exposure causes greater weight loss up to one year, after which no additional population-level benefit was observed. Tirzepatide demonstrated rapid weight reduction within the first year, after which the weight-loss effects were more pronounced among overweight individuals. Across all F-AOMs, substantial inter-individual variability was observed, largely independent of exposure duration. Subgroup analyses showed similar variability regardless of sex, race, diagnosis, or age at initiation.

Weight changes after AOMs discontinuation

We evaluated body-weight changes across six non-overlapping periods following F-AOMs (Fig. 3) and O-AOMs (Supp Figs. 9 and 10) discontinuation. Despite varied individual responses, consistent patterns emerged. Among F-AOMs, phentermine-topiramate, phentermine, and bupropion-naltrexone demonstrated the most pronounced trends in weight regain, reaching up to 3% at 2 years after treatment discontinuation. In contrast, tirzepatide and semaglutide were associated with only modest weight regain up to 1·5 years post-discontinuation. Notably, in the COSMOS cohort co-administered metformin with either tirzepatide or semaglutide exhibited sustained weight-loss effects that was 1·9 − 2·6% (p < 0.0001) lower than monotherapy after discontinuation (Supp Fig. 10). Among O-AOMs, lisdexamfetamine, zonisamide, and topiramate were associated with substantial up to 5% weight regain across multiple post-exposure periods in both cohorts, whereas discontinuing SGLT2 inhibitors and metformin generally did not result in significant weight regain.

Discussion

With two data sources managed under different data models—the OMOP common data model and the COSMOS Caboodle data model—and study cohorts characterized by highly diversified population demographics, we achieved notably consistent insights regarding real-world responses to AOMs and changes in body weight after AOMs cessation. The shorter AOM exposure durations and slightly reduced weight loss effects observed in the UT-Physician cohort likely reflect the fragmented healthcare system and the significant diversity in social determinants of health within the Houston area. Overall, our findings highlight secondary use of EHR as a valuable and reliable strategy for retrospective verification of treatment responses in real-world scenarios, aligning with the increasing popularity of using EHR in RCTs [23] and to derive real-world evidence [24]. The consistency across data models underscores the potential of EHR to support comparative analyses and improve treatment evaluations across varied clinical questions [25].

Several F-AOMs showed significantly less weight loss effect compared to RCTs reports [8]. For instance, semaglutide achieved a 10% weight loss in at least 69% of participants after 68 weeks in a RCT [12], whereas our analysis indicated that up to 25% of individuals reached this threshold after a median exposure of 88 weeks. Similarly, tirzepatide’s effectiveness in our study (10% weight loss in [U:33%; C:42%] of individuals after [U:76; C:79] weeks) fell short of RCT results, where 69% of participants lost at least 10% of their weight over 72 weeks [13]. However, it’s worth noting that our findings align more closely with another RCT of tirzepatide involving obesity individuals with diabetes [26], where a 40-week treatment with 5 mg resulted in 47% of participants losing at least 5% body weight, similar to our [U:48%; C:50%] achieving this goal with an average of [U:39; C:38] weeks exposure.

The disparity can be explained as follows. Firstly, the doses used in real-world clinical settings tend to be more conservative for some AOMs. For example, available data indicates that approximately 80% of semaglutide prescriptions are at or below 0.5 mg weekly, starting from 0.25 mg and gradually increasing to 0.5 mg, which is significantly lower than the 2.4 mg weekly used in RCTs. Despite the observed weekly tirzepatide doses spanning from 2.5 mg to 15 mg, the median dose was only 5 mg/week, substantially lower than the recommended target dose of 15 mg/week. This likely reflects real-world clinical practice, where many patients are in the dose-escalation phase or remain on lower doses based on clinical judgment and tolerability. Secondly, the rigorous inclusion/exclusion criteria of RCTs also play a crucial role. For example, our findings on tirzepatide align better with the trial involving diabetes participants [26] than the trial without diabetes [13], which may reflect the confounding effect of comorbidities observed in real-world settings. Indeed, in the UT-Physician cohort about half of the individuals screened for overweight had at least one HbA1c measurement in the prediabetic range (5.7% to 6.5%), signifying the confounding effect of comorbidities in real-world practice. Thirdly, medication adherence in real-world practice is generally less strict than in RCTs. Adherence issues include non-fulfillment (never initiated), non-persistence (stop taking after initiation), non-conforming (skipping dose, incorrect does) [27] have been reported in various AOM adherence studies [28–30], and likely diminishes AOM effectiveness. Finally, we do not rule out the potential influence of conflicts of interest stemming from pharmaceutical industry sponsorship in many of these RCTs. Overall, our results reflect the general population’s response to AOMs amidst these real-world factors.

Our findings of weight regain after discontinuation of several F-AOMs and O-AOMs were consistently observed in both cohorts and aligned with previous research. Specifically, weight regain following the cessation of bupropion-naltrexone [31] and phentermine [22] (F-AOMs), as well as zonisamide [32] and topiramate [33] (O-AOMs), has been documented in earlier studies. In both cohorts, discontinuing lisdexamfetamine demonstrated remarkably significant weight regain, which however was not formally documented to the best of our knowledge. On the other hand, our analysis indicates that discontinuing tirzepatide or semaglutide only leads to weight regain in fewer than half of individuals, which contrasts with RCTs suggesting that stopping tirzepatide [34] or semaglutide [35] also leads to weight regain. These discrepancies may be explained by sample selection bias, differences in exposure length distributions, and variations in doses. This also underscores the importance of further research to bridge the gap between RCT data and real-world evidence.

We noted substantial variations in individual responses to AOMs, regardless of the duration of exposure. For O-AOMs, variations could be due to the treatment not always being aimed at obesity management, so early response might not influence the decision to continue or discontinue the treatment. This could explain the fluctuations in body weight changes over short to long term exposures. On the other hand, we didn’t specifically observe less variation in body weight responses to long-term F-AOMs, which are supposedly based on short-term effectiveness. This suggests that either some individuals may not be adjusting their treatment plans promptly based on their early response, or a potential tolerance after long-term exposure.

The remarkable variability in individual responses to various AOMs align with the recent shift away from the oversimplified classification of obesity by BMI [36] toward more sophisticated staging strategies [37] and phenotyping-guided therapies [38]. Emerging evidence further suggests that genetic factors may contribute to the heterogeneous response to incretin-based therapies [39–41]. Recent review articles [42, 43] highlighted the necessity of obesity deep phenotyping for precision medicine, as “when considering obesity, every person should be assessed based on their own specific and unique circumstances” [44]. Our findings of highly varied individual response to various AOMs reinforce the clinical significance of phenotype- and genotype-informed approaches to optimize AOM administration.

Conclusion

Our study highlighted the gaps between clinical trials and real-world evidence derived from EHR data regarding the effectiveness of AOMs. These discrepancies point to the challenges of generalizing clinical trial findings to broader, more diverse populations encountered in routine practice. The observed substantial variability in individual responses to both FDA-approved and off-label AOMs further emphasizes the need for a personalized approach to obesity pharmacotherapy, which may not only enhance treatment efficacy but also improve long-term adherence, paving the way for more sustainable obesity care strategies.

Limitations

Here we outline the limitations of this study from two perspectives. From the database perspective, analyses in the UT-Physician cohort were based on relatively small sample sizes for some medications and exposure durations, which may limit generalizability. In addition, some overlap between the UT-Physician and COSMOS databases may exist following UT-Physician’s transition to EPIC in 2020; however, because UT-Physician records account for less than 1% of COSMOS records, any resulting bias is expected to be minimal. Finally, due to computational constraints, COSMOS analyses were limited to data from 2018 to 2023, which may affect estimates for medications more commonly used before 2018 or after 2023.

From the medication exposure perspective, we focused on obesity-related outcomes and did not evaluate adverse events. Medication exposure was inferred from documented pharmacy dispensing rather than confirmed medication fills or adherence; therefore, unrecorded use of compounded AOMs, no actual medication pickup, medication nonadherence, or medications obtained outside participating health systems may not be fully captured. In addition, body weight and BMI measurements were collected across multiple clinical settings and may be subject to variability in measurement practices. Furthermore, body weight and BMI are imperfect measures of obesity and do not distinguish changes in body composition, such as fat mass and lean mass. Finally, detailed titration schedules, dose adjustments, and actual medication consumption patterns were not consistently available and therefore could not be incorporated into the analyses.
