The efficacy and safety of cold snare polypectomy in intermediate nonpedunculated polyps
Authors: Yi Song, Chen Yuan, Xiujie Wang, Gengxu Li, Jun Yang, Youhong Cao, Zhenguo Qiao, Qiang Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCold snare polypectomy (CSP) is increasingly popular because of its safety and convenience, but it is currently recognized as the best way to remove diminutive polyps (≤ 5 mm). CSP for nonpedunculated polyps ≥ 10 mm in size has not been well examined. We evaluate the safety and efficacy of CSP for benign-looking intermediate nonpedunculated polyps.MethodsWe reviewed intermediate nonpedunculated polyps that resected by CSP from 2021 to 2023. The lesions were carefully observed with high-definition white-light (HD-WL) endoscopy and magnifying endoscopy with narrow-band imaging (ME-NBI) before CSP. We analyzed lesion characteristics, complete resection, cold snare defect protrusion (CSDP), histopathology, adverse events, and recurrence/residual. We compared the differences between lesions 10–14 mm in size and lesions ≥ 15 mm in size. We also analyzed risk factors for recurrence/residual and incomplete resection.ResultsWe retrospectively analyzed 631 lesions in 482 patients. The average size was 11.1 ± 1.3 mm, of which 3.3% were ≥ 15 mm. The CSDP rate was 34.1%. The rates of post-polypectomy bleeding (PPB), delayed post-polypectomy bleeding (DPPB), post-polypectomy perforation (PPP) and delayed post-polypectomy perforation (DPPP) were 0.3%, 0.2%, 0.0% and 0.0%, respectively. Two lesions were carcinoma in situ (0.3%) and seven lesions were high-grade dysplasia (HGD) (1.1%). The histological complete resection rate (the horizontal margin) was 94.3%. There were significant differences in sex, histological complete resection rate, and CSDP rate between lesions 10–14 mm in size and lesions ≥ 15 mm in size. CSDP was the only risk factor for incomplete resection of the polyps.ConclusionCSP of intermediate nonpedunculated polyps is safe and effective. The recurrence/residual rate and the adverse event rate were low.

Introduction

Endoscopic resection of colorectal polyps can reduce the incidence of colorectal cancer (CRC) and associated mortality [1]. Polyps can be classified into diminutive (≤ 5 mm), small (6–9 mm), intermediate (10–19 mm), and large polyps (≥ 20 mm) [2]. The endoscopic resection methods of different polyps are different. Cold snare polypectomy (CSP) is currently recognized as the best technique for the removal of diminutive polyp. The European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) and U.S. Multi-Society Task Force on CRC suggested that CSP had high quality evidence for polyps<10 mm in size [3, 4]. Hot snare polypectomy (HSP) and endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) are standard treatments for colorectal polyps ranging from 10 to 19 mm [4]. The use of electrocautery can make the removal of thick mucosal tissue easier and ablate the marginal tissue to reduce the recurrence of polyps. It also can prevent immediate bleeding by thermal vascular coagulation. However, electrocautery heat carries a risk of delayed post-polypectomy bleeding (DPPB), post-polypectomy syndrome (PPS) or perforation [5, 6].

CSP does not require electrocautery, thus significantly reducing the incidence of adverse events. In recent years, the emergence of snare dedicated to cold snaring lead to a greater cutting efficiency [7]. Submucosal injection before CSP can make the lesion edges clearer and effectively reduce the risk of bleeding [8]. A piecemeal approach is gradually being applied to CSP, so that even large lesions can be completely removed [9]. However, there is limited evidence to support this technique for intermediate polyps, and most studies were focused on serrated polyps [10].

In this study, we aimed to investigate the feasibility of CSP for intermediate nonpedunculated colorectal polyps, including diagnostic accuracy, adverse events, histopathological classification, complete resection, recurrence/residual, and risk factors for recurrence/residual and incomplete resection.

Methods

This was a retrospective single-center study. We reviewed intermediate nonpedunculated polyps that were resect by CSP from January 1, 2021 to December 31, 2023. The authors had access to information that could identify individual participants during or after data collection. Lesions were evaluated using high-definition white-light (HD-WL) endoscopy and magnifying endoscopy with narrow-band imaging (ME-NBI). We used Paris Classification [11], JNET Classification [12] and Kudo pit pattern Classification [13] to record the overall characteristics of polyps. We used indigo carmine staining combined with magnifying endoscopy to infer the Kudo pit pattern when polyps were found in patients during colonoscopy. Any lesion suspected of submucosal infiltration (such as Kudo V or JNET 3) was excluded. Other exclusion criteria included: active colitis, severe coagulation dysfunction, coagulation factor deficiency, familial polyposis, and concurrent use of EMR and other thermal cautery methods during CSP. Hyperplastic polyps, tubular adenomas, tubulovillous adenoma, traditional serrated adenoma (TSA), and sessile serrated adenomas/polyps (SSA/P) were all included in the study. The final diagnosis was confirmed by histopathological examination of the CSP specimen. Included patients and related data were obtained electronically from medical records.

The study outcomes included polyp size, morphology, location, surface color, local vascular changes, use of antithrombotic drugs, incidence of cold snare defect protrusion (CSDP) at resection site, whole resection rate, histological complete resection rate, histopathological diagnosis, and adverse events. We analyzed follow-up time and recurrence/residual rate. In addition, we analyzed the risk factors of recurrence/residual and histological incomplete resection.

For histological incomplete resection, it is defined as the detection of diseased glands and cells at the identified resection margin, and for complete resection, it is defined as the absence of diseased glands and cells at the identified resection margin. Post-polypectomy bleeding (PPB) is defined as bleeding for more than 1 min after polypectomy that requires endoscopic intervention to stop bleeding. DPPB is defined as the appearance of bloody stool that requires endoscopic hemostasis, often occurring 24 h after resection [14].

The first surveillance colonoscopy (SC1) was at 3–6 months after CSP. If the postoperative pathology was carcinoma in situ or high-grade dysplasia (HGD), the follow-up colonoscopy was at 3 months after CSP. The Other lesions were followed up at 6 months after CSP. The second surveillance colonoscopy (SC2) was 1 year after CSP. At follow-up colonoscopy, recurrence and residual status were assessed. Recurrence is defined as a lesion on CSP scar [14]. Similarly, HD-WL and ME-NBI were used to observe the recurrent or residual lesions. If a scar was found but there was no polyp, a biopsy of the scar was performed to confirm. The recurrent/residual polyps found during follow-up were treated directly with appropriate methods according to their size and characteristics.

CSP procedure

All CSP procedures were performed by 1 of 3 experienced endoscopists with over 5000 colonoscopy examinations. The patients were instructed to have liquid diet the day before procedure and underwent a divided dose intestinal preparation consisting of polyethylene glycol (PEG), anhydrous sodium sulfate, sodium chloride, potassium chloride, and sodium bicarbonate. The anesthesiologist used propofol to sedate. According to the guidelines, all patients undergoing endoscopic surgery should not use antiplatelet and anticoagulant drugs, except aspirin [15]. We used an open snare to assess the size of the lesion. We used the endoscopes of GIF-HQ290 (Olympus Tokyo, Japan). The following snares were used: a 15-mm-diameter diamond shape snare with a wire diameter of 0.23 mm (Micro-Tech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) and a 24-mm-diameter diamond shape snare with a wire diameter of 0.23 mm (Micro-Tech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Pit and JNET were detected by HD-WL and ME-NBI, and polyps were removed by CSP technique (Fig. 1). According to the size of the lesion, the endoscopist decided whether the lesion should be resected as a whole or piecemeal resection. When the whole resection failed, subsequent piecemeal resection was performed. All polyps were resected without submucosal injection. Each lesion, along with a normal tissue margin of 2–3 millimeters, was resected using CSP. When some large flat polyps were difficult to resect whole, piecemeal resection was performed. CSP used a snare, and polyp was trapped, including the normal mucosa surrounding the lesion, to maintain the non-neoplastic mucosal margin around the resected specimen. The electrocoagulation was not used throughout the entire process. HD-WL and ME-NBI were used to observe CSP defects and edges. Any suspected residual polyp tissue was further resected using a cold snare. All resected tissue fragments were subjected to histological examination through a suction channel. Then, biopsy sampling was evenly spaced around the marginal circumference of the defect to assess for residual microscopic polyp tissue. If no residue was found in the biopsy specimens of the four quadrants, we determined a complete resection. These biopsy specimens were submitted separately from the polyp specimens and determined by the experienced pathologists. If bleeding occurred immediately after CSP, the resection site should be observed until bleeding stopped. If there was still continuous bleeding or splashing bleeding for more than 1 min, electrocoagulation was needed. If the polyp could not be resected with CSP, electrocoagulation could be used for resection. The resected lesion was aspirated and removed after completion of the CSP.

Fasting on the first day after procedure and returning to normal diet the next day. If patients experienced any bleeding after procedure that deviated from the expected routine CSP clinical course and required endoscopic intervention, it was considered clinically significant DPPB. All patients were reviewed in the outpatient department at 4 weeks after procedure. The focus of follow-up was to inform the histological results, identify the delayed adverse events, and plan surveillance colonoscopy.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 was used for statistical analysis of the data. The measurement data was tested for normality. All data conforming to normal distribution was represented by mean ± standard deviation (‾x ± S) and independent sample T-test was used for comparison between groups. Data that did not conform to normal distribution was represented by median (quartile), and comparison between groups was performed by Mann-Whitney U test. The categorical count data was represented by the number of cases (%), and χ2 test was used for comparison between groups. Logistic regression was used to analyze the risk factors affecting recurrence/residual and histological incomplete resection. p-Values < 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Baseline data analysis

We reviewed 631 intermediate polyps resected by CSP in 482 patients. Among the included patients, 23.4% with high blood pressure, 9.8% with diabetes, 1.5% with coronary heart disease, 0.8% with liver cirrhosis, and other diseases included rheumatoid arthritis (0.4%), cerebral infarction (1.0%) and so on.

We performed endoscopic treatments on a total of 1107 intermediate lesions. 63 lesions were pathologically indicated as intramucosal carcinoma or high-grade dysplasia. 25 lesions were removed by EMR and 29 lesions by endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD). We removed 57% of the lesions through CSP, 9 lesions were intramucosal carcinoma or high-grade dysplasia, only accounting for 1.4%. We identified 85.7% of the intramucosal carcinoma or high-grade dysplasia through HD-WL endoscopy and ME-NBI, and excluded them from this study.

Our main research subject was nonpedunculated colorectal polyps, but we also observed 50 pedunculated polyps. The polyp size was 11.1 ± 1.3 mm (range 10–16 mm). The incidence of polyps in males was 64.7%, which was significantly higher than that in female. 3.3% polyps were ≥ 15 mm. 28.7% polyps were located in the sigmoid colon, 25.0% in the ascending colon, 15.9% in the descending colon, 14.4% in the transverse colon, 11.1% in the rectum, and 4.9% in the ileocecal region. 2.3% patients used antithrombotic drugs. The whole resection rate was 99.7%. In the 2 cases with piecemeal resection, both were resected with two to three pieces. The endoscopic complete resection rate was 99.1%. The histological complete resection rate (the horizontal margin) was 94.3%. The CSDP rate was 34.1% (Fig. 2). At the end of the data collection for this study, 311 (64.5%) of 482 patients underwent SC1, and 130 (41.8%) of the 311 SC1 patients underwent SC2. Of 311 patients, recurrence/residual was found in 20 patients (6.4%) at SC1, and of 130 patients, recurrence/residual was found in 6 patients (4.6%) at SC2 (Table 1).

Clinical outcomes of CSP for intermediate polyps

After observed the lesions retrospectively, we found that 92 (14.6%) lesions with red surfaces and 10 (1.6%) lesions had local vascular changes. Recurrence/residual risk factor analysis

Regarding the comparison between non-recurrence/residual group and recurrence/residual group, there were not significant differences in location, age, sex, polyp size, Paris Classification, Kudo Classification, Vienna Classification, redness on the surface of the lesions, local vascular changes, JNET Classification, endoscopic complete resection, histological complete resection and CSDP rate (P > 0.05). With the recurrence/residual as the dependent variable (no = 0, yes = 1), binary logistic regression was established with location, age, sex, polyp size, Paris Classification, Kudo Classification, Vienna Classification, redness on the surface of the lesion, local vascular changes, JNET Classification, endoscopic complete resection, histological complete resection and CSDP rate as independent variables, respectively. The results showed that there was no significant correlation between each variable and recurrence/residual (P > 0.05).

Incomplete resection risk factor analysis

Regarding the comparison between histological complete resection group and histological incomplete resection group, there were not significant differences in location, polyp size, Paris Classification, Kudo Classification, Vienna Classification, redness on the surface of the lesion, local vascular changes, JNET Classification and resection method (P > 0.05). The CSDP rate in the histological incomplete resection group was significantly higher than that in the histological complete resection group (P < 0.05). Binary logistic regression was established with whether the lesion was completely resected as the dependent variable (no = 1, yes = 0). The results showed that there was significant correlation between polyp size, resection method, CSDP rate and histological incomplete resection (P < 0.05) (Table 5). Using incomplete resection as the dependent variable (No = 0, Yes = 1), polyp size, resection method, and CSDP as independent variables, a logistic regression model was established. The results showed that CSDP had statistical significance (P < 0.001) in the model and was a risk factor for histological incomplete resection in patients (OR = 5.037, 95% CI: 2.329 ~ 10.896) (Table 6).

Safety

PPB occurred in 2 (0.3%) lesions, and DPPB occurred in 1 (0.2%) lesion. The PPB lesions were treated with snare tip soft coagulation of the bleeding vessel. The DPPB lesion was treated with hot forceps soft coagulation first, and then treated with hemostatic clips. Treatment of bleeding in all 3 lesions was successful. In addition, 3 (0.5%) lesions showed persistent bleeding after resection, but stopped spontaneously after observation in 1 min. Nine (1.4%) lesions developed hematoma after resection, but no enlargement was observed and no intervention required. One patient underwent colonoscopy due to hematochezia 6 days after CSP. However, that patient was found to have a clean wound, with granulation tissue on the surface, and the bleeding was caused by anal fissure. One patient developed hematochezia 10 days after CSP, and colonoscopy showed that the ascending colon wound began to heal without obvious bleeding. The hematochezia was considered to be caused by hemorrhoids. Another patient had bloody stool on the third day after CSP. Colonoscopy showed a clean-based post-CSP wound. Because there was no visible vessel, endoscopic intervention was not required, but an ulcer was visible in the descending colon. The bleeding was considered to be caused by ischemic bowel disease. There were no cases of PPP, DPPP, post-polypectomy syndrome (PPS), or deep mural injury (Table 1).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the largest study investigating safety and efficacy outcomes for CSP of intermediate nonpedunculated colorectal polyps. Many studies have reported the therapeutic effects and adverse events of CSP. However, there are few reports for lesions larger than 10 mm [16–18]. Our study of 482 patients with 631 polyps demonstrates that CSP is safe in intermediate polyps. The incidence of adverse events was 2.9%. Only 0.5% of lesions experienced bleeding that required endoscopic intervention. 0.5% of the lesions experienced bleeding but stopped spontaneously after endoscopic observation. 1.4% of the lesions experienced hematoma but no intervention required. No patients experienced PPP, DPPP, or PPS.

The incomplete resection is defined as the presence of neoplastic tissue in the marginal specimen. A study compared the histological polyp eradication rates of CSP and cold forceps polypectomy (CFP), and the results showed the CSP group was significantly higher than the CFP group (93.2% vs. 75.9%, P = 0.009) [19]. Subsequently, CSP was more widely used in clinic. CSP could be used as one of the standard techniques for 4–9 mm colorectal polyps. The complete resection rate of CSP for 4–9 mm colorectal adenomatous polyps was 98.2% compared with 97.4% of HSP [18]. A multicenter randomized trial included patients with 5–9 mm polyps, and the results showed that the complete resection rate of CSP and HSP were 92.5% and 94.0%, respectively. There was no difference in complete resection and bleeding rates between CSP and HSP, but CSP reduced the intensity and duration of abdominal pain after colonoscopy [20]. For colorectal polyps larger than 10 mm, the application prospect of CSP is not very clear. Currently, it is considered that large sessile serrated lesions (SSLs) can be a candidate for CSP. For particularly large lesions, cold piecemeal EMR or piecemeal cold snare polypectomy (p-CSP) seems to be the good new method [21]. To the large (≥ 20 mm) SSLs, p-CSP is technically equally efficacious to EMR but virtually eliminates the risk of delayed bleeding and perforation [22]. We included intermediate polyps in our study, with the vast majority being nonpedunculated and a few being pedunculated. All lesions were carefully observed by HD-WL and ME-NBI before resection. After resection, the edges were observed again by HD-WL and ME-NBI. The complete resection was evaluated by performing four quadrant biopsies at the resection edge. In our study, the whole resection rate was 99.7%, the piecemeal resection rate was 0.3%, the endoscopic complete resection rate was 99.1%, and the histological complete resection was 94.3%. These results were comparable to EMR reported before, but the probability of adverse events occurring in CSP was significant lower.

CSDP may cause residual polyp. Some researchers found that CSDP occurred in 14% of polyps, and were associated with polyps ≥ 6 mm. CSDP did not represent vascular structure, and was not associated with adverse outcomes at short-term follow-up. However, CSDP represented incomplete mucosal layer resection [23]. Satoki Shichijo et al. recruited patients with 4–9 mm nonpedunculated polyps undergoing polypectomy. They found that the overall incidence of incomplete mucosal layer resection was 63% (159/250), 76% (68/90) with CSDP and 57% (91/159) without CSDP (P < 0.01). Univariate and multivariate analyses showed that size (≥ 6 mm), resection time (≥ 5 s), and serrated lesions were risk factors for CSDP. The incidence of CSDP was 36%, which was a good indicator of incomplete mucosal excision [24]. In our study, we retrospectively analyzed the incidence of CSDP and found that 215 out of 631 lesions had CSDP, with an incidence rate of 34.1%. Unfortunately, we did not biopsy the mucosal defect center, so we could not further histologically verify the relationship between CSDP and incomplete resection. We evaluated histological complete resection with 4 quadrant biopsies around mucosal defects, but it was worth noting that this referred to complete resection of the horizontal margin. Based on previous studies, CSDP was considered to be a good indicator of incomplete mucosal resection (vertical margin). Through univariate and multifactor regression analysis, we showed that CSDP was a risk factor for histological incomplete resection (horizontal margin). We speculated that CSDP was prone to occur when the captured tissue was hard and difficult to transect. A positive horizontal cutting edge often meant that the trap did not completely transect the less rigid tissue, while stiff tissue, such as the muscular mucosa, was more likely to remain in situ and could not be resect.

HSP and EMR are recognized as the standard methods for the resection of colorectal polyps ≥ 10 mm [4]. The use of electrocautery not only makes transverse resection through thick mucosal tissue easier, but also ablates the residual margin of dysplastic tissue, reducing the recurrence and residue of polyps. However, adverse events such have been associated with electrogenic injury. Due to the benefits of CSP, it is gradually being used for larger lesions. A study using cold snare EMR in duodenal adenomas ≥ 15 mm and the results showed that CS-EMR reduced PPB and DPPB. However, it increased the risk of immediate perforation and was significantly associated with an increased recurrence rate of first surveillance endoscopy at 6 months [9]. A systematic review and meta-analysis included eight studies with an average lesion size of 17.5 mm (range 10–60 mm). The results showed that the total adverse event rate was 1.1%. During a follow-up period of 154–258 days, the total residual rates for polyps, adenomas, and SSPs were 4.1%, 11.1%, and 1.0%, respectively. Therefore, SSPs ≥ 10 mm could be safely resected with a low residual polyp rate, and polyp size ≥ 20 mm was a significant factor for residual [25]. Klein et al. found that thermal ablation of the post-EMR mucosal defect margin reduced polyp recurrence at first surveillance colonoscopy significantly [26]. In our study, tubular adenomas accounted for 76.4%, hyperplastic polyps accounted for 11.4%, and sessile serrated polyps accounted for 3.9%. The recurrence/residual rate was 6.4% at SC1 and 4.6% at SC2, and was comparable to the conclusion drawn by Klein et al. We believed that the low recurrence/residual rate in our study was related to the detailed lesion evaluation with HD-WL and ME-NBI before and after resection. The snare trapped a generous margin of normal tissue at resection. After resection, the mucosal defect was carefully examined again and any remaining problematic tissue was removed. Therefore, careful lesion assessment and standardized resecting procedures are prerequisites for CSP to benefit greatly.

We retrospectively analyzed the color and local vascular changes of all lesions, and classified them according to Paris Classification, Kudo Classification and JNET Classification. The results showed that 14.6% of the lesions had surface redness, while only 1.6% of the lesions had local vascular changes. 56.6% of polyps were 0-Is type, 30.9% were 0-IIa type, 4.6% were 0-IIb type, and 7.9% pedicled polyps were also included in our study. Our data suggest that CSP is safe and effective to the intermediate nonpedunculated polyps. In addition, a large percentage of our cases were adenomas, and the recurrence rate was significantly lower than the previously reported 18% [27]. It suggesting that CSP is feasible, safe, and effective not only for SSA/P, but also for tubular and tubulovillous adenoma. Although no adverse events were reported in the included pedicled polyps, the sample size was very small, and we do not recommend the use of CSP for them. According to the histology results, we found that 82.7% was LGD, 1.1% was HGD, and 0.3% was carcinoma in situ. This also showed that the pathological diagnosis was consistent with our clinical evaluation, and further verified the necessity of the application of Kudo Classification and JNET Classification in polypectomy. We closely followed up with 7 patients with HGD and 2 patients with carcinoma in situ, none of them recurred before SC2. Detailed endoscopic evaluation can help endoscopists identify tumor lesions as much as possible. Therefore, in the future, we will provide systematic training to endoscopists. We hope to include cases of accidental resection of carcinomas during CSP through multicenter studies to understand the endoscopic characteristics of these lesions and help identify tumor lesions.

There were two CSP methods for the polyps ≥ 10 mm, one with injection and one without injection [10, 28]. Some researchers believed that injection could make the lesion edge clear and prevent perioperative bleeding [29]. However, we could observe the edge of the lesion in detail and accurately through HD-WL and ME-NBI, and the bleeding rate did not significantly increase in our results. We analyzed 610 lesions of 10–14 mm and 21 lesions of ≥ 15 mm. We found that in lesions of ≥ 15 mm, the male incidence rate was higher, the histological complete resection was significantly lower, and the incidence of CSDP was higher. The HGD and carcinoma in situ all occurred in 10–14 mm lesions. Therefore, we believe that regardless of the size of the polyp, a detailed endoscopic evaluation is needed before resection.

We also performed risk factor analyses for recurrence/residual and incomplete resection. Previous studies found that female sex was an independent risk factor for incomplete resection, incomplete resection was not associated with the polyp characteristics [30]. Another study identified CSDP as the only risk factor of incomplete resection [24]. Our study showed that recurrence/residual had no significant correlation with polyp size, location, morphology, classification, CSDP and other factors. Univariate analysis showed that polyp size, resection method, CSDP were related to histological incomplete resection, while multivariate analysis showed that CSDP was a risk factor for histological incomplete resection.

There are several limitations in this study. First, this was a single-center study, our results could not be applied to other hospitals. The snares and endoscopes we used were not necessarily universal, so multi-center studies are needed to further verify our results in the future. Second, this was a retrospective study. We did not compare CSP with other procedures such as EMR, and we plan to conduct prospective controlled trial in groups next. Third, our assessment of scars was mainly based on endoscopic images. Some scar could not be identified, which might be potential sites for recurrence. Therefore, our recurrence rate might be underestimated. Fourth, our histological complete resection was based on the horizontal margin. However, we did not biopsy at the resection center, so we did not perform a histological assessment of the vertical margin. In the future, we hope to find an optimal method to more accurately determine the integrity of the CSP specimens. Fifth, although we conducted risk factor analysis for recurrence/residual, the small number of recurrence/residual cases might have influenced the results to some extent. Sixth, in our study, the proportion of polyps larger than 15 mm was relatively small, and the polyp sizes was ranging from 10 to 16 mm. In the future, we will include more polyps larger than 15 mm to validate our conclusions. Seventh, the low follow-up rate might introduce a potential bias, which might limit the generalizability and accuracy of our recurrence estimates. In the future, we will pay more attention to the compliance of the patients with follow-up visits to improve the quality of the conclusions.

In conclusion, even considering the limitations of our retrospective study design, our data strongly suggest that CSP is safe and feasible for intermediate nonpedunculated polyps, but detailed endoscopic evaluation is an essential prerequisite.
