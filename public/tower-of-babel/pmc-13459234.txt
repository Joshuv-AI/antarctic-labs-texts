Cervical cancer screening among HIV-positive women in conflict-affected and resource limited settings: the case of Tigray region, Ethiopia
Authors: Haftom Gebrehiwot Woldearegay, Gebreamlak Gidey Abebe, Solomon Weldemariam, Feven Gebregziabher Tesfay, Mulugeta Gebregziabher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical cancer remains a significant public health concern, particularly among women living with HIV/AIDS, who are at increased risk due to compromised immunity. Despite the high burden of cervical cancer in sub-Saharan Africa, including Tigray, Ethiopia, the magnitude of cervical lesions within screening programs and its associated determinants remain inadequately addressed. This study assesses the magnitude and risk factors of precancerous cervical lesion among HIV-infected women in conflict-prone and resource- limited settings of Tigray, Ethiopia.MethodsWe conducted a facility-based cross-sectional study involving 2004 women attending antiretroviral therapy clinics. As part of the routine standard of care for cervical cancer screening in Ethiopia, participants underwent Visual Inspection with 5% Acetic Acid (VIA) at five sites of Mekelle public health institutions between June, 21, 2023, to May 30, 2024. Data were collected through structured interviews using Kobo toolbox and analyzed using SPSS version 23 with the use of logistic regression to examine associations between VIA positivity, demographics, and other risk factors.ResultsOf 2004 clients with HIV/AIDS who received cervical cancer screening, 378 (18.9%) had a VIA+ result. The odds of being VIA+ were 2.39 times higher among women with a history of sexually transmitted infections (AOR = 2.39; 95% CI: 1.72–3.31). Women who had multiple life time sexual partners had 1.94 times higher odds of being VIA+ (AOR = 1.94; 95% CI: 1.45–2.58). Similarly, women whose spouse with unknown life time number of sexual partners had 1.7 times higher odds of being VIA + as compared with monogamous [AOR = 1.71, 95% CI = 1.09, 2.68].The odds were also higher among women with increased parity (AOR = 2.06; 95% CI: 1.28–3.30) and those who initiated sexual activity before the age of 18 (AOR = 1.57; 95% CI: 1.23–2.01).ConclusionsThis study showed a high magnitude of precancerous cervical lesions, affecting nearly to one- fifth of women living with HIV. Key risk factors identified multiple sexual partners, history of sexually transmitted infections (STIs), parity, and early sexual debut. This study demonstrates the need to support implementation efforts that prioritize and facilitate STIs prevention and safer sexual practices.Keywords: Cervical cancer, Lesions, Conflict affected, Resource limited setting, HIV infected Tigray Ethiopia

Introduction

Cervical cancer is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women, disproportionately affecting those in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The disease is primarily caused by persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV), a common sexually transmitted infection. While most HPV infections are cleared naturally by the immune system, persistent infections can lead to precancerous lesions, which may progress to invasive cervical cancer if left untreated [1]. Women living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) are particularly vulnerable, with studies indicating that they have a six-fold increased risk of developing cervical cancer compared to HIV-negative women [2]. This association is primarily due to the immunosuppressive effects of HIV, which reduce the body’s ability to clear HPV infections and control neoplastic transformation. The late detection of these lesions is often linked to poor survival rates following surgical or radiotherapeutic interventions. While some HPV infections and associated precancerous lesions regress spontaneously, chronic infections pose a significant risk of progression to invasive cervical cancer [3].

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends a multi-pronged approach to cervical cancer prevention, emphasizing HPV vaccination for girls aged 9–14 years, regular screening for early detection, and timely treatment of precancerous lesions [4]. In resource-limited settings, WHO advocates for visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA) as a cost-effective and accessible screening method [5, 6]. VIA allows for immediate identification of abnormalities and facilitates prompt treatment, thereby reducing the risk of progression to cervical cancer [7]. Despite these recommendations, the burden of cervical cancer remains disproportionately high in Sub-Saharan Africa, where an estimated 80% of cases are diagnosed at advanced stages, limiting treatment options and survival outcomes [8]. Recent WHO guideline updates for women living with HIV recommend initiating cervical cancer screening at age 25, with repeat screening every 3 to 5 years. HPV DNA testing is increasingly emphasized as the preferred primary screening modality, with triage strategies using VIA, cytology, or colposcopy for women who test positive. This approach aims to improve early detection and reduce cervical cancer incidence and mortality more effectively than VIA alone [6, 7].

Cervical cancer remains a significant public health concern in Ethiopia, ranking as the second most common cancer among women. According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), in 2020, there were approximately 7,445 new cases of cervical cancer in Ethiopia, with 5,338 (71.7%) deaths attributed to the disease [9]. However, these figures likely underrepresent the actual burden due to low awareness, limited access to screening and treatment services, and the absence of a comprehensive national cancer registry [10]. Several risk factors contribute to the high magnitude of cervical cancer in Ethiopia, including persistent HPV infection, tobacco smoking, high parity, prolonged hormonal contraceptive use, and co-infection with HIV [11]. Women living with HIV face an elevated risk of developing both precancerous lesions and invasive cervical cancer due to immune suppression [12]. Despite the availability of effective preventive measures, cervical cancer screening coverage remains alarmingly low. Globally, screening coverage in low-income countries is estimated at 19%, compared to 63% in high-income countries [13]. In Ethiopia, coverage is even lower, with some regions reporting screening rates below 1%, particularly in rural and conflict-affected settings [14].

In February 2021, a collaborative effort involving Wings of Healing Ethiopia, the Federal Ministry of Health, and the Tigray Regional Health Bureau launched cervical cancer prevention strategies, including VIA and cryotherapy, primarily targeting people living with HIV/AIDS [15]. However, the ongoing conflict in Tigray has severely disrupted healthcare services, leading to the collapse of screening and treatment programs, shortages of essential medical supplies, and increased vulnerability among women at risk of cervical cancer.

Despite international efforts to restore healthcare services, there remains a significant gap in epidemiological data on cervical cancer lesions and screening uptake among HIV-infected women in conflict-affected and resource-limited settings of the Tigray region. Using data from the Wings of Healing cervical cancer screening program, which provides insights into preventive care services in the region, this study aims to assess the magnitude of cervical cancer lesions and associated factors among HIV-infected women in conflict-affected, low-resource settings: The case of Tigray region, Ethiopia. Understanding the burden of disease and screening status in this fragile context is essential for informing policy, strengthening post-conflict health system recovery, and improving access to preventive care for high-risk populations.

Methods

This was a cross-sectional, facility-based study using project-collected cervical cancer screening data from 2,076 HIV-positive women who underwent Visual Inspection with 5% Acetic Acid (VIA) for the detection of precancerous cervical lesions. Of these, 72 women had missing data for certain variables and were excluded. Thus, a total of 2004 women were included in the final analysis. The study was conducted between 21 st June 2023 and 30th May 2024 at five public health facilities in Mekelle, Tigray - Ayder Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (2 sites), Mekelle General Hospital, Semen Health Center, and Kasech Health Center. All facilities were operated by Wings of Healing Ethiopia as part of the regional cervical cancer prevention initiative. Data were obtained from the cervical cancer screening project records, which included structured interviews and facility-based assessments, enabling evaluation of both the magnitude of precancerous cervical lesions and associated risk factors.

A wing of Healing (WoH) is a non-governmental organization dedicated to improving women’s health in Ethiopia by providing free cervical cancer screening and treatment services. The organization addresses the high rates of cervical cancer through early detection and intervention, with a particular focus on underserved women who have limited access to healthcare. In partnership with organizations such as Studio Samuel, WoH Ethiopia works to reduce cervical cancer incidence and mortality by promoting awareness and increasing access to screening and treatment services, ultimately improving the health outcomes of women [16, 17].

Sample size determination

The sample size was defined by the total number of women screened as part of the cervical cancer prevention project during the study period. Accordingly, all 2,076 HIV-positive women who underwent VIA screening for precancerous cervical lesions between 21 June 2023 and 30 May 2024 were included. Since the study used data from an existing project rather than routine medical records, no a priori sample size calculation was required. This approach ensured that the analysis captured the entire eligible population within the program.

Sampling technique:

A census sampling approach was employed, whereby all eligible HIV-positive women who underwent VIA screening during the study period were included in the analysis. All women were assessed across the cervical cancer screening sites in Mekelle, ensuring representativeness of the study population.

Outcome variable

Primary outcome variable:

Presence of precancerous cervical lesion (VIA positive vs. VIA negative) among women living with HIV.

Secondary/related outcome variable:

Lesions suspicious for cervical cancer (requiring further evaluation, biopsy, or referral), which is a more severe category identified during screening.

These outcome variables are dichotomous (yes/no) and are analyzed in relation to demographic, reproductive, and behavioral risk factors such as history of STIs, number of lifetime sexual partners, parity, and age at first sexual intercourse.

Measurement

VIA results were interpreted following the Ethiopian National Cervical Cancer Screening and Treatment Training Material [18].

A positive VIA result was characterized by the presence of raised, thickened white plaques or acetowhite epithelium near the squamo-columnar junction (SCJ).

A negative VIA result was defined by a smooth, pink, uniform cervix without acetowhite changes, with findings such as cervical ectropion, polyps, cervicitis, inflammation, or nabothian cysts noted after applying dilute acetic acid. Eligibility for treatment was based on lesion characteristics: Eligible for cryotherapy or thermal ablation: lesions not suspicious for cancer, occupying < 75% of the cervix, not extending onto the vaginal wall, and not exceeding 2 mm beyond the cryoprobe diameter. Ineligible for cryotherapy: pregnant women, women < 12 weeks postpartum, cervicitis or pelvic inflammatory disease, or lesions > 75% of the cervix, extending into the vaginal wall, or exceeding 2 mm beyond the cryoprobe (Fig. 1)

Data processing and statistical analysis

Data were collected using the Kobo tool. In cases of uncertainty regarding VIA test results, consultations with gynecologists and oncologists were conducted as deemed necessary to minimize bias. For example, ambiguous or borderline VIA findings were reviewed collaboratively before final classification. Completed questionnaires were entered into the Kobo Toolbox daily and reviewed for completeness, accuracy, and consistency by supervisors and investigators. Any errors identified were addressed through corrective team discussions to ensure data integrity.

Descriptive statistics were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Multicollinearity was assessed by computing tolerance and VIF values, with all values within acceptable limits (tolerance > 0.75, VIF < 5). For variables that were not applicable to certain participants, such as “History of STI for the spouse” and “Lifetime number of sexual partners” an “Unknown” category was created and retained in both descriptive and regression analyses. The “Unknown” category represented women who were single or widowed or those who could not provide applicable information. These categories were analyzed separately and were not merged with other exposure groups to avoid misclassification bias.

Logistic regression analysis was employed for identifying predictors of VIA positive result. Variables with p-values < 0.2 in bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable model. A backward stepwise selection method was used to systematically remove variables that did not contribute significantly to the model, with variable retention based on the likelihood ratio test and a significance level of p < 0.05. The model’s fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (p = 0.396). Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 27.0.

Reproductive health characteristics of study participants

Approximately two third (n = 1274) of the participants reported having their first sexual intercourse after the age of 18 years. The mean age at first sexual intercourse was 18.5 years (SD = 3.1), with a minimum of 11 years and a maximum of 35 years, resulting in a range of 24 years. In terms of parity, six out of ten women were multiparous, while 19.5% were primiparous, and 16.3% were nulliparous. Among the total screened women, 435(21.7%) were using contraceptives at the time of study. Additionally, one thousand two hundred twenty women (61%) reported having had more than one life time sexual partner. Regarding sexually transmitted infections, about 322 (16.1%) of the participants were diagnosed with a current STI. Furthermore, approximately three-fourths (n = 1,530) of the women had regular follow-up appointments for antiretroviral therapy (ART) (Table 2).

Magnitude of pre-cancerous lesions of the cervix among women living with HIV

In this study, the magnitude of pre-cancerous cervical lesions among women living with HIV was 18.9% (n = 378), as determined through Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid (VIA) screening. The majority of the screened women had negative VIA results (81.1%, n = 1,626), while 3.5% (n = 72) were found to have lesions suspicious for cervical cancer, necessitating further diagnostic evaluation (Table 3).

Factors associated with pre-cancerous cervical lesion of the cervix among women living with HIV

Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with pre-cancerous cervical lesion. The following variables: menstrual bleeding pattern, having symptoms of cervical cancer, life time number of sexual partners of the mother and spouse, history of STIs of the mother and the spouse, current status of sexual transmitted infections, parity and age at first sexual contact were significantly associated with the outcome variable. These variables were transferred into the multivariable analysis and after controlling the confounders; life time number of sexual partners of the mother and spouse, history of STIs, parity, and age at first sexual contact were significantly associated with pre-cancerous cervical lesion.

Therefore, the odds of having pre-cancerous cervical lesion among HIV positive women who had multiple life time number of sexual partners were 1.94 [AOR = 1.94, 95% CI = 1.45, 2.58]. Women whose spouse with unknown life time number of sexual partners were 1.7 times more likely to have pre-cancerous cervical lesion as compared to women with monogamous spouse [AOR = 1.71, 95% CI = 1.09, 2.68]. Women with history of STIs were more than two times more likely to develop pre-cancerous cervical lesion when compared to their counterparts [AOR = 2.39, 95% CI = 1.72, 3.31]. The odds of having pre-cancerous cervical lesion among multipara and primi-para were 2 and 2.7 respectively as compared to nulli-para women [AOR = 2.06, 95% CI = 1.28, 3.30] and [AOR = 2.71, 95% CI = 1.79, 4.09]. Moreover, women who started sexual contact at the age of less than 18 years old were 1.6 times more likely to develop pre-cancerous cervical lesion as compared to women who initiated sexual contact at greater than age of 18 years old [AOR = 1.57, 95% CI = 1.23, 2.01] (Table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the magnitude and associated factors of pre-cancerous cervical lesions among women living with HIV in conflict-affected and resource-limited settings of Tigray, Ethiopia. Our findings indicate that nearly one-fifth of the participants were affected by pre-cancerous cervical lesions, highlighting a substantial burden in this vulnerable population. This high magnitude underscores the increased susceptibility of women living with HIV to cervical abnormalities, driven by factors such as high-risk sexual behavior, early age at first sexual intercourse, multiple lifetime sexual partners, and a history of STIs. In a broader context, these findings are consistent with trends observed in other African countries, where the prevalence of pre-cancerous cervical lesions remains alarmingly high among women living with HIV.

In this study, the magnitude of pre-cancerous lesions of the cervix among women was 378 (18.9%). The finding of this study is almost consistent with the study conducted in South Ethiopia (22.1%) [20], Central Ethiopia (16%) [21], a systematic review report from Ethiopia (15.34%) [22], Northwest Ethiopia (15.3%) and reports from Malawi (18%) [23, 24]. However, the findings of this study are slightly lower in magnitude as compared to studies conducted in Central Kenya (26.7%) [25], SSA metanalysis (25.6%) [26], Gondar hospital (24.48%) [27], and Wollega (27.4%) [28], where women were positive for VIA screening in resource limited settings. The observed discrepancy in the magnitude of precancerous lesion between this study and the studies conducted previously could be partly due to the differences in time of the research conducted, the sample size, the socio-demographic variation among the study participants and sexual behavior of the participants.

The time span between studies can bring differences in the magnitude of the disease. Moreover, there could be variation in sexual practice, and health seeking behavior due to cultural difference between Kenyan and Ethiopian. Having multiple sexual partners and unsafe sexual practice increases the risk of contracting human papilloma virus (HPV) infections and in turn the development of the cervical lesion. The meta-analysis study conducted in SSA also includes large sample size from different countries of SSA with different sexual behavior due to culture difference, and time difference between the studies. On the other side our finding was slightly higher in magnitude of precancerous cervical lesion compared to previous studies conducted in Tigray region (8.95%) [29], south Ethiopia (14%) [30], Amhara Region (9.9%) [31], Northwest Ethiopia (13.9%) [32] and Togo (11.4%) [33]. The discrepancy in magnitude between our study and previous studies could be due to variations in sample size, time elapsed between studies, and the target population of the studies. For instance, the sample size of all the studies conducted previously was small sample size whereas the current study conducted among large size. The target population of the two studies conducted in Tigray and Gojam-Amhara were general population whereas the current study includes women living with HIV, which is a vulnerable group of people for contracting HPV highly and progressing to cervical abnormality due to low immunity.

In this study, risky sexual behavior was a significant factor in developing precancerous cervical lesions. Women with precancerous cervical lesions were nearly twice as likely to report multiple lifetime sexual partners compared to monogamous women. Previous similar studies from Ethiopia and other African countries consistently support this finding [34, 35]. Research, including case-control and meta-analysis studies, has demonstrated a significant association between multiple lifetime sexual partners and cervical lesion development [36, 37]. While previous studies suggest that husbands’ multiple sexual partners increase cervical lesion risk in women, our study found no direct association. However, an unknown number of lifetime sexual partners in husbands significantly increased the risk, possibly due to underreporting or lack of awareness [38].

Persistent human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, especially with high-risk strains like HPV 16 and 18, is the leading cause of cervical lesions and cancer [39]. HPV is the most common sexually transmitted infection globally, and HIV further compromises immune clearance, increasing the risk of persistent HPV infection and cervical neoplasia [40]. Multiple lifetime sexual partners-both for women and their partners - remain the primary risk factor for high-risk HPV infections [41].

Early sexual debut (< 18 years) was significantly associated with precancerous cervical lesions, with affected women 1.5 times more likely to develop lesions. Similar findings conducted in different regions of the world including Ethiopia and other African countries consistently supported that initiation of sexual intercourse at early age was a factor for the development of precancerous cervical lesion ultimately which leads to cervical cancer at later life [42, 43], this is possibly due to the increased susceptibility of an immature cervix to HPV infection [33]. Additionally, early sexual debut is linked to chronic HPV infection and cervical neoplasia, especially when partners are older men with multiple sexual partners [44, 45].

A present study also reported that women who had a history of STIs doubled the risk of precancerous cervical lesions, this is consistent with studies in Ethiopia and Africa [46]. STIs indicate high-risk sexual behavior, increasing HPV co-infection risks. Ulcerative STIs may further facilitate HPV transmission, while HIV immunosuppression exacerbates HPV persistence and lesion progression [47]. Studies confirm that women living with HIV have a fivefold higher risk of HPV infection, and those co-infected with HPV and HIV have a 40-fold increased risk of cervical neoplasia [48].

Multiparity was also associated with precancerous cervical lesions. Multiparous women had 2.7 times higher odds, and primiparous women had twice the risk compared to nulliparous women. This aligns with research from Ethiopia, sub-Saharan Africa, and beyond [49–51] that cervical lacerations from multiple vaginal deliveries may contribute to cervical cancer development. The possible justification supporting these findings could be that frequent vaginal delivery may expose the women for tear and laceration of the vagina and cervix which is favorable condition to acquire HPV easily. This implies that repeated cervical trauma during childbirth may increase HPV susceptibility, and high parity has been linked to an elevated risk of squamous-cell carcinoma in HPV-infected women. Moreover, the high magnitude of precancerous cervical lesions among women living with HIV in Tigray underscores an urgent need for strengthened cervical cancer prevention and control efforts in conflict-affected and resource-limited settings. Regular and accessible cervical cancer screening, timely management of detected lesions, and integration of HIV and reproductive health services are critical to reduce the burden of cervical cancer in this vulnerable population. Health education programs should address high-risk sexual behaviors, early sexual debut, and sexually transmitted infections to prevent HPV transmission. Policymakers and healthcare providers can use these findings to inform evidence-based strategies, allocate resources effectively, and implement targeted interventions to improve women’s reproductive health outcomes and reduce cervical cancer-related morbidity and mortality.

Strength and limitation of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the assessment was based on data extracted from the project dataset, which lacked some pertinent variables. Second, the study was conducted in one city, with screening carried out at four health facilities across five screening sites, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to all HIV-positive women at the national level. Additionally, the use of the VIA method for screening is inherently subjective and may lead to overestimation or underestimation of the true magnitude of precancerous cervical lesions.

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths. The inclusion of a large sample size enhances the reliability of the findings. Moreover, the involvement of experienced gynecologists and oncologists in the screening process likely improved the accuracy and reliability of VIA assessments. Finally, recall bias was minimized, as the data were collected directly through VIA testing rather than relying on participant recall.

Conclusion

This study revealed a relatively high magnitude of precancerous cervical lesions, affecting nearly one in five HIV-positive women in conflict-prone and resource-limited areas of Tigray. Significant factors associated with these lesions included early sexual debut, multiple lifetime sexual partners, and a history of sexually transmitted infections, indicating that both behavioral and clinical risks contribute to cervical abnormalities in this population. Based on these findings, it is essential to strengthen and expand cervical cancer screening programs, prioritizing high-risk women and underserved areas. Targeted behavioral interventions, including community education on sexual health, STI prevention, and delaying sexual initiation, should be implemented. Additionally, collaboration between the Ministry of Health, regional health bureaus, and partners such as Wings of Healing Ethiopia is needed to ensure access to screening, appropriate follow-up treatment, and preventive services. Ongoing surveillance and research should continue to monitor trends in HPV infection and precancerous lesions, evaluate intervention effectiveness, and guide evidence-based policy and programming.
