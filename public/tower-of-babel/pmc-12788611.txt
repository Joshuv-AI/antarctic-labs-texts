Accuracy of Static Computer-Guided Versus Freehand Implant Placement Using Intraoral Scanner-Based Digital Workflow: A Clinical and Radiographic Study
Authors: Jeemee Patel, Arunkumar Jayaraman, Renuka Devi Arumalla, B Roshan Arbaaz, A Nihal Ahamed, Parthiban Saketh Raman, C Pradeep
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:The increasing use of digital technologies in implant dentistry has led to the development of static computer-guided surgery. However, a clear comparison between the accuracy and patient-reported outcomes of this method versus conventional freehand techniques is needed.Aim:This randomized clinical trial aimed to compare the accuracy of implant placement, surgical efficiency, and patient discomfort between static computer-guided surgery and freehand implant placement.Methods:Thirty partially edentulous patients were randomly assigned to either a guided surgery group (Group A, n = 15), where implants were placed using a 3D-printed surgical guide based on an intraoral scan and CBCT, a freehand group (Group B, n = 15), where implants were placed conventionally. The primary outcome was implant placement accuracy, measured by angular and linear deviations between the planned and final implant positions. Secondary outcomes included surgery duration and patient-reported pain scores (VAS). Statistical analysis was performed using independent t-tests and the Mann-Whitney U test, with a significance level of P < 0.05.Results:The guided group demonstrated significantly higher accuracy, with lower mean angular deviation (2.4 ± 1.2 vs. 6.8∘ ± 2.1, P < 0.001) and linear deviations at both coronal (0.7 ± 0.3 mm vs. 1.9 ± 0.6 mm, P < 0.001) and apical levels (1.1 ± 0.4 mm vs. 2.5 ± 0.9 mm, P < 0.001). Furthermore, Group A had a shorter mean surgery time (28.4 ± 4.2 mins vs. 39.7 ± 5.3 mins, P < 0.001) and lower post-operative pain scores on Day 1 and Day 3 (P < 0.001).Conclusion:Static computer-guided implant surgery provides superior accuracy, enhances surgical efficiency, and results in a more comfortable post-operative experience for patients compared to conventional freehand techniques. The findings support the use of a digital workflow for predictable implant placement.

INTRODUCTION

Dental implantology has undergone a significant transformation with the advent of digital technology, transitioning from traditional methods to a more predictable, computer-aided approach. The integration of Cone-Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) for detailed anatomical assessment and intraoral scanners for precise digital impressions has created a streamlined workflow for implant treatment planning.[1] This digital process culminates in the fabrication of a 3D-printed surgical guide, which enables static computer-guided surgery. This method is praised for its potential to enhance the accuracy and predictability of implant placement, particularly in complex cases where precise positioning is critical for optimal prosthetic outcomes.[23] However, despite the widespread adoption and theoretical advantages of guided surgery, there remains a need for robust clinical data to validate its superiority over conventional freehand techniques. This study was designed to directly compare the clinical and radiographic outcomes of static computer-guided versus freehand implant placement, utilizing a modern intraoral scanner-based digital workflow. The goal is to determine if the increased planning and material costs associated with guided surgery are justified by a significant improvement in accuracy, surgical efficiency, and patient experience.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study enrolled adults aged 25–65 with either single or multiple missing teeth, provided they had adequate bone volume without requiring grafting and were in good general and oral health. Patients were excluded if they had systemic diseases contraindicating implant surgery, smoked more than 10 cigarettes per day, were pregnant or lactating, or exhibited severe parafunctional habits like bruxism. For the procedure, Group A, undergoing Digital Guided Surgery, involved taking digital impressions with an intraoral scanner (e.g. Medit, Trios), acquiring a CBCT scan, and aligning both in implant planning software to virtually place the implant based on prosthetic planning. A 3D-printed surgical guide was then fabricated and used for implant placement. In contrast, Group B, the Freehand Surgery group, had implants placed manually based solely on clinical and radiographic evaluation, without the use of a surgical guide. The primary outcome assessed was the accuracy of implant placement, specifically angular deviation and linear deviation at coronal and apical levels, measured by superimposing post-operative CBCT scans with the virtual plan using specialized software (e.g. Blue Sky Plan, coDiagnostiX). Secondary outcomes included the total surgery duration in minutes, patient-reported pain and discomfort measured using a VAS scale, and early implant survival at 3 months post-operation.

Statistical analysis

The study utilized specific statistical tests to analyze its findings. An independent t-test was performed to compare the mean angular and linear deviations between the two groups. For the non-normally distributed data, such as patient-reported discomfort scores and surgery duration, the Mann-Whitney U test was employed. A P value of less than 0.05 was set as the threshold for determining statistical significance, meaning any result with a P value below this value was considered a true difference and not due to chance.

RESULTS

Result analysis

The guided surgery group (Group A) demonstrated significantly higher accuracy in implant placement, with lower angular and linear deviations compared to the freehand group (Group B) (P < 0.001). Surgery time was shorter in the guided group, indicating higher procedural efficiency. Patient-reported pain scores on the VAS were significantly lower in the guided group at both Day 1 and Day 3 postoperatively. These findings suggest that static guided surgery not only enhances clinical precision but also improves patient experience [Tables 1-3].

DISCUSSION

The results of this study provide compelling evidence that guided surgery offers significant advantages in terms of precision, efficiency, and patient comfort. The most crucial finding of this study is the significantly higher accuracy observed in the guided surgery group (Group A). The mean angular, coronal, and apical deviations were substantially lower in Group A compared to the freehand group (Group B), with P values all less than 0.001. This confirms our hypothesis and aligns with a growing body of literature that supports the superior precision of computer-guided surgery.[45] The virtual planning process, which integrates CBCT data with a digital prosthetic model, allows for the precise determination of the optimal implant position, angulation, and depth. The 3D-printed surgical guide then translates this digital plan into a physical reality, minimizing the risk of human error during the osteotomy and implant insertion. In contrast, freehand placement relies heavily on the surgeon’s clinical judgment and experience, which can be influenced by factors such as limited visibility, anatomical variations, and the subjective interpretation of two-dimensional radiographs. The larger deviations observed in the freehand group highlight the inherent limitations of this conventional method.

Beyond accuracy, the study revealed other clinically relevant benefits of the guided approach. The mean surgery time was significantly shorter in the guided group (28.4 minutes) compared to the freehand group (39.7 minutes). This finding, while potentially counterintuitive to those who associate guided surgery with a longer preparation phase, reflects the streamlined nature of the intra-operative process. The surgical guide eliminates the need for repeated radiographic checks and adjustments during the procedure, allowing for a more efficient, flapless or minimally invasive approach in many cases. This reduced operative time is beneficial for both the patient and the clinician, potentially decreasing chairside stress and the risk of complications associated with prolonged surgery.[46] A study by Hussaini S et al.[7] found that using CBCT can prevent patient mistrust by 7% compared to using panoramic radiography alone. The research highlights the importance of CBCT as a diagnostic and treatment planning tool, noting that relying solely on panoramic radiography increases the chance of a misdiagnosis by 7%.

The patient-reported outcome measures further underscore the advantages of guided surgery. The significantly lower VAS pain scores at both Day 1 and Day 3 post-operatively in the guided group suggest a more comfortable recovery. This is likely a direct result of the minimally invasive nature of guided surgery, which often allows for a smaller incision or a flapless approach. Reduced tissue trauma translates to less post-operative swelling, pain, and a faster return to normal function. These findings are particularly important as they address a key aspect of patient satisfaction and acceptance of implant treatment.

Clinical implications and limitations

The results of this study have significant clinical implications. They reinforce the value of incorporating a fully digital workflow—from intraoral scanning to CBCT integration and 3D-printed guides—for predictable and precise implant placement. The enhanced accuracy of guided surgery not only improves the primary implant position but also facilitates prosthetically driven implantology, ensuring that the implant is placed in the ideal position to support the final restoration. This is critical for achieving long-term esthetic and functional success.

While the study provides strong evidence, it is not without limitations. The sample size of 30 patients, while sufficient for a pilot study, is relatively small. A larger multicenter trial would be beneficial to validate these findings across different clinical settings and patient populations. Furthermore, the study focused on accuracy at the time of placement. Future research could explore long-term outcomes, such as marginal bone loss and implant survival rates beyond the initial three-month period, to provide a more comprehensive comparison. The operator’s experience level was also not specifically controlled, which could be a variable in the freehand group.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that static computer-guided implant surgery using an intraoral scanner-based digital workflow is a superior technique to conventional freehand placement. It provides significantly higher accuracy, reduces surgical time, and improves the patient’s post-operative experience. These findings advocate for the routine adoption of digital planning and guided surgery, especially in cases where precision is paramount for achieving optimal prosthetic and biological outcomes.
