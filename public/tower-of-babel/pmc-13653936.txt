Multifunctional hydrogel scaffolds for endogenous osteochondral regeneration
Authors: Ling Zhang, Xuan Liu, Jinping Liang, Boyu Kuang, Haojie Zhang, Mengxiao Wei, Linjia Wu, Nanzhen Liu, Qidi Ai, Songwei Yang, Yantao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteochondral defects, frequently resulting from trauma, infection, or degenerative joint disease, present a significant clinical challenge due to their limited intrinsic healing capacity. Conventional cell-based therapies face limitations including immune rejection, high costs, and regulatory hurdles. Endogenous tissue engineering, which harnesses the body's own repair machinery through biomaterial-mediated recruitment and differentiation of host stem cells, offers a promising alternative. Hydrogel scaffolds, owing to their extracellular matrix-mimetic architecture, tunable physicochemical properties, and capacity for bioactive factor delivery, have emerged as versatile platforms for endogenous osteochondral regeneration. This review provides a strategic overview of hydrogel-based endogenous tissue engineering, covering: (1) the biological rationale for leveraging host cell sources; (2) the design of hydrogels as extracellular matrix -mimetic niches; (3) key regulatory factors; (4) innovative material design strategies. We also discuss current challenges related to long-term functionality, scaffold integration, and clinical translation, offering insights into the development of next-generation hydrogel systems for minimally invasive and effective osteochondral regeneration.Keywords: Osteochondral defect, Endogenous tissue engineering, Hydrogel scaffold, Mesenchymal stromal cells, Regeneration

Introduction

Articular cartilage is a highly specialized, avascular, and aneural connective tissue that enables smooth joint movement and distributes mechanical loads. Its limited self-repair capacity is attributed to the absence of vascularization and innervation, as well as the low cellularity [1]. Consequently, untreated focal cartilage defect — whether caused by acute trauma or chronic degeneration — often progress to involve the subchondral bone, resulting in osteochondral (OC) defect, a process that significantly accelerate joint degeneration and increase the risk of developing osteoarthritis [2,3]. By 2050, osteoarthritis is projected to affect nearly 130 million people worldwide, representing a major public health challenge with profound economic implications [4].

Current clinical management strategies include drug therapy, minimally invasive surgery, and tissue engineering (TE). The primary objective of clinical pharmacological interventions is to manage symptoms, including controlling inflammation and alleviating pain, thereby improving joint function [5]. However, while these approaches can effectively provide symptomatic relief and reduce patient discomfort, they do not address the underlying structural defects in OC tissue and consequently fail to achieve genuine tissue regeneration [6]. The microfracture technique can recruit bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (BM-MSCs) and facilitate the formation of reparative fibrocartilage, the resulting functional improvement is often transient, typically lasting 12-24 months, due to the poor migration, retention, and survival rate of the cells [7]. Moreover, the neocartilage differs significantly from native hyaline cartilage in its biomechanical properties and matrix composition, leading to a long-term failure rate as high as 60%, which is primarily attributed to the suboptimal integration between the repair tissue and the surrounding native cartilage [8]. Although allogeneic OC transplantation allows for histological integration, issues such as donor scarcity and immune rejection impede its long-term clinical application [9].

Cell-based TE technologies, which hold considerable clinical promise, can be broadly classified into exogenous and endogenous strategies based on cell source. Conventional exogenous TE relies on the implantation of in vitro-expanded mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs, formerly frequently termed mesenchymal stem cells represent a heterogeneous cell population with multi-lineage differentiation potential) or induced pluripotent stem cells, yet it faces challenges such as teratoma risk, ethical concerns, high costs, and complex manufacturing processes [10]. In contrast, endogenous TE aims to deliver a dynamic mechanical microenvironment and biochemical cues to induce cell differentiation by constructing a biomimetic microenvironment and facilitating the directional recruitment of endogenous cells from synovial membranes, bone marrow, and other niches to injury sites [11]. This strategy avoids ex vivo cell expansion, transplantation procedures, and the associated risks of immune rejection and logistical complexity.

Among available scaffold systems, hydrogels are particularly promising owing to their high water content, excellent biocompatibility, and innate structural and biochemical mimicry of the native extracellular matrix (ECM). These inherent properties can be precisely engineered through physical and chemical modifications to facilitate host cell homing, guide OC differentiation, and modulate the inflammatory microenvironment (Fig. 1).

Currently, although numerous reviews have covered osteochondral repair hydrogels [12], endogenous cell recruitment techniques [13], fabrication processes of injectable hydrogels [14], gradient scaffold construction strategies [15], and acellular tissue engineering [16], none has established a systematic framework that integrates the endogenous regeneration paradigm with multifunctional hydrogel design, leaving a conspicuous gap in the existing literature (Table 1). This review innovatively integrates material design strategies with endogenous repair mechanisms and clinical translation pathways, effectively addressing the deficiencies in systematic integration and core research dimensions found in prior reviews, thereby delineating its unique academic positioning and theoretical contributions.

Anatomical and biological rationale for endogenous OC regeneration

Structure–function hierarchy of the OC unit

The OC unit consists of three distinct yet continuous zones: the articular cartilage layer (further divided into superficial, middle, deep, and calcified cartilage zones), the subchondral bone plate, and the subchondral trabecular bone. Each zone exhibits gradients in cellular distribution, matrix composition (including collagen types, glycosaminoglycan content, and water content), biomechanical properties, and nutrient transport mechanisms, which collectively vary progressively from the hyaline cartilage of the articular surface to the underlying subchondral bone region [26]. This structural complexity necessitates regenerative strategies that can recapitulate such heterogeneity.

Why endogenous regeneration is a superior strategy

The intrinsic repair capacity of articular cartilage is extremely low due to its avascularity, aneural nature, and low cellularity [1]. Full-thickness OC defects must rely on bone marrow-derived endogenous stem cells for any repair attempt [27]. In contrast, exogenous cell delivery faces multiple bottlenecks: limited cell sources, phenotypic dedifferentiation, immune rejection, high costs, and teratoma risk [10].

Endogenous TE offers distinct advantages: it recruits the patient's own stem cells from bone marrow, synovium, or adipose tissue, avoiding ex vivo expansion and transplantation [28]. This approach is more aligned with the clinical desire for minimally invasive, safe, and cost-effective therapies. Moreover, host-derived cells exhibit self-renewal capacity, multi-lineage differentiation potential, tissue-repair capabilities, and immunomodulatory effects [29].

The triad of endogenous regeneration

Successful endogenous regeneration requires three elements: (1) a pool of recruitable host stem cells; (2) chemical/physical signals that modulate the inflammatory microenvironment, guide cell homing, and induce differentiation; (3) a scaffold that supports cell engraftment and tissue formation. Hydrogels are uniquely positioned to integrate all three functions [30,31]. Among these, cells are the primary driver of repair. This section focuses on the major endogenous stem cell populations available in the OC environment. The design of hydrogel scaffolds and the delivery of signaling cues will be discussed in Sections 3, 4, respectively.

Three principal sources of host MSCs have been most extensively characterized in OC regeneration: BM-MSCs, synovium-derived MSCs (SM-MSCs), and adipose-derived MSCs (AD-MSCs). BM-MSCs situated on subchondral bone surfaces and within marrow cavities, directly sense joint damage signals and migrate locally to injury sites without requiring long-distance cross-tissue migration [32]. SM-MSCs exhibit enhanced paracrine signaling restores ECM balance by stimulating anabolism while suppressing catabolic degradation [33]. Animal studies indicate intra-articular injection of human-derived SM-MSCs significantly promotes type II collagen (COL2) expression and cartilage regeneration. AD-MSCs, derived from adipose tissue, yield significantly greater cell numbers than those obtained from other sources [34]. In contrast, BM-MSCs exhibit higher osteogenic and chondrogenic potential, whereas AD-MSCs favor adipogenesis [29]. The functionality of AD-MSCs is highly susceptible to the host's metabolic status. Obesity impairs their immunomodulatory capacity, thereby compromising tissue repair in metabolic disorders [35].

In general, MSCs primarily mediate OC tissue repair through three mechanisms: (1) OC differentiation within permissive microenvironments (Fig. 2); (2) Immunomodulation and anti-inflammatory actions that mitigate local inflammation; (3) Their paracrine action promotes the secretion of cytokines, thereby facilitating host cell repair, preventing cell death, and normalizing metabolic balance [36].

Hydrogel scaffolds as ECM-mimetic niches for endogenous TE

Basic advantages and source materials

The inefficiency in treating OC defects is largely due to the absence of suitable tissue-engineered matrices that can promote tissue regeneration and replace damaged areas [37]. Hydrogels can serve as artificial ECM substitutes, not only offer three-dimensional support for cell growth but also function as bioactive stimulators for in situ tissue regeneration, thereby facilitating endogenous repair [28]. Compared to pre-formed rigid scaffolds, hydrogels provide a dynamic, tunable, and highly hydrated three-dimensional microenvironment. They function as a powerful tool for actively modulating endogenous host repair processes [38]. Natural hydrogels (alginate, hyaluronic acid) offer inherent bioactivity but limited tunability. Synthetic hydrogels (Polyethylene glycol, Poval) provide controlled mechanics and degradation but lack cell-adhesive motifs. Hybrid hydrogels combine the advantages of both (see Table 2 for detailed comparison of advantages and disadvantages).

Design strategies

Hydrogels, with their three-dimensional networks, encapsulate bioactive molecules (e.g., such as growth factors, cytokines) for sustained controlled release. Through design of encapsulation methods, release mechanisms, and spatial structures, they serve as single-administration, multi-stage cascade release platforms that enhance synergy [48]. Their physicochemical properties (stiffness, pore size, degradability) are highly tunable, and allowing scaffolds to match defect sites, and replicate the OC unit's gradient architecture [49]. Strategies such as multi-layer printing, gradient crosslinking, and sequential assembly enable biomimetic gradients in composition, mechanics, and bio-signals from cartilage to bone [50]. Such anisotropic systems direct region-specific BM-MSC differentiation into chondrocytes and osteoblasts, supporting concurrent cartilage and bone regeneration [51]. In addition, materials can be modified to achieve responsive properties. For example, Lu et al. [52] engineered a bilayer hydrogel loaded with diclofenac sodium and exosomes. The reactive oxygen species -responsive upper layer degrades under high reactive oxygen species to release diclofenac sodium reducing oxidative stress. The lower layer then provides sustained exosomes release to support chondrogenesis. This sequential release bridges the inflammatory and proliferative phases, mitigating the negative impact of inflammation on BM-MSC differentiation and chondrocyte function.

Guiding factors involved in endogenous TE

OC defects do not heal spontaneously; they extend deeper, penetrating the cartilage to involve the subchondral bone. Once the lesion reaches well-vascularized bone tissue, local bleeding occurs and forms a blood clot. Concurrently, growth factors released from the bone marrow cavity and recruited blood-borne stem/progenitor cells initiate the spontaneous repair process [53]. Tissue repair and regeneration represent a highly coordinated biological process, the process encompasses hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling [54]. However, repair efficacy is often limited by poor migration, retention, and survival of host/progenitor cells, along with the highly differentiated and weak self-renewal capacity of human tissues [28]. Thus, building on innate repair mechanisms, TE strategies can modulate immune responses and recruit/differentiate endogenous cells to enhance tissue repair and regeneration (Fig. 3).

Inflammation regulatory factors

Inflammatory signaling pathways represented by nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), Interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and TNF-α exert context-dependent dual regulatory roles during OC repair. Transient, low-level inflammatory activation at early injury stages recruits endogenous MSCs and initiates reparative cascades [55]; in contrast, persistent overactivation of NF-κB and pro-inflammatory cytokine release triggers chondrocyte apoptosis, ECM degradation, M1 macrophage polarization, and fibrotic tissue formation, which permanently impairs cartilage regeneration [56]. Consequently, hydrogel design aims to balance transient physiological inflammation and suppress sustained pathological inflammatory signaling to optimize endogenous repair efficiency.

Chinese herbal medicine single agent

Current anti-inflammatory treatments for OC damage primarily involve non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and glucocorticoids. However, both drug classes are associated with various adverse effects, including hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, immune dysfunction, and increased infection risk [57]. Chinese herbal medicine single agent, due to their favorable safety profile and demonstrated efficacy, emerging as a valuable therapeutic avenue [58].

Icariin (ICA), the main active compound in the Chinese herb Epimedium. Administration of ICA significantly reduces levels of oxidative stress markers and pro-inflammatory cytokines [59]. ICA protects BM-MSCs from oxygen-glucose deprivation-induced apoptosis via endoplasmic reticulum stress-associated MAPK pathway-mediated autophagy [60]. Incorporated ICA into chitosan hydrogels, which showed superior anti-inflammatory effects to plain chitosan hydrogels in lipopolysaccharide-induced RAW264.7 cells.

Curcumin (Cur), the main bioactive component of turmeric, exhibits chondroprotective effects [61]. It counteracts IL-1β-induced loss of chondrocyte viability, ECM degradation, and downregulation of SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9), COL2A, and Aggrecan (ACAN) [62]. Cur also enhances calcium deposition, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity, and osteogenic gene expression in BM-MSCs. When integrated into scaffolds via functional coatings, Cur rapidly promotes M2 macrophage polarization, downregulates pro-inflammatory genes, and modulates the peri-scaffold inflammatory microenvironment [63].

Rhein has demonstrated potent anti-inflammatory effects in treating arthritis, can self-assemble into hydrogels via non-covalent interactions [64]. Combining rhein-based self-assembled hydrogels with spirulina-derived extracellular vesicles yields composite hydrogels with both anti-inflammatory and energy-supplying properties for alleviating joint inflammation [65]. Similarly, puerarin (Pue) self-assembles into hydrogels, demonstrating anti-inflammatory and anti-matrix degradation effects in rat arthritis models [66].

Metal ions

Metal ions participate in critical biochemical processes, possess demonstrated therapeutic benefits such as immunomodulatory, antimicrobial, and antioxidant activities. Medically relevant anti-inflammatory metal ions include magnesium (Mg2+), zinc (Zn2+), and silver (Ag+). Notably, magnesium and zinc are essential trace elements vital for joint health and implicated in osteoarthritis pathophysiology [67].

P2X purinergic receptor 7 is a key mediator of IL-1β secretion. MgSO4 inhibits IL-1β-mediated inflammation in HUVECs by downregulating P2X purinergic receptor 7 expression during early inflammation [68]. Xu et al. [69] prepared Mg2+-crosslinked hydrogel microspheres, where Mg2+ incorporation stimulates chondrogenic differentiation of MSCs and promotes macrophage polarization from M1 to M2 phenotype.

Zinc negatively regulates NF-κB signaling, a major inflammatory pathway controlling pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and other mediators [70]. Zinc deprivation increases lipopolysaccharide-induced IL-1β production and mRNA expression in monocytes, whereas zinc supplementation upregulates the zinc-finger protein A20, which inhibits NF-κB activation [71].

Silver ions exhibit excellent antibacterial properties. A substantial body of literature has demonstrated that silver ions possess anti-inflammatory effects. For instance, Yang et al. [72] developed folate-modified silver nanoparticles capable of active delivery to M1 macrophages. These nanoparticles synergistically induce M1 macrophage apoptosis and repolarisation from M1 to M2 phenotypes.

Physical factors

Macrophage response determines the fate of implanted materials. The physical, chemical, and biological properties of TE scaffolds influence macrophage behaviors. To improve the immune environment and enhance damaged OC tissue repair, scaffolds are often designed to promote M2 anti-inflammatory polarization [73]. Physical properties (e.g., stiffness, surface morphology, pore structure) affect macrophage responses [74]. For example, in hydrogels with identical composition but varying stiffness (2.55, 34.88, 63.53 kPa), the softest matrix (2.55 kPa) promoted pro-inflammatory factors and M1-like polarization [75]. Higher porosity or larger pore sizes induce moderate hypoxia, promoting M2 polarization and angiogenesis while inhibiting inflammation [76].

Directed migration factors

Endogenous cell-based TE represents a primary approach for OC repair. It utilizes key cell homing factors to either promote the migration of more endogenous MSCs to the injury sites or enhance the OC differentiation and paracrine effects of locally recruited MSCs [77]. Recently, targeted MSC recruitment has been achieved through integration of biomimetic scaffolds with homing factors [78].

Stromal cell-derived factor-1α

Stromal cell-derived factor-1α (SDF-1α) and its receptor CXCR4 constitute a core chemotactic signaling pathway, exerting dual effects in both tissue repair and degenerative progression [79]. Following OC defects, moderate local secretion of SDF-1α recognizes CXCR4 on the surface of MSCs, activates the PI3K/ERK pathway, induces directed homing of stem cells to the defect site, and replenishes the cell pool for regeneration [80]. Hypoxic conditions further stimulate BMSCs to upregulate CXCR4 expression, thereby enhancing cell recruitment efficiency [81,82]. However, in the chronic inflammatory milieu of osteoarthritis (OA), excessive SDF-1α is secreted from the subchondral bone, which on one hand activates osteoclasts and induces trabecular bone resorption, and on the other hand stimulates chondrocytes to overexpress matrix metalloproteinase-13 (MMP13) and ADAMTS5, leading to matrix degradation; additionally, it promotes hypertrophic differentiation of BMSCs, inhibits hyaline cartilage repair, and exacerbates joint degeneration [83,84].

Peptide-based recruitment

The E7 peptide (amino acid sequence “EPLQLKM”) exhibits high specificity and affinity for BM-MSCs. Previous studies show that conjugating E7 peptide to scaffold materials significantly increases BM-MSC recruitment to cartilage defects [85]. Compared with the RGD motif, the unique sequence of E7 peptide serves as a superior adhesion target for BM-MSCs. Furthermore, E7 peptide promotes chondrogenic differentiation of BM-MSCs via the H19/miR-675 axis [86].

The PFS peptide (amino acid sequence “PFSSTKT”) is a bone marrow homing peptide with specific binding affinity for BM-MSCs [87]. Huang et al. [88] constructed a 3D scaffold using PFS-modified ECM particles combined with GelMA hydrogel, enhancing BM-MSC homing efficiency in vitro and in vivo. As PFS mimics natural bone marrow homing peptides, the recruited MSCs likely originate from the bone marrow. Additionally, cells from synovial/adipose tissues and intra-articular vasculature may also be mobilized [89].

Substance P is an 11 amino-acid neuropeptide [90]. Substance P can upregulates expression of the SDF-1 chemokine and its receptor CXCR4, directing cell migration to injury sites. Studies demonstrate it enhances chemotaxis by activating PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK signalling pathways, while activating NF-κB signalling pathway to increase integrin α4β1 expression, thereby enhancing cell homing efficiency [91].

Aptamers

Aptamers (Apt) are artificially synthesised oligonucleotides that specifically recognise and bind to target molecules [92]. Apt-modified hydrogels targeting cell surface receptors facilitate selective adhesion and capture of cells. Gong et al. [93] developed a mesh-like porous PEG hydrogel scaffold functionalized with BM-MSCs-targeting Apt for in situ bone regeneration. Wang et al. [94] demonstrated that it upregulates SDF-1α gene expression, enhances SDF-1α/CXCR4 protein production, and activates the SDF-1α/CXCR4 signalling axis with downstream MAPK pathways to drive BM-MSC migration.

Physical factors

The migration of MSCs is essential for tissue regeneration and repair [95]. The optimal pore size for promoting MSC migration generally lies between 50 and 200 μm. Pores that are too small can physically hinder cell migration, while excessively large pores may reduce migration efficiency due to insufficient adhesion sites [96]. The mechanical properties of hydrogel scaffolds also regulate cellular behavior through mechanotransduction. Research showns that cells exhibit migrating from softer to stiffer regions [97]. Microtopographical cues, such as grooves or stripes on hydrogel surfaces, can guide cell migration along defined paths [98]. Gu et al. [99] fabricate scaffolds with lotus root-like channels in the bottom layer guiding BM-MSC upward migration for bone regeneration, and radial pores combined with vertical channels in the upper layer directing cartilage progenitor cells for cartilage regeneration. Thus, an ideal migration-promoting hydrogel scaffold should establish a physically penetrable, mechanically perceptible, and structurally guidable microenvironment to direct cell migration toward target regeneration areas.

Current evidence indicates MSCs are guided by diverse signalling molecules, as injured tissues express unique receptors/ligands to promote MSC migration. Beyond the aforementioned growth factors, chemokines, and homing peptides, Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha, IL-1β, IL-3, IL-8, insulin-like growth factor-1, and platelet-derived growth factor-AB also demonstrate chemotactic activity toward MSCs in vitro and in vivo, although they lack cellular specificity [13].

Controllable differentiation factors

Beyond targeted MSC recruitment, controlled differentiation is essential for OC defects repair. Recent studies confirm that biophysical and biochemical cues within OC defects microenvironments can direct BM-MSCs toward osteogenic and chondrogenic differentiation.

Chondrogenic differentiation

Kartogenin (KGN), a hydrophobic small molecule, possesses chondroprotective and chondroregenerative bioactivities [100], undergoes rapid in vivo hydrolysis to generate 4-aminobiphenyl. This metabolite targets PSK-3 to trigger the PI3K-Akt signaling cascade, which drives chondrogenic differentiation of MSCs [101,102]. Separately, intact KGN directly interacts with filamin A (FLNA) to displace cytoplasmic CBFβ, facilitating the nuclear translocation of CBFβ. Once translocated into the nucleus, CBFβ binds the transcription factor RUNX1 to activate the CBFβ-RUNX1 signaling axis, which mediates three core chondroprotective biofunctions: (1) accelerating chondrogenic differentiation and cellular proliferation; (2) retaining low expression of RUNX2 to restrain chondrocyte hypertrophy and osteogenic transdifferentiation; (3) upregulating the expression of COL2, ACAN, and MMP inhibitors, which synergistically boosts extracellular matrix anabolism and suppresses matrix degradation [103].

ICA exerts multidimensional regulation on the skeletal system to enhance joint cartilage repair. It stimulates chondrocyte biosynthesis of Glycosaminoglycans and COL2 while upregulating cartilage-forming genes [104]. Additionally, ICA promotes BM-MSC proliferation and chondrogenic differentiation via the Wnt/β-catenin and BMP/SMAD pathways, increasing COL2A1, proteoglycans, and BMP-2 expression [105]. ICA also enhances BM-MSC migration through estrogen receptors by regulating the SDF-1α/HIF-1α/CXCR4 axis and involves MAPK-mediated stress fiber formation [106,107].

TGF-β superfamily members induce chondrogenic differentiation of MSCs through canonical Smad-dependent signaling, upregulating SOX9 and promoting extracellular matrix synthesis [108]. TGF-β1 and TGF-β3 are widely utilized chondrogenic inductive agents [109]. For instance, both TGF-β1 and TGF-β3 exhibit potent chondrogenic capacity for bone marrow-derived MSCs under standard pellet culture conditions, although their specific effects on proliferation, hypertrophy, and matrix mineralization can vary depending on the experimental context [[110], [111], [112]]. Regardless of the specific isoform, prolonged TGF-β stimulation may trigger chondrocyte hypertrophy and matrix mineralization, representing a major hurdle for clinical translation [113].

Osteogenic differentiation

Bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) are members of the TGF-β superfamily and play pivotal roles in skeletal development, homeostasis maintenance and bone regeneration. They bind to BMP receptors on the cell membrane and activate the SMAD signaling pathway to regulate osteogenic differentiation [114]. Its biological activity exhibits a narrow therapeutic window. Supraphysiological concentrations of BMP-2 trigger severe off-target effects, including excessive chondrocyte hypertrophy, heterotopic ossification within cartilage tissue, and accelerated subchondral bone remodeling that disrupts the OC interface balance [115,116]. Such dual dose-dependent effects necessitate localized, sustained low-dose delivery systems to segregate osteogenic signals in the bone compartment without impairing hyaline cartilage formation [117].

Dexamethasone, a glucocorticoid analog, promotes MSC osteogenic differentiation by binding to the glucocorticoid receptor, activating BMP-Smad signaling, facilitating β-catenin nuclear translocation, and upregulating Runx2 and Osterix. This enhances osteogenic markers and mineralized nodule formation [118]. However, high concentrations or prolonged dexamethasone exposure inhibit BM-MSC osteogenesis, reduce bone formation, increase resorption, and may cause osteonecrosis of the femoral head [119,120]. Dexamethasone exerts dual regulatory effects on MSC osteogenic differentiation, therefore requiring optimized delivery strategies to maximize efficacy within a therapeutic window [121].

Osteogenic growth peptide is an endogenous peptide it enhances MSC proliferation and differentiation via RhoA/ROCK and MAPK/ERK signaling [122,123]. Osteogenic growth peptide -functionalized scaffold promote osteogenesis and bone regeneration. Liu et al. [124] designed a trifunctional peptide coating incorporating RGD, Osteogenic growth peptide and Ang (angiopoietin-mimetic peptide), which improved BM-MSC adhesion, migration, osteogenic differentiation, and mineralization, while also supporting HUVEC adhesion, migration, and vascularization [125]. This sequential release profile aligned with the temporal requirements of cell homing, angiogenesis, mineralization, and bone formation during regeneration.

Physical factors

The biomechanical microenvironment of hydrogel scaffolds regulates cell behavior. Matrix stiffness influences differentiation via YAP/TAZ mechanotransduction: high stiffness promotes osteogenesis, while low stiffness favors chondrogenesis [126]. Micro/nanoscale structural design is pivotal for neotissue regeneration, Pore sizes of 150–250 μm better support cartilage formation than larger pores [127]. Biomimetic structural scaffolds (e.g., conch-shaped, lotus-like) show promise for complex tissue regeneration [128,129]. Electrical stimulation promotes bone and cartilage regeneration, suggesting benefits for cartilage repair [130]. Liu et al. [131] developed a piezoelectric/conductive scaffold using decellularized cartilage ECM and modified gelatin with diphenylalanine (piezoelectric) and polythiophene (conductive). Scaffold deformation generates a piezoelectric potential difference; positive charges on the upper layer attract BM-MSCs, enhancing recruitment, chondrogenesis, and repair.

Other factors

In addition to the aforementioned core factors, the following biological processes must also be coordinated to optimize tissue regeneration efficiency, such as promoting MSCs proliferation, inhibiting chondrocyte hypertrophy, and reducing oxidative stress [132,133]. This paper will not explore these topics in depth.

Strategies for promoting OC regeneration based on TE scaffolds

The core objective of TE scaffold design is to create a biomimetic microenvironment. Traditional single-layer scaffolds fail to replicate the heterogeneous structure of native OC tissue, whereas multi-layer or gradient hydrogel scaffolds better recapitulate OC biological compartmentalization for functional regeneration [23]. This paradigm shift focuses on biomimetic substitutes with gradient mechanical. Injectable scaffold

Injectable hydrogels offer key advantages in minimally invasive delivery and dynamic responsiveness, enabling precise filling of irregular OC defects via arthroscopy [134] (Fig. 5A). Based on cross-linking mechanisms and structure, they fall into three categories: in situ-gelling, shear-thinning/self-healing, and micro/nanoparticulate systems [20]. These platforms can incorporate bioactive factors or cells to establish a pro-regenerative microenvironment, showing great promise for critical-sized bone defects. Chen et al. [135] loaded magnesium oxide nanoparticles into a phosphocreatine-functionalized chitosan hydrogel, which promoted MC3T3-E1 cell osteogenic differentiation by upregulating BSP, OPN, and Osterix, and enhancing mineralization. However, in situ gelling systems suffer from susceptibility to physiological environments [14]. particularly inadequate wet-tissue adhesion for superficial cartilage repair. To Address this, Zhang et al. [136] developed an adhesive hydrogel based on dynamic Schiff base bonds and hydrogen bonding networks, composed of alginate-dopamine, chondroitin sulfate, and regenerated silk protein. This system exhibits strong wet adhesion, hemostatic capability, and can deliver BM-MSC-derived exosomes to activate endogenous repair, offering a novel strategy for superficial cartilage defects.

For mechanical control, injectable hydrogel properties can be optimized by adjusting component ratios. Research on the carrageenan-sericin-chondroitin sulfate ternary system shows that carrageenan exceeding 0.75% raises the hydrogel gelation temperature above 40°C, and incorporating ECM components enhances GelMA hydrogel mechanical properties [88,139]. These findings indicate that optimizing component ratios requires balancing mechanical performance and biocompatibility. Micro/nano hydrogel systems have made significant progress: particulate systems (1-100 μm) not only have superior injectability but also enable biomimetic structural assembly via shear-thinning behavior and in vivo secondary cross-linking [140]. Recent studies show microparticle hydrogel systems outperform bulk hydrogels in maintaining chondrocyte phenotype and promoting cartilage matrix deposition. Anna Puiggalí-Jou et al. [141] designed growth factor-loaded microparticles that effectively enhance hMSC migration and chondrogenic differentiation through spatiotemporal controlled release. These advances reflect the evolution of OC repair technologies toward precision delivery, functional enhancement, and intelligent design.

Implantable scaffolds

Implantable scaffolds require surgical creation of OC defects at the trochlear groove via drill osteotomy to accommodate scaffold implantation. Subsequent fixation through suturing or adhesive bonding ensures stabilization and prevents displacement. However, these scaffolds share a fundamental limitation common to most TE constructs: poor interfacial integration between the engineered tissue and host environment, which compromises therapeutic efficacy [142,143] (Fig. 5B).

Single-layer scaffold

In OC TE, single-layer scaffolds Characterized by limited design objectives, typically lack integrated bioactive or dynamic regulatory capabilities. Representing one of the earliest developed technologies for OC repair, such constructs are generally fabricated from homogeneous materials or simplistic composites. They incorporate relatively isolated growth factors while exhibiting elementary pore architectures and surface topographies. Exemplary systems include silk protein hydrogels augmented with PRP, composites incorporating graphene oxide with decellularized cartilage extracellular matrix [144,145]. Their simplistic architectures fail to recapitulate the complex gradient transitions and heterogeneous microenvironmental features inherent to native OC tissue [19]. Single-layer scaffolds are better suited for tissues with homogeneous cellular and ECM distribution, typically found in low-load regions (e.g., small-area or thin-nasal cartilage, skull). As the foundational approach in OC TE, single-layer scaffolds are increasingly being superseded by multi-layer or composite scaffolds due to their constrained functionality.

Discrete gradient scaffold

Biomimetic multi-phase scaffolds more effectively replicate native tissue organization than homogeneous constructs. The core design principle involves faithfully recapitulating natural tissue architecture while simultaneously catering to the distinct physiological characteristics and functional requirements of both cartilage and subchondral bone [146]. Typically comprising two to three layers, each layer can be individually tailored in porosity, material composition, and bioactive factor incorporation to establish structural and biochemical interlayer heterogeneity [147]. Dual-layer scaffolds comprise distinct chondral and osseous compartments. The upper layer replicates articular cartilage through dense microarchitecture, while the lower layer simulates subchondral bone via high porosity [148]; Material systems may combine collagen fibers in the chondral region to emulate cartilaginous ECM, with hydroxyapatite in the osseous region mimicking the mineralized calcified zone [149]. Spatiotemporal control of bioactive factors enables layer-specific regulation: KGN release in the chondral layer directs chondrogenesis, while alendronate delivery in the osseous layer induces osteogenesis [150]. Through spatiotemporal biomolecule regulation within a stratified framework, the scaffold temporally orchestrates MSC-mediated repair mechanisms for integrated OC restoration. Zhang et al. [151] developed a dual-functionalized scaffold system integrating: KGN-loaded GelMA hydrogel mimicking cartilage-specific ECM (upper layer), PCL scaffold replicating subchondral bone microstructure (lower layer), Surface engineering via tannic acid (TA) coating and E7 peptide conjugation conferred multifunctionality: TA provides antioxidant/anti-inflammatory activity, E7 peptide facilitates BM-MSC homing, and KGN enhances chondrogenic differentiation of endogenous marrow-derived cells.

Owing to the distinct structural, compositional, and mechanical properties of bone and cartilage tissues, multilayer scaffolds incorporating calcified cartilage interfaces have been developed [152] (Fig. 6). The calcified layer not only acts as a physical barrier to inhibits vascular invasion into cartilage and prevent full-thickness cartilage ossification but also functions to support the load-bearing articular cartilage [153]. Scaffolds featuring calcified cartilage layers demonstrate superior outcomes, including enhanced cartilage coverage, improved repair quality, optimized subchondral bone restoration, and superior OC integration [154]. In multilayer scaffold design, the osseous layer requires sufficient compressive strength for load-bearing capacity, while the chondral layer should facilitate hydrated collagen-rich tissue formation. The intermediate transition zone must withstand interfacial shear stresses [155]. 3D printing technology offers distinct advantages for reconstructing biomimetic cartilage architectures through precise control over scaffold microstructures and macroscopic morphologies. Zeng et al. [48] developed a trilayered scaffold emulating native OC tissue organization through: double-network hydrogel matrix (calcium alginate/polyacrylamide), surface-modified hydroxyapatite nanofibers (m-HANFs) reinforcement. The gradient architecture features: osseous layer: 70 wt% m-HANFs, calcified interface: 40 wt% m-HANFs, chondral layer: 0% m-HANFs. Exhibiting depth-dependent compressive properties analogous to natural cartilage, this design maintains critical structural stability post-implantation while providing physiological load-bearing capacity. The optimized mechanical gradient consequently enhances biophysical signal transduction and long-term functional integration in vivo. Dong et al. [156] engineered a functionally graded triphasic scaffold featuring: TGF-β3 releasing chondral layer with anisotropic architecture, mineral-dense calcified interface, BMP-2-eluting trabecular-mimetic osseous layer. This calcified partition maintains biochemically distinct microenvironments for simultaneous cartilage and bone regeneration. Compartmentalized growth factor delivery creates spatially defined inductive zones that precisely guide hUC-MSC differentiation along tissue-specific lineages. By biomimetically reconstructing the mechanical gradients and biological zonation of native OC tissue, this stratified approach represents an advanced regenerative strategy for functional OC regeneration.

Continuous gradient scaffold

Conventional multilaminate scaffolds create functional zones through layered materials assembly. Yet this approach generates abrupt microstructural and mechanical transitions between adjacent strata, establishing well-defined interfacial discontinuities [153]. These demarcated boundaries frequently: compromise interfacial integration integrity, predispose to delamination in dynamic physiological environments, disrupt neotissue continuity, induce localized stress concentrations (12-15 MPa) at transition zones. In contrast, TE continuous gradient scaffolds enable precise control over material composition and distribution (Fig. 7). Examples include: hyaluronic acid content progressively increasing from superficial to deep zones, pore size gradients transitioning from 150 nm (superior) to 750 nm (inferior) [1], stiffness gradients varying linearly from 23 kPa to 133 kPa [159,160]. This biomimetic approach achieves superior recapitulation of native tissue transition zones compared to discrete layered scaffolds [161]. Continuous gradient scaffolds facilitate seamless tissue transitions that mimic native joint architecture, significantly enhancing interfacial integrity after implantation [162].

Zhang et al. [78] engineered three-dimensional gradient fiber aerogels via electrospinning, achieving spatial gradients of glycosaminoglycans and nanophosphate. [164] fabricated continuous gradient scaffolds using chitosan/collagen/nano-hydroxyapatite (nHA) composites through freeze-drying and thermal gelation techniques. These scaffolds recapitulate native mechanical gradients of the OC unit by progressively increasing nHA content from chondral to osseous regions while correspondingly decreasing collagen concentration. Mechanical characterization revealed depth-dependent elastic moduli: Osseous layer 42.95 ± 4.3 kPa (maximum), Chondral layer 1.49 ± 0.3 kPa (minimum). Furthermore, vertically graded pore geometries with interconnected lumen enable convective transport of synovial fluid across the scaffold, ensuring nutrient supply to resident cells.

Scaffolds with gradient pore architectures enable spatially specific modulation of tissue regeneration functions: highly porous zones promote vascularization and bone matrix deposition for osteogenesis, while gradual transitions to denser regions regulate cellular mechanical microenvironments to drive chondrogenesis. This biomimetic gradient design provides an effective engineering strategy for constructing OC composite tissues with seamless interfacial integration, demonstrating unique advantages in multi-tissue synergistic regeneration. Gradient scaffolds represent a significant paradigm shift in TE from “homogeneous” towards “biomimetic heterogeneous” design. Despite these advances, clinical translation faces persistent challenges including scalable manufacturing and validation of long-term in vivo efficacy. Challenge in future

The regeneration of articular cartilage represents a persistent clinical challenge. While endogenous TE approaches employing hydrogel scaffolds show considerable therapeutic potential, significant translational barriers impede their clinical implementation.

The primary challenge involves achieving precise spatiotemporal control over cellular recruitment and differentiation. The homing and differentiation of endogenous cells constitute a complex process requiring tightly regulated mechanisms to ensure targeted migration to injury sites and specification into desired lineages [174]. Despite elucidation of numerous chemokine pathways, achieving efficient and specific in vivo cellular recruitment remains challenging [175]. On the one hand, microenvironment heterogeneity — discrepancies between in vitro and in vivo conditions (e.g., chemotactic gradients, pericellular niche composition) yield inconsistent experimental outcomes. On the other hand, pathological interference — under disease states, cytokine crosstalk disrupts migratory pathways, reduces homing efficiency, and triggers non-specific migration of inflammatory/endothelial cells, potentiating inflammatory cascades that inhibit chondrogenesis [176]. Qin et al. [177] demonstrated that the SDF-1/CXCR4 axis may inadvertently promote subchondral bone degradation through aberrant MSC recruitment and osteoclast activation. Furthermore, while factors like SDF-1α and BMP-2 exhibit therapeutic potential, their optimal dosing, release kinetics, and synergistic interactions with other signaling molecules require rigorous optimization across diverse disease states and patient populations. To address these challenges, advanced strategies including targeted drug delivery systems, stem cell genetic engineering, and magnetic guidance technologies show promise for enhancing site-specific MSC homing efficiency [178]. Scaffold biomimicry and mechanical properties require further optimization. Natural cartilage uniquely combines high stiffness (compressive resistance) with exceptional toughness (deformation resistance), a dual mechanical performance that current hydrogel materials inherently struggle to replicate. OC defects involve concurrent damage to both articular cartilage and subchondral bone. Consequently, repair strategies must address: the distinct mechanical requirements of osseous and chondral tissues, and the critical bone-cartilage interfacial integration [179].

Hydrogel scaffolds require adequate strength and compressive modulus to mimic mechanics, yet insufficient mechanical properties often accelerate degradation. For instance: dECM hydrogels exhibit low mechanical strength and rapid degradation rates, compromising structural integrity during multilayer scaffold maintenance and tissue regeneration [170]; polysaccharide-based nanofibers demonstrate excellent biocompatibility but undergo fast degradation in physiological environments [180]; novel protein hydrogels achieve stiffness-toughness balance through entangled protein networks; however, their long-term stability necessitates further validation [181]. Although existing studies demonstrate the biocompatibility of hydrogel scaffolds, their long-term safety profile requires comprehensive investigation. Future research should focus on developing advanced biomaterials that emulate the structural and compositional complexity of OC unit to achieve multifunctional hydrogel scaffolds with optimized biomechanical, biochemical, and biointegrative properties.

Developing a more accurate evaluation system for clinical transformation is critically needed. A key limitation in current preclinical research lies in the prevalent use of small animal models with acute OC defects. These models inadequately replicate human joint anatomy. Critical differences include joint thickness, synovial volume, and the concentration gradients of IL-1β and Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha within the synovial fluid. Furthermore, acute defect models in small animals exhibit a greater capacity for spontaneous recovery compared to the clinical reality [182]. Therefore, future research should consider utilizing large animal models, such as horses and pigs. Compared to small animals, large animals possess joint dimensions, cartilage thickness, and weight-bearing loading patterns that more closely resemble those of humans. This anatomical and physiological similarity makes them better suited for long-term experimental studies employing chronic OC defects models. Furthermore, the evaluation criteria for such studies should incorporate biomechanical assessments and molecular imaging analyses [15].
