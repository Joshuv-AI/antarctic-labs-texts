Can Cord Entanglement Occur in Monochorionic Diamniotic Twins? A Rare Case of Spontaneous Septostomy of Dividing Membranes
Authors: Reem Bolbol, Anfal Al Hadithi, Sara Kidher, Zainb Eltom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTwin pregnancies are known to be complicated, with monochorionic monoamniotic (MCMA) pregnancies holding more risks compared to dichorionic diamniotic (DCDA) and monochorionic diamniotic (MCDA) pregnancies. Cord entanglement is a common complication in the context of MCMA twins, however can this occur in MCDA twins?CaseA 26-year-old woman was referred to a high-risk tertiary unit at 18 weeks gestation, where scan confirmed MCDA pregnancy. She was regularly followed up in clinic. After presenting at 29 weeks with preterm labor that was managed at the time; she later presented at 31 weeks, distressed with labor pains. Repeated scans showed unexpected severe intrauterine growth restriction of both babies. During her Cesarean section at 32 weeks, clinicians noted both umbilical cords were tightly tangled, which uniquely occurs with MCMA rather than MCDA. Upon reviewing the literature for similar cases, an extremely rare phenomenon called spontaneous septostomy of dividing membranes (SSDM) had occurred. This led to an MCDA twin pregnancy functionally becoming MCMA with its high risks of cord entanglement and fetal demise.DiscussionThe incidence of SSDM is truly unknown due to the rarity of the condition, but it is associated with increased morbidity and mortality. Limited literature states that possible causes of this are abdominal trauma during pregnancy, iatrogenic procedures, and chorioamnionitis. Despite the great mortality rate of this complication, both neonates in our case were discharged after a month in the NICU in good condition, showcasing the effectiveness of close monitoring and immediate response.ConclusionMCDA twin pregnancy becoming MCMA due to SSDM is a rare phenomenon. Our case highlights the peculiar presentation of SSDM and importance of prompt management.

Introduction

Monozygotic twins occur when a single fertilized ovum splits into two separate embryos. If the zygote splits from 4 to 8 days, it is monochorionic diamniotic (MCDA) where the twins share the placenta; if the split occurs from 9 to 12 days, it is monochorionic monoamniotic (MCMA) where the twins share the placenta and amniotic sac. Monochorionic twins occur in 20% of twin pregnancies, and are more challenging in nature due to placental vascular connections between the fetal circulations. Subsequent complications that may arise are twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome (TTTS), twins’ anemia-polycythemia sequence (TAPS), twin reversed arterial perfusion (TRAP) and selective intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR). MCMA make up approximately 1% of monochorionic twin pregnancies and have the “highest perinatal mortality 30–70%”1 due to complications mentioned above, intrauterine fetal death of one or both twins, prematurity, congenital anomalies and umbilical cords entanglement. However, “with more intensive fetal surveillance, perinatal mortality has declined to 8–11% after 24–26 weeks gestation”.2 This case report details an unexpected finding of cord entanglement in a confirmed MCDA twin pregnancy, possibly due to compromised integrity of the dividing membrane, a rare phenomenon called spontaneous rupture of dividing membranes (SSDM).

Materials and Methods

Written informed consent to publish the case along with the images (without any identifiers) has been obtained from the patient. Institutional approval was not required to publish the case. Similar cases in the literature were reviewed using PubMed search engine and Wiley online library. Cited texts were appropriately referenced.

Case Report

A 26 years old woman, Para 1, with spontaneous MCDA pregnancy presented to our high-risk maternity unit in a top-tier tertiary hospital at 18 weeks of gestation. She was following with another maternal-fetal medicine facility earlier and early imaging in pregnancy confirmed MCDA twins. Her past medical history was insignificant and surgical history was positive for bariatric surgery. Her previous delivery was a normal vaginal delivery four years prior. Her scan at 18 weeks showed one placenta with a clear dividing membrane between both sacs (Figure 1). She followed up in obstetrics and maternal-fetal medicine clinics. At 29 weeks + 3 days, the patient presented to the emergency room complaining of lower abdominal pain and reduced fetal movements of both babies. Scan showed normal growth of twins with discordance of 5%, normal amniotic fluid volumes, normal UADs, and a short V-shaped cervix (2.0 cm long). Subsequently, she was admitted for preterm labor and received Atosiban along with two doses of betamethasone and discharged one week later in a good condition. She presented again at 31 weeks + 2 days distressed with labor pains, cervical os was 3 cm dilated, and 0.5 cm long. Unexpectedly, repeated scan showed both babies were IUGR with all centiles less than 2.3%, with normal amniotic fluid volumes and UADs. Daily assessment of amniotic fluids and UADs were normal, however umbilical cord was seen around the second twin’s neck. Decision to deliver was taken due to the unexplained static growth of both babies over the two weeks. She was counselled for delivery vaginal delivery as both were cephalic; however, the patient opted for Cesarean section. She underwent elective Cesarean section at 32 weeks of gestation after receiving magnesium sulphate infusion. Interestingly during the Cesarean section, after delivery of both babies, both umbilical cords were noted to be tightly tangled in a braid-like fashion (Figure 2), which is not expected to occur in MCDA twins. First twin’s APGAR scores were 3, 5 and 8 at 1, 5 and 10 minutes respectively; second twin’s APGAR scores were 7, 8 and 9 at 1, 5 and 10 minutes respectively. Both babies were admitted to neonatal intensive care due to prematurity and low birth weights, and both were discharged a month later at corrected gestational age 37 weeks + 4 days. Histopathology later on confirmed it was indeed a MCDA twins’ placenta, and there was no evidence of cords entanglement on the antenatal scans. Discussion

In twin pregnancies, it is crucial to determine the chorionicity and amnionicity early in pregnancy to tailor the antenatal care, anticipate antenatal and perinatal complications and to manage accordingly. Chorionicity of twin pregnancy is best determined before 14 weeks, based on the appearance of the intertwin membrane attachment to the placenta and the membrane thickness. Monochorionic placentas contain vascular anastomoses running between the two umbilical cords, either arterial-arterial, venous-venous or arterial-venous. Along with the routine nuchal translucency measurements in first trimester and detailed scan between 18 and 20 weeks for fetal anomalies, fetal ultrasound assessment should be done every two weeks from 16 weeks onwards till birth. With every ultrasound examination, the following should be measured and documented: amniotic fluid in both sacs, fetal biometries along with the difference in estimated fetal weights, umbilical artery pulsatile index from 16 weeks and middle cerebral artery peak systolic volume from 20 weeks. Also, fetal bladders should be visualized and their presence documented. These rigorous follow up scans are for early detection of complications and prompt management. According to the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (RCOG) “TTTS complicates about 15–20% of monochorionic twins, TAPS up to 2%, selective IUGR up to 20%, and TRAP approximately 1%”.3 Timing of birth of MCDA twins should be planned from 36 weeks unless there are complications. If preterm birth is planned up to 34 weeks + 6, then antenatal corticosteroids should be administered; if before 34 weeks, then magnesium sulfate should be administered as well. It is appropriate to aim for vaginal birth of MCDA twins unless there are other specific indications for Cesarean section.3

MCMA pregnancies are associated with an even higher risk of fetal/perinatal loss, most commonly before 24 weeks of gestation due to discordant fetal anomalies or associated TRAP syndrome.3

Aside from the complications mentioned above, a complication unique to MCMA twins is cord entanglement due to the lack of a protective barrier, ie, the dividing membrane. This leads to restricted blood flow, growth restriction or even fetal demise. These twins require close surveillance, often with hospitalization for continuous monitoring. The RCOG recommends delivery of MCMA twin pregnancy between 32 and 34 weeks by Caesarean section to minimize the risk of stillbirth.

Our case was an uncomplicated MCDA twin pregnancy antenatally; however, at the time of delivery, the umbilical cords were peculiarly tangled mimicking an MCMA gestation. The functional transition of MCDA gestation to MCMA has been reported in the literature to occur with an uncommon phenomenon called spontaneous septostomy of the dividing membranes (SSDM). The incidence of SSDM is truly unknown due to the rarity of the condition. In a case study by Chmait et al in 2009, they found a rate of SSDM at 1.8% among a highly selected cohort of MCDA twins referred to the tertiary care center for potential fetal therapy.1

Postulated causes of SSDM in the literature include maternal abdominal trauma or traumatic disruption of the membrane due to invasive procedures like amniocentesis, amnioreduction and laser ablation. Other possible causes are chorioamnionitis, fetal movement, amniotic plica (fold in membrane), and polyhydramnios.

The pseudo-monoamniotic environment following SSDM poses heightened risks of cord entanglement causing fetal demise, amniotic band syndrome (remnant of ruptured membrane wraps around fetal limbs) and preterm labor. A similar case was reported in 2015, where MCDA twins were both cephalic and first twin was diagnosed as IUGR at 35 weeks + 2. On the day of induction of labor at 35 weeks + 6, first twin’s heart was not visualized and was delivered by emergency Cesarean section as a fresh stillborn.4 Fortunately in our case, new finding of the twins’ static growths at 31 weeks prompting delivery at 32 weeks (in accordance with MCMA timing of delivery) and maternal refusal of vaginal delivery ensured their wellbeing despite the tight entanglement of their cords. One case in 2018 of MCDA twins delivered by Cesarean section at 32 weeks, reported that one of the twins at time of birth had a broad band of transparent membrane material attached to her abdomen. She developed respiratory distress and persistent pulmonary hypertension postnatally. Unfortunately, she passed away day one after birth, and postmortem examination showed severe lung hypoplasia and the attached material was of the dividing septum. It was hypothesized that SSDM has occurred, with the affected twin being stuck in the defect of the dividing membrane leading to local oligohydramnios and lung hypoplasia. This condition is known as amniotic band syndrome, which is extremely rare.5

The most recent reported case in 2021 was diagnosed with SSDM at 28 weeks by detailed sonographic assessment, was managed as pseudo-monoamniotic pregnancy and was delivered by Cesarean section at 32 weeks.6

Cases of SSDM are often diagnosed retrospectively due to unfamiliarity with the condition, and lack of clear diagnostic criteria to follow. Early sonographic indicators of SSDM can be visualization of a hole in the dividing membrane, multilayer folding of the membrane or residual membrane fragments on fetal parts. Chmait et al described the following sonographic findings were suggestive of SSDM, if a prior invasive procedure had not been performed: absence or interruption of the dividing membrane; both fetuses occupying the same side of the dividing membrane; umbilical cord entanglement and excess amniotic fluid on both sides of the dividing membrane, with characteristics suggestive of TTTS.1

Thorough assessment of dividing membrane integrity on antenatal scans should be implemented to prevent the risks of cord entanglement and subsequent fetal demise.

Conclusion

Twin pregnancies are high-risk pregnancies by default and require close fetal surveillance for early diagnosis and management of complications if they do arise. Our case of SSDM transitioning MCDA twins into MCMA, which is a very rare complication, highlights the importance of assessment of membrane integrity during antenatal follow ups. SSDM should be suspected if MCDA twins show unexpected new findings on antenatal scans such as interruption of intertwin membrane, shared amniotic space or cord entanglement.
