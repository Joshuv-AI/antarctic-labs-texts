Accelerating Spanning-Centrality Approximation with Sampling-Aware Vectorized Random Walks
Gökhan Göktürk, Kamer Kaya

Abstract
Spanning centrality quantifies an edge’s importance through its share of a graph’s spanning trees, and approximating it for all edges (AESC) requires processing large volumes of irregular random walks under a prescribed error bound. Its applications include phylogenetic analysis and network robustness assessment. We focus on this random-walk processing bottleneck and propose Sampling-Aware Bouquet Arrangement (SABA), a CPU sampling and scheduling scheme that orders walks initialized on a uniform grid so that adjacent walks execute together in SIMD-width groups, called bouquets; for fixed per-walk random inputs, the ordering preserves the multiset of sampled walks while improving locality during neighbor access. On synthetic benchmarks, SABA achieves geometric-mean speedups of 6.95 × 6.95\times and 7.41 × 7.41\times over traditional random-walk generation for 2048 2048 and 16,384 16{,}384 walks per vertex, respectively, and up to ≈ 49 × \approx 49\times in the latter setting. Within AESC, the complete 16-thread SABA implementation is 58.23 × 58.23\times faster than the original single-threaded TGT+ implementation on average.

Introduction

Spanning centrality
(
SC
) measures the importance of an edge in a graph for maintaining the graph’s connectivity. For an edge
e
in a connected graph
G

G
that contain

\textsc{SC}(e)=\frac{\text{Number of spanning trees in $G$ containing }e}{\text{Total number of spanning trees in }G}.

The metric quantifies how crucial an edge is for the graph to remain connected, reflecting the edge’s role in the overall structure and stability of the network.
SC has been used in phylogenetic analysis
Teixeira et al. (2016)
and to identify edges whose removal disrupts information propagation
Mavroforakis et al. (2015)

Local metrics such as

and distance-based metrics such as

Although spanning centrality appears to be

phylogeny analysis
or
resiliency/robustness analysis

Teixeira et al. (2016)
.
This gives a different view of edge importance from shortest-path-based metrics.

Let

|V|=n

|E|=m
edges. As expected, computing

by taking all the spanning trees into account is not computationally feasible. In the literature, the fastest algorithm to compute the spanning centrality of all the edges in a graph has time complexity
\mathcal{O}(mn^{\frac{3}{2}})
, which is not practical for large networks. This is why

have been the standard practical approach, and various algorithms have been proposed in the last decade
Zhang et al. (2023)
;
Peng et al. (2021)
;
Hayashi et al. (2016)
;
Mavroforakis et al. (2015)

Existing approaches include uniform spanning-tree sampling

Peng et al. (2021)
.
TGT+

Zhang et al. (2023)
combines truncated deterministic traversals with random-walk sampling while keeping every visited vertex available to the application; this property makes it the integration target of our walk-acceleration scheme.

As the exact
SC
algorithms suffer from computationally expensive matrix kernels, several approximation algorithms also incur the cost of processing many long random walks. In graphs, a
random walk
is a path that traverses the vertices of a graph
randomly

Pearson (1905)
.
They have been proven to be extremely useful in many randomized algorithms.
In physics, they are used to model the behaviour of particles or molecules in a medium. In network science, random walks are used to detect communities and simulate the spread of information or diseases. This work focuses on their role in approximating spanning centrality.

Recently, Zhang et al. proposed two approximation algorithms,
TGT
and

, to efficiently approximate the spanning centrality of edges in large graphs

Zhang et al. (2023)
.
A major contribution is the improved theoretical bounds for truncating long random walks, which significantly enhances the efficiency and accuracy of the approximation process. By optimizing truncated lengths and reducing the number of random paths processed,

Even so, these costs remain prohibitive in practice, as our own measurements on large graphs confirm (Section

Based on the above, three research challenges are addressed in this study: First, since each step of a walk samples a uniformly chosen neighbor, the memory accesses incurred when many walks are processed are irregular, and the memory subsystem dominates the runtime. Third, the traditional scheme lengthens a single walk at a time, which precludes vectorized processing on modern CPUs.

In this work, we focus on how to approximate the spanning centrality of the edges in large networks by processing random walks much faster on modern CPUs with SIMD instructions, wide registers, and vectorization opportunities. The contributions of this study are summarized below:

A novel hash-based sampling that improves data locality by clustering random walks into bouquets is proposed.

•

A vectorized random-walk processing scheme based on this sampling is designed and implemented.

•

The proposed techniques can be applied to various path-based Monte Carlo algorithms on graphs.

2.1
Spanning Centrality

Spanning centrality measures edge importance through graph-wide connectivity. Its applications include phylogenetic analysis
Teixeira et al. (2016)
and studies of network resilience and information propagation
Mavroforakis et al. (2015)
.

For a connected undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, the
spanning centrality
of an edge
e\in E
,
\textsc{SC}(e)
is defined as the fraction of spanning trees of
G
that contain
e
relative to the total number of spanning trees of the entire graph
G
. Hence, when
\textsc{SC}(e)=1
, the subgraph obtained by removing
e
from the edge set, i.e.,
G\setminus\{e\}
, is disconnected. Formally,

\textsc{SC}(e)=1-\frac{|T(G\setminus\{e\})|}{|T(G)|}

where
T(G)
denotes the set of spanning trees of
G
and
|T(G)|
their number; this is equivalent to the ratio form used in the introduction,
\textsc{SC}(e)=|\{T\in T(G):e\in T\}|/|T(G)|
. In particular,
\textsc{SC}(e)=1
precisely when
e
is a bridge.

For disconnected inputs, the definition is applied within each edge’s component.
Unfortunately,
T
, therefore
SC
, is expensive to compute, especially for real-world networks.

As a worked example, consider the sample graph in Figure
1
, which has
|T(G)|=8
spanning trees. Four of them contain
\{B,C\}
, namely
\{\{A,C\},\{B,C\},\{B,D\}\}
,
\{\{A,B\},\{B,C\},\{B,D\}\}
,
\{\{A,C\},\{B,C\},\{C,D\}\}
, and
\{\{A,B\},\{B,C\},\{C,D\}\}
; hence
\textsc{SC}(\{B,C\})=1-\frac{4}{8}=\frac{1}{2}
, i.e., removing
\{B,C\}
leaves the remaining four trees. Five spanning trees contain
\{A,B\}
:
\{\{A,B\},\{A,C\},\{B,D\}\}
,
\{\{A,B\},\{A,C\},\{C,D\}\}
,
\{\{A,B\},\{B,C\},\{B,D\}\}
,
\{\{A,B\},\{B,C\},\{C,D\}\}
, and
\{\{A,B\},\{B,D\},\{C,D\}\}
; hence
\textsc{SC}(\{A,B\})=1-\frac{3}{8}=\frac{5}{8}
. The three remaining edges are symmetric to
\{A,B\}
and also have
\textsc{SC}=\frac{5}{8}
.

In this work, we will be focusing on the problem of spanning centrality computation for all edges, i.e.,
All Edges Spanning Centrality
(
AESC
). The current state-of-the-art
Teixeira et al. (2013)
for

is based on Kirchhoff’s matrix-tree theory and exhibits a time complexity of

, which is infeasible for analyzing large graphs. To overcome the computational burden, many approximation methods, based on random walks, have been proposed
Zhang et al. (2023)
;
Peng et al. (2021)
;
Hayashi et al. (2016)
;
Mavroforakis et al. (2015)
. In the approximation setting, given the graph
G=(V,E)
, an
\epsilon
-approximation algorithm outputs

for each

such that

|\hat{\textsc{SC}}(e)-{\textsc{SC}}(e)|\leq\epsilon,~~\forall e\in E

where

is an upper bound on the errors of reported

An output satisfying the above condition is called an

\epsilon
-approximation. For randomized algorithms, the probability
of satisfying this condition is stated separately.

2.4
Hash-based Random Number Generation

Pseudo-random number generators produce sequences by updating an internal state. Hash-based sampling combines a simulation seed with a hash of the graph element being sampled. In the edge-sampling method of Göktürk and Kaya
Göktürk and Kaya (2020)
, a seed
\mathcal{X}_{r}
identifies the
r
th simulation, and each edge
\{u,v\}\in E
receives the value

P(\{u,v\})_{r}=\frac{\mathcal{X}_{r}\oplus h(u,v)}{h_{max}},

(2)

where
P(\{u,v\})_{r}
is the pseudo-random number compared against the sampling probability of
\{u,v\}
while generating the
r
th sample. In the equation,
h(u,v)
is the hash value of the edge
\{u,v\}
,
\mathcal{X}_{r}
is an
independently chosen
random seed for the
r
th sample, and
h_{max}
denotes the size of the hash domain, so hash values lie in
\{0,\ldots,h_{max}-1\}
; throughout this work,
h_{max}=2^{32}
.

For random walks, we retain this seed-and-hash composition but use the current vertex hash and an evolving per-walk state. Section
3.2
gives the neighbor-selection rule and degree-based state update.

2.5
Vectorized Processing and SIMD

Single Instruction Multiple Data
(SIMD) is a parallelization technique that enables a single instruction to perform the same operation on multiple data items concurrently. SIMD units are available in almost all modern CPUs and GPUs to achieve computational efficiency for tasks allowing data-level parallelism. In these units, the data is often organized into vectors, and a single instruction is applied to all elements in the vector. Modern processors often feature SIMD instruction sets such as Intel’s AVX2 (Advanced Vector Extensions 2) or ARM’s NEON.
These instructions typically improve the throughput, though they may have slightly higher latency.

GPUs expose analogous units as Streaming Multiprocessors or Compute Units executing warps/wavefronts of 32–64 threads in lockstep; the bouquet arrangement extends naturally to such architectures.

3
Sampling-Aware Bouquets for Approximate
AESC

In modern processors, when the computation and data layout incur highly irregular memory accesses, such as sparse matrix, graph, and sparse tensor operations, performance is drastically impacted. As in many random-walk-based Monte Carlo implementations on graphs, in AESC, each walk is processed individually, and each step in a walk iteratively samples only
a single
vertex to traverse. This yields irregular memory access patterns since the vertex neighborhoods are irregular by nature. However, for most Monte Carlo algorithms, it is possible to rearrange the order of operations so that many random walks are processed together. In this work, instead of handling the sampling and processing steps individually for a single random walk, we propose to
fuse
them within the course of a randomized algorithm. As explained later, this yields a memory-efficient lockstep processing scheme, which can be used to
regularize
the computation. This regularization improves both the spatial and temporal locality of the operations performed and allows more efficient use of the computing resources.
When
n_{req}
is large, which is the case for AESC especially if the desired approximation error is small, these walks share vertices which can be exploited via vectorization.

3.1
SIMD Execution and its Locality Bottleneck

Each walk maintains a streaming state
\mathcal{X}_{W}
, initialized on a uniform grid. At each streaming step, the state is XOR’ed with the hash
h(cur)
of the current vertex to select a neighbor, and is then multiplied by the degree
d(cur)
of the departure vertex, with 32-bit wraparound. Thus, the hash does not depend on the walk level. Revisiting a vertex can use a different state, although the recurrence does not guarantee that repeated selections or cycles are avoided. The initial grid excludes zero. If the state becomes zero, only the affected lane switches to conventional AVX2 sampling for its remaining hops, as specified in (
6
). For fixed per-walk fallback draws, each labeled walk is determined by its initial state and starting vertex; Proposition 1 states the conditions under which reordering preserves its outcome.

Although vectorization can significantly improve performance, with the traditional random-walk generation process, exploiting the CPU’s vector capabilities is impossible since each step lengthens
a single walk
by one. In this work, although we have implemented our methods only using AVX2 intrinsics on the CPU, they can also be applied while processing many random walks on GPUs.

The basic random walk generation process given in Algorithm
1
, by its design, is not SIMD-friendly and furthermore, incurs an unpredictable memory access pattern. In simple terms, the algorithm performs iterative random number generation and random neighbor selection. The state-of-the-art RNGs are fast and only require a few simple instructions like integer addition and exclusive-or. In addition, the (short) latency of these instructions can be hidden, which makes them negligible compared to the overall runtime. This leaves edge traversal, i.e., random memory access, as the major bottleneck for the performance.
Furthermore, the expensive modulus operation executed while selecting the new vertex has a long
latency
that cannot be hidden since its result is immediately required. Both problems can be remedied by first scheduling vectorized random walks into
bouquets
as a first step.

3.2
SABA: Sampling-Aware Bouquet Arrangement

A vectorized processing scheme alone does not solve the memory access problems of random-walk processing without a naive sampling. In addition to vectorization,
sampling-aware bouquet arrangement
(SABA) arranges the random walks with respect to their potential memory accesses by manipulating and exploiting the underlying random number generation scheme. Let
cur\in V
be the current vertex for a random walk
W
. The straightforward neighbor-vertex sampling, given in (
3
), generates a pseudo-random number and constrains the result within the cardinality of the current vertex’s neighborhood using the
modulus
operator, which induces a significant latency.

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k_{W,l}=\mathcal{R}_{W,l}~\bmod~d(cur)}

(3)

W_{4}
to

Processing multiple random walks indeed allows us to perform vectorized operations for vertex selection and random number generation. However, when the corresponding memory accesses require different cache lines as in Fig.
2(b)
, the performance of the memory subsystem becomes the bottleneck. The proposed approach seeks to remedy this by sampling similar paths together. The computation is regularized by

1.

a per-walk state
\mathcal{X}_{W}
, initialized on a uniform grid before execution and advanced by degree multiplication, and

2.

the hash
h(cur)
of the current vertex.

Sampling-aware bouquet arrangement
(SABA) uses the hash-based RNG scheme below and
arranges the walks within bouquets by ordering their initial grid states
\mathcal{X}_{W}
;

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f(\mathcal{X}_{W},cur)=\left\lfloor\frac{{\mathcal{X}}_{W}\oplus h(cur)}{h_{max}}\times d(cur)\right\rfloor}

(4)

where
h_{max}=2^{32}
and
0\leq f(\mathcal{X}_{W},cur)<d(cur)
. After selecting the neighbor, we stream the state forward using

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{X}_{W}\leftarrow\bigl(\mathcal{X}_{W}\times d(cur)\bigr)\bmod h_{max}.}

(5)

Here
cur
denotes the departure vertex: its degree must be retained before advancing to the selected neighbor. The initial grid value is attached to the walk descriptor, whereas
\mathcal{X}_{W}
evolves during execution. The state update uses the low 32 bits of the product; neighbor selection uses the high 32 bits of
(\mathcal{X}_{W}\oplus h(cur))d(cur)
.

Before each hop, each lane checks its streaming state. A nonzero state uses the streaming selection and update above; a zero state uses a fresh pseudo-random draw as in (
3
):

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k_{W,l}=\begin{cases}f(\mathcal{X}_{W},cur),&\mathcal{X}_{W}\neq 0,\\
\mathcal{R}_{W,l}\bmod d(cur),&\mathcal{X}_{W}=0.\end{cases}}

(6)

Initial grid values exclude zero, so every lane starts in streaming mode. If a streaming update makes
\mathcal{X}_{W}=0
, that lane switches to conventional AVX2 sampling from the next hop onward, using the same fresh pseudo-random draws and neighbor-selection rule as the Naive method. Its state remains zero, and it uses fallback for the remainder of the walk. Other lanes continue streaming while their own states are nonzero.

Proposition 1

(Execution Schedule Invariance)
.

Let
S=\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{N}\}
denote the fixed set of simulation identifiers. Each simulation
s\in S
defines a deterministic outcome
X(s)
, with its initial grid value, starting vertex, and any fallback random draws fixed independently of the execution schedule. The Monte Carlo estimator is

\hat{\mu}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s\in S}X(s).

Let
\pi:S\rightarrow S
be an arbitrary permutation representing the execution schedule. Since
\pi
is bijective, every simulation identifier appears exactly once in both the original and reordered schedules. Therefore,

\sum_{s\in S}X(\pi(s))=\sum_{s\in S}X(s),

which immediately implies

\hat{\mu}_{\pi}=\hat{\mu}.

A simulation identifier corresponds to a labeled walk with its initial grid value and any fallback random draws attached to that identity. Reordering preserves the finite set of walk outcomes when these inputs remain fixed. In particular, fallback draws must follow the walk identity for the pointwise equality in Proposition 1 to apply; assigning draws solely by their consumption order in a shared generator does not establish that equality. The proposition concerns execution order and does not establish independent sampling or the estimator error bound for uniform-grid initialization.

Theorem 1

(Range-Reduction Bias)
.

For a fixed degree
d>0
, let
U
be uniform over
\{0,\ldots,h_{max}-1\}
and let
K=\lfloor Ud/h_{max}\rfloor
. For any edge index
k
,
0\leq k<d
, we have

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Pr[K=k]=}\frac{\#\{u:\;kh_{max}/d\leq u<(k{+}1)h_{max}/d\}}{h_{max}},

where the count in the numerator is either
\lfloor h_{max}/d\rfloor
or
\lceil h_{max}/d\rceil
. Therefore,

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\max_{0\leq k<d}\left|\Pr[K=k]-\frac{1}{d}\right|}~\leq~\frac{1}{h_{max}}.

This theorem isolates range-reduction bias under uniform input. It does not establish that the uniform-grid initialization or the subsequent streaming states provide such input. Multiplication modulo
2^{32}
need not preserve a uniform state distribution. A lane switches to Naive sampling when its streaming state becomes zero, as in (
6
); this safeguard does not itself prove uniformity during streaming. Agreement with the reference implementation is assessed empirically in Section
5.1
. Under the same uniform-input assumption, modulus-based range reduction also has a per-neighbor deviation of at most
1/h_{max}
.

For fixed initial states and fixed per-walk fallback draws, Proposition 1 gives the same estimator under any permutation of the execution order. This statement applies to the complete sampling scheme and does not imply equivalence to a different random-walk generator.

The comparison with the original
TGT+
implementation is empirical: Section
5.1
compares their mean absolute errors against the same reference centralities.

The main benefit of SABA is that constructing bouquets from consecutive grid values places walks with nearby initial states together. Nearby states often share a high-order bit prefix. When lanes have the same current vertex, XOR with the common value
h(cur)
preserves that shared prefix. The contiguous range reduction in (
4
) then tends to map those lanes to the same or nearby adjacency indices. This locality effect is strongest at the first steps. Degree multiplication can change shared prefixes, and lanes reaching different vertices use different hashes. A lane whose state becomes zero switches to conventional AVX2 sampling, which no longer uses its ordered streaming state. Other lanes retain their streaming states until they also become zero or complete their walks. Later-step locality is therefore a graph-dependent heuristic, evaluated through the cache and branching measurements in Section
5.2
. The schedule-preservation conditions, including fallback draws, are stated in Proposition 1.

Implementation Details

Parallelizing the original sequential
TGT+
implementation
Zhang et al. (2023)
requires synchronizing its shared runtime accumulators and edge-keyed containers, which do not admit concurrent writers.

The outer source loop of Algorithm
2
admits parallel execution. Both the deterministic and sampled contributions for source
v_{i}
update its directed quantities
\hat{g}_{\tau}(v_{i},v_{j})
, whose entries are distinct from those of other sources. If contributions from both endpoints are accumulated directly into a shared undirected edge-result slot, concurrent updates to that slot require synchronization.

In our implementation, each source vertex is assigned to one worker, giving that worker exclusive ownership of its source-local computation. Shared edge-result updates use atomic accumulation, and workers do not keep private copies of the complete
\hat{g}
array. The extra storage per thread consists of scratch buffers of size
\Theta(n)
; hence, the overall footprint is
\Theta(m+T\cdot n)
for
T
threads.

Each walk descriptor occupies
8
bytes, giving
\Theta(n_{req})
descriptors per source, and a bouquet holds
\Theta(B)
words of transient state during processing; walk paths are never materialized, so this footprint is independent of
L
.

The original
TGT+
implementation relies on sparse data structures, specifically the C++
std::map
, to store edge-specific information. Swapping compilers and tweaking compilation flags can significantly increase its performance. Instead of relying on compiler optimizations, in our implementation, we have refrained from using these data structures. Wherever intermediate data structures are used, e.g., the
map
with edges as keys in the original
TGT+
implementation, we have skipped these steps and update the single shared result array in place.

In SABA and other vectorized baselines, we have used explicit AVX2 intrinsics to exploit instruction-level parallelism for hashing, XOR, and multiplication operations for generating random numbers. As the hash function, we used a vectorized implementation of MurmurHash3’s 32-bit finalizer,
fmix32

Appleby (2016)
. Neighbor selection in (
4
) uses the high 32 bits of an unsigned 32-by-32-bit product. The state update in (
5
) retains the low 32 bits of the state-degree product. Both streaming operations avoid integer division and modulus instructions.

In the AESC experiments, Baseline includes the data-structure and memory optimizations of our
TGT+
reimplementation. SABA adds uniform-grid streaming, per-lane fallback, and ordered bouquets as a combined sampling and scheduling design. These components are designed to work together, and the Baseline/SABA ratio measures their joint benefit beyond the shared optimizations.

The scalar and conventional SIMD walk kernels use the C++
std::mt19937
implementation of Mersenne Twister
Matsumoto and Nishimura (1998)
for their random draws. SABA uses the streaming state and vertex hash defined in Section
3.2
, with conventional AVX2 sampling for a lane once its streaming state becomes zero, as in (
6
). Fallback uses the same fresh pseudo-random draws and modulo-based neighbor selection as the Naive random-walk method in Algorithm
1
; it does not introduce a separate generator.

5.4
Applicability and Limitations

SABA is expected to help when lanes reuse current vertices or nearby adjacency locations.
When duplicates are present in bouquets, the edges can be retrieved from the cache. Otherwise, even with bouquets, the same number of retrievals are done with additional register and memory pressure.
Bouquet ordering is therefore a useful heuristic whose effectiveness depends on the occurrence of duplicates within the processed walks.

The bouquet width
B
is also bounded by the available SIMD lane count; with AVX2 registers, eight 32-bit lanes are processed per instruction.

For a bouquet with
B
random walks, let
\beta
be the number of elements the samples come from and
\eta
be the number of distinct items in a single level of a bouquet.
To obtain a tractable expression, the lanes are assumed to sample independently and uniformly from a fixed pool of
\beta
elements; this independent-lane model serves as a diagnostic only, since SABA processes state-dependent walks whose seed-sorted lanes are
conditionally dependent.
Under these assumptions, the ratio of the number of distinct items to the number of selected items can be written as

\frac{\mathbb{E}[\eta]}{B}=\frac{\beta}{B}\left(1-\left(1-\frac{1}{\beta}\right)^{B}\right)\approx\frac{\beta}{B}\left(1-e^{-B/\beta}\right).

(8)

Intuitively, independently sampling a small number of vertices from a large number of vertices, e.g.
\beta\gg B
, makes duplicate items not frequent.
Deviations of SABA from these assumptions imply that the formula indicates a trend rather than a bound.

Bouquets pay off when the effective support of a level is small or concentrated, so many lanes revisit the same vertices or cache lines; when neighborhoods are large and nearly uniform, lanes rarely converge on the same neighbors and the locality gain disappears. Longer walks spread lane states apart level by level, so the gains concentrate on the early levels.

Our experiments cover social, collaboration, and peer-to-peer graphs, together with the California road network and Delaunay-N21 mesh. The two structured networks show some of the largest combined synthetic speedups and substantial cache-miss reductions, so large diameter alone does not imply a small benefit. These synthetic comparisons combine sampling, range reduction, and ordering; they do not isolate ordering. California has a completed AESC benchmark, whereas Delaunay-N21 does not. We therefore distinguish the synthetic results from end-to-end AESC behavior and do not generalize beyond the tested graph families.

On almost-regular graphs, nearby initial states can still produce the same or nearby first-step selections. Later steps depend on the visited vertices, state multiplication, and fallback. The independent-lane occupancy model above is a diagnostic rather than a bound on these scheduled walks. The measured synthetic and cache results, rather than degree or diameter alone, characterize the benefit on California and Delaunay-N21.

6
Related Work

In the literature, there exist two ways to improve random walk performance on graphs; first, approximation methods can utilize underlying graph structures to generate diffusion matrices that estimate the probability of random walk visits.
ARK
, proposed by Kang et al.
Kang et al. (2012)
, utilizes low-rank structures of graphs and estimates random walk diffusion processes using a few ranks of eigenvectors. In addition, Sarma et al. proposed several algorithms
Das Sarma et al. (2013)
that employ dynamic programming to improve the asymptotic performance of random-walk problems, including destination-only
k
-random walks, source-destination pair
k
-random walks, and only the path position of vertices from
k
-random walks. In AESC, low-rank eigenvector methods can introduce errors, and they do not perform well for a relatively small number of walks or sources. Existing dynamic programming methods focus on single-source walks and might not be applicable when every step of the walk is required to be processed, i.e., when the vertices are required, in order, as in the case of AESC.
For spanning centrality, Hayashi et al.
Hayashi et al. (2016)
sample uniform spanning trees using Wilson's algorithm, Mavroforakis et al.
Mavroforakis et al. (2015)
combine random projections with Laplacian solvers, and Peng et al.
Peng et al. (2021)
use truncated random walks.
TGT+

Zhang et al. (2023)
combines truncated deterministic traversals with random walks while keeping every visited vertex available to the application. Recently, Liu et al.
Liu et al. (2026)
proposed near-linear algorithms for random-walk centrality based on incomplete Cholesky factorization and rooted spanning-tree sampling; random-walk centrality is a node hitting-time metric and is not the AESC metric studied here, but it illustrates the continued use of walk sampling in large-scale centrality computation.

The second category of performance improvements is utilizing compute resources efficiently,
SkyWalker+
, proposed by Wang et al.

Wang et al. (2023)
, uses compressed aliasing and speculative execution to speed up random walk workloads. There also exists a GPU-based method,
C-Saw
, proposed by Pandey et al.
Pandey et al. (2020)
, that exploits the sampling stage to improve performance and uses inverse transform sampling. A similar approach is applied by
NextDoor
, proposed by Jangda et al.
Jangda et al. (2020)
, via rejection sampling. These methods, even though they may be relevant, do not focus on CPU vectorization and spatial locality.
More recent systems broaden this design space:
LightRW

Tan et al. (2023)
pipelines dynamic graph random walks on FPGAs with degree-aware caching,
FastRW

Gao et al. (2024)
combines dataflow scheduling with hybrid memory on FPGAs,
FlowWalker

Mei et al. (2024)
employs a memory-efficient GPU sampler with dynamic scheduling, and
GRAPE

Cappelletti et al. (2023)
provides parallel CPU implementations for graph processing and random-walk-based embedding. These systems focus on high-throughput random-walk generation for graph embedding and machine-learning pipelines, exploiting the parallelism available in GPUs, FPGAs, and modern multicore CPUs.

Across both categories, three axes organize this landscape: the underlying approach, either structure-aware approximation or compute-efficient walk execution; the target hardware, ranging from single-threaded CPUs through multicore CPUs, GPUs, and FPGAs; and the graph structures handled well, where approximation methods favor static graphs amenable to eigendecomposition or factorization, whereas the locality benefit examined here varies across the tested social, collaboration, peer-to-peer, road, and mesh networks (Section
5.4
).

Boundary with our prior work: our influence-maximization kernels
Göktürk and Kaya (2020)
;
Göktürk and Kaya (2024)
employ seed-aware, hash-based simulation without mutable per-sample PRNG state and explore the graph in a breadth-first manner. This work extends that principle to random walks: each walk combines its evolving state with the hash of its current vertex, and ordering the walks by these seeds renders their execution locality-focused and depth-first-like, as lanes with similar seeds traverse similar paths through the graph. The contribution of this work is the identification of this bouquet structure together with the locality-focused random-walk processing it enables.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we presented a sampling-aware random walk method that clusters paths as
bouquets
. We tested the proposed approach on a real-world use case,
All Edges Spanning Centrality
. Using the proposed techniques, we provide a fast
AESC
implementation that utilizes multi-threading and
AVX2
instructions. We compared the performance with
the state-of-the-art
TGT+
implementation
on real-world datasets and illustrate that our implementation can be an order of magnitude faster.

Performance depends on the graph and workload: California and Delaunay-N21 show large gains from the combined sampling and bouquet design, while the California AESC execution scales non-monotonically and Delaunay-N21 has no completed AESC result. The tested graph families do not establish a universal performance guarantee (Section
5.4
).

Moreover, all results are obtained on Broadwell-era Xeons; wider vectors (AVX-512) and newer core architectures may shift the balance between vectorization and memory-bound behavior.

Since GPU execution of Monte-Carlo-based algorithms on graphs using SABA and hash-based sampling raises architecture-specific memory-management issues and would divert attention from the central contribution of this work, namely the bouquet structure, we defer its study to future work.
