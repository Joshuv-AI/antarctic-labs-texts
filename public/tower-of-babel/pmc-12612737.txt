Introducing an on-site Helicopter Emergency Medical Service (HEMS) physician in a Norwegian Emergency Medical Communication Centre: a focus group study on the experiences of operators and physicians
Authors: Kari Sand, Ole Erik Ulvin, Line Melby
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThe aim of this study was to explore the experiences of emergency medical communication centre (EMCC) operators and helicopter emergency medical service (HEMS) physicians following the introduction of an on-site HEMS physician in a Norwegian EMCC.DesignThe study was designed as a qualitative focus group study. Data from the focus groups were analysed using qualitative thematic analysis.SettingThe intervention was implemented at the regional EMCC and one HEMS base in mid-Norway.ParticipantsSeven HEMS physicians and 22 EMCC operators from the EMCC and HEMS base in Trondheim, Norway, participated in the focus groups.InterventionFrom 1 February to 5 July 2024, an on-site EMCC physician was introduced in Trondheim EMCC, being responsible for medical decision-making in HEMS dispatches during working hours in the intervention period.ResultsThe analysis revealed two overarching themes: (1) perceived impact on the decision-making process in HEMS dispatch and (2) increased trust through mutual learning and closer relationships. Overall, the study participants reported positive experiences with the intervention. They experienced that an on-site physician improved the basis for decision-making in HEMS dispatch, allowing for quicker decisions. The participants were uncertain regarding whether this led to more accurate HEMS dispatch decisions. They valued the opportunity to better understand each other’s skills and build stronger professional relationships.ConclusionsBoth EMCC operators and HEMS physicians experienced improved decision-making and faster dispatch conclusions in HEMS dispatch following the intervention. The high level of medical education and guideline adherence among EMCC operators questions the impact on HEMS dispatch accuracy following the intervention.Keywords: Organisation of health services, ACCIDENT & EMERGENCY MEDICINE, Emergency Departments

Background

The workload on prehospital emergency medical services (EMS) is steadily increasing worldwide, mainly due to population ageing and a general growth in the use of health services.1,3 This calls for measures to optimise dispatch of prehospital resources, including the limited and specialised helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS).4,6 Fundamental to prehospital resource optimisation is the handling of emergency calls and decision-making processes in emergency medical communication centres (EMCCs), which has been assessed in various studies previously.7,10

In Norway, EMCC operators in 16 EMCCs across the country answer emergency calls, conduct initial triage, provide first aid advice and dispatch and coordinate prehospital resources supported by the Norwegian Index for Emergency Medical Assistance.11,13 Most EMCCs in Norway are localised near or as a part of the emergency ward in hospitals. In certain situations, requesting HEMS is indicated based on national guidelines for HEMS activation.14 The decision to respond to a HEMS mission request is ultimately made based on medical judgement by the on-call HEMS physician and operational factors such as weather conditions, which are evaluated by the pilot.

According to national legislation, every EMCC in Norway is required to have a consultant physician available around the clock to assist the EMCC operator on request.12 Norwegian EMCCs are currently not staffed with a dedicated EMCC physician (EP); instead, this responsibility is normally handled by the prehospital anaesthesiologist who is concurrently on duty at the nearest HEMS base.15 However, several official reports have raised concerns regarding this dual role of the EP and the HEMS physician on-call.15 16 The availability of the EP has been disputed, as the HEMS physician might be unavailable for EMCC consultations regarding other ongoing emergencies whenever occupied in a HEMS mission. Due to flight duty time and crew rest legislations, the HEMS physician might also be inaccessible for several hours following a busy HEMS activity period. Furthermore, the official reports have recommended that the EP should be able to be physically present at the EMCCs when necessary—such as during mass casualty incidents—with access to the same tools and information as EMCC operators. This is, however, not feasible, since HEMS bases are not generally located in the immediate vicinity of the EMCC.16

Another challenge associated with the current dual-role arrangement is the potential mixing of roles and clinical considerations related to a mission.16 17 While the EP should support the EMCC operator in allocating the appropriate prehospital resources to a patient based on available clinical information, the HEMS physician will have to assess the request for HEMS dispatch in another context as part of the three-person HEMS crew. For instance, the decision of the HEMS physician might potentially be affected by opinions from other crew members, operational flight aspects, fatigue, previous personal experiences in similar missions and so forth. Thus, it has been suggested that the decision on whether or not to dispatch HEMS should not be made by the HEMS physician responding to the mission.16 17

To assess the reported concerns related to the combined EMCC and HEMS physician model, these roles were split in a recent study by introducing an on-site EP responsible for medical decision-making in HEMS dispatches during a 5-month intervention period in Trondheim EMCC.18 The aim of this study was to explore the experiences of EMCC operators and physicians who participated in this intervention.

Methods

Study setting

Among a total of eight hospitals in the region of Central Norway, St. Olav’s University Hospital in Trondheim serves as the tertiary referral centre. The regional EMCC is located in Trondheim and staffed by six EMCC operators at daytime, five in the evenings and four at night on weekdays. Since 2019, a specially trained EMCC operator is also present as the regional HEMS coordinator around the clock, responsible for dispatching the most appropriate HEMS unit on request.19 In Norway, all EMCC operators are healthcare professionals (eg, emergency medical technicians (EMTs), paramedics or nurses).12

The Norwegian HEMS system currently consists of 14 helicopters operating from 13 geographically distributed bases across the country, with the Trondheim HEMS base located in the central region of Norway. The base is staffed with a HEMS crew member (HCM), a pilot and an anaesthesiologist. HCMs hold a bachelor’s degree in either nursing or paramedicine, or relevant prehospital EMS experience without a bachelor’s degree.20 The basic requirements for physicians in the Norwegian HEMS system include being a certified anaesthetist, maintaining regular and relevant clinical medical practice in hospitals and ensuring continuity in their prehospital work by completing a minimum number of HEMS shifts annually.21 In missions close to the HEMS base, or when weather conditions do not permit flying, the crew is dispatched by a rapid response car located at the HEMS base. In addition to responding to HEMS mission requests, the HEMS physician on-call also provides medical guidance to the Trondheim EMCC in their role as the EP. Such consultations primarily focus on medical treatment and prehospital logistics in missions where HEMS is not activated.

The intervention

The intervention was implemented at the Trondheim EMCC from 1 February 2024 to 5 July 2024, involving the presence of an on-site HEMS physician during weekday daytime hours (Monday to Thursday from 10:00 to 22:00, and Fridays from 08:00 to 16:00 pm) except for public and school holidays. Intervention times were chosen to balance practicality and coverage of high HEMS activity periods. The seven participating physicians were recruited from Trondheim HEMS base and continued their ordinary HEMS shifts during the intervention period. For intervention hours from 08:00 to 16:00, the participating EPs were reallocated from other administrative duties during the study period, meaning these hours did not constitute additional working time. The physicians received additional compensation for intervention hours between 16:00 and 22:00.

Instructions on collaboration between EMCC operators and physicians were provided ahead of the intervention period. Whenever an EMCC operator received an emergency call where HEMS dispatch was either indicated by established criteria or considered relevant based on the operator’s clinical judgement, the EP was to be contacted without delay to make the final decision regarding HEMS dispatch. If the HEMS crew was alarmed, the on-call HEMS physician was expected to accept the mission without further medical inquiry. However, the HEMS physician retained the authority to decline a mission despite the EP’s decision if significant disagreements that could impact HEMS crew performance were present.

With the aim of standardising the EP’s role in the EMCC, the EPs were generally instructed to adopt a passive role during their EMCC shifts, intervening only when directly addressed (‘speak when spoken to’). EMCC operators had the option to contact the EP when needed but were obligated to do so for HEMS dispatch decisions. The EP was also instructed to passively monitor all acute 113 calls to ensure rapid response when requested by the operator. However, the EP was permitted to autonomously engage in an emergency call when considered necessary, provided that such involvement was clearly documented.

Data collection

Seven focus groups were conducted with participating EPs and operators. Two researchers (KS and LM) facilitated each focus group. They are both health services researchers, with academic backgrounds in sociology and applied linguistics, respectively, and are experienced in qualitative methods. The focus groups were conducted at the Trondheim HEMS base, the Trondheim EMCC and one external location during a professional development day for HEMS coordinators. The discussions were based on a semistructured interview guide developed by the entire research team (online supplemental file 1). Based on the outlined aims of the study and relevant literature on how changes in workflow may affect tasks, roles, responsibilities and decision-making, the interview guide was developed in a stepwise process and revised in collaboration with stakeholders from the HEMS base and EMCC.22 The interview guide was not pilot tested. Separate focus groups were conducted for HEMS physicians and EMCC operators to explore their distinct roles and perspectives related to the intervention. The focus groups were conducted during daytime hours; some of the physicians and operators participated while on duty, others attended before or after their shifts and a few came in on their scheduled day off. The focus groups took place between 19 September 12024 and 9 October 2024 and lasted 60–90 min.

Participants

All physicians and EMCC operators working in Trondheim EMCC during the 5-month intervention period were considered eligible for attending the focus groups. All seven HEMS physicians who participated as EPs during the intervention agreed to participate. Out of approximately 60 EMCC operators employed at Trondheim EMCC, 22 operators were recruited by the leader of Trondheim EMCC and took part in the focus groups. A strategic sampling approach was employed to recruit EMCC operators who took part in the intervention. To ensure a rich and diverse dataset, we sought variation in gender, professional background, work experience and roles within the EMCC, ensuring both EMS coordinators, shift leaders, resource planners and medical dispatchers were included in the focus groups. Recruitment was also shaped by practical factors including participants’ shift availability. Participation was voluntary, and the invitation emphasised openness and the opportunity to contribute without obligation. The participants were not compensated for their time. The number of participants in each focus group ranged from 3 to 6, and 16 men and 13 women participated. All the seven physicians were men, and they had 11–20 years of experience as HEMS physicians. The EMCC operators included 9 men and 12 women with a range of work experience varying from 11 months to 29 years. Participant characteristics are shown in table 1.

The focus group discussions were audio recorded and manually transcribed by student assistants. The transcriptions were analysed using qualitative thematic analysis, employing a combination of deductive and inductive coding.23 Deductive coding was applied to predefined themes and categories based on the project aims and research questions, while inductive coding was used to identify themes that emerged directly from the data.24 The analysis process consisted of four steps, each of which was initially performed independently by KS and LM, followed by joint discussions and refinement in collaboration with OEU.

Results

The analysis revealed two overarching themes that summarise the participants’ experiences of an on-site EP in the EMCC: (1) perceived impact on the decision-making process in HEMS dispatch and (2) the experience of increased trust through mutual learning and closer relationships. The first theme comprises two subthemes: (1) changes in the basis for making a decision and (2) changes in the actual dispatch decision.

Theme 1: perceived impact on the decision-making process in HEMS dispatch

Two inter-related steps in the decision-making process were identified as particularly relevant for presenting the participants’ experiences of introducing an on-site HEMS physician in the EMCCs. The first step pertains to the informational basis for decision-making—that is, the nature of the information received, the timing and mode of its communication, the context in which it is processed and the conditions under which processing takes place. The second step involves the decision itself, ie, the actual decision to dispatch the HEMS crew. These two steps are presented below as subtheme (i) and subtheme (ii).

Subtheme (i): changes in the basis for making a decision

The participants experienced that the basis for decision-making prior to HEMS dispatch changed with an on-site EP compared with when the physician was located at the HEMS base. First, being on-site at the EMCC allowed the EP immediate access to information from the caller, as they could listen to the emergency call from the beginning and capture all details of the conversation, including both verbal and non-verbal cues. The EPs were also able to observe the actions and emotions of the operators during the call.

You were involved right from the start [of the emergency conversation]. You can really hear a lot in how people speak, the tone of voice tells you if they’re stressed or not. And then with the video link, sometimes we’d get these dramatic messages coming in. (…) So with the video, hearing them talk, and being able to look up their medical records. We had a completely different information base and a new way of forming our decisions (participant A, focus group 1)

In contrast, the physicians noted that when they are located at the HEMS base and therefore not involved in the emergency calls from the outset, they receive information relayed by an EMCC operator, who may already have communicated with others—such as hospital physicians in relevant departments—before reaching the HEMS physician. This may result in loss of both time and important details.

Second, the on-site EP had immediate access to various sources of information, such as medical records, real-time data, live feeds from monitors in the ambulances, maps, resource overviews and video links. The physicians told that although they possess many of the same tools at the HEMS base, these are in reality not as easily accessible. This is due to the physician not being continuously logged into digital platforms and frequently being occupied with other tasks when calls from the EMCC are received.

The basis for decision-making was also altered due to the physician’s understanding of which information was clinically relevant for the EMCC operators during an emergency call. The on-site EP was able to provide live support that usually is not available for the EMCC operators while they are operating alone, through providing additional information as well as suggestions or questions (ie, prompting operators to request further information) directly into the EMCC digital support system in the early stages of an emergency call.

Third, the on-site EP’s knowledge about efficient logistics and time management of prehospital resources impacted the basis for making a decision in the EMCC. Due to the on-site EP’s expertise as prehospital physicians, they asked other types of questions during the decision-making process and thereby obtained different information than the EMCC operators:

I feel that they approached certain situations and asked slightly different questions than we might, because they possess a different set of skills (participant C, focus group 5)

Finally, having an on-site EP influenced the basis for making a decision because the on-site EP provided additional information based on his prior knowledge of the types of details the on-call HEMS physician typically requires.

Then there was the additional information the physician could provide. The physician in the EMCC knew what the physician [at the HEMS base] was looking for. They knew what kind of information he wanted, right? So, for all borderline cases, the EMCC physician would, for example, search through old records (participant A, focus group 4)

In summary, the basis for decision-making was perceived to be improved because a competent and available on-site EP had immediate access to the emergency call conversation and supplementary information.

Subtheme (ii): changes in the actual dispatch decision

Both physicians and EMCC operators experienced that decisions were made more quickly with the on-site EP. The reasons for swifter decisions were assumed to be related to changes in the basis of assessment as described above. Additionally, the EPs’ medical expertise allowed them to swiftly evaluate the seriousness of the situation, which in some cases was assumed to lead to faster decisions.

Participants also experienced that the dispatch decision was made quicker because the pre-decision dialogue was shorter with an on-site EP, as fewer stakeholders were needed in this dialogue since the on-site EP was immediately available for consultation. In contrast, the EMCC operators said that they outside the study setting often involved several persons before deciding to alert the HEMS crew.

I also observed that it became much easier to dispatch them when an EP was present in the EMCC. We often experience that some HEMS physicians wish to consult various parties following a HEMS alarm, which essentially uses up the golden time for a critically ill patient. There’s a lot of back and forth about which helicopter is the fastest, and time passes very quickly. Fifteen minutes can be gone before it’s decided who will take the mission. I found this process to be much smoother (participant C, focus group 7)

When the HEMS crew was dispatched during working hours with the on-site EP, the HEMS physician felt confident that a thorough assessment had already been made by a trusted colleague at the EMCC. Consequently, the HEMS physician did not spend time asking additional questions when tasked on a mission. According to the participating physicians, this also contributed to the impression of shorter predecision dialogues. The EMCC operators also observed that HEMS physicians who had served as on-site EPs developed a greater trust in and understanding of the operators’ assessments.

I talk to them a lot over the radio, it’s really easy to dispatch someone who’s been here [in the EMCC] before, because they just say «yeah, got it, we’ll take it». But someone who hasn’t been here usually asks a lot more questions, and that takes time (participant A, focus group 4)

Despite the experiences of improved basis for decision-making and swifter conclusions in HEMS dispatch considerations, both physicians and EMCC operators expressed uncertainty whether this necessarily led to better dispatch decisions. One physician said that it is difficult to say exactly how their contribution affected HEMS decision-making, except for the experience of speeding it up. Further, there were different opinions among the participants on what ‘better decisions’ actually implied, including quicker decisions, increased precision, improved outcomes for patients, improved conditions for EMCC operators or enhanced emergency services. One example of decisions being perceived as ‘better’ with the on-site EP was the prioritisation of which emergency resources to use in various situations, especially during concurrencies.

When we had multiple tasks to manage simultaneously, we got really good help from the EP to prioritize. He contacted the requesters who wanted the helicopter. It turned out that one task was unnecessary as the patient could just as well travel by car. If it had been the flying HEMS physician who was supposed to be my consultant, he would have been in the helicopter flying; he wouldn't have had the time or capacity to understand the issues and make those calls and discussions. So, I found it very helpful to have an on-site EP to consult with, who figured out what we didn't know and could provide a clear [priority] list (participant C, focus group 3)

Theme 2: increased trust through mutual learning and closer relationship

Being together in the EMCC led to enhanced insight into each other’s competence and skills, which both physicians and EMCC operators recognised as key benefits of the intervention. These experiences were not exclusively related to HEMS dispatch.

For the EMCC operators, a shared experience was that they learnt much from the physicians through debriefings of the cases they had just managed, discussions about previous cases and general medical issues, which most operators found ‘interesting’ and ‘enriching’. The EMCC operators explained that the physicians had engaged outside their primary task—assisting in HEMS decisions—to varying degrees. Some were highly proactive and eager to discuss professional matters, while others remained more passive. Most EMCC operators appreciated the EPs’ engagement and the improved access to medical competence:

I found it extremely educational when we could discuss things, and go through cases, and we had the opportunity to bring up topics. I thought it was very informative (participant A, focus group 5).

Some EPs gave short lectures if time was available. One physician elaborated:

When I sat there, there were certainly things I contributed to, and probably the others did too, and these were professional matters. Things that we know more about than the nurses and EMCC operators, such as identifying risk factors and understanding medical conditions. We discussed some of the cases, without making a direct difference to the actual mission. We also provided some teaching when things were calmer—not when people were busy—but in a more conversational way, like ‘Oh, that’s interesting’, right? That kind of thing. I did that a lot. (Participant A, focus group 2)

The physicians said that they learnt a lot about the EMCC operators’ work practices, capabilities and workload during the intervention period, particularly in terms of their information gathering, assessments and decision-making processes. This included insight into how much uncertainty the EMCC operators face, all the multitasking they need to do and how many clarifications they have done before dispatching HEMS. One of them said:

I was impressed, really. They were much, much more proficient than I had imagined. They stayed incredibly calm under pressure, made excellent assessments, and were great at consulting specialists at the hospital. I found it truly impressive. And on top of that, it was a really pleasant experience—so impressive (participant C, focus group 1)

These new insights into EMCC work practices were described as an ‘eye-opener’ and as ‘highly trust-building’ by two of the physician participants. The insight led to enhanced confidence in the EMCC operators’ work and made the physicians realise why the operators sometimes do not have all the desired information when the HEMS crew is dispatched. Also, the EMCC operators appreciated physicians gaining insights into their daily work routines and competencies. They perceived this as an increased recognition of their job from the EPs—’an acknowledgement that we’re doing a good job’ (participant A, focus group 4).

Both physicians and EMCC operators described that being together on-site in the EMCC positively affected their mutual interaction, enabling quick, reassuring non-verbal signals such as thumbs up, eye contact or nods which were perceived as reassuring signals or validations of decision agreements—as a form of team building. EMCC operators experienced increased confidence in their own decisions when receiving immediate confirmation and support from the EP, affirming that they had made sound decisions.

There was that extra reassurance with an on-site physician, and you could quickly check what he thought about the decision I made. An added sense of security (participant B, focus group 6)

The EMCC operators greatly appreciated getting to know the physicians better, and several of them expressed that the strengthened relation lowered the threshold for contacting and consulting the HEMS physician in decision-making processes also outside intervention hours with an on-site EP.

It’s easier to communicate with them over the radio, and it’s easier to reach out to them afterwards when you know a bit about who they are. They’re not so intimidating after all; they're actually quite friendly (participant C, focus group 5)

Despite the positive experiences of both EMCC operators and physicians related to increased trust and closer acquaintance during the intervention period, about one-third of the EMCC operators retrospectively expressed some level of uncertainty about the benefits of having an on-site physician in the EMCC. These operators emphasised that they make sound decisions regardless of the presence of a physician, due to their well-defined decision-making procedures, their proficiency in collaboratively finding effective solutions and a supportive work environment.

The criteria are quite clear. So, there isn’t really much to be uncertain about (participant A, focus group 6)

The EMCC operators also described that they were accustomed to facing uncertain decision-making situations every day and skilled in managing this uncertainty, implying that an on-site EP would not change their decisions:

I felt that at times I could get a slightly more comprehensive picture [when the physician was on-site], but is it necessary? No, I don’t think the assessments would have been made differently. I’m not sure how much of a difference it would have made [if he was permanently on-site] (participant B, focus group 6)

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the experiences of separating the combined roles of the HEMS and EP by introducing an on-site EP in a Norwegian EMCC. Key findings were that participants experienced an improvement in the basis for decision-making in HEMS dispatch, as well as a perceived improvement in how quickly these decisions were made. However, the participants did not have a clear perception of whether an on-site EP led to better HEMS dispatch decisions. Another key finding was that participants greatly appreciated getting to know each other and each other’s competencies better, which fostered improved communication, mutual understanding and increased trust between various professions.

Internationally, various HEMS dispatch models exist, including dispatch with or without involvement of clinicians in the EMCC.26,29 In this study, we assessed the experiences of transferring the clinical judgements in HEMS dispatch from a HEMS physician remotely located at the HEMS base to a present HEMS physician in the EMCC, as suggested in recent official Norwegian reports.16 17 As such, the dispatch model, including HEMS dispatch criteria, was unchanged during the study period except from the location of the physician. A key finding in this study was the EMCC operators’ experiences of how the basis for decision-making was enhanced with the on-site EP, in terms of a shared understanding of the case based on the EP’s immediate access to all information sources and proximity to the dialogue between the caller and the EMCC operator. In addition to the medical and logistical competence of the HEMS physician, this made thorough assessments of the proper response to the emergency call possible in the early stages of the timeline. As time consumption in HEMS dispatch has been shown to be related to the number of persons involved, the reported experiences of most EMCC operators that HEMS dispatch decisions were made swifter with an on-site EP is noteworthy.30 However, when actually measuring the duration of the HEMS dispatch process in the quantitative assessment of the intervention, both HEMS alarm time (time from emergency call to HEMS alarm) and HEMS activation time (time from HEMS alarm to HEMS take-off) were found to be unchanged with an on-site EP.18

Although the participants experienced that the basis for decision-making in HEMS dispatch improved during the intervention period, and that HEMS decisions in some cases could be made more quickly, there was uncertainty regarding whether this actually led to better HEMS dispatch decisions. The presence of the EP was clearly appreciated by the EMCC operators; however, several stated that the HEMS dispatch criteria are quite clear and that the decision to dispatch the HEMS crew often is quite straightforward. There are probably multiple reasons for this debatable benefit of an on-site EP in HEMS dispatch decisions. First, EMCC operators in Norway are all healthcare professionals, in terms of registered nurses, EMTs or paramedics.12 As such, they have professional clinical competence when assessing emergency calls, in contrast to other EMCC systems with non-clinical dispatchers.31 32 Second, EMCC operators in Norway respond to emergency calls following the Norwegian Index for Emergency Medical Assistance, with a high level of protocol adherence according to previous reports.33 Clinically experienced dispatchers supported by a well-proved index system who are used to working autonomously might not necessarily feel the need for an on-site EP. The fact that most of the EPs stated that they were impressed by the clinical assessments of the EMCC operators during the intervention period supports this. Finally, the on-call HEMS physician at the HEMS base is mostly available for telephone consultation when needed, although not on-site in the EMCC. Interestingly, although both EMCC operators and physicians were blinded for the endpoints of the quantitative assessment and these results were unavailable at the time of the focus groups, HEMS dispatch precision defined by the proportion of HEMS missions without medical or logistical benefit was found to be unchanged following the intervention.18

Logistical considerations are often central in HEMS dispatch considerations, such as identifying the most effective transport for time-critical conditions and deciding optimal landing zones for patient hand-over between ground ambulances and HEMS. Throughout the last decade, HEMS coordinators have been introduced in all four regional EMCCs in Norway to improve control over HEMS flights, avoid unwanted delays in HEMS dispatch and ensure proper flight following.34 To become a HEMS coordinator, selected operators are specially trained on different aspects of HEMS operations, including emergency medicine, rescue techniques and crew resource management principles.35 In HEMS dispatch, the HEMS coordinator should task the most appropriate HEMS resource on request from the EMCC operator. This demands specific knowledge regarding HEMS operations such as capabilities and restrictions of different helicopters in the region, landing zones and flight times to hospitals. As such, the potential advantage of transferring the HEMS physicians’ logistical expertise regarding HEMS operations to the EMCC through an on-site EP may be limited, as this knowledge is already partly represented by the regional HEMS coordinators.19 35

According to the study protocol, EPs were instructed to ‘speak when spoken to’ and should only be mandatorily consulted in HEMS dispatch decisions during the intervention. It might be that an on-site EP could offer greater benefits in other triage categories of emergency calls, for instance, by deciding to treat and leave patients at home in non-urgent cases.36 Following this, it is possible that the participants in our study could have considered the on-site EP even more beneficial with other task instructions or a different physician with other medical competence than a HEMS physician. However, we deliberately allocated a HEMS physician with these task instructions during our intervention in order to address the identified challenges regarding the dual role of the EMCC consultant physician and the HEMS physician on-call.16 17

By being on-site in the EMCC, the HEMS physicians observed the early phase of emergency calls and the EMCC operators’ initial medical advice and response to these calls. Although all physicians were experienced in HEMS operations, this was an unfamiliar setting as they are often contacted by the EMCC operators for HEMS dispatch considerations later in the timeline of emergency calls. As the physicians stated that they were impressed by the way the EMCC operators handled their calls, and that operators’ assessments generally were deemed sufficient and supported by the physicians, a major effect from this study might be the trust-building and improved relationship between HEMS physicians and the EMCC operators. Although they were uncertain whether this familiarity directly influenced HEMS dispatch decisions and if the benefit persisted long-term (ie, 11–14 weeks) after the intervention was ended, some of the EMCC operators even reported this trust-building and positive feedback to be the primary advantage of the intervention. As lack of support and acknowledgement for the challenging and stressful work EMCC operators face has been outlined as a challenge in various studies previously, this is an important finding in our study.37 38

Strengths and limitations

Given the current lack of data to support future decision-making processes regarding the organisation of the EP in Norway, we considered it valuable to complement a recent quantitative study of this intervention with qualitative insights. All participating EPs and a varied and strategic sample of EMCC operators were included in the study, providing broad insights into their experiences. Conducting focus groups allowed for discussions among participants but could also limit participants from saying what they really thought. This could be a possible explanation for the almost complete absence of reported negative experiences from the intervention. The researchers’ non-clinical background may have encouraged participants to provide more detailed responses, resulting in richer descriptions. However, it may also have limited the researchers’ ability to identify relevant topics that could have been further explored.

For feasibility reasons, the focus groups were conducted 11–14 weeks after the intervention. Hence, we did not have the opportunity to capture the immediate reactions of the participants during or right after the intervention period. This represents a limitation and could introduce recall bias in the reported experiences; however, this delay also enabled us to include the participants’ reflections and potential changes in perceived benefits in retrospect. As the intervention was limited to weekday office hours only, experiences with a present EP during night-time and weekends were not captured in this study. Economic and human resource considerations were deemed outside the scope of this study, although such factors are widely recognised as highly relevant in the evaluation of healthcare interventions. As EMCC systems and HEMS dispatch algorithms vary internationally, conducting this study at one single Norwegian EMCC with the associated HEMS base may limit the external validity of the study.

Conclusions

In this focus group study exploring the introduction of an on-site HEMS physician in a Norwegian EMCC, we found that both EMCC operators and physicians experienced an improved basis for decision-making and swifter conclusions in HEMS dispatch considerations. Increased insight into each other’s competence and skills was mutually trust-building and was recognised as a key benefit of the intervention. However, given the educational level and guideline adherence in the Norwegian EMCC system, whether the on-site EP led to better HEMS dispatch decisions remains unclear.
