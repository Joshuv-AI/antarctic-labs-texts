Anatomical characteristics of aortic valve diseases: Implications for transcatheter aortic valve replacement
Authors: Yanren Peng, Xiaorong Shu, Yongqing Lin, Weibin Huang, Shuwan Xu, Jianming Zheng, Ruqiong Nie
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe etiology of aortic stenosis (AS) significantly impacts transcatheter heart valve (THV) implantation, with rheumatic etiology posing challenges. The concept of valve anchoring during transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) for patients with aortic regurgitation (AR) remains unclear.ObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the clinical and CT anatomical characteristics of various aortic valve diseases.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on consecutive patients who underwent CT for severe aortic diseases between April 2019 and February 2023. CT analysis was performed in eight anatomical landmarks: left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT), aortic annulus, sinus of Valsalva (SOV), sinotubular junction (STJ), ascending aorta (AAO), coronary height, aortic angle, and aortic valve calcification volume.Results121 patients with severe aortic valve disease were included, divided into AS (71 cases, 59%) and AR (50 cases, 41%) groups. In patients with AR, the absolute diameters of the annulus, LVOT, SOV, STJ, and AAO, as well as the heights of SOV and STJ and the cardiac angle, are larger than those in patients with AS (all P < 0.05). In normalized aortic root dimensions, the AR group had a higher SOV and STJ diameter-to-annulus ratio than the AS group (STJ-SOV-annulus: 1.51–1.44–1.00 vs 1.33–1.28–1.00). The bicuspid and rheumatic AS groups had smaller sinuses (STJ-SOV-annulus:1.27–1.35–1.00, 1.17–1.30–1.00, respectively), necessitating the downsizing of the THV. For 74% of AR patients, the sinotubular junction could not be used as a second anchoring zone, and anchoring relied primarily on the annulus.ConclusionsPatients with rheumatic etiology require smaller valves, and anchoring in AR patients depends on the valve annulus. These structural characteristics will influence TAVR selection.Keywords: Aortic valve diseases, Transcatheter heart valve, Aortic stenosis, Aortic regurgitation, Computed tomography, Anatomical characteristics

Introduction

Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement (TAVR) has emerged as one of the most rapidly advancing and productive fields in cardiovascular medicine in recent years. The guidelines have expanded the indications for TAVR to include intermediate- and low-risk surgical patients. [1] However, there are still areas within TAVR that require further exploration, such as bicuspid aortic stenosis, rheumatic aortic stenosis, and pure aortic valve regurgitation. [2], [3] In the past, these conditions were not included in large-scale clinical studies due to their unique anatomical characteristics, [4] but ongoing clinical research has yielded satisfactory outcomes. [5], [6] Although they are no longer viewed as absolute contraindications for TAVR, these cases still present distinct challenges and considerations for the procedure, including potential post-procedure complications. Therefore, there is still a need for a comprehensive understanding and comparison of the clinical characteristics and anatomical structures of patients with these conditions.

TAVR lacks the ability to directly visualize the valve and determine the suitable transcatheter heart valve (THV) as in the case of open-heart surgery. Consequently, supplementary methods must be employed to gather information about the aortic root structure to select the appropriate THV and the corresponding transcatheter procedure strategy. Computed tomography (CT) serves as an indispensable tool in this regard, facilitating precise evaluation and quantitative measurement of the aortic root anatomy. Its extensive utilization in clinical practice enables accurate THV selection. [7].

Current literature indicates distinct characteristics among various aortic conditions. Compared to tricuspid aortic stenosis, bicuspid aortic stenosis often features larger annular dimensions, an elliptical orifice geometry, and a higher calcification score. Pure aortic valve regurgitation typically has a larger annulus but often shows minimal to no calcification. In contrast, rheumatic aortic stenosis frequently exhibits pronounced valve thickening, albeit with milder calcification. [8], [9], [10] Although studies have utilized transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography to investigate these diseases, CT data remain scarce, especially for the latter two conditions. [11], [12], [13], [14], [15] Moreover, no standardized image visually presents these structures and their interactions with existing prosthetic valves. [16] Therefore, this study aims to understand better: 1. The aortic root structure in different aortic valve diseases; 2. Materials and methods

Patient cohort and grouping

We conducted a consecutive retrospective analysis of 124 patients who underwent CT scans due to severe aortic valve disease at Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital (Guangdong, China) between April 2019 and February 2023. Patients who underwent surgical aortic valve replacement were excluded from the study. The AS group was defined as having a mean gradient of ≥ 40 mmHg, peak velocity ≥ 4 m/s, or an aortic valve area < 1.0 cm2. Patients who met the AS criteria but also had concomitant AR were included in the AS group, as these patients exhibited varying degrees of calcification. The severity of AR was determined based on the effective regurgitant orifice area, color flow jet area, and pressure half-time. The AR group was defined as aortic regurgitation grade 3 (moderate to severe) and 4 (severe) on echocardiogram, with a mean transvalvular gradient not exceeding 25 mmHg. Within the AS group, further classification was made based on the etiology into bicuspid aortic stenosis (BAS), degenerative calcific aortic stenosis (CAS), and rheumatic aortic stenosis (RAS). The AR group is further categorized according to the phenotypes of the ascending aorta: aortic root aneurysms (sinuses of Valsalva > 45 mm, ARA), tubular ascending aneurysm (sinuses of Valsalva < 40–45 mm, TAA), and isolated aortic regurgitation (all aortic diameters < 40 mm, IAR). [1] Baseline data, echocardiographic information, and CT images were retrieved from the hospital database.

Anatomical measurements

All computed tomography angiography (CTA) images, evaluated at the 30–40% RR interval, were analyzed by two independent researchers (2 and 4 years) using 3mensio software (Pie Medical, The Netherlands). Inconsistencies were resolved by measuring again. The following measurements were obtained: perimeter-derived diameter for annulus (AnPd), area-derived diameter for annulus (AnAd), average diameter of the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT), sinus of Valsalva (SOV), sinotubular junction (STJ), and ascending aorta (AAO). For cases of bicuspid aortic stenosis, the aortic valve annulus was determined by initially identifying the nadirs of the two sinuses and then identifying the plane with the smallest cross-sectional area within the planes defined by these nadirs as the annulus plane. [17] The eccentricity index (EI) was calculated as (1 – minimum diameter/maximum diameter) × 100%, except for the SOV, where EI was calculated as the maximum diameter divided by the minimum diameter. Additionally, the height of the STJ/SOV was automatically measured on the central line between the STJ/SOV and the annulus dimension. The aortic angle was also automatically calculated once the annular plane was determined. Aortic valve calcification volume was measured using a calcification threshold set at 850 HU. [18] A calcification volume less than 200 mm3 indicates no or mild calcification, while a score of 200 mm3 or higher indicates moderate or severe calcification.

Valve sizing

For our analysis, we included three representative THVs: the Evolut PRO+ (Medtronic, USA), JenaValve (München, Germany), and Sapien3 (Edwards Lifesciences, USA). Based on the recommended diameter provided by the manufacturer, we selected the corresponding valve types (Table 1). To investigate the impact of SOV size on THV sizing, we specifically chose the Evolut PRO+ valve for evaluation to determine whether the size (height and diameter) of the SOVe affects valve sizing. Considering the significant difference between the minimum and maximum size of the bicuspid valve sinus, we conducted a secondary evaluation using the minimum sinus diameter. Additionally, we calculated the THV crown-to-STJ diameter ratio in AR patients to assess the STJ's suitability as a secondary anchoring zone.

Drawing schematic diagrams

We employed a normalization method for data analysis to provide a more visually intuitive representation of the anatomy of the aortic root. The specific method is as follows: for each individual, we calculated the diameter-to-AnPd ratio of LVOT, SOV, STJ, and AAO, as well as the height-to-AnPd ratio of the SOV and STJ. Subsequently, we computed the mean, median, variance, and other statistical parameters for the aforementioned parameters. If the data did not exhibit a normal distribution, the median was used as the reference value for subsequent analysis; otherwise, the mean was used. Finally, by multiplying the ratios of the other parameters to AnPd by the mean value of AnPd in different aortic valve diseases, we obtained the corresponding parameters, which were then utilized to draw the corresponding schematic diagrams. [19] To elucidate the relationship between the aortic root and THV, we generated schematic diagrams illustrating the interaction between the aortic roots of different diseases and the corresponding Evolut PRO+ models, aiming to determine the general interaction patterns.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R language (version 4.1.2). Figures were generated using R language (version 4.1.2) or Python (version 3.9.12). The normality of all measured variables was confirmed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using Student’s t-test between two groups when they followed a normal distribution. The median and interquartile range were reported for non-normally distributed variables and compared using the Mann-Whitney U-test between the two groups. Categorical data were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. ANOVA was employed for comparing multiple groups with normal distributions, while the Kruskal-Wallis for non-normal distributions. For further comparisons between, the Tukey method or Holm-Bonferroni method was applied. Correlation analysis was performed using Spearman’s method. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical and anatomical characteristics of the AS and AR groups

The clinical characteristics of the AS and AR group are presented in Supplemental Table 1. Compared to the AS group, the AnPd in the AR group was significantly larger (26.1 ± 2.2 mm vs. 24.2 ± 2.8 mm, P < 0.001), along with the average diameter of SOV, LVOT, and STJ. Additionally, patients in the AR group had a greater height of SOV and STJ. Still, no significant differences existed in the height of the left coronary artery (LCA) and right coronary artery (RCA). Surprisingly, the cardiac angles in the AR group were more extensive (Supplemental Fig. 1). In the AS group, a positive pairwise correlation was found between various diameters and heights of the aortic root (all P < 0.05). In contrast, in the AR group, the SOV diameter positively correlated with other parameters (all P < 0.05). As expected, in both the AS and AR groups, the diameter of annulus and LVOT, as well as the diameter of STJ and AAO, exhibited a strong positive pairwise correlation (all P < 0.05)(Supplemental Table 2, Supplemental Fig. 2).

Clinical and anatomical characteristics of different etiologies of AS

The clinical characteristics of different etiologies of the AS group are presented in Supplemental Table 3. The RAS group exhibited milder calcification compared to the other groups (all P < 0.05), and the overall structure of the aortic root was smaller, as indicated by smaller diameters of the SOV, STJ, and AAO, as well as lower STJ height compared to the other two groups (all P < 0.05). It is noteworthy that the EI of the SOV in the BAS group was significantly greater than that of the other two groups (all P < 0.05)(Supplemental Table 4, Supplemental Figure 3).

Clinical and anatomical characteristics of different phenotypes of AR

Among the AR group, 32 cases (64%) in the IAR group had the highest proportion. In comparison, the TAA group comprised 12 cases (24%), and the ARA group consisted of 6 cases (12%) (Supplemental Table 5). The patients in the IAR group had the smallest valve annulus (25.3 ± 2.0 mm). In contrast, the patients in the ARA group had the largest annulus (28.5 ± 1.5 mm), and the difference between the two was statistically significant (P < 0.001). Significant differences were observed among the three groups in terms of SOV diameter, SOV height, STJ diameter, and STJ height (P < 0.001 for all pairwise comparisons), with the IAR group having the smallest measurements, the TAA group falling in between, and the ARA group having the largest measurements. However, there are no significant differences in coronary height and cardiac angle among the three groups (Supplemental Table 6, Supplemental Figure 4).

The impact of aortic valve disease on valve sizing

Significant differences in valve sizing were observed between AS and AR patients regardless of the type of THV selected (P < 0.001, Table 2). The AS group showed a relatively even distribution among small, medium, and large-size valves, whereas AR patients are predominantly associated with large-sized valves. For both Evolut PRO+ and Sapien3 valves, it is rare (approximately 2%) for patients to fall outside the recommended valve sizing. A small aortic SOV may increase the risk of coronary artery obstruction and sinus isolation; therefore, a sufficiently large SOV is required to accommodate the THV. We utilized Evolut PRO+ to determine whether the recommended THV size based on the AnPd could match the actual SOV size (Table 1). Interestingly, there were no cases of valve mismatch among AR patients due to small sinuses (i.e., 100% fit). However, in the case of AS patients, 10 cases (14%) may require a smaller valve size due to small sinuses. Among them, 32 of 35 (91%) bicuspid valves were deemed appropriate, 24 of 28 (87%) for calcific valves, and 3 of 6 (50%) for rheumatic valves. However, when evaluating whether the sinus size was appropriate for patients in the BAS group using the minor diameter of the SOV, up to 20 cases (57%) were deemed inappropriate. Specifically, corresponding THV sizes were available for 42 cases (84%) in the AR group if at least 15% oversizing was required. However, only 24 cases (48%) had corresponding THV sizes available if at least 20% oversizing was required with Evolut PRO+ . Additionally, since the maximum valve size of Evolut PRO and Sapien3 is 29 mm, only 18 cases (36%) would meet at least 15% oversizing, and only 5 cases (10%) would meet at least 20% oversizing.

Discussion

This study comprehensively evaluates consecutive patients referred for CT scans of severe aortic diseases. The patients were initially divided into two groups: AS and AR. The AS group was further categorized into BAS, CAS, and RAS based on etiology. In contrast, according to the aortic root phenotype, the AR group was divided into IAR, TAA, and ARA groups. The study analyzed the clinical and anatomical characteristics and their impact on THV selection and strategy.

Differences between the AS group and the AR group

It is widely recognized that the aortic annulus in AR patients is larger than in AS, and our research corroborates this finding (26.1 ± 2.2 mm vs 24.2 ± 2.8 mm, P < 0.001). [20], [21], [22] Furthermore, our research utilized commonly used THVs (the Evolut PRO+, JenaValve, Sapien 3) and found that regardless of the type of THVs, the choice of valve size differs between AR and AS (all P < 0.001). In the AR group, over 90% of the patients required a medium or large THV, whereas a more even distribution of sizes is ideally employed in cases of AS patients. It's worth noting that although the JenaValve is indicated for use in patients with AR, the existing size only covers 68% (34 patients). Therefore, a larger size is needed to accommodate the AR population.

Differences in subgroups of AS based on etiology

Consistent with other studies, the BAS group in our study exhibited a younger age, significant dilation of the ascending aorta, and a larger SOV EI. On the other hand, the CAS group is relatively older and has severe leaflet calcification. [23], [24] We observed that, among the three groups, the RAS group had the most minor annulus diameter (21.9 ± 2.0 mm). Interestingly, our findings align with a study by Ben-Dor et al., where transesophageal echocardiography was performed on 11 patients with rheumatic aortic valve stenosis. That study also revealed notably small annulus dimensions, measuring 21.8 ± 2.2 mm. [15] Besides having the smallest annulus, the RAS group exhibited notably reduced dimensions in SOV. These anatomical characteristics suggest that existing THVs may occupy the entire sinus. Remarkably, in our study, half of the patients in the RAS group had sinuses insufficient to accommodate a THV. This suggests that the development of THVs tailored for patients with rheumatic etiologies may necessitate THVs with reduced structural dimensions, particularly above the annulus anchoring area.

Differences in subgroups of AR based on the AAO phenotype

We did not stratify AR patients based on etiology or mechanism due to the high rates (34%−43%) of unknown etiology and low prevalence (30%) of isolated AR mechanisms, such as cusp prolapse, restriction/retraction, or aortic root dilatation. [20], [25] Instead, we divided the patients into three groups (IAR, TAA, ARA) based on the ascending aorta phenotype, as there was a significant correlation between the diameter of SOV and various dimensions of the aortic root in AR patients. This division effectively differentiated the aortic root structure among AR patients. Generally, the IAR group had the most diminutive dimensions of the aortic root, the TAA group exhibited intermediate dimensions, and the ARA group had the largest dimensions. To our knowledge, we are the first to describe the anatomical classification of AR based on this grouping method and provide a detailed description in the current literature.

Different aortic valve diseases have varying implications for the selection of THV

Differences between AR and AS manifest primarily in two respects. Firstly, patients with AR generally exhibit a larger annulus. Secondly, AR is often characterized by a lack of calcification. These factors contribute to a less stable anchoring force for THVs, increasing the likelihood of valve embolism or displacement. Thus, it becomes critically important to select THV sizes that are sufficiently large to ensure adequate oversizing. Fortunately, the SOV's ample dimensions in AR patients render them suitable candidates for the corresponding Evolut PRO+ valves. Through a visual schematic diagram, we found that the CAS group represents the subtype most compatible with existing valve models among AS patients, generally allowing for a favorable interaction between the THV and the aortic root. For the BAS group, a higher rate of sinus eccentricity has been observed; this resulted in 57% of these patients demonstrating a mismatch between the aortic root and the Evolut PRO+ valve, necessitating the use of smaller valve sizes. The RAS group presents a similar challenge, where restricted SOV dimensions led to half of the patients requiring smaller valve sizes. Considering these unique challenges, valve manufacturers could consider judiciously reducing the valve structure's volume without compromising the orifice area. This strategy ensures sufficient annulus oversizing while minimizing the risks associated with coronary obstruction or sinus isolation.

The patients in the AR group exhibit larger aortic sinuses, in absolute values of each parameter and the SOV to the annulus ratio. This results in a higher and larger STJ. Wang et al. demonstrated that the height and diameter of the STJ are related to the success rate of TAVR in AR patients with self-expandable valves. [26] In our cohort, about half of the patients with AR had an STJ smaller than the crown of THV. Furthermore, only a quarter of these AR patients showed more than a 10% oversizing of the THV crown relative to the STJ. This implies that the STJ cannot serve as a secondary anchoring zone for most AR patients. If the THV is insufficiently oversized, the risk of embolism may increase, potentially leading to severe paravalvular leak and necessitating valve-in-valve procedures. [3] Experts recommend using larger oversizing at the annulus, up to 15% or even 20%, for AR patients. [27] Therefore, we compared the proportion of patients who meet these requirements and found that using the Evolut PRO+ valve, 98% of patients can be covered within the valve size range, and 84% can be covered with at least 15% oversizing. On the other hand, if the Evolut PRO or Sapien 3 valve is used, it can only cover 46% of patients within the valve size range and 36% of patients when 15% oversizing is needed. For the JenaValve, which has dual indications for AS and AR, 68% of patients in the AR group may be candidates for this valve. These findings suggest a significant potential market for AR patients.

Conclusion

The AR patients demonstrate increased dimensions of the annulus, LVOT, SOV, and STJ, as well as elevated heights of SOV and STJ. These variations are evident in absolute measurements and the relative ratios of SOV and STJ diameters and heights to the AnPd. Furthermore, In patients with AS, those with rheumatic etiology often require downsizing due to smaller sinuses, while those with BAV commonly require downsizing due to severe sinus asymmetry. Patients with calcific heart disease tend to have better compatibility with THV. In AR patients, the wider STJ and AAO often cannot provide adequate anchoring, making anchoring the annulus and surrounding structures (such as leaflets) particularly important. Current THVs can cover the majority of patients with aortic valve diseases. However, different aortic valve diseases have distinct structural characteristics that require careful analysis to determine the appropriate THV size during TAVR to avoid complications such as valve embolism and coronary artery obstruction.

Table of contents summary

This study investigates the anatomical characteristics of different aortic valve diseases and their implications for transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). The study includes patients with severe aortic stenosis (AS) and severe aortic regurgitation (AR) who underwent computed tomography (CT) scans. AR patients exhibited larger aortic root dimensions compared to AS patients, and the ratios of the sinus to the annulus differed between the groups. The findings suggest that AS patients with rheumatic etiology require smaller valves, and in AR patients, anchoring primarily relies on the annulus. These insights can guide THV selection and TAVR strategies.
