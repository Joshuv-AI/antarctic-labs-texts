Tighter Analysis for Decentralized Stochastic Gradient Method: Impact of Data Homogeneity
Qiang Li, Hoi-To Wai

Abstract
This paper studies the effect of data homogeneity on multi-agent stochastic optimization. We consider the decentralized stochastic gradient (DSGD) algorithm and perform a refined convergence analysis. Our analysis is explicit on the similarity between Hessian matrices of local objective functions which captures the degree of data homogeneity. We illustrate the impact of our analysis through studying the transient time, defined as the minimum number of iterations required for a distributed algorithm to achieve comparable performance as its centralized counterpart. When the local objective functions have similar Hessian, the transient time of DSGD can be as small as 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 2 / 3 / ρ 8 / 3 ) {\cal O}(n^{2/3}/\rho^{8/3}) for smooth (possibly non-convex) objective functions, 𝒪 ⁡ ( n / ρ ) {\cal O}(\sqrt{n}/\rho) for strongly convex objective functions, where n n is the number of agents and ρ \rho is the spectral gap of graph. These findings provide a theoretical justification for the empirical success of DSGD. Our analysis relies on a novel observation with higher-order Taylor approximation for gradient maps that can be of independent interest. Numerical simulations validate our findings.

We assume that (

The study of decentralized optimization algorithms for (
1
) can be traced back to the 1980s
[
20
]
. Popular algorithms are based on gradient descent and gossip communication mechanism where information flows along the edges are specified by the graph. Decentralized stochastic gradient descent (
DSGD
) algorithm was first proposed in
[
21
]
for tackling problem (
1
) using stochastic oracles of the (sub)gradients of
f_{i}
when
f_{i}
is convex. Improvements to the analysis and/or the algorithms have been developed since then. For example,
[
22
]
showed that
DSGD
converges to a stationary point asymptotically when
f
is possibly non-convex. An important class of variants includes the use of gradient tracking: Nedić et al.
[
23
]
combined the inexact gradient method with gradient tracking to develop the DIGing algorithm, Di Lorenzo et al.
[
24
]
developed the NEXT algorithm using gradient tracking for time-varying graph and analyzed its asymptotic behavior in non-convex setting. The recent work
[
25
]
proposed
D^{2}
algorithm to reduce the large variance of stochastic gradients. In
[
26
]
, an Exact Diffusion algorithm was designed under exact and stochastic gradient settings. For decentralized directed graph,
[
27
]
studied stochastic gradient push algorithm which blends
DSGD
and PushSum.

Despite its simple structure,
DSGD
is efficient in tackling practical machine learning problems. Early works such as
[
28
]
found that
DSGD
delivers comparable asymptotic performance as a centralized algorithm while assuming that all local functions
f_{i}(\cdot)
have the same minimum. For the general case,
[
13
,
29
]
showed that
DSGD
achieves a
linear speedup
in convergence rate for (strongly-)convex and non-convex objective functions. Their results show that the iterates of
DSGD
converge to an optimal solution (or stationary point) at the same
asymptotic rate
as an equivalent centralized stochastic gradient (
CSGD
) algorithm utilizing the same number of stochastic samples per iteration. In other words, the extra cost of applying a decentralized algorithm vanishes since the asymptotic rate is
network independent
.

An important extension to the above works is to characterize the
transient time
of decentralized algorithms, i.e., the number of iterations required to achieve the aforementioned rate of
CSGD
that is network independent.
Studying the
transient time
has been the topic of interest recently:
[
11
]
showed that for strongly convex loss, the transient time of
DSGD
is
{\cal O}(n/\rho^{2})
, where
\rho
is the spectral gap for the communication graph between agents; also see the recent work
[
30
]
which improved the transient time to
{\cal O}(n/\rho)
for a restricted class of graph topologies.
Other works have studied more advanced algorithms, e.g.,
[
14
]
showed that the transient time of distributed stochastic gradient tracking (
DSGT
) is
{\cal O}(n/\rho^{3})
which is later on improved to
{\cal O}(n/\sqrt{\rho})
in
[
12
]
using a new spectral condition on the mixing matrix.
As
n\gg 1
,
\rho\ll 1
in large and sparse networks, the above results indicate that the convergence rate of decentralized algorithms may still be heavily influenced by the network topology.
It motivated recent works to develop algorithms focusing on improved transient time, e.g.,
[
19
]
proposed an
MG-DSGD
algorithm which utilizes multiple gossiping steps to obtain an optimal convergence rate for decentralized stochastic optimization and
[
18
]
proposed a related idea,
[
16
,
15
]
considered the
D^{2}
/Exact-diffusion method and provide a tightened analysis through studying the algorithm from a primal-dual optimization perspective, also see
[
17
]
which uses a different proof idea. Table
I
gives a selective summary of the recent results.

Instead of seeking further sophistication for decentralized algorithms, this paper focuses on the plain
DSGD
algorithm and develops tighter convergence theories.
In particular, we study conditions on (
1
) that can be leveraged by
DSGD
to accelerate its convergence.
We are motivated by the empirical successes of
DSGD
seen in various studies
[
31
]

and propose a novel convergence analysis from the perspective of
data homogeneity
.
Note that a common scenario is that workers/agents acquire data in an i.i.d. fashion, as such the local empirical loss
f_{i}(\cdot)
tends to be similar to each other
[
32
]
.
In this light, we discover that when data held by agents are (close to) homogeneous such that the Hessians are close, i.e.,
{\nabla}^{2}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\approx{\nabla}^{2}f_{j}({\bm{\theta}})
, for any
i,j
,
{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the transient time for
DSGD
can be significantly shortened. In such setting, the plain
DSGD
algorithm enjoys comparable performance to sophisticated algorithms such as gradient tracking.
Concretely, our contributions are summarized as:

•

We present a tight analysis for the expected convergence rate of
DSGD
. Our results focus on revealing the effects of data homogeneity on the convergence rates; see Theorems
1
and
2
. Our analysis relies on a novel use of the high order Taylor approximation of the local gradient maps and exploits the structure of
DSGD
update. The use of this technique can be of independent interest.

•

We derive improved bounds on transient time for the plain
DSGD
algorithm, i.e., the minimum iteration number required to achieve network independence rate when considering the case of (near) homogeneous data. In particular, we show that the transient time with smooth (possibly non-convex) objective function is
T_{\sf ncvx}={\cal O}(n^{\frac{2}{3}}/\rho^{\frac{8}{3}})
, and for strongly convex objective function, it is
T_{\sf cvx}={\cal O}(\sqrt{n}/\rho)
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first improved transient time results for
DSGD
over the existing bounds of
T_{\sf ncvx}={\cal O}(n^{2}/\rho^{4})
,
T_{\sf cvx}={\cal O}(n/\rho^{2})
obtained without data homogeneity.

•

We extend the transient time analysis in
DSGD
to study the convergence rate of decentralized
{\sf TD(0)}
learning with linear function approximation
[
3
]
. The latter considers the policy evaluation problem for multi-agent Markov decision process, where the data model falls into the homogeneous data setting. Encouragingly, we show that the algorithm in
[
3
]
enjoys asymptotic network independence and zero transient time, i.e., an asymptotic convergence rate of
{\cal O}(1/t)
, where
t
is the iteration number. This improves over the original analysis in
[
3
]
that shows an
asymptotic
convergence rate of
{\cal O}(\log t/\rho t)
.

Besides, this work presents new proof technique for exploiting a
second order smoothness
property in the analysis of decentralized optimization, which can be of independent interest.
Finally, we present simulation examples to verify our theoretical guarantees.
This paper is significantly extended from its preliminary version in
[
33
]
. We have included the analysis for strongly convex losses, distributed
{\sf TD(0)}
learning, and provided an extensive set of numerical experiments.

We remark that the effects of data homogeneity and the exploitation of high order smoothness property have been studied for several sophisticated algorithms. In detail,
[
32
]
derived improved convergence rates for a preconditioned accelerated gradient method with the help of statistical function similarity;
[
34
]
studied distributed convex-concave saddle-point problem and proved a lower bound complexity that accounts for data similarity as well as a multi-gossip update algorithm to achieve the lower bound;
[
35
,
36
]
studied SONATA/ACC-SONATA that utilized partial majorization-minimization and explored data similarity to boost the convergence rate. Compared to the simple
DSGD
algorithm studied in our paper, these works studied algorithms that require either multi-gossip or gradient tracking that adds a considerable complexity to their implementation. Our work is also the first to take high order smoothness as an analysis tool to obtain
tighter bounds
for decentralized optimization.

\displaystyle\|{\nabla}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})-{\nabla}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\|\leq L\|{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\theta}}\|,

(3)

For the cases of strongly convex objectives, we also consider

A​​ 2
.

The objective function is
\mu

{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime},{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(5)

The communication graph

A​​ 3
.

The matrix

The above assumption is standard. It can be satisfied when

\epsilon>0
is the desired optimality/stationarity gap.

III
Main Results

We first provide an illustrating example that shows data homogeneity can strongly influence the transient time of
DSGD
. Consider the following quadratic loss function:

\displaystyle f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})=\mathbb{E}\big[(1/2){\bm{\theta}}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{i}{\bm{\theta}}+{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{b}}_{i}\big],

(15)

where the expectation is taken w.r.t.
\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{i},\widetilde{\bm{b}}_{i}
such that
\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\bm{b}}_{i}]={\bm{b}}_{i}
and
\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{i}]={\bm{A}}_{i}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix.

Assume homogeneous data such that there exists a common positive definite matrix
{\bm{A}}
shared among the agents with
{\bm{A}}_{i}={\bm{A}}
. Consider the following stochastic gradient map

\displaystyle{\nabla}\ell({\bm{\theta}};Z_{i})=\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{i}{\bm{\theta}}+\widetilde{\bm{b}}_{i},

(16)

where
Z_{i}=(\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{i},\widetilde{\bm{b}}_{i})
are independent random variable. The variances are assumed as bounded with
\mathbb{E}[\|\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{i}-{\bm{A}}\|^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}
,
\mathbb{E}[\|\widetilde{\bm{b}}_{i}-{\bm{b}}_{i}\|^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}
.
This implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|{\nabla}\ell({\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t};Z_{i})-{\nabla}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t})\|^{2}]\lesssim\sigma^{2}(1+\left\|{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}\right\|^{2}),

for any
{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\lesssim
hides the numerical constants that are independent of
\sigma^{2}
.

With (
16
), the
DSGD
algorithm reads:

\displaystyle\textstyle{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t+1}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\bm{W}}_{ij}{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t}-\gamma_{t+1}\big(\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{i}{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t}+\widetilde{\bm{b}}_{i}\big).

(17)

Using the fact that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bm{W}}_{ij}=1
, the averaged iterates are updated as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t+1}&\textstyle=\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t}-{\gamma_{t+1}}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{i}{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t}/n+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{\bm{b}}_{i}/n\big).\end{aligned}

(18)

Importantly, due to
\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{i}]={\bm{A}}
, the last term is an
unbiased estimate
of the gradient of
f({\bm{\theta}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})
with

\textstyle\mathbb{E}[{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{i}{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t}+\widetilde{\bm{b}}_{i}\big)]={\nabla}f(\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t}).

The variance of the gradient estimator is bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{i}{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t}+\widetilde{\bm{b}}_{i})-{\nabla}f(\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t})\Big\|^{2}\Bigg]\!\lesssim\!\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\left[1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\|{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}\|^{2}}{n}\right],

where
\lesssim
omits numerical constant for the upper bound. In comparison, the
CSGD
algorithm applied to
f({\bm{\theta}})
with a batch size
n
admits a variance of
{\cal O}(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}(1+\|\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}\|^{2}))
. We observe that the only difference between (
18
) and the
CSGD
algorithm lies in the extra error term in the
variance
bound due to the consensus error
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t}-\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t}\|^{2}
. We anticipate the transient time for
DSGD
to be much less than (
14
).

We generalize the above example to non-quadratic objective functions by considering the following set of additional conditions.
First, notice that one of the keys to accelerating the transient time lies in the similarity between the
Hessians
of the objective functions. To this end, we impose:

A​​ 5
.

There exists
\varsigma_{H}\geq 0
such that for any
i=1,\ldots,n
,

\displaystyle\|{\nabla}^{2}f({\bm{\theta}})-{\nabla}^{2}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\|\leq\varsigma_{H},~\forall~{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(19)

The constant
\varsigma_{H}
quantifies the similarity between the Hessians of the component function
f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})
. While both A
1
-
(b)
and A
5
hold under the setting of homogeneous data, we note that
\varsigma=0
in A
1
-
(b)
implies
\varsigma_{H}=0
in A
5
, but not vice versa.
Furthermore, as shown in
[
32
]
, for empirical risk minimization (ERM) problems with
m
i.i.d. data samples split across the agents, one has
\varsigma_{H}={\cal O}(1/\sqrt{m})
.

We also require the following technical assumptions:

A​​ 6
.

There exists
L_{H}\geq 0
such that for any
i=1,\ldots,n
,

\displaystyle\|{\nabla}^{2}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})-{\nabla}^{2}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\|\leq L_{H}\|{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\theta}}\|,~\forall~{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(20)

A​​ 7
.

For any
i=1,\ldots,n
and fixed
{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}\in\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f({\bm{\theta}})
. There exists
\bar{\sigma}_{0},\bar{\sigma}_{1}\geq 0
with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z_{i}\sim{\sf B}_{i}}[\|{\nabla}\ell({\bm{\theta}};Z_{i})\!-\!{\nabla}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\|^{4}]\leq\bar{\sigma}_{0}^{4}+\bar{\sigma}_{1}^{4}\left\|{\bm{\theta}}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}\right\|^{4},

and it holds that
\mathbb{E}_{Z_{i}\sim{\sf B}_{i}}[{\nabla}\ell_{i}({\bm{\theta}};Z_{i})]={\nabla}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})
.

For quadratic functions, A
6
is satisfied with
L_{H}=0
; the assumption is further satisfied with common loss functions such as the logistics loss, see
[
44
]
. Meanwhile, A
7
controls the
4
th order moment bound on the variance akin to A
4
. In fact, both A
4
, A
7
are consequences of an almost sure bound on the gradient noise with the growth condition
[
45
]

\sup_{z\in{\rm supp}({\sf B}_{i})}\|{\nabla}\ell({\bm{\theta}};z)\!-\!{\nabla}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\|\leq\tilde{\sigma}_{0}+\tilde{\sigma}_{1}\|{\nabla}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})\|
. The latter holds in the finite-sum optimization setting or in learning problems with bounded data.

IV
Proof of Main Results

To setup the analysis, we observe that the average iterate,
\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t}=(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t}
, satisfies the recursion for any
t\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\textstyle\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t+1}=\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t}-(\gamma_{t+1}/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\nabla}\ell_{i}({\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t};Z_{i}^{t+1}).

(25)

We shall use
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\cdot]
to denote the expectation conditional on the filtration given by the sigma-field
\sigma({\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{0},Z_{i}^{s},s\leq t,\forall i)
.
To facilitate our discussions, we define the quantities

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{t}:=\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t}-\bm{\theta}^{\star},~~\bm{\Theta}_{o}^{t}:=\left(\bm{\theta}_{1}^{t}\cdots\bm{\theta}_{n}^{t}\right)-\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t}\mathbf{1}^{\top},

(26)

From (
25
), it is clear that the
DSGD
recursion differs from that of a
CSGD
algorithm only by the iterate that the stochastic gradient map evaluates on. The latter would update
\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t}
through the direction
(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\nabla}\ell_{i}(\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t};Z_{i}^{t+1})
.

The above observation suggests that the analysis of
DSGD
hinges on how to control the difference
\sum_{i=1}^{n}({\nabla}\ell_{i}({\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t};Z_{i}^{t+1})-{\nabla}\ell_{i}(\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t};Z_{i}^{t+1}))
, which has the expected value
\sum_{i=1}^{n}({\nabla}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t})-{\nabla}f_{i}(\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t}))
. Under A
1
, the latter may be bounded as
L\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t}-\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t}\|
.

We depart from using such a crude bound obtained by A
1
. The key technique in our refined analysis is to study the expected difference vector via Taylor expansion and the second order smoothness property A
6
. Consider the following approximation error for the gradient map
{\nabla}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})
:

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{i}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime};{\bm{\theta}})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}={\nabla}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})-{{\nabla}}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})-{\nabla}^{2}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}})({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\theta}})

(27)

By A
6
, it holds that
[
46
, Lemma 1.2.5]

\displaystyle\|{\cal M}_{i}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime};{\bm{\theta}})\|\leq\frac{L_{H}}{2}\left\|{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\theta}}\right\|^{2},\forall{\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(28)

Importantly, it can be derived that

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}[{\nabla}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t})-{\nabla}f_{i}(\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t})]

(29)

\displaystyle\textstyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left({\cal M}_{i}({\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t};\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t})+[{\nabla}^{2}f_{i}(\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t})-{\nabla}^{2}f(\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t})]({\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t}-\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t})\right),

where we have used the linearity property
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\nabla}^{2}f(\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t}){\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t}={\nabla}^{2}f(\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t})\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t}
. When
\varsigma_{H}=0
, the last term on the RHS of (
29
) vanishes and

\textstyle\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}[{\nabla}f_{i}({\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t})-{\nabla}f_{i}(\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t})]\right\|\leq\frac{L_{H}}{2}\|{\bm{\Theta}}_{o}^{t}\|_{F}^{2}.

(30)

This yields a
quadratic upper bound
that decays faster than the crude bound from A
1
. In the remainder of this section, we develop tighter bounds for the convergence of
DSGD
utilizing the above observation.

V
Application: Decentralized TD(0) Learning

This section studies the policy evaluation problem in multi-agent reinforcement learning (RL) via the decentralized
{\sf TD(0)}
algorithm
[
3
]
as an application of our tightened analysis for decentralized stochastic algorithms.
By recognizing that
[
3
]
for multi-agent Markov Decision Process (MDP) shares many similarities with
DSGD
taking homogeneous data, we show that the existing convergence analysis for decentralized
{\sf TD(0)}
in
[
3
]
can be improved. For the mean squared error in the value function estimation, the convergence rate can be accelerated from
{\cal O}(\log t/(\rho t))
to
{\cal O}(1/t)
with a transient time of
zero
under the same step size rule.

Policy Evaluation.
We briefly review the setup for policy evaluation of multi-agent MDP. Consider a network of
n
agents where agents collaborate under private local rewards and global state-action pairs. We consider the multi-agent MDP described by
(\mathcal{S},\{\mathcal{A}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\mathcal{P}^{a},\{r_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\gamma)
, where
\mathcal{S}
,
|\mathcal{S}|<\infty
is a finite state space and
\mathcal{A}_{i}
,
|\mathcal{A}_{i}|<\infty
is a finite action space for agent
i
. The matrix
{\cal P}^{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\cal S}|\times|{\cal S}|}
is the state transition probabilities under a joint action
{a}\in{\cal A}:={\cal A}_{1}\times\cdots\times{\cal A}_{n}
. The local reward obtained by agent
i
after taking joint action
{a}
at state
s
is
r_{i}(s,a)
, where
r_{i}:{\cal S}\times{\cal A}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is measurable.
The local reward is private and is known for agent
i
, while both system state
s
and joint action
{a}
are observed by all agents. Lastly, a policy
\pi
is defined through the conditional probability
\pi({a}|s)
. The reward function is defined as average of the local rewards: at state
s
, we have
R(s)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(s)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{{A}\sim\pi(\cdot|s)}[r_{i}(s,A)]
.

Policy evaluation is a common problem in RL. The aim is to compute the value function
V_{\pi}:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, defined to be the average discounted reward generated by
\pi
, i.e., let
\gamma\in(0,1)
be the discount factor,

\displaystyle V_{\pi}(s)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\textstyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t+1}{R}(S_{t})|S_{0}=s\right],

(43)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\cdot]
is the expectation taken over the MDP trajectory generated by the policy
\pi
.
Instead of evaluating (
43
), a popular formulation is to adopt linear function approximation. We consider a parametric family of linear functions
\{\phi^{\top}(s){\bm{\theta}}:{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
, where
{\bm{\theta}}
is the parameter and
\phi:{\cal S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a feature map.
Our goal is then to find
{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}_{i}
,
i\in[n]
such that
\phi^{\top}(s)\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}\approx V_{\pi}(s)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
.

The decentralized
{\sf TD(0)}
algorithm in
[
3
]
is a natural extension for the
{\sf TD(0)}
algorithm
[
47
]
. At time
t
, agent
i
observes the tuple
\zeta_{i}^{t+1}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(S_{t},A_{t},S_{t}^{+})
and the local reward
r_{i}(S_{t},A_{t})
. The value function parameter is updated by

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t+1}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\bm{W}}_{ij}{\bm{\theta}}_{j}^{t}+\alpha_{t+1}\,g({\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t+1},\zeta_{i}^{t+1}),

(44)

where
\alpha_{t+1}>0
is the step size, and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &g({\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t+1},\zeta_{i}^{t+1})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\phi(S_{t})\delta({\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t+1},\zeta_{i}^{t+1})\\
&\delta({\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t},\zeta_{i}^{t+1})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=r_{i}(S_{t},A_{t})+(\gamma\phi(S_{t}^{+})-\phi(S_{t}))^{\top}{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{t}.\end{aligned}

(45)

It is known
[
3
, Theorem 2]
that
\mathbb{E}[\|\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}\|^{2}]={\cal O}(\log t/(\rho t))
under standard conditions such as
[\phi(s)]_{s\in{\cal S}}
is full-rank, where
{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}
is the fixed point to the projected Bellman equation.

V-A

Convergence Analysis

We analyze the decentralized
{\sf TD(0)}
algorithm (
44
) under a simplified setting with
independent samples
. We assume

A​​ 8
.

For all
t\geq 0
, the tuple
\zeta^{t+1}=(S_{t},A_{t},S_{t}^{+})
is drawn i.i.d. following the unique stationary distribution,
\mu_{\pi}
, of a Markov chain induced by
\pi
on the multi-agent MDP. In particular, one has
S_{t}\sim\mu_{\pi}
,
A_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot|S_{t})
,
S_{t}^{+}\sim{\cal P}^{A_{t}}(S_{t},\cdot)
.
Moreover, the same tuple
\zeta^{t+1}
is observed by all agents.

The assumption is common for algorithms that use a replay buffer, e.g.,
[
48
]
, in lieu of using a single trajectory of samples. This is called the
global state model
in
[
3
,
49
]
.

To simplify our notations, we define the quantities:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &{\bm{A}}(\zeta^{t+1})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\phi(S_{t})[\phi^{\top}(S_{t})-\gamma\phi^{\top}(S_{t}^{+})],\\
&{\bm{b}}_{i}(\zeta^{t+1})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=r_{i}(S_{t},A_{t})\phi(S_{t}),~~\bar{\bm{b}}(\zeta^{t+1})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bm{b}}_{i}(\zeta^{t+1}),\\
&{\bm{A}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\pi}}[{\bm{A}}(\zeta)],~~\bar{\bm{b}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\pi}}[\bar{\bm{b}}(\zeta)].\end{aligned}

The averaged iterate update is formulated as

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t+1}=\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t}+\alpha_{t+1}\left[\bar{\bm{b}}(\zeta^{t+1})-{\bm{A}}(\zeta^{t+1})\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t}\right].

As seen from the above update, the decentralized
{\sf TD(0)}
admits a structure that is analogous to
DSGD
with
\varsigma_{H}=0
. Lastly, we assume

A​​ 9
.

For any
i\in[n],s\in{\cal S},a\in{\cal A}
, it holds
\left\|\phi(s)\right\|\leq 1,|r_{i}(s,a)|\leq{r}_{\text{max}}
. The matrix
{\bm{A}}
is full rank and Hurwitz.

The assumption is again standard for
{\sf TD(0)}
learning
[
50
,
51
,
52
]
.
As a consequence of A
9
, the matrix
{\bm{A}}
is positive definite and we define
{\lambda}_{\text{min}},{\lambda}_{\text{max}}
as the minimum, maximum eigenvalue of
\frac{{\bm{A}}+{\bm{A}}^{\top}}{2}
, respectively. We also observe that:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\left\|{\bm{A}}(\zeta)\right\|\leq 1+\gamma=:\beta,~\forall~\zeta\in{\cal S}\times{\cal A}\times{\cal S},\\
&\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\pi}}[\left\|\bar{\bm{b}}-\bar{\bm{b}}(\zeta)\right\|^{2}]\leq\frac{2{r}_{\text{max}}^{2}}{n}=:\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}.\end{aligned}

We proceed by observing the descent lemma for (
44
):

Lemma 8
.

Under A
8
, A
9
, if the step size
\alpha_{t}
satisfies
\sup_{t\geq 1}\alpha_{t}\leq{\lambda}_{\text{min}}/\beta^{2}
, then, it holds for any
t\geq 1
that,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}\|\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t+1}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\alpha_{t+1}{\lambda}_{\text{min}}\right)\|\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\alpha_{t+1}^{2}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}+4\beta^{2}\|{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}\|^{2}\right)

The proof can be found in Appendix
D-A
.
Solving the recursion in the above lemma yields:

Theorem 9
.

Under A
8
, A
9
. Suppose that the step size satisfies,

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\alpha_{t+1}}\leq\sqrt{1+({\lambda}_{\text{min}}/2)\alpha_{t+1}},~~\alpha_{t}\leq\frac{{\lambda}_{\text{min}}}{\beta^{2}},~\forall~t\geq 1.

Then, for any
t\geq 1
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{t+1}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\textstyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{t+1}\left(1-{{\lambda}_{\text{min}}}\alpha_{i}\right)\|\overline{\bm{\theta}}^{0}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2}{{\lambda}_{\text{min}}}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}+4\beta^{2}\|{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}\|^{2}\right)\alpha_{t+1}.

Theorem
9
is a direct consequence of Lemma
8
and
13

If we let
\alpha_{t}={\cal O}(1/t)

{\sf TD(0)}

{\cal O}(1/t)
