Quantum-modified photo-thermoelastic wave propagation in semiconductors with temperature-dependent thermal conductivity
Authors: Samar Alshalhoub, Fulin Shang, Gamal M. Ismail, Lotfi Jlali, Ibrahim S. Elshazly, Khaled Lotfy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A generalized quantum-modified photo-thermoelastic model is developed to investigate wave propagation in semiconductor media subjected to ramp-type laser heating while accounting for temperature-dependent thermal conductivity. The model incorporates variable thermal conductivity into the heat equation and introduces quantum carrier transport through a density-gradient correction, enabling a more realistic description of the coupled interactions between elastic deformation, thermal diffusion, and photo-generated carrier dynamics. Using the normal-mode analytical technique, closed-form solutions and dispersion characteristics are derived for a two-dimensional semiconductor medium. The analysis demonstrates that variable thermal conductivity significantly redistributes thermal energy, resulting in smoother temperature gradients and reduced thermoelastic stress localization compared with classical constant-conductivity models. In addition, quantum carrier diffusion further enhances wave attenuation and suppresses carrier accumulation near the surface, leading to more stable thermo-mechanical responses under laser excitation. The combined effects of temperature-dependent thermal conductivity and quantum carrier transport are shown to play a critical role in controlling thermoelastic wave propagation in semiconductor materials. These findings provide new insights into coupled thermal–mechanical–electronic interactions in laser-excited semiconductors and offer a useful theoretical framework for the design of surface acoustic wave devices, optoelectronic sensors, and micro- and nano-scale thermal management technologies.

Introduction

The interaction between thermal, mechanical, and electronic fields plays a central role in the behavior of semiconductor materials subjected to optical excitation, particularly under laser irradiation and high-frequency thermal loading. Photo-thermoelasticity provides a unified theoretical framework for describing the coupled evolution of elastic deformation, heat conduction, and photo-generated charge carriers in such media. These interactions are fundamental to the operation of optoelectronic devices, photodetectors, surface acoustic wave sensors, and micro- and nano-scale semiconductor systems, where localized heating and carrier transport significantly affect performance and reliability. Under intense optical excitation, strong temperature gradients arise near the irradiated surface, making classical assumptions, such as constant thermal conductivity, increasingly inaccurate. As a result, a realistic description of laser–semiconductor interaction requires accounting for variable thermal conductivity, which governs nonlinear heat transport and alters thermoelastic wave propagation.

The theoretical foundations of thermoelasticity were originally established by Biot1, who formulated the coupling between mechanical deformation and heat conduction. To overcome the paradox of infinite thermal wave speed predicted by Fourier’s law, Lord and Shulman2 introduced thermal relaxation time, while Green and Lindsay3 proposed an alternative temperature-rate-dependent formulation. Later, Green and Naghdi4,5 developed models that allow undamped thermal wave propagation. These theories significantly advanced the understanding of thermoelastic wave behavior; however, they generally assume constant material properties. Subsequent generalized formulations incorporated microstructural effects, nonlocal behavior, and alternative heat transport mechanisms to better represent thermal responses at short time scales and high frequencies6,7.

In recent decades, increasing attention has been directed toward extending generalized thermoelastic theories by incorporating additional physical effects such as magnetic fields, rotation, porosity, diffusion, and fractional-order heat conduction8–12. These developments have improved the predictive capability of thermoelastic models under complex loading conditions. However, many of these studies continue to treat thermal conductivity as a constant parameter, despite experimental evidence indicating that thermal conductivity in semiconductors varies strongly with temperature, particularly under laser heating. The inclusion of temperature-dependent thermal conductivity has been shown to significantly influence thermal wave speed, attenuation, and energy redistribution, especially in optically excited materials operating far from equilibrium13–17.

More recent investigations have emphasized the importance of nonlinear heat transport mechanisms, including variable thermal conductivity, fractional heat conduction, and multi-temperature formulations, to accurately describe thermoelastic behavior in advanced materials18–20. These studies demonstrate that variable thermal conductivity leads to smoothing of thermal gradients, redistribution of thermal energy, and modified thermoelastic damping characteristics. Furthermore, the combined influence of variable thermal conductivity with additional physical effects, such as intrinsic rotations, microstructural interactions, and nonlinear bioheat transfer, has broadened the applicability of thermoelastic theories to multifunctional materials and emerging technologies21–23.

Semiconductors constitute a particularly important class of materials in photo-thermoelastic research due to their strong sensitivity to optical excitation and carrier transport phenomena. Classical photo-thermoelastic semiconductor theories were developed to analyze laser-induced thermal diffusion, elastic wave propagation, and photogenerated carrier dynamics24–27. These models successfully explained many macroscopic observations by coupling lattice deformation with classical carrier diffusion and recombination mechanisms. Subsequent extensions incorporated generalized heat conduction models, magnetic fields, rotation, porosity, and diffusion effects to represent realistic semiconductor environments28–32. Nevertheless, most existing photo-thermoelastic semiconductor models still rely on constant thermal conductivity, which limits their accuracy under intense laser excitation, where temperature variations are substantial.

Experimental and theoretical studies on laser–semiconductor interaction have revealed that thermal conductivity decreases with increasing temperature, leading to nonlinear heat transport and altered thermoelastic responses33–37. Neglecting this variation can result in overestimation of thermal gradients, stress localization, and wave attenuation. Recent works incorporating variable thermal conductivity into thermoelastic and photoacoustic models have demonstrated its critical role in modifying temperature fields, stress distributions, and wave propagation characteristics, particularly in micro- and nano-scale semiconductor structures38–42. These findings highlight the necessity of incorporating variable thermal conductivity into photo-thermoelastic semiconductor formulations to achieve physically realistic predictions.

The incorporation of variable thermal conductivity into thermoelastic and photo-thermoelastic theories represents a significant advancement toward realistic modeling of laser–matter interaction in semiconductors. Classical photo-thermoelastic formulations commonly assume constant thermal conductivity, an approximation that becomes inadequate under intense optical excitation, where large temperature gradients arise near irradiated surfaces40. Experimental observations and theoretical analyses have demonstrated that the thermal conductivity of semiconductors is strongly temperature dependent, typically decreasing with increasing temperature due to enhanced phonon–phonon scattering and carrier–phonon interactions41. Motivated by this behavior, several studies extended generalized thermoelastic frameworks by introducing temperature-dependent thermal conductivity into the heat conduction equation, leading to nonlinear heat transport and modified thermal wave characteristics. In the context of photo-thermoelasticity, variable thermal conductivity has been shown to significantly redistribute temperature fields, reduce localized thermal peaks, and alter thermoelastic damping and stress evolution under laser excitation43. More recent developments have combined variable thermal conductivity with advanced heat conduction models, such as fractional-order, multi-temperature, and Moore–Gibson–Thompson formulations, revealing its crucial role in controlling wave attenuation, dispersion, and stability in optically excited semiconductor media. These investigations clearly indicate that neglecting variable thermal conductivity can result in overestimation of thermal gradients and stress localization, particularly in micro- and nano-scale photo-thermoelastic applications, thereby underscoring the necessity of incorporating this effect in modern photo-thermoelastic semiconductor theories.

At reduced length scales and under high optical excitation, carrier transport in semiconductors is further influenced by quantum mechanical effects such as confinement, tunneling, and nonlocal diffusion. Classical drift–diffusion theory becomes inadequate in these regimes, motivating the introduction of quantum-corrected transport models44–47. Among these, density-gradient (Bohm potential) formulations provide an efficient macroscopic approach for incorporating quantum effects into carrier diffusion equations while preserving a continuum framework48–52. Although such models have been widely used in nanoelectronic device simulations, their integration into photo-thermoelastic semiconductor theories, particularly in conjunction with nonlinear heat transport mechanisms such as variable thermal conductivity, remains limited53,54.

Recent studies have further expanded semiconductor photo-thermoelastic modeling toward more realistic laser-heating and nonclassical transport regimes. Abouelregal55 investigated magnetophotothermal interactions in a rotating semiconductor cylinder under time-dependent heat flow, demonstrating the strong influence of thermal loading history on the coupled field responses. Abouelregal et al.56 later analyzed pulsed excitation in semiconductor materials within a fourth-order Moore–Gibson–Thompson framework, showing that higher-order thermal relaxation significantly modifies photothermal wave behavior. More recently, Alhashash and Abouelregal57 examined wave propagation in size-dependent functionally graded semiconductors using a nonlocal modified thermoelastic model, while Alanazi et al.58 studied photothermal dynamics in functionally graded semiconductor media under pulsed laser excitation, highlighting the growing importance of size-dependent and nonclassical effects in modern semiconductor analysis. These recent contributions confirm the need for advanced coupled models that move beyond classical assumptions; however, the simultaneous incorporation of temperature-dependent thermal conductivity and quantum-modified carrier diffusion in a fully coupled photo-thermoelastic semiconductor framework remains largely unexplored. The present work is intended to address this gap.

The present study introduces several important extensions to existing photo-thermoelastic semiconductor models. First, the model incorporates temperature-dependent thermal conductivity, which enables the description of nonlinear heat transport and provides a more realistic representation of thermal behavior under laser-induced heating. Second, quantum carrier transport effects are included through a density-gradient correction in the carrier diffusion equation, allowing quantum-scale carrier redistribution to influence the coupled thermoelastic response. Third, the formulation considers ramp-type laser heating, which more accurately represents gradual thermal excitation in practical laser–semiconductor interactions and avoids the singularities associated with instantaneous thermal loading. Finally, the coupled governing equations are analyzed within a two-dimensional framework using the normal-mode analytical technique, allowing the combined effects of variable thermal conductivity and quantum carrier diffusion on wave propagation, temperature distribution, displacement fields, and stress behavior to be systematically investigated. These features collectively distinguish the present work from earlier photo-thermoelastic studies and provide a more comprehensive theoretical framework for analyzing coupled thermo-mechanical-carrier interactions in advanced semiconductor materials.

The analytical approach adopted in the present work offers several advantages compared with conventional formulations used in thermoelastic and photo-thermoelastic semiconductor studies. First, the normal-mode technique enables the coupled governing equations to be reduced to a tractable eigenvalue problem, allowing clear identification of wave propagation and attenuation mechanisms. This analytical framework provides direct insight into the influence of physical parameters on thermoelastic and carrier-induced wave behavior, which is often difficult to obtain from purely numerical simulations. Second, the application of the Kirchhoff transformation allows the nonlinear heat conduction equation associated with temperature-dependent thermal conductivity to be reformulated in a mathematically manageable form while preserving the essential physics of nonlinear heat transport. Third, the incorporation of quantum carrier transport through the density-gradient model extends classical photo-thermoelastic theories to regimes relevant for micro- and nano-scale semiconductor devices. Together, these features provide a unified analytical framework capable of capturing nonlinear thermal transport, quantum carrier effects, and multiphysics coupling within a single model, offering improved physical interpretation and predictive capability compared with many existing approaches.

In quantum-modified semiconductor systems subjected to optical excitation, the assumption of constant thermal conductivity becomes increasingly inadequate due to the coexistence of strong temperature gradients and nonlocal carrier transport. At elevated temperatures and reduced length scales, thermal conductivity in semiconductors exhibits a pronounced temperature dependence arising from enhanced phonon–phonon scattering, carrier–phonon interactions, and quantum confinement effects. When quantum carrier diffusion is incorporated through density-gradient corrections, the resulting redistribution of carrier density alters local recombination rates and heat generation, thereby intensifying the coupling between thermal and electronic fields. In this context, variable thermal conductivity plays a critical role in regulating nonlinear heat transport by smoothing thermal gradients, redistributing thermal energy over extended regions, and mitigating excessive localization of thermoelastic stresses. The combined action of temperature-dependent thermal conductivity and quantum carrier diffusion introduces complementary nonlocal mechanisms that govern energy dissipation, wave attenuation, and stability in laser-excited semiconductors. Consequently, neglecting variable thermal conductivity in quantum-modified photo-thermoelastic models can lead to significant overestimation of thermal peaks and stress concentrations, particularly in micro- and nano-scale devices, underscoring its essential role in achieving physically realistic predictions for advanced optoelectronic and quantum-electronic applications.

To facilitate the analytical treatment of the heat conduction equation with temperature-dependent thermal conductivity, the Kirchhoff transformation is employed to eliminate the variable coefficient associated with thermal conductivity. This transformation introduces a new temperature-like variable defined as the integral of the thermal conductivity with respect to temperature, thereby recasting the nonlinear diffusion term into a form with an effective constant conductivity. By mapping the original temperature field onto the transformed variable, the spatial divergence of the heat flux becomes linear, allowing the governing heat equation to be expressed with uniform coefficients. As a result, the complexity arising from the temperature dependence of thermal conductivity is absorbed into the transformation itself, while the transformed heat equation retains the essential physics of nonlinear heat transport. This approach preserves the influence of variable thermal conductivity on the thermal response while enabling the use of standard analytical techniques for solving the coupled thermoelastic system. Normal-mode analysis

To examine the propagation, attenuation, and dispersion characteristics of the coupled photo-thermoelastic carrier waves in a semiconductor medium, the governing partial differential equations are treated using the normal-mode technique. This method assumes harmonic dependence of all field quantities along the propagation direction and in time, which converts the coupled system into ordinary differential equations in the depth coordinate. The approach is particularly efficient for surface and guided-wave problems because it yields admissible bounded solutions through exponentially decaying eigenmodes. In the present formulation, the temperature-dependent thermal conductivity affects the thermal field through the transformed heat equation (after applying the Kirchhoff mapping), while the quantum modification enters through a higher-order spatial term in the carrier diffusion equation. Surface boundary conditions

To uniquely determine the unknown constants arising in the bounded normal-mode solutions, physically meaningful boundary conditions are imposed at the free surface of the semiconductor. These conditions are chosen to realistically represent a laser-irradiated semiconductor half-space subjected to ramp-type thermal loading, where the surface experiences a gradual increase in temperature rather than an instantaneous thermal shock. Such ramp heating more accurately reflects experimental laser excitation processes and avoids unphysical thermal singularities at the initial time. The mechanical boundary conditions correspond to a traction-free surface, ensuring vanishing normal and shear stresses, which is appropriate for surface acoustic wave propagation and optoelectronic device applications. In addition, a thermal boundary condition is prescribed to model ramp-type heat flux or temperature input at the surface, explicitly incorporating the time-dependent nature of laser heating and the effect of variable thermal conductivity. Finally, a carrier boundary condition is applied to account for surface recombination of photo-generated carriers, which plays a crucial role in governing carrier redistribution and recombination-induced heating near the surface. Together, these five boundary conditions provide a closed and physically consistent framework for determining the unknown modal constants and for capturing the coupled elastic, thermal, and quantum carrier responses under ramp-type photo-thermal excitation.

Ramp heat-flux boundary

Ramp-type thermal loading provides a realistic representation of heating conditions encountered in many semiconductor and optoelectronic devices. In practical systems such as photodetectors and optical sensors, incident radiation is absorbed within the semiconductor material, producing a gradual increase in carrier density and temperature rather than an instantaneous thermal pulse. This time-dependent heating process is typically governed by the temporal profile of the incident optical power and the thermal response of the device structure. Similarly, in surface acoustic wave (SAW) devices and laser-excited semiconductor components, thermal fields often evolve progressively due to modulated or continuous-wave excitation. Modeling the thermal input as a ramp-type heat flux, therefore, provides a physically meaningful approximation for these operating conditions, enabling the analysis of thermoelastic wave propagation and stress evolution under realistic device heating scenarios. To model laser-induced thermal loading in a physically realistic manner, a ramp-type heat flux boundary condition is imposed at the surface of the semiconductor. Unlike an instantaneous heat pulse, ramp heating represents a gradual increase in thermal energy input, which more accurately reflects experimental laser excitation and avoids unphysical thermal singularities at the initial time. This boundary condition allows the thermal response to evolve smoothly while accounting for the influence of variable thermal conductivity on heat transport. Consequently, the ramp heat-flux formulation provides a reliable framework for analyzing transient photo-thermoelastic behavior under controlled laser heating conditions.

Scope, validation, and limitations of the numerical results

Despite the insights provided by the present model, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the formulation assumes a homogeneous and isotropic semiconductor medium, whereas real semiconductor crystals may exhibit anisotropic mechanical, thermal, and electronic transport properties depending on crystallographic orientation. Second, the analysis considers a single continuous medium and therefore does not explicitly account for multilayer or heterostructure configurations that are commonly used in modern semiconductor devices. In such systems, additional effects such as interfacial thermal resistance, carrier recombination at interfaces, and wave reflection or transmission between layers may significantly influence the thermoelastic response. Furthermore, the present model focuses on the coupled interaction between thermal, elastic, and carrier fields while neglecting other possible physical effects such as electromagnetic coupling or complex device geometries. Extending the present framework to anisotropic materials, multilayer semiconductor structures, and more complex boundary conditions represents a promising direction for future research.

Although the present study is primarily theoretical, the modeling assumptions are supported by numerous experimental investigations of heat transport in nanoscale semiconductor structures. Experimental techniques such as time-domain thermoreflectance (TDTR), Raman thermometry, and scanning thermal microscopy have demonstrated that thermal conductivity in semiconductor thin films and nanostructures exhibits strong temperature and size dependence due to enhanced phonon–phonon and phonon–boundary scattering. For example, Cahill et al.59 reported extensive experimental measurements of nanoscale thermal transport showing significant deviations from constant-conductivity Fourier predictions. Similarly, Minnich et al.60 demonstrated the importance of non-diffusive phonon transport mechanisms in semiconductor materials at micro- and nano-scales. Comprehensive theoretical and experimental discussions of these effects are also provided in the monograph by Chen61. These experimental observations confirm that the effective thermal conductivity in semiconductor devices can vary with temperature and structural dimensions, supporting the incorporation of temperature-dependent thermal conductivity in thermoelastic modeling. Consequently, the present formulation, which combines variable thermal conductivity with quantum-modified carrier diffusion, is consistent with experimentally observed thermal transport behavior in modern semiconductor nanostructures.

Although the present model provides useful insights into coupled thermoelastic–carrier wave propagation in semiconductor media, several limitations should be noted when applying it to more complex material systems. The current formulation assumes a homogeneous and isotropic semiconductor medium with uniform material properties. In practical semiconductor devices, however, materials may exhibit anisotropic mechanical, thermal, and electronic transport properties, particularly in crystalline or engineered semiconductor structures. In such cases, thermal conductivity, elastic constants, and carrier mobility become direction-dependent, which can modify the propagation characteristics of thermoelastic waves. Furthermore, many modern semiconductor devices are composed of multilayer or heterostructure configurations, where interfaces between layers introduce additional phenomena such as interfacial thermal resistance, carrier recombination effects, and wave reflection or transmission at material boundaries. These effects are not explicitly included in the present model. Future work could extend the proposed framework to anisotropic or layered semiconductor systems by incorporating tensorial material properties and interface conditions within the coupled thermoelastic–carrier formulation.

The present model extends earlier photo-thermoelastic semiconductor formulations1–5 by incorporating temperature-dependent thermal conductivity3,4 and quantum-modified carrier diffusion6,7, while recovering the classical results as limiting cases.

Compared with quantum photo-thermoelastic models that assume constant thermal conductivity, the present formulation provides improved predictive capability by accounting for the experimentally observed temperature dependence of thermal conductivity in semiconductor materials. Under laser-induced heating, thermal conductivity typically decreases with increasing temperature, which alters the spatial distribution of heat and significantly influences thermoelastic wave propagation. Constant-conductivity models tend to predict sharper temperature gradients and stronger stress localization near the heated surface, whereas the present model demonstrates smoother thermal profiles and reduced peak stresses due to nonlinear heat redistribution. When coupled with quantum carrier diffusion, this mechanism produces more realistic attenuation behavior and spatial field distributions. Consequently, the inclusion of temperature-dependent thermal conductivity allows the model to better represent thermal transport processes in optically excited semiconductors, particularly in regimes involving strong temperature gradients or micro- and nano-scale heat transport.

A direct quantitative comparison with recent fractional-order thermoelastic models should be interpreted with care because the published studies from 2020–2025 typically consider different physical settings, such as rotating hydro-semiconductors, nonlocal nanorods, or generalized nonlocal nanobeams, with different boundary conditions and field variables. Nevertheless, the present results are quantitatively consistent at the trend level with these recent models: introducing additional nonlocal or memory-driven transport mechanisms leads to lower field amplitudes, smoother thermal distributions, and stronger attenuation than in classical thermoelastic formulations. In particular, recent fractional/nonlocal studies reported that fractional heat conduction and nonlocality reduce thermal and mechanical peak responses and modify wave damping behavior relative to integer-order models, while the current model shows that the combined action of temperature-dependent thermal conductivity and quantum carrier diffusion produces the same stabilizing tendency and further suppresses carrier accumulation and stress localization. Therefore, compared with recent fractional-order nonlocal models, the present formulation yields qualitatively similar attenuation-enhancing behavior, but extends the physics by explicitly coupling nonlinear thermal conductivity with quantum-modified carrier transport in a two-dimensional photo-thermoelastic semiconductor medium.

A further comparison can be made with recent semiconductor thermoelastic studies based on multi-temperature and Moore–Gibson–Thompson (MGT) heat transport formulations. Although an exact numerical superposition is not possible because those studies often consider different geometries, thermal loadings, and constitutive assumptions, the overall trends are consistent. In multi-temperature and MGT-based models, the introduction of additional thermal relaxation or multiple thermal fields generally produces smoother thermal responses, delayed heat propagation, and lower stress peaks than classical formulations. The present results exhibit the same stabilizing tendency in the temperature, displacement, and stress fields; however, the attenuation and smoothing mechanisms here are additionally governed by temperature-dependent thermal conductivity and quantum carrier diffusion. Consequently, the present model extends earlier multi-temperature and MGT formulations by showing that nonlinear conductivity and quantum carrier transport can further suppress stress localization and modify wave attenuation in laser-excited semiconductor media.

Conclusion

A key contribution of the present study is the unified incorporation of temperature-dependent thermal conductivity and quantum-modified carrier diffusion within a coupled photo-thermoelastic semiconductor framework. While many earlier models consider these effects independently, the present formulation demonstrates that their combined action produces distinct modifications in thermoelastic wave propagation under laser excitation. In particular, temperature-dependent thermal conductivity redistributes thermal energy and smooths temperature gradients, whereas quantum carrier diffusion suppresses carrier localization and alters the coupled thermo-mechanical response. The interaction between these mechanisms leads to enhanced attenuation of the coupled fields and reduced stress concentration near the heated surface. This combined treatment therefore provides a more physically realistic representation of heat and carrier transport in semiconductor materials operating at micro- and nano-scales.

The results demonstrate that variable thermal conductivity significantly alters the thermal response by redistributing heat and smoothing temperature gradients, which leads to reduced thermoelastic stress localization compared with constant-conductivity models. The inclusion of photo-generated carrier diffusion further moderates the thermoelastic response by providing an additional mechanism for energy redistribution. Moreover, the quantum density-gradient correction suppresses carrier accumulation and enhances wave attenuation, resulting in smoother spatial variations of temperature, displacement, and stress fields.

In general, the combined influence of temperature-dependent thermal conductivity and quantum carrier transport plays a crucial role in stabilizing thermoelastic wave propagation in laser-excited semiconductors. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating nonlinear thermal transport and quantum diffusion effects in photo-thermoelastic modeling. The proposed framework provides useful insights for understanding laser-induced multiphysics interactions in semiconductors and may contribute to the development of advanced optoelectronic devices, surface acoustic wave technologies, and micro- and nano-scale thermal management systems.

Beyond its theoretical contributions, the present study has potential implications for several technological and industrial applications involving laser–semiconductor interactions. A deeper understanding of the coupled behavior between thermal transport, elastic waves, and carrier diffusion is essential for improving the performance and reliability of optoelectronic devices, photodetectors, and semiconductor sensors, where localized heating and carrier transport strongly influence device stability. The inclusion of temperature-dependent thermal conductivity and quantum carrier effects provides a more realistic description of heat and carrier dynamics at micro- and nano-scales, which are increasingly relevant in modern semiconductor manufacturing and nanoelectronics. In addition, the insights obtained from this model may support the optimization of surface acoustic wave devices, laser-based sensing systems, and advanced thermal management technologies, contributing to more efficient electronic systems and improved energy management in high-performance semiconductor applications.

The findings of the present study also have practical implications for the design and thermal management of optoelectronic and semiconductor devices. In high-power devices such as laser diodes, photodetectors, and microelectronic sensors, localized heating generated by optical excitation or electrical operation can lead to strong thermal gradients and mechanical stresses that degrade device reliability. The present results show that temperature-dependent thermal conductivity can redistribute thermal energy more effectively and reduce stress concentration near heated regions. In addition, the inclusion of quantum carrier diffusion provides a more accurate description of carrier transport in nanoscale semiconductor structures, which influences the coupled thermo-mechanical response. These insights may assist in optimizing device geometries, material selection, and heat-dissipation strategies in advanced semiconductor technologies where precise thermal management is essential for stable operation and long device lifetimes.

Future work

Several directions may be considered for extending the present theoretical framework in future investigations. One possible extension involves applying the model to anisotropic semiconductor materials, where thermal conductivity, elastic constants, and carrier transport properties depend on crystallographic orientation. Such an extension would provide a more accurate description of thermoelastic wave propagation in crystalline semiconductor devices. Another promising direction is the study of multilayer or heterostructure semiconductor systems, where interfacial effects and layer-dependent properties can significantly influence thermal and carrier transport. In addition, experimental validation of the model could be pursued through laser-based thermal characterization techniques such as time-domain thermoreflectance or Raman thermometry, which enable nanoscale measurements of thermal transport in semiconductor materials. Finally, coupling the present photo-thermoelastic framework with nonlinear optical or electromagnetic effects may provide further insight into laser–matter interactions in optoelectronic devices operating under high optical intensities. These extensions would enhance the applicability of the model to a wider range of semiconductor technologies and advanced photonic systems.
