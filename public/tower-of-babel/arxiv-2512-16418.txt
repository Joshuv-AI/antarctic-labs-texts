An Euler scheme for BSDEs via the Wiener chaos decomposition
Pere Díaz Lozano, Giulia Di Nunno

Abstract
The Euler scheme is a standard time discretization for BSDEs, but its implementation hinges on approximating conditional expectations and the associated martingale terms at each time step. We propose an implementation based on the Wiener chaos decomposition to approximate these quantities. In contrast to many numerical schemes that rely on a forward–backward (Markovian) structure, our approach accommodates arbitrary ℱ T \mathcal{F}_{T} -measurable square-integrable terminal conditions. We provide a comprehensive convergence analysis and illustrate the method on several numerical examples.



Keywords:
Backward stochastic differential equations, numerical methods for BSDEs, Wiener chaos decomposition, Malliavin calculus

1  Introduction

We are interested in the numerical approximation of the solution of a backward stochastic differential equation (BSDE for short) of the following form:

\displaystyle Y_{t}=\xi+\int_{t}^{T}f(s,Y_{s},Z_{s})ds-\int_{t}^{T}Z_{s}\cdot dB_{s},\quad t\in[0,T],

(1.1)

where
B
is a
d
-dimensional standard Brownian motion,
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is its natural and augmented filtration,
\xi
is an
\mathbb{R}
-valued
\mathcal{F}_{T}
-measurable random variable, and the generator
f\colon[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a measurable
\mathbb{F}
-adapted random field. A solution to this equation is essentially defined to be a pair of adapted processes
(Y,Z)=(Y_{t},Z_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
satisfying equation (
1.1
). BSDEs were introduced by
[
3
]
in the case of a linear generator, and were later generalized by
[
23
]
to the nonlinear case. Several applications of BSDEs in finance can be found for example in
[
12
]
, where they are used in pricing and hedging of financial products. We also mention the connection between BSDEs and dynamic risk measures, see
[
24
]
.

There is already extensive research on the numerical approximation of (
1.1
), with most methods relying on an Euler-type discretization scheme on a time grid
\pi\coloneqq\{t_{i}\}_{0\leq i\leq m}\subset[0,T]
, leading to the recursion
Y_{t_{m}}=\xi
, and for
i=m,\dots,1
,
t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i})
,

\displaystyle F_{i}^{\pi}=Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi}+\Delta_{i}f\big(t_{i},Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi},\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi}\big),\quad Y_{t}^{\pi}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t}(F_{i}^{\pi}),\quad\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi}=\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{\Delta_{i+1}}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}Z_{s}^{\pi}ds\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}}\Big)

(1.2)

where
\Delta_{i}=t_{i}-t_{i-1}
and
(Z_{s}^{\pi})_{s\in[t_{i},t_{i+1})}
is given by the martingale representation theorem applied to
F_{i}^{\pi}
. We also use the convention
\langle Z\rangle_{m}^{\pi}=0
. This is generally called the backward Euler scheme for BSDEs. The main references in this topic are
[
5
]
and
[
25
]
. However, the basic ideas already appear in
[
8
]
for generators independent of
Z
, and in
[
1
]
for a random time mesh.

In
[
5
]
and
[
25
]
, the convergence of the backward Euler scheme was proved to be of order
1/2
, under the assumption that the terminal condition is a Lipschitz functional of the forward SDE. In
[
17
]
, the same order was obtained also for general terminal conditions satisfying suitable Malliavin regularity assumptions.

The critical challenge in implementing the scheme is the computation of conditional expectations and martingale terms at each time step. Some approaches include least-squares regression methods (
[
5
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
), Malliavin weights (
[
4
]
,
[
14
]
) and cubature methods (
[
9
]
). More recently, there has been a surge in the use of deep learning based models to estimate these conditional expectations, which outperform more classical approaches in high-dimensional settings, see
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
18
]
and
[
11
]
. These can also be interpreted as nonlinear least-squares Monte Carlo. For a comprehensive survey on the topic of numerical methods for BSDEs, see
[
7
]
.

Instead, in this work we propose a novel approximation scheme based on the Wiener chaos decomposition of the random variables
(F_{i}^{\pi})_{1\leq i\leq m}
, namely

\displaystyle F_{i}^{\pi}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{|a|=k}d_{a}\prod_{j\geq 1}H_{a_{j}}\Big(\int_{0}^{t_{i}}h_{j}^{i}(s)\cdot dB_{s}\Big),

where
H_{k}
denotes the Hermite polynomial of degree
k
,
(h_{j}^{i})_{j\geq 1}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}([0,t_{i}];\allowbreak\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
|a|=\sum_{j\geq 1}a_{j}
for any sequence of non-negative integers
a=(a_{j})_{j\geq 1}
and
(d_{a})_{a}
is a set of coefficients ensuing from the decomposition of
F_{i}^{\pi}
.

Moreover, if we consider a finite-dimensional truncation of this decomposition,

\displaystyle F_{i}^{\pi}\approx\sum_{k=0}^{P}\sum_{|a|=k}d_{a}\prod_{j=1}^{M}H_{a_{j}}\Big(\int_{0}^{t_{i}}h_{j}^{i}(s)\cdot dB_{s}\Big),\quad a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{M}),

we then get a fully implementable numerical scheme. The reason to use such a representation is that, for smartly chosen functions
(h_{j}^{i})_{1\leq j\leq M}
, we can obtain closed-form formulas for both the conditional expectations and the martingale terms appearing in (
1.2
).

We remark that the method can handle general square-integrable terminal conditions without additional effort. This stands in contrast to most methods in the literature (such as those listed above), where one typically assumes that the terminal condition depends only on the terminal value of a forward SDE–so that the problem becomes a Markovian FBSDE–or on a discrete functional of the forward path. In the latter case, the standard approach is to recover a Markovian formulation by augmenting the forward state, which often leads to a high-dimensional system.

To the best of our knowledge, one of the few schemes that does not require a Markovian forward–backward (FBSDE) representation is
[
6
]
, which can be viewed as a Picard-type analog of our approach. There, the solution of (
1.1
) is approximated through a Picard-type iteration, and the conditional expectations and martingale terms are computed via truncated Wiener chaos decompositions, in a spirit close to ours. A key difference is that our convergence analysis treats all parameters of the algorithm, whereas
[
6
]
assumes that time integrals can be computed exactly; moreover, our results and proofs can be stated in a more concise form and require fewer auxiliary definitions. From a practical standpoint, our scheme gives direct control of the solution at each time step, while in their setting, new simulations require rerunning the full Picard iteration.

After a brief review of background concepts in Section
2
, we present the full implementation of the numerical scheme in Section
3
. Its convergence analysis appears in Section
4
, where we quantify the errors introduced by each approximation tool. Section
5
compares our method with existing approaches. We conclude with numerical examples in Section
6
that assess the method’s performance. Some technical proofs are postponed to the Appendix.

•

\mathbb{H}_{\tau}=L^{2}([0,\tau];\mathbb{R}^{d})

;

•

The Malliavin derivative of a random variable

d

\displaystyle D_{s}^{j}F=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_{i}^{j}}\big(\mathbf{B}(h_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{B}(h_{n})\big)h_{i}^{j}(s),\quad s\in[0,\tau]\quad j=1,\dots,d.

Notice that

can be regarded as a random variable taking values in
\mathbb{H}_{\tau}
;

•

More generally, for

, we define the

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})\in\{1,\dots,d\}^{k}

as the real-valued random variable

\displaystyle D_{\mathbf{s}}^{\alpha}F\coloneqq\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{k}\phi}{\partial x_{i_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{i_{k}}^{\alpha_{k}}}\big(\mathbf{B}(h_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{B}(h_{n})\big)h_{i_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}(s_{1})\cdots h_{i_{k}}^{\alpha_{k}}(s_{k}).

The
k
-th Malliavin derivative of
F

is then defined as

can be viewed as an
\mathbb{H}_{\tau}^{\otimes k}
-valued r.v., which by construction is symmetric in the

variables;

•

\mathbb{D}_{\tau}^{k,p}
,

, denotes the closure of
\mathcal{S}_{\tau}
with respect to the norm given by

\displaystyle\lVert F\rVert_{\mathbb{D}_{\tau}^{k,p}}^{p}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}(|F|^{p})+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\big(\lVert D^{j}F\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{\tau}^{\otimes j}}^{p}\big),

where

\displaystyle\lVert D^{j}F\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{\tau}^{\otimes j}}^{p}=\Bigg(\sum_{\alpha\in\{1,\dots,d\}^{j}}\int_{[0,\tau]^{j}}\lvert D_{\mathbf{s}}^{\alpha}F\rvert^{2}d\mathbf{s}\Bigg)^{p/2};

2.2  BSDEs and their numerical approximation

The precise meaning of a solution to a BSDE is clarified in the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
f\colon[0,T]\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{F})\otimes\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
-measurable, and let
\xi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be an
\mathcal{F}_{T}
-measurable random variable. We say that a pair of stochastic processes
(Y,Z)
is a solution of the BSDE with data
(\xi,f)
if the following conditions are met:

(i)

equation (
1.1
) is satisfied
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.;

(ii)

Y
is continuous
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.;

(iii)

(Y,Z)\in H_{T}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\times H_{T}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The most standard set of assumptions on the generator and the terminal condition under which (
1.1
) has a unique solution is the following.

Assumption 2.2
.

(i)

f
is
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{F})\otimes\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
-measurable and uniformly Lipschitz in
(y,z)
, i.e. there exists a constant
[f]_{L}>0
such that
dt\otimes d\mathbb{P}
-a.e.

\displaystyle|f(t,y_{1},z_{1})-f(t,y_{2},z_{2})|\leq[f]_{L}(|y_{1}-y_{2}|+|z_{1}-z_{2}|)\quad\forall(y_{1},z_{1}),(y_{2},z_{2});

(ii)

\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\int_{0}^{T}|f(t,0,0)|dt\big)^{2}\Big]<\infty
;

(iii)

\xi\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{T})
.

Let us now state the well-known result on the well-posedness of (
1.1
). The proof can be found in
[
26
, Theorem 4.3.1]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Under Assumption
2.2
, the BSDE (
1.1
) has a unique solution
(Y,Z)
in
H_{T}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\times H_{T}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which actually belongs to
S_{T}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\times H_{T}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

2.3  A numerical scheme for BSDEs

We recall the backward Euler-type numerical scheme for BSDEs proposed in
[
25
]
. Let
\pi:=\{0=t_{0}<\cdots<t_{m}=T\}
denote a partition of the interval
[0,T]
, and let
\Delta_{i}\coloneqq t_{i}-t_{i-1}
for
i=1,\dots,m
. We define the following backward recursion, starting from the terminal condition
Y_{t_{m}}^{\pi}\coloneqq\xi
, and for
i=m,\dots,1
,

\displaystyle Y_{t}^{\pi}

\displaystyle=Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi}+\Delta_{i}f\big(t_{i},Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi},\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi}\big)-\int_{t}^{t_{i}}Z_{s}^{\pi}\cdot dB_{s}\quad\text{for $t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i})$},

where

\displaystyle\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}\Big(\frac{1}{\Delta_{i+1}}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}Z_{s}^{\pi}ds\Big),

with the convention
\langle Z\rangle_{m}^{\pi}=0
. For each
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, let us define

\displaystyle F_{i}^{\pi}\coloneqq Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi}+\Delta_{i}f\big(t_{i},Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi},\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi}\big).

(2.1)

It then follows that, for
t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i})
, we have
Y_{t}^{\pi}=\mathbb{E}_{t}(F_{i}^{\pi})
and

\displaystyle\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi}=\frac{1}{\Delta_{i+1}}\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[F_{i+1}^{\pi}(B_{t_{i+1}}-B_{t_{i}})],

while
(Z_{s}^{\pi})_{s\in[t_{i-1},t_{i})}
is obtained by applying the martingale representation theorem to the square-integrable random variable
F_{i}^{\pi}
, since

\displaystyle F_{i}^{\pi}=\mathbb{E}_{t_{i-1}}\big(F_{i}^{\pi}\big)+\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}Z_{s}^{\pi}\cdot dB_{s}.

(2.2)

Moreover, by the Clark–Ocone formula we have that, if
F_{i}^{\pi}\in\mathbb{D}_{t_{i}}^{1,2}
, then
(Z_{s}^{\pi})_{s\in[t_{i-1},t_{i})}
can be expressed as the measurable projection of the Malliavin derivative of
F_{i}^{\pi}
, i.e.
Z_{t}^{\pi}=D_{t}\mathbb{E}_{t}\big(F_{i}^{\pi}\big)=D_{t}Y_{t}^{\pi}
,
t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i})
.

Remark 2.4
.

We highlight the fact that this method cannot be implemented in a straightforward way, since it requires the computation of conditional expectations and martingale terms. As we have already noted in the introduction, our goal is to propose an implementation based on the Wiener chaos decomposition of the random variables
F_{i}^{\pi}
.

2.3.1  Convergence rate of the scheme

We state the convergence rate of the scheme introduced above, following
[
17
]
. For readability, we restrict to the case of a deterministic generator
f
; analogous results for random generators can be obtained under additional conditions. The theorem is proved under the following assumption.

Assumption 2.5
.

Fix
q>4
.

(i)

\xi\in\mathbb{D}_{T}^{2,q}
, and there exists
\mu>0
such that for all
s,r\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(|D_{s}\xi-

\displaystyle D_{r}\xi|^{2}\big)\leq\mu|s-r|,\qquad\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}\mathbb{E}\big(|D_{s}\xi|^{q}\big)<\infty,

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}\sup_{0\leq r\leq T}\mathbb{E}\big(|D_{s}D_{r}\xi|^{q}\big)<\infty;

(ii)

f\in C_{b,(y,z)}^{1:2}
and
\int_{0}^{T}\lvert f(t,0,0)\rvert^{2}dt<\infty
;

(iii)

there exists
[f]_{H}>0
such that

\displaystyle\lvert f(s,y,z)-f(r,y,z)\rvert\leq[f]_{H}\lvert s-r\rvert^{1/2}\quad\text{for all $s,r\in[0,T]$ and $(y,z)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let Assumption
2.5
be satisfied. Let
\pi:=\{0=t_{0}<\cdots<t_{m}=T\}
be such that
\max_{1\leq i\leq m-1}\Delta_{i}/\Delta_{i+1}\leq L
, where
L
is a constant. Then there exist constants
C,\eta>0
, independent of the partition
\pi
, such that

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq i\leq m}\mathbb{E}\Big(\lvert Y_{t_{i}}-Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi}\rvert^{2}\Big)+\mathbb{E}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\lvert Z_{t}-Z_{t}^{\pi}\rvert^{2}dt\Big)\leq C\lvert\pi\rvert

whenever
|\pi|<\eta
.

Remark 2.7
.

The constants
C
and
\eta
appearing in Theorem
2.6
can depend on
\mu
,
L
,
[f]_{L}
,
[f]_{H}
,
\lVert\partial_{(y,z)}^{2}f\rVert_{\infty}
,
T
and
d
.

Remark 2.8
.

A stronger result is actually proved in
[
17
]
, which states that there exist constants
C,\eta>0
, independent of the partition
\pi
, such that, for
\lvert\pi\rvert<\eta
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\lvert Y_{t}-Y_{t}^{\pi}\rvert^{2}\Big)+\mathbb{E}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\lvert Z_{t}-Z_{t}^{\pi}\rvert^{2}dt\Big)\leq C\lvert\pi\rvert.

2.4  The Wiener chaos decomposition

We now present an elementary introduction to the Wiener chaos decomposition of
L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{\tau})
for a fixed
\tau\in[0,T]
. There are two equivalent approaches: using Hermite polynomials and using iterated integrals. We refer to
[
22
, Section 1.1]
or
[
10
, Chapter 1]
for more details on this topic.

2.4.1  Wiener chaos decomposition and Hermite polynomials

We start with the Wiener chaos decomposition given by Hermite polynomials, which is the one we will be using in the implementation. Let
H_{n}(x)
denote the
n
th Hermite polynomial, defined by

\displaystyle H_{n}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}e^{\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}\big(e^{\frac{-x^{2}}{2}}\big),\quad n\geq 1,

and
H_{0}(x)=1
. These are the coefficients of the decomposition in powers of
t
of the function
F(x,t)=\exp\big(tx-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\big)
. In fact, we have

\displaystyle F(x,t)=\exp\Big(\frac{x^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}(x-t)^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\Big(\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}e^{-\frac{(x-t)^{2}}{2}}\Big)\big|_{t=0}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}H_{n}(x).

Furthermore, since
\partial_{x}F(x,t)=t\exp\Big(\frac{x^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}(x-t)^{2}\Big)
, one has that

\displaystyle H_{n}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=H_{n-1}(x),\quad n\geq 0,

with the convention
H_{-1}(x)\equiv 0
.

Consider the set of all sequences
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots)
,
a_{j}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, such that all the terms, except a finite number of them, vanish. Set
a!=\prod_{j\geq 1}a_{j}!
and
|a|=\sum_{j\geq 1}a_{j}
, and define

\displaystyle\Phi_{a}=\sqrt{a!}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}H_{a_{j}}\Big(\int_{0}^{\tau}h_{j}(s)\cdot dB_{s}\Big),

where
(h_{j})_{j\geq 1}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{H}_{\tau}
. Notice that the above product is well defined because
H_{0}(x)=1
and
a_{j}\neq 0
only for a finite number of indices.

For each
n\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{H}_{\tau}^{n}
denote the closed linear subspace of
L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{\tau})
generated by the random variables
\{\Phi_{a},|a|=n\}
. This space is called the Wiener chaos of order
n
.

We then have the following orthogonal decomposition, see Theorem 1.1.1 and Proposition 1.1.1 in
[
22
]
, respectively.

Theorem 2.9
.

1.

The space
L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{\tau})
can be decomposed into the infinite orthogonal sum of the subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{\tau}^{n}
;

2.

The collection of random variables
\{\Phi_{a},|a|=n\}
form a complete orthonormal system in
\mathcal{H}_{\tau}^{n}
.

We therefore deduce the following result, called the Wiener chaos decomposition of
L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{\tau})
.

Theorem 2.10
.

For any
F\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{\tau})
, we have the decomposition

\displaystyle F=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{|a|=n}\langle\Phi_{a},F\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{\tau})}\Phi_{a},

(2.3)

where the sum converges in
L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{\tau})
.

For convenience, sometimes we will also write

\displaystyle F=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{|a|=n}d_{a}\prod_{j\geq 1}H_{a_{j}}\Big(\int_{0}^{\tau}h_{j}(s)\cdot dB_{s}\Big),

where

\displaystyle d_{a}\coloneqq a!\;\mathbb{E}\Big[F\times\prod_{j\geq 1}H_{a_{j}}\Big(\int_{0}^{\tau}h_{j}(s)\cdot dB_{s}\Big)\Big].

2.4.2  Wiener chaos decomposition and iterated integrals

The Wiener chaos decomposition can also be expressed through iterated integrals. As this is used exclusively for the convergence analysis, readers interested only in the implementation can proceed to the next section.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}\coloneqq([0,\tau]\times\{1,\dots,d\})^{n}
, and define the
d
-simplex
\mathcal{S}_{n}\subset\mathcal{T}_{n}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n}\coloneqq\big\{\big((s_{1},\alpha_{1}),\dots,(s_{n},\alpha_{n})\big)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\ \colon\ 0\leq s_{1}\leq\cdots\leq s_{n}\leq\tau\big\}.

Let
L^{2}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
be the space of square-integrable functions defined on
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, equipped with the inner product

\displaystyle\langle g,f\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{S}_{n})}\coloneqq\sum_{\alpha\in\{1,\dots,d\}^{n}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{0}^{s_{n}}\cdots\int_{0}^{s_{2}}(g\times f)\big((s_{1},\alpha_{1}),\dots,(s_{n},\alpha_{n})\big)ds_{1}\cdots ds_{n}.

For
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
, define the
n
-fold iterated Itô integral as

\displaystyle J_{n}(f)\coloneqq\sum_{\alpha\in\{1,\dots,d\}^{n}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{0}^{s_{n}}\cdots\int_{0}^{s_{2}}f\big((s_{1},\alpha_{1}),\dots,(s_{n},\alpha_{n})\big)dB_{s_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots dB_{s_{n}}^{\alpha_{n}}.

Now consider
\widetilde{L}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
the space of square-integrable symmetric functions defined on
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and for each
f\in\widetilde{L}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
, define

\displaystyle I_{n}(f)=\sum_{\alpha\in\{1,\dots,d\}^{n}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\cdots\int_{0}^{\tau}f\big((s_{1},\alpha_{1}),\dots,(s_{n},\alpha_{n})\big)dB_{s_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots dB_{s_{n}}^{\alpha_{n}}\coloneqq n!J_{n}(f).

Notice that
\langle f,g\rangle_{\widetilde{L}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{n})}=n!\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{S}_{n})}
. One can check (see page 9 in
[
22
]
) that for any
f\in\widetilde{L}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{m})
and
g\in\widetilde{L}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(I_{m}(f)I_{n}(g)\big)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $m\neq n$,}\\
m!\langle f,g\rangle_{\widetilde{L}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{m})},&\text{if $m=n$.}\end{cases}

(2.4)

The following result shows the relationship between Hermite polynomials and iterated integrals; see Propositions 1.1.3 and 1.1.4 in
[
22
]
.

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
h\in\mathbb{H}_{\tau}
have norm one. Then, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle n!H_{n}\Big(\int_{0}^{\tau}h(t)\cdot dB_{t}\Big)=I_{n}(h^{\otimes n}),

where
h^{\otimes n}\colon\mathcal{T}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the symmetric function defined by

\displaystyle h^{\otimes n}\big((s_{1},\alpha_{1}),\dots,(s_{n},\alpha_{n})\big)=h^{\alpha_{1}}(s_{1})\cdots h^{\alpha_{n}}(s_{n}),

with
h^{\alpha_{k}}
denoting the
\alpha_{k}
-th coordinate of
h
. Moreover, for any
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{M})
, one has

\displaystyle a!\prod_{j=1}^{M}H_{a_{j}}\Big(\int_{0}^{\tau}h_{j}(s)\cdot dB_{s}\Big)=I_{n}\big(h_{1}^{\otimes a_{1}}\widetilde{\otimes}\cdots\widetilde{\otimes}h_{M}^{\otimes a_{M}}\big),

(2.5)

where
|a|=n
and
\widetilde{\otimes}
denotes the symmetrization of the tensor product. In particular,
I_{n}
maps
\widetilde{L}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
onto the
n
th Wiener chaos
\mathcal{H}_{\tau}^{n}
.

As a consequence we have the following version of the Wiener chaos decomposition.

Theorem 2.12
.

For any
F\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{\tau})
there exists a unique sequence
(g_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, with
g_{n}\in\widetilde{L}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
,
n\geq 1
, such that

\displaystyle F=\mathbb{E}(F)+\sum_{n\geq 1}I_{n}(g_{n}).

(2.6)

Proposition 2.13

(Stroock’s formula)
.

Let
F\in\mathbb{D}_{\tau}^{k,2}
be a random variable with representation (
2.6
). Then, for all
n\leq k
and for all
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in\{1,\dots,d\}^{n}
,

\displaystyle g_{n}\big((s_{1},\alpha_{1}),\dots,(s_{n},\alpha_{n})\big)=\frac{1}{n!}\mathbb{E}\big(D_{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}}^{\alpha}F\big).

Definition 2.14
.

Let
F\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{\tau})
be a random variable with representations (
2.3
) and (
2.6
). Let
n,P,M\in\mathbb{N}
. We define:

•

\mathcal{P}_{\tau}^{n}
the projection onto the
n
th Wiener chaos
\mathcal{H}_{\tau}^{n}
. That is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\tau}^{n}(F)\coloneqq\sum_{|a|=n}d_{a}\prod_{j\geq 1}H_{a_{j}}\Big(\int_{0}^{\tau}h_{j}(s)\cdot dB_{s}\Big)=I_{n}(g_{n}).

•

\mathcal{P}_{\tau}^{n,M}
the
n
th Wiener chaos when truncating the basis
(h_{j})_{j\geq 1}
to
M
elements. That is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\tau}^{n,M}(F)\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a|=n\\
\#a=M\end{subarray}}d_{a}\prod_{j=1}^{M}H_{a_{j}}\Big(\int_{0}^{\tau}h_{j}(s)\cdot dB_{s}\Big).

•

\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{P}
the chaos decomposition of
F
up to order
P
. That is,
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{P}(F)\coloneqq\sum_{n=0}^{P}\mathcal{P}_{\tau}^{n}(F)
;

•

\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{P,M}
the chaos decomposition of
F
up to order
P
when truncating the basis
(h_{j})_{j}
to
M
elements. That is,
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{P,M}(F)\coloneqq\sum_{n=0}^{P}\mathcal{P}_{\tau}^{n,M}(F)
.

Whenever the value of
\tau
is clear, to ease the notation we will write
\mathcal{P}^{n}
,
\mathcal{P}^{n,M}
,
\mathcal{C}^{P}
and
\mathcal{C}^{P,M}
.

The following result is a consequence of Proposition
2.11
.

Proposition 2.15
.

For
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{M})
, let us define
\widetilde{h}_{a}=\sqrt{n!}/\sqrt{a!}\ h_{1}^{\otimes a_{1}}\widetilde{\otimes}\cdots\widetilde{\otimes}h_{M}^{\otimes a_{M}}
. We then have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\tau}^{n,M}(F)=I_{n}\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a|=n\\
\#a=M\end{subarray}}\langle g_{n},\widetilde{h}_{a}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{n})}\widetilde{h}_{a}\Big).

Remark 2.16
.

One can easily check that
(\widetilde{h}_{a})_{|a|=n}
forms an orthonormal basis of
\widetilde{L}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
.

The following gives us some useful properties about the truncation of the chaos decomposition, which are straightforward to prove.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
F\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{\tau})
. Then, for all
P,M,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{s}\in[0,\tau]^{k}
, we have that:

(i)

\mathbb{E}(|\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{P,M}(F)|^{2})\leq\mathbb{E}(|\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{P}(F)|^{2})\leq\mathbb{E}(|F|^{2})
;

(ii)

If
F\in\mathbb{D}_{\tau}^{k,2}
, then for any
\alpha\in\{1,\dots,d\}^{k}
we have
D_{\mathbf{s}}^{\alpha}\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{P}(F)=\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{P-k}(D_{\mathbf{s}}^{\alpha}F)
and
D_{\mathbf{s}}^{\alpha}\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{P,M}(F)=\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{P-k,M}(D_{\mathbf{s}}^{\alpha}F)
, where we use the convention
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{m}(\cdot)=\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{m,M}(\cdot)=0
for

.

2.5  Error given by truncating the order of the chaos

In this section we analyze the error between a random variable and the truncation of its chaos decomposition to order
P
. This will be later used in Section
4
to estimate the error given by the numerical scheme.

See page 28 in
[
22
]
for the following result.

Lemma 2.18
.

For any
F\in\mathbb{D}_{\tau}^{k,2}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\lVert D^{k}F\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{\tau}^{\otimes k}}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)\mathbb{E}\big(\lvert\mathcal{P}^{n}(F)\rvert^{2}\big).

Lemma 2.19
.

Let
F\in\mathbb{D}_{\tau}^{k,2}
. Then, for any
P\geq k-1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\lvert F-\mathcal{C}^{P}(F)\rvert^{2}\big)\leq\mathbb{E}\big(\lVert D^{k}F\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{\tau}^{\otimes k}}^{2}\big)\frac{(P-k+1)!}{(P+1)!}.

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\lvert F-\mathcal{C}^{P}(F)\rvert^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq P+1}\mathbb{E}(\lvert\mathcal{P}^{n}(F)\rvert^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq P+1}n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)\frac{\mathbb{E}(\lvert\mathcal{P}^{n}(F)\rvert^{2})}{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(P+1)P\cdots(P-k+2)}\sum_{n\geq P+1}n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)\mathbb{E}(\lvert\mathcal{P}^{n}(F)\rvert^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(P+1)P\cdots(P-k+2)}\sum_{n\geq 0}n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)\mathbb{E}(\lvert\mathcal{P}^{n}(F)\rvert^{2}).

The result follows then by using Lemma
2.18
.
∎

2.6  Error due to the truncation of the basis

The following lemma tells us that the error between
\mathcal{C}^{P,M}(F)
and
\mathcal{C}^{P}(F)
only depends on the
\widetilde{L}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
-error of the truncation of the chosen basis for the kernels
g_{n}
appearing in the chaos decomposition of
F
.

Lemma 2.20
.

Let
F\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{\tau})
be a random variable with chaos decomposition given by (
2.6
). Let
\widetilde{h}_{a}
be defined as in Proposition
2.15
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\lvert(\mathcal{C}^{P,M}-\mathcal{C}^{P})(F)\rvert^{2}\big)=\sum_{n=1}^{P}n!\Big\|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a|=n\\
\#a=M\end{subarray}}\langle g_{n},\widetilde{h}_{a}\rangle_{\widetilde{L}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{n})}\widetilde{h}_{a}-g_{n}\Big\|_{\widetilde{L}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{n})}^{2}.

(2.7)

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\lvert(\mathcal{C}^{P,M}-\mathcal{C}^{P})(F)\rvert^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big(\Big|\sum_{n=1}^{P}\big(\mathcal{P}^{n,M}-\mathcal{P}^{n}\big)(F)\Big|^{2}\Big)=\sum_{n=1}^{P}\mathbb{E}\big(\big|(\mathcal{P}^{n,M}-\mathcal{P}^{n})(F)\big|^{2}\big),

by orthogonality of different chaos orders. Using Proposition
2.15
and (
2.4
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big(\lvert(\mathcal{P}^{n,M}-\mathcal{P}^{n})(F)\rvert^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big(\Big|I_{n}\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a|=n\\
\#a=M\end{subarray}}\langle g_{n},\widetilde{h}_{a}\rangle_{\widetilde{L}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{n})}\widetilde{h}_{a}-g_{n}\Big)\Big|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=n!\Big\|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a|=n\\
\#a=M\end{subarray}}\langle g_{n},\widetilde{h}_{a}\rangle_{\widetilde{L}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{n})}\widetilde{h}_{a}-g_{n}\Big\|_{\widetilde{L}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{n})}^{2}.

∎

3  Description of the algorithm

We now proceed to describe an implementation of the scheme outlined in Section
2.2
. The key idea is to approximate the random variables
F_{i}^{\pi}
defined in (
2.1
) by a finite-dimensional Wiener chaos projection. By choosing an appropriate truncated basis
(h_{j}^{i})_{1\leq j\leq M}
of
\mathbb{H}_{t_{i}}
, we will obtain closed-form formulas for the conditional expectations and martingale terms in (
2.2
).

3.1  Description of the truncated basis

For notational simplicity, we present the one-dimensional case (
d=1
); the corresponding definitions and formulae for the multi-dimensional case are provided in Section
A
.

We start by setting some notation. For
i=m
, we let
\overline{\pi}_{m}\coloneqq\{s_{j}^{m}\coloneqq jT/M\}_{0\leq j\leq M}
be a uniform partition of size
M
of the interval
[0,T]
. Now, for any
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, we consider the partition of
[0,t_{i}]
given by

\displaystyle\overline{\pi}_{i}\coloneqq\{s\in\overline{\pi}_{m}:s<t_{i}\}\cup\{t_{i}\}.

We set
M(i)\coloneqq\#\overline{\pi}_{i}-1
and
\theta(i)=(P,M(i))
. We also denote the elements of
\overline{\pi}_{i}
by
\{s_{j}^{i}\}_{0\leq j\leq M(i)}
. Notice that
M(i)\leq M(i+1)
.

For each
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, we then define

\displaystyle h_{j}^{i}(t)\coloneqq\mathbf{1}_{(s_{j-1}^{i},s_{j}^{i}]}(t)/\sqrt{\delta_{j}^{i}},\quad\delta_{j}^{i}\coloneqq s_{j}^{i}-s_{j-1}^{i},\quad j=1,\dots,M(i).

(3.1)

This can be seen as the truncation of an orthonormal system in
L^{2}([0,t_{i}];\mathbb{R})
, for example by completing it with the Haar basis on each sub-interval. We then let

\displaystyle B(h_{j}^{i})\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t_{i}}h_{j}^{i}(s)dB_{s}=\frac{B_{s_{j}^{i}}-B_{s_{j-1}^{i}}}{\sqrt{\delta_{j}^{i}}},\quad j=1,\dots,M(i).

We then denote by
\mathcal{C}_{t_{i}}^{\theta(i)}(\cdot)
the truncated chaos decomposition with truncated basis given by (
3.1
). That is, for a random variable
F\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}})
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{t_{i}}^{\theta(i)}

\displaystyle(F)\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{P}\sum_{|a|=k}d_{a}\prod_{j=1}^{M(i)}H_{a_{j}}\big(B(h_{j}^{i})\big),\quad\#a=M(i),

(3.2)

\displaystyle d_{a}\coloneqq a!\mathbb{E}\Big(F\times\prod_{j=1}^{M(i)}H_{a_{j}}\big(B(h_{j}^{i})\big)\Big).

3.2  Description of the implementation

Fix
P,M\in\mathbb{N}
. We define the recursion
Y_{t_{m}}^{\pi,\theta}\coloneqq\mathcal{C}^{\theta(m)}(\xi)
, and for
i=m,\dots,1
,

\displaystyle F_{i}^{\pi,\theta}\coloneqq Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}+\Delta_{i}f_{i}^{\pi,\theta},\quad f_{i}^{\pi,\theta}\coloneqq f(t_{i},Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta},\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi,\theta}),

where

\displaystyle Y_{t}^{\pi,\theta}=\mathcal{C}^{\theta(i)}(F_{i}^{\pi,\theta})-\int_{t}^{t_{i}}Z_{s}^{\pi,\theta}\cdot dB_{s}\quad\text{for $t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i})$,}

and

\displaystyle\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi,\theta}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}\Big(\frac{1}{\Delta_{i+1}}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}Z_{s}^{\pi,\theta}ds\Big),\quad\text{for $i\in\{1,\dots,m-1\}$,}

with the convention that
\langle Z\rangle_{m}^{\pi,\theta}=0
. Similarly as before, for
t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i})
, we have that
Y_{t}^{\pi,\theta}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{t}\big(\mathcal{C}^{\theta(i)}(F_{i}^{\pi,\theta})\big)
,

\displaystyle\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi,\theta}=\frac{1}{\Delta_{i+1}}\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}[\mathcal{C}^{\theta(i+1)}(F_{i+1}^{\pi,\theta})(B_{t_{i+1}}-B_{t_{i}})],

and
(Z_{s}^{\pi,\theta})_{s\in[t_{i-1},t_{i})}
is given by the martingale representation theorem applied to
\allowbreak\mathcal{C}^{\theta(i)}(F_{i}^{\pi,\theta})
. In fact, since a random variable with finite chaos decomposition belongs to
\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
, we have the representation

\displaystyle Z_{t}^{\pi,\theta}\coloneqq D_{t}\mathbb{E}_{t}\big(\mathcal{C}^{\theta(i)}(F_{i}^{\pi,\theta})\big),\quad t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i}).

We emphasize the fact that the method can be implemented in the case of a random generator
f
.

3.3  Wiener chaos decomposition formulae

We now present closed-form formulae for the computation of
Y_{t}^{\pi,\theta}
,
Z_{t}^{\pi,\theta}
, and
\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi,\theta}
on
[t_{i-1},t_{i})
whenever we choose an appropriate truncated orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{H}_{t_{i}}
, with an approach similar to that in
[
6
]
. The formulas for the multi-dimensional case (
d>1
) are presented in Section
A
.

For each
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, we denote by
d_{a}^{i}
the chaos coefficient of
\mathcal{C}^{\theta(i)}(F_{i}^{\pi,\theta})
associated to
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{M(i)})
. The proofs for the next two results can be found in
[
6
, Proposition 2.7]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
r\in\{1,\dots,M(i)\}
be such that
t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i})\cap(s_{r-1}^{i},s_{r}^{i}]
. Then

\displaystyle Y_{t}^{\pi,\theta}=d_{0}^{i}+\sum_{k=1}^{P}\sum_{|a(r)|=k}d_{a}^{i}\prod_{j<r}H_{a_{j}}\big(B(h_{j}^{i})\big)\times\Bigg(\frac{t-s_{r-1}^{i}}{\delta_{r}^{i}}\Bigg)^{a_{r}/2}H_{a_{r}}\Bigg(\frac{B_{t}-B_{s_{r-1}^{i}}}{\sqrt{t-s_{r-1}^{i}}}\Bigg),

where for
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{M(i)})
,
a(r)
stands for
(a_{1},\dots,a_{r},0,\dots,0)
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i})\cap(s_{r-1}^{i},s_{r}^{i}]
. Then

\displaystyle Z_{t}^{\pi,\theta}=(\delta_{r}^{i})^{-1/2}\sum_{k=1}^{P}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a(r)|=k\\
a_{r}>0\end{subarray}}d_{a}^{i}

\displaystyle\prod_{j<r}H_{a_{j}}\big(B(h_{j}^{i})\big)\times\Bigg(\frac{t-s_{r-1}^{i}}{\delta_{r}^{i}}\Bigg)^{\frac{a_{r}-1}{2}}H_{a_{r}-1}\Bigg(\frac{B_{t}-B_{s_{r-1}^{i}}}{\sqrt{t-s_{r-1}^{i}}}\Bigg),

where
a(r)=(a_{1},\dots,a_{r},0,\dots,0)
.

We finally find a closed-form formula for
\langle Z\rangle_{i-1}^{\pi,\theta}
. The proof for the multi-dimensional case can be found in Proposition
A.3
in the Appendix.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
u
be such that
t_{i-1}\in(s_{u-1}^{i},s_{u}^{i}]
. Let

\displaystyle C_{1}(i)=\frac{s_{u}^{i}-t_{i-1}}{\sqrt{\delta_{u}^{i}}},\quad C_{2}(i)=\frac{t_{i-1}-s_{u-1}^{i}}{\delta_{u}^{i}}.

Then
\langle Z\rangle_{i-1}^{\pi,\theta}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{P}\Bigg\{C_{1}(i)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a(u)|=k\\
a_{u}>0\end{subarray}}d_{a}^{i}\prod_{j<u}H_{a_{j}}\big(B(h_{j}^{i})\big)\times\big[C_{2}(i)\big]^{\frac{a_{u}-1}{2}}H_{a_{u}-1}\Bigg(\frac{B_{t_{i-1}}-B_{s_{u-1}^{i}}}{\sqrt{t_{i-1}-s_{u-1}^{i}}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle+\sum_{r=u+1}^{M(i)}\sqrt{\delta_{r}^{i}}\sum_{|a_{r}(u)|=k}d_{a}^{i}\prod_{j<u}H_{a_{j}}\big(B(h_{j}^{i})\big)\times\big[C_{2}(i)\big]^{\frac{a_{u}}{2}}H_{a_{u}}\Bigg(\frac{B_{t_{i-1}}-B_{s_{u-1}^{i}}}{\sqrt{t_{i-1}-s_{u-1}^{i}}}\Bigg)\Bigg\},

where
a_{r}(u)\coloneqq(a_{1},\dots,a_{u},0,\dots,\underset{r\text{-th}}{1},\dots,0)
.

3.4  Monte Carlo approximation of the chaos coefficients

In order to implement the previous method, one needs to compute the coefficients
d_{a}^{i}
of the chaos decomposition of the random variables
F_{i}^{\pi,\theta}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}})
,
which, recall, amount to expectations of the form

\displaystyle\frac{d_{a}^{i}}{a!}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big(F_{i}^{\pi,\theta}\times\prod_{j=1}^{M(i)}H_{a_{j}}\big(B(h_{j}^{i})\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big(Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}\times\prod_{j=1}^{M(i)}H_{a_{j}}\big(B(h_{j}^{i})\big)\Big)+\Delta_{i}\mathbb{E}\Big(f(t_{i},Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta},\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi,\theta})\times\prod_{j=1}^{M(i)}H_{a_{j}}\big(B(h_{j}^{i})\big)\Big).

(3.3)

Notice that Proposition
3.1
already gives us the coefficients of
\mathcal{C}^{\theta(i)}(Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta})
in closed-form. Indeed, for
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{M(i)},0,\dots,0)
with
\#a=M(i+1)
, we have that

\displaystyle t_{i}\in(s_{M(i)-1}^{i+1},s_{M(i)}^{i+1}],\quad a!\;\mathbb{E}\Big(Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}\times\prod_{j=1}^{M(i)}H_{a_{j}}\big(B(h_{j}^{i})\big)\Big)=d_{a}^{i+1}\times\bigg(\frac{t_{i}-s_{M(i)-1}^{i+1}}{\delta_{M(i)}^{i+1}}\bigg)^{a_{M(i)}/2}.

We therefore only have to compute the second expectation in (
3.3
). A standard procedure to approximate these is to use a Monte Carlo simulation, as it is done for example in
[
6
]
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be the number of samples that we use to approximate the expectations. We will use different Brownian motions to approximate the coefficients at each time step. We therefore consider:

•

\overline{B}=(B^{i,n})_{0\leq i\leq m,\,1\leq n\leq N}
and
B^{\xi}=(B^{\xi,n})_{1\leq n\leq N}
an
(m+1)N
-dimensional and an
N
-dimensional Brownian motion, respectively, independent of
B
and of each other.

We also write
B^{i,0}=B
for any
0\leq i\leq m
, where
B
is the original Brownian motion driving the BSDE;

•

(\overline{\Omega},\overline{\mathcal{F}},\overline{\mathbb{P}},\overline{\mathbb{F}})
the enlarged filtered probability space carrying the Brownian motions
\overline{B}
,
B^{\xi}
and
B
;

•

\overline{\mathbb{E}}(\cdot)
will denote the expectation taken under this probability space, and
\overline{D}^{(i,n)}
the corresponding Malliavin derivative;

•

\mathbb{F}^{i,n}=(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{i,n})_{t\in[0,T]}
the natural augmented filtration generated by
B^{i,n}
. The same convention applies to
\mathbb{F}^{\xi,n}=(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\xi,n})_{t\in[0,T]}
;

•

\mathbb{F}^{i}=(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{i})_{t\in[0,T]}
the natural augmented filtration generated by
(B^{i,1},\dots,B^{i,N})
. The same convention applies to
\mathbb{F}^{\xi}=(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\xi})_{t\in[0,T]}
;

•

\mathcal{F}^{i\leq}\coloneqq\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}}^{i}\vee\mathcal{F}_{t_{i+1}}^{i+1}\vee\cdots\vee\mathcal{F}_{t_{m}}^{m}\vee\mathcal{F}_{t_{m}}^{\xi}
. We set the convention
\mathcal{F}^{m+1\leq}=\mathcal{F}_{t_{m}}^{\xi}
;

•

(\xi^{1},\dots,\xi^{N})
an
N
-dimensional copy of
\xi
, with
\xi^{n}
being
\mathcal{F}_{T}^{\xi,n}
-measurable;

•

H_{a}^{(i,n)}=\prod_{j=1}^{M(i)}H_{a_{j}}\big(B^{i,n}(h_{j}^{i})\big)
and
\widehat{H}_{a}^{(i,n)}=\prod_{j=1}^{M(i)}H_{a_{j}}\big(B^{i-1,n}(h_{j}^{i})\big)
. Similarly,
H_{a}^{(\xi,n)}=\prod_{j=1}^{M(m)}H_{a_{j}}\big(B^{\xi,n}(h_{j}^{m})\big)
.

The random variables
H_{a}^{(i,n)}
are going to be used for the Monte Carlo approximation of the coefficient
d_{a}^{i}
, while
\widehat{H}_{a}^{(i,n)}
is going to be used to simulate the (approximated) chaos decomposition at step
i
.

We then recursively define the following approximation of the truncated chaos decomposition of
F_{i}^{\pi,\theta}
.

Definition 3.4
.

For
n=0,\dots,N
, let

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{C}}^{\theta(m),n}(\xi)\coloneqq\hat{d}_{0}^{\xi}+\sum_{k=1}^{P}\sum_{|a|=k}\hat{d}_{a}^{\xi}\,H_{a}^{(m,n)},\quad\frac{\hat{d}_{a}^{\xi}}{a!}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\xi^{l}\times H_{a}^{(\xi,l)}.

We then recursively define
\big(\widehat{Y}_{t_{m}}^{\pi,\theta,n},\langle\widehat{Z}\rangle_{t_{m}}^{\pi,\theta,n}\big)\coloneqq\big(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}^{\,\theta(m),n}(\xi),0\big)
, and for
i\in\{m,\dots,1\}
,
t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i})
,

\displaystyle\widehat{Y}_{t}^{\pi,\theta,n}=\overline{\mathbb{E}}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}^{\>\theta(i),n}(\widehat{F}_{i}^{\,\pi,\theta})\;|\;\mathcal{F}_{t}^{i-1,n}\vee\mathcal{F}^{i\leq}\big),\quad\widehat{Z}_{t}^{\pi,\theta,n}=\overline{D}_{t}^{(i-1,n)}\widehat{Y}_{t}^{\pi,\theta,n},

(3.4)

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{C}}^{\,\theta(i),n}(\widehat{F}_{i}^{\,\pi,\theta})\coloneqq\hat{d}_{0}^{i}+\sum_{k=1}^{P}\sum_{|a|=k}\hat{d}_{a}^{i}\;\widehat{H}_{a}^{(i,n)},

with

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{d}_{a}^{i}}{a!}\coloneqq\overline{\mathbb{E}}\Big(\widehat{Y}_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta,0}\times H_{a}^{(i,0)}\;\Big|\;\mathcal{F}^{i+1\leq}\Big)+\frac{\Delta_{i}}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(t_{i},\widehat{Y}_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta,n},\langle\widehat{Z}\rangle_{i}^{\pi,\theta,n})\times H_{a}^{(i,n)}.

(3.5)

Remark 3.5
.

The random variables
\hat{d}_{a}^{i}
are
\mathcal{F}^{i\leq}
-measurable. Moreover, for each
n\geq 1
,
\hat{d}_{a}^{i}
is independent of
\widehat{H}_{a}^{(i,n)}
(they are built from different Brownian families).

Consequently, the random variables
\{\widehat{Y}_{t}^{\pi,\theta,n},\widehat{Z}_{t}^{\pi,\theta,n},\langle\widehat{Z}\rangle_{i-1}^{\pi,\theta,n}\}
can be obtained via the formulas in Propositions
3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3
, respectively, by replacing
d_{a}^{i}
with
\hat{d}_{a}^{i}
and using the Brownian motion
B^{i-1,n}
in place of
B
.

Remark 3.6
.

The random variable
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}^{\>\theta(i),0}(\widehat{F}_{i}^{\pi,\theta})
is

\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}}\vee\mathcal{F}^{i\leq}
-measurable and square-integrable.
Therefore, by a conditional martingale representation theorem (treating

\mathcal{F}^{i\leq}
as fixed), we have

\displaystyle\widehat{Y}_{t_{i-1}}^{\pi,\theta,0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}^{\>\theta(i),0}(\widehat{F}_{i}^{\pi,\theta})\;\big|\;\mathcal{F}_{t_{i-1}}\vee\mathcal{F}^{i\leq}\big)=\widehat{\mathcal{C}}^{\>\theta(i),0}(\widehat{F}_{i}^{\pi,\theta})-\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\widehat{Z}_{t}^{\pi,\theta,0}\;dB_{t},

where
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
denotes the natural augmented filtration of the original Brownian motion
B
.

4  Convergence analysis

In this section, we present a detailed convergence analysis of the method introduced above. To simplify the notation, we focus on the one-dimensional case (
d=1
); the extension to the multi-dimensional setting follows directly by the same arguments. Throughout the section, we also assume that the generator
f
is deterministic, since several parts of the convergence analysis do not carry over directly to the random case.

Some proofs are deferred to Section
B
in the Appendix.

4.1  Error given by the truncation of the chaos

We begin by recalling a fundamental structural property of the Wiener chaos. Whenever a random variable is measurable with respect to a finite number of Brownian functionals, its entire
L^{2}
-decomposition can be expressed in terms of multivariate Hermite polynomials of those functionals.

This observation will later allow us to control truncation errors uniformly along the time grid and to ensure that all random variables involved in the recursion remain finite-dimensional within a fixed chaos level.

Proposition 4.1
.

Consider an orthonormal set
(h_{j})_{1\leq j\leq M}\subset L^{2}([0,\tau])
. For any
F\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{\tau})
measurable w.r.t.
\big(B(h_{1}),\dots,B(h_{M})\big)
we have that

\displaystyle F=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{|a|=k}d_{a}\prod_{j=1}^{M}H_{a_{j}}\big(B(h_{j})\big),\quad a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{M}).

Consequently,
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{P,M}(F)=\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{P}(F)
for any
P\in\mathbb{N}
.

Remark 4.2
.

Notice that, by the formulas in Section
3.3
and the fact that
f
is deterministic, the random variable
f_{i}^{\pi,\theta}
is measurable w.r.t.
(B(h_{1}^{i}),\dots,B(h_{M(i)}^{i}))
. Therefore, by Proposition
4.1
, the random variables
F_{i}^{\pi,\theta}
are such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{P,M(i)}(F_{i}^{\pi,\theta})=Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}+\Delta_{i}\ \mathcal{C}^{P}\big(f_{i}^{\pi,\theta}\big),

where we used the fact that
Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}
has a finite-dimensional chaos decomposition of order
P
. We therefore have that the error introduced at each step in the projection of the chaos of
F_{i}^{\pi,\theta}
only comes from truncating the maximum order
P
of its non-linear part
f_{i}^{\pi,\theta}
.

We now focus on the error between the numerical scheme presented in Section
2.2
and the implementation outlined in Section
3
, assuming that we can compute the coefficients of the chaos exactly. The next lemma shows that the resulting error is controlled by the sum, over each time-step, of the
L^{2}
-distance between the random variable
f_{i}^{\pi,\theta}
and its finite-dimensional chaos projection
\mathcal{C}^{P}(f_{i}^{\pi,\theta})
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\pi=\{0=t_{0}<\cdots<t_{m}=T\}
be such that
\max_{0\leq i\leq m-1}\Delta_{i}/\Delta_{i+1}\leq L
. We then have that, for
|\pi|
sufficiently small,

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq i\leq m}\mathbb{E}\Big(

\displaystyle\lvert Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi}-Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}\Big)+\mathbb{E}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\lvert Z_{u}^{\pi}-Z_{u}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}du\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C\Big\{\mathbb{E}(\lvert\xi-\mathcal{C}^{\theta(m)}(\xi)\rvert^{2})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta_{i}\mathbb{E}\big(\lvert f_{i}^{\pi,\theta(i)}-\mathcal{C}^{P}(f_{i}^{\pi,\theta(i)})\rvert^{2}\big)\Big\},

where
C>0
is a constant possibly depending on
[f]_{L}
,
T
and
L
.

One might be tempted to simply use Lemma
2.19
with for example
k=1
to obtain the bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\lvert f_{i}^{\pi,\theta}-\mathcal{C}^{P}(f_{i}^{\pi,\theta})\rvert^{2}\big)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\big(\lVert Df_{i}^{\pi,\theta}\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{t_{i}}}^{2}\big)}{P+1}.

However, notice that the numerator depends on
P
, since both
Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}
and
\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi,\theta}
— and therefore
f_{i}^{\pi,\theta}
— are themselves defined recursively in terms of truncated chaos decompositions. Hence, a direct application of Lemma
2.19
would not yield a uniform bound. To overcome this, we first establish energy-type estimates on the Malliavin derivatives of
Y^{\pi,\theta}
and
\langle Z\rangle^{\pi,\theta}
, which allow us to control
\mathbb{E}(|Df_{i}^{\pi,\theta}|_{\mathbb{H}_{t_{i}}}^{2})
independently of
P
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume
f\in C_{b,(y,z)}^{1:1}
. Then we have that

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\mathbb{E}\big(\lVert DY_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{t_{i}}}^{2}\big)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta_{i}\mathbb{E}\big(\lVert D\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi,\theta}\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{t_{i}}}^{2}\big)\leq C\mathbb{E}\big(\lVert D\xi\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{t_{m}}}^{2}\big),

(4.1)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta_{i}\mathbb{E}\big(\lVert Df_{i}^{\pi,\theta}\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{t_{i}}}^{2}\big)\leq C\mathbb{E}\big(\lVert D\xi\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{t_{m}}}^{2}\big),

(4.2)

where
C
is a constant depending on
L
,
\lVert\partial_{(y,z)}^{1}f\rVert_{\infty}
and
T
.

We now analyze the truncation error associated with the parameter
M
, which controls the number of basis functions in the chaos decomposition of the terminal condition
\xi
. As noted in Remark
2.16
, the family
(\widetilde{h}_{a})_{|a|=n}
forms an orthonormal basis of
\widetilde{L}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
, so truncating it to the first
M
elements introduces a projection error that vanishes with
M
.

The next result establishes the rate of this decay under a Hölder-type regularity assumption on the chaos kernels, stated for the piecewise constant basis used in the implementation.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{N}
,
\delta=\tau/M
, and define
s_{i}=\delta\times i
for
i\in\{0,\dots,M\}
and
h_{i}(t)=\mathbf{1}_{(s_{i-1},s_{i}]}(t)/\sqrt{\delta}
for
i\in\{1,\dots,M\}
.

Let
g:\mathcal{T}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a symmetric function such that

\displaystyle\big|g(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})-g(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\big|\leq\kappa\big(|r_{1}-u_{1}|^{\beta}+\cdots+|r_{n}-u_{n}|^{\beta}\big)

(4.3)

for some constants
\kappa,\beta>0
. Then

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a|=n\\
\#a=M\end{subarray}}\langle g,\widetilde{h}_{a}\rangle_{\widetilde{L}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{n})}\widetilde{h}_{a}-g\Big\lVert_{\widetilde{L}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{n})}^{2}\leq\kappa^{2}\times n^{2}\times\tau^{2\beta+n}\times\Big(\frac{1}{M}\Big)^{2\beta}.

Consequently, whenever the Wiener-chaos kernels of a random variable satisfy the Hölder-continuity condition (
4.3
), Lemma
2.20
immediately gives an explicit rate for the decay of the truncation error
\mathbb{E}\big(\lvert(\mathcal{C}^{P,M}-\mathcal{C}^{P})(F)\rvert^{2}\big)
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
F
have chaos decomposition (
2.6
), and assume that, for
n=1,\dots,P
, the functions
\mathbb{E}\big[D^{n}_{\mathbf{s}}F\big]\in\widetilde{L}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
satisfy (
4.3
) with uniform
\kappa,\beta>0
. Then we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\lvert(\mathcal{C}^{P}-\mathcal{C}^{P,M})(F)\rvert^{2}\big)\leq\Big(\frac{1}{M}\Big)^{2\beta}\kappa^{2}\tau^{2\beta}e^{\tau}(\tau+\tau^{2}).

(4.4)

Proof.

Combining Proposition
2.13
, Lemma
2.20
and Lemma
4.5
we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\lvert(\mathcal{C}^{P}-\mathcal{C}^{P,M})(F)\rvert^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{1}{M}\Big)^{2\beta}\kappa^{2}\tau^{2\beta}\sum_{n=1}^{P}\frac{n}{(n-1)!}\tau^{n}.

We then conclude by using the fact that
\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{n}{(n-1)!}\tau^{n}=e^{\tau}(\tau+\tau^{2})
.
∎

Assumption 4.7
.

We say that a random variable
F\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{\tau})
satisfies
\mathscr{A}_{\tau}^{P}
if
F\in\mathbb{D}_{\tau}^{P,2}
and there exist constants
\kappa,\beta>0
(with
\kappa
possibly depending on
P
), such that

\displaystyle\lvert\mathbb{E}(D_{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}}^{(n)}F-D_{r_{1},\dots,r_{n}}^{(n)}F)\rvert\leq\kappa\big(|s_{1}-r_{1}|^{\beta}+\cdots+|s_{n}-r_{n}|^{\beta}\big)

for all
1\leq n\leq P
.

Example 4.8
.

Let
b\colon[0,\tau]\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
\sigma\colon[0,\tau]\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
be two
C^{0,P}
functions, with bounded derivatives w.r.t.
x
and Hölder continuous of parameter
\frac{1}{2}
w.r.t.
t
. Let
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,\tau]}
be the solution to the SDE

\displaystyle X_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{t}b(u,X_{u})dt+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(u,X_{u})dB_{u},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Let
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{P}
function with bounded derivatives.

Then, any random variable of the form
g(X_{\tau})
,
\int_{0}^{\tau}g(X_{t})dt
satisfies Assumption
4.7
.

4.2  Error of the Monte Carlo approximation of the coefficients

The goal of this subsection is to analyze the error due to the Monte Carlo approximation of the chaos coefficients described in Subsection
3.4
. To simplify the notation, let us write

\displaystyle\widehat{f}_{i}^{\pi,\theta,n}=f(t_{i},\widehat{Y}_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta,n},\langle\widehat{Z}\rangle_{i}^{\pi,\theta,n}),\quad\widehat{F}_{i}^{\pi,\theta,n}=\widehat{Y}_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta,n}+\Delta_{i}\widehat{f}_{i}^{\pi,\theta,n}.

Additionally, whenever we omit the
n
in the superscript, it will mean that we are setting
n=0
, e.g.
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}^{\,\theta,0}(\cdot)=\widehat{\mathcal{C}}^{\,\theta}(\cdot)
,
\widehat{f}_{i}^{\pi,\theta}=\widehat{f}_{i}^{\pi,\theta,0}
,
\widehat{Y}_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}=\widehat{Y}_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta,0}
and so on.

For an
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{t_{i}}
-measurable random variable
F
, we define the quantity

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{i}(F)\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{P}\sum_{|a|=k}a!\,\overline{\mathbb{V}}\big(F\times H_{a}^{(i,0)}\big),\quad\#a=M(i),

(4.5)

where
\overline{\mathbb{V}}(\cdot)
indicates the variance taken under
(\overline{\Omega},\overline{\mathcal{F}},\overline{\mathbb{P}})
. Notice that for a general square-integrable random variable
F
, the quantity
\mathbf{V}_{i}(F)
can still be infinite.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\pi=\{0=t_{0}<\cdots<t_{m}=T\}
be such that
\max_{0\leq i\leq m-1}\Delta_{i}/\Delta_{i+1}\leq L
. Let
\theta=(P,M)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
. We then have that, for
|\pi|
sufficiently small,

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq i\leq m}\overline{\mathbb{E}}\Big(\lvert Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}-\widehat{Y}_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}\Big)+\overline{\mathbb{E}}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\lvert Z_{u}^{\pi,\theta}-

\displaystyle\widehat{Z}_{u}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}du\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{N}\Big[\mathbf{V}_{m}(\xi)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta_{i}^{2}\mathbf{V}_{i}(\widehat{f}_{i}^{\pi,\theta})\Big]

(4.6)

where
C>0
is a constant possibly depending on
[f]_{L}
,
T
and
L
.

Assuming that
\textbf{V}_{m}(\xi)<\infty
, and using the linear growth condition on
f
, then one can easily prove that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta_{i}^{2}\mathbf{V}_{i}(\widehat{f}_{i}^{\pi,\theta})<\infty
. Additionally, if we assume that
f
is bounded, then we obtain a rate of convergence for (
4.6
).

Lemma 4.10
.

Assume that
f\in C_{b}
. Then we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{i}(\widehat{f}_{i}^{\pi,\theta})\leq\binom{P+M(i)}{M(i)}\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}^{2}.

(4.7)

Proof.

For a fixed
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{M})
, we have

\displaystyle a!\overline{\mathbb{V}}\big(\widehat{f}_{i}^{\pi,\theta}\times H_{a}^{(i,0)}\big)\leq a!\overline{\mathbb{E}}\big(|\widehat{f}_{i}^{\pi,\theta}\times H_{a}^{(i,0)}|^{2}\big)\leq a!\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\mathbb{E}(|H_{a}^{(i,0)}|^{2})=\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}^{2}.

Taking into account that there are
\binom{P+M(i)}{M(i)}
vectors
a
such that
\#a=M(i)
and
|a|\leq P
, we arrive at (
4.7
).
∎

4.3  Main error estimates

We end this section by combining all the previous results to bound the error between the solution of a BSDE and the approximation obtained by our implementation.

We provide two results, one assuming that we can compute the coefficients of the chaos decomposition in exact form, and one approximating them with a Monte Carlo.

Through this section we make use of the following notation and assumptions. Let
\pi\subset[0,T]
be a partition such that
\max_{i}\Delta_{i}/\Delta_{i+1}\leq L
. Let
\theta=(P,M)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
. Let Assumption
2.2
be satisfied.

Theorem 4.11
.

There exists a constant
C
, independent of
\pi
,

and

, such that

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq i\leq m}\mathbb{E}\Big(

\displaystyle\lvert Y_{t_{i}}-Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}\Big)+\mathbb{E}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\lvert Z_{u}-Z_{u}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}du\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C\Bigg(\lvert\pi\rvert+\mathbb{E}\big(\lvert\xi-\mathcal{C}^{\theta}(\xi)\rvert^{2})+\frac{\mathbb{E}(\lVert D\xi\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{T}}^{2})}{P+1}\Bigg),

Moreover, assuming that
\xi
satisfies
\mathscr{A}_{T}^{P}
, we have that

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq i\leq m}\mathbb{E}\Big(\lvert Y_{t_{i}}-Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}\Big)+\mathbb{E}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}

\displaystyle\lvert Z_{u}-Z_{u}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}du\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\lvert\pi\rvert+\frac{\mathbb{E}(\lVert D\xi\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{T}}^{2})}{P+1}\Big)+C_{P}M^{-2\beta},

where

is a constant possibly depending on

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume

is bounded. Then there exists a constant
C
, independent of
\pi
,
P
,

and

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq i\leq m}\overline{\mathbb{E}}\Big(

\displaystyle\lvert Y_{t_{i}}-\widehat{Y}_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}\Big)+\overline{\mathbb{E}}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}\lvert Z_{u}-\widehat{Z}_{u}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}du\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C\Bigg(\lvert\pi\rvert+\mathbb{E}\big(\lvert\xi-\mathcal{C}^{\theta}(\xi)\rvert^{2})+\frac{\mathbb{E}(\lVert D\xi\rVert_{\mathbb{H}_{T}}^{2})}{P+1}+\frac{C_{P,M}}{N}\Bigg),

where

Remark 4.13
.

•

The constants
C
appearing in these theorems can depend on

,
[f]_{L}
,
[f]_{H}
,
\lVert\partial_{(y,z)}^{1}f\rVert
and

.

•

Under Assumption

, the random variable

has absolute moments of fourth order, and hence

can be proven to be finite.

Remark 4.14
.

Some care is needed when selecting the parameters

,
M
, and

. If

and

are chosen too large, the number of Monte Carlo samples

required to keep the approximation error small becomes prohibitively high.

To illustrate this, consider

,
\|f\|_{\infty}=1
, and
\xi=1
almost surely. In this case,
C_{P,M}=\binom{P+M}{M}(1+|\pi|)
.
For

and

, we have
\binom{P+M}{M}=39{,}711
. If we take

, then

, which already represents a non-negligible error.
Similarly, for

and

,
\binom{P+M}{M}=230{,}230
, and even with

we still obtain

Nevertheless, in most cases, relatively small values of

.

5  Comparison with alternative methods

As presented in the introduction, many numerical methods have been proposed to approximate the conditional expectations and martingale terms appearing in the Euler-type scheme (
1.2
). The main advantage of our approach is that it does not rely on an underlying forward–backward SDE representation. In particular, we do not restrict ourselves to the standard Markovian setting considered in much of the literature, where the terminal condition is assumed to be of the form
\xi=g(X_{T})
for a forward SDE

\displaystyle X_{t}=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(s,X_{s})\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,X_{s})\,dB_{s},

where
b\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\sigma\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
. This assumption is often used not only in the convergence analysis, but also in implementations, since it yields deterministic functions
(u,v)\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
Y_{t}=u(t,X_{t})
and
Z_{t}=v(t,X_{t})
. Numerical schemes then typically discretize the forward SDE (e.g. by Euler–Maruyama) and approximate
u
and
v
on the grid via regression or other conditional-expectation approximations.

While some methods can be extended to path-dependent terminal conditions, this usually requires augmenting the forward state to recover a Markovian formulation, often leading to a high-dimensional system, see for instance
[
25
, Section 4]
and
[
2
]
.

On the downside, our method is affected by the curse of dimensionality in the Brownian dimension
d
. This limitation is shared by most existing approaches, and is mainly mitigated by regression-based methods that are tackled with deep learning.

Comparison with the Picard-type scheme

A special comparison should be made with the method presented in
[
6
]
, which can be seen as the Picard-type counterpart of our Euler-based method.

In
[
6
]
, the authors use Picard iterations. Written in forward form, they start from
(Y^{0},Z^{0})=(0,0)
and, for
q\in\mathbb{N}
, define

\displaystyle Y_{t}^{q+1}=\mathbb{E}_{t}\big(F^{q}\big)-\int_{0}^{t}f(s,Y_{s}^{q},Z_{s}^{q})\,ds,\qquad F^{q}=\xi+\int_{0}^{T}f(s,Y_{s}^{q},Z_{s}^{q})\,ds,

with
Z^{q+1}
given by the martingale representation theorem applied to
F^{q}
. To compute the conditional expectations and martingale terms, they replace
F^{q}
by its chaos projection
\mathcal{C}_{T}^{\theta}(F^{q})
, leading to

\displaystyle Y_{t}^{q+1,\theta}=\mathbb{E}_{t}\big(\mathcal{C}_{T}^{\theta}(F^{q,\theta})\big)-\int_{0}^{t}f(s,Y_{s}^{q,\theta},Z_{s}^{q,\theta})\,ds,\qquad Z_{t}^{q+1,\theta}=D_{t}Y_{t}^{q+1,\theta},

(5.1)

where
F^{q,\theta}=\xi+\int_{0}^{T}f(s,Y_{s}^{q,\theta},Z_{s}^{q,\theta})\,ds
. The procedure is stopped after a prescribed number of iterations
Q
. We now proceed to explain the advantages and disadvantages of the Euler-based scheme over the Picard-based one.

From a theoretical perspective, as we showed in Section
4
, the Euler scheme admits a full convergence analysis with respect to all algorithmic parameters. This is not available for the Picard scheme, where the proof assumes that the time integrals in (
5.1
) can be computed exactly. In addition, the convergence arguments for the Euler scheme are more direct and technically simpler.

Practically, in the Picard-based approach, simulating
(Y_{t}^{Q,\theta},Z_{t}^{Q,\theta})
for any
t>0
requires generating Brownian paths and then computing all the corresponding intermediate iterates
(Y^{q,\theta},Z^{q,\theta})_{1\leq q\leq Q}
sequentially. For the Monte Carlo estimation of the chaos coefficients of
F^{q}
,
q=0,\dots,Q
, one may either

•

use fresh Brownian paths at each iteration, which forces one to rerun the Picard loop for every
q
; or

•

reuse the same Brownian paths for all
F^{q}
. In this case, computing the coefficients of
F^{q+1}
requires storing samples of
(Y^{q},Z^{q})
from the previous step, which can be memory intensive, especially on GPUs.

To obtain a more efficient implementation,
[
6
]
adopt the second option. However, in their convergence analysis with respect to the Monte Carlo sample size, they assume that the Brownian paths are independently resampled at each
q
.

In contrast, in the Euler-based scheme we have explicit control of
(Y_{t}^{\pi,\theta},Z_{t}^{\pi,\theta})_{t\in[t_{i-1},t_{i})}
through the random variables
F_{i}^{\pi,\theta}
, and sampling reduces to drawing from
\mathcal{C}^{\theta}(F_{i}^{\pi,\theta})
. In particular, we can generate new samples at each time step and discard them afterwards, making the procedure more memory friendly.

As a disadvantage of our approach, the Picard-based method requires only
Q
chaos decompositions (typically
Q\in\{6,\dots,10\}
), whereas the Euler-based scheme requires
m
decompositions, with
m
usually between
10
and
60
. In view of current computational resources, this difference is not a practical bottleneck, as we illustrate in Section
6
.

Remark 5.1
.

In the implementation of the Picard–based scheme, the time integral is discretized over a uniform grid with
M
points. For better comparability with our method, however, we will allow this number to differ in the numerical experiments and denote it by
m
, as it plays a role analogous to the parameter
m
in the Euler-based scheme.

7  Conclusions

We have introduced a fully implementable numerical scheme for BSDEs that combines an Euler time discretization with the Wiener chaos decomposition. Unlike many existing schemes, our method does not rely on a forward SDE state representation and can treat general
\mathcal{F}_{T}
-measurable square-integrable terminal conditions. We provide a complete convergence analysis with respect to all discretization and truncation parameters, and illustrate the practical performance of the scheme through several numerical experiments, including comparisons with the Picard–based chaos algorithm of
[
6
]
. The results indicate that the Euler–chaos approach offers a robust and competitive alternative for the numerical resolution of BSDEs.

Appendix A
Multi-dimensional Wiener chaos formulae

We now present the framework whenever we work with a
d

(P,M)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
and
\overline{\pi}_{i}

For each

\displaystyle h_{j,l}^{i}(t)\coloneqq e_{l}\,\mathbf{1}_{(s_{j-1}^{i},s_{j}^{i}]}(t)/\sqrt{\delta_{j}^{i}},\quad\delta_{j}^{i}\coloneqq s_{j}^{i}-s_{j-1}^{i},\quad j=1,\dots,M(i).

(A.1)

This can be seen as the truncation of an orthonormal system in

. We then let

\displaystyle B(h_{j,l}^{i})\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t_{i}}h_{j,l}^{i}(s)\cdot dB_{s}=(B_{s_{j}^{i}}^{l}-B_{s_{j-1}^{i}}^{l})/\sqrt{\delta_{j}^{i}},\quad j=1,\dots,M(i).

For
l=1,\dots,d
, let

, and we write
a=(a^{1},\dots,a^{d})
,
|a|=|a^{1}|+\cdots+|a^{d}|
and

. We also write
\#a=M(i)
to indicate that each component
a^{l}
has length
M(i)
.

We denote by
\mathcal{C}_{t_{i}}^{\theta(i)}(\cdot)
the truncated chaos decomposition with basis given by (
A.1
). That is, for a random variable
F\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}})
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\theta(i)}

\displaystyle(F)\coloneqq d_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{P}\sum_{|a|=k}d_{a}\prod_{l=1}^{d}\prod_{j=1}^{M(i)}H_{a_{j}^{l}}\big(B(h_{j,l}^{i})\big),

(A.2)

\displaystyle d_{a}\coloneqq a!\mathbb{E}\Big(F\times\prod_{l=1}^{d}\prod_{j=1}^{M(i)}H_{a_{j}^{l}}\big(B(h_{j,l}^{i})\big)\Big),\quad\#a=M(i).

stands for

\displaystyle=\big[F_{i}^{\pi}-\mathcal{C}^{\theta(i)}(F_{i}^{\pi,\theta})\big]-\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\Delta^{\theta}Z_{u}^{\pi}dB_{u}

The cross term between the

(a+b)^{2}\leq(1+\Delta_{i})a^{2}+\frac{2}{\Delta_{i}}b^{2}
(valid for

),

\displaystyle I_{i-1}\leq(1+\Delta_{i})\mathbb{E}\big(|F_{i}^{\pi}-F_{i}^{\pi,\theta}|^{2}\big)+2\Delta_{i}\mathbb{E}\big(|f_{i}^{\pi,\theta(i)}-\mathcal{C}^{P}(f_{i}^{\pi,\theta(i)})|^{2}\big).

(B.1)

We focus on the first term on the right hand side. For any
\epsilon>0
, using the inequality
(a+b)^{2}\leq(1+\frac{\Delta_{i}}{\epsilon})a^{2}+(1+\frac{\epsilon}{\Delta_{i}})b^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(|F_{i}^{\pi}-F_{i}^{\pi,\theta}|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big(\big|\Delta^{\theta}Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi}+\Delta_{i}[f(t_{i},Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi},\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi})-f(t_{i},Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta},\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi,\theta})]\big|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(1+\frac{\Delta_{i}}{\epsilon}\Big)\mathbb{E}\big(|\Delta^{\theta}Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi}|^{2}\big)+\Delta_{i}(\Delta_{i}+\epsilon)\mathbb{E}\big(|f(\cdot)-f(\cdot)|^{2}\big).

By the Lipschitz property of

, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(|F_{i}^{\pi}-F_{i}^{\pi,\theta}|^{2}\big)\leq\Big(1+\frac{\Delta_{i}}{\epsilon}+2\Delta_{i}(\Delta_{i}+\epsilon)

\displaystyle[f]_{L}^{2}\Big)\mathbb{E}\big(|\Delta^{\theta}Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi}|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle+2\Delta_{i}(\Delta_{i}+\epsilon)[f]_{L}^{2}\mathbb{E}\big(|\Delta^{\theta}\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi}|^{2}\big).

(B.2)

Choose
\epsilon\coloneqq\frac{1}{8L[f]_{L}^{2}}
, and assume
|\pi|\leq\min(1,\epsilon)
. Then

, and using this in (
B.2
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(|F_{i}^{\pi}-F_{i}^{\pi,\theta}|^{2}\big)\leq\Big(1+\frac{\Delta_{i}}{\epsilon}+\frac{\Delta_{i}}{2L}\Big)\mathbb{E}\big(|\Delta^{\theta}Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi}|^{2}\big)+\frac{\Delta_{i}}{2L}\mathbb{E}\big(|\Delta^{\theta}\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi}|^{2}\big).

(B.3)

Insert (
B.3
) into (
B.1
) and absorb harmless factors into a constant
C
(depending only on
L
and
[f]_{L}
):

\displaystyle I_{i-1}\leq(1+C\Delta_{i})\mathbb{E}\big(|\Delta^{\theta}Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi}|^{2}\big)+\frac{\Delta_{i}}{2L}\mathbb{E}\big(|\Delta^{\theta}\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi}|^{2}\big)+2\Delta_{i}\mathbb{E}\big(|f_{i}^{\pi,\theta(i)}-\mathcal{C}^{P}(f_{i}^{\pi,\theta(i)})|^{2}\big).

(B.4)

By Jensen and Cauchy–Schwarz, using the definition

\langle Z\rangle_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{t_{i}}\!\big[\frac{1}{\Delta_{i+1}}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}Z_{u}du\big],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(|\Delta^{\theta}\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi}|^{2}\big)\leq\frac{1}{\Delta_{i+1}}\mathbb{E}\Big(\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}|\Delta^{\theta}Z_{u}^{\pi}|^{2}du\Big).

(B.5)

Using (
B.5
) in (

\displaystyle I_{i-1}\leq(1+

\displaystyle C\Delta_{i})\mathbb{E}\big(|\Delta^{\theta}Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi}|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}\mathbb{E}\big(|\Delta^{\theta}Z_{u}^{\pi}|^{2}\big)du+2\Delta_{i}\mathbb{E}\big(|f_{i}^{\pi,\theta(i)}-\mathcal{C}^{P}(f_{i}^{\pi,\theta(i)})|^{2}\big).

(B.6)

on both sides of (

(B.8)

Summing (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}|\Delta^{\theta}Z_{u}^{\pi}|^{2}du\Big)\leq 2C\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta_{i}I_{i}+4\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta_{i}\mathbb{E}\big(|f_{i}^{\pi,\theta(i)}-\mathcal{C}^{P}(f_{i}^{\pi,\theta(i)})|^{2}\big).

Using (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}|\Delta^{\theta}Z_{u}^{\pi}|^{2}du\Big)\leq C\Big\{\mathbb{E}(\lvert\xi-\mathcal{C}^{\theta(m)}(\xi)\rvert^{2})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta_{i}\mathbb{E}\big(|f_{i}^{\pi,\theta(i)}-\mathcal{C}^{P}(f_{i}^{\pi,\theta(i)})|^{2}\big)\Big\}.

Proof.

g

\#a=M\end{subarray}}\langle g,\widetilde{h}_{a}\rangle\widetilde{h}_{a}-g\Big\rVert_{\widetilde{L}^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{n})}^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a|=n\\
\#a=M\end{subarray}}\frac{n!}{a!}\int_{E_{a}}\big|m_{a}-g(u)\big|^{2}du.

4.3

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|a|=n\\

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}\coloneqq Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}-\widehat{Y}_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta},\quad\widehat{\Delta}Z_{u}^{\pi,\theta}\coloneqq Z_{u}^{\pi,\theta}-\widehat{Z}_{u}^{\pi,\theta},\quad\widehat{\Delta}\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi,\theta}\coloneqq\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi,\theta}-\langle\widehat{Z}\rangle_{i}^{\pi,\theta},

and define

\displaystyle I_{i}\coloneqq\overline{\mathbb{E}}\Big(\lvert\widehat{\Delta}Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}+\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}\lvert\widehat{\Delta}Z_{u}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}du\Big).

Using (
B.14
), we get

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta}Y_{t_{i-1}}^{\pi,\theta}+\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\widehat{\Delta}Z_{u}^{\pi,\theta}dB_{u}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}^{\theta(i)}(F_{i}^{\pi,\theta})-\widehat{\mathcal{C}}^{\theta(i)}(\widehat{F}_{i}^{\pi,\theta}).

Squaring and taking expectations, noting that the two terms on the left-hand side are uncorrelated, and using Lemma
B.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{i-1}\leq\overline{\mathbb{E}}\big(\lvert\widehat{F}_{i}^{\pi,\theta}-F_{i}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}\big)+\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{N}\mathbf{V}_{i}(\widehat{f}_{i}^{\pi,\theta}).

(B.15)

We now expand the first term on the right hand side of the previous inequality. Using
(a+b)^{2}\leq(1+\frac{\Delta_{i}}{\epsilon})a^{2}+(1+\frac{\epsilon}{\Delta_{i}})b^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbb{E}}\big(\lvert F_{i}^{\pi,\theta}-\widehat{F}_{i}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}\big)=

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbb{E}}\Big(\big|\widehat{\Delta}Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}+\Delta_{i}[f_{i}^{\pi,\theta}-\widehat{f}_{i}^{\pi,\theta}]\big|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(1+\frac{\Delta_{i}}{\epsilon}\Big)\,\overline{\mathbb{E}}\big(\lvert\widehat{\Delta}Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}\big)+\Delta_{i}(\Delta_{i}+\epsilon)\,\overline{\mathbb{E}}\big(\lvert f_{i}^{\pi,\theta}-\widehat{f}_{i}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}\big).

Using the Lipschitz property on
f
,

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbb{E}}\big(\lvert F_{i}^{\pi,\theta}-\widehat{F}_{i}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}\big)\leq\Big(1+\frac{\Delta_{i}}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle+2\Delta_{i}(\Delta_{i}+\epsilon)[f]_{L}^{2}\Big)\overline{\mathbb{E}}\big(\lvert\widehat{\Delta}Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}\big)

\displaystyle+2\Delta_{i}(\Delta_{i}+\epsilon)[f]_{L}^{2}\overline{\mathbb{E}}\big(\lvert\widehat{\Delta}\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}\big)

(B.16)

Choose
\epsilon\coloneqq\frac{1}{8L[f]_{L}^{2}}
and assume
|\pi|\leq\min(1,\epsilon)
, so that
2\Delta_{i}(\Delta_{i}+\epsilon)[f]_{L}^{2}\leq\frac{\Delta_{i}}{2L}
. Plug (
B.16
) into (
B.15
), which gives us

\displaystyle I_{i-1}\leq(1+C\Delta_{i})\overline{\mathbb{E}}\big(\lvert\widehat{\Delta}Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}\big)+\frac{\Delta_{i}}{2L}\overline{\mathbb{E}}\big(\lvert\widehat{\Delta}\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}\big)+\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{N}\mathbf{V}_{i}(\widehat{f}_{i}^{\pi,\theta}),

(B.17)

where
C>0
is a constant depending on
[f]_{L}
and
L
. By conditional Jensen and Cauchy–Schwarz,

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbb{E}}\big(\lvert\widehat{\Delta}\langle Z\rangle_{i}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}\big)\leq\frac{1}{\Delta_{i+1}}\overline{\mathbb{E}}\Big(\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}|\widehat{\Delta}Z_{u}^{\pi,\theta}|^{2}du\Big).

(B.18)

Plugging (
B.18
) in (
B.17
) and using the mesh regularity
\Delta_{i}/\Delta_{i+1}\leq L
,

\displaystyle I_{i-1}\leq(1+C\Delta_{i})\overline{\mathbb{E}}\big(\lvert\widehat{\Delta}Y_{t_{i}}^{\pi,\theta}\rvert^{2}\big)+\frac{1}{2}\overline{\mathbb{E}}\Big(\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}|\widehat{\Delta}Z_{u}^{\pi,\theta}|^{2}du\Big)+\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{N}\mathbf{V}_{i}(\widehat{f}_{i}^{\pi,\theta}).

Hence, adding
\frac{1}{2}\overline{\mathbb{E}}\Big(\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}|\widehat{\Delta}Z_{u}^{\pi,\theta}|^{2}du\Big)
on both sides,
we obtain, for
i=1,\dots,m
,

\displaystyle I_{i-1}+\frac{1}{2}\overline{\mathbb{E}}\Big(\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}|\widehat{\Delta}Z_{u}^{\pi,\theta}|^{2}du\Big)\leq(1+C\Delta_{i})I_{i}+\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{N}\mathbf{V}_{i}(\widehat{f}_{i}^{\pi,\theta}).

(B.19)

Applying Gronwall inequality, and using that
I_{m}=\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{V}_{m}(\xi)
,

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq i\leq m}I_{i}\leq\frac{C}{N}\Big[\mathbf{V}_{m}(\xi)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta_{i}^{2}\mathbf{V}_{i}(\widehat{f}_{i}^{\pi,\theta})\Big].

(B.20)

Moreover, summing both sides of (
B.19
) over
i=1,\dots,m
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}I_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\overline{\mathbb{E}}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\Delta}Z_{u}^{\pi,\theta}|^{2}du\Big)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big(1+C\Delta_{i}\big)I_{i}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta_{i}^{2}\mathbf{V}_{i}(\widehat{f}_{i}^{\pi,\theta}).

Canceling terms, this implies

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbb{E}}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\Delta}Z_{u}^{\pi,\theta}|^{2}du\Big)\leq 2C\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta_{i}I_{i}+\frac{2}{N}\mathbf{V}_{m}(\xi)+\frac{2}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta_{i}^{2}\mathbf{V}_{i}(\widehat{f}_{i}^{\pi,\theta}).

Using (
B.20
), we conclude with

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbb{E}}\Big(\int_{0}^{T}|\widehat{\Delta}Z_{u}^{\pi,\theta}|^{2}du\Big)\leq\frac{C}{N}\Big[\mathbf{V}_{m}(\xi)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta_{i}^{2}\mathbf{V}_{i}(\widehat{f}_{i}^{\pi,\theta})\Big].

∎
