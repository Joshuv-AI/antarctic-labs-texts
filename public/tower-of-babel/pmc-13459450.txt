Hyperoxemia reveals a blood gas stability gap
Authors: Megan Palacio, Mina Ghobrial, Diana Hylton, Spencer Seely, Jeremy Armstrong, Michael J Kelner, Robert L Fitzgerald, Raymond T Suhandynata
Published: 2026

To the Editor

Accurate measurement of partial pressure of oxygen (pO2) is essential for assessing oxygenation status, adjusting fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2), evaluating ventilator or oxygenator performance, and calculating oxygenation indices [1]. However, pO2 is also one of the least stable blood gas measurands because oxygen can diffuse between whole blood and ambient air until the respective partial pressures reach equilibrium. This creates a particular concern for hyperoxemic arterial specimens, where pO2 exceeds room-air oxygen tension and delayed analysis may cause substantial downward drift.

Blood gas stability studies have provided useful guidance for routine specimens, but hyperoxemic samples remain incompletely addressed. Zavorsky and van Wijk excluded specimens with baseline pO2 >150 mmHg [2], and Kristinsdottir et al. did not examine high-pO2 specimens in their multi-analyte delay study [3], leaving a gap in pO2 stability data for patients receiving high FiO2 in the operating room, on cardiopulmonary bypass, or on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). Additionally, stability studies often lack clarity regarding whether “time zero” specimens are analyzed immediately after collection or after transport to the laboratory, a critical distinction given immediate post-collection drift. Therefore, we evaluated the stability of blood gas specimens collected in ICU Medical Pro-Vent 3 mL dry lithium heparin syringes on GEM Premier 7000 blood gas analyzers. Although the manufacturer allows for room-temperature storage for up to 30 min before analysis, we sought to assess whether this storage interval was appropriate for hyperoxemic specimens.

Residual arterial and venous whole blood from patients on cardiopulmonary bypass were analyzed immediately after collection, then serially through 90 min. Air was expelled, syringes were recapped, and specimens were gently remixed before each measurement. Baseline testing was performed at the collection site, with arterial baseline pO2 ranging from 243 to 428 mmHg, extending beyond the ranges evaluated in prior studies.

The direction and magnitude of pO2 change differed by baseline oxygen tension (Fig. 1). By approximately 30 min, the manufacturer’s limit, most hyperoxemic arterial specimens exceeded the ± 15% allowable error per CLIA [4], with a mean bias of -16.1 ± 5.6%. This suggests the manufacturer stated stability window may not be appropriate when partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood (PaO2) is used to guide decisions such as FiO2 titration, oxygenator assessment, or calculation of the PaO2/FiO2 ratio. By approximately 60 min, all hyperoxemic specimens exceeded allowable error, with relative changes of -16.4% to -28.3% and decreases of 45–121 mmHg, which are large enough to misrepresent oxygen reserve or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) severity classification. In contrast, venous specimens (baseline pO2 46–92 mmHg) demonstrated positive bias at 60 min (8.1 ± 6.3%), consistent with equilibration toward ambient oxygen tension. This bidirectional divergence – negative bias above and positive bias below 150 mmHg – reflects the direction of the oxygen partial pressure gradient between specimen and environment and was captured by a strong inverse correlation between baseline pO2 and pO2 bias (Pearson r = -0.831, p < 0.0001). Elapsed time alone was less informative, supporting baseline pO2 as the primary determinant of stability error.

These findings are consistent with the physics of gas exchange in plastic syringes and studies using tonometered hyperoxic blood [5]. Our data extend this observation into a modern clinical workflow using dry lithium heparin syringes and current instrumentation. Our study is limited by small sample size, single-center workflow, and repeated sampling from the same syringe, though air was immediately expelled and syringes recapped to minimize air exposure. Finally, the magnitude of pO2 drift observed may reflect the extreme hyperoxemia of cardiopulmonary bypass; patients with milder hyperoxemia from conventional supplemental oxygen may experience smaller but clinically relevant bias.

The clinical importance of hyperoxemic pO2 instability spans perioperative and critical care settings and may be particularly relevant in patients receiving high FiO2, including those with severe ARDS, those supported with ECMO, and mechanically ventilated patients undergoing frequent reassessment of oxygenation status. In these settings, PaO2 values are routinely used to guide FiO2 titration, assess oxygen reserve, monitor oxygenator performance, and calculate oxygenation indices such as the PaO2/ FiO2 ratio. Even in environments with nearby analyzers, transport, competing clinical priorities, and processing delays can accumulate, allowing hyperoxemic specimens to be analyzed outside of their true stability window. Because management decisions in these patients are often influenced by relatively modest changes in oxygenation metrics, artifactually low pO2 measurements resulting from delayed analysis may be misinterpreted as worsening gas exchange rather than ex vivo drift. This could prompt unnecessary increases in oxygen delivery, inaccurate characterization of ARDS severity, inappropriate concern regarding oxygenator dysfunction, or escalation of ventilatory or extracorporeal support despite stable underlying physiology. Recognition of this pre-analytical source of error may therefore be important when interpreting unexpectedly low PaO2 values obtained from delayed analyses in patients receiving high levels of oxygen support and may warrant reconsideration of specimen handling recommendations for hyperoxemic samples in these high-risk clinical settings.
