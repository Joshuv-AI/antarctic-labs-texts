Lipid Trajectory Patterns in China
Authors: Kevin S Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: cardiovascular disease, dyslipidemia, lipid trajectories

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of death worldwide, with dyslipidemia serving as one of its most modifiable drivers.1,2 Current guideline-informed clinical practice relies on lipid measurements collected at single time points that are then interpreted against static thresholds. Although risk stratification and initiation of lipid-lowering therapy in this fashion are effective in reducing long-term CVD risk and mortality, emerging evidence suggests that a significant proportion of cumulative CVD risk may be attributable to dynamic lipid trends across decades of life.3,4 In this issue of JACC: Asia, Zhang et al5 present an important contribution to this growing pool of evidence through characterization of life-course lipid trajectories in a large Chinese cohort with data from the China-PAR (Prediction for Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease Risk in China) study.

The China-PAR study is a pooled dataset of tens of thousands of participants including prospective cohorts from the China Multi-center Collaborative Study of Cardiovascular Epidemiology (MUCA), the International Collaborative Study of Cardiovascular Disease in Asia (InterASIA), and Community Intervention of Metabolic Syndrome in China & Chinese Family Health (CIMIC) studies.5,6 Its pooled design of participants drawn from multiple provinces in both urban and rural settings across China, rigorous cardiovascular outcomes adjudication through a mix of medical records and death registries, and long-term follow-up allow for population-level longitudinal analyses and have been instrumental in the development of a 10-year atherosclerotic CVD risk prediction tool that outperforms the conventional American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association pooled cohort equation in the Chinese population.6 Conversely, these same attributes introduce heterogeneity through variable measurement protocols across different studies as well as attrition bias due to the study’s longitudinal nature. The database also includes mostly middle-aged Han Chinese adults, with relatively little representation of young and older adults or other ethnic minorities.5 Finally, because the various prospective cohorts making up the China-PAR study took place over decades of prodigious change in China, over which there has been rapid variation in urbanization and modernization, air pollution, health care reform, obesity rates, and lifestyle factors, there may be secular time period effects that affect data interpretation.

Within this context, Zhang et al5 present a comprehensive analysis of repeated lipid measurements for over 83,000 adults followed longitudinally for a median of 11 years. Several key patterns should be noted. First, major atherogenic lipid fractions, including total cholesterol, low-density-lipoprotein cholesterol, and non–high-density-lipoprotein cholesterol, followed a 3-phase trajectory consisting of early life increases, midlife plateau, and modest decline in later adulthood. In contrast, triglycerides and remnant cholesterol exhibited an inverted U-shaped pattern: rising through early life before declining in later adulthood.5 Second, marked sex differences were demonstrated in lipid trajectories. Men experienced earlier peaks and plateaus, whereas women demonstrated delayed transitions (by around 13 to 17 years) followed by higher lipid levels in later adulthood.5 Notably, the age at which female lipid levels surpassed those of male lipid levels closely aligned with the menopausal transition, thus reinforcing the central role of hormonal regulation in lipid metabolism. Finally, both body mass index and waist circumference as measures of adiposity were strongly associated with adverse lipid profiles through adulthood, although the magnitude of such effects varied by age and sex. In men, the lipid-lowering benefits of adiposity reduction attenuated substantially with advancing age. In contrast, women exhibited more stable associations across the life course, with modest attenuation but persistent effects with increasing senescence.5 These findings suggest that the metabolic consequences of excess adiposity may differ fundamentally between sexes and across stages of life.

Taken together, these results highlight the importance of considering lipid trajectories rather than isolated single-point measurements when evaluating cardiometabolic risk. The concept of “window periods” may offer a useful framework for prevention in identifying life periods during which risk factors are liable to accelerate. In men, the early and middle adult years may represent a particularly important window for aggressive lifestyle interventions targeting metabolic health. Meanwhile, for women, midlife transitions surrounding menopause appear to represent the most critical period for risk stratification and risk factor optimization. These sex-specific transition periods have been alluded to in prior research and are known to be especially pronounced in patients with familial hypercholesterolemia.7,8

Beyond their clinical insights, these findings also provide important context for the evolving global epidemiology of dyslipidemia and CVD risk factors. Over the preceding decades, the absolute prevalence of CVD in Asia has risen sharply due to a combination of lifestyle factors, environmental pollution, and demographic aging.9 Nowhere has this transition been more evident than in China, where rapid economic development, urbanization, and lifestyle change have contributed substantially to rising incidence of CVD.10 China’s health care system has simultaneously undergone substantial reform during this period. Since the major national health care reforms initiated in 2009, the country has expanded primary care coverage, public health surveillance, and chronic disease management programs.11 Large-scale population cohorts such as China-PAR thus play a critical role in informing preventive strategies tailored to China’s specific regional risk profiles and will be increasingly important as the world’s largest population continues its rapid demographic and epidemiologic transitions.

As CVD prevention moves toward greater regional and demographic precision, the work put forth by Zhang et al may have far-reaching implications. It reinforces a growing body of evidence suggesting that lifetime exposure to atherogenic lipids provides incremental value beyond single midlife measurements in assessing cardiometabolic risk. Incorporating trajectory-based models and sex-specific patterns into CVD risk stratification frameworks may therefore enhance future CVD prevention guidelines, both in Asia and more broadly.
