A complex network analysis of São Paulo rail-based urban mobility from smart-card data in 2019 and 2020
Authors: M. B. Izidoro, P. H. T. Schimit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper analyses rail-based urban mobility in the São Paulo Metropolitan Region using anonymised smart card validations from the electronic ticketing system. We reconstruct estimated origin–destination flows from single-tap records and model the system as daily weighted mobility networks, where nodes represent stations and edges represent inferred passenger flows between stations. Classical and weighted network metrics, together with rank dynamics and community detection, are used to characterise the temporal evolution of rail mobility before and during the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic (2019–2020). The results reveal a sharp contraction and spatial reorganisation of the network in early 2020, with reduced system-wide efficiency, increased effective distances and stronger fragmentation into communities, consistent with more localised patterns and with survey-based evidence reported by the São Paulo Metro Company’s (Metrô) official Origin–Destination Survey 2023. The main contributions of this paper are as follows: (i) a Transaction Sequence Number-based procedure to infer inter-station OD flows from single-tap validations; (ii) the construction of daily rail-mobility networks for 2019–2020; and (iii) a network framework to track structural changes in demand, connectivity and community structure organisation over time.

Introduction

Background: human and urban mobility

Human mobility emerges from repeated decisions made by individuals under spatial, temporal, and socio-economic constraints, and therefore tends to exhibit both variability and regularity, with strong empirical evidence of scaling laws in travel distances1, recurrent visitation patterns2, and measurable limits of predictability in individual trajectories3. These regularities motivate quantitative approaches that characterise mobility through flows, distances and network representations, ranging from universal mobility and migration models4 and cross-city comparative regularities5 to data-driven frameworks that integrate heterogeneous sensing sources at urban scale6.

In parallel, the complex-network perspective has been widely adopted to model transport and mobility systems7, including classical work on efficiency and small-world structure in subway networks8, as well as more recent applications to station importance and passenger-flow networks in metro systems9. During COVID-19, high-frequency mobility data enabled detailed assessments of disruption and reorganisation in urban movement, using diverse modalities and contexts such as inter- and intra-city mobility linked to epidemic diffusion10, network-based changes in metropolitan structure11, multiscale geospatial network analyses in shared-mobility systems12, and longer-horizon spatial reconfigurations in population movement13.

Related works also include mining mobility patterns from GPS trajectories via network metrics14, methodological advances to infer demand from electronic ticketing data15,16, and network-based analyses of pandemic impacts in public transport, including recent evidence for São Paulo17,18, as well as studies linking mobility networks to vulnerability and resilience in urban rail systems19,20 and to inequality-related dynamics in mobility responses21–23. A review of recent advances connecting mobility data, models and real-world applications can be found in24.

A complex-network perspective represents an urban transport system as a graph in which locations are nodes and interactions between them are edges, allowing mobility to be analysed as a structured set of connections rather than as isolated counts7. In public transport, this representation can incorporate both the physical infrastructure (stations and track connectivity) and the realised mobility demand (passenger flows), with edge weights capturing the intensity of travel between pairs of nodes; classic results illustrate how network concepts such as efficiency can be used to characterise subway systems beyond purely topological descriptions8.

Network models are useful because they provide a unified framework to quantify connectivity, concentration, and redundancy through well-established metrics, and to detect modular organisation such as communities that often correspond to sub-regions or corridor-based structures7. Moreover, when networks are constructed at high temporal resolution, they enable the tracking of how effective connectivity and station prominence evolve, for example through passenger-flow networks and station-importance rankings9 or through time-varying assessments of vulnerability that jointly consider topology and demand20. In this sense, network analysis complements traditional mobility indicators by linking demand to topology and by offering interpretable measures of fragmentation, efficiency, and resilience in public-transport systems, including during shocks such as the COVID-19 pandemic11,17.

From this perspective, public-transport systems are often studied with complementary graph abstractions: (i) infrastructure graphs (stations/lines and their physical adjacency), and (ii) demand graphs (OD flows, typically weighted), sometimes using alternative “spaces” (e.g., stop-to-stop adjacency vs. route-based connectivity) to capture transfers and functional proximity. Within this research field, early subway/metro studies used real systems to discuss efficiency and “small-world-like” connectivity, and to compare how large systems differ structurally8,25. A second research line focuses on vulnerability/robustness: how failures or targeted disruptions propagate through transfer stations and bridges, and how centrality metrics help identify critical nodes for operations and contingency planning14,26,27. Authors also focus on scaling/regime-change perspectives in rail systems, e.g., transitions between small-world and fractal-like organisation28, and the more recent shift toward multilayer/multiplex modelling to represent multimodality (rail, bus, etc.) explicitly, enabling richer resilience and accessibility analyses29. Finally, network structure is also considered to planning questions (design, connectivity trade-offs, competition/coordination effects), where analytical results on the structure of transit networks provide useful baselines for interpreting observed metro graphs30.

The São Paulo Metropolitan Region concentrates one of the largest urban populations in Latin America and relies on a multi-operator metro-rail system as a backbone for high-capacity mobility. Rail-based travel is provided by the urban metro network (Metrô) and the suburban/commuter rail network (CPTM), complemented by concessionary operators (ViaQuatro and ViaMobilidade) that run specific lines under the same integrated fare environment. This system connects the historical centre to major business and service hubs and, crucially, to peripheral areas characterised by long daily commutes and strong peak-hour demand.

The main resource for strategic decisions and planning in São Paulo’s public-transport system is the Pesquisa Origem–Destino do Metrô (Metrô Origin–Destination Survey)31, conducted by the Metrô. This survey is a large-scale household survey covering the São Paulo Metropolitan Region, organised into Origin–Destination (OD) zones that constitute the smallest geographical unit with statistical representativeness. The survey design combines a technical zoning scheme with a probabilistic sampling plan, and the household sample is drawn via stratified sampling in five household-income strata, yielding a target sample of about 32,000 valid households drawn from a federal census-based address registry. In fieldwork, a larger pool of households is contacted to achieve the planned valid sample; interviews are then conducted (tablet-based, structured questionnaire) with all residents aged 12+ (or their guardians), collecting (i) detailed trip information for the working day preceding the interviewer visit and (ii) socioeconomic attributes of the household and individuals. After fieldwork, data are subjected to consistency checks and georeferencing, and are expanded through appropriate weighting (including imputation procedures when needed) to produce OD matrices and indicators representative at the OD-zone level. The most recent survey was conducted in 2023 (OD 2023), and its results were published in 202531.

In terms of main findings, OD 2023 estimates that the São Paulo Metropolitan Region performed about 35.6 million daily trips, with 70.5% by motorised modes and 29.5% by non-motorised modes. Compared to 2017, OD 2023 reports a 15% reduction in total daily trips, driven by declines in both motorised trips (-11.1%) and non-motorised trips (-23.3%); within motorised travel, the drop is markedly concentrated in collective transport (-19.8%, about 3 million fewer trips/day), while individual transport changes only marginally (-0.9%). Consistently, the overall mobility index decreases from 2.02 to 1.68 trips/person between 2017 and 2023 (about -17%), with the largest relative reductions associated with collective and non-motorised travel. OD 2023 also highlights that trips internal to each sub-region remain predominant (about 86% of total trips), and spatial indicators point to a reduction of high-density trip areas (e.g., zones above 300 motorised trips/ha), especially in the more central parts of the São Paulo Metropolitan Region. Finally, OD 2023 includes pandemic-related questions, showing that the modes most frequently abandoned during the pandemic period were collective ones, including metro and metropolitan rail.

Traditional Origin–Destination surveys provide a comprehensive and socioeconomically rich picture of urban mobility, but their periodic and sample-based nature limits their ability to monitor short-term fluctuations and abrupt shocks31,32. In contrast, smart card (ticketing) data offer continuous, high-frequency observations of realised public-transport usage, enabling fine-grained monitoring of temporal variability and network-level reorganisation in transit systems9,15,16,33. This paper investigates how network modelling built from smart card records can reveal structural patterns and potential inequalities in rail mobility in São Paulo, particularly across the disruption induced by COVID-19. The main contributions of this paper are: (i) a Transaction Sequence Number-based procedure to reconstruct approximate origin–destination flows from single-tap validations; (ii) the construction of daily rail-mobility networks for 2019–2020; and (iii) a network-level characterisation of the system before and during the pandemic shock.

While OD 2023 provides a multi-modal and socioeconomically detailed snapshot of mobility based on household-reported trips (with statistical expansion from a stratified sample and OD-zone aggregation), our approach is operational and rail-focused, built from anonymised smart card validations observed at high temporal resolution. In practical terms, the OD survey directly reports trip purposes, socioeconomic profiles and full trip chains across modes, whereas ticketing records capture realised usage of the metro-rail system without user attributes and without explicit alighting information, requiring OD inference from sequential validations based on the Transaction Sequence Number (TSN). Conversely, the proposed framework delivers daily origin–destination networks and network metrics that allow continuous monitoring of structural changes and disruptions (e.g., the COVID-19 shock) at the level of stations and corridors, which is beyond the temporal granularity of periodic survey waves.

Our methodological novelty lies in bridging the gap between single-tap smart card records and interpretable, time-resolved OD representations of rail mobility in the São Paulo Metropolitan Region. Because the ticketing system registers validations rather than complete trip chains, OD information is not directly observed; we therefore introduce a TSN-based reconstruction procedure that infers approximate OD flows from sequences of validations, producing a weighted demand graph for each day. This daily network perspective enables the system to be analysed not only in terms of aggregate trips but also in terms of its evolving structure, using classical complex-network metrics such as weighted degree/strength, betweenness and closeness centrality, shortest-path-based efficiency, clustering, assortativity, modularity and community structure, as well as rank dynamics of station prominence. By tracking these measures day by day across 2019–2020, the proposed framework reveals changes that are difficult to detect from totals alone: in particular, it captures the abrupt disruption in the first months of the COVID-19 pandemic, when the network contracts sharply.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. “Data and study area” section describes the study area and the smart card dataset used in the analysis. “Methods” section presents the methodological framework, including pre-processing, TSN-based reconstruction of OD flows, network construction and the set of network indicators. “Results” section reports the results on demand evolution, rank dynamics, spatial contraction, temporal trajectories of network metrics, and community patterns. “Discussion” section discusses the main findings, their interpretation and limitations, and their relation to survey-based evidence. Finally, “Conclusions and future work” section concludes the paper and outlines directions for future work.

Data and study area

The São Paulo Metropolitan Region is the largest metropolitan area in Brazil, comprising 39 municipalities and about 20.7 million inhabitants. Rail-based public transport plays a central role in this urban context by providing high-capacity corridors that connect the historical centre to major employment and service hubs and to peripheral areas with long commuting patterns. The metro-rail system is operated by multiple companies under an integrated fare environment: the Companhia do Metropolitano de São Paulo (Metrô) operates Lines 1–Azul, 2–Verde, 3–Vermelha and 15–Prata (63 stations and about 71.4 km of track), while Line 4–Amarela is operated under concession by ViaQuatro (11 stations, 12.8 km) and Line 5–Lil’as by ViaMobilidade (17 stations, about 20.1 km). In the metropolitan-rail layer, CPTM operates Lines 7–Rubi, 10–Turquesa, 11–Coral, 12–Safira and 13–Jade (about 196 km and 57 stations), while ViaMobilidade operates Lines 8–Diamante (41.6 km and 22 stations) and 9–Esmeralda (37.3 km and 21 stations). In this paper, the empirical focus is on rail-based mobility captured through station-level interactions (metro and metropolitan rail), which allows the analysis to be expressed as daily weighted networks whose nodes are stations and whose edges represent inferred passenger flows between stations.

The datasets used in this study came from different operational systems and were first organised into the same analytical structure. Validation records from the TOP system were extracted from Autopass operational databases, stored in a MySQL environment, in a transactional table. Bilhete Único records were obtained separately from SPTrans in QVD format, converted to CSV, and then inserted into the same analytical environment and into the same transactional table used for the TOP data. This integration was possible because the systems share project identifiers and because Autopass maintains the control layer that integrates the devices used in the rail mode. In this layer, important identifiers, such as station and line codes, are standardised through mapping tables, which define the correspondence between codes from different systems. After this step, the harmonisation consisted of aligning field names, data types, timestamp formats, station identifiers, and modal classifications. Station and line codes were also consolidated using a unified reference table, ensuring consistency across operators. Once the schema was aligned, all records were consolidated into a single database and the same preprocessing rules were applied to all sources. These rules included the removal of operational tests, duplicate records, invalid entries, and transactions not associated with rail-based transport. The final integrated table contained the harmonised validation records. From this table, a second table was created to store the inferred origin–destination pairs in a normalised format, obtained from validation sequences ordered by the TSN. This structure allowed indexing by anonymised card identifiers and consistent temporal ordering, supporting the reconstruction of OD flows for the full dataset.

The observation window covers the period from January 2019 to December 2020. For each validation, the fields used include: (i) date and time of validation; (ii) station/validator identifier; (iii) mode and line/service information; (iv) an anonymised card identifier; and (v) the Transaction Sequence Number (TSN), which provides an ordering of successive uses of the same card. All records are anonymised and the raw dataset is not publicly available.

Ticketing data provide high temporal and spatial granularity, enabling daily monitoring of rail usage and facilitating the construction of time-resolved mobility networks. At the same time, the data record only entries (validations) and do not include explicit exits (alighting), so origin–destination flows must be inferred from successive validations (here using TSN). Moreover, the records are limited to observed usage in the formal ticketing system and do not directly represent walking, private vehicles, or informal modes, and they do not contain trip purposes or socio-demographic information about users. For these reasons, ticketing-based network reconstructions should be interpreted as an operational, rail-focused picture of realised mobility demand. They therefore complement, but do not replace, periodic OD surveys: surveys provide multi-modal trip chains and socioeconomic context, whereas ticketing data provide continuous, high-frequency observations that support the identification of short-term fluctuations, seasonal patterns, and abrupt structural changes in rail mobility.

Ethics and privacy

This study analysed secondary smart-card validation records provided by AutoPass and SPTrans in a de-identified form for research purposes. The datasets contained no personal identification information (e.g., names, national IDs, addresses, phone numbers) and only included pseudonymous card identifiers; the linkage key (if any) remained with the data providers and was not available to the authors. No attempt was made to re-identify individuals, and all analyses were conducted at the level of stations, OD pairs, and aggregated network measures.

One author (M.B. Izidoro) is employed by AutoPass; however, he has no access to any personal customer information for this research. The dataset used in this study was provided as a de-identified research extract, and neither author had access to any direct identifiers or to any re-identification key. The data handling and analysis procedures are consistent with Brazil’s General Data Protection Law (Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados Pessoais, LGPD; Law No. 13,709/2018).

Methods

This section describes the process used to transform raw electronic ticketing validations into daily rail-mobility networks and to quantify their temporal evolution through standard complex-network indicators. The steps follow a sequential workflow: (i) pre-processing and cleaning of the validation records, (ii) reconstruction of approximate OD flows using the TSN, (iii) construction of one weighted network per day, and (iv) computation of network-level metrics and complementary descriptive analyses (rank dynamics, visualisations, and period comparisons).

From raw validations to rail OD pairs

The raw dataset consists of anonymised validation records from the electronic ticketing system. Since the objective is to characterise rail-based mobility, we first filter the database to include only validations associated with the metro-rail operators (Metrô, CPTM, ViaQuatro and ViaMobilidade) that are recorded under the same ticketing infrastructure.

Next, we remove non-representative and inconsistent records. In particular, we discard operational test validations, corporate/operational cards, duplicated transactions generated by retransmission or reprocessing, and records with missing or invalid modal identifiers, station/validator identifiers, or timestamps. After these filters, each remaining record represents one individual entry validation in the metro-rail system and contains the fields required for OD inference: date/time, station/validator identifier, modal/line information, anonymised card identifier, and the TSN.

All extraction, filtering, and aggregation procedures are implemented in Python, querying a MySQL database that stores the raw validations (partitioned by month for efficiency). The processing produces daily tables of validated transactions, indexed by anonymised card ID and ordered chronologically.

During the study period (January 2019 to December 2020), the Electronic Fare Collection System recorded a total of 4.79 billion validated trips across all public transport modes in the São Paulo Metropolitan Region. Of these, approximately 1.40 billion trips were performed on the metro-rail system (Metrô, CPTM, ViaQuatro and ViaMobilidade), corresponding to about 29.2% of all recorded journeys. This study analyses the full set of metro-rail validations observed in this period, ensuring complete coverage of rail-based mobility demand.

After applying this procedure, the origin–destination inference retained 41.6% of metro-rail validations in 2019 and 45.6% in 2020. The records not retained are mainly card-day sequences with only one validation, incomplete chains, or trips that are not followed by another rail validation on the same day. In these cases, the destination cannot be inferred from the available single-tap data. Therefore, this reduction is a limitation imposed by the ticketing system, and not simply a data-quality filtering step. The retained records represent the part of rail mobility for which consecutive validations allow an inter-station movement to be inferred. This may introduce bias if single-tap patterns differ across user groups or trip purposes, including multimodal trips, one-directional rail use, and essential or discretionary travel. Even so, the large number of retained OD pairs and the consistency of the temporal patterns suggest that the reconstructed flows capture the main structural organisation of metro-rail mobility, although they should not be read as a complete diary of all trips.

The São Paulo ticketing system records entries but does not provide explicit exit information. To approximate OD flows from single-tap data, we use the Transaction Sequence Number, which orders the successive uses of the same card.

For each anonymised card and for each day t, validations are sorted by TSN, with the timestamp used as a consistency check, producing a sequence\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {TSN}_{t,1}< \text {TSN}_{t,2}< \text {TSN}_{t,3} < \ldots$$\end{document}Consecutive validations within the same day are then mapped into inferred OD pairs. If a card validates at station x and later validates at station y on the same day, we treat (x, y) as an approximate OD movement, assuming that the second validation is the next observed use of the rail system by that card.

Operationally, the reconstruction is done at the level of anonymised card-day sequences. If a card has m \ge 2 valid metro-rail validations on the same day, ordered by TSN and checked against the timestamps, the procedure generates m-1 inferred OD pairs from consecutive validations. For example, a sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x \rightarrow y \rightarrow z$$\end{document} contributes the pairs (x, y) and (y, z). Card-day sequences with only one validation do not contain a subsequent observed station from which a destination can be inferred. These records are therefore not used in the construction of OD pairs, and no destination is imputed for them.

To avoid spurious links, OD reconstruction is restricted to pairs that do not cross midnight, so cross-day TSN continuations are ignored. Self-pairs are also removed: if two consecutive validations occur at the same station, the implied self-loop is discarded. This procedure does not try to reconstruct the actual route taken between x and y. Instead, it provides an approximation of inter-station movements that can be used for network modelling at the system level.

Thus, the reconstructed OD matrix should not be read as a complete diary of all trips, but as the part of mobility that can be inferred from consecutive validations. The method may underrepresent one-directional rail uses, trips followed by another mode, or trips without a subsequent rail validation. For this reason, the inferred OD flows are interpreted as an operational approximation of station-to-station interactions derived from the available single-tap ticketing records.

The outcome of this step, for each day t, is an inferred OD flow table that counts how many consecutive-validation pairs are observed between each station pair.

The daily window was adopted as the basic temporal unit because one day is a natural scale for analysing urban transport, especially for commuting and other recurrent activities. This choice may miss some intra-day patterns, such as morning and evening peaks, but using shorter windows or fixed time-gap thresholds would require additional assumptions about when a movement should be considered complete. Since the interval between two rail validations may vary across users and trip purposes, a fixed threshold could remove valid movements separated by work, study, leisure, or other activities. On the other hand, exploratory tests using weekly or monthly aggregation produced highly connected networks, making the graph metrics less informative. Daily aggregation was therefore adopted as a compromise between temporal resolution and network interpretability. Possible limitations of the TSN-based rule include missed one-directional trips, multimodal continuations without a later rail validation, and valid movements separated by long intervals.

Network construction

A public transport system can be represented as a graph (network) whose structure summarises the connectivity and intensity of mobility flows. Formally, a (simple) graph is a pair G=(V,E), where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V=\{1,\dots ,n\}$$\end{document} is the set of nodes and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E\subseteq \{\{i,j\}: i\ne j\}$$\end{document} is the set of (undirected) edges. In mobility applications, nodes typically represent stations (or spatial aggregates such as neighbourhoods), and edges encode the existence of passenger flows between them. When the intensity of interaction matters, the graph is naturally weighted: each edge e∈ E carries a nonnegative weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_e$$\end{document} measuring the volume of flow associated with that connection.

In this work we reconstruct, for each day t, an origin–destination (OD) flow matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F^{(t)}=[f^{(t)}_{ij}]$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f^{(t)}_{ij}\ge 0$$\end{document} is the inferred number of movements from node i to node j on day t. Self-loops are removed by construction, i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f^{(t)}_{ii}=0$$\end{document}, so that the analysis focuses on inter-station mobility.

From \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F^{(t)}$$\end{document} we build an undirected weighted graph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_t=(V,E_t,w^{(t)})$$\end{document}. For each unordered pair of stations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{i,j\}$$\end{document}, the edge weight is defined as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w^{(t)}_{ij}=f^{(t)}_{ij}+f^{(t)}_{ji}.$$\end{document}Thus, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{i,j\}\in E_t$$\end{document} whenever \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w^{(t)}_{ij}>0$$\end{document}. In other words, the graph preserves the total volume of inferred interaction between two stations, but not the direction of that interaction.

In practice, one network \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_t$$\end{document} is generated for each day of the study period. This daily construction allows the analysis of fine temporal variability (weekday/holiday effects, seasonal patterns, and abrupt disruptions) while keeping the spatial resolution at the station level. When comparisons across broader windows are required (e.g., year-to-year contrasts or month-level rank summaries), daily networks and their associated quantities are aggregated by the corresponding period.

The use of undirected graphs is a methodological choice consistent with the objective of this paper, which is to describe the daily structure of realised station-to-station interaction in the rail system. The indicators analysed below focus on overall connectivity, effective distances, clustering, fragmentation, assortativity, and community structure. For these analyses, the total intensity of interaction between two stations is more relevant than the direction of each inferred movement. Moreover, since OD flows are inferred from consecutive single-tap validations, directional counts may be affected by incomplete daily chains and by the separation between outward and return movements. Aggregating both directions therefore gives a conservative representation of the effective interaction structure.

This choice also has limitations. The undirected representation does not capture directional imbalance, source–sink relations, or asymmetric commuting flows. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as describing the structural organisation of station-pair interactions, rather than the full directional dynamics of travel demand. A directed-network analysis would be a natural extension for future work.

Temporal segmentation and special periods

The analysis covers the full interval from January 2019 to December 2020. To support interpretation, we consider 2019 as a pre-pandemic baseline and 2020 as the pandemic-affected year, while retaining the day-by-day resolution of the networks.

In addition to year-level comparisons, we highlight specific windows that are informative for the COVID-19 shock. In particular, we perform a focused comparison of March 2019 versus March 2020, which captures the onset of behavioural and operational changes during the early phase of the pandemic. This comparison is implemented both at the network-metric level (daily trajectories and period averages) and at the edge level through spatial visualisations of relative changes in link weights.

Network metrics

For each daily graph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_t$$\end{document}, we compute a set of network-level indicators that capture: (i) total transported volume and link activation, (ii) weighted interaction around stations, (iii) effective-distance structure and efficiency, (iv) local cohesion, (v) modular fragmentation via communities, and (vi) mixing patterns. Metrics are computed daily, producing time series that support period comparisons and the identification of structural regime changes. In total, we evaluated on the order of 70 candidate network measures (spanning node/edge-level aggregations, weighted and unweighted variants, distance-based and community-based diagnostics, and alternative normalisations), and we retained the subset reported below because it best distinguished the periods while remaining stable under day-to-day variability and robust to changes in network density and total weight.

For path-based computations we define a cost (effective distance)1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} c^{(t)}_{ij} \;=\; \frac{1}{w^{(t)}_{ij}+\varepsilon }, \qquad \varepsilon >0, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}so that larger flows correspond to smaller traversal costs. Given these costs, shortest paths are computed via Dijkstra’s algorithm. When the undirected graph is disconnected, distances and path-based statistics are computed on the largest connected component \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_t'=(V_t',E_t')$$\end{document}34–36. In all analyses, we used \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon =10^{-12}$$\end{document}. This smoothing term is used only to avoid numerical problems and is several orders of magnitude smaller than the positive OD weights analysed here. Therefore, it does not affect the ordering of edge costs or the qualitative behaviour of the path-based metrics.

Edge-volume and connectivity. The total transported volume encoded by the network is summarised by the total weight2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} W_G(t) \;=\; \sum _{\{i,j\}\in E_t} w^{(t)}_{ij}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}As a purely topological measure of how many connections are active on day t, we use the undirected density3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} D_G(t) \;=\; \frac{2|E_t|}{n(n-1)}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}which is the fraction of realised edges among all possible node pairs35,37.

Strength and weighted interaction. In weighted mobility networks, the analogue of degree is the node strength. For each node i∈ V, define4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} s^{(t)}_i \;=\; \sum _{j:\,\{i,j\}\in E_t} w^{(t)}_{ij}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}We then consider the average strength5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} S_G(t) \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum _{i\in V} s^{(t)}_i, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}which captures the typical daily flow intensity incident to a station35,38.

Path structure and efficiency. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d^{(t)}_c(i,j)$$\end{document} denote the shortest-path distance between i and j in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_t$$\end{document} when edge lengths are given by the costs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c^{(t)}_{ij}$$\end{document}. The (cost-based) average shortest path length is6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} l_G(t) \;=\; \frac{1}{|V_t'|(|V_t'|-1)} \sum _{\begin{array}{c} i,j\in V_t'\\ i\ne j \end{array}} d^{(t)}_c(i,j), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}so that smaller values indicate that high-flow induce shorter effective distances across the network.

Complementarily, we compute the global efficiency7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} E_G(t) \;=\; \frac{1}{n(n-1)} \sum _{\begin{array}{c} i,j\in V\\ i\ne j \end{array}} \frac{1}{d^{(t)}_c(i,j)}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}with the convention that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1/d^{(t)}_c(i,j)=0$$\end{document} for disconnected pairs (i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d^{(t)}_c(i,j)=\infty$$\end{document}). This metric increases when a large fraction of node pairs can exchange flow through low-cost (high-volume) routes39.

Triadic closure. To quantify the tendency of flows to form locally cohesive structures, we use the mean weighted clustering coefficient8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} C_G(t) \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum _{i\in V} C^{(t)}_{i,w}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C^{(t)}_{i,w}$$\end{document} is the weighted clustering coefficient of node i (computed using the standard weighted generalisation). Intuitively, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_G(t)$$\end{document} is higher when strong flows tend to close triangles, indicating locally tight station triplets38,40.

Community structure. We detect communities by maximising the weighted modularity via a greedy agglomeration procedure41, yielding a partition \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{ \mathcal {C}^{(t)}_1,\dots ,\mathcal {C}^{(t)}_{K_t}\}$$\end{document} of V. The metric9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} K_G(t) \;=\; K_t \end{aligned}$$\end{document}is the number of detected communities, providing an indicator of how fragmented (large \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_G(t)$$\end{document}) or integrated (small \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_G(t)$$\end{document}) the network appears under this criterion42.

Mixing patterns. Finally, we quantify degree mixing through the degree assortativity coefficient. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_i$$\end{document} be the (unweighted) degree of node i in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_t$$\end{document}. The assortativity10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} A_G(t) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}is the Pearson correlation of degrees at the two ends of an edge (with edge contributions weighted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w^{(t)}_{ij}$$\end{document}, as in the implementation used here). Positive values indicate that highly connected stations tend to connect to other highly connected stations, while negative values indicate disassortative mixing (hubs preferentially connected to low-degree nodes)34,43.

Together, the set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{W_G(t),D_G(t),S_G(t),l_G(t),E_G(t),C_G(t),K_G(t),A_G(t)\}$$\end{document} provides a compact summary of how daily rail mobility evolves: from overall demand and link activation, to effective-distance structure, local cohesion, mesoscale fragmentation, and mixing tendencies across stations.

Rank dynamics, visualisation, and comparison with the OD 2023

Beyond network-level summaries, we analyse how the relative prominence of stations and OD pairs evolves over time by building monthly Top-k rankings based on inferred station activity (entries/exits in the OD-pair representation), OD-pair volumes, and station utilisation along shortest paths under the effective-cost definition \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c^{(t)}_{ij}$$\end{document}. These rank trajectories are visualised with bump charts, allowing us to identify persistent hubs, emerging stations, and re-ordering patterns associated with the pandemic period. To localise structural changes in space, we also produce edge-level maps in which colour encodes the relative change in link weights between two periods (e.g., March 2019 vs March 2020) and line thickness represents the absolute magnitude of change, complementing the time series of metrics by showing where contractions or increases concentrate. Finally, we interpret the ticketing-based network results in light of the official São Paulo Metro Origin–Destination survey (OD 2023): since the survey is declaratory and socio-demographic while ticketing data are operational and entry-based, we use OD 2023 as contextual evidence for qualitative consistency (e.g., overall reduction, localisation, and spatial reorganisation), framing the network approach as a complementary tool for continuous monitoring of rail mobility rather than as a full reconstruction of metropolitan OD matrices.

Results

This section presents the empirical patterns extracted from the daily rail-mobility networks reconstructed from smart card data over 2019–2020. We first describe the system-level evolution of demand and of the spatial scale of trips, using time series of trip counts, mean travel distance, distance distributions, and station-level entropy. We then analyse how the relative prominence of stations and OD pairs changes over time through monthly rank dynamics (bump charts), and we localise the initial COVID-19 disruption by comparing edge-level flows in March 2019 versus March 2020. Next, we illustrate representative daily networks to support the interpretation of the quantitative indicators. Finally, we report the temporal trajectories of network metrics, from total weight and density to effective-distance measures, clustering, community fragmentation, and assortativity, and we conclude with the corresponding community structure and its spatial patterns.

Spatial contraction of the network: March 2019 vs March 2020

To complement the system-level indicators, we analyse the disruption at the corridor scale by comparing edge weights between matched calendar windows. Specifically, we compute, for each network segment, the relative change in inferred passenger flow between March 2019 and March 2020 and project these changes onto the geographical rail layout. This edge-level view makes it possible to identify where demand contraction concentrates spatially, and whether the shock acts as a fairly uniform scaling of flows or instead induces a heterogeneous reallocation across corridors.

Figure 7 summarises these edge-wise differences. Colours encode the relative change (blue for increases, red for decreases), while line width reflects the magnitude of the change (as represented in the figure). By contrast, several peripheral branches exhibit lighter shades and thinner segments, suggesting smaller absolute (and often moderate relative) decreases away from the core. Topological connectivity also contracts: density \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_G(t)$$\end{document} declines in 2020, suggesting that fewer station pairs remain connected by positive inferred flows on a typical day. Local cohesion measured by the mean weighted clustering coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_G(t)$$\end{document} shows a moderate reduction, consistent with the weakening of triadic flow structures. The number of detected communities \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_G(t)$$\end{document} increases, indicating a more fragmented structure in which flows are more readily partitioned into relatively separated groups.

Importantly, the more negative values observed in 2020 relative to 2019 are consistent with an increase in network heterogeneity: as demand contracts and many weaker links disappear, the remaining OD structure becomes more strongly organised around a core–periphery pattern, in which stations with fewer active connections (plausibly in peripheral areas) are disproportionately linked to a smaller set of highly connected hubs. This interpretation is coherent with the spatially uneven contraction evidenced by the edge-level heatmap (where the largest absolute decreases concentrate on major central corridors) and with the entropy-based evidence discussed above, which indicates a redistribution of origin-flow diversity across stations towards a more locally structured and less uniformly spread pattern.

The selected examples (holidays and working days, and distinct months) also illustrate an important point: even when the overall OD network looks broadly similar from one day to another, the modularity-based partitions can still exhibit subtle differences in boundaries and memberships, including between days that are structurally very different in demand (e.g., 1 January versus typical working days). Despite the day-to-day variability, Fig. 10 consistently reveals persistent community “blocks” that are spatially coherent. In particular, a prominent block emerges on the eastern side of the system, indicating a relatively cohesive subnetwork in which OD flows are dense internally and comparatively weaker across its boundary. This visual pattern is consistent with the rank-dynamics evidence in the bump charts (“Rank dynamics of stations and OD pairs” section), which highlighted the prominence of stations and OD pairs concentrated in the eastern part of the city, and it supports the interpretation of high local mobility intensity within that area.

The visual comparison is also supported by the Jaccard values computed with respect to the reference partition. The Jaccard index measures the overlap between two sets, defined as the size of their intersection divided by the size of their union. In this case, it compares the stations in a community on the reference date with the stations in the closest community on each analysed date. When the southeastern sector appears as a separate community, its similarity with the reference southeastern module is higher, with values around 0.56–0.63. When the central and southeastern sectors are assigned to the same community, these values decrease to about 0.20–0.31. This suggests that, on these dates, the southeastern sector is not simply displaced in the partition, but is partly absorbed into a broader central community. The central module is more persistent, with intermediate Jaccard values, although its composition also changes when the two sectors merge.

In this sense, the community plots complement the bump-chart analysis by showing where high-volume rank persistence translates into spatially clustered mesoscale structure, and by providing a qualitative bridge to the metric-based results discussed in the temporal trajectories section.

Consistency with survey-based evidence

The network-based findings are consistent with the main qualitative messages reported by the official Origin–Destination (OD) survey for the São Paulo Metropolitan Area. In particular, our sharp contraction of rail demand in early 2020 aligns with the survey’s broader evidence of a downward shift in daily mobility after the pandemic period: OD 2023 documents a reduction in total daily trips relative to the previous survey wave, with the largest losses concentrated in collective transport. This supports the interpretation that the collapse captured by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_G(t)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_G(t)$$\end{document} reflects a genuine system-level shock rather than an artefact of rail-only measurement.

However, the main value of the ticketing-based framework is precisely what the OD survey cannot deliver. First, by operating at daily resolution, the inferred graphs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_t$$\end{document} allow the disruption to be dated, quantified, and decomposed into phases (onset, stabilisation, partial recovery) and to reveal strong day-of-week and holiday effects that are intrinsically smoothed out in periodic household surveys. Second, the OD survey provides OD matrices and indicators at the level of OD zones with statistical representativeness, but it cannot resolve station-to-station interactions and corridor-level re-weighting at scale; in contrast, our edge-level comparisons (Fig. 7) identify which rail segments lose (or preserve) flow intensity, and the rank dynamics reveal how station and OD-pair prominence is redistributed over time. Third, the survey captures “how much” people travel and “by which mode” together with rich socioeconomic context, but it does not yield a direct, high-frequency structural description of effective connectivity: the cost-based metrics (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l_G(t)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_G(t)$$\end{document}), the fragmentation signatures (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_G(t)$$\end{document}), and the localisation diagnostics derived from distance ratios and station-level entropies (Figs. 3 and 4) provide interpretable measures of network reorganisation that can be monitored continuously and compared across short windows.

In practical terms, OD 2023 is best interpreted as a socio-spatial baseline and a multi-modal benchmark, while the ticketing-derived networks provide an operational “monitoring layer” for the metro-rail backbone: they make it possible to track where and when mobility becomes more localised, how the effective-distance backbone is reshaped, and how prominence shifts across stations and corridors during shocks. Used jointly, the two sources support both interpretability (through OD-zone socioeconomics and trip purposes) and high-frequency structural diagnosis (through \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_t$$\end{document} and its time-varying metrics), which is essential for understanding the temporal anatomy of disruptions and the form of subsequent recoveries.

It is important to note that OD 2023 does not capture the immediate mobility shock observed in 2020. The official Origin–Destination surveys conducted by Metrô are traditionally carried out every ten years. After OD 2017, the next survey would normally be expected in 2027, but it was anticipated to 2023 because of the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on urban mobility. Therefore, OD 2023 should be interpreted as a post-pandemic reference for the broader reorganisation of mobility in the São Paulo Metropolitan Region, rather than as a direct measure of the abrupt disruption observed in 2020. In this sense, the comparison with OD 2023 is used only as contextual evidence, and the agreement between the two sources should be understood qualitatively.

Discussion

The 2019 baseline suggests a comparatively regular aggregate regime: although individual trajectories are heterogeneous, system-level signals remain stable and largely reflect calendar rhythms. This is consistent with core empirical findings in human-mobility research, where individual movement exhibits strong regularities (recurrence to a limited set of locations) and aggregate patterns remain reproducible32,2,3. In 2020, this regime is disrupted by a sharp contraction and an incomplete recovery, indicating a change not only in the amount of mobility but also in its organisation. The simultaneous decline in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_G(t)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_G(t)$$\end{document}, the reduction in density \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_G(t)$$\end{document}, the weakening of efficiency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_G(t)$$\end{document}, and the increase in effective distances \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l_G(t)$$\end{document} together indicate fewer and weaker interactions and a reduced ability of remaining flows to sustain low-cost connectivity across the rail system. From a mobility-theory point, this is consistent with reduced spatial reach and exploration under constraints, with travel concentrated on essential activities and corridors32,1–3. Importantly, rank dynamics and corridor-level changes indicate that the system does not simply scale down uniformly: the functional backbone is reshaped, echoing evidence that shocks alter where mobility concentrates and how connectivity is redistributed44,11,13,12.

Recent studies on São Paulo during the COVID-19 pandemic provide useful context for our results. Li et al. showed that the effects of COVID-19 in São Paulo were strongly associated with socioeconomic inequalities, including differences in the ability to reduce mobility and self-isolate45. Pérez-Martínez et al. related COVID-19 deaths in São Paulo to commuting time, public transport use, demographic density, income, and air pollution, indicating that long-distance daily travel and the unequal urban structure were relevant factors during the pandemic46. More directly related to public transport, Goulardins et al. showed that many workers in São Paulo continued to work in person during the pandemic and mainly used public transport for commuting. They also reported an association between public transport use and walking as part of the commuting trip47. These findings are consistent with broader evidence from Brazil showing that the pandemic changed urban mobility profiles and preferences, especially through a reduction in public transport use and an increase in the relative importance of private and active modes48. Taken together, these studies support the interpretation that the disruption observed in 2020 was not only a reduction in total mobility, but also a spatially and socially uneven reorganisation of movement. Our rail-based network results add to this discussion by showing how this disruption appears in the internal structure of station-to-station interactions.

Distance-distribution contrasts and origin-level entropy shifts further support an interpretation of increased localisation and modularity. Classic results show that mobility is not well described by memoryless diffusion; rather, constrained exploration and strong recurrence shape population flows1–3. Under disruption, these constraints can intensify, producing a contraction of long-distance OD interactions and a reweighting towards repeated, nearby destinations. In our results, increased fragmentation (higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_G(t)$$\end{document}) and reduced efficiency are consistent with a more partitioned functional structure. This aligns with network-based evidence that pandemic shocks reorganise urban movement patterns heterogeneously across space and activity types, and that mobility predictability varies over time under policy and behavioural drivers22,23,44. From a transit-network perspective, the findings also complement recent vulnerability work emphasising that performance depends on fixed topology and time-varying demand, so that station importance and system degradation can change nonlinearly during disruptions20,27,49.

These structural changes have implications for inequalities and socio-spatial segregation in a metropolis where peripheral residence and long commutes are central. While ticketing data lack socio-demographics, the observed spatial heterogeneity suggests that reductions are uneven across corridors. This is consistent with studies linking mobility changes to socioeconomic factors and showing that network roles (hubs/bridges) shift differently across communities, potentially exacerbating pre-existing disparities22,23. Localisation does not imply improved equity: if opportunities remain unevenly distributed, reduced system-wide connectivity may disproportionately affect populations reliant on long rail trips for access to jobs and services. The persistence of negative assortativity indicates that hub-to-periphery organisation remains structurally relevant even during disruption, consistent with weighted-network perspectives on transport systems34,38.

Methodologically, the main contribution is the construction of daily rail-mobility networks from single-tap validations using a TSN-based OD inference procedure, enabling high-frequency tracking of abrupt changes and recovery. This approach is aligned with the established potential of AFC and e-ticketing data to infer OD flows and support planning when combined with inference rules and quality checks15,16. The network representation complements standard ridership series by providing interpretable structural indicators and linking aggregate changes to station- and corridor-level shifts, consistent with the broader tradition of analysing metro systems as complex weighted networks8,25,49. The use of performance-aware measures such as efficiency and effective distance also follows the principle that transport analysis should go beyond topology alone39. The convergence of multiple outputs (demand signatures, distance changes, rank dynamics, corridor maps, and network trajectories) supports the robustness of the main conclusions, though sensitivity analyses to OD pairing rules and thresholds remain a natural extension15,16,24.

Limitations include the inference nature of OD flows under single-tap systems, the absence of socio-demographic and trip-purpose attributes, the rail-only scope (with potential multimodal substitution), and restricted data availability for external replication15,16. Even so, the framework has practical implications: daily indicators could support operational dashboards to detect regime changes, characterise recovery, and identify corridors with faster contraction or rebound. This aligns with work arguing that dynamic demand must be incorporated into vulnerability and resilience assessments20. Finally, combining high-frequency ticketing-based diagnostics with periodic survey evidence (e.g., OD 2023) can strengthen interpretation by joining socio-spatial context and trip purposes with timely structural monitoring31.

Conclusions and future work

This paper proposed and applied a framework to characterise rail-based urban mobility in the São Paulo Metropolitan Region using smart-card validations. From anonymised single-tap records, we reconstructed approximate origin–destination flows via the Transaction Sequence Number (TSN) and built one weighted rail-mobility network per day for 2019–2020. We then combined demand and distance signatures, station-level entropy, rank dynamics (stations and OD pairs), corridor-level spatial comparisons, and time series of network measures capturing connectivity, efficiency and effective distance, clustering, fragmentation and assortativity, following established approaches that use AFC/e-ticketing data for OD inference and operational analytics15,16 and the broader tradition of analysing metro systems as complex networks8,25,49.

The results show a clear structural break in early 2020: an abrupt contraction of rail demand followed by a slow and incomplete recovery, consistent with the widespread mobility collapse observed under COVID-19 interventions11–13. Beyond volume, the rail system undergoes a reorganisation of effective connectivity: density and efficiency decline, effective distances increase, and fragmentation strengthens, indicating more localised and partitioned mobility. Such changes align with the idea that mobility under shocks becomes more constrained and recurrent, with altered predictability and spatial reach1–3,44. Rank dynamics and corridor maps further show that the disruption is spatially heterogeneous, reshaping the relative importance of stations and OD pairs rather than producing a uniform scaling, in line with network-based evidence that the pandemic reconfigured hubs, bridges, and connectivity patterns in urban systems20,22. These network findings are qualitatively consistent with survey-based evidence on reduced metropolitan mobility and sharp declines in collective transport reported in OD 2023. However, OD 2023 reflects a post-pandemic mobility pattern and should not be interpreted as a direct measure of the 2020 shock. In this sense, ticketing-based networks complement OD surveys by providing daily indicators that capture short-term variability and abrupt shocks31.

The comparison between 2019 and 2020 should be interpreted with caution because the available smart-card dataset does not include an additional pre-pandemic baseline year processed under the same procedure. Thus, the observed differences cannot fully separate COVID-19 effects from ordinary year-to-year variability. However, the changes reported here are abrupt, concentrated in 2020, and temporally aligned with the onset of the pandemic. The OD 2017 and OD 2023 surveys provide a broader reference, showing mobility changes over a multi-year interval in the São Paulo Metropolitan Region31,50. In this sense, our results are better interpreted as evidence of a marked disruption in rail-based mobility during 2020, rather than as a complete causal attribution of all 2019–2020 differences to COVID-19.

From a planning perspective, the proposed indicators provide a compact monitoring layer for metro-rail operations. Daily networks quantify not only how much demand changes but also how the spatial organisation of flows shifts, through metrics such as total weight, density, average strength, cost-based average shortest path length, global efficiency, mean weighted clustering coefficient, number of communities, and degree assortativity. In practice, this supports early detection of disruptions, identification of corridors with disproportionate contractions or faster recoveries, and assessment of whether the system is becoming more fragmented or less efficient in connecting stations, complementing work that highlights the joint role of fixed topology and time-varying demand in vulnerability and resilience20,39. The results also speak to equity and accessibility: if contraction and fragmentation disproportionately weaken cross-subregion connectivity, users reliant on long commutes may face stronger constraints. While smart-card data lack socio-demographics, structural proxies, such as increases in effective distance and loss of cross-community connectors, can be monitored alongside external evidence linking mobility disparities to socioeconomic factors22,23.

Future work can strengthen both behavioural interpretation and policy relevance. A first direction is to incorporate additional modes (especially buses) to build multimodal or multilayer mobility networks, capturing substitution patterns and cross-modal dependencies7,29. A second direction is to adopt temporal and dynamic-network methods (e.g., change-point detection on network metrics, temporal community detection, and dynamic centrality) to better model evolving interactions under disruptions42,51. Third, integrating socio-economic and land-use indicators at station-catchment scale would enable a more direct assessment of inequalities and differentiated impacts23,52. Finally, comparative replications across cities and longer time windows, plus formal sensitivity analyses of TSN-based OD inference (pairing rules, time-gap thresholds, partial validation when available), would improve robustness and help separate system-specific effects from more general mobility mechanisms15,16,24.
