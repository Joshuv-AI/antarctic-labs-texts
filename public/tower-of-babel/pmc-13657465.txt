Epidemiologic and clinicopathologic characteristics of nasopharyngeal carcinoma at a tertiary care center in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Abdullah O. Alqthmi, Renad H. Alosaimi, Rawaf K. Altowairqi, Sali A. Sayed, Zainab A. Alalqam, Ahmed A. Bahaj, Haddad Hussain Alkaf, Jabir Alharbi, Sherif Abdelmonim, Hani Elkhatib, Faisal Albadainah, Maha Alidrisi, Mohammed Y. Almaghrabi, Sahal Alzahrani, Ameen Alherabi, Mohammed A. Alessa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is rare globally; however, in Saudi Arabia, it is the most common head and neck cancer and the 17th most common cancer overall. This study aimed to describe the epidemiological characteristics, clinical features, and histopathological subtypes of NPC in patients at a tertiary care center in Saudi Arabia.Methods:This retrospective cohort study included 244 patients with histologically confirmed nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) managed at a tertiary referral hospital between January 2014 and June 2024. Demographic characteristics, clinical presentation, risk factors, histopathology, Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) status, and treatment modalities were extracted from medical records and analyzed using SPSS version 23.Results:Among the 244 patients, 75% were male and 25% were female, with the most affected age groups being 50–64 years (39%), followed by those aged ≥65 years (30%). neck mass was the most common presenting symptom (31%). Histopathologically, undifferentiated carcinoma (WHO Type III) was predominant (77%). Most patients were diagnosed at an advanced stage, with 20% at stage III and 56% at stage IV. Smoking, EBV infection, and demographics showed no significant association with any histopathological subtype. Concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) was the most common treatment modality (37%) followed by induction chemotherapy with CCRT (31%).Conclusion:In Saudi Arabia, NPC predominantly affects older males, with neck masses being the most frequent symptom. Smoking and EBV infection showed no significant association with histopathological subtypes. Most cases were undifferentiated carcinomas (WHO Type III) and diagnosed at advanced stages, highlighting the need for continued surveillance and multicenter studies in Saudi Arabia.

Introduction

Cancer is a global health concern and is the leading cause of mortality in the industrialized world and the second leading cause of death in the developing world[1]. The number of cancer-related deaths in Saudi Arabia has consistently increased over the past 25 years, from only 5% in 1990 to 12% in 2016[2]. Head and neck cancers, including nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), are among the most common cancers in Saudi Arabia. It is defined by malignant epithelial cell proliferation in the nasopharynx, which is located posterior to the nasal cavity and superior to the oropharynx.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), NPC is classified histologically into three types as follows: keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma (Type I), differentiated non-keratinizing carcinoma (Type II), and undifferentiated carcinoma (Type III)[3]. NPC is considered a rare disease in several communities worldwide, with incidence rates below one per 100 000 individuals[4]. However, it is common in certain regions with an incidence rate of 20 per 100 000 individuals per year. These regions include southern China, where the incidence is the highest, as well as other regions such as Southeast Asia, North Africa, and the Middle East. In Saudi Arabia, NPC ranks first among all head and neck cancers and 17th among all cancer types, with an incidence rate of 0.8 per 100 000 individuals[3,4].

Furthermore, the crude mortality rate of NPC in Saudi Arabia is 0.5 per 100 000 individuals, ranking second-highest among countries in the Middle East and North Africa[3].

The etiology of NPC is believed to be multifactorial, and the disparity in the geographical distribution of NPC incidence suggests the influence of various environmental and genetic factors. These risk factors include Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection, particularly with elevated antibody titers against EBV, salt-preserved fish consumption, tobacco smoking, chronic respiratory tract conditions, family history of NPC, and specific human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class I genotypes[5–7]. The use of herbal medicine and occupational exposure to fumes, smoke, dust, or chemicals have been investigated, although findings in these areas have been inconsistent[8].

This study aimed to describe the characteristics of patients with NPC, including relevant demographic data, clinical manifestations, histological subtypes, risk factors, and treatment modalities, as observed at a tertiary referral center in Saudi Arabia.

This study has been reported in line with the 2025 STROCSS guidelines[9].

No artificial intelligence (AI) tools were used in the preparation of this manuscript.

Methods

Study Design and Population: This single-center, observational, retrospective cohort study involved a chart review of all eligible patients treated between January 2014 and June 2024. Patients were included if they had a histologically confirmed diagnosis of nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), were undergoing or had completed treatment, and had sufficient clinical data available for analysis. Patients were excluded if clinical data were insufficient or if they had a diagnosis other than NPC.

Study Procedure: Data were collected on patient demographics (age, sex, and nationality); clinical history (chronic diseases and presenting symptoms); histopathological classification; EBV immunohistochemistry; and treatment modalities.

Data were extracted from the hospital’s information system and individual patient files. Subsequently, data were recorded in an Excel spreadsheet. To ensure patient confidentiality, unique codes were assigned to medical record numbers (MRNs), and access to documents linking the original MRNs to their corresponding codes was strictly limited to the research team.

Statistical Analysis: Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. For outcome analysis, the chi-square test was used for categorical variables that met the test assumptions, and Fisher’s exact test was applied when the expected cell count was less than 5. Statistical significance was set at a P-value of <0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS software, version 23.

Discussion

NPC is one of the most common head and neck cancers. Our findings revealed that NPC was more prevalent in males, with a male-to-female ratio of 3:1, which is like the results of other studies[4]. These differences may be attributed to variations in exposure to environmental risk factors such as tobacco use and dietary habits. Furthermore, at the genetic level, X-chromosome variations may play a role in NPC progression[3].

Our results indicate that the incidence of NPC increases with age, particularly after the age of 50 years. This observation is in contrast with earlier findings from a local study that reported a peak in the incidence of NPC during the fifth decade of life[10]. A potential contributing factor is the cumulative effect of long-term exposure to environmental and lifestyle risk factors that may increase the likelihood of genetic alterations leading to cancer[1].

Additionally, the majority of patients (84%) in our cohort reported no family history of cancer, which suggests no evident association between family history and the development of NPC, a finding that contrasts with previous literature indicating a clear risk of developing NPC in individuals with a first-degree relative diagnosed with nasopharyngeal or any other head and neck malignancy[3,5]. However, other studies have indicated that familial aggregation may not be a predominant risk factor in all populations. A study comparing head and neck cancer subtypes revealed that only 14.3% of NPC patients had a positive family history, which was significantly lower than in other subtypes, suggesting that environmental and viral factors, such as environmental exposures and EBV infection, may play a more substantial role in NPC pathogenesis[11]. These findings highlight the complexity of NPC etiology and suggest that the influence of family history may vary across different populations and environmental contexts.

The clinical symptoms of NPC are influenced by the stage and extent of the tumor[12]. In our study, neck mass was the most prevalent symptom, which is in line with existing research. For instance, Alsafadi et al[4] emphasized that a neck mass was the most common presenting symptom in 84% of patients diagnosed with NPC at the Princess Norah Oncology Center in Jeddah.

In our study, the majority of patients were diagnosed with stage III or IV disease, which is consistent with the findings reported by Al-Safadi et al[4] and Al-Rajhi et al[13]. Similarly, Zhang et al reported that 70% of the patients were diagnosed with stage III or IV disease[14]. The propensity for late-stage diagnosis of NPC stems from the anatomical inaccessibility of the nasopharynx, where tumors commonly originate in the fossa of Rosenmüller, thereby impeding early detection and intervention[15]. These findings highlight the need for prompt diagnostic protocols and enhanced public awareness of the subtle and asymptomatic onset of NPC. This is particularly crucial given the favorable treatment outcomes associated with early-stage detection, with studies indicating a 5-year disease-specific survival rate of up to 97.3% for stage II disease[16].

In Saudi Arabia, a significant increase in NPC occurrence has been observed over the past few years, likely due to several risk factors, such as smoking habits[3]. Our results revealed that no significant association was observed between smoking and NPC, as most patients (76%) were non-smokers, which is consistent with the existing literature. However, Jia and Qin[17] reported that the association between tobacco use and NPC incidence is more pronounced in low-risk populations than in high-risk populations, which aligns with our findings[17].

The International Agency for Research on Cancer has classified EBV as a confirmed etiological factor for several human malignancies, including NPC. Almost all patients with advanced NPC demonstrate the presence of EBV in their cancer cells, which contributes to disease development[18]. However, in our cohort, only 76 of the 244 patients underwent EBV immunostaining, of whom 34 (14% of the total cohort) tested positive for EBV. This limited sample (31% of the cohort) substantially reduces statistical power and introduces the risk of selection bias, so the observed EBV prevalence may not be representative of the full population; consequently, our ability to draw robust or generalizable conclusions about EBV as an etiological factor in this cohort is weakened, and these findings should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should aim for EBV testing in all cases or a prospectively defined, consecutive sample and include complementary molecular assays (e.g., EBER in situ hybridization or PCR) in larger, preferably multicenter cohorts to validate these results.

In our study, most cases were undifferentiated or poorly differentiated carcinomas (WHO Type III). This aligns with previous literature, which emphasizes that Type III (undifferentiated carcinoma) is the most common histopathological subtype, resembling high-incidence regions such as China and South Korea in histopathological profile[3–5]. Moreover, smoking was not associated with the development of any histopathological type, which is consistent with a previous meta-analysis noting that smoking has a stronger association with differentiated NPC (Type I) and low-risk populations, such as the United States and parts of Europe, rather than with high-risk populations, where Type III is more common[3]. Interestingly, despite the lack of an overall association, most smokers in our study were classified within the Type III subgroup.

A key limitation of this study was its retrospective design. This was partly mitigated by the inclusion of a relatively large series from a single tertiary care center in Saudi Arabia; nevertheless, EBV immunostaining was performed in only 76 of 244 patients (31%), which reduces statistical power and risks selection bias. Future prospective, multicenter studies across Saudi Arabia with comprehensive EBV testing are needed to confirm and generalize the epidemiological characteristics of NPC.

Conclusion

This retrospective cohort study described the epidemiological characteristics of patients with NPC at a single tertiary center in Saudi Arabia. The findings revealed that NPC was more common among older males, suggesting that age and sex may be contributing factors. A neck mass was the most common presenting symptom. Notably, no significant associations were found between smoking, EBV infection, and the development of NPC subtypes, which contrasts with some existing literature. Undifferentiated or poorly differentiated carcinoma (Type III) was the most prevalent histopathological subtype, particularly among smokers. Although this was a single-center study, future multicenter studies across different regions of Saudi Arabia are recommended to validate these findings and further investigate related risk factors.
