Evaluating the efficacy and feasibility of manual digital meibomian gland compression in meibomian gland dysfunction
Authors: Jingru Wang, Xianxian Luo, Ling Zhang, Wenjian Shi, Feng Zheng, Yingying Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the therapeutic efficacy and clinical feasibility of manual digital meibomian gland compression compared to instrument-assisted mechanical compression in patients with meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD).MethodsMedical records of 70 MGD patients treated between January and December 2022 were retrospectively analyzed. Participants were divided into manual (n = 35) and mechanical compression groups (n = 35). Primary outcomes included Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) scores and ocular surface parameters at 2-week follow-up (14 ± 2 days), and 8-week follow-up (56 ± 2 days). Secondary outcomes assessed manipulation pain intensity (Visual Analogue Scale, VAS), treatment duration, and learning curve length.ResultsBoth groups showed significant improvements in OSDI scores, gland plugging, Schirmer I test, and non-invasive break-up time (NIBUT) at both follow-ups (all P < 0.05). The manual group demonstrated earlier meibum quality improvements (2-week follow-up and 8-week follow-up, P < 0.05) compared to mechanical group’s 8-week follow-up improvement (P = 0.01), along with superior lid margin regularity at 8-week follow-up (P = 0.04). Significant intergroup differences favored manual compression in gland plugging (P = 0.02), meibum quality (P = 0.03), and NIBUT (P < 0.001) at 8-week follow-up. Manual compression consistently yielded lower pain scores (all P < 0.001) and required fewer training cases (13 vs. 20).ConclusionsManual digital compression offers effective MGD management with enhanced patient comfort and faster clinical proficiency compared to mechanical methods. Its demonstrated therapeutic advantages support broader clinical implementation.Keywords: Meibomian gland dysfunction, Dry eye disease, Gland compression, Ocular surface, Treatment efficacy

Introduction

Meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) stands as a prevalent chronic ocular disorder, primarily characterized by the obstruction of meibomian gland ducts and alterations in the quality or quantity of their secretions [1]. Recognized as the leading cause of evaporative dry eye disease, the global prevalence of MGD exhibits substantial variation across populations, with epidemiological studies reporting rates ranging from 3.6% to 71.0% [2–8]. While clinical management strategies encompass a combination of physical therapies, topical medications, and artificial tear supplementation, mechanical expression of meibum remains a cornerstone intervention in addressing this condition [9].

Although advancements in technology have introduced sophisticated therapeutic options such as thermal pulsation systems [10], their widespread clinical adoption is hindered by factors including considerable costs, technical complexities, and limitations in patient accessibility. These constraints underscore the continued relevance of traditional mechanical compression techniques utilizing specialized instruments—a practice that persists as a common element of routine ophthalmic care despite the evolution of treatment modalities [11].

Manual digital meibomian compression, a manual technique involving the application of finger pressure without the use of auxiliary instruments, represents an alternative approach that has received limited research attention. Despite its occasional use in clinical practice, the therapeutic potential of manual compression remains poorly defined in comparison to instrument-based methods.

Therefore, this study aims to conduct a comprehensive comparison between manual and mechanical compression modalities, evaluating therapeutic outcomes, technical feasibility, and patient comfort to provide an evidence-based foundation for clinical decision-making in the management of MGD.

Methods

Study design and population

The analysis included adults (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with MGD [1] who completed eight weekly treatment sessions. Exclusion criteria encompassed concurrent ocular pathologies (e.g., pterygium, cicatricial pemphigoid), lid malpositions, lacrimal disorders, systemic autoimmune diseases (e.g., Sjögren’s syndrome), prior ocular surgery, contact lens use, pregnancy, and incomplete treatment courses.

Clinical management protocol

During the initial six-month phase (January-June 2022), mechanical gland compression using specialized instruments was standard practice. Operational challenges including prolonged procedure duration, patient discomfort, and limited secretion visualization prompted a protocol revision. From July 2022, manual digital compression—manual gland expression using fingertips—was systematically implemented.

Participants were stratified into two cohorts based on treatment chronology: instrument-assisted mechanical compression group (n = 35) and manual digital compression group (n = 35). All patients underwent tobramycin eye drops (Tobrex®) administered twice 5 min preoperatively. Post-intervention care included daily warm compresses, preservative-free artificial tears (Tears Naturale®) four times daily, and nocturnal tobramycin-dexamethasone ointment (TobraDex®), with intraocular pressure monitoring biweekly. Topical steroid use was discontinued if intraocular pressure exceeded 21 mmHg.

Manual gland compression was conducted under slit-lamp visualization without anesthetic agents. The clinician positioned the index finger on the upper eyelid and thumb on the lower eyelid, applying controlled manual pressure to approximate the tarsal plates. This maneuver induced mechanical depression of the meibomian glands, followed by sequential compression from the fornix toward the lid margin to facilitate meibum expression (Fig. 1A and B). The technique leveraged tactile feedback to optimize secretion evacuation while maintaining direct visualization of glandular output.

For mechanical compression, topical anesthesia was administered using 0.1% tetracaine (two instillations at 5-minute intervals). Specialized meibomian forceps were then applied with one paddle contacting the tarsal conjunctiva and the opposing paddle positioned externally. Gradual handle pressure generated targeted glandular compression across five to six strategic lid zones per eyelid, ensuring comprehensive meibum extraction from both upper and lower lids (Fig. 1C).

All procedures were performed by a single board-certified ophthalmologist to eliminate inter-operator variability. The manual method capitalized on anatomical precision through bimanual manipulation, enabling real-time adjustment of pressure vectors based on secretion dynamics. Conversely, mechanical compression relied on instrument-mediated force application, requiring precise spatial alignment of forceps paddles relative to glandular architecture. Both techniques maintained strict aseptic protocols throughout implementation.

The study will be conducted as a retrospective analysis over a period of 8 weeks. Temporal measurements spanned baseline (visit 0), intermediate (2-week follow-up), and endpoint (8-week follow-up) evaluations.

Outcome measurements

The evaluation protocol incorporated multidimensional outcome measures to assess therapeutic efficacy and procedural characteristics. Primary endpoints focused on ocular surface rehabilitation, quantified through the Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) questionnaire and slit-lamp examinations at 2-week follow-up, and 8-week follow-up. Clinical grading scales evaluated four morphological parameters [12–15]: lid margin irregularity (0–4), vascular engorgement (0–4), gland orifice plugging (0–4), and meibomian gland dropout (meiboscore 0–6). Total meiboscores derived from combined upper/lower eyelid assessments were complemented by chromatic and viscosity-based meibum quality scoring, with heterogeneous secretions classified according to the most pathological presentation (Fig. 2).

Secondary analyses addressed operational practicality through three metrics: patient-reported pain intensity measured immediately post-procedure via 100-mm visual analogue scale (VAS) [16], total procedural duration (not including anesthesia time), and operator learning curves quantified through cumulative summation (CUSUM) modeling [17]. The CUSUM algorithm plotted sequential case numbers against operation time deviations, with curve-fitting optimization guided by coefficient of determination (R²) maximization and statistical significance thresholds (p < 0.05). Proficiency benchmarks were identified at CUSUM inflection points. All inspections and assessments were performed by the same evaluator, who was not the ophthalmologist involved in the prior procedure.

Statistics analysis

Demographic profiles, systemic comorbidities, and bilateral ocular parameters were extracted from institutional electronic records, with right-eye data selected for analysis given bilateral symmetry. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR), categorical data as counts/percentages. The choice between parametric and non-parametric tests was based on the evaluation of normality assumptions using the Shapiro-Wilk test and homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test. Longitudinal comparisons employed repeated-measures ANOVA for normally distributed parameters, supplemented by Friedman and Mann-Whitney U tests for nonparametric data. Intergroup differences were considered significant at p < 0.05. Post-hoc power analysis was used to evaluate the power of statistical tests.

Results

Participant characteristics

The study enrolled 70 patients with MGD, evenly distributed between manual (n = 35) and mechanical (n = 35) compression groups. Baseline demographic parameters including age, sex distribution, and systemic comorbidities demonstrated no intergroup differences (all P > 0.05). Initial ocular surface characteristics (OSDI scores, meibum quality, and gland morphology metrics) showed comparable profiles across both cohorts (Table 1). Post-hoc power analysis showed that the statistical power for this comparison was 0.823, indicating that the sample size was sufficient to support our study.

Therapeutic efficacy outcomes

Meibum quality showed divergent progression patterns: manual compression induced progressive improvements at both 2-week follow-up (mean difference: 0.457, 95% CI: 0.216 ~ 0.698, P < 0.001) and 8-week follow-up (mean difference: 1.057, 95% CI: 0.757 ~ 1.357, P < 0.001) intervals, whereas mechanical methods only achieved significance at 8-week follow-up (mean difference: 0.486, 95% CI: 0.170 ~ 0.802, P = 0.004). Objective metrics including Schirmer I test (tear production) (manual group: 2-week follow-up: mean difference: -1.686, 95% CI: -2.335~-1.036, P < 0.001, 8-week follow-up: mean difference: -2.400, 95% CI: -3.146~-1.654, P < 0.001; mechanical group: 2-week follow-up: mean difference: -1.486, 95% CI: -2.164~-0.807, P < 0.001, 8-week follow-up: mean difference: -1.971, 95% CI: -2.586~-1.356, P < 0.001) and non- invasive breakup time (NIBUT) (manual group: 2-week follow-up: mean difference: -1.759, 95% CI: -2.230~-1.288, P < 0.001, 8-week follow-up: mean difference: -6.311, 95% CI: -6.890~-5.731, P < 0.001; mechanical group: 2-week follow-up: mean difference: -1.596, 95% CI: -2.176~-1.016, P < 0.001, 8-week follow-up: mean difference: -2.817, 95% CI: -3.338~-2.296, P < 0.001) exhibited comparable enhancements across groups (Table 2).

Comparative performance analysis

At the 8-week follow-up, manual compression demonstrated superior efficacy to mechanical methods across three critical metrics: achieving greater glandular patency (plugging: mean difference: -0.543, 95% CI: -1.002~-0.084, P = 0.022), higher-grade meibum normalization (meibum quality: mean difference: -0.600, 95% CI: -1.128~-0.072, P = 0.027), and significantly prolonged NIBUT (mean difference: 3.531, 95% CI: 2.574 ~ 4.489, P < 0.001). No intergroup differences emerged in vascular engorgement(visit 0: mean difference: -0.143, 95% CI: -0.507 ~ 0.222, P = 0.431; 2-week follow-up: mean difference: -0.200, 95% CI: -0.541 ~ 0.141, P = 0.242; 8-week follow-up: mean difference: -0.143, 95% CI: -0.498 ~ 0.212, P = 0.419), meiboscore(visit 0: mean difference: 0.000, 95% CI: -0.707 ~ 0.707, P = 1.000; 2-week follow-up: mean difference: 0.000, 95% CI: -0.672 ~ 0.672, P = 1.000; 8-week follow-up: mean difference: -0.057, 95% CI: -0.797 ~ 0.683, P = 0.876), or corneal fluorescein staining(visit 0: U = 606.500, P = 0.932; 2-week follow-up: U = 612.500, P = 1.000; 8-week follow-up: U = 577.500, P = 0.599). Meibomian gland dropout remained stable throughout the study, suggesting neither technique reversed gland atrophy (Table 2).

Procedural metrics and safety

Manual compression demonstrated superior patient tolerability, with mean VAS pain scores 62% lower than mechanical methods at all timepoints (Fig. 3, Visit 0: mean difference: -15.143, 95% CI: -21.174~-9.111, P < 0.001; 2-week follow-up: mean difference: -13.200, 95% CI: -19.071~-7.329, P = < 0.001; 8-week follow-up: mean difference: -12.543, 95% CI: -18.299~-6.787, P = < 0.001). Both techniques showed progressive pain reduction over treatment courses (8-week follow-up: manual group: mean difference: 5.171, 95% CI: 4.603 ~ 5.739, P < 0.001; mechanical group: mean difference: 7.771, 95% CI: 6.981 ~ 8.562, P < 0.001 ).

Operational efficiency analysis revealed distinct learning curves (Fig. 4). Manual compression achieved proficiency after 13 cases (cubic model: R²=0.988, P < 0.001), compared to 20 cases for mechanical methods (R²=0.992, P < 0.001). A total of five treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) were documented during the study. In the mechanical group, three cases of bacterial conjunctivitis were reported. Based on the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) [18], these cases were likely classified as Grade 2 (moderate) events, as they required medical intervention with topical antibiotic therapy but were not considered severe or life-threatening. The management involved a standard course of antibiotic treatment, and all cases resolved without sequelae. In the manual group, two patients developed subconjunctival hemorrhages. These were assessed as Grade 1 (mild) events due to their self-limiting nature, causing no or minimal discomfort without needing specific treatment beyond reassurance, and they resolved spontaneously within one to two weeks. Importantly, no episodes of high intraocular pressure or other vision-threatening complications.

Discussion

This study compared manual and mechanical meibomian gland compression for chronic MGD. Both methods significantly improved subjective symptoms (OSDI scores) and objective signs (gland plugging, NIBUT, Schirmer I test) over eight weeks. Early intervention effects (2-week follow-up) manifested primarily in subjective symptom relief (OSDI) and tear secretory function (Schirmer I). Delayed morphological changes (lid regularity, meibum quality) became statistically significant by 8-week follow-up, particularly in the manual cohort. These findings confirm that gland compression effectively clears obstructions and enhances meibum expression, thereby improving the ocular surface environment in MGD patients. Meanwhile, this biphasic response pattern suggests initial therapeutic effects target glandular patency, with structural remodeling requiring sustained intervention.

Despite the demonstrated efficacy of both techniques, our data reveal specific advantages associated with manual compression. It yielded a superior rate of normalization in meibum quality and uniquely produced a significant improvement in lid margin regularity by the end of the treatment period, suggesting potential benefits in restoring meibum properties and addressing chronic lid margin changes. Critically, patient-reported pain was significantly lower with manual compression, enhancing patient tolerance and likely adherence, which are crucial for managing this chronic condition.

A distinct advantage of manual meibomian gland compression lies in its seamless integration into the clinical workflow, typically performed by the ophthalmologist during slit-lamp examination. This approach allows direct, real-time observation of expressed meibum and gland patency, facilitating immediate, personalized adjustments [19, 20]. In contrast, mechanical compression is often delegated, relying on subsequent reports rather than firsthand data [21], potentially affecting the refinement and immediacy of therapeutic adjustments.

Participants presented with long-standing MGD, having experienced persistent symptoms despite prior conventional treatments. The introduction of meibomian gland compression resulted in noticeable symptom relief, validating its role, particularly when standard therapies prove insufficient. Considering the collective evidence—especially the superior outcomes for meibum quality, lid margin regularity, and patient comfort—the results suggest manual compression’s overall therapeutic effect may be better than that of conventional mechanical compression for MGD.

Several factors may contribute to explain the observed superiority of manual compression. The force applied through the finger pulp is likely distributed more evenly across the tarsal plate, potentially conforming better to the natural curvature of the eyelid and reducing localized pressure points. Additionally, the natural warmth of the operator’s finger mayprovide supplementary thermal effects, possiblyaiding in softening viscous meibum—though this remains speculative and requires further empirical validation. Importantly, the significantly lower pain associated with manual compression enhances patient tolerance and compliance, which likely supports treatment adherence and overall efficacy.

It is noteworthy that no significant changes were detected in meibomian gland dropout or lid margin vascular engorgement during the observation period. This aligns with the understanding that gland dropout is generally an irreversible structural change resulting from chronic obstruction and atrophy [22]. While this short-term study did not show structural reversal, clearing the glands through compression theoretically alleviates intraglandular pressure, potentially slowing further gland loss long-term. Lid margin vascular engorgement primarily reflects underlying inflammation [23]. Since compression mainly addresses physical obstruction, its direct impact on inflammation is limited. These findings suggest supplementary anti-inflammatory therapy may be necessary for MGD patients with significant inflammatory signs. Additionally, since patients with Sjögren’s syndrome were excluded from this study, corneal staining scores were already minimal at baseline, and thus, no statistically significant changes were observed in either group following treatment.

Regarding patient experience, the significantly lower pain scores in the manual group likely stem from differences in force application. Manual compression allows for broader contact, more uniform pressure, and better tactile feedback compared to the potentially higher, less precisely controlled focal pressure from forceps. An interesting finding was the significant reduction in reported pain levels over time in both groups. This suggests that as treatment progressed, the continued expression of meibum increased ductal patency, making subsequent compressions less forceful and less painful, consistent with observed improvements in meibum quality.

All meibomian gland massage procedures in this study were performed by a single board-certified ophthalmologist. This approach enhanced the internal consistency of the research and ensured the standardization of interventions, allowing for a clearer evaluation of the intervention’s intrinsic effects. However, it also introduced operator dependence. To minimize operator dependence introduced by having a single ophthalmologist perform all meibomian gland massages (which otherwise enhanced internal consistency and intervention standardization), we utilized learning curve analysis. This method quantified the reliance of the technique’s outcomes on the operator’s experience, transforming an abstract bias into a measurable and manageable parameter.

The CUSUM of the learning curve consists of three phases: the initial learning phase, competence acquisition, and mastery [24]. This study found that the peak points of the CUSUM curves for the manual and mechanical groups occurred at the 13th and 20th procedures, respectively, indicating that manual meibomian gland expression can be mastered more quickly compared to device-assisted expression. This difference may be related to the need for controlling the specialized meibomian forcep in mechanical expression, as well as lower patient cooperation due to pain in the mechanical group.

Standard adjunctive therapies, including lid hygiene and warm compresses, were performed by all participants. These fundamental measures target key MGD pathogenetic factors like ductal epithelial hyperkeratosis and altered meibum viscosity. Lid hygiene helps remove debris, while warm compresses help melt altered lipids, improving flow and tear film stability [25, 26]. The observed therapeutic benefits thus represent the combined effect of compression and these essential baseline treatments.

Furthermore, patients utilized artificial tears and topical steroid eye ointments as needed. Artificial tears augment tear volume and reduce friction, while steroid ointments control inflammation [27, 28]. However, prior history indicated these measures alone were insufficient for this chronic MGD population. Given comparable baseline characteristics and standard care, the observed improvements and inter-group differences are primarily attributed to the distinct compression methods employed.

Adverse events occurred infrequently in both groups: acute bacterial conjunctivitis (mechanical group) and subconjunctival hemorrhage (manual group). Speculated causes include microtrauma from forceps increasing infection risk, or excessive force causing hemorrhage. These events underscore the importance of caution, proper technique, and adequate operator training for both methods to ensure safety.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Firstly, the study did not stratify patients based on MGD severity or specific subtypes. Consequently, detailed information regarding which specific patient profiles might derive the most benefit from manual compression remains limited. Secondary, this study compared manual compression with traditional mechanical compression. Further research could compare manual compression against more advanced MGD treatments to better ascertain its position within the evolving therapeutic landscape for MGD. Thirdly, manual meibomian gland compression is the difficult to perform in blepharoplasty patients whose eyelid tissue adhesion making it hard to evert the eyelid and perform the manual procedure. Fourthly, the follow-up period in this study was only 8 weeks and could not predict the long-term effect of treatment. Future studies with longer-term follow-up can be conducted. And finally, the nonrandomized, retrospective, single-center nature of this study introduces an inherent risk of selection bias, and limiting the findings to exploratory associations. To validate these results, future research should adopt prospective study designs, aim for larger sample sizes, and incorporate data from multiple centers to enhance the robustness and generalizability of the findings.

Conclusions

In summary, while both manual and mechanical compression effectively treat MGD, manual compression showed superior efficacy for meibum quality and lid margin regularity, along with significantly better patient tolerance due to less pain. Manual compression is a practical, cost-effective technique integrating assessment and treatment, suitable for diverse clinical settings due to its simplicity and favorable profile. Although mechanical compression remains viable, the distinct advantages of the manual method warrant its broader consideration, underscoring the value of refining manual therapeutic skills in MGD care.
