Opioid Therapy Discontinuation in General Practice: Associations with Adjunct Therapies and Somatoform Pain Disorders
Authors: Sabrina Brinkmöller, Regina Poß-Doering, Cornelia Straßner, Gunter Laux
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic non-cancer pain is a common reason for consultation in primary care. Although opioid therapy may be considered a treatment option for selected patients, guideline-recommended discontinuation can be challenging for both patients and general practitioners. This study investigated predictors of opioid discontinuation in primary care.MethodsWe analyzed German claims data (2021) from patients aged ≥ 18 years with at least one oral or transdermal opioid prescription from general practitioners in the first three quarters of 2021. Exclusion criteria were malignant diseases or palliative care. Opioid discontinuation was operationalized as the absence of an opioid prescription in the fourth quarter. Multivariable logistic regression was used to examine associations between prespecified predictors and the absence of an opioid prescription in the fourth quarter.ResultsThe cohort included 94,125 patients (mean age 65 years; 62% female). Weak, long-acting opioids were most commonly prescribed. In multivariable regression analyses, adjunct therapy prescriptions were associated with higher odds of the absence of an opioid prescription (OR 1.834), whereas somatoform pain disorders (OR 0.801) and substance use disorders (OR 0.814) were associated with lower odds of the absence of an opioid prescription (all p < 0.001).ConclusionsThese findings are consistent with current guideline recommendations emphasizing the use of adjunct therapies in chronic non-cancer pain management in general practice. Caution is warranted when prescribing opioids to patients with somatoform pain disorders or concurrent substance use disorders. Further research with longer observation periods is needed to confirm these associations and better understand their underlying mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40801-026-00573-7.

Introduction

Chronic non-cancer pain (CNCP) is commonly defined as pain that persists or recurs for more than 3 months and is considered a distinct long-term condition requiring structured care [1]. Pain is a subjective experience that should be taken seriously, even in the absence of a clear somatic explanation, as clinical consequences can be substantial [2]. In general practice, chronic pain is a common reason for consultation, affecting approximately one in five patients with moderate to severe pain intensity [3, 4]. It is particularly prevalent among adults over 50 years and frequently co-occurs with mental health comorbidities such as depression and anxiety, which are relevant for both symptom burden and treatment planning [1–6]. The conceptualization of chronic pain has shifted toward a biopsychosocial framework, in which biological, psychological, and social factors shape pain experience and disability [1, 2, 7]. CNCP is a heterogeneous condition requiring multimodal management strategies, including patient education, functional goal setting, and non-pharmacological interventions [7]. Guidelines recommend structured assessment across domains and classification by pain mechanism to support targeted treatment selection [1, 8]. Within this framework, opioids may be considered in selected patients.

Although the short-term benefits of opioid therapy for CNCP are well documented, evidence beyond 3–6 months remains limited [9, 10]. Systematic reviews suggest that only a minority of patients experience a sustained clinically meaningful benefit from long-term opioid therapy (i.e., treatment durations exceeding 3–6 months), with responder rates estimated at approximately 12–30%, although the overall quality of the evidence is considered low [9–11]. Furthermore, robust data demonstrating sustained benefits of opioid therapy beyond 1 year are largely lacking [9, 10, 12]. Randomized and observational studies indicate that opioids do not provide greater improvements in function or quality of life compared with non-opioid treatments and may be associated with more adverse effects [13]. Consequently, long-term opioid therapy remains controversial owing to limited evidence of sustained benefit and potential harms [8, 9]. Several guidelines emphasize cautious and indication-based opioid use in CNCP. The German guideline “long-term use of opioids for chronic non-cancer pain (LONTS)” [8] recommends careful patient selection, goal setting focused on function, time-limited trials, and regular reassessment, including dose reduction or opioid discontinuation when benefits are insufficient or harms outweigh them [8]. Similarly, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guideline [14] highlights ongoing reassessment and collaborative opioid discontinuation when risks exceed benefits, while the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) [7] generally de-emphasizes opioids for chronic primary pain. Together, these recommendations frame opioid discontinuation as a routine component of medication management when clinical benefit is limited or risks increase [7, 8, 14]. Primary care plays a central role, as long-term CNCP management—including continuation and cessation of opioids—largely occurs in general practice [1, 15, 16]. Deprescribing is described as a structured process involving preparation, reassessment, shared decision-making, and monitoring [15, 16].

Opioid discontinuation can be challenging for both patients and clinicians, particularly after prolonged use [17]. Common barriers include fear of withdrawal, worsening pain, and loss of stability [17–19]. However, some patients report improvements in functioning and quality of life after dose reduction or discontinuation, suggesting that long-term opioid exposure itself may contribute to symptom burden [20, 21]. Reviews indicate that opioid discontinuation is often associated with stable or improved pain and functional outcomes, although evidence quality remains limited [22–24]. At the same time, risks such as adverse effects and dependence persist during ongoing therapy [9, 12]. Evidence from deprescribing research suggests that discontinuation is often feasible but requires careful follow-up owing to possible relapse [16]. Importantly, tapering context appears to influence clinical outcomes. Observational studies suggest that rapid or poorly supported dose reductions may be associated with increased risks of overdose and mental health crises in vulnerable patients [25]. These findings highlight that tapering strategies, patient context, and continuity of care are critical determinants of outcomes [25–27].

Overall, the literature highlights three key points: CNCP is prevalent and shaped by biopsychosocial factors; opioid therapy should be used cautiously with ongoing reassessment and planned reduction [7, 8, 14]; and opioid discontinuation, when structured and supported, is often not associated with worsening pain but may carry risks in certain contexts [22–27].

Against this background, it is important to better understand factors associated with successful opioid discontinuation of general practitioner (GP)-prescribed opioid therapy in CNCP. Identifying predictors of opioid discontinuation in routine primary care data may help to target supportive interventions. This study, therefore, aims to identify predictors of opioid discontinuation among patients with CNCP receiving opioid therapy in general practice in Germany.

Methods

Context

This analysis is part of the Resource-Oriented Case Management to Implement Recommendations for Patients with Chronic Pain and Frequent Analgesic Use in General Practice (RELIEF) project [28], funded by the German Federal Ministry of Research, Technology and Space (formerly BMBF/now BMFTR; grant number 01GY2106). The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Heidelberg University’s Medical Faculty (S-087/2023), and the German Federal Office for Social Security authorized the use of claims data. Both authorities confirmed that the planned procedures meet applicable national and institutional standards for claims data research. Since only secondary claims data were analyzed, the requirement for informed consent was waived in agreement with the data holder, AOK Baden–Württemberg [29], a regional statutory health insurer, and in accordance with the study protocol (S-087/2023). Further details on the data holder’s decision process are provided in the section titled “Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate.”

Study Design

This is an observational claims-data study using data from AOK Baden-Württemberg. The observation period covered from 1 January to 31 December 2021.

Study Population

Claims data were used to identify eligible patients on the basis of the following prespecified criteria. Eligible cases were patients aged ≥ 18 years who initiated opioid therapy in 2021 quarter 1 (Q1), quarter 2 (Q2), or quarter 3 (Q3), operationalized as the first opioid prescription observed in 2021 occurring in Q1–Q3. Patients whose first opioid prescription in 2021 occurred in quarter 4 (Q4) were excluded. Opioid prescriptions had to involve oral or transdermal opioid formulations and belong to the anatomical therapeutic chemical classification system (ATC) groups and codes specified in Supplementary Data 1 (Supplementary Data 1—Table 1: ATC codes included and excluded for case selection on the basis of claims data from AOK Baden-Württemberg, Germany, 2021). To reflect prescribing in primary care, prescriptions were required to have been issued by a GP. Several exclusion criteria were applied. We excluded patients with opioid prescriptions issued by other specialist physicians as well as patients receiving palliative care or diagnosed with malignant disease, indicated by ICD-10-GM codes Z51.5, Z51.82, Z85.-, and C00–C97. In addition, to reduce the likelihood of capturing opioid discontinuations primarily driven by acute intolerance or toxicity, we excluded patients with coded diagnoses indicating opioid-related adverse effects and/or poisonings during Q1–Q3 (ICD-GM: T40.0, T40.2, T40.6, T50.9, T88.7,Y57, Y57.9). Figure 1 provides a schematic overview of the operational definitions used to derive the study population. All patients with prescriptions corresponding to excluded ATC codes were removed from the dataset. This particularly concerned ATC codes specific to opioid substitution or misuse treatment, and codeine-containing medications, as codeine is predominantly used for mild acute pain or cough. Measures

For the multivariable logistic regression, the dependent (outcome) variable was the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 of the observation period. In the present study, this measure was used as an operational definition of opioid discontinuation. We examined a set of predictors, including: age, sex, somatoform pain disorders (ICD-10-GM F45.40), severe depressive episodes (ICD-10-GM F32.2, F32.3, F33.2, F33.3), substance use disorders (ICD-10-GM F10, F13, F17, F55), adjunct therapy prescriptions, including predominantly physiotherapy- and exercise-based interventions as well as passive modalities such as massage and thermal applications (see Supplementary Data 2: Adjunct Therapy Prescriptions Included in the 2021 Claims-Data Analysis), comorbidity burden (Charlson Comorbidity Index), strong opioids, psychotherapy, and care level (Pflegegrad). This variable requires further explanation. In Germany, the need for long-term care is assessed using a standardized scoring system. The assessment considers impairments across several domains, including mobility, cognitive and communication abilities, behavioral and psychological problems, self-care, the ability to manage health- and treatment-related demands, and the organization of everyday life. On the basis of the total score, individuals are assigned to one of five care levels (Pflegegrad 1–5), reflecting increasing degrees of care dependency [30]. In the present study, only the presence or absence of an assigned care level was recorded. Distinctions between the individual care levels (1–5) were not considered. For the regression analyses, opioid formulation was operationalized as a categorical exposure with three mutually exclusive groups: (1) long-acting opioids (LAOs) only, (2) short-acting opioids (SAOs) only, and (3) combined LAO/SAO therapy. Assignment was based on the mapping of dosage forms provided by a German pharmacy portal [31]. Patients with concurrent prescriptions of strong and weak opioids, prescriptions that could not be assigned uniquely to one of the three predefined formulation groups, or prescriptions of opioid dosage forms outside the scope of this study (e.g., non-oral or non-transdermal formulations) were excluded from the regression analyses. These restrictions were applied to reduce exposure misclassification and confounding, and to ensure a clear comparison between the predefined opioid formulation groups. Accordingly, the number of patients included in the regression analyses differs from that reported in the descriptive analyses. While the descriptive analyses summarized all eligible patients, the regression analyses required each patient to be assigned uniquely to one predefined opioid formulation group according to the study-specific operationalization. A detailed overview of how the variables were processed and defined is provided in Supplementary Data 3 (see Supplementary Data 3: Predictors in the Regression Model).

Data Transfer and Data Storage

Data handling complied with the General Data Protection Regulation, the data-protection provisions of the German Social Code, and applicable national legislation. Pseudonymized service and claims data were released only after the competent supervisory authority had approved an application under the Social Code submitted by AOK Baden–Württemberg. As part of the evaluation of the family doctor-centered care programme (HZV), AOK Baden–Württemberg assembled the dataset and transmitted it to the Institute for Applied Quality Improvement and Research in Health Care (aQua). Personal identifiers were removed and the data were repseudonymized; the reidentification key remained exclusively with authorized personnel at AOK Baden–Württemberg, while record linkage was performed solely via a project-specific linkage table accessible only to authorized staff at the aQua Institute. The aQua Institute subsequently transferred the data to the Department of Primary Care and Health Services Research at Heidelberg University Hospital, where they were stored in accordance with data-protection requirements. Data access was limited to members of the RELIEF team (G.L., S.B., R.P.D., C.S.).

Data Analysis

Data storage and preparation were performed using MySQL (version 10.6.3-MariaDB), and descriptive and regression analyses were performed using SPSS (version 28.0.1.0) and RStudio (R-4.5.1). Descriptive analyses included age, sex, care level, Charlson Comorbidity Index, the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4, Diagnoses of chronic noncancer pain (F62.80, R52.1, F45.4, R52.2), and opioid prescription characteristics by potency, formulation, and active substance (tramadol, tilidine, tapentadol, morphine, oxycodone, hydromorphone, buprenorphine, fentanyl). Multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine the associations between the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 and the prespecified predictors, as described in the Measures section. Significance was set at p < 0.05. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. The model fit was assessed using Nagelkerke’s R2, discriminative performance for predicting the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 by receiver operating characteristic—area under the curve (ROC-AUC), and multicollinearity by correlation matrix.

Results

A total of n = 94,125 patients were included in the analyses from a dataset of n = 2,540,716 insured individuals. Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of the cohort was 65.5 years. Female patients accounted for 62.0%. A CNCP diagnosis was documented in 49.3% of patients. The most frequently coded CNCP diagnosis was “other chronic pain” (ICD-10-GM: R52.2), present in 76.3% of patients. The absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 was observed in 45.5% of patients.

As shown in Fig. 2, opioid prescribing by general practitioners for patients with CNCP in 2021 varied by the number of quarters with ≥ 1 opioid prescription (i.e., duration of prescribing across 2021, irrespective of which calendar quarters these were). For any opioid, 94,125 patients received ≥ 1 opioid prescription during 2021; 31.9% had prescriptions in 1 quarter (30,046), 19.0% in 2 quarters (17,903), 13.3% in 3 quarters (12,560), and 35.7% in all 4 quarters (33,616). For weak opioids, 74,179 patients received ≥ 1 opioid prescription (78.8%); 40.0% had prescriptions in 1 quarter (29,648), 20.8% in 2 quarters (15,433), 12.8% in 3 quarters (9,501), and 26.4% in 4 quarters (19,597). For strong opioids, 27,324 patients received ≥ 1 opioid prescription (29.0%); 21.0% had prescriptions in 1 quarter (5,733), 14.2% in 2 quarters (3,893), 12.7% in 3 quarters (3,458), and 52.1% in 4 quarters (14,240). In addition to the overall weak- and strong-opioid categories, Fig. 2 also displays, the most frequently prescribed weak opioid, tilidine, and the most frequently prescribed strong opioid, oxycodone. Tilidine was prescribed to 60,898 patients overall, distributed as 43.5% in 1 quarter (26,462), 20.0% in 2 quarters (12,209), 12.0% in 3 quarters (7,320), and 24.5% in 4 quarters (14,907). Oxycodone was prescribed to 10,744 patients, with 27.7%, 14.5%, 12.0%, and 45.8% receiving prescriptions in 1–4 quarters, respectively. Regarding opioid formulation, LAOs predominated and were prescribed to 88,918 (94.4%) patients (33.3%, 18.6%, 13.1%, 35.0% across 1–4 quarters, respectively). SAOs were prescribed to 8,156 (8.7%) patients (47.1%, 16.3%, 10.6%, 26.0% across 1–4 quarters, respectively). Other opioids assessed but not shown in Fig. 2 demonstrated similar distributions across duration groups: fentanyl (n = 7,935; 21.7%, 14.6%, 11.8%, and 51.9% across 1–4 quarters, respectively), tramadol (n = 17,289; 44.3%, 17.7%, 11.9%, 26.1% across 1–4 quarters, respectively), tapentadol (n = 3,085; 33.9%, 15.8%, 12.5%, 37.8% across 1–4 quarters, respectively), buprenorphine (n = 3,093; 27.8%, 16.6%, 14.6%, 41.0% across 1–4 quarters, respectively), hydromorphone (n = 3,202; 24.8%, 15.7%, 13.3%, 46.2% across 1–4 quarters, respectively), and morphine (n = 2,736; 37.2%, 14.1%, 10.3%, 38.3% across 1–4 quarters, respectively).

Predictors of the Absence of an Opioid Prescription in Q4

In the multivariable logistic regression (n = 90,535), increasing age was associated with lower odds of the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 (OR 0.986 per year, 95% CI 0.985–0.987; p < 0.001), corresponding to a 1.4% decrease in the odds per additional year of age. Male sex (reference: female) was associated with slightly lower odds of the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 (OR 0.948, 95% CI 0.920–0.977; p < 0.001), indicating a 5.2% decrease. A higher Charlson Comorbidity Index was also associated with lower odds of the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 (OR 0.966 per point; 95% CI 0.959–0.974; p < 0.001), corresponding to a 3.4% decrease per additional point. Having an assigned care level was associated with 30.0% lower odds of the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 (OR 0.700, 95% CI 0.675–0.727; p < 0.001).

Among the predictors defined up to Q3, adjunct therapies were associated with substantially higher odds of the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 (OR 1.834, 95% CI 1.782–1.888; p < 0.001), corresponding to an 83.4% increase. By contrast, substance use disorder diagnoses were associated with 18.6% lower odds (OR 0.814, 95% CI 0.784–0.846; p < 0.001). A diagnosis of somatoform pain disorders was associated with 19.9% lower odds of the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 (OR 0.801, 95% CI 0.755–0.849; p < 0.001), and severe depressive episodes were associated with 9.7% lower odds (OR 0.903, 95% CI 0.853–0.956; p < 0.001). Strong opioid use was associated with markedly lower odds of the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 (OR 0.527, 95% CI 0.519–0.536; p < 0.001), corresponding to a 47.3% decrease. Opioid formulation was also associated with the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 (overall p < 0.001). Using LAOs as the reference, SAOs were associated with 36.7% lower odds of the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 (OR 0.633, 95% CI 0.594–0.675; p < 0.001). Combination therapy with LAOs and SAOs was associated with 19.0% lower odds of the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 (OR 0.810, 95% CI 0.710–0.910; p < 0.001). Psychotherapy was not significantly associated with the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4. A total of n = 3,590 patients were excluded from the regression analyses because their opioid prescriptions could not be classified into one of the predefined opioid formulation groups.

Goodness-of-fit statistics indicated moderate model performance, with Nagelkerke’s R2 = 0.204. The AUC was 0.722, indicating moderate discrimination for the study outcome. Multicollinearity between predictors was ruled out using a correlation matrix. The results of the logistic regression analyses are presented in Table 2. Discussion

In our model, prescriptions for adjunct therapies showed the strongest positive association with the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4. Conversely, the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 was less common among patients with somatoform pain disorders, substance use disorders and among those receiving strong opioids. Compared with the exclusive use of LAOs, the exclusive use of SAOs and combined LAO/SAO therapy were associated with lower odds of the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4. Taken together, these results highlight the importance of providing concurrent non-pharmacological care and suggest that somatoform pain disorders, as well as the type and formulation of prescribed opioids, may influence opioid discontinuation in primary care. However, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. The identified predictors are discussed below.

Somatoform Pain Disorders

German guidelines clearly identify somatoform pain disorders as an inappropriate indication for opioid therapy. The LONTS guideline [8] explicitly recommends against the use of opioids for persistent somatoform pain disorder (ICD-10 F45.40) [8]. The guideline on functional physical limitations [32] states that there is no evidence supporting the benefits of opioids in treating functional disorders. It also recommends attempting opioid withdrawal when opioids have been initiated, ideally as part of a multimodal treatment plan [32]. Nevertheless, German claims data indicate opioid exposure in this population. This observation is supported by several claims-data analyses: F45.4 diagnoses were 2.3 times more prevalent among women. GPs were the primary specialists involved in care (41%), and 38% of patients received opioid prescriptions [33]. Patients with F45.- diagnoses (F45.0-F45.9, ICD-10-GM) obtained significantly more opioids, as well as more prescriptions of antidepressants and anxiolytics in out-of-hours primary care than patients without F45.- diagnoses [34]. Opioid therapy in somatoform pain disorders has been associated with opioid misuse, abuse, and addiction [35]. Moreover, recent evidence indicates that opioids continue to be prescribed to patients with somatoform pain disorders, even when physicians may seem aware of the diagnosis [36]. This underscores the persistence of guideline-discordant prescribing. Against this background, our finding of a negative association between patients with somatoform pain disorders and the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 may reflect a high symptom burden, psychosocial drivers of pain, and the persistence of opioid prescribing despite the limited expected benefits, rather than a favorable to opioid response [8, 32, 36 –]. Our data cannot determine whether opioid therapy had been initiated recently or had been ongoing for several years, nor can they determine whether opioid dependence influenced these findings. However, the findings suggest an association with continued opioid therapy. In summary, our findings underscore the importance of carefully assessing for somatoform pain disorders before initiating opioid therapy, as these disorders were inversely associated with the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4. This can be achieved using validated assessment instruments and/or a comprehensive clinical evaluation [32].

Adjunct Therapies

In our regression model, prescriptions for adjunct therapies, which mainly included physiotherapy- and exercise-based interventions but also passive modalities such as massage and thermal applications, showed the strongest positive association with the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 (OR 1.834). This aligns with guideline frameworks that position non-pharmacological care as central to chronic pain management [7, 8, 14]. NICE recommends supervised exercise programmes for chronic primary pain [7], and the World Health Organization (WHO) guideline for chronic primary low back pain in primary care [37] similarly highlights education, exercise, and physical therapies as core interventions [37]. Evidence indicates that exercise provides clinically meaningful benefits in chronic low back pain, although no single modality can be considered clearly superior owing to heterogeneity across trials [38]. By contrast, the evidence for passive modalities, such as massage, is more mixed and suggests an adjunctive role [39]. However, evidence from Germany indicates that non-pharmacological interventions remain underused, particularly among older patients receiving long-term opioid therapy [40]. Consistent with these findings, nearly half of the patients in our study did not receive prescriptions for adjunct therapies alongside opioid treatment, suggesting barriers to the implementation of guideline-recommended care. Overall, our findings are consistent with the hypothesis that engagement in non-pharmacological measures may support opioid discontinuation in primary care. However, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects and require confirmation in studies with longer follow-up periods.

Opioid Prescribing Patterns

During 2021, the distribution of opioid prescribing in our dataset showed a modest U-shaped pattern: many patients had ≥ 1 opioid prescription in only one quarter, followed by a marked decline among those with ≥ 1 opioid prescription in two quarters and a further decline among those with ≥ 1 opioid prescription in three quarters. Notably, the number of patients with ≥ 1 opioid prescription in all four quarters increased sharply and exceeded the one-quarter group. This pattern suggests heterogeneous care trajectories, with a larger group characterized by short-term opioid exposure and a second group with persistent, year-long prescribing. These findings should not be interpreted as representative of opioid prescribing in Germany, as the study-specific inclusion criteria influenced the study population and the distribution of opioid formulations. Instead, the analysis was designed to address a specific clinical research question using multivariable regression. The drivers of transitions from short-term to long-term opioid prescribing warrant further study. Some evidence suggests that long-term opioid therapy is associated with older age, a higher burden of comorbidities, substance use disorders, anxiety disorders, and a longer initial days’ supply of opioids [41, 42]. Furthermore, one study reported that patients with a history of long-term opioid prescribing were less likely to discontinue opioid therapy than patients without such a history [43]. Our regression analyses further showed that several predictors were inversely associated with the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4. These included clinical characteristics that have consistently been identified as risk factors for problematic opioid use (misuse, opioid use disorder, or addiction), particularly substance use disorders and depressive disorders, as well as male sex [35, 44]. In addition, the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 was less frequent among patients with greater clinical complexity, reflected by higher comorbidity burden (Charlson Comorbidity Index) and an assigned care level. This is plausible, as opioid therapy among older adults with chronic pain is often linked to higher morbidity and more complex clinical profiles [45].

Strong Opioids

The inverse association between strong opioid therapy and the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 was one of the strongest associations observed in the present study. Although strong opioids are conventionally classified as agents with higher analgesic potency and greater potential for pain relief, key risks of opioid therapy apply to both strong and weak opioids [46]. The lower odds of the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 among patients receiving strong opioids may be owing to the fact that these are more often prescribed to patients with severe pain and greater disease burden. These patients are more likely to require stronger analgesics [46–48], which may contribute to the lower odds of an absence of an opioid prescription in Q4 observed in this study.

Opioid Formulation

Our findings regarding opioid formulations were also noteworthy. Compared with the exclusive use of LAOs, both the exclusive use of SAOs and combined LAO/SAO therapy were associated with lower odds of the absence of an opioid prescription in Q4. Similarly, opioid formulations may reflect differences in patient characteristics and prescribing practices rather than a direct effect of the formulation itself. Evidence comparing LAOs and SAOs is heterogeneous and current reviews do not clearly demonstrate the superiority of one formulation over the other in terms of effectiveness or safety [49–52]. International guidance varies: the German LONTS guideline discusses the potential advantages of LAOs when taken on a fixed schedule (e.g., better pain control and improved adherence), but acknowledges that these assumptions are not supported by high-quality evidence [8]. Our findings are in line with this recommendation. By contrast, US guidance recommends exercising particular caution with LAOs, citing concerns about serious harms and dose-related risks [14, 53]. Our results lay a foundation for further research investigating the underlying mechanisms and causal pathways linking these factors to opioid discontinuation among patients with CNCP in primary care. The restriction to predefined opioid formulation groups reduced exposure misclassification. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted in the context of patients receiving exclusively oral or transdermal opioid formulations, which were the focus of the present study.

Strengths and Limitations

A major strength of this study is the large patient cohort, which enabled robust multivariable regression analyses with precise effect estimates, reflected in narrow confidence intervals. Claims data provide valuable insight into real-world healthcare delivery and allow transparent cohort construction using prespecified inclusion and exclusion criteria. Despite opioid discontinuation being a complex and highly patient-specific process that is difficult to capture in claims data, our model demonstrated moderate fit (Nagelkerke’s R2), suggesting that routinely available predictors still explain a proportion of the variation in the operationalized opioid discontinuation.

Several limitations should be considered. First, one major limitation is the definition of opioid discontinuation. We operationalized opioid discontinuation as the absence of any opioid prescription in Q4 2021. While pragmatic, this proxy may misclassify discontinuation (e.g., temporary gaps in prescribing, care transitions, or prescriptions outside the captured setting). A longer observation window would have allowed a more robust definition and verification of discontinuation over time. Second, claims data cannot confirm actual medication intake; prescribing indicates treatment intent, but adherence, dose changes, and patient-initiated discontinuation cannot be observed directly. In addition, reasons for tapering or continuation (e.g., clinical decision-making, patient preference, adverse effects, or worsening pain) are not available, and residual confounding remains possible given limited information on symptom severity and psychosocial factors. Another limitation is that the study was based on data from 2021, a period still affected by the COVID-19 pandemic in Germany. As pandemic-related changes in healthcare delivery and patient management may have influenced prescribing and treatment patterns, the results should be interpreted within this context. Furthermore, non-opioid analgesics were not considered in the present analyses. As these medications may influence opioid discontinuation, this aspect should be addressed in future research. In addition, study-specific inclusion criteria, such as the focus on oral and transdermal opioids, may have excluded patients receiving other opioid formulations, which may also have affected the representation of combination therapy and SAOs. Finally, we did not assess the involvement of multiple prescribers, which may influence prescribing continuity and discontinuation patterns.

Conclusions

The associations identified in this study highlight the importance of psychosomatic assessment and the systematic use of adjunct therapies when managing opioid therapy for chronic pain. Our findings are consistent with current guideline recommendations emphasizing a multimodal approach and cautious opioid prescribing, particularly for patients with somatoform pain disorders or concurrent substance use disorders. To facilitate implementation in primary care, appropriate structures and resources are needed to support multimodal care and opioid discontinuation strategies. Given the observational design and the operational definition of opioid discontinuation, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Future studies with longer observation periods are needed to confirm and further explore these findings.
