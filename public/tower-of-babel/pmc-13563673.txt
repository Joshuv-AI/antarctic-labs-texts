BOMIFA: biologically informed multi-omics integration with graph contrastive learning for cancer prognosis in women
Authors: Zixiao Lu, Jiajun Wang, Yuping Liang, Zhenghao Lin, Yingyin Tan, Qian Ma, Wu Zhou, Yi Zhao, Siwen Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate survival prediction remains a central challenge in precision oncology, particularly for female patients whose sex-specific molecular characteristics are often under-modeled in prior studies. Although multi-omics integration enables a deeper exploration of prognostic biomarkers, existing methods rely on mathematically driven fusion strategies, which tend to dilute omics-specific signals and fail to capture biological regulatory hierarchies across omics layers. To address these limitations, we propose BOMIFA (Biologically informed Omics representation and Multi-omics Integration Framework), a deep graph-based framework for survival prediction and biomarker discovery in female patients using DNA methylation, mRNA, and miRNA expression data. BOMIFA incorporates two key innovations. First, graph contrastive learning is leveraged within each omics encoder to enhance intra-omics representation learning and amplify prognostically relevant signals. Then, a biologically informed cross-omics attention mechanism is deployed to explicitly model directional regulatory dependencies, enabling inter-omics information exchange aligned with known molecular hierarchies. Extensive benchmarking on eight cancer cohorts demonstrates that BOMIFA consistently outperforms existing prognostic methods in female patients. Moreover, saliency map-based gradient attribution enables the identification of female-associated prognostic biomarkers that were overlooked in prior mixed-sex analyses.Keywords: multi-omics, cancer prognosis, female, cross attention, graph contrastive learning

Background

Accurate prognosis prediction is crucial for guiding treatment decisions and improving clinical outcomes in cancer research. Over the past decades, numerous computational prognostic models have been developed to stratify patients into distinct survival risk groups, either across pan-cancer cohorts [1, 2] or within specific cancer types [3, 4]. Despite these advancements, most existing models treat sex as a covariate or ignore it altogether, overlooking the role of sex chromosomes in survival risk stratification. This omission is critical, as mounting evidence shows that sex-linked genetic and epigenetic factors significantly affect the cancer onset, progression, and mortality, even in many non-reproductive cancers [5, 6]. For example, in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), female patients exhibit a higher prevalence of EGFR mutations than males, resulting in distinct therapeutic responses and survival outcomes [7]. These disparities stem from both environmental exposures (e.g. smoking) and inherent biological differences, calling for more targeted sex-specific cancer biomarkers. However, due to the historical underrepresentation of women in clinical trials, current treatment guidelines and prognostic models are largely optimized for mixed or male-dominated populations. Such imbalance has slowed progress in women’s cancer research. Therefore, tailored prognostic models are urgently demanded to achieve more precise survival risk stratification in female patients and improve their treatment outcomes.

As cancer is directly driven by genetic mutations and dysregulated gene expression that disrupt normal cellular mechanisms, omics data has become a critical resource for prognostic modeling. Previous studies have leveraged single-omics profiles, such as DNA mutations, DNA methylation, or mRNA expression, to stratify patients into different risk groups and identify cancer prognostic biomarkers [8–10]. The Cox proportional hazards model and its penalized variants are classical statistical methods that have been widely applied to link molecular features with survival outcomes [11]. For example, Ma et al. proposed XGBLC that combines Lasso-Cox regression and XGBoost using gene expression data for survival prediction across 20 cancer types [12]. Other Cox variants incorporated feature selection and regularization to improve performance [13]. More recently, deep learning models have been introduced to automatically learn latent representations from omics data and boost predictive accuracy [14]. Among them, DeepSurv employs a deep feed-forward neural network within the Cox proportional hazards framework to model nonlinear effects and has since been widely extended to enhance survival prediction [15]. These studies have led to the discovery of additional cancer biomarkers, which broadened our knowledge within specific cancer types. Nevertheless, since each omics modality only captures a single dimension of tumor heterogeneity, single-omics models have limitations in characterizing multifactorial biological processes that drive cancer progression.

Recent research have increasingly focused on multi-omics integration to provide complementary information for accurate prognosis prediction [16]. Early studies performed feature fusion by projecting multi-modal representations into a shared latent space that is tailored for Cox model [17]. For instance, [18] proposed a Cox-driven multi-constraint latent representation learning framework, which constructed a task-oriented shared feature space for survival analysis. Although these studies carefully designed feature fusion mechanisms, their methods did not adaptively learn omics-specific representations and thus were sensitive to data noise and the curse of dimensionality. Recently, multi-modal deep learning techniques have been applied to refine representations from heterogeneous omics data for improved prognosis prediction. These models first employ omics-specific encoders to extract features from each modality and then performed multi-modal feature integration for survival prediction [19, 20]. Beyond these, several studies utilized graph convolutional networks (GCNs) to simultaneously capture omics-derived representations and their topological relationships [21]. For instance, [22] proposed a multilevel graph neural network that leverages gene regulatory networks and pathway information to enhance survival risk prediction. Nevertheless, most of these studies adopt concatenation- or summation-based fusion strategies, which neglect to capture the intrinsic correlations and complementarities among omics layers.

The attention mechanism offers a powerful solution to the above limitation by enabling models to adaptively weight omics-specific signals and capture key cross-omics interactions. [23] applied sequential channel-spatial attention modules within a generative adversarial network to refine multimodal features and reduce redundancy in prognosis prediction. DeepKEGG designed a pathway-level self-attention module to model inter-sample correlations and generate biological pathway representations for cancer recurrence prediction [24]. Moreover, various multi-modal cross-attention mechanisms have been designed to directly model the dependencies among multi-omics representations. For instance, MOSGAT designed specificity-aware graph attention networks (GATs) to perform modality-specific feature extraction and then devised a cross-modal attention mechanism to uncover potential inter-omics correlations [25]. Pathformer developed a biological pathway informed Transformer, where a criss-cross attention mechanism was introduced to capture the crosstalk between pathways and omics modalities [26].

Even though attention mechanisms have advanced multi-omics integration for cancer prognosis, several critical challenges remain unresolved, particularly in the context of female patients. First, existing attention-based approaches often ignore the hierarchical regulatory relationships across omics layers. For example, DNA methylation regulates gene expression, which subsequently drives downstream pathways and ultimately impacts prognosis. Such dependencies should be harnessed and incorporated into multi-omics integration. Second, the most representative signals within each omics layer are not sufficiently characterized and distilled. Although some existing methods performed omics encoding before fusion, their refinement remains at a generic level, failing to highlight omics-specific biological representations that are crucial for the complementary benefits of integration. Third, biological interpretability remains limited, particularly in female populations. Prognostic biomarkers are expected to act synergistically across omics layers, and women may harbor unique molecular drivers of cancer progression. Yet most models only provide population-wide feature rankings within each omics, making it difficult to investigate multi-omics biomarkers for female prognosis.

To address the aforementioned issues, we present BOMIFA, a Biologically informed Omics representation and Multi-omics Integration Framework for cancer prognosis prediction in female patients. BOMIFA consists of four major modules, i.e. omics-enhancement encoders, a cross-omics fusion module, a survival prediction module, and a biological interpretability module. The omics-enhancement encoders employ multi-head self-attention and graph contrastive learning to distill representative intra-omics features while suppressing noise. The cross-omics fusion module is designed with biologically informed cross-attention and low-rank multimodal fusion (LMF) to explicitly model multi-omics regulatory dependencies and integrate all features. The survival prediction module outputs risk scores using a Cox-based survival objective. After model training, the biological interpretability module employs a saliency map-based gradient backpropagation approach to identify key multi-omics biomarkers associated with prognosis in female populations. Extensive experiments on eight TCGA datasets (including both female cancers and female patient subsets of common cancers) demonstrate that BOMIFA not only outperforms state-of-the-art survival prediction methods, but also identifies interpretable biomarkers in female populations.

Methods

Data collection and pre-processing

We collected multi-omics and clinical data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) for both female-associated and common cancer types. The female-associated cancers include ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma (OV), cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC), and uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC). Breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA) was also included; given the very small number of male patients, they were excluded for consistency, and BRCA was also categorized as a female-associated cancer. The common cancers include lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), and lower-grade glioma (LGG). For common cancers, only female patients were included. Three types of omics data (DNA methylation, mRNA expression, and miRNA expression) were used for prognosis prediction. Only patients with matched data for all three omics and available survival follow-up information were retained.

To reduce noise and ensure comparability across datasets, all omics data underwent standardized preprocessing. All preprocessing steps were performed independently within each training fold of the 5-fold cross-validation to avoid information leakage. Specifically, for DNA methylation and mRNA expression, features with no signal (zero mean values) or low variance were removed. In addition, for DNA methylation, only probes corresponding to the Illumina Infinium HumanMethylation27 BeadChip were retained. For miRNA expression, features with zero variance were discarded. ANOVA F-test was performed using survival labels (0/1) as the grouping variable to rank features. A primary component analysis (PCA)-based adaptive strategy was then applied to control feature dimensionality, retaining features contributing to the dominant principal component (variance explained >50%), with an upper limit of 1000 features per omics modality. Finally, each type of omics data was independently normalized to the range [0, 1] using min-max scaling. Details of the datasets used for developing BOMIFA are summarized in Table 1.

Overview of BOMIFA

BOMIFA is a multi-omics integrative framework built based on GCN for cancer prognosis. By taking each patient as a node and their multi-omics profiles as multi-modal node features, BOMIFA jointly captures both omics feature dependencies and patient relationships to distinguish patients with good and poor prognoses. The workflow of BOMIFA can be summarized into four components (Fig. 1). (i) Omics-enhancement encoders. For each omics modality, a GCN-based encoder with multi-head self-attention and graph contrastive learning extracts representative intra-omics features while preserving informative signals that distinguish prognostic patient subgroups. (ii) Cross-omics fusion. Biologically informed cross-attention and low-rank multimodal fusion (LMF) are applied to explicitly model regulatory dependencies across omics layers and integrate heterogeneous features into joint representations. (iii) Survival prediction. The LMF fused features are translated into risk scores for each patient and estimated with a Cox-based survival objective. (iv) Biological interpretability analysis. After model training, saliency map-based gradient backpropagation is applied to each omics encoder and the cross-omics module to identify multi-omics biomarkers. We describe each component in detail in the following sections.

Overview of BOMIFA. The omics-enhancement encoders extract modality-specific representations by combining graph convolutional networks (GCNs) with multi-head self-attention and graph contrastive learning. The cross-omics fusion module integrates the learned omics representations through a biologically informed cross-attention and low-rank multimodal fusion (LMF). The cross-attention follows the regulatory hierarchy from DNA methylation to mRNA expression to miRNA regulation: methylation provides upstream epigenetic cues, mRNA “queries” its transcriptional regulation, and miRNA contributes downstream post-transcriptional effects. The survival prediction module transforms the fused representation into patient-level risk scores using a Sigmoid activation function. A Cox-based survival loss is applied to the risk scores to optimize BOMIFA.

Biological interpretability analysis

To interpret BOMIFA’s predictions and identify prognostic biomarkers, we conducted a saliency map-based gradient backpropagation approach on the three omics modalities. For each modality, the gradients of the output risk score with respect to the input features were calculated, and the absolute gradient values were aggregated to quantify feature importance. During this process, each omics encoder was first analyzed individually and then jointly incorporated to update the global attribution weights, ensuring that cross-omics interactions were also captured. The top 100 features from each modality were selected as candidate biomarkers based on their saliency scores. For a consistent interpretation at the gene level, methylation probes and miRNAs were mapped to their corresponding gene symbols. Finally, a unified multi-omics biomarker sets was yielded for each cancer type.

To further identify prognostic drivers in female populations, we performed pan-cancer biomarker analysis across all the eight cancer types. Specifically, genes were ranked by their recurrence frequency across the 24 cancer-omics combinations (8 cancers × 3 omics), under the assumption that repeatedly selected genes are more likely to represent robust markers. The shared genes were then analyzed using protein–protein interaction (PPI) network and functional enrichment analysis to explain their regulatory relationships and biological pathways in female patients.

Implementation

The 5-fold cross-validation was conducted on each dataset to train and evaluate BOMIFA. BOMIFA was implemented in Python (version 3.9) using the Pytorch framework (version 2.2.0) on an NVIDIA RTX 4060 GPU machine and Intel Xeon CPUs with 8GB RAM. Model parameters were initialized using the Xavier initialization method and optimized using the Adam optimizer. BOMIFA was trained in an end-to-end manner, where parameters in all modules are jointly optimized. To ensure robustness, all results were averaged over five independent runs with different random seeds. The detailed hyperparameter settings are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The source code is publicly available at https://github.com/anbai01/BOMIFA/.

All biological interpretability analyses were implemented in R (version 4.4.1). PPI networks were constructed and visualized using the tidyverse and circlize packages. Functional enrichment analyses were conducted based on KEGG and GO databases using the clusterProfiler package.

Benchmarking

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of BOMIFA, we benchmarked it against eight state-of-the-art methods: six end-to-end survival prediction models (DeepSurv [15], DeepProg [28], MOGONET [29], MPK-GNN [30], DeepKEGG [24], and CATfusion [31]) and two multi-omics representation learning approaches (DILCR [32] and seOMLR [33]) combined with a Cox proportional hazards model for survival prediction. For fair comparison, all methods were trained and evaluated using the same 5-fold cross-validation, with identical feature selection procedures applied within each training fold. For DeepSurv not originally designed for multi-omics data, we extended their architectures by independently encoding each omics modality followed by feature concatenation. For CATfusion originally designed for image-genomic fusion, we adapted its cross-attention mechanism to model pairwise interactions between omics modalities. In addition, the C-index for MPK-GNN could not be computed because its original implementation does not generate patient-level continuous risk scores required for concordance estimation. All benchmarking methods used the same omics-only input configuration without including any clinical covariates. All methods were implemented following their original publications and evaluated under the same data preprocessing and experimental settings. When necessary, hyperparameters were adjusted to ensure stable and competitive performance.

Ablation studies

To investigate the contributions of multi-omics integration and key architectural components in BOMIFA, three sets of ablation experiments were performed. First, to evaluate the effectiveness of the biologically motivated cross-omics attention design, we constructed variants with alternative query-key-value (Q-K-V) assignments by permuting the roles of methylation, mRNA, and miRNA while keeping all other components unchanged. Then, for multi-omics integration, we constructed BOMIFA variants using each single modality (DNA methylation, mRNA, and miRNA) and three dual-omics combinations (mRNA and miRNA by removing DNA methylation; DNA methylation and mRNA by removing miRNA expression; DNA methylation and miRNA by removing mRNA expression) to compare their prognostic performance with the full three-omics setting. For architectural design, we examined three ablated variants by individually removing the multi-head self-attention, graph contrastive learning, and cross-omics attention to compare their results with those of the complete BOMIFA.

In female-associated cancers (OV, CESC, UCEC, and BRCA), BOMIFA exhibited the most notable performance gains (Fig. 2; Table 2). For example, in CESC, it achieved an AUC of 0.958 and a C-index of 0.862, outperforming the second-best method by 3.0% and 5.0%, respectively. In UCEC and BRCA, BOMIFA also consistently led across all four metrics, with C-index values of 0.782 and 0.800, respectively. Among the competing methods, CATfusion and MOGONET were generally the strongest baselines, but neither was consistently second-best across all datasets or all metrics. For example, in BRCA, MOGONET achieved a C-index of 0.751 and CATfusion reached 0.754, both trailing BOMIFA (0.800). DeepProg and DeepSurv, which mainly rely on linear Cox models and single-omics autoencoders, showed lower performance overall, with C-index values ranging 0.679–0.801 and 0.633–0.695, respectively. The representation learning-based methods DILCR and seOMLR achieved only moderate performance, with C-index values noticeably lower than BOMIFA.

In female subsets of common cancers, BOMIFA maintained strong and stable performance, again surpassing or matching the best baselines across all metrics (Fig. 2; Table 2; Table S2). It obtained C-index values ranging 0.724–0.811, exceeding the second-best model by ~2%–6%. Among the benchmarking methods, MPK-GNN and DeepKEGG showed moderate performance, with AUC values ranging from ~0.76 to 0.92 across the four cohorts. Their use of pathway-level priors contributed to relatively stable predictions in LUAD and HNSC but reduced generalization in smaller cohorts such as STAD. The attention-only CATfusion demonstrated more competitive average performance but higher variability across datasets, with AUC values fluctuating by over 10% between cohorts (e.g. 0.908 in HNSC versus 0.799 in STAD), indicating greater sensitivity to data scale and modality imbalance. DILCR and seOMLR exhibited more pronounced variability across datasets, with performance gaps relative to BOMIFA becoming larger in STAD and LGG, suggesting limited robustness of two-stage learning strategies under heterogeneous data conditions.

BOMIFA enables more accurate risk stratification of female patients

We compared the survival risk stratification ability of BOMIFA and the benchmarking methods across four representative TCGA cohorts, including two female-associated cancers (CESC and OV) and female subsets of two common cancers (HNSC and LGG). As shown in Fig. 3, BOMIFA achieved the widest separation of KM curves and the most significant log-rank statistics, with adjusted P < .0001 in all four datasets. In contrast, the best competing methods (CATfusion and MOGONET) yielded statistically significant separations (mostly adjusted P < .0001), but exhibited smaller gaps between survival curves. MPK-GNN, DeepProg, and DeepSurv showed weaker or only marginal separation in several cohorts. The representation learning-based DILCR and seOMLR produced less distinct separations, with more overlapping survival curves and reduced discrimination compared with BOMIFA.

BOMIFA identifies prognostic biomarkers in female populations

We first selected the top-ranking genes most frequently appearing across all eight TCGA cancer types to uncover female-associated biomarkers identified by BOMIFA. As shown in Fig. 4A, BOMIFA consistently identified a recurrent set of high-importance genes shared across multiple cancer types, including XK1, PRDM16, PIK3R1, LPP, SLC12A7, NLGN1, PRKCB, LIMCH1, PEG3, and PDE7B. Several of these genes, such as FOXK1, PIK3R1, PRDM16, and LPP, appeared across nearly all female-associated cancers (BRCA, CESC, OV, and UCEC), suggesting conserved regulatory roles in tumor progression among female patients. In addition, genes such as PRKCB, NLGN1, SLC12A7, PEG3, and LIMCH1 were commonly identified in both female-associated cancers and female subsets of common cancers (HNSC, LUAD, LGG, and STAD), indicating robust cross-cancer relevance of these features.

We subsequently examined the regulatory relationships and biological functions of the selected genes. The PPI network (Fig. 4B, left) revealed a tightly connected interaction module involving key signaling mediators (AKT1, PIK3R1, and PRKCB), apoptosis regulators (e.g. BCL2 and CASP8), and receptor and adhesion molecules (e.g. CD44, EPHA2, and ESR1). This network structure indicates that these genes act in concert to integrate survival, growth-factor, and stress-response signaling rather than functioning as isolated effectors. Functional enrichment analysis (Fig. 4B, right; Fig. 4C) further demonstrated that the identified genes were significantly enriched in pathways such as estrogen signaling, endocrine resistance, EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance, proteoglycans in cancer, and Rap1 signaling (adjusted P < .01). Many of these pathways are closely associated with hormone responsiveness, signal transduction, and cellular stress adaptation, which are fundamental biological processes contributing to sex-specific differences in tumor behavior.

Ablation study I: Effect of Q-K-V assignment in cross-omics attention

To evaluate the effectiveness of the Q-K-V assignment in the cross-omics attention module, we compared the original BOMIFA design with three alternative configurations, where mRNA, miRNA, and DNA methylation were used as (i) Q, K, V (Q_mRNA); (ii) V, Q, K (Q_miRNA); and (iii) K, V, Q (Q_Meth), respectively. As shown in Fig. 5, the original assignment (Ours) consistently achieved the best performance across all datasets, while alternative configurations led to varying degrees of performance degradation. For example, in CESC, the C-index decreased from 0.862 (Ours) to 0.812 (Q_mRNA), 0.816 (Q_miRNA), and 0.809 (Q_Meth). Across datasets, alternative Q-K-V assignments typically lead to C-index reductions of ~2%–7% compared with the original design.

Ablation study II: Contribution of multi-omics integration

To investigate the sources of BOMIFA’s superior performance, we compared BOMIFA with its single- and dual-omics variants. As shown in Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table S3, BOMIFA consistently achieved the best performance under all evaluation metrics, with dual-omics variants ranking second and single-omics models performing the weakest. In female-associated cancers such as CESC, BOMIFA reached C-index of 0.862, outperforming the best dual-omics variant (0.824) by 4.6% and the best single-omics variant (0.798) by 8.0%. In female subsets of common cancers like STAD, BOMIFA similarly maintained superior performance (C-index of 0.798), exceeding the best dual-omics (0.781) and single-omics (0.763) variants by 2.2% and 4.6%, respectively. Among individual modalities, mRNA-only models exhibited better standalone predictive ability than methylation-only and microRNA-only models. On average, integrating all three omics improved the C-index by ~10% and the AUC by ~8% compared with the best single-omics models.

Ablation study III: Contribution of key architectural components

To quantify the contribution of each key architectural component, we compared the full BOMIFA framework with three ablated variants: (i) without graph contrastive learning (w/o Contrastive), (ii) without the multi-head self-attention encoder (w/o SA), and (iii) without the cross-omics attention fusion (w/o Cross-attention). As shown in Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table S4, all ablations resulted in measurable decreases in predictive performance. Removing cross-omics attention caused the largest degradation, with C-index reductions of 3%–9% on average. For instance, w/o Cross-attention reduced C-index from 0.862 to 0.808 in CESC (−6.3%) and AUC from 0.930 to 0.898 in HNSC (−3.4%). Removing graph contrastive learning also reduced performance; e.g. the AUC decreased from 0.858 to 0.810 in BRCA and from 0.896 to 0.893 in LGG. Excluding the self-attention in encoder produced moderate but consistent reductions, typically around 1%–3% across datasets.

Contributions of model designs in female-associated cancers

To further understand how different components of BOMIFA contribute to performance in female-associated cancers, we jointly analyzed all ablation results across BRCA, CESC, OV, and UCEC. First, the original Q-K-V assignment consistently achieved the best performance, while alternative configurations led to C-index reductions of ~2%–7%, with larger gaps observed in CESC and UCEC (Fig. 5). Second, multi-omics integration provided clear improvements over single-omics models (Fig. 6). Third, cross-omics attention contributed most significantly among architectural components (Fig. 7). Removing this module reduced the C-index from 0.862 to 0.808 in CESC and from 0.800 to 0.730 in BRCA, while removing contrastive learning or self-attention resulted in smaller reductions (typically 1%–4%). These results indicate that cross-omics interaction modeling and multi-omics integration are the primary drivers of BOMIFA’s performance in female-related cancers.

Discussion

Although cancer prognosis prediction has been extensively studied, most computational models were developed and validated on mixed-gender populations, rarely considering sex-specific biological differences that affect tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic response. This limitation has contributed to systematic clinical biases, as multiple targeted therapeutic strategies confer greater benefit in men than in women [34, 35]. To address this imbalance, we propose BOMIFA, a biologically informed multi-omics integration framework explicitly designed for cancer prognosis in female patients. BOMIFA not only achieved superior prognostic performance across both female-associated cancers and female subsets of common cancers, but also identified both well-established and less-characterized biomarkers that may contribute to distinct prognostic outcomes in women. Overall, BOMIFA represents an early step toward gender-aware prognostic modeling, and we hope it will encourage sex-specific biology into future computational frameworks to advance precision oncology for women.

Most existing multi-omics integration strategies were based on mathematical aspects, overlooking the inherent biological hierarchy among them [36, 37]. Whether Bayesian, network-based, deep learning-based, kernel-based, or matrix factorization-based, the majority of them treat each omics modality as an abstract mathematical object [38–40], which are limited in capturing the causal relationships that link multi-omics data. BOMIFA overcomes these limitations through a biologically informed cross-attention mechanism that aligns omics information flow following their regulatory hierarchy in gene expression. It is worth noted that although this cross-attention is developed using only DNA methylation, mRNA, and miRNA, it can be flexibly adapted to other omics combinations, only by assigning them to the appropriate positions in the Query-Key-Value structure. Moreover, recent advances show that graph contrastive learning can enhance topological representations by leveraging structure- and feature-level augmentations [41, 42]. Inspired by these successes, we incorporate graph contrastive learning to improve the discriminative ability of learned node embeddings in multi-omics data. Our ablation study confirms that this design contributes to improved prognostic performance. Together, BOMIFA can capture both intra- and inter-omics relationships more effectively across diverse cancer types.

The “black-box” nature of most deep learning-based models makes it challenging to understand which biological features or mechanisms drive the prognosis predictions [43]. Our interpretability analysis showed that BOMIFA focuses on learning molecular features with biological relevance to female cancer outcomes. Among the top-ranked genes, several well-established oncogenic regulators were identified. For example, AKT1 and PIK3R1 are core components of the PI3K-Akt pathway that drives survival and proliferative signaling [44]; PRKCB and BCL2 contribute to kinase-mediated inflammatory regulation and apoptosis control [45]. More notably, BOMIFA also identified several genes that may contribute to cancer progression in female populations but have received less attention previously. For instance, LIMCH1 and LPP as regulators of cytoskeletal organization and cell-matrix adhesion [46] were highly ranked in CESC, HNSC, and LGG; membrane transport-related genes such as SLC12A7 and NLGN1 emerged as important markers in BRCA, LUAD, and OV [47, 48]. In addition, metabolic and immune-associated genes such as PDE7B were consistently prioritized in LGG and STAD, indicating regulatory processes that may contribute to prognostic variation in female populations [49]. Together, these findings indicate that BOMIFA not only reinforces established biomarkers but also uncover overlooked molecular markers that may be uniquely relevant to female patients, offering potential candidate targets for future cancer research.

There are also several limitations of BOMIFA. First, the framework was evaluated mainly on retrospective TCGA datasets without independent external validation. Prospective test on larger multi-omics cohorts will be necessary to verify its generalizability. Second, while BOMIFA identifies biologically meaningful biomarkers through interpretability analysis, it still depends on post hoc explanations rather than direct mechanistic modeling. This limitation could be addressed by integrating causal inference to better quantify feature contributions.

In conclusion, our study presents BOMIFA, a biologically informed multi-omics integration framework for female cancer prognosis. By combining graph contrastive learning, multi-head self-attention, and biologically guided cross-omics fusion, BOMIFA improves survival prediction and identifies potential female-associated biomarkers across multiple cancer types. Future work should address limitations and build on strengths.
