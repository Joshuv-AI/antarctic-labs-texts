TDO2 inhibition counters Benzo[a]pyrene-induced immune evasion and suppresses tumorigenesis in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: İsa Taş, Mücahit Varlı, Sultan Pulat, Hyun Bo Sim, Jong-Jin Kim, Hangun Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBenzo[a]pyrene (BaP) is a toxic polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon known as an exogenous AhR ligand. This study investigates the role of BaP in inducing immune checkpoint expression in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and the underlying mechanisms involving the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) and tryptophan (Trp) metabolism.MethodsWe assessed the expression of immune checkpoint molecules, including PD-L1 and ICOSL, in lung epithelial cell lines (BEAS-2B and H1975) exposed to BaP. The involvement of AhR in BaP-induced immune checkpoint expression was examined using AhR silencing (siAhR). Additionally, the role of Trp metabolism in BaP-mediated immune evasion was explored through culturing in Trp (-/+) condition media, treatments with the inhibitors of rate-limiting enzymes in Trp metabolism (TDO2 and IDO1) and analyses of Trp-catabolizing enzymes. The therapeutic potential of targeting Trp metabolism, specifically TDO2, was evaluated in vivo using C57BL/6 mice orthotopically inoculated with LUAD cells.ResultsBaP exposure significantly upregulated the mRNA and surface expression of PD-L1 and ICOSL, with AhR playing a crucial role in this induction. Trp metabolism was found to enhance BaP-mediated immune evasion, as indicated by stronger induction of immune checkpoints in Trp (+) media and the upregulation of Trp-catabolizing enzymes. TDO2 inhibition markedly suppressed the surface expression of PD-L1 and ICOSL, demonstrating the importance of Trp metabolism in BaP-induced immune evasion. Further analysis confirmed the high TDO2 expression in lung adenocarcinoma and its association with poor patient survival. Using an orthotopic implantation mouse model, we demonstrated the inhibitory effect of two different TDO2 inhibitors on tumorigenesis, immune checkpoints, and tryptophan metabolism.ConclusionsThis study highlights the key mechanisms behind BaP-induced immune evasion in LUAD, particularly through the TDO2/AhR axis. It reveals how TDO2 inhibitors can counteract immune checkpoint activation and boost anti-tumor immunity, suggesting new paths for targeted lung cancer immunotherapy. The findings significantly improve our understanding of immune evasion in LUAD and underscore the therapeutic promise of TDO2 inhibition.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40170-024-00365-z.Keywords: Benzo[a]pyrene, TDO2, ICOSL, PD-L1, AhR, Immune evasion, Tryptophan

Introduction

Lung cancer is a leading cause of cancer death worldwide [1]. Smoking is universally recognized as the main cause of lung cancer events [2]. Cigarette smoke contains more than 7000 chemicals, of which at least 81 have been identified for their involvement in the biology of tumorigenesis and progression. Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons such as benzo[a]pyrene (BaP) and aromatic amines such as 4-aminobiphenyl are some of the carcinogens found in cigarettes [3].

Immune checkpoints attenuate the function of T cells and play critical roles in immune evasion [4]. The well-known immune checkpoint PD-L1 (programmed cell death 1 ligand) is induced by cytokines, oncogenes, epigenetic modifiers, and microRNAs, which leads to the inhibition of CD8 + T-cell activity. Currently, PD-L1/PD-1 blockade has shown impressive clinical effects on lung cancer patients [5]. Moreover, numerous immune checkpoint genes, including VISTA, ICOSL, LAG3 and VTCN1, are involved in the development of immunotherapies to overcome potential obstacles such as resistance [6]. Recently, it was reported that cigarette smoke and the carcinogen benzo[a]pyrene induce the expression of PD-L1 on lung epithelial cells in vitro and in vivo, which is mediated by the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) [5]. However, the effect of environmental carcinogens on PD-L1 and other immune checkpoints requires further investigation to elucidate how they contribute to immune evasion and promote carcinogenesis.

Indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) and tryptophan-2,3-dioxygenase (TDO) are the enzymes responsible for catabolizing the essential amino acid tryptophan (Trp) into kynurenine (Kyn). Along with Kyn, its metabolite kynurenic acid has been identified as an endogenous ligand for the AhR [7, 8]. Once activated by its ligand, AhR translocates to the nucleus, where it forms heterodimers with the aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator (ARNT), thus inducing the transcription of target genes [9]. The Trp/Kyn/AhR cascade leads to the generation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and suppresses antitumor immunity [7]. Moreover, it was reported that the Kyn/Trp ratio, which is associated with clinical and histopathological parameters, may serve as a biomarker in lung cancer patients [10]. Recently, an untargeted metabolomics approach using ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography‒mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) was used to investigate the BaP-induced metabolic reprogramming of H460 lung cancer cells. The data demonstrated alterations in 14 amino acid metabolic pathways, including Trp metabolism, in BaP-exposed H460 cells [11]. However, whether Trp metabolism is involved in BaP-mediated immune evasion in lung cancer and the mechanism behind remains unclear.

We hypothesized that BaP, a tobacco carcinogen, may endogenously modulate immune evasion in lung cancer via Trp metabolism, which is associated with AhR activity. Here, we reported that other immune checkpoint markers including ICOSL, not only PD-L1, is upregulated by BaP exposure through AhR in lung cancer. Moreover, Trp metabolism contributes to immune checkpoint upregulation through TDO2 activation. Finally, lung cancer formation and the expression of immune checkpoints, including ICOSL, were significantly suppressed by TDO2 inhibition in vivo.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cell lines H1650 (EGFR E746_A750), H292 (WT), A549 (KRAS G12S), H460 (LCC KRAS_Q61H), and H1975 (EGFR_L858R, T790M) and the normal human bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS-2B were cultured in RPMI 1640 (GenDEPOT, Katy, TX, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin solution under a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C in an incubator.

siRNA interference experiments

We purchased the si-negative control (ON-TARGETplus Nontargeting Pool, D-001810-10-05) and si-AhR (ON-TARGETplus Human AHR (196) siRNA – SMARTpool, L-004990-00-0005) from Dharmacon™. The siRNA duplexes were prepared and transfected according to a protocol provided by Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX.

Quantitative RT‒qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells using RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa, Otsu, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Thereafter, 1 µg of RNA was converted to cDNA using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Relative gene expression was analyzed using SYBR Green (Enzynomics, Seoul, Korea). The thermal cycling conditions used in the qPCR were 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. A CFX instrument (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) was used to perform the analysis. A list of primers can be found in Tables 1 and 2.

Flow cytometry

For surface staining, H1975 cells were digested with 0.5% trypsin. Cells were stained with the target antibodies (PD-L1 and ICOSL) or with the corresponding isotype control monoclonal antibody IgG for 1 h at room temperature. The cells were washed twice with PBS by centrifugation and then stained with anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) and the F(ab′)2 fragment (Alexa Fluor® 488 Conjugate) for 30 min. After washing, the cells were analyzed by a flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Krefeld, Germany).

For analysis of lung tissue, collagenase D digestion was performed for 20 min at 37 °C after mouse lung cancer tissues were cut into 2 mm fragments. A 200 mm stainless steel wire mesh was used to generate a single-cell suspension. The cells were then stained with fluorescence-labeled antibodies specific for the following markers: anti-mouse CD45-APC, CD8a-PE, CD279-FITC, CD4-PE, CD111b-FITC and CD163-PE. After 30 min of incubation on ice, the cells will be washed with FACS buffer and measured using a FACSCanto II. Data analysis was performed using Flow Jo software. A list of antibodies can be found in Table 3.

Database analysis

The Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) platform (available at http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/index.html) was used to assess the expression levels of IDO1 and TDO2 in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). GEPIA is an advanced interactive web server designed to facilitate the analysis of RNA sequencing expression data [12]. It encompasses data from 9,736 tumor samples and 8,587 normal specimens derived from both The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) projects and processed through a uniform pipeline for accuracy and consistency.

To further validate the expression patterns of IDO1 and TDO2 in LUAD, we utilized the UALCAN database (accessible at http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/). UALCAN is an inclusive, intuitive, and dynamic online platform for the in-depth analysis of cancer OMICS data, enabling users to conduct comprehensive evaluations and comparisons with ease [13].

In vivo antitumor study

Six-week-old C57BL/6 male mice weighing 20–22 g each were purchased from Orient Bio (Seongnam, Korea). The animal experiment protocol was approved by the Sunchon National University Research Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. The handling of animals and all in vivo experiments were performed according to the Guiding Principles in the Care and Use of Animals (DHEW publication, NIH 80–23). C57BL/6 mice were inoculated with LLC/iRFP cells (5 × 106, 0.1 ml of PBS per mouse) via the trachea. Four days after inoculation, the mice were randomized into groups and treated with DMSO, 680c91 (10 mg/kg) or LM10 (10 mg/kg). During the 4–19 days, the treatment was carried out by intraperitoneal injection. Body weight was measured with a caliper every 3 days. Bioimaging of near-infrared fluorescence was obtained immediately using a fluorescence-labeled organism bioimaging instrument (FOBI) system (Cellgentek, Osong, Korea). After the mice were sacrificed, lung and liver tissues were successfully isolated, and lung fluorescence was measured for each sample.

Statistics

All experiments were performed at least 2–3 times. The results are expressed as the mean ± SD. When indicated, the data were analyzed for statistical significance using 1- or 2-way ANOVA, and the results are reported as P values (< 0.05). The analysis was conducted using Prism (GraphPad software, version 9.2).

Results

BaP induces the expression of immune checkpoints on lung epithelial cells

We first examined the endogenous expression of a well-known ligand of the immune checkpoint marker programmed cell death 1, PD-L1, in lung epithelial cells. BEAS-2B, H1650, H292, A549, H460, and H1975 cells prior to exposure to the tobacco carcinogen BaP. PD-L1 was highly expressed at both the mRNA and protein levels in the BEAS-2B, H460 and H1975 cell lines (Fig. S1). We conducted further research on BEAS-2B and H1975 cells. Consistent with previous report, our findings showed that BaP significantly upregulated the mRNA level of PD-L1 [5]. To address whether immune checkpoints other than PD-L1 are involved in BaP-induced immune suppression, we analyzed the mRNA levels of several immune checkpoint molecules, including 4-1BBL, GAL-9, HVEM, ICOSL, and PVR, in lung epithelial cells. Only the ICOSL mRNA level was markedly upregulated in both cell lines (Fig. 1A-B). We further investigated the surface expression of selected immune checkpoints, PD-L1 and ICOSL, in BaP-exposed lung epithelial cells. BaP dose-dependently increased the surface expression of both PD-L1 and ICOSL, as revealed by flow cytometry (Fig. 1C-F). Our findings demonstrate that BaP induces the expression of multiple immune checkpoint molecules to enhance immune suppression in LUAD.

Aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) mediates BaP-induced PD-L1 and ICOSL expression

It was reported that polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (e.g. BaP) exposure induces the occurrence and progression of lung cancer through AhR [5]. To determine whether AhR is involved in the activation of immune checkpoints other than PD-L1, we further investigated the expression of BaP-derived immune checkpoints in the context of AhR silencing. Here, we demonstrated that the mRNA level of BaP-induced ICOSL was significantly suppressed by siAhR in addition to PD-L1 (Fig. 2A-B). We further confirmed that the surface expression of PD-L1 and ICOSL was suppressed by siAhR in the lung cancer cell line H1975 (Fig. 2C-F). BEAS-2B did not exhibit the same pattern as that observed in H1975 cells. These data indicate that AhR plays an active role in mediating multiple immune checkpoints to induce immune suppression in LUAD.

Tryptophan (Trp) metabolism is involved in Bap-induced immune checkpoint expression

Trp is catabolized by IDO1/TDO2 to Kyn, which is a key endogenous ligand that activates AhR [7]. To investigate whether Trp metabolism engages in BaP-mediated immune evasion in lung cancer, we first incubated the cells in Trp-/- medium supplemented with BaP at different time points. The results shown at 3 different time points (24, 48, and 72 h) are consistent with each other. BaP exposure significantly increased the surface protein expression of PD-L1 and ICOSL in Trp (+) media. However, under Trp (-) conditions, BaP-induced PD-L1 and ICOSL expression was completely abolished in BEAS-2B cells and notably reduced in H1975 cells (Fig. 3. A-D, Fig. S2). Moreover, the transporter genes (SLC1A5 and SLC7A5) and rate-limiting enzymes (TDO2, IDO1 and AFMID) involved in Trp catabolism were examined under BaP exposure. TDO2 and IDO1 mRNA expression was consistently upregulated by BaP in both BEAS-2B and H1975 cells. These data indicate that Trp metabolism has involved in enhancing BaP-mediated immune evasion, which means that BaP endogenously induces immune suppression via the Trp/Kyn/AhR axis in addition to the well-known exogenous activation of AhR.

The nuclear translocation of AhR is endogenously activated by trp-catabolizing enzymes

To better understand the role of Trp metabolism in BaP-induced immune checkpoints, we investigated the effects of inhibiting the Trp catabolism enzymes IDO1 and TDO2 in the context of AhR nuclear translocation. Exposure to BaP, an exogenous ligand of AhR, clearly induced the nuclear translocation of AhR in both BEAS-2B and H1975 cells. Furthermore, we investigated the BaP-induced nuclear translocation of AhR upon treatment with TDO2 and the IDO1 inhibitors 680c91 and epacatostat, respectively (Fig. 4). TDO2 inhibition reduced AhR nuclear translocation in BEAS-2B but not in H1975 cells. Coadministration of the TDO2 inhibitor with BaP effectively blocked nuclear translocation in both cell lines. In addition, Trp (-) condition and TDO2i significantly abolished the activation of BaP-induced AhR and ARNT transcription complex (Fig. S3). The IDO1 inhibitor suppressed the nuclear translocation of AhR in both BEAS-2B and H1975 cells, though coadministration with BaP significantly prevented nuclear translocation in H1975 cells but not in BEAS-2B. Taken together, our findings revealed that the effects of BaP are largely mediated through the canonical AhR/ARNT pathway, with BaP inducing AhR nuclear translocation both directly as an exogenous ligand and indirectly by stimulating endogenous Kyn production via IDO1 and TDO2 activation.

TDO2 plays a key role in the Trp metabolism pathway to activate BaP-mediated immune evasion

To further investigate the impact of the inhibiting Trp-degrading enzymes TDO2 and IDO1 on BaP-induced immune checkpoint expression and determine which enzyme plays a key role in this process, surface protein expression of the cells was examined. We treated cells with the IDO1 inhibitor, epacatostat, and the TDO2 inhibitor ,680c91, upon BaP exposure. Both early (24–48 h) and late time (72 h) point results consistently demonstrated that the IDO1 inhibitor had no effect on BaP-induced ICOSL and PD-L1 level in H1975 and Beas2B cells. In contrast, TDO2 inhibition consistently suppressed BaP-induced ICOSL and PD-L1 level by approximately 30% in both H1975 and Beas2B cells (Fig. 5). This suppression was also observed consistently at early time points (Fig. 5A-D and Fig. S4). These data demonstrate that the contribution of the Trp metabolism to BaP-induced immune evasion is moderated by the rate-limiting enzyme TDO2, not IDO1.

TDO2 is highly expressed and correlated with survival in lung adenocarcinoma patients

We further explored the expression differences of TDO2 and IDO1 between LUAD and normal lung tissues using the online databases GEPIA and UALCAN. GEPIA analysis revealed that TDO2 mRNA expression was significantly higher in LUAD tissues compared to normal lung tissues (Fig. 6A), while IDO1 expression remained consistent between cancerous and normal tissues (Fig. 6B). Additionally, we investigated the impact of TDO2 and IDO1 expression on overall survival in LUAD patients. TDO2 expression was found to be significantly associated with overall survival in these patients. These findings were further supported by similar patterns observed in the UALCAN database (Fig. S5). Collectively, these results highlights that TDO2, rather than IDO1, may serve as a potential therapeutic target in lung adenocarcinoma.

Inhibition of TDO2 treatment significantly decreases lung adenocarcinoma formation

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of TDO2 inhibition, we used C57BL/6 mice inoculated with murine LUAD LLC/iRFP cells via intratracheal transplantation. We tested two different TDO2 inhibitors, 680c91, which has low bioavailability and poor aqueous solubility, and LM10, which has improved solubility and bioavailability [14, 15]. TDO2 inhibitors were injected intraperitoneally every day for 2 weeks. After all the mice were sacrificed on the 20th day, the lungs were removed and analyzed (Fig. 7A). As shown by imaging analysis, the control group exhibited intense tumor formation compared to that in healthy lung tissue, while the tumors in the treated group were effectively suppressed by the inhibitors LM10 and 680c91. Bodyweight data revealed that the treatments did not cause toxicity to the mice (Fig. 7D). These findings indicate that TDO2 inhibition, particularly with 680c91, effectively suppresses tumor growth in lung adenocarcinoma models without causing toxicity, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic approach.

TDO2 inhibition enhances antitumor immunity

For further analysis, we measured the mRNA levels of Trp-catabolizing markers and AhR in lung tissue. We observed that the mRNA levels of Slc7a5, Tdo2 and Ahr markedly increased compared to those in healthy lung tissue. TDO2 inhibitors significantly downregulated the expression of Slc7a5, Tdo2, Ido1, Afmid, and Ahr (Fig. 8A). Additionally, in liver samples, TDO2 inhibitors downregulated the expression of markers related to Trp metabolism (Fig. S6). We next investigated the mRNA expression of immune checkpoint markers (4-1bbl, Gal-9, Hvem, Icosl, Pd-l1, and Pvr) and Treg markers (Foxp3 and Cd25). The results showed that almost all markers, especially 4-IBBL and ICOSL, were significantly upregulated in the control group compared to the healthy group. TDO2 inhibitors significantly reduced the expression of immune checkpoint and Treg markers (Fig. 8B-C).

Furthermore, we showed differences in tumor-associated macrophages, cytotoxic T cells, and T helper cell-mediated immunosuppression between the treatment and control groups. We observed a significant reduction in the tumor-associated macrophage (CD45 + CD11b + CD163+) and cytotoxic T-cell (CD45 + CD8 + PD-1+) populations, with almost no differences between the 680c91 and LM10 treatments (Fig. 8D-E). Moreover, the CD45 + CD4 + PD-1 + population was reduced by 680c91 compared to that of LM10. Taken together, consistent with the suppression of tumor formation, the populations of TAMs, CD8 + PD-1 + cells, and CD4 + PD-1 + cells were reduced in the treatment group.

Discussion

In 2020, lung cancer accounted for 18% of cancer-related deaths, and 1.8 million people died [16]. Carcinogens such as arsenic, asbestos, radon, and tobacco smoke play a significant role in the development of lung cancer [17–19]. These agents can cause genetic and epigenetic alterations in tumor genomes, leading to the development of this disease [17]. The mechanisms by which these carcinogens cause lung cancer include the induction of genetic damage to both oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes [20]. Despite the strong association between these carcinogens and lung cancer, the exact mechanisms by which they cause this disease are not fully understood. For this reason, it is necessary to investigate the mechanism of action of the carcinogens that induce lung cancer formation and progression.

Immune checkpoints play a crucial role in cancer by inhibiting the body’s natural antitumor immune response [21]. Overcoming these immune checkpoints, particularly by checkpoint blockade, has shown promise in generating antitumor activity [22, 23]. The carcinogen exposure studies are critical for elucidating the immune evasion mechanisms of tumor. Tobacco carcinogen, nicotine-derived nitrosaminoketone (NNK), induces tryptophan metabolism and immune suppression via induction of IDO1 [24]. Wang et al. (2019) reported that cigarette smoke and the carcinogen BaP-induce PD-L1 expression on lung epithelial cells in vitro and in vivo, which is mediated by AhR [5]. Here, we showed that BaP exposure upregulated the expression of multiple immune checkpoints, including PD-L1 and ICOSL. Recently, Zhan et al. reported that ICOS/ICOSL might be associated with prognosis of lung cancer in Chinese patients [25]. ICOSL can enhance tumor cell growth by inducing Foxp3 + Treg cell differentiation and reversing p38 pathway activation in triple-negative breast cancer [26]. Moreover, acute myeloid leukemia cells express ICOSL to promote the expansion of regulatory T cells [27]. Our findings revealed that activation of immune evasion mechanisms by carcinogen exposure (i.e. BaP) enables to identify potential therapeutic targets in lung cancer.

AhR can act as a transcription factor essential for maintaining various biological activities crucial for health, and its dysregulation plays a significant role in tumorigenesis [28]. It has been found to be overexpressed in human lung carcinomas, particularly in adenocarcinomas, suggesting a potential role in the development of lung cancer [29]. Wang et al. (2019) also demonstrated that BaP-induced PD-L1 expression was mediated by AhR and suggested the combination of ANF (an AHR inhibitor)/anti-PD-L1 as a novel therapeutic strategy [5]. Our results consistently indicate that ICOSL is mediated by AhR, suggesting that multiple immune checkpoints contribute to BaP-mediated immune evasion in lung cancer through AhR activation.

The activation of AhR can occur through interaction with both endogenous ligands (i.e. kynurenine, kynurenic acid), and exogenous ligands (i.e. BaP) [30]. Ye et al. conducted a comprehensive metabolomic analysis, revealing that tryptophan metabolism was significantly altered in benzo[a]pyrene-exposed H460 cells [11]. Since kynurenine is a key metabolite of tryptophan catabolism, we delved into the connection between the effects of BaP and Trp metabolism. Here, we observed a significant reduction at different time points in the BaP-induced PD-L1 and ICOSL levels in tryptophan (-) media, indicating that tryptophan metabolism involved in BaP-induced immune evasion. Activation of IDO1/TDO2 promotes the formation of kynurenine, an endogenous ligand of AhR [31]. We further investigated the effect of BaP exposure on tryptophan rate-limiting enzymes IDO1 and TDO2, revealing a significant upregulation of both. This induction underscores the critical role of Trp metabolism in BaP-driven immune evasion, primarily through endogenous AhR activation. Given the inherent differences between healthy BEAS-2B cells and LUAD-derived H1975 cells, distinct responses to carcinogen exposure were anticipated. Our findings indicate that in H1975, BaP-induced PD-L1 and ICOSL expression is governed by both endogenous (Trp metabolism) and exogenous (AhR) activation, whereas in BEAS-2B cells, it is driven predominantly by endogenous mechanisms.

Combination therapy with an IDO1 inhibitor and a PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor is expected to be a novel and effective treatment option and is currently attracting much attention [13]. Co-expression of TDO2 and PD-L1 was observed in clinical colon and gastric cancer specimens [14, 15]. However, whether TDO2 is correlated with PD-L1 or other immune checkpoint molecules in lung cancer has not been reported. Our results showed that TDO2i significantly suppressed the surface expression levels of PD-L1 and ICOSL in both cell lines whereas we did not observe any impact of IDO1 inhibition on the surface expression of immune checkpoints. These findings suggest that TDO2, plays a critical role in regulating immune checkpoint expression, highlighting its potential as a promising therapeutic target for LUAD treatment. Cui et al.. (2022) reported that TDO2 is highly expressed in 20 types of cancer, including LUAD [32]. Consistently, we identified through the online databases GEPIA and UALCAN that TDO2 expression is significantly higher in LUAD tissue compared to normal lung tissue, while no significant difference was observed in IDO1 expression. Additionally, high TDO2 levels were associated with poorer overall survival, further supporting its potential as a therapeutic target for LUAD.

Hsu et al. evaluated the effect of TDO2 inhibition on lung cancer transplanted via tail vein, revealing that after 16 days of treatment, the 680c91 significantly reduced tumor nodules compared to the control group [33]. In our study, C57BL/6 mice were orthotopically inoculated with LUAD cells (LLC/iRFP) and treated with TDO2 inhibitors 680c91 and LM10, both of which significantly reduced tumor growth. The significant induction of TDO2 and the non-significant change in IDO1 observed in our orthotopic LUAD model were consistent with LUAD patient data from the online databases GEPIA and UALCAN. Liu et al. indicated that SLC7A5 is a LUAD-specific prognostic biomarker and participates in forming immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [34]. Consistently, we investigated dramatically high mRNA expression of Slc7a5 in our orthotopic LUAD model. The significant induction of ICOSL and PD-L1 in the orthotopic LUAD model underscores the importance of studying carcinogen exposure in vitro. This approach not only helps to elucidate key molecular mechanisms driving cancer formation and progression but also enables the identification of potential therapeutic targets involved in immune evasion and tumor growth, offering valuable insights for the development of more effective cancer treatments.

An increasing number of studies have revealed the effects of the IDO/TDO2-Kyn-AhR axis on T cells [35–37]. More importantly, tryptophan depletion causes T cells to increase apoptosis and decreases cell proliferation [38]. CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, known as helper T cells (Th), and cytotoxic T cells (Tc) are white blood cell fractions that are thought to be involved in tumor-fighting cellular immune responses [39]. Inhibition of the immune checkpoint causes an increase in CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, while it causes a decrease in regulatory T cells by blocking different immunological modulations [40]. Additionally, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) may mediate the depletion of amino acids and the production of immunosuppressive metabolites [41]. Here, we demonstrated a significant reduction in the tumor-associated macrophage (CD45 + CD11b + CD163+) and cytotoxic T-cell (CD45 + CD8 + PD-1+) populations.

Conclusion

This study sheds light on the intricate mechanisms of BaP-induced immune evasion in LUAD, highlighting the critical role of the TDO2/AhR axis. By demonstrating the potential of TDO2 inhibitors to disrupt carcinogen-mediated immune checkpoint upregulation and enhance anti-tumor immunity, it opens new avenues for the development of targeted immunotherapeutic strategies in LUAD treatment. This study significantly enhances our comprehension of the immune evasion strategies employed by LUAD, while simultaneously emphasizing the promising therapeutic potential of TDO2 targeting in the fight against this malignancy (Fig. 9).
