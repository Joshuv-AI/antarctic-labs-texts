Involuntary treatment for substance use: application of Kass’ ethical framework to Alberta’s Compassionate Intervention Act
Authors: Patty Wilson, Bonnie Larson, Kate Colizza
Published: 2025

Key points

The intervention process outlined in Alberta’s Compassionate Intervention Act (CIA) — which will allow for the involuntary admission of people who use substances, even when they have been deemed to have capacity to make decisions about their care — lacks sufficient evidence of effectiveness and poses substantial ethical concerns.

In Canada, more than 50 000 opioid-related deaths have occurred since September 2024, mostly in British Columbia, Alberta, and Ontario, where governments are all in various stages of developing legislation for involuntary addiction treatment. Involuntary treatment is not new; for example, Alberta’s existing Mental Health Act allows for involuntary admission of people with impaired capacity and severe mental illness who would benefit from treatment. However, Alberta’s new Compassionate Intervention Act (CIA) expands the scope of the Mental Health Act to include involuntary admission of people who use substances, even when they have been deemed to have capacity to make decisions about their care.1 The process by which an individual can be apprehended and detained by the CIA is outlined in Figure 1. Since such policy changes are proposed as a public health response to the drug poisoning crisis, provincial legislation for involuntary addiction treatment should be interrogated through the lens of public health ethics. Applying Kass’ ethical framework2 involves determining a program’s goals and the evidence underpinning its effectiveness; examining potential burdens and strategies to minimize these burdens; and considering how the benefits and burdens are fairly balanced. Here, we apply Kass’ ethical framework to Alberta’s CIA.

According to Kass, a public health program must primarily aim to reduce morbidity or mortality for the intended beneficiaries of the program.2 Preventing deaths related to substance use is a clear public health imperative. Legislators have stated that the CIA is meant to stabilize and treat those with severe addiction who are “likely to cause [imminent] harm” to themselves or others.1

Factors used to estimate risk of harm in the CIA include a person being unable to meet their basic needs, having a history of drug poisonings, or being a frequent health system user.1 These criteria risk conflating likelihood of harm from substance use with harms caused by structural determinants of health, such as lack of housing, food security, and income. Characteristics that make an individual eligible for apprehension under the CIA are often the same barriers that preclude access to very limited existing voluntary addiction services. By relying on indicators of risk that are not necessarily caused by substance use, the CIA’s directive risks exacerbating social vulnerability rather than accurately assessing for substance use–related harm.

Next, Kass2 posits that the quality of evidence for efficacy of an intervention should increase proportionately to its ethical burdens. The Alberta government cites treatment program completion, ongoing employment, and patient satisfaction as potential benefits of mandated treatment; however the referenced studies enrolled professional health care providers, whose secure access to housing and health care makes them a poor proxy for the CIA’s target population.3 Additionally, a literature review concluded that evidence supporting involuntary treatment of adult nonoffenders is limited and methodologically weak.4 Key weaknesses include the use of outdated addiction treatment modalities and uncertainty as to whether positive outcomes were confounded by factors such as access to housing and medical care.4 Involuntary treatment is purported to provide care to those who would not otherwise access it. However, the limited research available most often relied on a voluntary treatment group, rather than a group that did not receive treatment, as a control, which highlighted the overall inferior outcomes in the involuntary treatment group, including decreased abstinence and a higher risk of fatal drug poisoning after treatment.4,5 This heightened risk is likely owing to the loss of opioid tolerance during detention combined with a higher likelihood of return to substance use upon release.4

The Kass framework categorizes ethical burdens into risks to privacy, liberty, and justice.2 The CIA grants a commission panel the authority to compel health care providers to provide information it “deems necessary.”1 It also permits disclosure of individually identifying health information to a provincially funded evaluation organization.1 Although policy-makers insist that data handling will comply with privacy laws, the CIA itself creates a legal gateway to information sharing that would normally require explicit patient consent, potentially compromising not only patients’ privacy rights but also the consciences of care providers.

In terms of risks to liberty, the CIA legislates that a person can be apprehended and held for up to 6 months under a care plan. Detention limits patients’ opportunity to access other voluntary services, including harm reduction. Patients cannot refuse prescribed medications for substance use regardless of their assessed capacity to consent.1 Interventions that violate a person’s autonomy and bodily integrity may expose policy-makers to litigation related to the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms.

Regarding risks to the principle of justice, the CIA’s criteria for assessing harm disproportionately targets people marginalized by systemic barriers to health and social services. For example, Indigenous patients who are subject to systemic racism within Alberta’s emergency departments6 may have misleading chart notations related to substance use that can be used against them under the CIA’s criteria.

Kass’ framework urges consideration of strategies to reduce ethical burdens. Although modern involuntary treatment is traditionally stewarded by extensively trained professionals, the CIA broadens these parameters to family members, peace officers, and unlicensed practitioners, thereby decreasing the potential for risk mitigation that stricter stewardship could provide. Of crucial relevance is Kass’ statement that whenever there is a choice between 2 interventions, the less ethically burdensome should be chosen if it is not less effective.2

The next step in Kass’ framework scrutinizes the fairness of a program’s implementation, ensuring equitable benefits and avoiding discriminatory application.2 The CIA stipulates an appeals process, but the qualifications required by appointees to the review commission are unclear. Concerns have been raised about the lack of transparency and accountability in the existing Mental Health Review Panels,7 and the new CIA fails to address these issues. Furthermore, involuntary treatment diverts resources away from voluntary programs known to be more effective.5 An additional aspect of equity and discrimination is that the CIA relies, in part, on trusted providers to identify those with severe substance use who meet criteria for involuntary treatment. As a result, people with unmet health and social needs may avoid seeking any care for fear of apprehension and detention.5

The final step in Kass’ framework involves weighing the expected benefits of a program against its identified burdens.2 Provincial governments’ enthusiasm for broadly expanding involuntary treatment focuses on addressing the burden of the drug poisoning crisis. However, in other jurisdictions, involuntary treatment has proven more expensive than voluntary treatment.8 The Government of Alberta has allocated $180 million to create 300 involuntary treatment beds, compared with $140 million to expand voluntary treatment by 4000 spaces.9,10 We argue that legislation like Alberta’s CIA raises serious concerns about the justification for such an expensive, enforced program, given that its compromises to privacy, liberty, and justice are far from counterbalanced by evidence for its efficacy and risk mitigation strategies. Although the Alberta government intends to evaluate this intervention, whether the data will be publicly available is unknown. An evaluation study showing program effectiveness would still be unlikely to justify the CIA’s foundational ethical challenges that violate personal autonomy, risk discriminatory application, and erode the therapeutic trust essential to a safe health care system.4,5 Resourcing evidence-based, communityled voluntary services would likely yield better substance use and mental health outcomes than involuntary care.4

Instead of instituting ethically fraught interventions with weak evidentiary support, Alberta and other provinces should strengthen and evaluate a full spectrum of patient-centred, evidence-based services, including supportive housing and community resources for patients who already require care under the existing Mental Health Act or are seeking trusted care on their own terms.
