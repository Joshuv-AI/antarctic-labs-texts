Romosozumab super-responders in clinical practice: insights from a large Italian multicenter cohort of women with severe postmenopausal osteoporosis

ABSTRACT
AbstractSummaryLimited data on romosozumab super-responders. In 149 Italian women with severe osteoporosis, mean LS BMD gain was + 13.7%; 18% were super-responders (≥ 20%). Predictors: high CTX/age/low Z-score for LS; prior femoral fracture for FN. Highlights site-specific response drivers in real-world setting.PurposeTo evaluate romosozumab effectiveness in a large Italian multicentre cohort of women with severe postmenopausal osteoporosis and identify predictors of high-magnitude densitometric response. Real-world evidence on romosozumab is expanding, yet data on high-magnitude response variability and clinical determinants of “super-responder” phenotypes remain limited.MethodsRetrospective analysis of postmenopausal women who completed 12 months of romosozumab across five Italian centers. DXA scans were performed at baseline and after treatment. Data included clinical history, morphometric and clinical fractures, and prior anti-osteoporotic therapies. “Very good responders” were defined as ≥ 10% BMD gain; “super-responders” as ≥ 20%. Multivariate logistic regression identified independent predictors.Results149 women (mean age 69.4 ± 9.9 years) were included. Prevalent vertebral (85.2%), femoral (30.9%), and non-vertebral (49.0%) fractures were common; 82% had prior therapy. Mean BMD gains were + 13.7% at lumbar spine (LS), + 5.9% at femoral neck (FN), and + 4.4% at total hip (TH). Very good responders (≥ 10%) occurred in 65.2% at LS, 17.8% at FN, and 13.3% at TH; super-responders (≥ 20%) in 17.8% at LS and 5.9% at FN. LS super-response was independently associated with higher baseline CTX (OR 1.42; p = 0.018), older age (OR 1.10 per year; p = 0.036), and lower baseline LS Z-score (OR 0.29; p = 0.003). FN super-response linked to prior femoral fracture (OR 14.6; p = 0.020).ConclusionRomosozumab demonstrated high effectiveness in this real-world cohort, with a substantial proportion achieving high-magnitude BMD responses despite extensive prior treatment. Distinct site-specific predictors emerged: bone turnover and age/Z-score for LS, prior femoral fracture for FN.Keywords: bone mineral density, osteoporosis, romosozumab, treatment response

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a chronic metabolic bone disease characterized by reduced bone mass, deterioration of bone microarchitecture, and increased risk of fragility fractures. These fractures, particularly at vertebral and hip sites, represent a major cause of morbidity, mortality, and healthcare burden worldwide, including in Italy [1].

Romosozumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that binds and inhibits sclerostin, exerting a dual-effect by stimulating bone formation and reducing bone resorption. This mechanism leads to rapid and substantial increases in bone mineral density (BMD) and significant fracture risk reduction, as demonstrated in pivotal trials [2, 3]. However, both observational and interventional studies of anti-osteoporosis therapies including bisphosphonates, denosumab, and teriparatide, have shown that prior treatment may influence the subsequent response to romosozumab [4–14]. In particular, long-term bisphosphonate exposure appears to attenuate the anabolic response [6], likely due to persistent suppression of bone turnover. After teriparatide, BMD gains with romosozumab appear somewhat attenuated compared with treatment-naïve patients, although generally less markedly than after prolonged bisphosphonate exposure. A recent multicenter case–control study reported smaller BMD increases in patients switched from teriparatide compared with treatment-naïve individuals after 12 months of romosozumab. Earlier prospective studies also showed that prior teriparatide exposure may reduce the anabolic window during romosozumab therapy and blunt the early bone formation marker response (PINP) [10, 15, 16]. In contrast, prior denosumab therapy presents specific challenges. Indeed, the rebound increase in bone resorption after its discontinuation may blunt the early anabolic effect of romosozumab and limit hip BMD gains. [10–17]. Overall, while treatment-naïve patients tend to exhibit the greatest BMD response, substantial inter-individual variability has been consistently observed in both naïve [2, 18] and pretreated populations. Notably, trial data and real-world cohorts [2, 8, 18] indicate that a subset of patients achieves exceptionally large BMD gains, suggesting that factors beyond prior treatment status modulate romosozumab efficacy.

In the Italian clinical setting, romosozumab is generally reserved for women with established fragility fractures and substantial prior treatment exposure, resulting in cohorts enriched with severe skeletal phenotypes. However, prior therapy is not unique to Italy, as many candidates worldwide have already received antiresorptive or anabolic agents. Understanding response heterogeneity in this pretreated, high-risk population is therefore clinically relevant beyond the national context.

The present multicentre, real-life Italian study aims to quantify the proportion of patients achieving a very high BMD response to romosozumab and to identify clinical, densitometric, and biochemical factors associated with such outcomes. Recognizing predictors of optimal response may help refine patient selection and improve the personalization of osteoporosis treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This multicentre retrospective observational study included postmenopausal women with osteoporosis who initiated romosozumab from September 2022, when romosozumab first became available in Italy, to September 2024, across five Italian referral centres: the Endocrine Unit, Fondazione IRCCS Cà Granda Ospedale Maggiore Policlinico (Milan); the Bone Metabolism and Diabetes Unit, IRCCS Istituto Auxologico Italiano (Milan); the Endocrinology and Metabolism Unit, University Hospital Santa Maria della Misericordia (Udine); the Endocrinology and Diabetes Unit, Fondazione Policlinico Universitario Campus Bio-Medico (Rome) and the Endocrinology Unit, ASST Grande Ospedale Metropolitano Niguarda (Milan).

Eligible participants were postmenopausal women fulfilling the clinical requirements established by AIFA-Nota 79 [19], which regulates the reimbursable use of romosozumab in Italy, and completed 12 consecutive months of romosozumab therapy. According to these criteria, patients had to present severe osteoporosis documented by a T-score ≤ −2.5 at the LS or proximal femur with ≥ 1 prior fragility fracture, or alternatively a T-score ≤ –2.0 in the presence of ≥ 2 moderate/severe vertebral fractures or of a recent femoral fracture within the preceding two years. Romosozumab prescription further required a history of inadequate response, contraindication, or intolerance to at least one previous osteoporosis therapy, unless prior treatment was considered clinically inappropriate based on fracture risk. Furthermore, women could be treated with romosozumab if a T-score ≤ −2.5 at LS or femur was associated with ≥ 2 non-vertebral, non-femoral fragility fractures.

All patients were required to have baseline and end-of-treatment dual X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) assessments performed on the same certified densitometry instrument within each centre, ensuring consistency. All participating centres used Hologic densitometers (Hologic Inc., Marlborough, MA, USA), and measurements were performed according to manufacturer recommendations and local quality control procedures. Baseline DXA had to be obtained within a window ranging from two months before to one month after treatment initiation, and follow-up DXA within two months after the final monthly dose. Lumbar spine BMD was calculated using only evaluable vertebrae. Vertebrae affected by fractures or degenerative changes likely to artificially increase BMD were excluded, based on DXA assessment and available spinal imaging. Lumbar spine measurements were considered valid when at least two vertebrae were available for analysis. Clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records and included demographics, fracture history, including morphometric vertebral fractures assessed through Genant semiquantitative criteria [20], biochemical parameters, and prior osteoporosis treatments. Biochemical evaluation included serum C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (when available), and 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D]. Analyses were performed in the local laboratories of each participating centre using standardized automated assays. Reference intervals were consistent across centres and are reported in the tables. All patients received calcium and vitamin D supplementation when needed. Vitamin D supplementation was guided by serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D concentrations, with the aim of maintaining levels ≥ 30 ng/mL (≥ 75 nmol/L), consistent with commonly accepted thresholds for vitamin D sufficiency in patients with osteoporosis. Patients were classified as “previously treated” if they had received ≥ 12 months of continuous anti-osteoporotic therapy before starting romosozumab. Those with < 12 months of prior therapy were classified as “naïve/minimally pretreated”. This definition ensured a clear separation between sustained antiresorptive exposure and limited or negligible prior treatment, since for an anti-fracture effect to be obtained (with the exception of romosozumab) at least 12 months of therapy is needed [21]. In patients previously treated with denosumab or teriparatide, romosozumab was generally initiated immediately or shortly after treatment discontinuation. In contrast, the interval following bisphosphonate therapy was more variable, ranging from approximately 12 to 30 months after zoledronate infusion and from 0 to 18 months after oral bisphosphonates. Uniform exclusion criteria were applied across centres. Patients were excluded if they had metabolic bone diseases other than osteoporosis (e.g. Paget’s disease), primary bone malignancies, severe renal or hepatic impairment (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73m2 or Child–Pugh classes B or C), uncontrolled endocrine disorders affecting bone metabolism, including primary hyperparathyroidism or hypocalcaemia, or any previous myocardial infarction or stroke prior to romosozumab initiation. Because of the real-world design of the study, other potential contributors to secondary osteoporosis were not formal exclusion criteria. However, no patients in the cohort had active rheumatologic or gastrointestinal diseases requiring chronic glucocorticoid therapy or biologic agents at the time of romosozumab initiation. All data were fully anonymized before analysis. The study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the ethics committees of all participating institutions.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the percentage change in BMD at the LS, FN, and TH after 12 months of romosozumab, assessed by DXA. Secondary outcomes included the identification of patients achieving high-magnitude densitometric responses. To further characterize variability in treatment response, two clinically meaningful densitometric categories were predefined: a “very good response,” defined as a ≥ 10% BMD increase, and a “super-response,” defined as a ≥ 20% increase. The ≥ 10% threshold was based on responder definitions used in FRAME [2] and supported by another real-world data study. [18], where a substantial proportion of naïve patients exceeded this level. Moreover, both studies [2, 18] reported a non-negligible subset of individuals achieving exceptionally large gains, although not formally quantified; this observation informed the inclusion of a ≥ 20% cut-off to capture high-magnitude responses. These thresholds were applied across all skeletal sites, and response categories were subsequently analyzed in relation to clinical and densitometric predictors.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with distribution assessed by visual inspection and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages. Comparisons between naïve and pre-treated patients were performed using the independent-samples t test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the χ2 or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Binary logistic regression models were used to identify independent predictors of densitometric response. Separate site-specific models were built for: i) LS super-response (ΔLS ≥ 20%); ii) FN super-response (ΔFN ≥ 20%); iii) TH response (ΔTH ≥ 10%). Covariates were selected a priori based on biological plausibility and previous literature, and included age, prior osteoporosis therapy, baseline skeletal status at the corresponding site, baseline bone turnover (CTX), and fracture history. For LS models, the presence of isolated vertebral fractures at baseline was included as a clinically relevant marker of disease severity, supported by its significant difference between super-responders and non-super-responders in descriptive analyses. Baseline skeletal status was modeled using LS Z-score for LS models and FN T-score for FN models to account for site-specific differences and minimize collinearity with age. Age was included a priori as a clinically relevant confounder, regardless of its univariate association with the outcome.

In addition, variables showing clinically relevant differences in descriptive analyses were considered to ensure adequate adjustment for potential confounders.

Because baseline CTX exhibited wide variability, an additional model was run after scaling CTX by 100 (CTX_100 = CTX/100) to improve interpretability of the odds ratios without altering model performance.

Given the limited number of super-response events, multivariable models were considered exploratory; therefore, parsimonious site-specific models were also tested as sensitivity analyses to reduce overfitting. Accordingly, multivariable analyses—particularly at femoral sites—were intentionally restricted to a limited number of clinically selected covariates, and findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) are reported, and a two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Among the 149 included women, 122 (81.9%) were classified as previously treated (≥ 12 months of prior anti-osteoporotic therapy), while 27 were considered naïve/minimally pretreated. Before starting romosozumab, most pretreated patients had been exposed to bisphosphonates: 114 (93.4%) had received a bisphosphonate at any time and 108 (88.5%) as the last therapy prior to romosozumab. Exposure to other agents was less frequent: 20 patients had received denosumab, with only 6 (4.9%) as the most recent treatment, and 25 had previously used teriparatide, with 8 (6.6%) receiving it last. Treatment sequences were heterogeneous, with 16 patients receiving two therapeutic classes and 6 receiving three classes before romosozumab. Notably, 25 pretreated patients (20.5%) had been off therapy for more than 12 months before romosozumab initiation.

Among the naïve/minimally pretreated group, none had received ≥ 6 months of anti-osteoporotic therapy in the preceding 2 years, confirming negligible prior exposure. Among previously treated patients, 68.9% met criteria for non-response to prior therapy [16], defined as ≥ 1 new fragility fracture and/or lack of BMD improvement during ≥ 12 months of anti-osteoporotic treatment. This underscores the high-risk nature of the study population.

Mean age, renal function, bone turnover markers, and vitamin D levels were comparable between naïve/minimally pretreated patients and previously treated patients. Baseline BMD was markedly reduced at all skeletal sites, with mean T-scores being comparable between groups. Fragility fractures were highly prevalent, with vertebral, femoral, and non-vertebral fractures present in 85.2%, 30.9%, and 49.0% of patients, respectively. The SDI, prior femoral fractures, vertebral fractures, and combined vertebral–femoral fractures were comparable between naïve and pretreated patients.

Densitometric response to romosozumab

In the overall cohort, BMD significantly increased at all sites (Table 1). Naïve patients showed slightly greater gains, particularly at the LS and TH, although differences were not statistically significant. Clinically relevant responses were frequent across all skeletal sites.

At the LS, 65.2% of patients achieved a ≥ 10% BMD increase, and 17.8% reached ≥ 20%.

At the FN, 17.8% of the cohort attained a ≥ 10% gain, and 5.9% achieved a ≥ 20% increase, highlighting a subset of patients with marked femoral responsiveness. At the TH, 13.3% reached ≥ 10% improvement and 2.8% met the ≥ 20% threshold (Fig. 1). Naïve patients tended to show higher proportions of both ≥ 10% and ≥ 20% responders at all sites, but these differences were not statistically significant.

Super-responder patients at the LS showed higher baseline CTX, greater CTX suppression at 6 months, and lower baseline LS Z-score, reflecting more severe baseline skeletal impairment. Isolated vertebral fractures were also more frequent among LS super-responders. No differences were observed in age, renal function, vitamin D levels, or previous therapy between LS super-responders and the remaining patients. The FN super-responder group displayed a higher prevalence of prior femoral fractures and a lower baseline FN T-score. Other clinical and biochemical parameters did not differ significantly between groups.

Only 4 patients were TH super-responders and, thus, their characteristics should be interpreted cautiously. Super-responder TH patients had lower baseline TH T-score (–3.7 ± 1.1) and were more likely to have a history of femoral fracture (100%) as compared with not-super-responder patients (−2.4 ± 0.7, p = 0.004 and 28.1%, p = 0.002, respectively). Mean age (64.5 ± 9.9 vs 69.2 ± 9.0 years), CTX (528.5 ± 349.6 vs 349.3 ± 266.0 ng/L), ALP (94.2 ± 44.2 vs 73.2 ± 25.1 U/L), eGFR (72.6 ± 25.1 vs 80.9 ± 14.9 mL/min/1.73m2), and vitamin D levels (43.7 ± 14.6 vs 47.3 ± 16.4 ng/mL) were not significantly different between TH super-responders and TH not-super-responder patients. Absolute BMD increases were also greater among responders, with mean TH changes of 0.074 ± 0.035 g/cm2 compared with 0.018 ± 0.030 g/cm2 in non-responders (p < 0.001) (Tables 4 and 5).

In the multivariable model for LS super-response, age, baseline LS Z-score, and baseline CTX emerged as independent predictors. Prior therapy and the presence of vertebral fractures were not significantly associated with the probability of achieving ≥ 20% LS gain.

Given the limited number of super-response events, a parsimonious model including age, baseline LS Z-score and baseline CTX was also tested. In this model, baseline CTX remained independently associated with LS super-response (OR 1.45 per 100 pg/mL increase, 95% CI 1.13–1.86, p = 0.003), whereas age and baseline LS Z-score were not statistically significant.

For the FN super-response, the logistic model showed that a previous femoral fracture was the strongest independent predictor of a ≥ 20% FN increase, with an approximately 15-fold higher probability of achieving super-response.

Given the very limited number of FN super-response events, FN analyses should be considered exploratory. In a highly parsimonious model including only prior femoral fracture, this variable remained strongly associated with FN super-response (OR 18.6, 95% CI 2.21–156.7, p = 0.007).

For the TH, the analysis focused on a ≥ 10% BMD increase because the number of patients achieving ≥ 20% TH gain was too small for reliable multivariate modelling. In the model including age, prior therapy, prior femoral fracture, and baseline TH T-score, both prior femoral fracture (OR 3.74, 95% CI 1.05–13.35, p = 0.042) and lower baseline TH T-score (OR 0.40, 95% CI 0.17–0.95, p = 0.037) were independently associated with a ≥ 10% TH increase, whereas age and prior treatment history were not significant predictors.

Discussion

In this multicenter real-world study, romosozumab produced substantial BMD improvements across all skeletal sites, with mean gains of + 13.7% at the LS, + 5.9% at the FN, and + 4.4% at the TH. Clinically meaningful responses were frequent: nearly two-thirds of patients achieved a ≥ 10% LS increase and almost one in five exceeded 20%. It should also be noted that LS BMD increases below 10% do not necessarily represent poor response to romosozumab. In both clinical trials and real-world studies, clinically meaningful improvements are observed across a wide range of responses; therefore, patients with < 10% LS gain likely represent a heterogeneous group of moderate responders rather than a clearly defined non-response phenotype. The ≥ 20% threshold was chosen to capture the extreme upper tail of the BMD response distribution, which has been consistently observed in randomized trials and real-world cohorts [2, 16]. It should also be acknowledged that percentage BMD changes may partly reflect lower baseline values, as individuals with very low baseline BMD can exhibit larger proportional increases. However, in our cohort, high-magnitude responders also showed substantially greater absolute BMD gains, supporting the robustness of these findings. At femoral sites, 18% and 13% of women attained ≥ 10% improvement at the FN and TH, respectively, with a smaller but consistent subgroup achieving ≥ 20% gains. Although naïve patients showed a numerical trend toward greater BMD gains, particularly at the LS and TH, these differences were not statistically significant, and prior treatment exposure did not influence the likelihood of achieving a super-response. The comparable baseline bone turnover between naïve/minimally pretreated and previously treated patients may partly explain the similar magnitude of BMD gains observed between groups. In this real-world cohort, most previously treated patients had been exposed to bisphosphonates, with only a small proportion receiving denosumab or teriparatide as their most recent therapy. In addition, the interval between prior treatment and romosozumab initiation was variable, and some patients had discontinued therapy for a prolonged period. These heterogeneous treatment histories, including sequences of anabolic therapy followed by antiresorptive treatment and variable intervals between therapies, may have allowed partial recovery of bone turnover, contributing to the comparable CTX levels observed at baseline. Two distinct predictors of high-magnitude response emerged. At the LS, higher baseline CTX, lower LS Z-score and age independently predicted super-response after adjustment, supporting the concept that romosozumab exerts its greatest anabolic effect in a metabolically active, trabecular-rich environment. At the FN, prior femoral fracture was the strongest determinant of high-magnitude response.

These associations remained robust across adjusted analyses

Compared with the pivotal trials FRAME and ARCH [2, 3] and most previously published real-world cohorts [5, 8, 9, 22, 26, 27], our population exhibited a generally more severe fragility profile, characterized by lower baseline BMD and a markedly higher burden of fractures, particularly hip fractures, affecting nearly one in three women. Furthermore, the proportion of previously treated patients was substantially higher than in most cohorts [5, 6, 8, 27], apart from another real-world Italian study [9], likely for the same prescription-driven reasons. Despite this unfavorable baseline profile, the magnitude of BMD improvement observed in our cohort was similar to that reported in FRAME and ARCH [1, 2], as well as in the Danish [28] and Korean cohorts [22], and exceeded the responses observed in other studies [5, 8, 9, 27]. The lower gains described in Gielen’s cohort [27] may partly reflect the older age of participants, consistent with previous observations linking advanced age to attenuated BMD responses [8]. In addition, the modest effects reported in other studies [5, 9] are likely related to the higher proportion of patients transitioning from denosumab, a therapeutic sequence known to blunt subsequent BMD increases more than prior bisphosphonate or teriparatide exposure [10]. Taken together, our findings indicate that romosozumab can achieve substantial densitometric improvements even in real-world populations characterized by advanced skeletal fragility and complex treatment histories.

When considering higher-response thresholds, our findings can be compared with those from the FRAME trial [2] and the Japanese real-world cohort reported by Kashii et al. [18]. This comparison, however, applies only to “very good responders”, as both studies included patients with larger responses but did not quantify super-responders.

At the lumbar spine, the proportion of very good responders in our cohort was broadly comparable to that observed in FRAME [2], both overall and among treatment-naïve women, consistent with the lack of significant differences between naïve and pretreated patients. In contrast, LS responses were smaller than those reported by Kashii et al. [18], whose fully naïve population displayed larger gains. This difference is plausible given the smaller naïve subgroup in our cohort and prior treatment exposure, although the lower baseline LS-BMD, previously identified as a predictor of spine response [5, 8], may explain why LS improvements remained comparable to FRAME despite extensive pretreatment.

For the TH, very good responder rates were comparable to those reported in FRAME [2] and by Kashii et al. [18], despite extensive prior treatment in our cohort. At the FN, very good responder rates in naïve patients exceeded those reported by Kashii et al. [18], although their population was entirely treatment-naïve. FRAME [2] did not report FN responder categories, preventing direct comparison at this site. Taken together, these findings suggest that the generally favorable femoral responses observed in our cohort, both the similarity at the TH and the higher gains at the FN, may partly reflect the markedly higher prevalence of prior femoral fractures in our population, a characteristic that emerged as a potential driver of enhanced cortical-site responsiveness.

Previous studies have identified several baseline predictors of BMD response to romosozumab, including lower LS-BMD, higher bone turnover markers, treatment-naïve status, and shorter prior antiresorptive exposure [5, 8, 17]. However, these analyses focused on overall or low-threshold responses and did not examine very good or super-response categories. Our analysis specifically targeted the extreme upper tail of the BMD response distribution (≥ 20% increase), a phenotype that may be driven by different biological determinants than those associated with mean treatment effects. In our study, by specifically evaluating LS super-response, we found that lower baseline LS Z-score, age, and higher baseline CTX were independently associated with this outcome. These factors align with the patterns reported for general treatment response in earlier work, suggesting that they reflect a metabolic profile particularly favourable to the anabolic action of romosozumab, regardless of prior treatment history. Higher CTX likely reflects a metabolically active skeletal environment in which the anabolic effect of romosozumab can be more pronounced. The direction of the age effect likely reflects a conditional association emerging after adjustment for age-matched bone density and baseline skeletal status, rather than a direct biological advantage of older age per se. Baseline CTX levels in our cohort likely also reflect the heterogeneous treatment histories typical of real-world populations, including variable intervals from previous antiresorptive therapy, which may have allowed partial recovery of bone turnover before romosozumab initiation. At the FN, however, our findings introduce a novel element: previous femoral fracture emerged as a strong, independent predictor of high-magnitude response. This association has not been reported in earlier studies and may reflect the structural vulnerability of cortical-rich sites in hip-fracture patients, offering a new perspective on individual variability in response to romosozumab.

A potential structural explanation for the predictive role of prior femoral fracture can be hypothesized. A previous hip fracture may reflect a degree of cortical deterioration that standard DXA cannot fully capture [26], thereby potentially creating a greater “reserve” for measurable improvement under romosozumab. Although DXA remains the reference tool for assessing treatment response, it provides only areal BMD and may underestimate cortical deterioration in patients with hip fractures. Evidence from DXA-based 3D modelling (3D-Shaper) shows that postmenopausal women with prior hip fracture exhibit substantial cortical thinning and reduced trabecular volumetric density despite areal BMD values often overlapping with non-fractured controls. In a dedicated case–control analysis using 3D-Shaper [23], hip-fracture patients demonstrated markedly impaired cortical structure and reduced femoral strength indices, highlighting that standard DXA may underestimate the extent of cortical deterioration in this phenotype.

This framework is highly relevant for interpreting our findings. Recent 3D-Shaper analyses from the FRAME and ARCH trials [25] demonstrated that romosozumab significantly increases cortical vBMD, cortical thickness, and estimated femoral strength, with similar effects later confirmed in an independent real-world cohort [26]. This concept is supported by HR-pQCT studies showing increased cortical porosity in women with hip fractures [29]. In addition, structural imaging analyses from the ARCH trial using quantitative CT and finite-element analysis demonstrated that romosozumab significantly improves volumetric bone density and bone strength parameters compared with alendronate [30]. Together, these studies indicate that romosozumab effectively targets cortical deficits that remain partly invisible to aBMD. This provides a biologically coherent framework to interpret the enhanced femoral responsiveness we observed in women with prior femoral fracture, who likely carry a greater reservoir of “recoverable” cortical microstructural damage. However, in the absence of direct structural imaging, this interpretation should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Clinically, these findings are relevant because they show that romosozumab remains highly effective even in previously treated patients, indicating that prior therapy does not limit the chance of achieving substantial BMD gains. Moreover, our results highlight two practical predictors of enhanced response: lower baseline bone mass at the spine and a history of femoral fracture at cortical-rich sites. In particular, the identification of prior hip fracture as a strong determinant of femoral improvement is notable, as it suggests that patients with the most severe cortical deficits, often considered difficult to treat, may benefit disproportionately from romosozumab. This information may help clinicians better identify patients who stand to gain the most from anabolic therapy. Moreover, recent studies have also explored the effects of shorter romosozumab treatment courses (31, 32). In this context, identifying patients with a “super-responder” phenotype may have important clinical implications, as individuals achieving particularly large BMD gains could potentially benefit from shorter anabolic treatment durations.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design may introduce selection and information bias, and causal inference cannot be established. Second, the proportion of treatment-naïve patients was relatively small, limiting our ability to compare naïve versus pretreated groups or to explore interaction effects between prior therapies and response. Third, although most women had been previously treated, the sample size was insufficient to discriminate the specific impact of individual drug classes, particularly denosumab or sequential therapies, on BMD outcomes or super-response likelihood. Fourth, bone formation markers such as P1NP were not systematically available in this real-world cohort, as they are not routinely assessed in Italian clinical practice, precluding their inclusion in the analysis. Fifth, all participants were of Caucasian ethnicity; therefore, the generalizability of these findings to other ethnic groups, particularly Asian populations in whom romosozumab has been extensively studied, may be limited. Sixth, the analysis of cortical-site responsiveness relied on DXA-derived outcomes, and our mechanistic interpretation regarding greater cortical deficits in hip-fracture patients cannot be confirmed directly in the absence of 3D-SHAPER or HR-pQCT imaging, which would be required to quantify cortical porosity or volumetric deficits.

In conclusion, this multicenter real-world study confirms that romosozumab is a highly effective therapeutic option for postmenopausal women with severe osteoporosis, including those with extensive fracture history and prior anti-osteoporotic treatment. Beyond the overall robust densitometric gains, our findings identify two clinically meaningful predictors of high-magnitude response: a turnover and baseline BMD dependent pattern at the LS, and a cortical fragility pattern at the proximal femur, where previous femoral fracture emerged as a key determinant of enhanced responsiveness. These results provide new insight into the skeletal phenotypes that derive the greatest benefit from romosozumab and may help clinicians tailor therapeutic choices for patients at highest fracture risk. Further prospective and imaging-based studies are warranted to validate these predictors and to clarify the structural mechanisms underlying femoral-site response.
