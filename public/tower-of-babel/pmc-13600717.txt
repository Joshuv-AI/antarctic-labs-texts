Getting your publication right – Part 3 structuring a research manuscript: A guide to writing clinical trials
Authors: Aditya Mitra, R. S. Mohan Kumar, Shishir Singh, Velmurugan Natanasabapathy, Sonali Sharma, Geeta Asthana, Sonali Taneja, Suparna Ganguly Saha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clinical trials form the cornerstone of evidence-based healthcare and play a crucial role in translating research into clinical practice. However, successful publication of clinical trials requires not only scientific rigor but also strategic planning, ethical transparency, accurate reporting, and effective scientific writing. This narrative review explores the “art” of publishing clinical trials, highlighting essential components from protocol development to manuscript submission and peer-review navigation. Key aspects discussed include formulation of a focused research question, trial registration, adherence to Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines, ethical considerations, statistical reporting, authorship integrity, and journal selection. Common reasons for manuscript rejection and practical approaches to improve acceptance rates are also reviewed. The review further emphasizes the importance of clarity, reproducibility, data presentation, and avoidance of publication bias. By integrating methodological standards with effective communication skills, researchers can enhance the quality, visibility, and impact of their clinical trial publications. This review aims to guide clinicians, academicians, and early-career researchers in preparing scientifically robust and publishable clinical trial manuscripts.

1. INTRODUCTION

Clinical research forms the foundation of evidence-based dental practice, enabling clinicians to make informed decisions regarding diagnosis, prevention, and treatment strategies. In dentistry, the transition from empiricism to evidence-based care has been largely driven by the increasing reliance on well-designed clinical studies, particularly randomized controlled trials (RCTs).[1] A clinical trial is an evaluation technique that can be used to measure whether a particular programme is working: whether it has any impact, and how large that impact is.

Endodontics, being a procedure-intensive specialty, relies heavily on clinical trials to validate treatment protocols, materials, and technologies. RCTs are considered the gold standard for evaluating therapeutic effectiveness and safety, especially for interventions such as root canal disinfection techniques, obturation materials, and regenerative procedures.[2] Clinical trials in endodontics help in establishing the efficacy of novel materials (e.g., bioceramics, irrigants, comparing treatment outcomes (e.g., single- vs. multiple-visit RCT) and assessing long-term prognosis of therapies.

The value of clinical trials is not only determined by their design but also by the quality of reporting. Poorly reported trials can lead to misinterpretation of results, biased conclusions, and ultimately flawed clinical decision-making.[3]

2. OVERVIEW OF CLINICAL TRIALS

2.1. Definition and purpose of clinical trials

Clinical trials are systematically designed research studies conducted on human participants to evaluate the safety, efficacy, and clinical performance of therapeutic, diagnostic, or preventive interventions. They represent the highest level of evidence in evidence-based dentistry, particularly in endodontics, where clinical decision-making often relies on outcomes such as pain reduction, healing of periapical lesions, and long-term tooth survival.[4]

The primary purpose of clinical trials is to generate reliable and unbiased data that can inform clinical practice, guide treatment protocols, and improve patient outcomes. In endodontics, clinical trials play a crucial role in assessing new materials (e.g., sealers, irrigants), techniques (e.g., rotary instrumentation, obturation methods), and adjunctive therapies (e.g., lasers, disinfection strategies).[5] Furthermore, well-designed trials help bridge the gap between laboratory research and real-world clinical applicability, ensuring that innovations translate into meaningful patient benefits.[67]

2.2. Types of clinical trials

Clinical trials can be broadly categorized based on their methodological design into randomized and nonrandomized studies.

Randomized controlled trials

RCTs are considered the gold standard for evaluating clinical interventions due to their ability to minimize bias. In RCTs, participants are randomly allocated to different intervention groups, ensuring that confounding variables are evenly distributed.[8] This randomization enhances internal validity and allows for a more accurate estimation of the true effect of the intervention.

Nonrandomized trials

Nonrandomized trials, also referred to as quasi-experimental studies, do not involve random allocation of participants. Instead, group assignment may be based on clinical judgment, patient preference, or logistical considerations.[9] While these studies are easier to conduct and may be more feasible in certain clinical scenarios, they are more susceptible to selection bias and confounding factors.

In endodontics, nonrandomized trials are often employed when ethical or practical constraints limit the use of randomization, such as in studies involving rare conditions or emergency interventions. Although they provide valuable preliminary data, their findings must be interpreted with caution due to reduced internal validity.[10]

2.3. Classification of randomized controlled trials

RCTs can be further classified based on their underlying hypothesis, type of control, and level of blinding.

Based on hypothesis

Superiority trials aim to demonstrate that one intervention is more effective than another. These are particularly relevant in endodontics when newer techniques offer advantages such as reduced chair time or improved patient comfort[12]Equivalence trials are designed to show that two interventions have similar clinical effects within a specified range. These trials are useful when comparing alternative materials or techniques that may differ in cost or availability but are expected to perform similarly.[13]

3. FORMULATING A RESEARCH QUESTION

A well-formulated research question is the cornerstone of a robust clinical trial. In endodontics, where multiple variables such as host response, microbial complexity, and procedural techniques influence outcomes, a clearly defined question ensures methodological precision and clinical relevance.[20]

3.1. Identifying knowledge gaps

The process of formulating a research question begins with identifying gaps in existing knowledge. In endodontics, these gaps may arise from conflicting evidence, lack of high-quality randomized trials, or evolving technologies such as advanced imaging modalities and contemporary treatment approaches. Knowledge gaps can be recognized through critical appraisal of published literature, observing inconsistencies in clinical outcomes, or identifying areas where current treatment protocols lack strong evidence. Importantly, not all gaps warrant investigation. The selected research question must be clinically meaningful, ethically justifiable, and capable of contributing to improved patient care.

3.2. Literature review and evidence mapping

A comprehensive literature review is essential to refine the research question and avoid duplication of existing studies. This involves systematic searching of databases such as PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane Library, followed by critical appraisal of relevant studies.[21] Evidence mapping further enhances this process by visually or systematically organizing available data to identify trends, strengths, and deficiencies in the literature. In addition, reviewing systematic reviews and meta-analyses provides insight into the quality of existing evidence and helps define the need for further trials.

3.3. PICO framework

The PICO framework is a widely accepted tool for structuring clinical research questions and enhancing their clarity and focus. It consists of four key components:

4. STUDY DESIGN AND METHODOLOGY

A rigorously designed methodology is fundamental to the validity, reliability, and clinical applicability of a trial.[22]

4.1. Study protocol development

The study protocol serves as the blueprint of a clinical trial, detailing its scientific rationale, methodology, and operational framework.

Aims and objectives

The aim of a clinical trial is a broad statement reflecting the overall purpose of the study, while objectives are specific, measurable steps designed to achieve that aim. Objectives are typically divided into:

Primary objectives, which focus on the main outcome (e.g., periapical healing or microbial reduction)Secondary objectives, which assess additional parameters such as postoperative pain, procedural time, or patient satisfaction.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Defining inclusion and exclusion criteria is essential for selecting an appropriate study population and ensuring homogeneity within study groups. Inclusion criteria specify characteristics required for participation, such as age range, diagnosis (e.g., necrotic pulp with apical periodontitis), and tooth type.[22] Exclusion criteria eliminate confounding variables that may influence outcomes, such as systemic diseases, previously treated teeth, or poor patient compliance.[4]

Outcome measures

Outcome measures must be clinically relevant, valid, reliable, and reproducible. In endodontics, commonly used outcomes include:

Clinical parameters (pain, swelling, and tenderness)Radiographic healing (periapical index scores)Microbiological reduction (colony-forming unit counts, molecular assays)Patient-reported outcomes (quality of life and discomfort).

4.2. Sample size estimation

Adequate sample size is crucial to ensure that the study has sufficient statistical power to detect a meaningful difference between groups. Underpowered studies risk false-negative results, while excessively large samples may be resource-intensive and ethically questionable.[24]

Power and effect size

Statistical power is the probability of correctly rejecting the null hypothesis when a true effect exists, typically set at 80% or 90%. Accurate estimation of effect size is critical, as overestimation can lead to underpowered studies and unreliable conclusions.[25]

Type I and Type II Errors

Appropriate selection of randomization technique enhances internal validity and strengthens the credibility of trial outcomes.[26]

4.4. Allocation concealment

Allocation concealment refers to the process of preventing investigators and participants from knowing upcoming group assignments during recruitment. It is distinct from blinding and is critical for avoiding selection bias.[27] Common methods include sealed opaque envelopes, centralized randomization, or computerized allocation systems.

4.5. Blinding methods

Blinding reduces performance and detection bias by preventing participants, clinicians, and outcome assessors from knowing group allocation.[28] As mentioned before, it can be single blinding, double blinding, or triple blinding.

4.6. Confounding factors and bias control

Confounding factors are variables that may influence both the intervention and outcome, leading to distorted results. In endodontics, these may include operator experience, canal anatomy, microbial diversity, and patient-related factors such as systemic health.

5. ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS AND TRIAL REGISTRATION

Ethical integrity is a fundamental pillar of clinical research, ensuring the protection, dignity, and rights of participants while maintaining scientific credibility. Adherence to established ethical guidelines not only safeguards participants but also enhances the acceptability and publishability of the study in peer-reviewed journals. International frameworks such as the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines provide the ethical foundation for designing and conducting clinical trials.[2930]

5.1. Institutional ethical approval (institutional review board/institutional ethics committee)

Before initiating a clinical trial, approval must be obtained from an Institutional Review Board or Institutional Ethics Committee. These bodies are responsible for evaluating the scientific validity, ethical soundness, and risk–benefit ratio of the proposed study and reviews aspects such as study design, participant selection, informed consent process, confidentiality measures, and safety monitoring protocols. Obtaining ethical approval ensures that the study complies with national and international regulations and provides an additional layer of accountability.[313233]

5.2. Informed consent

Informed consent is a keystone of ethical clinical research and reflects respect for patient autonomy. It involves providing participants with comprehensive information about the study, including its purpose, procedures, potential risks and benefits, alternative treatments, and the right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Consent forms must be written in a language understandable to the participant and should avoid technical jargon.[34]

5.3. Trial Registration (e.g., Clinical Trials Registry of India)

Prospective registration of clinical trials in publicly accessible databases is now a mandatory requirement for publication in most high-impact journals. Trial registries promote transparency, prevent selective reporting, and reduce duplication of research. In India, the Clinical Trials Registry of India serves as the primary platform for trial registration.[35]

5.4. Reporting adverse events

Monitoring and reporting adverse events (AEs) are critical components of ethical clinical research. They must be systematically recorded, assessed for severity and causality, and reported to the ethics committee as per regulatory guidelines.[36]

6. CONDUCT OF CLINICAL TRIALS

The conduct phase of a clinical trial translates the planned protocol into real-world execution. In endodontics, meticulous implementation is essential due to the technique-sensitive nature of procedures and the potential influence of operator variability on outcomes. Deviations during this phase can compromise internal validity and lead to biased or inconclusive findings.[37]

6.1. Data collection and standardization

Accurate and systematic data collection is fundamental to the success of a clinical trial. Data should be collected using predefined formats and standardized procedures to ensure consistency across participants and study centers.[38]

6.2. Calibration and reliability

Calibration refers to the process of training investigators and examiners to ensure consistency in clinical procedures and outcome assessment. Reliability, on the other hand, reflects the degree of agreement or reproducibility of measurements. In endodontic trials, calibration is crucial for both clinical procedures (e.g., working length determination, and obturation techniques) and outcome evaluation (e.g., radiographic interpretation). Interexaminer and intraexaminer reliability should be assessed using statistical measures such as kappa coefficients or intraclass correlation coefficients. High reliability ensures that observed differences between groups are attributable to the intervention rather than measurement variability. Lack of calibration can introduce significant bias, particularly in subjective assessments such as pain or radiographic healing.[39]

6.3. Monitoring and quality control

Continuous monitoring and quality control are essential to ensure adherence to the study protocol and maintain data integrity. Monitoring may be conducted internally by the research team or externally by independent bodies, depending on the scale and regulatory requirements of the trial.

6.4. Maintaining case report forms

Case Report Forms (CRFs) are structured documents used to record all data related to each participant in a clinical trial. They serve as the primary source of data for analysis and must be meticulously maintained. CRFs may be paper-based or electronic (eCRFs), with the latter offering advantages such as real-time data entry, automated validation checks, and secure data storage. Well-maintained CRFs not only facilitate reliable statistical analysis but also enhance transparency during peer review and regulatory audits, thereby contributing to the overall credibility of the clinical trial.[40]

7. REPORTING GUIDELINES IN CLINICAL TRIALS

Accurate and transparent reporting is a critical component of clinical research, ensuring that study findings are reproducible, interpretable, and clinically applicable. Poor reporting not only limits the utility of research but also contributes to research waste and misinformed clinical decisions.

7.1. Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines

The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines represent the most widely accepted framework for reporting RCTs. The CONSORT statement provides a 25-item checklist and flow diagram designed to improve the completeness and transparency of trial reporting.[41]

7.2. Preferred Reporting Items for Randomized Trials in Endodontics guidelines (endodontic trials)

The Preferred Reporting Items for Randomized Trials in Endodontics (PRIRATE) guidelines were specifically developed to address the unique methodological and reporting requirements of the International Endodontic Journal. While CONSORT provides a general framework, PRIRATE offers specialty-specific recommendations tailored to endodontic research.[42]

8.1. Common writing errors (based on Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials deficiencies)

Common writing errors in clinical trial manuscripts largely stem from incomplete adherence to CONSORT guidelines. One major issue is inadequate reporting of randomization and allocation concealment, which makes it difficult to assess internal validity. Another frequent deficiency is poor description of interventions, particularly in endodontics where procedural details are critical for reproducibility. Missing information on materials, techniques, or operator variability can significantly limit the usefulness of the study. Selective outcome reporting is also a major concern, where predefined outcomes are omitted or inconsistently reported. This introduces bias and reduces transparency. Additionally, lack of reporting of harms is a critical issue, especially in clinical trials involving invasive procedures such as endodontic treatments.

9. PEER REVIEW AND PUBLICATION PROCESS

The publication process is a critical stage in the dissemination of clinical trial findings, ensuring that research undergoes rigorous evaluation before being integrated into scientific literature. In endodontics, where clinical decisions are increasingly guided by evidence-based practice, peer-reviewed publication serves as a benchmark for scientific credibility and clinical applicability.

9.1. Journal selection and scope

Selecting an appropriate journal is a strategic decision that significantly influences the likelihood of manuscript acceptance. Authors must consider the journal’s scope, target audience, impact factor, indexing status, and acceptance rate. In endodontics, journals such as the Journal of Endodontics and the International Endodontic Journal prioritize high-quality clinical trials with robust methodology and adherence to reporting guidelines. Submitting a manuscript that aligns with the journal’s scope and formatting requirements enhances the chances of favorable review.

9.2. Submission process

The submission process involves preparing the manuscript according to journal-specific guidelines, including formatting, referencing style, ethical declarations, and supplementary documents such as CONSORT checklists and flow diagrams. Most journals use online submission systems that require authors to upload the manuscript, cover letter, graphical abstracts (if required), and conflict of interest statements. A well-written cover letter highlighting the novelty and clinical relevance of the study can positively influence editorial decisions.

9.3. Peer review mechanism

Peer review is a structured evaluation process in which experts assess the scientific quality, originality, and relevance of a manuscript. The peer review process ensures that only scientifically sound and clinically meaningful studies are published, thereby maintaining the integrity of the literature.

9.4. Revision and resubmission

Most manuscripts undergo one or more rounds of revision based on reviewer feedback. Authors must respond systematically to each comment, providing clear explanations and making necessary revisions. Constructive engagement with reviewers, rather than defensive responses, improves the likelihood of acceptance. Timely and thorough revision demonstrates professionalism and commitment to scientific rigor, which are highly valued in the publication process.[44]

10. POSTPUBLICATION IMPACT

The impact of a clinical trial extends beyond publication, influencing clinical practice, future research, and policy development. Evaluating and enhancing postpublication impact is an important aspect of the research lifecycle.

10.1. Citation tracking

Citation metrics are commonly used to assess the academic impact of a publication. Tools such as Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar allow tracking of citation counts and research influence.

10.2. Research visibility and promotion

Active dissemination strategies, including conference presentations, academic networking, and digital platforms, enhance research visibility. Social media and academic platforms such as ResearchGate and LinkedIn play an increasingly important role in promoting scientific work.

10.3. Clinical translation of research

The ultimate goal of clinical trials is to inform and improve patient care. Translation of research findings into clinical practice involves integration into guidelines, educational programs, and routine treatment protocols.

10.4. Collaboration and future research

Published research often serves as a foundation for future studies and collaborations. Multicenter trials, interdisciplinary research, and international collaborations enhance the generalizability and impact of findings. Ongoing research builds upon existing evidence, contributing to continuous advancement in endodontic science and clinical practice.

11. FUTURE PERSPECTIVES IN CLINICAL TRIAL PUBLISHING

The landscape of clinical trial publishing is rapidly evolving, driven by technological advancements, increasing emphasis on transparency, and the need for reproducibility in research. In endodontics, these developments are expected to enhance the quality, accessibility, and clinical relevance of published trials. Emerging trends such as artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted writing, open science practices, and evolving reporting standards are reshaping how clinical research is conducted, reported, and disseminated.

Role of artificial intelligence in research writing

AI is increasingly being integrated into various stages of clinical research, including literature review, data analysis, and manuscript preparation. AI-powered tools can assist in grammar correction, reference management, plagiarism detection, and even generation of structured drafts, thereby improving efficiency and consistency in scientific writing.[45]

However, the use of AI also raises concerns regarding authorship, accountability, and potential biases in generated content. Ethical use of AI requires transparency, proper disclosure, and human oversight to ensure scientific accuracy and integrity.

Open access and transparency

Open access publishing has gained significant momentum as a means of improving the accessibility and dissemination of scientific knowledge. By removing paywall barriers, open access enables clinicians, researchers, and policymakers to freely access research findings, thereby accelerating the translation of evidence into practice. Despite its advantages, open access publishing presents challenges such as publication costs and the emergence of predatory journals. Careful selection of reputable journals is essential to maintain research quality and credibility.

12. CONCLUSION

Clinical trials represent the highest level of evidence in endodontics and play a pivotal role in guiding evidence-based clinical decision-making. The process of designing, conducting, and publishing a clinical trial is complex and requires meticulous attention to methodological, ethical, and reporting standards.

In endodontics, where treatment outcomes are influenced by multiple biological and procedural variables, strict adherence to standardized protocols and validated outcome measures is essential. Methodological rigor not only enhances internal validity but also improves external generalizability, enabling findings to be effectively translated into clinical practice.

In conclusion, the art of publishing clinical trials in endodontics lies in the seamless integration of scientific rigor, ethical responsibility, and effective communication. Strengthening these aspects will not only enhance the quality of research but also contribute to the advancement of clinical practice and patient care.
