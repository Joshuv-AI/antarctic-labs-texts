Abstract
The sources of turbulence in our Galaxy may be diverse, but core-collapse supernovae (SNe) alone provide enough energy to sustain a steady-state galactic turbulence cascade. By localizing and analyzing supernova remnants (SNRs) in high-resolution SNe-driven galactic disk cut-out simulations from Beattie et al. 2025c , I show that SNRs radiate incompressible turbulence through baroclinic vorticity generation, localized at the interface where the hot and warm plasma phases mix near the cooling radius. I provide evidence that this process is seeded by a Vishniac 1994 -type instability, which corrugates and folds the interface. I present an analytical relation for a baroclinicity-fed incompressible mode spectrum, which matches that observed in the simulated SNRs. The unstable layer produces a spectrum of incompressible modes ∝ k − 3 / 2 \propto k^{-3/2} locally within the SNRs. Through the inverse cascade mechanism described and measured in Beattie et al. 2025c , this opens the possibility that the ∝ k − 3 / 2 \propto k^{-3/2} spectrum, arising from corrugated folds in the unstable SNR layer, can imprint itself on kiloparsec scales, thereby connecting small-scale instabilities in the layer to the large-scale incompressible turbulence cascade.

Introduction

The vast length scales within a galaxy, combined with the very low viscosities of warm and cold plasma in the interstellar medium
(

)

;

;

;

;

;
Federrath 2016
;
Beuther et al. 2015
;
Krumholz & Ting 2018
;
Kolborg et al. 2022
)
. It also exerts turbulent pressures that provide support to pressure-balanced components of the galaxy, such as the galactic disk and star-forming molecular clouds

;
Hennebelle & Falgarone 2012
;
Federrath & Klessen 2012
;

;

)
. Furthermore, turbulence fuels the magnetic fluctuation dynamo

;
Federrath et al. 2014
;

;
Kriel et al. 2022
;
Kriel et al. 2025

(

;
Hopkins et al. 2021
;
Kempski et al. 2023
;
Ruszkowski &
Pfrommer 2023
;
Sampson et al. 2025
)
. It is difficult to truly encapsulate the vast array of roles that turbulence plays within a galaxy.

Core-collapse supernovae (SNe) have more than enough energy to be one of the primary drivers of a galactic turbulence cascade
(
Mac Low & Klessen 2004
;
Hill et al. 2012
;
Beattie et al. 2025c
;
Connor et al. 2025
)
. Simulations have demonstrated that SNe create and sustain steady-state turbulence, which in turn contributes to galactic magnetic dynamos

;
Padoan et al. 2016
;
Bacchini et al. 2020
;
Gent et al. 2021
;
Gent et al. 2023
;

;

;
Gent et al. 2023

)

In this Letter, by analyzing a population of supernova remnants (SNRs) clustered and extracted from global SNe-driven turbulence simulations, I show that interactions between multiple SNe are not required to generate turbulence. Instead, SNe sustain turbulence via baroclinicity in a fractal mixing layer at the interface between warm and hot plasma in the SNRs (similar to those formed around stellar wind bubbles;
Lancaster et al. 2021
;
Lancaster et al. 2024
). The layer becomes fractal and folded, with a
\mathcal{P}_{\rm B}(k)\propto k^{3/2}
baroclinic spectrum, due to what I argue is a thin-shell instability with properties similar to the nonlinear
Vishniac 1994
instability. In the nonlinear regime, this instability grows modes with a rate
\gamma(k)\propto k^{3/2}

(
Vishniac 1994
;
McLeod &
Whitworth 2013
)
, which likely sets the structure of the layer. At the sites of the SNRs, an incompressible velocity spectrum develops, following
\mathcal{P}_{\bm{u}_{s}}(k)\propto k^{-3/2}
. This is the same spectrum observed in global SNe-driven turbulence simulations, extending from the disk into the galactic winds
(
Padoan et al. 2016
;
Fielding et al. 2018
;
Beattie et al. 2025c
;
Connor et al. 2025
)
. Recently,
Beattie et al. 2025c
showed that through incompressible–compressible–incompressible triad velocity-mode interactions, SNe-driven turbulence creates an inverse cascade from the cooling radius of the SNe, where the fractal mixing layer forms, to the galactic winds. Hence, I suggest that the large-scale
\mathcal{P}_{\bm{u}_{s}}(k)\propto k^{-3/2}
spectrum originates from instabilities in the layer and is transported to the outer scales of the galaxy via the inverse cascade.

This Letter is organized as follows. In
Section II
I describe the numerical simulations, the clustering method used to extract SNRs from the simulations of
Beattie et al. 2025c
, and the definitions of the Fourier power spectra used in my analysis. In
Section III
I provide an analytical prediction for the scaling between the vorticity–baroclinic interaction spectrum and the incompressible mode spectrum, assuming that baroclinicity directly sources vorticity at the SNR scale. I show that this relation holds in the SNR models and that the incompressible velocity spectrum follows
k^{-3/2}
at the SNR scale. In
Section IV
I provide evidence that the structure of the fractal layer is driven by a thin-shell-type instability. Finally, in
Section V
I summarize the work and discuss the broader implications of my results for SNe-driven turbulence in galaxies.

II.1
Gravitohydrodynamical stratified, multiphase, SNe-driven turbulence model

For a detailed description of the numerical setup analyzed in this study, see

Beattie et al. 2025b

Localizing SNR and building a catalog

I focus on studying the spectral properties of SNe locally, as opposed to the global treatment in
Beattie et al. 2025c

Because hot shells of plasma beyond the cooling radius of the SNR are also captured by this mask, I apply a binary opening filter, commonly used in computer vision, to ensure that any thin shells are disconnected from the bulk of the SNR. After processing the mask in this way, I apply a friend-of-friends algorithm

III
Baroclinicity and incompressible mode correspondence

The principle goal of this study is to address the correspondence and origin of incompressible turbulence in an SNe-driven system.
Beattie et al. 2025c
argued that this turbulence does not arise from blast-wave interactions, as conjectured by
Balsara et al. 2004
, but instead from baroclinicity,
\bm{\nabla}\rho\times\bm{\nabla}P/\rho^{2}
, generated in the cooling layer of the SNR. This occurs through misaligned radial pressure gradients,
\bm{\nabla}P
, from the expansion, combined with instabilities in the layer that produce corrugations in the density gradient,
\bm{\nabla}\rho
. I qualitatively demonstrate this correspondence for the same 48 SNRs shown in
Figure 1
, in
Figure 2
.

In statistical steady state, the isotropically integrated, Fourier-transformed enstrophy equation (
\partial_{t}\int k^{2}\operatorname{d}\!{\Omega}_{k}\,\bm{\omega}(\bm{k})\cdot\bm{\omega}^{\dagger}(\bm{k})=0
) is

\displaystyle 0=\frac{d\,\Pi_{\bm{\omega}}(k)}{dk}+\mathcal{P}_{\omega\rm B}(k)-D(k),

(4)

where
\Pi_{\bm{\omega}}(k)
is the enstrophy flux rate from
k^{\prime}>k
, i.e., the flux from all cascade terms, and
D(k)
is the enstrophy flux sink due to diffusion. If all of the flux sourced from
\bm{\nabla}\rho\times\bm{\nabla}P/\rho^{2}
balances the flux feeding the incompressible velocity cascade, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\omega\rm B}(k)

\displaystyle=-\frac{d\,\Pi_{\bm{\omega}}(k)}{dk}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{t_{\rm nl}}\int\operatorname{d}\!{\Omega}_{k}\,k^{2}\,\bm{\omega}(\bm{k})\cdot\bm{\omega}^{\dagger}(\bm{k})\sim\frac{k^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{u}_{s}}(k)}{t_{\rm nl}},

(5)

where
t_{\rm nl}\sim(ku_{k})^{-1}
is the nonlinear timescale of the turbulence, with
u_{k}^{2}\sim k\mathcal{P}_{\bm{u}_{s}}(k)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\omega\rm B}(k)\sim k^{7/5}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{u}_{s}}^{3/2}(k),

(6)

or equivalently,

IV
Evidence for a Vishniac-type instability

I now turn to the unstable shell itself. A detailed linear or nonlinear analysis of the shell, requiring spatio-temporal information about the layer, is beyond the scope of this Letter, where the focus is on linking turbulence to the baroclinicity generated in the layer. It is clear from the preceding analysis, and explicitly shown in the right sub-panels of
Figure 2
, that the layer is closely traced by
\bm{\nabla}\rho\times\bm{\nabla}P/\rho^{2}
. Therefore, the pure
\bm{\nabla}\rho\times\bm{\nabla}P/\rho^{2}
spectrum,
\mathcal{P}_{\rm B}(k)
, can be used to characterize the
k
-space structure of the layer.

I plot
\mathcal{P}_{\rm B}(k)
in the bottom panel of
Figure 4
, averaged and normalized in the same way as the previous spectra.
\mathcal{P}_{\rm B}(k)
follows a
\propto k^{3/2}
power law across a broad range of scales. In fluctuating dynamo theory, such a spectrum (the
Kazantsev 1968
spectrum) is associated with an underlying folded geometry, where gradients pile up on small scales, producing folds in the layer down to the grid scale. The
k^{3/2}
power law also corresponds to the mode growth rate,
\gamma(k)\propto k^{3/2}
, of the nonlinear
Vishniac 1994
instability
(
McLeod &
Whitworth 2013
)
. Hence, if the layer is initially uniformly perturbed, after a short time—when
\exp{\gamma t}\approx\gamma t+\cdots
—the
Vishniac 1994
instability would be expected to drive the layer toward a
\propto k^{3/2}
spectrum. This interpretation remains tentative, and I now turn to a more qualitative analysis.

In
Figure 5
I plot the
\bm{\nabla}\rho\times\bm{\nabla}P/\rho^{2}
layer for four different clustered SNRs at increasing radii, all taken from the same time realization in the global simulation. Because the radius and age of an SNR are tightly correlated, the figure can be interpreted as showing young, small-radius SNRs on the left and progressively older, larger-radius SNRs toward the right. This is intended as an illustrative example. As expected for a thin-shell instability, high-
k
modes appear in the young SNRs, and as the remnants expand and age, the modes become longer and larger in amplitude. By the time the SNRs expand to
\sim 100\,\rm{pc}
(far right), the corrugated modes have carved large cavities into the layer, folding it and producing fractal-like structures that are eventually washed out by numerical viscosity in the simulation. For much higher-resolution local simulations without expansion or mean curvature in the mixing layer (driven by a KHI instead of a thin-shell instability), see
Fielding et al. 2020
. In summary, the baroclinic spectrum exhibits hallmark signatures of a folded geometry, and the thin-shell layer between the hot and cold plasma phases qualitatively corrugates into a fractal surface as it expands. Both results suggest that the SNR undergoes a thin-shell instability, which determines the
k
-mode structure of the mixing layer.

V
Summary and conclusions

In this study I have constructed a unique dataset of three-dimensional SNRs (
n=87
), identified, clustered, and extracted from multiphase gravitohydrodynamical simulations of SNe-driven turbulence in a galactic disk cut-out
(
Beattie et al. 2025c
)
. The simulations are parameterized (e.g., by mass, gravitational potential, cooling function, SNe explosion rate) to match the large-scale (
L\gtrsim 10\,\rm{pc}
) properties of the Milky Way Galaxy.

I present several key aspects of SNe-driven turbulence that have not previously been revealed. Firstly, in
Figure 1
and
Figure 2
I show that every SNR hosts a fractal layer, bright in baroclinicity
\bm{\nabla}\rho\times\bm{\nabla}P/\rho^{2}
, wedged between hot and warm plasma. These layers were previously identified by
Beattie et al. 2025c
, but not at this level of detail. In
Section III
I demonstrate that if baroclinicity generates all of the incompressible turbulence at the SNR scale, then an analytical relation should hold between the baroclinic–vorticity power spectrum,
\mathcal{P}_{\omega\rm B}(k)
, and the incompressible velocity-mode spectrum,
\mathcal{P}_{\bm{u}_{s}}(k)
(
Equation 6
). In
Figure 3
I show that the simulated SNRs follow this relation, illustrating that incompressible turbulence is fully generated by the corrugated layer. I also show that the layer exhibits a
k^{3/2}
power spectrum in
\bm{\nabla}\rho\times\bm{\nabla}P/\rho^{2}
, a signature of a highly folded geometry (well known from small-scale dynamo theory), and provide evidence that the corrugations arise from a thin-shell
Vishniac 1994
-type instability.

The spectrum generated by baroclinicity is
\mathcal{P}_{\bm{u}_{s}}(k)\propto k^{-3/2}
, which develops on the local SNR scale. This is one of the most important results of this Letter, because simulations of SNe-driven turbulence in galactic disks also show a
\mathcal{P}_{\bm{u}_{s}}(k)\propto k^{-3/2}
spectrum
(
Padoan et al. 2016
;
Beattie et al. 2025c
)
, extending from the disk into the galactic winds
(
Connor et al. 2025
)
.
Beattie et al. 2025c
demonstrated that incompressible modes can couple to compressible modes in young SNRs, creating an inverse cascade from the scale of the SNe cooling radius (
r\lesssim 10\,\rm{pc}
;
Cioffi et al. 1988
) to the outer scale of the turbulence (
\mathcal{O}(\rm{kpc})
;
Connor et al. 2025
). Therefore, I suggest that the
\mathcal{P}_{\bm{u}_{s}}(k)\propto k^{-3/2}
spectrum generated in the mixing layer couples to compressible modes generated by the SNR and imprints itself on the largest scales of the galactic turbulence cascade. This provides a striking example of how small-scale physics can shape the large-scale properties of galaxies, defining a new phenomenology for SNe-driven turbulence.

Several outstanding problems remain. (1) The geometry of the layer, which exhibits a highly folded structure, generates incompressible turbulence in a manner directly tied to the geometry and the misaligned gradients in
\bm{\nabla}\rho\times\bm{\nabla}P/\rho^{2}
. This is an analytically tractable problem, reducible to modeling
\mathcal{P}_{\omega\rm B}
, and could ultimately yield a first-principles model for the
\mathcal{P}_{\bm{u}_{s}}\propto k^{-3/2}
spectrum and, in turn, the galactic velocity power spectrum. Developing such a model is beyond the scope of this Letter. (2) Based on
Beattie et al. 2025c
and
Connor et al. 2025
, this spectrum appears to survive transport from the galactic disk into the winds. However, at some length scale, galactic shear could deform the expanding modes, altering the spectrum and preventing it from being perfectly imprinted on the largest scales. The survival of the spectrum under shear must be tested with future simulations. (3) The role of magnetic fields will be crucial in this phenomenology, since they are generated proportionally with
\omega
through the Biermann battery
(
Biermann 1950
;
Beattie et al. 2025c
)
. A detailed investigation of the Biermann effect and the associated dynamo is left for future work, once this problem setup is ported to the GPU-enabled
Stone et al. 2024
code.

Appendix A
Incompressible velocity mode and vorticity spectrum correspondence

In
Equation 6
I derived a relation between the vorticity–baroclinic interaction spectrum,
\mathcal{P}_{\omega\rm B}(k)
, and the incompressible mode spectrum,
\mathcal{P}_{\bm{u}_{s}}(k)
. This relied on the fact that the vorticity spectrum,
\mathcal{P}_{\omega}(k)
, can be written simply as
\mathcal{P}_{\omega}(k)\sim k^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{u}_{s}}(k)
.
Connor et al. 2025
, in their Appendix, showed that this relation also holds for the compressible mode
\bm{u}_{c}
, where
|\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{u}_{c}|=0
, with the counterpart relation
\mathcal{P}_{\bm{u}_{c}}(k)\sim k^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}}(k)
, exactly as one would hope and expect. For completeness, I confirm the incompressible–vorticity spectrum relation here. In
Figure 6
I show
\mathcal{P}_{\bm{u}_{s}}(k)
and
\mathcal{P}_{\omega}(k)/k^{2}
(with the same normalizations as
Figure 4
), illustrating good agreement between the two power spectra,
\mathcal{P}_{\omega}(k)\sim k^{2}\mathcal{P}_{\bm{u}_{s}}(k)
, across all long wavelengths (
k\ell_{0}/2\pi\lesssim 10
). These are the modes that are not influenced by numerical viscosity in these simulations. Since
\mathcal{P}_{\bm{u}_{s}}\propto k^{-3/2}
(as shown in
Figure 4
), the vorticity follows
\mathcal{P}_{\omega}(k)\propto k^{1/2}
.

, but for the incompressible-velocity mode spectrum,
