Role of non-coding RNAs in radiotherapy sensitivity in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Peipei Yang, Rui Chen, Huijuan He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lung cancer (LC) is consistently the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. Radiotherapy is a crucial means of treating LC, and about 60–70% of LC patients require radiotherapy. However, radiotherapy resistance remains a significant challenge for treatment outcomes. Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) are widely engaged in multiple processes of tumorigenesis and progression. Extensive studies have shown that ncRNAs play an essential role in drug resistance in tumor therapy, including radiotherapy. Importantly, aberrantly expressed ncRNAs affect the efficacy of radiotherapy, whereas radiation response can result in the dysregulated expression of ncRNAs. Additionally, since the abnormal expression of ncRNAs in LC can respond to radiotherapy, circulating ncRNAs are also measurable in body fluids. Here, we describe the functions and mechanisms of ncRNAs in LC radiotherapy sensitivity and introduce the future directions and applications of ncRNAs in LC radiotherapy research.

Introduction

Radiotherapy involves the targeted delivery of a precisely calibrated high-dose radiation to the tumor. DNA is the most critical target of radiation in cells [1]. Ionizing radiation (IR) can result in one or more double-strand breaks in DNA coupled with several single-strand breaks (SSB) and base damage [2]. Ultimately, the leading cause of IR-induced cell death is tangible cytokinesis mutations that result in uneven distribution of chromosomes or loss of chromosome segments. Moreover, the metabolic imbalance from massive DNA loss will not allow for complete cell proliferation [2, 3].

Radiosensitivity denotes the biological response of cells or tissue to IR exposure. Based on the radiation therapy, tumors are categorized into three groups: radiosensitive, moderately sensitive, and radiation-resistant [4]. Meanwhile, with the advancement of radiotherapy technology, the radiosensitivity of tumors deserves to be re-conceptualized [5, 6]. For example, sophisticated radiotherapy technology can increase the irradiation dose of tumors and shorten the radiotherapy duration. Therefore, modern radiotherapy can make moderately sensitive tumors more sensitive and less sensitive tumors more sensitive. Indeed, multiple factors contribute to the radiosensitivity of cancer [7]. Radiation resistance is also more complicated with the diversity of tumor treatment pathways. Importantly, radiotherapy resistance is a stumbling block preventing the success of tumor treatment [8]. In consequence, it is imperative to investigate novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets to overcome therapeutic resistance.

Notably, ncRNAs have garnered extensive attention for their role in regulating therapeutic resistance. Increasing studies have demonstrated the diagnostic and prognostic value of ncRNAs in cancer. The expression of ncRNAs has been observed in different cancers [9, 10]. In addition, at various stages of the tumor, the expression of ncRNAs varies. Biological information and high-throughput sequencing studies have shown that dysregulated ncRNAs mediate therapeutic resistance, including radiotherapy resistance [11]. This review provides an overview of ncRNAs related to the development of tolerance during LC radiation therapy, summarizes the effects of ncRNAs on radiotherapy response, and discusses future research direction.

The role of radiotherapy in the treatment of NSCLC

For years, LC has had the highest mortality rate among all malignancies worldwide, posing a serious threat to human health. Global cancer prevalence statistics show that globally in 2020, there were an estimated 2.207 million new cases of LC and an estimated 1.796 million deaths [12]. Further, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for 80% to 85% of all LC patients [13, 14]. The prognosis for patients with advanced NSCLC remains poor, with the 5-year overall survival rate not exceeding 5% [13, 14].

Even in the era of precision medicine with the rapid development of systemic treatment for NSCLC, radiotherapy is “promising” for the elimination of all stages of NSCLC. For example, surgery is the standard treatment for stage I NSCLC, but stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) is preferred for stage I patients who are inoperable or refuse surgery [15, 16]. Moreover, synchronized radiotherapy is the standard of care for patients with inoperable stage II/III NSCLC [16]. Additionally, for advanced cancer, radiotherapy can be used as a palliative symptom-reducing treatment. Whole-brain radiotherapy is effective in reducing neurological symptoms of brain metastases, and localized palliative radiotherapy of bone metastases contributes to pain relief in 70–90% of patients with bone metastases [17]. Furthermore, radiotherapy also has a significant impact on the treatment of SCLC. Radiotherapy can be used in both extensive and limited-stage SCLC patients, especially in patients who receive prophylactic radiotherapy to the brain [18, 19]. It has been found that the incidence of natural brain metastases was significantly reduced in patients who received prophylactic radiotherapy.

Importantly, in the era of targeted and immunotherapy, modern radiotherapy technology has also undergone rapid development. As the cornerstone of the traditional treatment, the combination of radiotherapy and systemic therapy has a sufficient theoretical basis and excellent research data in the treatment of NSCLC [20]. The PACIFIC study has dramatically revolutionised the treatment of advanced NSCLC [21]. Immunotherapy effectively prolonged both progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS). Interestingly, the synergistic interaction between radiotherapy and immunotherapy has yielded remarkable and unexpected outcomes [22]. Radiotherapy possesses the capacity to reprogram the tumor microenvironment (TME), consequently enhancing systemic anti-tumor efficacy.

Likewise, targeted therapy has introduced precision and personalisation in the treatment of NSCLC patients [23, 24]. However, the mechanisms underlying acquired resistance to targeted therapy remain poorly elucidated. The efficacy of radiotherapy in combination with targeted therapy is showing promise. A recent multicenter prospective study demonstrated that SBRT combined with EGFR-TKI significantly delayed the onset of their secondary resistance and prolonged PFS and OS in patients with oligometastatic stage IV NSCLC [25]. Meanwhile, for NSCLC patients with EGFR gene mutation and brain metastasis, local radiotherapy of brain lesions can improve the disease control rate and increase the permeability of the blood–brain barrier to a certain extent [26]. EGFR-TKIs also have a certain degree of sensitizing effect on radiotherapy, which can improve the local control rate [27].

Generally, radiotherapy plays a pivotal role in the different treatment stages of NSCLC. The exploration of radiotherapy sensitivity is particularly critical in the treatment of NSCLC.

Biogenesis and characteristics of ncRNAs

At first, ncRNAs were previously considered to be useless “garbage”. The rapid development of high-throughput sequencing technologies and computational platforms has unravelled their mysteries. ncRNAs are a category of functional RNA molecules that do not translate into proteins [9]. Numerous studies have shown that ncRNAs are ubiquitous. In particular, they are involved in regulating nearly all physiological and pathological processes, including embryonic development, cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, infection, and immune response [10, 28]. More importantly, as a class of ncRNAs with regulatory functions, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), microRNAs (miRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs) have been extensively studied, with their regulatory targets being predominantly associated with messenger RNAs (mRNAs) [29].

First, lncRNAs are a series of long ncRNAs with lengths greater than 200 nucleotides [30]. Extensive research has demonstrated that aberrantly expressed lncRNAs are actively involved in the pathogenesis and progression of various diseases, including inflammatory disorders, infectious diseases, and diverse types of malignancies [31]. LncRNAs have become a focal point of genetic research.

According to their relative position to coding genes, lncRNAs are divided into intergenic lncRNAs, intronic lncRNAs, antisense lncRNAs, and sense-overlapping lncRNAs [32]. Most lncRNAs are produced by RNA polymerase II (Pol II) transcription with a 5’ cap structure and a 3’ poly (A) tail structure [33]. Still, they are processed with low splicing efficiency and show an evident nuclear localization phenomenon [33]. Localized in the nucleus, lncRNAs can interact with DNA, RNA, protein, and other molecules to regulate chromosome structure and function [32, 34]. For example, lncRNAs regulate gene transcription through either a cis or trans mechanism to influence mRNA splicing, stability, and translation. Spliced and fully processed lncRNAs are translocated to the cytoplasm and trans-regulate gene expression at the post-transcriptional level (such as regulating mRNA translation and degradation) [31, 34].

Second, miRNAs are a class of endogenous ncRNAs with regulatory functions, with sizes ranging from about 18 to 25 nucleotides [35]. Mature miRNAs are produced by long primary transcripts that are sheared and processed by a series of nuclease enzymes [36]. In animals, the miRNA gene is first transcribed by RNA polymerase II to produce a primary miRNA (pri-miRNA), and pri-miRNA forms a precursor of miRNA (pre-miRNA) under the action of the nuclease Drosha[0]. Subsequently, the pre-miRNA is transported to the cytoplasm in response to transporter proteins, and Dicer enzymes cleave the stem-loop structure of the pre-miRNA into a double-stranded RNA fragment approximately 22 nucleotides in length [36, 37]. Finally, one strand is degraded, and the other strand forms a mature miRNA. Furthermore, mature miRNAs assembled into an RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) recognize the target mRNA through complementary base pairing and direct the silencing complex to either degrade the target mRNA or inhibit its translation, depending on the degree of complementarity [38, 39]. In general, the seed sequence of mature miRNAs (2–8 nucleotides from the 5’ end to the 3’ end) is not entirely complementary to the 3’ UTR of mRNA, thus inhibiting mRNA translation [38].

Most miRNAs exist in the genome in the form of single copies, multiple copies, or clusters of genes. Each miRNA can have multiple target genes, and several miRNAs can also regulate the same gene [38, 39]. This complex regulatory network can not only regulate the expression of many genes through a single miRNA but also fine-regulate the expression of a particular gene by a combination of several miRNAs [39]. Additionally, miRNAs are involved in a range of essential processes in life.

Finally, circRNAs are a new kind of ncRNAs. circRNAs form a closed loop through covalent bonds at the beginning and the end, and exhibit different properties from linear RNAs [40]. CircRNAs are abundantly present in eukaryotic transcriptomes, exhibiting evolutionary conservation across species, stable expression patterns, and tissue- and developmental stage-specific regulation [41, 42]. Based on their genetic origin, circRNAs can be classified into four categories: exonic circRNAs (ecircRNAs), intronic circRNAs (ciRNAs), exon-intronic circRNAs (elciRNAs), and intergenic circRNAs.Among them, ecircRNAs are the most prevalent class in organisms [43, 44]. EcircRNAs are derived from the reverse splicing of pre-mRNAs. In the process of reverse splicing, the downstream of the 5’ shear site is linked to the upstream of the 3’ shear site to form a 3’-5’ phosphodiester bond [40, 45].

CircRNAs are not easily degraded by exonuclease (RNase R). They are more stable than linear RNAs in body fluids, which makes circRNAs have great potential in developing novel diagnostic and therapeutic methods for disease [46]. Studies have shown that circRNAs can act as a molecular sponge for miRNA and proteins [44, 46].

Therefore, as novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets, the diagnostic and prognostic value of ncRNAs in cancer is indisputable. There is now substantial evidence that ncRNAs remarkably affect the radiosensitivity of NSCLC cells in a tumor-suppressive or oncogenic manner. LncRNAs

The research value and applications of lncRNAs in tumor progression have continued to represent a central focus of scientific investigation. A representative example is that lncRNA metastasis-associated lung cancer transcript 1 (MALAT1) is recognized as one of the most extensively investigated oncogenic factors, which holds promise as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target in human cancers [47]. Interestingly, the identification of MALAT1's role in NSCLC metastasis in 2003 catalyzed increased research on lncRNAs [48]. LncRNAs are extensively involved in a variety of malignant processes, particularly in the development of resistance to different cancer therapies.

The upregulation of lncRNA cytoskeleton regulator (CYTOR) in NSCLC may promote cell proliferation [49]. Specifically, miR-206 functions as a competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) for CYTOR, and CYTOR activates prothymosin α (PTMA) by targeting it, which consequently reduces the radiosensitivity of NSCLC [49]. Similarly, CYTOR also sponges up miR-195 to enhance its target, further fostering the growth and metastasis of NSCLC cells and inducing radiation resistance [50]. The lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA network has been extensively studied in terms of its functional efficacy. In other research, it was found that lncRNA SBF2 antisense RNA1 (SBF2-AS1) could facilitate the formation of NSCLC irradiation resistance via modulating miR-302a/muscleblind-like3 (MBNL3) [51].

During radiotherapy, IR induces DNA damage through mechanisms such as SSB, double-strand breaks (DSB), and base damage, thereby triggering cellular apoptosis or necrosis [1]. The DNA repair capacity of tumor cells significantly impacts the efficacy of radiotherapy [3]. Research findings indicate that tumor cells with defective DNA repair mechanisms exhibit heightened sensitivity to radiotherapy, whereas those with efficient repair systems are more likely to develop resistance [52]. LncRNAs can play a role in modulating the DNA damage repair pathway. DSB repair predominantly depends on homologous recombination (HR) mechanisms. According to Liu et al., lncRNA antisense non-coding RNA in the INK4 locus (ANRIL) has been identified as a facilitator of HR-mediated DNA repair [53]. Overexpression of ANRIL substantially improves cellular resistance to IR [53]. The direct molecular interaction between ANRIL and ATR enhances protein stability by preventing ubiquitination-mediated degradation, thereby contributing to the development of enhanced anti-tumorigenic properties[0]. Interestingly, their research team made a further discovery that ANRIL, acting as a ceRNA, binds to miR-7-5p and upregulates the expression of poly (ADP-ribose) (PAR) polymerase 1 (PARP1) [54]. Among the myriad proteins involved in repair pathways, PARP1 within the PARP family stands out as a prominent molecular entity [55]. The tumor suppressor WDR11-DT (a lncRNA) contributes to HR deficiency to enhance the radiosensitivity of NSCLC [56]. WDR11-DT destabilizes PARP1 by promoting its interaction with TRIP12, its E3 ubiquitin ligase, thereby inhibiting the SSB repair pathway mediated by PARP1 [56]. Furthermore, WDR11-DT can also interact with the RNA-binding protein HNRNPK, thereby suppressing the RNA stability of HR-related genes and reducing their expression levels, particularly BRCA1, ATM, BLM, and RAD50 [56]. The dual regulatory effects of WDR11-DT on HR repair result in the accumulation of DNA double-strand breaks, which exert tumor-suppressive effects [56].

The critical regulatory functions of lncRNAs within the TME warrant considerable attention. For example, lncRNA prostate cancer-associated ncRNA transcripts 1 (PCAT1) is overexpressed and demonstrates a negative association with immune cell infiltration in NSCLC [57]. The cGAS/STING signalling pathway is restrained by activation of SOX2 by PCTA1, which helps to promote tumorigenesis and contributes to immunosuppressive processes [57]. Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) promotes immune evasion within the TME, which impairs the host's capacity for immune surveillance and elimination of malignant cells [58]. A recent study has demonstrated that the lncRNA MALAT1 exerts oncogenic effects and decreases radiosensitivity in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) [59]. MALAT1 functions as a molecular sponge for miR-140 to promote PD-L1 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels [59].

In addition to the aberrant expression of lncRNAs in tumors affecting the efficacy of radiotherapy, radiation therapy itself can modulate lncRNA expression within tumor cells, which leads to the development of acquired resistance or enhanced sensitivity. For instance, lncRNA CBR3-AS1 has a crucial role in promoting radioresistance during radiotherapy [60]. RBM15 enhances the stability of CBR3-AS1 via the m6A-IGF2BP3 pathway [60]. More specifically, CBR3-AS1 mediates the recruitment of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) by upregulating the expression of CXCL1 and CXCR2, and targeting miR-409-3p within NSCLC [60]. Besides, lncRNA LINC01224 has been shown to facilitate stem cell-like properties to regulate radio-resistance in NSCLC. Radiation exposure leads to an upregulation of LINC01224 expression [61]. LINC01224 facilitates the formation of a DNA loop between the ZNF91 enhancer and promoter, thereby enhancing ZNF91 transcription [61]. Silencing of LINC01224 or ZNF91 reduces the sphere-forming capacity of radioresistant NSCLC cells and inhibits tumor cell invasion and migration [61].

Within the TME, in addition to cellular components, the non-cellular constituents contained in extracellular vesicles represent a critical component, collectively known as the extracellular matrix (ECM) [62]. Tumor cells are capable of perceiving mechanical signals originating from both cell–cell and cell-ECM interactions [63], which subsequently trigger intracellular physical and biochemical changes, ultimately affecting the organization of the cytoskeletal architecture [64]. The transcriptional coactivators Yes-associated protein (YAP) and its homologous counterpart, transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ), function as key mediators in cellular mechanotransduction pathways, allowing cells to sense the structural organization and mechanical characteristics of their surrounding microenvironment [65]. Zheng et al. investigated the mechanical microenvironment of LC cells following radiotherapy, demonstrating that IR-induced overexpression of lncRNA CRYBG3 disrupts cytoskeletal organization and mechanotransduction signaling pathways [66]. At the molecular level, the long non-coding RNA CRYBG3 interferes with the mechanotransduction pathway by disrupting the structural organization of F-actin, activates the Hippo signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting the nuclear localization and transcriptional activity of YAP/TAZ, and ultimately restricts tumor growth and metastatic potential [66]. Furthermore, the contribution of CRYBG3 to radioresistance in NSCLC is also associated with the MDM2-p53 signaling pathway [67]. Downregulation of CRYBG3 decreases MDM2 expression, resulting in reduced MDM2-mediated p53 degradation in wild-type p53 NSCLC cells, which consequently enhances radiosensitivity[0].

In summary, lncRNAs can regulate radiosensitivity not only by directly modulating tumor cell activation through target genes but also by altering metabolic processes within the TME to influence radiosensitivity. These findings, which establish a connection between lncRNAs and NSCLC radiosensitivity, highlight the potential for developing novel therapeutic strategies for NSCLC. The interaction between lncRNAs and specific miRNAs regulates gene expression, influences intracellular pathological processes, and modulates the molecular mechanisms underlying radiotherapy resistance. LncRNAs serve as critical molecular mediators, coordinating complex communication networks between tumor cells and their surrounding microenvironment. Moreover, the IR-induced DNA damage response (DDR) promotes the release of cytokines and chemokines, leading to subsequent activation of inflammatory responses and modifications in the TME. The released factors and activated immune cells establish a complex regulatory network in conjunction with lncRNAs. Even lncRNAs can directly participate in the DDR repair pathways. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms through which lncRNAs modulate the radiosensitivity of NSCLC cells provides a crucial theoretical basis for improving radiotherapeutic outcomes. miRNAs

The role of miRNAs in cancer was first characterized in 2002 [68]. Early studies revealed that the MIR15 and MIR16 genes were frequently deleted in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), with a corresponding decrease in their transcriptional levels [68]. Contemporary research has established that miRNA dysregulation is a widespread phenomenon across multiple cancer types under investigation [68]. An intriguing phenomenon has been observed, whereby the same miRNA exhibits oncogenic properties in a kind of cancer, yet demonstrates tumor-suppressive functions in another [69]. Extensive research has been conducted to investigate the expression, regulation, and functional roles of miRNAs in NSCLC, along with their clinical significance, offering novel insights into tumor progression, metastasis, and therapeutic outcomes in NSCLC.

It is well established that radiotherapy can modulate miRNA expression, thereby influencing therapeutic outcomes. Radiotherapy has been demonstrated to suppress significantly the migratory and invasive potential of NSCLC cells through alterations in miRNA expression levels. For example, radiation substantially upregulates the expression of miR-155-5p and miR-760 in NSCLC cells, which subsequently suppresses tumor cell metastasis [70]. Furthermore, the knockdown of miR-155-5p and miR-760 alleviates the inhibitory effects of radiation on these cells. Radiation downregulates the expression of HIF-α, a target gene of miR-155-5p, and IL-6, a target gene of miR-760, respectively [70]. However, radiation therapy is not capable of eliminating all tumor cells to achieve the intended therapeutic outcome. Residual tumor cells are more likely to acquire invasive properties and develop resistance to radiotherapy, which ultimately leads to an unfavorable prognosis for the tumor. Radiotherapy-resistant NSCLC cells can promote the aggressive behavior of radiotherapy-sensitive cells through the production of plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) [71]. Downregulation of PAI-1 expression following low-dose radiation (LDR) sensitizes NSCLC cells to high-dose radiation (HDR) [71]. Mechanistic analysis indicates that miR-30a and miR-30b induced by LDR exert inhibitory effects on PAI-1 expression [71]. As post-transcriptional regulators that suppress PAI-1 expression, miR-30a and miR-30b reduce both mRNA and protein levels of PAI-1 in cells transfected with their mimics [71]. On one hand, elevated expression of miR-30a and miR-30b inhibits PAI-1-induced activation of AKT and ERK1/2, while promoting caspase-3 activity to enhance apoptosis [71]. On the other hand, the suppression of PAI-1 by miR-30a and miR-30b attenuates epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and reduces the migratory capacity of NSCLC cells [71]. Collectively, these findings suggest that miR-30a and miR-30b may serve as potential radiosensitizers in NSCLC through the downregulation of PAI-1 [71].

Indeed, the simplicity of miRNA detection in both tumor tissues and biological fluids represents a significant advantage. A study by Matthias et al. has demonstrated that circulating miR-16, miR-29a, miR-144, and miR-150 are associated with the response to radiotherapy and the prognosis of patients with NSCLC [72]. They measured the plasma levels of these miRNAs in blood samples from 178 patients with locally unresectable NSCLC at three time points: before, during, and after radiotherapy, using digital droplet PCR [72]. The results demonstrated that radiotherapy significantly decreased plasma miR-150 levels in patients with NSCLC; however, this reduction was not associated with patient prognosis [72]. Although the plasma levels of miR-16, miR-29a, and miR-144 do not exhibit significant changes following radiotherapy, multivariate Cox regression analysis reveals that NSCLC patients with lower expression levels of these three miRNAs are at an increased risk of mortality at the conclusion of radiotherapy [72]. Moreover, another study shows the impact of different brain radiotherapy modalities on serum miR-21 levels in patients with LC and brain metastases [73]. This study evaluates the efficacy and serum miR-21 levels in conventional intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) and combined radiotherapy modalities, including whole-brain radiotherapy and IMRT [73]. The results indicate that the combined radiotherapy group exhibits significantly higher efficacy, along with reduced miR-21 and tumor marker levels. A positive correlation was observed between miR-21 levels and tumor markers [73]. Furthermore, lower miR-21 levels in patients with brain metastatic LC were associated with prolonged OS [73]. In addition, in patients with NSCLC, the expression levels of miR-219a-5p in both serum and tumor tissues are significantly reduced in the radioresistant group compared to those in the radiosensitive group [74]. Overexpression of miR-219a-5p enhances the radiosensitivity of NSCLC cells both in vitro and in vivo, while suppressing tumor cell viability and metastatic potential [74]. Luciferase reporter gene assays indicate that miR-219a-5p directly targets the 3'UTR region of CD164, leading to the downregulation of CD165 expression [74]. Meanwhile, the miR-219a-5p-mediated upregulation of γ-H2A histone family member X (γ-H2AX) is suppressed by the overexpression of CD164 [74]. γ-H2AX serves as a specific biomarker for the detection of cellular DNA damage. The results suggest that miR-219a-5p plays a role in radiation-induced cytotoxicity through the inhibition of CD164, induction of DNA damage, and activation of apoptosis.

Exosomes are small vesicular structures actively secreted by cells into the extracellular environment, where they function as signaling molecules to mediate intercellular communication [75]. Nearly all human body cells are capable of producing exosomes, including tumor cells. There is evidence indicating that exosomes secreted by tumor or stromal cells play an indispensable role during tumor progression [75]. More importantly, exosomes containing miRNAs represent a critical pathway for mediating communication between tumor cells and their surrounding cellular environment [76]. For instance, miR-26b-5p, a novel miRNA that has been identified, is downregulated in radiation-resistant LUAD cells [77]. miR-26b-5p effectively suppresses tumor growth, and this inhibitory effect is particularly pronounced under conditions of radiotherapy-induced miR-26b-5p overexpression [77]. miR-26b-5p negatively regulates ATF2-mediated DNA damage, induces apoptosis in LUAD cells, and enhances sensitivity to radiotherapy [77]. Notably, miR-26b-5p secreted by parental cells can be transferred into radiation-resistant LUAD cells through exosomal encapsulation, thereby exerting an inhibitory effect via the mechanism as mentioned earlier to enhance radiotherapy sensitivity [77]. Additionally, miR-26b-5p is detectable in exosomes isolated from the serum of LUAD patients, and low expression levels of miR-26b-5p are correlated with shorter overall survival in these patients [77].

Furthermore, intercellular communication within the TME extends beyond interactions between tumor cells to include those between tumor cells and surrounding stromal cells. A recent study has found that exosomes secreted by LC cells following radiation exposure can modulate the phenotype of macrophages [78]. Accumulation and activation of macrophages can trigger an inflammatory response that contributes to fibrosis and ultimately results in chronic radiation-induced damage, such as radiation pneumonitis [79]. Co-culturing macrophages with exosomes enhances the expression of inflammatory factors, including IL-1β, CSF2, and IL-10, at both the mRNA and protein levels [78]. miRNA microarray analysis reveals that exosomal miR-4655-5p plays a particularly critical role, as it is the most significantly differentially expressed miRNA [78]. Functional experiments show that miR-4655 overexpression activates the expression of CSF2 and IL-10 and promotes macrophage proliferation, activation, and M2 polarization [78]. Further investigations indicate that miR-4655 negatively regulates M1D1 and indirectly activates PP2Ac, thereby exerting its regulatory effects on macrophages [78].

In addition to tumor cells, exosomal miRNAs can originate from stromal cells within the TME [76]. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) represent key components of the TME and play a crucial role in tumorigenesis and malignant progression. Fei et al. have identified that LC cells exhibit significantly increased radiotherapy resistance and enhanced viability when co-cultured with CAF-derived exosomes [80]. Notably, miR-196a-5p expression is elevated in lung-derived CAFs [80]. The dual-luciferase reporter gene assay has confirmed that miR-196a-5p can directly bind to the 3'-untranslated region (3'-UTR) of nuclear factor kappa-B inhibitor alpha (NFKBIA), thereby suppressing its expression. Overexpression of NFKBIA decreases nuclear p65 levels and partially counteracts the miR-196a-5p-induced increase in atomic p65 [80]. Collectively, CAF-derived exosomal miR-196a-3p enhances the radiosensitization of LC cells by suppressing NFKBIA and subsequently activating the NF-κB signaling pathway.

IR modulates the expression levels of miRNAs throughout its therapeutic process. These IR-induced miRNAs can influence the efficacy of radiotherapy in NSCLC cells through multiple molecular pathways. For example, miRNAs are involved in key signaling pathways such as AKT/ERK, where they play crucial roles in regulating tumorigenesis, disease progression, and mechanisms of radiotherapy resistance [71]. Additionally, miRNAs can act as signaling molecules that mediate communication between NSCLC cells and surrounding stromal cells, including those involved in immune responses. These interactions not only influence tumor initiation and progression but also play a critical role in shaping a radioresistant TME. A deeper understanding of these mechanisms may expand the clinical applications of miRNAs in cancer therapy. CircRNAs

Compared with linear genes, circRNAs exhibit greater stability and expression specificity in organisms due to their unique closed-loop structure [81]. The role of circRNA in cancer was first elucidated through deep sequencing analyses, which identified numerous abundant and differentially expressed circular transcripts across various cancer cell lines in 2013 [82]. Furthermore, this observation has been empirically validated in a clinical patient cohort [81]. Recent research findings indicate that dysregulated circRNA profiles are involved in mechanisms of therapeutic resistance in oncology, particularly radiation therapy resistance [83]. Significantly, circRNAs within the TME and tumor cells influence the efficacy of radiation therapy [84].

For instance, circPVT1 is upregulated in NSCLC following radiation treatment and contributes to the promotion of its malignant phenotype [85]. Functional studies have demonstrated that knockdown of circPVT1 suppresses the growth and invasion of non-small cell lung cancer NSCLC cells and promotes apoptosis [85]. Moreover, mechanistic experiments have confirmed that circPVT1 decreases the radiosensitivity of NSCLC by downregulating miR-1208 and inhibiting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway [85]. Similar to lncRNAs, serving as a miRNA sponge represents an essential mechanism by which circRNAs exert their functional roles [86].Circ_0010235 expression is significantly elevated in NSCLC tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues [87]. Silencing circ_0010235 inhibits cell proliferation, invasion, and migration while promoting apoptosis and enhancing radiosensitivity. Circ_0010235 functions as a ceRNA of miR-588 to regulate HOXA10, thereby attenuating the efficacy of radiotherapy. Silencing miR-588 reversed the regulatory effect in circ_0010235-inhibited cells [87].

Furthermore, pyroptosis is a form of programmed cell death that has been increasingly well-characterized in recent years. Unlike apoptosis, pyroptosis is a form of inflammatory cell death characterized by the coordinated release of inflammatory mediators during cell death, which subsequently triggers a systemic inflammatory response [88]. Recent studies have demonstrated that pyroptosis plays a significant role in response to radiation therapy. circNEIL3 is aberrantly downregulated in LUAD cells following radiation [89]. The loss of circNEIL3 enhances radiation-induced pyroptosis and improves radiation efficacy in vivo [89]. Further analysis confirms that circNEIL3 contains a binding site for miR-1184, which regulates PIF1, forming a post-transcriptional regulatory network. Indeed, the direct interaction between circNEIL3 and miR-1184 enhances PIF1 expression, leading to substantial DNA damage, which subsequently activates the AIM2 inflammasome and triggers pyroptotic cell death [89]. Silencing PIF1 reverses the reduction in γ-H2AX expression caused by circNEIL3 overexpression [89].

Wild-type p53 NSCLC cells can develop acquired resistance to radiation therapy [90]. In a study by Zhao et al., circ-0006420 is highly expressed in p53-wild-type NSCLC cells following radiation, suggesting that circ-0006420 is positively correlated with radioresistance in NSCLC [90]. Depletion of p53 inhibits the upregulation of circ-0006420 expression following radiotherapy [90]. Upregulated circ-0006420 mediates the DDR, induces G2/M arrest, and enhances the radiation resistance of lung epithelial cells [90]. Meanwhile, circ-0006420 overexpression promotes radiation-induced EMT. Target gene analysis has shown that, in the presence of p53, circ-0006420 translocates to the nucleus after irradiation and interacts with HUR, serving as a scaffold for the HUR/PTBP1 complex [90]. The formation of the circ-0006420/HUR/PTBP1 complex prevents the nuclear export of PTBP1 mRNA and suppresses PTBP1 expression, thereby promoting radiation resistance [90]. Here, p53-responsive circ-0006420 contributes to radiotherapy resistance in NSCLC through multiple mechanisms.

Furthermore, most studies investigating the role of circRNA in tumor radioresistance are limited in scope. Although research on the regulatory role of circRNAs in tumor radioresistance is still in its early stages, we propose that circRNAs may provide new insights into developing radiotherapy strategies in oncology. Future and prospect of clinical application of ncRNA

Aberrant expression of ncRNAs is present across all stages of tumorigenesis and exerts functional significance. The expression, regulation, function, and clinical relevance of ncRNAs have been intensely studied in solid cancers (including NSCLC), which has furthered the understanding of the processes of tumor transformation, metastasis formation, and treatment efficacy [28, 91, 92]. Theoretically, the repertoire of functional ncRNAs is extensive; thus, investigating ncRNA-related therapeutic strategies in tumors holds great promise.

Biomarkers in bodily fluids hold significant clinical value due to their reliability and reproducibility in tumor detection. Biomarkers that are readily obtainable from bodily fluids include circulating ncRNAs. Being primarily secreted by cells, ncRNAs offer valuable insights into associated diseases and biological processes. In patients with NSCLC, circulating miR-29a and miR-150-5p have been identified as radiation dose-dependent biomarkers, with their intracellular levels increasing in NSCLC cells following irradiation [72]. Consequently, it may be postulated that aberrant levels of circulating miR-29a and miR-150 could potentially serve as predictive biomarkers for unexpected responses to radiotherapy [93]. Furthermore, NSCLC patients with lower levels of miR-29a at the completion of radiotherapy exhibited the most unfavorable prognosis [72]. NcRNAs can predict the treatment response in specific patients before, during, or after radiotherapy. This correlation is partially attributed to the functional roles of ncRNAs in intercellular communication, which subsequently contribute to radiation therapy resistance.

However, the clinical translation of all RNA-based therapeutics has been limited by challenges in achieving specificity, efficient delivery, and acceptable tolerability [94, 95]. Although lncRNAs have become a focal point of recent research, clinical studies evaluating their potential as therapeutic targets have not been conducted. Clinical applications based on miRNAs have been increasingly explored in phase II and phase III clinical trials [96].

A variety of RNA-based therapies have been developed, including antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs), miRNA mimics or inhibitors, and CRISPR/Cas9-based editing approaches [97, 98]. ASOs are short, single-stranded nucleic acids that bind to specific RNA sequences via precise base pairing, thereby promoting the degradation of target RNA or modulating gene expression by inhibiting translation [99]. siRNAs are synthetic, double-stranded RNA molecules that induce transient gene silencing by complementary base pairing with target sequences [100]. shRNAs effectively overcome the limitations of siRNAs by substantially extending their functional lifespan. miRNA mimics exert a substitution effect by replacing tumor-suppressive miRNAs or by inhibiting oncogenic miRNAs [101]. CRISPR/Cas9 represents a revolutionary advancement in genome editing technology [102].

Moreover, chemical modification of ncRNAs can enhance the stability and targeting specificity of RNA molecules and has shown progress in reducing immunogenicity [103, 104]. For example, 2′-ribose modifications on siRNAs can abolish TLR stimulation, while phosphacylmorpholine oligomers (PMOs) impede TLR interaction by neutralizing the charge of small RNA therapeutics [105]. Alterations to the ribose in siRNAs can reduce off-target effects by decreasing the thermal stability of siRNAs, thereby promoting more specific binding to target sequences [105]. In addition, chemical modifications of endogenous miRNAs may potentially elicit immune responses.

Furthermore, hydrophilic and negatively charged RNA therapeutics are unable to cross cell membranes and therefore require delivery vectors or chemical modifications to access their intracellular targets [103, 104]. Nanomaterials exhibiting biodegradability, biocompatibility, and low toxicity are considered ideal candidates for RNA delivery [106–108]. Among them, lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) represent one of the most extensively utilized non-viral delivery systems for RNA therapeutics [109]. For example, surface modification with hyaluronic acid (HA) can reduce nonspecific uptake, cytotoxicity, and aggregation, while enhancing properties such as tumor targeting and stability [110]. The HA-modified PLGA-PEI nanoparticles (NPs), designed for specific internalization by tumor cells, delivered miR-145 [110]. Following topical application of this construct to tumor-bearing mice that received intact miRNA plasmid vectors, miR-145 was stably expressed and demonstrated significant antitumor effects [110]. Additionally, cell-derived exosomes have been increasingly recognized as effective delivery vectors. However, large-scale production of exosomes remains challenging due to the high costs associated with current isolation and purification methods.

In summary, despite the challenges in the development of RNA therapy, technological advances aimed at improving tolerability, specificity, and delivery efficiency have facilitated the gradual exploration of ncRNA therapy in clinical practice.

Conclusion

ncRNAs have the potential to enable researchers and clinicians to better discover molecular signatures that help accurately diagnose and differentiate malignant tumors, determine prognosis, and judge the risk of metastasis and treatment effectiveness.
