Adverse experiences in childhood and young adulthood, genetic susceptibility, and neurodegenerative disease incidence: a lifespan analysis
Authors: Jie Li, Xiaoqin Xu, Ying Sun, Yanqi Fu, Xiao Tan, Ningjian Wang, Yingli Lu, Jiang Li, Bin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Much remains unknown about the associations between adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), adverse adulthood experiences (AAEs) and the risk of neurodegenerative diseases, including dementia and Parkinson’s disease (PD).Purpose: To examine the associations of ACEs and AAEs with incident dementia and PD, and to evaluate their interactions with genetic risk.Methods: We included 147,942 participants (mean [SD]: 55.9 [7.7] years) without dementia and PD at baseline from UK Biobank. ACEs and AAEs were assessed through an online mental health questionnaire, including emotional neglect, physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, and physical neglect. Polygenic risk scores (PRS) were constructed for dementia and PD. Replication analysis was conducted in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) cohort.Results: During a median follow-up of 15.1 years, 851 incident dementia and 729 PD cases occurred. A greater number of ACEs was associated with increased risks of dementia (HR, 1.14, 95% CI: 1.08–1.21, per additional ACE) and PD (1.11, 1.04–1.18). Similarly, a higher number of AAEs was linked to elevated risks of dementia (1.16, 1.09–1.24) and PD (1.02, 0.95–1.10), though the latter was not statistically significant. Moreover, significant additive interactions between ACEs, AAEs, and genetic risk were observed for dementia, which accounted for an additional 13% to 19% of dementia cases. Results from the CHARLS confirmed the associations of ACEs and AAEs with dementia and PD.Conclusions: Exposure to ACEs or AAEs was associated with increased risks of dementia and PD. The dementia risk associated with ACEs was amplified in individuals with AAEs or high genetic susceptibility. These findings highlight the importance of life-course prevention targeting both ACEs and AAEs in mitigating dementia and PD risks, particularly among individuals with high genetic susceptibility. These findings should be interpreted with caution due to potential recall bias, self-reported assessments, and selection bias.KEYWORDS: Adverse childhood experience, adverse adult experience, genetic risk, dementia, Parkinson’s disease

Introduction

Neurodegenerative diseases are a diverse group of disorders characterised by a progressive deterioration of the structure or function of neuronal cells, with devastating effects on individuals, families, and society. Dementia and Parkinson’s disease (PD), two most common neurodegenerative diseases, affect over 60 million individuals globally and rank as the seventh leading cause of mortality (Feigin et al., 2019; Nichols et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2024). To date, approved pharmacologic therapies for dementia and PD are limited and their clinical efficacy remains controversial, highlighting the pressing need to prioritise the identification of modifiable risk factors for disease prevention.

Beyond well-established risk factors, prior studies have documented associations between adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and increased risks of cognitive decline and dementia in later life (Ma et al., 2021; Norton et al., 2011; Radford et al., 2017; Schalinski et al., 2018; Whalley et al., 2013). However, these studies were constrained by cross-sectional designs, relatively small sample sizes, or simply treating ACEs as a dichotomous variable. To date, only one study has investigated the longitudinal associations of overall and individual ACEs with dementia incidence among older Japanese adults (Tani et al., 2020). Emerging evidence also suggests that early-life stress may elevate the risk of emotional disorders such as depression (Beydoun et al., 2022; Dallé & Mabandla, 2018; Ren et al., 2022), which are well-established risk factors for PD (Jeong et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2018). However, whether there is a potential direct link between ACEs and PD remains unclear. In addition, exposure to ACEs may increase the likelihood of occurrence of adverse experiences in adulthood, referred to as adverse adult experiences (AAEs) (Crielaard et al., 2021; Korkeila et al., 2010). Nevertheless, existing evidence regarding the associations of AAEs with the risks of dementia and PD, as well as the interactions and joint effects of ACEs and AAEs, remains limited.

Apart from environmental exposure, genetic factors also play important roles in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases (Beydoun et al., 2022; Jeong et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2018). Previous genome-wide association studies have identified numerous genetic variants related to dementia and PD (Chang et al., 2017; Lourida et al., 2019). In this context, constructing the polygenic risk score (PRS) serves as a useful tool to predict individual genetic risk. Despite interactions between ACEs and genetic variations have been suggested in psychiatric disorders (Kim et al., 2019), no studies have explored the influence of genetic susceptibility on the relationship between ACEs and dementia or PD incidence.

In this study, we utilised data from the UK Biobank to examine the associations of ACEs and AAEs with incident dementia and PD. Furthermore, we comprehensively investigated the joint and interaction effects of ACEs in combination with AAEs or genetic susceptibility on the risk of dementia and PD. Finally, we explored the potential mediating pathways linking ACEs to dementia and PD onset.

Methods

Study design and population

The UK Biobank is a population-based cohort study that enrolled over 500,000 participants aged 40–73 years in 22 assessment centres across England, Scotland, and Wales between 2006 and 2010 (Sudlow et al., 2015). The UK Biobank was approved by the North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee (11/NW/0382) and written informed consent was provided by all participants. During 2016 and 2017, almost one-third of the participants (n = 157,299) completed an online mental health questionnaire, including questions about their ACEs and AAEs (Figure S1A). After excluding those with missing data on ACEs or AAEs (n = 9,190), and prevalent dementia or PD at baseline (n = 167) (Figure S2A), a total of 147,942 participants were included.

To validate the effects of ACEs and AAEs on incidence of dementia and PD, we further repeated the primary analyses in an independent, prospective cohort of Chinese adults: China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Details of the cohort design and methods have been provided in Method S1.

Adverse childhood and adulthood experiences

In the UK Biobank, ACEs were assessed using the Childhood Trauma Screener, a cost-efficient, validated, and reliable screening tool in large epidemiological studies (Glaesmer et al., 2013). The ACEs were evaluated through five questions representing emotional neglect, physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, and physical neglect. Each item referred to adverse experiences as a child. AAEs were assessed using a questionnaire with the same structure, referring to experiences since the age of 16 years. The response to every question ranged from ‘never true’ to ‘very often true’ on a 5-point scale (coded as 0–4) (Table S1). The presence of each type of adverse experience was defined using validated thresholds (Soares et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2022), i.e. score ≤2 for emotional neglect, ≥ 1 for physical/emotional/sexual abuse, and ≤3 for physical neglect. The total score of the five items was generated by summing the number of ACEs or AAEs, ranging from 0 to 5, with a higher score indicating more adverse experiences in childhood or adulthood.

In the CHARLS, ten ACE indicators and four AAE indicators were identified based on previous studies (Lin et al., 2022; W. Wang et al., 2023). Details about assessment of ACEs and AAEs are provided in Table S2. For each indicator, the response was dichotomised (0 for absence and 1 for presence), with ACE scores ranging from 0 to 10 and AAE scores from 0 to 5.

Outcome ascertainment

The primary outcomes of this study were dementia and PD incidence. In the UK Biobank, the incident cases were extracted from either the first occurrence of health outcomes or algorithmically defined outcomes. Diagnostic information was obtained from self-reported medical conditions, hospital inpatient records, and linkages to primary care and death registers, using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) codes: all-cause dementia (F00–F03, G30) and PD (G20–G23). Participants were followed from baseline to the date of diagnosis, death, or censoring (30 April 2024), whichever occurred first. In the CHARLS, dementia and PD as a composite outcome (not recorded separately) were assessed based on self-reported diagnoses in follow-up surveys. The follow-up time was measured as the time elapsed from the date of baseline assessment to the first of either date of dementia or PD diagnosis, death, the latest interview before dropout, or the final interview date (31 August 2018).

Polygenic risk scores

The standard PRS in the UK Biobank (Category ID: 301) for Alzheimer’s disease (AD) or PD was generated from meta-analyses of multiple external genome-wide association studies, as described in detail elsewhere (Thompson et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2024). In brief, AD or PD-related single nucleotide polymorphisms were selected, and the number of AD or PD-related alleles for each participant was summed after weighing for the association strength of each allele with AD or PD to calculate the PRS for dementia and PD (See Method S2 for more details).

Covariates and mediators

Based on previous studies (Soares et al., 2022; Wen et al., 2024), the covariates considered as potential confounders in the UK Biobank cohort included age, sex, race (White/others), Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI, a higher score indicating a lower socioeconomic level), education level (college or university/others), maternal smoking, genetic kinship to other participants, PRS for dementia or PD, and the first 10 principal components of ancestry. Data on candidate mediators including lifestyle factors (current smoking, current drinking, healthy diet score, healthy sleep score, and regular physical activity), cardiometabolic health (obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia), mental health (depression and anxiety), and C-reactive protein (CRP) were collected at baseline (see details in Method S3). In the CHARLS cohort, covariates included age, sex, race (Han or others), residential location (rural or urban), and education level (college or university/others).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive characteristic statistics were presented as means with standard deviations (SDs) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables, and numbers (percentages) for categorical variables. For missing data, we applied mean imputation for continuous variables and a missing-indicator method (e.g. 99) for categorical variables (Li et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023). The missing percentages were as follows: 0.05% for race, 0.13% for TDI, 0.63% for education level, 2.10% for first 10 principal components of ancestry, 2.29% for genetic kinship, 2.45% for PRS for dementia or PD, and 12.41% for maternal smoking.

Cox proportional hazard models were used to examine the associations of ACEs and AAEs with incident dementia and PD. In the UK Biobank, three models were constructed. Model 1 was adjusted for basic demographic factors (age and sex). Model 2 was further adjusted for socioeconomic and early-life factors (race, TDI, education level, and maternal smoking) to account for their potential influence on adverse experiences. Model 3 was additionally adjusted for genetic factors (genetic kinship, PRS for dementia or PD, and first 10 principal components of ancestry). In this analysis, ACEs and AAEs were fitted as continuous variables (the total ACE/AAE score), binary variables (exposed to any ACE/AAE vs not exposed), or ordinal variables (0, 1, 2, or ≥3 ACEs/AAEs). Additionally, we conducted separate analyses for different subtypes of ACEs and AAEs. In the CHARLS, two models were built. Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 2 was further adjusted for race, residential location, and education level.

To examine the joint associations of ACEs and AAEs with incident dementia and PD, participants were divided into four groups based on their exposure to ACEs and AAEs (binary variables). We quantified both multiplicative and additive interactions. The multiplicative interaction was tested by including a cross-product term of ACEs and AAEs in the final model. The relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and the attributable proportion because of interaction (AP) were calculated as the measure of interaction on the additive scale (Knol et al., 2007, 2011). To explore the joint associations of ACEs and genetic risk with dementia and PD, participants were classified into six groups according to ACEs (binary variables) and tertiles of PRS for dementia or PD (low, moderate, or high genetic risk), with those at low genetic risk without ACEs serving as the reference group.

We further conducted mediation analyses to investigate the mediation effect of socioeconomic status, lifestyle factors, cardiometabolic health, mental health, or systemic inflammation on the associations of ACEs with dementia and PD incidence. Linear regression or logistic regression models with adjustment for covariates were used to assess the associations between ACEs (total ACE score) and potential mediators. Then, Cox regression models were applied to examine the associations of the mediators with dementia and PD. Mediation proportions were estimated through the ‘mediation’ package in R, with Quasi-Bayesian estimation (1,000 iterations) used to calculate 95% CIs for the mediation proportions.

Several additional analyses were conducted to test the robustness of our results. First, we further adjusted for potential mediators, including obesity, current smoking, current drinking, diet score, sleep score, physical activity, hypertension, stroke, depression, anxiety, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and CRP. Second, we excluded incident dementia and PD cases that occurred between the enrolment and mental health survey to reduce possible reverse causality. Third, the Fine-Gray proportional hazards model was used to account for competing risks of death. Fourth, a complete case analysis was performed by excluding participants with missing data on covariates. To assess potential effect modification, stratified analyses were performed by age (<60 or ≥60 years), sex (male or female), TDI (<50% or ≥50%), education level (college/university or others), and maternal smoking (yes or no).

All analyses were conducted using R statistical software version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A two-tailed p < .05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

This study included 147,942 UK Biobank participants. The mean age was 55.9 (SD, 7.7) years, and 82,862 (56.0) were females. Of the overall participants, 68,452 (46.2%) reported exposure to at least one ACE and 78,947 (53.4%) reported exposure to at least one AAE. Associations of ACE and AAE with incident dementia and PD

During a median follow-up of 15.1 years, a total of 851 incident dementia cases and 729 PD cases were identified in the UK Biobank. Dose-response associations were observed between ACEs and both dementia and PD. Each additional ACE was associated with a 14% (HR: 1.14, 95% CI: 1.08–1.21) higher risk of dementia and an 11% (1.11, 1.04–1.18) higher risk of PD (Figure 1). Compared with individuals without ACEs, the HRs (95% CIs) for incident dementia and PD were 1.34 (1.17–1.53) and 1.11 (0.96–1.28) for individuals with at least one ACE, respectively, which increased to 1.50 (1.19–1.90) and 1.52 (1.20–1.94) for those with three or more ACEs. Among the five types of ACEs, physical neglect and emotional abuse were associated with increased risks of dementia (HR: 1.87 [95% CI: 1.60–2.17] for physical neglect; 1.19 [0.96–1.49] for emotional abuse) and PD (1.35 [1.12–1.61] for physical neglect; 1.29 [1.03–1.62] for emotional abuse) (Table 2).

For AAEs, each additional AAE was associated with a higher risk of dementia (HR: 1.16, 95% CI: 1.09–1.24) but not PD (1.02, 0.95–1.10) (Figure 1). Compared with individuals without any ACEs, the HR (95% CI) for incident dementia was 1.41 (1.23–1.62) for those who had at least one AAE and 1.48 (1.12–1.94) for those who had three or more AAEs. Among different types of AAEs, physical neglect (HR: 1.46, 95% CI: 1.23–1.73) and emotional neglect (1.35, 1.17–1.55) were associated with an increased risk of dementia, while a significant relationship with PD was found for emotional neglect only (1.20, 1.03–1.41) (Table 2).

Joint and interaction effects of ACEs and AAEs on incident dementia and PD

The joint analysis of ACEs and AAEs demonstrated that participants exposed to both had the highest risk of dementia (HR: 1.64, 95% CI: 1.38–1.95) compared with those without exposure to either (Figure 2(A), Table S4). A significant additive interaction (p = .029) between ACEs and AAEs on dementia risk was observed. The RERI was 0.32 (0.02–0.67), indicating that individuals exposed to both ACEs and AAEs had a 32% higher relative risk of dementia than would be expected from the sum of their individual effects. Additionally, the AP was 19% (1%−40%), suggesting that 19% of dementia cases could be attributed to the interaction between ACEs and AAEs. For PD, the HR (95% CI) for individuals exposed to at least one ACE and AAE was 1.18 (0.98–1.43) (Figure 2(B), Table S5), without detecting significant multiplicative and additive interactions between ACEs and AAEs.

Joint and interaction effects of ACEs and genetic risk on incident dementia and PD

We observed positive associations of dementia and PD risk with PRS for corresponding disease (Table S6). Furthermore, we assessed the joint effects of the PRS and ACEs on incident dementia and PD. Compared to individuals with low PRS and no ACEs, those with high PRS and exposure to ACEs exhibited the highest risk of dementia (HR: 4.03, 95% CI: 3.03–5.37) (Figure 2(C), Table S7) and PD (2.79, 2.08–3.75) (Figure 2(D), Table S8). An additive interaction between ACEs and the PRS for dementia was detected (p = .045). The RERI was 0.38 (0.15–0.93) and the AP was 13% (7%−19%). This indicates that individuals with both ACEs and high genetic risk had a 38% greater relative risk of dementia than expected from the sum of their individual effects, and approximately 13% of dementia cases could be attributed to the interaction. In contrast, no multiplicative interactions (p = .536) or additive interactions (p = .077) were observed between ACEs and the PRS for PD.

Mediation analysis

All the potential mediators were significantly associated with ACEs except for physical activity (Table 3). Among them, nine showed significant associations with dementia and five with PD. The relationship between ACEs and the risk of dementia was mediated by TDI, obesity, current drinking, healthy sleep score, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, stroke, and depression, with the proportion of mediation effect ranging from 1.3% to 6.2% (Table 3, Table S9). For the relationship between ACEs and PD risk, the largest mediation proportion was found for depression (7.5%), followed by alcohol drinking (3.2%), diabetes (1.5%), and diet score (1.3%).

Sensitivity analyses and subgroup analyses

The results did not essentially change after excluding the incident cases occurring between the enrolment and mental health survey, considering competing risk from death, or excluding participants with missing covariates (Table S10–S12). In the sensitivity analysis with additional adjustment for all potential mediators, the corresponding HRs were slightly attenuated but remained statistically significant (Table S13).

Consistent results were observed across subgroups stratified by sex, TDI, education level, and maternal smoking. A significant interaction between ACEs and age was observed for PD risk (pinteraction = .007) (Figure 3). The HR (95% CI) for PD associated with per additional ACE was 1.20 (1.08–1.33) among individuals younger than 60 years and 1.06 (0.97–1.15) among those aged 60 years and older. Replication in the CHARLS study

The CHARLS cohort comprised 9,472 participants, with an average age of 58.0 (8.5) years and 5,052 (53.3%) were females. Of the participants, 8,301 (87.6%) reported exposure to at least one ACE and 3,681 (38.9%) reported exposure to at least one AAE. Baseline characteristics of the study population by the number of ACEs and AAEs are shown in Tables S14 and S15, respectively. During a median follow-up of 7.1 years, 394 incident dementia and PD cases were observed. Despite methodological differences from the UK Biobank, consistent associations between ACEs and AAEs and the risk of dementia and PD were observed in CHARLS. Each 1-number increment in ACEs and AAEs was associated with a 10% (HR: 1.10, 95% CI: 1.01–1.20) and 29% (1.29, 1.19–1.39) higher risk of composite dementia and PD, respectively (Table S16).

Discussion

In this cohort study of 147,942 participants from the UK Biobank, we found that exposure to ACEs was associated with an increased risk of incident dementia and PD in a dose-dependent manner. Exposure to AAEs was also linked to an increased risk of dementia. In the joint analysis, participants exposed to both ACEs and AAEs, as well as those with ACEs and high genetic risk, exhibited a substantially higher risk of dementia and PD. Additionally, we observed additive interactions between ACEs and AAEs, and between ACEs and genetic risk, in relation to dementia. As well, socioeconomic status, lifestyle factors, and cardiometabolic and psychological disorders mediated a modest proportion of the associations between ACEs and incident dementia and PD.

Previous studies have indicated the link between exposure to ACEs and risk of dementia, despite the wide variations in specific definitions of ACEs across studies (Norton et al., 2011; Radford et al., 2017; Whalley et al., 2013). In this study, we observed a graded increase in dementia risk with the number of ACEs (i.e. emotional neglect, physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, and physical neglect). This result was in line with a longitudinal cohort study among older Japanese adults (≥65 years), which assessed ACEs from seven dimensions: parental death, parental divorce, parental mental illness, family violence, physical abuse, psychological neglect, and psychological abuse (Tani et al., 2020). To reduce the confounding from genetic factors, we additionally adjusted for dementia-related PRS and found similar results, suggesting that the association between ACEs and dementia incidence was independent of genetic susceptibility. In addition, available evidence to date regarding the impact of ACEs on the development of PD is lacking. Our study extended the existing knowledge by demonstrating that exposure to ACEs was associated with an increased risk of PD. Thus, from the life course perspective, reducing ACEs may play an important role in the primary prevention of neurodegenerative diseases such as dementia and PD later in life.

Currently, the associations of AAEs with neurodegenerative diseases remain unclear. Our analysis found a positive association between AAEs and dementia. In fact, prior studies have reported that traumatic experiences such as sexual assault and the threat of injury or violence were associated with greater white matter hyperintensities, which were related to the development of cognitive decline and dementia (Debette & Markus, 2010; Thurston et al., 2022). Among the individual types of AAEs in this study, only emotional neglect showed a significant association with an increased risk of PD and the effect estimate was modest (HR: 1.20, 95% CI: 1.03–1.41). This may contribute to the lack of an association between AAEs and PD onset. Further studies are warranted to confirm these findings.

To our knowledge, the present study is the first to assess the joint effects and interactions of ACEs with AAEs and genetic susceptibility on neurodegenerative diseases. Our results revealed that ACEs in combination with AAEs or high genetic risk were associated with the highest risk of dementia and PD. Furthermore, the additive interactions between ACEs and AAEs or genetic risk on dementia were identified. Approximately 13%−19% of dementia risk could be explained by the additive interactions, suggesting the synergistic effect of co-exposure to ACEs and AAEs, as well as ACEs and high genetic risk, on the incidence of dementia. These findings have far-reaching public health implications for concerted efforts to avoid AAEs among individuals who have ever been exposed to ACEs as well as the priority of targeted interventions for individuals at high genetic risk and exposed to ACEs.

The mechanisms underlying the association between ACEs and dementia might involve the dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, alterations in the gut microbiome, persistent inflammation, oligodendrocyte dysfunction, hypomyelination, and aberrant adult hippocampal neurogenesis (Huang et al., 2023). Furthermore, it’s reported that ACEs were associated with smoking, obesity, and depression, which could mediate the link between ACEs and the risk of dementia (Danese et al., 2009; Hughes et al., 2017). For PD, although there was no direct link with ACEs, some studies have shown that early life stressors like maternal separation could contribute to the development of depression (Dallé & Mabandla, 2018; Ren et al., 2022). Depression is associated with dysfunction of the HPA axis, which plays a key role in the development of neurodegenerative diseases including PD (Dallé & Mabandla, 2018; Daniels et al., 2004). Our study provides evidence that exposure to ACEs was associated with an increased risk of incident PD in later life. Notably, among the five types of ACEs, physical neglect showed the strongest association with dementia and PD. This is likely due to its chronic nature and the profound neurobiological consequences of prolonged caregiving deficits, which can disrupt the oxytocin system, reward pathways, and HPA axis regulation (Bunea et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2025). Such disruptions may be especially deleterious when occurring during sensitive developmental periods. In contrast, sexual abuse, which has traditionally been associated with adverse outcomes in previous studies (Hailes et al., 2019), showed no significant associations with dementia and PD. This may be associated with its frequent underreporting in population-based cohorts, with its prevalence approximately half that of other ACEs in the UK Biobank (Borumandnia et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2025).

We further conducted mediation analyses to investigate the mediating role of socioeconomic status, lifestyle factors, cardiometabolic health, mental health, and systemic inflammation in the association between ACEs and dementia and PD. We found that depression was the strongest mediator linking ACEs and PD, emphasising the role of depression in the association between ACEs and PD. However, the mediation effects of these factors were relatively modest, which could be partly explained by the fact that the participants in UK Biobank are healthier, better educated, and have a more favourable lifestyle compared to the general population (Fry et al., 2017; Macpherson et al., 2021). On the other hand, given the absence of strong mediators, reducing exposure to ACEs may help reduce the risk of neurodegenerative diseases, including dementia and PD.

The main strengths of this study included large sample size, extended follow-up, and standardised exposure assessments, which enable us to obtain relatively robust results with high statistical efficiency. Furthermore, we are the first to quantify the interactions between ACEs, AAEs, and genetic risk on dementia and PD risk. The primary results were validated in an independent cohort from a different ethnic population, enhancing the generalizability of our findings. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, only around one-third of participants with a working email had mental health questionnaire information including ACEs and AAEs in the UK Biobank. Given that participants who completed the online questionnaire tended to be healthier, better educated and had higher socioeconomic status compared to the overall cohort (Zou et al., 2024), selection bias is likely to exist, potentially leading to underestimation of associations in more vulnerable groups and limiting generalizability. Second, ACEs and AAEs were retrospectively assessed via self-report, which may introduce recall bias, as participants could inaccurately remember or underreport traumatic experiences, particularly those from childhood. Third, the assessments lacked detailed information on the severity, frequency, duration, or timing of adversities, as well as other relevant types such as parental death or financial hardship, potentially resulting in incomplete capture of adverse experiences and reduced precision in risk estimates. Fourth, although the associations of ACEs and AAEs with dementia and PD were replicated in the CHARLS cohort, the heterogeneity in the assessments of exposures, outcomes, and covariates between cohorts should be noticed. In particular, the UK Biobank used a focused 5-item Childhood Trauma Screener emphasising neglect and abuse subtypes (scored 0–5), whereas CHARLS employed broader sets of 10 ACE and 4 AAE indicators, including additional adversities like economic hardship and parental separation (scored 0–10 for ACEs and 0–5 for AAEs), leading to differing definitions and potential inconsistencies in exposure assessments. These differences may affect the comparability of effect sizes across cohorts. Moreover, residual confounding from genetic factors could not be accounted for in the CHARLS due to a lack of genetic data. More prospective studies with rigorous design and comprehensive phenotyping are needed to further validate our findings. Finally, the observational nature of this study precluded causal inference regarding the observed associations.

In summary, this large prospective cohort study demonstrated that exposure to ACEs was associated with dementia and PD onset. More importantly, we found that the risk of dementia and PD was substantially higher in participants exposed to both ACEs and AAEs, and those with ACEs and high genetic risk, with evidence of additive interactions between ACEs and AAEs, and between ACEs and high genetic risk, on dementia. These findings emphasise the importance of implementing life-course prevention and intervention strategies targeting both ACEs and AAEs to mitigate the risk of neurodegenerative diseases, especially in individuals with high genetic risk.
