Synthetic Data Generation for Classifying Electrophysiological and Morpho-Electrophysiological Neurons from Mouse Visual Cortex
Xavier Vasques, Laura Cif, Institut du Neurone, Montferriez sur lez, France Département de Neurochirurgie, Hôpital Gui de Chauliac, Montpellier, France Service de Neurologie, Lausanne University Hospital, Lausanne, Switzerland

Abstract
The accurate classification of neuronal cell types is central to decoding brain function, yet remains hindered by data scarcity and cellular heterogeneity. Here, we benchmarked classical and deep generative synthetic data augmentation strategies—including SMOTE, GANs, VAEs, Normalizing Flows, and DDPMs—for supervised classification of both electrophysiological (e-type) and morpho-electrophysiological (mee-type) neuron types from the mouse visual cortex. Using a curated dataset annotated with 48 electrophysiological and 24 morphological features, we established baseline classifiers and introduced synthetic data generated by each method. Our results demonstrate that SMOTE-based augmentation yields the highest classification accuracies (absolute gains of 0.16 for e-types, 0.12 for mee-types), outperforming deep generative models. GANs approached similar performance when hyperparameters and sample sizes were optimized, but were more sensitive to model specification. In addition, we benchmarked synthetic neuron fidelity by comparing mean absolute errors between synthetic and real class profiles against the natural phenotypic variability observed between real neuronal classes.

Synthetic neurons can play a pivotal role in the preliminary phases of drug discovery and electroceuticals, enabling initial in silico evaluations on artificial models prior to in vivo implementations and aiding the exploration of neurological and neurodegenerative conditions and potential remedial interventions.

To provide biologically meaningful validation, we benchmarked synthetic-to-real similarity through classification performance, but also by comparing the variability of synthetic neurons to the natural diversity observed across real neuronal classes.

2.1
Electrophysiological and Morphological Data Selection

The electrophysiological and morphological data used in this research were acquired from the Allen Cell Types Database, accessible at
http://celltypes.brain-map.org

For the current project, 1857 cells exhibiting electrophysiological features and 459 cells with morphological features, originating from the primary visual cortex and proximate higher visual areas, were assessed. Of the electrophysiological cell entities (e-types), 885 were classified as spiny neurons (presumed excitatory), and 972 were categorized as aspiny or sparsely spiny (presumed inhibitory)

The data we analyzed were classified into 17 distinct e-types (4 spiny and 13 aspiny/sparsely spiny) and 38 m-types (19 spiny and 19 aspiny/sparsely spiny). By integrating electrophysiological and morphological neurons, a novel dataset consisting of 451 morpho-electrical neurons was selected. The neurons were classified into 17 distinct types (mee-types) according to their electrophysiological features. Of the 17 distinct mee-types, 4 were spiny neurons and 13 were aspiny/sparsely spiny neurons. 2.2
Data Classification With Supervised Learning Algorithms

To establish robust classification baselines for neuronal subtype prediction, we implemented a comprehensive supervised learning framework that combines feature rescaling, dimensionality reduction, and classifier optimization (Figure
1
). The classification task aimed to identify distinct neuron types based on electrophysiological or morpho-electrophysiological features, prior to the integration of synthetic data generation methods.

In the preprocessing stage, missing values were imputed using column-wise means, followed by systematic evaluation of multiple feature rescaling techniques: quantile transformation (with normal and uniform output distributions), min–max scaling, standard scaling, robust scaling, max-absolute scaling, normalization, and nonlinear power transforms (Box–Cox and Yeo–Johnson). Each rescaling method was independently applied to the input features prior to any dimensionality reduction or classification.

When dimensionality reduction was employed, the feature space was projected onto two components. Both linear and nonlinear reduction techniques were evaluated. Linear methods included principal component analysis (PCA), independent component analysis (ICA), linear discriminant analysis (LDA), truncated singular value decomposition (SVD), and sparse random projection. Nonlinear manifold learning algorithms included isometric mapping (Isomap), neighborhood components analysis (NCA), t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE), multidimensional scaling (MDS), and variants of locally linear embedding (LLE): standard LLE, modified LLE (MLLE), Hessian LLE, and local tangent space alignment (LTSA). Dimensionality reduction was always applied after feature rescaling, and its impact was jointly evaluated with classification accuracy.

For the classification task, a wide array of supervised learning algorithms was systematically benchmarked: support vector machines (SVM) with various kernel functions (linear, polynomial, radial basis function, sigmoid), multilayer perceptrons (MLPs), logistic regression with L1 and L2 regularization, random forest classifiers, extremely randomized trees (Extra Trees), linear discriminant analysis, Gaussian and multinomial Naïve Bayes, and decision trees. All classifiers were subjected to exhaustive hyperparameter optimization using grid search (
GridSearchCV
, scikit-learn), with 10-fold cross-validation.

SVMs were evaluated with multiple kernel functions (linear, polynomial, RBF, sigmoid). The regularization parameter
C
was tested over
\{0.1,\,1,\,10\}
, and the kernel coefficient
\gamma
was evaluated using both
scale
and
auto
options. For tree-based ensembles (Random Forest, Extra Trees), grid search varied the number of estimators (
n_estimators

\in\{50,100,200,300,500\}
), maximum tree depth (
max_depth

\in\{\texttt{None},10,20\}
), minimum samples to split a node (
min_samples_split

\in\{2,10,20\}
), and minimum samples per leaf (
min_samples_leaf

\in\{1,5,10\}
).

Logistic regression was assessed with both L1 and L2 penalties; the inverse regularization strength (
C
) ranged over
\{0.1,\,1,\,10\}
, using the
liblinear
solver. MLPs were tuned over several hidden layer sizes—
(50,)
,
(100,)
,
(50, 50)
—and activation functions (
identity
,
logistic
,
tanh
,
relu
). Solvers included
sgd
,

, and

; regularization strength (

) was varied over
\{0.0001,\,0.001,\,0.005,\,0.01,\,0.05\}
; learning rate schedules included

,

, and

. The maximum number of iterations was fixed at 5,000 for all MLP configurations.

For Naïve Bayes classifiers, Gaussian Naïve Bayes was used with default parameters; Multinomial Naïve Bayes was evaluated with smoothing parameter
\alpha\in\{0.1,\,1,\,10\}
. Decision trees were optimized for maximum depth (
max_depth

\in\{5,10,20\}
), minimum samples split (
min_samples_split

\in\{2,10,20\}
), and minimum samples per leaf (
min_samples_leaf

\in\{1,5,10\}
).

When dimensionality reduction methods required specification of a neighborhood size (e.g., Isomap, LLE and variants), values of 5, 10, and 15 were tested, balancing local structure preservation and computational tractability. Other linear reduction methods (PCA, ICA, LDA, truncated SVD, random projection) were applied with fixed
n_{\text{components}}=2
. For every pipeline, mean accuracy, standard deviation, and ranking were recorded, along with training performance to assess model generalization.

Model selection prioritized three criteria: (i) a maximum allowable difference of 3% between training and test accuracy (to avoid overfitting)
[
24

1

; (ii) low test accuracy variability across folds (standard deviation
\leq 0.02

These criteria were consistently applied across both datasets: 17 e-types (1,857 neurons) and 17 mee-types (459 neurons with combined features). For each dataset, the top classifier configuration was retained based on cross-validated test accuracy and stability. The associated rescaling and dimensionality reduction strategies were then adopted for subsequent synthetic data augmentation experiments.

All analyses were performed in Python using scikit-learn, with results stored in structured CSV files for reproducibility.

2.3
Generative Models for Synthetic Data Generation

To address data scarcity and enhance classification robustness, we employed multiple synthetic data generation techniques for augmenting the training dataset
[
51
]
, including Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)
[
18
]
, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE)
[
13
]
, Normalizing Flows
[
30
]
, Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs)
[
25
]
, and Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)
[
29
]
. These methods were integrated into a feature preprocessing and data augmentation pipeline. Feature rescaling and reduction techniques were chosen based on the best-performing classification algorithms across e-types and mee-types. Additionally, the sequence of preprocessing steps was systematically analyzed by comparing two distinct pipelines:

1.

Scaling before sampling (without dimensionality reduction):
Feature scaling was applied prior to synthetic data generation, without applying any dimensionality reduction technique.

2.

Scaling and reduction before sampling:
Both feature scaling and dimensionality reduction were applied before synthetic data generation.

SMOTE was applied to generate synthetic samples by interpolating between existing data points:

X_{\mathrm{synthetic}}=X_{\mathrm{real}}+\lambda(X_{\mathrm{nearest}}-X_{\mathrm{real}})

(2)

where
\lambda\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
, ensuring the generated samples maintain realistic feature distributions. The number of synthetic samples per class was varied between 1,000, 2,000, 5,000, and 10,000.

GANs employ a generator
G
and a discriminator
D
, where
G
learns to produce synthetic data while
D
distinguishes real from generated samples. The generator is a fully connected neural network that utilizes LeakyReLU activations to enable gradient flow in negative regions, improving stability during training. The discriminator is also a fully connected neural network but employs a sigmoid activation function for binary classification, distinguishing real from synthetic samples. The networks are trained in an adversarial setting over 300 epochs, using the Adam optimizer to adjust model weights. To generate synthetic data, the generator takes as input a noise vector
z
sampled from a standard normal distribution
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
. Each GAN model was trained separately for datasets that underwent different feature-scaling transformations. The optimization process was governed by the Adam optimizer, with a learning rate of 0.0002 and a momentum term of
\beta_{1}=0.5
to stabilize training.

The adversarial training objective follows the min-max loss function:

\min_{G}\max_{D}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\mathrm{real}}}\left[\log D(X)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{z\sim P_{z}}\left[\log(1-D(G(z)))\right]

(3)

where the generator aims to minimize the discriminator’s ability to distinguish real from fake samples, while the discriminator maximizes its classification accuracy. After training, synthetic neurons were generated by sampling 100, 1,000, and 10,000 synthetic neurons per class, providing additional data for classification.

Normalizing Flows are a class of generative models designed to map a simple probability distribution (such as a Gaussian) to a more complex data distribution by applying a sequence of invertible transformations. Unlike GANs, which learn an implicit data distribution, Normalizing Flows explicitly model the probability density of the data, enabling both sample generation and likelihood estimation. The probability density of a data point
X
in the transformed space is computed using the change of variables formula:

p(X)=p(z)\left|\det\left(\frac{df^{-1}}{dx}\right)\right|

(4)

where
p(z)
represents the probability density function of a base Gaussian distribution,
f^{-1}
denotes the learned invertible transformation function, and
\det\left(\frac{df^{-1}}{dx}\right)
is the Jacobian determinant, ensuring that the transformation preserves volume in the probability space.

In this study, we employed a Masked Autoregressive Flow (MAF) architecture consisting of four layers. The MAF model learns a bijective transformation that maps samples from a simple Gaussian prior distribution
p(z)
, with
p(z)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, to the complex probability distribution of the neuronal features. The inverse function
f^{-1}
was learned to map Gaussian samples to synthetic neuronal data. Once trained, new synthetic neurons were generated by sampling from
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and applying the learned transformation:

X_{\mathrm{synthetic}}=f^{-1}(z)

(5)

Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) are a class of generative models that synthesize samples by iteratively adding and removing noise in a multi-step process. Unlike traditional generative models such as GANs, which learn to map noise directly to realistic data, DDPMs model the probabilistic trajectory of data from noise to structure by gradually corrupting the data with Gaussian noise and then learning to reverse this process. The forward diffusion process gradually corrupts the input data
x_{0}
over
T
time steps by adding Gaussian noise at each step:

q(x_{t}|x_{t-1})=\mathcal{N}(x_{t};\alpha_{t}x_{t-1},(1-\alpha_{t})I)

(6)

where
x_{t}
represents the data at time step
t
,
\alpha_{t}
is a scaling factor that controls the amount of noise, and
I
is the identity matrix, ensuring isotropic noise addition. Once the dataset is fully transformed into pure Gaussian noise at step
T
, a reverse diffusion process is learned to reconstruct samples by gradually removing noise:

p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}|x_{t})=\mathcal{N}(x_{t-1};\mu_{\theta}(x_{t},t),\Sigma_{\theta}(x_{t},t))

(7)

where the neural network
p_{\theta}
estimates the mean
\mu_{\theta}
and variance
\Sigma_{\theta}
needed to reverse the process at each step. For this study, we utilized a pretrained DDPM model from Google’s “ddpm-cifar10-32” framework, which was fine-tuned for neuronal feature generation. The model was trained using 1,000 diffusion steps, ensuring a fine-grained denoising process. Synthetic samples were generated by first sampling pure Gaussian noise and applying the learned reverse diffusion process to progressively reconstruct neuronal feature distributions. The final synthetic dataset was obtained by running the trained model on randomly sampled noise and iteratively transforming it into neuronal features.

Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) are a type of generative model that learn a probabilistic latent representation of the input data and generate synthetic samples by sampling from the learned distribution. Unlike GANs, which directly generate synthetic data, VAEs map the original feature space into a continuous latent space and then reconstruct samples using a trained decoder. The encoder learns a probabilistic mapping from input data
X
to a latent variable distribution:

q_{\phi}(z|X)=\mathcal{N}(z;\mu,\sigma^{2})

(8)

where
z
is the latent variable representation,
\mu
and
\sigma^{2}
are the mean and variance of the learned latent space, and
q_{\phi}(z|X)
is modeled as a Gaussian distribution. To enable gradient-based optimization while allowing stochastic sampling, the reparameterization trick is used:

z=\mu+\sigma\cdot\epsilon,\quad\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)

(9)

where
\epsilon
is a standard normal random variable, ensuring that backpropagation can flow through the stochastic operation.

In this study, we implemented a VAE-based synthetic neuronal feature generator with the following architecture:

•

Latent Space:
The dimensionality of the latent space was set to 2D, allowing the model to learn a compact and structured representation of the neuron feature distributions.

•

Encoder:
A fully connected neural network with 64 neurons per layer and ReLU activations, responsible for learning the latent distribution parameters (
\mu,\sigma
).

•

Decoder:
A mirrored fully connected neural network, reconstructing the original feature space from the latent variable representation.

•

Loss Function:
The model was trained using a combined loss function, consisting of:

–

Reconstruction Loss
(
L_{\mathrm{rec}}
), which ensures that the generated samples resemble the real data:

L_{\mathrm{rec}}=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(z|X)}\left[\log p_{\theta}(X|z)\right]

(10)

–

Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence
(
L_{\mathrm{KL}}
), which regularizes the latent space by ensuring that the learned distribution is close to a standard Gaussian:

L_{\mathrm{KL}}=-D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q_{\phi}(z|X)\|p(z)\right)

(11)

The total objective function minimizes the combined loss:

L(\theta,\phi)=L_{\mathrm{rec}}+L_{\mathrm{KL}}

(12)

•

Optimization:
The VAE was trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001.

After training, synthetic neurons were generated by sampling from the learned latent distribution and passing them through the decoder network:

X_{\mathrm{synthetic}}=D\left(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\right)

(13)

where
D
represents the trained decoder, and
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
is the standard normal distribution from which latent vectors were sampled.

2.4
Evaluation and Visualization of Synthetic Data

To comprehensively assess the fidelity and utility of the generated synthetic data, we employed both quantitative and qualitative evaluation strategies. For each neuron class, we computed the mean absolute error (MAE) and Euclidean distance between the mean feature vectors of real (train and test) and synthetic neurons. These metrics provided a measure of how closely synthetic data reproduced the average profiles and variability of real neurons.

To compare the global distribution of real and synthetic neurons, we projected all data onto the first two principal components. The PCA model was fit on real neurons (train + test), and the same transformation was then applied to synthetic data. For normalizing flow-based synthetic datasets, outlier samples—defined as those with feature values exceeding three standard deviations from the real data mean—were removed prior to projection, to prevent distortion of the visualization.

We generated heatmaps of mean feature profiles for each (class, origin) pair to visually assess similarities and differences between real and synthetic data across all features. Violin and strip plots were used to display the distribution of classification accuracies, train-test gaps, and other performance metrics across pipeline configurations and augmentation strategies. All figures and visualizations were generated using open-source Python libraries:
matplotlib
and
seaborn
. All scripts for evaluation and figure generation are available in the accompanying GitHub repository.

To benchmark the fidelity of synthetic neurons in a biologically meaningful context, we performed a comparative analysis between synthetic and real neuronal profiles using the following procedure. First, we calculated the MAE between the mean feature profile of each synthetic neuron class (generated by each method and sample size) and the corresponding mean profile of the real test data for that class. For each class (e-type or mee-type), this provided a measure of phenotypic distance between synthetic and real neurons.

To contextualize these synthetic-to-real distances, we estimated the natural biological variability by computing the MAE between the mean profiles of all pairs of real classes in the test set (i.e., “inter-class” distances). This provided a reference distribution representing the phenotypic diversity observed among naturally occurring neuronal types. For each generative method and sample size, the distribution of synthetic-to-real MAEs was statistically compared to the distribution of real inter-class MAEs using the Mann–Whitney
U
test (alternative hypothesis: the synthetic-to-real distances are not greater than real inter-class distances).

All analyses were conducted using Python (
pandas
,
numpy
,
scipy
), with outputs saved as CSV files for transparency and reproducibility. This approach enables a direct assessment of whether the phenotypic variability of synthetic neurons remains within the bounds of natural biological diversity, as recently recommended for synthetic data benchmarking in computational biology and neuroscience
[
35
]
.

Results

Before integrating synthetic data generation techniques, we conducted baseline classification experiments to evaluate the discriminative power of various feature representations for neuronal subtype identification. Neurons were categorized based on electrophysiological features (e-types) and combined morpho-electrophysiological features using a labeling scheme based solely on electrophysiological identity, denoted as mee-types. The datasets consisted of neuron profiles derived from the mouse visual cortex and adjacent higher-order visual areas, previously characterized through
in vitro
single-cell electrophysiological and morphological recordings, as described by Gouwens et al.
[
19
]
.

These classification tasks were designed to assess the effectiveness of different machine learning pipelines in capturing subtype-specific properties. The full analysis pipeline—from data preprocessing and feature rescaling, through dimensionality reduction and supervised classification—is summarized in Figure

Moreover, they served as a reference framework for evaluating the potential added value of synthetic data generation strategies introduced in subsequent phases of the study.

3.3
Qualitative Assessment of Augmentation Strategies via PCA Visualization

Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct patterns in how each augmentation method populates the feature space of real neurons (Figure
5
). For e-type neurons, GAN-generated samples closely matched the distribution and spread of real neurons, with synthetic data progressively occupying the real data manifold as sample size increased. This strong overlap, coupled with the absence of obvious artifacts, indicates that GANs can faithfully reproduce both the diversity and structure of biological neuron populations.

In contrast, SMOTE augmentation produced synthetic neurons that tended to align along linear or planar trajectories, connecting real samples. This effect became more pronounced with larger synthetic datasets, reflecting the interpolation-driven nature of SMOTE. While this can improve coverage of the feature space, it may also introduce artificial linear relationships not present in the biological data, potentially reducing biological realism.

Initial attempts to visualize Flow-based synthetic neurons alongside real data in the PCA space resulted in highly distorted plots, with the majority of synthetic points compressed and a small number of extreme outliers dominating the projection. To enable a meaningful comparison, we systematically removed outlier synthetic neurons—those with feature values far outside the distribution of the real data—prior to PCA visualization. After outlier exclusion, the remaining Flow-based synthetic neurons formed more compact, centered clusters with lower variance compared to real data and other augmentation methods. This persistent tight clustering suggests that the Flow-based model tends to generate less diverse, mode-seeking samples, and may have limited capacity to reproduce the full biological variability observed in real neurons. Nevertheless, the necessity of outlier removal highlights a challenge with the Flow-based approach: without filtering, the synthetic data distribution can become highly unrealistic and overwhelm global structure in dimensionality reduction analyses.

3.5

4
Discussion

A fundamental challenge in neuroscience is the comprehensive classification and understanding of neuronal cell types, an endeavor complicated by the limited availability and inherent heterogeneity of high-quality, annotated datasets
[
44
,
49
,
48
,
19
]
. In this work, we provide a benchmark of classical and deep generative data augmentation methods—including SMOTE, GANs, VAEs, Normalizing Flows, and DDPMs—evaluated in the context of supervised classification of both electrophysiological and morpho-electrophysiological neuron types from the mouse visual cortex
[
20
,
41
]
. Our results demonstrate that synthetic data augmentation pipelines can improve classification performance. Among all augmentation approaches tested, SMOTE-based pipelines yielded the highest overall classification accuracies for both e-type (up to 0.79) and mee-type neurons (up to 0.74), representing absolute gains of 0.16 and 0.12, respectively, over the best non-augmented baselines.

However, deep generative models such as GANs also achieved strong performance—for example, the best GAN-based pipeline reached
0.76\pm 0.01
(GAN_10000) for e-types and
0.69\pm 0.03
(GAN_100) for mee-types. Although these values did not surpass SMOTE in our benchmarks, they demonstrate that GANs can approach the effectiveness of classical oversampling, particularly when sufficient synthetic data are generated and model training is carefully tuned.

Overall, these findings suggest that, for structured biological data with moderate sample sizes and well-defined class boundaries, interpolation-based methods like SMOTE currently provide the most consistent improvements, but advanced generative models represent a viable alternative—especially as architectures and hyperparameter optimization continue to evolve
[
13
,
23
,
35
]
.

SMOTE generates synthetic samples by interpolating between existing, labeled data points in feature space, preserving class boundaries and avoiding out-of-distribution artifacts. While SMOTE and its variants have demonstrated robustness in various tabular and biomedical datasets
[
13
,
16
,
10
]
, and can be effective for moderately sized and well-separated classes, their relative performance compared to deep generative models remains context-dependent. In particular, for complex biological datasets such as transcriptomics or neuronal phenotyping, the superiority of SMOTE over GANs or VAEs has not been universally established, and recent benchmarks suggest that deep generative models may outperform classical oversampling in settings with high intrinsic biological variability or non-linear class boundaries
[
35
,
32
]
. In our own data, the observed performance of SMOTE likely reflects both the moderate sample size and the structure of neuronal classes, but further work is needed to understand the precise conditions under which deep generative models may surpass classical oversampling, particularly in more complex, high-variance, or rare-class scenarios. In contrast, deep generative models—such as GANs and VAEs—require larger datasets to avoid mode collapse and overfitting, and their performance is highly sensitive to architectural choices, learning rates, optimizer parameters, and loss balancing
[
35
,
3
,
8
,
17
]
. Suboptimal hyperparameter settings, limited data, and class imbalance can readily lead to poor convergence or biologically implausible samples. GANs, for example, are known to struggle in minority class regimes, and although advances such as conditional GANs (cGANs) or Wasserstein GANs (WGANs) show promise, careful tuning and larger sample sizes are usually necessary to reach optimal fidelity
[
35
,
3
,
9
]
. In this study, we deliberately chose standard architectures and reasonable parameters for all models to enable fair comparison, but acknowledge that further optimization or domain-specific regularization may improve results for future work.

The benefit of synthetic data was sharply reduced or eliminated when dimensionality reduction (e.g., LDA) was introduced prior to augmentation. This underscores that the power of synthetic data to improve classification is tightly linked to the preservation of native high-dimensional structure—an observation in line with recent findings in multi-omic data analysis and single-cell genomics, where high-dimensional representations retain subtle but informative biological variation
[
57
]
. It follows that future generative models must explicitly address the challenges posed by dimensionality reduction, and that researchers should tailor augmentation strategies to the complete analytical pipeline
[
11
]
.

In our study, we also showed that, across all generative methods and sample sizes, the average MAE between synthetic and real class mean profiles is not greater than the natural MAE observed between real class means. For e-type, the mean inter-class MAE among real neurons was 0.2477; synthetic-to-real MAEs for top-performing generative methods were consistently lower (e.g., DDPM_35: 0.1785; GAN_1000: 0.2012; VAE_35: 0.1853). Statistical analysis via the Mann–Whitney U test confirmed that, for all methods except SMOTE, synthetic-to-real distances were significantly less than real inter-class distances (
p<0.05
), while for SMOTE, synthetic neurons were as similar to their target class as the real classes are to each other (
p=0.15
–
0.32
). Analogous results were obtained for the more complex mee-type classification, with a real inter-class MAE of 0.3570 and synthetic-to-real MAEs for the best generative methods (e.g., DDPM_35: 0.2624; GAN_1000: 0.2819; VAE_35: 0.2845) falling within or below this range. These results confirm that, at the population level, synthetic neurons generated by current methods do not introduce artificial phenotypic diversity that exceeds the bounds of natural biological variability—a critical requirement for synthetic data intended for
in silico
experimentation or downstream biological analysis.

Our detailed feature- and class-level analyses reveal limitations. While most neuronal classes and features are faithfully reproduced, certain inhibitory types (e.g., Inh_10, Inh_7) and a subset of features (e.g., feature_35, feature_21) remain challenging for current generative models. Outlier detection and removal were particularly important for Flow-based synthetic datasets, as these models tended to generate small clusters of unrealistic neurons unless carefully filtered. Similar findings have been reported in deep generative modeling for single-cell transcriptomics, where rare subpopulations and outliers are difficult to reproduce without targeted strategies. These observations highlight that, even when global metrics are satisfactory, local failures may persist and require explicit methodological attention.

Moreover, qualitative visualization (e.g., via PCA) revealed method-dependent biases: SMOTE produced synthetic neurons aligned along linear trajectories between real samples, potentially introducing geometric artifacts, while GANs more accurately filled the real data manifold, and Flow-based methods generated compact but less diverse clusters. These findings emphasize the importance of not only quantitative but also qualitative assessment when deploying synthetic data for biological discovery. The demonstrated benefit of SMOTE—and the systematic benchmarking of deep generative models—offers actionable guidelines for researchers aiming to overcome sample size limitations in other brain regions, cell types, or even modalities such as transcriptomics or multi-omics integration. Nonetheless, our results also highlight the need for next-generation generative models capable of explicitly controlling for biological priors, addressing class imbalance, and faithfully capturing rare or outlier subpopulations. Promising directions include hyperparameter optimization, conditional and semi-supervised generative models, and the integration of transcriptomic or connectivity constraints into generative framework.

5
Conclusion

In summary, we present a benchmark of classical and deep generative data augmentation techniques for the classification of neuronal subtypes using both electrophysiological and morpho-electrophysiological features from the mouse visual cortex. Our results showed that while SMOTE-based augmentation currently offers the most consistent performance improvements in structured and moderately sized datasets, deep generative models such as GANs are approaching similar efficacy, especially with careful hyperparameter tuning and increased data availability. Importantly, we introduce a validation framework—quantifying synthetic-to-real variability against natural phenotypic diversity—that establishes rigorous criteria for assessing the fidelity of synthetic neuronal data. This approach ensures that augmented datasets not only boost classifier performance but also faithfully recapitulate the complex diversity of real neurons, which is essential for downstream biological applications. The integration of advanced generative methods and standardized validation will be pivotal for unlocking the full potential of synthetic data in neuroscience, enabling scalable cell type classification, more robust in silico experimentation, and ultimately, a deeper understanding of brain complexity and function.
