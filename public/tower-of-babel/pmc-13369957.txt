Cervi-LLM for real time colposcopy lesion detection and interpretable diagnosis
Authors: Zhijun Hu, Ling Ma, Suizhi Huang, Yingting Wei, Liying Gu, Zubei Hong, Yue Ding, Anyue Wu, Wen Di, Hongtao Lu, Lihua Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Colposcopy, a key procedure in cervical cancer screening for patients with abnormal TCT or HPV test results, requires extensive training for accurate diagnosis, yet diagnostic performance varies significantly across regions. This study presents Cervi-LLM, a multimodal MoE framework designed to assist in colposcopic detection by providing precise lesion localization and stratified diagnoses. The dataset included 126 cervical cancer, 692 HSIL, 306 LSIL, and 999 normal cases. Cervi-LLM integrates three core components: 1) YOLOMed for real-time, multi-scale segmentation of three staining modalities (saline, acetic acid, iodine); 2) joint fine-tuning of a LLM on both image features and clinical text using LoRA and progressive unfreezing; and 3) a two-level MoE architecture that fuses rule-based and data-driven expert outputs via dynamic gating, calibrated with clinical rules to deliver interpretable diagnoses (Normal/LSIL/HSIL( +)). In segmentation, Cervi-LLM achieved PA of 94.51% and meanIoU of 78.23%, outperforming U-Net (91.83%, 72.76%) and Polyp-PVT (93.69%, 75.34%). For three-class classification, it reached an overall ACC of 91.52%, exceeding junior physicians (65.88%) and senior physicians (76.57%). In detecting HSIL( +), it achieved sensitivity of 95.96% and specificity of 94.17%, compared to 67.58% and 81.67% for the senior physicians. The system processes images at approximately 30 fps and delivers a final diagnosis in 0.30 ± 0.05 min. Cervi-LLM effectively overcomes conventional colposcopy limitations, offering an accurate, real-time intelligent tool for cervical lesion screening and biopsy guidance.

Introduction

Cervical cancer ranks as the fourth most common malignant tumor among women. According to statistics, there were 604,127 new cases of cervical cancer worldwide in 20201, with China accounting for 20% of these cases. Fortunately, the cervix is an easily accessible organ for clinical examination, which significantly enhances the feasibility of cervical cancer screening. It is precisely the widespread implementation of cervical cancer screening that has paradoxically led to an observed increase in incidence rates and a trend toward affecting younger demographics2.

Cervical cancer screening follows a three-tiered approach: TCT and HPV testing, colposcopy, and cervical biopsy with histopathological examination. Colposcopy, performed on patients with abnormal TCT or HPV results, constitutes a critical step in cervical cancer screening. Under enhanced illumination, colposcopy magnifies the epithelial and vascular structures of the cervix, vagina, and vulva by 3.75 to 30 times, enabling clinicians to visually identify even subtle lesions3. In cases of cervical SIL, these are characterized by abnormal epithelial cell proliferation and nuclear enlargement. Upon application of acetic acid solution, reversible protein coagulation occurs within the cells, leading to reduced epithelial transparency. The diagnosis of cervical lesions through colposcopy relies on the interpretation of images. The severity of SIL correlates with the thickness and clarity of the acetowhite epithelium observed. This also forms the basis for assessing the severity of lesions during colposcopy. The training of skilled colposcopists requires extensive practice, and there are significant regional variations in diagnostic accuracy.

AI has been extensively investigated in medical image analysis: deep learning approaches have markedly enhanced diagnostic performance in modalities such as CT and MRI4,5. Research dedicated specifically to colposcopy image analysis has also progressed. Li et al. developed a deep learning framework using time-lapsed colposcopy images to classify LSIL, HSIL, and cervical cancer with 78.33% accuracy6; Yue proposed a computer-aided algorithm for automatic segmentation of acetowhite lesion regions, significantly improving Dice similarity coefficients and Hausdorff distance metrics7. Liu constructed an end-to-end convolutional network pipeline to automatically identify and classify lesions under saline, acetic acid, and iodine staining conditions8; Peng further introduced a multimodal fusion network that jointly processes white-light, acetic acid, and iodine images to boost HSIL/HSIL + detection accuracy, yielding an approximately 8% increase in Dice score9. However, most existing methods remain confined to offline analysis and lack real-time lesion annotation and biopsy guidance capabilities. Real-time visualization of lesions not only provides precise biopsy targets but also informs laser therapy planning, thereby improving the clinical management of LSIL.

This study introduces Cervi-LLM, an intelligent diagnostic framework based on a multimodal MoE architecture, designed to enhance colposcopy detection by improving diagnostic accuracy, enabling precise lesion localization, facilitating disease stratification, and offering real-time biopsy guidance.

Discussion

Colposcopy diagnosis is a critical yet subjective step in cervical cancer screening. However, its diagnostic performance is inherently limited by subjectivity and operator dependence. Diagnostic accuracy relies on the colposcopist’s visual interpretation to guide biopsy site selection, resulting in reported sensitivities ranging from 44 to 77%. This variability is largely attributable to differences in clinical experience, training, and real-time decision-making. In addition, regional disparities in healthcare resources further contribute to inconsistent diagnostic quality. These limitations highlight the need for assistive technologies capable of providing accurate, objective, and real-time lesion assessment to support clinical decision-making during colposcopy.

Colposcopy diagnosis requires the accurate identification of images. In medical image analysis, image segmentation has been extensively investigated, with the U-Net architecture proposed by Ronneberger becoming a standard due to its efficient skip-connection design10. Building upon U-Net, advanced models such as Zhang et al.’s BUC-Net have further improved segmentation efficiency, enabling automatic segmentation and refinement of brain metastases within 10 min—tasks that traditionally require 3–6 h of manual effort by radiation oncologists11. In parallel, Mask R-CNN, introduced by He et al., unified two-stage object detection and segmentation, achieving strong accuracy and generalizability12. Unlike CT or MRI, where image interpretation is typically performed after acquisition, colposcopy requires real-time image processing to identify suspicious regions during examination and guide biopsy or treatment decisions. Since the introduction of YOLOv1 in 2015, the YOLO series has continuously advanced the balance between speed and accuracy for real-time applications13. Keerthana et al. reported an AI-assisted real-time colposcopy system achieving accuracies of 86.67% for iodine-negative regions and 85.71% for acetowhite detection14.In this study, our in-house YOLOMed model enables real-time lesion detection and segmentation during colposcopy, immediately annotating and localizing lesions upon detection to provide precise guidance for biopsy sampling and diagnostic decision-making. Accurate recognition of abnormalities across three staining modalities substantially improves procedural precision and clinical efficiency.

Colposcopic diagnosis is not solely based on image recognition but also requires integration of HPV testing and cervical cytology results; therefore, accurate understanding of clinical text information is equally critical for improving diagnostic performance. In recent years, multimodal fusion models, such as BLIP-215 and MedViL16, have begun exploring joint modeling of visual and textual information, establishing new paradigms for medical image report generation and diagnostic assistance. The role of LLMs in medical image interpretation and clinical reasoning has become increasingly prominent, particularly in radiology. Bhayana et al. evaluated the performance of GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 in generating structured summaries and determining surgical resectability from pancreatic cancer imaging reports, demonstrating that GPT-4 significantly outperformed earlier models in producing accurate summaries and reliable classifications, especially when sequential reasoning prompts were employed, thereby enhancing clinical decision efficiency17. Despite these advances, the application of LLMs in colposcopy remains limited. In this study, Cervi-LLM adopts DeepSeek-R1-32B as the backbone LLM, primarily due to its strong performance in biomedical applications, particularly in clinical text generation and medical image interpretation. While this model demonstrated promising results, it is important to note that other general or biomedical LLMs, such as GPT-3, GPT-4, or BioBERT, may offer complementary strengths. Future work will therefore include systematic benchmarking against these models to further validate the effectiveness of Cervi-LLM in cervical lesion diagnosis and multimodal fusion tasks.

A key methodological innovation of this study lies in the introduction of a two-level MoE architecture. At the diagnostic classification stage, we integrate a pretrained LLM with the MoE framework to fuse image-derived features and clinical text information, enabling automated classification of cervical lesions into Normal, LSIL, and HSIL( +) categories. The proposed system has been validated on internal multimodal dataset, demonstrating favorable accuracy and practical utility across multiple diagnostic tasks. By design, the MoE architecture allows the model to efficiently integrate multiple specialized submodels, each with clearly defined expertise, thereby facilitating the simultaneous processing of image and textual modalities. To reduce the deployment overhead of large models, lightweight fine-tuning techniques like LoRA18 have been widely adopted, which significantly decrease the number of trainable parameters by adjusting only low-rank matrices while preserving the representation capacity of the backbone. These methods have demonstrated high efficiency and scalability in multiple clinical applications.

While Cervi-LLM has been designed to work with frame-based inputs, it can also handle video recordings in real-time. The integration of real-time processing with the YOLOMed framework allows for continuous lesion detection and annotation during the colposcopy procedure, providing instant feedback to clinicians. This real-time capability fills a critical void in current research, as no prior studies have combined large pretrained biomedical language models with real-time colposcopy image analysis. Cervi-LLM, for the first time, integrates a pretrained large language model with our in-house YOLOMed image encoder within a two-level MoE framework via a three-stage LoRA fine-tuning strategy. This design not only achieves deep fusion of multimodal features but also enables real-time lesion annotation and interpretable diagnosis, addressing the critical need for both accurate diagnosis and real-time clinical support in colposcopy.

During Cervi-LLM evaluation, we emphasized model robustness and overfitting control through cross-validation, regularization, and early stopping, achieving stable performance on the internal dataset. Nevertheless, the single-center data may introduce domain shifts related to patient characteristics, imaging devices, and clinical workflows, limiting generalizability and underscoring the need for multicenter external validation. Remaining challenges include class imbalance due to the low prevalence of HSIL and the independent modeling of multiple images from the same examination. In the future, Cervi-LLM may improve diagnostic consistency by incorporating multicenter, multi-source data and multi-view information fusion, while model compression and inference optimization could facilitate clinical deployment.

Materials and methods

This study retrospectively analyzed patients who underwent colposcopy at the Cervical Outpatient Clinic of Renji Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, from January 2018 to September 2022. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) availability of clear saline, 3% acetic acid, and iodine images; (2) availability of HPV and TCT test results or DNA ploidy analysis results; (3) availability of colposcopy diagnostic results; (4) availability of pathological examination results. The exclusion criteria included patients who declined to participate in the study. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee (KY2023-009-B). Written informed consent was obtained from all enrolled participants. The colposcopy device used was LEISEGANG 3ML LED. During colposcopic examinations, both junior and senior physicians were provided with the patient’s age, HPV test results, and TCT results. Based on the colposcopic images, they independently evaluated each case and classified cervical findings as normal cervix, LSIL, HSIL, or cervical cancer according to their clinical judgment. The diagnostic performance of junior and senior colposcopists was subsequently assessed using histopathological biopsy results as the gold standard. The study was conducted by four experienced colposcopy physicians (ZB.H., LY. G., AY. W., ZJ. H.), who utilized Labelme software to annotate cervical lesion areas.

Study framework

The clinical text vectors consist of four clinical indicators—HPV test results, TCT test results, transformation zone coefficient, and age. The image vectors are extracted by the YOLOMed model and include lesion candidate localization features, lesion grade annotation features, as well as region area and depth measurements, capturing both spatial and semantic information of the lesions. YOLOMed

In the Cervi-LLM framework, the YOLOMed model20 extracts multi-scale, multi-task features from colposcopy images and comprises three main components: an encoder, task-specific decoders, and a Cross-Scale Task-Interaction Module (Fig. 5). The encoder uses CSPDarknet5321 as its backbone to efficiently propagate and reuse features while minimizing parameters for real-time performance. Its neck integrates multi-scale features via SPP22 followed by a FPN23 enhanced with CBAM channel attention24, boosting sensitivity to small, low-contrast cervical lesions. The decoders consist of a detection branch and a segmentation branch. The detection decoder employs a decoupled head design combined with a PAN25 to fuse low- level and high-level semantics, separating classification and bounding-box regression to reduce inter-task conflict. The segmentation decoder uses a top-down up sampling strategy over FPN features to produce high-resolution semantic masks. The Cross-Scale Task-Interaction Module fuses detection and segmentation features across scales using a Transformer. Image feature extraction

The candidate lesion location features refer to the coordinates of the bounding box that surrounds the lesion area, which are extracted by the detection head of the YOLOMed model to mark the location of the lesion. The bounding box coordinates include the x and y coordinates of the top-left corner of the lesion region, as well as the width and height, allowing for precise localization. Next, the lesion grade annotation features indicate the severity of the lesion, such as Normal, LSIL, HSIL, cancer. This feature is determined by jointly analyzing lesion morphological characteristics—including cervical vascular pattern alterations, the color, margins, contour, extent, duration, and anatomical distribution of acetowhite epithelium, as well as iodine staining patterns—in combination with the segmentation mask outputs and corresponding diagnostic labels. Finally, the region depth is measured. Region depth measurement reflects the spatial extent of the lesion within the image plane rather than true three-dimensional tissue thickness. This measurement is estimated from the segmentation output, where depth-related features are quantified using segmentation-derived metrics, including lesion area (pixel count), relative coverage within the detected bounding box, and the horizontal and vertical span of the segmented region. These metrics, collectively approximate lesion extent and severity in two-dimensional space and are used as surrogate markers of lesion involvement in cervical tissue.

Ensuring reliability in text generation

To ensure the reliability of the clinical text generated by the model, we applied prompting constraints and rule-based filters to ensure the generated text adheres strictly to medical logic and established clinical guidelines. In addition, clinician oversight was implemented at multiple stages to validate the model’s output. All generated content is reviewed by medical experts to confirm its accuracy before being used for decision-making.

Medical logic experts

In the Cervi-LLM framework, the Medical Logic Experts perform diagnostic reasoning by learning from cervical disease clinical guidelines and analyzing the relationships between clinical indicators such as HPV test results, TCT test results, and transformation zone type. In the diagnostic process, the Medical Logic Experts assign weights based on different infection types and the severity of lesions. HPV types 16 and 18 are considered the primary causative types for cervical cancer, so patients infected with these types of HPV are given higher weight. Other high-risk HPV types have a lower risk but still contribute to cervical lesions, so their weight is secondary. Next, for TCT test results, if the result shows HSIL, it indicates more severe cervical cell abnormalities, and a higher weight is assigned; while LSIL results indicate a lighter lesion, and thus, a lower weight is given. Model training

This study employs Deepseek-R1-32B as the backbone LLM27. Building on this, we apply LoRA lightweight adapters and a three-stage progressive unfreezing strategy to fine-tune the model on our mixed colposcopy image and clinical text dataset, thereby optimizing it for cervical lesion diagnosis. The three-stage progressive unfreezing strategy ensures stable multimodal fusion by gradually unfreezing the model components. In the first stage, we unfreeze the image feature extractor to allow the model to focus on learning image features without interference from text data. In the second stage, the text feature extractor is unfreezed to learn text-based features. Finally, in the third stage, the entire model is unfrozen to enable effective fusion of image and text features for diagnostic classification.

For image feature extraction, YOLOMed uniformly processes three colposcopy staining modalities (saline, acetic acid, and iodine images). All images are passed through a CSPDarknet53 + CBAM-FPN backbone to extract multi-scale features, which are then routed through a multi-task detection decoder (Decoupled Detection Head + PAN) and a segmentation decoder (Segmentation Head) to produce localization and semantic segmentation tokens, ultimately generating the image feature vectors. The experiments were conducted on a Linux-based system with RTX 6000 Ada 2 GPUs, providing the computational resources necessary for handling large datasets and the intensive model training required for both image and text processing.

Cervi-LLM adopts a two-level MoE architecture to finely integrate rule-based medical logic with data-driven inference. Structured clinical vectors and image vectors are first projected into a shared high-dimensional embedding space, then processed in parallel by two expert groups: the Medical Logic Experts (HPV test results, TCT test results, and Transformation zone type integration) and the Inference Experts (High-Dimensional Interaction Transformer and CrossModal Attention Expert). Each group’s outputs are combined via an intra-group gating network to produce two streams of logits—medical logic and data-driven—then dynamically balanced by an inter-group gating mechanism to yield the final prediction. A Clinical Rule Calibration Module subsequently applies soft boosts to HSIL and LSIL logits to better align with real-world diagnostic criteria. Training follows a three-stage progressive unfreezing strategy: in the first stage, only the three medical logic experts are unfrozen for warm-up while all other parameters remain frozen; in the second stage, all medical logic experts and the backbone projection layers are frozen, and only the inference experts are fine-tuned to improve multimodal fusion, in the final stage, all expert modules and the gating networks are unfrozen for end-to-end joint optimization. All stages use the Adam optimizer (learning rate 1e-4) for 1000 epochs, with performance monitored on the validation set.

Baseline models and metrics

The study provides a comprehensive evaluation of Cervi-LLM across multimodal diagnostic tasks. For object detection, we compare against biomedical models RetinaNet28 and YOLOv5s29, as well as a single-task detection baseline comprising only the encoder and detection head, using mAP@50% IoU and mAP@95% IoU as metrics. For semantic segmentation, we benchmark against the classic U-Net, the transformer-enhanced Polyp-PVT30, and a single-task segmentation baseline with only the encoder and segmentation head, evaluating performance via PA, meanIoU, DSC, HD, and NSD. In the classification task, we include Logic-Only (medical logic experts only), YOLOMed-Only (image transformer expert only), and additional classification baseline models, assessing results with overall ACC and AUC.
