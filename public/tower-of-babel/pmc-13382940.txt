Biopsies Can Predict Lung Adenocarcinoma IASLC Grade: A New Proposal and Grading Pitfalls
Authors: Fang Zhou, Atreyee Basu, Jose G. Mantilla, Navneet Narula, Andre L. Moreira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer grading system for resected invasive lung adenocarcinoma provides robust prognostic stratification for recurrence risk and overall survival. However, most patients present at advanced stages, where only small biopsy samples are available, leaving no established prognostic tool for such cases. We propose a practical biopsy-based grading method for pulmonary adenocarcinoma.MethodsWe analyzed 267 paired biopsies and resections. The following two biopsy grading (bxG) models were tested:•Model 1: Based on the predominant pattern—grade 1 (lepidic), grade 2 (acinar/papillary), grade 3 (solid/micropapillary/complex glandular).•Model 2: Any percentage of high-grade patterns classified as grade 3, regardless of predominance. BxG 1 and 2 are defined as predominant patterns as described.For analysis, grades 1 and 2 were combined (G1–2), and grade 3 was considered a separate category. Diagnostic performance was assessed using resections as the reference standard. Interobserver reliability was evaluated, and discrepant cases were reviewed.ResultsModel 1 achieved 79.8% accuracy (sensitivity 57%, specificity 98.6%, positive predictive value [PPV] 97.2%, negative predictive value 73.5%, area under the curve 0.78). Model 2 performed better, with 88.4% accuracy (sensitivity 83.5%, specificity 92.5%, PPV 90.2%, negative predictive value 87.1%, area under the curve 0.89), and was used for further analysis. Discrepancies (n = 21) were primarily due to sampling limitations and artifacts, such as tangential sectioning. Adjusting for these factors improved interobserver agreement from moderate to almost perfect.ConclusionBiopsy grading using the presence of any high-grade pattern correlates well with International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer–based resection grading. Although bxG1 to 2 biopsies may underestimate tumor grade, bxG3 biopsies demonstrate more than 90% specificity and PPV for predicting G3 resections, supporting their potential utility as a clinical prognostic marker.

Introduction

Tumor grading is a critical component of the pathologic evaluation of lung adenocarcinoma. Early studies established that the predominant histologic patterns correlated with the risk of recurrence in stage I tumors: solid and micropapillary-predominant tumors had the highest rate of recurrence and were therefore designated as high-grade patterns, followed by acinar and papillary patterns with intermediate risk, and, finally, lepidic-predominant tumors with the lowest risk of recurrence (low-grade pattern).1 This approach to classifying tumors by the predominant pattern also predicted the response to cisplatin-based adjuvant chemotherapy.2 In addition to the five traditional patterns of pulmonary adenocarcinoma recognized by the WHO, tumors predominantly composed of poorly formed glands were also recognized as having risks of recurrence similar to those of solid and micropapillary lesions; this pattern is now termed the complex glandular pattern.3, 4, 5

In 2020, the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) Pathology Committee introduced a standardized grading system for resected adenocarcinomas, consisting of a three-tier system (G1: well differentiated, G2: moderately differentiated, G3: poorly differentiated).6 This system uses the predominant pattern but classifies tumors with more than or equal to 20% high-grade components (solid, micropapillary, or complex glands) as G3, even if it is not the predominant pattern, acknowledging their prognostic significance. Multiple studies have validated and confirmed its clinical utility as a prognostic marker.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 Emerging data support the prognostic value of the IASLC grading system during the evaluation of frozen sections, including the association of tumor grade with PD-L1 expression and molecular subgroups.15, 16, 17 Digital spatial profiling has been found to further stratify the risk of recurrence in grade 3 adenocarcinomas.18

The use of the IASLC grading system for the evaluation of resected tumors post-neoadjuvant therapy has also been found to enhance prognostic value in patients with residual viable tumor.19,20 IASLC high-grade histology has been suggested for use in selecting patients for adjuvant therapy in early stage 1A and 1B patients.14,21, 22, 23 A retrospective study evaluating IASLC grade in adjuvant therapy revealed that stage 1B patients with grade 3 adenocarcinoma had a lower risk of recurrence after adjuvant therapy and a better response to therapy than patients with grade 2.21 A prior study of 222 cases revealed that applying IASLC grading criteria to preoperative biopsies achieved 82% concordance with resection specimens, with higher agreement for grades 1 and 3 compared with grade 2.24

Grading biopsy specimens remains an unmet need, particularly for advanced-stage patients who are not surgical candidates. Biopsies offer technical advantages than frozen sections, including better diagnostic tissue quality and allowing for more accurate pattern recognition, and they can provide early risk stratification before surgery or systemic therapy.

Our study evaluates the feasibility of a modified, practical biopsy grading approach for lung adenocarcinoma and has the potential to serve as a standardized tool for future prospective studies evaluating the role of histologic markers in response to therapy.

Methods

Case Selection and Specimen Characteristics

We conducted a single-institution retrospective study of paired preoperative biopsies and subsequent resections of pulmonary nonmucinous adenocarcinoma that spanned 5 years. Approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of New York University Grossman School of Medicine.

Pulmonary biopsy procedures included the following: computed tomography (CT)–guided transthoracic needle biopsy using 19-gauge or 20-gauge needles; bronchoscopic endobronchial or transbronchial biopsies using 21-gauge to 22-gauge needles; and bronchoscopic forceps biopsies.25 The bronchoscopic biopsies were performed with fluoroscopic and/or ultrasound guidance. For the latter, samples were collected using the fanning method to sample different areas of the lymph node. Tissue fragments were fixed directly in buffered formalin and processed in a paper biopsy bag. For biopsies, three levels from each block were routinely cut and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE). All stained slides were evaluated. In contrast, one HE-stained slide was evaluated from each block in the paired resected specimens. Slides from all submitted blocks from the resected tumors were evaluated. Comprehensive histologic assessment was performed for the resected tumors, evaluating the presence and percentage of the following histologic patterns: lepidic, acinar, papillary, solid, micropapillary, and complex glands (cribriform and fused glands), adding up to 100%. For biopsies, the presence of each histologic pattern was also recorded.

Grading Criteria

For each biopsy, a grade was assigned using the following grading criteria (bxGrade).

Model #1: Grade was assigned based on the predominant histologic pattern.

G1: lepidic predominant; G2: acinar or papillary predominant; and G3: solid, micropapillary, or complex gland predominant.

Model #2: Any amount of high-grade (HG) patterns was assigned as grade 3.

bxG1 was lepidic predominant with no HG pattern; bxG2 was acinar or papillary predominant with no HG pattern (Fig. 1).Figure 1Examples of different patterns of lung adenocarcinoma, HE. For the resections, the IASLC grading criteria were applied: G1 (well differentiated) was lepidic predominant with less than 20% HG pattern; G2 (moderately differentiated) was acinar or papillary predominant with less than 20% HG pattern; and G3 (poorly differentiated) was any tumor with 20% or more HG pattern.

Nuclear Grade

The utility of qualitative nuclear grading was analyzed by modifying biopsy grades according to nuclear grades. High nuclear grade was defined as follows: nuclear size larger than that of five lymphocytes; nuclear pleomorphism; and dense chromatin. Low-to-moderate nuclear grade was defined as follows: nuclear size up to that of five lymphocytes; relatively monotonous nuclei; and open chromatin. If a sample was heterogeneous, a higher grade was recorded. If a high nuclear grade was recorded, the original grade based on histologic pattern was increased to the next grade, or the grade was decreased if a low nuclear grade was assigned.

Statistical Analysis of Diagnostic Test Performance Characteristics for “bxG3”

For statistical analysis, the categories of grades 1 and 2 either in biopsy or in resected specimens were combined and considered as “negative for G3” and G3 either in biopsy or resection was considered as “positive for G3.” Using these two diagnostic categories, diagnostic test performance characteristics were calculated using biopsies as the test group and resections as the reference or standard group. The diagnostic test performance characteristics calculated included sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), accuracy, and area under the curve (AUC).26 The AUC was calculated using SPSS (IBM version 29.0.2.0 20). All other diagnostic test performance characteristics were calculated in Microsoft Excel.

Interobserver Reliability Analyses

For interobserver reliability analysis, 36 biopsies, evenly distributed among all three bxGrades, were randomly chosen. Four lung pathologists (FZ, NN, JGM, ALM) reviewed all slides and assigned a grade to each biopsy. Interobserver reliability was assessed using percent agreement and Gwet’s AC1 coefficient (a chance-corrected agreement coefficient), which were calculated in AgreeStat 2015.6.2/Windows (AgreeStat Analytics at http://agreestat.com/). Interpretation of the percent agreement and Gwet’s AC1 coefficient was based on the categories put forth by Landis and Koch in 1977 (<0.00: poor; 0.00–0.20: slight; 0.21–0.40: fair; 0.41–0.60: moderate; 0.61–0.80: substantial; and 0.81–1.00: almost perfect) considering only the raters as a source of variation.27 Cases with major disagreements were reviewed by all observers at a consensus meeting and assigned new grades, after which the interobserver reliability statistics were recalculated.

The concordance of the inclusion of nuclear grade in the biopsy with resection specimens was assessed for each pathologist using two methods based on high nuclear grade: first, upgrading only bxG1 to bxG2 and bxG2 to bxG3; second, upgrading any bxG1 to 2 to bxG3.

Results

Demographics

A total of 267 pairs of biopsies and resections were identified. Of the patients, 162 (61%) were women, whereas 105 (39%) were men. The mean and median age was 71 years (range 40–96; SD = 8.9).

Discrepant Grades on Paired Biopsies and Resections

Discrepant grades were observed in 31 (11.6%) of 267 paired cases. Of the 31 discrepancies, 20 (65%) were bxG1 to 2 on biopsy but G3 in the corresponding resection. Seven of these undercalls were bronchoscopic biopsies, whereas 13 were CT guided. Although these 20 cases represented 65% of the discrepancies, they accounted for only 7.5% of all cases. They were caused by the unavoidable issue of intratumoral heterogeneity and limited sampling. Most of these resections had a 20% to 40% HG pattern, and review of the discrepant biopsies confirmed that none of them had any conspicuous HG pattern. Despite this limitation, 135 of 155 bxG1 to 2 on biopsy remained G1 to 2 on resection (NPV 87.1%), and 135 of 146 G1 to 2 on resection were graded as bxG1 to 2 on biopsy (sensitivity 83.5%).

In the remaining 11 (35%) of the 31 discrepancies, G3 was assigned on the biopsy, whereas the resection was considered G2. Five were bronchoscopic biopsies, whereas six were CT guided. In eight (73% of 11) of these overcalls, an HG pattern was present in the resection, but the amount was less than 20%, leading to a final classification of G2 rather than G3. In the remaining three bxG3 biopsies that demonstrated no HG pattern on resection, on review, it was evident that the biopsies were too fragmented for confident pattern recognition (Fig. 2A and B). All three were obtained bronchoscopically. Interobserver Reliability Analyses of Biopsy Grading

For each bxGrade, 12 biopsies were randomly chosen for interobserver reliability analysis (total 36 biopsies). Furthermore, 19 were bronchoscopic and 17 were CT guided. They were assigned bxGrades by four pathologists, who were given instructions to carefully review every slide from each biopsy. The rate of agreement was 0.80 (substantial) (95% confidence interval: 0.71–0.89), and Gwet’s AC1 coefficient was 0.60 (moderate) (95% confidence interval: 0.42–0.79).

Moreover, seven cases underwent consensus review due to major interobserver discrepancies (bxG1 versus bxG3). Of these, five (71%) were bronchoscopic biopsies and two were CT guided. During consensus review, it was found that all the major discrepancies were due to overcalls based on limited evidence of high-grade patterns. Thus, it was decided that if any apparent high-grade pattern was found only in one level, it was insufficient for assigning bxG3. After consensus review and revision of the overcalled biopsies, the rate of agreement increased to 0.91 (almost perfect agreement) (95% confidence interval: 0.78–0.94), and the Gwet’s AC1 coefficient was 0.83 (almost perfect agreement) (95% confidence interval: 0.69–0.96). In addition, 35 of the 36 cases had majority consensus (at least three of four observers assigned the same grade).

Evaluating the Utility of Nuclear Grading

Using the subset of biopsies used in the interobserver reliability analysis, the addition of nuclear grade was evaluated by three pathologists. Two scenarios were evaluated. BxG1 was upgraded to bxG2, and bxG2 was upgraded to bxG3, or any bxG1 to 2 was upgraded to bxG3 based on the presence of high nuclear grade. Cases with high-grade histologic patterns were to be downgraded to bxG1 to 2 if low nuclear grades were assigned.

The addition of nuclear grade modified the histologic grade assigned to the biopsy in only six of 36 cases. Five cases were upgraded, and one case was downgraded. However, in only three of these six cases, the addition of nuclear grade resulted in better correlation with the resection grade (Supplementary Table 1).

The original concordance rate between biopsy grade and resection grade for these 36 pairs ranged from 78% to 81% among the different observers. The addition of nuclear grade did not improve the concordance rate (range: 75%–78%).

Furthermore, Gwet’s AC1 coefficient among the three pathologists decreased from 0.78 (almost perfect agreement) (95% confidence interval: 0.64–0.92) to 0.65 (moderate agreement) (95% confidence interval: 0.44–0.85), suggesting that the reproducibility of nuclear grading may be worse than that of pattern-based grading alone.

Discussion

In our study, we propose a novel, practical grading system for biopsy specimens of lung adenocarcinoma, in which the presence of any HG pattern in the biopsy is assigned grade 3 (bxG3). It is well known that lung adenocarcinomas are architecturally heterogeneous and that sampling limitations are inherent to small biopsies of such tumors.28 Even when HG components are a minority (20%) in the resection, they predict aggressive behavior. Thus, the presence of HG patterns in any amount in a small biopsy sample suggests a high likelihood that the resected tumor will also be classified as G3. In fact, our results revealed that 90.2% of tumors with bxG3 on biopsy were also G3 on resection (PPV), and 92.5% of G3 resections demonstrated bxG3 on biopsy (specificity).

A key lesson learned from our study is that, although it is feasible to grade lung adenocarcinoma on small biopsies, the grading should be performed cautiously and only when multiple levels are available to confirm the true presence of an HG pattern (in contrast to an artifact). In our study, interobserver reliability increased from “moderate” to “almost perfect agreement” when these principles were applied by all observers. When HG patterns appear only focally or appear in the same vicinity as low- or intermediate-grade patterns, the slides should be evaluated carefully for the possibility of tumor cell discohesion and tangential sectioning, which can mimic an HG pattern, such as micropapillary pattern.

Our analysis of grade modification according to nuclear grade and of a second grading method using predominant pattern only indicated that they were inferior as biopsy grading methods. These results suggest that architectural patterns are more important than nuclear morphology and that the presence of any high-grade pattern on biopsy is important to account for in the biopsy grade.

However, despite these lessons being learned, some overcalls on biopsies are unavoidable due to sampling issues. Other factors, such as radiographic correlations to enhance the prediction of IASLC grade,29 radiomics including positron emission tomography–CT features, or the application of artificial intelligence, have been explored as adjuncts to the IASLC grading system30 and may offer additional prognostic information.

A standardized grading system for biopsies of adenocarcinoma offers a tool that may facilitate studies investigating the utility of this biopsy grading system in therapeutic decision making.
