Predicting Outcomes of Traumatic Brain Injury Using Machine Learning Models Among Patients at Kilimanjaro Christian Medical Centre, Tanzania: A Registry‐Based Cohort Study
Authors: William Nkenguye, Edwin Joseph Shewiyo, João Vitor Perez De Souza, Timothy Antipas Peter, Alice Andongolile, Rosalia Njau, Baraka Moshi, Johnston George, Paige O'Leary, Catherine Staton, Blandina T. Mmbaga, Francis M. Sakita, João Ricardo Nickenig Vissoci, Innocent B. Mboya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTraumatic brain injury (TBI) remains a major global health burden, disproportionately affecting low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) where access to neurocritical care is limited. Accurate and context‐appropriate prognostic models are crucial to guide early clinical decision‐making and optimize resource allocation in such settings. This study aims to develop and evaluate machine learning (ML) models for predicting TBI outcomes among adult patients using trauma registry data from Kilimanjaro Christian Medical Centre (KCMC), Tanzania.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study utilized data from 4596 adult TBI patients recorded in the KCMC trauma registry between 2013 and 2024. The outcome was dichotomized Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS): poor (1–3) versus good (4–5). Ten supervised ML algorithms, including Random Forest (RF), Decision Tree (DT), Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), were trained on 70% of the data after applying multiple imputation and synthetic minority Over‐sampling Technique (SMOTE) to address missingness and class imbalance. Hyperparameter tuning was performed using 10‐fold cross‐validation. Model performance was assessed on a 30% test set using area under the ROC curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values.ResultsAmong the 4596 patients, 26.2% had poor outcomes. The RF and DT models achieved the highest AUCs of 0.83 and 0.82, respectively. RF also showed the highest accuracy (0.78) and strong positive predictive value (PPV = 0.87), while DT had the highest sensitivity for poor outcomes (84.5%). Predictors of poor outcomes included TBI severity, pupil non‐reactivity, low oxygen saturation, lack of CT scan, alcohol use, and abnormal vital signs.ConclusionML models, particularly RF and DT, demonstrated strong predictive performance for TBI outcomes using routinely collected variables in a resource‐limited LMIC setting. Their interpretability and reliance on admission‐level data make them potential tools for real‐time triage and risk stratification. Future research should focus on external validation and integration into clinical decision‐support systems to support scaleup across similar settings.

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a leading cause of death and disability globally, affecting an estimated 69 million people each year [1, 2]. While early diagnosis and timely surgical intervention are associated with improved outcomes [3, 4], most TBI‐related mortality occurs in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), where access to specialized neurotrauma care remains severely limited [2, 5]. Sub‐Saharan Africa, in particular, experiences a disproportionately high burden of TBI due to increasing rates of road traffic injuries, falls, and interpersonal violence, compounded by fragile emergency care systems and inadequate neurosurgical capacity [6, 7].

Structural barriers in LMIC health systems, including a lack of diagnostic technologies (e.g., CT scans), prehospital transport systems, and trained providers, contribute to substantial delays in care [8, 9, 10]. The “three‐delay” model has been adapted to describe delays in seeking, reaching, and receiving care for emergency conditions such as TBI in LMICs [11]. Each of these delays is independently associated with increased mortality, and collectively they highlight the urgent need for early, data‐driven decision‐making tools to support frontline care providers in resource‐constrained environments [12, 13].

Prognostic models represent one such tool, offering clinical decision support by estimating the likelihood of a specific outcome, such as mortality, using patient‐level data available at the point of care [14]. However, despite the proliferation of prognostic models for TBI in high‐income countries (HICs), few have been developed or validated using LMIC data. A systematic review identified over 100 TBI prognostic models, yet only a small fraction used data from LMIC populations [15]. Furthermore, most existing models were derived from tightly controlled clinical trial datasets, limiting their generalizability and real‐world applicability in low‐resource settings. To address this gap, our team previously developed a TBI prognostic model using data from Kilimanjaro Christian Medical Centre (KCMC), a tertiary referral hospital in northern Tanzania [12, 16]. The model was designed using routinely collected clinical variables, allowing for applicability in LMIC environments where advanced diagnostics may not be available. While initial performance metrics were promising, the model has not been recalibrated since its development in 2018. In the intervening years, shifts in TBI epidemiology, clinical workflows, and resource availability in the region may have altered the underlying distribution of predictors and outcome conditions known to degrade model performance over time if left unaddressed [17, 18].

In this study, we aimed to retrain our previously published TBI prognostic model using a newly accrued, real‐world dataset from the same hospital context. By leveraging machine learning techniques and updated patient‐level data, we seek to improve model performance, enhance external validity, and ensure alignment with current clinical realities in LMIC trauma care.

Methods

Ethics Approval

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Kilimanjaro Christian Medical University College Research Ethics and Review Committee (CRERC) in Moshi (KCMC/P.I/Vol.XI/2658). Clearance was also secured from the National Health Research Ethics Committee (NatHREC) under the National Institute for Medical Research (NIMR) in Tanzania (NIMR/HQ/R.8a/Vol.IX/4711). Patients were not consented as this was a standard healthcare quality improvement process, and the registry was observational and did not affect patient care. As such, consent was implied in the consent to treatment.

Study Setting

KCMC, a tertiary zonal referral hospital located in Moshi, Tanzania, serves over 15 million people across the northern regions of Arusha, Kilimanjaro, Manyara, and Tanga. Established in 1971, it has grown to encompass 630 official beds and provides a comprehensive range of medical services [19]. KCMC handles a high volume of emergency cases, particularly traumatic brain injuries (TBIs), receiving approximately 800–1200 TBI cases annually [20]. It is one of the few facilities in the region equipped with neurosurgical services, 24/7 CT imaging, and a multidisciplinary team experienced in acute neurotrauma care. KCMC has a greater capacity for TBI management compared to surrounding regional and district hospitals [19].

Data Source

This was a secondary analysis of cohort data from an ongoing Trauma registry at KCMC. The data for this study were obtained from the KCMC trauma registry established in 2013, with continuous data collection up to the current day [21]. These registries documented various aspects of patient care from the time of ED arrival to discharge, sociodemographic characteristics, pre‐hospital course, hospital course, and discharge information. Data collection was conducted by trained Tanzanian research nurses, who observed patient care, assisted with vital signs, and ensured data completeness. Data quality was assessed through medical records and treatment teams. Initial data were recorded on paper and later entered into REDCap [22], with continuous quality review by dedicated research personnel.

Study Population

The study population constituted all adult patients aged 18 years and above diagnosed with Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI) and recorded in these registries. The study included all patients who presented to the ED within 24 h of sustaining an injury and were evaluated for acute care. Patients were eligible if they survived ED evaluation. Those presenting for follow‐up visits, with non‐acute injuries, or who died prior to evaluation were excluded. The included participants were then divided into training and testing sets, respectively (Figure 1).

Study Variables

The dependent variable in this study was the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS), which was dichotomized into “good” (scores 4–5) and “poor” (scores 1–3) outcomes based on existing literature [12, 16, 23]. GOS measures recovery levels in trauma and head injury patients. GOS categorizes recovery after TBI into five levels: 1— death, 2—vegetative state, 3—severe disability (conscious but dependent), 4—moderate disability (independent with deficits), and 5—good recovery (resumed normal life with minimal or no deficits). GOS was assessed at the time of hospital discharge.

Seventeen independent variables were selected based on previous literature [12, 16], encompassing demographic information (age, sex), mechanism of injury, alcohol use on the day of injury, vital signs (temperature, respiratory rate, heart rate, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, pulse oxygen), neurological status (right and left pupil reactivity, pupils' size equal or unequal), TBI severity based on the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) categories (mild, moderate, severe), and times from injury to hospital arrival and to CT scan.

Statistical and Computational Analysis

Data management and analysis were performed using R version 4.4.0 [24]. Major packages included caret (v6.0‐94), mice (v3.16.0), pROC (v1.18.5), boot (v1.3‐30), and the packages used for SMOTE and SHAP analyses. A fixed random seed (seed = 123) was specified to ensure reproducibility. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the TRIPOD (Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis) guidelines [1]. Categorical variables were summarized with frequencies and proportions, and numeric variables with mean and standard deviation. The χ 2 test was used to examine relationships between independent variables and the dichotomized GOS. The dataset was initially divided into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets to ensure robust model training and unbiased evaluation. Missing data were handled using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) for variables with less than 25% missingness. The imputation process was performed using 10 iterations to ensure convergence of the imputation models [1]. A fixed random seed (seed = 123) was set to ensure reproducibility of the data partitioning and imputation procedures. Variables with more than 25% missingness were excluded from the analysis to reduce the risk of bias associated with excessive missing data. The proportion of missingness for each variable is presented in (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1). To avoid data leakage, the dataset was first split into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets, and imputation was conducted within the training data only. The imputation model derived from the training set was then applied to the test set. This approach ensured that no information from the test data influenced model training (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1). Given the imbalance in outcome distribution, the Synthetic Minority Over‐sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied exclusively to the training data after imputation. To prevent data leakage and overestimation of model performance, SMOTE was implemented within cross‐validation folds during model training, ensuring that synthetic samples were generated only from the training partitions and not from validation or test data. (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2). The SMOTE technique generates synthetic samples rather than oversampling with replacement, mitigating overfitting risks and ensuring the models do not develop a bias toward the majority class [25].

Model training/testing involved rigorous processes using the caret package in R [26], including 10‐fold cross‐validation. In 10‐fold cross‐validation, the dataset was divided into 10 parts (folds). The model was then trained on 9 of these folds and tested on the remaining set. This process is repeated 10 times, such that each fold is used as a test set once. Resampling and hyperparameter tuning were conducted using 10‐fold cross‐validation applied only to the training data. Within each fold, models were trained on resampled training partitions and evaluated on validation folds. The final performance is the average of all 10 test results, providing a reliable measure of the model's accuracy. Hyperparameter tuning was done through grid and random searches to optimize predictive performance. The final optimized hyperparameters for each machine learning model are presented in Supporting Information S1: Table S2 to facilitate reproducibility. Ten ML models were evaluated: Lasso Regression, Logistic Regression, Ridge Regression, Random Forest, C5.0 Decision Trees, Support Vector Machines (SVM), Decision Trees, Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Bagging, and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN).

Model evaluation was performed in the testing set, with metrics including (area under the curve) AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, NPV, and PPV. The 95% confidence intervals for these metrics were calculated using the bootstrapping package [27, 28]. ROC and PR curves were plotted using the pROC package [29], and Delong's test was used to compare ROCs between models [30], considering a p value < 0.05 as statistically significant. Confusion matrices provided detailed evaluations of model performance. A classification threshold of 0.5 was used to convert predicted probabilities into binary outcomes for the calculation of sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV.

Results

Demographic, Injury and Clinical Characteristics of Participants

This study involved 4596 adult patients with a mean age of 36 years (standard deviation, SD 15 years), and the majority (85.4%) were males. A quarter (25%) of patients had their TBI related to alcohol, 72.2% were involved in a road traffic injury, and about a tenth of all patients (9.7%) had severe TBI (GCS < 8). Among all 4596 TBI patients, 3390 (73.8%) had good outcomes (scores 4–5), while 1206 (26.2%) had poor outcomes. The proportion of poor outcomes was significantly higher among males (87.1%) compared to females (12.9%) (p = 0.045). Patients without alcohol involvement during the day of injury had a significantly higher proportion of poor outcomes (66.8%) compared to those with alcohol involvement (23.3%) and those with unknown status (9.9%) (p < 0.001). Regarding vital signs, patients with poor outcomes had slightly higher average temperatures (36.6°C, SD = 0.7°C) compared to those with good outcomes (36.5°C, SD = 0.6°C), which was statistically significant (p < 0.001). Oxygen saturation levels were also lower in patients with poor outcomes (95%, SD = 8%) compared to those with good outcomes (97%, SD = 5%), with a significant difference (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Predictive Performance of Machine Learning Models

Overall, model AUC on the test set ranged from 0.78 to 0.83. RF and C5 exhibited the highest AUC of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.81–0.85), indicating strong discriminative ability. Logistic regression, Ridge regression, and LASSO Regression had similar AUC values. SVM had the lowest AUC of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.75–0.80), significantly lower than logistic regression (AUC 0.81, 95% CI: 0.79–0.83, p = 0.003). No significant differences were found in AUC between logistic regression and GBM, ANN, ridge regression, lasso regression, DT, and bagging, but RF and C5 outperformed logistic regression (Table 2).

Performance of the Machine Learning Models in the Test Set

Based on other performance metrics, the RF model demonstrated the highest overall accuracy of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.76, 0.80) and strong positive predictive value (PPV) of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.85, 0.89). Similarly, the C5 model showed an accuracy of 0.77 (95% CI: 0.75, 0.79) and a PPV of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.85, 0.90). The Artificial Neural Network (ANN) model had the highest PPV of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.89, 0.93) but a lower accuracy of 0.72 (95% CI: 0.70, 0.74). Logistic Regression and Ridge Regression models both exhibited an accuracy of 0.75 with PPVs of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.88, 0.92) and 0.90 (95% CI: 0.88, 0.92), respectively (Table 3).

Calibration Performance of the Machine Learning Models

Quantitative calibration assessment demonstrated generally good agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes across the evaluated models (Table 4). The Random Forest model exhibited the best calibration, with the lowest Brier score (0.152), calibration intercept closest to zero (−0.01), and calibration slope closest to one (0.99). The C5.0 and Gradient Boosting Machine models also demonstrated good calibration performance (Table 5). These quantitative findings were consistent with the visual calibration plots (Figure 4), confirming that the Random Forest model produced the most reliable probability estimates.

Variable Importance in the Random Forest and Decision Tree Models

The SHAP values plot illustrates the variable importance for the DT and RF models as they excelled in predicting poor and good TBI outcomes (Table 4). Variables such as injury related to alcohol use, timely performance of CT scan, and TBI severity were the most influential characteristics in both models. Notably, injuries related to alcohol use and receiving a CT scan had high SHAP values in the RF model, indicating their importance in predicting TBI outcomes (Supporting Information S1: Table S1). Other important variables include higher body temperature, size of pupils, mechanism of injury, and oxygen saturation. The plot shows that while both models rely on similar variables, the RF model generally attributes higher importance to a broader range of variables (Figure 5). We further calibrated the DT and RF model (Figure 4), and the RF model was better calibrated compared to the DT model. Decision curve analysis (Supporting Information S1: Figure S3) demonstrated that the random forest model provided greater net benefit across a clinically relevant range of threshold probabilities compared to alternative strategies.

Discussion

This study trained 10 ML models to predict TBI outcomes using a trauma registry dataset from KCMC, Tanzania. Random Forest (RF) and Decision Tree (DT) models yielded the best performance, with AUCs of 0.83 and 0.82, respectively. RF provided superior accuracy and balance across performance metrics, while DT was especially sensitive to detecting poor outcomes. These results demonstrate that ML‐based predictive tools can be important in low‐ and middle‐income country (LMIC) trauma care settings with limited specialized resources.

Our findings advance two prior modeling studies at KCMC [12, 16]. The 2018 model by Rocha et al. used Bayesian generalized linear modeling with good performance (AUC 86.5%) and emphasized interpretability through predictors like GCS score and oxygen saturation. However, because the model was not implemented in practice, its real‐world performance regarding calibration, scalability, and external validation remained uncertain [16]. In contrast, the 2023 study by Zimmerman et al. innovatively incorporated time‐to‐care variables and achieved an AUC of 89.5%. It showed that delays to procedures like CT scans and ICU admissions substantially affect prognosis. However, as with the 2018 model, this model was not implemented clinically, and its real‐world usability, calibration, and scalability remain speculative. It also focused narrowly on time‐based predictors without comprehensive algorithmic benchmarking [12].

Importantly, both previous models utilized a limited subset of ML algorithms and incorporated standard model evaluation metrics, including AUC and confusion matrices. Our present study builds on this foundation by maintaining these methodological strengths, such as calibration and class balancing through SMOTE. However, this current study expands on the application of SMOTE using a broader set of ML models and a larger, more contemporary dataset. This continuity allows us not only to improve predictive performance but also to critically assess the relative utility of various ML approaches in LMIC contexts. Our approach thus offers a more holistic evaluation of TBI prognostic modeling in this setting.

Across performance metrics, RF and DT stood out in predictive power and operational utility. The RF model achieved an accuracy of 0.78 and PPV of 0.87, while DT identified 84.5% of patients with poor outcomes. Logistic regression and its regularized variants also performed competitively and were consistent with prior literature on traditional statistical approaches for TBI. Neural networks and SVMs, while promising in high‐income contexts, underperformed slightly likely due to their complexity and data dependency [31]. Although Random Forest and Decision Tree models demonstrated slightly higher AUC values, the differences across models were modest and may not be clinically meaningful. Therefore, model selection should also consider interpretability, ease of implementation, and availability of input variables, particularly in resource‐constrained settings.

Our findings reaffirm the predictive relevance of key clinical features, such as TBI severity, alcohol use, CT scan, and pupil reactivity, previously highlighted in the 2018 and 2023 models [12, 16]. While we did not include time‐to‐care variables as extensively as Zimmerman et al. [12], we demonstrated that good model performance can be achieved using core clinical variables readily available at admission. Importantly, this study focuses on predictive modeling rather than causal inference, and therefore, the inclusion of such variables is intended to improve predictive accuracy rather than to establish causal relationships, as recommended in prediction modeling frameworks. Variables like alcohol use on the day of injury should be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect underlying injury mechanisms and care‐seeking behaviors rather than a direct causal effect on outcomes. Previous studies have shown that alcohol is associated with high‐risk injury patterns and may influence both the severity of trauma and delays in accessing care, particularly in low‐resource settings. Similarly, the variable “CT scan performed” may partly reflect healthcare access, resource availability, clinician decision‐making, and local diagnostic practices rather than patient characteristics alone. Consequently, the predictive contribution of this variable may differ across healthcare settings with varying access to imaging services, potentially affecting the transportability of the model. Therefore, external validation in diverse healthcare environments is essential before widespread implementation.

This model's clinical utility lies in its ability to guide real‐time triage and risk stratification in resource‐constrained settings. For instance, a patient with unequal pupils and no CT scan performed could be flagged early for ICU care. Unlike black‐box models, our top‐performing models (RF, DT) use variables that are interpretable and routinely collected, increasing their potential for adoption [2]. Operationally, the model aligns with the WHO's call for context‐adapted tools in trauma systems and supports more efficient allocation of limited surgical, ICU, and radiologic resources [3]. The exclusion of advanced imaging or laboratory variables makes this model feasible for use even in lower‐tier facilities. While these models demonstrate potential to support clinical decision‐making, their use in real‐world settings should be preceded by external validation, prospective evaluation, and assessment of implementation feasibility.

Despite methodological improvements, this study shares limitations with earlier models. First, it is based on data from a single hospital and may not generalize to other LMIC settings. Second, while SMOTE improved class balance, it may introduce synthetic noise. Third, the model lacks detailed time‐to‐care and intervention data, reducing its value for procedural guidance, a strength seen in the 2023 model. Additionally, while calibration plots assessed prediction accuracy, quantitative metrics like Brier score, calibration slope, and intercept were not reported. Inclusion of these measures would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of model performance and should be considered in future external validation and model refinement studies. Nonetheless, this trade‐off favors a more streamlined model suitable for broader deployment. Compared to the 2018 model, it offers improved predictive capacity; compared to the 2023 model, it provides operational simplicity and broader algorithmic benchmarking. Furthermore, the developed models are intended for prediction rather than causal inference, and therefore, context‐dependent predictors should not be interpreted as causal determinants of patient outcomes. Before clinical implementation, the models should undergo external multicenter validation, prospective validation, and clinical impact assessment to establish their generalizability, real‐world performance, and potential to improve patient outcomes.

Future Research and Clinical Translation

External validation using multicenter LMIC datasets is essential to assess generalizability. Furthermore, usability and acceptability studies among clinicians should be prioritized to identify implementation barriers and opportunities for digital integration.

Conclusion

This study presents a third‐generation ML‐based TBI prognostic model from KCMC, advancing on prior work through methodological rigor, broader algorithm testing, and a strong balance of accuracy and feasibility. The RF and DT models offer practical, context‐relevant tools for TBI management in LMICs. These findings demonstrate promising predictive performance under internal evaluation; however, external validation is required before broader generalization or clinical application.
