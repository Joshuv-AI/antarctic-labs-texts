Inhibition of Glutamate Decarboxylase in the Spinal Cord Induces Motor Deficits and Motor Neuron Degeneration
Authors: Elizabeth Colín-Martínez, Uri Nimrod Ramírez-Jarquín, Ricardo Tapia, Clorinda Arias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMotor neuron (MN) diseases such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) are characterized by the loss of cortical and spinal MNs. Although the precise mechanisms of MN degeneration are still unknown, downregulation of GABAergic circuits has been identified in both ALS patients and transgenic models of the disease. GABA synthesis depends on the activity of the enzyme glutamate decarboxylase (GAD), of which two isoforms are known: GAD65 and GAD67. To study the effects of decreased GABA synthesis on spinal cord motor function, we analyzed the effects of administering three selective inhibitors of GAD: 3-mercaptopropionic acid (MPA), a competitive inhibitor of GAD, thiosemicarbazide (TSC), and pyridoxal phosphate γ-glutamyl hydrazone (PLPGH), two GAD cofactor blockers. A single dose of any of these inhibitors did not affect motor behavior or MN morphology. However, subchronic (3 days) and chronic (10 days) administration of MPA, TSC or PLPGH in rats caused motor alterations and cellular changes, including episodic myoclonus-like movements, flaccidity in the ipsilateral phalanges, loss of 35–50% of MNs, reactive astrogliosis and decreased GAD activity. These findings suggest that chronic inhibition of GAD can lead to MN degeneration and further suggest that GABA metabolism in the spinal cord may participate in the mechanisms that cause MN death in patients with neurodegenerative diseases such as ALS.Keywords: Motor neuron degeneration, GAD inhibitors, GABA metabolism, GAD isoforms, Rat spinal cord, ALS

Introduction

Several diseases can compromise the integrity and survival of motor neurons (MNs), with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) being the most common neurodegenerative disorder. ALS manifests as degeneration of upper and lower MNs. Upper MN degeneration is characterized by clonus, hyperreflexia, weakness and reduced reflexes, whereas lower MN involvement causes fasciculations, weakness, atrophy, and decreased reflexes, leading to palsy and death from respiratory failure within 3–5 years of onset (Tiryaki and Horak 2014). Although the mechanisms underlying MN degeneration in ALS remain unclear, one of the most consistent findings in both ALS patients and experimental models is an imbalance between excitatory and inhibitory synaptic circuits (Kiernan et al. 2011; Ramirez-Jarquin et al. 2014; Rothstein et al. 1990; Xie et al. 2023).

Compared with healthy subjects, patients with ALS have lower levels of GABA (Foerster et al. 2013), reduced content of GABAA receptor subunits in the motor cortex (Aronica et al. 2015; Petri et al. 2006), and, in those with the ALS-Ox subtype, which is characterized by oxidative stress and mitochondria dysfunction, higher expression of GAD2 (which encodes GAD65) in the cortex and spinal cord on postmortem evaluation (Eshima et al. 2023). In experimental models of ALS, a reduction in the number of inhibitory interneurons (Nieto-Gonzalez et al. 2011) and inhibitory synapses (Scekic-Zahirovic et al. 2021) has been described. Most studies of the role of GABAergic inhibition have focused on the cortex and, therefore, on the function of cortical hyperexcitability in individuals with ALS. Our group previously reported that chronic blockade of GABAA receptors, but not glycinergic receptors, in the rat spinal cord induces muscle overexcitability, loss of MNs and motor deficits and that these changes are prevented by administration of CNQX, an AMPA receptor antagonist (Ramírez-Jarquín and Tapia, 2017). These findings indicate that the spinal cord circuits modulated by GABA transmission are predominantly involved in controlling neuronal excitability and highlight the interplay between inhibitory and excitatory circuits in the pathophysiology of ALS.

GABA is the main inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system. Its synthesis depends on the activity of glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD), which exists in two isoforms, GAD65 and GAD67 (Martin et al. 1991). These isoforms interact differently with pyridoxal 5’-phosphate (PLP), a coenzyme that is necessary for GAD catalytic activity. Owing to its different affinity for PLP, GAD65 acts as an apoenzyme that can be activated by free PLP, allowing it to participate in dynamic responses, whereas GAD67 remains a holoenzyme, resulting in basal GABA production (Bayon et al. 1977). Therefore, reduced GABA synthesis can be achieved through the use of PLP inhibitors such as thiosemicarbazide (TSC) and γ-glutamyl hydrazone pyridoxal phosphate (PLPGH) or through the use of a competitive inhibitor of GAD, 3-mercaptopropionic acid (MPA).

Although an imbalance in GABAergic transmission has been implicated as an important factor in the pathophysiology of ALS, the question of whether reduced GABA synthesis caused by inhibition of GAD activity or modulation of GABA synthesis through changes in the availability of PLP (or both) play a role in some of the clinical manifestations of this neurodegenerative disease has not been explored.

To explore the effects of metabolic alterations on the GABAergic system, we studied the effects of single or prolonged administration of GAD inhibitors on the rat spinal cord in vivo and analyzed the effects of these interventions on motor performance and MN viability. We also analyzed whether inhibition of GAD65 or GAD67 by the PLP inhibitors TSC and PLPGH or by a molecule that competes for the glutamate-binding site on the enzyme (MPA) have different effects on motor performance and neuronal death.

Methods

Animals

Male Wistar rats weighing 280 to 300 g were used throughout the experiments. The animals were housed in a laboratory setting in groups of 4 with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and ad libitum access to food and water. Animals were handled with all precautions necessary to avoid suffering. Experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and the Rules for Research in Health Matters (México) with the approval of the local Animal Care Committee (approval number RT121/14). The sample size used in each group was determined based on a previous study (Ramírez-Jarquín and Tapia, 2017). A minimal sample size of n = 4 was determined considering a statistical β power of 0.8 and a statistical significance < 0.05 using G*power software (V 3.1.9.7) (Faul et al. 2007). The number of animals for each experimental condition was: single administration n = 4, subchronic and chronic treatments n = 6, GAD activity n = 7–10, for the control and experimental groups. A total number of 136 rats were utilized trough the study. Animals were included in the analysis only if the osmotic minipump remained correctly positioned at the time of euthanasia and the intrathecal cannula was clearly visible upon removal of the device. Animals were excluded if any component of the infusion system had become detached before euthanasia, including the minipump, connecting tubing, or dental acrylic used for fixation. Based on these criteria, 20 rats (15% of the total cohort) were excluded from further analyses. The animals in the control groups underwent the same surgical procedure, but instead of administering GAD inhibitors, the vehicle (PBS) was infused through the cannulas. The number of animals corresponding to each experimental group is indicated in the figure legends.

Reagents

PLPGH is synthesized by a condensation reaction (Schiff base formation) between equivalent amounts of the reagents, GAH (γ-glutamil hydrazide) and PLP (pyridoxal 5´phosphate) in an alcoholic solvent and under lightly acidic conditions to favor the nucleophilic attack of the hydrazide on the aldehyde. In this reaction, a double bond is formed with the aldehyde group of PLP (Tapia et al. 1967). The purity of the compound was verified by spectrophotometry (the absorption spectrum in 0.1 M in phosphate buffer with pH 6.3 showed a peak at 325 mµ corresponding to pyridoxamine phosphate, due to the C-N bond at position 4 PLP). MPA (CAS No. 107-96-0) and TSC (CAS No. 79196) were purchased from Sigma‒Aldrich, and L-glutamic acid C14 (ARC0240, specific activity 281,000 µCi/mmol) was obtained from ARC. Hyamine hydroxide (CAS No. 26248-39-5) was purchased from Research Products International, and antibodies against SMI-32 (801701) and GFAP (Z0334) were obtained from BioLegend and Dako Cytomation, respectively.

Inhibitory Mechanism of Action of used GAD Inhibitors

The GAD inhibitors used in this study have distinct mechanisms of action. MPA competes with glutamate for binding to both GAD isoforms (Tunnicliff 1990). TSC and PLPGH are carbonyl-containing compounds that trap free PLP and block its interaction with GAD65. TSC forms a double bond with the aldehyde group of free PLP, preventing its interaction with GAD65 (Abe and Matsuda 1979). PLPGH is synthesized through a condensation between GAH (glutamic acid γ-hydrazide) and PLP; in this reaction, a double bond is formed with the aldehyde group of PLP (Díaz-Muñoz and Tapia, 1988), resulting in a molecule that lacks activity as a coenzyme and that competes with PLP for the GAD active site. These three inhibitors reduce GABA levels and can induce seizure activity (Abe and Matsuda 1979; Díaz-Muñoz and Tapia, 1988; Lamar 1970).

Acute Administration of GAD Inhibitors by Microdialysis

GAD inhibitors were acutely administered to rats via microdialysis using a procedure similar to that described by Corona and Tapia (Corona and Tapia 2004). Each rat was anesthetized with isoflurane in carbogen (95% oxygen, 5% CO2), and anesthesia was maintained by a continuous flow of 2% isoflurane throughout the procedure. The rat was placed in a stereotaxic frame, its lumbar region was shaved, and a single, vertical ~ 3 cm-long incision was gently made on the skin, followed by two parallel incisions along the backbone so that it could be mounted on a spinal cord support. The second lumbar vertebra (L4 segment) was identified, and the muscle tissue covering it was removed. On the right side of the vertebral floor, a laminectomy (~ 2 mm in diameter) was performed, and the meninges were carefully opened. A microdialysis cannula (CM7 model) was placed on the exposed spinal cord and inserted into the tissue to a depth of 1 mm. Microdialysis was maintained at a flow rate of 2 µl per minute. First, 50 µl of Krebs solution was infused for stabilization; 125 µl of a solution of drug dissolved in Krebs solution was then infused, followed by 50 µl of Krebs solution. At the end of the procedure, the rats were sutured and placed in individual cages.

Subchronic and Chronic Administration using Osmotic Minipumps

Osmotic minipumps (ALZET, Model 2004) were implanted into the spinal cords of rats according to the method described by (Tovar-Y-Romo et al. 2007). The minipumps delivered a continuous flow of 0.25 µl/hour (6 µl/day). The surgical procedure was the same as that described in the previous section. A cannula held in a stereotaxic frame at a 25° angle was placed on the exposed L4 region of the lumbar spinal cord, inserted to a depth of 2 mm, and secured with dental cement. The cannula was then connected to the osmotic minipump, which was implanted subcutaneously on the animal’s back. Finally, the initial skin incision was closed using surgical staples, and the rat was placed in an individual cage. The osmotic minipump was operated for 3 days for subchronic treatment and for 10 days for chronic treatment.

Evaluation of Motor Function

Rotarod Test

The rotarod test primarily evaluates the motor performance of the proximal muscles of the hind limbs. Prior to surgery, the rats received one week of training in performing the rotarod motor test. The rotarod device was purchased from Columbus Instruments (Columbus, OH, USA). During the test, the rat walked on an accelerating rod (0.2 revolutions per second2) that was rotated at an initial speed of 10 rpm. The amount of time for which each animal remained on the rod before falling off was recorded as the latency to fall. The cutoff time was 120 s. Motor execution was analyzed 24 h after acute treatment and daily after implantation of the osmotic minipump during subchronic (3 days) and chronic (10 days) drug exposure.

Footprints Test

The rats’ overall stride patterns were quantitatively analyzed by inking their hind paws with nontoxic Chinese ink and allowing them to walk along a paper runway (10 × 100 cm) that was bound by two walls to form a corridor. The hind footprints of the animals were then analyzed in detail. This quantitative test was used to observe and record the position of the phalanges and to perform a detailed evaluation of the motor performance of the distal muscles. In the animals that received acute treatment, it was performed before and 24 h after surgery; in those that received subchronic treatment, it was performed before surgery and on day 3 of treatment, and in the animals that received chronic treatment, it was performed before surgery and on days 3 and 10 of treatment.

Phalangeal Tension Recording Test (PTR)

To detect early motor deficits in fine hind paw movements, we used the tension exerted by the phalanges; this was recorded using a novel technique developed in our laboratory (Colín et al. 2020) termed the phalangeal tension recording test (PTR). This method quantifies the tension generated by the distal phalangeal muscles when the hind paw squeezes a latex bulb. The bulb is connected to a water-filled plastic tube that contains a buoy at its distal end that is coupled to a FORT 10 transducer (WPI). This configuration allows the detection of changes in water level produced by squeezing motion and thereby records the tension exerted on the bulb. At baseline (when there is no tension), the recording trace displays a stable flat line. When the bulb is compressed, the resulting displacement of the water level deflects the trace upward (onset of tension), and the trace gradually returns to baseline when the bulb is released (end of tension). The analog signal (phalangeal tension) generated by the bulb is converted into a proportional voltage output through the transducer (FORT 10, WPI instruments). The PTR data were acquired and analyzed offline using Acqkowledge software version 3.7.1. Two parameters, area and amplitude, were extracted from the recording traces. The area under the curve was expressed as grams displaced per second, and the amplitude was measured as grams displaced at the point of maximum tension. The average values obtained in three trials for each hind paw (left and right) were used in the group analysis. Evaluation was performed 24 h after acute treatment, whereas in the chronic model, recordings were obtained on days 3 and 10 of treatment.

Nissl Staining and Immunofluorescence

For histological and immunohistochemical analyses, the animals were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital and perfused transcardially with 250 mL of ice-cold 0.9% saline, followed by 250 mL of ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB, pH 7.4). The animals’ spinal cords were dissected, postfixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for one week, and then cryoprotected through immersion in graded sucrose solutions (up to 30%). Transverse Sect. (40 μm thick) of the lumbar spinal cord -corresponding to the site of infusion- were obtained using a cryostat. Alternate sections were either stained with cresyl violet or processed for immunofluorescence. Six sections from each animal were subjected to coimmunostaining with antibodies against nonphosphorylated neurofilament H protein (SMI-32) for MNs staining, and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) for astrocytes. Morphologically intact MNs in the Nissl preparations (with a soma diameter > 25 μm and distinguishable nucleus) were counted in a 10× microscopic field and quantified on both sides of the dorsal spinal cord (contralateral and ipsilateral to the infused site). Twelve sections per subject were analyzed in total from the lesioned spinal cord or from sham animals (n = 4 per group) in a 20× field. Imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope. Z-stack reconstructions were generated over a total thickness of 30 μm, with optical sections acquired at 3-µm intervals. The surface area of GFAP immunoreactivity was quantified using ZEN 2 software (blue edition).

Measurement of GAD Activity

The isotopic method described by Díaz-Muñoz and Tapia (1988) was used to measure GAD activity, with minor modifications. At the end of the experiment, the rats were euthanized by decapitation, and 0.5 cm segments of the treated spinal cord tissue were obtained. The ipsilateral (treated) and contralateral ventral horns were processed separately and used to conduct independent measurements of GAD activity. Each horn was homogenized in 400 µl of 50 mM PB containing 1.13 mM dithiothreitol, adjusted to pH 6.5. From the homogenate, 6 µl were taken to measure the protein content according to the Lowry method (Lowry et al. 1951), and 80 µl were used to measure the enzymatic activity of GAD.The reaction mixture contained 80 µL of homogenate, 20 µL of 50 mM PBS, 20 µL of 0.5 mM PLP (for the exogenous PLP condition), and 20 µL of a premixed solution of [14C]-glutamate (0.1 µCi) and cold glutamate (10 mM). For the condition without exogenous PLP, an additional 20 µL of PBS was added to replace the coenzyme volume. The tube in which the reaction was performed was connected to a second tube (the collecting tube) containing 140 µL of 1 M hyamine hydroxide dissolved in methanol. The reaction was carried out in a water bath at 37 °C under continuous agitation; after 20 min, it was stopped by the addition of 100 µL of 2 N sulfuric acid (H2SO4). The system was then left to stand for one hour to allow the released CO2 to react with the hyamine hydroxide (see Fig. 6A). The collection tube was then placed in a vial containing 15 mL of scintillation fluid, and the radioactivity (counts per minute, CPM) was measured using a scintillation counter. A boiled homogenate was used as a negative control in each experiment. Enzyme activity is expressed as picomoles of CO2 produced per milligram of protein. A schematic representation of this experiment is shown in Fig. 6A.

Statistical Analysis

Comparisons of the changes observed under each experimental condition were made using Two-way ANOVA followed by Fisher’s post hoc test. For behavioral analysis (rotarod or gait analysis) Repeated Measures (RM ANOVA) test was used. A value of p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Acute Administration of GAD Inhibitors did not cause Motor Deficits or MN Degeneration

Acute infusion of 125 µL of any of the inhibitors (20 mM MPA, 15 mM TSC, or 25 mM PLPGH) into the spinal cord did not result in any visible motor alterations 24 h after treatment. No changes in fall latency were observed in the rotarod test (120 s) (Fig. 1A), and the animals’ footprints exhibited healthy morphologies characterized by extension of the five phalanges (Fig. 1B, half-red circles), an observation that is indicative of normal paw function. PTR recordings were similar across all groups before and after surgery. Representative paw traces are shown in Fig. 1 (C and D). The amplitude values ranged from 0.29 to 0.34 g (Fig. 1E), and the area under the curve ranged from 0.86 to 1.17 g/s (Fig. 1F). The morphology of the motoneurons in the spinal cord was apparently normal, as revealed by Nissl staining (Fig. 1G), and the number of MNs in the ventral horns of the animals in the treated groups did not differ significantly from those in the control group except a decrease in the ipsilateral site administered with MPA (p < 0.05, F (3,21) = 2.913) (Fig. 1H).

PTR representative traces of contralateral and ipsilateral recordings (C, D), and quantification of the amplitude and area under the curve of PTR recordings (E, F) did not show significant differences among groups. On the basis of these behavioral and histological findings, it can be concluded that a single dose of any of the GAD inhibitors did not alter either motor performance or MN survival.

Subchronic Infusion of GAD Inhibitors into the Rat Spinal Cord for 3 Days causes Cellular and Motor Alterations

To achieve continuous drug delivery, osmotic minipumps were used to administer GAD inhibitors for 3 days. This treatment induced muscular hyperexcitability, motor deficits and cellular alterations beginning on the first day of continuous infusion. The rats developed involuntary movements throughout the treatment period, characterized as episodic myoclonus-like movements of the hind limbs, with greater severity in the ipsilateral hind paw. These movements were episodic, lasting approximately 30 to 60 min, and were observed throughout the treatment. Evident alterations, including flaccidity, were observed in the distal muscles of the phalanges and metatarsus of the hind paw, whereas no alterations in the proximal muscles of the leg were detected. The rotarod test was performed daily and revealed a progressive decrease in latency to fall (Fig. 2A) from the first day to the third day of treatment; in the animals that were also treated with PLP inhibitors, this decrease was statistically significant (*p < 0.05, F (2.179, 43.58) = 31.24). Alterations in the footprints made by the ipsilateral paw were observed in all groups in which the animals were treated with GABA synthesis inhibitors. These changes were characterized by reduced phalangeal extension and a pronounced metatarsal impression (half-blue circles in Fig. 2B), both of which are consistent with flaccidity of the phalangeal muscles. In contrast, compared with the control hind paw, the contralateral hind paw displayed extended phalanges and maintained a healthy morphology (half-red circles in Fig. 2B).

Motor deficits were corroborated by the PTR test, as shown in Fig. 2 (C and D). A notable decrease in the area but not in the amplitude recorded for the ipsilateral paw was observed after treatment with any of the three GAD inhibitors (p < 0.01, p < 0.001, F (3,21) = 1.511 and F (3,21) = 1.105, respectively) (Fig. 2E and F). These results demonstrate that subacute exposure to GAD inhibitors is sufficient to induce ipsilateral motor deficits, as evidenced by both visual (footprint) and quantitative (PTR) assessment. Remarkably, unilateral alterations were evident in the distal muscles, and these were sufficient to cause motor impairment in a task, such as the rotarod test, that requires bilateral coordination and involves the proximal muscles of the hind limbs.

Morphological analysis (Fig. 3) of the contralateral (A) and ipsilateral ventral horns (B) revealed a significant increase in GFAP immunoreactivity on the ipsilateral side, particularly in the MPA and TSC groups, compared with the control group (Fig. 3C). GFAP immunoreactivity also tended to increase on the contralateral side after treatment with MPA or TSC (***p < 0.001, F (3,21) = 0.381) (Fig. 3C). Concomitant with these motor alterations, the number of MNs decreased significantly by approximately 40% (MPA and PLPGH) or 60% (TSC) after exposure to GAD inhibitors; this occurred on both sides but was more severe on the ipsilateral side (**p < 0.01, ****p < 0.0001, F (3,21) = 1.266) (Fig. 3D).

Chronic Infusion of GAD Inhibitors into the Rat Spinal Cord Induces Motor and Cellular Alterations

Given that ALS is a chronic disease, we wanted to determine whether prolonged treatment with GAD inhibitors leads to more pronounced motor deficits and MN loss. However, given the severe motor effects previously observed after treatment with 20 mM MPA, we aimed to assess whether this drug has a dose-dependent effect. Therefore, we tested the effects of MPA administered at concentrations of 3 and 10 mM. The motor impairments that resulted from chronic treatment were similar to those that resulted from treatment with the other inhibitors and resembled those observed in the animals in the subchronic treatment group. Persistent flaccidity of the distal muscles of the ipsilateral phalanges was observed; this began on the first day and continued until the end of treatment. Involuntary movements of the hind limbs, characterized as myoclonus-like, developed during the first three days of treatment. Like the involuntary movements observed in the subchronic group, these movements were episodic, occurring at intervals of 30–60 min. Interestingly, after three days of treatment, these alterations were no longer observed, despite continued infusion of the inhibitors into the spinal cord. The results of the rotarod test, which was performed daily, revealed a reduction in fall latency from the latency observed on day 1; thereafter, the fall latency remained relatively stable till days 7 to 8 (Fig. 4A). Footprint tests were conducted during the episodic myoclonus-like events as well as on the tenth day of treatment (Fig. 4B). During the presence of these events, the phalanges of the ipsilateral hind limb became spastic, forming a partial “fist” (indicated by half-yellow circles) during locomotion. Consequently, disruptions in the stride pattern were evident, although these changes disappeared when the myoclonus-like movements ceased. On the tenth day of treatment, the footprint revealed convergence of the ipsilateral phalanges (half-blue circles), a prominent metatarsal mark (blue line), and a shortened stride (green line). These were identified as permanent alterations in motor function that occurred as a consequence of treatment with any of the inhibitors. For reference, the normal gait pattern exhibited by the animals in the control group is shown as half-red circles in Fig. 4C. The PTR test was performed on day 10; the results are shown in Fig. 4 (C and D). The PTR analysis revealed a significant reduction in both the amplitude and the area parameters of both hind paws (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, F (6.432, 276.6) = 13.36 and F (5,55) = 0.412, respectively) (Fig. 4E and F); these alterations were more pronounced on the ipsilateral side. Interestingly, while the footprint test indicated motor alterations only in the ipsilateral paw, the more sensitive PTR test revealed deficits in both hind limbs, although it demonstrated that there was greater impairment in both amplitude and area on the ipsilateral side.

The Observed Changes in Motor Activity in the Subchronic Treatment Group Correlated with Decreased GAD Activity

To investigate whether the motor and cellular changes observed after treatment with the inhibitors were associated with decreased GAD function, we measured GAD enzyme activity in the spinal cords of rats that had been treated with GAD inhibitors for 3–10 days. The ventral horns of the lumbar spinal cord were processed individually for isotopic measurement of GAD activity (Fig. 6A). Endogenous GAD65 and GAD67 activities were determined in reactions that contained or did not contain exogenous PLP, respectively. On the third day of treatment with 20 mM MPA, 15 mM TSC, or 25 mM PLPGH, GAD activity was similarly reduced in both the contralateral and ipsilateral ventral horns of the treated animals. The effects of the individual inhibitors on GAD activity did not differ significantly. Addition of exogenous PLP did not increase GAD65 activity (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, F (9,135) = 7.409) (Fig. 6B). Rats that were treated with the inhibitors for 10 days tended to show decreased GAD activity; however, compared with the GAD activity in control tissues, these changes were not statistically significant for any of the three inhibitors (F (6,90) = 6.214) (Fig. 6C). Interestingly, after ten days of treatment, GAD activity was reduced by approximately 30%, a decrease that was not statistically significant, whereas after just treatment on Day 3, it was reduced by approximately 70%, a difference that was statistically significant. These results suggest that inhibition of GAD activity for 3 days is sufficient to induce muscle hyperexcitability, alterations in motor function, MN loss, and reactive astrocytosis. After administration of the inhibitors for ten days, GAD activity tended to recover, but motor performance and MN death remained altered.

Discussion

Hyperexcitability of MNs due to an imbalance between excitatory and inhibitory circuits is prominent in the etiopathogenesis of ALS. Direct inhibition of GABAA receptors in segments L4 and L5 of the spinal cord induces hyperexcitability, predominantly of the hindlimbs, and MN degeneration (Ramírez-Jarquín and Tapia 2016). Our study expands on these findings and demonstrates that inhibition of GAD activity in the lumbar spinal cord results in motor alterations and MN degeneration.

Since the L4 and L5 segments of the spinal cord innervate both the distal and the proximal muscles of the hindlimbs (Vejsada and Hník 1980), our study assessed motor performance that is linked to alterations in distal muscles (the metatarsus and phalangeal muscles). This was evaluated by examining footprints and the PTR test, and by having the animals perform tasks that involve both proximal and distal muscle function, such as the rotarod test. These tests revealed significant impairment following treatment with GAD inhibitors. Interestingly, the distal muscles of the hindlimbs are primarily composed of fast-resistant (FR) and fast-fatigable (FF) fibers, whereas the proximal muscles contain predominantly slow-twitch (S) fibers (Bobinac et al. 2000). After transection of the sciatic nerve in a rat model (Bobinac et al. 2000) and in ALS SOD mice (Tallon et al. 2016), FR and FF muscle fibers are more susceptible to denervation and to excitotoxic processes than are S-type fibers. These findings correlate with clinical observations in ALS patients, in whom motor impairments typically manifest first in the distal muscles, suggesting that these muscles possess inherent vulnerability (Liu et al. 2019). Similar to what was observed with glutamate excitotoxicity, we did not detect total limb paralysis after treatment, suggesting that the failure of the inhibitory circuits resembles the early stages of ALS, where spasms and alterations in fine movements, such as those in finger flaccidity, occur.

We found that acute administration of a single dose of any of three GAD inhibitors did not cause changes in motor performance or MN viability. However, after 3 days of exposure to any of these inhibitors, motor abnormalities such as myoclonus-like movements, reduced motor execution in various tasks, astrocytosis and MN loss occurred. This MN loss reached approximately 50%, a percentage reported as necessary to produce observable motor impairment (Ramírez-Jarquín and Tapia 2016). Interestingly, both the GAD activity and the astroglial response tended to return to near-baseline levels, after 10 days. Changes in astrocytes are prominent and generally preclinical abnormalities in ALS models, and treatments that delay onset or prolong survival in ALS models are often associated with decreased reactive astrogliosis (Vargas and Johnson 2010). Interestingly, three distinct molecular subtypes of ALS based on gene expression patterns have recently been identified. These groups include the neuroinflammatory subtype (ALS-Glia), characterized by glial activation; the subtype associated with transcriptional dysregulation (ALS-TD); and the subtype defined by oxidative stress and alterations in excitatory and inhibitory synaptic signaling (ALS-Ox). Within this latter subtype, a significant upregulation of GAD2 (GAD65), GABRA1 (GABAA receptor), and SLC17A6 (VGLUT2), has been reported (Eshima et al. 2023; O´Neil et al., 2025; Jammal et al. 2026).

Although the reasons for the differences observed in the pathological markers after 3 and 10 days of GAD inhibition, are not entirely clear, one possibility is that after 10 days, compensatory mechanisms are activated that allow the nervous system to cope with the loss of inhibitory control. One possible adaptive process is synaptic scaling, a form of plasticity that preserves synaptic homeostasis in response to prolonged changes in neuronal activity (Turrigiano 2008). For instance, suppression of neuronal firing through the overexpression of potassium channels initially reduces neuronal activity but firing rates normalize within hours (Burrone et al. 2002). Similarly, inhibition of excitatory circuits in cultures of spinal neurons via a glutamatergic antagonist initially reduces the frequency of action potentials. but this is later compensated for by increased AMPA receptor expression. In contrast, excessive activation of excitatory circuits, for example, by GABAergic inhibition, reduces AMPA receptor levels (O’Brien et al. 1998). These homeostatic responses may involve changes in receptor density, in the number of synaptic boutons, or in the content of synaptic vesicles (Turrigiano 2008).

The attenuation of GAD inhibition that occurred after long-term treatment in the present study may reflect a response in which the enzyme content of the tissue is increased. In fact, gad2 gene expression is upregulated by glutamate in some cellular models (Hao et al. 2004). Consistent with this possibility, postmortem analysis of ALS patients revealed elevated GAD expression in the frontal and temporal cortices but no increase in the number of GAD-positive interneurons, suggesting that the increase in the GAD signal is due to greater enzyme expression (Massieu et al. 1994). This finding is consistent with our data showing that the pronounced motor and cellular alterations that occurred during the first few days of treatment later subsided in parallel with an increase in GAD activity. Similar compensatory mechanisms in terms of excitability have been documented in vivo; in those studies, reduced postsynaptic GABAergic activity led to transient motor symptoms with similar timing and episodic characteristics (Ramírez-Jarquín and Tapia 2016). Nevertheless, further studies are required to determine the relative contribution of these factors. Another interesting result regarding the GAD inhibition observed after 3 days of drug exposure is that the inhibition at the ipsilateral treated site and the contralateral (untreated) side were of similar magnitude, consistent with the observed loss of MNs from the ventral horns on both sides. Although MN death and astrogliosis were present bilaterally in the spinal cord, our functional and histological data clearly demonstrate greater motor impairment, exacerbated MN death, and a significant GAD inhibition on the injected side. We hypothesize that the compounds can access the contralateral side, or that inhibition at the infused site triggers contralateral excitation through commissural interneurons, as demonstrated in previous studies (Berens et al. 2005). Regarding the magnitude of GAD inhibition on the contralateral side, potential limitations in the sensitivity and resolution of the assay employed must also be considered.

Several studies have documented the activity of specific GAD isoforms in the brains of mice and rats, but our study is one of the first to quantify GAD activity in the rat spinal cord. Radioisotopic assays of brain tissue have revealed that GAD65 activity ranges from 50 to 130 µmol in the absence of exogenous PLP (a condition in which only GAD67 activity is observed), whereas in the presence of exogenous PLP, a condition under which both GAD65 activity and GAD67 activity are measured, the activity increased to 70–234 nmol, depending on the region evaluated (Abe and Matsuda 1979; Massieu et al. 1994; Tapia et al. 1967). In our study, we found no significant difference in GAD activity between PLP-supplemented and non-PLP-supplemented conditions, suggesting that endogenous PLP may be sufficient to activate GAD. However, after 3 days of treatment with MPA, TSC, or PLPGH, GAD activity decreased significantly. Notably, in contrast to previous reports on GAD activity in the hippocampus (Tapia and Salazar 1991), exogenous PLP failed to increase GAD65 activity to control levels, suggesting that in our experiments GAD65 was already saturated with endogenous PLP or that GABA synthesis in the spinal cord relies primarily on GAD67, making PLP supplementation less impactful. In support of the latter possibility, there is evidence that GAD67 is responsible for most GABA synthesis in the brain since the brains of GAD65 knockout mice contain normal levels of GAD67 and GABA (Asada et al. 1997). Although direct measurements of GABA levels were not performed in this study, extensive evidence from both our group and others demonstrates that the compounds used alter the inhibitory/excitatory balance by inhibiting GAD, thereby decreasing GABA levels and promoting excitotoxic damage (Sawaya et al. 1978; Horton et al. 1980; Karlsson et al. 1974; Salazar and Tapia 2015; Ramírez-Jarquín and Tapia 2017). Therefore, even in the absence of direct quantification, the mechanism of disinhibition-mediated neurotoxicity is strongly supported by previous studies, yet we cannot rule out additional neurotoxic effects of these drugs.

Numerous studies have shown disruption of inhibitory circuits in both ALS patients and animal models (Xie et al. 2023) and, in fact, early dysfunction of cortical inhibitory pathways may precede peripheral nerve or muscle abnormalities. In patients with asymptomatic familial ALS (fALS), transcranial magnetic stimulation has been shown to reduce short-interval intracortical inhibition (SICI) and increase facilitation, suggesting the presence of cortical hyperexcitability due to GABAergic dysfunction (Awiszus and Feistner 1995; Geevasinga, 2017; Grieve et al. 2015; Higashihara et al. 2024; Menon et al. 2015). A decrease in inhibitory synaptic activity has also been reported in patients with ALS (Scekic-Zahirovic et al. 2021; Xie et al. 2023). Positron emission tomography has shown reduced activity in GABAergic interneuron-rich regions of the motor cortex (Turner et al. 2005), and cortical tissue analysis has revealed reduced expression of GABAA subunits (Petri et al. 2006).

Electrophysiological studies of ALS models have shown that vulnerable MNs in the spinal cord have lower GABAergic currents than do resistant neurons, such as those in the oculomotor system (Brockington et al. 2013). Taken together, these findings suggest that early dysfunction of the GABAergic system contributes to MN degeneration in patients with ALS. However, some limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, our experimental model relies on a pharmacological approach targeting GAD, which may not fully recapitulate the complex and multifactorial pathophysiology of ALS. Second, while we successfully verified the efficacy of the GAD inhibitors by measuring its activity, we did not quantify the precise threshold of GABA depletion associated with the observed motor impairments and neurodegeneration. Finally, although these phenotypic changes are highly consistent with spinal hyperexcitability, a direct assessment of glutamate-mediated neuronal injury would be necessary to fully characterize the underlying excitotoxic mechanisms in this model.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrated that acute administration of GAD inhibitors to the rat spinal cord was not sufficient to induce behavioral or cellular alterations. However, subchronic and chronic administration of the same inhibitors led to motor impairments, MN loss, astrogliosis, and a reduction in PLP-independent GAD activity by the third day of treatment. Our results indicate that GAD inhibition plays a role in spinal cord MN degeneration, suggesting that an imbalance in inhibitory circuits may contribute to the pathology of MN diseases. These findings strengthen the hypothesis that a sustained deficit in GABA function leads to motor dysfunction and MN death.
