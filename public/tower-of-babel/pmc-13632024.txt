HBV integration into KMT2B increases malignant phenotype of hepatocellular carcinoma by epigenetic reprogramming
Authors: Chao Fu, Ting Wu, Ran Meng, Wenbin Liu, Zishuai Li, Xi Chen, Qi Zhang, Xiaowei Ji, Guangwen Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lysine methyltransferase 2B (KMT2B, also named MLL4) is a frequently reported target of hepatitis B virus (HBV) genomic integration in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). However, the mechanism by which HBV integration at KMT2B (HBV-KMT2B) promotes HCC evolution remains largely unknown. We performed HBV capture sequencing and transcriptome sequencing on paired tumoral and adjacent hepatic tissues from 50 HBV-positive HCC patients, enabling systematic characterization of HBV-KMT2B and its aberrant transcripts. We used methylome sequencing, assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing, and cleavage under targets and tagmentation to assess the impact of HBV-KMT2B on epigenetic modifications and to identify key downstream molecules. It was found that the HBV integration loads accumulated with HCC evolution. HBV-KMT2B was the second most frequently detected HBV integration in HCC. HBV-KMT2B was mostly exclusive with other high-frequent integrations and the common oncogenic driver mutations. Integrated HBV fragments, predominantly from HBx, clustered within KMT2B exons 3–4, resulting in decreased transcription of full-length KMT2B and the generation of N-terminally truncated KMT2B (KMT2B-Ct). Both knockdown of full-length KMT2B and ectopic expression of KMT2B-Ct significantly promoted cell proliferation, migration, and invasion and inhibited apoptosis in HCC. KMT2B knockdown upregulated the stemness marker CD133 and suppressed the forward embryonic developmental pathways by regulating the promoter methylation. KMT2B-Ct suppressed vanin-1 (VNN1) by decreasing H3K4me3 enrichment at the VNN1 locus and reducing chromatin accessibility of VNN1. Overexpression of VNN1 suppressed the malignant phenotype of HCC cells. Thus, HBV-KMT2B drives HCC evolution by epigenetic modifications to upregulate CD133 and suppress VNN1, thereby inducing dedifferentiation in hepatocarcinogenesis.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for 75%–85% of primary liver cancer, the third leading cause of cancer death worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer death in China (Bray et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2021). Chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a major contributor, contributing to 57.1% of global HCC and 76.1% of HCC in China (Li et al., 2026). As compared with other etiologies-cased HCC, HBV-associated HCC (HBV-HCC) develops 8–12 years earlier and is significantly associated with a poorer prognosis (Lin et al., 2022), suggesting HBV has a more oncogenic potential than do other causes. HBV integration into the human genome is a key oncogenic event of HBV-induced hepatocarcinogenesis (Qian et al., 2024; Péneau et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2024, 2025; Sze et al., 2021). Accumulating evidence supports that HBV integrations are selected during hepatocarcinogenesis and enriched in genes such as telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT) and lysine methyltransferase 2B (KMT2B, also known as MLL4) (Qian et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025). Previous studies have demonstrated the oncogenic consequences of HBV integrations, including activation of cancer-related pathways, induction of genomic instability, and epigenetic modifications (Péneau et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2024, 2025). In particular, the functional mechanism of HBV integration into the hTERT promoter (HBV-hTERT) has been well elucidated (Zhao et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2024; Sze et al., 2021). However, the mechanisms by which HBV integration into KMT2B (HBV-KMT2B) contributes to HCC evolution remain largely unknown.

KMT2B encodes a histone methyltransferase that functions as the catalytic core of the complex of proteins associated with Set1 (COMPASS)-like complex, catalyzing methylation of histone H3 at lysine 4 (H3K4me), a modification associated with active transcription (Gold & Shilatifard, 2024). The human KMT2B gene contains 37 coding exons and undergoes extensive alternative splicing, producing multiple transcript variants that encode protein isoforms of various lengths. Beyond this transcriptional complexity, the full-length KMT2B protein itself is subject to post-translational cleavage by Taspase-1, which generates N-terminal and C-terminal fragments that dimerize to form a functional complex (Zhao et al., 2019). This intricate regulation of KMT2B at both the transcriptional and post-translational levels suggests that disruptions to its integrity, such as the generation of truncated variants, profoundly impact its function. The initiation and progression of HCC involve complex genomic and epigenetic alterations that cooperatively drive malignant transformation. Among these, aberrant histone modifications, particularly methylation dysregulation, constitute critical epigenetic events in tumorigenesis (Qian et al., 2024). HBV-KMT2B integration may serve as a bridge linking genomic abnormalities and epigenetic dysregulation to promote hepatocarcinogenesis.

In this study, we used three independent HBV-HCC cohorts from China to characterize the integration patterns of HBV-KMT2B. Through methylome assay, assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq), and in vitro mechanistic assays, we further revealed that HBV-KMT2B promotes the malignant phenotype of HCC via epigenetic reprogramming, that upregulates the stemness marker CD133. Notably, for the first time, we identified vanin-1 (VNN1), a critical regulator of forward embryonic development, as a key molecule downstream of HBV-KMT2B. Suppression of VNN1 serves as a key mechanism by which HBV-KMT2B promotes the malignant phenotype of HCC.

Results

Characterization of HBV integration into KMT2B

We firstly enrolled 50 well-characterized HBV-HCC patients as a discovery cohort (Table S1): 14 with primary HCC without postoperative recurrence for more than three years (early-stage evolution), 15 with postoperative recurrence between two and five years after the first surgery (middle-stage evolution), and 21 with postoperative recurrence within two years after the first surgery (late-stage evolution). Paired tumors (primary tumors in the early-stage group and recurrent tumors resected by the second surgery in the middle-stage and late-stage groups) and adjacent liver tissues were subjected to HBV-capture sequencing. A total of 9609 HBV integrations were identified across the human genome, detected in 94.0% (47/50) of tumors and 92.0% (46/50) of adjacent tissues. HBV integrations were significantly more abundant in the tumors than in the adjacent live tissues (Fig. 1(A)). The combined number of HBV integrations in the tumor and adjacent tissues significantly increased from early-stage evolution HCC to late-evolution HCC (Fig. 1(B)). A total of 239 Chinese HBV-HCC patients with whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data of tumor and 429 Chinese HBV-HCC patients with HBV capture sequencing data of tumor were assigned to validation cohorts 1 and 2, respectively (Zhao et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2024). In both validation cohorts, KMT2B was the second most frequent target of HBV integration after hTERT (Table 1), and HBV-KMT2B occurred exclusively in tumor tissues. In the discovery cohort, HBV integrations in hTERT, KMT2B, and FN1 were mostly exclusive (Fig. 1(C)). In validation cohort 1, which had WGS data, we validated the exclusivity of HBV-KMT2B with HBV integrations in hTERT and FN1. Furthermore, HBV-KMT2B was mostly exclusive with several common oncogenic single nucleotide variations (SNVs) in HCC. Compared to HBV-KMT2B-negative patients (n = 226), HBV-KMT2B-positive patients (n = 13) had significantly lower frequencies of HBV-hTERT integration (0/13 vs. 70/226, χ2 = 5.69, P = 0.012), hTERT promoter SNV (0/13 vs. 75/226, χ2 = 3.33, P = 0.011), and TP53 mutation (3/13 vs. 121/226, χ2 = 4.46, P = 0.044) (Fig. 1(D)). We next analyzed the origin of the viral insertion fragment and the genomic location of breakpoints. Regarding the viral fragment origin, HBV-KMT2B integrations did not differ from total HBV integrations. In both the discovery and validation cohorts, HBx-derived fragments were predominant across all HBV integrations including HBV-KMT2B (Fig. 1(E)). In contrast, the distribution of breakpoints across functional genomic regions were distinct for HBV-KMT2B compared to other HBV integrations. For all HBV integrations, breakpoints were concentrated in intronic (50.1%), intergenic (40.1%), gene regulatory (5.1%), and exonic regions (4.7%). Whereas breakpoints of HBV-hTERT were predominantly located in promoter regions (53.6%), breakpoints of HBV-KMT2B were mainly concentrated in intronic (64.3%) and exonic regions (35.7%) (Fig. 1(F)). In the discovery cohort, 95.73% of breakpoints proximal to KMT2B were clustered within exons 3–4 (chr19: 35721600–35722300), a clustering pattern that was confirmed in the validation cohorts (Fig. 1(G), Fig. S1A). The HBV-KMT2B breakpoints observed in the transcriptome sequencing data were consistent with those identified in genomic DNA from the same samples. However, no recurrent fusion transcripts were observed across different patients (Table S2). An N-terminally truncated KMT2B transcript (KMT2B-Ct) was identified by RNA-seq in HBV-KMT2B-positive tumor samples and corresponded to a variant containing only exons 20–37. This transcript is predicted to encode a protein spanning amino acid residues from 1524 to 2715 of the full-length KMT2B. This truncated protein retains the C-terminal catalytic module, including the SET [Su(var)3-9, Enhancer-of-zeste, Trithorax] domain responsible for histone methyltransferase activity, as well as the FYRN (Phe/Tyr-rich N-terminal) and FYRC (Phe/Tyr-rich C-terminal) domains. However, it lacks N-terminal domains, including the CXXC zinc finger, PHD fingers, bromodomain, and Menin-binding domain, which are essential for chromatin localization and complex assembly (Fig. 1(H)). Compared to tumor samples without HBV-KMT2B, those with HBV-KMT2B exhibited significantly lower levels of full-length KMT2B transcripts and higher levels of this N-terminally truncated transcript (Fig. 1(I and J)). These findings suggest that HBV-KMT2B integration does not generate stable fusion transcripts. Instead, it may disrupt the production of full-length KMT2B transcript and promote the generation of KMT2B-Ct.

Reduction of full-length KMT2B transcript and expression of KMT2B-Ct induce development of HCC by epigenetic reprogramming

To investigate the transcriptional dysregulation of KMT2B induced by HBV-KMT2B, we knocked down full-length KMT2B or transfected KMT2B-Ct in two HCC cell lines, Huh7 and SK-Hep-1 (Fig. S1B–S1D). Western blotting confirmed both the knockdown efficiency of wild-type KMT2B and the protein expression of HA-tagged KMT2B-Ct (Fig. S1D and S1E). Compared to Huh7 cells expressing full-length KMT2B alone, HCC cells with reduced full-length KMT2B or co-expressing full-length KMT2B and KMT2B-Ct both showed enhanced proliferation (Fig. 2(A) and (B)). Furthermore, KMT2B-Ct transfection in combination with full-length KMT2B knockdown further enhanced cell proliferation compared to KMT2B knockdown alone (Fig. 2(C)). Similar results were obtained in SK-Hep-1 cells (Fig. 2(D)–(F)). Furthermore, compared to HCC cells expressing only full-length KMT2B, those with reduced full-length KMT2B or co-expressing full-length KMT2B and KMT2B-Ct exhibited a significant increase in S-phase cells (Fig. 2(G) and (H)), reduced apoptosis (Fig. 3), and enhanced migration and invasion (Fig. S2). Reduction of full-length KMT2B transcripts and upregulation of KMT2B-Ct lead to epigenetic reprogramming

To further investigate the mechanism by which HBV-KMT2B promotes malignant phenotypes of HCC, we performed genome-wide methylation sequencing and RNA sequencing on HCC cells with normal full-length KMT2B levels, those with reduced full-length KMT2B, and those co-expressing full-length KMT2B and KMT2B-Ct. Compared to HCC cells with full-length KMT2B, KMT2B knockdown induced extensive genome-wide methylation reprogramming, with 42937 differentially methylated sites (DMSs) (19366 hypermethylated and 23571 hypomethylated). HCC cells co-expressing full-length KMT2B and KMT2B-Ct similarly exhibited extensive methylation reprogramming, with 10892 DMSs (3694 hypermethylated and 7198 hypomethylated) (Fig. 4(A) and (B)). In cells with KMT2B knockdown, the promoter methylation levels of stemness markers including CD133 (PROM1) and α-fetoprotein (AFP) were decreased, with correspondingly increased expression confirmed by RNA-seq (Table S3). DMSs within the CD133 and AFP promoter regions are detailed in Table S4. The upregulation of CD133 was further validated by RT-qPCR (Fig. 4(C)). We performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on genes with hypermethylated promoters and decreased expression levels confirmed by RNA-seq. We further investigated whether KMT2B-Ct affected histone methylation-mediated gene regulation. The C-terminal region of KMT2B interacted with ubiquitously transcribed tetratricopeptide repeat-containing protein UTX, to form the complex of proteins associated with Set1 (COMPASS-like complex), which catalyzes histone H3 lysine 4 (H3K4) methylation (Kim et al., 2014). Co-IP using an anti-HA antibody confirmed that HA-tagged KMT2B-Ct retains the ability to bind UTX subunit (Fig. 5(A)). Despite retaining the ability to bind UTX, ectopic expression of KMT2B-Ct still induced chromatin accessibility changes in cells, compared to full-length KMT2B. ATAC-sequencing revealed that KMT2B transcriptional abnormalities induced by HBV-KMT2B decreased chromatin accessibility of VNN1 (Fig. 5(B)). We performed cleavage under targets and tagmentation (CUT&Tag) assay using an antibody specific for H3K4me3 and found that enrichment of H3K4me3 at the VNN1 locus was markedly reduced in KMT2B-Ct-overexpressing cells compared to full-length KMT2B-overexpressing cells (Fig. 5(C)). RT-qPCR confirmed that VNN1 expression was significantly decreased in HCC cells with KMT2B knockdown and in cells co-expressing full-length KMT2B and KMT2B-Ct, compared to HCC cells expressing full-length KMT2B alone. Moreover, KMT2B-Ct transfection plus KMT2B knockdown further reduced the expression of VNN1 (Fig. 5(D)). To test whether reduced VNN1 expression is essential for HBV-KMT2B-induced HCC evolution, we performed rescue assays. In HCC cells with full-length KMT2B knockdown or KMT2B-Ct overexpression, ectopic expression of VNN1 significantly suppressed cell proliferation (Fig. 6(A)), reduced the proportion of S-phase cells (Fig. 6(B)), and increased apoptosis (Fig. 6(C)). Consistently, in xenograft mouse models, tumors from Huh7 cells stably overexpressing KMT2B-Ct were significantly heavier than those from control cells. Discussion

In this study, we characterized the oncogenic mechanism by which HBV-KMT2B promotes HCC evolution. We found that the HBV integrations were predominantly clustered within KMT2B exons 3–4, leading to decreased expression of full-length KMT2B and increased expression of N-terminally truncated variants. These transcriptional abnormalities induced epigenetic reprogramming, characterized by upregulation of the stemness marker CD133 and suppression of VNN1, a critical regulator of forward embryonic development. The inhibition of forward developmental programs and the activation of stemness features collectively promote malignant phenotype in HCC.

Our study characterized the transcriptional abnormalities induced by HBV-KMT2B. Although HBV integrations were predominantly clustered within the exons 3–4 of KMT2B across all three cohorts, the proportion of HBV integration in the exons 3–4 of KMT2B varied substantially, ranging from 33% to 96%. Notably, a similar integration pattern within KMT2B exons 3–4 has also been observed in adeno-associated virus 2 (AAV2)-infected HCC, suggesting that this region represents a fragile site serving as a common target for viral integration (Nault et al., 2015). However, these viral integration events do not generate virus-host fusion transcripts. Instead, HBV-KMT2B led to increased expression of N-terminally truncated KMT2B transcripts, accompanied by reduced abundance of full-length KMT2B transcripts, which is consistent with previous findings in Japanese HCC cohorts and embryonic stem cell models (Tsuchiya et al., 2025). Given that the HBx-encoding region, the most frequently integrated viral fragment at the KMT2B locus, contains well-characterized enhancer elements (Villanueva & Loyola, 2023), the integrated HBV fragments may potentially exert cis-regulatory effects on KMT2B transcription.

Our results demonstrate that the transcriptional abnormalities induced by HBV-KMT2B trigger methylation reprogramming, including alterations in both histone methylation and promoter DNA methylation. KMT2B functions as the catalytic subunit of the COMPASS-like complex, cooperating with UTX to catalyze histone H3K4 methylation, which maintains chromatin accessibility and promotes gene expression (Macrae et al., 2023). The HBV-KMT2B integration induced KMT2B-Ct retains the SET domain responsible for methyltransferase activity and the ability to bind UTX, but lacks N-terminal chromatin-targeting domains, including the CXXC zinc finger, PHD fingers, and Menin-binding domain. Thus, KMT2B-Ct may retain catalytic capacity while losing proper chromatin localization. In this study, VNN1 expression was reduced upon KMT2B knockdown and further suppressed by additional KMT2B-Ct overexpression, suggesting that KMT2B-Ct competes with full-length KMT2B to bind UTX, yet fails to effectively catalyze H3K4 methylation at the VNN1 locus, leading to reduced chromatin accessibility and subsequent transcriptional repression of VNN1. VNN1 has been identified as a tumor suppressor in sarcoma, where its expression correlates with improved survival and enhanced antitumor immunity (Lv et al., 2025). A proteomic study in 26 human immunodeficient virus (HIV)/HBV co-infected patients suggested VNN1 as a potential serum biomarker for HCC risk prediction (Ke et al., 2023). Previous studies have shown that single nucleotide variants in the VNN1 promoter influence the expression of VNN1 by affecting chromatin accessibility (Kaskow et al., 2014). As VNN1 interacts with the tumor immune microenvironment (Miallot et al., 2023), the inhibition of VNN1 might facilitate the selection of tumor stem cells in the tumor immune microenvironment in HCC. The present study reveals for the first time that HBV-KMT2B also regulates VNN1 expression through epigenetic modification. Knockdown of full-length KMT2B transcripts also triggers genome-wide DNA methylation reprogramming. Notably, genes with promoter hypermethylation and reduced expression were enriched in pathways regulating normal embryonic and tissue development, whereas hypomethylation was observed at stemness-associated genes including CD133, leading to their reactivation. This evidence suggests that HBV-KMT2B integration promotes HCC progression not only through histone methylation defects but also through direct DNA methylation reprogramming, collectively driving dedifferentiation and stemness acquisition in HCC.

We previously proposed the “Cancer Evolution-Development” (Cancer Evo-Dev) theory, indicating that chronic inflammation promotes carcinogenesis through a process of “mutation-selection-adaptation”. Under the selection pressure of the inflammatory microenvironment, cells with somatic mutations adapted to the microenvironment survive and undergo retro-differentiation, characterized by the re-expression of embryonic factors and a reverse developmental process that opposes normal embryonic differentiation (Liu et al., 2021). Through precise epigenetic regulation of target gene expression, KMT2B plays essential roles in cell differentiation, development, and tissue homeostasis (Kubo et al., 2024). By reducing full-length KMT2B and generating the KMT2B-Ct variant that competes with full-length KMT2B for UTX binding, HBV-KMT2B integration may serve as a key molecular event driving this reverse developmental process during HCC evolution.

Both epigenetic alterations and genomic mutations are well-established hallmarks of carcinogenesis. Classic gatekeeper mutations, such as those in hTERT and TP53, are considered early events that initiate subsequent genomic alterations. Interestingly, we found that the frequency of HBV-KMT2B was the second only to HBV-hTERT and was mutually exclusive with other common gatekeeper mutations, including HBV-hTERT, SNVs at the hTERT promoter, and TP53 mutations. This distinct pattern of mutual exclusivity strongly suggests that HBV-KMT2B may also act as an early gatekeeper event in a subset of HCC, bridging initial genomic damage with downstream epigenetic reprogramming. This exclusivity further implies that different initial driver mutations may dictate distinct evolutionary trajectories, thereby contributing to the molecular heterogeneity of HCC. TP53 and CTNNB1 mutations were reported to be mutually exclusive and define different molecular subclasses of HCC, indicating that alternative gatekeeper events can shape divergent paths of hepatocarcinogenesis (Calderaro et al., 2017). Patients who achieve a functional cure for chronic hepatitis B still harbor a significantly elevated risk of HCC (Shi et al., 2025). Our findings raise the possibility that HBV-KMT2B integration continues to drive cancer evolution after viral replication has been suppressed by antiviral therapy.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the effect of HBV-KMT2B on the occurrence of HCC in normal hepatocytes remains to be clarified. Second, although we found that HBV-KMT2B is an independent factor promoting HCC evolution, therapeutic options targeting HBV-KMT2B should be developed. Third, the predictive value of VNN1 for HCC risk in HBV-infected individuals, particularly those harboring HBV-KMT2B, should be validated in prospective cohort studies.

In summary, this study identifies HBV-KMT2B integration as an independent driver of HCC evolution through epigenetic reprogramming. By decreasing full-length KMT2B and generating truncated variants, HBV-KMT2B upregulates CD133 and suppresses VNN1, thereby promoting dedifferentiation, stemness, and malignant phenotypes in HCC. These findings establish VNN1 as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target for HBV-associated HCC.

Materials and methods

Patient cohorts and samples

The discovery cohort consisted of 50 HBV-HCC patients enrolled at the Eastern Hepatobiliary Surgery Hospital in Shanghai from 2009 to 2013. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) HCC without concomitant autoimmune or metabolic liver diseases; (2) seropositive for HBsAg or HBV DNA; (3) seronegative for hepatitis C virus (HCV) and human immune deficient virus. Tumor and adjacent normal tissues were independently confirmed by two pathologists. This study adhered to the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Second Military Medical University. Written informed consent for participation and publication was obtained from all participants. The discovery cohort is independent of the two validation cohorts (Chen et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2016).

HBV capture sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted using the Genomic DNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Sequence capture probes were designed based on nine genotypes (A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H, and I) of HBV genome sequences and produced by My Genostics (Beijing, China). The detailed targeted HBV DNA fragment capture sequencing and HBV integration detection procedure was performed according to a previous report (Zhao et al., 2016).

RNA-seq

RNA extraction, library preparation, sequencing, and expression quantification were performed as previously described (Liu, Liu, et al., 2022; Liu, Ji, et al., 2022). One pair of tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues from the discovery cohort was excluded from transcriptome analysis due to suboptimal RNA integrity. Thus, among the 50 pairs subjected to HBV capture sequencing, 49 pairs were processed for RNA-seq.

Establishment of stable cell lines

Human HCC cell lines Huh7 and SK-Hep-1 were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were routinely cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Shanghai Boguang Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Lentiviral vectors (Shanghai Heyuan Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) included shKMT2B (pSLenti-U6-shRNA(KMT2B)-CMV-EGFP-F2A-Puro-WPRE) and its empty vector, KMT2B-Ct expression vector (pcSLenti-EF1-mCherry-P2A-BSR-CMV-HA-KMT2B(1524-2715 aa)-WPRE) and its empty vector, as well as VNN1 overexpression vector (pcSLenti-EF1-GFP-P2A-BSR-CMV-VNN1-WPRE) and its empty vector. Cells were transduced with these lentiviruses plus 20 μL Polybrene-plus in 2 mL medium. After 48 h, selection was performed with puromycin (4 μg/mL) or blasticidin S (10 μg/mL) for one month, and efficiency was validated by RT-qPCR.

Western blot and co-immunoprecipitation

For Western blot, total protein was extracted from cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Yesen, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Yesen). Protein concentration was measured using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A55860). Proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Signals were visualized using the Touch Imager XLi system (e-BLOT Life Science, Shanghai, China). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ (v1.53a) and normalized to GAPDH. The following antibodies were used: anti-HA-Tag (#3724, CST, 1:1000), anti-KMT2B (#63735, CST, 1:1000), anti-UTX (#33510, CST, 1:1000), and anti-GAPDH (#2118, CST, 1:1000).

For co-immunoprecipitation, cells were lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Cell lysates were incubated with anti-HA antibody (#3724, CST, 1:50) or normal rabbit IgG (CST) overnight at 4 °C with rotation. Protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher, #88804) were added and incubated for an additional 2 h at 4 °C. The beads were washed three times with lysis buffer, and bound proteins were eluted by boiling in SDS loading buffer. The immunoprecipitates were then analyzed by Western blotting with anti-HA and anti-UTX (D3Q1I, #33510, CST, 1:1000) antibodies.

Cell proliferation, migration, invasion, apoptosis, and cell cycle assays

Cell proliferation, migration, invasion, apoptosis, and cell cycle progression were assessed following previously described protocols (Li et al., 2025). Briefly, cell proliferation was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay (Dojindo, Japan) at 6, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. Migration and invasion were assessed using Transwell chambers (Corning, #3422, USA), with Matrigel (Corning) pre-coated for invasion assays. For both assays, 1 × 105 cells were seeded in serum-free medium in the upper chamber, and medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) was placed in the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After 48 h, migrated or invaded cells were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and quantified using ImageJ. Apoptosis was detected using the Annexin V-APC/7-AAD Apoptosis Detection Kit (Absin, Shanghai, China) followed by flow cytometry analysis. Cell cycle distribution was assessed using DAPI staining (BD Pharmingen™, #564907, USA) and analyzed by flow cytometry with ModFit LT software.

DNA methylation analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from cell pellets collected during logarithmic growth. DNA concentration and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer and agarose gel electrophoresis. Bisulfite conversion was performed using the Zymo Research EZ DNA Methylation-Gold Kit. Methylation profiling was conducted using the Illumina Infinium MethylationEPIC v2.0 (935K) BeadChip (Shanghai Ouyi Biomedical Technology, Shanghai, China). All Huh7 cell samples were processed in a single batch on the same Illumina iScan system under identical experimental conditions. Raw data files were obtained via Illumina iScan and imported into R (version 4.5.3). Data processing, quality control, and normalization (BMIQ method) were performed using the ChAMP package (version 2.24.0). Differentially methylated sites were identified using a t-test with thresholds of Δβ > 0.1 and P < 0.05. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using Python scripts, with P < 0.05 considered significant.

ATAC-seq

ATAC-seq libraries were prepared using the TruePrep DNA Library Prep Kit V2 (Vazyme) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 150 bp paired-end reads, following previously described protocols (Buenrostro et al., 2013; Liu, Liu, et al., 2022). Briefly, Huh7 cells were harvested, lysed, and subjected to transposition. The transposed DNA was purified and amplified. Clean reads were obtained by removing adapter-containing, poly-N, and low-quality reads. Clean reads were mapped to the human genome (GRCh38) using BWA-MEM. Reads with unmapped, secondary, duplicated, or supplementary flags were filtered out using SAMtools (Danecek et al., 2021). Peak calling was performed using the MACS2 callpeak module with BAM files as input. The human genome effective size was set as reference, and a P-value threshold of 0.01 was used for peak detection. The fragment length model was not automatically built; instead, each aligned read was shifted 100 bp toward the 5’ end and extended to 200 bp toward the 3’ end to generate fixed-length fragments. All replicates were retained, and signal intensities were normalized to reads per million mapped reads using the SPMR method.

CUT&Tag assay

CUT&Tag assay was performed as previously described (Kaya-Okur et al., 2020). Briefly, Huh7 cells were harvested, washed, and counted. Approximately 1 × 105 cells were used for each reaction. Cells were permeabilized and incubated with primary antibody against H3K4me3 (#9751, CST, 1:50) overnight at 4 °C with rotation. After washing, cells were incubated with secondary antibody (anti-rabbit IgG) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with pA-Tn5 transposase complex for 1 h at room temperature. Tagmentation was initiated by the addition of MgCl2 and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The reaction was terminated by adding SDS and EDTA. Libraries were purified and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 150 bp paired-end reads. Peak calling was performed using MACS2 (--g hs, -q 0.01) with BAM files as input. Tracks were visualized using IGV (Integrative Genomics Viewer).

In vivo tumorigenicity assay

In vivo tumorigenicity assay was performed in 4- to 6-week-old male nude mice (BALB/c, Jihui Laboratory Animal Care Corporation, Shanghai, China) in a pathogen-free condition. Huh7 cells (1 × 107 cells/mL) were mixed with Matrigel (1:1) and 100 μL of the mixture was subcutaneously injected into the dorsal flank of each mouse. Tumors were harvested 15 days after injection and weighed. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Second Military Medical University and performed in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0), unless otherwise specified. Quantitative data from cellular and molecular experiments are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent biological replicates. Comparisons between two groups were performed using two-tailed Student's t-test. Multiple-group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. For differential expression, methylation, and chromatin accessibility analyses, statistical significance was determined using appropriate parametric or non-parametric tests as specified in the respective method sections. For all analyses, P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
