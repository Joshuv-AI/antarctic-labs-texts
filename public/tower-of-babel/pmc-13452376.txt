Oxidative DNA damage level and lipid peroxidation status in subclinical hypothyroid patients compared with euthyroid individuals
Authors: Heevi A Rajab, Hishyar A Najeeb, Dildar H Musa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study focused on measuring oxidative DNA damage and oxidative stress biomarkers in subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH) patients to gain insight on the condition's contribution to metabolic consequences. Additionally, it assesses the impact of levothyroxine replacement therapy on oxidative stress and DNA damage in patients with SCH over a specified duration of treatment.MethodsA case–control study was done at the Diabetes and Endocrine Center, Azadi Teaching Hospital, Duhok, Kurdistan Region, Iraq, from June 2024 to February 2025. The participants were divided into four groups: euthyroid controls (G1, n = 80), previously treated SCH (G2, n = 44), newly diagnosed SCH (G3, n = 105), and newly diagnosed SCH patients on follow-up levothyroxine therapy (LT4) (G4, n = 20). Oxidative stress and oxidative DNA damage biomarkers were assessed and compared in all study groups.ResultsThe research comprised 249 participants: 169 with SCH and 80 euthyroid controls. Compared to G1, oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) and total oxidant status (TOS) were higher in groups G2, G3, and G4 (p < 0.0001). The marker of oxidative DNA damage 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) was significantly higher in G3 and G4. The oxidative stress index (OSI) was higher in the SCH groups compared to controls. Total antioxidant status (TAS) was significantly decreased compared to controls (p < 0.0001). There were no significant differences regarding the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) between the groups.ConclusionSCH patients, especially newly diagnosed, show elevated oxidative stress, DNA damage, and reduced antioxidant capacity versus euthyroid controls, elucidating metabolic complications and validating oxidative biomarkers for early risk stratification.Keywords: 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine, Oxidized LDL, Subclinical hypothyroidism, Total antioxidant status, Total oxidant status

Introduction

Subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH) involves normal thyroxine (T4) levels and raised thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels.1 The prevalence of SCH is highly variable, ranging from 3% to 15%. Women, older adults, people with a family history of thyroid disorders, and those with low iodine levels have a higher risk of developing SCH. These large variations in prevalence are due to differences in age groups, gender, iodine intake, and uncertainty regarding the best setting of the TSH threshold for diagnosis.2 TSH acts on its receptors in the hepatocytes to stimulate the synthesis of cholesterol in the liver by upregulating the expression of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase, thereby increasing the levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-CH) and total cholesterol (T-CH), even when TSH levels are within the reference range.3 Secondary dyslipidemia caused by TSH is a substrate for lipid peroxidation, leading to the oxidative modification of LDL-CH particles, as shown by increases in the levels of thiobarbituric acid reactive substances/malondialdehyde (TBARS/MDA) and the presence of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL). The levels of ox-LDL are strongly related to the TSH levels, and more strongly in postmenopausal women and people with moderate SCH.3,4

Dyslipidemia appears to be an important mediator of oxidative stress in SCH because adjusting for T-CH often diminishes the TSH–TBARS relationship. Ox-LDL enhances the adhesion of monocytes, induces the formation of foam cells, and contributes to the inflammation of blood vessels. These processes increase the likelihood of subclinical atherosclerosis, which is correlated with a higher TSH level in SCH.5 The relationship between ox-LDL markers and the thickness of the carotid intima-media suggests that mild SCH may be associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease. 8-Hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) is a common marker of oxidative stress and an indicator of DNA damage. Overt hypothyroidism is associated with high urinary 8-OHdG levels, but similar findings have not been demonstrated in SCH. Some studies have shown that urinary 8-OHdG levels are consistently higher in women with overt hypothyroidism than euthyroid women, and that the 8-OHdG levels do not decrease after restoring euthyroidism by levothyroxine (LT4) therapy.5,6

Chronic oxidative damage may also be present in SCH, although possibly to a lesser extent. DNA damage indicated by 8-oxo-7,8-dihydro-2′-deoxyguanosine is apparent as persistent DNA lesions that cause G→T transversions. These mutations accumulate over time in an oncogene or tumor suppressor gene, thereby contributing to the induction of malignancy. In the case of tumor suppressor genes, they even lead to methylation of the promoter to cause a mutation that is epigenetically modified and malign.7

The thyroid gland is particularly susceptible to oxidative stress because thyroid hormone production depends on hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). Chronic oxidative exposure, specifically the activation of dual oxidase and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH)oxidase 4, has been associated with early thyroid carcinogenesis, genomic instability, and dedifferentiation.8 Studies have shown that the risk of thyroid cancer is significantly greater in patients with TSH ≈1.64 mIU/L, i.e., thyroid cancer has a higher occurrence (adjusted or ≈ 3.38). SCH is also associated with an increased risk of colorectal cancer and higher cancer mortality.9 Among the various markers of oxidative stress, the most reliable is MDA, which is a byproduct of lipid peroxidation. Significantly higher levels of MDA are found in the serum of patients with SCH compared with euthyroid controls.10 In both overt hypothyroidism and SCH, increases are found in protein oxidation and MDA, reflecting macromolecular damage.11 The total antioxidant status (TAS) is a biomarker that reflects the cumulative impact of all antioxidants present in plasma and other body fluids, comprising endogenous substances (e.g., uric acid, bilirubin, and glutathione) and dietary antioxidants (e.g., vitamins C and E, and polyphenols).12

The total oxidant status (TOS) is used to evaluate the overall oxidative stress level in the body, usually by measuring the oxidation of ferrous ions relative to their ferric forms by reactive oxygen and nitrogen species.13 The oxidative stress index (OSI), defined as the TOS/TAS ratio, is an integrative biomarker of oxidative stress, and an indicator of the interplay between oxidative and antioxidative systems.14 Changes in thyroid hormones can alter the mitochondrial respiratory chain in a natural way and lead to the increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Excessive ROS levels can cause a state of redox imbalance characterized by increased lipid peroxidation and increased DNA oxidation, as well as adversely affecting the vascular endothelium and genetic material, even in the absence of clear deficiency of thyroid hormones.15,16

Early diagnosis of oxidative stress in SCH may prompt lifestyle or pharmaceutical interventions that reduce the risk of developing overt hypothyroidism, cardiovascular disease, and other metabolic complications. Oxidative stress provides information regarding metabolic abnormalities that may not be apparent based on thyroid hormone levels alone.4,10

Monitoring TOS and TAS facilitates a more comprehensive assessment of a patient's redox balance, which is critical for minimizing oxidative damage to lipids, proteins, and DNA.17 In the present study, we focused on the biomarkers of oxidative stress in SCH, including serum total oxidants, total antioxidants, products of lipid peroxidation (MDA and ox-LDL), and the levels of oxidatively modified DNA (8-OHdG), and compared them to those in euthyroid controls. Moreover, we evaluated the effects of LT4 replacement therapy on oxidative stress and DNA damage levels in SCH patients over a defined treatment period.

Materials and Methods

Study design and participants

This case–control study was conducted at the Diabetes and Endocrine Center, Azadi Teaching Hospital, Duhok, Kurdistan Region, Iraq, from June 2024 to February 2025. Participants were assigned to four groups: (G1) a control group of 80 euthyroid participants with normal TSH levels (<4.0 mIU/L), normal free thyroxine (FT4), and anti-thyroid peroxidase antibodies (anti-TPO) levels ≤34 IU/mL; (G2) a group of 44 participants who had previously been treated for SCH and received LT4 therapy for at least one year before the study began; (G3) a group of 105 participants who had recently been diagnosed with SCH; and (G4) a group of 20 follow-up participants from the recently diagnosed group (G3) who met the study criteria due to having received LT4 for 6–12 weeks.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants in the present study were aged between 16 and 50 years. The participants in group G3 were recruited during their first visit to the Diabetes and Endocrine Center at Azadi Teaching Hospital if they were diagnosed with SCH (also known as compensated hypothyroidism) based on contemporary criteria: elevated serum TSH levels (moderate SCH at 4–9.9 mIU/L or severe SCH ≥10 mIU/L) with normal serum FT4 levels within the laboratory reference range (age- and trimester-specific where applicable).18,19 Thyroid function tests were repeated 6–12 weeks later, confirming persistent TSH elevation and stable normal FT4, and only these patients were included, none of whom had prior LT4 or other thyroid therapy.20 The following criteria were used for exclusion: pregnant or lactating women, smoking, history of thyroid surgery or treatment with radioactive iodine, alcohol use disorder, or use of medications that influence thyroid function or oxidative stress (including antioxidants and lipid-lowering medications). In addition, individuals with acute or chronic inflammatory conditions or cancer were excluded from the current study.

Sample collection and biochemical analysis

Fasting blood samples were collected from each participant. These samples were then centrifuged for 10 min at 3000 rpm. Subsequently, TSH, FT4, anti-TPO, and lipid profiles (triglyceride (TG), T-CH, LDL-CH, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and very low-density lipoprotein cholesterol) were analyzed using a COBAS 6000 auto-analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). Roche diagnostics kits were also used at the JEEN Specialized Medical Complex. Aliquots of the remaining serum samples were stored in Eppendorf tubes at −20 °C for future analyses, including measuring oxidative DNA damage markers and lipid peroxidation markers.

TOS was measured calorimetrically using Erel's method. In this method, oxidants convert a ferrous ion–o-dianisidine complex into ferric ions, and these ions produce a colored complex with xylenol orange. The results were expressed in terms of proton equivalents and calibrated against H2O2 (μmol H2O2 Eq/L).13 TAS was measured using Erel's method, where the suppression of Fe2+–o-dianisidine oxidation was expressed as Trolox equivalents.21 OSI was calculated as the ratio of TOS relative to TAS.14 Oxidative damage markers, such as MDA, ox-LDL, and 8-OHdG, were measured using sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits.

Anthropometric measurements

Systolic and diastolic blood pressures were measured in mmHg with a mercury sphygmomanometer. Body weight and height were measured using standard procedures, and the body mass index (BMI) was calculated using the equation: weight (kg)/height (m2). Waist circumference was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a nonelastic measuring tape located at the midpoint between the lower rib border and the iliac crest while participants were standing.

Statistical analysis

The mean and standard deviation or n (%) was used to represent the general and medical characteristics of SCH patients and healthy controls. Homogeneity was tested for the general and medical characteristics of the SCH group and healthy controls with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Pearson's chi-squared test. Differences in biomedical markers between SCH patients and euthyroid controls were tested with one-way ANOVA, and pairwise differences with Tukey's test. For SCH patients and euthyroid controls, Pearson's correlation coefficients were calculated to examine the relationships between oxidative stress and DNA damage biomarkers, and biomedical characteristics and DNA damage markers. The effects of oxidative stress biomarkers on SCH were compared with controls using binomial logistic regression. The threshold for statistical significance was set to p < 0.05. Statistical calculations were conducted using JMP, Version 18.0 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA, 1989–2023).

Power sample analysis

Considering the sample sizes reported in previous studies22 of between 15 and 46 cases and between 15 and 78 controls, we aimed to recruit the mean number of cases and controls in these ranges. Following months of data collection, we recruited 80 euthyroid controls, 44 previously treated cases, 105 newly diagnosed cases, and 20 newly diagnosed cases undergoing follow-up treatment.

Discussion

SCH is recognized as a state of minor but clinically significant metabolic and oxidative imbalance. SCH with normal thyroid function is characterized by high serum TSH, which is accompanied by increased ROS generation, compromised antioxidant systems, and systemic oxidative insults. In this case, oxidative stress is accompanied by the generation of stress markers, such as 8-OHdG, ox-LDL, and OSI, as well as a decrease in TAS. In SCH patients, metabolic dysregulation can trigger or worsen the already existing endothelial dysfunction, oxidative DNA injury, and early stages of atherogenesis.

In this study, circulating ox-LDL levels were significantly elevated in G2, G3, and G4. Multivariate analysis identified ox-LDL as an independent predictor of SCH, suggesting that the oxidative modification of lipoproteins may be enhanced in individuals with SCH. Accumulating evidence indicates that ox-LDL serves as a biomarker of oxidative stress but also as a biologically active mediator of cardiometabolic disorders.23

On a mechanistic level, ox-LDL interacts with scavenger receptors, such as cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36) and lectin-like oxidized low-density lipoprotein receptor-1 (LOX-1), thereby promoting the uptake of lipids by macrophages, foam cell formation, dysfunction of the endothelium, and sustained inflammation of the vascular system.24 These mechanisms and processes are the fundamental focus for the development of atherosclerosis and could help to explain the increase in cardiovascular disease in patients with SCH. Consequently, the oxidative alteration of LDL-CH particles is probably an important mechanistic link associated with thyroid dysfunction and the atherogenic changes seen in blood vessels. According to Cheserek et al.,10 the levels of ox-LDL increased by variable amounts during follow-up after LT4 treatment, whereas the changes in MDA were minimal across groups. These findings suggest that MDA may only partially reflect lipid peroxidation and might not be the dominant pathway in SCH. Therefore, multiple oxidative stress markers are needed to better characterize redox changes and disease progression.10

The TOS and OSI values recorded in the SCH groups showed that there were great differences in the redox state and possibly an abundance of ROS with little or no supporting antioxidant mechanisms. Among the study groups, OSI was most elevated in G3, indicating elevated oxidative stress, or greater manifestation of the disease process in this stage of thyroid dysfunction.

In G4, the participants had been treated with LT4 for 6–12 weeks at dosages of 25 μg/day, but the redox balance might not yet have been fully restored, or a persistent altered metabolic state associated with SCH could still have been present.25,26

The previously described oxidative stress findings were demonstrated by the decreased amounts of TAS in G2 and G4 compared with G1. The fact that the TAS values were lower in G2 and G4 indicates that the body's internal defense to fight off oxidative damage was not functioning correctly, thereby making tissues more susceptible to damage. Thus, the combination of higher oxidant measurements and lower TAS amounts showed that the oxidative environment was deteriorating in the SCH patients.27 Consistent with these biochemical findings, multivariate analysis showed that compared with the euthyroid subjects, the oxidative stress-related variables for SCH patients had the most significant levels of predictive power, with excess levels of ox-LDL, TAS, TOS, and OSI. Thus, inflammation was present in SCH patients and probably a driving factor in thyroid dysfunction.

Despite the absence of clinical symptoms, ongoing oxidative DNA damage was detected in G3 and G4, which is particularly concerning given the correlation of 8-OHdG with genomic instability, cellular senescence, and chronic degenerative illnesses, including cardiovascular and metabolic diseases.6 In patients with SCH who underwent LT4 treatment for 6–12 weeks at a dosage of 25 μg/day, TSH had significant inverse correlations with oxidative stress markers, suggesting that the biochemical normalization of TSH with LT4 treatment does not result in complete resumption of oxidant–antioxidant homeostasis. Therefore, the interpretation of these results should be based on a consideration of the time that elapsed since treatment initiation. LT4-related oxidative stress may arise from the metabolic activity induced by the increased levels of thyroid hormones that are substituted, or from ongoing autoimmune and inflammatory processes. Therefore, oxidative stress evaluation may provide valuable markers of the metabolic status of patients with treated SCH, in addition to thyroid function tests.28,29 Autoimmune thyroiditis indicated by positive anti-TPO antibodies was found in 17.5% of euthyroid control subjects, which is consistent with previous reports of subclinical autoimmunity in 10–15% of the general population.30,31

Multivariate analysis identified family history and anti-TPO positivity as the strongest independent predictors of SCH, highlighting the role of thyroid autoimmunity in disease development. A higher BMI was also significantly associated with SCH, consistent with the known effects of thyroid hormones on metabolic rate and body weight regulation. Furthermore, the relationship between SCH and lipid metabolism supports previous reports that thyroid dysfunction can affect lipoprotein handling, but not all lipid parameters had independent associations in the present model. Overall, these findings suggest that autoimmune markers and family history are more useful than isolated lipid variables for identifying SCH risk.32

In the present study, the increased ox-LDL, 8-OHdG, TOS, and OSI levels in patients with SCH may reflect oxidative stress, and could be useful for risk assessment and monitoring the response to hormonal replacement therapy. This clinical case–control study has many merits. First, the measurement of several oxidative stress indicators (ox-LDL, 8-OHdG, TOS, OSI, and TAS) simultaneously provided a better understanding of the redox equilibrium in SCH. Second, oxidative imbalance did not resolve after biochemical correction according to the comparisons between newly diagnosed, previously treated, and follow-up patients. Third, the combination of oxidative stress markers with anthropometric, biochemical, and hormonal data helped to provide a better understanding of the underlying mechanisms. Fourth, the presence of anti-TPO antibodies in the euthyroid controls highlights the relevance of subclinical autoimmunity in oxidative pathology.

Limitations

The present study also had some limitations. First, due to the case–control design, we were unable to make causal conclusions with respect to oxidative stress, lipid disorders, and the progression of SCH. Second, the small sample sizes, especially in particular groups (e.g., G4, n = 20), might have lowered the statistical power and generalizability. Third, the lack of time-dependent monitoring of oxidative stress markers or long-term evaluations after LT4 limited our understanding of the treatment effects.

Conclusion

In contrast to the euthyroid controls, SCH patients exhibited severe oxidative imbalance, possibly due to the higher levels for BMI, body weight, diastolic blood pressure, and anti-TPO antibodies. This was most severe in newly diagnosed patients, who had high ox-LDL, TOS, 8-OHdG, and OSI levels, and low TAS levels. These correlations between metabolism and oxidation demonstrate that thyroid dysfunction is associated with lipid dysregulation and its metabolic impact.
