Physically motivated extrapolation for quantum error mitigation
Pablo Díez-Valle, Gaurav Saxena, Jack S. Baker, Jun-Ho Lee, Thi Ha Kyaw

Abstract
Quantum error mitigation techniques are essential for the current NISQ and emerging Megaquop-era machines, which, despite their noise, are capable of performing utility-scale quantum computations. However, most QEM methods incur exponential sampling overhead to achieve unbiased estimates, limiting their practical applicability. Recently, it was shown that by using error mitigation by restricted evolution (EMRE), expectation values of a physical observable can be obtained in constant sampling overhead at the cost of a non-zero bias, which grows as the circuit size or hardware noise increases. To overcome this problem, we introduce the Physics-Inspired Extrapolation (PIE) method, built upon the EMRE framework, to achieve enhanced accuracy and robustness. Unlike traditional zero-noise extrapolation, our method assigns operational interpretation to the parameters in the extrapolation function used in PIE. We demonstrate the efficacy of this method on IBMQ hardware and apply it to simulate 84-qubit quantum dynamics efficiently. Our technique yields accurate results with significantly smaller variance, establishing PIE as a practical and scalable error mitigation strategy for near-term and early fault-tolerant quantum computing.



The ubiquitous presence of noise in various quantum hardware modalities presents a fundamental obstacle in realizing an ultimate quantum machine capable of executing industry-relevant applications.
Examples of such applications include simulating FeMoco molecules
Lee et al. 2021
, modeling spin defects
Baker et al. 2024
, designing solid-state battery materials
Delgado et al. 2022
, optimizing solar cell design
Motlagh et al. 2024
, and accelerating drug discovery
Santagati et al. 2024
, tasks that remain intractable for even the current best classical computers
alley 2023
;
Lee et al. 2023
.

To overcome the above challenge and achieve fault tolerance in quantum computers, quantum error correction (QEC) emerges as the ultimate tool. QEC refers to a set of techniques that protect quantum information against errors arising due to both environmental effects (such as decoherence) and imperfect gate operations (such as random bit-flip error). QEC achieves this through the use of quantum error correction codes (QECCs), which are mathematical constructs that allow encoding of quantum information in a way that enables error detection (via syndrome measurement) and correction without destroying the encoded information.
These codes are fundamental to the advancement of long-distance quantum communication and the development of large-scale quantum computers.
However, the measured success of any QECC is dependent on the physical hardware meeting stringent fidelity requirements.
The error rate of quantum hardware or gate infidelity must fall below a specified threshold value, which itself depends on a number of parameters, including code selection, system architecture, and the specifics of the encoding and decoding protocols.
If hardware infidelity goes beyond such critical threshold, the error correction protocol might aggravate the overall error of the system.

Despite the promise of fault-tolerant quantum computing, today’s quantum processors remain constrained to the noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) regime
Preskill 2018
, characterized by a limited number of qubits and susceptibility to various noise sources. Implementing fully error-corrected quantum algorithms capable of outperforming classical computations remains infeasible due to prohibitive resource overheads, with each logical qubit requiring hundreds to thousands of physical qubits. Estimates suggest that large-scale fault-tolerant quantum computing will demand millions of physical qubits
Lee et al. 2021
, far exceeding the capabilities of current hardware, which is typically limited to a few hundred qubits. Nonetheless, significant progress has been made in the development of QECCs, highlighted by the recent achievement of the
breakeven point

Rozgonyi and Széchenyi 2023
;
Sivak et al. 2023
;
AI 2025
, where a logical qubit’s lifetime equals or exceeds that of the best physical qubits used in its construction. However, such demonstrations are currently restricted to single logical qubits, lacking the scalability necessary for algorithmic utility. Consequently, until fully fault-tolerant quantum systems are realized, we must shift our focus to alternative strategies for enhancing computational accuracy on existing hardware.

;

;

. Unlike QECCs, QEM techniques do not require the costly overhead of encoding logical qubits. Instead, they aim to suppress or eliminate the impact of noise after measurement, enabling more accurate estimation of quantum observables even within the limitations of current NISQ devices.
QEM techniques aim to reduce the impact of noise through post-processing of measurement outcomes, often leveraging knowledge of the noise characteristics to extrapolate towards error-free outcomes.
These methods are algorithmic schemes designed to reduce the bias (absolute error between the obtained and ideal results) in the expectation value, and as such do not have a universal or a unique definition.
Calculating expectation values is of utmost essence in the current NISQ era.
The NISQ era quantum processors are highly susceptible to noise both from environmental factors (e.g., dephasing or depolarizing noise) and hardware imperfections (e.g., random Pauli errors and inaccurate state preparation and measurement).
Currently, quantum processors are typically used along with classical computers to approximately solve classically hard or intractable problems using heuristic and metaheuristic approaches
Bharti et al. 2022
;
Cerezo et al. 2021
.
The approach usually requires a parametrized quantum circuit whose outputs (in the form of probabilities or expectation values) are then processed using classical computers to update the parameters
Farhi et al. 2014
;
Peruzzo et al. 2014
;
Wecker et al. 2015
;
McClean et al. 2016
;
Kyaw et al. 2023
.
However, because of limitations on the number of qubits, circuit depth, and the absence of error correction, the expectation values obtained from quantum circuits are often inaccurate.
QEM techniques are thus essential to obtain near-accurate (or approximate) expectation values.
Most of the error mitigation schemes rely on classical post-processing of the results obtained from quantum circuits to estimate the expectation value.
Among the most widely used QEM methods is zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE)
Li and Benjamin 2017b
;
Temme et al. 2017b
, where noise is treated as a parameter.
By systematically amplifying the noise in the quantum circuit, one can obtain a series of noisy expectation values and extrapolate them back to the zero-noise limit, thereby estimating the ideal result.
Another prominent method is probabilistic error cancellation (PEC)
Temme et al. 2017b
which provides unbiased estimates of the expectation value by using quasi-probabilistic decomposition of the gates in the circuit. However, the unbiased estimate is achieved using a sampling overhead that scales exponentially with the number of gates in the circuit, rendering it impractical for large circuits.

Although current noisy quantum hardware lack enough high-quality physical qubits to construct a sufficient number of logical qubits for algorithmic relevance, an intermediate “early fault-tolerant (EFT)” era is anticipated before full fault-tolerance is achieved. In this EFT era, processors may offer a few hundred to a few thousand logical qubits enabled by imperfect error correction schemes.
Noise will not be fully eliminated, and algorithms will still suffer performance losses. Consequently, error mitigation will remain crucial, complementing partial or limited error correction to mitigate against logical noise
Suzuki et al. 2022b
;
Dutkiewicz et al. 2025
.
This approach is expected to produce the highest possible performance using EFT devices.
Thus, error mitigation schemes that do not incur large computational overhead will play a pivotal role in maximizing efficiency and accuracy of the outcomes during the EFT era.

Here, we introduce two new error mitigation techniques based on the constant runtime Error Mitigation by Restricted Evolution (EMRE) protocol we recently proposed
Saxena and Kyaw 2024
.
The primary contribution of this manuscript is the development and experimental demonstration of the EMRE-inspired extrapolation, termed Physics-Inspired Extrapolation (PIE), on IBMQ devices, with a benchmarking against standard resource-efficient ZNE approaches.
We show that the PIE method exhibits three principal advantages (see Fig.
1
).
First, parameters in the extrapolation function used in PIE have a clear operational interpretation, which is often missing in conventional extrapolation-based mitigation schemes; second, the computational runtime scales linearly; and third, the associated estimation variance remains low.
We demonstrate that in the low-noise regime, PIE method provides accurate estimates of the expectation values using only linear runtime, making it a strong candidate for deployment on EFT quantum processors, where hardware resources and error rates remain significant constraints.
Additionally, we also developed a second error mitigation protocol, the inverse EMRE, which leverages hardware noise characterization to obtain the generalized quasi-probabilistic decomposition (GQPD) of each gate in the circuit to perform EMRE.
Owing to noise characterization and the computation of optimal GQPD, the inverse EMRE method has comparatively longer runtime and larger deviations than the PIE method, and so, we have presented the full discussion of the inverse EMRE method including its implementation and numerical and experimental results, in the supplementary information.

Ising model with transverse-field

To benchmark the PIE method against various ZNE methods, let us look at the dynamical simulation of the ubiquitous one-dimensional quantum Ising spin chain Hamiltonian with periodic boundary condition (
\hbar=1
throughout),

{H}=-\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\sigma^{x}_{i}\sigma^{x}_{i+1}-\sigma^{x}_{N-1}\sigma^{x}_{0}-\Gamma\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\sigma^{z}_{i}\,,

(1)

with
N
representing the total number of spins or qubits, and
\Gamma=0.5
being the transverse field strength.
The
\sigma
’s are the usual Pauli matrices.
The time-evolution is approximated via the first-order Suzuki-Trotter decomposition,

\left|\Psi(t)\right\rangle=e^{-i{H}t}\left|\Psi(0)\right\rangle\approx U_{R}\left|\Psi(0)\right\rangle\,,

(2)

with

U_{R}=\prod_{j=0}^{R}\left[e^{i(t/R)\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}\sigma^{x}_{i}\sigma^{x}_{i+1}}e^{i(t/R)\sigma^{x}_{N-1}\sigma^{x}_{0}}e^{i\Gamma(t/R)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma^{z}_{i}}\right]\,,

(3)

where
\left|\Psi(0)\right\rangle
is an initial state of the quantum system,
j
denotes each Trotter step, and the time-evolution operator is being decomposed into a total of
R
Trotter steps.
In the limit
R\rightarrow\infty
, the simulation becomes exact.

We are interested in estimating the global transverse field magnetization
M_{z}(t)
, defined as

M_{z}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\dfrac{{\rm Tr}[\sigma^{z}_{i}U_{R}|\Psi(0)\rangle\langle\Psi(0)|U_{R}^{\dagger}]}{N}\,,

(4)

given the noisy hardware and various QEM techniques at hand.
We compute its evolution at different times
t
, starting from an initial state in which all spins are aligned in the positive
\sigma_{z}
direction such that
M_{z}(0)=1
.
While the one-dimensional Ising model described in (
1
) admits analytical solutions for an arbitrary number of spins
Suzuki et al. 2013
;
Auerbach 1994
, our primary focus is not on the simulation error introduced by the Trotter approximation in (
2
). Instead, we focus on evaluating the performance of error mitigation algorithms when applied to actual hardware demonstrations and theoretical studies under realistic and commonly encountered noise models, such as depolarizing, dephasing, and random Pauli noises.
In this respect, the noise-free output of the Trotter circuit in (
2

We benchmark the PIE method against different extrapolation-based error mitigation techniques by deploying them to estimate the global transverse magnetization for different evolution times.
The noise amplification is carried out by
circuit folding

Giurgica-Tiron et al. 2020
, where the trotterized time-evolution operator (
3
) is replaced by

U_{R}\rightarrow U_{R}\left(U_{R}^{\dagger}U_{R}\right)^{n}\,,

(5)

where
n
is a non-negative integer that corresponds to the number of unitary circuit foldings.
This transformation does not modify the noise-free expectation values of any observable, while it allows us to amplify the noise in the quantum circuit.
Noise amplification is parameterized by the scaling factor
\lambda=1+2n
, which can intuitively be interpreted as the effective noise level corresponding to the number of folded circuit layers.
The
\lambda=0
case formally corresponds to the ideal, noise-free circuit.
The error mitigation approach consists of computing the expectation value of the observable at different noise levels

M_{z}(t;\lambda)=f(\lambda;c_{0},\ldots,c_{m})\,,

(6)

where
(c_{0},\ldots,c_{m})
are free model parameters that must be adjusted to fit the experimental data.
Then, by using a function
f

\lambda=0

1

The variance associated with this estimate is determined from the variance of the fitting parameters.

Discussion

The proposed PIE technique offers a physical interpretation to the experimental data fitting process and its associated parameters, a feature that is often absent in extrapolation-based mitigation techniques.
Unlike purely phenomenological data fitting approaches, PIE leverages a noise model derived from the underlying quantum hardware based on the idea of EMRE without actually characterizing hardware noise.
Thus, it allows for the extraction of physically meaningful quantities that characterize the error mechanisms.
This fundamental aspect distinguishes EMRE-inspired extrapolation or PIE from an alternative error mitigation method such as ZNE that relies solely on empirical data fitting.

From an operational perspective, the PIE method offers efficient error mitigation with enhanced robustness and notably reduced variance compared to traditional non-linear exponential fitting techniques.
The improvement in stability and variance arises from the linear extrapolation model enabled by the restricted evolution framework of EMRE.
This linearity is especially advantageous in NISQ devices, where limited data and inherent noise fluctuations can significantly impact the reliability of standard fitting-based error mitigation techniques.
Consequently, the PIE method offers a more reliable and consistent approach to error mitigation, contributing to improved accuracy and reproducibility in quantum computations with noisy hardware.

Methods

Physics-Inspired Extrapolation (PIE)

Characterizing noise in physical hardware is a challenging and resource-intensive task.
Since error mitigation techniques aim to estimate the ideal expectation values, procedures that avoid any extra computational cost, such as those incurred from noise characterization, are generally preferred.
In this scheme of things, ZNE has proven to be very practical
Cai et al. 2023
.
ZNE works by systematically increasing the circuit fault rate, getting the erroneous expectation value for those fault rates, and then extrapolating the expectation values to the zero-noise limit.
To enhance circuit fault rate to implement ZNE, one of the widely used methods is through circuit folding, where identities are added to the circuit, increasing the length of the circuit and systematically increasing noise
Giurgica-Tiron et al. 2020
;
Majumdar et al. 2023
.
For example, if we assume that the whole circuit is represented by a unitary operator
U
, then folding the circuit once would mean that we implement the circuit
U\circ U^{\dagger}\circ U
which, in ideal non-erroneous circumstances, should yield the same result as the original case.
However, due to the faulty nature of the gates, circuit folding increases the errors in the circuit.
By folding the circuit multiple times, we can get erroneous results as a function of the circuit fault rate or the number of circuit foldings.
The expectation values obtained in the procedure are then extrapolated to the zero-noise limit.

An uncertainty that ZNE presents is in the choice of the extrapolation function.
For example, in
Li and Benjamin 2017b
a linear extrapolation function was employed to approximate the ideal expectation value, while
Temme et al. 2017b
demonstrated that the ideal expectation value can be approximated using a polynomial function for small circuit fault rates, whereas
Endo et al. 2018b
showed that exponential extrapolation offers improved bias compared to other extrapolation techniques. Other extrapolation functions used for error mitigation can be found in the review article
Cai et al. 2023
.
In addition, for extrapolations going beyond Richardson extrapolation, there are no analytical expressions for bias or variance in most cases
Cai et al. 2023
.
Furthermore, no operational meaning has been given to the extrapolation methods. Intuitive explanations on why the exponential extrapolation method works have been provided, but no solid rigorous explanation/reasoning has been given in regards to which exponential function should be used for extrapolation.

Here, inspired by the extrapolation-based techniques to perform error mitigation, we introduce a novel approach that uses EMRE to better estimate the expectation value without requiring explicit knowledge of the underlying hardware noise.
We use the theory of EMRE along with circuit folding to rigorously find the extrapolation function that enables accurate prediction of the ideal (noiseless) expectation value.
We use this EMRE-inspired extrapolation, referred to as PIE (physics inspired extrapolation) method, to estimate the expectation values and validate its effectiveness through extensive numerical simulations and experimental demonstrations using the IBMQ machine.
We benchmark our results against standard ZNE methods using various extrapolation models, including linear, quadratic, and exponential fits.
We find that the expectation value estimate using our PIE method yields highly accurate results while avoiding the large standard deviations often observed with exponential ZNE.
Thus, EMRE combined with extrapolation provides a practically motivated and efficient error mitigation technique, bringing the quantum utility era closer to reality.
Below, we provide the details on how EMRE and circuit folding are used to construct the corresponding extrapolation function.

EMRE relies on the GQPD of an ideal gate and mitigates errors by restricting the decomposition to its positive component, thereby approximating the ideal operation
Saxena and Kyaw 2024
.
To achieve minimum bias, it is necessary to employ the optimal GQPD.
However, obtaining this optimal decomposition requires hardware noise characterization, which in itself is both a time-consuming and a resource-intensive process.
Even if a (not necessarily optimal) GQPD is known, EMRE can be implemented using only a constant sample overhead.
This constant sample overhead comes at the cost of a finite bias which grows exponentially with the circuit size.
Since our objective is to mitigate errors without incurring significant runtime or bias, we need to find a way to circumvent noise characterization.
In order to do that, we propose constructing a GQPD for each ideal gate (and thus for the ideal circuit) using the noisy gates. We show that it is always possible to construct a GQPD (not necessarily optimal) of the ideal unitary operation from erroneous ones using max-relative entropy.

The max-relative entropy between a unitary operation
\mathcal{U}
and another operation
\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U}
where
\mathcal{E}
denotes the noise channel, is given by

\displaystyle D_{\max}(\mathcal{U}\|\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U})

\displaystyle=\log\min\{s:s\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U}\geq\mathcal{U},\,s\geq 1\}

(21)

\displaystyle=\log\min\{s:sJ^{\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U}}\geq J^{\mathcal{U}},\,s\geq 1\}

(22)

where
J^{\mathcal{U}}:={\rm id}\otimes\mathcal{U}(\Phi^{+}_{\tilde{A}A})
represents the Choi matrix of
\mathcal{U}
,
\Phi^{+}_{\tilde{A}A}
is the unnormalized maximally entangled state on the system
\tilde{A}A
where
|\tilde{A}|=|A|
, and the inequality in the above equations implies that the matrix
sJ^{\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U}}-J^{\mathcal{U}}
is a positive semidefinite matrix.
Denoting
s_{\rm opt}
to be the max-relative entropy between the two operations, we can express the ideal operation
\mathcal{U}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}=s_{\rm opt}\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U}-(s_{\rm opt}-1)\mathcal{M}

(23)

where
\mathcal{M}
is some quantum channel.
Note that if
\mathcal{U}
denotes an ideal unitary circuit, then
\mathcal{E}
can be considered as the effective noise channel defined as
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}
where
\mathcal{F}
is a quantum channel representing the noisy circuit.
Interestingly, this scheme, although not optimal, offers a hidden advantage by not requiring to compute the GQPD for any gate in the circuit and using their erroneous implementations instead. In standard EMRE, the GQPD of all the gates in the circuit must be known, and the original circuit is replaced with a modified one sampled according to the GQPD associated with each gate.
Here, only the gates affected by noise are effectively approximated by their noisy counterparts. As a result, the overall product of the generalized robustness of all gates remains small, as we see from our experimental and numerical results, which further contributes to a reduced bias.

Given the above, it is clear that a GQPD can be constructed for an ideal unitary operation in terms of its erroneous version.
This proof of existence of a GQPD is enough to perform error mitigation as we describe below, and we need not find the optimal coefficient
s_{\rm opt}
or the channel
\mathcal{M}
.

Since EMRE works by approximating the ideal unitary operation to the positive part of its GQPD, we can approximate the expectation value of an observable, say
\mathcal{O}
as follows

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{\mathcal{U}}\approx s_{\rm opt}\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U}}

(24)

where
\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{\mathcal{U}}:={\rm Tr}[\mathcal{O}\mathcal{U}(|0\rangle\langle 0|)]
.
Now, to enhance the errors in the circuit, we apply global circuit folding.
We assume that the same noise acts throughout the circuit even after increasing the circuit depth, and note that the max relative entropy between
\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}
and
\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}
is the same as the max relative entropy between
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U}
.
So, after
n
circuit foldings, we can approximate the expectation value of
\mathcal{O}
as follows:

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{\mathcal{U}}=\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{\mathcal{U}\circ(\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\circ\mathcal{U})^{n}}\approx s_{\rm opt}^{(2n+1)}\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U}\circ(\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\circ\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U})^{n}}\,.

(25)

Now by rearranging the terms, we get the following general expression

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{\rm err}\approx\frac{\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{\rm ideal}}{s_{\rm opt}^{2n+1}}

(26)

where
\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{\rm err}
represents the erroneous expectation value after
n
circuit foldings and
\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{\rm ideal}
denotes the ideal expectation value.
Using the above formula, if we plot the erroneous expectation values against the number of circuit folding, we can extrapolate it back to the zero-noise case.
If we let
\lambda=2n+1
, and denote the experimental expectation values as a function of
\lambda
, i.e.,
f(\lambda)=\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{\rm err}
, we get the following extrapolation function to be used:

\displaystyle f(\lambda)=\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{\rm ideal}s^{-\lambda}\,,

(27)

where
\lambda=0
indicates the case without error. Further, taking the log on both the sides, we can cast the above expression as

\displaystyle\log\left(f\right)=\log\left(\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{\rm ideal}\right)-\lambda\log(s)\,,

(28)

which is a linear extrapolation between the logarithm of the obtained experimental values on the
y
-axis against the number of circuit folding on the
x
-axis.
Then, the intercept on the
y
-axis at
\lambda=0
gives us the logarithm of the ideal expectation value estimate.
The slope of the linear curve is the max relative entropy between the ideal and the erroneous operations.
If the expectation values are negative, then one can take the log of their absolute values, do the extrapolation, get the estimate and multiply the result by -1. We have demonstrated this in our quantum chemistry examples (see Fig.
5
).
Finally, let us note that, beyond full circuit folding, PIE
may benefit from the use of more accurate noise amplification techniques, such as partial folding, probabilistic error amplification
Kim et al. 2023
, or circuit quantum unoptimization
Pelofske and Russo 2025
.

Supplemental Information for “Physically motivated extrapolation for quantum error mitigation”

S1
Inverse EMRE technique

In this section, we discuss another way to implement EMRE on a quantum computer. We call it the inverse EMRE technique inspired from the inverse method to implement PEC.
We also report numerical and experimental results obtained by using this technique and compare the performance of EMRE and HEMRE implemented using this way with that of PEC. We also mention the challenges in using this method and why the PIE method is preferred over this implementation method.

S1.1
Method

Error mitigation by Restricted Evolution (EMRE) works by restricting the evolution of the input quantum state
Saxena and Kyaw 2024
.
The output obtained from this restricted evolution gives the estimate of the ideal expectation value.
EMRE trades off sampling efficiency with a small non-zero bias.
The restriction is performed in such a way that sampling the circuit is efficient.
In Ref.
Saxena and Kyaw 2024
, two of the authors of this paper introduced the concept of a generalized quasi-probabilistic decomposition (GQPD) for a unitary channel
\mathcal{U}
. This decomposition is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}=s\mathcal{B}-(s-1)\mathcal{N}\,,

(S1)

where
\mathcal{B}
is a convex combination of implementable operations and
\mathcal{N}
is some quantum channel.
By restricting the evolution of the input state to
\mathcal{B}
, that is, by ignoring the negative part of the above decomposition, the authors showed that error mitigated results can be obtained with an efficient constant sampling overhead.

The above protocol relies on obtaining a generalized QPD for each gate in the circuit.
The smallest bias is achieved when the generalized QPD used is optimal. In
Saxena and Kyaw 2024
, it has been shown that finding the optimal generalized QPD can be cast as an optimization problem.
This optimization problem requires a characterization of the noise in the hardware to characterize the set of noisy operations, making it challenging to obtain the optimal solution.
Thus, with unknown hardware noise, it seems difficult to practically implement and mitigate noise using EMRE.

To overcome the above implementation challenge, inspired from the inverse method to perform PEC, we develop the inverse EMRE method.
Let
G
denote an ideal gate to be implemented in a circuit.
After noise acts on the gate, the erroneous gate
G^{\rm err}
can be written as
G^{\rm err}=\mathcal{E}\circ G
where
\mathcal{E}
represents the noise acting after the gate.
In other words, the ideal gate can be expressed as
G=\mathcal{E}^{-1}\circ G^{\rm err}
.
Thus, in order to apply the ideal gate, we need to apply an extra channel that is close to
\mathcal{E}^{-1}
after the erroneous gate
G^{\rm err}
to reduce the effects of noise.

To apply
\mathcal{E}^{-1}
experimentally and perform error mitigation, we first need to characterize the noise.
We assume that the noise acting on the system is a random Pauli noise.
Using gate set tomography, we can construct the Pauli transfer matrix (PTM) for the erroneous gates.
Denoting the PTM of the ideal gate, the erroneous gate and the noise as
R^{G},R^{G^{\rm err}},
and
R^{\mathcal{E}}
, respectively, we can represent the PTM of noise
\mathcal{E}
as
R^{\mathcal{E}}=R^{G^{\rm err}}\circ{R^{G}}^{-1}
.
By experimentally computing the PTM of the erroneous gate
R^{G^{\rm err}}
, we can compute the PTM of noise using the given equation (since the PTM of the ideal gate is assumed to be known in theory).
Next, by keeping Paulis as the set of implementable operations, we can find a generalized QPD of
{R^{\mathcal{E}}}^{-1}
.
The generalized QPD of
{R^{\mathcal{E}}}^{-1}
will have the following form:

\displaystyle{R^{\mathcal{E}}}^{-1}=s\sum_{i}p_{i}R^{P_{i}}-(s-1)R^{\mathcal{M}}\,,

(S2)

where
p_{i}
represents the probabilities of the occurrence of Pauli
P_{i}
.
To perform EMRE, we can restrict the above decomposition to the positive part and approximate the ideal gate as follows:

\displaystyle G\approx s\left(\sum_{i}p_{i}P_{i}\right)\circ G^{\rm err}\,.

(S3)

Practically, the above equation implies that we sample the Paulis from the probability distribution and apply them after the erroneous gate instance and multiply the final result by
s
.

S1.2
Numerical analysis

In the first place, the PTM representation
R^{\mathcal{E}^{-1}}
of the inverse noise channel
\mathcal{E}^{-1}
is estimated for each implemented single- and two-qubit gate.
This is done by computation of the PTM representation of the ideal
G
and erroneous
G^{\rm err}
such that
{R^{\mathcal{E}}}^{-1}=R^{G}\circ{R^{G^{\rm err}}}^{-1}
, where

R^{\Lambda}=\dfrac{1}{2^{N}}{\rm Tr}\left[P_{i}\Lambda(P_{j})\right]

(S4)

with
\Lambda
being the one- (
N=1
) or two-qubit (
N=2
) quantum channels
{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}
,
G
or
G^{\rm err}
, and
P_{i}
are the elements is the
N
-qubit Pauli basis (
\{I,X,Y,Z\}
for
N=1
and
\{II,IX,IY,IZ,XI,XX,XY,XZ,YI,YX,YY,YZ,ZI,ZX,ZY,ZZ\}
for
N=2
).
In Fig.
S6
and Fig.
S7
we show some examples of erroneous gate PTMs,
R^{G^{\rm err}}
, and ideal gate PTMs,
R^{G}
, obtained from numerical simulations and Eq. (
S4
).
Assuming random Pauli noise, the PTM representation of the inverse noise channel
{R^{\mathcal{E}}}^{-1}
together with the PTM representation of erroneous Pauli gates (where
\Lambda=P_{i}
) allows for the calculation of a GQPD
\{q^{G}_{i}\}
by solving an optimization problem as given in Ref.
Saxena and Kyaw 2024
, so that
{R^{\mathcal{E}}}^{-1}\approx\sum_{i}q^{G}_{i}P_{i}
and
\sum_{i}q^{G}_{i}=1
.
Recall that a QPD is also a GQPD.
In Fig.
S8
we show examples of QPDs of the inverse noise operations associated with the single-qubit X gate and the two-qubit controlled-NOT gate in numerical simulations.

Now, to implement the inverse EMRE technique, we restrict the QPD to its positive part.
The normalized coefficients of the positive part represent the probabilities
\{p^{G}_{i}=q^{G}_{i}/s^{G}|q^{G}_{i}>0\}_{i}
of the occurrence of Pauli gates
\{P_{i}\}_{i}
such that each ideal quantum gate can be approximated by sampling from the
N
-qubit Pauli basis
\{P_{i}\}
with probabilities
p^{G}_{i}
as depicted in Eq. (
S3
).
The robustness
s^{G}
of the gate
G
is the sum of positive coefficients of the QPD, i.e.,
s=\sum_{i}q^{G}_{i}
.
In practice, a number of circuits
K
is sampled where the gates are probabilistically chosen following Eq. (
S3
), the expectation value of the observable of interest is computed for each sampled circuit
\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{k}
with
k\in[1,2,...,K]
, and the mitigated estimate is calculated as the average of those expectation values,

\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{\textnormal{mitigated}}=\frac{s}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{k}\;\;,

(S5)

where
s=\prod_{G}s^{G}
. The inverse technique to perform Probabilistic Error Cancellation (PEC) follows similarly with
\{p^{(PEC)G}_{i}\}=\{|q^{G}_{i}|\}
and
s^{(PEC)G}=\sum_{i}p^{(PEC)G}_{i}
. Contrary to EMRE, PEC provides an unbiased result provided a sufficiently large number of circuit samples, however, it has been shown that EMRE achieves more accurate estimates in a scenario with reduced number of samples.
In
Saxena and Kyaw 2024
, the Hybrid Error Mitigation By Restricted Evolution (HEMRE) is also introduced, where only a subset of gates is restricted to positive QPDs. In particular, we consider an HEMRE protocol where
\{p^{(HEMRE)G}_{i}\}=\{q^{G}_{i}|q^{G}_{i}>0\}
if
G
is a single-qubit gate,
\{p^{(HEMRE)G}_{i}\}=\{|q^{G}_{i}|\}
if
G
is a two-qubit gate, and
s^{(HEMRE)G}=\sum_{i}p^{(HEMRE)G}_{i}
.

As done in the manuscript, we tested the previous mitigation protocols on the time dynamics simulation of the one-dimensional quantum Ising spin chain Hamiltonian with periodic boundary condition (
\hbar=1
throughout),

\hat{H}=-\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\sigma^{x}_{i}\sigma^{x}_{i+1}-\sigma^{x}_{N-1}\sigma^{x}_{0}-\Gamma\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\sigma^{z}_{i}\,,

(S6)

with
N
the number of qubits, and
\Gamma=0.5
the transverse field strength. The dynamics is approximated via the first-order Trotter-Suzuki decomposition using one Trotter step to compute the global transverse magnetization at time
t=0.5
. We test the three protocols using the described inverse technique with a constant number of sampled circuits
K=1200
. In Figure
S9
and
S10
we show the numerical and experimental results respectively. Note that the time between the QPD calculation and the sampling must be as small as possible to reduce errors coming from physical fluctuations occurring in the quantum computer.

We observe that this technique leads to approximation errors, notable computational overhead due to QPD or GQPD calculation and circuit sampling, and a large bias arising from the large value of robustness
s
, which motivates the use of more efficient implementations of EMRE such as the physics-inspired extrapolation (PIE) method presented in the manuscript.

.
