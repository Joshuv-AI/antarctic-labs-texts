Abstract
. The growing influence of Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems on decision-making in critical domains has exposed their potential to cause significant harms, often rooted in biases embedded across the AI lifecycle. While existing frameworks and taxonomies document bias or harms in isolation, they rarely establish systematic links between specific bias types and the harms they cause, particularly within real-world sociotechnical contexts. Technical fixes proposed to address AI biases are ill-equipped to address them and are typically applied after a system has been developed or deployed, offering limited preventive value. We propose ECHO , a novel framework for proactive AI harm anticipation through the systematic mapping of AI bias types to harm outcomes across diverse stakeholder and domain contexts. ECHO follows a modular workflow encompassing stakeholder identification, vignette-based presentation of biased AI systems, and dual (human–LLM) harm annotation, integrated within ethical matrices for structured interpretation. This human-centered approach enables early-stage detection of bias-to-harm pathways, guiding AI design and governance decisions from the outset. We validate ECHO in two high-stakes domains (disease diagnosis and hiring), revealing domain-specific, bias-to-harm patterns and demonstrating ECHO ’s potential to support anticipatory governance of AI systems.

2.1.
AI Harms taxonomies

Understanding the potential harms arising from AI technology is essential towards anticipating and preventing real-world consequences. Mapping how harms materialize through the interaction of technological mechanisms with social and cultural contexts enables practitioners to anticipate harms early and incorporate safeguards to minimize them
(
74
)
. Recent research has proposed various harm taxonomies to systematically identify and classify these systems’ effects
(
74
;
28
;
83
;
36
;
29
;
34
)
. Many existing frameworks, however, have a limited scope: some concentrate on specific technologies (e.g., LLMs)
(
83
;
36
)
, others focus on particular application domains (e.g., healthcare, privacy)
(
28
;
29
)
, and some are tailored to specific audiences (e.g., policy analysis
(
34
)
), thereby limiting their generalizability.

In this work, we adopt the prominent and highly cited Shelby et al.
(
74
)
harms taxonomy which provides broad coverage of known harms across multiple contexts and serves as a practical reference for both developers and practitioners. This taxonomy distinguishes four primary harm types, along with a fifth, that includes more systemic harm types
(
74
)
:

•

Allocative harms
occur when individuals lose access to information, opportunities, or essential supports (e.g., employment, housing, healthcare, finance). These include
opportunity loss
and
economic loss
.

•

Quality-of-service harms
arise when systems perform inconsistently or inadequately based on users’ identity characteristics (e.g. race, gender, disability, or ethnicity).
This can cause
alienation
, require
increased labor
to use the system, or lead to
lost services or benefits
.

•

Interpersonal harms
occur when algorithmic systems adversely shape relationships between individuals or communities, often by undermining autonomy, dignity, or emotional well-being. They include experiences such as
loss of agency or control
,
privacy violations
, and
technology-facilitated violence
or impacts on users’
health and well-being
.

•

Representational harms
result when systems
reinforce stereotypes
,
demean groups
or even
erase their identities
. These harms shape how
groups
are perceived and valued by both AI systems and broader society, contributing to cultural exclusion and
alienation
. Specific dimensions of representational harm also include
denying people the opportunity to self-identify
, and
reifying essentialist social categories
.

•

Social system harms
emerge at the macro level and refer to broad, systemic effects such as the reinforcement of institutional inequities or environmental degradation. These harms arise through the interaction of algorithmic systems with existing power structures and long-term social dynamics.

Categorizing harms, however, is not sufficient on its own, since preventing AI harms requires prompt understanding of their origins
(
70
;
15
)
. Biases arising at different stages of the AI lifecycle have been widely recognized as a major source of harm
(
25
;
9
;
77
;
71
)
. These biases encode, propagate, and amplify existing inequities, ultimately shaping the kinds of harms that emerge across application domains and usage contexts
(
15
)
. Therefore, minimizing AI harms demands a comprehensive examination of biases as underlying sources and mechanisms throughout the entire AI lifecycle.

2.2.
AI bias in a project’s lifecycle: Types and mitigation pathways

AI systems can exhibit computaional (AI) biases that distort model behaviour and lead to unfair and harmful outcomes. AI biases rise at different stages of the ML lifecycle, during data collection, model design, or deployment, and have been widely examined in the literature on algorithmic fairness in AI
(
77
;
55
;
81
;
25
;
46
)
. Building on these studies, we focus on five foundational bias types that are the most prominent and widely studied in the literature
(
78
)
:

•

Representation bias
arises from data quality issues when datasets fail to adequately capture the diversity of the population, often excluding underrepresented groups and thereby limiting a model’s generalizability. Closely related,
historical bias
reflects the pre-existing inequities embedded in data-generating processes: even if data are sampled flawlessly, the underlying social and institutional patterns they mirror may already privilege some groups while disadvantaging or excluding others
(
77
;
71
;
81
;
16
)
.

•

Measurement bias
arises from the use of features or labels as proxies for complex constructs when these proxies either poorly capture the intended construct or are computed inconsistently across groups, leading to inaccurate or misleading outputs
(
46
;
77
;
41
)
.

•

Algorithmic bias
results from model design choices (e.g., architecture, functions, or hyperparameters) that inadvertently produce uneven error rates across different groups, especially those underrepresented in the data
(
81
;
35
)
.

•

Evaluation bias
occurs when benchmark datasets used to assess model performance are not representative of the intended user population, or when inappropriate performance metrics are applied, leading to unnoticed or uncorrected disparities
(
46
;
81
)
.

•

Deployment bias
arises when a model is used in a different context than it was designed for, often shifting from assistive to autonomous use, which can lead to misuse and overreliance on the system’s outputs
(
77
;
43
)
.

Numerous approaches have been proposed to minimize AI harms by tackling bias at different stages of the lifecycle. Technical interventions, including fairness toolkits
(
12
;
13
;
22
)
and de-biasing methods such as data re-sampling and re-weighting
(

;
18
;
35
)
provide useful mechanisms to detect and mitigate bias but remain largely confined to statistical operations within the ML pipeline or rest on unrealistic assumptions
(
35
)
.
Fairness metrics
usually reduce complex sociotechnical harms to narrow statistical measures
(
62
;
55
)
. Process-oriented efforts have sought to improve validation and evaluation practices, yet they often emphasize accuracy over fairness, overlooking real-world disparities and sociotechnical harms
(
76
)
. While these approaches have advanced the field, most are applied post hoc (when harmful effects have already manifested and resources have been committed
(
70
;
15
)
) or rely solely on technical interventions that fail to capture the complexity of human behavior and decision-making. Yet, harms in AI systems emerge through intricate sociotechnical processes, deeply rooted in social relations, institutional practices, and contextual norms, dimensions that purely technical fixes cannot address
(
25
;
74
;

;
70
;

.

In response, recent work has shifted toward
anticipatory strategies
that aim to identify potential harms early in the development process

. Such approaches require those involved in the design, development, and oversight of AI systems

2.3.
Human-Centered analytical structures: Ethical Matrices and Vignettes

A growing body of work emphasizes the importance of
human-centered analytical structures
for systematically reasoning about the harms of AI systems
(
30
;
15
;
70
;
84
)
. Among these,
ethical matrices

(
56
)
have been proposed as a structured way to identify and organize potential stakeholder concerns. Originally developed to assess biotechnologies in food production, EMs have since been adapted for use in data science contexts, demonstrating how structured representations can support systematic reflection during system design and deployment
(
30
;
15
)
. They typically map problematic system behaviors or design factors -such as the use of non-representative data or bad proxies- (depicted in columns) against relevant affected stakeholders (depicted in rows), with potential concerns or harms represented in the cells. This structure provides a clear overview of
who
might be affected,
how
, and by
which
technical decisions that lead to problematic system behaviors (e.g. biases).

The
who
dimension concerns the diverse roles and social positions of stakeholders affect or/and are affected by AI systems
(
57
;
74
;
72
)
. Given the far-reaching consequences of many AI-based incidents
(
53
;
3
)
, to address the moral and ethical concerns arising with AI systems, the stakeholder perspective should identify a broad range of stakeholders when envisioning possible harms
(
15
;
21
)
, especially the passive ones
(
57
;
82
)
.
Passive stakeholders
are those who may be substantially affected by a system but do not actively contribute to its development or operation; they include decision subjects, vulnerable groups, and local communities.
Active stakeholders
, by contrast, are those who function as moral agents that specify, build, deploy, or operate AI systems and thereby influence their outcomes. They include
clients
such as operating organisations and platform owners,
designers
such as data analysts, data engineers, software developers, and practitioners, as well as
decision-makers
such as end users and program managers
(
57
;
82
)
. These asymmetric positions in the stakeholder landscape shape who is exposed to which harms
(
57
;
15
;
64
;
70
;
21
)
.

The
how
dimension has been addressed through structured reflection and
envisioning methods
, which prompt those involved in system design to imagine potential harm scenarios in advance
(
15
;
8
)
. Recent work leverages large language models (LLMs) to support this process by surfacing harms that even experienced developers might overlook
(
15
;
86
;
14
;
67
)
. Yet LLMs alone cannot capture the contextual nuance and lived experiences of stakeholders, so crowdsourced evaluations can be combined with LLM methods to enable a human-in-the-loop approach to harm anticipation
(

;
15
;
58
)
.

Regarding the
which
dimension, problematic system behaviors (biased behaviors in our case) must be clearly defined, as they provide the basis for both crowd annotators and LLMs to envision potential downstream effects. In the social sciences,
vignettes
-short, structured, often hypothetical scenarios- are commonly used to elicit judgments about complex situations
(
10
)
. Vignette-based methods are increasingly applied to AI to examine how people perceive and reason about potential impacts

;

;

)
. The

shapes each vignette’s salience and severity: similar biases can have very different consequences depending on the context in which they occur (e.g., medical diagnosis vs. news recommendation)

. By embedding biased behaviors within realistic domain and stakeholder contexts, vignettes act as reflective instruments able to surface potential harms.

Together, these human-centered structures provide a systematic foundation for reasoning about
who
may be harmed,
how
, and by
which
system behaviors that lead to harm. Building on these structures, the following section outlines the

3.
ECHO: Preliminaries and Framework Overview

This section formally defines the fundamental entities and the computational workflow of the proposed framework. Based on the previous section, we now define a sequence of procedural functions that specify how ECHO transforms structured inputs into stakeholder-centered ethical matrices. We begin by summarizing the notation used throughout the framework, and then present a formal proposition that frames the problem that underpins ECHO’s objective of harm anticipation. Next, we describe each phase of the workflow as a sequence of well-defined steps: stakeholder generation, vignette construction, harms evaluation, and ethical matrix construction. Finally, we present the algorithmic logic of ECHO in pseudocode, which integrates all steps into a coherent algorithmic workflow.

The following proposition formally establishes the mapping problem addressed by
ECHO
. The rest of this section defines how procedural functions transform structured inputs into ethical matrices that encode domain and stakeholder -specific, bias-harm relationships.

Proposition 1:

Given an application domain
d\in\mathcal{D}
,
a list of bias types
\mathcal{B}
,
and a list of harm types
\mathcal{H}
,
we can construct a domain-specific ethical matrix
EM^{d}

that systematically encodes the mapping

(s_{k},b_{m},d)\mapsto EM^{d}_{k,m}
.
In this matrix, rows correspond to stakeholders,
columns to bias types, and the entries capture the aggregated set of harms
that are most likely to arise for a given stakeholder
s_{k}

when exposed to an AI system exhibiting bias
b_{m}
within domain
d
.

Proof Sketch.

We support Proposition 1 through the implementation and empirical application of the
ECHO
framework. Specifically, for a given application domain,
ECHO
first generates a domain-specific stakeholder list and then constructs a vignette for each (domain, bias) pair, which is systematically combined with every stakeholder in that list. The resulting vignettes are augmented with harm-based multiple-choice questions (MCQs) and evaluated by multiple annotators, both human and LLM.
The collected annotations are aggregated to populate a descriptive ethical matrix
dEM^{d}
for the given domain
d
.
Each matrix entry
dEM^{d}_{k,m}
reflects the set of harms identified for stakeholder
s_{k}
under bias
b_{m}
within domain
d
.
This
descriptive
matrix is subsequently refined through inferential testing, yielding the
inferential
ethical matrix
iEM
. Our experiments across two high-stakes domains and multiple stakeholder roles demonstrate that such matrices can indeed be constructed in practice, validating the operational feasibility of the mapping defined in the proposition.
While the validity of each individual matrix depends on annotation quality and sampling robustness, the overall method provides evidence-based support for the proposition.

Figure 1

Overview of the
ECHO
workflow. 1.
The system takes as input an application domain and generates a domain-specific list of stakeholders. 2. It then takes this list, a list of bias, and a list of harms. For each (domain, bias) pair, it constructs a vignette that is combined with every stakeholder in the domain. Each resulting vignette is then augmented with a MCQ based on the list of harms. 3. The resulting augmented vignette is then annotated by multiple human annotators and an AI agent, each selecting potential harms. The collected annotations are aggregated into a descriptive ethical matrix, where rows represent stakeholders, columns represent bias types, and each cell reflects the consensus view of perceived harms. This matrix is finally refined via inferential testing yielding the inferential ethical matrix.

\tau=0.5

\pm 5\%

Let

\mathsf{Agg}_{k,m,d}\;=\;\{\,h\in\mathcal{H}\;:\;C(h)/N\geq\tau\,\}.

\pm 5\%

\mathsf{Agg}_{k,m,d}\;=\;\arg\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}C(h).

The aggregation summarizes crowd and LLM judgments by keeping harms endorsed by (at least) half of annotators for each vignette; when no clear majority emerges, the top-ranked harms are retained to reflect the strongest shared signals.

4.
ECHO in practice: Experimental Setup

To evaluate the applicability of the
ECHO
framework, we conducted a vignette-based experiment grounded in the five types of AI bias (Subsection
2.2
) and the perceived harms defined by the harm taxonomy proposed by Shelby et al.
(
74
)
(Subsection
2.1
).
The study was applied to two distinct domains: disease diagnosis and hiring. We selected these domains because policies such as the EU AI Act explicitly classify both as high-risk and require corresponding processes for risk management and assessment
(
80
)
. Moreover, both are widely identified in the literature as high-stakes contexts where AI systems increasingly support consequential decisions yet remain particularly vulnerable to bias
(
15
;
33
;
45
;
75
;
66
)
. Importantly, they differ in their proximity to human subjects and in the nature of their impacts. Hiring involves socio-economic judgments with potentially long-term effects on applicants’ livelihoods and opportunities, whereas disease diagnosis directly affects human health and well-being, with more immediate and critical consequences. Despite these differences, both domains rely on AI to support sensitive decisions that affect people’s lives, often in situations where information is incomplete or unevenly distributed. This makes them complementary settings for evaluating the framework across diverse stakeholder groups and deployment contexts.

Protocol

In this section, we detail the end-to-end protocol used to instantiate and evaluate
ECHO
in our two domains. We outline each phase of the framework so that the procedure can be understood and replicated.

Phase 1: Stakeholder Generation

We first constructed a one-shot learning prompt for GPT-4. The prompt included a domain-specific list of stakeholders that we manually defined for a loan application scenario, drawing on relevant real-world cases and prior studies

An illustration of this stakeholder generation procedure is provided in the Appendix

Phase 2: Vignette Construction

VignetteCon.
For each domain-bias pair,

Each vignette described an AI system exhibiting a specific bias within a domain and was completed with a targeted question addressed to a relevant stakeholder.A structured writing protocol guided the generation of each vignette and consisted of: (i) a domain-specific context anchor (e.g., recruitment process or medical consultation), (ii) an explicit and accessible instantiation of the bias type (e.g., use of a non-representative dataset or reliance on a poor proxy) making it understandable even to participants unfamiliar with AI bias terminology, and (iii) a stakeholder-centered narrative perspective. 35

4.2.
Participants

In line with human-in-the-loop principles that leverage human judgment where automated systems fall short (see subsection
2.3
)
(
44
;
15
;
58

, we integrated human annotators as part of Phase 3 of the ECHO workflow. They served as the primary annotator pool, complementing the LLM-based annotations. A total of 251 university students were recruited from two major universities in Thessaloniki. Among them 114 were women (45%) and 134 were men (53%) (3 of them picked "Other"). Most participants had a STEM background, predominantly in computer science. A smaller subset from the social sciences, mainly psychology, was also included to provide complementary viewpoints. The vast majority were between 18 and 25 years old. In addition to human participants, we also included an LLM (ChatGPT-4), which was asked to anticipate harms by responding to the same MCQs that were administered to the student participants. To enhance validity, we conducted a pilot test with a small subset of
30

Participant Briefing.
Before completing the survey, all participants attended a one-hour introductory lecture designed to clarify the key concepts of AI bias and AI harms. This session aimed to establish a common conceptual baseline, given that many participants had limited prior exposure to these topics. By ensuring a shared understanding of the core notions under examination, the briefing sought to improve the quality and consistency of their responses.

5.
Results and observations

This section presents the main findings of the study. We report both descriptive and inferential outcomes to provide a transparent and rigorous account of how bias–harm relationships emerge in the data. First, we introduce the EMs derived from the aggregated survey annotations, represented in two descriptive forms collectively termed the descriptive EMs (dEMs). Second, we present descriptive statistics that visualize the distribution of selected harms across bias types, stakeholders and domains. Finally, we report the inferential analyses that yields the inferential Ethical Matrix (iEM) that refines the dEM capturing the bias–harm associations that stand out statistically and form the actionable basis for harm anticipation in
ECHO
.

To align with the article’s contributions, this section empirically demonstrates how
ECHO
: (i) operationalizes bias–to–harm source mapping by grounding abstract bias types into concrete, annotated vignettes and and by statistically validating the bias–harm patterns that emerge from them (ii) supports proactive, lifecycle-oriented harm anticipation by tracing how different types of bias manifest as stakeholder-specific harms before system development and (iii) adopts a human-centered, context-sensitive approach that embeds harm identification within realistic sociotechnical settings.

5.1.
Descriptive Ethical Matrix

The compact, majority-based dEM highlights only the harms that reached consensus among annotators (as described in Phase 3 of subsection
3.2

This expanded form can be especially valuable for researchers, analysts, and auditing teams who wish to examine less dominant but recurring harm signals, explore ambiguity or variation in perceptions, or conduct fine-grained analysis across domains and stakeholder roles. bias, as skewed or selective data representations inherently reinforce generalized or reductive depictions of certain groups.
Representation
bias further leads to
erasure

reifying categories
for
evaluation
bias corresponds to a scenario in which a company’s hiring system was tested exclusively on data from a single urban office. This narrow evaluation context reinforced a fixed “city-office” applicant archetype, leading the system to treat this background as the default standard against which all candidates were assessed. As a result, applicants from different environments experienced a sense of
alienation
, perceiving the system as misaligned with their professional or cultural context (see Appendix
C

5.3.
Inferential Exploration of Bias–Harm Patterns Across Stakeholders and Domains

Finally, we present inferential analyses to test which bias–harm associations occur significantly more often than would be expected by chance. These results yield an evidence-based set of statistically robust bias–harm pathways, that are then used to tranform the initial dEM into a statistically validated iEM that captures the bias–harm associations that stand out statistically (including both old and newly surfaced significant pathways).

To determine whether the distribution of harms differs across bias types within each domain–stakeholder combination, we first apply an omnibus
x^{2}
test of homogeneity to the bias × harm contingency table. This test is standard for evaluating whether multiple categorical groups share the same distribution of outcomes, and is widely used in multilevel survey and social-science studies involving categorical vote or frequency data
(
54
;
73
)
. Because our research focus is based on whether any biases are associated with specific harm patterns, the omnibus
x^{2}
test provides the correct global assessment before conducting cell-level follow-ups.

Omnibus
\chi^{2}
test of homogeneity

We first assess, for each domain and stakeholder separately, whether the overall distribution of harms differs across bias types. For each pair, we constructed a (bias x harm) contingency table of annotator votes and tested the following hypotheses:

\displaystyle H_{0}:

\displaystyle\text{The distribution of harms is the same across all bias types.}

\displaystyle H_{1}:

\displaystyle\text{At least one bias type exhibits a different harm distribution.}

(

While the omnibus
\chi^{2}
test assesses whether the overall distribution of harms differs across bias types within each domain–stakeholder table, it does not indicate which specific bias–harm combinations contribute to these differences. To obtain cell-level insight, we compute adjusted standardized residuals, the recommended post-hoc technique for decomposing significant
\chi^{2}
results in contingency-table analyses
(
31
;
11
)
. Adjusted residuals quantify whether a given cell contains more or fewer responses than expected under the independence model, thereby identifying which bias–harm associations are disproportionately represented. This methodological approach is widely used in behavioral and survey-based research—particularly when analysing questionnaire-derived categorical data—where contingency tables may be moderately sparse and loglinear models may be unstable or biased
(
20
)
. The resulting residuals therefore provide interpretable, exploratory evidence about the specific patterns driving the global
\chi^{2}
findings.

Adjusted standardized residual analysis.

z

z>0

z<0

\chi^{2}
test; (Diagnosis–Patient, Patient group, Hiring–Applicant, Applicant group, Developer). Cells with
z>1.64
(
p<0.10
) are considered significantly overrepresented, meaning that these bias–harm relationships appear more often than expected by chance. Table

5.5.
Discussion of Main Findings

ECHO
produces two complementary ethical matrices-a descriptive Ethical Matrix (dEM) and an inferential Ethical Matrix (iEM)- each serving a distinct purpose. The dEM provides a structured, stakeholder-driven view of how participants intuitively perceived the relationships between bias types and potential harms. This descriptive layer is valuable for those involved in the design, development, and governance of AI systems, who require an
interpretable, user-centered snapshot of perceived vulnerabilities
early in the design and development process. It also serves as a
validity and interpretability check
, confirming that annotators meaningfully engaged with the task and differentiated among harm categories and bias types. Several of the descriptive patterns observed were non-uniform and theoretically aligned as discussed above, indicating that participants did not respond randomly or default to globally dominant harms. This strengthens confidence in the interpretability of the vignettes and in the reliability of the data on which the inferential analysis builds.

In contrast, the iEM offers an evidence-based refinement of these intuitions by identifying statistically significant bias-harm associations. Given that our study design establishes a conceptual direction from bias to harm (consistent with the literature that treats biases as sources of harm
(
25
;
9
;
77
;
71
)
), the inferential analyses provides significant associations that uncover meaningful bias-to-harm relationships within realistic domain-stakeholder contexts. As such, the iEM provides
the diagnostic and actionable patterns
that can be used not only by designers, developers and practitioners but also by auditors, policymakers, and risk-assessment teams for proactive harm anticipation, governance, and targeted mitigation.

Significant bias–harm associations were detected primarily for
decision subjects
-patients and job applicants-and their corresponding
social groups
. This indicates that, for those directly subjected to algorithmic decisions (passive stakeholders), the type of bias present is meaningfully associated with the kind of harm experienced.
Although our statistical analyses are associational, our questionnaire was explicitly designed to embed directionality, asking respondents to identify which harms were caused by or resulted from specific biases. This design enables interpretation of significant associations as reflecting directional tendencies rather than mere co-occurrence. Recent methodological work supports this approach: Pornprasertmanit et al.
(
65
)
demonstrate that directional dependencies can be inferred from observational associations under defined assumptions; Li et al.
(
51
)
show that questionnaire data analyzed through regression and association mining can reveal “cause → effect” patterns consistent with hypothesized causal links; and Ni et al.
(
60
)
extend this reasoning by applying Bayesian causal-structure modeling to survey data to extract likely causal relationships. Following these methodological precedents, we interpret our results as evidence that certain biases tend to lead to specific harms - consistent with the causal direction embedded in our instrument, while acknowledging that causality cannot be conclusively established without longitudinal or experimental data.

Additional significant results were observed for
developers
in the hiring domain, suggesting that biases embedded in model design, development, and deployment can correspond to reflective effects on the individuals responsible for creating these systems. Notably, no significant bias-harm differences were observed for developers in the healthcare domain, likely due to the low variance in responses. Participants appeared to perceive the effects of bias as relatively uniform, suggesting that perceptions of harm were influenced more by the strength and criticality of the domain itself than by the specific type of bias. Similarly, for
companies
and
healthcare institutions
, no statistically significant differences in harm distributions were observed across bias types. In these cases, the occurrence of harm appeared relatively uniform across bias categories. This likely reflects the fact that several active stakeholders are not direct subjects of algorithmic decisions and therefore experience bias more indirectly-through reputational, organizational, or systemic effects rather than through unequal treatment.

To facilitate interpretation, we categorize the five bias types into two groups that correspond to different stages of the AI lifecycle.
Data-related biases
(
representation
,
measurement
, and
evaluation
) arise from data-related processes that determine what information enters the system and how its performance is judged. The second group includes
design- and use-related biases
(
algorithmic
and
deployment
) that arise from architectural design decisions that shape model behavior and the context in which the system is used. Patterns across these two groups are discussed in the following subsections.

5.5.1.
Data-related Biases and cascading QoS and Allocative Harms in Passive Stakeholders.

The convergence of
representation
,
measurement
, and
evaluation
biases in Quality-of-Service (QoS) and allocative harms in the descriptive results (Figure
2
a and b) reflects their shared technical and data-related origins.

Representation bias
arises when the data used for training distort or underrepresent particular groups.
Measurement bias
occurs when the data used to represent a phenomenon rely on poorly defined proxies that mischaracterize the underlying construct, distorting what the system learns to value.

Evaluation bias
(rooted in unbalanced benchmark data in our study) occurs when the data used for testing is unrepresentative of the population, concealing disparities across groups. Although these biases arise at different stages of the AI lifecycle, they share a common origin in data and ultimately converge in effect: each distorts how systems perceive and serve individuals, leading to uneven accuracy, degraded service quality, and the reinforcement of structural inequities. These data-quality issues manifest primarily as
QoS harms
and when such disparities occur in real-world decision settings, they often cascade to
allocative harms
, influencing who receives resources or services
(
74
;
9
;
16
;
41
)
. This pattern is clearly visible in the
Diagnosis–Patient
radar (Figure
2
a) and (to a lesser extent) in the
Hiring–Applicant
radar (Figure
2
b).

Inferential tests
revealed significant associations between
evaluation bias
and QoS harms-specifically,
service or benefit loss
in the
diagnosis
domain-and allocative harms (
economic loss
) in the
hiring
domain. The other two data-related bias types (
representation
and
measurement
) did not reach statistical significance in this sample for reasons described in Section
5.5.5
. However, their descriptive patterns suggest potentially meaningful trends that merit further investigation.

5.5.2.
Data-related Biases and cascading Representational and Interpersonal Harms in Passive stakeholders.

Data-quality issues underpin
representational harms
, as non-representative or biased datasets directly shape how social groups are depicted, valued, or omitted
(
9
;
16
;
70
)
. This pattern is evident in the
Patient Group
and
Applicant Group
radars (Figure
3
a–b), where the three data-related biases produced the strongest peaks.

The
inferential
analysis indicated that
measurement bias
in the
diagnosis
domain was significantly associated with
denying self-identity
and
stereotyping
. This finding indicates that when machine learning developers operationalize complex phenomena using oversimplified proxy variables, they risk distorting self-identification and reinforcing group marginalization
(
61
;
41
)
. Similarly,
evaluation bias
was associated with
alienation
, likely reflecting validation practices based on narrow benchmarks that fail to capture population diversity. When such systems are deployed, individuals outside the model’s representational scope experience exclusion and a sense of being “unseen,” contributing to perceived alienation
(
77
;
16
;
9
)
. In the
hiring
domain, only data-related biases were linked to representational harms.
Evaluation bias
was linked to
reifying categories
, suggesting that narrow evaluation practices can harden social classifications into fixed, normative standards. Conversely,
representation bias
was associated with the representational harm of
erasure
: when specific groups are insufficiently represented in training data, the model not only performs poorly for them but effectively renders them invisible, failing to acknowledge their identity or experience.

Representational bias frequently co-occur with interpersonal harms
(
74
)
; when individuals are misrepresented or omitted by algorithmic systems, they lose the ability to define themselves within those systems, internalizing a diminished sense of agency, dignity, and well-being. This pattern explains the observed connection between representation bias and the interpersonal harm of
loss of agency or control
in the
diagnosis-patient
context.

5.5.3.
Design- and Use-related Biases associated with QoS and Representational Harms in Decision Subjects.

Algorithmic
and
deployment
biases emerge later in the lifecycle, where model architectures and contextual deployment decisions influence how earlier inequities are amplified or possibly mitigated.
Algorithmic bias
(rooted in architectural constraints in our study) was more clearly associated in our sample with QoS harms in both domains (see Fig.
2
). There is also a strong association with
diminished health
in Fig.
2
a but this was expected due to the clinical setting to which this figure refers. This pattern was also supported by the
inferential analysis
, which showed that algorithmic bias was significantly associated with all three subcategories of
QoS harms
. This aligns with theoretical accounts suggesting that harm in AI systems arises not only from data but also from model design choices. Overly simplistic or compressed architectures exacerbate errors on underrepresented "long-tail" data, as design and optimization criteria often correspond to systematic inequities in model performance
(
35
)
. Notably, the QoS harm
alienation
appeared in the disease-diagnosis domain, possibly reflecting the heightened sensitivity and deeply personal nature of healthcare decisions, while the association with
increased labor
emerged in the
hiring
domain, where harms seem to be less personal and more structural or procedural.

Deployment bias
arises when systems are used in contexts misaligned with their intended use, such as when advisory tools operate autonomously without human oversight. This bias showed statistically significant associations with
QoS harms
, particularly
alienation
, observed across both domains. Such harms reflect the experiential dimension of automation: when human judgment is removed, users often feel disconnected from decision-making and unable to contest outcomes. The resulting frustration and detachment underscore how deployment bias can degrade the quality of human–AI interaction itself
(
74
)
(an illustrative example scenario can be found in Appendix
C
.Vignette 4).

Regarding
representational harms
,
inferential
tests illustrate that these biases showed comparatively weaker associations than the earlier, data-related ones (
representation
,
measurement
,
evaluation
). In our experiment,
algorithmic bias
manifested primarily through
underfitting
. Such mechanisms can lead models to
reify essentialist categories
, treating socially complex attributes-or even biological ones, such as age in our scenario (see Appendix
C
.Vignette 3)-as fixed and universal determinants of outcomes (e.g., risky pregnancy)
(
74
;
35
)
. In this way, algorithmic systems oversimplify human diversity and medical variability and reduce individuals to reductive classifications.
Notably,
inferential
tests revealed a significant association between
deployment bias
and the response category
"None"
within representational harms. This suggests that participants did not interpret deployment-related failures as representational, but rather as systemic or functional-arising from
over-automation
and
contextual mismatch
, where systems operate without adequate human oversight or outside their intended scope of use (as illustrated in the chatbot scenario, Appendix
C
.Vignette 4).

5.5.4.
Active stakeholders and reflective Harms

Significant associations for the
developer
stakeholder in the Hiring domain underscore the reflective dimension of algorithmic harm, where biases embedded in the system feed back to those who build it.
Loss of agency or control
from deployment bias is the dominant spoke in Figure
4
b and this association is also confirmed from the
inferential
tests, reflecting developers’ limited ability to intervene once systems operate autonomously
(
43
)
.
Representation bias
was linked to
alienation
, suggesting that when developers produce systems later criticized for discrimination, they may experience professional isolation, moral distress or social backlash.
Measurement bias
was linked to
economic loss
, suggesting that when developers rely on poorly defined proxies, the resulting system failures can translate into tangible costs (ranging from wasted resources and to personal or professional setbacks). These findings broaden the discussion of AI harms beyond direct stakeholders, emphasizing that the development process itself constitutes a site of vulnerability, one in which creators may become secondary victims of the very biases they encode.

5.5.5.
Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, some harm categories were inherently complex or ambiguously defined, introducing interpretive variability that we sought to minimize through iterative refinements during vignette development with the LLM, as well as through the one-hour preparatory lecture conducted prior to data collection to clarify key distinctions.
However, participants occasionally struggled to differentiate nuanced harm types, potentially introducing overlap or noise into the dataset. For instance, in some cases respondents selected "technology-facilitated violence" (also associated with
deployment
bias in Table
4
), even when no form of violence was depicted in the vignette, indicating interpretive spillover across harm categories. Second, the high dimensionality of the dataset-spanning multiple domains, stakeholders, bias types, and harm categories-resulted in sparse contingency tables, containing cells with low expected frequencies. Thus, the statistical power of the omnibus
\chi^{2}
and adjusted residual analysis tests was reduced, increasing the likelihood of Type II errors. To mitigate this limitation, inferential tests were evaluated at a 10% significance level and interpreted in an exploratory manner. Nonetheless, even under this relaxed criterion, some theoretically expected association patterns (clearly visible in the descriptive statistics) did not reach statistical significance, such as the relationship between data-quality biases and allocative harms
(
9
;
70
)
. Future research could address these limitations through more precise harm definitions and larger annotation samples.

6.
Conclusions

This work introduced
ECHO
, a framework designed to systematically anticipate harms emerging from AI bias across sociotechnical contexts. The framework operationalizes harm anticipation through a modular workflow that integrates stakeholder generation, vignette construction, and both human and LLM annotation, all systematically organized within ethical matrices. In doing so, it follows a sociotechnical approach that provides a structured mechanism to trace harms back to their underlying biases, by extension, to the design, development, and deployment choices across the AI lifecycle from which these biases originate.

Addressing RQ2, the design of
ECHO
establishes a robust and extensible methodology for mapping how bias, conditioned by domain and stakeholder context, are associated with distinct harm types. A core component of this methodological rigor is the pair of complementary analytical outputs produced by ECHO, the descriptive Ethical Matrix (dEM) and the inferential Ethical Matrix (iEM). The dEM captures stakeholder-driven intuitions and provides an interpretability and task-validity layer, ensuring that participants meaningfully distinguished among the intended conceptual categories-bias types, harm types, stakeholder roles, and domain contexts. The iEM then refines these perceptions through evidence-based inference, identifying statistically reliable bias–harm associations. Together, these complementary matrices form a robust analytic structure that systematically uncovers how different types of bias tend to give rise to distinct types of harm. By revealing these pathways, ECHO enables proactive harm anticipation and informed intervention before system deployment or even development. Each domain is represented through its own set of EMs (dEM and iEM), which systematically links biases and stakeholder roles to their corresponding harms.
ECHO
’s modular architecture, underpinned by a mathematical formulation, combines methodological rigor with practical applicability, ensuring
adaptability
across domains, stakeholder roles, and evolving bias and harm taxonomies. Importantly,
ECHO
also prioritizes the
consistency
of its analytical outcomes: while results may vary across contexts, the framework is designed so that, given the same data, other researchers would discern similar bias–harm patterns and this reflects the framework’s overall
reliability

(
32
)
.

Addressing RQ1, the experimental application of
ECHO
to two high-stakes domains demonstrated the framework’s capacity to identify context-sensitive associations between specific types of AI bias and corresponding harms. Statistical analysis revealed significant associations primarily for the
passive stakeholders
(decision subjects-patients and applicants-and their corresponding marginalised groups), while more diffuse for
active stakeholders
.

Data-related bias
(
representation, measurement,evaluation
) tend to give rise to QoS and representational harms that cascade into interpersonal harms.
Design-and use-related biases
(
algorithmic, deployment
) are primarily associated with QoS and representational harms. Regarding the
application domain
, in the
disease diagnosis
domain, biases seem to operate within a more personal and trust-dependent environment, where technical misalignments triggered psychosocial harms such as alienation, stereotyping, or denial of identity. By contrast, in the
hiring
domain, harms that take on a more distributive form were spotted, manifesting as lost benefits, economic disadvantage, or representational erasure. Taken together, these findings highlight how the nature of harm is shaped by the type of bias and the degree of social proximity of stakeholders to algorithmic decisions, while also emphasizing the critical role of the application domain in conditioning how these dynamics unfold in practice. This underscores the need for frameworks that integrate both technical and contextual dimensions to anticipate harm.

Ultimately, the goal of
ECHO
is not merely to reveal where and how AI systems fail, but to provide a structured means of understanding and preventing such failures. Bias and harms are not isolated phenomena; they reflect the data assumptions, design choices, cognitive processes, and value judgments embedded throughout the AI lifecycle. By mapping these relationships systematically and integrating technical and social dimensions into a unified pipeline,
ECHO
supports the development of AI systems that are transparent, inclusive, and responsive to stakeholder needs. In this sense, it serves as a principled contribution to Responsible AI, shifting the focus from reactive correction to proactive harm anticipation and providing both active and passive stakeholders with a structured way to trace harm pathways, identify who is most affected, and guide design governance and targeted mitigation strategies in this emerging human-and-AI-symbiotic era.

Response:

For an AI system used to screen job candidates—especially one that replaces the HR manager—we can identify potential active and passive stakeholders based on their roles in influencing or being affected by the system. Here’s a structured breakdown:

Active Stakeholders

(They shape the system’s outcomes through design, deployment, or operational influence.)

(1)

The company (as an entity) – It made the decision to adopt the AI system and sets hiring goals/policies.

(2)

Company executives / decision-makers – Define hiring criteria, approve the AI’s use, and evaluate its performance.

(3)

AI system developers / vendors – Build and train the AI system, choose features/proxies, and define model architecture.

(4)

Data scientists / ML engineers within the company (if applicable) – Fine-tune or audit the model, maintain the pipeline.

(5)

Hiring managers / remaining HR staff -- May still intervene in final hiring decisions or adjust how the AI is used.

(6)

Regulators or compliance officers -- Influence requirements for fairness, explainability, non-discrimination, etc.

Passive Stakeholders

(They are affected by the AI system but do not directly influence it.)

(1)

Job applicants (in general) -- Directly impacted by the AI’s screening decisions (shortlisted or rejected).

(2)

Applicants from historically marginalized groups (e.g., women, racial/ethnic minorities, disabled applicants) -- May face disproportionate harms due to biased data or design.

(3)

Society at large -- Influenced indirectly via labor market access, fairness perceptions, and long-term societal inequalities.

(4)

Families or dependents of job applicants -- Affected economically or emotionally by hiring outcomes.

(5)

Other companies in the industry --May adopt similar practices or face competition pressure, leading to ecosystem-level impact.

(6)

Employees of the company -- Existing staff may experience cultural, diversity, or workload shifts depending on who is hired.

(7)

Labor unions / advocacy groups -- Though not always involved, they may become stakeholders if harms are reported.

applicant, marginalized applicant groups, AI system developer, and hiring company.

Appendix C
Sample vignettes

This appendix presents representative vignettes used in the ECHO study, illustrating how different bias types emerge in AI systems across various domains. Each vignette narrates a concise case of an AI system exhibiting a specific bias type within a given context of use.

Vignette 1: Hiring, Representation Bias

TechNova Solutions uses a computer system to screen resumes for a sales job. The system was trained using data from past employees-most of whom were men from elite universities who worked in urban branches. Because of this, the system tends to favor candidates who match these profiles. Applicants from different backgrounds-such as women, people from smaller towns, or those who attended less well-known schools-are often rejected, even if they have the right skills for the job.

Vignette 2: Disease Diagnosis, Evaluation Bias

A dental AI system was developed to detect tooth decay using X-ray images. However, while the system was trained on a diverse range of patient data, it was only tested in urban dental clinics with advanced imaging equipment. Because the AI was never properly evaluated in other settings, it works well in high-tech clinics but struggles in community health centers that use older X-ray machines or different imaging techniques. As a result, some cavities go undetected because the system fails to recognize decay patterns in lower-quality scans, while some patients may receive false positives.

Vignette 3: Disease Diagnosis, Algorithmic Bias

A hospital uses an AI system to assess pregnancy risks. However, instead of analyzing how different health factors interact, the system relies on a simplified formula that assumes age is the most important risk factor, leading directly to a high-risk pregnancy. This system is too simplistic to capture the real complexity of maternal health. As a result, it overlooks how other factors such as high blood pressure and borderline glucose levels interact in complex ways and contribute to complications. Because of this, a young mother with slightly high blood pressure may be classified as low-risk, even though her condition could be dangerous. Meanwhile, an older mother with no other health issues may be labeled high-risk simply because the system prioritizes age over all other factors.

Vignette 4: Disease Diagnosis, Deployment Bias

An AI chatbot (an AI assistant that provides automated responses in conversations) was originally designed to assist therapists by flagging potential signs of depression and anxiety during online consultations. However, in some clinics, it’s used as the sole decision-maker for prescribing therapy sessions. Without human oversight, it misses complex cases-such as patients masking symptoms or presenting atypically-leading to misdiagnoses or delays in appropriate care.

Vignette 5: Hiring, Evaluation Bias

TechNova uses a computer system to select customer support staff. The system was trained using a wide range of employee data but was only tested using information from workers in a single large city office. Because it was only checked against this narrow sample, the AI now works well for urban candidates but struggles to fairly evaluate applicants from other environments-like rural towns, small cities, or international offices. As a result, qualified candidates who don’t match the "city office" profile are often rejected, not because they lack skills, but because the AI was never properly tested to handle their backgrounds.
