Concentration Inequalities for Exchangeable Tensors and Matrix-valued Data
Chen Cheng, Rina Foygel Barber

Abstract
We study concentration inequalities for structured weighted sums of random data, including (i) tensor inner products and (ii) sequential matrix sums. We are interested in tail bounds and concentration inequalities for those structured weighted sums under exchangeability, extending beyond the classical framework of independent terms. We develop Hoeffding and Bernstein bounds provided with structure-dependent exchangeability. Along the way, we recover known results in weighted sum of exchangeable random variables and i.i.d. sums of random matrices to the optimal constants. Notably, we develop a sharper concentration bound for combinatorial sum of matrix arrays than the results previously derived from Chatterjee’s method of exchangeable pairs. For applications, the richer structures provide us with novel analytical tools for estimating the average effect of multi-factor response models and studying fixed-design sketching methods in federated averaging. We apply our results to these problems, and find that our theoretical predictions are corroborated by numerical evidence.

Introduction

A central goal in concentration inequalities involves deriving tail bounds for a centered random variable
Z-\mathbb{E}Z
that equals to the weighted sum of random data, with the simplest form as

\displaystyle Z=\langle w,X\rangle:=w_{1}X_{1}+w_{2}X_{2}+\cdots+w_{N}X_{N},

\mathbb{R}

[-1,1]

1.1
Examples

Before formalizing the mathematical framework and introducing the concentration bounds, we first preview two real-world examples when conventional i.i.d. or exchangeable assumptions break, while the richer structures of symmetry in randomness allow us to utilize the novel analytical tools. We will return to these examples later on in Section
5
.

The following first example concerns tensor-valued data that exhibit exchangeability along each mode.

Example 1
(Average effect of a multi-factor response)
:

Consider a multi-factor model, where factor
k\in[K]
has discrete levels
[N_{k}]
. The multi-index
(i_{1},\cdots,i_{K}),i_{l}\in[N_{l}]
corresponds to an experimental condition with the
k
-th factor being
i_{k}
. For each experimental setup, running the test produces a random and possibly censored response
Y_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
\sum_{i_{l}\in[N_{l}],l\in[K]}p_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}=1
be a probability distribution
P
on
[N_{1}]\times\cdots\times[N_{K}]
, we are interested in estimating the average effect of the multi-factor response

\displaystyle\mu=\mathbb{E}_{(i_{1},\cdots,i_{K})\sim P}[Y_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}]=\sum_{i_{l}\in[N_{l}],l\in[K]}p_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}Y_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}.

In practice, it may be infeasible to observe
Y_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}
for all
N_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot N_{K}
possible combinations of factor levels.
Instead, we can consider the following estimation procedure of the average effect by sub-sampling a Cartesian product of factor-level subsets. Namely, we choose without replacement
I_{l}\subset[N_{l}]
for all
l\in[K]
with
|I_{l}|=n_{l}
, and let
X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}=\prod_{l=1}^{K}\mathds{1}_{i_{l}\in I_{l}}
be the indicator random variable of the sampled experiments—evidently,
X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}
are exchangeable along each fixed factor level (as we shall define formally in the following in Def.
2.1
,
X
is mode-exchangeable). In various real-world problems, this Cartesian product sub-sampling scheme can be attractive, even compared to independent sampling with the same number of samples, as changing factor setups for individual experiment can be costly while batch experiments greatly reduce cost. Several practical domains motivate such cost-reduction schemes, including Lithium-ion batteries aging under multiple factors (temperature, state-of-charge windows, charge/discharge C-rates and depth-of-discharge)
[
36
,
38
]
, high-throughput drug combination screening in cancer (joint effect of drug A, drug B, dose levels and cell lines)
[
20
]
and semiconductor manufacturing process optimization (factors including gas flows, chamber pressure, RF power and electrode spacing)
[
32
]
.

Suppose we know
p_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}
(or we have empirical estimates of the frequencies). Conditioned on the true responses
Y_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}
and let
W_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}=p_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}Y_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}
, we can use the classical Horvitz-Thompson estimator for the average effect:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}=\frac{N_{1}\cdots N_{K}}{n_{1}\cdots n_{K}}\cdot Z=\frac{N_{1}\cdots N_{K}}{n_{1}\cdots n_{K}}\cdot\llangle W,X\rrangle:=\frac{N_{1}\cdots N_{K}}{n_{1}\cdots n_{K}}\sum_{i_{l}\in[N_{l}],l\in[K]}W_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}.

The question is then to study the concentration of
\widehat{\mu}-\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mu}\mid\{Y_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}\}_{i_{l}\in[N_{l}],l\in[K]}]
, and in particular how to minimize the estimation error given a fixed sub-sampling budget
n_{1}\cdots n_{K}\leq B
.

In the next example, we turn to sketching techniques widely used as a type of dimensional reduction method in statistical machine learning, where
W
,
X
and the quantity of interest
\llangle W,X\rrangle
belong to the matrix-valued venue in Table
1
. In classical setups such as Gaussian sketch and count sketch, the sketching matrices are independent. While still being random, we consider sub-sampling from a pool of fixed-design sketching matrices as an alternative to the existing approaches, reducing the requirement of independence to exchangeability.

Example 2
(Fixed-design sketching matrices)
:

Consider a sequence of
N
exchangeable parameters
\theta_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}
, and we are interested in the average of parameters
\overline{\theta}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\theta_{k}
. In distributed optimization setup where
\theta_{k}
are local parameters, such as federated averaging
[
24
,
13
]
and collaborative learning
[
12
]
, computation is intensive in local agents and oftentimes there are constraints in communication cost between the central server and local agents. It is thus desirable to apply sketching techniques to compress local data
\theta_{k}
: we will take sketching matrices
U_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{q^{\prime}\times q}
, for some dimension
q^{\prime}\ll q
, which satisfy

\sum_{k=1}^{N}U_{k}^{\top}U_{k}\approx I_{q}.

The local data
\theta_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}
is then compressed to
z_{k}=U_{k}\theta_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{q^{\prime}\times r}
, and the local agents communicate
z_{k}
to the central server, which then aggregates the compressed data:

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}U_{k}^{\top}z_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}U_{k}^{\top}U_{k}\theta_{k}.

Since
\sum_{k=1}^{N}U_{k}^{\top}U_{k}\approx I_{q}
, we can then expect
\widehat{\theta}\approx\overline{\theta}
.

Standard approaches utilize independent sketching matrices
U_{k}
such as Gaussian and Rademacher random matrices, and sub-sampled random Fourier transform (SRFT) matrices
[
18
]
. Other popular methods include count sketch and row-column sub-sampling techniques, which can reduce computational cost since
U_{k}
are sparse
[
8
,
31
]
.

In our setup, given exchangeability in local agents
\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{N}
, we may apply a fixed sequence of sketching matrices
U_{k}
with the exact equality
\sum_{k=1}^{N}U_{k}^{\top}U_{k}=I_{q}
, which is helpful in reducing the approximation error
\sum_{k=1}^{N}U_{k}^{\top}U_{k}\approx I_{q}
that arises in a random sketching setup. Writing
W_{k}=U_{k}^{\top}U_{k}
and
X_{k}=\theta_{k}
, we then have
\hat{\theta}=Z=\llangle W,X\rrangle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}X_{k}
, and our results then establish concentration guarantees subject to matrix-valued exchangeability in Table
1
.

Notation

For any integer
n\geq 1
,
[n]
denotes the set
\{1,\cdots,n\}
. For a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
p\geq 1
, we use
\|x\|_{p}
for its
\ell_{p}
norm and reserve
\|x\|
for
p=2
. For a square matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, we use
\|A\|
for its operator norm and
\|A\|_{F}
for its Frobenius norm. We use
\mathbb{S}^{n}
for the vector space of all symmetric real-valued matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}
for the convex cone of all symmetric positive semidefinite matrices in
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. Let
\mathbb{S}_{++}^{n}
be the cone of symmetric positive definite matrices. Let
\preceq
be the partial order induced by the cone
\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}
and
\prec
be that induced by
\mathbb{S}_{++}^{n}
. For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
with
m\leq n
, let its pseudo-inverse be
A^{\dagger}
. We define the projection matrix onto the column space of
A
by
\mathsf{P}_{A}:=A(A^{\top}A)^{\dagger}A^{\top}
and its orthogonal complement as
\mathsf{P}_{A}^{\perp}:=I_{n}-A(A^{\top}A)^{\dagger}A^{\top}
. In particular, for a unit vector
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathsf{P}_{w}=ww^{\top}
and
\mathsf{P}_{w}^{\perp}=I_{n}-ww^{\top}
.
Let
\mathscr{S}_{N}
be the set of all permutation mappings in
[N]
and
\mathscr{P}_{N}
be the collection of all permutation matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
. We denote by
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{d}}}{{=}}
equality in distribution.

Next, we define some additional notation and terminology for working with tensors. For two tensors
A\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{K}}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}^{\prime}\times\cdots\times N_{K^{\prime}}^{\prime}}
, let their outer product
A\otimes B\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{K}\times N_{1}^{\prime}\times\cdots\times N_{K^{\prime}}^{\prime}}
be

\displaystyle(A\otimes B)_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}i_{1}^{\prime}\cdots i_{K^{\prime}}^{\prime}}=A_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}B_{i_{1}^{\prime}\cdots i_{K^{\prime}}^{\prime}}\qquad\textrm{for all }\,\,i_{l}\in[N_{l}],i_{l^{\prime}}^{\prime}\in[N_{l^{\prime}}^{\prime}],\,\,l\in[K],l^{\prime}\in[K^{\prime}].

We will also frequently use the inner product,

\langle A,B\rangle_{\mathbb{T}}=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N_{1}}\ldots\sum_{i_{K}=1}^{N_{K}}A_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}B_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}\in\mathbb{R}.

Recall the
k
-mode tensor-matrix product (cf.
[
25
, Sec. 2.5]
) for
A\in\mathbb{T}
and a matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N_{k}}
,
A\times_{k}B\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{k-1}\times M\times N_{k+1}\times\cdots\times N_{K}}
whose entries are

\displaystyle(A\times_{k}B)_{i_{1}\cdots i_{k-1}ji_{k}\cdots i_{K}}=\sum_{i_{k}=1}^{N_{k}}B_{ji_{k}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}.

We will write

\overline{A}:=\frac{1}{N_{1}\cdots N_{K}}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N_{1}}\cdots\sum_{i_{K}=1}^{N_{K}}A_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}=\langle A,\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{1}_{N_{1}}}\otimes\cdots\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{1}_{N_{K}}}\rangle

to denote the mean of all entries in a tensor. Analogous to the Frobenius norm for matrices, we can define the norm of a tensor
A
in the Hilbert space
\mathbb{T}
by
\left\|{A}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}:=\sqrt{\langle A,A\rangle_{\mathbb{T}}}
. We also write
\left\|{A}\right\|_{1}:=\sum_{i_{l}\in[N_{l}],l\in[K]}|A_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}|
and
\left\|{A}\right\|_{\infty}:=\max_{i_{l}\in[N_{l}],l\in[K]}|A_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}|
.

2
Concentration inequalities for mode-exchangeable tensors

In this section, we will work with tensor-structured data
X\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{K}}
, where
X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}\in[-1,1]
for all
i_{k}\in[N_{k}],k\in[K]
. Here
K
is the number of modes of the tensor, with special cases
K=1
(a vector) and
K=2
(a matrix). Before defining the problem for the setting of a general
K
, we first single out these two special cases. For the vector case (
K=1
), as studied in
[
3
]
, we have

Z=\langle w,X\rangle:=w^{\top}X=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}X_{i}.

In this vector setting,
exchangeability means the joint distribution of
(X_{1},\cdots,X_{N})
is invariant under any permutation of the index, i.e.
(X_{1},\cdots,X_{N})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{d}}}{{=}}(X_{\pi(1)},\cdots,X_{\pi(N)})
for all
\pi\in\mathscr{S}_{N}
. Next, for the matrix case (
K=2)
, we have

Z=\langle W,X\rangle:=\Tr(W^{\top}X)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{M}W_{ij}X_{ij},

where now the exchangeability assumption is row-column-exchangeability:
(X_{ij})_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{d}}}{{=}}(X_{\pi(i)\pi^{\prime}(j)})_{i\in[N],j\in[M]}
for all
\pi\in\mathscr{S}_{N},\pi^{\prime}\in\mathscr{S}_{M}
. In each of these two problems, the weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
or weight matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
are fixed.

We now move to the general formulation of
mode-exchangeable
tensors, which naturally generalizes the above two scenarios and allows for fine-grained analyses of more complex data structures. In this section,
W
and
X
both lie in
\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{K}}
. We are interested in the concentration of their inner product in the tensor space,

Z=\langle W,X\rangle_{\mathbb{T}}:=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N_{1}}\cdots\sum_{i_{K}=1}^{N_{K}}W_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}},

when
W
is a fixed
K
-mode tensor and while
X
is a random tensor that satisfies mode exchangeability, as defined next.

Definition 2.1

(Mode exchangeability)
.

For a random tensor
X\in\mathbb{T}
, we say it is mode-exchangeable if for any
l\in[K]
and permutation matrix
\Pi_{l}\in\mathscr{P}_{N_{l}}
,

\displaystyle X\times_{l}\Pi_{l}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{d}}}{{=}}X.

That is, the distribution of
X
is invariant under permutation along any mode. Equivalently, for any set of permutation matrices
\Pi_{k}\in\mathscr{P}_{N_{k}},k=1,2,\cdots,K
,

\displaystyle(X\times_{1}\Pi_{1}\times_{2}\cdots\times_{K}\Pi_{K})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{d}}}{{=}}X,

i.e., the distribution
X
is unchanged when we apply a permutation along each mode.

In this setting, we are interested in concentration inequalities for
\langle W,X\rangle_{\mathbb{T}}-\mathbb{E}[\langle W,X\rangle_{\mathbb{T}}]
, where
\mathbb{E}[\langle W,X\rangle_{\mathbb{T}}]=N_{1}\cdots N_{k}\cdot\overline{W}\cdot\overline{X}
. To obtain one-sided tail bound
\mathbb{P}\{\langle W,X\rangle_{\mathbb{T}}-\mathbb{E}[\langle W,X\rangle_{\mathbb{T}}]>t\}
hinges on upper bounding its moment generating function
\mathbb{E}[\exp\{\lambda(\langle W,X\rangle_{\mathbb{T}}-\mathbb{E}[\langle W,X\rangle_{\mathbb{T}}])\}]
for appropriate values of
\lambda
. The remainder of this section develops two such bounds: first a Hoeffding type bound, and then a more refined Bernstein type bound.

Before proceeding, we first need to introduce some additional notation for computing mode-wise averages within a tensor.
For any tensor
A\in\mathbb{T}
, we can define a sequence of tensors
\overline{A}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{k}}=:\mathbb{T}_{k}
given by

\overline{A}_{k}:=A\times_{k+1}\frac{1}{N_{k+1}}\mathbf{1}_{N_{k+1}}\times_{k+2}\cdots\times_{K}\frac{1}{N_{K}}\mathbf{1}_{N_{K}}.

In other words,
\overline{A}_{k}
is obtained by averaging over modes
k+1,\dots,K
, and has entries

(\overline{A}_{k})_{i_{1}\cdots i_{k}}=\frac{1}{N_{k+1}\cdots N_{K}}\sum_{i_{k+1}=1}^{N_{k+1}}\ldots\sum_{i_{K}=1}^{N_{K}}A_{i_{1}\cdots i_{k}i_{k+1}\cdots i_{K}}.

We also define a sequence
A_{k}\in\mathbb{T}
given by

A_{k}:=A\times_{k+1}\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{1}_{N_{k+1}}}\times_{k+2}\cdots\times_{K}\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{1}_{N_{K}}},

i.e., we have
(A_{k})_{i_{1}\cdots i_{k}i_{k+1}\cdots i_{K}}=(\overline{A}_{k})_{i_{1}\cdots i_{k}}
for any
i_{1},\dots,i_{K}
. For
k=0
, we additionally have that
\overline{A}_{0}=\overline{A}\in\mathbb{R}
is the average of all entries of
A
, and
A_{0}=\overline{A}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{N_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{1}_{N_{K}}
. On the other hand,
A_{K}=\overline{A}_{K}=A
.

2.1
The Hoeffding bound

As in
[
3
]
, we define a sequence of auxiliary constants
\epsilon_{n}
with
\epsilon_{1}=0
, and when
n\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n}=\frac{H_{n}-1}{n-H_{n}},\qquad\qquad H_{n}:=1+\frac{1}{2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n}.

The following result gives a Hoeffding-type MGF bound for
Z=\langle W,X\rangle_{\mathbb{T}}-\mathbb{E}[\langle W,X\rangle_{\mathbb{T}}]
.

Theorem 1

(
Hoeffding-type MGF bound under mode-exchangeability
)
.

For a fixed
W\in\mathbb{T}
and a mode-exchangeable
X\in\mathbb{T}
, it holds for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\exp\left\{{\lambda Z}\right\}}\right]\leq\exp\left\{{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\cdot\prod_{l=1}^{K}N_{l}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1+\epsilon_{N_{k}}}{N_{k}}\cdot\sigma^{2}_{W,k}}\right\},\qquad\textnormal{where }\sigma^{2}_{W,k}:=\left\|{W_{k}\times_{k}\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{1}_{N_{k}}}^{\perp}}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}.

(1a)

Consequently for all
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{{Z\geq\sqrt{2\log\frac{1}{\delta}\cdot\prod_{l=1}^{K}N_{l}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1+\epsilon_{N_{k}}}{N_{k}}\sigma^{2}_{W,k}}}\right\}\leq\delta.

(1b)

The proof of this result is given in Appendix
B.1
.

2.2
The Bernstein bound

We define a sequence of variance parameters
\sigma^{2}_{X,k}
and
\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}_{X,k}
for the tensor
X
for
k\in[K]
,

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{X,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\left\|{\overline{X}_{k}\times_{k}\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{1}_{N_{k}}}^{\perp}}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}_{k}}^{2}=\frac{1}{\prod_{l=k}^{K}N_{l}}\cdot\left\|{X_{k}\times_{k}\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{1}_{N_{k}}}^{\perp}}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}_{X,k}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}_{X,k}+\epsilon_{N_{k}}\cdot\prod_{l=0}^{k-1}N_{l}\cdot\left\|{\overline{X}_{k}\times_{k}\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{1}_{N_{k}}}^{\perp}}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}=\sigma^{2}_{X,k}+\epsilon_{N_{k}}\cdot\prod_{l=0}^{k-1}N_{l}\cdot\left\|{X_{k}\times_{k}\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{1}_{N_{k}}}^{\perp}}\right\|_{\infty}^{2},

analogous to the definition of
\sigma^{2}_{W,k}
above.
Define a vector
\overline{w}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{k}}
for
k\in[K]
whose
i
-th coordinate is
\sum_{i_{l}\in[N_{l}],l\in[k-1]}\left|(\overline{W}_{k}\times_{k}\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{1}_{N_{k}}}^{\perp})_{i_{1}\cdots i_{k-1}i}\right|
. We are now ready to present the Bernstein-type MGF bound. The proof is in Appendix
B.2
.

Theorem 2

(
Bernstein-type MGF bound under mode-exchangeability
)
.

For a fixed
W\in\mathbb{T}
and a mode-exchangeable
X\in\mathbb{T}
, let
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{X}=\sigma(\widetilde{\sigma}_{X,1},\cdots,\widetilde{\sigma}_{X,K})
, then for
|\lambda|\leq\min_{k\in[K]}\frac{3}{2\left\|{\overline{w}_{k}}\right\|_{\infty}(1+\epsilon_{N_{k}})}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\exp\left\{{\lambda Z}\right\}\mid\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{X}}\right]\leq\exp\left\{{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\left({1+\epsilon_{N_{k}}}\right)\cdot\prod_{l=k+1}^{K}N_{l}\cdot\sigma^{2}_{W,k}\cdot\widetilde{\sigma}_{X,k}^{2}}{1-\frac{2|\lambda|}{3}\left\|{\overline{w}_{k}}\right\|_{\infty}(1+\epsilon_{N_{k}})}}\right\}

(2a)

Consequently for all
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{{Z\geq\sqrt{2\log\frac{1}{\delta}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{K}\prod_{l=k+1}^{K}N_{l}\cdot\left({1+\epsilon_{N_{k}}}\right)\sigma^{2}_{W,k}\widetilde{\sigma}_{X,k}^{2}}+\frac{2\max_{k\in[K]}(1+\epsilon_{N_{k}})\left\|{\overline{w}_{k}}\right\|_{\infty}}{3}\cdot\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\right\}\leq\delta.

(2b)

We can apply further relaxations to Eqs. (
2a
) and (
2b
) to simplify the bounds, for greater interpretability. Note that

\sigma^{2}_{W,k}=\left\|{W_{k}\times_{k}\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{1}_{N_{k}}}^{\perp}}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}=\left\|{W_{k}}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}-\left\|{W_{k}\times_{k}\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{1}_{N_{k}}}}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}=\left\|{W_{k}}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}-\left\|{W_{k-1}}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
, which indicates
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma_{W,k}^{2}=\left\|{W}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}-\left\|{W_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}\leq\left\|{W}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
. Therefore

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\prod_{l=k+1}^{K}N_{l}\cdot\left({1+\epsilon_{N_{k}}}\right)\sigma^{2}_{W,k}\widetilde{\sigma}_{X,k}^{2}\leq\left\|{W}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}\cdot\max_{k\in[K]}\prod_{l=k+1}^{K}N_{l}\cdot\left({1+\epsilon_{N_{k}}}\right)\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}_{X,k}.

This leads immediately to the following corollary:

Corollary 2.1
.

In the setting of Theorem
2
, it also holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{{Z\geq\sqrt{2\log\frac{1}{\delta}\cdot\left\|{W}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}\cdot\max_{k\in[K]}\prod_{l=k+1}^{K}N_{l}\cdot\left({1+\epsilon_{N_{k}}}\right)\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}_{X,k}}+\frac{2\max_{k\in[K]}(1+\epsilon_{N_{k}})\left\|{\overline{w}_{k}}\right\|_{\infty}}{3}\cdot\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\right\}\leq\delta.

(3)

2.2.1
Comparing exchangeability and independence: the role of dimension

To help interpret these results, we next consider the role of the dimensions,
N_{1},\dots,N_{K}
, in these bounds. As a baseline, suppose that the entries of
X
are i.i.d. unbiased signs—that is,
X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}\sim\textnormal{Unif}(\{\pm 1\})
. In this case,
Z=\langle X,W\rangle
has mean zero and variance
\left\|{W}\right\|^{2}_{\mathbb{T}}
, and thus we have
|Z|=O_{P}(\left\|{W}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}})
. On the other hand, in Corollary
2.1
, if we consider only the leading term then we have
|Z|=O_{P}(\left\|{W}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}\cdot\sqrt{\prod_{k=2}^{K}N_{K}})
.

However, we will now see that this is unavoidable, due to the way that exchangeability is substantially weaker than independence as soon as
K>1
. In particular, consider the following mode-exchangeable tensor: let
Y=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{N_{1}})
where
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{N_{1}}\sim\textnormal{Unif}(\{\pm 1\})
are i.i.d., and let
X=Y\otimes\mathbf{1}_{N_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{1}_{N_{K}}
. Therefore
X
is constructed with
N_{1}
independent samples (rather than
\prod_{k=1}^{K}N_{k}
). In particular,
Z=\langle X,W\rangle_{\mathbb{T}}
again has mean zero, but now has variance
\|W_{1}\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}\cdot\prod_{k=2}^{K}N_{k}
. This explains why the rate of concentration differs by a factor of
\sum_{k=2}^{K}N_{k}
between the exchangeable and i.i.d. cases.

3
Concentration inequalities for matrix-valued data

In this section, we consider concentration inequalities for sequential data of matrix-valued entries. Recall that, in this setting, we work with a sequence of weight matrices
W_{1},\cdots,W_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
, and random matrices
X_{1},\cdots,X_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}
that are assumed to be exchangeable—i.e., for any
\pi\in\mathscr{S}_{N}
, it holds that
(X_{1},\cdots,X_{N})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{d}}}{{=}}(X_{\pi(1)},\cdots,X_{\pi(N)})
. Using the tensor notation, we denote by
W\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p\times q}
and
X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times q\times r}
the tensors
(W_{1},\cdots,W_{N})
and
(X_{1},\cdots,X_{N})
. We then consider the bilinear mapping
\llangle\cdot,\cdot\rrangle:\mathbb{R}^{N\times p\times q}\times\mathbb{R}^{N\times q\times r}\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
,

Z=\llangle W,X\rrangle:=\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}X_{k}.

For any
A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, this bilinear mapping satisfies the following property (similar to that of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{T}}
):

\displaystyle\llangle W\times_{1}A,X\rrangle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left({\sum_{l=1}^{N}A_{kl}W_{l}}\right)X_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}\left({\sum_{l=1}^{N}A_{lk}X_{l}}\right)=\llangle W,X\times_{1}A^{\top}\rrangle.

Let
\overline{W}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}
and
\overline{X}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}X_{k}
. Since
Z
is now matrix-valued, in this setting we can study the concentration of
Z
via tail bounds on
\left\|{\llangle W,X\rrangle-N\cdot\overline{W}\;\overline{X}}\right\|
measured in operator norm. As the natural generalization of MGF in the scalar case, we will see that can derive Chernoff-type concentration bounds—including Hoeffding and Bernstein bounds—by studying the matrix MGF defined through trace exponentials (see the following Sec.
3.1
).

3.1
The Hoeffding bound (general case)

To begin with, we highlight a key difference between the matrix-valued case and the scalar-valued case is that the symmetry breaks. Even if
p=r
, we cannot expect
\llangle W,X\rrangle=\llangle X,W\rrangle
to hold generically unless
p=q=r=1
. This is due to the non-commutativity of matrix multiplication. As we shall see in the following, this asymmetry introduces some challenges for deriving concentration bounds. Nevertheless, we can obtain a generic Hoeffding-type tail bound without any additional assumptions on the commutativity between
W
and
X
as follows.

First we recall some notation for working with matrix-valued data. For a square matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, recall the matrix exponential
\exp A:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}A^{k}
. We define the following normalized exponential operator
\overline{\exp}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle\overline{\exp}(A):=\frac{1}{n+m}\Tr\left\{{\exp\begin{bmatrix}0&A\\
A^{\top}&0\end{bmatrix}}\right\}.

The enlarged symmetrized matrix in
\mathbb{S}^{n+m}
on the right hand side is the dilated matrix of
A
. The so-called dilation trick
[
35
]
serves as a powerful idea in the operator theory. These tools enable the following bound.

Theorem 3

(
Generic Hoeffding-type bound for exchangeable matrix-valued data
)
.

For any positive integers
N,p,q,r
, let
W_{1},\cdots,W_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
be a fixed sequence of weight matrices and
X_{1},\cdots,X_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}
be an exchangeable sequence of random data where
\left\|{X_{k}}\right\|\leq 1
for all
k\in[N]
.
Then it holds for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\overline{\exp}\left\{{\lambda\left({\llangle W,X\rrangle-N\cdot\overline{W}\;\overline{X}}\right)}\right\}}\right]\leq\exp\left\{{8\lambda^{2}(1+\epsilon_{N})^{2}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\|{W_{k}}\right\|^{2}}\right\},

(4a)

and consequently,
for all
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{{\left\|{\llangle W,X\rrangle-N\cdot\overline{W}\;\overline{X}}\right\|\geq 4(1+\epsilon_{N})\sqrt{2\log\frac{p+r}{\delta}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\|{W_{k}}\right\|^{2}}}\right\}\leq\delta.

(4b)

This result holds in general, without any commutativity type conditions. However, as we will see next, under additional conditions we can derive a much stronger result—in particular, by improving the term
\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\|{W_{k}}\right\|^{2}
.

3.2
The Hoeffding bound (under commutativity conditions)

Although commutativity does not hold in general, we can improve the tail bound derived above in settings where we may assume additional constraints in the form of Sylvester equations on the weight matrices
W_{k}
and the random matrices
X_{k}
. We introduce the assumption as follows.

Assumption 1

(Commutativity conditions)
.

For the given weight
W\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p\times q}
, we say the exchangeable matrix-valued sequence
X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times q\times r}
satisfy the commutativity constraint, if there exist a positive integer
\widetilde{q}
, an associated weight
V\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times\widetilde{q}\times r}
and an injection
\iota:\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times\widetilde{q}}
such that the following holds on the support of
X_{1}
,

\displaystyle W_{k}X_{1}=\iota(X_{1})V_{k}=:\widetilde{X}_{1}V_{k},\qquad\qquad\text{for all }k\in[N].

Denoting by
\widetilde{X}=(\widetilde{X}_{1},\cdots,\widetilde{X}_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times\widetilde{q}\times r}
, we say
(\widetilde{X},V)
is the commutativity pair of
(W,X)
.

Remark 1
.

It is apparent that
\widetilde{X}
is also an exchangeable sequence of matrices following from the push-forward distribution of
X
by
\iota
. Therefore, the concentration of the quantity
\llangle W,X\rrangle
is equivalent to studying
\llangle\widetilde{X},V\rrangle
, reconciling the aforementioned asymmetry of left multiplication on the data. As an illustration of the special case when
q=r
and
X_{k}=x_{k}I_{q}
for an exchangeable scalar sequence
(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N})
, we can set
\widetilde{q}=p
for any weight
W
and
\widetilde{X}_{k}=x_{k}I_{p},V_{k}=W_{k}
.

The commutativity conditions, in fact, apply much more broadly than simply the case of scalar random sequences in the remark above—we will see several concrete examples in Sections
4
and
5
below.

With this assumption in place, we are now ready to give a more powerful Hoeffding-type bound for the matrix setting.

Theorem 4

(
Hoeffding-type MGF bound for exchangeable matrix-valued data
)
.

For any positive integers
N,p,q,r
, let
W_{1},\cdots,W_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
be a fixed sequence of weight matrices and
X_{1},\cdots,X_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}
be an exchangeable sequence of random data where
\left\|{X_{k}}\right\|\leq 1
for all
k\in[N]
. If Assumption
1
holds for the commutativity pair
(\widetilde{X},V)
where
\left\|{\widetilde{X}_{k}}\right\|\leq 1
for all
k\in[N]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\overline{\exp}\left\{{\lambda\left({\llangle W,X\rrangle-N\cdot\overline{W}\;\overline{X}}\right)}\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{{8\lambda^{2}(1+\epsilon_{N})\cdot\max\left\{{\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}W_{k}^{\top}}\right\|,\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}V_{k}^{\top}V_{k}}\right\|}\right\}}\right\}.

(5a)

Consequently, for all
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{{\left\|{\llangle W,X\rrangle-N\cdot\overline{W}\;\overline{X}}\right\|\geq 4\sqrt{2\log\frac{p+r}{\delta}\cdot(1+\epsilon_{N})\max\left\{{\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}W_{k}^{\top}}\right\|,\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}V_{k}^{\top}V_{k}}\right\|}\right\}}}\right\}\leq\delta.

(5b)

To compare these bounds to the results of the previous theorem, we can see that the tail bounds established in Theorem
4
rely on
\max\{\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}W_{k}^{\top}}\right\|,\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}V_{k}^{\top}V_{k}}\right\|\}
, rather than
\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\|{W_{k}}\right\|^{2}
as in Theorem
3
. This is potentially a much tighter bound: indeed, we always have

\max\left\{\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}W_{k}^{\top}}\right\|,\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}V_{k}^{\top}V_{k}}\right\|\right\}\leq\max\left\{{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\|{W_{k}}\right\|^{2},\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\|{V_{k}}\right\|^{2}}\right\}

and the gap in this inequality may be as large as a factor of
O(N)
(e.g., if each
W_{k}
and
V_{k}
are rank-1 matrices). However, the commutativity conditions assumed in Theorem
4
can be restrictive, where as Theorem
3
always applies offering greater flexibility.

3.3
The Bernstein bound (under commutativity conditions)

We then move on to deriving Bernstein-type bounds for exchangeable matrix-valued data when the commutativity conditions hold. Let

\displaystyle\Sigma=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(X_{k}-\overline{X})(X_{k}-\overline{X})^{\top},\qquad\widetilde{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\widetilde{X}_{k}-\overline{\widetilde{X}})^{\top}(\widetilde{X}_{k}-\overline{\widetilde{X}}),

and we can define the variance parameters
\sigma_{X}^{2}=\left\|{\Sigma}\right\|
and
\sigma_{\widetilde{X}}^{2}=\left\|{\widetilde{\Sigma}}\right\|
, as well as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}_{X}^{2}=\sigma_{X}^{2}+\epsilon_{N}\max_{k\in[N]}\left\|{X_{k}-\overline{X}}\right\|^{2},\qquad\qquad\widetilde{\sigma}_{\widetilde{X}}^{2}=\sigma_{\widetilde{X}}^{2}+\epsilon_{N}\max_{k\in[N]}\left\|{{\widetilde{X}}_{k}-\overline{{\widetilde{X}}}}\right\|^{2}.

Theorem 5

(
Bernstein-type MGF bound for exchangeable matrix-valued data
)
.

Let Assumption
1
hold for
N\geq 2
. If
\left\|{X_{k}}\right\|\leq 1
and
\left\|{\widetilde{X}_{k}}\right\|\leq 1
for all
k\in[N]
, we have for
w=(\left\|{W_{1}}\right\|,\cdots,\left\|{W_{N}}\right\|)^{\top}
,
v=(\left\|{V_{1}}\right\|,\cdots,\left\|{V_{N}}\right\|)^{\top}
and
|\lambda|\leq\frac{3}{2\min\{\left\|{w}\right\|_{\infty},\left\|{v}\right\|_{\infty}\}(1+\epsilon_{N})}
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\overline{\exp}\left\{{\lambda\left({\llangle W,X\rrangle-N\cdot\overline{W}\;\overline{X}}\right)}\right\}\mid\widetilde{\sigma}_{X}^{2},\widetilde{\sigma}_{\widetilde{X}}^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\exp\left\{{\frac{\lambda^{2}(1+\epsilon_{N})^{2}}{2\left({1-\frac{2|\lambda|(1+\epsilon_{N})}{3}\min\{\left\|{w}\right\|_{\infty},\left\|{v}\right\|_{\infty}\}}\right)}\cdot\max\left\{{\widetilde{\sigma}_{X}^{2}\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}W_{k}^{\top}}\right\|,\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}_{\widetilde{X}}\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}V_{k}^{\top}V_{k}}\right\|}\right\}}\right\}.

(6a)

Consequently, for all
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigg\{\left\|{\llangle W,X\rrangle-N\cdot\overline{W}\;\overline{X}}\right\|\geq(1+\epsilon_{N})\sqrt{2\log\frac{p+r}{\delta}\cdot\max\left\{{\widetilde{\sigma}_{X}^{2}\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}W_{k}^{\top}}\right\|,\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}_{\widetilde{X}}\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}V_{k}^{\top}V_{k}}\right\|}\right\}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{2\min\{\left\|{w}\right\|_{\infty},\left\|{v}\right\|_{\infty}\}}{3}(1+\epsilon_{N})\log\frac{p+r}{\delta}\Bigg\}\leq\delta.

(6b)

We defer its proof to Appendix
C.2
.

4
Theoretical implications

In this section, we examine several implications of our general results, deriving bounds for a range of different special cases.

4.1
Weighted sum of exchangeable random variables

We first apply our results in Sec.
2
and
3
where the random terms consist of an exchangeable sequence of random variables
\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{N}\in[-1,1]
. Specifically, we consider the following general weighted sum of exchangeable random variables, where the weights may be scalar-valued or matrix-valued:

(Scalar-valued weights)

\displaystyle\langle w,\xi\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{k}\xi_{k},

(Matrix-valued weights)

\displaystyle\llangle W,\xi I_{r}\rrangle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}\xi_{k}.

To relate these sums to our general notation from before, in the first case we simply have
X_{k}=\xi_{k}
, while in the second case we are working with matrix-valued random variables
X_{k}=\xi_{k}I_{r}
.

Let
\overline{\xi}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\xi_{k}
,
\sigma^{2}_{\xi}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\xi-\overline{\xi})^{2}
and
\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}_{\xi}:=\sigma^{2}_{\xi}+\epsilon_{N}\left\|{\xi-\overline{\xi}}\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}_{\xi}+4\epsilon_{N}
. For the scalar-valued weights case, we apply Theorems
1
and
2
with
K=1
. Note that these bounds exactly recover (with the same constants) the main results in
3
.

Corollary 4.1

(
Scalar weighted sum of exchangeable r.v.s
)
.

For
w=(w_{1},\cdots,w_{N})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and all
\delta\in(0,1)
, assume
\overline{w}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}w_{k}=0
.

1.

(Hoeffding bound)
For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\exp\left\{{\lambda\langle w,\xi\rangle}\right\}}\right]\leq\exp\left\{{\frac{\lambda^{2}(1+\epsilon_{N})}{2}\cdot\left\|{w}\right\|^{2}}\right\},

and consequently
\mathbb{P}\left\{{\langle w,\xi\rangle\geq\sqrt{2(1+\epsilon_{N})\log 1/\delta}\cdot\left\|{w}\right\|}\right\}\leq\delta
.

2.

(Bernstein bound)
In addition, we have for
|\lambda|\leq\frac{3}{2\left\|{w}\right\|_{\infty}(1+\epsilon_{N})}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\exp\left\{{\lambda\langle w,\xi\rangle}\right\}\mid\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}_{\xi}}\right]\leq\exp\left\{{\frac{\lambda^{2}(1+\epsilon_{N})}{2\left({1-\frac{2|\lambda|}{3}\left\|{w}\right\|_{\infty}(1+\epsilon_{N})}\right)}\cdot\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}_{\xi}\left\|{w}\right\|^{2}}\right\},

and consequently
\mathbb{P}\left\{{\langle w,\xi\rangle\geq\sqrt{2(1+\epsilon_{N})\log 1/\delta}\cdot\widetilde{\sigma}_{\xi}\left\|{w}\right\|+\frac{2}{3}\left\|{w}\right\|_{\infty}(1+\epsilon_{N})\log 1/\delta}\right\}\leq\delta
.

Proof

The results follow from taking
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}^{N},W=w-\overline{w}\mathbf{1}_{N}
and
X=\xi
in Theorems
1
and
2
.
∎

Next we apply Theorems
4
and
5
to a sequence of fixed matrices
W_{1},\cdots,W_{N}
. The result that follows recovers the Hoeffding bound in
43
and Bernstein bound in
42
up to a multiplicative factor
(1+\epsilon_{N})
, as
43
’s results are on independent random variables. (In the next section, we discuss how to recover transport exchangeable results to i.i.d. results, which removes the
(1+\epsilon_{N})
factor.)

The numerical constants however, comparing to the scalar-valued weights case, are indeed improvable provided
\xi_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{d}}}{{=}}-\xi_{k}
. We refer the readers to
[
43
, Remark 7.4]
for further discussions.

Corollary 4.2

(
Matrix weighted sum of exchangeable r.v.s
)
.

For
W_{1},\cdots,W_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
and all
\delta\in(0,1)
, assume
\overline{W}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}=0
and let
\sigma^{2}_{W}:=\max\{\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}W_{k}^{\top}}\right\|,\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}^{\top}W_{k}}\right\|\}
.

1.

(Hoeffding bound)
For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\overline{\exp}\left\{{\lambda\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}\xi_{k}}\right\}}\right]\leq\exp\left\{{8\lambda^{2}(1+\epsilon_{N})\cdot\sigma_{W}^{2}}\right\},

and consequently
\mathbb{P}\left\{{\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}\xi_{k}}\right\|\geq 4\sqrt{2(1+\epsilon_{N})\log\frac{p+r}{\delta}}\cdot\sigma_{W}}\right\}\leq\delta
.

2.

(Bernstein bound)
Additionally, suppose
\left\|{W_{k}}\right\|\leq L
for all
k\in[N]
. We have for
|\lambda|\leq\frac{3}{2L(1+\epsilon_{N})}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\overline{\exp}\left\{{\lambda\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}\xi_{k}}\right\}\mid\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}_{\xi}}\right]\leq\exp\left\{{\frac{\lambda^{2}(1+\epsilon_{N})}{2\left({1-\frac{2|\lambda|L}{3}(1+\epsilon_{N})}\right)}\cdot\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}_{\xi}\sigma_{W}^{2}}\right\},

and consequently
\mathbb{P}\left\{{\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}\xi_{k}}\right\|\geq\sqrt{2(1+\epsilon_{N})\log\frac{p+r}{\delta}}\cdot\widetilde{\sigma}_{\xi}\sigma_{W}+\frac{2L}{3}(1+\epsilon_{N})\log\frac{p+r}{\delta}}\right\}\leq\delta
.

Proof

We first verify Assumption
1
by taking
X_{k}=\xi_{k}I_{r}
,
\widetilde{X}_{k}=\xi_{k}I_{p}
and
V_{k}=W_{k}
. The MGF inequalities then follow from Theorems
4
and
5
. The tail bounds are consequences of Lemma
A.5
.

4.2
Connections to the i.i.d. setting

Next, we explore the connection between our results derived under the assumption of exchangeability, and the more standard setting of i.i.d. data.

To introduce this question, first recall the the scalar setting studied in
[
3
]
, where the aim is to establish concentration of the weighted sum
w_{1}X_{1}+\dots+w_{N}X_{N}
for exchangeable random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}\in\mathbb{R}
. It is shown in
[
3
]
that if
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
can be embedded into longer exchangeable sequence
X_{1},\dots,X_{\widetilde{N}}
(for
\widetilde{N}\geq N
), then the concentration bounds of Corollary
4.1
hold with
\epsilon_{\widetilde{N}}
in place of
\epsilon_{N}
. At the extreme, if the
X_{i}
’s are i.i.d., we can take
\widetilde{N}
to be arbitrarily large; since
\epsilon_{\widetilde{N}}\to 0
as
\widetilde{N}\to\infty
, this means that the results of Corollary
4.1
recover the usual Hoeffding and Bernstein bounds for sums of independent random variables. In this section, we will see that the same type of conclusion holds for the tensor and matrix settings considered in this paper.

4.2.1
Comparing to the i.i.d. case for mode-exchangeable tensors

As previewed in Section
2.2.1
, for the setting of a mode-exchangeable tensor, we can compare to a setting where the data is “i.i.d. along one mode”—that is, we sample i.i.d.
(K-1)
-mode tensors.
Specifically, suppose that
X\in[-1,1]^{N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{K}}
is i.i.d. along the
K
-th mode: that is, the
(K-1)
-mode tensors

\mathscr{X}_{l}:=X\times_{K}e_{l}=(X_{i_{1}\dots i_{K-1}l})_{i_{1}\in[N_{1}],\dots,i_{K-1}\in[N_{K-1}]}\in\mathbb{T}_{K-1}

are i.i.d. for each
l=1,\dots,N_{K}
following a probability distribution
P
on
\mathbb{T}_{K-1}
, whose real-valued mean and variance parameters are

\displaystyle\mu_{P}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{X}\sim P}[\overline{\mathscr{X}}],\qquad\sigma_{P}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{X}\sim P}\left[{\left\|{\mathscr{X}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{X}\sim P}[\mathscr{X}]}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}_{K-1}}^{2}}\right].

Similarly, we can define the
N_{K}
weight tensors
\mathscr{W}_{1},\cdots,\mathscr{W}_{N_{K}}\in\mathbb{T}_{K-1}
. In this case, the relevant inner product can be written as a sum of
N_{K}
independent terms as

\displaystyle Z=\langle W,X\rangle_{\mathbb{T}_{K}}=\sum_{l=1}^{N_{K}}\langle\mathscr{W}_{l},\mathscr{X}_{l}\rangle_{\mathbb{T}_{K-1}}.

Let
w\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{K}}
be the vector whose
i
-th coordinate is
\sum_{i_{l}\in[N_{l}],l\in[K-1]}|W_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K-1}i}|=\left\|{\mathscr{W}_{i}}\right\|_{1}
. In order to invoke classical results concerning sums of independent random variables, we observe that the
l
-th summand
\langle\mathscr{W}_{l},\mathscr{X}_{l}\rangle_{\mathbb{T}_{K-1}}
satisfies

\left|\langle\mathscr{W}_{l},\mathscr{X}_{l}\rangle_{\mathbb{T}_{K-1}}\right|\leq\left\|{\mathscr{W}_{l}}\right\|_{1}\cdot\left\|{\mathscr{X}_{l}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq w_{l},\quad\mathsf{Var}(\langle\mathscr{W}_{l},\mathscr{X}_{l}\rangle_{\mathbb{T}_{K-1}})=\mathbb{E}\left[{\left({\langle\mathscr{W}_{l},\mathscr{X}_{l}-\mu_{P}\rangle_{\mathbb{T}_{K-1}}}\right)^{2}}\right]\leq\left\|{\mathscr{W}_{l}}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}_{K-1}}^{2}\cdot\sigma_{P}^{2}.

By applying the classical Hoeffding inequality for sums of independent random variables, we obtain for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp\{\lambda(Z-\mathbb{E}[Z])\}]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\cdot\sum_{l=1}^{N_{K}}\left\|{\mathscr{W}_{l}}\right\|_{1}^{2}}\right\},

while the classical Bernstein bound yields for all
|\lambda|\leq\frac{3}{2\left\|{w}\right\|_{\infty}}
the following MGF bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp\{\lambda(Z-\mathbb{E}[Z])\}]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\left({1-\frac{2|\lambda|}{3}\left\|{w}\right\|_{\infty}}\right)}\cdot\sum_{l=1}^{N_{K}}\left\|{\mathscr{W}_{l}}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}_{K-1}}^{2}\cdot\sigma_{P}^{2}}\right\}.

To better relate to our bounds, we apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
\left\|{\mathscr{W}_{l}}\right\|_{1}^{2}\leq\prod_{l=1}^{K-1}N_{l}\cdot\left\|{\mathscr{W}_{l}}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}_{K-1}}^{2}
(which is an equality when
K=2
) and the identity
\sum_{l=1}^{N_{K}}\left\|{\mathscr{W}_{l}}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}_{K-1}}^{2}=\left\|{W}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
to arrive at the following.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp\{\lambda(Z-\mathbb{E}[Z])\}]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\cdot\prod_{l=1}^{K-1}N_{l}\cdot\left\|{W}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle\text{for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{R},

(7a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp\{\lambda(Z-\mathbb{E}[Z])\}]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\left({1-\frac{2|\lambda|}{3}\left\|{w}\right\|_{\infty}}\right)}\cdot\left\|{W}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}\sigma_{P}^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle\text{for all }|\lambda|\leq\frac{3}{2\left\|{w}\right\|_{\infty}}.

(7b)

4.2.2
Interpolation between exchangeability and i.i.d. bounds

Next we will see how our results, under exchangeability, agree with these bounds in the limit. Suppose that
\widetilde{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times\dots\times N_{K-1}\times\widetilde{N}_{K}}=:\widetilde{\mathbb{T}}
is mode-exhangeable, for some
\widetilde{N}_{K}\geq N_{K}
, and
X\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times\dots\times N_{K}}
is obtained by simply taking the appropriate sub-tensor of
\widetilde{X}
.
We will also need an additional assumption:

Definition 4.1

(Balanced weights along mode-
K
)
.

We say
W
is balanced along mode-
K
if
\overline{W}_{K-1}=\overline{W}\mathbf{1}_{N_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{1}_{N_{K-1}}
.

Then, assuming that
W
is balanced along mode-
K
, the MGF bound in Theorem
1
admits significant simplification through

\displaystyle\sigma_{W,k}=\left\|{W_{k}\times_{k}\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{1}_{N_{k}}}^{\perp}}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}=\left\|{\overline{W}_{K-1}\times_{k}\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{1}_{N_{k}}}^{\perp}}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}_{K-1}}^{2}=0,\qquad\textnormal{for all }k\in[K-1].

and thus (see details of the reduction from
\widetilde{\mathbb{T}}
to
\mathbb{T}
in Appendix
D.1
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp\{\lambda(Z-\mathbb{E}[Z])\}]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\cdot\prod_{l=1}^{K-1}N_{l}\cdot(1+\epsilon_{\widetilde{N}_{K}})\left\|{W}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle\text{for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

In particular, taking
\widetilde{N}_{K}\to\infty
(i.e., if
X
is i.i.d. along the
K
-th mode), we see that this exactly recovers the classical calculation (
7a
), with the correct constants. Similarly, for the Bernstein bound, again assuming that
W
is balanced along mode-
K
, Theorem
2
yields (with details in Appendix
D.1
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp\{\lambda(Z-\mathbb{E}[Z])\}]\leq\exp\left\{{\frac{\lambda^{2}\cdot(1+\epsilon_{\widetilde{N}_{K}})}{2\left({1-\frac{2|\lambda|}{3}\left\|{w}\right\|_{\infty}(1+\epsilon_{\widetilde{N}_{K}}}\right)}\cdot\left\|{W}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}\widetilde{\sigma}_{\widetilde{X},K}^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle\text{for all }|\lambda|\leq\frac{3}{2\left\|{w}\right\|_{\infty}},

which again exactly recovers the classical calculation (
7b
), with the correct constants, if we take
\widetilde{N}_{K}\to\infty
and the convergence of sample variance
\widetilde{\sigma}_{\widetilde{X},K}^{2}\to\sigma_{P}^{2}
almost surely.

In other words, we can interpret our results as providing a natural interpolation between the exchangeable and i.i.d. settings, where if the dimension
\widetilde{N}_{K}
along mode-
K
is simply equal to
N_{K}
(the finite exchangeable setting) we have a weaker concentration bound, while if
\widetilde{N}_{K}\to\infty
(which limits to the setting of data that is “i.i.d. along mode-
K
”), then we recover the standard bounds for i.i.d. data.

Remark 2
.

Indeed, if
W
is balanced along mode-

, letting
\mu_{P}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{X}\sim P}[\overline{\mathscr{X}}]\in[-1,1]
,
Z-\mathbb{E}Z
admits the following simplification

Z-\mathbb{E}Z=\sum_{l=1}^{N_{K}}\langle\mathscr{W}_{l},\mathscr{X}_{l}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathscr{X}\sim P}[\mathscr{X}]\rangle_{\mathbb{T}_{K-1}}=\langle W,X-\mu_{P}\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{T}}\rangle_{\mathbb{T}}.

4.2.3
Connections to the i.i.d. matrix concentration inequalities

Similarly, we can generalize Theorems
4
and
5
by embedding into a longer exchangeable sequence of random matrices
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{\widetilde{N}}
with or without the commutativity conditions, and approaching i.i.d. results by taking
\widetilde{N}\to\infty
. As the general case provides limited insight with heavy notations, for simplicity, we only sketch the generalization to Cor.
4.2
in the previous section where we have
W_{1},\cdots,W_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
and
X_{k}=\xi_{k}I_{r}
in what follows. First we recall the i.i.d. case where
\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{iid}}}{{\sim}}P
on
[-1,1]
. For
Z=\llangle W,X\rrangle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\xi_{k}W_{k}
,
42
’s well-established i.i.d. results
[
43
,
42
]
imply that for the parameter

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{W}=\max\left\{{\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}W_{k}^{\top}}\right\|,\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}^{\top}W_{k}}\right\|}\right\},\qquad L=\max_{k\in[N]}\left\|{W_{k}}\right\|,

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\overline{\exp}\{\lambda(Z-\mathbb{E}[Z])\}]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{{8\lambda^{2}\cdot\sigma_{W}^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle\text{for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{R},

(8a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\overline{\exp}\{\lambda(Z-\mathbb{E}[Z])\}]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\left({1-\frac{2|\lambda|L}{3}}\right)}\cdot\sigma_{W}^{2}\mathsf{Var}(\xi)}\right\}

\displaystyle\text{for all }|\lambda|\leq\frac{3}{2L}.

(8b)

Now we see given only exchangeability, but if we can embed
\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{N}
into a longer sequence of length
\widetilde{N}
, our results in Cor.
4.2
immediately imply

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\overline{\exp}\{\lambda(Z-\mathbb{E}[Z])\}]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{{8\lambda^{2}(1+\epsilon_{\widetilde{N}})\cdot\sigma_{W}^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle\text{for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\overline{\exp}\{\lambda(Z-\mathbb{E}[Z])\}]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{{\frac{\lambda^{2}(1+\epsilon_{\widetilde{N}})}{2\left({1-\frac{2|\lambda|L}{3}(1+\epsilon_{\widetilde{N}})}\right)}\cdot\sigma_{W}^{2}\widetilde{\sigma}_{\xi}^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle\text{for all }|\lambda|\leq\frac{3}{2L}.

Taking
\widetilde{N}\to\infty
and we have
\epsilon_{\widetilde{N}}\to 0
and
\widetilde{\sigma}_{\xi}^{2}\to\mathsf{Var}(\xi)
almost surely. We therefore recover the standard i.i.d. matrix concentration inequalities in (
8a
) and (

), again with the correct constants.

4.3
Combinatorial sum of matrix arrays

As the final theoretical demonstration of our tools, we revisit the combinatorial sum of matrices
[
30
,
19
]
.
30
considered a deterministic array of Hermitian matrices
\{A_{i,j}\}_{i,j\in[N]}
, and studied the concentration of the random matrix
\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{k,\pi(k)}
, using the celebrated Chatterjee’s method of exchangeable pairs
[
10
]
(first appeared in
9
’s PhD thesis
[
9
]
). We generalize to the rectangular case, assuming
A_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. Overloading the tensor product
\otimes
in the previous sections by Kronecker product of matrices. For
A=(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},B=(b_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}\times n^{\prime}}
, let
A\otimes B\in\mathbb{R}^{mm^{\prime}\times nn^{\prime}}
be

\displaystyle A\otimes B:=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}B&\cdots&a_{1n}B\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{m1}B&\cdots&a_{mn}B\end{bmatrix}.

We make the crucial observation that we can find commutativity pair
(W,X)
and
(\widetilde{X},V)
satisfying Assumption
1
, such that
\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{k,\pi(k)}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}X_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\widetilde{X}_{k}V_{k}
. Concretely,

\displaystyle W_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{k,1}&\cdots&A_{k,N}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times nN},

\displaystyle X_{k}=e_{\pi(k)}\otimes I_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{nN\times n};

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}_{k}=e_{\pi(k)}^{\top}\otimes I_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times mN},

\displaystyle V_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{k,1}\\
\vdots\\
A_{k,N}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{mN\times n}.

X_{1},\cdots,X_{N}
are then naturally a sequence of exchangeable matrices. We are now ready to present the Bernstein-type concentration inequalities for the combinatorial sum of matrix array.

Corollary 4.3

(
Combinatorial matrix Bernstein inequality
)
.

Suppose the array of matrices
\{A_{i,j}\}_{i,j\in[N]}
satisfies
\sum_{i,j\in[N]}A_{i,j}=0
and
\left\|{A_{i,j}}\right\|\leq R
. It holds for

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=\max\left\{{\left\|{\sum_{i,j\in[N]}A_{i,j}A_{i,j}^{\top}}\right\|,\left\|{\sum_{i,j\in[N]}A_{i,j}^{\top}A_{i,j}}\right\|}\right\}

that when
|\lambda|<\frac{3}{2(1+\epsilon_{N})R}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\overline{\exp}\left\{{\lambda\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{k,\pi(k)}}\right\}}\right]\leq\exp\left\{{\frac{\lambda^{2}(1+\epsilon_{N})^{2}}{2\left({1-\frac{2|\lambda|(1+\epsilon_{N})R}{3}}\right)}\cdot\left({\frac{1}{N}+\epsilon_{N}\cdot\left({1-\frac{1}{N}}\right)}\right)\sigma^{2}}\right\},

and consequently for
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{{\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{k,\pi(k)}}\right\|\geq(1+\epsilon_{N})\sigma\sqrt{2\left({\frac{1}{N}+\epsilon_{N}\cdot\left({1-\frac{1}{N}}\right)}\right)\cdot\log\frac{m+n}{\delta}}+\frac{2(1+\epsilon_{N})R}{3}\log\frac{m+n}{\delta}}\right\}\leq\delta.

We defer the proof to Appendix
D.2
. This result recovers
[
30
, Cor. 10.3]
to the same rate in
N
, while achieving tighter constants.

5.1
Experimental design for estimating multi-factor average effect

We revisit Example
1.1
, where our goal is to estimate a weighted mean

\mu=\sum_{i_{l}\in[N_{l}],l\in[K]}p_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}Y_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}

while only subsampling a block of the tensor
Y
: we will only observe
Y_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}
for indices
(i_{1},\dots,i_{K})\in I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{K}
. We will use the Horvitz-Thompson estimator

\widehat{\mu}=\frac{\prod_{l=1}^{K}N_{l}}{\prod_{l=1}^{K}n_{l}}\cdot\sum_{i_{1}\in I_{1},\dots,i_{K}\in I_{K}}p_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}Y_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}=\frac{\prod_{l=1}^{K}N_{l}}{\prod_{l=1}^{K}n_{l}}\cdot\langle W,X\rangle_{\mathbb{T}},

where
X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}=\prod_{l=1}^{K}\mathds{1}_{i_{l}\in I_{l}}
. By construction,
\widehat{\mu}
has mean
\mu
, and variance

\sigma^{2}=\prod_{l=1}^{K}N_{l}\cdot\sum_{i_{l}\in[N_{l}],l\in[K]}p_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}^{2}Y_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}^{2}-\mu^{2}.

Tail bounds for the estimation error.

The next estimation error bound then follows from Theorem
2
. See its proof in Appendix
D.3
.

Corollary 5.1
.

For
k\in[K]
, let
B_{k}>0
be that for all
i_{k}\in[N_{k}]
,
\sum_{i_{l}\in[N_{l}],l\neq k}p_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}|Y_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}|\leq B_{k}
, and
\alpha_{k}\in(0,1]
be the sub-sampling rates such that
\prod_{l=1}^{k}n_{l}=\alpha_{k}\prod_{l=1}^{k}N_{l}
. For all
\delta\in(0,1)
, it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigg\{\widehat{\mu}-\mu\geq\sigma\sqrt{2\log\frac{1}{\delta}\cdot\max_{k\in[K]}\frac{1+\epsilon_{N_{k}}}{N_{k}\alpha_{k}}\cdot\left({1-\frac{n_{k}}{N_{k}}+\frac{\epsilon_{N_{k}}}{\alpha_{k}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{n_{k}<N_{k}}}\right)}+\frac{2\max_{k\in[K]}B_{k}(1+\epsilon_{N_{k}})}{3\alpha_{K}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\Bigg\}\leq\delta.

(9a)

We can also consider a special case, where we subsample along one mode only: we will take
I_{k}=[N_{k}]
for all
k=1,\dots,K-1
and subsample only along mode
K
.
For an
\alpha\in(0,1)
such that
N_{K}\alpha\geq 1
is an integer, we have the following tail bound under the Cartesian sub-sampling configuration
(n_{1},\cdots,n_{K})=(N_{1},\cdots,N_{K-1},N_{K}\alpha)
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigg\{\widehat{\mu}-\mu\geq\sigma\sqrt{2\log\frac{1}{\delta}\cdot\frac{1+\epsilon_{N_{K}}}{N_{K}\alpha}\cdot\left({1-\alpha+\frac{\epsilon_{N_{K}}}{\alpha}}\right)}+\frac{2\max_{k\in[K]}B_{k}(1+\epsilon_{N_{k}})}{3\alpha}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\Bigg\}\leq\delta.

(9b)

Informally speaking (assuming the variance term dominates in the preceding tail bounds), we can infer from Cor.
5.1
that under a sub-sampling rate constraint
\alpha\geq 1/N_{K}
, we can control deviation with
1-\delta
probability not exceeding

\displaystyle O\left({\sigma\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\log N_{K}}}{\alpha N_{K}}\vee\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha N_{K}}}}\right),

which recovers the i.i.d. convergence rate for
N_{K}
independent Bernoulli random variables with
p=\alpha
.

As a comparison, we can consider a different sampling strategy: suppose
X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}
are all i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables with
p=\alpha
. In this case, we should expect the tail bound to be

\displaystyle O\left({\sigma\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha N_{1}\cdots N_{K}}}}\right)

by standard concentration inequality. We recommend using Cartesian-product sub-sampling primarily in situations where one dimension is much larger than all the others combined, i.e., when
N_{K}\gg N_{1}N_{2}\cdots N_{K-1}
. More generally, as mentioned in Section
1
, Cartesian product sub-sampling may be needed for practical reasons of implementation even if it does not lead to better concentration.
(Of course, since we are free to reorder
(N_{1},\cdots,N_{K})
in Cor.
5.1
, from Eq. (
9b
), we can see that for any
i,j\in[K]
, the tail bound is tighter for sub-sampling along mode-
i
against mode-
j
under the same rate
\alpha
if
N_{i}>N_{j}
.)

5.2
Fixed-design sketching by discrete sine transforms

Next, we apply our mathematical tools to Example
1.1
. Consider
X_{k}=\theta_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}
a sequence of exchangeable matrices, where our goal is to estimate
\overline{\theta}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\theta_{k}
while reducing communication cost via sketching.

Let
U\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
be a fixed orthogonal matrix. Let
D_{1},\dots,D_{N}
be nonnegative diagonal matrices, each with at most
q^{\prime}
nonzero entries, such that
\sum_{k=1}^{N}D_{k}=I_{q}
. For each
k
, we can construct a matrix
U_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{q^{\prime}\times q}
such that
U_{k}^{\top}U_{k}=U^{\top}D_{k}U
—that is, the rows of
U_{k}
are constructed by selecting (and rescaling) the rows of
U
corresponding to nonzero entries of
D_{k}
.
These fixed matrices
U_{1},\dots,U_{N}
will then be used as the sketching matrices.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{N}
be an exchangeable sequence of data matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}
. Let
U_{1},\dots,U_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{q^{\prime}\times q}
be fixed matrices constructed as above.
Assume there are constants
L,M>0
such that
\left\|{\theta_{k}}\right\|_{F}\leq 1,\left\|{U_{k}^{\top}U_{k}}\right\|=\left\|{D_{k}}\right\|\leq L
and
\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}U_{k}^{\top}U_{k}U_{k}^{\top}U_{k}}\right\|=\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}D_{k}^{2}}\right\|\leq M
, and define the variance parameter

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}_{\theta}^{2}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\|{\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta}}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\epsilon_{N}\max_{k\in[N]}\left\|{\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta}}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

For all
\delta\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{{\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}U_{k}^{\top}U_{k}\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta}}\right\|\geq\widetilde{\sigma}_{\theta}\sqrt{2(1+\epsilon_{N})q^{\prime}M\log\frac{q+r}{\delta}}+\frac{2L}{3}(1+\epsilon_{N})\log\frac{q+r}{\delta}}\right\}\leq\delta.

(10)

To see how our result applies in a concrete setting, consider the orthogonal discrete sine transform (DST) matrix

6
Discussion

We provide concentration bounds of Hoeffding and Bernstein types in mode-exchangeable tensors and weighted sums of exchangeable matrix-valued data. The bounds we obtain recover and improve previously known results, notably in the i.i.d. case, weighted sum of random scalars, and combinatorial sums of matrix arrays. We are able to leverage the richer class of distributional symmetry studied in our paper to analyze wider class of problems, including estimating average effect of multi-factor models and fixed-design sketching applying to federated averaging. Still, intriguing questions remain as future directions.

Nonlinear exchangeable sums.

The results in our paper focus on concentration inequalities for the bilinear mapping
Z=\llangle W,X\rrangle
. It is interesting to ask if concentration occurs for a general nonlinear mapping
Z=f(W,X)
under the same assumptions of exchangeability in tensors or matrix-valued data. In the unweighted and independent case, McDiarmid’s inequality
[
33
]
holds and suggests similar results should hold for the weighted and exchangeable case, under mild assumptions such as Lipschitz continuity of
f
. As a concrete example, for matrix-valued data, developing concentration bounds for nonlinear sum
Z=\sum_{k=1}^{N}A_{k}W_{k}B_{k}
where
X=(A_{k},B_{k})
is of crucial interest in the analyses for problems like blind deconvolution
[
11
]
.

Nonasymptotic optimality.

As is previously raised in
[
3
]
, an interesting question to ask is the nonasymptotic optimality for the multiplicative terms
(1+\epsilon_{N_{k}})
in our theoretical results. In the unweighted case of sampling without replacement of random variables, Serfling’s inequality
[
39
]
suggests such inflated constants are unnecessary (and indeed, sampling without replacement can lead to concentration that is even be stronger than the i.i.d. bounds); on the other hand,
[
3
]
shows that for a general exchangeable setting some inflation is unavoidable.
In the setting of tensor data and matrix data studied here, are there settings where this inflation may be unnecessary (analogous to sampling without replacement)? And, what are concrete examples such that the inflated tails occur? A separate question is that of the role of the tensor structure: our bounds (cf. Theorems
4
and
5
) for multi-way tensors in general depend on the ordering of the modes
(N_{1},\cdots,N_{K})
. It is of course possible to optimize the bounds by choosing the best ordering, but it also suggests potentiality for improvement to results independent of the mode ordering.

Richer symmetry structures for matrix-valued data.

Our paper generalizes
3
’s results to two separate branches shown in Table.
1
. A natural future direction is to develop general results of sums like
Z=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1}}\cdots\sum_{i=1}^{N_{K}}W_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}
where
W_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}
and
X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}
are all matrix-valued, and
X_{i_{1}\cdots i_{K}}
are mode-exchangeable, providing a unified framework allowing richer symmetry structures for matrix-valued data. This can be helpful in simplifying proofs for combinatorial sum of matrix arrays in Sec.
4.3
, as naturally we can take
K=2
,
X_{ij}=A_{\pi(i),\pi^{\prime}(j)}
and
W_{ij}=\mathds{1}_{i=j}
, avoiding the careful construction of commutativity pairs in our paper.

Other symmetry groups and distributional invariances.

A higher level view of exchangeability is the relaxation of independence to distributional symmetry. Mode-wise and row-column-wise exchangeability are special cases of invariance under a group action and weaker symmetries, compared to the sequential exchangeability. However, the notion of distributional symmetry under group action can be much more general, such as cyclic group symmetry when
(X_{1},\cdots,X_{N})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{d}}}{{=}}(X_{2},\cdots,X_{N},X_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{d}}}{{=}}\cdots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathsf{d}}}{{=}}(X_{N},X_{1},\cdots,X_{N-1})
. This occurs for
(X_{1},\cdots,X_{N})\sim\mathsf{N}(0,\Sigma)
where
\Sigma
is a general semi-positive definite Toeplitz matrix, where the usual exchangeability breaks. The future direction is to develop concentration bounds given a general set of group action.

Lemma A.2
.

For any matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that
A\mathbf{1}_{n}=0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\Pi_{n}\in\mathscr{P}_{n}}\Pi_{n}^{\top}A\Pi_{n}=\frac{\Tr(A)}{n-1}\cdot\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{1}_{n}}^{\perp}.

Proof

Let
A^{\prime}
be the left hand side quantity. From
\Pi_{n}^{\top}A^{\prime}\Pi_{n}=A^{\prime}
for any
\Pi_{n}\in\mathscr{P}_{n}
,
A^{\prime}
must have identical off-diagonal entries and identical diagonal entries, namely
A^{\prime}=aI_{n}+b\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{\top}
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle A^{\prime}\mathbf{1}_{n}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\Pi_{n}\in\mathscr{P}_{n}}\Pi_{n}^{\top}A\Pi_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}=A^{\prime}\mathbf{1}_{n}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\Pi_{n}\in\mathscr{P}_{n}}\Pi_{n}^{\top}A\mathbf{1}_{n}=0

enforces
A^{\prime}=c\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{1}_{n}}^{\perp}
. To find the constant
c
, we take trace on both sides which implies

\displaystyle(n-1)c

\displaystyle=c\Tr(\mathsf{P}_{\mathbf{1}_{n}}^{\perp})=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\Pi_{n}\in\mathscr{P}_{n}}\Tr(\Pi_{n}^{\top}A\Pi_{n})=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\Pi_{n}\in\mathscr{P}_{n}}\Tr(A\Pi_{n}\Pi_{n}^{\top})=\Tr(A).

∎

Lemma A.3
.

When

Lemma A.5
.

For any random matrix
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}

\mathbb{E}[\overline{\exp}\{\lambda Z\}]\leq\exp\{\lambda^{2}a^{2}/2\}

\mathbb{E}[\overline{\exp}\{\lambda Z\}]\leq\exp\{\lambda^{2}a^{2}/(2-2b|\lambda|)\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{{\left\|{Z}\right\|\geq a\sqrt{2\log\frac{n+m}{\delta}}+b\log\frac{n+m}{\delta}}\right\}\leq\delta,\qquad\text{for all }\delta\in(0,1).

(in particular, Eq (
2b
) and Corollary
2.1
) such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigg\{\widehat{\mu}-\mu\geq\sigma\sqrt{2\log\frac{1}{\delta}\cdot\max_{k\in[K]}\Delta_{k}}+\frac{2\max_{k\in[K]}B_{k}(1+\epsilon_{N_{k}})}{3\alpha_{K}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\Bigg\}\leq\delta,

where
\sigma^{2}=\prod_{l=1}^{K}N_{l}\cdot\left({\left\|{W}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}-\left\|{W_{0}}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}^{2}}\right)
, and

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\prod_{l=1}^{K}N_{l}}\cdot\frac{\prod_{l=1}^{K}N_{l}^{2}}{\prod_{l=1}^{K}n_{l}^{2}}\cdot\prod_{l=k+1}^{K}N_{l}\cdot\left({1+\epsilon_{N_{k}}}\right)\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}_{X,k}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\prod_{l=1}^{K}N_{l}}{\prod_{l=1}^{K}n_{l}^{2}}\cdot\prod_{l=k+1}^{K}N_{l}\cdot\left({1+\epsilon_{N_{k}}}\right)\cdot\left\{{\frac{\prod_{l=1}^{k}n_{l}}{\prod_{l=1}^{k}N_{l}}\cdot\left({1-\frac{n_{k}}{N_{k}}}\right)+\epsilon_{N_{k}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{n_{k}<N_{k}}}\right\}\cdot\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}N_{l}\cdot\left({\frac{n_{k+1}\cdots n_{K}}{N_{k+1}\cdots N_{K}}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{l=1}^{k}N_{l}}{\prod_{l=1}^{k}n_{l}^{2}}\cdot\left({1+\epsilon_{N_{k}}}\right)\cdot\left\{{\frac{\prod_{l=1}^{k}n_{l}}{\prod_{l=1}^{k}N_{l}}\cdot\left({1-\frac{n_{k}}{N_{k}}}\right)+\epsilon_{N_{k}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{n_{k}<N_{k}}}\right\}\cdot\frac{\prod_{l=1}^{k}N_{l}}{N_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\epsilon_{N_{k}}}{N_{k}}\cdot\left\{{\frac{\prod_{l=1}^{k}N_{l}}{\prod_{l=1}^{k}n_{l}}\cdot\left({1-\frac{n_{k}}{N_{k}}}\right)+\epsilon_{N_{k}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{n_{k}<N_{k}}\left({\frac{\prod_{l=1}^{k}N_{l}}{\prod_{l=1}^{k}n_{l}}}\right)^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\epsilon_{N_{k}}}{N_{k}\alpha_{k}}\cdot\left({1-\frac{n_{k}}{N_{k}}+\frac{\epsilon_{N_{k}}}{\alpha_{k}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{n_{k}<N_{k}}}\right).

This completes the first part of the proof. For the second half, since
N_{K}\alpha\geq 1
, we can substitute
(n_{1},\cdots,n_{k})=(N_{1},\cdots,N_{K-1},\alpha N_{K})
into the preceding bound. The proof is done since under this choice

\displaystyle\frac{1+\epsilon_{N_{k}}}{N_{k}\alpha_{k}}\cdot\left({1-\frac{n_{k}}{N_{k}}+\frac{\epsilon_{N_{k}}}{\alpha_{k}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{n_{k}<N_{k}}}\right)=\mathds{1}_{k=K}\cdot\frac{1+\epsilon_{N_{K}}}{N_{K}\alpha}\cdot\left({1-\alpha+\frac{\epsilon_{N_{K}}}{\alpha}}\right).

D.4
Proof of Corollary
5.2

Let
U=\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}&\cdots&u_{q}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{u}_{1}&\cdots&\widetilde{u}_{q}\end{bmatrix}
and
\theta_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}\theta_{k,1}&\cdots\theta_{k,r}\end{bmatrix}
. Recall
U_{k}^{\top}U_{k}=U^{\top}D_{k}U
where
D_{k}=\mathop{\rm diag}(d_{k,1},\cdots,d_{k,q})\succeq 0
is a diagonal matrix with at most
q^{\prime}
nonzero elements.

Step 1: Check commutativity constraints by defining
(\widetilde{X},V)
.

The commutativity condition
1
holds for
W_{k}=U_{k}U_{k}^{\top}
and
X_{k}=\theta_{k}
. In fact

\displaystyle W_{k}X_{k}

\displaystyle=U^{\top}D_{k}U\theta_{k}=\sum_{l=1}^{q}d_{l}u_{l}u_{l}^{\top}\theta_{k}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}^{\top}\theta_{k}\,\,\cdots\,\,u_{q}^{\top}\theta_{k}&&&\\
&u_{1}^{\top}\theta_{k}\,\,\cdots\,\,u_{q}^{\top}\theta_{k}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&u_{1}^{\top}\theta_{k}\,\,\cdots\,\,u_{q}^{\top}\theta_{k}\end{bmatrix}}_{q\times q^{2}r}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}d_{k,1}u_{l,1}I_{r}\\
\vdots\\
d_{k,q}u_{q,1}I_{r}\\
d_{k,1}u_{l,2}I_{r}\\
\vdots\\
d_{k,q}u_{q,2}I_{r}\\
\vdots\\
d_{k,q}u_{q,q}I_{r}\end{bmatrix}}_{q^{2}r\times r}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left({I_{q}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}^{\top}\theta_{k}&\cdots&u_{q}^{\top}\theta_{k}\end{bmatrix}}\right)}_{:=\widetilde{X}_{k}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}D_{k}\widetilde{u}_{1}\otimes I_{r}\\
\vdots\\
D_{k}\widetilde{u}_{q}\otimes I_{r}\end{bmatrix}}_{=:V_{k}}=\widetilde{X}_{k}V_{k}.

Step 2: Checking boundedness conditions and weight parameters.

We verify conditions for Theorem
5
. For the data
X_{k}
and
\widetilde{X}_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|{X_{k}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|{\theta_{k}}\right\|\leq\left\|{\theta_{k}}\right\|_{F}\leq 1,

\displaystyle\left\|{\widetilde{X}_{k}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left\|{\widetilde{X}_{k}\widetilde{X}_{k}^{\top}}\right\|}=\sqrt{\left\|{\left({I_{q}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}^{\top}\theta_{k}&\cdots&u_{q}^{\top}\theta_{k}\end{bmatrix}}\right)\left({I_{q}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}^{\top}\theta_{k}&\cdots&u_{q}^{\top}\theta_{k}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}}\right)}\right\|}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left\|{I_{q}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}^{\top}\theta_{k}&\cdots&u_{q}^{\top}\theta_{k}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}^{\top}\theta_{k}&\cdots&u_{q}^{\top}\theta_{k}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}}\right\|}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left\|{I_{q}\otimes\sum_{l=1}^{q}u_{l}^{\top}\theta_{k}\theta_{k}^{\top}u_{l}}\right\|}=\sqrt{\Tr\left({U\theta_{k}\theta_{k}^{\top}U}\right)}=\left\|{\theta_{k}}\right\|_{F}\leq 1.

For the weight matrices, we have
\min\{\left\|{w}\right\|_{\infty},\left\|{v}\right\|_{\infty}\}\leq\left\|{w}\right\|_{\infty}=\max_{k\in[N]}\left\|{W_{k}}\right\|=\max_{k\in[N]}\left\|{D_{k}}\right\|=L
, and

\displaystyle\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}W_{k}^{\top}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}D_{k}^{2}}\right\|\leq M,

\displaystyle\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}V_{k}^{\top}V_{k}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{q}\left({D_{k}\widetilde{u}_{l}\otimes I_{r}}\right)^{\top}\left({D_{k}\widetilde{u}_{l}\otimes I_{r}}\right)}\right\|=\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{q}\widetilde{u}_{l}^{\top}D_{k}^{2}\widetilde{u}_{l}\otimes I_{r}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{q}\widetilde{u}_{l}^{\top}\left({\sum_{k=1}^{N}D_{k}^{2}}\right)\widetilde{u}_{l}=\Tr\left({U^{\top}\sum_{k=1}^{N}D_{k}^{2}U}\right)=\Tr\left({\sum_{k=1}^{N}D_{k}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq q^{\prime}\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}D_{k}^{2}}\right\|=q^{\prime}M.

In the last inequality, we make use of the fact that each
D_{k}
is diagonal with at most
q^{\prime}
nonzero entries.

Step 3: Bounding the data variance parameters.

Next, we calculate the variance parameters
\widetilde{\sigma}_{X}^{2}
and
\widetilde{\sigma}_{\widetilde{X}}^{2}
. Straightforwardly as
X_{k}=\theta_{k}
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}_{X}^{2}=\left\|{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})(\overline{\theta}-\theta_{k})^{\top}}\right\|+\epsilon_{N}\max_{k\in[N]}\left\|{\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta}}\right\|^{2}.

While for
\widetilde{X}
, since

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\widetilde{X}_{k}-\overline{\widetilde{X}})^{\top}(\widetilde{X}_{k}-\overline{\widetilde{X}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left({I_{q}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}^{\top}\theta_{k}&\cdots&u_{q}^{\top}\theta_{k}\end{bmatrix}}\right)^{\top}\left({I_{q}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}^{\top}\theta_{k}&\cdots&u_{q}^{\top}\theta_{k}\end{bmatrix}}\right)-\left({I_{q}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}^{\top}\overline{\theta}&\cdots&u_{q}^{\top}\overline{\theta}\end{bmatrix}}\right)^{\top}\left({I_{q}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}^{\top}\overline{\theta}&\cdots&u_{q}^{\top}\overline{\theta}\end{bmatrix}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}I_{q}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}\theta_{k}^{\top}u_{i}u_{j}^{\top}\theta_{k}\end{bmatrix}_{i,j\in[q]}-I_{q}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}\overline{\theta}u_{i}u_{j}^{\top}\overline{\theta}\end{bmatrix}_{i,j\in[q]},

we have

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\widetilde{X}}=\left\|{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}I_{q}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}\theta_{k}^{\top}u_{i}u_{j}^{\top}\theta_{k}\end{bmatrix}_{i,j\in[q]}-I_{q}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}\overline{\theta}u_{i}u_{j}^{\top}\overline{\theta}\end{bmatrix}_{i,j\in[q]}}\right\|=\left\|{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\begin{bmatrix}(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})^{\top}u_{i}u_{j}^{\top}(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})\end{bmatrix}_{i,j\in[q]}}\right\|.

Consequently

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\widetilde{X}}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\begin{bmatrix}(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})^{\top}u_{i}u_{j}^{\top}(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})\end{bmatrix}_{i,j\in[q]}}\right\|=\left\|{\frac{1}{N}\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})^{\top}u_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})^{\top}u_{q}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})^{\top}u_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})^{\top}u_{q}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|{\frac{1}{N}\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})^{\top}u_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})^{\top}u_{q}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})^{\top}u_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})^{\top}u_{q}\end{bmatrix}}\right\|=\left\|{\sum_{l=1}^{q}u_{l}^{\top}\left({\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})^{\top}}\right)u_{l}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\Tr\left({U\left({\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})^{\top}}\right)U^{\top}}\right)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\|{\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta}}\right\|_{F}^{2}.

Combining with

\displaystyle\left\|{\widetilde{X}_{k}-\overline{\widetilde{X}}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|{I_{q}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}^{\top}(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})&\cdots&u_{q}^{\top}(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})\end{bmatrix}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}^{\top}(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})&\cdots&u_{q}^{\top}(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})\end{bmatrix}}\right\|^{2}=\sum_{l=1}^{q}\left\|{u_{l}^{\top}(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Tr\left({U(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})(\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta})U^{\top}}\right)=\left\|{\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta}}\right\|_{F}^{2},

it follows that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}_{X}^{2}\leq\widetilde{\sigma}_{\widetilde{X}}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\|{\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta}}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\epsilon_{N}\max_{k\in[N]}\left\|{\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta}}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\widetilde{\sigma}_{\theta}^{2}.

Step 4: Concluding the proof.

We are now ready to substitute the above calculations into Theorem
5
. As

\displaystyle\min\{\left\|{w}\right\|_{\infty},\left\|{v}\right\|_{\infty}\}\leq L,

\displaystyle\max\left\{{\widetilde{\sigma}_{X}^{2}\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}W_{k}W_{k}^{\top}}\right\|,\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}_{\widetilde{X}}\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}V_{k}^{\top}V_{k}}\right\|}\right\}\leq q^{\prime}\widetilde{\sigma}_{\theta}^{2}M,

applying Lemma
A.5
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{{\left\|{\sum_{k=1}^{N}U_{k}^{\top}U_{k}\theta_{k}-\overline{\theta}}\right\|\geq\widetilde{\sigma}_{\theta}\sqrt{2(1+\epsilon_{N})q^{\prime}M\log\frac{q+r}{\delta}}+\frac{2L}{3}(1+\epsilon_{N})\log\frac{q+r}{\delta}}\right\}\leq\delta.

This completes the proof.
