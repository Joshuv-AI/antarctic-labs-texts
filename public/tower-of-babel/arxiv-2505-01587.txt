More efficient sifting for grid norms, and applications to multiparty communication complexity
Zander Kelley, Xin Lyu

Abstract
Building on the techniques behind the recent progress on the 3-term arithmetic progression problem [ 15 ] , Kelley, Lovett, and Meka [ 14 ] constructed the first explicit 3-player function f : [ N ] 3 → { 0 , 1 } f:[N]^{3}\rightarrow\{0,1\} that demonstrates a strong separation between randomized and (non-)deterministic NOF communication complexity. Specifically, their hard function can be solved by a randomized protocol sending O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) bits, but requires Ω ⁡ ( log 1 / 3 ⁡ ( N ) ) \Omega(\log^{1/3}(N)) bits of communication with a deterministic (or non-deterministic) protocol. We show a stronger Ω ⁡ ( log 1 / 2 ⁡ ( N ) ) \Omega(\log^{1/2}(N)) lower bound for their construction. To achieve this, the key technical advancement is an improvement to the sifting argument for grid norms of (somewhat dense) bipartite graphs. In addition to quantitative improvement, we qualitatively improve over [ 14 ] by relaxing the hardness condition: while [ 14 ] proved their lower bound for any function f f that satisfies a strong two-sided pseudorandom condition, we show that a weak one-sided condition suffices. This is achieved by a new structural result for cylinder intersections (or, in graph-theoretic language, the set of triangles induced from a tripartite graph), showing that any small cylinder intersection can be efficiently covered by a sum of simple “slice” functions.

Cylinder Intersections.

The basic combinatorial object of interest in the study of multiparty NOF communication complexity is the
cylinder intersection
.
In the
3
-party setting, a cylinder intersection
S
is a subset of a product space
X\times Y\times Z

whose indicator function has the form

\mathbbm{1}_{S}(x,y,z)=f(x,y)g(x,z)h(y,z).

Here,
f,g,
and
h
are indicator functions, each of which depends on only
2
out of
3
coordinates (so,
S
is the intersection of three “combinatorial cylinders”).
For the purpose of obtaining complexity lower bounds for NOF communication protocols,
it is important to understand the structural limitations of cylinder intersections (or at least, some particular limitation, which we can try to target).

Our main result is the following structural statement about small cylinder intersections (over
X\times Y\times Z
), which states that they can be somewhat efficiently covered by a small collection of simpler functions. To state it, we first describe this family of simple functions.

Definition 1.1

(Slice Function)
.

A slice function
s(x,y,z)
(on
\Omega:=X\times Y\times Z
) is an indicator function of the form

s(x,y,z)=g(x,y)h(z)\quad\text{or}\quad g(x)h(y,z)\quad\text{or}\quad g(x,z)h(y).

That is, a slice function is just a combinatorial rectangle on
(X\times Y)\times Z
or
X\times(Y\times Z)
or
(X\times Z)\times Y
.

Theorem 1.2

(Slice Function “Removal Lemma”)
.

Let
f(x,y),g(x,z),h(y,z)
be some indicator functions on
\Omega:=X\times Y\times Z
, and consider the cylinder intersection

F(x,y,z):=f(x,y)g(x,z)h(y,z).

Suppose that
F
is small, i.e.

\E_{(x,y,z)\in\Omega}[F(x,y,z)]\leq 2^{-d}.

Then,
F
has a reasonably small (pointwise) cover
F^{\prime}
,

F(x,y,z)\leq F^{\prime}(x,y,z):=\sum_{i=1}^{R}s_{i}(x,y,z),

for some slice functions
s_{i}(x,y,z)
. Specifically,

•

\E_{(x,y,z)\in\Omega}[F^{\prime}(x,y,z)]\leq 2^{-\Omega(d^{1/2})}\log|\Omega|
, and

•

\E[s_{i}]\geq 2^{-O(d)}
for every
i\in[R]
(consequently,
R\leq 2^{O(d)}\cdot\log(|\Omega|)
).

Triangle Removal Lemma.

Consider the following (unusual) formulation of Szemerédi’s triangle removal lemma. For the purpose of comparing with
Theorem

1.2
, we formulate it as a statement
about cylinder intersections
.

Theorem 1.4

(Triangle Removal Lemma)
.

Let
f(x,y),g(x,z),h(y,z)
be some indicator functions on
\Omega:=X\times Y\times Z
, and consider the cylinder intersection

F(x,y,z):=f(x,y)g(x,z)h(y,z).

Suppose that
F
is small, i.e.

\E_{(x,y,z)\in\Omega}[F(x,y,z)]\leq\varepsilon.

Then
F
has a reasonably small (pointwise) cover
F^{\prime}
, which has the form

F(x,y,z)\leq F^{\prime}(x,y,z):=f^{\prime}(x,y)+g^{\prime}(x,z)+h^{\prime}(y,z).

Specifically,

\E_{(x,y,z)\in\Omega}[F^{\prime}(x,y,z)]\leq\delta:=\delta(\varepsilon)

for some
\delta(\varepsilon)\rightarrow 0
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.

Recall that the standard formulation of the triangle removal lemma is stated in the language of graphs: it says that if an
N
-vertex graph has a small number of triangles (i.e., at most
\varepsilon N^{3}
), then it is possible to remove a reasonably small number of edges (i.e., at most
\delta N^{2}
) in order to eliminate all triangles. One can observe that the two formulations differ only cosmetically.
10
10

10

Perhaps the only non-obvious step is to notice that the triangle removal lemma is equivalent (up to constant factors) to the special case of itself on tripartite graphs.

It is not known what should be the “right” quantitative dependence of
\delta
on
\varepsilon
in the triangle removal lemma. Indeed, there is a large gap between the known upper bounds on
\delta(\varepsilon)
(which are tower-type, see e.g.
[
10
]
), and the best known lower bounds on plausible values for
\delta(\varepsilon)
, which follow from Behrend-type constructions of large sets of integers
A\subseteq[N]
without
3
-term arithmetic progressions. In light of these constructions, the best plausible dependence of
\delta
on
\varepsilon
would be (roughly)

\delta(\varepsilon)\approx 2^{-\sqrt{\log(1/\varepsilon)}}.

Coincidentally, this corresponds to the same parameters we obtain in our slice function removal lemma. So, to conclude our comparison, we note the following. At present, the statements (
Theorem

1.2
and
Theorem

1.4
) are incomparable: the former obtains substantially better parameters, but the latter provides a cover by a (strictly) simpler collection of functions –
cylinders
instead of
slice functions
. However, in the event that dramatically improved bounds for the triangle removal lemma were to be discovered, one could conceivably obtain a result strictly improving both statements.
11
11

11

We point out that, as far as we are aware, it could be possible to give a strict
quantitative
improvement to just our slice function removal lemma itself. That is, we do not know of any sort of obstruction (Behrend-type or otherwise) to obtaining substantially improved parameters when we cover by slice functions. It would be interesting to find such an obstruction if there is one.

It is interesting to understand to what extent the techniques introduced in

Preliminaries

We usually work with a finite universe
\Omega
. We write
w\sim\Omega
to denote that
w

\Omega
uniformly at random. Alternatively, we also use
\mathcal{U}
to denote the uniform distribution. A function

is called a probability density function if
\E_{w\sim\Omega}[p(w)]=\sum_{w}\frac{p(w)}{|\Omega|}=1
. To sample from
p
, we draw each
w\in\Omega
with probability
\frac{p(w)}{|\Omega|}
. For any set
S\subseteq\Omega
,
w\sim S
denotes the uniform distribution over
S
. Finally, we will frequently use the following change-of-distribution identity:

\displaystyle\E_{w\sim p}[S(w)]=\frac{|S|}{|\Omega|}\cdot\E_{w\sim S}[p(w)].

(1)

Let
X
be a finite domain. The
\ell_{p}
-norm of a vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{X}
is defined as
\|u\|_{p}\coloneqq\E_{x\sim X}[|u_{x}|^{p}]^{1/p}
. For two vectors
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{X}
of the same dimension, their inner product is
\langle u,v\rangle\coloneqq\E_{x\sim X}[u_{x}\cdot v_{x}]
. For every
p\geq 1
, let
p^{\star}\coloneqq\frac{p}{p-1}
be the Hölder conjugate of
p
. The Hölder inequality says that
\langle f,g\rangle\leq\|f\|_{p}\cdot\|g\|_{p^{\star}}
. We collect some elementary equations regarding Hölder conjugates that we frequently use without notice:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p-1}=p^{\star}-1\text{~~~~,~~~~}p^{\star}=\frac{p}{p-1}\text{~~~~,~~~~}\frac{1}{p}-1=-\frac{1}{p^{\star}}.

(2)

For a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{X\times Y}
and vectors
u\in\mathbb{R}^{X},v\in\mathbb{R}^{Y}
, we define the
normalized
matrix-vector product
Mg\in\mathbb{R}^{X}
as
(Mg)(x)=\E_{y}[M(x,y)\cdot g(y)]
and vector-matrix-vector product
f^{\top}Mg=\E_{x,y}[f(x)M(x,y)g(y)]
. Finally, given

, the

-grid norm of

is defined as

\displaystyle\|M\|_{U(\ell,k)}:=\left(\E_{x_{1:\ell}}\E_{y_{1:k}}\left[\prod_{i\in[\ell]}\prod_{j\in[k]}M(x_{i},y_{j})\right]\right)^{1/\ell k}.

(3)

2.1
Norms and Flat Norms of Vectors

For a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{X}
, we may define its
\ell_{p}
-norm by

\displaystyle\|v\|_{p}\coloneqq\E_{x\sim X}[(v_{x})^{p}]^{1/p}=\max_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{X}}\left[\frac{\langle v,u\rangle}{\|u\|_{p^{\star}}}\right].

(4)

We consider a related notion of the
flat

p
-norm
12
12

12

p
-norm, and is well known, but seems to not go by any common name. See e.g. Exercise 1.1.12 in

.

, defined as

\displaystyle\|v\|_{\overline{p}}\coloneqq\max_{S\subseteq X}\left\{\|v|_{S}\|_{1}\left(\frac{|S|}{|X|}\right)^{1/p}\right\}.

(5)

Thus, the flat
p
-norm is just the result one obtains by writing down the dual characterization of the
p
-norm, but allowing only

The following proposition states the flat

-norm and the usual

-norm are closely related for all
smooth

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
k\geq 1
be a parameter. Suppose
v\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{X}
is such that
\frac{\|v\|_{\infty}}{\|v\|_{1}}\leq 2^{k}
. Then, for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1/10)
and
p\geq\frac{10\log(k)}{\varepsilon}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|v\|_{p}\geq\|v\|_{\overline{p}}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\|v\|_{p}.

(6)

In fact, the first inequality holds for any
p\geq 1
without any restriction on
p
.

Proof.

To see the first inequality, simply note that for any
S\subseteq X
, we may consider the indicator vector
f\in\mathbb{R}^{X}
defined by
f_{x}=\mathbf{1}_{\left\{x\in S\right\}}
. We have

\displaystyle\frac{\langle v,f\rangle}{\|f\|_{p^{\star}}}=\frac{|S|}{|X|}\cdot\|v|_{S}\|_{1}\cdot\left(\frac{|S|}{|X|}\right)^{-\frac{1}{p^{\star}}}=\|v|_{S}\|_{1}\left(\frac{|S|}{|X|}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

This means any candidate maximizer
S\subseteq X
for
\|v\|_{\overline{p}}
gives a candidate maximizer for
\|v\|_{p}
. Optimizing over all possible
S
proves the inequality.

We turn to establish the second inequality. By properly scaling, we can assume W.L.O.G.
\|v\|_{\infty}=1
. With this in mind, we consider a geometric decomposition

\displaystyle v=\sum_{j=0}^{-\infty}v^{j}

where
v^{j}
is the vector that contains entries of
v
with value
(2^{\varepsilon(-j-1)},2^{\varepsilon j}]
. Namely,
(v^{j})_{x}=v_{x}
if
v_{x}\in(2^{\varepsilon(-j-1)},2^{\varepsilon j}]
and
v^{j}_{x}=0
otherwise. Since
v^{j}
’s have disjoint support, we have

\displaystyle\|v\|_{p}^{p}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\|v^{j}\|_{p}^{p}.

For a suitable
d\geq\frac{\log(\frac{4\|v\|_{\infty}}{\|v\|_{p}})}{\varepsilon}\approx\frac{k}{\varepsilon}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{j\geq d}v^{j}\right\|_{p}\leq\left\|\sum_{j\geq d}v^{j}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 2^{-\varepsilon d}\leq\frac{\|v\|_{p}}{4}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\left\|v^{j}\right\|_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle=\|v\|_{p}^{p}-\left\|\sum_{j\geq d}v^{j}\right\|_{p}^{p}\geq\frac{1}{2}\|v\|_{p}^{p}.

By averaging principle, there exists
j^{*}<d
such that
\left\|v^{j^{*}}\right\|_{p}^{p}\geq\frac{1}{2d}\|v\|_{p}^{p}
. Letting
S
be the support of
v^{j^{*}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle~~~~\|v|_{S}\|_{1}\cdot\left(\frac{|S|}{|X|}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\geq 2^{(-j^{*}-1)\varepsilon}\left(\frac{|S|}{|X|}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\geq 2^{\varepsilon}\|v^{j^{*}}\|_{p}\geq 2^{\varepsilon}\cdot(\frac{1}{2d})^{1/p}\cdot\|v\|_{p}

\displaystyle\geq(1-O(\varepsilon))\|v\|_{p}.

(7)

To replace
(1-O(\varepsilon))
in (
7
) with a
(1-\varepsilon)
, we can work with a smaller
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\Theta(\varepsilon)
in this argument. the proof will go through so long as we have, say,
p\geq\frac{2d}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
.

\min(\ell,r)\geq\frac{20\log d}{\varepsilon}
, we have

\displaystyle\|M\|_{\ell,r}\geq\|M\|_{\overline{\ell},\overline{r}}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\|M\|_{\ell,r}.

(10)

Proof.

To see the first inequality, simply note that for any
S\subseteq X,T\subseteq Y
, we may consider the indicator functions
f\coloneqq\mathbf{1}_{x\in S}
and
g\coloneqq\mathbf{1}_{y\in T}
, and derive that

\displaystyle~~~~\frac{f^{\top}Mg}{\|f\|_{\ell^{\star}}\|g\|_{r^{\star}}}

\displaystyle=\|M\mid_{S\times T}\|_{1}\cdot\left(\frac{|S|}{|X|}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{\ell^{\star}}}\left(\frac{|T|}{|Y|}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{r^{\star}}}

\displaystyle=\|M\mid_{S\times T}\|_{1}\cdot\left(\frac{|S|}{|X|}\right)^{\frac{1}{\ell}}\left(\frac{|T|}{|Y|}\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}

This means any candidate subsets
S,T
for
\|M\|_{\overline{\ell},\overline{r}}
give a candidate maximizer for
\|M\|_{\ell,r}
as well. Optimizing over all possible
S,T
proves the first inequality.

We now prove the second inequality. Assume without loss of generality that
\|M\|_{1}=1
. So we have
\|M\|_{\infty}\leq 2^{d}
and consequently
\|M\|_{\infty\leftarrow p}\leq 2^{d}
for any
p\geq 1
. Say
\tau\coloneqq\|M\|_{\ell,r}
is achieved by witnesses
f:X\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
g:Y\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
\|f\|_{\ell^{\star}}=\|g\|_{r^{\star}}=1
. Consider the following two optimization problems

\displaystyle\max_{h:X\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}\left\{\frac{h^{\top}Mg}{\|h\|_{\ell^{\star}}}\right\}\text{~~~~vs.~~~~}\max_{h:X\to\{0,1\}}\left\{\frac{h^{\top}Mg}{\|h\|_{\ell^{\star}}}\right\}

The optimum of the left problem is realized by taking
h=f
and, by Proposition
2.1
, there exists
\overline{f}:X\to\{0,1\}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\overline{f}^{\top}Mg}{\|\overline{f}\|_{\ell^{\star}}}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{f^{\top}Mg}{\|f\|_{\ell^{\star}}},

provided that
\ell\geq\frac{10\log d}{\varepsilon}
. Next, one can similarly find
\overline{g}:Y\to\{0,1\}
(provided that
r\geq\frac{10\log d}{\varepsilon}
) such that

\displaystyle\frac{\overline{f}^{\top}M\overline{g}}{\|\overline{f}\|_{\ell^{\star}}\|\overline{g}\|_{r^{\star}}}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\overline{f}^{\top}Mg}{\|\overline{f}\|_{\ell^{\star}}\|g\|_{r^{\star}}}\geq\frac{(1-O(\varepsilon))\cdot\tau}{\|\overline{g}\|_{r^{\star}}\|\overline{f}\|_{\ell^{\star}}}.

The sets indicated by
\overline{f},\overline{g}
would then witness that
\|M\|_{\overline{\ell},\overline{r}}\geq(1-O(\varepsilon))\|M\|_{\ell,r}
, as desired.
∎

This section refines, simplifies, and improves the technique from

3.1
Proof Overview

Before developing the results, let us comment on how we obtain a better sifting argument than prior works
[
15
,
14
]
.

Review of
[
14
]
.

At the core of all the “sifting” arguments is a “density increment” argument. To review the
[
14
]
proof: we start with a non-negative matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
that is (1) bounded:
\|M\|_{\infty}=1
and (2) relatively dense:
\|M\|_{1}=\E_{(x,y)}[M(x,y)]\geq 2^{-d}
. We inspect how “pseudorandom”
M
is, as measured by its
U(2,k)
-grid norm
\|M\|_{U(2,k)}
. There are two cases: If
M
is “pseudorandom” enough in the sense that
\|M\|_{U(2,k)}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|M\|_{1}
, we can proceed to argue other nice properties of
M
. Otherwise,
M
is not pseudorandom and we have
\|M\|_{U(2,k)}\geq(1+\varepsilon)\|M\|_{1}
. In this case, one tries to find a sub-matrix
X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}\subseteq[N]\times[N]
, such that the average of
M
increases by a multiplicative factor of
(1+\varepsilon)
where
\varepsilon
is a small constant, and the sub-matrix is of size at least
\frac{1}{2^{O(kd)}}
. On the sub-matrix, one can iterate this argument. Since each iteration increases the density of
M
by at least
(1+\varepsilon)
. The density increment step only happens for at most
d/\varepsilon
times, and we are guaranteed a final submatrix of density at least
2^{-O(d^{2}k)}
. In the most relevant parameter regime, one first fixes
d=\log(\frac{1}{\text{density of the object}})
and chooses
k\approx d
. This leads to a
2^{O(d^{3})}
loss in the size of the final sub-matrix, and is ultimately the reason that
[
14
]
ended up with a
\Omega((\log N)^{1/3})
lower bound for the
3
-party NOF communication model.

Our improvement.

We show that, with a new and simpler way to sift, one gets a trade-off between how much larger the mean of
M
increases, and how much smaller the sub-matrix becomes. In particular, we show the following: in each iteration of the density increment argument, if the average of
M
only increases by a single
(1+\varepsilon)
factor, then the sub-matrix
X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}
only gets smaller by a factor of
2^{-O(k)}
. On the other hand, if we can only increase onto a sub-matrix
X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}
of size much smaller than
2^{-\Omega(k)}
, then the average of
M
will increase by a significantly larger amount. Roughly speaking, there always exists a parameter
1\leq\gamma\leq d
and a sub-matrix
X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}
of size
2^{-\gamma\cdot k}
such that
\E_{(x,y)\sim X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}}[M(x,y)]
increases by a factor of
(1+\varepsilon)^{(d-\gamma)k}
. Iterating based on this claim allows us to find a sub-matrix
M
with the desired pseudorandom property, yet the size of the final sub-matrix is at least
2^{-O(kd)}N^{2}
.

One might find it easier to understand the improvement by drawing an analogy to vector
\ell_{k}
-norms. Say we have a vector
v\in[0,1]^{N}
that we know
\|v\|_{1}\geq 2^{-d}
and
\|v\|_{k}:=\E[v_{i}^{k}]^{1/k}>(1+\varepsilon)\E[v_{i}]
. This can happen for (at least) two possible reasons: (1) there is a subset
S\subseteq[N]
such that
|S|\geq 2^{-k}N
and
v_{i}>(1+\frac{1}{2})\|v\|_{1}
for
i\in S
, or (2) there is a much smaller subset
S\subseteq[N]
of size only
|S|>2^{-dk}N
, but
v_{i}>2^{d}\cdot\|v\|_{1}
for
i\in S
. Both cases lead to a significantly larger
\ell_{k}
-norm of
v
. Still, to run an efficient “density increment” argument on
v
, one must account for the trade-off between
|S|
and
\min_{i\in S}\{v_{i}\}
.

Currently, our improvement only applies to the
U(2,k)
-grid norms
13
13

13

We can extend this to
U(\ell,k)
-norms by a tensoring trick. Still, the improvement is only significant in the regime that
\ell\ll k
.
, mostly due to the nice interpretation of
U(2,k)
norm as “the
k
-th moment of the pairwise inner products between rows of
M
”, as well as the flat-vs-operator norm connection established in Section
2
. It remains to be seen whether a similar improvement can be made for the case of 3-APs
[
15
]
.

Organization of the section.

With the setup from Section
2
, the density increment argument is compactly presented in Section
3.2
. We also refine the “spectral positivity” part of the
[
14
]
argument and present a simplified proof in Section
3.3
. Finally, Theorem

combines the two ingredients and concludes this section.

3.2
Spreadness of Matrices

We define a notion of spreadness for matrices. Roughly, it says that a spread matrix has no large sub-matrix with significantly larger entries on average.

Definition 3.1
.

A matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{X\times Y}
is
(t,\varepsilon)
-spread, if
\|M\|_{\overline{t},\overline{t}}\leq\|M\|_{1}\cdot(1+\varepsilon)
.

We compare Definition
3.1
with the definition of spreadness from
[
14
]
. The spreadness of
[
14
]
is parameterized by two parameters
k\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon>0
. A matrix
M
is spread in the sense of
[
14
]
, if for any submatrix
M^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}}
of size
|X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}|\geq 2^{-k}|X\times Y|
, one has that
\|M^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq\|M\|_{1}\cdot(1+\varepsilon)
. Namely, the density of every “reasonably large” subractangle can only be larger than the density of
M
be at most a
(1+\varepsilon)
factor. Our Definition
3.1
, on the other hand, should be (roughly) read as for every submatrix
M^{\prime}
of size
(1-\varepsilon)^{k\cdot\tau}
for some
\tau\geq 1
, one has
\|M^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq\|M\|_{1}\cdot(1+\varepsilon)^{\tau}
. So, our definition is stronger in that we will consider submatrices of size significantly smaller than
2^{-k}
, and we impose proportionally more relaxed density upper bounds for smaller submatrices.

The key technical lemma of
[
14
]
asserts that a spread matrix has bounded
U(2,k)
-norm. We state and prove our version of the lemma in the following.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
k,d\geq 1,\varepsilon\in(0,1/5)
be parameters such that
k\geq 20d/\varepsilon
. Suppose
M\in[0,1]^{X\times Y}
is a matrix that is
(k,\varepsilon)
-spread and
\|M\|_{1}\geq 2^{-d}
. Then,
\|M\|_{U(2,k)}\leq(1+O(\varepsilon))\|M\|_{1}
.

Proof.

The condition of
M
implies that
\frac{\|M\|_{\infty}}{\|M\|_{1}}\leq 2^{d}
. As such, Definition
3.1
and Lemma
2.2
imply that
\|M\|_{k,k}\leq(1+O(\varepsilon))\|M\|_{\overline{k},\overline{k}}\leq(1+O(\varepsilon))\|M\|_{1}
. For each
x\in X
, let
M_{x}
be the
x
-th row vector of
M
. For a proper choice of
p\approx\frac{10d}{\varepsilon}
, we have

\displaystyle~~~~\|M\|_{U(2,k)}^{2k}

(11)

\displaystyle=\E_{x,x^{\prime}\sim X}[\langle M_{x},M_{x^{\prime}}\rangle^{k}]

\displaystyle=\E_{x\sim X}[\|M\cdot M_{x}^{\top}\|_{k}^{k}]

\displaystyle\leq\E_{x\sim X}\left[\left(\|M\|_{k,p}\cdot\|M_{x}\|_{p^{\star}}]\right)^{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\|M\|_{k,k}^{k}\cdot\E_{x}\left[\left(\|M_{x}\|_{1}^{1/p^{\star}}\|M_{x}\|_{\infty}^{\frac{p^{\star}-1}{p^{\star}}}\right)^{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\|M\|_{k,k}^{k}\cdot\|M\|_{\frac{k}{p^{\star}},1}^{\frac{k}{p^{\star}}}\cdot 2^{\frac{dk}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\|M\|_{k,k}^{2k}\|M\|_{k,k}^{\frac{k}{p^{\star}}-k}\cdot 2^{\frac{dk}{p}}.

Here, the fourth-line is using the definition of operator norm, and the last line is due to
\|M\|_{k,k}\geq\|M\|_{\frac{k}{p^{*}},1}
. Raising both sides to the power of
\frac{1}{2k}
and using the fact
\|M\|_{k,k}\geq\|M\|_{1}\geq 2^{-d}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|M\|_{U(2,k)}

\displaystyle\leq\|M\|_{k,k}\cdot\|M\|_{k,k}^{-\frac{1}{p}}\cdot 2^{\frac{d}{2p}}

\displaystyle\leq\|M\|_{k,k}\cdot 2^{2d/p}

\displaystyle\leq(1+O(\varepsilon))\|M\|_{1}.

(12)

This completes the proof.
∎

3.3
Product of Spread Matrices is Near-Uniform

For a matrix
f:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, define
R_{f}:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by

(R_{f})(x,y)=\E_{y^{\prime}\sim Y}[f(x,y^{\prime})].

Namely,
R_{f}
is the row-averaging of
f
. We make the following claim.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
f:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a matrix with
\|f-R_{f}\|_{U(2,k)}\geq 6\sqrt{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbb{E}[f]
. Suppose for every
x\in X
, it holds
\mathbb{E}_{y}[f(x,y)]\geq(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}[f]
. Then, for
p\geq\frac{2k}{\varepsilon}
, we have
\|f\|_{U(2,p)}\geq(1+\varepsilon)\|f\|_{1}
.

We mostly use Lemma
3.3
in its contrapositive form: Suppose we can prove a matrix
f
is such that
\|f\|_{U(2,p)}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|f\|_{1}
. Then, provided with the additional assumption
\E_{y}[f(x,y)]\geq(1-\varepsilon)\E[f]
, we may conclude
\|f-R_{f}\|_{U(2,k)}\leq O(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\|f\|_{1})
.

To prove Lemma
3.3
, we will need the following lemma from
[
15
]
.

Lemma 3.4

(See Proposition D.1 of
[
15
]
)
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1/4)
. Suppose
A
is a random variable with
\mathbb{E}[A^{k}]\geq(2\varepsilon)^{k}
for some even
k
and
\mathbb{E}[A^{t}]\geq 0
for every
t\geq 1
. Then, for all
p\geq\frac{k}{\varepsilon}
, it holds that
\E[|1+A|^{p}]\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{p}
.

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

Consider a random variable
A
which takes value
\langle(f-R_{f})(i,\star),(f-R_{f})(j,\star)\rangle
with probability
\frac{1}{|X|^{2}}
for every
(i,j)
. We know that

\E[A^{k}]=\|f-R_{f}\|_{U(2,k)}^{2k}\geq(6\sqrt{\varepsilon}\E[f])^{2k},

and that for every
t\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\E[A^{t}]

\displaystyle=\E_{y_{1},\dots,y_{t}}\E_{x_{1},x_{2}}\left[\prod_{x_{i},y_{j}}(f-R_{f})(x_{i},y_{j})]\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{y_{1},\dots,y_{t}}\left(\E_{x}\left[\prod_{y_{j}}(f-R_{f})(x,y_{j})\right]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

Let
\varepsilon^{\prime}=2\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{2}
. Using Lemma
3.4
on
\frac{A}{\E[f]^{2}(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})}
, we obtain that
\E[(A+(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\E[f]^{2})^{p}]\geq((1+\varepsilon)\E[f]^{2})^{p}
for every
p\geq k/\varepsilon
. Observe that each row of
(f-R_{f})
sums to zero. Let
J=1^{X\times Y}
be the all-one matrix. We calculate

\displaystyle~~~~\|f-R_{f}+(1-\varepsilon)\E[f]J\|_{U(2,p)}^{2p}

\displaystyle=\E_{i,j}\Big\langle(f-R_{f}+(1-\varepsilon)\E[f]J)(i,\star),

(13)

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~(f-R_{f}+(1-\varepsilon)\E[f]J)(j,\star)\Big\rangle^{p}

\displaystyle=\E_{A}[((1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\E[f]^{2}+A)^{p}]

\displaystyle\geq((1+\varepsilon)\E[f]^{2})^{p}.

Recall that we have the entry-wise bound of
R_{f}(x,y)\geq 1-\varepsilon
. Hence, for every
i,j
, we have

\displaystyle~~~~~\langle f(i,\star),f(j,\star)\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\Big\langle(f-R_{f}+(1-\varepsilon)\E[f]J)(i,\star),

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~(f-R_{f}+(1-\varepsilon)\E[f]J)(j,\star)\Big\rangle.

This is to say, the random variable
A+(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\E[f]
is
stochastically dominated
by
\langle f(\mathbf{i},\star),f(\mathbf{j},\star)\rangle
.

One may now be tempted to conclude that the
p
-th moment of
A+(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\E[f]
gives a lower bound for the
p
-th moment of
\langle f(\mathbf{i},\star),f(\mathbf{j},\star)\rangle
. However, this is generally not true as the random variables can be negative (for example, the random variable
\mathbf{0}
stochastically dominates
\mathbf{-1}
, but the latter has a larger
p
-th moment). Nonetheless, since
A
satisfies that
\E[A^{t}]\geq 0
for every
t\geq 1
, we know
\E[(A+(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\E[f]^{2})^{p}]\geq 0
for every odd
p
. Therefore, as long as the
p
-th moment of
A+(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\E[f]^{2}
is concerned, the contribution from the “positive” instantiation of the r.v. is no less than the contribution from the negative part. By only considering the non-negative instantiation of
A+(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\E[f]^{2}
and using stochastic domination, we may consequently conclude

\displaystyle\|f\|_{U(2,p)}^{2p}

\displaystyle\geq\E_{A}[(A+(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\E[f]^{2})^{p}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{A\geq 1\}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\E_{A}[(A+(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\E[f]^{2})^{p}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}((1+\varepsilon)\E[f]^{2})^{p}

\displaystyle\geq(1+\varepsilon-O(1/p))^{p}\E[f]^{2p}.

as desired. For even
p
, we can work with
p-1
and appeal to the monotonicity of norms.
∎

Decoupling inequality.

The rest of the argument is largely similar to
[
14
]
. We nonetheless give a self-contained exposition here. First, let
f:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},g:Z\times Y\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be two non-negative matrices that are both spread and smooth. We consider their product
f\circ g:X\times Z\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
defined as

\displaystyle(f\circ g)(x,z)=\E_{y\sim Y}[f(x,y)\cdot g(z,y)].

(14)

Assuming the spreadness and smoothness of
f,g
, we claim that
f\circ g
is close to the matrix
R_{f}\circ R_{g}
(which we note is a rank-
1
matrix).

To prove the claim, we use a moment method. For any even
k\geq 1
of our choice, we can write

\displaystyle~~~~\|f\circ g-R_{f}\circ R_{g}\|_{k}^{k}

\displaystyle=\|(f-R_{f})\circ(g-R_{g})\|_{k}^{k}

\displaystyle=\E_{x\sim X,z\sim Z}\left\langle(f-R_{f})(x,*),(g-R_{g})(z,*)\right\rangle^{k}

\displaystyle=\E_{x,z}\E_{y_{1},\dots,y_{k}}\left(\prod_{1\leq j\leq k}(f-R_{f})(x,y_{j})(g-R_{g})(z,y_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=\E_{y_{1},\dots,y_{k}\sim Y}\left(\E_{x\sim X}\prod_{1\leq j\leq k}(f-R_{f})(x,y_{j})\right)\times

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\left(\E_{z\sim Z}\prod_{1\leq j\leq k}(g-R_{g})(z,y_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\E_{y_{1},\dots,y_{k}\sim Y}\left(\E_{x\sim X}\prod_{1\leq j\leq k}(f-R_{f})(x,y_{j})\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\times

\displaystyle~~~~~~\left(\E_{y_{1},\dots,y_{k}\sim Y}\left(\E_{z\sim Z}\prod_{1\leq j\leq k}(g-R_{g})(z,y_{j})\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\|f-R_{f}\|_{U(2,k)}^{k}\|g-R_{g}\|_{U(2,k)}^{k}.

(15)

Therefore, as long as we can establish that
f,g
satisfy the prerequisites of Lemma
3.3
, we can then upper bound the deviation
\|f\circ g-R_{f}\circ R_{g}\|_{k}
by
O(\varepsilon^{2}\|f\|_{1}\|g\|_{1})
, which would then imply that all but a tiny fraction (roughly
2^{-k}
) of pairs
(x,z)
satisfy that
\left|\langle f(x,*),g(z,*)\rangle-R_{f}(x)\cdot R_{g}(z)\right|\leq O(\varepsilon^{2}\|f\|_{1}\|g\|_{1})
.

Overall, we compile everything we have discussed so far and formulate the following theorem.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
f\in[0,1]^{X\times Y}
and
g\in[0,1]^{Z\times Y}
be two matrices. Let
d\geq 10
be an integer. Assume that

•

f,g
are
(100d/\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
-spread,

•

\E_{y}[f(x,y)]\geq 1-\varepsilon
,
\E_{y}[g(z,y)]\geq 1-\varepsilon
for every
x,z
, and

•

\|f\|_{1},\|g\|_{1}\geq 2^{-d}
.

Then, it follows that

\displaystyle\|f\circ g-R_{f}\circ R_{g}\|_{d}\leq O(\varepsilon\|f\|_{1}\cdot\|g\|_{1}).

Proof.

From the spreadness and density lower bound of
f
we obtain that
\|f\|_{U(2,10d/\varepsilon)}\leq(1+O(\varepsilon))\|f\|_{1}
by Lemma
3.2
. Using Lemma
3.3
in its contrapositive form we then get
\|f-R_{f}\|_{U(2,d)}\leq O(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\|f\|_{1})
. Repeat the same argument on
g
. We obtain
\|g-R_{g}\|_{U(2,d)}\leq O(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\|g\|_{1})
. Finally, by the decoupling inequality as shown above, we conclude that

\displaystyle\|f\circ g-R_{f}\circ R_{g}\|_{d}\leq\|f-R_{f}\|_{U(2,d)}\cdot\|g-R_{g}\|_{U(2,d)}\leq O(\varepsilon\|f\|_{1}\|g\|_{1}),

as claimed.
∎

4
Proof of the Removal Lemma

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.2
by proving the following “fractional cover” version.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
f(x,y),g(x,z),h(y,z)
be some indicator functions on
\Omega=X\times Y\times Z
. Consider their cylinder intersection
F(x,y,z)=f(x,y)\cdot g(x,z)\cdot h(y,z)
. Let
d\geq 5
and suppose
F
is such that

\mathbb{E}_{(x,y,z)\sim\Omega}[F(x,y,z)]\leq 2^{-d}.

Then,
F
is covered by a sum of slice functions:

F(x,y,z)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{R}c_{i}\cdot s_{i}(x,y,z),

where each
s_{i}
is of size at least
2^{-O(d)}
and

\E_{(x,y,z)\sim\Omega}\left[\sum_{i=1}c_{i}s_{i}(x,y,z)\right]\leq 2^{-\Omega(d^{1/2})}.

Rounding.

We sketch how to round the fractional cover from Theorem
4.1
to prove Theorem
1.2
. Say we have
F\leq\sum_{i=1}^{R}c_{i}s_{i}(x,y,z)
. Consider now a distribution over slice functions where we draw
s_{i}
with probability
\frac{c_{i}}{\sum_{i^{\prime}}c_{i^{\prime}}}
. Then, for a given
(x,y,z)\in F
, drawing a random
s_{i}
from the distribution can cover
(x,y,z)
with probability at least
\frac{1}{\sum_{i^{\prime}}c_{i^{\prime}}}
. Therefore, drawing
O(\log(|\Omega|)\cdot\sum_{i^{\prime}}c_{i^{\prime}})
slices ensures that one of the slices covers
(x,y,z)
with probability
1-\frac{1}{|\Omega|^{2}}
. So, to obtain an integral covering, we just independently draw
O(\log(|\Omega|)\cdot\sum_{i^{\prime}}c_{i^{\prime}})
functions from the distribution. By a simple union bound, the resulting cover will cover every
(x,y,z)\in F
with high probability. By Markov’s inequality, we can ensure that the total size of the integral cover is larger than the fractional cover by at most a factor of
O(\log(|\Omega|))
. We conclude by noting that
|\sum_{i^{\prime}}c_{i^{\prime}}|\leq 2^{O(d)}
(this follows from the upper bound of
\E\left[\sum_{i=1}c_{i}s_{i}(x,y,z)\right]
and the lower bound of
\E[s_{i}]
). Hence, the number of slices functions in the integral cover is at most
2^{O(d)}\cdot\log(|\Omega|)
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

We prove the theorem by duality of linear programming. Let
\mathcal{S}
be the collection of all large slice functions (i.e., slice functions of size
2^{-O(d)}
). We construct a
\Omega\times\mathcal{S}
matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\times\mathcal{S}}
. Here, rows and columns are naturally indexed by
(x,y,z)\in\Omega
and
s\in\mathcal{S}
. The entry indexed by row
(x,y,z)
and column
s
is equal to
s(x,y,z)
. We also define the weight of a slice
s\in\mathcal{S}
to be
w(s)=\E[s]
. Slightly abusing notation, we understand
w
as both a
\mathcal{S}
-dimensional column vector and a function mapping
\mathcal{S}
to reals. Similarly, identify
F
with a
\Omega
-dimensional column vector where the
(x,y,z)
-th entry of
F
is
F(x,y,z)
. With this setup, let us consider the following linear program:

\displaystyle\min_{c\in(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})^{\mathcal{S}}}

\displaystyle\sum_{s}c_{s}\cdot w(s)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle F\leq A\cdot c.

Intuitively, this is the covering problem considered in Theorem
4.1
: we want to choose a weight
c_{s}
for each slice function so that the weighted combination of slices covers every non-zero entry of
F
. Then, the conclusion of Theorem
4.1
is equivalently saying that the optimal value of this problem is less than
2^{-\Omega(d^{1/2})}
.

We can now write down the dual problem:

\displaystyle\max_{p\in(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})^{\Omega}}

\displaystyle\sum_{(x,y,z)}F(x,y,z)\cdot p(x,y,z).

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle w\geq A^{\top}\cdot p.

Let us study this linear program more closely. This problem asks for a packing, subject to the constraint that every slice function
s
upper bounds the weight placed inside the set indicated by
s
, and we would like to maximize the amount of weight placed in the set indicated by
F
. By strong duality, to prove Theorem
4.1
, it suffices to show that every feasible solution
p
to the dual problem has value at most
2^{-\Omega(d^{1/2})}
.

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
p
is a feasible solution with too large a value. We can assume W.L.O.G. that
p(x)=0
whenever
F(x)=0
. Indeed, for any feasible
p
, it is easy to see that
(p\cdot F)
is also feasible and attains the same value. To employ the pseudorandomness machinery, we would like interpret
p
as a probability density function.
Note that
p
is non-negative by definition. After re-scaling
p
by
\overline{p}=\frac{p}{\|p\|_{1}}
, we obtain a probability density function
\overline{p}
and let
\mathcal{D}
be the distribution described by
\overline{p}
.
Note that
\mathcal{D}
is supported only on the support of
F
. Moreover, assuming
\sum_{x,y,z}p(x,y,z)\geq 2^{-c\cdot d^{1/2}}
for some absolute
c>0
, we have
\|p\|_{1}\geq|\Omega|^{-1}\cdot 2^{-c\cdot d^{1/2}}
. Thus, for any slice function
s
it holds that

\E_{\mathcal{D}}[s]\leq\frac{|s|}{|\Omega|}\E_{s}[\overline{p}]\leq\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\cdot\sum_{(x,y,z)\in s}p(x,y,z)\cdot|\Omega|\cdot 2^{c\sqrt{d}}\leq 2^{c\sqrt{d}}\E_{\mathcal{U}}[s].

In the following, we call any distribution
\mathcal{D}
satisfying the condition above a
2^{c{d^{1/2}}}
-
evasive
distribution to slice functions.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
k,d\in\mathbb{N}
. A distribution
\mathcal{D}
supported on
X\times Y\times Z
is
(k,d)
-evasive, if for every slice function
s
of density
\|s\|_{1}\geq 2^{-d}
, it holds that
\E_{w\sim\mathcal{D}}[s(w)]\leq 2^{k}\E_{w\sim\mathcal{U}}[s(w)]
. A set
S\subseteq X\times Y\times Z
is
(k,d)
-evasive, if the uniform distribution over
S
is
(k,d)
-evasive.

It has been clear from the above discussion that, to rule out the existence of a high-value dual solution, it suffices to prove the following proposition.

Proposition 4.3
.

There is an absolute constant
c>0
for which the following is true. Let
F
be a cylinder interaction. Suppose
\mathcal{D}
is a
(cd^{1/2},d/c)
-evasive distribution supported on
F
.
Then, it holds that
\E[F]>2^{-d}\cdot|\Omega|
.

4.1
Proof of Proposition
4.3

This subsection is devoted to the proof of Proposition
4.3
.

In this subsection, we slightly abuse notation by using
p
to denote the density function of
\mathcal{D}
. By Identity (
1
) (namely,
\E_{w\sim\mathcal{D}}[s(w)]=\E_{w\sim s}[p(w)]\cdot{\E_{w\sim\mathcal{U}}[s(w)]}
), the condition of the proposition can be equivalently written as
\E_{w\sim s}[p(w)]\leq 2^{cd^{1/2}}
for every “large” slice function.

Density increment step.

Let
t=c\cdot d^{1/2}
where
c>0
is a small but absolute constant. Also let
\varepsilon>0
be a small constant. We would like to find a subcube
C^{*}
such that the distribution
\mathcal{D}
becomes spread in the subcube. An iterative greedy approach taken by
[
14
]
is the following: initialize with
C^{*}=\Omega
, whenever these is a “large” subcube in which
\mathcal{D}
has a noticably higher density, pass
C^{*}
to the said subcube. Iterate this until no new “density increment” step can be made.

It turns out that any greedy approach based on “locally” adjusting the solution can be conveniently carried out via a potential analysis: one defines a suitable potential function
\Phi
measuring the “quality” of the solution. Then, one proves that the potential function strictly improves throughout the greedy procedure. When the procedure halts at a solution
C^{*}
, the solution achieves a local maximal for the potential. Moreover, once one has the potential function in hand, it is no longer necessary to explicitly track the greedy procedure. Instead, one can just
define

C^{*}
to be a local optimum of the potential.

We will follow this plan. In particular, let
C^{*}
be a subcube maximizing the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle C^{*}=\arg\max_{C:|C|\geq 2^{-\frac{1}{c}t^{2}}|\Omega|}\left\{\Phi(C)\coloneqq\E_{w\sim C}[p(w)]\cdot\left(\frac{|C|}{|\Omega|}\right)^{1/t}\right\}.

(16)

Intuitively, we restrict to a smaller cube
C
and try to balance between two terms: the “penalty” term of
\left(\frac{|C|}{|\Omega|}\right)^{1/t}
and the “reward” term of
\E_{w\sim C}[p(w)]
.
We observe some properties regarding
C^{*}
. First, since
C=\Omega
is a feasible solution to the problem, we have

\displaystyle\Phi(C^{*})\geq\Phi(\Omega)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}}[p]=1.

(17)

Since every subcube
C
with
|C|\geq 2^{-\frac{1}{c}t^{2}}|\Omega|
is also a large slice function, we have
\E_{w\sim C}[p(w)]\leq 2^{t}
. That is, the “gain” from
\E_{w\sim C^{*}}[p(w)]
is upper bounded by
2^{t}
. Hence, it follows that
\left(\frac{|C^{*}|}{|\Omega|}\right)^{1/t}\geq 2^{-t}
or equivalently
|C^{*}|\geq 2^{-t^{2}}|\Omega|
.

Now, write
C^{*}=X^{*}\times Y^{*}\times Z^{*}
. Let
p_{C^{*}}
be the conditional distribution of
p
onto
C^{*}
. By (
17
), we have that
p_{C^{*}}(w)=\frac{p(w)}{\E_{w\sim C^{*}}[p(w)]}<p(w)
for every
w\in C^{*}
. We define the projection of
p_{C^{*}}
onto the three two-marginals (a.k.a. faces):

\displaystyle f(x,y)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\E_{z}[p_{C^{*}}(x,y,z)],

\displaystyle g(x,z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\E_{y}[p_{C^{*}}(x,y,z)],

\displaystyle h(y,z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\E_{x}[p_{C^{*}}(x,y,z)].

Note that by construction, we get
\|f\|_{1}=\E_{(x,y)\sim X^{*}\times Y^{*}}[f(x,y)]=\E_{(x,y,z)\in C^{*}}[p_{C^{*}}(x,y,z)]=1
and similarly we have
\|g\|_{1}=\|h\|_{1}=1
.

The faces are spread.

We now show that each face is spread with respect to large rectangles. We take
f
as a demonstrating example here and note that similar arguments apply equally well to
g
and
h
. Our main claim is that, for any rectangle
X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}
of density
\delta=\frac{|X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}|}{|X^{*}\times Y^{*}|}
, as long as
\delta>2^{-t^{2}}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\E_{(x,y)\sim X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}}[f(x,y)]\leq\delta^{-1/t}\E_{(x,y)\sim X^{*}\times Y^{*}}[f(x,y)].

(18)

To briefly justify this, assume otherwise and observe that
(X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}\times Z^{*})
has density at least
2^{-2t^{2}}
, and it attains a higher value for the optimization problem of (
16
). This contradicts the optimality of
C^{*}
. Note that (
18
) is almost the condition of Definition
3.1
, with the caveat that we only consider
X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}
of size at least
\delta
. Before we argue for rectangles
X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}
with size smaller than
2^{-t^{2}}
, we first truncate
f
and enforce an
\ell_{\infty}
condition for it.

Truncating the faces: Step 1.

We consider the
\ell_{\infty}
-truncated version of these functions:

\displaystyle\overline{f}(x,y)=\min(f(x,y),2^{t}),

\displaystyle\overline{g}(x,z)=\min(g(x,z),2^{t}),

\displaystyle\overline{h}(y,z)=\min(h(y,z),2^{t}).

We claim that
\|\overline{f}-f\|_{L^{1}(X^{*}\times Y^{*})}\leq 2^{-\Omega(t^{2}/c)}
, and similar conditions hold for
g
and
h
.

Indeed, consider

S\coloneqq\{(x,y):f(x,y)>2^{t}\}\times Z^{*}.

Note that
S
is a slice. If
|S|>2^{-t^{2}/c}|\Omega|
, it would follow that

\displaystyle\E_{w\sim p}[S(w)]

\displaystyle=\Pr_{w\sim p}[w\in C^{*}]\E_{w\sim p_{C^{*}}}[S(w)]

\displaystyle=\frac{|C^{*}|}{|\Omega|}\E_{w\sim C^{*}}[p(w)]\E_{w\sim p_{C^{*}}}[S(w)]

\displaystyle\text{(By \eqref{equ:change-of-dist})}

\displaystyle=\frac{|C^{*}|}{|\Omega|}\E_{w\sim C^{*}}[p(w)]\cdot\frac{|S|}{|C^{*}|}\E_{w\sim S}[p_{C*}(w)]

\displaystyle\text{(By \eqref{equ:change-of-dist})}

\displaystyle=\frac{|C^{*}|}{|\Omega|}\cdot\frac{|S|}{|C^{*}|}\E_{w\sim S}[p(w)]

\displaystyle>\frac{|S|}{|\Omega|}\cdot 2^{t}

\displaystyle=2^{t}\cdot\Pr_{w\sim\Omega}[S(w)].

This contradicts our assumption on
p
. Therefore we must have
|S|<2^{-t^{2}/c}|\Omega|
.

Now we know that
S
must be too small so that it is not subject to the evasiveness condition. Still, we can include some extra pairs
(x,y)
into
S
to get a larger slice function
\overline{S^{\prime}}=S_{xy}\times Z^{*}
such that
|S^{\prime}|\approx 2^{-t^{2}/c}|\Omega|
. Noting that
f(x,y)=\overline{f}(x,y)
for every
(x,y)\notin S_{xy}
, we proceed as

\displaystyle\|\overline{f}-f\|_{L^{1}(X^{*}\times Y^{*})}

\displaystyle=\E_{(x,y)\sim X^{*}\times Y^{*}}[|f(x,y)-\overline{f}(x,y)|]

\displaystyle=\frac{|S_{xy}|}{|X^{*}\times Y^{*}|}\E_{(x,y)\sim S_{xy}}[|f(x,y)-\overline{f}(x,y)|]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|S_{xy}|}{|X^{*}\times Y^{*}|}\E_{(x,y)\sim S_{xy}}[f(x,y)]

\displaystyle=\frac{|S_{xy}|}{|X^{*}\times Y^{*}|}\E_{(x,y,z)\sim S}[p_{C^{*}}(x,y,z)]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|S_{xy}|}{|X^{*}\times Y^{*}|}\E_{(x,y,z)\sim S}[p(x,y,z)]

\displaystyle\text{(Recall $p_{C^{*}}\leq p$ pointwise)}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{t-t^{2}/c}.

\displaystyle\text{(By evasiveness)}

Truncating the faces: Step 2.

To apply Theorem
3.5
, we also need to enforce the left-min-degree condition on
\overline{f}
and
\overline{h}
. Toward that goal, we simply remove all
x\in X^{*}
such that
\E_{y}[\overline{f}(x,y)]<(1-\varepsilon)\|\overline{f}\|_{1}
and similarly all
z\in Z^{*}
such that
\E_{y}[\overline{h}(z,y)]<(1-\varepsilon)\|\overline{h}\|_{1}
.

We actually only remove very few
x\in X^{*}
and
z\in Z^{*}
in this process. To see this, we note that the original
f
satisfies a stronger min-degree assumption: namely we have
\E_{y}[f(x,y)]\geq(1-\varepsilon/2)\E[f]
. Indeed, suppose this fails at a row
x
. Let us consider
X^{\prime}=X^{*}\setminus\{x\}
and calculate the average of
f
on
X^{\prime}\times Y
:

\displaystyle~~~~\E_{(x,y)\sim X^{\prime}\times Y}[f(x,y)]

\displaystyle>\frac{|X^{*}|}{|X^{*}|-1}\left(1-\frac{(1-\varepsilon/2)}{|X^{*}|}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{|X^{*}|-1+\varepsilon/2}{|X^{*}|-1}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\varepsilon/2}{|X^{*}|-1}.

Thus, it is clearly seen that

\displaystyle~~~~\E_{(x,y)\sim X^{\prime}\times Y}[f(x,y)]\cdot\left(\frac{|X^{\prime}|}{|X^{*}|}\right)^{1/t}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon/2}{|X^{*}|-1}\right)^{t}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{|X^{*}|}\right)\right)^{1/t}>1.

This shows that
f
is not spread, as witnessed by
X^{\prime}\times Y
, a contradiction, contradicting to (
18
).

We have shown that
\overline{f}
is obtained from
f
be removing at most
2^{t-t^{2}/c}\E[f]
“mass” (measured in
\ell_{1}
) from
f
. On the one hand, the decrease of
\E[\overline{f}]
, as compared with
\E[f]
, is negligible. On the other hand, one needs to remove
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\E[f]
of the “mass” from a row
x
to compromise the min-degree condition on that row. Thus, a simple application of Markov’s inequality implies that at most
2^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2c}}\cdot|X^{*}|
rows are removed in Step 2.

Scaling back.

After the two steps, say we currently have functions
\overline{f},\overline{g},\overline{h}
and the ambient space
\overline{X}\times Y^{*}\times\overline{Z}
(
\overline{X},\overline{Z}
may be ever so slightly smaller than
X^{*},Z^{*}
). We scale up
\overline{f},\overline{g},\overline{h}
and obtain:

\displaystyle\tilde{f}=\overline{f}/\|\overline{f}\|_{1},~~~\tilde{g}=\overline{g}/\|g\|_{1}~~~,\tilde{h}=\overline{h}/\|\overline{h}\|_{1}.

Note that
\|\overline{f}\|_{1}
is understood as
\E_{(x,y)\sim\overline{X}\times Y^{*}}[\overline{f}(x,y)]
(and similarly for
\|\overline{g}\|_{1},\|\overline{h}\|_{1}
).

We now prove that the functions
\tilde{f},\tilde{g},\tilde{h}
are almost as spread as
f,g,h
. We prove the claim for
\tilde{f}
as the claims for
\tilde{g},\tilde{h}
are similarly established. The only property we need here is that
\tilde{f},\tilde{g}
and
\tilde{h}
are extremely close to
f,g,h
in
\ell_{1}
distance. Take
\tilde{f}
as a demonstrating example: for every
X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}
of density
\delta>2^{-t^{2}/4c}
, we have

\displaystyle\E_{w\sim X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}}[\tilde{f}(w)]

\displaystyle\leq\|f-\tilde{f}\|_{1}\cdot\frac{|X^{*}\times Y^{*}|}{|X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}|}+\E_{w\sim X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}}[f(w)]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2c}+\frac{t^{2}}{4c}+O(t)}+\delta^{-1/t}\E_{w\sim X^{*}\times Y^{*}}[f(w)]

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{-1/2t}\E_{w\sim X^{*}\times Y^{*}}[\tilde{f}(w)].

Also, for
X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}
of density
\delta<2^{-t^{2}/4c}
, we have
\|\tilde{f}|_{X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}}\|_{1}\cdot\delta^{1/t}\leq\|\tilde{f}\|_{\infty}\cdot\delta^{1/t}\leq\|\tilde{f}\|_{1}
. These two cases combined imply that
\|\tilde{f}\|_{\overline{t},\overline{t}}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|f\|_{1}
.

Recall that
\|\tilde{f}\|_{1}=1
and
\|\tilde{f}\|_{\infty}\leq 2^{t}
. We now make use of Lemma
3.2
and conclude that

\|\tilde{f}\|_{U(k,2)}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|\tilde{f}\|_{1}

for some
k=\Theta(t/\varepsilon)
.

Lower-bounding the cylinder intersection.

We are finally ready to lower-bound the size of the cylinder intersection
F
. Let
\overline{C}=\overline{X}\times Y^{*}\times\overline{Z}
. Recall we have the bound that
\|\tilde{f}\|\leq 2^{t}\cdot(1+o(1))
(and similarly for
\tilde{g}
and
\tilde{h}
. We thus get the following pointwise bound:

\displaystyle F(x,y,z)\geq\frac{1}{4\cdot 2^{3t}}\tilde{f}(x,y)\cdot\tilde{g}(x,z)\cdot\tilde{h}(y,z)

(19)

Write
(\tilde{f}\circ\tilde{h})(x,z)=\E_{y\sim Y^{*}}[\tilde{f}(x,y)\cdot\tilde{h}(z,y)]
. Applying Theorem
3.5
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\|\tilde{f}\circ\tilde{h}-R_{\tilde{f}}\circ R_{\tilde{h}}\|_{k}\leq O(\varepsilon^{2}\|\tilde{f}\|_{1}\|\tilde{h}\|_{1}).

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle~~~~\E_{(x,y,z)\sim\overline{C}}[\tilde{f}(x,y)\cdot\tilde{g}(x,z)\cdot\tilde{h}(y,z)]

\displaystyle=\left\langle\tilde{f}\circ\tilde{h},g\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\left\langle R_{\tilde{f}}\circ R_{\tilde{h}},g\right\rangle-\|\tilde{f}\circ\tilde{h}-R_{\tilde{f}}\circ R_{\tilde{h}}\|_{k}\cdot\|g\|_{k^{\star}}

\displaystyle\geq(1-O(\varepsilon))\|\tilde{f}\|_{1}\|\tilde{g}\|_{1}\|\tilde{h}\|_{1}-O(\varepsilon^{2}\|\tilde{f}\|_{1}\|\tilde{g}\|_{1}\|\tilde{h}\|_{1})

\displaystyle\geq(1-O(\varepsilon))\|\tilde{f}\|_{1}\|\tilde{g}\|_{1}\|\tilde{h}\|_{1}.

Plug this back in (
19
), we get

\displaystyle\E_{(x,y,z)\sim\overline{C}}[F(x,y,z)]\geq\Omega\left(2^{-3t}\right).

Combining this with the bound
|\overline{C}|\geq\Omega(2^{-t^{2}}|\Omega|)
, the proof is complete.

5
Number-on-Forehead Communication Lower Bound

We describe a couple of consequences of Theorem
1.2
. The first is a quantitative improvement of the main pseudorandomness result from
[
14
]
.

Theorem 5.1
.

There is an absolute constant
c>0
for which the following is true. Let
t\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
D\subseteq[N]^{3}
be a set that is
(c\sqrt{t},\frac{t}{c})
-evasive w.r.t. slice functions. For any cylinder intersection
F
of density
\E[F(x,y,z)]\leq 2^{-t}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\E_{(x,y,z)\sim D}[F(x,y,z)]\leq 2^{-\Omega(\sqrt{t})}.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.1
, we can write
F\leq\sum_{i=1}^{R}c_{i}\cdot s_{i}(x,y,z)
where each
s_{i}
is of size at least
2^{-O(t)}
and
\sum_{i}c_{i}\E[s_{i}]\leq 2^{-\Omega(\sqrt{t})}
. Then, by the evasiveness of
D
and assuming
c>0
is small enough, we obtain:

\displaystyle\E_{w\sim D}[F(w)]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{R}c_{i}\E_{w\sim D}[s(w)]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{R}c_{i}\cdot 2^{c\sqrt{t}}\cdot\E_{w\sim[N]^{3}}[s(w)]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-\Omega(\sqrt{t})},

as claimed.
∎

We cite a construction of evasive sets from
[
14
]
.

Theorem 5.2

(See Lemma 2.16 and Lemma 2.17 of
[
14
]
)
.

Let
q\geq 1
be a prime power and
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be the finite field of order
q
. Let
k\geq 1
and
N=q^{k}
. Identify
[N]
with
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
. Then, the set

\displaystyle D=\{(x,y,z)\in[N]^{3}:\langle x,y\rangle=\langle x,z\rangle=\langle y,z\rangle\}

is
(O(1),\Omega(k\log(q)))
-evasive against slice functions. Here
\langle x,y\rangle:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}y_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
denotes the inner product between
x
and
y
.

We sketch the proof of Theorem
5.2
below and refer the readers to
[
14
]
for the complete proof of a stronger statement. Note that here we only need to establish the evasiveness of
D
against slice functions. In
[
14
]
, a much stronger pseudorandomness property of
D
is proved.

Proof sketch.

Let
S=S_{xy}\times S_{z}\subseteq(X\times Y)\times Z
be a relatively large slice function (i.e., of size at least
q^{-ck}\cdot N^{3}
where
c>0
is a small but absolute constant). The evasiveness condition on
S
is equivalent to
\frac{|S\cap D|}{|D|}\leq O\left(\frac{|S|}{|\Omega|}\right)
. So, to prove Theorem
5.2
we must upper-bound
\frac{|S\cap D|}{|S|}
by roughly
\frac{|D|}{|\Omega|}\approx q^{-2}
. We may decompose
S_{xy}=\bigsqcup_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}S_{xy}^{(c)}
where
S_{xy}^{(c)}:=\{(x,y)\in S_{xy}:\langle x,y\rangle=c\}
and write
|S\cap D|=\sum_{c}|\{(x,y,z)\in S_{x,y}^{(c)}\times S_{z}:\langle x,z\rangle=\langle y,z\rangle=c\}|
. For each
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we upper-bound the term by applying counting lemmas between
z
and
x,y
(Alternatively, one can appeal to the property that Inner Product is a good extractor).
∎

We prove Theorem
1.3
as a corollary below.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

The randomized NOF communication upper bound can be obtained by running a (randomized) equality check among three players.

To see the deterministic (or non-determinisitic) NOF lower bound, it is well-known that if
D
is solved by a non-deterministic NOF protocol with complexity
c\log^{1/2}(N)
where
c>0
is sufficiently small, then there is a cylinder intersection
F\subseteq D
that covers at least
2^{-c\log^{1/2}(N)}
points of
D
. Namely, for
t=c^{2}\log(N)
, we have

\displaystyle\E_{(x,y,z)\sim D}[F(x,y,z)]>2^{-\sqrt{t}}.

Using Theorem
5.1
in its contrapositive form, this implies that
\frac{|F|}{|\Omega|}\geq 2^{-t}\geq N^{-O(c)}
. For
c>0
small and
k
(as in Theorem
5.2
) large enough, we have
\frac{|F|}{|\Omega|}\geq N^{-O(c)}\gg N^{-2/k}\approx\frac{|D|}{|\Omega|}
. This implies that
F
cannot be a subset of
D
, a contradiction.
∎
