On some affine semigroups characterized by a finite-state automata
J. I. Farrán, J. C. Rosales, R. Tapia-Ramos, A. Vigneron-Tenorio

Abstract
This work introduces a new kind of affine semigroups called P P -semigroups. Within the framework of 𝒞 \mathcal{C} -semigroups, we define a finite-state automaton associated to them. Moreover, this automaton determines whether a 𝒞 \mathcal{C} -semigroup is a P P -semigroup, which represents a bridge between affine semigroups and Discrete Mathematics. Furthermore, some algorithms for computing all the P P -semigroups with a fixed Frobenius element, genus, or multiplicity are provided. Key words: affine semigroup, automata, 𝒞 \mathcal{C} -semigroup, Frobenius element, genus, rooted tree. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 20M14, 20M35, 05C05.



Introduction

Let
\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{Q}
, and
\mathbb{R}
be the sets of non-negative integers, rationals, and real numbers, respectively.
For any
d\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{d}
be a non-negative integer cone finitely generated, and assume that it has at least
d
extremal rays.
In general, a monoid
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{d}
is a semigroup (that is, a non-empty set closed under the usual addition in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
) containing the zero element. A finitely generated monoid is called an affine semigroup.
If the monoid
S
is a subset of
\mathcal{C}
, and
\mathcal{C}\setminus S
is finite, then
S
is finitely generated and is called a
\mathcal{C}
-semigroup. This class of semigroups naturally generalizes numerical semigroups and, in particular, includes the class of generalized numerical semigroups first introduced in
[
5
]
.
Hence, concepts such as the set of gaps of
S
(
\mathcal{C}\setminus S
), and its genus (cardinality of
\mathcal{C}\setminus S
) are defined as extensions of the corresponding ones for numerical semigroups (
[
8
]
).
Moreover, once a total order on
\mathbb{N}^{d}
is given, the Frobenius element of
S
, and the multiplicity of
S
, are defined as the maximum element in
\mathcal{C}\setminus S
and as the minimum element in
S\setminus\{0\}
, respectively. In this work, a total order
\preceq
in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
is fixed. A more detailed discussion on
\mathcal{C}
-semigroups is provided in
[
6
]
.

Given a non-empty finite set
P\subset\mathbb{N}^{d}
, this work introduces a family of affine semigroups denoted by
P
-semigroups. We say that an affine semigroup is a
P
-semigroup if
\left(\{s\}+P\right)\cap S\neq\emptyset
, for all
s\in S\setminus\{0\}
. Trivially, when
0\in P
, any affine semigroup in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
is a
P
-semigroup. So, assume that the zero element does not belong to
P
.
In this setting, we can associate to each
\mathcal{C}
-semigroup a certain automaton. In particular,
we characterize when a
\mathcal{C}
-semigroup is a
P
-semigroup in terms of the language that is recognized by this automaton (Theorem
8
). This result provides an unexpected link between Semigroup Theory and Automata theory.

We recall that (deterministic) finite state automata, as a computation model in Computer Science, are abstract machines having a finite number of states so that, starting at an initial state, the current state changes for a given Input following a transition function. In this way, a string of Inputs is recognized by the automaton if the final state is considered valid by this model, and the set of recognized strings is just the (formal) language recognized by the machine (see
[
7
]
for further details).

In this work, in addition to introducing the concept of
P
-semigroups, we also study several properties of
P
-semigroups. The obtained results allow us to design and implement some algorithmic methods to compute all the
P
-semigroups with a given Frobenius element, genus, or multiplicity for a fixed integer cone, a set
P
, and a monomial order.

The content of this work is organized as follows. Section
1
provides the necessary background on affine semigroups and includes a characterization of
P
-semigroups from the minimal generating set of an affine semigroup.
In Section
2
, we recall basic notions of automata theory and show how a specifically defined automaton serves to detect whether a
\mathcal{C}
-semigroup satisfies the
P
-semigroup condition.
Sections
3
,
4
, and
5
present several results on
P
-semigroups, together with some algorithms to compute all the
P
-semigroups satisfying some prescribed properties.

1
Preliminaries and affine
P
-semigroups

A real cone in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the intersection of finitely many linear closed half-spaces. This set can also be defined from a set of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that is, a real cone is the set
\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}v_{i}\mid a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\}
where
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
\mathbb{R}_{+}
corresponds with the set of non-negative real numbers). We consider that an integer non-negative cone
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{d}
is the affine monoid given by
\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}v_{i}\mid a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\}\cap\mathbb{N}^{d}
where
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{Q_{+}}^{d}
.
Since
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{Q_{+}}^{d}
, the integer cone
\mathcal{C}
is finitely generated (see
[
1
]
). Hence, any
\mathcal{C}
-semigroup is also a finitely generated semigroup. Recall that, in this work, we also assume that the cone
\mathcal{C}
has at least
d
extreme rays.

It is well known that any
\mathcal{C}
-semigroup
S
admits a unique minimal generating set, denoted by
\operatorname{msg}(S)
. Any element belonging to
\mathcal{C}\setminus S
is called a gap of
S
, and the set of all gaps of
S
is denoted by
\mathcal{H}(S)
. An important invariant related to
S
is its genus, which is defined as
\operatorname{g}(S)=\sharp\big(\mathcal{H}(S)\big)
, where
\sharp(L)
is the cardinality of any set
L
.
A gap
x
of
S
is called special gap of
S
if
2x\in S
and
x+s\in S
, for all
s\in S\setminus\{0\}
. We denote by
\operatorname{SG}(S)
the set of all special gaps of
S
.

In this work, we fix a monomial order
\preceq
on
\mathbb{N}^{d}
, that is, a total order compatible with addition, where
0\preceq x
for any
x\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
(see
[
3
]
). With respect to this fixed order
\preceq
on
\mathbb{N}^{d}
, the Frobenius element of
S
is
\operatorname{Fb}(S)=\max_{\preceq}\mathcal{H}(S)
. When
\mathcal{H}(S)
is empty,
\operatorname{Fb}(S)=(-1,-1,\dots,-1)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. The multiplicity of
S
is the element
\operatorname{m}(S)=\min_{\preceq}\big(S\setminus\{0\}\big)
, or equivalently,
\operatorname{m}(S)=\min_{\preceq}\big(\operatorname{msg}(S)\big)
. We say that an element
s
of
S
is a small element when
s\prec\operatorname{Fb}(S)
. The set of all small elements of
S
is denoted by
\operatorname{N}(S)
.
Obviously,
\operatorname{Fb}(S)
,
\operatorname{g}(S)
and
\operatorname{N}(S)
depend on the fixed order.

Let
P
be a non-empty subset of
\mathbb{N}^{d}
. Recall that a
P
-semigroup is an affine semigroup satisfying
\left(\{s\}+P\right)\cap S\neq\emptyset
, for all
s\in S\setminus\{0\}
. The set of all
P
-semigroups is denoted by
\mathcal{S}(P)
.

The following result provides a characterization of
P
-semigroups, which can be used to check computationally whether an affine semigroup is a
P
-semigroup.

Proposition 1
.

Let
S
be an affine semigroup. Then,
S
is a
P
-semigroup if and only if
\left(\{a\}+P\right)\cap S\neq\emptyset
, for all
a\in\operatorname{msg}(S)
.

Proof.

The direct implication is immediate. Conversely, let
s
be a non-zero element of
S
. Then, there exist
a\in\operatorname{msg}(S)
and
s^{\prime}\in S
such that
s=a+s^{\prime}
. By hypothesis, there exists
p\in P
such that
a+p\in S
. It follows that
s+p=(a+s^{\prime})+p\in S
. Hence,
S
is a
P
-semigroup.
∎

.

Consider the non-negative integer cone
\mathcal{C}
spanned by

\{(1,1),(1,2),(1,3),(2,1)\},

and let
P=\{(1,2),(2,0)\}
. Define the
\mathcal{C}
-semigroup
S
minimally generated by

\operatorname{msg}(S)=\{(1,1),(2,3),(3,8),(3,9),(4,3),(4,7),(4,8),(4,11),(4,12),\\
(5,14),(5,15),(6,4),(7,4),(8,4),(10,5),(12,6),(14,7)\},

whose set of gaps is

\mathcal{H}(S)=\{(1,2),(2,1),(1,3),(3,2),(2,4),(4,2),(2,5),(2,6),(3,5),(5,3),\\
(3,6),(5,4),(6,3),(3,7)\}.

Using Figure

, it can be checked graphically that
S
is a
P
-semigroup. The empty circles correspond to its set of gaps, the blue squares are its minimal generators, and the red circles are elements belonging to it.

2
Automata and
P
-semigroups

Recall that the notion of being a
P
-semigroup is defined for affine semigroups. From now on, we focus on
\mathcal{C}
-semigroups.
In this section, we introduce the basic notions of automata theory and define the automaton associated to a
\mathcal{C}
-semigroup. More details on automata and formal languages can be seen in
[
7
]
.

Definition 3
.

A (deterministic) finite-state machine (automaton in short) is a tuple
M=(\Sigma,{\cal A},\sigma_{0},F,f)
where:

•

\Sigma
is a finite set of states.

•

{\cal A}
is a finite alphabet of symbols.

•

\sigma_{0}
is the initial state.

•

F\subseteq\Sigma
is the set of accepted final states.

•

A transition function
f:\Sigma\times{\cal A}\rightarrow\Sigma
.

By concatenation of symbols we get the set
{\cal A}^{\ast}
of strings, and by iterating the transition
f
, one can extend
f
on strings

f^{\ast}:\Sigma\times{\cal A}^{\ast}\rightarrow\Sigma.

In this way, a string
s\in{\cal A}^{\ast}
is said to be recognized by the automaton
M
if
f^{\ast}(\sigma_{0},s)\in F
. Otherwise,
s
is rejected by
M
.

The set
L(M)\subseteq{\cal A}^{\ast}
of all the strings (or words) recognized by the automaton
M
is called the (formal) language recognized by
M
.

By convention, if
\varepsilon
is the empty string, we set
f^{\ast}(\sigma_{0},\varepsilon)=\sigma_{0}
, so that
\varepsilon
is accepted by the automaton if and only if
\sigma_{0}\in F
.

On the other hand,
\sigma\in\Sigma
is said to be a dead state if
f(\sigma,\alpha)=\sigma
for every
\alpha\in{\cal A}
.

Finally, an automaton is said to be connected if every state in
\Sigma
can be reached from the initial state
\sigma_{0}
for a particular string
s\in{\cal A}^{\ast}
.

Now we define the automaton associated to a
\mathcal{C}
-semigroup. Let
P\subset\mathbb{N}^{d}
and
S
be a
\mathcal{C}
-semigroup.
To define the automaton associated to
S
, it is necessary to assume finiteness of the set
\operatorname{N}(S)
. From this fact, the fixed total order
\preceq
have to satisfy that
\{m\in\mathcal{C}\mid m\prec f\}
is finite, for any
f\in\mathcal{C}
. For instance, a graded monomial order is a total order of this kind (see
[
3
]
).
The underlying idea is that, starting from any element of
S
and moving only along the directions in
P
, we remain inside
S
. This observation inspires the definition of the following automaton. Denote by
G
the set of minimal generators of
S
that belong to
\operatorname{N}(S)
, and let
Q_{p}
be the set
\{s\in\operatorname{N}(S)\setminus\{0\}\mid s-p\notin S,\text{ for all }p\in P\}
. This last concept recalls the notion given in
[
9
]
. The automaton associated to
S
works as follows.

Definition 4
.

Let
S
be a
\mathcal{C}
-semigroup. The automaton associated to
S
is the tuple
M(S)=(\Sigma,{\cal A},\sigma_{0},F,f)
where:

•

\Sigma=\operatorname{N}(S)\cup\{\kappa,\chi\}
, where
\kappa
represents any element of
\mathcal{C}
strictly greater than
\operatorname{Fb}(S)
, and
\chi
denotes a dead state.

•

{\cal A}=P\cup G\cup Q_{P}
(note that this union may not be disjoint).

•

\sigma_{0}=0\in S
.

•

F=\Sigma\setminus\{\sigma_{0},\chi\}
.

•

The transition function
f
defined as follows:

f(0,\alpha)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\alpha&\mbox{if $\alpha\in\operatorname{N}(S)\setminus\{0\}$}\\
\kappa&\mbox{else if $\alpha\in S$}\\
\chi&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

For
q\in\operatorname{N}(S)\setminus\{0\}
, then

f(q,\alpha)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}q+\alpha&\mbox{if $\alpha\in P$ and $q+\alpha\in\operatorname{N}(S)$}\\
\kappa&\mbox{else if $\alpha\in P$ and $q+\alpha\in S$}\\
\chi&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

Finally,

f(\kappa,\alpha)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\kappa&\mbox{if $\alpha\in P$}\\
\chi&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

and
f(\chi,\alpha)=\chi
for all
\alpha\in{\cal A}
.

Remark 5
.

Notice that the set
Q_{P}
is necessary, since otherwise the automaton may not be connected.

The following result is a direct consequence of the definition of the automaton
M(S)
.

Lemma 7
.

The language recognized by
M(S)
is contained in the set of all the strings
\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{n}
such that
\alpha_{1}\in{\cal A}\setminus P
, and
\alpha_{i}\in P
for all
i>1
. In fact, the words recognized by
M(S)
correspond to paths inside the cone
\mathcal{C}
starting at points in
G\cup Q_{P}
, and following directions in
P
, which always keep inside
S
.

The following result states that
M(S)
detects if
S
is actually a
P
-semigroup.

Theorem 8
.

A
\mathcal{C}
-semigroup
S
is a
P
-semigroup if and only if every string
\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{n}
recognized by
M(S)
can be extended to a
string
\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{n}\alpha_{n+1}
also recognized by
M(S)
.

Proof.

It is clear that if
S
is a
P
-semigroup and the string is recognized by
M(S)
, one can choose a suitable symbol
\alpha\in P
that, when applied to the accepted state
\sigma_{n}
, leads again to an accepted state.

Conversely, if
S
is not a
P
-semigroup, then there exists an element
s\in\operatorname{N}(S)\setminus\{0\}
such that
s+p\not\in S
for all
p\in P
.
Since the automaton is connected, there exists a string
\alpha=\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{n}
recognized by
M(S)
such that
f(0,\alpha)=s
, and this string cannot be extended to any recognized string.

∎

3
The elements of
\mathcal{S}(P)
with a given genus

Let
P\subset\mathbb{N}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
be a finite set. Recall that the set of all
P
-semigroups is denoted by
\mathcal{S}(P)
.
In this section, we compute all
P
-semigroups with a given genus using a rooted directed graph, and we illustrate the procedure with an example. Note that, if
P\cap\mathcal{C}=\emptyset
, then it is not guaranteed that the set
\mathcal{S}(P)
is not empty.
So, from now on, we assume that
P\cap\mathcal{C}\neq\emptyset
. In this situation, we can state that
\mathcal{S}(P)
is infinite, since for any
f\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\{0\}
, the
\mathcal{C}
-semigroup
\Delta(f)=\{x\in\mathcal{C}\mid x\succ f\}\cup\{0\}
is a
P
-semigroup. For numerical semigroups, such a semigroup is known as a half-line or an ordinary semigroup. For non-numerical
\mathcal{C}
-semigroups, we refer to them as ordinary
\mathcal{C}
-semigroups. The terminology is inspired by
[
11
]
, although the authors use the notion of ordinary semigroup based on the conductor rather than the Frobenius element.
The concept of ordinary semigroup adopted here is also not equivalent to the definition presented in
[
2
]
.

Observe that the maximum element of
\mathcal{S}(P)
with respect to the inclusion is
\mathcal{C}
. The following lemma is needed for the upcoming definition.

Lemma 9
.

Let
S
be a
P
-semigroup. If
S\neq\mathcal{C}
, then
S\cup\{\operatorname{Fb}(S)\}
is also a
P
-semigroup.

Proof.

Clearly,
S\cup\{\operatorname{Fb}(S)\}
is a
\mathcal{C}
-semigroup. It remains to verify that for every
s\in S\cup\{\operatorname{Fb}(S)\}
there exists
p\in P
such that
s+p
belongs to
S\cup\{\operatorname{Fb}(S)\}
. If
s\in S
, then
s+p\in S
for some
p\in P
, since
S\in\mathcal{S}(P)
. If
s=\operatorname{Fb}(S)
, then
s+p\succ\operatorname{Fb}(S)
, and thus
s+p\in S\cup\{\operatorname{Fb}(S)\}
.
∎

Consider the graph
G\left(\mathcal{S}(P)\right)
, whose vertex set is
\mathcal{S}(P)
, and where a pair
(S,T)\in\mathcal{S}^{2}(P)
is an edge if
T=S\cup\{\operatorname{Fb}(S)\}
. In this case, we say that
S
is a child of
T
. The following result describes the structure of
G\left(\mathcal{S}(P)\right)
and provides an explicit characterization of the children of each vertex, which is a key step in the recursive construction of the graph.

Theorem 10
.

The graph
G\left(\mathcal{S}(P)\right)
is a tree with root
\mathcal{C}
. Moreover, the set of children of any
T\in\mathcal{S}(P)
is given by

\{T\setminus\{a\}\in\mathcal{S}(P)\mid a\in\operatorname{msg}(T),\ a\succ\operatorname{Fb}(T)\}.

Proof.

Let
S\in\mathcal{S}(P)
and consider the sequence
\{S_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
defined by starting at
S_{0}=S
, and for each
i\geq 0
, define
S_{i+1}=S_{i}\cup\{\operatorname{Fb}(S_{i})\}
if
S_{i}\neq\mathcal{C}
, and
S_{i+1}=\mathcal{C}
, otherwise. Clearly, this sequence stabilizes at
S_{\operatorname{g}(S)}=\mathcal{C}
. By the uniqueness of the Frobenius element, we conclude that
G\left(\mathcal{S}(P)\right)
is a tree with root
\mathcal{C}
.

Now, assume that
S
is a child of
T
in
G\left(\mathcal{S}(P)\right)
, that is,
T=S\cup\{\operatorname{Fb}(S)\}\in\mathcal{S}(P)
. So,
S=T\setminus\{a\}\in\mathcal{S}(P)
, where
a=\operatorname{Fb}(S)
, which implies that
a\in\operatorname{msg}(T)
and
a\succ\operatorname{Fb}(T)
.
Conversely, let
T\setminus\{a\}\in\mathcal{S}(P)
for some
a\in\operatorname{msg}(T)
satisfying
a\succ\operatorname{Fb}(T)
. Then,
(T\setminus\{a\},T)\in\mathcal{S}(P)^{2}
is an edge in
G\left(\mathcal{S}(P)\right)
, since
T=\big(T\setminus\{a\}\big)\cup\big\{\operatorname{Fb}(T\setminus\{a\})\big\}
, and
\operatorname{Fb}(T\setminus\{a\})=a
.
∎

Notice that the condition
T\setminus\{a\}\in\mathcal{S}(P)
is not straightforward to check computationally. To address this, the following statement presents a characterization that facilitates the computational verification of membership.

Proposition 11
.

Let
S\in\mathcal{S}(P)
and
a\in\operatorname{msg}(S)
. Then,
S\setminus\{a\}\notin\mathcal{S}(P)
if and only if there exists
p\in P
such that
a-p\in S\setminus\{0\}
, and
a-p+p^{\prime}\notin S
for all
p^{\prime}\in P\setminus\{p\}
.

Proof.

Assume
S\setminus\{a\}\notin\mathcal{S}(P)
. So, there exists
s\in S\setminus\{0,a\}
such that
(\{s\}+P)\cap(S\setminus\{a\})=\emptyset
.
Since
S
is a
P
-semigroup, we deduce that
(\{s\}+P)\cap S=\{a\}
.
Let
p\in P
with
s+p=a
. Then,
a-p=s\in S\setminus\{0\}
.
In particular,
a-p+p^{\prime}\notin S
for all
p^{\prime}\in P\setminus\{p\}
.
Conversely, clearly
a-p\in S\setminus\{a,0\}
and
(\{a-p\}+P)\cap(S\setminus\{a\})=\emptyset
.
∎

Theorem
10
can be applied repeatedly without limitation, and the set
\mathcal{S}(P)
contains infinitely many elements. However, from a computational perspective, it is not feasible without imposing restrictions. Therefore, to design an algorithm, we fix the genus as indicated.

Input:

A non-negative integer cone
\mathcal{C}
, a finite subset
P
, a monomial order
\preceq
, and a positive integer
g
.

Output:

The set
\{S\in\mathcal{S}(P)\mid g(S)=g\}
.

if

g=0

then

1

return

\mathcal{C}
X\leftarrow\{\mathcal{C}\}
;

2

for

1\leq i\leq g

Fix
f\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\{0\}
. We denote by
\mathcal{S}(P,f)
the set of all
P
-semigroups with Frobenius element equal to
f
. A simple example of an element in
\mathcal{S}(P,f)
is the
\mathcal{C}
-semigroup
\Delta(f)=\{x\in\mathcal{C}\mid x\succ f\}\cup\{0\}
, which is the minimal element of
\mathcal{S}(P,f)
with respect to the inclusion.

The main goal of this section is to compute the set
\mathcal{S}(P,f)
. To this end, we construct a rooted tree whose vertex set is
\mathcal{S}(P,f)
. An observation in this regard is summarized in the next lemma.

Lemma 13
.

Let
S\in\mathcal{S}(P,f)\setminus\{\Delta(f)\}
. Then,
S\setminus\{\operatorname{m}(S)\}\in\mathcal{S}(P,f)
.

Proof.

Clearly,
S\setminus\{\operatorname{m}(S)\}
is a
\mathcal{C}
-semigroup. Since
S
is not an ordinary, then
\operatorname{m}(S)\prec\operatorname{Fb}(S)=f
, and thus
\operatorname{Fb}(S\setminus\{\operatorname{m}(S)\})=f
. For any
s\in S\setminus\{0,\operatorname{m}(S)\}
, the
P
-semigroup condition for
S
ensures the existence of
p\in P
with
s+p\in S
. Since
s+p\neq\operatorname{m}(S)
, then
s+p\in S\setminus\{\operatorname{m}(S)\}
.
∎

We define the graph
G\left(\mathcal{S}(P,f)\right)
as follows:
the vertex set is
\mathcal{S}(P,f)
, and
(S,T)\in\mathcal{S}^{2}(P,f)
is an edge if
T=S\setminus\{\operatorname{m}(S)\}
. We now proceed with the central result of this section.

Theorem 14
.

The graph
G\left(\mathcal{S}(P,f)\right)
is a tree with root
\Delta(f)
. Furthermore, the set of children of any
T\in\mathcal{S}(P,f)
is

\{T\cup\{x\}\mid x\in\operatorname{SG}(T)\setminus\{f\},\;x\prec\operatorname{m}(T),\text{ and }x+p\in T\text{ for some }p\in P\}.

Proof.

For any
S\in\mathcal{S}(P,f)
, define the sequence
\{S_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
where
S_{0}=S
, and for each
i\geq 0
,
S_{i+1}=S_{i}\setminus\{\operatorname{m}(S_{i})\}
if
S_{i}\neq\Delta(f)
, and
S_{i+1}=\Delta(f)
, otherwise. By Lemma
13
, each
S_{i}
belongs to
\mathcal{S}(P,f)
.
Let
\operatorname{n}(S)
be the number of elements of
S
strictly less than
f
with respect to
\preceq
. Then, the sequence stabilizes at
S_{\operatorname{n}(S)-1}=\Delta(f)
, and, by using the uniqueness of
\operatorname{m}(S_{i})
, we deduce that every
S\in\mathcal{S}(P,f)
admits a unique directed path to the root
\Delta(f)
, and the graph
G(\mathcal{S}(P,f))
is a tree.
For the second statement, suppose that
S=T\cup\{x\}
, with
x=\operatorname{m}(S)
is a child of
T
. Since
S\in\mathcal{S}(P,f)
, then
x\in\operatorname{SG}(T)\setminus\{f\}
and at least there exists
p\in P
such that
x+p\in S\setminus\{x\}=T
. Furthermore,
x=\operatorname{m}(S)=\min_{\preceq}\big((T\setminus\{0\})\cup\{x\}\big)\prec\min_{\preceq}(T\setminus\{0\})=\operatorname{m}(T)
. Conversely, let
x\in\operatorname{SG}(T)\setminus\{f\}
such that
x+p\in T
for some
p\in P
. So,
S=T\cup\{x\}\in\mathcal{S}(P,f)
. If
x\prec\operatorname{m}(T)
, then
S\setminus\{x\}=T
with
\operatorname{m}(S)=x
, and thus
S
is a child of
T
.
∎

Let

. Let
\mathcal{S}(P)_{m}
be the set formed by all the

m
. The main objective in this section is to compute the set
\mathcal{S}(P)_{m}
. First, we study the finiteness of the set
\mathcal{S}(P)_{m}
, distinguishing several cases.

Set
p\in P\cap\mathcal{C}
, and consider the
P
-semigroup
\widetilde{S}_{a}=\{0\}\cup\langle m,p\rangle\cup\{x\in\mathcal{C}\mid x\succ a\}
for some
a\in\mathcal{C}
with
a\succ m
. Note that if
p\prec m
, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
kp\succ m
, and we define
\widetilde{S}_{a}^{\prime}=\widetilde{S}_{a}\setminus\{p,2p,\ldots,(k-1)p\}
, which is an element of
\mathcal{S}(P)_{m}
.
Otherwise, that is
p\succeq m
, then
\widetilde{S}_{a}^{\prime}=\widetilde{S}_{a}\in\mathcal{S}(P)_{m}
.
For both cases, we have that

\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathcal{C}\\
a\succ m\end{subarray}}\widetilde{S}^{\prime}_{a}\subseteq\mathcal{S}(P)_{m}.

Observe that, when
\langle m,p\rangle
is not a
\mathcal{C}
-semigroup, then
\mathcal{S}(P)_{m}
contains an infinite union of different
P
-semigroups with multiplicity
m
. So, analyzing the finiteness of
\mathcal{S}(P)_{m}
is equivalent to determine when
\langle m,p\rangle
is a
\mathcal{C}
-semigroup.
Therefore, for those cones
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{N}^{d}
with at least
d\geq 3
extreme rays, it is known that
\langle m,p\rangle
is not a
\mathcal{C}
-semigroup (see
[
10
]
). For
d=2
,
\langle m,p\rangle
is a
\mathcal{C}
-semigroup if and only if
\langle m,p\rangle=\mathcal{C}
(see
[
4
]
). For
d=1
, that is, for numerical semigroups, we present the following result.

Proposition 16
.

The set
\mathcal{S}(P)_{m}
is finite if and only if
\langle m,p\rangle
is a numerical semigroup for every
p\in P
.

Proof.

Suppose that there exists a natural number
p\in P
such that
\langle m,p\rangle
is not a numerical semigroup, and let us see that
\mathcal{S}(P)_{m}
is infinite. By
[
8
, Lemma 2.1]
, we have that
\gcd(m,p)=t\neq 1
. Observe that
\{0\}\cup\{m+kt\mid k\in\mathbb{N}\}\cup\{x\in\mathbb{N}\mid x\geq am\}
is an element of
\mathcal{S}(P)_{m}
for every non-zero natural number
a
. Hence,
\mathcal{S}(P)_{m}
is infinite.

Conversely, assume that
S\in\mathcal{S}(P)_{m}
. Then,
m
and
m+p
belong to
S
for some
p\in P
, and
\langle m,m+p\rangle\subseteq S
. Consequently,

\mathcal{S}(P)_{m}\subseteq\bigcup_{p\in P}\big\{S\text{ numerical semigroup}\mid\langle m,m+p\rangle\subseteq S\big\}.

Each
\langle m,m+p\rangle
is a numerical semigroup, since
\langle m,p\rangle
is a numerical semigroup. Therefore,
\mathcal{S}(P)_{m}
is contained in a finite union of finite sets, and thus
\mathcal{S}(P)_{m}
is finite.
∎
