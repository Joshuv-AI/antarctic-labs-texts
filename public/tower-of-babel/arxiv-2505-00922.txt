Cluster deletion in cographs, permutation graphs, and graphs with bounded clique number
Nicola Galesi, Tony Huynh, Arnaud Patey, Fariba Ranjbar

Abstract
The Cluster Deletion problem asks for a minimum-size edge set whose deletion turns a graph into a disjoint union of complete graphs. Equivalently, the Clique Partition problem asks for a partition of the vertex set into cliques that maximizes the number of edges within the parts. We give a simpler proof of a result of Gao, Hare, and Nastos [ 16 ] , that Cluster Deletion is polynomial-time solvable on cographs. In addition, we show that the natural linear programming formulation of Clique Partition is exact on cographs. We then show that Cluster Deletion is NP-complete on permutation graphs, which are a superclass of cographs. This answers an open question of Konstantinidis and Papadopoulos [ 25 ] . We also exhibit a permutation graph on nine vertices for which the linear programming formulation is not exact. Finally, for graphs with clique number at most c c , we give a polynomial-time 2 ​ ( c 2 ) / ( ( c 2 ) + 1 ) 2\binom{c}{2}/(\binom{c}{2}+1) -approximation algorithm for Clique Partition . More generally, the algorithm runs in polynomial time on every graph class for which a maximum clique can be found in polynomial time. For each fixed c ≥ 3 c\geq 3 , we also construct infinitely many examples attaining the stated approximation ratio. The same examples show that, for Cluster Deletion , the algorithm is a 2 2 -approximation and no better, for every fixed c ≥ 3 c\geq 3 .

Introduction

Graph clustering is a fundamental problem in graph theory with numerous
real-world applications, including the identification of functionally
related genes in computational biology
[
5
]
, community
detection in social networks
[
14
]
, image segmentation

[
33
]
, and machine learning
[
3
,
26
]
. The
goal is to partition the vertices into
clusters
with many edges
within clusters and few edges between clusters. Since a complete graph
is the densest possible graph, this motivates the following approach.

Cluster Deletion
:

Input:
A graph
G
.

Output:
A minimum size set of edges
X\subseteq E(G)
such that
G-X
is a disjoint union of complete graphs.

A disjoint union of complete graphs is also called a
cluster graph
. Given a graph
G
as input, the
Cluster Deletion
problem is equivalent to maximizing the number of edges in a spanning subgraph
H
of
G
which is a cluster graph. We call the equivalent ‘complementary’ version the
Clique Partition
problem.

Clique Partition
:

Input:
A graph
G
.

Output:
A partition
(X_{1},\dots,X_{s})
of
V(G)
such that each
X_{i}
is a clique of
G
and
\sum_{i=1}^{s}|E(X_{i})|
is maximum.

Whenever we speak of NP-completeness, we refer to the natural decision versions of these problems. Since
Cluster Deletion
has a solution of size at most
k
if and only if
Clique Partition
has a solution of size at least
|E(G)|-k
, the two decision problems are equivalent, and both are clearly in
\mathsf{NP}
.

Previous work.
Shamir et al.
[
32
]
proved that
Cluster Deletion
is NP-hard for general graphs. Despite its computational difficulty, the problem has been extensively explored within the realms of parameterized complexity
[
19
,
9
,
6
,
16
,
4
,
22
]
, approximation algorithms
[
8
,
10
,
30
,
35
,
36
,
2
]
, and restricted graph classes.

For instance,
Cluster Deletion
is polynomial time solvable on cographs
[
16
]
, split graphs
[
7
,
25
]
, (proper) interval graphs
[
7
,
25
]
,
P_{4}
- and paw-free graphs
[
21
]
and graphs with maximum degree at most three
[
24
]
. On the other hand, NP-hardness persists on
C_{4}
-free graphs with maximum degree four
[
24
]
, planar graphs
[
17
]
, chordal graphs
[
7
]
,
C_{5}
-free graphs and (2
K_{2}
, 3
K_{1}
)-free graphs
[
16
]
.

Dessmark et al.
[
10
]
introduced a simple greedy strategy (which we call
Greedy
)
of repeatedly choosing maximum cliques, and proved that
Greedy
is a 2-approximation for both
Cluster Deletion
and
Clique Partition
. However,
Greedy
does not necessarily run in polynomial time, since it requires
Max Clique
as a subroutine. Gao et al.
[
16
]
proved that
Greedy
optimally solves
Clique Partition
(and hence also
Cluster Deletion
) on cographs in polynomial time.

Interestingly, the weighted version of
Cluster Deletion
often exhibits greater computational hardness even on instances where the unweighted version is tractable. For instance, while unweighted
Cluster Deletion
is tractable on split graphs and cographs, the weighted version remains NP-hard on these classes
[
7
]
.
There are also other popular generalizations of
Cluster Deletion
, including
correlation clustering
[
3
,
32
]
, where one is allowed to delete or add edges. In this work, we focus on the unweighted variant of
Cluster Deletion
.

From the perspective of approximation algorithms, there have been a series of results for
Cluster Deletion
with progressively improved guarantees. The first approximation algorithm for
Cluster Deletion
achieved an approximation ratio of 4 using a linear programming (LP) relaxation
[
8
]
. Recent work of Balmaseda et al.
[
2
]
refined the analysis of two earlier algorithms, improving the approximation ratio from 4 to 3. The state of the art is the
2
-approximation of Veldt et al.
[
35
]
. However, the LP-based approaches are computationally expensive due to the large number of constraints involved. To address this, recent work has introduced faster algorithms by leveraging alternative LP formulations such as Strong Triadic Closure (STC) labeling
[
34
]
, resulting in practical
4
-approximations
[
36
]
. Veldt
[
36
]
also proposed the first combinatorial algorithm,
MatchFlipPivot
, which achieves similar approximation guarantees at significantly reduced runtime.

The approximation status of the
vertex
version of
Cluster Deletion
, in
which vertices rather than edges are deleted, was settled by Aprile
et al.
[
1
]
. They gave a
2
-approximation algorithm, which
is best possible assuming the
Unique Games Conjecture
.

Our contributions.

We begin by giving a much simpler proof of correctness of the main result from
[
16
]
.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
16
]
)
.

For every cograph
G
,
Greedy
outputs an optimal clique partition of
G
.

The proof in
[
16
]
uses a quite subtle induction, which requires carefully extending a given clique partition while controlling the number of parts of the clique partition. We instead use the well-known fact that every cograph is either the disjoint union of two smaller cographs or the join of two smaller cographs. This gives a very clean inductive proof of
Theorem

1.1
. We also note that Theorem 4 from
[
16
]
is a direct consequence of the majorization inequality (Karamata’s inequality
[
27
]
). Our method also proves that the natural linear programming formulation is exact on cographs. To be precise, let
\mathcal{C}(G)
be the family of all
nonempty cliques of
G
, and consider

\displaystyle\max

\displaystyle\sum_{K\in\mathcal{C}(G)}\binom{|K|}{2}x_{K}

(LP)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\in\mathcal{C}(G)\\
v\in K\end{subarray}}x_{K}=1

\displaystyle(v\in V(G)),

\displaystyle x_{K}\geq 0

\displaystyle(K\in\mathcal{C}(G)).

Theorem 1.2
.

For every cograph
G
, (
LP
) has an optimal
solution in which every variable is in
\{0,1\}
.

We next analyze
Clique Partition
and
Cluster Deletion
on
permutation graphs
, which are a superclass of cographs. We prove that both problems are NP-complete on permutation graphs.

Theorem 1.3
.

Cluster Deletion
and
Clique Partition
are NP-complete on permutation graphs.

Theorem

1.3
settles an open question of Konstantinidis and Papadopoulos
[
25
]
, and suggests that cographs are at the boundary of tractability and intractability for
Cluster Deletion
. The proof is a polynomial reduction from the
Optimum Cost Chromatic Partition
problem, which Jansen
[
23
]
proved to be NP-complete on permutation graphs. Since permutation graphs are exactly the graphs that are both comparability and cocomparability graphs,
Theorem

1.3
also implies that
Cluster Deletion
is NP-complete on comparability graphs and on cocomparability graphs (
Corollary

5.7
).

Theorem

1.3
also has consequences for the linear program (
LP
). Although (
LP
) has exponentially many variables, it can be solved in polynomial time on permutation graphs via the ellipsoid method, so
Theorem

1.3
implies that
Theorem

1.2
does not extend to permutation graphs unless
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}
. In fact, we give an explicit permutation graph on nine vertices for which (
LP
) is not exact (
Proposition

4.6
).

Finally, we introduce another variant of
Greedy
. For a graph
G
, we let
\omega(G)
and
\omega^{\prime}(G)
be the number of vertices and edges, respectively, in a maximum clique of
G
. The main idea is very simple: if
\omega(G)\leq 2
, then a maximum clique partition is just a maximum matching, and hence can be found in polynomial-time via Edmonds’ matching algorithm
[
12
]
. Therefore, we propose the following modification of
Greedy
. We run
Greedy
as long as
\omega(G)\geq 3
. Once
\omega(G)\leq 2
, we instead use Edmonds’ matching algorithm on the remaining graph and add the maximum matching to the clique partition. We call this algorithm
Greedy Edmonds
. We prove that for every graph
G
,
Greedy Edmonds
is a
\frac{2\omega^{\prime}(G)}{\omega^{\prime}(G)+1}
-approximation algorithm for
Clique Partition
.

Theorem 1.4
.

For every graph
G
,
Greedy Edmonds
returns a clique partition
\mathcal{X}
such that

{\mathsf{OPT}}\leq\frac{2\omega^{\prime}(G)}{\omega^{\prime}(G)+1}|E(\mathcal{X})|,

where
{\mathsf{OPT}}
is the number of edges in an optimal clique partition of
G
.

Although optimal solutions to
Clique Partition
correspond to optimal solutions to
Cluster Deletion
, it is not necessarily true that an
\alpha
-approximate solution to
Clique Partition
corresponds to an
\alpha
-approximate solution to
Cluster Deletion
. Indeed, for general
n
-vertex graphs, Dessmark et al.
[
10
]
proved that there is no polynomial time
n^{1-O(\frac{1}{(\log n)^{\gamma}})}
-approximation algorithm for
Clique Partition
(for some constant
\gamma
), unless
\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{ZPTIME}(2^{(\log n)^{O(1)}})
. On the other hand, as already mentioned, there are constant factor approximations for
Cluster Deletion
.

Note that
Greedy Edmonds
runs in polynomial time on any graph class for which a maximum clique can be found in polynomial time. Examples of such graph classes include graphs of bounded treewidth, perfect graphs (which include interval graphs and chordal graphs), distance-hereditary graphs, and graphs with bounded clique number.
To our knowledge, for graphs of bounded clique number,
Greedy Edmonds
is
the first polynomial-time algorithm that achieves an approximation
ratio better than
2
for
Clique Partition
. For general graphs
G
, the best
approximation algorithm for
Clique Partition
is the
\omega(G)
-approximation
algorithm from
[
10
]
.

In view of
Theorem

1.3
, it is natural to ask for approximation algorithms for
Clique Partition
on permutation graphs. Since maximum cliques and maximum matchings can be computed efficiently in permutation graphs,
Theorem

1.4
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 1.5
.

Given an
n
-vertex permutation graph
G
,
Greedy Edmonds
returns in

O(n^{2}\log n)
time a clique partition
\mathcal{X}
such that

{\mathsf{OPT}}\leq\frac{2\omega^{\prime}(G)}{\omega^{\prime}(G)+1}|E(\mathcal{X})|,

where
{\mathsf{OPT}}
is the number of edges in an optimal clique partition
of
G
.

Proof.

Let
G
be an
n
-vertex permutation graph. A permutation representation of
G
can be found in
O(n+|E(G)|)
time
[
28
]
. Since a maximum clique in a permutation graph can be found in
O(n\log n)
time from such a representation
[
15
]
, the greedy portion of
Greedy Edmonds
can be made to run in
O(n^{2}\log n)
time.
Finally, Rhee and Liang
[
31
]
proved that a maximum matching of an
n
-vertex permutation graph can be found in
O(n\log\log n)
-time. Thus, the total running time is
O(n^{2}\log n)
.
∎

Finally, we show that the approximation ratio in
Theorem

1.4
is best possible for
Greedy Edmonds
, for every fixed value of the clique number, and that the same examples show that for
Cluster Deletion
the approximation ratio of
Greedy Edmonds
is exactly
2
.

Theorem 1.6
.

For each fixed
\ell\geq 3
, there are infinitely many graphs
G
with
\omega(G)=\ell
and clique partitions
\mathcal{X}
that can be output by
Greedy Edmonds
such that

{\mathsf{OPT}}=\frac{2\binom{\ell}{2}}{\binom{\ell}{2}+1}|E(\mathcal{X})|\qquad\text{and}\qquad|E(\overline{\mathcal{X}})|=2\,{\mathsf{OPT}}_{\mathrm{CD}},

where
{\mathsf{OPT}}
is the maximum number of edges in a clique partition of
G
and
{\mathsf{OPT}}_{\mathrm{CD}}=|E(G)|-{\mathsf{OPT}}
is the minimum number of edges whose deletion turns
G
into a cluster graph.

2
Preliminaries

All graphs in this paper are assumed to be simple and undirected. We use standard graph theoretical notation and terminology (see
[
11
]
). For example, we say that a graph
G
is
H
-free
, if it does not contain
H
as an induced subgraph. Note that
G
is a cluster graph if and only if
G
is
P_{3}
-free, where
P_{3}
is a 3-vertex path. A graph
G
is a
cograph
if
it is
P_{4}
-free. The
complement
of a graph
G
is denoted by
\overline{G}
. The
disjoint union
of two graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
is denoted by
G_{1}\sqcup G_{2}
, and the
join

G_{1}\vee G_{2}
is the graph obtained from
G_{1}\sqcup G_{2}
by adding all edges between
V(G_{1})
and
V(G_{2})
.

A
clique
in a graph
G
is a set of pairwise adjacent vertices of
G
, and a
stable set
is a set of pairwise nonadjacent vertices. Thus, the stable sets of
G
are exactly the cliques of
\overline{G}
. A
clique partition
of
G
is a partition of
V(G)
into cliques of
G
. If
\mathcal{X}
is a clique partition of
G
, we let
E(\mathcal{X})
and
E(\overline{\mathcal{X}})
denote the set of edges of
G
contained (respectively, not contained) in the cliques of
\mathcal{X}
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we let
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
and
S_{n}
denote the set of all permutations of
[n]
. We write a permutation as a word

\pi=[\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}]
, and write
\operatorname{pos}_{\pi}(v)
for the position of the value
v
in this word. The
permutation graph
associated with
\pi
, denoted by
G_{\pi}
, has vertex set
[n]
and edge set

E(G_{\pi}):=\left\{\{u,v\}:u<v\text{ and }\operatorname{pos}_{\pi}(u)>\operatorname{pos}_{\pi}(v)\right\}.

Equivalently, if
i<j
and
\pi_{i}>\pi_{j}
, then
\{\pi_{i},\pi_{j}\}
is an edge. Thus, cliques of
G_{\pi}
are precisely the decreasing subsequences of the word
\pi
, and stable sets of
G_{\pi}
are precisely the increasing subsequences of
\pi
. In particular, if
\pi^{\mathrm{R}}:=[\pi_{n},\dots,\pi_{1}]
is the reverse of
\pi
, then
G_{\pi^{\mathrm{R}}}=\overline{G_{\pi}}
, so the class of permutation graphs is closed under complementation. A graph is a permutation graph if and only if it is isomorphic to
G_{\pi}
for some permutation
\pi
. Given a permutation graph, such a permutation can be found in linear time
[
28
]
. Permutation graphs were originally introduced by
[
13
]
and
[
29
]
. A comprehensive overview of their structural and algorithmic properties is provided in
[
18
]
.

Let
\alpha\geq 1
. An
\alpha
-approximation algorithm
is an algorithm which returns a solution which is within a multiplicative factor of
\alpha
to an optimal solution.

3
A Greedy Algorithm

In this section we review the algorithm for
Clique Partition
introduced by Dessmark et al.
[
10
]
.
The algorithm requires
Max Clique
as a subroutine, and therefore does not run in polynomial time for general graphs. However, in every graph class for which
Max Clique
can be computed in polynomial time, the algorithm runs in polynomial time. We call the algorithm
Greedy
, since it proceeds greedily. The description of
Greedy
is as follows. Let
G
be a graph and
X
be a maximum clique of
G
.
Greedy
adds
X
to the clique partition and then recurses on
G-X
.

Algorithm 1:

Greedy

Input:

A graph
G
.

Output:

A clique partition
\mathcal{X}
of
G
.

1.

Set
\mathcal{X}=\emptyset
.

2.

While
V(G)\neq\emptyset
:

choose a maximum clique
X
of
G
;

set
\mathcal{X}\leftarrow\mathcal{X}\cup\{X\}
and
G\leftarrow G-X
.

3.

Return
\mathcal{X}
.

Dessmark et al.
[
10
]
proved that
Greedy
is a 2-approximation algorithm for both
Cluster Deletion
and
Clique Partition
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For every graph
G
,
Greedy
returns a clique partition
\mathcal{X}
such that
2|E(\mathcal{X})|\geq{\mathsf{OPT}}
, where
{\mathsf{OPT}}
is the number of edges in an optimal clique partition of
G
.

Theorem 3.2
.

For every graph
G
,
Greedy
returns a clique partition
\mathcal{X}
such that
|E(\overline{\mathcal{X}})|\leq 2{\mathsf{OPT}}
, where
{\mathsf{OPT}}
is the number of edges in an optimal solution to
Cluster Deletion
, and
E(\overline{\mathcal{X}})
are the edges in
G
which are not contained in a clique of
\mathcal{X}
.

For completeness, we include proofs of both
Theorem

3.1
and
Theorem

3.2
in
Appendix

A
.

4
Cluster deletion in cographs

In this section, we give a simpler proof that
Greedy
computes an optimal clique partition in cographs, which was first proved in
[
16
]
. We require the following well-known property of cographs.

Lemma 4.1
.

Every cograph
G
with at least two vertices has a partition
(A,B)
of
V(G)
such that either
G=G[A]\sqcup G[B]
or
G=G[A]\vee G[B]
.

For a clique partition
\mathcal{P}
, let

p_{1}\geq p_{2}\geq\cdots
be its clique sizes in nonincreasing order, padded by zeros. We say that
\mathcal{P}

majorizes
a clique partition
\mathcal{Q}
, whose padded size sequence is
q_{1}\geq q_{2}\geq\cdots
, if

\sum_{i=1}^{j}p_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{j}q_{i}\qquad\text{for every }j\geq 1.

Lemma 4.2
.

If
\mathcal{P}
majorizes
\mathcal{Q}
, then

|E(\mathcal{P})|\geq|E(\mathcal{Q})|
.

Proof.

Both size sequences have the same sum, namely
|V(G)|
. The function

x\mapsto\binom{x}{2}
is convex on the nonnegative reals, so the
majorization inequality (equivalently, Karamata’s inequality
[
27
]
) gives

|E(\mathcal{P})|=\sum_{i}\binom{p_{i}}{2}\geq\sum_{i}\binom{q_{i}}{2}=|E(\mathcal{Q})|.

∎

We prove the following statement, which is stronger than optimality.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
G
be a cograph. Every clique partition of
G
output by

Greedy
majorizes every clique partition of
G
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
|V(G)|
. The claim is immediate when

|V(G)|\leq 1
. Let
\mathcal{X}
be a partition output by
Greedy
,
and let
\mathcal{Y}
be any clique partition of
G
. By

Lemma

4.1
, there is a nontrivial partition
(A,B)

of
V(G)
such that
G=G[A]\sqcup G[B]
or

G=G[A]\vee G[B]
.

First suppose that
G=G[A]\sqcup G[B]
. Every clique lies wholly in

A
or wholly in
B
. Moreover, the restriction of the greedy run to
each side is clearly a greedy run on that side. Thus, the
size sequence of
\mathcal{X}
is the nonincreasing merge of greedy
size sequences
p^{A}
and
p^{B}
for
G[A]
and
G[B]
. Likewise, the
size sequence of
\mathcal{Y}
is the nonincreasing merge of size
sequences
q^{A}
and
q^{B}
for clique partitions of the two sides. By
induction,
p^{A}
majorizes
q^{A}
and
p^{B}
majorizes
q^{B}
. For each

j\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{j}|Y_{i}|

\displaystyle=\max_{r+s=j}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}q^{A}_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}q^{B}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{r+s=j}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}p^{A}_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}p^{B}_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{j}|X_{i}|,

where the parts of
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are ordered by
nonincreasing size.

Now suppose that
G=G[A]\vee G[B]
. A maximum clique in every induced
subgraph of this join is the union of a maximum clique on each
nonempty side. Consequently, there are greedy size sequences

p^{A}=(p^{A}_{i})
and
p^{B}=(p^{B}_{i})
for the two sides, padded by zeros,
such that the greedy size sequence for
G
is

p_{i}=p^{A}_{i}+p^{B}_{i}\qquad(i\geq 1).

Both component sequences are nonincreasing, so
(p_{i})
is also
nonincreasing. Order the parts
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots
of
\mathcal{Y}
by
nonincreasing size, and put
a_{i}=|Y_{i}\cap A|
and

b_{i}=|Y_{i}\cap B|
. The nonempty intersections form clique partitions
of
G[A]
and
G[B]
. If
a^{\downarrow}
and
b^{\downarrow}
denote
the two intersection-size sequences in nonincreasing order, induction
gives, for every
j
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{j}|Y_{i}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{j}(a_{i}+b_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{j}a^{\downarrow}_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{j}b^{\downarrow}_{i}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{j}(p^{A}_{i}+p^{B}_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{j}p_{i}.

Thus,
\mathcal{X}
majorizes
\mathcal{Y}
in both cases.
∎

Combining
4.2
and
4.3
proves

Theorem

1.1
.

Remark 4.4
.

Since the majorization inequality holds for every convex function,
Theorem

4.3
gives more than
Theorem

1.1
. Let
f
be a function on the nonnegative integers with
f(0)=0
and with
f(x+1)-f(x)
nondecreasing in
x
. Extending
f
piecewise linearly to
[0,\infty)
gives a convex function, so Karamata’s inequality applies to integer sequences. Then, for every cograph
G
, every clique partition
\mathcal{X}
output by
Greedy
maximizes
\sum_{X\in\mathcal{X}}f(|X|)
over all clique partitions of
G
. Taking
f(x)=\binom{x}{2}
recovers
Theorem

1.1
. Taking
f(x)=-1
for all
x\geq 1
shows that
Greedy
also uses the minimum possible number of cliques. That is, it also computes the
clique cover number
of a cograph.

4.1
A clique-partition linear program

The preceding majorization argument actually shows that the natural linear
programming relaxation of
Clique Partition
is exact on cographs. Recall that
\mathcal{C}(G)
is the family of all
nonempty cliques of
G
, and that (
LP
) asks to maximize
\sum_{K\in\mathcal{C}(G)}\binom{|K|}{2}x_{K}
subject to
x\geq 0
and
\sum_{K\ni v}x_{K}=1
for every
v\in V(G)
.
If the vertex constraints are instead written as inequalities, their
slack can be assigned to singleton cliques without changing the
objective. The incidence vector of every clique partition is a feasible solution of (
LP
), so the optimal value of (
LP
) is always at least the maximum number of edges in a clique partition of
G
. We say that (
LP
) is
exact
for
G
if equality holds.

The feasible region of (
LP
) is not integral,
even for cographs. Indeed, if
G=K_{3}
with vertex set
\{1,2,3\}
,
then

x_{\{1,2\}}=x_{\{1,3\}}=x_{\{2,3\}}=\frac{1}{2}

and all other variables zero give a fractional feasible solution. The
three positive columns form the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\
1&0&1\\
0&1&1\end{pmatrix},

whose determinant is
-2
. Thus, this solution is a basic feasible
solution. However, we will show that (
LP
) always has an optimal solution that is
\{0,1\}
-valued when
G
is a cograph.

To prove this statement, we extend majorization to fractional clique
partitions. A
weighted size profile
is a finite family

\mathcal{Q}=\{(q_{\alpha},\lambda_{\alpha}):\alpha\in I\},\qquad q_{\alpha}\geq 0,\quad\lambda_{\alpha}\geq 0.

Let
q^{\downarrow}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be the decreasing
step function in which the value
q_{\alpha}
occupies an interval of
length
\lambda_{\alpha}
, padded by zeros, and define

L_{\mathcal{Q}}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}q^{\downarrow}(s)\,ds.

Equivalently,
L_{\mathcal{Q}}(t)
is the maximum total size obtainable
by taking total weight
t
from
\mathcal{Q}
, allowing a fractional
amount of an entry.

Every feasible solution
x
of (
LP
) has the
weighted size profile

\mathcal{Q}_{x}:=\{(|K|,x_{K}):K\in\mathcal{C}(G)\}.

The covering constraints imply

\sum_{K\in\mathcal{C}(G)}|K|x_{K}=\sum_{v\in V(G)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\in\mathcal{C}(G)\\
v\in K\end{subarray}}x_{K}=|V(G)|.

(1)

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
G
be a cograph, let
\mathcal{P}
be a clique partition output
by
Greedy
, and let
p_{1}\geq p_{2}\geq\cdots
be its clique sizes,
padded by zeros. For every feasible solution
x
of
(
LP
) and every
t\geq 0
,

L_{\mathcal{Q}_{x}}(t)\leq\int_{0}^{t}p^{\downarrow}(s)\,ds,

where
p^{\downarrow}
is the step function that takes the value
p_{i}

on the interval
[i-1,i)
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
|V(G)|
. The claim is immediate when

|V(G)|\leq 1
. Choose a nontrivial cograph decomposition
(A,B)
as
in
Lemma

4.1
.

First suppose that
G=G[A]\sqcup G[B]
. Every clique is contained in
one side, so
\mathcal{Q}_{x}
is the union of feasible fractional
profiles
\mathcal{Q}_{A}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{B}
for the two sides. The
restriction of the greedy run to each side is a greedy run; denote its
profiles by
p^{A}
and
p^{B}
. By induction, for every
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle L_{\mathcal{Q}_{x}}(t)

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}r,s\geq 0\\
r+s=t\end{subarray}}\bigl(L_{\mathcal{Q}_{A}}(r)+L_{\mathcal{Q}_{B}}(s)\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}r,s\geq 0\\
r+s=t\end{subarray}}\bigl(L_{p^{A}}(r)+L_{p^{B}}(s)\bigr)=L_{p}(t),

where
p
is the nonincreasing merge of
p^{A}
and
p^{B}
.

Now suppose that
G=G[A]\vee G[B]
. For every clique
K
of
G
, put

K_{A}=K\cap A
and
K_{B}=K\cap B
. Using weight
x_{K}
on the entries

|K_{A}|
and
|K_{B}|
gives feasible fractional profiles

\mathcal{Q}_{A}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{B}
on the two sides; zero entries may
simply be retained. For any choice of total weight
t
, the contribution
of the entries
|K_{A}|+|K_{B}|
is at most the sum of the
t
largest
entries from the two component profiles. Hence,

L_{\mathcal{Q}_{x}}(t)\leq L_{\mathcal{Q}_{A}}(t)+L_{\mathcal{Q}_{B}}(t).

There are greedy profiles
p^{A}
and
p^{B}
, padded by zeros, such that
the greedy profile of the join satisfies

p_{i}=p^{A}_{i}+p^{B}_{i}\qquad(i\geq 1).

Since both component profiles are nonincreasing, induction gives

L_{\mathcal{Q}_{x}}(t)\leq L_{p^{A}}(t)+L_{p^{B}}(t)=L_{p}(t).

This completes the induction.
∎

We are now ready to prove
Theorem

1.2
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

Let
x
be any feasible solution, and let
\mathcal{P}
be a clique
partition output by
Greedy
, with size sequence

p_{1}\geq p_{2}\geq\cdots
. By

Equations

1
and
4.5
, the
profile
p
majorizes the weighted profile
\mathcal{Q}_{x}
, and both
profiles have total size
|V(G)|
. The weighted form of Karamata’s
inequality (see
[
27
]
), applied to the convex function

f(z)=\binom{z}{2}
, therefore gives

\sum_{K\in\mathcal{C}(G)}x_{K}\binom{|K|}{2}\leq\sum_{i}\binom{p_{i}}{2}=|E(\mathcal{P})|.

The incidence vector of
\mathcal{P}
is a feasible integral solution
of (
LP
). It is therefore at least as good as
every fractional solution, and hence is optimal.
∎

5
Cluster deletion in permutation graphs is NP-complete

In this section, we prove
Theorem

1.3
, which resolves an open problem of Konstantinidis and Papadopoulos
[
25
]
. As explained in the introduction, the decision versions of
Clique Partition
and
Cluster Deletion
are equivalent and belong to
\mathsf{NP}
, so it suffices to show that
Clique Partition
is NP-hard on permutation graphs.

We give a polynomial reduction from the
Optimum Cost Chromatic Partition
(OCCP) problem.

Optimum Cost Chromatic Partition
:

Input:
A graph
H
with
n
vertices and nonnegative integers
k_{1}\leq k_{2}\leq\dots\leq k_{n}
.

Output:
A partition
\mathcal{S}=(S_{1},\dots,S_{n})
of
V(H)
into stable sets of
H
(some of which may be empty) such that
{\mathsf{cost}}(\mathcal{S}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}|S_{i}|
is minimum.

In other words, color
i
has cost
k_{i}
, and OCCP asks for a proper coloring of
H
of minimum total cost; since
H
has only
n
vertices, allowing
n
colors is not a restriction. We call a partition of
V(H)
into
n
stable sets a
coloring
of
H
. In the decision version of OCCP, we are additionally given an integer
c
, and the question is whether
H
has a coloring of cost at most
c
. Jansen
[
23
]
proved that OCCP is NP-complete on permutation graphs. Moreover, in the instances constructed in his hardness proof, the costs
k_{i}
are bounded by a polynomial in
n
(see
[
23
, Section 4]
). We only use this restricted form of Jansen’s result.

Theorem 5.1

(Jansen
[
23
]
)
.

There is a constant
d
such that the decision version of OCCP is NP-hard when restricted to instances
(H,(k_{1},\dots,k_{n}),c)
in which
H
is a permutation graph and
k_{n}\leq n^{d}
.

5.1
Scores

It is convenient to measure clique partitions by a slightly different quantity than the number of edges. For a clique partition
\mathcal{K}=(K_{1},\dots,K_{m})
of a graph
G
, we define the
score
of
\mathcal{K}
to be

{\mathsf{sc}}(\mathcal{K}):=\sum_{j=1}^{m}|K_{j}|^{2}.

Since
\sum_{j=1}^{m}|K_{j}|=|V(G)|
, we have

|E(\mathcal{K})|=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\binom{|K_{j}|}{2}=\frac{{\mathsf{sc}}(\mathcal{K})-|V(G)|}{2}.

(2)

Thus, maximizing
|E(\mathcal{K})|
is the same as maximizing
{\mathsf{sc}}(\mathcal{K})
, and for every integer
t
,
G
has a clique partition with at least
t
edges if and only if
G
has a clique partition of score at least
2t+|V(G)|
.

5.2
The construction

Fix an instance
(H,(k_{1},\dots,k_{n}),c)
of OCCP, where
H
is a graph with vertex set
V(H)
of size
n
, and
k_{1}\leq\dots\leq k_{n}
are nonnegative integers. Put

S:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i},\qquad M:=n^{2}+1,\qquad a_{i}:=1+M(S-k_{i})\quad(i\in[n]).

Note that
a_{1}\geq a_{2}\geq\dots\geq a_{n}\geq 1
. Let
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}
be pairwise disjoint sets of vertices, disjoint from
V(H)
, with
|A_{i}|=a_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
, and let

G:=\overline{H}\vee\bigl(K[A_{1}]\sqcup K[A_{2}]\sqcup\dots\sqcup K[A_{n}]\bigr),

where
K[A_{i}]
denotes the complete graph with vertex set
A_{i}
. Explicitly,
V(G)=V(H)\cup A_{1}\cup\dots\cup A_{n}
; two vertices of
H
are adjacent in
G
if and only if they are nonadjacent in
H
; every vertex of
H
is adjacent to every vertex of
A_{1}\cup\dots\cup A_{n}
; each
A_{i}
is a clique of
G
; and there are no edges between
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
for
i\neq j
. Finally, put

C:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}+2n+2MSn\qquad\text{and}\qquad T:=C-2Mc.

(3)

We record two simple observations. First, since there are no edges between distinct sets
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
, and since the cliques of
\overline{H}
are the stable sets of
H
, we have the following description of the cliques of
G
.

Fact 5.2
.

The cliques of
G
are exactly the sets of the form
B\cup Q
, where
B\subseteq A_{i}
for some
i\in[n]
and
Q
is a stable set of
H
. In particular, no clique of
G
meets two of the sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}
.

Second,
|V(G)|=n+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\leq n+n(1+MS)=O(n^{3}S)
. Hence, if
k_{n}\leq n^{d}
, then
S\leq n^{d+1}
and
|V(G)|=O(n^{d+4})
, so
G
can be constructed in time polynomial in
n
.

For a coloring
\mathcal{S}=(S_{1},\dots,S_{n})
of
H
, we define

\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}:=(A_{1}\cup S_{1},\dots,A_{n}\cup S_{n}).

Lemma 5.3
.

For every coloring
\mathcal{S}=(S_{1},\dots,S_{n})
of
H
,
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}
is a clique partition of
G
, and

{\mathsf{sc}}(\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}})=C+\sum_{i=1}^{n}|S_{i}|^{2}-2M\cdot{\mathsf{cost}}(\mathcal{S}).

Proof.

Each
A_{i}\cup S_{i}
is a clique of
G
by
Fact

5.2
, and these sets partition
V(G)
. Using
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|S_{i}|=n
, we compute

\displaystyle{\mathsf{sc}}(\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}+|S_{i}|)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}|S_{i}|^{2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}|S_{i}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}|S_{i}|^{2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(1+M(S-k_{i})\bigr)|S_{i}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}|S_{i}|^{2}+2n+2MSn-2M\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}|S_{i}|

\displaystyle=C+\sum_{i=1}^{n}|S_{i}|^{2}-2M\cdot{\mathsf{cost}}(\mathcal{S}).\qed

The next lemma shows that, as far as maximum scores are concerned, we may restrict our attention to clique partitions of the form
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a clique partition of
G
of maximum score. Then
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}
for some coloring
\mathcal{S}
of
H
. Consequently, for every clique partition
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
of
G
there is a coloring
\mathcal{S}
of
H
with
{\mathsf{sc}}(\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}})\geq{\mathsf{sc}}(\mathcal{K}^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We first show that each
A_{i}
is contained in a single part of
\mathcal{K}
. Suppose not, and let
K
and
K^{\prime}
be distinct parts of
\mathcal{K}
that both meet
A_{i}
. By
Fact

5.2
,
K=B\cup Q
and
K^{\prime}=B^{\prime}\cup Q^{\prime}
, where
B
and
B^{\prime}
are disjoint nonempty subsets of
A_{i}
, and
Q,Q^{\prime}\subseteq V(H)
are stable sets of
H
. By symmetry, we may assume that
|Q|\geq|Q^{\prime}|
. Let
\mathcal{K}^{*}
be obtained from
\mathcal{K}
by replacing
K
and
K^{\prime}
by
K\cup B^{\prime}=B\cup B^{\prime}\cup Q
and
K^{\prime}\setminus B^{\prime}=Q^{\prime}
(and discarding
Q^{\prime}
if it is empty). By
Fact

5.2
,
K\cup B^{\prime}
is a clique of
G
, so
\mathcal{K}^{*}
is a clique partition of
G
, and

\displaystyle{\mathsf{sc}}(\mathcal{K}^{*})-{\mathsf{sc}}(\mathcal{K})

\displaystyle=\bigl(|B|+|B^{\prime}|+|Q|\bigr)^{2}+|Q^{\prime}|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\bigl(|B|+|Q|\bigr)^{2}-\bigl(|B^{\prime}|+|Q^{\prime}|\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=2|B^{\prime}|\bigl(|B|+|Q|-|Q^{\prime}|\bigr)>0,

since
|B^{\prime}|\geq 1
,
|B|\geq 1
and
|Q|\geq|Q^{\prime}|
. This contradicts the maximality of
{\mathsf{sc}}(\mathcal{K})
.

Hence, for each
i\in[n]
there is a unique part
K_{i}
of
\mathcal{K}
containing
A_{i}
, and by
Fact

5.2
we can write
K_{i}=A_{i}\cup S_{i}
with
S_{i}\subseteq V(H)
a stable set of
H
. The parts
K_{1},\dots,K_{n}
are distinct, since by
Fact

5.2
no clique of
G
meets two of the sets
A_{i}
, and every other part of
\mathcal{K}
is a nonempty subset of
V(H)
. Suppose that there is such a part
K_{0}\subseteq V(H)
. Then
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|S_{i}|\leq n-|K_{0}|<n
, so
S_{i}=\emptyset
for some
i\in[n]
, that is,
K_{i}=A_{i}
. By
Fact

5.2
,
A_{i}\cup K_{0}
is a clique of
G
, and replacing the two parts
K_{i}
and
K_{0}
by the single part
A_{i}\cup K_{0}
increases the score by
(a_{i}+|K_{0}|)^{2}-a_{i}^{2}-|K_{0}|^{2}=2a_{i}|K_{0}|>0
, again contradicting the maximality of
{\mathsf{sc}}(\mathcal{K})
.

Therefore,
\mathcal{K}=(A_{1}\cup S_{1},\dots,A_{n}\cup S_{n})=\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}
, where
\mathcal{S}=(S_{1},\dots,S_{n})
is a coloring of
H
. The last assertion follows since a clique partition of maximum score exists.
∎

We are now ready to establish the correctness of the reduction.

Theorem 5.5
.

H
has a coloring of cost at most
c
if and only if
G
has a clique partition of score at least
T
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
\mathcal{S}=(S_{1},\dots,S_{n})
is a coloring of
H
with
{\mathsf{cost}}(\mathcal{S})\leq c
. By
Lemma

5.3
,

{\mathsf{sc}}(\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}})=C+\sum_{i=1}^{n}|S_{i}|^{2}-2M\cdot{\mathsf{cost}}(\mathcal{S})\geq C-2Mc=T.

Conversely, suppose that
G
has a clique partition of score at least
T
. By
Lemma

5.4
, there is a coloring
\mathcal{S}=(S_{1},\dots,S_{n})
of
H
with
{\mathsf{sc}}(\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}})\geq T
. By
Lemma

5.3
,

2M\bigl({\mathsf{cost}}(\mathcal{S})-c\bigr)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}|S_{i}|^{2}\leq\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|S_{i}|\Bigr)^{2}=n^{2}<2M.

Since
{\mathsf{cost}}(\mathcal{S})
and
c
are integers, it follows that
{\mathsf{cost}}(\mathcal{S})\leq c
.
∎

The same computation shows that the reduction maps optimal solutions to optimal solutions. Indeed, let
\mathcal{K}
be a clique partition of
G
of maximum score. By
Lemma

5.4
,
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}
for some coloring
\mathcal{S}
of
H
, and if some coloring
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
of
H
had
{\mathsf{cost}}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})<{\mathsf{cost}}(\mathcal{S})
, then
Lemma

5.3
would give
{\mathsf{sc}}(\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}})-{\mathsf{sc}}(\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}})\geq 2M-n^{2}>0
, a contradiction. Thus,
\mathcal{S}
is a minimum cost coloring of
H
(in fact, one maximizing
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|S_{i}|^{2}
among all minimum cost colorings).

5.3
Preserving permutation graphs

It remains to show that the reduction preserves the property of being a permutation graph. This also follows from the facts that the class of permutation graphs is closed under complementation, disjoint unions and joins (see
[
18
]
), but we give an explicit permutation.

Lemma 5.6
.

If
H
is a permutation graph, then so is
G
. Moreover, given a permutation
\pi
with
H\cong G_{\pi}
, a permutation
\sigma
with

|V(G)|
.

Proof.

We may assume that
V(H)=[n]
and
H=G_{\pi}
for a permutation

of

N:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
and
n_{i}:=a_{1}+\dots+a_{i}
for

, so that
n_{0}=0
and

be the following permutation of

, written as a word:

\displaystyle\sigma:=[

\displaystyle\pi_{n}+N,\ \pi_{n-1}+N,\ \dots,\ \pi_{1}+N,

\displaystyle n_{1},\ n_{1}-1,\ \dots,\ n_{0}+1,

\displaystyle n_{2},\ n_{2}-1,\ \dots,\ n_{1}+1,

\displaystyle\vdots

•

An improved approximation algorithm

In this section we present an improved approximation algorithm for
Clique Partition
. The description of the algorithm is simple. While the current graph has clique number at least
3
, we compute a maximum clique
X
, put
X
into the clique partition, and delete
X
. Once the remaining graph has clique number at most
2
, an optimal clique partition consists of a maximum matching together with singleton cliques. We call the algorithm
Greedy Edmonds
.

Algorithm 2:

Greedy Edmonds

Input:

A graph

.

Output:

A clique partition

of

1.

\mathcal{X}=\emptyset

.

3.

Compute a maximum matching
\mathcal{M}
of
G
.

4.

Add the edges of
\mathcal{M}
as two-vertex cliques and all
uncovered vertices as singleton cliques.

5.

Return
\mathcal{X}
.

Let
G
be a graph,
\mathcal{Y}
be a clique partition of

x\in X_{1}
.
Therefore,

|E(\mathcal{Y})|-|E(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})|\leq\sum_{x\in X_{1}}y(x)\leq\sum_{x\in X_{1}}(k-1)=k(k-1).

We also clearly have

|E(\mathcal{X})|-|E(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})|=\frac{k(k-1)}{2}.

By induction,
|E(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})|\geq\frac{{\mathsf{OPT}}^{\prime}}{2}\geq\frac{|E(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})|}{2}
. Since

|E(\mathcal{Y})|\leq|E(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})|+k(k-1)
, the preceding
two inequalities give

|E(\mathcal{X})|\geq\frac{|E(\mathcal{Y})|}{2}
, as required.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{r})
be the sequence of cliques
constructed by
Greedy
, where
X_{1}
is a maximum clique of
G
.
We induct on
r
. The case
r=1
is immediate. Let

\mathcal{Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m})
be an optimal clique partition, and
put

\begin{split}G^{\prime}&=G-X_{1},\qquad\mathcal{X}^{\prime}=(X_{2},\ldots,X_{r}),\\
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}&=(Y_{1}\setminus X_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}\setminus X_{1}),\\
\mathcal{Z}&=(X_{1},Y_{1}\setminus X_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}\setminus X_{1}),\end{split}

omitting empty parts. Write
D(\mathcal{P})=E(\overline{\mathcal{P}})

for any clique partition
\mathcal{P}
. By induction,

|D(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})|\leq 2|D(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})|.

Since
D(\mathcal{Z})
is the disjoint union of

\delta_{G}(X_{1})
and
D(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})
, it follows that

|D(\mathcal{X})|\leq|\delta_{G}(X_{1})|+2|D(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})|=|D(\mathcal{Z})|+|D(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})|.

(10)

Let

P=D(\mathcal{Z})\setminus D(\mathcal{Y})\quad\text{and}\quad Q=D(\mathcal{Y})\setminus D(\mathcal{Z}).

Put
k=|X_{1}|
and
x_{i}=|X_{1}\cap Y_{i}|
. Because every
Y_{i}
is a
clique and
X_{1}
is a maximum clique,
|Y_{i}|\leq k
, and hence

\displaystyle|P|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}(|Y_{i}|-x_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}(k-x_{i})=2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq m}x_{i}x_{j}=2|Q|.

Here
Q
consists precisely of the edges of the clique
X_{1}
whose
ends lie in different parts of
\mathcal{Y}
. Moreover,

D(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})
and
Q
are edge-disjoint subsets of

D(\mathcal{Y})
: the former contains edges with both ends outside

X_{1}
, while the latter contains edges with both ends inside
X_{1}
.
Using (
10
), we obtain

\displaystyle|D(\mathcal{X})|

\displaystyle\leq|D(\mathcal{Z})|+|D(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=|D(\mathcal{Y})|+|P|-|Q|+|D(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq|D(\mathcal{Y})|+|Q|+|D(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})|
