Cultivating competent public health professionals: the role of research participation and academic motivation
Authors: Xinyu Wang, Xinquan Li, Jiahui Zhang, Sihan Bao, MaoWang Bian, Jiabin Sun, Weiqin Cai, Yang Xiao, Hongqing An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe growing complexity of global public health issues necessitates a stronger focus on cultivating public health professionals with robust job competency. This study investigates the impact of research participation on the job competency of public health students and its underlying mechanisms.MethodsIn April-June 2021, a cross-sectional study was conducted using a cluster sampling method to recruit 906 public health students from three universities in Shandong Province, China. A self-administered questionnaire was used for data collection. The entropy weight method was applied to assign weights to a self-constructed competency index system for evaluating individual job competency. Propensity score matching (PSM) was employed to assess the impact of research participation on competency. A mediation analysis was used to examine the role of academic motivation in this relationship.ResultsPSM analysis indicates that participation in research activities significantly enhances students' workplace competence. Among the three matching methods—nearest neighbour matching, radius matching, and kernel matching—the average treatment effect values for the intervention group (ATT) ranged between 0.037 and 0.041, all statistically significant at the 1% level. Mediation analysis indicates that research participation not only directly enhances competence but also produces a significant indirect statistical association (β = 0.0340, p = 0.001) by strengthening academic motivation. This mediating effect accounts for 12.6% of the total effect.ConclusionEven after adjusting for observable confounding factors, there remains a significant association between public health students' participation in research activities and the enhancement of their professional competencies. This association encompasses both a direct link between research involvement and professional development, as well as an indirect link mediated through enhanced academic motivation. Given the cross-sectional design and analytical limitations of this study, the findings cannot establish causality but provide empirical evidence supporting the encouragement of student participation in public health education research. Consequently, it is recommended that supportive mechanisms be established to foster student involvement in research, thereby cultivating a cohort of public health professionals equipped with specialised competencies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09145-3.Keywords: Research participation, Public health students, Job competency, Entropy weight method, Mediation effect

Introduction

Amidst the growing complexity of global health issues, the field of public health is confronted with multifaceted challenges such as infectious disease prevention and control, chronic disease management, and responses to public health emergencies. This makes the cultivation of public health professionals particularly crucial [1]. However, while China's public health education system has expanded in scale, it faces a structural contradiction where the quality of talent cultivation is misaligned with industry demands [2, 3]. The 2023 Guidelines on Public Health Education Reform issued by China’s Ministry of Education explicitly called for establishing a job competency-oriented talent cultivation system, strengthening students’ core competencies in “epidemic surveillance, risk assessment, and emergency response”. Job competency for public health students encompasses not only professional knowledge but also practical skills, innovative thinking, teamwork, and other dimensions [4, 5]. As the future backbone of public health endeavours [6], the competency of these students directly impacts the quality and efficiency of public health services. Nevertheless, a disconnect between theory and practice persists in current public health education. Surveys indicate that only 58.3% of undergraduate students have participated in research projects, with opportunities concentrated among those with strong academic records and clear intentions for further studies—reflecting selective barriers in the allocation of research resources.

Research, as a vital component of higher education, serves as a key pathway for enhancing students' comprehensive capabilities [7]. In terms of professional knowledge and skills, participation in research projects enables students to develop technical competencies such as laboratory techniques and data analysis capabilities [8]. Through interaction and collaboration with peers and mentors, students may also develop improved coordination abilities and interpersonal skills [9]. Research experience also plays a significant role in shaping personal qualities. Engaging in scientific research broadens the depth and breadth of students' thinking [10] and is positively correlated with an increase in self-efficacy [11]. Furthermore, the process of applying and analyzing theoretical knowledge fosters the development of critical thinking skills [12].

Existing research has primarily focused on the relationship between participation in research and the enhancement of technical skills, whilst studies providing empirical analysis of its systematic association with job competence remain relatively scarce. From the perspective of research subjects, current findings are largely concentrated on the job competency of practicing professionals, such as clinical general practitioners, while research focusing on public health students remains relatively scarce. Research engagement is closely associated with factors such as the academic environment [13], research platforms, and faculty-student relationships [14], which are also relevant to capability development, creating complex confounding effects. By contrast, propensity score matching enhances the robustness of association analysis in observational studies by simulating randomised controlled trial conditions through the construction of counterfactual frameworks [15, 16]. Therefore, this study focuses on public health undergraduates and employs propensity score matching to investigate the specific impact of research participation on their job competency. This study draws upon the core construct of the Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT) — experiential mastery — positing that the locally adapted ‘mentor-directed’ research participation in China constitutes a pivotal experiential mastery for students, thereby providing the theoretical foundation for the research hypotheses. The findings aim to provide scientific evidence for talent cultivation in public health education within higher medical institutions and contribute to strengthening the public health workforce development.

Theoretical analysis and research hypotheses

The effect of research participation on job competency

Participating in scientific research activities is a key practical approach related to the improvement of college students' job competency, and its mechanism of action can be effectively interpreted through McClelland's competency theory (Iceberg Model). This model divides human capabilities into two distinct parts: the visible knowledge and skills "above the surface," and the deeper, underlying intrinsic traits such as motivation, self-concept, attitudes, and values hidden "below the surface" [17].

Scientific research is essentially a highly comprehensive practical field that can directly relate to the cultivation of surface-level skills. By conducting extensive literature reviews, students master the latest professional knowledge in their field. Hands-on activities such as performing experiments, analyzing data, and utilizing specialized software, to further help students develop relevant technical abilities [8, 18]. Moreover, writing reports and engaging in academic exchanges hone their logical thinking, communication, and expression abilities [19].

More importantly, participating in scientific research activities is closely related to the cultivation of in-depth abilities. It fosters systematic thinking and critical analytical abilities when confronting complex problems. The process cultivates resilience and stress tolerance through repeated experimentation and manuscript revisions. Furthermore, iscientific research practice can also be related to the formation of a sense of active responsibility and internal driving force—develops proactive responsibility and intrinsic motivation for solving practical problems through independent exploration and teamwork, along with value recognition for rigour, innovation, and academic ethics.

Thus, research provides students with an authentic, challenging environment that not only directly related to the cultivation of the hard knowledge and technical skills required for the position, but more importantly, it is related to the shaping of valuable intrinsic qualities through the process of solving practical problems. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1: Participation in research is significantly positively correlated with the job competency of public health major students.

The mechanism behind the impact of academic motivation on job competency

Participating in scientific research activities, as a key practical path related to the improvement of college students' job competency, operates not only through direct knowledge and skill acquisition but also, more critically, by stimulating and reinforcing academic motivation—a deep psychological variable that plays a core statistical mediating role in this process. According to Lent and Brown's Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT), an individual’s career interests, goal-setting, and ultimate achievements are driven by both self-efficacy and outcome expectations [20]. And the experience of personally controlling and mastering things is a core way to shape self-efficacy and outcome expectations. In the local context of public health education in China, research participation is mostly a "tutor-led" model. The successful experiences that students gain through participating in data collection, analysis, paper writing and other links are typical experiences of personal control and mastery. They can directly enhance students' research self-efficacy and form positive expectations for research results, and these two are the core components of academic motivation.In the context of this study, academic motivation can be regarded as a higher-order construct, whose core is jointly constituted and driven by research self-efficacy and positive expectations of research outcomes. Research participation serves as a key source of learning experiences that shape both.

When students successfully overcome difficulties in scientific research—such as completing experiments, publishing papers, or gaining recognition from their supervisors—they not only accumulate valuable experience but also have a positive correlation with the improvement of self-efficacy in related fields [21, 22]. Simultaneously, they personally experience the intrinsic satisfactions of research, such as the fulfillment of intellectual curiosity and the sense of achievement from problem-solving, as well as extrinsic rewards such as skill enhancement and advantages in further education or employment. These experiences are related to the formation of positive outcome expectations. Together, this enhanced self-efficacy and these positive outcome expectations constitute a powerful and enduring academic motivation, manifested as a strong curiosity for knowledge exploration, an intrinsic drive for excellence, resilience in the face of challenges, and persistence in problem-solving.

Based on the Iceberg Model of competency, deep-level competencies represent the core drivers of behaviour and long-term performance. Strong academic motivation constitutes precisely such a deep-seated driving force. It encourages students to actively seek challenges, pursue excellence, and persist in the face of difficulties, thus being positively correlated with the enhancement of resilience. This motivation can also promote the internalization of knowledge and skills into professional identity, cultivate professional self-awareness, and be related to continuous learning and ability improvement [23].

Therefore, research participation not only directly enhances the surface elements of competency but, more importantly, by providing successful experiences and positive feedback, it is positively correlated with the enhancement of students' self-efficacy in scientific research and their expectations of positive outcomes, thereby being related to the strengthening of integrated academic motivation. This strengthened academic motivation, as a key deep-seated trait, is positively correlated with the development and performance of core competencies. Accordingly, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H2: Academic motivation plays a statistical mediating role in the relationship between research participation and college students' job competency.

Methods

Sampling design: stratified random cluster sampling

To ensure geographical representativeness, a stratified random cluster sampling method was adopted, with the following steps:

Stratification by geography: Shandong Province was divided into three geographical strata: Eastern, Central, and Western regions, based on administrative divisions and economic-developmental homogeneity.

Random selection of universities within strata: One representative medical university was randomly selected from each stratum (Eastern, Central, Western) through consultation with Shandong Provincial Department of Education and university academic affairs offices, ensuring each selected university had a mature public health discipline and sufficient final-year students.

The selection of research sites was not arbitrary but based on the typicality and representativeness of Shandong’s public health education system and the gradient characteristics of medical education development in China. Shandong Province is a key province for the construction of medical education and public health systems in China, and its undergraduate public health education strictly aligns with “Outline of the Plan for Building a Strong Education Nation (2024–2035)”. The curriculum setting, talent training model, and research participation system of the selected universities are highly consistent with the mainstream development status of public health undergraduate education in public medical universities across China. Meanwhile, the three sampled universities cover different levels of school-running strength, scientific research platform configuration and faculty resources, which can reflect the characteristics of research participation and job competency of public health undergraduates in Chinese medical universities with different development levels. This sampling strategy ensures the internal validity of the study conclusions and makes the findings representative of the public health undergraduate education in public medical universities in China.

Cluster definition and selection: Within each selected medical university, all intact classes of final-year public health majors were regarded as "clusters." All clusters were included in the sampling (cluster sampling), as the goal was to cover all eligible final-year students in the selected universities.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Weifang Medical University (Approval No.: 2021YX130; Note: Weifang Medical University was renamed Shandong Second Medical University in December 2023.). All participants were informed of the study's purpose and procedures before the survey, and online informed consent was obtained from each individual.

Instrument

Public health students' job competency

This study treated public health students' job competency as the dependent variable. Based on relevant literature This study treated public health students' job competency as the dependent variable. Based on relevant literature [24, 25] and expert consultation, a job competency evaluation framework was constructed comprising 5 primary dimensions, 24 secondary dimensions, and 52 tertiary items. The primary dimensions include Personal Traits, Professionalism, Professional Knowledge and Skills, Fundamental Professional Competencies, and Cognitive Characteristics. The secondary dimensions encompass elements across multiple aspects such as self-regulation, professional ethics, and communication skills.

To obtain quantitative data, a 5-point Likert scale was utilized, where responses ranging from "Strongly Disagree" to "Strongly Agree" were assigned values from 1 to 5 points, respectively (Further details regarding the questionnaire, including its full composition and thematic structure, are provided in Supplementary File). The measurement demonstrated excellent reliability and validity with an overall Cronbach's α of 0.98, a KMO measure of 0.979, and Bartlett's test of sphericity showing statistical significance (p < 0.001). Exploratory factor analysis was employed to examine the construct validity of the job competency scale. Results indicated a KMO value of 0.979 and a significant Bartlett's sphericity test (χ2 = 44,857.684, df = 1,326, p < 0.001), confirming the data's suitability for factor analysis. Following principal component analysis and rotation, five common factors with eigenvalues exceeding 1 were extracted, cumulatively explaining 68.756% of the total variance. The average commonality of each item was 0.674, indicating the scale possesses sound multidimensional structural characteristics. The entropy weight method was applied to assign weights to indicators at all levels, ultimately generating a total job competency score for each participant.

Referring to the analytical approach used in the study by Rho et al., we conducted confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to validate the structural validity of the measurement scale in our research [26]. Further CFA results showed that the observed indicators of each latent variable all had high factor loadings, ranging from 0.68 to 1.11, and all reached a significant level (p < 0.001), indicating that each item could effectively reflect its latent variable. The overall Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.98, indicating that the scale had extremely high internal consistency. The KMO value was 0.979, and the Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (p < 0.001), further supporting that the data were suitable for factor analysis. The model fit indices showed RMSEA = 0.069, SRMR = 0.073, CFI = 0.883, and TLI = 0.876, all within acceptable ranges, indicating that the model had good construct validity. The entropy weight method was used to assign weights to indicators at all levels, and finally, a comprehensive job competency score for each participant was generated. This composite score was used as the core dependent variable to systematically examine the correlation mechanism between research participation and the job competency of public health major students. (To verify the robustness of the results of the entropy weight method, this study also used the equal weight method and factor score method to recalculate the comprehensive job competency scores. Subsequently, the analysis results of the three methods will be compared to test the consistency of the core conclusions).

This composite score served as the core dependent variable to systematically examine the impact mechanism of research participation on public health students' job competency.

Research participation

The core independent variable of this study is the research participation status of undergraduates majoring in public health. It is quantitatively measured using a binary categorical variable, and the assignment of this variable is strictly based on students' responses to specific questionnaire items. The core item of the questionnaire is: "During your undergraduate studies, have you participated in any form of teacher-guided research projects or independently carried out research practice activities? (Specifically including substantive research behaviors with practical operation and research attributes such as laboratory hands-on work, data collection and analysis in the field of public health, research report/academic paper writing, participation in research competitions, and application for and implementation of related research projects, etc.)".

Students who answered "Yes" to this item were uniformly coded as 1, indicating that they had at least one or more of the above-mentioned substantial research participation experiences during their undergraduate studies; students who answered "No" were coded as 0, indicating that they did not participate in any of the above-mentioned forms of research practice activities throughout their undergraduate studies. In the definition of variables in this study, marginal academic behaviors that involve no substantial research operations and practices, such as only attending academic lectures, literature reading courses, and scientific research popularization activities, are explicitly excluded. This is to ensure the content validity of the measurement of the research participation variable and to make sure that all research participation experiences included in the analysis are effective behaviors with actual research experience.

Academic motivation

Academic motivation refers to the intrinsic and extrinsic psychological drive that energizes and directs students' engagement in academic activities. It manifests specifically as a thirst for knowledge exploration, the pursuit of academic achievement, and persistence demonstrated when confronting academic challenges [27].

This construct was measured using students' self-reported attitudes and cognitive evaluations toward academic research. Data were collected through a validated Academic Motivation Scale employing a 5-point Likert format (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree). Higher composite scores indicate stronger levels of academic motivation. The items used in this study were selected through a literature review and expert deliberation to ensure their content validity.

The academic motivation scale in this study includes two dimensions: internal motivation and external motivation. Among them, internal motivation corresponds to research self-efficacy in the SCCT theory, and external motivation corresponds to research outcome expectations in the SCCT theory. These two dimensions together constitute the core of academic motivation, which is highly consistent with the constructs of the SCCT theory.

Control variables

This study is based on the core assumption of the propensity score matching method — the preprocessed covariates must be exogenous variables that are fixed before the treatment occurs and are not affected by research participation behavior [16]. Based on the setting of confounding factors in relevant studies in the existing field of public health education [18, 28], the control variables are divided into two categories: objective prior covariates and retrospective perceptual covariates, so as to comprehensively control the confounding factors that may simultaneously affect students' decision-making on scientific research participation and the development of professional competence.

Objective prior covariates: These variables are all inherent characteristics of students that were stably present before they participated in scientific research activities. They are completely unaffected by subsequent research participation behaviors, with an absolutely clear chronological order, and fundamentally meet the core requirements of the PSM preprocessing assumptions. Specifically, they include: gender (binary variable, 1 = male, 2 = female), student leadership role (binary variable, 0 = not holding a student leadership position, 1 = holding a student leadership position, referring to the position status before participating in scientific research), age (continuous variable, a fixed demographic characteristic), grade (categorical variable, with a fixed enrollment sequence, occurring before the research participation behavior), family location (categorical variable, a fixed family background characteristic), school attributes (categorical variable, inherent characteristics of the school determined at the time of enrollment), baseline academic performance before participating in scientific research (continuous variable, referring to academic performance before participating in scientific research, strictly occurring before the treatment variable), and family economic conditions (categorical variable, a fixed family background characteristic).

Retrospective perceptual covariates: These variables are retrospective measurements of students' perceptions of environmental characteristics before their participation in scientific research, including: academic platforms, teacher-student relationships, learning methods, academic guidance support, academic environment, training models, cognition of degree requirement policies, and teaching models — mainly measured based on students' own attitudes and perceptions towards these variables. These variables are assessed using a 5-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 1 to 5, corresponding to the lowest to the highest level of agreement or perception. Methodological studies have shown that single-item measurement is a reliable and effective choice for such constructs that are easy to be evaluated holistically in the mind [29]. The advantages lie in minimizing common method bias and respondent fatigue while ensuring the conceptual independence of each variable.

Common method bias

To assess the potential impact of common method variance (CMV), Harman's single-factor test was first conducted by including all self-reported Likert-type items in an unrotated factor analysis. The first factor accounted for 43.62% of the total variance, suggesting that CMV may exist to a certain extent. Therefore, the full collinearity variance inflation factor (VIF) was used for additional diagnosis. All VIF values were well below the recommended threshold of 3.3 (maximum VIF = 1.05, average VIF = 1.02), indicating that common method bias is unlikely to seriously threaten the validity of the results.

Methods

This study utilized STATA (version 18.0) for data description, analysis, and processing. The analysis of professional competence in this study is divided into two independent steps: the first step is to complete the psychometric validation of the Likert scale through exploratory factor analysis and confirmatory factor analysis, ensuring that the scale's dimensional structure, reliability, and validity meet psychometric standards; the second step is to use the entropy weight method to objectively assign weights to the multi-dimensional competence indicator system that has passed validity verification, and construct a comprehensive evaluation index of professional competence, rather than using the entropy weight method to measure potential psychological constructs. As an objective weighting method, it determines corresponding weights through original data, thereby avoiding biases introduced by subjective weighting [30] The applicability of the entropy weight method to ordered categorical Likert scale data has been confirmed by methodological research: the rating scores of the Likert scale have clear ordinal and discrete characteristics, and the entropy weight method can measure the information contribution of indicators by capturing the degree of dispersion of the scores, which is completely consistent with the calculation logic of information entropy for ordered categorical data. The information dispersion inherent in these rating differences can be directly captured by the entropy weight method, aligning with the entropy calculation principles for ordered data proposed by Tastle and Wierman [31]; the other hand, retaining the ordered nature of the Likert scale for entropy weight calculation enables more precise alignment with the relative importance hierarchy among indicators, rather than simplistically treating them as unordered categorical data. This approach can better reflect the relative importance of internal indicators and level differences across hierarchies within university students' job competency, ultimately enabling the evaluation of individual job competency.

To verify the robustness of the weighting results from the entropy weight method and eliminate the interference of the choice of weighting methods on the core conclusions, this study simultaneously used two internationally mainstream scale synthesis methods to recalculate the comprehensive scores of professional competence and conducted parallel analyses: ① Equal weight mean method: Calculate the simple arithmetic mean of the 52 competence items and standardize it to the 0–1 interval. This method does not presuppose indicator weights, treats all measurement items completely equally, and serves as a benchmark method for scale synthesis; ② Factor score method: Extract 1 common factor based on principal component analysis (with a cumulative variance explanation rate of 51.14%) and use the regression method to predict the factor scores. Subsequent core analyses were repeated based on the two alternative scores to test whether the conclusions depend on the weighting strategy of the entropy weight method.

As shown in Table 1, the ranking of entropy weights for the five dimensions in this study is as follows: public health practice ability (w = 0.248) > professional knowledge and skills (w = 0.225) > professional literacy (w = 0.217) > cognitive characteristics (w = 0.165) > personal traits (w = 0.145). The weight ranking is highly consistent with the talent training objectives of public health education. Public health practice ability, as the dimension with the highest weight, reflects the core training requirement of "emphasizing practice and strengthening application" in public health majors, and also verifies the practical rationality of the weighting results of the entropy weight method in this study. Professional knowledge and skills, as well as professional literacy, follow closely, reflecting that professional foundation and professional ethics are important supports for the competence of public health students. Personal traits and cognitive characteristics are basic dimensions with relatively low weights, which conforms to the actual composition logic of public health job competency.

Propensity Score Matching (PSM) analysis was conducted using Stata 18.0 software to simulate randomized experimental conditions as closely as possible, thereby minimizing the impact of self-selection bias on research conclusions. Propensity scores were estimated via a Logit model, with covariates including gender, student leadership roles, academic platforms, teacher-student relationships, learning methods, mentorship-based academic support, academic environment, training models, perceived degree requirements, and teaching modes.

This study systematically controlled for two types of preprocessing covariates—strictly pre-treatment objective covariates and retrospective perceptual covariates, comprehensively covering demographic characteristics, baseline social roles, academic background before participating in the study, and environmental cognition. This was done to exclude confounding factors that simultaneously affect students' decisions to participate in research and the development of their professional competence, thereby accurately assessing the true association between research participation and job competency. A retrospective design was employed in the survey, with all questionnaire items explicitly requiring respondents to answer based on their circumstances before joining the current research project. This design logically established the temporal sequence of variables—meaning the measured characteristics conceptually preceded research participation. Second, balance tests revealed no statistically significant differences between groups for any variables except “learning style,” which showed a statistically significant but extremely small effect size (p = 0.016, d = 0.16). Thus, these variables generally satisfy the conditions for pre-treatment confounding factors and can be used for correlation analysis.

To control for potential pre-existing differences between students who participated in research and those who did not, a robustness check was performed using Propensity Score Matching (PSM) to compare competency differences between the two groups. Students involved in research were designated as the treatment group, while those without research experience formed the control group. The propensity score for research participation was estimated using a logit model. Multiple PSM methods—including nearest neighbour matching, radius matching, and kernel matching—were employed to identify control group samples with characteristics closely resembling those of the treatment group. The Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT) was applied to evaluate the strength of the association between research participation and the competence of public health major students.

This study employed three matching methods—nearest neighbour matching (K = 4), radius matching (calliper = 0.01, K = 1), and kernel matching (bandwidth = 0.03, K = 1)—to construct comparable samples between the treatment and control groups. All matching processes were conducted without replacement. To satisfy the shared support hypothesis, observations with propensity scores outside the shared support interval were excluded. The final matching method was selected based on the degree of improvement in covariate balance post-matching, primarily assessed through the standardized mean difference (SMD) of covariates and the magnitude.

Table 3 reports the standardized mean differences (SMDs) of covariates and their changes under different propensity score matching methods. Before matching, the average SMD of covariates in the sample was 6.2%, indicating a certain degree of imbalance between the treatment and control groups on some covariates. After applying PSM, all matching methods improved covariate balance to varying degrees. Among them, radius matching and kernel matching significantly reduced the absolute SMD for all covariates, both controlling it below 2%, demonstrating excellent balancing effects. In contrast, nearest neighbour matching showed relatively limited improvement in balance for individual covariates.

Common support distribution of propensity scores

The post-matching treatment effect is expressed as the average treatment effect (ATT). Considering the propensity score as the first-stage estimator, this paper employs Abadie–Imbens robust standard errors to estimate the uncertainty of ATT [32]. This method simultaneously accounts for propensity score estimation error and matching incompleteness in matching estimates, making it applicable to the nearest-neighbour matching, radius matching, and kernel matching methods used herein. The effects of study participation on job competency are significantly positive at the 1% significance level. Estimation results across different matching methods exhibit high consistency in magnitude and significance levels, indicating robust conclusions regarding the choice of matching algorithm. The PSM results demonstrate that promoting research participation is an effective pathway for enhancing job competency, and this conclusion remains robust to potential sample selection bias.

Sensitivity analysis of matching methods

To further test the sensitivity of the research conclusions to the choice of matching algorithms, this study added two internationally mainstream matching methods—Mahalanobis distance matching and nearest neighbor matching within a caliper—to the original nearest neighbor matching, radius matching, and kernel matching. It then re-estimated the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) of scientific research participation on professional competence. Mahalanobis distance matching performs 1:1 matching directly based on the Mahalanobis distance of covariates and does not rely on the propensity score model; nearest neighbor matching within a caliper, on the other hand, conducts 1:1 matching with a caliper limit of 0.01 based on the propensity score.

The results are shown in Table 5: the ATT values estimated by the five matching methods range from 0.0372 to 0.0445, and all are significant at the 1% statistical level. This result indicates that regardless of the matching algorithm used, the positive promoting effect of research participation on the professional competence of public health students remains stable. The core conclusion does not change with the choice of matching methods, showing a high degree of robustness.

Robustness test based on alternative competence scores

Robustness test on the impact of scientific research participation on professional competence

Based on two alternative competency scores, the same propensity score matching strategy as the main analysis was adopted (with radius matching as the core method, and nearest neighbor matching and kernel matching as robustness methods) to re-estimate the average treatment effect (ATT) of research participation. The results show that regardless of the competency synthesis method used, the positive promotion effect of research participation on professional competency is highly significant, which is highly consistent with the results of the main analysis.

Under the equal-weighted average method, the ATT value for radius matching is 0.0379 (T = 3.86, p < 0.001), while the ATT values for nearest neighbor matching and kernel matching are 0.0399 (p < 0.001) and 0.0362 (p < 0.001) respectively. These values are completely consistent with the ATT value (0.0391) from the main analysis using the entropy weight method in terms of effect magnitude, direction, and significance. Under the factor score method, the ATT value for radius matching is 0.2631 (T = 3.84, p < 0.001), and the ATT values for the other two matching methods range from 0.251 to 0.276, all significant at the 1% level. The standardized mean difference (SMD) for all matching methods is controlled within 10%, meeting the covariate balance requirement and indicating good matching quality. The complete estimation results for different methods are detailed in Table 2 of the supplementary materials.

robustness test of the mediating effect of academic motivation

Based on the Bootstrap method (100 repeated samplings, 95% confidence interval), a repeated mediating effect analysis was conducted on the scores of the two alternative competencies, and the results were completely consistent with the conclusions of the main analysis.

Under the equal-weighted mean method, research participation not only had a significant direct effect on professional competence (β = 0.0328, p = 0.002), but also had a significant indirect effect by enhancing academic motivation (β = 0.0049, p = 0.003). The mediating effect accounted for 13.0% of the total effect, which was almost completely consistent with 12.6% in the main analysis. Under the factor score method, both the direct effect (β = 0.2271, p = 0.002) and the indirect effect (β = 0.0348, p = 0.003) were significant, with the mediating effect accounting for 13.3%. Under both methods, the 95% confidence intervals of the indirect effects did not include 0, confirming that the mediating effect of academic motivation exists stably and does not depend on the weighting method of competency scores. The complete estimation results of the mediating effect are shown in Table 3 of the supplementary materials.

In conclusion, the core conclusion of this study — that research participation significantly and positively predicts the professional competence of public health students, and academic motivation plays a partial mediating role in this process — does not depend on the weighting strategy of the entropy weight method and has good methodological robustness.

Discussion

Within the public health education system, the cultivation of students' job competency has become an important indicator for measuring educational quality and career readiness. As public health issues grow increasingly complex, evidence suggests that a primary reliance on traditional curricular instruction may be insufficient to fully develop the practical skills and comprehensive literacy required in the field [30, 33]. Research participation is increasingly integrated into public health training systems as a form of educational practice, and its relationship with job competency has consequently garnered scholarly interest [34]. Given that academic motivation is closely related to research engagement, it may also function as a significant mediator of the relationship between such participation and job competency. Therefore, investigating the relationships among research participation, academic motivation, and job competency holds substantial theoretical and practical significance for optimizing public health talent cultivation models and enhancing students' professional competitiveness.

Based on the results of multi-dimensional subgroup analysis of research participation and combined with the research conclusions, targeted practical suggestions are put forward from three parties: colleges and universities, public health practice institutions, and education management departments:

Colleges and universities: Establish a hierarchical research participation training system. Carry out research enlightenment for lower-grade students, implement one-on-one tutor guidance for higher-grade students, encourage students to engage in in-depth research participation for more than 6 months (such as research design, thesis writing), and incorporate research participation into academic evaluation to strengthen students' academic motivation. Establish a research incentive mechanism to stimulate students' intrinsic academic motivation through academic forums, research competitions, and achievement rewards.

Public health practice institutions: Take high-quality research participation experience at the undergraduate stage as a signal to identify learning agility in the recruitment of fresh graduates, and give priority to selecting students with in-depth research participation; carry out progressive research ability training for new employees to promote the integration of research thinking and practical work.

Education management departments: Increase investment in public health research resources, build university-local research cooperation platforms, and break down selective barriers in the distribution of research resources; build university-local collaborative research practice platforms, promote cooperation between colleges and universities and centers for disease control and prevention as well as primary health institutions, so that students' research participation is in line with actual public health work scenarios; promote colleges and universities to establish a "research + teaching + practice" talent training model, and integrate research ability training throughout the entire cycle of undergraduate education.

The results of this study indicate that participation in scientific research activities is positively correlated with the workplace competence of public health students, which is consistent with the view in existing research that emphasizes the correlation between scientific research training and the improvement of students' comprehensive abilities [35, 36]. Research engagement is associated with students’ ability to apply professional knowledge as well as higher levels of multidimensional competencies such as critical thinking, problem-solving, and teamwork—all of which constitute core components of job competency [4, 5]. Furthermore, our analysis identified a significant mediating effect of academic motivation. This suggests that the association between research participation and job competency may vary according to students’ levels of academic interest and intrinsic motivation. Students with strong academic motivation may be more inclined to actively and deeply participate in research projects, and this way of participation may, in turn, be positively correlated with the transformation of research experience into practical work abilities.

Meanwhile, the robustness test conducted in this study on the covariate preprocessing assumptions further verifies the reliability of the conclusions: when only using strictly pre-treatment objective covariates with clear chronological order for PSM analysis, the positive promoting effect of scientific research participation on professional competence remains significant, which is highly consistent with the results of the main model. This rules out the interference of the temporal ambiguity of covariates and selection bias on the core conclusions, further enhancing the rigor of the correlation analysis.

Further mediation analysis revealed that academic motivation plays a partial mediating role between research participation and job competency. This indicates that research participation influences competency through both direct and indirect pathways, with the latter operating by stimulating students' academic motivation. This finding underscores the importance of moving beyond merely encouraging research participation to also intentionally fostering students' academic interest and intrinsic drive. Establishing incentive mechanisms [37], providing personalized guidance and mentorship [38], and cultivating a supportive research environment represent strategies to strengthen this key mediator, academic motivation. By directly fostering it, these approaches can enhance student engagement and fulfillment, which in turn amplifies the translation of research experience into job competency.

The important innovation of this study lies in combining the experiential mastery experience of the SCCT theory with the local context of public health education in China, and for the first time verifying the association between tutor-led research participation and students' job competency. Different from the "Undergraduate Research Experience (URE)" in Western countries that emphasizes students' autonomy, research participation in China's public health majors is characterized by tutor leadership. Students gain structured experiential mastery experience by participating in tutors' research projects. This localized model is also positively correlated with the enhancement of students' self-efficacy and academic motivation, providing empirical evidence for the cross-cultural application of the SCCT theory and supplementing local data for public health education research in non-Western contexts.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, since cross-sectional studies collect data only at a single point in time, they cannot track the temporal evolution of variables. Consequently, they cannot rigorously infer the causal relationship or directionality between research engagement, academic motivation, and job competence. At the same time, retrospective self-assessment may be subject to recall bias. While propensity score matching (PSM) was employed to enhance comparability between groups, it can only adjust for observed confounders; unmeasured confounding factors may still influence the results. This study further reduces the estimation bias caused by the ambiguity in the temporal order of covariates and enhances the rigor of correlation analysis through a robust Propensity Score Matching (PSM) analysis that only includes strict pre-treatment objective covariates. However, it still cannot completely address the endogeneity limitations of the cross-sectional design. Therefore, this study can only confirm the statistical associations among these three variables and cannot draw causal conclusions such as "research participation directly improves job competency" or "academic motivation is a causal antecedent of job competency." Future studies should adopt a longitudinal follow-up design to verify potential causal pathways.

Second, the reliance on self-reported questionnaires for key variables (e.g., research participation, academic motivation) may introduce social desirability and recall bias. Participants might overestimate or underestimate their experiences and competencies. Affected by social expectation bias, participants may overestimate their own job competency and academic motivation levels to meet society's expectations for the abilities of public health students. Meanwhile, the Dunning-Kruger effect may cause participants with lower ability levels to have an overconfident assessment of their own competence, while those with higher ability levels tend to underestimate themselves. Both may lead to deviations between the results of variable measurement and the actual situation. Furthermore, the use of a self-constructed competency index system, despite the application of the entropy weight method, requires further validation for its construct validity and reliability across different populations.

Third, the generalizability of our findings is constrained by the sampling frame. The participants were exclusively public health students from three universities in a single Chinese province (Shandong). Nevertheless, the sampling was based on the typicality of Shandong’s public health education system that is highly consistent with the national mainstream model, and the stratified sampling across eastern, central and western Shandong ensured the representativeness of the sample for public medical universities in China. Thus, the conclusions of this study are applicable to the undergraduate public health education scenario of public medical universities in China. However, they cannot be directly extrapolated to non-medical universities offering public health majors, private medical institutions, or public health education systems in other provinces with distinct educational resource allocation in China. Moreover, due to the differences in educational models, cultural backgrounds and research participation mechanisms between China and other countries, the findings are not directly generalizable to the international academic community and foreign public health education systems.

Finally, although we identified academic motivation as a mediator, the underlying mechanisms are likely more complex. Other potentially significant variables, such as the quality of mentorship, the specific nature of the research projects, or students' prior academic achievement, were not included in our model. In the future, there is a need to adopt longitudinal or intervention studies with more diverse samples (e.g., multi-provincial, multi-type institutions in China) and combined subjective and objective measurement indicators.

Long-term follow-up should be conducted on public health students after their graduation. Once they enter the workforce, objective data such as employer evaluations and practical assessments should be cross-validated with self-reported data to verify causal relationships and further clarify the complex mechanisms involved. Additionally, cross-cultural comparative studies with overseas universities should be carried out to further verify the cross-context generalizability of the conclusions. Due to the limitations of the cross-sectional retrospective questionnaire design and data collection in this study, further fine-grained analysis of the heterogeneous characteristics of research participation, such as duration, depth, and type, has not yet been carried out. The exploration of related differential effects will be the core direction of subsequent research.
