Spectral Theory of Fractional Cooperative Systems and Threshold Dynamics in Epidemic Models
Cong-Bang Trang, Hoang-Hung Vo

Abstract
. The spectral analysis has long been recognized as a fundamental tool in the study of existence, uniqueness and qualitative behavior of solutions to semilinear elliptic and parabolic equations, as well as their long-time dynamics. In the contemporary mathematics, fractional Laplacian are used to model nonlocal or long-range diffusion processes in biology, such as anomalous movement, long-distance dispersal, or Lévy-flight migration of organisms, cells, epidemic. In this paper, we adopt the definition of the spectral fractional Laplacian introduced by Caffarelli and Stinga [ 15 ] to develop the eigentheory of a cooperative system modeling an infectious epidemic process and to analyze its long-term behavior. Building on Fredholm theory and the technique in Lam and Lou [ 43 ] , we establish a sharp criterion guaranteeing the existence and simplicity of the principal eigenvalue λ p \lambda_{p} , together with its variational characterizations and implications for the validity of maximum principles. In addition, the asymptotic behavior of λ p \lambda_{p} with respect to the diffusion coefficients, fractional orders, and domain scaling is derived, thereby complementing the results of Zhao and Ruan [ 75 ] and Feng, Li, Ruan, and Xin [ 31 ] . As an application of this spectral analysis, we further establish the existence, uniqueness, and threshold long-time dynamics of solutions for an endemic reaction–diffusion system with fractional diffusion, in contrast to the approach of Hsu and Yang [ 36 ] . This work contributes to the modern trend of combining spectral and nonlocal analysis in applied mathematics, besides recent advances in [ 7 , 11 , 54 , 75 , 31 , 32 , 52 ] .

For decades, the spectral theory of elliptic systems is a central theme in partial differential equations, as the principal eigenvalue determines positivity properties, maximum principles, bifurcation thresholds and the dynamics of nonlinear parabolic models. In the classical

Their framework established existence, simplicity and asymptotic behavior of the generalized principal eigenvalue, with important applications to biological dynamics. For cooperative systems, the Krein–Rutman theorem ensures the existence of a positive principal eigenfunction and bifurcation theory has been applied to describe steady states
[

In an elegant work, Lam and Lou

analyzed the existence and asymptotic behavior of the principal eigenvalue of general linear cooperative elliptic systems with small diffusion rates.

Motivated by the connection between spectral theory and population dynamics, significant attention has been devoted to epidemic models, where eigenvalue-based thresholds determine whether a disease persists or dies out. Such models have a long history, particularly in the study of man–environment–man diseases. A classical example is the work of Capasso and Paveri-Fontana
[
16
]
, who introduced a model for the cholera epidemic that struck the Mediterranean regions in 1973. The model is given by

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}u_{t}=-au+\alpha v,\\
v_{t}=-bv+G(u),\end{array}\right.t>0

Here,
u,\penalty\ v

-au,\penalty\ -bv
are the nature diminishing rate of the bacterial population and the infectious population, respectively and
\alpha v

on the human population under the assumption that the number of susceptibles remains constant during the epidemic. As noted in
[
14

u

G

[
17

18

introduced partially degenerate reaction–diffusion models for fecal–oral transmission in Mediterranean regions. These frameworks have attracted sustained attention in the theory of reaction–diffusion equations, with contributions in both the pure and applied mathematics. For example, to investigate the endemic model via cooperative system, Hsu and Yang
[
36

\displaystyle\textbf{v}=((-\textbf{d}\Delta_{N})^{\textbf{s}}+\beta I)^{-1}[\beta I+\textbf{B}+\mathcal{G}]\textbf{v}=((-\textbf{d}\Delta_{N})^{\textbf{s}}+\beta I)^{-1}{\bf f};\penalty\ {\bf f}\succeq{\bf 0}.

It then follows from
[
76
, Theorem 3.1, Step 3, Case 1]
that
\textbf{v}>{\bf 0}
. Similarly,
\textbf{w}>{\bf 0}
. By applying Theorem
19
, one has
\mathbf{v}\leq\mathbf{w}
. Similarly,
\mathbf{w}\leq\mathbf{v}
and, hence,
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{w}
.

(ii)
. Since
\mathcal{R}_{0}\leq 1
, one has
\lambda_{p}\geq 0
. For any
\epsilon>0
, it is known that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lllll}(-d_{1}\Delta_{N})^{s_{1}}\epsilon\varphi_{1,1}=\lambda_{p}\epsilon\varphi_{1,1}-a(x)\epsilon\varphi_{1,1}+H^{\prime}(0)\epsilon\varphi_{1,2}\geq-a(x)\epsilon\varphi_{1,1}+H(\epsilon\varphi_{1,2}),\\
(-d_{2}\Delta_{N})^{s_{2}}\epsilon\varphi_{1,2}=\lambda_{p}\epsilon\varphi_{1,2}-b(x)\epsilon\varphi_{1,2}+G^{\prime}(0)\epsilon\varphi_{1,1}\geq-b(x)\epsilon\varphi_{1,2}+G(\epsilon\varphi_{1,1}),\end{array}\right.\end{split}

which implies that
\epsilon\varphi_{1}>0
is a positive super-solution of (
1.3
).

We prove by contradiction. Assume that there exists a non-trivial
\textbf{u}\geq\textbf{0}
is a solution of (
1.3
). Then, it follows from Theorem
19
that

\displaystyle 0\leq\textbf{u}\leq\epsilon\varphi_{1},\penalty\ \forall\epsilon>0.

Letting
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, we obtain that
\textbf{u}\equiv 0
, which is a contradiction.

This concludes the proof.

∎

5.2.
Stability of the steady state

In this section, we investigate the stability of the steady state.

Proof of second part of Theorem
5
.

Let us investigate the two separate cases.

(i)
. The proof is divided by two steps.

Step 1:
Assume that
t\mapsto\textbf{u}(t;\textbf{u}_{0})
is monotone. Define
\textbf{w}=(w_{1},w_{2}):=\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}\textbf{u}(t;\textbf{u}_{0})
point-wise limit. Suppose further that
w
is non-trivial. We will show that
\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}\textbf{u}(t;\textbf{u}_{0})=\textbf{u}_{1}
uniformly on
\overline{\Omega}
.

Since
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{X}
, it follows from (
4.2
) that

\displaystyle\|\textbf{u}(t;\textbf{u}(s,\textbf{u}_{0}))-\textbf{u}(t;\textbf{w})\|_{\mathcal{X}}\leq C_{0}\|\textbf{u}(s,\textbf{u}_{0})-\textbf{w}\|_{\mathcal{X}}e^{C_{0}Kt}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }s\rightarrow\infty,\penalty\ \forall t>0.

Here, we use the Lebesgue dominant theorem to prove the last convergence. As the results, one can check that

\displaystyle\textbf{w}=\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\textbf{u}(t+s;\textbf{u}_{0})=\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\textbf{u}(t;\textbf{u}(s,\textbf{u}_{0}))=\textbf{u}(t;\textbf{w})\text{ on }\mathcal{X},\penalty\ \forall t>0.

This shows that
w
is a solution of (
1.3
), which further implies that
\textbf{w}=\textbf{u}_{1}
. By applying Dini’s theorem, we get the desired results.

Step 2:
Prove the main result.

Thanks to the first part Theorem
5
, we know that

\displaystyle\textbf{0}<\textbf{u}(t;\textbf{u}_{0})\leq(M_{1},M_{2})\text{ on }\overline{\Omega},\penalty\ \forall t>0,

where
(M_{1},M_{2})
is a super-solution of (
1.2
). For
T_{0}>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle(\delta,\delta)<\textbf{u}(T_{0};\textbf{u}_{0})\text{ on }\overline{\Omega}.

Let
\varphi_{1}\in\mathcal{C}
be the positive eigenfunction associated with
\lambda_{p}
. Since
\lambda_{p}<0
, we can choose
\epsilon>0
small enough so that

\displaystyle\epsilon\varphi_{1}\text{ is a sub-solution and }\epsilon\varphi_{1}<\textbf{u}(T_{0};\textbf{u}_{0}).

By the comparison principle in Section
4
and the uniqueness, one has

\displaystyle t\mapsto\textbf{u}(t;\epsilon\varphi_{1})\text{ is non-decreasing},

\displaystyle t\mapsto\textbf{u}(t;(M_{1},M_{2}))\text{ is non-increasing},

\displaystyle\textbf{u}(t;\epsilon\varphi_{1})\leq\textbf{u}(t+T_{0};\textbf{u}_{0})\leq\textbf{u}(t;(M_{1},M_{2})),\penalty\ \forall t>0.

Passing to limit
t\rightarrow\infty
and applying
Step 1
, we obtain the desired result.

(ii)
. Choose
C>0
large enough so that

\displaystyle\textbf{u}_{0}\leq C\varphi_{1}.

Define
\overline{\bf v}(t,x)=(v_{1},v_{2})=Ce^{-\lambda_{p}t}\varphi_{1}(x)
for
t>0
and
x\in\overline{\Omega}
. From (
5.1
), one can check that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lllll}(v_{1})_{t}+(-d_{1}\Delta_{N})^{s_{1}}v_{1}=-a(x)v_{1}+H^{\prime}(0)v_{2},\\
(v_{2})_{t}+(-d_{2}\Delta_{N})^{s_{2}}v_{2}=-b(x)v_{2}+G^{\prime}(0)v_{1}.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

Because
H
and
G
are concave,
\overline{\mathbf{v}}
serves as a super-solution of (
1.3
). Furthermore, as
\overline{\mathbf{v}}(0,x)\geq\mathbf{u}_{0}(x)
for every
x\in\overline{\Omega}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle 0\leq\textbf{u}(t;\textbf{u}_{0})\leq Ce^{-\lambda_{p}t}\varphi_{1},\penalty\ \forall t>0.

Since
\lambda_{p}>0
, the desired result follows.

∎
