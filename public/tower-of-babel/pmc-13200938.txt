Baseline immune status associates respiratory viral infection in preterm infants: A prospective cohort study
Authors: José Antonio Cañas, José Manuel Rodrigo-Muñoz, Laura Sánchez-García, Maite Beato Merino, Patricia Alonso-López, Leticia Labanda, Marta Gil-Martínez, Zahara García de Castro, Daniel Rodríguez-González, Sonia Alcolea, Adelina Pellicer, Inmaculada Casas, María Luz García-García, Cristina Calvo, Victoria Del Pozo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Preterm infants (PI) are particularly vulnerable to respiratory viral infections (RVI), which may affect their lung development and immune responses, increasing the risk of recurrent wheezing (RW) and asthma. This study aimed to evaluate the cytokine immune profile in PI admitted to neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) and to explore its association with RVI and subsequent respiratory outcomes during the first year of life. A prospective cohort of 118 PI (<32 weeks gestational age) was studied in 2 NICUs. Nasopharyngeal aspirates were collected at birth and discharge for virological and immunological analyses. Infants were classified into 4 groups based on RVI and RW presence. Cytokine levels were measured using Luminex and ELISA. Infants with RVI and/or RW showed higher rates of BPD and respiratory readmissions. Distinct cytokine patterns were observed: V-/W+ infants showed elevated baseline TNF-α and reduced IL-13 and TGF-β over time. V+/W- infants had increasing TNF-α levels during hospitalization. Notably, immune profiles at birth differed even in the absence of RVI, suggesting innate predispositions. Correlation analyses revealed significant associations between cytokines and clinical factors like birth weight and oxygen need. Preterm infants without RVI who developed RW appeared to exhibit a distinct pro-inflammatory cytokine profile early in life. This cytokine pattern may be associated with respiratory outcomes later in infancy, although predictive value cannot be inferred from this study and requires confirmation in larger, more representative cohorts.

1. Introduction

Preterm infants (PI), born before 37 weeks gestational age, can be classified into extremely preterm (< 28 weeks), very preterm (28–31+6 weeks), or moderate-late preterm (32–36+6 weeks),[1] with approximately 84% of preterm births in the moderate-late stage. Preterm birth is an important issue in infants, because it is associated to higher risk of bacterial and viral infections, including lower respiratory tract infections. Additionally, preterm birth is associated with alterations in the development of lung structure and systemic immunity, increasing the risk of respiratory diseases in early childhood such as wheezing and asthma.[2] These PI are highly vulnerable to respiratory viral infections (RVI), including respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), human adenovirus, human metapneumovirus, human bocavirus, human rhinovirus or human coronavirus, which are not uncommon in Neonatal Intensive Care Units (NICU), although they are frequently underdiagnosed due to their clinical non-specificity.[3–6] Previous studies of our group demonstrated that PI infected by RSV showed a different respiratory epithelium immune profile, observing a dysregulation of filaggrin.[7] However, there is very little data on the immune response in this vulnerable population to viral respiratory infections.

Therefore, we aimed to analyze the immune response profile of the PI admitted to NICU at birth, comparing those with/without RVI, and its association with the infant’s respiratory outcomes considering their vulnerability to suffer acute RVI during their stay in NICU and the development of recurrent wheezing (RW) within the first year of life.

2. Methods

2.1. Study population

To conduct this study, 118 PI (< 32 weeks of gestation) were prospectively enrolled at 2 NICUs (the Severo Ochoa University Hospital, Leganés, Madrid and La Paz University Hospital, Madrid). Selection bias was reduced by prospectively enrolling all eligible preterm infants < 32 weeks of with uniform inclusion and exclusion criteria. The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Severo Ochoa University Hospital (Leganés, Madrid) and La Paz University Hospital (Madrid) (PI 2255). Parents of infants accepted and signed the informed consent before the inclusion in the study.

The following clinical parameters were collected from the medical charts: sex, birth weight, gestational age, length of stay in NICU, supplementary oxygen during admission, bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) (defined as treatment with oxygen > 21% for at least 28 days and categorized the severity at 36 weeks of postmenstrual age or discharge to home),[8] mechanical ventilation, including type and duration (invasive, noninvasive, and continuous positive airway pressure [CPAP]), familiar history of asthma and/or atopy, daycare attendance, exposure to tobacco smoke and development of wheezing episodes during the first year of age diagnosed by a pediatrician. Data on their follow-up was collected through a telephone questionnaire administered to parents and through a review of their medical records.

2.2. Sample collection and viral detection

Nasopharyngeal aspirates (NPA) for immunological assays were collected by nasal wash within the first 3 days after birth and just before discharge.

Furthermore, NPAs for virological studies were taken weekly during NICU admission and additionally if infants presented symptoms of possible viral infection (apnea, rhinorrhea, fever and increased need for respiratory assistance). Samples were analyzed at the Influenza and Respiratory Viruses Laboratory at the National Centre for Microbiology (Madrid, Spain) where 4 independent real time multiplex PCR (RT-PCR) were performed to detect a total of 18 respiratory viruses.[9] However, after discharge from the neonatology unit, samples were not collected for viral identification in the wheezing episodes because the children were followed by their pediatricians on an outpatient basis.

PI were grouped according to RVI status during hospitalization period and/or presence of wheezing on evolution: i) Virus+/Wheezing+ (V+/W+) (n = 16), ii) Virus+/Wheezing- (V+/W-) (n = 27), iii) Virus-/Wheezing+ (V-/W+) (n = 38), and iv) Virus-/Wheezing- (V-/W-) (n = 37). All these groups were analyzed in clinical data, but no cytokine studies were performed in the V-/W- group, due to limited sample availability.

2.3. Evaluation of cytokines in nasopharyngeal aspirates

To determine cytokine concentration in NPA supernatant, we used a commercial Luminex panel according to manufacturer’s instruction (Magpix, Merck Millipore, Massachusetts, USA) for the determination of interferon‐gamma (IFN-γ), IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-13, IL-17A, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, TNF-α. The levels of TGFβ1 and IL-33 were determined by conventional ELISA (R&D Systems, Abingdon, UK). The manufacturer-reported lower limit of detection values, minimum detectable concentrations, intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation, as well as the proportion of samples with values below the low limit detection for each cytokine, are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. For further analyses, cytokine values below the lower limit of detection were imputed as the corresponding lower limit of detection for each cytokine evaluated.

2.4. Statistical analysis

Descriptive data were expressed as mean and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for continuous variables, and percentages for categorical variables. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Normal continuous unpaired variables were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis for nonparametric data or one-way ANOVA for parametric data. For correction of multiple test comparisons, we used Dunn’s or Bonferroni post hoc tests. For comparisons within the same group at different time points (admission and discharge), the Wilcoxon matched-pairs test was used for nonparametric data, and the paired t-test for parametric data. The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of data distributions. Correlation analyses were performed using Spearman’s correlation tests. The strength of the correlations was interpreted according to standard criteria: weak (|r| < 0.3), moderate (|r| = 0.3–0.7), and strong (|r| > 0.7). Effect sizes were calculated using r de Rosenthal to provide estimates of the precision and magnitude of observed effects. Analyses were carried out using Graph-Pad Prism 8 (San Diego, CA, USA). P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population

Regarding the clinical and demographic (Table 1), groups were comparable in almost all clinical data, except for family history of asthma/atopy and readmission due to acute respiratory episodes, which were significantly higher in infants who on evolution presented wheezing episodes or viral infection. Moreover, V+/W+ and V-/W+ infants were more likely to be readmitted for respiratory episodes during follow-up. Moreover, the V+/W+ group exhibited higher rates of BPD compared to the other groups, although the differences were not statistically significant (Table 1). Trends pointed towards longer length of stay in NICU and higher rates of supplemental oxygen needs in infants with RVI compared to those infants without respiratory virus detection, although no significant differences were found. The perinatal history was very similar among the 4 groups described; 88/118 (74%) of the infants were delivered by cesarean section, 111/118 (94%) received antenatal corticosteroids, and 59/118 (50%) of the mothers received antibiotics. The causes of premature birth were very varied and distributed similarly across groups, with spontaneous labor and premature rupture of membranes being the most common cause (two-thirds of cases), followed by preeclampsia (20/18; 17%), with chorioamnionitis (9/118) in 7%. A miscellany of causes including intrauterine growth restriction, or loss of fetal well-being was also present.

3.2. Cytokine profiles in NPA supernatants differ among preterm infants depending on the presence or absence of RVI and/or RW

Regarding the of cytokine profile evolution during observational period, and focusing on V+/W+, V+/W- and V-/W+ groups to dilucidated the effect of RVI in the development of RW (Fig. 1 and Fig. S1), we observed a clear, although not statistically significant, trend toward increased in TNF-α throughout the whole hospitalization period in V+/W- infants (10.33 (4.11–16.56) vs 16.26 (5.35–7.17) pg/mL, Fig. 1A). Additionally, a trend towards decreased IL-13 (7.06 (4.81–9.31) vs 4.91 (2.79–7.04) pg/mL, P = .076, Fig. 1F) and TGFβ-1 (26.68 (16.89–36.46) vs 17.63 (15.11–20.14) pg/mL, P = .084, Fig. S1C) levels in the V-/W+ patients was observed. For the remaining cytokines that were evaluated no relevant changes were found during the observational period (Fig. 1B, C, D, E, and Fig. S1A, B, D and E).

Regarding to comparisons between groups, significant lower levels of TNF-α (7.70 (1.97–13.43) vs 22.72 (13.78–31.65) pg/mL, P-adjusted < .05, Fig. 1A) were observed in V+/W+ infants in comparison to V-/W+ at discharge, and a decrease of IL-5 were observed in V+/W- infants in comparison to V+/W+(1.18 (0.64–1.73) vs 1.73 (−0.55–4.02) pg/mL, P-adjusted < .05, Fig. 1E) and V-/W+(1.18 (0.64–1.73) vs 1.95 (0.57–3.33) pg/mL, P-adjusted < .05, Fig. 1E) at discharge. However, V+/W- group showed significant higher levels of IL-13 at discharge in comparison to V+/W+ infants (2.38 (0.94–3.83) vs 1.74 (−1.32–4.81) pg/mL, P-adjusted < .05, Fig. 1F). Additionally, at admission, V+/W+ preterm infants showed significant lower levels of IL-13 (5.21 (−3.71–14.12) vs 7.06 (4.81–9.31) pg/mL, P-adjusted < 0.05, Fig. 1F) but higher levels of IFN-γ (5.20 (0.16–10.24) vs 2.72 (1.83–3.61) pg/mL, P-adjusted = 0.082, Fig. S1A) in comparison to V-/W+ group. This fact suggests that these infants that developing wheezing, have a different immunity profile even in the absence of RVI, making them highly likely to develop asthma at later stages.

Across all groups, the magnitude of change in cytokine levels between admission and discharge was small, with effect sizes (Rosenthal’s r) generally below 0.25, indicating minimal within-group variation over time. Between-group comparisons at each time point showed small to moderate effect sizes (in all cases below 0.40), suggesting only modest differences in cytokine profiles among the study groups. These observations indicate that, although some cytokine patterns varied across groups, the overall magnitude of these differences was limited.

Although Luminex assays were performed on samples from the V–/W- group, most cytokine concentrations were below the lower limit of detection, providing very limited measurable data for each analyte (Supplementary Table 1). To avoid inaccurate or biased interpretations, cytokine analyses were therefore restricted to the remaining study groups.

4. Discussion

This study conducted in a population of PI admitted to the NICU reveals a distinct immune profile pattern in the NPA of neonates who suffer RVI and later develop or not RW. In addition, those who do not have a viral infection, but develop wheezing, also show a different cytokine profile at baseline and on evolution compared to wheezing infants who suffered RVI during their admission. Previous studies have reported a different profile of epithelial-derived cytokines in PI with RVI.[7]

Interestingly, in this study we observed higher levels of TNF-α (a central mediator of the inflammatory response) in V-/W+ infants at baseline, that has been associated with childhood wheezing.[10] It is well known that higher levels of TNF-α are associated with increased inflammation and poorer lung function,[11] and plays a central role in the early inflammatory cascade in the immature lung. It is produced by multiple cell types, including alveolar macrophages, fibroblasts, type II pneumocytes, and endothelial cells, in response to hypoxic or hyperoxic stress and microbial components.[12] Elevated TNF-α levels have been detected in airway secretions and bronchoalveolar samples from infants developing BPD, supporting its contribution to inflammation-driven lung injury and fibrotic remodeling.[13] Specifically, early increases in TNF-α in preterm infants have been linked to the development of BPD.[14] A negative correlation was observed between TNF-α levels and the birth weight in V+/W+ and V-/W+ groups, and a positive correlation between TNF-α levels and oxygen supplementation in V-/W+. Considering that the more premature newborns are at the greatest risk to develop pulmonary inflammatory diseases,[15] these findings could indicate increased susceptibility to RVI and lung dysfunction among infants with the lowest birth weight. Moreover, it has been described by our own group and by others that viral infection induced Th2 and Th17 cytokines and it is associated with a history of BPD and higher rates of respiratory morbidity during the first 2 years of life, as a result of recurrent respiratory episodes.[16,17] However, in this study we have not found differences between groups in terms of BPD or type of ventilation (considered as confounding factors). However, our sample size may represent a limitation of our study, and the results should therefore be interpreted with caution. Despite this, our findings are consistent with recent studies that have advanced the understanding of immune and inflammatory mechanisms underlying lung injury and the development of BPD in preterm infants. Pereira-Fantini et al demonstrated that proteomic analysis could identify distinct acute injury responses to different respiratory strategies at birth.[18] Zhang et al reported that BPD may be influenced by an imbalance between the microbiome and cytokine expression, particularly by increased TNF-α levels.[19] More recently, Moreira et al identified a transcriptomic signature in preterm neonates that predicted BPD severity as early as day 5 of life and stratified disease progression, providing a potential early biomarker related to T2-eosinophilic inflammation that could share pathophysiology between BPD and asthma.[20] Collectively, these studies support the concept that early immune dysregulation contributes to both structural lung injury and long-term respiratory morbidity in preterm infants. Nevertheless, our findings should be interpreted cautiously, and validation in larger, prospective cohorts is required to confirm these associations and clarify the causal pathways involved.

Our previous studies, conducted in hospitalized young infants during their first wheezing episode, provide further support to the role of severe RVI in T2 airway inflammation, with elevated nasal levels of TSLP, IFN-γ and IL‐10.[21] We have also shown that premature infants with symptomatic infections during their NICU stay have higher long-term morbidity and development of RW.[16] Similarly, Pérez et al, demonstrate that severely premature children have increased airway secretion of Th2 and Th17 cytokines during RV infections, which is associated with more respiratory morbidity in the first 2 years of life.[17]

However, this study demonstrates, going further, that the immune response of PIs could be an early marker of susceptibility to developing RW, regardless of the presence or absence of viral infections. These infections could possibly be an additional risk factor and not just a trigger.

Although the study shows promising results, we are aware of its limitations, particularly the small sample size and power limitations, which likely prevented it from yielding significant differences in some relevant comparisons, and some confounding factors, such as preexisting maternal conditions, were not considered. Moreover, effect size power assessment indicated that the study was powered to identify medium-large effects, and smaller changes may have remained undetected. In this context, although statistically significant differences were observed for some cytokines, effect sizes were small, and clinical relevance should be interpreted with caution. Therefore, careful interpretation of these results is advised and they should be considered as exploratory, so validation in larger cohorts is therefore necessary. Another limitation of this study is that no virological analyses were performed during outpatient wheezing episodes after NICU discharge. As a result, we could not determine whether these events were associated with new viral infections or reflected persistent immune dysregulation. Therefore, future prospective studies could include longitudinal viral monitoring after discharge which will be important to clarify this relationship. Another important consideration is that cytokine expression in early life is characterized by considerable intraindividual variability, reflecting the dynamic maturation of the neonatal immune system and its interactions with environmental exposures.[22] PI and neonates exhibit rapidly evolving innate and adaptive immune responses, influenced by factors such as microbial colonization, antigen exposure, and developmental regulation of immune signaling pathways.[23,24] This biological variability can contribute to fluctuations in cytokine levels over short time scales, potentially affecting the interpretation of single time-point measurements.[25] Although, in this study, we assessed cytokine profiles longitudinally during the NICU stay, the absence of repeated measurements beyond this period limits our ability to determine whether early cytokine patterns remain stable, diminish, or fluctuate prior to the onset of respiratory symptoms later in infancy. Future studies incorporating extended longitudinal sampling would be valuable and longitudinal immune profiling may enhance understanding of immune development and improve the contextualization of associations observed in exploratory studies. Despite these limitations, it is, to our knowledge, one of the only studies analyzing the immune status of PI during NICU stay, including a relatively large number of infants followed prospectively.

In summary, our findings suggest that preterm infants who do not experience respiratory viral infection during their NICU stay but later develop recurrent wheezing may display a distinct immune profile in their NPA supernatants. This cytokine pattern postulates a potential association with respiratory outcomes, but it cannot be considered predictive and requires confirmation in larger, adequately powered cohorts.
