Cortical organoids reveal human-specific role of METTL5 in neurodevelopment via regulation of CHCHD2
Authors: Elena M. Turkalj, Gugene Kang, Isabelle Liu, Mariami Kuljanishvili, Neda Keyhanvar, Michael C. Oldham, Caroline Vissers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMethyltransferase-like 5 (METTL5) catalyzes N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification on 18S rRNA. In humans, loss-of-function mutations in METTL5 cause severe microcephaly and intellectual disability, whereas Mettl5 knockout (KO) animal models display inconsistent and milder phenotypes. To better model human disease, we generated METTL5-KO human-induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-derived cortical organoids, which exhibit impaired neural progenitor cell (NPC) proliferation and differentiation, leading to reduced ventricle-like structures and significant reductions in cortical organoid diameter. Mechanistically, Ribo-seq analysis revealed broad translational changes in METTL5-KO NPCs consistent with cellular stress responses rather than transcript-specific translational changes. Single-cell RNA-seq identified downregulation of coiled-coil-helix-coiled-coil-helix domain containing 2 (CHCHD2), a mitochondrial regulator of oxidative metabolism. Overexpression of CHCHD2 in METTL5-KO NPCs rescued proliferation and partially rescued oxidative metabolism in NPCs and ventricle formation in organoids. This highlights a previously uncharacterized connection between CHCHD2, oxidative metabolism, and METTL5-mediated regulation of human neurogenesis.

Introduction

Hundreds of different modifications occur across mRNA, tRNA, and rRNA. Disentangling the methyltransferases and individual functions of each modification has been challenging (Vissers et al., 2020; Cerneckis et al., 2024). In 2019, Methyltransferase-like 5 (METTL5) was identified as the enzyme responsible for installing N6-methyladenosine (m6A) on a single position (A1832) in human 18S ribosomal RNA. The m6A1832 modification occurs just a few nucleotides away from the ribosome decoding center, suggesting a role in translation regulation (van Tran et al., 2019).

Human genetics studies of METTL5 have identified two bi-allelic frameshift mutations (NM_014168.2:c.344_345delGA, NM_014168.2:c.571_572delAA) (Richard et al., 2019), a homozygous SNP in the methyltransferase domain of exon 3 (NM_014268.3:c362A>G) (Torun et al., 2022), a homozygous deletion mutation in the donor splice site of exon 2 (NM_014168:c.223_224+8del) (Shakarami et al., 2023), and a homozygous intron mutation (NM_014168.4: c.224 + 5 G > A) that causes exon 2 skipping (Zhou et al., 2025). All these mutations cause intellectual disability (ID) and microcephaly, with additional symptoms including seizures, craniofacial defects, growth retardation, and aggression. The average severity of microcephaly from these studies is 3.6 standard deviations below the mean head circumference, which is classified as severe microcephaly (DeSilva et al., 2017). Surprisingly, in four studies published using Mettl5-KO mouse models, microcephaly is not a clear or consistent phenotype (Ignatova et al., 2020; Sepich-Poore et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022; Lei et al., 2023). Micro-computed tomography (CT) images of mouse skulls at 6 and 14 weeks do not show obvious reductions in brain size, and microcephaly was not listed as a phenotype (Ignatova et al., 2020; Lei et al., 2023). Cognitive impairment was also unclear, with some studies reporting impairments in learning or memory and others finding no significant effects (Ignatova et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022). Notably, evaluation of ID in mice remains inherently indirect, as behavioral assays cannot fully capture the higher order cognitive impairments characteristic of human ID (Damianidou et al., 2022).

The inconsistent phenotypes observed in mice and across species suggest that METTL5 function may have cell-type or species-specific effects. In this study we generated METTL5-KO human-induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) to model human neural development with hiPSC-derived cortical forebrain organoids. This is the first human-specific, 3D model of METTL5 function and specifically highlights the importance of METTL5 in human neural development.

The mechanisms of action for loss of METTL5 function have largely focused on regulation of translation due to the precarious positioning of m6A1832 near the ribosomal decoding center. However, work over the past 5 years has shown that loss of METTL5 does not affect ribosomal biogenesis (Richard et al., 2019; Sepich-Poore et al., 2022) or rRNA maturation (Leismann et al., 2020). Reports on whether changes in translation are global or transcript-specific have largely conflicted. Global reductions in translation upon METTL5 loss were reported in HEK293 and mESCs, while transcript-specific changes were reported in mESCs, HeLa, HepG2, and Hct116 cells (Hao Chen 2020; Ignatova et al., 2020; Rong et al., 2020; Xing et al., 2020; Sepich-Poore et al., 2022). These conflicting results suggest there may be certain contexts in which METTL5 is especially important, indicating a need for studies that directly model the most severe phenotypes seen in humans.

Here, we show that METTL5 is highly expressed in neural stem cells also known as radial glial cells (RGCs) in hiPSC-derived forebrain organoids. Furthermore, neural progenitor cells (NPCs) are profoundly impaired in proliferation and neural differentiation upon knockout (KO) of METTL5, creating a strong phenotype in METTL5-KO cortical organoids. Mechanistically, we find a reduction in translation with no clear evidence for transcript specificity. Instead, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) reveals highly significant downregulation of CHCHD2, a gene involved in mitochondrial function and oxidative respiration. We show that METTL5-KO NPCs cannot properly recover from mitochondrial stress, and overexpression (OE) of CHCHD2 rescues the proliferation and partially rescues oxidative metabolism defects and ventricle-like structure formation of METTL5-KO NPCs and organoids, respectively. Recent work has implicated CHCHD2 in multiple human neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative disorders (Shimojima et al., 2015; Li et al., 2023; Lisowski et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025). Some of the severity of brain phenotypes seen in METTL5-mutant human patients could reflect downstream effects of CHCHD2 dysregulation. Altogether, we propose that METTL5 is especially important in contexts where cells have a high metabolic demand—such as in the developing human brain—and that indirect downstream effects on key genes such as CHCHD2 may drive the specific neural phenotypes seen in humans.

METTL5-KO cortical forebrain organoids recapitulate human microcephaly phenotypes

We grew cortical organoids using an adapted protocol from (Kadoshima et al., 2013; Bershteyn et al., 2017; Ziffra et al., 2021; Bertucci et al., 2023) (Figure 1E). Organoids were collected at days 40, 55, and 75. METTL5-KO caused significant reductions in the number of ventricle-like structures at all time points, and significant reductions in diameter at days 55 and 75. (Figures 1F–1H). H9 and WTC11-derived organoids confirmed the reduced ventricle-like number, and also showed no differences in diameter at day 40 (Figure S1). KOLF2.1J organoids were more neurogenic in terms of ventricle-like structure formation compared to H9 and WTC11, so we focused remaining experiments on the KOLF2.1J hiPSC line. Overall, the strong phenotype seen in cortical organoids reinforces the hypothesis that METTL5 significantly regulates human neural development and that this organoid model recapitulates certain human phenotypes that were less clear in animal models.

Loss of METTL5 impairs early neurogenesis and the formation of subventricular-like zones

Ventricle-like structures are defined by a thick layer of PAX6+ RGC/NPCs around a ventricle and an outer layer of TBR2+ intermediate progenitor cells (IPCs). At day 40, METTL5-KO organoids had fewer PAX6+ cells—a neural stem cell/RGC marker—per 100 μm2 of ventricular area relative to controls and had not yet started producing TBR2+ cells—an IPC marker (Figures 2B and 2D). By day 55, the number of PAX6+ cells was comparable between KO organoids and controls, but KOs still had fewer TBR2+ IPCs (Figures 2C–2E). This delay extended into neurogenesis; at day 75, KO organoids had significantly fewer CTIP2+ cells—a marker of deep layer neurons—compared to controls (Figures 2F and 2G). These data show that METTL5 is required to maintain the normal pace of cellular differentiation during cortical development.

METTL5 drives RGC proliferation and differentiation in 3D and 2D

The significant reduction in METTL5-KO organoid diameter and the reduction in PAX6+ neural stem cells at day 40 suggest a deficit in the proliferation capacity of METTL5-KO NPCs. Consistent with this hypothesis, day 55 METTL5-KO organoids had fewer actively cycling (Ki67+; marker for cells in cell cycle) and dividing (PH3+; marker for cells in mitosis) cells along the walls of the ventricle-like structures (Figures 3A and 3B). To further validate this finding, we differentiated control and METTL5-KO hiPSCs into NPCs (Qi et al., 2017; Karch et al., 2019) and quantified NPC proliferation rates in 2D. Next, we performed 2D neural induction (Qi et al., 2017; Karch et al., 2019) from hiPSCs to form TUJ1+ (a neuronal cell marker) neurons (Figure 3D). By day 20, control cells formed neurons that expressed METTL5 and TUJ1 (Figures 3E–3G). In comparison, METTL5-KO hiPSCs had significantly reduced TUJ1+ staining and maintained NPC-like morphology and density in culture. By day 30, control cells were positive for early deep-layer markers CTIP2 and TBR1, while METTL5-KO cells were negative for neuronal markers, but positive for the earlier intermediate progenitor cell marker TBR2 (Figure 3H). These results recapitulate the delay in neurogenesis observed in our organoid model. To rule out confounding effects of apoptosis, we performed a cleaved-caspase 3 (CPP3) assay. This assay revealed no significant differences in apoptosis between control and METTL5-KO NPCs at baseline, though addition of staurosporine as a positive control did elicit a larger apoptotic response in METTL5-KO NPCs compared to controls (Figures S3C and S3D). Finally, increased cell density in METTL5-KO cells at day 20 of neural differentiation is caused by a maintenance of cycling, PAX6+ NPCs in the KO, compared to a significant reduction in the proportion of PAX6+ NPCs in the control; the proportion of Ki67+ PAX6+ NPCs was higher in the control than KO at day 5, and not significantly different at day 20, reinforcing that the high cell density seen in the KO comes from impaired differentiation, not increased proliferation (Figures S3E–S3G). In conclusion, both proliferation and neural differentiation are significantly impaired upon loss of METTL5.

METTL5 regulates global translation levels in iPSCs and NPCs

Previous studies on the impact of METTL5 loss-of-function (LOF) mutations reached conflicting conclusions on whether they cause global or transcript-specific changes in translation (Hao Chen 2020; Ignatova et al., 2020; Rong et al., 2020; Sepich-Poore et al., 2022). To assess how METTL5 loss alters ribosome organization and overall translation, we performed polysome profiling in hiPSCs and NPCs.

In hiPSCs, METTL5 loss resulted in a clear redistribution of ribosomes, characterized by an increase in the monosome peak and a 70% reduction in the polysome-to-monosome (P/M) area under the curve (AUC) ratio relative to control cells (Figure 4A). This shift was consistently observed across replicates and is indicative of reduced ribosome engagement. In NPCs, we observed a similar but more modest shift in ribosome organization. METTL5-KO NPCs showed an increase in the monosome peak and a 57% reduction in the P/M ratio relative to controls (Figure S4A). While changes across the polysome region were less pronounced and exhibited greater variability, the increase in monosomes was the most consistent feature across replicates, supporting a reproducible alteration in ribosome distribution. Additionally, a small peak immediately following the monosome was observed in NPC profiles, which may represent disomes or early polysome intermediates. This feature appeared more prominent in METTL5-KO samples, suggesting potential alterations in ribosome loading or elongation dynamics. These findings indicate that METTL5 loss alters ribosome organization and global translation across cell types, with a consistent increase in monosomes and a reduction in polysome association, while the magnitude and clarity of this effect vary depending on cellular context.

Next, we performed ribosome sequencing (Ribo-seq) on control and METTL5-KO NPCs and used the Ribolog method to quantify translation efficiency (Navickas et al., 2023). Overall, there was a downward shift in translation efficiency in the KO, with 2,936 transcripts (23.1%) showing a significant reduction in translation efficiency and 1,224 transcripts (9.6%) showing an increase in translation efficiency compared to controls (Figures 4B and S4B; Table S1). Sample coverage plots of unchanged (Figure 4C, GAPDH), decreased (Figure 4D, PREX2), and increased (Figure 4E, SP100) translation are shown. Motif analysis showed enrichment for general GC-rich repeats in both the up- and down-regulated translated transcripts (Figures S4C and S4D), suggesting there is no singular motif or subset of transcripts that is exclusively susceptible to changes in m6A1832. However, narrowing our analysis to the 100 most significantly translationally downregulated transcripts revealed a significant enrichment of a pyrimidine-rich translational element (PRTE) motif, which was present in the 5′ UTRs of 21 out of the 100 transcripts. (Figure S4E). PRTE motifs are known modulators of translation efficiency, often found in the 5' UTRs of transcripts regulated by nutrient availability and cellular stress (Morita et al., 2013; Hochstoeger et al., 2024).

Our Ribo-seq analysis incorporated RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of input RNA. Differential expression analysis of the underlying RNA-seq data revealed that 10,452 genes were significantly differentially expressed in METTL5-KO versus control NPCs (Figure 4F; Table S2). In comparison, only 4,160 gene transcripts had significant changes in translation efficiency, with 2,492 genes showing significant changes at both the transcriptional and translational levels (Figure 4G). These 2,492 genes were highly enriched for gene ontology categories involving translation and ribosomal biogenesis, suggesting a potential feedback loop that reinforces changes in translation at a global level (Figure 4H). There was no clear relationship between directionality of transcriptional regulation and translation efficiency regulation (Figure S4F). Together, these results indicate that METTL5 loss leads to broad, global shifts in translation efficiency rather than selective regulation of a specific subset of transcripts.

scRNA-seq reveals differential cellular composition of METTL5-KO and control day 75 organoids

We performed scRNA-seq on day 75 cortical organoids using particle-templated instant partition sequencing (PIP-seq) (Clark et al., 2023). Following quality control, we retained 41,933 control and 33,973 KO cells derived from three independent experimental replicates, each replicate consisting of 3–4 organoids from METTL5-KO and control. Using Seurat (Hao et al., 2024), we identified 16 distinct cellular populations across KO and control organoids (Figures 5A and S5A). METTL5-KO organoids exhibited an increased proportion of metabolically stressed cells, which had elevated expression of genes associated with mitochondrial dysfunction(Guo et al., 2020) and the integrated stress response (Li et al., 2014; Kress, Jessen et al., 2023) (MT-ND1, DDIT3 [CHOP], HSPA5 [BiP]), alongside a reduction in RGCs (Figure 5B). Differential expression analysis of pseudo-bulked replicates identified 1,218 genes that were significantly differentially expressed in METTL5-KO day 75 organoids (Figure 5C; Table S3). Gene set enrichment and gene ontology analysis identified “regulation of transcription” and “Wnt signaling” as upregulated in control organoids, while “synapse” and “MHC protein complex” cellular components as upregulated in METTL5-KO organoids (Figure 5D; Tables S4 and S5). Together, these findings indicate that METTL5 loss alters cellular composition, characterized by increased cellular stress and reduced progenitor populations, consistent with impaired neurodevelopmental capacity.

CHCHD2 is massively downregulated in METTL5-KO organoids and NPCs

Among all genes, CHCHD2 was the most significantly downregulated in METTL5-KO cells (Figures 5C and S6; Tables S3). CHCHD2 regulates mitochondrial function and the mitochondrial integrated stress response, with known involvement in neurodevelopmental processes (Zhu et al., 2016; Ruan et al., 2022). Specifically, CHCHD2 is a mitochondrial intermembrane space protein that promotes oxidative phosphorylation by regulating electron transport chain activity and mitochondrial respiration, and can translocate to the nucleus under stress to modulate transcription of mitochondrial stress response genes (Aras et al., 2013; Aras et al., 2015). We validated CHCHD2 downregulation via qPCR (Figure 5E) and western blot (Figure 5F) and found low to no expression of CHCHD2.

CHCHD2 OE significantly improves METTL5-KO cell metabolism, proliferation, and organoid growth

To determine the contribution of CHCHD2 to the overall phenotype of METTL5-KO NPCs, we generated a doxycycline-inducible CHCHD2 OE line in the METTL5-KO background (Figures 6A and 6B). Because CHCHD2 is a mitochondrial protein involved in oxidative phosphorylation, we examined how METTL5 loss affects cellular metabolism using Seahorse XF metabolic stress tests. Following sequential injection of oligomycin, carbonyl cyanide p-(trifluoromethoxy)phenylhydrazone (FCCP), and rotenone/antimycin A, control NPCs displayed the expected dynamic changes in oxygen consumption rate (OCR), including a robust increase in maximal respiration after FCCP addition. In contrast, METTL5-KO NPCs showed a blunted OCR response following FCCP treatment, indicating impaired mitochondrial respiratory capacity. This phenotype was partially rescued (∼50%) by CHCHD2 overexpression (+dox), but not uninduced controls (-dox) (Figures 6C and 6D). Notably, maximal and spare respiratory capacity showed the most consistent decreases upon loss of METTL5, supporting a defect in the ability of METTL5-KO cells to respond to energetic stress. Consistent with these metabolic changes, CHCHD2 overexpression also rescued the proliferation defect of METTL5-KO NPCs. Upon doxycycline induction, METTL5-KO + CHCHD2-OE cells exhibited increased proliferation rates and reached levels comparable to control NPCs by day 6 (Figure 6E).

Finally, METTL5-KO + CHCHD2-OE + Dox significantly increase the number of ventricle-like structures in day 40 METTL5-KO organoids, generating on average a 53% rescue effect. (Figures 6F and 6G). Overall, the significant yet partial rescue by CHCHD2 highlights its importance in the context of METTL5 neural regulation, though it is not singularly responsible for the full phenotype. In particular, the full rescue of 2D NPC proliferation yet partial rescue of ventricle-like structure formation in cortical organoids reinforces the importance and complexity of 3D human models.

Discussion

We show that METTL5-KO hiPSC-derived cortical forebrain organoids recapitulate the microcephaly phenotype observed in human patients with METTL5 mutations. Furthermore, prior studies have reported variable impacts for METTL5 LOF mutations, with some suggesting globally dysregulated translation (Ignatova et al., 2020; Rong et al., 2020), and others pointing to transcript-specific effects (Hao Chen 2020; Xing et al., 2020; Sepich-Poore et al., 2022). These conflicting reports underscore the importance of studying METTL5 in relevant cellular contexts. Using human cortical organoids, we observed that METTL5 loss significantly impairs NPC proliferation and neurogenesis, leading to a significant developmental delay. These phenotypes were accompanied by broad translational repression and striking changes in the transcription of neurodevelopmental and metabolic genes. This study is the first to highlight scRNA-seq data that showed that downstream changes in transcription due to loss of METTL5, rather than direct effects on translation, may be the key driver of phenotypes seen in human patients with mutations in METTL5.

One of the most notable transcriptional changes in METTL5-KO NPCs was the highly significant downregulation of CHCHD2, which encodes a mitochondrial protein involved in oxidative phosphorylation and cellular stress response. Our data show that METTL5-KO NPCs exhibit impaired oxidative metabolism at baseline, with further deficits under mitochondrial stress. Notably, CHCHD2 overexpression rescues METTL5-KO NPC proliferation, partially rescues METTL5-KO NPC oxidative metabolism, and partially rescues ventricle formation in day 40 METTL5-KO cortical organoids, suggesting that CHCHD2 loss is a key driver of the observed phenotype in METTL5-KO NPCs. Interestingly, Chchd2 expression is not altered in Mettl5-KO mouse models (Sepich-Poore et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022), suggesting possible species differences in the role of CHCHD2 in neural development. Supporting this idea, CHCHD2 has recently been implicated in human neurodevelopmental disorders including lissencephaly (Shimojima et al., 2015) and autism spectrum disorder (Li et al., 2023), as well as in neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s disease and ALS (Shi et al., 2016; Lisowski et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). Human NPCs have high metabolic demands during early development (Zheng et al., 2016) and may be particularly sensitive to perturbations in mitochondrial function. Notably, CHCHD2-deficient mouse mitochondria can still maintain oxidative phosphorylation (Nguyen et al., 2022), highlighting a potential difference in human mitochondrial vulnerability.

The observation that CHCHD2 is significantly downregulated at the RNA level but not in Ribo-seq suggests that its regulation in METTL5-KO cells is likely primarily transcriptional. The strength of CHCHD2 downregulation at the transcriptional level merits further investigation, given that METTL5 is canonically understood as a translational regulator. Several mechanisms could account for this: METTL5 loss may impair translation of a transcriptional activator of CHCHD2; the broad cellular stress induced by METTL5 loss could indirectly suppress CHCHD2 transcription (Huang et al., 2019; Ruan et al., 2022); or loss of m6A on 18S rRNA may trigger broader transcriptional reprogramming, with CHCHD2 among the most sensitive targets in NPCs. While the transcriptional regulation of CHCHD2 remains incompletely defined, prior studies suggest that CHCHD2 can autoregulate its own expression under stress conditions (Aras et al., 2015), raising the possibility that reduced CHCHD2 levels in METTL5-KO cells may be amplified through a feedforward mechanism. In addition, although our data are consistent with transcriptional downregulation, we cannot exclude contributions from post-transcriptional mechanisms, as METTL5-dependent ribosome modifications may alter the translation of factors involved in RNA stability or decay, indirectly affecting CHCHD2 mRNA levels. It is especially important to recognize that epitranscriptomic changes do not happen in isolation from other regulatory mechanisms, and that perhaps some of the strongest phenotypes, as shown here, arise from downstream pathways in specific contexts of development or stress.

Although there were no specific system-wide motifs enriched among differentially translated mRNAs, we did find that the 100 most significantly downregulated transcripts in METTL5-KO NPCs were enriched for PRTE motifs. These motifs are regulated by mTORC1, with LARP1 binding to 5′ TOP and pyrimidine-rich PRTE regions to control ribosome recruitment in an mTOR-dependent manner (Hochstoeger et al., 2024). This suggests that METTL5 loss may be intertwined with translational regulation via the mTOR pathway, or that the METTL5-KO translational phenotype is muddied by other forms of translational regulation related to cellular stress. This was also hypothesized by Rong et al. (Rong et al., 2020), who noted that loss of METTL5 in breast cancer cell lines phenocopies MCF7 breast cancer cells treated with mTOR inhibitors.

Together, these findings highlight a novel pathway in which METTL5 loss compromises mitochondrial function via downregulation of CHCHD2, leading to significant impairments in neurogenesis. Though there are broad changes in both translation and transcription in METTL5-KO NPCs, the discovery that CHCHD2 overexpression in METTL5-KO NPCs can rescue proliferation, metabolism and organoid growth defects strongly suggests that this pathway is a major driver of microcephaly seen in humans with METTL5 mutations. The human-specific aspects of METTL5 function and rRNA methylation merit further research, as the additional layers of RNA regulation mediated by RNA methyltransferases may be necessary for the cortical expansion and massive neurogenesis specific to human development.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

hiPSC culture

Human iPSCs and hESCs, including KOLF2.1J (Jackson Laboratories JIPSC001000; male, 55–59 years, white/British), H9 (WiCell WAe009-A; female, from blastocyst), and WTC11 lines (Coriell Institute GM25256; male, 30 years, Asian/Japanese), were maintained under feeder-free conditions on Matrigel-coated plates. KOLF2.1J and WTC11 were obtained via MTA from Jackson Laboratories and the Coriell Institute, respectively; H9 via MTA from the Goodarzi lab (UCSF). KOLF2.1J and H9 were cultured in StemFlex medium (Thermo Fisher), while WTC11 cells were maintained in mTeSR Plus medium (STEMCELL Technologies). Medium was replenished every other day. Cells were passaged every 4–6 days (∼80% confluence) using ReLeSR (STEMCELL Technologies) and replated at a 1:2 ratio. Only compact, morphologically defined colonies were retained. All lines were karyotyped at ∼10 passages (KaryoStat qPCR, STEMCELL Technologies) to confirm genomic stability, regularly authenticated by sequencing, and tested for mycoplasma (including post-thaw; 1×106 cells per test) and sterility (every thaw). Cells were frozen in Stem Cell Banker (Amsbio Llc 11924) and thawed rapidly. Passages 3–20 post-thaw were used for all experiments. Pluripotency was validated every 3 passages by qPCR for OCT4, SOX2, and NANOG.

Knockout lines

CRISPR-mediated knockout was performed in KOLF2.1J, H9, and WTC11 cells using the Synthego Gene Knockout Kit v2, per manufacturer’s protocol. Chemically modified synthetic single gRNAs (AGUCGCCUGCAACAAGUGGA, AUUUUUAUCACAGAGUAGAC, GCCGCCCUACCUGCAAUGUG) were designed using Synthego’s online tool to target early exons of the gene and were complexed with recombinant Cas9 protein into ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes. RNPs were delivered into iPSCs via electroporation using the Lonza 4D-Nucleofector system with the P3 Primary Cell Kit. Post-electroporation, cells were plated and supplemented with 10 μM ROCK inhibitor (Y-27632) (Ri). Editing efficiency was assessed 48 h post-electroporation by Sanger sequencing and ICE (Inference of CRISPR Edits) analysis. Successfully edited single-cell clones were expanded, genotyped, and validated for indel formation at the target locus.

TET-on 3G lines

A doxycycline-inducible CHCHD2 construct with a C-terminal 3xFLAG tag was introduced into METTL5-KO KOLF2.1J iPSCs using the Tet-On 3G system (Takara Bio). Lentivirus was produced by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with the transfer plasmid and packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G using the TransIT-Lenti transfection reagent (Mirus Bio). Knockout iPSCs were transduced with the constructs in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene then selected with 0.5 μg/mL puromycin for 3–5 days. Stable lines were expanded and validated for doxycycline-inducible expression (10 μg/mL - 25 μg/mL) of CHCHD2 by sanger sequencing, qPCR, western blot, and immunofluorescence staining.

Method details

hNPC culture

hiPSCs at 80–90% confluency were cultured in NI supplemented with 10 μM SB431542, 100 nM LDN-193189, and 5 μM XAV939 to generate NPCs. Medium was changed daily for 7 days. On day 7, NPCs were dissociated with Accutase and seeded on Matrigel-coated 6-well plates in MM1 with 10 μM Ri; Ri was removed the following day.

Cortical organoid differentiation

This protocol was adapted from (Kadoshima et al., 2013; Bershteyn et al., 2017; Ziffra et al., 2021; Bertucci et al., 2023). hiPSCs were cultured feeder-free and dissociated with Accutase (STEMCELL Technologies). For aggregation, 10,000 cells in 100 μL iPSC medium +10 μM Ri were seeded into 96-well low-attachment slit-well plates (Sbio MS-9096SZ) at 37°C, 5% CO2 (Day 0). On Day 3, medium was replaced with Neural Induction Medium (NI: GMEM 76.8%, KSR 20%, NEAA, sodium pyruvate, antibiotic-antimycotic, 0.18 mM β-mercaptoethanol) supplemented with 5 μM SB431542, 3 μM IWR-1-endo, 100 nM LDN-193189, and 10 μM Ri. On Day 6, NI was refreshed with inhibitors without Ri.

From Days 9–25, organoids were maintained in MM1 (DMEM/F12 47.4%, Neurobasal 47.4%, B27 minus vitamin A, N2, GlutaMAX, NEAA, antibiotic-antimycotic, 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol supplemented fresh with EGF 10 ng/mL and FGF10 ng/mL). Medium was partially or fully changed every other day. On Day 18, organoids were transferred to an orbital shaker (57–90 rpm). From Days 26–35, MM1 without growth factors were used; Days 35–55 used MM2 (same as MM1 but with B27 + vitamin A and supplemented fresh with 200 nM ascorbic acid). From Days 56–62, MM2 was supplemented fresh with 10 ng/mL LIF. From Days 63–70, MM2 supplemented with 10 ng/mL BDNF, and 10 ng/mL NT-3. After Day 70, MM2 was used. Organoids were fixed overnight in 4% PFA at 4°C (60 rpm), cryoprotected in 30% sucrose/PBS overnight, embedded in Tissue Freezing Medium (Fisher Scientific), and snap-frozen for downstream analysis.

In vitro neurogenesis

Adapted from (Kadoshima et al., 2013; Bershteyn et al., 2017; Qi et al., 2017; Ziffra et al., 2021). 800,000 NPCs were seeded into poly-L-ornithine/laminin-521/fibronectin-coated 35 mm glass-bottom wells with MM1 with Ri (Ri removed next day). Media schedule (days):

1-7: iPSC cultured in NI supplemented with 10 μM SB431542, 100 nM LDN-193189, and 5 μM XAV939 to make NPCs.

7-9: MM1+ EGF + FGF.

9-11: MM1 without growth factors.

11-13: MM2 without growth factors.

13-30: MM2 with 500 μM dibutyryl-cAMP, 200 μM ascorbic acid, 20 ng/mL BDNF, and 20 ng/mL GDNF.

Proliferation assay

NPCs were grown in Matrigel (Corning)-coated 6 well plates to approximately 85% confluency. On Day 0, NPCs were passaged using Accutase (STEMCELL Technologies), centrifuged at 250g for 5 min, and resuspended in MM1 supplemented with 10 μM Ri. Cells were counted on a hemacytometer in triplicate. 110,000 cells were plated in triplicate per timepoint in Matrigel-coated 6-well plates. 24 h after seeding, media was changed to MM1 without Ri. Samples were collected at 2, 4, and 6 days after seeding by washing once with PBS, adding 0.5 mL Trypsin per well and incubating for 5 min at 37°C, then gently pipetting up and down to generate a single cell suspension. 10 μL of the cells in Trypsin were loaded onto a hemacytometer and counted in duplicates. Media was changed at 24, 48, and 96 h after seeding.

Apoptosis assay

KOLF2.1J control and M5-KO NPCs were stained for cleaved caspase-3 to assess apoptosis. For a positive control, cells were treated with 1 μM staurosporine (Sigma-Aldrich) for 4 h at 37°C to induce apoptosis. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature (RT), then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. Cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-cleaved caspase-3 antibody (Cell Signaling), then washed, and incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:300, Thermo Fisher) plus DAPI for 2 h at RT.

Seahorse assay

Mitochondrial function was assessed using the Seahorse XF Mito Stress Test (Agilent Technologies) according to manufacturer’s instructions. KOLF2.1J control, METTL5-KO, and CHCHD2 OE NPCs passage 2 or 3 were seeded into Seahorse XF24 cell culture microplates at 10,000 cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight in NPC MM1 with Ri (10 μg/mL). CHCHD2 OE line was already incubated with dox (10 μg/mL) 24 h before seeding and maintained in + dox media. Media was changed to NPC MM1 the next day, then replaced the following day with Seahorse XF assay medium (Agilent) supplemented with 10 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate, and 2 mM glutamine, and cells were incubated at 37°C in a non-CO2 incubator for 1 h prior to measurement.

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured using the Seahorse Xfe24 Analyzer following sequential injections of 1 μM oligomycin (ATP synthase inhibitor), 2 μM FCCP (uncoupler), and 0.5 μM rotenone and 0.5 μM antimycin A (complex I and III inhibitors, respectively). Basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, maximal respiration, spare respiratory capacity, and non-mitochondrial respiration were calculated using Seahorse Wave software. Data were normalized to Hoechst fluorescence per well.

Single cell RNA isolation; organoid dissociation

Control (KOLF2.1J) and METTL5-KO cerebral organoids (three biological replicates per genotype; 3–4 organoids pooled per replicate) were dissociated with papain. Organoids were incubated for 15 min at 37°C in papain (20UmL-1) in EDTA/β-mercaptoethanol/L-cysteine + DNAse I (0.005%, w/v) under gentle agitation. Tissue was gently triturated, digested for a further 10 min, then triturated again until a uniform single-cell suspension formed. Digestion was quenched with ovomucoid inhibitor, cells were pelleted (300 x g, 5 min), resuspended in 0.04% BSA/HBSS, and filtered through a 37 μm strainer. Viability, assessed by trypan blue staining, exceeded 85% for all samples.

Library preparation and sequencing

Single cells were processed with the PIPseq-V T10 kit (Fluent BioSciences) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (2 × 150 bp). All FAIR and CARE data principles were followed for all subsequent data analysis.

rRNA fragment purification

A 40-nt rRNA fragment was purified twice using a modified version of (Ma et al., 2019). For each sample, 3 tubes (33 μg total RNA +4 μg biotinylated DNA probe targeting A1832) were mixed in 3.33X hybridization buffer (250 mM HEPES pH 7, 500 mM KCl; 150 μL/tube) and incubated at 90°C for 7 min, then cooled to RT over 3.5 h. RNase A (0.5 μg, Thermo Scientific) was added and incubated 30 min at 37°C, followed by Mung Bean Nuclease (1X buffer, NEB) for 30 min at 37°C. Streptavidin T1 beads (20 μL/tube; Invitrogen) were washed 3X with 1 mL 2.5X IP buffer (375 mM NaCl, 125 mM Tris pH 7.9, 0.25% NP-40) and resuspended in 100 μL 2.5X IP buffer. Beads (100 μL) were added to each tube and rotated at 4°C for 1 h, then washed 3X with 1X IP buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris pH 7.9, 0.1% NP-40), 2X with nuclease-free water, then resuspended in 30 μL nuclease-free water. RNA was released by heating beads at 70°C for 5 min and supernatants collected on ice. Supernatants from the 3 tubes per sample were combined, and the purification was repeated.

m6A quantification

Double-purified rRNA fragments were used in combination with the EpiQuik m6A RNA Methylation Quantification Kit (Colorimetric; Epigentek, Farmingdale, NY, USA). Briefly, 30 ng of purified rRNA fragment were analyzed alongside a control standard curve (range 0.02–1 ng of m6A) in a 96 well plate. Absorbance was read at 450 nm via CLARIOstar Platereader (BMG Labtech). Samples were normalized to a blank well negative control. m6A quantity was calculated as:m6A(ng)=(SampleOD−NegativeCtrlOD)/Slopeofstandardcurve

Polysome profiling

Control or METTL5-KO cells were grown on 15 cm plates to ∼80% confluency and treated with cycloheximide (100 μg/mL, 3 min, 37°C). Cells were washed once with ice-cold PBS + cycloheximide, scraped, pelleted (200 X g, 2 min), and lysed in 400 μL polysome lysis buffer [20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM DTT, 100 μg/mL cycloheximide, 25 U/mL DNase I] on ice for 20–30 min. Lysates were clarified (10,000 X g, 10 min, 4°C), RNA was quantified, and 10–15 μg RNA per sample was used for sucrose gradients.

A 10–50% sucrose gradient was prepared in gradient buffer [20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 100 μg/mL cycloheximide] using a BioComp Gradient Master. Equal RNA inputs (10–15 μg) were centrifuged in an SW41 Ti rotor (40,000 rpm, 1 h 45 min, 4°C) and fractionated using a BioComp Piston Gradient Fractionator with continuous UV absorbance at 254 nm.

Ribosome footprint isolation (adapted from (McGlincy and Ingolia 2017; Jowhar et al., 2024; Bibel et al., 2025))

Control and METTL5-KO NPCs were grown on 15cm plates (3 plates/replicate, n = 3 biological replicates each) and harvested at 80% confluency, immediately treated with cycloheximide (100 μg/mL) for 3 min at 37°C, washed once with ice-cold PBS containing cycloheximide (100 μg/mL), lysed in polysome lysis buffer [20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM DTT, 100 μg/mL cycloheximide, DNAse I 25U/mL), H20], and incubated 30 min on ice. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 10,000 X g for 10 min at 4°C. 1-2μg RNA was reserved for RNA-seq; 30 μg was incubated with 30U/mL RNAse P1 for 1h at 37°C. The Illustra MicroSpin Columns S-400HR were used for monosome isolation and downstream steps were the same as reported in (McGlincy and Ingolia 2017).

Western blot

Cells at 80–90% confluency were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer (1X) with protease/phosphatase inhibitors (1X; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Lysates were incubated on ice for 15 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C. Supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were measured with the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (15–20 μg) were mixed with 4X LDS Sample Buffer (Bio-Rad) and 10X NuPAGE Reducing Agent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), then denatured at 70°C for 10 min.

Proteins were resolved on 4–12% Bis-Tris gels (Bio-Rad) in 1X NuPAGE MES SDS Running Buffer (Life Technologies) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes using Power Blotter Select (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Membranes were blocked and incubated with primary antibodies in 2% milk/2% BSA in 1X TBS-T overnight at 4°C, washed three times, and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5,000) for 2 h at RT. Bands were detected with SuperSignal West Dura substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Organoid sectioning and immunohistochemistry staining

Organoids were washed in PBS and fixed in 4% PFA at 4°C overnight, transferred to sterile filtered 30% sucrose in PBS at 4°C for 24 h, then embedded in OCT (TissueTek) and sliced to 14–16 μm (Leica CM3050S cryostat). Sections were incubated for 30 min at RT with blocking solution (5% NDS, 3% BSA, and 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS), then incubated with primary antibodies diluted in blocking solution at 4°C overnight. Sections were washed 3X in PBST for 10 min at RT, then incubated with secondary antibodies in blocking solution for 1 h at RT. 0.1 mg/mL 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to the secondary antibody mixture. Stained sections were mounted with ProLong Gold anti-fade mounting media (Thermo Fisher P36930) and imaged on a Nikon TE2000 epifluorescent confocal microscope with Zeiss software. Raw images were processed in Fiji ImageJ 1.54p; organoid images were stitched with Stitching Plugin (Preibisch et al., 2009).

Quantification and statistical analysis

scRNA-seq analysis

Read processing and alignment

FASTQs were processed with PIPseeker v3.3. Cellular barcodes and intrinsic molecular identifiers (IMIs) were extracted (Hamming distance ≤1), added to read headers, and technical sequences (template-switch oligo, leading T, poly-A) were trimmed. Trimmed R2 reads were aligned to GRCh38.p13 (GENCODE v40, Ensembl 106) using STAR v2.7.11a with default PIPseeker settings.

Molecule counting

PIPseeker collapsed identical IMIs within the same three-base start site bin, corrected amplification bias (≤1% inflation), and probabilistically assigned multi-mapped reads. The output was a raw count matrix (matrix.mtx.gz, barcodes.tsv.gz, features.tsv.gz).

Quality control and integration

Cells with <300 or >6,500 detected genes or >12% mitochondrial content were excluded (thresholds from violin plots, Figures S3B and S3C). Samples were normalized with SCTransform (regressing out percent.mt), merged, and annotated as CT or KO. PCA was performed (top 22 PCs from an elbow plot). Batch effects were corrected using Harmony, followed by UMAP, SSN construction, and Louvain clustering.

Cell type annotation

Clustering resolution was optimized with a cluster tree, and 0.5 was selected to avoid over-splitting. Annotations were generated using ScType (Lanevskij et al., 2022) with three marker gene sets, cross-validated across UMAP embeddings. Cluster markers were further refined using Seurat’s FindAllMarkers and manual inspection of canonical genes with literature support.

Differential expression/pseudobulking

Raw UMI counts were aggregated into pseudobulk samples by summation to avoid pseudoreplication bias(Squair et al., 2021). For CT vs. KO comparisons, expression vectors were aggregated by individual (6 samples). For CT vs. KO within clusters, data were aggregated by individual and cluster (96 samples). Pseudobulk matrices were analyzed with edgeR v4.4.2 using the glmLRT pipeline (default settings). Differentially expressed genes were defined as FDR <0.05 and log2 fold change >1.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

DE genes were analyzed for enrichment MsigDB(Liberzon et al., 2011) and curated in-house collection (>8,500 sets from ∼90 studies spanning human and murine cell types, diseases, and processes) by one-sided Fisher’s exact test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction.

Ribosome sequencing analysis

Reads preprocessing and ribolog

Raw single-end Ribo-seq reads were adapter-trimmed using cutadapt v4.9 with options “—trimmed-only -m 15 -e 0.2 -a TAGACAGATCGGAAGAGCACACGTCTGAACTCCAGTCAC,” then aligned in two steps using bowtie2 v2.5.4. Reads mapping to a custom rRNA/tRNA index were discarded. Remaining reads were aligned end-to-end to a GRCh38-based mRNA transcriptome (Ensembl release v96). The associated total RNA-Seq sample reads were aligned to the same longest CDS reference transcriptome using bowtie2.

RNA-seq differential analysis

RNA-seq transcript counts were extracted using the bam2count function in Ribolog. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v.1.42.1) to compare the control and METTL5-knockout samples.

IGV-like track plot generation

BAM files were first converted to bedGraph files (bedtools genomecov v2.31.1), normalized by counts per million (CPM). The resulting bedGraph files were used to generate transcriptome-level track plots (svist4get package v.1.3.1.1) (Egorov et al., 2019).

TE analysis with ribolog

Ribo-seq and matched RNA-seq BAM files were imported into Ribolog v0.0.0.9. Ribo-seq reads were filtered to retain only in-frame ribosome-protected fragments (RPFs) 40–44 nucleotides long. Codon-level counts were corrected for ribosome stalling using the CELP method. CELP-corrected Ribo-seq and total RNA-seq reads were median-normalized; transcripts with <5 RNA-seq reads per sample or <2 RPFs average across Ribo-seq samples were excluded. TE was modeled using logistic regression with Benjamini-Hochberg correction (Ribolog’s logit_seq).

TE analysis with DESeq2

Ribolog-derived transcript counts were reanalyzed using DESeq2 to assess differential TE. Ribo-seq counts were filtered for CDS P-site reads with footprint lengths of 40–44 nucleotides. RNA-seq counts were extracted using bam2count. Differential TE was modeled in DESeq2, incorporating sample condition (control vs. knockout) and sequencing assay type (RNA-seq vs. Ribo-seq): ∼ assay_type + condition + assay_type:condition.

Motif discovery with MEME

Genes with significantly altered TE were defined as those overlapping in both Ribolog and DESeq2 (FDR or p-adjusted <0.05). 5′UTR sequences were extracted separately for TE-upregulated and TE-downregulated genes, and 5′UTRs >20 nucleotides were retained. The command-line MEME suite v5.5.5 was used for de novo motif discovery, with motif widths restricted to 5–18 nucleotides.

GSEA analysis

GSEA was performed with fgsea R package v1.28.0 on differential TE results using the Ribolog method. Genes were ranked by the ratio of log-fold change to its standard error from the logistic regression model. The fgsea function was used to calculate enrichment across the MsigDB v2022.1 Hallmark, GO Biological Process, and KEGG pathway gene set collections.

Polysome/monosome A.U.C. Ratio

The polysome AUC was calculated from 30 to 50% fractions, the monosome AUC from 25 to 29% fractions, and ratios were determined by dividing polysome AUC by monosome AUC. Percent reduction was calculated as ((control ratio - KO ratio)/control ratio) X 100.
