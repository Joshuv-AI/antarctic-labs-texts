Development and validation of an Occupational Hazards Index (OHI) and its association with hypertension-diabetes comorbidity in steel workers: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Mingyue Liu, Xinyang Chen, Xue Ma, Meng Zhang, Xianghui Xu, Zhenghao Luo, Nan Wang, Jianhui Wu, Ling Xue, Xiaoming Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSteelworkers are exposed to complex occupational hazards; however, evidence regarding the joint effects of these hazards on hypertension-diabetes comorbidity (HDC) remains limited due to the absence of a comprehensive exposure assessment tool.MethodsA prospective cohort study was conducted using data from the “Health Effect Cohort of Occupational Populations in the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei Region”. A total of 5310 steelworkers without HDC at baseline (2017) were followed up until 2024. The Occupational Hazards Index (OHI) was constructed by integrating measured levels of 22 physical and chemical hazards (e.g., high temperature, noise, dust, carbon monoxide [CO], metals) using the Euclidean norm model, which is based on hazard quotients (measured concentration/Occupational Exposure Limit [OEL]). HDC was defined as the co-occurrence of hypertension and type 2 diabetes. Cox proportional hazards models were employed to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between the OHI (analyzed both as a continuous variable and categorized into high/low risk groups based on a restricted cubic spline-derived cut-off value of 102.75) and HDC risk, with adjustment for potential confounders.ResultsDuring a median follow-up period of 72 months, 595 incident cases of HDC were identified, with a cumulative incidence of 11.2%. The OHI showed a right-skewed distribution (median: 102.76; interquartile range [IQR]: 79.38-132.38). A significant non-linear dose-response relationship was observed between OHI and HDC risk (P for overall association < 0.001, P for non-linearity < 0.001). After full adjustment for demographic characteristics, family history of chronic diseases, lifestyle factors, anthropometric measurements, and occupational factors, each one-unit increase in the OHI was associated with a 0.3% higher risk of HDC (HR = 1.003; 95% CI: 1.001–1.005). Workers in the high-risk OHI group (≥ 102.75) had a 30.8% higher risk of HDC compared to those in the low-risk group (HR = 1.308, 95% CI: 1.108–1.543). Stratified analyses suggested that the association might be more pronounced in males, individuals aged 40–49, current smokers, and those with unhealthy lifestyles (e.g., poor diet, obesity).ConclusionWe developed a novel Occupational Hazards Index (OHI) that effectively quantifies combined occupational exposure. Our prospective findings indicate that higher OHI scores are significantly associated with an elevated risk of HDC among steel workers, highlighting the importance of comprehensive occupational hazard assessment for the primary prevention of this comorbidity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27229-z.

Background

The global burden of chronic diseases constitutes a major challenge to public health systems worldwide. Hypertension-diabetes comorbidity (HDC), defined as the co-occurrence of hypertension and type 2 diabetes in an individual, has attracted growing clinical and research interest [1, 2]. This is attributed to its high prevalence and the synergistic increase in the risk of severe complications, including cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and renal diseases [3–5]. The comorbidity rate is notably high; for example, in China, the prevalence of hypertension among patients with diabetes ranges from 49.9% to 60.6% [6], which imposes a substantial burden on healthcare resources and impairs patients’ quality of life.

The etiology of HDC is multifactorial. While well-established individual risk factors—including genetic predisposition, obesity, physical inactivity, and unhealthy dietary patterns—undoubtedly play a central role [7, 8], there is increasing recognition that environmental exposures, particularly those in occupational settings, are significant and modifiable contributors to the development and progression of these conditions. In China, the working-age population accounts for a substantial proportion of the total population (50–70%), and their health is closely linked to socioeconomic stability and productivity [9]. The steel industry, a cornerstone of the national economy, employs a massive workforce routinely exposed to a complex mixture of occupational hazards. These include physical factors (e.g., high temperatures and noise), diverse chemical agents (e.g., dust, carbon monoxide, metal fumes such as manganese and chromium, organic solvents like benzene and toluene, and irritant gases), and organizational stressors (e.g., shift work and occupational stress) [10–12].

To date, most epidemiological studies investigating the association between occupational exposures and chronic diseases have adopted a reductionist approach, focusing on the health effects of individual hazards in isolation. Although these studies have yielded valuable insights, they do not capture the reality of modern industrial environments, where workers are continuously exposed to complex mixtures of hazards that may interact synergistically or antagonistically. This discrepancy between the simplistic single-exposure paradigm and the reality of combined occupational exposure impedes the comprehensive and accurate assessment of occupational health risks. A key methodological limitation in this field is the absence of a robust, quantitative, and holistic tool for assessing the burden of combined occupational exposures.

To address this limitation, the present study aimed to: (1) develop a novel, comprehensive Occupational Hazards Index (OHI) for the quantitative assessment of combined exposure to a broad range of physical and chemical hazards among steelworkers; and (2) prospectively examine the association between the OHI and incident HDC. This research aims to provide robust scientific evidence to inform targeted primary prevention strategies and promote a shift toward an integrated risk assessment framework in occupational health.

Methods

Study design and population

This prospective cohort study used data from the “Health Effect Cohort of Occupational Populations in the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei Region”. The baseline survey was conducted from March to September 2017 among workers from a large steel complex in Tangshan, China. Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were formal employees with at least one year of work experience and had provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were age > 60 years, pre-existing severe organic diseases, refusal to participate in follow-up, or incomplete baseline information. Participants were followed annually from 2018 to 2024 for HDC assessment. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of North China University of Science and Technology (No.16040). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Of the 6184 permanent employees initially assessed for eligibility, 5310 were ultimately included in the prospective cohort analysis, as detailed in the study flow diagram (Fig. 1). The sample size was calculated a priori using the following parameters: an assumed HDC incidence of 17.5% in the exposed group (steel workers), and 11.9% in a non-exposed group. The incidence for the non-exposed group was estimated based on published regional health data for non-industrial working populations during the same period, reflecting the background risk. With a statistical power of 0.9, a two-sided alpha of 0.05, and accounting for an anticipated 10% loss to follow-up, the minimum required sample size was 1844.

Assessment of occupational hazards and OHI construction

A total of 22 occupational hazards (4 physical and 18 chemical) were quantitatively assessed for this study. The physical hazards included high temperature, noise, ultraviolet (UV) radiation, and power frequency electric fields. The chemical hazards comprised carbon monoxide, dust (total particulate), nitrogen monoxide, nitrogen dioxide, ozone, benzene, toluene, xylene, ammonia, manganese and its compounds, chromium and its compounds, zinc oxide, sodium hydroxide, hydrochloric acid, sulfuric acid, fluorides, phenol, and calcium oxide. Detailed methodologies for the onsite monitoring and laboratory analysis of these hazards, including the specific Chinese national standards followed (e.g., GBZ/T 189 series, GBZ/T 300 series), sampling strategies, and instrumentation used, are provided in the Supplementary Material (Table S1).

For each hazard “i”, the hazard quotient (HQi) was calculated as HQi = Ci/ OELi, where Ci is the time-weighted average measured concentration or intensity for an individual worker, and OELi is the corresponding national Occupational Exposure Limit (primarily the Permissible Concentration-Time Weighted Average, PC-TWA). For hazards that were unmeasured, confirmed absent for a specific job role, or with measured concentrations below the limit of detection (LOD), the Ci was conservatively set to 0, implying no significant exposure above the background level. This approach minimizes the potential for overestimation of exposure in the composite index.

The Occupational Hazards Index (OHI) was computed for each worker to integrate the HQs of all 22 hazards into a single metric. We employed the Euclidean norm (L2-norm) model for this synthesis. The choice of the Euclidean norm over alternative methods, such as the arithmetic sum (which assumes perfect linear additivity) or a weighted sum (which requires prior, often uncertain, toxicological weights), was based on its superior mathematical properties for representing a combined exposure burden. The Euclidean norm effectively captures the “magnitude” of the exposure vector in a multi-dimensional space. It is sensitive not only to the number of hazards present but also, and more importantly, to their intensity (as reflected by the HQ values). By squaring the HQs before summation, the model inherently gives greater weight to higher exposure levels, which is toxicologically plausible given that the risk from many stressors may increase disproportionately with dose. In this study, a scaling factor “k” was applied. The “k” was set to 100. This scaling was applied solely to enhance the clarity and readability of the resulting index values by transforming them into a more convenient numerical range. It is crucial to note that this linear scaling does not alter the underlying statistical distribution of the index or its relative ranking of individuals, and thus has no impact on the effect estimates (e.g., hazard ratios) derived from subsequent regression models.

To facilitate risk stratification and public health interpretation, the continuous OHI was dichotomized into “Low Risk” and “High Risk” groups. The cut-point value for this classification was determined empirically based on the relationship between OHI and HDC risk, rather than using arbitrary distribution-based percentiles (e.g., median). Specifically, we performed a restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis within a Cox proportional hazards model to flexibly model the dose-response relationship between OHI (as a continuous variable) and incident HDC. The inflection point for the increase in HDC risk was identified from the RCS curve. This data-driven approach ensures that the risk classification is directly grounded in the observed health outcome, enhancing its biological and clinical relevance.

Outcome definition

Hypertension-diabetes comorbidity (HDC) was defined as the co-occurrence of hypertension and type 2 diabetes in the same individual during follow-up. Hypertension was defined in accordance with the 2024 Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension: average systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg from three separate measurements, or current use of antihypertensive medications. Type 2 diabetes (T2D) was defined in accordance with the 2024 Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Type 2 Diabetes: fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, self-reported physician diagnosis, or current use of glucose-lowering medications.

Covariate assessment

Baseline data were collected through questionnaires, physical examinations, and laboratory tests. Covariates included: Demographics: age, sex, ethnicity, marital status, education level, household monthly income. Family History: history of hypertension or diabetes in first-degree relatives. Lifestyle Factors: Smoking: categorized as never, former, or current smoker. Drinking: categorized as never, former, or current drinker. Diet: assessed using the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) score (range 8–40), dichotomized at the median (low: 8–24, high: 25–40). Physical Activity: assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) and categorized as light, moderate, or heavy. Anthropometric Measures: Body mass index (BMI, kg/m²) and waist circumference (WC), classified according to Chinese guidelines. Occupational Factors: length of service, shift work status (never, former, current), occupational stress (yes/no). Detailed definitions, classification criteria, and data sources for all covariates are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Data management and analysis were conducted using SPSS 23.0 and R 4.4.1. Descriptive statistics were utilized to summarize participant characteristics, with continuous variables presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) based on their distribution, and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages). The association between occupational hazard exposure and incident HDC was primarily evaluated using survival analysis. Kaplan-Meier curves along with log-rank tests were employed to compare cumulative incidence between OHI risk groups over the follow-up period. The strength and independence of this association were quantified through univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models, which estimated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for OHI analyzed as continuous (per-unit and per-standard-deviation) and categorical variables. To rigorously control for confounding factors, a series of regression models with sequential adjustment were constructed: starting with a crude model, followed by progressive adjustment for demographic factors, family history, and lifestyle factors, and finally a fully adjusted model that also included occupational factors and anthropometric measurements. Furthermore, stratified analyses and tests for interaction were performed to examine the consistency of the association across key subgroups, including those defined by sex, age, and lifestyle factors, by introducing multiplicative interaction terms into the Cox models and assessing them with likelihood ratio tests. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics and HDC incidence

To assess potential selection bias due to loss to follow-up, we compared baseline characteristics between participants included in the final prospective analysis (study cohort, n = 5310) and those lost to follow-up (n = 414). The results are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

The two groups were largely comparable on most measured characteristics, including age, sex, ethnicity, marital status, education, income, family history of hypertension or diabetes, smoking status, BMI, waist circumference, physical activity, dietary score (DASH), shift work status, length of employment, and occupational stress (all P > 0.05). A statistically significant difference was only observed in alcohol consumption status (P = 0.026), with a slightly higher proportion of never-drinkers among participants lost to follow-up.

A total of 5310 steel workers (92.6% male, mean age 43.0 ± 8.2 years) were included in the final analysis, with a follow-up rate of 92.77%. By 2024, 595 new HDC cases were identified, yielding a cumulative incidence of 11.2%. The incidence was significantly higher in males, older individuals, those who were single, those with lower education and income, those with a family history of hypertension or diabetes, current smokers/drinkers, those with lower DASH scores, and those who were overweight/obese or had abnormal WC(all P < 0.05; Table 1). Longer length of employment, former or current shift work, and occupational stress were also associated with higher HDC incidence (all P < 0.05).

Development of the Occupational Hazards Index (OHI)

Selection of Occupational Exposure Limits (OELs) for occupational hazards

The OELs used in this study were determined in accordance with the Chinese National Occupational Health Standards: Occupational Exposure Limits for Hazardous Agents in the Workplace–Part 1: Chemical Hazardous Agents (GBZ 2.1–2007) and Occupational Exposure Limits for Hazardous Agents in the Workplace–Part 2: Physical Agents (GBZ 2.2–2007). The selection of specific limit values adhered to the following prioritized principles:

For the majority of chemical agents and for noise, the Permissible Concentration-Time Weighted Average (PC-TWA) was prioritized as the OEL. This limit is designed to protect workers against chronic health effects from repeated long-term exposure over an 8-hour workday and 40-hour workweek. For agents with multiple assigned limits, including PC-TWA, Maximum Allowable Concentration (MAC), or Permissible Concentration-Short Term Exposure Limit (PC-STEL), the PC-TWA remained the primary criterion for evaluation to ensure a consistent approach to assessing chronic health risks. For high temperature, a physical agent, the OEL was individually determined based on the Wet-Bulb Globe Temperature (WBGT) index measured at the worksite, in conjunction with the measured labor intensity and work–rest regimen. The actual labor intensity for each job category was cross-referenced with Table 8 (Sect. 10) of the GBZ 2.2–2007 standard to assign a job-specific OEL for heat stress, which was subsequently used to calculate the individual hazard quotient (HQ).

Based on the principles outlined above, the specific OELs selected for each of the 22 occupational hazards investigated in this study are detailed in Table 2.

Definition and calculation of the Occupational Hazards Index (OHI) for steel workers

The Occupational Hazards Index (OHI) is a quantitative metric developed to assess the combined exposure level to multiple occupational hazards among steel workers. This index synthetically integrates monitoring data of 22 occupational hazards measured onsite for each worker. Constructed based on hazard quotient calculations and the Euclidean norm model, the OHI provides a comprehensive quantitative assessment of the integrated intensity of coexposure to multiple occupational hazards. The formula is as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{O}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{I}=100\times\:\sqrt{\sum\limits_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{n}}{\left(\frac{{\mathrm{C}}_{\mathrm{i}}}{{\mathrm{O}\mathrm{E}\mathrm{L}}_{\mathrm{i}}}\right)}^{2}}$$\end{document}

In the formula, Ci represents the on-site measured concentration or intensity level of occupational hazard “i”, and OELi is the corresponding national Occupational Exposure Limit. The index comprehensively covers all occupational hazards to which each worker is exposed, including heat stress, noise, ultraviolet (UV) radiation, power-frequency electric field, carbon monoxide (CO), dust, nitric oxide (NO), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), manganese and its compounds (Mn), ozone (O3), benzene, toluene, xylene, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), hydrochloric acid (HCl), ammonia (NH3), chromium and its compounds (Cr), sulfuric acid (H2SO4), fluorides (as F), zinc oxide (ZnO), phenol, and calcium oxide (CaO)—22 hazards in total.

Analysis of individual occupational hazards

We examined the univariable associations between each of the 22 individual occupational hazards (modeled by their hazard quotient, HQ) and the risk of HDC. The complete results are presented in Table S4.

Several key patterns emerged: (1) A limited number of prevalent physical and chemical hazards—notably heat, carbon monoxide (CO), total dust, and calcium oxide—demonstrated statistically significant positive associations with HDC risk in unadjusted analyses (all P < 0.05). For example, each unit increase in HQ for total dust was associated with a 93% increased risk of HDC (HR = 1.93, 95% CI: 1.39–2.69).(2) More notably, most chemical agents—including most metals (e.g., manganese, chromium), organic solvents (e.g., benzene, toluene, xylene), and acids—showed no statistically significant associations. For several of these hazards (e.g., nitric oxide, chromium), the hazard ratios could not be reliably estimated due to extremely low exposure levels or few outcome events among the exposed, as indicated by non-estimable (NE) values or implausibly wide confidence intervals. This pattern underscores a critical observation: while a few dominant, high-exposure hazards are individually associated with increased HDC risk, no single agent appears to be solely responsible for the overall disease burden in this cohort. The health risk seems to be dispersed across multiple factors, many of which show only weak or non-measurable effects when examined in isolation.

Dose-response relationship between the occupational hazards index and HDC risk

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis revealed a significant non-linear dose-response relationship between the OHI and the risk of HDC (P for overall association < 0.001; P for non-linearity < 0.001), as shown in Fig. 5. The hazard ratio demonstrated a monotonically increasing trend with rising OHI values. While the risk increase was relatively gradual at OHI values below 102.75, it accelerated markedly beyond this threshold. Based on the inflection point (OHI = 102.75) identified from the RCS curve, the combined occupational hazard exposure levels were categorized into two grades: low-risk (OHI < 102.75) and high-risk (OHI ≥ 102.75).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Stratified analyses by key demographic and lifestyle factors revealed important nuances in the association between high-risk occupational exposure (OHI ≥ 102.75) and HDC incidence, with several significant interaction effects observed (Table S5-S12).

In demographic subgroups, a significant interaction was identified by sex (P for interaction = 0.005). While the association was significant in both genders, the point estimate was substantially higher in females (HR = 3.373, 95% CI: 1.226–9.282) than in males (HR = 1.255, 95% CI: 1.065–1.479). A strong interaction was also observed by age (P for interaction < 0.001), where the association was most pronounced and solely significant among workers aged 40—49 years (HR = 1.882, 95% CI: 1.442–2.457).

Regarding lifestyle factors, significant interactions were found for smoking and drinking status (Both P for interaction < 0.001). The elevated HDC risk associated with high OHI was primarily driven by current smokers (HR = 1.480, 95% CI: 1.197–1.829) and both never (HR = 1.418, 95% CI: 1.127–1.785) and current drinkers (HR = 1.391, 95% CI: 1.099–1.760). Furthermore, the association was significantly modified by body composition. Strong interactions were detected for both BMI (P for interaction < 0.001) and waist circumference status (P for interaction < 0.001), with significantly elevated risks observed exclusively among individuals who were overweight (HR = 1.471, 95% CI: 1.125–1.785), obese (HR = 1.451, 95% CI: 1.074–1.961), or had abnormal waist circumference (HR = 1.350, 95% CI: 1.105–1.648). In contrast, no significant interactions were detected for DASH diet score (P for interaction = 0.085) or physical activity level (P for interaction = 0.005 for the overall test, but the association was significant in both moderate and heavy activity groups).

Discussion

This prospective cohort study aimed to develop a comprehensive composite index—the OHI—to quantitatively assess combined occupational exposure and evaluate its prospective association with an elevated risk of HDC in a large cohort of steelworkers.

Our principal findings are fourfold. First, we successfully constructed the OHI, which synthesizes complex exposure information from 22 physical and chemical hazards into a single, quantifiable metric. Second, we observed a significant, non-linear, positive dose-response relationship between the OHI and HDC incidence, with a critical inflection point at an OHI of 102.75. Third, compared to the low-risk group, workers in the high-risk group (OHI ≥ 102.75) had a 30.8% higher risk of developing HDC, even after comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders. Finally, stratified analyses revealed that this association was particularly pronounced in specific subgroups, including males, middle-aged workers (40–49 years), and individuals with unhealthy lifestyles. Collectively, these findings underscore the critical importance of assessing occupational health risks from a holistic, combined-exposure perspective.

Methodological implications of the Occupational Hazards Index (OHI)

The primary innovation of this study is the development of the OHI. Traditional occupational epidemiology has long been constrained by a “single-hazard” paradigm, which fails to reflect the reality of complex industrial environments where workers are consistently exposed to hazardous mixtures [13–15]. The OHI addresses this fundamental limitation. By integrating 22 different hazards via the Euclidean norm of their hazard quotients, the OHI advances beyond simple categorical or additive models [16]. We selected the Euclidean norm for its toxicological plausibility: by squaring the hazard quotients (HQs) before summation, the index inherently assigns greater weight to higher-intensity exposures, which frequently exhibit non-linear, steeper dose-response relationships with health outcomes [17]. Consequently, the OHI reflects not only the diversity of exposures but, more importantly, the overall magnitude of the exposure burden. The application of a scaling factor of 100 was a pragmatic step to enhance interpretability without influencing statistical outcomes. Moreover, the “high” and “low” risk classification was not based on an arbitrary statistical distribution (e.g., the median) but was directly derived from the dose-response relationship with HDC itself, ensuring that the categories are biologically grounded and clinically relevant. This data-driven approach enhances the OHI’s utility as a practical tool for risk stratification in occupational health practice.

Interpretation of key findings in context of existing evidence

The 30.8% increased HDC risk observed in the high-OHI group provides robust evidence that the health risk in the steel industry is not merely the sum of individual hazards but likely arises from their complex interplay. This finding aligns with, yet substantially extends, previous literature linking isolated factors such as noise [18], shift work [19], and particulate matter [20] to hypertension or diabetes individually. For instance, whereas prior studies indicated that chronic noise exposure elevates blood pressure via stress pathway activation [21] and that polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) from coke production impair insulin secretion [22], our study demonstrates that concurrent exposure to noise, dust, chemicals, and physical stressors poses a substantially greater threat. The identified non-linear dose-response relationship, characterized by a steeper risk increase beyond an OHI of 102.75, strengthens the argument for a causal association and suggests the possibility of a toxicity threshold or synergistic interactions. The sustained separation of the Kaplan-Meier curves throughout follow-up further supports a long-term, cumulative effect of combined occupational exposure on cardiometabolic health.

For several other agents, particularly those with very low or non-detectable exposure levels across the cohort (e.g., fluorides, chromium compounds), the Cox regression models failed to yield reliable or interpretable hazard ratios, indicated by non-estimable (NE) values or implausibly extreme estimates in Table S4. This phenomenon is highly informative. It indicates that for many chemicals in the modern steel plant, individual exposure levels are so low and homogeneous that they fall below the threshold where traditional single-agent epidemiological analysis can discern a clear dose-response relationship. Yet, these agents are still part of the overall exposure mixture. This directly underscores the necessity of a composite index like the OHI, which can integrate these ubiquitous, low-level exposures into a meaningful measure of total burden, rather than dismissing them as “non-detectable” or analytically intractable.

Biological plausibility: pathways linking steel industry exposures to HDC

The pathophysiological pathways linking the complex mixture of occupational hazards in steelmaking, as quantified by the OHI, to HDC are multifactorial and likely interconnected. We posit that the predominant exposures in this setting—notably intense noise, cyclic heat stress, metal-rich particulate matter (e.g., containing manganese and chromium), carbon monoxide, and occupational stressors—converge to disrupt cardiometabolic homeostasis through the following key mechanisms:

Systemic Inflammation and Oxidative Stress Induced by Particulate and Metal Exposures [23]. The steel production environment is rich in complex dusts and metal fumes (e.g., from manganese, chromium, and zinc oxide operations). When inhaled, these particulates, especially the fine and ultrafine fractions, can induce pulmonary oxidative stress and local inflammation [24]. This is not a generic response; the presence of bioactive transition metals in steel dust can catalyze the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) via Fenton-like reactions [25]. This primary pulmonary insult can spill over into a state of chronic, low-grade systemic inflammation, characterized by elevated circulating cytokines [e.g., interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α)]. Systemic inflammation is a well-established cornerstone in the pathogenesis of both insulin resistance (a precursor to diabetes) and endothelial dysfunction (a precursor to hypertension) [26]. Thus, the specific metallo-particulate profile of steelwork may provide a continuous inflammatory stimulus that underlies both components of HDC.

Dysregulation of the Neuroendocrine Stress Axis by Physical and Psychosocial Stressors. The steel mill is an environment of chronic, high-decibel noise and intermittent but severe heat stress, compounded by the pressures of production targets and shift work. This triad constitutes a potent, chronic activator of the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. Acute noise and heat are direct physiological stressors, while organizational factors contribute to sustained psychological stress [27]. Chronic activation leads to elevated levels of catecholamines (e.g., norepinephrine) and cortisol. Catecholamines directly increase heart rate, cardiac output, and peripheral vascular resistance, raising blood pressure [28]. Concurrently, both catecholamines and cortisol promote hepatic gluconeogenesis, impair insulin signaling in skeletal muscle, and contribute to visceral adiposity, thereby fostering hyperglycemia and insulin resistance [29]. The synergistic effect of these concurrent physical and organizational stressors, endemic to steelmaking, provides a plausible pathway for the simultaneous development of hypertension and diabetes.

Autonomic Nervous System Imbalance Driven by Acoustic and Chemical Exposures. Beyond its role as a stressor, chronic occupational noise exposure has been directly linked to impaired cardiovascular autonomic regulation, typically manifesting as reduced heart rate variability (HRV)—a marker of shifted autonomic balance toward sympathetic dominance and reduced parasympathetic tone [30]. This imbalance is a recognized contributor to hypertension. Furthermore, certain chemical agents present in the mill atmosphere, such as carbon monoxide, can impair oxygen delivery and may also indirectly affect autonomic function. An autonomic nervous system persistently tilted toward sympathetic overactivity creates a pro-hypertensive and pro-diabetogenic milieu, facilitating the development of HDC.

Synthesis and Interaction of Pathways: Crucially, these pathways are not mutually exclusive. For instance, systemic inflammation (Pathway 1) can sensitize the HPA axis (Pathway 2) and exacerbate autonomic dysfunction (Pathway 3). The OHI, by integrating exposures that drive all these pathways (e.g., dust/metals for inflammation, noise/heat for neuroendocrine stress, noise/CO for autonomic imbalance), effectively captures the total physiological “allostatic load” borne by the steelworker. This persistent, multi-system strain likely explains the accelerated risk of HDC observed with higher OHI scores, moving beyond the effect of any single agent.

Public health and practical implications

The stratified analyses offer critical insights for refining targeted prevention strategies. The significantly stronger association observed among males and individuals aged 40–49 identifies these as particularly vulnerable subgroups. The heightened effect in middle-aged workers may signify a critical window during which cumulative occupational exposures coincide with age-related physiological decline, thereby amplifying susceptibility. Furthermore, significant interactions with smoking, obesity, and unhealthy diet suggest that occupational and personal risk factors do not act in isolation but may exert synergistic effects. These findings highlight the need for integrated workplace health programs that combine rigorous hazard control with tailored lifestyle interventions, especially in high-risk subgroups.

The Occupational Hazards Index (OHI) and its empirically derived risk threshold (OHI ≥ 102.75) are not merely research metrics but can serve as actionable tools for a paradigm shift in occupational health management—from reactive, compliance-driven monitoring to proactive, risk-based prevention. To realize this potential, we propose a structured, four-phase implementation framework for real-world application.

Phase 1: Comprehensive Exposure Profiling and OHI Calculation. This foundational phase involves integrating OHI methodology into routine occupational hygiene surveillance. For each job role or workshop, standardized monitoring of the relevant hazards (the full set of 22 or a locally adapted core set) is conducted. Using a simplified spreadsheet tool or dedicated software, individual hazard quotients (HQs) and the composite OHI score are calculated automatically. This process should be repeated periodically (e.g., biennially) or when significant process changes occur, creating a dynamic exposure registry.

Phase 2: Risk Stratification and Resource Prioritization. The calculated OHI scores are mapped against the validated risk threshold of 102.75. Work units (departments, teams, or specific job titles) are thereby classified into “Priority for Intervention” (OHI ≥ 102.75) and “Monitor and Maintain” (OHI < 102.75) categories. This binary, evidence-based stratification enables health and safety managers to direct limited resources (engineering controls, personal protective equipment budgets, inspector time) efficiently toward the areas of greatest need, maximizing the return on investment for health protection.

Phase 3: Diagnostic Analysis and Targeted Intervention Design. A key advantage of the OHI over single-hazard metrics is its diagnostic capability. For units in the “Priority for Intervention” category, a decomposition of the OHI score is performed to identify the driving hazards. This pinpoints whether the high risk stems predominantly from physical factors (e.g., noise and heat), chemical agents (e.g., CO and dust), or a combination. Interventions can then be precisely tailored. For example, a unit with a noise- and heat-driven OHI would benefit most from combined engineering controls (acoustic enclosures) and administrative measures (optimized work-rest schedules), rather than a generic intervention.

Phase 4: Integration with Health Surveillance and Evaluation. The OHI framework should be linked to the company’s health surveillance program. Workers in high-OHI units are flagged for enhanced cardiometabolic monitoring, such as annual (versus standard biennial) checks of blood pressure, fasting glucose, and lipids. Concurrently, these workers can be offered targeted health promotion (e.g., on-site nutrition counseling, stress management workshops) to build resilience. Finally, the impact of interventions must be evaluated by tracking changes in OHI scores and, ultimately, in health outcome rates (e.g., HDC incidence) over time, closing the loop on a continuous improvement cycle.

Interpretation of the subgroup finding in female workers

Our stratified analysis revealed a notably higher point estimate for the association between high-risk OHI and HDC among female steelworkers (HR = 3.373, 95% CI: 1.226–9.282) compared to their male counterparts (HR = 1.255, 95% CI: 1.065–1.479), with a significant interaction (P for interaction = 0.005). This finding warrants cautious and nuanced interpretation due to important contextual factors. First, the female subgroup was relatively small (n = 392, 7.4% of the cohort), with only 16 incident HDC cases. This limited sample size results in low statistical precision, as reflected in the exceptionally wide confidence interval, and makes the point estimate unstable and prone to chance variation. Therefore, this result should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive.

Notwithstanding the statistical limitations, if future studies with larger female samples replicate this pattern, several biologically and socially plausible explanations could be explored. Physiologically, potential gender differences in hormonal profiles (e.g., estrogen’s role in vascular and metabolic regulation) [31], body composition (e.g., fat distribution affecting toxicokinetics of lipophilic chemicals), or susceptibility to specific stressors (e.g., differential autonomic or inflammatory responses to noise/heat) might modulate the impact of occupational exposures [32]. This observation powerfully underscores a critical gap in occupational health research—the frequent under-representation of women in heavy industry cohorts. It highlights an urgent need for future studies to intentionally recruit adequate numbers of female workers to robustly investigate gender-specific risks and protections, ensuring that occupational health guidelines are equitable and evidence-based for all.

Strengths and limitations

Key strengths of this study include its prospective design, large sample size, prolonged follow-up, high retention rate, and the development of a novel quantitative composite index for occupational exposure based on standardized measurements. In addition, we adjusted for a comprehensive set of potential confounders encompassing demographic, lifestyle, familial, and occupational factors, strengthening the validity of our findings.

Several limitations must also be considered. First, the OHI assigned equal weight to all hazards, which may not accurately reflect their relative toxicities; future studies could investigate weighted models based on toxicological potency.

Second, exposure assessment was conducted only at baseline, and potential changes in job roles or exposure controls during the 7-year follow-up were not captured. This may have resulted in non-differential misclassification and an underestimation of the true effect size. Exposure assessment was conducted only at baseline; changes in job roles or controls during follow-up could have led to misclassification of long-term exposure. In this prospective design, such misclassification is likely non-differential, meaning any exposure misassignment is independent of future HDC status. For ordered or continuous exposures, non-differential misclassification generally biases effect estimates toward the null. Thus, if some workers with high baseline OHI later experienced reduced exposure, their continued classification as high-risk would attenuate observed risk differences. Consequently, our hazard ratios (e.g., HR = 1.308 for high OHI) are likely conservative underestimates of the true association. The relative stability of core steelmaking processes and their inherent hazards over a 7‑year period partly mitigates this concern, though individual-level changes cannot be ruled out.

Third, despite extensive adjustment, residual confounding from unmeasured or imprecisely measured variables (e.g., detailed dietary intake, non-occupational psychosocial factors) cannot be ruled out. Finally, as the study population was drawn from a single steel company in Northern China, the generalizability of our findings to other settings may be limited, although the core production processes and associated hazards are representative of the global steel industry.

Conclusions

By developing and prospectively validating the Occupational Hazards Index (OHI), this study provides a quantitative framework for assessing combined exposures and establishes their significant association with hypertension-diabetes comorbidity in steelworkers. Our evidence thereby supports a fundamental shift toward holistic risk assessment models in occupational health. Moving forward, the primary prevention of chronic diseases in this workforce will depend on the implementation of strategies that effectively mitigate the complex mixture of hazards captured by the OHI.
