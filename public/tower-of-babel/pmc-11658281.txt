Relationship between state anxiety, heart rate variability, and shooting performance in adolescent shooters
Authors: Chaochao Zhao, Kun Wang, Dan Li, Yan Li, Zhuo Wang, Yongsen Liu, Tingran Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveBy comparing the differences in pre-competition state anxiety, inter-competition heart rate variability (HRV), and shooting performance of young athletes of different grades, and revealing the correlation between these variables, it provides a potential basis for improving athletes’ shooting performance.MethodsA total of 45 adolescent shooters were selected in Chongqing, China, and completed 40 effective shots at self-pace in real shooting scenes. The pre-competition state anxiety, inter-competition HRV, and shooting performance of the athletes were monitored and evaluated respectively.Results(1) There was a significant difference in the pre-match state anxiety of the adolescent shooters, which showed that the cognitive state anxiety and physical state anxiety of the first-level athletes were significantly lower than those of the second-level athletes, while the state confidence was significantly higher than that of the second-level athletes. (2) There were significant differences in HRV of athletes with different sports levels during the competition, showing that the root mean square differences of the standard deviation (RMSSD), total power (TP), and high-frequency of normal (HFn) of the first-level athletes were significantly higher than those of second-level athletes, while low-frequency of normal (LFn) was significantly lower than the latter. (3) The shooting performance of first-level athletes was significantly better than that of second-level athletes. (4) Among adolescent shooters of different grades, there was a significant correlation between pre-competition state anxiety, inter-competition HRV, and shooting performance. The negative dimension of state anxiety was positively correlated with sympathetic activity in HRV, negatively correlated with parasympathetic activity, and negatively correlated with shooting performance. The positive dimension of state anxiety was negatively correlated with sympathetic activity in HRV, positively correlated with parasympathetic activity, and significantly positively correlated with motor performance.ConclusionAdolescent shooters with higher sports levels usually have better pre-competition mental state and emotional regulation ability, and could better maintain autonomic nerve dynamic balance and focus attention resources on shooting tasks during the competition, so it was easier to achieve good shooting performance. Meanwhile, pre-competition state anxiety and HRV during competition could be used as objective indicators to monitor and evaluate the competitive state of young shooters.

Introduction

Shooting is a typical closed precision sport, it requires athletes to have high accuracy, stability, concentration, and other abilities, and in training or competition to maintain physical and mental consistency, technical movements before and after to maintain a high degree of unity [1]. Therefore, shooting sports put forward extremely high requirements for athletes’ emotional stability and psychological stress adaptability [2], which was also an important basis for achieving excellent shooting performance. Shooting involves cognitive tasks such as target orientation and attention concentration. It requires cooperative activities of sensory and motor systems, local processing of attention, continuous monitoring of external stimuli, and flexible switching of focus of attention [3, 4]. However, due to the relatively high technical requirements required by shooting sports, athletes’ shooting performance was susceptible to many factors, including psychological regulation ability and physiological stress state.

Anxiety usually refers to the emotional state of fear, fear, or worry produced by individuals in the face of potentially threatening and challenging events [5]. It is often manifested as tension, unease, worry, anxiety, and the activation or awakening of the autonomic nervous system, which could be divided into two types: transient emotional state (state anxiety) and anxiety tendency as a personality trait (trait anxiety) [6]. In the field of sports psychology, state anxiety is considered to be closely related to shooting performance. State anxiety is one of the most common pre-competition psychological states in competitive games, which is mainly due to the emotional reaction of athletes who do not adapt to the stressful situation [7], it is believed that it could activate the physiological state of athletes, but inhibit their behavior performance, which is easy to cause the athletes’ performance disorder at the critical moment in the competition [8]. For example, high match state anxiety could interfere with volleyball players’ serving performance and adversely affect athletic performance [9], meanwhile, it will also have different degrees of impact on the performance of shooters [10, 11]. This may be related to the fact that anxiety damages the top-down goal-directed attention system and makes the bottom-up stimulus-driven attention system dominate, thus breaking the balance between the two attention systems [12]. However, Robinson et al. [13, 14] pointed out that improving state anxiety was positively correlated with better attentional control performance, and state anxiety improves the accuracy of inhibited responses in sustained attention-to-response tasks. The repeated study of Grillon et al. [15] also obtained the same result. A study points out that state anxiety could improve arousal levels and motivation, and a low level of state anxiety may compensate for ego depletion, while an appropriate level of state anxiety could prompt individuals to stabilize their attention on performance, to observe the adverse effects of state anxiety on athletic performance, it may be necessary to induce a high degree of state anxiety [16]. The results suggested that state anxiety is closely related to the athletic performance of shooters, but there may be a dose effect of anxiety degree.

Meanwhile, if the autonomic nerve reaches a balanced state, it will be conducive to the implementation of training and the performance of the game; on the contrary, if the imbalance or the activity is reduced for a long time, the training effect and sports performance will be decreased [17], which indicates that the physiological stress level such as autonomic nerve activity will also affect sports performance. The effective monitoring of autonomic nerves can reflect the cognitive resources of athletes in the shooting process [18, 19]. Among them, the heart rate variability (HRV) was an effective non-invasive indicator to monitor and evaluate cardiac autonomic activity [20], it quantifies the HRV in the R-R interval or each cardiac cycle, reflects autonomic nerve activity, and quantitatively evaluates cardiac sympathetic and vagal tone and balance [21, 22]. The neurovisceral integration model suggests that HRV can be used as a biomarker for self-regulation and environmental adaptation, and parasympathetic nervous system (PNS) -mediated high HRV resting level was correlated with executive function (such as cognitive performance and emotional regulation) [23]. Moreover, the increase in PNS activity was closely related to athletes’ shooting performance [19]. Individuals with high HRV (that is increased elasticity or activity of the parasympathetic nerve) have better performance and impulse control in cognitive and attentional tasks [24], as well as better autonomic nerve activity, it could respond quickly to internal and external stimuli, while lower HRV shows poor autonomic nervous activity and decreased ability to cope with changing stimuli and pressures [25]. Therefore, HRV may also be one of the important factors affecting the athletic performance of shooting athletes. Meanwhile, in the targeting phase of shooting activities, there were usually different psychophysiological responses between elite shooters and novices, and the attention level and shooting performance of the former were significantly higher than that of the latter, while the latter may have higher anxiety levels [26]. It should be noted that, as a performance accuracy project of leading skill and mental ability, the psychological monitoring and adjustment of the mental state of shooters have gradually become the focus of research in related fields [27]. Based on this, this study intends to focus on psychological regulation and physiological stress and take sports grades as the classification basis to investigate whether there were differences in pre-competition state anxiety, inter-competition HRV, and shooting performance of young shooters of different sports levels, as well as the internal correlation between variables, to provide a reference for improving shooting performance.

Methods

Participants

A total of 45 young air pistol athletes were recruited from Shooting Sports Technical School in Chongqing, China to participate in this study, and the G*power statistical test power of this sample size under the experimental design of this study was 0.83, and the participant’s average age was 16.78 ± 1.72 years old, average height was 1.66 ± 0.18 m, and the average weight was 52.19 ± 4.88 kg. There were 32 boys and 13 girls, and there were 20 elite athletes (that is first-level, and 5.35 ± 1.60 years of shooting experience) and 25 non-elite athletes (that is second-level, and 3.07 ± 1.26 years of shooting experience). It should be noted that athletes at both levels were young professional athletes with more than two years of shooting experience and were paid by the state or sports administration. Among them, the first-level athletes refer to those who have won the ranking in national or provincial competitions, and the shooting results in the competition have reached the standard of China’s unified first-level shooting level (men: a total of 60 shots, the score reached 567 points; women: a total of 60 shots, the score reached 565 points). The second-level athletes were those who have won the ranking in national or provincial competitions, and the shooting results in the competition have reached the standard of the second-level shooting level recognized by China (men: a total of 60 shots, a score of 560 points; women: a total of 60 shots, a score of 558 points) [28].

Inclusion criteria: (1) Professional athletes (in the professional period and have not yet retired). (2) All were right-handed and used the right eye to aim at the target. (3) Engaged in the standard 10 m air pistol project. (4) The sports level has reached the level of second-level or above. (5) Volunteer to participate in this study. Exclusion criteria: (1) Have a mental illness or history of mental illness. (2) Have heart disease or a history of cardiovascular disease. (3) Abnormal vision or corrected vision and hearing. All participants voluntarily participated in this study, so there was no need to pay remuneration, and understood the experimental procedure and purpose of the whole study, and signed informed consent. Meanwhile, the study involved minors (under 16 years of age), and before the study, we also obtained statements of consent from parents and/or legal guardians for their participation in the study.

Research procedures

In this study, the 45 participants who were screened for inclusion were asked to abstain from taking drugs or nutrients (such as caffeine or alcohol products or beta-blockers) that could affect cardiovascular exercise response for 48 h before the experiment, and maintain a normal sleep schedule. All participants trained according to the regular training schedule (about 2 h of shooting training in the morning, one hour of physical training, and 30 min of psychological stress training in the afternoon, etc.) from Monday to Friday. Meanwhile, to conform to the practice in the real shooting scene, the order of participants’ testing is determined by drawing lots, and then the corresponding testing and shooting tasks are completed in sequence.

Firstly, all participants were required to complete questionnaires (such as personal information and state anxiety scale) before the shooting task, to grasp the pre-competition anxiety state of each participant. Second, to capture the HRV of the participant during the firing, a single electrocardiogram (ECG) electrode with Ag/AgCl was attached to the participant’s right midclavicular line and left anterior axillary line, respectively, before the firing task began, using dual leads of V4R and V5, and the electrodes were connected to the heart rate variable port. The participants then freely practiced 5 rounds with their pistols to adapt to the test environment. After 5 rounds have been fired, clear the data on the electronic display and enter the formal firing link. Finally, in the whole shooting process, the period was divided into 5 min (the HRV recording cycle was 5 min). Participants carry out the shooting task every 5 min and can control the shooting frequency according to their habits until the number of effective shots reaches 40 [29, 30]. The results of each stroke were fed back to the athletes in real-time via an electronic display, and the HRV of the participants was continuously collected during this process. There was a one-minute interval between each period. Participants could adjust their competitive state in time, and the next round will be held immediately after the rest. The test lasted about 40 min for each participant.

Measure tools

Heart rate variability monitor

The heart rate variability monitoring system (Healink-R211B, HeaLink Ltd, China) was used to collect ECG data of participants during the period from the preparation period to the time of firing, and the bandwidth of the device was 0.5–40 Hz, and the sampling frequency was 400 Hz. A single-use ECG with Ag/AgCl was used to attach V4R and V5 dual leads to the right midclavicular line and left anterior axillary line, respectively. During the test, subjects maintained normal breathing rhythm and normal shooting movements, avoided large body shaking, and kept the surrounding environment as quiet as possible, and the test lasted for 5 min. The main acquisition indicators included time-domain indicators: standard deviation of normal-to-normal intervals (SDNN), and root mean square differences of the standard deviation (RMSSD). Frequency domain indicators: total power (TP), low-frequency of normal (LFn), high-frequency of normal (HFn), and low high-frequency ratio (LF/HF). The device was easy to carry, could reflect the subject’s electrocardiogram in real-time, and synchronously import into the computer port.

Competitive state anxiety Inventory-2 (CSAI-2)

The Competitive State Anxiety Inventory compiled by Martens et al. [31] and revised by Zhu et al. [32] according to Chinese norms was adopted. The scale has 27 questions in total, and adopts Likert’s 4-point scoring method to investigate athletes’ state anxiety of sports competition from three dimensions, namely “cognitive state anxiety”, “somatic state anxiety” and “state confidence”. The three dimensions were scored independently. The higher the score, the higher the cognitive state anxiety, somatic state anxiety, and state confidence. According to the internal consistency test, the Cronbach α coefficients of cognitive state anxiety, somatic state anxiety, and state confidence were 0.84, 0.87, and 0.82, respectively, and the Cronbach α coefficients of the whole scale were 0.85. It showed that the scale has good measurement reliability.

Measurement of shooting performance

Each shooting performance of the athlete was recorded by a professional electronic target and monitor, and the shooting results were reflected in the range of 0 rings to 10.9 rings (10.9 rings was the best score, namely the bull’s eye), respectively. When the bullet was of the electronic target paper, there would be no corresponding record on the monitor, so the shot was regarded as 0 points.

Data analysis

ECG viewer software (HeaLink Ltd., Bengbu, China) was used to extract and preprocess HRV data at R-R Interval (RRI), and Heartlink software (HeaLink Ltd., Bengbu, China) was used for statistical analysis and automatic correction. The SPSS statistical software (SPSS21.0, IBM, USA) was used for the final processing and analysis of all data in this study. An independent sample t-test was used to analyze the differences in HRV, state anxiety, and shooting performance of participants, and the Pearson correlation analysis was used to test the relationship between HRV, state anxiety, and shooting performance. When the data did not conform to the spherical test, the Greenhouse & Geisser method was adopted for correction, and the significance level of the statistical test was set as p < 0.05.

Discussion

Difference analysis

Before or during the competition, shooters often have pre-competition state anxiety, such as uneasiness, unclear thinking, and lack of confidence, and when the anxiety exceeds the level they can bear, it will cause adverse physical and mental reactions and operational performance [33]. Excessive anxiety in pre-competition will affect the normal physiological functions of athletes, resulting in decreased excitability during competition, which is not conducive to the performance of sports [34]. However, this study found that there were significant differences in the pre-competition state anxiety of athletes with different levels, namely, the cognitive state anxiety and somatic state anxiety of first-level athletes were significantly lower than those of second-level athletes, while the state confidence of second-level athletes was significantly higher. These results suggested that the athletes with a higher level of sports have a better psychological state of competition, and could timely adjust and effectively overcome the anxiety caused by rivals, environment, and pressure, which may be the reason why they have more experience in competition or higher level of sports skills.

Meanwhile, the HRV of athletes of different levels was also significantly different during the competition, which showed that the RMSSD, TP, and HFn of first-level athletes were significantly higher than those of second-level athletes, while LFn was significantly lower than the latter. This suggested that parasympathetic nerves dominate autonomic activity in the former and that the heart rate was correspondingly lower during shooting. Research points out that the HRV index can reflect the psychological and physiological state of athletes before the competition to a certain extent, and provide a basis and reference for coaches to conduct scientific regulation and control [35, 36], SDNN, TP, LF/HF, LF, and HF could also be used as the objective basis to evaluate the state of shooters before and during competitions [37]. Such as TP represents the overall activity of the autonomic nervous system, reflecting the autonomic nervous system’s ability to regulate the body state, and the higher the TP value, the greater the HRV. Within a certain range, the greater HRV fluctuation indicates that the autonomic nervous system and cardiovascular system of the human body have good adaptability to the outside world; otherwise, it indicates that the autonomic nervous system lacks certain adaptability to changes in the external environment [38]. Individuals with high HRV have greater emotional and self-regulation abilities in cognitive and attentional tasks [23, 24]. Meanwhile, the higher the LF value was, the stronger the autonomic nervous system can double dominate the heart activity, in which the sympathetic nerve activity was dominant [39], and the heart rate was usually higher at this time. On the contrary, HF and RMSSD mainly reflect parasympathetic nerve activity, and the higher the value was, the more parasympathetic nerve activity was dominant [40], and the heart rate was usually low at this time. The shooting was an external attention-oriented sport, so it was necessary to guide the attention to the information related to the external target during the execution of the action [41]. According to the participation-rejection hypothesis, when athletes accept the external environmental stimulus, the heartbeat will decrease [42], and cardiac deceleration limits the effect of medulla oblong inhibition on the cortex [43], thus promoting the attentional process of extrinsic stimuli [44]. Moreover, this study also found that the shooting performance of first-level athletes is significantly better than that of second-level athletes, which indicates that the higher the competitive level or more experienced shooters are usually more likely to achieve better shooting performance, which is not only related to their shooting skills but also may be related to the psychological and physiological state before and during the shooting.

Correlation analysis

The results of this study found that there was a significant correlation between state anxiety, HRV, and athletic performance among young shooters of different grades. Among them, the negative dimension of state anxiety (such as cognitive state anxiety and somatic state anxiety) has a positive correlation with the sympathetic activity (such as LFn) in HRV, a negative correlation with the parasympathetic activity (such as RMSSD and HFn), and a significant negative correlation with athletic performance. The positive dimension of state anxiety (such as state confidence) was negatively correlated with sympathetic activity (such as LFn) in HRV, positively correlated with parasympathetic activity (such as RMSSD and HFn), and significantly positively correlated with athletic performance. Pre-competition anxiety was likely to lead to athletes’ attention stability, technical movements were not in place, shooting rhythm disorders, and other adverse phenomena [33]. On the contrary, athletes with low pre-competition anxiety will perform better in competition [45]. According to the theory of cognitive interference, the worrying state caused by anxiety occupies cognitive resources and will lead to the decline of operational performance [46], while individuals in the anxious state were more inclined to pay attention to threatening stimuli, and were more likely to interpret pressure as an adverse factor to performance [47, 48]. Meanwhile, when an individual is in a state of tension and anxiety, parasympathetic nerve activity will be inhibited and sympathetic nerve activity will increase. At this time, the HF value reflecting parasympathetic nervous system activity will decrease, and LF or LF/HF representing the sympathetic nervous system activity will increase [49]. The dynamic balance and activity level of the autonomic nervous system were the main factors affecting athletic performance [37, 50], and a high PNS-mediated resting level of HRV was associated with cognitive performance and self-regulation [23, 51]. The mental load brought by cognitive state anxiety and somatic state anxiety will increase the arousal level of athletes, excited sympathetic nerves, and the secretion of catecholamines [52], which is not conducive to shooting performance. However, this study did not find a significant relationship between LF/HF and athletic performance and state anxiety, which may be related to the technical level of adolescent athletes.

In addition, whether it was an average athlete or an excellent athlete, it was inevitable to have state anxiety and other emotions in the process of competition, and the difference lies in the degree of anxiety was different. It was found that proper state anxiety can make athletes concentrate their attention, and improve their thinking and reaction speed; however, if the anxiety is too serious, the competitive level of athletes will be greatly reduced [7]. Zhu et al. [53] used a reverse skipping task to test the relationship between state anxiety and individual inhibition and switching function and found that inhibition and switching function were both affected under high state anxiety. Therefore, athletes are more likely to get good results only if they maintain an appropriate state of anxiety level [54, 55]. The results suggested that the shooters with higher competitive levels were better able to regulate their emotional state before the competition and usually showed lower pre-competition state anxiety. Meanwhile, it was easier to maintain good autonomic homeostatic balance (the parasympathetic activity is dominant) during the competition, which helps them to lower their heart rate during the shooting process and effectively focus their attention resources on external shooting task cues, which ultimately contributes to good shooting performance. However, a study has also pointed out that when sympathetic nerve activity dominates, the competitive performance of extreme athletes can be improved [56]. This difference may be caused by differences in sports, that is, extreme sports require athletes to have excellent rapid reaction ability and attention coordination ability, but shooting sports require strong attention control and emotional stability. Therefore, the shooting performance not only depends on the skill level of the shooters themselves but also is affected by the psychological state and cardiac autonomic nerves before and during the competition.

Limitation analysis

This study systematically investigated the difference and correlation of pre-competition state anxiety, inter-competition HRV, and shooting performance of shooters of different levels, which has reference value for effectively evaluating and improving the shooting performance of young shooters. However, there were still many limitations in the current research: First, this study mainly investigated the influence of athletes’ pre-competition state anxiety and inter-competition HRV on shooting performance, but there were many other factors that affect the performance, such as competition season, the competition experience of the athlete, and resting heart rate of athletes before competition. These indicators or variables can be appropriately increased or controlled in the future. Second, this study was a preliminary study, which only confirmed the correlation between state anxiety, HRV, and shooting performance of shooting athletes. However, whether there is a causal correlation between these major variables is still unknown, and further studies are needed to provide more direct evidence. Moreover, this study confirmed the positive correlation between higher parasympathetic nerve activity and better shooting performance of athletes during shooting, but insufficient evidence was obtained to support the relationship between sympathetic nerve activity and shooting performance, which needs to be further explored in subsequent studies. Finally, this study focuses on the comparison of differences in pre-competition state anxiety, inter-competition HRV, and shooting performance of shooters of different levels. However, due to the small number of female athletes, gender differences were not compared, and it is impossible to determine whether there are significant gender differences in pre-competition state anxiety, inter-competition HRV, and shooting performance of athletes.

Conclusions

There were significant differences in pre-competition state anxiety, inter-competition HRV, and shooting performance of young shooters. Among them, athletes with higher athletic levels have better pre-competition psychological state and higher HRV levels during the competition, which was reflected in that parasympathetic nerve activity was more dominant in cardiac autonomic nerve activity, which was more conducive to maintaining autonomic dynamic balance and focusing attention resources on shooting tasks, so it was easier to achieve good shooting performance. In addition, there was a significant correlation between pre-competition state anxiety, inter-competition HRV, and shooting performance among young shooters of different grades. Therefore, both pre-competition state anxiety and inter-competition HRV can be used as objective indicators to monitor the competitive state of young shooters.
