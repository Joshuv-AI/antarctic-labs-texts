Subcutaneous apomorphine-induced pneumonitis: An uncommon pulmonary adverse event
Authors: Enrique Barrueco-Otero, Vanesa Hidalgo-Sierra, Patricia Torres Piñero, Isabel González Fernández, Laura Gil Pintor, María Bartol Sánchez, María Jesús Corbi Cobo-Losey, Carlos Montilla, Miguel Ángel Hernández-Mezquita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionApomorphine is a potent dopaminergic agonist widely used in the management of motor fluctuations in advanced Parkinson's disease. Although generally well tolerated, rare pulmonary adverse drug reactions (ADRs) may occur. We report a case of probable apomorphine-induced pneumonitis associated with subcutaneous administration.Case presentationA 62-year-old Caucasian male with multiple comorbidities presented to the emergency department with headache, non-productive cough, central chest pain, fever, and dyspnea over the past 48 hours (3 weeks after initiation of subcutaneous apomorphin). Chest X-ray revealed bilateral hilar infiltrates and basal consolidation. CT scan showed organizing pneumonia (OP)-like pattern. Extensive microbiological evaluation of bronchoalveolar lavage and bronchial aspirate samples, including bacterial, fungal, mycobacterial, viral, and atypical pathogen testing, was negative. Lack of response to broad-spectrum antibiotics raised the suspicion of drug-induced pneumonitis due to subcutaneous apomorphine. Antibiotics were discontinued, and corticosteroid therapy (methylprednisolone 40 mg/day) was initiated with gradual tapering resulted in rapid clinical improvement and complete radiological resolution within one month.ConclusionThe temporal relationship between apomorphine initiation and symptom onset, compatible clinical and radiological findings, extensive microbiological exclusion of infectious causes, and favorable response after drug withdrawal and corticosteroid therapy supported the diagnosis of probable apomorphine-induced pneumonitis.

Introduction

Advanced Parkinson's disease often requires complex treatment strategies due to decreased efficacy of standard oral therapies. Apomorphine, a potent dopaminergic agonist administered via intermittent subcutaneous injections or continuous subcutaneous infusion, offers an effective alternative [1,2]. However, its safety profile necessitates careful clinical monitoring [1].

Cases of retroperitoneal fibrosis, pulmonary infiltrates, pleural effusion, pleural thickening, and cardiac valvulopathy have been reported in patients treated with ergot-derived dopaminergic agents. It remains unclear whether non-ergot dopamine agonists, such as apomorphine hydrochloride, can induce these reactions.

Additionally, the Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC) for apomorphine states that pulmonary fibrosis or pleural effusion are potential but extremely rare adverse reactions that may sometimes require drug withdrawal and corticosteroid therapy [5].

Post-marketing reports have also described immune-mediated pulmonary toxicity, including fibrotic or inflammatory changes in the lung parenchyma and mediastinal tissues without clear infectious etiology [6].

Case Presentation

We report the case of a 62-year-old, Caucasian, male, with a history of type 1 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, obesity, obstructive sleep apnea syndrome, and Parkinson's disease diagnosed 10 years earlier and treated with Apomorphine 50 mg/24 hours. The patient presented to the emergency department with several days of headache, non-productive cough, central pleuritic chest pain, and fever and dyspnea over the preceding 48 hours. Physical examination revealed bibasilar crackles on pulmonary auscultation, without wheezing or rhonchi.

Initial chest X-ray (Fig. 1) showed bilateral hilar infiltrates and basal consolidation. Laboratory tests showed: glucose 372 mg/dL; urea 84 mg/dL; creatinine 1,33 mg/dL; LDH 303 IU/L; troponin 17,7 ng/L; proBNP 0,28 ng/mL; hemoglobin 12,5 g/dL; leukocytes 8530/μL; neutrophils 6560/μL; platelets 210,000/μL. Blood and urine cultures were negative. Arterial blood gas analysis showed hypoxemic respiratory failure (pH 7,31; PO2 54 mmHg; pCO2 48 mmHg). The patient was admitted with a diagnosis of community-acquired pneumonia and respiratory failure. Despite empirical antibiotic therapy with levofloxacin and ceftriaxone and supplemental oxygen (FiO2 28%), the clinical course worsened. Bronchoscopy was unremarkable. Extensive microbiological evaluation of bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) and bronchial aspirate samples (BAS) was negative, including bacterial, fungal, and mycobacterial cultures; galactomannan detection; Pneumocystis jirovecii PCR; and multiplex respiratory PCR testing for atypical bacteria and respiratory viruses. No infectious etiology was identified. Flow cytometry analysis of BAL fluid revealed 56% macrophages, 32% lymphocytes, 10% neutrophils, and <5% eosinophils. The CD4/CD8 ratio was <1; findings consistent with an inflammatory process.

Due to progressive respiratory deterioration despite extended antibiotic coverage and FiO2 up to 35%, pulmonology consultation was obtained. Suspecting drug-induced pneumonitis related to subcutaneous apomorphine, the medication was discontinued, and intravenous corticosteroids were initiated. Fever resolved, and clinical and laboratory parameters improved (O2 saturation 95% on room air) in two days.

The patient was discharged with the diagnosis of probable drug-induced pneumonitis secondary to subcutaneous apomorphine. Following withdrawal of the suspected drug, oral methylprednisolone was initiated at 40 mg/day with gradual tapering (40-32-16-8) to 4 mg/day over the following four weeks, according to clinical and radiological response. Outpatient follow-up was scheduled one month later.

At the follow-up visit, the patient was asymptomatic. Chest X-ray (Fig. 4) showed near-complete resolution of previous infiltrates. Pulmonary function tests (spirometry and arterial blood gas) were normal, confirming the suspected diagnosis of apomorphine-induced pneumonitis.Fig. 4At the follow-up visit, the patient was asymptomatic. Discussion

The therapeutic efficacy of apomorphine must be balanced with its tolerability. Gastrointestinal and cardiovascular side effects are usually manageable with antiemetic prophylaxis (e.g., domperidone) and gradual dose titration [1,4]. However, psychiatric and behavioral complications warrant close monitoring, especially in patients with underlying vulnerabilities [5]. Local reactions can be minimized through proper injection technique and site rotation [2,4].

The patient was receiving multiple chronic medications; however, apomorphine was the only recently introduced drug before symptom onset. While the contribution of other agents cannot be completely excluded, the temporal relationship supports apomorphine as the most likely causative agent.

Pulmonary adverse events such as pneumonitis, though rare, must be considered in patients with unexplained respiratory symptoms during apomorphine therapy. Early recognition and management, including drug withdrawal and corticosteroids, are crucial for favorable outcomes, and in the absence of a lung biopsy that would establish a definitive diagnosis, the rapid clinical improvement after discontinuation of the drug can support a presumptive diagnosis with high probability.

The exact pathogenic mechanism remains unclear; however, a possible immune-mediated inflammatory reaction may be involved, similar to other forms of drug-induced interstitial lung disease. Unlike ergot-derived dopamine agonists, which are associated with pleuropulmonary fibrosis, apomorphine may trigger an idiosyncratic hypersensitivity-related alveolar inflammatory response in susceptible individuals.

Conclusion

The safety profile of apomorphine is generally favorable when adverse effects are actively managed. Recognizing rare but potentially severe events like pneumonitis enhances clinical vigilance and supports better patient selection and monitoring.
