TGR5 is essential for protecting from chronic stress-induced learning and memory impairments in mice by modulating inflammation associated with the gut-brain axis
Authors: Shuai Zhang, Xiaoyang Liu, Hao Xu, Wenqian Zhang, Chenchen Shi, Meimei Wang, Guangmin Zhang, Xin Cheng, Xuanpan Ding, Haoyang Tan, Yuan Zhao, Honggang Fan, Guiyan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCS impairs brain function, causing long-term changes in neural systems linked to anxiety, depression, and cognition. TGR5 is a key receptor crucial for modulating various physiological processes, impacting metabolic homeostasis and inflammation. However, the role of TGR5 in regulating CS-induced learning and memory impairments along the gut-brain axis remains incompletely understood.MethodsWT and TGR5 KO male C57BL/6J mice underwent 21-day chronic restraint stress to model stress-induced memory deficit. Stress severity and cognitive function were evaluated by behavioral tests. Hippocampal and intestinal integrity were assessed by H&E, Nissl, AB-PAS staining, and TEM ultrastructural analysis. CORT and 5-HT levels were quantified by ELISA. BAs and gut microbiota were profiled by UPLC-MS/MS and 16S rRNA sequencing. Hippocampal and colonic transcriptomes were analyzed by RNA-seq. Electrophysiological LTP was recorded in hippocampal CA1. Inflammatory cytokines were detected by qPCR. Fkbp51, TGR5, and LCN2 proteins were quantified by Western blot, while TGR5 and LCN2 were localized by IHC/IF.ResultsThis study found that 21 days of CRS suppressed body weight gain, triggered anxiety-like behaviors, and impaired spatial learning and memory in mice. CS induced significant damage to the hippocampal CA1 region and colon, accompanied by elevated TGR5 expression in both tissues. Moreover, CS altered gut microbiota composition and BAs metabolism (most notably increasing TCDCA levels) potentially contributing to neuroinflammation along the gut-brain axis. Using TGR5 KO mice, this study demonstrated that TGR5 deficiency exacerbated CS-induced hippocampal neuroinflammation, as evidenced by increased expression of pro-inflammatory markers including IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and LCN2. CS also induced decreased 5‑HT levels and severely impaired LTP, disrupting synaptic plasticity and neurotransmission, which ultimately led to learning and memory deficits. In the colon, TGR5 deficiency similarly worsened CS-induced tissue injury. These findings highlight a protective role of TGR5 in both the hippocampus and the colon.ConclusionsIn summary, TGR5 is essential for protecting from CS-induced learning and memory impairments in mice by modulating inflammation associated with the gut-brain axis.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03898-w.

Introduction

Chronic stress (CS) results from repeated or prolonged exposure to various stressors, leading to physiological changes such as headaches, stomachaches, heartburn, fatigue, and appetite alterations [1, 2]. In general, dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis induced by CS impairs various physiological functions and contributes to the development of stress-related disorders [3–5]. Previous studies have shown that CS can cause various disorders, such as intestinal barrier damage [6], neuroinflammation [7], and tumor development [8, 9]. Prolonged exposure to CS can induce structural and functional changes in the brain, especially in the hippocampus, a key region for memory formation and retrieval [6, 10].

Recently, an increasing number of studies have focused on the gut-brain axis and its link to CS-induced stress-related disorders. The gut-brain axis plays a crucial role in modulating the HPA axis, which is central to the body’s stress response [11]. This axis involves complex interactions between the gastrointestinal tract and the central nervous system (CNS), facilitated by neural, endocrine, and immune pathways. CS can significantly impact the gut-brain axis, leading to a range of physiological and psychological disorders, such as disorders affecting metabolic processes, immunity, and inflammatory responses [12–16]. Critically, CS alters gut microbiota composition and function, leading to abnormal bile acid (BA) levels, which serve as key modulators of Takeda G protein-coupled receptor 5 (TGR5) signaling [17]. TGR5 deactivation leads to gut dysbiosis, accompanied by anxiety- and depression-like behaviors [18]. These alterations in the microbiota-BA-TGR5 axis can impair intestinal barrier integrity, promote low-grade inflammation, and dysregulate gut hormone secretion, thereby contributing to conditions such as irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and even mental health disorders, including anxiety and depression [19].

TGR5 is a membrane-bound BA receptor that, upon activation by bile acids (BAs), stimulates intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) production to regulate metabolic processes including energy expenditure, glucose homeostasis, and inflammation [20]. TGR5 is extensively expressed in various tissues such as the intestine, adipose tissue, gallbladder, and brain, where it is essential for metabolic homeostasis [21]. Activation of TGR5 by BAs and other endogenous ligands initiates intracellular signaling cascades that affect various cellular functions. In the brain, studies have shown that TGR5 is also expressed in microglia, astrocytes, and neurons [22, 23]. TGR5 activation has been shown to exert neuroprotective effects in animal models of stroke, atherosclerosis, and ischemia/reperfusion injury [24]. BA-TGR5 signaling in the central nervous system modulates appetite by suppressing AgRP/NPY mRNA expression and neuropeptide release in mice [25]. In the brain, TGR5 activation elevates cAMP levels [26], whereas 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) receptor stimulation activates G-protein-coupled receptors, triggers cAMP synthesis by adenylyl cyclase (AC), thereby enhancing neuroplasticity and memory formation [27]. In the gut, TGR5 activation enhances intestinal barrier function by promoting tight junction formation and decreasing permeability, which prevents pathogen translocation and reduces gut inflammation [28]. In the colon, TGR5 is located on enterochromaffin cells, which secrete 5-HT when stimulated by bile salts [29, 30]. However, the exact mechanisms by which TGR5 regulates CS-induced learning and memory impairments along the gut-brain axis remain unclear and require further investigation.

This study investigated the regulatory role of TGR5 in learning and memory within the gut-brain axis in CS mouse models. Our findings identify that TGR5 is essential in cognition involving the gut-brain axis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for cognitive impairments associated with stress.

Materials and methods

Animals and ethics

Six-week-old male C57BL/6J wild-type (WT) mice, weighing 18–20 g, were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. TGR5 knockout (KO) (Gpbar1−/−) C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc (Shanghai, China). Unless otherwise specified, n = 6; any specified n values are detailed in the figure legends. Before the experiments, the mice were acclimated for at least 2 weeks under standard conditions: 12-h light/12-h dark cycle, ad libitum access to food and water, and ambient temperature of 21 ± 1 °C. All experiments involving animals were conducted in strict accordance with ethical policies and procedures approved by the Ethics Committee of Northeast Agricultural University (Approval No. NEAUEC20220345, Harbin, Heilongjiang, China). As females and males differ in the cognitive consequences of repeated stress, with males showing impairment of hippocampal-dependent memory [31], whereas females do not only male mice used to eliminate potential sex-related confounding effects of sex, only male mice were used in these experiments.

Experimental design

Part I: Twelve C57BL/6J mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 6 per group) and housed in separate cages according to group assignment: (1) CON group, normally housed and fed, with no other intervention except that food and water were withheld during the 9:30 AM-3:30 PM restraint stress period for the chronic restraint stress (CRS) group; (2) CRS group, subjected to CRS for 21 consecutive days. Subsequently, behavioral tests and related assessments were conducted in both groups to investigate the impact of CRS on behavioral phenotypes associated with anxiety and cognition, and to identify corresponding biomarkers or neurobiological changes.

Part II: Twelve C57BL/6J WT and twelve TGR5 knockout (KO) mice were studied in parallel, with each genotype group randomly subdivided into CON and CRS subgroups (n = 6 per group). This generated four experimental groups: (1) WT CON, (2) WT CRS, (3) TGR5 KO CON, and (4) TGR5 KO CRS. Both CRS groups were subjected to daily restraint stress for 21 consecutive days, during which food and water were withheld (9:30 AM-3:30 PM). All CON groups were normally housed and fed, with food and water removed only during the same daily period to match the stress procedure. The four experimental groups were housed separately (one group per cage). Subsequently, behavioral tests and related assessments were conducted in all four groups to determine whether TGR5 deficiency modulates stress-induced behavioral and physiological responses, including impairments in learning and memory, thereby elucidating the potential protective role of TGR5 signaling in CS-related disorders.

Body weight, food and water intake monitoring

Body weight was measured individually for each mouse before starting CRS daily. while food and water intake were measured at the group level (total consumption per group, not per individual) for 21 consecutive days. Immediately after the daily CRS session, each group was provided with 50 g of standard food and 50 mL of water. The daily intake was calculated as: provided amount (50 g food, 50 mL water) minus the remaining amount measured the next day before CRS.

The CS model and behavioral tests

21-day CRS-induced CS model

Animal models of CS induced by 21-day CRS are widely used to model learning and memory impairments, as well as hippocampal neuronal damage [32]. In the CRS groups, the mice underwent restraint stress daily for 6 h in ventilated 50-mL centrifuge tubes. This procedure was conducted from 9:30 AM to 3:30 PM over 21 consecutive days (Figs. 1A and 6A). During each CRS session, mice had no access to food and water. Immediately after the daily CRS session, mice were returned to their cages with ad libitum access to standard food and water until the next CRS session.

Behavioral tests

The successful establishment of the CS model was verified by assessing anxiety-like behavior and locomotor activity via the open-field test (OFT), elevated plus maze (EPM) test, and light-dark box (LDB) test, performed one day after completion of the 21-day CRS procedure (day 22). Subsequently, spatial learning and memory were evaluated using the Morris water maze (MWM) test, which was conducted from day 22 to day 28. The MWM protocol consisted of a 7-day acquisition phase, followed by a probe trial on day 28 to assess memory retention.

OFT

The test was performed in a quiet room with stable ambient lighting. The OFT utilized an uncovered rectangular arena made of black wood, with dimensions of 100 cm × 100 cm × 40 cm. The arena was partitioned into 16 equal squares, each with dimensions of 25 cm by 25 cm. Zone 1 consisted of the central four yellow squares, while Zone 2 comprised the remaining squares. A camera was mounted overhead to capture the mouse behavior. Each mouse was initially positioned in the upper left corner of the arena at the beginning of the test and monitored for 10 min. Behavioral parameters, including total distance traveled, average speed, distance traveled in the central zone, and number of entries into the central zone, were recorded using the Super Maze tracking system (Xinruan Information Technology, Shanghai, China).

EPM test

The EPM test is commonly used in rodents to assess exploratory behavior, locomotor activity, unconditioned fear, and anxiety-like behavior [33]. The EPM consisted of open arms and closed arms extending perpendicularly from a central platform. Each arm is 35 cm in length and 5 cm in width, and the closed arms are 20 cm in height. The maze was placed 60 cm above the floor in a quiet room with stable ambient lighting. Each mouse was positioned at the center of the maze, facing one closed arm. Over a 5-minute testing period, the total distance traveled, time spent in arms, and number of arm entries were recorded using the Super Maze tracking system (Xinruan Information Technology, Shanghai, China).

LDB test

In the LDB test, the apparatus consisted of a light chamber (30 cm × 28 cm × 28 cm) illuminated by a bright light at the center and a dark chamber (15 cm × 28 cm × 28 cm) with black walls and no light source. The two chambers were connected by an opening (5 cm × 5 cm). Each mouse was placed in the light chamber, facing away from the connecting passage, and allowed to freely explore the entire apparatus for 5 min. Behavioral parameters, including the number of entries into each chamber and the time spent in the light and dark chambers, were recorded over a 5-min testing period using the Super Maze tracking system (Xinruan Information Technology, Shanghai, China).

MWM test

The MWM test is commonly used to evaluate spatial learning and memory in rodents [2]. For this test, a circular pool (120 cm in diameter, 50 cm in depth) was filled with water (26 °C) made opaque with non-toxic white milk powder. A hidden round platform (10 cm in diameter) was submerged 1 cm beneath the water surface and positioned in one of the quadrants. Six mice from each group were used for MWM training. Mice were trained with one trial per day for 7 consecutive days, starting on the day after the open-field test (day 22).

In each trial, the mice were placed in the third quadrant of the water maze as the fixed starting position. If mice successfully located the platform within 60 s independently, they were allowed to remain on it for extra30 s, and only data recorded before platform discovery were analyzed. For mice unable to locate the hidden platform within 60 s, the trial was ended after they were manually guided to the platform and allowed to stay on it for 30 s, with only the first 60 s of data being recorded. On day 28, following the MWM test, the hidden platform was removed to perform the probe trial. Swimming path, total distance, average speed, latency to locate the hidden platform, and number of platform crossings were recorded using the Super Maze tracking system (Xinruan Information Technology, Shanghai, China).

Sample collection

On day 29, the mice were euthanized using 1.2% isoflurane and 0.5% O₂ in a sealed, transparent anesthesia chamber. Blood samples were collected, and serum was obtained by centrifugation at 1500 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, then stored at -80 °C until use.

For brain collection, mice were perfused with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 10 mL/min, 20 mL) via a needle inserted into the left ventricle, with the right auricle incised to allow drainage. The perfusion was considered complete when the outflow from the right auricle became clear (no blood streaks). Brains were then rapidly dissected. Subsequently, tissue samples including the brain, colon, and cecum were collected and processed as follows:

Hippocampal long-term potentiation (LTP) (n = 6 per group): The hippocampal cornu ammonis 1 (CA1) region from one hemisphere of each brain was used for LTP recording.

Histopathological analysis (n = 3 per group): The contralateral hemisphere of each brain, as well as corresponding segments of the colon and cecum, were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for at least 24 h for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, immunofluorescence (IF), and immunohistochemistry (IHC).

Ultrastructural analysis (n = 3 per group): Fresh tissues were promptly excised on ice, rinsed with PBS, and sectioned into 1 mm³ blocks. Primary fixation: Tissues were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.2) at 4 °C for at least 2 h, protected from light. Post-fixation: Following three washes with 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.2, 15 min each), samples were post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M PBS at 4 °C for 1.5 h, protected from light, and subsequently rinsed three times with 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.2, 15 min each).

Other analysis (n = 3–6 per group): The remaining tissues were promptly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Hippocampal LTP detection

Fresh brains were used for detecting hippocampal LTP. LTP electrophysiological recordings in hippocampal CA1 brain slices were conducted following established protocols [34, 35]. Brain samples were promptly extracted and placed in modified artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) with 220 mM sucrose, 2 mM KCl, 0.2 mM CaCl2, 6 mM MgSO4, 26 mM NaHCO3, 1.3 mM NaH2PO4, and 10 mM D-glucose, adjusted to pH 7.4 through aeration with 95% O2 and 5% CO2. Coronal brain slices (300 μm) were prepared in ice-cold ACSF using a DTK-1000 D.S.K. Microslicer (TedPella, Redding, CA, USA). Post-recovery, the brain slices were immersed for 40 min at 35 °C in an ACSF solution composed of 126 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, 26 mM NaHCO3, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, and 10 mM Dglucose, with a pH of 7.4, maintained by aeration with 95% O2 and 5% CO2. Following a minimum 1-hour incubation at room temperature, the hemi-slices were moved to the recording chamber and continuously perfused with standard ACSF at a steady rate of 2 mL/min. Field excitatory postsynaptic potentials (fEPSPs) were recorded in the CA1 region’s stratum radiatum of the hippocampus following of Schaffer collateral afferent stimulation. Extracellular recording electrodes were crafted from borosilicate capillaries (1.5 μm outer diameter) (Sutter Instruments, Novato, CA, USA) and filled with 3 M NaCl. After establishing a stable baseline (the last 10 min was shown), high-frequency stimulation at test stimulus intensity induced LTP, with synaptic responses were monitored for 60 min post-induction. Data were acquired using a Multiclamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices, CA, USA), with a 2 kHz filter, and digitization at 10 kHz. The fEPSP slope values were quantified from responses, analyzed using pClamp software (version 10.3; Molecular Devices).

Biochemical analysis

Corticosterone (CORT) levels in mouse serum and 5-HT levels in mouse serum, colon, and brain tissues (tissue homogenates were prepared from the unilateral hippocampus) were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Data analysis was performed using ELISA Calc with a four-parameter logistic fitting model. 5-HT and CORT concentrations were determined using their respective standard curves.

BAs quantification and data analysis

The targeted BAs from cecal contents in this study were detected by Metabo-Profile Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). BAs in the cecal contents were quantified using an ABSciex QTRAP 6500 + ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) system (ABSciex, USA). Chromatographic separations were utilized via an ACQUITY UPLC Cortecs C18 1.6 μm VanGuard precolumn (2.1 × 5 mm) in tandem with an ACQUITY UPLC Cortecs C18 1.6 μm analytical column (2.1 × 100 mm). The mobile phase was composed of 10 mM ammonium acetate with 0.25% formic acid (A) and a mixture of acetonitrile, methanol, and isopropanol in an 8:1:1 volume ratio (B). The flow rate was maintained at 0.40 mL/min with a gradient elution program follows: 0–0.3 min (5% B), 0.3–0.5 min (5–10% B), 0.5–2 min (10–15% B), 2–3 min (15–30% B), 3–6 min (30% B), 6–8 min (30–35% B), 8–9 min (35–40% B), 9–10 min (40% B), 10–15 min (40–75% B), 15–15.5 min (75–100% B), 15.5–16.2 min (100% B), 16.2–16.3 min (100-5% B), and 16.3–17 min (5% B). The column temperature was maintained at 45 °C with a consistent injection volume of 5 µL for all samples. The mass spectrometer was operated in negative ion mode with a 4.5 kV capillary voltage. The source and desolvation gas temperatures were set at 500 °C. UPLC-MS/MS raw data were processed with MassLynx software (version 4.1; Waters, Milford, MA, USA) for metabolite peak integration, calibration, and quantitation of each metabolite. Data analysis was performed using iMAP (version 1.0, Metabo-Profile, Shanghai, China).

16S rRNA gene sequencing and data analysis

Gut microbiota by 16S rRNA sequencing in this study was conducted by Metabo-Profile Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The total genomic DNA was extracted from cecal content (n = 5 to 8) using the E.Z.N.A. Bacterial DNA Kit (Omega Biotek, Norcross, GA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR with primers (338F-5’barcode + ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3’, where ‘Barcode’ is a 7–10 base oligonucleotide sequence; 806R-5’ GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). Purified amplicons were used to construct sequencing libraries and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform to generate paired-end sequencing data.

Raw sequencing files were preprocessed for bioinformatics analysis via QIIME 2 software. The original sequence data underwent quality filtering, denoising, stitching, and mosaic removal. The sequences obtained above were merged based on 100% sequence similarity to create characteristic sequence amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and corresponding abundance data tables. Differential abundance analyses were performed using the metagenomeSeq method [36]. The “fitFeatureModel” was used to fit the distribution of each ASV/OTU with the zero-inflated log-normal model, and the fitting results of this model were used to determine the significance of the differences. Microbial community structure and diversity were evaluated using alpha diversity, beta diversity, and taxonomic composition analysis. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was used to assess the differences in dominant bacteria between groups (LDA significance threshold > 2). The metabolic function of microbiota was predicted using PICRUSt 2.0 within the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the hippocampal and colonic tissues of both the CON and CRS groups (n = 4–6). The high-throughput RNA sequencing analysis for this study was conducted by Biotech Biotechnology Inc. (Shanghai, China). Raw sequence reads (FASTQ) data were mapped to the reference genome using Hisat2 v2.0.5. featureCounts v1.5.0-p3 was used to count the reads numbers mapped to each gene. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package (1.20.0). Genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.05 and fold change ≥ 2 found by DESeq2 were assigned as differentially expressed. KEGG pathway analysis of DEGs was performed using the clusterProfiler R package.

Histopathological assessment

H&E staining

The entire hippocampus including CA1, cornu ammonis 2 (CA2), cornu ammonis 3 (CA3), and dentate gyrus (DG) regions from one hemisphere of each brain, as well as colon and cecum tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for at least 48 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned in the coronal plane at 5 μm throughout. The sections were dewaxed with an environmentally friendly solution (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and then rehydrated in a graded ethanol series. These sections underwent H&E staining. The sections underwent a 5-minute hematoxylin stain, followed by a 5-minute rinse with tap water, a 2-second differentiation in hematoxylin solution, another rinse, and a 10-minute bluing process. The sections were then stained with eosin for 60 s, rinsed for 10 min, briefly adjusted in 95% ethanol for 10 s, dehydrated in two stages of 100% ethanol for 2 min each, cleared in xylene for 2 min, and mounted with neutral gum. Images were captured at 200× magnification using an Olympus CKX41 microscope equipped with a Canon EOS 550D camera (Canon, Tokyo, Japan).

Histopathological scores of the colon and cecum tissues were evaluated by an investigator blinded to the experimental groups, based on four parameters: inflammation, ulceration, hyperplasia, and extent of involvement, as previously described [37]. Each parameter was scored on a semi-quantitative scale: 0 (normal), 2 (minimal), 15 (mild), 40 (moderate), 80 (marked), and 100 (severe). Inflammation: 0 = normal (within normal limits); 2 = minimal (mixed inflammatory cells, small focal or widely separated, limited to lamina propria); 15 = mild (multifocal mixed inflammation, often extending into submucosa); 40 = moderate (large multifocal lesions with mixed inflammation involving mucosa and submucosa); 80 = marked (extensive mixed inflammation with edema and erosions); 100 = severe (diffuse inflammation with transmural lesions and multiple ulcers). Ulceration: 0 = normal (none); 2 = minimal (single small focus involving fewer than 5 crypts); 15 = mild (a few small ulcers up to 5 crypts); 40 = moderate (multifocal ulcers up to 10 crypts); 80 = marked (multifocal to coalescing ulcers involving more than 10 crypts each); 100 = severe (extensive to diffuse ulceration with multiple ulcers covering more than 20 crypts each). Hyperplasia: 0 = normal; 2 = minimal (some areas with elongated crypts and increased mitoses); 15 = mild (multifocal areas with crypts elongated up to twice normal thickness, normal goblet cells present); 40 = moderate (extensive areas with crypts up to 2 × normal thickness, reduced goblet cells); 80 = marked (mucosa over twice normal thickness, hyperchromatic epithelium, reduced or rare goblet cells, possible arborization); 100 = severe (mucosa ≥ 2 × normal thickness, marked hyperchromasia, crowding or stacking, absent goblet cells, high mitotic index, and arborization). Extent: 0 = normal (rare or inconspicuous lesions); 2 = minimal (< 5% involvement); 15 = mild (multifocal conspicuous lesions, 5–10% involvement); 40 = moderate (multifocal prominent lesions, 10–50% involvement); 80 = marked (coalescing to extensive lesions or areas of inflammation with some architectural loss, 50–90% involvement); 100 = severe (diffuse lesions with effacement of normal architecture, > 90% involvement).

Alcian blue-periodic acid Schiff (AB-PAS) staining

Colon and cecum tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for at least 48 h, then paraffin-embedded and sectioned at 5 μm. The mucus secretion capacity of the colon and cecum in each group was evaluated via AB-PAS staining. After dewaxing and rehydration, the paraffin-embedded sections were stained with Alcian blue solution for 10 min and then rinsed with tap water until colorless. The sections were subsequently stained with 0.5% periodic acid solution for 15 min, followed by rinsing with tap water and two washes with distilled water. The sections were incubated in Schiff reagent at room temperature in the dark for 30 min, followed by a 5-minute rinse. The sections were subsequently dehydrated using graded ethanol for 5 min each, cleared with xylene for 2 min, and mounted using neutral gum. The results were observed under an Olympus CKX41 microscope equipped with a Canon EOS 550D camera (Canon, Tokyo, Japan). For quantitative analysis, blue-stained goblet cells in each sample were counted in six randomly selected fields per sample, with results expressed as the mean number of cells per field.

Ultrastructural observation

The fixed tissues were washed thrice with 0.1 M PBS for 15 min each, fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h, and dehydrated in a graded series of 50%, 70%, 90%, and two 100% steps, each for 10 min. The tissue was immersed twice in a 1:1 ethanol-acetone for 10 min each at 4 °C, followed by 10-minute immersion in 100% acetone at the same temperature. The tissue was sequentially immersed in a 1:1 and then a 1:2 acetone to embedding solution mixture, each for 1 h. It was then embedded in fresh epoxy resin and polymerized at 60 °C for 2 h. Ultrathin Sect. (60 nm) were stained with lead citrate and uranyl acetate and imaged using a Hitachi HT7650 transmission electron microscope (TEM) (Tokyo, Japan) at 80 kV with Kodak Electron Microscopy Film 4489 (San Francisco, CA).

IHC

The tissue sections were prepared following the same procedure used for histopathological assessment. The sections were subsequently rehydrated and underwent antigen retrieval using 10 mM citrate buffer at pH 6. The peroxidase activity in the sections was then quenched via the addition of 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in methanol for 25 min. Sections were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 30 min post-washing and incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibody used for IHC was an anti-TGR5 antibody (1:200, Abcam, UK) and was incubated with an S-vision immunohistochemical polymer secondary antibody (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 20 min at room temperature. The IHC color reaction utilized a 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Images were captured via an Olympus CKX41 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a Canon EOS 550D camera (Canon, Tokyo, Japan) at high magnification (200 ×). The IHC Profiler ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, USA) was used for quantitative analysis and scoring of IHC [38].

IF

Briefly, 5-µm-thick sections embedded in paraffin were used for IF. Following dewaxing, the sections were heated and incubated with 3% H2O2 for 20 min, blocked using 3% BSA in PBS for 1 h, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary anti-lipocalin-2 (LCN2) antibody (1:150; Affinity Biosciences, Jiangsu, China). The sections were incubated with a fluorescent secondary antibody (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark following washing. The fluorescent secondary antibody was selected based on the primary antibody, with its dilution ratio determined as per the instructions. The sections were subsequently mounted using 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI) (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Images were captured using a TE2000 Nikon Eclipse Ni inverted microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). The IF quantitative analysis and scoring method was implemented via IF Profiler ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, USA).

Real-time PCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated from tissues using the TransZol Up system (Promega, Shanghai, China). A total of 5 µg of RNA was converted into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Dalian, China). Primers for interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) were designed according to prior methods (as shown in Table S1) [39] and synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The GAPDH gene served as an endogenous control. The transcripts were quantified via a Light Cycler 480 II (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). To quantify the relative mRNA expression, the cycle threshold (Ct) values of the target genes were normalized to the Ct values of the reference gene GAPDH. The results are presented as the fold change via the 2−ΔΔCt method. The PCR results were calculated via the following equations: ΔCt = Ct target gene − Ct GAPDH, and ΔΔCt = ΔCttreated group − ΔCt control group.

Western Blotting (WB)

Samples were lysed in ice-cold radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with a protease inhibitor, then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected, and the total protein concentrations were quantified via a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein quantification kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Proteins were separated by 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), then blocked in QuickBlockTm Universal Protein-Free Blocking and Antibody Dilution Buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) at room temperature for 10 min. The primary antibodies used were anti-GPCR TGR5 antibody (1:3000, Abcam, UK), anti-LCN2 antibody (1:2000, Affinity Biosciences, Jiangsu, China), anti-Fkbp51 (1:2000, Affinity Biosciences, Jiangsu, China) and anti-β-actin mouse mAb (1:2000, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Following washing, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (1:10,000; Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The protein bands were visualized via the Tanon 5200 Multi-image system (Tanon Science & Technology Co., Shanghai, China) and analyzed with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, USA). The results are presented as the ratio of the intensity of the target protein band density to the β-actin band intensity.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism Version 10.0 (San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical comparisons were performed using the unpaired Student’s t-test between groups, two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), or repeated measures (RM) two-way ANOVA for multiple comparisons. Additionally, the nonparametric Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney U test was conducted to compare differences in histopathological score scores. Data are expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD). Results

21-day CRS-induced anxiety-like behaviors contribute to learning and memory impairments in mice

This study established a CS model induced by 21-day CRS (Fig. 1A). CRS significantly suppressed body weight gain over time during the restraint period (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1B). Daily water intake in the CRS group was increased (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1D). However, CRS did not affect food intake (Fig. 1E). Although the CON and CRS groups had comparable daily food intake, the CRS group exhibited significantly reduced average daily weight gain during the restraint period compared to the CON group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1C), indicating that 21-day CRS impaired the growth performance in the mouse model.

The OFT results indicated that the CRS group exhibited significantly reduced total distance traveled, average speed, and central zone entries compared to the CON group (p < 0.05) (Figs. 1F-I). These findings preliminarily confirmed that the mice exhibited anxiety-like behavior after 21-day CRS.

The MWM test confirmed that 21-day CRS induced learning and memory impairments in mice (Figs. 1J-N). The mice in the CON group found the platform first on day 4, while the mice in the CRS group found the platform first on day 5 (Fig. 1J). While the time taken to locate the platform was comparable, there was a notable difference in the swimming path. In the CON group, the swimming paths of the mice became progressively more direct and efficient from day 1 to day 7 as they learned to locate the platform. In contrast, the swimming paths in the CRS group remained disordered and inefficient throughout the testing period. Compared to the CON group, escape latency was significantly prolonged in the CRS group on days 5–7 (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1K). Within-group analysis revealed that, for the CON group, latency significantly decreased on days 3–7 compared to day 1 (p < 0.05). However, in the CRS group, latency significantly decreased on days 5–7 compared to day 1 (p < 0.05).

The swimming path was correlated with the total distance traveled (Fig. 1J and L). Compared to the CON group, total distance was significantly increased in the CRS group on days 2–7 (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1L). Within-group analysis revealed that, for the CON group, total distance significantly decreased on days 3–7 compared to day 1 (p < 0.05). However, no significant change in total distance was observed in the CRS group from day 1 to day 7. In addition, average speed in the CON and CRS groups tended to increase (Fig. 1M). The CRS group presented a greater average speed than did the CON group only during the first two days of training (p < 0.05). The probe trial demonstrated that the mice in the CON group successfully recalled the platform’s location in quadrant 1 (Fig. 1J). In contrast, the mice subjected to the CRS had difficulty remembering the platform location. The CRS group exhibited a reduced number of platform crossings compared to the CON group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1N). These results showed that CS significantly impaired the spatial learning and memory capabilities of the mice, with a notable effect on their search strategy, as evidenced by persistent circling and inefficient trajectory patterns throughout the acquisition phase.

CS induced inflammation and tissue damage to the brain and the colon

In the hippocampus, including the (a) CA1, (b) CA2, (c) CA3, and (d) DG regions, the neurons in the CON group were structurally intact, tightly arranged, and featured intact nuclei (Fig. 2A). However, hippocampal damage, characterized by mild neuroinflammation, was noted in the CRS group, particularly in the CA1 and DG regions of the hippocampus (Fig. 2A). Therefore, the impairment of spatial learning and memory in mice subjected to CS may result from alterations in the microstructure of hippocampal cells.

In the colon, tissue in the CON group contained abundant glands with well-defined U-shaped crypts and goblet cells (Fig. 2E). These cells were densely packed, with no signs of inflammatory cell infiltration. In contrast, the CRS group presented compromised mucosal integrity, with disorganized U-shaped crypts and goblet cells, as well as evident inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 2E(a)). In the CON group, the cecum exhibited abundant glands, distinct U-shaped crypts, and goblet cells, with tightly packed cells and no signs of inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 2E(b)). In the CRS group, mucosal integrity was poor, characterized by a significant increase in U-shaped crypts and goblet cells, accompanied by inflammatory cell infiltration and disruption of the mucosal layers. Histopathological scoring indicated significantly elevated gut injury scores in the CRS group compared to the CON group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2F).

Proinflammatory factors, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in the hippocampus, colon, and cecum were assessed (Figs. 2B-D). The CRS group had elevated IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α mRNA levels in the hippocampus, colon, and cecum compared to the CON group (p < 0.001). Additionally, LCN2 is a critical mediator of innate immunity and inflammation. LCN2 has been shown to promote immune cell recruitment and activation, contributing to neuroinflammation and further impairing cognitive function [40]. LCN2 expression is markedly upregulated in response to various inflammatory stimuli, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, through nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB)-dependent signaling pathways [41]. Therefore, LCN2 protein expression was measured by WB. LCN2 levels were elevated in the hippocampus and colon of the CRS group compared to the CON group (p < 0.01) (Figs. 2G-J). However, no significant difference in LCN2 expression in the cecum was found between the CON and CRS groups (Figs. 2K and L).

CS increased the level of taurochenodeoxycholic acid (TCDCA) and TGR5 protein expression in mice

The quality control (QC) sample correlation heatmap revealed a very high correlation coefficient among the samples, suggesting excellent stability and quality control of the experimental data (Fig. 3A). The orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) plot showed a significant difference (Fig. 3B). The relative abundance of BAs is shown in Fig. 3C. The study found that the CRS group exhibited a higher relative abundance of primary BAs and a lower abundance of secondary BAs compared to the CON group. The volcano plot showed that TCDCA and muroCA, as well as 6-KetoLCA, THDCA, HDCA, TωMCA and TαMCA were differentially altered metabolites between the CON and CRS groups based on the OPLS-DA model (Fig. 3D). A single-dimensional volcano plot was further used to screen for differential BAs (Fig. 3E). The TCDCA concentration was notably higher in the CRS group than in the CON group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3E and F (a)).

TCDCA, a conjugated primary BA, specifically activates TGR5, initiating intracellular signaling cascades that influence various physiological processes. Therefore, this study analyzed TGR5 protein expression in the hippocampus, colon, and cecum to explore its roles in the gut-brain axis (Figs. 3G-L). The CRS group exhibited significantly elevated TGR5 protein levels in both the hippocampus and colon compared to the CON group (p < 0.05) (Figs. 3G, H, K, and L). No differences were observed in the cecum of the mice between the CON and CRS groups (Figs. 3I and J). These findings suggested that CS caused notable alterations in BA metabolism and TGR5 expression, particularly in the colon and hippocampus. These alterations could influence the impact of stress on the gut-brain axis. In addition, based on the results of LCN2 and TGR5 protein expression, the impact of CS on the colon was substantially more pronounced than that on the cecum. Consequently, this study selected the colon as the primary site for subsequent investigation.

CS altered the gut microbiota associated with BA metabolism in mice

Dysbiosis is accompanied by BA metabolic dysfunction. Therefore, 16S rRNA gene sequencing was further performed on the cecal contents (Fig. 4). Alpha diversity analyses using the Chao1 estimator, Simpson and Shannon diversity indices, Pielou’s evenness index, observed species richness, Faith’s phylogenetic diversity, and Goods’ coverage revealed no significant differences between the CON and CRS groups (Fig. 4A(a)-(g)). Beta diversity analysis, conducted using Bray-Curtis-based principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), demonstrated a clear separation between samples from the CRS and CON groups, indicating that CS treatment significantly altered the gut microbiota (Fig. 4B(a)). A Venn diagram of the amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) revealed that the CRS group had 3432 unique ASVs, representing 57.52% of the total, whereas the CON group had 1,689 unique ASVs, accounting for 28.31%. Additionally, 846 ASVs were common to both groups, constituting 14.18% of the total number of ASVs (Fig. 4B(b)). The composition of bacteria at the phylum level based on relative abundance was different between groups (Fig. 4C(a)). Bacterial phylum importance analysis revealed that Proteobacteria, Cyanobacteria, Deferribacteres, Gemmatimonadetes, and Firmicutes played important roles in shaping the gut microbial community structure (Fig. 4C(b)). The relative abundance of Firmicutes was reduced in the CRS group compared with the CON group. The Manhattan plot based on differential ASV analysis revealed that the relative abundance of Firmicutes, including Clostridiales and Erysipelotrichales, in the CON group was higher than that in the CRS group (adj-p value < 0.05) (Fig. 4D). LEfSe analysis revealed that g_Ruminococcus, a genus within Firmicutes, was dominant in the CON group, not in the CRS group (LDA > 2, p < 0.05) (Fig. 4E).

CS enhanced neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions and cAMP signaling, affecting 5-HT levels and cognition

Venn diagrams illustrated the number of transcripts detected between the CON and CRS groups in the hippocampus and colon, respectively (Fig. 5A and C). The heatmaps showed the clustering of DEGs between these groups (Fig. 5B and D). In the hippocampus, the CON group had 521 unique genes, whereas the CRS group had 493 unique genes, with 13,773 genes expressed in both groups (Fig. 5A). The volcano plot showed DEGs (|log2Fold Change| ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value (FDR) ≤ 0.05) between the CON and CRS groups, including upregulated Fkbp5, Rorc, Pck1, Cideb, and Olr1 in the CRS group (Fig. 5E). KEGG pathway analysis showed that biological processes such as neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions, the cAMP signaling pathway, and the calcium signaling pathway were upregulated in the hippocampus by CS (Fig. 5H). Notably, the enriched cAMP signaling pathway is specifically activated by TGR5 upon BA binding. In the colon, the CON group had 563 unique genes, whereas the CRS group had 361 unique genes, and 12,775 genes were expressed in both groups (Fig. 5C). The volcano plot showed DEGs between the CON and CRS groups, including upregulated Fkbp5, Hif3a, Rtp1, Pkp2, and Myoc in the CRS group (Fig. 5F). KEGG pathway analysis showed that biological processes such as inflammatory bowel disease, the NF-κB signaling pathway, and Th17 cell differentiation were upregulated in the colon by CS (Fig. 5J).

Notably, gene Fkbp5 (FK-506-binding protein 51; also known as Fkbp51) was upregulated in both the hippocampus and colon (Fig. 5E and F). Fkbp51 is a co-chaperone protein that negatively regulates glucocorticoid receptor (GR) signaling by reducing ligand binding affinity and impairing GR nuclear translocation, thereby playing a key role in HPA axis negative feedback and stress response [42, 43]. Therefore, this study measured Fkbp51 protein expression by WB in both the hippocampus and colon (Fig. 5G and I). The CRS group had elevated Fkbp51 protein levels in both the hippocampus and colon compared to the CON group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5K and L).

Furthermore, the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction was enriched in the hippocampus. Analysis of neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction (Figure S1) indicated that 5-HT, a crucial neurotransmitter in the CNS and gastrointestinal tract, plays a vital role as a transducer in neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction. Figure 5M illustrated a significant decrease in hippocampal 5-HT levels in the CRS group compared to the CON group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5M(a)). However, the CRS group exhibited significantly elevated 5-HT levels in both the serum and colon compared to the CON group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5M(b) and (c)).

These findings suggested that CS-induced learning and memory impairments, as well as colonic damage, were associated with altered 5-HT levels, which might regulate neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions.

The following sections focus on the effects of TGR5 deficiency (Fig. 6A). In WT mice, the CON and CRS groups exhibited phenotypes consistent with those shown in Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5. Results consistent with those described above are omitted in subsequent sections.

In TGR5 KO mice, 21-day CRS significantly reduced body weight and average daily weight gain of the mice over time (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6B and C). Under both CON and CRS conditions, body weight and average daily weight gain in TGR5 KO mice were significantly lower than in WT mice (p < 0.05). 21-day CRS did not affect food intake in any of the 4 groups, but significantly increased water intake in WT mice (p < 0.001) (Fig. 6D and E). These results indicated that TGR5 deficiency impaired the growth performance in mice.

CORT, the primary glucocorticoid in rodents, plays a central role in the HPA axis response to stress [44]. In both WT and TGR5 KO mice, serum CORT concentration in the CRS group was significantly higher compared to the CON group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 6F), indicating that 21-day CRS activated the HPA axis.

For OFT, in TGR5 KO mice, the CRS group exhibited significant decreases in the total distance traveled, the number of entries, and average speed into the central zone compared to the CON group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6G-J). Under CON and CRS conditions, the total distance traveled and the number of entries into the central zone in TGR5 KO mice were significantly decreased compared to WT mice (p < 0.05). For EPM, in both WT and TGR5 KO mice, time spent and distance traveled in the closed arms increased significantly in the CRS group compared to the CON group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6K-M). Under CON and CRS conditions, time spent and distance traveled in the closed arms increased significantly in TGR5 KO mice compared to WT mice (p < 0.05). For LDB, in both WT and TGR5 KO mice, the number of light-dark transitions and percentage of time spent in the light side decreased significantly in CRS group compared to the CON group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6N-P). These results confirmed that mice exhibited anxiety-like behavior after 21-day CRS, and TGR5 deficiency exacerbated this behavior.

Further analysis using the MWM revealed that, over time, the total distance traveled and the latency to find the platform significantly decreased in TGR5 KO mice (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6Q-T). However, these improvements occurred significantly earlier in WT mice. Moreover, under CRS conditions, the TGR5 KO mice exhibited severe learning and memory deficits compared to WT mice, as evidenced by significantly longer escape latencies (p < 0.01). The probe trial also showed that the CRS group exhibited a reduced number of platform crossings compared to the CON group in TGR5 KO mice (p < 0.01) (Fig. 6U). Under CRS conditions, the TGR5 KO mice exhibited fewer platform crossings compared to WT mice (p < 0.05).

Collectively, these results demonstrated that TGR5 deficiency exacerbated CS-induced learning and memory impairments. Thus, TGR5 played an essential role in protecting against hippocampal cognitive impairment.

TGR5 deficiency exacerbated CS-induced hippocampal neuroinflammation, contributing to learning and memory impairments

H&E staining showed that TGR5 deficiency alone, without CRS, directly led to a mild hippocampal impairment, such as reduced hippocampal neuron numbers (Fig. 7A(a)- (d)). In TGR5 KO mice, the CRS group exhibited more severe hippocampal damage compared to the CON group, characterized by a reduced number of neurons and pronounced neuroinflammation, particularly in the CA1 and DG regions. In both WT and TGR5 KO mice, hippocampal TEM further showed that neurons in the CON group were structurally intact, with well-organized mitochondria and intact nuclei (Fig. 7A(e)). However, the CRS group exhibited mitochondrial defects, including swelling, disorganization, reduced or absent cristae, as indicated by the red arrows, and neuronal pyknosis, as indicated by the green arrows. These results indicated a synergistic negative effect of TGR5 deficiency and CRS on neuronal integrity.

Ultrastructural observations of the colon showed intact nuclear morphology (N), well-defined mitochondria with clearly discernible cristae, and normal intestinal villi in the CON group (Fig. 8A(c-d)). Discussion

CS impacts brain function, inducing persistent alterations in neural circuits underlying anxiety, depression, and cognition. CS constitutes a key factor in dementia progression and the onset of neurological disorders including Alzheimer’s disease and major depressive disorder [26]. Our study demonstrated that 21-day CRS activated the HPA axis and elevated CORT levels, thereby inducing anxiety-like behaviors and cognitive deficits in mice, whereas TGR5 activation suppressed neuroinflammation and protected against learning and memory impairments, effects potentially mediated through gut-brain communication.

The gut-brain axis is described as a bidirectional communication system that integrated peripheral signals from the gastrointestinal tract with CNS functions, thereby influencing both gastrointestinal and cognitive processes [13]. Given the critical role of this axis in stress responses, we investigated CS-induced effects on the hippocampus and colon. We first focused on the hippocampus, a region essential for learning and memory [45]. We demonstrated that CS caused notable hippocampal damage, particularly in the CA1 region. The CA1 region of the hippocampus is essential for learning and memory within the hippocampal formation and is particularly susceptible to various disruptive events including hypoxia/ischemia, seizures, and CS, potentially resulting in cognitive impairments and neurodegenerative diseases [46–50]. Memory consolidation, irrespective of type, was linked to molecular events in the hippocampal CA1 region that were involved in LTP [51]. Previous studies demonstrated that inactivation of the CA1 region impaired integrative memory for objects (what), their locations (where), and temporal order (when) in rats [52]. This may account for the integrative memory deficits observed in the CRS group. Additionally, the colon was the primary target of CS-induced intestinal damage in our study. A recent study found that CS exacerbated dextran sodium sulfate-induced colitis through mechanisms involving the HPA axis, enteric glia, and colonic monocytes [53]. Consistent with these findings, our results indicated that CS might trigger colonic inflammation via the peripheral nervous system or the HPA axis. Overall, CS not only affected cognitive function through hippocampal damage but also induced colonic inflammation.

Recent findings have highlighted BAs as crucial regulators of metabolic homeostasis through the gut-brain axis, affecting brain function under both physiological and pathological conditions [54]. Accumulating evidence has confirmed that CS significantly alters the gut microbiota composition and structure, particularly affecting the relative abundances of Ruminococcus, a genus within Firmicutes [13, 55]. Firmicutes, particularly Clostridium, have been demonstrated to transform primary BAs into secondary BAs, including deoxycholic acid (DCA) and lithocholic acid (LCA), through 7α/β-dehydroxylase activity [56]. In our study, elevated TCDCA levels in the CRS group were concomitant with altered relative abundance of Firmicutes. TGR5, a well-established receptor for TCDCA, was identified as extensively expressed in the gastrointestinal tract, liver, and brain, and was shown as essential for regulating metabolic signaling pathways and maintaining metabolic homeostasis [57, 58]. In our study, CS induced TGR5 upregulation in both the colon and hippocampus. This increased expression may reflect an endogenous adaptive mechanism that suppressed the adverse effects of CS on intestinal and cognitive function. Moreover, TGR5 was identified as a cell-surface receptor that significantly regulated various physiological processes, including inflammation and energy balance [59, 60]. In the hippocampus, TGR5 upregulation was primarily localized to the CA1 and DG regions. Concurrently, elevated pro-inflammatory factors indicated neuroinflammation in these regions of chronically stressed mice. Notably, increased LCN2 levels in the colon were probably linked to intestinal barrier disruption and inflammation, potentially leading to increased permeability and mucosal damage in the CRS group. This finding aligned with earlier research showing that TGR5 activation regulated inflammatory responses and preserved gut barrier integrity [61].

In parallel with TGR5-mediated metabolic regulation, the HPA axis underwent adaptive changes through glucocorticoid feedback modulation. Fkbp51, a key gene associated with stress and depression susceptibility, reduced GR activity through a negative feedback mechanism [42, 43, 62]. Consistent with this role, elevated Fkbp51 expression in both the hippocampus and colon after CS indicated activation of the Fkbp51-mediated negative feedback mechanism, representing a compensatory response to restore glucocorticoid homeostasis. However, in our study, TGR5 deficiency did not significantly alter Fkbp51 expression levels, suggesting that TGR5 might not directly regulate Fkbp51-mediated GR feedback. Instead, these two systems appeared to operate through distinct yet complementary pathways in coordinating the organismal adaptation to CS.

KEGG enrichment analysis revealed enrichment in neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathways, including 5-HT and cAMP signaling pathways associated with TGR5. 5-HT serves as a key transducer in the gut-brain axis, playing significant roles both peripherally and centrally [63, 64]. Abnormal 5-HT secretion can significantly affect gastrointestinal and central nervous system function. In particular, excessive 5-HT release in the gut can result in increased gut motility and altered gut microbiota composition, contributing to gastrointestinal disorders such as IBS and IBD [65, 66]. Additionally, peripheral changes can communicate with the brain via the gut-brain axis, potentially affecting mood, appetite, and cognitive function [67–69]. In turn, CNS disturbances, including stress and anxiety, can influence gut 5-HT levels. This bidirectional feedback mechanism complicated gut-brain interactions, explaining why psychological stress often exacerbates gut symptoms and why individuals with gut disorders are prone to mood disorders. A prior study indicated that increased 5-HT levels contributed to CS-induced intestinal barrier disruption by suppressing the AMPK-PGC-1α pathway [70]. Although gut and serum 5-HT cannot cross the blood-brain barrier, enterochromaffin cells that synthesize 5-HT are intimately associated with vagal afferent nerves [67, 71, 72]. 5-HT released from these cells activated 5-HT₃ and 5-HT₄ receptors on sensory nerve endings, thereby creating a direct gut-brain neural signaling pathway [73, 74]. Beyond this direct neurotransmission, gut-derived 5-HT engaged in paracrine signaling with intrinsic enteric neurons and mucosal immune cells [68, 75]. These signals were integrated and transmitted polysynaptically to higher brain regions through ascending autonomic pathways and circulating mediators [76, 77], ultimately modulating affective and cognitive processes [78, 79]. In the hippocampus, 5-HT was shown to be crucial for memory processes, and its reduction led to CA1 pyramidal neuron hyperexcitability, potentially causing cognitive impairment and neurodegenerative diseases [80, 81]. These findings suggested that cognitive impairment from CS might be linked to the gut-brain axis through 5-HT regulation.

TGR5 and 5-HT both impacted the central nervous system via the gut-brain axis [82, 83]. TGR5 influenced brain function by affecting intestinal barrier integrity and immune responses, whereas 5-HT modulated intestinal signaling by regulating enteric neuron activity. Our study suggested that alterations in TGR5 and 5-HT levels may jointly impact gut-brain axis functionality, potentially causing inflammatory damage and impairing cognitive function. Therefore, we further investigated these mechanisms using TGR5 knockout mice.

TGR5 deficiency aggravated CS-induced neuroinflammation, accompanied by reduced 5-HT levels and impaired LTP. These alterations likely disrupted the synaptic plasticity and neurotransmission that are crucial for cognitive functions, leading to deficits in learning and memory capabilities [84]. These findings aligned with the known role of TGR5 in suppressing inflammation [85]. The activation of astrocytes and microglia, the resident immune cells in the CNS, likely played a crucial role in learning and memory impairments [86]. In the absence of TGR5, these cells became hyperactive, releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines that might disrupt neuronal function and lead to cognitive impairment. Notably, TGR5 knockout directly promoted the pro-inflammatory factors production, suggesting its important role in suppressing inflammation. In the colon, TGR5 also played a protective role against colonic injury. The protective effects of TGR5 in the colon were likely mediated by similar anti-inflammatory mechanisms, which helped to preserve the integrity of the intestinal barrier and reduced inflammation-induced tissue damage. Furthermore, gut-brain interactions suggested that the protective effects of TGR5 in the colon might also confer secondary benefits for cognitive function. A healthy gut microbiota and reduced intestinal inflammation were associated with improved cognitive outcomes [87]. Changes in gut microbiota composition, often induced by CS, could lead to metabolite production that affected brain inflammation and cognitive function [88]. In our study, TGR5 deficiency not only sensitized the brain to the detrimental effects of inflammation but also compromised the gut’s ability to resist inflammatory stress. This dual vulnerability indicated that the impact of TGR5 deficiency on hippocampal neuroinflammation, particularly following CS, involved the gut-brain axis. TGR5, a BA receptor expressed in both the gut and brain, might modulate this interaction, thereby influencing gut-brain communication.

A limitation of the present study was that we did not dissect the precise signaling cascades through which TGR5 exerted its protective effects on gut-brain axis-associated learning and memory. RNA-seq data showed that several pathways, including the calcium signaling pathway, cAMP signaling, and NF-κB signaling, were affected by TGR5 status, but their causal contributions remained to be elucidated. Future studies employing pathway-specific perturbations would be required to determine which cascades were both necessary and sufficient for TGR5-mediated neuroprotection against CS-induced learning and memory impairments.

Conclusion

In summary, TGR5 was demonstrated to be essential for protecting from CS-induced learning and memory impairments in mice by modulating inflammation associated with the gut-brain axis. Elucidation of these mechanisms might enable the development of therapeutic strategies targeting TGR5 to alleviate the effects of chronic stress-induced inflammation on cognitive and intestinal health.
