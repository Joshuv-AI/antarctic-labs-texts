Evaluation of safety and health risk characterization of some baby foods in Ghana
Authors: Juliana Nyasordzi, Setutsinam Awude, Nii Korley Kortei, Fidelis Mawunyo Kpodo, Crossby Osei Tutu, Theophilus Annan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The presence of fungal species, fungal loads, mycotoxins, and toxic metals was assessed in commercial pureed baby food products in different markets in Ghana. Fungal studies was performed using standard protocols. The analyses of aflatoxins B1, B2, G1, G2, and M1 as well as Ochratoxin A (OTA) were undertaken using high-performance liquid chromatography connected to a fluorescence detector (HPLC-FLD). Toxic metals such as copper (Cu), lead (Pb), arsenic (As), cadmium (Cd), mercury (Hg) and chromium (Cr) were also determined by an atomic absorption spectrophotometer. The results showed the presence of fungal contaminants in all tested baby food samples, with counts ranging from 3.2 to 4.52 log CFU/g. Yeasts, Aspergillus, and Fusarium species were the most frequently isolated. The quantities of aflatoxins, ochratoxins were in the ranges of 0.315–11.446 and 1.991–2.510 µg/kg respectively. There were trace amounts of lead, cadmium, mercury, arsenic, and copper. The health risk assessments indicated no adverse health effects, since the calculated estimated daily intake (EDI) was lower than the Provisional Tolerable Daily Intake (PTDI), and also all Incremental Life Cancer Risk (ILCR) values were < 106. Regular monitoring of these food contaminants in baby foods is essential to ensure the public health safety of babies.

Introduction

The commercial baby food landscape is rapidly changing with the proliferation of various brands of foods targeted at young children globally1,2. Similar trends have been observed on the Ghanaian market with the emergence of pureed baby foods that add to the list of various commercial baby foods readily available to caregivers of infants and young children. The increase in the commercial baby food market has been caused by reduced available time dedicated to the preparation of home meals for infants and young children, convenience, minimal effort required to prepare and feed these foods, and attractive packaging have made these products appealing to caregivers3. Breastmilk is the optimum nutrition for infants for the first six (6) months of life and has health and other benefits for the child and the mother beyond meeting the child’s nutrient requirements4. However, globally only 41% of babies are exclusively breastfed up to 6 months of age whilst sales of commercial infant foods for the various age categories continue to increase rapidly. Though there are differences between and within countries and in the rural-urban divide as well as wealth groups, globally poor feeding practices have been reported among infants and young children.

With the rising increase in commercial foods used in infant and young child feeding3, it is pertinent that these commercial foods are safe and adequate for infant feeding at the appropriate age. Infancy is a vulnerable and crucial stage in the development of a child, thus optimal, safe, and hygienic nutrition is important in childhood for adequate growth and development5. The production of commercial baby foods is usually indicated to be strictly regulated and rigorously monitored during the various production stages. However, some contamination and food safety concerns can occur along the value chain of food processing and production6. Harmful bacteria, fungi, and mycotoxins have been reported in some infant commercial foods7 which when ingested over a prolonged period, impact neurotoxicity, neuroimmunotoxicity, malnutrition, hepatoxicity, and genotoxicity in children8.

Aflatoxins and ochratoxins have been classified as class 1 carcinogenic materials by the International Agency for Research on Cancer9,10 and such, and early exposure to aflatoxin has been linked to stunting and other growth impairments11. Furthermore, as individuals mature, there is a chance that early exposure to aflatoxins will cause synergistic interactions with other toxins12,13. According to a recent scoping review by Soriano et al.13there was high aflatoxin contamination in organs and biological samples from children affected by kwashiorkor, the brain, heart, kidney, lungs, etc., exhibited a higher proportion of these aflatoxins14. Increased exposure to aflatoxin-contaminated foods after weaning may be one-factor causing poor child health in poorer nations15.

Assessment of fifty cereal-based products intended for feeding infants and young children in Ghana showed high levels of aflatoxin contamination above the EU maximum limit of 2 µg/kg for AFB116. Similarly, contamination of different varieties of cereal-based products for children with trace metals has been reported in some parts of the country17. With the recent global18,19 and regional concerns on the safety of commercial infant foods, it is important to conduct frequent evaluations of the safety of these products. Interestingly, mostly wet commercial baby foods are displayed and sold in open markets and exposed to high temperature conditions in most markets in the country, which can increase the probability of microbial and fungal growth.

According to Beal20 and Kortei et al.21, long-term oral exposure to heavy metals such as As, Cd, Hg, and Pb is linked to cancer, as well as detrimental non-cancer health impacts. When ingested, As and Pb may cause some cancers since both are classified as recognized human carcinogens. Oral exposure to As, Cd, Hg and Pb has also been shown to cause noncancerous systemic and target organ toxicity, with elements causing adverse health effects that include neurological, reproductive, developmental, cardiovascular, hematologic, gastrointestinal, renal, musculoskeletal, and dermal systems22.

Although there has been a proliferation of commercial wet (pureed) baby foods in some markets in Ghana, to the best of our knowledge, the determination of the safety of these products sold in different markets in the various regions is minimal. Thus, this study sought to determine the safety of imported commercial pureed baby food products sold in some major markets in three regions in Ghana. We evaluated the presence of fungal species, fungal loads, mycotoxin levels, and toxic metals in commercial pureed baby food products in different markets in Ghana.

Materials and methods

Study design

A random sampling technique was used to buy products from the markets. Samples consisted of pureed commercial baby foods in various flavors and brands. The products were purchased from different market vendors in three (3) different regions of Ghana (Fig. 1). The various safety analyses of the 90 different samples (30 samples/region x 3 regions) purchased were conducted in the laboratory using standardized methods. Fungal plating24–26, Fungal Enumeration and Identification23,27, Mycotoxin determinations28, and heavy metals were undertaken using standard procedures.

Study site description

The study was conducted within Kumasi Metropolis, Accra, and Ho municipalities. These administrative capitals are the economic hubs of three regions: the Ashanti, Greater Accra, and Volta Regions, as shown in Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4. The study sites have respective populations of 2,096,053 (Kumasi Metropolis), 2,052,341 (Accra Metropolis) and 218,650 (Ho Municipal), according to the Ghanaian Population and Housing Census for 2020. These study sites were chosen because they portray a good representation of the dynamic demographic, cultural, social, and economic backgrounds of the people of Ghana.

A total of 90 baby food products were randomly purchased from the markets. Six (6) replicates of 5 different product types were obtained per region (Table 1). The sample size was determined using a Raosoft sample size calculator (https://www.raosoft.com/samplesize.htm) with the following parameters: error margin = 5%, confidence interval (CI) = 95%, Accra, Kumasi, and Ho population was approximately 4.5 million and a response distribution of 50%. The products purchased were kept in an ice chest (Thermos, China) at a temperature of 4 ° C and transported to the laboratory for analyses29. The purchased products were kept in conditions similar to those before purchase.

Sample preparation

About 1 g of sample was weighed into 10 ml 0.1% peptone water in 250 ml Erlenmeyer flasks using electronic weighing balance (OHAUS ®, UK) with a readability of 0.01 g. The samples were shaken in a Gallenkamp Orbital Shaker (140 rev/minute) for 30 min. From the stock suspension, decimal serial dilutions up to 1:103 were prepared. Exactly 1 ml aliquots of each dilution level were dispensed into 20 ml of media Dichloran Rose Bengal Chloramphenicol Agar (DRBC CM0727B, Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) and Oxytetracycline Glucose Yeast Extract Agar (OGYE CM0545B, Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) as previously outlined by24,30. There were triplicate samples for each media and dilution level. The plates were incubated at 28–30 °C for up to 7days.

Aflatoxins determination

Extraction of samples

The presence of four aflatoxins, namely AFB1, AFB2, AFG1, and AFG2, was identified using the official method EN1412331 of the European Committee for Standardization (CEN) by the High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC). 25 g of the sample was extracted using Methanol in water (200 ml) (8 + 2) and 5 g NaCl. Fat samples with a fat content of over 50% were extracted using 100 ml of hexane in the standard methanol extraction solvent. After the homogenization, the mixture was homogenized for three minutes at a speed of 3000 rpm for two minutes and 3500 rpm for one minute.

Using a preconditioned immunological affinity column that is specific for AFB1, AFB2, AFG1, and AFG2, 10 milliliters of the filtrate that was filtered from the extract was added to 60 milliliters of phosphate buffer saline (PBS) for solid-phase extraction. After being placed onto the preconditioned column, the 70 ml filtrate-phosphate-buffered saline combination was allowed to elute by gravity at a flow rate of 1 ml min-1. Following this procedure, a 15 ml distilled water cleanup was started at a 5 ml min-1 flow rate. In a 5 ml volumetric flask, aflatoxins were eluted in two stages using 0.5 ml and 0.75 ml of HPLC-grade methanol, respectively, and allowed to elute by gravity.

After adding deionized water to bring the eluate’s volume to 5 ml, the eluate was vortexed and 2 ml was pipetted into HPLC vials for measurement.

Extraction and derivatization of aflatoxins (B1, B2, G1 and G2) from samples

After weighing a 20 g homogenized sample, 80 ml of either 70% MeOH or an acetonitrile-water solution (84:16) was added. A magnetic stirrer was used for two hours to mix the samples. After rinsing the extraction jar with the extraction solution, a sucker was used to filter the material via a Buhner funnel. The material was subjected to a nitrogen stream until it dried out. After adding 200 µl of trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and closing the Vortex blender’s cover, the mixture was stirred for a minute and then mixture was incubated at 250 °C for 30 min. After diluting the sample to 500 µl with a 30:70 acetonitrile-water solution and mixing it for a minute, a 0.2 μm membrane filter (GHP) was used to filter it. To prevent degradation, the samples were examined using fluorescence detection and high pressure liquid chromatography on the same analysis day.

HPLC parameters

The injection volume was 10 µl, the flow rate was 1 ml/min, the column temperature is 35 °C, the excitation and emission wavelengths are 360 and 440 nm, the mobile phase composition is 65:15:20 v/v/v water/acetonitrile/MeOH, and Kobra cells are used after column derivatization. Spheris orb ODS1-Excel (4.6 mm x 25 cm), 5 μm particle size, 250 A pore size HPLC column specification.

Measurement precision

The method’s measurement precision was validated using repeatability and intermediate precision studies of an internal reference material (IRM). The same analyst performed ten simultaneous extractions of the IRM at the same time using the same HPLC, and for repeatability analyses, the relative standard deviation between the results was computed.

As stated by32, Ten IRM extractions were performed by various analysts on separate days, and the relative standard deviation between the outcomes was computed for intermediate precision. This is how the relative standard deviation was computed:

[Standard deviation/mean] * 100 (CEN official method EN14123)31.

Required performance criteria for accuracy and precision

Among repeatable outcomes, the repeatability relative standard deviation was less than 15%.

Less than 20% separated the findings obtained under intermediate precision settings from the intermediate precision relative standard deviation.

The measurement procedure’s percent recovery fell between 80 and 120%.

The limit of detection was less than 1 ug/kg for all aflatoxins.

The limit of quantification was less than 3 ug/kg for all aflatoxins.

The linearity of the regression curve was 0.99 for B1, B2, G1 and 0.98 for G2.

Determination of Ochratoxin A (OTA)

To find the amount of ochratoxin A in the samples, the CEN official technique EN1412328 was employed. About 250 ml of various baby foods were tasted, and the full batch of items was well blended. Using a pipette, the baby food was split into four equal portions. Until a representative 500 ml sample was obtained, the remaining two portions were stored and the process was repeated. 5 g of sodium chloride and 200 ml of methanol were extracted from 25 ml of samples in distilled water at a 4:1 ratio. The infant food mixture was homogenized for three minutes (that is, at 3000 rpm for two minutes and 3500 rpm for one minute) after 100 milliliters of hexane was added.

The baby food mixture created two organic layers: an upper layer made of hexane and a lower one made of methanol. The lowest methanol layers of the infant food combinations were filtered using Whatman number 4 filter paper. There was a solid-phase extraction and cleanup, using 10 milliliters of filtrates. 10 ml of filtrates were combined with Phosphate Buffer Saline (PBS), and the mixture was swirled. Preconditioning and activating antibodies in immunoaffinity columns specific for Ochratoxin A was accomplished by eluting 10 ml of phosphate buffer saline through columns at a flow rate of 3 ml min-1. Exact 50 milliliters of the 160-milliliter filtrate-PBS combination were added to the pre-conditioned immunoaffinity columns designed specifically for ochratoxin A, and the columns were then left to drain naturally.

Before eluting at a rate of 5 ml min− 1, the columns were cleaned three times using 5 ml of PBS. Using a vacuum pump, air was forced through the columns to eliminate every molecule of wash solvent. In order to elute ochratoxin A, 1 ml of the highest-grade methanol was added first, then after a minute, another 1 ml of methanol was added to a 5 ml volumetric flask. Air was forced into the column to gather all the eluates. The eluates were vortexed before being pipetted into HPLC vials for measurement after being diluted with 1% aqueous acetic acid.

HPLC parameters

An Agilent high-performance liquid chromatography system (HPLC 1260 Agilent series, USA) equipped with a quaternary pump and fluorescence detection was utilized to quantify OTA. Data collection and quantification were conducted using the Chem Station (Open Lab Edition). The column compartment temperature was set to 30 °C, and the Agilent HPLC, equipped with a fluorescence detector, was programmed to excite at 333 nm and emit at 467 nm. A 40:30:30 mixture of 5 mM sodium acetate, acetic acid (pH 2.4), methanol, and acetonitrile made up the mobile phase. An isocratic administration method was used, with a flow rate of 1 ml/min and an injection volume of 10 ul respectively. Ten minutes was the timer’s setting (CEN official method EN14123)28.

Determination of toxic metals

The sample’s organic matter was broken down using either high-pressure microwave digestion, dry digestion, or wet digestion. The amount of heavy metals (Pb, Hg, Cd and As) was determined using a flow injection analysis system atomic absorption spectrophotometer (FIAS-AAS), graphite furnace atomic absorption spectrophotometer (GF-AAS), and flame atomic absorption spectrophotometer (FAAS). For arsenic (As), the Hydride Generation Technique was used, and for mercury (Hg), the Cold Vapor Technique was employed.

Reagents

Nitric acid (HNO3), hydrochloric acid (HCl), sulfuric acid (H2SO4), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), reductant, and all other reagents were of analytical grade. Distilled water, deionized water, 1.1% w/v sodium borohydride in 3% v/v hydrochloric acid, or 0.2% w/v sodium borohydride in 0.05% sodium hydroxide, 1000 µg/mL is the standard calibration stock solution concentration. All reagents were purchased from Sigma Aldrich Chemie, St. Louis, USA.

Procedure

Sample Preparation: The sample was not added during the reagent blank phase of sample preparation. Both liquid and solid sample preparation were accomplished using wet digestion techniques. The process involved utilizing thermal and radiant energy (microwave and UV) to wetly dissolve and decompose both organic and inorganic samples in both open and closed settings. The objective of acid-treated sample preparation was to extract the relevant components from the sample matrix and move them to a liquid matrix for further examination. The straightforward process of dissolving a material in an appropriate liquid at a somewhat low temperature, with or without a chemical reaction, is commonly referred to as dissolution.

Sample digestion

For wet digestion of heavy metals

A precise weight measurement of the organic sample’s weight—between 0.1 and 1.0 g—was made and placed into a digestive tube. The sample was mixed with 10 mL of concentrated nitric acid (HNO3) and 5 mL of sulfuric acid (H2SO4), covered, and left to stand for the entire night. A hot plate or block heater was used to heat the contents to 95 °C for one to three hours. After cooling the digesting solution to room temperature, 1 mL of 30% H2O2 was added, and it was heated for 20 min on a hot plate. After adding 1 mL more of 30% H2O2, the heating process was continued until the digesting solution’s color turned colorless or transparent.

Deionized water was added, thoroughly washed, and filtered through Whatman no. 42 filter paper into a 100 mL volumetric flask once the mixture had cooled to room temperature. Deionized water was added to the final volume to make it equal 100 mL for analysis.

Risk assessment for toxic metals

Estimated daily intake (EDI) and provisional tolerable daily intake (PTDI)

Body weight, food intake, and metal concentration are all directly correlated with the estimated daily intake (EDI). The PTDI values and the EDI values were contrasted. To determine the high risk of heavy metals from infant food consumption, the following assumptions were established in this study: cooking had no effect on the pollutants34, the average Ghanaian kid weighed 9.3 kg35, and the swallowed dose was equivalent to the absorbed pollutant dose33. In Ghana, the average person consumes 160 g of baby food every day, according to36.

Consequently, the following formula was used to determine the EDI of heavy metals for children:

where C is the concentration of heavy metals in baby food (mg/kg wet weight), C cons is the average daily consumption of baby food (160 g/day Bw), and Bw represents the body weight (9.3 kg). Table 2 shows the international guidelines for each heavy metal.

Determination of target hazard quotient (THQ)

The risk of non-carcinogenic consequences is represented by the THQ, which is the ratio of the exposure dosage to the reference dose (RfD). The exposure level is below the RfD if it is less than 1. This indicates that everyday exposure at this level is unlikely to result in contradictory effects throughout an individual’s lifetime, and vice versa. The dosage estimates were conducted using conventional assumptions from the combined and were conducted in accordance with USEPA risk analysis37 protocols. The following equation was utilized to estimate THQ using the model outlined by34:

Where; FIR is the food ingestion rate (g/day), EFr is the exposure frequency (365 days per year), EDtot is the exposure duration (65–0.64 years), and 10− 3 is the unit conversion factor; Bw is the average weight of a child (9.3 kg); ATn is the average exposure time for non-carcinogens (365 days/year × number of exposure years, assuming 30 years); C is the concentration of heavy metals in infant food (mg/kg wet weight); and RfDo is the oral RfD (mg/kg day− 1) (Table 3).

where RfD is the reference dosage (mg kg–1 day–1) and HQ is the hazard quotient. Unlikely negative health impacts are indicated by HQ values less than 1, while likely negative health effects are indicated by HQ values greater than 1.

Incremental lifetime cancer risk (ILCR)

Carcinogenic risk assessment, according to USEPA37, assesses a person’s lifetime chance of getting cancer as a result of exposure to a probable carcinogen. In general, an ILCR value below 10− 6 is regarded as insignificant, those between 10− 4 and 10− 6 as borderline/moderate, and those beyond 10− 6 as unsatisfactory38,39.

The EDI of heavy metals, based on lifetime exposure, was converted in our estimations to the probability of an individual acquiring cancer using a cancer slope factor40.

Statistical analysis

The quantities of ochratoxin and aflatoxins were estimated using linear regression analysis. Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (DMRT) was used post-hoc to evaluate the separation of means and analysis of variance (single factor ANOVA). A p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed significant. Analysis was conducted using version 22 of the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software.

Results

Fungal counts

The mean fungal counts obtained on DRBC agar for the pureed baby foods (HEC, HRP, HBP, PBY, and HMF) obtained from Kumasi ranged from 3.8, 4.02, 3.76, 4.1, and 3.74 log10 CFU/g, respectively. The means for the same samples, HRP, HBP, PBY, and HMF obtained from Ho were 4.35, 4.28, 4.48, 4.44, and 4.19 log10 CFU/g, respectively. The means for the samples HEC, HRP, HBP, PBY, and HMF obtained from Accra were 3.78, 3.77, 3.95, 3.62, and 3.81 log10 CFU/g, respectively. Figure 5 shows the results for the mean fungal counts that were calculated.

The samples obtained from Ho, especially in the DRBC media, were significantly (p < 0.05) greater than samples obtained from Kumasi and Greater Accra regions (Fig. 5).

The results of the mean fungal counts obtained on OGYE agar media from the samples (HEC, HRP, HBP, PBY, and HMF) obtained from Kumasi were 4.01, 4.52, 4.42, 3.69, and 4.25 log10 CFU/g, respectively. Similarly, for the results obtained from Ho samples, HEC, HRP, HBP, PBY, and HMF were 3.83, 3.81, 3.87, 3.78, and 3.85 log10 CFU/g, respectively. Lastly, results obtained from Accra with samples HEC, HRP, HBP, PBY, and HMF were as follows: 3.2, 3.3, 3.59, 3.51, and 3.55 log10 CFU/g, respectively (Fig. 6).

There were statistically significant (p < 0.05) differences observed among the samples obtained from Greater Accra, Kumasi, and Ho (Fig. 6).

According to the International Commission for Microbiology Specifications for Food (ICMSF)44 shown in Table 4, the quality of the infant and baby food samples obtained from Kumasi, Ho and Accra was below standard (marginal - potentially injurious) and unfit for human consumption (Fig. 7).

Fungal species identification

Table 5 shows the fungal species identified in samples of pureed baby foods selected from each region and isolated on Dichloran Rose Bengal Chloramphenicol (DRBC) agar. It presents the percentage occurrence of different fungal species found in the five pureed baby foods - HEC, HRP, HBP, PBY and HMF - obtained from Kumasi, Accra and Ho.

The results indicate that the most predominant species isolated on DRBC agar were Fusarium oxysporum, yeasts and Aspergillus niger. Fusarium oxysporum showed the highest contamination levels, occurring in 57.5–70% of the samples across the three regions. Yeasts were the second most common fungal contaminant, found in 10–50% of the different pureed baby foods. Aspergillus niger was isolated in 7.5–35% of the samples.

Other fungal species isolated included Aspergillus fumigatus, Aspergillus flavus, Fusarium verticillioides, Trichoderma harzianum, Penicillium digitatum, Penicillium chrysogenum, Mucor racemosus, Cladosporium cladosporoides, Eurotium species, Rhizopus stolonifer, Alternaria alternata, Rhodotorula species and Paecilomyces variotii. However, these occurred at lower frequencies compared to Fusarium oxysporum, yeasts and Aspergillus niger.

The results indicate some variations in fungal profile across the different baby foods and regions. But overall, the predominant contaminants on DRBC agar were Fusarium oxysporum and yeasts, while Aspergillus niger was the most common Aspergillus species isolated. Table provides a useful summary of key fungal contaminants in the tested commercial pureed baby foods isolated on DRBC media.

Table 5 also shows the fungal species identified in samples of pureed baby foods selected from each region and isolated on Oxytetracycline Glucose Yeast Extract (OGYE) agar. The table presents the percentage occurrence of different fungal species found in the five pureed baby food samples - HEC, HRP, HBP, PBY and HMF - obtained from three regional capitals, namely Kumasi, Accra and Ho.

The results indicate the predominant fungal species isolated were yeasts, Aspergillus niger, Aspergillus fumigatus, Fusarium oxysporum and Penicillium digitatum. Across the samples from all three regions, yeasts showed the highest percentage occurrence of between 10 and 60%. Aspergillus niger occurred in the range of 5-30.8%, while Aspergillus fumigatus was isolated in 12–32% of the samples.

Fusarium oxysporum was the fourth most common fungal contaminant, found in 14–45% of the different pureed baby foods. Penicillium digitatum occurred in 15–25% of the samples. Other fungal species isolated at lower frequencies included Aspergillus flavus, Aspergillus terreus, Fusarium verticillioides, Trichoderma harzianum, Penicillium chrysogenum, Mucor racemosus, Cladosporium cladosporoides, Eurotium species, Rhizopus stolonifer, Alternaria alternata and Rhodotorula species.

The results show some variations in the mycoflora isolated from the different pureed baby foods and regions; however, yeasts and Aspergillus species were predominant overall. Aflatoxin and ochratoxins content in samples

Table 7 presents the levels of different mycotoxins - aflatoxins B1, B2, G1, G2, and total aflatoxins along with ochratoxin A detected in the various baby food samples from the three regional capitals i.e. Kumasi, Accra and Ho.

Aflatoxins B1 and G1 were found in some samples from Kumasi and Ho, in the range of 0.173–10.188 µg/kg. Aflatoxin G2 was detected only in two samples from Accra at 0.838 –0.142 µg/kg. Total aflatoxin levels ranged from 0.315 to 11.446 µg/kg in a few samples. No aflatoxins were detected in the samples from Accra except two.

Ochratoxin A was present in 5 samples, with levels in the range of 1.535–2.510 µg/kg. The baby foods obtained from Kumasi and Ho showed some incidence of mycotoxin contamination. However, the levels were lower than the regulatory limits. Mycotoxins were largely absent in the samples from Accra.

Table 7 indicates that while mycotoxin contamination was not a serious concern based on the current analysis, continuous monitoring is necessary considering the health risks especially for infants. The results provide a useful summary of the occurrence of major mycotoxins across the baby food samples analyzed in this study.

Toxic metal detection

Table 8 shows the results of the heavy metal analysis from the pureed baby food samples obtained from the three regional capitals - Kumasi, Ho and Accra. The levels of five heavy metals - copper (Cu), lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), mercury (Hg) and arsenic (As) - are presented for each sample.

The results indicate the presence of low levels of some heavy metals in the samples. Copper was detected in all the samples, with values ranging from 0.001 ± 0.0001–0.008 ± 0.0004 mg/Kg. Lead and Cadmium were found in most samples in traces ranging from 0.010 ± 0.0002–0.161 ± 0.0006 and 0.011 ± 0.0001–0.065 ± 0.0008 mg/Kg respectively.

Mercury was detected in only a few of the 15 samples, with very low levels in the range of 0.0002 ± 0.0002–0.0016 ± 0.0001 mg/Kg. Traces of arsenic were found in 11 samples, in the range of 0.018 ± 0.0009–0.044 ± 0.0004 mg/Kg. Some samples showed no detection of lead, cadmium, mercury, and arsenic.

The maximum residues of all heavy metals were within the permissible limits for baby foods. The results highlight the need for continued monitoring of heavy metal levels in commercial infant foods to ensure compliance with safety standards.

Health risk assessment

Estimated Daily Intake (EDI) and Provisional Tolerable Daily Intake (PTDI) of the toxic metals were compared as established by the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA).

The estimated daily intake (EDI) values are shown in Tables 8, 9, 10 and 11. In Accra, the values ranged between nd-9.12 × 10− 4, nd-0.723, nd -2.279, and nd-7.752 × 10− 3 for Estimated Daily Intake (EDI), Harzard Quotient (HQ), Target Harzard Quotient (THQ) and Incremental Lifetime Cancer risk (ILCR) respectively. Ho recorded ranges of nd-1.806 × 10− 3, nd-0.9118, nd-0.91176, and nd-0.015 respectively. Lastly in Kumasi, values recorded were nd-2.97 × 10-3, nd-0.8774, nd-0.8, and nd-0.0235. None of the samples were above the Provisional Tolerable Daily Intake (PTDI) value of all the toxic metals investigated. Nonetheless, EDIs calculated for Pb in the Kumasi samples were very close to the PTDI.

Generally, an ILCR value lower than 10− 6 is considered negligible, and those between 10− 4- 10− 6 are considered borderline/moderate and above 10− 6 is considered unacceptable38. Our results indicate acceptable levels since all calculated ILCR values were below 10− 6. Nevertheless, published findings of some researchers21 suggest a likelihood of the development of cancer if foods are consumed over a protracted period of time irrespective of the quantities detected. The risk is even higher in infants and children49.

Discussion

The key aim of this research was to provide a comprehensive assessment of the safety of commercially pureed baby foods sold in markets across three major regions of Ghana. Safety was evaluated by analyzing four key contaminant groups: fungi, mycotoxins, ochratoxins and toxic metals.

Fungal count

The results showed the presence of fungal contamination in all tested baby food samples and they had fungal counts ranging from 3.2 to 4.52 log10 CFU/g. In a study in Nigeria, Oluwafemi et al.50 reported fungal counts of 6,500 cfu/g in seven different local infant formulas in a market. Again the results recorded in this study were comparable to values of 1.1–4.7 log10 CFU/g reported by Edirisah et al.51 in infant formulas in North Africa. Ismail et al.52 also reported fungal counts in the range of range 1.40–4.69 log CFU/g in local cereal foods produced in Uganda. In another study by Ismail et al.53 on imported foods in Uganda, they recorded counts of 5.0-2.6 × 104 CFU/g. However, greater fungal counts in the range of 6.54–7.82 log CFU/g which was not in line with results in this present study were reported by Aziz et al and Odo et al.54,55 in some selected infant cereal flours sold in Nigeria.

The finding of this research shows that the different infant foods studied were heavily contaminated with fungi. These high contamination level with xerophiles indicate that the baby foods may have stayed in shops and supermarkets for a long time where they might have been invaded by storage fungi. According to Fleurat-Lessard (2017) and Pitt and Hocking (2022) 56,57, foodstuffs in shops and markets are actually under storage, hence fungal contamination is likely to occur.

The elevated fungus population seen in these baby meals may be the consequence of inadequate processing, leading to cross-contaminations of the goods from the staff and the milling equipment58. The increased moisture level of the pureed baby feeds may potentially be the cause of the elevated fungus populations. High levels of moisture in cereal grains have been linked to increased fungal invasion59.

According to Alkuwari et al.58, foods with higher fungal populations tend to spoil more quickly than those with lower fungal populations. This is because a higher fungal population causes the fungal cells’ rate of metabolism to increase on the product, which causes a high proportion of some undesirable metabolites to be produced. These metabolites then cause off-flavors and general changes in the chemical composition of products made from fruits and vegetables.

Fungal species

The predominant fungal genera identified in this study were not notable mycotoxin producers.

However, their presence still raises concern given that certain strains, even among the isolated genera, can synthesize mycotoxins, particularly aflatoxins and ochratoxins, under favorable conditions60,61. Further, fungal spoilage can compromise the nutritional quality of foods60. More stringent control is required across the baby food production chain to minimize fungal contamination.

Oluwafemi et al.50 isolated fungal species of moulds which included mainly Aspergillus niger, A. flavus, A. glaucus, Cladosporium sp., and Penicillium sp. Yeasts, Aspergillus, and Fusarium speciesfrom some major weaning foods in Nigeria. Similarly Edirisah et al.51, recorded twenty-four fungal isolates consisting of Aspergillus. spp, Penicillum. spp, Mucor. spp and an unidentified species from some commercial baby foods in North Africa. About 45.8% of the isolates were mycotoxigenic.

In their studies, Ismail et al.52 and Ismail et al.53 found that the most common genera in infant meals were Aspergillus, Penicillium, Fusarium, and Cladosporium. According to prior reports62, Penicillium and Aspergillus are the most often isolated genera on starches (potatoes, rice, maize, and wheat) meant for human consumption. These fungal genera are similar to those isolated in this current study.

However, in another study on baby foods imported into Uganda by Ismail et al.52, Aspergillus and Penicillium species were more prevalent in locally produced foods, while Cladosporium and Fusarium species were more prevalent in imported foods.

According to El-Sawi and El-Sawi63, Aspergillus species were the most often prevalent fungi in Egyptian infant and child food samples, followed by Penicillium species, Alternaria species, Fusarium species, and Cladosporium species. Previously Pitt and Hocking64, reported on these species as food spoilage fungi. Earlier reports of R. stolonifer have also been made from barley65 and soya beans66 which are ingredients used in the preparation of infant weaning foods.

Similar fungal contamination of commercial baby foods has been documented in other research50,67–69. These investigations have attributed the fungal contamination to raw ingredients, processing settings, or storage factors that facilitate fungal development.

Mycotoxins

Despite the isolation of toxigenic fungi, the mycotoxin analysis did not detect concerning disturbing amounts of aflatoxins and ochratoxins, in the baby food samples in this study. This aligns with studies by Rubert et al. (2012) and Braun et al. (2021)70,71 that found mycotoxins in commercial baby foods to be within stipulated limits as per EU and FDA regulations.

Mycotoxins in cereal-based infant foods.

In Italy, Juan et al.67, reported values of OTA in the range of 0.050–0.120 (mean 0.06) µg/kg in infant and children’s cereal foods. Our results disagreed with the findings of Adetunji et al. 2017, who reported greater AFB1 values of 324.7 µg/kg in stored maize grains to be used for porridge for consumption by infants and young children in Nigeria. In another study from Nigeria Ojuret al.72, reported greater values of 0-26.4, 0.12–473.8, and 0.88–589.8 µg/kg for OTA, AFB1, and total AFs, respectively, in foods consumed by infants and young children. In Ankara, Turkey, Baydar et al.73, reported a high incidence of AFB1 of 55 (87%), which ranged between 0.10 and 6.04 ppb in infant and children’s foods. In the same work, a range of 0.27–4.50 ppb was recorded for OTA.

Coppa et al.74 reported AFB1 in the ranges of limit of detection (LOD) < LOD-0.209 and < LOD-3.11 in cereal-based infant formulae, respectively, from Portugal and Spain. In the USA, Oueslati et al.75, also reported AFB1 concentrations of 2.4–5.9 µg/kg. Tam et al.76 found that 50% of breakfast and infant cereal samples, as well as mixed grain infant cereal samples, contained AFB1 (limit of detection = 0.002 ng g–1). AFB1 concentrations in baby meals and processed cereal-based foods for infants and young children in the European Union are limited to 0.1 ng g–1, which is exceeded by just 4% of the breakfast cereals and 1% of the infant cereals.

The samples of infant formula that had the greatest quantities of mycotoxins were AFB1 (87,400 ng/kg), with 84% of the samples being positive, and OTA (3200 ng/kg), both of which had a prevalence of 8%, exceeding the European limits for both metabolites74,77. Similarly, Kortei et al. (2019)78, reported a range of 0.28–45.1 µg/kg of AFB1 in cereal-based foods, including infant foods in Ghana.

OTA was also detected in Canada with a prevalence of 41% of 627 samples analyzed, in Turkey in 19% of 21 samples74 and Syria in 43% of the 30 samples analyzed79. OTA Concentrations of ranges LOD-690, LOD-136, LOD-184 µg/kg from Italy, Portugal and Turkey74.

Mycotoxins in milk-based infant foods

Skaug80 examined milk samples for the presence of OTA, including conventional, organic, and cow’s milk-based baby formulae. Approximately 6 out of 40 conventional cow’s milk samples (range: 11–58 ng L–1) and five out of forty-seven organic milk samples (range: 15–28 ng L–1) have the toxin.

Beretta et al.81 examined 338 baby food samples for OTA and found that 16.8% had measurable levels, and four (3.4% of the total) had levels higher than 0.5 µgkg–1. A study from Brazil found mycotoxin to be widespread in 44% of positive samples with a mean of 24 ng/kg, which makes AFM1 stand out in America. In Canadian samples, the highest concentration of OTA was found to be 886 ng/kg74.

Conversely82,80, detected no OTA in any of the infant formula samples investigated in the USA and Norway, respectively.

Mycotoxins in fruit based puree infant foods

Baby fruit purees, compote, and juices do not have a very high prevalence of mycotoxins. According to various reviewed research, patulins (PAT) was the sole mycotoxin present in most fruit items made from apples that young children ate77,83. Except for a study done in Syria79 where OTA was detected in 4 fruit purees at a frequency of 67% and a range that fluctuated between 0.019 and 0.156 µg/kg, other researchers72,84–86 have reported a higher prevalence of mycotoxins in baby foods exceeding regulatory thresholds, attributed to poor quality raw materials and processing controls.

Mycotoxins pose a substantial health risk to infants, given their potential carcinogenic, nephrotoxic, neurotoxic, fetal deformities, and teratogenic effects8,87,88. Their absence in the current study is reassuring, but continuous monitoring for mycotoxins is prudent considering the isolated toxigenic fungi and the ability of environmental factors to induce toxin production.

Toxic metals

The evaluation of toxic metals showed trace amounts of lead, cadmium, mercury, arsenic, and copper in some samples in this study. However, maximum levels were within stipulated limits for baby foods as per WHO/FAO regulations89. The results of the various toxic metals reported in this present study agreed with the range of values of 0.006–0.057, 0.043–0.064, 0.113–0.33, 0.000-0.002, 1.720–3.568, 0.065–0.183, 0.061–0.368 and 0.017-0.1 mg/Kg respectively for As, Cd, Cr, Hg, Mn, Ni, Pb, and Sb in a recent study reported by (36) in some baby foods in Wa, Ghana.

Alharbi et al.90 also reported high mean levels of As, Cd, and Pb in infant cereal-based meals in Saudi Arabia in the ranges of 15.5–11.1, 5.18–8.76, and 35.2–53.8 µg/kg, respectively. Similarly, in Poland, Mania et al. (2015) reported 10.2, 3.50, and 6.40 µg/kg, respectively, for As, Cd, and Pb. Again, the Bundesinstitut für Risikobewertung in Germany reported values of 4.00, 19.0, and 8.00 µg/kg, respectively, for the aforementioned toxic metals in baby foods91.

Conversely92, (Poland)40, (China)93, (Nigeria)94, (Serbia and Spain) as well as95 (United States) reported 0.00 µg/kg (nil) for all toxic metals investigated in infant and children foods obtained from the various countries.

Heavy metal contamination has been frequently reported in commercial baby foods and is a matter of concern considering the heightened vulnerability of infants to their neurotoxic, carcinogenic, and other adverse effects, even at low doses96. Environmental and agricultural measures are imperative to minimize heavy metal food contamination. The results emphasize the need for constant monitoring of baby foods to ensure levels remain below risk thresholds.

Risk assessment of toxic metals

Infants and toddlers typically consume bigger portions of food with smaller body-weight ratios than adults do. This exposes this vulnerable group to higher dietary hazards linked with the hazardous substances that are currently being studied40,96. Heavy metals cannot be eliminated from food and drinking water due to their natural prevalence in both nature and food97,98.

A risk assessment conducted by Alharbi et al (2023)90 in Saudi Arabia revealed EDI values of 0.04–0.07, 0.02–0.1, and 0.1–0.50. THQ also ranged from 0.17 to 0.23, 0.01–0.17, and not detected (nd). For HI, 0.23–0.33, 0.14–0.22, and nd respectively for As, Cd, and Pb in infants and children. In summary, their study revealed newborns to 6-month-old infants were estimated to be the age group highly exposed to Cd and Pb (0.08 and 0.36 µg/kg bw/day), while infants aged 7–12 months old were highly exposed to As. Based on their parameters, the exposure levels from the selected baby foods to As, Cd, and Pb posed low potential chronic risks to both infant age groups.

Armarh et al.36 also reported a risk assessment of toxic metals in infant foods in Ghana. From their study, Health risk assessment indices like the Estimated Daily Intake (EDI), Target Hazard Quotient (THQ), Cancer Risk (CR), and Hazard Index (HI) were calculated. EDI values of Hg, Cr, and As were below their recommended tolerable daily intake, that of Ni and Mn were lower in 95% of samples, and Cd was also lower in 50% of the samples. THQ values for As, Cd, Cr, Hg, Mn, Ni, and Pb were 0.32–3.21, 0.75–1.10, 0.65–1.94, 0.00–0.37, 0.21–0.44, 0.08–0.12, and 0.26–1.13, respectively. The CR values were greater than 10− 6, making them unacceptable for human consumption. HI values were between 2.68 and 6.83 (greater than 1), which implied that these metals are likely to pose non-carcinogenic health risks to infants.

There is a great deal of interaction between biological systems and heavy metals. Many ligands, both essential and non-essential, are present in human cells that attach to chemical components. More than 200 enzymes can be inhibited by metals’ interactions with their loci, which have a special affinity for binding sulfhydryl groups of proteins. Additionally, heavy metals may imitate cell behavior. These non-essential components can mimic essential elements to adhere to physiological locations that are ordinarily reserved for them, interfering with normal physiological and/or biochemical processes. Furthermore, heavy metals can generate oxidative changes to proteins and DNA by serving as catalysts in oxidoreductase processes with oxygen or other endogenous oxidants. This is most likely the mechanism via which heavy metals cause cancer99.

Because of their high intestine absorption capacities, poor effective excretion, and high “food intake/body weight” ratio, children are more susceptible to harmful substances99. The frequency of exposure to a particular metal determines the toxicity of heavy metals in children. According to Notario-Barandiaran et al. (2023) and Nickel et al. (2001)100,101, children eat three times as much food as adults do relative to their body mass.

Therefore, it can be inferred that they are at least three times as likely as adults to accumulate harmful substances from their diet. The fact that diets for infants and early childhood are frequently prepared using ingredients that have been discovered to have greater levels of environmental contaminants further complicates this.

The strength of this study is that the pureed baby foods were obtained from different major markets and the capital towns as well as economic hubs of three regions in Ghana, namely Greater Accra region (Accra) the capital town of Ghana, Volta region (Ho) and Ashanti region (Kumasi). These study sites seem to portray the dynamic demographic, cultural, social, and economic backgrounds of the people of Ghana. In addition, a number of food safety parameters were analysed on the commercial baby food samples such as fungal loads, identification of fungal species, aflatoxin and ochratoxins levels, toxic metal levels and health risk assessment.

A limitation of this study is the small sample size. Additionally, emerging mycotoxins, processing contaminants were not analyzed.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides preliminary data on key food safety parameters of commercial pureed baby foods sold in major markets across three regions of Ghana. The results indicate high microbial presence of serious fungal loads above acceptable limits, identification of toxicogenic species, presence of mycotoxin such as aflatoxin, ochratoxins and traces of toxic metals were detected.

Additionally, health risk assessment revealed that HQ values were all below 1 (< 1) suggesting a low possible adverse health risk among this age group. Again, the mean cancer risk values obtained were above the acceptable range of cancer risk, the de minus (10− 6) suggesting a possible incremental cancer health effect which raises public health concerns. As the actual health risk situations could be higher than the modeled values. This study underscores the necessity for ongoing monitoring and management of contamination of infant and young children’s food sold in the markets studied and the country at large.

While maximum contaminant levels were within stipulated limits, their presence warrants concern given the vulnerability of infants to adverse effects even at low doses. The findings highlight the need for continued surveillance and stringent control across the baby food production and retail chain to minimize risks and promote optimal nutrition during this critical phase of infant growth and development. More extensive national assessments encompassing a wider array of contaminants, detailed risk assessments, and value chain studies would generate robust evidence to formulate appropriate policies, standards, and interventions to safeguard baby food safety in Ghana. As consumption of commercial baby foods rises, ensuring their safety and nutritional quality is imperative to secure the health of future generations. This study provides valuable baseline data to direct further research and proactive measures aimed at strengthening baby food safety systems in Ghana.
