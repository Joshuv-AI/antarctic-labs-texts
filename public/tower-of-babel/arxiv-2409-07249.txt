The H ∞ H^{\infty} -functional calculus for bisectorial Clifford operators
F. Mantovani, P. Schlosser

Abstract
. The aim of this article is to introduce the H ∞ H^{\infty} -functional calculus for unbounded bisectorial operators in a Clifford module over the algebra ℝ n \mathbb{R}_{n} . This work is based on the universality property of the S S -functional calculus, which shows its applicability to fully Clifford operators. While recent studies have focused on bounded operators or unbounded paravector operators, we now investigate unbounded fully Clifford operators and define polynomially growing functions of them. We first generate the ω \omega -functional calculus for functions that exhibit an appropriate decay at zero and at infinity. We then extend to functions with a finite value at zero and at infinity. Finally, using a subsequent regularization procedure, we can define the H ∞ H^{\infty} - functional calculus for the class of regularizable functions, which in particular include functions with polynomial growth at infinity and, if T T is injective, also functions with polynomial growth at zero.

Keywords: Clifford operators,
S
-spectrum, Bisectorial operators,
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus.

1.
Introduction

In complex Banach spaces, the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus is an extension of the Riesz-Dunford functional calculus
[
29
]
, also called holomorphic functional calculus,

(1.1)

f(T)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial U}(z-T)^{-1}f(z)dz,

to functions which do not decay fast enough for the integral (
1.1
) to converge. The idea of the corresponding regularization procedure has been introduced by A. McIntosh in
[
43
]
and further explored in various studies as
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
30
]
. It is extensively used in the investigation of evolution equations, particularly in maximal regularity and fractional powers of differential operators. Additionally, there exists a close relation to Kato’s square root problem and pseudo-differential operators. For a comprehensive exposition of this calculus, see the books
[
35
,
38
,
39
]
and the references therein.

For quaternionic and Clifford-paravector operators on the other hand, the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus has already been considered in works such as
[
2
,
13
]
, with additional discussions in
[
32
,
33
]
. These operators are relevant in various scientific and technological contexts.

A significant motivation for our investigation is to extend this functional calculus on the one hand to unbounded fully Clifford operators, as suggested in
[
17
]
, and on the other hand to bisectorial operators in order to allow spectrum also on the negative real axis. One particular application is the gradient operator, considered as a scalar Clifford operator. While the quaternionic case corresponds to the three dimensional problem

\nabla=i\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+j\frac{\partial}{\partial y}+k\frac{\partial}{\partial z},

the Clifford case allows the generalization to
n
dimensions

\nabla=e_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}+\dots+e_{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{n}}.

See also
[
19
]
, where this gradient operator is investigated with different boundary conditions on bounded and unbounded domains. Also the spectral theorem has recently been extended from quaternionic operators in
[
1
]
, to fully Clifford operators in
[
18
]
.

The
H^{\infty}
-functional calculi also has a broader context in the recently introduced fine structures on the
S
-spectrum. These are function spaces of nonholomorphic functions derived from the Fueter-Sce extension theorem, which connects slice hyperholomorphic and axially monogenic functions, the two main notions of holomorphicity in the Clifford setting. The connection is established through powers of the Laplace operator in a higher dimension, see
[
31
,
44
,
45
]
and also the translation
[
21
]
. These function spaces and the related functional calculi are introduced and studied in the recent works
[
11
,
12
,
25
,
26
]
and the respective
H^{\infty}
-version are investigated in
[
20
,
27
]
.

Unlike complex holomorphic function theory, the noncommutative setting of the Clifford algebra allows multiple notions of hyperholomorphicity for vector fields. Consequently, different spectral theories emerge, each based on the concept of spectrum that relies on distinct Cauchy kernels. The spectral theory based on the
S
-spectrum was inspired by quaternionic quantum mechanics (see
[
9
]
), is associated with slice hyperholomorphicity, and began its development in 2006. A comprehensive introduction can be found in
[
16
]
, with further explorations done in
[
3
,
4
,
22
]
. Applications on fractional powers of operators are investigated in
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
and some results from classical interpolation theory, see
[
8
,
10
,
41
,
46
]
, have been recently extended into this setting
[
24
]
. In order to fully appreciate the spectral theory on the
S
-spectrum, we recall that it applies to sets of noncommuting operators, for example with the identification
(T_{1},...,T_{2^{n}})\leftrightarrow T=\sum_{A}T_{A}e_{A}
it can be seen as a theory for several operators.

At this point, we also want to mention the spectral theory on the monogenic spectrum initiated in
[
40
]
. It is based on monogenic functions (functions in the kernel of the Dirac operator) and their associated Cauchy formula (see
[
28
]
). This theory is well developed in the books
[
34
,
42
]
, where the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus in the monogenic setting is extensively considered.

The major difference between classical complex spectral theory and spectral theory on the
S
-spectrum is that, for bounded linear operators
T:V\rightarrow V
, acting in a Clifford module
V
, the series expansion of the resolvent operator is expressed as

(1.2)

\sum\nolimits_{n=0}^{\infty}T^{n}s^{-n-1}=(T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2})^{-1}(\overline{s}-T),\qquad|s|>\|T\|,

where
s=s_{0}+s_{1}e_{1}+\dots+s_{n}e_{n}
is a paravector. It is important to point out that the sum of the series
(
1.2
) is independent of the fact that the operators
T_{A}
in
T=\sum_{A}T_{A}e_{A}
commute among themselves. The reason why the value of the series (
1.2
) does not simplify to the classical resolvent operator
(s-T)^{-1}
, is due to the noncommutativity
sT\neq Ts
. The explicit value of the sum (
1.2
) now motivates that, even for unbounded
T
, the spectrum has to be associated with the invertibility of the operator
T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2}
. This leads us to the definition of the
S
-spectrum
\sigma_{S}(T)
and the
S
-resolvent set
\rho_{S}(T)
in Definition
2.7
, see
[
22
,
16
]
for the original definition. For every
s\in\rho_{S}(T)
we then define the
left
S
-resolvent operator

S_{L}^{-1}(s,T):=(T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2})^{-1}\overline{s}-T(T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2})^{-1},

where, compared to (
1.2
), the operator
T
is shifted to the left of
(T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2})^{-1}
in order for
S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)
to be everywhere defined. As a generalization of (
1.1
), for bounded linear Clifford operators
T
and for left slice hyperholomorphic functions
f
, the
S
-functional calculus is then defined as

(1.3)

f(T):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial U\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)ds_{J}f(s).

Here,
J\in\mathbb{S}
is an arbitrary imaginary unit from (
2.2
) and
U
is an open set such that it contains the
S
-spectrum of
T
and
f
is holomorphic in a neighbourhood of
\overline{U}
. Moreover, the path integral is understood in the sense (
2.5
). A similar definition holds for right slice hyperholomorphic functions, involving the right
S
-resolvent operator

S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)=(\overline{s}-T)(T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2})^{-1}.

Although all the results of the Sections
3
&
4
can be extended to right slice hyperholomorphic functions, it is still unclear how to generalize the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus in Section
5
to right slice hyperholomorphic functions, see also Remark
5.4
. For this reason we avoid considering right slice hyperholomorphic functions in this article at all, to make the presentation of the results clearer.

The aim of this article is now to develop a functional calculus for unbounded bisectorial operators of angle
\omega
, see Definition
2.11
, based on the integral formula (
1.3
). Roughly speaking, these are closed operators for which the
S
-spectrum is contained in the double sector of angle
\omega
and where the
S
-resolvent operator behaves as
\|S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)\|\lesssim\frac{1}{|s|}
at zero and at infinity. This extension is a delicate issue and requires a three step procedure.

In Section
3
, a version of the
S
-functional calculus (
1.3
), the so called
\omega
-functional calculus, is used to generate
f(T)
for functions that suitably decay at zero and at infinity, i.e. all left slice hyperholomorphic functions in a double sector
D_{\theta}
for which

\int_{0}^{\infty}|f(te^{J\varphi})|\frac{dt}{t}<\infty,

for every
J\in\mathbb{S}
and every angle
\phi\in(-\theta,\theta)\cup(\pi-\theta,\pi+\theta)
. Although the original operator
T
is unbounded, the decay of the function
f
is responsible for the resulting operator
f(T)
in Definition
3.5
again being everywhere defined and bounded. Moreover, in Theorem
3.11
, the important product rule is proven, i.e that for a left hyperholomorphic function
f
and an intrinsic function
g
there holds

(gf)(T)=g(T)f(T).

An essential result, which makes this functional calculus a good choice, is that if we apply polynomials
p
(or rational functions
\frac{p}{q}
) we get the expected result. More precisely, Corollary
3.14
, Proposition
3.15
and Theorem
3.16
prove that

(p\circ g)(T)=p[g(T)],\qquad(pf)(T)=p[T]f(T),\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Big(\frac{p}{q}\Big)(T)=p[T]q[T]^{-1},

where the square brackets
p[T]
is understood as formally replacing the variable
s
in the polynomial
p(s)
by the operator
T
, see also Definition
3.12
. In the final part of this section, in Theorem
3.17
and Corollary
3.18
the problem of the commutation of
g(T)
with other operators is investigated.

In Section
4
, the
\omega
-functional calculus is extended to functions which admit finite limits at zero and at infinity, i.e. to functions of the form
f(s)=f_{\infty}+(1+s^{2})^{-1}(f_{0}-f_{\infty})+\widetilde{f}(s)
, where
f_{0},f_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
and
\widetilde{f}
is a function which satisfies the assumptions of the
\omega
-functional calculus in Section
3
. The extended
\omega
-functional calculus is then defined as the bounded operator

f(T):=f_{\infty}+(1+T^{2})^{-1}(f_{0}-f_{\infty})+\widetilde{f}(T).

All the above mentioned results of the
\omega
-functional calculus in Section
3
are in an adequate way also proven for this extended functional calculus. Moreover, the very important spectral mapping theorem, Theorem
4.15
, gives a close connection between
\sigma_{S}(g(T))
and
g(\sigma_{S}(T))
. While for bounded operators
T\in\mathcal{B}(V)
, this connection is simply
\sigma_{S}(g(T))=g(\sigma_{S}(T))
, see
[
16
, Theorem 4.2.1]
, the limit points of
g
at
0
and at
\infty
do not fit into this elegant equality in the case of bisectorial operators.

In Section
5
, the extended
\omega
-functional calculus is finally generalized to the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus of regularizable functions. These are all left slice hyperholomorphic functions
f
, for which there exists some intrinsic function
e
which decays fast enough at
0
and at
\infty
, such that for
e
and for
ef
, the extended
\omega
-functional calculus of Definition
4.3
is applicable. Moreover,
e(T)
is assumed to be injective. For those functions the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus is defined as

f(T):=e(T)^{-1}(ef)(T),

and clearly gives an unbounded operator because
e(T)^{-1}
is not everywhere defined. In particular, functions which are polynomially growing at infinity are regularizable, and if
T
is injective also polynomial growth at zero is allowed, see Proposition
5.2
. Also here, the basic properties like product rule in Theorem
5.7
, the connection to the polynomial functional calculus in Corollary
5.8
, Theorem
5.9
and Proposition
5.10
, as well as the commutation relations in Corollary
5.11
are proven for the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus. Note that many identities which hold with equality for the
\omega
- and the extended functional calculus, are only operator inclusions for the
H\infty
-functional calculus. This is due to the fact that
f(T)
is unbounded here, and the operator domain sometimes shrink under certain manipulations. A big missing part is the extension of the spectral mapping theorem to
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus, where it is not known whether this is possible.

2.
Preliminaries on Clifford Algebras and Clifford modules

In this section we will fix the algebraic and functional analytic setting of this paper. In Section
2.1
we introduce the notion of slice hyperholomorphic functions with values in the Clifford algebra
\mathbb{R}_{n}
. This algebra will also be the foundation on which the Banach modules and the
S
-spectrum will be build on in Section
2.2
.

2.1.
Slice hyperholomorphic functions

Let
\mathbb{R}_{n}
be the real
Clifford algebra
over
n

imaginary units

e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
which satisfy the relations

e_{i}^{2}=-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad e_{i}e_{j}=-e_{j}e_{i},\qquad i\neq j\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

More precisely,
\mathbb{R}_{n}
is given by

\mathbb{R}_{n}:=\Big\{\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}s_{A}e_{A}\;\Big|\;s_{A}\in\mathbb{R},\,A\in\mathcal{A}\Big\},

using the index set

\mathcal{A}:=\big\{(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})\;\big|\;r\in\{0,\dots,n\},\,1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}\leq n\big\},

and the
basis vectors

e_{A}:=e_{i_{1}}\dots e_{i_{r}}
. Note that for
A=\emptyset
the empty product of imaginary units is the real number
e_{\emptyset}:=1
. Furthermore, we define for every Clifford number
s\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
its
conjugate
and its
absolute value
by

(2.1)

\overline{s}:=\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}(-1)^{\frac{|A|(|A|+1)}{2}}s_{A}e_{A}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|s|^{2}:=\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}s_{A}^{2}.

An important subset of the Clifford numbers are the so called
paravectors

\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:=\big\{s_{0}+s_{1}e_{1}+\dots+s_{n}e_{n}\;\big|\;s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\big\}.

For any paravector
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, we define
\operatorname{Im}(s):=s_{1}e_{1}+\dots+s_{n}e_{n}
, and the conjugate and the modulus in (
2.1
) reduce to

\overline{s}=s_{0}-s_{1}e_{1}-\dots-s_{n}e_{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|s|^{2}=s_{0}^{2}+s_{1}^{2}+\dots+s_{n}^{2}.

The sphere of purely imaginary paravectors with modulus
1
is defined by

(2.2)

\mathbb{S}:=\big\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\;\big|\;s_{0}=0,\,|s|=1\big\}.

Any element
J\in\mathbb{S}
satisfies
J^{2}=-1
and hence the corresponding hyperplane

\mathbb{C}_{J}:=\big\{x+Jy\;\big|\;x,y\in\mathbb{R}\big\}

is an isomorphic copy of the complex numbers. Moreover, for every paravector
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
we consider the corresponding
(n-1)
–sphere

[s]:=\big\{x_{0}+J|\operatorname{Im}(s)|\;\big|\;J\in\mathbb{S}\big\}.

A subset
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is called
axially symmetric
, if
[s]\subseteq U
for every
s\in U
.

Next, we introduce the notion of slice hyperholomorphic functions
f:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{n}
, defined on an axially symmetric open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

Definition 2.1

(Slice hyperholomorphic functions)
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be open, axially symmetric and consider

\mathcal{U}:=\big\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\;\big|\;x+\mathbb{S}y\subseteq U\big\}.

A function
f:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{n}
is called
left
(resp.
right
)
slice hyperholomorphic
, if there exist continuously differentiable functions
f_{0},f_{1}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{n}
, such that for every
(x,y)\in\mathcal{U}
:

i)

The function
f
admits for every
J\in\mathbb{S}
the representation

(2.3)

f(x+Jy)=f_{0}(x,y)+Jf_{1}(x,y),\quad\Big(\text{resp.}\;f(x+Jy)=f_{0}(x,y)+f_{1}(x,y)J\Big).

ii)

The functions
f_{0},f_{1}
satisfy the
compatibility conditions

(2.4)

f_{0}(x,-y)=f_{0}(x,y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{1}(x,-y)=-f_{1}(x,y).

iii)

The functions
f_{0},f_{1}
satisfy the
Cauchy-Riemann equations

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f_{0}(x,y)=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}f_{1}(x,y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial y}f_{0}(x,y)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f_{1}(x,y).

The class of left (resp. right) slice hyperholomorphic functions on
U
is denoted by
\mathcal{SH}_{L}(U)
(resp.
\mathcal{SH}_{R}(U)
). In the special case that
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
are real valued, we call
f

intrinsic
and the space of intrinsic functions is denoted by
\mathcal{N}(U)
.

Remark 2.2
.

A slightly different definition of slice hyperholomorphic functions (equivalent if
U\cap\mathbb{R}\neq\emptyset
), also referred to as slice monogenic functions, is presented in
[
22
]
, along with the Cauchy formula and its associated properties. From a historical perspective, the quaternionic function theory was originally developed for slice monogenic functions. However, the definition used in this article is the one from
[
16
]
and is inspired by the Fueter-Sce mapping theorem. It has in particular proven to be very useful in relation to operator theory.

For slice hyperholomorphic functions we now introduce path integrals. Since it is sufficient to consider paths embedded in only one complex plane
\mathbb{C}_{J}
, the idea is to reduce it to a classical complex path integral.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be open, axially symmetric and
g\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}(U)
,
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(U)
. For
J\in\mathbb{S}
and a continuously differentiable curve
\gamma:(a,b)\rightarrow U\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}
, we define the integral

(2.5)

\int_{\gamma}g(s)ds_{J}f(s):=\int_{a}^{b}g(\gamma(t))\frac{\gamma^{\prime}(t)}{J}f(\gamma(t))dt.

In the case that
a,b
are
\infty
or
\gamma(a)
,
\gamma(b)
lie on the boundary
\partial U\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}
, the functions
g,f
need to satisfy certain decay properties in order for the integral to exist.

Let us now consider for every
s,p\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with
s\notin[p]
, the
left
and the
right Cauchy kernel

(2.6)

S_{L}^{-1}(s,p):=Q_{s}(p)^{-1}(\overline{s}-p)\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{R}^{-1}(s,p):=(\overline{s}-p)Q_{s}(p)^{-1},

with the polynomial function
Q_{s}(p):=p^{2}-2s_{0}p+|s|^{2}
. Then it turns out that

\circ

S_{L}^{-1}(s,p)
is right slice hyperholomorphic in
s
and left slice hyperholomorphic in
s
;

\circ

S_{R}^{-1}(s,p)
is left slice hyperholomorphic is
s
and right slice hyperholomorphic in
p
.

With these functions we now recall from
[
16
, Lemma 2.1.27 & Theorem 2.1.32]
the following version of the Cauchy integral formula in the Clifford algebra setting.

Theorem 2.4

(Cauchy integral formula)
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be open, axially symmetric and
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(U)
(resp.
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}(U)
). Then for every
p\in U
there holds

(2.7a)

\displaystyle f(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial U^{\prime}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,p)ds_{J}f(s),

(2.7b)

\displaystyle\bigg(\text{resp.}\quad f(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial U^{\prime}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}f(s)ds_{J}S_{R}^{-1}(s,p)\bigg).

In these integrals
J\in\mathbb{S}
is arbitrary and
U^{\prime}
is any subset with
\overline{U^{\prime}}\subseteq U
and where the boundary
\partial U^{\prime}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}
consists of finitely many continuously differentiable curves.

2.2.
Clifford modules

For a real Banach space
V_{\mathbb{R}}
with norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{R}}
, we define the corresponding
Clifford module
by

V:=\Big\{\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}v_{A}\otimes e_{A}\;\Big|\;v_{A}\in V_{\mathbb{R}},\,A\in\mathcal{A}\Big\},

and equip it with the norm

\|v\|^{2}:=\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\|v_{A}\|_{\mathbb{R}}^{2},\qquad v\in V.

For any vector
v=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}v_{A}\otimes e_{A}\in V
and any Clifford number
s=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{A}}s_{B}e_{B}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
, we establish the left and the right scalar multiplication

\displaystyle sv:=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}(s_{B}v_{A})\otimes(e_{B}e_{A}),

(left-multiplication)

\displaystyle vs:=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}(v_{A}s_{B})\otimes(e_{A}e_{B}).

(right-multiplication)

From
[
19
, Lemma 2.1]
we recall the well known properties of these products

(2.8a)

\displaystyle\|sv\|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\frac{n}{2}}|s|\|v\|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|vs\|\leq 2^{\frac{n}{2}}|s|\|v\|,\qquad\text{if }s\in\mathbb{R}_{n},

(2.8b)

\displaystyle\|sv\|

\displaystyle=\|vs\|=|s|\|v\|,\hskip 137.42685pt\text{if }s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.

Let us now introduce operators between those Clifford modules, starting with closed operators.

Definition 2.5

(Closed operators)
.

Let
V
be a Clifford module. A right-linear operator
T:\operatorname{dom}(T)\rightarrow V
with right-linear domain
\operatorname{dom}(T)\subseteq V
is called
closed
, if for every sequence
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\operatorname{dom}(T)
and any
v,w\in V
, there holds:

\text{If }\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}v_{n}=v\text{ and }\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}Tv_{n}=w\text{, then }v\in\operatorname{dom}(T)\text{ and }Tv=w.

We will denote the set of closed right-linear operators by
\mathcal{K}(V)
.

For closed operators
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
and Clifford numbers
s\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
, we define

(2.9a)

\displaystyle(sT)(v):=

\displaystyle s(Tv),\qquad v\in\operatorname{dom}(sT):=\operatorname{dom}(T),

(2.9b)

\displaystyle(Ts)(v):=

\displaystyle T(sv),\qquad v\in\operatorname{dom}(Ts):=\big\{v\in V\;\big|\;sv\in\operatorname{dom}(T)\big\}.

Next we consider bounded operators, a particular subclass of closed operators.

Definition 2.6

(Bounded operators)
.

Let
V
be a Clifford module. An everywhere defined right-linear operator
B:V\rightarrow V
is called
bounded
, if its
operator norm

\|B\|:=\sup\limits_{0\neq v\in V}\frac{\|Bv\|}{\|v\|}<\infty

is finite. We will denote the set of all bounded right-linear operators by
\mathcal{B}(V)
.

Due to (
2.8a
) and (
2.8b
), the operator norms of the products (
2.9a
) and (
2.9b
), satisfy

\displaystyle\|sB\|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\frac{n}{2}}|s|\|B\|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|Bs\|\leq 2^{\frac{n}{2}}|s|\|B\|,\qquad\text{if }s\in\mathbb{R}_{n},

\displaystyle\|sB\|

\displaystyle=\|Bs\|=|s|\|B\|,\hskip 140.84105pt\text{if }s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.

We also recall the notion of
operator inclusion
: For
T,S\in\mathcal{K}(V)
we say
T\subseteq S
if and only if

(2.10)

\operatorname{dom}(T)\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(S)\qquad\text{and}\qquad Tv=Sv,\qquad v\in\operatorname{dom}(T).

In complex Banach spaces the spectrum is connected to the bounded invertibility of the operator
A-\lambda
. However, in the noncommutative Clifford setting it is due to the explicit value of the series expansion (
1.2
), and the discussion afterwords, that it is natural to connect the spectrum with the bounded invertibility of the operator

Q_{s}[T]:=T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2},\qquad\text{with }\operatorname{dom}(Q_{s}[T])=\operatorname{dom}(T^{2}).

This suggests the following definition of the
S
-spectrum.

Definition 2.7

(
S
-spectrum)
.

For every
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
we define the
S
-resolvent set
and the
S
-spectrum
by

Remark 2.8
.

Note, that although

is closed as well. However, since for every

Remark 2.9
.

In (

) there clearly commutes

. However, if we replace

everywhere defined, we interchange

.

Remark 2.10
.

The main differences between classical spectral theory and the
S
-spectrum in terms of holomorphicity is that the mapping

is not slice hyperholomorphic in the sense of Definition
2.1
. This operator

is only used for the definition of the

-spectrum. The

-resolvent operators

-functional calculus, see (

The advantage of the bounded

-functional calculus (

, the respective

-spectrum is potentially unbounded as well. So, integration paths which surround

are not compact any more, and we have to ensure the convergence of the integral by a certain decay of the integrand. As a result, this cannot be done for any unbounded operator, but requires additional assumptions on the one hand on the location of the spectrum (to allow certain integration paths), but also on the decay of the resolvent operator (to make the integral converge). In this article we will concentrate on the class of bisectorial operators, which will be defined in the following. For every
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
, we consider the

\omega

Here,

For every

Corollary 3.18
.

Let

i)  For every

Using also

, we get

ii)  For every

. However, since
\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})
only includes functions which vanish at zero and at infinity, this class is too small, in particular for non-injective operators
T
. This is why in this section we will also give meaning to
f(T)
for functions which have finite values at zero and at infinity, see Definition
4.1
. Another reason why the extended
\omega
-functional makes sense, is that it gives a larger class of functions for which the operator
f(T)
is still bounded. See also
[
13
]
, where this method was already used.

Definition 4.1
.

For every
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
let
D_{\theta}
be the double sector from (
2.12
). Then we define the function spaces

i)

\displaystyle\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta}):=\Big\{f(s)=f_{\infty}+\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}(f_{0}-f_{\infty})+\widetilde{f}(s)\;\Big|\;f_{0},f_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}_{n},\,\widetilde{f}\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})\Big\},

ii)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta}):=\Big\{g(s)=g_{\infty}+\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}(g_{0}-g_{\infty})+\widetilde{g}(s)\;\Big|\;g_{0},g_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R},\,\widetilde{g}\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})\Big\}.

Note, that for every
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
the components
f_{0},f_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
,
\widetilde{f}\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})
are unique. Moreover, by Lemma
3.2
, the function
f
clearly admits for every
\varphi\in(0,\theta)
the limits

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\sup\limits_{J\in\mathbb{S},\phi\in I_{\varphi}}|f(te^{J\phi})-f_{0}|=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}\sup\limits_{J\in\mathbb{S},\phi\in I_{\varphi}}|f(te^{J\phi})-f_{\infty}|=0.

The next lemma gives a useful characterization of the spaces
\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
and
\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
and
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(D_{\theta})
(resp.
f\in\mathcal{N}(D_{\theta})
) be bounded. Then there is
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
(resp.
f\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
) if and only if there exists
0<r<R
and
f_{0},f_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
(resp.
f_{0},f_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}
) such that for every
J\in\mathbb{S}
and
\phi\in I_{\theta}
there holds

(4.1)

\int_{0}^{r}|f(te^{J\phi})-f_{0}|\frac{dt}{t}<\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{R}^{\infty}|f(te^{J\phi})-f_{\infty}|\frac{dt}{t}<\infty.

Proof.

For the first implication assume that
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
, i.e., we can write

f(s)=f_{\infty}+\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}(f_{0}-f_{\infty})+\widetilde{f}(s),

for some
f_{0},f_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
and
\widetilde{f}\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})
. Rewriting this equation, gives

(4.2)

f(s)-f_{0}=\frac{s^{2}}{1+s^{2}}(f_{\infty}-f_{0})+\widetilde{f}(s)\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(s)-f_{\infty}=\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}(f_{0}-f_{\infty})+\widetilde{f}(s).

Since
\widetilde{f}\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})
we know that
\int_{0}^{\infty}|\widetilde{f}(te^{J\phi})|\frac{dt}{t}<\infty
, and hence the representations (
4.2
) show that also the integrals (
4.1
) are finite.

For the inverse implication, we assume that there exists
f_{0},f_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
, such that the integrals (
4.1
) are finite. Let us define

\widetilde{f}(z):=f(z)-f_{\infty}-\frac{1}{1+z^{2}}(f_{0}-f_{\infty}).

This function then satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r}|\widetilde{f}(te^{J\phi})|\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r}|f(te^{J\phi})-f_{0}|\frac{dt}{t}+|f_{0}-f_{\infty}|\int_{0}^{r}\frac{t^{2}}{|1+t^{2}e^{2J\phi}|}\frac{dt}{t}<\infty,

\displaystyle\int_{r}^{R}|\widetilde{f}(te^{J\phi})|\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle<\infty,

\displaystyle\int_{R}^{\infty}|\widetilde{f}(te^{J\phi})|\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{R}^{\infty}|f(te^{J\phi})-f_{\infty}|\frac{dt}{t}+|f_{0}-f_{\infty}|\int_{R}^{\infty}\frac{2}{|1+t^{2}e^{2J\phi}|}\frac{dt}{t}<\infty,

which means that
\widetilde{f}\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})
. Hence we can write
f(z)=f_{\infty}+\frac{1}{1+z^{2}}(f_{0}-f_{\infty})+\widetilde{f}(z)
for
f_{0},f_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
and
\widetilde{f}\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})
, which means that
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
.
∎

Definition 4.3

(Extended
\omega
-functional calculus)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then for every
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
we define the
extended
\omega
-functional calculus

(4.3)

f(T):=f_{\infty}+(1+T^{2})^{-1}(f_{0}-f_{\infty})+\widetilde{f}(T),

where
f_{0},f_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
,
\widetilde{f}\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})
are the coefficients from Definition
4.1
. Moreover,
\widetilde{f}(T)
is understood as the
\omega
-functional calculus in Definition
3.5
, and the inverse
(1+T^{2})^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)
is bounded since
Q_{J}[T]=T^{2}+1
is boundedly invertible because
\mathbb{S}\subseteq\rho_{S}(T)
by Definition
2.11
.

For every
f,g\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
and
a\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
there holds

(f+g)(T)=f(T)+g(T)\qquad\text{and}\qquad(fa)(T)=f(T)a.

Remark 4.4
.

In the special case that
0\in\rho_{S}(T)
, we already mentioned in Remark
3.6
that we are able to extend the
\omega
-functional calculus to functions where only the integrability at
\infty
matters. Consequently, if
0\in\rho_{S}(T)
, we can also extend the extended
\omega
-functional calculus to functions
f
for which there exists some
f_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
, such that
\widehat{f}(s):=f(s)-f_{\infty}
satisfies the integrability condition (
3.14
). Then
\widehat{f}(T)
is understood as in (
3.13
), and we can define

(4.4)

f(T):=f_{\infty}+\widehat{f}(T).

The next proposition shows that for certain subclasses of functions, the extended
\omega
-functional calculus coincides with the well established functional calculus for unbounded operators, see e.g.
[
15
, Definition 3.4.2]
or
[
23
, Theorem 4.12]
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
and
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus K)
for some axially symmetric compact
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\overline{D_{\omega}}
. Moreover, assume that
\lim_{|s|\rightarrow\infty}f(s)=f_{\infty}
, for some
f_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
. Then there exists some
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
such that
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
and the extended
\omega
-functional calculus (
4.3
) can be written as

(4.5)

f(T)=f_{\infty}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial U\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)ds_{J}f(s),

where
J\in\mathbb{S}
is arbitrary and
U
is some axially symmetric open set with
K\subseteq U\subseteq\overline{U}\subseteq\rho_{S}(T)
.

Proof.

Since the compact set
K
is located outside the closed double sector
\overline{D_{\omega}}
, there exists some
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
, such that
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\overline{D_{\theta}}
. This means that
f
can be restricted to a function
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(D_{\theta})
. Since
f
is slice hyperholomorphic on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus K
, the value
f_{0}:=f(0)
exists. Let us now define the set
\widetilde{K}:=K\cup[J]
, as well as the function

(4.6)

\widetilde{f}(s):=f(s)-f_{\infty}-\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}(f_{0}-f_{\infty}),\qquad s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\widetilde{K}.

Then
\widetilde{f}\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\widetilde{K})
with values
\widetilde{f}(0)=0
and
\lim_{|s|\rightarrow\infty}\widetilde{f}(s)=0
. In the same way as in Proposition
3.8
, we conclude that
\widetilde{f}\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})
. Consequently there is
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
. According to Definition
4.3
and Proposition
3.8
, the extended
\omega
functional calculus for
f
is given by

(4.7)

f(T)=f_{\infty}+(1+T^{2})^{-1}(f_{0}-f_{\infty})-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial\widetilde{U}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)ds_{J}\widetilde{f}(s),

where
\widetilde{U}
is some axially symmetric open set with
\widetilde{K}\subseteq\widetilde{U}\subseteq\overline{\widetilde{U}}\subseteq\rho_{S}(T)
. Moreover, it follows from the hyperholomorphicity of the constant function
1
, that

\int_{\partial\widetilde{U}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)ds_{J}=0,

and from
[
27
, Equation (54)]
, that

\frac{-1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial\widetilde{U}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)ds_{J}\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}=(1+T^{2})^{-1}.

These two identities, together with the definition of
\widetilde{f}
in (
4.6
), reduce (
4.7
) to

f(T)=f_{\infty}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial\widetilde{U}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)ds_{J}f(s).

Finally, since
f
is slice hyperholomorphic on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus K
, we can replace the set
\widetilde{U}
, which is a superset of
\widetilde{K}=K\cup[J]
, by any axially symmetric open set
U
with
K\subseteq U\subseteq\overline{U}\subseteq\rho_{S}(T)
. So we have proven the formula (
4.5
).
∎

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
and
g\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
,
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then, with
g_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
from Definition
4.1
, there holds

\ker(T)\subseteq\ker(g(T)-g_{0}).

Proof.

Let
v\in\ker(T)
. Then
(1+T^{2})v=v
and hence
(1+T^{2})^{-1}v=v
. Moreover, there is also
\widetilde{g}(T)v=0
by Proposition
3.9
. Using this in the definition (
4.3
) of
g(T)
, gives

g(T)v=g_{\infty}v+(1+T^{2})^{-1}(g_{0}-g_{\infty})v+\widetilde{g}(T)v=g_{\infty}v+(g_{0}-g_{\infty})v=g_{0}v.\qed

Next, we generalize the product rule of Theorem
3.11
to the extended
\omega
-functional calculus.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then, for
g\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
and
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
,
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
, we have
gf\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
and there holds the product rule

(4.8)

(gf)(T)=g(T)f(T).

Proof.

Since
g\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
and
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
, there exists
g_{0},g_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\widetilde{g}\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
and
f_{0},f_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
,
\widetilde{f}\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})
, such that

g(s)=g_{\infty}+\frac{g_{0}-g_{\infty}}{1+s^{2}}+\widetilde{g}(s)\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(s)=f_{\infty}+\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}(f_{0}-f_{\infty})+\widetilde{f}(s).

Hence the product
gf
admits the decomposition

\displaystyle g(s)f(s)

\displaystyle=g_{\infty}f_{\infty}+\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}(g_{0}f_{0}-g_{\infty}f_{\infty})+g_{\infty}\widetilde{f}(s)+\widetilde{g}(s)f_{\infty}+\widetilde{g}(s)\widetilde{f}(s)

(4.9)

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}\widetilde{f}(s)}_{=:\widetilde{h}_{1}(s)}(g_{0}-g_{\infty})+\underbrace{\frac{\widetilde{g}(s)}{1+s^{2}}}_{=:\widetilde{h}_{2}(s)}(f_{0}-f_{\infty})-\underbrace{\frac{s^{2}}{(1+s^{2})^{2}}}_{=:\widetilde{h}_{3}(s)}(g_{0}-g_{\infty})(f_{0}-f_{\infty}).

Since
\widetilde{g}\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta}),\widetilde{f}\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})
, there is also
\widetilde{h}_{1},\widetilde{h}_{2},\widetilde{h}_{3},\widetilde{g}\widetilde{f}\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})
by Theorem
3.11
. Hence the representation (
4.9
) shows that
gf\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
, with coefficients
h_{0}=g_{0}f_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
,
h_{\infty}=g_{\infty}f_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
, and

\widetilde{h}=g_{\infty}\widetilde{f}+\widetilde{g}f_{\infty}+\widetilde{g}\widetilde{f}+\widetilde{h}_{1}(g_{0}-g_{\infty})+\widetilde{h}_{2}(f_{0}-f_{\infty})-\widetilde{h}_{3}(g_{0}-g_{\infty})(f_{0}-f_{\infty})\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta}).

Hence, we can write the extended
\omega
-functional calculus of the product
gf
as

(4.10)

(gf)(T)=g_{\infty}f_{\infty}+(1+T^{2})^{-1}(g_{0}f_{0}-g_{\infty}f_{\infty})+\widetilde{h}(T).

Let us now further investigate, how to rewrite
\widetilde{h}_{1}(T)
,
\widetilde{h}_{2}(T)
and
\widetilde{h}_{3}(T)
. First, we note that
(1+s^{2})\widetilde{h}_{1}=\widetilde{f}\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})
and hence by Proposition
3.15
there is
\operatorname{ran}(\widetilde{h}_{1}(T))\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(T^{2})
and

(1+T^{2})\widetilde{h}_{1}(T)=((1+s^{2})\widetilde{h}_{1})(T)=\widetilde{f}(T).

Since
1+T^{2}
is boundedly invertible, there is
\widetilde{h}_{1}(T)=(1+T^{2})^{-1}\widetilde{f}(T)
. Analogously, we also get
\widetilde{h}_{2}(T)=(1+T^{2})^{-1}\widetilde{g}(T)
. Finally, by Theorem
3.16
there is
\widetilde{h}_{3}(T)=T^{2}(1+T^{2})^{-2}
. Plugging these three operators into (
4.10
), we obtain

\displaystyle(gf)(T)

\displaystyle=g_{\infty}f_{\infty}+(1+T^{2})^{-1}(g_{0}f_{0}-g_{\infty}f_{\infty})+g_{\infty}\widetilde{f}(T)+\widetilde{g}(T)f_{\infty}+(\widetilde{g}\widetilde{f})(T)

\displaystyle\quad+\widetilde{h}_{1}(T)(g_{0}-g_{\infty})+\widetilde{h}_{2}(T)(f_{0}-f_{\infty})-\widetilde{h}_{3}(T)(g_{0}-g_{\infty})(f_{0}-f_{\infty})

\displaystyle=g_{\infty}f_{\infty}+(1+T^{2})^{-1}(g_{0}f_{0}-g_{\infty}f_{\infty})+g_{\infty}\widetilde{f}(T)+\widetilde{g}(T)f_{\infty}+\widetilde{g}(T)\widetilde{f}(T)

\displaystyle\quad+(1+T^{2})^{-1}\widetilde{f}(T)(g_{0}-g_{\infty})+(1+T^{2})^{-1}\widetilde{g}(T)(f_{0}-f_{\infty})

(4.11)

\displaystyle\quad-T^{2}(1+T^{2})^{-2}(g_{0}-g_{\infty})(f_{0}-f_{\infty}).

On the other hand, we also have

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle(T)f(T)=\big(g_{\infty}+(1+T^{2})^{-1}(g_{0}-g_{\infty})+\widetilde{g}(T)\big)\big(f_{\infty}+(1+T^{2})^{-1}(f_{0}-f_{\infty})+\widetilde{f}(T)\big)

\displaystyle=g_{\infty}f_{\infty}+(1+T^{2})^{-2}(g_{0}-g_{\infty})(f_{0}-f_{\infty})+g_{\infty}\widetilde{f}(T)+\widetilde{g}(T)f_{\infty}+\widetilde{g}(T)\widetilde{f}(T)

(4.12)

\displaystyle\quad+(1+T^{2})^{-1}\big(g_{\infty}(f_{0}-f_{\infty})+(g_{0}-g_{\infty})f_{\infty}+(g_{0}-g_{\infty})\widetilde{f}(T)+\widetilde{g}(T)(f_{0}-f_{\infty})\big),

where in the second equation we used that
\widetilde{g}(T)
and
(1+T^{2})^{-1}
commute by Corollary
3.18
(i). Since the right hand sides of (
4.11
) and (
4.12
) coincide, we deduce the product rule (
4.8
).
∎

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
,
g\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
,
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
, and
p
an intrinsic polynomial. Then there is
p\circ g\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
and

(p\circ g)(T)=p[g(T)].

Proof.

The proof is the same as the one of Corollary
3.14
, with the only difference that here we also allow the constant term
p_{0}
in the polynomial. However, this term is clearly contained in the space
\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
.
∎

The next proposition shows the compatibility of the extended
\omega
-functional calculus (
4.3
) with the polynomial functional calculus.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
,
p
an intrinsic polynomial of degree
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
,
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
. If
pf\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
, then there is
\operatorname{ran}(f(T))\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(T^{n})
and

(4.13)

(pf)(T)=p[T]f(T).

Proof.

Note, that it is enough to prove (
4.13
) for monomials
p(s)=s^{n}
. The case of general polynomials then follows by linearity. We will prove this fact via induction with respect to
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. The induction start
n=0
is trivial. For the induction step
n\rightarrow n+1
, we have
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
and
s^{n+1}f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
, which means that we can write

(4.14)

f(s)=f_{\infty}+\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}(f_{0}-f_{\infty})+\widetilde{f}(s)\quad\text{and}\quad s^{n+1}f(s)=g_{\infty}+\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}(g_{0}-g_{\infty})+\widetilde{g}(s).

If we multiply the first equation with
s^{n+1}
and compare both equations, we obtain

s^{n+1}\Big(f_{\infty}+\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}(f_{0}-f_{\infty})+\widetilde{f}(s)\Big)=g_{\infty}+\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}(g_{0}-g_{\infty})+\widetilde{g}(s).

Since this equation has to be true for every
s\in D_{\theta}
, we conclude
g_{0}=0
when we take the limit
|s|\rightarrow 0^{+}
. Moreover, in the limit
|s|\rightarrow\infty
we get

\lim\limits_{|s|\rightarrow\infty}s^{n+1}\Big(f_{\infty}+\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}(f_{0}-f_{\infty})+\widetilde{f}(s)\Big)=g_{\infty}.

The fact that this limit is finite, implies that

\lim_{|s|\rightarrow\infty}\Big(f_{\infty}+\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}(f_{0}-f_{\infty})+\widetilde{f}(s)\Big)=0,

i.e.
f_{\infty}=0
by Lemma
3.2
. The two values
g_{0}=f_{\infty}=0
now reduce the equations (
4.14
) to

(4.15)

f(s)=\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}f_{0}+\widetilde{f}(s)\quad\text{and}\quad s^{n+1}f(s)=g_{\infty}-\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}g_{\infty}+\widetilde{g}(s).

Hence the corresponding extended
\omega
-functional calculi (
4.3
) are given by

(4.16)

\begin{split}f(T)=(1+T^{2})^{-1}f_{0}+\widetilde{f}(T)\quad\text{and}\quad(s^{n+1}f)(T)&=g_{\infty}-(1+T^{2})^{-1}g_{\infty}+\widetilde{g}(T)\\
&=T^{2}(1+T^{2})^{-1}g_{\infty}+\widetilde{g}(T).\end{split}

Next, we will show that for
h(s):=sf(s)\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
there holds
\operatorname{ran}(f(T))\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(T)
with

(4.17)

h(T)=Tf(T).

\circ
If
n=0
, we can use the two equations (
4.15
), to write

s\Big(\widetilde{f}(s)-\frac{s}{1+s^{2}}g_{\infty}\Big)=\widetilde{g}(s)-\frac{s}{1+s^{2}}f_{0}\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta}).

Since also
\widetilde{f}(s)-\frac{s}{1+s^{2}}g_{\infty}\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})
, we have
\operatorname{ran}(\widetilde{f}(T)-T(1+T^{2})^{-1}g_{\infty})\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(T)
by Proposition
3.15
and

T\big(\widetilde{f}(T)-T(1+T^{2})^{-1}g_{\infty}\big)=\widetilde{g}(T)-T(1+T^{2})^{-1}f_{0},

where in the second equation we used the identity
(\frac{s}{1+s^{2}})(T)=T(1+T^{2})^{-1}
from the rational functional calculus in Theorem
3.16
. Hence we have
\operatorname{ran}(\widetilde{f}(T))\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(T)
and using
f(T)
in (
4.16
) also
\operatorname{ran}(f(T))\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(T)
, with

Tf(T)=T(1+T^{2})^{-1}f_{0}+T\widetilde{f}(T)=\widetilde{g}(T)+T^{2}(1+T^{2})^{-1}g_{\infty}=(sf)(T)=h(T).

\circ
If
n\geq 1
we can use (
4.15
) to rewrite

\displaystyle h(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s^{n}}\Big(\frac{s^{2}}{1+s^{2}}g_{\infty}+\widetilde{g}(s)\Big)=\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{|s|^{n}}\Big),\quad\text{as }|s|\rightarrow\infty,

\displaystyle h(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{1+s^{2}}f_{0}+s\widetilde{f}(s)=\mathcal{O}(|s|),\quad\text{as }|s|\rightarrow 0.

These asymptotics prove that
h\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta})
, and hence by (
4.15
) also

s\widetilde{f}(s)=h(s)-\frac{s}{1+s^{2}}f_{0}\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{0}(D_{\theta}).

Then, by Proposition
3.15
there is
\operatorname{ran}(\widetilde{f}(T))\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(T)
, hence
\operatorname{ran}(f(T))\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(T)
by the representation (
4.16
), and

h(T)=T\widetilde{f}(T)+T(1+T^{2})^{-1}f_{0}=Tf(T).

In both cases we now have verified that
\operatorname{ran}(f(T))\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(T)
and the identity (
4.17
). Since we have also shown that
h\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
and
s^{n}h=s^{n+1}f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
, it follows from the induction assumption that
\operatorname{ran}(h(T))\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(T^{n})
and

\circ

Theorem 4.15

.

be bisectorial of angle

, and

4.7
, with
h_{0}=(g_{0}-c)^{2}=0
and
h_{\infty}=(g_{\infty}-c)^{2}\neq 0
. An interior zero of infinite order, or zeros which accumulate at an interior point, would cause
h
to vanish identically on this connected component, but this is not possible since
\lim_{|s|\rightarrow\infty}h(s)=(g_{\infty}-c)^{2}\neq 0
. Hence, there exist finitely many points
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
q_{1},\dots,q_{m}\in D_{\theta}\setminus\mathbb{R}
, such that the zeros of
h
are given by
p_{1},\dots,p_{n},[q_{1}],\dots,[q_{m}]
. Let us call the multiplicities of those zeros
k_{1},\dots,k_{n}
and
l_{1},\dots,l_{m}
, respectively. Let us then consider the function

(4.33)

r(s)=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\frac{s-p_{i}}{1+s^{2}}\Big)^{k_{i}}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{m}\Big(\frac{Q_{q_{j}}(s)}{1+s^{2}}\Big)^{l_{j}},\qquad s\in D_{\theta}.

It is then clear that every factor
\frac{s-p_{i}}{1+s^{2}},\frac{Q_{q_{j}}(s)}{1+s^{2}}\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
and hence also
r\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
. Then, by Lemma
4.2
, the shifted functions

\widehat{h}(s):=h(s)-h_{\infty}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{r}(s):=r(s)-r_{\infty},\qquad s\in D_{\theta},

satisfy the integrability condition (
3.14
). For every
s\in D_{\theta}\setminus\big(\{p_{1}\},\dots,\{p_{n}\}\cup[q_{1}]\cup\dots\cup[q_{m}]\big)
, we can then define

k(s):=\frac{r(s)}{h(s)}=\big(r_{\infty}+\widehat{r}(s)\big)\Big(\frac{1}{h_{\infty}}-\frac{\widehat{h}(s)}{h_{\infty}h(s)}\Big)=\frac{r_{\infty}}{h_{\infty}}+\underbrace{\frac{\widehat{r}(s)}{h_{\infty}}-\frac{r_{\infty}\widehat{h}(s)}{h_{\infty}h(s)}-\frac{\widehat{r}(s)\widehat{h}(s)}{h_{\infty}h(s)}}_{=:\widehat{k}(s)}.

This function
\widehat{k}
then also satisfies the condition (
3.14
). Moreover, since
r
and
h
have the same zeros with the same multiplicity,
k
extends to an intrinsic function on
D_{\theta}
. For this extension we can then define the functional calculus
k(T)
as in (
4.4
), and get

(4.34)

r(T)=(hk)(T)=h(T)k(T)=(Q_{c}\circ g)(T)k(T)=Q_{c}[g(T)]k(T),

where in the last equation we used Corollary
4.8
.

Let us now assume that
c\notin g(\sigma_{S}(T))
. Since
g
is intrinsic and
\sigma_{S}(T)
axially symmetric, this means
g(s)\notin[c]
for every
s\in\sigma_{S}(T)
. However, since
p_{1},\dots,p_{n},[q_{1}],\dots,[q_{m}]
are zeros of
Q_{c}\circ g
, there is
g(p_{1}),\dots,g(p_{n}),g(q_{1}),\dots,g(q_{m})\in[c]
, from which we conclude that
p_{1},\dots,p_{n},q_{1},\dots,q_{m}\notin\sigma_{S}(T)
. Hence the operator

r(T)=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}\big((T-p_{i})(1+T^{2})^{-1}\big)^{k_{i}}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{m}\big(Q_{q_{j}}[T](1+T^{2})^{-1}\big)^{l_{j}},

is bijective and by (
4.34
) also
Q_{c}[g(T)]k(T)
. Since moreover
Q_{c}[g(T)]k(T)=k(T)Q_{c}[g(T)]
commute, there follows the bijectivity of
Q_{c}[g(T)]
. Since
Q_{c}[g(T)]
is bounded, the inverse
Q_{c}[g(T)]^{-1}
is bounded as well and hence
c\in\rho_{S}(g(T))
. This is a contradiction to
c\in\sigma_{S}(g(T))
, which means that the assumption
c\notin g(\sigma_{S}(T))
was wrong and we have proven
c\in g(\sigma_{S}(T))
.

Case 4: If
c\notin\{g_{0},g_{\infty}\}
, we again consider the function
h
from (
4.32
) and
r
from (
4.33
). Contrary to above, here the values
h_{0}=(g_{0}-c)^{2}\neq 0
and
h_{\infty}=(g_{\infty}-c)^{2}\neq 0
are both nonzero. It is now straight forward to show that in the decomposition of Definition
4.1
, we can write for every
s\in D_{\theta}\setminus\big(\{p_{1}\},\dots,\{p_{n}\}\cup[q_{1}]\cup\dots\cup[q_{m}]\big)
the function
k(s):=\frac{r(s)}{h(s)}
as

\displaystyle k(s)-\frac{r_{0}}{h_{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{s^{2}}{1+s^{2}}(r_{\infty}h_{0}-r_{0}h_{\infty})+\widetilde{r}(s)h_{0}-r_{0}\widetilde{h}(s)}{h_{0}h(s)},\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle k(s)-\frac{r_{\infty}}{h_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}(r_{0}h_{\infty}-r_{\infty}h_{0})+\widetilde{r}(s)h_{\infty}-r_{\infty}\widetilde{h}(s)}{h_{\infty}h(s)}.

This form shows that there exists some
0<r<R
, such that the integrability conditions (
4.1
) are satisfied. As above, the function
k
extends to an intrinsic function on
D_{\theta}
and from Lemma
4.2
we then get
k\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
. This means that
k(T)
is defined via the extended
\omega
-functional calculus and there is

r(T)=(hk)(T)=h(T)k(T)=(Q_{c}\circ g)(T)k(T)=Q_{c}[g(T)]k(T),

where in the last equation we used Corollary
4.8
. Since this is the same formula as in (
4.34
), we also conclude by the same argument that
c\in g(\sigma_{S}(T))
.
∎

5.
The
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus

Taking advantage of the extended
\omega
-functional calculus from Section
4
, we will now define the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus for the so called class of regularizable functions. In particular, functions with polynomial growth at infinity will be covered by this calculus, and under the additional assumption that
T
is injective, also a polynomial growth at zero is allowed.

Definition 5.1
.

For every
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
let
D_{\theta}
be the double sector from (
2.12
). Then we define the function spaces of

i)

\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm reg}(D_{\theta}):=\Big\{f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(D_{\theta})\;\Big|\;\exists e\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta}):ef\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta}),\,e(T)\text{ injective}\Big\}

ii)

\mathcal{N}^{\rm reg}(D_{\theta}):=\Big\{g\in\mathcal{N}(D_{\theta})\;\Big|\;\exists e\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta}):eg\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta}),\,e(T)\text{ injective}\Big\}

For every function
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm reg}(D_{\theta})
, the function
e
is called a
regularizer
of
f
. Note that the operator
e(T)
is well defined via the extended
\omega
-functional calculus (
4.3
).

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then for every
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
, we have

\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(D_{\theta})\;\bigg|\;\begin{array}[]{l}\exists r>0,f_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}:\int_{0}^{r}|f(te^{J\phi})-f_{0}|\frac{dt}{t}<\infty,\;J\in\mathbb{S},\phi\in I_{\theta},\\
\exists k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\,C\geq 0:|f(s)|\leq C(1+|s|^{k}),\,s\in D_{\theta}.\end{array}\bigg\}\subseteq\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm reg}(D_{\theta}).

If
T
is injective, we even have

\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(D_{\theta})\;\bigg|\;\exists k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\,C\geq 0:|f(s)|\leq C\Big(\frac{1}{|s|^{k}}+|s|^{k}\Big),\,s\in D_{\theta}\bigg\}\subseteq\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm reg}(D_{\theta}).

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(D_{\theta})
, such that there exists
r>0
,
f_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
C\geq 0
with

|f(s)|\leq C(1+|s|^{k})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{0}^{r}|f(te^{J\phi})-f_{0}|\frac{dt}{t}<\infty,\qquad J\in\mathbb{S},\,\phi\in I_{\theta}.

Then let us choose the regularizer

e(s):=\frac{1}{(1+s^{2})^{k+1}},

which is clearly an element
e\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
. The corresponding functional calculus is according to Theorem
4.10
given by
e(T)=(1+T^{2})^{-k-1}
. Since
1+T^{2}
is bijective due to
J\in\rho_{S}(T)
, for every
J\in\mathbb{S}
, the operator
e(T)
is injective. Moreover, we can write the product
ef
as

\displaystyle e(s)f(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}f_{0}+\frac{1}{(1+s^{2})^{k+1}}\big(f(s)-(1+s^{2})^{k}f_{0}\big)

(5.1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}f_{0}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{(1+s^{2})^{k+1}}(f(s)-f_{0})}_{=:\widetilde{g_{0}}(s)}-\sum\limits_{l=1}^{k}{k\choose l}\underbrace{\frac{s^{2l}}{(1+s^{2})^{k+1}}}_{=:\widetilde{g}_{l}(s)}f_{0}.

In order to show
ef\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
, we have to verify
\widetilde{g}_{l}\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
for every
l\in\{0,\dots,k\}
. The function
\widetilde{g}_{0}
can be estimated in two ways, namely

|\widetilde{g}_{0}(s)|=\frac{|f(s)-f_{0}|}{|1+s^{2}|^{k+1}}\leq\Big(\frac{2}{1+\cos(2\theta)}\Big)^{\frac{k+1}{2}}\frac{|f(s)-f_{0}|}{(1+|s|^{2})^{k+1}}\leq\Big(\frac{2}{1+\cos(2\theta)}\Big)^{\frac{k+1}{2}}\begin{cases}|f(s)-f_{0}|,\\
\frac{C(1+|s|^{k})+|f_{0}|}{(1+|s|^{2})^{k+1}},\end{cases}

where we used that
\sup_{s\in D_{\theta}}\frac{1+|s|^{2}}{|1+s^{2}|}=\big(\frac{2}{1+\cos(2\theta)}\big)^{1/2}
, which can easily be verified. This estimate shows that the integral

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle|\widetilde{g}_{0}(te^{J\phi})|\frac{dt}{t}=\int_{0}^{r}|\widetilde{g}_{0}(te^{J\phi})|\frac{dt}{t}+\int_{r}^{\infty}|\widetilde{g}_{0}(te^{J\phi})|\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{2}{1+\cos(2\theta)}\Big)^{\frac{k+1}{2}}\bigg(\int_{0}^{r}|f(te^{J\phi})-f_{0}|\frac{dt}{t}+\int_{r}^{\infty}\frac{C(1+|t|^{k})+|f_{0}|}{(1+|t|^{2})^{k+1}}\frac{dt}{t}\bigg)<\infty,

is finite and hence
\widetilde{g}_{0}\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
. Next, the functions
\widetilde{g}_{l}
,
l\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, can be estimated by

|\widetilde{g}_{l}(s)|=\frac{|s|^{2l}}{|1+s^{2}|^{k+1}}\leq\Big(\frac{2}{1+\cos(2\theta)}\Big)^{\frac{k+1}{2}}\frac{|s|^{2l}}{(1+|s|^{2})^{k+1}}.

This estimate shows that
\widetilde{g}_{l}\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
. Altogether we have proven that the product
ef
in the representation (
5.1
) is an element
ef\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
. This shows that
e
is a regularizer of
f
according to Definition
5.1
and hence
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm reg}(D_{\theta})
.

Let us now additionally assume that
T
is injective. Then, for every
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}(D_{\theta})
, which satisfies the estimate

|f(s)|\leq C\Big(\frac{1}{|s|^{k}}+|s|^{k}\Big),

we choose the regularizer

e(s):=\frac{s^{k+1}}{(1+s^{2})^{k+1}}.

Then clearly
e\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})\subseteq\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
and by Theorem
4.10
, the corresponding functional calculus is given by
e(T)=T^{k+1}(1+T^{2})^{-k-1}
. Since
1+T^{2}
is bijective due to
J\in\rho_{S}(T)
for every
J\in\mathbb{S}
, and since we also assumed that
T
is injective, also
e(T)
is injective. Finally, we can estimate the product
ef
by

|e(s)f(s)|\leq C\frac{|s|^{k+1}}{|1+s^{2}|^{k+1}}\Big(\frac{1}{|s|^{k}}+|s|^{k}\Big)\leq C\Big(\frac{2}{1+\cos(2\theta)}\Big)^{\frac{k+1}{2}}\frac{|s|+|s|^{2k+1}}{(1+|s|^{2})^{k+1}}.

This estimate shows that
ef\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})\subseteq\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
. Hence
e
is a regularizer of
f
according to Definition
5.1
and we conclude that
f\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm reg}(D_{\theta})
.
∎

For those regularizable functions in Definition
5.1
we now define the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus.

Definition 5.3

(
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then for every
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm reg}(D_{\theta})
,
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
, we define the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus

(5.2)

f(T):=e(T)^{-1}(ef)(T),

where
e
is a regularizer of
f
according to Definition
5.1
.

Note, that definition (
5.2
) is independent of the choice of the regularizer
e
. This is because from the product rule (
4.8
) we obtain for any two regularizers
e_{1},e_{2}
the equality

\displaystyle e_{1}(T)^{-1}(e_{1}f)(T)

\displaystyle=e_{1}(T)^{-1}e_{2}(T)^{-1}e_{2}(T)(e_{1}f)(T)=(e_{2}e_{1})(T)^{-1}(e_{2}e_{1}f)(T)

\displaystyle=(e_{1}e_{2})(T)^{-1}(e_{1}e_{2}f)(T)=e_{2}(T)^{-1}e_{1}(T)^{-1}e_{1}(T)(e_{2}f)(T)

\displaystyle=e_{2}(T)^{-1}(e_{2}f)(T).

Remark 5.4
.

Note that the natural choice of the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus (
5.2
) for right slice hyperholomorphic functions, would be
f(T):=(fe)(T)e(T)^{-1}
. However, this is not a good definition since
f(T)
is defined on
\operatorname{ran}(e(T))
and hence depends on the choice of the regularizer. It is an open problem how the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus for right slice hyperholomorphic functions has to be defined.

Let us now collect some basic properties of the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus (
5.2
).

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
, and
f,g\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm reg}(D_{\theta})
,
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then we have:

i)

The operator
f(T)\in\mathcal{K}(V)
is right-linear and closed, with domain

(5.3)

\operatorname{dom}(f(T))=\big\{v\in V\;|\;(ef)(T)v\in\operatorname{ran}(e(T))\big\},

where
e\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
is an arbitrary regularizer of
f
.

ii)

For every
a\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
there holds the linearity properties

(5.4)

(f+g)(T)\supseteq f(T)+g(T)\qquad\text{and}\qquad(fa)(T)=f(T)a.

Proof.

i)  The operators
e(T)
and
(ef)(T)
in the definition (
5.2
) are bounded, and
e(T)^{-1}
is closed as the inverse of a bounded operator. Hence
f(T)=e(T)^{-1}(ef)(T)
is closed as the product of a closed and a bounded operator. The explicit form of the domain (
5.3
) is exactly the one of the product of unbounded operators.

ii)  If
e_{1}
is a regularizer of
f
and
e_{2}
a regularizer of
g
, then it is clear that the product
e_{1}e_{2}
is a regularizer for both functions
f
and
g
as well as for their sum
f+g
. Hence we can write the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus of the sum as

\displaystyle(f+g)(T)

\displaystyle=(e_{1}e_{2})^{-1}(e_{1}e_{2}(f+g))(T)

\displaystyle=(e_{1}e_{2})^{-1}\big((e_{1}e_{2}f)(T)+(e_{1}e_{2}g)(T)\big)

\displaystyle\supseteq(e_{1}e_{2})^{-1}(e_{1}e_{2}f)(T)+(e_{1}e_{2})^{-1}(e_{1}e_{2}g)(T)=f(T)+g(T),

where from the second to the third line we used the inclusion
A(B+C)\supseteq AB+AC
of unbounded operators.

For the second equation in (
5.4
) we note that the regularizer
e_{1}
of
f
is also a regularizer of
fa
. Hence we can write the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus (
5.2
) for
fa
as

(fa)(T)=e_{1}(T)^{-1}(e_{1}fa)(T)=e_{1}(T)(e_{1}f)(T)a=f(T)a.\qed

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. If
T
is not injective, then for every
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm reg}(D_{\theta})
there exists the limit

f_{0}:=\lim\limits_{|s|\rightarrow 0^{+}}f(s).

Moreover, if
f\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm reg}(D_{\theta})
is intrinsic, we also have

\ker(T)\subseteq\ker(f(T)-f_{0}).

Proof.

Since
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm reg}(D_{\theta})
, there exists some
e\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
, such that
ef\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
and
e(T)
is injective. Then by Proposition
4.6
there holds the inclusion

(5.5)

\ker(T)\subseteq\ker(e(T)-e_{0}).

Since
e(T)
is injective and
T
is not injective, there has to be
e_{0}\neq 0
and consequently also
e(s)\neq 0
for
|s|
small enough. Hence, there exists the limit

\lim\limits_{|s|\rightarrow 0^{+}}f(s)=\lim\limits_{|s|\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{1}{e(s)}(ef)(s)=\frac{1}{e_{0}}(ef)_{0}.

Next, for every
v\in\ker(T)
we have
e(T)v=e_{0}v
by (
5.5
) and hence
v\in\operatorname{dom}(e(T)^{-1})
with

e(T)^{-1}v=\frac{1}{e_{0}}v.

Moreover, since
f\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm reg}(D_{\theta})
is intrinsic, there also holds

(ef)(T)v=(ef)_{0}v

by Proposition
4.6
, and we obtain

f(T)v=e(T)^{-1}(ef)(T)v=e(T)^{-1}(ef)_{0}v=\frac{1}{e_{0}}(ef)_{0}v=f_{0}v.\qed

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
,
g\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm reg}(D_{\theta})
,
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm reg}(D_{\theta})
,
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then there holds the product rule

(5.6)

(gf)(T)\supseteq g(T)f(T),

and the domains of the both sides are connected by

(5.7)

\operatorname{dom}(g(T)f(T))=\operatorname{dom}((gf)(T))\cap\operatorname{dom}(f(T)).

Proof.

Let
e_{1}
be a regularizer of
f
and
e_{2}
a regularizer of
g
. Then it is clear that
e_{1}e_{2}
is a regularizer of the product
fg
. Since
g
is intrinsic, there holds the commutation relation

e_{1}(T)(e_{2}g)(T)=(e_{2}g)(T)e_{1}(T)

from Corollary
4.12
iii). Consequently, we get

\displaystyle e_{1}(T)^{-1}(e_{2}g)(T)

\displaystyle\supseteq e_{1}(T)^{-1}(e_{2}g)(T)e_{1}(T)e_{1}(T)^{-1}

(5.8)

\displaystyle=e_{1}(T)^{-1}e_{1}(T)(e_{2}g)(T)e_{1}(T)^{-1}=(e_{2}g)(T)e_{1}(T)^{-1}.

Hence the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus (
5.2
) of the product
(gf)(T)
can be written as

\displaystyle(gf)(T)

\displaystyle=(e_{1}e_{2})(T)^{-1}(e_{1}e_{2}gf)(T)=e_{2}(T)^{-1}e_{1}(T)^{-1}(e_{2}g)(T)(e_{1}f)(T)

\displaystyle\supseteq e_{2}(T)^{-1}(e_{2}g)(T)e_{1}(T)^{-1}(e_{1}f)(T)=g(T)f(T).

For the domain identity (
5.7
) we start with
v\in\operatorname{dom}(g(T)f(T))
. Then there is clearly
v\in\operatorname{dom}(f(T))
and by the already proven product rule (
5.6
) also
v\in\operatorname{dom}((gf)(T))
.

For the inverse inclusion in (
5.7
), let
v\in\operatorname{dom}((gf)(T))\cap\operatorname{dom}(f(T))
. Then there is clearly
v\in\operatorname{dom}(f(T))
, and from the commutation relation (
5.8
) there follows the identity

\displaystyle(e_{2}g)(T)f(T)v

\displaystyle=(e_{2}g)(T)e_{1}(T)^{-1}(e_{1}f)(T)v

(5.9)

\displaystyle=e_{1}(T)^{-1}(e_{2}g)(T)(e_{1}f)(T)v=e_{1}(T)^{-1}(e_{1}e_{2}gf)(T)v.

Since also
v\in\operatorname{dom}((gf)(T))
, there is
(e_{1}e_{2}gf)(T)v\in\operatorname{ran}((e_{1}e_{2})(T))
and hence

(5.10)

e_{1}(T)^{-1}(e_{1}e_{2}gf)(T)v\in\operatorname{ran}(e_{2}(T)).

Combining now the identity (
5.9
) with (
5.10
), shows that
(e_{2}g)(T)f(T)v\in\operatorname{ran}(e_{2}(T))
, which means
f(T)\in\operatorname{dom}(g(T))
. Hence we have proven
v\in\operatorname{dom}(g(T)f(T))
.
∎

Corollary 5.8
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
,
g\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm reg}(D_{\theta})
,
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
, and
p
an intrinsic polynomial. Then there is
p\circ g\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm reg}(D_{\theta})
and

(p\circ g)(T)\supseteq p[g(T)].

Proof.

Note that by the linearity property (
5.4
) it is enough to consider monomials
p(s)=s^{n}
. Let
e
be a regularizer of
g
. Then it is clear that
e^{n}
is a regularizer of
g^{n}
. Using the relations

(e^{n})(T)=e(T)^{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad(e^{n}g^{n})(T)=(eg)(T)^{n},

which follow from the product rule (
4.8
), as well as the inclusion

e(T)^{-1}(eg)(T)\supseteq(eg)(T)e(T)^{-1},

from (
5.8
), we can write the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus of of
g^{n}
as

(g^{n})(T)=(e^{n})(T)^{-1}(e^{n}g^{n})(T)=e(T)^{-n}(eg)(T)^{n}\supseteq\big(e(T)^{-1}(eg)(T)\big)^{n}=g(T)^{n}.\qed

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
and
p,q
be two intrinsic polynomials, such that
q
does not admit any zeros in
\overline{D_{\omega}}
. Then,
q[T]
is bijective, there exists some
\theta\in(\omega,\pi)
such that
\frac{p}{q}\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm reg}(D_{\theta})
, and we have

(5.11)

\Big(\frac{p}{q}\Big)(T)=p[T]q[T]^{-1}.

Proof.

If we denote
n:=\deg(p)
, we choose the regularizer

e(s):=\frac{1}{(1+s^{2})^{n}}.

Then it is clear that
e\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
. Since
q
has no zero at
s=0
, we obtain the asymptotics

e(s)\frac{p(s)}{q(s)}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}(1),&\text{as }|s|\rightarrow 0^{+},\\
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{|s|^{n}}),&\text{as }|s|\rightarrow\infty,\end{cases}

which implies that
e\frac{p}{q}\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
. Since, by Theorem
4.10
,
e(T)=(1+T^{2})^{-n}
is also injective,
e
is a regularizer of
\frac{p}{q}
. Using also Theorem
4.10
, the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus of
\frac{p}{q}
is then given by

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{p}{q}\Big)(T)

\displaystyle=e(T)^{-1}\Big(e\frac{p}{q}\Big)(T)

\displaystyle=(1+T^{2})^{n}p[T]\big((1+s^{2})^{n}q\big)[T]^{-1}

\displaystyle=(1+T^{2})^{n}p[T](1+T^{2})^{-n}q[T]^{-1}

\displaystyle=p[T](1+T^{2})^{n}(1+T^{2})^{-n}q[T]^{-1}=p[T]q[T]^{-1}.\qed

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
,
p
an intrinsic polynomial, and
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}^{\rm reg}(D_{\theta})
,
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then we have

(pf)(T)\supseteq p[T]f(T).

Proof.

If we use the product rule (
5.6
) and the rational functional calculus (
5.11
) with
q=1
, we immediately get

(pf)(T)\supseteq p(T)f(T)=p[T]f(T).\qed

Corollary 5.11
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
,
g\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm reg}(D_{\theta})
,
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then we have the following commutation relations:

i)

For every
B\in\mathcal{B}(V)
with
TB\supseteq BT
, we have

Bg(T)\subseteq g(T)B.

ii)

For every
f\in\mathcal{N}^{\rm bnd}(D_{\theta})
, we have

f(T)g(T)\subseteq g(T)f(T).

Proof.

Let
e
be a regularizer of
g
.

i)  By Corollary
4.12
i) we have
B(eg)(T)=(eg)(T)B
and
Be(T)=e(T)B
. Hence, we get

e(T)^{-1}B\supseteq e(T)^{-1}Be(T)e(T)^{-1}=e(T)^{-1}e(T)Be(T)^{-1}=Be(T)^{-1},

from which we conclude

g(T)B=e(T)^{-1}(eg)(T)B=e(T)^{-1}B(eg)(T)\supseteq Be(T)^{-1}(eg)(T)=Bg(T).

ii)  From the operator inclusion (
5.8
), we obtain

\displaystyle g(T)f(T)

\displaystyle=e(T)^{-1}(eg)(T)f(T)=e(T)^{-1}f(T)(eg)(T)

\displaystyle\supseteq f(T)e(T)^{-1}(eg)(T)=f(T)g(T).\qed
