Characterization of Efficient Influence Function for Off-Policy Evaluation Under Optimal Policies Journal: arxiv
Haoyu Wei

Abstract
Off-policy evaluation (OPE) provides a powerful framework for estimating the value of a counterfactual policy using observational data, without the need for additional experimentation. Despite recent progress in robust and efficient OPE across various settings, rigorous efficiency analysis of OPE under an estimated optimal policy remains limited. In this paper, we establish a concise characterization of the efficient influence function (EIF) for the value function under optimal policy within canonical Markov decision process models. Specifically, we provide the sufficient conditions for the existence of the EIF and characterize its expression. We also give the conditions under which the EIF does not exist.

1
Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) focusing on developing optimal policies for sequential decision-making to maximize long-term rewards,
(
Sutton & Barto 2018
)
have been serving as more and more important frontier in various fields. A critical component of RL is off-policy evaluation (OPE), which estimates the mean reward of a policy, termed the evaluation policy, using data collected under another policy, known as the behavior policy. OPE is essential in offline RL, where only historical datasets are available, precluding new experiments
(
Luedtke & Van Der Laan 2016
;
Agarwal et al. 2019
;
Uehara et al. 2022
)
.

Recent years have witnessed substantial progress in developing statistically efficient OPE methods, with various approaches demonstrating semiparametric efficiency under different model settings
(
Jiang & Li 2016
;
Kallus & Uehara 2020
;
Shi et al. 2021
)
. However, all of these existing analyses focus on scenarios where the evaluation policy is fixed and predetermined.
A more challenging yet practical scenario arises when the evaluation policy itself is estimated from data, particularly when this policy is designed to be optimal with respect to some criterion. In this context, the statistical properties of OPE become more complex due to the additional estimation uncertainty introduced by the policy optimization process.

In contrast, in the causal inference literature, such phenomena have been studied extensively in the
optimal treatment regime
literature
(
Laber et al. 2014
;
Kosorok & Laber 2019
;
Athey & Wager 2021
)
. These works have established important results regarding the estimation of value functions under optimal treatment rules, but their direct application to the sequential decision-making context of RL presents additional challenges due to the temporal dependencies and potentially infinite horizons involved.

In this paper, we provide a simple result for the characterization of the efficient influence function (EIF) for the value function under optimal policy within canonical Markov decision process models. Specifically, we establish sufficient conditions for the existence of the EIF and derive its explicit expression under the condition that the optimal policy is unique and deterministic. We also identify and prove conditions under which the EIF does not exist.
Additionally, we demonstrate that various existing methods, including the value functions in
Fitted Q Iteration

(
Munos & Szepesvári 2008
;
Shi et al. 2024a
, FQI, for example)
and
Fitted Actor-Critic

(
Xu et al. 2021
;
Kumar et al. 2023
, FAC, see)
, do achieve semiparametric efficiency without parameterization or fixing the policies in cases where the optimal policy is unique and deterministic.

2
Problem Formulation

2.1
Data Generating Process and Parameter of Interest

We consider observational data generated from a canonical
Markov decision process (MDP).
Specifically, at a given time
t
, let
(S_{t},A_{t},R_{t})
denote the observed state-action-reward tripleruplet.
Let
O
be a shorthand for a data tuple
(S,A,R,S^{\prime})
.
Given an offline observable dataset

\big\{O_{it}:1\leq i\leq N,0\leq t\leq T\big\}
with

O_{it}=(S_{it},A_{it},R_{it})

generated by a behavior policy
b(\cdot\mid S)
, where
i
indexes the
i
-th episode and
t
indexes the
t
-th time point.
For any
fixed
target policy
\pi(a\mid s)
, OPE geneally aim to evaluate the mean return under this fixed policy as

\eta(\pi)=\mathrm{E}^{\sim\pi}\bigg[\sum_{t=0}^{+\infty}\gamma^{t}R_{t}\bigg]

with a valid confidence interval,
where
\mathrm{E}^{\sim\pi}
denotes expectation when the system follows the policy
\pi
.
Slightly different from the existent semiparametric study on OPE, instead of considering the arbitrary
\pi
, in this paper we will focus on a specific target policy: the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
, which maximize the
\eta(\pi)
in the set of all possible policies
\mathcal{P}
. Specially, the parameter of insterests we will focus on is

\displaystyle\eta^{*}:=\eta(\pi^{*})=\mathrm{E}^{\sim\pi^{*}}\bigg[\sum_{t=0}^{+\infty}\gamma^{t}R_{t}\bigg]\quad\text{ such that }\quad\pi^{*}=\arg\max_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}}\eta(\pi).

To ensure the value function is identifiable, as commonly used in various OPE literature, we present the following assumptions.
For simplicity, we use
f(x\mid y)
to represent the conditional density of
X
given
Y=y
.

{assumption}

[Data Structure & Observations]
The observations are i.i.d. copies of the trajectory
\{(S_{t},A_{t},R_{t},S_{t+1})\}_{t\geq 0}
, following the data-generating process (DGP):

S_{t+1}\sim f(s_{t+1}\mid A_{t},S_{t}),\qquad R_{t}\sim f(r_{t}\mid A_{t},S_{t}),\qquad A_{t}\sim b(a_{t}\mid S_{t}).

{assumption}

[Markov, Conditional Independence, & Time-Homogeneity]

f(a_{t},s_{t}\mid a_{t-1},s_{t-1},a_{t-2},s_{t-2},\ldots)=f(a_{t},s_{t}\mid a_{t-1},s_{t-1})
for any
t\geq 1
;

f(a_{t}\mid a_{t-1},s_{t})=b(a_{t}\mid s_{t})
. The reward
R_{t}
only depends on
A_{t}
and
S_{t}
;
All conditional density functions
b(a\mid s)
,
f(r\mid a,s)
, and
f(s^{\prime}\mid a,s)
remain fixed over time.

2.2
Two Estimators under Fixed Policies

As shown in various literature, Assumption
2.1
and Assumption
2.1
is sufficient for identifying
\eta(\pi)
for any given policy
\pi\in\mathcal{P}
.
Here we first discuss the commonly-used estimating methods.

The first estimating method involves analyzing the aggregate mean return under any target policy
\pi
, and we introduce two “state-action” value functions, commonly referred to as the
Q
-function. The
Q
-function evaluates the expected cumulative reward given a specific action at a specific state
(
Uehara et al. 2022
)
under a fixed hypothetical policy
\pi
, defined as

Q(a,s;\pi):=\mathrm{E}^{\sim\pi}\bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\gamma^{k}R_{t+k}\mid A_{t}=a,S_{t}=s\bigg]

(1)

Then the value function can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\eta(\pi)=\mathrm{E}^{\sim\pi}\Bigg[\mathrm{E}^{\sim\pi}\bigg[\sum_{t=0}^{+\infty}\gamma^{t}R_{t}\mid A_{0},S_{0}\bigg]\Bigg]=\int Q(a_{0},s_{0};\pi)\pi(a_{0}\mid s_{0})f(s_{0})\mathrm{d}a_{0}\,\mathrm{d}s_{0},

under any policy
\pi
where
f(s_{0})
is the density of the initial state distribution. Usually, the above display ensures it is sufficient to learn the cumulative rewards for any given policy of interest
\pi
, and we also define a weighted average of the
Q
-function as
V(s;\pi)=\int Q(a,s;\pi)\pi(a\mid s)\,\mathrm{d}a
.

The second estimator is the marginal importance sampling
(MIS) estimator, which aims to address the curse of horizon. The marginal ratio is defined as

\omega(s;\pi):=(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\gamma^{t}f_{\sim\pi,t}(s)}{f_{+\infty}(s)},

(2)

where
f_{\sim\pi,t}(s)
is the density function of the state
S_{t}
under policy
\pi
, and
f_{+\infty}(s)
is the marginal density function of the stationary state distribution
S_{+\infty}
. Under the stationarity assumption, we have the following identity

\eta(\pi)=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\mathrm{E}\big[\omega(S;\pi)\mathrm{E}^{\sim\pi}[R\mid S]\big].

An important observation is that both the
Q
-function (
1
) and MIS ratio (
2
) are included in the semiparametric efficient estimator for
\eta(\pi)
.

2.3
Characterization and Issues

Assume the trajectory
O=O_{1:T}\sim P_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
and let
\Psi^{*}:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be defined as

\Psi^{*}(P):=\mathrm{E}_{P}\big[Q(P)\big(A_{0},S_{0};\pi^{*}(P)\big)\pi^{*}(P)(A_{0}\mid S_{0})\big]

where
\pi^{*}(P)(\cdot\mid s)=\arg\max_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}}Q(P)(a,s;\pi)
is the optimal policy
under the law
P
. Then
\Psi^{*}(P_{0})=\eta(\pi^{*})
is well-defined. Instead of focusing on
\pi^{*}(P_{0})
itself, here we only focus on the value function under the optimal policy
\Psi^{*}(P_{0})
, whereas the discussion for
\pi^{*}(P_{0})
can be found in
Kosorok & Laber 2019
;
Athey & Wager 2021
;
Luo et al. 2024
.
Define the suboptimal gap between
\pi
and the optimal policy
\pi^{*}:=\pi^{*}(P)
as

\Delta(P)(\pi;A,S):=Q(P)(A,S;\pi^{*})-Q(P)(A,S;\pi)

which also implies
\pi^{*}(P)(A,S)=\arg\min_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}}\Delta(P)(\pi;A,S)
.
Define the new functional

\Psi(P;\pi)=\mathrm{E}_{P}\big[Q(P)\big(A_{0},S_{0};\pi\big)\pi(A_{0}\mid S_{0})\big]

then
\Psi\big(P;\pi^{*}(P)\big)=\Psi^{*}(P)
, but in general, we will not have
\Psi\big(P_{1};\pi^{*}(P_{2})\big)=\Psi^{*}(P_{1})
if
P_{1}\neq P_{2}
.

Suppose
\{S_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is stationary, as shown in
Uehara et al. 2020
;
Shi et al. 2024b
;
Shi et al. 2021
, when for any fixed
\pi
, the efficient influence function (EIF) for
\Psi(P;\pi)
at
P_{0}
evaluated with
O
on the full non-parametric space
\mathcal{M}_{\text{nonpar}}
is

\displaystyle S^{\text{eff, nonpar}}\{\Psi(P;\pi)\}\Big|_{P=P_{0}}(O)

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\omega(P_{0})(S;\pi)\frac{\pi(A\mid S)}{b(P_{0})(A\mid S)}\big[R+\gamma V(P_{0})(S^{\prime};\pi)-Q(P_{0})(A,S;\pi)\big]

\displaystyle+V(P_{0})(S;\pi)-\Psi(P_{0};\pi).

Since for any regular asymptotic normal (RAL) estimator
\widehat{\Psi}(\widehat{P};\pi)
, there exists a unqiue influence function
(
Tsiatis 2006
, IF,)
such that

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}(\widehat{P};\pi)-\Psi(P_{0};\pi)=\sqrt{N}\mathbb{P}_{N}\operatorname{IF}(P_{0};\pi)(O)+o_{P_{0}}\big(N^{-1/2}\big),

the EIF
S^{\text{eff, nonpar}}\{\Psi(P;\pi)\}\big|_{P=P_{0}}(O)
serves an crucial role in semiparametric statistics, as it is a vaid IF minimizes the variance
\operatorname{var}_{P_{0}}\big(\operatorname{IF}(P_{0};\pi)(O)\big)
.

The set of all valid influence functions
\operatorname{IF}(P_{0};\pi)(O)
is uniquely characterized by the tangent space
\mathcal{T}
, i.e., the mean-square closure of the parametric submodels (for both nuisance tangent space and full tangent space), which is equivalently characterized by the collection of score functions of
any differentiable
paths at
P_{0}

(
Van Der Vaart et al. 1996
, see discussion in)
. Thus, specially, for any differentaible path
\{P_{\epsilon}:\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}\}\subsetneq\mathcal{M}
that goes through
P_{0}
at
\epsilon=0
, the parameter
\Psi(P;\pi)
must be pathwise differentiable along this path at
P_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon}\Psi(P_{\epsilon};\pi)\Big|_{\epsilon=0}=\mathrm{E}_{P_{0}}\Big[S^{\text{eff, nonpar}}\{\Psi(P;\pi)\}\big|_{P=P_{0}}(O)\dot{\ell}(O)\Big]

(4)

where
\dot{\ell}(O)
is the score function uniquely determined by this parametric submodel introduced by this path.

However, when we turn to the optimal value functional
\Psi^{*}(P)=\Psi\big(P;\pi^{*}(P)\big)
.
Intuitively, when there are no additional conditions, (
4
) may not be true anymore, as the optimal policy
\pi^{*}(P_{\epsilon})
along this specific path may also contribute to this derivative when: (i)
\Psi\big(P;\pi\big)
is sensitive to
\pi
; (ii) and
\pi^{*}(P)
is sensitive to
P
. This means even when the EIF of
\Psi^{*}(P)
,
S^{\text{eff, nonpar}}\{\Psi^{*}(P)\}
, does exist, the chain rule of Gateaux differentials
(
Shapiro 1990
)
implies

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{P_{0}}\Big[S^{\text{eff, nonpar}}\{\Psi^{*}(P)\}\big|_{P=P_{0}}(O)\dot{\ell}(O)\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon}\Psi\big(P_{\epsilon};\pi^{*}(P_{\epsilon})\big)\Big|_{\epsilon=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon}\Psi\big(P_{\epsilon};\pi^{*}(P_{0})\big)\Big|_{\epsilon=0}+\mathrm{D}\Big|_{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon}\pi^{*}(P_{\epsilon})|_{\epsilon=0}}\Psi\big(P_{0};\pi^{*}(P_{\epsilon})\big)\Big|_{\epsilon=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{P_{0}}\Big[S^{\text{eff, nonpar}}\{\Psi(P;\pi)\}\big|_{P=P_{0},\pi=\pi^{*}(P_{0})}(O)\dot{\ell}(O)\Big]+\mathds{D}\Big|_{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon}\pi^{*}(P_{\epsilon})|_{\epsilon=0}}\Psi\big(P_{0};\pi^{*}(P_{\epsilon})\big)\Big|_{\epsilon=0}

where
\mathds{D}|_{h}
is the Gateaux differential along the specified direction
h
. Therefore, if the second term above is not zero, then

S^{\text{eff, nonpar}}\{\Psi^{*}(P)\}\big|_{P=P_{0}}\neq S^{\text{eff, nonpar}}\{\Psi(P;\pi)\}\big|_{P=P_{0},\pi=\pi^{*}(P_{0})}.

Our goal is to establish a neat expression for
S^{\text{eff, nonpar}}\{\Psi^{*}(P)\}=\psi(P)(\eta^{*},\zeta;O)
with
\eta^{*}=\eta(\pi^{*})
and
\zeta
representing the nuisance functionals.
Then we can easily construct an RAL estimator with this IF by solving
\widehat{\eta}^{*}
in the following estimation equation

\mathbb{P}_{N}\Big[\psi(P_{0})(\eta^{*},\zeta;O)-\mathrm{E}_{P(\eta^{*},\widehat{\zeta}(\eta^{*}))}[\psi(P_{0})(\eta^{*},\zeta;O)]\Big]=0,

with

\displaystyle\sqrt{N}\|\widehat{\zeta}(\eta^{*})-\zeta(P_{0})\|_{P_{0},\infty}={O}_{P_{0}}(1)

(5)

From Chapter 3.3 of
Tsiatis 2006
, we know that
\widehat{\eta}^{*}
from (
5
) is an RAL estimator with the IF
S^{\text{eff, nonpar}}\{\Psi^{*}(P)\}
and thus achieves the semiparametric efficiency.

3
Existence of Efficient Influence Function and Its Expression

To establish a sufficient condition for ensuring the EIF
S^{\text{eff, nonpar}}\{\Psi^{*}(P)\}
exists, we present give the following additional regularity conditions on the distribution of
S
,
A
, and
R
respectively. Let
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
be the supports of states and actions respectively.

{assumption}
[Regularity]

•

States: the state space
\mathcal{S}
is compact and
S_{0}
is not one-pointly distributed;

•

Actions: the action space
\mathcal{A}
is finite;

•

Policies: There exist positive constants
\underline{c}_{\pi}
and
\overline{c}_{\pi}
such that
\underline{c}_{\pi}\leq\inf_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}}\operatorname{essinf}_{(a,s)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}}\pi(a\mid s)\leq\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}}\|\pi\|_{\infty}\leq\overline{c}_{\pi}
;

•

Rewards:
R
is bounded;

All conditions in Assumption
3
is commonly-used in various MDP literature including OPE as well as
Q
-learning
(
Levine et al. 2020
;
Uehara et al. 2022
;
Shi et al. 2022
;
Shi 2025
, for example, see the review papers)
, except the assumptions for the policies.
However, this assumption is also quite mild: as one can see that if
\mathcal{S}
is finite, then it will be automatically satisfied, and such assumption (
\mathcal{S}
is finite) is also quite commonly-used in OPE literature
(see
Xu et al. 2021
;
Shi et al. 2024b
;
Bian et al. 2024
, for instance)
.

Our first result shows that when the optimal policy is unique and deterministic, then the EIF
S^{\text{eff, nonpar}}\{\Psi^{*}(P)\}
exists and is equal to
S^{\text{eff, nonpar}}\{\Psi(P;\pi)\}
evaluated at
\pi=\pi^{*}
exactly. We first state the
unique and deterministic
requirement formally in the following assumption.

{assumption}

[Unique Deterministic Optimal Policy]
The optimal policy
\pi^{*}\in\mathcal{P}
exists uniquely and it is deterministic such that

\pi^{*}(P)(a\mid s)=\mathds{1}\Big\{a=\arg\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}Q(P)(a^{\prime},s;\pi^{*})\Big\}\quad\text{ for }\quad s\in\mathcal{S}

Indeed, in the context of infinite horizon MDPs, an optimal policy is often deterministic when the reward structure and transition dynamics are such that the value function’s maximization leads to a unique, deterministic action at each state
(
Puterman 2014
)
.
Additionally, as discussed in
Sutton et al. 1998
, in many practical MDPs, especially those with deterministic or mildly stochastic transition dynamics and rewards, the optimal policy tends to be deterministic.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
,
2.1
,
3
, and
3
hold.
Then the efficient influence function of
\Psi^{*}
exists and satisfies

S^{\text{eff, nonpar}}\{\Psi^{*}(P)\}\big|_{P=P_{0}}=S^{\text{eff, nonpar}}\{\Psi(P;\pi)\}\big|_{P=P_{0},\pi=\pi^{*}(P_{0})}.

Our second result is that when the optimal policy is not unique such that there exists a large number of states
S
that are indifferent (or incompatible) for at least two optimal polcies as stated in the following assumption, the influence function for
\Psi^{*}(P)
does not exist at
P=P_{0}
.

{assumption}

[Unrestricted Optimal Rules]
There exists at least one
\mathcal{P}\ni\pi^{\star}\neq\pi^{*}
such that

Q(P)\big(a,s;\pi^{\star}(P)\big)=Q(P)\big(a,s;\pi^{*}(P)\big)=\max_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}}Q(P)\big(a,s;\pi\big)

and

\mu\big\{s\in\mathcal{S}:\{a\in\mathcal{A}:\pi^{\star}(a\mid s)=\pi^{*}(a\mid s)\}\neq\varnothing\big\}>0.

Assumption
3
is commonly referred to as
Unrestricted Optimal Rules
in the literature
(
Robins & Rotnitzky 2014
, see discussion in)
, as it potentially leads to non-identification of the optimal policy. More significantly, this condition results in
\Psi^{*}(P_{0})
no longer being pathwise differentiable, which has profound implications for OPE under optimal policies.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
,
2.1
,
3
, and
3
hold. Then
\Psi^{*}(P)
does not have any influence function at
P=P_{0}
.

4
Examples

We present several commonly-used existing methods that achieve semiparametric efficiency without parameterization or fixing the policies
in cases where the optimal policy is unique and deterministic
.

Example 4.1
.

The first example is
Shi et al. 2024b
. Their Theorem 3 shows that conditional on the initial policy
\pi_{\text{old}}
and given fixed policy
\pi
, the value difference estimator
\widehat{\eta}_{1}(\pi,\pi_{\text{old}})
for

\eta_{1}(\pi,\pi_{\text{old}}):=\sum_{a,s}\big(\pi(a\mid s)-\pi_{\text{old}}(a\mid s)\big)\big(Q(a,s;\pi_{\text{old}})-V(s;\pi_{\text{old}})\big)d(s;\pi_{\text{old}}),

where
d(s^{\prime};\pi)=(1-\gamma)\sum_{a,s}\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^{t}f_{\sim\pi,t}(s^{\prime}\mid a,s)f_{+\infty}(s)
is the integrated discounted visitation probability function, achieves semiparametric efficiency. Given the conditions in their Theorem 2, the learned policy,
\widehat{\pi}_{\text{new}}
, satisfies (
5
) as
O_{P_{0}}(\sqrt{N}(NT)^{-\frac{\kappa_{0}(2\alpha+1)}{\alpha+1}})+o_{P_{0}}(\sqrt{N}(NT)^{-1/2})=O_{P_{0}}(1)
. Therefore, under the conditions in Theorem
3.1
, we establish that
\widehat{\eta}_{1}(\widehat{\pi}_{\text{new}},\pi_{\text{old}})
still achieves the semiparametric efficiency conditional on
\pi_{\text{old}}
.

Example 4.2
.

The second example is the widely-recognized Off-Policy Actor-Critic algorithm
(
Degris et al. 2012
;
Wang et al. 2020
;
Xu et al. 2021
, see for example)
, in which the policy
\pi
is smooth enough and indexed by some parameter
w
. In this case, using the doubly robust off-policy actor-critic algorithm
(
Xu et al. 2021
, Algorithm 1 in)
for the standard MDP, suppose we obtain the estimated policy as
\pi_{\widehat{w}}
from Algorithm 1. If we regard their constructed doubly robust policy gradient error formula eq.(4) as a function of both
Q
and
w
, denoted by
G_{\text{DR}}(Q;w)
. Then the estimation
\widehat{\eta}^{*}:=\mathbb{P}_{N}[\widehat{Q}(A_{0},S_{0};\pi_{\widehat{w}})\pi_{\widehat{w}}(A_{0}\mid S_{0})]
with
\widehat{Q}
derived from solving the estimation equation
\mathbb{P}_{NT}G_{\text{DR}}(Q;\widehat{w})=0
will achieve the local semiparametric efficiency when the conditions in their Theorem 2 and our Theorem
3.1
are satisfied and the
\pi_{w}
is correctly specified.

Example 4.3
.

The last example is the combination of fitted-Q iteration (FQI) algorithm with doubly-robust FQE. When the deterministic optimal policy is unique, by plugging in the estimated policy learned by FQI into the doubly-robust FQE estimator, we obtain a semiparametrically efficient estimator. A simultaneous approach is also possible,
which we leave for further study.

5
Final Remarks

In this paper, we have argued for the existence and characterization of the efficient influence function for
\eta(\pi^{*})
under the canonical MDP framework widely recognized in statistical inference literature. However, our results can be straightforwardly extended to other OPE settings. For instance, both Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.2
can be directly applied to OPE scenarios when there exist mediators or instrumental variables
(
Shi et al. 2024b
;
Xie et al. 2023
;
Liao et al. 2024
, see)
.

We should note that we have not established the necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of the EIF. We acknowledge that even when the optimal policy is not unique, the EIF may still exist, as demonstrated in various optimal treatment regime literatures
(for instance
Robins & Rotnitzky 2014
;
Luedtke & Van Der Laan 2016
;
Kosorok & Laber 2019
, although we do not find corresponding results for OPE)
. Such necessary and sufficient conditions remain an open question for future research.

Appendix A
Technical Proofs

A.1
Preliminaries (under the same law
P
)

To prove the main results in Section
3
, we first establish the connection between the distance between two policies and the value function via
Q
-functions or MIS functions. For notational clarity, we omit the underlying law
P
for all functionals when they all follow the same probability distribution.

Lemma A.1
(Upper Bound).

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
and
2.1
hold. Then

\big\|\omega(\cdot;\pi_{2})-\omega(\cdot;\pi_{1})\big\|_{1}\leq\frac{2\gamma}{1-\gamma}\mathrm{E}_{S\sim\omega(s;\pi_{1})}\big[\operatorname{TV}(\pi_{2}\|\pi_{1})(S)\big].

Proof A.2
.

Define the transition between
S_{t}\rightarrow S_{t+1}
as

K_{\pi}(s^{\prime}\mid s):=\int f(s^{\prime}\mid a,s)\pi(a\mid s)\,\mathrm{d}a.

By the fact that
\{S_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
has been stationary under
b
, and we obtain

\displaystyle\omega(\cdot;\pi_{2})-\omega(\cdot;\pi_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\gamma}{f_{0}(\cdot)}\sum_{t=0}^{+\infty}\gamma^{t}\big(f_{t}^{\pi_{2}}(\cdot)-f_{t}^{\pi_{1}}(\cdot)\big)=\frac{1-\gamma}{f(\cdot)}\sum_{t=0}^{+\infty}\big(\gamma^{t}K_{\pi_{2}}^{t}-\gamma^{t}K_{\pi_{1}}^{t}\big)f_{0}(\cdot)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\gamma}{f_{0}(\cdot)}\gamma(I-\gamma K_{\pi_{2}})^{-1}(K_{\pi_{2}}-K_{\pi_{1}})(I-\gamma K_{\pi_{1}})^{-1}f_{0}(\cdot)

\displaystyle=\gamma(I-\gamma K_{\pi_{2}})^{-1}(K_{\pi_{2}}-K_{\pi_{1}})\frac{(1-\gamma)(I-\gamma K_{\pi_{1}})^{-1}f_{0}(\cdot)}{f_{0}(\cdot)}

\displaystyle=\gamma(I-K_{\pi_{2}})^{-1}(K_{\pi_{2}}-K_{\pi_{1}})\omega(\cdot;\pi_{1}).

Note that
K_{\pi}
is a probability kernel, we have

\|(I-\gamma K_{\pi_{2}})^{-1}\|_{1}\leq\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^{t}\|K_{\pi_{2}}\|_{1}^{t}\leq\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^{t}1=(1-\gamma)^{-1}

which implies

\displaystyle\big\|\omega(\cdot;\pi_{2})-\omega(\cdot;\pi_{1})\big\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\big\|(I-K_{\pi_{2}})^{-1}\big\|_{1}\big\|(K_{\pi_{2}}-K_{\pi_{1}})\omega(\cdot;\pi_{1})\big\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\big\|(K_{\pi_{2}}-K_{\pi_{1}})\omega(\cdot;\pi_{1})\big\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\int\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\Big|(K_{\pi_{2}}-K_{\pi_{1}})(s^{\prime}\mid s)\omega(s;\pi_{1})\mathrm{d}s\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\int f(s^{\prime}\mid a,s)\big|\pi_{2}(a\mid s)-\pi_{1}(a\mid s)\big|\omega(s;\pi_{1})\,\mathrm{d}(s^{\prime},a,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\int\big|\pi_{2}(a\mid s)-\pi_{1}(a\mid s)\big|\omega(s;\pi_{1})\,\mathrm{d}(a,s)=\frac{2\gamma}{1-\gamma}\mathrm{E}_{S\sim\omega(s;\pi_{1})}\big[\operatorname{TV}(\pi_{2}\|\pi_{1})(S)\big],

by the definition of the total variation distance
\operatorname{TV}(\pi_{2}\|\pi_{1})
.

Lemma A.3
(Lower Bound).

Suppose the conditions in Lemma
A.1
hold. If

\underline{c}_{\pi}\leq\operatorname{essinf}_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}}\operatorname{essinf}_{(a,s)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}}\pi(a\mid s)\leq\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}}\|\pi\|_{\infty}\leq\overline{c}_{\pi}

then

\big|\omega(s;\pi_{2})-\omega(s;\pi_{1})\big|\geq\frac{2\gamma\sqrt{\overline{c}_{\pi}^{-1}\underline{c}_{\pi}\mathrm{E}_{S^{\prime}\sim\omega(s;\pi_{1})}\big[\chi^{2}(\pi_{2}\|\pi_{1})(S^{\prime})\big]}}{\overline{c}_{\pi}^{1/2}+\overline{c}_{\pi}^{-1}\operatorname{essinf}_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\inf_{a\in\mathcal{A}}|\pi_{2}(a\mid s)-\pi_{1}(a\mid s)|}\sqrt{f(s)}

for any
s\in\mathcal{S}
.

Proof A.4
.

Similar as in the proof of Lemma
A.1
, we have the identity

\displaystyle\omega(s;\pi_{2})-\omega(s;\pi_{1})=\gamma(I-\gamma K_{\pi_{2}})^{-1}(K_{\pi_{2}}-K_{\pi_{1}})\omega(s;\pi_{1}).

For any positive density
h
,

\big((I-\gamma K_{\pi_{2}})^{-1}h\big)(s)=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}K_{\pi_{2}}^{t}h(s)=\bigg(h+\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}K_{\pi_{2}}^{t}h\bigg)(s)\geq 1\cdot h(s)=h(s)

holds pointwisely for any
s
. Thus, an initial lower bound can be obtained as

\displaystyle\omega(s;\pi_{2})-\omega(s;\pi_{1})

\displaystyle=\gamma(I-\gamma K_{\pi_{2}})^{-1}(K_{\pi_{2}}-K_{\pi_{1}})\omega(s;\pi_{1})

\displaystyle\geq\gamma(K_{\pi_{2}}-K_{\pi_{1}})\omega(s;\pi_{1})

\displaystyle=\gamma\int f(s\mid a,s_{\star})\big[\pi_{2}(a\mid s_{\star})-\pi_{1}(a\mid s_{\star})\big]\omega(s_{\star};\pi_{1})\,\mathrm{d}a\,\mathrm{d}s_{\star}.

To include the Kullback-Leibler divergence in the lower bound, we first apply reverse Pinsker inequalities
(
Cattiaux & Guillin 2009
, for example, see)
as

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(\pi_{2}\|\pi_{1})(s)\leq\frac{2}{\underline{c}_{s}}\operatorname{TV}^{2}(\pi_{2}\|\pi_{1})(s)=\frac{1}{2\underline{c}_{s}}\bigg(\int\big|\pi_{2}(a\mid s)-\pi_{1}(a\mid s)\big|\,\mathrm{d}a\bigg)^{2}.

(6)

The second tool for obtaining the lower bound is using the reverse Cauchy inequality
(
Aldaz et al. 2015
, see the result for integrals in Corollary 6.1 of)
. The bounds for the ratio are

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{essinf}_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\inf_{a\in\mathcal{A}}|\pi_{2}(a\mid s)-\pi_{1}(a\mid s)|}{\sqrt{\overline{c}_{\pi}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\inf_{a\in\mathcal{A}}|\pi_{2}(a\mid s)-\pi_{1}(a\mid s)|}{\sqrt{\overline{c}_{\pi}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|\pi_{2}(a\mid s)-\pi_{1}(a\mid s)|}{\sqrt{\pi_{1}(a\mid s)}}\leq\frac{\overline{c}_{\pi}}{\sqrt{\underline{c}_{\pi}}},

and thus we obtain

\displaystyle\bigg(\int f(s\mid a,s_{\star})\big[\pi_{2}(a\mid s_{\star})-\pi_{1}(a\mid s_{\star})\big]\omega(s_{\star};\pi_{1})\,\mathrm{d}a\,\mathrm{d}s_{\star}\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigg(\int\omega(s_{\star};\pi_{1})f(s\mid a,s_{\star})\big[\pi_{2}(a\mid s_{\star})-\pi_{1}(a\mid s_{\star})\big]\,\mathrm{d}a\,\mathrm{d}s_{\star}\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{4\underline{c}_{\pi}^{-1/2}\overline{c}_{\pi}\times\overline{c}_{\pi}^{-1/2}\operatorname{essinf}_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\inf_{a\in\mathcal{A}}|\pi_{2}(a\mid s)-\pi_{1}(a\mid s)|}{\big(\underline{c}_{\pi}^{-1/2}\overline{c}_{\pi}+\overline{c}_{\pi}^{-1/2}\operatorname{essinf}_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\inf_{a\in\mathcal{A}}|\pi_{2}(a\mid s)-\pi_{1}(a\mid s)|\big)^{2}}

\displaystyle\int\omega(s_{\star};\pi_{1})f(s\mid a,s_{\star})\bigg(\frac{\pi_{2}(a\mid s_{\star})-\pi_{1}(a\mid s_{\star})}{\sqrt{\pi_{1}(a\mid s_{\star})}}\bigg)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}a\,\mathrm{d}s_{\star}\int\omega(s_{\star};\pi_{1})f(s\mid a,s_{\star})\times\big[\sqrt{\pi_{1}(a\mid s_{\star})}\big]^{2}\,\mathrm{d}a\,\mathrm{d}s_{\star}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\operatorname{essinf}_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\inf_{a\in\mathcal{A}}|\pi_{2}(a\mid s)-\pi_{1}(a\mid s)|}{\underline{c}_{\pi}^{-1/2}\overline{c}_{\pi}^{1/2}\big(\overline{c}_{\pi}^{1/2}+\overline{c}_{\pi}^{-1}\operatorname{essinf}_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\inf_{a\in\mathcal{A}}|\pi_{2}(a\mid s)-\pi_{1}(a\mid s)|\big)^{2}}\times f(s)

\displaystyle\times\int\omega(s_{\star};\pi_{1})f(s\mid a,s_{\star})\bigg(\frac{\pi_{2}(a\mid s_{\star})-\pi_{1}(a\mid s_{\star})}{\sqrt{\pi_{1}(a\mid s_{\star})}}\bigg)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}a\,\mathrm{d}s_{\star}.

By using the definition of chi-square divergence, the integral in the above expression can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\int\omega(s_{\star};\pi_{1})f(s\mid a,s_{\star})\bigg(\frac{\pi_{2}(a\mid s_{\star})-\pi_{1}(a\mid s_{\star})}{\sqrt{\pi_{1}(a\mid s_{\star})}}\bigg)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}a\,\mathrm{d}s_{\star}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\omega(s_{\star};\pi_{1})f(s\mid a,s_{\star})\pi_{1}(a\mid s_{\star})\bigg(\frac{\pi_{2}(a\mid s_{\star})}{\pi_{1}(a\mid s_{\star})}-1\bigg)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}a\,\mathrm{d}s_{\star}=\mathrm{E}_{S^{\prime}\sim\omega(s;\pi_{1})}\big[\chi^{2}(\pi_{2}\|\pi_{1})(S^{\prime})\big],

which leads to the result in the lemma.

To our best knowledge, our novel result in Lemma
A.3
is the first one to find the lower bound for the divergence between different policies, while there exists study on the upper bounds for
\omega(s;\pi_{2})-\omega(s;\pi_{1})

(
Achiam et al. 2017
;
Huang & Jiang 2024
;
Krishnamurthy et al. 2025
, for example, see)
. We does not compare the tightness of these upper bounds with ours here, as ours is enough for obtaining our results. Additionally, the lower bound in Lemma
A.3
, which is first studied to our best knowledge, is more important than the these upper bounds, as it can help connect the difference between policies with the evalution
Q
-function. To do this, we introduce some helpful tools and define the

The advantage function
\mathbb{A}(a,s;\pi)
will help us connect the difference

with

\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
come from greedy policy (may not be unique).

\Psi^{*}(P_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\Psi^{*}(P_{\epsilon})-\Psi^{*}(P_{0})

Lemma A.8
(Policy Decomposition Lemma, see Lemma 2 in
Achiam et al. 2017
).

Suppose that Assumption
2.1
and
2.1
hold. For any function
f:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and any policies
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
, define

\delta_{f}(s^{\prime},a,s):=\mathrm{E}[R\mid S^{\prime}=s,A=a,S=s]+\gamma f(s^{\prime})-f(s)\quad\text{and}\quad{\updelta}_{f}(s;\pi):=\mathrm{E}_{A\sim\pi}[\delta_{f}(S^{\prime},A,S)\mid S=s].

Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{S\sim\omega(\boldsymbol{\cdot};\pi_{2}),A\sim\pi_{2}}\delta_{f}(S^{\prime},A,S)

\displaystyle=\big\langle\omega(S;\pi_{1}),\,{\updelta}_{f}(S;\pi_{2})\big\rangle_{P}+\big\langle\omega(S;\pi_{2})-\omega(S;\pi_{1}),\,{\updelta}_{f}(S;\pi_{2})\big\rangle_{P}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{S\sim\omega(\boldsymbol{\cdot};\pi_{1}),A\sim\pi_{1}}\bigg[\frac{\pi_{2}(A\mid S)}{\pi_{1}(A\mid S)}\delta_{f}(S^{\prime},A,S)\bigg]+\big\langle\omega(S;\pi_{2})-\omega(S;\pi_{1}),\,{\updelta}_{f}(S;\pi_{2})\big\rangle_{P}.
