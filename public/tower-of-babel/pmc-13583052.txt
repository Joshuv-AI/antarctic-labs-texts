Operationalizing Alzheimer's disease trials in the era of targeted therapies
Authors: Jagan A. Pillai, Suvarna Alladi, Brian S. Appleby, Jared Robert Brosch, Kristian Steen Frederiksen, Douglas Galasko, Oscar L. Lopez, James B. Leverenz, Andrew J. Saykin, Jonathan M. Schott, Sudha Seshadri, Babak Tousi, David A. Wolk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe therapeutic landscape of Alzheimer's disease (AD) is rapidly evolving with the clinical adoption of anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) despite an incomplete understanding of disease mechanisms, progression, and heterogeneity. Designing both observational longitudinal cohorts and clinical trials with novel mAbs in mind is imperative. These studies should capture mAb type, dosing, duration, degree of amyloid clearance, and downstream effects on neurodegeneration to interpret outcomes. Observational cohorts can compare treated and untreated populations, track biomarker trajectories, assess co‐pathologies, and evaluate social determinants of health. Future trials must address variability in treatment response, identify resistant subgroups, and include long‐term follow‐up to assess durability and post‐treatment effects. Combination therapy trials should be stage specific, pairing novel agents with mAbs and incorporating broader stratification beyond traditional biomarkers to reflect clinical and pathological heterogeneity. Integrating biological and clinical outcomes, with digital biomarkers and responder‐enrichment strategies will enable more precise, mechanism‐driven, and individualized therapeutic approaches in AD.

INTRODUCTION

Recent advances in Alzheimer's disease (AD) diagnosis and management have created multiple groundbreaking options for clinical diagnosis, therapy, and management of AD symptoms. Among these innovations, disease‐targeted anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) that lower amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques have emerged as a breakthrough. 1 , 2 However, AD is a multifactorial disorder. Evidence supports key roles for other co‐pathologies in influencing clinical outcomes beyond changes in Aβ. 3 While anti‐amyloid therapies represent a major advance, they arguably complicate the landscape for developing and delivering future treatments in AD. While prior reviews 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 have summarized prevention strategies, therapeutic outcomes, and discussed strategic implications of anti‐amyloid therapies, this article provides a comprehensive, operational framework for designing the next generation of both observational studies and therapeutic trials in AD.

To chart the path forward, it is critical to understand the clinical contributions of mAbs, reassess trial design strategies, and integrate insights from disease course and biomarker studies. As mAbs targeting amyloid become established as standard of care worldwide, the clinical trial landscape will necessarily evolve, with important implications for recruitment feasibility, comparator selection, and overall study design. In particular, future trials of novel agents will need to account for reduced availability of treatment‐naïve populations and will increasingly rely on add‐on or combination trial designs rather than monotherapy comparisons. Key challenges and opportunities for AD trials in the era of mAbs are noted in Table 1. In this Perspective article, we also argue that the future of AD therapeutics depends on recognizing biological heterogeneity as a key factor to be addressed in next‐generation AD trials. We propose a framework in which biomarkers enable patient stratification, longitudinal observational cohorts define disease trajectories, and combination therapeutic approaches address the multiple pathological processes contributing to disease progression. These components should not be viewed as independent developments but as interconnected elements of a precision medicine strategy. However, although this article focuses on symptomatic and early‐stage AD, trials for preclinical AD are also an important direction that needs future consideration.

ANTI‐AMYLOID THERAPIES: MOVING FROM CHALLENGES TO NEXT STEPS

The recent approvals and clinical availability of lecanemab 1 and donanemab 2 have marked a significant inflection point in AD therapy. While the mechanism of action appears sound and amyloid reduction robust, the translation to meaningful clinical benefit remains modest, and several critical issues around heterogeneity, trial design, biomarker interpretation, real‑world generalizability, amyloid related imaging abnormalities (ARIAs), and risks including apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 and cerebrovascular disease persist. A summary of key challenges is detailed in the following sections.

Disease heterogeneity

AD heterogeneity spans genetics (e.g., sex, APOE ε4 carrier status), age at onset, clinical phenotype (amnestic vs. atypical), co‑pathologies, tau burden and distribution, cognitive reserve, and sociodemographic context. For example, individuals with older‑onset AD frequently decline more slowly than younger onset cases, 10 yet many clinical studies enroll broad populations without stratification. Real‐world data in patients receiving lecanemab suggest a possible age‐related difference in the rate of cognitive decline, raising the question of whether treatment response varies by age in early AD. 11 Sex differences have been reported for clinical outcomes; for example, the lecanemab clinical trial subgroup analyses were suggestive of 31% greater benefit in males compared to females, but was not powered to more definitively address this question. 1 However, additional statistical modeling provided some support that this efficacy difference may be real. 12 Atypical AD clinical phenotypes (e.g., initial language and dysexecutive syndrome) are also known to have a faster rate of decline. 13 , 14 AD clinical trials often do not report which cognitive domains, such as memory, executive function, or language, are most affected by therapy. Consequently, specific cognitive domains that contribute most to global outcomes like Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB) 15 and Integrated Alzheimer's Disease Rating Scale (iADRS) 16 remain uncertain although psychometric analyses show that memory‐related items (e.g., word recall, delayed recall, word recognition) consistently have the highest sensitivity to change and factor loadings within CDR‑SB 17 and the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale 13‐item Cognitive subscale (ADAS‑Cog13, 18 which underlies iADRS). 16 Lower tau burden tends to be associated with greater treatment‐related slowing of cognitive decline in the TRAILBLAZER‐ALZ 2 trial, likely reflecting earlier disease stage. 2 Factors related to cognitive reserve, including sociodemographic, cultural, and environmental exposures along with co‐pathology differences also contribute to rates of cognitive decline. 19 Given this variability, conclusions about treatment efficacy or natural‐history decline rates cannot assume uniformity across populations.

Biomarker interpretation and surrogate endpoint validity

While Aβ reduction leads to slower cognitive decline in early AD stages, the details of the causal mechanism remain unclear. Among patients with very mild cases, lecanemab therapy is associated with reduction of tau accumulation in the temporal lobe on tau positron emission tomography (PET), suggesting that amyloid reduction may affect tau pathology downstream as indicated by cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) levels of microtubule binding regions (MTBR)‐tau243. 20 We are unable to fully interpret the molecular links between amyloid reduction and cognitive outcomes, as these pathways are still being investigated. Given limited understanding of disease‐modifying mechanisms in AD, biomarkers are increasingly used as surrogate endpoints in preclinical and early‐phase trials. 21 However, biomarker trajectories vary across individuals and phenotypes, 22 , 23 complicating interpretation of treatment effects. Systemic co‐morbidities, including renal dysfunction, may also influence circulating levels and interpretation of AD biomarkers, particularly plasma‐based measures. These factors should be accounted for in both trial design and biomarker modeling to avoid misclassification and improve interpretability of treatment effects.

Biomarker selection driven by the therapeutic target, and monitoring amyloid, tau, and neurodegeneration (ATN) class biomarkers remain appropriate for agents claiming disease‐modifying potential. Notably, recent phase 3 randomized placebo‐controlled trials of semaglutide 24 showed no amyloid reduction, and changes in metabolic or inflammatory biomarkers, while exploratory, did not predict cognitive slowing. Similarly, in ceperognastat 25 early biomarker effects lacked consistent clinical correlation. Together, these findings highlight the need for caution in interpreting isolated or exploratory biomarker changes, particularly those not yet well validated, as indicators of clinical benefit. Biomarker‐based trial designs in the future should incorporate stratification by phenotype, longitudinal trajectory, and individual variability, and rather than relying on a single biomarker cut point, should explore multimodal biomarker models aligned with mechanisms of action.

Generalizability in real‐world populations

Beyond biological heterogeneity, participation in mAb trials and registries is strongly influenced by health‐care access, health literacy, socioeconomic conditions, caregiving burden, transportation barriers, and broader psychosocial context. These structural determinants affect eligibility, enrollment, retention, adherence, and long‐term follow‐up. mAb clinical trials, like most completed AD clinical trials, enrolled participants with relatively high education levels, who were predominantly White and had fewer medical co‐morbidities than similarly aged untreated groups. 1 , 2 Current and legacy AD cohorts and registries are disproportionately composed of participants from urban, higher socioeconomic status populations with access to specialty care. This limits generalizability to rural communities, the global South, and under‐resourced health‐care systems. Evidence for mAb efficacy and safety also remains limited globally, as most data come from high‐income countries. Pivotal mAb trials had strict exclusion criteria, which may not reflect the more complex real‑world population. This further underscores the urgent need to expand and adapt clinical trials to understudied populations worldwide. 26

HOW CAN WE ADDRESS THESE QUESTIONS THROUGH LONGITUDINAL OBSERVATIONAL STUDIES?

The emergence of multiple mAbs now in clinical use for AD has enabled the field to identify key gaps that must be addressed to advance future therapeutic strategies. Recognizing that patients differ substantially in their underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, rates of progression, and treatment responses, biomarker‐based stratification provides an essential means of identifying biologically distinct subgroups. Longitudinal cohort studies further enhance this framework by characterizing disease trajectories, identifying critical windows for intervention, and revealing factors associated with progression. Together, these advances enable more informed patient‐specific therapeutic selection, aligning interventions with an individual's biological profile and disease stage. The field has reached a stage of maturity at which lessons from these trials are beginning to inform the design and interpretation of longitudinal cohort studies, translating into concrete methodological and analytical refinements. These include using existing and establishing new core observational cohorts and registries including mAb‑treated patients with long‑term follow‑up and systematic biomarker, imaging, and clinical data capture. There is a clear need for continued longitudinal cohort observational studies, such as the cohorts investigated by Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers (ADRCs) across the United States and access through the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) and other large national and international databases such as the international Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), Longitudinal Early‐Onset Alzheimer's Disease Study (LEADS), Dominantly Inherited Alzheimer Network (DIAN), and Biomarkers For Identifying Neurodegenerative Disorders Early and Reliably (BioFINDER) study. In addition to their longitudinal follow‐up, allowing for more abridged testing and data collection in the context of observational studies, such as within ADRCs, may facilitate larger enrollment numbers and greater willingness to participate, especially in the context of receiving anti‐amyloid therapies.

These studies help integrate longitudinal Aβ‐, tau‐, and neurodegeneration‐related biomarker changes to study trajectories of related biomarkers, including immune, blood–brain barrier (BBB), vascular, metabolic, and other novel biomarkers of interest, pre‐ and post‐mAb therapy. There is potential value in comparing mAb versus untreated populations to better understand the causal relationship between amyloid accumulation and cognitive change. Legacy datasets are being used as a proxy for untreated patients in open label longer term extension studies. 27 , 28 The NACC Uniform Data Set version 4 (UDSv4) forms now include a section to capture mAb therapy in addition to diagnostic biomarkers, medical, and neuropsychiatric histories. These additions support harmonization across ADRCs, enabling more consistent and comprehensive data collection on emerging AD treatments and control populations. There is, however, room to improve modality‐specific treatment exposure (intravenous [iv] vs. subcutaneous [sc] routes, frequency, and cumulative dose) and granular safety data needed for real‐world analyses of mAb. The Alzheimer's Association Alzheimer's Network for Treatment and Diagnostics (ALZ‐NET) registry provides a comprehensive registry of patients with AD treated with novel therapeutics including mAbs. However, several critical treatment‐specific variables are currently not captured or inconsistently recorded to help understand real‐world effectiveness and safety of mAbs, including harmonized longitudinal biomarker measures, quantitative tracking of ARIA events linked to treatment exposure, and detailed therapy administration data (e.g., iv vs. sc route, dosing dates). Importantly, beyond its role as a safety endpoint, ARIAs may also provide mechanistic insight into vascular vulnerability, BBB integrity, and treatment‐response biology, and should therefore be systematically characterized rather than treated solely as an adverse event. There is a clear need to expand and harmonize clinical (Phase 4) data collection and to integrate these efforts with established research cohorts, including ADRCs. Such integration would support cross‐cohort harmonization, enabling more consistent and comprehensive data capture on emerging AD therapies as well as an appropriate control population.

Other considerations include stratified enrollment, domain‑specific cognitive measures, and multi‑modal biomarkers. When atypical AD phenotypes are included, differences in cognitive and biomarker profiles must be accounted for and because these sub‑types are less prevalent, larger sample sizes may be needed to maintain statistical power, which has implications for study timeline planning. BBB‐penetrant therapies delivered via transporter‐mediated shuttles (e.g., trontinemab 29 ) may enhance central nervous system drug exposure. If this translates into larger or more consistent treatment effects, it could enable smaller sample sizes and potentially shorter study durations. In many observational studies, data on younger‐onset and atypical AD remain limited and represent a continuing gap that is being addressed in LEADS. Validated cognitive and functional outcome measures tailored to these subgroups are needed. On the biomarker front, while changes in blood‐based biomarkers such as Aβ, tau, 30 and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) have been demonstrated, 31 it remains unclear whether these biomarker changes are consistent across patient sub‑groups given the limited head‑to‑head data and the non‑specificity of some markers. Broader multiplexed high‐sensitivity proteomic panels could enable more comprehensive and unbiased characterization of disease biology and co‐pathology, particularly in heterogeneous or treatment‐exposed populations. How biomarkers evolve across different therapies is still unclear, and study designs should explicitly account for biomarker variability rather than assuming fixed or uniform trajectories. Comparative studies are needed to clarify these biomarker trajectories across therapies and their relationship to the extent of amyloid removal. Biomarker measurement variability across assay platforms, laboratories, and imaging protocols poses a significant challenge to harmonization. Cross‐platform variability in plasma assays, CSF measurements, and PET quantification may confound longitudinal comparisons and multi‐site trials. Furthermore, PET‐based infrastructure remains limited in rural regions and much of the global South, raising concerns about equitable access to biomarker‐driven trial eligibility and monitoring. Scalable blood‐based biomarkers and portable digital tools will be essential to mitigate this structural limitation. Plasma biomarker–guided enrollment strategies using phosphorylated tau (p‐tau)217, p‐tau231, eMTBR‐tau243, GFAP, and neurofilament light chain (NfL) may enable biologically informed participant selection and subgroup enrichment, although optimal thresholds and generalizability across populations remain to be established in longitudinal validation studies. Given their scalability, low participant burden, and increasing adoption in both clinical care and research settings, plasma biomarkers have the potential to improve recruitment efficiency, reduce reliance on PET and CSF confirmation, and enrich trials for individuals with specific pathological or disease‐stage characteristics. Nevertheless, assay standardization, population‐specific performance, AD clinico‐pathological and prospective validation remain important prerequisites for their routine use in trial enrollment strategies.

These issues highlight that heterogeneity across clinical, genetic, biomarker, and co‐pathology domains is a defining feature of AD rather than a source of noise. Age and APOE genotype are viewed as key determinants of disease risk and temporal trajectory, while clinical phenotypes and co‐pathologies reflect divergent downstream manifestations of underlying pathobiological processes. Integrating these dimensions with the addition of biomarker readouts into stratification frameworks may enable more precise patient classification and support precision medicine approaches in AD clinical studies.

Embedding demographic and geographic representation is also important. Longitudinal data on mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and AD in global populations, especially in the global South, are scarce, limiting understanding of disease natural history. Socioeconomic factors, comorbidities, and biomarker profiles remain understudied in these populations, making it difficult to assess real‐world effectiveness of anti‐amyloid therapies in different global contexts. To ensure equitable evaluation of mAb efficacy and generalizability, designing contextually adapted clinical trials and/or observational studies and registries in low‐resource settings is essential.

Additionally, modeling effects of co‐pathologies will help us better interpret clinical trial outcomes. Neuropathologic comorbidities are commonly recognized among AD clinical syndromes, 32 including limbic‐predominant age‐related transactive response DNA binding protein 43 kDa encephalopathy (LATE), and alpha‐synuclein. The clinical significance and geographic distribution of these conditions are still being investigated, and the absence of reliable ante mortem biomarkers further complicates interpretation of treatment effects. In addition, vascular risk factors and co‐pathologies are more prevalent in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) 33 and may influence both AD risk 34 and the effectiveness of mAb therapies. Observational studies should incorporate vascular and other non‐amyloid biomarkers to better characterize AD–vascular interactions in the anti‐amyloid therapy era. Vascular contributions to cognitive impairment and dementia (VCID) represent a particularly actionable co‐pathology, with well‐characterized magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) biomarkers (e.g., white matter hyperintensities, lacunes, microbleeds) as demonstrated in initiatives such as Biomarkers for Vascular Contributions to Cognitive Impairment and Dementia (MarkVCID). Emerging blood‐based vascular biomarkers further enhance the feasibility of integrating VCID measures into large‐scale studies. Deep phenotyping of these comorbidities and greater number of autopsy studies of people in trials and registries to improve understanding of co‐pathologies and the impact of novel therapies are urgently needed.

We must also address incorporating high‐interest populations, including incomplete responders and those at high risk for side effects on mAbs. One distinct population of interest in observation trials is the anti‐amyloid mAb–treated patients who did not reach amyloid‐negative threshold on PET. Studies showed that 19% of lecanemab‐ and 14.6% of donanemab‐treated participants did not achieve amyloid lowering to below PET threshold by the end of the randomized phase. 1 , 2 Clarifying the molecular and other factors contributing to these findings is an important step toward understanding downstream, and possibly upstream, effects of the amyloid cascade hypothesis and designing novel adjuvant therapeutics. Those at high risk for side effects on mAbs (e.g., APOE ε4 homozygotes) and those with exclusionary medical co‐morbidities (e.g., small cortical infarcts) may be preferred candidates for future combination or alternative therapies to mAb and are therefore a potentially high‐interest population for observational studies to better characterize disease course in mixed pathology. Specific medications targeting APOE ε4 homozygotes with early AD may be attractive to be considered in the future.

Engaging pharma and regulators to increase transparency, especially regarding the quality and quantity of trial data made available for independent and combinatorial modeling of AD outcomes, is also important. Availability of clinical trial data to understand clinical effects related to AD heterogeneity is essential to advance predictive models and refine therapeutic strategies. Anonymized patient‐level data from current and future AD therapeutic trials should be made available for independent analysis to better understand the clinical benefits of these therapies. Future studies are likely to benefit from more detailed knowledge to implement stratification and subgroup analysis from knowledge gained from prior Phase 3 trials. In addition, greater transparency is needed regarding immunogenicity, particularly the development, prevalence, and clinical impact of anti‐drug antibodies (ADAs) 35 , 36 on long‐term maintenance dosing outcomes. Engagement should extend beyond consultation to structured mechanisms such as data‐sharing consortia, harmonized common data elements across registries, transparency agreements on post‐marketing real‐world evidence, and joint regulatory–scientific workshops on surrogate endpoints.

Harnessing real‐world clinical data

Using electronic medical record (EMR) data and promoting sharing of biosamples can help capture clinical, genetic, and biomarker information across diverse populations. For example, integrating multi‐center EMR datasets has enabled identification of at‐risk subgroups, 37 and assessment of treatment effectiveness in real‐world settings is ongoing in efforts including ALZ‐NET in the United States. However real‐world data face challenges like selection bias, and fragmented care, necessitating systematic data collection and local cost‐effectiveness analyses for mAb treatments that need detailed planning by stakeholders to maximize benefit. Meaningful longitudinal cohort observational studies in the era of anti‐amyloid therapy must also include provisions for multi‐year follow‐up to ensure that data are collected in the “wash‐out period,” if any, after the mAb treatment is stopped. We are otherwise at risk of missing delayed or enduring effects of mAbs that are vital to understanding their full impact regarding their safety, persistence of benefits, and individualized utility.

Assessing the impact of social determinants of health (SDOH) in this evolving therapeutic landscape remains challenging. Underserved populations are less likely to receive US Food and Drug Administration–approved mAbs and are often underrepresented in clinical trials, limiting the generalizability of both trial and real‐world evidence. As a result, available datasets may not fully capture the effects of socioeconomic status, health literacy, geography, insurance coverage, and health‐care access on treatment uptake and outcomes. While EMR‐based and registry studies may help address some gaps, a comprehensive evaluation of SDOH‐related disparities and potential mitigation strategies is beyond the scope of this review and warrants dedicated investigation.

Artificial intelligence–driven phenotyping may also enable more precise enrichment strategies for combination trials and help disentangle biological from social determinants of treatment response in real‐world data. In addition, leveraging routinely acquired clinical data such as structural MRI and standardized neuropsychological testing offers an opportunity for co‐pathology risk stratification. Where feasible, incorporation of emerging tools such as α‐synuclein seeding amplification assays (SAA), dopamine transporter (DaT) imaging, and skin biopsy for α‐synuclein may further refine in vivo detection of mixed pathologies.

Key considerations for designing observational cohort studies in the anti‐amyloid therapy era are summarized in Table 2. Feasibility of implementing these recommendations is dependent on integration with existing electronic health records (EHRs), adoption of harmonized common data elements (CDEs), and collaboration across major registries (e.g., NACC, ALZ‐NET, ADNI). In addition, international registries should be established or expanded in countries where anti‐amyloid therapies are approved, to ensure globally representative, harmonized data collection and enable cross‐country comparisons of real‐world effectiveness and safety. Timelines may vary depending on funding and regulatory approvals. Importantly, the strategies discussed here are largely derived from high‐resource health‐care systems with access to specialist care, advanced diagnostics, and anti‐amyloid therapies, and may not be readily generalized to broader real‐world or low‐resource settings. Future work should prioritize context‐specific approaches that address limitations in diagnostic infrastructure, treatment availability, workforce capacity, and equitable representation of underserved populations in research. Furthermore, while large longitudinal cohorts and registries are essential for identifying candidate biomarkers, defining patient subgroups, and informing trial design, definitive evaluation of causal relationships and therapeutic efficacy ultimately requires appropriately powered randomized controlled trials.

Studies using multi‐domain lifestyle interventions (e.g., Finnish Geriatric Intervention Study to Prevent Cognitive Impairment and Disability [FINGER], 38 Study to Protect Brain Health Through Lifestyle Intervention to Reduce Risk [POINTER] 39 trials) suggest that these approaches can improve or support cognitive function, with FINGER demonstrating modest cognitive benefits and POINTER providing feasibility evidence, although their effects on neurodegeneration are less clear. 40 The Metformin FINGER (MET‐FINGER) trial demonstrates one approach to combining lifestyle and pharmacologic therapy that might be used with mAbs in a combination therapy approach. 41 Lessons from combination therapies in human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and cancer demonstrate that multiple‐agent approaches can be implemented safely providing valuable guidance for the next generation of AD therapeutics.

A common approach among HIV and cancer therapies is a “sequential add‐on,” based on safety and tolerability being established with one medication before the next one is started. 42 In the use of mAbs, starting new medications sequentially must consider the timeline of ARIAs. ARIA with edema and ARIA with cerebral microhemorrhages can occur mostly before 6 months, but up to 12 months or longer, after the start of treatment. 2 , 43 However, these side effects were infrequent in APOE ε4 non‐carriers after 3 months of lecanemab infusion. 43 This likely provides an initial milestone for initiating a secondary add‐on therapy to mAb in future clinical trials that do not require the same sponsor to develop both agents in a combination regimen. Waiting for 6 months after initiation of the highest dose of a mAb has been suggested as a conservative approach for this issue. 44

Future AD clinical studies must not only advance mechanistic understanding of novel therapies but also evaluate how these interventions interact with existing mAbs, whether they preserve, diminish, or enhance the benefits observed with amyloid‐targeting therapy. In silico modeling of drug–drug interactions with mAb and downstream pathways may further inform safety, efficacy, and optimal treatment sequencing. Importantly, trial design must account for differences in standard of care across countries: in the United States, where mAbs are approved and reimbursed, testing new therapies against placebo may be challenging, whereas in countries such as the UK and much of Europe, where mAbs are licensed but not widely covered, opportunities exist to conduct novel cross‐country–designed studies. Mechanistic insights, patient stratification, and biomarker evaluation will be critical in all contexts, and complementary observational studies in humans will help clarify the biological effects of mAbs and inform the next generation of AD therapeutics. Dual targeting of amyloid and tau is also an area of high interest, as seen with amyloid removal via lecanemab and tau targeting by E2814, an anti‐tau antibody that is ongoing on the DIAN Trials Unit (DIAN‐TU) platform.

Phase 1 studies focused on safety and interaction data of new potential AD therapies in a small number of patients already on anti‐amyloid therapy become important. However, these studies are ethically challenging given limited advantages for a person on a mAb regimen to add an unproven novel therapeutic agent and scientifically limited in scope given limited understanding of interactions and the small number of participants in Phase 1 trials. Potential solutions could be to include “cognitive non‐responders” to mAbs, particularly those with disease progression exceeding the 95% confidence interval of expected outcome, 1 , 2 and include populations among whom mAbs are not widely available or have not been approved in diverse parts of the world. In addition, apparent “non‐response” to mAbs may reflect factors beyond biological heterogeneity, including disease stage at initiation, variability in drug exposure, adherence, and inter‐individual pharmacokinetic differences, complicating enrichment based on clinical response alone. Potential approaches include careful phenotyping of individuals with progression beyond expected trajectories, while recognizing that such definitions may reflect heterogeneous underlying causes rather than discrete biological subtypes.

Phase 2 and 3 trials of new therapies should demonstrate mechanistic effects beyond current treatments including mAbs, such as measurable changes or accelerated trajectories in ATN biomarkers in Phase 2, and additive or superior clinical benefits in Phase 3. Interpretation of ATN biomarker changes alongside mAbs may require refinement, given the complexity of overlapping mechanisms. Given the well‐recognized variability in AD clinical rate of progression noted earlier, a well‐reasoned stratification beyond that of ATN and APOE ε4 is needed based on the mechanism of action and target engagement data of the new therapy being trialed. Importantly, integrated use of ATN measures has still been limited in practice; for example, while donanemab trials have used A+T stratification, relationships involving neurodegeneration (N) and its association with tau (T) burden and cognition may provide additional insight into relative cognitive reserve and the influence of co‐pathologies. 45 Cognitive reserve and resilience factors, which vary globally, 46 should be incorporated via proxy measures in trials to assess their impact on response to mAbs. Deep phenotyping informed by mechanistic understanding can define more homogeneous subgroups, enhancing the ability to detect meaningful treatment effects and associations even with smaller sample sizes. 47

Pragmatically, combination therapy should allow only one type of mAb therapy given the differences in titration, dosage, and subtleties of mechanisms of action 6 . Platform trial designs, including master protocols and adaptive randomization, should be more explicitly leveraged to enable efficient evaluation of combination strategies. The Alzheimer's Tau Platform (ATP) trial illustrates this approach, using a shared‐placebo, platform design to test multiple tau‐directed therapies alone or in combination with anti‐amyloid treatment and with tau biomarkers enabling real‐time assessment. Such designs improve efficiency, reduce costs, and enhance understanding of therapeutic interactions.

To ensure cross‐comparability between trials and to ensure consistency when evaluating interactions of new therapies with existing mAbs, standardized metrics such as treatment‐related amyloid clearance (TRAC) should be used to quantify and compare amyloid reduction across trials, following emerging consensus guidelines. 48 Given the disease‐modifying potential and long‐term cognitive benefits of mAbs, a sequential approach to outcome assessment might be considered. In this approach, an initial assessment would function as the primary endpoint, establishing efficacy, confirming target engagement, and identifying non‐responders or partial responders, while subsequent longitudinal assessments in extension studies or real‐world registries would capture durability of cognitive and clinical effects as well as health‐care use outcomes.

Timing of treatment initiation in add‐on strategies is also critical to avoid confounding effects from mAb‐associated adverse events such as ARIAs. A conservative interval (e.g., the risk of newly emerging ARIAs substantially subsides by 6 to 12 months of therapy initiation) may help ensure clearer attribution of safety and efficacy signals. Given the disease‐modifying nature of mAbs and the long‐term cognitive benefits now reported to extend beyond the initial 18‐month treatment duration of donanemab, two key end points for novel therapeutic agents become equally important. The primary/secondary end point model could lead to personalized treatment plans for AD using combination therapies based on what best works for an individual biomarker profile at the first end point driving subsequent therapy decisions longer term. Lessons learned from amyloid‐ and tau‐directed combination therapy approaches could inform future trials targeting neuroinflammatory, vascular, metabolic, and resilience‐promoting pathways, particularly in biologically heterogeneous populations.

Biomarker‐guided adaptive trial designs can leverage early mechanistic data to optimize therapy decisions, improving trial efficiency and supporting personalized treatment plans based on individual biomarker profiles. 49 Biomarker‐guided adaptive trial designs would also allow for expanding inclusion criteria to include milder patients to map treatment effects, analogous to adaptive enrichment approaches used in stroke clinical trials, by enrolling individuals with a relatively low tau burden but also lower cognitive scores (e.g., Mini‐Mental State Examination 18–22). Including individuals with low tau and lower cognition may help disentangle whether clinical deficits reflect co‐pathology, diminished cognitive reserve, or non–tau‐driven mechanisms, thereby clarifying effect modification by tau burden and better defining the therapeutic window for mAbs across the disease spectrum. This may allow evaluation of effect modification by tau pathology on treatment response to mAbs and help clarify the therapeutic window across disease stages informed by biomarker data.

The longer term multiyear follow‐up phase of clinical trials in the context of mAbs and combination therapies presents an opportunity to integrate next‐generation digital biomarkers, 50 which passively collect data in real‐world settings at regular intervals (e.g., monthly) without necessitating frequent in‐person assessments. In real‐world settings, digital biomarkers used to monitor long‐term cognitive trajectories may include tools such as structured telephone‐based cognitive screens 51 or brief, self‐administered cognitive tasks embedded in digital diaries or mobile apps. 52 These approaches would enable frequent, low‐burden, real‐world monitoring of cognitive trajectories; complement traditional biomarkers; and enhance understanding of therapy effects over time. There remain significant regulatory hurdles to overcome to enable novel digital data and trial designs to be implemented and enable therapeutic decisions to be made.

mAb therapy‐based prevention studies in at‐risk but asymptomatic populations (preclinical AD), focusing on early biomarker changes and long‐term cognitive outcomes to assess prophylactic efficacy are ongoing and are on the horizon. Given the multi‐year duration of these studies and the need for a placebo group to extend for many years, in silico modeling using digital twins to simulate expected placebo trajectories based on retrospective data from prior study participants, could support the interpretation of new therapy trials. 53 However prior high‐quality biomarker data to validate the quality of digital twins is currently lacking. Historically, AD clinical trials have involved highly homogeneous populations, making it difficult to generalize their findings to future AD clinical trials due to concerns about limited population diversity.

To ensure ethical and equitable access to clinical trials, and to address challenges in interpreting clinical utility, patients treated with mAbs who do not achieve amyloid negativity on PET within an expected early treatment window may represent a high‐priority subgroup for future studies of novel AD therapies. However, this definition must account for delayed responders, as a subset of patients may achieve amyloid negativity with extended exposure beyond ≈ 6 to 12 months of treatment. Accordingly, enrichment strategies should be based on longitudinal response trajectories rather than single time‐point status. Individuals with persistently incomplete or slow amyloid clearance despite adequate and sustained dosing, comprising ≈ 15% to 19% of mAb‐treated patients in early reports, may continue to experience faster cognitive decline and could therefore benefit from therapies with mechanisms distinct from existing mAbs. Enriching Phase 2 and 3 trials with this “suboptimal responder” group allows evaluation of combination or rescue strategies, while also providing a motivated cohort for testing additional agents. If successful, this subgroup data could be used to inform possible future clinical trials in the overall population. Beyond clinical trial design, studying patients with incomplete amyloid clearance may yield insights into AD subtypes based on structural amyloid profiles or other disease mechanisms that remain poorly understood.

CONCLUSION

The central challenge facing the field is not merely the development of additional therapeutic agents, but the integration of biomarker‐guided stratification, longitudinal phenotyping, individualized treatment selection, and combination therapeutic strategies into a coherent precision medicine framework capable of addressing the biological heterogeneity of AD. Future AD therapies in clinical AD will likely need to be assessed as add‐on combinations to anti‐amyloid treatments, presenting both scientific and ethical challenges. Longitudinal observational cohorts with both ATN and novel biomarkers including incorporating multi‐modal digital biomarkers and detailed demographic data are essential to capture real‐world heterogeneity, including incomplete amyloid clearance and co‐pathologies. Ethical considerations and patient burden must be carefully addressed to ensure scientifically rigorous and humanistically responsible research that balances scientific rigor with attention to participant dignity, caregiver burden, equitable access, transparency, and long‐term social implications. Planning of observational studies and therapeutic clinical trials in AD should emphasize diverse geographic and socio‐demographic representation, long‐term follow‐up, and integration of mechanistic insights.
