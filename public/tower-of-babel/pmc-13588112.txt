Gender-Specific Network Structures of Depression and PTSD Symptoms in Chinese Firefighters
Authors: Yidi Wang, Xiaoli Ni, Mengze Li, Jiaying Zhang, Zhujing Ma, Yifeng Gai, Zhongying Wu, Jinming Fan, Bin Liu, Lei Song, Qun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFirefighters are frequently exposed to severe stressors, leading to high rates of comorbid depression and PTSD, yet research exploring gender differences in these conditions remains limited.ObjectiveThis study employed symptom network analysis to examine the comorbid network structure and gender-specific bridge symptoms of depression and PTSD among Chinese firefighters.MethodsData from 6240 firefighters (5357 males, 883 females, M age = 29.33, SD age = 5.57) were analyzed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and the PTSD Checklist-Civilian Version (PCL-C). Network analysis was adopted to explore the interconnections between depression and PTSD symptoms, and network comparison test was conducted to examine gender differences.ResultsThe results indicated that female firefighters had significantly higher PTSD and depression scores compared to male firefighters. Across all firefighters, PHQ4 (feeling tired), PHQ9 (suicide), and PCL-13 (sleep difficulties) acted as bridge symptoms connecting depression and PTSD; however, bridge nodes differed markedly by gender. PHQ4 (feeling tired) and PHQ7 (trouble concentrating) served as female-specific bridge symptoms tied to internalized affective and cognitive struggles, whereas male-specific bridge symptoms were PHQ8 (impaired motor skills) and PHQ9 (suicide), corresponding to externalized dysfunction and acute crisis symptoms.ConclusionThis study emphasizes the importance of gender-specific interventions.

Introduction

Firefighting is a high-risk profession, with individuals frequently exposed to traumatic and high-stress situations. As a result, mental health issues such as depression and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) are highly prevalent among firefighters.1 Studies indicate that the prevalence rates of depression and PTSD among firefighters are 28% and 37%, respectively,1,2 significantly higher than the general population, where depression affects 8.1% and PTSD affects 3.4%-26.9%.3,4 In addition to occurring independently, depression and PTSD often co-occur, which is known as comorbidity.5 This phenomenon is common in firefighters and can lead to more severe consequences, such as functional impairment, decreased quality of life, and even an increased risk of suicide.6 Therefore, studying comorbidity of depression and PTSD among firefighters is of great significance.

Moreover, gender plays a significant role in PTSD and depression research. For example, studies have consistently demonstrated that women exhibit higher rates of both PTSD and depression compared to men.7 Biological factors may partly explain these differences. Research has revealed that women show greater brainstem activation in response to threat stimuli, which may contribute to the higher prevalence of PTSD in females.8 Additionally, biological markers such as elevated levels of inflammation, neurotrophic factors, and serotonergic markers have been observed in women, correlating with greater severity of depressive symptoms.9 Inflammatory responses and dysfunction of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis are considered important biological underpinnings of the comorbidity between PTSD and depression. They may contribute to dysregulation of neuroendocrine processes, thereby influencing key symptom manifestations such as sleep disturbances and impaired attention.10,11 Furthermore, sleep- and attention-related symptoms may exhibit sex-dependent regulatory patterns, which could be associated with differences in HPA axis reactivity, levels of inflammation, and sex hormone regulation mechanisms.12 Therefore, these symptoms may not only play a key role in the comorbidity between PTSD and depression, but also act as bridge symptoms connecting different disorder dimensions, thereby reflecting potential sex-specific pathways within the symptom network structure. These findings underscore the importance of investigating gender differences in the context of PTSD and depression, particularly in high-risk professions like firefighting, where exposure to trauma is frequent. Such research could provide valuable insights into tailoring gender-specific interventions for mental health in this field.

Network analysis has emerged as a novel approach for studying comorbidity, particularly in psychiatric research.13 The psychopathological network theory (network theory of psychopathology) offers a different perspective, proposing that mental disorders should be better understood as complex dynamic systems formed by direct interactions among symptoms.13 Within this framework, the comorbidity of PTSD and depression is not attributed to a single latent disease dimension but may be sustained by “bridge relationships” (bridge symptoms) between transdiagnostic symptoms, which serve to connect and propagate across different disorders.14 More specifically, network analysis constructs networks of nodes and edges, offering a new perspective for exploring complex relationships between diseases.15 In the context of comorbidity research, each symptom of a disease can be represented as a node in the network, while the connections between symptoms are depicted as edges. Bridge symptoms refer to the key nodes that connect different clusters of disorder symptoms, serving as a crucial structural foundation for explaining the mechanisms underlying comorbidity. This approach provides an intuitive way to reveal interrelationships between diseases, identify potential comorbidity patterns, and uncover underlying pathogenic mechanisms.16 Moreover, network analysis facilitates the identification of key nodes or central symptoms within the network, offering valuable insights for early intervention and precision treatment. As such, this method holds significant promise for advancing our understanding of comorbidity in psychiatric disorders.

Recent network analysis studies have increasingly focused on the comorbidity of PTSD and depression, particularly within high-risk populations such as nurses,17 veterans,18 sexual minorities,19 and earthquake survivors.20 These studies aim to elucidate the structural relationships between PTSD and depressive symptoms by identifying central and bridge symptoms that facilitate the interconnection of these disorders. For example, a recent study targeting firefighters—a group frequently exposed to traumatic events—found that the symptoms “Numb” and “High alertness” served as bridge symptoms linking PTSD and depressive symptomatology.21 These bridge symptoms are critical as they may perpetuate the co-occurrence of PTSD and depression, highlighting potential targets for therapeutic interventions. By understanding the specific pathways through which PTSD and depression interact, particularly in vulnerable populations, researchers and clinicians can develop more effective strategies to address the complex interplay of these mental health conditions.

Previous studies have begun to use network analysis to explore the symptom structure of PTSD and depression, but most have not fully considered the mechanism of bridging symptoms in the context of comorbidity and their differential significance across genders.21–23Bridge symptoms, as key nodes connecting different clusters of disorder symptoms, can reveal transdiagnostic propagation pathways, thereby providing a more refined analytical perspective for delineating gender differences in comorbid structures. Therefore, this study employs network analysis to explore the network structure of depressive and PTSD symptoms among Chinese firefighters, with a particular focus on gender differences. The research hypotheses are as follows: (1) The severity of PTSD and depressive symptoms differ between female and male firefighters; (2) In the PTSD and depressive symptom network of female firefighters, bridge symptoms tend to be emotion-related.

Method

Participants and Procedure

The current study recruited 8471 Chinese firefighters. Excluded questionnaires with incomplete demographic information, a total of 6240 valid questionnaires were obtained, including 5357 males and 883 females (Mage=29.33, SDage=5.57). Data were collected through online surveys from 16 March 2023 to 10 May 2023. Before doing surveys, firefighters are required to fill out Informed Consent Form, meanwhile, all staff need to be trained in quality control, including uniform guidelines and requirements. All participants provided informed consent, in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. When completing the surveys, all firefighters need to be brought together in a quiet classroom or conference room, where staff could explain the requirements and instruct them to complete in a uniform manner. The study approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of the Air Force Medical University (No. KY20202063-F-2).

Measures

Depression

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) was used to assess depression. PHQ-9 is a validated depression screening instrument, consists of 9 items scored on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = not at all, 3 = nearly every day), yielding a total score range of 0–27.24 The scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency in this study (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.91).

PTSD Symptoms

The Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder Checklist-Civilian Version (PCL-C) was utilized to assess PTSD symptoms. The PCL-C, developed by the National Center for PTSD, was employed to assess 17 PTSD symptoms over the past month.25 Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all bothersome, 5 = extremely bothersome), with total scores ranging from 17 to 85. The PCL-C exhibited excellent internal consistency in this study (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.96).

Data Analysis

Descriptive Statistics and Gender Difference

We used SPSS to calculate the mean scores for depression and PTSD, and conducted t-tests to analyze gender differences in both depression and PTSD.

Network Estimation and Visualization

Gaussian graphical model (GGM) is a relatively more realistic way of presenting complex networks that can explain conditional dependencies between two variables after excluding the influence of other variables.26 If two random variables have conditional dependence, which is regarded as a non-zero corresponding element of the precision matrix (or inverse covariance matrix), it means that there is an edge between the two nodes.27 One of the most widespread methods is called graphical lasso (GLASSO), which is used in several fields to identify sparse approximations to regularized precision matrices and can solve problems such as those arising from small sample sizes.28

Centrality and Bridge Estimation

Three commonly utilized centrality indices are strength, closeness, and betweenness, where strength reflects directed connectivity, while closeness and betweenness imply indirect associations.29 Notably, recent research has demonstrated that strength is a more stable and reliable measure compared to closeness and betweenness, indicating that these latter two indices are not optimal for assessing node centrality in psychological networks.30 Therefore, this study opted to utilize strength and expected influence (EI) as the primary centrality index for network nodes. To compute these indices, we employed the R package “qgraph”. Furthermore, we calculated bridge strength using the R-Package “networktools”14 to gauge how effectively symptoms within one disorder connect to those of another, given that our network comprised two distinct symptom communities: anxiety and depression. Higher bridge strength signifies a greater likelihood of comorbidity when specific symptoms are activated.

Network Accuracy and Stability

The experiment tested the robustness of the network by using the R package “bootnet”.31 The accuracy of the edge weights was assessed by calculating 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using a non-parametric bootstrap method (2000 bootstrap samples). The experiment assessed the stability of the expected influences of nodes by calculating the correlation stability (CS) coefficient using the case-dropping bootstrap method. The value of correlation stability should be higher than 0.5 and not lower than 0.25.31

Network Comparison

Utilizing the R package “Network Comparison Test” (NCT), the network comparison test was administered to assess the disparities between dataset1 (male) and dataset2 (female).32 This analysis entailed evaluating three invariances: network structure, global strength, and edge strength. Specifically, “network structure” denotes the magnitude of divergence in the pairwise edges between two networks. “Global strength” represents the cumulative sum of edge weights within each network, whereas “edge strength” captures the variations in the individual absolute edge weights within each network, thereby reflecting disparities in local network characteristics. The Holm-Bonferroni procedure was applied to adjust p-values for multiple testing.

Accuracy and Stability Estimation

The accuracy of the edge weights, assessed using the bootstrapping approach, shows that the 95% CIs for the networks of male and female firefighters were relatively narrow. This narrowness of the CIs suggests a high level of accuracy, attributable to the large sample size and the limited number of measurement variables in this study. The CS coefficients for node strength, EI, and bridge strength were all 0.75 for both groups of networks, which confirms the reliability of these indices in measuring network node characteristics.

Discussion

In this study, we employed network analysis and a large sample to explore the network structure and interconnections of depression and PTSD symptoms among Chinese firefighters. Notably, for the first time, we delved into the exploration of gender differences in this context. Specifically, our analysis yielded three main findings: (1) The levels of depression and PTSD in female firefighters are significantly higher than those in males. (2) The bridge symptoms in the depression-PTSD network for firefighters are PHQ4 (Feeling tired), PHQ9 (Suicide), and PCL13 (Sleep difficulties). (3) Gender differences were found, with female firefighters’ bridge symptoms tending to be more related to emotion/cognition-related symptoms, such as PHQ4 (Feeling tired) and PHQ7 (Trouble concentrating), while male firefighters’ bridge symptoms were more inclined toward functional/crisis-related symptoms, such as PHQ8 (Impaired motor skills) and PHQ9 (Suicide). These findings highlight the importance of considering gender differences when developing interventions for depression and PTSD in firefighter populations. It should be noted that the findings of this study are primarily based on a sample of Chinese firefighters. The expression of symptoms and network structure may be influenced by specific cultural and organizational contexts; therefore, caution should be exercised when generalizing the results to other cultural settings.

Firstly, our findings reveal that female firefighters exhibit higher levels of depression and PTSD compared to their male counterparts. This result is consistent with existing literature.33 The factors contributing to this disparity may be multifaceted. First, women may differ from men in how they cope with stress and trauma. Research has shown that women are more likely to internalize stress and trauma, which may manifest as higher levels of depressive symptoms, while men are more likely to externalize these emotions.34 Additionally, physiological and hormonal differences between men and women—especially hormonal fluctuations related to the menstrual cycle and pregnancy—may affect how women experience and respond to stress, making them more susceptible to mental health disorders.35 Another possible explanation is that, under traditional male role norms, men are expected to display resilience and emotional restraint, which may lead them to suppress or ignore trauma-related symptoms, delaying their help-seeking behavior. In contrast, women may be more likely to express emotions and seek support, making their psychological distress more noticeable and diagnosable.36

Secondly, In the depression symptom network for all the firefighters, the strongest edges are observed between PHQ1 (Little interest) and PHQ2 (Feeling down), consistent with previous research. Specifically, “Feeling down” encompasses prolonged and excessive feelings of sadness, while “Little interest” entails a marked diminution of interest or pleasure in all or nearly all activities. These two symptoms constitute primary diagnostic criteria and core features of depressive symptoms. Furthermore, “Little interest” and “Feeling down” are also the two items of the PHQ-2, a brief and practical screening tool for depression. Within the symptoms of PTSD, the strongest edges are noted between PCL6 (Avoidance of thoughts) and PCL7 (Avoidance of activities). This finding aligns with previous research.23 PCL6 (Avoidance of thoughts) and PCL7 (Avoidance of activities) refer to efforts to avoid stimuli that trigger reminders of the traumatic event. Research has shown that these two symptoms share similar psychological foundations.37

Thirdly, the concept of bridge symptoms, which are nodes that facilitate the transition or co-occurrence of symptoms between different psychological disorders, is crucial for understanding comorbidity. In terms of bridge symptoms for all firefighters, PHQ9 (Suicide) showed the highest bridge strength, followed by PCL13 (Sleep difficulties) and PHQ4 (Feeling tired). Notably, the symptom PCL13 (Sleep difficulties) served as a common bridge symptom for both men and women, indicating its pivotal role in the comorbidity of these disorders across genders. Specific manifestations are difficult to fall asleep, or sleep lightly and wake up easily. Research has shown that sleep disorders are not only common symptoms of depression and PTSD, but may also exacerbate other symptoms of these diseases, thus forming a vicious circle.38 Sleep disorders are closely related to the pathogenesis of depression, which may exacerbate depressive symptoms by affecting the neurotransmitter system, especially the functions of serotonin and norepinephrine.39 For PTSD patients, sleep disorders, especially nightmares and disturbing dreams, are not only one of the core symptoms of PTSD, but also considered as a key factor for the persistence of this symptom.40 Nightmares and nighttime awakenings may lead patients to fear sleep, thus aggravating sleep disorders and further worsening PTSD symptoms.

From a comprehensive perspective, gender differences in the comorbidity of depression and PTSD and their bridge symptoms may result from the combined effects of biological, psychological, and social factors. Previous studies have indicated that sex hormones (such as estrogen and progesterone) and gender-related differences in the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis involved in stress response regulation may influence individuals’ sensitivity to traumatic events as well as their emotion regulation capacity.12,41 In addition, socially constructed gender role expectations and individual coping styles (such as internalizing or externalizing tendencies) may further modulate the pathways of symptom expression.34 These factors may act together, thereby influencing the structural characteristics of symptom networks and bridge mechanisms among firefighters of different genders.

Lastly, our analysis also revealed distinct differences in bridge symptoms linking depression and PTSD among Chinese firefighters. In the female network, the prominence PHQ7 (Trouble concentrating) and PHQ4 (Feeling tired) as bridge symptoms suggests that cognitive and emotional distress play critical roles in the comorbid relationship between depression and PTSD. This aligns with the broader literature that indicates women are more likely to internalize stress and experience cognitive symptoms such as concentration difficulties and self-critical thoughts.35 These symptoms may serve as conduits through which depressive and PTSD symptoms reinforce each other, thereby exacerbating the overall psychopathological state. Interventions focusing on cognitive restructuring and self-esteem enhancement could be particularly beneficial for female firefighters in mitigating the impact of these symptoms.

Conversely, the bridge symptoms in men are mainly focused on physical and behavioral manifestations, particularly PHQ8 (Impaired motor skills) and PHQ9 (Suicide). Men tend to externalize stress when facing depression and PTSD, manifesting as restlessness, irritability, and even suicidal tendencies.42 Among these, impaired motor skills and suicidal ideation are key indicators of psychological distress in men, especially following trauma, where men often express their distress through intense emotional reactions and physical symptoms.43 This externalized expression of distress can make it harder for men to seek help, and may even delay intervention. However, it should be noted that although suicidal ideation has been identified as an important bridging symptom connecting PTSD and depression, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as centrality in network theory does not imply causal influence.13,44 Given its clinical severity and relatively low base rate in the general population, suicidal ideation may not serve as a primary driver of symptom activation within the network.45 Rather, it is more likely to represent a downstream consequence or an endpoint manifestation of accumulated psychological distress. Therefore, interventions for male firefighters should focus more on addressing somatic symptoms and severe emotional distress, utilizing strategies such as behavioral activation and crisis intervention to help them cope more effectively with emotional and behavioral challenges.

Limitations and Future Directions

Although this study provides valuable insights into the gender differences in the comorbidity of depression and PTSD, there are still several limitations. First, several inherent limitations related to our network analytical approach should be noted. To begin with, all symptom items from the PCL-5 and PHQ-9 are measured on ordinal Likert scales. Although we constructed GGMs using Spearman correlations to accommodate ordered categorical data, this analytical framework is unable to capture potential nonlinear associations between symptoms and may introduce minor bias when estimating weak symptom edges. Subsequent network studies with ordinal questionnaire data may adopt polychoric correlation-based GGM or nonlinear network models to reduce estimation bias and better reflect non-linear symptom connections. Additionally, the sample featured prominent gender imbalance inherent to firefighter occupational demographics. Although this skewed gender distribution aligns with the real-world occupational demographics of firefighters and case-dropping bootstrap analyses confirmed that the female subgroup network reached acceptable stability for estimating overall network topology, unequal group sizes may still lower the statistical sensitivity of edge-level NCTs. This imbalance could impair our ability to detect subtle small-to-moderate gender differences in pairwise symptom edges. Future replications should adopt gender-matched sampling strategies to balance subgroup sample sizes and enhance the reliability of cross-gender network comparisons. Furthermore, centrality metrics and bridge centrality metrics only quantify statistical covariation among symptoms in this cross-sectional network. These centrality metrics cannot establish causal pathways, predict long-term clinical trajectories, or serve as definitive evidence to design targeted clinical interventions. Accordingly, all interpretations of centrality results in this study are restricted to symptom-level correlational patterns without overreaching clinical speculation. Hence, longitudinal network designs and experimental intervention trials are needed to disentangle directional causal relationships between core symptoms and verify whether high-centrality symptoms can act as effective targets for clinical intervention. Second, the current study exclusively recruited Chinese firefighters, whose cultural and organizational contexts differ substantially from those in other nations-including hierarchical management structures, the level of mental illness stigma, and the availability of mental health support services. Such contextual factors may shape symptomatic expression and the structural properties of symptom networks. Accordingly, the cross-cultural generalizability of our findings should be interpreted with caution, and further replications across diverse cultural settings are required to verify their robustness. Third, limited individual trauma exposure data were obtained in this study. Considering the large sample size, we failed to collect detailed personal trauma-related information, including the type, frequency, and recency of traumatic emergency missions. Given that PTSD is an event-bound disorder, the lack of fine-grained event-specific trauma information makes it impossible to clarify whether the identified gender-specific symptom patterns are driven by certain specific traumatic events, which limits the contextual interpretation of our network analysis results. Future research should collect comprehensive data on trauma type, occurrence frequency and recency to conduct more in-depth and precise network comparisons. Finally, the data in this study were collected in 2023, during the post-COVID-19 pandemic period. The study population may still have been affected by ongoing psychological and social consequences related to the pandemic. Previous studies have shown that levels of depression, anxiety, sleep disturbances, and post-traumatic stress symptoms may increase in the post-pandemic period, thereby altering baseline mental health status.46,47 Therefore, the symptom levels and network structure observed in this study may have been partially influenced by residual stressors associated with the pandemic. Future studies should further validate the stability and external validity of these findings in non-pandemic or long-term post-pandemic contexts, in order to disentangle the potential effects of occupational stress from those of broader public health events.

Conclusion

This study employed network analysis to investigate the comorbidity of depression and PTSD symptoms among Chinese firefighters and explore potential gender distinctions in symptom co-occurrence networks. Our findings showed that female firefighters have higher levels of depression and PTSD scores than males. Furthermore, our analysis highlighted the symptom PCL13 (Sleep difficulties) as a common bridge linking depression and PTSD across genders. Although overall NCT analyses did not detect statistically significant structural differences across gender subgroups, tentative divergent patterns of bridge symptom centrality were observed between female and male firefighters. For females, internalized emotion/cognition-related symptoms, PHQ7 (Trouble concentrating) and PHQ4 (Feeling tired), were identified as significant bridge symptoms, while for males, externalized functional/crisis-related symptoms, PHQ8 (Impaired motor skills) and PHQ9 (Suicide), emerged as critical bridge symptoms. These preliminary cohort-specific correlational observations may offer tentative insights for gender-informed intervention design. Nevertheless, these network patterns cannot be regarded as definitive evidence to guide standardized targeted clinical treatments for depressive-PTSD comorbidity, and further replications across diverse trauma-exposed occupational samples are required to validate their generalizability before translating these findings into formal personalized mental health programs for firefighters and comparable high-risk occupational groups.
