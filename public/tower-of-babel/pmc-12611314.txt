Bacteraemia due to Extibacter muris in an Immunocompetent Patient: First Case, Review and a Phylogenetical Analysis
Authors: Martin Gourdel, Caroline Garandeau, Chloé Plouzeau, Anthony Michaud, Lauranne Broutin, Julie Cremniter, Christophe Burucoa, Maxime Pichon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Extibacter muris is a recently identified anaerobic Gram‐positive rod belonging to the Lachnospiraceae family. Initially identified in the murine gut microbiota, it has recently been identified in the human gut microbiota. This article presents the case of an immunocompetent patient who was hospitalized with symptoms of sepsis. E. muris bacteraemia was diagnosed. This species was identified through 16S rRNA gene sequencing after comparison with published sequences. However, further studies are necessary to assess the impact of E. muris on the infection and to investigate potential clinical factors influencing these infections.

Introduction

Extibacter muris is a mesophilic, rod‐shaped, anaerobic Gram‐positive bacillus. The genus Extibacter is monotypic, comprising a single species, E. muris. This organism belongs to the Lachnospiraceae family, part of the Clostridia class, Eubacteriales order, and Bacillota phylum.

The microbe was initially identified in the gut microbiota of mice [1, 2, 3, 4]. As a constituent of the human intestinal microbiota, Extibacter is involved in the metabolism of secondary bile acids [5]. In a subset of women, a higher prevalence of E. muris has been observed to correlate with a lower incidence of premenstrual symptoms [6].

To the best of our knowledge, no cases of E. muris bacteraemia have been described in the scientific literature. This case presents a 72‐year‐old immunocompetent patient who was admitted due to malaise without a loss of consciousness. Following a phylogenetic analysis of the 16S rDNA gene, E. muris was identified in a bacteraemia.

Case Report

Clinical Presentation

A 72‐year‐old patient with an undisclosed medical history, employed as a funeral host, presented at Angouleme Hospital's emergency department on August 30, 2023 (“D0”), due to fever (39.4°C) and malaise without any gastrointestinal symptoms, without loss of consciousness.

Upon admission, the clinical examination revealed photophobia but no neck stiffness or neurological sensory–motor impairment. In addition to these observations, the clinical examination yielded no noteworthy findings.

Laboratory Findings

The initial blood count revealed lymphopenia (0.29 G/L), monocytopenia (0.05 G/L), thrombocytopenia (128 G/L), without anemia (hemoglobin: 154 g/L). No abnormalities were identified in the ionogram, blood glucose, or renal function assays, and the patient did not exhibit any signs of an inflammatory syndrome (CRP: 3.7 mg/L).

Microbiological Identification

Blood cultures (anaerobic and aerobic) were obtained on admission. E. muris grew under anaerobic conditions on chocolate agar (Figure 1). Furthermore, the presence of Gram‐positive bacilli was authenticated by observation under a light microscope following Gram staining (Figure 2). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was conducted on D1 using the disk diffusion method, in accordance with the recommendations of the French Society for Microbiology (CA‐SFM). The results of this testing revealed resistance profiles in the identified species and demonstrated that E. muris (identified by the laboratory of the University Hospital of Poitiers, on D5, using 16S rDNA gene sequencing, as described previously [7, 8, 9]), exhibited complete susceptibility to amoxicillin, piperacillin‐tazobactam, imipenem, rifampicin, linezolid, vancomycin, clindamycin, and metronidazole. However, it demonstrated only intermediate resistance to moxifloxacin. At the same time, cytobacteriological analysis of urine showed moderate leukocyturia at a concentration of 15,000 cells/mL without significant bacteriuria. Newly collected blood cultures were analyzed and showed the presence of anaerobic bacteria after 49 h on D3 compared to the time of admission. Identification by MALDI‐TOF and gallery was unsuccessful this time also.

Treatment and Outcome

A new biological analysis was performed on D1 and revealed the onset of an inflammatory syndrome (C‐reactive protein: 157.2 mg/L). Under antibiotic treatment guided by the AST, the patient completely recovered.

Phylogenetic Analysis

For phylogenetic analysis, the standard sequence of E. muris and 31 other bacterial sequences was retrieved from databases (Figure 3). All these sequences belonged to the Lachnospiraceae family and were compared to the identified strain.

Phylogenetic analysis of Extibacter muris among Eubacteriales order. Phylogenetic tree showing the relationship of E. muris (pink) with related Lachnospiraceae species. Human pathogenic strains are highlighted in red, non‐pathogenic in blue, animal isolates in yellow, and environmental strains in green. Only bootstrap values greater than 70 are included in this figure.

The phylogenetic analysis was based on nucleotide sequences, after the removal of all ambiguous positions. Evolutionary analyses were conducted in MEGA11 with the resulting dataset including 625 positions [10]. The evolutionary history of the taxa was determined using the Neighbor‐Joining method [11]. The optimal tree is displayed, along with the percentage of replicate trees that feature the associated taxa clustering together in the bootstrap test (1,000 replicates), calculated using the Tamura 3‐parameter method [12, 13].

The analysis confirmed the initial identification as E. muris and confirmed the closest related strains to be Lachnoclostridium hylemonae, L. scindens, and Clostridium fusiformis (all identified in healthy human fecal samples) and Sporofaciens musculi (identified in mouse fecal content).

In the broader context, of the species examined, fifteen (15/31; 48.4%) were derived from samples of healthy humans (except for Lachnospiraceae bacterium and Coprococcus phoceensis, which were obtained from intestinal biopsy samples). The remaining samples were associated with animal samples (10/31; 32.3%), human infections (3/31; 9.7%), or purely environmental strains (3/31; 9.7%). This analysis enabled the identification of the bacterial strain; however, it is important to note that the sequencing of the gene coding for 16S rRNA could not be considered sufficient to characterize its pathogenicity, as the present strains clustered with non‐pathogenic strains.

Discussion

This article presents the first description of human Bacteraemia caused by E. muris, the only known species belonging to the genus Extibacter to date. A search for the term “Extibacter muris” in the online database Pubmed yielded a total of five articles, of which four were exclusively concerned with the utilization of a murine model and no one demonstrates bacteremia. Until recently, this bacterium was limited to the murine intestinal microbiota [1]. The initial documentation of this bacterium as a component of the human gut microbiota was recorded in 2022, in a study conducted exclusively among Japanese women [6].

The bacterial cultures were cultivated on distinct agar variants. Only the anaerobic conditions demonstrated the requisite conditions for the study, thereby illustrating the obligate anaerobic characteristics of E. muris. Upon microscopic observation, the presence of Gram‐positive bacilli was confirmed. These findings align with previous research, confirming the identification of E. muris and reducing the likelihood of contamination of the strain when identified using 16S rRNA gene sequencing.

Also, as a bacterium described as commensal to the human gut, it would seem relevant to investigate a possible immune deficiency, but in this case it appears that the patient did not have conditions that would make him more susceptible to infection. However, it is plausible that the patient had factors that weakened his natural immunity, rendering him more susceptible to infection, even if this bacterium could be considered as “opportunistic”.

However, although E. muris was associated with the hospital‐acquired infection in this case, its involvement in the pathogenesis remains unclear. The bacterial species closely related to E. muris according to phylogenetic analysis (Figure 3) are not known to be pathogenic. Indeed, no cases of infection with [Lachno]clostridium hylemonae, L. scindens, Clostridium fusiformis or Sporofaciens musculi, which are the closest species to E. muris, have been reported. In addition, the phylogenetic tree tends to show that the species most closely related to E. muris are either species found in the human microbiota, mainly in the gut, but not associated with infections, or simply isolated from animals. The closest species of E. muris most likely to be involved in human infections are Faecalicatena contorta, which was isolated from tissue obtained from human gangrenous appendicitis, and Robinsoniella peoriensis , the closest species for which infections have been confirmed [14, 15]. Mediterraneibacter gnavus (formerly known as Ruminococcus gnavus ) has also been identified as a cause of bacteraemia [16, 17].

Conclusion

The present case demonstrated that E. muris , a constituent of the microbiota, was able to exploit the patient's compromised condition to gain entry into his bloodstream, thereby necessitating therapeutic adaptation (antibiotic therapy introduction, based on AST determination). Further case reports would be needed to determine the degree of pathogenicity of E. muris and its potential involvement in bloodstream infections.
