Single-agent or Multi-agent Systems? Why Not Both?
Mingyan Gao *, Yanzi Li *, Banruo Liu, Yifan Yu, Phillip Wang, Ching-Yu Lin, Fan Lai

Abstract
Multi-agent systems (MAS) decompose complex tasks and delegate subtasks to different large language model (LLM) agents and tools. Prior studies have reported the superior accuracy performance of MAS across diverse domains, enabled by long-horizon context tracking and error correction through role-specific agents. However, the design and deployment of MAS incur higher complexity and runtime cost compared to single-agent systems (SAS). Meanwhile, frontier LLMs, such as OpenAI-o3 and Gemini-2.5-Pro, have rapidly advanced in long-context reasoning, memory retention, and tool usage, mitigating many limitations that originally motivated MAS designs. In this paper, we conduct an extensive empirical study comparing MAS and SAS across various popular agentic applications. We find that the benefits of MAS over SAS diminish as LLM capabilities improve, and we propose efficient mechanisms to pinpoint the error-prone agent in MAS. Furthermore, the performance discrepancy between MAS and SAS motivates our design of a hybrid agentic paradigm, request cascading between MAS and SAS, to improve both efficiency and capability. Our design improves accuracy by 1.1-12% while reducing deployment costs by up to 20% across various agentic applications.

Introduction

In the face of growing complexity and interactivity in LLM-based applications, multi-agent systems (MAS) are gaining traction across a wide range of domains. In MAS architectures, complex tasks are decomposed and delegated to specialized agents—each instantiated as an LLM with a defined role—who coordinate through structured natural language exchanges
[
37
,
4
]
. This paradigm is increasingly adopted in areas such as software engineering, where agent roles like product manager, architect, developer, and tester collaborate to transform high-level requirements into production-quality code
[
19
,
32
]
; mathematical reasoning, where agents debate and refine proofs
[
13
,
27
]
; and scientific discovery, including hypothesis generation, peer critique, and experiment design
[
15
,
23
]
.

Prior studies have shown the superior accuracy performance of MAS over single-agent systems (SAS) in collaborative reasoning and human-aligned workflows
[
28
]
. By modularizing and distributing responsibilities across agents with dedicated working memory, external tool access, and the ability to critique peer outputs, MAS paradigms support long-horizon context and planning, iterative refinement, and error correction within and across agents.

However, the design and deployment of MAS introduce significant complexity. Defining agent roles, capabilities, and coordination logic demands substantial engineering effort upfront, while runtime execution incurs overheads such as increased latency and token costs. Diagnosing failure modes in MAS becomes even more challenging, with recent work showing that MAS can severely degrade due to coordination breakdowns or misaligned agent behaviors
[
3
]
. Meanwhile, frontier LLMs—such as OpenAI-o3 and Gemini 2.5 Pro—are rapidly improving in long-context reasoning, memory, and tool usage, challenging many limitations that originally motivated MAS designs. This evolving landscape raises two pressing questions:

How does the MAS paradigm compare to the increasingly capable SAS paradigm? Moreover, how can we navigate the tradeoffs between output quality and cost-efficiency for the practical deployment of agentic systems?

To this end, as shown in Figure
1
, we first studied the performance of both MAS and SAS paradigms across 15 representative and widely adopted agentic tasks (§
3
), spanning code generation, mathematical reasoning, and travel planning. Our evaluation includes both proprietary models (Gemini-2.0-Flash, Gemini-2.0-Pro, and GPT-4o) and open-source models (e.g., LLaMA-4). We notice that (i) MAS are capable of solving complex tasks that SAS fails to handle; (ii) However, as LLMs grow more capable, the performance gap between MAS and SAS narrows on complex tasks, while MAS continues to face significant efficiency challenges; and (iii) In simple tasks, MAS may even underperform SAS due to overthinking.

To investigate the root causes underlying these findings, we adopt a first-principles approach and abstract MAS execution as a
dependency graph
, where nodes represent agents and edges denote inter-agent communications (i.e., responses). We identify three primary sources of MAS defects:

•

Node-Level Defect
: The performance of both MAS and SAS are bottlenecked by a
critical agent
responsible for the most difficult subtask, capping MAS performance.

•

Edge-Level Defect
: Errors arise when a downstream agent becomes overwhelmed by (overthinking) inputs from upstream agents, compromising its ability to reason effectively.

•

Path-Level Defect
: Indecisive errors can propagate through chains of agent interactions, ultimately leading to incorrect final outputs.

However, identifying and mitigating these defects is challenging due to the variety of agentic applications. To address this, we propose a
confidence-guided tracing
method that considers both the confidence and output quality of agents to pinpoint
critical agents
—those that bottleneck overall MAS performance, whereby we prioritize augmenting the critical agents for better cost-effectiveness. In light of the performance discrepancy between SAS and MAS, we further introduce
agent routing
and
agent cascade
paradigms to selectively offload requests between SAS and MAS, thereby unlocking new accuracy-efficiency tradeoffs in agentic system deployments. Our evaluations show that our hybrid paradigm improves accuracy by 1.1-12% while reducing costs by up to 88.1%.

Overall, we make the following contributions:

•

We conduct an extensive study of MAS and SAS deployments across 15 representative datasets over 7 tasks using 9 frameworks.

•

We propose a novel attribution method to categorize and diagnose MAS defects by identifying critical agents that bottleneck overall performance.

•

We introduce agent routing and cascade paradigms to integrate SAS and MAS, unlocking improved accuracy-efficiency tradeoffs.

3
Multi-agent or Single-agent Systems?

With the skyrocketing interest in MAS and the rapid advancement of LLM capabilities, it is pressing to reassess agentic system designs. We begin by formalizing the quality and efficiency requirements of agentic applications (§
3.1
). Next, we present a comprehensive performance study comparing MAS and SAS across a range of tasks (§
3.2
), followed by an analysis of the MAS defects (§
3.3
).

3.1
Problem Formulation.

We model the agentic execution flow as a directed graph,
G=(V,E)
. Each node
v_{i}\in V
, where
V=V_{llm}+V_{tool}
, represents an entity such as an LLM agent or an external tool. A directed edge,
e_{i,j}=(v_{i},v_{j},w_{i,j})
, denotes a dependency between nodes, where a message
w_{i,j}
is generated by
v_{i}
and consumed by
v_{j}
with possible post processing.
Then
e_{i,i}
can represent self-reflection. Under this formulation, Single-Agent System (SAS) corresponds to
\{G(V,E):|V_{llm}|=1\}
, and Multi-Agent System (MAS) corresponds to
\{G(V,E):|V_{llm}|>1\}
.

Objectives.

Practical agentic systems aim for high accuracy while also maintaining low deployment cost (e.g., monetary cost for API calls). Specifically, let a user request
r\in R
be processed through the agentic execution graph
G
, producing a final output
G(r)
. We denote the task-specific evaluation function as
f
, where
f(G(r))
quantifies the output quality, which can be a binary
\{0,1\}
(e.g., task success) or a continuous value (e.g. , accuracy score).

Similarly, we can define the cost of executing request
r
as the cumulative cost of all messages:

C(r)=\sum_{e_{i,j}\in E}|w_{i,j}|\times(c_{out}(i)+c_{in}(j))

Here,
c_{out}
and
c_{in}
are the node-specific cost functions, such as the token cost in the input (prefilling) and output (decoding) stage of LLM agents, or a customized function for tools.

Then, the deployment objective of agentic systems is mostly to maximize
f(G(r))
and/or minimize
C(r)
for many requests, highlighting the need for cost-effective deployment
[
17
]
.

Going beyond the diminishing accuracy benefits, we observe that MAS incurs substantially higher costs due to complex interactions among agents. Table

3.3
Why MAS Is Losing Its Edge?

We next investigate why the performance gains of MAS diminish, and in some cases, even fall behind SAS. To this end, we conduct a datapoint-level analysis: for each example in the dataset, we compare the correctness of the outputs from MAS and SAS paradigms. Specifically,
Both Pass
indicates that both MAS and SAS produce correct answers;
Both Fail
means both are incorrect;
MAS Win
denotes that MAS is correct while SAS is not;
SAS Win
follows similar logic.

Leveraging our graph-based formulation of agentic execution (§
3.1
), we next summarize the underlying factors leading to MAS underperformance.

Node-Level Defect:

The performance of both MAS and SAS is bottlenecked by the capability of the critical agent responsible for solving the most challenging subtask.

Our conclusion stems from
Observation 1
, where the majority of cases fall under
Both Pass
or
Both Fail
, suggesting that task decomposition in MAS often has limited impact: if the task is simple, SAS can solve it; if it is too difficult, MAS still fails.
Observation 3
reinforces our conclusion, as the increase in
Both Pass
cases with stronger base models does not correspond to a notable rise in
MAS Wins
. This indicates that stronger models benefit both systems similarly, and MAS fails to yield a higher win rate than SAS under more capable LLMs.

There are exceptions like AIME, which is considered the most difficult math dataset. As such, MAS is able to consistently outperform SAS on this dataset, illustrating its ability to solve extremely difficult tasks.

Edge-Level Defect:

The downstream agent gets overwhelmed by inputs from other agents.

In MAS, agents often engage in multi-way conversations or prolonged iterative refinements. It is common for certain nodes in the MAS execution graph to exhibit high in-degree, such as the summarizer in math debates or the response synthesizer in RAG-based documentation systems
[
25
,
13
]
. However, the influx of information from upstream agents can overwhelm the receiving agent, leading to overthinking on edge cases and ultimately producing incorrect results.

We hypothesize that this contributes to
Observation 2
, where SAS consistently outperforms MAS on a non-trivial subset of examples. To validate our hypothesis, we conducted an extensive case study manually. For instance, in the code generation task using the SelfCol.
[
6
]
framework, the problem analyst and tester agents introduce too many unnecessary corner cases. This overloads the coder agent, leading to task failure. We provide more examples in the appendix.

4
Towards Cost-Effective Agentic Deployments

The intricate accuracy-cost tradeoff in MAS and SAS has led to substantial deployment challenges. We next explore the design space for automatically improving agentic systems. Specifically, we (i) identify and augment the critical execution path in MAS to enhance accuracy (§
4.1
), and (ii) introduce a hybrid SAS-MAS deployment paradigm for better accuracy and cost efficiency (§
4.2
).

4.1
Augmenting MAS Critical Path.

Our studies (§
3.3
) reveal that MAS can suffer from node-, edge-, and path-level defects that limit accuracy. However, identifying these defects is challenging due to the diversity of tasks and the intricate interactions among agents. Verifying every intermediate message introduces exponential complexity and overhead as workflow depth increases. Moreover, most existing MAS frameworks invoke LLM agents through public APIs (e.g., OpenAI or Gemini)
[
20
,
31
,
13
]
, which offer limited control over internal mechanisms such as KV cache management or fine-tuning, further constraining optimization opportunities. A strawman solution is to use the most advanced model for every agent, but this incurs excessive cost, as these models are significantly more expensive to run.

As discussed in Section
3.3
, one promising strategy is to selectively upgrade critical agents rather than uniformly upgrading all agents. However, this is nontrivial, as it requires systematically identifying error-prone nodes and edges in the execution graph—a challenge that remains open-ended. To address this, we propose a lightweight, automated probing mechanism to detect critical agents.

Figure 3:
We propose a lightweight, confidence-guided probing method to identify critical agents for improvement (left), and further improve cost-effectiveness by integrating SAS and MAS paradigms.

Confidence-Guided Critical Path Tracing.

Our key insight is to assign differentiated rewards to agents based on both their confidence and the quality of the final output. This enables us to prioritize the augmentation of agents that bottleneck successful outcomes. Consider the MATH-DEBATE framework
[
13
]
, where agents propose solutions and a majority vote determines the final answer. If the final output is correct (or incorrect), each agent contributing to it receives a reward (or penalty).

However, this naive scheme treats all contributing agents equally, failing to account for their differing contribution levels. To address this, we incorporate confidence scores into the reward function. Each agent is prompted to self-report its confidence level on a scale from 1 (least confident) to 10 (most confident), formatted as
“Agent Response [Confidence Level]”
. Understandably, an agent that is both confident and correct should be rewarded more than one that is unsure or incorrect.

As shown in Figure
3
, after completing a request
r\in R
, we propagate the quality of its final output
Q_{r}
, which is defined by a task-specific evaluation metric (e.g.,
Q_{r}=1
if the output is correct, and 0 otherwise). Together with the response confidence
c_{i}
of agent
i
, its reward score,
I_{i}
, is updated as:

I_{i}=\sum_{r\in R}Q_{r}\cdot c_{i}+(1-c_{i})(1-Q_{r}),\quad\text{where }Q_{r}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if final output correct}\\
0&\text{if final output wrong}\end{cases}

Our confidence-based critical path tracing identifies the agent that presents the greatest reward bottleneck.
To show the effectiveness of our design, we use node augmentation to boost the MAS performance in a cost-efficient way.
Specifically, we identify the agent with the lowest reward score and upgrade its model to a more capable version (e.g., replacing
Gemini-1.5-Flash
with
Gemini-2.0-Flash
). This targeted enhancement reduces the likelihood of localized errors and strengthens the agent’s ability to solve previously unresolvable subtasks.

We use a math reasoning task (AIME) to demonstrate the effectiveness of our design. Here we assign three agents (solvers) with different model series, Llama series (Llama3.1-8b, Llama3.1-70b) for agent A, GPT series (GPT-3.5, GPT-4) for agent B, and Gemini series (Gemini-1.5-Flash, Gemini-2.0-Flash) for agent C. We performed two experiment setup: (i)
Agent Upgrading
, where the original agent pipeline consists of (Llama3.1-8b, GPT-3.5, Gemini-1.5-Flash) and we hope to identify which agent to upgrade to its premium model to maximize accuracy performance; and (ii)
Agent Downgrading
, where the original agent pipeline consists of (Llama3.1-70b, GPT-4, Gemini-2.0-Flash) and we hope to identify which agent to downgrade to its basic model to minimize accuracy drop. Figure
4(a)
shows the accuracy difference after flipping each model, and it reports that our guided tracing approach identifies the most important agent to flip when the budget only allows one. Our decision is optimal for both cases, bringing the most (least) performance gain (drop).

4.2
Integrating SAS-MAS Paradigms.

Augmenting the critical path makes MAS more suitable for complex problems that benefit from specialized roles. However, this still comes at deployment cost. In contrast, SAS tends to be more efficient and effective for simpler, well-scoped tasks. More subtly, investing more resources in MAS does not necessarily yield better performance than SAS (§
3.2
).
As such, one would expect a hybrid paradigm that strategically combines MAS and SAS to leverage their respective design sweet spots.

However, integrating MAS and SAS paradigms is non-trivial due to the wide variability in task complexity and application domains. Moreover, a practical solution must be request-dependent and cost-efficient. To this end, we introduce two primary strategies:
Agent Routing
and
Agent Cascade
for different scenarios.

Agent Routing.

The key idea behind agent routing is to use an LLM-based rater to assess the complexity of each request and decide whether to route it to an SAS or MAS system. This strategy is broadly applicable across diverse workloads.
Here, the rater assigns a difficulty score to each incoming request. Based on a user-defined threshold, requests with scores above the threshold are routed to MAS, while those below are handled by SAS.

The routing design offers users flexibility to balance accuracy and cost. Suppose a proportion
p
of requests are routed to MAS, and the remaining (
1-p
) to SAS. Then, the total cost of agent routing can be defined as:

C_{\text{Rater}}(r)+p\cdot C_{\text{MAS}}(r)+(1-p)\cdot C_{\text{SAS}}(r),

where
C(r)
denotes the cost of processing request
r
. The rater adds only minimal overhead because it relies on a system prompt that can be effectively cached and produces only a small, constant number of tokens representing the difficulty score. The accuracy of this routing approach depends on the rater’s capability and the choice of the threshold value.

Figure
4(b)
shows the results on the math reasoning task (GSM8K + AIME dataset) with
Gemini-2.0-Flash
as the rater, achieving 2% better accuracy at 50% cost compared with MAS baseline. Note that we did not fine-tune a difficulty rating model to demonstrate the generalizability of our solution, so the reported performance can be viewed as a lower bound. Nonetheless, we observe that agent routing generally achieves higher accuracy than SAS and, with a properly selected threshold value (routing easy requests to SAS and hard requests to MAS), it can even outperform MAS (§
3.3
). Figure
4(c)
also shows that the overhead introduced by the LLM rater is negligible.

Agent Cascade improves overall accuracy while incurring lower cost than running MAS per request.

In many tasks (e.g., exact math problems and code generation), the final output of agentic systems can be efficiently verified. This enables an
agent cascade
design that first routes the request to SAS to obtain an initial output. The output is then evaluated for its quality (e.g., correctness). If the result from SAS is satisfactory, it is returned directly; otherwise, the request is escalated to MAS.

Suppose a proportion
p
of requests are passed through SAS, while the remaining (
1-p
) requests have to be escalated to MAS, the cost of agent cascade can be defined as:

(1-p)\cdot C_{\text{MAS}}(r)+C_{\text{SAS}}(r)

As shown in the formula, this cascade paradigm is justified as long as the cost of SAS is significantly lower than that of MAS, which has been confirmed in Table
3
. This makes the overhead of a failed SAS attempt negligible. Conversely, a successful SAS attempt—the “short path”—yields substantial savings by avoiding the costly MAS.

We evaluate our cascade paradigm across a diverse set of agentic applications. As shown in Table
4
, agent cascade achieves consistent accuracy improvement, up to 12% over MAS and SAS, while lowering cost by 20%.

5
Conclusion

We present a comprehensive study comparing MAS and SAS across 15 tasks, finding that while MAS excels on complex tasks, SAS is often more efficient and equally effective on simpler ones. We attribute MAS underperformance to node-, edge-, and path-level defects, and introduce a confidence-guided tracing method to identify critical agents. To optimize cost-effectiveness, we propose a hybrid agentic paradigm that selectively offloads requests between MAS and SAS, achieving up to 12% better accuracy and 88.1% cost savings. Our findings offer actionable insights for deploying adaptive, cost-efficient agentic systems.

Limitation
: Although in this paper, we did extensive analysis across tasks, frameworks, and models, our experiments are mostly focused on agentic systems built upon general-purpose models, rather than fine-tuned models. In addition, this paper focuses on agentic systems for task solving, despite they are the most popular ones. We left those unexplored areas as future work.

Broader Impact
: This paper challenges the prevailing assumption that MAS outperforms SAS in terms of accuracy. We identify that this misconception arises from fundamental limitations inherent to agentic frameworks. We further advocate for future research in multi-agent systems to place greater emphasis on deployment efficiency rather than accuracy alone.

: These tasks involve integrating multiple functionalities and managing multi-file codebases. We evaluate on SWE-Bench and MAST datasets, using frameworks such as ChatDev, MetaGPT, and HyperAgent.

Math Reasoning
: This category includes tasks that require step-by-step problem solving, serving as a standard benchmark for evaluating reasoning capabilities. We include MATH-500, AIME, and GSM8K datasets, and use the MATH-DEBATE framework.

B.2
Confidence Based Tracing

We request the agent to output the confidence by concatenating the following prompt to the original prompt:

⬇

Also
,

provide

your

confidence

in

your

answer

on

a

scale

from

1

(
least

confident
)

to

10

(
most

confident
),

in

the

form
:

Confidence
:

X
.

We will extract the confidence score using regular expressions. The confidence score is recorded for each round. When neither agent reaches an agreement during the majority voting, we will choose the agent with the highest confidence score.

Though we hope the confidence to be normalized from 0 to 1, it would be extremely hard for LLM to output an accurate estimation of a float confidence. Therefore, we instead request the model to output the confidence on a scale from 0 to 10 as shown above. Suppose the confidence ranges from 0 to
s
, we reformulate the importance score calculation as shown in Section 4.1 as below:

I_{i}=\sum_{r\in R}Q_{r}\cdot c_{i}+(s-c_{i})(1-Q_{r}),\quad\text{where }Q_{r}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if final output correct}\\
0&\text{if final output wrong}\end{cases}

We accumulate the importance score over multiple rounds. For round
t
, we update the importance score calculation as below:

I_{i}^{t}=I_{i}^{t-1}+\sum_{r\in R}Q_{r}\cdot c_{i}+(s-c_{i})(1-Q_{r}),\quad\text{where }Q_{r}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if final output correct}\\
0&\text{if final output wrong}\end{cases}

Impact of Number of Agents.

We next study whether the system’s performance grows as we increase the number of agents in it. We use the Debate framework in the Math Reasoning task, and change the number of solves during the debate.

Figure
8
shows that most of the time, when we increase the number of solves, the accuracy does not increase. Actually, we notice that even if we only use one agent, the result is still comparable to using more agents. This finding only confirms our
Node Defect
hypothesis that the whole performance is bounded by the critical agents—weak agents cannot solve the math problem are essentially guessing, and it does not help when we just increase the number of random guesses.

We also conduct the experiment of the effect of

Summarize
(i.e., pass the summary of the previous round to the next round) or just
Concatenate
(i.e., just dump everything from the previous round to the next round). From Figure
8
, we observe that there is not much difference for the
Gemini-2.0-Flash
model, while
Summarize
is worse than
Concatenate
when using weak models. This also confirms our hypothesis on
Path Defect
, where the weak summarizer will inevitably lose critical information from the previous round, leading to an accuracy drop.

Impact of Number of Reflection Rounds.

We further investigate whether self-reflection helps improve MAS performance. We use the Debate framework for the Math Reasoning task. Figure
9
shows that increasing the number of rounds of debate (reflection) increases accuracy in AIME, but decreases accuracy in GSM8K. This further confirms our
Edge Defect
hypothesis, where too much redundant information on easy tasks actually makes agents confused and obsessed with corner cases, thus losing accuracy, while reflection on complicated problems is effective.

Impact of Early Stopping.

To further confirm our hypothesis of the
Edge Defect
, we employ a naive “cut off” experiment: In the code generation task with the SelfCol framework, suppose that the agent analyst needs to send to the agent coder its plan that guides it future generation
Msg
, we choose a cutoff percentage
x\in[0,1]
, and we only send
Msg[:len(Msg) * x]
to the agent coder. Here,
x=0
means no coder has to write the code on their own, and
x=1
is the original MAS setup.

Figure
10
shows that only around 25% of messages actually help in the final accuracy. And for advanced models like
