Abstract
This paper analyzes Stackelberg Gaussian signaling games under linear sensitivity mismatch, generalizing standard additive and constant-bias models. We characterize the Stackelberg equilibrium structure for both noiseless and noisy signaling regimes. In the noiseless case, we show that the encoder selectively reveals information along specific eigenspaces of a cost-mismatch matrix. We then extend the analysis to the noisy regime and derive analytical thresholds for the existence of informative equilibria, demonstrating a sharp phase transition where communication collapses into silence if the sensitivity mismatch is sufficiently high, in contrast with the fully revealing equilibria often found in constant-bias models.

Introduction

In decentralized control and cyber-physical systems, information exchange frequently involves agents with misaligned incentives. Unlike classical communication, which prioritizes reliable data reconstruction,
Strategic Information Transmission
(SIT) arises when an informed sender communicates with a receiver whose actions impact both parties’ costs. In this setting, divergent objectives create a fundamental tension between information revelation and strategic manipulation. Understanding the limits of communication under such misalignment is crucial for designing robust decentralized systems, with applications ranging from smart grids
11
and federated learning
(
16
;
6
)
to economic and political interactions
8
.

In the foundational cheap-talk model of
4
, costless but non-verifiable communication between misaligned agents leads to coarse, quantized information transmission. The Bayesian persuasion framework of
7
considers a complementary setting where the sender commits to an information disclosure policy, yielding a Stackelberg game structure that often enables richer communication.

Over the past decade, these ideas have been extensively developed in control, signal processing, and information theory, where the sender is typically a sensor or encoder, the receiver is an estimator or controller, and both agents have quadratic objectives with Gaussian information structures
2
;
12
;
5
. This has produced a rich body of results on signaling games, including conditions for linear equilibria (a fundamental issue dating back to
20
), dynamic extensions,
13
;
14
, privacy-signaling games
1
;
9
, and multidimensional geometric characterizations
10
. In parallel, the information-design literature has developed semidefinite programming methods and spectral characterizations for quadratic Gaussian persuasion
17
;
15
;
19
, revealing a geometric principle: the sender reveals state-space directions where information benefits her objective and suppresses directions where it is harmful.

A common thread in the signaling literature is that preference misalignment is typically modeled through
additive bias
, where the encoder prefers the decoder’s action to track a shifted target rather than the true state. Under this assumption, a key result in Bayesian persuasion (or Stackelberg cheap talk) is that equilibria are fully revealing: the encoder, anticipating the decoder’s optimal response, gains nothing from withholding information
12
;
13
. However, in many cyber-physical and human–machine systems, misalignment arises not from a shift in location but from a difference in
sensitivity
. Two agents may both care about a vector-valued state but weigh its components differently. A strategic sensor, for instance, may be over-sensitive to certain state dimensions due to privacy constraints
1
or safety considerations, effectively preferring the decoder’s action to track a
scaled
or
rotated
version of the state rather than a shifted one.

This paper investigates how such
linear sensitivity mismatch
fundamentally alters strategic Gaussian signaling. We consider a sender who observes a Gaussian source and communicates over either a noiseless (cheap-talk) or noisy channel to a receiver. The receiver minimizes standard mean-squared estimation error (MMSE), while the sender’s objective is centered at a linear transformation of the state. The sender announces her policy first, and the receiver best responds, yielding a Stackelberg game. Our main contributions are as follows:

1.

We introduce a Gaussian signaling game where misalignment is captured by a general matrix
A
that transforms the state. This formulation generalizes the standard additive bias models, showing that equilibrium behavior is governed by the eigenvalues of the sensitivity mismatch rather than the magnitude of a bias vector.

2.

For multidimensional cheap talk, we specialize quadratic Gaussian persuasion results
(
17
;
15
)
to the linear sensitivity mismatch model. We show that equilibrium disclosure is governed by the spectral decomposition of a transformed
mismatch
matrix, and we provide an explicit linear encoder that realizes the optimal posterior covariance.

3.

Distinct from the persuasion literature, we analyze the
noisy
channel setting with transmission costs. We derive necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of informative equilibria in the scalar case and a necessary condition in the vector case with isotropic sensitivity. We demonstrate a phase transition where communication collapses if the transmission cost exceeds a threshold determined by the channel capacity and the degree of sensitivity mismatch.

2.2
Objective Functions with Linear Bias

The encoder and decoder aim to minimize their respective expected costs. These objectives are
non-aligned
, making the problem a game rather than a joint optimization problem.

We define the
instantaneous cost functions
for the decoder and encoder as follows:

\displaystyle c^{\mathrm{d}}(\bm{m},\bm{u})

\displaystyle=\|\bm{m}-\bm{u}\|^{2},

(1)

\displaystyle c^{\mathrm{e}}(\bm{m},\bm{x},\bm{u})

\displaystyle=\|A\bm{m}-\bm{b}-\bm{u}\|^{2}+\rho\|\bm{x}\|^{2}.

(2)

Here,
c^{\mathrm{d}}
represents the standard squared error distortion. For the encoder,
c^{\mathrm{e}}
incorporates a
linear bias
through a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, an
additive bias

\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and a soft transmission power constraint weighted by
\rho\geq 0
.

Decision makers seek to minimize the expected value of these instantaneous costs over the joint distribution induced by the source, the channel noise, and their chosen policies.

The decoder’s expected cost is

J^{\mathrm{d}}(\gamma^{\mathrm{e}},\gamma^{\mathrm{d}})=\mathbb{E}\left[c^{\mathrm{d}}(\bm{m},\bm{u})\right].

(3)

The encoder’s expected cost is

J^{\mathrm{e}}(\gamma^{\mathrm{e}},\gamma^{\mathrm{d}})=\mathbb{E}\left[c^{\mathrm{e}}(\bm{m},\bm{x},\bm{u})\right].

(4)

Remark 1

The matrix
A
captures the
sensitivity mismatch
between the encoder and decoder. If
A=I
, this setup recovers the classical model of
12
with additive bias
\bm{b}
.

Remark 2

(Cheap talk vs. Signaling)

When
\rho=0
and the channel is noiseless, the transmitted signal
\bm{x}
has no direct cost and serves purely as a message. This setting is referred to as Stackelberg
cheap talk

(
4
)
(with commitment), effectively making the problem one of
Bayesian Persuasion

(
7
)
. When
\rho>0
or the channel is noisy, the problem becomes a
signaling game
, where the encoder faces a trade-off between communication fidelity and transmission cost.

2.3
Stackelberg Equilibrium

We study the
Stackelberg game
where the encoder acts as the
leader
and the decoder as the
follower
. This models scenarios where the encoder’s policy is designed and committed to before the decoder optimizes its response.

Definition 3

(Stackelberg Equilibrium)

A pair of policies constitutes a Stackelberg Equilibrium (SE) with the encoder as the leader if the encoder commits to a policy
\gamma^{\mathrm{e}}
and the decoder plays the optimal best response. The policy
\gamma^{*,e}
satisfies

J^{\mathrm{e}}(\gamma^{*,e},\gamma^{*,d}(\gamma^{*,e}))\leq J^{\mathrm{e}}(\gamma^{\mathrm{e}},\gamma^{*,d}(\gamma^{\mathrm{e}}))\quad\forall\gamma^{\mathrm{e}}\in\Gamma^{\mathrm{e}},

where
\gamma^{*,d}(\gamma^{\mathrm{e}})
is the decoder’s best response to
\gamma^{\mathrm{e}}
:

\gamma^{*,d}(\gamma^{\mathrm{e}})=\arg\min_{\gamma^{\mathrm{d}}\in\Gamma^{\mathrm{d}}}J^{\mathrm{d}}(\gamma^{\mathrm{e}},\gamma^{\mathrm{d}}).

Proposition 4

(Decoder’s best response)

For any fixed encoder policy
\gamma^{\mathrm{e}}
, the unique optimal decoder strategy is the conditional expectation of the source given the observation:

\gamma^{*,d}(\bm{y})=\mathbb{E}[\bm{m}\mid\bm{y}].

(5)

{pf}

Let
\widehat{\bm{m}}(\bm{y})=\mathbb{E}[\bm{m}\mid\bm{y}]
and let
\bm{u}(\bm{y})
be any arbitrary estimator. We decompose the mean squared error as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{m}-\bm{u}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|(\bm{m}-\widehat{\bm{m}})+(\widehat{\bm{m}}-\bm{u})\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{m}-\widehat{\bm{m}}\|^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\|\widehat{\bm{m}}-\bm{u}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\quad+2\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{\bm{m}}-\bm{u})^{\top}(\bm{m}-\widehat{\bm{m}})].

By the law of iterated expectations, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(\widehat{\bm{m}}-\bm{u})^{\top}(\bm{m}-\widehat{\bm{m}})\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{\bm{m}}-\bm{u})^{\top}(\bm{m}-\widehat{\bm{m}})\mid\bm{y}]\Big]=0.

Thus, the cost is minimized if and only if
\mathbb{E}[\|\widehat{\bm{m}}-\bm{u}\|^{2}]=0
, which implies
\bm{u}(\bm{y})=\widehat{\bm{m}}(\bm{y})
almost surely.

∎

We define the following equilibrium outcomes.

Definition 5

We say that an SE is
informative
if the encoder reveals information related to the source, i.e., the source
\bm{m}
and the message
\bm{x}
are not independent random variables. An SE is
non-informative
if the signal provided by the encoder does not alter the decoder’s belief relative to the prior mean, resulting in
\bm{u}=\mathbb{E}[\bm{m}]
almost surely. Furthermore, an SE is
fully revealing
if the signal
\bm{x}
allows the decoder to perfectly reconstruct the source (i.e.,
\bm{u}=\bm{m}
almost surely).

3
Noiseless Case: Bayesian Persuasion

3.1
Scalar Case

We begin by analyzing the scalar instance (
n=1
) under the cheap-talk assumption with no noise (
w=0,\rho=0
). In this setting, the signal is
y=x
. By Proposition
4
, for any encoding policy
\gamma^{\mathrm{e}}
, the decoder’s unique optimal response is

u=\mathbb{E}[m\mid x].

(6)

The encoder, anticipating this response, seeks a policy
\gamma^{\mathrm{e}}
that minimizes
\mathbb{E}[(am-b-u)^{2}]
.

Theorem 6

(Stackelberg informative threshold)

The nature of the SE is determined solely by the multiplicative bias parameter
a
:

1.

If
a>1/2
, every SE is fully revealing.

2.

If
a<1/2
, every SE is non-informative (babbling).

3.

If
a=1/2
, the encoder is indifferent and every policy is an SE.

{pf}

The game proceeds sequentially. Given the decoder’s optimal response
u=\mathbb{E}[m\mid x]
(Prop.
4
), the encoder selects
\gamma^{\mathrm{e}}
to minimize

J^{\mathrm{e}}=\mathbb{E}\left[(am-b-u)^{2}\right].

Expanding the quadratic term,

\displaystyle J^{\mathrm{e}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[((am-u)-b)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(am-u)^{2}\right]-2b\mathbb{E}[am-u]+b^{2}.

We analyze the linear and quadratic terms separately. By the law of iterated expectations,
\mathbb{E}[u]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[m|x]]=\mathbb{E}[m]
. Thus, the linear term becomes

-2b(a\mathbb{E}[m]-\mathbb{E}[u])=-2b(a-1)\mathbb{E}[m].

This term depends only on the prior statistics of the source and the constants
a
and
b
; it is independent of the encoder’s policy
\gamma^{\mathrm{e}}
.

Now consider the quadratic term
\mathbb{E}\left[(am-u)^{2}\right]
. We can decompose the source
m
into the estimate
u
and the estimation error
m-u
. Then
am-u=(a-1)u+a(m-u)
, and we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\big((a-1)u+a(m-u)\big)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=(a-1)^{2}\mathbb{E}[u^{2}]+a^{2}\mathbb{E}[(m-u)^{2}]+2a(a-1)\mathbb{E}[u(m-u)].

By the orthogonality principle of conditional expectation, the error
m-u
is orthogonal to any function of the observation
x
, and specifically to the estimator
u
. Thus,
\mathbb{E}[u(m-u)]=0
. Furthermore, applying orthogonality to the variance of
m
, we have
\mathbb{E}[m^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[u^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[(m-u)^{2}]
, which implies
\mathbb{E}[(m-u)^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[m^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[u^{2}]
. Substituting this back gives

\displaystyle(a-1)^{2}\mathbb{E}[u^{2}]+a^{2}(\mathbb{E}[m^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[u^{2}])

\displaystyle=\left((a-1)^{2}-a^{2}\right)\mathbb{E}[u^{2}]+a^{2}\mathbb{E}[m^{2}]

\displaystyle=(1-2a)\mathbb{E}[u^{2}]+a^{2}\mathbb{E}[m^{2}].

The total cost for the encoder is therefore

J^{\mathrm{e}}=(1-2a)\mathbb{E}[u^{2}]+\text{cte},

where
\text{cte}=a^{2}\mathbb{E}[m^{2}]-2b(a-1)\mathbb{E}[m]+b^{2}
is a constant independent of
\gamma^{\mathrm{e}}
. The minimization problem reduces to minimizing
(1-2a)\mathbb{E}[u^{2}]
. To minimize the cost, the encoder must choose
\mathbb{E}[u^{2}]
subject to
(\mathbb{E}[m])^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[u^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[m^{2}]
.

We have the following cases:

1.

If
a>\tfrac{1}{2}
, the coefficient
(1-2a)
is negative. The encoder minimizes cost by maximizing
\mathbb{E}[u^{2}]
. The upper bound
\mathbb{E}[u^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[m^{2}]
is achievable by any injective policy, such as the identity map
\gamma^{\mathrm{e}}(m)=m
. In this case,
u=m
almost surely, resulting in a fully revealing equilibrium.

2.

If
a<\tfrac{1}{2}
, the coefficient
(1-2a)
is positive. The encoder minimizes cost by minimizing
\mathbb{E}[u^{2}]
. The lower bound
\mathbb{E}[u^{2}]=(\mathbb{E}[m])^{2}
is achievable by any constant policy, such as
\gamma^{\mathrm{e}}(m)=0
. In this case, the signal carries no information,
u=\mathbb{E}[m]
, and the equilibrium is non-informative.

3.

If
a=\tfrac{1}{2}
, the coefficient is zero. The cost is independent of the policy
\gamma^{\mathrm{e}}
, meaning any admissible policy constitutes a Stackelberg equilibrium.
∎

3.2
Multidimensional Case

We now consider the multidimensional cheap-talk game described in Section
2
, where the source is
\bm{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\Sigma_{m})
with
\Sigma_{m}\succ O
, and the encoder has a bias matrix
A
. In this cheap-talk setting, we have no noise (
\Sigma_{w}=O
), and signaling is cost-free (
\rho=0
).

For any fixed encoder policy
\gamma^{\mathrm{e}}
, the decoder observes
\bm{x}=\gamma^{\mathrm{e}}(\bm{m})
. The decoder’s objective is to minimize the mean squared error; the unique optimal best response (by Prop.
4
) is the conditional mean:

\gamma^{*,d}(\bm{x})=\mathbb{E}[\bm{m}\mid\bm{x}=\gamma^{\mathrm{e}}(\bm{m})].

Let
\bm{u}=\gamma^{*,d}(\bm{x})
denote the decoder’s estimate. By the properties of conditional expectation, we can decompose the source into the estimate and the error
\bm{m}-\bm{u}
. The error is orthogonal to the estimate, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\bm{u}(\bm{m}-\bm{u})^{\top}]=O
. Consequently, the covariance of the source decomposes as
\Sigma_{m}=\Sigma_{u}+\Sigma_{e}
, where
\Sigma_{u}=\mathbb{E}[\bm{u}\bm{u}^{\top}]
and
\Sigma_{e}=\mathbb{E}[(\bm{m}-\bm{u})(\bm{m}-\bm{u})^{\top}]
. Since covariance matrices are positive semidefinite, any achievable posterior mean covariance
\Sigma_{u}
must satisfy the constraint
\Sigma_{m}\succeq\Sigma_{u}\succeq O
.

Expanding the norm and noting that
\mathbb{E}[\bm{m}]=\mathbb{E}[\bm{u}]
(by the law of iterated expectations), the linear terms involving
\bm{b}
boil down to constants independent of the policy. The encoder’s problem reduces to minimizing the quadratic term:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|A\bm{m}-\bm{u}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|(A-I)\bm{u}+A(\bm{m}-\bm{u})\|^{2}].

Using the orthogonality
\mathbb{E}[\bm{u}(\bm{m}-\bm{u})^{\top}]=O
and introducing the trace operator (recall that
\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{z}\|^{2}]=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbb{E}[\bm{z}\bm{z}^{\top}])
), we rewrite the cost as

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left((A-I)^{\top}(A-I)\Sigma_{u}\right)+\operatorname{Tr}\left(A^{\top}A\Sigma_{e}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left((A-I)^{\top}(A-I)\Sigma_{u}\right)+\operatorname{Tr}\left(A^{\top}A(\Sigma_{m}-\Sigma_{u})\right).

Grouping terms dependent on
\Sigma_{u}
, the encoder’s objective becomes

J^{\mathrm{e}}(\Sigma_{u})=\operatorname{Tr}(V\Sigma_{u})+\mathrm{const},

(7)

where
\mathrm{const}=\operatorname{Tr}\left(A^{\top}A\Sigma_{m}\right)
, and

V\coloneq(A-I)^{\top}(A-I)-A^{\top}A=I-(A+A^{\top})

is the cost kernel. The optimization is over the set of achievable posterior mean covariances
\Sigma_{u}
, which must satisfy
\Sigma_{m}\succeq\Sigma_{u}\succeq O
.

Directly optimizing over
\Sigma_{u}
is difficult due to the generalized inequality constraint
\Sigma_{m}\succeq\Sigma_{u}
. To solve this, we utilize a transformation to standardize the constraint, adapted from
18
.

Lemma 7

(Equivalent SDP formulation)

Let
\Sigma_{m}\succ O
. The optimization problem

\min_{\Sigma_{u}}\quad\operatorname{Tr}(V\Sigma_{u})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\Sigma_{m}\succeq\Sigma_{u}\succeq O

(8)

can be equivalently written as

\min_{\Pi\in\mathbb{S}^{n}}\quad\operatorname{Tr}(B\Pi)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad I\succeq\Pi\succeq O,

(9)

where
B=\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}V\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}
,
\mathbb{S}^{n}
denotes the set of positive semi-definite matrices of dimension
n\times n
, and the optimization variable is transformed via
\Sigma_{u}=\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}\Pi\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

{pf}

Since
\Sigma_{m}\succ O
, the matrix
\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}
exists and is invertible. We introduce the change of variable
\Pi=\Sigma_{m}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Sigma_{u}\Sigma_{m}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. First, we transform the objective function using the definition
\Sigma_{u}=\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}\Pi\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}
and the cyclic property of the trace operator
1
1

1

i.e.,
\operatorname{Tr}(XYZ)=\operatorname{Tr}(ZXY)
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}(V\Sigma_{u})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}(V\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}\Pi\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}V\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}\Pi)=\operatorname{Tr}(B\Pi).

Second, we transform the constraints. Recall that for any invertible matrix
M
,
A\succeq B
if and only if
MAM^{\top}\succeq MBM^{\top}
. We apply this with
M=\Sigma_{m}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{m}\succeq\Sigma_{u}\succeq O

\displaystyle\iff\Sigma_{m}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Sigma_{m}\Sigma_{m}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\succeq\Sigma_{m}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Sigma_{u}\Sigma_{m}^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\succeq\Sigma_{m}^{-\frac{1}{2}}O\Sigma_{m}^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\iff I\succeq\Pi\succeq O.

Thus, the problem is equivalent to minimizing
\operatorname{Tr}(B\Pi)
subject to
I\succeq\Pi\succeq O
.

∎

Remark 8

(Relation to quadratic persuasion)

The sender’s problem in (
7
) coincides with the quadratic persuasion formulations in
(
17
;
15
)
, where the sender’s cost is linear in
\Sigma_{u}
and the feasible set is
\{0\preceq\Sigma_{u}\preceq\Sigma_{m}\}
. In our setting, the linear functional
V
is induced by the sensitivity mismatch matrix
A
, and Proposition
10
below shows how to construct an explicit linear encoder achieving the optimal
\Sigma_{u}
, thereby instantiating the persuasion solution in a Gaussian SIT context.

Applying Lemma
7
with the sender’s cost kernel
V=I-(A+A^{\top})
, we define the weight matrix
B=\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}V\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}
. The solution to the transformed problem (
9
) allows us to characterize the SE as follows.

Theorem 9

(Equilibrium information structure)

Let
\beta_{1}\leq\beta_{2}\leq\dots\leq\beta_{n}
be the ordered eigenvalues of
B=\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}V\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}
, and let
\bm{q}_{1},\dots,\bm{q}_{n}
be the corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors. Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
denote the number of strictly negative eigenvalues (i.e.,
\beta_{k}<0
and
\beta_{k+1}\geq 0
).

Among the set of optimal policies, let us select the solution minimizing the rank of
\Sigma_{u}
2
2

2

If any eigenvalues are exactly zero, the equilibrium is not unique. Similar to
18
;
15
, we effectively select the solution with the minimum rank (least informative) among the set of optimal policies.
. The resulting unique SE posterior mean covariance is given by

\Sigma_{u}^{*}=\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}\Pi^{*}\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}},

(10)

where
\Pi^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\bm{q}_{i}\bm{q}_{i}^{\top}
is the projection onto the subspace spanned by the eigenvectors associated with the strictly negative eigenvalues of
B
. In particular:

1.

If
k=0
(all
\beta_{i}\geq 0
), the equilibrium is non-informative (
\Sigma_{u}^{*}=O
).

2.

If
k=n
(all
\beta_{i}<0
), the equilibrium is fully revealing (
\Sigma_{u}^{*}=\Sigma_{m}
).

3.

If
0<k<n
, the equilibrium is partially revealing.

{pf}

Following Lemma
7
, we minimize
\operatorname{Tr}(B\Pi)
subject to

O\preceq\Pi\preceq I
. We perform the spectral decomposition

B=Q\Lambda Q^{\top}
, where
\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})

and
Q=[\bm{q}_{1}\dots\bm{q}_{n}]
. Let
F=Q^{\top}\Pi Q
.
Using the cyclic property of the trace, we obtain

\operatorname{Tr}(B\Pi)=\operatorname{Tr}(Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\Pi)=\operatorname{Tr}(\Lambda Q^{\top}\Pi Q)=\operatorname{Tr}(\Lambda F).

The constraint
O\preceq\Pi\preceq I
is equivalent to
O\preceq F\preceq I

since
Q
is orthogonal. Thus, we are solving

\min_{F\in\mathbb{S}^{n}}\ \operatorname{Tr}(\Lambda F)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad O\preceq F\preceq I.

This is the same SDP (up to a sign change in the cost matrix) as in

(
17
, Thm 1)
, where it is shown that there exists an
optimal solution to the SDP that is an orthogonal projection matrix in the eigenbasis
of the cost matrix. Thus, without loss of generality we may restrict attention to
F
that
are diagonal in the eigenbasis of
B
, with diagonal entries in
\{0,1\}
. Hence we can write
F=\operatorname{diag}(f_{11},\dots,f_{nn})
with

f_{ii}\in\{0,1\}
, and the objective becomes the following sum

\operatorname{Tr}(\Lambda F)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}f_{ii}.

To minimize it, each
f_{ii}
is chosen based on the sign of
\beta_{i}
:

f_{ii}^{*}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\beta_{i}<0,\\
0&\text{if }\beta_{i}\geq 0.\end{cases}

(For
\beta_{i}=0
, the choice does not affect the cost; we set
f_{ii}=0

to obtain the minimum-rank, least-informative solution.)

Since the eigenvalues are sorted such that the first
k
are negative, the
optimal matrix
F^{*}
is diagonal with the first
k
entries equal to
1
and
the rest
0
. Transforming back yields

\Pi^{*}=QF^{*}Q^{\top}=Q\begin{bmatrix}I_{k}&O\\
O&O\end{bmatrix}Q^{\top}=Q_{k}Q_{k}^{\top},

where
Q_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
is the matrix containing the first

k
columns of
Q
(the eigenvectors corresponding to negative eigenvalues).

∎

The following proposition, adapted from
(
17
, Thm 2)
, characterizes an encoding policy that achieves the equilibrium described in Theorem
9
.

Proposition 10

(Equilibrium signaling policy)

Let
k
be the number of strictly negative eigenvalues of
B
, and let
Q_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
be the matrix of the corresponding eigenvectors (the first
k
columns of
Q
).

The equilibrium covariance
\Sigma_{u}^{*}
characterized in Theorem
9
is achieved by a deterministic linear encoder policy
\gamma^{*,e}(\bm{m})=L\bm{m}
, where
L\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is constructed as

L=\begin{bmatrix}Q_{k}^{\top}\Sigma_{m}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\\
O_{(n-k)\times n}\end{bmatrix},

(11)

where
O_{(n-k)\times n}
is a zero matrix padding the remaining dimensions.

{pf}

The decoder’s best response to a linear Gaussian map
\bm{x}=L\bm{m}
is the linear MMSE estimator, explicitly given by
\bm{u}=\Sigma_{mx}\Sigma_{xx}^{\dagger}\bm{x}
, where
(\cdot)^{\dagger}
denotes the pseudoinverse of a matrix
\Sigma_{xx}^{\dagger}
which naturally handles the rank deficiency of the noiseless signal covariance, ensuring the estimator is well-defined on the signal subspace.

First, we compute the covariance of the signal
\bm{x}
:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{xx}

\displaystyle=L\Sigma_{m}L^{\top}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}Q_{k}^{\top}\Sigma_{m}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\\
O\end{bmatrix}\Sigma_{m}\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{m}^{-\frac{1}{2}}Q_{k}&O^{\top}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}Q_{k}^{\top}Q_{k}&O\\
O&O\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{k}&O\\
O&O_{n-k}\end{bmatrix},

where we used the orthonormality condition
Q_{k}^{\top}Q_{k}=I_{k}
.
Next, the cross-covariance
\Sigma_{mx}
is

\Sigma_{mx}=\Sigma_{m}L^{\top}=\Sigma_{m}\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{m}^{-\frac{1}{2}}Q_{k}&O^{\top}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}Q_{k}&O\end{bmatrix}.

The posterior estimate is
\bm{u}=\Sigma_{mx}\Sigma_{xx}^{\dagger}\bm{x}
. Noting that
\Sigma_{xx}^{\dagger}=\Sigma_{xx}
(as it is a diagonal projection matrix), the covariance of the estimate is

\displaystyle\Sigma_{u}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{mx}\Sigma_{xx}^{\dagger}\Sigma_{mx}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}Q_{k}&O\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{k}&O\\
O&O\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}Q_{k}^{\top}\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
O^{\top}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}Q_{k}I_{k}Q_{k}^{\top}\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}=\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}(Q_{k}Q_{k}^{\top})\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Since
\Pi^{*}=Q_{k}Q_{k}^{\top}
(from the proof of Theorem
9
), we have
\Sigma_{u}=\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}\Pi^{*}\Sigma_{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}=\Sigma_{u}^{*}
. Thus, the policy achieves the SE.

∎

4
Noisy Case: Signaling Game

We now turn to the general setting where the channel is noisy and the encoder faces a transmission cost (
\rho>0
). Unlike the cheap-talk setting, the signal is corrupted by noise, preventing the decoder from perfectly inverting the encoder’s map even if it is injective.

4.1
Scalar Case

We first analyze the scalar case (
n=1
) where
m\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{m}^{2})
and
w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{w}^{2})
with
\sigma_{w}^{2}>0
. The encoder chooses a policy
\gamma^{\mathrm{e}}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, resulting in
x=\gamma^{\mathrm{e}}(m)
and
y=x+w
.

Theorem 11

(Stackelberg signaling thresholds)

The existence and structure of SE are determined by the bias parameter
a
:

1.

If
a\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
, the unique SE is non-informative (
P^{*}=0
).

2.

If
a>\tfrac{1}{2}
, an informative SE exists if and only if the transmission cost satisfies

0<\rho<\frac{\sigma_{m}^{2}}{\sigma_{w}^{2}}(2a-1).

(12)

where the optimal transmission power is given by

P^{*}=\sigma_{w}\sqrt{\frac{(2a-1)\sigma_{m}^{2}}{\rho}}-\sigma_{w}^{2}.

(13)

Otherwise, the SE is non-informative (
P^{*}=0
).

{pf}

We analyze the game where
m\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{m}^{2})
and
w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{w}^{2})
. Let
D\coloneq\mathbb{E}[(m-u)^{2}]
and
P\coloneq\mathbb{E}[x^{2}]
denote the scalar mean squared estimation error and average transmission power, respectively.

Step 1: Encoder’s cost.

By Proposition
4
, the decoder’s best response is
u=\mathbb{E}[m|y]
. Following the same orthogonal decomposition logic used in Section
3.1
, we substitute
\mathbb{E}[u^{2}]=\sigma_{m}^{2}-D
into the encoder’s cost function (
2
). The expected cost simplifies to

J^{\mathrm{e}}=(2a-1)D+\rho P+(a-1)^{2}\sigma_{m}^{2}+b^{2}.

(14)

The encoder’s problem is to minimize (
14
) subject to the physical constraints imposed by the channel.

Step 2: Information-theoretic lower bound.

First, we relate the mutual information to the distortion using differential entropy:

\displaystyle I(m;y)

\displaystyle=h(m)-h(m|y)=h(m)-h(m-\mathbb{E}[m|y]\mid y)

\displaystyle\geq h(m)-h(m-\mathbb{E}[m|y])

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\geq}\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(2\pi e\sigma_{m}^{2})-\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(2\pi eD)=\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}\left(\frac{\sigma_{m}^{2}}{D}\right).

Inverting this relation yields
D\geq\sigma_{m}^{2}2^{-2I(m;y)}
. Using the data-processing inequality and the definition of channel capacity
C(P)=\sup_{p(x):\mathbb{E}[x^{2}]\leq P}I(x;y)
, we obtain:

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\geq}\sigma_{m}^{2}\,2^{-2I(x;y)}

\displaystyle\geq\sigma_{m}^{2}\,2^{-2C(P)}\overset{(c)}{=}\sigma_{m}^{2}\,2^{-2\left[\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}\left(1+{P}/{\sigma_{w}^{2}}\right)\right]}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow D

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(m-u)^{2}]\geq\frac{\sigma_{m}^{2}}{1+P/\sigma_{w}^{2}}.

(15)

Here, (a) holds since
h(m)=\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}(2\pi e\sigma_{m}^{2})
for a Gaussian source, (b) follows from the data-processing inequality, and (c) substitutes the Gaussian channel capacity.

Step 3: Optimization.

The encoder seeks to minimize the cost
J^{\mathrm{e}}=(2a-1)D+\rho P+\text{const}
. We analyze the two regimes for
a
separately.

1.

Case
a\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
. Then
(2a-1)\leq 0
. To minimize
(2a-1)D
, the encoder must maximize the distortion
D
. The maximum MSE (error variance) is the prior variance
\sigma_{m}^{2}
, implying
D\leq\sigma_{m}^{2}
. Simultaneously, the encoder seeks to minimize the power cost
\rho P
(since
\rho>0
).
Both terms
(2a-1)D
and
\rho P
are minimized when the encoder transmits no information (
x=0
), resulting in
P=0
and maximal distortion
D=\sigma_{m}^{2}
. Thus, the unique global minimum is at
P^{*}=0
.

2.

Case
a>\tfrac{1}{2}
. The coefficient
(2a-1)
is positive. In this regime, the encoder faces a trade-off between reducing distortion and saving power. Substituting the lower bound (
15
) into (
14
) yields

J^{\mathrm{e}}\geq(a-1)^{2}\sigma_{m}^{2}+b^{2}+\underbrace{\frac{(2a-1)\sigma_{m}^{2}\sigma_{w}^{2}}{\sigma_{w}^{2}+P}+\rho P}_{\coloneq f(P)}.

(16)

We minimize
f(P)
over
P\geq 0
. Since
f^{\prime\prime}(P)>0
, the function is strictly convex. Setting the derivative
f^{\prime}(P)=\rho-\frac{(2a-1)\sigma_{m}^{2}\sigma_{w}^{2}}{(\sigma_{w}^{2}+P)^{2}}
to zero yields the unique unconstrained minimizer
P^{*}
given in (
13
).

Thus, an informative equilibrium exists if and only if
P^{*}>0
. Imposing this inequality yields
\sqrt{\frac{(2a-1)\sigma_{m}^{2}}{\rho}}>\sigma_{w}
, which simplifies to condition (
12
). If this condition is not met,
f^{\prime}(0)\geq 0
and the minimum occurs at the boundary
P^{*}=0
(non-informative).

Step 4: Achievability.

The bound (
15
) is tight for Gaussian sources over AWGN channels using linear encoding. Specifically, a linear policy
\gamma^{\mathrm{e}}(m)=\alpha m
with
\alpha^{2}=P^{*}/\sigma_{m}^{2}
results in the lower bound MMSE
D=\frac{\sigma_{m}^{2}}{1+P^{*}/\sigma_{w}^{2}}
.
Thus, the linear policy achieves the global lower bound of the cost function
J^{\mathrm{e}}
.
Consequently:

1.

If
a\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
,
P^{*}=0
, leading to
x=0
(non-informative).

2.

If condition (
12
) holds, the optimal policy is the linear map corresponding to
P^{*}>0
(informative).
∎

4.2
Multidimensional Signaling

We now extend the analysis to the multidimensional signaling game with noisy channel and transmission cost. The source is
\bm{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\Sigma_{m})
with
\Sigma_{m}\succ O
, and the encoder has a general bias matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

By the same decomposition as in Section
3.2
, the encoder’s expected cost can be written as

J^{\mathrm{e}}=\operatorname{Tr}(V\Sigma_{u})+\rho P+\mathrm{const},

(17)

where
V=I-(A+A^{\top})
is the cost kernel from the cheap-talk analysis,
\Sigma_{u}=\mathbb{E}[\bm{u}\bm{u}^{\top}]
is the posterior mean covariance,
P=\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{x}\|^{2}]
is the transmission power, and
\mathrm{const}=\operatorname{Tr}(A^{\top}A\Sigma_{m})+\|\bm{b}\|^{2}
is independent of the encoder policy. The key difference from the cheap-talk setting is that the achievable pairs
(\Sigma_{u},P)
are now constrained by the channel capacity.

First, following the approach of
12
, we derive bounds on the achievable posterior covariance.

Lemma 12

(Determinant bound)

For any encoder policy with total transmission power
P=\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{x}\|^{2}]
, the error covariance
\Sigma_{e}=\Sigma_{m}-\Sigma_{u}
satisfies

|\Sigma_{e}|\geq|\Sigma_{m}|\,2^{-2C_{\mathrm{tot}}(P)},

(18)

where
C_{\mathrm{tot}}(P)
is the total capacity of the
n
-dimensional additive Gaussian noise channel with power constraint
P
.

{pf}

By the data-processing inequality and the definition of channel capacity,

\displaystyle I(\bm{m};\bm{y})

\displaystyle=h(\bm{m})-h(\bm{m}|\bm{y})

\displaystyle=h(\bm{m})-h(\bm{m}-\mathbb{E}[\bm{m}|\bm{y}]\mid\bm{y})

\displaystyle\geq h(\bm{m})-h(\bm{m}-\mathbb{E}[\bm{m}|\bm{y}])

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}((2\pi e)^{n}|\Sigma_{m}|)-\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}((2\pi e)^{n}|\Sigma_{e}|)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}\left(\frac{|\Sigma_{m}|}{|\Sigma_{e}|}\right).

Since
I(\bm{m};\bm{y})\leq I(\bm{x};\bm{y})\leq C_{\mathrm{tot}}(P)
, we obtain
|\Sigma_{e}|\geq|\Sigma_{m}|\,2^{-2C_{\mathrm{tot}}(P)}
.

∎

For the colored Gaussian noise channel with covariance
\Sigma_{w}
, we use the water-filling capacity expression and the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality to bound the capacity term.

Lemma 13

(Capacity bound)

The capacity
C_{\mathrm{tot}}(P)
of the additive Gaussian noise channel with covariance
\Sigma_{w}\succ O
satisfies

2^{-2{C_{\mathrm{tot}}(P)}/{n}}\geq\frac{|\Sigma_{w}|^{1/n}}{P/n+\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{w})}.

(19)

{pf}

The capacity of the additive colored Gaussian noise channel is achieved by the water-filling power allocation. The total capacity is given by
(
3
, Eq. (9.166))
:

C_{\mathrm{tot}}(P)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\max(\nu-\lambda_{i},0)}{\lambda_{i}}\right),

where
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
are the eigenvalues of
\Sigma_{w}
, and
\nu
is the water level chosen such that the total power constraint is satisfied:
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max(\nu-\lambda_{i},0)=P
.

We examine the term
2^{-2{C_{\mathrm{tot}}{(P)}}/{n}}
:

\displaystyle 2^{-2C_{\mathrm{tot}}(P)/n}

\displaystyle=2^{-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\max(\nu-\lambda_{i},0)}{\lambda_{i}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(1+\frac{\max(\nu-\lambda_{i},0)}{\lambda_{i}}\right)^{-1/n}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\max(\nu,\lambda_{i})}\right)^{1/n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i})^{1/n}}{(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\max(\nu,\lambda_{i}))^{1/n}}.

The numerator is the geometric mean of the eigenvalues, which is
|\Sigma_{w}|^{1/n}
. For the denominator, we apply the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means (AM–GM):

\displaystyle 2^{-2C_{\mathrm{tot}}(P)/n}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\geq}}\frac{|\Sigma_{w}|^{1/n}}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max(\nu,\lambda_{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\Sigma_{w}|^{1/n}}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\max(\nu-\lambda_{i},0)+\lambda_{i}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\Sigma_{w}|^{1/n}}{\frac{1}{n}\left(P+\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{w})\right)}.

Here, (a) follows as the geometric mean is less than or equal to the arithmetic mean.

∎

Combining Lemmas
12
and
13
with the AM–GM inequality yields bounds on the achievable posterior covariance.

Proposition 14

(Posterior covariance bounds)

The trace of the posterior mean covariance
\Sigma_{u}
is upper-bounded by

\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{u})\leq\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{m})-n^{2}\,|\Sigma_{m}|^{\frac{1}{n}}|\Sigma_{w}|^{\frac{1}{n}}\left(P+\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{w})\right)^{-1}.

(20)

{pf}

By the AM–GM inequality applied to the diagonal entries of
\Sigma_{e}
,

\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{e})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Sigma_{e}(i,i)\geq n\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Sigma_{e}(i,i)\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{\geq}}n\,|\Sigma_{e}|^{\frac{1}{n}}.

Where (*) follows from the Hadamard inequality (as
\Sigma_{e}
is positive semi-definite). From Lemma
12
, we have
|\Sigma_{e}|^{\frac{1}{n}}\geq|\Sigma_{m}|^{\frac{1}{n}}2^{-2\frac{C_{\mathrm{tot}}(P)}{n}}
. Substituting the bound from Lemma
13
gives

\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{e})\geq n\,|\Sigma_{m}|^{\frac{1}{n}}\left(\frac{n|\Sigma_{w}|^{{\frac{1}{n}}}}{P+\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{w})}\right)=\frac{n^{2}|\Sigma_{m}|^{\frac{1}{n}}|\Sigma_{w}|^{\frac{1}{n}}}{P+\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{w})}.

The result follows from
\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{u})=\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{m})-\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{e})
.

∎

We now analyze the potential for informative equilibria. To maintain tractability, we consider the case of isotropic sensitivity
A=aI
.

Theorem 15

(Signaling potential)

Consider the case where
A=aI
for scalar
a>0
. The cost kernel is
V=(1-2a)I
.

1.

If
a\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
, the unique equilibrium is non-informative for any
\rho>0
.

2.

If
a>\tfrac{1}{2}
, the information-theoretic lower bound on the encoder’s cost, derived from Proposition
14
, is minimized by a strictly positive power
P>0
if and only if

\rho<(2a-1)\frac{n^{2}|\Sigma_{m}|^{\frac{1}{n}}|\Sigma_{w}|^{\frac{1}{n}}}{(\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{w}))^{2}}.

(21)

This condition characterizes the regime where the fundamental information-theoretic constraints allow for a cost reduction via signaling.

{pf}

With
V=(1-2a)I
, the encoder minimizes
J^{\mathrm{e}}=(1-2a)\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{u})+\rho P
.

1.

If
a\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
. The coefficient
(1-2a)
is non-negative. Minimizing the cost requires minimizing
\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{u})
and
P
. The global minimum is attained at
\Sigma_{u}=O
and
P=0
, which is achievable by transmitting no signal. Thus, the equilibrium is non-informative.

2.

If
a>\tfrac{1}{2}
. The coefficient
(1-2a)
is negative. Substituting the bound from Proposition
14
into the objective yields the lower bound function
f(P)
:

J^{\mathrm{e}}(P)\geq f(P)\coloneqq(1-2a)\left[\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{m})-\frac{\kappa}{P+\tau}\right]+\rho P,

where
\kappa=n^{2}|\Sigma_{m}|^{\frac{1}{n}}|\Sigma_{w}|^{\frac{1}{n}}
and
\tau=\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{w})
. We minimize
f(P)
over
P\geq 0
. Computing the derivatives:

f^{\prime}(P)=-\frac{(2a-1)\kappa}{(P+\tau)^{2}}+\rho,\quad f^{\prime\prime}(P)=\frac{2(2a-1)\kappa}{(P+\tau)^{3}}.

Since
a>1/2
,
f^{\prime\prime}(P)>0
for all
P\geq 0
, so
f(P)
is strictly convex. The unique global minimum occurs at
P^{*}>0
if and only if
f^{\prime}(0)<0
, which is equivalent to

\rho<\frac{(2a-1)\kappa}{\tau^{2}}=(2a-1)\frac{n^{2}|\Sigma_{m}|^{\frac{1}{n}}|\Sigma_{w}|^{\frac{1}{n}}}{(\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{w}))^{2}}.

Thus, condition (
21
) is necessary and sufficient for the lower bound
f(P)
to be minimized at a non-zero power.
∎

Corollary 16

(The i.i.d. case)

Consider the case where
\Sigma_{m}=\sigma_{m}^{2}I
and
\Sigma_{w}=\sigma_{w}^{2}I
. In this setting, an informative affine equilibrium exists if and only if

\rho<(2a-1)\frac{\sigma_{m}^{2}}{\sigma_{w}^{2}}.

(22)

recovering the scalar threshold.

{pf}

In the i.i.d. case,
\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{m})=n\sigma_{m}^{2}
and
|\Sigma_{m}|^{1/n}=\sigma_{m}^{2}
, so
\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{m})=n|\Sigma_{m}|^{1/n}
. Similarly,
\operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma_{w})=n|\Sigma_{w}|^{1/n}
. Consequently, the AM–GM inequalities used in Lemmas
13
and Proposition
14
hold with equality at
P=0
and for uniform power allocations. Specifically,
f(0)=J^{\mathrm{e}}(0)
, and the lower bound
f(P)
is achievable by scalar linear policies
\bm{x}=\alpha\bm{m}
. Therefore, the condition (
21
), which simplifies to (
22
) in this case, becomes necessary and sufficient for the existence of an informative equilibrium.

∎

6
Conclusion and Perspectives

This paper investigated a Gaussian signaling game where objective misalignment arises from a linear sensitivity mismatch. By modeling this interaction as a Stackelberg game, we derived analytical conditions for informative equilibria, revealing that linear bias introduces a sharp phase transition in information disclosure. We showed that informative signaling is only possible above a critical sensitivity threshold or along specific eigenspaces of a mismatch matrix, effectively filtering out components where the conflict of interest is too high.

Future research directions include characterizing Nash equilibria without commitment, addressing the general non-commutative noisy case, and applying this framework to dynamic control systems with an evolving sensitivity mismatch.
