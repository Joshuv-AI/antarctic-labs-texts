Incidence of diaphragmatic dysfunction after non-intubated video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery: a prospective, single-centre observational study protocol
Authors: Karim Guessous, Yoann Elmaleh, Fehmi Kattou, Matthieu Coblence, Pierre Dore, Nabil Zanoun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPersisting diaphragmatic dysfunction (DD) for more than 24 hours after thoracic surgery is associated with higher rates of pulmonary complications and prolonged intensive care unit stays. Despite advances in less invasive techniques, the incidence of DD remains significant, affecting ~55%–68% of patients after video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS). Non-intubated VATS (NIVATS) has gained popularity for its safety, but its impact on outcomes, particularly DD, remains unclear. We aim to determine the incidence of DD following NIVATS in adult patients.Methods and analysisThe DYNASTY (Diaphragmatic dYsfunction after Non-intubated video ASsisted Thoracic surgerY) study is a prospective, single-centre, single-arm observational study including 100 adult patients undergoing NIVATS for minor elective thoracoscopic procedures. Diaphragmatic function is assessed using bedside ultrasound preoperatively (baseline) and at 2 and 24 hours postoperatively. The primary outcome is the incidence of DD at 24 hours (defined as diaphragmatic excursion ≤10 mm on quiet breathing). Secondary outcomes include the incidence of postoperative respiratory complications (eg, pneumonia, acute chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbation) and their association with DD. Statistical analyses will estimate DD incidence with 95% CIs and employ multivariable logistic regression to identify predictors of DD, adjusting for potential confounders.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol was approved by an institutional ethics committee (ID 2024-A009121-45), and all participants will provide written informed consent. Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations. De-identified data will be made available on reasonable request, and the trial registry will be updated with data availability in line with International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) recommendations.Trial registration numberNCT06486168.

Background

Diaphragmatic dysfunction (DD) is a common and significant complication following thoracic surgery, even for brief procedures. The incidence of postoperative DD in traditional intubated thoracic surgeries ranges from 68% to 82%.13 DD is characterised by the diaphragm’s inability to generate adequate pressure for effective lung expansion, leading to impaired ventilatory function. It has been associated with increased postoperative pulmonary complications such as pneumonia and atelectasis, resulting in prolonged hospital stays and higher healthcare costs.4 To mitigate the risk of DD and enhance recovery, thoracic surgery has increasingly adopted minimally invasive techniques. Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) has largely supplanted open thoracotomy for many procedures and is associated with reduced postoperative pain, improved pulmonary function and shorter hospital stays, facilitating better patient mobility and recovery. In contrast, standard anaesthetic management for thoracic surgery typically involves general anaesthesia with neuromuscular blockade, one-lung ventilation via double-lumen endotracheal tube and mechanical ventilation. While effective for surgical exposure, this approach carries several adverse effects. Double-lumen tubes pose a risk of airway trauma (glottic or tracheal injury in ~3%–12% of cases).5 Mechanical ventilation can induce ventilator-associated lung injury, especially during one-lung ventilation, and neuromuscular blockade may contribute to atelectasis, allergic reactions and increased morbidity.6 The deleterious relationship between prolonged mechanical ventilation and diaphragmatic function is well-established in critical care. Notably, even short periods of mechanical ventilation during thoracic surgery have been shown to cause selective diaphragmatic muscle weakness.7 Non-intubated VATS (NIVATS) offers a promising alternative by allowing thoracic surgery to be performed without endotracheal intubation, general anaesthesia or profound muscle paralysis. Under NIVATS, patients maintain spontaneous respiration under regional anaesthesia and light sedation.8 For thoracoscopic procedures, a thoracic paravertebral block (TPVB) provides effective analgesia and is recommended as a gold standard in current PROSPECT (PROcedure-SPECific postoperative pain managemenT) guidelines.9 Avoidance of general anaesthesia and mechanical ventilation in NIVATS may reduce the stress response, inflammation and immune suppression associated with surgery, potentially lowering the risk of DD and other complications.10 Early reports suggest NIVATS is feasible and may confer advantages such as faster recovery and fewer airway complications,11 but its impact on diaphragmatic function and clinical outcomes has not been fully elucidated.

Objective

The primary objective of the DYNASTY (Diaphragmatic dYsfunction after Non-intubated video ASsisted Thoracic surgerY) study is to determine the incidence of DD following routine use of NIVATS. We hypothesise that NIVATS will be associated with a lower incidence of postoperative DD compared with rates previously reported for intubated VATS. Secondary objectives include evaluating the relationship between DD and postoperative respiratory complications.

Methods

Study design and population

This study is a prospective, single-centre observational protocol. We will enrol all consecutive patients aged 18 years or older who are scheduled for elective thoracoscopic surgery under a NIVATS at our institution and who provide written informed consent. In our centre, NIVATS is primarily used for minor thoracic procedures, including wedge resections, bullectomies for spontaneous pneumothorax and pleural biopsies or talc pleurodesis for pleural effusions. Patients with neuromuscular disorders, significant psychiatric illness, not affiliated with the French social security system or inability to understand the study information will be excluded, as these conditions may independently affect diaphragmatic function or compromise the feasibility of NIVATS. Patients found to have pre-existing DD on preoperative ultrasound (diaphragmatic excursion (DE) ≤1 cm at baseline) will also be excluded. Recruitment began in September 2024 and is expected to conclude by September 2025. The protocol was developed in accordance with the SPIRIT (Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials) guidelines, and a SPIRIT checklist is provided in online supplemental file 1. A copy of the patient information and consent form is available in online supplemental file 2.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research, due to the technical intraoperative focus of the intervention. However, patient-centred outcomes (such as postoperative comfort and respiratory function) are central to the study objectives (see online supplemental figure S1)

Diaphragmatic function assessment by ultrasound

Bedside ultrasound is used to assess diaphragmatic function, offering a non-invasive and readily available method to quantify diaphragm motion. Two key parameters are measured: DE and diaphragmatic thickening fraction (DTF). DTF correlates with transdiaphragmatic pressure, while DE is simpler to measure and more reproducible in spontaneously breathing patients during the postoperative period. All ultrasound assessments are performed by trained operators under standardised conditions to ensure reliability. Five clinicians with formal training in diaphragmatic ultrasound (each having performed at least 50 diaphragm scans) will conduct the measurements. For each hemidiaphragm, DE (in mm) and thickness (in mm) are measured with the patient in a 30° semirecumbent position during quiet tidal breathing. Each reported value represents the average of three consecutive respiratory cycles for that side. Ultrasound examinations will be performed at three time points relative to surgery:

Standard subcostal views are used to measure DE, whereas lateral intercostal views are used to measure diaphragm thickness and compute the thickening fraction. If obtaining a clear subcostal view of the left hemidiaphragm is challenging, a lateral approach at the midaxillary line (8th–10th intercostal space) is used. When measuring excursion, angle correction is applied if necessary using the anatomical motion-mode technique as described by Pasero et al.12 The primary endpoint for diaphragmatic function is based on the excursion measurement. We define postoperative DD as a DE ≤10 mm during quiet breathing (on either side), based on normal reference values established by Boussuges et al.13 All ultrasound measurements will be made using a portable ultrasound device (GE Healthcare Venue) with a 1–5 MHz phased-array probe for excursion and a 5–12 MHz linear probe for thickness measurement. Anaesthetic protocol

All patients will receive a standardised anaesthetic regimen aimed at minimising any influence on spontaneous breathing. Patients are positioned in the lateral decubitus position (operative side up) to facilitate surgical access. Oxygen is administered via facemask at 5–6 L/min with end-tidal CO2 monitoring, to maintain SpO2 >92%. Conscious sedation is achieved with target-controlled infusion of propofol, targeting an effect-site concentration of ~1 µg/mL (Schnider model). Systolic blood pressure is maintained within 90% of the baseline value using low-dose norepinephrine infusion (16 µg/mL diluted solution) as needed, per institutional protocol. Regional anaesthesia is provided with a multilevel TPVB. Two injections of 0.35% ropivacaine (20 mL each) are administered at the T3–T4 and T7–T8 levels, respectively, to provide anaesthesia of the hemithorax on the side of surgery. Additionally, 5 mL of 0.35% ropivacaine is infiltrated at the incision site to supplement analgesia, targeting intercostal nerves that may not be fully covered by the TPVB (eg, lateral cutaneous branches innervating the latissimus dorsi and serratus anterior muscles). Although a high thoracic paravertebral injection (eg, at T3–T4) lies near the origin of the phrenic nerve, the risk of significant hemidiaphragmatic paralysis from this block is considered very low with the concentrations and volumes used, especially given the predominantly posterior spread of solution. To date, our centre has not observed any clinically apparent hemidiaphragm paresis attributable to the TPVB technique.

Intraoperative management of hypoxaemia

Management of intraoperative hypoxaemia will follow a stepwise protocol (online supplemental figure S2). In most cases, a facial mask will be sufficient to maintain SpO2 >92%. If inadequate, high-flow nasal oxygen therapy will be initiated. If oxygenation remains suboptimal, the procedure may be paused, sedation deepened and a laryngeal mask inserted in the lateral position to increase FiO2 while preserving spontaneous ventilation and the pneumothorax.

If controlled ventilation becomes necessary, neuromuscular blockade will be administered and endotracheal intubation performed with a single-lumen tube via the laryngeal mask. A bronchial blocker will then be positioned under fibreoptic guidance to achieve lung isolation.

The conversion rate from non-intubated to intubated general anaesthesia varies between 1% and 4%, depending on surgical complexity and patient selection.14 Throughout hypoxaemia management, continuous communication with the surgical team will be maintained to allow timely adjustments to the operative approach.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study is the incidence of DD at 24 hours after NIVATS, defined as a DE ≤1 cm (10 mm) on ultrasound. We will calculate this incidence along with its 95% CI. Key secondary outcomes include the DE at 2 hours postoperatively and the occurrence of postoperative pulmonary complications within 7 days of surgery. Specifically, we will record any episodes of pneumonia or acute exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), defined according to established clinical criteria.15 16 Pain will be assessed using a simple numeric scale ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain ever experienced) at hours 2 and 24. We will explore the association between the presence of DD and the incidence of these complications.

Statistical analysis and sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated based on an anticipated reduction in DD incidence with NIVATS compared with historical rates under general anaesthesia. Prior studies report DD incidence of roughly 75% following traditional intubated thoracic surgery. We hypothesised that avoiding intubation and mechanical ventilation would achieve approximately a one-third relative reduction in DD (due to minimising ventilator-induced DD and neuromuscular blockade effects). Since no published data on DD incidence after NIVATS were available at study conception, this estimate was informed by the only comparative study examining diaphragmatic function in NIVATS versus intubated VATS, which suggested substantially less postoperative impairment with the non-intubated approach.17 Assuming an expected incidence around 50% under NIVATS, with a precision (margin of error) of ±10%, we determined that approximately 96 patients would be required to estimate this proportion with 95% CI. To account for potential dropouts, we set a target sample size of 100 patients. All analyses will include only patients who undergo the intervention and for whom full data are available (per-protocol population). Baseline characteristics of the study cohort will be summarised using descriptive statistics: categorical variables by counts and percentages, and continuous variables by mean±SD or median (IQR) as appropriate. The primary outcome (incidence of DD at 24 hours) will be reported with its 95% CI. We will use univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses to identify potential predictors of DD. Variables such as patient demographics, comorbidities, type and extent of surgical procedure and operative duration will be evaluated. In the multivariable models, we will adjust for key potential confounders (including surgical extent, duration of surgery and relevant patient comorbid conditions) to assess independent associations. We will also examine the relationship between DD and postoperative respiratory complications using logistic regression. A two-sided p value <0.05 will be considered statistically significant for hypothesis testing.

Study rationale for single-arm design

We acknowledge that the single-arm design (all patients managed with NIVATS, no concurrent control group) limits direct comparison to traditional intubated anaesthesia. However, our aim is not to compare NIVATS outcomes against general anaesthesia within this study, but rather to characterise diaphragmatic function and its clinical consequences in the NIVATS setting. References to historical dysfunction rates under general anaesthesia are provided as context to underscore the potential benefit of avoiding intubation, not as formal controls. NIVATS has become the standard of care at our institution for suitable minor thoracic procedures, based on accumulated experience suggesting advantages in recovery and safety. Introducing a control arm with intubation and mechanical ventilation was considered ethically and logistically challenging in our context. Thus, a single-arm observational design was deemed most appropriate to investigate our research question within our current practice model. We recognise that this design may introduce selection biases and limit external comparisons. Nonetheless, by maintaining a homogeneous anaesthetic approach for all patients, we reduce variability due to anaesthetic technique, allowing a focused evaluation of diaphragmatic performance under NIVATS. Importantly, our protocol uses objective ultrasound measurements and standardised outcome definitions to ensure methodological rigour. The knowledge gained will address a gap in the literature regarding diaphragmatic function in non-intubated surgery and may help refine our institutional protocols. These results will also serve as a foundation for planning future studies, including potential randomised trials comparing NIVATS with conventional approaches.

Ethics

The protocol follows SPIRIT guidelines and French regulations governing research involving human participants. The study is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06486168) and received approval from the regional Ethics Committee for the Protection of Persons (IRB No. 2024-A009121-45). The sponsor is the Ramsay Santé cooperative group for teaching and research. Written informed consent is obtained from all participants before inclusion. All adverse events will be monitored and reported in accordance with Good Clinical Practice, and patient confidentiality will be preserved.

Dissemination plan

The findings of the DYNASTY trial will be disseminated through presentations at national and international conferences in anaesthesiology and thoracic surgery and through publication in peer-reviewed journals. There are no commercial sponsors or restrictions on publication. All participants will be offered a summary of the study outcomes in lay terms. The study team will ensure transparency in reporting and intends to contribute to the evidence base guiding optimal management in thoracic surgery.

Discussion

The DYNASTY study will provide new insights into the incidence and ramifications of DD in the context of NIVATS. We anticipate that postoperative DD will be markedly less frequent in patients managed with NIVATS compared with the rates historically observed with intubated thoracic surgery. This expectation is based on the premise that preserving spontaneous breathing and avoiding mechanical ventilation and neuromuscular blockade can mitigate diaphragmatic impairment. Our hypothesis aligns with known physiological effects of anaesthesia on the diaphragm. Mechanical ventilation and systemic inflammation during surgery are key contributors to diaphragmatic weakness.10 By forgoing these elements, NIVATS may attenuate the inflammatory cascade and preserve diaphragmatic contractility, thereby improving early postoperative respiratory function. We acknowledge the lack of a concurrent control group as a limitation necessitated by ethical and practical considerations at our centre. Given that NIVATS is our routine practice for minor thoracic cases, a randomised comparison to intubated anaesthesia was not feasible. This observational study is therefore designed to characterise outcomes in a uniform NIVATS population. While this limits comparative conclusions, it allows us to gather detailed physiological data in a real-world setting where NIVATS is standard care. The study will also shed light on the role of regional anaesthesia in maintaining respiratory mechanics. By minimising airway instrumentation and muscle paralysis, techniques like TPVB may reduce risks of atelectasis, ventilator-induced lung injury and DD, potentially translating into lower rates of complications such as pneumonia or COPD exacerbation. We have taken measures to enhance the validity of our findings. All diaphragmatic measurements are performed by experienced operators using a consistent protocol, which should minimise interobserver variability. Nonetheless, ultrasound assessment of the diaphragm is inherently operator-dependent and can be influenced by patient factors (body habitus, cooperation) and technical factors (probe positioning). We have mitigated this by training and standardising our operators and by averaging multiple measurements for each endpoint. Another consideration is the single-centre nature of the study, which may affect generalisability. However, focusing on a single centre ensures consistency in surgical and anaesthetic technique, strengthening internal validity. In summary, this protocol targets a significant knowledge gap regarding diaphragmatic function in non-intubated thoracic surgery. If NIVATS is shown to be associated with a low incidence of DD and favourable respiratory outcomes, it would support broader adoption of lung-protective anaesthetic strategies in thoracic surgery. Conversely, if DD remains common despite avoiding intubation, this would highlight the need for additional interventions or the careful selection of patients for NIVATS. The findings of the DYNASTY study will inform clinicians and help shape future trials aimed at optimising perioperative care for thoracic surgical patients.
