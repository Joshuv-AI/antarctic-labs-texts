Automated skull orientation classification in Thai population using CNN-based transfer learning
Authors: Thadsanan Chantorn, Sangkab Sudsaward, Wansuree Massagram, Sutasinee Chimlek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate classification of skull image orientations is a foundational prerequisite for advanced downstream applications, including automated sex estimation, ethnicity classification, and 3D cranial reconstruction. However, geometric variations and anatomical feature overlaps in cranial photographs present persistent challenges for computer vision models. This study introduces a confidence-based prediction rejection framework to establish a high-fidelity image preprocessing pipeline across five standard viewpoints: anterior (Front), posterior (Back), lateral (Side), anterolateral (Diagonal-Front), and posterolateral (Diagonal-Back). A dataset of 4,673 images derived from 43 dry skulls of Thai individuals was used to develop four convolutional neural network (CNN) models: ResNet50, VGG-16, MobileNetV2, and a Custom CNN. All models were evaluated using 20-fold cross-validation and validated on an independent test set of 760 images from 8 distinct skulls. VGG-16 achieved the highest cross-validation accuracy of 94.59% ±1.50% and lowest loss of 0.15 ± 0.04, whereas ResNet50 demonstrated superior generalization on the independent dataset. ResNet50 and the Custom CNN also exhibited stable and congruent convergence trajectories during optimization. A threshold-based filtering mechanism was deployed to mitigate false-positive errors, with τ > 0.90 identified as the optimal confidence cutoff. At this threshold, ResNet50 achieved an accuracy of 98.49% at a rejection rate of 29.87%. This framework serves as a reliable computational foundation for ensuring accurate skull view classification in forensic anthropology, craniofacial surgery, ethnicity classification, and sex estimation.Subject terms: Anatomy, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

The Medical University of Graz (MUG) has developed the MUG500 + digital skull database1, which comprises high-resolution skull images derived from 3D computed tomography (CT) scans. This dataset has been widely used for various studies, including sex estimation research2–6. However, performing skull imaging via CT scanning remains complex and expensive. Cranial morphology varies significantly across different ancestral populations, with distinct characteristics in terms of their dimensions, suture patterns, and overall structures. These population-specific differences are crucial for achieving accurate forensic identification, performing anthropological research, and implementing medical applications. Significantly, there is currently no established digital cranial database specifically for the Thai population.

To address this limitation, the conventional photographic methods provide faster and more accessible approaches for capturing cranial structures while preserving sufficient morphological details for computational analysis purposes. Such images have been employed in diverse research domains, including forensic identification7, ethnic classification8, stature estimation6,9 fracture detection and anatomical reconstruction models10–14, and sex estimation15–17.

However, a critical prerequisite for these tasks is the proper categorization of cranial images by their orientations to facilitate consistent and effective computational modeling. Most prior studies utilized single-view images—typically frontal or lateral images—for classification tasks such as sex estimation18–20. In contrast, this study focuses on multi-view classification.

To overcome these challenges and advance the field, we propose a deep learning-based framework for the automated classification of skull image orientations acquired from still images or video data. This automated classification system serves as a foundational step for downstream morphological analysis, forensic identification, and computer vision applications, potentially transforming cranial analysis practices. The proposed approach employs state-of-the-art Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)21,22 with transfer learning23, using architectures23–25 based on three pretrained models: ResNet26, VGG27, and MobileNet28, alongside a custom CNN architecture. All four models were trained and evaluated to identify the most effective architecture for skull orientation classification, enabling more accurate and accessible cranial analyses for Thai populations. Classification of various skull image perspectives offers significant utility: in clinical applications, it facilitates preoperative planning29, while in forensic science, it enables morphological analysis for sex estimation and ancestry identification.

Anatomical and morphometric skull study

A total of 43 human skulls were examined, comprising 24 male, 11 female, and 8 samples for testing. These included both intact skulls and partially damaged skulls, either because of fractures or anatomical sectioning. Video recordings were systematically captured from the frontal view and while the skulls were horizontally rotated (excluding the top and bottom views). The video loop recorded images of the skull twice in a straight-on anterior plane. After still frames were extracted at one-second intervals (i.e., one frame per second). These frames were manually selected based on distinctive anatomical features and categorized into five standardized view groups, i.e., anterior, posterior, lateral, anterolateral, and posterolateral perspectives, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

The classification scheme was developed in consultation with anatomical experts to ensure its anatomical accuracy and consistency for use in machine learning applications. The definitions of the different view groups are as follows.

Front (Anterior View): The frontal surface of the skull is fully visible, including both eye orbits and temporal regions. The borders of both the left and right eye sockets must be clearly discernible.

Back (Posterior View): The posterior portion of the skull, including both mastoid processes, is clearly visible. Minor portions of the zygomatic arches (cheekbones) may be visible on either side. No frontal features—such as teeth or facial bones—should be visible, except in cases where some teeth may be seen through the inferior space between the mastoid processes.

Side (Lateral View – Left and Right): One complete side of the skull is visible, including a single-eye orbit, and the tip of the nasal bones, the occipital protrusion, the external ear, and the auditory canal are used.

Diagonal-Front (Anterolateral View – Left and Right): One eye orbit is clearly visible, while the other is partially obscured by the nasal cavity. This view lies between the front-left and front-right diagonal perspectives.

Diagonal-Front (Anterolateral View – Left and Right): One eye orbit is clearly visible, while the other is partially obscured by the nasal cavity. This view lies between the front-left and front-right diagonal perspectives.

Diagonal-Back (Posterolateral View – Left and Right): The occipital region is clearly visible along with partial visibility of the anterior teeth. The nasal tip should not be visible in this view. This perspective lies between the posterior-left and posterior-right angles.

Diagonal-Back (Posterolateral View – Left and Right): The occipital region is clearly visible along with partial visibility of the anterior teeth. The nasal tip should not be visible in this view. This perspective lies between the posterior-left and posterior-right angles.

Left and right oblique views were consolidated into a single category due to their morphological mirror symmetry.

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs)

In this study, advanced deep learning techniques were employed to classify skull images based on their anatomical viewpoints using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). CNNs are particularly well suited for image recognition tasks because of their ability to automatically extract and learn hierarchical features in a manner analogous to human visual perception. The models were rigorously trained to distinguish five skull orientations—front, back, side, diagonal-front, and diagonal-back views—based on their distinctive cranial morphological characteristics.

To optimize the classification performance of the proposed approach, the state-of-the-art transfer learning technique was implemented using three well-established CNN architectures: ResNet50, VGG-16, and MobileNetV2. These models, which were pretrained on large-scale datasets such as ImageNet, were systematically fine-tuned on our skull image dataset to adapt to domain-specific anatomical features. Additionally, a custom CNN architecture was developed and implemented to serve as a comprehensive baseline for conducting a comparative evaluation against the pretrained models.

These pretrained models leverage frozen base layers as feature extractors alongside fine-tuned layers trained specifically on the skull dataset. Simultaneously, the lower pathway depicts the Custom CNN architecture, which consists of four sequential convolutional layers with Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation functions (C1 to C4) interspersed with Max Pooling layers to optimize morphological feature learning.

Following feature extraction, the flattened outputs are passed through fully connected layers. The final prediction output employs a SoftMax activation function coupled with one-hot encoding, assigning a probability score ranging from 0 to 1 across the five predefined anatomical classes (Front, Back, Side, Diagonal-Front, and Diagonal-Back). The class with the highest probability is selected as the final predicted view. This framework establishes a robust foundation for subsequent forensic anthropological analyses, including sex estimation and ancestry inference.

All the images were resized to 192 × 108 pixels to ensure consistency across the model inputs. Duplicate and low-quality frames were removed prior to the training. Four convolutional neural network (CNN) models—a custom CNN architecture and three established pretrained models, i.e., ResNet50, VGG-16, and MobileNetV2—were established using transfer learning, as illustrated in Fig. 2. For transfer learning, the base convolutional layers were frozen, and new classification layers were appended and fine-tuned using the skull dataset, as shown in Fig. 3. Training was conducted using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001, a batch size of 16, and a total of 100 epochs iterations in 20-fold cross-validation. One-hot encoding was employed to label the five anatomical classes. The final prediction was determined by selecting the class with the highest probability within the 0–1 range.

Model training

The skull image used for computer learning is a TIFF (Tagged Image File Format) file type, using three layers: Red, Green, and Blue, for learning purposes. After the skull images were categorized into five anatomical view classes, 95% of the images contained in each class were used for training, and the remaining 5% were used for validation. These datasets were processed through a deep learning pipeline comprising our proposed CNN architecture and the three transfer learning models: ResNet5030, VGG-1631, and MobileNetV228, as shown in Fig. 3. The primary objective was to conduct a comparative evaluation of their classification effectiveness in distinguishing skull orientations based on their distinctive morphological features.

The architectural configuration and layer-by-layer specifications of the proposed Custom CNN model are illustrated in the figure above and detailed in Table 1. The framework consists of four sequential convolutional blocks designed to learn morphological features from skull images directly without leveraging transfer learning. The input image size is set to 192 × 108 pixels with 3 channels. Each block features a convolutional layer utilizing a 3 × 3 filter size with a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function, followed by a 2 × 2 Max Pooling layer for downsampling. As the network deepens from Conv-1 to Conv-4, the feature map dimensions sequentially contract while the channel depth transitions from 128 to 32, as quantified in Table 1. This sequential design optimizes spatial feature extraction while maintaining computational efficiency.

To ensure a robust evaluation, the dataset of 4,673 images was partitioned into k = 20 folds using a stratified sampling approach to maintain a nearly equal distribution across all subsets. This allocation resulted in 13 folds containing 234 images each and 7 folds containing 233 images each. During the 20-fold cross-validation process, each fold served as an independent test set exactly once, while the remaining 19 folds were used for model training. This iterative procedure ensures that every image is utilized for both training and validation, minimizing bias and providing a reliable estimate of the model’s generalization performance on unseen data.

The retraining strategy is preferred in craniofacial analysis due to the high cost of expert annotation. To prevent the underutilization of critical data—specifically the 5–10% typically reserved for validation—we implemented a two-stage training pipeline. Following hyperparameter optimization via K-fold cross-validation, the final model was trained on the full dataset32.

Model training and evaluation were performed on a system equipped with an AMD Ryzen 7 7735HS processor, an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4050 GPU (6 GB VRAM, 3.2 GHz), and 16 GB of RAM. The implementation was developed in Python using the TensorFlow-GPU library for hardware-accelerated deep learning.

Prediction workflow design

The inference phase was performed using a total of 760 skull images collected from eight individual skulls—four males (389 images) and four females (371 images)—as detailed in Table 2 and illustrated with sample images in Fig. 5. These images were captured as still frames during a 360-degree horizontal rotation starting from the anterior view.

The final model, trained on the comprehensive dataset, was deployed to perform inference on an independent test set of 760 images derived from eight held-out skulls. This study evaluates the identification of five skull views, irrespective of biological sex and ancestry. The dataset comprises eight specimens: three near-intact (M1, M3, M4), one severely damaged (M2), two moderately damaged (FM1, FM3), and two slightly damaged skulls (FM2, FM4). Notably, specimen M3 was captured against a distinct background to evaluate the model’s robustness under varying environmental conditions. This setup rigorously benchmarks the models against severe domain shifts and structural occlusions.

The prediction workflow is illustrated in Fig. 6. All four trained models were independently evaluated on this test set to assess and compare their effectiveness in classifying skull images into predefined view categories. The prediction workflow is illustrated in Fig. 6. Initially, the models underwent evaluation via 20-fold cross-validation. For the final training phase, to maximize feature learning from the entire development dataset, the comprehensive dataset of 4,673 images was utilized to train the models without an internal validation set. Based on the optimal hyperparameters identified during the cross-validation phase, the training duration was extended to 150 epochs to ensure proper convergence on the expanded data volume. During the inference phase, all four finalized models were independently deployed on the held-out test set (n = 760) to predict the five predefined skull views and compute classification accuracies. The models generated prediction probabilities for each test image, which were subsequently processed through a rejection option framework. This framework excluded low-probability and ambiguous predictions, thereby retaining only high-confidence classifications across all skull views.

Prediction workflow for skull orientation classification.

For each input image, the model produced a probability distribution across the five view classes: front, back, side, diagonal-front, and diagonal-back. These probability values ranged between 0 and 1, with their sum not exceeding 1. The class with the highest probability was selected as the predicted label. For example, a model output of [0.00071, 0.00001, 0.72858, 0.27057, 0.00013] would result in a prediction of the side view based on the maximum value. The proposed approach incorporates a selective classification (rejection option) framework to minimize error rates by abstaining from low-confidence decisions.

However, to address any potential misclassifications arising from ambiguous features or overlapping visual characteristics between adjacent views, experiments were conducted using both the full dataset and probability thresholds, confidence threshold values of 0.80, 0.85 and 0.90 were applied. Only predictions with maximum class probabilities exceeding these thresholds were considered valid for further analysis. Images with confidence values below these thresholds were excluded to enhance the reliability of the models and reduce noise in the evaluation. Follow the process shown in Fig. 6.

Evaluation of model predictions on skull images

The overall classification performance across all four deep learning architectures demonstrated high diagonal dominance, indicating robust feature extraction for skull view identification. Notably, the ‘Side’ view achieved the highest classification accuracy across all models, peaking at 224 correct predictions in the VGG architecture. However, a shared limitation observed among all models was the misclassification between diagonal and standard views, particularly between ‘D-Back’ and ‘Back’. MobileNetV2 exhibited the highest confusion in this category, misclassifying 40 ‘D-Back’ images as ‘Back’. Conversely, the Custom model demonstrated superior robustness in resolving the ‘D-Back’ class, yielding 127 correct predictions and outperforming both VGG and MobileNetV2. These findings suggest that while standard views possess distinct visual features, the anatomical similarities between diagonal and orthogonal projections present an inherent challenge for feature differentiation.

Quantitative evaluation on the independent test set (n = 760) revealed that ResNet50 achieved the highest overall classification efficacy, yielding an accuracy of 89.08% and a macro-averaged F1-score of 89.20%, marginally outperforming VGG16 (88.95% accuracy). Class-specific metrics exposed critical trade-offs in feature extraction; specifically, for the challenging ‘D-Back’ class, both VGG16 and MobileNetV2 exhibited a striking disparity between high precision (≥ 94.83%) and severely degraded recall rates (≤ 66.87%). This quantitative drop aligns with the previously observed confusion where diagonal projections were frequently misclassified as orthogonal ‘Back’ views. Conversely, ResNet50 demonstrated optimal robustness with balanced precision and recall metrics of 83.74% for ‘D-Back’, while achieving a flawless recall rate (1.00) for the standard ‘Back’ view. These empirical results underscore that residual network architectures offer superior generalization stability over mobile-optimized or custom sequential frameworks when processing non-standardized skull radiographic variations.

Consequently, the combination of a residual network architecture with a strict threshold of (τ ≥ 0.9) provides the most robust framework for high-stakes skull image interpretation, ensuring maximum diagnostic safety by eliminating false-positive risk.

Discussion

To support the preparation of cranial image datasets for downstream applications such as sex estimation, ethnicity classification, and cranial defect and abnormality detection, deep learning approaches employing convolutional neural networks (CNNs) with transfer learning have been widely adopted. Previous studies have implemented architectures such as ResNet and VGG for conducting ethnicity classification based on cranial images8, cranial reconstruction11,13,14, sex estimation from CT images of the skull17, sex estimation according to infant pelvic bones33, and humeral image classification34. Additionally, deep learning has been integrated with IoT-enabled systems for stroke diagnosis purposes using CT images35, often by leveraging ResNet, VGG, or MobileNet architectures.

The experimental results reveal a compelling generalization disparity between localized cross-validation and independent testing. While VGG16 achieved the highest cross-validation accuracy, ResNet50 demonstrated superior generalization resilience, outperforming all architectures when evaluated on the unseen independent dataset. This performance inversion highlights a distinct architectural trade-off. VGG16’s dense sequential parameters fit tightly to the training cohort but incur marginal overfitting. Conversely, ResNet50 leverages residual shortcut connections that function as implicit regularizers, allowing the network to extract adaptable, high-level cranial features that generalize successfully to novel biological specimens.

Furthermore, optimization analysis showed that ResNet50 and the sequential Custom CNN exhibited highly stable and congruent convergence trajectories. For the Custom CNN, this rapid stabilization stems from its streamlined architecture and low parameter complexity, which minimizes initial optimization variance. For ResNet50, the smooth convergence profile is driven by identity mappings that ensure a stable gradient flow during backpropagation. Underperforming across all metrics, MobileNetV2’s constrained results emphasize the limitations of mobile-optimized, depthwise separable convolutions in capturing fine-grained anatomical structures.

Integrating the prediction rejection framework at τ > 0.90 transforms these baseline models into a robust quality-control layer. The framework successfully filters out the geometric ambiguities between D-Back and Back projections identified in the confusion matrices.

By withholding 29.87% of low-confidence samples for manual inspection, the ResNet50 framework elevated its predictive accuracy to 98.49%. In a complete data pipeline, securing this near-perfect input precision is critical; it prevents error propagation in subsequent multi-task learning networks, thereby guaranteeing the performance and validity of advanced downstream computer vision tasks such as automated demographic profiling, 3D skull defect repair, ethnicity classification, sex estimation, and preoperative craniofacial modeling.

As illustrated in Table 7, correctly classified images (e.g., Front and Side) exhibited high prediction probabilities (0.998). Conversely, certain visually similar categories led to misclassifications, such as the Back and Diagonal-Back views, which yielded high probabilities of 0.938 and 0.889, respectively. Conclusions

This study demonstrates the effectiveness of applying deep learning techniques—specifically, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) with transfer learning—for classifying skull image orientations. The primary contributions of this research are summarized as follows.

The precise and anatomically consistent categorization of skull image orientations is critical. Well-defined and anatomically grounded labels enable models to effectively learn and distinguish among skull views.

This study demonstrates that ResNet50 outperforms VGG16, MobileNetV2, and Custom CNN architectures in automated skull orientation analysis, primarily due to its superior capability in capturing complex morphological features through residual learning. A critical finding is that implementing a 0.90 confidence threshold significantly enhances system reliability by filtering out ambiguous predictions, particularly in challenging oblique perspectives. The resulting high accuracy combined with minimal standard deviation proves that this framework provides a robust and dependable tool for high-stakes clinical and forensic applications. Future research should focus on validating this model across more diverse population datasets to further generalize its diagnostic utility.

These findings highlight the importance of skull view classification as a foundational step for downstream applications such as sex estimation, ethnicity classification, and skull abnormality detection. The proposed deep learning approach offers significant potential for advancing research in the medical imaging, forensic analysis, and computational anatomy fields.

In conclusion, this study validates the synergy of deep convolutional neural networks and a confidence-based prediction rejection framework for automated skull orientation classification. Quantitative evaluation demonstrated that while VGG16 provides optimal performance under localized cross-validation, ResNet50 delivers the most dependable framework for out-of-sample deployment due to its superior generalization capabilities on isolated datasets. Moreover, both ResNet50 and the Custom CNN demonstrated highly stable, congruent convergence behaviors during optimization. Implementing a stringent confidence threshold of τ > 0.90 effectively mitigates false-positive risks arising from anatomical feature overlaps in oblique perspectives, escalating ResNet50’s accuracy to 98.49% at a manageable 29.87% rejection rate. Ultimately, this high-precision preprocessing pipeline eliminates upstream classification errors, establishing a dependable computational foundation for subsequent deep learning tasks in forensic anthropology, ethnicity classification, sex estimation, craniofacial surgery planning, and automated 3D skull morphology.
