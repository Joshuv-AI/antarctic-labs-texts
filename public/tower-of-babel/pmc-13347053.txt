A chromosome-level genome assembly of the Tristram’s Bunting (Emberiza tristrami)
Authors: Yinfan Cai, Jingru Lu, Kangjie Dou, Xiaonan Li, Liling Ji, Pablo Orozco-terWengel, Tingli Hu, Baowei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Emberiza tristrami is a migratory passerine within the genus Emberiza, a taxonomic group that has undergone rapid diversification despite broadly conserved avian karyotypes. Here, we present the first high-quality, chromosome-level reference genome for E. tristrami, generated using HiFi long reads combined with Hi-C sequencing. The final assembly spans 1.33 Gb, with a contig N50 of 22.42 Mb and a scaffold N50 of 64.94 Mb. Hi-C scaffolding anchored 1.23 Gb of assembled sequences into 40 pseudochromosomes, consistent with the known karyotype of the species. We annotated 17,401 protein-coding genes and identified 427.25 Mb of repetitive sequences, accounting for 32.09% of the genome. Genome completeness was assessed using BUSCO, recovering 99.3% of expected single-copy orthologs in genome mode and 91.7% in gene annotation mode, indicating a highly complete genome assembly and a high-quality set of annotated gene models. This chromosome-level genome provides a foundational resource for comparative analyses within Emberiza and other passerines, enabling investigations into chromosome evolution, repeat dynamics, and lineage-specific genomic features associated with rapid species radiation and ecological differentiation.

Background & Summary

Emberiza is the type genus of the family Emberizidae within the order Passeriformes, a genus comprising approximately 43 extant species widely distributed across Eurasia and North Africa1. As typical Old World passerines2, Emberiza species show remarkable ecological adaptability, inhabiting environments from coniferous forests to alpine shrublands and wetlands. Despite this ecological diversity, genomic resources for the genus remain limited, restricting comparative analyses of genome evolution, chromosome organization, and lineage-specific genomic features. Among Emberiza, Tristrams’s bunting (E. tristrami) breeds in mixed and coniferous forests of the Russian Far East and the Korean Peninsula, migrating in winter to subtropical and tropical forests of southern China and Southeast Asia1,3–5. Previous studies of E. tristrami have focused mainly on morphology, geographical distribution and breeding behavior, with a lack of genetic analyses to characterize the species phylogeographic or adaptive histories despite its ecological significance6–8. Recent advances in long-read sequencing and chromosome-conformation capture technologies now enable the generation of high-quality reference genomes to address these questions.

Here, we report a high-quality, chromosome-level genome of E. tristrami generated from PacBio HiFi, BGI short reads, and Hi-C data. The final assembly spans 1.33 Gb, of which 1.23 Gb (92.46%) was anchored into 40 chromosomes through Hi-C scaffolding, consistent with the known karyotype of the species. Its high contiguity (scaffold N50 = 64.94 Mb) and genome completeness was evaluated using BUSCO9 (passeriformes_odb10), recovering 99.3% of expected single-copy orthologs in genome mode, confirm the robustness of the assembly. This reference genome not only fills a critical gap in Emberiza genomic data but also provides a foundation for investigating the molecular basis of migration and adaptation, the genetic mechanisms of speciation, and the population genetic structure of E. tristrami10. It further enables comparative genomic analyses within Emberiza and across passerines to explore chromosome evolution, repeat landscape dynamics, lineage-specific genomic features associated with species radiation, and population-level processes relevant to adaptation and conservation.

Methods

Sample collection and sequencing

A healthy adult male Emberiza tristrami was captured in Hefei, Anhui Province, China, on 10 October 2024. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Anhui University (approval no. AHU2021HF0103). The animal was euthanised using an overdose of sodium pentobarbital, and high-molecular-weight genomic DNA was extracted from pectoral muscle tissue using a standard CTAB protocol11, followed by RNase A treatment to remove RNA contamination. DNA quality and quantity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis.

In parallel, multiple tissues (lung, spleen, liver, brain, heart, tongue, and trachea) were collected from the same individual and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for RNA extraction. Total RNA was isolated using the TRIzol reagent, and RNA integrity was evaluated using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA).

Four sequencing data types were generated for genome assembly and annotation (Table 1), including PacBio HiFi long reads, short-read sequencing data for genome survey and polishing, Hi-C sequencing data for chromosome-scale scaffolding, and RNA sequencing data for gene annotation. All sequencing libraries were prepared and sequenced by Frasergen Bioinformatics Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). PacBio HiFi sequencing was performed on the PacBio Revio platform, generating 78.70 Gb of long-read data for primary genome assembly. Short-read sequencing was conducted on a BGI platform, producing 183.54 Gb of paired-end reads used for genome survey analyses and assembly polishing. Hi-C sequencing generated 245.07 Gb of proximity-ligation read pairs for chromosome-level scaffolding. Genome assembly

A de novo genome assembly was generated from PacBio HiFi reads using Hifiasm v0.19.9 (-l3)15. To obtain a chromosome-level assembly, Hi-C reads were processed with Juicer v1.616 and scaffolding performed using 3D-DNA17, followed by manual refinement in Juicebox v1.11.0818.

Sex determination and Z chromosome validation

To assess the sex of the sequenced individual and validate the Z chromosome identification, we utilized whole-genome re-sequencing data from the reference individual and five additional population samples (three putative females and two males). Clean reads were mapped to the assembly using BWA v0.7.17 (mem)19 with default parameters. The average sequencing depth for each chromosome was calculated using Samtools v1.920 depth command. We calculated the Z-to-autosome depth ratio by dividing the average depth of the putative Z chromosome (Chr 40) by the mean depth of the first five autosomes. Expected depth ratios of approximately 0.5 and 1.0 were used as references for ZW (female) and ZZ (male) individuals, respectively.

Genome annotation

A comprehensive annotation of the final genome assembly was performed to identify repetitive sequences, protein-coding genes, and non-coding RNA (ncRNA) elements.

Repetitive sequence annotation: Repetitive sequences were annotated using a combination of de novo and homology-based approaches. For de novo prediction, we used LTR_FINDER v1.0.721 with parameters -w2 -C to identify full-length LTR retrotransposons, and RepeatModeler v2.0.122 to construct a species-specific repeat library. For homology-based prediction, RepeatMasker v.4.1.223 was used with parameters -nolow, -no_is, and -norna to screen the genome against both the newly constructed library and the public Repbase database24,25. Additionally, Tandem Repeats Finder v. 4.09.126 was used to identify tandem repeats with parameters 2 7 7 80 10 50 2000 -d -h.

Protein-coding gene annotation

Protein-coding gene annotation Gene prediction was conducted by integrating three evidence sources: homology-based, transcriptome-based, and ab initio prediction. For homology-based prediction, protein sequences from four related species (Taeniopygia guttata, Serinus canaria, Emberiza aureola, and Gallus gallus) were aligned to the genome using TBLASTN v.2.11.0+27 with an E-value threshold of 1e−5, and gene models were predicted using Exonerate v.2.4.028 using the protein2genome model. Transcriptome-based prediction used RNA-Seq reads from multiple tissues aligned to our genome using HISAT2 v2.2.129, and transcripts were assembled with StringTie v2.1.729. These assembled transcripts were then used by PASA v2.4.130 to generate transcript-based gene models. Ab initio prediction was carried out using Augustus v.3.4.031–33 with parameters–noinFrameStop = true–strand = both., Genscan v1.034 was employed to predict gene structures based on intrinsic genomic signals. Finally, all evidence was integrated using Maker v3.01.0335 to generate a unified gene set.

Functional annotation

Functional annotation was performed by searching the predicted proteins against the Swiss-Prot36, TrEMBL36, NR, InterPro37, Gene Ontology (GO)38, and KEGG databases39 using Diamond v2.0.740 and InterProScan v5.50–84.041.

Non-coding RNA annotation

Non-coding RNA (ncRNA) elements were annotated using a combination of specialized tools. Transfer RNA (tRNA) genes were predicted with tRNAscan-SE v2.0.942 using default parameters. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes were identified with RNAmmer v1.243. Other small ncRNAs, including microRNAs (miRNAs) and small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), were detected by searching the genome against the Rfam database v14.644 using Infernal v1.1.245. Technical Validation

Assembly quality and completeness

The quality and reliability of the E. tristrami genome assembly were assessed using multiple complementary approaches. Illumina short reads and PacBio HiFi reads were independently mapped back to the final assembly using BWA and minimap2, respectively. Both datasets exhibited mapping rates exceeding 99.8%, indicating high base-level accuracy.

Assembly consensus quality was further evaluated using Merqury, which estimated a consensus quality value (QV) of 60.06, corresponding to an exceptionally low base error rate. Potential biases in sequencing coverage were assessed by examining the relationship between GC content and sequencing depth (Fig. 2A,B). While macrochromosomes generally displayed a broad GC distribution, additional narrow, high GC content peaks were observed, which likely correspond to highly repetitive regions such as centromeres and telomeres. Genome completeness assessment

Genome completeness was evaluated using BUSCO (v5.2.2) against the passeriformes_odb10 database. The genome assembly recovered 99.3% of expected single-copy orthologs (S: 99.1%, D: 0.2%, F: 0.4%, M: 0.3%). The annotated gene set recovered 91.7% of BUSCO genes (S: 91.4%, D: 0.3%, F: 0.1%, M: 8.2%) (Fig. 3; Table 4). While the assembly itself is highly complete, the BUSCO score for the annotated gene set suggests that approximately 10% of genes may be missing. Genome assembly overview and annotation summary

We generated a high-quality, chromosome-level genome assembly for Emberiza tristrami by integrating PacBio HiFi long reads and Hi-C data. The final assembly spanned 1.33 Gb, with a contig N50 of 22.42 Mb and a scaffold N50 of 64.94 Mb. Hi-C scaffolding anchored 1.23 Gb (92.46%) of assembled sequences into 40 chromosome-scale scaffolds, consistent with the expected karyotype of the species. A total of 17,401 protein-coding genes were annotated, and repetitive elements accounted for 32.09% of the genome, with long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons representing the most abundant repeat class.

Protein-coding gene annotation and structural validation

Protein-coding gene annotation resulted in 17,401 predicted genes supported by homology-based, transcriptome-based, and ab initio evidence. The structural characteristics of the annotated gene models were evaluated by comparing distributions of gene length, coding sequence (CDS) length, exon length, intron length, and exon number with four well-annotated avian genomes (Taeniopygia guttata, Serinus canaria, Emberiza aureola, and Gallus gallus). Hi-C–based chromosome scaffolding

Chromosome-level scaffolding was further evaluated using Hi-C interaction heatmaps. The heatmap displayed strong intra-chromosomal interaction signals along the diagonal and minimal inter-chromosomal contacts (Fig. 7), indicating accurate clustering and ordering of contigs within chromosomes. Regions showing reduced interaction signals likely correspond to highly repetitive or centromeric regions, where read mapping is inherently ambiguous.Fig. 7Hi-C interaction heatmap of the chromosome-level assembly. The heatmap visualizes the interaction frequencies between genomic regions. The strong signal along the diagonal indicates a high frequency of intra-chromosomal interactions, validating the accuracy of the chromosome scaffolding. Off-diagonal signals represent inter-chromosomal interactions, which are minimal, as expected for a high-quality assembly. The signal loss observed in certain regions, such as on chromosome 9, may correspond to potential centromeres, which are typically rich in repetitive sequences that cause Hi-C read mapping ambiguities.

Comparative synteny and karyotype conservation

Whole-genome synteny analysis was performed between E. tristrami and five closely related congeners (E. aureola, E. pusilla, E. rustica, E. rutila, and E. spodocephala).
