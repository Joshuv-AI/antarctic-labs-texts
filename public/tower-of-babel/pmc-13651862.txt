Effects of probiotics on joint homeostasis and osteoarthritis: Development of microbe-gut-cartilage axis-on-a-chip
Authors: Jun Liu, Ning Zhang, Yiting Lei, Xiaoshuai Wang, Xiang Zhang, Wei Huang, Changhai Ding, Denghui Xie, Jun Yu, Rocky S. Tuan, Zhong Alan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although emerging evidence implicates gut microbiome-cartilage cross-talk in the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis (OA), the most common joint disease, studying this microbe-gut-joint axis remains challenging due to limitations of animal models and conventional in vitro systems. Here, we report a microbe-gut-cartilage axis-on-a-chip (MGCAoC) that recreates tissue-specific microenvironments with oxygen gradients and physiological fluid shear. The computer simulation–optimized chip design generates an anoxic-oxic interface for the gut module and enables perfusion of probiotic metabolites to human cartilage organoids without cross-contamination. Multiomics profiling suggested that probiotic metabolites may promote cartilage anabolism, potentially involving PI3K and MAPK pathway, and this effect was associated with up-regulated collagen type II and aggrecan expression while suppressing metalloproteinases. In inflamed cartilage organoids, these metabolites were associated with reduced proinflammatory signaling while preserving matrix integrity. The chondroprotective effects were confirmed using cartilage organoids derived from patients with OA treated with identified metabolites. This MGCAoC enables mechanistic investigation of gut-joint axis physiology and provides a versatile preclinical tool for screening probiotic-based OA therapeutics.

INTRODUCTION

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic degenerative disorder affecting ∼5% of the global population (1). As the most prevalent clinical joint condition worldwide, OA imposes substantial health, economic, and social burdens, with its incidence steadily rising over the past three decades. Current therapeutic strategies, including physical therapy, pharmacological treatments (e.g., nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and opioids), and joint replacement, primarily alleviate symptoms but do not modify disease progression (2, 3). Therefore, there is an urgent need for disease-modifying interventions with minimal side effects for OA treatment.

Mounting evidence implicates the crucial role of gut microbiota in joint health and OA development (4, 5). Gut microbiota influences joint function through multifaceted mechanisms (4). Gut microbiota–produced short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) can suppress the level of proinflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), and cartilage-degrading enzymes, such as metalloproteinase-13 (MMP13) (6). Increased serum zonulin levels and impaired intestinal tight junctions have been found to precede joint inflammation (7). Several gut microbial metabolites, including glutarate, oxoglutarate, and tryptophan-derived metabolites, have been found to correlate with posttraumatic OA severity (8–10). Notably, probiotic interventions including Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species have recently been demonstrated to exhibit chondroprotective effects, although the strength and disease specificity of the evidence differ among strains (11, 12). In particular, Lactobacillus plantarum (LP) GKD7 has been reported to improve subchondral bone architecture, reduce synovial inflammation, and protect articular cartilage in anterior cruciate ligament transection-induced OA rat models (13, 14). In contrast, evidence for Bifidobacterium is derived mainly from genus-level probiotic studies or arthritis-related models rather than direct OA-specific investigations. For example, strains such as Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum G7 and Bifidobacterium breve NCIM 5671 have been shown to attenuate inflammatory and joint-destructive responses in experimental arthritis and OA-related settings (12, 15). Additionally, probiotic formulations containing Bifidobacterium bifidum 1 (BB) or Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis Probio-M8 have been reported to enhance the therapeutic effects of chondroitin sulfate in OA rats (16, 17). Although these prior findings support the broader relevance of gut probiotics to joint health, our understanding of the microbe-gut-cartilage axis (MGCA) remains largely limited to correlative studies partly due to the lack of physiologically relevant models.

Specifically, humanized microbiota mice, while valuable, cannot recapitulate human-specific host-microbe cross-talk or provide real-time monitoring of multitissue interactions. Conventional in vitro systems are incapable of modeling the MGCA due to three fundamental constraints. First, conventional in vitro models do not support the coculture of anaerobes/obligate anaerobes (e.g., BB) with mammalian cells. Second, they are often characterized by microenvironmental oversimplification, lacking oxygen gradients, fluid shear stress, and intestinal peristalsis. Third, there are inherent barriers to controllable tissue cross-talk due to the need to avoid cross-contamination. Moreover, probiotic strains differ substantially in their physiological characteristics, including oxygen requirements, posing a practical challenge for in vitro modeling of microbiota-host interactions. A platform capable of supporting bacterial strains with distinct culture requirements would thus be valuable for mechanistic studies and could serve as a basis for future screening of candidate probiotics.

Recent regulatory paradigm shifts have paved the way for the adoption of new alternative methodologies, including organs-on-chips (OoCs) and organoids, as essential components of preclinical research (18, 19). OoC platforms address the limitations of conventional models by emulating human tissue-specific microenvironments through microengineered architectures, incorporating dynamic fluidic flow to model circulatory and interstitial flow, and enabling real-time assessment of multiorgan interactions. Previous studies have separately demonstrated the feasibility of establishing gut- and cartilage-mimicking OoCs. However, a major barrier to on-chip emulation of the MGCA is the need to concurrently sustain the specialized phenotypes and functions of both intestinal and cartilaginous tissues while enabling functional cross-talk between them. Additionally, technical challenges in scaling these platforms for high-throughput use, ensuring reproducibility, and overcoming diffusional constraints have further impeded the development of robust multiorgan platforms.

To address these challenges, we engineered an integrated MGCA-on-a-chip (MGCAoC) platform that enables functional communication between the gut and cartilage modules with tissue-specific microenvironments (Fig. 1). With optimized chip design and flow conditions, we successfully maintained the anoxic-oxic interface (AOI) at the double-layer microchannels of the gut module and physiological shear stresses on the intestinal epithelium and cartilage organoids (COs). By coupling the gut epithelium derived from human epithelial Caco-2 cells seeded with probiotic combinations (BB and LP) to human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell (BMSC)–derived COs, we showed that probiotic introduction to the gut module could enhance cartilage maturation and confer anti-inflammatory properties. Multiomics, biochemical, and pathway-intervention analyses further supported that these effects were associated with, and at least partly mediated through, modulation of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/extracellular signal–regulated kinase (ERK) signaling, accompanied by increased chondrogenic marker expression and reduced catabolic responses in healthy and inflammatory COs. Furthermore, we identified selected candidate metabolites and directly demonstrated their protective effects in inflamed COs derived from patients with OA, further supporting the translational and physiological relevance of our MGCAoC platform. This study modeled in vitro the human MGCA, enabling investigations into the dynamic regulatory role of probiotic metabolites on cartilage development and degeneration. Our MGCAoC platform represents an effective in vitro model for both mechanistic and translational research that targets gut microbiome for OA treatment.

RESULTS

Design, computer simulation, and fabrication of the microfluidic chip

The recapitulation of MGCA in vitro requires the creation of tissue-specific microenvironments and microcirculation that enable tissue communication without cross-contamination. To establish the multiorgan MGCAoC, a microfluidic chip was fabricated through three-dimensional (3D) printing and photolithography (Fig. 2A). The chip features two bionic microenvironments connected by a simulated internal circulation channel. The chip contains two layers, with the upper layer containing a serpentine channel that mimics the gut and the lower layer featuring a serpentine channel that mimics the inner circulation (red part of lower channel) and leads to a microwell array capable of housing 18 COs (Fig. 2B). The upper- and lower-layer serpentine channels overlap and are separated by a porous membrane. Two microvalves (μV1 and μV2) are introduced to the internal circulation channel (Fig. 2, A and C), which can be conveniently opened/closed to connect and disconnect the gut module to CO module. An assembled microfluidic chip is shown in Fig. 2C. To construct an AOI for the gut module, computational simulation was conducted to optimize the serpentine channel heights and medium flow rates. It was found that, at a flow rate of 45 μl/hour, channel heights of 600 and 300 μm for the upper and lower serpentine channel, respectively, enable the generation and maintenance of a stable AOI in the gut module (Fig. 2D; fig. S1, A to D; and table S1). The flow velocity and flow-induced shear stress in the upper channel, which would be applied to the surface of the intestinal epithelium, were also simulated (Fig. 2, E and F). The flow rates across all microwells at different locations of the array remained consistent throughout (Fig. 2, G to J). Thus, the similar flow velocities in the microwells subjected the COs to uniform fluid shear forces (Fig. 2, I and K; and fig. S1E).

Establishing the MGCAoC platform

To establish the MGCAoC platform, human BMSCs, Caco-2 cells, and two probiotic strains, namely, BB and LP, were introduced into the appropriate MGCAoC modules at different time points (Fig. 3A). The intestinal epithelium was generated from Caco-2 cells under perfusion, with its barrier function evidenced by a gradual decrease in apparent permeability (Papp) and an increase in transepithelial resistance (TEER) over 7 days (Fig. 3, B and C). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image shows the microvilli generated on the 3D Caco-2–derived intestinal epithelium (Fig. 3D). When the intestinal epithelium was covered with a continuous and dense layer of mucus, the probiotics BB and LP were inoculated onto the mucus layer (Fig. 3E and fig. S2). Confocal imaging of the probiotic-colonized epithelium revealed ZO-1 tight junction protein (green) localized at the intercellular junctions and viable probiotic (red) populations (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, confocal images are consistent with SEM observation, showing probiotics colonizing the mucus layer on the Caco-2 cells (Fig. 3G). These data confirm that the MGCAoC platform supports the growth of both obligate anaerobic and aerobic bacteria and human intestinal epithelial cells within the same channel, validating the presence of an AOI in the gut module.

To generate COs in the cartilage module, human BMSC suspension in chondrogenic medium was injected into the inlet of the microwell array, which was subsequently kept static for 24 hours to allow spontaneous spheroid formation at the microwell bottom. Medium perfusion started 24 hours after BMSC injection. To confirm the MGCAoC platform’s ability to support sustainable coculture of gut and cartilage modules, the internal circulation channel was connected to the cartilage module by opening valve 2 (μV2), exposing the COs to the bottom-channel effluents from the probiotic-colonized gut module (Fig. 3A). Hereafter, “effluents” refer to bottom-channel effluents from the gut module, unless otherwise specified. Compared with those grown on 96-well plates and CO-on-a-chip (COoC; not connected to the gut module), COs cultured on the MGCAoC platform showed more consistent and larger sizes (Fig. 3, H and I). Dynamic on-chip culture, together with live probiotic colonization in the gut module, was associated with enhanced CO growth and improved size uniformity in the integrated MGCAoC system. On day 16, COs from the MGCAoC system were 33.09 and 10.30% larger than those cultured on round-bottom plates and COoC system, respectively (Fig. 3I). Furthermore, COs in COoC and MGCAoC chips both showed significantly decreased terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase–mediated deoxyuridine triphosphate nick end labeling (TUNEL)–positive, apoptotic cells compared with those from static 96-well plates, suggesting higher chondrocyte viability under dynamic culture conditions (Fig. 3, J and K). In addition, spread-plate colony-forming unit (CFU) assays under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions at days 2, 7, and 14 post–probiotic inoculation revealed the presence of bacteria, as indicated by visible bacterial colonies, in the apical, but not basal, channel effluents of the gut module. Using the same assay, no bacteria colonies were detected for the day 14 cartilage module effluent samples under either condition. These results suggest that bacterial carryover across modules was not detectable in the MGCAoC platform (fig. S3).

Selected probiotics promote maturation of BMSC-derived COs with associated changes in Akt and ERK phosphorylation

The underlying mechanisms for the probiotic-promoted maturation of the COs were next investigated (Fig. 4A). After 17 days of exposure to probiotic-colonized gut module effluent, the COs in MGCAoC showed significantly higher expression levels of genes associated with chondrogenesis, i.e., collagen type II (COL2A1), SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9), and aggrecan (ACAN), than COs cultured on COoC and gut-cartilage coculture chips without probiotic inoculation (denoted as GCAoC) (Fig. 4B). No difference in the expression level of these chondrogenic genes between COs from COoC and GCAoC (Fig. 4B), suggesting that effluents from probiotic-colonized gut module, but not from the probiotic-free gut epithelium, promoted chondrogenesis. Immunofluorescence images consistently showed the highest level of chondrogenic marker expression among the three groups (Fig. 4, C and D; and fig. S4, A and B).

Given that COoC- and GCAoC-generated COs showed no difference in the parameters evaluated above, only COs from MGCAoC and GCAoCs were compared hereafter. COs from MGCAoC exhibited lower levels of COL10, COL1, and MMP13 than those from GCAoC, suggesting that the presence of live probiotics in the gut module was associated with reduced chondrocyte hypertrophy and ossification (Fig. 4, C and D; and fig. S4, C and D). In addition, Safranin O and Alcian blue staining showed higher proteoglycan deposition in COs from the MGCAoC compared with those from the GCAoC (fig. S5A). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging revealed denser collagen fibers in the extracellular matrix (ECM) of MGCAoC-produced COs than GCAoC-generated ones (Fig. 4E). In addition, the chondrocytes in MGCAoC-derived COs showed more abundant rough endoplasmic reticulum (ER) (white arrow) and ribosomes (black arrow) compared with chondrocytes in COs from GCAoC (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, the morphology of Golgi apparatus appeared similar in the chondrocytes of the two chips (fig. S5B). Collectively, these findings suggest that exposure to probiotic-seeded gut module effluents promoted the production and secretion of cartilage ECM.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was next performed to elucidate the molecular pathways underlying the chondropromotive effects of probiotics. Transcriptomic analysis revealed a total of 1440 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between COs from MGCAoC and GCAoC (fig. S5C). Compared with GCAoC-generated COs, those from MGCAoC showed significantly up-regulated chondrogenic marker genes (COL2A1, SOX9, and ACAN) and suppressed ossification genes (RUNX2 and COL1A1) and degenerative markers (ADAMTS5 and MMP13) (Fig. 4G). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that biological pathways associated with extracellular region, ER lumen, collagen-containing ECM, and protein binding were enriched (fig. S5D). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis revealed enrichment in pathways including PI3K/Akt and MAPK signaling (Fig. 4H). Genome-wide gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed the enrichment of processes such as collagen trimer and collagen-containing ECM (fig. S5, E and F). Analysis of signaling pathway by Western blotting showed that the ratios of phosphorylated Akt (p-Akt)/Akt and phosphorylated ERK (p-ERK)/ERK were found to be significantly higher in COs from MGCAoC than those from GCAoC (Fig. 4, I and J). Together, these findings suggest that probiotic-colonized gut module effluents enhanced chondrogenic differentiation and matrix formation, potentially through activation of Akt and ERK signaling. In addition, the level of ADAM with thrombospondin motif 4 (ADAMTS4), a cartilage matrix-degrading protein, was lower in COs from MGCAoC than GCAoC, demonstrating the beneficial role of gut module effluent in inhibiting cartilage degradation.

Pretreatment with probiotic-seeded gut module effluent enhances the anti-inflammatory ability of COs with functional involvement of PI3K and MAPK signaling

We then explored whether treatment with probiotic-seeded gut module effluent during chondrogenesis can make COs in the MGCAoC more resistant to subsequent inflammatory challenge. The COs were first cultured on MGCAoC in the presence of probiotics in the gut module till day 14, when the internal circulation channel was disconnected (by closing μV2 shown in Fig. 2A) and the proinflammatory cytokine IL-1β was added to the cartilage maintenance medium (Fig. 5A). Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) data revealed significantly increased release of IL-6, MMP13, and C-terminal cross-linked telopeptide of collagen type II (CTX2) by the COs following IL-1β challenge (Fig. 5B). These markers indicate cartilage inflammation, matrix degradation, and degradation product, respectively, highlighting the catabolic cascade of “inflammation-degradation-destruction.” On the other hand, the concentrations of the above catabolic markers were significantly lower in the MGCAoC group (Fig. 5B). Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) results were in agreement with the ELISA data. Generally, under both healthy and inflammatory conditions, MGCAoC-derived COs exhibited significantly lower mRNA expression levels of IL6, MMP13, and COL10A1, but higher levels of COL2A1, SOX9, and ACAN, than the GCAoC group (Fig. 5C). This trend was further confirmed by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 5, D and E; and fig. S6, A and B). Histological staining with Safranin O and Alcian blue demonstrated that, after inflammatory challenge, COs from the MGCAoC retained more proteoglycan than those from the GCAoC (fig. S6C). Corroborating the above findings, TEM images show reduced ER fragmentation and swelling in MGCAoC-derived COs compared with GCAoC-cultured COs after IL-1β treatment (Fig. 5F and fig. S6D). Furthermore, MGCAoC-derived COs still retained high collagen fiber fidelity and normal Golgi apparatus morphology after inflammatory insult, while GCAoC-cultured COs exhibited sparse distribution of fragmented extracellular collagen fibers, as well as Golgi apparatus swelling and fragmentation (Fig. 5G and fig. S6E).

Transcriptomic analysis was conducted to elucidate the mechanisms for the observed chondroprotective effects of probiotic-seeded gut module effluent treatment. After IL-1β stimulation, 6678 DEGs (4774 up-regulated and 1904 down-regulated) in GCAoC-cultured COs and 6113 DEGs (4251 up-regulated and 1862 down-regulated) in MGCAoC-derived COs were observed (fig. S7, A and B). Both groups exhibited significantly up-regulated inflammatory and degenerative markers such as IL8, IL6, and MMP13 and significantly down-regulated chondrogenic markers such as COL2A1, SOX9, and ACAN (Fig. 6A). For IL-1β–challenged COs, those derived from GCAoC and MGCAoC chips showed 980 DEGs, with MGCAoC-cultured COs expressed significantly lower levels of markers of inflammation, degeneration, hypertrophy, and ossification, including IL6, IL1β, CCL20, MMP1, MMP3, MMP13, and BMP6, and higher levels of chondrogenic genes, such as COL2, SOX9, and ACAN, compared with GCAoC-grown COs (Fig. 6, B and C). GO analysis revealed that biological pathways associated with ECM, collagen-containing ECM, ECM organization, and protein bonding were significantly enriched in MGCAoC-derived, gut module effluent-pretreated COs (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, GSEA revealed the enrichment of processes such as chondrocyte differentiation and protein glycosylation in COs pretreated with gut module effluent in MGCAoC (Fig. 6, E and F). KEGG analysis showed that, for COs with (from MGCAoC) and without (from GCAoC) gut module effluent pretreatment, IL-1β challenge consistently activated MAPK and PI3K signaling pathways, but the enrichment degree differed (Fig. 6G; and fig. S7, C and D). While inflammatory stimulation up-regulated Akt and ERK phosphorylation in COs grown on both MGCAoC and GCAoC chips, the extent of this increase was significantly lower for MGCAoC-derived COs, suggesting that pretreatment with basal channel effluents of probiotic-colonized gut module enabled COs to suppress hyperphosphorylation (Fig. 6, H and I). These findings suggest that probiotic-seeded gut module effluent reduced CO inflammation, an effect associated with inhibited hyperactivation of Akt and ERK signaling.

To further explore the potential involvement of these signaling pathways, we performed pathway-activation experiments under inflammatory conditions and comprehensively evaluated CO responses via ELISA, Western blotting, and immunofluorescence (figs. S8 and S9). Reactivation of either Akt or ERK signaling attenuated the protective effects of the aforementioned effluent pretreatment. Collectively, these results suggest that pretreatment with basal channel effluents of probiotic-colonized gut module rendered COs more resistant to inflammatory insults and was associated with reduced chondrocyte hypertrophy and cartilage catabolism, with modulated PI3K and MAPK signaling potentially contributing to these effects.

Probiotic-seeded gut module effluent treatment following inflammatory stimulation, with or without combined hyaluronic acid supplementation, alleviates CO catabolism

To explore the therapeutic effects of the probiotic combination on inflamed cartilage, BB and LP were introduced to the gut module of GCAoC, which had not received any effluent treatment during organoid maturation, after exposure of the COs to IL-1β for 2 days (Fig. 7A). During the next 3 days of treatment, the secretion levels of IL-6, MMP13, and CTX2 were significantly decreased, while, in the untreated group (no probiotic inoculation), the levels of these markers remained unchanged or only slightly decreased over the same period (Fig. 7B). In addition, compared with the untreated group, COs treated with probiotic-supplemented gut module effluent showed higher expression levels of COL2A1, SOX9, and ACAN and lower levels of IL6, MMP13, and COL10A1 (Fig. 7C). Immunofluorescence staining results showed consistent trends (Fig. 7, D and E; and fig. S10, A and B). Moreover, Safranin O and Alcian blue staining revealed that treatment with the probiotic-supplemented gut module effluent promoted greater recovery of proteoglycan deposition in IL-1β–challenged COs (fig. S10C). TEM images revealed that the diameter and fragmentation level of chondrocyte ER in COs were significantly reduced after probiotic treatment, albeit not fully restored (Fig. 7F and fig. S10D). Furthermore, the collagen fibers in CO ECM treated with probiotics appeared less fragmented (Fig. 7G). No obvious difference in Golgi apparatus morphology was observed between the probiotic-treated and untreated groups (fig. S10E). Together, our results support the therapeutic efficacy of probiotic-supplemented gut module effluent in treating inflammatory and degrading cartilage.

We then used RNA-seq to elucidate the observed therapeutic effects. Compared with the untreated group, the probiotic-supplemented gut module effluent-treated COs exhibited obvious up-regulation of chondrogenic genes and down-regulation of various catabolic markers (Fig. 8A). We then explored potential synergistic effects of probiotic supplementation combined with “intraarticular injection” of hyaluronic acid (HA), widely known as viscosupplementation in clinical management of OA. After CO inflammation induction, clinical-grade HA was added directly to the cartilage culture medium to simulate intraarticular injection, while the probiotics were seeded at the same time onto the intestinal epithelium of the gut module (fig. S11). Immunofluorescence staining results showed that, in comparison with the probiotic treatment-only group, combined probiotic supplementation and HA treatment further reduced the expression of degradation, inflammation, and hypertrophy markers, whereas the expression level of COL2, a major component of cartilage ECM, was further increased (fig. S12). These findings were corroborated by the RNA-seq results, indicating that the combined treatment was more effective than probiotic treatment alone in inhibiting cartilage inflammation and promoting cartilage ECM formation (fig. S13A). The underlying pathways were explored by KEGG analysis and Western blotting, which consistently revealed that the combined therapy more significantly reduced Akt, ERK, and P38 phosphorylation than probiotic treatment alone (fig. S13, B to D).

Collectively, the above results indicate that the introduction of probiotics to the gut module suppressed the excessive phosphorylation of P38, Akt, and ERK, which was accompanied by reduced cartilage ECM degradation and increased COL2 and aggrecan synthesis, consistently supporting a notable therapeutic effect on inflamed COs (Fig. 8H). In addition, combined HA supplementation enhanced the observed therapeutic effects of the probiotics.

Chondroprotective metabolites produced by probiotic colonization of gut epithelium of MGCAoC: Signaling pathways

Next, we sought to identify the specific probiotic metabolites responsible for the observed chondroprotective effects. To reveal changes in the gut module metabolites induced by probiotic inoculation, we performed metabolite profiling of the effluent from the internal circulation channel of MGCAoC (with seeded probiotics) and GCAoC (without seeded probiotics). The effluents were collected with μV2 closed and μV1 open (Fig. 9A). Notable differences in gut metabolites were observed between the two groups in both unsupervised principal components analysis (PCA) analysis and orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) (Fig. 9, B and C). The differential metabolites identified are shown in Fig. 9 (D and E). Among them, 3-indolepropionic acid (IPA), nicotinic acid (NA), butyric acid (BA), cholic acid (CA), and indole-3-lactic acid (ILA) were among the top enriched metabolites for the MGCAoC group and, therefore, selected for subsequent functional validation (Fig. 9E). KEGG analysis revealed tryptophan metabolism and primary bile acid metabolism as key enriched pathways (Fig. 9F). Additionally, our targeted analysis results showed that the levels of IPA and ILA, which are products of tryptophan metabolism, were both significantly increased in the MGCAoC group, whereas levels of metabolites such as 5-hydroxytryptophol and cyclic adenosine 5′-monophosphate were decreased (Fig. 9G and fig. S14A). Moreover, molecular docking analysis identified potential interactions between several up-regulated metabolites and their receptors or key proteins associated with PI3K and MAPK pathways, including IPA with the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), ILA with AhR, CA with takeda G protein–coupled receptor 5 (TGR5), BA with histone deacetylase 2, and NA with hydroxycarboxylic acid receptor 2 (fig. S14B and table S2). The predicted binding modes suggest that these metabolite-target pairs could be accommodated within plausible binding pockets through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. To further examine the docking-predicted interactions, we performed cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) using day 14 COs. Based on the major enriched pathways by KEGG analysis, IPA and CA were selected as representative metabolites for CETSA validation. Compared with vehicle controls, IPA increased the thermal stability of AhR, whereas CA increased the thermal stability of TGR5 (fig. S14, C and D). These results provide proof-of-concept experimental support for the docking-based prediction that selected gut probiotic metabolites may engage molecular targets associated with PI3K and MAPK signaling.

Enriched probiotic metabolites exhibit therapeutic effects on COs derived from patients with OA

To gain further insight and validation toward clinical translation of our findings on beneficial probiotic metabolites for OA treatment, we evaluated selected compounds identified in our metabolomics analysis using COs constructed using chondrocytes derived from patients with OA. The COs were formed from the OA chondrocytes via self-aggregation in the cartilage module of the chip and used to test the therapeutic effects of selected metabolites by directly perfusing the COs with medium containing IPA, ILA, BA, CA, or NA (Fig. 10A). After a 72-hour treatment, the secretion levels of IL-6, MMP13, and CTX-2 were significantly lower compared with the untreated groups (Fig. 10B). In addition, the expression levels of COL2A1, SOX9, and ACAN were up-regulated, whereas those of COL10A1, IL6, and MMP13 were down-regulated in all treatment groups (Fig. 10C and fig. S15A). The radar chart in Fig. 10D shows the ability of the five compounds to regulate the mRNA levels of the markers shown in Fig. 10C, with their relative efficacy indicated by a normalized score of one to five. ILA promoted chondrogenesis effectively, whereas the others demonstrated notable capabilities to inhibit cartilage catabolism. Immunofluorescence staining images showed that all five compounds rescued the inflamed COs derived from patients with OA to varying degrees (Fig. 10E). For example, ILA most significantly inhibited the inflammatory marker IL-6, whereas IPA showed the highest efficacy in inhibiting chondrocyte hypertrophy. In addition, their abilities to rescue cartilage ECM also varied, with IPA demonstrating the most notable overall efficacy (Fig. 10, E and F; and fig. S15, B and C).

DISCUSSION

Motivated by the lack of effective in vitro platforms to study the MGCA, this study constructed an MGCAoC platform and found that the metabolites of the probiotic combination of BB and LP colonizing the intestinal epithelium could promote BMSC chondrogenesis, enhance the anti-inflammatory ability of COs, and, to a certain extent, rescue inflamed COs. Multiomics profiling together with pathway-validation experiments supports the interpretation that these effects are mediated through MAPK/ERK and PI3K/Akt signaling. Furthermore, by analyzing the composition of probiotic metabolites in the internal circulation channel of MGCAoC, we identified a series of potential compounds that collectively contributed to the above beneficial effects on articular cartilage under healthy and inflammatory conditions. Last, we verified the therapeutic effects of the identified compounds with COs established with articular chondrocytes derived from patients with OA. The engineered MGCAoC system offers a physiologically relevant platform for deciphering the molecular mechanisms underlying gut-joint cross-talk and holds significant promise for the evaluation of potential therapeutics targeting the MGCA in OA treatment.

Mounting evidence supports the critical role of gut microbiota and its metabolites in regulating the homeostasis of articular cartilage, via what has been proposed as the gut-cartilage axis. Recent animal studies found that gut microbiota dysbiosis affects joint metabolism and promotes OA development after joint injury (20, 21). Additionally, administration of Bifidobacterium (22, 23), Lactobacillus (24), and their combinations consistently down-regulated inflammatory biomarkers and histological OA severity in rats. However, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, and the findings from animal models warrant further validation in human tissue-based models. In this context, BB and LP were selected as representative probiotic strains in this study not only because of their reported beneficial roles in gut-mediated joint homeostasis but also for their contrasting oxygen tolerances. This choice allowed us to evaluate the MGCAoC’s capability to support microbiota-host interactions under distinct microbial culture conditions, thereby serving as a proof of concept for future applications involving more diverse bacterial strains or communities.

To model the MGCA in vitro, our multiorgan MGCAoC system was designed to allow spatially organized, continuous communication of engineered gut and cartilage tissues over long culture durations while maintaining their individual phenotypes. Previous studies have shown that bilayer microfluidic chips not only can support the spontaneous 3D morphogenesis of intestinal epithelial cells in vitro but also can enable the coculture of aerobic intestinal epithelial cells and anaerobic microorganisms (25, 26). The curved microchannel in the gut module can induce physiologically relevant nonlinear flow and in vivo–like biomechanics, triggering multiaxis cellular deformation (27). The generation of phenotypically stable COs is critical to successful disease modeling and compound testing. Spheroid culture is well established for BMSC chondrogenesis–based CO engineering for therapeutic testing and cartilage repair. However, due to the lack of vascularization, mass transfer within large COs is limited, leading to compromised cell metabolism as well as intra- and inter-CO heterogeneity. The microwell array in our MGCAoC provides geometrical confinement and enables high throughput in situ production of uniform COs. The flowing medium generates shear stress on the COs, mimicking synovial fluid-induced shear experienced by articular cartilage, and facilitates the delivery of soluble factors (e.g., chondrogenic factors) and efficient waste removal (28). Compared with discontinuous conditioned-medium transfer, this platform enables dynamic, continuous metabolite transport between organ modules under defined conditions, offering a clear advantage for mechanistically dissecting gut-cartilage cross-talk. Notably, we were able to continuously track the on-chip evolution of BMSC aggregates to fully mature COs at the single-organoid level.

Our mechanistic study showed the involvement of the PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK signaling pathways in the observed chondropromoting and chondroprotective functions of the probiotic combination. The effects of Akt and ERK signaling on cartilage are context dependent. On the one hand, the PI3K/Akt pathway promotes the chondrogenic differentiation of BMSCs, enhances ECM deposition, and inhibits cell apoptosis during the early stage of cartilage formation (29). The MAPK/ERK pathway works in synergy with growth factors such as transforming growth factor–β (TGF-β) to up-regulate the expression of chondrogenic markers such as SOX9 and COL2A1, thereby maintaining cartilage homeostasis (30, 31). On the other hand, in an inflammatory environment, such as under IL-1β stimulation, both PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK pathways are overactivated. PI3K/Akt signaling promotes the expression of enzymes such as MMP13 and ADAMTS to accelerate cartilage ECM degradation (32). The MAPK family comprises several signaling cascades, most notably P38 and ERK pathways, and drives proinflammatory cytokine production and cartilage breakdown (33).

The introduction of probiotics to the gut module appeared to contribute to the maintenance of CO homeostasis. During CO development, increased Akt and ERK phosphorylation was associated with enhanced ECM synthesis (Fig. 4, H and I). In the presence of IL-1β, the excessive activation of Akt and ERK pathways associated with cartilage inflammation and catabolism was reduced in COs affected by probiotic-seeded gut module effluent (Fig. 6, G and H). For COs experiencing IL-1β–induced degradation, subsequent probiotic colonization on intestinal epithelium was associated with maintenance of moderate Akt and ERK activities, along with reduced cell apoptosis (Fig. 8, C and D). Consistent with these observations, pathway-activation experiments further supported that appropriate modulation of these signaling pathways contributes to the protective effects of probiotic-colonized gut module effluent (figs. S8 and S9). Besides, the expression of MMP13 and ADAMTS4 was down-regulated with reduced cartilage destruction (Fig. 8, F and H). Ultimately, the inflammatory cascade was attenuated in COs, resulting in higher CO integrity than the untreated group (GCAoC). These findings suggest that the probiotic colonization may modulate homeostatic process across different microenvironments, balancing the anabolic and anti-inflammatory responses of COs.

To explore the translational relevance of our MGCAoC-based findings, we performed metabolomics analysis to identify probiotic-associated compounds that potentially promoted cartilage anabolism and protection. Among the enriched metabolites, ILA and IPA were found to show free radical-scavenging activity, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, and immune regulation function (34, 35). The levels of ILA and IPA in the intestine were reported to be negatively correlated with OA (36–38). Under inflammatory conditions, these metabolites were found to down-regulate catabolic markers such as TNF-α, IL-6, MMP3, MMP13, and ADAMTS4, and up-regulate COL2 and ACAN to protect the cartilage matrix (Fig. 10) (39–42). Furthermore, IPA can relieve the inhibition of SOX9 (43, 44). NA has been shown to reduce the secretion of inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-6, and MCP1 (45, 46). In addition, NA was found to enhance energy metabolism, chondrocyte proliferation, and matrix synthesis (47, 48). CA and its derivative were reported to promote chondrocyte anabolism (48–50), reduce MMP/ADAMTS-mediated matrix degradation, and suppress chondrocyte senescence and inflammation in an inflammatory environment (51). As a SCFA, BA regulates epigenetic remodeling, up-regulates anti-inflammatory and chondrogenic genes, and down-regulates inflammatory genes to promote cellular homeostasis and ECM synthesis (52, 53). In addition, we also identified some significantly reduced compounds in the metabolites in MGCAoC effluents (Fig. 9C). Among them, homogentisic acid has been proven to cause abnormal cartilage morphology and mechanics, as well as the degradation of ECM (54, 55). Collectively, these molecules likely participate in a coordinated regulatory network spanning metabolism, signaling, and potentially epigenetic regulation. Our data suggest that, by modulating intermediates within the PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK pathways and by providing metabolic substrates that support anabolic processes, these metabolites can synergistically favor ECM formation and attenuate inflammation (Fig. 10 and fig. S15). Further studies will aim to define the precise contribution of each metabolite and to determine whether epigenetic reprogramming is directly involved.

We acknowledge several limitations in the present study while emphasizing its value as a proof-of-concept platform for gut-joint cross-talk. The current MGCAoC is intentionally designed as a simplified and versatile human cell-derived model that physically separates the two compartments to enable controlled mechanistic interrogation of the gut-joint axis. However, as a reductionist model, it does not yet include additional tissues components, such as the immune, hepatic, or endocrine elements, that may modulate gut microbiome regulation in vivo. Similarly, we selected two representative probiotic strains, BB and LP, as representative probiotic strains with differential oxygen requirements to establish and biologically validate the MGCAoC platform, rather than as the only or optimal candidates for cartilage protection. We also focused on a limited set of their metabolites to confirm platform functionality, which does not fully capture the strain diversity, temporal dynamics, or interindividual variability of the human gut microbiome. Furthermore, while the protective effects of the metabolites were verified using COs derived from patients with OA, the donor pool was limited and included only female donors. Thus, patient heterogeneity and sex differences should be addressed in future studies. Lastly, we recognize that variability arising from both biological and device-level factors in multitissue chips will require pilot variance estimates to inform power calculations. Future studies will adopt this framework to ensure robust experimental design. Looking ahead, we plan to integrate more tissue types, a broader microbial community, and diverse donor sources to further enhance the platform’s physiological relevance and translatability.

In summary, the MGCAoC platform offers a valuable tool for dissecting the mechanisms underlying gut-joint cross-talk mediated by microbiota-derived factors. This versatile platform facilitates the screening of specific probiotic strains and identification of potentially beneficial metabolites. It may also serve as a useful preclinical system for evaluating factors relevant to probiotic development for OA, including strain-specific effects, the complexity of multistrain formulations, dosing considerations, safety assessment, and interindividual variation in gut microbiota. The MGCAoC platform is poised to enable the development of standardized treatment protocols and streamline preclinical prediction of probiotic efficacy. To further enhance the clinical relevance of the MGCAoC model, future work should focus on integrating immune components and vascularization into the model to more effectively recapitulate systemic circulation of probiotic metabolites. Additionally, it is highly valuable to leverage the MGCAoC platform for personalized screening of probiotic combinations tailored to individual microbiome profiles and joint conditions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Platform design and fabrication

The microscale system was composed of two layers of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS; Sylgard 184, Dow Corning) polymerized in casts at a 10:1 weight ratio of base to curing agent at 60°C for at least 6 hours and a PDMS porous membrane. The upper layer had an intestinal epithelial channel (1000 μm wide, 10 mm long, and 600 μm high), whereas the lower layer was composed of an internal circulation channel (1000 μm wide, 10 mm long, and 300 μm high) at the same location of the intestinal epithelial channel but with opposite inlet and outlet directions. The lower layer also has a spherical array (3000 μm in diameter and 700 μm high) and the channel (1000 μm wide and 300 μm high) connecting the internal circulation channels and the arrays, as well as two unidirectional microvalves. The intestinal epithelial channel and internal circulation channel were separated by a porous membrane (1200 μm wide, 3000 mm long, and 30 μm high). Master molds of upper and lower layers were 3D printed with a Formlabs Form4B printer using bioink (BioMed Clear). The PDMS stamps were peeled off the molds and assembled layer by layer. Before assembling, the top layer was treated with biopsy punchers: Cell in/out ports had a 1-mm diameter. The lower chip layer was first bonded to one side of the porous membrane after air plasma treatment and then further bonded with the upper chip layer to achieve irreversible bonding after an additional 30 min of incubation at 80°C.

Computer simulations

The oxygen concentrations inside the intestinal and internal circulation channels were evaluated by modeling the theoretical gas transfer capabilities in COMSOL Multiphysics 5.4 (COMSOL Inc., Stockholm, Sweden). Laminar fluid flow and transport of diluted species in the channels were simulated by the software to create two-dimensional models of oxygen transport by controlling the Navier-Stokes equations. Fluid motion within the chip was described by assuming an incompressible fluid and no-slip boundary conditions for all channel walls and interfaces; convection of the fluid was also considered. Steady-state oxygen molecule transport was modeled with Fick’s second law of diffusion, with certain simulation parameters (table S3). The temperature (37°C), air pressure (1 atm), and oxygen diffusion coefficients through the culture medium/PDMS/membrane/ECM were set as constants. The fluid flow through the microfluidic chips was simulated by solving the Navier-Stokes equations for mass and momentum continuity (Eqs. 1 and 2)∇v→=0(1)∇⋅(ρ v→ v→)=−∇p+μ ∇2v→+ρ g→(2)where v→ is the velocity vector, p is the static pressure, ρ is the fluid density, μ is the dynamic viscosity, and g→ is the gravity vector.

Cells and organoids

Cells

The epithelial cell line, Caco-2, which is widely used as a model of the intestinal epithelial layer, was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Caco-2 cells were cultured in flasks in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Invitrogen) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% streptomycin/penicillin (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37°C. BMSCs were cultured in minimum essential medium α (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). The cells were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2, and the culture medium was changed every 2 to 3 days. Only cells in the exponential growth phase were used for the experiments.

BMSC-derived organoids

For CO cultures, BMSCs were resuspended at 1 × 106 cells/ml in BMSC culture medium, injected into the cartilage arrays to form cell aggregates with μV2 closed, and kept in static culture for 24 hours. Next, chondrogenic differentiation medium was introduced via the inlet and was composed of high-glucose DMEM (Gibco), 1% insulin-transferrin-selenium-ethanolamine (Gibco), TGF-β3 (5 ng/ml; PeproTech), ascorbic acid (50 μg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich), and 0.1 μM dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich).

COs formed from chondrocytes derived from patients with OA

OA chondrocytes were isolated from the smooth-looking parts of the tibial plateau and femoral condyles obtained by intraoperative osteotomy in patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty surgeries, under an IRB-approved protocol (ethical approval no. 2025-ER-149). The cartilage tissues were minced and digested overnight [high-glucose DMEM (Gibco), 10% fetal FBS (Gibco), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 0.15% collagenase 2 (Worthington Biochemical Corporation), 0.01% hyaluronidase (Merck), and 37°C, 5% CO2]. The cell suspension was strained using a 70-μm cell strainer (Corning, 352350), centrifuged, and resuspended to 1 × 106 cells/ml in DMEM/F12 medium. Primary chondrocytes isolated from three OA donors were pooled and injected into the cartilage module of the MGCAoCs to form organoids.

Bacterial strains culture

For proof-of-concept evaluation of the chip model, two representative probiotic strains with distinct oxygen requirements, BB and LP, were selected. BB was used as an oxygen-sensitive obligate anaerobe, whereas LP was used as an oxygen-tolerant strain. This combination enabled us to assess the platform’s capacity to support microbiota-host interaction studies involving bacteria with differential oxygen demands. BB (ATCC, 11863) was cultured in BBL liquid medium (HB8777, Qingdao Hope BioTechnology Co. Ltd., China) supplemented with 0.05% (w/v) cysteine at 37°C for 24 to 48 hours under anaerobic conditions generated using anaerobic jars (Anaero Pack Kenki, Mitsubishi Gas Chemical, Japan) and anaerobic gas-generating bags (Mitsubishi Gas Chemical, Japan). LP (ATCC, 8014) was cultured in the Lactobacillus broth medium (HB8637-1, Qingdao Hope BioTechnology Co. Ltd., China) supplemented with 0.05% (w/v) cysteine at 37°C under aerobic conditions.

On-chip culture

Healthy GCAoC culture

The intestinal epithelial channel was coated with collagen matrix composed of rat-tail collagen I (final concentration, 4.5 mg/ml; Corning) and Matrigel (1%, Corning) in a 1:1 ratio at 37°C for 2 hours. After sucking out the excess collagen matrix solution, the Caco-2 cell suspension (8 × 106 cells/ml) was seeded on the precoated membrane without injection for 4 hours. Then, perfusion culture was initiated using microfluidic pumps at a fluid speed of 45 μl/hour. Both the upper intestinal epithelial channel and lower internal circulation channels were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 5 min, and the fresh medium without antibiotics was added to both microchannels 24 hours before seeding of the bacteria. The bacterial suspensions were then exposed to the apical side at a multiplicity of infection (ratio of bacterial number to epithelial cell number) of 1 at 37°C with 5% CO2. Dynamic culture was resumed 2 hours after microbial inoculation. The upper channel was injected with anaerobic and antibody-free medium (deoxygenated in an anaerobic tank for 24 hours at 4°C), whereas the lower channel was injected with antibody-free medium.

Before constructing the gut-cartilage axis, the two valves were adjusted to prevent the fluid in the internal circulation channel from flowing into the cartilage part.

Inflammation induction

IL-1β (10 ng/ml) was used to induce cartilage inflammation on-chip. Specifically, for studies assessing the protective effect of gut microbes on cartilage, IL-1β (20 ng/ml) was initially added to the chondrogenic differentiation medium. Following a 1:1 mixture of this medium with the gut module bottom channel effluent, the final IL-1β concentration reaching the COs was 10 ng/ml. For studies evaluating the therapeutic effects of gut microbes on inflamed COs, the intestinal and the cartilage modules were separated by μVs. In this setup, the COs were first stimulated with IL-1β (10 ng/ml), after which the μVs were opened to allow perfusion of gut microbial metabolites to the COs.

Evaluation of intestinal epithelial barrier integrity

The monolayer integrity was assessed by quantification of two complementary functions: transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) and apparent paracellular permeability (Papp, cm/s). The TEER value was calculated with Eq. 3TEER (Ω·cm2)=(Ωt−Ωblank)×A=πr2(3)where Ωt is the resistance at each time point, Ωblank is the resistance of a cell-free blank chip, and A is the surface area (square centimeters) of the microchannel lined by Caco-2 cells. TEER was measured with Ag/AgCl electrode wires [0.01 inches (0.0254 cm) in diameter] connected to a voltage-ohm meter (Fluke Corporation, Everett, WA, USA).

Papp values of the intestinal cell monolayer were determined by the apical-tobasolateral transport of fluorescein isothiocyanate-labeled dextran 4000 (FD-4; 4 kDa; Sigma-Aldrich) over time. FD-4 (10 μg/ml) was introduced to the epithelium channel (at 60 ml/hour), and the fluorescence intensities (490 nm/525 nm) of the top and bottom channel effluents were measured using a multimode plate reader (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices). The apical-to-basolateral flux of the paracellular marker was calculated using Eq. 4Papp=(dQ/dt)/AdC(4)where dQ/dt (g/s) is molecular flux, A (square centimeters) is the total area of diffusion and dC (milligrams per milliliter) is the average gradient. For each measurement, the area under the curve was calculated using the GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software Inc.).

Assessment of potential bacterial transfer across the epithelial barrier

To directly evaluate potential bacterial carryover across the epithelial barrier, spread-plate CFU assays were performed for MGCAoC effluents, which were collected separately from the apical and basal channels of the gut module, at days 2, 7, and 14 after bacterial inoculation. Because the bacterial inoculum contained both aerobic and anaerobic species, each plated sample was cultured under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions. Plates were incubated for 48 hours before CFU enumeration. Additionally, effluents from the cartilage module were collected after 14 days of MGCAoC culture and similarly plated and incubated under both conditions for CFU assessment.

TUNEL assay

The one-step TUNEL apoptosis assay kit (C1090, Beyotime) was used to evaluate cell apoptosis in cryosections of COs. Images were collected with a confocal microscope (Leica SP8, Leica Microsystems). The percentage of TUNEL+ cells to 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI)+ cells was analyzed using ImageJ 1.54 [National Institutes of Health (NIH), USA].

RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and then reverse transcribed using Hifair II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yeasen) and amplified using primers targeting the marker genes (see table S4), obtained from the Primer Library (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene/) and synthesized by BGI Genomics. Transcript levels were normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase and quantified via the ΔΔCt method. Untreated cells served as controls.

Immunofluorescence staining

For gut module, intestinal epithelium on the porous membrane was washed with PBS, fixed with 10% formalin, permeabilized in Triton X-100, and blocked with bovine serum albumin. For cartilage module, intact COs were fixed, permeabilized, and blocked as whole-mount samples without sectioning. The blocked samples were incubated with primary antibodies and the corresponding secondary antibodies (table S5). Cell nuclei were costained with DAPI (Invitrogen, D1306). Microscopy was performed with a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica SP8, Leica Microsystems). For COs, confocal Z-stack images were acquired from the exposed hemispheric surface of the organoids. Fluorescence signals were quantified using ImageJ 1.54 (NIH, USA).

Histological evaluation of proteoglycan deposition

For histological evaluation of proteoglycan deposition, COs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 7 μm. Sections were stained with Safranin O (S8884, Sigma-Aldrich) and Alcian blue (pH 1.0; C0153M, Beyotime) according to standard protocols. Stained sections were imaged under a bright-field microscope to evaluate proteoglycan distribution and matrix deposition within the COs.

Transmission electron microscopy

For TEM observation of cartilage sections, the COs were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 48 hours. Then, they were rinsed in PBS, stained in 1% OsO4 for 1 hour, dehydrated and embedded in a Spurr-based resin. Sections were made at a thickness of 80 nm with a Leica EM UC7 ultratome and were examined with a Talos F200C microscope at 200 kV.

Western blotting

The COs were lysed posttreatment in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the proteins were separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and electrotransferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Bio-Rad). The membranes were probed with primary antibodies (sources listed in table S6), followed by horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated secondary antibodies. Visualization was achieved using a chemiluminescence-based protocol (SuperSignal West Femto, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ 1.54 (NIH).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

At the scheduled time points, the supernatant from the outlets of the chips was collected and stored at −80°C. Cytokines were detected by commercial kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions: IL-6 (SEKH-0014, Solarbio), MMP13 (SEKH-0259, Solarbio), and CTX-II (D711707, Sangon). Briefly, the supernatant samples were incubated in precoated micro 96-well plates for 90 min followed by incubation with biotin-conjugated antibody for 60 min. Subsequently, HRP-conjugated streptavidin was added to each well for 30 min. After reacting with the substrate reagent for about 15 min, the reaction was terminated with the stop solution. The absorbance of each well was detected at 450 nm in a microplate reader (SpectraMax i3X, Molecular platforms).

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from the samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Purity and concentration were verified using a NanoDrop ND-1000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and integrity was assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies). Polyadenylated mRNA was enriched with poly-dT magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific), fragmented at 94°C for 5 min, and reverse transcribed into cDNA with SuperScript IV Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Second-strand synthesis was performed using ribonuclease H and DNA polymerase I (New England Biolabs). Libraries were constructed via end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation. This was followed by UDG-mediated digestion of uracil-containing strands. PCR amplification (8 cycles of 98°C denaturation, 60°C annealing, and 72°C extension) generated fragments of 300 ± 50 base pairs. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Lianchuang Biotechnology, China).

Metabolomic analysis

The culture media derived from the lower microchannel in the gut module were collected for targeted metabolomic analysis. Briefly, the culture media were centrifuged at 12,000g for 15 min, and the supernatant was filtered using 0.22-μm filter and analyzed by Biotree, China, using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (Agilent 1290 infinity II, Agilent; 6500+ AB Sciex) and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-2030, Shimadzu; QP2020 NX, Shimadzu). Metabolite quantification was performed using calibration curves together with internal-standard normalization. Metabolite abundance data were normalized on the basis of internal standards before statistical analysis. Unsupervised PCA and supervised OPLS-DA were performed to assess the overall separation of metabolic profiles between groups. Variable-importance-in-projection (VIP) values were derived from the OPLS-DA model, whereas fold changes and P values were calculated by univariate analysis. Differential metabolites were defined as those meeting the criteria of VIP > 1, fold change > 1.5, and P < 0.05.

Based on the targeted metabolomic profiling of gut module effluents, IPA, ILA, NA, BA, and CA, among others, were identified as enriched metabolites in the MGCAoC group relative to those in controls. These metabolites were selected for subsequent functional tests because they were among the most prominently enriched compounds and have also been reported previously to exhibit anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, immunomodulatory, and/or cartilage-protective effects (39–53). The concentrations used in the subsequent in vitro tests (IPA, 0.55 μM; ILA, 1.75 μM; NA, 5.5 μM; BA, 0.45 mM; and CA, 0.4 μM) were determined with reference to the targeted metabolomic results.

Molecular docking analysis

Molecular docking analysis was conducted using molecules (IPA, ILA, CA, NA, and BA) and proteins (Protein Data Bank IDs: 5nj8, 4tuu, 7cfm, 8ija, and 8j24). Molecular docking software AutoDock Vina 1.2.3 was used. This program continuously conducts Local Search and repeated iteration through Iterated Local Search algorithm to find the best molecular docking conformation and select the scheme with the lowest binding energy as the final scheme. Specifically, first of all, using the AutoDockTools 1.5.7, the compounds and proteins were pretreated, and the appropriate docking sites were selected, and the configuration files for docking with the AutoDock Vina software were exported. Then, the AutoDock Vina software was continuously used for local search and repeated iteration to find the best molecular docking conformation. Last, PyMOL 2.3.0 was used for result analysis and plotting operations.

Cellular thermal shift assay

CETSA was performed in day 14 COs to examine representative metabolite-target interactions predicted by molecular docking. Following 14 days of on-chip culture, the COs were treated for an additional 24 hours with culture medium supplemented with IPA (Sigma-Aldrich, 0.55 μM), CA (Sigma-Aldrich, 0.4 μM), or an equivalent volume of DMSO. After treatment, COs were collected from the chip and lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors, and the lysates were centrifuged at 12,000g for 15 min at 4°C to collect the supernatants. Subsequently, the supernatants were heated at 37°C, 42°C, 47°C, 52°C, 57°C, or 62°C for 5 min. After heat treatment, the samples were cooled down and centrifuged to obtain soluble protein fractions, which were then analyzed by Western blotting with anti-AhR or anti-TGR5 antibodies. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ and normalized to the corresponding 37°C sample.

Drug testing

The selected compounds—BA (0.45 mM, Sigma-Aldrich), ILA (1.75 μM, MCE), NA (5.5 μM, Sigma-Aldrich), 3-indolepropionic-acid (0.55 μM, Sigma-Aldrich), and CA (0.4 μM, Sigma-Aldrich)—were diluted respectively to the final working concentrations respectively. After the patient-derived chondrocytes formed organoids on the chip (24 hours), the five compounds were added individually to the culture medium, respectively, and treated for 72 hours.

Ethical approval statement of participants

Human cartilage tissues were obtained from three patients with knee OA who underwent total knee arthroplasty at The Third Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University. The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of The Third Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University (ethical approval no. 2025-ER-149). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable regulations.

Statistical analysis

Each chip was independently fabricated, seeded, cultured, and considered one biological replicate for statistical analysis. Thus, for chip-derived data, n refers to the number of independent chips analyzed for each condition.

Data are presented as means ± SD. Statistical analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism 10. Comparisons were performed via unpaired t tests, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), or two-way ANOVA, as specified in the figure legends. Significance was set at P < 0.05. All experiments included ≥3 biological replicates.
