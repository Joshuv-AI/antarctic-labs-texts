Proton Radiotherapy Alone versus Combined with Immunotherapies or Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors for Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer Stage B or C Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Rodney Cheng-En Hsieh, Willy Po-Yuan Chen, Jin-Chiao Lee, Chia-Hsin Lin, Yu-Chen Chang, Chao-Wei Lee, Kun-Ming Chan, Hao-Chien Hung, Tony Kuo, Shen-Yen Lin, Tse-Ching Chen, Ting-Jung Wu, Chung-Wei Su, Hong-Shiue Chou, Chih-Hsien Cheng, Yi-Chung Hsieh, Chen-Yang Huang, Khac Chien Tran, James Welsh, Jason Chia-Hsun Hsieh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to compare the outcomes of proton radiotherapy alone versus its combination with immuno-oncology agents (Proton-IO) or tyrosine kinase inhibitors (Proton-TKI) in patients with intermediate- to advanced-stage hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed the medical records of 137 patients with Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage B or C HCC treated with proton radiotherapy at Linkou Chang Gung Memorial Hospital between 2020 and 2023. Patients were stratified into three groups: proton radiotherapy alone (n = 64), Proton-IO (n = 44), and Proton-TKI (n = 29). The most frequently used immuno-oncology agents were atezolizumab-bevacizumab (n = 33) and pembrolizumab (n = 5). Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) included lenvatinib (n = 16) and sorafenib (n = 13).ResultsWith a median follow-up of 30 months, patients in the Proton-IO group were significantly associated with higher 2-year overall survival (OS) rates compared with those receiving Proton-TKI or proton radiotherapy alone (77.0% vs. 47.2% vs. 52.7%; p = 0.002). Proton-IO was also associated with significantly longer time to progression (TTP) and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS) (2-year TTP: 50.5% vs. 28.1% vs. 24.2%, p = 0.003; 2-year DMFS: 83.4% vs. 61.1% vs. 67.2%, p = 0.027). No significant differences in 2-year local control rates were observed among the treatment groups (97.7% vs. 92.9% vs. 86.8%; p = 0.230). Multivariate analysis identified Proton-IO as an independent predictor of improved OS (p < 0.001), TTP (p < 0.001), and DMFS (p = 0.004). Grade 3–4 upper gastrointestinal (UGI) bleeding was observed in 2 (1.5%) patients (proton monotherapy, n = 1; Proton-IO, n = 1). There were no significant differences among the groups in the incidence of grade ≥3 UGI bleeding, liver toxicity, colitis, rib fractures, or hematologic adverse events.ConclusionIn BCLC stage B/C HCC, proton radiotherapy combined with immunotherapy was significantly associated with higher OS, TTP, and DMFS without an increase in grade ≥3 toxicity compared with proton radiotherapy alone or Proton-TKIs.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the most common primary malignancy of the liver, represents a major global health burden due to its aggressive nature and poor prognosis [1]. Owing to its often silent progression, a substantial proportion of patients present with intermediate to advanced disease, at which point curative surgical interventions are no longer viable, leaving transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) or systemic therapy as the primary treatment options [2]. Recent advancements in systemic therapies – particularly tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and immuno-oncology (IO) agents – have transformed the therapeutic landscape of HCC [3]. However, these therapies are limited by modest objective response rates (5%–30%), with median overall survival (OS) ranging from 6.5 to 19.2 months and progression-free survival (PFS) of 2.8 to 7.4 months [2–5]. These limitations have prompted increasing interest in combination strategies that could enhance therapeutic efficacy. In this context, high-precision radiotherapy has emerged as a promising modality capable of improving local tumor control and potentially acting synergistically with systemic agents to augment clinical outcomes [6–9].

Recent randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that modern radiotherapy techniques achieve excellent local control (LC) rates of 80–95% in patients with HCC, offering efficacy comparable to radiofrequency ablation [10, 11]. Despite the ablative effectiveness of high-dose irradiation, the risk of distant metastasis or recurrence outside the irradiated field remains substantial, particularly in patients with high tumor burden [12–14]. Radiotherapy not only induces lethal DNA damage in tumor cells but also triggers immunogenic cell death, enhancing tumor antigen release and facilitating uptake and cross-presentation by antigen-presenting cells [15, 16]. Nonetheless, preclinical studies have shown that HCC cells can exploit DNA repair pathways to upregulate PD-L1 expression, enabling them to evade phagocytic clearance and suppress adaptive immune responses [17–19]. Combining radiotherapy with PD-L1/PD-1 blockade can overcome this immune resistance by enhancing the phagocytosis of dying cancer cells and activating cytotoxic T cells [17, 20]. This approach may transform the irradiated tumor into an in situ vaccine, promoting a systemic antitumor immune response in preclinical models [17]. However, whether this combination can translate into improved clinical outcomes in HCC patients remains to be determined.

Proton radiotherapy provides a highly conformal treatment modality, enabling precise dose delivery to tumor tissue while sparing adjacent normal structures such as the uninvolved liver, upper gastrointestinal (UGI) tract, and spleen [21, 22]. This dosimetric advantage is due to the Bragg peak phenomenon, allowing rapid dose fall-off beyond the target volume. Such precision is particularly beneficial in high-burden HCC, where underlying cirrhosis and portal hypertension increase the risk of treatment-related toxicity [23]. Compared to conventional photon-based techniques, proton therapy is associated with reduced rates of radiation-induced liver disease (RILD), UGI bleeding, and lymphopenia [24, 25]. These tissue-sparing properties may enhance the therapeutic synergy of systemic agents while minimizing overlapping toxicities [26, 27]. However, clinical data on the efficacy and safety of combining proton radiotherapy with TKIs (Proton-TKIs) or IO (Proton-IO) agents in intermediate to advanced HCC remain limited. This study aimed to evaluate treatment outcomes in patients with Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage B or C HCC treated with definitive proton radiotherapy, either alone or in combination with TKIs or IO therapies.

Materials and Methods

Patients

We retrospectively reviewed medical records of 178 consecutive patients with HCC classified as BCLC stage B or C, who received proton radiotherapy at Linkou Chang Gung Memorial Hospital between 2020 and 2023. Patients were excluded if they had disseminated distant metastases (n = 19) or diffuse liver tumors (n = 10) that could not be encompassed by proton radiotherapy, concurrent invasive malignancies (n = 2), no posttreatment follow-up data (n = 8), or a prior history of liver radiotherapy or Y-90 radioembolization (n = 2). After applying these criteria, 137 patients were included for analysis (Fig. 1).

The study design flowchart.

The diagnosis was established based on histopathological findings or imaging criteria outlined in international guidelines [28]. Staging work-up was conducted following our institutional liver cancer protocols, as previously described [29]. Tumor staging was classified using the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition and the BCLC staging system [2].

Treatments

All patients underwent four-dimensional computed tomography simulation. When tolerable, abdominal compression was used to reduce respiratory motion. For patients whose tumors exhibited motion greater than 10 mm, respiratory gating at the end-expiration phase was implemented using the Anzai system (Anzai Medical Co., Tokyo, Japan) [30]. Gadoxetate disodium-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging simulation was performed in patients without a known contrast allergy. All detectable HCC lesions were treated with proton radiotherapy using either passive scattering or pencil beam scanning techniques (Sumitomo Heavy Industries, Tokyo, Japan), guided by an On-Board Imager kV X-ray system. Dose calculations were based on a relative biological effectiveness of 1.1, expressed in cobalt gray equivalents (CGEs) [29]. Two fractionation regimens were used: 72.6 CGE in 22 fractions for tumors situated within 1 cm of critical structures such as the hepatic hilum, bowel, or heart, and 66 CGE in 10 fractions for tumors positioned more than 1 cm away from these organs [24]. The clinical target volume was defined by expanding the gross tumor volume by 0–5 mm and included a 5-mm margin around the internal target volume for robust treatment planning and evaluation [12, 29].

The most commonly used TKIs were lenvatinib and sorafenib, whereas the IO agents included atezolizumab plus bevacizumab (Atezo-Bev), pembrolizumab, nivolumab, and nivolumab combined with lenvatinib. In clinical practice, systemic agents were more frequently prescribed for patients presenting with tumor vascular thrombosis, multiple intrahepatic lesions, or nodal/distant metastases. Throughout the study period, various TKIs and IO agents were gradually approved and reimbursed by Taiwan’s National Health Insurance, with reimbursement criteria undergoing multiple policy revisions. In general, systemic therapy was eligible for reimbursement in patients with extrahepatic metastasis, Vp2–Vp4 major vascular invasion, or persistent viable tumors after three sessions of TACE within 1 year. Both TKIs and IO agents were administered concurrently with proton radiotherapy and continued thereafter until disease progression or the occurrence of intolerable toxicity. According to the reimbursement regulations, IO agents are covered for a maximum treatment duration of 2 years in patients achieving partial or complete response, whereas patients with stable disease are eligible for up to 6 months of therapy. For patients not meeting reimbursement criteria, TKIs and IO agents could be obtained through private insurance coverage or out-of-pocket payment. In the absence of standardized treatment guidelines for combining TKIs or IO agents with proton therapy, final treatment decisions were made based on physician judgment, reimbursement eligibility, and patient preference. All patients received comprehensive counseling regarding the potential benefits and risks before treatment initiation.

Outcome Assessment

Posttreatment evaluation involved clinical examination, liver function testing, and baseline abdominal imaging with computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, or both. Radiological assessments were conducted every 3–4 months during the first 2 years after proton radiotherapy, and every 4–6 months thereafter. Chest imaging was performed at baseline and repeated every 6 months, or earlier if there were clinical indications. HCC progression was assessed using the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 guidelines [31, 32]. Local failure was defined as either progressive disease or the emergence of new HCC lesions within or at the edge of the treatment field (clinical target volume plus a 1-cm margin).

Acute toxicities occurring within 4 months following radiotherapy were documented. RILD was recorded in patients who did not exhibit intrahepatic tumor progression during this period. Non-classic RILD was identified by either a ≥2-point increase in the Child-Pugh score or grade ≥3 liver toxicity according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE, version 5.0). Classic RILD was characterized by the presence of nonmalignant ascites and an alkaline phosphatase level exceedingly twice the upper limit of normal [29, 33–36]. UGI bleeding was defined as bleeding originating from the lower esophagus, stomach, or duodenum. Grade 3 UGI bleeding involved events requiring blood transfusion, invasive intervention, or hospitalization, while grade 4 referred to life-threatening bleeding requiring urgent intervention. Grade 2 colitis was characterized by endoscopic evidence of inflammation accompanied by symptoms such as abdominal pain, or the presence of mucus or blood in the stool. Leukopenia was categorized as grade 1 (white blood cell count 3,999–3,000/mm3), grade 2 (2,999–2,000/mm3), and grade 3 (1,999–1,000/mm3). Neutropenia was defined by absolute neutrophil count nadirs of 2,000–1,500/mm3 (grade 1), 1,499–1,000/mm3 (grade 2), and 999–500/mm3 (grade 3). Lymphopenia was classified as grade 1 (1,000–800/mm3), grade 2 (799–500/mm3), grade 3 (499–200/mm3), and grade 4 (<200/mm3).

Statistical Analysis

Pearson’s chi-squared test and Student’s t test were employed to assess differences between groups for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. OS, time to progression (TTP), distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS), and LC were measured from the initiation of radiotherapy to the occurrence of the corresponding event or the date of last follow-up if censored. Event definitions included death for OS, disease progression for TTP, distant metastasis for DMFS, and local failure for LC. Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed using the Cox proportional hazards model, with results reported as hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals. A stepwise backward conditional approach was applied in the multivariate model to minimize the risk of overfitting while allowing clinically relevant covariates to be retained based on their statistical contribution to the model [37, 38]. The model incorporated the following prognostic variables: treatment group, age, sex, performance status, hepatitis virus infection, albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) grade, AJCC 8th edition T stage, presence of nodal or distant metastasis, BCLC stage, tumor size, tumor number, tumor vascular thrombosis, and prior treatments. Given the collinearity between Child-Pugh class and ALBI grade, and the superior prognostic performance of the ALBI grade over the Child-Pugh system reported in recent studies [39, 40], only ALBI grade was included in the multivariate model. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the clinical and treatment characteristics of patients who received proton radiotherapy alone (Proton) compared to those treated in combination with TKIs or IO agents. Sixty-four patients received proton radiotherapy as monotherapy. Additional 29 patients were treated with a combination of proton radiotherapy and TKIs, referred to as the Proton-TKI group. Of these, 16 patients (55%) received lenvatinib at a daily dose of either 12 mg or 8 mg, while 13 patients (45%) were administered sorafenib at doses ranging from 200 to 400 mg once or twice daily. A total of 44 patients received Proton-IO. The immunotherapy regimens included atezolizumab (1,200 mg) plus bevacizumab (15 mg/kg) in 33 patients (75%), pembrolizumab (200 mg) in 5 patients (11%), nivolumab (3 mg/kg) in 4 patients (9%), and a combination of nivolumab and lenvatinib in 2 patients (5%). All patients received IO therapy concurrently with proton radiotherapy, and 38 patients (86%) continued IO treatment following completion of radiotherapy.

Patients in the Proton group demonstrated a trend toward earlier T-stage disease (p = 0.054), absence of nodal metastasis (p = 0.078), and lack of tumor vascular thrombosis (p = 0.091), compared to the Proton-TKI and Proton-IO groups. No significant differences were observed among the treatment groups with respect to age, sex, performance status, hepatitis viral infection, Child-Pugh score, ALBI score, tumor size, presence of distant metastasis, number of tumors, BCLC stage, or treatment-naïve status.

All patients received proton radiotherapy using either the 72.6 CGE in 22-fraction or 66 CGE in 10-fraction regimen, determined by tumor location. Six patients (4.4%) did not complete the planned treatment course. There was no significant difference in treatment discontinuation rates among the groups. Detailed information on the delivered dose and fractionation is provided in online supplementary Table 1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000550067). A total of 114 patients (83%) were treated using pencil beam scanning, while 23 patients (17%) received passive scattering. There was no significant difference in the distribution of proton radiotherapy techniques among the treatment groups.

Survival and Tumor Control

The median follow-up duration among surviving patients was 30 months (range, 2–61 months). Patients in the Proton-IO group were significantly associated with longer survival compared with those treated with Proton-TKI or Proton (2-year OS: 77.0% vs. 47.2% vs. 52.7%, respectively; p = 0.002; Fig. 2; Table 2). Importantly, significantly higher TTP and DMFS rates were observed in the Proton-IO group relative to the Proton-TKI and Proton groups (2-year TTP: 50.5% vs. 28.1% vs. 24.2%, respectively; p = 0.003; 2-year DMFS: 83.4% vs. 61.1% vs. 67.2%, respectively; p = 0.027). LC rates were high across all groups, with no significant differences in 2-year LC outcomes observed among the treatment arms (Proton-IO vs. Proton-TKI vs. Proton: 97.7% vs. 92.9% vs. 86.8%; p = 0.230).

Univariate and Multivariate Analyses

Univariate analysis (Table 3) showed that treatment with Proton-IO was significantly associated with improved OS (p = 0.001), TTP (p = 0.001), and DMFS (p = 0.019). In addition, tumor size greater than 5 cm (p = 0.023) and performance status of 2 (p = 0.033) were linked to poorer survival outcomes. Advanced T stage (AJCC 8th edition T3–4) was significantly associated with inferior TTP compared to T1–2 (p = 0.017), and BCLC stage C predicted lower DMFS compared to stage B (p = 0.025). Sex (p = 0.004) and performance score (p = 0.003) were significantly associated with LC (online suppl. Table 3).

Multivariate analysis confirmed that Proton-IO was an independent predictor of superior OS (p < 0.001), TTP (p < 0.001), and DMFS (p = 0.004; Table 3). Tumor size >5 cm remained an independent predictor of poorer OS (p = 0.037). Additionally, BCLC stage C was significantly associated with worse OS (p = 0.047) and DMFS (p = 0.023), while AJCC 8th edition T3–4 stage independently correlated with inferior TTP (p = 0.001). Sex (p = 0.005) and performance status (p = 0.007) showed significant associations with LC (online suppl. Table 3).

Treatment-Related Adverse Events

No significant differences were observed among treatment groups in the incidence of a ≥2-point increase in Child-Pugh score or CTCAE grade ≥3 liver toxicity (Table 4). Additionally, no cases of nonmalignant ascites accompanied by a greater than two-fold elevation in alkaline phosphatase – the defining features of classic RILD – were observed. Notably, only 2 patients (1.5%) experienced UGI bleeding following radiotherapy, with no significant differences across groups. One event occurred in the proton monotherapy group, where duodenal ulcer bleeding developed 4 months after treatment in close proximity to the irradiated field. The second case was in a Proton-IO patient with marked baseline splenomegaly (377 mL) and Vp4 portal vein thrombosis, who developed esophageal variceal bleeding 6 months after radiotherapy while continuing Atezo-Bev. The bleeding site was outside the irradiated field, suggesting portal hypertension-related variceal rupture rather than radiation-induced injury. One case of grade 2 colitis occurred in the Proton-TKI group and resolved spontaneously. There were also no significant differences in the incidence of leukopenia, neutropenia, or lymphopenia among the treatment groups. Furthermore, no rib fractures were reported in the entire cohort.

Discussion

For patients with intermediate- to advanced-stage HCC, curative treatment options remain scarce – particularly among those with tumor vascular thrombosis, extensive intrahepatic disease, or extrahepatic metastases [4]. In this subset, systemic therapy alone has historically constituted the mainstay of management [2–4], albeit with limited efficacy and poor long-term outcomes [41–46]. The present study demonstrates that the integration of proton radiotherapy with immunotherapies yields significantly enhanced oncologic outcomes in patients with high-burden disease, achieving an encouraging 2-year OS rate of 77%. These findings position this combined modality approach as a promising, potentially curative therapeutic strategy in a patient population traditionally considered beyond the reach of definitive interventions.

Our previous study demonstrated that cancer cells can exploit ATR-mediated DNA repair signaling pathways to upregulate PD-L1 expression following photon or proton radiotherapy, thereby evading immune surveillance and phagocytic clearance [17]. The combination of radiotherapy with PD-L1/PD-1 blockade enhances the phagocytosis of dying tumor cells, facilitating antigen uptake and cross-presentation by antigen-presenting cells, which in turn stimulates a more robust systemic antitumor T-cell response [17]. Preclinical models have shown that this combinatorial approach promotes the infiltration of stem-like CD8+ T cells and restores effector function and cytolytic activity [17, 20, 47]. However, it remains unclear whether these proinflammatory and antitumor immune effects translate into meaningful clinical responses in patients with HCC. In this study, we observed that proton radiotherapy delivered with contemporary techniques, when combined with PD-L1/PD-1 blockade, was significantly associated with a lower risk of distant metastasis and longer TTP compared with Proton or in combination with TKIs. Importantly, these oncological benefits were achieved without a corresponding increase in treatment-related toxicity, reinforcing the association between the Proton-IO strategy and a survival benefit. These favorable findings align with preclinical evidence, providing further support for the therapeutic synergy of combining radiotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibition in HCC.

The improved therapeutic outcomes observed with proton radiotherapy combined with immunotherapy are further corroborated by recent studies involving X-ray-based radiotherapy. Chiang et al. [48] retrospectively compared 25 patients with BCLC stage A–C HCC treated with stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) and anti-PD-1 immunotherapy to 50 matched patients treated with SBRT alone. Their findings demonstrated that the SBRT-IO combination was associated with significantly improved 3-year OS (63.9% vs. 43.3%) and TTP (61.3% vs. 32.5%) compared to SBRT alone. Similarly, Ning et al. [49] analyzed 29 matched pairs of BCLC stage C HCC patients and found that adding X-ray radiotherapy to immunotherapy plus antiangiogenic therapy resulted in improved median OS (not reached vs. 9.7 months) and PFS (8.3 vs. 4.2 months) after a median follow-up of 15.5 months. These findings highlight the synergistic potential of radiotherapy and immunotherapy. In our study, which included a larger cohort and a longer median follow-up of 30 months, the 3-year OS and TTP rates in the Proton group (42.4% and 24.2%) were consistent with published clinical trial data for BCLC stage B and C patients [23, 50]. Remarkably, the addition of IO agents significantly improved 3-year OS and TTP to 71.5% and 50.5%, respectively, underscoring the synergistic antitumor benefit of this combination.

The efficacy of combining radiotherapy with TKIs remains an area of active investigation. The RTOG 1112 phase III randomized controlled trial compared outcomes in patients with locally advanced HCC treated with SBRT followed by sorafenib versus sorafenib alone, reporting a trend toward improved median OS in the SBRT-sorafenib group (15.8 vs. 12.3 months) [51]. Similarly, in a large retrospective cohort of 453 BCLC stage C HCC patients treated with either sorafenib or lenvatinib, the addition of X-ray-based radiotherapy was associated with longer OS (8.6 vs. 4.4 months) and PFS (4.5 vs. 2.3 months) compared to TKI monotherapy [52]. These findings suggest that integrating radiotherapy with TKIs may enhance survival outcomes relative to systemic therapy alone. However, whether radiotherapy combined with TKIs offers greater benefit than radiotherapy alone or in combination with immunotherapy remains to be determined. Intriguingly, a recent study by Wang et al. [53] involving 214 BCLC stage B and C patients demonstrated that triple combination therapy with SBRT, lenvatinib, and PD-1 inhibitors significantly improved median OS (31.2 vs. 17.4 months) and PFS (15.6 vs. 8.8 months) compared to SBRT combined with lenvatinib alone. These findings suggest that combining radiotherapy with immunotherapy may offer greater therapeutic benefit than radiotherapy combined with TKIs. Consistent with these observations, our study demonstrated that Proton-IO agents resulted in significantly superior OS, TTP, and DMFS compared to the Proton-TKI group. While the addition of TKIs to proton therapy led to a modest, nonsignificant improvement in 1-year TTP (44.9% vs. 34.7%), this benefit diminished over longer follow-up. Moreover, no significant difference in DMFS was observed between the Proton-TKI and proton monotherapy groups. These findings suggest that the addition of IO agents to proton radiotherapy may elicit a more robust systemic antitumor (abscopal) response, whereas combining proton therapy with TKIs does not appear to confer a similar synergistic effect.

Historically, patients with extrahepatic metastatic disease have been excluded from curative-intent locoregional therapies. However, advancements in systemic treatments have shifted this paradigm. Recent randomized trials suggest that combining radiotherapy with systemic therapy can improve survival in patients with oligometastatic disease [54–57], a condition characterized by a limited number of metastatic lesions. In a prospective phase II study by Choi et al. [58], SBRT to all detectable metastatic sites in 40 patients with 1–4 lesions resulted in a favorable 2-year OS rate of 80% and a median PFS of 5.3 months, highlighting SBRT as a viable and effective approach for oligometastatic HCC. Similarly, Leng et al. [39] evaluated 154 HCC patients with oligoprogression after first-line systemic therapy and found that those who received radiotherapy to progressing lesions while continuing first-line systemic treatment experienced significantly longer median PFS compared to patients receiving second-line systemic therapy, with or without radiotherapy (8.6 vs. 5.8 vs. 3.1 months). These findings support the role of radiotherapy in prolonging disease control in the setting of oligoprogression of metastatic HCC. With the advent of pencil beam scanning [59], proton therapy now enables precise delivery to multifocal liver tumors and oligometastatic sites while sparing adjacent normal tissues. In our study, 10 patients with extrahepatic metastasis received proton therapy to all metastatic lesions, which was associated with favorable outcomes. Notably, in both univariate and multivariate analyses, the presence of extrahepatic disease did not correlate with poorer oncologic outcomes. These results highlight the potential utility of combining proton radiotherapy with systemic therapy in the management of oligometastatic HCC. The safety profile of proton radiotherapy in combination with systemic therapies is of major clinical relevance. In our cohort, no significant differences were observed among treatment groups in the incidence of objectively verifiable toxicities across treatment groups, including grade ≥3 UGI bleeding, RILD, grade 2 colitis, rib fractures, or hematologic adverse events. Notably, 75% of patients in the Proton-IO group received Atezo-Bev. Given that bevacizumab has been associated with an elevated risk of gastrointestinal bleeding [60], concerns have been raised regarding potential synergistic toxicity when combined with radiotherapy. Supporting this concern, a recent study from MD Anderson Cancer Center reported a 14.3% incidence of UGI bleeding among patients receiving X-ray-based radiotherapy combined with Atezo-Bev [61]. By contrast, our previous dosimetric analysis demonstrated that proton therapy markedly reduces radiation dose to the UGI tract, thereby mitigating bleeding risk [24, 62]. Consistent with this, the present study did not observe an increased incidence of UGI bleeding or other adverse events in the Proton-IO group, underscoring the dosimetric advantages of proton therapy in safely facilitating systemic therapy combinations. However, 1 patient with pronounced baseline splenomegaly and Vp4 portal vein thrombosis – known risk factors for portal hypertension-related hemorrhage [62, 63] – developed esophageal variceal bleeding 6 months after proton radiotherapy while continuing Atezo-Bev. Importantly, the bleeding site was outside the irradiated field, suggesting an etiology related to portal hypertension rather than radiation-induced mucosal injury. These observations emphasize that while proton-based radio-immunotherapy appears safe in terms of radiation-related toxicity, patients with portal hypertension features warrant heightened surveillance for UGI bleeding during prolonged bevacizumab exposure.

This study is a single-institution retrospective analysis and, as such, is subject to several inherent limitations. While proton radiotherapy is not universally established as a standard of care for BCLC stage B/C disease, multiple prospective trials have shown that Proton provides excellent local tumor control in this setting, with reported 3-year LC rates of approximately 90% [23, 50, 64]. Recent investigations have also demonstrated that proton beam monotherapy yields survival outcomes comparable to those of TACE in this population [65–67]. Based on this evidence, many patients with localized HCC were treated with Proton during the study period. In the absence of standardized guidelines for combining TKIs or IO agents with radiotherapy, the use of systemic therapy was determined by physician judgment, reimbursement eligibility, and patient preference rather than protocolized randomization, introducing the possibility of selection bias. Although not reaching statistical significance, there was a discernible trend toward more frequent use of systemic agents in patients presenting with tumor vascular thrombosis, multifocal intrahepatic disease, or nodal metastasis, reflecting a tendency to reserve combination therapy for patients with higher disease burden. Additionally, given the heterogeneity of systemic agents and the predominance of Atezo-Bev in the IO cohort, the outcomes mainly reflect proton radiotherapy combined with Atezo-Bev rather than IO agents as a whole. Furthermore, due to the retrospective nature of the study, we may have been unable to reliably capture certain treatment-related adverse events, particularly subjective symptoms such as fatigue and diarrhea. Nevertheless, the assessment of key objective toxicities – including liver toxicity, UGI bleeding, colitis, rib fracture, and hematologic toxicity – focused on clinically relevant outcomes and encompassed the most concerning toxicities associated with radiotherapy. Moreover, the Proton-TKI subgroup consisted of only 29 patients, which limits the statistical power to detect between-group differences and increases the risk of type II error. Accordingly, findings related to this subgroup should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. Finally, proton radiotherapy is not reimbursed by Taiwan’s National Health Insurance, meaning all patients included in this study likely had higher socioeconomic status. We acknowledge that the generalizability of our results should be interpreted within the context of the specific cohort evaluated.

Despite these limitations, the markedly prolonged survival observed in the Proton-IO group is encouraging, particularly in light of the limited therapeutic options available for this population. These findings provide a compelling rationale for prospective clinical investigation. Building upon promising preclinical and retrospective evidence, we have launched three phase II clinical trials to assess the safety and efficacy of combining radiotherapy with Atezo-Bev: the ProtonAB trial (NCT06133062), evaluating proton radiotherapy plus Atezo-Bev; the PhotonAB trial (NCT06339424), assessing photon radiotherapy plus Atezo-Bev; and the RT alone trial (NCT06379256), serving as a control arm. All three studies share comparable eligibility criteria and aim to define the clinical utility of radio-immunotherapy combinations in HCC. Larger scale prospective studies will be essential to validate and expand upon these findings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that proton radiotherapy combined with immunotherapy agents is significantly associated with better survival, longer TTP, and a reduced incidence of distant metastasis in patients with BCLC stage B or C HCC, compared to Proton or Proton-TKIs. Importantly, no significant differences were observed across treatment groups in the incidence of objectively documented toxicities, including grade ≥3 UGI bleeding, RILD, grade 2 colitis, rib fractures, or hematologic adverse events. Given the limited therapeutic options for high-burden HCC, these findings highlight the potential clinical value of integrating immunotherapy with proton radiotherapy. The observed survival benefit and favorable safety profile provide a strong rationale for prospective clinical trials to further validate this approach and define its role in the treatment landscape of advanced HCC.
