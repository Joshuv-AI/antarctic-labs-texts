From Fragile to Certified: Wasserstein Audits of Group Fairness Under Distribution Shift
Ahmad-Reza Ehyaei, Golnoosh Farnadi, Samira Samadi

Abstract
Group-fairness metrics (e.g., equalized odds) can vary sharply across resamples and are especially brittle under distribution shift, undermining reliable audits. We propose a Wasserstein distributionally robust framework that certifies worst-case group fairness over a ball of plausible test distributions centered at the empirical law. Our formulation unifies common group fairness notions via a generic conditional-probability functional and defines ε \varepsilon -Wasserstein Distributional Fairness ( ε \varepsilon -WDF) as the audit target. Leveraging strong duality, we derive tractable reformulations and an efficient estimator (DRUNE) for ε \varepsilon -WDF. We prove feasibility and consistency and establish finite-sample certification guarantees for auditing fairness, along with quantitative bounds under smoothness and margin conditions. Across standard benchmarks and classifiers, ε \varepsilon -WDF delivers stable fairness assessments under distribution shift, providing a principled basis for auditing and certifying group fairness beyond observational data.

Introduction

Group–fairness metrics such as statistical parity and equalized odds are widely used to assess algorithmic equity, yet they are highly sensitive to small perturbations in the training data
Besse et al. 2018
;
Barrainkua et al. 2023
;
Cooper et al. 2024
(Fig.
1
). Even mild changes in dataset composition or train–test splits can cause large swings in measured fairness
Friedler et al. 2019
;
Du & Wu 2021
, eroding trust in reported guarantees
Ji et al. 2020
. Because distributions drift in practice, fairness measured on a single empirical sample is unreliable.

To obtain trustworthy assessments, distributionally robust optimization (DRO) evaluates
worst-case fairness
over a set of plausible distributions (e.g., a Wasserstein ball), rather than only the observed data. This guards against distribution shift and promotes models whose fairness and accuracy remain stable when test data diverge from the training set
Rahimian & Mehrotra 2022
;
Lin et al. 2022
;
Montesuma et al. 2025
.

When designing an ambiguity set for DRO, two choices are paramount: (i) the

f

\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}

W_{q}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}):=\inf_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}):\ [\pi]_{1}=\mathbb{P},\ [\pi]_{2}=\mathbb{Q}}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{(z,z^{\prime})\sim\pi}\big[c(z,z^{\prime})^{q}\big]\Big)^{1/q},

where

\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}

\delta

To handle distributional uncertainty in empirical fairness evaluation

\varepsilon

\varepsilon
-Wasserstein Distributional Fairness

3

\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P})}\big|\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{Q},\theta)\big|\;\leq\;\varepsilon,

(1)

This certifies that the worst-case fairness disparity within a geometrically plausible neighborhood of
\mathbb{P}

\varepsilon

Enforcing Eq.
1

Moreover, standard DRO analyses typically assume Lipschitz or smooth objectives, whereas common group-fairness metrics are indicator-based and discontinuous, so off-the-shelf bounds do not apply. A further difficulty is observability: we cannot access the population ball
\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P})
and only have its empirical proxy
\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}^{N})

\mathbb{P}^{*}

\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}^{N})

In the out-of-sample problem, we only observe the empirical law
\mathbb{P}^{N}

\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}^{N})}\big|\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{Q},\theta)\big|

The central question is how to calibrate

under trusted labels and sensitive attributes. For this class, we characterize the DRO worst-case, obtain an explicit regularizer with an efficient algorithm, and upper and lower bounds. In the out-of-sample case, we establish finite-sample certification. Our main contributions are:

Background and Foundations

Data Model.

Let
\mathbf{Z}=(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y})
be a random vector on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{Y})
with joint distribution
\mathbb{P}
. We assume feature space
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, binary labels
\mathcal{Y}\in\{0,1\}
and discrete sensitive attribute
\mathcal{A}\in\{1,\dots,k\}
. The classifier
h_{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is deterministic, trained without using
\mathbf{A}
, and has parameter
\theta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{K}
.

Fairness Notions.
Many group‐fairness metrics (e.g., equalized odds) are defined as the difference between a classifier’s conditional expectations over specific, disjoint subsets of
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}
. Formally, let
\left\{S_{0}^{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
and
\left\{S_{1}^{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
be disjoint subsets of
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}
with positive measure, indexed by a finite set of
\mathcal{I}
of size
m
. A classifier
h_{\theta}
satisfies the
\varepsilon
-fairness if it meets all
m
constraints:

\left|\mathbb{P}\left(h_{\theta}(\mathbf{X})\mid S_{0}^{i}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(h_{\theta}(\mathbf{X})\mid S_{1}^{i}\right)\right|\leq\varepsilon\ \ \text{or}\ \ \left|\underset{z\sim\mathbb{P}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[h_{\theta}(x)\left(\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{S_{0}^{i}}(a,y)}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\mathbbm{1}_{S_{0}^{i}}]}-\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{S_{1}^{i}}(a,y)}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\mathbbm{1}_{S_{1}^{i}}]}\right)\right]\right|\leq\varepsilon,\ \forall i\in\mathcal{I},

where
\mathbbm{1}_{S}
denotes the indicator of set
S
, and
\varepsilon
is a tolerance for deviations from perfect fairness.
To compactly encode
m
fairness constraints, introduce the random vector
\mathbf{U}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y})\in\{0,1\}^{2m}
with:

\mathbf{U}(a,y)=(\mathbbm{1}_{S_{0}^{1}}(a,y),...,\mathbbm{1}_{S_{0}^{m}}(a,y),\mathbbm{1}_{S_{1}^{1}}(a,y),...,\mathbbm{1}_{S_{1}^{m}}(a,y))

We can then view the fairness constraints in terms of the value
h_{\theta}(\mathbf{X})
, the vector
\mathbf{U}
, and
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{U}]
. Specifically, define a function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{2m}\times\mathbb{R}^{2m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
by:

\varphi_{i}(U,\mu)\;=\;\frac{U_{i}}{\mu_{i}}\;-\;\frac{U_{i+m}}{\mu_{i+m}},\quad\text{where}\mu=E[U],\quad\forall i\in[m].

(2)

Then all constraints collapse into the generic notion of group fairness
\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{P},\theta)

Si et al. 2021
;
Kim et al. 2022
:

\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{P},\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\bigl[h_{\theta}(\mathbf{X})\varphi(\mathbf{U},\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\mathbf{U}])\bigr].

(3)

So
h_{\theta}
is
\varepsilon
-fair if it meets all
m
constraints,
\left\|\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{P},\theta)\right\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon

where
\left\|x\right\|_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\,\bigl|x_{i}\bigr|
.

Example 1

(
Equalized Odds
)
.

Let us consider the sensitive attribute is binary (e.g., gender). A classifier satisfies equalized odds if its true positive and false positive rates agree across
\mathbf{A}\in\{0,1\}
:

\displaystyle\bigl|\mathbb{P}(h_{\theta}(\mathbf{X})=1\mid\mathbf{Y}=1,\mathbf{A}=0)-\mathbb{P}(h_{\theta}(\mathbf{X})=1\mid\mathbf{Y}=1,\mathbf{A}=1)\bigr|\leq\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\bigl|\mathbb{P}(h_{\theta}(\mathbf{X})=1\mid\mathbf{Y}=0,\mathbf{A}=0)-\mathbb{P}(h_{\theta}(\mathbf{X})=1\mid\mathbf{Y}=0,\mathbf{A}=1)\bigr|\leq\varepsilon.

Define
S^{1}_{a}=\{z:\mathbf{Y}=0,\mathbf{A}=a\}
and
S^{2}_{a}=\{z:\mathbf{Y}=1,\mathbf{A}=a\}
for
a\in\{0,1\}
. Then

\bigl|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\bigl[h_{\theta}(\mathbf{X})\,(\tfrac{\mathbbm{1}_{S^{1}_{0}}(a,y)}{\mathbb{P}(S^{1}_{0})}-\tfrac{\mathbbm{1}_{S^{1}_{1}}(a,y)}{\mathbb{P}(S^{1}_{1})})\bigr]\bigr|\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\bigl|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\bigl[h_{\theta}(\mathbf{X})\,(\tfrac{\mathbbm{1}_{S^{2}_{0}}(a,y)}{\mathbb{P}(S^{2}_{0})}-\tfrac{\mathbbm{1}_{S^{2}_{1}}(a,y)}{\mathbb{P}(S^{2}_{1})})\bigr]\bigr|\leq\varepsilon.

Let
\mathbf{U}(a,y)=(\mathbbm{1}_{S^{1}_{0}}(a,y),\mathbbm{1}_{S^{2}_{0}}(a,y),\mathbbm{1}_{S^{1}_{1}}(a,y),\mathbbm{1}_{S^{2}_{1}}(a,y))
. By Eq.
2
,

\varphi(\mathbf{U},\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\mathbf{U}])=\Bigl(\tfrac{\mathbbm{1}_{S^{1}_{0}}(a,y)}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\mathbbm{1}_{S^{1}_{0}}]}-\tfrac{\mathbbm{1}_{S^{1}_{1}}(a,y)}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\mathbbm{1}_{S^{1}_{1}}]},\tfrac{\mathbbm{1}_{S^{2}_{0}}(a,y)}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\mathbbm{1}_{S^{2}_{0}}]}-\tfrac{\mathbbm{1}_{S^{2}_{1}}(a,y)}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\mathbbm{1}_{S^{2}_{1}}]}\Bigr).

Hence equalized odds is

\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\,h_{\theta}(\mathbf{X})\,\varphi(\mathbf{U},\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\mathbf{U}])\,]\right\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon
(for another example, see Example
2
).

Strong Duality Theorem.

The DRO framework is particularly powerful when we can efficiently characterize the worst-case scenario. Given a function
\psi:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
, its worst-case expectation over an ambiguity set is defined as
\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P})}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathbb{Q}}[\psi(x)]
, where this quantity depends on the ambiguity radius
\delta
and the reference probability distribution
\mathbb{P}
.
A central tool for evaluating worst-case is the
strong duality Theorem

Gao et al. 2017
;
Mohajerin Esfahani & Kuhn 2018b
;
Blanchet & Murthy 2019
. This theorem transforms the original hard optimization problem into a tractable, finite-dimensional one. Specifically, for any
q\in[1,\infty]
, it states:

\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P})}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathbb{Q}}[\psi(z)]=\begin{cases}\inf_{\lambda\geq 0}\left\{\lambda\delta^{q}+\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathbb{P}}[\psi_{\lambda}(z)]\right\}&1\leq q<\infty,\\
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[\sup_{z^{\prime}:c(z,z^{\prime})\leq\delta}\psi(z^{\prime})\right]&q=\infty,\end{cases}

(4)

where
\psi_{\lambda}(z):=\sup_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\{\psi(z^{\prime})-\lambda c^{q}(z,z^{\prime})\right\}
.

Remark 1

(
Robust Optimization
)
.

When we take
q=\infty
, the Wasserstein ball
\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P})
enforces that every outcome
z
can be perturbed by at most a distance
\delta
. Consequently, the DRO objective

\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\psi(z)]

collapses to the classic robust-optimization form

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[\sup_{c(z,z^{\prime})\leq\delta}\psi(z^{\prime})\Bigr].

Distributionally Robust Unfairness Quantification

In fairness-aware classifier learning, the training procedure is modified to promote equitable predictions with respect to protected attributes by incorporating fairness constraints into the optimization objective. The resulting training task is formulated as the following constrained optimization problem:

\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\quad\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{N}}\left[\ell(h_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}),\mathbf{Y})\right]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{N}}\left[h_{\theta}(\mathbf{X})\varphi(\mathbf{U},\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{N}}[\mathbf{U}])\right]\right\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon

(5)

Here,
\ell:\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
is the loss function measuring prediction error. However, traditional fairness-aware learning assumes the training distribution perfectly represents the test environment, which is often violated due to sampling bias, covariate shift, or adversarial perturbations.
To address this issue, a distributionally robust fair optimization problem is formulated as:

\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\bigg\{\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}^{N})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\ell(h_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}),\mathbf{Y})\right]\bigg\}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}^{N})}\bigg\{\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[h_{\theta}(\mathbf{X})\varphi(\mathbf{U},\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\mathbf{U}])\right]\right\rVert_{\infty}\bigg\}\leq\varepsilon.

(6)

This formulation guarantees that the model
h_{\theta}
minimizes the worst-case fairness violation over all plausible distributions, thereby certifying fairness under shifts within a Wasserstein ball around
\mathbb{P}^{N}
.

(
\varepsilon

)

A classifier

\varepsilon\text{-WDF}

\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}^{N})}\bigg\{\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[h_{\theta}(\mathbf{X})\varphi(\mathbf{U},\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\mathbf{U}])\right]\|_{\infty}\bigg\}\leq\varepsilon.

(7)

(i)

Here
q^{\ast}

(i) is standard and covers many classifier families, including linear/GLM, SVM, kernel, and neural networks with continuous activations.

In Section

The worst-case behavior depends on the distance between

7
Discussion

We introduced
\varepsilon\text{-WDF}
, which certifies worst-case group fairness over a Wasserstein ball centered at the empirical distribution
\mathbb{P}^{N}
. When a classifier satisfies the
\varepsilon\text{-WDF}
constraint on
\mathbb{P}^{N}
, our theory shows that certificate transfers to the true distribution
\mathbb{P}
up to a small radius inflation
\delta\mapsto\delta+\alpha/\sqrt{N}
(Thm. 4; Prop. 5), and the worst-case bound dominates the non-robust fairness measured at
\mathbb{P}
.

Our goal was not to design a new fair-learning algorithm, but to quantify a robust fairness constraint that can be plugged into existing pipelines. In practice, our DRUNE estimator (Alg. 1) computes the
\varepsilon\text{-WDF}
regularizer efficiently and can be used for audits or as a constraint during training.

Although our theoretical framework is presented for binary classifiers, it is flexible and can be extended to multi-class settings. While some research addresses the challenge of non-continuity in fairness notions using relaxation techniques such as softmax, we avoid these approaches because they alter the original definition of fairness.
Finally, the theoretical estimation in Section
5
suggests that improving the finite-sample rate is possible, which we leave as a direction for future work.

A.1
Generic Notion of Fairness

The general group fairness formulation in Eq.
3
encompasses a wide range of fairness metrics by appropriately specifying the sets
{S_{0}^{i},S_{1}^{i}}
and the corresponding transformation
\varphi(\cdot,\cdot)
. To illustrate the flexibility and generality of this framework, we present two concrete examples—demographic parity and equalized odds—and show how each can be expressed as a special case of Eq.
3
with suitable choices of sets and mappings.

Example 2

(
Demographic Parity
)
.

A classifier satisfies demographic parity if its positive prediction rate is equal across all sensitive groups
\mathbf{A}\in\{1,\dots,k\}
:

\bigl|\mathbb{P}(h_{\theta}(\mathbf{X})=1\mid\mathbf{A}=a)-\mathbb{P}(h_{\theta}(\mathbf{X})=1\mid\mathbf{A}=b)\bigr|\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{for all }a,b\in\{1,\dots,k\}.

Define

S_{a}=\{\,z\in\mathcal{Z}:\mathbf{A}=a\},\quad a=1,\dots,k.

Then, each pairwise constraint can be written as

\left\|\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathbb{P}}\Bigl[h_{\theta}(x)\,\Bigl(\tfrac{\mathbbm{1}_{S_{a}}(a,y)}{\mathbb{P}(S_{a})}-\tfrac{\mathbbm{1}_{S_{b}}(a,y)}{\mathbb{P}(S_{b})}\Bigr)\Bigr]\right\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon,\quad a,b\in\{1,\dots,k\}.

Let

\mathbf{U}(a,y)=(\mathbbm{1}_{S_{1}}(a,y),\mathbbm{1}_{S_{2}}(a,y),\dots,\mathbbm{1}_{S_{k}}(a,y))\in\mathbb{R}^{k}.

By Eq.
2
, choose the
k(k-1)/2
-dimensional vector

\varphi(\mathbf{U},\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\mathbf{U}])=\Bigl(\tfrac{\mathbbm{1}_{S_{i}}(a,y)}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\mathbbm{1}_{S_{i}}]}-\tfrac{\mathbbm{1}_{S_{j}}(a,y)}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\mathbbm{1}_{S_{j}}]}\Bigr)_{i,j\in[k]:\ i<j}.

Hence, demographic parity is equivalent

\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\bigl[h_{\theta}(\mathbf{X})\,\varphi(\mathbf{U},\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\mathbf{U}])\bigr]\right\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon.

Proof of Proposition
3
.

By assumption
(iv)
the cost function
c
is defined as:

c\big((x,a,y),\ (x^{\prime},a^{\prime},y^{\prime})\big)=d(x,x^{\prime})+\infty\cdot\mathbb{I}(a\neq a^{\prime})+\infty\cdot\mathbb{I}(y\neq y^{\prime}),

The cost function imposes a constraint that if the actions
a
and
a^{\prime}
are not equal or
y
and
y^{\prime}
are not, the cost becomes infinite. This implies that in the Wasserstein distance computation between distributions
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{P}
, the marginal distributions over actions
\mathbf{A}
and labels
\mathbf{Y}
must match exactly, i.e.,
\mathbb{Q}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y}}=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y}}
.

Let
\mathbb{P}
be a nominal probability distribution and consider the Wasserstein ambiguity set:

\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P})=\left\{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y})\mid W_{q}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\leq\delta\right\}.

By the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality
Villani et al. 2009
, Theorem 1.14
, the
q
-Wasserstein distance between two probability distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
is given by:

W_{q}^{q}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})=\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1}\left(\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}f(x,a,y)\ \mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}-\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}f(x,a,y)\ \mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}\right),

where
f
is a
1
-Lipschitz function respect to the cost function
d^{q}
.

Now, applying this dual form of the Wasserstein distance to the distributions
\mathbb{Q}_{a,y}
and
\mathbb{P}_{a,y}
, we have:

\displaystyle\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1}\left(\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}f(x,a,y)\ \mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}-\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}f(x,a,y)\ \mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}\right)=

\displaystyle\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1}\bigg(\int_{\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x,a,y)\ \mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{a,y}(x)\ \mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y}}(a,y)-\int_{\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x,a,y)\ \mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{a,y}(x)\ \mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y}}(a,y)\bigg)=

\displaystyle\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1}\bigg(\sum_{(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y}}(a,y)\bigg(\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x,a,y)\ \mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{a,y}(x)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x,a,y)\ \mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{a,y}(x)\bigg)\bigg)=

\displaystyle\sum_{(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y}}(a,y)\bigg(\sup_{\|f_{a,y}\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1}\bigg(\int_{\mathcal{X}}f_{a,y}(x)\ \mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{a,y}(x)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}f_{a,y}(x)\ \mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{a,y}(x)\bigg)\bigg)=

\displaystyle\sum_{(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y}}(a,y)W_{q}^{q}(\mathbb{Q}_{a,y},\mathbb{P}_{a,y})

where
f_{a,y}(x)=f(x,a,y)
. Since the total Wasserstein distance is bounded by
\delta
, summing over all
(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}
, the ambiguity set
\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P})
restricts the Wasserstein distances as:

\sum_{(a,y)\in\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{Y}}(a,y)W^{q}_{q}(\mathbb{Q}_{a,y},\mathbb{P}_{a,y})\leq\delta^{q}

where
W_{q}\big(\mathbb{Q}_{a,y},\mathbb{P}_{a,y}\big)
is the
q
-Wasserstein distance between these conditional distributions computed with the cost
d
. ∎
