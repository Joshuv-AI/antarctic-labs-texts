Pilot randomized controlled trial to assess the effectiveness of a heat risk reduction decision support platform and barriers and facilitators of its implementation
Authors: Jeremy Hess, Marci Burden, Tania M Busch Isaksen, Kristie L Ebi, Nicole A Errett, Chelsea Gridley-Smith, C Bradley Kramer, Clare McCarthy, Oma McLaughlin, Resham Patel, Anna Reed, Mary Hannah Smith, Stefan Wheat, Kenneth Sherr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClimate-sensitive hazards such as extreme heat are increasing in frequency and severity. Protecting population health requires hazard-specific risk assessment, selection of potential interventions, and support for intervention implementation. This process typically takes several years, constraining health adaptation to climate-sensitive hazards. The investigators have developed an online decision support platform, CHaRT, that links evidence-based, location-specific heat-health risk assessment with transparent analyses of risk drivers and evidence-based risk reduction guidance for use in local health departments (LHDs). CHaRT’s effectiveness in supporting delivery of effective health interventions has not been evaluated. MethodsLHDs are the organizational unit being studied. In this pilot randomized controlled trial, LHDs from the conterminous US will be recruited in coordination with the National Association of City and County Health Officials (NACCHO). Thirty LHDs will be selected at random from interested participants. Fifteen will be randomized to receive the intervention, a facilitated engagement with CHaRT, and 15 will receive the control, a package with information on heat vulnerability assessment and potential interventions. Intervention and control packages will be delivered simultaneously. Facilitated engagement will include an introduction to the tool, user exploration of the tool, elective inclusion of site-specific data into the tool, follow-up sessions to address additional questions, and discussion with investigators regarding planning and implementation needs. Pre- and post-study surveys will be used to assess CHaRT’s effectiveness using the RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Acceptability, Implementation, Maintenance) framework and by comparing each site’s intentions to implement specific interventions. After the trial, barriers and facilitators of the CHaRT platform’s implementation will be assessed through key informant interviews with the intervention group and analyzed using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR).DiscussionThis study will allow investigators to evaluate the public health impact of a decision support platform and to identify barriers and facilitators of its implementation. Background

Extreme heat is causing significant adverse impacts on health and wellbeing in the US on multiple fronts [1] and projected impacts are significant [2–4]. Heat-health impacts are worsening; the rate of heat-related emergency medical service activations increased by 50% in the last four years [5]. Emergency room visits for heat-related illness are increasing steadily [6] and U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) data show a significant increase in heat-related mortality in the last decade [7].

While concerning, these impacts are unsurprising to climate and health practitioners. There is strong etiologic evidence linking extreme heat exposure with both acute heat illness and exacerbation of a wide range of health conditions [4]. While heat-health impacts are largely preventable [8], the distribution of heat-health impacts is highly inequitable [9], and there have been limited investments in needed adaptations [10].

Adaptation activities are lagging, however, for multiple reasons, not least the lack of sufficient funding to support activities with known efficacy [10]. As warming accelerates, widespread adaptation activities will be needed, including implementation of effective interventions at scale. Supporting adaptation at scale requires tools and resources that support each step in the adaptation process, including vulnerability and adaptation assessment, adaptation planning, and implementation [11]. These processes involve governance [12, 13], necessitating that innovative tools not only provide information but also support the activities and decision making that take place through heat governance.

To protect population health against climate-sensitive hazards like extreme heat, communities need insights into local risk drivers, and information about interventions to mitigate the hazard, limit exposure, and reduce vulnerability, as well as support for synthesizing this information and resources to implement health protections [14]. It requires significant expertise, effort, and time to identify and analyze the required data, translate risk assessments into action plans, and prioritize interventions based on the best available scientific evidence [15]. The effort and expense of this process, as well as lack of expertise, slows adaptation in many locations, which constrains adaptation at scale [11]. The lack of decision support tools that support the linked processes of hazard- and location-specific risk assessment, adaptation planning, and implementation may constrain large scale extreme weather and health adaptation [16]. This limits the health sector’s ability to implement adaptations at the rate needed to adequately protect health during extreme weather.

Vulnerability maps, which provide information on the geographic distribution of factors associated with vulnerability to extreme heat, can facilitate decision-making [17], but many are multihazard [18] and provide limited insight into location-specific risk drivers, obscuring information relevant for policymakers at a local level. In addition, vulnerability mapping platforms themselves do not support linkage with specific information about interventions to reduce risks, including their relative protective effects, their implementation timelines, and their costs [19].

To increase the efficiency of decision making and the availability of relevant heat health data, the investigators developed an innovative decision support platform to support LHDs and other users with the challenges of risk assessment and adaptation planning. The Community Heat Resilience Tool (CHaRT) is an online decision support platform designed to support evidence-based climate change adaptation. CHaRT has a risk assessment platform that provides estimates of heat-health risks at a census tract level under historic conditions. CHaRT pairs that data with a decision support platform that links drivers of risk in a given location with information about potential risk reduction activities such as hazard mitigation and vulnerability reduction and includes information useful to policymakers regarding intervention efficacy, timing, and cost.

CHaRT links information on top-level risk drivers, including ranked lists, histograms, and statistical information on the data underlying input variables, with information on interventions to address these drivers and thereby protect population health. For instance, in areas with relatively high impervious surface cover and low levels of tree canopy coverage, CHaRT provides guidance on strategies to increase land cover albedo, use of green and white roofs, and tree planting campaigns, providing information on the nature of the intervention, a summary of the intervention’s effect magnitude, and information on implementation timing, costs, and potential unintended consequences. CHaRT also provides information on cooling centers, behavioral risk reduction interventions, worker protections, air conditioning, heat early warning systems and action plans, heat-health surveillance, fans, and adaptation in Indigenous communities.

CHaRT is designed to provide information on heat-health risks based on heat hazard information for current and future conditions. It is also designed to provide maximum transparency regarding risk drivers and to link this information with intervention strategies to reduce population health risks. The modeling framework is flexible, and local data can easily be integrated into the platform to provide a higher resolution or more current risk estimates through collaboration with the CHaRT modeling team and local public health officials.

CHaRT represents an innovation over conventional vulnerability maps and risk assessment platforms, many of which are multi-hazard [18] and use dimension reduction techniques that reduce collinearity, but also obscure detail on risk drivers that are commonly location specific [20]. The most similar tool in the US was the CDC’s Heat and Health Tracker, which offered high-resolution data to assess heat vulnerability at the local level. This tool is no longer maintained. Another available data source with relevant information is the US Census Bureau’s Community Resilience Estimates for Heat. However, CHaRT goes substantially further than the Heat and Health Tracker by providing transparent information on risk drivers as well as detailed, context-specific information on interventions—highlighting their relative protective benefits, implementation timelines, and associated costs—whereas the CDC tool primarily links users to general heat and health guidance. CHaRT also goes much farther than the Community Resilience Estimates for Heat by providing a mapping platform integrated with intervention guidance.

CHaRT was released for Washington State in June 2023 and is expanding its domain to the United States (US). CHaRT’s risk estimates have been evaluated against observations of emergency medical service calls and emergency department visits during an extreme heat event in Washington State in 2021 and found to be more highly correlated with both outcomes than other common heat vulnerability indices [21]. However, CHaRT’s effectiveness in advancing heat governance and increasing the rate of adoption of strategies to reduce heat-health impacts have not been formally evaluated.

Methods

To evaluate CHaRT’s public health impact, the investigators propose to conduct a pilot exploratory randomized controlled trial. Upon completion of the trial, the investigators will conduct a follow-on qualitative study to assess barriers and facilitators of its implementation. TIDIeR [Additional file 1] and CONSORT [Additional file 2] checklists and a CONSORT checklist [Additional file 3] have been completed for the study design.

The specific aims of the study are:

To assess the public health impact of a facilitated engagement with the CHaRT decision support tool on the breadth and depth of LHDs’ heat-health activity by comparing the reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, and maintenance of the intervention and the organization’s heat-health activities compared with an information-only control.

To assess the public health impact of a facilitated engagement with the CHaRT decision support tool on the breadth and depth of LHDs’ heat-health activity by comparing the reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, and maintenance of the intervention and the organization’s heat-health activities compared with an information-only control.

To assess barriers and facilitators of CHaRT’s implementation by identifying study participants’ perspectives on its alignment with constructs from the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR).

To assess barriers and facilitators of CHaRT’s implementation by identifying study participants’ perspectives on its alignment with constructs from the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR).

This pilot randomized controlled trial will compare the intervention, a facilitated engagement with the CHaRT online decision support platform provided to 15 LHDs, with information-only control provided to 15 LHDs. The control will consist of a package with information on heat vulnerability assessment and potential interventions. Intervention and control packages will be delivered simultaneously. Facilitated engagement will include an introduction to the tool, independent exploration of the tool by the users, follow-up sessions to address additional questions, and discussion with investigators regarding priority interventions, planning, and implementation needs.

This is a pilot study and therefore not designed for formal hypothesis testing of a predefined difference in endpoint between intervention and control groups.

Participants

LHDs are the organizational unit being studied in the trial. While state health departments also share responsibility for public health activities in the US, there are over 3,000 LHDs in the US and they are primarily responsible for initiating, managing, and evaluating local public health programs and policies. LHDs are one of the primary users identified in the development of the CHaRT decision support tool.

Study participants will be employees of the LHD that are designated as the point of contact for study activities. Organizational inclusion criteria include being a local (i.e., city or county) health department with at least partial autonomy to pursue the development and implementation of heat-health activities (i.e., not a state where all public health activities are administered at a state level) and the availability of a staff member to participate in all elements of the trial. Organizational exclusion criteria will include being in a state where public health activities are exclusively managed at a state level and not having staff availability to participate. Current employees of included LHDs aged 18 or greater are eligible for inclusion. Children and prisoners are excluded from enrollment.

Recruitment

Recruitment will be carried out in conjunction with the National Association of City and County Health Officials (NACCHO), the professional organization for local health department officers in the US. Recruitment will take place through outreach at professional meetings, webinars, and newsletters to NACCHO’s membership. After recruitment of at least 30 LHDs, a sample of 30 LHDs will be selected using a random number generator. If more than six LHDs from each region of the US (Northeast, Southeast, Midwest, Southwest, and West) express interest, the sample will be stratified by region prior to selection of study sites. Once the 30 LHDs are selected, the baseline survey will be conducted, and then the participants will be randomized to the intervention or control group using a random number generator.

Intervention and control packages

CHaRT can be used without support from the CHaRT modeling team, but investigators have found through pilot projects with several users that a facilitated engagement leads to rapid development of familiarity with the tool and comfort using it in risk assessment, communication with community partners, and formulation of decision making. For this study, facilitated engagement will take the form of a series of activities provided individually to each participating LHD, as outlined in Fig. 1.

Workflow of facilitated engagement with CHaRT for the intervention group

Facilitated engagement is a remote/virtual process. It begins with an hour-long introduction to the CHaRT modeling tool, led remotely by a member of the CHaRT modeling team. The initial engagement will focus on conveying the tool’s layout and functionality and demonstrating its use, reporting, and data download functions to personnel at the LHD site. Following the introduction, site staff will explore the tool independently. After a suitable period of independent exploration, the investigators will provide follow-up check-ins with discussions to answer questions and provide further insight into the tool’s functionality as needed. The investigators will also query LHD sites regarding their interest in providing data (e.g., proprietary health data not available publicly) that can be used to refine risk estimates for their jurisdictions and, if data are provided, will modify the jurisdiction’s risk models using established methods. Data integration is not required. Finally, the investigators will hold a focused discussion with the sites regarding priority interventions and planning and implementation needs, including the need for and approach to community engagement. The overall process requires approximately five hours of direct engagement and potentially up to five days of additional CHaRT modeling team programming time depending on the need for and extent of bespoke data integration. The facilitated engagement process can be done in as little as two weeks or over several months, depending on user processes and availability. The number of engagements with each site, the types of queries posed, and any collaborative analytical activities will be recorded in standardized meeting notes and used to stratify the effectiveness analysis.

Study participants in the control group will be provided one time with an electronic package of information supportive of heat-health vulnerability, risk assessment, and planning for risk reduction activities through built environment hazard mitigation and public health programming. This package will include an annotated list of online resources, including those available on Heat.gov and the CDC website, and a selected set of review papers on heat-health vulnerability [22], heat-health risk assessment [23], heat hazard mitigation through built environment strategies [24], and heat action planning and preparedness [25]. Control group participants will not receive any synthesis or site-specific information other than being given resources for relevant US and potentially state-specific data.

There are no anticipated harms to the people or agencies involved in the study. There may be indirect benefits and/or indirect harms, but neither are anticipated, and there are no objectives related to either.

Data collection

Data collection over the course of the study is depicted in Fig. 2.

Timing and nature of data collection over the course of the study

All participants will undertake the pre-intervention baseline assessment. The goals of the baseline assessment are to gather information related to 1) the LHD’s organizational demographics and demographics of the communities that the LHD serves; 2) the LHD’s current activities relevant to extreme heat, and 3) contextual and organizational factors that may affect intervention implementation. Information collected via the baseline assessment will allow for comparison of the intervention and control groups, serve as a baseline against which changes in heat-related activities will be measured to determine intervention effectiveness, and allow for insights into contextual and organizational factors that may correlate with successful intervention implementation. The scope of information to be collected will include the LHD's approach to and extent of heat-health risk assessment activities and the nature, extent, and administration of current and definitively planned (defined as activities for which there are firm plans, staffing, and resource allocation within the next calendar year) programming related to extreme heat, including surveillance and program evaluation activities.

Information to be collected on these activities will be framed according to the RE-AIM framework, which assesses public health impact according to an intervention’s Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance [26]. Specifically, for major activity categories (e.g., cooling centers during periods of extreme heat), the LHD will be queried regarding the number of people participating in the intervention (Reach), the activity’s effectiveness relative to its stated goal (Effectiveness), the proportion of partner organizations willing to support or provide the activity (Adoption), the fidelity with which the activity is implemented (Implementation), and the duration over which the activity has been maintained (Maintenance) [27].

The baseline assessment will be conducted by online survey using REDCap [28]. The survey instrument will be developed, piloted, and revised during the recruitment period. Each study site will receive a unique study ID that will be attached to all study activities and records for that site.

Post-intervention assessment will take place one year after the intervention and control package delivery is initiated. The post-intervention assessment will be conducted through an online survey. The goal of the post-intervention assessment is to gather updated information regarding the LHD's current activities relevant to extreme heat. The post-intervention survey will query participants regarding meaningful changes in the demographics of the organization or service area or in the LHD's activities and profile (e.g., the occurrence of a regional disaster that disrupted service provision across the organization), and repeat questions from the pre-intervention assessment related to specific heat-health activities. The post-intervention survey will also include RE-AIM questions related to CHaRT specifically for intervention sites. Investigators will also gather information on the number of interventions implemented by the LHDs.

Separate from evaluating CHaRT’s public health impact, outlined in our first specific aim, our investigators are also interested in assessing factors that affected CHaRT’s implementation in the intervention group. To explore barriers and facilitators of CHaRT implementation and gain insight into implementation outcomes using linkages to theoretical frameworks, which can support systematic insights into real-world implementation outcomes [29], investigators will conduct key informant interviews (KIIs) with all 15 of the primary site contacts for the sites in the intervention group. In this case, investigators will reference the CFIR [30] as a source of constructs for exploring factors affecting CHaRT’s implementation. As an established determinants framework developed to guide assessment of multilevel factors that influence intervention implementation and effectiveness, the CFIR is well suited to this aim.

Data will be collected via semi-structured KIIs with the primary site personnel responsible for engaging with the CHaRT intervention during the trial. KIIs will be conducted remotely via the online platform Zoom. After obtaining the interviewees’ consent, interviews will be recorded for analysis. The 15 interviews will be conducted using an interview guide to be developed and piloted in advance. The interview guide will be based on prior work on implementation of computerized technology [31], use of CFIR in implementation evaluation [30], and implementation of decision support tools for climate change adaptation [16, 23, 32]. The interviews will include both close-ended and open-ended questions. Close-ended questions will focus on the respondents’ perceptions regarding aspects of communications related to CHaRT implementation, institutional prioritization of CHaRT implementation, management support for implementation of CHaRT, availability of time to implement CHaRT, and resources available to support its implementation. Open-ended questions will focus on the pre-implementation context related to climate change adaptation and heat-health risk assessment and risk reduction, experiences related to implementation and resources available for implementation activities, management support for implementation, and overall endorsement of facilitated engagement to CHaRT, including perceptions of the success of the intervention and whether the respondent would recommend the intervention to other sites. All interviews will be fully transcribed verbatim for analysis.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics of the organizations and their service areas for all participants and differences in proportions will be evaluated using z scores and comparing differences in averages using t-tests.

Post-intervention activity-specific public health effectiveness (PHE) scores will be assessed and scored using the same methods described for the pre-intervention analysis. Differences in activity-specific PHE scores in the intervention and control groups will be compared using z scores.

Investigators will generate summary metrics of overall heat-health activity PHE for each site. Pre- to post-intervention differences in overall heat-health activity PHE metrics will be assessed, and CHaRT’s treatment effect will be estimated using descriptive statistics of changes in these metrics. As this is a pilot study and part of an exploratory grant, investigators have not done formal power analysis using an expected effect size.

Key informant interviews will be thematically analyzed using the framework approach to qualitative research [33]. A preliminary codebook will be developed based on the study aims and its guiding framework, the CFIR [34]. The CFIR has five principal domains: Intervention characteristics, outer setting or overall context in which the intervention was implemented, inner setting of the organization implementing the evaluation, and the implementation process [35]. The study team will select specific constructs within these domains based on their relevance for the facilitated CHaRT intervention. The codebook will contact code names, definitions and examples of when to apply the code.

Next, researchers will familiarize themselves with transcribed data by reading it in its entirety. They will then augment the preliminary codebook to align with the data, including by developing additional codes or subcodes, removing irrelevant codes, or adjusting code definitions to align with how informants communicated about the construct(s). Two researchers will then independently code 10% of interview transcripts. Code application decisions will be compared and discrepancies adjudicated through consensus building discussion. The codebook will be refined to clarify code definitions and examples, with the goal of creating an instrument that can be reliably applied by multiple coders. A single researcher will then code the remainder of the transcripts.

A contact sheet will then be developed for each interview. This contact sheet will include code-level summaries, developed by reading and summarizing coded text within that interview for each code. A matrix, where each interview constitutes a row and each code constitutes a column, will be developed, and code-level summaries from each of the contact sheets will be entered into the appropriate cell. An analysis memo will be developed for each code by synthesizing code-level summaries across interviews (i.e., synthesizing data provided in each column of the matrix.

Data sharing

The data, tools, and code produced by the project are intended and expected to be of interest to a broad scientific and practice community and will be shared to the extent possible. Some data may not be made available due to privacy, copyright, or other legally binding restrictions.

Our approach to de-identifying and sharing data will be as follows. De-identified individual and aggregated survey data, including raw and re-coded data, will be shared. De-identification will remove direct and indirect respondent identifiers. Identifiers will be removed once data are final. Interview data will be shared to promote transparency and reproducibility but sharing will be limited to data that do not allow identification of study participants. Digital recordings will be deleted once transcribed. Respondent identifiers will not be shared. Raw transcripts will be maintained but not shared to protect participant privacy. Case memos will be maintained and de-identified and redacted of sensitive content prior to sharing. Coding summaries will be shared. Administrative data will be de-identified prior to sharing.

Privacy of all data will be managed with respect to contingencies for maintaining anonymity and privacy outlined in any University of Washington (UW) Human Subjects Division’s approval. Participant identities will not be reported in project outputs unless permission is granted otherwise. Scientific data and metadata will be archived in the Qualitative Data Repository (QDR), an archive for storing and sharing digital qualitative data and accompanying documentation. Information about shared data will be included on the project’s web page. All shared data and metadata will be findable and identifiable using the standard data indexing tools in QDR, which will assign unique persistent identifiers to data and metadata files that can be referenced. Data will be curated following QDR protocols to make de-identified data publicly available and retained for future use. Links to the QDR workspace where data can be found will be included in all publications, progress reports, and on the project’s web page.

Discussion

While the promise of applying implementation science to health sector climate change adaptation activities has been noted [36–39], there has been limited progress on specific activities to leverage this potential. The proposed work outlines several innovations that will support measurable progress in integrating implementation science with health sector adaptation activities. This work has several limitations. It is a small trial, the size of which is limited by funding, and the study period is relatively short, allowing only for evaluation of process endpoints rather than health outcomes. The small sample size may limit generalizability, and the intervention platform is currently only available in the conterminous US, limiting its application in other settings. The surveys and other instruments used for data collection were developed for this study and have not been previously validated. Some of the interventions in the decision support tool are not administered primarily by the health sector and require close collaboration with other organizations and sectors, and it is unclear how intentions to implement specific interventions may or may not translate into future actions.
