Consider

\begin{cases}\dot{x}=f(t,x),\\
x(t_{0})=x_{0},\end{cases}

(1)

where
f:[0,\infty]\times D\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is locally Lipschitz in
x
on
{[}{0}{,}{\infty}{]\times}{D}
, and piecewise continuous in
t
. Also,
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an open set containing
x=0
. The origin is the equilibrium point for system (
1
) and
t_{0}
is the initial time.

We recall from
7
that the origin of (
1
) is uniformly stable when

\displaystyle\text{for all }\varepsilon>0,\quad\text{there exists }\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)>0,\quad s.t.\ \|x_{0}\|<\delta\Rightarrow\|x(t)\|<\varepsilon

(2)

\displaystyle\text{for all }t\geq t_{0}\geq 0.

In fact, if the equilibrium point of (
1
) is uniformly stable,
\delta
does not depend on
t_{0}
explicitly.

Additionally, the origin of equation (
1
) exhibits the following stability properties:

•

Uniformly asymptotically stable
if it is uniformly stable and there exists a positive constant
c
, independent of
t_{0}
, such that for all initial conditions
\lVert x(t_{0})\rVert<c
, the solution
x(t)
approaches zero as
t\to\infty
, with uniformity over
t_{0}
. Specifically, for every
\eta>0
, there exists a time
T=T(\eta)>0
satisfying

\lVert x(t)\rVert<\eta,\quad\forall t\geq t_{0}+T(\eta),\quad\forall\lVert x(t_{0})\rVert<c

(3)

•

Globally uniformly asymptotically stable
if it is uniformly stable, and
\delta(\epsilon)
can be selected such that
\lim_{\epsilon\to\infty}\delta(\epsilon)=\infty
. Moreover, for any pair of positive numbers
\eta
and
c
, there exists a time
T=T(\eta,c)>0
such that

\lVert x(t)\rVert<\eta,\quad\forall t\geq t_{0}+T(\eta,c),\quad\forall\lVert x(t_{0})\rVert<c

(4)

Theorem 1
Let
x=0
be an equilibrium point for equation (
1
), and let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a domain containing
x=0
. Suppose there exists a pair of functions
(V,W^{*})
where
V:[0,\infty)\times D\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuously differentiable function satisfying

V_{1}(x)\leq V(t,x)\leq V_{2}(x),

(5)

and
W^{*}:[0,\infty)\times D\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function with
W^{*}(t,0)=0
, such that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\max\{W^{*}(\tau,x(\tau)),0\}\,d\tau\leq M(x_{0}),

(6)

where
M(x_{0})
is a bounded and continuous function of
x_{0}
. Additionally, the following holds:

\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}f(t,x)-\max\{W^{*}(t,x),0\}\leq 0,

(7)

for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in D
, with
V_{1}(x)
and
V_{2}(x)
being continuous positive definite functions on
D
. Under these conditions,
x=0
is uniformly stable.

Theorem 2
Assume that the conditions of Theorem 1 hold, with inequality (
7
) strengthened to

\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}f(t,x)-\max\{W^{*}(t,x),0\}\leq-V_{3}(x),

(8)

for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in D
, where
V_{3}(x)
is a continuous positive definite function on
D
. Then, the equilibrium point
x=0
is uniformly asymptotically stable. Furthermore, if
D=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
V_{1}(x)
is radially unbounded, then
x=0
is globally uniformly asymptotically stable.

Definition 1
8

\dot{W}(x,t)
is definitely not equal to zero in the ensemble

\alpha

,
(0<\alpha<A<H)
numbers

,

,
\xi>0
, can be found such that
|\dot{W}(x,t)|>\xi
for
\alpha<\lVert x\rVert<A
,
\rho(x,E(V^{*}=0))<r_{1}
,
t\geq 0
where

\rho(x,x^{0})=\sqrt{(x_{1}-x_{1}^{0})^{2}+\dots+(x_{n}-x_{n}^{0})^{2}}

\rho(x,M)=\inf\{\rho(x,x^{0}),x^{0}\in M\}

is called distance of the point

to the point
x^{0}
in

Theorem 3
8
Let there be functions
V(x,t)
,
\dot{W}(x,t)
, having in
D
the following properties:

1.

The function
V(x,t)
is positive definite and admits an infinitely small upper limit.

2.

The derivative
\dot{V}(x,t)\leq V^{*}(x)\leq 0
.

3.

The function
W(x,t)
is bounded.

4.

\dot{W}(x,t)
is definitely not equal to zero in the ensemble

.

Then the origin of system (

x_{0}
,
t_{0}

Corollary 1
Consider the non-autonomous system described by (
1
). For this systems, if the assumptions of Theorem 3 is satisfied, then, there exists a pair of
(V,W^{*})
that satisfy the conditions of Theorem 2.

Proof.
According to Assumptions 1 and 2 in Theorem 3, the origin of system (
1
) is uniformly stable. Consequently, by Lemma 4.5 in
7
, there exist a class-
\mathcal{K}
function
\alpha_{1}
and a positive constant
c
such that

\|x(t)\|\leq\alpha_{1}(\|x_{0}\|),\quad\text{for all }t\geq 0\text{ and }\|x_{0}\|<c.

(9)

Since
\dot{W}(t,x)
is continuous and bounded, Lemma 4.3 in
7
implies that for any ball
B_{r}\subset D
with
r>0
, there exists a class-
\mathcal{K}
function
\alpha_{2}
such that

W(t,x(t))\leq\alpha_{2}(\|x(t)\|)\leq\alpha_{2}(\alpha_{1}(\|x_{0}\|))=:\alpha_{3}(\|x_{0}\|).

(10)

Now, let the function
V(t,x)
in Theorem 2 be the same as that given in Theorem 3, and define

W^{*}(t,x)=\begin{cases}0,&V^{*}(x)<0,\\
|\dot{W}(t,x)|,&V^{*}(x)=0.\end{cases}

(11)

Suppose
x(t)\in E:=\{x\in D\mid V^{*}(x)=0\}
. We claim that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\max\{W^{*}(\tau,x(\tau)),0\}\,d\tau=\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}|\dot{W}(x(\tau),\tau)|\,d\tau

is bounded by a continuous function of
x_{0}
.

Define the set

U:=\left\{x\in D\,\middle|\,\alpha<\|x\|<A,\;\rho(x,E)<r_{1}\right\},

(12)

where
\rho(x,E)
denotes the distance from
x
to the set
E
.

Following a similar argument to that in the proof of Theorem 1.1 in
8
, any trajectory of system (
1
) starting in
E
cannot remain in
U
for an interval of time longer than
\frac{2L}{\xi}
, where
|\dot{W}|>\xi
and
|W|\leq L
in
U
.

Let
\{[t_{k},t_{k}+\Delta t_{k}]\}_{k=1}^{N}
denote the collection of time intervals where
x(t)\in U
. Then by Theorem 1.1, each interval satisfies
\Delta t_{k}\leq\frac{2L}{\xi}
, and by (
10
),

\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k}+\Delta t_{k}}|\dot{W}(x(t),t)|\,dt\leq 2\alpha_{3}(\|x_{0}\|).

(13)

Summing over all such intervals, we obtain:

\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}|\dot{W}(x(\tau),\tau)|\,d\tau\leq\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k}+\Delta t_{k}}|\dot{W}(x(\tau),\tau)|\,d\tau\leq N\cdot 2\alpha_{3}(\|x_{0}\|).

(14)

We now claim that
N
must be finite. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
N\to\infty
. Using similar reasoning as in
8
, every time the trajectory enters
U
, the value of
V(t,x)
decreases by at least

a:=\frac{\xi r_{1}}{2X\sqrt{n}}>0.

Thus, after
k
entries, we have

V(x(t_{k}),t_{k})\leq V(x_{0},t_{0})-ka.

Letting
k\to\infty
, this implies
V(x(t_{k}),t_{k})\to-\infty
, which contradicts the boundedness of
V
. Hence,
N
must be finite.

Therefore, from (
14
), it follows that
\int_{0}^{\infty}\max\{W^{*}(\tau,x(\tau)),0\}\,d\tau
is bounded by a continuous function of
\|x_{0}\|
.

Finally, consider the expression
\dot{V}(t,x)-\max\{W^{*}(t,x),0\}
, where

\dot{V}(t,x)=\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}f(t,x).

We then have:

\dot{V}(t,x)-\max\{W^{*}(t,x),0\}\leq\begin{cases}V^{*}(x),&V^{*}(x)<0,\\
-|\dot{W}(t,x)|,&V^{*}(x)=0,\end{cases}

(15)

which shows that
\dot{V}(t,x)-\max\{W^{*}(t,x),0\}
is negative definite if we define

V_{3}(x):=\begin{cases}-V^{*}(x),&V^{*}(x)<0,\\
|\dot{W}(t,x)|,&V^{*}(x)=0.\end{cases}

This completes the proof.

Here, by applying Theorem 1, we analyze the stability properties of a system considered in
9
, described as:

\dot{x}_{i}=\frac{\beta(t)x_{i}}{1+h(x_{i})},\quad i=1,\dots,n,

(16)

where
\beta(t)>0
with
\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}\beta(\tau)\,d\tau=M_{1}<\infty
, and
h(x_{i})\geq 0
. Although this system is known to be uniformly stable, a smooth Lyapunov function of the form
V(x)
cannot be constructed to establish its stability via classical Lyapunov methods, as shown in
9
.

However, Theorem 1 allows us to analyze the stability of system (
16
) using the simple Lyapunov candidate
V(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}
. Starting from (
16
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{dx_{i}}{x_{i}}&=\frac{\beta(t)\,dt}{1+h(x_{i})}\\
\Rightarrow\int_{x_{i}(0)}^{x_{i}(t)}\frac{dx_{i}}{x_{i}}&=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{\beta(\tau)}{1+h(x_{i}(\tau))}\,d\tau\\
&\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\beta(\tau)\,d\tau\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}\beta(\tau)\,d\tau=M_{1},\\
\Rightarrow\ln|x_{i}(t)|-\ln|x_{i}(0)|&\leq M_{1}\\
\Rightarrow\ln\left(\frac{|x_{i}(t)|}{|x_{i}(0)|}\right)&\leq M_{1}\\
\Rightarrow|x_{i}(t)|&\leq|x_{i}(0)|e^{M_{1}}.\end{aligned}

(17)

This inequality holds for all
i=1,\dots,n
. Hence, we have:

\|x(t)\|^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}(0)e^{2M_{1}}=\|x_{0}\|^{2}e^{2M_{1}}.

(18)

Now, using the Lyapunov function
V(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}
, we compute its time derivative:

\dot{V}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\beta(t)x_{i}^{2}}{1+h(x_{i})}>0.

(19)

To apply Theorem 1, define

W^{*}(t,x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\beta(t)x_{i}^{2}}{1+h(x_{i})}.

Using (
18
), we estimate the integral of
W^{*}
as:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\max\{W^{*}(\tau,x(\tau)),0\}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta(\tau)x_{i}^{2}(\tau)\,d\tau

(20)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta(\tau)\|x_{0}\|^{2}e^{2M_{1}}\,d\tau

\displaystyle=\|x_{0}\|^{2}e^{2M_{1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta(\tau)\,d\tau=M_{1}\|x_{0}\|^{2}e^{2M_{1}}.

Therefore,
W^{*}(t,x)
satisfies condition (
6
) in Theorem 1. Furthermore, we observe that

\dot{V}(x)-\max\{W^{*}(t,x),0\}=0.

This confirms, by Theorem 1, that the origin of system (
16
) is uniformly stable—even though, as shown in
9
(Theorem 3), no smooth Lyapunov function of the form
V(x)
exists that can directly prove stability through classical methods.
