Jingbo Liu

Abstract
We develop an information-theoretic framework for bounding the expected supremum and tail probabilities of stochastic processes, offering a simpler and sharper alternative to classical chaining and slicing arguments for generalization bounds. The key idea is a lifting argument that produces information-theoretic analogues of empirical process bounds, such as Dudley’s entropy integral. The lifting introduces symmetry, yielding sharp bounds even when the classical Dudley integral is loose. As a by-product, we obtain a concise proof of the majorizing measure theorem, providing explicit constants. The information-theoretic approach provides a soft version of classical localized complexity bounds in generalization theory, but is more concise and does not require the slicing argument. We apply this approach to empirical risk minimization over Sobolev ellipsoids and weak ℓ q \ell_{q} balls, obtaining sharper convergence rates or extensions to settings not covered by classical methods.

Introduction

Sharp control of empirical processes is central to modern statistical learning theory, underpinning generalization bounds, minimax rates, and risk analysis for high-dimensional estimators.
Classical tools such as Dudley’s entropy integral have played a central role in this analysis, with applications ranging from nonparametric regression

(
Van de Geer, 1990
;
Wainwright, 2019
)

to sparse high-dimensional models

(
Raskutti et al., 2011
)
.
Although certain special cases can be handled by simpler techniques, the localized version of Dudley’s integral
(
Van de Geer, 1990
)
has remained a fundamental tool in many general settings.
However, its application to statistical learning may still be limited since
(i) Dudley’s integral, while widely used, can be suboptimal even for basic examples such as Sobolev ellipsoids, and
(ii) the slicing (or peeling) technique for localization often requires delicate covering number estimates, sometimes invoking deep results from approximation theory or the local theory of Banach spaces, e.g. in sparse regression over the
\ell_{q}
ball
(
Raskutti et al., 2011
)
.

In this paper, we introduce a “lifting” method for deriving new information-theoretic generalization bounds, which overcomes the limitations discussed above.
The key idea is to introduce replicated copies of a system and apply the method of types from information theory,
so that traditional “hard” inequalities for the supremum of a stochastic process for an index set yield “soft” information-theoretic inequalities for a distribution on that set.
The strength of the lifting approach lies in leveraging geometric insights involving covering and packing numbers, such as the chaining argument in Dudley’s integral,
yielding new information-theoretic inequalities that are not easily derived from traditional information-theoretic methods.
This method yields sharper localized generalization bounds and new results for empirical risk minimization over ellipsoids and sparse regression.

For example, consider a Gaussian process
(X_{t})_{t\in T}
and a measure
\mu
on
T
.
With the lifting approach, both Dudley’s integral and the majorizing measure theorem yield the following equivalences for Gaussian processes (up to universal constant factors):

\displaystyle\sup\mathbb{E}[X_{Z}]\asymp\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\inf_{P_{Z\hat{Z}}\in\Pi_{\sigma}(\mu)}I(Z;\hat{Z})}d\sigma\asymp\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{R_{\mu}(\sigma^{2})}d\sigma

(1)

where
\Pi_{\sigma}(\mu)
denotes the set of couplings of
\mu
and itself with mean square error
\sigma^{2}
,

I(Z;\hat{Z})
is the mutual information, and
R_{\mu}(\sigma^{2})
denotes the rate-distortion function
(
Thomas and Joy, 2006
)
.
The supremum in (
1
) is over all couplings of the index and the process satisfying the marginal condition
P_{Z}=\mu
.
While the analogy between the rate-distortion function
R_{\mu}(\sigma^{2})
and covering numbers is well-known
(
Thomas and Joy, 2006
;
Csiszár and Körner, 2011
)
,
we are not aware of any prior literature explicitly stating (
1
).
Previously, similar lifting arguments were used for problems in selected problems from network information theory
(
Wu et al., 2018
;
Liu, 2022
;
Liu, 2021
;
Liu, 2023
)
, but the application to empirical processes appears to be new.
More generally, “soft” versions of traditional measure concentration techniques are known to provide sharper convergence in some hypothesis testing and information theory problems
(
Liu, 2018
;
Liu et al., 2020
;
Liu, 2020
;
Liu and Özgür, 2020
)
.

We use information-theoretic inequalities such as (
1
) to control the generalization error in empirical risk minimization (ERM),
and this approach offers several advantages over the traditional approach based on slicing and Dudley’s integral:

First, our approach yields sharper convergence rate in some basic estimation problems.
The reason is that (
1
) is a sharp two-sided inequality for any
\mu
,
which might be surprising since we derive (
1
) from the Dudley integral,
and the latter is not sharp for some
T
.
The reason for the sharpness of (
1
) is that the lifting argument transforms the process into a stationary one - i.e., invariant under a transitive group of permutations - even if the original process lacks this symmetry.
Since Dudley’s integral is sharp for stationary processes
(see Section
2.1
for background),
(
1
) inherits the sharpness, even if the original process is nonstationary.
The sharpness of (
1
) also has an interesting consequence:
by essentially
maximizing over
\mu
and applying the date processing inequality,
we obtain a concise new proof of the nontrivial lower bound part of the classical majorizing measure theorem.
This new proof of the majorizing measure theorem is conceptually simple, as it closely mirrors the argument used for the Dudley integral upper bound, essentially reversing its key steps.
Furthermore, many nuisance parameters in the traditional proof disappear due to taking limits in the lifting argument.

Second, our approach makes
localization
of generalization bounds more straightforward.
Localization refers to the phenomenon where the index
Z
of the process concentrates on a subset much smaller than the full index set
T
, so that taking the supremum over all of
T
may significantly overestimate the typical value of the process at
Z
.
For example, in empirical risk minimization (ERM),
Z
denotes the estimator, which tends to concentrate near zero as the sample size increases. Traditionally, localization is achieved by the “slicing” (or “peeling”) argument, which yields sharp convergence rate for some empirical risk minimization problems

Wainwright, 2019
.
Slicing typically involves controlling the modulus of continuity of the process through careful controls of the deviation magnitude for different slices.
In contrast, using properties of the rate-distortion function, we can often directly upper bound the right side of (
1
) using the variance of
Z\sim\mu
, which is much smaller than the size of
T
.
Similarly, tail probability bounds on
X_{Z}
, based on the moment generating function of
\|Z\|_{2}
, can also be derived using a lifting technique.
This new method of localization does not require slicing.

To demonstrate our approach, we derive new and sharper statistical rates in two classical ERM problems:

•

For mean estimation via ERM over the Sobolev ellipsoid with smoothness parameter
\beta>1/2
, a setting that plays a fundamental role in nonparametric statistics
(
Tsybakov, 2009
)
, the slicing method based on Dudley’s integral yields only an upper bound of order
O\left(\tfrac{1}{2\beta-1}\right)n^{-2\beta/(2\beta+1)}
for the mean squared error. In contrast, our method achieves the sharper rate
O\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\beta-1}}\right)n^{-2\beta/(2\beta+1)}
. The suboptimal factor
O(\tfrac{1}{2\beta-1})
also appears in Dudley’s integral for the supremum of a Gaussian process indexed by the Sobolev ellipsoid, a classical example of the looseness of Dudley’s bound
(
Talagrand, 2014
)
, where the optimal dependence is
O(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\beta-1}})
.

•

For sparse regression over the
\ell_{q}
-ball,
q\in(0,1]
,
Raskutti et al.
(
Raskutti et al., 2011
)
showed that the mean square prediction error is upper bounded by
(\frac{\ln d}{n})^{\frac{2-q}{2}}R
up to a constant depending on
q
, where
d
is the feature dimension,
n
is the sample size,
R^{1/q}
is the
\ell_{q}
radius, the noise is standard normal, and the columns of the design matrix are normalized to have
\ell_{2}
norm at most
\sqrt{n}
.
The proof in
Raskutti et al., 2011
was based on the slicing method and estimating covering numbers of the
q
-absolutely convex hull,
using deep results from convex geometry
(
Guedon and Litvak, 2000
;
Carl and Pajor, 1988
)
; see proof of
(
Raskutti et al., 2011
, Lemma 3)
.
We provide a new proof without citing the convex geometry literature,
using only simple properties of the entropy, such as the chain rule, to extend elementary one-dimensional estimates to higher dimensions.
In fact, we show that the same convergence rate holds for weak
\ell_{q}
balls (
q\in(0,1)
), which is new and appears to be beyond the reach of prior techniques in the literature.

Related work:

A closely related line of recent research,
started by

Russo and Zou, 2016
and further developed by other authors including

proved information-theoretic generalization bounds in terms of the mutual information between the random index and the process.
A basic version (see for example
Russo and Zou, 2016
) is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{Z}]\lesssim\sup_{t\in T}\|X_{t}\|_{\Psi_{2}}\sqrt{I(Y;Z)}

(2)

where
the sub-Gaussian norm
\|\cdot\|_{\Psi_{2}}
will be defined in (
6
),
and

Y:=(X_{t})_{t\in T}
denotes the stochastic process (in the case of finite
T
, we have
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{|T|}
).
While this can be interpreted as a strengthening of a maximal inequality
(
Jiao et al., 2018
)
,
further refinement using the chaining argument,
strengthening the Dudley integral, is also possible

(
Asadi et al., 2018
)
.
This type of results are useful in statistics and machine learning,
where
Z
represents the output of an algorithm which has limited mutual information with the training data,
potentially much smaller than the covering number of
T
.
Though also concerning a random index framework, their results differ from and are not directly comparable with (
1
), since their strengthening is in terms of the mutual information between
Z
and the data, rather than the rate-distortion function of
Z
.
Their proofs typically involve information-theoretic arguments such as chain rules and nonnegativity of the relative entropy,
but not the lifting technique in the present paper.
Nevertheless, we will show in Theorem
2
a result that encompasses both (
1
) and (
2
),
highlighting the versatility of the lifting technique.

In related work on empirical risk minimization (ERM), it has been shown that under favorable conditions, such as convex regression classes with Gaussian noise, the mean-squared error (MSE) can often be characterized by a fixed-point equation involving a localized Gaussian width
(
Chatterjee, 2014
)
.
For ellipsoids, this localized Gaussian width can then be controlled directly via the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality
(
Wei et al., 2020
)
, thereby avoiding the slicing and Dudley-integral machinery and mitigating the looseness of Dudley’s bound. However, this shortcut relies essentially on concentration of the error, which can fail when the regression class is nonconvex (e.g., sparse regression under the
\ell_{q}
constraint with
q<1
) or when the noise is sub-Gaussian but does not satisfy a measure concentration inequality (such as log-Sobolev or transportation-cost inequality). Moreover, the Cauchy-Schwarz step exploits linear structure: for example, consider a regression class given by the image of a Sobolev ellipsoid under a Lipschitz map (e.g., induced by a nonlinear neural network). This generalizes the setting of
Wei et al., 2020
, but their argument no longer applies.
These limitations are expected to be artifacts of the proof: tail probability bounds need not require concentration (as naturally follows from the Dudley integral; see Lemma
2
), and Lipschitz maps do not increase the supremum of the relevant stochastic process (a consequence of the majorizing measure theorem; see (
13
)). Unfortunately, existing tools do not suffice to rigorously establish these intuitions, and our work introduces an alternative framework that overcomes these limitations.

2.1
Upper Bounds for Stochastic Processes

Classically, Dudley’s integral provides upper bounds on a sub-Gaussian process in terms of the covering numbers.
Let
(T,d)
be a metric space,
and suppose that
(X_{t})_{t\in T}
is a centered sub-Gaussian process,
that is,
\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]=0
, and the natural metric

\displaystyle d(t,t^{\prime}):=\|X_{t}-X_{t^{\prime}}\|_{\Psi_{2}},\quad\forall t,t^{\prime}\in T,

(5)

where, following
Vershynin, 2018
, we defined the sub-Gaussian norm

\displaystyle\|X\|_{\Psi_{2}}=\inf\{K>0\colon\mathbb{E}[e^{X^{2}/K^{2}}]\leq 2\}.

(6)

Dudley’ integral states that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\in T}X_{t}]\leq K\int_{0}^{\Delta}\sqrt{\ln N(T,\lambda)}d\lambda

(7)

where
K
is a universal constant,
N(T,\lambda)
denotes the
\lambda
-covering number of
T
,
i.e. the minimal number of (open) balls of radius
\lambda
required to cover
T
,
and
\Delta
denotes the diameter of
T
.
While usually called Dudley’s integral, (
7
) is occasionally attributed to other authors as well;
see
Dudley, 2016
;
Maurey, 2024
;
Talagrand, 2014
for more about the history.
In the case of Gaussian processes, the natural metric is defined as

\displaystyle d(t,t^{\prime}):=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}-X_{t^{\prime}}|^{2}]}.

(8)

Note that (
8
) differs from (
5
) by a universal constant factor
(
Vershynin, 2018
)
.
Throughout this paper, when we discuss Gaussian processes specifically, we define the natural metric by (
8
) rather than (
5
).
In the Gaussian case, a commonly referenced explicit bound is
K<24

(
Ledoux and Talagrand, 2013
)
.
As pointed out in
(
Dudley, 2016
, Section 5.3)
, better estimates
K
exist in the literature, and the proof essentially relies on the estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\max_{1\leq j\leq N}X_{j}]\leq\sqrt{2\ln N}

(9)

where
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
are centered, jointly Gaussian, with variances not exceeding 1.
If we drop Gaussianity and instead assume that
\|X_{1}\|_{\Psi_{2}},\dots,\|X_{N}\|_{\Psi_{2}}\leq 1
,
then
\mathbb{E}[\max_{1\leq j\leq N}X_{j}]\leq\sqrt{2\ln(2N)}
for
N\geq 1

(
Vershynin, 2018
, Section 2.7.3)
, which is slightly worse than the Gaussian case (
9
).
We will see, however, that the difference will vanish when we apply the lifting argument, which effectively takes
N\to\infty
.

For stationary Gaussian processes, it is known since
Fernique, 1975
that Dudley’s integral is tight:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\Delta}\sqrt{\ln N(T,\lambda)}d\lambda\leq K^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\in T}X_{t}]

(10)

for a universal constant
K^{\prime}>0
.

Maurey, 2024
gives a bound of
K^{\prime}\leq 432
.
In the stationary case, the smaller balls in the chaining argument are equivalent in each level, so the chaining process in the proof of the Dudley integral can be reversed using the so-called super-Sudakov minoration (see Lemma
10
ahead).

For general nonstationary processes, the same paper of Fernique
(
Fernique, 1975
)
conjectured what was later known as the majorizing measure theorem,
whose nontrivial lower bound part was proved more than a decade later by Talagrand
(
Talagrand, 1987
)
using a more sophisticated
generic chaining
argument.
Given a probability measure
\mu
on
T
, define

\displaystyle I_{\mu}(t):=\int_{0}^{\Delta}\sqrt{\ln\frac{1}{\mu(B(t,\lambda))}}d\lambda

(11)

The majorizing measure theorem states that the left side of (
7
) equals, up to a universal constant factor,

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}(T):=\inf_{\mu}\sup_{t\in T}I_{\mu}(t).

(12)

The usual definition of
\gamma_{2}(T)
in terms of admissible partitions (cf.
Talagrand, 2014
) is equivalent to (
12
) up to a constant factor.
Generally speaking,
\gamma_{2}(T)
is more difficult to evaluate than Dudley’s integral:

\mu
needs to be optimized for a quantity depending on
\mu(B(t,\lambda))
over different scales of
\lambda
,
whereas in Dudley’s integral, we can calculate the covering number by optimizing for each scale separately.

Despite this, the majorizing measure theorem is appealing due to its sharpness for the Gaussian process.
A nontrivial application of
\gamma_{2}
is sub-Gaussian comparison:
suppose that
(X_{t})
is a separable sub-Gaussian process,

(Y_{t})
is a separable Gaussian process,
and both processes are zero mean with the same natural metric on
T
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\in T}X_{t}]\lesssim\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\in T}Y_{t}].

(13)

The special case where
(X_{t})
is also Gaussian is called the Gaussian comparison inequality, usually proved by an interpolation argument which is highly Gaussian-specific
(
Ledoux and Talagrand, 2013
)
.
The more general sub-Gaussian case can be immediately seen from the majorizing measure theorem.
As noted in
Van Handel, 2014
,
this version of sub-Gaussian comparison is a nontrivial consequence of the majorizing measure theorem, for which no other proof is known.

It is also known (see
Talagrand, 2014
) that
the following quantities are equivalent to
\gamma_{2}(T)
up to constants:

\displaystyle{\rm Fer}(T)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\mu}\int_{T}I_{\mu}(t)d\mu(t);

(14)

\displaystyle\delta_{2}(T)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\mu}\inf_{t\in T}I_{\mu}(t).

(15)

Once the expected value is understood, tail bounds can be established either by applying concentration of measure, or by reworking the chaining steps to control the tail probability instead of the expectation.
For technical convenience, our discussion of tail bounds will be restricted to processes of the form
X_{t}=\left<Y,t\right>
, where
t\in T\subseteq\ell_{2}
.
Following
Vershynin, 2018
, the
sub-Gaussian norm
of a random vector
Y\in\ell_{2}
is defined by taking the supremum over one-dimensional projections:

\displaystyle\|Y\|_{\Psi_{2}}:=\sup_{v\in\ell_{2}}\|\left<Y,v\right>\|_{\Psi_{2}}.

(16)

A bound on
\|Y\|_{\Psi_{2}}
can be guaranteed, for example, if
Y
has independent coordinates with uniformly bounded sub-Gaussian norms
(
Vershynin, 2018
)
.

Concentration inequalities control the deviation probability from the mean. We say
P_{Y}
satisfies the

log-Sobolev inequality
(LSI), if for any continuously differentiable function
f\colon\ell_{2}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle{\rm Ent}(f^{2})\leq 2\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(Y)\|_{2}^{2}],

(17)

where
{\rm Ent}(f):=\mathbb{E}[f(Y)\ln f(Y)]-\mathbb{E}[f(Y)]\ln\mathbb{E}[f(Y)]
.
The log-Sobolev inequality is satisfied when the coordinates of
Y
are independent and each satisfies the log-Sobolev inequality (tensorization property).
The log-Sobolev inequality implies sub-Gaussian concentration. In fact, the following property is well-known:

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that
X_{t}:=\left<t,Y\right>
, where
Y
is a zero-mean random vector in
\ell_{2}
satisfying the log-Sobolev inequality,
and
T
is totally bounded (i.e., covering numbers are all finite) in
\ell_{2}
.
Let
\sigma^{2}:=\sup_{t\in T}\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{2}]
,
and let
\xi:=\sup_{t\in T}X_{t}
.
Then for all
u>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\xi-\mathbb{E}[\xi]>u]\leq e^{-\frac{u^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}.

(18)

The proof follows by showing the Lipschitz condition

\|\nabla\max_{t\in T}\left<t,y\right>\|_{2}\leq\max_{t\in T}\|t\|_{2}
,
and using the fact that the log-Sobolev inequality implies sub-Gaussian concentration; see
(
Boucheron et al., 2013
, Theorem 5.8)
.
In fact, from the proof we can see that the LSI assumption in Lemma
1
can be weakened to Talagrand’s optimal transport inequality, which also implies sub-Gaussian deviation of Lipschitz functions
(
Boucheron et al., 2013
)
.

It is possible, however, to obtain tail bounds without assuming concentration:

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that
Y
is a zero-mean random vector in
\ell_{2}
satisfying
\|Y\|_{\Psi_{2}}\leq 1
,
and
\sup_{t\in T}\|t\|_{2}\leq\Delta
.
Then there exists a universal constant
C>0
such that for any
x\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\sup_{t\in T}\left<t,Y\right>\geq CJ(T)+x]\leq Ce^{-\frac{x^{2}}{C\Delta^{2}}},

(19)

where
J(T):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\ln N(T,\epsilon)}\epsilon
.

A proof of Lemma
2
can be found in
Van Handel, 2014
and the references therein.
Statistical literature, such as
Van de Geer, 1990
, also used similar tail probability bounds for sub-Gaussian processes.
As noted in
Van Handel, 2014
,
although Lemma
2
requires weaker an assumption on the distribution than Lemma
1
,
it can give a weaker bound when
J(T)
is larger than
\mathbb{E}[\xi]
.

2.2
Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM)

In a general mean estimation problem,
T
is a closed subset of
\ell_{2}
, and
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. random vectors in
\ell_{2}
whose mean is an unknown
m\in T
.
For any
t\in\ell_{2}
,
define the empirical risk

\displaystyle\hat{L}(t):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|t-X_{i}\|_{2}^{2}.

(20)

The (population) risk is

L(t):=\mathbb{E}[\hat{L}(t)]
.
The empirical risk minimizer (ERM) is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{m}:=\argmin_{t\in T}\hat{L}(t).

(21)

We have

\displaystyle\|\hat{m}-m\|^{2}

\displaystyle=L(\hat{m})-L(m)

(22)

\displaystyle\leq-\hat{L}(\hat{m})+\hat{L}(m)+L(\hat{m})-L(m)

(23)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\chi_{m-\hat{m}}

(24)

where
\chi_{z}:=-2\sqrt{n}\left<z,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-m\right>
, and
(\chi_{z})_{z\in T-T}
is a centered sub-Gaussian process, whose natural metric is the
\ell_{2}
metric (up to a universal constant factor).
An example of such a mean estimation problem, which plays a fundamental role in nonparametric statistics,
will be discussed in Section
3.4
, where
T
is a Sobolev ellipsoid.

In a general regression problem,
T
is a closed subset in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
Y=\sqrt{n}m+w
, where
m\in T
is an unknown vector to estimate, and

is a zero-mean noise vector satisfying
\|w\|_{\Psi_{2}}\leq 1
.
An example of sparse regression with fixed designs will be discussed in Section
3.5
.
The case of random designs, which is traditionally tackled by local Rademacher complexity techniques
(
Lugosi and Wegkamp, 2004
;
Bartlett et al., 2005
)
, is not considered here, although it would be interesting to consider such extensions for future work.
Define

\displaystyle\hat{L}(t):=\frac{1}{n}\|\sqrt{n}t-Y\|_{2}^{2}

(25)

and define

and
\hat{m}
the same way as in the mean estimation problem.
Then (
24
) still holds,
if we now define
\chi_{z}:=-2\left<z,w\right>
, and
(\chi_{z})_{z\in T-T}
is a centered sub-Gaussian process, whose natural metric is the
\ell_{2}
metric (up to a universal constant factor).

With a slight change of notation, it is possible to unify the formulations of the two problems: both are essentially about projecting a random vector onto a subset in
\ell_{2}
.
We present them as estimation and regression problems separately simply to make the sample size
n
emerge naturally and consistently with the nonparametric estimation and sparse regression literature.
More generally, the framework also encompasses many other problems including functional estimation, matrix completion, shape-restricted regression, and isotonic regression; see
Chatterjee, 2014
;
Chatterjee et al., 2015
.

In both problems, the inequality (
24
) plays a key role in upper bounding the mean square error
\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{m}-m\|_{2}^{2}]
; see e.g.
(
Van de Geer, 1990
, eq (2))
.
Since
\mathbb{E}[\hat{L}(m)-L(m)]=0
, the expected generalization error equals
\mathbb{E}[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\chi_{m-\hat{m}}]
, which is also connected to (
24
).
If we take the expectation of both sides of (
24
), and upper bound the right side by the Dudley integral using the covering number of
T
, we generally do not get sharp convergence rates of the mean square error.
In fact, even for the simplest case of 1-dimensional parameter estimation, such a naive approach yields a
1/\sqrt{n}
rate rather than the optimal
1/n
rate.

The traditional approach for improving the bound is a “slicing” argument that yields local complexity bounds;
see for example
Van de Geer, 1990
;
Wainwright, 2019
;
Raskutti et al., 2011
or
(
Van Handel, 2014
, Theorem 5.32)
.
Roughly speaking, if
\|\hat{m}-m\|^{2}=r
, then
\chi_{m-\hat{m}}\leq\sup_{z\in B(r)\cap(T-T)}\chi_{z}
, which is bounded by some function
\omega(r)
with high probability.
The function
\omega(r)
typically grows slower than
r^{2}
, so
r^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\omega(r)
yields an upper bound on
r
.
More precisely, a union bound argument is applied to control the generalization error at different scales of
r
with high probability.
While it may seem that the uniform control by the union bound could lose logarithmic factors,
this can actually be avoided by carefully scheduling of the error tolerance at each scale.

In Section
8.1
, we give a more detailed review of how the traditional slicing approach can yield sharp convergence for the one-dimensional toy problem.
Local complexity bounds, which are based on Dudley’ integral for local sets, can still be loose for general
T
where Dudley’ integral is loose.
The majorizing measure theorem, though sharp, is hard to evaluate, and is rarely used in the analysis of ERM.

2.3
Information-Theoretic Tools

We recall standard information-theoretic measures in
Thomas and Joy, 2006
, using the natural base of the logarithm.
The entropy of a discrete random variable
X\sim\mu
is
H(X):=\sum_{x}\mu(x)\ln\frac{1}{\mu(x)}
.
The relative entropy between
\mu
and
\nu
on the same measurable space is
D(\mu\|\nu):=\int\ln\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}d\mu
.
Mutual information between
(X,Y)\sim P_{XY}
is defined as
I(X;Y):=D(P_{XY}\|P_{X}\times P_{Y})
.
Conditional versions of these quantities are defined by averaging over the random variable which is conditioned on.
For example,
D(P_{\hat{Z}|Z}\|\mu|P_{Z}):=\int D(P_{\hat{Z}|Z=z}\|\mu)dP_{Z}(z)
.
The lifting argument relies on the
method of types
, a standard method in information theory
(
Csiszár and Körner, 2011
)
and large deviation analysis
(
Dembo, 2009
)
.
The type of a sequence of length
N
is simply its empirical distribution.
Since not all distributions can be a type, we need the following:

Definition 1
.

Suppose that
\mu
is a distribution on a finite set
\mathcal{Z}
. We say
\mu
is
rational
if there exists integer
N>0
such that
N\mu
is
integer
, i.e.,
N\mu(z)
is integer for any
z\in\mathcal{Z}
.

If
N\nu
is integer, we denote by
C_{\nu}
the
\nu
-type set
, i.e., the set of sequences with type
\nu
.
Then the following result is standard and can be found in
(
Csiszár and Körner, 2011
, Chapter 1)
:

Lemma 3
.

Let
S_{N}
be the set of all possible types of
\mathcal{Z}^{N}
,
where
\mathcal{Z}
is a set satisfying the cardinality bound
|\mathcal{Z}|\leq n
.
Then

|S_{N}|={n+N-1\choose n-1}\leq(N+1)^{n}
.
Furthermore, if
\mu
is a distribution on
\mathcal{Z}
and
N\mu
is integer,
then

\displaystyle(N+1)^{-n}\exp(-ND(\nu\|\mu))\leq\mu^{\otimes N}(C_{\nu})\leq\exp(-ND(\nu\|\mu)).

(26)

3
Main Results

3.1
A Rate-Distortion Integral

Our first main result concerns
\mathbb{E}[X_{Z}]
, where
Z
is a random index that may depend on the realization of the process,
which is a natural consequence of our information-theoretic technique.
We first clarify some measure-theoretic aspects regarding the random index formulation.
First, if
T=\{t_{0},t_{1},\dots\}
is countable,
we assume that for each
t\in T
,
X_{t}(\omega)
is a measurable function of
\omega\in\Omega
(where
\Omega
denotes the sample space).
Suppose that
Z(\omega)\in\{t_{0},t_{1},\dots\}
is also a measurable function of
\omega\in\Omega
.
Then
X_{Z(\omega)}(\omega)
is a measurable function of
\omega
, because for any Borel set
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\{\omega\colon X_{Z(\omega)}(\omega)\in B\}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}\{\omega\colon Z(\omega)=t_{i}\}\cap\{\omega\colon X_{t_{i}}(\omega)\in B\}

(27)

which is measurable.

If
T
is uncountable, measurability is not so immediate since the union in (
27
) is uncountable.
The standard approach is to assume
separability
, i.e., there is a dense countable subset
T_{0}=\{t_{0},t_{1},\dots\}\subset T
, and
X_{t}
is continuous in
t\in T
almost surely, so that
X_{t}
for
t\notin T_{0}
can be defined by passing a limit.
Continuity here is with respect to the topology compatible with the metric
d
.
We note that separability and continuity ensure that
X_{Z(\omega)}(\omega)
is measurable, as long as
Z(\omega)
is measurable,
which should suffice for most statistical applications.
Indeed, we can construct random variable
Z_{k}:=t_{i}
where

\displaystyle i=\argmin_{j\in\{0,1,\dots,k\}}d(Z(\omega),t_{j})

(28)

with ties broken arbitrarily but respecting measurability.
Assuming measurability of
Z(\omega)
, we have measurability of
d(Z(\omega),t_{j})
for each
j
, so that
Z_{k}
, and hence
X_{Z_{k}}
, is measurable.
Density of
T_{0}
ensures
\lim_{k\to\infty}Z_{k}=Z
almost surely, and continuity implies that
X_{Z}=\lim_{k\to\infty}X_{Z_{k}}
is measurable, being a sequential limit of measurable functions.

We recall the rate-distortion function in information theory
(
Thomas and Joy, 2006
)
,
which will be a counterpart of the covering number in Dudley’s integral.
Given a probability measure
\mu
on
T
and
\sigma>0
, define

\displaystyle\Pi_{\sigma}(\mu):=\{P_{Z\hat{Z}}\colon P_{Z}=P_{\hat{Z}}=\mu;\,\mathbb{E}[d^{2}(Z,\hat{Z})]\leq\sigma^{2}\}.

(29)

Definition 2
.

Given
\mu
on a metric space
(T,d)
, the rate-distortion function
R_{\mu}(\sigma^{2}):=\inf_{P_{UZ}}I(U;Z)
, where the infimum is over
P_{UZ}
satisfying
P_{Z}=\mu
and
\mathbb{E}[d^{2}(U,Z)]\leq\sigma^{2}
.
Define

i(\sigma):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}P_{Z\hat{Z}}\in\Pi_{\sigma}(\mu)\end{subarray}}I(Z;\hat{Z})
.

Observe that

\displaystyle R_{\mu}(\sigma^{2})\leq i(\sigma)\leq R_{\mu}(\sigma^{2}/4).

(30)

Indeed, the first inequality is obvious from the definition (relaxing the
P_{U}=\mu
constraint in the infimum).
The second holds since given
\mathbb{E}[d^{2}(U,Z)]\leq\sigma^{2}/4
, we can construct
\hat{Z}
such that
\hat{Z}
and
Z
are conditionally i.i.d. given
U
. Then
\mathbb{E}[d^{2}(\hat{Z},Z)]\leq\mathbb{E}[(d(\hat{Z},U)+d(U,Z))^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}
and
I(Z;\hat{Z})\leq I(U;Z)
by the data-processing inequality.
Due to (
30
), our results can be stated in terms of either
\inf_{P_{Z\hat{Z}}\in\Pi_{\sigma}(P_{Z})}I(Z;\hat{Z})
or the rate-distortion function (up to constant),
though we mostly use the latter since it is a better-studied quantity in information theory.

For
Z\sim\mu
,
define

\displaystyle\sigma_{\rm m}(\mu):=\inf_{z\in T}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[d^{2}(Z,t)]},

(31)

abbreviated as
\sigma_{\rm m}
if no confusion.
Clearly
R_{\mu}(\sigma^{2})=0
whenever
\sigma>\sigma_{\rm m}
.
Therefore
\sigma_{\rm m}
can be thought of as a soft version of the diameter of
T
,
since the in the upper limit of the traditional Dudley integral can be set as the diameter.

Definition 3
.

Given a stochastic process
(X_{t}(\omega))_{t\in T}
,
define the
complexity

\displaystyle\mathsf{w}(\mu):=\sup\mathbb{E}[X_{Z}]

(32)

where the supremum is over all
Z=Z(\omega)
satisfying
P_{Z}=\mu
.

Clearly
\mathsf{w}(\mu)
is analogous to the notion of Gaussian complexity of a set.

Theorem 1
.

If
(X_{t})_{t\in T}
is a centered sub-Gaussian process on a countable set
T
, and
\mu
is a distribution on
T
for which
\sigma_{\rm m}<\infty
, then

\displaystyle\mathsf{w}(\mu)\leq 48\int_{0}^{\sigma_{\rm m}}\sqrt{R_{\mu}(\sigma^{2})}d\sigma.

(33)

The proof is given in Section
4
.
It will be seen in Lemma
6
that if the right side of (
33
) is finite, then
\mathbb{E}[|X_{Z}|]<\infty
, so no need to worry about absolute integrability on the left side of (
33
).
The constant 48 in the theorem is from the commonly referenced estimate
K<24
in the Dudley integral (
7
) for Gaussian processes (see comments after (
7
) for the extension to the sub-Gaussian case).
Actually, according to
Dudley, 2016
, the best published estimate appears to be
K\leq 4\sqrt{2}
given in
Lifshits, 2012
, which would imply that 48 in Theorem
1
can be improved to
8\sqrt{2}
.

Another type of information-theoretic improvements of traditional generalization bounds, discussed around (
2
), have recently been studied in the literature.
While this type of results are not directly comparable to Theorem
2
,
we can use the lifting approach to obtain a strengthening of Theorem
2
that encompass both improvements.
For simplicity, assume that
|T|<\infty
, and we have the following improvement of Theorem
1
:

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that
(X_{t})_{t\in T}
is a centered sub-Gaussian process,
and let
Y:=(X_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{|T|}
.
Let
Z
be a random variable on
T
arbitrarily correlated with
Y
, and set
\mu:=P_{Z}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{Z}]\leq 48\int_{0}^{\sigma_{\rm m}}\sqrt{R_{\mu}(\sigma^{2})\wedge I(Y;Z)}d\sigma.

(34)

The proof of Theorem
2
is given in Section
4.1
.
Since
\sigma_{\rm m}\lesssim\sup_{t\in T}\|X_{t}\|_{\Psi_{2}}
, it is easy to see that Theorem
2
implies (
2
).
Since the rest of the paper does not concern Theorem
2
, we will not discuss its extension beyond finite
T
.
While Theorem
1
does not cover the case of uncountable
T
,
it is sufficient for our statistical applications
due to the following observation:

Proposition 1
.

If
(X_{t})
is a separable, continuous process, then for
Z\sim\mu
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{Z}]\leq 48\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\sup_{\mu^{\prime}\colon W_{\infty}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\leq\epsilon}\int_{0}^{\sigma_{\rm m}}\sqrt{R_{\mu^{\prime}}(\sigma^{2})}d\sigma.

(35)

The proof of Proposition
1
is in Section
4.2
.
Naturally, we would like to show that the right side of
(
35
) reduces to the right side of (
33
) by dominated convergence,
but this requires showing that

\sqrt{R_{\mu^{\prime}}(\sigma^{2})}
can be dominated by an absolutely integrable function,
which is challenging in the regime of vanishing
\sigma
where the rate-distortion function may grow fast.
While it is possible to impose on certain growth condition for dominated convergence to apply, we will not dive into those complications.
Instead, in many statistical applications, we will see that
R_{\mu^{\prime}}(\sigma^{2})
can controlled using the second moments of
\mu
, so the right side of (
35
) can be directly evaluated using the continuity of the bound in the second moment, without the need to establish the equivalence of the right sides of (
35
) and (
33
).

3.2
Sharpness for Gaussian Processes

While the traditional Dudley integral is not sharp,
somewhat surprisingly, its information-theoretic counterpart, Theorem
1
, is sharp:

Theorem 3
.

In the setting of Theorem
1
,
suppose that
(X_{t})_{t\in T}
is a Gaussian process (with metric defined in (
8
)).
For any
\mu
on
T
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{w}(\mu)\geq c\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{i(\sigma)}d\sigma\geq c\int_{0}^{\sigma_{\rm m}}\sqrt{R_{\mu}(\sigma^{2})}d\sigma

(36)

where

\displaystyle c:=\max_{\tau\in(0,4/5)}\left\{\frac{4-5\tau}{2\sqrt{2}(4-\tau)\ln\frac{4}{\tau}}\right\}\approx 0.0935918,

(37)

numerically evaluated at
\tau=0.23457905
.

Theorem
3
suggests that Theorem
1
may yield sharper results in certain statistical applications where the traditional method based on Dudley’s integral is suboptimal.
Three proofs of Theorem
3
are given in Section
5
:
First, the result can be proved using the lifting argument, by applying the majorizing measure theorem to the
\mu
-type set.
Secondly, we can apply the lifting argument and use Fernique’s theorem that Dudley’s integral is sharp for stationary Gaussian processes, i.e., the case where the metric on
T
is invariant under the action of a transitive group
(see
(
Dudley, 1999
, Cor 2.7.5)
or
(
Fernique, 1975
)
), which preceded Talagrand’s striking proof of the majorizing measure theorem
(
Talagrand, 1987
)
.
Even though
T
in Theorem
3
may not be permutation-invariant,
the lifting argument in our proof lower bounds the expected supremum over the typical set, which satisfies permutation invariance.
The third proof derives an information-theoretic counterpart of the super-Sudakov inequality
(
Talagrand, 1992
;
Van Handel, 2014
)
, and finishes off by purely analytic arguments (without chaining or packing constructions), which gives the constant stated in Theorem
3
.

Classically, equivalent formulations of the majorizing measure theorem,
(
14
)-(
15
), were established by Fernique convexity argument; see
(
Talagrand, 2014
, Chapter 3)

for a proof using combinatorial arguments such as tree packing.
Similarly, the rate-distortion integral can also be expressed in alternative forms by applying the minimax theorem to a certain convex-concave functional;
see Remark
1
for discussions.

We can derive from Theorem
3
the classical the majorizing measure theorem, with an explicit estimate of constant.
Recall
I_{\mu}
defined in (
11
).

Corollary 1
.

For any Gaussian process (not necessarily stationary),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\in T}X_{t}]\geq\bar{c}\inf_{\mu}\sup_{t\in T}I_{\mu}(t).

(38)

Here
\bar{c}:=\sup_{a>1}\frac{\sqrt{1-a^{-2}}}{\frac{2a}{c}+\sqrt{2\pi h(a^{-2})}}\approx 0.0218988

(evaluated at
a=1.4392
),
where
h
is the binary entropy function in nats and

c
was defined in Theorem
3
.

The proof of Corollary
1
is given in Section
6
,
which is essentially based on lower bounding the rate-distortion function by the probability of a set using the data processing inequality.
We have not found a reference on the majoring measure theorem that estimated the constant to compare with Corollary
1
.

Another application of Theorem
3
is sub-Gaussian comparison theorems, which, in statistical applications, sometimes allow us to reduce the problem to simpler “linear” cases where straightforward Cauchy-Schwarz or Hölder inequality arguments apply.

Corollary 2
.

Suppose that
(X_{t}(\omega))_{t\in T}
is a centered sub-Gaussian process on a countable
T
,
with complexity function
\mathsf{w}_{1}
(Definition
3
) and natural metric
d_{1}
.
Suppose that
(G_{t}(\omega^{\prime}))_{t\in T}
is a Gaussian process
with complexity function
\mathsf{w}_{2}
and natural metric
d_{2}
, and
d_{1}
is dominated by
d_{2}
.
Then we have
\mathsf{w}_{1}(\mu)\lesssim\mathsf{w}_{2}(\mu)
for any
\mu
on
T
.

The proof immediately follows from Theorem
1
-
3
, since the rate-distortion functions under the two metrics can be directly compared.
Corollary
2
can be viewed as a “soft” version of (
13
) (in fact, it is easy to see that it implies (
13
)).
The Euclidean special case of Corollary
2
is of particular interest in practice:

Corollary 3
.

If
T
is a countable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and the covariance matrix
\cov(Z)=\Sigma
,
and the natural metric on
T
for a centered sub-Gaussian process
(X_{t})_{t\in T}
(see (
5
)) is dominated by the Euclidean metric,
then for any
P_{Z}
on
T
,
we have
\mathbb{E}[X_{Z}]\lesssim\tr(\sqrt{\Sigma})
.

Proof.

Consider a Gaussian process
(G_{t})_{t\in T}
, where
G_{t}=\left<G,t\right>
and
G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{N})
. The natural metric for this Gaussian process coincides with the Euclidean metric.
Then by Cauchy-Schwarz we have
\mathbb{E}[G_{Z}]=\mathbb{E}[\left<\Sigma^{1/4}G,\Sigma^{-1/4}Z\right>]\leq\tr(\sqrt{\Sigma})
.
The claim then follows from Corollary
2
.
∎

Although Corollary
3
looks like a simple Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, it would not be true if sub-Gaussianity is replaced by a second-moment bound.
More precisely, “the natural metric on
T
for a centered sub-Gaussian process
(X_{t})_{t\in T}
is dominated by the Euclidean metric”
cannot be replaced by
“
(X_{t})
is a stochastic process satisfying
\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}-X_{t^{\prime}}|^{2}]\lesssim\|t-t^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}
”.
Indeed, consider
T
as a maximal
\epsilon
-packing of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where
N=3
,
and suppose that
X_{t}
is an independent zero mean random variable with
\epsilon^{2}
variance for each
t\in T
.
Then it is true that
\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}-X_{t^{\prime}}|^{2}]\lesssim\|t-t^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}
.
However, for fixed
N=3
and vanishing
\epsilon
,
it is possible that
\mathbb{E}[X_{Z}]=\mathbb{E}[\max_{t\in T}X_{t}]\asymp\epsilon\sqrt{|T|}\asymp\epsilon^{1-N/2}
diverges, for certain heavy-tailed
X_{t}
.
On the other hand, since
Z
is in the unit ball,
\tr(\sqrt{\Sigma})=O(1)
as
\epsilon
vanishes, confirming that this is a counterexample.

3.3
Tail Bounds for ERM

In this section we consider processes in the form of an inner product in
\ell_{2}
.
In this case, the complexity of an index distribution
\mu
(Definition
3
) is written as

\displaystyle\mathsf{w}(\mu):=\sup_{P_{YZ}\in\Pi(\mu,\gamma)}\mathbb{E}[\left<Y,Z\right>],

(39)

where
\gamma
is a given distribution on
\ell_{2}
, which is assumed to have zero mean in this section.
We may denote
\mathsf{w}(\mu)
by

\mathsf{w}(\mu;\gamma)
to make the dependence on
\gamma
explicit.
The following regularity condition will be used in this section:

Definition 4
.

T\subseteq\ell_{2}
is
totally bounded
if the covering number
N(T,\epsilon)
is finite for every
\epsilon>0
.

In empirical process theory, total boundedness is a rather mild condition which is almost always assumed; see for example
Ledoux and Talagrand, 2013
.
It is sometimes also referred to as precompactness in topology.
It is also equivalent to

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{i>n}t(i)^{2}=0.

(40)

Indeed, if (
40
) is true, then for every
\epsilon>0
there exists
n
such that
\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{i>n}t(i)^{2}<(\epsilon/4)^{2}
.
We can cover the projection of
T
onto the first
n
coordinates with finitely many balls of radius
\epsilon/2
, whose centers are the projections of
t_{1},\dots,t_{M}\in T
.
Then
B(t_{1},\epsilon),\dots,B(t_{M},\epsilon)
also cover
T
.
Conversely, total boundedness implies that every sequence in
T
has a convergent subsequence, which implies (
40
).

Definition 5
.

Suppose that
T
is a subset of
\ell_{2}
.
We call
\mathsf{G}\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
a
width-moment function
of
T
if for any random vector
Z\in T
satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\|Z\|_{2}^{2}]\leq E
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{w}(P_{Z})\leq\mathsf{G}(E).

(41)

Consider the setting of the empirical risk minimization problem in Section
2.2

(either the mean estimation or the regression problem).
Our main result, for
\gamma
satisfying the log-Sobolev inequality, is the following:

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that
m\in T
, where
T
is a closed, totally bounded subset of
\ell_{2}
, and
T-T
has a width-moment function
\mathsf{G}
.
Assume that
\gamma
has zero mean and satisfies the log-Sobolev inequality (
17
).
Let
\psi(\lambda):=\ln\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda\|m-\hat{m}\|_{2}^{2})]
.
Then for any
\lambda\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\psi(\lambda)\leq\sup_{\epsilon>0}\left\{-\epsilon+\frac{2\lambda}{\sqrt{n}}\mathsf{G}\left(\frac{\epsilon+\psi(\lambda)}{\lambda}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{8\epsilon\lambda(\epsilon+\psi(\lambda))}{n}}\right\}.

(42)

The proof of Theorem
4
is given in Section
7
.
The log-Sobolev inequality assumption in Theorem
4
can in fact be weakened to sub-Gaussianity.
In that case, define

\displaystyle\mathsf{w}_{\rm g}(\mu):=\sup_{P_{YZ}\in\Pi(\gamma_{\rm g},\mu)}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}Y(i)Z(i)\right],

(43)

where
\gamma_{\rm g}
denotes the distribution of an infinite sequence of i.i.d. standard normal random variables,
and the infinite sum is defined in terms of convergence in second moment.
Similarly, define

\mathsf{G}_{\rm g}(E)
by replacing the left side of (
41
) with
\mathsf{w}_{\rm g}(P_{Z})
.
Note that if
\gamma
satisfies the log-Sobolev inequality (hence also sub-Gaussian), then Corollary
2
implies that

\mathsf{w}_{\rm g}
and
\mathsf{G}_{\rm g}
dominate
\mathsf{w}
and
\mathsf{G}
respectively.
We have the following result:

Theorem 5
.

In the setting of Theorem
4
, suppose that
\gamma
is only assumed to have zero-mean and satisfy
\|Y\|_{\Psi_{2}}\leq 1
,
Y\sim\gamma
.
Assume that
\mathsf{G}_{\rm g}(\cdot)
is continuous.
Then for any
\lambda\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\psi(\lambda)\leq\sup_{\epsilon>0}\left\{-\frac{\epsilon}{C}+\frac{2K^{\prime}C\lambda}{\sqrt{n}}\mathsf{G}_{\rm g}\left(\frac{\epsilon+\psi(\lambda)}{\lambda}\right)+K^{\prime}C\sqrt{\frac{8\epsilon\lambda(\epsilon+\psi(\lambda))}{n}}\right\},

(44)

where
C,K^{\prime}>0
are universal constants in Lemma
2
and (
10
), respectively.

The proof of Theorem
5
is given in Section
7.2
.
Similar to the remark following Lemma
2
, Theorem
5
assumes a weaker condition on
\gamma
than (
4
), but may yield a weaker bound if
\mathsf{G}_{\rm g}
is larger than
\mathsf{G}
(for example, in the case where
Y
has i.i.d. coordinates following the uniform distribution on
[-1,1]
).

3.4
ERM for the Sobolev Ellipsoid

We apply the general technique in Section
3.3
to Sobolev ellipsoids,
which plays a prominent role in nonparametric statistics
(
Van de Geer, 1990
;
Wainwright, 2019
;
Tsybakov, 2009
)
.
The precise problem of estimation over a Sobolev ellipsoid was previously studied in
Wei et al., 2020
.
Generally, an ellipsoid in
\ell_{2}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}:=\left\{t\colon\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{t_{i}^{2}}{a_{i}^{2}}\leq 1\right\},

(45)

where
a_{1},a_{2},\dots
is a decreasing sequence of nonnegative numbers.
This is recurring example in
Talagrand, 2014
,
in particular an example showing that Dudley’s integral is loose.
A Sobolev ellipsoid is an ellipsoid with
a_{i}=i^{-\beta}
for some
\beta>0
.
By considering
t(1),t(2),\dots
as the Fourier coefficients of a regression function from the
\beta
-Sobolev space, we see that the Sobolev ellipsoid can be identified with a nonparametric function class

(
Tsybakov, 2009
)
.
In Section
9.1
, we will review the supremum of a Gaussian process indexed by
\mathcal{E}
, and show that the rate-distortion integral yields a sharp bound.

Consider ERM in the setting of Section
2.2
,
where
T=\phi(\mathcal{E})
is the image of the Sobolev ellipsoid (for
\beta>1/2
, where
1/2
is the critical exponent for ERM) under a 1-Lipschitz map
\phi\colon\ell_{2}\to\ell_{2}
.
The purpose of introducing
\phi
is that if
\phi
is linear, an elementary proof exists,
while the rate-distortion integral approach can handle more general settings.
Furthermore, we assume that
Y
is a random vector in
\ell_{2}
satisfying
\|Y\|_{\Psi_{2}}\leq 1
.
Under these assumptions, we have the following main result:

Theorem 6
.

There exists a function
c(\beta)>0
satisfying
c(\beta)=O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\beta-1}})
as
\beta\downarrow\frac{1}{2}
, and a universal constant
b>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\|m-\hat{m}\|_{2}>\sqrt{c(\beta)}n^{-\frac{\beta}{2\beta+1}}]\leq\exp(-bc(\beta)n^{\frac{1}{2\beta+1}}).

(46)

Theorem
6
shows that ERM achieves square error
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\beta-1}})n^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}}
with high probability.
Furthermore, from the proof of Theorem
6
we can also see that the expected generalization error,

\mathbb{E}[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\chi_{m-\hat{m}}]
,
also converges at the rate
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\beta-1}})n^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}}
.
For comparison, the traditional techniques will only prove an upper bound at the rate
O(\frac{1}{2\beta-1})n^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}}
(see our analysis in Remark
3
), which has the optimal exponent of
n
,
but has a worse preconstant.

We present two approaches towards Theorem
6
in Section
9.2
.
The first one does not use any new tools introduced in this paper, but only works for a special case of Theorem
6
where
\phi
is the identity map and
Y
satisfies the stronger log-Sobolev inequality:
We use the Cauchy-Schwarz argument to bound the mean of the square error,
and then use a concentration inequality to control the deviation from the mean.
This approach is a slightly streamlined version of the method in
Chatterjee, 2014
;
Wei et al., 2020
.
The second proof follows the general recipe in Section
3.3
, and works for general sub-Gaussian
Y
and nonlinear Lipschitz
\phi
,
where the rate-distortion integral is used to bound
\mathsf{G}_{\rm g}
.

3.5
ERM for Sparse Regression

This section provides new upper bounds for empirical risk minimization for the weak
\ell_{q}
ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined in (
4
),
extending prior results for the
\ell_{q}
ball
(
3
).
Consider a special case of the general regression model in Section
2.2
,
where
T
is chosen as a linear transform of
wB_{q}(r)
.
More precisely, suppose that the columns of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{X}
have unit
\ell_{2}
norm,

\beta^{*}\in wB_{q}(r)
,

q\in(0,1)
,
and

Y=\mathbb{X}\beta^{*}+w
, where
w
is a zero mean random vector satisfying
\|w\|_{\Psi_{2}}\leq 1
.
Let
\hat{\beta}:=\argmin_{\beta\in wB_{q}(r)}\|Y-\mathbb{X}\beta\|_{2}
.
Throughout, we set

\displaystyle R:=r^{q},

(47)

and we caution the reader that some prior work, including
Raskutti et al., 2011
, uses the notation
B_{q}(R)=B_{q}(r^{q})
to refer to what we denote by
B_{q}(r)
.
Applying the technique in Section
3.3
, we obtain the following result:

Theorem 7
.

There exists
C_{q}>0
(depending only on
q\in(0,1)
) such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\|m-\hat{m}\|_{2}^{2}>C_{q}(\frac{\ln d}{n})^{\frac{2-q}{2}}R\right]\leq\exp(-(\ln d)^{\frac{2-q}{2}}Rn^{\frac{q}{2}}).

(48)

The proof of Theorem
7
is given in Section
10
, which is based on the rate-distortion integral and the general technique for tail bounds in Section
3.3
.

Previously,
Raskutti et al., 2011
established the sharp convergence of ERM when

\beta\in B_{q}(r)
where
q\in(0,1]
.
The analysis for the case of
\beta\in B_{1}(r)
follows by a simple Hölder inequality argument (see
Raskutti et al., 2011
),
whereas the
q<1
case was proved by the slicing technique,
based on the Dudley integral where the covering numbers were bounded using deep results from the convex geometry
(
Carl and Pajor, 1988
;
Guedon and Litvak, 2000
)
.
In contrast, we prove Theorem
7
using the rate-distortion integral,
where the rate-distortion function (a priori smaller than the log covering number, hence easier to upper bound) is upper bounded using purely information-theoretic tools,
without invoking the convex geometry literature.
Furthermore, Theorem
7
shows that the minimax rate for
wB_{q}(r)
is the same as
B_{q}(r)
when
q\in(0,1)
,
a nontrivial extension of the result of
Raskutti et al., 2011
which appears to be beyond the previous techniques.
The
q=1
case is somewhat special: by considering
\mathbb{X}
with identical rows, we can see that the rate for
wB_{1}(r)
can be worse than that of
B_{1}(r)
by a logarithmic factor.
On the other hand, in the special case where
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{X}
is the identity,
the rate
(\frac{\ln d}{n})^{\frac{2-q}{2}}R
is achievable for
wB_{q}(r)
for all
q\in(0,2)
; see
Rigollet and Hütter, 2023
;
Donoho and Johnstone, 1994
;
Zhang, 2010
.

4
Proof of the Rate-Distortion Integral

In this section we prove Theorem
1
, Theorem
2
, and Proposition
1
.

4.1
Proof in the Finite Case

Recall the following basic property of the sub-Gaussian distribution:

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that
X_{1},X_{2}
are independent random variables, and
\|X_{i}\|_{\Psi_{2}}\leq K_{i}
,
i=1,2
.
Then
\|X_{1}+X_{2}\|_{\Psi_{2}}\leq\sqrt{K_{1}^{2}+K_{2}^{2}}
.

Proof.

By Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\frac{X_{1}^{2}+X_{2}^{2}}{K_{1}^{2}+K_{2}^{2}})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\frac{X_{1}^{2}}{K_{1}^{2}+K_{2}^{2}})\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(\frac{X_{2}^{2}}{K_{1}^{2}+K_{2}^{2}})\right]

(49)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}^{\frac{K_{1}^{2}}{K_{1}^{2}+K_{2}^{2}}}\left[\exp(\frac{X_{1}^{2}}{K_{1}^{2}})\right]\mathbb{E}^{\frac{K_{2}^{2}}{K_{1}^{2}+K_{2}^{2}}}\left[\exp(\frac{X_{2}^{2}}{K_{2}^{2}})\right]

(50)

\displaystyle\leq 2.

(51)

∎

We also need the following estimate of the covering number:

Lemma 4
.

For given
\sigma>0
independent of
N
, we have

\displaystyle NR_{\mu}(4\sigma^{2})+o(N)\leq\ln N(\mathcal{C},2\sqrt{N}\sigma)\leq NR_{\mu}(\sigma^{2})+o(N)

(52)

where
o(N)
hides constants depending on
P_{Y}
and
\sigma
.

Proof.

For any
t^{N}\in\mathcal{C}
, a standard application of Sanov’s theorem
(
Csiszár and Körner, 2011
, Chapter 1)
shows

\displaystyle\ln\frac{1}{\mu^{\otimes N}(B(t^{N},\sqrt{N}\sigma))}=NR_{\mu}(\sigma^{2})+o(N)

(53)

where the implicit constant in
o(N)
may depend on
\mu
.
Let
P(\mathcal{C},2\sqrt{N}\sigma)
denote the packing number, i.e. the maximal number of points
\mathcal{C}
separated by distance at least
2\sqrt{N}\sigma
.
Then
N(\mathcal{C},2\sqrt{N}\sigma)\leq P(\mathcal{C},2\sqrt{N}\sigma)
(see e.g.
Van Handel, 2014
),
and standard volume estimates yields
P(\mathcal{C},2\sqrt{N}\sigma)\leq\frac{1}{\min_{t^{N}\in\mathcal{C}}\mu^{\otimes N}(B(t^{N},\sqrt{N}\sigma))}
, proving the second inequality in (
52
).
Moreover, by a volume argument we also have
N(\mathcal{C},2\sqrt{N}\sigma)\max_{t^{N}\in\mathcal{C}}\mu^{\otimes N}(B(t^{N},2\sqrt{N}\sigma))\geq\mu^{\otimes N}(\mathcal{C})=\exp(o(N))
.
Then using (
53
) with
\sigma
replaced by
2\sigma
, we obtain the first inequality in (
52
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
1
(for finite
T
).

For any integer
N>0
, let
\mathcal{C}
be the
P_{Z}
-typical set

(
Thomas and Joy, 2006
)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}:=\mathcal{C}_{N}:=\{z^{N}\in T^{N}\colon\|\widehat{P}_{z^{N}}-\mu\|_{1}\leq N^{-1/3}\}

(54)

where
\widehat{P}_{z^{N}}
denotes the empirical distribution of
z^{N}
,
and
\|\widehat{P}_{z^{N}}-\mu\|_{1}:=\sum_{z\in T}|\widehat{P}_{z^{N}}(z)-\mu(z)|
can in fact be replaced by any norms, which are equivalent up to constants due to finiteness of
T
.
Moreover, let
n:=|T|
.
Without loss of generality, we can identify
T
with the standard basis vectors
e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{n}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and define
Y_{i}:=X_{e_{i}}
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Then the process can be represented by inner product:

\displaystyle X_{z}=\left<Y,z\right>,\quad\forall z\in T.

(55)

It is not necessary to adopt the inner product representation for this proof, but it helps making the notations clear.
Suppose that
P_{YZ}\in\Pi(P_{Y},\mu)
is arbitrary, and let
(Y^{N},Z^{N})\sim P_{YZ}^{\otimes N}
.
We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left<Y,Z\right>]=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\frac{1}{N}\left<Y^{N},Z^{N}\right>].

(56)

By Proposition
2
,
(\left<Y^{N},z^{N}\right>)_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{C}}
is a sub-Gaussian process with respect to the metric

d_{N}(z^{N},\hat{z}^{N}):=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}d^{2}(z_{i},\hat{z}_{i})}
.
Let
1_{\mathcal{C}}
be the indicator of the event that
Z^{N}\in\mathcal{C}
.
Since

\left<Y^{N},Z^{N}\right>1_{\mathcal{C}}\leq\max_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{C}}\left<Y^{N},z^{N}\right>1_{\mathcal{C}}=\max_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{C}}\left<Y^{N},z^{N}\right>-\max_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{C}}\left<Y^{N},z^{N}\right>1_{\mathcal{C}^{c}}
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left<Y^{N},Z^{N}\right>]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\left<Y^{N},Z^{N}\right>1_{\mathcal{C}^{c}}]+\mathbb{E}[\max_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{C}}\left<Y^{N},z^{N}\right>]-\mathbb{E}[\max_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{C}}\left<Y^{N},z^{N}\right>1_{\mathcal{C}^{c}}]

(57)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\max_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{C}}\left<Y^{N},z^{N}\right>]+\mathbb{E}[\max_{z^{N},\hat{z}^{N}\in\mathcal{Z}^{N}}\left<Y^{N},z^{N}-\hat{z}^{N}\right>1_{\mathcal{C}^{c}}].

(58)

The last term above has vanishing contribution:
Indeed, note that for each
z^{n},\hat{z}^{n}\in\mathcal{Z}^{N}
, we have

\|\left<Y^{N},z^{N}-\hat{z}^{N}\right>\|_{\Psi_{2}}\leq C\sqrt{N}

for some
C
independent of
N
(may depend on
P_{YZ}
).
Taking the max over
\exp(O(N))
such sub-Gaussian random variables will increase the sub-Gaussian norm by a factor of no more than
\sqrt{N}

(
Vershynin, 2018
)
, so

\displaystyle\|\max_{z^{N},\hat{z}^{N}\in\mathcal{Z}^{N}}\left<Y^{N},z^{N}-\hat{z}^{N}\right>\|_{\Psi_{2}}\leq CN.

(59)

Moreover, by Hoeffding’s inequality we also have

\mathbb{E}[1_{\mathcal{C}^{c}}]=\exp(-\Theta(N^{1/3}))
,
so we can use Cauchy-Schwarz (or
(
Talagrand, 2014
, Lemma 7.8.4)
) to show the second expectation in (
58
) is
o(N)
.
Therefore, we have verified that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left<Y,Z\right>]\leq\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}[\max_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{C}}\left<Y^{N},z^{N}\right>].

(60)

Furthermore, applying Dudley’s integral (see (
7
) and the subsequent comments) to
\mathcal{C}
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\max_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{C}}\left<Y^{N},z^{N}\right>]\leq 24\int_{0}^{\Delta_{\rm m}}\sqrt{\ln N(\mathcal{C},2\sqrt{N}\sigma)}2\sqrt{N}d\sigma

(61)

where

\Delta_{\rm m}:=\sup_{N\geq 1}\frac{{\rm diam}(\mathcal{C}_{N})}{2\sqrt{N}}<\infty
.
Now we multiply
1/N
to (
61
), take
N\to\infty
, and apply Lemma
4
.
We can apply dominated convergence,
since

\frac{1}{N}\sqrt{\ln N(\mathcal{C},2\sqrt{N}\sigma)}2\sqrt{N}\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{N}}\sqrt{\ln(n^{N})}=2\sqrt{\ln n}
, which is independent of
N
and absolutely integrable on
\sigma\in[0,\Delta_{\rm m}]
.
We have thus proved Theorem
1
for finite
T
.
∎

The proof of Theorem
2
will be almost identical to the proof of Theorem
1
, but we will use an additional technique about the projection to the set of sequences of a given type. More precisely:

Lemma 5
.

Let
\mu
be a rational distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Fix
N\geq 1
such that
N\mu
is integer.
For any
y^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{nN}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\min_{s^{N}\colon\widehat{P}_{s^{N}}=\mu}\|y^{N}-s^{N}\|_{2}=W_{2}(\widehat{P}_{y^{N}},\mu).

(62)

Proof.

The
\geq
part is obvious from the definition.
For the
\leq
part, fix an arbitrary
\bar{s}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{nN}
satisfying
\widehat{P}_{\bar{s}^{N}}=\mu
.
Denote the supports of
\widehat{P}_{y^{N}}
and
\mu
be
\mathcal{A}

and
\mathcal{B}
, respectively.
Consider the following function

\displaystyle F(M)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\|y_{i}-\bar{s}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}M_{i,j}

(63)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{A},\,s\in\mathcal{B}}\|y-s\|_{2}^{2}\sum_{i\colon y_{i}=y}\sum_{j\colon\bar{s}_{j}=s}M_{i,j}

(64)

on
\mathcal{S}
, which is defined as the set of doubly stochastic matrices
M\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
.
We can see from (
64
) that
\inf_{M\in\mathcal{S}}F(M)\leq W_{2}^{2}(\widehat{P}_{y^{N}},\mu)
.
Since
F
is linear, by the Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem, there exists a permutation matrix
M
achieving
\inf_{M\in\mathcal{S}}F(M)
.
This permutation sends
\bar{s}^{N}
to an
s^{N}
, achieving
\inf_{M\in\mathcal{S}}F(M)=\frac{1}{N}\|y^{N}-s^{N}\|_{2}
.
This proves the
\leq
part.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

As in the proof of Theorem
1
, we can identify
T=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}
as the standard basis vectors, so that
X_{z}=\left<Y,z\right>
.
The assumption of zero-mean process implies that
\mathbb{E}[Y]=0
.
Then, shifting
Z\sim\mu
by a deterministic vector does not change the value of
\mathbb{E}[\left<Y,Z\right>]
, so we can assume without loss of generality that
\mathbb{E}[Z]=0
.
Let us assume that
P_{YZ}
is rational; the general case will then following by approximation.
Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}:=\{N\geq 1\colon\textrm{$NP_{YZ}$ is integer}\}.

(65)

For each
N\in\mathcal{I}
, let
\mathcal{C}_{\mu}
denote the
\mu
-type set, consisting of all sequences of type
\mu=P_{Z}
.
Select

\displaystyle L:=\exp(NI(Y;Z)+N^{1/3})

(66)

sequences from
\mathcal{C}_{\mu}
uniformly at random with replacement, and call this random set
\mathcal{A}
.
Let
Y^{N}\sim P_{Y}^{\otimes N}
.
Then we will show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left<Y,Z\right>]\leq\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\sup_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{N}\left<Y^{N},z^{N}\right>],

(67)

where the limit is for
N\in\mathcal{I}
.
Indeed, set

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle:=\{(y^{N})\colon\widehat{P}_{y^{N}}=P_{Y}\};

(68)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle:=\{(y^{N},z^{N})\colon\widehat{P}_{y^{N}z^{N}}=P_{YZ}\},

(69)

and for any
y^{N}\in\mathcal{B}
, define
\mathcal{D}(y^{N}):=\{z^{N}\colon(y^{N},z^{N})\in\mathcal{D}\}
.
A standard result states that (see Lemma
3
, or
(
Csiszár and Körner, 2011
, Chapter 1)
):

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\ln|\mathcal{D}(y^{N})|=H(Z|Y)+O(\frac{\ln N}{N}),\quad\forall y^{N}\in\mathcal{B}.

(70)

Let
\pi
be the projection from
\mathbb{R}^{nN}
to
\mathcal{B}
(i.e., map to the closest point in
\mathcal{B}
, with ties broken arbitrarily).
Define
\mathcal{E}
as the event that
\mathcal{D}(\pi(Y^{N}))\cap\mathcal{A}=\emptyset
.
Then, (
70
) implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}]=\left(1-\exp(-NI(Y;Z)+O(\ln N))\right)^{L}\leq\exp(-CN^{1/3})

(71)

for some
C>0
depending only on
P_{YZ}
, and for all
N\in\mathcal{I}
.
From the definitions it is clear that

\displaystyle\max_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{N}\left<\pi(Y^{N}),z^{N}\right>1_{\mathcal{E}^{c}}\geq\mathbb{E}[\left<Y,Z\right>]1_{\mathcal{E}^{c}}

(72)

for all
N\in\mathcal{I}
almost surely.
Since
\mathbb{E}[Z]=0
is assumed, we see that 0 belongs to the convex hull of
\mathcal{C}_{\mu}
, therefore

\displaystyle\max_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{N}\left<\pi(Y^{N}),z^{N}\right>\geq\max_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{N}\left<\pi(Y^{N}),z^{N}\right>1_{\mathcal{E}^{c}}

(73)

almost surely.
Furthermore,

\displaystyle\max_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{N}\left<Y^{N},z^{N}\right>\geq\max_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{N}\left<\pi(Y^{N}),z^{N}\right>-\max_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{N}\left<\pi(Y^{N})-Y^{N},z^{N}\right>

(74)

From Lemma
5
, we have

\displaystyle\max_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{N}\left<\pi(Y^{N})-Y^{N},z^{N}\right>

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\|\pi(Y^{N})-Y^{N}\|_{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\max_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{A}}\|z^{N}\|_{2}

(75)

\displaystyle\leq W_{2}(\mu,\widehat{P}_{Y^{N}})\cdot O(1)

(76)

which converges to 0 in expectation as
N\in\mathcal{I}
tends to infinity.
Thus (
67
) is verified by collecting the bounds above.

It remains to apply the Dudley inequality to the right side of (
67
).
Since
\mathcal{A}
is contained in the set
\mathcal{C}
in Lemma
4
, the upper bound part of Lemma
4
also applies to covering numbers of
\mathcal{A}
.
Furthermore, we have the trivial bound
|\mathcal{A}|\leq L
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\ln N(\mathcal{A},2\sqrt{N}\sigma)\leq R_{\mu}(\sigma^{2})\wedge I(Y;Z).

(77)

The claim then follows by applying dominated convergence (see proof of Theorem
1
).
∎

4.2
Beyond the Finite Case

In this section we extend the proof of Theorem
1
for finite
T
to the countable case by a limiting argument,
and also prove Proposition
1
.
We first prove an auxiliary result about absolute integrability of
|X_{Z}|
, which will be used later to apply dominated convergence.

Lemma 6
.

Suppose that
(X_{t})_{t\in T}
is a zero-mean sub-Gaussian process on a countable set
T
.
Let
Z\sim\mu
be a random variable on
T
,
for which
\sigma_{\rm m}:=\sigma_{\rm m}(\mu)<\infty
.

•

Consider
\bar{T}:=T\times\{-1,1\}
,
and a process
(\bar{X}_{\bar{t}})_{\bar{t}\in\bar{T}}
, where

\bar{X}_{\bar{t}}:=sX_{t}
for
\bar{t}=(t,s)
.
Let
\bar{d}
be the natural metric for this sub-Gaussian process on
\bar{T}
,
and
\bar{Z}:=(Z,S)
be a random variable on
\bar{T}
, satisfying
P_{Z}=\mu
.
Define

\bar{\sigma}_{\rm m}^{2}:=\inf_{\bar{t}\in\bar{T}}\mathbb{E}[\bar{d}^{2}(\bar{Z},\bar{t})]
,
and let
\bar{R}_{P_{\bar{Z}}}(\cdot)
be the rate-distortion function for
\bar{Z}
on
(\bar{T},\bar{d})
.
Then

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\bar{\sigma}_{\rm m}}\sqrt{\bar{R}_{P_{\bar{Z}}}(\sigma^{2})}d\sigma\leq\int_{0}^{\sigma_{\rm m}}\sqrt{R_{Z}(\sigma^{2})}d\sigma+\sqrt{2\ln 2}\sigma_{\rm m}.

(78)

•

If

\int_{0}^{\sigma_{\rm m}}\sqrt{R_{\mu}(\sigma^{2})}d\sigma<\infty
, then

\mathbb{E}[|X_{Z}|]<\infty
.

It is interesting to compare
Lemma
6
with the classical observation that for symmetric processes,
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\in T}|X_{t}|]<\infty
is equivalent to
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\in T}X_{t}]<\infty
, both equivalent to boundedness of the process in the Gaussian case; see
(
Talagrand, 1987
, p2)
(
Talagrand, 2014
, Exercise 2.2.2.)
and the references therein.

Proof.

We first prove (
78
).
Clearly, the natural metric for the new process satisfies
\bar{d}((t,s),(t^{\prime},s))=d(t,t^{\prime})
, for any
s\in\{-1,+1\}
and
t,t^{\prime}\in T
.
Now suppose that
P_{\bar{Z}}=P_{Z,S}
is an arbitrary distribution on
\bar{T}
.
We can assume without loss of generality that there is
t_{0}\in T
satisfying
X_{t_{0}}\equiv 0

and
\sigma_{\rm m}=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[d^{2}(t_{0},Z)]}
.
By the triangle inequality we can show that

\bar{d}(\bar{Z},(t_{0},+1))\leq d(Z,t_{0})+\|2X_{t_{0}}\|_{\Psi_{2}}=d(Z,t_{0})
,
implying that
\bar{\sigma}_{\rm m}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[\bar{d}^{2}(\bar{Z},(t_{0},+1))]<\sigma_{\rm m}^{2}
.
In addition, suppose that
U
is any random variable such that
R_{P_{Z}}(\sigma^{2})=I(U;Z)
and

It remains to prove the second claim.

\displaystyle\leq I(U_{k},E;Z_{k})

Now applying the result of Theorem

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[{\rm sign}(X_{Z_{k}})X_{Z_{k}}]

5.1
Proof via the Majorizing Measure Theorem

We first give a proof of the lower bound direction assuming a version of the majorizing measure theorem, i.e. the
\delta_{2}
functional defined in (

) lower bounds the supremum of a Gaussian process.
It is relatively easy (compared to the proof of the majorizing measure theorem) to show the equivalence of
\delta_{2}
and
\gamma_{2}
up to universal constants,
and the direction of
\gamma_{2}\gtrsim\delta_{2}
can be found in

(
Talagrand, 2014

We first consider the case where
n:=|T|<\infty
.
Recall the representation of
X_{z}
as an inner product in (
55
),
which embeds
Y,Z
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

is a normal vector,
and recall the typical set

defined in (

Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}:=\{y^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{nN}\colon W_{2}(P_{Y},\widehat{P}_{y^{N}})\leq N^{-\frac{1}{2n}}\}.

(92)

Then by the Wasserstein law of large numbers,
\mathbb{P}[Y^{N}\in\mathcal{D}]=1-o(1)

Given

\mu

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{Z}]

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}[\sup_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{C}}\left<Y^{N},z^{N}\right>1_{Y^{N}\in\mathcal{D}}]

\displaystyle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}[\sup_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{C}}\left<Y^{N},z^{N}\right>]

\displaystyle\gtrsim\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\delta_{2}(\mathcal{C})

(95)

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\inf_{t\in\mathcal{C}}I_{\mu^{\otimes N}}(t).

(96)

The above steps are justified below:
to show (

for any

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sup_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{C}}\left<y^{N},z^{N}\right>

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\sup_{P_{Y^{\prime}Z^{\prime}}}\mathbb{E}[\left<Y^{\prime},Z^{\prime}\right>]

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\sup_{P_{Y^{\prime}Z^{\prime}}}\frac{\mathbb{E}\|Y^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\|Z^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}-W_{2}^{2}(P_{Y^{\prime}},P_{Z^{\prime}})}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\|Y\|_{2}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\|Z\|_{2}^{2}-W_{2}^{2}(P_{Y},P_{Z})}{2}

The step

Using the estimate in Lemma

We now use a limiting argument to extend the result to the case of
\mu

Assume that

Let

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{Z}]=\mu(T_{k})\mathbb{E}[X_{Z_{k}}].

Moreover, if

achieves the infimum in the definition of

In particular, for any given

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}R_{\mu_{k}}(\sigma^{2})\geq R_{\mu}(2\sigma^{2})

5.2
Proof via Sharpness of Dudley’s Integral

The second proof does not use the majorizing measure theorem, but merely the sharpness of the Dudley’s Integral for stationary processes (
10
), which was due to Fernique
(
Fernique, 1975
)
a decade before the majorizing measure theorem. Recall (
94

\mathbb{E}[X_{Z}]\geq\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}[\sup_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{C}}\left<Y^{N},z^{N}\right>]
, which is further lower bounded (up to a constant) by

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\ln N(\mathcal{C},\sqrt{N}\sigma)}\sqrt{N}d\sigma

In turn, it is further lower bounded by
\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{R_{\mu}(\epsilon^{2})}d\epsilon
,
by invoking
Lemma
4
and Fatou’s Lemma.

5.3
Proof via Super-Sudakov

In this section, we give another proof of Theorem
3
which will yield the constant stated therein.
Traditionally, the majorizing measure theorem was proved by a sophisticated generic chaining argument
(
Talagrand, 1987
;
Talagrand, 2014
)
,
and in the special case of stationary processes by a simpler reverse chaining argument based on a “super Sudakov” theorem
(
Van Handel, 2014
;
Talagrand, 1992
)
.
Here, we derive an information-theoretic counterpart of the super-Sudakov theorem, and then obtains Theorem
3

using purely analytic arguments, without
any combinatorial constructions like packing.

Suppose that
\mu
,
\gamma
are distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\epsilon>0
.
Define

\displaystyle\phi(\epsilon,\mu,\gamma):=\sup_{P_{ZZ^{*}G}}\mathbb{E}[\left<G,Z\right>]

(105)

where the supremum is over
P_{ZZ^{*}G}
satisfying

P_{GZ^{*}}=\gamma\times\mu
,

P_{Z}=\mu
,
and
\mathbb{E}[\|Z-Z^{*}\|_{2}^{2}]\leq\epsilon^{2}
.
The parameter
\epsilon
can be thought of as an analogue of the scale in the reverse chaining argument.
The geometric meaning of
\phi(\epsilon,\mu,\gamma)
is shown by the following:

Lemma 7
.

Consider
\phi
defined in (
105
),
where
\mu
is rational, and
\gamma
has finite second moment.
For each
N
such that
N\mu
is integer,
define

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle:=\{z^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{nN}\colon\widehat{P}_{z^{N}}=\mu\};

(106)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle:=\{z^{N}\in T\colon\|z^{N}-z^{*N}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\sqrt{N}\}.

G^{N}\sim\gamma^{\otimes n}
.
Then for any

, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}[\sup_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{C}}\left<G^{N},z^{N}\right>]=\phi(\epsilon,\mu,\gamma)

(108)

where the limit is along the subsequence for which
N\mu
is integer.

The proof involves constructing coupling distributions and approximation by types,
which uses the following standard result about the transportation polytope:

Lemma 8
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m\times n}
have integer row sums and integer column sums.
Then there exists
B\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{m\times n}
with the same row and column sums as
A
and

\max_{i,j}\,|B_{ij}-A_{ij}|\;\leq\;1.

Proof.

Write
F:=A-\lfloor A\rfloor
, so
F_{ij}\in[0,1)
for all

.
Let
r_{i}:=\sum_{j}F_{ij}
and
c_{j}:=\sum_{i}F_{ij}
.
Because the row and column sums of
A
are integers and
\sum_{j}\lfloor A_{ij}\rfloor,\sum_{i}\lfloor A_{ij}\rfloor
are integers, we have
r_{i},c_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Form the bipartite graph
G
with left vertices
\{1,\dots,m\}
(rows) and right vertices
\{1,\dots,n\}
(columns),
and put an edge
(i,j)
iff
F_{ij}\in(0,1)

If some connected component of

were a tree, it would have a leaf

incident to a unique edge

or

to equal

Hence every component of
G
contains a cycle.

Fix a simple cycle

in
G

Along the edges of
C
, alternately add
+\varepsilon
and
-\varepsilon
to the corresponding entries of
F
(leaving all other entries unchanged), where

\varepsilon\;:=\;\min\{\,F_{ij},\,1-F_{ij}:(i,j)\in C\,\}.

This preserves all row and column sums, since at each vertex on

+\varepsilon
and
-\varepsilon
.
Moreover, all modified entries remain in

, and at least one becomes
0
or

Remove edges for which
F_{ij}
becomes 0 or 1.
Iterating this operation, which terminates in finitely many steps, we obtain a modified matrix

\displaystyle\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}[\max_{z^{N}\in\mathcal{C}(\epsilon_{N})}\left<\pi(G^{N}),z^{N}\right>]

(120)

Lemma 9

For any

\displaystyle\phi(\epsilon_{1})\geq\frac{\epsilon_{2}}{4}\sqrt{2(i(\epsilon_{2})-i(\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{2}/4))}+\phi(\epsilon_{2}/4).

The super-Sudakov inequality, originally proved by Talagrand

Lemma 10

(Super-Sudakov)

Let

be a Gaussian process on a metric space

be an
\epsilon

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\in T}X_{t}]\geq\frac{\epsilon}{4}\sqrt{2\ln|\mathcal{N}|}+\min_{s\in\mathcal{N}}\mathbb{E}[\sup_{t\in T_{s}}X_{t}].

(123)

Proof of Lemma
9
.

It suffices to consider the case of rational
\mu
, as the general case will then follow by approximation by ration
\mu
in the Wasserstein distribution.
Suppose that
N>0
is an integer for which

is integer.
Define

as in (

), and fix arbitrary

.

, define

-packing of

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}|\mu^{\otimes N}(B(\sqrt{N}\epsilon_{2}))\geq\mu^{\otimes N}(B(\sqrt{N}r)).

Lemma 11
.

\displaystyle\sup_{P_{ZG}\in\Pi(\mu,\gamma)}\mathbb{E}[\left<G,Z\right>]\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4\ln\frac{4}{\tau}}\cdot\frac{4-5\tau}{4-\tau}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{i(\epsilon)}d\epsilon.

Proof.

\displaystyle\phi(\epsilon)-\phi(\tfrac{\tau\epsilon}{4})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\tau\epsilon}{4}\sqrt{2i(\tau\epsilon)}-\frac{\tau\epsilon}{4}\sqrt{2i((1-\tfrac{\tau}{4})\epsilon)}.

6
New Proof of the Majorizing Measure Theorem

In this section, we prove Corollary
1
.
First, observe the following auxiliary result, which allows us to rewrite integrals in a “penalized optimization” form:

Lemma 12
.

Suppose that
y\in(0,\infty)
is a decreasing function of
x\in(0,\infty)
.
Then

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{\infty}ydx\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\min_{x}\{\alpha^{-2}x^{2}+y^{2}\}d\alpha\leq 4\int_{0}^{\infty}ydx

(132)

Proof.

For any
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
, there is unique
x(\alpha)
satisfying
\alpha=\frac{x(\alpha)}{y(x(\alpha))}
. Then
x
and
y
can be parameterized by
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
, and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}(\alpha^{-2}x^{2}+y^{2})d\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{\alpha=0}^{\infty}2y^{2}\cdot\frac{ydx-xdy}{y^{2}}

(133)

\displaystyle=4\int_{0}^{\infty}ydx.

(134)

The claim then follows since for any
\alpha
,
\frac{\min_{x\in(0,\infty)}\{\alpha^{-2}x^{2}+y^{2}(x)\}}{\alpha^{-2}x^{2}(\alpha)+y^{2}(x(\alpha))}\in[1/2,1]
.
∎

A similar idea (though in the form of series summation rather than integral) was previously used in proving equivalent forms of the majorizing measure theorem
(
Talagrand, 1994
)
.
Here, we find Lemma
12
very convenient in forming information-theoretic functionals that are
exactly
convex-concave (Remark
1
), and also simplifying evaluations of the rate-distortion integral in applications to ERM.

To prove Corollary
1
, it suffices to consider the case of finite
T
, since the general case (where the lattice supremum in (
38
) is defined as
\sup_{T_{0}\subseteq T,|T_{0}|<\infty}\sup_{t\in T_{0}}
; see
Talagrand, 2014
) will then follows by taking the limit.
Then
T
can be identified with points in
\mathbb{R}^{|T|}
equipped with the
\ell_{2}
metric.
For
i(\epsilon)
defined in (
121
),
Lemma
12
implies

\displaystyle 4\sup_{\mu}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{i(\epsilon)}d\epsilon\geq\sup_{P_{Z}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\min_{P_{Z\hat{Z}}\in\Pi(P_{Z},P_{Z})}\{\alpha^{-2}\mathbb{E}[\|Z-\hat{Z}\|_{2}^{2}]+I(Z;\hat{Z})\}d\alpha

(135)

where both supremums are over distributions on
T

and the minimum is over couplings of
P_{Z}
and
P_{Z}
.
The right side of (
135
) is lower bounded by

\displaystyle\sup_{P_{Z}}\inf_{\mu}\int_{0}^{\infty}\min_{P_{U|Z}}\{\alpha^{-2}\mathbb{E}[\|Z-U\|_{2}^{2}]+D(P_{U|Z}\|\mu|P_{Z})\}d\alpha,

(136)

since equality would be achieved if
\inf_{\mu}
were restricted to
\mu=P_{Z}

and
\min_{P_{U|Z}}
were restricted to
P_{U|Z}
satisfying
P_{U|Z}P_{Z}\in\Pi(P_{Z},P_{Z})
.
Since the relative entropy is jointly convex (i.e.,
D(\frac{\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}}{2}\|\frac{\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}}{2})\leq\frac{1}{2}D(\nu_{1}\|\mu_{1})+\frac{1}{2}D(\nu_{2}\|\mu_{2})
),
we can show that the integral in (
136
) is convex in
\mu
and linear in
P_{Z}
.
Then Sion’s minimax theorem implies that (
136
) equals

\displaystyle\quad\inf_{\mu}\sup_{P_{Z}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\min_{P_{U|Z}}\{\alpha^{-2}\mathbb{E}[\|Z-U\|_{2}^{2}]+D(P_{U|Z}\|\mu|P_{Z})\}d\alpha

\displaystyle=\inf_{\mu}\sup_{z}\int_{0}^{\infty}\min_{P_{U}}\{\alpha^{-2}\mathbb{E}[\|z-U\|_{2}^{2}]+D(P_{U}\|\mu)\}d\alpha.

(137)

Now fix
\mu
,
z
,
\alpha
, and
P_{U}
.
Define

r=a\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\|z-U\|^{2}]}
,
where
U\sim P_{U}
and
a>1
will be specified later.
We obtain, from the data processing inequality,

\displaystyle D(P_{U}\|\mu)\geq d(P_{U}(B(z,r))\|\mu(B(z,r)))

(138)

where
d(a\|b):=a\ln\frac{a}{b}+(1-a)\ln\frac{1-a}{1-b}
denotes the binary relative entropy function.
By the Markov inequality we have
P_{U}(B(z,r))\geq 1-a^{-2}
.
Then we have

\displaystyle d(P_{U}(B(z,r))\|\mu(B(z,r)))\geq(1-a^{-2})\ln\frac{1-a^{-2}}{\mu(B(z,r))}-a^{-2}\ln a^{2}

(139)

because if
\mu(B(z,r))>1-a^{-2}
then the right side of (
139
) is negative and the inequality is obviously true; if
\mu(B(z,r))\leq 1-a^{-2}
then by monotonicity of the binary relative entropy function we have
d(P_{\hat{Z}}(B(z,r))\|\mu(B(z,r)))\geq d(1-a^{-2}\|\mu(B(z,r)))=(1-a^{-2})\ln\frac{1-a^{-2}}{\mu(B(z,r))}+a^{-2}\ln\frac{a^{-2}}{1-\mu(B(z,r))}

and (
139
) also holds.
Now the integral in (
137
) is lower bounded by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\inf_{r>0}\left\{\frac{r^{2}}{a^{2}\alpha^{2}}+\left[(1-a^{-2})\ln\frac{1-a^{-2}}{\mu(B(z,r))}-a^{-2}\ln a^{2}\right]_{+}\right\}d\alpha

(140)

which, by Lemma
12
, is lower bounded by twice of

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{{\rm diam}(T)}\sqrt{\left[(1-a^{-2})\ln\frac{1-a^{-2}}{\mu(B(z,r))}-a^{-2}\ln a^{2}\right]_{+}}\frac{dr}{a}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{{\rm diam}(T)}\sqrt{(1-a^{-2})\ln\frac{1}{\mu(B(z,r))}}\frac{dr}{a}-\int_{0}^{{\rm diam}(T)}\sqrt{h(a^{-2})}\frac{dr}{a}

(141)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{1-a^{-2}}}{a}I_{\mu}(z)-\frac{{\rm diam}(T)}{a}\sqrt{h(a^{-2})}

(142)

where we used the basic inequality
\sqrt{[x-y]_{+}}\geq\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{y}
for
x,y\geq 0
.
Thus Theorem
3
shows that

\displaystyle\frac{2}{c}\mathbb{E}[\sup_{z\in T}X_{z}]\geq\inf_{\mu}\sup_{z}\frac{\sqrt{1-a^{-2}}}{a}I_{\mu}(z)-\frac{{\rm diam}(T)}{a}\sqrt{h(a^{-2})}.

(143)

It is easy to see, by considering two points in
T
, that
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{z\in T}X_{z}]\geq{\rm diam}(T)\mathbb{E}[G\vee 0]=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi}}{\rm diam}(T)
, where
G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2}{c}+\frac{\sqrt{2\pi h(a^{-2})}}{a}\right)\mathbb{E}[\sup_{z\in T}X_{z}]\geq\frac{\sqrt{1-a^{-2}}}{a}\inf_{\mu}\sup_{z}I_{\mu}(z)

(144)

as desired.

Remark 1
.

The minimax inequality step (

)-(

) crucially leverages Lemma

and the joint convexity of the relative entropy.
Specifically, the integral in (

) is convex in
\mu
and linear in

,
and this property is not true for the functional

\int_{0}^{\infty}\min_{P_{U|Z}\colon\mathbb{E}[\|Z-U\|_{2}^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}}\sqrt{D(P_{U|Z}\|\mu|P_{Z})}d\epsilon
before we applied Lemma

.
Moreover, had we not used the relative entropy approach,
then the integral in (

) might be replaced by the functional

\int_{0}^{\infty}\min_{r_{Z}}\{\alpha^{-2}\mathbb{E}[r_{Z}^{2}]+\ln\frac{1}{\mu(B(Z,r_{Z}))}\}d\alpha

.

7
Tail Bounds for the ERM

In this section, we prove the results in Section

.

7.1
Under the Log-Sobolev Inequality

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
4
.
For any
\epsilon>0
, define

\displaystyle s(\epsilon,\mu;\gamma)=\sup_{\nu\colon D(\nu\|\mu)\leq\epsilon}\{\mathsf{w}(\nu;\gamma)+\sqrt{2\epsilon\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|Z\|^{2}]}\}.

(145)

We may abbreviate
\mathsf{w}(\mu;\gamma)
as
\mathsf{w}(\mu)
, or
s(\epsilon,\mu;\gamma)
as
s(\epsilon,\mu)
or
s(\epsilon)
, when there is no confusion.

Our key tool is the following observation, which can be viewed as an information-theoretic counterpart of Lemma
1
.

Lemma 13
.

Suppose that
\mu
is a probability measure supported on a totally bounded set
T\subseteq\ell_{2}
.
Let
\gamma
be a probability measure on
\ell_{2}
which has zero-mean, satisfies the log-Sobolev inequality (
17
),
and such that
\mathbb{E}_{\gamma}[\|Y\|_{2}]<\infty
.
For any
\lambda\geq 0

and any
P_{YZ}
such that
P_{Z}=\mu
and
P_{Y}=\gamma
, we have

\displaystyle\ln\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda\left<Y,Z\right>)]\leq\sup_{\epsilon>0}\{\lambda s(\epsilon,\mu;\gamma)-\epsilon\}.

(146)

Proof.

We first suppose that
\mu
is a rational distribution supported on at most
n
points.
For
N>0
such that
N\mu
is integer,
let
S_{N}
be the set of types of
N
-sequences over the
n
points. Then
|S_{N}|\leq{n+N-1\choose n-1}
which grows polynomially in
N
.
For each
\nu\in S_{N}
, let
C_{\nu}
be the type class of
\nu
.
Let
\xi_{\nu}:=\max_{z^{N}\in C_{\nu}}\left<Y^{N},z^{N}\right>

and

\sigma_{\nu}^{2}:=\max_{z^{N}\in C_{\nu}}\mathbb{E}[\left<Y^{N},z^{N}\right>^{2}]=\max_{z^{N}\in C_{\nu}}\|z^{N}\|_{2}^{2}=N\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|Z\|_{2}^{2}]
.
Using Lemma
1
,
we see that for each
\nu

and
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\xi_{\nu}-\mathbb{E}[\xi_{\nu}]>\sigma_{\nu}\sqrt{2N\epsilon}]\leq\exp(-N\epsilon).

(147)

Therefore,
setting
S_{N}^{\prime}:=\{\nu\in S_{N}\colon D(\nu\|\mu)\leq\epsilon\}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\bigcup_{\nu\in S_{N}^{\prime}}\left\{\xi_{\nu}-\mathbb{E}[\xi_{\nu}]>\sigma_{\nu}\sqrt{2N\epsilon}\right\}\right]\leq|S_{N}|\exp(-N\epsilon)

(148)

which implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\max_{\nu\in S_{N}^{\prime}}\xi_{\nu}>Ns(\epsilon)\right]\leq|S_{N}|\exp(-N\epsilon).

(149)

Let
(Y^{N},Z^{N})\sim P_{YZ}^{\otimes N}
,
and let
\mathcal{E}
be the set of
Y^{N}
in the event in (
149
).
Let
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
be the event that

D(\widehat{P}_{Z^{N}}\|\mu)>\epsilon
.
Note that under
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{c}
, we have
\left<Y^{N},Z^{N}\right>\leq\max_{\nu\in S_{N}^{\prime}}\xi_{\nu}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left<Y^{N},Z^{N}\right>>Ns(\epsilon)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}[\tilde{\mathcal{E}}]+\mathbb{P}\left[\left<Y^{N},Z^{N}\right>>Ns(\epsilon)\cap\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{c}\right]

(150)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}[\tilde{\mathcal{E}}]+\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{E}\cap\tilde{\mathcal{E}}^{c}\right]

(151)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}[\tilde{\mathcal{E}}]+\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{E}\right]

(152)

\displaystyle\leq 2(N+1)^{n}\exp(-N\epsilon)

(153)

where we used Lemma
3
to estimate
\mathbb{P}[\tilde{\mathcal{E}}]
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda\left<Y^{N},Z^{N}\right>)]

\displaystyle=\int_{u=-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}[\left<Y^{N},Z^{N}\right>>u]d\exp(\lambda u)

(154)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(N\lambda s(0))+\int_{\epsilon=0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}[\left<Y^{N},Z^{N}\right>>Ns(\epsilon)]d\exp(\lambda Ns(\epsilon))

(155)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(N\lambda s(0))+2(N+1)^{n}\int_{\epsilon=0}^{+\infty}\exp(-N\epsilon)d\exp(\lambda Ns(\epsilon)).

(156)

Since the support of
\mu
is finite, we see that
s(\epsilon)\lesssim_{\mu}\sqrt{\epsilon}+1
, which implies that the integral in (
156
) is finite, and

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\ln\int_{\epsilon=0}^{+\infty}\exp(-N\epsilon)d\exp(\lambda Ns(\epsilon))\leq\sup_{\epsilon>0}\{\lambda s(\epsilon)-\epsilon\}.

(157)

Therefore, (
156
) shows that

\displaystyle\ln\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda\left<Y,Z\right>)]=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\ln\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda\left<Y^{N},Z^{N}\right>)]\leq\sup_{\epsilon>0}\{\lambda s(\epsilon)-\epsilon\}.

(158)

Next, we extend the result to the case where
T
is a bounded set in a finite-dimensional Euclidean space. Note that if
\mu
has bounded support, we can find a sequence
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots
converging to
\mu
in the
W_{\infty}
distance (on the metric space
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\ell_{2})
), such that
\mu_{n}
is rational and supported on at most
n
points.
Write
s(\epsilon)
as
s(\epsilon,\mu)
to emphasize the dependence on
\mu
in (
145
).
We see that
s(\epsilon,\mu)
is continuous in
\mu
with respect to
W_{\infty}
:
indeed, suppose that
W_{\infty}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\leq\delta

P_{Z^{\prime}|Z}
such that that transforms
\mu
to
\mu^{\prime}
, and that
\|Z^{\prime}-Z\|_{2}\leq\delta
almost surely for
(Z^{\prime},Z)\sim P_{Z^{\prime}|Z}\mu
.
Suppose that
\nu
achieves the supremum in (

\nu^{\prime}
as the transformation of
\nu
under
P_{Z^{\prime}|Z}
, which is possible since
\nu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu
.
By the data processing inequality we have
D(\nu^{\prime}\|\mu^{\prime})\leq D(\nu\|\mu)
,
and
W_{\infty}(\nu^{\prime},\nu)\leq W_{\infty}(\mu^{\prime},\mu)\leq\delta
.
This proves the continuity of

.
Consequently, we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\epsilon>0}\{\lambda s(\epsilon,\mu_{n})-\epsilon\}=\sup_{\epsilon>0}\{\lambda s(\epsilon,\mu)-\epsilon\}
.

\left<Y,Z_{n}\right>

in probability, hence also converges almost surely by passing to a subsequence (by the Borel-Cantelli lemma), so that
\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda\left<Y,Z\right>)]\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda\left<Y,Z_{n}\right>)]

Finally, we remove the assumption of finite dimensionality.

the truncation of

be the remaining coordinates.

\mu_{n}

Since

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|Y_{>n}\|_{2}=0

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|Z_{>n}\|_{2}=0

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we can thus show that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left<Y_{>n},Z_{>n}\right>=0

Then by Fatou’s lemma we have

\displaystyle\ln\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda\left<Y,Z\right>)]\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\ln\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda\left<Y_{\leq n},Z_{\leq n}\right>)]\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\epsilon>0}\{\lambda s(\epsilon,\mu_{n};\gamma)-\epsilon\}.

(159)

It remains to prove that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\epsilon>0}\{\lambda s(\epsilon,\mu_{n};\gamma)-\epsilon\}\leq\sup_{\epsilon>0}\{\lambda s(\epsilon,\mu;\gamma)-\epsilon\}.

(160)

For given
n>0
,
suppose that
\nu_{n}
is a distribution achieving the supremum in the definition of

.
Then we can extend
\nu_{n}
to a

, by using the same channel from

to

under

Suppose that

.
Then we can extend it to a

.
For

, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{w}(\nu;\gamma)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}[\left<Y,Z\right>]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\left<Y_{\leq n},Z\right>]+\mathbb{E}[\left<Y_{>n},Z\right>]

(162)

\displaystyle\geq\mathsf{w}(\nu_{n};\gamma)-\mathbb{E}[\|Y_{>n}\|_{2}\|Z_{>n}\|_{2}]

\displaystyle\geq\mathsf{w}(\nu_{n};\gamma)-\sup_{t\in T}\|t_{>n}\|_{2}\mathbb{E}[\|Y_{>n}\|_{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{w}(\nu_{n};\gamma)+o(1).

Moreover, we clearly have
\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{n}}[\|Z\|_{2}^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\|Z\|_{2}^{2}]
.
Thus (
160
) is true, and we have extended the result to totally bounded
T
.
∎

Lemma 14
.

Suppose that
Q
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
, and
\psi(\lambda):=\ln\mathbb{E}_{X\sim Q}[\exp(\lambda X^{2})]
.
Then for any
P
absolutely continuous with respect to
Q
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}[X^{2}]\leq\inf_{\lambda>0}\frac{D(P\|Q)+\psi(\lambda)}{\lambda}
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the case of
P=P_{\lambda}
defined according to
\frac{dP_{\lambda}}{dQ}(x)\propto\exp(\lambda x^{2})
.
Note that
\psi
is a nonnegative convex function, and
\psi(0)=0
.
Then the claim follows since

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\lambda}}[X^{2}]=\psi^{\prime}(\lambda)

and

D(P_{\lambda}\|Q)=\lambda\psi^{\prime}(\lambda)-\psi(\lambda)
.
∎

Lemma 15
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure with finite support on
T
for which
\mathsf{G}
is a width-moment function,
and
Z\sim\mu
.
Let
\psi(\lambda):=\ln\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda\|Z\|_{2}^{2}}]
.
Then

\displaystyle\ln\mathbb{E}[\exp(\eta\left<Y,Z\right>)]\leq\sup_{\epsilon>0}\left\{-\epsilon+\eta\mathsf{G}\left(\frac{\epsilon+\psi(\lambda)}{\lambda}\right)+\eta\sqrt{2\epsilon\cdot\frac{\epsilon+\psi(\lambda)}{\lambda}}\right\}

(166)

for any
\lambda,\eta>0
and any
P_{YZ}
for which
P_{Y}
is standard normal and
P_{Z}=\mu
.

Proof.

The result follows from Lemma
13
, Lemma
14
, and the data processing inequality for the KL divergence.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Recall that

\displaystyle\|\hat{m}-m\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\chi_{m-\hat{m}}

(167)

where
\chi_{z}:=-2\left<z,w\right>
.
We then have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda\|\hat{m}-m\|_{2}^{2})]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\frac{2\lambda}{\sqrt{n}}\left<\hat{m}-m,w\right>\right)\right].

(168)

Using Lemma
15
we have that the logarithm of the right side of (
168
) is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\sup_{\epsilon>0}\left\{-\epsilon+\frac{2\lambda}{\sqrt{n}}\mathsf{G}\left(\frac{\epsilon+\psi(\lambda)}{\lambda}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{8\epsilon\lambda(\epsilon+\psi(\lambda))}{n}}\right\}

(169)

and the result is proved.
∎

7.2
Under Sub-Gaussianity

In this section, we prove Theorem
5
, which is a counterpart of Theorem
4
under sub-Gaussian
\gamma
.
Since the proof is mostly similar to the proof of Theorem
4
,
we only point out the parts that are changed.
It suffices to first prove a counterpart of Lemma
13
, namely

\displaystyle\ln\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda\left<Y,Z\right>)]\leq\sup_{\epsilon>0}\{432C\lambda s(\epsilon)-\epsilon/C\}.

(170)

We first suppose that
\mu
is a rational distribution supported on at most
n
points,
which can be embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Recall the notations associated with the type class in the proof of Lemma
13
.
Using Lemma
2
, we now have that for
each type
\nu

and
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\xi_{\nu}>CJ(C_{\nu})+\sigma_{\nu}\sqrt{N\epsilon}]\leq C\exp(-N\epsilon/C),

(171)

where
J(C_{\nu})
denotes the Dudley integral for
C_{\nu}
.
Therefore,
setting
S_{N}^{\prime}:=\{\nu\in S_{N}\colon D(\nu\|\mu)\leq\epsilon\}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\bigcup_{\nu\in S_{N}^{\prime}}\left\{\xi_{\nu}>CJ(C_{\nu})+\sigma_{\nu}\sqrt{N\epsilon}\right\}\right]\leq C|S_{N}|\exp(-N\epsilon/C).

(172)

Since Dudley’s integral is sharp for stationary processes, with a constant bounded by 432 (see (
10
)),
we see that

\displaystyle J(C_{\nu})

\displaystyle\leq 432\mathbb{E}[\max_{z^{N}\in C_{\nu}}\left<G^{N},z^{N}\right>]

(173)

\displaystyle\leq 432N\mathbb{E}_{P_{YZ}\in\Pi(\widehat{P}_{G^{N}},\nu)}[\left<Y,Z\right>]

(174)

where
G^{N}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{nN})
.
It is easy to see that
\rho\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{P_{YZ}\in\Pi(\rho,\nu)}[\left<Y,Z\right>]

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\max_{\nu\in S_{N}^{\prime}}\xi_{\nu}>432CNs_{\rm g}(\epsilon)\right]\leq C|S_{N}|\exp(-N\epsilon/C),

(175)

where

8
ERM in One Dimension

In this section, we study the toy problem of empirical risk minimization in one dimension,
merely for the purpose of illustrating the application of the rate-distortion integral in ERM.
We first review the traditional slicing approach from generalization theory, adapting the key ideas from
Van de Geer, 1990
;
Wainwright, 2019
,
and then give another proof using the new rate-distortion integral approach.

8.1
Review of the Traditional Slicing Approach

Suppose that
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. whose mean
m\in\mathbb{R}
is unknown,
and
X_{i}-m
is 1-sub-Gaussian.
Recall the empirical loss, population loss, and empirical risk minimizer (ERM) defined in Section
2.2
.
For simplicity, let us assume that
T=[-1,1]
,
and then the ERM becomes

\displaystyle\hat{m}:=\argmin_{a\in[-1,1]}\hat{L}(a).

(176)

In this example, we can directly show that
\hat{m}
is the projection of
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
to the interval
[-1,1]
,
and obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\hat{m}-m|^{2}=O(\frac{1}{n}).

(177)

However, in more sophisticated ERM problems, an explicit expression may be unavailable.
Let us review how (
177
) can be derived from the slicing technique.
We recall (
24
) and the process
(\chi_{z})_{z\in T-T}
defined there,
whose natural metric is equivalent to the usual distance on
\mathbb{R}
.
The covering number is estimated as

\displaystyle N([-1,1],\lambda)\asymp 1\vee\frac{1}{\lambda}.

(178)

Applying Dudley’s integrals (
7
) to (
24
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\hat{m}-m|^{2}]\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\ln^{+}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)}d\lambda\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}

(179)

which is not sharp.
The reason for the looseness is that the estimated parameter is close to the true parameter,
but the generalization bounds is a worst-case estimate for any parameter in
[-1,1]
.
Therefore, the bound is expected be improved by using a local version of the complexity.
The classical literature improves the estimate using a “slicing” argument to control the modulus of continuity.
For example, using
(
Van Handel, 2014
, Theorem 5.32)

we obtain that with high probability,

\displaystyle|\hat{m}-m|^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\omega(C|\hat{m}-m|)

(180)

for some constant
C
,
where the modulus of continuity of the process
(\chi_{z})
is bounded as

\displaystyle\omega(\delta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\delta}\sqrt{\ln N([-1,1],\lambda)}d\lambda

(181)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{\delta}\sqrt{\ln^{+}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)}d\lambda

(182)

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta\sqrt{\ln\frac{1}{\delta}}

(183)

for
\delta<0.1
.
In particular, it follows that
|\hat{m}-m|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\ln n}{n}}
with high probability,
which is better than (
180
) but still has a logarithmic factor of suboptimality due to requiring uniform convergence over all slices.
Such extra log factors are typical in the analysis of the modulus of continuity;
see for example the local and global modulus of continuity of the Wiener process
(
Koralov and Sinai, 2007
)
.
For the analysis of ERM,
it is possible to remove the nuisance log factors by further noting that
1) we only need the inequality (
180
) to hold for all
\hat{m}

but
fixed

m
,
and
2) we can design the slicing scheme so that
\omega(\delta)\asymp\delta
for the “right scale”
\delta\asymp\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
only,
and slightly overestimate
\omega(\delta)
for other scales, yielding a converging sum in the union bound,
yet the inequality
\delta^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\omega(C\delta)
still yields
\delta\lesssim 1/\sqrt{n}
.
Details about constructing the slicing scheme and justifying the regularity conditions can be found, for example, in
Van de Geer, 1990
;
Wainwright, 2019
.

8.2
Rate-Distortion Integral Approach

In this section,
we give another proof of the rate (
177
) using the rate-distortion integral approach.
This new method controls the supremum directly using the second moment,
without the need of slicing in the traditional method.

Recall (
24
), that

|\hat{m}-m|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\chi_{m-\hat{m}}
,
where
\chi_{z}=-2\sqrt{n}z(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-m)
,
z\in T-T
is a centered
O(1)
-sub-Gaussian process.
Let
Z:=m-\hat{m}
and
\sigma_{\rm m}:=\sqrt{\var(Z)}
.
Using Proposition
1
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\chi_{Z}]

\displaystyle\lesssim\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\sup_{\mu^{\prime}\colon W_{\infty}(P_{Z},\mu^{\prime})\leq\epsilon}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\sqrt{R_{\mu^{\prime}}(\sigma^{2})}d\sigma.

(184)

Effectively, we can just think of the right side of (
184
) as
\int_{0}^{\sigma_{\rm m}}\sqrt{R_{P_{Z}}(\sigma^{2})}d\sigma
, since we will bound the rate-distortion integral by a continuous function of the second moment, and the limiting argument easily applies.

To move forward, one possible approach is to show that, under the constraint
\var(Z)\leq\sigma_{\rm m}^{2}
, there is

\displaystyle R_{P_{Z}}(\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle\leq R_{\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\rm m}^{2})}(\sigma^{2})

(185)

\displaystyle=[\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{\sigma_{\rm m}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}]_{+},

(186)

where (
186
) follows from the Gaussian rate-distribution function
(
Thomas and Joy, 2006
)
,
then by change-of-variable we have

\int_{0}^{\sigma_{\rm m}}\sqrt{R_{P_{Z}}(\sigma^{2})}d\sigma\lesssim\sigma_{\rm m}
.
We thus obtain
\mathbb{E}[|\hat{m}-m|^{2}]\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\var(\hat{m})}\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[|\hat{m}-m|^{2}]}
and hence

\mathbb{E}[|\hat{m}-m|^{2}]\lesssim\frac{1}{n}
,
which is sharp.
To justify the key step (
185
), one possible approach is to use Lieb’s rotation argument for proving Gaussian optimality; see
Liu et al., 2018
;
Courtade and Liu, 2021
for the analysis of a similar problem called the
reverse Brascamp-Lieb inequality.
We will not provide the details of this approach here.

An alternative, simpler approach, is to leverage the penalized version of the rate-distortion integral:
from Lemma
12
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\sigma_{\rm m}}\sqrt{R_{P_{Z}}(\sigma^{2})}d\sigma\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\inf_{P_{U|Z}}\{\alpha^{-2}\mathbb{E}[|U-Z|^{2}]+I(U;Z)\}d\alpha.

(187)

This penalized form is helpful, because
P_{U|Z}
is optimized over a set independent of
P_{X}
, so that saddle point analysis applies.
We have the following:

Theorem 8
.

For any given
\alpha>0
, and
Z
satisfying
\var(Z)\leq\sigma_{\rm m}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{P_{U|Z}}\{\alpha^{-2}\mathbb{E}[|U-Z|^{2}]+I(U;Z)\}

\displaystyle\leq(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{2\sigma_{\rm m}^{2}}{\alpha^{2}})1\{\alpha<\sqrt{2}\sigma_{\rm m}\}+\alpha^{-2}\sigma_{\rm m}^{2}1\{\alpha\geq\sqrt{2}\sigma_{\rm m}\}

(188)

and hence
\int_{0}^{\infty}\inf_{P_{U|Z}}\{\alpha^{-2}\mathbb{E}[|U-Z|^{2}]+I(U;Z)\}d\alpha\lesssim\sigma_{\rm m}
.

Proof.

By translation invariance, it suffices to assume that
\mathbb{E}[|Z|^{2}]\leq\sigma_{\rm m}^{2}
.
Define the functional
f(P_{Z},P_{U|Z}):=\alpha^{-2}\mathbb{E}[|U-Z|^{2}]+I(U;Z)
, which is convex in
P_{U|Z}
and concave in
P_{Z}
, and we have the saddle point condition

\displaystyle\sup_{P_{Z}\colon\mathbb{E}[|Z|^{2}]\leq\sigma_{\rm m}^{2}}f(P_{Z},P_{U|Z}^{*})\leq f(P_{Z}^{*},P_{U|Z}^{*})\leq\inf_{P_{U|Z}}f(P_{Z}^{*},P_{U|Z})

(189)

where
P_{Z}^{*}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\rm m}^{2})
and
P_{U|Z}^{*}
is an additive noise Gaussian channel that achieves the infimum;
see
Thomas and Joy, 2006
.
Then
\inf_{P_{U|Z}}f(P_{Z}^{*},P_{U|Z})\geq\sup_{P_{Z}\colon\mathbb{E}[|Z|^{2}]\leq\sigma_{\rm m}^{2}}f(P_{Z},P_{U|Z}^{*})\geq\sup_{P_{Z}\colon\mathbb{E}[|Z|^{2}]\leq\sigma_{\rm m}^{2}}\inf_{P_{U|Z}}f(P_{Z},P_{U|Z})
.
This shows that under
\mathbb{E}[|Z|]\leq\sigma_{\rm m}^{2}
, the right side of (
187
) is maximized by
P_{Z}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\rm m}^{2})
, in which case

\displaystyle\inf_{P_{U|Z}}\{\alpha^{-2}\mathbb{E}[|U-Z|^{2}]+I(U;Z)\}

\displaystyle=\inf_{D>0}\left\{\alpha^{-2}D+\frac{1}{2}[\ln\frac{\sigma_{\rm m}^{2}}{D}]_{+}\right\}

(190)

\displaystyle=(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{2\sigma_{\rm m}^{2}}{\alpha^{2}})\vee\alpha^{-2}\sigma_{\rm m}^{2}.

(191)

The last claim follows since

\int_{0}^{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{\rm m}}(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{2\sigma_{\rm m}^{2}}{\alpha^{2}})d\alpha+\int_{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{\rm m}}^{\infty}\alpha^{-2}\sigma_{\rm m}^{2}d\alpha\lesssim\sigma_{\rm m}
.
∎

To finish,
if
\var(Z)\leq\sigma_{\rm m}^{2}
and
W_{\infty}(P_{Z},\mu^{\prime})\leq\epsilon
,
then the variance under
\mu^{\prime}
is bounded by
(\epsilon+\sigma_{\rm m})^{2}
,
and
we deduce from Theorem
8
and (
187
) that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\sqrt{R_{\mu^{\prime}}(\sigma^{2})}d\sigma\lesssim\sigma_{\rm m}+\epsilon.

(192)

Taking
\epsilon\downarrow 0
and applying (
184
) and (
24
), we obtain the sharp rate of
\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{m}-m\|_{2}^{2}]\lesssim\frac{1}{n}
.

9
ERM for the Sobolev Ellipsoid

This section provides details of the analysis of the Sobolev ellipsoid in Section
3.4
,
and prove Theorem
6
.

9.1
Supremum over an Ellipsoid

Let
X_{z}:=\left<G,z\right>
, where
G=(G(1),G(2),\dots)
has i.i.d. standard normal coordinates.
The majorizing measure theorem shows that

(
Talagrand, 2014
, Section 2.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}}X_{z}]\asymp\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}2^{i}a_{2^{i}}^{2}}\asymp\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}^{2}}.

(193)

On the other hand, a nontrivial calculation
(
Talagrand, 2014
)
shows
that the Dudley integral is
\asymp\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}2^{i/2}a_{2^{i}}

hence may not be sharp.

In this section we show that the technique in Section
3.2
can recover the sharp upper bound in (
193
).
More generally, the upper bound holds when the index set is an image of
\mathcal{E}
under a Lipschitz map, in view of Corollary
3
.
Although this result is not new, it serves to illustrate the technique.
Suppose that
Z
is a random variable taking values in
\mathcal{E}
and
\Sigma:=\cov(Z)
.
Henceforth, we will first assume that the support of
P_{Z}
is countable to derive bounds on
\mathbb{E}[X_{Z}]
in terms of
\Sigma
, and then use the approximation argument in
Proposition
1
to extend the bound to the possibly uncountable case, noting that the covariance is continuous with respect to the Wasserstein-2 metric.
Then by Corollary
3
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{Z}]

\displaystyle\lesssim\tr(\sqrt{\Sigma})

(194)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\Sigma_{ii}}

(195)

\displaystyle\leq\|(a_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\|_{2}

(196)

where
(
195
) follows from the Schur concavity of the square root function and the fact that the eigenvalues majorizes the diagonal values,
and (
196
) follows from the ellipsoid constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Sigma_{ii}}{a_{i}^{2}}\leq 1
and Cauchy-Schwarz.
This bound recovers the sharp upper bound in (
193
).

For the case of the Sobolev ellipsoid, see that for
\beta>1/2
, the optimal rate in (
193
) is
O(\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\beta-1}})
for
\beta=O(1)
.
In contrast, the Dudley integral is

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}2^{i/2}a_{2^{i}}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}2^{-\beta i+i/2}=O(\frac{1}{2\beta-1})

(197)

for
\beta=O(1)
, which is worse; see
(
Wainwright, 2019
, Example 5.12)
or
(
Talagrand, 2014
, Section 2.13)
.

As a further example,
consider the near critical case
a_{i}=i^{-1/2}(\ln i)^{-1}
.
The rate-distortion integral gives a finite bound, whereas the traditional Dudley gives a vacuous bound of
+\infty
.

9.2
Error Bounds for ERM

In this section,
we give two proofs of Theorem
6
.
The first one only applies to a special case of Theorem
6
where
T
is the identity map and
Y
satisfies the stronger log-Sobolev inequality.
The second uses the tools developed in Section
3.3
and applies to the more general setting in Theorem
6
.

9.2.1
A Simple Proof in a Special Case

We first provide a simple proof of Theorem
6
in a special setting, stated in the following theorem:

Theorem 9
.

In Theorem
6
, assume that
Y
satisfies the log-Sobolev inequality (
17
), and
T=\mathcal{E}
.
For any
C>1/2
, there exists
C^{\prime}>0
such that the following holds:
for any
\beta\in(1/2,C]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{m}-m\|^{2}]\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2\beta-1}}n^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}},

(198)

and if
T
is convex and
X_{i}
satisfies the log-Sobolev inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\|\hat{m}-m\|>\frac{C^{\prime}}{(2\beta-1)^{1/4}}n^{-\frac{\beta}{2\beta+1}}\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{C^{\prime}\sqrt{2\beta-1}}n^{\frac{1}{2\beta+1}}\right).

(199)

Remark 2
.

For fixed
\beta>1/2
, this is the standard nonparametric rate
(
Tsybakov, 2009
)
.
The assumption of
2\beta>1
is standard for ensuring the finiteness of the Dudley integral; see
(
Wainwright, 2019
, Example 5.12)
.

The proof we provide here is essentially that of

Chatterjee, 2014
;
Wei et al., 2020
.
We control the “soft” version of the Gaussian width in Definition
3
, in lieu of the traditional Gaussian width in
Chatterjee, 2014
.
This difference is not essential for convex
T
due to concentration of the error.
The bound from this approach is also sharp; see discussion on the converse in
Chatterjee, 2014
.

Proof of Theorem
6
.

Recall (
24
),
where
\chi_{z}=-2\sqrt{n}\left<z,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-m\right>
,
z\in 2\mathcal{E}
is a centered sub-Gaussian process, whose metric is the
\ell_{2}
metric (up to universal constant).
Invoking Corollary
3
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\chi_{Z}]\lesssim\tr(\sqrt{\Sigma})

(200)

where we defined the covariance matrix
\Sigma:=\cov(Z)
, and
Z:=m-\hat{m}
.
Now define

\epsilon:=\tr(\Sigma)
.
From the definition of
\mathcal{E}
, we also have

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Sigma_{ii}a_{i}^{-2}\leq 4
.
Therefore for any
\lambda>0
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(a_{i}^{-2}+\lambda)\Sigma_{ii}\leq\lambda\epsilon+4.

(201)

Then choosing
\lambda=1/\epsilon
,

\displaystyle(\tr(\sqrt{\Sigma}))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\Sigma_{ii}})^{2}

(202)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{i}^{-2}+\lambda}{\lambda\epsilon+4}\Sigma_{ii}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda\epsilon+4}{a_{i}^{-2}+\lambda}\right)

(203)

\displaystyle\leq 5\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}^{2}\wedge\epsilon,

(204)

where the first inequality follows from concavity of
\sqrt{\cdot}
and the fact that the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
majorizes its diagonal values.
In the case of
a_{i}=i^{-\beta}
, we have

\displaystyle\tr(\sqrt{\Sigma})\lesssim\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}^{2}\wedge\epsilon}\asymp\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\beta-1}\epsilon^{1-\frac{1}{2\beta}}}

(205)

where we assumed
\beta=O(1)
.
Combining this with (
24
) and (
200
), we obtain

\epsilon\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\beta-1}\epsilon^{1-\frac{1}{2\beta}}}
, which yields

\epsilon\lesssim[(2\beta-1)n]^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}}
, as desired.

To show the concentration inequality (
199
),
we treat
\hat{m}=\hat{m}(\bar{X})
as a function of
\bar{X}
, which is the projection onto the convex set
T
.
For any two
x,x^{\prime}\in\ell_{2}
, we have
\left<\hat{m}(x)-\hat{m}(x^{\prime}),x-\hat{m}(x)\right>\geq 0

and
\left<\hat{m}(x^{\prime})-\hat{m}(x),x^{\prime}-\hat{m}(x^{\prime})\right>\geq 0
, by the hyperplane separation.
This implies that
\|\hat{m}(x)-\hat{m}(x^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{2}
, hence
\|\hat{m}-m\|_{2}
is a Lipschitz function.
Therefore
\left\|\nabla\|\hat{m}-m\|_{2}^{2}\right\|_{2}\leq 2\|\hat{m}-m\|_{2}\leq 2
.
Since the log-Sobolev inequality implies sub-Gaussian concentration
(
Boucheron et al., 2013
)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\|\hat{m}-m\|>\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{m}-m\|]+t]\leq\exp(-cnt^{2})

(206)

for any
t>0
, where
c>0
is a universal constant.
Then (
199
) follows by choosing
C^{\prime}
large enough.
∎

The above simple proof leverages the linearity and concentration, and does not seem to extend to the case of general
\phi
in Theorem
6
.
In that case, the entropy integral type argument seems to be essential.

9.2.2
Second Proof

We now give a full proof of Theorem
6
using the technique in Section
3.3
.
Recall in (
24
) we defined the
O(1)
-sub-Gaussian process

(\chi_{z})_{T-T}
,
where
T
is now taken to be a Lipschitz image of the ellipsoid,
and recall definitions of
\mathsf{G}(E)
and
\mathsf{G}_{\rm g}(E)
in Section
3.3
.
Our proof is based on the following key estimate of the width-moment function:

Theorem 10
.

For
(\chi_{z})_{z\in T-T}
, and any
E>0
,
we have
\mathsf{G}(E)\lesssim\mathsf{G}_{\rm g}(E)\lesssim C_{\beta}E^{\frac{2\beta-1}{4\beta}}
.

Remark 3
.

In
(
Van de Geer, 1990
, Lemma 5.1)
,
the ERM error for the Sobolev space of functions was bounded by localizing the Dudley integral,
where
\beta\geq 1
is assumed an integer.
Suppose that we extend the approach of
Van de Geer, 1990
to the setting of Theorem
9
.
The covering number is bounded as

\displaystyle\ln N(\delta,\mathcal{E})\lesssim\delta^{-\frac{1}{\beta}}

(207)

when
\beta=O(1)
.

(
Van de Geer, 1990
, Example 2.1)
estimated the covering number for fixed
\beta
.
For the Sobolev ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
,

(
Wainwright, 2019
, Example 5.12)

provided a proof of (
207
),
from which we can see that the best preconstant in (
207
) is the order of a constant independent of
\beta
when
\beta=O(1)
.
For local covering numbers,
Wei et al., 2020
showed that the estimate in (
207
) still holds, where the preconstant is a universal constant.
We can then calculate the Dudley integral up to the scale of the error
\sqrt{E}
, resulting in a bound of
\int_{0}^{\sqrt{E}}\delta^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}}d\delta\leq O(\frac{1}{2\beta-1})E^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4\beta}}
when
\beta=O(1)
.
Using our proof instead,
we get
\mathbb{E}[\chi_{Z}]\leq O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\beta-1}})E^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4\beta}}
,
which is almost the same, but with a better preconstant.
Thus Dudley’s integral results in

\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{m}-m\|^{2}]=O(\frac{1}{2\beta-1})n^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+1}}
for
\beta=O(1)
,
where the preconstant
O(\frac{1}{2\beta-1})
is not
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\beta-1}})
in Theorem
9
.
This gap is related to the looseness of the Dudley integral: as we see in (
197
), Dudley’s integral gives an
O(\frac{1}{2\beta-1})
bound on the supremum over
\mathcal{E}
, rather than the optimal

O(\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\beta-1}})
bound.

How does the rate-distortion integral improve the preconstant?
Naively, we can show a result that for a random variable
Z\in 2\mathcal{E}
,
R_{P_{Z}}(\delta^{2})\lesssim\delta^{-\frac{1}{\beta}}
, for
\beta=O(1)
,
which is analogous to (
207
), and proceed by integrating.
But this will still result in a suboptimal preconstant
O(\frac{1}{2\beta-1})
.
The reason is that the
P_{Z}
maximizing
R_{P_{Z}}(\delta^{2})
changes with
\delta
.
Instead, we leverage the penalized version Lemma
12
and evaluate the integrand therein for given
\cov(Z)
, calculate the integral, and optimize
\cov(Z)
in the end.
The proof is made simple by properties of information-theoretic measures such as chain rules of the entropy.

We first need a few preparations. Define

\displaystyle f(t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}t/2&0\leq t\leq 1\\
\frac{1+\ln t}{2}&t>1\end{array}\right..

We have

Lemma 16
.

t\int_{0}^{1/t}f(\frac{1}{u^{2}})du\leq 4f(t)
, for all
t>0
.

Proof.

For
0<u_{0}\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{u_{0}}f(\frac{1}{u^{2}})du=\int_{0}^{u_{0}}\frac{1+\ln\frac{1}{u^{2}}}{2}du=\frac{3}{2}u_{0}+u_{0}\ln\frac{1}{u_{0}}\leq\frac{3}{2}u_{0}(1+\ln\frac{1}{u_{0}}).

(210)

For
u_{0}>1
,

\int_{1}^{u_{0}}f(\frac{1}{u^{2}})du=\int_{1}^{u_{0}}\frac{1}{2u^{2}}du=\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{u_{0}})
, so that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{u_{0}}f(\frac{1}{u^{2}})du=\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{u_{0}})=2-\frac{1}{2u_{0}}\leq 2.

(211)

The result follows by taking
u_{0}=1/t
.
∎

Lemma 17
.

Assume that
W\in\ell_{2}
is a random variable and
\Sigma:=\cov(W)
.
Let
Z=\phi(W)
, where
\phi
is a 1-Lipschitz function, and suppose that
\mathbb{E}[\|Z\|^{2}]\leq\epsilon
.
Then, for any
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\inf_{P_{U|Z}}\{\alpha^{-2}\mathbb{E}[\|U-Z\|^{2}]+I(U;Z)\}\leq\alpha^{-2}\epsilon\wedge\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f(2\alpha^{-2}\Sigma_{ii}).

(212)

Proof.

By considering
U=0
, it is easy to see that

\inf_{P_{U|X}}\{\alpha^{-2}\mathbb{E}[\|U-Z\|^{2}]+I(U;Z)\}\leq\alpha^{-2}\epsilon
.
Thus it remains to show that

\displaystyle\inf_{P_{U|Z}}\{\alpha^{-2}\mathbb{E}[\|U-Z\|^{2}]+I(U;Z)\}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f(2\alpha^{-2}\Sigma_{ii}).

(213)

It suffices to prove (
213
) in the special case where the Lipschitz map
\phi
is the identity,
since the left side of (
213
) only decreases with Lipschitz
\phi
.
Furthermore, the left side of (
213
) is upper bounded by
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\inf_{P_{U_{i}|Z_{i}}}\{\alpha^{-2}\mathbb{E}[|U_{i}-Z_{i}|^{2}]+I(U_{i};Z_{i})\}
,
because given
P_{U_{i}|Z_{i}}
we can define
P_{U|Z}=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}P_{U_{i}|Z_{i}}
,
and then
I(U;Z)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}I(U_{i};Z_{i})
.
Then the result in (
213
) follows from the one-dimensional case, which we already proved in Theorem
8
.
∎

Lemma 18
.

Suppose that
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Sigma_{ii}i^{\beta}\leq 4
, where
\beta>1/2
.
For any
\epsilon>0
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha^{-2}\epsilon\wedge\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f(2\alpha^{-2}\Sigma_{ii})d\alpha\leq C_{\beta}\epsilon^{\frac{2\beta-1}{4\beta}},

(214)

where
C_{\beta}
is a constant defined around (
235
), which satisfies
C_{\beta}=O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\beta-1}})
as
\beta\downarrow\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

Set

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}:=\epsilon^{\frac{1+2\beta}{4\beta}}.

(215)

We have

\displaystyle\int_{\alpha_{0}}^{\infty}\alpha^{-2}\epsilon\wedge d\alpha

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon^{\frac{2\beta-1}{4\beta}}.

(216)

Moreover, by Lemma
16

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\alpha_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f(2\alpha^{-2}\Sigma_{ii})d\alpha

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{2\Sigma_{ii}}\int_{0}^{\frac{\alpha_{0}}{\sqrt{2\Sigma_{ii}}}}f(\frac{1}{u^{2}})du

(217)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}4f(\frac{\sqrt{2\Sigma_{ii}}}{\alpha_{0}}).

(218)

It will become clear that
\alpha_{0}
was chosen so that (
216
) and (
218
) are both upper bounded by
\epsilon^{\frac{2\beta-1}{4\beta}}
.
Therefore, it remains to consider the optimization problem:

\displaystyle M_{1}:=\sup_{(\Sigma_{i})}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f(\frac{\sqrt{2\Sigma_{i}}}{\alpha_{0}})

(219)

under the constraint

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Sigma_{i}a_{i}^{-2}\leq 4
.
Also, consider another optimization problem:

\displaystyle M_{2}:=\sup_{\Sigma(\cdot)}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(\frac{\sqrt{2\Sigma(t)}}{\alpha_{0}})dt

(220)

under the constraint
\int_{0}^{\infty}\Sigma(t)t^{2\beta}dt\leq 1
, where
\Sigma(\cdot)
is a nonnegative continuous function.
Clearly
M_{2}\geq M_{1}
, since given
(\Sigma_{ii})
we can define
\Sigma(t)=\Sigma_{\lceil t\rceil}
.

Since
f
has continuous, strictly increasing first derivative, we can see that an optimizer
\Sigma(\cdot)
exists, which is continuous.
Since
f(\cdot)
is increasing,
\Sigma(\cdot)
must be decreasing.
There exists unique
t_{0}>0
such that
\frac{\sqrt{2\Sigma(t)}}{\alpha_{0}}\geq 1
,
t\in(0,t_{0}]
and
\frac{\sqrt{2\Sigma(t)}}{\alpha_{0}}\leq 1
,
t\in[t_{0},\infty)
.
Stationarity condition implies that there exists
\lambda>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\Sigma(t)}

\displaystyle=\lambda t^{2\beta},\quad t\in(0,t_{0}],

(221)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\alpha_{0}\sqrt{2\Sigma(t)}}

\displaystyle=\lambda t^{2\beta}\quad t\in[t_{0},\infty).

(222)

Setting
t=t_{0}
in both equations and canceling
\Sigma(t_{0})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{1}{2\alpha_{0}^{2}t_{0}^{2\beta}}

(223)

Also, we can express
\Sigma(t)
as a function of
t
from the above, and
\int_{0}^{\infty}\Sigma(t)t^{2\beta}dt=1
must be achieved, implying

\displaystyle 4

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\frac{1}{4\lambda}dt+\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{8\alpha_{0}^{2}\lambda^{2}t^{2\beta}}dt

(224)

\displaystyle=\frac{t_{0}}{4\lambda}+\frac{1}{8(2\beta-1)\alpha_{0}^{2}\lambda^{2}t_{0}^{2\beta-1}}

(225)

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta}{2\beta-1}\alpha_{0}^{2}t_{0}^{2\beta+1}.

(226)

Now that we are able to express
t_{0}
and
\lambda
as functions of
\alpha
, we can evaluate the optimal value:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{0}}f(\frac{\sqrt{2\Sigma(t)}}{\alpha_{0}})dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\frac{1+\ln\frac{\sqrt{2\Sigma(t)}}{\alpha_{0}}}{2}dt

(227)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\frac{1+\beta\ln\frac{t_{0}}{t}}{2}dt

(228)

\displaystyle=\frac{t_{0}(1+\beta)}{2},

(229)

and

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}f(\frac{\sqrt{2\Sigma(t)}}{\alpha_{0}})dt

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{\sqrt{2\Sigma(t)}}{2\alpha_{0}}dt

(230)

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{t_{0}^{2\beta}}{2t^{2\beta}}dt

(231)

\displaystyle=\frac{t_{0}}{2(2\beta-1)}.

(232)

In summary, we have shown that

\displaystyle M_{1}\leq M_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1+\beta}{2}+\frac{1}{2(2\beta-1)}\right)t_{0}

(233)

\displaystyle\leq c_{\beta}\alpha_{0}^{-\frac{2}{1+2\beta}}

(234)

where we defined

\displaystyle c_{\beta}=\left(\frac{1+\beta}{2}+\frac{1}{2(2\beta-1)}\right)\left(\frac{\beta}{4(2\beta-1)}\right)^{-\frac{1}{1+2\beta}}.

(235)

Hence

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha^{-2}\epsilon\wedge\sum_{i=1}^{d}f(2\alpha^{-2}\Sigma_{ii})d\alpha\leq 4\alpha_{0}M_{1}+\epsilon^{\frac{2\beta-1}{4\beta}}=(4c_{\beta}+1)\epsilon^{\frac{2\beta-1}{4\beta}}.

(236)

The proof is completed by setting
C_{\beta}=4c_{\beta}+1
.
∎

It is obvious from Corollary

that

, hence we only need to prove the second inequality.

Proof of Theorem

The proof is based on (

10
ERM for Sparse Regression

In this section, we give two proofs of Theorem
7
.
The first one, based on Hölder’s inequality, is simpler but only applies to a special case;
the second uses the rate-distortion integral and proves the theorem in full generality.

10.1
Simple Proof in a Special Case

First, we give a simple and direct proof of the following special case of Theorem
7
:

Theorem 11
.

Suppose that
A:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{X}
is a square matrix,
and its largest and smallest singular values satisfy

\sigma_{\rm max}(A)\lesssim 1
and
\sigma_{\rm max}(A)\gtrsim 1
.
Let
q\in(0,1)
. Then

\mathbb{E}[\|\beta^{*}-\hat{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}]\lesssim_{q}R\left(\frac{\ln d}{n}\right)^{\frac{2-q}{2}}
.

Before proving Theorem
11
,
let us recall some auxiliary results.
Note that if
x\in wB_{q}(r)
, then for any
p>q
we have

\displaystyle\|x\|_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{d}1_{|x_{i}|>t}d(t^{p})

(246)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t\in(0,r)}(\frac{r}{t})^{q}d(t^{p})

(247)

\displaystyle=\frac{r^{p}}{1-q/p}

(248)

implying the inclusion
wB_{q}(r)\subset B_{p}((1-q/p)^{-\frac{1}{p}}r)
.
Therefore risk bounds for
\ell_{p}
balls imply risk bounds for weak
\ell_{q}
balls, but this naive argument will not give the sharp rate in Theorem
7
,
since
p
must be chosen to satisfy the strict inequality
p>q
.

Nevertheless, we will see that in the setting of
Theorem
11
, which is a special case of Theorem
7
,
there is a simple proof based on the interpolation inequality,
and it is well-known in functional analysis that interpolation can improve “weak
\ell_{p}
estimates” at the boundary to an “
\ell_{p}
estimate” at the interpolant.
More precisely, we will use the following result, which can be viewed as a special case of the Marcinkiewicz interpolation theorem:

Lemma 19
.

Suppose that
X
is a nonnegative random variable,

\sup_{t>0}t^{a}\mathbb{P}[X>At]\leq 1
,
\sup_{t>0}t^{a}\mathbb{P}[X>Bt]\leq 1

for
A,B,a,b>0
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, where
a\neq b
and
\frac{\alpha}{a}+\frac{1-\alpha}{b}=1
.
Then
\mathbb{E}[X]\lesssim_{a,b}A^{\alpha}B^{1-\alpha}
.

Proof.

By a rescaling argument, we see that it suffices to consider the case of
A=B
.
Then the proof is completed since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[X>s]ds

(249)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}1\wedge\frac{A^{a}}{s^{a}}\wedge\frac{A^{b}}{s^{b}}ds

(250)

\displaystyle=A\int_{0}^{\infty}1\wedge\frac{1}{t^{a}}\wedge\frac{1}{t^{b}}dt\lesssim_{a,b}A.

(251)

∎

Proof of Theorem
11
.

Let
E:=\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{\beta}-\beta^{*}\|_{2}^{2}]
and
Z:=\hat{\beta}-\beta^{*}
.
Suppose that
\theta\in[0,\frac{q}{2-q})
and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}=\frac{\theta}{q}+\frac{1-\theta}{2}.

(252)

We think of
p>1
as a function of
\theta
, which is close to 1.
Let
R_{j}:=\sup_{t}t\mathbb{P}^{1/q}[|Z_{j}|>t]
and
E_{j}:=\mathbb{E}[|Z_{j}|^{2}]
for each
j
.
Then
\sum_{j=1}^{d}E_{j}=E
.
Since
\beta^{*},\hat{\beta}\in wB_{q}(r)
, we have
\sum_{j=1}^{d}|Z_{j}|^{q}\leq 2r^{q}=2R
, hence

\sum_{j=1}^{d}R_{j}\leq 2R
.
By the interpolation inequality (Lemma
19
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|Z_{j}|^{p}]

\displaystyle\lesssim_{q}R_{j}^{\frac{\theta p}{q}}E_{j}^{\frac{1-\theta}{2}p}.

(253)

Hence for
\theta=0
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|Z\|_{p}^{p}]

\displaystyle\lesssim_{q}\sum_{j=1}^{d}R_{j}^{\frac{\theta p}{q}}E_{j}^{\frac{1-\theta}{2}p}

(254)

\displaystyle\leq\|(R_{j}^{\frac{\theta p}{q}})\|_{\frac{q}{\theta p}}\|(E_{j}^{\frac{1-\theta}{2}p})\|_{\frac{2}{(1-\theta)p}}

(255)

\displaystyle\leq(2R)^{\frac{\theta p}{q}}E^{\frac{(1-\theta)p}{2}}.

(256)

Moreover, (
256
) is also trivially true when
\theta=0
(in which case
p=2
).

Recall (
322
). We have, by Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{E}[\|Z\|_{p}\|A^{\top}w\|_{p^{\prime}}]

(257)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{E}^{\frac{1}{p}}[\|Z\|_{p}^{p}]\cdot\mathbb{E}^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}[\|A^{\top}w\|_{p^{\prime}}^{p^{\prime}}]

(258)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(2R)^{\frac{\theta}{q}}E^{\frac{1-\theta}{2}}d^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\sqrt{p^{\prime}},

(259)

where we used the fact that for
G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
we have
\mathbb{E}^{1/p^{\prime}}[|G|^{p^{\prime}}]\leq 3\sqrt{p^{\prime}}
, and
p^{\prime}=(1-1/p)^{-1}
is the Hölder conjugate.
Rearranging, we obtain

\displaystyle E^{\frac{1+\theta}{2}}\lesssim_{q}\sqrt{p^{\prime}}d^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{\theta}{q}}.

(260)

Define

\displaystyle\theta_{0}:=\frac{q}{2-q}.

(261)

Comparing with (
252
), we see that
p
is close to 1 when
\theta
is close to
\theta_{0}
.

Case I:
(\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{2})\ln d+\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{(1-q/2)E}{(2R)^{\frac{2}{q}}}\leq\frac{1}{\theta_{0}}
.
The assumption of this case implies

\displaystyle E\lesssim_{q}R^{\frac{2}{q}}d^{-\frac{2-q}{q}}.

(262)

Take
\theta=0
,
p=p^{\prime}=2
, we get from (
260
) that

\displaystyle\sqrt{E}\lesssim_{q}1.

(263)

Raising (
262
) to the power of
q/2
and (
263
) to the power of
2-q
gives

.

Case II:
(\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{2})\ln d+\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{(1-q/2)E}{(2R)^{\frac{2}{q}}}>\frac{1}{\theta_{0}}
.
From (
252
) and (
261
) we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=(\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{2})(\theta_{0}-\theta).

(264)

Choosing

\displaystyle\theta=\theta_{0}-\frac{1}{(\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{2})\ln d+\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{(1-q/2)E}{(2R)^{\frac{2}{q}}}}\in(0,\frac{q}{2-q})

(265)

yields

\displaystyle d^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\left(\frac{(1-q/2)E}{(2R)^{2/q}}\right)^{\frac{\theta_{0}-\theta}{2}}=\left(\frac{d^{\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{1-q/2}E^{\frac{1}{2}}}{(2R)^{\frac{1}{q}}}\right)^{\theta_{0}-\theta}=e

(266)

and

\displaystyle\sqrt{p^{\prime}}=\sqrt{\ln d+(\frac{2}{q}-1)^{-1}\ln\frac{(1-q/2)E}{(2R)^{\frac{2}{q}}}}\leq\sqrt{\ln d}

(267)

where we used

due to (

Then (

) shows that

\displaystyle E^{\frac{1+\theta_{0}}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{q}d^{-1/2}R^{\frac{\theta_{0}}{q}}\cdot\sqrt{p^{\prime}}d^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\left(\frac{(1-q/2)E}{(2R)^{2/q}}\right)^{\frac{\theta_{0}-\theta}{2}}

(268)

\displaystyle\lesssim d^{-1/2}R^{\frac{\theta_{0}}{q}}\cdot\sqrt{\ln d}.

(269)

Equivalently, we have

\displaystyle E\lesssim_{q}R\left(\frac{\ln d}{n}\right)^{\frac{2-q}{2}}.

(270)

∎

10.2
Proof Using the Rate-Distortion Integral

In this section, we give a proof of Theorem
7
in full generality, using the technique in Section
3.3
.
The key part is the following estimate of the width-moment function:

Lemma 20
.

Suppose that
q\in(0,1)
,
Z\in wB_{q}(r)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\mathbb{E}[\|AZ\|_{2}^{2}]\leq E
, where
A
is an

matrix whose columns have

Then we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{w}(P_{AZ})\lesssim_{q}\sqrt{\ln d}r^{\frac{q}{2-q}}E^{\frac{1-q}{2-q}}.

(271)

In particular, for the set
A(wB_{q}(r))
, we have
\mathsf{G}(E)\lesssim_{q}\sqrt{\ln d}r^{\frac{q}{2-q}}E^{\frac{1-q}{2-q}}
.

To prove Lemma

using the rate-distortion integral, we need to establish upper bounds on the rate-distortion function.
Let
g\colon(0,\infty)\mapsto(0,\infty)
be the concave envelope of
x\ln\frac{1}{x}\vee\ln x
.
It is easy to see that
g
has continuous first derivative, and there exists
a_{1}\in(0,1)
and
a_{2}\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle=x\ln\frac{1}{x},\quad\forall x<a_{1};

(272)

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle=\ln x,\quad\forall x>a_{2},

(273)

and
g
is linear for
x\in[a_{1},a_{2}]
.
We show that this function controls the rate-distortion function of random variables in
\mathbb{R}
satisfying a weak
\ell_{q}
condition:

Lemma 21
.

Suppose that
q\in(0,1)
,
r\in(0,\infty)
, and
Z\in\mathbb{R}
is a random variable satisfying
\sup_{t>0}t^{q}\mathbb{P}[|Z|>t]\leq r^{q}
.
Then there exists a random variable
U
such that
|U|\leq|Z|
almost surely,
\mathbb{E}[|U-Z|]\leq b
,
and

\displaystyle H(U)\lesssim_{q}g\left(\left(\frac{r}{b}\right)^{\frac{q}{1-q}}\right).

(274)

Proof.

By scaling invariance of the entropy, it suffices to consider the case of
r=1
, which we shall assume henceforth.
Let
t_{k}=k(\frac{1-q}{2-q}b)^{\frac{1}{1-q}}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Given
Z
, define
U=t_{k}
where
k=\argmin_{l\colon|t_{l}|\leq|Z|}|Z-t_{l}|
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|U-Z|]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[|Z|1_{|Z|\leq t_{1}}]+t_{1}P_{Z}(\mathbb{R}\setminus[t_{-1},t_{1}])

(275)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\frac{dt}{t^{q}}+t_{1}^{1-q}

(276)

\displaystyle=\frac{2-q}{1-q}t_{1}^{1-q}=b.

(277)

To bound the entropy,
first consider the case of
t_{1}^{-q}\leq 2/3
.
For
k\geq 1
, define
p_{k}:=p_{-k}:=\frac{1}{2}t_{1}^{-q}\left(\frac{1}{k^{q}}-\frac{1}{(k+1)^{q}}\right)
,
and
p_{0}:=1-t_{1}^{-q}
.
We see that
p_{k}
is a symmetric sequence and decreasing for
k\geq 0
by our assumption.
Then
P_{\xi}(kt_{1})=p_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
is the solution to the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{P_{\xi}}H(\xi),\textrm{ where $\xi\in t_{1}\mathbb{Z}$ satisfies
$\mathbb{P}[|\xi|\geq kt_{1}]\leq(kt_{1})^{-q}$ for all $k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}$.
}

(278)

Indeed, this is a convex optimization problem, and using the Lagrange multiplier method, we see that the local optimality condition can be expressed as
P_{\xi}(kt_{1})=\sum_{l=|k|}^{\infty}\lambda_{l}
for some nonnegative
\lambda_{0},\lambda,\dots
,
where
\lambda_{k}=0
if
\mathbb{P}[|\xi|\geq kt_{1}]<(kt_{1})^{-q}
(complementary slackness).
Since
P_{\xi}(kt_{1})=p_{k}
satisfies local optimality, it is also globally optimal.
Clearly
H(U)
is upper bounded by the value of (
278
).
Setting
l_{k}:=\frac{1}{k^{q}}-\frac{1}{(k+1)^{q}}
, we have

\sum_{k\geq 1}l_{k}=1
,
and

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}l_{k}\ln\frac{1}{l_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{q}{k^{q+1}}\ln\frac{(k+1)^{q+1}}{q}\lesssim_{q}1.

(279)

Hence

\displaystyle H(U)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}p_{k}\ln\frac{1}{p_{k}}

(280)

\displaystyle\leq p_{0}\ln\frac{1}{p_{0}}+\sum_{k\geq 1}2p_{k}\ln\frac{1}{p_{k}}

(281)

\displaystyle\leq t_{1}^{-q}+t_{1}^{-q}\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}l_{k}\ln\frac{2t_{1}^{q}}{l_{k}}\right)

(282)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{q}g(t_{1}^{-q}).

(283)

Next, consider the case of
t_{1}^{-q}>2/3
.
Since
p_{k}
is no longer a decreasing sequence for
k\geq 0
,
the optimizer for (
278
) cannot be expressed in the same way as before.
However, it is possible to bound
H(U)
for this case using the bound in the previous case.
We choose
\bar{t}_{1}=mt_{1}
where
m=\lceil 1.5^{1/q}t_{1}^{-1}\rceil\geq 2
is the minimum integer such that
\bar{t}_{1}^{-q}\leq 2/3
.
Define
\bar{t}_{k}=k\bar{t}_{1}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Given
Z
, define
\bar{U}=\bar{t}_{k}
where
k=\argmin_{l\colon|\bar{t}_{l}|\leq|Z|}|Z-\bar{t}_{l}|
.
Then since
U
can take at most
2m-1
possible values given
\bar{U}
, we have
H(U|\bar{U})\leq\ln(2m+1)
, hence

\displaystyle H(U)

\displaystyle\leq H(\bar{U})+\ln(2m-1)

(284)

\displaystyle\lesssim g(\bar{t}_{1}^{-q})+\ln m

(285)

where we used (
283
) to bound
H(\bar{U})
.
Since
m\asymp_{q}t_{1}^{-1}
, we have
\bar{t}_{1}^{q}\asymp_{q}1
, and
H(U)\lesssim_{q}1+\ln\frac{1}{t_{1}}\lesssim_{q}g(t_{1}^{-q})
.
In summary, in either case, we have established

H(U)\lesssim_{q}g(t_{1}^{-q})
in the case of
r=1
,
and the proof for general
r
is completed by rescaling.
∎

By subadditivity of entropy, Lemma
21
implies a bound on the rate-distortion function of weak
\ell_{q}
balls:

Lemma 22
.

Suppose that
Z\in wB_{q}(r)
is a random variable, and
b>0
is arbitrary.
Then we can find
U=(U_{1},\dots,U_{d})
such that
U\in wB_{q}(r)
almost surely,
\mathbb{E}[\|U-Z\|_{1}]\leq b
, and

\displaystyle H(U)\lesssim_{q}dg\left(d^{-1}\left(\frac{r}{b}\right)^{\frac{q}{1-q}}\right).

(286)

Proof.

Let
r_{j}=\sup_{t}t\mathbb{P}^{1/q}[|Z_{j}|>t]
, so that
\sum_{j}r_{j}^{q}\leq r^{q}
.
Let
b_{j}:=\frac{r_{j}^{q}b}{r^{q}}
.
By Lemma
21
,
we can find
U_{1},\dots,U_{d}
such that
|U_{i}|\leq|Z_{i}|
,
i=1
,…, and that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|U-Z\|_{1}]=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}[|U_{j}-Z_{j}|]\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}b_{j},

(287)

and that

\displaystyle H(U)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}H(U_{j})

(288)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{q}\sum_{j=1}^{d}g\left(\left(\frac{r_{j}}{b_{j}}\right)^{\frac{q}{1-q}}\right)

(289)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}g\left(r_{j}^{q}\left(\frac{b}{r^{q}}\right)^{-\frac{q}{1-q}}\right)

(290)

\displaystyle\leq dg\left(d^{-1}\left(\frac{r}{b}\right)^{\frac{q}{1-q}}\right)

(291)

where the last step used Jensen’s inequality to see that the maximum value is achieved when
r_{j}^{q}=r^{q}/d
.
∎

Next, we prove the following information-theoretic version of Sudakov minoration,
which will be our main tool for upper bound on the “
\ell_{2}
rate-distortion function”.

Lemma 23
.

Suppose that
Z
is a random variable in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and

.
Then for any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\inf_{P_{V|Z}\colon\mathbb{E}[\|V-Z\|_{2}^{2}]\leq\epsilon^{2}}I(V;Z)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\sup_{P_{ZG}}|\mathbb{E}[\left<Z,G\right>]|^{2}

(292)

where the supremum is over couplings of
P_{Z}
and
P_{G}
.

Proof.

The proof follows by applying the lifting argument to the standard Sudakov minoration (see for example
(
Boucheron et al., 2013
, Theorem 13.4)
),
which should upper bound the left side of (
292
) by
\frac{2}{\epsilon^{2}}|\mathbb{E}[\left<Z,G\right>]|^{2}
.
Alternatively, the result follows by taking
\epsilon_{1}\to\infty
in Lemma
9
of the present paper,
although this is somewhat overkill, as Lemma
9
itself is a consequence of the super-Sudakov inequality-a strengthening of the standard Sudakov inequality.
∎

Next, we prove auxiliary results showing that the inner product in (

) can be “essentially” controlled by
\mathbb{E}[\|Z\|_{\ell_{1}}]

Lemma 24
.

Suppose that
Z\in\mathbb{R}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[|Z|]\leq a
,
and
|Z|\leq b
almost surely, where
a\geq 0
and
b>0
.
Let
G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
Then
\mathbb{E}[ZG]\leq 3ebf(a/b)
,
where we defined the function:

\displaystyle f(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}x\sqrt{\ln\frac{1}{x}}&x\in(0,e^{-1})\\
e^{-1}&x\in[e^{-1},\infty)\end{array}\right..

Proof.

First, consider the case of
a=1
and
b>e
.
For any
p>1
, we obtain
\mathbb{E}[|Z|^{p}]\leq b^{p-1}
from the interpolation inequality for the norm.
Therefore, if
p
is such that
\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1-\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1}{\ln b}
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[ZG]

\displaystyle\leq\|G\|_{p^{\prime}}\|Z\|_{p}

(295)

\displaystyle\leq 3\sqrt{p^{\prime}}\left(b^{p-1}\right)^{1/p}

(296)

\displaystyle\leq 3e\sqrt{\ln b}

(297)

where we defined
\|Z\|_{p}:=\mathbb{E}^{1/p}[|Z|^{p}]
.
If
b\leq e
, set
p=\infty
and
p^{\prime}=1
. We get
\mathbb{E}[ZG]\leq 3b
.
This establishes the claim for
a=1
.
For general
a\geq 1
,
consider
Z^{\prime}=Z/a

and we have

|Z^{\prime}|\leq ba^{-1}
almost surely. The result follows by applying the
a=1
case we just proved to
Z^{\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 25
.

Suppose that
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=1}^{d}|Z_{i}|]\leq a
,
and
Z\in wB_{q}(r)
almost surely for some
q>0
.
Let
A
be a matrix with
d
columns and arbitrarily many rows,
where the column vectors satisfy
\|A_{i}\|_{2}\leq 1

.
Let
G
be a standard normal vector whose dimension equals the number of rows of
A
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left<G,AZ\right>]\lesssim rdf(a/rd),

(298)

and

\displaystyle\inf_{P_{V|AZ}\colon\|V-AZ\|^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}}I(V;AZ)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}r^{2}d^{2}f^{2}(a/rd).

(299)

Proof.

Let
g_{i}:=(A^{\top}G)_{i}
, and
a_{i}=\mathbb{E}[|Z_{i}|]

i=1,\dots,d
.
Since
Z\in wB_{q}(r)
,
we have
|Z_{i}|\leq r
, almost surely (this is the only place we use

Then since

is an increasing concave function, and

, we apply Lemma

to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left<g,Z\right>]

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=1}^{d}rf(a_{j}/r)

The claim then follows from Lemma

.

Lemma 26
.

There exists
c_{q}\geq 2
depending only

, such that the following holds:

,

,
\epsilon>0
, and the

norms of the columns of

are not exceeding 1,

\mathbb{E}[\|A(V-Z)\|_{2}^{2}]\leq\epsilon^{2}

\displaystyle I(V;AZ)\lesssim_{q}\left(\frac{r}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{2q}{2-q}}\ln d.

(302)

Proof.

By Lemma
22
, for any
b>0
, we can find
U
such that
U\in wB_{q}(r)
almost surely,
\mathbb{E}[\|U-Z\|_{1}]\leq b
,
and

\displaystyle H(U)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{q}dg\left(d^{-1}\left(\frac{r}{b}\right)^{\frac{q}{1-q}}\right).

(303)

For any

, let

,
and
\epsilon_{u}:=\sqrt{\frac{b_{u}}{b}}\epsilon
.
Note that

We can apply Lemma

\displaystyle I(V;AZ|U)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{q}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\epsilon_{U}^{2}}r^{2}d^{2}f^{2}(b_{U}/rd)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{br^{2}d^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}b_{U}}f^{2}(\frac{b_{U}}{rd})\right]

(305)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{q}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{br^{2}d^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}b_{U}}\cdot(\frac{b_{U}}{rd})^{2}\ln\frac{rd}{b_{U}}\right]

(306)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{b^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\ln\frac{rd}{b}

(307)

where

•

(

) follows since by (

),

(

) follows by applying Jensen’s inequality to the concave function

.

Then (

) and (

\displaystyle I(V;AZ)

\displaystyle\leq I(U;AZ)+I(V;AZ|U)

(308)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{q}dg\left(d^{-1}\left(\frac{r}{b}\right)^{\frac{q}{1-q}}\right)+\frac{b^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\ln\frac{rd}{b}.

We now consider two cases:

Case I:

.

\displaystyle d^{-1}\left(\frac{r}{b}\right)^{\frac{q}{1-q}}=d^{-1}\left(\frac{r}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{2q}{2-q}}<2.

(310)

Since

(

)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{r}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{2q}{2-q}}(\ln(4d)-\frac{2q}{2-q}\ln\frac{r}{\epsilon})+\left(\frac{r}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{2q}{2-q}}(\ln(4d)+\frac{2-2q}{2-q}\ln\frac{r}{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\frac{b^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\ln\frac{rd}{b}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\frac{r}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{2q}{2-q}}\ln d,

(314)

and

\displaystyle dg\left(d^{-1}\left(\frac{r}{b}\right)^{\frac{q}{1-q}}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{q}d\ln\left(d^{-1}\left(\frac{r}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{2q}{2-q}}(\ln\frac{r}{\epsilon})^{\frac{q}{2(1-q)}}\right).

(316)

Let

(

\displaystyle\lesssim_{q}\frac{1}{M}\left(\frac{r}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{2q}{2-q}}(\ln M+\ln\ln\frac{r}{\epsilon})

where the last step is justified as
