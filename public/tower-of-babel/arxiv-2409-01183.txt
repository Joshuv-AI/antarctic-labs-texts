Abstract
. We prove that the Fisher information is monotone decreasing in time along solutions of the space-homogeneous Boltzmann equation for a large class of collision kernels covering all classical interactions derived from systems of particles. For general collision kernels, a sufficient condition for the monotonicity of the Fisher information along the flow is related to the best constant for an integro-differential inequality for functions on the sphere, which belongs in the family of the Log-Sobolev inequalities. As a consequence, we establish the existence of global smooth solutions to the space-homogeneous Boltzmann equation in the main situation of interest where this was not known, namely the regime of very soft potentials. This is opening the path to the completion of both the classical program of qualitative study of space-homogeneous Boltzmann equation, initiated by Carleman, and the program of using the Fisher information in the study of the Boltzmann equation, initiated by McKean. From the proofs and discussion emerges a strengthened picture of the links between kinetic theory, information theory and log-Sobolev inequalities.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The space-homogeneous Boltzmann equation

We consider the space-homogeneous Boltzmann equation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(1.1)

\partial_{t}f=q(f),

where the collision operator is defined by

q(f)=\int_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}(f(w^{\prime})f(v^{\prime})-f(w)f(v))B(|v-w|,\cos\theta)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\;\mathrm{d}w.

We recall that the function
B\colon[0,+\infty)\times[-1,1]\to(0,+\infty)
is known as the
collision kernel
of the operator and velocities
v^{\prime}
and
w^{\prime}
are given by the formulas

v^{\prime}=\frac{v+w}{2}+\frac{|v-w|}{2}\sigma\quad\text{ and }\quad w^{\prime}=\frac{v+w}{2}-\frac{|v-w|}{2}\sigma\quad\text{ and }\quad\cos\theta=\frac{v-w}{|v-w|}\cdot\frac{v^{\prime}-w^{\prime}}{|v^{\prime}-w^{\prime}|}.

A common class of collision kernels
B
considered in the literature, ever since Maxwell pioneered the field, is written as a product of two nonnegative functions
B(|v-w|,\cos\theta)=\alpha(|v-w|/2)\,b(\cos\theta)
. This class includes hard spheres and inverse power-law potentials (force proportional to the power
q
of the distance between molecules,
q\geq 2
):

•

For hard-spheres:
\alpha(r)=r
and
b(\cos\theta)=(\sin(\theta/2))^{-(d-3)}
; in particular,
b\equiv 1
in three dimensions.

•

For inverse power-law potentials:
\alpha(r)=r^{\gamma}
and
b(c)\approx(1-c^{2})^{-(d-1+2s)/2}
, for
\gamma=(q-2d+1)/(q-1)
and
2s=(d-1)/(q-1)
.

The case of Coulomb interaction does not, strictly speaking, fall in the realm of the Boltzmann equation, because the angular singularity is too strong. The obstruction here is physical as well as mathematical: In fact, the most generic condition for the operator
q(f)
to make sense is

(1.2)

\forall e\in S^{d-1},\qquad\int_{S^{d-1}}(1-(e\cdot\sigma)^{2})b(e\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma<+\infty.

(Note that the left-hand side of (
1.2
) is independent of the choice of
e\in S^{d-1}
.) In the case of Coulomb interaction in dimension 3, the integral above is borderline divergent. This was resolved by Landau through a diffusive approximation of the Boltzmann equation, which is important both for theoretical and practical applications in plasma physics and more generally kinetic theory
[
38
]
.

It was in the 1930’s that Carleman initiated the mathematical study of the space-homogeneous Boltzmann equation, asking for the basic properties of localization (decay at infinity), integrability, regularity, and positivity (lower bound). While the assumption of space-homogeneity is of course a huge simplification, it is a legitimate, and even mandatory, step to understand the properties of the Boltzmann equation and in particular the complexity of the Boltzmann operator itself. Carleman
[
7
]
proved the existence of classical solutions for hard spheres in dimension 3, under some symmetry assumptions, and established their convergence to Maxwellian (Gaussian) equilibrium in large time.

This program was then carried and extended by many researchers, covering more and more singular situations. Arkeryd
[
3
]
pioneered the systematic study of hard potentials with cutoff, and later
[
2
]
hard or moderately soft potentials with cutoff. Bobylev
[
6
]
tackled Maxwellian molecules with considerable depth. Goudon
[
15
]
and the third author
[
39
]
considered more general soft and very soft potentials, respectively. Over the years, a number of regularity techniques have been introduced and refined to tackle these situations. These include moment inequalities, integrability and regularity of Boltzmann’s gain operator, Fourier analysis of the angular singularity, integral regularity estimates, iterative schemes in Moser style, maximum principles of various kinds, Harnack inequalities for jump processes. Far from being exhaustive, among contributors we may mention Arkeryd, Desvillettes, Gustafsson, Lions, Mischler, Mouhot, Wennberg and the three authors themselves.
A review of these methods before 2002 can be found in
[
40
]
; further references will be given along the way.

In spite of this, regularity in the most singular range, that is, very soft potentials, has long remained elusive, forcing authors to focus on ad hoc notions of time-integrated weak solutions involving entropy production
[
1
,
8
,
14
,
39
]
.
It was known that
L^{p}
integrability for
p
large enough would yield regularity, but the integrability estimate was nowhere to be found with known tools, leaving experts to wonder about the possibility of regularity blow up.

We are, in this paper, precisely closing this gap by establishing a new a priori estimate in
L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, or more generally
(L^{d/(d-2)}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
(
L^{p}
for any
p
if
d=2
), for a wide class of collision kernels, factorized or not, actually including all classical inverse power-law potentials: hard, soft, very soft, cutoff or noncutoff, and hard spheres, in any dimension. This will be obtained by showing a result of mathematical and physical interest in itself: For a wide class of collision kernels, the usual Fisher information

i(f):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\nabla f|^{2}}{f}\;\mathrm{d}v

is monotone decreasing in time along solutions of the space-homogeneous Boltzmann equation (
1.1
).

Not only does this result allow us to envision the completion of Carleman’s program on the qualitative analysis of the space-homogeneous Boltzmann equation, but it is also, in some sense, the completion of a program initiated by McKean
[
25
]
on Kac’s one-dimensional caricature of the Boltzmann equation. Before saying more on this, let us describe our results more precisely. This will lead us to a detour into the theory of entropic inequalities for diffusion processes.

1.2.
Monotonicity of the Fisher information

Let us start with a simple informal statement about interaction potentials proportional to the inverse power
q
of the intermolecular distance, with
q\geq d-1
(that is, decaying at least as fast as
d
-dimensional Coulomb) and
q>(d+1)/2
(to ensure the convergence of the cross-section for momentum transfer, (
1.2
)).

Theorem 1.1

(The Fisher information decreases along the Boltzmann flow)
.

Let
B
be the collision kernel for hard-spheres, or for any of the physically relevant inverse power-law potentials, then the Fisher information of a solution
f
to (
1.1
) is nonincreasing in time.

Our main result applies to a much wider class of kernels, factorized or not, under some condition. In order to explain it, we first consider the factorized case in which
B
is the product of
\alpha(r/2)
with an angular cross-section,
b(\cos\theta)
. Keeping only the angular variation of the kernel, define the spherical linear Boltzmann operator
\mathcal{B}
, acting on functions
f\colon S^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
, by

(1.3)

\mathcal{B}f(\sigma):=\int_{S^{d-1}}\left(f(\sigma^{\prime})-f(\sigma)\right)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}.

This integro-differential operator defines a diffusion semigroup, and there is a corresponding
carré du champ
operator in the terminology of Bakry-Émery
[
4
]
. Let
\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}
be the iterated carré du champ operator obtained by differentiation first along the flow of the usual heat equation and then along the flow of
\mathcal{B}
(see Section
4
for a precise definition and practical formulas, Lemmas
4.6
and
B.2
). Let
\Lambda_{b}\geq 0
be the largest constant such that the following functional inequality holds: For every function
f\colon S^{d-1}\to(0,\infty)
such that
f(\sigma)=f(-\sigma)
,

(1.4)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{d-1}}

\displaystyle\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}(\log f,\log f)f\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\geq\Lambda_{b}\iint_{S^{d-1}\times S^{d-1}}\frac{(f(\sigma^{\prime})-f(\sigma))^{2}}{f(\sigma^{\prime})+f(\sigma)}b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Obviously the value of
\Lambda_{b}
is a property of
b
; we shall provide recipes to evaluate
\Lambda_{b}
, and examples in which it is positive and other ones for which it is zero. But before we move on to this, let us see how the constant
\Lambda_{b}
allows us to formulate our monotonicity theorem.

Theorem 1.2

(The Fisher information decreases along the Boltzmann flow)
.

Assume that
\alpha
and
b
are such that, for all
r>0
,

\frac{r|\alpha^{\prime}(r)|}{\alpha(r)}\leq 2\sqrt{\Lambda_{b}},

then the Fisher information of any solution
f
to (
1.1
) with collision kernel
B=\alpha(r/2)b(\cos\theta)
is nonincreasing in time.

Remark 1.3
.

Note that in the common case that
\alpha(r)=r^{\gamma}
, the left hand side of the assumption of Theorem
1.2
is
r|\alpha^{\prime}(r)|/\alpha(r)=|\gamma|
. Therefore, the assumption of Theorem
1.2
takes the form of a restriction on the range of
\gamma\in[-2\sqrt{\Lambda_{b}},2\sqrt{\Lambda_{b}}]
.

Remark 1.4
.

In the special case
d=2
, the inequality (
1.4
) simplifies and can be stated in an elementary way: It says that for every function
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
that is
\pi
-periodic, we have

(1.5)

\displaystyle\iint_{[0,\pi]\times[0,\pi/2]}

\displaystyle\bigl|\partial_{\theta}\log f(\theta+h)-\partial_{\theta}\log f(\theta)\bigr|^{2}f(\theta)b(h)\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}h

\displaystyle\geq\Lambda_{b}\iint_{[0,\pi]\times[0,\pi/2]}\frac{(f(\theta+h)-f(\theta))^{2}}{f(\theta+h)+f(\theta)}b(h)\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}h.

To state our more general results, it will suffice to apply the previous criterion to the angular cross-section
b(\cos\theta)=B(r,\cos\theta)
, for each fixed value of
r>0
: Explicitly,

Theorem 1.5

(The Fisher information decreases along the Boltzmann flow, more general version)
.

Assume that
B=B(r,\cos\theta)
satisfies, for all
r>0
and
\theta\in[0,\pi]
,

\frac{r|\partial_{r}B(r,\cos\theta)|}{B(r,\cos\theta)}\leq 2\sqrt{\Lambda_{B(r,\cdot)}}.

Then the Fisher information of any solution
f
to (
1.1
) with collision kernel
B
is nonincreasing in time.

The proof of Theorem
1.5
is the same as that of Theorem
1.2
, through very simple changes and a bit of cumbersome notation, so we shall focus on just Theorem
1.2
. The proof adapts the main ideas of
[
17
]
from the purely diffusive setting of the Landau equation with Coulomb interaction, to the integro-differential setting of the Boltzmann equation. In doing so, we face a major new challenge, namely to explicitly estimate
\Lambda_{b}
from below for concrete kernels
b
. We will explain how this problem is related to integro-differential versions of the log-Sobolev inequality on the sphere, or more properly on its quotient by
\{\pm{\rm Id}\}
, the real projective space – an area of research that is connected to a large body of literature but which in itself had remained relatively unexplored. We will also give two complementary recipes to address this estimate in concrete settings. Along the way, dimension and curvature effects will appear. Here is a main example:

•

In dimension
d=2
there are singular kernels such that
\Lambda_{b}=0
;

•

In dimensions 3 and higher, for any kernel
b
, singular or not, it holds
\Lambda_{b}>0
.

These results are proved in Section
8
and Proposition
7.11
, respectively. Estimating
\Lambda_{b}
explicitly and with fair degree of precision will turn out to be the most difficult part of the analysis in this paper. We shall establish in particular that:

•

When
d\geq 3
, for any kernel satisfying merely (
1.2
), (
1.4
) holds with

\Lambda_{b}\geq d-2.

This is proved in Proposition
7.11
.

•

If
b
is a constant kernel (and in particular for hard spheres in dimension three), we prove that (
1.4
) holds with
\Lambda_{b}\geq d
.
This is proved in Proposition
9.11
.

•

If
b
is the hard sphere kernel in dimension 2, then
\Lambda_{b}\geq\sqrt{2}
. This is explained in Subsection
1.3
.

•

For any dimension, assume that the kernel
b
can be written in the form

(1.6)

b(c)=\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{t}(c)\omega(t)\;\mathrm{d}t,

where
h_{t}
is the heat kernel on the sphere. In this case we show that (
1.4
) holds with
\Lambda_{b}>d
(Proposition
9.3
).

•

Assume that
b_{0}
is a reference kernel for which (
1.4
) holds with parameter
\Lambda_{0}
. Let
b
be another kernel such that the following inequality holds for all
c\in[-1,1]
,

(1.7)

c_{1}\bigg(b_{0}(c)+b_{0}(-c)\bigg)\leq b(c)+b(-c)\leq C_{2}\bigg(b_{0}(c)+b_{0}(-c)\bigg).

Then (
1.4
) holds for
b
with
\Lambda_{b}\geq c_{1}\Lambda_{0}/C_{2}.

1.3.
Comments on the class of kernels satisfying our assumptions

First about high dimensions. In dimensions 4 and higher, hard spheres, as well as inverse power-law kernels decaying at least as fast as
d
-dimensional Coulomb interaction, are all covered by Proposition
7.11
. Then we are left with dimensions 2 and 3.

Next about low-dimensional hard spheres. For
d=3
they are covered both by Proposition
7.11
and by Proposition
9.11
. For
d=2
the hard spheres kernel is
b_{hs}(\cos\theta)=\sin(\theta/2)
and
\alpha_{hs}(r)=r
. Since inequality
1.4
is only required for even functions,
\Lambda_{b}
is unchanged upon replacement of
b
by its symmetric version
[b(c)+b(-c)]/2
. But then it is readily seen that

1\leq b_{hs}(c)+b_{hs}(-c)\leq\sqrt{2}.

Thus, we can apply (
1.7
) to compare the kernels
b_{hs}
and
b_{constant}
. For constant kernels in 2D, we have
\Lambda_{constant}\geq 2
, and then the inequality (
1.4
) holds for
b_{hs}
with parameter
\Lambda_{b_{hs}}\geq\sqrt{2}
, and indeed
2\sqrt{\Lambda_{b}}>1
.

Now, the class of kernels
b
that are written as an integral of a nonnegative weight
\omega(t)
times the heat kernel on the sphere as in (
1.6
) is relatively common in probability theory. These are the jump kernels of Lévy processes constructed from subordination of Brownian motion. For example, the kernel corresponding to the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}
on the sphere takes the form (
1.6
) with the weight
c_{s}t^{-1-s}
(for some
c_{s}>0
depending on
s
and dimension).

Another physically relevant class of collision kernels are those that correspond to a repulsive potential with a power law with exponent
q\in(2,\infty)
in three dimensions. In this case, the kernel
B
has the form
B(r,\cos\theta)=b(\cos\theta)r^{\gamma}
. The exponent is
\gamma=(q-5)/(q-1)
. The angular part
b(\cos\theta)
does not have an explicit formula. It has a nonintegrable singularity as
\theta\to 0
of the form
b(\cos\theta)\approx\theta^{-2-2s}
for
s=1/(q-1)
, similarly to the kernel of
(-\Delta)^{s}
. Its precise description can be found in
[
9
]
or
[
40
]
.
When
q=2
, a classical computation due to Rutherford
[
26
]
yields,

b(\cos\theta)=\sin(\theta/2)^{-2(d-1)}.

Remarquably, it coincides in dimension
d=2
with the kernel of the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}
restricted to
\pi
-periodic functions (see for instance
[
21
, Proposition 3]
).

In general, the exact formula for the angular kernel
b
is only defined implicitly in terms of certain complicated integrals.

When
s\in[0,3/4]
(which corresponds to
q\geq 7/3
), we have
\gamma\in[-2,1]
. In this range, Theorem
1.2
applies since
\Lambda_{b}\geq 1
for just any kernel
b
in three dimensions, and
|\gamma|\leq 2
.

It is not absurd to expect the power-law kernels to belong to the class of subordinate kernels, since it is a smooth function away from
\theta=0
with a similar singularity as the fractional Laplacian. But we have no clue how to attack such a conjecture. So we gave ourselves a more modest task, and still sufficient for applications to the Boltzmann equation, which is to numerically check that these inverse power law kernels remain close enough to kernels corresponding to a subordinate function. It turns out that such is the case: If
c_{1}
and
C_{2}
are the optimal constants in (
1.7
), we found empirically, for all values of
q
in the relevant range, a weight function
\omega
with a ratio
c_{1}/C_{2}
larger than
0.9
. The value of
\Lambda_{b}
can be computed for this weight function
\omega
using the formula in Proposition
9.3
. Our computations show convincingly that the assumption of Theorem
1.2
is satisfied by a confortable margin for all power-law kernels in the full range of parameters in 3D and 2D. We review our numerical computations in Appendix
A
.

Summarizing, while Theorem
1.2
requires a certain assumption on the collision kernel
B
, this assumption is satisfied by all the common scenarios of physical relevance, including the whole range of 3D inverse power law forces and hard spheres, and also the whole classical range in other dimensions.

1.4.
Global smooth solutions of the Boltzmann equation with very soft potentials

In
[
17
]
, the monotonicity of the Fisher information for the Landau equation is used to prove that there exist global-in-time smooth solutions. In a similar way, we establish here the existence of global smooth solutions for the homogeneous non-cutoff Boltzmann equation with very soft potentials. It is a consequence of the monotonicity of the Fisher information proved in our Theorem
1.2
and Sobolev embedding, together with existing results in the literature. Thus, the solutions of the space-homogeneous non-cutoff Boltzmann equation, under the assumption of Theorem
1.2
, cannot blow up even in the very soft potential range.

For simplicity we consider a collision kernel of the form
B(r,\cos\theta)=\alpha(r/2)b(\cos\theta)
. The typical non-cutoff assumptions are that

(1.8)

\alpha(r)=r^{\gamma}\qquad\text{and}\qquad b(\cos\theta)\approx|\sin\theta|^{-d+1-2s}.

The existence of global smooth solutions for the space-homogeneous Boltzmann equation with
\gamma+2s\geq 0
is currently well understood (see for example
[
18
]
). In the case of very soft potentials (i.e.
\gamma+2s<0
), the possibility of finite time blow up has been a well known open problem for some time. Our monotonicity theorem resolves this problem.

Theorem 1.6

(Global existence for very soft potentials)
.

Let
d=3
and
B
be a collision kernel of the form (
1.8
) with
s\in(0,1)
and
\gamma\in(-d,0]
. Assume that
B
satisfies the hypothesis of Theorem
1.2
. There exists an exponent
q
(large enough) so that if
f_{0}
is any initial data in
L^{\infty}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
the Boltzmann equation (
1.1
) has a global classical solution in
[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
with initial data
f_{0}
.

The restriction to three dimensions, as well as the precise assumption
f_{0}\in L^{\infty}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, are the assumptions of the short-time existence results that we reference from the literature. Any advancement in the short-time well posedness theory would immediately be reflected in an enhancement of Theorem
1.6
to a larger class of initial data. While we do not wish to be overoptimistic, everything seems to indicate that present-day tools are enough to work out more general dimensions as well as relaxed conditions, say
f_{0}\in L^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
p
large enough.

The amount of smoothness of the solution
f
depends on the decay of the initial data
f_{0}
. If the initial data
f_{0}
belongs to
L^{\infty}_{q}
for every exponent
q>0
, then the solution
f
is
C^{\infty}
. If
f_{0}\in L^{\infty}_{q}
for some large exponent
q
, then the solution
f
will be
C^{k}
differentiable for some
k
depending on
q
. This is simply from the regularity results for the non-cutoff Boltzmann equation with soft potentials that are already available in the literature (see
[
22
,
23
,
19
]
).

We prove Theorem
1.6
in Section
10
as a simple consequence of Theorem
1.2
together with some results in the literature.

1.5.
Integro-differential Log-Sobolev inequalities on the sphere

As explained above, the proof of the monotonicity of the Fisher information for the space-homogeneous Boltzmann equation consists first in reducing the problem to the proof of a functional inequality on the sphere, namely (
1.4
) (Theorem
1.2
), and then checking that this inequality holds true in various scenarios (Subsection
1.3
). To accomplish this program, we shall combine three inequalities: the first one relates the iterated carré du champ
\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}
to the Fisher information (see (
5.1
)), the second one is of Poincaré-type (see (
5.2
)) and the third one is an elementary inequality between nonlinear integral quantities (see (
5.3
)).

The first of those inequalities, (
5.1
). is interesting in itself, and also implies, by a classical semigroup argument à la Bakry–Émery, the following new inequality (see Definition
4.7
and Proposition
5.4
):

(1.9)

\iint_{S^{d-1}\times S^{d-1}}(f^{\prime}-f)\log(f^{\prime}/f)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\geq 4c_{K}\left(\int_{S^{d-1}}f\log f\;\mathrm{d}\sigma-\left(\int_{S^{d-1}}f\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)\log\left(\fint_{S^{d-1}}f\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)\right).

In the diffusive limit when
b
concentrates on grazing collisions, inequality (
1.9
) reduces to the classical log-Sobolev inequality on the sphere
[
5
]
. But on the other hand, at the other extreme when
b
is the constant kernel, (
1.9
) becomes the spherical version of the entropy-entropy production inequality established for the Boltzmann equation with super-hard spheres by the third author
[
41
]
, following a previous joint work with Toscani
[
34
]
. So (
1.9
) bridges these two families of entropic functional inequalities coming from the study of heat equation on the one hand, and Boltzmann equation on the other hand.

1.6.
Review of literature

We do not aim at exhaustivity here, and refer to the lecture notes by the third author
[
38
]
for a broader perspective, historical notes and many other references on Fisher information and kinetic theory. Below is a selection of some of the works which are most directly relevant to the present study.

1.6.1.
Fisher information and kinetic theory

H. P. McKean
[
25
]
introduced Fisher information in kinetic theory with a view of understanding the convergence to equilibrium. He proved that the Fisher information is nonincreasing along solutions of the Kac equation, a one-dimensional caricature of the spatially homogeneous Boltzmann equation with Maxwell molecules. He also established the very first result of exponential convergence for an equation of Boltzmann type. A quarter of century later, G. Toscani
[
32
]
extended McKean’s monotonicity result to the case of the space-homogeneous Boltzmann equation with Maxwell molecules in 2D. Then the third author
[
35
]
extended Toscani’s result to any dimension, still for Maxwell molecules. In
[
36
]
, he provided a direct proof for the space-homogeneous Landau equation, again with Maxwell molecules. Then the subject remained still for nearly another quarter of century, and many experts believed that this monotonicity was a peculiarity of Maxwell molecules. True, there was some numerical evidence indicating that the decrease would hold in more generality, but it was generally considered as non-conclusive. However, recently, N. Guillen and the second author
[
17
]
proved that the Fisher information decreases for the Landau equation for a general family of interaction potentials including the Coulomb potential in 3D. Our technique of reduction to a nonlinear integro-differential inequality on the sphere originates from that work.

1.6.2.
Log-Sobolev inequalities

The log-Sobolev inequality was first established for the Gaussian measure by A.J. Stam
[
31
]
in the context of information theory, and rediscovered independently by L. Gross
[
16
]
for the purpose of infinite-dimensional Sobolev analysis.
Then D. Bakry and M. Émery proposed a robust proof based on the study of the carré du champ operator
\Gamma
and the iterated carré du champ
\Gamma^{2}

[
4
]
. Their analysis based on curvature-dimension bounds opened the path to a rich field with a number of ramifications in statistical physics, Riemannian geometry, and more generally the analysis of diffusion processes
[
5
,
37
]
.

N. Guillen and the second author proved in
[
17
]
that the
\Gamma^{2}
criterion appearing in
[
5
, Proposition 5.7.3]
holds true on the projective plane
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
with an explicit constant – see (
9.2
). This estimate plays a key role in their proof of the monotonicity of the Fisher information for the Landau equation, and there was need to get a good estimate on the constant, applying known results was not sufficient. The equation (
1.4
) can be understood as an integro-differential version of this
\Gamma_{2}
criterion on
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{d-1}
. The constant obtained in
[
17
]
for the three dimensional case was recently improved and extended to any dimension by S. Ji
[
24
]
.

The study of log-Sobolev inequalities in integro-differential forms is less advanced. The reader is referred first to
[
42
,
10
]
and then to
[
11
]
and the numerous references therein.

1.6.3.
Existence of global solutions for the space-homogeneous Boltzmann equation

The study of existence of classical solutions for the space-homogeneous Boltzmann equation in the cutoff case is a classical subject going back to the work of Carleman
[
7
]
. In the noncutoff case, the most complete result currently in the literature is by L. He in
[
18
]
when
\gamma+2s\geq 0
. In the same work, for the case of very soft potentials
\gamma+2s<0
, the existence of solutions is presented only in a small interval of time. The existence of global strong solutions for very soft potentials (at least in the range
-2<\gamma+2s<0
where integral in time weak solutions can be constructed under only finite entropy and moment assumptions), has been a rather well known open problem in the community of kinetic theory for quite some time. The first weak solutions for this regime were constructed by the third author using entropy production estimates
[
39
]
at the end of the nineties; these “
H
-solutions” exist for all times and can be used for some qualitative results, see in particular the proof of convergence to equilibrium by Carlen–Carvalho–Lu
[
8
]
. But it is not clear how regular or irregular they are, neither whether a uniqueness result can be obtained. This motivated a number of studies in the past decade. In particular, the second author obtained a conditional
L^{\infty}
estimate in
[
30
]
. There is an estimate in weighted
L^{p}
spaces for the entropy production by J. Chaker and the second author in
[
12
]
. A partial regularity result was obtained by F. Golse and the first and second authors
[
14
]
. C. Henderson, S. Snelson and A. Tarfulea established short-time existence and continuation criterion in
[
19
]
and
[
20
]
that apply also in the space inhomogeneous case. All those results were partial or conditional: we finally solve this quest, at least for all classical physically relevant kernels, in our Theorem
1.6
, thanks to the new Fisher information a priori estimate. As for the strongly related problem of global smoothness for the Landau equation with Coulomb potential, it was addressed similarly by N. Guillen and the second author in
[
17
]
.

Preliminaries

2.1.
Short time existence and continuation criterion

The best short-time existence result and continuation criterion currently available for the Boltzmann equation in the very soft potential range is due to Chris Henderson, Stanley Snelson, and Andrei Tarfulea in
[
19
]
and
[
20
]
. In the context of the homogeneous Boltzmann equation with very soft potentials, their results can be summarized in the following theorem.
Its statement involves the following norm,

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}=\|\langle v\rangle^{q}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}.

Theorem 2.1

(Short-time existence and continuation criterion –
[
19
,
20
]
)
.

Assume
d=3
and that the collision kernel
B
has the form (
1.8
).
If
f_{0}\in L^{\infty}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for some
q>3+2s
, then there exists a solution
f
to the Boltzmann equation (
1.1
) in
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
for some
T>0
.

This solution
f
is strictly positive, and for any exponents
k,p\geq 0
, there exists
q=q(k,p)
so that if
f_{0}\in L^{\infty}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, then for any
\tau>0
,

D^{k}f\in L^{\infty}([\tau,T],L^{\infty}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})).

Moreover, if such a solution exists for all
T<T_{\max}
, but it does not exist for any
T>T_{max}
, then necessarily

\lim_{t\to T_{max}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{k_{1}}f^{p_{1}}\;\mathrm{d}v=+\infty

Here,
k_{1}=1-3(\gamma+2s)/2s
and
p_{1}
is any exponent such that
p_{1}>3/(3+\gamma+2s)
.

Remark 2.2
.

The results in
[
19
,
20
]
apply to space-inhomogeneous equations as well. Much of the difficulty of these papers is due to this. There is an assumption in the main result of these papers about a lower bound for
f_{0}
in certain balls that is only relevant in the space inhomogeneous setting.

2.2.
Smooth decaying solutions

We recall that the Fisher information is finite for smooth and decaying functions. The result is stated in terms of the following weight Sobolev norms: for an integer
k\geq 1
and a positive exponent
q>0
,

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H^{k}_{q}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}\|\langle v\rangle^{q}D^{\alpha}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{k}_{q}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\|\langle v\rangle^{q}D^{\alpha}f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Lemma 2.3

(Smooth decaying functions have finite Fisher information –
[
33
]
)
.

For all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
C_{\varepsilon}>0
(only depending on dimension and
\varepsilon
) such that,

I(f)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\left(\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{2}_{(d/2)+\varepsilon}}+\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{1}_{(d/2)-1+\varepsilon}}+\|f\|_{L^{2}_{(d/2)-2+\varepsilon}}\right)\leq C_{\varepsilon}\|f\|_{H^{2}_{(d/2)+\varepsilon}}.

Theorem
2.1
ensures that there exists a strictly positive classical solution
f
of (
1.1
) at least in a short interval of time
[0,T]
. Assuming that
f_{0}
has an appropriate decay, the solution satisfies
\langle v\rangle^{q}f(t,v)\in L^{\infty}
, for some large exponent
q
. Moreover, for any
\tau>0
,

D^{2}f\in L^{\infty}([\tau,T],L^{\infty}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{3})).

Lemma
2.3
above ensures that these solutions have a finite Fisher information for any
t>0
. This is the only notion of solution we need to consider in this paper. In Theorem
1.6
we prove that these solutions can be extended globally in time without singularities. Thus, weak solutions are no longer necessary in this context.

2.3.
Commutation of
\Delta
and
\mathcal{B}

Lemma 2.4

(The operators
\mathcal{B}
and
\Delta
commute)
.

For any smooth function
f:S^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\Delta\mathcal{B}f=\mathcal{B}\Delta f
.

Proof.

Let us assume without loss of generality that both
f
and
b
are smooth. The general case follows by a standard approximation argument.

We compute
\Delta\mathcal{B}f
by differentiating inside the integral.

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{B}f

\displaystyle=\Delta\int_{S^{d-1}}(f(\sigma^{\prime})-f(\sigma))b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{d-1}}-\Delta f(\sigma)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)+(f(\sigma^{\prime})-f(\sigma))\Delta_{\sigma}[b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)]\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}.

A practical way to compute the spherical Laplacian of a function is to extend the function to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and use the formula for the Laplacian in polar coordinates. With this in mind, we write
u(x)=b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot x)
for
x=r\sigma
and compute

\displaystyle\Delta_{x}[b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot x)]

\displaystyle=\partial_{rr}[b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot x)]+\frac{d-1}{r}\partial_{r}[b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot x)]+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Delta_{\sigma}[b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot x)].

We can compute the Laplacian in Euclidian coordinates in the left hand side and partial derivatives w.r.t.
r
in the right hand side and get,

\displaystyle b^{\prime\prime}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot x)

\displaystyle=b^{\prime\prime}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot x)(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)^{2}+\frac{d-1}{r}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)b^{\prime}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot x)+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Delta_{\sigma}[b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot x)].

Evaluating at
r=1
, we obtain,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\sigma}[b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)]

\displaystyle=\left(1-(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)^{2}\right)b^{\prime\prime}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)-(d-1)(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)b^{\prime}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma).

The only thing that matters for this proof is that
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
are exchangeable in the formula above. It means that
\Delta_{\sigma}[b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)]=\Delta_{\sigma^{\prime}}[b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)]
. Introducing this fact in our computation above for
\Delta\mathcal{B}f
, and then integrating by parts, we get

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{B}f

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{d-1}}-\Delta f(\sigma)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)+(f(\sigma^{\prime})-f(\sigma))\Delta_{\sigma^{\prime}}[b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)]\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{d-1}}-\Delta f(\sigma)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)+\Delta f(\sigma^{\prime})b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}\Delta f.\qed

3.
Lifting and polar coordinates

3.1.
Lifting the problem

Following
[
17
]
, we lift the problem to the double variables
(v,w)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+d}
. For a function
F:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to[0,\infty)
, we set

I(F):=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\frac{|\nabla F|^{2}}{F}\;\mathrm{d}w\;\mathrm{d}v.

For a function
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we can consider the marginals with respect to the two variables
v
and
w
.
If
F(v,w)=F(w,v)
, these marginals coincide. We write
\pi F
to denote this marginal

\pi F(v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(v,w)\;\mathrm{d}w.

A classical property of the Fisher information is that it is larger than the sum of the Fisher information of marginals.
If
F
is symmetric, this means that

(3.1)

I(F)\geq 2i(\pi F).

We also define
Q(F)
so that
q(f)=\pi Q(f\otimes f)
,

(3.2)

Q(F):=\int_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}(F(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})-F(v,w))B(|v-w|,\cos\theta)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}.

Note that
Q(F)
is a linear elliptic integro-differential operator whose kernel is supported on
(d-1)
-dimensional spheres.

We write the integral in
Q
with respect to the variable
\sigma^{\prime}\in S^{d-1}
because we will soon write a change of variables
v=z+r\sigma
and
w=z-r\sigma
. We want to differentiate between the
\sigma\in S^{d-1}
from
(v,w)
after the change of variables, and the
\sigma^{\prime}
of the integral in the expression for
Q
so that
v^{\prime}=z+r\sigma^{\prime}
and
w^{\prime}=z-r\sigma^{\prime}
.

Because of (
3.1
), it is explained in
[
17
, Lemma 3.4 and Remark 3.5]
that we have for
F=f\otimes f
,

\langle i^{\prime}(f),q(f)\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\langle I^{\prime}(F),Q(F)\rangle.

In order to prove Theorem
1.2
, it is thus sufficient to prove that the following inequality holds for every smooth function
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to[0,\infty)
that is symmetric
F(v,w)=F(w,v)
,

(3.3)

\langle I^{\prime}(F),Q(F)\rangle\leq 0.

3.2.
Polar coordinates

It is useful to consider the following change of variables,

\displaystyle v=z+r\sigma,

\displaystyle w=z-r\sigma.

Here
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\sigma\in S^{d-1}
and
r\in[0,\infty)
.

We can write the Fisher information with respect to these variables,

(3.4)

\displaystyle I(F)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iiint\left(\frac{|\nabla_{z}F|^{2}}{F}+\frac{|\partial_{r}F|^{2}}{F}+\frac{|\nabla_{\sigma}F|^{2}}{r^{2}F}\right)r^{d-1}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}z,

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle I_{parallel}+I_{radial}+I_{spherical}.

For the sake of clarity, we abuse notation from now on by writing
F
both for the function defined in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and the one defined in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,+\infty)\times S^{d-1}
in terms of polar coordinates.

We also write
Q(F)
in terms of the new variables and realize that it takes a very simple form. In fact, it only acts on the spherical variable
\sigma
,

Q(F)=\alpha(r)\int_{\sigma^{\prime}\in S^{d-1}}(F(z,r,\sigma^{\prime})-F(z,r,\sigma))b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}.

The operator
Q
consists in applying the spherical diffusion operator
\mathcal{B}
to the function
F(z,r,\cdot)
, for every fixed value of
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
r>0
, and then multiplying it by the scalar function
\alpha(r)
.

To prove Theorem
1.2
, we differentiate each term
I_{parallel}
,
I_{radial}
and
I_{spherical}
in the direction of
Q(F)
.

Lemma 3.1

(Differentiating
I_{parallel}
)
.

The following identity holds,

\langle I^{\prime}_{parallel}(F),Q(F)\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\iiiint-\alpha\left|\nabla_{z}\log F^{\prime}-\nabla_{z}\log F\right|^{2}(F^{\prime}+F)\ b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)r^{d-1}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}z.

Here, we write
F=F(z,r,\sigma)
and
F^{\prime}=F(z,r,\sigma^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We start from the integral defining
I_{parallel}
and differentiate in the direction
Q
. Using
\nabla_{z}\alpha=0
, we symmetrize the integral.

\displaystyle\langle

\displaystyle I^{\prime}_{parallel}(F),Q(F)\rangle=\iiint\left(2\nabla_{z}\log F\cdot\nabla_{z}Q-|\nabla_{z}\log F|^{2}Q\right)\ r^{d-1}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\iiiint\alpha\big(2\nabla_{z}\log F\cdot(\nabla_{z}F^{\prime}-\nabla_{z}F)-|\nabla_{z}\log F|^{2}(F^{\prime}-F)\big)\ b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)r^{d-1}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}z,

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\iiiint\alpha\Big(2(\nabla_{z}\log F^{\prime}-\nabla_{z}\log F)\cdot(\nabla_{z}F^{\prime}-\nabla_{z}F)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-(|\nabla_{z}\log F^{\prime}|^{2}-|\nabla_{z}\log F|^{2})(F^{\prime}-F)\Big)\ b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)r^{d-1}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\iiiint\alpha(\nabla_{z}\log F^{\prime}-\nabla_{z}\log F)\cdot\Big(2(\nabla_{z}F^{\prime}-\nabla_{z}F)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-(\nabla_{z}\log F^{\prime}+\nabla_{z}\log F)(F^{\prime}-F)\Big)\ b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)r^{d-1}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\iiiint\alpha|\nabla_{z}\log F^{\prime}-\nabla_{z}\log F|^{2}(F^{\prime}+F)\ b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)r^{d-1}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}z.\qed

Lemma 3.2

(Differentiating
I_{radial}
)
.

The following inequaliy holds,

\langle I^{\prime}_{radial}(F),Q(F)\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2}\iiiint\frac{(\alpha^{\prime})^{2}}{\alpha}\frac{(F^{\prime}-F)^{2}}{F^{\prime}+F}\ b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)r^{d-1}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}z.

Here, we write
F=F(z,r,\sigma)
and
F^{\prime}=F(z,r,\sigma^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We write down the integral and symmetrize as in the previous lemma.

\displaystyle\langle

\displaystyle I^{\prime}_{radial}(F),Q(F)\rangle=\iiint\left(2\partial_{r}\log F\cdot\partial_{r}Q-|\partial_{r}\log F|^{2}Q\right)\ r^{d-1}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}z,

note that the integrand in
Q
includes a factor
\alpha
that depends on
r
,

\displaystyle=\iiiint\alpha(r)\big(2\partial_{r}\log F\cdot(\partial_{r}F^{\prime}-\partial_{r}F)-|\partial_{r}\log F|^{2}(F^{\prime}-F)\big)\ b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)r^{d-1}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\qquad+\iiiint 2\alpha^{\prime}(r)\ \partial_{r}\log F(F^{\prime}-F)\ b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)r^{d-1}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}z,

we symmetrize in
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\iiiint\alpha|\partial_{r}\log F^{\prime}-\partial_{r}\log F|^{2}(F^{\prime}+F)\ b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)r^{d-1}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\qquad+\iiiint\alpha^{\prime}(r)\ (\partial_{r}\log F-\partial_{r}\log F^{\prime})(F^{\prime}-F)\ b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)r^{d-1}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}z,

we complete squares,

\displaystyle\leq\iiiint\frac{\alpha^{\prime}(r)^{2}}{2\alpha(r)}\frac{(F^{\prime}-F)^{2}}{F^{\prime}+F}\ b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)r^{d-1}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}z.\qed

We are left with differentiating the spherical part of the Fisher information. It is a more delicate computation that we explain in a later section. The case
d=2
is much simpler and we explain it right here.

Lemma 3.3

(Differentiating
I_{spherical}
in 2D)
.

Let us assume that
d=2
. In this case we can write the function
F=F(z,r,\theta)
where
\theta
is an angular variable and
F
is
\pi
-periodic with respect to
\theta
.

The following identity holds

\langle I^{\prime}_{spherical}(F),Q(F)\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\iiiint-\alpha\left|\partial_{\theta}\log F^{\prime}-\partial_{\theta}\log F\right|^{2}(F^{\prime}+F)\ b(\cos(\theta^{\prime}-\theta))r^{d-3}\;\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}z.

Here, we write
F=F(z,r,\theta)
and
F^{\prime}=F(z,r,\theta^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We write down the integral and symmetrize as in Lemma
3.1
.
∎

4.
Carré du champ operators on the sphere

In this section we analyze the
carré du champ
operators and the corresponding Bakry-Émery formalism on the sphere corresponding to the heat flow and the flow by the integro-differential diffusion
\mathcal{B}
.

4.1.
Definitions and first properties

The
carré du champ
operator associated to the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the sphere
S^{d-1}
consists in the following formula,

(4.1)

\Gamma_{\Delta}(f,g):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta(fg)-g\Delta f-f\Delta g\right).

A direct computation shows that
\Gamma_{\Delta}(f,g)
is simply equal to
\nabla f\cdot\nabla g
.

We may consider the carré du champ operator associated to the operator
\mathcal{B}
instead of the Laplacian,

(4.2)

\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}(f,g):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{B}(fg)-g\mathcal{B}f-f\mathcal{B}g\right).

From a direct computation, one can verify that

\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}(f,g)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{S^{d-1}}\left(f(\sigma^{\prime})-f(\sigma)\right)\left(g(\sigma^{\prime})-g(\sigma)\right)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}.

Some familiar looking integration-by-parts formulas hold with the operators
\Delta
,
\mathcal{B}
and their corresponding carré du champ operators. We state them in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1

(Carré du champ operators and integration by parts)
.

Given any two functions
f,g:S^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
, the following identities hold.

\displaystyle\int_{S^{d-1}}\Gamma_{\Delta}(f,\mathcal{B}g)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma=\int_{S^{d-1}}\Gamma_{\Delta}(\mathcal{B}f,g)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma,

\displaystyle\int_{S^{d-1}}\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}(f,\Delta g)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma=\int_{S^{d-1}}\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}(\Delta f,g)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Proof.

This is a simple consequence of the fact that
\Delta
and
\mathcal{B}
commute, and they are self-adjoint.
∎

For a function
f\colon S^{d-1}\to(0,\infty)
, we may write its usual entropy as
\mathcal{S}(f)
and its Fisher information
\mathcal{I}(f)
,

\mathcal{S}(f):=-\int_{S^{d-1}}f\log f\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{I}(f):=\int_{S^{d-1}}\frac{|\nabla f|^{2}}{f}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.

When we differentiate the entropy with respect to the heat flow, we obtain the usual Fisher information.

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(f),\Delta f\rangle

\displaystyle=-\int_{S^{d-1}}\Delta f\cdot\log f\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{d-1}}\Gamma_{\Delta}(f,\log f)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{d-1}}f\Gamma_{\Delta}(\log f,\log f)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{d-1}}\frac{|\nabla f|^{2}}{f}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}(f).

When we differentiate the entropy along the flow of
\mathcal{B}
instead, we obtain a different integral quantity.

(4.3)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(f),\mathcal{B}f\rangle

\displaystyle=-\int_{S^{d-1}}\mathcal{B}f\cdot\log f\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{d-1}}\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}(f,\log f)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{S^{d-1}\times S^{d-1}}(f(\sigma^{\prime})-f(\sigma))\log\left(\frac{f(\sigma^{\prime})}{f(\sigma)}\right)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.

It is easy to check that, due to the monotonicity of the
\log
function,
\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}(f,\log f)\geq 0
pointwise. However, unlike the case of the Laplacian, in general we have
\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}(f,\log f)\neq f\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}(\log f,\log f)
.

We will now discuss the second order
carré du champ
operators associated to
\Delta
and
\mathcal{B}
. The usual second order carré du champ is given by

\Gamma_{2}(f,g)=\Gamma^{2}_{\Delta,\Delta}(f,g)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta\Gamma(f,g)-\Gamma(\Delta f,g)-\Delta\Gamma(f,\Delta g)\right).

The second order carré du champ operators that we want to consider mix the flow of
\Delta
and
\mathcal{B}
. We define

(4.4)

\begin{cases}\Gamma^{2}_{\Delta,\mathcal{B}}(f,g)&:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}(f,g)-\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}(\Delta f,g)-\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}(f,\Delta g)\right),\\[4.30554pt]
\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}(f,g)&:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{B}\Gamma_{\Delta}(f,g)-\Gamma_{\Delta}(\mathcal{B}f,g)-\Gamma_{\Delta}(f,\mathcal{B}g)\right),\\[4.30554pt]
\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{B}}(f,g)&:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{B}\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}(f,g)-\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{B}f,g)-\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}(f,\mathcal{B}g)\right).\end{cases}

Since the operators
\Delta
and
\mathcal{B}
commute, it is not difficult to verify that
\Gamma_{\Delta,\mathcal{B}}^{2}(f,g)=\Gamma_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}^{2}(f,g)
. We include the definition of
\Gamma_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{B}}
here for completeness only, since it is not used in this paper.

4.2.
Derivatives of the Fisher information along the flow of
\mathcal{B}

With these operators, we can write an expression for the derivative of the Fisher information along the flow of
\mathcal{B}
.

Lemma 4.2

(Differentiating Fisher along the flow of
\mathcal{B}
)
.

\langle\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(f),\mathcal{B}f\rangle=-2\int_{S^{d-1}}f\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}(\log f,\log f)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Proof.

We start from the identity for the integrand
|\nabla f|^{2}/f=f\Gamma_{\Delta}(\log f,\log f)
and differentiate it along the direction
\mathcal{B}f
.

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(f),\mathcal{B}f\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{d-1}}\mathcal{B}f\Gamma_{\Delta}(\log f,\log f)+2f\Gamma_{\Delta}(\log f,\mathcal{B}f/f)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{d-1}}f\mathcal{B}\Gamma_{\Delta}(\log f,\log f)+2\nabla f\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{\mathcal{B}f}{f}\right)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{d-1}}f\mathcal{B}\Gamma_{\Delta}(\log f,\log f)+2\nabla\log f\cdot\nabla\mathcal{B}f-2\mathcal{B}f\Gamma_{\Delta}(\log f,\log f)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=-2\int_{S^{d-1}}f\Gamma_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}^{2}(\log f,\log f)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.

To get the last line, we used the fact that
\mathcal{B}
is self-adjoint together with the following equality,

\int\nabla\log f\cdot\nabla\mathcal{B}f\;\mathrm{d}\sigma=\int\nabla\mathcal{B}\log f\cdot\nabla f\;\mathrm{d}\sigma=\int f\Gamma_{\Delta}(\mathcal{B}\log f,\log f)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.\qed

The integrand in Lemma
4.2
is nonnegative for all
\sigma\in S^{d-1}
. To see that, we will derive some explicit expressions for
\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}
. It involves the quantity
\nabla\mathcal{B}f
. Naively, we would expect its expression to contain a factor
(\nabla f^{\prime}-\nabla f)
in the integrand. However,
\nabla f(\sigma^{\prime})
and
\nabla f(\sigma)
belong to the tangent bundle of
S^{d-1}
at different points. We cannot directly subtract them. We propose two methods to circunvent this difficulty and derive a correct expression for
\nabla\mathcal{B}f
. One is to differentiate along the vector fields used in
[
17
]
for the Landau equation, the other is to use the maps from the tangent spaces at different points that are defined in
[
35
]
. In this paper, we use the second approach, which is more natural in the context of the Boltzmann equation. The vector fields used in
[
17
]
are more natural in the context of the Landau equation. In Appendix
B
, we explain how the formulas involving
\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}
can be written in terms of these vector fields.

4.3.
Comparing vectors in different tangent spaces

A possible approach to make computations involving
\nabla\mathcal{B}f
is to use the linear transformations between
T_{\sigma}S^{d-1}
and
T_{\sigma^{\prime}}S^{d-1}
described in
[
35
]
. We describe the method here.

4.3.1.
The gradient of the linear integro-differential operator

Following
[
35
]
, we define the linear transformation
M_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

(4.5)

M_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}(x)=(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)x-(\sigma\cdot x)\sigma^{\prime}.

Note that
M_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}(x)\perp\sigma
for any values of
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in S^{d-1}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The following lemma is proved in
[
35
, Lemma 2]
. The proof is essentially to integrate by parts with respect to
\sigma^{\prime}
, and work out the resulting formulas.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\sigma\in S^{d-1}
be fixed, and
f:S^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
. Then

\int_{S^{d-1}}f(\sigma^{\prime})\Pi_{\sigma^{\perp}}(\sigma^{\prime})\;b^{\prime}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}=\int_{S^{d-1}}M_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}(\nabla f(\sigma^{\prime}))\;b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}.

Here
b^{\prime}
is the derivative of the function
b
, and
\Pi_{\sigma^{\perp}}(x)=x-(x\cdot\sigma)\sigma
stands for the projection operator onto the tangent space
T_{\sigma}S^{d-1}
.

Lemma
4.3
is useful to provide an expression for
\nabla\mathcal{B}f(\sigma)
. This is expressed in the following lemma, which is similar to Lemma
B.4
but in terms of these new operators
M_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}
.

Lemma 4.4

(Gradient of the integro-differential operator)
.

Let
f:S^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
be an arbitrary smooth function. The following expression holds for
\nabla\mathcal{B}f(\sigma)
.

\nabla\mathcal{B}f(\sigma)=\int_{S^{d-1}}\left(M_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}\nabla f(\sigma^{\prime})-\nabla f(\sigma)\right)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}.

Proof.

We start from the integral expression for
\mathcal{B}f
and differentiate inside the integral sign.

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{B}f(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\nabla\int(f(\sigma^{\prime})-f(\sigma))b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime},

we differentiate the integrand with respect to
\sigma
,

\displaystyle=\int-\nabla f(\sigma)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)+(f(\sigma^{\prime})-f(\sigma))b^{\prime}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\Pi_{\sigma^{\perp}}(\sigma^{\prime})\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime},

we apply Lemma
4.3
to the second term,

\displaystyle=\int-\nabla f(\sigma)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)+M_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}\nabla_{\sigma^{\prime}}(f(\sigma^{\prime})-f(\sigma))b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{d-1}}\left(M_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}\nabla f(\sigma^{\prime})-\nabla f(\sigma)\right)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}.\qed

4.3.2.
Formula for an iterated carré du champ

We also define

(4.6)

P_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}(x):=M_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}(x)+(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot x)\sigma=(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)x+(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot x)\sigma-(\sigma\cdot x)\sigma^{\prime}.

Note that if
x\perp\sigma^{\prime}
, then
P_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}(x)=M_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}(x)
, and in particular
P_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}(x)\perp\sigma
. The operator
P_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}(x)
maps
T_{\sigma^{\prime}}S^{d-1}
into
T_{\sigma}S^{d-1}
.

We will only apply
P_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}(x)
to vectors in
T_{\sigma^{\prime}}S^{d-1}
. In this subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the operators
P_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}(x)
and
M_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}(x)
are indistinguishable. The operator
P_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}(x)
has a simple description in the full space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is worth keeping in mind. When
\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}
, it is clear that
P_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}(x)
is the identity. When
\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}
, the operator
P_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}(x)
is a rotation on the two-dimensional subspace generated by
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
that maps
\sigma^{\prime}
to
\sigma
. Moreover,
P_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}(x)
is
(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)
times the identity on the orthogonal complement to
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
. The following lemma, whose proof can be found in
[
35
, Lemma 4]
, reflects this characterization of
P_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

For any vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have
|P_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(x)|\leq|x|
.

Moreover, if
\Pi
is the orthogonal projection of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
onto the subspace generated by
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
, then

|P_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}(x)|^{2}=(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)^{2}|\Pi_{\perp}x|^{2}+|\Pi x|^{2}.

The operator
P_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}
is not self-adjoint. Its adjoint coincides with switching the indices
\sigma^{\prime}
and
\sigma
.

P_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}^{\ast}(x)=(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)x-(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot x)\sigma+(\sigma\cdot x)\sigma^{\prime}=P_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}.

For a vector
x
tangent to
S^{d-1}
at
\sigma^{\prime}
and
y
tangent to
S^{d-1}
at
\sigma
, we define

(4.7)

\displaystyle|y-x|_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle:=|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}-2x\cdot P_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}(y)

\displaystyle=|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}-2P_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(x)\cdot y.

We stress that
|y-x|_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}^{2}
is defined by abuse of notation. The vectors
x
and
y
belong to tangent spaces at different points. The difference
y-x
inside the bars does not make sense by itself.

Using the notation above, we provide an expression for
\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}
.

Lemma 4.6

(Formula for an iterated carré du champ)
.

For any smooth function
f:S^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
, the following expression holds,

\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}(f,f)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{S^{d-1}}|\nabla f(\sigma^{\prime})-\nabla f(\sigma)|_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}^{2}b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}.

In particular,
\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}(f,f)\geq 0
.

Here, where we write
|y-x|_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}
we mean the expression defined in (
4.7
).

Proof.

By the definition of
\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}(f,f)
we have

\displaystyle\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}(f,f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{B}|\nabla f|^{2}-\nabla f\cdot\nabla\mathcal{B}f,

we use Lemma
4.4
for the second term,

\displaystyle=\int\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla f^{\prime}|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}|\nabla f|^{2}-2\nabla f\cdot\left(M_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}\nabla f^{\prime}-\nabla f\right)\right)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\left(|\nabla f^{\prime}|^{2}+|\nabla f|^{2}-2\nabla f\cdot M_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}\nabla f^{\prime}\right)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}

we note that
\nabla f^{\prime}\perp\sigma^{\prime}
. In particular,
M_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}\nabla f^{\prime}=P_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}\nabla f^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\left(|\nabla f^{\prime}|^{2}+|\nabla f|^{2}-2\nabla f\cdot P_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}\nabla f^{\prime}\right)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int|\nabla f^{\prime}-\nabla f|_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}^{2}b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}.\qed

4.4.
The entropy production by integro-differential diffusion.

In the previous section, we related the left hand side of (
1.4
) with the second order carré du champ operator
\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}
and to the second derivative of the entropy in the directions of
\Delta f
and
\mathcal{B}f
. It is natural to wonder if there is a similar characterization for the right hand side of (
1.4
) as some sort of integro-differential Fisher information. In this section, we discuss that question and show some relevant inequalities.

In analogy with the classical Fisher information, let us name the quantity in (
4.3
) as
-\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{B}}(f)
.

Definition 4.7

(Entropy production for
\mathcal{B}
)
.

Given a kernel function
b\colon[-1,1]\to[0,+\infty)
, the
integro-differential Fisher information
is defined as,

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{B}}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{S^{d-1}\times S^{d-1}}(f^{\prime}-f)\log(f^{\prime}/f)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.

We recall the two other useful forms of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{B}}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{B}}(f)

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{d-1}}\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}(f,\log f)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(f),\mathcal{B}f\rangle.

The quantity
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{B}}
is the derivative of the entropy by the flow of
\mathcal{B}
, in the same way that the Fisher information is the derivative of the entropy by the heat flow. In that sense, the quantity
\mathcal{B}
might be interpreted as some integro-differential form of the Fisher information.

The following proposition, is similar to Lemma
4.2
but with the opposite order of the operators.

Lemma 4.8

(Differentiating the integro-differential Fisher along the heat flow)
.

\langle\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}(f),\Delta f\rangle=-2\int_{S^{d-1}}f\Gamma^{2}_{\Delta,\mathcal{B}}(\log f,\log f)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{B}}(f)=\langle\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(f),\mathcal{B}f\rangle
. The quantity
\langle\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}(f),\Delta f\rangle
corresponds to differentiating
\mathcal{S}(f)
first in the direction of
\mathcal{B}
and then in the direction of
\Delta
. Thus,

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}(f),\Delta f\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}(f)\mathcal{B}f,\Delta f\rangle+\langle\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(f),\mathcal{B}\Delta f\rangle.

Observe that since
\mathcal{S}^{\prime\prime}
is symmetric and
\mathcal{B}\Delta f=\Delta\mathcal{B}f
, we obtain the same expression as if we differentiate in the direction of
\mathcal{B}f
and
\Delta f
in the opposite order.

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}(f),\Delta f\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(f),\mathcal{B}f\rangle

\displaystyle=-2\int_{S^{d-1}}f\Gamma^{2}_{\Delta,\mathcal{B}}(\log f,\log f)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.

We applied Lemma
4.2
to get the last line.
∎

When
a,b
are two arbitrary positive numbers, the following elementary inequalities hold.

(a-b)\log(a/b)\geq 4(\sqrt{a}-\sqrt{b})^{2}\geq 2\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{a+b}.

Therefore, the following chain of elementary inequalities holds starting from the right-hand side of (
1.4
).

\displaystyle\iint_{S^{d-1}\times S^{d-1}}\frac{(f(\sigma^{\prime})-f(\sigma))^{2}}{f(\sigma^{\prime})+f(\sigma)}b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle\leq 2\iint_{S^{d-1}\times S^{d-1}}(\sqrt{f^{\prime}}-\sqrt{f})^{2}b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\iint_{S^{d-1}\times S^{d-1}}(f^{\prime}-f)\log(f^{\prime}/f)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{B}}(f).

In the cases where we can establish the inequality (
1.4
) with some constant
\Lambda_{b}>0
in this paper, we will actually be proving the following slightly stronger inequality.

\int_{S^{d-1}}\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}(\log f,\log f)f\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\geq 2\Lambda_{b}\iint_{S^{d-1}\times S^{d-1}}(\sqrt{f^{\prime}}-\sqrt{f})^{2}b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.

It remains to be seen if a similar inequality holds with
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{B}}
on the right-hand side. In this paper, we do not prove an inequality of the form

(4.8)

\int_{S^{d-1}}\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}(\log f,\log f)f\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\geq\tilde{\Lambda}_{b}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{B}}(f),

for any constant
\tilde{\Lambda}_{b}>0
.

5.
The inequality on the sphere implies the monotonicity of the Fisher information

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.2
. In order to do so, we recall that we split the Fisher information in three terms
I_{parallel}
,
I_{radial}
and
I_{spherical}
, see (
3.4
). We already obtained suitable expressions for the derivatives of the first two in the direction of
Q(F)
. We are left with differentiating
I_{spherical}
.

Lemma 5.1

(Differentiating
I_{spherical}
)
.

The following identity holds,

\langle I^{\prime}_{spherical}(F),Q(F)\rangle=-2\iint\alpha(r)r^{d-3}\int F(z,r,\sigma)\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}(\log F(z,r,\cdot),\log F(z,r,\cdot))\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}z.

Proof.

For each fixed value of
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
r>0
, we consider the function
F(z,r,\cdot):S^{d-1}\to(0,\infty)
. The gradient
\nabla_{\sigma}F
corresponds to the gradient of this function on the tangent space of the sphere. The value of the operator
Q(F)
defined in (
3.2
) is the same as
\alpha(r)\mathcal{B}[F(z,r,\cdot)](\sigma)
.

With this interpretation, Lemma
5.1
is the same as Lemma
4.2
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

In view of (
3.4
) and Lemmas
3.1
,
3.2
and
5.1
, we have

2\langle I^{\prime}(F),Q(F)\rangle\leq-D_{parallel}-D_{spherical}+R

with

\displaystyle D_{parallel}

\displaystyle=\iiiint\alpha\left|\nabla_{z}\log F^{\prime}-\nabla_{z}\log F\right|^{2}(F^{\prime}+F)\ b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)r^{d-1}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}z,

\displaystyle D_{spherical}

\displaystyle=4\iint\alpha(r)r^{d-3}\int F(z,r,\sigma)\left[\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}(\log F(z,r,\cdot),\log F(z,r,\cdot))\right](\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}z,

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\iiiint\frac{(\alpha^{\prime})^{2}}{\alpha}\frac{(F-F^{\prime})^{2}}{F^{\prime}+F}\ b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)r^{d-1}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}z.

We now claim that
D_{spherical}\geq R
. To verify this, for each value of
z
and
r
, we apply the inequality (
1.4
) to
f(\sigma)=F(z,r,\sigma)
. Using the assumption
r^{2}\alpha^{\prime}(r)^{2}/\alpha^{2}\leq 4\Lambda_{b}
, we obtain
D_{spherical}\geq R
. This proves (
3.3
) and consequently also Theorem
1.2
.
∎

In order for Theorem
1.2
to be useful, we need explicit lower bounds on
\Lambda_{b}
that can be computed in practice. We will justify (
1.4
) by combining the following three inequalities.

(5.1)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{d-1}}\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}(\log f,\log f)f\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle\geq c_{K}\int_{S^{d-1}}\frac{|\nabla f|^{2}}{f}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.

(5.2)

\displaystyle C_{P}\int_{S^{d-1}}|\nabla f|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle\geq\iint_{S^{2}\times S^{2}}(f(\sigma^{\prime})-f(\sigma))^{2}b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.

(5.3)

\displaystyle\iint_{S^{d-1}\times S^{d-1}}(\sqrt{f(\sigma^{\prime})}-\sqrt{f(\sigma)})^{2}b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\iint_{S^{d-1}\times S^{d-1}}\frac{(f(\sigma^{\prime})-f(\sigma))^{2}}{f(\sigma^{\prime})+f(\sigma)}b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Lemma 5.2
.

For any function
f:S^{d-1}\to(0,\infty)
, the inequality (
5.3
) holds.

Proof.

The integrands satisfy the inequality pointwise. This is a consequence of the elementary inequality

(\sqrt{a}-\sqrt{b})^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{a+b},

which holds for any values of
a,b>0
.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Assume that (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) hold for any positive function
f:S^{d-1}\to(0,\infty)
such that
f(\sigma)=f(-\sigma)
. Then (
1.4
) also holds with
\Lambda_{b}\geq 2c_{K}/C_{P}
.

Proof.

We apply (
5.1
), then (
5.2
) to
\sqrt{f}
, and finally (
5.3
).

\displaystyle\int_{S^{2}}\Gamma^{2}_{\Delta,\mathcal{B}}(\log f,\log f)f\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle\geq c_{K}\int_{S^{d-1}}\frac{|\nabla f|^{2}}{f}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=4c_{K}\int_{S^{d-1}}\left|\nabla\sqrt{f}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle\geq\frac{4c_{K}}{C_{P}}\iint_{S^{2}\times S^{2}}\left(\sqrt{f(\sigma^{\prime})}-\sqrt{f(\sigma)}\right)^{2}b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2c_{K}}{C_{P}}\iint_{S^{2}\times S^{2}}\frac{(f(\sigma^{\prime})-f(\sigma))^{2}}{f(\sigma^{\prime})+f(\sigma)}b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.\qed

The purpose of the next sections is to provide a proof of (
1.4
) by proving (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) for various assumptions on the kernel
b
. As we will see, we can obtain the inequality (
5.2
) in great generality with relatively precise constants
C_{P}
(see Propositions
7.1
and
9.2
below). The inequality (
5.1
) is harder to prove. We provide an example in Section
8
of a kernel
b
on
S^{1}
where it fails for any
c_{K}>0
. We prove in Section
6
that it holds in dimension
d\geq 3
for some constant
c_{K}>0
. Under stronger structural assumptions on the kernel
b
, we prove in Section
9
that both (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) hold in any dimension with relatively precise constants
c_{K}
and
C_{P}
.

The inequality (
5.1
) can be used to derive an integro-differential version of the log-Sobolev inequality on the sphere in the following way.

Proposition 5.4

(A
\Gamma^{2}
-criterion for an integral log-Sobolev inequality)
.

The inequality (
5.1
) implies the following entropy-production/entropy inequality

\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{B}}(f)\geq 2c_{K}\left(\int_{S^{d-1}}f\log f\;\mathrm{d}\sigma-\left(\int_{S^{d-1}}f\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)\log\left(\fint_{S^{d-1}}f\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)\right).

Here,
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{B}}
is the entropy-production by
\mathcal{B}
explained in Definition
4.7
.

Proof.

We proceed as in the proof by Bakry and Émery of the log-Sobolev inequality. We solve the heat equation with initial data
f
.

\displaystyle u(0,\sigma)

\displaystyle=f(\sigma),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=\Delta u.

Note that
u(t,\sigma)
converges to the average of
f
as
t\to\infty
.

We proceed using that
\partial_{t}\mathcal{S}(u)=\mathcal{I}(u)
and Lemma
4.8
, together with the inequality (
5.1
),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{S}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{c_{K}}\int_{S^{2}}\Gamma^{2}_{\Delta,\mathcal{B}}(\log f,\log f)f\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2c_{K}}\partial_{t}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{B}}.

Integrating the inequality on
t\in(0,\infty)
, we conclude the proof.
∎

In Corollary
6.2
and Lemma
9.8
, we compute the value of
c_{K}
for some classes of kernels
b
.

6.
Lower bounds for any collision kernel using the curvature of the sphere

In the previous section, we proved the monotoncity of the Fisher information of any solution
f
of (
1.1
) under the assumption of Theorem
1.2
. In order for this theorem to be applicable to any collision kernel of physical relevance, we have to compute explicit lower bounds for the constant
\Lambda_{b}
in (
1.4
). Studying this constant
\Lambda_{b}
is the purpose of the rest of the paper. In this section we develop some tools to take advantage of the curvature of the sphere and eventually prove that
\Lambda_{b}\geq d-2
.

Given any
x\perp\sigma
and
y\perp\sigma^{\prime}
. We observe that
|y-x|_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}^{2}
can be written as the sum of two positive terms

|y-x|_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}^{2}=|y-P_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}x|^{2}+\left(|x|^{2}-|P_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}x|^{2}\right).

Recall that
|\cdot|_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}
,
P_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}
and
M_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}
are defined in (
4.7
), (
4.6
) and (
4.5
), respectively. The second term in the previous equality is positive because of Lemma
4.5
.

In this section, we take advantage of this decomposition of
|y-x|_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}^{2}
to derive a generic lower bound for the expression in Lemma
4.6
for
\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}
.

Lemma 6.1

(Lower bound for the iterated carré du champ using curvature)
.

Assume
d>2
. The following inequality holds for any function
f:S^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
.

\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}(f,f)\geq c_{K}|\nabla f|^{2}.

Here

c_{K}=\frac{d-2}{2(d-1)}\int_{S^{d-1}}(1-(e\cdot\sigma^{\prime})^{2})b(e\cdot\sigma^{\prime})\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime},

is a constant independent of the choice of
e\in S^{d-1}
.

Proof.

From the decomposition explained above, we know that

\displaystyle|y-x|_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(|x|^{2}-|P_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}x|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\bigg(1-(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)^{2}\bigg)|\Pi_{\perp}x|^{2},

where
\Pi_{\perp}
is the orthogonal projection to the codimension-2 space orthogonal to
\sigma^{\prime}
and
\sigma
(recall Lemma
4.5
).

We start from the expression in Lemma
4.6
and apply this inequality to the integrand.

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}^{2}(f,f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{S^{d-1}}|\nabla f(\sigma^{\prime})-\nabla f(\sigma)|_{\sigma^{\prime},\sigma}^{2}b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{S^{d-1}}\bigg(1-(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)^{2}\bigg)\left|\Pi_{\perp}\nabla f(\sigma)\right|^{2}b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}.

We express this last integal in spherical coordinates. We write
\sigma^{\prime}=c\sigma+\omega
, with
c\in(-1,1)=\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma
and
\omega\perp\sigma
. The value of
\omega
belongs to the
(d-2)
-sphere of radius
(1-c^{2})^{1/2}
on the orthogonal complement of
\sigma
, which coincides with
T_{\sigma}S^{d-1}
. We observe that
\Pi_{\perp}\nabla f(\sigma)=\nabla f(\sigma)-(\omega\cdot\nabla f(\sigma))\omega/|\omega|^{2}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}^{2}(f,f)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\omega\perp\sigma,\\
|\omega|^{2}=1-c^{2}\}\end{subarray}}(1-c^{2})b(c)\left(|\nabla f(\sigma)|^{2}-(\nabla f(\sigma)\cdot\omega)^{2}/(1-c^{2})\right)\;\mathrm{d}\omega\;\mathrm{d}c

\displaystyle=c_{K}|\nabla f(\sigma)|^{2}.

Here,

\displaystyle c_{K}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\omega\perp\sigma,\\
|\omega|^{2}=1-c^{2}\}\end{subarray}}(1-c^{2})b(c)\left(1-(e\cdot\omega)^{2}/(1-c^{2})\right)\;\mathrm{d}\omega\;\mathrm{d}c

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{S^{d-2}}\left(1-(e\cdot\bar{\omega})^{2}\right)\;\mathrm{d}\bar{\omega}\right)\int_{-1}^{1}(1-c^{2})^{\frac{d}{2}}b(c)\;\mathrm{d}c

\displaystyle=\frac{d-2}{d-1}\cdot\frac{\omega_{d-2}}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}(1-c^{2})^{d/2}b(c)\;\mathrm{d}c.\qed

Corollary 6.2

(Fisher bounds from below the integrated iterated carré du champ operator)
.

Assume
d>2
. For any function
f:S^{d-1}\to(0,\infty)
, inequality (
5.1
) holds,

\int_{S^{d-1}}f\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}(\log f,\log f)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\geq c_{K}\int_{S^{d-1}}\frac{|\nabla f|^{2}}{f}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Here, for any
e\in S^{d-1}
,

c_{K}=\frac{d-2}{2(d-1)}\int_{S^{d-1}}(1-(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot e)^{2})b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot e)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}.

Proof.

We integrate the inequality from Lemma
6.1
.
∎

The next step is to control the right hand side of (
1.4
) using the usual Fisher information. We do it precisely in the next section.

7.
A Poincaré-type inequality

We will prove the following estimate that provides an upper bound for integral Dirichlet forms in terms of the
\dot{H}^{1}
norm of a function on the sphere. We will later apply this lemma to
\sqrt{f}
in order to get estimates involving the Fisher information.

Proposition 7.1

(A Poincaré-type inequality)
.

For any function
f:S^{d-1}\to(0,\infty)
such that
f(\sigma)=f(-\sigma)
, the following inequality holds

(7.1)

C_{P}\int_{S^{d-1}}|\nabla f|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\geq\iint_{S^{d-1}\times S^{d-1}}(f^{\prime}-f)^{2}b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Here, the constant
C_{P}
is given for any fixed
e\in S^{d-1}
by the following formula,

C_{P}=\frac{1}{d-1}\int_{S^{d-1}}(1-(\sigma\cdot e)^{2})b(e\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Remark 7.2
.

When the kernel
b
is constant, the inequality (
7.1
) is exactly the Poincaré inequality on the sphere for functions with average zero.

7.1.
Spectral decomposition

The operators
-\Delta
and
\mathcal{B}
are self-adjoint and commute. Thus, they have a common base of eigenfunctions. Recall that the eigenfunctions of
-\Delta
are spherical harmonics, corresponding to the restriction to
S^{d-1}
of homogeneous harmonic polynomials in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Spherical harmonics have been studied extensively. A modern reference for the subject is
[
13
]
.

Generically, we expect
\Delta
and
\mathcal{B}
to have the same eigenspaces. We will prove only one inclusion. That is, we prove that all eigenfunctions of
-\Delta
with eigenvalue
\lambda
are eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{B}
with the same eigenvalue
\tilde{\lambda}
.

Lemma 7.3

(Eigenspaces of
\Delta
and
\mathcal{B}
)
.

Each eigenspace of
\Delta
(which is a finite dimensional space of spherical harmonics) is contained in some eigenspace of
\mathcal{B}
.

Proof.

It is well known that the eigenspace corresponding the eigenvalue
\lambda_{\ell}=\ell(\ell+d-2)
for
-\Delta
consists in the restriction to the sphere of
\ell
-homogeneous harmonic polynomials. It is a finite dimensional space of smooth functions.

Since
\mathcal{B}
and
\Delta
commute, this finite-dimensional eigenspace is an invariant space by
\mathcal{B}
.

Observe also that both
\Delta
and
\mathcal{B}
commute with rotations. Thus, axially symmetric functions (which are those that depend only on
e_{1}\cdot\sigma
on
S^{d-1}
) are invariant by both
\Delta
and
\mathcal{B}
. We may restrict these two operators to the space of axially symmetric functions and repeat the above analysis. If an axis is chosen arbitrarily, the spherical harmonics of order
\ell
that are axially symmetric around that axis is a subspace of dimension one (see
[
13
, Theorem 4.10]
). They consist of functions of the form

Y_{\ell}(\sigma)=aL_{\ell}(\sigma\cdot e_{1}),

for
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
L_{\ell}
being the Legendre polynomial of degree
\ell
. Since this eigenspace of
\Delta
has dimension one, and
\mathcal{B}
commutes with
\Delta
, then it is also a one-dimensional eigenspace for
\mathcal{B}
. Thus, the function
Y_{\ell}
above is an eignfunction for
\mathcal{B}
.

It is well known that any generic (not necessarily axially symmetric) spherical harmonic
Y
of order
\ell
can be written as a linear combination of rotations of the function
Y_{\ell}
above (see
[
13
, Theorem 4.13]
). Since any rotation of
Y_{\ell}
is going to be also an eigenfunction for
\Delta
and
\mathcal{B}
with the same eigenvalue, then the full eigenspace of spherical harmonics of order
\ell
are eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{B}
with the same eigenvalue.
∎

Let us recall that the eigenvalue
\lambda_{\ell}
of
-\Delta
corresponding to spherical harmonics of order
\ell
equals
\ell(\ell+d-2)
. Let us write
\tilde{\lambda}_{\ell}
for the corresponding eigenvalue of
-\mathcal{B}
for spherical harmonics of order
\ell
. Given any generic function
f:S^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
, we write its expansion in terms of spherical harmonics.

f=\sum_{\ell=0}^{+\infty}x_{\ell}Y_{\ell}.

Here, each
Y_{\ell}
is a spherical harmonic of order
\ell
. Observe that

\displaystyle\int_{S^{d-1}}|\nabla f|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{d-1}}(-\Delta f)f\;\mathrm{d}\sigma,

\displaystyle\iint_{S^{d-1}\times S^{d-1}}(f^{\prime}-f)^{2}b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=2\int_{S^{d-1}}(-\mathcal{B}f)f\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Therefore

\displaystyle\int_{S^{d-1}}|\nabla f|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{+\infty}\lambda_{\ell}x_{\ell}^{2},

\displaystyle\iint_{S^{d-1}\times S^{d-1}}(f^{\prime}-f)^{2}b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{+\infty}2\tilde{\lambda}_{\ell}x_{\ell}^{2}.

The first term on the right-hand side vanishes, so we may exclude it from our analysis.

Recall that
Y_{\ell}
is the restriction to the sphere of a homogeneous harmonic polynomial of degree
\ell
. In particular,
Y_{\ell}
is an odd function if
\ell
is odd. When the function
f
is even (i.e. it satisfies
f(-\sigma)=f(\sigma)
for all
\sigma
), then
x_{2\ell+1}=0
for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.

The following lemma follows from the discussion above.

Lemma 7.4

(First formula for the constant
C_{P}
)
.

The inequality (
7.1
) holds true with

(7.2)

C_{P}=2\sup_{\ell=1,2,3,\dots}\frac{\tilde{\lambda}_{2\ell}}{\lambda_{2\ell}}.

7.2.
An inequality for Legendre polynomials

In order to get a useful formula for the constant
C_{P}
appearing in (
7.1
), we are going to use
a pointwise estimate on Legendre polynomials of even order.

We start with a formula for the eigenvalues
\tilde{\lambda}_{\ell}
of
-\mathcal{B}
.

Lemma 7.5
.

The eigenvalue
\tilde{\lambda}_{\ell}
for
-\mathcal{B}
corresponding to spherical harmonics of order
\ell
is given by the formula.

\tilde{\lambda}_{\ell}=\int_{S^{d-1}}\left(1-P_{\ell}(e_{1}\cdot\sigma^{\prime})\right)b(e_{1}\cdot\sigma^{\prime})\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime},

where
P_{\ell}
is the Legendre polynomial of order
\ell
with the normalization
P_{\ell}(1)=1
.

Proof.

We recall that the unique axially symmetric spherical harmonic
Y_{\ell}
of order
\ell
such that
Y_{\ell}(e_{1})=1
is given by

Y_{\ell}(\sigma)=P_{\ell}(e_{1}\cdot\sigma).

Therefore, for this function
Y_{\ell}
, we have
-\Delta Y_{\ell}(e_{1})=\lambda_{\ell}
and

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{B}Y_{\ell}(e_{1})

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{d-1}}\left(1-P_{\ell}(e_{1}\cdot\sigma^{\prime})\right)b(e_{1}\cdot\sigma^{\prime})\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}.\qed

Proposition 7.6

(An inequality for Legendre polynomials)
.

Given the Legendre polynomials
P_{\ell}
normalized so that
P_{\ell}(1)=1
, we have for all
x\in[-1,1]
and
\ell\geq 1
,

(7.3)

1-P_{2\ell}(x)\leq\frac{\lambda_{2\ell}}{\lambda_{2}}(1-P_{2}(x)).

Here
\lambda_{\ell}=\ell(d+\ell-2)
is the eigenvalue of the spherical Laplacian corresponding to
Y_{\ell}(\sigma):=P_{\ell}(e_{1}\cdot\sigma)
.

Legendre polynomials
P_{\ell}(x)
correspond to homogeneous harmonic functions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which are invariant by rotations over the
e_{1}
axis. In 2D, these homogeneous harmonic functions can easily be computed using complex analysis as the real part of
(x+iy)^{\ell}
. In dimension
d>2
, we may start with one of these 2D profiles written in terms of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, and average over all possible rotations over the
e_{1}
axis. We are left with the following formula that appears in
[
13
, Theorem 4.26]
.

Lemma 7.7

(Integral representation of Legendre polynomials –
[
13
, Theorem 4.26]
)
.

Assuming
d\geq 3
, the following equality holds

P_{\ell}(x)=\frac{\omega_{d-3}}{\omega_{d-2}}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(x+is\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)^{\ell}(1-s^{2})^{\frac{d-4}{2}}\;\mathrm{d}s.

It is useful to understand the weight in the integral. For any
p>1
, the following identity holds,

\omega_{p-1}\int_{-1}^{1}(1-s^{2})^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\;\mathrm{d}s=\omega_{p}.

Here,
\omega_{p}
is the surface area of
S^{p}
. In fact, all the quantities involved are computable.
We recall that (see for instance
[
13
, Proposition 2.3]
),

\omega_{p}=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}.

In particular,

\int_{-1}^{1}(1-s^{2})^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\;\mathrm{d}s=\sqrt{\pi}\frac{\Gamma(p/2)}{\Gamma((p+1)/2)}.

It is easy to verify that the right hand side of Lemma
7.7
is a real number by averaging the values of the integral at
s
and
-s
.

Observe that we have
P_{\ell}(1)=1
. In view of this, we write

(7.4)

1-P_{\ell}(x)=\frac{\omega_{d-3}}{\omega_{d-2}}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(1-\left(x+is\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)^{\ell}\right)(1-s^{2})^{\frac{d-4}{2}}\;\mathrm{d}s.

Proof of Proposition
7.6
.

The proof proceeds in several steps.

Step 1.

We first compute explicitely the right hand side of (
7.3
).
The polynomial
P_{2}
can be computed explicitly,

P_{2}(x)=\frac{d}{d-1}x^{2}-\frac{1}{d-1}.

We quickly justify this classical fact. The function
Y_{2}
is the restriction to the sphere of the unique homogeneous quadratic harmonic polynomial that is invariant by any rotation fixing
e_{1}
and
Y_{2}(e_{1})=1
. Thus, it has to be
Y_{2}(x)=x_{1}^{2}-(x_{2}^{2}+\dots+x_{d}^{2})/(d-1)
, and then
P_{2}(x)=x^{2}+(1-x^{2})/(d-1)
. In particular,

(7.5)

1-P_{2}(x)=\frac{d}{d-1}(1-x^{2}).

Since
\lambda_{2}=2d
, the inequality we want to prove reduces to

(7.6)

1-P_{2\ell}(x)\leq\frac{\lambda_{2\ell}}{2(d-1)}(1-x^{2})=\frac{\ell(d-2+2\ell)}{(d-1)}(1-x^{2}).

Our plan is to use (
7.4
) to prove (
7.6
). To get there, we need to prove a series of lemmas first.

Step 2. The 2D case.

When
d=2
, the polynomials
P_{\ell}(x)
correspond to
\cos(\ell\theta)
if
x=\cos\theta
. The inequality (
7.3
) that we want to prove takes the form

\ell^{2}\geq\frac{1-\cos(2\ell\theta)}{1-\cos(2\theta)}.

This inequality can be proved relatively easily using trigonometric identities and induction. We observe that
1-\cos(2\theta)=2\sin(\theta)^{2}
. Therefore

\frac{1-\cos(2\ell\theta)}{1-\cos(2\theta)}=\frac{\sin(\ell\theta)^{2}}{\sin(\theta)^{2}}.

The following inequality is easy to prove using induction on
\ell
and the formulas for the sine of a sum of the angles.

\frac{|\sin(\ell\theta)|}{|\sin(\theta)|}\leq\ell.

Indeed, the inequality is obvious for
\ell=1
. Once we know it for any value of
\ell
, we use the formula for the sine of sums to get

\displaystyle|\sin((\ell+1)\theta)|

\displaystyle=|\sin((\ell)\theta)\cos(\theta)+\cos((\ell)\theta)\sin(\theta)|

\displaystyle\leq|\sin((\ell)\theta)|+|\sin(\theta)|

\displaystyle\leq(\ell+1)|\sin(\theta)|.

These two steps that are used to prove the inequality in 2D correspond to the first two lemmas that are presented in the general case. Without the 2D case, their statements would look admittedly mysterious.

Step 3. Dimension greater than 2.

Lemma 7.8
.

For any
\ell=1,2,3,\dots
, the following identity holds for
x,s\in[-1,1]
,

\Re\left(1-\left(x+is\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)^{2\ell}\right)=2\left\{\Im\left(x+is\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)^{\ell}\right\}^{2}+1-\left(x^{2}+s^{2}(1-x^{2})\right)^{\ell}.

Proof.

For any complex number
z=a+ib
, we observe that

\displaystyle 1-\Re z^{2}=1-a^{2}+b^{2}=1-|z|^{2}+2b^{2}.

If we set
z=\left(x+is\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)^{\ell}
, we conclude the statement of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 7.9
.

The following inequality holds for all
\ell=1,2,3,\dots
and
x,s\in[-1,1]
,

\left|\Im\left(x+is\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)^{\ell}\right|\leq\ell|s|\sqrt{1-x^{2}}.

Proof.

We prove it by induction. It is a trivial identity for
\ell=1
. Then, we observe

\displaystyle\left|\Im\left(x+is\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)^{\ell+1}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\Im\left(x+is\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)^{\ell}x+\Re\left(x+is\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)^{\ell}s\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\Im\left(x+is\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)^{\ell}\right||x|+\left|\Re\left(x+is\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)^{\ell}\right||s|\sqrt{1-x^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\Im\left(x+is\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)^{\ell}\right|+|s|\sqrt{1-x^{2}}

and then we use the inductive hypothesis,

\displaystyle\leq(\ell+1)|s|\sqrt{1-x^{2}}.\qed

Combining Lemmas
7.9
and
7.8
, we obtain

\Re\left(1-\left(x+is\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)^{2\ell}\right)\leq 2\ell^{2}s^{2}(1-x^{2})+1-\left(x^{2}+s^{2}(1-x^{2})\right)^{\ell}.

Using the convexity of the function
(1-z)^{\ell}
for
z\in(0,1)
, we have

1-(1-z)^{\ell}\leq\ell z.

In particular, if we pick
z=(1-s^{2})(1-x^{2})
, we have

1-\left(x^{2}+s^{2}(1-x^{2})\right)^{\ell}\leq\ell(1-s^{2})(1-x^{2}).

We thus get

\displaystyle\Re\left(1-\left(x+is\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)^{2\ell}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\ell^{2}s^{2}(1-x^{2})+(1-x^{2})\ell(1-s^{2})

\displaystyle=(1-x^{2})\ell(1-s^{2}+2\ell s^{2}).

Finally, we integrate in
s
to prove (
7.6
) using (
7.4
).

\displaystyle 1-P_{2\ell}(x)

\displaystyle=\Re\frac{\omega_{d-3}}{\omega_{d-2}}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(1-\left(x+is\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)^{2\ell}\right)(1-s^{2})^{\frac{d-4}{2}}\;\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\omega_{d-3}}{\omega_{d-2}}\int_{-1}^{1}(1-x^{2})\ell(1-s^{2}+2\ell s^{2})(1-s^{2})^{\frac{d-4}{2}}\;\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{d-3}}{\omega_{d-2}}\ell(1-x^{2})\int_{-1}^{1}\left\{(1-2\ell)(1-s^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}+2\ell(1-s^{2})^{\frac{d-4}{2}}\right\}\;\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{d-3}}{\omega_{d-2}}\ell(1-x^{2})\left((1-2\ell)\sqrt{\pi}\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{\Gamma((d+1)/2)}+2\ell\sqrt{\pi}\frac{\Gamma(d/2-1)}{\Gamma((d+1)/2-1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\ell\left((1-2\ell)\frac{d-2}{d-1}+2\ell\right)\ (1-x^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell(d-2+2\ell)}{(d-1)}(1-x^{2}).

And that is the inequality we wanted to prove.
∎

Corollary 7.10

(Second formula for
C_{P}
)
.

For
\ell=1,2,3,\dots
, we have
\frac{\tilde{\lambda}_{2\ell}}{\lambda_{2\ell}}\leq\frac{\tilde{\lambda}_{2}}{\lambda_{2}}
. In other words,
C_{P}
defined by (
7.2
) equals
2\frac{\tilde{\lambda}_{2}}{\lambda_{2}}
.

Proof.

In view of Lemma
7.5
, we can use Proposition
7.6
to estimate
\tilde{\lambda}_{2\ell}
from above,

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{2\ell}

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{d-1}}(1-P_{2\ell}(e_{1}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}))b(e_{1}\cdot\sigma^{\prime})\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda_{2\ell}}{\lambda_{2}}\int_{S^{d-1}}(1-P_{2}(e_{1}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}))b(e_{1}\cdot\sigma^{\prime})\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{2\ell}}{\lambda_{2}}\tilde{\lambda}_{2}

where we used Lemma
7.4
to get the last line.
∎

We are now ready to prove Proposition
7.1
.

Proof of Proposition
7.1
.

We combine Lemmas
7.4
with Corollary
7.10
and get that (
7.1
) holds with
C_{P}=2\frac{\tilde{\lambda}_{2}}{\lambda_{2}}
.

It is well known that
\lambda_{2}=2d
. To compute
\tilde{\lambda}_{2}
, we use Lemma
7.5
and (
7.5
),

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{d-1}}\left(1-P_{2}(e_{1}\cdot\sigma^{\prime})\right)b(e_{1}\cdot\sigma^{\prime})\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime},

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{d-1}}\frac{d}{d-1}\left(1-(e_{1}\cdot\sigma^{\prime})^{2}\right)b(e_{1}\cdot\sigma^{\prime})\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}.

And the formula for
C_{P}=2\frac{\tilde{\lambda}_{2}}{\lambda_{2}}
follows.
∎

7.3.
The key inequality on the sphere for large dimensions

Proposition 7.11

(The key inequality on the sphere in large dimensions)
.

Assume
d>2
. For any kernel
b
, the inequality (
1.4
) holds with
\Lambda_{b}\geq d-2.

Proof.

Corollary
6.2
establishes the inequality (
5.1
) for

c_{K}=\frac{d-2}{2(d-1)}\int_{S^{d-1}}(1-(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot e)^{2})b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot e)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}.

Proposition
7.1
establishes the inequality (
5.2
) for

C_{P}=\frac{1}{d-1}\int_{S^{d-1}}(1-(\sigma\cdot e)^{2})b(e\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Therefore, we prove (
1.4
) using Lemma
5.3
with

\Lambda_{b}=2c_{K}/C_{P}=d-2.\qed

8.
Example of a singular collision kernel

In this section, we show an example of a collision kernel
b
for
d=2
so that the inequality (
1.4
) does not hold for any
\Lambda_{b}>0
.

The example consists in a very singular kernel
b
which is a Dirac delta concentrated at the deviation angle
\pi/2
. We will see that for this collision kernel the inequality (
1.4
) can only hold with
\Lambda_{b}=0
.

Lemma 8.1

(A counter-example in 2D)
.

For a given
\Lambda>0
arbitrarily large, there exists a
\pi
-periodic function
f
such that the following inequality is false,

(8.1)

\displaystyle\int_{[0,2\pi]}

\displaystyle|\partial_{\theta}\log f(\theta+\pi/2)-\partial_{\theta}\log f(\theta)|^{2}(f(\theta+\pi/2)+f(\theta))\;\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle\geq\Lambda\int_{[0,2\pi]}\frac{(f(\theta+\pi/2)-f(\theta))^{2}}{f(\theta+\pi/2)+f(\theta)}\;\mathrm{d}\theta.

Proof.

In order to do so, we consider
f=(1+A\psi)h
with
\psi,h
that are smooth,
\pi
-periodic. We also choose
h
so that,

h(\theta)=\begin{cases}2&\text{ if }\theta\in[\pi/4,\pi/2]\\
1&\text{ if }\theta\in[3\pi/4,\pi]\end{cases}

and
h
increasing in
[0,\pi]
and decreasing in
[\pi,2\pi]
. As far as
\psi
is concerned, we choose it non-negative, vanishing on
[0,\pi/4]
and
\pi/2
-periodic.

Since
\psi
is
\pi/2
-periodic and
h
has zero derivative on the support of
\psi
, we observe that

\displaystyle\log f(\theta+\pi/2)-\log f(\theta)

\displaystyle=\log h(\theta+\pi/2)-\log h(\theta),

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}\log h(\theta+\pi/2)-\partial_{\theta}\log h(\theta)

\displaystyle=0\text{ on the support of }\psi.

These two facts imply that

\int_{[0,2\pi]}|\partial_{\theta}\log f(\theta+\pi/2)-\partial_{\theta}\log f(\theta)|^{2}(f(\theta+\pi/2)+f(\theta))\;\mathrm{d}\theta\text{ does not depend on $A$}.

But

\displaystyle\int_{[0,2\pi]}\frac{(f(\theta+\pi/2)-f(\theta))^{2}}{f(\theta+\pi/2)+f(\theta)}\;\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,2\pi]}(1+A\psi(\theta))\frac{(h(\theta+\pi/2)-h(\theta))^{2}}{h(\theta+\pi/2)+h(\theta)}\;\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle\geq A\int_{[0,2\pi]}\psi(\theta)\frac{(h(\theta+\pi/2)-h(\theta))^{2}}{h(\theta+\pi/2)+h(\theta)}\;\mathrm{d}\theta.

Taking
A\to\infty
, we conclude that (
8.1
) cannot hold true unless
\Lambda=0
.
∎

9.
Subordinate diffusions

Our example of a two dimensional kernel for which
\Lambda_{b}=0
suggests that the lower bound
\Lambda\geq d-2
may not be improvable in full generality. However, this lower bound also appears to be suboptimal in many cases of interest. In this section we study a special kind of kernels of the form (
1.6
). It is a fairly generic class that includes for example the kernels of the fractional Laplacian on the sphere. We obtain explicit lower bounds for
\Lambda_{b}
for kernels
b
in this class that are always larger than the dimension
d
. These improved estimates for kernels of subordinate processes is what eventually allows us to verify the assumptions of Theorem
1.2
for power-law kernels in the very soft potential range.

The objective of this section is to prove (
1.4
) for kernels
b
that are of the form

(9.1)

b(c)=\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{t}(c)\omega(t)\;\mathrm{d}t.

Here,
h_{t}
is the heat kernel on
S^{d-1}
after time
t>0
, and
\omega\geq 0
is an arbitrary weight function. We will obtain that for these kernels, the inequality (
1.4
) holds with a constant
\Lambda_{b}
that is always larger than the dimension
d
.

In this work, it is convenient to call heat kernel the function
h_{t}
so that

u(t,\sigma)=\int_{S^{d-1}}f(\sigma^{\prime})h_{t}(\sigma\cdot\sigma^{\prime})\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}

is the representation formula for the heat equation on the sphere,

\displaystyle u(0,\sigma)

\displaystyle=f(\sigma),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=\Delta u.

By the rotational symmetry of the heat equation, the heat kernel
h_{t}
depends only on the angle between
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
. We write it as
h_{t}(\sigma\cdot\sigma^{\prime})
to match the notation for the angular part of the collision kernel
b(\sigma\cdot\sigma^{\prime})
.

9.1.
Explicit formulas for constants in key inequalities

We state first a proposition that improves Corollary
6.2
for kernels
b
of the form (
9.1
). In this case, we can prove that (
5.1
) holds with a larger constant

and in any dimension.

Proposition 9.1

(Integrated
\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}
criterion for subordinate diffusions)
.

Let
b
be any kernel of the form (
9.1
). For any function
f:S^{d-1}\to(0,\infty)
such that
f(\sigma)=f(-\sigma)
, the inequality (
5.1
) holds with

c_{K}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\omega(t)(1-\exp(-2\Lambda_{local}t))\;\mathrm{d}t.

We then give a simpler proof of the Poincaré-type inequality from Proposition
7.1
for collision kernels of the form (
9.1
). We also provide the reader with a handy formula for the constant
C_{P}
in which the weight
\omega
appears.

Proposition 9.2

(Poincaré-type inequality, again)
.

For any function
f:S^{d-1}\to(0,\infty)
such that
f(\sigma)=f(-\sigma)
, the inequality (
5.2

C_{P}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\omega(t)\frac{1-e^{-2dt}}{d}\;\mathrm{d}t.

Finally, we prove (
1.4
) for kernels of the form (

).

Proposition 9.3

(The key inequality on the sphere for subordinate diffusions)
.

Let

be a kernel of the form (

. The inequality (

\Lambda_{b}\geq d\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}\omega(t)(1-\exp(-2\Lambda_{local}t))\;\mathrm{d}t}{\int_{0}^{\infty}\omega(t)(1-e^{-2dt})\;\mathrm{d}t}.

In particular,

).

9.2.
Comments on subordinate diffusions

Recall that the fractional Laplacian, as well as a large family of integral operators, can be written in the form (
9.1
) for some weight function
\omega\geq 0
. In terms of the corresponding stochastic processes, we are analyzing the integral operators that arise as infinitesimal generators of a process obtained from the Brownian motion by subordination. For the kernel
b
of the fractional Laplacian
-(-\Delta)^{s}
, we would have
\omega(\tau)=c_{s}\tau^{-1-s}
for some constant
c_{s}
that only depends on dimension and
s
.

The purpose of this section is to establish a proof of (
1.4
) for collision kernels
b
of the form (
9.1
). We do it by establishing (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) and applying Lemma
5.3
. Because of the example in Section
8
, we know that (
1.4
) (and in particular (
5.1
)) cannot hold for just any kernel
b
. The condition (
9.1
) is the further structural assumption on the kernel that we make to be able to prove (
5.1
) with a precise constants

.

The kernels of the form (
9.1
) comprise a relatively general family of kernels. In
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, this type of kernels has been studied extensively. They correspond to the jump kernels of subordinate Brownian motions, and they are identified as kernels
k(x-y)
that coincide with a completely monotone function applied to
|x-y|^{2}
. Indeed, the jump kernel is the density of the Lévy measure of the subordinate process. It is given by the following formula,

k(z)=\mathcal{L}(|z|^{2})=\int_{0}^{\infty}(4\pi s)^{-d/2}e^{-\frac{|z|^{2}}{4s}}\rho(\;\mathrm{d}s)

(see
[
27
, (30.8), p.198]
). Now Bernstein’s theorem (see
[
28
, Theorem 1.4]
) asserts that
\mathcal{L}
is a completely monotone function. While we are not able to provide a direct proof that the Boltzmann collision kernels corresponding to power-law potentials can indeed be written in the form (
9.1
), numerical computations show that this identification holds at least up to a negligibly small error. Due to the criterion (
1.7
), that is enough for us to obtain a useful estimate for the parameter
\Lambda_{b}
.

This is merely a consequence of the fact that the Fisher information

.

Lemma 9.9

For any function
f:S^{d-1}\to(0,\infty)
such that

C_{P}\int_{S^{d-1}}|\nabla f|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma\geq\iint_{S^{d-1}\times S^{d-1}}(f^{\prime}-f)^{2}b_{t}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

We start by expanding the right hand side.

\displaystyle\iint_{S^{d-1}\times S^{d-1}}(f^{\prime}-f)^{2}b_{t}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=-2\int f\mathcal{B}f\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=2\int f(f-f_{t})\;\mathrm{d}\sigma.

We write
f
in terms of the spectral decomposition of the Laplace-Beltrami operator

on the sphere. Let

be the normalized even eigenfunctions with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq\dots

\displaystyle\int_{S^{d-1}}|\nabla f|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}a_{\ell}^{2}

\displaystyle\iint_{S^{d-1}\times S^{d-1}}(f^{\prime}-f)^{2}b_{t}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(1-e^{\lambda_{\ell}t})a_{\ell}^{2}.

\max_{\ell=2,3,4,\dots}\frac{1-\exp(-\lambda_{\ell}t)}{\lambda_{\ell}}=\frac{1-\exp(-2dt)}{2d},

10.
Existence of global smooth solutions

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.6
as a consequence of Theorem
1.2
.

The first ingredient to establish the global existence of smooth solutions is a local-in-time existence result. Then, we combine it with a-priori estimates and a suitable conditional regularity result.

In view of the assumptions for short-time existence / blow-up (Theorem
2.1
), in order to prove that the non-cutoff Boltzmann equation with soft potentials does not blow up, we only need to establish an a-priori estimate in
\|f(t)\|_{L^{p_{1}}_{q_{1}}}
.

Theorem
2.1
provides a strong solution in some interval of time
[0,T]
. Provided that the initial data is in
L^{\infty}_{q}
for sufficiently large
q
, this solution will be
C^{2}
and
D^{2}f\in L^{\infty}_{p}
for some arbitrary exponent
p
. Because of Lemma
2.3
, for any small time
\tau>0
, the Fisher information of
f(\tau,\cdot)
is finite.

For suitable collision kernels
B
, Theorem
1.2
tells us that the Fisher information remains bounded for all later time. In particular, since
I(f)=4\|\sqrt{f}\|_{\dot{H}^{2}}^{2}\gtrsim\|f\|_{L^{3}}
, we know that
f\in L^{\infty}([\tau,T],L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
for any given
\tau>0
.

From the standard conservation of energy, we know that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{2}f(t,v)\;\mathrm{d}v\leq M_{0}+E_{0},

where
M_{0}
and
E_{0}
are the mass and energy of the initial data
f_{0}
.

Interpolating between these two bounds, we get that for all
t\in[\tau,T]
,

(10.1)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{k}f(t,v)^{p}\;\mathrm{d}v\leq C_{k,p},

for
p\in[1,3]
and
k{}=3-p
. This is enough to apply Theorem
2.1
for a range of parameters
\gamma
and
2s
but not for all them. Indeed, for power-law collision kernels approaching Coulomb, we would have
\gamma\to-3
and
2s\to 2
, in which case we would need
k_{1}=5/2
and
p_{1}>3/2
in order to apply Theorem
2.1
, which is outside our range.

This restriction can easily be overcome using the propagation of higher moments. Let us recall a result from
[
8
]
. In our context, it says that

Theorem 10.1

(Propagation of moments –
[
8
, Theorem 1(i)]
)
.

Any solution
f:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
of the Boltzmann equation (
1.1
) so that
f_{0}\in L\log L\cap L^{1}_{k}
for

, satisfies
f\in L^{\infty}([0,T],L^{1}_{k})
.

The precise upper bound for
\|f\|_{L^{1}_{k}}
obtained in
[
8
]
deteriorates linearly as
t\to\infty
. For the purpose of proving the global existence of smooth solutions, we only use that it does not blow up in finite time.

Let us assume that
f_{0}\in L^{1}_{k_{2}}
for some
k_{2}
large enough. Applying Theorem
10.1
, we know that the
k_{2}
-moment of

does not blow up

\sup_{t\in[0,t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\langle v\rangle^{k_{2}}f(t,v)\;\mathrm{d}v\leq M_{k}.

Then, we can interpolate with our bound in
L^{\infty}(L^{3})
from the Fisher information and get (

) for

. If we take

, we get

To satisfy the continuation criterion in Theorem
2.1
, we would need

to be larger than some number depending on
\gamma
and
2s
. For some pairs
\gamma
and
2s
, this value of

is smaller or equal to

and it follows from the conservation of mass and energy. For some values of
\gamma
and
2s
,
k_{2}
is larger than
2
and the result requires some further propagation of moments that is provided by Theorem

.

In any case, we observe that Theorem
1.6
follows as a consequence of Theorem
1.2
together with Theorems
2.1
and

.

and
b_{col}(\cos\theta)
being a spherical kernel with a singularity at
\theta=0
.

To explain the formula for
b_{col}(\cos\theta)
, we should first compute the deviation angle
\theta
as a function of the impact parameter
p
as

\theta(p):=\pi-2p\int_{r_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{\;\mathrm{d}r}{r^{2}\sqrt{1-\frac{p^{2}}{r^{2}}-\frac{4\psi_{0}}{r^{q-1}}}}.

Here,
r_{0}
is defined to be the positive root of

1-\frac{p^{2}}{r_{0}^{2}}-\frac{4\psi_{0}}{r_{0}^{q-1}}=0.

And then, the collision kernel
b_{col}
is given by

b_{col}(\cos\theta)=-\left(\frac{p}{\sin\theta(p)}\right)^{d-2}\frac{dp}{d\theta}.

The parameter
\psi_{0}
can be scaled out. It becomes a multiplicative factor of the form
\psi_{0}^{2s}
in front of the formula for
b_{col}(\cos\theta)
. We can assume without loss of generality that
\psi_{0}=1/4
.

The formula for
b_{col}
described above is admittedly complicated. We cannot write an explicit formula for
b_{col}(\cos\theta)
. A few asymptotic properties can be established and are standard. We know its behavior as
\theta\to 0
. For
2s=(d-1)/(q-1)
, we have

\lim_{\theta\to 0}\left\{b_{col}(\cos\theta)\theta^{d-1+2s}\right\}=\frac{1}{q-1}\left(\sqrt{\pi}\frac{\Gamma(q/2)}{\Gamma(q/2-1/2)}\right)^{2s}.

It is not too difficult to compute the values
b_{col}(\cos\theta)
numerically, by basically following the same steps described above.

A.2.
The three dimensional case

Let us focus on the three dimensional case:
d=3
.

In dimension three, it can be verified that
b_{col}(\cos\theta)
converges to a constant away from
\theta=0
as
q\to\infty
(and equivalently
s\to 0
). Thus, the collision kernel of inverse power-law potentials converges to the standard hard-spheres kernel as
q\to\infty
.

The fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}
on the sphere
S^{2}
is defined using spectral calculus in terms of how it acts on spherical harmonics

(-\Delta)^{s}Y_{\ell}^{m}=\lambda_{\ell}^{s}Y_{\ell}^{m}.

The kernel of
(-\Delta)^{s}
on the sphere
S^{2}
, which we may write
b_{(-\Delta)^{s}}(\cos\theta)
, has a similar asymptotic behavior as the collision kernel
b_{col}(\cos\theta)
. There is no explicit formula for
b_{(-\Delta)^{s}}
either. It can be written in the form (
9.1
) with

\omega_{s}(t)=t^{-1-s}/C_{s},

where

C_{s}=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-t})t^{-1-s}\;\mathrm{d}t=-\Gamma(-s).

If we consider other weight functions
\omega(t)
which are comparable to
\omega_{s}(t)
as
t\to 0
, we obtain other kernels
b_{\omega}
with the same asymptotic behavior at
\theta\to 0
as
b_{(-\Delta)^{s}}
, which coincides with the asymptotic behavior of
b_{col}
. For any such kernel, we are able to precisely compute the limit

\lim_{\theta\to 0}\frac{b_{col}(\cos\theta)}{b_{\omega}(\cos\theta)}.

Thus, the challenge to compute the constants
c_{1}
and
C_{2}
in the inequality (
1.7
) arises mostly from values of
\theta
that are not too close to zero or
\pi
.

Computing
b_{\omega}
numerically is nontrivial. All we know is that the integro-differential operator with kernel
b_{\omega}
has the spherical harmonics as eigenfunctions with computable eigenvalues. For any smooth function
f
, we can compute the integro-differential operator

\mathcal{B}_{\omega}f(\sigma):=\int_{S^{d-1}}(f(\sigma^{\prime})-f(\sigma))b_{\omega}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime},

by decomposing the function
f
as a sum of spherical harmonics and multiplying each term by the corresponding eigenvalue. We must reverse engineer the kernel
b_{\omega}
from the values of this operator. A simple way to achieve it is by considering a function
f
which is smooth but it is very concentrated around
\sigma^{\prime}\cdot e_{1}=x_{0}
. From the value of
\mathcal{B}_{\omega}f(e_{1})
, we estimate the value of
b_{\omega}(x_{0})
. To implement this computation with reasonable accuracy, we need to use a large resolution and many modes. This method works farily well, especially if
\theta
is not too close to zero. As we discussed before, we already know the approximate value of the ratio
b_{col}/b_{\omega}
when
\theta
is almost zero.

Using the ideas above, we ran numerical simulations to estimate the ratio of
c_{1}/C_{2}
for the inequality (
1.7
). When we compared the collision kernel
b_{col}
with the kernel of the spherical fractional Laplacian, we observed that
c_{1}/C_{2}
is approximately
0.71
for
s=3/4
. The ratio improves as
s\to 1
. We experimented with reference kernels of the form (
9.1
) with different weight functions
\omega(t)
of the form
\omega(t)=t^{-1-s}\omega_{1}(t)
, for some nonnegative function
\omega_{1}
so that
\omega_{1}(0)=1
.

The kernel
b_{\omega}
matches the collision kernel of power-law potentials
b_{col}
very closely for
s\in[3/4,1)
with the following choice of weight function

(A.1)

\omega(t):=\left\{1-\min\left(\frac{13}{8}-\frac{3}{2}s,\frac{4}{10}\right)(1-\exp(-2t))\right\}t^{-1-s}.

Appendix B
Vector fields generating rotations

Following
[
17
]
, we construct a family of vector fields
\mathfrak{b}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{b}_{N}
, for
N=d(d-1)/2
that are tangential to the sphere
S^{d-1}
and generate the Lie algebra
so(d)
. They can be used as an alternative to the transformations
P_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}
described in Section
4.3
to derive formulas that involve
\Gamma^{2}_{\Delta,\mathcal{B}}
and
\nabla\mathcal{B}f
.

For
d=2
, we would consider just the single vector field
\mathfrak{b}_{1}(\sigma)=\sigma^{\perp}
.

In three dimensions
d=3
it is the set of three vector fields used in
[
17
]
.

(B.1)

\mathfrak{b}_{1}(\sigma)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
-\sigma_{3}\\
\sigma_{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathfrak{b}_{2}(\sigma)=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{3}\\
0\\
-\sigma_{1}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathfrak{b}_{3}(\sigma)=\begin{pmatrix}-\sigma_{2}\\
\sigma_{1}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

In higher dimension
d\geq 4
, there are
d(d-1)/2
vector fields that general the Lie group
so(d)
. They can be written in terms of pairs of indices
1\leq i<j\leq d
as

\mathfrak{b}_{ij}(\sigma)=\sigma_{i}e_{j}-\sigma_{j}e_{i}.

The flow of the vector field
\mathfrak{b}_{ij}
consists in a rotation of the sphere on the
ij
-plane. In particular, the flow of any of these vector fields is an isometry, and the vector fields are divergence free.

By reindexing if necessary, we write these vector fields as
\mathfrak{b}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{b}_{N}
.

The following simple lemma is justified in
[
17
]
.

Lemma B.1

(Gradient and Laplacian through the
\mathfrak{b}_{i}
’s)
.

For any smooth functions
f,g:S^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta f

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathfrak{b}_{i}\cdot\nabla(\mathfrak{b}_{i}\cdot\nabla f),

\displaystyle|\nabla f|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\mathfrak{b}_{i}\cdot\nabla f)^{2},

\displaystyle\nabla f\cdot\nabla g

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\mathfrak{b}_{i}\cdot\nabla f)(\mathfrak{b}_{i}\cdot\nabla g).

With the help of these vector fields, we can provide an explicit expression for
\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}(f,f)
.

Lemma B.2

(Iterated carré du champ through the
\mathfrak{b}_{i}
’s)
.

For any smooth function
f:S^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have,

\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}(f,f)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{S^{d-1}}\bigg(b_{i}(\sigma^{\prime})\cdot\nabla f(\sigma^{\prime})-b_{i}(\sigma)\cdot\nabla f(\sigma)\bigg)^{2}b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}.

In particular,
\Gamma^{2}_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}(f,f)\geq 0
.

We first prove the following technical lemmas.

Lemma B.3
.

Given
b:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, consider the function from
S^{d-1}\times S^{d-1}
given by
(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\mapsto b(\sigma\cdot\sigma^{\prime})
. Then, for any of the vector fields
\mathfrak{b}_{k}
on
TS^{d-1}
, we have

\mathfrak{b}_{k}(\sigma)\cdot\nabla_{\sigma}[b(\sigma\cdot\sigma^{\prime})]=-\mathfrak{b}_{k}(\sigma^{\prime})\cdot\nabla_{\sigma^{\prime}}[b(\sigma\cdot\sigma^{\prime})].

Proof.

We start with the explicit computations

\displaystyle\nabla_{\sigma}[b(\sigma\cdot\sigma^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=b^{\prime}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\ \sigma^{\prime},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\sigma^{\prime}}[b(\sigma\cdot\sigma^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=b^{\prime}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\ \sigma.

Since
\mathfrak{b}_{k}(\sigma)\in T_{S^{d-1}}(\sigma)
, then
\mathfrak{b}_{k}(\sigma)\perp\sigma
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}_{k}(\sigma)\cdot\nabla_{\sigma}[b(\sigma\cdot\sigma^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=b^{\prime}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\ \mathfrak{b}_{k}(\sigma)\cdot\sigma^{\prime}

\displaystyle=b^{\prime}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\ \mathfrak{b}_{k}(\sigma)\cdot(\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma).

The vector field
\mathfrak{b}_{k}(\sigma)
is given by
\mathfrak{b}_{k}(\sigma)=A_{k}\sigma
for some antisymmetric matrix
A_{k}
. In particular, it extends as a linear operator in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

\mathfrak{b}_{k}(\sigma)-\mathfrak{b}_{k}(\sigma^{\prime})\perp\sigma-\sigma^{\prime}
. Therefore
\mathfrak{b}_{k}(\sigma)\cdot(\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma)=\mathfrak{b}_{k}(\sigma^{\prime})\cdot(\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma)
, and we get

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}_{k}(\sigma)\cdot\nabla_{\sigma}[b(\sigma\cdot\sigma^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=b^{\prime}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\ \mathfrak{b}_{k}(\sigma^{\prime})\cdot(\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma)

\displaystyle=-b^{\prime}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\ \mathfrak{b}_{k}(\sigma^{\prime})\cdot\sigma

\displaystyle=-\mathfrak{b}_{k}(\sigma^{\prime})\cdot\nabla_{\sigma^{\prime}}[b(\sigma\cdot\sigma^{\prime})].\qed

Lemma B.4
.

For any of the vector fields
\mathfrak{b}_{i}
on
TS^{d-1}
, the following expression holds

\mathfrak{b}_{i}(\sigma)\cdot\nabla_{\sigma}\mathcal{B}f=\int_{\sigma^{\prime}\in S^{d-1}}(\mathfrak{b}_{i}(\sigma^{\prime})\cdot\nabla f^{\prime}-\mathfrak{b}_{i}(\sigma)\cdot\nabla f)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that the kernel
b
is smooth. The general case follows by a standard approximation procedure.

We differentiate within the integral expression for
\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}_{i}(\sigma)\cdot\nabla_{\sigma}\mathcal{B}f

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{b}_{i}(\sigma)\cdot\left(\int(f^{\prime}-f)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\int-\mathfrak{b}_{i}(\sigma)\cdot\nabla f\ b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)+(f^{\prime}-f)\mathfrak{b}_{i}(\sigma)\cdot\nabla_{\sigma}[b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)]\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}

We apply Lemma
B.3
for the second term.

\displaystyle=\int-\mathfrak{b}_{i}(\sigma)\cdot\nabla f\ b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)-(f^{\prime}-f)\mathfrak{b}_{i}(\sigma^{\prime})\cdot\nabla_{\sigma^{\prime}}[b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)]\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}

We integrate by parts the second term recalling that
\mathfrak{b}_{i}(\sigma^{\prime})
is divergence free.

\displaystyle=\int(\mathfrak{b}_{i}(\sigma^{\prime})\cdot\nabla f^{\prime}-\mathfrak{b}_{i}(\sigma)\cdot\nabla f)\ b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}.\qed

We can now prove Lemma
B.2
.

Proof of Lemma
B.2
.

By definition

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathcal{B},\Delta}^{2}(f,f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{B}\Gamma_{\Delta}(f,f)-\Gamma_{\Delta}(f,\mathcal{B}f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\left(|\nabla f(\sigma^{\prime})|^{2}-|\nabla f(\sigma)|^{2}\right)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}-\nabla f(\sigma)\cdot\nabla\left(\int(f^{\prime}-f)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\right),

using Lemma
B.1
,

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\frac{1}{2}\int\left((b_{i}(\sigma^{\prime})\cdot\nabla f^{\prime})^{2}-(b_{i}(\sigma)\cdot\nabla f)^{2}\right)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}

\displaystyle\qquad-\bigg(b_{i}(\sigma)\cdot\nabla f(\sigma)\bigg)\left(b_{i}(\sigma)\cdot\nabla\int(f^{\prime}-f)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\right),

we apply Lemma
B.4
for the second term,

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\frac{1}{2}\int\left((b_{i}(\sigma^{\prime})\cdot\nabla f^{\prime})^{2}-(b_{i}(\sigma)\cdot\nabla f)^{2}\right)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}

\displaystyle\qquad-\bigg(b_{i}(\sigma)\cdot\nabla f(\sigma)\bigg)\left(\int\big(b_{i}(\sigma^{\prime})\cdot\nabla f^{\prime}-b_{i}(\sigma)\cdot\nabla f\big)b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\frac{1}{2}\int\left(b_{i}(\sigma^{\prime})\cdot\nabla f^{\prime}-b_{i}(\sigma)\cdot\nabla f\right)^{2}b(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma)\;\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\prime}.\qed
