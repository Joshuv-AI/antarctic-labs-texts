Causal Effects of Gut Microbiota on Morning Chronotype, Insomnia and Sleep Duration: A Two-Sample Mendelian Randomization Study
Authors: Yuqin He, Jiahuan Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe gut microbiota has been shown to be closely associated with brain function; however, whether it exerts a causal influence on sleep traits remains to be further explored. Mendelian randomization (MR) is an emerging epidemiological approach that uses whole-genome sequencing data to infer causal relationships. In this study, we conducted a two-sample MR analysis to investigate the causal effects of gut microbiota on three domains of sleep traits: morning chronotype, insomnia, and sleep duration.MethodsSingle nucleotide polymorphisms strongly associated with 196 gut microbiota taxa were selected as instrumental variables. Morning chronotype, insomnia, and sleep duration were used as outcomes. MR and sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the causal relationships between gut microbiota and sleep traits.ResultsThree taxa (Bifidobacteriales, Bifidobacteriaceae, and Bifidobacterium) were negatively associated with morning chronotype, while Tyzzerella 3 showed a positive causal effect on morning chronotype. Oscillibacter was negatively associated with insomnia, whereas four taxa (Negativicutes, Selenomonadales, the Clostridium innocuum group, and Lachnoclostridium) were identified as risk-increasing factors for insomnia. Lentisphaerae and Victivallaceae were positively associated with sleep duration. Actinobacteria and Alistipes had negative effects on long sleep duration, whereas Ruminiclostridium 6 was positively associated with long sleep duration. Four taxa (Victivallales, Anaerofilum, Lentisphaerae, and Lentisphaeria) were negatively associated with short sleep duration.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that specific gut microbiota taxa may be positively or negatively associated with sleep traits. These results offer new insights into the potential role of gut microbiota in sleep regulation and provide a basis for future studies aimed at understanding whether modulating microbial composition could influence sleep health.

Background

Sleep is a periodic, reversible physiological phenomenon in higher vertebrates, including humans, characterized by a resting state with transient reduced responsiveness to external stimuli and temporary interruption of consciousness. Sleep plays a crucial role in relieving physical fatigue and maintaining overall health. Previous studies have shown that the prevalence of sleep disturbance is increasing, with approximately 30-35% of adults suffering from insomnia, and up to 75% in the elderly population.1,2 Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify approaches for regulating sleep rhythm and developing effective interventions for sleep disorders.

Gut microbiota refers to the community of microorganisms residing in the intestinal tract, which synthesizes essential vitamins and amino acids, participates in carbohydrate and protein metabolism, and facilitates mineral absorption. Emerging evidence has revealed that sleep traits are closely associated with gut microbiota, which may influence sleep through various mechanisms, including immune interactions, the serotonergic system, the vagus nerve, the blood-brain barrier, and bacterial metabolites. 3 However, whether specific gut microbiota taxa exert a causal influence on sleep traits remains unclear. To address this gap, we conducted Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses to explore the causal relationships between gut microbiota and three domains of sleep traits: morning chronotype, insomnia, and sleep duration, including five sleep-related outcomes.

Mendelian randomization offers distinct advantages for investigating such causal relationships. Unlike traditional observational studies, two-sample MR uses genetic variants as instrumental variables, which helps avoid confounding factors such as lifestyle and dietary habits, thereby improving the accuracy of causal inference. While observational studies can reveal correlations between gut microbiota and sleep traits, MR directly assesses causal effects by analyzing how heritable variations influence both gut microbiota and sleep outcomes. Inverse variance weighted (IVW) and MR-Egger regression were used to estimate causal effects, and sensitivity analyses were performed to test the robustness and validity of the results. By addressing potential confounding and pleiotropy, two-sample MR enhances the reliability of causal inference. Furthermore, MR analysis can provide insights into the underlying biological mechanisms linking gut microbiota to sleep traits, offering scientific evidence for future studies aimed at modulating specific microbiota to improve sleep health.

Methods

Study Design

A two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was conducted to explore the causal effects of gut microbiota on sleep traits. A brief overview of the study design is presented in Figure 1. The sleep traits investigated included morning chronotype, sleep duration, and insomnia. A total of five MR analyses were performed to examine the causal relationships between gut microbiota and sleep disturbances. Three core assumptions were followed: (1) genetic instrumental variables (IVs) are strongly associated with the exposure; (2) genetic IVs are independent of confounders; and (3) genetic IVs affect the outcome only through the exposure.Figure 1.Study design and workflow

Study design and workflow

Genetic Associations With Gut Microbiota

The genome-wide association study (GWAS) data for gut microbiota used in this study were obtained from the Mibiogen consortium. This multi-ethnic, large-scale GWAS included 18,340 individuals from 24 cohorts across Europe, the Middle East, East Asia, Hispanic America, African America, and admixed ancestry 4 (Supplementary Table 1, 2).

Genetic Associations With Sleep-Associated Traits

Sleep health is a multidimensional concept encompassing sleep timing, sleep duration, ease of falling asleep, sleep satisfaction, and attentive wakefulness. 5 To investigate the causal associations of gut microbiota with sleep disorders, we sourced GWAS data for morning chronotype, sleep duration, and insomnia. The GWAS for morning chronotype (preference for earlier bedtime and waking) was derived from the UK Biobank, including 449,734 European individuals 6 (Supplementary Table 3). A total of 351 loci were found to be associated with morning chronotype independently of sleep duration or quality. A sleep duration of 7-8 hours per night was considered normal, whereas >9 hours and <6 hours were defined as long and short sleep duration, respectively. A GWAS identified 78 loci associated with sleep duration in 446,118 adults of European ancestry from the UK Biobank 7 (Supplementary Table 4). Another study performed two parallel GWAS and identified 57 loci for insomnia in 453,479 individuals from the UK Biobank, with replication in the HUNT study 8 (Supplementary Table 5).

Selection of Genetic Instrumental Variables

Because few single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) reached genome-wide significance (p < 5×10-8), SNPs with p < 1×10-5 were selected as candidate IVs. These candidate SNPs were then pruned using a clumping threshold of R2 < 0.1 within a window of < 500 kilobases. The F-statistic was calculated for each SNP to evaluate instrument strength, and weak instruments (F < 10) were excluded.

The F-statistic was calculated as follows: F=N−k−1k×R21−R2 where N is the sample size, k is the number of IVs, and R2 represents the proportion of variance in the exposure explained by the IVs. R2 was derived using the following formula 9 : R2=β2β2+SE2✕N where β is the effect size, SE is the standard error, and N is the sample size.

MR Analysis

Mendelian randomization was applied to estimate the causal effects of gut microbiota on morning chronotype, sleep duration, and insomnia. Five methods were used: MR-Egger, weighted median, random-effects inverse variance weighted (IVW), simple mode, and weighted mode. Among these, IVW was the primary method for determining causal relationships. Associations were considered potentially causal when both of the following conditions were met: (1) the directions of the β estimates were consistent across MR-Egger, weighted median, IVW, and MR-PRESSO; and (2) the IVW p-value was <0.05.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses were performed in three domains. First, heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic; a p-value <0.05 indicated the presence of heterogeneity, although this did not invalidate the IVW results. Second, horizontal pleiotropy was tested using the MR-Egger intercept test and MR-PRESSO; a p-value >0.05 suggested no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy. Third, leave-one-out analysis was conducted to evaluate the influence of individual SNPs by recalculating the effect estimates after removing each SNP one at a time.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the TwoSampleMR package in R software (version 4.2.3). All tests were two-sided. Nominal statistical significance threshold was set at p < 0.05. To control for multiple testing across all tested exposure-outcome associations, Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied globally to all IVW-derived causal p-values. Associations with FDR-adjusted Q-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Effect estimates were expressed as odds ratios per standard deviation increment of the corresponding exposure.

Results

Associations of Gut Bacterial Taxa With Morning Chronotype

The results of the analyses examining associations between gut bacterial taxa and morning chronotype are presented in Figure 2. Among the 196 bacterial taxa, four taxa were found to be associated with morning chronotype (Supplementary Table 6). The two-sample MR results indicated that three taxa were negatively associated with morning chronotype: order Bifidobacteriales (IVW OR = 0.955, 95% CI 0.933-0.978, p = 1.54×10-4, Q = 1.70×10-3), family Bifidobacteriaceae (IVW OR = 0.955, 95% CI 0.933-0.978, p = 1.54×10-4, Q = 1.70×10-3), and genus Bifidobacterium (IVW OR = 0.963, 95% CI 0.941-0.986, p = 1.40×10-3, Q = 8.82×10-3). In addition, genus Tyzzerella 3 was found to have a positive causal effect on morning chronotype (IVW OR = 1.030, 95% CI 1.009-1.052, p = 5.79×10-3, Q = 1.70×10-2).Figure 2.Mendelian randomization results of causal effects between gut microbiota and morning chronotype, insomnia

Associations of Gut Bacterial Taxa With Insomnia

We explored the potential causal associations between gut bacterial taxa and insomnia (Figure 2, Supplementary Table 6). The two-sample MR results showed that genus Oscillibacter was negatively associated with insomnia (IVW OR = 0.985, 95% CI 0.974-0.996, p = 5.39×10-3, Q = 1.70×10-2). Four taxa were identified as risk-increasing factors for insomnia: class Negativicutes (IVW OR = 1.031, 95% CI 1.016-1.047, p = 7.53×10-5, Q = 1.66×10-3), order Selenomonadales (IVW OR = 1.031, 95% CI 1.016-1.047, p = 7.53×10-5, Q = 1.66×10-3), genus Clostridium innocuum group (IVW OR = 1.018, 95% CI 1.005-1.031, p = 5.67×10-3, Q = 1.70×10-2), and genus Lachnoclostridium (IVW OR = 1.029, 95% CI 1.007-1.052, p = 8.61×10-3, Q = 2.23×10-2).

Associations of Gut Bacterial Taxa With Sleep Duration

The associations between gut bacterial taxa and sleep duration were investigated using two-sample MR (Figure 3, Supplementary Table 6). Two taxa were causally associated with overall sleep duration: phylum Lentisphaerae (IVW OR = 1.029, 95% CI 1.012-1.047, p = 1.00×10-3, Q = 8.80×10-3) and family Victivallaceae (IVW OR = 1.016, 95% CI 1.004-1.028, p = 8.45×10-3, Q = 2.23×10-2).Figure 3.Mendelian randomization results of causal effects between gut microbiota and sleep duration, long sleep duration, short sleep duration

Mendelian randomization results of causal effects between gut microbiota and sleep duration, long sleep duration, short sleep duration

Associations of Gut Bacterial Taxa With Long Sleep Duration

Overall, three bacterial taxa were found to be causally associated with long sleep duration (Figure 3, Supplementary Table 6). Among these, genus Ruminiclostridium 6 showed a potential causal effect in extending sleep duration (IVW OR = 1.009, 95% CI 1.001-1.016, p = 1.95×10-2, Q = 3.81×10-2). The other two taxa were negatively associated with long sleep duration: phylum Actinobacteria (IVW OR = 0.987, 95% CI 0.979-0.995, p = 2.27×10-3, Q = 1.11×10-2) and genus Alistipes (IVW OR = 0.988, 95% CI 0.980-0.997, p = 1.12×10-2, Q = 2.62×10-2).

Associations of Gut Bacterial Taxa With Short Sleep Duration

Four taxa were identified as negatively associated with short sleep duration (Figure 3, Supplementary Table 6): phylum Lentisphaerae (IVW OR = 0.989, 95% CI 0.983-0.996, p = 1.38×10-3, Q = 8.82×10-3), class Lentisphaeria (IVW OR = 0.989, 95% CI 0.982-0.997, p = 5.50×10-3, Q = 1.70×10-2), order Victivallales (IVW OR = 0.989, 95% CI 0.982-0.997, p = 5.50×10-3, Q = 1.70×10-2), and genus Anaerofilum (IVW OR = 0.991, 95% CI 0.985-0.997, p = 2.93×10-3, Q = 1.29×10-2).

Discussion

In this two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) study, we systematically investigated the causal effects of gut microbiota on three domains of sleep traits: morning chronotype, insomnia, and sleep duration. Using multiple MR methods (MR-Egger, weighted median, inverse-variance weighted, simple mode, and weighted mode), we identified a set of microbial taxa with putative causal roles in sleep regulation. These findings address a knowledge gap by providing genetic evidence for a causal contribution of gut microbiota to sleep health, and may inform novel strategies for sleep rhythm modulation and disorder management. Notably, the modest magnitude of causal estimates is consistent with the inherent constraints of MR studies, wherein individual genetic variants typically explain limited phenotypic variance.

Mechanistic Links Between Identified Taxa and Sleep Regulation

The gut-brain axis provides a well-established framework for microbiota-host communication. In the following sections, we elaborate on how the specific taxa identified in our study may influence sleep traits through established biological pathways, including short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) metabolism, neurotransmitter modulation, and immune-inflammatory signaling.

Morning Chronotype

Chronotype refers to an individual’s behavioral preference to getting up early or sleeping late or interposing affected by the interaction of individual biological circadian rhythm and external social schedules. People are divided into three types: morning, evening, and intermediate. 10 For morning chronotype, we observed that Tyzzerella 3 was positively associated with morning preference, whereas Bifidobacteriales, Bifidobacteriaceae, and Bifidobacterium showed inverse associations.

The circadian rhythm-modulating effects of these taxa are likely mediated by SCFAs and their impact on core clock genes. For instance, Tyzzerella 3, a member of the Lachnospiraceae family, is a known butyrate-producing genus. Butyrate can inhibit histone deacetylase 3 (HDAC3), thereby modulating chromatin accessibility and transcriptional activity of core clock genes such as Per2 and Bmal1. 11 In addition to epigenetic regulation, SCFAs activate enteroendocrine cells via G-protein-coupled receptors (e.g., GPR43), promoting the secretion of serotonin (5-HT), a key precursor for melatonin that orchestrates circadian entrainment. 12

Conversely, the inverse association with Bifidobacterium is particularly noteworthy. Bifidobacteria are well-recognized for their capacity to metabolize dietary tryptophan, the primary precursor for serotonin and subsequently melatonin. 13 Reduced abundance of Bifidobacterium may therefore limit tryptophan availability, leading to diminished central serotonin synthesis and disrupted circadian entrainment. Furthermore, Bifidobacterium contributes to SCFA production, and its depletion may compromise the microbial capacity to support rhythmic clock gene expression.

Insomnia

Insomnia is defined as a subjective experience of difficulty falling asleep, decreased sleep quality, and reduced sleep time concomitant with fatigue, somatic symptoms, reduced memory and concentration. Approximately one third of adult population are suffering from insomnia worldwide. 14 Our results identified Selenomonadales, Negativicutes, Lachnoclostridium, and the Clostridium innocuum group as risk-increasing taxa for insomnia, while Oscillibacter was negatively associated.

Several of these taxa have been directly implicated in neurotransmitter pathways relevant to sleep regulation. GABA is the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system, promoting sedation and slow-wave sleep. Emerging evidence indicates that specific gut bacteria can synthesize GABA or stimulate enterochromaffin cells to secrete GABA precursors. 15 Oscillibacter has been associated with GABAergic activity, and its reduced abundance in individuals with insomnia may contribute to an imbalance between excitatory (e.g., glutamate) and inhibitory neurotransmission, thereby impairing sleep initiation and maintenance. 16

In addition to GABA, serotonin synthesis also plays a critical role. Approximately 90% of the body’s serotonin is produced by enterochromaffin cells in the gut, and its level directly influences melatonin synthesis. 17 Dysbiosis involving taxa such as Lachnoclostridium—which has been linked to altered tryptophan metabolism—may reduce serotonin availability, contributing to difficulties in sleep initiation and increased sleep fragmentation.

Immune-mediated pathways further warrant consideration. Gram-negative bacteria, including certain Clostridium species, release lipopolysaccharide (LPS) as a component of their cell wall. When gut barrier integrity is compromised, LPS can enter the systemic circulation, activate microglial Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling, and trigger neuroinflammation. 18 Elevated LPS levels have been shown to prolong non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep while suppressing rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, and chronic LPS exposure can lead to sleep fragmentation and reduced total sleep time. 19 Additionally, gut dysbiosis can activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis through stimulation of corticotropin-releasing hormone, increasing cortical excitability and prolonging wakefulness. Under chronic stress, disruption of the gut barrier establishes a vicious cycle of “stress-insomnia-microbiota disorder”. 20

Sleep Duration

The findings of a pooled longitudinal cohort study suggest that a sleep duration of 7 hours in a 24-hour period is the best for physical and mental health, the sleep duration longer or shorter than 7 hours could increase the cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality. 21 The results of an epidemiological research showed only 65.2% adults had a healthy sleep duration. 22 For sleep duration, we found that Lentisphaerae and Victivallaceae were positively associated with overall sleep duration. Actinobacteria and Alistipes showed negative effects on long sleep duration, while Ruminiclostridium 6 was positively associated with long sleep duration. Victivallales, Anaerofilum, Lentisphaerae, and Lentisphaeria were negatively associated with short sleep duration.

The associations observed for Victivallis may be explained by its involvement in SCFA production and bile acid metabolism. SCFAs such as butyrate and propionate exert anti-inflammatory effects and help maintain intestinal barrier integrity. 23 Butyrate-producing bacteria have been shown to reduce systemic inflammation, which is known to interfere with sleep stability. Moreover, microbial metabolites such as methionine can upregulate BMAL1 and CLOCK expression in intestinal epithelial cells, reducing oxidative stress and mitigating inflammation associated with sleep deprivation. 24

Conversely, Alistipes has been implicated in pro-inflammatory states and may contribute to sleep fragmentation through immune-mediated pathways. Some intestinal bacteria can synthesize dopamine and norepinephrine, indirectly affecting sleep architecture by modulating arousal and attention. Dysbiosis involving Alistipes may therefore influence sleep duration through catecholamine signaling. Notably, emerging evidence suggests that gut microbiota can stimulate enteroendocrine cells to secrete cholecystokinin and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1). 25 These hormones enhance satiety and inhibit stress responses via the vagus nerve, thereby indirectly promoting deep sleep. The positive association with Victivallis may partly reflect this pathway.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, horizontal pleiotropy—the possibility that genetic variants influence sleep outcomes through pathways independent of the microbiota—remains an inherent limitation of MR studies. Although we employed multiple MR methods (MR-Egger, weighted median, MR-PRESSO) to detect and correct for pleiotropy, and observed no evidence of directional pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept p > 0.05), no statistical approach can fully exclude undetected pleiotropic pathways. Triangulation with evidence from orthogonal approaches, such as experimental models or randomized controlled trials, is therefore necessary to strengthen causal inference.

Second, our microbiota data were derived from 16S rRNA sequencing, which provides relative abundance estimates that are compositional in nature. This means that changes in one taxon may reflect shifts in others rather than absolute quantitative changes, limiting the interpretability of effect estimates and potentially affecting generalizability to absolute microbial loads. Future studies using quantitative metagenomic approaches (e.g., shotgun metagenomics with internal standards) will be valuable to validate our findings.

Third, sleep traits were assessed using self-reported measures (chronotype, insomnia symptoms, sleep duration), which are susceptible to measurement error and recall bias. While such subjective measures are commonly used in large-scale genome-wide association studies due to feasibility, non-differential misclassification may bias effect estimates toward the null. Replication using objective measures (e.g., accelerometry-based sleep assessment) is warranted.

Fourth, our analyses relied on summary-level genome-wide association study data derived predominantly from individuals of European ancestry. This limits the generalizability of our findings to non-European populations, as genetic architectures, allele frequencies, and linkage disequilibrium patterns may differ across ancestries. Future MR studies leveraging expanding multi-ancestry resources (e.g., from the Global Biobank Meta-Analysis Initiative, Biobank Japan, or the Million Veteran Program) will be essential to assess the cross-ancestry generalizability of the observed causal relationships.

Conclusion

In summary, this MR study provides genetic evidence suggesting potential roles for several gut microbial taxa in relation to morning chronotype, insomnia, and sleep duration. The identified taxa show associations with sleep-related biological pathways, including short-chain fatty acid metabolism, neurotransmitter synthesis (serotonin, GABA, dopamine), and immune-inflammatory signaling. However, given the inherent limitations of MR studies—such as residual pleiotropy and the use of relative abundance data—these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as definitive causal conclusions. Further validation through experimental models, intervention studies, or multi-ancestry genetic data is required before any translational or clinical implications can be considered.
