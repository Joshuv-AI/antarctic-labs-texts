Protocol to measure glucose utilization in mouse tissues using radiolabeled 2-deoxy-D-glucose
Authors: Cyun-Ming Wu, Hao-Yun Li, Wen-Yi Li, Chih-Neng Hsu, Wei-Shun Yang, Gwo-Tsang Chuang, Tony Pan-Hou Che, Tung-Yuan Lee, Hsiao-Lin Lee, Siow-Wey Hee, Jiin-Horng Lee, Daniel Liao, Karen Chia-Wen Liao, Ya-Wen Liu, Chin-Feng Hsuan, Yi-Cheng Chang, Shao-Lun Chu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Summary2-deoxy-D-glucose (2DG) is a glucose analog converted to 2-deoxy-D-glucose-6-phosphate (2DG-6P) by hexokinase in glycolysis. While 2DG commonly measures glucose uptake, 2DG-6P detects glucose utilization. Here, we present a protocol to measure glucose utilization in various tissues after entering a mouse’s body using radiolabeled 2DG. We describe steps for preparing mice and chemicals, extracting blood, adding chemicals, and dissolving tissue. We then detail procedures for calculating glucose utilization using the trapezoid rule.Subject areas: Health Sciences, Metabolism, Metabolomics

Before you begin

The following protocol describes the specific steps to measure glucose utilization and can also be used to measure glucose uptake if you stop at the step involving Perchloric acid without using the Somogyi reagent. This protocol is a close adaptation of a detailed protocol theoretically and experimentally described in Ferre et al., Biochem. J. (1985) 228, 103-110 for rat tissues.1 In addition, this protocol also partially refers to the methods proposed by Sokoloff in 1977 and Yamamoto in 2015.2,3 This experiment did not use the clamp technique to maintain blood glucose levels within a steady state in the mice, which may have introduced some error in the final calculations. However, performing the clamp procedure in mice is technically challenging. Our method is intended to offer a simpler, more convenient, and practically feasible way to measure glucose utilization across different tissues.4

2-Deoxy-D-glucose (2DG) is structurally similar to glucose but lacks the ability to undergo further metabolism, which has the 2-hydroxyl group replaced by hydrogen. It is often used as a radioactive tracer to measure glucose uptake.5 It competitively inhibits the production of glucose-6-phosphate from glucose at the phosphoglucoisomerase level (step 2 of glycolysis). Here, we provide a technique to detect the glucose utilization in vivo by using radioactive 2DG.

We choose brown adipose tissue (BAT) and muscle as our sample tissues in this protocol for some reasons. First, they are tissues having high glucose utilization in humans, with muscle accounting for 50% of total body glucose utilization, and BAT has about 1% of total body glucose utilization, but it has a very high metabolic rate.6 Second, muscle and BAT express insulin-responsive glucose transporters GLUT4, making them suitable for assessing insulin-stimulated glucose utilization. They show increased glucose utilization when insulin levels are high.7,8 Although the brain has high rates of glucose metabolism, we do not choose them because they mainly express GLUT3.9 The 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG) method is not applicable in the liver due to the significant activity of glucose-6-phosphatase, an enzyme capable of dephosphorylating 2-DG-6-phosphate.1 For the heart, studies show that fatty acids are a more favorable substrate than glucose for metabolism, contributing 40%–70% and 20%–30% of ATP generation, respectively.10 In the fasting situation, the glucose-sparing effect of lipid-derived substrates has been clearly demonstrated in vitro in the heart and diaphragm.11 In this experiment, we use fasted animals, whose hearts majorly use fatty acids as their fuel. In this experiment, glucose uptake in tissue is the sum of 2-DG and 2-DG6P when we consider there’s no reverse transportation from tissue to blood, while 2DG-6P represents glucose utilization which is phosphorylated by hexokinase.

The major principle of this protocol is that both 2DG and 2DG-6P can dissolve in the Perchloric acid (4.5% PCA), but only 2DG can dissolve in the Somogyi reagent (0.15 M Ba(OH)2(aq) + 0.15 M ZnSO4(aq)). 2DG-6P will precipitate to the bottom of the solution.1 By measuring the difference in radiation between the two solutions, we can determine glucose utilization(2DG-6P) of tissues and convert it into an approximation of the absolute value. This method provides a relatively easy way to let us know the glucose utilization in body tissues compared to glucose clamp technique.

Expected outcomes

Experiment 1

In this experiment, 2DG is like the radioactive indicator of glucose, and it only accounts for a small percentage of the glucose we inject. Therefore, the amount of 2DG shouldn’t have an effect on the absolute glucose utilization. But as an indicator, the 2DG utilization in tissue will increase.

Our goal is to verify that increasing the ratio of 2-deoxyglucose (2DG) to glucose leads to a linear increase in radioactive measurement of 2DG utilization in tissues per mass while maintaining a similar number of glucose utilization. Additionally, to ensure the ratio of radioactive measurement in the blood to blood glucose also shows a linear increase. Establishing this correlation, we can draw a standard curve and make sure this protocol for measuring glucose utilization in the tissue is accessible.

After fasting the mice for 5 h, we conduct this experiment by using the protocol above. We divide the mice into 4 groups(n = 5), and each will have (0 μCi/25 g mice, 10 μCi/25 g mice, 20 μCi/25 g mice, 40 μCi/25 g mice), and in the ratio of 2DG stock and 20% glucose will be (0:15, 1:15, 2:15, 4:15). We should inject the mixture of 2DG and 20% glucose 5∗weight microliter.

The results show that the utilization of 2DG in tissue is proportional to the ratio of 2DG we mix in 20% glucose, showing a strong linear relationship with Rˆ2 in muscle and BAT = 0.9999 and 0.9981, respectively (Figures 2A and 2B). Furthermore, the ratio of 2DG and blood glucose (calculated in steps 23&24) also exhibits a similar outcome with Rˆ2 = 0.9946 (Figure 2C), which indicates that it can have a precise conversion from 2DG utilization(net DPM/45 min per mg weight ) to the number of glucose utilization (ng/45 min per mg weight). As for the glucose utilization, it shows no significant statistical differences in 3 different groups (10 μCi/25 g mice, 20 μCi/25 g mice, 40 μCi/25 g mice), with the utilization in muscle per mg (701.51 ± 456.43 vs. 619.27 ± 275.57 vs. 691.84 ± 179.48 ng/mg, ANOVA p = 0.949) and the utilization in BAT per mg (1553.12 ± 969.67 vs. 1831.52 ± 1590.39 vs. 1771.77 ± 536.71 ng/mg, ANOVA p = 0.942) (Figures 2D and 2E) (Table S1). In conclusion, with the results above, we can clearly understand that the 2DG is an ideal radioactive tracer that has a good linear relationship when adding it more while having little influence on the final glucose utilization in the tissue.

Experiment 2

Studies show that insulin can stimulate glucose utilization in tissues while they express glucose transporter,20 both muscle and brown adipose tissue (BAT). With that in mind, we can design an experiment to ensure this protocol is accessible. Therefore, when we inject the insulin by intraperitoneal injection, we should observe an increase in glucose utilization by the 2DG utilization.

After fasting the mice overnight (16 h), for the reason that the insulin in the blood is low, we conducted this experiment by using the protocol mentioned above. We divided the mice into 2 groups (n = 5), one group is the control group with no insulin injection, and the other group is the experimental group which should be injected 10∗body weight (g) mL of 0.1 IU/mL insulin. The final dose of insulin will be 1 IU/body weight(g). The ratio of 2DG stock and 20% glucose will be 1:15. We should inject the mixture of 2DG and 20% glucose 5∗weight microliter, making it to be 10 μCi/25 g mice. We injected insulin before the injection of 2DG glucose in 2 min, for it takes time for insulin to affect blood circulation.

The results show that injection of insulin can make the detection of glucose utilization by this protocol increase, and both tissues share the same effect. It shows a significant statistical difference in control and experimental group (0 IU/ g mice vs. 10 IU/ g mice), with the utilization in muscle per mg (481.01 ± 229.78 vs. 879.94 ± 330.50 ng/mg, p = 0.029) and the utilization in BAT per mg (726.45 ± 415.95 vs. 2618.48 ± 1618.68 ng/mg, p = 0.018) (Figures 3A and 3B) (Table S2). In conclusion, with the results above, we can clearly understand that the 2DG is an ideal radioactive tracer. By using the protocol, we can detect the approximation of absolute glucose utilization and the observed trends of insulin and glucose utilization align with existing evidence.

Limitations

Low utilization in certain tissues (e.g., WAT) is difficult to detect

Measurement of glucose utilization in tissues with inherently low metabolic activity, such as white adipose tissue (WAT), can be challenging due to low levels of detected utilization. Indeed, white adipose tissue plays a significant role in glucose absorption and metabolism in the body. However, the amount of glucose utilized per unit weight is relatively small.1The instrument & biological error has a minimal impact on tissues with high radioactivity concentrations (high glucose utilization), such as BAT and muscle. Conversely, for tissues with lower glucose utilization per unit weight, such as WAT, this error significantly affects the accuracy of the test.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

In this protocol, the processed samples are ultimately measured for their radioactivity concentration using an instrument called the Beta counter. However, the readings from this instrument have a certain margin of random error. This may cause an impact on accuracy. Also, due to the biological individual differences in mice, it may also cause some biological errors (Step 22).

Potential solution

Ensure consistent experimental conditions and handling procedures. By conducting multiple measurements for each sample and using the average value, we can minimize the impact of random errors. Also, consider increasing the number of biological replicates to mitigate the impact of individual variability. If possible, use more sensitive instruments or methods designed to measure low radioactivity levels with higher accuracy.

Problem 2

In this protocol, it is necessary to perform intravenous injection on mice. If we inject tracer into the subcutaneous tissue, the data for that mouse will show a significant deviation (Step 7).

Potential solution

Given that the tail veins of mice are not easily visible, it is advisable to place the mice in a warm environment first to cause vasodilation, making them more obvious. Additionally, performing the intravenous injection in a well-lit area can increase the success rate. Since IV injection can be challenging, it is advisable to practice the technique beforehand to ensure proficiency before starting the experiment.

Problem 3

When we collect blood from a mouse’s tail, the external stimulus can induce stress in the mouse. This stress response may cause fluctuations in the mouse’s blood glucose levels, leading to potential inaccuracies in the measurement results of the conversion of 2DG (DPM) to glucose. Such stress-induced variations can complicate the interpretation of glucose utilization (Step 1).

Potential solution

Performing blood collection in a quiet, controlled environment can minimize external stressors. Additionally, gentle and consistent handling techniques help reduce stress during blood collection. Acclimating the mice to handling and the blood collection procedure over several days before the actual experiment can further reduce stress responses.

Problem 4

Ideally, having more sampling points allows for a more accurate construction of the trapezoidal area under the curve. This, in turn, enables a more precise determination of the ratio relationship between 2DG and glucose. However, due to the necessity of processing blood samples after collection, it becomes difficult to take multiple samples within a short period. Therefore, we have opted for 15-min intervals for blood collection to ensure that we have sufficient time to process each sample properly (Steps 23, 24).

Potential solution

If your laboratory has adequate staffing, it may be possible to shorten the interval between sampling points. Your colleague can take over the processing of the subsequent blood samples, allowing the experimenter to continue drawing blood. Shorter intervals would provide a more detailed picture of the ratio between 2DG and glucose, which would enhance the accuracy of the measurements obtained.

Problem 5

Under normal circumstances, the content of 2DG will naturally decrease over time. Radioactive 2DG is particularly unstable at room temperature(25°C), leading to potential degradation and affecting the reliability of measurement results. Additionally, prolonged experimental durations can result in the elimination of unused 2DG, introducing further errors (before you begin, Step 6).

Potential solution

Ensure that radioactive 2DG is measured immediately after the completion of the experiment to minimize degradation and data inaccuracies. Also, store and handle radioactive 2DG in a low-temperature environment whenever possible to maintain its stability. If feasible, conduct all related experiments consecutively to reduce the time 2DG is subjected to potentially destabilizing conditions. Regularly calibrate and validate measurement instruments to account for any potential discrepancies caused by 2DG instability.
