Comparison of the efficacy of alginate nanoparticles containing Cymbopogon citratus essential oil and citral on melanoma and breast cancer cell lines under normoxic and hypoxic conditions
Authors: Farnaz Karami, Mahmoud Osanloo, Hiva Alipanah, Elham Zarenezhad, Fatemeh Moghimi, Ali Ghanbariasad
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSolid tumors often develop hypoxic regions, leading to aggressive behavior and increased drug resistance.MethodsThe chemical composition of Cymbopogon citratus essential oil (EO) was analyzed using GC-MS. Alginate nanoparticles containing the EO and its primary component, citral, were synthesized via the ionic gelation method. Encapsulation was confirmed using ATR-FTIR analysis. The anticancer efficacy of C. citratus EO, citral, and their respective alginate nanoparticles was evaluated under normoxic (21% oxygen) and hypoxic (1% oxygen) conditions on breast cancer (MDA-MB-231) and melanoma (A-375) cell lines. Additionally, qPCR and flow cytometry were used to assess apoptosis gene expression ratios (Bax/Bcl-2) and levels of apoptosis.ResultsCitral (80.98%) was identified as the major component of the EO. Alginate nanoparticles containing C. citratus EO and citral (C. citratus-AlgNPs and citral-AlgNPs) were synthesized with particle sizes of 195 ± 4 nm and 222 ± 9 nm, and zeta potentials of -22 ± 3 mV and − 17 ± 1 mV, respectively. Both samples demonstrated significantly greater efficacy under hypoxic conditions. Citral and C. citratus-AlgNPs had IC50 values of 27 (19–39) µg/mL and 25 (4-147) µg/mL, respectively, against MDA-MB-231 and A-375 cells. Flow cytometry showed increased apoptosis under hypoxic conditions, with the highest rates observed for citral-AlgNPs and C. citratus-AlgNPs (84 ± 5 and 92 ± 5% in MDA-MB-231 and A-375 cells, respectively).ConclusionThis study demonstrates that alginate nanoparticles enhance the anticancer activity of C. citratus-AlgNPs and citral, particularly under hypoxic conditions, highlighting their potential for hypoxia-targeted cancer therapies.

Introduction

Cancer remains a leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for approximately one in four global deaths [1]. Chemotherapy, a well-established treatment modality, faces significant challenges, particularly drug resistance [2, 3]. Drug resistance in tumors is complex and can be attributed to inadequate pharmacokinetic properties, intrinsic tumor cell characteristics, and the tumor microenvironment [4]. A crucial element of the tumor microenvironment is hypoxia, which results from rapid tumor growth and insufficient blood supply [5]. As tumors outgrow their blood vessels, oxygen levels drop from a normal range of 2–9% to hypoxic levels below 2% [6, 7]. This oxygen deficiency arises from an imbalance between increased oxygen consumption and inadequate supply [8, 9].

Hypoxia not only contributes to drug resistance but also makes tumor cells more aggressive, worsening patient outcomes. It triggers gene expression and protein production changes, impacting various cellular functions [10, 11]. Therefore, studying anticancer treatments under hypoxic conditions has become increasingly important [12, 13]. For instance, research on rapamycin’s effects on cervical cancer cells under hypoxia showed that rapamycin increased the expression of genes related to cell death (apoptosis) and cell recycling (autophagy). Notably, rapamycin induced a higher rate of cell death via autophagy in hypoxic conditions compared to normal oxygen levels, suggesting its potential as a more effective treatment in hypoxic environments typical of solid tumors [14].

Further studies have demonstrated that hypoxia can either enhance or diminish drug effectiveness. For example, a 2013 study evaluated the cytotoxic activity of 19 drugs on various cancer cell lines at different oxygen levels. It found that some drugs, like Sorafenib and Irinotecan, were more effective in high-oxygen environments, while others, like Cisplatin and Mitomycin C, were more potent under hypoxic conditions [15, 16].

The challenges posed by drug resistance have led to renewed interest in exploring alternative therapies, such as medicinal plants [17, 18]. Essential oils (EOs) are volatile compounds from aromatic plants with numerous biological properties, including antioxidative, antibacterial, and anticancer effects [19, 20]. Cymbopogon citratus, from the Poaceae family, has demonstrated notable antitumor properties and is recognized as a promising herbal therapy for reducing resistance to prostate cancer treatments [21, 22]. Citral, a monoterpene aldehyde and major compound in C. citratus EO, possesses various biological effects, including anticancer, antioxidant, and antibacterial activities [23, 24]. Citral has also shown antitumor effects in mouse models of breast cancer [25].

Challenges remain despite the advantages of using EOs and their main components, such as reduced side effects. Enhancing their efficacy and water solubility is crucial. Loading EOs or herbal compounds into nanoparticles is a promising approach to address these issues [26]. Alginate nanoparticles are a promising platform for cancer drug delivery due to their biocompatibility, biodegradability, and ability to encapsulate and release drugs in a controlled manner [27, 28].

This study aims to compare the efficacy of C. citratus EO, citral, and alginate nanoparticles containing these compounds on breast and melanoma cells under normoxic and hypoxic conditions. The process of apoptosis was investigated to assess the treatments’ effectiveness. The balance between pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic factors determines whether a cancer cell undergoes apoptosis or resists treatment. Specifically, the expression levels of the pro-apoptotic gene Bax and the anti-apoptotic gene BCL-2 using qPCR were examined to assess how these treatments modulate these critical genes. The flow cytometry analysis was also used to understand cell death mechanisms following treatment. Combining gene expression studies with direct analysis of cell death mechanisms, this dual approach provides a comprehensive understanding of how these treatments induce apoptosis under normoxic and hypoxic conditions.

Materials

Sodium alginate powder, calcium chloride, DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl), MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide), and citral were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). C. citratus EO was obtained from Giahessence (Gorgan, Iran). Breast cancer cell line (MDA-MB-231) and melanoma cell line (A-375) were sourced from the Pasteur Institute of Iran. RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis kits were obtained from Yekta Tajhiz Azma Company (Tehran, Iran). A qPCR master mix was purchased from Amplicon (Denmark). Primers were acquired from Bioneer (Korea).

Methods

C. citratus EO analysis by GC-MS

The chemical composition of C. citratus EO was analyzed using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS). Agilent 6890 gas chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA) equipped with a 30 m x 0.25 mm x 0.25 μm 5BPX column was employed for separation. The diluted EO sample (1 µL in n-hexane) was injected into the GC/MS system. A temperature program starting at 50 °C (held for 5 min) with a 3 °C/min ramp to 240 °C, followed by a 15 °C/min ramp to a final temperature of 300 °C (held for 3 min) was used for separation. Helium served as the carrier gas (0.5 mL/min), and the injection port temperature was maintained at 250 °C with a 1:35 split ratio. Agilent 5973 mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA) operated in EI mode with an ionization voltage of 70 eV and a scan range of 40–500 m/z [26]. Component identification was achieved by comparing acquired spectra with reference databases and library information [29].

Preparation and characterization of nanoparticles

To prepare nanoparticles, 10 µL of EO or citral was initially mixed with 12.5 µL of tween 20 at 2000 rpm under ambient conditions. A 2.5 mL sodium alginate solution (0.5% w/v) was added dropwise, followed by 3 min of continuous stirring. Subsequently, 2.5 mL of calcium chloride solution 0.1% w/v was added, and the mixture was stirred for 40 min. The prepared nanoparticles were named C. citratus-AlgNPs and citral-AlgNPs, respectively. Additionally, alginate nanoparticles prepared according to the same protocol (without adding EO or citral) were designated as AlgNPs [30].

The prepared samples’ size, particle size distribution, and Zeta potential (AlgNPs, C. citratus-AlgNPs, and citral-AlgNPs) were analyzed using a Dynamic Light Scattering apparatus (K-one Nano Ltd, Seoul, South Korea). The solutions were poured into a quartz cell and situated in the hole of the apparatus for analysis.

Morphology of the samples was investigated using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). One drop of each sample was impregnated on a copper grid (200-mesh carbon-coated). Next, it was stained with phosphotungstic acid (2%) solution, dried at room temperature, and subjected to a Philips CM30 TEM microscope (Philips, Eindhoven, Netherlands).

The Attenuated Total Reflectance-Fourier Transform InfraRed Spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) technique was used to investigate the loading of the EO and citral in the nanoparticles. Spectra of C. citratus EO, citral, AlgNPs, C. citratus-AlgNPs, and citral-AlgNPs were recorded at 450–4000 cm− 1 using Tensor II spectrometer (Bruker, Ettlingen, Germany).

Investigation of anticancer effects of the samples

MDA-MB-231 and A-375 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% carbon dioxide. Then, 10.000 cells/well were added to the 96-well plates and incubated for 24 to reach 80%. Next, media were replaced with 50 µl/well of the fresh supplemented medium cell culture. After that, 50 µl/well of serial dilutions of samples (C. citratus EO, citral, C. citratus-AlgNPs, and citral-AlgNPs dissolved in PBS containing 0.5% DMSO) in a concentration of (3.9–1000 µg/mL) were added. PBS containing 0.5% DMSO and AlgNPs (50 µl/well) were used as control and negative control groups.

The treated plates were then incubated for 24 h at normoxic (74% N2, 5% CO2, and 21% O2) or hypoxic (94% N2, 5% CO2, and 1% O2) conditions. After that, liquid media were replaced with 50 µl/well of MTT solution (0.5 mg/ml in RPMI) and incubated for 4 h. Next, 100 µl/well of DMSO was added to dissolve formazan crystals, and optical density was read at 570 nm by a SYNERGY-HTX multimode reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, USA). The cell viability was computed using (absorption of treated cell lines/absorption of control cell lines) × 100. The inhibitory concentration of each sample was calculated by putting the cell viability in different concentrations in the Free version of CalcuSyn software (Biosoft, Cambridge, UK) [30].

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

For RNA extraction, plates treated with samples were incubated after 24 h under normoxic or hypoxic conditions using an RNA extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Next, cDNA was synthesized using a kit per the manufacturer’s protocols (Yekta Tajhiz Azma Company, Tehran, Iran). The qPCR reaction conditions were optimized and set as follows: 95 °C for 10 min (1×), 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 20 s (41×). All qPCR amplifications were performed in triplicate, and primers are listed in Table 1. The device used was a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR machine (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, USA).

Apoptosis evaluation by flow cytometry test

Early and late apoptotic cell distributions and healthy populations of cells were investigated by BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, USA). The apoptotic capability of the C. citratus EO, citral, C. citratus-AlgNPs, and citral-AlgNPs at normoxic and hypoxic conditions was studied by staining the treated cells with Annexin V-FTIC and Propidium Iodide (PI) by using MabTag FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit with PI according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The results were analyzed using FlowJo software version 7.6.1 (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, USA).

Statistical analyses

All tests were performed in triplicate; the results were expressed as mean ± Standard Deviation. CalcuSyn software (free version, BIOSOFT, UK) was used to calculate the IC50 values of the samples. For the comparison of IC50 values, the upper and lower confidence limits were compared against each other. Overlap between the values indicated no significant difference (P > 0.05). One-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test with a P value of 0.05, was used to compare the flow cytometry results. SPSS software version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, USA) was used for this analysis. Susceptibility was used to compare the results of the qPCR analysis. This ratio is obtained by dividing the apoptotic gene (Bax) expression by the anti-apoptotic gene (Bcl-2) expression. Bax/Bcl-2 > 1.00 indicates cancer-sensitive cells. + low sensitivity (1–2), ++ mild sensitivity (2–3), +++ high sensitivity (3–6), ++++ indicates very high sensitivity (6< ) [30].

ATR-FTIR spectra

The ATR-FTIR spectra of C. citratus EO, citral, AlgNPs, C. citratus-AlgNPs, and citral-AlgNPs are illustrated in Fig. 4A-E.

ATR-FTIR spectrum of C. citratus EO showed the band 3485 cm− 1 corresponded to OH stretching vibration of phenolic compounds. The medium bands at 2967, 2915, and 2856 cm− 1 presented stretching vibration of –CH due to sp3 hybridization, and the band at 1717 cm− 1 attributed to the carbonyl group. The characteristic bands at 1672 and 1441 cm− 1 are related to C = C, the peak at 1376 cm− 1 can be allocated to the aromatic ring of phenolic moiety in the EO compound, and the peak at around 1120 cm− 1 is attributed to C-O bending vibration.

ATR-FTIR spectrum of citral displayed the band at 2967, 2915, and 3856 cm − 1, which can be attributed to the stretching vibration of CH in sp2 groups due to alkene structures. The bands at 2967, 2915, and 2856 cm− 1 can be attributed to the stretching vibration of CH in sp3 groups, and the peak at 1671 cm− 1 corresponded to the stretching vibration of C = O. The 1631 and 1441 cm− 1 peaks correspond to unsaturation C = C in the citral.

ATR-FTIR spectrum of AlgNPs displayed a broad peak between 3300 and 3700 cm− 1, attributed to the stretching vibration of hydroxyl groups in water, tween 20, and alginate. The peaks at 2919 and 2853 cm− 1 can be attributed to the stretching vibration of CH. The bands at 2361 and 2336 cm− 1 can be related to CO2. The characteristic band at 1732 cm− 1 related to C = O in tween 20. The stretching vibration at 1578 and 1361 cm− 1 corresponded to the symmetric and asymmetric carbonyl groups due to monovalent ions (sodium) displacing the proton. These peaks confirmed an increase in hydrogen bonding. The vibration at 1080 cm− 1 can be attributed to the presence of C-O groups.

ATR-FTIR spectrum of C. citratus-AlgNPs displayed the broadband at about 3238–3761 cm− 1, related to OH groups, due to hydrogen bonding in EO, alginate, and water. The stretching vibration at 2923 and 2856 cm− 1 was attributed to C-H, and the vibration at 1730 was related to the carbonyl group in EO, tween 20, and alginate. The 1590 and 1362 cm− 1 vibration was allocated to symmetric and asymmetric C = O groups. The characteristic solid band at 1092 cm− 1 is related to the reaction between calcium ion and carboxyl (CO-Ca-CO group structure), which increased C-O vibration. This characteristic band demonstrated ionic crosslinking. The presence of other bands in EO and AlgNPs demonstrated the successful loading of EO in the prepared AlgNPs.

ATR-FTIR spectrum of citral-AlgNPs showed broadband between 3238 and 3700 cm− 1 related to OH groups, according to hydrogen bonding in alginate, EO, and water. The stretching vibration at 2918 and 2862 cm− 1 is attributed to C-H stretching vibration. The band at 1732 cm− 1 corresponded to the stretching vibration of the carbonyl group in EO, tween 20, and alginate. The vibrations at 1594 and 1363 cm− 1 were allocated to symmetric and asymmetric carbonyl groups. The strong peak at 1094 cm− 1 corresponds to the reaction between calcium ion and carboxyl (CO-Ca-CO group structure), which increased C-O vibration; this vibration confirmed ionic crosslinking. Other peaks in citral and AlgNPs confirmed the successful loading of citral in the prepared citral-AlgNPs.

The absorption frequency of the characteristic band at 1092 and 1094 cm− 1 in C. citratus-AlgNPs and citral-AlgNPs, respectively, showed a significant increase compared with AlgNPs (1080 cm− 1), which confirmed ionic crosslinking in alginate nanoparticles containing C. citratus EO and citral.

Anticancer effects of samples

MTT results and obtained IC50 values of samples against MDA-MB-231 cells under normoxic and hypoxic conditions are shown in Fig. 5; Table 3. C. citratus-AlgNPs and citral-AlgNP with less than 10% cell viability at 1000 µg/mL showed the best efficacy (P < 0.05) under normoxic conditions. Interestingly, after treating the cells with C. citratus-AlgNPs in hypoxic conditions, cell viability reached ~ 0. However, the efficacy of citral in lower concentrations (32 and 63 µg/mL) under hypoxic conditions was better than other samples. For this reason, it significantly (P < 0.05) showed the best efficacy with an IC50 of 27 (19–39) µg/mL under normoxic and hypoxic conditions.

MTT results and obtained IC50 values of samples against A-375 cells under normoxic and hypoxic conditions are shown in Fig. 6; Table 3. Interestingly, after treating the cells with C. citratus-AlgNPs and citral-AlgNP in normoxic conditions, cell viability reached ~ 0. In the hypoxic condition, all the samples showed about 90% efficacy at 1000 µg/mL. The obtained IC50 C. citratus-AlgNPs (25 (4-147) µg/mL) is much better than other samples, although this difference is not statistically significant with other samples (P > 0.05).

Percent of apoptotic cells

Figures 7, 8, 9 and 10 illustrate the percentage of viable (Annexin V- PI-), necrotic (Annexin V- PI+), early apoptotic (Annexin V + PI-), and late apoptotic (Annexin V + PI+) MDA-MB-231 and A-375 cells after treatment with various samples under normoxic and hypoxic conditions. The percentage of apoptotic cells increased under hypoxic conditions compared to normoxic conditions. The highest percentages of apoptotic cells were observed in MDA-MB-231 and A-375 cells treated with citral-AlgNPs (84 ± 5%) and C. citratus-AlgNPs (92 ± 5%), respectively.

In MDA-MB-231 cells, treatment with citral-AlgNPs under hypoxic conditions resulted in significantly more apoptotic cells (P < 0.05) than any other treatment, except for C. citratus-AlgNPs (79 ± 4%) under hypoxic conditions, where no significant difference was observed (P > 0.05). In A-375 cells, treatment with C. citratus-AlgNPs under hypoxic conditions led to significantly more apoptotic cells (P < 0.05) than all other samples under normoxic and hypoxic conditions.

Discussions

GC-MS analysis is essential for identifying and quantifying compounds within complex mixtures like essential oils. It enables the precise separation and detection of individual components, offering detailed insights into their chemical composition [31, 32]. In this study, GC-MS analysis identified citral as the major constituent of C. citratus EO. Both C. citratus EO and its primary component, citral, have been previously recognized in the literature for their anticancer properties, and they were thus utilized in this study as anticancer agents. For example, C. citratus EO has demonstrated cytotoxicity against MCF-7 breast cancer cells [33], and studies have shown that it can induce apoptosis through specific pathways [34, 35]. Citral, in particular, has shown potential in breast cancer treatment as a natural ALDH1A inhibitor, blocking the enzyme aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 A, which plays a role in breaking down toxins and chemicals [36, 37]. In another study, citral was identified as the most effective inhibitor of ALDH1A3 in MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-468, and SKBR3 breast cancer cells out of twelve drugs tested [38]. Additionally, research on melanoma mouse cell lines found that citral’s anticancer effects involve inducing oxidative stress, reducing nitric oxide levels, and disrupting cell proliferation pathways [39].

Polymer nanoparticles, such as alginate nanoparticles, are promising drug delivery systems for solid cancers, particularly under hypoxic conditions, which often affect the performance of traditional anticancer drugs [40, 41]. In this study, we explored the anticancer properties of C. citratus EO, citral, and alginate nanoparticles loaded with them (C. citratus-AlgNPs and citral-AlgNPs) against two aggressive cancer cell lines, MDA-MB-231 (breast cancer) and A-375 (melanoma). Notably, the anticancer efficacy of all samples was enhanced under hypoxic conditions compared to normoxic conditions. Results from three different assays—MTT assay, qPCR sensitivity test, and flow cytometry—revealed varying effects on cancer cell types.

The MTT assay, which measures cellular metabolic activity, showed that citral had the highest efficacy in inhibiting breast cancer cells, with an IC50 of 27 (19–39) µg/mL. C. citratus-AlgNPs also demonstrated proper efficacy, with an IC50 of 52 (41–67) µg/mL for MDA-MB-231 cells and 25 (4-147) µg/mL for A-375 cells. In the qPCR sensitivity test, which measures the expression ratio of apoptosis-related genes to apoptosis inhibitor genes (Bax/Bcl-2), MDA-MB-231 cells showed the highest sensitivity to citral-AlgNPs (4 stars), while citral and C. citratus-AlgNPs both exhibited 3 stars sensitivity for MDA-MB-231 cells. A-375 cells displayed the highest sensitivity to C. citratus-AlgNPs (4 stars).

Flow cytometry analysis further revealed that 58 ± 3% of breast cancer cells treated with citral underwent apoptosis, whereas 79 ± 4% of cells treated with C. citratus-AlgNPs underwent apoptosis. Moreover, 92 ± 5% of A-375 cells treated with C. citratus-AlgNPs underwent apoptosis. While the high efficacy of citral in the MTT assay indicates a reduction in cellular metabolic activity, the lower apoptosis percentage observed in flow cytometry, coupled with the 3-star sensitivity in the qPCR test for MDA-MB-231 cells, suggests that citral’s efficacy may not solely stem from apoptosis induction. According to existing literature, citral may prevent cell proliferation, which could explain the 58% apoptosis rate observed [42, 43].

In contrast, C. citratus-AlgNPs exhibited superior performance across all three tests, indicating that these nanoparticles effectively induce apoptosis in cancer cells. This suggests that their anticancer effects may be due to direct activation of the apoptosis pathway. These findings highlight the significant potential of C. citratus-AlgNPs as an anticancer treatment. The results also underscore the importance of using multiple assessment methods to comprehensively evaluate anticancer drugs, as each method can provide different insights into a drug’s efficacy and mechanism of action.

Conclusion

This study investigated the anticancer potential of C. citratus EO, its main component citral, and their alginate nanoparticles (AlgNPs) against breast and melanoma cancer cells under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions. The results revealed a significant increase in the efficacy of all tested samples under hypoxic conditions compared to normoxic conditions. Notably, C. citratus-AlgNPs were highly effective in inducing apoptosis, showing strong potential for treating aggressive cancer cell lines. These findings emphasize the importance of considering environmental factors, such as oxygen levels, when evaluating anticancer treatments. Future studies should assess the efficacy of C. citratus-AlgNPs in an animal model, focusing on direct tumor injections, which more accurately replicate the hypoxic microenvironment of tumors.
