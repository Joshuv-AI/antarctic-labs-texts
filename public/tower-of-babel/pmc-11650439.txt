Association between mitophagy and NLRP3 inflammasome in uric acid nephropathy
Authors: Xiao-qian Li, Yong-qing Gu, Yuan-yuan Ling, Mei Wang, Jin Miao, Li Xue, Wei Ji, Jun Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study was recruited to investigate the role of mitophagy in activating NLRP3 inflammasome in the kidney of uric acid (UA) nephropathy (UAN) rats.MethodsThis study developed a uric acid nephropathy (UAN) rat model divided into five groups: Negative control (NC), UAN model (M), UAN + autophagy inhibitor (3-MA), UAN + lysosome inhibitor (CQ), and ROS scavenger (N-acetylcysteine, N). H&E staining assessed renal structure, ROS levels were measured with 2, 7-dichlorofluorescin diacetate, and ELISA measured serum markers (creatinine, UA, cystatin C, NGAL, IL-1β, IL-18). Western blot and qRT-PCR evaluated autophagy and inflammation-related protein (LC3 II/I, p62, Pink1, Parkin, NLRP3, Caspase1, IL-1β) expression. NRK-52E cells treated with uric acid and shRNA were analyzed by western blot.ResultsRenal injury in UAN rats was aggravated by ROS accumulation, which promoted mitophagy and activated the NLRP3 inflammasome. Eliminating ROS reduced mitophagy, inhibited NLRP3 activation, lowered IL-1β and IL-18 levels, and alleviated renal injury. Notably, inhibiting mitophagy increased ROS accumulation, up-regulated NLRP3, Caspase1, and IL-1β expression, further worsening renal injury. In vitro, uric acid treatment of NRK-52E cells altered autophagy-related protein and pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, highlighting the interplay between mitophagy and inflammation in uric acid nephropathy.ConclusionMitophagy influences renal injury in uric acid nephropathy (UAN) by regulating ROS accumulation and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, suggesting that mitophagy may serve as a potential therapeutic target for UAN.

Introduction

As the economy advances and the lifestyle of people changes, the global incidence and prevalence of hyperuricemia (HUA) tend to increase steadily [1]. Based on the findings from two nationally representative cross-sectional surveys in 2015–16 and 2018–19, the estimated prevalence of HUA among Chinese adults was 11.1% [2]. A recent study of USA has also showed that 14.6% of the US population suffered from HUA [3]. Overall, HUA has become a major public health problem worldwide [4].

HUA is defined as persistent serum uric acid (UA) concentrations of > 7 mg/dL (> 420 μmol/L) in men and > 6 mg/dL (> 360 μmol/L) in women [5]. HUA is resulted from overproduction or insufficient excretion of UA and can lead to gout and nephrolithiasis [6]. Moreover, HUA has been implicated as an indicator for diseases such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, cardiovascular disease, and chronic renal disease [7]. Some studies have shown that HUA is closely correlated with kidney diseases [8, 9]. Serum urate levels at a continually high concentration can cause monosodium urate (MSU) crystal deposition in kidney tubules or interstitium, and give rise to kidney disease, named UA nephropathy (UAN) [10]. Studies have demonstrated that inflammation plays a central and direct role in the pathogenesis of UAN [11, 12]. The main pathway leading to the development of inflammation involves the activation of inflammasomes, in which NLRP3 inflammasome is most widely studied [13, 14]. A recent research has stated that the release of mitochondrial contents plays an important role in the assembly and activation of NLRP3 inflammasome [15]. The prevention of mitochondrial stress can be a relevant mechanism, which mediates NLRP3 inhibition in gout [16]. However, the activation mechanism of NLRP3 inflammasome in UAN remains unclear. Indeed, several landmark studies have collectively shown that the autophagy mechanism constitutes a key cellular monitoring system that prevents excessive NLRP3 inflammasome activation in some auto-inflammatory and immune diseases [17–20]. Therefore, we suppose that mitophagy may play a negative regulatory role in NLRP3 inflammasome in UAN. Selective autophagy in mitochondria is essential in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis [21]. The massive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative stress can trigger mitochondrial autophagy to remove dysfunctional mitochondria. However, dysregulation of mitophagy homeostasis also leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and further induces cell death [22].

In this study, UAN rat model was established by intragastric administration of UA and potassium oxyazine solution. Then, 3-methyladenine (3-MA), chloroquine (CQ) and N-acetylcysteine (N) were used to explore the role of mitophagy in the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome in the renal tissues of UAN rats.

Materials and methods

UAN model and treatment

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (n = 30, 180–220 g) were obtained from the Laboratary Animal Center of Nantong University (SCXK Su 2019-0001; Jiangsu, China). The rats were housed under standard conditions of temperature (24 ± 2 °C), humidity (40–70%), and light (12:12-h light/dark cycle), with free access to food and water. After 1 week of acclimatization, the rats were randomly divided into five groups (six rats per group) as follows: negative control (NC) group, UAN model group (M), UAN + 3-MA (autophagy inhibitor) (M9281, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) group, UAN+CQ (lysosome inhibitor) (C6628, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) group, and UAN+N (ROS scavenger) (A7250, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) group. The NC rats were administrated with 0.5% sodium carboxymethylcellulose (CMC-Na) (C5013, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The remaining rats were administrated with the mixture of UA (300 mg/kg/day) (U2625, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and potassium oxonate (1500 mg/kg/day) (237086, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) dissolved in 0.5% CMC-Na (C5013, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 28 consecutive days. This study confirmed the successful establishment of the model through the increased levels of serum creatinine (SCr), uric acid (UA), cystatin C (Cys-C), and neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) [23]. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nantong Hospital to Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine, Nantong Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (S20220501-002).

The drug administration was kept the same pace with the UAN model induction. Briefly, 0.5 mL solution of 3-MA (15 mg/kg/day), CQ (25 mg/kg/day), or N (20 mg/kg/day) was injected into enterocoelia of rats in each group, respectively. All drugs were dissolved in a solvent consisted of 99% sterile saline and 1% dimethyl sulfoxide (D2650, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The rats in the NC and the UAN groups were given equal volume of vehicle solution through intraperitoneal injection. All animals were given drugs for 28 consecutive days.

After last treatment, rats were fasted for 12 h. Then, all rats were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection) (P3761, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Next, the blood sample was collected and centrifuged at 3000×g. After 10 min, the serum was separated and subject to enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. In addition, renal tissue was collected for hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining and Western blot analysis, with the Western blot experiments using renal medulla tissue. The serum and renal tissue were tested directly or stored at −80 °C for later use.

Cell culture

In the cell experiment, male Sprague-Dawley rat renal tubular epithelial cells (NRK-52E, ATCC, Manassas, USA) were initially selected. The cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, BRL, Waltham, MA, USA) medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 100 U/mL of penicillin and streptomycin in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2 until reaching 80% confluence. Then, the cells were divided into four groups for treatment: The control group (control group) underwent no treatment; the UA group was treated with uric acid (UA, 800 μM) for 24 h to assess the effect of uric acid on the cells; the UA+sh-NC group received a negative shRNA transfection followed by treatment with UA (800 μM) [12, 24] for 24 h as a transfection control; and the UA+sh-Pink1 group was transfected with Pink1 shRNA and then treated with UA (800 μM) for 24 h to investigate the role of Pink1 in uric acid treatment. Through these different treatment groups, a pathological model of NRK-52E cells was established to evaluate the effects of uric acid and its related factors on the cells.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The levels of serum creatinine (SCr) (YH-SCr-ELISA, Shanghai Yunhan Corp., Shanghai, China), UA (YH-UA-ELISA, Shanghai Yunhan Corp, Shanghai, China), cystatin-C (Cys-C) (YH-CysC-ELISA, Shanghai Yunhan Corp, Shanghai, China), neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL) (YH-NGAL-ELISA, Shanghai Yunhan Corp, Shanghai, China), interleukin (IL)-1β (YH-IL1B-ELISA, Shanghai Yunhan Corp, Shanghai, China), and IL-18 (YH-IL18-ELISA, Shanghai Yunhan Corp, Shanghai, China) were detected using ELISA kits following the manufacturer instructions.

DCFH-DA (2, 7-Dichlorofluorescin Diacetate) probe detection

Half of the left renal tissues in each group were immediately put into precooled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). These tissues were cut into small pieces and then ground on ice using a mechanical grinder to ensure the tissues were thoroughly broken down. The ground tissue was filtered through a cell strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension. The collected cells were then resuspended in diluted DCFH-DA (2,7-Dichlorofluorescin Diacetate) (D6883, Shanghai Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) probe for fluorescence detection.

Histomorphology

The renal tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (P6148, Shanghai Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) for 30 min after washing with PBS for three times. Next, the tissues were dehydrated with a series of graded ethanol (E7023, Shanghai Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China), infiltrated with xylene (X7012, Shanghai Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China), and then embedded in paraffin before being cut into 5-μm thick sections. The sections were given hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining following the routine staining procedure. Finally, a light microscope was utilized to capture the images.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Trizol (T9424, Shanghai Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) solution was employed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration was detected with Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Waltham, MA, USA). RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA following a protocol provided by RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (K1622, Shanghai Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). The instrument was programmed with the following PCR conditions: 5 s of denaturation at 95 °C and 34 s of amplification at 60 °C for 40 cycles. All reactions were repeated three times and normalized according to the housekeeping gene glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used are listed in Table 1.

Western blot

We used RIPA lysis buffer (R0278, Shanghai Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) containing the protein phosphatase inhibitor complex (4693132001, Roche, Mannheim, Germany) and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) (P7626, Shanghai Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) to lyse the kidney medulla tissue samples. The concentration of the protein extracts was determined using the BCA protein assay (C503021-0050, Beyotime Biotechnology Co., LTD, Shanghai, China). The protein sample (20 μg) was separated with sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel (C631100-0100, Shanghai Sangong Bioengineering Co., LTD, Shanghai, China) and transferred to the polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (IPVH00010, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Subsequently, the membrane was sealed in Tris-buffered brine with 5% skimmed milk powder at room temperature for 1 h. Then, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against the following proteins: microtubule-associated protein LC3 II/I (4108S, 1:200, Cell signaling, Danvers, MA, USA), p62 (18420-1-AP, 1:400, Proteintech, Illinois, USA), Parkin (14060-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech, Illinois, USA), Pink1 (ab75487, 1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), NLRP3 (ab214185, 1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), Caspase1 (22915-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech, USA), IL-1β (ab9722, 1:1000, Abcam, UK), and GAPDH (ab181602, 1:10000, Abcam, UK). The membrane was then incubated at room temperature with appropriate horseradish peroxidase conjugate goat anti-rabbit (7074S, 1:1000, Cell signaling, Danvers, MA, USA) or anti-mouse IgG (7076S, 1:1000, Cell signaling, Danvers, MA, USA) secondary antibody for 1 h. Lastly, protein blots were visualized using an ECL kit (P0018M-2, Beyotime Biotechnology Co., LTD, Shanghai, China).

Immunofluorescence

In the immunofluorescence experiment, rat kidney tissues from each experimental group were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution at room temperature for 10–15 min. After fixation, tissues were washed three times with PBS for 5 min each. The samples were then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h to prevent nonspecific binding. Specific primary antibodies were added, diluted according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, samples were washed three times with PBS for 5 min each. Fluorescent secondary antibodies were added: (A-11008, A-11001, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and incubated for 1 h in the dark. Next, DAPI dye (1 μg/mL) was used to stain the nuclei for 5 min in the dark. After staining, samples were mounted with an anti-fade medium and observed using a fluorescence or confocal microscope.

Statistical analysis

All data were demonstrated as mean ± standard deviation (SD). GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA) was employed for data analysis. One-way ANOVA was adopted for comparisons between two or among more groups. p < 0.05 indicated a statistical significance.

Results

Mitophagy inhibitors CQ and 3-MA aggravate uric acid nephropathy in rats

A rat uric acid nephropathy (UAN) model was established by continuous administration with UA and potassium oxide for 28 days first to investigate the impact of mitophagy and ROS homeostasis on UAN. As shown in Figure 1, ROS accumulated more in renal tissues of UAN rats compared with the NC group, and the levels of ROS were significantly reduced after the treatment of ROS scavenger N. In contrast, administration of autophagy inhibitors CQ and 3-MA increased the accumulation of ROS.

Effect of mitophagy inhibition on the histopathology of UAN

Histopathological changes of the renal tissues showed abnormalities in the UAN group compared to the NC group. In the NC group, the renal tissue structure was complete and clear, no obvious urate crystals were observed, and the renal interstitium was normal, with few inflammatory cells. In the UAN group, renal tubules were dilated, and the lumen was arranged in a disorderly and irregular manner; the renal interstitium was infiltrated by a large number of inflammatory cells, accompanied by urate crystal deposition, resulting in pathological kidney injury. Renal tubule dilation was more obvious and the injury was more serious in the mitophagy inhibition groups. Notably, the renal tubule dilatation was reduced and the injury was improved when ROS scavenger was given (Figure 3(A)–(E)).

Inhibition of mitophagy promotes the release of inflammatory factors in UAN

Local inflammatory response in the kidney is a pathological feature of UAN [25]. Therefore, the effect of autophagy on UAN inflammatory response was further explored by detecting the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18. To be specific, the levels of IL-1β and IL-18 in the UAN group were significantly higher than those in the NC group. Furthermore, the levels of inflammatory cytokines were further increased after inhibiting autophagy and lysosomal function. However, these indicators were significantly decreased after ROS depletion (Figure 4).

Changes of mitophagy-associated and NLRP3 inflammasome pathway mRNA levels in UAN

Mitophagy homeostasis is essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis and stable renal function [26]. Hence, we further assessed the mRNA expression of genes related to mitophagy-related and the NLRP3 pathway. Specifically, in the kidneys of rats with UAN, the mRNA expression of LC3 II/I, Pink1, and Parkin significantly increased, while the expression of p62 decreased. Simultaneously, the mRNA expression of NLRP3 pathway-related genes, including Caspase1, NLRP3, and IL-1β, also significantly increased. In contrast, after the depletion of ROS, the levels of LC3 II/I, Pink1, and Parkin mRNA were inhibited, while the expression of p62 increased. Additionally, the mRNA levels of Caspase1, NLRP3, and IL-1β also significantly decreased (Figure 5(A–B)).

Crosstalk between mitophagy and NLRP3 inflammasome pathway in UAN

The expression of mitophagy-related and NLRP3 pathway proteins in renal tissues was evaluated by Western blot to assess the impact of mitophagy on inflammasome activation. As shown in Figure 6(A–B), the protein expression of LC3 II/I, Pink1, and Parkin significantly increased in the kidneys of rats with UAN, while the expression of p62 decreased. Additionally, UAN significantly increased the protein levels of Caspase1, NLRP3, and IL-1β. Inhibiting mitophagy significantly increased the levels of Caspase1, NLRP3, and IL-1β, whereas the depletion of ROS reversed the UAN-induced increase in inflammasome-related proteins.

Immunofluorescence expression of Pink1 and Parkin in renal tissue of rats with UAN

Immunofluorescence analysis showed that the fluorescence expression of Pink1 in the uric acid model group (M group) was significantly lower than that in the control group (NC group) (Figure 7(A)). Additionally, the expression of Pink1 in the CQ and 3-MA groups was also significantly lower than that in the M group, while the expression level in the N group was low but did not reach statistical significance. For Parkin, the fluorescence expression in the M group was similarly significantly lower than that in the NC group (Figure 7(B)). The expression of Parkin in the CQ and 3-MA groups was also significantly lower than that in the M group, whereas the N group exhibited moderate expression levels. These results indicate that uric acid nephropathy can significantly inhibit the expression levels of Pink1 and Parkin in rat renal tissues.

Effects of Pink1 inhibitor on autophagy-related proteins and inflammatory factors in NRK-52E cells induced by UAN

In the Western blot analysis (Figure 8), the UA group showed significantly elevated protein expression levels of LC3 II/I, Pink1, and Parkin compared to the control group. Conversely, the expression level of p62 was significantly decreased. Compared to the UA+sh-NC group, the UA+sh-Pink1 group, which was transfected with Pink1 shRNA, exhibited a significant reduction in the expression levels of LC3 II/I, Pink1, and Parkin, while the expression level of p62 was significantly increased. Additionally, the Western blot analysis in the UA group revealed significantly higher protein expression levels of Caspase1, NLRP3, and IL-1β compared to the control group, indicating that uric acid-induced nephropathy may enhance inflammatory responses. When compared to the UA+sh-NC group, the UA+sh-Pink1 group showed significantly lower protein expression levels of Caspase1, NLRP3, and IL-1β. These results suggest that uric acid-induced nephropathy significantly affects the expression of autophagy-related proteins in NRK-52E cells, highlighting the potential role of autophagy in UAN. Inhibition of autophagy may play an important role in UAN by influencing the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome.

Discussion

The deposition of MSU crystals in the kidneys is considered a major factor in triggering renal inflammation. MSU crystals stimulate the kidneys to release proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, ROS, prostaglandin E2, and lysosomal enzymes, leading to an inflammatory response [27]. Recent studies have shown that NLRP3, a pattern recognition receptor of the NLR family, acts as a critical sensor of tissue damage. By detecting danger signals, NLRP3 interacts with the adaptor protein ASC and recruits pro-Caspase1 to form the NLRP3 inflammasome complex [28, 29]. This complex further activates pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18, inducing a proinflammatory response. However, excessive activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome has been associated with several autoinflammatory and autoimmune diseases, such as diabetes, neurodegenerative diseases, and cardiovascular diseases [30]. Previous studies have indicated that activation of the NLRP3 inflammasomes is closely related to the inflammatory response in UAN [13, 14, 31]. Against this background, mitochondria play a central role in the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, as they are the primary intracellular source of ROS. Pathogen-associated molecular patterns or damage-associated molecular patterns, such as MSU crystals, can cause mitochondrial damage, leading to increased mitochondrial ROS production and the release of mitochondrial DNA, which promotes the assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome [32, 33]. In our study, increased levels of mitophagy markers LC3 II/I, Pink1, and Parkin in the renal tissue of UAN rats, along with a decrease in p62 levels, suggest an enhancement of mitophagy activity. This finding aligns with other studies that demonstrate mitophagy as a negative regulator of the NLRP3 inflammasome [34, 35].

Furthermore, the use of ROS scavengers significantly reduced both ROS levels and the expression of mitophagy-related factors, supporting the idea that ROS accumulation acts as an upstream trigger of mitophagy in UAN [33]. Additionally, the cell experiments further corroborate this perspective. NRK-52E cells showed significant changes in autophagy-related protein expression following uric acid treatment, suggesting that autophagy may play an essential regulatory role in UAN. The inhibitors 3-MA and CQ inhibit mitophagy at different stages of the autophagy pathway by blocking PI3K activity and lysosomal function, respectively, leading to ROS accumulation in renal tissue and exacerbating the inflammatory response. We propose that the inhibition of mitophagy reduces ROS clearance, thereby enhancing NLRP3 inflammasome activation and worsening the inflammatory response. Previous studies have mainly focused on the effects of autophagy inhibition or activation on inflammation and pyroptosis. For example, some studies have explored the role of Cathepsin B (CTSB) in pro-inflammatory processes [36], while another study has analyzed in detail how the AMPK, ERK, and JNK pathways regulate autophagy [37].

Overall, these findings provide new insights into targeting mitophagy as a therapeutic strategy for UAN. While this study reveals the potential role of mitophagy in regulating the NLRP3 inflammasome and inflammatory response, further investigation is needed to elucidate the specific relationship between mitophagy and ROS accumulation and its effect on NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Future studies will explore how these findings can be applied in clinical settings to validate the role of mitophagy as an essential regulator of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway in UAN.

Conclusions

This study explored mitophagy’s role in activating the NLRP3 inflammasome in UAN. Using a UAN rat model with inhibitors such as 3-MA, CQ, and N-acetylcysteine, results showed that mitophagy inhibition leads to ROS buildup, promotes NLRP3 activation, and worsens renal injury, while ROS scavenging alleviates these effects. These findings highlight mitophagy as a potential target for managing inflammation in UAN and related kidney diseases.
