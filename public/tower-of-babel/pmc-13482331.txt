Aliquat IL336 vs. Cyphos IL101 extractants for the solvent extraction of heavy metals from zinc plants residues
Authors: Fatemeh Najafi, Leila Dolatyari, Hassan Shayani-Jam, Mohammad Reza Yaftian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydrometallurgical zinc production generates residues containing valuable but environmentally hazardous metals like cadmium, zinc, cobalt and nickel. This study compares two ionic liquid extractants, Aliquat IL336 and Cyphos IL101, for the recovery of cadmium, zinc, cobalt and nickel from chloride solutions. Dichloromethane was used as the diluent. The metals are extracted via an anion-exchange mechanism as ion-pairs of the form [R⁺]2[MCl2]2– (M = Cd or Zn), where R⁺ is the cation of the ionic liquid extractant. Both extractants showed high selectivity and efficiency for Cd(II) and Zn(II) over Co(II) and Ni(II), which was attributed to the greater stability of their chloro-complexes. Using HCl as the chloride source yielded higher extraction percentages for Cd(II) and Zn(II) with respect to NaCl. The difference was explained by the hydration energies of H⁺ and Na⁺. Efficient back-extraction was achieved using sodium hydroxide for cadmium and ammonia for zinc. Different behavior was observed for the temperature dependency of cadmium and zinc ions by the studied extractants. The results demonstrate the potential of these methods for the separation and recovery of metals from industrial residues.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56637-9.Subject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences

Introduction

The hydrometallurgical production of zinc typically involves acidic leaching of raw materials, followed by refining steps to purify the solution into a zinc-rich electrolyte for electrolysis. The refining processes generate solid residues containing heavy metals, notably cadmium, zinc, cobalt, and nickel. The significant metal content within these residues provides both economic and environmental incentives for developing more efficient strategies to separate and recover these metals. Leakage of these toxic metals from stored residues (e.g. by exposure to rainwater) pollutes soil, surface water, and groundwater. Thus, reclaiming these metals not only mitigates environmental pollution risks but also utilizes them as valuable secondary resources1.

Extensive research has been conducted on recovering metals from industrial residues2. Among the techniques utilized for such purpose, solvent extraction techniques are the most widely employed method. Despite the limitations, they remain a practical and advantageous choice for industrial applications due to their efficiency and scalability3–5. Extractants play a crucial role in solvent extraction. They transfer metal species from the aqueous phase to the organic phase and directly affect both efficiency and selectivity. Extractants are mainly classified as acidic, basic, ion pairing and solvating agents. The selection of an appropriate extractant depends on the nature of target species found primary in the aqueous phase (e.g., cationic, anionic, or ion-pair).

Ionic liquids (ILs), composed of organic cations and inorganic anions, are a unique class of materials with low melting points, remaining liquid at room temperature6. Although their eco-friendly credentials are debated to some extent7, ILs exhibit negligible vapor pressure. This minimizes atmospheric release and pollution, leading to their categorization as “green solvents"8. Offering significant advantages over traditional solvents, ILs are reusable and non-volatile. These properties significantly reduce environmental risks and enhance efficiency in both fundamental research and industrial processes. The combination of low environmental impact and functional adaptability positions them as pivotal tools in sustainable chemistry9.

Ionic liquids are also widely used as extractants in solvent extraction of metals10,11 and organic compounds12. Ionic liquids intervene in the extraction of chemical species via its role as a solvating agent13 or as an anion exchanger14. In the latter case, the ionic liquid contains an exchangeable anion (such as Cl–) that can be replaced by anionic metal complexes from the aqueous phase. However, some of the extraction studies involving ionic liquids are those in which the ILs have been used as diluent rather than extractant15.

Extraction-separation of heavy metals (cadmium, zinc, cobalt, and nickel) using ionic liquids has been extensively reported. Wassink et al. studied the separation of cadmium and zinc as their anionic chloro-complexes from cobalt and nickel by using Aliquat IL336 dissolved in mixed aromatic-aliphatic diluent16. The proposed system allowed possible the separation of cadmium(II) and zinc(II), from cobalt(II) and Ni(II). The analysis of the extraction data showed the extracted species are [R3R’N+][MCl42−], where M is cadmium and zinc ions, and R3R’N+Cl− is Aliquat IL336 (trioctylmethylammonium chloride). This study did not report on the application of the proposed method for the separation of target ions in real samples. Smirnova et al. employed the undiluted ionic liquids tetraoctylammonium N-lauroyl sarcosinate and trioctylmethylammonium salicylate for the extraction-separation of cadmium, zinc, cobalt, and lead17. This study confirms the efficiency of the investigated ionic liquids for the extraction of lead(II), copper(II) and cadmium(II) from alkaline solutions. The extraction process for the recovery of cobalt as its anionic chloro-complexes from acidic nickel leach solutions containing chloride by Cyphos IL101 (triethyltetradecylphosphonium chloride) in the industrial diluent ShellSol A150 demonstrated the suitability of such strategy for the designed application of this ionic liquid18. Cyphos IL102 (trihexyltetradecylphosphonium bromide) is very similar ionic liquid to Cyphos IL101, in which the chloride counter anion is replaced by bromide ion. The solution of this ionic liquid in toluene was used for the recovery of zinc in chloride solution obtained from zinc-plating mud19. Łukomska et al. have reported the synthesis of ammonium ionic liquids and applied the prepared ionic liquids (ILs) without diluent for the extraction of zinc and cobalt20. The recovery of nickel and cobalt from leach solution of spent lithium-ion batteries by using 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide is described by Zante et al.21. The separation of zinc and cobalt, from nickel as their anionic chloro-complexes by employing the phosphonium ionic liquid Cyphos IL101 without diluent is reported22. Recently the studies concerned on the application of ionic liquids for the extraction-recovery of cobalt and nickel from spent lithium-ion batteries have been reviewed23.

The primary motivation for designing and conducting the studies discussed above stems from the well-established significance of ionic liquids in separation applications. Nevertheless, the synthesized and employed ionic liquids are usually costly and/or their synthesis encountered various technical difficulties. A solution for resolving these disadvantages is the application of the solution of commercially accessible ionic liquids such as Aliquat IL336 and Cyphos IL101 in conventional organic diluents. Swain et al.13 employed a similar approach. They studied the extraction of cadmium from ternary solutions containing zinc and nickel from sulfate solutions by the ionic liquids Aliquat IL336 and Cyphos IL101 dissolved in kerosene. The extracted species were identified as CdSO4·3IL (IL represents Aliquat IL336 or Cyphos IL101) and the metal transfer occurred via a solvation mechanism with formation of [Cyphos IL102]2ZnCl2.

The present study intends to appraise and compare the applicability and performance of the commercially available ionic liquids Aliquat IL336 and Cyphos IL101 dissolved in dichloromethane, for the extraction and separation of cadmium, zinc, cobalt, and nickel from residues generated in hydrometallurgical zinc production plants. While both Aliquat IL336 and Cyphos IL101 have been individually used in metal extraction studies, a direct comparative evaluation of these extractants under identical conditions has not been previously reported. The novelty of this study lies in the first direct head-to-head comparison of Aliquat IL336 and Cyphos IL101 under strictly identical conditions, including the same diluent (dichloromethane), chloride source, metal concentrations, and a selective two-step back-extraction strategy. Furthermore, the applicability of both extractants was validated using real residues from a hydrometallurgical zinc production plant.

Experimental

Chemicals and reagents

The extractants used in this study were trioctylmethylammonium chloride (Aliquat IL336, ≥ 98%, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and trihexyltetradecylphosphonium chloride (Cyphos IL101, ≥ 95%, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Dichloromethane served as the diluent throughout the study. Solutions of the studied metal ions were prepared using the following salts: cadmium(II) nitrate hexahydrate (> 98%, Sigma-Aldrich, Switzerland), zinc(II) nitrate tetrahydrate (≥ 98%, Merck, Germany), cobalt(II) nitrate hexahydrate (≥ 99%, Merck, Germany), nickel(II) nitrate hexahydrate (≥ 99%, Merck, Germany). Other chemicals employed included hydrochloric acid (37 wt%, Dr. Mojallali, Iran), nitric acid (65 wt%, Dr. Mojallali, Iran), oxalic acid (≥ 99.5%, Carlo Erba, France), ammonia solution (25 wt%, Merck, Germany), sodium chloride (≥ 99%, Dr. Mojallali, Iran), sodium nitrate (≥ 99.5%, Merck, Germany), sodium hydrogen carbonate (≥ 99.5%, Merck, Germany), sodium hydroxide (≥ 95%, Dr. Mojallali, Iran), and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt dihydrate (≥ 99%, Merck, Germany). Deionized water (resistivity ≥ 18.2 MΩ cm) was used throughout the study.

Instrumentation

The instruments used in this study included an Ohaus GA200D analytical balance (USA), a Heidolph RZR 2040 mechanical stirrer (Germany), a Julabo MP-5 digital thermostated water circulating system (Germany), a Heraeus centrifuge model Labofuge (Germany), a Memert universal oven (Germany), a Zolalan M-UV-3 + water deionizer system (Iran), and a Varian AA220 flame atomic absorption spectrometer (Australia).

Extraction/back-extraction procedures

All extraction and back-extraction experiments were performed batchwise. All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3), and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. The standard deviation for all measurements was less than 3%, indicating high reproducibility of the experimental data. The organic diluent was first washed twice with deionized water to remove stabilizers. In the extraction step, equal volumes (5 ml) of the aqueous and organic phases were combined and mechanically stirred for 30 min. After phase separation assisted by centrifugation (2 min, 1500 rpm), the metal content of the aqueous phase was analyzed directly by FAAS, following dilution with deionized water if needed. The metal content in the organic phase was determined either by mass balance or by flame atomic absorption spectrometer (FAAS) analysis of the aqueous phase obtained after the back-extraction step using appropriate back-extraction reagent discussed in the corresponding sections.

Preparation of the real samples

The applicability of the proposed extraction procedures was assessed by extracting cadmium and zinc ions from leached solutions obtained from residues of hydrometallurgical zinc production plants in Zanjan province, Iran. For sample preparation, residues were first dried in an oven (105 °C). A 1 g portion of the dried sample was ground and digested in 30 ml of hydrochloric acid (0.5 mol l⁻¹) using magnetic stirring for 15 min at ambient temperature (40 °C). The mixture was filtered, and the filtrate was diluted to 100 ml with hydrochloric acid/deionized water.

Results and discussion

Selection of diluent

In liquid–liquid extraction, the analyte transfers from the aqueous source solution into the organic phase. The efficiency and, to some extent, the selectivity of this process depend on many factors, including the transfer mechanism, aqueous phase conditions, extractant, and diluent. The interactions between the organic diluent and the extracted species, the polarity of the diluent used, and cohesive energy of the diluents affect the extraction process24. To select an organic diluent for this study, the extraction of cadmium, zinc, cobalt and nickel ions (initial concentration 0.001 mol l−1) from 0.5 mol l−1 HCl solutions into the organic phases including the extractants Aliquat IL336 and Cyphos IL101 was followed by contacting 5 ml of the aqueous and organic phases for 30 min at room temperature. The results (Fig. S1, Supplementary File) confirm the diluent dependency of liquid-liquid extraction on the selected diluent and the complexity to give a clear and straightforward description of this effect25. These complications may come from the different and independent parameters which determine the diluent-diluent, diluent-extractant and diluent-extracted species interactions such as dielectric constant, polarity, and cohesive forces of the examined diluents24. The results also revealed that the selected diluent could also play a role in the selectivity of the extraction systems. Although dichloromethane (DCM) as diluent results in selectivity towards Cd(II), the organic phase containing Aliquat IL336 in DCE preferably extracts Zn(II) ions. Moreover, this extractant showed no extraction of Zn(II) when it was dissolved in cyclohexane. The poorest extraction characteristics of Aliquat IL336 were observed by selecting chloroform as the employed diluent. The extraction percentage of cadmium ions by the organic phase containing Aliquat IL336 dissolved in the examined diluents varies as: DCM > Kerosene ~ Xylene > n-Hexane > DCE (Dichloroethane) > Ethylbenzene ~ cyclohexane > chloroform. Overall, the highest extraction efficiency of the examined ions by the Cyphos IL101 solutions was also observed when dichloromethane was used. The arranged extraction percentage of cadmium as an example by Cyphos IL101 in the examined organic diluents follows as: DCM > Kerosene > DCE > Xylene > n-Hexane > Chloroform > Cyclohexane > Ethylbenzene. It is noteworthy that control experiments confirmed that no detectable metal extraction occurred with pure dichloromethane in the absence of extractants.

Variation of the extraction performance with aqueous phase HCl concentration

As shown in Fig. 1, extraction of Cd(II) and Zn(II) depends strongly on the hydrochloric acid concentration in the aqueous phase. Assuming the anionic chloro-complexes are the extractable species, the dependence on chloride concentration can be readily explained. In contrast, a weaker dependency was observed for Co(II) and Ni(II), most likely due to their lower tendency to form stable anionic chloro-complexes.

The extraction of the studied metal ions as a function of hydrochloric acid concentration exhibited a peak-shaped trend. This behavior can be attributed to the formation of different chloro-complexes at different chloride concentrations. At lower chloride concentrations, extractable anionic complexes form preferentially. At higher concentrations, higher charge density chloro-complexes become dominant and are less extractable.

Figure 1 reveals that the maximum extraction of Cd(II) by Aliquat IL336 occurs at hydrochloric acid concentrations between 0.2 and 0.5 mol l⁻¹, whereas for Cyphos IL101 and other metal ions with both extractants, the highest extraction was observed at 0.5 mol l⁻¹ HCl. The results also indicate that, regardless of the extraction system (whether using Aliquat IL336 or Cyphos IL101), the greatest difference in extraction percentages between cadmium and zinc occurs at the HCl concentration of 0.2 mol l⁻¹.

HCl vs. NaCl as the source of chloride ions

Considering that acidic media are commonly used in hydrometallurgy, the effect of replacing NaCl with HCl was examined while keeping the total chloride concentration constant at 0.5 mol l−1. The results (Fig. 2) confirm that, regardless of the extractant used, extraction of both Cd(II) and Zn(II) increased when sodium chloride was replaced with hydrochloric acid. This observation can be explained by considering that the chloride ions incorporated in the anionic chloro-complexes of Cd(II) and Zn(II) depends on the source of chloride ions accompanied by the hydrogen and/or sodium ion. The hydration energies of these ions are −1050 and − 365 kJ mol−1 for H+ and Na+, respectively26. These hydration energies allow considering less activity for water molecules in acid solutions than sodium chloride solutions. Thus, the anionic chloro-complexes may be more extractable from HCl solutions than NaCl media.

Aliquat IL336 showed slightly higher extraction percentages for Cd(II) and Zn(II) than Cyphos IL101. The results also show a significant tendency of both extractants for cadmium and zinc ions with respect to the two other examined metal ions (i.e. cobalt and nickel), with a higher predominant towards cadmium ions.

Considering the anion exchange nature of both examined extractants, their selectivity attitude can be related to the chloro-complexes formation affinity of the examined metal ions. It is assumed that under employed experimental conditions the MCl42− species (M2+ is the metallic cation), which will be discussed later (see Sect. 3.7) are the most probable extractable species. Although the stability formation constants of the assumed chloro-complexes under employed experimental conditions are not reported, but that reported for cadmium ions in different conditions is 1.41 × 10627. However, those for zinc and cobalt ions have been also found which were determined under different conditions as 1.78 × 106 and 1.7428. It is noteworthy that there is no report for the stability constants of chloro-complexes of nickel.

Temperature dependency of the processes

The extraction equilibria may be affected by the temperature and thus the applicability of the corresponding process may vary by this parameter. This highlights the importance of investigating temperature effects on the extraction process and reveals possible limitations in working temperature conditions. The variation of extraction percentages of Cd(II) and Zn(II) from hydrochloric acid solutions (0.5 mol l−1) by the solutions of Aliquat IL336 and Cyphos IL101 dissolved in dichloromethane (0.05 mol l−1) as a function of temperature (15–55 °C) was followed (Figure S3, Supplementary file).

The extraction efficiency of cadmium was practically not affected by temperature in both investigated extraction systems. The results of the zinc extraction with both ionic liquids were increasing in the range 15–35 °C, then it decreased.

The interpretation of the results is not so straightforward. The complication may result from this fact that the extraction process is followed by the chloro-metal complex formation step, which are temperature dependent. The enthalpy changes during the complex formation step may be exothermic while it is encountered with an endothermic process of dehydration of the hydrated ions. Besides, an increase in the entropy resulted from the water molecules released during the dehydration process of the hydrated ions compensated for a decrease in the entropy changes of complex formation. In addition to the chloro-complex formation process, the extraction equilibrium involves enthalpy and entropy changes. The dehydration of the extracted chloro-complex during the aqueous phase to organic phase transfer and the ion-pair formation in the organic phase are endothermic and exothermic processes, respectively. On the other hand, the positive entropy change of the dehydration of the extracted anionic chloro-complex is compensated with decrease during the ion-pair formation in the organic phase. Thus, the overall output of this investigation can be described by practically invariant extraction performance for the cadmium ions to the temperature variation in the studied range, while a peak form of the zinc extraction variations was observed by the examined metal ions extraction systems regardless of the extractant used.

Time dependency of the processes

The equilibrium time for the extraction systems was determined by agitating 5 ml of the aqueous phase (0.001 mol l⁻¹ of Cd(II) and Zn(II) in 0.5 mol l⁻¹ HCl) with 5 ml of the organic phase (0.05 mol l⁻¹ Aliquat IL336 or Cyphos IL101) for the time intervals ranging from 5 to 120 min (Figure S2, Supplementary file). The kinetics of the extraction processes were comparable for the metals studied, with equilibrium achieved after 30 min of stirring. No further change in extraction percentage was observed beyond this time.

Back-extraction studies

An efficient back-extraction step is as crucial as the extraction stage in liquid-liquid extraction processes. Firstly, back-extraction enables recovery of the organic phase, offering both environmental and economic benefits. Furthermore, in hydrometallurgical processes, the final stage typically involves electrolysis, which produces the target metal from an aqueous solution. Additionally, selective back-extraction may sometimes result in enhancing the separation efficiency in liquid-liquid systems when the initial extraction fails to achieve the desired mixture separation29,30.

To select an efficient back-extraction reagent, the extraction of cadmium(II) and zinc(II) was investigated. A 5 ml aqueous solution of the metal ions (0.001 mol l⁻¹ each in 0.5 mol l⁻¹ HCl) was stirred for 30 min with 5 ml of the extractants Aliquat IL336 or Cyphos IL101. The extracted values of the metals into the organic phase were calculated by the mass balance of their concentration after analysis of the remaining metals in the aqueous phase. The amount of the extracted Cd(II) by Aliquat IL336 and Cyphos IL101 were 99.9 ± 0.1% and 94.4 ± 0.7%, respectively. The amounts of Zn(II) extracted by Aliquat IL336 and Cyphos IL101 were 80.8 ± 0.8% and 73.8 ± 0.8%, respectively. This means that the concentration ratio of Cd(II) to Zn(II) in the organic phase increases to 1.23 and 1.28, for the system using Aliquat IL336 and Cyphos IL101 as extractant, respectively.

For back-extraction process, the loaded organic phase (5 ml) was contacted for 30 min with an equal volume of a stripping solution. The reagents tested were nitric acid (1 mol l⁻¹), oxalic acid (0.5 mol l⁻¹), ammonia (0.5 mol l⁻¹), sodium hydroxide (0.1 mol l⁻¹), sodium nitrate (0.1 mol l⁻¹), sodium hydrogen carbonate (0.1 mol l⁻¹), and EDTA/ammonia at varying concentrations (see Table 1 for details).

Characterization of the extracted species

To characterize the Cd(II) and Zn(II) extracted species, a series of extraction experiments by adjusting the aqueous phase at 0.5 mol l−1 of HCl and 0.001–0.07 mol l−1 with respect to the metal ions into the organic phase (0.05 mol l−1 of Aliquat IL336 and Cyphos IL101 in dichloromethane) was followed. The mole ratio method is a well-established technique for determining the stoichiometry of extracted species in solvent extraction. This method is based on the principle that at low metal concentrations, all extractant molecules are available for complexation, resulting in a linear increase in the metal/extractant ratio, while at higher concentrations, the extractant becomes saturated and the slope changes, indicating the formation of a specific metal-to-extractant complex. The extraction experiments were realized by the back-extraction experiments using sodium hydroxide (0.1 mol l−1) and ammonia (0.5 mol l−1) solutions for the recovery of Cd(II) and Zn(II), respectively. Figure 5 includes the variation of the mole ratios of Cd(II)/extractant and Zn(II)/extractant as a function of the metals’ initial concentrations. The extraction mechanism in this study is based on anion exchange, which is characteristic of both Aliquat IL336 and Cyphos IL101. Under the experimental conditions (0.5 mol l–1 HCl), the target metal ions (Cd(II), Zn(II)) form anionic chloro-complexes, predominantly [MCl]2–. The obtained curves for the extraction of Cd(II) and Zn(II) by both extractants show the inflections at the mole ratio (Cd(II)/extractants and Zn(II)/extractants) close to 0.5–0.6. The inflection point observed at approximately ~ 0.5 mol ratio confirms the formation of a 1:2 metal-to-extractant complex, consistent with the ion pair [R+]2[MCl42−] as proposed in Eq. 3. This allows considering Eq. 3 as the main extraction equilibrium for the extraction of Cd(II) and Zn(II) by the extractants Aliquat IL336 and Cyphos IL101.

This anion exchange mechanism is supported by: (1) the chloride concentration dependence observed in Fig. 1, (2) the mole ratio analysis showing a 1:2 metal-to-extractant stoichiometry, and (3) the successful back-extraction using reagents that disrupt chloro-complex formation (NaOH for Cd(II) and NH3 for Zn(II)).

Application of the extraction systems investigated

To evaluate the ability of the compared solvent extraction systems, three samples (each 1 g of residues taken from a zinc production plant located in Zanjan province (Iran) was leached by 10 ml solution of hydrochloric acid (0.5 mol l−1) by 30 min magnetically agitation at 40 °C. Then the mixture was cooled to the room temperature. After filtration and appropriate dilution, the metal contents were analyzed by FAAS. The solution was diluted with hydrochloric acid in such a way that the final concentration of chloride ion was adjusted to 0.5 mol l−1, and that of cadmium was 0.001 mol l−1. The separation-recovery of the metal contents was followed by the steps shown in Fig. 6. These results are like those presented in Fig. 6, revealing the applicability of the extraction systems studied for the real samples as it was observed in the synthetic samples. The extraction percentages from the industrial zinc plant residues were calculated as follows: For Aliquat IL336: Cd 97.0%, Zn 77.8%, Co 14.1%, Ni 6.9%. For Cyphos IL101: Cd 97.0%, Zn 72.2%, Co 14.1%, Ni 10.0%. Conclusion

The studied solvent extraction systems using Aliquat IL336 and Cyphos IL101 in dichloromethane showed efficient and selective extraction of cadmium and zinc ions from chloride solutions. Aliquat IL336 exhibited superior extraction efficiency and selectivity compared to Cyphos IL101. The extraction mechanism involves the formation of anionic chloro-complexes [MCl4]2– (M = Cd, Zn), which are extracted via anion-exchange as ion pairs [R+]2[MCl42–]. Using HCl as the chloride source yielded higher extraction percentages than NaCl, attributed to the differences in hydration energies of H+ and Na+ ions. Selective back-extraction using sodium hydroxide (0.1 mol L–1 and ammonia (0.5 mol L–1 enabled separate recovery of cadmium and zinc, respectively. The proposed method was successfully validated using real industrial residues from zinc production plants in Zanjan, Iran, confirming its practical applicability.

The proposed solvent extraction method offers several advantages for industrial application, including the use of commercially available extractants, high selectivity for Cd(II) and Zn(II) over Co(II) and Ni(II), and a two-step back-extraction process enabling separate recovery of target metals. However, some limitations should be acknowledged: incomplete separation of Co and Ni from the process stream, single-stage extraction limitations, scale-up challenges, partial Zn back-extraction efficiency, and the single-acid HCl digestion which simulates industrial chloride leaching but lacks comprehensive residue characterization (XRD, XRF, SEM-EDS). For higher purity products and complete process evaluation, multi-stage back-extraction, and multi-cycle extractant recycling studies, and detailed residue mineralogical analysis are recommended in future work. Moreover, an economic comparison of the extractants and diluents was beyond the scope of this study but is recommended for industrial application.
