Diagnostic efficacy and subgroup heterogeneity of SHOX2, RASSF1A, Septin9, and HOXA9 methylation in pleural effusion lymphoma
Authors: Jiaqi Bo, Zhenyao Lei, Suxia Zhang, Miaoxia He, Xinxin Huang, Bin She, Xiaosha Ren, Jie Li, Shunli Wang, Weiwei Rui, Yu Zeng, Xianghua Yi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDNA methylation profiling has emerged as a promising tool for improving pathological diagnosis. This study investigates the diagnostic efficacy and subgroup heterogeneity of SHOX2, RASSF1A, Septin9, and HOXA9 methylation in pleural effusion lymphoma, aiming to enhance diagnostic accuracy and identify high-risk molecular subgroups.MethodsA cohort of 109 patients (73 lymphoma, 36 benign pleural effusions) was analyzed using quantitative methylation-specific PCR for the four biomarkers. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to determine optimal cutoff values for each marker, and their diagnostic performance was evaluated in different lymphoma subgroups.ResultsThe cycle threshold values for the internal control β-actin predominantly ranged from 18 to 23, suggesting consistent DNA input for methylation analysis. ROC curve revealed that the area under the curve (AUC) values were 0.871 (SHOX2), 0.779 (HOXA9), 0.611 (Septin9), and 0.548 (RASSF1A). When combined, the four markers achieved an AUC of 0.896, with an overall diagnostic sensitivity of 83.6% and a specificity of 94.4%. SHOX2 and HOXA9 demonstrated higher sensitivity within diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) subgroups, with SHOX2 achieving 100% sensitivity in the non-GCB subgroup and HOXA9 achieving 90.9% sensitivity in the GCB subgroup. Septin9 methylation was significantly associated with the presence of B symptoms (P = 0.041). Importantly, RASSF1A methylation was more strongly associated with TP53 deletion rather than with Bcl-2/IGH, Bcl-6, or c-MYC rearrangements.ConclusionIntegrating methylation markers SHOX2, HOXA9, Septin9, and RASSF1A exhibits significant diagnostic potential in pleural effusion lymphoma, especially within DLBCL subgroups. These markers could prove valuable in identifying high-risk subgroups and guiding clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Lymphoma, a heterogeneous group of malignancies arising from lymphocytes, poses significant diagnostic challenges, particularly when presenting with pleural effusion [11]. The occurrence of various lymphoma subtypes differs in pleural effusion linked to lymphoma. DLBCL, accounting for 30–40% of all non-Hodgkin lymphomas (NHLs), exhibits pleural effusion in up to 30% of cases. Non-DLBCL lymphomas, including other large B-cell lymphomas and T/NK—cell lymphomas, are less common, but also associated with pleural effusion. The incidence of T/NK—cell lymphomas is around 10–15%, with pleural effusions identified in 20.0% of T-cell lymphomas [22]. The exact proportions of these subtypes in lymphoma-associated pleural effusion may be influenced by factors such as the patient’s immune status, geographical region, and underlying conditions [1]. Accurate differentiation between malignant lymphoma and benign pleural effusion (BPE) is critical for timely therapeutic intervention and prognosis assessment. Conventional diagnostic methods, such as cytology and immunohistochemistry (IHC), often lack sensitivity, especially in cases with low tumor cellularity or ambiguous morphological features [13]. For instance, cytological examination of pleural fluid has a reported sensitivity of only 60% for lymphoma detection [1]. These diagnostic limitations underscore the critical need for complementary molecular biomarkers to improve diagnostic accuracy and enable timely therapeutic intervention.

DNA methylation, an epigenetic modification involving adding methyl groups to cytosine residues in CpG islands, has emerged as a promising tool in cancer diagnostics [6, 10]. Aberrant methylation patterns, including hypermethylation of tumor suppressor genes and hypomethylation of oncogenes, are hallmarks of malignant transformation and can be detected with high sensitivity using techniques such as quantitative methylation-specific PCR (qMSP) [5]. Methylation analysis offers several key advantages: fast, objective, highly accurate, and cost-effective. Even primary care hospitals with basic PCR instruments can perform qMSP, thereby improving the overall diagnostic standards across different levels of healthcare. Notably, methylation markers like SHOX2, HOXA9, Septin9, and RASSF1A have shown diagnostic potential in various cancers, including lung [23] and colorectal malignancies [15]. Recent studies have also highlighted the utility of novel methylated DNA markers in detecting malignant pleural effusion, demonstrating their potential to improve diagnostic accuracy and complement traditional methods [7]. However, their utility in distinguishing lymphoma-associated pleural effusions remains underexplored, particularly in diverse lymphoma subtypes and molecular profiles [9].

This study evaluated the diagnostic efficacy and subgroup heterogeneity of SHOX2, HOXA9, Septin9, and RASSF1A methylation in pleural effusion lymphoma. We hypothesize that these markers will exhibit distinct diagnostic performance across lymphoma subtypes and correlate with necessary molecular alterations. This study seeks to address critical gaps in lymphoma effusion diagnosis by integrating methylation profiling with conventional diagnostic approaches.

Materials and methods

Patients and clinical specimens

Patients with pleural effusion treated at Shanghai Tongji Hospital, from April 2019 to December 2024, were carefully chosen for this study. The total sample size includes 109 patients, with 73 diagnosed with lymphoma and 36 with benign pleural effusion (BPE).

In the lymphoma group, the median age was 61.5 years with a standard deviation of 16.8 years, and the age range was 26 to 87 years. For the BPE group, the median age was 71.8 years with a standard deviation of 11.5 years, and the age range was from 38 to 90 years. Across all patients, the median age was 66.5 years with a standard deviation of 14.7 years, and the overall age range was from 26 to 90 years. Among lymphoma patients, 45 were male and 28 were female, while in the BPE group, 26 were male and 10 were female. Within the lymphoma group, the majority (59 patients) had B-cell lymphoma, 13 had T/NK-cell lymphoma, and 1 had classical Hodgkin’s lymphoma (CHL) (Table 1). Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, and all participants provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The inclusion criteria for this study were patients possessing comprehensive clinical data, and patients who had given their written informed consent. The exclusion criteria were as follows: a life expectancy of less than three months; absence of written informed consent; involvement in another clinical trial within the past 12 weeks; concurrent diseases that would preclude treatment as described in the protocol; active infections requiring systemic antibiotic or antiviral treatment; women who were lactating, pregnant, or of child-bearing potential and not using a reliable contraceptive method; and severe psychiatric illness.

Cell block preparation

During thoracentesis or thoracoscopy procedures at Shanghai Tongji Hospital, at least 100 ml of pleural effusion was collected from patients. The collected pleural effusion was processed to create a cell paraffin block. The cell-containing effusion was transferred to a 10-mL disposable centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 2108 rpm for 10 min. After discarding the supernatant, anhydrous ethanol was added to the pellet, followed by vigorous vortexing to ensure thorough mixing. The sample was then centrifuged again for 5 min. Subsequently, the pellet was carefully wrapped in filter paper and transferred to an embedding cassette for further processing.

Methylation-specific PCR

Following the manufacturer’s instructions, the CWhipro Circulating DNA Midi Kit (CWBIO, Jiangsu, China) was utilized for the DNA extraction. Briefly, 1 mL of the pleural effusion sample was mixed with Proteinase K and Buffer CL, and then incubated at 60 °C for 30 min. After adding Buffer CB, the mixture was filtered through a spin column under vacuum pressure. The column was washed with Buffer GW1, GW2, and absolute ethanol, then centrifuged to remove residual ethanol. Finally, the purified DNA was eluted with Buffer EB and stored at − 20 °C for further analysis. The Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) was used in conjunction with the Qubit Fluorometer 3.0 to measure the DNA concentration. DNA was subsequently subjected to bisulfite conversion using the Methy-All-In-One Kit (Tellgen Co., Shanghai, China). Methylation-specific PCR (MS-PCR) was performed to determine DNA methylation levels using OncoMe (Tellgen, Shanghai, China), an in vitro diagnostic (IVD) test. The PCR protocol was established with the following thermal conditions: an initial pre-denaturation step at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles consisting of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 58 °C for 35 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. The process concluded with a final extension phase at 72 °C for 8 min. The amplification reactions were performed using an ABI 7500 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Four indicators were detected in two separate PCR reactions. Tube 1 was used to detect SHOX2, RASSF1A, and the internal reference gene(β-actin), while Tube 2 was used to detect HOXA9, Septin9, and β-actin. The result was deemed valid if the CY5 fluorescence signal’s amplification curve presents a smooth “S” shape and the corresponding cycle threshold (Ct) value for β-actin is within the range of 18 to 30. Data analysis was based on CT values, where SHOX2(VIC) and HOXA9(VIC) genes were considered positive if CT < 32, while RASSF1A(FAM) and Septin9(FAM) genes were considered positive if CT < 35.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The paraffin block was sectioned into thin slices. They were fixed in 10% neutral formalin, routinely dehydrated, embedded, and sliced with a thickness of 4 μm for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. The morphology, apoptosis and mitotic figures of lymphocytes were observed. Cell paraffin blocks were continuously sectioned at 3 μm thick for immunocytochemical staining. Two lymphoid marker antibodies were used; CD20 (L26, working fluid), CD3 (MX036, 1:200), CD30 (MX080, 1:50), CD5 (MX052, 1:150), and CD56 (MX039, 1:100) were purchased from Fuzhou Maixin Technology Co., Ltd; BCL6 (LN22, 1:150), CD10 (UMAB235, 1:150), MUM1 (OTI6F6, 1:150), and Perforin (ZM159, 1:100) were purchased from Beijing Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology Co. Ltd.; CD15 (MMA, 1:100) was purchased from Shanghai GeneTech Co. Ltd.

CD20 is the B lymphocyte marker, and CD3 is the T lymphocyte marker. Three antibodies, CD10, BCL6, and MUM1, were used for classification by the DLBCL Hans Criteria (GCB and non-GCB). Rare types such as classical Hodgkin lymphoma (CHL) markers are CD15 and CD30, and NK/T-cell lymphoma markers are CD56 and Perforin.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization assays and Epstein–Barr virus small RNA in situ hybridization

According to operating instructions, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assays were performed on the cellblock sections (Guangzhou LBP Medicine Science & Technology Co.,Ltd.). Assays were completed using the following probes: break-apart probes to detect Bcl-2/IGH, Bcl-6 or c-MYC gene rearrangements. The status of TP53 gene was detected by TP53 (CSP17) deletion probe. The FISH results were analyzed by fluorescence microscopy (BX51, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Among 100 nuclei, more than 15% of tumor cells showed a separation signal (Bcl-2/IGH, Bcl-6 or c-MYC), or more than 20% of tumor cells that showed a deletion signal (TP53) were deemed positive. Epstein–Barr virus was detected by EBV-encoded small RNA in situ hybridization according to operating instructions (HENAN Celnovtebio Biotechnology CO., Ltd).

ROC curve analysis

ROC curves were constructed utilizing the ΔCt values for SHOX2 and RASSF1A methylation markers as independent predictors, with the pathological outcomes of MPE or BPE serving as dependent variables. Logistic regression modeling was applied to estimate the likelihood of MPE diagnosis, considering the methylation status of SHOX2 and RASSF1A as predictive factors. Following this, the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was determined to evaluate the diagnostic utility of the combined methylation markers SHOX2 and RASSF1A in distinguishing MPE from BPE.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 26.0 and GraphPad Prism version 8.4.0 were conducted to perform the statistical analyses. The chi-square test was employed to examine the disparities among groups. The efficacy of patient indices and the integration of multiple indices were assessed through the ROC curve, with the AUC being computed. A p-value below 0.05 was deemed to indicate statistical significance.

DNA concentration and internal control stability

The DNA concentration in pleural effusion samples from patients with lymphoma and those with BPE was quantitatively measured (Fig. 2). The results showed that the DNA concentration varied between the two groups, with lymphoma patients generally exhibiting higher DNA levels than benign controls. Specifically, among the lymphoma group, 6.2% of the samples had a DNA concentration less than 1 ng/μL, indicating low DNA content. In the benign control group, 25.6% of the samples had a DNA concentration below 1 ng/μL. For the methylation analysis, 50 ng of DNA was added to each PCR reaction. In both groups, the Ct values of the internal control β-actin were primarily concentrated between 18 and 23, indicating a consistent amount of DNA added to each PCR reaction. ROC curve analysis for determining the cutoff values of each methylation marker

All samples were analyzed using quantitative methylation-specific PCR (qMSP) for four biomarkers: SHOX2, HOXA9, Septin9, and RASSF1A. The ΔCt values for each marker were calculated. ROC curves were constructed based on ΔCt values combined with clinical diagnostic outcomes (benign vs. malignant) in pleural effusions, and the Youden index was applied to determine optimal cutoff values.

For SHOX2 methylation, the optimal ΔCt cutoff was 9.92, which was unified to 10. HOXA9 methylation showed a cutoff of 6.21, rounded to 6.0. Septin9 methylation had a cutoff of 8.09, standardized to 8. For RASSF1A methylation, the cutoffs were 15.76. Based on prior PCR stability studies [14], the maximum allowable cutoff for RASSF1A was set at 12. ROC analysis of pleural effusions demonstrated patients AUC values of 0.871 (SHOX2), 0.779 (HOXA9), 0.611 (Septin9), and 0.548 (RASSF1A). When all four markers were combined, diagnostic performance significantly improved, achieving an AUC of 0.896 (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Diagnostic value of methylation markers in lymphoma

Diagnostic value of methylation markers in lymphoma

The ΔCt values of SHOX2, HOXA9, Septin9, and RASSF1A methylation were analyzed and plotted as scatter plots to compare their distributions between malignant and BPE groups (Fig. 4). The analysis revealed that SHOX2 demonstrated a sensitivity of 74.0% and specificity of 94.4% for diagnosing malignant pleural effusion. Similarly, HOXA9 showed a sensitivity of 60.3% and specificity of 94.4%. In contrast, Septin9 exhibited a lower sensitivity of 24.7% but maintained a specificity of 100.0%, while RASSF1A had the lowest sensitivity (8.2%), but also achieved a specificity of 100.0%. These results indicate that SHOX2 and HOXA9 are highly effective in diagnosing pleural effusion lymphoma, with sensitivity and specificity exceeding 60% and 90%, respectively. Although Septin9 and RASSF1A showed lower sensitivity, their specificity of 100% suggests their potential utility as confirmatory markers. When combined into the OncoMe panel, the four markers achieved a comprehensive diagnostic sensitivity of 83.6% while maintaining a specificity of 94.4% (Table 2). Sensitivity and specificity of methylation markers in lymphoma

Diagnostic efficacy of methylation markers in different pleural effusion lymphoma subgroups

The diagnostic efficacy of methylation markers (SHOX2, HOXA9, Septin9, and RASSF1A) was assessed in pleural effusion lymphoma. The detection was performed across various subgroups of pleural effusion lymphoma, including B-cell lymphoma, T/NK-cell lymphoma, DLBCL, and non-DLBCL. Additionally, DLBCL subgroups were further classified according to the Hans criteria into germinal center B-cell (GCB) and non-GCB subtypes. Data from Table 3 shows that in B-cell lymphoma, SHOX2 exhibited the highest sensitivity (76.3%), followed by HOXA9 (66.1%), Septin9 (27.1%), and RASSF1A (10.2%). For T/NK-cell lymphoma, the sensitivities were lower: SHOX2 (69.2%), HOXA9 (38.5%), SEPT9 (15.4%), and RASSF1A (0%). However, no significant differences were observed between B-cell and T/NK-cell lymphoma subgroups for any marker (P > 0.05). Furthermore, in DLBCL, SHOX2 demonstrated a sensitivity of 81.6%, HOXA9 78.9%, Septin9 36.8%, and RASSF1A 10.5%. In contrast, for non-DLBCL, the sensitivities were notably lower: SHOX2 (66.7%), HOXA9 (42.9%), Septin9 (9.5%), and RASSF1A (9.5%). Significant differences were observed for HOXA9 and Septin9 between DLBCL and non-DLBCL subgroups (P < 0.05), highlighting their potential utility in distinguishing these subtypes (Table 4). Within the Hans classification (Table 5), SHOX2 showed higher sensitivity in the non-GCB subgroup (100%) compared to the GCB subgroup (72.7%), while HOXA9 exhibited sensitivities of 90.9% (GCB) and 75.0% (non-GCB). No significant differences were found between GCB and non-GCB subgroups for any marker (P > 0.05). However, this suggests that SHOX2 and HOXA9 may serve as complementary markers. Combining these two markers could potentially enhance diagnostic accuracy in DLBCL classification. Overall, the methylation markers demonstrated varying diagnostic efficacies across different lymphoma subgroups. Correlation between methylation and molecular markers in lymphoma

The methylation levels of four markers (SHOX2, HOXA9, Septin9, and RASSF1A) were evaluated in 17 DLBCL samples to assess the correlation with various molecular markers, including Bcl-2/IGH, Bcl-6, c-MYC, and specific genetic variation profiles such as non-hit lymphoma (NHL), single-hit lymphoma (SHL), and double-hit lymphoma (DHL). NHL refers to cases with no rearrangements in Bcl-2/IGH, Bcl-6, or c-MYC; SHL refers to cases with one rearrangement among these markers; and DHL refers to cases with co-occurrence of c-MYC and either Bcl-2/IGH or Bcl-6. The overall positivity rate for SHOX2 methylation was 88.2%, with a notable deviation observed in the Bcl-2/IGH rearrangement-positive group, where the positivity rate was 66.7%. For HOXA9, the average positivity rate was 76.5%, but this varied across groups, with a positivity rate of 33.3% in the Bcl-2/IGH rearrangement-positive group and 57.1% in the single-hit lymphoma (SHL) group. Septin9 methylation exhibited an overall positivity rate of 35.3%, which was higher in the Bcl-6 rearrangement-positive group, reaching 66.7%. In contrast, RASSF1A methylation was not highly prevalent in any genetic variation group, with low positivity rates across all categories. These differences did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the limited sample size. The small number of cases in each genetic variation group may have restricted the ability to detect clear associations. Therefore, while these findings suggest potential variability in methylation status across different genetic backgrounds, further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm these observations and to determine the actual impact of specific genetic variations on methylation profiles in lymphoma.

Notably, RASSF1A methylation was positive in only three out of the 17 samples, and none of these cases exhibited variations in Bcl-2/IGH, Bcl-6, or c-MYC. However, in an expanded cohort of 34 cases, including DLBCL, non-DLBCL, and T-cell lymphoma, we identified two cases with TP53 deletions, both positive for RASSF1A methylation. These two cases also exhibited SHOX2 and HOXA9 methylation but were negative for Septin9 methylation. These findings may suggest that RASSF1A methylation is more closely associated with TP53 deletion rather than with rearrangements in c-MYC, Bcl-2/IGH, or Bcl-6. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the potential relationship between RASSF1A methylation and TP53 deletion in lymphoma (Table 6). Correlation between methylation markers and clinical features in pleural effusion lymphoma

The methylation status of SHOX2, HOXA9, Septin9, and RASSF1A was evaluated in relation to various clinical features in pleural effusion lymphoma. The analysis included age, gender, B symptoms, tumor location, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels, International Prognostic Index (IPI) score, and bone marrow involvement. The results indicated that these methylation markers did not show significant correlations with most clinical features. Specifically, no significant differences were observed in the methylation status of SHOX2, HOXA9, Septin9, and RASSF1A between patients of different ages, genders, tumor locations, LDH levels, IPI scores, or bone marrow involvement status. However, a notable exception was found for Septin9 methylation, which exhibited a significant association with the presence of B symptoms (P = 0.041) (Table 7). This suggests that Septin9 methylation may be related to the clinical manifestation of B symptoms in lymphoma patients. Given the potential clinical relevance of this finding, further studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to explore the relationship between Septin9 methylation and B symptoms and determine the broader clinical significance of these methylation markers in pleural effusion lymphoma. Discussion

Pleural effusion is a common clinical manifestation of lymphohematopoietic tumors [8, 18]. While diagnosing recurrent cases is relatively straightforward, identifying cases where effusion is the initial symptom presents significant challenges. Cytomorphological examination is essential for diagnosis and can offer valuable insights into the disease’s nature. Nonetheless, the cytological diagnosis of lymphocyte-rich pleural effusions based solely on morphology is often difficult [8, 18]. In nonmalignant effusions, the lymphocytes are mature with small, round and hyperchromatic nuclei, while in malignant effusions, the lymphocytes are immature with enlarged, round or irregular nuclei. These distinctions are subtle, especially in atypical cases. Although immunohistochemistry can identify the distribution of lymphocytes in pleural fluid, differentiating between reactive lymphocytes and lymphoma in atypical cases remains challenging, even with this technique [14]. The findings of this study demonstrate the diagnostic potential of SHOX2, HOXA9, Septin9, and RASSF1A methylation in differentiating malignant pleural effusion lymphoma from benign effusions, while also highlighting significant subgroup heterogeneity in their performance. The integration of these methylation markers, particularly SHOX2 and HOXA9, offers a promising approach to complement conventional diagnostic methods such as cytology and immunohistochemistry, which often lack sensitivity in detecting lymphoma-associated effusions [8, 18].

In establishing the cutoff values for the indicators, a balance between sensitivity and specificity was carefully considered. Given that the clinical application of methylation in this context is to assist in the differential diagnosis of benign and malignant pleural effusion caused by lymphoma, higher specificity was given priority. The four indicators were evaluated based on their performance in detecting malignant and benign samples, and the optimal cutoff values were determined using the Youden index. A higher value indicates better specificity. Among the four indicators, RASSF1A had the best specificity (ΔCt = 12), followed by SHOX2 (ΔCt = 9) and Septin9 (ΔCt = 8). HOXA9 showed weak methylation signals in benign lesions, so the cutoff value for delta Ct was set to a relatively low value (6.0) to maintain high specificity (94.4%). This approach ensures that the methylation markers can accurately identify malignant cases while minimizing false-positive results, which is crucial for diagnosing and managing lymphoma-associated pleural effusion.

The combination of SHOX2 and HOXA9 methylation markers demonstrated a high AUC value of 0.896, with a comprehensive sensitivity of 83.6% and specificity of 94.4% in diagnosing pleural effusion lymphoma. This significantly improved over traditional cytology, with a reported sensitivity of only 60%. Lymphoma cytology can be challenging due to the often indistinct morphological features, making it difficult to differentiate between reactive lymphoid proliferation and malignant lymphoma. When methylation markers are positive, they can provide additional evidence supporting a diagnosis of malignancy, thereby aiding in the differentiation between benign and malignant conditions. In such cases, methylation analysis can be a valuable adjunct to traditional morphological assessment.

SHOX2 emerged as the most robust biomarker, achieving an AUC of 0.871 and a sensitivity of 74.0% with 94.4% specificity. Notably, SHOX2 demonstrated stable diagnostic sensitivity across different lymphoma subtypes, including DLBCL (81.6%), non-DLBCL (66.7%), and T/NK-cell lymphoma (69.2%). Its high sensitivity in DLBCL subgroups, particularly in the non-GCB subtype (100%), aligns with previous studies [4]. The methylation of SHOX2 impairs its transcriptional function, thereby triggering the activation of the Wnt/β- catenin pathway, which is crucial in embryonic development. This activation, in turn, facilitates the proliferation and migration of lymphoma cells and confers chemoresistance upon them, potentially contributing to the increased aggressiveness of lymphoma. Similarly, HOXA9 exhibited moderate diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.779). The diagnostic sensitivity of HOXA9 varied across different subgroups, ranging from 38.5% in T/NK-cell lymphoma to 78.9% in DLBCL with notable sensitivity in the GCB subgroup (90.9%). Studies have shown that HOXA9 regulates the formation of germinal centers and class switch recombination during normal B-cell differentiation. Methylation of HOXA9 leads to its silencing, disrupts pathways associated with differentiation (such as NF-κB and PI3K/AKT), and promotes clonal expansion in the GCB subtype of lymphoma [16, 17]. When SHOX2 was combined with HOXA9, the diagnostic sensitivity was further enhanced, reaching 100% in non-GCB, 90.9% in GCB, and 71.4% in non-DLBCL. In T/NK-cell lymphoma, the sensitivity of SHOX2 was 69.2%, while that of HOXA9 was only 38.5%. This suggests that the methylation spectrum of T-cell lymphoma may differ significantly from that of B-cell-derived lymphoma. Therefore, exploring T-cell-specific markers, such as TET2 and DNMT3A, is necessary to improve the diagnostic efficacy in T/NK—cell lymphoma [3, 12].

However, the limited sensitivity of Septin9 (24.7%) and RASSF1A (8.2%) highlights their role as complementary confirmatory markers rather than standalone diagnostic tools. Septin9 methylation was significantly associated with B symptoms (P = 0.041). This may be related to its role in cytoskeleton regulation, and methylation may promote the migration of tumor cells to the pleural cavity, resulting in systemic symptoms such as fever and weight loss [2]. RASSF1A methylation was only found in cases with TP53 deletion, which may suggest that abnormalities in the RAS pathway and p53 inactivation may synergistically drive the progression of lymphoma [21]. Further studies are needed to verify the co-occurrence mechanism of these two factors in genomic instability.

The limitations of this study are primarily related to the relatively small sample size, particularly for certain subgroups such as T/NK-cell lymphoma (n = 13). This constraint may diminish the statistical power necessary to confirm the subtype-specificity of the methylation markers. Future studies should consider expanding the cohort size to validate the diagnostic efficacy of these markers across various lymphoma subtypes. Additionally, when identifying the source of malignant pleural effusions, only referring to the methylation markers in this study would be limited, because these methylation markers are not unique to lymphomas, they may also be positive in other malignancies, including lung and colorectal cancer [19, 20]. Therefore, in the diagnosis of malignant pleural effusion, it is necessary to first rely on cytology and IHC to distinguish the origin of tumor cells. Carcinomas have obvious immunohistochemical positivity for pan cytokeratin (CK); lymphoproliferative disorders show significant immunohistochemical positivity for leukocyte common antigen (LCA), CD20, and CD3, and the majority of malignant pleural effusions can be characterized through cytology and IHC, which facilitates the determination of tumor cell origin. Therefore, we propose that methylation analysis be incorporated into a comprehensive diagnostic evaluation of pleural fluid, in conjunction with cytology and IHC. Specifically, methylation analysis is primarily employed for the differentiation between benign and malignant pleural effusions and for the identification of high-risk molecular subtypes of lymphoma.

Based on the findings of this study, we propose the integration of the OncoMe methylation panel into the diagnostic workflow for pleural effusion. This panel should be utilized in combination with FISH assays (to detect MYC/Bcl-2 rearrangements) and EBER-ISH (to exclude EBV-associated lymphoma), thereby establishing a multi-modal diagnostic model that capitalizes on the strengths of each technique.

In the future, tracking the treatment response and survival data of patients with methylation-positive results would be valuable. This approach will help evaluate whether SHOX2 and HOXA9 methylation are associated with adverse prognostic outcomes, such as chemotherapy resistance or early recurrence, thereby providing insights for personalized treatment strategies.
