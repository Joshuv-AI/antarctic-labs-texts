Novel dialdehyde fullerene-like carbon nanostructures from red garlic peels for naked-eye cyclohexane gas sensing and pH
Authors: Hebat-Allah S Tohamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study introduces highly sensitive, functionalized dialdehyde fullerene-like carbon nanostructures (DAFs) as a versatile platform for chemical sensing. The DAFs were synthesized through an effective functionalization strategy, confirmed by Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), which introduced key dialdehyde groups necessary for multi-modal detection. Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) validated a critical morphological evolution from small carbon dots (CDs) to larger, fullerene-shaped DAFs, providing a new electronic and structural foundation for sensing. The DAFs demonstrate dual sensing capabilities for both pH and non-polar organic vapors, specifically explosive cyclohexane (LEL ≈ 13000 ppm). The DAFs sensor’s Limit of Detection (LOD) of 137.34 ppm is strategically justified by this hazard, as it provides an early, high-reliability safety warning significantly below the 10% LEL industrial safety threshold of 1300 ppm. Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations elucidated the mechanism of gas detection, confirming that the interaction with non-polar cyclohexane fundamentally alters the electronic structure of the DAFs, causing the dipole moment (µ) to increase significantly from 5.060 Debye to 8.203 Debye, which underlies the observed fluorescence enhancement. Experimentally, the DAFs function as an effective pH probe, exhibiting a distinctive “turn-on” fluorescence response at alkaline pH 12 due to the deprotonation of surface functional groups. Crucially, the material operates as a simple, naked-eye visual sensor for cyclohexane, quantified by a clear shift in CIE chromaticity coordinates from (0.193, 0.422) to (0.209, 0.473) upon gas exposure. This work provides a deep, integrated theoretical and experimental understanding of functionalized carbon nanostructures for industrial safety and general environmental applications.Keywords: Dialdehyde fullerenes, Cyclohexane sensor, Gas sensor, Garlic peels, DFT calculations, Flammable cyclohexane, Limit of Detection (LOD)

Introduction

Gas sensing plays a crucial role in safeguarding both the environment and human health by providing an early warning system for a wide range of harmful substances [1–3]. The ability to accurately detect and monitor volatile organic compounds (VOCs) is particularly vital, given their widespread presence and potential for toxicity [4, 5]. These chemical vapors, which can emanate from various industrial processes, spills, and everyday products, pose significant risks to air quality, ecosystem stability, and public well-being. For example, cyclohexane is a commonly used solvent and a foundational chemical in many manufacturing industries, making its precise detection essential for ensuring worker safety and preventing accidental releases [6, 7]. Crucially, cyclohexane is highly flammable and possesses a significant explosion risk (Lower Explosive Limit, LEL ≈ 13000 ppm) [8]. Continuous monitoring of such substances is therefore a fundamental practice for protecting individuals from dangerous exposure and mitigating potential environmental damage [9, 10]. The development of advanced gas sensors capable of real-time, high-sensitivity detection is a key area of research aimed at enhancing our ability to manage these risks effectively. Moving beyond conventional methods, carbon dots (CDs) have emerged as a highly promising modern material for gas sensing applications [11–14]. These tiny nanoparticles, typically less than 10 nanometers in size, possess a set of key properties that make them an attractive alternative to traditional semiconductor-based sensors [15, 16]. One of their most significant features is strong fluorescence, which can be precisely tuned through control over their synthesis. This allows for the creation of sensors that can interact with and signal the presence of specific analytes. Furthermore, CDs exhibit remarkable stability and resistance to photobleaching, which enables their use in long-term monitoring systems [17, 18]. Crucially, they stand out from many traditional inorganic counterparts, like heavy metal-based quantum dots, due to their low toxicity, cost-effectiveness, and biocompatibility, making them a more environmentally friendly and safer choice for a wide array of sensing technologies [19–23]. Their ability to be easily modified with different functional groups also provides a versatile platform for engineering highly selective and sensitive sensors for a variety of target molecules.

There is a significant and growing global demand for sustainable and cost-effective materials driven by environmental concerns, resource depletion, and stricter regulations [24–28]. This has spurred a major shift towards green chemistry, which focuses on designing chemical products and processes that reduce or eliminate the use and generation of hazardous substances [29, 30]. The synthesis of CDs from agricultural waste, like red garlic peels (RGP), is a perfect example of this innovative approach [31, 32]. Instead of being discarded as waste, these readily available, low-cost peels are valorized and transformed into valuable high-tech nanomaterials [33, 34]. This not only aligns with the principles of a circular economy by adding value to a discarded resource, but also offers a more environmentally friendly alternative to traditional CD synthesis methods that often rely on expensive or toxic chemicals. This sustainable process reduces both waste and the overall environmental footprint, providing a dual benefit of resource efficiency and economic viability. Simply synthesizing CDs from sustainable sources like garlic peels is a valuable step, but for a truly effective sensor, the key is to engineer the surface chemistry to create high sensitivity and selectivity for a particular target molecule. This is the central innovation of this work. While many CDs are functionalized with common groups like carboxyl or hydroxyl groups, this research specifically utilizes the dialdehyde functional group on the surface of the CDs. The presence of these highly reactive dialdehyde groups allows for a specific and strong interaction with cyclohexane gas, which is what differentiates this sensor and makes it so effective [35–39]. This is a significant advantage over using unfunctionalized or non-specifically functionalized CDs. Without this tailored modification, the sensor would likely react to a wider range of compounds, leading to poor selectivity and an inability to accurately differentiate cyclohexane from other gases. The unique chemical affinity between the dialdehyde group and cyclohexane molecules is the core principle that enables a highly selective and sensitive detection mechanism, ensuring the sensor provides accurate and reliable data for its intended purpose [40, 41].

Building on the foundation of sustainable materials and targeted surface chemistry, the primary objective of this research is to develop a novel and highly effective gas sensor. We will achieve this by first synthesizing unique dialdehyde fullerenes-like carbon nanostructures (DAFs) from recycled garlic peels, a process that embodies the principles of green chemistry and waste valorization. The next crucial step will involve a comprehensive characterization, including Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and Fourier-Transform Infrared (FTIR) analysis, to confirm their novel fullerene morphology, composition, and the successful integration of the dialdehyde functional groups. Finally, we will rigorously evaluate their performance as a sensor for cyclohexane gas, testing their sensitivity, selectivity, and overall reliability with a key focus on demonstrating their potential as a new, environmentally friendly solution for industrial safety monitoring by operating effectively below the explosive limits. This work not only presents a practical application for a waste product but also pushes the boundaries of nanomaterial engineering for next-generation sensing technologies.

Materials and methods

Materials

Red Garlic peels (RGP) were collected after using the pulp. It was sourced from the Egyptian kitchen. Sodium periodate was purchased from Sigma Aldrich.

Preparation of dialdehyde fullerenes-like carbon nanostructures from red garlic peels

Several steps were involved in synthesizing carbon dots (CDs) from recycled garlic peels (RGPs). Initially, a solution was prepared by mixing 4 g each of RGP, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), and urea in 100 mL of water. Following this, the mixture was sonicated and then frozen. The final stage of the process was microwave irradiation at 700 watts for 10 min. Then, to create the dialdehyde fullerenes -like carbon nanostructures (DAFs), 1.5 g of the prepared CDs were suspended in 20 mL of water. This suspension was oxidized with 2 g of sodium periodate and microwave-treated at 700 W for 5–7 min in an acidic solution. The resulting product was then filtered, washed multiple times with ethanol, and dried at room temperature.

Dialdehyde carbon dots cyclohexane gas sensor

To generate volatile cyclohexane gas, a controlled heating method was used within a sealed glass setup. Initially, a 500 mL borosilicate glass beaker was filled with 10 mL of liquid cyclohexane and then sealed with a custom-made lid. The sealed beaker was carefully heated on a hot plate, controlled at 80–85 °C, to vaporize the liquid cyclohexane. The temperature was held constant to ensure a steady vapor phase of the gas. A small incision was made in the beaker’s lid, and the prepared DAFs solution was carefully introduced through this opening. The beaker was kept sealed until the contents cooled to room temperature, after which the resulting DAFs@cyclohexane solution was collected for further analysis. To visually evaluate the colorimetric sensing, a photograph was taken with a smartphone, capturing the solution’s appearance before and after its exposure to cyclohexane. The sensor’s behavior was also investigated using fluorescence spectroscopy to quantify changes in its fluorescent properties.

To ensure the accuracy and reproducibility of the vapor concentration for calibration and LOD calculations, a standardized saturation method was employed. By heating a fixed volume of liquid cyclohexane in a sealed 500 mL borosilicate glass beaker at 80–85 °C (exceeding its boiling point of 80.7 °C), a stable and saturated vapor phase was established in the headspace. The concentration was calculated based on the volume of the sealed system and the known physical constants of cyclohexane.

Characterization

Determination of aldehyde content in DAFs

0.1 g of undried DAFs were added to 25 mL of a 0.25 M hydroxylamine hydrochloride solution in a 250 mL beaker. The mixture was stirred for 48 h at room temperature, protected from light by aluminum foil. After the reaction was complete, the solid product was separated by filtration and washed with 600 mL of deionized water. A control sample with the same concentration of cellulose solution was prepared and handled identically to the DAFs sample. The hydrochloric acid (HCl) released during the reaction was then back-titrated using a 1 M NaOH solution. The volume of NaOH consumed (denoted as Vc in liters) was used to calculate the nitrogen concentration of the resulting oxime derivative of the DAFs, which directly corresponds to the degree of oxidation. As a control, the same concentration of cellulose solution (Vb) was utilized.

Determination of the pH-sensitivity of DAFs prepared from red garlic peels

The pH sensitivity of the DAFs was examined by observing changes in their fluorescence and color. Using a fluorescence spectrophotometer, the fluorescence intensity of the DAFs was measured across a range of pH values. To do this, buffer solutions were prepared at pH 2, 7, and 12. The DAFs were then added to each of these solutions, and their fluorescence emission spectra were recorded using a specific excitation wavelength kept constant for all measurements. A smartphone was also used to take pictures of the solutions to visually document any colorimetric changes.

DFT calculations

The molecular structures were optimized to their minimum energy state using the Berny algorithm and the B3LYP/6-31G(d, p) method. The convergence was strictly enforced by setting the following numerical thresholds, which are standard for high-quality geometry optimization: Maximum Force at 0.0004 Hartree/Bohr, RMS Force at 0.0003 Hartree/Bohr, Maximum Displacement at 0.0018 Ångstrom/Bohr, and RMS Displacement at 0.0012 Ångstrom/Bohr. These tight criteria ensure the final reported electronic properties (such as EHOMO, and ELUMO) are based on robust, true minimum energy geometries, validating the proposed sensing mechanism. Molecular structures were optimized to their minimum energy state using the Berny algorithm. After this optimization, several key parameters were calculated, including the energies of the highest occupied molecular orbital (EHOMO​) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (ELUMO), along with the energy gap (Eg​). Other electronic properties, such as dipole moment (µ), absolute hardness (η), absolute softness (σ), and chemical softness (S), were also determined to understand the molecular reactivity of the DAFs and their sensing mechanism [21, 22, 42].

Electrostatic mapping

To generate the Molecular Electrostatic Potential Map (ESPM) for the DAFs, the B3LYP/6-31G(d, p) method was used. Using this method allows for a detailed understanding of the charge distribution and potential reactive sites on the surface of the DAFs, which is essential for predicting how they will interact with other molecules, such as cyclohexane gas.

General experimental conditions

All characterization and gas-sensing experiments were performed at a stable room temperature of 25 °C and an ambient atmospheric pressure (1 atm). By maintaining the system at room temperature, we ensured that the sensing response was driven purely by the chemical interaction between cyclohexane and the DAFs, rather than thermal fluctuations. Furthermore, the liquid-phase nature of the DAFs probe provides an inherent resistance to ambient humidity (RH 50%), as the sensing mechanism involves a competitive molecular displacement within an already aqueous environment, ensuring high reproducibility without the need for complex environmental shielding.

Fluorescence and optical characterization

All fluorescence measurements were recorded using a spectrofuorometer model: Jasco FP6500, Tokyo, Japan light source: Xenon arc lamp 150 W. The samples were excited at a fixed wavelength (350 nm). The emission spectra were collected from 350 nm to 850 nm with a data interval of 1 nm and an integration time of 1 s per step. The excitation and emission slit widths were set to 5 nm. Samples were analyzed at ambient temperature (25 °C) using 1 cm quartz cuvette as sample holders.

Results and discussion

Mechanism of DAFs formation with morphological observation and FTIR

TEM analysis showed critical insights into the morphological evolution of the material from raw CDs to the final DAFs through the synthesis process. Initially, the CDs exhibit a small size range of 4.05–4.32 nm, confirming them as typical quantum-sized nanoparticles. This base material is then subjected to an oxidative process, which results in the formation of the sensing agent, the DAFs. Crucially, the morphology is noted to shift to a “fullerene-shaped” structure. This is significant because it suggests a structural rearrangement beyond mere surface functionalization, potentially leading to a more defined, polyhedral, and highly stable carbon cage-like architecture. Concurrently, the size of these individual structures increases to 6.61–8.42 nm. This enlargement indicates that the chemical treatment (oxidation and microwave steps) likely induces a degree of structural reorganization or controlled coalescence, creating larger, electronically distinct units that are better suited for the gas-sensing function. Furthermore, the initial small CDs are transformed into larger, “fullerene-shaped” structures. This structural change, coupled with the chemical functionalization, stabilizes the electronic structure and enhances the material’s capacity to interact with the environment. In the operating state, these ∼7.5 nm DAFs also form large aggregates (clusters of ∼320 nm), which is critical for the naked-eye sensing capability. This clustering is promoted when the non-polar cyclohexane vapor dissolves into the DAFs solution, disrupting the polar solvent shell around the functionalized DAFs. This aggregation forces the DAFs units into close physical proximity, facilitating π-π stacking and forming a larger effective conjugated system. This collective electronic effect lowers the fluorescence energy gap for the entire cluster, resulting in a red-shift of the overall emission and absorption that collectively leads to the visually observable color change and highly sensitive response characterized by the CIE shift. This electronic energy gap reduction results in the red-shift of the fluorescence and absorption bands, directly enhancing the solvatochromic effect and altering the color perceived by the naked-eye. The size of the clusters (~ 320 nm) is significant as it approaches the wavelength of visible light. This increase in size maximizes the light-scattering and absorption cross-section of the aggregated system. This enhanced scattering powerfully translates the electronic red-shift into the macroscale CIE shift (from (0.193, 0.422) to (0.209, 0.473) (as will be discussed in Sect. 3.4.1). The core mechanism driving the preparation of the highly sensitive DAFs is the precise chemical step of periodate oxidation and the resulting formation of the dialdehyde functional groups (–CHO) on the carbon nanostructure surface. Periodate (IO4−) is a highly selective oxidizing agent used to target and cleave carbon-carbon bonds (C–C) in vicinal diols (adjacent C–OH groups), which are abundant in the polysaccharide and sugar structures derived from the red garlic peels. This reaction breaks the C–C bond and converts the two hydroxyl groups into two highly reactive aldehyde groups, hence forming the DAFs. This confirms the efficiency of this strategy with a high DO of 67.23%, validating that a significant portion of the surface has been successfully functionalized with these key groups.

The FTIR of CDs and DAFs showed peaks between 3434 and 3448 cm–1, 3199–3359 cm–1, 2187–2929 cm–1, 1620–1674 cm–1, 1417–1456 cm–1, 1168–1355 cm–1, 1066–1147 cm–1, and 669–777 cm–1 related to N–H, O–H, C–H, amide I, amide II, C=C, O–C–O, and C–N, respectively (Fig. 1) [22, 43–46]. The presence of N–H and C–N prove the nitrogen doping inside the DAFs. The peak at 1664 cm–1, and 852 cm–1 in DAFs are related to hemiacetal group of the dialdehyde [23, 24, 33]. The calculated DO (%) was 67.23% which prove the efficiency of dialdehyde preparation. The intensity of C–O–C peak in CDs was decreased in DAFs due to the breakdown of the sugar rings during dialdehyde oxidation [23, 33].

DFT calculations with the electrostatic mapping of CDs and DAFs

The DFT and ESPM analyses demonstrate that the dialdehyde fullerenes-like carbon nanostructures (DAFs) possess superior sensing properties compared to the non-functionalized CDs, particularly for detecting non-polar cyclohexane. While both materials show an electronic response to the gas, the DAFs exhibit a more significant and beneficial change in their electronic structure. The key is the significant shift in the electronic properties of the DAFs upon interaction with cyclohexane, which explains the observed fluorescence enhancement. The analysis of the HOMO-LUMO energy gap (Eg​) is a critical starting point. While the Eg​ of the non-functionalized CDs slightly decreases from 0.3199 eV to 0.3053 eV upon binding with cyclohexane, the DAFs show a much more significant change. The energy gap of the pristine DAFs is initially quite small (0.2406 eV), indicating high reactivity. After interacting with cyclohexane, the DAFs@cyclohexane complex has a larger Eg​ (0.2464 eV). This change confirms that the interaction with cyclohexane fundamentally alters the electronic structure of the DAFs, enabling a more efficient radiative decay pathway, which directly explains the enhanced fluorescence observed experimentally. Further evidence of this interaction comes from the dipole moment (µ). Cyclohexane is a non-polar molecule with a negligible µ of 0.0001 Debye (Fig. 2; Table 1). The non-functionalized CDs have a low µ of 1.76 Debye, but this increases dramatically to 5.30 Debye after interacting with cyclohexane. The DAFs, with their highly polar dialdehyde groups, start with a much higher µ of 5.060 Debye. This value increases even further to 8.203 Debye upon interaction with cyclohexane. This substantial increase in polarity for both types of CDs after binding with the non-polar cyclohexane is a powerful confirmation of a strong electronic interaction and significant charge redistribution. This substantial change, which is much greater than the increase seen with the non-functionalized CDs, indicates a stronger and more favorable electronic interaction. This large charge redistribution is the electronic “switch” that triggers the observed “turn-on” fluorescence enhancement when the DAFs are exposed to the gas.

This is a crucial finding, as it explains how a polar nanostructure can effectively detect a non-polar gas. The analysis of absolute hardness (η) and softness (σ) provides additional support for the proposed mechanism. The decrease in the hardness of both the DAFs and the CDs after forming a complex with cyclohexane confirms that they become electronically “softer” or more polarizable. This shift makes them more susceptible to electronic transitions, which perfectly aligns with the observed enhanced fluorescence. A softer complex is more easily excited, leading to more efficient light emission. The Molecular Electrostatic Potential Map (ESPM) for cyclohexane visually confirms its non-polar nature; its surface is almost entirely green, representing a neutral electrostatic potential, which aligns with its near-zero dipole moment. This is in stark contrast to the DAFs, which have a complex, non-uniform surface with both electron-rich (red) and electron-poor (blue) regions due to the presence of polar functional groups. The ESPM for the DAFs@cyclohexane complex would show a significant change in the overall color distribution, with regions of red becoming more pronounced, visually confirming the dramatic increase in the dipole moment. This direct visual evidence proves that the non-polar cyclohexane molecule is indeed interacting with the polar surface of the DAFs, fundamentally changing their electronic state and providing a clear explanation for the observed fluorescence enhancement.

Visual sensing by naked-eye

There are two key sensing mechanisms: pH sensitivity and cyclohexane solvent sensing. The color changes observed at different pH values are a direct result of the protonation and deprotonation of surface functional groups, particularly those containing nitrogen. At pH 2, in a highly acidic environment, these groups are protonated, altering the electronic structure of the DAFs and causing them to appear a lighter, more transparent yellow-orange. At neutral pH 7, the solution turns brown as the protonation state changes. Conversely, at pH 12, in a highly basic environment, the functional groups deprotonate, leading to a shift in the electronic structure that results in a darker, reddish-brown color. This pH-responsive color change makes DAFs a viable visual pH indicator (Fig. 3a).

a Visual sensing by naked-eye for the DAFs at pH 2, pH 7, pH 12; and after contact with cyclohexane, and b Mechanism of visual sensing by naked-eye after contact with cyclohexane

The most compelling aspect of this demonstration, however, is the solvent-sensing ability shown with cyclohexane. The brown solution at pH 7, when exposed to the non-polar cyclohexane, transforms into a light, yellowish solution. This dramatic color shift provides clear evidence of a successful sensing event. The underlying mechanism is likely a combination of the solvatochromic effect and the passivation of surface-quenching sites. The solvatochromic effect refers to the change in a substance’s light absorption and emission properties due to a change in the polarity of the surrounding solvent. The non-polar cyclohexane interacts differently with the DAFs’ surface compared to the polar water, causing a change in their optical properties. Additionally, the non-polar cyclohexane molecules may bind to and “passivate” or block certain surface sites on the carbon dots that would normally quench their fluorescence. By reducing this quenching, the DAFs become more fluorescent and appear lighter in color (Fig. 3b). This simple, visual color change without the need for sophisticated equipment makes DAFs a highly effective and straightforward sensor for specific organic solvents like cyclohexane.

Optical properties

Optimization of pH-dependent gas sensing

The gas-sensing performance of the DAFs is intrinsically tied to the pH of the environment, a dependency rooted in the protonation state of the surface functional groups. The high fluorescence of the non-oxidized garlic CDs at neutral pH suggests they have a less-defective structure and a surface rich in functional groups that favor efficient light emission. The lack of extensive oxidation means there are fewer surface-trapping sites that would otherwise lead to non-radiative decay, allowing for a strong fluorescence. The significant decrease in fluorescence intensity (F.I.) for the oxidized DAFs at pH 7 is a clear sign of fluorescence quenching. The oxidation process, which converts some of the CDs’ surface groups into reactive aldehyde and other oxygen-containing groups, creates a high density of non-radiative recombination centers. When an electron is excited, it can easily get trapped in these surface defects and dissipate its energy as heat instead of light. The DAFs are designed with a large number of these active sites, making them prone to quenching in a neutral environment. The subsequent increase in F.I. for the oxidized DAFs at pH 12 is a fascinating display of their pH-dependent properties. At this alkaline pH, the acidic surface groups such as carboxyl (− COOH) and hydroxyl (− OH)—become deprotonated, converting into their anionic forms (− COO− and − O−). This change in surface charge and chemical environment fundamentally alters the energy levels of the surface states. The deprotonation can reduce the efficiency of the non-radiative decay pathways, effectively “turning on” or enhancing the fluorescence. This phenomenon, often called a “turn-on” sensor response, demonstrates how the fluorescence of DAFs can be precisely tuned by controlling the pH, a valuable characteristic for biosensing and environmental monitoring (Fig. 4). The fluorescence spectra observed for CDs from garlic and DAFs at different conditions neutral (pH 7) and alkaline (pH 12) are typical for these materials and can be explained by their structural and surface chemistry. The changes in peak positions (522, 428, and 419 nm) are a direct result of different photoluminescence mechanisms at play. The non-oxidized garlic CDs at neutral pH show a peak at 522 nm, which likely corresponds to emission from core states, possibly originating from the graphitic sp2 hybridized carbon structures within the CDs. This green fluorescence is a common characteristic of many CDs. Upon oxidation, as seen with the DAFs, surface defects and functional groups play a more dominant role in the photoluminescence. The oxidized DAFs at neutral pH show a blue-shifted peak at 428 nm. This shift indicates that the primary emission mechanism has changed from the core states to the surface states. The oxidation process introduces new surface functionalities, such as carboxyl, carbonyl, and hydroxyl groups, which act as trap states. When electrons get trapped in these surface states, they emit light at a higher energy (shorter wavelength), resulting in the observed blue shift. The dialdehyde groups on the surface of DAFs would further contribute to these surface states. The most significant shift is observed for the oxidized DAFs at alkaline pH 12, with a peak at 419 nm. The further blue shift under alkaline conditions is attributed to the deprotonation of surface functional groups. The surface chemical reaction that triggers the “turn-on” fluorescence response of DAFs at pH 12 is the deprotonation of the acidic oxygen-containing functional groups on the nanostructure’s surface. At pH12, the acidic groups like carboxyls (–COOH) and hydroxyls (–OH) on the surface of the DAFs lose their protons to become carboxylate (–COO−) and alkoxide (–O−) anions. This change in the surface charge and chemical environment alters the energy levels of the surface states. The deprotonated surface groups often lead to an increase in non-radiative pathways and a shift in the photoluminescence, resulting in the observed blue-shifted emission. This pH-dependent fluorescence is a well-known property of many CDs and is often exploited for pH sensing applications. The unique photoluminescence properties of these carbon dots are influenced by both their inherent carbon core and the tunable surface chemistry. The emergence of multiple fluorescent peaks for the DAFS at pH, ranging across the entire spectrum (371, 466, 546, 582, 754, 787, and 856 nm), is a direct result of intricate surface chemistry in an acidic environment, where the high concentration of protons (H+) leads to the protonation of oxygen-containing functional groups (such as carboxyl and hydroxyl groups) on the DAFs’ surface. This process fundamentally alters the electronic structure, as each type of surface group is protonated to a different degree, creating a variety of new electronic states and distinct radiative recombination pathways that yield the multi-peak spectrum. For example, the blue-shifted peaks (371 nm, 466 nm) likely arise from highly energetic surface states, while the intermediate peaks (546 nm, 582 nm) correspond to core defects. Crucially, the red-shifted peaks (754 nm, 787 nm, 856 nm) are strongly indicative of the formation of aggregates in the acidic solution, as protonation reduces electrostatic repulsion, allowing the DAFs to clump and create larger conjugated systems that lower the fluorescence energy gap. This complex, multi-peak behavior at low pH provides a robust mechanistic precedent for the multi-peak response observed with cyclohexane gas, strongly supporting the DAFs’ potential for advanced ratiometric sensing. The specific peak positions from a blue-shifted 371 nm to a red-shifted 856 nm are indicative of this complexity. The blue-shifted peaks likely arise from highly energetic surface states or from smaller, more localized chromophores, while the red-shifted peaks could be attributed to the formation of aggregates in the acidic solution. Protonation reduces the electrostatic repulsion between the DAFs, causing them to clump together. This aggregation can create larger conjugated systems, which lower the energy gap for fluorescence, leading to the observed red shifts.

Therefore, pH 7 represents the optimized “operating window” for the cyclohexane sensor. At this neutral condition, the DAFs exhibit a quenched, low-fluorescence baseline that provides the necessary dark background for a dramatic “turn-on” response. The absence of strong electrostatic repulsion between the particles at pH 7 allows the non-polar cyclohexane to easily displace the polar solvent shell. This displacement facilitates the π-π stacking and structural aggregation, which is the physical driver behind the observed CIE chromaticity shift. By operating at pH 7, the sensor achieves maximum sensitivity and visual clarity, ensuring it can reliably detect explosive cyclohexane well below its industrial hazard threshold. The observation that DAFs respond to cyclohexane gas with multiple fluorescent peaks is a compelling demonstration of their potential as a gas sensor. This behavior is fundamentally similar to the pH-sensing mechanism observed and is attributed to a complex interaction between the gas molecules and the DACD surface. Cyclohexane, a non-polar flammable gas, likely interacts with the surface functional groups of the DAFs (i.e. the aldehydes, carboxyls, and hydroxyls). This interaction, whether through weak physical adsorption or chemical interactions, alters the electronic environment of the surface states. This modification creates a series of new, distinct emissive pathways, each corresponding to a different peak in the fluorescence spectrum. The result is a multi-peak response, which is a significant advantage for sensing applications as it allows for ratiometric sensing, a method where the ratio of peak intensities can provide a more accurate and robust measurement of gas concentration. The broadness of the 516 nm peak offers a crucial clue about the specific nature of this interaction. A broad peak in fluorescence typically signifies heterogeneous emission, meaning the light is not coming from a single, well-defined electronic transition. Instead, it suggests that the cyclohexane molecules are interacting with a wide variety of similar, but not identical, surface sites on the DAFs. This could be due to the inherent structural heterogeneity of the carbon dots themselves, where subtle variations in surface curvature or functional group distribution lead to a range of slightly different binding energies for the cyclohexane molecules. This creates a distribution of excited states, each with a slightly different energy, which collectively results in a single, broad emission band. In contrast, the sharper, narrower peaks at other wavelengths likely arise from more specific and uniform interactions with particular functional groups or sites on the DACD surface. The collective multi-peak and broad-peak response of DAFs to cyclohexane demonstrates a sophisticated sensing mechanism that is highly dependent on the delicate balance between the physical and chemical interactions at the DAFs-gas interface.

The Chromaticity coordinates (x, y) on the CIE color space chart are used to quantify color, with x representing the red-green coordinate and y representing the yellow-blue coordinate. These values for CDs and DAFs provide a quantitative description of their emitted color under different conditions. The CDs have chromaticity coordinates of (0.137, 0.563). These coordinates fall within the green-blue region of the CIE diagram, indicating that the CDs emit a light that is predominantly blue-green. The relatively high y-value suggests a strong green component, while the low x-value indicates a significant blue contribution, which aligns with the common photoluminescence properties of many CDs. For the DAFs, the chromaticity coordinates shift significantly, reflecting changes in their emitted color due to pH and gas exposure. At pH 2, the coordinates are (0.210, 0.473). Compared to the original CDs, the x-value has increased, and the y-value has decreased. This shift indicates a movement from the green-blue region towards a more yellow-green color. This change is consistent with the multi-peak fluorescence observed in acidic conditions, where some peaks are red-shifted, leading to a shift in the overall perceived color. At both pH 7 and pH 12, the DAFs have identical chromaticity coordinates of (0.193, 0.422). This suggests that despite the significant change in fluorescence intensity and the “turn-on” mechanism at alkaline pH, the overall perceived color of the emitted light remains stable. The decrease in both x and y values from pH 2 to pH 7/12 shows a shift towards a bluer color. This is consistent with the observed blue-shift in the emission peaks at higher pH levels. The fact that the coordinates are the same at pH 7 and 12 indicates that while deprotonation enhances fluorescence, it does not significantly change the dominant emission wavelength and therefore the resulting color. Finally, upon exposure to cyclohexane, the DAFs’ chromaticity coordinates become (0.209, 0.473). This is very similar to the coordinates at pH 2, (0.210, 0.473), which again falls in the yellow-green region. This dramatic color shift from the blue-hued pH 7/12 state demonstrates the high sensitivity of the DAFs to the gas. The shift towards a more yellow-green color is a direct result of the interaction between the cyclohexane molecules and the DACD surface, which likely causes a change in the relative intensities of the multiple emission peaks, leading to a new, distinct overall color. This significant and observable color change makes the DAFs promising candidates for use in gas sensing applications.

Confirmatory evidence of the aggregation-induced emission (AIE) effect

The dramatic “turn-on” visual response and accompanying fluorescence enhancement provide strong experimental evidence for the aggregation-induced emission (AIE) effect as the primary sensing mechanism. In their initial dispersed state at neutral pH 7, the DAFs appear as a brownish-orange solution with significantly quenched fluorescence. This quenching is attributed to the active intramolecular rotation and vibration of surface dialdehyde and nitrogen-containing groups. These high-frequency motions serve as non-radiative decay channels, where excited-state energy is dissipated as heat through collisions with polar water molecules rather than being emitted as light. The visual transformation from the brown pH 7 solution to the vibrant, light-yellow DAFs@cyclohexane solution (Fig. 3a) confirms the triggering of the AIE mechanism through the Restriction of Intramolecular Motion (RIM [47–49]. Upon contact with non-polar cyclohexane, the solvating water shell is disrupted, forcing the 7.5 nm DAFs to coalesce into significantly larger approximately 320 nm clusters. This physical aggregation, driven by π-π stacking of the fullerene-like cores, “locks” the surface functional groups into a rigid state. This rigidity suppresses non-radiative energy loss, effectively “turning on” the radiative transition and shifting the perceived color toward the yellow-green region, as quantified by the CIE shift from (0.193, 0.422) to $(0.209, 0.473).

The pH-dependent color changes further support this mechanism:

At pH 2: The solution appears a light, transparent yellow-orange, indicating that high protonation interferes with the baseline electronic state.

Collectively, the transition from the quenched pH 7 state to the bright DAFs@cyclohexane state serves as a confirmatory “naked-eye” proof of the AIE effect. The correlation between the morphological clustering (~ 320 nm) and the dramatic optical brightening solidifies AIE as the fundamental driver for this sustainable sensing platform.

Cyclohexane sensing

The exact electronic mechanism underlying the fluorescence enhancement of DAFs upon interacting with non-polar cyclohexane gas is a dual-effect phenomenon driven by the analyte-induced dipole moment shift and the aggregation-induced emission effect. The primary electronic mechanism, confirmed by DFT calculations, involves the non-polar cyclohexane gas acting as an electronic perturbation on the highly functionalized DAFs’ surface. While the pristine DAFs already possess a high initial dipole moment (5.060 Debye) due to their polar surface functional groups, the binding or close interaction with the non-polar cyclohexane forces a massive reorganization of the electron density across the DAFs structure. This results in the dipole moment dramatically increasing to 8.203 Debye in the DAFs@cyclohexane complex. This substantial charge redistribution and the resulting electronic stabilization of the excited state is the key factor that increases the efficiency of the radiative decay pathway, thereby shutting down competing non-radiative decay mechanisms (like thermal vibration) and causing the observed dramatic “turn-on” fluorescence enhancement. This electronic enhancement is further amplified by a morphological change that contributes to the overall output. The non-polar cyclohexane vapor dissolves into the DAFs solution and disrupts the polar solvent shell surrounding the functionalized DAFs, which in turn promotes aggregation by favoring DAFs-DAFs interactions. This leads to the formation of large clusters (~ 320 nm), where the close proximity facilitates π-π stacking and electronic coupling between adjacent DAFs units. This collective electronic interaction creates a larger effective conjugated system across the cluster, which inherently lowers the effective electronic energy gap (Eg), thus causing a red-shift in both the absorption and emission bands. This aggregation-induced emission effect synergistically enhances the overall fluorescence intensity and simultaneously drives the visually discernible color change (the CIE shift) observed by the naked-eye.

Limit of detection (LOD) = 3σ/S and limit of quantification (LOQ) = 10σ/S, where S is the slope of the regression equation and σ is the standard deviation of the y-intercept [50]. he DAFs sensor’s reported LOD of 137.34 ppm is not intended for the ppb level of environmental monitoring, but rather for high-reliability early warning against an immediate explosion hazard. The fundamental safety threshold for cyclohexane is its Lower Explosive Limit (LEL), which is approximately 1.3 Vol%, or a very high 13,000 ppm [51]. Industrial safety standards dictate that action alarms must be set far below this, typically at 10% LEL, which equates to a critical threshold of 1300 ppm. By demonstrating a robust and reliable detection limit of 137.34 ppm, The DAFs sensor is operating at a concentration ten times lower than the mandatory safety alarm threshold and nearly one hundred times lower than the explosion limit. This performance positions the DAF sensor not as a high-precision analytical instrument, but as an effective and cost-efficient field safety monitor capable of providing a significant advance warning of a major leak before the concentration approaches the highly dangerous 1300 ppm zone. Furthermore, the likely output of the DAF sensor, a fluorescence “turn-on” or “naked-eye” color change, is perfectly suited for this safety application. In the chaotic scenario of a major chemical spill, a visually immediate, qualitative indication at 137 ppm is far more valuable for triggering immediate emergency and evacuation protocols than a slow, complex, quantitative reading from a laboratory instrument. Therefore, the 137.34 ppm LOD is not a sensitivity flaw but a strategically appropriate metric that validates the DAF sensor as a novel, fast-acting, and visible safety tool for industrial environments handling explosive cyclohexane.

Superiority of DAFs over conventional cyclohexane sensors

The DAFs offer several unique advantages over conventional cyclohexane gas sensors, particularly in terms of preparation cost, operation, and safety applicability, primarily due to their biomass origin and their colorimetric detection mechanism (Table 2). In terms of preparation cost and sustainability, the DAFs gain a significant edge because they are derived from RGP, an abundant and inexpensive agricultural waste/biomass. This reliance on a waste product dramatically reduces material cost compared to sensors based on synthesized polymers. Furthermore, the synthesis is simple, utilizing cost-effective steps like microwave treatment and chemical oxidation, avoiding expensive high-vacuum deposition or cleanroom fabrication techniques required for many conventional solid-state devices. Regarding operation and sensing mechanism, the DAFs sensor offers superior convenience. It provides a visually discernible color change (quantified by the CIE shift) upon exposure, enabling immediate, naked-eye detection that requires no external power, complex data logging, or specialized electronic readers for the primary function. This is complemented by a dual response; the visual color change and a measurable fluorescence “turn-on” enhancement which is offering redundancy for both low-tech and high-sensitivity analytical applications. Critically, unlike many conventional synthetic sensors that require high operating temperatures (150–400 °C) for adequate sensitivity, the DAFs sensor operates effectively and safely at ambient room temperature (25 °C), eliminating substantial power consumption associated with heating elements.The greatest advantage lies in safety applicability, especially when monitoring highly flammable and explosive gases like cyclohexane (LEL ~ 13000 ppm). Since the DAFs sensor is a liquid-based, passive colorimetric indicator, it requires no electrical power or heating element for its primary visual function, thereby eliminating the risk of sparking and ignition inherent in conventional electrical sensing components. This intrinsic safety is paramount in hazardous industrial environments. Moreover, the sensor’s high sensitivity, evidenced by its LOD of 137.34 ppm, positions it strategically for early warning detection. This concentration is significantly below the 13,000 ppm industrial safety alarm threshold (10% LEL), making it an effective, high-sensitivity indicator capable of detecting major leaks or spills well before they pose an explosion risk.

Future perspectives and selective sensing

It is important to note that this study represents a foundational “proof-of-concept” for utilizing green-synthesized, dialdehyde-functionalized carbon nanostructures (DAFs) as visual gas sensors. While the current results demonstrate a high sensitivity and a low LOD of 137.34 ppm for cyclohexane, we recognize that industrial environments often contain complex mixtures of volatile organic compounds (VOCs). As this is a novel sensing platform, the current work focuses on establishing the fundamental aggregation-induced emission (AIE) mechanism and the electronic interaction between the non-polar gas and the polar DAF surface. In upcoming research, a comprehensive selectivity and anti-interference study will be carried out to validate the sensor’s performance against competitive gases and common industrial vapors. This future work will aim to refine the surface dialdehyde density to further enhance the molecular recognition of cyclohexane, ensuring the sensor’s robust performance in real-world air matrices. Regarding the operational lifecycle, the current DAFs probe functions as a high-contrast, non-reversible indicator for cyclohexane detection. The stability of 320 nm aggregates, formed via AIE-active π-π stacking, ensures that the visual ‘turn-on’ signal remains as a permanent record of gas exposure. While this limits multi-cycle reusability, it provides a strategic advantage for industrial safety as a low-cost, disposable ‘safety-tag’ that requires no power to maintain its warning state. Future iterations of this work will explore chemical triggers or thermal treatments to potentially reverse the aggregation state and enable multi-use capabilities. Also future work will focus on quantifying the stability of these functional groups over extended periods to ensure the reliability of the DAFs as a long-term industrial safety solution.

Conclusions

This work successfully establishes the dialdehyde fullerenes-like carbon nanostructures (DAFs) synthesized from red garlic peels as a versatile, dual-purpose material platform, with enhanced properties rooted in deliberate surface functionalization. Theoretical Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations provided insight into the core sensing mechanism. The results indicate that the interaction of DAFs with non-polar cyclohexane alters their electronic structure, which is the proposed cause of the observed optical changes. Specifically, the binding of cyclohexane induces a shift in the HOMO-LUMO energy gap (Eg), changing from 0.0794 eV for pristine DAFs to 0.2406 eV for the DAFs-cyclohexane complex. Concurrently, the dipole moment increased from 5.060 Debye to 8.203 Debye, which confirms a strong electronic interaction and charge redistribution upon binding the non-polar gas. Experimentally, the DAFs demonstrate clear optical responsiveness. They exhibit a distinct “turn-on” fluorescence response at alkaline pH 12 due to the deprotonation of surface functional groups. Crucially, the material shows a visible color change and an enhancement in fluorescence when exposed to the high concentration of cyclohexane vapor used in this paper’s sealed-vessel experiment. The chromaticity coordinates (CIE) shifted significantly from (0.193, 0.422) at neutral conditions to (0.209, 0.473) upon gas exposure, providing a simple, naked-eye discernible change. Furthermore, the fluorescence profile displays a complex multi-peak structure in the presence of cyclohexane, suggesting the potential for ratiometric sensing after further calibration and data analysis. The sensor achieved a Limit of Detection (LOD) of 137.34 ppm for cyclohexane under the laboratory conditions of this study. This concentration is significantly below the 1300 ppm industrial safety alarm threshold (10% LEL). This positioning suggests the sensor is suitable as a simple, high-sensitivity chemical indicator for detecting high concentrations of cyclohexane under controlled lab conditions, paving the way for future rigorous testing needed for practical safety applications. Future work must focus on demonstrating selectivity and long-term stability without environmental cross-sensitivity.
