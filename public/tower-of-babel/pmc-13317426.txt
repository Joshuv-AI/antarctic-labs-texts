BMI-dependent association between mammographic breast density and osteoporosis in postmenopausal women using automated density assessment
Authors: Hong-Seon Lee, Seung-hyun Song, Chaeri Park, Sungjun Kim, Young Hoon Youn, Ji Hyun Youk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo evaluate the association between automatically measured mammographic breast density (MBD) and osteoporosis in postmenopausal women and determine whether this relationship differs across body mass index (BMI).MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study included 7,143 postmenopausal women who underwent both digital mammography and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). MBD (%) was automatically quantified using the Laboratory for Individualized Breast Radiodensity Assessment (LIBRA). Osteoporosis was defined as a DXA T-score ≤ -2.5. BMI-dependent associations were evaluated using logistic regression with a BMI×MBD interaction (continuous model) and prespecified categorical analyses (median split). Discrimination (AUC) was compared between models with and without MBD.ResultsOsteoporosis was present in 12.8% (912/7143) of participants. In the continuous interaction model including age, BMI, MBD, and a BMI×MBD interaction, the BMI×MBD interaction was significant (P = 0.007). The estimated odds ratio (OR) for osteoporosis per 1% higher MBD was 0.989 (95% CI 0.981–0.997) at BMI 20, 0.976 (95% CI 0.964–0.989) at BMI 25, and 0.963 (95% CI 0.943–0.984) at BMI 30. In a prespecified median split (MBD < 25.3% vs. ≥ 25.3%), the absolute risk difference was larger in women with BMI ≥ 25 (9.28% vs. 4.59%). Adding MBD to an age + BMI model minimally changed AUC (0.682 to 0.684).ConclusionHigher automated mammographic breast density was associated with lower odds of osteoporosis, and this inverse association was stronger at higher BMI. Although mammographic breast density added little incremental discrimination beyond age and BMI, these findings may provide biologically and epidemiologically relevant insight but do not support clinical decision-making based on mammographic breast density alone.

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a major public health concern, particularly in postmenopausal women, due to its strong association with increased fracture risk, morbidity, and mortality [1, 2]. After menopause, declining estrogen levels disrupt bone homeostasis by reducing bone formation and increasing resorption, which accelerates bone loss and predisposes women to fragility fractures in the spine, hip, and wrist [1, 2]. Although bone mineral density (BMD) assessment via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) is the gold standard for diagnosing osteoporosis and guiding treatment decisions [1], DXA screening is commonly targeted to women at higher clinical risk (e.g., older age, low body mass index [BMI], prior fracture), which may leave some women undiagnosed until fracture [3]. As a result, many postmenopausal women at risk for osteoporosis may remain undiagnosed until a fracture occurs, highlighting the value of identifying easily obtainable clinical or imaging factors associated with osteoporosis risk.

Mammographic breast density (MBD) has been explored as a potential imaging correlate (proxy indicator) of bone health due to its strong dependence on estrogen levels and metabolic factors [4–7]. MBD reflects the relative composition of fibroglandular and fatty tissue in the breast, with higher density associated with greater estrogenic influence [8]. Since estrogen influences both MBD and bone health, it has been hypothesized that higher density, possibly reflecting hormonal environment, could be associated with lower osteoporosis risk. However, findings remain inconsistent [4–7, 9–11]. In some reports, higher breast density was associated with higher BMD and lower osteoporosis risk [12, 13], whereas other studies found no meaningful association between the two measures [14, 15]. One important explanation for these discrepancies is the complex interplay between BMI, MBD, and BMD. Specifically, higher BMI tends to increase BMD through mechanical loading, while simultaneously reducing percentage MBD owing to increased adipose tissue. Prior studies have also shown that BMI differentially affects percent versus absolute breast density measures, supporting the biologic plausibility that adiposity may modify density-related associations. In addition, prior literature on mammographic density and bone mineral density/osteoporosis has been heterogeneous [9], which may partly reflect differences in adiposity distributions and in the handling of BMI across studies. Therefore, although direct prior evidence for adiposity-specific effect modification is limited, analyses that explicitly model BMI as an effect modifier (e.g., BMI×MBD interaction) are biologically and epidemiologically justified [15].

To efficiently analyze MBD in a large volume of mammograms, automated software can provide objective and reproducible measurements. The Laboratory for Individualized Breast Radiodensity Assessment (LIBRA; University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia, PA, USA) is a publicly available tool that automatically quantifies dense area, total breast area, and percent density from full-field digital mammography. Traditional breast density assessment is subject to interobserver variability [16], whereas automated tools provide more objective assessments [17]. Using LIBRA enhances measurement consistency and facilitates large-scale analysis by reducing reader-dependent variability and minimizing human error. As mammography is routinely performed in postmenopausal women, automated breast density assessment may offer an additional imaging marker associated with osteoporosis risk. Although automated breast density analysis is not conceptually new, its application in large, BMI-stratified modeling provides an objective framework to examine the relationship between breast density and bone health at the population level. Its open-source design further supports reproducibility across institutions. Thus, we aimed to evaluate the association between automated percent MBD and osteoporosis and to test BMI as an effect modifier using a BMI×MBD interaction, with interpretable categorical summaries.

Materials and methods

From January 2014 to December 2023, 21,937 postmenopausal women who underwent both screening mammography and bone mineral density assessment by DXA during the same routine health screening visit were initially identified, accounting for 35,052 mammograms. After applying exclusion criteria (women with a history of breast cancer surgery or augmentation, current or past hormone replacement therapy, or incomplete imaging or clinical data), a total of 7,143 patients with 7,143 mammograms were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Analyses were therefore performed using complete-case data, and no imputation was applied for missing covariates. Postmenopausal status was identified from the electronic medical records based on menopause-related information recorded at the time of health screening, and age at menopause was also available in the dataset.

Breast density was quantitatively assessed using LIBRA version 1.0.4 [18, 19], a publicly available, fully automated software tool (https://www.med.upenn.edu/sbia/libra.html). LIBRA has been used in prior studies for automated density assessment. LIBRA analyzes 2D mammographic images to determine the proportion of dense breast tissue relative to total breast area, providing an objective and reproducible method for density estimation. LIBRA-generated parameters included absolute dense area (cm²), total breast area (cm²), and percentage breast density (%). The mean percentage breast density across right and left mediolateral oblique (MLO) views was used as the measure of MBD. LIBRA was applied to raw DICOM full-field digital mammography images obtained using routine institutional screening protocols during the study period, and its automated breast boundary and dense-tissue segmentation outputs were reviewed for gross failures (e.g., incomplete breast boundary). Participants with such failures were excluded from analyses requiring MBD. In total, 10 mammograms from 10 participants were excluded due to quality control failure of automated segmentation.

BMD was assessed using DXA scans (Hologic Discovery, Hologic Inc., Marlborough, MA, USA) at two anatomical sites: the lumbar spine (L1-L4) and femoral neck. For each participant, the lowest T-score from these two sites was used for analysis. Osteoporosis was defined according to WHO criteria as T-score ≤ − 2.5.

Comprehensive demographic, clinical, and lifestyle data were collected from electronic medical records, including age, BMI (kg/m²), and medication use (antihypertensive agents, lipid-lowering drugs, diabetes medications, and painkillers). Lifestyle factors, including smoking status (never, former, current), alcohol consumption (yes or no), and self-reported regular exercise (yes or no), were also recorded based on the health screening questionnaire. Parity was recorded as yes/no (any childbirth). For descriptive purposes, BMI categories were also summarized using Asian cut points as follows: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5 to < 23 kg/m²), overweight (23 to < 25 kg/m²), and obesity (≥ 25 kg/m²) [20].

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using the Student t test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate, according to distributional characteristics. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were small. The primary analysis used multivariable logistic regression with osteoporosis as the outcome and included age (continuous), BMI (continuous), continuous MBD (%), and a BMI×MBD interaction term. Age and BMI were selected a priori as the main adjustment variables because of their established associations with both osteoporosis risk and mammographic breast density. The significance of the BMI×MBD interaction term was assessed using a likelihood ratio test (LRT) comparing models with and without the interaction term. To aid interpretation, we reported the conditional odds ratio (OR) for osteoporosis per 1% higher MBD at BMI values of 20, 25, and 30, which were selected as representative interpretable points spanning lower BMI to obesity. Prespecified categorical sensitivity analyses evaluated a median split of MBD defined in the overall group; within BMI strata (< 25 and ≥ 25), we reported crude prevalence with 95% confidence intervals, risk differences (RD), and number needed to screen (NNS = 1/RD). Model discrimination was assessed using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) for an age + BMI model and an age + BMI + MBD model. Analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.3). Before fitting the logistic regression models, we assessed whether continuous predictors (age, BMI, and MBD) satisfied the linearity assumption in the logit and evaluated multicollinearity among predictors. No substantial violations were identified.

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at the breast screening center of Gangnam Severance Hospital after obtaining approval from the institutional review board (IRB) of Gangnam Severance Hospital (No. 3-2025-0002). This study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring ethical conduct and patient welfare. The need for patient consent was waived by the IRB due to the retrospective nature of the study. To protect patient privacy and confidentiality, all data were anonymized and handled in compliance with applicable institutional and local data protection policies.

Results

Among 7,143 postmenopausal women (mean age, 60.2 ± 7.2 years; mean BMI, 22.9 ± 3.0 kg/m²), osteoporosis was present in 912 (12.8%). Women with osteoporosis were older and had lower BMI, while mean MBD did not differ materially between groups in unadjusted comparisons (Table 1).

As a secondary descriptive analysis, we prespecified an MBD median split (25.3%) to provide absolute risk estimates in a more interpretable format. Within BMI ≥ 25, osteoporosis prevalence was 9.28% vs. 4.59% for low vs. high MBD (RD 4.69% [95% CI 0.23–8.27]; NNS 21.3), whereas the corresponding RD in BMI < 25 was smaller and imprecise (1.93% [95% CI − 0.66–4.54]) (Table 3). In an age-adjusted median-split model, the interaction between obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m²) and low MBD reached statistical significance (OR 1.83 [95% CI 1.01–3.31], P = 0.048), although stratum-specific adjusted estimates remained non-significant and imprecise. Adding MBD to an age + BMI model changed AUC from 0.682 to 0.684 (ΔAUC 0.002), indicating minimal incremental discrimination.

Discussion

We observed a BMI-dependent inverse association between automated mammographic breast density (MBD) and osteoporosis in postmenopausal women (Fig. 3). In the primary continuous interaction model, the inverse association between MBD and osteoporosis strengthened with increasing BMI, consistent with BMI acting as an effect modifier rather than a simple confounder. In an interaction model, the coefficient for MBD represents the conditional effect when BMI is zero; therefore, we emphasized BMI-specific conditional ORs at representative BMI values of 20, 25, and 30 kg/m² for more interpretable presentation. Although the per-1% change in OR appears modest, the interaction is more meaningfully interpreted across realistic differences in breast density and in terms of absolute risk. In this regard, the absolute risk separation was larger in women with BMI ≥ 25, among whom osteoporosis prevalence was 9.28% in the low-MBD group versus 4.59% in the high-MBD group.

Despite this BMI-dependent association, adding MBD to an age + BMI model produced only minimal incremental discrimination (ΔAUC 0.002). Therefore, these findings do not support automated MBD as a standalone screening or prediction tool for osteoporosis, nor do they indicate meaningful improvement in model performance beyond age and BMI alone. Instead, the present findings are better interpreted as providing biologically and epidemiologically informative insight into a BMI-dependent association rather than as supporting clinical risk stratification or decision-making based on MBD. Our primary model was intentionally parsimonious and focused on age and BMI as the key prespecified covariates, given that the aim of this study was to evaluate the BMI-dependent association between mammographic breast density and osteoporosis rather than to construct a fully adjusted prediction model. The directional change in the age-adjusted subgroup odds ratios in Table 3 should not be overinterpreted, as both estimates were non-significant and likely sensitive to dichotomization of MBD and small subgroup counts, particularly in the BMI ≥ 25/high-MBD subgroup. Accordingly, the continuous interaction model should be regarded as the primary analysis, whereas the median-split results are best viewed as descriptive support for interpretation.

Our results are biologically plausible given shared hormonal and metabolic influences on breast composition and bone health. Estrogen exposure is linked to both fibroglandular tissue and skeletal maintenance, whereas higher adiposity can increase BMD through mechanical loading while simultaneously lowering percent breast density. Prior studies have reported heterogeneous associations between breast density and BMD/osteoporosis, and differences in populations, adiposity distributions, and density measurement approaches likely contribute to these discrepancies [4–7, 21]. Modeling BMI as an effect modifier (BMI × MBD interaction) may help reconcile prior inconsistencies. Our interaction analysis was motivated by the known dependence of percent mammographic density on adiposity and by the heterogeneous prior literature on mammographic density and bone mineral density/osteoporosis [15].

Recent automated-density studies have supported the feasibility and reproducibility of quantitative mammographic density assessment in large digital mammography cohorts. In contrast, contemporary studies specifically examining the relationship between automated mammographic density and osteoporosis remain limited; thus, our study extends this contemporary imaging approach to a different but biologically related outcome in a routine screening population [22–24].

This study has limitations. First, the retrospective cross-sectional design limits causal inference and may introduce selection bias related to health screening utilization and the inclusion of only women with available mammography, DXA, and complete clinical data. Second, density was quantified using an area-based tool; volumetric density measures may yield different relationships. Third, the study was conducted at a single center in a Korean population, and the findings may not be directly generalizable to populations with different obesity prevalence, body composition, or mammographic breast density distributions. Fourth, although we adjusted for key factors, the observed association may still reflect residual confounding by metabolic or lifestyle factors rather than a direct biological relationship. Finally, categorical summaries in BMI ≥ 25 included relatively small numbers in the high-density category, contributing to imprecision of some stratum-specific estimates.

In conclusion, automated MBD associated a BMI-dependent inverse association with osteoporosis, with stronger inverse association at higher BMI. While mammographic breast density adds little incremental discrimination beyond age and BMI, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and do not support its use for clinical decision-making regarding DXA assessment. Prospective validation across diverse populations and density measurement methods is warranted.
