Magneto : Combining Small and Large Language Models for Schema Matching
Yurong Liu Eduardo Pena Aécio Santos Eden Wu Juliana Freire

Abstract
. Recent advances in language models opened new opportunities to address complex schema matching tasks. Schema matching approaches have been proposed that demonstrate the usefulness of language models, but they have also uncovered important limitations: Small language models (SLMs) require training data (which can be both expensive and challenging to obtain), and large language models (LLMs) often incur high computational costs and must deal with constraints imposed by context windows. We present Magneto , a cost-effective and accurate solution for schema matching that combines the advantages of SLMs and LLMs to address their limitations. By structuring the schema matching pipeline in two phases, retrieval and reranking, Magneto can use computationally efficient SLM-based strategies to derive candidate matches which can then be reranked by LLMs, thus making it possible to reduce runtime without compromising matching accuracy. We propose a self-supervised approach to fine-tune SLMs which uses LLMs to generate syntactically diverse training data, and prompting strategies that are effective for reranking. We also introduce a new benchmark, developed in collaboration with domain experts, which includes real biomedical datasets and presents new challenges to schema matching methods. Through a detailed experimental evaluation, using both our new and existing benchmarks, we show that Magneto is scalable and attains high accuracy for datasets from different domains.

Schema Matching and Language Models.

There has been renewed interest in data integration given the new capabilities introduced by language models
(
Feuer et al. 2024
;
Narayan et al. 2022
)
. For schema matching, approaches have been proposed that have shown promising results
(
Castro Fernandez et al. 2018
;
Cappuzzo et al. 2020
;
Du et al. 2024
;
Tu et al. 2023
)
.
A key challenge in schema matching is the estimation similarities between two columns. Pre-trained Language Models (PLMs)
,referred to as SLMs in this paper to distinguish them from large language models (LLMs),
support the creation of representations, or embeddings, for columns enriched with semantic information.
The similarity between two embeddings can serve as a proxy for column-matching scores
(
Castro Fernandez et al. 2018
;
Cappuzzo et al. 2020
)
.
SLMs have also been fine-tuned for schema matching
(
Du et al. 2024
)
and general matching tasks
(
Tu et al. 2023
)
, leading to accuracy gains compared to the general models.

LLMs are trained using large, generic data corpora and thus contain knowledge that can assist in obtaining additional semantics necessary to identify matches. Prompting strategies combined with fine-tuned models have been shown effective to improve table understanding and help in integration tasks
(
Li et al. 2024a
)
.

While prior work has shown the usefulness of SLMs and LLMs for SM, they also present significant practical practical challenges:

\bullet

Challenge 1
: Fine-tuning SLMs can lead to significant performance improvements, but this requires the availability of manually curated training data, which can be expensive to create.

\bullet

Challenge 2
: LLMs usually do not need fine-tuning, but they have a fixed context window: if a prompt exceeds this limit, it may need to be truncated before it is sent to the model.
Since small context windows are common in LLMs, this can lead to losing critical context and limit their use for large schemata and tables.
While recent LLMs are starting to support larger windows, their cost scales with input and output size.
Besides, recent studies have shown that the accuracy of LLMs can significantly decrease when given long prompts for complex tasks
(
Li et al. 2024b
)
. Last but not least, calls to LLM APIs can have high latency, especially for large prompts.

Our Approach.

We introduce
Magneto
, a framework that combines small (pre-trained) language models (SLMs) and large language models (LLMs) to derive cost-effective and general solutions for SM. As illustrated in Figure
2
,
Magneto
is structured in two phases:
candidate retrieval
selects a subset of the possible matches; and reranker, which
ranks the candidates to make it easier for users to examine and select matches.
We use this framework to explore three new strategies that use SLMs and LLMs: Using SLM as the candidate retriever and LLMs as the reranker;
an automated, LLM-powered fine-tuning process for SLMs; and a combination of these.
We propose new methods that address the context window and cost challenges associated with LLMs and the creation of training data to fine-tune SLMs.

To address
Challenge 1
, we leverage LLMs to automate the process of fine-tuning SLMs. Instead of relying on manually created training data and structure-based augmentation (e.g., row shuffling and sampling
(
Fan et al. 2023
;
Du et al. 2024
)
), we use LLMs to derive data. Using LLMs, we can add syntactic diversity and capture different representations for column names and their values.

We use a multi-pronged approach to address
Challenge 2
. By structuring the matching process in two phases,
Magneto
can use cheaper SLM-based methods for filtering candidates, thus reducing the number of matches that need to be checked by more costly rerankers, e.g., LLM-based rerankers, thereby reducing the cost and latency.
We must also ensure that all necessary details are included in the prompt while staying within the context window limit. Thus we need to create representative samples the contents of the columns in candidate matches to be ranked by the LLM.
In addition, LLMs are designed for textual data. To use them for tabular data, we must serialize the column content. Selecting the right serialization strategy is still an open research problem that has attracted substantial attention
(
Fang et al. 2024
;
Dong and Wang 2024
)
.
We explore different alternatives for both sampling and serialization.

Our ultimate goal is to devise strategies that have high accuracy and low cost.
However, there is often a trade-off: high-accuracy is associated with higher costs and running times.
Figure
1
illustrates the trade-offs between runtime and accuracy for existing schema matching algorithms and
Magneto
.
Traditional methods tend to cluster at relatively high accuracy with long runtimes or relatively low runtimes with reduced accuracy. In contrast,
Magneto
variants strike a balance across both dimensions and have the advantage of requiring no prior training data.

We conduct an extensive experimental study of
Magneto
on various datasets from two benchmarks. The first is a new benchmark, that we developed in collaboration with domain experts, which includes real biomedical datasets.
Our experiments show that many existing SM solutions struggle with this benchmark, particularly given the complexity and syntactic variability present in its datasets.
In contrast,
Magneto
demonstrates superior accuracy and generally faster runtimes.
We also evaluate
Magneto
on the Valentine
benchmark
(
Koutras et al. 2021
)
and observe that it also performs well for the Valentine datasets which cover a variety of domains.

Figure 2
.
Magneto
overview. Given source and target tables, the framework identifies matches in two phases: A small language model, automatically fine-tuned using LLM-generated data, retrieves and ranks candidate matches, which are then assessed and reranked by an LLM. This process makes it possible to efficiently identify matches and generalize across domains and datasets. Different schema matching strategies can be derived by adopting different combinations of methods for these phases.

Contributions.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

\bullet

We introduce the
Magneto
framework that effectively combines SLMs and LLMs and allows the creation of SM strategies that attain a balance between accuracy and runtime for diverse data and SM tasks (Section
2
).

\bullet

We propose an LLM-powered method that automatically generates training data needed to fine-tune SLMs, making it possible to
effectively use these low-cost models for specific SM tasks.
We also present a fine-tuning pipeline that uses contrastive learning and, by combining triplet loss and online triplet mining, effectively utilizes the generated data to enhance the discriminative power of column embeddings for
SM (Section
3
).

\bullet

Unlike previous approaches that use LLMs for SM,
we show that reranking candidate matches with LLMs, derived from less costly methods, achieves high accuracy at lower costs. (Section
4

\bullet

In collaboration with biomedical researchers, we created a new benchmark that represents a real schema matching effort
(
Li et al. 2023
)
, encompassing characteristics not present in existing benchmarks and presents new challenges for SM methods (Section

).

\bullet

We perform an extensive experimental evaluation of different strategies derived using our framework, comparing them against SM methods over datasets that cover a wide range of domains. Motivation and Solution Overview

Biomedical Data Integration: Opportunities and Challenges.

There have been substantial investments in infrastructure to share biomedical data
(
National Institutes of Health 2020
)
. Today, many datasets are available in repositories and published with scientific articles
(
pdc 2023
;
National Cancer Institute 2024a
)
. The ability to pool these data can advance research in multiple diseases and across different populations. However, established technologies are limited in their ability to integrate these data.
Our research was motivated by this challenge as part of a collaboration with biomedical researchers in the context of the ARPA-H Biomedical Data Fabric (BDF) program
(
ARPA-H 2024
)
, which aims to develop data integration and usability technologies.

We conducted formative interviews with biomedical experts and found that they rely heavily on manual processes and dataset-specific scripts to perform schema matching
(
Mertins et al. 2016
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Cheng et al. 2024
)
.

The example described in Section
1
illustrates some of the difficulties encountered in one study.
Besides the time required, the process they follow is error-prone and difficult to reproduce.
As data integration is a key step in most of their studies, they would benefit from automated (and semi-automated) techniques that streamline integration and are also transparent and reproducible.

We have identified two important requirements based on the researchers’ needs. They need approaches that:
generalize
across different kinds of data (e.g., genomics, proteomics, electronic health records), for different diseases (e.g., different types of cancers, auto-immune and rare diseases), from many sources (e.g., publications, hospitals, data commons); and that

facilitate curation
, since in many cases it’s difficult (even for experts) to determine correct matches. These requirements have guided the design of

.

2.1.
Definitions and Evaluation Metrics

Before we describe our approach, we introduce the terminology used in the remainder of this paper.
We formally define the problem we are addressing and the performance metrics we use to evaluate the methods we propose for schema matching.

Definition 2.0.

(Schema Matching)

Let
\mathbf{S}(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
be a source table and
\mathbf{T}(B_{1},\dots,B_{m})
be a target table, where
A_{i}\in\mathbf{S}
and
B_{j}\in\mathbf{T}
are columns that define the schemata. Each column
A\in\mathbf{S}\cup\mathbf{T}
has an associated
domain
, denoted
\mathcal{D}(A)
, representing the set of possible values that the column can take; note that
\mathcal{D}(A)
may be empty.
Schema matching focuses on aligning the table schemata by establishing correspondences between columns representing the same real-world concept or entity. A matching algorithm (or matcher) aims to identify pairs
(A_{i},B_{j})
that represent the same (or semantically equivalent) column based on various factors, such as their domains and names.
Thus, a matcher
\mathcal{M}
can be seen as a function that generates a schema mapping
M\subseteq\mathbf{S}\times\mathbf{T}
, where each element
(A,B)\in M
represents a correspondence between a source column
A
and a target column
B
,
where
\mathcal{D}(A)\approx\mathcal{D}(B)
, meaning that the domains of columns
A
and
B
are related or overlap.
∎

Matching algorithms often associate a score with each match they derive. These scores are used to generate ranked lists containing the derived matches, which help users explore matches by prioritizing the highest-scoring candidates. These lists provide a global ranking of best matches
(A_{i},B_{j})
among all pairs of possible matches of a given pair of tables
S
and
T
or a per-column ranking of the best matches
B_{j}
for a given source attribute
A_{i}\in\textbf{S}
.
Thus, a common approach to evaluate schema matching methods is to assess their ability to produce high-quality ranked lists of matches
(
Koutras et al. 2021
;
Du et al. 2024
)
.
We use two measures in our evaluation, Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) and Recall at Ground-Truth Size (Recall@GT), which
capture different aspects of matching methods:
user experience and result completeness.

Definition 2.0.

(Mean Reciprocal Rank)

Let
matches[A]
denote the ranked list of matches produced by a schema-matching algorithm for the source column
A\in\textbf{S}
, and
r_{A}
be the position of the first correct target column
B
within the ordered list
matches[A]
.
The reciprocal rank (RR) of an individual column
A
is the multiplicative inverse of the rank, i.e.,
\frac{1}{r_{A}}
. For instance, the RR is
1
if the match is found in the first place,
\frac{1}{2}
for the second place,
\frac{1}{3}
for the third place, and so on.
The
mean reciprocal rank
(MRR) for a table
S
is the average RR over the subset of columns
\textbf{S}^{\prime}
that contain a correct match in the ground truth:

(1)

\text{MRR}=\frac{1}{|\textbf{S}^{\prime}|}\sum_{A\in\mathbf{S}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{r_{A}}.

Intuitively, MMR measures how long it takes to find the first relevant match when examining the ranked list of matches. ∎

MRR is commonly used to evaluate methods that produce a ranked list of items, including the evaluation of search engine results and responses derived by question-answering systems
(
Schütze et al. 2008
;
Voorhees et al. 1999
)
.
In short, MRR evaluates the quality of the ranking.
The prioritization of the top-ranked correct result aligns with an enhanced user experience. In our problem, this corresponds to ranking the most relevant match for a given source column as highly as possible: a high MRR score indicates that a user can find the correct match more easily when judging multiple matches for a column.

We also use the recall at ground truth size (Recall@GT), a standard measure used in recent schema matching works
(
Koutras et al. 2021
)
.
In contrast to MRR, Recall@GT is computed over a global ranking of the matches, i.e., instead of considering the ranking for each source column, it considers a merged list of all matches produced.

Definition 2.0.

(Recall@GT)

Let
matches
denote the global ranked list containing all matches produced by a matcher that considers all pairs of possible matches between columns from
S
and
T
, and let
\mathcal{M}
denote a set containing only the top-
k
best results in
matches
.
Moreover, let
\mathcal{G}
be the set of ground truth matching pairs
(A,B)
. Recall@GT measures the fraction of relevant matches in the ground truth that also appears in
\mathcal{M}
,
where
k
is given by the size of the ground truth,
k=|\mathcal{G}|
. More formally,

(2)

\text{Recall@GT}=\frac{|\mathcal{G}\cap\mathcal{M}|}{k}\vskip-2.84544pt

Intuitively, it measures how well the matcher can place all correct matches of a table
S
at the top of the global ranked list.
∎

2.2.
Magneto
: Overview

Magneto
consists of two main components,
a candidate retriever and a reranker, which use small and large language models to efficiently derive accurate matches. The intuition is to first apply a cheaper approach to retrieve and filter candidates so that a more sophisticated (and expensive) methods can be used to accurately identify the correct matches from a smaller candidate set. By doing so, we reduce the overall cost of schema matching and, at the same time, attain high accuracy.
Figure
2
shows one variant of
Magneto
that uses a fine-tuned language model as the retriever, and a large language model as the reranker.
The use of language models enables
Magneto
to serve as general solution that supports schema matching across multiple domains.

Candidate Retriever.

The
candidate retriever
leverages SLMs to generate a ranked list of potential matches from the target table for each input column.
It uses column embeddings to estimate column pair similarities
(
Cappuzzo et al. 2020
;
Fan et al. 2023
)
. SLMs are a good choice for this step given their ability to capture semantic similarity and efficiency.

Pre-trained (generic) SLMs such as BERT
(
Devlin 2018
)
, RoBERTa
(
Liu 2019
)
, and MPNet
(
Song et al. 2020
)
, can struggle with nuanced syntactic differences and lack contextual knowledge for complex schema matching tasks involving domains not represented in their training data.
To perform complex tasks effectively, these models need to be fine tuned. We propose to leverage LLMs to automatically generate the data needed to fine tune SLMs. We show that LLMs can help derive high-quality training data that reflect instances of variability for semantically similar columns that arise in real data. This approach, which we describe in Section
3
, improves the robustness of the SLM-based retriever, making it possible for it handle complex matches without requiring human-labeled training data.
We refer to
Magneto
configurations that use this approach using the label
ft
.
Note that
Magneto
-
ft
only invokes LLMs once, during the training phase for a given domain. Once trained, the fine-tuned SLM can perform multiple inferences efficiently without making calls to LLMs.

Match Reranker.

While fine-tuning improves the performance of SLMs, it is not sufficient to handle schema matching tasks that include domains and heterogeneity not covered during the fine tuning. To improve generalizability,
Magneto
uses LLMs as
rerankers
to refine the candidates identified by the SLM-based retriever. This approach, which we refer to as
Magneto
-
llm
, enhances accuracy by leveraging carefully designed prompts and techniques that enable the LLM to judiciously assess matches and discern subtle semantic nuances, which are challenging for the SLM to detect independently. As discussed in Section
4
,
Magneto
-
llm
also strives to minimize costs by optimizing LLM resources.

Varying Retrievers and Rerankers.
The architecture of
Magneto
allows the combination of different alternatives for retrievers and rerankers.
This is useful for studying the tradeoffs of various approaches and to derive solutions that match the accuracy requirements and computational constraints for different applications.

For the candidate retriever, we can combine traditional matching techniques with the embedding strategies. For example, when columns in a match have the same name, module minor variations in case and punctuation, we can assign a perfect similarity score of 1.0 to the match.
During our experiments, this simple technique consistently improved overall accuracy, albeit slightly, in most cases.

We implemented an alternative match reranker, the
bipartite-graph reranker
, which adapts the a filtering technique from
(
Melnik et al. 2002
)
. This approach offers an algorithmic alternative for reranking, making it particularly suitable for scenarios where LLMs are unavailable or where strict runtime constraints must be met.
This solution combines all ranked match candidates of each source column into one global ranked list and transforms it into an undirected bipartite graph where the nodes represent columns. There are two (disjoint) node sets consisting of the columns in the source and target tables (respectively).
A match is represented as an edge in the graph, with a corresponding score as its edge weight.
To identify the
best
match for the set of attributes in the source column, it uses the algorithm from
(
Crouse 2016
)
, which solves the assignment problem in polynomial time and is efficient even for large graphs.
We prioritize the matches in the assignment by placing the corresponding edges at the top of the list. To account for the remaining matches, we move them to the bottom of the list while preserving their relative order..

3.
Using LLMs to Fine-Tune SLMs

SLMs for Candidate Match Retrieval.

Pre-trained small language models (SLMs)
have been used to help with schema-matching related tasks by encoding semantic information from column names and values into dense vector representations–
embeddings

(
Tu et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Fan et al. 2023
;
Cong et al. 2023
;
Dong et al. 2023
;
Du et al. 2024
)
. To identify matches, embeddings of source and target columns
can be compared using
cosine similarity
: high similarity scores indicate a higher likelihood that the columns match.

SLMs are pre-trained on and work well for general natural language tasks, but their ability to interpret tabular data is limited. These models must often be fine-tuned to handle tasks that involve tables.
However, fine-tuning approaches require large amounts of labeled data for training
(
Tu et al. 2023
;
Dong et al. 2023
)
.
Unfortunately, this is impractical in many scenarios such as the integration of biomedical data (Example
1.1
), for which training data is hard to obtain.

In the absence of training data, it is possible to apply augmentation techniques to automatically generate variations of data to be used as positive examples. For example, given a column, different versions can be generated by shuffling rows, sampling values, and applying perturbations to values
(
Fan et al. 2023
;
Dong et al. 2023
)
. However, these variations may not fully capture the heterogeneity found in real data.

We introduce a new method that leverages LLMs to generate training data and present a pipeline to fine tune SLMs for schema matching (Section
3.2
).
Another important consideration in the use of SLMs, that we discuss this in Section
3.1
,
is how to represent tables and their columns.

.

3.1.
Column Serialization and Value Sampling

Column Serialization.

Since SLMs interpret inputs as sequences of tokens (i.e., regular text), we must transform each column into a token sequence that the model can process. Recent research has explored various approaches to serializing columns into a textual format
(
Badaro et al. 2023
)
. Here, we explore the impact of different approaches to column serialization for schema matching.

Given column
A
with column type
type(A)
and sample values
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{m}
, we consider the following approaches:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{default}}(A)

\displaystyle=\texttt{[CLS]}A\,\texttt{[SEP]}\,type(A)

\displaystyle\quad\texttt{[SEP]}\,v_{1}\,\texttt{[SEP]}\,\ldots\,\texttt{[SEP]}\,v_{m},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{verbose}}(A)

\displaystyle=\texttt{[CLS]}\text{Column: }A\,\texttt{[SEP]}\,\text{Type: }type(A)

\displaystyle\quad\texttt{[SEP]}\,\text{Values: }v_{1}\,\texttt{[SEP]}\,\ldots\,\texttt{[SEP]}\,v_{m},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{repeat}}(A)

\displaystyle=\texttt{[CLS]}\underbrace{A\,\texttt{[SEP]}\dots\texttt{[SEP]}\,A\,\texttt{[SEP]}}_{\text{k times}}\,type(A)

\displaystyle\quad\texttt{[SEP]}\,v_{1}\,\texttt{[SEP]}\,\ldots\,\texttt{[SEP]}\,v_{m},

where the [CLS] token is used as a special symbol to indicate the start of a column, and [SEP] separates the components within a serialized column.

\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{default}}
is a variant of a widely-adopted serialization strategy
(
Suhara et al. 2022
)
. Instead of employing commas to separate sample values, we utilize the
[SEP]
token. This modification prevents the model from interpreting the values as an ordered text sequence, thereby treating them as unordered, discrete features. This change is crucial in schema matching as it emphasizes the structured and independent nature of the data in each column, enabling more accurate alignment of similar but non-identical schemata.

\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{verbose}}
is an extension of
\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{default}}
where prefixes are added to delineate each component (i.e., column name and values) and provide additional context for the SLM.

\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{repeat}}
repeats the column name multiple times to reinforce its importance, nudging the model to prioritize column names.
It attempts to mitigate attention drift in SLMs, which can disproportionately focus on values rather than on column names, especially in zero-shot settings. Specifically, we chose
k=5
because it strikes a balance between reinforcing column names without overwhelming the model with redundancy.

In addition to column name and values, we incorporate data types into the column representation. For column type inference, we classify columns into basic types: numerical, categorical, date, or binary. We classify columns with a high proportion of unique values (e.g., over 90% distinct values) as “key” columns, as they often represent unique identifiers. When column value is unavailable or type detection fails, we label the column type as “unknown”.

Priority Sampling for Column Values.

To efficiently retrieve candidate matches, we generate embeddings by including a sample of column values in the serialized column representation.
Different sampling techniques have been explored for various table management tasks, such as column type annotation
(
Feuer et al. 2024
)
and table union search
(
Fan et al. 2023
)
. A common approach is to use weighted sampling and assign higher weights to more frequent values. We adopt this approach and incorporate coordination into the sampling process with
priority sampling

(
Daliri et al. 2024
)
.
Priority sampling was used for inner product sketching and aimed to maximize the likelihood of selecting corresponding values across vectors, emphasizing those with larger magnitudes.

In our setting, priority sampling is adapted to increase the probability of sampling similar values
during the fine-tuning and inference stages.
Specifically, we use a random seed
s
to select a uniformly random hash function
h:\{1,\dots,N\}\to[0,1]
, where
N
represents the maximum number of unique values across all columns. For each value
v_{i}
, we compute a rank:

R_{i}=\frac{\text{freq}(v_{i})}{h(v_{i})},

where
\text{freq}(v_{i})
denotes the frequency of
v_{i}
. Then, we select the sample’s first
m
values with the largest priorities
R_{i}
.

In
Magneto
, we use serialization as a
hyperparameter
. We study the impact
of different serialization strategies for schema matching in Section
6
, where we also investigate different approaches to sampling column values.
We experimented with varying column value sample sizes (from 10 to 30) and observed only marginal differences.
Interestingly, smaller sample sizes (e.g., ten values) sometimes outperformed larger ones, likely because of reduced noise. Thus, we fixed the sample size to 10 as a default.

3.2.
LLM-Powered Fine Tuning

By fine-tuning a model, our goal is for it to learn column representations so that its embeddings can spatially cluster related columns. This spatial arrangement helps the retrieval of semantically related representations using cosine similarity. With this goal in mind, the basic intuition behind our training methodology is to minimize the distance between embeddings of identical or semantically-related columns while maximizing the distance between distinct columns.

LLM-derived Training Data.

As discussed above, an important challenge in fine-tuning lies in obtaining high-quality training data.
With the goal of capturing syntactic heterogeneity common in real datasets, we introduce LLM-based augmentation (
llm-aug
), an approach that generates variations of columns that are semantically equivalent but syntactically different.
The derived columns serve as the training data fine-tuning process, which adopts a self-supervised contrastive learning approach (Figure
3
).

Synthetic columns are derived from an input (anchor) column as follows:
1) The anchor name and a sample of its values to the LLM using the structured prompt depicted in Figure
4
, and 2)
the LLM outputs columns that are semantically similar to the anchor. Examples of synthetic columns are shown in Table
2
. This method establishes a class of columns considered matches (derived from the same anchor); columns within the same class serve as positive
examples and those from different classes as negative examples.

Triplet Loss and Online Triplet Mining.

To leverage the generated synthetic columns, we implement a contrastive learning framework using
triplet loss
and
online triplet mining

(
Schroff et al. 2015
)
. Triplet loss improves upon previous contrastive loss functions by more directly optimizing for relative similarities, which, as we discovered in our experiments, yields more precise schema alignments.

In the training pipeline, each triplet consists of an
anchor
column
a
, a
positive
column
p
, and a
negative
column
n
;
a
and
p
are semantically related (i.e., they belong to the same class), while
n
is drawn from a different class. The goal is to learn an embedding space such that the distance between the
a
and
p
is minimized while the distance between
a
and
n
is maximized.
Specifically, we incorporate Batch Hard triplet loss
(
Schroff et al. 2015
)
to refine the learning of discriminative embeddings for column classification. Given
P
classes and
K
columns per class, the loss function is defined as follows:

(3)

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{P}\sum_{a=1}^{K}\big[\max\big(0,\,m+\max_{p=1\dots K}D(a,p)-\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\dots P\\
n=1\dots K\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}D(a,n)\big)\big]

Here,
a
is the anchor column embedding from a specific class
i
,
p
is the positive column embedding within the same class as the anchor, and
n
is the negative column embedding from a different class. The function
D
denotes the distance metric, cosine similarity in our case, which measures the similarity between embeddings. The margin
m
is a hyperparameter that ensures a minimum distance between the positive and negative embeddings relative to the anchor, thereby enhancing the separability between classes. This loss function iterates over all classes (
P
) and multiple examples per class (
K
) within each training batch, optimizing the embeddings to emphasize inter-class distinctions and intra-class similarities.

Additionally, we apply
Online Triplet Mining

(
Schroff et al. 2015
;
Hermans et al. 2017
)
, which enhances the learning process by dynamically selecting the most challenging positive and negative examples within each training batch. This technique prioritizes triplets that maximize learning efficiency, specifically focusing on:

\bullet

Hardest Positives:
Triplets where the distance between the anchor and the positive is maximized:

D(a,p)
is maximized such that
D(a,p)<D(a,n)

\bullet

Hardest Negatives:
Triplets where the distance between the anchor and the negative is minimized, yet still greater than the anchor-positive distance plus the margin:

D(a,n)
is minimized but still
D(a,n)>D(a,p)+m

\bullet

Semi-Hard Negatives:
These are negatives that are not the closest to the anchor but are still closer than the positive plus the margin:

D(a,p)<D(a,n)<D(a,p)+m

These conditions help the model not settle for easy examples and instead learn to distinguish subtle differences, developing more robust and discriminative features to distinguish columns.
By combining triplet loss and online triplet mining, our model can effectively utilize the synthetic columns to enhance the discriminative power of the embeddings for
schema matching.

Model Selection for Schema Matching.

To select the optimal fine-tuned model during training, we must define an effective validation metric specifically tailored for effective schema matching tasks.
Our selection process uses the measures described in Section
2
, mean reciprocal rank (MRR) and recall at ground truth (Recall@GT), to evaluate model performance on synthetic data.
Since no ground-truth data is available, we rely on synthetic datasets to simulate real-world scenarios. These metrics are specifically chosen to ensure that the model identifies correct matches and ranks them in a manner that reflects their true relevance.

The intuition behind using these metrics, rather than a traditional loss metric, lies in their ability to reflect the practical requirements of schema matching. Loss metrics typically provide a general sense of model error but do not differentiate between the types of errors that are more consequential for the task. In schema matching, failing to rank a highly relevant match at the top (as evaluated by MRR) or missing a relevant match entirely (as evaluated by recall) can be more detrimental than the errors captured by average loss.

We compute a validation score that averages the MRR and recall at ground truth:

\text{Validation Score}=\big(\text{MRR}+\text{Recall@GT}\big)/2.

We implement early stopping when the validation accuracy remains unchanged for 5 epochs, selecting the model with the highest validation score as the best fine-tuned model.

4.
LLMs as Rerankers

SLMs can serve as efficient retrievers, but they may fail to capture complex semantic relationships.

Moreover, models fine-tuned with LLM-derived data are inherently domain-specific.
To mitigate these limitations,
Magneto
incorporates a Large Language Model (LLM) as a reranker.
Unlike existing methods that adjust match rankings based on heuristic rules and similarity metrics scores
(
Madhavan et al. 2001
;
Melnik et al. 2002
;
Do and Rahm 2002
;
Gal 2006
;
Radwan et al. 2009
;
Alserafi et al. 2020
)
,
Magneto
leverages the knowledge accumulated by the LLM to complement the initial retrieval and improve the overall performance of SM.

A natural question arises: Why not just use an LLM for SM? While there are approaches that employ LLMs for SM
(
Li et al. 2024a
;
Parciak et al. 2024
)
, their applicability in scenarios that involve many or large tables is limited due to high computational costs and
challenges related to context windows (
Challenge 2
, Section
1
). We posit that LLMs must be used judiciously and designed
Magneto
accordingly.

The core of our reranking approach is a carefully designed prompt template that converts the abstract task of column-pair similarity assessment into a more structured and interpretable process.
We designed this prompt inspired by recent lessons learned from column type annotation
(
Feuer et al. 2024
)
and trends in prompt engineering
(
White et al. 2023
;
Narayan et al. 2022
)
.

shows the structure of the prompt.

Scoring-Oriented Prompt Design.

The
Scoring-Oriented Instruction
is a critical component of the prompt that aligns the model with the schema matching task. Specifically, this instruction involves sending one source column and the top
k
candidate target columns retrieved by the SLM to the LLM. Previous works, such as
(
Li et al. 2024a
)
and
(
Parciak et al. 2024
)
, typically employ table-wise prompts that ask LLMs to generate a ranked list without providing individual scores, which poses scalability challenges when entire tables are involved. Furthermore, column-wise ranking without scoring prevents effective table-wise comparisons, such as evaluating the recall of matches against the ground truth (recall@GT) for each pair of source and target columns.

To address these limitations, we propose a novel prompt design that requires the model to assign scores from 0.00 to 1.00 to each column pair, rather than merely producing a ranked list. This not only facilitates direct comparisons across different pairs but also allows the model to adopt a holistic view by considering all
k
target columns simultaneously during scoring. Additionally, our serialization of the column data clarifies to the model that a text sequence comprising a column name followed by its values represents a column in a relational table. This assists in more accurately scoring matches based not only on the semantic and contextual relevance but also on the structural characteristics of the column data.

Few-Shot In-Context Learning.

By providing a few examples of a task to an LLM,
few-shot learning
can lead to significant improvement in LLM performance
(
Brown 2020
;
Dong et al. 2022
)
.
This method has been shown effective in various data management tasks, enabling robust performance without the need for extensive fine-tuning
(
Hegselmann et al. 2023
;
Shraga et al. 2020
)
.

In the
One-shot Example
strategy, we provide a single example of the schema matching task, which outputs a ranked list with scores. This approach clarifies both the objective of the task and expected output format, as well as establishes a uniform scoring standard, enhancing the comparability of matches across all source and target column pairs. Utilizing only one example helps maintain a lightweight prompt structure, crucial for minimizing input and output token counts. The number of tokens directly impacts runtime and cost, and it can also influence accuracy
(
Li et al. 2024b
)
.

Optimization of Model Cost.

For the implementation of
Magneto
, we use
GPT-4o-mini
, a low-cost LLM, which has shown good performance for our prompts. To ensure reliability, we attempt to parse the model’s output up to three times. If parsing fails after these attempts, we revert to using the embedding scores as a fallback mechanism. During testing, output-related issues were infrequent; however, they occurred more commonly with larger tables and in scenarios lacking a candidate retriever.

Note that the design of
Magneto
makes it possible to balance accuracy with computational cost. Based on operational constraints (or requirements), the number of candidates sent to the reranker can be adjusted. The scores for candidates not assessed by the reranker are normalized such that the maximum score aligns with the lowest score received from the reranker. Therefore, a complete ranked list can still be returned to the user.

5.
The GDC Benchmark

A key problem in evaluating schema-matching algorithms is the availability of benchmarks that cover a wide variety of challenges present in real data. While there have been efforts to address this issue, benchmarks are often synthetically fabricated from real data
(
Koutras et al. 2021
;
Das et al. [n.d.]
)
, which can introduce biases.
Moreover, as we show in Section
6
(and as seen in
(
Koutras et al. 2021
)
), publicly available benchmarks based on real data such as Magellan
(
Das et al. [n.d.]
)
and WikiData
(
Koutras et al. 2021
)
are quickly becoming “saturated” since many algorithms attain near-perfect performance and leave small room for algorithmic improvements. This makes it difficult to extract useful insights about the strengths of different algorithms.

To address this problem, we developed a new benchmark dataset based on the real data harmonization scenario described in Example
1.1
.
We collaborated with experts from a medical school team to design a benchmark that reflects the challenges they face when working with biomedical data. We obtained datasets from ten studies related to tumor analysis
(
Cao et al. 2021
;
Clark et al. 2019
;
Dou et al. 2020
;
Gillette et al. 2020
;
McDermott et al. 2020
;
Huang et al. 2021
;
Krug et al. 2020
;
Satpathy et al. 2021
;
Vasaikar et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2021
)
, and, with the help of the experts, manually aligned and matched these datasets to the Genomics Data Commons (GDC) standard
(
National Cancer Institute 2024a
)
.

The GDC is a program of the US National Institutes of Health responsible for receiving, processing, and distributing genomic, clinical, and biospecimen data from cancer research initiatives.
Its standard dictionary describes data using a graph model that includes names and descriptions for nodes and attributes, as well as acceptable values for some attributes.
Still, for compatibility with existing solutions, we transformed the model to a relational schema that contains only column names and domain information (i.e., we disregard column descriptions).
We created a simplified table reflecting the GDC format, the “target” table, listing domain values for each column without repetition. In a future version, it is possible to further extend the benchmark to include multiple tables, and column and value descriptions.

Table
1
illustrates a few samples of the data published by Dou et al.
(
Dou et al. 2020
)
alongside their corresponding GDC format. As we can observe, matching clinical data with the GDC standard poses several challenges, including terminology mismatches and data format variations.
The benchmark includes 10 pairs of input-target tables. The number of columns in the source tables ranges from 16 to 179, and the number of rows ranges from 93 to 225. Our simplified GDC target schema comprises a single table with 736 columns. While some columns have a small number of distinct values (e.g., binary
yes/no
attributes), some contain up to 4478 distinct values.
The ground truth was manually curated by multiple annotators, who used a mix of manual and automated methods for identifying possible candidate matches (e.g., GDC search tools
(
National Cancer Institute 2024b
)
and bdi-kit
(
The bdi-kit contributors 2024
)
).
Given that the correctness of some matches is very challenging to determine even for bioinformatics experts (e.g., it may require reading the original papers or asking data producers), the final match decisions were made by consensus based on what users would expect from an algorithm given the limited context.

In zero-shot settings,
\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{repeat}}
consistently outperforms other methods, demonstrating the effectiveness of emphasizing column header when training data is absent.
\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{verbose}}
is more effective in fine-tuned scenarios, demonstrating the benefits of integrating semantic details when domain-specific training is applied. The Header Only approach shows the lowest performance in all datasets, confirming that column headers alone are insufficient for effective schema matching in complex scenarios.

The results further support our assumption that that fine-tuning can greatly improve the performance gains on the GDC dataset. This improvement is likely related to the requirement for external knowledge, which we capture with the LLM-derived training data.

Match Reranker

We performed two experiments to evaluate the match reranker component. The first examines the impact of using the bipartite strategy (Section
2.2
). Figure
10
shows the absolute increase/decrease in accuracy from
Magneto
-zs, which directly returns the retriever matches, to
Magneto-zs-bp
, which applies the bipartite reranking to the matches produced by
the retriever.
Note that the bipartite approach can significantly improve Recall@sGT. All datasets benefit from the technique at some point, except Magellan, whose scores are already (very) high. The improvements can reach nearly 0.6 in absolute terms. The mean improvement varies across datasets; average recall scores across datasets other than Magellan range from 0.03 (GDC) to 0.17 (OpenData).
The improvements for MRR are lower but still sizeable for ChemL and Open Data. While reranking with bipartite generally works, we observed that it can occasionally lead to decreased MRR and Recall@GT.

Our final experiment evaluates: (1) how the LLM-based reranker improves upon the traditional bipartite approach, and (2) how its performance varies as we increase the number of match candidates sent to the LLM.
We focus on the GDC dataset, which has a higher column count. For this experiment, we used the zero-shot settings for the retriever.

shows accuracy and runtime for different numbers of candidates (

The LLM-based re-ranking approach improves over the bipartite. For example, at
k=5
, we observe a 12.3% absolute improvement in Recall@GT over the bipartite baseline, rising from 0.404 to 0.454. Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) demonstrates even more promising gains, from 0.551 to 0.605—a 9.8% increase.
While MRR continues to improve with more columns, processing all target table columns becomes computationally infeasible for large schemas. That is why we argue for combining a retriever with an LLM-based re-ranker for schema matching. Note that candidates that are not forwarded to the reranker are assigned lower scores, this
leads to a decrease in Recall@GT since it considers all pairs based on these scores.

As the number of columns increases, computational costs grow. The runtime reflects the latency of API-based LLM requests. However, we can expect similar trends with alternative LLM approaches, as prompts with increasing length become more challenging for LLMs
(
Wan et al. 2024
)
.
Still, the runtime remains practical and competitive for usable values of
k
, as demonstrated in our scalability experiment.
We used
k=20
for our other experiments, as it balances good MRR, Recall@GT, and practical runtime across GDC and other datasets.

7.
Related Work

A wide range of methods have been proposed for
schema matching
, an important research area in the database field
(
Rahm and Bernstein 2001
)
.
Related approaches to our work can be broadly categorized into three main groups: traditional methods, small language model-driven methods, and large language model-based methods.
Below, we review representative work in each category.

Traditional Methods.

Over the years, researchers have developed and combined various approaches for automatic schema matching
(
Bellahsene et al. 2011
;
Doan et al. 2012
;
Koutras et al. 2021
)
. A straightforward yet effective approach is detecting overlap in column names
(
Madhavan et al. 2001
;
Melnik et al. 2002
;
Do and Rahm 2002
)
and overlap in column values
(
Aumueller et al. 2005
;
Zhang et al. 2011
)
. Some incorporate relaxations when measuring overlaps, such as accounting for syntactic and semantic similarities between column names and values
(
Madhavan et al. 2001
;
Aumueller et al. 2005
)
. Others also consider factors like data type relevance and value distribution
(
Aumueller et al. 2005
;
Zhang et al. 2011
)
. Among these, the
COMA
algorithm stands out for integrating the most strategies and weighting their outputs to achieve better accuracy
(
Do and Rahm 2002
;
Aumueller et al. 2005
)
, often remaining competitive even against more recent approaches
(
Koutras et al. 2021
)
.

However, these approaches often struggle to capture complex relationships and deeper semantics within datasets, resulting in poor accuracy when identifying semantically valid matches
(
Khatiwada et al. 2023
)
.

Small Language Model-Driven Methods.

Small Language Models (SLMs) have transformed schema matching by using embeddings similarity
(
Cappuzzo et al. 2020
)
, effectively encoding and comparing textual and contextual data within columns.
Furthermore, contrastive learning refines SLMs’ ability to distinguish between matching and non-matching column pairs
(
Fan et al. 2023
;
Cong et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
. Meanwhile, synthetic tabular data generation facilitates self-supervised learning, allowing models to improve without ground truth
(
Fan et al. 2023
;
Du et al. 2024
)
.

Among these methods,
ISResMat
customizes pre-trained models for dataset-specific adaptation, generating training pairs from table fragments, and applying pairwise comparison losses to refine matching accuracy
(
Du et al. 2024
)
. It also addresses cardinality constraints and manages numerical columns with advanced techniques;
Unicorn
, a state-of-the-art general matching model, employs contrastive learning and a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layer within its architecture to discern matches but relies on supervised training
(
Tu et al. 2023
)
.

Large Language Model-Driven Methods.

Recent works have leveraged large language models (LLMs) for various aspects of tabular data management, predominantly focusing on single-table tasks
(
Kayali et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024a
;
Feuer et al. 2024
;
Hegselmann et al. 2023
)
. Some studies discuss LLM applications in schema matching, highlighting the potential of LLMs in this area. However, these approaches often rely solely on prompting strategies—either fine-tuned or zero-shot—which suffer from scalability issues and high computational costs
(
Li et al. 2024a
;
Parciak et al. 2024
;
Fang et al. 2024
;
Feng et al. 2024
)
.

Furthermore,
Sheetrit et al. 2024
and
Zhang et al. 2024
utilize zero-shot pre-trained LLMs for schema matching.
Sheetrit et al. 2024
addresses multiple-table matching, whereas our work focuses on the less-explored two-table schema matching, particularly for tables with numerous columns.
Zhang et al. 2024
uses rule-based feature extraction and trains an XGBoost classifier with gold data, a supervised approach distinct from our unsupervised method. Hence, we do not compare our findings directly with these studies.

8.
Conclusions

We have proposed
Magneto
, a novel framework that leverages small and large language models to derive schema matching strategies that generalize across domains and balance accuracy and runtime tradeoffs. We introduce a new benchmark that captures some of the complexities involved in biomedical data integration and that also presents new challenges for schema matching methods.
Through a detailed experimental evaluation, including comparisons against state of the art methods and ablations, we demonstrate the effectiveness of
Magneto
and our design choices.

Magneto
