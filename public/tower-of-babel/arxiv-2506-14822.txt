Analysis and conditional optimization of projection estimates for the distribution of random variable using Legendre polynomials
T. A. Averina, K. A. Rybakov

Abstract




Abstract.
Algorithms for jointly obtaining projection estimates of the density and distribution function of a random variable using the Legendre polynomials are proposed. For these algorithms, a problem of the conditional optimization is solved. Such an optimization allows one increasing the approximation accuracy with a minimum computational costs. The proposed algorithms are tested on examples with different degree of smoothness of the density.

Keywords:
Monte Carlo method, projection estimate, density, distribution function, Legendre polynomials, conditional optimization

1.
Introduction

Statistical projection estimates in the Monte Carlo method was first proposed by N.N. Chentsov
[
1
]
. He developed a general technique for optimizing such estimates, which, however, requires a clarification in specific problems
[
2
]
. In the paper
[
3
]
, projection estimates based on the Legendre polynomials for marginal probability densities of the solution to a stochastic differential equation were proposed. The mean square error of estimates was investigated, and a comparison of both the obtained projection estimates and the histogram was carried out on examples. Analysis of the results showed that, on the same sample size, the projection estimate more accurately approximates the density. In addition, such an estimate is specified analytically, and it is a smooth function. So, it is preferable, e.g., in filtering problems and in the control of nonlinear systems
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
.

This paper presents two statistical algorithms, based on algorithms using the Legendre polynomials from
[
3
]
, for jointly obtaining projection estimates of the density and distribution function of a random variable.

Solving different problems by statistical estimation algorithms, it is important to make an optimal (consistent) choice of parameters for these algorithms when finding the mathematical expectation of a certain functional depending on a random variable. Therefore, this paper analyzes the mean square error of the projection estimate from the point of view of the optimal choice of parameters. Such parameters are the projection expansion length and sample size. We solve a conditional optimization problem considered by G.A. Mikhailov
[
8
]
. The objective of this problem is to minimize the algorithm complexity while achieving the required level of approximation accuracy. The possibility of minimizing the mean square error of projection estimates of the density and distribution function is explored by balancing its deterministic and stochastic components. The accuracy of projection estimates also depends on the degree of smoothness of the density; therefore, in this paper, we consider the dependence of error not only on the projection expansion length, but also on the degree of smoothness of the approximated function.

The obtained theoretical results are confirmed on examples using a two-parameter family of densities that allow one both a choice of the degree of smoothness and a simple calculation of expansion coefficients with respect to the Legendre polynomials.

The rest of this paper has the following structure. Section
2
contains the necessary information on Fourier–Legendre series and definition of projection estimates of the density and distribution function; in this section, relations for expansion coefficients of the density and distribution function with respect to Legendre polynomials are obtained. Section
3
presents algorithms for jointly obtaining randomized projection estimates of the density and distribution function. The analysis and conditional optimization of randomized projection estimates are carried out in Section
4
. Section
5
proposes a two-parameter family of densities with different degrees of smoothness and related distribution functions; it presents an algorithm for modeling the corresponding random variables, obtains expansion coefficients of the considered densities and distribution functions, and studies the convergence rate of their expansions. Numerical experiments and their analysis are discussed in Section
6
. Brief conclusions on the paper are given in Section
7
.

2.
Using Legendre Polynomials for Randomized Projection Estimates

2.1.
Fourier–Legendre series

The standardized Legendre polynomials
\{P_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
are defined on the set
\Omega=[-1,1]
as follows
[
9
]
:

P_{i}(x)=\frac{1}{2^{i}i!}\,\frac{d^{i}(x^{2}-1)^{i}}{dx^{i}},\ \ \ i=0,1,2,\dots

They form a complete orthogonal system of functions in the space
L_{2}(\Omega)
and satisfy well-known recurrence relations:

\displaystyle(i+1)P_{i+1}(x)=(2i+1)xP_{i}(x)-iP_{i-1}(x),\ \ \ i=1,2,\dots,

(1)

\displaystyle(2i+1)P_{i}(x)=P^{\prime}_{i+1}(x)-P^{\prime}_{i-1}(x),\ \ \ i=1,2,\dots,

(2)

where
P_{0}(x)=1
and
P_{1}(x)=x
. These relations can be formally applied for
i=0
when
P_{-1}(x)=0
. Recall
[
10
]
that

L_{2}(\Omega)=\biggl\{u\colon\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{2}dx<\infty\biggr\}.

The explicit formula for the standardized Legendre polynomials is

P_{i}(x)=\frac{1}{2^{i}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\lfloor i/2\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{k}(2i-2k)!}{k!(i-k)!(i-2k)!}\,x^{i-2k},\ \ \ i=0,1,2,\dots,

(3)

where
\lfloor\,\cdot\,\rfloor
is the floor function. The explicit formulae can be specified separately for even indices (
i=2j
):

P_{2j}(x)=\frac{1}{2^{2j}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}\frac{(-1)^{k}(4j-2k)!}{k!(2j-k)!(2j-2k)!}\,x^{2j-2k},

(4)

and for odd indices (
i=2j+1
):

P_{2j+1}(x)=\frac{1}{2^{2j+1}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}\frac{(-1)^{k}(4j-2k+2)!}{k!(2j-k+1)!(2j-2k+1)!}\,x^{2j-2k+1}.

(5)

Using the normalization, we obtain the Legendre polynomials
\{\hat{P}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
, which form a complete orthonormal system of functions in
L_{2}(\Omega)
:

\hat{P}_{i}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2i+1}{2}}\,P_{i}(x),\ \ \ i=0,1,2,\dots

(6)

The expansion of an arbitrary function
u\in L_{2}(\Omega)
into the Fourier–Legendre series has the form

u(x)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}U_{i}\hat{P}_{i}(x),

(7)

where coefficients
U_{i}
are defined as inner products:

U_{i}=(u,\hat{P}_{i})_{L_{2}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}u(x)\hat{P}_{i}(x)dx,\ \ \ i=0,1,2,\dots

This expansion is a basis for the approximation of
u
:

u(x)\approx u^{\langle n\rangle}(x)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}U_{i}\hat{P}_{i}(x),

(8)

and the approximation accuracy is estimated depending on the chosen metric (or norm). For example, in
L_{2}(\Omega)
we have
[
11
]

\|u-u^{\langle n\rangle}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{C}{n^{s}},

(9)

where
C>0
does not depend on
n
, with the condition that generalized derivatives of
u
up to order
s
belong to
L_{2}(\Omega)
. There is also a sharper estimate
[
12
,
13
]
:

\|u-u^{\langle n\rangle}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leqslant\tilde{C}\,\biggl(\frac{(n-s+1)!}{(n+s+1)!}\biggr)^{1/2},

(10)

for which it is assumed that
\tilde{C}>0
does not depend on
n
, and generalized derivatives of
u
up to order
s
belong to
L_{2}(\Omega)
with weight
\rho(x)=(1-x^{2})^{s}
,
s\leqslant n+1
.

We also present an estimate corresponding to
C(\Omega)
, the space of all continuous functions on
\Omega

[
9
]
. If a function
u
is continuously differentiable
r
times on
\Omega
, i.e.,
u\in C^{r}(\Omega)
, and its derivative
u^{(r)}
satisfies the Lipschitz condition with parameter
\alpha\in(0,1]
, then

|u(x)-u^{\langle n\rangle}(x)|\leqslant\frac{\hat{C}}{n^{r+\alpha-1/2}}\ \ \ \forall\,x\in\Omega,

where
\hat{C}>0
does not depend on
n
,
r+\alpha>1/2
.

Remark 1
.

In the inequality (
9
), the parameter
s
can be a real non-negative number
[
14
]
. For this, we should use the condition that
u
belongs to the Sobolev space
W_{2}^{s}(\Omega)
for
s\geqslant 0
instead of the condition that generalized derivatives of
u
up to order
s
belong to
L_{2}(\Omega)
. If
s
is a natural number, then
W_{2}^{s}(\Omega)
is defined in the usual way
[
10
]
:

W_{2}^{s}(\Omega)=\bigl\{u\colon u^{(k)}\in L_{2}(\Omega),~k=0,1,\dots,s\bigr\}.

If
s
is not a natural number, then
W_{2}^{s}(\Omega)
(Sobolev–Slobodetskij space) is possible defined by the complex interpolation between
W_{2}^{\lfloor s\rfloor}(\Omega)
and
W_{2}^{\lceil s\rceil}(\Omega)

[
10
,
15
]
, where
\lceil s\rceil=\lfloor s\rfloor+1
,
W_{2}^{0}(\Omega)=L_{2}(\Omega)
. Another definition for
W_{2}^{s}(\Omega)

[
15
,
16
,
17
]
:

W_{2}^{s}(\Omega)=\biggl\{u\in L_{2}(\Omega)\colon\int_{\Omega^{2}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,dxdy<\infty\biggr\},\ \ \ s=\sigma\in(0,1),

(11)

and

W_{2}^{s}(\Omega)=\bigl\{u\in W_{2}^{\lfloor s\rfloor}(\Omega),\ \ \ u^{(\lfloor s\rfloor)}\in W_{2}^{\sigma}(\Omega),\ \ \ \sigma=s-{\lfloor s\rfloor}\bigr\},\ \ \ s\geqslant 1.

The parameter
s
in the inequalities (
9
) and (
10
) can be related with the condition that derivatives of fractional order
s
for
u
belong to
L_{2}(\Omega)
. In the inequality (
10
) for non-integer
s
, we should replace the ratio
(n-s+1)!/(n+s+1)!
on the ratio
\Gamma(n-s+2)/\Gamma(n+s+2)
, where
\Gamma
is the gamma function.

2.2.
Projection estimates of density and distribution functions

We assume that
\xi
is the random variable with the range
\Omega=[-1,1]
, density
g
, and distribution function
f
. The function
g
equals to zero outside
\Omega
, and the function
f
is expressed through
g

[
18
]
:

f(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}g(\theta)d\theta.

(12)

Further, we consider projection estimates of
g,f\in L_{2}(\Omega)
using their expansions into the Fourier–Legendre series (the restriction of the density and distribution function to
\Omega
is implied). Note that the condition
f\in L_{2}(\Omega)
follows from the condition
g\in L_{2}(\Omega)
, which is assumed to be satisfied.

As a function
u
in the above formulae, we can use the density
g
and distribution function
f
(below we denote corresponding expansion coefficients by
G_{i}
and
F_{i}
). In this case, the sequence
f^{\langle n\rangle}
converges to
f
faster than the sequence
g^{\langle n\rangle}
converges to
g
, where

g^{\langle n\rangle}(x)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}G_{i}\hat{P}_{i}(x),\ \ \ f^{\langle n\rangle}(x)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}F_{i}\hat{P}_{i}(x),

(13)

since the degree of smoothness of
f
is more by one than the degree of smoothness of
g
, which follows from their relationship (
12
).

For example, let the density
g
be continuous on
\Omega
, differentiable in a generalized sense only, i.e.,
s=1
,
r=0
, and satisfy the Lipschitz condition with parameter
\alpha=1
. Then

\displaystyle\|g-g^{\langle n\rangle}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{C^{g}}{n}\ \ \ \text{or}\ \ \ \|g-g^{\langle n\rangle}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{\tilde{C}^{g}}{\sqrt{(n+1)(n+2)}},

\displaystyle|g(x)-g^{\langle n\rangle}(x)|\leqslant\frac{\hat{C}^{g}}{n^{1/2}},

and for the distribution function
f
the following estimates hold (
s=2
,
r=1
,
\alpha=1
):

\displaystyle\|f-f^{\langle n\rangle}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{C^{f}}{n^{2}}\ \ \ \text{or}\ \ \ \|f-f^{\langle n\rangle}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{\tilde{C}^{f}}{\sqrt{n(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}},

\displaystyle|f(x)-f^{\langle n\rangle}(x)|\leqslant\frac{\hat{C}^{f}}{n^{3/2}},

where
C^{g},\tilde{C}^{g},\hat{C}^{g},C^{f},\tilde{C}^{f},\hat{C}^{f}>0
are some constants,
x\in\Omega
.

Projection estimates of the density and distribution function are given by expressions based on their approximations (
13
):

\bar{g}^{\langle n\rangle}(x)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}\bar{G}_{i}\hat{P}_{i}(x),\ \ \ \bar{f}^{\langle n\rangle}(x)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}\bar{F}_{i}\hat{P}_{i}(x),

(14)

where
\bar{G}_{i}
and
\bar{F}_{i}
are estimates of expansion coefficients
G_{i}
and
F_{i}
based on observations of the random variable
\xi

[
19
,
20
]
,
i=0,1,\dots,n
.

2.3.
On expansion coefficients of density and distribution function

The relationship between expansion coefficients
G_{i}
and statistical characteristics of the random variable
\xi
is established using a linear functional
J
defined by this density:

J\varphi=(g,\varphi)_{L_{2}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}g(x)\varphi(x)dx=\mathrm{E}\varphi(\xi),

(15)

where
\mathrm{E}
means the mathematical expectation. As a function
\varphi
in the formula (
15
), it is required to substitute the Legendre polynomials (
6
), then

G_{i}=\mathrm{E}\hat{P}_{i}(\xi),\ \ \ i=0,1,2,\dots

A series representation and the corresponding approximation by a partial sum of this series, similar to (
7
) and (
8
), can be obtained for an arbitrary orthonormal basis of
L_{2}(\Omega)
. But the Legendre polynomials have an important advantage, namely expansion coefficients
G_{i}
are easily expressed via initial moments of the random variable
\xi
:

\mathrm{M}_{k}=\mathrm{E}\xi^{k},\ \ \ k=1,2,\dots

Indeed, based on the formulae (
3
) and (
6
) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\hat{P}_{i}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{i}}\sqrt{\frac{2i+1}{2}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\lfloor i/2\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{k}(2i-2k)!}{k!(i-k)!(i-2k)!}\,\mathrm{E}\xi^{i-2k}

(16)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{i}}\sqrt{\frac{2i+1}{2}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\lfloor i/2\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{k}(2i-2k)!}{k!(i-k)!(i-2k)!}\,\mathrm{M}_{i-2k},

where the constant
\mathrm{M}_{0}=1
is additionally defined.

Another advantage of the chosen basis is that the antiderivative of any Legendre polynomial with index
i>0
is represented as a linear combination of two Legendre polynomials with neighboring indices according to the recurrence relation (
2
) (for
i=0
, one of polynomials in the linear combination is also
\hat{P}_{0}
). Thus, having a projection estimate of the density, one can find a projection estimate of the distribution function by the simplest operations. Expressions required for this are obtained below.

Integrating the left-hand and right-hand sides of the relation (
2
) and taking into account the property of the Legendre polynomials
[
9
]
, namely the equality
P_{i+1}(-1)=P_{i-1}(-1)
, we have

(2i+1)\int_{-1}^{x}P_{i}(\theta)d\theta=P_{i+1}(x)-P_{i-1}(x),\ \ \ i=1,2,\dots,

(17)

or

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2i+1}{2}}\int_{-1}^{x}P_{i}(\theta)d\theta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(2i+1)}}\,P_{i+1}(x)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(2i+1)}}\,P_{i-1}(x)

\displaystyle~~~=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2i+1)(2i+3)}}\,\sqrt{\frac{2i+3}{2}}\,P_{i+1}(x)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2i-1)(2i+1)}}\,\sqrt{\frac{2i-1}{2}}\,P_{i-1}(x),

i.e.,

\int_{-1}^{x}\hat{P}_{i}(\theta)d\theta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2i+1)(2i+3)}}\,\hat{P}_{i+1}(x)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2i-1)(2i+1)}}\,\hat{P}_{i-1}(x),\ \ \ i=1,2,\dots

(18)

Given
i=0
, we obtain

\int_{-1}^{x}P_{0}(\theta)d\theta=\int_{-1}^{x}d\theta=x+1=P_{1}(x)+P_{0}(x),

or

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{-1}^{x}P_{0}(\theta)d\theta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,P_{1}(x)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,P_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\,\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\,P_{1}(x)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,P_{0}(x),

i.e.,

\int_{-1}^{x}\hat{P}_{0}(\theta)d\theta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\,\hat{P}_{1}(x)+\hat{P}_{0}(x).

(19)

The relationship (
12
) between functions
g
and
f
, as well as recurrent relations (
18
) and (
19
) imply that

F_{0}=G_{0}-\frac{G_{1}}{\sqrt{3}},\ \ \ F_{i}=\frac{G_{i-1}}{\sqrt{(2i-1)(2i+1)}}-\frac{G_{i+1}}{\sqrt{(2i+1)(2i+3)}},\ \ \ i=1,2,\dots

(20)

Remark 2
.

The condition
\Omega=[-1,1]
does not reduce the generality of the above reasoning. Similar relations can be written if the range of the random variable
\xi
is
\Omega=[a,b]
and
g,f\in L_{2}(\Omega)
. In this case, the orthonormal Legendre polynomials
\{\hat{P}_{i}^{*}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
are defined by the expression

\hat{P}_{i}^{*}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2i+1}{b-a}}\,P_{i}\biggl(\frac{2x-(b+a)}{b-a}\biggr).

3.
Algorithms for Jointly Obtaining Randomized Projection Estimates of Density and Distribution Functions

The randomization of the projection estimate of the density
g
is obtained via the first of the formulae (
14
) as a result of calculating the linear functional (
15
) for the Legendre polynomials (
6
) by the Monte Carlo method with independent realizations of the random variable
\xi
:

\bar{G}_{i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{N}\hat{P}_{i}(\xi_{l}),\ \ \ i=0,1,2,\dots,

(21)

where
\xi_{l}
is the
l
th realization,
N
is the sample size (number of realizations).

Estimates
\bar{G}_{i}
can also be obtained from the expression (
16
):

\bar{G}_{i}=\frac{1}{2^{i}}\sqrt{\frac{2i+1}{2}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\lfloor i/2\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{k}(2i-2k)!}{k!(i-k)!(i-2k)!}\,\bar{\mathrm{M}}_{i-2k},

(22)

where
\bar{\mathrm{M}}_{k}
are statistical estimates of initial moments of the random variable
\xi
:

\bar{\mathrm{M}}_{k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{N}\xi_{l}^{k},\ \ \ k=1,2,\dots,

(23)

and
\bar{\mathrm{M}}_{0}=1
. Using the formulae (
4
) and (
5
), we can write expressions for estimates of density expansion coefficients separately for even indices (
i=2j
):

\bar{G}_{2j}=\frac{1}{2^{2j}}\sqrt{\frac{4j+1}{2}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}\frac{(-1)^{k}(4j-2k)!}{k!(2j-k)!(2j-2k)!}\,\bar{\mathrm{M}}_{2j-2k},

(24)

and for odd indices (
i=2j+1
):

\bar{G}_{2j+1}=\frac{1}{2^{2j+1}}\sqrt{\frac{4j+3}{2}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{j}\frac{(-1)^{k}(4j-2k+2)!}{k!(2j-k+1)!(2j-2k+1)!}\,\bar{\mathrm{M}}_{2j-2k+1}.

(25)

The randomization of the projection estimate of the distribution function
f
is obtained via the second of the formulae (
14
) and recurrence relations (
20
):

\bar{F}_{0}=\bar{G}_{0}-\frac{\bar{G}_{1}}{\sqrt{3}},\ \ \ \bar{F}_{i}=\frac{\bar{G}_{i-1}}{\sqrt{(2i-1)(2i+1)}}-\frac{\bar{G}_{i+1}}{\sqrt{(2i+1)(2i+3)}},\ \ \ i=1,2,\dots

(26)

Here and below, the dependence of estimates of both expansion coefficients and functions on the sample size
N
is not indicated for simplicity.

Remark 3
.

To obtain a projection estimate of the distribution function
f
based on the first
n+1
Legendre polynomials, it is necessary to find estimates of expansion coefficients of the density
g
with respect to the first
n+2
Legendre polynomials.

Further, we present the first algorithm for jointly obtaining randomized projection estimates of the density and distribution function of the random variable
\xi
.

Algorithm 1
.

0. Set the projection expansion length
n
and the sample size
N
.

1. Simulate
N
realizations
\xi_{l}
of the random variable
\xi
,
l=1,2,\dots,N
.

2. Find statistical estimates
\bar{\mathrm{M}}_{k}
of initial moments of the random variable
\xi
using the formula (
23
),
k=1,2,\dots,n+1
. Set
\bar{\mathrm{M}}_{0}=1
.

3. Find estimates of expansion coefficients
\bar{G}_{i}
of the density
g
using the formula (
22
) or the formulae (
24
) and (
25
),
i=0,1,\dots,n+1
.

4. Find estimates of expansion coefficients
\bar{F}_{i}
of the distribution function
f
using the formulae (
26
),
i=0,1,\dots,n
.

5. Find randomized projection estimates
\bar{g}^{\langle n\rangle}
and
\bar{f}^{\langle n\rangle}
of the density and distribution function, respectively, using the formulae (
14
).

At step 3 in Algorithm
1
, when calculating expansion coefficients
\bar{G}_{i}
, errors can occur due to peculiarities of machine arithmetic. To avoid this, it is recommended to use the formula (
21
) together with the recurrence relation (
1
) and the expression (
6
).

Next, we formulate the second algorithm for jointly obtaining randomized projection estimates of the density and distribution function.

Algorithm 2
.

0. Set the projection expansion length
n
and the sample size
N
.

1. Simulate
N
realizations
\xi_{l}
of the random variable
\xi
,
l=1,2,\dots,N
.

2. Find estimates of expansion coefficients
\bar{G}_{i}
of the density

using the formula (

) together with the recurrence relation (

) and the expression (

),

.

3. Find estimates of expansion coefficients
\bar{F}_{i}
of the distribution function

using the formulae (

),
i=0,1,\dots,n
.

4. Find randomized projection estimates
\bar{g}^{\langle n\rangle}
and
\bar{f}^{\langle n\rangle}

4.
Analysis and Conditional Optimization of Randomized Projection Estimates

In this section, we estimate the error of the projection estimate
\bar{g}^{\langle n\rangle}
relative to the density
g
of the random variable
\xi
in the space
{L_{2}}(\Omega)
. Jensen’s inequality
[
21
]
implies that

B^{2}(g,\bar{g}^{\langle n\rangle})=\bigl(\mathrm{E}\|g-\bar{g}^{\langle n\rangle}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\bigr)^{2}\leqslant\mathrm{E}\int_{\Omega}\bigl(g(x)-\bar{g}^{\langle n\rangle}(x)\bigr)^{2}dx.

According to Parseval’s identity
[
9
]
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\bigl(g(x)-\bar{g}^{\langle n\rangle}(x)\bigr)^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\biggl(\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}G_{i}\hat{P}_{i}(x)-\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}\bar{G}_{i}\hat{P}_{i}(x)\biggr)^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\biggl(\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}(G_{i}-\bar{G}_{i})\hat{P}_{i}(x)+\sum\limits_{i=n+1}^{\infty}G_{i}\hat{P}_{i}(x)\biggr)^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}(G_{i}-\bar{G}_{i})^{2}+\|g-g^{\langle n\rangle}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

therefore, taking into account that the unbiased estimate of mathematical expectation is used
[
18
]
, namely
\mathrm{E}\bar{G}_{i}=G_{i}
, we obtain

\displaystyle B^{2}(g,\bar{g}^{\langle n\rangle})

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathrm{E}\biggl(\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}(G_{i}-\bar{G}_{i})^{2}+\|g-g^{\langle n\rangle}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\biggr)

(27)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}\mathrm{E}(G_{i}-\bar{G}_{i})^{2}+\|g-g^{\langle n\rangle}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}\mathrm{D}\bar{G}_{i}+\|g-g^{\langle n\rangle}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

where
\mathrm{D}
denotes the variance.

The equality
\bar{G}_{0}=G_{0}
is satisfied since
\hat{P}_{0}(x)=\mathrm{const}
, i.e.,
\mathrm{D}\bar{G}_{0}=0
. For
i>0
, the variance of estimates
\bar{G}_{i}
and the sample size
N
are inversely proportional
[
22
]
; therefore, using additionally the inequality (
9
) under condition
g\in W_{2}^{s}(\Omega)
(see Remark
1
), we find

B^{2}(g,\bar{g}^{\langle n\rangle})\leqslant\frac{C_{1}^{g}n}{N}+\frac{C_{2}^{g}}{n^{2s}},

(28)

where
C_{1}^{g},C_{2}^{g}>0
are constants independent of
n
and
N
.

The error of the projection estimate
\bar{f}^{\langle n\rangle}
relative to the distribution function
f
of the random variable
\xi
in the space
{L_{2}}(\Omega)
is estimated similarly:

B^{2}(f,\bar{f}^{\langle n\rangle})\leqslant\frac{C_{1}^{f}n}{N}+\frac{C_{2}^{f}}{n^{2s+2}},

(29)

where
C_{1}^{f},C_{2}^{f}>0
are constants independent of
n
and
N
.

From the obtained estimates, it is clear how the mean square error depends on the projection expansion length
n
and the sample size
N
.

Further, we consider a problem of the optimal (consistent) choice of parameters for statistical algorithms to obtain projection estimates of the density and distribution function: the projection expansion length
n
and the sample size
N
. For jointly obtaining these estimates, which is provided by Algorithms
1
and
2
, it is sufficient to consider the optimal choice of parameters for the density estimation only and use them for the distribution function estimation. This is because the degree of smoothness of
f
is higher than the degree of smoothness of
g
, so the sequence
\bar{f}^{\langle n\rangle}
converges to
f
faster than the sequence
\bar{g}^{\langle n\rangle}
converges to
g
.

Theorem 1
.

Let the density
g
of the random variable
\xi
belong to
W_{2}^{s}(\Omega)
, and let
\bar{g}^{\langle n\rangle}
be the randomized projection estimate of the density obtained by Algorithm
1
or
2
. Then the minimum complexity of obtaining the estimate
\bar{g}^{\langle n\rangle}
is achieved with parameters

N_{\mathrm{opt}}\asymp n_{\mathrm{opt}}^{2s+1},\ \ \ n_{\mathrm{opt}}\asymp\gamma^{-1/s},\ \ \ N_{\mathrm{opt}}\asymp\gamma^{-(2s+1)/s},

where
\gamma
is the required approximation accuracy for the function
g
in the space
L_{2}(\Omega)
, i.e.,

B(g,\bar{g}^{\langle n\rangle})=\mathrm{E}\|g-\bar{g}^{\langle n\rangle}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}=\gamma.

Proof.

To find optimal parameters
n_{\mathrm{opt}}
and
N_{\mathrm{opt}}
for the estimate
\bar{g}^{\langle n\rangle}
, it is sufficient to equate errors
[
8
]
in the formula (
28
) and obtain the required approximation accuracy
\gamma
from the relation

\frac{C_{1}^{g}n}{N}+\frac{C_{2}^{g}}{n^{2s}}=\gamma^{2},

then the following parameters’ relationships for estimating the density
g
are conditionally optimal:

N_{\mathrm{opt}}\asymp n_{\mathrm{opt}}^{2s+1},\ \ \ n_{\mathrm{opt}}\asymp\gamma^{-1/s},\ \ \ N_{\mathrm{opt}}\asymp\gamma^{-(2s+1)/s}.

Choosing parameters in this way, we have

B(g,\bar{g}^{\langle n\rangle})\leqslant cN^{-s/(2s+1)},

where
c>0
is a constant independent of
n
and
N
.
∎

Theorem
1
establishes the relationship between parameters in Algorithms
1
and
2
:
n
and
N
, as well as the dependence of approximation accuracy on parameter
s
.

Remark 4
.

The error estimate (
28
) of the projection estimate
\bar{g}^{\langle n\rangle}
relative to the density
g
is based on the inequality (
9
), but the inequality (
10
) can also be used. Then, taking into account Remark
1
, we have

B^{2}(g,\bar{g}^{\langle n\rangle})\leqslant\frac{C_{1}^{g}n}{N}+C_{2}^{g}\,\frac{\Gamma(n-s+2)}{\Gamma(n+s+2)}.

It is possible to formulate an analogue of Theorem
1
and show that the following parameters’ relationship for estimating the density
g
is conditionally optimal:

N_{\mathrm{opt}}\asymp n_{\mathrm{opt}}\,\frac{\Gamma(n_{\mathrm{opt}}+s+2)}{\Gamma(n_{\mathrm{opt}}-s+2)}.

Remark 5
.

If conditions of Theorem
1
are satisfied, but it is only necessary to estimate the distribution function
f
of the random variable
\xi
, then the minimum complexity of obtaining the estimate
\bar{f}^{\langle n\rangle}
is achieved with parameters

N_{\mathrm{opt}}\asymp n_{\mathrm{opt}}^{2s+3},\ \ \ n_{\mathrm{opt}}\asymp\gamma^{-1/(s+1)},\ \ \ N_{\mathrm{opt}}\asymp\gamma^{-(2s+3)/(s+1)},

where
\gamma
is the required approximation accuracy for the function
f
in the space
L_{2}(\Omega)
. The proof is similar to the proof of Theorem
1
.

Another parameters’ relationship can be found if we take as a basis the inequality (
10
) instead of the inequality (
9
) (see Remark
4
).

5.
Two-parameter Family of Densities with Different Degrees of Smoothness

In this section, a special example is constructed to test statistical algorithms for obtaining projection estimates depending on the projection expansion length, sample size, and smoothness of the estimated function.

5.1.
Densities with different degrees of smoothness and related distribution functions

Let
\xi
be the random variable defined by the density
g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
:

g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(x)=\gamma_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1-(-x)^{\nu_{1}}&\text{for}~x\in[-1,0),\\
1-x^{\nu_{2}}&\text{for}~x\in[0,1],\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

(30)

where
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
are parameters (natural numbers), and
\gamma_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
is a normalizing constant:

\frac{1}{\gamma_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}}=\int_{-1}^{0}\bigl(1-(-x)^{\nu_{1}}\bigr)dx+\int_{0}^{1}(1-x^{\nu_{2}})dx=\frac{\nu_{1}}{\nu_{1}+1}+\frac{\nu_{2}}{\nu_{2}+1},

because

\int_{-1}^{0}\bigl(1-(-x)^{\nu}\bigr)dx=\int_{0}^{1}(1-x^{\nu})dx=\biggl(x-\frac{x^{\nu+1}}{\nu+1}\biggr)\bigg|_{0}^{1}=1-\frac{1}{\nu+1}=\frac{\nu}{\nu+1}.

Further, we assume that
\nu_{1}\neq\nu_{2}
. The function
g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
has the following properties:

(a)
g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
is continuous on the set of real numbers;

(b) the support of
g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
is the set
\Omega=[-1,1]
;

(c) the normalization condition holds:

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(x)dx=\int_{-1}^{1}g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(x)dx=1;

(d)
g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
is differentiable at
x=0
only
r
times:

g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}\in C^{r}(\Omega),\ \ \ r=\min\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\}-1;

(e) the derivative
g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}^{(r+1)}
exists almost everywhere on
\Omega
; if the derivative is understood in a generalized sense, then

g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}^{(s)}\in L_{2}(\Omega),\ \ \ g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}\in W_{2}^{s}(\Omega),\ \ \ s=r+1=\min\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\}.

Next, we define the distribution function
f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
for the random variable
\xi
with density (
30
):

f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(\theta)d\theta.

(31)

It is easy to see that
f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(x)=0
for
x<-1
and
f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(x)=1
for
x>1
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{x}\bigl(1-(-\theta)^{\nu}\bigr)d\theta=\biggl(\theta+(-1)^{\nu+1}\frac{\theta^{\nu+1}}{\nu+1}\biggr)\bigg|_{-1}^{x}=\frac{\nu}{\nu+1}+x+\frac{(-x)^{\nu+1}}{\nu+1},\ \ \ x\in[-1,0],

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}(1-\theta^{\nu})d\theta=\biggl(\theta-\frac{\theta^{\nu+1}}{\nu+1}\biggr)\bigg|_{0}^{x}=x-\frac{x^{\nu+1}}{\nu+1},\ \ \ x\in[0,1],

consequently,

f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle 0&\text{for}~x<-1,\\
\displaystyle\gamma_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}\biggl(\frac{\nu_{1}}{\nu_{1}+1}+x+\frac{(-x)^{\nu_{1}+1}}{\nu_{1}+1}\biggr)&\text{for}~x\in[-1,0),\\
\displaystyle\gamma_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}\biggl(\frac{\nu_{1}}{\nu_{1}+1}+x-\frac{x^{\nu_{2}+1}}{\nu_{2}+1}\biggr)&\text{for}~x\in[0,1],\\
\displaystyle 1&\text{for}~x>1.\end{array}\right.

(32)

The function
f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
is differentiable
r+1
times at
x=0
since
f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}^{(r+1)}=g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}^{(r)}
(it follows from the relationship (
31
)). Thus,

f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}\in C^{r+1}(\Omega),\ \ \ f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}^{(s+1)}\in L_{2}(\Omega),\ \ \ f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}\in W_{2}^{s+1}(\Omega).

If we do not restrict ourselves to the space
W_{2}^{s}(\Omega)
with natural parameter
s
(see Remark
1
), then we can show that
s<\min\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\}+1/2
and
\sup s=\min\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\}+1/2
.

Consider the case
\nu_{1}>\nu_{2}
. Then the generalized derivative
g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}^{(\nu_{2})}
is represented as a linear combination of functions
\eta(x)
and
x^{\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}}\eta(-x)
, where
\eta
is the indicator of
(0,\infty)
. Next, it suffices to find a condition for parameter
\sigma
, which ensures that the function
\eta
belongs to
W_{2}^{\sigma}(\Omega)
, where
W_{2}^{\sigma}(\Omega)
is defined by the formula (
11
),
\sigma\in(0,1)
.

The expression
|\eta(x)-\eta(y)|
takes the value 0 if
x,y
have the same sign, and the value 1 if
x,y
have different signs, hence

\int_{\Omega^{2}}\frac{|\eta(x)-\eta(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{1+2\sigma}}\,dxdy=\int_{-1}^{0}\biggl[\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dy}{|x-y|^{1+2\sigma}}\biggr]dx+\int_{0}^{1}\biggl[\int_{-1}^{0}\frac{dy}{|x-y|^{1+2\sigma}}\biggr]dx.

The integrals on the right-hand side of the latter equality coincide, since the integrand does not change when signs of
x,y
change simultaneously. The convergence condition for them is
\sigma<1/2
. Indeed, for
\sigma<1/2
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{0}\biggl[\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dy}{|x-y|^{1+2\sigma}}\biggr]dx=\int_{-1}^{0}\biggl[\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dy}{(y-x)^{1+2\sigma}}\biggr]dx=-\frac{1}{2\sigma}\int_{-1}^{0}\frac{1}{(y-x)^{2\sigma}}\bigg|_{0}^{1}dx

\displaystyle\ \ \ =-\frac{1}{2\sigma}\int_{-1}^{0}\biggl[\frac{1}{(1-x)^{2\sigma}}-\frac{1}{(-x)^{2\sigma}}\biggr]dx=\frac{1}{2\sigma(2\sigma-1)}\biggl[-\frac{1}{(1-x)^{2\sigma-1}}\bigg|_{-1}^{0}+\frac{1}{(-x)^{2\sigma-1}}\bigg|_{-1}^{0}\biggr]

\displaystyle\ \ \ =\frac{1}{2\sigma(2\sigma-1)}\biggl[\frac{1}{2^{2\sigma-1}}-2\biggr]=\frac{2^{-2\sigma}-1}{\sigma(2\sigma-1)}

and

\int_{\Omega^{2}}\frac{|\eta(x)-\eta(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{1+2\sigma}}\,dxdy=\frac{2(2^{-2\sigma}-1)}{\sigma(2\sigma-1)}.

For
\sigma\geqslant 1/2
, these integrals obviously diverge. Therefore,
g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}^{(\nu_{2})}\in W_{2}^{\sigma}(\Omega)
for
\sigma<1/2
and
g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}\in W_{2}^{s}(\Omega)
for
s<\nu_{2}+1/2
.

The case
\nu_{1}<\nu_{2}
is similar to the considered case, so finally
g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}\in W_{2}^{s}(\Omega)
and
f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}\in W_{2}^{s+1}(\Omega)
provided
s<\min\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\}+1/2
and
\sup s=\min\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\}+1/2
.

5.2.
Modeling random variables with test density using Monte Carlo method

The modeling formula for the random variable
\xi
with distribution function
f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
for parameters
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
can be derived using the inverse function method
[
22
]
:
\xi=f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}^{-1}(\alpha)
, where
f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}^{-1}
is the inverse function to
f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
, and
\alpha
is the random variable having a uniform distribution on
(0,1)
.

Given the distribution function
f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
, we can obtain the algorithm for modeling the random variable
\xi
.

Algorithm 3
.

0. Set parameters
\nu_{1}
,
\nu_{2}
, calculate
\gamma_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
and
f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(0)
:

\gamma_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}=\biggl(\frac{\nu_{1}}{\nu_{1}+1}+\frac{\nu_{2}}{\nu_{2}+1}\biggr)^{-1},\ \ \ f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(0)=\gamma_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}\,\frac{\nu_{1}}{\nu_{1}+1}=\frac{\nu_{1}(\nu_{2}+1)}{\nu_{1}+2\nu_{1}\nu_{2}+\nu_{2}}.

1. Obtain a realization of the random variable
\alpha
having a uniform distribution on
(0,1)
.

2. If
\alpha<f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(0)
, then
\xi
is a root of the algebraic equation

\frac{\nu_{1}}{\nu_{1}+1}+x+\frac{(-x)^{\nu_{1}+1}}{\nu_{1}+1}-\frac{\alpha}{\gamma_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}}=0

(33)

from
(-1,0)
, otherwise
\xi
is a root of the algebraic equation

\frac{\nu_{1}}{\nu_{1}+1}+x-\frac{x^{\nu_{2}+1}}{\nu_{2}+1}-\frac{\alpha}{\gamma_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}}=0

(34)

from
[0,1)
.

For small values
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
, roots can be found analytically. Next, we obtain the modeling formulae for two cases used below.

Proposition 1
.

For the random variable
\xi
with density

g(x)=\frac{6}{7}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1+x&\text{for}~x\in[-1,0),\\
1-x^{2}&\text{for}~x\in[0,1],\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

the modeling formula is as follows:

\xi=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{7\alpha}{3}}-1&\text{for}~\displaystyle\alpha<\frac{3}{7},\\[-8.61108pt]
\\
\displaystyle-\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}A+\sqrt{3}\,\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}A&\text{for}~\displaystyle\alpha\geqslant\frac{3}{7},\end{array}\right.

(35)

where

A=\sqrt[3]{\frac{3-7\alpha}{4}+\sqrt{\frac{(7\alpha-3)^{2}}{16}-1}}.

Proof.

The density
g
of the random variable
\xi
is included to a two-parameter family (
30
) for
\nu_{1}=\nu
,
\nu_{2}=\nu+1
,
\nu=1
:
g=g_{1,2}
. For given parameters, we have

\frac{\nu_{1}}{\nu_{1}+1}=\frac{1}{2},\ \ \ \gamma_{1,2}=\frac{6}{7},\ \ \ f_{1,2}(0)=\frac{3}{7}.

First, we consider the case
\alpha<f_{1,2}(0)=3/7
, i.e., we should solve the equation (
33
). This is the quadratic equation

\frac{1}{2}+x+\frac{x^{2}}{2}-\frac{7\alpha}{6}=0,\ \ \ \text{or}\ \ \ x^{2}+bx+c=0,

where
b=2
,
c=1-7\alpha/3>0
.

The function
v(x)=x^{2}+2x+1-7\alpha/3
has a minimum at
x^{*}=-1
, since
v^{\prime}(x^{*})=2x^{*}+2=0
,
v^{\prime\prime}(x^{*})=2>0
, and
v(x^{*})=-7\alpha/3<0
. This means that the quadratic equation has two real roots (the discriminant is positive) and the largest of them determines
\xi
:

\xi=\max\{x_{1},x_{2}\}>x^{*},\ \ \ x_{1,2}=-\frac{b}{2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{b^{2}}{4}-c}=-1\pm\sqrt{\frac{7\alpha}{3}},\ \ \ \xi=\sqrt{\frac{7\alpha}{3}}-1\in(-1,0).

Second, we consider the case
\alpha\geqslant f_{1,2}(0)=3/7
, i.e., we should solve the equation (
34
). This is the cubic equation

\frac{1}{2}+x-\frac{x^{3}}{3}-\frac{7\alpha}{6}=0,\ \ \ \text{or}\ \ \ x^{3}+px+q=0,

where
p=-3
,
q=(7\alpha-3)/2>0
.

The function
w(x)=x^{3}-3x+(7\alpha-3)/2
has extrema at
x_{1,2}^{*}=\pm 1
:
w^{\prime}(x^{*})=3(x_{1,2}^{*})^{2}-3=0
. A minimum is reached at
x_{1}^{*}=1
:
w^{\prime\prime}(x_{1}^{*})=6x_{1}^{*}=6>0
and
w(x_{1}^{*})=7(\alpha-1)/2<0
. A maximum is reached at
x_{2}^{*}=-1
:
w^{\prime\prime}(x_{2}^{*})=6x_{2}^{*}=-6<0
and
w(x_{2}^{*})=(1+7\alpha)/2>0
. This means that the cubic equation has three real roots (the discriminant is positive), and
\xi
is determined by the root that lies between the smallest and largest roots. Using Cardano’s formulae for roots
[
23
]
, we have

x_{1}=A+B,\ \ \ x_{2,3}=-\frac{A+B}{2}\pm\frac{A-B}{2}\,\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{i},

where

A=\sqrt[3]{-\frac{q}{2}+\sqrt{Q}},\ \ \ B=\sqrt[3]{-\frac{q}{2}-\sqrt{Q}},\ \ \ Q=\biggl(\frac{p}{3}\biggr)^{3}+\biggl(\frac{q}{2}\biggr)^{2},

and
Q<0
, which corresponds to the positive discriminant; therefore,
A
and
B
are complex conjugate numbers:

\frac{A+B}{2}=\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}A,\ \ \ \frac{A-B}{2\,\mathrm{i}}=\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}A\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ x_{1}=2\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}A,\ \ \ x_{2,3}=-\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}A\mp\sqrt{3}\,\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}A.

Let
z=-q/2+\sqrt{Q}
. Since
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}z=-q/2<0
and
\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}z=\sqrt{-Q}>0
, we obtain
\arg z\in(\pi/2,\pi)
; therefore,
\arg A\in(\pi/6,\pi/3)
and

\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}A,\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}A>0,\ \ \ \tan\frac{\pi}{6}<\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}A}{\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}A}<\tan\frac{\pi}{3},\ \ \ \text{or}\ \ \ \mathop{\mathrm{Re}}A<\sqrt{3}\,\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}A<3\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}A.

Thus,

x_{2}<x_{3}<x_{1},\ \ \ \xi=-\frac{A+B}{2}-\frac{A-B}{2}\,\sqrt{3}\,\mathrm{i}=-\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}A+\sqrt{3}\,\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}A\in(0,1),

i.e., the formula (
35
) is valid.
∎

Proposition 2
.

For the random variable
\xi
with density

g(x)=\frac{12}{17}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1+x^{3}&\text{for}~x\in[-1,0),\\
1-x^{2}&\text{for}~x\in[0,1],\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

the modeling formula is as follows:

\xi=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle-\sqrt{\frac{Y}{2}}+\sqrt{-\frac{Y}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{Y}}}&\text{for}~\displaystyle\alpha<\frac{9}{17},\\[-8.61108pt]
\\
\displaystyle-\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}A+\sqrt{3}\,\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}A&\text{for}~\displaystyle\alpha\geqslant\frac{9}{17},\end{array}\right.

(36)

where

Y=\omega-\frac{17\alpha-9}{9\omega},\ \ \ \omega=\sqrt[3]{1+\sqrt{1+\frac{(17\alpha-9)^{3}}{729}}},\ \ \ A=\sqrt[3]{\frac{9-17\alpha}{8}+\sqrt{\frac{(17\alpha-9)^{2}}{64}-1}}.

Proof.

The density
g
of the random variable
\xi
is included to a two-parameter family (
30
) for
\nu_{1}=\nu+1
,
\nu_{2}=\nu
,
\nu=2
:
g=g_{3,2}
. In this case,

\frac{\nu_{1}}{\nu_{1}+1}=\frac{3}{4},\ \ \ \gamma_{3,2}=\frac{12}{17},\ \ \ f_{3,2}(0)=\frac{9}{17}.

The proof is the same as for Proposition
1
, so some details are omitted. We only note that for
\alpha<f_{3,2}(0)=9/17
the equation (
33
) is the quartic equation:

\frac{3}{4}+x+\frac{x^{4}}{4}-\frac{17\alpha}{12}=0,\ \ \ \text{or}\ \ \ x^{4}+bx+c=0,

where
b=4
,
c=3-17\alpha/3>0
, and the polynomial
v(x)=x^{4}+4x+3-17\alpha/3
has a minimum at
x^{*}=-1
, and
v(x^{*})=-17\alpha/3<0
. The quartic equation has two real roots, and the largest of them determines
\xi
. It is convenient to find roots using Ferrari’s formulae
[
23
]
.

For
\alpha\geqslant f_{3,2}(0)=9/17
, the equation (
34
) is the cubic equation, which is solved similarly to the cubic equation from the proof of Proposition
1
. Such reasoning leads to the formula (
36
).
∎

5.3.
Expansion coefficients of test functions (Fourier–Legendre series)

To exactly calculate the second term of the projection estimate error (
27
) in examples, we find expansion coefficients
G_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},i}
of the density
g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
, as well as expansion coefficients
F_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},i}
of the distribution function
f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
with respect to the Legendre polynomials (
6
):

g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(x)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}G_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},i}\hat{P}_{i}(x),\ \ \ f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(x)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}F_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},i}\hat{P}_{i}(x).

First, we obtain the following values:

Q_{\nu,i}^{-}=\int_{-1}^{0}x^{\nu}P_{i}(x)dx,\ \ \ Q_{\nu,i}^{+}=\int_{0}^{1}x^{\nu}P_{i}(x)dx.

(37)

Multiply the left-hand and right-hand sides of the relation (
1
) by
x^{\nu-1}
and integrate over the interval
[-1,0]
:

(i+1)\int_{-1}^{0}x^{\nu-1}P_{i+1}(x)dx=(2i+1)\int_{-1}^{0}x^{\nu}P_{i}(x)dx-i\int_{-1}^{0}x^{\nu-1}P_{i-1}(x)dx,

or

(i+1)Q_{\nu-1,i+1}^{-}=(2i+1)Q_{\nu,i}^{-}-iQ_{\nu-1,i-1}^{-},\ \ \ Q_{\nu,i}^{-}=\frac{(i+1)Q_{\nu-1,i+1}^{-}+iQ_{\nu-1,i-1}^{-}}{2i+1}.

(38)

Similarly, multiplying the left-hand and right-hand sides of the relation (
1
) by
x^{\nu-1}
and integrating over the interval
[0,1]
, we obtain

Q_{\nu,i}^{+}=\frac{(i+1)Q_{\nu-1,i+1}^{+}+iQ_{\nu-1,i-1}^{+}}{2i+1}.

These relations can be formally applied for
i=0
when
Q_{\nu,-1}^{-}=Q_{\nu,-1}^{+}=0
, but not for
\nu=0
. Therefore, we should consider the case
\nu=0
separately:

Q_{0,i}^{-}=\int_{-1}^{0}P_{i}(x)dx,\ \ \ Q_{0,i}^{+}=\int_{0}^{1}P_{i}(x)dx.

For
i=0
and
i=1
, we have

Q_{0,0}^{-}=\int_{-1}^{0}P_{0}(x)dx=\int_{-1}^{0}dx=1,\ \ \ Q_{0,1}^{-}=\int_{-1}^{0}P_{1}(x)dx=\int_{-1}^{0}xdx=-\frac{1}{2},

and then we use the relation (
17
):

Q_{0,i}^{-}=\int_{-1}^{0}P_{i}(x)dx=\frac{P_{i+1}(0)-P_{i-1}(0)}{2i+1},\ \ \ i=1,2,\dots

If
i\neq 0
is even, then
P_{i+1}(0)=P_{i-1}(0)=0
according to the formula (
5
), and
Q_{0,i}^{-}=0
. If
i
is odd, then we can apply the explicit formula (
4
) or obtain an additional recurrence relation. We choose the latter variant and take into account the relation (
1
) for
x=0
:

(i+1)P_{i+1}(0)=-iP_{i-1}(0).

Then

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{0,i}^{-}}{Q_{0,i-2}^{-}}

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{i+1}(0)-P_{i-1}(0)}{2i+1}\,\frac{2i-3}{P_{i-1}(0)-P_{i-3}(0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\frac{i}{i+1}\,P_{i-1}(0)-P_{i-1}(0)}{2i+1}\,\frac{2i-3}{P_{i-1}(0)+\frac{i-1}{i-2}\,P_{i-1}(0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\frac{i}{i+1}-1}{2i+1}\,\frac{2i-3}{1+\frac{i-1}{i-2}},

i.e.,

Q_{0,i}^{-}=\frac{2-i}{i+1}\,Q_{0,i-2}^{-},\ \ \ i=3,5,7,\dots

The same reasoning leads to the following results:

Q_{0,0}^{+}=\int_{0}^{1}P_{0}(x)dx=\int_{0}^{1}dx=1,\ \ \ Q_{0,1}^{+}=\int_{0}^{1}P_{1}(x)dx=\int_{0}^{1}xdx=\frac{1}{2},

and

Q_{0,i}^{+}=\frac{P_{i-1}(0)-P_{i+1}(0)}{2i+1},\ \ \ i=1,2,\dots,

where
Q_{0,i}^{+}=0
for even
i\neq 0
, and for odd
i
we have

Q_{0,i}^{+}=\frac{2-i}{i+1}\,Q_{0,i-2}^{+},\ \ \ i=3,5,7,\dots

Thus, we obtain a general formula for calculating
Q_{\nu,i}^{-}
and
Q_{\nu,i}^{+}
with arbitrary non-negative integers
\nu
and
i
:

Q_{\nu,i}^{\pm}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle 1&\text{for}~\nu=i=0,\\
\displaystyle\pm\frac{1}{2}&\text{for}~\nu=0,~i=1,\\
\displaystyle 0&\text{for}~\nu=0,~i=2,4,6,\dots,\\
\displaystyle\frac{2-i}{i+1}\,Q_{0,i-2}^{\pm}&\text{for}~\nu=0,~i=3,5,7,\dots,\\
\displaystyle\frac{(i+1)Q_{\nu-1,i+1}^{\pm}+iQ_{\nu-1,i-1}^{\pm}}{2i+1}&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

on the basis of which the expansion coefficients of the density
g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
with respect to the Legendre polynomials (
6
) are expressed as follows (the relation (
37
) is also used here):

\displaystyle G_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},i}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(x)\hat{P}_{i}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\gamma_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}\,\sqrt{\frac{2i+1}{2}}\biggl(\int_{-1}^{0}\bigl(1-(-x)^{\nu_{1}}\bigr)P_{i}(x)dx+\int_{0}^{1}(1-x^{\nu_{2}})P_{i}(x)dx\biggr)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}\,\sqrt{\frac{2i+1}{2}}\,\bigl(Q_{0,i}^{-}-(-1)^{\nu_{1}}Q_{\nu_{1},i}^{-}+Q_{0,i}^{+}-Q_{\nu_{2},i}^{+}\bigr).

Expressions for expansion coefficients of the distribution function
f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
are similarly obtained:

\displaystyle F_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},i}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(x)\hat{P}_{i}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\gamma_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}\,\sqrt{\frac{2i+1}{2}}\biggl(\int_{-1}^{0}\biggl[\frac{\nu_{1}}{\nu_{1}+1}+x+\frac{(-x)^{\nu_{1}+1}}{\nu_{1}+1}\biggr]P_{i}(x)dx

\displaystyle~~~~~~+\int_{0}^{1}\biggl[\frac{\nu_{1}}{\nu_{1}+1}+x-\frac{x^{\nu_{2}+1}}{\nu_{2}+1}\biggr]P_{i}(x)dx\biggr)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}\,\sqrt{\frac{2i+1}{2}}\,\biggl(\frac{\nu_{1}}{\nu_{1}+1}\,Q_{0,i}^{-}+Q_{1,i}^{-}+\frac{(-1)^{\nu_{1}+1}}{\nu_{1}+1}\,Q_{\nu_{1}+1,i}^{-}

\displaystyle~~~~~~+\frac{\nu_{1}}{\nu_{1}+1}\,Q_{0,i}^{+}+Q_{1,i}^{+}-\frac{1}{\nu_{2}+1}\,Q_{\nu_{2}+1,i}^{+}\biggr).

These expressions for expansion coefficients of the density
g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
and distribution function
f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
are used for their approximation:

g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(x)\approx g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}^{\langle n\rangle}(x)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}G_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},i}\hat{P}_{i}(x),\ \ \ f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(x)\approx f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}^{\langle n\rangle}(x)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}F_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},i}\hat{P}_{i}(x),

and for the approximation error:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{g}^{\langle n\rangle}

\displaystyle=\|g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}-g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}^{\langle n\rangle}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}=\biggl(\int_{-1}^{1}g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}^{2}(x)dx-\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}G_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},i}^{2}\biggr)^{1/2},

(39)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{f}^{\langle n\rangle}

\displaystyle=\|f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}-f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}^{\langle n\rangle}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}=\biggl(\int_{-1}^{1}f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}^{2}(x)dx-\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}F_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},i}^{2}\biggr)^{1/2}.

5.4.
Analysis of convergence rate for expansions of test functions

Consider the function

y_{\nu}^{-}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x^{\nu}&\text{for}~x\in[-1,0),\\
0&\text{for}~x\in[0,1],\end{array}\right.

where
\nu
is a parameter (natural number). Its expansion coefficients
Y_{\nu,i}^{-}
with respect to the Legendre polynomials (
6
) are expressed through the previously found values
Q_{\nu,i}^{-}
:

y_{\nu}^{-}(x)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}Y_{\nu,i}^{-}\hat{P}_{i}(x),\ \ \ Y_{\nu,i}^{-}=\sqrt{\frac{2i+1}{2}}\,Q_{\nu,i}^{-}.

Below we derive the recurrence relation for
Q_{\nu,i}^{-}
, different from the relation (
38
). Multiply the left-hand and right-hand sides of the relation (
1
) by
x^{\nu}
and integrate over the interval
[-1,0]
:

(i+1)\int_{-1}^{0}x^{\nu}P_{i+1}(x)dx=(2i+1)\int_{-1}^{0}x^{\nu+1}P_{i}(x)dx-i\int_{-1}^{0}x^{\nu}P_{i-1}(x)dx.

Next, we use the rule of integration by parts:

\int_{-1}^{0}x^{\nu+1}P_{i}(x)dx=x^{\nu+1}\Theta_{i}(x)\bigg|_{-1}^{0}-(\nu+1)\int_{-1}^{0}x^{\nu}\Theta_{i}(x)dx,\ \ \ \Theta_{i}(x)=\int_{-1}^{x}P_{i}(\theta)d\theta,

consequently, taking into account the equality (
17
), we obtain

\int_{-1}^{0}x^{\nu+1}P_{i}(x)dx=-\frac{\nu+1}{2i+1}\int_{-1}^{0}x^{\nu}\bigl(P_{i+1}(x)-P_{i-1}(x)\bigr)dx,

therefore,

(i+1)\int_{-1}^{0}x^{\nu}P_{i+1}(x)dx=-(\nu+1)\int_{-1}^{0}x^{\nu}\bigl(P_{i+1}(x)-P_{i-1}(x)\bigr)dx-i\int_{-1}^{0}x^{\nu}P_{i-1}(x)dx,

or

(i+1)Q_{\nu,i+1}^{-}=-(\nu+1)(Q_{\nu,i+1}^{-}-Q_{\nu,i-1}^{-})-iQ_{\nu,i-1}^{-},

i.e.,

Q_{\nu,i+1}^{-}=\frac{\nu-i+1}{\nu+i+2}\,Q_{\nu,i-1}^{-}\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ Y_{\nu,i+1}^{-}=\sqrt{\frac{2i+3}{2i-1}}\,\frac{\nu-i+1}{\nu+i+2}\,Y_{\nu,i-1}^{-}.

Since
y_{\nu}^{-}\in L_{2}(\Omega)
, the series formed by the squared expansion coefficients
Y_{\nu,i}^{-}
converges. This series can be represented as a sum of two series:

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}(Y_{\nu,i}^{-})^{2}=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}(Y_{\nu,2j}^{-})^{2}+\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}(Y_{\nu,2j+1}^{-})^{2}.

The Raabe–Duhamel test
[
24
]
implies that the first series (similarly, for the second one) converges in the same way as the series

\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{j^{2\nu+2}},

since the sequence

R_{j}=j\biggl(\frac{(Y_{\nu,2j}^{-})^{2}}{(Y_{\nu,2j+2}^{-})^{2}}-1\biggr)=j\biggl(\frac{4j-1}{4j+3}\biggl[\frac{\nu+2j+2}{\nu-2j+1}\biggr]^{2}-1\biggr)

has the following limit:
\lim\limits_{j\to\infty}R_{j}=2\nu+2
, but the convergence of this series is equivalent to the convergence of the integral

\int_{1}^{+\infty}\frac{dt}{t^{2\nu+2}}=-\frac{1}{(2\nu+1)\,t^{2\nu+1}}\bigg|_{1}^{+\infty}=-\frac{1}{2\nu+1}\biggl(\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t^{2\nu+1}}-1\biggr),

which takes place under condition
2\nu+1>0
, or
\nu>-1/2
.

As a result, using Parseval’s identity, we find

\|y_{\nu}^{-}-y_{\nu}^{-\langle n\rangle}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\sum\limits_{i=n+1}^{\infty}(Y_{\nu,i}^{-})^{2}\leqslant\frac{C^{2}}{n^{2\nu+1}}\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \|y_{\nu}^{-}-y_{\nu}^{-\langle n\rangle}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{C}{n^{\nu+1/2}},\ \ \ C>0,

where

y_{\nu}^{-\langle n\rangle}(x)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}Y_{\nu,i}^{-}\hat{P}_{i}(x),

and this corresponds to the estimate (
9
) with the limit value
s=\nu+1/2
(see Remark
1
).

The obtained result can be transferred to the function

y_{\nu}^{+}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{for}~x\in[-1,0),\\
x^{\nu}&\text{for}~x\in[0,1],\end{array}\right.

and its expansion coefficients
Y_{\nu,i}^{+}
with respect to the Legendre polynomials (
6
). The easiest way to prove this is using the equality
|Y_{\nu,i}^{-}|=|Y_{\nu,i}^{+}|
, which follows from the relationship between expansion coefficients of an arbitrary function from
L_{2}(\Omega)
and its reflection
[
25
]
. Therefore, the same result holds for the function
g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
. This result can be extended to the function
f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
given that its degree of smoothness is more by one than the degree of smoothness of
g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
.

Thus,

\varepsilon_{g}^{\langle n\rangle}\approx\frac{C^{g}}{n^{\min\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\}+1/2}},\ \ \ \varepsilon_{f}^{\langle n\rangle}\approx\frac{C^{f}}{n^{\min\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\}+3/2}},

(40)

where
C^{g},C^{f}>0
are some constants. Moreover, we can assume that
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
are real positive numbers, and if we consider
g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}
not as a density, but as some function not bound by the probability theoretical framework, then the condition
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}>-1/2
is admissible. Such a convergence reflects that
g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}^{(s)}\in W_{2}^{s}(\Omega)
and
f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}^{(s)}\in W_{2}^{s+1}(\Omega)
subject to
s<\min\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\}+1/2
,
\sup s=\min\{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\}+1/2
. It corresponds to the estimate (
9
).

Numerical Experiments

In this section, we present the results of joint estimation of the density and distribution function for two examples that use a two-parameter family (
30
) of densities with different degrees of smoothness. In these examples, the results are presented in tables that contain errors of projection estimates of the density and distribution function in
L_{2}(\Omega)
. We study the dependence of error on the projection expansion length (for the maximum degree
n
of the Legendre polynomials, values 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 are used, i.e.,
n=2^{k+2}
for
k=0,1,\dots,4
), on the sample size
N=2^{m+9}
for
m=0,1,\dots,18
, and on the degree of smoothness of the approximated density (see Examples
1
and
2
below). Algorithm
2
is applied for estimation.

These examples show the approximation errors
\varepsilon_{g}^{\langle n\rangle}
and
\varepsilon_{f}^{\langle n\rangle}
, which are calculated using the formulae (
39
), i.e., deterministic components of projection estimate errors. In tables, they are in rows marked by the symbol
*
. The remaining rows contain errors that include deterministic and stochastic components. The formulae for these errors follow from Parseval’s identity:

\displaystyle\bar{\varepsilon}_{g}^{\langle n\rangle}

\displaystyle=\biggl(\int_{-1}^{1}g_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}^{2}(x)dx-\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}G_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},i}^{2}+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}(G_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},i}-\bar{G}_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},i})^{2}\biggr)^{1/2},

\displaystyle\bar{\varepsilon}_{f}^{\langle n\rangle}

\displaystyle=\biggl(\int_{-1}^{1}f_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}^{2}(x)dx-\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}F_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},i}^{2}+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}(F_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},i}-\bar{F}_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2},i})^{2}\biggr)^{1/2},

Conclusions

A distinctive feature of algorithms proposed in this paper is the jointly obtaining projection estimates of the density and distribution function of a random variable. In addition, a problem of conditional optimization of algorithms is solved under assumption that estimated functions belong to the Sobolev–Slobodetskij space. This assumption allows one to use a more precise estimate of the convergence rate for projection expansions compared to the case, when the ordinary Sobolev space or the space of continuously differentiable functions are used. Numerical experiments confirm the obtained theoretical results.

The proposed algorithms and conditionally optimal relationships for their parameters ensure the minimum complexity when obtaining projection estimates to achieve the required approximation accuracy.
