Mandarin fish ILF2 negatively regulates type I interferon signaling by promoting autophagy–lysosome‑dependent turnover of IRF3
Authors: Xiang Yang Wu, An Ning Pang, Shan Nan Chen, Mei Shan Lu, Xiao Rui Liu, Zheng Liu, Xue Yun Peng, Bo Li, Lan Hao Liu, Nan Li, Pin Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The ilf2 was expressed constitutively in S. chuatsi and induced in IHSV-infected MFF-1 cells.•The overexpression of ILF2 inhibited significantly the expression of type I IFN and ISGS.•ILF2 reduced markedly the IFNh and ISRE promoter activities induced by RLR signaling molecules.•ILF2 modulated negatively the antiviral immunity by reducing IRF3 protein expression in mandarin fish.

Introduction

Innate immunity serves as the first line of defense against pathogenic microbial invasion. As a central part of innate immune response, the IFN system has been extensively investigated. During viral infection, retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I) and melanoma differentiation-associated protein 5 (MDA5) can recognize viral double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) via their C-terminal regulatory domains, although multiple cytosolic receptors are capable of sensing dsRNA which can be produced by RNA and DNA viruses, and even transposon transcripts and mitochondrial RNAs [1]. Upon recognition, RIG-I and MDA5 undergo conformational changes, which subsequently enable binding and interaction with the downstream adaptor protein mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein (MAVS) [2,3], followed by recruiting of TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1), phosphorylation of IRF3 and IRF7, leading to the production of type I IFNs [4,5]. IFNs can further activate the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, inducing the expression of various IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) with the establishment of host antiviral defense system [6].

Appropriate levels of IFNs are crucial for host antiviral defense, whereas abnormal IFN expression can cause adverse effects on the organism [7,8]. Thus, hosts have evolved diverse regulatory mechanisms to control the precise expression of IFNs. Previous studies have revealed that multiple host proteins can interact with various critical factors in the IFN signaling pathway, thus influencing the expression of IFNs. For example, porcine integrin β1 (ITGβ1) can interact specifically with the caspase activation and recruitment domains (CARDs) of MDA5, thereby enhancing its recruitment of dsRNA derived from porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV), which in turn facilitates IFN production [9]. In humans, ubiquitin-specific protease 5 (USP5) can suppress the expression of IFNs and ISGs by deubiquitinating MAVS and IRF3, thereby promoting the replication of human enterovirus A71 (EV-A71) [10]. In zebrafish, N-myc downstream-regulated gene 1a (NDRG1a) can inhibit IFN expression by mediating the ubiquitination and degradation of IRF7 [11]. In contrast, crucian carp HERC7 can promote the degradation of IRF7 mRNA, thereby exerting negative regulation on IFN expression [12]. Apart from the above regulatory mechanisms governing IFN signaling, other regulatory pathways remain to be explored. As a multifunctional protein closely associated with transcriptional regulation and innate immunity, ILF2 deserves an in-depth investigation for its immunoregulatory role in modulating the IFN signaling cascade.

ILF2 is an important member of the NFAR family, and the encoded protein contains a zinc finger-associated domain (DZF) and a double-stranded RNA-binding domain [13]. At present, ILF2 has been extensively studied in humans. On the one hand, it can be assembled into diverse complexes with various related proteins via the DZF domain; on the other hand, ILF2 can specifically form heterodimers with ILF3 to collaboratively regulate gene expression at transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels [[14], [15], [16]]. Research on the biological function of ILF2 has a rather long history. It was initially reported that ILF2 mediated the cell-specific regulation of interleukin-2 (IL-2) expression and also acted as a pivotal regulator of immune cell activation [17]. Accumulating evidence has confirmed that ILF2 is involved widely in multiple core physiological processes, including RNA transcription, protein synthesis, DNA damage repair, cell proliferation and apoptosis [[18], [19], [20], [21], [22]]. Recently, the regulatory role of ILF2 in disease progression has attracted increasing attention, especially in tumor and inflammatory disorders. ILF2 was found to promote abnormal proliferation of tumor cells by regulating cell cycle progression and apoptotic balance [23], and it is highly transcribed in human breast cancer cells, indicating its involvement in breast cancer progression [24].

On the other hand, ILF2 has been reported in inflammatory processes. In psoriasis, ILF2 can mediate the excessive proliferation of keratinocytes and the occurrence of cutaneous inflammation in a KLHDC7B divergent transcript (KLHDC7B-DT)-dependent manner [25]. Meanwhile, circRNA 406,961 can bind to ILF2 and activate the STAT3/JNK signaling pathway, thereby modulating PM2.5-triggered inflammatory responses in human bronchial epithelial cells [26]. In addition, it is verified that NF45 (ILF2) and NF90 (ILF3) can bind to HIV-1 RNA to regulate viral gene expression and further affect HIV replication [27].

Studies on ILF2 have also been reported in other animals, including avian and fish. In mallard duck, ILF2 was found to mediate the degradation of IRF7 through the autophagy–lysosomal pathway, thus negatively regulating type I IFN expression [28]. But, duck-derived ILF2 was even reported to suppress the replication of duck hepatitis A virus type 1 by disrupting the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase activity of 3D polymerase [29]. However, research on ILF2 in fish has been related mostly with gene characterization and expression analysis. In tongue sole Cynoglossus semilaevis, two isoforms of ILF2 were both reported with immunomodulatory properties [30], while in grouper Epinephelus coioides and grass carp Ctenopharyngodon idellus, a single ILF2 gene was identified, and fish ILF2 was responsive to various stimuli, implying its role in innate immune responses [31,32]. The immune regulatory role of ILF2 remains to be elucidated in fish.

Mandarin fish Siniperca chuatsi, a perciform fish with economical importance in aquaculture in China, is highly susceptible to infections of various viruses [33,34]. In this study, ILF2 gene sequence was cloned in mandarin fish, and its constitutive and virus-induced expression was examined in fish and in cell line, respectively. The role of ILF2 in influencing the transcription of ifnh and ifnc, and the transcriptional activity of ifnh promoter and ISRE promoter was further investigated through transfection. The effect of ILF2 on MDA5, MAVS, TBK1 initiated type I IFN production was further analyzed, and the mechanism by which ILF2 modulates autophagy-lysosome‑dependent IRF3 protein turnover was characterized in fish in this study. These findings provide novel insights and theoretical support for deciphering inhibition mechanism of fish type I IFN transcription, which should have a value in developing antiviral infection strategy in fish.

Materials and methods

Fish, cell culture and virus

Mandarin fish weighing approximately 30 g each were purchased from a fish farm in Foshan City, Guangdong Province, and cultured in fish ponds for two months prior to experiments. All animal experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of the Institute of Hydrobiology, Chinese Academy of Sciences.

Human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK293T), mandarin fish fry (MFF-1) and Chinese perch stomach (CPS) cell lines were cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). HEK293T cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, whereas MFF-1 and CPS cells were maintained at 28 °C with 5% CO₂.

The infectious hemorrhagic syndrome virus (IHSV), a member in the Siniperhavirus within the family Rhabdoviridae, has been identified as a lethal pathogen to Siniperca chuatsi [35]. The IHSV strain CB2017 (GenBank accession number: OQ730265) utilized in the present study was originally described in a previous report [35]. IHSV was propagated in CPS cells; for infection, cells were incubated with IHSV in serum-free MEM. After 1 h viral adsorption, the inoculum was discarded, and cells were further cultured in DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS until sampling.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, gene cloning and quantitative real‑time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from various tissues of mandarin fish with Trizol® reagent (Ambion). To eliminate genomic DNA contamination, RNA samples were treated with RNase-free DNase I prior to reverse transcription. The integrity and concentration of RNA were determined using agarose gel electrophoresis and a spectrophotometer, respectively, followed by reverse transcription into cDNA using the HiScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme). Primers were designed according to the mRNA sequence of mandarin fish ilf2 (XM_044170529.1) obtained from the NCBI database, and the open reading frame (ORF) of ilf2 was amplified and cloned. Primer amplification efficiencies were verified, and stably expressed cellular endogenous housekeeping genes were screened under viral infection. Endogenous housekeeping RNA was used to normalize viral gene expression levels to eliminate quantitative errors resulted from viral cytotoxicity.

For primer efficiency validation, cellular cDNA was serially diluted in 5 folds to generate five concentration gradients. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) amplification was performed with three biological replicates and three technical replicates for each gradient. Standard curves were constructed with the log value of cDNA dilution fold as the x axis and Ct values as the y axis, and amplification efficiencies were calculated from the slope. Primers with amplification efficiencies ranging from 90% to 110% and a linear correlation coefficient R2 > 0.98 were used for subsequent quantification. Primers for ifnh, ifnc, IHSV-N, IHSV-P and IHSV-G genes were synthesized according to a previous report [35,36], and all primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

The qRT-PCR was performed on a QuantStudio 3 Real‑time PCR System. β-actin served as the reference gene, whose expression stability was confirmed among all tested samples. Forward and reverse primers were prepared as 10 μM stock solution, with each primer used at a final concentration of 0.5 μM in the 20 μL reaction system. Each reaction was carried out in 20 μL volume containing 10 μL SupRealQ Purple Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme), 1 μL of each forward and reverse primer, 1 μL template cDNA, and 7 μL ddH₂O. The amplification program was set as the followings: 50 °C for 2 min; 95 °C for 2 min; followed by 35 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 56 °C for 35 s. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method.

Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis

The amino acid sequences of ILF2 from mandarin fish and other species were retrieved from NCBI database. Sequence alignment and visualization were performed using DNAMAN 9.0 software. A phylogenetic tree was constructed with MEGA11 software using the Neighbor-joining method with 1000 bootstrap replicates, before being further optimized with the online tool ITOL (https://itol.embl.de/).

Expression analysis of ILF2

To examine the expression of ilf2 in different tissues of mandarin fish, three individuals with a body weight of approximately 30 g were sacrificed, and seven tissues, including heart, liver, spleen, trunk kidney, intestine, muscle and brain, were harvested. The mRNA level of ilf2 was analyzed by qRT-PCR.

To determine whether ilf2 was induced by viral infection, MFF-1 cells (about 5 × 105 cells per well) were seeded uniformly in 12‑well plates. Cells were infected with IHSV at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1, with parallel mock-infected controls. Cells of each group were collected at 0, 12 and 24 h post-infection (hpi), and qRT-PCR was also performed to determine the mRNA level of viral genes.

Plasmid construction, extraction and cell transfection

The ORF of mandarin fish ilf2 was inserted into the p3 × FLAG-CMV14 vector (FLAG-empty) for the construction of the recombinant expression plasmid, which was extracted using the E.Z.N.A.® Endo‑free Plasmid Mini Kit Ⅱ (Omega). Recombinant vectors were transfected into MFF‑1 cells and HEK293T cells using FUGENE® HD (Promega) and Exfect Transfection Reagent (Vazyme), respectively, for subsequent experiments.

Western blotting and coimmunoprecipitation

Western blotting was performed as follows: cell lysates were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate‑polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‑PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) non-fat milk; afterwards, the membranes were sequentially incubated with primary and corresponding secondary antibodies. Primary antibody incubation was allowed at 4 °C overnight, while secondary antibody incubation was conducted at room temperature for 1 h. Both primary and secondary antibodies were diluted at a ratio of 1:5000. Blot images were captured with the ChemiDoc MP imaging system, and densitometric quantification of protein bands was carried out using ImageJ software.

Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) was conducted as described below: proteins were lysed with 500 μL lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors for 2 h. After centrifugation, 100 μL of the supernatant was reserved as input. The remaining lysate was mixed with 30 μL magnetic beads and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Beads were washed five times with 1 mL pre‑cooled PBS buffer, resuspended in 80 μL PBS and 20 μL loading buffer, followed by boiling, and then Western blotting. Primary antibodies used were anti‑FLAG (Vazyme, RA1003–01) and anti‑HA (Vazyme, RA1004–01).

Dual‑luciferase reporter assay

Luciferase reporter plasmids containing ifnh and ISRE promoter sequences were constructed separately and validated in previous studies [36]. MFF‑1 cells (about 5 × 104 cells per well) were seeded uniformly in 48‑well plates and transfected at 50% confluence. For single transfections: 0.1 μg FLAG empty vector or ILF2 recombinant vector, 0.1 μg ifnh or ISRE promoter reporter plasmid, and 0.01 μg PRL‑TK as internal reference were co‑transfected. Cells were harvested 24 h post‑transfection for detection. For poly(I:C) stimulation groups: the same transfection system was used. At 24 h post‑transfection, 1 μg/mL poly(I:C) was added to each well, and cells were harvested 6 h after stimulation. For co‑transfection groups: 0.1 μg IFN‑β or ISRE promoter reporter plasmid and 0.01 μg PRL‑TK were co‑transfected with the following plasmid combinations (0.1 μg each): FLAG-empty + HA-empty; FLAG-empty + HA-MDA5, HA-MAVS, HA-TBK1, HA-IRF3 or HA-IRF7; FLAG-ILF2 + HA-MDA5, HA-MAVS, HA-TBK1, HA-IRF3 or HA-IRF7. Cells were collected 24 h after transfection. The cells were washed twice with PBS, lysed with 100 μL lysis buffer per well on a shaker for 30 min. Luciferase activities were measured using the Dual‑Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) and GloMax®‑Multi JR Detection System (Promega). All results were calculated from three independent experiments.

Antiviral assay

MFF-1 cells were evenly seeded into 12-well (about 5 × 105 cells per well) culture plates and transfected separately with recombinant FLAG-ILF2 plasmid or FLAG-empty. At 48 h post-transfection, one batch of cells was harvested to detect the transcription of immune-related genes. The remaining cells were infected with IHSV at an MOI of 0.1. After 48 h continuous infection, cell samples were collected to analyze the expression of viral genes. Meanwhile, the cell culture supernatant from each group was collected, and viral titers were determined using the Tissue Culture Infectious Dose 50 (TCID₅₀) assay.

Investigating the effect of ILF2 on the protein level of IRF3

Eukaryotic cells rely on three canonical pathways to mediate protein degradation: the ubiquitin-proteasome system, autophagy, and the lysosomal pathway. In laboratory studies, MG132, 3‑methyladenine (3‑MA), and ammonium chloride (NH₄Cl) are commonly used pharmacological reagents to interfere with the above three protein‑degradation‑related routes [37]. It should be noted that these pharmacological reagents are not completely specific, and cross‑effects between pathways may exist. To further elucidate the molecular mechanism by which ILF2 negatively regulates type I IFN expression and its downstream signaling cascade, the impact of ILF2 on the protein abundance of IRF3 was investigated. HEK293T cells (about 5 × 105 cells per well) were seeded into 12-well plates for overnight incubation, then co-transfected with 0.5 μg HA-IRF3 together with either 0.5 μg empty FLAG vector or 0.5 μg FLAG-ILF2. Twenty-four hours after transfection, cells were treated with indicated reagents for 6 h; one set was treated with 20 mM NH₄Cl, 4 mM 3-MA or 20 μM MG132 alongside a DMSO control, while the other set was treated solely with DMSO. Total cell lysates were prepared for Western blotting with anti-Flag and anti-HA antibodies.

Statistical analysis

Data were displayed as mean ± standard error (SE) based on three independent experiments. Statistical comparisons were conducted with one-way ANOVA in SPSS 22.0 software. Asterisks denote significant differences, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant (* P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001).

Results

Sequence characters of mandarin fish ILF2

The ORF of the ILF2 gene isolated from mandarin fish spans 1164 base pairs (bp), which encodes a peptide consisting of 387 amino acids. The molecular weight of the ILF2 protein predicted from the amino acid sequence is 42.82 kDa, and its theoretical isoelectric point is 5.219. A multiple sequence alignment was carried out by utilizing the ILF2 amino acid sequences from several representative species, such as Cynoglossus semilaevis, Danio rerio, Homo sapiens, and gallus. The alignment showed that the ILF2 protein of mandarin fish exhibits high homology with the ILF2 proteins of the aforementioned species, and all these ILF2 proteins contain a highly conserved DZF and a double-stranded RNA-binding domain (Fig. 1A). Phylogenetic analysis was conducted based on the ILF2 amino acid sequences of multiple representative species, covering a wide range of taxa from insects to mammals. To explore the role of ILF2 in antiviral immune response, FLAG-ILF2 vector was transfected into MFF-1 cells, and qRT-PCR was used to determine the expression of type I IFNs. Similarly, ILF2 inhibited significantly poly(I:C)-induced activation of ifnh and ISRE promoters (Fig. 3C, 3D), and ILF2 also suppressed significantly the transcription levels of ifnh and ifnc (Fig. 3E, 3F). Compared with control cells, the overexpression of ILF2 in MFF-1 cells resulted in a significant increase in the expression levels of IHSV N, P, and G genes (Fig. 3G–I). ILF2 promotes autophagy–lysosome‑dependent turnover of IRF3

Following the co-transfection into HEK293T cells with a fixed dose of IRF3 plasmid and increasing amounts of ILF2 plasmid, ILF2 was observed to markedly decrease the protein expression of IRF3 (Fig. 5C), suggesting that ILF2 negatively regulates the type I IFN response by reducing IRF3 protein levels. The autophagy inhibitor 3‑MA and the lysosomal inhibitor NH₄Cl could reverse the ILF2‑mediated reduction in IRF3 protein abundance, whereas the proteasome inhibitor MG132 exhibited no significant rescue effect (Fig. 5D). Collectively, these results support the notion that ILF2 promotes autophagy–lysosome‑dependent turnover of IRF3.

Discussion

As a multifunctional factor, ILF2 in mallard duck was confirmed to regulate type I IFN response through IRF7 degradation [28]. In this study, ILF2 in a teleost was found to contain conserved features, with a DZF domain and a double-stranded RNA-binding domain. In particular, it may be indicated in mandarin fish that ILF2 can reduce type I IFN response activated by RLR signaling molecules through promoting autophagy–lysosome‑dependent turnover of IRF3.

In fish, ILF2 shows a pattern of wide expression across all tissues, with high expression levels in various tissues. In tongue sole, two isoforms of ilf2, named as nuclear factor (NF) 45 and NF45i in the original report, exhibited high expression levels in gills and muscle, respectively [30]. By contrast, ilf2 in grouper had a relatively high expression in skin and heart [31]. In the present study, ilf2 was ubiquitously distributed in various tissues of mandarin fish, with the most abundant expression observed in heart and liver. At present, it seems unlikely that a general expression pattern of ILF2 can be outlined in fish before its functional diversity can be further revealed. In mammals, ILF2 can be detected in a diversity of cell types [38], and is responsive to various stimuli [39,40]. In mandarin fish, ilf2 was significantly induced upon IHSV infection, implying its involvement in the host antiviral immune response. Even with limited upregulation, ilf2 still exerts biological functions during IHSV infection. Similarly, ilf2 expression in tongue sole was induced following viral and bacterial infection [41], and ilf2 in gilthead sea bream (Sparus aurata) was upregulated in response to parasite infection [42]. Collectively, ilf2 may be responsive to viral, bacterial as well as parasitic infections, although a general pattern cannot be revealed due to rather limited literature on ILF2 in fish.

However, available evidence indicates that ILF2 is involved in antiviral immunity. In mallard duck, ILF2 was reported to negatively regulate type I IFN response by degrading IRF7, thus facilitating the replication of duck Tembusu virus (DTMUV) [28]. In contrast, human ILF2 can significantly inhibit encephalitis virus replication in vitro [39], suggesting that the antiviral function of ILF2 may differ in different species or in relation with different pathogens. In this study, the reduction in the expression of ifnh and ifnc was observed in ILF2 transfected cells, with also lower activities of ifnh and ISRE promoters, which promoted the replication of IHSV. It is thus indicated that mandarin fish ILF2 functions as a negative regulator in the antiviral immune response. On the other hand, ILF2 was found to suppress markedly ifnh and ISRE promoter activities induced by RLR cascade components, MDA5, MAVS, TBK1, IRF3 and IRF7. Interestingly, in the present study, mandarin fish ILF2 was found to bind with IRF3 to promote or mediate autophagy–lysosome‑dependent turnover of IRF3. Previously, ILF2 in mallard duck has been reported to have a role in regulating negatively type I IFN production by mediating the degradation of IRF7 [28]. Since IRF3 has not been so far reported in duck, IRF7 may then have a central role in regulating type I IFN response [43]. However, in the present study, ILF2‑mediated IRF3 protein turnover, rather than IRF7, was examined in HEK293T cells for comparison with the duck‑derived findings [28]. The proposed ILF2 and IRF3 interaction mechanism may need to be further confirmed in mandarin fish cells with preferable endogenous proteins.

In conclusion, ILF2 as a key immune regulator shows conserved sequence features in vertebrates. In mandarin fish, ILF2 can negatively regulate type I IFN transcription by promoting or mediating autophagy–lysosome‑dependent turnover of IRF3. This mechanism lays a theoretical foundation for in-depth analysis of the immune regulatory network in teleost fish.
