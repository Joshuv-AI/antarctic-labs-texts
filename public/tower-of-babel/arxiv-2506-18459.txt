X-ray emission in IllustrisTNG circum–cluster environments
Celine Gouin, Daniela Galárraga-Espinosa, Massimiliano Bonamente, Stephen Walker, Mohammad Mirakhor, Richard Lieu, Clotilde Laigle, Etienne Bonnassieux, Charlotte Welker, Stefano Gallo, Tony Bonnaire, Jade Paste

Abstract
Context. An excess of soft X–ray emission ( ∼ 0.2 − 1 \sim 0.2-1 keV) above the contribution from the hot intra–cluster medium (ICM) has been detected in a number of galaxy clusters, including the Coma cluster. The physical origin of this emitting medium above hot ICM has not yet been determined, especially whether it be thermal or non-thermal. Aims. We aim to investigate which gas phase and gas structure more accurately reproduce the soft excess radiation from the cluster core to the outskirts, using simulations. Method. By using the IllustrisTNG simulation (TNG300), we predict the radial profile of thermodynamic properties and the Soft-X–ray surface brightness of 138 clusters within 5 × r 200 5\times r_{200} . Their X–ray emission is simulated for the hot ICM gas phase ( T ≥ 10 7 ​ K T\geq 10^{7}\ K ), the entire Warm–Hot medium at temperature T = 10 5 − 7 ​ K T=10^{5-7}K (WARM), and for the diffuse and low–density Warm–Hot Intergalactic Medium (WHIM). Results. Inside clusters, the soft excess appears to be produced by substructures of the WARM gas phase which host dense warm clumps (i.e, the Warm Circum–Galactic Medium, WCGM), and in fact the inner soft excess is strongly correlated with substructure and WCGM mass fractions. Outside of the virial radius, the fraction of WHIM gas that is mostly inside filaments connected to clusters boosts the soft X–ray excess. The more diffuse the gas is, the higher the soft X-ray excess beyond the virial region. Conclusion. The thermal emission of WARM gas phase, in the form of WCGM clumps and WHIM diffuse filaments, reproduces well the soft excess emission that was observed up to the virial radius in Coma and in the inner regions of other massive clusters. Moreover, our analysis suggests that soft X–ray excess is a proxy of cluster dynamical state, with larger excess being observed in the most unrelaxed clusters.



II - Possible origins of the soft X–ray excess emission

1
Introduction

A hot and diffuse intra–cluster medium (ICM) is the dominant reservoir of baryonic matter in galaxy clusters, with a typical mass fraction (
f_{\mathrm{gas}}
) of 10-20% of the total mass that is required to keep the hot gas in hydrostatic equilibrium with an unseen dark matter component
(
Allen et al. 2004
;
Landry et al. 2013
;
Mantz et al. 2022
, e.g.)
.
With typical temperatures of
\log_{10}T(\mathrm{K})\geq 7

(
Leccardi & Molendi 2008
, e.g.)
, the hot ICM is readily observable with imaging X–ray detectors, especially at photon energies
\gtrsim 1
keV where the inter–stellar medium (ISM) Galactic absorption is less significant
(
Dickey & Lockman 1990
;
Kalberla et al. 2005
;
HI4PI Collaboration et al. 2016
, e.g.)
.

At large scales, different gas phases dominate different cosmic web environments, according to cosmological hydrodynamical simulations
(
Martizzi et al. 2019
)
. In general, densest structures such as cluster cores are mainly in the form of hot plasma. In contrast, filaments and sheets are mostly populated by warm gas, and voids are dominated by cold gas in the form of Intergalactic Medium (IGM). Matter flows from filaments to clusters in the large scale filamentary network
(
Hahn et al. 2015
)
. In this context, hydrodynamical cosmological simulations are our best option for understanding the physics of this gas accreting from filaments to clusters. Numerous physical processes are thus explored in state-of-the-art simulations, such as the turbulence induced by large cosmic flow
(
Vallés-Pérez et al. 2021
;
Mohapatra et al. 2022
)
, merging events and accretion shocks
(
Shi et al. 2020
, see e.g.)
, the presence of cool-core
(
Braspenning et al. 2023
)
, and the dependence on baryonic physic models, such as Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) feedback, in pushing out gas from cluster centers
(
Hahn et al. 2017
)
.
In particular, by using the recent results from eROSITA,
Bahar et al. 2024
have recently attempted to constraint AGN feedback from X-ray emission of groups.
Recently,
Shreeram et al. 2025a
have proposed a novel forward model to constrain the AGN X-ray luminosity jointly with the radial hot gas distribution, based mock X-ray emission from IllustrisTNG simulation
(
Shreeram et al. 2025b
)
.

At soft X-ray energies (
\sim
0.1-1 keV), some clusters show an excess of soft X-ray emission within the virial radius, defined as an excess of counts above the expected soft X-ray emission from the hot ICM gas, modeled from its harder X-ray emissions.
This soft excess was first observed in
EUVE
observations of the Coma and Virgo clusters
(
Lieu et al. 1996b
;
Lieu et al. 1996a
;
Bowyer et al. 1996
)
, and later observed by
ROSAT
in different clusters
(
Bonamente et al. 2001a
;
Bonamente et al. 2001b
;
Bonamente et al. 2002
)
.
This excess was also observed by
XMM–Newton

(
Nevalainen et al. 2003
;
Kaastra et al. 2003
;
Bonamente et al. 2005
, e.g.)
,
Beppo-SAX

(
Bonamente et al. 2001c
)
and even
Chandra

(
Bonamente & Nevalainen 2011
)
.
Measurements of the soft excess rely especially on the accuracy of
(a) the estimate of the hot ICM contribution to the soft band,
(b) the background subtraction, and
(c) the effect of the foreground Galactic absorption, which is both time– and space–variable
(
Snowden et al. 1994
;
Snowden et al. 1995
, e.g.)
.
Although some of the detection have been questioned or revised based on uncertainties on those aspects of the data analysis
(
Bowyer et al. 1999
;
Nevalainen et al. 2007
;
Bonamente & Nevalainen 2011
, e.g.)
, the soft excess is now an established observational feature of some galaxy clusters. In particular, we focus here on the comparison with the strong soft excess out to the virial radius for the Coma cluster, one of the most massive and nearby clusters
(
Bonamente et al. 2003
;
Bonamente et al. 2009
;
Mirakhor & Walker 2020
;
Bonamente et al. 2022
)
.

The interpretation of this soft excess has so far been elusive, given that it has not been possible to confirm with high statistical probability the presence of emission lines associated with the excess
(
Kaastra et al. 2003
;
Finoguenov et al. 2003
, as reported by)
, given the limited resolution of the X–ray instruments.
It is unclear whether the soft excess originates from within the cluster’s virial radius
(
Cheng et al. 2005
, e.g., as originally investigated by)
, or from external filaments that are projected against the cluster
(
Churazov et al. 2023b
, e.g., as investigated in)
. It is therefore of interest to study the properties of the baryonic phases both within the virial radius, where the hot gas is the dominant baryonic form, and in its immediate vicinity, where warm sub–virial gas is still accreting towards clusters, and is expected to be densest and therefore more readily observable.

In
Gouin et al. 2023
(hereafter Paper I), we began investigating the gas properties and soft X-ray emission in the circum-cluster environment, defined as the region within a few virial radii of galaxy clusters, using the IllustrisTNG simulation
(
Pillepich et al. 2018
;
Nelson et al. 2019
)
. In this follow-up paper, we focus on the origin of the soft X–ray emission, and its dependence on gas properties.
This paper is structured as follows:
in Sect.
2
, we describe our numerical methods to predict soft X–ray emission in the circum-cluster environment.
In Sect.
3
, we compare the soft X–ray predictions to current observations, and discuss the detectability of warm gas.
In Sect.
4
, we investigate the possible origin of this soft excess as a function of gas properties and gas structures. In Sect.
5
, we discuss our results in the context of recent observations and predictions, and we present our conclusions in Sect.
6
.

2
Numerical predictions of the soft X–ray emission

The IllustrisTNG simulation and the methods of analysis used for this project were presented in
Gouin et al. 2023
, and they are briefly summarized here.
We use the TNG300-1 simulation at
z=0
, the largest volume (
\sim 300\ \textrm{Mpc}^{3}
) in the IllustrisTNG suite with the highest spatial resolution,
which provides both a statistically significant cluster sample and detailed modeling of baryonic physics
(
Pillepich et al. 2018
;
Nelson et al. 2019
)
.
We divide the baryons into different phases defined in Tab.
1
: the hot ICM at
\log T(\mathrm{K})\geq 7
, and the warm gas at
5\leq\log T(\mathrm{K})\leq 7
(WARM), which is further subdivided into the more dense warm–hot circumgalactic medium (WCGM) at
n_{e}\geq 10^{-4}
cm
-3
and the more diffuse warm–hot intergalactic medium (WHIM) at lower density that is typically found in filaments
(
Galárraga-Espinosa et al. 2021
;
Gouin et al. 2022
, see e.g.)
. Thermodynamic quantities of interest such as the density, temperature, chemical abundance and clumpiness of the gas are averaged in spherical shells out to
5\times r_{200}
, which is the outermost radius considered.
As explained in Paper I, we do not compute the thermodynamic profiles (and thus the soft X–ray emission) of the WCGM gas alone, because there are not enough particles to obtain smooth profiles due to the large fluctuations in their distribution.
Instead of the WCGM phase alone, we combined the WCGM and WHIM gas into the WARM gas phase to explore the
soft X–ray emission of the diffuse (WHIM) and clumpy (WCGM) warm gas, in contrast to the hot ICM.

2.1
Thermodynamic quantities

We begin by providing a summary of the gas profiles for the two dominant gas phases in cluster environment, the hot ICM and the WARM gas.
These two phases are necessary to simulate the X-ray emission, given
that they are the two dominant gas phases in the circum-cluster environments
(
Martizzi et al. 2019
;
Gouin et al. 2022
)
.
The methodology for computing the radial profiles of thermodynamic properties is detailed in Paper I, and it is summarized here. For each cluster, we compute radial profiles of thermodynamic quantities within spherical shells centered on the cluster, with cluster-centric distance normalized by
r_{200}
. Each profile is obtained by averaging over gas cells of a given phase, using a volume-weighted average for each gas cell.

Figure
1
illustrates the averaged radial profiles of gas density, temperature, and metal abundance for our three main cluster mass bins.
The top left panel displays the mean density within spherical radial shells centered on the clusters. We found that the radial density of WARM gas are significantly influenced by cluster mass. Compared to high-mass clusters, low-mass clusters contain denser WARM gas within approximately the virial radius, with a different behavior beyond it.
The radius where the WARM density becomes higher that HOT gas density occurs at about
r=0.9\ r_{200}
for
M_{1}
,
1.3\ r_{200}
for
M_{2}
, and
2.1\ r_{200}
for
M_{3}
, showing that the hot ICM dominates the cluster region out to larger radii for more massive clusters.
In the top right panel, we can see that there is no dependence of WARM temperature on cluster mass, unlike for the hot ICM gas. As expected and observationally well established, clusters with higher masses have hotter ICM temperatures compared to lower mass ones, due to their deeper gravitational wells, which also naturally accounts for the faster transition from HOT-to-WARM in lower mass clusters.
In the bottom left panel, the WARM gas exhibits significantly higher metal abundance than the ICM, with a radial gradient starting from near-Solar levels at the center and decreasing to approximately 10% Solar at the virial radius. Beyond this radius, the WARM phase shows considerably sub-Solar abundance. The metallicity profile aligns with findings from the TNG-Cluster Simulation
(
Nelson et al. 2023
)
, indicating minor sensitivity to halo mass and achieving about
0.4\ Z_{\odot}
in the cluster centers.
Comparisons between our simulated density, temperature, and metallicity profiles and both observations and other simulations were presented in Paper I, showing good agreement.

The X–ray surface brightness is proportional to the square of the density, and thus, it is highly sensitive to inhomogeneities of the emitting phases. The radial profiles of the clumpiness factor of the various phases is defined as

C=\dfrac{\langle n^{2}\rangle}{\langle n\rangle^{2}}\,,

(1)

with
n
the gas density
(see
Nagai & Lau 2011
, for a definition)
.
In the bottom right panel of Fig.
1
, we show the clumpiness profile of the HOT and WARM gas. The WARM gas is substantially more clumpy than the hot ICM, both within and beyond the virial radius, and for all cluster mass bins. Moreover, there is a positive correlation between the clumping of the WARM gas and the cluster mass beyond
0.5\times r_{200}
, such that more massive clusters have clumpier WARM gas around them. The clumpiness of the gas plays a crucial role when investigating subsequent X–ray emissions. In fact, we will demonstrate that the clumpier the WARM gas, the higher the soft X–ray excess.
Equivalent clumpiness measurements, along with comparisons between our analysis, observations, and other simulations, were also presented in Paper I.

The mass of the various gas phases is obtained as a straightforward integration of the density profiles of Fig.
1
. An estimate of the total mass implied by the assumption of hydrostatic equilibrium (HSE) can be also calculated (as shown in Paper I). In this paper, we focus instead of the relative ratios of baryonic masses, and we do not discuss hydrostatic masses.

Figure
2
shows the radial profiles of the integrated ICM, WARM and WHIM gas masses (top panel) as a function of cluster mass.
In the bottom panels, we illustrates the mass ratios of WARM–to–HOT, WHIM–to–HOT and WCGM–to–HOT, computed as integrated gas mass profiles.
This analysis of the IllustrisTNG simulation provides a number of useful clues as to the whereabouts of baryons in the circum–cluster environments:

•

In the inner regions of clusters (
r\leq 0.5\times r_{200}
), only lower–mass clusters have a significant amount of WARM gas, at a level of
\sim 0.1-1
% of the hot ICM. This WARM gas is in the WCGM gas phase, as expected previously from the high density of the WARM gas inside this low mass clusters.

•

Near the virial radius, the majority of WARM gas in lower–mass clusters is in the form of the diffuse WHIM gas, while high–mass clusters have comparable amounts of WCGM and WHIM gas mass. This is expected from the ICM radial extension of higher–mass clusters that are located relatively further away from their center compared to lower–mass clusters.

•

Interestingly, clusters appear to have a constant radius of around
2\times r_{200}
where the WARM gas mass is approximately
2\times 10^{13}
M
⊙
, independently of the cluster mass. Beyond this radius, the WARM mass increases in proportion to cluster mass, as expected. At these large radii (
r>2\times r_{200}
), almost all the WARM gas is in the form of the diffuse WHIM, whereas the WCGM clumps represent only about
\penalty\ 5\%
of the WARM gas mass.

2.3
The X–ray surface brightness

The X–ray surface brightness is defined as

S_{X}(\theta)=\dfrac{1}{4\pi(1+z)^{4}}\int_{l}n_{e}n_{H}\Lambda(T,A)\,dl(\theta),

(2)

and it is in units of [erg cm
-2
s
-1
Srad
-1
], and isotropy of the radiation is assumed by the
4\pi
factor (see Eq. 5 of Paper I).
In Eq.
2
,
\Lambda(T,A)
is the emissivity of the plasma, calculated in [keV s
-1
cm
3
] by the
pyAtomDB
software as shown in Paper I, and it is a function of the average plasma temperature and abundance;
n_{e}
and
n_{H}
are respectively the electron and hydrogen number density in [cm
-3
], and the integration
is along the sightline at a give projected radial distance
\theta
.
The X–ray surface brightness is also known as the intensity of radiation
I
, or energy emitted per unit time, solid angle and detector area perpendicular to the radiation. The intensity is a convenient astrophysical quantity since it is a (nearly) distance or redshift–independent quantity, given that the solid angle of the emitted radiation and the surface area of the detector scale in opposite ways with distance between the detector and the source
(
Rybicki & Lightman 1979
, e.g.)
.
Since the radiation is optically thin, the surface brightness is obtained as an integration along the sightline at a constant projected angular distance
\theta
of
n_{e}n_{H}\Lambda(T,A)
, in 50 radial bins between 0.05 and
5\times r_{200}
that correspond to the same number of cylindrical shells, as described in detailed in Paper I.

Considering this formalism, we use the thermodynamic radial profiles of each cluster to predict it associated X–ray surface brightness profile. Figure
3
shows the average surface brightness profiles for our three cluster mass ranges, and by considering the X–ray band of 0.2-0.4 keV.
Notice that the result and their interpretation are very similar for the second soft X–ray band of interest (0.2-1 keV), and that band is not illustrated in the figure.
We highlight the relative importance of the X–ray surface brightness from the different gas phase (ICM, WARM and WHIM) as a function of the cluster–centric distance.
This emission ratio between HOT and WARM gas must be consistent with the observed soft X-ray excess, defined as the excess counts above the expected hot ICM emission, and will be compared to recent observations later in Sect.
3
.
We can first see that the hot ICM dominates the soft X–ray emission up to approximately the virial radius (
<1\ r_{200}
) for all mass ranges, as expected.
To highlight the importance of other gas phase on Soft-X-ray emission, the lower panels of Figure
3
plot the WARM–to-ICM and WHIM–to-ICM surface brightness ratios for the the three mass samples.
Based on these radial profiles, we interpret the main features of the soft X–ray emission from the different gas phases in CCE:

•

At all radii and at for all mass ranges, it appears that the WCGM gas present in the WARM phase systematically boosts the soft-X emission, due to its higher density. In contrast,
the diffuse WHIM gas alone produces less soft-X ray emission per unit mass.

•

Within the virial radius (
1\times r_{200}
), the dominant source of soft X–ray emission is the hot ICM. The second major contribution from the sub–virial gas is by the high–density WCGM, with the diffuse WHIM providing almost no emission up to
0.5\times r_{200}
. This is true regardless of cluster mass.
Indeed, comparing the WARM–to-ICM and WHIM–to-ICM surface brightness ratios, we can see that the WARM gas contribution is much more significant than that of the WHIM, inside clusters (
<1\times r_{200}
).
This strong radial evolution of the WARM–to–ICM surface brightness ratio, reaching
100\%
at
1\times r_{200}
, is due to the rapid increase of WARM gas mass inside clusters illustrated in Fig.
2
.

•

At the periphery of the cluster and beyond (
>1\times r_{200}
), the WARM gas predominantly contributes to the soft X–ray surface brightness. Notably, in this radial range, the greater the cluster mass, the more pronounced the emission from WARM gas. Analyzing the WARM-to-ICM and WHIM-to-ICM surface brightness ratios at these cluster distances reveals that the low-density WHIM gas primarily accounts for the soft X-ray excess, with contributions from the high-density WCGM serving as an enhancement. As highlighted in Fig.
2
, the WCGM gas mass is considerably lower than that of the WHIM gas at these radii.

3
Comparison between IllustrisTNG predictions and soft X–ray observations

Notice that the X–ray surface brightness has a large cluster–to–cluster scatter beyond the mass dependency. Therefore, we will focus more in depth on the relation between relevant gas properties (fraction of gas phase, location in sub–halos and filaments) and soft X–ray excess in Sec.
4
, after presenting the main comparison
between simulations and observations in this section.

Comparison with observations of the soft X–ray excess

The analysis of the soft X–ray excess described in Sec.
2.3
can now be used to compare the IllustrisTNG predictions with cluster observations. The soft excess emission is usually reported as a fractional excess of counts, in a given annular region, above the contribution from the hot ICM, which is equivalent to the ratio of the soft X–ray surface brightness of the HOT and WARM phases shown in Fig.
3
.

Figure

In the inner regions of clusters, the ICM temperature and abundance structure can be complex

Near the virial radius, the soft excess becomes of comparable surface brightness to the hot ICM, and therefore the details of the hot ICM are less important towards the detection of the soft excess. At those radii, the main challenge is an accurate background subtraction, given that the soft excess is typically lower than the diffuse soft X–ray background. The ideal means for the detection of the soft excess near and beyond the virial radius are large–scale surveys such as the ROSAT All–Sky Survey

\sim 2-3\times r_{200}

The soft excess emission is therefore detectable both in the inner cluster regions, and near the virial radius, consistent with the available observations.

To date, there are no available measurements of the soft excess in the region beyond the virial radius.

1-2\times r_{200}

4.2
Gas fraction definitions and computation

The fraction of gas phases

To explore the impact of gas phase abundance on the soft X–ray excess, we computed their mass fraction as:

f_{i}(r)=\frac{M_{i}(r)}{M_{\mathrm{all\ gas}}(r)}\,,

(3)

with
i
is the gas phase (WCGM or WHIM), and
r
is the cluster-centric radius enclosing the total gas mass (
M_{\mathrm{all\ gas}}
) and the mass of
the
i
–th gas phase (
M_{i})
.
We choose a radial range from
R=0
to
5\ r_{200}
in intervals of
1\times\ r_{200}
to accurately probe the radial evolution.

The gas fraction in filaments

To explore if the soft X–ray emission is the result of either WCGM gas surrounding galaxies and/or WHIM gas inside filaments, we select the gas as a function of their location, namely inside filaments and inside subhalos.
We consider gas located in filaments as gas cells enclosed within 2 Mpc/h around the filament spine
(
Galárraga-Espinosa et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2024
, which is a conservative value for the typical radial thickness of filaments based on their density profiles )
. Notice that we exclude the gas cells from galaxies, defined as those inside twice the half mass radius of each galaxy with stellar mass
M_{*}>10^{9}\ M_{\odot}/h
. The cosmic filaments are detected by using the T-REX algorithm on galaxies with stellar mass
M_{*}>10^{9}\ M_{\odot}/h
inside spheres of radius
10\times r_{200}
centered on each cluster
(
Gouin et al. 2021
, similar to)
.

We quantify the gas in filaments via

f^{\rm fil}_{\rm gas}(r)=\frac{M^{\in\mathrm{filaments}}_{\mathrm{all\ gas}}(r)}{M_{\mathrm{all\ gas}}(r)}\,.

(4)

Notice that, for each cluster, we remove the contribution of gas included in galaxies which are located in filaments, to separately investigate the impact of galaxies and filaments on the soft X–ray excess.

The gas fraction in substructures

Similarly, we compute the fraction of gas surrounding massive galaxies (
M_{*}>10^{9}\ M_{\odot}/h
) as:

f^{\rm gal}_{gas}(r)=\frac{M^{\in\mathrm{galaxies}}_{\mathrm{all\ gas}}}{M_{\mathrm{all\ gas}}}\,.

(5)

Notice that, for each cluster, we do not consider the main central galaxy, to exclude the contribution of the cluster itself. Again, we consider the gas cells in galaxies as the gas inside twice the half mass radius of each galaxy.

The subhalo mass fraction

Finally, the subhalo mass fraction is estimated from:

f_{\rm sub}(r)=\frac{\sum_{\rm sub\in r}M_{\rm sub}}{M_{\rm main}}\,,

(6)

where
M_{\rm sub}
is the total mass of a given subhalo inside the radial annulus
r
, and
M_{\rm main}
is the total mass of main subhalo for each cluster. Only for the computation of
f_{\rm sub}
, we consider all the subhalos extracted by the
Subfind
algorithm (without any mass selection) to fully capture the mass contained in clumps and thus reflect the relative clumpiness of the matter distribution.

We observe a smooth radial evolution in the soft excess influences, with a gradual transition. As detailed in Fig.

Beyond this mass-driven effect, we interpret the correlations between the soft X–ray excess and gas properties as follows:

(i)

Inside clusters (
r<r_{200}
), the soft X–ray excess correlates strongly with the WCGM gas fraction, the fraction of substructures, and the fraction of gas inside satellite galaxies. It suggests that the soft X–ray excess is mainly produced by substructures which host WCGM gas (with correlation factor of 0.5 in the top panel of Fig

0.3

Marini et al. 2025
recently discovered that luminous soft X–ray galaxy groups are more recently formed compared to their fainter counterparts with similar masses. Consequently, we speculate that the soft excess inside clusters, which correlates with the substructure fraction, is a proxy for a different dynamical state that is induced by a different mass assembly history.
One should keep in mind that
Marini et al. 2025
explore the soft X-ray luminosity, and not specifically the soft X-ray excess.

(ii)

Around clusters (
1<r/r_{200}<2
), the role of WHIM gas become more significant, with a correlation factor of
0.4

However, the correlation with other properties have high
p

The reason might be that these cluster boundaries are transitioning regions, where WARM gas is in-falling, out-flowing, and shocking during the accretion process
(
Rost et al. 2021
)
.

(iii)

Outside clusters (
2<r/r_{200}<5
), the soft excess is mostly induced by WHIM gas inside cosmic filaments. Indeed, we found that soft X–ray excess tend to correlate with the fraction of gas in filaments (only when excluding gas in galaxies), and strongly with the WHIM fraction (blue and yellow points in Fig.

The strong anti-correlation with the WCGM and other substructure fractions also suggests that the more diffuse the gas is, the higher the soft X–ray excess is in this region.

5
Discussion

Interpretations of the soft excess inside clusters

The soft excess detected inside clusters can be explained by the thermal emission of sub-virial WARM gas. Inside the virial radius the WARM gas is substantially more clumpy that the hot ICM (see Fig.
1
), with a significant mass fraction (from 0.1 to more than 10%, see Fig.
2
) producing the inner soft excess at a level of 0.1 to more than 10% of the hot ICM emission (see Fig.
3
). Its large scatter is consistent with the detection of only
\sim 40
% of clusters with a soft excess with ROSAT (see Fig.
5
).

Exploring the origin of inner soft X–ray excess, we found that the large observed scatter might be explained be the large variety of cluster dynamical state (see Fig.
10
). Indeed, we found that soft excess is stronger for the more clumpy clusterss. This trend is significant, with the excess decreasing from 1–10% for the more clumpy objects to less than 0.1% for the most homogeneous ones (see Fig.
10
).
Our results suggests that the soft X–ray excess is mainly produced by substructures which host WCGM gas (see Fig.
7
). This interpretation of a soft X–ray ”boost” inside clusters is also in agreement with past studies who found that inhomogeneities boost estimates of the X-ray emissivity
(
Planelles et al. 2017
;
Ghirardini et al. 2018
, see e.g.)
.Recently,
Marini et al. 2024
found that X-ray bright objects in their cores (i.e., within
r_{500}
) exhibit rapid mass accretion during the later stages of their evolution in the Magneticum simulation
(
Dolag et al. 2016
)
. Additionally,
Gouin et al. 2022
demonstrated a strong correlation between a cluster’s formation time, accretion rate, and degree of relaxation with the asymmetries in its gas distribution.
According to these studies one can infer that a large excess of soft X-rays is related to an unrelaxed clusters, as a consequence of a late-stage mass assembly history. In contrast, early-formed relaxed clusters should preferentially have lower soft excess.

Notice that even though we tend to reproduce most of the soft excess in the central regions as thermal emission from warm gas clumps, we remain limited by the simulation’s resolution (
\sim
kpc). In addition to the contribution from unresolved clumps, we also do not account here for non-thermal processes, as turbulence and shocks, injected in the ICM
(
Vallés-Pérez et al. 2021
;
Lebeau et al. 2025
)
.

Interpretations of the soft excess around clusters

Near the virial radius, the WARM gas density becomes dominant over the hot ICM. The exact value of this transition from ICM–to–WARM depends on the cluster mass (see Fig.
2
), in such a way that massive cluster have an ICM phase more spatially extended than low–mass ones. This finding suggests that the soft excess may preferentially occur at larger radii in more massive clusters, as was indeed observed in the case of Coma (see Fig.
5
,
Bonamente et al. 2003
).
The Coma cluster is the only case to date where a strong soft excess emission was detected up to the virial radius. The
Bonamente et al. 2002
study of a large sample of ROSAT clusters did not attempt to measure the soft excess beyond this radius where the data were dominated by background (see Fig.
6
), and therefore there is no statistically significant sample of clusters where these predictions have been tested to date. Our predictions of the soft X–ray excess reproduce very well the Coma observations near virial radius by considering both WCGM clumps and diffuse WHIM gas. This result is consistent with the
Churazov et al. 2023b
finding that the Coma soft excess cannot be reproduced by assuming WHIM gas alone.

Further insights into the origin of the soft X-ray excess in this region remain challenging to obtain, as evidenced by the fact that only the WHIM fraction shows a strong correlation with the excess (see Fig.
9
). This is due to complicated physical processes in cluster peripheries that are induced by both diffuse material collapsing towards filaments, and gas shock processes triggered by substructures.
In details,
Rost et al. 2021
have studied the complex velocity field of gas in this regions, showing that gas preferentially enters the cluster as part of filaments, and leaves the cluster outside filaments
(
Rost et al. 2024
, see also)
.
Additionally, the main gas accretion shock in the clusters is expected in this radial regions, e.g. near
1.5\times r_{200}
, according to
Arthur et al. 2019
. Similarly,
Vurm et al. 2023
have also found a formation of gas shock where filaments connected the hot atmosphere of clusters in C-EAGLE simulation.
Power et al. 2020
demonstrated that baryonic physics models on simulations converge on when and where shocks occur, but differing strength of shocks. Moreover,
Lebeau et al. 2024
discussed the distinction between gas and dark matter splashback radius, arguing that the detection of the splashback radius from pressure profiles could be more related to an accretion shock.
In this line,
Churazov et al. 2023a
have studied the complex geometry of the accretion shock in this region around Coma cluster.
This transition from HOT-to-WARM gas contains a complex mixture of infalling and outflowing gas, including potential “backsplash” gas
(
Gouin et al. 2022
;
Kotecha et al. 2022
)
, complicating the study of this peripheral region.

Interpretations of the soft excess outside clusters

It is important to point out that the meaning of soft X–ray excess outside clusters (
>2\ r_{200}
)
is different from its usual definition as an excess of soft-X emission above the hot ICM inside a given cluster. In this circum–cluster medium, the excess now refers to the relative importance
between soft X–ray emission from warm and that of hot gas induced by any structures that are neighbor to the central cluster, and not just the central cluster itself. An investigation
of this region will be the subject of a follow–up paper that will focus on the X-rays emission by distinguishing the structures (clusters, groups, filaments, and clumps) rather than the current X-ray ratio between gas phases. This study goes beyond the scopes of this paper.

In general, we predict that clusters of all masses feature a total WARM mass of
\sim 2\times 10^{13}
M
⊙
within
2\times r_{200}
, which corresponds to approximately 10% of the ICM mass for the more massive clusters, and
\sim 50
% for the low-mass clusters (see Fig.
2
).
We show that at
5\times r_{200}
the WARM halo mass is comparable to that of the hot ICM, specifically for the cluster mass range
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
with
\log M<14.5
.
Therefore, it indicates that the circum–cluster warm gas is a significant reservoir of baryons, of which the soft excess emission near the virial radius represents only the “tip of the iceberg”. This outer region, from 2 to 5
r_{200}
is dominated by WHIM gas, mostly located inside cosmic filaments
(
Galárraga-Espinosa et al. 2021
;
Tuominen et al. 2021
)
.
At this scale, our findings indicate that the soft excess is correlated with the WHIM fraction and gas abundance in filaments (refer to Fig.
9
), showing that lower fraction of substructures, leads to a higher soft excess (refer to Fig.
10
).

Detectability of warm gas in cluster peripheries

At present there are only a few detections of individual WHIM filaments in emission, starting from filaments towards superclusters
(
Kull & Böhringer 1999
;
Zappacosta et al. 2005
)
, “spider legs” around Abell 2744
(
Eckert et al. 2015
;
Gallo et al. 2024
)
, up to WHIM bridges between cluster pairs
(
Sakelliou & Ponman 2004
;
Werner et al. 2008
)
.
More recently, the detection of a Soft-X–ray filament between Abell 3391 and Abell 3395 with
eRosita
was reported by
Reiprich et al. 2021
, also studied by
Alvarez et al. 2018
, and a filament between Abell 2029 and Abell 2033
(
Mirakhor et al. 2022
)
, previously identified from ROSAT
(
Walker et al. 2012
)
.
Recently,
Dietl et al. 2024
identified an X-ray excess beyond thrice the virial radius of Abell 3667, indicative of an inter-cluster filament. Recent research focused on stacking inter-cluster gas filaments to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio for detecting warm gas
(
Tanimura et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
.
Another possibility is the detection of the WARM gas via their absorption lines.
Zhang et al. 2024a
have recently used the 30 most massive and relaxed IllustrisTNG clusters, to show that it enables the use of emission lines to measure the thermodynamic, chemical, and kinematic properties of the gas up to
r_{200}
and beyond
(
Tuominen et al. 2023
, see also)
. For further exploration,
Zhao et al. 2025
have recently provided forecasts for WHIM detections outside clusters with the future Hot Universe Baryon Surveyor missions.
Our results indicate that the soft X–ray emission from WARM gas in the cluster environment might be detectable towards the region immediately outside of the virial radius, as shown in Sec.
3.3
.
At those radii the gas is majority in the form of diffuse WHIM filaments, known to feature higher density towards galaxy clusters
(
Li et al. 2025
)
, we argue that the region outside of the virial radius of massive clusters is ideal to detect WHIM filaments in emission. The recent detection of soft X–ray emission near clusters
(
Reiprich et al. 2021
;
Mirakhor et al. 2022
;
Dietl et al. 2024
, e.g.)
support this scenario, and point at the circum–cluster environment as the region where to search of X–ray emission of filaments.

6
Conclusions

This paper has investigated the origin of soft X–ray emission in and near IllustrisTNG galaxy clusters.
In general, our results suggest that the WARM gas phase is responsible for an excess of soft X–ray emission above the contribution from the virialized HOT phase, as it has been observed by a variety of X–ray mission
(
Lieu et al. 1996b
;
Lieu et al. 1996a
;
Bonamente et al. 2002
;
Bonamente et al. 2003
, e.g.,)
. In detail, we distinguish three different radial regimes with different gas structures and properties responsible of this soft excess.
In the inner regions of clusters, this soft excess should be primarily due to the high–density and high–clumpiness WCGM phase. Towards the virial radius, we find that the soft excess is given by a combination of two distinct WARM gas phases, i.e. the high–density WCGM gas and the low–density WHIM gas. The soft X–ray predictions for the most massive clusters match very well with the observations of the Coma cluster, which is the only cluster to date to have reported soft excess emission to the virial radius
(
Bonamente et al. 2003
;
Bonamente et al. 2009
;
Bonamente et al. 2022
)
.
Beyond the virial radius, the diffuse WHIM phase becomes the dominant driver of the soft excess in cluster environments, consistent with the established picture of galaxy clusters being the nodes where WHIM filaments converge
(
Cen 1998
;
Davé et al. 2001
, e.g.)
. We find that the soft X–ray emission from WHIM filaments projecting towards the region immediately outside of the virial radius of clusters (i.e., within a few
\times r_{200}
) is a promising avenue for the detection of WHIM filaments in emission.
This radial picture is the natural way to interpret the soft excess as due to, WCGM in the inner regions, a combination of WCGM and WHIM near the virial radius
(
Bonamente et al. 2022
, e.g., as in Coma)
, and WHIM beyond the virial radius
(
Reiprich et al. 2021
;
Mirakhor et al. 2022
;
Dietl et al. 2024
)
.
Our analysis offers additional clues about the physical conditions that favor soft excess. In particular, clusters with larger substructure fraction are expected to have a larger central soft excess, and clusters surrounding by a larger fraction of diffuse WHIM will naturally feature a large excess beyond the virial radius. While additional details remain to be investigated, these findings provide a relatively simple first–order picture of the origin of the soft X–ray excess in circum–cluster environments.
