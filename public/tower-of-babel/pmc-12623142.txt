Scalp Actinomycosis Presenting as Chronic Discharging Sinuses: A Rare Case Report
Authors: Muhammad Umar Ahsan, Muhammad Saeed, Hafiz Arslan Mahmood, Nisar Ahmed, Somaiya Ahmed, Arooj Khan, Saba Shaheen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTActinomycosis is a rare, chronic granulomatous infection caused by anaerobic, filamentous bacteria of the genus Actinomyces, typically involving the cervicofacial, thoracic, or abdominopelvic regions. Scalp involvement is exceptionally rare and frequently misdiagnosed as a neoplastic or tubercular condition. We report the case of a 47‐year‐old male tandoor worker with a three‐year history of recurrent scalp infections who presented with firm, non‐tender swellings over the parietal and occipital scalp, complicated by discharging sinus tracts. MRI revealed ill‐defined subcutaneous lesions with no intracranial extension, raising suspicion for inflammatory or neoplastic pathology. Histopathological analysis of both incisional and excisional biopsies revealed basophilic bacterial colonies with radiating filaments surrounded by neutrophils—findings consistent with actinomycosis. Microbiological cultures and species‐level identification were not performed due to resource limitations. The patient underwent wide surgical excision followed by transposition flap reconstruction and completed a one‐month course of oral doxycycline and amoxicillin‐clavulanate. Recovery was uneventful, and no recurrence was observed on follow‐up. This case highlights the importance of considering actinomycosis in the differential diagnosis of chronic scalp lesions and underscores the diagnostic value of histopathology in resource‐constrained settings and highlights the importance of surgical management in achieving optimal clinical outcomes.

Summary Early recognition of scalp actinomycosis is essential, as prompt surgical excision with appropriate antibiotics leads to optimal outcomes and prevents misdiagnosis as malignancy or other chronic infections.

Introduction

Actinomycosis is a rare subacute to chronic infection characterized by granulomatous and suppurative infection. The chronic form has multiple abscesses that form sinus tracts and are associated with sulfur granules. The infection is caused by the gram‐positive filamentous nonacid‐fast anaerobic to microaerophilic bacteria, Actinomyces. There are currently around 40 known species of Actinomyces. About 70% of infections are due to either Actinomyces israelii or Actinomyces gerencseriae [1, 2].

Actinomycosis typically affects the abdominopelvic tract (20%), thoracopulmonary region (30%), or cervicofacial region (50%). This uncommon cause of head and neck enlargement with suppuration and leaking sinuses is a persistent bacterial infection [3]. The cervicofacial variant is the most prevalent; however, scalp involvement is extremely uncommon and frequently misdiagnosed due to its presentation mimicking other diseases, such as solid tumors, active Mycobacterium tuberculosis infections, nocardiosis, fungal infections, or other granulomatous disorders [4]. Actinomycosis is difficult to diagnose because of its vague symptoms and physical manifestations, unless a clinical examination reveals the characteristic “lumpy jaw” [5].

Cervicofacial actinomycosis has been called the great masquerader of head and neck disease. When masses first appear, they are frequently mistaken for benign or malignant neoplasms and the formation of pyogenic abscesses [6]. When lung diseases such as TB or cancer coexist, diagnosis might become much more difficult [7].

Scalp involvement in actinomycosis often presents a diagnostic challenge, as it can mimic other conditions such as tuberculosis or cutaneous malignancy. While CT and MRI are not specific for diagnosis, they are valuable in assessing the lesion's extent and anatomical localization. Notably, structures like the pericranium and loose connective tissue of the scalp are typically not seen on imaging and are only identified histopathologically. Inflammatory infiltration may manifest as abnormal subcutaneous signal intensities on MRI, although radiologic features are often indistinguishable from other nontraumatic scalp lesions [8, 9, 10]. Histopathologically, sulfur granules and the filamentous gram‐positive bacteria are the most significant findings [2]. All forms of actinomycosis can be treated with a high dose of intravenous penicillin for 2–6 weeks, followed by either amoxicillin or penicillin for 6–12 months. Clindamycin and tetracycline are examples of alternate antibiotic therapy for patients with penicillin allergies. Most of the time, excisional biopsy combined with prolonged penicillin treatment led to remission without recurrence [3, 6]. This report describes a rare case of chronic scalp actinomycosis in a middle‐aged migrant worker presenting with multiple discharging sinuses, successfully managed through wide surgical excision and scalp flap reconstruction. Given the exceptional rarity of scalp involvement in actinomycosis and its clinical resemblance to neoplastic conditions, this case highlights the diagnostic value of histopathology in the absence of culture‐based confirmation. The report contributes to the limited literature on scalp actinomycosis by presenting comprehensive clinical, radiological, histological, and surgical findings and offers practical insights for clinicians managing similarly complex presentations.

Case Presentation

A 47‐year‐old male migrant worker from Pakistan, employed as a tandoor worker in Saudi Arabia, presented to the outpatient department (OPD) with complaints of multiple fluid‐filled scalp lesions, with two large, prominent swellings in the parietal and occipital areas. The patient had a history of recurrent scalp infections associated with hair loss over the past 3 years. He received intermittent antibiotic treatment over 3 years prescribed empirically by local practitioners. The exact antibiotics and treatment durations could not be verified, but he reported multiple short courses that provided only temporary relief. There was no documented history of specific antitubercular, antifungal, or anti‐nocardial therapy. Tuberculosis was initially suspected due to the chronicity of symptoms, but neither microbiological testing nor imaging supportive of TB was ever performed. Two years ago, he developed multiple small scalp lesions that gradually increased in size over time and after that the patient developed multiple discharging sinuses. The patient had received treatment for this but relapses occurred.

On clinical examination, two large, hard, non‐tender, firm, fixed swellings in the parietal and occipital areas were found with parietal swelling of 5 × 6 cm and occipital swelling of 6 × 8 cm in size with the absence of fluctuation. The overlying skin of the lesions was inflamed. Localized patchy alopecia was evident in the occipital and posterior auricular region, with evidence of pus and crusting at sinus openings, along with mild scarring and post‐inflammatory changes in the surrounding skin. No cervical or occipital lymphadenopathy was observed on clinical examination. There was no neurological deficit or cranial nerve involvement. Apart from mild fever, the patient denied constitutional symptoms such as weight loss, fatigue, and night sweats. The patient had a negative history of trauma or dental infections.

Diagnostic Evaluations

Prior to the current presentation, no imaging or microbiological investigations were performed. Tuberculosis, fungal infection, and neoplasm were considered possible differentials. An brain MRI of the patient was performed to evaluate the scalp masses and rule out intracranial involvement. An incisional biopsy was advised to rule out the cause of the retro auricular swelling.

Imaging Findings

An MRI of the brain (without contrast) was performed which showed hypo‐intense soft lesions: one in the left occipital region and another in the left parietal region causing asymmetrical thickening. These findings were suggestive of infective inflammatory processes versus a neoplastic etiology. No bone marrow edema, intracranial involvement or midline shift was observed, which strengthened the argument against malignancy. The ventricular chain and seventh and eighth nerve complexes appeared normal. Differential diagnosis included the inflammatory process or some neoplastic mass. Right‐sided maxillary sinusitis was observed.

Histopathology

Incisional Biopsy

Sample was taken from the inflamed skin. On microscopic examination, the section of skin revealed inflammation with colonies of microorganisms. A Grocott methenamine silver (GMS) nitrate stain was performed and was negative for fungi, excluding the fungal causes.

An excisional biopsy was performed to rule out tumor and inflammatory causes. The microscopic examination of inflammatory skin fragments showed no evidence of malignancy. The fragments showed multiple basophilic bacterial colonies with radiating filaments surrounded by neutrophils and lymphocytes. The final opinion made was scalp actinomycosis. Actinomycosis can form soft tissue masses and may mimic tumor. Microbiological culture and species‐level identification were not performed due to limited resources. However, histopathological findings were diagnostic of actinomycosis.

Discussion

Scalp actinomycosis is an extremely rare manifestation of a chronic suppurative infection caused by the anaerobic bacterium Actinomyces. The typical cervicofacial form, often referred to as “lumpy jaw,” accounts for approximately 50% of all actinomycosis cases. Scalp involvement is exceedingly uncommon and can present a significant diagnostic challenge [11, 12]. This report presents a rare case of scalp actinomycosis in a 47‐year‐old male, underscoring the diagnostic complexities and its effective treatment with a combination of wide surgical excision and antibiotic therapy.

The clinical presentation of our patient with recurrent scalp swellings and multiple discharging sinuses over a three‐year period was highly characteristic of chronic actinomycosis [13]. The classic features of this infection are its insidious onset, slow progression, and history of relapse following intermittent and noncompliant antibiotic treatments. This mimics other granulomatous diseases and even neoplastic processes [14, 15]. The absence of typical “lumpy jaw” and the isolated scalp involvement further underscore the diagnostic difficulty. The patient's profession as a tandoor worker and the associated exposure to heat and potential microtrauma could have created a favorable environment for the entry of the Actinomyces bacteria.

The differential diagnosis of chronic scalp lesions with discharging sinuses is broad and includes infections like tuberculosis, nocardiosis, and deep fungal infections, as well as cutaneous malignancies and metastatic tumors [14]. Tuberculosis was the primary concern due to the patient's nationality and the chronicity of symptoms. However, the patient did not exhibit constitutional symptoms such as night sweats or considerable weight loss, and a thorough workup for tuberculosis was negative. To rule out intracranial involvement and assess the extent of the lesions, imaging such as MRI was necessary. The MRI findings of hypo‐intense soft tissue masses with asymmetrical thickening, without evidence of bone marrow edema or intracranial extension, were suggestive of inflammatory or infectious processes rather than a malignancy [16].

Although Actinomyces requires strict anaerobic processing, prolonged incubation (up to 2 weeks), and enriched media for successful isolation, and molecular tests such as 16S rRNA sequencing or PCR, it can also be diagnosed by histopathological examination. Microbiological culture and species‐level identification were not performed due to limited resources. However, histopathology of the excisional biopsy proved to be diagnostic. The presence of characteristic basophilic bacterial colonies with radiating filaments, often referred to as “sulfur granules,” surrounded by a suppurative inflammatory infiltrate of neutrophils and lymphocytes, is pathognomonic for actinomycosis [13, 17]. The GMS nitrate stain was negative, which helped to exclude a fungal etiology. This case highlights the crucial role of histopathology in the diagnosis of rare or atypical presentations of infectious diseases when culture‐based methods are not feasible [18].

Actinomycosis's management typically involves a long course of high‐dose antibiotics, with penicillin being considered the first‐line treatment [12, 15]. Actinomyces is a slow‐growing bacterium that can survive in tissues; hence, a longer treatment period is necessary to avoid recurrence. In our case, the patient was treated with oral doxycycline and amoxicillin‐clavulanic acid. Penicillin or amoxicillin is the standard of care, but doxycycline is a well‐established alternative for patients with penicillin allergy [12]. The combination of amoxicillin‐clavulanic acid was chosen to provide broader coverage and avoid any possible coinfection. The surgical intervention was necessary for debridement and removal of the chronic inflammatory masses. A wide local excision of the lesions with scalp flap reconstruction was done. This combined approach of surgical debulking and long‐term antibiotic therapy has been shown to be highly effective in achieving a cure and preventing recurrence in localized forms of actinomycosis [12]. Besides occupational exposure to heat and potential scalp microtrauma, other possible predisposing factors were considered. The patient was otherwise healthy, without diabetes, immunosuppressive drug use, poor oral hygiene, or a history of local trauma or surgery. While the exact portal of entry of the organism remains uncertain, repeated minor scalp abrasions from occupational exposure could have facilitated infection in the absence of systemic comorbidities. Microbiological culture and specie‐level identification would have provided confirmatory evidence, which is a limitation. However, histopathological findings were evident. Although standard management of actinomycosis includes prolonged antibiotic therapy—commonly 6–12 months—to prevent relapse, our patient only received one month of antibiotic medication due to compliance issues and resource constraints. The absence of recurrence during short‐term follow‐up suggests that thorough surgical debridement may allow reduced antibiotic duration, although extended therapy remains the recommended standard, and longer follow‐up is warranted to confirm durable remission.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case of scalp actinomycosis highlights the difficulties in diagnosing this rare entity. Scalp actinomycosis should be considered in the differential diagnosis of chronic scalp swellings, especially those with discharging sinuses that don not respond to conventional antibiotic treatment. A high index of suspicion, combined with appropriate imaging to assess the extent of the disease is essential for correct management. The successful outcome in our patient with a combination of wide surgical excision and prolonged antibiotic therapy underscores the importance of a multimodal approach to treating this “great masquerader” of head and neck disease. This report adds to the limited literature on scalp actinomycosis and provides valuable information for clinicians encountering similar complex presentations.
