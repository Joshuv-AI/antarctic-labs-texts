Economic burden of multiple sclerosis in an Italian cohort of patients on disease-modifying therapy: analysis of disease cost and its components
Authors: Michela Ponzio, Laura Santoni, Michela Molina, Eleonora Tavazzi, Roberto Bergamaschi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In this observational cross-sectional study conducted in Italy, sociodemographic and disease data were collected from a multiple sclerosis (MS) population treated with disease-modifying therapies (DMTs). A prevalence-based cost-of-illness analysis was subsequently carried out. In total, 393 MS patients were enrolled, 65.9% female, average age of 45 ± 11.5 years. 94.9% had RRMS and EDSS median value of 1.5 (1.0, 2.0). The mean total cost per MS patient was estimated at €20,299.4 per year. Total costs of MS increase by disability level, varying from 16,812.0 (minor) to 20,407.0 (mild), 27,640.7 (moderate), and 44,404.0 (severe). DMT costs accounted for 62.5% of total costs, while productivity loss accounted for 22.0%. Indeed, besides having low quality of life decline and fatigue levels, most patients enrolled retained full working capacity while 31.3% had some productivity loss. The results obtained are in line with previous studies showing that as the disease progresses, the quality of life, fatigue and working capacity worsen alongside a rise in total MS costs for either the society or the healthcare service. Clinical relapses cause a significant cost increment. This cross-sectional study provides a representation of MS quality of life and MS cost in a cohort of Italian MS patients treated with DMT, among which the majority had RRMS. This cost-of-illness analysis can provide useful information for resource allocation and help estimate the savings that can be obtained by slowing the progression of the disease. It also constitutes a primary source of data useful for conducting a complete economic evaluation analysis, including cost-effectiveness and cost-utility analyses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00415-024-12729-y.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a progressive neurological disorder driven by an immune-mediated demyelinating process [1]. MS represents the most common non-traumatic disabling disorder [2], affecting 2.9 million people worldwide [3]. In Italy, the prevalence is 221:100.000 inhabitants, with approximately 3600 new diagnoses per year [4]. Although MS has a limited effect on a patient's lifespan, the debilitating nature of symptoms has a detrimental impact on physical and psychosocial well-being [5]. Moreover, since symptoms start during productive years (usually from 20 to 40 years of age), this disease undermines working capacity [6]. Indeed, the disability caused by MS can prevent or reduce the working activities of patients, thus causing absenteeism or early retirement, but can also reduce employment opportunities and earnings [7]. Currently, available disease-modifying treatments (DMTs) effectively reduce the clinical relapse rate and improve the long-term prognosis, with a consequent positive impact on quality of life (QoL). Depending on the disease stage and the level of clinical disability, other approaches, such as symptomatic treatments, educational interventions on lifestyle, rehabilitation treatments and psychological support, can be applied to minimize QoL deterioration and productivity loss [8]. Costs related to MS cause a high economic burden on societies and depend on all the above-mentioned factors and can be divided into direct (e.g., DMTs, symptomatic treatments, hospitalization) and indirect, mainly related to productivity loss [9]. In Italy, the mean cost related to MS has been estimated at €37,948 in 2011. Costs rise as MS progresses (€22,750 at an EDSS score of 0–3 and €63,047 at an EDSS score ≥ 7), and the burden increases in the case of relapses, while the total cost decreases with a better quality of life [10]. In a later work, the mean annual costs of MS in Italy were estimated at €39,307/patient (€29,676, €43,464, and €53,454 in mild, moderate and severe cases, respectively) in 2019. The major component was represented by direct healthcare costs (€21,069), followed by indirect costs (€15,004). The overall cost of MS was €4.8 billion; 80% of the costs were borne by the Health Service, especially direct healthcare costs, while almost all non-healthcare expenses were out-of-pocket [7]. The estimated total annual cost associated with MS in Europe was €40,303 [11]. However, the advent of new DMTs in recent years has dramatically changed the disease evolution as traditionally conceived, and costs continuously vary accordingly [7]. Therefore, updates through burden-of-illness studies are essential.

The purpose of this study is to conduct an observational cross-sectional study in Italy to collect sociodemographic and disease data and information relating to resource consumption, health-related QoL (HRQoL), and working ability of a cohort of subjects with MS. This work also aims to estimate the costs of MS and its components in this population in which all the patients are on DMT.

Patients and methods

Study design

This is a single-center observational cross-sectional study. From the data collected, a prevalence-based cost-of-illness analysis was carried out from a social perspective.

Patients

Patients who visited the Mondino Foundation MS Centre of Pavia in Region Lombardia, in Italy, from 2019 to 2020 according to standard clinical practice were enrolled in the study. The criteria of inclusion were the following: a definite diagnosis of MS according to McDonald's 2017 criteria [12], age ≥ 18 years old, treatment with DMT and the willingness to participate in the study by signing the informed consent form. Patients who accepted to be enrolled were given a comprehensive description of the study.

Collected data were stored in an ad hoc database of the Mondino Foundation MS Center. The data were collected using questionnaires filled in directly by patients. The questionnaire was developed by Kobelt et al. [14] to collect data specifically related to MS and has been used since with adaptations for each country. Respondents were instructed to only include healthcare consumption relating to their MS, which, in view of the well-defined nature of the disease, is considered to yield reliable answers. The questionnaires were paper-based, and data entry and data quality control has been carried out.

MS-related resource consumption

The resource consumption was collected through a questionnaire already used in previous studies [10, 14, 15]. The collected data were retrospective with respect to the time of the start of the study. The recall period was varied among different items (1 month, 3 months or 12 months), considering the different easiness of remembering a serious but infrequent event (e.g., purchase of a wheelchair) compared to an event that occurs with greater frequency (e.g., used drugs). To estimate the resources consumption related to MS rather than the resources consumption of a patient with MS, patients were instructed to include only consumption related to MS. In view of the well-defined nature of the disease and of the limited unrelated co-morbidities, it is considered to yield reliable answers.

Workforce participation in MS (only for patients employed)

Job activity was assessed by the validated Multiple Sclerosis Questionnaire for Job Difficulties (MSQ-Job) developed in the Italian version [16]. It is a 42-item questionnaire, each to be rated on a 1–5 grading scale addressing the degree to which the disease and its symptoms impact work-related difficulties (from no impact to complete impact). A cut-off score of 15.8, or more than eight items scored ≥ 3, indicated a person with work-related difficulties and at risk of job loss [17]. For patients not working, the information requested related to the reason, that is, due to MS or other reasons (student, unemployed, homemaker, retired); for those not working because of MS, it was inquired about official invalidity status or voluntary early retirement.

HRQoL and fatigue

HRQoL data were collected using a generic, preference-based instrument, the European Quality of Life 5-Dimensions 3-Levels (EQ-5D-3L) two-part questionnaire [18]: EQ-5D descriptive system and EQ-5D visual analog scale (VAS). EQ-5D has been validated in Italy [19]. The first part is the descriptive system and includes five single-item dimensions of health (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression). Each dimension is measured on a 3-point scale, and a single weighted score—the utility index—is obtained from these five questions. The EQ-5D results were translated into utilities based on the Italian tariff established by Scalone et al. [20], giving a utility value between 0 (death) and 1 (full health) [15], as negative values were put to 0 in this analysis. For comparability across countries, utilities were also estimated with the original value set developed in the UK [21].

Fatigue was assessed by the Modified Fatigue Impact Scale (MFIS) [22]. This instrument consists of 21 items and provides an assessment of the physical, cognitive, and psychosocial functioning, which may be affected by fatigue. Physical subscale can range from 0 to 36, cognitive subscale can range from 0 to 40, and psychosocial subscale can range from 0 to 8. The total MFIS score can range from 0 to 84. It is computed by adding scores on the physical, cognitive and psychosocial subscales. The cut-off value is 38, where scores ≥ 38 indicate a significant impact of fatigue on a person's activities.

Cost calculation

Direct healthcare costs, direct non-healthcare costs, intangible costs and indirect costs (productivity and income loss) were estimated from the societal perspective and are a function of the quantity of resources used or lost. All costs, independently of who was responsible for them, were included. To obtain the cost per patient per year, the resources used were annualized, assuming that the recall period of each resource is representative of similar recall periods throughout the year, then multiplied by the relevant unit costs. Unit costs were derived from national tariffs used to fund providers by the Italian National Healthcare Service (NHS) or in direct contact with institutions or experts. The Human Capital Approach [23] was used to estimate production losses for the patients and caregivers due to MS. Patients' paid time off work was evaluated based on the average annual costs of labor, according to the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) 2019 [24]. The cost of short-term sick leave was estimated using average labor costs adjusted by the actual working hours of the patients concerned. Production losses due to long-term sick leave and early retirement were valued using labor costs for full-time work. Informal care provided by caregivers was evaluated according to the nature of time spent assisting (leisure or working time). Leisure time dedicated to direct care (food preparation, personal hygiene) or providing surveillance and company was evaluated according to the replacement value. The National Agreement for Home Labour Services was used to evaluate the time invested by caregivers (for direct care and surveillance) [25]. The intangible costs were estimated using as population norms for the EQ-5D the values reported by Scalone and colleagues for Italy [26] and comparing the utility of the MS sample with age and gender-matched values of the Italian general population [27]. The cost of clinical relapse was calculated as the difference in quarterly costs between patients with or without relapse, with the hypothesis that clinical relapses were seldom longer than 3 months [28]; for patients who were unsure, we assumed that the answer was no relapse. Resources unlikely to be directly affected during the quarter by a relapse were excluded from the calculations (early retirement, DMTs). All costs were estimated in Euros (€) in 2019. The sources and mode of calculation are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Data analysis

A descriptive analysis was performed. Continuous variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges or means with standard deviations. Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages. The comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables with more than two groups. The DMTs were classified based on their efficacy, as reported by Filippi and co-workers, in standard efficacy DMT and high efficacy DMT. The standard efficacy DMTs include interferons (IFNs), glatiramer acetate (GA), dimethyl fumarate (DMF) or teriflunomide (TFU) while the high efficacy DMTs include alemtuzumab, cladribine (CLAD), fingolimod (FTY), natalizumab (NTZ), ocrelizumab (OCRE), siponimod, ozanimod, or ofatumumab [29]. The incremental cost associated with clinical relapse was calculated using a generalized linear model with log link and gamma distributions and adjusted for sex, age, and disability level. A p value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using Stata Version 17 (Stata-Corp, College Station, TX).

Results

Demographic and disease characteristics

A total of 393 MS patients were enrolled in this study; all subjects were of white race, 65.9% were female, and the average age was 45 ± 11.5 years. 373 (94.9%) had RRMS and EDSS mean and median values of 1.9 (± 1.5) and 1.5 (1.0, 2.0), respectively. Patients' categorization by EDSS level showed that the majority of the population exhibited lower levels of disability, with 60.0% of patients displaying minor (EDSS 0–1.5), 24.5% mild (EDSS 2.0–3.0), 8.4% moderate (EDSS 3.5–5.5), and 6.1% severe (EDSS ≥ 6) disability. Higher degrees of disability were positively associated with older age and with a longer disease history compared with those with milder disability (p < 0.001). Most patients (73.8%) were employed or self-employed, being that 77.6% of them worked full-time (on average 34.8 h/week) while 10.3% had to change working hours (part-time) due to MS. All patients were treated with DMT when completing the questionnaire and 72.5% of them were on standard DMT [30]. Regarding education levels, 19.4% of patients had basic education, 48.9% had a high school degree, and 31.7% had a university degree. Total cost of MS patient

Overall, the mean total cost per MS patient was estimated at €20,299.4 per year. Direct healthcare costs represented approximately 72.0% and included hospital in-patient care or day admission (hospital ward, rehabilitation clinic), special visits (mainly neurological), tests (mainly MRI of the brain and spine), and medication (DMTs, other prescriptions and non-prescription drugs, Table 3; for details see Supplementary Table 2). DMTs, amounting to approximately 62.5% of all costs, represented the item that impacted MS costs the most. The second item that most impacted MS cost is productivity loss, which accounted for 22.0% of all costs. Intangible costs

A total of 20.19 QALYs were lost in the sample, corresponding to 0.05 QALYs lost per MS patient. Using the benchmark value of €50,000 per QALY, this translates into an intangible cost of €2581.3 per patient/year.

Focus on productivity loss

In view of the low disability level of our population and the importance of maintaining a job for this population, we focused on MS-related productivity loss and changes in working capacity. MS caused productivity loss in 123 (31.3%) patients (29.1% of the females and 32.4% of the males), with mean annual costs attributable to production losses estimated at €4535.3. The major cost components were early retirement (52.0%), working day loss (21.3%), and loss of working activity (25.0%) (Table 6). Patients' productivity loss

Productivity losses were associated with age, educational level (University degree vs. primary school), EDSS level, illness duration, MS course, presence of anxiety/depression, DMT type, employment type, MSQ-Job, and QoL. After multivariate adjustment, it was possible to observe an association between productivity loss and either age or higher MSQ-Job scores (higher work-related difficulties) (Supplementary Table 4).

The employment status was negatively impacted by the increase in the severity level of the disease, which forced patients to leave the labor force. A drop in the percentage of employed MS patients from about 80.0% at levels 0 and 1 to 12.5% at levels ≥ 6 was observed (Fig. 3). It was also possible to observe that the percentage of employment in males was higher than in females (81.3% compared to 69.9%) and this trend was maintained across all EDSS classes (see Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Fig. 1). Approximately 36.4% of the sample showed a critical situation (risk of job loss) with respect to work-related difficulties based on MSQ-Job (cut off ≥ 15.8). Discussion

This cross-sectional study provides a representation of MS costs, QoL, fatigue and productivity in a cohort of Italian MS patients from a social perspective. While the Italian MS scenario in 2023 showed that the RRMS and PPMS represented, respectively, 84% and 10–15% of all cases and 80% of the patients were on DMT [4], in our population, the vast majority of patients had the RRMS form (94.9%), and all patients were on DMT. To our knowledge, this is the first MS analysis studying an Italian cohort in which all subjects are treated with DMT.

Comparing this analysis with the available literature, the methodology was in line with other published studies on the Italian MS setting, but the results don't always appear aligned. The cohort enrolled in the study conducted at the MS Center of the Mondino Foundation in Pavia exhibited low disability levels (median EDSS 1.5); the majority had RRMS (94.9%) and were still able to work. On the one hand, we observed that, despite the mean disease duration of 12 years, the analyzed cohort, in treatment with standard and high-efficacy DMTs, showed a low level of disability and a moderate QoL. On the other hand, this justifies the lower total mean cost per MS patient, estimated at €20,299.4 per year, with respect to studies including patients with higher disability levels [15] and compared to the annual cost of €39.307 per patient in Italy estimated in 2023 [4]. Our results are similar to what was reported for patients with mild disability levels [7]. In our sample, DMT costs predominated (62.5%) and were followed by productivity loss (22.0%). These results are in accordance with the fact that patients with lower disability need fewer non-healthcare resources, such as home and car modifications, transportation, and informal care, which accounted for only 5.8% of total costs in our population. As the level of disability increases, the costs related to informal care, health professionals (nursing, physiotherapist, psychologist), and production losses become more relevant. It is interesting to note that MS patients at low disability levels (EDSS 0–1.5) were in 80.4% (male 91.0%, female 75.3%) of cases employed. These data are in agreement with the general population matched the same age and during the same period: the rate of employment in Lombardia was 84% (male 93.0%, female 75.0%) [31]. However, employment tends to decrease with the increase in the disability level, as previously observed [14].

Costs related to acute clinical relapses were significant and mostly due to increased productivity loss, symptomatic treatment, and the need for hospitalization, informal care, mobility aids, and adaptations in the environment to keep on with daily activities. According to what was reported in the literature, we also observed that MS costs rise with increasing EDSS levels while utilities (QoL) decrease. In fact, the total annual mean costs per patient increased from €16,812.4 at an EDSS score of 0–1.5 to €44,042.5 at an EDSS ≥ 6. Overall, our population showed only slight reductions in QoL which is reflected in the low intangible costs estimated at €2581.3 per patient/year when compared to previous studies that estimated values of €15.500 [10]. Intangible costs were not included in mean annual costs. Interestingly, the level of fatigue in our population was mild, and therefore, fatigue had only a slight impact on cognitive, psychosocial and physical functioning. When we compare the population of this study with the population enrolled in a previous Italian study, it is possible to observe that, while in this work, the entire population was on DMT, in the work of Battaglia et al. [15], 68.7% of patients were on DMT. Moreover, in our study, patients displayed lower EDSS levels (1.9 compared to 3.7) and a much lower frequency of relapse (5.3% versus 14.1%). Indeed, our results differ from what was published by Battaglia et al. [15], who observed much higher levels of fatigue (96.0% of the participants), although measured as perceived fatigue through a VAS scale and in a population with a higher degree of disability (mean EDSS 3.7). The low levels of fatigue observed in our population probably reflect the low level of disability, as it has been shown that fatigue levels increase as the severity of disability worsens [15]. The efficient treatment of RRMS patients could significantly contribute to the maintenance of patient working capacity and avoid MS costs that are related to higher levels of disability.

We must acknowledge that the study has some limitations, such as the rather small sample from only one MS center, which makes it difficult to generalize to the Italian MS population. Moreover, the questionnaires were self-completed, which poses the risk of collecting data on the cost of a patient with MS rather than the costs of MS. Then, the answers could be affected by a certain degree of subjectivity, and some resource consumption could be at least partly attributable to other diseases. Having objective data sources, such as healthcare resources derived from administrative data, could have been useful for validating self-reported data. To limit this risk, we used an MS resource use questionnaire, which was adapted and translated into different languages and is already used in many cost studies [14, 28], and patients were told to include only MS-related resources. In view of the well-defined nature of the disease and of the limited unrelated co-morbidities, it is considered to yield reliable answers. Given the retrospective and cross-sectional nature of the present study, it is difficult to accurately establish the temporal relationship between co-variables and cost outcomes in particular for the multivariate analysis used to evaluate variables associated with patients' productivity losses. In addition, the cost of infusion has not been considered in the case in which the route of DMT administration was intravenous. Furthermore, there may be an overestimation of the total annual costs of DMTs since 100% treatment adherence and ex-factory price, excluding any discounts for Italian NHS were considered to calculate them.

To conclude, our study showed the costs, QoL, fatigue, and productivity related to MS in an Italian cohort of patients treated with DMTs. Importantly, this is one of the few studies in MS that also evaluated intangible costs. Our population represents MS patients who are on DMT, have limited necessity for in-patient or informal care and who, for the majority, are still able to work. For this reason, the costs reported in this study should be compared with those of MS populations with lower EDSS scores. This study is in line with previous studies that showed increases in MS costs with disability progression. Therefore, hindering the progression of the disease can improve the QoL, reduce fatigue, and possibly maintain patients' working capacity for longer periods of time. These effects might imply a reduction of the economic impact on the NHS and society.

These results can contribute to the existing literature on the cost of MS, adding data on a population being treated with DMT. Our data characterize various aspects of the burden of MS on patients, Italian NHS and society and capture patterns of resource utilization that could inform future policy recommendations. Indeed, information on the utilization of medical and social services is essential for good healthcare planning, and awareness of the medical and social costs is a prerequisite for cost optimization. The cost of drugs largely drives the annual cost per patient. However, from the cost increase and QoL decrease with worsening disease, the economic benefit of treatment can be easily visualized. The use of effective treatments designed to reduce the frequency of relapses and slow disease progression may lead to a reduction in both the healthcare and socioeconomic costs of MS. Therefore, careful fine-tuning of their use can help optimize both patient management by clinicians and resource allocation by healthcare providers. This cost-of-illness analysis can provide useful information to help define the priorities, determine the best allocation of resources and estimate the savings that can be obtained by maintaining patients for longer times at a low level of disability. Also, these data can serve as a base for the conduction of pharmaco-economic models and cost-effectiveness or cost-consequence analyses that could help determine future policy recommendations for reimbursement of medicines.
