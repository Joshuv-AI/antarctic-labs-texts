Integrating Smoking Cessation With Lung Cancer Screening in Aotearoa New Zealand: Equity Considerations and Implications for Policy Development
Authors: Nina Bevin, Phyu Sin Aye, Nathan J Harrison, Kate Parker, Karen Bartholomew, Andrew Waa, Natalie Walker, Sue Crengle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInternational evidence increasingly supports integrating smoking cessation services within lung cancer screening programs, yet limited guidance exists for Aotearoa New Zealand, where smoking‐related harm and lung cancer disproportionately affect Māori. In this commentary, we summarise literature on smoking cessation interventions delivered alongside lung screening, focusing on intervention modality, intensity, timing and equity implications relevant to a future national program. Evidence from 25 studies and four systematic reviews showed wide variation in reported outcomes, including complete cessation of nicotine use (quitting) and switching from tobacco to less harmful products (switching), reflecting heterogeneity in intervention design and outcome measurement. Overall, intensive, multi‐session behavioural support combined with pharmacotherapy achieved higher quit rates than brief advice or single‐session interventions, particularly when cessation support was delivered immediately or co‐located with screening. Equity considerations were central to the interpretation of the international findings in our review. Māori face structural, systemic and access barriers that limit the effectiveness of standard cessation approaches, highlighting the need for culturally safe, whānau (family)‐centred and Māori‐led interventions. Integrating intensive smoking cessation support into the lung screening pathway represents a critical opportunity to reduce inequities and improve outcomes for Māori within a future national lung screening program.Keywords: Aotearoa New Zealand, equity, lung cancer screening, Māori, policy, smoking cessation

Introduction

International studies show that in low dose computed tomography (LDCT) lung cancer screening (LCS) pathways, integrating tobacco treatment improves quit rates and enhances the cost‐effectiveness of LCS programs in achieving their intended health outcomes, while reducing morbidity and mortality from tobacco‐related conditions [1, 2, 3, 4]. Motivation to quit often peaks around LCS, particularly when imaging abnormalities are reported, making the LCS encounter an optimal time to provide accessible cessation support [5, 6, 7, 8]. LCS participants typically differ from the general population of people who smoke, reflecting the targeted criteria of LCS programs: they are older, have heavier lifetime exposure to tobacco and carry more comorbidity; hence, they are more likely to be nicotine dependent and to require more intensive support [3, 9]. Evidence on tobacco treatment outcomes within LCS pathways is diverse, but three patterns recur in existing literature: (i) multi‐session behavioural support plus pharmacotherapy outperforms brief advice alone; (ii) support offered immediately at the screening visit (co‐located or same‐day telehealth) increases uptake of cessation support and (iii) opt‐out models extend reach by systematically offering tobacco treatment to all eligible individuals, thereby increasing participation, while maintaining comparable effectiveness in achieving cessation outcomes [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. In Aotearoa New Zealand, the central challenge is not only the effectiveness and safety of the smoking cessation treatment but equity: despite rapid declines in daily smoking for Māori (the Indigenous population), inequities persist relative to non‐Māori [19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. Unless designed from the outset with Māori‐led approaches that centre Māori values and worldviews and address cost and access barriers, LCS program could inadvertently widen existing gaps rather than close them [19, 23, 24, 25, 26].

Te Oranga Pūkahukahu is a Māori‐led LCS research program informing national implementation, recognising key enablers: Māori partnership, whānau friendly processes, Māori navigators, use of te reo Māori (the Māori language), and addressing structural barriers that reduce Māori engagement with conventional health services. When deciding how best to incorporate smoking cessation support into our program, we undertook a review to consider international evidence from LCS settings and applied an Aotearoa lens by including research on effective smoking cessation approaches for Māori incorporating evidence beyond the LCS context. Finally, we considered important practical differences in the Aotearoa LCS setting (e.g., more low‐volume LDCT screening sites) to devise a smoking cessation function which is both feasible and highly effective.

Summary of Intervention Approaches

This section summarises evidence on intervention approaches used within LCS, based on findings from 25 original studies and four systematic reviews. The studies defined quitting as cessation of combustible tobacco smoking, measured by self‐report or biochemical verification, and did not preclude ongoing nicotine use through other delivery mechanisms such as vaping or nicotine replacement therapy (NRT).

Brief Advice

Brief advice, which consists of a short, tailored conversation delivered verbally at the time of LCS appointment advising all people who smoke to quit and offers support, is foundational and inexpensive, but typically insufficient on its own for a highly cigarette dependent LCS cohort. Routine, supportive advice coupled with an opt‐out referral pathway and rapid pharmacotherapy access can meaningfully improve cessation, yet sustained abstinence generally requires repeated behavioural support and pharmacotherapy [14, 27, 28, 29]. Therefore, brief advice should function as the universal entry point to a stepped, more intensive offer.

Counselling (Telephone or In‐Person)

Counselling from cessation advisors is effective when timely, proactive and repeated. Trials delivering same‐day, in‐person sessions at LCS appointments, and those offering subsequent multiple structured telephone counselling within the LCS service, outperform usual care, such as brief cessation advice and/or provision of self‐help materials [15, 16, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34]. For Aotearoa, flexible delivery is essential, potentially co‐locating cessation advisors in higher‐volume LDCT screening sites, while in lower‐volume or rural areas ensuring immediate connection to trained practitioners via telehealth and same‐day access to cessation medicines to minimise attrition [15, 16, 35].

Counselling Plus Pharmacotherapy

Combining behavioural support with medicines consistently yields higher cessation than counselling alone in LCS settings. Cytisine has demonstrated substantially higher biochemically confirmed abstinence versus usual care in an LCS trial [36], while combination NRT and varenicline remain the most effective options [16, 37, 38, 39, 40]. Some studies have explored nicotine vapes that may be used to aid cessation—although findings for validated abstinence are mixed, reductions in cigarette consumption have been observed [41, 42]. The reviewed studies provided limited evidence on the use of some pharmacotherapies available in Aotearoa, such as bupropion [43]. For an older, heavier‐smoking LCS cohort, longer courses and combination NRT (patch plus oral NRT) are often required, guided by preference and prior quit history. These should be provided free at point of care, within regulated and integrated prescribing pathways, to avoid reproducing cost barriers that disproportionately affect Māori and those on lower incomes [16, 23, 39, 40].

Intensity of Interventions

Across trials, multi‐session approaches, typically three or more counselling contacts with facilitated pharmacotherapy, achieve higher abstinence than brief or single‐session approaches, particularly at early follow‐up, although effects may attenuate over longer intervals [11, 30, 38, 39, 44]. Programs should resource proactive follow‐ups, relapse prevention contacts such as scheduled follow‐ups to support sustained abstinence, and flexible delivery modes (including in‐person, telehealth and text).

Timing of Interventions

Timing matters. The LDCT screening visit and results disclosure are potent motivational windows. Trials that provide immediate cessation, co‐located at LCS appointments or initiated on the day via telehealth, report superior early outcomes compared with deferred referral [14, 15, 16]. Although definitive timing comparisons are few and sometimes under‐powered, pilot studies suggest pre‐LDCT initiation may outperform post‐LDCT in the short term, and re‐offering at each screening encounter sustains engagement [45, 46]. Operationally, immediate and repeated offers of support at multiple points across the screening pathway are recommended, reflecting pro‐equity approaches adopted in other health settings.

Intervention by Provider

Tobacco treatment delivered by Radiology staff can normalise routine cessation offers and reduce service referrals, but outcomes vary with implementation fidelity. Training participant‐facing LCS team members, such as radiology staff, in brief advice, opt‐out referral and pharmacotherapy initiation expands capacity, while partnerships with dedicated cessation practitioners, including Māori providers, ensure intensive, culturally safe support. A mixed model is likely optimal in Aotearoa, tailored to site volumes and geographic context [35, 47].

Relevance to Aotearoa New Zealand

Aotearoa stop‐smoking guidelines recommend culturally‐appropriate multi‐session behavioural support combined with stop‐smoking medicines or nicotine vapes, tailored to preference and prior quit attempts [23, 40, 41], although the inclusion of switching as a successful outcome reflects a broad definition and remains an area of ongoing debate in Aotearoa. These principles align closely with the needs of an LCS cohort, who are typically older, more nicotine‐dependent and often have a history of repeated prior quit attempts. Access to combination NRT, varenicline where appropriate, and cytisine (subject to availability) should be straightforward and free [16, 23, 36, 39, 48, 49]. Cessation in LCS should be universally offered to everyone who smokes, regardless of LCS LDCT result or stated readiness to quit, and extended to all whānau who wish to quit alongside participants [23, 45].

Challenges in Interpreting Evidence Across LCS Smoking Cessation Studies

This review synthesised a broad and diverse evidence base on smoking cessation interventions delivered within LCS programs; however, substantial heterogeneity across studies limited direct comparison of quit rates. Variation in eligibility criteria, definitions of current smoking, intervention uptake models (opt‐in vs. opt‐out), outcome measures, and follow‐up periods contributed to inconsistent findings and potential healthy volunteer bias. Differences in intervention timing, delivery context, and background population quit rates further complicate interpretation of reported outcomes. Generalisability to Māori is limited, as most studies included few Indigenous participants, and findings may be less applicable to the Aotearoa health system due to differences in funding models and structural determinants such as racism and colonial legacies.

Conclusion

To maximise health gain for Māori, a future national LCS program must include smoking cessation services that are both feasible and highly effective for Māori. This review outlined the rationale for our recommended approach and provided an example of how health services implementation can be an enabler of achieving health equity for Māori and other Indigenous groups. The design of a future national program should place equity at its centre.
