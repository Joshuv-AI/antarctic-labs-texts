The Mordell-Schinzel conjecture for cubic diophantine equations
János Kollár and Jennifer Li

Abstract
. Building on the works of Mordell (1952) and Schinzel (2015), we prove that cubic diophantine equations x ​ y ​ z = G ⁡ ( x , y ) xyz=G(x,y) have infinitely many solutions.



Mordell stated
[
Mor52
]
that the diophantine equation

xyz=G(x,y)
has infinitely many solutions for every polynomial
G\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y]
.
This turned out to be not completely true; counterexamples for
\deg G=2
were already known to Jacobsthal
[
Jac39
]
.
A detailed study was undertaken by Schinzel
[
Sch15
,
Sch18
]
; see also
[
Gre24
, Sec.5.5]
.

Notation 1
.

By changing
z
, the terms in
G
that are divisible by
xy
can be absorbed into
xyz
, thus, setting
c:=G(0,0)
we can simplify the equation to

[formula]

xyz=A(x)+B(y)-c,

where
A(x):=\textstyle{\sum}_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}x^{i}
,

B(y):=\textstyle{\sum}_{j=0}^{m}b_{j}y^{j}
, and
c=a_{0}=b_{0}
.
(Thus
c
appears 3 times in (
1
.1).)
We write
a:=a_{n}
,
b:=b_{m}
and, as in
[
Sch15
]
,
assume from now on that
abc\neq 0
.
The corresponding surfaces are denoted by

[formula]

S_{A,B}:=\bigl(xyz=A(x)+B(y)-c\bigr)\subset{\mathbb{A}}^{3}.

As in
[
KVP24
]
, these surfaces can be defined over an arbitrary integral domain
R
. Here we work over
{\mathbb{Z}}
, but some general results are stated over
{\mathbb{C}}
.

Writing the equation as

[formula]

xyz=ax^{n}+by^{m}+c+\textstyle{\sum}_{i=1}^{n-1}a_{i}x^{i}+\textstyle{\sum}_{j=1}^{m-1}b_{j}y^{j}

suggests that one should view them as perturbations of the
trinomial series
xyz=ax^{n}+by^{m}+c
.
See Paragraph
15
for a geometric viewpoint.

Somewhat surprisingly, the cases where
\min\{m,n\}\in\{1,2\}
are especially problematic. In particular, the case
m=n=2
is not completely settled

[
Sch15
,
Sch18
]
. Several of these equations are also discussed in
[
Gre24
, Chap.5]
.

However, the situation seems better for
m,n\geq 3
. Based on

[
Sch15
, Thm.4]
, we propose the following conjecture.

Conjecture 2

(Mordell-Schinzel)
.

If
m,n\geq 3
and
A(t),B(t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
, then
(
1
.1) has infinitely many integer solutions.

For historical accuracy, note that neither author stated this as a conjecture.
[
Mor52
]
asserted this as a true statement, while

[
Sch15
, Thm.4]
proved most cases, without speculating about the general version; see Paragraph
16
for further details. To the best of our knowledge, there is not a single
pair
(m,n)
for which Conjecture
2
has been fully proven.
Our aim is to settle the simplest case.

Theorem 3
.

For every
a,b\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}
and
a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2},c\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, the cubic surface

\bigl(xyz=ax^{3}+by^{3}+c+a_{2}x^{2}+a_{1}x+b_{2}y^{2}+b_{1}y\bigr)\subset{\mathbb{A}}^{3}

has infinitely many integral points.

4
Plan of the proof.

If
c=0
then we have the trivial solutions
(0,0,z)
, so we may assume that
c\neq 0
.

[
Sch15
, Thm.4]
establishes all the cases when

|abc|>1
. Thus it remains to deal with the possibilities

a,b,c\in\{1,-1\}
.

These are very special, but for
m=n=2
all
known equations (
1
.3) with only finitely many solutions satisfy
a,b,c\in\{1,-1\}
;
see
[
Jac39
,
Sch18
]
.
So there is reason to think that the
a,b,c\in\{1,-1\}
cases could behave differently.

By changing the signs of
x,y,z
, we can achieve
a=b=c=1
. Thus from now on we work with the surfaces

[formula]

S_{a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}}:=\bigl(xyz=x^{3}+y^{3}+1+a_{2}x^{2}+a_{1}x+b_{2}y^{2}+b_{1}y\bigr)\subset{\mathbb{A}}^{3}.

The observation of
[
KVP24
]
is that these surfaces have an infinite automorphism group over
{\mathbb{Z}}
.
In retrospect, these automorphisms are behind the computations in

[
Mor52
]
; more explicit versions appear in
[
Yos85
,
Sch15
,
GS24
]
.

Thus we aim to find
one
solution, and hope to prove that its orbit under the automorphism group is infinite.
The easiest is to start with the
trivial solutions

(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
, where
x_{0}=\pm 1,y_{0}=\pm 1
.

In most cases the automorphism group is infinite cyclic, but its
generator is unexpectedly complicated. It is given by
polynomials of degrees
13
,
34
, and
55
, containing
110
,
998
, and
2881
monomials; see
[
KVP24
, Exmp.2]
. So it is almost impossible to compute with.

Therefore we work instead with a closely related isomorphism-groupoid

\Sigma_{A,B}
involving 4 surfaces, called the
companion surfaces/equations
of (
1
.2); see Paragraph
8
.

Using the explicit forms of the generators of
\Sigma_{A,B}

it is easy to see that if
\max\{|x_{0}|,|y_{0}|\}
is large enough, then
the
\Sigma_{A,B}
-orbit of
p_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
is infinite; see
Corollary
10
. This holds for all
m,n\geq 3
.

The rest of the argument assumes that
m=n=3
.
The required condition on
\max\{|x_{0}|,|y_{0}|\}
is not satisfied by the trivial solutions of (
4
.1), but straightforward estimates show that if

\max\{|a_{1}|+|a_{2}|,|b_{1}|+|b_{2}|\}\geq 7
,
then there is a trival solution of one of the companion equations,
whose
\Sigma_{A,B}
-orbit is infinite.
This is proved in
Corollary
11
.

This shows that Conjecture
2
holds for cubics,
save possibly for a finite list of exceptions, all of which satisfy

|a_{1}|+|a_{2}|,|b_{1}|+|b_{2}|\leq 6
.

For these equations we rely on computer searches.
First, in Proposition
12
we list all cubics

S_{a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}}
for which the

\Sigma_{A,B}
-orbits of all trivial solutions are finite.
There are only 3 such, up to isomorphism.

In these cases we searched for other solutions. Once we find one
integral point
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
with
\max\{|x_{0}|,|y_{0}|\}\geq 6
,
its
\Sigma_{A,B}
-orbit is infinite by
Corollary
10
. ∎

Example 5
.

The above approach does not work directly for

m=n=4
, since there are infinitely many quartic polynomials

A(x)+B(y)-c
with
|abc|=1
for which
the

\Sigma_{A,B}
-orbits of all trivial solutions are finite.

If
A(x)
and
B(y)
map
\{1,-1\}
to itself, then

\Sigma_{A,B}
maps trivial solutions to trivial solutions.
Thus the orbit of all trivial solutions is finite.
An infinite series of such polynomials is given by

x^{4}-x^{2}+1+r(x^{3}-x)
for
r\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Remark 6
.

As in
[
GS24
]
, we also get some information about the equations

[formula]

mxyz=x^{3}+y^{3}+1+a_{2}x^{2}+a_{1}x+b_{2}y^{2}+b_{1}y

for fixed
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}
.

For
m\neq\pm 1
, these equations do not have automorphisms,
so we look at the action of the automorphism group on the solutions of
(
4
.1) modulo
m
.
If (
4
.1) has a solution

(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
whose orbit is infinite, and

z_{0}\equiv 0\mod m
, then the orbit contains infinitely many other solutions

(x_{r},y_{r},z_{r})
such that
z_{r}\equiv 0\mod m
.
All these give solutions of (
6
.1).

Thus, using Corollary
10
,
we get that if there is at least one solution
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
of (
6
.1) satisfying

\max\{|x_{0}|,|y_{0}|\}>2+\max\{|a_{2}|+|a_{1}|,|b_{2}|+|b_{1}|\},

then there are infinitely many.

7
The isomorphisms
\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y}
.

Let
S_{A,B}
be as in (
1
.2–3) and
assume that
a=b=c=1
. Set

[formula]

\bar{A}(x):=x^{n}A(x^{-1})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\bar{B}(y):=y^{m}B(y^{-1}).

The surfaces
S_{A,B},S_{\bar{A},B},S_{A,\bar{B}},S_{\bar{A},\bar{B}}
are the
companion surfaces
of

S_{A,B}
; their equations are the

companion equations.

By
[
KVP24
, 27]
there are isomorphisms

[formula]

\sigma_{y,A,B}:S_{A,B}\cong S_{\bar{A},B}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sigma_{x,A,B}:S_{A,B}\cong S_{A,\bar{B}}.

The most transparent are the rational forms on the
(x,y)
-coordinates

[formula]

\sigma_{y,A,B}:(x,y)\mapsto\bigl(B(y)/x,y\bigr)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sigma_{x,A,B}:(x,y)\mapsto\bigl(x,A(x)/y\bigr);

see also
[
Sch15
, Formulas 52-53]
.
For
xy=0
we need to use the versions

[formula]

(x,y)\mapsto\bigl(yz-\textstyle{\sum}_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}x^{i-1},y\bigr)\quad\mbox{and}\quad(x,y)\mapsto\bigl(x,xz-\textstyle{\sum}_{j=1}^{m}b_{j}y^{j-1}\bigr).

These are involutions in the sense that
\sigma_{y,\bar{A},B}\circ\sigma_{y,A,B}=1

and
\sigma_{x,A,\bar{B}}\circ\sigma_{x,A,B}=1
.

We will usually write
\sigma_{x}:=\sigma_{x,A,B}
and

\sigma_{y}:=\sigma_{y,A,B}
. Then
\sigma_{x}^{2}=1
and
\sigma_{y}^{2}=1
.

Remark
7
.5.
If
(0,y_{0},z_{0})
is a point on
S_{A,B}
then
y_{0}\neq 0
since
c\neq 0
, and
z_{0}
can be arbitrary.
Thus applying
\sigma_{y}
as in (
7
.4) we get points with

x_{1}
arbitrarily large. Thus Conjecture
2
holds for such surfaces. We can thus always work with the simpler forms (
7
.3) of
\sigma_{x}
and
\sigma_{y}
.

8
The isomorphism groupoid.

Let
S_{A,B}
be as in (
1
.2–3) and
assume that
a=b=c=1
.
The isomorphisms (
7
.2) of the companion surfaces
sit in a diagram

[formula]

\begin{array}[]{ccc}S_{A,B}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma_{x}}}{{\longleftrightarrow}}&S_{A,\bar{B}}\\[4.30554pt]
\sigma_{y}\updownarrow&&\hphantom{\sigma_{y}}\updownarrow\sigma_{y}\\
S_{\bar{A},B}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma_{x}}}{{\longleftrightarrow}}&S_{\bar{A},\bar{B}}\end{array}

The groupoid generated by these 8 isomorphisms
\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y}

is denoted by
\Sigma_{A,B}
.

By
[
KVP24
, Thm.3]
, going around the diagram (
8
.1)
clockwise gives an infinite order automorphism

[formula]

\sigma_{A,B}:=\sigma_{y}\circ\sigma_{x}\circ\sigma_{y}\circ\sigma_{x}\in\operatorname{Aut}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}(S_{A,B}).

Moreover, if
m=n=3
then
\langle\sigma_{A,B}\rangle
has finite index in

\operatorname{Aut}_{{\mathbb{C}}}(S_{A,B})
by
[
KVP24
, Thm.29]
, and
every infinite
\sigma_{A,B}
-orbit is
Zariski dense by
[
KVP24
, 51]
. Most likely these also hold for all
n,m\geq 3
.

Our plan is to start with an integral point on one of the companion surfaces of

S_{A,B}
, and then move it around using

\Sigma_{A,B}

to get infinitely many other integral points.
We need an elementary root estimate.

Lemma 9
.

Let
f(x)=x^{n}+\textstyle{\sum}_{i=0}^{n-1}a_{i}x^{i}
be a complex polynomial.
Then

(9.1)

all roots
r_{j}
of
f(x)
satisfy
|r_{j}|\leq\max\bigl\{1,\textstyle{\sum}_{i=0}^{n-1}|a_{i}|\bigr\}
, and

(9.2)

|f(x)|>|x^{m}|
for
0\leq m<n
and

|x|\leq-1+\textstyle{\sum}_{i=0}^{n-1}|a_{i}|
. ∎

We measure the size of points using the norm

|(x,y,z)|:=\max\{|x|,|y|\}
.

Corollary 10
.

With
S_{A,B}
as in (
1
.2–3),
assume that
a=b=c=1
and
n,m\geq 3
.
Let
p_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})\in S_{A,B}
be a complex point.
If

|p_{0}|>1+\max\bigl\{1,\textstyle{\sum}_{i=0}^{n-1}|a_{i}|,\textstyle{\sum}_{j=0}^{m-1}|b_{j}|\bigr\},

then

\max\bigl\{|\sigma_{x}(p_{0})|,|\sigma_{y}(p_{0})|\bigr\}>|p_{0}|.

Therefore
the
\Sigma_{A,B}
-orbit of
p_{0}
is infinite.

Proof. We may assume that
|x_{0}|\geq|y_{0}|
and consider

p_{1}:=\sigma_{x}(p_{0})=\bigl(x_{0},A(x_{0})/y_{0},z_{1}\bigr).

Here
\bigl|A(x_{0})\bigr|>|x_{0}|^{2}
by (
9
.2) and
y_{0}\neq 0
since

x_{0}
is not a root of
A(x)
. So

\bigl|A(x_{0})/y_{0}\bigr|>|x_{0}|
.

Alternating
\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y}
gives a sequence of points

p_{i}
in the
\Sigma_{A,B}
-orbit of
p_{0}

such that
|p_{i}|
is stricty increasing with
i
.
∎

Corollary 11
.

Let
S_{A,B}:=S_{a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}}
be as in (
4
.1).
If
\max\{|a_{2}|+|a_{1}|,|b_{2}|+|b_{1}|\}\geq 7
, then there is a
trivial solution
p_{0}=(\pm 1,\pm 1,z_{0})
of a companion equation
such that the
\Sigma_{A,B}
-orbit of
p_{0}
is infinite.

Proof. Working with a suitable companion equation, we may assume that

|a_{2}|\leq|a_{1}|\quad\mbox{and}\quad|a_{2}|+|a_{1}|\leq|b_{2}|+|b_{1}|=:d\geq 7.

Choosing
y_{0}=\pm 1
such that
b_{2}y_{0}^{2}
and
b_{1}y_{0}
have the same sign, we get that

|B(y_{0})|\geq|b_{2}|+|b_{1}|-2=d-2
. Thus

(x_{1},y_{1}):=\sigma_{x}(x_{0},y_{0})
such that
|x_{1}|\geq d-2
and
y_{1}=\pm 1
.
Then applying
\sigma_{y}
we get
(x_{2},y_{2}):=\sigma_{y}(x_{1},y_{1})
where

\begin{array}[]{rcl}|y_{2}|&\geq&(d-2)^{3}-|a_{2}|(d-2)^{2}-|a_{1}|(d-2)-1\\
&\geq&(d-2)^{3}-\lfloor{d/2}\rfloor(d-2)^{2}-d(d-2)-1.\end{array}

It is easy to check that this is
>d+2
for
d\geq 7
.
Thus the
\Sigma_{A,B}
-orbit of
p_{2}
is infinite
by Corollary
10
. ∎

Proposition 12
.

For
S_{A,B}:=S_{a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2}}
as in (
4
.1), the

\operatorname{Aut}(S_{A,B})
-orbit of every trivial solution is finite iff
S_{A,B}
is a companion surface of one of the following.

\begin{array}[]{lccl}(\ref{fin.triv.orb.prop}.1)&xyz-x^{3}-y^{3}-1&=&-x^{2}-y^{2},\\
(\ref{fin.triv.orb.prop}.2)&xyz-x^{3}-y^{3}-1&=&-2x^{2}-x-2y^{2}-y,\\
(\ref{fin.triv.orb.prop}.3)&xyz-x^{3}-y^{3}-1&=&-2x^{2}-x-y^{2},\\
(\ref{fin.triv.orb.prop}.4)&xyz-x^{3}-y^{3}-1&=&-x^{2}-2y^{2}-y.\end{array}

(The last 2 are isomorphic to each other.)

Proof. By
[
KVP24
, Thm.29]
,
\langle\sigma_{A,B}\rangle
has finite index in

\operatorname{Aut}_{{\mathbb{C}}}(S_{A,B})
. Thus we need to enumerate all cases
for which the

\Sigma_{A,B}
-orbit of every trivial solution is finite.
By Corollary
11
, this can happen only for

|a_{1}|+|a_{2}|,|b_{1}|+|b_{2}|\leq 6
. So we have a finite list
of possible exceptions.
The computer program is available at
https://web.math.princeton.edu/~jl5270/Mordell-Schinzel-code
. ∎

Remark 13
.

There are 4 polynomials
t^{3}+a_{2}t^{2}+a_{1}t+1\in{\mathbb{Z}}[t]

that map
\{1,-1\}
to itself. These are

t^{3}-t+1,t^{3}-t^{2}+1,t^{3}-t^{2}-2t+1,t^{3}-2t^{2}-t+1
.
Thus, combining with the list (

.1–4), we see that either
\Sigma_{A,B}
maps trivial solutions to trivial solutions, or the
\Sigma_{A,B}
-orbit of trivial solutions is infinite.

We do not have a direct proof of this.

15
The geometry of the surfaces
S_{A,B}
.

Let
\bar{S}_{A,B}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
denote the closure of the affine cubic surface

S_{A,B}\subset{\mathbb{A}}^{3}
. The curve at infinity is the nodal cubic curve

(xyz=ax^{3}+by^{3})\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
, and
\bar{S}_{A,B}
has an
A_{2}
-singularity at the node. That is, in suitable local analytic coordinates, it can be written as
(uv=w^{3})
.

Conversely, assume that we are over a field
k
of characteristic different from 2 or 3. Let
S\subset{\mathbb{A}}^{3}
be a cubic surface with projective closure

\bar{S}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
. Assume that the curve at infinity is a nodal cubic, and
\bar{S}
has an
A_{2}
-singularity at the node. Then, in suitable coordinates,

S=S_{A,B}
for some
A,B
as in (
1
.2);
see
[
KVP24
, Sec.2]
for details.

The presence of the
A_{2}
-singularity at infinity leads to the
infinite automorphism group of such surfaces; see
[
Kol24
,
KVP24
]
for descriptions of such automorphism groups.
If we allow
c=0
, the singularity becomes
A_{3}
-type,
(uv=w^{4})
.

16
Comments on
[
Mor52
]
and
[
Sch15
]
.

From the point of view of
[
KVP24
]
, the proof in

[
Sch15
]
has 2 main steps, though they are not separated there.

A preliminary observation is that if
|abc|>1
, then the equation (
1
.1) has infinitely many
{\mathbb{Z}}[(abc)^{-1}]
-integral solutions: we can take
x,y
to be arbitrary monomials in
a,b,c
, and set

z=G(x,y)/(xy)
.
Call these
monomial solutions
(though
z
is not assumed monomial).
The automorphism
\sigma_{A,B}
of (
8
.2) is also defined over
{\mathbb{Z}}[(abc)^{-1}]
.

The first step is then to show that for every
r>0

there is a
monomial point
p_{r}:=(x_{r},y_{r},z_{r})
such that

\sigma_{A,B}^{r}(p_{r})
is a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-integral point.
The
x_{r},y_{r}
are explicitly given in the form

a^{\lambda_{r}}b^{\mu_{r}}c^{\nu_{r}}
, where

\lambda_{r},\mu_{r},\nu_{r}
satisfy certain Fibonacci-type recursions;
see
[
Sch15
, Formula 30]
.
This is surprising, but the proof is relatively short; see
[
Sch15
, Lem.14]
.
This is clearly the plan in

[
Mor52
]
, though the general form in
[
Sch15
]
is a nontrivial extension.

The second step is to show that these
\sigma_{A,B}^{r}(p_{r})
do give
infinitely many different points. This is clear in the examples

xyz=ax^{3}+by^{3}+c
considered in
[
Mor52
]
, provided
|abc|\geq 2
.
However, neither
[
Mor52
]
nor
[
Mor69
, Sec.30]
gives any hint on how the general case should be treated.

This is the harder part of Schinzel’s papers.
The treatments require subtle inductive assumptions and estimates. At the end, for
m,n\geq 3
this approach works whenever
|abc|>1
; see
[
Sch15
, Thm.4]
.

Thus we are left with the cases
a,b,c\in\{1,-1\}
.
The disadvantage is that inverting
abc
does not give new solutions,
so we have only the
trivial solutions
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
where
x_{0},y_{0}\in\{1,-1\}
to start with.
The advantage is that, by
[
KVP24
, Thm.3]
the automorphism group
of
S_{A,B}
is infinite, and in most cases the orbit of trivial solutions is infinite.

Our proof works for
m=n=3
since there are only finitely many
exceptions, and they can be treated by hand.

17
Counting solutions.

Let
N_{A,B}(C)
denote the number of integral points on
S_{A,B}
satisfying

-C\leq x,y\leq C
.
Extensions of the Batyrev–Manin conjectures to K3 surfaces
(see for example
[
vL08
]
)
suggest the following.

(17.1)

Maps
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\to S_{A,B}
defined over
{\mathbb{Z}}
contribute

O(C^{\epsilon})
to
N_{A,B}(C)
.

(17.2)

All other integral points contribute
(\log C)^{r}
to
N_{A,B}(C)
for some
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

A detailed study of integral points on cubic surfaces is given in

[
BW24
]
.
More limited computations of
N_{A,B}(C)
were done in connection with
[
KVP24
]
. Both of these support the above general description, but unfortunately suggest different powers of
\log C
.

It is not easy to find maps
{\mathbb{A}}^{1}\to S_{A,B}
defined over
{\mathbb{Z}}
; see

[
TU22
]
,
[
Gre24
, Sec.5.5]
or
[
KVP24
, 51]
for some examples and further references.
