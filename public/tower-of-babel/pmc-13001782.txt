Neuroprotective mechanisms of dexmedetomidine in perioperative neurocognitive disorders: From molecular pathways to clinical translation
Authors: Zhi Shang, Yuyang Wu, Chen Gu, Ju Gao, Cunjin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPerioperative neurocognitive disorders (PNDs) are common severe complications observed in elderly patients following surgery, primarily characterized by a decline in postoperative cognitive function and memory. Dexmedetomidine, a highly selective α2-adrenergic receptor agonist, has garnered increasing attention due to its notable neuroprotective properties. This article comprehensively reviews the multifaceted neuroprotective mechanisms of dexmedetomidine in the prevention and management of PNDs, encompassing inhibition of neuroinflammation, mitigation of oxidative stress, reduction of cellular apoptosis, preservation of the blood-brain barrier, and regulation of metabolic reprogramming. Furthermore, this review delves into the clinical translational applications and future research directions of dexmedetomidine, offering a theoretical foundation for the development of more efficacious strategies in the prevention and treatment of PNDs.Keywords: Dexmedetomidine, Perioperative neurocognitive disorders, Neuroprotection, Molecular pathways, Clinical translation

Introduction

Perioperative neurocognitive disorders (PNDs), encompassing postoperative delirium (POD) and postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD), represent a pressing issue in perioperative medicine due to their profound impacts on patient prognosis and socioeconomic burden.1, 2 With the growing elderly population and rising volume of major surgeries, addressing PNDs has become a critical clinical priority.3 Conventional anesthetics, while indispensable for surgery, are increasingly linked to neurotoxicity and PND pathogenesis, creating an urgent demand for safer alternatives. Dexmedetomidine, a highly selective α₂ adrenergic receptor agonist with sedative, analgesic, and potential neuroprotective effects,4 has garnered attention as a promising option. This review synthesizes the clinical burden of PNDs, critically evaluates the limitations of conventional anesthetics, and delineates the therapeutic value of dexmedetomidine in optimizing perioperative cognitive outcomes.

Clinical burden of perioperative neurocognitive disorders

Notably, POD typically onsets within 1–3 days post-surgery with a short duration, while POCD may persist for weeks, months, or even years. The adverse consequences of PNDs are substantial: they significantly prolong hospitalization, escalate economic costs, and elevate mortality. Research indicates that 30-day mortality in POD patients reaches 7 %–10 %, compared to only 1 % in non-POD patients.5 Among patients who die one year after major surgery, mental health problems are common and often accompanied by cognitive dysfunction.6 More noteworthy is that postoperative cognitive impairment may persist for a long time and even develop into permanent damage, causing incalculable physical and mental pain and life pressure to patients and their families.7

Postoperative delirium is an acute complication of the central nervous system after surgery, characterized by disorientation, fluctuating levels of consciousness, impaired cognitive function, sleep disorders, abnormal neural movements, and emotional disorders. The exact cause of the disease remains incompletely understood, but a large number of studies have shown that its onset and progression are influenced by a variety of risk factors. These include internal pathological factors—such as sleep disorders, preoperative vitamin D deficiency, hypertension, vascular dementia risk factors, depression, high ASA scores, diabetes, physical weakness—as well as external factors, including anesthesia type, patient age, preoperative cognitive function, central nervous system inflammatory reaction and environmental influences.8, 9, 10 The harm of postoperative delirium is far-reaching and lasting, particularly for elderly patients. It will significantly prolong the hospital stay, increase the risk of postoperative complications, reduce the quality of life, increase medical costs, and lead to higher morbidity and mortality, posing a serious threat to their health and safety.11, 12

Postoperative cognitive impairment (POCD) is a special type of cognitive disorder that involves neurological activity impairments such as selective attention, alertness, perception, learning, memory, executive function, speech and language abilities, emotions, visual space, and visual motor abilities.13, 14 It manifests as cognitive changes ranging from mild to extensive, affecting one or more cognitive domains, and occurring without head trauma or other brain related injuries.15, 16 Postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) is a serious public health problem. According to the data, 20 %–50 % of patients will suffer from it, especially after long-term and high-intensity major invasive surgery such as cardiac,17 non-cardiac17, 18, 19 and carotid artery. This not only greatly increases the physical and mental burden of patients themselves, but also brings extensive and heavy socio-economic impact.3 The development of POCD can lead to a range of symptoms, seriously influencing the living quality of patients, and may induce additional physiological and psychological diseases, ultimately affecting social stability.1, 20, 21

Current Theoretical Limitations on other anesthetic drugs

Currently, a variety of anesthetic drugs are widely used in clinical practice, including propofol, opioid drugs represented by morphine, remimazolam, etomidate, and so on. These drugs can effectively meet the different needs of patients during surgery and play a crucial role in the medical field. However, numerous studies have indicated that they may possess certain neurotoxicity, which can lead to various adverse effects, such as cognitive impairment, respiratory and circulatory system depression, and other problems.

Take propofol as an example: over the past 30 years, its favorable pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties have made it one of the most widely used anesthetic agents in clinical practice.22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27 In basic research and clinical trials, propofol has indeed demonstrated significant neuroprotective effects. Nevertheless, many studies have also pointed out that it has neurotoxicity and can cause cognitive impairment. The strong evidence of the toxicity of propofol to the human body stems from a phenomenon known as "propofol infusion syndrome" (PIS).28, 29 When patients receive prolonged propofol infusion(> 48 h) at high doses (> 4 mg/kg/h), they are at high risk of developing PIS.28 Some studies have shown that propofol can cause damage to the mitochondria of hippocampal neurons in a dose-dependent manner. Moreover, it has also been reported that propofol can induce the death of new neurons in the developing brain, ultimately leading to cognitive function disorders, specifically involving various aspects such as learning, memory, planning, and reasoning.30 Relevant research has indicated that propofol can induce brain dysfunction in young and adult rats by activating the γ-GABA receptor and reducing the expression of neurotransmitters and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF).31, 32, 33 In addition, for elderly rats, long-term exposure to propofol will result in long-term learning and memory impairment.34

Opioids have long played a pivotal role in clinical practice, and as the most effective analgesics available, they remain the cornerstone of pain management for cancer patients.35 When pain is not effectively controlled, the harm is multidimensional and severe. Physically, it can cause a series of adverse effects such as metabolic disorders and increased cardiovascular burden; Psychologically and spiritually, persistent pain erodes patients' autonomy and dignity while significantly weakening their decision-making ability, leaving them in a state of physical and mental exhaustion.35, 36

However, with the widespread use of opioid drugs, many problems have gradually become prominent. In the field of non-cancer pain treatment, there is currently a lack of strong evidence to support its long-term effectiveness. At the same time, the rampant abuse of opioid drugs has posed significant challenges to public health. Perhaps most concerning is the paucity of data on adverse effects associated with chronic opioid use, such as endocrine disorders, immune suppression, and opioid induced hyperalgesia, which leaves clinicians facing considerable uncertainties when making medication decisions.37, 38, 39 These issues have triggered widespread and profound concerns about the increasing use of opioid drugs in non-cancer pain treatment.

Specifically, in terms of the mechanism of action of opioid drugs, such as fentanyl and morphine, the main target of action is the μ-opioid receptor (MOR), which plays a powerful analgesic role based on this. Fentanyl, fentanyl analogues, and other novel synthetic opioid drugs are all full agonists that exhibit different valences against the μ-opioid receptor.36, 39 Once they bind to the μ-opioid receptor, they quickly trigger a series of typical clinical effects, including pupil constriction and significant inhibitory effects on respiration and the central nervous system. However, in previous research and clinical applications, there has long been a misconception: an excessive focus on the interaction between drugs and μ-opioid receptors that ignores the potential impact on other types of receptors.36 In fact, in-depth research has found that the side effects caused by opioid drugs are closely related to the receptors they act on. Among them, the strong addictive nature and the high risk of inducing respiratory depression in clinical applications are particularly prominent and harmful. These adverse manifestations clearly reflect the inherent defects of traditional anesthetics, underscoring an urgent need for collaborative efforts of researchers and clinical workers to explore safer and more effective pain treatment plans.

Treatment positioning of dexmedetomidine

Dexmedetomidine is a newly developed highly efficient and selective α₂ adrenergic receptor agonist, with a binding ratio of 1:1600 to α₁ and α₂ adrenergic receptors. It has extremely high selective binding ability to α₂ receptors, with an α₂ receptor affinity 8 times that of clonidine, reflecting its superior binding potency at this receptor subtype.40 This drug is known for its sedative, anti-anxiety, and analgesic effects, while causing minimal respiratory depression.41 It has been widely used in surgical procedures and for the prevention of postoperative mental illness.42, 43, 44

In the intensive care unit, dexmedetomidine plays an important role in patient sedation and as an adjuvant to general anesthesia due to its unique high selective receptor activity, short half-life, and special pharmacological effects.4, 41 Its distribution half-life is only about 6 min, and its elimination half-life is 2 h, which makes it suitable for clinical needs of short-term or continuous infusion. The drug is mainly metabolized by the liver (95 % through glucuronic acid binding), and the metabolites are excreted by the kidneys.45 Consequently, dose adjustment is recommended for patients with liver dysfunction, whereas no dosage modification is typically required for those with renal dysfunction.46

From the perspective of mechanism of action, the sedative effect of dexmedetomidine mainly originates from the activation of the central locus coeruleus α2A receptor. After activating the receptor, the release of norepinephrine (NE) is inhibited, and sympathetic nervous tension decreases, inducing a sedative state similar to natural sleep. Of particular note is the unique "awakenability" of dexmedetomidine. Research has shown that this characteristic is closely related to the activation of dopamine (DA) neurons in the ventral tegmental area (VTA). After activation of the DA system, it can effectively counteract excessive inhibition of the locus coeruleus, thereby maintaining a dynamic balance between wakefulness and sedation.47

In terms of analgesia, dexmedetomidine mainly exerts its effects through the mediation of spinal cord and peripheral α2 receptors. This mechanism of action enables it to reduce the dosage of opioid drugs used, thereby lowering the risk of respiratory depression.48

Further exploration reveals that the sedative effect of dexmedetomidine mainly relies on precise regulation of the locus coeruleus α2A receptor, while its neuroprotective effect is achieved through a wider range of receptor subtypes (such as peripheral α2A receptors, imidazoline I receptors) and downstream complex signaling pathways.48, 49 It can be seen that there are significant differences in the distribution of targets and pathways of action for the sedative and neuroprotective effects of dexmedetomidine.

Molecular mechanisms

Classic anti-inflammatory pathways

Dexmedetomidine, as a highly selective α2-adrenergic receptor (α2-AR) agonist, has demonstrated unique advantages in the anti-inflammatory field.50, 51 Its anti-inflammatory effects involve multiple molecular pathways, mainly achieved by inhibiting pro-inflammatory signals and regulating immune cell function. The following provides a detailed analysis of its classic anti-inflammatory pathway:

α2-adrenergic receptor-mediated signaling pathway

Dexmedetomidine can activate α2-AR, especially α2A subtype, which can effectively inhibit sympathetic nervous system activity, reduce the release of catecholamines such as norepinephrine, and reduce systemic inflammatory response from the source.

In terms of downstream effects, dexmedetomidine can inhibit adenylate cyclase (AC), leading to a decrease in intracellular cAMP levels and protein kinase A (PKA) activity, ultimately inhibiting pro-inflammatory transcription factors such as NF-κB and reducing the transmission of inflammatory signals.46, 52 At the same time, dexmedetomidine can activate G-protein coupled inward rectifying potassium channels (GIRK), causing cell hyperpolarization, inhibiting neuronal excitability, and indirectly reducing the release of inflammatory mediators to alleviate inflammatory reactions.53

Inhibit the NF-κB pathway

Dexmedetomidine plays a crucial role in inhibiting the NF-κB pathway. It first inhibits the IκB kinase (IKK), blocks the phosphorylation of IκBα, thereby preventing the degradation of IκBα and allowing NF-κB to persist in the cytoplasm.54 In addition, dexmedetomidine can reduce the nuclear translocation of NF-κB, inhibit the entry of p65 subunit into the nucleus, reduce the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and inhibit the development of inflammation at the gene expression level.52, 55 Extensive experimental studies have shown that dexmedetomidine can significantly reduce NF-κB activity and downstream inflammatory cytokine levels in both sepsis and acute lung injury models, fully demonstrating its inhibitory effect on the NF-κB pathway (Table 1, Fig. 1).56, 57, 58, 59, 60, 61, 62, 63, 64, 65, 66, 67

Regulating the MAPK pathway

When dexmedetomidine acts on the MAPK pathway, it can accurately inhibit the phosphorylation of p38 MAPK, JNK, and ERK.68, 69, 70, 71 This inhibitory effect brings multiple positive effects, including not only the reduction of the expression of inflammatory mediators such as COX-2 and iNOS, but also the effective inhibition of the activation of macrophages and neutrophils, along with a significant attenuation of oxidative stress response (Table 2). In ischemia-reperfusion injury, dexmedetomidine effectively reduces tissue inflammation by inhibiting p38 MAPK, fully demonstrating its important role in regulating the MAPK pathway and reducing inflammation.72

Regulating the JAK-STAT pathway

Dexmedetomidine regulates the JAK-STAT pathway by reducing STAT3 phosphorylation levels, blocking the signaling of cytokines such as IL-6.58 In the POCD model, dexmedetomidine effectively reduces neuroinflammation by inhibiting the JAK2/STAT3 pathway, providing new ideas and methods for clinical treatment of POCD (Table 2).

Inhibit the release of HMGB1

Dexmedetomidine can reduce the release of high mobility group protein B1 (HMGB1), block the binding of HMGB1 to TLR4/RAGE receptors, and thus inhibit late stage inflammatory responses. In animal models of sepsis, dexmedetomidine reduces plasma HMGB1 levels and improves prognosis, fully demonstrating its value in controlling late stage inflammation and improving disease prognosis (Table 1).73

Indirect regulation of cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway

Dexmedetomidine indirectly regulates the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway by activating central α2-AR, enhancing vagus nerve activity, promoting acetylcholine release, activating α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (α7nAChRs) on macrophages, inhibiting NF-κB (Table 2).74, 75, 76, 77 In intestinal ischemia-reperfusion injury, dexmedetomidine effectively reduces inflammation through this pathway, indicating its potential in regulating the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway and alleviating inflammation.78

Emerging mechanisms: beyond inflammation

Dexmedetomidine, as a highly selective α2-adrenergic receptor agonist, has been traditionally used in sedation and analgesia. However, with deepening research, especially recent findings, dexmedetomidine has revealed a new mechanism of action in neuroprotection. Its function far exceeds traditional cognition and involves the fine regulation of neuroimmune and metabolic networks.

DEX reduces oxidative stress and neuronal apoptosis

Dexmedetomidine has significant antioxidant effects, which can reduce the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and inhibit the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome associated with oxidative stress (Fig. 2).41, 59, 79 Researchers have studied a mouse CHF model established by transverse aortic constriction (TAC) and H9c2 cardiomyocytes, discovering that dexmedetomidine inhibits PARP-1-dependent cell death in CHF mice and cardiomyocytes by regulating ROS-mediated NLRP3 inflammasome activation.59 Oxidative stress is closely related to POCD. Previous studies have confirmed that oxidative stress can induce POCD in aged rats (Fig. 2).80 DEX shows significant potential in improving cognitive function, which may effectively improve the cognitive level of aged rats by inhibiting the expression of relaxin-3.81 At the level of mechanism of action, DEX can regulate JNK/SP1 and STAT4/SP1 signaling pathways (Fig. 2),82, 83 and improve cell survival by inhibiting oxidative stress and inflammatory response triggered by α2 adrenergic receptor activation84; It can also alleviate nerve and brain injury induced by intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) by inhibiting oxidative stress caused by PGC-1 α pathway inactivation and mitochondrial dysfunction.85 In addition, studies have shown that DEX can reduce the activities of enzyme complexes I, II, III and IV in the mitochondrial respiratory chain, thereby reducing oxidative stress and improving POCD in patients.86

In the regulation of neuronal apoptosis, DEX also shows good inhibitory effect. Studies have shown that DEX combined with s (+)-ketamine can significantly reduce apoptosis triggered by incomplete cerebral ischemia/reperfusion.87 In the face of lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced neuronal apoptosis, DEX can effectively alleviate neuronal apoptosis by upregulating the expression of antiapoptotic protein Bcl-2 and downregulating the expression of Pro apoptotic protein Bax.88 In recent years, more mechanisms have been revealed. DEX can regulate apoptosis by regulating sigma-1 receptor (Sig-1R)89, 90 and PI3K/AKT signaling pathway (Table 2). In addition, DEX can also mediate the upregulation of neuroglobulin, alleviate oxidative stress injury, and improve neuronal apoptosis in the process of POCD.91

Microglia reprogramming: from proinflammatory to repair

As immune sentinels of the central nervous system, microglia play a crucial role in neuroinflammation and repair processes due to their phenotype polarization (M1/M2). Microglia are innate immune cells of the central nervous system, which remain in a resting state and perform immune surveillance functions under physiological conditions.92 Under pathological stimulation, microglia can be activated and differentiate into classically activated M1 type and alternatively activated M2 type (Fig. 2, Table 2).93 M1 type microglia mainly secrete pro-inflammatory factors, exacerbating neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and neurotoxicity; M2 type microglia mainly express inhibitory factors, which play a role in inhibiting neuroinflammation and promoting neural function recovery (Fig. 2).94, 95, 96

The application of single-cell sequencing technology revealed the deep-seated mechanism of dexmedetomidine on the phenotype of microglia. Dexmedetomidine, mediated by α2 receptor, inhibits sympathetic nerve activity, thereby downregulating the expression of pro-inflammatory factors, such as IL-1β and TNF-α,97 while upregulating the secretion of anti-inflammatory factors, such as IL-10 and TGF-β, driving microglia from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to a repaired M2 phenotype (Fig. 1).67 This phenotypic switch is of great significance. The M2 phenotype can promote tissue repair and inflammation resolution, bringing new hope for alleviating chronic inflammatory injury in neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer's disease.96 During the occurrence and development of neuroinflammation, the NF-κB signaling pathway plays a crucial role in regulating the activation status of M1/M2 in microglia.98 Previous studies have shown that inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway can effectively promote the polarization of microglia towards M2 type.99 As a drug with unique pharmacological properties, dexmedetomidine can precisely regulate the phenotype of microglia by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway and activating the STAT3 signal, thereby potentially affecting synaptic pruning regulation in the neuroinflammatory microenvironment and participating in the alleviation of neuroinflammation and neural function repair process (Table 1, Fig. 2).100

Regulation of synaptic pruning

In disease models such as brain injury or epilepsy, as well as chronic sleep restriction mouse models, dexmedetomidine may exhibit significant regulatory ability on synaptic pruning.

In the chronic sleep restriction mouse model, mice showed reduced learning and memory abilities, decreased expression of synaptic proteins PSD95 and synaptophysin, and decreased number of dendritic spines. At the same time, microglia are activated, and the expression of complement pathway related proteins C1q, C3, and C3aR significantly increases, leading to increased phagocytosis of synapses by microglia and ultimately exacerbating synaptic loss (Fig. 2).

After intravenous injection of dexmedetomidine, it can effectively improve cognitive dysfunction in mice, prolong their non rapid eye movement sleep duration, and increase the proportion of delta waves during sleep. Dexmedetomidine can reverse the adverse effects of decreased expression of PSD95 and synaptophysin, as well as reduced dendritic spine numbers. It can also inhibit the activation of microglia and the expression of complement pathway related proteins C3 and C3aR, thereby suppressing microglia phagocytic behavior towards synapses.

Further investigation revealed that dexmedetomidine inhibits the release of complement C3 by activating the α2A receptor in astrocytes. Under normal circumstances, C3 acts on C3aR in microglia, mediating their phagocytosis of synapses, while the mechanism of action of dexmedetomidine reduces synaptic loss after sleep deprivation. In addition, administering the C3aR inhibitor SB290157 to block the C3-C3AR signal can also reverse the cognitive decline and synaptic loss in mice with chronic sleep restriction, and the effect is no different from that of dexmedetomidine. This further confirms the key role of complement mediated synaptic elimination in microglia101 (Fig. 2).

Therefore, in disease models such as brain injury or epilepsy, similar to Wogonin,102 dexmedetomidine also affects the recognition and clearance of abnormal synapses by microglia by regulating complement pathways such as C1q/C3 and neural activity dependent signals. Moderate synaptic pruning is crucial for maintaining neural network stability, effectively preventing excessive excitotoxicity and improving cognitive function after traumatic brain injury.103, 104, 105

Metabolic coupling: a new perspective of energy support and gut brain dialogue

The role of dexmedetomidine in neuroprotection is also reflected in the regulation of neural cell metabolic interaction and gut brain axis, which provides a new idea for the maintenance of neural homeostasis.

Astrocyte neuron lactate shuttle

The regulation of glutamate release plays a key role in the neuroprotective effect of dexmedetomidine. Dexmedetomidine can reduce the release of catecholamines (CAT), thereby regulating the release of glutamate (Fig. 2).106

Astrocytes play an important role in energy metabolism: they primarily utilize glycogen stored within the cell to synthesize lactate, which is then transported to adjacent neurons.107 This process is known as astrocyte neuron lactate shuttle (ANLS).108, 109 Previous studies have shown that various neurotransmitters, such as norepinephrine and glutamate, can promote the synthesis of lactate in astrocytes and transport lactate to neurons. On the other hand, once absorbed by neurons, lactate can serve as the main energy metabolism pathway for ATP synthesis, providing a priority shortcut. These pathways include glycolysis, tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation in neurons (Fig. 2).107, 110

Extensive in vivo and in vitro studies have consistently shown that lactate produced by astrocytes is a multifaceted mediator that affects synaptic plasticity and basic neuronal function.111 This complex metabolic interaction ensures that neurons receive the necessary support, highlighting the crucial role of astrocyte neuron metabolic cooperation in stress-induced behavior or symptoms.107, 112

So dexmedetomidine may promote lactate production by enhancing the glycolysis process mediated by α2 receptors in astrocytes. The generated lactate is transported to neurons through glutamate, providing support for the survival of neurons under stress conditions such as ischemia and trauma, optimizing metabolic coupling, and helping to alleviate neural energy crisis.

Gut brain axis SCFAs regulation

Short chain fatty acids (SCFAs) derived from gut microbiota, such as butyric acid, can affect microglial maturation and neuroinflammation through the blood-brain barrier or vagus nerve. Dexmedetomidine may improve intestinal permeability and microbiota composition by regulating sympathetic tone, thereby increasing SCFAs levels.113 The anti-inflammatory and epigenetic regulatory effects of SCFAs work synergistically with the central effects of dexmedetomidine, demonstrating substantial therapeutic potential in diseases such as sepsis related encephalopathy (Table 2).114, 115, 116

Besides, dexmedetomidine can increase the abundance of intestinal bacteria such as Butyricimonas and Parasottella that exert anti-inflammatory effects, improve intestinal pathology and permeability in ADHD model mice, protect intestinal barrier function, alleviate intestinal and brain inflammation, and improve ADHD like behavior. The direct therapeutic effect of DEX on ADHD can be simulated by transplanting feces of SHR rats treated with DEX. Research has shown that DEX works by reshaping the host microbiota gut brain axis, making it a potential and promising intervention for ADHD117 (Fig. 2).

Prevents the damage of the blood-brain barrier

Tight junction protein is a key component to maintain the integrity of the blood-brain barrier (BBB), and its reduced expression after surgery will lead to increased BBB permeability, which will become a potential factor to induce POCD.118 Anesthesia and surgical operation can activate mast cells (MCS), promote their release of inflammatory cytokines, and then destroy the normal permeability of the BBB, ultimately leading to the occurrence of POCD.119, 120, 121

The dexmedetomidine has shown significant efficacy in protecting BBB integrity and improving cognitive function. Studies have shown that DEX can strengthen tight junction structures and effectively improve cognitive function by inhibiting the activation of NF-κB and NLRP3 signaling pathways (Fig. 1).60, 122 In addition, the preventive use of DEX can stabilize the integrity of the blood spinal cord barrier and significantly reduce the adverse effects caused by spinal cord ischemia / reperfusion injury in rat,123 which further highlights the important value of DEX in the field of neuroprotection.

Clinical treatment progress of dexmedetomidine

Efficacy in POD prevention

Dexmedetomidine (DEX) has shown significant clinical value in reducing the incidence of postoperative delirium (POD), and has shown differential effects in different types of surgery and clinical scenarios. A large number of studies have shown that in the field of noncardiac surgery, the effect of low-dose and long-term DEX infusion strategy is significant.124 If elderly patients receive 0.1 μg/kg/h continuous infusion in ICU after surgery, the incidence of POD can be reduced from 23 % to 9 %; For elderly patients undergoing hip arthroplasty, perioperative administration of 0.5 μg/kg loading dose + 0.2 μg/kg/h maintenance dose can significantly reduce the incidence of POD from 20 % in the control group to 2 %. Multiple meta-analyses125, 126, 127 also confirmed that DEX application during surgery can reduce the risk of POD in patients undergoing cardiac and noncardiac surgery.

In cardiac surgery, the application effect of DEX is controversial: the preventive effect of a single intraoperative infusion on POD is not clear, but the continuous infusion strategy from anesthesia induction to the end of surgery can effectively improve the incidence and duration of POD. However, a recent multicenter study involving adult cardiac surgery patients128 showed that perioperative use of DEX did not significantly reduce the incidence of POD. Another meta-analysis129 pointed out that although perioperative use of DEX can reduce the risk of postoperative ventricular tachycardia and delirium, it may increase the risk of bradycardia and hypotension.

It is worth noting that DEX also performed well in children's surgery. Taking children's tonsillectomy as an example, the application of DEX significantly reduced the incidence of POD from 48.3 % to 16.1 %, while simultaneously decreasing both the frequency and severity of POD. With the expansion of research scope, scholars began to explore the universality of DEX in the prevention of POD in ICU adults.130, 131 A number of meta-analyses 130, 131, 132 have shown that DEX has the potential to reduce the incidence of delirium in the ICU environment irrespective of postoperative status, providing a broader theoretical support and practical basis for its clinical application.

Efficacy in POCD prevention

Postoperative cognitive impairment (POCD) is quite common in the elderly population, greatly affecting the quality of life of patients.133 Dexmedetomidine, a widely used anesthetic, has been increasingly recognized in recent studies for its potential to improve POCD.134, 135, 136

Surgical trauma and anesthesia drugs can activate microglia, causing them to release pro-inflammatory factors such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α).137, 138, 139 These pro-inflammatory factors can impair hippocampal synaptic plasticity, ultimately leading to cognitive decline. On the contrary, dexmedetomidine can reduce the expression of inflammatory factors such as interleukin-17A (IL-17A) and S-100β, thereby alleviating inflammatory damage to the central nervous system.136, 140

Meanwhile, surgical stress can lead to the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the body, causing oxidative damage to neurons.141, 142 And dexmedetomidine can enhance the activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD), reduce the level of malondialdehyde (MDA), and effectively alleviate oxidative stress response.143, 144 Moreover, dexmedetomidine has a synergistic effect with other anesthetics such as opioids and propofol, which can reduce the dosage of these drugs and mitigate their long-term adverse effects on synaptic transmission.48 Metabolomics studies have shown that dexmedetomidine can improve synaptic function and energy metabolism by regulating pathways such as glycerophospholipid metabolism and amino acid metabolism (such as glycine, serine, gamma aminobutyric acid), thereby protecting cognitive function.

Extensive clinical studies have confirmed that dexmedetomidine can reduce the incidence of POCD. In elderly patients undergoing abdominal surgery, the incidence of POCD in the group treated with dexmedetomidine was significantly lower than that in the control group, and the 72 h Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE) score was higher after surgery. For patients undergoing cardiac surgery, dexmedetomidine can improve cerebral oxygen saturation and significantly reduce the incidence of postoperative cognitive impairment from 37 % to 29 %. A multicenter randomized controlled trial showed that intraoperative use of dexmedetomidine can reduce the incidence of cognitive impairment from 24.6 % to 16.8 % one month after surgery. Moreover, dexmedetomidine can also shorten the postoperative recovery time, and patients treated with this drug have shorter extubation time and directional recovery time, as well as a lower incidence of postoperative delirium.145

Additional studies have explored the role and possible mechanism of dexmedetomidine in POCD. In a rat model, 24 male Sprague Dawley rats were randomly divided into sham operation group, POCD group, and dexmedetomidine + POCD group, with 8 rats in each group. Cognitive function was evaluated through Morris water maze (MWM), while the proportion of Treg cells in peripheral blood was determined, factors related to inflammation were identified, and the hippocampus and spleen tissues of rats were analyzed. This study confirms the effect and possible mechanism of dexmedetomidine on POCD, that is, dexmedetomidine can improve POCD, reduce HMGB1 release, and regulate HMGB1-induced Treg cell activity. This provides a reference for the clinical treatment and prognosis of POCD in the future, and helps to enrich the treatment strategies for POCD.146

Dose-dependent risks

In the intensive care unit (ICU), delirium is a common complication, especially in patients receiving mechanical ventilation and sedation. Studies have shown that the use of certain medications is closely associated with the risk of delirium. For example, high doses of sedatives may increase the incidence of delirium. In one study, it was found that high doses of propofol (>1 μg/kg/h) were significantly associated with delirium, with a risk ratio (RR) of 1.32.147

In addition, the use of dexamethasone is also believed to be associated with delirium. In a large retrospective cohort study, patients who received dexamethasone had a significantly higher incidence of delirium compared to those who did not receive it.148 This suggests that caution should be exercised when using dexamethasone, especially in critically ill patients.

In addition, another study examined the use of propofol and remifentanil in patients with septic shock. The results showed that remifentanil can reduce the need for norepinephrine, potentially lowering the risk of delirium.149 This suggests that remifentanil may be a safer option when selecting a sedative.

Unlike the linear correlation between dose toxicity of such drugs, the dose-response relationship of dexmedetomidine (DEX) is significantly unique: at low to moderate doses (0.2–0.7 μg/kg/h), DEX inhibits microglial activation by activating α2 adrenergic receptors (reducing IL-6 and TNF-α release), upregulates the expression of GluN2B receptors on the postsynaptic membrane to stabilize synaptic function, and exerts neuroprotective effects.150 When the dose exceeds 1.0 μg/kg/h, there is a relatively reduced risk of inhibiting leukocyte mediated lung injury and lung tissue apoptosis in a rat sepsis model.151

In conclusion, drug dosage and selection play an important role in the occurrence of delirium, and clinicians should weigh the pros and cons when using these drugs, carefully select and adjust the dose to reduce the risk of delirium.

Dexmedetomidine (DEX), a widely used sedative and anesthetic agent, exhibits dose-dependent risks that may vary across different genetic backgrounds. While the APOE ε4 genotype is known to modulate the metabolism and efficacy of certain drugs, accumulating evidence indicates that it does not significantly alter DEX-related drug responses or risks in clinical practice.

Notably, APOE ε4 is strongly associated with increased risk of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), as it contributes to the pathological progression of neurodegeneration. However, in the context of DEX administration, APOE ε4 carriers show no significant differences in drug reactions or safety profiles compared to non-carriers—likely due to the distinct mechanism of DEX’s neuroprotective effects, which diverges from the AD-related pathogenic pathways mediated by APOE ε4.152, 153, 154

This drug-specificity of APOE ε4 is further supported by other pharmacogenetic studies: for instance, while CYP2D6 and CYP2C19 genotypes profoundly affect the concentration and efficacy of atomoxetine, APOE ε4 exerts no such regulatory role.155, 156

Collectively, current evidence does not support a meaningful impact of APOE ε4 on DEX’s dose-dependent risks. Thus, in clinical practice, DEX dosage adjustment should prioritize patient-specific factors such as overall health status and concurrent medication use, rather than the APOE ε4 genotype.157, 158, 159

Clinical heterogeneity

The clinical evidence of dexmedetomidine (DEX) in the prevention and treatment of perioperative neurocognitive disorders (PND) during cardiac surgery presents notable inconsistencies: while some studies demonstrate that DEX can significantly reduce the incidence of postoperative delirium (POD),160 multicenter trials have failed to observe its efficacy in delayed neurocognitive dysfunction (DNCD).161 The primary sources of this heterogeneity can be summarized into three aspects: First, there are significant differences between patients at baseline. For example, in elderly patients with diabetes and cerebrovascular diseases, the expression of α2-AR in their brain is downregulated, which may weaken the neuroprotective response of DEX. Although the carrying status of APOE ε4 genotype does not directly affect the efficacy of DEX, it will aggravate the risk of cognitive impairment by overlapping with surgical trauma, leading to the differentiation of subgroup results. Secondly, there is a lack of unified standards for the dosing regimen. The regimen of intravenous pump injection (0.5 μg/kg) 30 min before extracorporeal circulation versus continuous maintenance during surgery (0.2 μg/kg/h) may produce vastly different protective effects due to the mismatch between the peak drug concentration in the brain and the time window of ischemia-reperfusion injury. Thirdly, the definition of outcomes and evaluation tools are confusing. Some studies use the CAM-ICU scale to diagnose POD (within 72 h after surgery), while others use the MoCA scale to evaluate DNCD (1 month after surgery). The differences in evaluation dimensions and time windows directly contribute to the divergent results. The above-mentioned heterogeneity poses a key challenge for clinical translation: on the one hand, it is difficult to form accurate recommendations for "surgical type patient subgroup medication regimen" based on existing evidence. Consequently, clinicians frequently rely on empirical judgment rather than evidence-based medication decisions. On the other hand, variable confounding may mask the potential benefits of DEX for specific subgroups (such as valve replacement patients without severe comorbidities), making it difficult to replicate positive results in multicenter studies and delaying its widespread application in the standardized prevention and treatment of PND in cardiac surgery.

Future perspectives

There are multiple limitations in exploring the neuroprotective effects of dexmedetomidine on perioperative neurocognitive disorders (PNDs) in this article. Firstly, there is significant heterogeneity in clinical evidence, and the improvement effect of dexmedetomidine on PNDs in cardiac surgery related trials is contradictory to non-cardiac surgery. Additionally, the administration regimen (such as loading dose and maintenance dose combination), outcome evaluation tools (such as CAM-ICU and MoCA scales), and evaluation time points are not consistent, making it difficult to compare the results horizontally; Secondly, there is insufficient research on dose related risks and individual differences, and the safe dose range for different populations (such as those with liver and kidney dysfunction and the elderly) is not clear. Except for the APOE ε4 genotype, the impact of other gene polymorphisms on drug efficacy is not explored; Furthermore, mechanism research often focuses on a single pathway (such as inflammation and oxidative stress), and the synergistic effects between multiple mechanisms such as gut brain axis and metabolic coupling have not been systematically validated. Additionally, basic mechanisms (such as synaptic pruning regulation) lack clinical biomarker translation; Finally, research designs are mostly single center small sample trials, with significant differences in baseline characteristics among populations. The neuroprotective effects, dose adjustments, and long-term safety data for special populations such as children and critically ill patients are also relatively limited.

Dexmedetomidine is expected to make significant breakthroughs in the field of postoperative cognitive impairment. Based on the current research of the mechanism of dexmedetomidine (DEX) intervention in perioperative neurocognitive disorders (PND), future investigations should prioritize mechanism-driven precision exploration and translational research: at the gut brain axis level, the pathway of DEX regulating gut microbiota and reducing endotoxin translocation can be verified through the PND model of elderly rats combined with 16S rRNA sequencing and metabolomics. A randomized controlled trial of "DEX + probiotics" will be conducted for elderly abdominal surgery patients to simultaneously detect microbiota diversity, inflammation, and gut derived markers. In terms of metabolic coupling mechanism, a rat cardiac surgery extracorporeal circulation model can be used to investigate the effects of DEX on lactate transporters (MCT1/MCT4) and changes in brain metabolism. The clinical focus should be on high-risk surgical populations with ischemia and hypoxia, and a DEX dose gradient experiment can be designed to monitor brain lactate levels in combination with magnetic resonance spectroscopy, exploring the synergistic effect of "DEX + lactate supplements". In the context of synaptic pruning direction, an elderly mouse model can be used to detect the regulation of DEX on microRNA-132 and synaptic structure. Clinically, the effects of DEX on synaptic function indicators in patients of different age groups can be analyzed through age stratification, and the interaction can be explored by including the APOE ε4 genotype subgroup. At the same time, attention should be paid to multi mechanism collaboration (such as the association between gut brain axis and metabolic coupling), constructing a mechanism network through multi omics, conducting multi center large sample trials to unify PND diagnosis and evaluation criteria for clinical heterogeneity, establishing a DEX dose prediction model based on patient baseline characteristics, promoting the upgrade of DEX from broad-spectrum application to precise intervention, and improving perioperative cognitive outcomes.
