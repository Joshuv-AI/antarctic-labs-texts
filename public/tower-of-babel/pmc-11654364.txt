The moderating effect of dietary fiber intake on the association between sleep pattern and liver fibrosis in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease: a study from NHANES
Authors: Guoqing Jia, Mengzhen Jia, Chuntao Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInsufficient nocturnal sleep was associated with a higher risk of fibrosis in patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). Dietary fiber intake may improve the stimulate the secretion of sleep cytokines, inhibit the inflammatory pathway, contribute to regulating sleep disorders and alleviate liver fibrosis. The associations of dietary fiber intake, sleep patterns, with liver fibrosis remain unclear. The study aimed to explore the associations between dietary fiber, sleep, and liver fibrosis, as well as the moderating effect of dietary fiber intake between sleep patterns and liver fibrosis in MASLD patients.MethodsUsing data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database spanning from 2017 to 2020, a cross-sectional study included participants with MASLD was performed to assess the relationship between sleep patterns, dietary fiber intake, and liver fibrosis. Weighted univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were used to examine the linear connection between sleep pattern, dietary fiber intake, and liver fibrosis. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) method was also performed to describe the nonlinear relationship. A two-part linear regression model was also used to estimate threshold effects. All results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsTotally, 1343 MASLD patients were included for final analysis. Among them, 207 (15.41%) have liver fibrosis. Dietary fiber intake did not correlate significantly with sleep pattern in patients with MASLD (Spearman’s r = -0.028, P = 0.1678). No connection was observed between poor sleep patterns and liver fibrosis in MASLD patients with higher dietary fiber intake, with moderate dietary fiber supplementation beneficial in mitigating poor sleep patterns associated with liver fibrosis. The similar findings were also found in patients aged < 50 years old, ≥ 50 years old, females, those with and without CVD groups, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. Particularly, dietary fiber intake also moderates the relationship between sleep patterns and liver fibrosis in the F4 stage (OR = 13.26, 95%CI: 4.08–43.11).ConclusionDietary fiber intake affects the relationship between sleep patterns and liver fibrosis in MASLD patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-024-03538-8.Keywords: Dietary fiber intake, Sleep, Liver fibrosis, Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, NHANES

Background

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, is a prevalent condition characterized by hepatic steatosis and can progress to more severe stages such as liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and even hepatocellular carcinoma [1]. MASLD has emerged as a global health concern, affecting 25% of the global population [1, 2]. The prevalence of MASLD is increasing yearly owing to changes in diet, lifestyle, and health conditions [3]. Liver fibrosis is the major determinant of adverse outcomes in patients with MASLD, and is related to 10-year cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk [4, 5]. Hence, early and accurate identification of patients with fibrosis is essential.

The progression of fatty liver disease involves chronic parenchymal injury, persistent activation of inflammatory response, dysregulation of lipid metabolism, changes in intestinal microbiota, and insulin resistance [6]. Sleep could affect the prognosis of liver disease by regulating a series of secretory signaling and metabolic processes. Sleep insufficiency could inhibit energy production and increase fat accumulation, which in turn induces metabolic disorders, increasing the risk of MASLD [7, 8]. Insufficient nocturnal sleep was associated with a higher risk of fibrosis in patients with MASLD [9]. Sleep disturbances are also related to increasing liver stiffness risk [10].

Increased dietary fiber intake could improve the damaged intestinal barrier, stimulate the secretion of sleep cytokines, inhibit the inflammatory pathway, contribute to regulating sleep disorders and alleviate liver fibrosis [11, 12]. A healthy lifestyle on sleep and diet is associated with lower MASLD risk and is inversely associated with liver stiffness and insulin resistance [13, 14]. Based on the studies above, we speculate that adequate dietary fiber intake could affect the association between sleep and liver fibrosis. The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) (www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes) is designed to assess the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States. The survey is distinctive in that it incorporates both physical examinations and interviews. Questions about demographics, health insurance status, dietary habits, acute and chronic medical issues, mental health, and prescription drug use are all included in the health interview. Exam components can change between survey cycles but typically include blood pressure, dental exams, vision, hearing, dermatology, fitness, balance and strength testing, respiratory testing, taste and smell, and body measurements (weight, height, skin folds, body composition scans). Hematology, organ and endocrine function (e.g., thyroid, kidney), environmental exposure, dietary biomarkers, metabolic and cardiovascular health, and infectious disease are a few examples of laboratory components.

The study aims to explore the moderating effect of dietary fiber intake on the association between sleep pattern and liver fibrosis in MASLD patients based on the NHANES database. The results of this study may contribute to the development of personalized approaches for MASLD management and show the importance of healthy lifestyle.

Methods

Study design and participants

Data of patients with MASLD were obtained from the NHANES database 2017–2020 for this population-based study. The NHANES study has been approved by the National Ethics Review Board for Health Statistics Research. The requirement of ethical approval for this was waived by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Shunyi Hospital, because the data was accessed from NHANES (a publicly available database). The need for written informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Shunyi Hospital due to the retrospective nature of the study. All methods were performed following the relevant guidelines and regulations.

MASLD was determined based on hepatic steatosis and metabolic dysfunction [15]. Individuals were considered to have hepatic steatosis with a controlled attenuation parameter > 248 dB/m [16, 17]. Metabolic dysregulation was defined as the presence of at least one of the following criteria: (1) body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m2 or waist circumference > 94 cm for men and > 80 cm for women (adjusted for race), (2) fasting blood glucose ≥ 5.6 mmol/L, 2 h postprandial blood glucose ≥ 7.8 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥ 5.7%, or a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes or current treatment for type 2 diabetes, (3) blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg or taking hypotensive drugs, (4) plasma triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL (1.70 mmol/L) or specific drug treatment, (5) plasma high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ≤ 1.0 mmol/L for male or ≤ 1.3 mmol/L for female, or treatment for elevated blood lipids [15]. MASLD patients were excluded with the following criteria: (1) age < 20 years old, (2) hepatitis B or hepatitis C infection, (3) missing data on sleep, (4) missing data on dietary fiber intake, (5) missing data on liver fibrosis, (6) with extreme energy intake (< 500 kcal or > 5000 kcal for female, and < 500 kcal or > 8000 kcal for male), (7) metabolic dysfunction and alcohol-associated liver disease (MetALD) or alcoholic liver disease (ALD), and (8) missing data on important covariates.

Sleep pattern assessment

The sleep pattern was determined according to sleep duration, sleep problems, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), day sleepiness, social jetlag, and sleep debt. Sleep duration was calculated based on two questions “Number of hours usually sleep on weekdays or workdays” and “Number of hours usually sleep on weekends or non-workdays” [18]. Sleep duration is classified as short (< 7 h/d), long (> 9 h/d), and regular (7–9 h/d) recommended by the National Sleep Foundation [19]. Patients would be considered sleep problems with a “yes” answer to the question “Ever told doctor had trouble sleeping”. The criteria for OSA were as follows: (1) snore ≥ 3 nights/week, (2) snort/stop breathing ≥ 3 nights/week, (3) feel excessively sleepy during the day 16–30 times per month despite sleeping ≥ 7 h per night on weekdays or work nights, and feel overly sleepy ≥ 4 times/day. Day sleepiness was defined as feeling excessively or overly sleepy during the day ≥ 5 times/month. Sleep debt refers to the disparity between the sleep duration that a person needs and the actual sleep duration, which is calculated based on the absolute difference between the free days and the average weekly sleep duration [20]. Social jet lag refers to a circadian rhythm disorder caused by a mismatch between the biological clock and the social clock, calculated from the absolute difference in the sleep midpoints (midpoint between the sleep time and the wake time) between workdays and free days [20]. A positive status for each element above was scored 1, otherwise with 0 score. The total score was the sum of six elements. Sleep patterns were categorized as healthy (total score 0), moderate (total score 1–2), and poor (total score 3–6) [21].

Dietary fiber intake assessment

Dietary fiber intake data were obtained using the first 24-hour dietary recall interview. The interview was conducted in a Mobile Examination Center with a standardized set of guidelines. In this study, the daily dietary fiber intakes were calculated as the average of fiber intake from foods and supplements, and divided into two groups according to median. There are no universally accepted criteria for categorizing dietary fiber intake, and median has been used in published literature [22, 23]. High dietary fiber intake defined as ≥ 15 gm/day, and low dietary fiber intake defined as < 15 gm/day.

Covariates

Factors included in the regression models to control the potential effects of confounding variables were the following: age, gender (male and female), race (non-Hispanic white, Non-Hispanic black, Mexican American, and others), education level (less than high school, high school, and above high school), CVD, and hypertension. CVD was defined by self-reported diagnosis and use of cardiovascular agents. Self-reported diagnosis of CVD was determined with a response “yes” to the question “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had angina or angina pectoris/heart attack/coronary heart disease/stroke/congestive heart failure” [24]. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 80 mmHg, or taking hypotensive drugs [25, 26].

Liver fibrosis assessment

Liver stiffness measurement (LSM) was the indicator of liver fibrosis and was measured by using vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE). FibroScan model 502 V2 Touch (Echosens, Paris, France) was used for VCTE, equipped with a medium (M) or extra-large (XL) probe. The M probe was used first unless the machine indicated that the XL probe shall be used. A median LSM ≥ 8.2 kPa was considered indicative of significant fibrosis [27, 28]. A median LSM ≥ 8.2 kPa was considered indicative of significant fibrosis (≥ F2), whereas values ≥ 9.7 kPa and 13.6 kPa were considered indicative of advanced fibrosis (F3), and cirrhosis (F4), respectively [28].

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using WTMECPRP, SDMVPSU, and SDMVSTRA. Continuous variables were presented as mean with standard error (S.E), while categorical variables were expressed as number and percentage (%). T-tests and χ2 tests were employed to examine continuous and categorical variables. Potential covariates were identified through weighted univariate logistic regression models, with results detailed in Table S1. The associations of sleep pattern, dietary fiber intake, with liver fibrosis were explored utilizing weighted univariate and multivariate logistic regression models. Additionally, the restricted cubic spline (RCS) method was applied to describe the nonlinear relationship. The association was further analyzed across subgroups defined by age, gender, CVD, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. We also assessed the moderating effect of dietary fiber intake on the association between sleep patterns and liver fibrosis. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were utilized to present the findings. All analyses were conducted with R version 4.2.3, and P < 0.05 was considered statistical significance.

Associations of sleep pattern, dietary fiber intake, with liver fibrosis in MASLD patients

The associations of sleep pattern, dietary fiber intake, with liver fibrosis in MASLD patients were shown in Table 2. After adjusting age, gender, race, and education level, poor sleep pattern was related to higher odds of liver fibrosis (OR = 3.68, 95%CI: 1.13–11.97), dietary fiber ≥ 15 gm/day was associated with lower liver fibrosis risk (OR = 0.52, 95%CI: 0.32–0.83). Similarly, after adjusting all covariates, the association between poor sleep pattern and higher liver fibrosis risk (OR = 3.23, 95%CI: 1.05–9.90) or the association of dietary fiber ≥ 15 gm/day with lower liver fibrosis risk (OR = 0.51, 95%CI: 0.32–0.83) remained existed in patients with MASLD. Figure 2 suggests that nonlinear relationship of dietary fiber intake, sleep pattern, with liver fibrosis in MASLD. Dietary fiber intake did not correlate significantly with sleep pattern in patients with MASLD (Spearman’s r = -0.028, P = 0.1678).

Moderating effect of dietary fiber intake in subgroups

In subgroups, the MASLD patients were grouped into dietary fiber intake < 15 gm/day and ≥ 15 gm/day groups based on the median dietary fiber intake. The associations of dietary fiber intake, sleep pattern, with liver fibrosis in diverse subgroups were shown in Table S2. In MASLD patients aged < 50 years old and ≥ 50 years old, poor (< 50 years, OR = 10.06, 95%CI: 2.30-44.02; ≥50 years, OR = 46.77, 95%CI: 3.20-683.29) sleep pattern was associated with higher odds of liver fibrosis with dietary fiber intake < 15 gm/day group. Similarly, the relationships between poor sleep patterns (OR = 10.19, 95%CI: 2.57–40.34) and higher liver fibrosis risk were also observed in females with dietary fiber intake < 15 gm/day group. In MASLD patients with (OR = 35.81, 95%CI: 2.85–45.06) and without (OR = 11.07, 95%CI: 2.73–44.82) CVD, having hypertension (OR = 13.09, 95%CI: 3.29–52.11), and having dyslipidemia (OR = 12.60, 95%CI: 3.19–49.70), poor sleep pattern was also related to higher odds of liver fibrosis under dietary fiber intake < 15 gm/day group (Table 4).

Associations of sleep pattern, dietary fiber intake, with severity of liver fibrosis in MASLD patients

After adjusting age, gender, race, educational level, hypertension, CVD, poor sleep pattern (OR = 5.57, 95%CI: 1.51–20.59) was linked to elevated odds of live fibrosis in F4 stage, and dietary fiber intake ≥ 15 gm/day (OR = 0.39, 95%CI: 0.19–0.77) was related to decreased odds of live fibrosis in F4 stage (Table 5). No statistical significance was observed between sleep pattern, dietary fiber intake, and liver fibrosis in F2 or F3 stage. As depicted in Table 6, similar to the analysis in liver fibrosis, dietary fiber intake also moderates the association between sleep pattern and liver fibrosis in the F4 stage (OR = 13.26, 95%CI: 4.08–43.11).

Discussion

Our study investigated the moderating effect of dietary fiber intake on the relationship between sleep patterns and liver fibrosis in MASLD patients. The findings showed the association between poor sleep patterns and higher odds of liver prognosis, as well as the relationship between dietary fiber intake ≥ 15 gm/day and lower odds of liver fibrosis. Furthermore, dietary fiber intake < 15 gm/day exacerbates the association between poor sleep patterns and liver fibrosis in MASLD patients. In particular, dietary fiber intake could moderate the association between poor sleep patterns and liver fibrosis in the F4 stage.

Similar to our findings, the associations between sleep patterns, dietary fiber intake, and liver fibrosis have been reported [29–31]. The association between OSA and liver fibrosis has been reported, and OSA could be a predictor of liver fibrosis in MASLD patients [30, 32]. Sleep disorder was related to increased liver stiffness in patients with metabolic comorbidities [29]. In diabetes patients with MASLD, insufficient nocturnal sleep was related to higher fibrosis risk [9]. Dietary fiber consumption has been linked to improvement in various metabolic diseases, including MASLD, through the modulation of gut microbiota. Li et al. [12] reported increasing dietary intake could prevent liver fibrosis. Indolepropionic acid, which mainly depends on dietary fiber, has a beneficial effect in the mitigation of MASLD [31]. Notably, we found the moderating effect of dietary fiber intake on the association of sleep patterns with liver fibrosis in MASLD patients. Healthy lifestyle modification with diet and others remain the core of management of MASLD [33]. Our findings indicated the importance of sleep and diet management in patients with MASLD.

The mechanism underlying the relationship between sleep patterns, dietary fiber intake, and liver fibrosis in MASLD patients is complex and likely involves multiple pathways. First, poor sleep pattern related to systematic inflammation, insulin resistance, and dysregulation of lipid metabolism, all of which are important implicated in the pathogenesis of liver fibrosis [34, 35]. Sleep disturbances could disrupt circadian rhythms, leading to alterations in liver metabolism and promoting liver injury and fibrosis [36]. Moreover, inadequate sleep was associated with changes in gut microbiota and increased intestinal permeability, which could contribute to liver inflammation and fibrosis [37]. Adequate dietary fiber intake promotes the growth of beneficial bacteria, which could produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) through fermentation [38, 39]. SCFAs have anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, which may have beneficial effects on liver health [40]. Dietary fiber, especially soluble fiber, has shown a beneficial influence on insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and obesity [41, 42].

The moderating effect of dietary fiber intake was found in females, which may be attributed to the hormonal differences that affect the metabolism and utilization of dietary fiber. The finding of moderation effect in both CVD and non-CVD subgroups suggests that the benefits of dietary fiber intake in reducing liver fibrosis risk are not exclusive to individuals with cardiovascular comorbidities but may be relevant to the general population as well. In hypertensive individuals, poor sleep can exacerbate inflammation and oxidative stress, which in turn may accelerate liver damage. Dietary fiber could improve gut health and modulate inflammatory responses, potentially mitigating these effects. Similarly, in dyslipidemia patients, disrupted sleep has been linked to disturbed lipid metabolism and increased fatty liver progression. Fiber intake may positively influence lipid profiles and reduce hepatic fat accumulation by enhancing lipid excretion and improving insulin sensitivity.

Our study suggested the importance of considering both sleep patterns and dietary fiber intake in the management of MASLD. Healthcare providers should assess patients’ sleep quality and dietary habits as part of routine clinical care for MASLD to identify individuals at higher risk of liver fibrosis. Promoting healthy sleep patterns, adequate dietary fiber intake, and a balanced diet rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and legumes may be integral components of lifestyle interventions aimed at reducing liver fibrosis risk in MASLD patients. Education and counseling on the benefits of adequate sleep and dietary fiber intake may empower patients to make positive lifestyle changes that support liver health and overall well-being.

Our study offers several notable strengths that contribute to the understanding of MASLD. Some studies indicate lower dietary fiber intake in MASLD patients, whereas others note no significant difference compared to non-MASLD individuals [42–45]. Our study is unique in its focus on the potential moderating effect of dietary fiber intake on the relationship between sleep patterns and liver fibrosis. This novel approach highlights the potential for nutritional interventions to improve health outcomes in this group, particularly for those with advanced fibrosis (F4 stage). Additionally, our research addresses the inconsistencies found in previous studies regarding sleep characteristics in NASLD patients by providing clear associations between poor sleep patterns and liver fibrosis. This comprehensive analysis is grounded in a robust dataset from the NHANES, enhancing the validity and generalizability of our findings.

Despite the valuable findings in our study, several limitations need to be considered. Firstly, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causality, and longitudinal studies are required to confirm the observed association. Secondly, the data on dietary fiber intake is based on self-reported, which may introduce recall bias. Objective measurements, such as dietary records or biomarkers, would strengthen the validity of the findings. Additionally, covariates unmeasured in the database could not be ruled. Lastly, the variability and long-term patterns of sleep disturbances could not be captured in our study. Future research with more comprehensive sleep assessments, including objective measures like polysomnography, would provide a more accurate representation of sleep quality.

Conclusion

Our study indicates that dietary fiber intake moderates the relationship between sleep patterns and liver fibrosis in patients with MASLD, especially in those with advanced fibrosis (F4 stage). The findings highlight the necessity of comprehensive lifestyle interventions that address both sleep quality and dietary habits in managing MASLD. Future longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to confirm these results and further explore the mechanisms behind these associations.
