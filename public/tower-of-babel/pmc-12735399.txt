Road Traffic Accidents in the Northern Border Region of Saudi Arabia: A Retrospective Cross-Sectional Study Using Secondary Data
Authors: Fatima Alnoor, Ebtesam Babeker, Ehssan Ahmed, Alaa Mustafa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Road traffic accidents (RTAs) are a global public health concern and a major concern in developing countries such as Saudi Arabia.Objective:To describe the characteristics, patterns, and contributing factors of RTAs in the Northern Border area of Saudi Arabia for the time period from 2016 to 2018.Methods:The data used for this retrospective cross-sectional study were obtained from the Saudi Open Data Platform. Variables included the frequency, type, cause, driver age, and license status of the accidents. The data were analyzed using SPSS v25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and descriptive statistics were calculated, and chi-square tests were used to determine significance (P < 0.05).Results:Out of 34,311 RTAs recorded, 96.81% were damage only, 2.31% were injury, and 0.88% were fatal (statistically significant). Speeding was the major cause (54.6% - 60.75%). Young drivers 18-30 represented the majority (52.98%). Most had an expired or no license, and a significant association was found between age group and accident outcome (P < 0.05).Conclusions:The high incidence of RTAs involving young drivers with expired or no license, the prevalence of speeding as a cause of accidents, and the global prevalence of RTAs emphasize the need for more effective enforcement of traffic law with targeted educational interventions.

INTRODUCTION

Road Traffic Accidents (RTAs) are the primary cause of death and injury of many innocent people and account for huge property damages worldwide, especially in rapidly developing countries. The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia is no exception to this global dilemma.[1] Globally, RTAs remain the leading cause of injury-related deaths, accounting for approximately 1.26 million deaths annually, or 20.7% of all injury-related fatalities, based on data from 2013 and 2014 (Ofosu et al.[2]; Saad et al.[3]; World Health Organization[4]; Saudi Gazette[5]; GBD[6]; Mansuri et al.[7]; Alshammari et al.[8]; El-Fetoh et al.[9]). While the majority of low-income countries have seen an increase in deaths from traffic accidents, almost all high-income countries have seen a decrease in death rates. The death rate in Africa is the highest (24.1 per 100,000 inhabitants), while the lowest rate is found in Europe (10.3 per 100,000 inhabitants). Middle-income countries have the highest rate of road traffic deaths, with 20 deaths per 100,000 inhabitants, accounting for 80% of all global road fatalities, despite owning only 52% of the world’s vehicles, according to WHO and GBD data from 2013.[46] In Saudi Arabia, RTA victims occupy approximately 20% of hospital beds, and 81% of hospital deaths are attributed to road traffic injuries, based on Ministry of Health statistics from the early 2000s and 2015 reports.[710] Over a period of 20 years (1993–2013), the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia reported approximately 86,000 fatalities and 611,000 injuries resulting from RTAs, with around 7% of these injuries leading to lifelong impairments, according to data published in 2013 and 2015.[57] According to estimates, the total number of active vehicles in Saudi Arabia continues to increase annually, contributing to higher accident exposure.[3] A study of self-reported incidents involving inexperienced male and female drivers has shown that distinct driving circumstances (day/night, etc.) influence the crash risk levels of distinct driver classes.[1112] An earlier study found no connection between inexperienced drivers’ collision frequency resulting from slippery road conditions and their driving confidence in those situations.[1113] Additionally, it has been shown that inexperienced drivers take more risks, overestimate their driving abilities, and see threats less holistically than experienced drivers.[1114] Errors in attention and visual searching, speeding, hazard awareness, and emergency maneuvers were revealed to be the main causes of non-fatal accidents.[1115] In Qatar, a 2012 study reported that drivers aged 25–34 years had the highest prevalence of RTIs (35%). Males were more likely to sustain injuries, and 27.9% of injured drivers reported being distracted by eating or drinking while driving (Bener, 2012).[16]

Previous research in urban locations such as Riyadh and Jeddah has found speeding, distracted driving (e.g., mobile phone use), and driver inexperience as major causes of RTAs, particularly among young males. Despite these national-level findings, there remains a significant gap in region-specific research, particularly in Saudi Arabia’s Northern Border region. This region includes distinct geographic and demographic characteristics that may influence the nature of RTAs, such as extensive highway systems, reduced population density, and limited public transportation options. As a result, findings from other areas of the Kingdom may not be applicable. This is one of the first retrospective cross-sectional studies that focuses exclusively on this area, as far as we are aware. Knowing the local dynamics of traffic accidents in the Northern Border can help develop more focused, efficient interventions that take into account the unique sociodemographic and infrastructure circumstances of the area. This study thus seeks to characterize the features of RTAs in the Northern Border region during the years 2016–2018 and to pinpoint important contributing elements such age, license status, and common behavioral violations. The results are meant to back up local and national initiatives aiming at enhancing road safety and lowering fatality and injury rates.

METHODS

Study design and setting

This is a retrospective cross-sectional study conducted in Saudi Arabia’s Northern Border region. The study examined road traffic accident (RTA) data obtained from the Saudi Arabia Open Data Platform in 2016, 2017, and 2018. The Northern Border region is located in northern Saudi Arabia and encompasses numerous cities.

Participants and source of data

All reported traffic accidents that occurred within the Northern Border region over the course of 3 years were included in the study.

Data on accident frequency, causes, types, driver characteristics (age and license status), and accident patterns were gathered from traffic department records on the Open Data Platform.

Variables

The primary variables studied were:

Type of accident (e.g., vehicle-vehicle, overturning, and pedestrian-related), cause of accident (e.g., speeding and use of mobile phone), driver age groups, driver license status, and pattern of accident outcome damage, injury, and death.

Statistical analysis

Collected data were coded and analyzed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA Version 25). Descriptive statistics for the qualitative and quantitative variables were used. The χ2-test was used as a test of significance, and differences were considered significant at a P value of 0.05 or less.[8]

DISCUSSION

This study was conducted in the northern borders of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. Data were collected from the Open Data Platform in Saudi Arabia.[17] The major purpose of this study is to determine the contributing factors to RTCs and RTIs in the northern borders, Saudi Arabia.

During the three-year period (2016-2018), a total of 34,311 damages, 819 injuries and 311 deaths took place with in northern borders. with a percentage of 96.81% damages, 2.31% injuries, and 0.88% deaths. According to a different study,[1] During the four-year period (1985-1988), a total of 8,346 accidents, 11,336 injuries and 936 fatalities took place within Jeddah city.

The data presented in the current study revealed that the highest percentage of accidents takes place on Thursdays. While the lowest percentage is on Fridays, it indicates that the average monthly accident rates ranged between 7.49 and 9.10 over the three years.

According to our analysis, the most common type of RTAs was a vehicle accident, accounting for 87.62% in 2016 of all incidents. Other popular categories were overturning–vehicle (5.73%), collisions vehicle–pedestrian (1.65%), out of the way (3.52%), a fire (0.89%), and other (1.48%). Another cross-sectional study including 407 drivers revealed that angle collisions (40.8%) and back collisions (19.9%) were the most common types of RTAs, followed by hitting a fixed object (13.3%); car crashes, however, only account for 8.3% of RTAs.[8]

Our study revealed that RTAs were frequent on average during 2016-2018 among persons aged between 18 and 30 years old, with a percentage of 52.98%, followed by 24.58% in the age group between 31 and 40 years old. According to a different study,[18] the age groups below 10 years old and 20–29 years old had the fewest and greatest traffic accidents, respectively. Other studies conducted in Lazio, Italy[19] and Mexico[20] that discovered youths were the most frequent victims of traffic accidents[18192122] corroborate our findings.

According to the causes of the accident, our study reported that speed is the most common cause of accidents during the period 2016-2018, as it ranged between 54.6% in 2016, 56.24% in 2017 and 60.75% in 2018, improper passing has decrease from 3.68% in 2016 to 2.96% in 2017 and 2.52% in 2018 and use of mobile phone has increased from 28.48% in 2016 to 30.63% in 2017 and decrease 14.99% in 2018. According to a different research, the most common accidents were parking violations (18.1%), speed limit violations (36.7%), and red light violations (13.4%).[16] Compared to a different research, the most frequent infractions involving vehicles were going above the speed limit (36.6%), parking infractions (11.1%), and red light violations (5.4%).[8] In their investigation, Barrimah et al.[23] discovered that as many as 43.11% of RTAs were caused by extremely high speed.

Our study estimated that the average of 6.14% does not have a license in 2016, 2.44% a license, and 91.42% license that had expired. Another study estimated that approximately one-fifth of drivers involved in accidents were unlicensed.[24]

By offering comprehensive data on RTAs in an area that had not received much attention before, the study closes a significant gap. The statistical reliability was improved by incorporating thousands of cases from three years in a row. The study’s conclusions are more credible because it used organized, officially reported accident data. Because the study is based on current traffic records, incomplete data entry or underreporting may have an impact. Data on the extent of injuries, the use of safety precautions (such as seat belts), and the medical results following an accident were not included in the dataset. Absence of environmental and road condition variables: There were no available variables known to affect the risk of accidents, such as lighting, weather, and road quality.

The results of the study are essential to the field of public health. The concentration of young, unlicensed, or improperly licensed drivers alongside the rampant speeding and distracted driving noted calls for:

Age-specific educational programs aimed at youths and novice drivers, enhanced traffic enforcement concerning speed restrictions and licensing adherence, improvement of integrated health, police, and traffic department surveillance and data systems dedicated to monitoring RTAs more proactively, and changes to the physical features of accident-prone locations aimed at reducing incidents.

This study assists in the formulation of regional traffic safety initiatives by highlighting the major contributing factors of RTAs in the Northern Border region while aiming to customize strategies to reduce accident rates and improve road safety outcomes tailored to the region.

CONCLUSION

The study, looking back at specific years, reveals that RTAs from 2016 to 2018 in the Northern Border region of Saudi Arabia were mainly due to avoidable human actions like speeding and distracted driving. Most of the accidents were also with younger male drivers within the age bracket of 18-30 who were found to be driving with an expired or any license and a significant portion of the sample under research had no license whatsoever.

The data provided reinforces the need for strategies aimed at fixing the underlying issues associated with RTAs in that particular region. Such strategies would include stricter implementation of traffic laws, especially where issuing a driver’s license is involved, and campaigns that advocate education about the use of the road for young drivers. Additionally, more advanced methods of collecting data as well as merging police and health records, would enhance the management of future surveillance and policymaking.

The primary risk factors and key trends presented in this work provide invaluable information to policymakers, public health officials, and traffic authorities to more precisely target the issues associated with the growing concerns of road safety in that particular area.
