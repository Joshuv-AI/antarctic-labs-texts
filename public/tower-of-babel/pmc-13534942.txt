Exploring the Regenerative Role of Exosomes as a Potential Therapy for Peripheral Nerve Injury: A Systematic Review
Authors: Anita Soraya Soetoko, Dwi Cahyani Ratna Sari, Arta Farmawati, Nur Arfian, Dhite Bayu Nugroho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExosomes are nano-sized extracellular vesicles loaded with bioactive cargo that mediate intercellular communication. Their emerging role in tissue regeneration has garnered increasing attention, especially in the context of peripheral nerve injury (PNI), which poses a significant therapeutic challenge. Although many studies have explored exosome-based therapies for PNI, the findings are often fragmented and lack a comprehensive synthesis. This systematic review aims to synthesize the current evidence and elucidate the molecular mechanisms through which exosomes contribute to neural regeneration. This systematic review, conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines, included 33 eligible studies identified through Scopus, ScienceDirect, PubMed, and Google Scholar. Exosomes derived from diverse cellular sources were assessed for their capacity to enhance repair outcomes. Reported cargo, including miRNAs and neurotrophic factors, has been shown to activate key signaling pathways that promote neurite outgrowth, modulate Schwann cell and immune responses, stimulate angiogenesis, and activate perineurial cells. By orchestrating these processes, exosomes emerge as a promising therapeutic strategy to enhance nerve regeneration and functional recovery.Keywords: Exosomes, Extracellular vesicles, Peripheral nerve injury, Nerve regeneration, Systematic review

INTRODUCTION

Peripheral nerves are complex anatomical structures comprised of sensory and motor axons surrounded by protective layers of connective tissue and blood vessels. Axons are ensheathed by Schwann cells (SC), which are essential for development, functional support, and regeneration (Grinsell and Keating, 2014; Oliveira et al., 2023). These nerves transmit sensory input from the body to the central nervous system (CNS) and deliver motor commands to target effectors. Damage to peripheral nerves can result in both sensory and motor dysfunctions, manifesting as hyperesthesia, allodynia, paresthesia, anesthesia, gait disturbances, and muscle atrophy (Fig. 1) (Park et al., 2025; Song et al, 2025a).

Peripheral nerve injury (PNI) is a common cause of such dysfunctions and carries significant clinical impact. Epidemiological data from the United States (2009-2018) identified an upward trend in PNI, with an incidence of 36.9 per 1,000,000 individuals per year (Li et al., 2021). Falls, motorcycle accidents, and sports-related trauma are among the most common etiological factors contributing to PNI, with adolescents being particularly vulnerable (Bekelis et al., 2015; Missios et al., 2014).

Although peripheral nerves possess intrinsic regenerative capacity, recovery is often incomplete, leaving patients with persistent deficits (Gordon, 2020; Namini et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024). Current therapies rarely achieve optimal functional recovery, prompting exploration of novel strategies. Among these, exosomes, nano-sized extracellular vesicles (EVs) secreted by most cell types, have emerged as promising candidates for regenerative therapy. Their cargo, including proteins, nucleic acids, microRNA (miRNA/miR), lipids, and trophic factors, is known to mediate intracellular communication (Park et al., 2023; Qing et al., 2018). Through the transfer of their cargo, exosomes mediate signaling pathways involved in axonal growth, immune response, and vascular regeneration (Li et al., 2024; Qing et al., 2018; Rahimian et al., 2024).

In light of these regenerative properties, exosomes are increasingly studied in PNI models. However, the findings remain scattered and lack a comprehensive synthesis. To address this gap, this systematic review aims to synthesize current evidence and elucidate the molecular mechanisms through which exosomes contribute to neural regeneration. The specific research question is: "What is the current evidence on the regenerative role of exosomes in facilitating peripheral nerve repair in preclinical models of PNI, and what molecular mechanisms underlie their therapeutic effects?" In particular, the review synthesizes evidence on (i) cellular sources and regenerative cargo; (ii) preparation strategies, including engineering and preconditioning; (iii) delivery strategies and nerve injury type; and (iv) mechanisms underlying their therapeutic effects.

LITERATURE SEARCH AND SELECTION

The review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines, with the protocol registered in the Open Science Framework (OSF; https://osf.io/a9u3w). Relevant studies were identified through Scopus, ScienceDirect, PubMed, and Google Scholar, covering publications up to December 2025 (Fig. 2).

Study selection of flow diagram according to PRISMA guidelines.

Search terms were derived from MeSH headings and combined with Boolean operators. Keywords included “exosome” OR “exosomes” OR “extracellular vesicles” OR “EVs” AND “peripheral nerve injuries” OR “peripheral neuropathies” OR “sciatic nerve injury” OR “nerve crush” OR “nerve transection.” In Google Scholar, results were sorted by relevance, and the first 300 records were screened (Haddaway et al., 2015). Forward and backward citation tracking was performed to ensure comprehensive coverage.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they investigated exosomes administration in PNI, were published in English, available in full text, and had final publication status. Reports of EVs ≤200 nm were included regardless of terminology (exosomes, sEVs, EVs), with stratification by original labels to minimize ambiguity (Table 1). For consistency, the term “exosomes” is used throughout the review, while original terminology is retained in tables.

Exclusion criteria comprised reviews, meta-analyses, case reports, studies without regenerative outcomes, those limited to in vitro data, and studies where exosomes were not the primary therapy. In vitro findings reported within included studies were extracted as supplementary mechanistic evidence and clearly distinguished from in vivo outcomes.

Exosomes were considered the primary intervention when evaluated as the active agent. Biomaterial scaffolds (conduits, hydrogels) were permitted only as passive carriers. Studies validating scaffolds as active therapies were excluded. For combination approaches, inclusion required a scaffold-only control group to isolate the exosomal effect. Study selection was performed independently by two reviewers using Rayyan.ai, with disagreements resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer.

Data extraction

Extracted variables included study characteristics (country, species, strain, sex, age), injury model (type, nerve, severity), intervention details (source, dose, route, timing, follow-up), and exosome characterization (isolation method, morphology, size, markers, cargo). Primary outcomes focused on indicators of nerve regeneration, including axonal regeneration and remyelination, and functional recovery (such as behavioral and electrophysiological measures). Secondary outcomes addressed mechanistic effects such as Schwann cell modulation, immune regulation, angiogenesis, and other processes documented in the studies.

Risk of bias assessment

Methodological quality of in vivo studies was assessed using SYRCLE’s risk of bias tool (10 domains adapted from the Cochrane framework; Hooijmans et al., 2014). Each domain was rated as low, high, or unclear risk (see Supplementary Table 2). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Reporting quality assessment

In vitro studies were not assessed for risk of bias. Reporting quality was instead evaluated according to MISEV 2023 guidelines (Welsh et al., 2024), focusing on exosome isolation, morphology, size, and marker validation. Studies were classified as complete or incomplete with respect to minimal characterization criteria, and disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

ANALYSIS AND PRESENTATION OF EXTRACTED DATA

A total of 33 studies were included. Most were conducted in China (n=26) and Taiwan (n=3), with single contributions from Chile, Germany, Korea, and Russia. All studies were preclinical in vivo experiments, predominantly using adult male Sprague-Dawley rats. The sciatic nerve was the main target, with crush injury applied most frequently (n=17), followed by transection (n=12), ligation (n=2), and chronic constriction injury (CCI) (n=2). Several studies also incorporated in vitro experiments with neuronal and non-neuronal cells to clarify exosomes activity under physiological and injury conditions.

Exosomes originated from diverse sources. The majority were derived from mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSC, n=18). Other sources included SC (n=3), skin precursor-derived SC (SKP-SC, n=3), SC-like cells (n=1), induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC, n=2), endothelial cells (EC, n=2), neural crest cells (NCC, n=2), olfactory ensheathing cells (OEC, n=1), and fibroblasts (n=1) (Table 1).

Terminology varied: 25 studies explicitly used “exosomes,” 6 referred to “EVs,” and 2 used “sEVs” in line with MISEV 2023 guidelines. Differences in labeling did not correspond to systematic differences in vesicle characterization. Instead, variability reflected adherence to MISEV 2023 reporting standards, particularly in morphology, size, and marker validation. Thus, heterogeneous terminology introduced some conceptual ambiguity but was not the main source of outcome variability.

Across all studies, exosomes consistently promoted regeneration and functional recovery in PNI models, regardless of terminology, source, or experimental design. Evidence is organized into four themes: (i) cellular sources and regenerative cargo, (ii) preparation strategies (engineering and preconditioning), (iii) delivery strategies and nerve injury type, and (iv) mechanisms underlying therapeutic effects.

Cellular sources and regenerative cargo

The regenerative potential of exosomes is closely tied to their cellular origin, reflecting the molecular and functional traits of the parent cells (Chen et al., 2025b). Across sources, exosomes consistently supported nerve regeneration and functional recovery. Most studies reported improvements in the sciatic functional index (SFI), while others documented enhanced electrophysiological parameters (e.g., nerve conduction velocity and compound muscle action potential (CMAP)), as well as modulation of pain responses.

Across MSC sources, exosomes consistently support peripheral nerve regeneration by enhancing neurite outgrowth, promoting SC repair phenotypes, and modulating inflammatory responses. These effects have been demonstrated in both in vivo and in vitro studies. Exosomes from human umbilical cord (hUCMSC) deliver growth factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor C (VEGF-C), fibroblast growth factor (FGF)-2, angiopoietin-2, and epidermal growth factor (EGF). Administration of these exosomes in nerve injury models reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β) while increasing anti-inflammatory cytokines and neurotrophic factors (IL-10, glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)). These changes were associated with pain relief and reduced inflammation in crush injury models (Hsu et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022; Shiue et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2023). Complementary in vitro studies showed that hUCMSC exosomes enhanced neurite length in primary neurons, promoted neurite outgrowth in PC12 cells, and improved SC proliferation and viability (Shiue et al., 2019; Tang et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2023). While hUCMSC-derived exosomes show translational promise, unresolved issues in large-scale production, cargo consistency, and regulatory pathways continue to pose barriers to clinical use (Cao et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025b).

Compared to hUCMSC, ADMSC-derived exosomes are distinguished by their enrichment in neurotrophic cargo such as GDNF, BDNF, nerve growth factor (NGF), and NT-3. In nerve injury models, administration increased neurotrophic factor expression and facilitated M2 macrophage polarization in facial nerve injury, indicating immunoregulatory activity (Xue et al., 2024). In vitro assays demonstrated enhanced neurite outgrowth in DRG and PC12 cultures and regulation of SC autophagy via miRNA-26b, reinforcing their supportive role in regeneration (Bucan et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2022; Yin et al., 2021).

Bone marrow MSC (BMMSC)-derived exosomes also contribute to repair, acting through multiple mechanisms. Administration in crush injury models upregulated regenerative markers such as VEGF-A, NGFR, PMP22, and S100b in L4-L6 DRG. These changes reflect angiogenesis, neuronal survival, remyelination, and Schwann cell activity. These in vivo findings aligned with in vitro evidence, where DRG cultures exposed to BMMSC-derived exosomes showed enhanced neurite outgrowth and SC repair phenotypes (Li et al., 2022b; Zhao et al., 2020). Immunomodulatory cargos further contributed: miR-181c-5p reduced IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and COX-2 in the spinal cord, with similar anti-inflammatory effects in LPS-induced microglia (Zhang et al., 2022), while TSG-6 promoted M2 macrophage polarization in injured tissue and increased IL-10 and TGF-β while reducing IL-1β and IL-6 in RAW264.7 cells (Li et al., 2022a). Beyond these well-studied sources, human placenta MSC (hPMSC)-derived exosomes demonstrated neuroprotective activity by reducing neuronal death. Meanwhile, human gingiva MSC (hGMSC) and muscle-derived stem cell (MDSC) exosomes consistently acted on SC and DRG cultures, enhancing proliferation, repair phenotypes, and axonal extension (Rao et al., 2019; Zeng et al., 2023b). Cargo miR-214 identified in MDSC exosomes reinforced SC repair phenotype and facilitated axon growth, highlighting a mechanistic basis for their regenerative effects (Zeng et al., 2023b).

Among MSC sources, hUCMSC and hPMSC offer translational advantages, including scalability, ethical acceptability, donor consistency, and low immunogenicity (Chen et al., 2025a, 2025b; de Laorden et al., 2025), whereas ADMSC and BMMSC sources are limited by invasive procurement and donor variability, thereby restricting scalability and reproducibility (Beane et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2020).

Another cellular source of exosomes investigated in PNI can be stratified into four categories: (i) primary SC, (ii) immortalized SC lines and modified SC such as SKP-SC and SCLC, (iii) reprogrammed sources, and (iv) other somatic cells such as EC, OEC, and fibroblasts. Primary SC are physiologically relevant, as they mirror the native repair environment in vivo. Following nerve injury, SC adopts a repair phenotype and releases exosomes enriched in molecules such as p75NTR and miRNA-21, which promote neurite outgrowth (López-Leal et al., 2020; Lopez-Verrilli et al., 2013). In vitro studies further demonstrated that SC-derived exosomes stimulate intraneural revascularization, an effect linked to miR-21-5p cargo (Sun et al., 2024). These findings highlight their intrinsic regenerative potential, although clinical application remains constrained by invasive procurement and high cost (Ghosh and Pearse, 2023).

In addition to primary SC, other SC-based sources have also been explored. Exosomes derived from immortalized SC lines have been associated with modulation of inflammatory responses in both in vivo and in vitro studies, mediated by cargo such as miR-146a-5p, which supports axonal and myelin regeneration by shaping a microenvironment conducive to growth (Sun et al., 2023). In a similar vein, SKP-SC and SCLC exosomes have been implicated in nerve repair through angiogenic responses. For SKP-SC exosomes, this activity is linked to miRNA-30a-5p cargo that enhances endothelial proliferation; consistent with this, in vitro assays using human umbilical vein endothelial cells (hUVEC) cultures showed increased proliferation, migration, and tube formation (Shen et al., 2025). SCLC exosomes demonstrated comparable angiogenic activity. In addition, they promoted SC repair phenotypes in vitro, though their cargo profiles remain less well defined (Hu et al., 2023). Exosomes from SC lines and modified SC sources offer strengths in accessibility and reproducibility. However, their translational application is constrained by variability across differentiation protocols and incomplete recapitulation of native SC heterogeneity, which ultimately limit scalability and clinical yield (Chen et al., 2025b; Huang et al., 2023b).

Exosomes derived from reprogrammed cells, such as iPSC and NCC from urine-iPSC, have broadened the landscape of regenerative approaches. iPSC-derived exosomes promote SC proliferation, reinforcing the repair phenotype in injured nerves (Aldali et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2021). Beyond proliferation, they also promote angiogenesis and upregulate BDNF expression, which together enhance nerve regeneration and functional recovery (Aldali et al., 2025).

Lineage-specific NCC exosomes further diversify this regenerative profile. For instance, NCC-derived exosomes from urine iPSC are enriched in NGF, which reduces neuronal apoptosis, promotes neuronal proliferation, and suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokines. Likewise, hfNCC-derived exosomes carry miR-21-5p, activating perineurial cells and strengthening blood-nerve barrier integrity, thereby creating a supportive environment for axonal growth (Huo et al., 2026).

Reprogrammed cell sources can be generated from fibroblasts, peripheral blood mononuclear cells, and urine stem cells, making them relatively accessible and expanding donor availability (Aldali et al., 2025; Huo et al., 2026; Pan et al., 2021; Yeo et al., 2025). These sources are potentially scalable and display high stability. Nevertheless, clinical translation remains challenging due to complex isolation procedures with low efficiency, the high cost and technical demands of reprogramming, and issues of reproducibility, all of which limit large-scale application and yield (Zhang et al., 2025a).

Other sources such as EC, OEC, and fibroblasts have also been investigated. Although not stem cells, OEC and EC retain specialized repair functions that support axonal regeneration, remyelination, and functional recovery (Huang et al., 2023a, 2024; Xia et al., 2019). EC-derived exosomes are enriched in miR-199-5p, which exerts anti-apoptotic effects in injured nerves and enhances repair phenotypes when incubated with the RSC96 cell line (Huang et al., 2023a).

Fibroblast-derived exosomes are highly accessible and scalable due to ease of procurement and robust expansion. They contain protein cargo such as TFAP2C, which has been implicated in axon elongation (Zhao et al., 2023). Nevertheless, current evidence suggests their regenerative potential remains limited by the terminally differentiated nature of fibroblasts, which restricts cargo diversity and overall functional breadth (Chen et al., 2025b). Preparation strategies (engineering & preconditioning)

Preparation of exosomes is critical for therapeutic efficacy, as isolation and characterization directly influence purity, cargo integrity, and reproducibility (Zhang et al., 2020). Ultracentrifugation was the most common method, suitable for large volumes but limited by time, equipment demands, and protein contamination (Malik et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a). To improve purity, some studies combined filtration with ultracentrifugation, reducing contaminants but risking vesicle loss and scalability issues (Yuan et al., 2023). Commercial kits offered simpler workflows and higher yields, though residual reagents, cost, and reproducibility remain concerns (Mercadal et al., 2020; Serrano-Pertierra et al., 2019).

Beyond isolation, therapeutic efficacy can be optimized through engineering and preconditioning strategies. Engineering approaches enrich exosomal cargo with neurotrophic factors or regulatory miRNA, consistently amplifying regenerative efficacy. Several studies highlight the benefits of neurotrophic factor enrichment. NT-3-engineered ADMSC exosomes enhanced nerve regeneration, with NT-3 levels in transected nerves comparable to autografts (Yang et al., 2021). Similarly, exosomes from NCC engineered with NGF reduced neuronal apoptosis, inflammation and activated ERK/AKT signaling, resulting in enhanced remyelination (Yeo et al., 2025).

MiRNA-based engineering has also yielded promising outcomes. For instance, enrichment with miR-218 increased SC proliferation, thereby supporting remyelination and axonal regeneration (Wang et al., 2022). BMMSC-derived exosomes carrying miR-181c-5p alleviated CCI-induced allodynia through modulation of spinal cord inflammation (Zhang et al., 2022), while MDSC-derived exosomes engineered with miR-214 further improved regenerative outcomes through activation of JAK2/STAT3 (Zeng et al., 2023b). These examples illustrate the versatility of miRNA cargo in modulating both neuroregenerative and immunomodulatory pathways. Engineering has also been leveraged to achieve dual neurovascular benefits as EC-derived exosomes overexpressing netrin-1 not only accelerated axonal and myelin regeneration but also promoted angiogenesis (Huang et al., 2024).

Engineering stabilizes cargo and improves targeting, strengthening translational potential (Chen et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2023a). Certain loading methods, such as electroporation or freeze-thaw, may compromise vesicle integrity or lead to inconsistent in vivo delivery (Chen et al., 2024; Luan et al., 2017). Thus, while engineering represents a promising avenue to improve exosomes therapy, optimization of loading techniques remains essential for reliable clinical translation.

Alongside engineering, preconditioning strategies aim to enhance exosome efficacy by exposing source cells to defined stimuli, thereby inducing adaptive changes in cargo composition (Song et al., 2025b). Hypoxia preconditioning has been related to improved regenerative outcomes. For example, exosomes from hypoxia-treated SC enriched with miR-21-5p promoted superior functional recovery compared to non-preconditioned exosomes (Sun et al., 2024). Similarly, hypoxia-preconditioned hUMSC exosomes reduced cell death and preserved axonal integrity, leading to improved pain responses (Zhu et al., 2023). Other stimuli also showed benefits. Hypocapnic-preconditioning enhanced cargo delivery efficiency in ADMSC-derived exosomes enriched with miR-218 (Wang et al., 2022). LPS-treated BMMSC exosomes increased TSG-6 content, facilitating nerve recovery and reducing muscle atrophy (Li et al., 2022a), while fibroblast-derived exosomes preconditioned with chitosan oligosaccharide carried TFAP2C and actively promoted axonal growth (Zhao et al., 2023).

However, preconditioning does not uniformly yield superior outcomes. FK506-treated ADMSC exosomes showed higher neurotrophic factor expression but no clear advantage over unmodified exosomes (Rau et al., 2021). Moreover, preconditioning may provoke off-target effects, including inflammatory signaling (Song et al., 2025b). Hypoxia-treated hUCMSC exosomes increased IL-1β and TNF-α levels in injured tissue, even though they still improved neuropathic pain (Zhu et al., 2023). These findings highlight the dual nature of preconditioning: it can potentiate therapeutic outcomes but also introduce risks that require careful refinement.

In summary, engineered exosomes consistently enhance therapeutic efficacy through precise cargo modification, while preconditioning yields more variable outcomes by leveraging physiological stimuli. Their relative effectiveness appears context dependent, and direct comparative studies are needed to clarify translational value.

Delivery strategies and nerve injury type

Optimizing exosomes therapy requires careful selection of the delivery route, which can related to the type of nerve injury. In the reviewed studies, PNI models were categorized according to Seddon’s classification. Axonotmesis injuries (crush, ligation, CCI) disrupt axons and myelin but preserve the epineurial sheath, providing a natural guidance channel for regrowth. Neurotmesis injuries (transection) completely sever nerve continuity, eliminating intrinsic guidance and necessitating supportive strategies such as grafts or conduits (Hussain et al., 2020; Sumarwoto et al., 2021).

In axonotmesis models, local administration was most common, typically via direct injection into the injured nerve. This ensured high exosome concentrations at the lesion site, with hUCMSC- and iPSC-derived exosomes showing safety and no systemic toxicity (Aldali et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2024). However, the invasive nature of direct injection limits repeated dosing and reduces practicality for long-term therapy. Other local methods, such as spray application or embedding in collagen or hydrogel matrices, were less frequently reported. Systemic routes, including intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous, and intrathecal injection, were explored but used less often than local delivery.

In neurotmesis models, scaffold and conduit systems were employed to provide mechanical support and sustained release. Materials included alginate hydrogels, silicone with Matrigel, acellular nerve grafts, and conduits such as chitosan, polydopamine-coated chitosan, and chitin. Exosome incorporation consistently enhanced efficacy compared with biomaterials alone, indicating a synergistic effect.

Alginate hydrogels improved local retention and enabled controlled release, with matrix stiffness modulating release kinetics (Liu et al., 2022). Chitosan conduits coated with nanosized polydopamine achieved sustained release and improved outcomes in transection models (Li et al., 2022b). Exosomes from hfNCC delivered via silicone conduits and hUCMSC exosomes delivered through PLGA/collagen/HA constructs prevented muscle atrophy and promoted axonal regeneration, remyelination, and angiogenesis, with some results comparable to autografting (Huo et al., 2026; Tang et al., 2024). Although autografting remains the gold standard, its clinical use is constrained by donor scarcity, neuroma formation, donor site morbidity, mismatch in nerve diameter, and limited availability for long defects (Dodla et al., 2019). Exosome-based implantation strategies therefore represent a promising alternative, though they are still at the preclinical stage and require further validation.

In addition to local and scaffold-based strategies, systemic administration has also been explored. Intravenous delivery in combination with scaffold or conduit implantation allows repeated dosing due to ease of access (Wang et al., 2022), whereas direct incorporation of exosomes into scaffolds provides more targeted exposure at the injury site and reduces off-target risks. However, no head-to-head studies have compared systemic versus scaffold-based administration using the same exosome source, underscoring the need for standardized comparative designs.

Beyond the delivery route, dosing regimen, timing, and follow-up critically shaped reported efficacy. These parameters varied widely, with timing inconsistently reported and nearly half of the studies omitting it altogether. Follow-up intervals also differed, obscuring recovery timelines. Notably, accelerated regeneration was consistently observed, with some axonotmesis models reporting axonal growth as early as day 4 (Lopez-Verrilli et al., 2013; Wu et al., 2020), while in neurotmesis models the earliest regrowth was reported around day 14 (Huo et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2023). Such heterogeneity complicates synthesis, as regenerative events may have occurred outside observed timepoints.

From a translational perspective, each delivery route offers distinct advantages and limitations. Local injection achieves high concentrations at the lesion but is invasive and unsuitable for repeated dosing. Systemic routes expand applicability but face clearance issues. Intrathecal delivery provides direct CNS access but requires technical expertise. Scaffold and conduit systems enable controlled release, though biomaterial safety and manufacturing complexity remain concerns (Chen et al., 2025b; Dong and Lv, 2016; Farahzadi et al., 2024; Gurung et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022).

Mechanisms underlying therapeutic effects

The therapeutic effects of exosomes in PNI are mediated through interactions with distinct cellular targets and activation of diverse signaling pathways (Fig. 3).

Neuron

Neurite outgrowth consistently emerges as a hallmark effect, facilitated by the upregulation of neurotrophic factors such as GDNF, BDNF, and NGF, together with growth-related factors including IGF-1 and FGF-1 in DRG neurons and motoneurons (Bucan et al., 2019; Hsu et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2020; Yeo et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2023b; Zhao et al., 2023).

Canonical PI3K/AKT signaling is frequently implicated, although downstream routes vary depending on exosomes' origin and cargo. Representative studies illustrate this diversity: SKP-SC-derived exosomes activate AKT/mTOR/p70s6K in injury-conditioned motoneurons and transected axons (Wu et al., 2020); Netrin-1-overexpressing EC-derived exosomes activate PI3K/AKT in DRG neurons (Huang et al., 2024); NGF-overexpressing NCC-derived exosomes activate ERK and AKT in SH-SY5Y cells under oxidative stress (Yeo et al., 2025); and fibroblast-derived exosomes preconditioned with chitosan oligosaccharide deliver TFAP2C cargo that suppresses miR-312-5p activity in DRG neurons, thereby activating CAMKK1/AKT signaling (Zhao et al., 2023).

Apart from AKT-related cascades, MDSC-derived exosomes promote JAK2/STAT3 activation through PTEN suppression mediated by miR-214 (Zeng et al., 2023b). Another mechanism involves cytoskeletal regulation, where SC-derived exosomes inhibit RhoA in DRG neurons, thereby improving growth cone dynamics and supporting neurite elongation (Lopez-Verrilli et al., 2013).

Schwann cell

In addition to their neuronal effects, exosomes also modulate SC activity. Several studies demonstrate that exosomes enhance the SC repair phenotype, characterized by increased proliferation, migration, and upregulation of neurotrophic factors (e.g., NGF, BDNF, GDNF, CNTF, VEGF), immune-related cytokines, and myelin regulators, a profile that is essential for promoting functional recovery after nerve injury (Hu et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2023a; Li et al., 2022a; Liao et al., 2024). Mechanistically, iPSC-derived exosomes activate PI3K/AKT and focal adhesion pathways to promote SC proliferation (Aldali et al., 2025), while hUVEC-derived exosomes exert similar effects via miR-199-5p, which suppresses PTEN and enhances PI3K/AKT signaling (Huang et al., 2023a). Complementing proliferative signaling, ADMSC-derived exosomes carrying miRNA-26b attenuate autophagy by targeting KPNA 2, thereby reducing autophagy-related proteins and increasing p62 expression, which supports SC survival after injury (Yin et al., 2021). Under injury-like conditions such as radiation exposure, hypoxia-preconditioned hUCMSC-derived exosomes upregulate ZEB2, ERK1/2, and c-JUN pathways, thereby preserving SC growth, proliferation, and viability (Zhu et al., 2023).

Immune cells

In addition to modulating SC activity, exosomes exert immunomodulatory effects on macrophages and microglia. In LPS-stimulated microglia, BMMSC-derived exosomes enriched in miR-181c-5p attenuate activation by reducing IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and COX-2 (Zhang et al., 2022). In a complementary manner, NCCs overexpressing NGF modulate inflammatory responses in LPS-induced BV2 microglial-like cells by influencing NF-κB signaling, which is associated with reduced TNF-α, Iba-1, and cleaved caspase-3 (Yeo et al., 2025).

Exosomes from diverse sources further promote macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, shifting the cytokine profile toward an anti-inflammatory state characterized by elevated IL-10 and TGF-β alongside reduced IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α (Li et al., 2022a; Liu et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2023; Xue et al., 2024). Mechanistically, hADMSC-derived exosomes inhibit the p38MAPK/NF-κB pathway (Xue et al., 2024); LPS-preconditioned BMMSC-derived exosomes deliver TSG-6, which inactivates NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling (Li et al., 2022a); and SC-derived exosomes carrying miR-146a-5p suppress TRAF6/NF-κB pathway (Sun et al., 2023). these findings highlight convergent regulation of inflammatory pathways that drive M2 polarization, enhance anti-inflammatory cytokine production, and reduce pro-inflammatory responses, thereby establishing a microenvironment conducive to axonal growth and regeneration.

Endothelial cell

Furthermore, exosomes also contribute to vascular remodeling by stimulating angiogenesis. Newly formed vessels supply metabolic and trophic support essential for axonal growth, with endothelial cells playing a pivotal role in this process (Malekzadeh et al., 2025). Exosomes derived from SKP-SC and hypoxia-preconditioned SC enhance endothelial function by promoting proliferation, migration, and tube formation, while simultaneously inducing a metabolic shift toward glycolysis and reducing oxidative phosphorylation. Mechanistic studies have shown that SKP-SC-derived exosomes carrying miRNA-30a-5p downregulate ANGPT2 and LIF (Shen et al., 2025), whereas hypoxia-SC-derived exosomes deliver miR-21-5p, which promotes glycolysis via suppression of von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) and upregulation of hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF-1α), and attenuates oxidative phosphorylation through downregulation of pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 alpha (PDH E1α) (Sun et al., 2024). These findings highlight that exosomes orchestrate both phenotypic and metabolic reprogramming of endothelial cells, thereby facilitating angiogenesis and contributing to nerve repair.

Perineural cell

More recently, exosomes have been shown to modulate perineurial glia, which are increasingly recognized as important yet underexplored contributors to peripheral nerve regeneration. These cells respond to injury by bridging nerve gaps and coordinating debris clearance, processes essential for axonal regrowth (Lewis and Kucenas, 2014; Petrova and Kolos, 2022). Recent experimental studies using cultured perineurial cells demonstrate that exosomes can directly regulate their activity. For instance, hfNCC-derived exosomes carrying miR-21-5p suppress Smad7, thereby inhibiting TGF-β/mothers against decapentaplegic homolog (SMAD) signaling and upregulating hyaluronan synthase 2 (HAS2) (Huo et al., 2026). This cascade promotes proliferation, migration, tube formation, and tight junction protein expression, ultimately reinforcing blood-nerve barrier integrity and enhancing the regenerative environment.

Taken together, current preclinical evidence demonstrates that exosomes exert multifaceted therapeutic effects in peripheral nerve regeneration. They promote neurite outgrowth, modulate SC activity, regulate immune responses, stimulate angiogenesis, and activate perineurial cells. This diversity of cellular targets underscores the broad biological processes that exosomes can harness to foster nerve repair.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias assessment using the SYRCLE’s tool revealed systematic methodological limitations that affect the reliability and generalizability of the included studies (Fig. 4). The most consequential finding was the near-universal unclear risk of bias in random housing (100% unclear). Explicit reporting of housing randomization is essential in preclinical nerve regeneration research, as non-random housing introduces environmental variability that can alter neuroinflammatory responses, SC activation, and cytokine levels. Because regenerative outcomes such as axonal growth and remyelination are sensitive to these factors, failure to report housing randomization introduces confounding that cannot be corrected post hoc. Consequently, effect sizes favoring exosome treatment may be inflated by uncontrolled housing effects.

Proportion of SYRCLE’s RoB assessments of included studies.

Risk of bias assessment further revealed inadequate reporting of blinding. Caregiver blinding was universally unclear (100%), and outcome assessor blinding was unclear in 94% of studies. Transparent blinding of both caregivers and assessors is essential to minimize performance and detection bias in preclinical nerve regeneration research. Outcome ascertainment, particularly behavioral measures such as SFI and pain response, is highly susceptible to observer bias. Unblinded assessors systematically overestimate treatment effects, with inflation of up to 30% reported in animal studies (van der Worp et al., 2010; Watzlawick et al., 2019). The lack of blinding thus introduces performance and detection bias that could account for a meaningful fraction of the positive outcomes reported.

Sequence generation was also inadequately described. Only 6% of studies provided sufficient detail, while the majority merely stated that “random allocation was performed” without specifying the method. Notably, one study explicitly allocated animals according to body weight, constituting a high risk of bias by preferentially assigning healthier subjects to the treatment arm. Inadequate reporting or biased allocation undermines confidence in group comparability and weakens causal inference (O'Connor and Sargeant, 2014).

Incomplete outcome data carried 70% unclear and 21% high risk of bias, indicating widespread failure to account for all animals included in experiments. This attrition bias, if non-random (e.g., excluding animals with poor outcomes), would systematically overestimate treatment efficacy (Holman et al., 2016). By contrast, selective outcome reporting was generally adequate (91% low risk), suggesting that most studies adhered to their prespecified outcomes. However, this strength does not offset the pervasive weaknesses in other domains.

These methodological gaps suggest that the apparent consistency of positive findings may reflect shared reporting biases rather than uniformly robust therapeutic effects. Reported outcomes should therefore be interpreted as preliminary evidence, requiring confirmation in well-designed studies that explicitly address SYRCLE domains such as housing randomization, caregiver and assessor blinding, and complete reporting of all allocated animals. Meta-analytic pooling of effect sizes would be premature until these issues are resolved.

Reporting quality assessment

Most studies described exosomes as round or cup-shaped (31 studies), with diameters ranging from 40 to 200 nm. Positive markers commonly reported included TSG101, CD9, CD81, CD63, HSP70, HSP90, flotillin-1, and Alix, while negative markers included calnexin, GM130, Grp94, Rab5, Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase, vimentin, and cytochrome c (Supplementary Table 1). Seventeen studies fulfilled minimum MISEV 2023 characterization criteria, reporting isolation method, morphology, size, and both positive and negative markers. Thirteen studies showed partial compliance, most often due to missing negative marker validation, and in some cases omission of size characterization.

Compliance with MISEV 2023 strengthened the reliability of evidence, as studies meeting minimum requirements provided more robust insights into exosome cargo and mechanisms, while incomplete reporting limited comparability and contributed to heterogeneity. Standardized reporting in line with MISEV 2023 is therefore essential to reduce heterogeneity, enhance reproducibility, and improve translational potential.

Limitations

Despite encouraging preclinical findings, several limitations constrain the current evidence base. First, terminological inconsistency remains across studies. According to MISEV2023 guidelines, the term "exosomes" should be reserved for vesicles of endosomal origin with biogenetic evidence, whereas vesicles smaller than 200 nm lacking such evidence are more appropriately classified as sEVs. Nevertheless, many PNI studies label vesicles up to 200 nm as exosomes without biogenetic evidence, creating conceptual ambiguity. To address this, we stratified studies by terminology and adopted “exosomes” as a general descriptor, while retaining original labels in tables for transparency.

Methodological variability in isolation and characterization further complicates cross-study comparisons. Partial compliance to MISEV2023 recommendations obscures whether observed effects are attributable to exosomes themselves or to co-isolated particles. Sample sizes were generally small (n=3 for in vitro assays; 6-8 animals per group in vivo), restricting statistical power and generalizability. Comparative analyses across different cellular sources remain limited. In addition, variability in injury models, delivery routes, and outcome measures introduces further heterogeneity, restricting the ability to draw standardized conclusions or to identify the most effective exosome type.

Future directions and challenges

Future research should prioritize standardized protocols for exosome isolation and characterization, alongside comparative evaluation of engineering versus preconditioning strategies. Optimization of delivery systems that balance therapeutic efficacy with clinical practicality will be critical. Rigorous safety and biodistribution studies in clinically relevant large-animal models are needed to complement rodent data and strengthen translational relevance.

Translational gaps must also be addressed. Current evidence derives predominantly from rodent models, which do not fully capture the complexity of human peripheral nerve injury. Progression toward clinical application will require dose-finding studies and early-phase human safety trials to define therapeutic windows, immunogenicity, and regulatory pathways. Until such studies are conducted, clinical feasibility remains uncertain.

Expanding comparative analyses across different cellular sources will help identify the most promising candidates for clinical application. In addition, harmonization of terminology (exosomes, EVs, and sEV) and outcome measures will reduce ambiguity and enable meaningful cross-study comparisons. Collectively, these efforts will be essential to consolidate the evidence base and enable the safe, effective, and standardized clinical translation of exosome-based therapies for PNI.

CONCLUSIONS

Exosomes exert multifaceted regenerative effects in PNI through cellular, immunological, and vascular mechanisms that support nerve repair. These varied effects highlight exosomes as versatile mediators of regeneration. In cases of axonotmesis, where spontaneous recovery is possible, exosomes may serve as supportive adjuncts to enhance and accelerate endogenous regeneration. In contrast, for neurotmesis, where autografting is still the clinical gold standard, exosome-based strategies represent a promising alternative to overcome donor scarcity and morbidity. However, methodological heterogeneity, limited comparative analyses, and incomplete safety data underline the gap between experimental promise and clinical translation. Addressing these challenges through standardized protocols, innovative engineering and preconditioning strategies, optimized delivery systems, and rigorous translational research will be essential to transform exosome-based therapies into clinically feasible applications for PNI.
