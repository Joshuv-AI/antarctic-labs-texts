Revascularization for Acute Limb Ischemia
Authors: Kota Shukuzawa, Hirotsugu Ozawa, Takao Ohki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute limb ischemia (ALI) is a sudden decrease in lower limb arterial perfusion that poses a threat not only to limb viability but alsoto life and requires urgent evaluation and management. The etiology of ALI is broadly divided into embolism and thrombosis, along with various comorbidities. Severity and treatment strategy should be determined based on physical and image findings. Revascularization can be performed surgically (thromboembolectomy or bypass), endovascularly, or through a hybrid procedure. In all cases, it is important to conduct intraoperative angiography and perform the necessary additional procedures. A prompt response to myonephropathic metabolic syndrome or compartment syndrome during or after revascularization is also essential. ALI is a serious condition that requires emergency treatment, and it is important to be familiar with all treatment options, including surgical and endovascular treatments, fasciotomy, and primary limb amputation, and to promptly provide the best available initial treatment at each facility. Introduction

Acute limb ischemia (ALI) is a condition characterized by a sudden decrease in arterial blood flow to a limb leading to ischemia.1) It is defined as limb ischemia that occurs within 2 weeks of acute onset.

If appropriate treatment is not provided, ALI can lead to major limb amputation and poor survival prognosis, and high rates of limb loss and mortality are still reported. Because mortality rates of 15%–20% have been reported due to comorbid cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, as well as ischemia–reperfusion injury, ALI requires careful systemic management in addition to local treatment.2) This study outlines the etiology, diagnosis, revascularization-centered treatment, and postoperative management of ALI.

I. Etiology

The cause of ALI is broadly classified as embolism and thrombosis. According to the 2018 vascular surgery annual report based on the National Clinical Database, embolism accounted for 40%, thrombosis for 50%, and other causes (including traumatic and iatrogenic) for 10%, with 78% of occlusions occurring distal to the abdominal aorta.3) Embolism is classified into cardiogenic and non-cardiogenic types.

Most emboli are cardiogenic and are associated with atrial fibrillation, whereas other cardiogenic causes include left ventricular thrombus following myocardial infarction and cardiac tumors. Non-cardiogenic causes include atheroembolism from atherosclerotic lesions and mural thrombi from aortic or popliteal artery aneurysms. In cases without atherosclerotic changes, emboli are often trapped at arterial bifurcations, and such cases present with severe ischemia and rapid progression due to poor development of collateral circulation. Thrombosis results from preexisting local lesions, such as atherosclerosis, and leads to acute occlusion. Although collateral circulation from preexisting disease often results in a more gradual time course than embolism, extensive lesions complicate treatment, and the overall prognosis may be poor. Acute occlusion due to popliteal artery aneurysms or graft thrombosis lacks collateral circulation and follows a rapid clinical course that requires careful attention.

II. Diagnosis

The physical findings characteristic of ALI are the 5 Ps: pain, pulselessness, pallor, paresthesia, and paralysis. Recently, prostration has been added, making them the 6 Ps. ALI generally progresses to irreversible changes in the order of nerves, muscles, and skin within 4–6 h after onset. Sensory loss, contracture, and irreversible purpura appear as ischemia progresses. Drop foot, which is caused by peroneal nerve palsy, is often an early manifestation of motor paralysis and indicates an urgent condition. Diagnosis includes medical history taking, peripheral arterial pulse palpation, Doppler examination, and neurological evaluation. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) is essential not only to establish the diagnosis but also to determine the treatment strategy, including assessment of the extent of occlusion, the presence of atherosclerosis, the embolic source, and differentiation from embolism to other organs. CT imaging should include not only the affected limb but also the head, chest, abdomen, and pelvis to identify embolic sources and other affected sites. When contrast CT cannot be performed because of severe renal dysfunction or allergy, non-contrast CT should be performed to assess arterial calcification and the presence of aneurysms that may serve as embolic sources (Fig. 1). Electrocardiography should be performed to identify atrial fibrillation, and echocardiography should be considered to evaluate for intracardiac thrombus if possible. Blood tests should include routine hematology, biochemistry, and coagulation studies, as well as evaluation for congenital coagulation disorders, such as protein C/S deficiency, anticardiolipin antibodies, and AT-III deficiency. Blood and urine myoglobin levels, serum creatine kinase (CK), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), potassium, and blood gas analysis, including acidosis and lactate levels, are important markers for assessing ischemic severity and predicting the development of myonephropathic metabolic syndrome (MNMS).

III. Treatment

Once ALI is diagnosed, unfractionated heparin (70–100 units/kg) should be administered intravenously as soon as possible to prevent proximal and distal thrombus propagation. Argatroban should be considered in patients diagnosed with heparin-induced thrombocytopenia.4) Systemic thrombolytic therapy is not recommended. The treatment strategy should then be determined according to the disease severity. Revascularization is required within 6 h for Rutherford category IIa and IIb and within 24 h for category I.5) Revascularization options for ALI include surgical revascularization (thromboembolectomy or bypass surgery), endovascular treatment (EVT), and hybrid treatment. Primary limb amputation should be considered for Rutherford category III, which indicates irreversible ischemia. In cases of extensive ischemia caused by high-level occlusion distal to the iliac arteries, the risk of severe MNMS is high, and life-saving primary amputation should be considered.

1. Surgical revascularization

Using the Fogarty catheter developed by Thomas Fogarty in 1960s,6) thromboembolectomy revolutionized ALI treatment, and open thromboembolectomy remains the standard technique. Previously, thromboembolectomy was performed without intraoperative fluoroscopy, using back bleeding as the endpoint; however, for safety and reliability, current practice favors the use of over-the-wire type Fogarty catheters under fluoroscopic guidance. Intraoperative angiography allows hybrid treatment by performing EVT, such as balloon angioplasty or stent placement, after thromboembolectomy as needed. Hybrid treatment is particularly useful for acute-on-chronic lesions associated with atherosclerotic disease and has significantly contributed to improved outcomes in patients with ALI. The femoral artery is the most common site of access for thromboembolectomy. Proximal and distal control of the femoral artery by taping is mandatory to prevent distal embolization. Distal embolization into the deep femoral artery should be avoided whenever possible because it compromises collateral circulation. Arteriotomy is generally performed in the femoral artery; however, incision of the superficial femoral artery may be chosen to maintain flow to the deep femoral artery. Transverse arteriotomy is important for preventing stenosis at the insertion site.

Popliteal artery access is useful for infrapopliteal embolism to avoid vessel injury caused by a size mismatch with Fogarty catheters designed for larger arteries (Fig. 2).

Selective thromboembolectomy of each tibial artery is possible using below-knee approaches. The goal is to establish at least one straight-line flow, with the contralateral limb serving as a reference for selecting the target vessel. On the basis of intraoperative angiography, timely conversion to bypass surgery without hesitation is critical when necessary. Although surgical thromboembolectomy can be performed under local anesthesia, general anesthesia managed by anesthesiologists is preferable because of systemic instability and potential complications, such as MNMS and compartment syndrome.

2. EVT

Representative EVT modalities include catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDT) and percutaneous thrombus aspiration therapy. In the latter, treatment with this modality alone is difficult because the inner diameter of the devices used (such as sheaths and catheters) is often smaller than the size of the thrombus; therefore, concomitant use with balloon dilation, stent placement, or CDT is essential. In Western countries, favorable outcomes of EVT, mainly centered on CDT, have been reported.7)

In a meta-analysis comparing surgical revascularization and EVT, no significant differences in limb salvage rates or 30-day mortality were observed between the 2 groups; however, higher incidences of major bleeding and distal embolic complications were reported in the EVT group. Nevertheless, in interpreting reports from Western countries, it should be noted that tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) preparations—which are not approved for insurance coverage for ALI in Japan—as well as high-dose urokinase exceeding insurance reimbursement limits and mechanical thrombectomy devices, have been used.

Therefore, EVT should be limited as an alternative therapy to cases such as:

(1) severe atherosclerotic disease in which thrombectomy catheter insertion is difficult;

(2) patients whose general condition is unsuitable for surgical treatment; and

(3) cases in which rapid surgical thrombus removal is difficult to perform.

In Japan, following the discontinuation of the urokinase supply, 4 related academic societies submitted an urgent request for regulatory approval of thrombus aspiration devices. Consequently, the Indigo aspiration system (Penumbra, Alameda, CA, USA) received regulatory approval in April 2023 and was subsequently covered by national health insurance in September of the same year.

As of January 2024, the device was available at a limited number of institutions under mandatory all-case post-marketing surveillance and appropriate use guidelines.

In a multicenter prospective study conducted in Italy involving 150 patients who developed ALI within 14 days, the achievement rate of thromboaspiration in peripheral ischemia classification 2 (incomplete or partial recanalization with distal flow) or 3 (complete recanalization with normal distal flow) after Indigo treatment was 88.7%. This rate increased to 95.3% with additional treatment following thrombus aspiration, and the incidence of major bleeding or device-related adverse events was 0%.

At 1 month, improvement of at least 1 class in the Rutherford classification was observed in 98.7% of patients, primary patency was 92%, and the amputation-free rate was 99.4%.8) Mechanical thrombectomy using the Indigo aspiration system has been reported to be a safe and effective first-line treatment for reperfusion in ALI.

According to the aforementioned Japanese appropriate use guidelines, the indications include the following:

(1) ALI of the lower extremity arteries (including the iliac arteries);

(2) presence of acute ischemic signs requiring prompt treatment; and

(3) cases in which surgical thrombectomy is not feasible or is expected to be difficult to achieve effective thrombus removal, or cases in which residual thrombus is expected even after surgical thrombectomy.

At present, surgical thrombectomy remains the first-line revascularization strategy for ALI.

3. Hybrid therapy

As described above, when thrombi or stenotic lesions remain after surgical thromboembolectomy, subsequent EVT is useful. In a retrospective study comparing conventional standalone thromboembolectomy with hybrid therapy, although no significant differences were observed between the 2 groups in early complications or long-term survival, the hybrid therapy group demonstrated superior patency rates, limb salvage rates, and freedom from reintervention.9)

In Japan, where fluoroscopic equipment is widely available, surgical revascularization combined with angiography is the first-line treatment for ALI, and transition to hybrid therapy should be considered based on angiographic findings. On the other hand, as the primary goal of ALI treatment is limb salvage, it is important to provide appropriate treatment without excessive insistence on complete revascularization.

IV. Conditions Requiring Attention after Revascularization

When blood flow is restored to ischemic tissue, local toxic substances can induce severe local and systemic disorders (ischemia–reperfusion injury). After revascularization for ALI, attention to the following 2 conditions is required, and appropriate management is essential.

1. MNMS

Muscle cell necrosis and lysis occur when ischemia persists for a prolonged period, leading to the accumulation of potassium, myoglobin, CK, LDH, and other substances in the affected limb. These substances enter the systemic circulation upon restoration of blood flow, resulting in hyperkalemia and metabolic acidosis. Furthermore, injury to distant organs may occur, causing lethal arrhythmias, respiratory failure, acute renal failure (myoglobinuria), and bacterial translocation. This systemic disease entity has been proposed as MNMS,10) and it is a serious complication that determines the prognosis after revascularization for ALI.

Preventive measures for MNMS include controlled limb reperfusion—the femoral vein of the affected limb is clamped before reperfusion, and perfusate is infused from the artery to remove ischemia-related metabolites extracorporeally—as well as intraoperative blood purification. However, is no established preventive strategy exists. If systemic improvement cannot be achieved despite these measures, life-saving limb amputation should be considered.

2. Compartment syndrome

Increased capillary permeability during ischemia–reperfusion causes local edema and elevated pressure within muscle compartments, leading to circulatory disturbance and subsequent neuromuscular dysfunction. When swelling of the affected limb, pain at rest, pain on passive stretch, sensory disturbance, or motor dysfunction is present, compartment syndrome should be suspected, and intracompartmental pressure should be measured.

The lower leg contains 4 compartments, and when intracompartmental pressure exceeds 30 mmHg (normal pressure ≤8 mmHg), irreversible nerve injury and muscle necrosis may occur, potentially necessitating limb amputation even if revascularization is successful. The anterior compartment, in particular, contains a relatively large muscle mass relative to its compartment size and is most prone to developing compartment syndrome.

The treatment for this condition is fasciotomy. Although various cutoff values for intracompartmental pressure have been proposed, persistent ischemia associated with elevated pressure leads to irreversible changes in muscles and nerves. Therefore, fasciotomy should be performed as promptly as possible once the condition is diagnosed (Fig. 3).

V. Postoperative Management

Anticoagulation therapy with heparin should be continued to prevent early re-occlusion, and investigation of the underlying cause should also be continued postoperatively to prevent recurrence.

The efficacy of anticoagulation therapy has been established for ALI caused by cardiogenic embolism,11) and warfarin or direct oral anticoagulants should be administered. Although not an established treatment, anticoagulation therapy may be considered to prevent recurrence even in the absence of an identified embolic source.4)

Antiplatelet agents and statins are recommended as secondary prevention for ALI caused by atherosclerosis, similar to standard treatment for peripheral arterial disease.12) In particular, antithrombotic therapy should be selected based on the devices used during EVT.

Conclusion

In the management of ALI, accurate assessment of disease severity and prompt therapeutic intervention—including treatment strategy planning—are essential and influence not only limb salvage but also overall survival. Treatment strategy decisions require intraoperative angiography and preoperative examinations to determine the need for additional interventions.

The greatest advantage of surgical revascularization performed by vascular surgeons is the rapid relief of ischemia; however, clinicians should be proficient not only in surgical thrombectomy but also in 1-stage bypass surgery, endovascular therapy, fasciotomy, and primary limb amputation and should have a comprehensive understanding of all available treatment options.
