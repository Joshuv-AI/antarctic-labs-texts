EUS‐Guided Gallbladder Drainage for Acute Cholecystitis in the Western World: Heterogeneity in Current Practice and the Relation With Patient Outcome
Authors: Louis De Wispelaere, Eva Hufkens, Hannah van Malenstein, Wim Laleman, Michiel Bronswijk, Schalk van der Merwe, Roberto Di Mitri, Michele Amata, Daniela Scimeca, Niels G Venneman, Koen R Beukema, Alberto Larghi, Antonio Pelegrino, Felice Molinario, Dominiek De Wulf, Elise Devolder, Dennis Yang, Mustafa Arain, Natalie Cosgrove, Muhammad K Hasan, Maham Hayat, Kambiz Kadkhodayan, Christophe Snauwaert, Roberto Trasolini, Cynthuja Thilakanathan, Shirley Jiang, Douglas G Adler, Lionel S D'Souza, Rogier P Voermans, Anouk G Overdevest, Roy L J Van Wanrooij, Enrique Pérez‐Cuadrado‐Robles, Willem J Lammers, Pieter Jan de Jonge, Marco J Bruno, Helena Degroote, Thomas Billiet, Alexander Arlt, Sebastian Stefanovic, Emine Gökce

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLaparoscopic cholecystectomy is the standard treatment for acute cholecystitis (AC), but some patients are unfit for (immediate) surgery. Endoscopic ultrasound‐guided gallbladder drainage (EUS‐GBD) using a lumen‐apposing metal stent (LAMS) is increasingly used in this population.ObjectiveThis multicenter cohort study aimed to evaluate practice patterns in EUS‐GBD across Western centers and to identify factors that influence outcomes.DesignWe retrospectively analyzed data from patients undergoing EUS‐GBD for AC across 18 centers. Patient‐related, procedural and outcome data were collected. The primary outcomes of interest were LAMS‐related adverse events (AEs), recurrent biliary disease, recurrent acute cholecystitis, and all‐cause mortality. Time‐to‐event analyses were performed using center‐stratified Cox proportional hazards and Fine–Gray competing‐risk models to identify LAMS procedural factors associated with clinical outcomes.ResultsWe included 496 patients with a median follow‐up of 176 days (IQR 44–569). Technical and clinical success rates of EUS‐GBD were 98.2% and 93.5%, respectively. LAMS‐related AEs and recurrent biliary disease occurred in 11.1% and 18.8% of patients, respectively. Transduodenal access was associated with a lower cumulative incidence of LAMS‐related AEs compared with transgastric access (SHR 0.42, 95% CI 0.23–0.76, p = 0.004) and lower rates of recurrent biliary disease and recurrent acute cholecystitis during follow‐up. Larger LAMS diameters (> 10 mm) were associated with a lower risk of recurrent acute cholecystitis and all‐cause mortality. No association between coaxial DPPS placement and improved long‐term outcomes could be demonstrated after competing‐risk adjustment. Among patients undergoing interval cholecystectomy (N = 46), LAMS‐related conversion from laparoscopy to laparotomy occurred in 3 cases (6.5%).ConclusionsIn this large cohort study, transduodenal access route and choice of a large (> 10 mm) LAMS diameter were associated with improved outcomes of EUS‐GBD. EUS‐GBD may be a feasible option as a bridge to laparoscopic cholecystectomy, with limited risk of conversion to laparotomy. The findings of our study require prospective validation.

Introduction

Acute cholecystitis (AC) is a common cause of emergency hospital admissions. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is the standard treatment, as recommended by international guidelines [1]. However, a substantial proportion of patients are deemed unfit for (immediate) surgery because of a high operative risk and/or important comorbidities. If antibiotic treatment alone fails in these patients, gallbladder drainage is indicated, either as a bridge to subsequent surgery or as definitive treatment. Where percutaneous transhepatic gallbladder drainage (PT‐GBD) traditionally has been the method of choice, endoscopic transpapillary gallbladder drainage (ET‐GBD) via ERCP and endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)‐guided gallbladder drainage (EUS‐GBD) with lumen‐apposing metal stents (LAMS) have been proposed as alternative strategies. The DRAC 1 trial, a multicenter randomized controlled trial comparing percutaneous and EUS‐GBD for inoperable patients with AC, found EUS‐GBD to be superior in terms of adverse events (AEs), re‐interventions and recurrent AC [2]. In observational studies, the technical success was significantly higher in the EUS‐guided group in comparison with ET‐GBD group, resulting in higher clinical success and fewer episodes of recurrent cholecystitis [3, 4, 5]. Based on these studies, international guidelines (ESGE and ASGE) recommend EUS‐GBD over ET‐GBD and PT‐GBD in centers with appropriate expertise for patients with AC not suitable for surgery [6, 7].

Although the technical approach to EUS‐GBD appears straightforward, practice variation remains significant and AEs can be severe. Decisions concerning access route (transgastric vs. transduodenal), LAMS diameter, use of coaxial double plastic pigtail stents (DPPS), and whether to remove or leave the LAMS in situ are inconsistently applied across studies. These decisions may be influenced by patient‐specific factors such as future operability and the estimated life expectancy. However, robust scientific data to guide the optimal strategy, during and after EUS‐GBD, are lacking.

The aim of this multicenter retrospective large cohort study was to identify practice patterns in EUS‐GBD for AC in the Western world, and to identify potential factors associated with improved outcome.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a multicentric retrospective cohort study involving 18 centers experienced in interventional endoscopic ultrasound, including EUS‐GBD. Participating centers comprised both academic and non‐academic hospitals throughout the Western world (Europe, The United States of America and Canada). The study was coordinated with the approval of the Ethics Committee of University Hospital Ghent (ONZ‐2024‐0460). All participating centers obtained local IRB approval or waiver according to local regulations. Data were collected for all consecutive adult patients per center who had undergone EUS‐GBD with LAMS for acute cholecystitis between October 2016 and April 2025 for AC as defined by the Tokyo guidelines [8].

Data Collection

Data on EUS‐GBD procedures were collected using a predefined data extraction file including patient‐related, procedural, and outcome data.

Patient‐related data included age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [9], ASA score, type of cholecystitis, severity (Tokyo grade), imaging modalities used pre‐procedure, and immunosuppressive status and use of anticoagulation.

Procedural variables included the date of the EUS‐GBD, access route, LAMS size and coaxial DPPS use at the index procedure. Table S1 shows an overview of procedural characteristics by center.

The primary outcome measures of interest were LAMS related Adverse Events (AE), biliary disease (BD), recurrent acute cholecystitis (AC) and overall mortality rate. Recurrence of biliary disease included cholecystitis, cholangitis, symptomatic biliary stones and biliary pancreatitis.

Secondary outcome data included technical success, clinical success, AE rate, and re‐intervention rate. Technical success was defined as successful transluminal deployment of the LAMS into the gallbladder. Clinical success was defined as the resolution of symptoms with improvement in inflammatory markers and/or imaging in the first 10 days after the procedure. Adverse events were graded using the AGREE classification [10]. Adverse events were categorized as LAMS‐ or non‐LAMS related. They were attributed based on a retrospective chart review by local investigators at each participating center, using predefined criteria and clinical judgment. Attribution was categorized according to likelihood (e.g., unlikely, possible, probable/likely), based on overall clinical assessment. LAMS‐related AEs included perforation related to stent maldeployment, stent migration (including buried LAMS), stent occlusion and bleeding directly related to the stent. Non‐LAMS‐related AEs were defined as any complications occurring after the procedure but not directly attributable to the LAMS itself.

In the subcohort of patients who received interval cholecystectomy after EUS‐GBD, timing of cholecystectomy, type of surgery (open or laparoscopic), intraoperative conversion rate from laparoscopic to open approach, and postoperative AEs (using the Clavien‐Dindo classification) were recorded [11].

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were applied to all patient, procedural, and outcome variables. Continuous variables are reported as medians with range and categorical variables as absolute counts and proportions. Although all descriptive tables were generated on all available follow‐up data, all survival analyses were truncated at 1‐year post‐procedure to ensure a clinically relevant and comparably sized risk set, given the heterogeneous observation periods inherent to the retrospective multicentric design. No imputation of missing data was performed.

LAMS‐related adverse events (AEs), recurrent biliary disease, recurrent acute cholecystitis, and all‐cause mortality were defined as the primary outcomes of interest. The primary estimand was the total causal effect of access route, LAMS size, and coaxial DPPS placement on the predefined outcomes. As a secondary goal, the clinical benefit or harm of LAMS removal over time is evaluated. Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) were constructed to identify minimal sufficient adjustment sets for each exposure–outcome pair (Figures [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link]).

Center‐stratified Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate pooled hazard ratios for all‐cause mortality. For outcomes subject to competing risks (LAMS‐related adverse events, biliary recurrence, and recurrent acute cholecystitis), center‐stratified Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models were applied. All regression models were adjusted with the minimal sufficient adjustment sets obtained in the DAGS without additional data‐driven variable selection. In the secondary analysis, LAMS‐removal was included as a time‐varying covariate.

The significance level was set α = 0.05 for statistical testing and 95% confidence intervals were reported for hazard ratios. Descriptive analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (v29.0; IBM Corp.). Survival analyses and visualisations were carried out in R (v4.4.3) using the survival (v3.8‐3) [12, 13], and tidycmprsk (v1.1.1) [14] packages. DAGs were constructed in DAGitty (v3.0) [15]. Full statistic methodological details are provided in the Supporting Information [Link], [Link], [Link], [Link].

Median available follow‐up was 176 days (IQR 44–569). Adverse events occurred in 114 patients (23.0%), of which 55 (48.2%) AEs were LAMS‐related. The median time to a LAMS‐related AE was 29 days (IQR 6–51). The most frequent LAMS‐related adverse events were stent occlusion and migration, each occurring in 14 cases (2.8%). Maldeployment of the LAMS was uncommon (1.0%, 5/496) and was managed endoscopically in most cases (3/5), while interventional radiology or surgery was required in the remainder. The vast majority of LAMS‐related adverse events could be managed with an additional endoscopic procedure (76.4%, 42/55). More information on LAMS‐related and ‐unrelated AEs can be found in Table 3 and Tables S2 and S3 respectively.

Recurrence of biliary disease was seen in 93 out of 496 patients (18.8%). The median time to recurrence was 59 days (IQR 24–176). All but 4 patients (95.7%, 89/93) were successfully managed with antibiotics and/or endoscopic reintervention. More information on can be found in Table S4.

A significant proportion of our patient cohort had died at the moment of our data collection (36.5%, 181/496), with a median time to death of 74 days (IQR 30–252). The most common cause of death was progressive oncologic disease (40.3%, 73/181). Death was considered likely attributable to EUS‐GBD in 4 patients (0.8%), including three cases of gastrointestinal perforation and one case of gastrointestinal bleeding. Further information can be found in Table S5.

Notably, most LAMS‐related adverse events occurred early after the index procedure, suggesting that stent‐related complications predominantly arise during the initial postprocedural period. In contrast, recurrent biliary disease, recurrent acute cholecystitis, and all‐cause mortality accumulated more gradually over time.

For patients within the same center, the transduodenal access route (vs. transgastric access) was independently associated with a lower cumulative incidence of LAMS‐related adverse events (SHR 0.42, 95% CI 0.23–0.76, p = 0.004), biliary recurrence (SHR 0.60, 95% CI 0.37–1.00, p = 0.049) and recurrence of acute cholecystitis (SHR 0.35, 95% CI 0.16–0.79, p = 0.011). The distribution of LAMS‐related adverse events for the transduodenal and transgastric access routes is displayed in Table S6.

Choice of a larger LAMS diameter (> 10 mm as compared to 10 mm) was associated with a lower risk of recurrent acute cholecystitis (SHR 0.24, 95% CI 0.08–0.73, p = 0.012) and death (HR 0.63, 95% CI 0.42–0.94, p = 0.023).

No significant association was observed between coaxial DPPS placement and our primary outcome variables.

Discussion

This large multinational cohort study of patients who underwent EUS‐GBD for acute cholecystitis identified the transduodenal access route and a large (> 10 mm as compared to 10 mm) LAMS diameter as independent favorable outcome predictors.

Transduodenal LAMS placement was independently associated with a significantly reduced risk of LAMS‐related AEs. This is likely explained by the fact that the gastric antrum is more exposed to ingested food and is subject to more peristaltic motion and contractions leading to a higher risk of occlusion, stent migration and buried LAMS. A similar rationale may explain the lower incidence of recurrent acute cholecystitis and any biliary disease observed with the transduodenal approach. These observations support the growing favor for a transduodenal access route when technically feasible [16, 17].

Use of a large (> 10 mm as compared to 10 mm) diameter LAMS was associated with a lower incidence of recurrent acute cholecystitis and all‐cause mortality. Larger stent diameters likely limit the risk of recurrent cholecystitis by preventing stent obstruction by sludge, stones, or food impaction.

Randomized control trials have confirmed the added value of a coaxial DPPS within an LAMS in the context of walled‐off necrosis and malignant distal biliary obstruction [18, 19]. In the setting of EUS‐GBD, a coaxial DPS could theoretically improve the long‐term LAMS patency and reduce the risk of recurrent cholecystitis [20].

After applying center‐stratified time‐to‐event and competing‐risk models, no statistically significant independent association between coaxial DPPS use and one of our primary outcome measures could be retained. We propose selectively using coaxial DPPS on a case‐by‐case basis until higher‐quality prospective data become available.

A particular subgroup of interest are the patients who underwent interval cholecystectomy following EUS‐GBD. Previous reports have inconsistently suggested a risk of conversion to open or subtotal cholecystectomy of 2.5%–14%, at least if the LAMS is left in situ [21, 22, 23]. In our subgroup analysis, LAMS‐related laparotomy was only observed in 3/46 cases (6.5%). In all three patients, the LAMS had been left in situ prior to surgery. This conversion rate is exactly the same as the one reported in a very recent subgroup meta‐analysis comparing the outcome of interval cholecystectomy after PT‐GBD and EUS‐GBD. Of note, the pooled conversion rate after PT‐GBD in their subgroup analysis was 16.5%. The rate of postoperative complications was 15%, which can be deemed acceptable, considering the risk profile of the patients in our cohort. Hence, our data suggest that if cholecystectomy is required after EUS‐GBD, laparoscopic surgery could be feasible and safe. These findings are in line with the recent literature evidence [24, 25]. Exchange of the LAMS to a double pigtail stent prior to surgery can be considered to reduce the fistula size.

In the study population of patients not fit for surgery, our data did not support a systematic elective exchange/removal of the LAMS to reduce LAMS‐related adverse events and recurrence of biliary events. A previous Spanish study concluded that long‐term indwelling of LAMS after EUS‐GBD is acceptable but lacked a direct comparison group [26]. In a recently published nation‐wide US cohort, a numerical trend toward more biliary recurrence and LAMS‐specific adverse events was seen when the LAMS was left in situ, but without statistical significance [27]. Given the significant risk of confounding in our and previous studies, no firm recommendation can be made and we therefore suggest making the decision for elective LAMS removal/exchange on an individualized basis, considering the patient's condition and overall prognosis.

The strengths of this study include its multicenter design including both academic and non‐academic centers throughout the Western world, the large sample size, and the comprehensive systematic data collection. Limitations are inherent to its retrospective nature. Although advanced statistical methodology, including center‐stratified time‐to‐event analyses, competing‐risk models, and DAG‐guided adjustment strategies, was applied to minimize bias, residual confounding can obviously not be excluded.

We anticipate that the findings of this study will contribute to greater standardization of clinical practice in EUS‐GBD, particularly through preferential transduodenal LAMS placement and choice for a large (> 10 mm) diameter LAMS whenever feasible. In addition, our data indicate that EUS‐GBD may be safely applied in a “bridge to surgery setting” and deserves further study in this subcohort of patients.
