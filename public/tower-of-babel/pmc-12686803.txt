Coordination of inflammatory and adrenocortical stress response during pregnancy and variation by mental health
Authors: Heidemarie K. Laurent, Sherryl H. Goodman, Douglas A. Granger, Penina M. Backer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Well-regulated coordination of a multi-system stress response is particularly consequential for intergenerational health during pregnancy, when maternal stress systems typically adapt to protect the fetus. However, little is known about patterns of combined immune/inflammatory mediator (IM) and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis responding to acute stress in human pregnancy, let alone variations associated with maternal health risks. In this study we aimed to define normative perinatal stress response coordination and potential risk profiles by testing relations among pregnant women's salivary IM's and cortisol in response to psychosocial stress, as well as both concurrent affective distress symptomatology and lifetime diagnoses of affective disorders. A community sample of pregnant women (n = 158) participating in the first timepoint of a longitudinal study completed the Trier Social Stress Task and contributed five saliva samples assayed for cortisol; pro-inflammatory cytokines IL1β, IL6, TNFα; and CRP. They self-reported symptoms reflecting common (general distress) and distinct (anhedonic depression, anxious arousal) components of affective distress, and were interviewed about mental health history with a structured clinical interview. Multilevel modeling of IM and cortisol trajectories revealed IM and cortisol responses were positively coordinated on average, but varied across women. Greater affective distress predicted higher/increasing IM stress responses that diverged from cortisol recovery; history of mood disorder specifically related to increasing post-stress IM's, while current general distress related to non-coordination/divergence of IM from cortisol response. These findings highlight a novel stress response pathway characterizing both previous and current affective distress that may help efforts to ameliorate intergenerational impacts of perinatal stress.

Introduction

The perinatal period from pregnancy through early postnatal development represents a time when humans experience stressors that profoundly impact their own and their offspring's wellbeing. Thus, it is a critical context for understanding both normative patterns of stress responsiveness—meaning what is typical or observed on average in healthy child-bearers—and variations associated with maladjustment (e.g., McCarthy et al., 2021; Van den Bergh et al., 2020). Known mammalian stress-protective adaptations during pregnancy involve lowered reactivity of the mother's neuroendocrine stress system—i.e., the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis—and modulation of the immune system to create an anti-inflammatory environment supporting fetal growth (see Coussons-Read, 2013). At the same time, disturbance in these protective mechanisms have been documented that in turn predicts harms to fetal development, birth outcomes, and the offspring's lifelong stress vulnerability (e.g., Creutzberg et al., 2021). To date, the work upon which this knowledge rests has been conducted in non-human animal models and/or fails to examine psychological stress-induced patterns of activation across these neurobiological systems. Understanding typical profiles of human responsiveness to perinatal stressors and deviations associated with mental health disturbance has the potential to inform underlying risk pathways. The present study was designed to shed light on this critical area by investigating pregnant women's salivary inflammatory mediator (IM) and HPA axis responses to psychosocial stress, testing both normative coordination of stress response/recovery dynamics across systems and variation by affective distress. Such work is necessary to determine what to expect and what to flag and intervene on to prevent intergenerational risk transmission.

Inflammatory and adrenocortical stress system functions and connections

Among the mechanisms the human body employs to respond to stressors are immune and neuroendocrine (HPA axis) systems designed to mobilize the organism's defenses against potential threat. Detection of a physical or psychological stressor sets off a neurophysiological response chain that may involve release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin 1 beta (IL1β), interleukin 6 (IL6), and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) to ward off infection, as well as the downstream acute-phase protein C-reactive protein (CRP), as the IM system is deployed. This pro-inflammatory (macrophage 1/M1) response is complemented by an anti-inflammatory (macrophage 2/M2) response that allows for repair once the threat has been neutralized. The stress response may also involve activation of the HPA axis resulting in release of the steroid hormone cortisol from the adrenal cortex, which in turn impacts neural and peripheral physiology to enhance vigilance and access metabolic resources, among other functions (Dickerson and Kemeny, 2004).

The IM and HPA systems show complex bidirectional associations involving both stimulatory and inhibitory connections that may depend on timing of observation, as well as intensity and type of stressor: On the one side, IM's are known to stimulate cortisol release, though this signaling path varies for physical vs. psychological stressors (Herman et al., 2003). On the other side, cortisol has long been recognized for immunosuppressive effects (e.g., Coutinho and Chapman, 2011), yet there is also evidence for a biphasic regulation relationship whereby mid-range cortisol elevation—such as that observed during a moderate acute stress response—exerts a preparative stimulatory effect on inflammatory response to subsequent stress (Yeager et al., 2011). This foundational research in a general population lays out what we might expect to observe in healthy human adults.

Stress system adaptations during pregnancy

There are also individual difference factors that play a role in activation of these systems and their coordination, including pregnancy. A healthy pregnancy is characterized by modulation of stress-responsive systems to protect fetal development. The immune system progresses through (a) a pro-inflammatory (Th1) stage in the first trimester necessary for embryo implantation and placentation, followed by (b) a shift to an anti-inflammmatory (Th2) stage across the main period of gestation in the 2nd-3rd trimesters fostering fetal growth, and finally (c) a pro-inflammmatory switch at the end of the 3rd trimester that induces labor (see Mor et al., 2017). At the same time, the HPA axis undergoes profound changes as placental release of corticotrophin-releasing hormone (pCRH) drives up levels of circulating cortisol, particularly later in pregnancy, while the diurnal rhythm and reactivity of the system are dampened (Dickens and Pawluski, 2018). While increased cortisol is necessary for fetal development, this must be balanced by inactivation of excess cortisol by the enzyme 11 beta hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase 2 (11β-HSD2). Finally, there is evidence from rodent models that the connection across these systems changes during pregnancy, resulting in inhibition of the bidirectional IM-HPA stimulatory path (Brunton and Russell, 2008). Although these adaptations are observed on average in healthy populations, there is substantial variability that has implications for maternal-fetal health.

Differences in stress system functioning related to affective distress

Differences in both IM and HPA systems been observed in relation to mental health status. In the general population of (non-pregnant) humans, depression and related affective disorders are often characterized by elevated cortisol and inflammation; this dual high-cortisol/high-inflammation “sickness phenotype” is indeed thought to help explain depressive symptoms (see Beurel et al., 2020). Despite being well documented, the basis for this profile remains unclear. Earlier glucocorticoid resistance theory posited impaired glucocorticoid receptor-mediated negative feedback loops, but this has been challenged by a failure to observe the proposed glucocorticoid resistance-inflammation link in depression (Perrin et al., 2019). A newer pro-inflammatory cortisol model suggests chronic, unpredictable stress gives rise to elevated cortisol that triggers the pro-inflammatory response in depression (Amasi-Hartoonian et al., 2022).

How these profiles apply to affective distress during pregnancy—a time when such distress is more prevalent—remains under debate. On the one hand, there is evidence from both human and non-human animal research associating perinatal distress with elevated IM's and/or exaggerated inflammatory response to challenge (e.g., Christian, 2014). However, other research reports nonsignificant associations or the opposite pattern—i.e., an inverse relation between antenatal depressive symptoms and specific IM's (e.g., Blackmore et al., 2011; Shelton et al., 2015). Findings for the HPA axis are similarly mixed, with reviews showing either no distress-related differences in cortisol among pregnant women or profiles that differ from depression during other periods (Seth et al., 2016; Orta et al., 2018). At the same time, research specifically tapping pregnant women's cortisol during an acute stressor revealed an association between elevated cortisol and increasing depressive symptoms across pregnancy-postpartum (Laurent et al., 2018). It is possible that a more specific focus on stress system patterns during acute stress would yield insight into perinatal distress pathology missed by a predominant focus on blood levels outside of a stress context.

Another dimension meriting further attention is the characterization of perinatal distress itself. Recent perinatal research underlines the importance of distinguishing components of affective difficulties that arise across pregnancy-postpartum as the structure and correlates of these may shift and differ from what is observed in non-perinatal populations (see Cochran et al., 2020; Miller and O’Hara, 2023). One established model of affective disorders that has shown utility in research and clinical contexts distinguishes the negative affectivity/general distress common to anxiety and depression from anxiety-specific anxious arousal and depression-specific anhedonia components (Clark and Watson, 1991). Given that these components may have different neurobiological correlates and treatment implications (e.g., Craske et al., 2015; Watson et al., 1995; Young et al., 2019), further efforts to understand stress-related processes that give rise to affective distress difficulties during pregnancy should attend not only to overall symptoms, but also the extent to which stress response profiles mark common vs. distinct components. This could help to determine whether particular IM response patterns can best be used to characterize risk for perinatal affective distress more generally, and/or to distinguish risk for anxiety vs. depressive disorders during this time.

Another dimension to consider is the relevance of lifetime diagnostic history, including past mood and/or anxiety disorders, compared to current symptom severity. Previous research reveals similarities but also some distinctions among effects of self-reported perinatal depression symptoms vs. diagnosed depressive disorders for mothers and their children (see Goodman et al., 2011; Goodman and Tully, 2009). To characterize a potential inflammatory stress risk phenotype, it will be important to clarify the extent to which a history of clinical-level distress (mood- and/or anxiety-related), in addition to more proximal experiences of symptoms, contribute to differences in women's perinatal stress responding.

Assessing stress responding and cross-system associations: findings from salivary measures

Taken as a whole, the above research might lead us to expect overall positive coordination across IM and HPA system activation during acute stress responding, but that such coordination may be stronger or weaker for certain populations such as pregnant people and/or those suffering from affective distress. Further complicating the picture is the observation that different types of stressors (physical vs. psychosocial) give rise to different cytokine-cortisol response profiles (Du Preez et al., 2021). In order to discern “healthy” and potentially risky stress response profiles in human pregnancy, it is crucial to examine women responding to psychosocial stressors that map onto daily life experiences. Given the difficulty of repeatedly sampling blood or brain tissue across a stress episode in humans, salivary measures have more recently been employed. Although the extent to which oral/salivary measures reflect systemic inflammation is debated (e.g., Engeland et al., 2019; Slavish et al., 2015), there is accumulating evidence that salivary IM's offer a relevant index of stress response associated with adjustment, as in the research reviewed below.

Studies employing salivary measures add to evidence of reciprocal connections across HPA and IM systems under stress. For example, researchers found elevated levels of salivary cytokines IL6 and TNFα related to patterns of daily cortisol output—i.e., lower cortisol awakening response, less diurnal decline, but higher cortisol levels overall—observed in chronic stress (DeSantis et al., 2012). In response to an acute stressor, increases in salivary pro-inflammatory cytokines and CRP have further been associated with cortisol elevations (Semsi et al., 2023; Winful et al., 2024).

As for the systemic inflammation markers reviewed above, salivary measures reveal individual differences in stress response and coordination with the HPA axis related to psychological adjustment. Higher salivary pro-inflammatory cytokines have been observed in cases of major depressive disorder (Yui et al., 2022), and interventions to regulate stress and mood such as mindfulness-based training result in salivary IM reductions (e.g., Lengacher et al., 2018; Sanada et al., 2020; Walsh et al., 2016). Although there is as yet no direct evidence of such differences in a pregnant sample, there is indirect evidence through findings of an oral microbiome profile furthering inflammatory cytokines in women with clinically elevated symptoms of perinatal depression (Agranyoni et al., 2025).

Regarding distress-related differences in HPA-IM system coordination, the predominant focus has been on ratios of absolute levels of salivary cortisol and IM's rather than dynamics of stress response and recovery. Specifically, researchers have documented higher salivary cortisol/CRP related to depression (Fong et al., 2022) and lower cortisol/CRP related to symptom improvement (Wheat et al., 2015). One study that did examine salivary cytokines and cortisol in relation to an acute stress event—a first responder training exercise—showed an association between psychological resilience and lower immediate stress-related pro-inflammatory cytokine (TNFα and IL6) elevations, as well as differing cytokine vs. cortisol changes during a post-stress recovery period (Wong et al., 2023). This suggests profiles of HPA-IM coordination across acute stress exposure may contribute to affective distress risk vs. resilience. However, much remains unknown about the nature of such profiles across different IM's and in response to more typical psychosocial stressors. It is further unclear how this might generalize to pregnant people experiencing distress.

The current study

From the above-reviewed research, it is clear that the nature of maternal multi-system stress responsivity during pregnancy is consequential yet poorly characterized, both as a typical or normative phenomenon and in terms of risk-related variability. We sought to address this gap with an investigation of women from a larger longitudinal study of perinatal stress and mental health who contributed salivary measures of IM's (pro-inflammatory cytokines IL1β, IL6, TNF; CRP) and cortisol before and after a standard psychosocial stress task known to activate the HPA axis during their third trimester of pregnancy. We used these salivary biomarkers in conjunction with self-report and diagnostic measures of affective distress to address the following research questions:1)Are salivary IM responses to psychosocial stress normatively coordinated with cortisol responses in pregnancy? To best capture dynamic response coordination, we separated within-person covariation of IM and cortisol response trajectories from between-person concordance of mean levels of activation. Based on the limited evidence for potential links across these systems, we hypothesized positive coordination of IM and cortisol trajectories in this stress context, but that the degree of coordination would vary across women.2)Are there differences in salivary IM response and/or cortisol-IM response coordination associated with pregnant women's affective distress? Guided by the considerations noted above, we examined affective distress in terms of both current self-reported symptoms and lifetime affective disorder diagnoses. We predicted affective distress would relate to higher IM responses and differences in cross-system coordination but did not make specific hypotheses regarding direction of effects for coordination given the paucity of prior research to guide such hypotheses.

To fully address these questions while limiting the number of statistical tests used to address hypothesized associations, primary analyses focused on a composite IM measure to test average response trajectories and differences associated with current affective distress symptoms (including subscales for general distress and depression-specific anhedonia and anxiety-specific anxious arousal) and lifetime diagnoses. To probe more specific sources of effects, secondary analyses tested each individual IM outcome and separate diagnostic codes (i.e., current and past mood and anxiety disorders). This approach is supported by previous antenatal research consolidating multiple inflammatory markers into a single composite score (e.g., Borders et al., 2022) while allowing us to explore whether different patterns are apparent for each IM (see Ross et al., 2022).

Method

Participants

Participants were 158 pregnant self-identified women who completed the first timepoint of a larger longitudinal study of mother-infant stress development. The sample was recruited from local health centers and community agencies serving pregnant people, as well as through social media advertising. To be eligible, participants had to be adult English speakers, expecting a singleton birth with no health complications that would preclude participating in stress tasks, and willing/able to complete a mix of remote and in-person assessments until their child was 2 years old. The latter required living within approximately a 1-h travel radius of the research lab at which the PI was located (central IL from T1 dates November 2017–September 2020; central PA from T1 dates June 2022–June 2024).

At the time of the first assessment, participants ranged in age from 19 to 40 (M = 30.98, SD = 4.92) and in gestational age from 19 to 40 weeks (M = 31.25, SD = 2.74). The majority identified as White European-American (75.8 %), followed by Black/African (10.8 %), Latina (4.5 %), Asian/Asian American (6.4 %), and Multiracial (2.5 %). Procedures

The longitudinal study from which the present data are drawn consisted of five assessments with the pregnant mother and/or her infant: T1 was intended to occur during the third trimester of pregnancy, T2 at 3 months postnatal, T3 at 8 months postnatal, T4 at 15 months postnatal, and T5 at 24 months postnatal. Each assessment involved Qualtrics questionnaire completion, taking part in a psychosocial stress task and saliva sampling, and clinical interviewing. All procedures were performed in compliance with ethical guidelines according to the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the University of Illinois (protocol 18002 approved December 8, 2017) and/or Pennsylvania State University (protocol 19133 approved August 2, 2022) Institutional Review Boards. Participants gave written informed consent prior to taking part in study procedures.

Participating women were scheduled for their first session either in the laboratory or remotely via zoom as necessitated by COVID-19 pandemic restrictions, and by participant preference following the lifting of such restrictions. A comparison of stress biomarker trajectories across in-person and remote sessions revealed no significant differences in cortisol or IM responses or their coordination. All sessions were scheduled in the afternoon to reduce variability related to biomarker diurnal rhythms. Following written informed consent, participants completed pre-task questionnaires covering information relevant to salivary stress measures; if the participant was sick with a fever they were rescheduled, and other information relevant to oral and general health was considered in covariate testing as described below.

Following an initial saliva sample, participants were given instructions for the stress task, the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST; Kirschbaum et al., 1993; see Gunnar et al., 2021 for validation of an online version of the task). Following a 5-min speech preparation period, the participant engaged in 5 min of public speaking and 5 min of mental arithmetic in front of a “judge” (trained research confederate). Immediately after the task, the second saliva sample was collected, after which the participant continued completing study questionnaires and/or the clinical interview (dependent on whether the latter could be scheduled during the session). The third saliva sample was collected 25 min after the start of the TSST, and the fourth and fifth samples at 20 min increments following the preceding sample. For each sample, the participant was asked to passively drool into a collection tube for 2 min (timed by the researcher). All samples were placed in a laboratory or home freezer before being transferred to a −60° freezer for storage until they could be shipped on dry ice for assay at the Institute for Interdisciplinary Salivary Bioscience or Salimetrics Laboratory. These procedures ae in keeping with current best practice recommendations for salivary IM measurement (see Riis et al., 2021) while allowing for home sample collection to accommodate remote sessions.

Measures

Salivary biomarkers

All samples were assayed in duplicate for cortisol and inflammatory mediators (IL1β, IL6, TNFα, CRP), with averages of duplicate results used in analyses (see Riis et al., 2021). On day of assay, samples were allowed to thaw to room temperature and centrifuged at 3500 rpm 15 min to pellet remaining mucins. The Human CRP (Vascular Injury Panel 2) V-Plex Meso Scale Discovery (MSD)® multi-spot Assay (Ref# K0080900) measured salivary CRP, samples diluted 5-fold (MSD® Assay Diluent) prior to assay. The Human ProInflammatory V-Plex Meso Scale Discovery (MSD)® multi-spot Assay (Ref# K008074) measured cytokines, samples diluted 2-fold in (MSD® Assay Diluent) prior to assay. Salivary analyte concentrations (pg/mL) were determined with MSD Discovery Workbench Software (v. 4.0) using curve fit models (4-PL with weighting function option of 1/y2). MSD® determines the detection limit for each analyte by interpolating mean concentration minus 2.5 SDs (Avg Concentration blank + 2.5 SDblank) over several kit lots. Intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were, on average, <10 % and 15 %, respectively (see also Martinez et al., 2018).

Per testing center recommendations, all biomarkers were screened for assay values outside of detection limits and low values substituted with ½ the lower limit and high values with the upper limit. In total, this impacted a relatively small proportion of sample values (.26 %–6.58 % across biomarkers). Given positive skew of biomarker distributions, all scores were natural log-transformed for further analysis.

Control variables

A number of factors potentially influencing measurement of salivary biomarkers were assessed for influence. These included session start time, participant-reported recent food/drink; tooth brushing, cavities, dental work, and oral injuries; medications and other substance use; sleep/wake times; and exercise. They also covered oral health habits (i.e., frequency of dental appointments and tooth brushing) and body mass index pre- and post-pregnancy, as well as demographic variables including participant age and gestational age, race/ethnicity, education, and income. Initial model testing to identify relevant controls suggested participants' current use of certain medications—antihistamines, thyroid medication, and antibiotics—and education were associated with levels of multiple IM's and/or cortisol, and several other variables (BMI, recent oral injury or dental work, nicotine use within past 24 h) showed associations with a single IM. Given the questionable value of including covariates that only applied to a small number (6 or fewer) of participants, all models were tested both with and without control variables to determine whether their inclusion changed findings. No meaningful changes were detected—i.e., same pattern of significant effects, <15 % change in coefficient—for all primary model effects; therefore, the simpler models without covariates are reported below.

Mood and anxiety symptoms questionnaire

(MASQ-D30; Wardenaar et al., 2010). A shortened 30-item version of the MASQ was used to tap both shared and depression- or anxiety-specific components of affective distress. Participants rated each item on a scale from 1 = “not at all” to 5 = “extremely” within the past week. Ten items assessed General Distress or negative affect (e.g., “Felt dissatisfied with everything”); 10 items assessed Anhedonic Depression or low positive affect (e.g., “Felt optimistic” reverse coded); and 10 items assessed Anxious Arousal (e.g., “Was startled easily”). Scores for the three scales were calculated as means with potential range from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating more symptoms. Good internal consistency was found for each scale in the current sample: Cronbach's alpha = .89 for General Distress, .93 for Anhedonic Depression, and .81 for Anxious Arousal. Although not developed specifically to tap perinatal symptoms, previous research supports the use of this measure in pregnant samples (Davis et al., 2023; Hirsch et al., 2017).

Structured clinical interview for the DSM 5 – research version

(SCID-5-RV; First et al., 2015). Participants were interviewed about their history and current symptoms of psychiatric disorder by trained research assistants using the SCID-5-RV. Those who met criteria for a past and/or current affective diagnosis were coded positive for lifetime mood (i.e., depressive or bipolar) or anxiety (i.e., panic, social anxiety, agoraphobia, specific phobia, or generalized anxiety) disorder. These lifetime codes were further broken down by past vs. present disorder to localize effects in model testing below.

Analytic strategy

We used multilevel modeling with the HLM 7.0 program to test our research questions. As discussed in Laurent et al. (2013), growth curve models of repeated biomarker measures can separate “matched phase coordination,” or within-person alignment of rising/falling biomarker trajectories across a sampling period, from “average level coordination,” or similarity in overall levels of biomarkers. This approach has previously revealed adjustment-related differences in salivary cortisol-IM coordination across the TSST (Laurent et al., 2016), though with a more limited measure of inflammation (CRP only) and in a different type of sample (non-perinatal African American adults). HLM uses full information maximum likelihood estimation to arrive at parameter estimates in the presence of missing data at Level 1, though listwise deletion for cases missing data at Level 2 resulted in an n of 153 participants for analyses.

Here, we model inflammatory measures—the composite index, followed by individual IM's—as the outcome at Level 1, with an intercept and (for coordination models) concurrent cortisol as a time-varying covariate centered around the participant's own mean. At Level 2, these terms are allowed to vary across participants, and each participant's mean cortisol centered around the grand mean for the sample is entered to predict the intercept. These models allow us to address our first research question; the significance of the Level 1 cortisol covariate speaks to within-person stress system coordination, the significance of the Level 2 mean cortisol predictor speaks to between-person stress system concordance, and the Level 2 variability of the cortisol covariate speaks to coordination differences across participants.

To address our second research question, measures of participant affective distress—MASQ scale scores or SCID diagnostic codes—are entered at Level 2 as predictors of IM trajectory terms. For illustration, the model used to test affective symptom-related differences in cortisol-IM coordination is given below:

Level 1

IM = β0 + β1 (concurrent cortisol) + error.

Preliminary testing – IM composite and baseline trajectories

First, correlations across IM's were tested to determine if a composite IM index could plausibly be examined. All were significantly associated with one another (r's = .35-.63, mean = .48) and showed adequate reliability when considered as indicators of a common construct (Cronbach's alpha = .78). On this basis, standardized IM scores were averaged to create a composite IM variable to be tested as the primary outcome—in addition to individual IM variables as secondary outcomes—in multilevel models.

Baseline models without explanatory predictors were fit to each IM index to describe participants' response trajectories; across IM's, adding linear and quadratic terms to the intercept-only model resulted in significant fit improvements as reflected by change in the deviance statistic, χ2 (3–4) = 11.78–100.55, p's < .05. On average, a negative linear slope and positive quadratic slope showed that IM levels were falling across the first part of the session—during and following the TSST—before starting to rise again in the final samples. At the same time, significant between-participant variability in all trajectory terms (χ2 [154] = 192.17–3478.71, p's < .05) suggested IM levels and dynamics differed across women, justifying the addition of explanatory predictors.

Hypothesis testing – IM explanatory models

To test our first research question, cortisol was added as both a time-varying covariate (Level 1) and mean-level predictor of IM's (Level 2) as outlined above. For the IM composite and for each individual IM, the cortisol covariate term, but not the mean cortisol predictor, proved significant. This means that participants overall showed within-person positive coordination, but not concordance in activation levels, across systems. Cross-system coordination effect sizes based on standardized coefficients were small-medium, with the largest coordination effects observed for the IM composite and IL6. There was also significant between-person variability in cortisol covariation for the IM composite and for individual IM's—τ′s = .076-.213, χ2 (153) = 227.38–290.06, p's < .001—that could be explained by participant characteristics.

To test our second research question, affective distress measures were added as Level 2 predictors of (a) IM response terms (i.e., intercepts, linear and quadratic slopes), and (b) cortisol-IM response coordination (i.e., the time-varying cortisol covariate). In the MASQ model, General Distress showed a trend-level positive effect on IM composite linear slopes, and secondary analyses revealed a significant positive effect on IL6 linear slopes. Total MASQ score additionally positively predicted IL6 linear slopes and CRP intercepts. SCID lifetime mood disorder diagnosis significantly predicted more positive IM composite linear slopes, with a trend-level effect on intercepts. Further decomposition of lifetime codes revealed effects were attributable to past (vs. current) mood disorder; past mood disorder significantly predicted more positive linear slopes for all IM measures, as well as higher intercepts for the IM composite and IL6 (marginal effects on IL1β and CRP intercepts). There were no significant effects of anxiety disorder diagnoses, and neither affective symptoms (MASQ) nor diagnoses (SCID) predicted cortisol trajectories. Effect sizes based on standardized coefficients were in the small range, with affective distress predictors explaining up to 10 % of the variance in IM trajectory terms. Discussion

The present investigation provides evidence of a common activation to psychosocial stress across inflammatory cytokines and CRP in pregnancy that normatively aligns with cortisol response dynamics. Multilevel modeling revealed a sample-wide pattern of similarly falling salivary IM and cortisol levels from before to after acute stress exposure, but no relation between overall levels of HPA axis and IM system activation. We further found variability in IM response trajectories and cortisol-IM coordination associated with pregnant women's affective distress such that women with a history of clinically significant distress and/or greater current general distress showed a breakdown of this cortisol-IM coordination and rising IM's following the stressor. These findings offer novel insights into typical inflammatory stress profiles during pregnancy and deviations that may underlie risk for affective disorders.

The average cross-system stress response pattern detected here—i.e., falling from a pre-stress peak through an hour-long recovery period—could represent part of the protective prenatal environment discussed above that normatively allows mammals to withstand stressors during mid-late pregnancy without undue harm to the developing fetus. Although direct comparison with responses outside a prenatal context will be needed to determine if this is particularly characteristic of pregnancy, it appears both the HPA axis and IM system as reflected in salivary measures show limited reactivity and/or a readiness to recover quickly once a known stressor has been met. This would be consistent with previous observations of blunted HPA stress reactivity during late pregnancy (Dickens and Pawluski, 2018). Adding to a growing understanding of the complex interplay between inflammatory and neuroendocrine stress systems via both stimulatory and inhibitory paths, the positive direction of association between cortisol and IM's in this study echoes what has previously been observed for moderate acute psychosocial (as opposed to physical) stress (Herman et al., 2003). Importantly, this association was only evident at the within-person level of parallel rising/falling biomarkers and not at the between-person level of similar overall system activation. This suggests these systems normatively coordinate with one another while responding to acute stress, but that this coordination is a nuanced person-specific process undetectable through comparison of activation levels themselves, underlining the importance of looking to dynamics over time to comprehend how stress systems work together in health and disorder.

In addition to these average patterns, we found differences in women's cortisol-IM response profiles related to affective distress, offering further insight into the variability of stress response coordination and implications for psychological adjustment. The most consistent findings involved higher/increasing inflammatory responses among women with a history of mood disorder, and disrupted cross-system coordination with greater current affective distress, apparently due to a divergence of increasing IM from decreasing cortisol across the latter part of the post-stress recovery period. This is broadly consistent with the idea of a pro-inflammatory phenotype contributing to and/or following from affective disorder, though we did not detect the elevated cortisol typically considered part of this phenotype (Amasi-Hartoonian et al., 2022). Given that previous work in a pregnant sample similarly showed associations between affective symptoms and inflammatory cytokines but not cortisol (Shelton et al., 2015), it may be that pregnancy status alters which aspects of the phenotype are present. This would be consistent with the shift across pregnancy toward pCRH as a main driver of circulating cortisol, which could blunt effects of maternal psychological factors (see Voegtline et al., 2013). It is further worth noting that an absence of effects on stress-reactive cortisol does not rule out potential impacts of maternal stress/distress via disrupted 11β-HSD2 neutralization of circulating cortisol (Jahnke et al., 2021), and future research should address multiple points in stress system activation and paths of effect to fully characterize harmful profiles.

Our findings diverge from those of the one study to our knowledge testing mental health-relevant individual differences in salivary cytokine and cortisol responses to stress, which suggested a delayed post-stress IM increase contrasting with ongoing cortisol decrease characterized resilience (Wong et al., 2023). Caution in directly comparing these studies is warranted, given differences in sample (pregnant women vs. a mostly male first responders) and stress context (moderate psychosocial stressor vs. intense combined physical/psychosocial stressor), each of which could contribute to differing cortisol-IM associations. Further systematic investigation of how each of these factors may condition the degree and/or direction of association will be needed; for now, the current findings underline a potential role of “escaped” inflammatory reactivity in the genesis, perpetuation, and/or consequence of affective distress.

Our findings add to existing evidence that prenatal experiences of affective distress—especially the negative affect component common to both depression and anxiety—is characterized by a pro-inflammatory environment in blood and/or saliva (Agranyoni et al., 2025; Christian et al., 2009; Gustafsson et al., 2020). Given the preponderance of null findings for the anxiety- or depression-specific components of affective distress in this sample, it appears this phenotype is linked to affective distress more broadly in this population rather than to specific anxious arousal or anhedonic features. The fact that a greater than expected proportion of our community sample scored in the elevated range for symptoms—especially anhedonic depression—suggests there was sufficient variability to detect such effects, though more targeted research in both clinical and healthy samples will be needed to reach firmer conclusions. Replicating effects in different sociohistorical contexts would also be useful to determine the extent to which the current study's overlap with the worst of the COVID-19 pandemic may have influenced women's levels of distress and/or effects of such distress.

Beyond current symptom levels, our results highlight effects of past mood disorder even in the absence of current clinical-level symptoms on IM hyperactivation/non-recovery. This may speak to genetically based differences, scarring effects, or some combination of these that express themselves in less controlled inflammatory responses to stress. One potential upstream pathway to explore is the woman's own history of caregiving; there is evidence that childhood maltreatment gives rise to a cluster of depressive symptomatology and inflammation that bidirectionally reinforce one another across development (Miller and Cole, 2012), which could produce heightened vulnerability to stress-induced dysregulation during pregnancy. Indeed, research has shown that childhood maltreatment predicts both affective distress and inflammation during pregnancy, with maltreatment history amplifying effects of prenatal affective symptoms on concurrent inflammation (Bublitz et al., 2022; Kleih et al., 2022).

Given links between excess prenatal inflammation and a host of poor birth and child developmental outcomes (e.g., Graham et al., 2018; Miller et al., 2017), it will be critical to better understand the paths leading women to inflammatory hyperreactivity in the face of perinatal stress to make a dent in intergenerational transmission of adversity. Prospective longitudinal designs will be needed to shed light on the predisposing factors (such as childhood maltreatment) and progression of effects during this time—e.g., whether elevated IM activation to stress in women with a history of mood disorder exacerbates distress during pregnancy, whether misaligned HPA axis and IM stress responses represent an early sign of dysregulation contributing to worsening affective distress, or some other sequence. For now, our findings underline the utility of considering both past (diagnostic-level) and current subjective distress in defining an inflammatory stress risk phenotype to target in intergenerational risk prevention efforts.

Based on (non-perinatal) research examining psychological contributors to inflammatory stress reactivity, proximal explanatory mechanisms in risk profiles detected here may include heightened attention to negative stimuli (Maydych et al., 2018), anger and/or anxiety responses to stressors (see La Fratta et al., 2018), and a more generalized stance of cynicism or hopelessness (Sjögren et al., 2006). Further investigation of proposed mechanisms via observational and intervention designs will be needed to help clarify their relevance and malleability in pregnant women at risk for disorder. Results of this study offer a starting point to guide what we expect to see in pregnant women confronting psychosocial stressors, as well as what to flag as potential risk indices and attempt to ameliorate through intervention. In particular, this study supports screening to identify women with either a history of significant mood-related distress or experiencing current (even subclinical) general affective distress, and providing resources that can help them to regulate inflammatory stress. Based on previous work in non-perinatal samples, mindfulness-based training may offer a particularly promising route by which to impact both HPA axis and IM stress systems (e.g., Hoge et al., 2018).

Limitations of this study should be born in mind to inform next steps in this line of research. As noted earlier, the degree to which individual salivary IM measures reflect systemic inflammation remains in question; even though research supports correlations between blood and salivary measures especially under acute stress conditions (e.g., La Fratta et al., 2018), more comprehensive investigations sampling multiple tissues must be done to clarify converging vs. distinct roles of each and their relative utility predicting health outcomes, particularly during the perinatal period. It is worth noting that CRP—the salivary IM most consistently related to serum levels (Riis et al., 2020)—showed robust associations with affective distress in this study (i.e., higher reactivity, divergence from cortisol related to past mood diagnosis; higher levels related to current anhedonic and total symptoms). This strengthens the case that the present findings speak to larger (systemic) inflammatory processes, though systematic testing will be required to confirm.

Similarly, it would be useful to further probe the specificity and predictive utility of IM response to the type of (standardized, performance-based psychosocial) stressor examined here as compared to others. Although the psychosocial threat focus of the TSST could have made it more likely to elicit both cortisol and IM responses concurrently, the absence of research examining these systems responding to different types of stressors makes it difficult to determine whether the patterns observed here would be stronger, weaker, or different in nature under other stress conditions. Given the key role of supportive social relationships in risk for perinatal distress (e.g., Bedaso et al., 2021; Kay et al., 2024), it may be particularly instructive to examine pregnant women responding to interpersonal/relational stressors to uncover processes giving rise to affective disorder in this population.

Finally, the scope of the current investigation was necessarily constrained to focus on providing an initial description of cortisol-IM stress response coordination and differences associated with current psychological adjustment. To gain a fuller picture of the conditions giving rise to health-relevant profiles and consequences thereof, studies addressing a variety of conditions influencing pregnant women's stress responding—e.g., their own experiences of early care, current social relational risks and buffers, proximal factors such as stress appraisals—as well as downstream birth/infant outcomes should be conducted. Based on previous evidence that prenatal inflammation mediates effects of maternal depression on infant negative affect (Gustafsson et al., 2020), a better understanding of these paths could be critical in efforts to mitigate intergenerational transmission of risk for affective disorder. By uncovering links between multiple indices of pregnant women's affective distress and combined HPA-inflammatory stress response profiles, the present study represents an important step in this direction.
