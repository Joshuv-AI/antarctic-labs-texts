Second order free cumulants: product, commutator, and anti-commutator
Daniel Munoz George, Daniel Perales

Abstract
. Given two second order free random variables a a and b b , we study the second order free cumulants of their product a ​ b ab , their commutator a ​ b − b ​ a ab-ba , and their anti-commutator a ​ b + b ​ a ab+ba . Given a a and b b two second order freely independent random variables, we provide formulas to compute each of the cumulants ( κ n , m a ​ b ) n , m ≥ 1 (\kappa_{n,m}^{ab})_{n,m\geq 1} , ( κ n , m a ​ b − b ​ a ) n , m ≥ 1 (\kappa_{n,m}^{ab-ba})_{n,m\geq 1} , and ( κ n , m a ​ b + b ​ a ) n , m ≥ 1 (\kappa_{n,m}^{ab+ba})_{n,m\geq 1} in terms of the individual cumulants ( κ n a ) n ≥ 1 (\kappa_{n}^{a})_{n\geq 1} , ( κ n , m a ) n , m ≥ 1 (\kappa_{n,m}^{a})_{n,m\geq 1} , ( κ n b ) n ≥ 1 (\kappa_{n}^{b})_{n\geq 1} , and ( κ n , m b ) n , m ≥ 1 (\kappa_{n,m}^{b})_{n,m\geq 1} . 1.
Introduction

Free probability is a useful tool to study large random matrices. By now there is a extensive list of results that confirm that independent random matrices tend to free random variables when the size of the matrix is large
[
Voi91
,
VDN92
,
Voi98
]
. Second order freeness, initiated in
[
MS06
]
helps to get a more detailed study, by extending the relation between random matrices and free probability theory from the level of expectations to the level of
fluctuations. Since then, different works have shown that some classical ensembles of independent random matrices become second order free when the size of the matrix tends to infinity. In
[
MS06
]
, it was shown that orthogonal families of Gaussian and Wishart random matrices are asymptotically free of second order. In the same direction, it was shown in
[
MŚS07
]
that Haar unitary and independent random matrices with a second order distribution are second order free. Asymptotic second order freeness is not generally satisfied for real ensembles of random matrices, this motivated the introduction of
real second order freeness
in
[
Red14
]
. Later, it was shown in
[
MM13
]
that independent and Haar orthogonal random matrices are asymptotically real second order free.

An important question is how one can understand the distribution of polynomial expression
P(A,B)
of two independent random matrices
A
and
B
, in terms of the distributions of
A
and
B
itself. Even in the limiting case, that is usually simpler, this is not an easy question. In the limit the problem amounts to study the distribution of
P(a,b)
in terms of the distributions of
a
and
b
, two free random variables.
The basic cases of addition
a+b
, and multiplication
ab
, are very well understood. However, other simple polynomials, such as the commutator
i(ab-ba)
, or the anti-commutator
ab+ba
are considerably harder.

From a combinatorial point of view, the problem reduces to computing the free cumulants of the polynomial expression
\left(\kappa_{n}^{{}_{P(a,b)}}\right)_{n\geq 1}
in terms of the free cumulants of each of the variables,
(\kappa_{n}^{a})_{n\geq 1}
and
(\kappa_{n}^{b})_{n\geq 1}
. The free cumulants were introduced by Speicher
[
Spe94
]
as functionals that linearize the addition:
\kappa_{n}^{a+b}=\kappa_{n}^{a}+\kappa_{n}^{b}
for all
n
. Since then, free cumulants have been also used to study the multiplication
[
NS96
]
, the commutator
[
NS98
]
, and the anti-commutator
[
Per23
]
. In the case of the anti-commutator, the formula requires the study of graphs that are associated to each partition.

Definition 1.1

(
[
Per23
, Definition 1.1]
)
.

Given a set partition
\pi
of
[2n]:=\{1,2,\dots,2n\}
one can associate a graph
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
, where the vertices are blocks of
\pi
, and for

we draw an undirected edge between the block containing element

and the block containing element
2k
. We allow for loops and multiple edges, thus

has exactly

Letting

be the set of non-crossing partitions of
[2n]
, we denote

\mathcal{X}_{2n}:=\{\pi\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{C}(2n):\mathcal{G}_{\pi}\mbox{ is connected and bipartite}\}.

A partition
\pi\in\mathcal{X}_{2n}
has a natural
bipartite decomposition

\pi=\pi^{\prime}\sqcup\pi^{\prime\prime}
. Denoting by
V_{1}
the block of
\pi
which contains the number
1
, we have that
\pi^{\prime}
consists of the blocks of
\pi
which are at even distance from
V_{1}
in the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
, while
\pi^{\prime\prime}
consists of the blocks of
\pi
which are at odd distance from
V_{1}
in that graph.

With this notation in hand,
[

, Theorem1.4]
expresses the cumulants of the anti-commutator

in terms of the cumulants of each
a
and
b

(1)

\kappa_{n}^{ab+ba}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\mathcal{X}_{2n}\\
\pi=\pi^{\prime}\sqcup\pi^{\prime\prime}\end{subarray}}\left(\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}}^{a}\kappa_{\pi^{\prime\prime}}^{b}+\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}}^{b}\kappa_{\pi^{\prime\prime}}^{a}\right),\qquad\text{for }n\geq 1,

where for a variable
c
and partition
\sigma
we use the notation

(2)

\kappa_{\sigma}^{c}:=\prod_{V\in\sigma}\kappa_{|V|}^{c}.

Understanding the second order distribution of polynomial expressions
P(a,b)
of two second order free random variables
a
and
b
is even more challenging. In the last of a series of papers concluding with
[
CMŚS07
]
, the authors introduced the concept of the second (and even higher) order cumulants. In the second order framework, the question is how to compute the second order free cumulants of the polynomial
\left(\kappa_{n,m}^{{}_{P(a,b)}}\right)_{n,m\geq 1}
in terms of the individual free cumulants sequences
(\kappa_{n}^{a})_{n\geq 1}
,
(\kappa_{n,m}^{a})_{n,m\geq 1}
,
(\kappa_{n}^{b})_{n\geq 1}
and
(\kappa_{n,m}^{b})_{n,m\geq 1}
. Notice that the problem is already more complicated, as one may need to involve the free cumulants of first order. Some of the few results in this direction include a formula for the second order cumulants of products as arguments
[
MST09
]
, and a computation of the second order cumulants of even and
R
-diagonal variables
[
AM23
]
.

The goal of the present work, is to generalize (
1
) to the second order. Our study parallels the one done in
[
Per23
]
for the first order version. Instead of set partitions, the main combinatorial object in second order freeness is the set of non-crossing annular partitioned permutations
PS_{\mathcal{NC}}(n,m):=S_{\mathcal{NC}}(n,m)\cup S_{\mathcal{NC}}^{\prime}(n,m)
, see Definition
2.6
. It is important to keep in mind that every permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
can be seen as a partition
P_{\pi}
by considering the cycles (of its cycle decomposition) as the blocks of the partition.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
S_{2n}
and be the set of permutations of

1.

Given a permutation

A permutation

A partitioned permutation

Remark 1.6
.

One can check that the second sum in Theorem
1.5
vanishes completely, unless
n
and
m
are both even. Notice also that the second sum is cancellation free, as there are only positive signs. This property is basically inherited from the first order case. On the contrary, the first sum in Theorem
1.5
is not cancellation-free, in Section
5.6
we will see how in the computation of
\kappa_{2,1}^{ab-ba}
, one needs to cancel some terms associated to admissible permutations in
\mathcal{A}_{4,2}
.

It is not hard to check that the set
\mathcal{A}_{2n,2m}
of admissible permutations, which indexes the first sum, does not contain any permutations with fixed points (cycles of size 1). Since such terms do not appear in the second sum either, then this means that the right hand side does not contain any terms of the form
\kappa_{1}^{a}
or
\kappa_{1}^{b}
at all. Therefore, the commutator
ab-ba
does not depends on the expected values of
a
and
b
, meaning that one can always consider centered variables.

However, the set
\mathcal{A}_{2n,2m}
of admissible permutations may contain odd cumulants
\kappa_{2n+1}^{a}
or
\kappa_{2n+1}^{b}
with
n\geq 1
. In fact, in Remark
5.9
we will see that the formula for
\kappa_{4,2}^{ab-ba}
already contains a term of the form
8(\kappa_{3}^{a})^{2}(\kappa_{3}^{b})^{2}
that does not cancel. To put things into perspective, recall that in the first order case the formula for the commutator only involves even cumulants. Thus, the dependence of the commutator on odd cumulants is a new phenomenon appearing only in the second order case. Ultimately, this is one the reasons why finding an indexing set in the first sum that does not have cancellations is a challenging task.

Remark 1.8
.

A result in the same direction of Theorem
1.7
was derived in
[
AM23
, Theorem 7.3]
, where the authors compute the second order cumulants of the product of second order free random variables. However they assume the extra hypothesis that the second order cumulants of the individual variables are all
0
, which ultimately leads to a cancellation of the second and third sums in the right hand side of (
5
). In this sense, our result generalizes
[
AM23
, Theorem 7.3]
. A parallel version for higher order cumulants of the product of free variables was derived in
[
CMŚS07
, Theorem 7.9]
. They index the sum over partitioned permutations though, while our version is indexed by non-crossing permutations and their Kreweras complement.

Finally, Theorem
1.7
has two alternative versions where the sums are rather indexed by non-crossing pairings (Lemma
6.3
) and graphs that are either trees or have a single cycle (Lemma
6.4
). Our approach using graphs proves useful to easily compute the second order free cumulants of the product of centered second order variables, see Corollary
6.5
.

As an application, we use our results in the case where the variables are second order free semicircular variables, which are characterized by having all cumulants equal to zero, except for
\kappa_{2}
and

Proposition 1.9
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\varphi,\varphi^{2})
be a second order non-commutative probability space and let
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
be two second order free semicircular variables such that
\kappa_{2}^{a}=\kappa_{2,2}^{a}=\kappa_{2}^{b}=\kappa_{2,2}^{b}=1
. Then the second order cumulants of their anti-commutator are given by

\kappa_{n,m}^{ab+ba}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}2\frac{(n+m-1)!}{(n-1)!(m-1)!}&\text{if }n\text{ and }m\text{ are odd, }\\
2\frac{(n+m-1)!}{(n-1)!(m-1)!}+2nm&\text{if }n\text{ and }m\text{ are even,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

The term
\frac{(n+m-1)!}{(n-1)!(m-1)!}
counts non-crossing pairings with a very specific structure, we leave the precise description of this set to Section
7
. We can also deal with the general case, where the cumulants
\kappa_{2}^{a},\kappa_{2,2}^{a},\kappa_{2}^{b}
, and
\kappa_{2,2}^{b}
have arbitrary values, see Remark
7.1
.

The formula for the commutator is almost the same, except for a sign.

Proposition 1.10
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\varphi,\varphi^{2})
be a second order non-commutative probability space and let
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
be two second order free semicircular variables such that
\kappa_{2}^{a}=\kappa_{2,2}^{a}=\kappa_{2}^{b}=\kappa_{2,2}^{b}=1
. Then the second order cumulants of their commutator are given by

\kappa_{n,m}^{ab-ba}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}-2\frac{(n+m-1)!}{(n-1)!(m-1)!}&\text{if }n\text{ and }m\text{ are odd, }\\

Same as with the anti-commutator, we can also deal with the general case. Finally, the formula for the product, is the following.

Proposition 1.11
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\varphi,\varphi^{2})
be a second order non-commutative probability space and let
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
be two second order free semicircular variables.
Then the second order cumulants of their product are given by

\kappa_{n,m}^{ab}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}n(\kappa_{2}^{a}\kappa_{2}^{b})^{n}&\text{if }n=m,\\

Notice that there are no individual cumulants of second order appearing in this formula.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we discuss the preliminaries on non-crossing objects, namely, non-crossing partitions, permutations and partitioned permutations that concern our results. We also recall the concepts of freeness and cumulants.

2.1.
Partitions

We begin with a brief introduction to the definitions and notations used in this paper regarding partitions of a set. For detailed discussion of all of these concepts the standard reference is
[
NS06
]
. A
partition
\pi
of a finite set
S
is a set of the form
\pi=\{V_{1},\dots,V_{k}\}
where
V_{1},\dots,V_{k}\subset S
are pairwise disjoint non-empty subsets of
S
such that
V_{1}\cup\dots\cup V_{k}=S
. The subsets
V_{1},\dots,V_{k}
are called
blocks
of
\pi
, and we write
\#(\pi)
for number of blocks (in this case
k
). We denote by
\mathcal{P}(S)
the set of all partitions of
S
, and we further write
\mathcal{P}(n)
in the special case where
S=[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
.

Given a partition
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(n)
, we say that
V\in\pi
is an
interval block
if it is of the form
V=\{i,i+1,\dots,i+j\}
for some integers
1\leq i<i+j\leq n
. We further say that
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(n)
is an
interval partition
if all the blocks
V\in\pi
are intervals.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(n)
is a
non-crossing partition
if for every
1\leq i<j<k<l\leq n
such that
i,k
belong to the same block
V
of
\pi
and
j,l
belong to the same block
W
of
\pi
, then it necessarily follows that all
i,j,k,l
are in the same block, namely
V=W
. We will denote by
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{C}(n)
the set of all non-crossing partitions of
[n]
.

We can equip
\mathcal{P}(n)
with a lattice structure using the reverse refinement order
\leq
. For
\pi,\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(n)
, we write “
\pi\leq\sigma
” if every block of
\sigma
is a union of blocks of
\pi
. The maximal element of
\mathcal{P}(n)
with this order is
1_{n}:=\{\{1,\dots,n\}\}
(the partition of
[n]
with only one block), and the minimal element is
0_{n}:=\{\{1\},\{2\},\dots,\{n\}\}
(the partition of
[n]
with
n
blocks). With this order,
(\mathcal{N}\mathcal{C}(n),\leq)
is a sub-poset of
(\mathcal{P}(n),\leq)
.

Definition 2.2
.

Given two partitions
\pi,\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(n)
, the
join of
\pi
and
\sigma
in the lattice of all partitions
, denoted as
\pi\lor\sigma
, is defined as the smallest partition that is bigger than
\pi
and
\sigma
in the inverse refinement order. Given two partitions
\pi,\sigma\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{C}(n)
, the
join of
\pi
and
\sigma
in the lattice of non-crossing partitions
, denoted as
\pi\lor_{\mathcal{NC}}\sigma
, is defined as the smallest non-crossing partition that is bigger than
\pi
and
\sigma
in the reverse refinement order.

Notice that the definition of the join depends on which lattice we are working with. We are only concerned with the lattice
\mathcal{N}\mathcal{C}(n)
, but we will work with a special case where both joins coincide, and in this case we can make use of a explicit description of
\pi\lor\sigma
. In this paper, we are particularly interested in partitions
\pi
such that
\pi\lor I_{2n}=1
for
I_{2n}:=\{\{1,2\},\{3,4\},\dots,\{2n-1,2n\}\}
. Since
I_{2n}
is an interval partition, this means that
\pi\lor\sigma=\pi\lor_{\mathcal{NC}}I_{2n}
(see Exercise 9.43 of
[
NS06
]
). One can rephrase this relation in terms of the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
from Definition
1.1
. Actually, the reason to construct the edges of
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
by joining the blocks containing
2i-1
and
2i
is specifically to store information regarding
\pi\lor I_{2n}
.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
Per23
, Lemma 3.3]
)
.

Fix a partition
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(2n)
. Then
\pi\lor I_{2n}=1_{2n}
if and only if
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
is connected.

2.2.
Permutations

We now briefly discuss permutations and its relation with partitions. This will motivate the introduction of non-crossing permutations which we explore in detail in Subsection
2.3
. We can always regard a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
as a partition by considering the cycles (of its cycle decomposition) as the blocks of the partition. When necessary for a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
we will denote by
P_{\pi}
the corresponding partition. Biane
[
Bia97
]
showed that every permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
satisfies the following inequality

\#(\pi)+\#(\pi^{-1}\gamma_{n})\leq n+1,

where
\gamma_{n}=(1,\dots,n)\in S_{n}
and
\#(\cdot)
denotes the number of cycles of the permutation in its cycle decomposition. This inequality can also be expressed in terms of the length function
|\pi|=:n-\#(\pi)
as

(6)

|\pi|+|\pi^{-1}\gamma_{n}|\geq|\gamma_{n}|.

This means that the length function
|\cdot|
satisfies the triangle inequality. Moreover, the equality holds only if
P_{\pi}\in\mathcal{NC}(n)
. The converse is however not that simple. Given a partition, there might be more than one permutation depending on the cyclic order of the blocks. Thankfully, given a non-crossing partition
\pi
, the only permutation that attains equality in (
6
) is the one whose cycles are the blocks of
\pi
with the elements arranged in increasing order. With this in mind, throughout this paper we will think of non-crossing partition as a non-crossing permutation and vice-versa with the convention that the cycles of the permutation are described by the blocks of the partition with the elements arranged in increased order. This interpretation is very useful in many contexts and provides a natural definition to the notation of annular non-crossing permutations. More explicitly, the inequality (
6
) is actually true for every pair of permutations
\pi,\gamma\in S_{n}
. Consequently, a natural question is for a fixed
\gamma
which permutations
\pi
attain the equality in (
6
). This question has been extensively explored in the context offree probability. The case where the fixed permutation
\gamma
has one cycle was studied in
[
Bia97
]
, when
\gamma=(12\cdots n)
the permutations
\pi
satisfying equality are referred as disk non-crossing permutation. The case where
\gamma
with two cycles was studied in
[
MN04
]
, the
\pi
that attain equality are called annular non-crossing permutations. For the general case where
\gamma
has several cycles, we refer to
[
MS17
, Section 1.8]
. In this paper we are specially interested in two cycles and we will introduce it in detail in Subsection
2.3
.

To finish this subsection let us introduce the Kreweras complement of a non-crossing partition. This concept emerges naturally in our formulas for the cumulants of the product of two free variables.

Definition 2.4

(Kreweras complement)
.

Given
\pi\in\mathcal{NC}(n)
, its
Kreweras comeplemt
,
Kr_{n}(\pi)\in\mathcal{NC}(n)
, is the non-crossing permutation
\pi^{-1}\gamma_{n}
, where
\gamma_{n}=(1,\dots,n)\in S_{n}
.

The map
Kr_{n}:\mathcal{NC}(n)\to\mathcal{NC}(n)
is actually a lattice anti-isomorphism of
\mathcal{NC}(n)
. Another important property of this map is that
\#(Kr_{n}(\pi))=n+1-\#(\pi)
for all
\pi\in\mathcal{NC}(n)
, thus we actually have that
Kr_{n}(1_{n})=0_{n}
and
Kr_{n}(0_{n})=1_{n}
.

In terms of the topology of the non-crossing partitions, the Kreweras complement map has a deeper interpretation, see (
[
NS06
, Exercise 18.25]
). Given
\pi\in\mathcal{NC}(n)
, consider additional numbers
\{1^{\prime},\dots,n^{\prime}\}
. Then, the
Kreweras complement of
\pi
, is the partition
Kr_{n}(\pi)\in\{1^{\prime},\dots,n^{\prime}\}\cong\mathcal{NC}(n)
that is the largest element among those
\sigma\in\mathcal{NC}(\{1^{\prime},\dots,n^{\prime}\})
which have the property that
\pi\cup\sigma\in\mathcal{NC}(\{1,1^{\prime},\dots,n,n^{\prime}\})
. See, for instance, a graphical representation of this in Figure
1
.

Figure 1.
The non-crossing partition
\pi=(1,3,6,7)(4,5)(8,10)(9)
in solid lines and its Kreweras complement
\pi^{-1}\gamma_{10}=(1,2)(3,5)(4)(6)(7,10)(8,9)
in dot lines.

This interpretation of the Kreweras complement is the standard definition one finds in the literature concerning non-crossing partitions. Our definition, however, allows us to extend in a more natural way the concept of Kreweras complement of a disk non-crossing partition to the concept of Kreweras complement of an annular non-crossing permutation. We introduce such a concept at the end of Subsection
2.3
.

2.3.
Partitioned permutations

In free probability in order to define the notion of free cumulants and freeness we make use of the set of non-crossing partitions. In second order, to define the notion of second order cumulants and second order freeness we rather look at non-crossing permutations. Let us give a brief introduction to the definitions and notations used in this paper regarding permutations. For a further explanation we refer to
[
MS17
]
. Given
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
we will extensively use the following special permutation

\gamma_{n,m}:=(1,\dots,n)(n+1,\dots n+m)\in S_{m+n}.

Given
\pi\in S_{m+n}
we say that
\pi
is a
non-crossing annular permutation
if

(1)

\pi\lor\gamma_{n,m}=1_{m+n}
, and

(2)

\#(\pi)+\#(\pi^{-1}\gamma_{n,m})=m+n
.

Here
\lor
is defined as in Subsection
2.1
and the permutation is regarded as a partition by letting the cycles of the permutation to be the blocks of the partition. We denote the set of non-crossing annular permutations as
S_{\mathcal{NC}}(n,m)
. In the spirit of Definition
2.4
we introduce the Kreweras complement in the annular case.

Definition 2.5
.

Given
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}(n,m)
, its
Kreweras complement
is defined as the permutation
Kr_{n,m}(\pi):=\pi^{-1}\gamma_{n,m}
.

Notice that
Kr_{n,m}(\pi)
is again an annular non-crossing permutation. Topologically, it has the same interpretation as in the first order, if we include
\pi
and
Kr_{n,m}(\pi)
in the same annulus alternating the numbers (see Figure
2
) then we still get a annular non-crossing permutation. Moreover, given
\pi
, then
Kr_{n,m}(\pi)
is the largest annular non-crossing permutation with this property.

Figure 2.
The non-crossing permutation
\pi=(2,3,8,6,1)(4,7)(5)\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}(5,3)
in solid lines and its Kreweras complement
\pi^{-1}\gamma_{5,3}=(1)(2)(3,7)(4,5,6)(8)
in dot lines.

A
partitioned permutation
is a pair
(\mathcal{U},\pi)\in\mathcal{P}(n+m)\times S_{n+m}
where any cycle of
\pi
is contained in a block of
\mathcal{U}
. We will focus on the following subset:

Definition 2.6
.

The pair
(\mathcal{U},\pi)\in\mathcal{PS}(n+m)
is a
non-crossing annular partitioned permutation
if it is of one of the two following types.

(1)

Type 1:

\pi\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}(n,m)
and
\mathcal{U}=P_{\pi}.
We will abuse notation and denote this set simply by
S_{\mathcal{NC}}(n,m)
, associating
(P_{\pi},\pi)
with
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}(n,m)
.

(2)

Type 2:

\pi=\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\in\mathcal{NC}(n)\times\mathcal{NC}(m)
and every cycle of
\pi
is a block of
\mathcal{U}
except for one block of
\mathcal{U}
which is the union of two cycles
A,B\in\pi
with
A\in\pi_{1}
and
B\in\pi_{2}
. We denote this set by
S_{\mathcal{NC}}^{\prime}(n,m)
.

We denote by
PS_{\mathcal{NC}}(n,m):=S_{\mathcal{NC}}(n,m)\cup S_{\mathcal{NC}}^{\prime}(n,m)
the whole set of non-crossing annular partitioned permutations.

We should mention that the original definition of non-crossing annular partitioned permutation has a more algebraic motivation, coming from an inequality similar to (
6
). One first need to define a length function on the set of partitioned permutations that satisfies the triangle inequality. Then a partitioned permutation
(\mathcal{U},\pi)
is non-crossing if we have equality in the triangle inequality and
\mathcal{U}\lor P_{\pi^{-1}\gamma_{n,m}}=1_{n,m}
. For more details we refer the reader to
[
CMŚS07
, Section 4-5]
. The description of non-crossing annular partitioned permutation that we use here as a Definition
2.6
was proved in
[
CMŚS07
, Proposition 5.11]
.

To finish this subsection let us introduce the following result concerning non-crossing permutations. Given a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
and a set
A\subset[n]
we let
\pi|_{A}
to be the permutation of the elements of
A
whose cycles are the cycles
C\cap A
where
C
is a cycle of
\pi
and we respect the cyclic order of the elements in
C
. Given two permuations
\pi,\sigma\in S_{n}
we say that
\sigma\leq\pi
if every cycle of
\sigma
is contained in a cycle of
\pi
and for each cycle
C
of
\pi
the permutation
\sigma|_{C}
is non-crossing with respect to
C
, that is

\#(\sigma|_{C})+\#(\sigma|_{C}^{-1}C)=|C|+1.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\pi,\sigma\in S_{n}
. Then
\pi\leq\sigma
if and only if
|\pi|+|\pi^{-1}\sigma|=|\sigma|
.

Proof.

Suppose
\pi\leq\sigma
, let
C_{1}\cdots C_{w}
be the cycle decomposition of
\sigma
and let
\pi_{i}
be the restriction of
\pi
to each
C_{i}
. Then,

\#(\pi_{i})+\#(\pi_{i}^{-1}C_{i})=|C_{i}|+1.

Summing over
i
gives,

\#(\pi)+\#(\pi^{-1}\sigma)=n+\#(\sigma),

hence
|\pi|+|\pi^{-1}\sigma|=|\sigma|
. The converse follows directly from
[
MST09
, Lemma 8]
.
∎

2.4.
Free cumulants

A
non-commutative probability space
is a pair
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
, where
\mathcal{A}
is a unital algebra over
\mathbb{C}
and
\varphi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{C}
is a linear functional, such that
\varphi(1_{\mathcal{A}})=1
. The
n
-th multivariate moment
is the multilinear functional
\varphi_{n}:\mathcal{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
, such that
\varphi_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}):=\varphi(a_{1}\cdots a_{n})\in\mathbb{C}
, for elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathcal{A}
. In this framework, Voiculescu’s original definition of freeness for random variables explains how to compute the mixed moments in terms of the moments of each variable (see
[
VDN92
]
). Since this definition is out of the scope of this paper, we will restrict our presentation to the characterization of freeness using free cumulants.

Notation 2.8
.

Given a family of multilinear functionals
\{f_{m}:\mathcal{A}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}\}_{m\geq 1}
and a partition
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(n)
, we define
f_{\pi}:\mathcal{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
to be the map

f_{\pi}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}):=\prod_{V\in\pi}f_{|V|}(a_{V}),\qquad\forall a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathcal{A},

where for every block
V:=\{{v_{1}},\cdots,{v_{k}}\}
of
\pi
(such that
{v_{1}}<\cdots<{v_{k}}
are in natural order) we use the notation
f_{|V|}(a_{V}):=f_{k}(a_{v_{1}},\ldots,a_{v_{k}})
.

With this notation in hand we can define the free cumulants.

Definition 2.9

(Free cumulants)
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
be a non-commutative probability space. The
free cumulants
are the family of multilinear functionals
\{\kappa_{n}:\mathcal{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}\}_{n\geq 1}
recursively defined by the following formula:

(7)

\varphi_{n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{C}(n)}\kappa_{\pi}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}).

If we are just working with one variable, and all the arguments are the same
a_{1}=a_{2}=\dots=a_{n}=a
, then we adopt the simpler notation
\kappa_{n}^{a}:=\kappa_{n}(a,\dots,a)
and
\kappa_{\pi}^{a}:=\kappa_{\pi}(a,\dots,a)

Remark 2.10
.

Cumulants are well defined since the right-hand side of the equation contains only one
\kappa_{n}
term and the other terms are monomials of cumulants of smaller sizes. Thus we can recursively define
\kappa_{n}
in terms of
\varphi_{n}
and
\kappa_{n-1},\kappa_{n-2},\dots,\kappa_{1}
.

A
second order non-commutative probability space
is a triple
(\mathcal{A},\varphi,\varphi^{2})
, where
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
is a non-commutative probability space and
\varphi^{2}:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a bilinear functional which is tracial in both arguments and which satisfies

\varphi^{2}(a,1)=0=\varphi^{2}(1,b)\text{ for all }a,b\in\mathcal{A}.

Similarly as in the first order case, the
n,m
-multivariate moment
is the multilinear functional
\varphi_{n,m}:\mathcal{A}^{n}\times\mathcal{A}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, such that
\varphi_{n,m}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n+m}):=\varphi^{2}(a_{1}\cdots a_{n},a_{n+1}\cdots a_{n+m})\in\mathbb{C}
, for elements
a_{1},\dots,a_{n+m}\in\mathcal{A}
. In this setting, one defines second order freeness as a rule to compute the mixed moments in terms of the first and second order moments of the individual variables (see
[
MŚS07
, Definition 2.5]
). For our work we will rely on the equivalent definition of second order freeness as the vanishing of the mixed cumulants which we later introduce.

Notation 2.11
.

Given a family of multilinear functionals
\{f_{m}:\mathcal{A}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}\}_{m\geq 1}
and
\{f_{n,m}:\mathcal{A}^{n}\times\mathcal{A}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}\}_{n,m\geq 1}
and a non-crossing annular partitioned permutation
(\mathcal{U},\pi)\in\mathcal{PS}(n+m)
, we define
f_{(\mathcal{U},\pi)}:\mathcal{A}^{n}\times\mathcal{A}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}
to be the map such that for all
a_{1}\dots,a_{n+m}\in\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle f_{(\mathcal{U},\pi)}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n+m})

\displaystyle:=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\pi\end{subarray}}f_{|V|}(a_{V}),

\displaystyle\text{if }(\mathcal{U},\pi)\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}(n,m),\text{ or}

\displaystyle f_{(\mathcal{U},\pi)}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n+m})

\displaystyle:=f_{|A|,|B|}(a_{A\cup B})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\pi\setminus\{A,B\}\end{subarray}}f_{|V|}(a_{V}),

\displaystyle\text{if }(\mathcal{U},\pi)\in S_{NC}^{\prime}(n,m).

Here
A=(v_{1},\dots,v_{j_{1}})
and
B=(v_{{j_{1}}+1},\dots,v_{j_{1}+j_{2}})
are the two cycles of
\pi
contained in the same block of
\mathcal{U}
as in definition
2.6
and we use the notation

f_{|A|,|B|}(a_{A\cup B}):=f_{j_{1},j_{2}}(a_{v_{1}},\dots,a_{v_{j_{1}+j_{2}}}),

We are now in place to the define the second order free cumulants in a similar way to the free cumulants.

Definition 2.12

(Second order free cumulants)
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\varphi,\varphi^{2})
be a second order non-commutatve probability space. The
second order free cumulants
are the family of multilinear functionals
\{\kappa_{n,m}:\mathcal{A}^{n}\times\mathcal{A}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}\}_{n,m\geq 1}
recursively defined by the following formula:

\displaystyle\varphi_{n,m}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n+m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{(\mathcal{U},\pi)\in\mathcal{PS}_{NC}(n,m)}\kappa_{(\mathcal{U},\pi)}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n+m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{(\mathcal{U},\pi)\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}(n,m)}\kappa_{(\mathcal{U},\pi)}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n+m})+\sum_{(\mathcal{U},\pi)\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}^{\prime}(n,m)}\kappa_{(\mathcal{U},\pi)}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n+m}).

Notice that in the first term of the last line, the sum runs over pairs
(P_{\pi},\pi)
with
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}(n,m)
, thus

\kappa_{(P_{\pi},\pi)}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n+m})=\kappa_{\pi}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n+m})

recovers only first order free cumulants which we simply call free cumulants. On the other hand, the second term runs over partitions that have only one block of
\mathcal{U}
which is a union of two cycles of
\pi
and therefore
\kappa_{(\mathcal{U},\pi)}(a_{1},\dots,\allowbreak a_{n+m})
is the product of a single second order free cumulant and free cumulants. Further, the unique pair
(1_{n+m},\gamma_{n,m})\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}^{\prime}(n,m)
contributes the second order free cumulant
\kappa_{n,m}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n+m})
while all the rest of pairs contribute cumulants of smaller sizes. Hence, as in the first order case, the second order cumulants are well defined.

Theorem 2.13

(Vanishing of mixed cumulants, see
[
NS06
, Lecture 11]
)
.

Given a non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
and
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
two random variables. Then the following two statements are equivalent:

1.

a
and
b
are free.

2.

Every mixed cumulant vanishes. Namely, for every
n\geq 2
and
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\{a,b\}
which are not all equal, we have that
\kappa_{n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=0
.

Theorem 2.14

(Vanishing of mixed second order cumulants, see
[
CMŚS07
, Section 7.3]
)
.

Given a second order non-commutative probability space
(\mathcal{A},\varphi,\varphi^{2})
and
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
two random variables. Then the following statements are equivalent:

1.

a
and
b
are second order free.

2.

Every mixed cumulant vanishes. Namely, for every
n\geq 2
and
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\{a,b\}
which are not all equal, we have that
\kappa_{n}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=0
and for every

.

Finally, when working with cumulants whose entries have products of the underlying algebra
\mathcal{A}
, there is an efficient formula that allows us to write this cumulant as a sum over cumulants with more entries, where the products are now separated into different entries. The general formula was found in
[

]
and is known as the products as arguments formula. Here we will just use a particular case.

Theorem 2.15

(Products as arguments formula)
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
be a non-commutative probability space and fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{2n}\in\mathcal{A}
be random variables, and consider

the unique interval pair partition. Then we have that

(8)

\kappa_{n}(a_{1}a_{2},a_{3}a_{4},\dots,a_{2n-1}a_{2n})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\mathcal{N}\mathcal{C}(2n)\\
\pi\lor I_{2n}=1_{2n}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{\pi}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4},\dots,a_{2n-1},a_{2n}).

For the second order free cumulants, a formula for the cumulants of products was also derived in
[
MST09
]
. Here we will use a particular case.

Theorem 2.16

(Product as argument for second order free cumulants)
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\varphi,\varphi^{2})
be a second order non-commutative probability space and fix
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{2n+2m}\in\mathcal{A}
be random variables and consider the permutation
\gamma:=(1,\dots,2n)(2n+1,\dots,2n+2m)\in S_{2n+2m}
. Then we have that

(9)

\kappa_{n,m}(a_{1}a_{2},\dots,a_{2n+2m-1}a_{2n+2m})=\sum_{(\mathcal{U},\pi)\in\mathcal{PS}_{NC}(2n,2m)}\kappa_{(\mathcal{U},\pi)}(a_{1},\dots,a_{2n+2m}),

where the sum is over all pairs
(\mathcal{U},\pi)\in\mathcal{PS}_{NC}(2n,2m)
such that the cycles of
\pi^{-1}\gamma
separates even numbers, that is, no cycle of
\pi^{-1}\gamma
has two even numbers.

One may notice that the conditions in (
8
) and (
9
) look different. However, let us recall that any non-crossing partition can be regarded as a permutation. In this setting, it turns out that the condition
\pi\lor I_{2n}=1_{2n}
is equivalent to
\pi^{-1}\gamma_{2n}
separates even numbers where
\gamma_{2n}=(1,\dots,2n)\in S_{n}
, see
[
MST09
, Lemma 14]
. This explains how both conditions are related.

3.
Anti-commutator

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.3
. The approach is to adapt the proof of formula (
1
) to the second order setting. Thus, throughout this section it is important to keep in mind that the proof of (
1
) required the use of products as arguments formula and the multilinearity of the cumulants, and also relies on two crucial properties

(P1)

\pi\lor I_{2n}=1_{2n}
if and only if
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
is connected.

(P2)

If there exist
\varepsilon\in\{1,*\}^{n}
such that
\{A(\varepsilon),B(\varepsilon)\}\geq\pi
, then
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
is bipartite.

Through this section we are going to fix two free random variables
a,b
, and natural numbers
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
. Our ultimate goal is to describe the second order
(n,m)
-cumulant of the anti-commutator,
\kappa_{n,m}^{ab+ba}
, in terms of the cumulants of
a
and
b
. By multilinearity of the cumulants this amounts to study
(n,m)
-cumulants with entries given by
ab
or
ba
. To keep track of this kind of expressions we use the notation introduced in
[
Per23
, Notation 3.1]
.

Notation 3.1
.

Given non-commutative variables
a,b
, we use the notation
(ab)^{1}:=ab
and
(ab)^{*}:=ba
. Given a
n
-tuple
\varepsilon\in\{1,*\}^{n}
, we denote by
(ab)^{\varepsilon}:=((ab)^{\varepsilon(1)},(ab)^{\varepsilon(2)},\dots,(ab)^{\varepsilon(n)})
the
n
-tuple with entries
ab
or
ba
dictated by the entries of
\varepsilon
. Furthermore, we will denote by
(a,b)^{\varepsilon}
the
2n
-tuple obtained from separating the
a
’s from the
b
’s in the
n
-tuple
(ab)^{\varepsilon}
. To keep track of the entries in
(a,b)^{\varepsilon}
that contain an
a
we use the notation

A(\varepsilon):=\{2i-1|1\leq i\leq n,\ \varepsilon(i)=1\}\cup\{2i|1\leq i\leq n,\ \varepsilon(i)=*\}.

Then the entries in
(a,b)^{\varepsilon}
that contain a
b
are given by

B(\varepsilon):=[2n]\backslash A(\varepsilon)
.

For example, if we consider
\varepsilon=(1,*,*,1,*,1)\in\{1,*\}^{6}
, then
(ab)^{\varepsilon}=(ab,ab,ba,ab,ba,ab)
and if we split each entry we get

(a,b)^{\varepsilon}=(a,b,a,b,b,a,a,b,b,a,a,b).

This means that

A(\varepsilon)=\{1,3,6,7,10,11\},\qquad\text{and}\qquad B(\varepsilon)=\{2,4,5,8,9,12\}.

Using the products as entries formula together with the vanishing of mixed cumulants, we can easily rewrite the
(n,m)
-cumulant of the anti-commutator.

Proposition 3.2
.

The second order free cumulants of the anti-commutator
ab+ba
of two second order free random variables
a,b
satisfy the following formula for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
:

(10)

\kappa_{n,m}^{ab+ba}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathcal{U},\pi)\in\mathcal{PS}_{NC}(2n,2m),\\
\pi^{-1}\gamma\text{ sep. even}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varepsilon\in\{1,*\}^{n+m}\\
\{A(\varepsilon),B(\varepsilon)\}\geq\mathcal{U}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{(\mathcal{U},\pi)}((a,b)^{\varepsilon}).

Here, we use the notation
\gamma:=(1,\dots,2n)(2n+1,\dots,2n+2m)\in S_{2n+2m}
.

Proof.

For a fixed
\varepsilon\in\{1,*\}^{m+n}
, products as arguments formula for second order cumulants (
9
) asserts that

\kappa_{n}((ab)^{\varepsilon})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathcal{U},\pi)\in\mathcal{PS}_{NC}(2n,2m),\\
\pi^{-1}\gamma\text{ sep. even}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{(\mathcal{U},\pi)}((a,b)^{\varepsilon}),

where
\pi^{-1}\gamma
sep. even means that there are no two even numbers in a cycle of
\pi^{-1}\gamma
.

Therefore, if we sum over all possible
\varepsilon\in\{1,*\}^{n}
we get that

\displaystyle\kappa_{n,m}(ab+ba,\dots,ab+ba)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\varepsilon\in\{1,*\}^{n+m}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathcal{U},\pi)\in\mathcal{PS}_{NC}(2n,2m),\\
\pi^{-1}\gamma\text{ sep. even}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{(\mathcal{U},\pi)}((a,b)^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathcal{U},\pi)\in\mathcal{PS}_{NC}(2n,2m),\\
\pi^{-1}\gamma\text{ sep. even}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\varepsilon\in\{1,*\}^{n+m}}\kappa_{(\mathcal{U},\pi)}((a,b)^{\varepsilon}),

where in the second equality we just changed the order of the sums. Finally, since
a
and
b
are second order free, every mixed free cumulant and second order free cumulant will vanish. Thus we require that
\{A(\varepsilon),B(\varepsilon)\}\geq\mathcal{U}
in order to get
\kappa_{(\mathcal{U},\pi)}((a,b)^{\varepsilon})\neq 0
.
∎

A key idea is that the right-hand side of (
10

\varepsilon
satisfying
\{A(\varepsilon),B(\varepsilon)\}\geq\mathcal{U}
for a fixed partitioned permutation
(\mathcal{U},\pi)
.

Unless otherwise stated, in the remaining of the paper we will use the notation

Proposition 3.4
.

If
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}(2n,2m)
is such that

separates even elements, then
P_{\pi}\vee I=\boldsymbol{1}

Proof.

Suppose

[2m]

and

P_{\pi}\leq P_{\pi}\vee I\leq\mathcal{V}
in either we would have each cycle of
\pi

[2n]
or
[2m]
which is a contradiction as

Let
\gamma_{A}:=\gamma|_{A}

\gamma_{B}:=\gamma|_{B}

\gamma_{A}

[

\pi|_{A}\in\mathcal{NC}(\gamma_{A})

[

\pi|_{A}\in\left(\mathcal{NC}(C_{1})\times\mathcal{NC}(C_{2})\right)\sqcup S_{\mathcal{NC}}(C_{1},C_{2})

\#(\pi|_{A})+\#(\pi|_{A}^{-1}\gamma_{A})=|A|+1,

\#(\pi|_{A})+\#(\pi|_{A}^{-1}\gamma_{A})=|A|+2,

\#(\pi|_{A})+\#(\pi|_{A}^{-1}\gamma_{A})=|A|,

and

So we conclude that

\#(\pi)+\#(\pi^{-1}\gamma_{A}\gamma_{B})\in\{2n+2m+1,2n+2m+2,2n+2m\}.

On the other hand, since each block

\gamma_{A}\gamma_{B}=\gamma(u_{1},u_{2})(u_{2},u_{3})\cdots(u_{r}u_{r+1})(v_{1},v_{2})(v_{2},v_{3})\cdots(v_{s},v_{s+1}),

into two cycles, one that contains the elements of

while the cycle that contains the elements of

\gamma_{B}
(which might be empty in which case we take no transpositions). Similarly when multiplying by the transpositions

\gamma_{A}
and
\gamma_{B}

\displaystyle\#(\pi^{-1}\gamma_{A}\gamma_{B})

\displaystyle=\#(\pi^{-1}\gamma(u_{1},u_{2})(u_{2},u_{3})\cdots(u_{r},u_{r+1})(v_{1},v_{2})(v_{2},v_{3})\cdots(v_{s},v_{s+1}))

By the observed before it means the unique possible solution is

Remark 3.5

(Bipartite graphs)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V_{\mathcal{G}},E_{\mathcal{G}})
be an undirected graph (where loops and multiedges are allowed), we say that
\mathcal{G}
is
bipartite
if there exist a partition
\{V^{\prime}_{\mathcal{G}},V^{\prime\prime}_{\mathcal{G}}\}
of the set of vertices
V_{\mathcal{G}}
such that there are no edges
E_{\mathcal{G}}
connecting two vertices in
V^{\prime}_{\mathcal{G}}
or connecting two vertices in
V^{\prime\prime}_{\mathcal{G}}
. In other words, all vertices connect a vertex in
V^{\prime}_{\mathcal{G}}
with a vertex in
V^{\prime\prime}_{\mathcal{G}}
.

Moreover, if
\mathcal{G}
is connected and bipartite, then the bipartition
\{V^{\prime}_{\mathcal{G}},V^{\prime\prime}_{\mathcal{G}}\}
is unique (up to the permutation
\{V^{\prime\prime}_{\mathcal{G}},V^{\prime}_{\mathcal{G}}\}
). This is because once we identify a vertex
v\in V^{\prime}_{\mathcal{G}}
, then the set of any other vertex
u\in V_{\mathcal{G}}
is determined by its distance to
v
. If the distance is even, then
u\in V^{\prime}_{\mathcal{G}}
, and if the distance is odd, then
u\in V^{\prime\prime}_{\mathcal{G}}
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}(2n,2m)
be such that
\pi^{-1}\gamma
separates even elements. Assume that there exists an
\varepsilon\in\{1,*\}^{n+m}
such that
\{A(\varepsilon),B(\varepsilon)\}\geq\pi
, then
\pi\in\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
. Moreover if
\pi\in\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
there are exactly two tuples
\varepsilon\in\{1,*\}^{n+m}
satisfying
\{A(\varepsilon),B(\varepsilon)\}\geq\pi
. Furthermore, these tuples are completely opposite, that is, they do not coincide in any entry.

Proof.

The existence of an
\varepsilon\in\{1,*\}^{n}
such that
\{A(\varepsilon),B(\varepsilon)\}\geq\pi
implies that we can write
\pi=\{A_{1},\dots,A_{r},B_{1},\dots,B_{s}\}
such that
A_{i}\subset A(\varepsilon)
for
i=1,\dots,r
and
B_{j}\subset B(\varepsilon)
for
j=1,\dots,s
. If we consider
V^{\prime}=\{A_{1},\dots,A_{r}\}
and
V^{\prime\prime}=\{B_{1},\dots,B_{s}\}
, then
\{V^{\prime},V^{\prime\prime}\}
is a bipartition of
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
. Indeed, by construction of
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
, if
e\in E_{\mathcal{G}_{\pi}}
, then
e
connects the blocks containing elements
2k-1
and
2k
for some
k=1,\dots,n
. However, by definition of
A(\varepsilon)
and
B(\varepsilon)
, one must contain
2k-1
while the other contains
2k
. Thus
e
connects vertices from different sets and
\{V^{\prime},V^{\prime\prime}\}
is actually a bipartition of
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
. Since
\pi^{-1}\gamma
separates even by assumption, we conclude that
\pi\in\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
.

Given
\pi\in\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
, from Proposition
3.4
we obtain that
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
is connected. By definition of
\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
we also know that
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
is bipartite. Thus Remark
3.5
asserts that there exist a unique bipartition
\{\pi^{\prime},\pi^{\prime\prime}\}
of the vertices of
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
(blocks of
\pi
), say
\pi^{\prime}=\{V_{1},\dots,V_{r}\}
and
\pi^{\prime\prime}=\{W_{1},\dots,W_{s}\}
, where
V_{1}
contains the element 1. Then there are only two options,
A(\varepsilon)=V_{1}\cup\dots\cup V_{r}
or
A(\varepsilon)=W_{1}\cup\dots\cup W_{s}
. The set
A(\varepsilon)
clearly determines
\varepsilon
, since
\varepsilon(i)=1
if and only if
2i-1\in A(\varepsilon)
. So there are two possible
\varepsilon
. Furthermore, since
(V_{1}\cup\dots\cup V_{r})\cap(W_{1}\cup\dots\cup W_{s})=\emptyset
, then the two possible
\varepsilon
do not coincide in any entry.
∎

From the last section, it is clear that even for small values of

is not simple. The goal of this section is to further understand the permutations in this set. We will do this using three different approaches, first we directly study the partitions
\pi
in
\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
, then we study them through their Kreweras complement
\pi^{-1}\gamma

4.1.
\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
and connected graphs

Let us recall that in the first order version of our main result the sum is indexed by connected graphs, or equivalently, by
(P1)
,
\pi\lor I_{2n}=1_{2n}
. The latter is also equivalent to the condition that
\pi^{-1}\gamma_{2n}
separates even, see Lemma
[
MST09
, Lemma 14]
. In the second order case, this is no longer true, in Lemma
4.2
below we explicitly find its equivalent statement in the annulus case. By Proposition
3.4
we know that if
\pi^{-1}\gamma
separates even, this implies that
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
is connected, but the converse is not true. Naturally, one might wonder how small is this set compared to the set of connected graphs. To answer this question, in this section we prove that every
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}(2n,2m)
such that
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
is connected must be of one of the following two types:

(C1)

\pi^{-1}\gamma
separates even, or

(C2)

\pi^{-1}\gamma
separates even except two even number
r,s
where
r\in[2n]
and
s\in[2m]
.

This provides a better understanding of the possible non-crossing pairings for which the graph is connected. Recall that we use the notation
I
,
\boldsymbol{1}
and
\gamma
from (
11
), (
12
), and (
13
). Further, for any two permutations
\pi,\sigma\in S_{2n+2m}
we may use the notation

, where

is the join in the lattice
\mathcal{P}(2n+2m)
as in Definition

.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}(2n,2m)
such that

. Then there is no
r,s
even in the same cycle of
\gamma
such that both are in the same cycle of
\pi^{-1}\gamma
.

Proof.

Suppose there exist
r,s
in the same cycle of
\gamma
and the same cycle of
\pi^{-1}\gamma
. By Lemma
2.7
it follows that
|(r,s)|+|(r,s)\pi^{-1}\gamma|=|\pi^{-1}\gamma|.
Thus

\displaystyle|\pi|+|\pi^{-1}\gamma(r,s)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\pi|+|\pi^{-1}\gamma|-|(r,s)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2n+2m-\#(\pi)+2n+2m-\#(\pi^{-1}\gamma)-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2n+2m-1.

We conclude that

\#(\pi)+\#(\pi^{-1}\gamma(r,s))=2n+2m+1.

On the other hand, from
[
MN04
, Equation 2.10]
we know that

\#(\pi)+\#(\pi^{-1}\gamma(r,s))+\#(\gamma(r,s))\leq 2n+2m+2\#(\pi\vee\gamma(r,s)),

so

2n+2m+1+\#(\gamma(r,s))\leq 2n+2m+2\#(\pi\vee\gamma(r,s)).

Recall that from hypothesis,
r,s
are in the same cycle of
\gamma
, so

2n+2m+1+3=2n+2m+1+\#(\gamma(r,s))\leq 2n+2m+2\#(\pi\vee\gamma(r,s)),

from where we conclude

. Finally, it is clear that

must meet at least two clycles of

as otherwise

. Therefore, it must hold that

, but

implies that

, which contradicts

.
∎

if and only if either

separates even or

and

\displaystyle|\pi|+|\pi^{-1}\gamma(r,s)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\pi|+|\pi^{-1}\gamma|-|(r,s)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2n+2m-\#(\pi)+2n+2m-\#(\pi^{-1}\gamma)-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2n+2m-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\gamma(r,s)|.

Thus

4.2.
Understanding the Kreweras complement of

Another way to understand the indexing set

is through its Kreweras complement

. Namely, the set

\mathcal{K}_{2n,2m}:=\{\sigma\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}(2n,2m):\mathcal{G}_{\gamma\sigma^{-1}}\mbox{ is bipartite and }\sigma\text{ separates even}\}.

For the remaining of the paper, we will denote by

(15)

\mathcal{E}:=\{2,4,6,\dots,2n+2m\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{O}:=\{1,3,5,\dots,2n+2m-1\}

the sets of even and odd numbers, respectively.

Proposition 4.3
.

If
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{2n,2m}
has a cycle
C=(c_{1}c_{2}\cdots c_{r})
with only odd numbers
c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{r}\in\mathcal{O}
, then
C
has even size (
r
is even).

Proof.

Let us denote
\pi=\gamma\sigma^{-1}
. Recall from Definition
1.2
that
\pi\in\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
has a natural bipartite decomposition
\pi=\pi^{\prime}\sqcup\pi^{\prime\prime}
where blocks in the same part do not share an edge of
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
. Let us denote
A^{\prime}=\cup\pi^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}=\cup\pi^{\prime\prime}
. Clearly
A^{\prime}\cup A^{\prime\prime}=[2n+2m]
and
A^{\prime}\cap A^{\prime\prime}=\emptyset
. Moreover, for each

, we have that
|A^{\prime}\cap\{2i-1,2i\}|=1=|A^{\prime\prime}\cap\{2i-1,2i\}|
. In other words, one element of
\{2i-1,2i\}
is in
A^{\prime}
and the other is in
A^{\prime\prime}
. In particular,

\gamma(A^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{O})=A^{\prime\prime}\cap\mathcal{E},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\gamma(A^{\prime\prime}\cap\mathcal{O})=A^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{E}.

On the other hand, by how
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}
are constructed, we also know that
\pi(A^{\prime})=A^{\prime}
and
\pi(A^{\prime\prime})=A^{\prime\prime}
. Equivalently

\pi^{-1}(A^{\prime})=A^{\prime},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\pi^{-1}(A^{\prime\prime})=A^{\prime\prime}.

Putting these two together, we obtain that
\sigma=\pi^{-1}\gamma
satisfies

(16)

\sigma(A^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{O})\subset A^{\prime\prime},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma(A^{\prime\prime}\cap\mathcal{O})\subset A^{\prime}.

In particular, if
C=(c_{1}c_{2}\dots c_{r})
is a cycle of
\sigma
with only odd elements, then the elements of
C
alternate between
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}
. Namely
c_{2j+1}\in A^{\prime}
and
c_{2j}\in A^{\prime\prime}
or viceversa (
c_{2j+1}\in A^{\prime\prime}
and
c_{2j}\in A^{\prime}
). In any case, since
c_{r}
and
c_{1}=\sigma(c_{r})
belong to different sets, we conclude that

is even. Thus, the cycles of
\sigma
containing only odd elements are of even size.
∎

\gamma^{\prime}=\gamma\tau

\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{NC}(\gamma^{\prime})
. That is,

\#(\pi^{\prime})+\#(\pi^{\prime-1}\gamma)=2n+2m+1.

In the last equality we use that
u
and
v
are in the same cycle of
\pi
so that
\#(\pi^{\prime})=\#(\pi)+1
. Once we are back in the disk case (i.e. the permutation
\gamma^{\prime}
has only once cycle) then we are able to use all the results already obtained in
[
Per23
]
. In particular,
[
Per23
, Proposition 1.2]
shows that the cycles of
\pi^{\prime-1}\gamma^{\prime}
with only odd numbers determine the cycles of
\mathcal{G}_{\pi^{\prime}}
. Note that the last claim follows directly from the fact that
\gamma^{\prime}
is a permutation that has only even numbers at its even positions and odd numbers at its odd positions. In addition, the edges that join a number at an odd position
d
with
\gamma^{\prime}(d)
are still the same edges
(1,2),\dots,(2n+2m-1,2n+2m)
. Moreover, observe that the cycles with only odd numbers of
\pi^{\prime-1}\gamma^{\prime}
are the same as the cycles with only odd numbers of
\sigma
. In order to determine if
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
is bipartite, it is enough to prove that all cycles of
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
have even size. The advantage is that the graphs
\mathcal{G}_{\pi^{\prime}}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
are strongly related as they have exactly the same edges and vertices except by two vertices of
\mathcal{G}_{\pi^{\prime}}
which are merged into the same vertex of
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
, see for instance figure

. These vertices correspond to the cycles of
\pi^{\prime}
that contain
u
and

. The cycle of
\pi^{\prime}
that contains

also contains
o_{l+1}
while the cycle that contains

also contains
o_{1}
. Part

of the proof of
[

, Proposition 1.2]
precisely shows that the cycles
(o_{1},\dots,o_{p})
with only odd numbers of

correspond to a cycle of
\mathcal{G}_{\pi^{\prime}}
where each

belongs to a vertex of
\mathcal{G}_{\pi^{\prime}}
and the vertices containing
o_{t}
and
o_{t+1}
are consecutive vertices in the cycle. From this fact we know that the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
is obtained from merging the vertices that contains
o_{1}
and
o_{l+1}
of
\mathcal{G}_{\pi^{\prime}}
which at the same time belong to a cycle of
l+s
size. In order to get only cycles of even size in
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
we need two conditions to be satisfied. First, the cycles of
\sigma
with only odd numbers distinct of

must have even size. Secondly, we need to ask that
o_{1}
and
o_{l+1}
are at even distance, that is,
l
and

are even, so that when merging the vertices of
\mathcal{G}_{\pi^{\prime}}
that belong to one cycle produces two new cycles of even size each in
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
.
∎

Before moving on, let us comment that the main difficulty of a general proof is having two even numbers,

and

where

and

Remark 4.6
.

One could also come up with other necessary conditions for

. An example of such condition is the following.

If
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{2n,2m}
and

, then the cycle contains at least two elements of

.

However this condition, together with the previous ones, is not enough to guarantee that a permutation is in

(18)(27)(356)(4)\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}(4,4)

An interesting question for future work, is to give a description of

purely in terms of permutations. In other words, to express the condition that the graph associated to

4.3.
The action of
I
on
\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}

To finish this section, we give another approach to better understand the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
using permutations. This approach will be very useful in Section
7
to give a concise proof of Proposition
1.9
and to prove Theorem
1.5
.
The idea is to study the permutation
I\pi
where
I
is the unique permutation that pairs consecutive elements

I:=(12)(34)(56)\cdots(2n+2m-1,2n+2m)\in S_{NC}(2n,2m).

Two simple but useful facts are that
I^{2}=id
is the identity permutation, and
I
changes the parity of the numbers, namely,
I(\mathcal{E})=\mathcal{O}
and
I(\mathcal{O})=\mathcal{E}
.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\pi\in\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
. Then the permutation
I\pi
has cycle decomposition

I\pi=C^{\text{out}}C^{\text{inn}}O_{1}\dots O_{k},

where:

(1)

Every cycle has even length.

(2)

C^{\text{out}}
is a cycle containing all the elements
2,4,\dots,2n
appearing in increasing order (with possibly odd numbers in between).

(3)

C^{\text{inn}}
is a cycle containing all the elements
2n+2,2n+4,\dots,2n+2m
in increasing order (with possibly odd numbers in between).

(4)

The cycles
O_{1},\dots,O_{k}
contain only odd elements, and coincide precisely with the cycles of
\gamma^{-1}\pi
that only contain odd elements.

Proof.

To prove part 1, recall from Definition
1.2
that
\pi\in\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
has a natural bipartite decomposition
\pi=\pi^{\prime}\sqcup\pi^{\prime\prime}
where blocks in the same part do not share an edge of
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
. Let us denote
A^{\prime}=\cup\pi^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}=\cup\pi^{\prime\prime}
. Clearly
A^{\prime}\cup A^{\prime\prime}=[2n+2m]
and
A^{\prime}\cap A^{\prime\prime}=\emptyset
. Moreover, for each
i=1,\dots,2n+2m
, we have that
|A^{\prime}\cap\{2i-1,2i\}|=1=|A^{\prime\prime}\cap\{2i-1,2i\}|
. In other words, one element of
\{2i-1,2i\}
is in
A^{\prime}
and the other is in
A^{\prime\prime}
. In particular,

I(A^{\prime})=A^{\prime\prime},\qquad\text{and}\qquad I(A^{\prime\prime})=A^{\prime}.

On the other hand, by how
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}
are constructed, we also know that

\pi(A^{\prime})=A^{\prime},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\pi(A^{\prime\prime})=A^{\prime\prime}.

Putting these two together, we obtain that
I\pi
satisfies

(17)

I\pi(A^{\prime})\subset A^{\prime\prime},\qquad\text{and}\qquad I\pi(A^{\prime\prime})\subset A^{\prime}.

In particular, if
C=(c_{1}c_{2}\dots c_{r})
is a cycle of
I\pi
, then the elements of
C
alternate between
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}
. Namely
c_{2j+1}\in A^{\prime}
and
c_{2j}\in A^{\prime\prime}
or viceversa (
c_{2j+1}\in A^{\prime\prime}
and
c_{2j}\in A^{\prime}
). In any case, since
c_{r}
and
c_{1}=I\pi(c_{r})
belong to different parts (
A^{\prime}
or
A^{\prime\prime}
), we conclude that
r
is even. Thus, the cycles of
I\pi
are of even size.

To prove the remaining parts, recall that
\pi\in\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
implies that
\pi^{-1}\gamma
separates even elements, thus its inverse
\gamma^{-1}\pi
also separates even, meaning that it can be expressed as

\gamma^{-1}\pi=E_{2}E_{4}\cdots E_{2n+2m}O_{1}\dots O_{k}

where cycles
O_{i}
contains only odd elements for
i=1,2,\dots,k
and for
j=2,4,\dots,2n+2m
, the cycles containing an even element are of the form
E_{j}=(j,o_{j,1},o_{j,2},\dots,o_{j,l(j)})
with
l(j)\geq 0
, and
o_{j,i}
odd for all
i=1,\dots,l(j)
.

On the other hand, one can notice that

I\gamma=(1)(3)\dots(2m+2n-1)(2,4\dots,2n)(2n+2,\dots,2m+2n),

fixes all odd numbers. Therefore,

I\pi=(I\gamma)(\gamma^{-1}\pi)=C^{\text{out}}C^{\text{inn}}O_{1}\dots O_{k}

where
O_{1},\dots O_{k}
are the same cycles of
\gamma^{-1}\pi
(containing only odd elements), this proves part 4. Finally, by looking at the orbit of
2
one can check that cycles
E_{2},E_{4},\dots,E_{2n}
are glued together. Namely,

C^{\text{out}}:=(2,o_{2,1},\dots,o_{2,l(2)},4,o_{4,1},\dots,o_{4,l(4)},6,\dots\dots,2n,o_{2n,1},\dots,o_{2n,l(2n)})

is a cycle of
I\pi
. And similarly, the cycles
E_{2n+2},\dots,E_{2n+2m}
are glued together into the cycle

C^{\text{inn}}:=(2n+2,o_{2n+2,1},\dots,o_{2n+2,l(2n+2)},2n+4,\dots\dots,2n+2m,\dots)

of
I\pi
, as desired.
∎

We now explain how the permutation
I\pi
is helpful to understand the graph of
\pi
. The intuition is very clear, the cycles of
I
are precisely the edges of the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
, while the cycles of
\pi
are the vertices of the graph. Thus one can think of the permutation
I\pi
as a way to travel through
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
. This can be formally stated as follows.

Lemma 4.8
.

For every
j\in[2n+2m]
, the cycle of
\pi
containing
j
and the cycle of
\pi
containing
I\pi(j)
are connected by an edge in
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
.

Proof.

Notice that
\pi(j)
and
j
are in the same cycle of
\pi
. We conclude by noticing that the cycles of
\pi
containing
\pi(j)
and
I(\pi(j))
are connected by definition.
∎

Remark 4.9
.

Observe that the previous lemma implies that the cycles
C^{\text{out}},C^{\text{inn}}O_{1},\dots,O_{k}
of
I\pi
correspond to cycles of the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
. Moreover, since
C^{\text{out}},C^{\text{inn}}
together contain all the even numbers
\mathcal{E}
, then the two corresponding cycles in
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
contain all the edges (and thus all the vertices) in
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
.

Another implication is that if we construct a graph
\vec{\mathcal{G}}_{\pi}
with the cycles of
\pi
as vertices and for every
j\in[2n+2m]
we draw an directed edge from the cycle containing
j
and the cycle containing
I\pi(j)
. Then, by pairing directed edges with the same endpoints (but opposite directions) into one undirected edge, we end up with
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
.

We will explain this process to draw
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
in more detail. Let us begin by separating the cycles
C\in\pi
in 3 types:

•

C
is an
outer
block if
C\subset[2n]:=\{1,\dots,\dots,2n\}
.

•

C
is an
inner
block if
C\subset[2m]:=\{2n+1,\dots,2n+2m\}

•

C
is a
intermediate
block if it contain at least one element of each
[2n]
and
[2m]
.

To draw the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
using
I\pi
the procedure is as follows:

•

Step 1.
We first draw the cycle of
G_{\pi}
corresponding to
C^{\text{out}}
in clockwise direction. Namely we put the cycles (vertices) containing the elements of
C^{\text{out}}
(say starting in 2) and we join consecutive vertices with a directed edge. One should notice that there is a simple cycle that contains all the inner and middle blocks (with possibly some outer blocks), we will call this the
exterior core cycle
. To help with the intuition, we should draw the remaining (outer) blocks in the “exterior” of this core cycle.
Notice that while drawing
C^{out}
, we may go back to the same vertex several times (forming simple cycles in the way).
Notice also that we may draw the same edge twice (but at most twice, each in a different direction). These edges correspond to pairs
(2i-1,2i)
with
i\leq n
where both elements appear in
C^{\text{out}}
. We call these
flexible exterior edges
.

•

Step 2.
We now do a similar process with
C^{\text{inn}}
, but we will only draw in the interior of the “exterior core cycle” from step 1. Notice that each inner and middle block appearing in the exterior core cycle will also contain elements in
C^{\text{inn}}
, so we should make these coincide. In order to do this we will draw the vertices with elements in
C^{\text{inn}}
in the order they appear, but now in counter-clockwise direction. Same as before we join consecutive vertices with a directed edge. After this procedure we should also be able to identify an
interior core cycle
that is simple and contains all the outer and middle blocks (with possibly some inner blocks). Notice that some edges from the interior core coincide with the edges of the exterior core but have opposite direction.
Finally we may have drawn the same edge twice (not in the core cycle), corresponding to pairs
(2i-1,2i)
with
i\geq n+1
where both elements appear in
C^{\text{out}}
, we call these
flexible interior edges
.

•

At this stage we already drew all the vertices and edges in
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
. So, one can simply convert all the (directed) edges into undirected edges to obtain
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
. However, in order to complete the directed graph
\vec{\mathcal{G}}_{\pi}
, one should repeat the previous procedure with the remaining blocks of
I\pi
, namely
O_{1},\dots,O_{k}
. By drawing the vertices and directed edges in counter-clockwise direction we will notice that each cycle
O_{i}
yields a cycle of
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
. Moreover these cycles are formed precisely by edges that where only drawn in one direction (after Step 1 and Step 2), and we are now drawing the edges in the opposite direction. Notice also that these cycles might be in the exterior (if
O_{i}\subset[2n]
), in the interior (if
O_{i}\subset[2m]
) or embedded in between the interior core cycle and the exterior core cycle.

Check Figure
4
for an example on how the graph of

\pi=(1,3,8)(2)(4,5,7,16,9)(6)(10,11)(12,13)(14,15)

is constructed.

From this construction one should be clear that the set of graphs
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
that can be obtained from an arbitrary
\pi\in\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
is very particular. First of all, the graph must be planar. And it contains two distinguished simple cycles (the interior and exterior core cycles) that coincide in some vertices. These core cycles divide the plane in two regions (interior and exterior) where the remaining vertices belong. Regarding the connection to
I\pi
one can draw the graph in such a way that one can go around all the exterior vertices by following
C^{\text{out}}
and one can go around all the interior edges by following
C^{\text{inn}}
. Finally, let us point out a fact that will play a fundamental role when studying the commutator in Section
5
.

Remark 4.10
.

The edges that appear twice in a cycle of
I\pi
, namely, those pairs of elements
2i-1,2i
that are both in
C^{\text{out}}
or both in
C^{\text{inn}}
, correspond to the flexible edges (interior or exterior) of the graph. Notice that the flexible edges are precisely the
cutting edges
of
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
. Recall that a connected graph
G
has a cutting edge
e
if the graph
G\setminus\{e\}
, obtained by removing
e
from
G
, is no longer connected. Alternatively a connected graph has a cutting edge, if there is an edge that does not belong to any simple cycle. This observation means that
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
has no cutting edges if and only if
\pi
is admissible, as in Definition
1.4
.

5.
Commutator

The commutator
ab-ba
of a pair of free random variables can be seen as a signed counterpart of the anti-commutator. When studying the cumulants, one can parallel the approach used to study the anti-commutator to express it as sum of products of cumulants of
a
and
b
. The difference here, is that the terms in the sum may have positive or negative signs, which may lead to cancellation of terms in the sum.

5.1.
Adapting the formulas for the anti-commutator

The first step is just a modification of Proposition
3.2
, and its proof is analogous, so we omit it. Recall from Notation
3.1
that given a
n
-tuple
\varepsilon\in\{1,*\}^{n}
, we denote by
(ab)^{\varepsilon}:=((ab)^{\varepsilon(1)},(ab)^{\varepsilon(2)},\dots,(ab)^{\varepsilon(n)})
the
n
-tuple with entries
ab
or
ba
dictated by the entries of
\varepsilon
, and
(a,b)^{\varepsilon}
is the
2n
-tuple obtained from separating the
a
’s from the
b
’s in
(ab)^{\varepsilon}
. To study the commutator we also need to keep track of the sign associated to each
n
-tuple, so we multiply by
-1
for each
*
in
\varepsilon
:

s(\varepsilon):=(-1)^{|\{1\leq i\leq n:\,\varepsilon(i)=*\}|}.

With this notation in hand, Proposition
3.2
can be readily adapted to the commutator.

Proposition 5.1
.

The second order free cumulants of the commutator
ab-ba
of two second order free random variables
a,b
satisfy the following formula for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
:

(18)

\kappa_{n,m}^{ab-ba}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathcal{U},\pi)\in\mathcal{PS}_{NC}(2n,2m),\\
\pi^{-1}\gamma\text{ sep. even}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varepsilon\in\{1,*\}^{n+m}\\
\{A(\varepsilon),B(\varepsilon)\}\geq\mathcal{U}\end{subarray}}s(\varepsilon)\kappa_{(\mathcal{U},\pi)}((a,b)^{\varepsilon}).

Here, we use the notation
\gamma:=(1,\dots,2n)(2n+1,\dots,2n+2m)\in S_{2n+2m}
.

Same as before, one can check that only permutations
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}(2n,2m)
that actually contribute to the sum are those such that
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
is connected and bipartite. Namely, only partitions
\pi\in\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
contribute to the sum. Moreover, for each
\pi
that contributes there are exactly two
(m+n)
-tuples that contribute,
\varepsilon_{\pi}
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{\pi}
.
Notice that since these two tuples do not coincide in any entry, then
s(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{\pi})=(-1)^{m+n}s(\varepsilon_{\pi})
. Given a permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
we use the notation
s(\pi)=s(\varepsilon_{\pi})
. Recall that one of the parts in the bipartition is
\pi^{\prime}:=\pi|A(\varepsilon_{\pi})
where

A(\varepsilon_{\pi}):=\{2i-1|1\leq i\leq n,\ \varepsilon_{\pi}(i)=1\}\cup\{2i|1\leq i\leq n,\ \varepsilon_{\pi}(i)=*\}.

Thus we have the alternative description

(19)

s(\pi):=(-1)^{|\{k\in A(\varepsilon_{\pi}):\,k\text{ is even}\}|}

Then Theorem
1.3
can be adapted to the commutator case as follows.

Theorem 5.2
.

Consider two second order free random variables
a
and
b
, and let
(\kappa_{n}^{a})_{n\geq 1}
,
(\kappa_{n,m}^{a})_{n,m\geq 1}
,
(\kappa_{n}^{b})_{n\geq 1}
,
(\kappa_{n,m}^{b})_{n,m\geq 1}
and
(\kappa_{n,m}^{ab-ba})_{n,m\geq 1}
be the sequence of first and second order free cumulants of
a
,
b
and
ab-ba
, respectively. Then, for every
n,m\geq 1
one has

\displaystyle\kappa_{n,m}^{ab-ba}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}\\
\pi=\pi^{\prime}\sqcup\pi^{\prime\prime}\end{subarray}}s(\pi)\left(\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}}^{a}\,\kappa_{\pi^{\prime\prime}}^{b}+(-1)^{m+n}\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}}^{b}\,\kappa_{\pi^{\prime\prime}}^{a}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathcal{U},\pi)\in\mathcal{X}_{2n,2m}\\
\pi=\pi^{\prime}\sqcup\pi^{\prime\prime}\sqcup A\sqcup B\end{subarray}}s(\pi)\left(\kappa_{|A|,|B|}^{a}\,\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}}^{a}\,\kappa_{\pi^{\prime\prime}}^{b}+(-1)^{m+n}\kappa_{|A|,|B|}^{b}\,\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}}^{b}\,\kappa_{\pi^{\prime\prime}}^{a}\right).

Contrary to the anti-commutator case where all the terms where positive, now each partition
\pi
has a sign
s(\pi)
that can potentially cancel with other term. We can actually cancel several terms to simplify the sum and obtain Theorem
1.5
advertised in the introduction. To achieve this, we need to cancel several terms in both sums. We will firs explain how to simplify the second sum by reducing to the first order case and using the results of Nica and Speicher
[
NS98
]
. For the first sum we can still cancel terms following the same philosophy used in
[
NS98
]
, but finding such canceling pairs is not a simple task and becomes technical. Since the underlying idea for the cancellation coming from the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
is easier to grasp, we will first give an intuitive explanation of the cancellation phenomenon, followed by rigorous proof that relies on the permutation
I\pi
studied in Section
4.3
.

5.2.
Intuition behind the cancellation in first sum.

Simplifying the second sum in Theorem
5.2
follows easily from reducing to the first order case and using the results of Nica and Speicher
[
NS98
]
. Simplifying the first sum requires more effort, but we can still cancel terms following the same philosophy used in
[
NS98
]
. More specifically, we will find pairs of partitions
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
such that

(C1)

\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}_{1}}^{a}\,\kappa_{\pi^{\prime\prime}_{1}}^{b}=\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}_{2}}^{a}\,\kappa_{\pi^{\prime\prime}_{2}}^{b}
, and

(C2)

s(\pi_{1})=-s(\pi_{2})
.

Notice that these two conditions together imply that the terms corresponding to
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
cancel each other:

(20)

s(\pi_{1})\left(\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}_{1}}^{a}\,\kappa_{\pi^{\prime\prime}_{1}}^{b}+(-1)^{n}\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}_{1}}^{b}\,\kappa_{\pi^{\prime\prime}_{1}}^{a}\right)+s(\pi_{2})\left(\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}_{2}}^{a}\,\kappa_{\pi^{\prime\prime}_{2}}^{b}+(-1)^{n}\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}_{2}}^{b}\,\kappa_{\pi^{\prime\prime}_{2}}^{a}\right)=0.

In what follows, we will identify a subset of
\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
that contains pairs of permutations that satisfy both
(C1)
and
(C2)
and thus cancel each other. After canceling them we end up with a sum indexed by a smaller set of
admissible
permutations. Despite that our formula is simpler, it is not cancellation free, so it might be possible to simplify it further.

In order to identify partitions satisfying
(C1)
, the key observation is that the product
\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}}^{a}\,\kappa_{\pi^{\prime\prime}}^{b}
only depends on the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
, this is because the bipartition of
\pi=(\pi^{\prime},\pi^{\prime\prime})
is determined by the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
. Moreover, the sizes of the blocks of
\pi
(that ultimately govern the product) are precisely the sizes of degrees of the vertices of the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
. This means that the permutations having the same associated graph will have the same product. Namely:

\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}\text{ and }\mathcal{G}_{\pi_{1}}=\mathcal{G}_{\pi_{2}}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\ref{condition.1}.

The second key observation is that to obtain a pair of partitions that cancel each other we need to focus on the graphs
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
with a
cutting edge
, or equivalently in permutations
\pi
such that there is pair
(2i-1,2i)
where both elements are in the same cycle of
I\pi
(either in

C^{\text{out}}
or
C^{\text{inn}}
), see Remark
4.10
.

Our approach is to find a partition
\pi_{1}
such that
\mathcal{G}_{\pi_{1}}
has a cutting edge, and construct another partition
\pi_{2}
such that
\mathcal{G}_{\pi_{2}}=\mathcal{G}_{\pi_{1}}
and
s(\pi_{2})=-s(\pi_{1})
, thus providing a pair of partitions satisfying
(C1)
and
(C2)
.

Constructing the bijection
(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
mentioned above is the main technical part. Let us give a final piece of intuition on how this is done. Given a partition
\pi_{1}
such that
\mathcal{G}_{\pi_{1}}
has a cutting edge, we can separate the vertices of the graphs in two, by grouping the vertices in each connected component obtained after removing the edge. Notice that one component has the all the vertices in core cycles, we call this the
fixed
component and the other the
moving
component. For instance, if the flexible edge is exterior, then the vertices in the moving component will have only consecutive elements in
[2n]
(assuming 1 is next to
2n
). To construct a partition
\pi_{2}
we fix all the cycles of
\pi_{1}
that belong to the fixed component, and then modify the cycles in
\pi_{1}
in the moving component (shifting by 2 most of the elements) in such a way that we get the same graph, the new partition
\pi_{2}
is still not crossing, and such that
s(\pi_{1})=-s(\pi_{2})
.

5.3.
Rigorous explanation of cancellation in the first sum.

Recall from Definition
1.4
that a permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
is said to be admissible if
I\pi
separates
2i-1
from
2i
for all
1\leq i\leq n+m
. The permutations that are not admissible will be the ones that we can cancel.

Definition 5.3
.

Given a permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
for
1\leq i\leq n+m
if the pair
(2i-1,2i)
belong to
C^{\text{out}}
or both belong to
C^{\text{inn}}
then we say that the pair is flexible. If
\pi
has at least one flexible pair, then we say that
\pi
is cancelable, and denote the set of all cancelable permutations by
\mathcal{F}_{2n,2m}
.

Notice that
\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}=\mathcal{F}_{2n,2m}\sqcup\mathcal{A}_{2n,2m}
is the disjoint union of cancelable and admissible permutations. To construct our bijection, for a
\pi\in\mathcal{F}_{2n,2m}
we want to have a distinguished flexible pair. Thus we will use the following notation.

Notation 5.4
.

Given
\pi\in\mathcal{F}_{2n,2m}
we order the elements of
C^{\text{out}}\cup C^{\text{inn}}
by listing in order all the elements of
C^{\text{out}}
(starting with 2) followed by the elements of
C^{\text{inn}}
(starting with
2n+2
). Notice that list looks as follows:

L_{\pi}=2,\dots,4,\dots,6,\dots,\dots,2n+2m,\dots.

where the empty spaces may contain some odd elements. We denote by
f_{\pi}
the first element in the list that belongs to a flexible pair. Letting
i
be such that
f_{\pi}\in\{2i-1,2i\}
then we say that
(2i-1,2i)
is
smallest flexible pair
. If
f_{\pi}
is even we say the
\pi
is an even cancelable permutation, and denote the set of such permutations by
\mathcal{F}^{E}_{2n,2m}
. Otherwise, if
f_{\pi}
is odd, we say that
\pi
is an odd cancelable permutations, and denote the set by
\mathcal{F}^{O}_{2n,2m}
.

Clearly, every cancelable partition is either even or odd, thus
\mathcal{F}_{2n,2m}=\mathcal{F}^{E}_{2n,2m}\sqcup\mathcal{F}^{O}_{2n,2m}
. We will construct a bijection from
\mathcal{F}^{E}_{2n,2m}
to
\mathcal{F}^{O}_{2n,2m}
.

Definition 5.5
.

Given
\pi\in\mathcal{F}^{E}_{2n,2m}
with
f_{\pi}=2i
, let
j\geq i
be such that
2j
is the largest even number appearing before
2i-1
in the list
L_{\pi}
. Namely, the list looks as follows:

L_{\pi}=\dots,2i,\dots,2j,\dots,2i-1,\dots,2j+2,\dots.

We construct the auxiliary permutation
\tau\in S_{2n+2m}
by letting

\displaystyle\tau(2i-1)

\displaystyle=2j,

\displaystyle\tau(2i)

\displaystyle=2j-1,

\displaystyle\tau(t)

\displaystyle=t-2,\qquad\text{whenever }t\in\{2i+1,\dots,2j\}

\displaystyle\tau(t)

\displaystyle=t,\qquad\qquad\text{whenever }t\in\{1,2,\dots,2n+2m\}\setminus\{2i-1,\dots,2j\}.

Then, we define the permutation

T_{\pi}:=\tau\pi\tau^{-1}.

Example 5.6
.

Let us give an example of the map
T_{\pi}
. Let

\pi=(1,3,8)(2)(4,5,7,16,9)(6)(10,11)(12,13)(14,15)\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}(8,8),

whose graph
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
can be seen in Figure
4
. In this case
f_{\pi}=2=2i
and
j=i
so that
\tau=(1,2)\in S_{2n+2m}
. Thus,

T_{\pi}=\tau\pi\tau^{-1}=(2,3,8)(1)(4,5,7,16,9)(6)(10,11)(12,13)(14,15)\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}(8,8).

Proposition 5.7
.

For every
\pi\in\mathcal{F}^{E}_{2n,2m}
it holds that
T_{\pi}\in\mathcal{F}^{O}_{2n,2m}
. Moreover,
\pi
and
T_{\pi}
satisfy
(C1)
and
(C2)
.

Proof.

There are several little facts that we need to check. Since our approach relies on understanding
IT_{\pi}
, the order in which we proof these claims might no be the most intuitive. Let us make a list of the facts in the order they are proved.

(F1)

T_{\pi}
is an odd cancelable permutation.

(F2)

Kr_{2n,2m}(T_{\pi})
separates even.

(F3)

\#(T_{\pi})+\#(Kr_{2n,2m}(T_{\pi}))=2m+2n.

(F4)

Exists
x\in[2n]
such that
T_{\pi}(x)\in[2m]
.

(F5)

\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{G}_{T_{\pi}}
.

(F6)

s(T_{\pi})=-s(\pi)
.

First we explain why the proposition follows from these facts. Notice that
(F4)
implies that
T_{\pi}\lor\gamma=1_{2m+2n}
, so using
(F3)
we conclude that
T_{\pi}
is a non-crossing annular permutation. On the other hand, since
\pi\in\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
, then
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
is bipartite. Thus
(F5)
guarantees that
\mathcal{G}_{T_{\pi}}
is bipartite too. The previous claims together with
(F2)
yield that
T_{\pi}\in\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
. By
(F1)
we conclude that
T_{\pi}\in\mathcal{F}^{O}_{2n,2m}
. Finally,
(F5)
and
(F6)
imply that
\pi
and
T_{\pi}
satisfy
(C1)
and
(C2)
.

Now, we proceed to prove these 7 facts. We begin by understanding the permutation
IT_{\pi}
. First notice that
\tau I=I\tau
. Indeed, this follows form a direct computation

\displaystyle\tau I(2i)

\displaystyle=2j=I\tau(2i),

\displaystyle\tau I(2i-1)

\displaystyle=2j-1=I\tau(2i-1),

\displaystyle\tau I(2c)

\displaystyle=2c-3=I\tau(2c),

\displaystyle\text{for }2c\in\{2i+1,\dots,2j\},

\displaystyle\tau I(2c-1)

\displaystyle=2c-2=I\tau(2c-1),

\displaystyle\text{for }2c-1\in\{2i+1,\dots,2j\},

\displaystyle\tau I(c)

\displaystyle=I(c)=I\tau(c),

\displaystyle\text{for }c\in\{1,2,\dots,2n+2m\}\setminus\{2i-1,\dots,2j\}.

As a consequence, we obtain that

(21)

IT_{\pi}=I\tau\pi\tau^{-1}=\tau I\pi\tau^{-1}

is the conjugate of
I\pi
by
\tau
.

Recall from Proposition
4.7
that

(22)

I\pi=C^{\text{out}}C^{\text{inn}}O_{1}\dots O_{k},

where the
O_{r}
contain only odd elements and coincide with the blocks of
\sigma:=(Kr_{2n,2m}(\pi))^{-1}=\gamma^{-1}\pi
, while the
C^{\text{out}}
and
C^{\text{inn}}
are union of blocks of
\sigma
that contain even numbers.
In particular, since
2i-1
sits between
2j
and
2j+2
in the list
L_{\pi}
, then we know that
2i-1
and
2j
are in the same cycle of
\sigma
. This means that every other cycle
C
of
\sigma
satisfies that either
C\subset\{2i,2i+1,\dots,2j-1\}
or
C\subset\{1,2,\dots,2n+2m\}\setminus\{2i-1,\dots,2j\}
. Since
\tau
is the identity in
\{1,2,\dots,2n+2m\}\setminus\{2i-1,\dots,2j\}
, in the latter case we obtain that
\tau(C)=C
. Furthermore, from the topological interpretation of
Kr_{2n,2m}(\pi)
, see Figure
2
,we also know that
\pi(\{2i,\dots,2j\})=\{2i,\dots,2j\}\subset[2n]
.

From equations (
21
) and (
22
) we obtain that

IT_{\pi}=D^{\text{out}}D^{\text{inn}}O^{\prime}_{1}\dots O^{\prime}_{k},

where
D^{\text{out}}:=\tau(C^{\text{out}})
,
D^{\text{inn}}:=\tau(C^{\text{inn}})
and
O^{\prime}_{r}:=\tau(O_{r})
for
r=1,\dots,k
. Now we analyze each of these cycles using our knowledge of
I\pi
. Let us assume without loss of generality that
f_{\pi}\in C^{\text{out}}
(the case
f_{\pi}\in C^{\text{inn}}
is analogous).

First, since
C^{\text{inn}}
is the union of cycles of
\sigma
containing even numbers of the form
2k>2n
, then we must have
C^{\text{inn}}\subset\{1,2,\dots,2n+2m\}\setminus\{2i-1,\dots,2j\}
and we obtain

D^{\text{inn}}=C^{\text{inn}}
.

On the other hand, for the cycles
O_{r}
with only odd elements we have two cases:

•

Either
O_{r}\subset[2n+2m]\setminus\{2i-1,\dots,2j\}
, and then
O^{\prime}_{r}=O_{r}
,

•

or
O_{r}\subset\{2i+1,\dots,2j\}
, and then
O^{\prime}_{r}=O_{r}-2
, still has the same size and only odd elements.

Finally, the cycle
C^{\text{out}}
of
I\pi
is of the form

C^{\text{out}}:=(\mathbf{a}_{1},2i-2,\mathbf{o}_{1},2i,\mathbf{o}_{2},2i+2,\mathbf{a}_{2},2j,\mathbf{o}_{3},2i-1,\mathbf{o}_{4},2j+2,\mathbf{a}_{3}),

where
\mathbf{o}_{1},\mathbf{o}_{2},\mathbf{o}_{3},\mathbf{o}_{4}
are strings of odd numbers, while
\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{2},\mathbf{a}_{3}
are strings of numbers. Therefore

D^{\text{out}}=(\mathbf{a}_{1},2i-2,\mathbf{o}_{1},2j-1,\mathbf{o}_{2}-2,2i,\mathbf{a}_{2}-2,2j-2,\mathbf{o}_{3}-2,2j,\mathbf{o}_{4},2j+2,\mathbf{a}_{3}),

where for a string of numbers

. Notice that the structure of the even numbers is preserved here, in the sense that

still appear in order in

. Moreover since since the pairs

are preserved by

, the smallest flexible pair of

is now
(2j-1,2j)
with

. Thus

is an odd cancelable permutation, proving

.

Overall, we conclude that

IT_{\pi}=D^{\text{out}}D^{\text{inn}}O^{\prime}_{1}\dots O^{\prime}_{k},

where the

contain only odd elements while

contains

in that order and

contains

in that order. This formula has several implications.

First, by reverse engineering Proposition
4.7
, we get that

(Kr_{2n,2m}(T_{\pi}))^{-1}=\gamma^{-1}T_{\pi}=(\gamma^{-1}I)(IT_{\pi})

separates even, and thus

separates even. This proves
(F2)
.

Secondly, by the form of

we can check that

\#(Kr_{2n,2m}(T_{\pi}))=n+m+k=\#(Kr_{2n,2m}(\pi)).

On the other hand, we also know that
\#(T_{\pi})=\#(\tau\pi\tau^{-1})=\#(\pi)
. Thus,
(F3)
follows from the fact that

is a non-crossing annular permutation:

\#(T_{\pi})+\#(Kr_{2n,2m}(T_{\pi}))=\#(\pi)+\#(Kr_{2n,2m}(\pi))=2m+2n.

To check that there is an

such that

we also use the fact that
\pi
is a non-crossing annular permutation, and in particular there exists
x^{\prime}\in[2n]
such that
\pi(x^{\prime})\in[2m]
. We separate in two cases:

•

If
x^{\prime}=2i-1
, then we directly have
T_{\pi}(2j)=\tau(\pi(x^{\prime}))\in[2m]
.

•

If
x^{\prime}\neq 2i-1
, then

, because
\pi(\{2i,\dots,2j\})=\{2i,\dots,2j\}\subset[2n]
. We conclude that
T_{\pi}(x^{\prime})=\pi(x^{\prime})\in[2m]
.

Thus
(F4)
is proved.

Now, to corroborate
(F5)
we provide a bijection from
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
to
\mathcal{G}_{T_{\pi}}
that preserves the edges. Let
\Phi
be a map from the cycles of
\pi
to the cycles of
T_{\pi}
such that the cycle
C=(c_{1},\dots,c_{r})
of
\pi
is mapped to the cycle
\Phi(C):=(\tau(c_{1}),\dots,\tau(c_{n}))
of
T_{\pi}
.

To check that
\Phi
preserves edges, recall that two cycles
C
and
D
are connected if and only if each contain one element of the pair
2i-1,2i
for some
1\leq i\leq n+m
. Using that
I\tau=\tau I
we then notice that
\tau(2i-1)=\tau(I(2i))=I(\tau(2i))
, then each of
\Phi(C)
and
\Phi(D)
contain one element of the pair
\tau(2i)
and
I(\tau(2i))
that by definition of

is of the form

for some

. Thus
\Phi
is the desired bijection.
Notice in particular that if
\pi=\pi^{\prime}\sqcup\pi^{\prime\prime}
is a bipartition of
\pi
, then
T_{\pi}=\Phi(\pi^{\prime})\sqcup\Phi(\pi^{\prime\prime})
is a bipartition of
T_{\pi}
, so
\pi
and
T_{\pi}
satisfy
(C1)
.

Finally, to prove that the two permutations have opposite signs, we notice that the permutation
\tau
preserves the parity for all the elements except for
2i-1
and
2i
, where the parity changes. Thus, if we let
C
be the cycle of
\pi
containing
2i
, and
D
the cycle containing
2i-1
, then
C
and
D
are connected by an edge and thus in different parts of the bipartition
\pi=\pi^{\prime}\sqcup\pi^{\prime\prime}
. Assume that

\pi^{\prime}:=\{C,C_{1},\dots,C_{r}\}
and
\pi^{\prime\prime}:=\{D,D_{1},\dots,D_{s}\}
. By comparing
\pi^{\prime}
with the corresponding part
\Phi(\pi^{\prime})=\{\Phi(C),\Phi(C_{1}),\dots,\Phi(C_{r})\}
of

, we notice that

|\{k\in C_{s}:\,k\text{ is even}\}|=|\{k\in\Phi(C_{s}):\,k\text{ is even}\}|\qquad\text{ for }s=1,\dots,r,

whereas

|\{k\in C:\,k\text{ is even}\}|=|\{k\in\Phi(C):\,k\text{ is even}\}|+1.

We conclude that the number of even elements in

A(\varepsilon_{\pi}):=\cup\{C,C_{1},\dots,C_{r}\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad A(\varepsilon_{T_{\pi}})=\cup\{\Phi(C),\Phi(C_{1}),\dots,\Phi(C_{r})\}

differs in 1. So we conclude that

s(T_{\pi}):=(-1)^{|\{k\in A(\varepsilon_{T_{\pi}}):\,k\text{ is even}\}|}=(-1)^{|\{k\in A(\varepsilon_{\pi}):\,k\text{ is even}\}|+1}=-s(\pi),

which is

.

5.4.
Cancellation in the second sum.

Let us briefly recall the results from
[
NS98
, Section 3]
used to commutator formula in the first order. The key fact is that they prove the existence of an involution
\Psi
in the set of non-crossing partitions of
\{1,\dots,n\}
which have at least one block with an odd number of elements. Such involution
\Psi
is explicitly assigning to each block
V
of a partition
\mathcal{V}
a corresponding block of the same size
\Psi(V)
in
\Psi(\mathcal{V})
. More over,
\Psi
changes the sign associated to the partition
\mathcal{V}
, where the sign is defined analogously to ours sign
s(\pi)
and keep tracks of the signs of the terms in the sum of the commutator. By pairing
\mathcal{V}
with
\Psi(\mathcal{V})
Nica and Speicher managed to canceled all the terms in a sum indexed by this type of partitions (having at least one block of odd size). Thus greatly simplifying the sum.

By considering the canonical permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{NC}(n)
in the disk associated to the partition one can easily extrapolate such involution to permutations. Specifically, denoting by
\mathcal{NC}\mathcal{O}(2n)
the set of non-crossing permutations in the disk which have at least one cycle with an odd number of elements, there exists an involution
\Psi:\mathcal{NC}\mathcal{O}(2n)\to\mathcal{NC}\mathcal{O}(2n)
such that

•

\Psi(\pi)
has the same cycle type as
\pi
.

•

s(\pi)=-s(\Psi(\pi))
.

Moreover, to every cycle
C
of
\pi
we can associate a unique cycle
\Psi(C)
of
\Psi(C)
with the same size.

We can further extend
\Psi
to an involution
\Phi
in the set of
\mathcal{X}\mathcal{O}_{2n,2m}
of permutations
\pi\in\mathcal{X}_{2n,2m}
such that
\pi
has at least one cycle of odd size. The new involution
\Phi
will allow us to cancel several terms in the second sum of Theorem
5.2
.

First notice that the set of partitioned permutations
(\mathcal{U},\pi)\in\mathcal{X}_{2n,2m}\subset S_{\mathcal{NC}}^{\prime}(2n,2m)
can be alternatively described by quadruples
(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},C_{1},C_{2})
where
\pi_{1}\in\mathcal{NC}(2n)
,
\pi_{2}\in\mathcal{NC}(2m)
,
C_{1}
is a block of
\pi_{1}
and
C_{2}
is a block of
\pi_{2}
. Given such quadruple, then
\pi
is retrieved as
\pi=\pi_{1}\sqcup\pi_{2}
and
\mathcal{U}
is the partition corresponding to
\pi
after merging
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
.

We now define the map
\Phi:\mathcal{X}\mathcal{O}_{2n,2m}\to\mathcal{X}\mathcal{O}_{2n,2m}
as follows:

•

If
\pi_{1}
has a cycle of odd length, then
(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},C_{1},C_{2})\mapsto(\Psi(\pi_{1}),\pi_{2},\Psi(C_{1}),C_{2})
.

•

Otherwise,
\pi_{2}
must have a cycle of odd length and then
(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},C_{1},C_{2})\mapsto(\pi_{1},\Psi(\pi_{2}),C_{1},\Psi(C_{2}))
.

The fact that
\Phi
is an involution follows easily from the fact that
\Psi
is an involution. Following the same procedure as in the first sum, we can check that
\Phi
preserves the graph structure while changes the sign. Thus, if
\pi
has the natural decomposition
\pi=\pi^{\prime}_{1}\sqcup\pi^{\prime\prime}_{1}\sqcup A_{1}\sqcup B_{1}
and
\Phi(\pi)
has the natural decomposition
\Phi(\pi)=\pi^{\prime}_{2}\sqcup\pi^{\prime\prime}_{2}\sqcup A_{2}\sqcup B_{2}
, then
|A_{1}|=|A_{2}|
,
|B_{1}|=|B_{2}|
,
\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}_{1}}^{a}=\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}_{2}}^{a}
,
\kappa_{\pi^{\prime\prime}_{1}}^{b}=\kappa_{\pi^{\prime\prime}_{2}}^{b}
and
s(\pi)=-s(\Phi(\pi))
. So we conclude that the two terms in the sum cancel each other.

(23)

s(\pi)\kappa_{|A_{1}|,|B_{1}|}^{a}\,\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}_{1}}^{a}\,\kappa_{\pi^{\prime\prime}_{1}}^{b}+s(\Phi(\pi))\kappa_{|A_{2}|,|B_{2}|}^{a}\,\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}_{2}}^{a}\,\kappa_{\pi^{\prime\prime}_{2}}^{b}=0.

By canceling all this terms we end up with a sum indexed only by the set
\mathcal{X}\mathcal{E}_{2n,2m}
of permutations
\pi\in\mathcal{X}_{2n,2m}
such that every cycle of
\pi
has even size.

5.5.
Proof of main formula for the commutator.

We are now ready to proof the main result of this section, Theorem
1.5
that gives a simplified formula to compute the second order cumulants of the commutator
ab-ba
in terms of the cumulants of
a
and
b
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

By Theorem
5.2
, we know that

\displaystyle\kappa_{n,m}^{ab-ba}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}\\
\pi=\pi^{\prime}\sqcup\pi^{\prime\prime}\end{subarray}}s(\pi)\left(\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}}^{a}\,\kappa_{\pi^{\prime\prime}}^{b}+(-1)^{m+n}\kappa_{\pi^{\prime}}^{b}\,\kappa_{\pi^{\prime\prime}}^{a}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathcal{U},\pi)\in\mathcal{X}_{2n,2m}\\

5.6.
A concrete formula for small

and

In order to exemplify how our formula works, we will compute the second order cumulants for small values of

For the case

, one can check that
\mathcal{J}_{2,2}=\mathcal{A}_{2,2}
, as the unique permutation in the set,
\pi=(1,3)(2,4)
, is admissible. On the other hand, since

is odd, the second sum vanishes. Thus, we conclude that given second order free random variables

and

, the
(1,1)
-cumulant of their commutator is

\kappa_{1,1}^{ab-ba}=2\kappa_{2}^{a}\kappa_{2}^{b}.

For the case
n=2
,
m=1
, one can check that out of the 14 permutations in
\mathcal{J}_{4,2}
, 12 have flexible edges. Thus, there are only two admissible partitions:

(1,6,4)(2,3,5)\qquad\text{and}\qquad(1,5,4)(2,3,6).

By looking at the signs we obtain that the first sum is

2\kappa_{3}^{a}\,\kappa_{3}^{b}-2\kappa_{3}^{a}\,\kappa_{3}^{b}=0,

Since

is odd, the second sum also vanishes, so we conclude that

\kappa_{2,1}^{ab-ba}=\kappa_{1,2}^{ab-ba}=0.

Notice, that this is already an instance where our formula is not cancellation free.

Lett us briefly mention the case
n=m=2
. Using the very structured form of

one can find that there are 20 admissible permutations in
\mathcal{A}_{4,4}
. On the other
\mathcal{X}\mathcal{E}_{4,4}
has only four partitioned permutations
(\mathcal{V},\pi)
. After checking that all of the terms in the sums have a positive signs, we conclude that

\kappa_{2,2}^{ab-ba}=4\kappa_{4}^{a}\kappa_{4}^{b}+12(\kappa_{2}^{a}\kappa_{2}^{b})^{2}+12\kappa_{4}^{a}(\kappa_{2}^{b})^{2}+12\kappa_{4}^{b}(\kappa_{2}^{a})^{2}+4\kappa_{2,2}^{a}(\kappa_{2}^{b})^{2}+4\kappa_{2,2}^{b}(\kappa_{2}^{a})^{2}.

Remark 5.9
.

Finally, we would like to highlight and interesting phenomenon appearing in the second order commutator formula. There might be terms in the first sum that are indexed by the permutations containing cycles of odd size. In particular, the second order free commutator does depend on the moments of odd size. This is in contrast to the first order case, in which the sum is indexed only by partitions with all blocks of even size, thus having the important fact that the commutator in the first order only depends on the even moments. For an example of this behavior, one need to check the formula for the (2,4) and (3,3) cumulants. Computing the exact formula might require some machine help, but one can focus on some particular terms that are sure to survive.
For instance, when computing the first sum in the formula for
\kappa_{3,3}^{ab-ba}
one needs to find admissible partitions in
\mathcal{A}_{6,6}
. If we only focus on all the admissible permutations that yield a term of the form

(\kappa_{3}^{a})^{2}(\kappa_{3}^{b})^{2}.

Then we are only interested in permutations
\pi
with 4 cycles, each of size 3. An example of an admissible permutation of that type is

\pi_{0}=(1,8,6)(2,12,7)(3,10,11)(4,5,9).

It is not hard to check that there are in total 9 permutations of this type. To compute them, one may conjugate
\pi_{0}
by
\tau=(135)(246)(7)(8)(9)(10)(11)(12)
or by
\delta=(1)(2)(3)(4)(5)(6)(7,9,11)(8,10,12)
.
One can also notice that the sign assigned to each of these permutations is always negative. Thus we conclude that

\kappa_{3,3}^{ab-ba}=-18(\kappa_{3}^{a})^{2}(\kappa_{3}^{b})^{2}+c,

where
c
does not have any term of the form
(\kappa_{3}^{a})^{2}(\kappa_{3}^{b})^{2}
.

Similarly, when looking at the same term in the expansion for

\kappa_{4,2}^{ab-ba}
, one can find that there 4 admissible permutations of the required type, each obtained by conjugating

\pi_{0}=(1,9,8)(2,3,12)(4,5,11)(6,7,10),

by
(1)(2)\dots(8)(9,10,11,12)
. In this case all the signs are positive and we obtain

\kappa_{4,2}^{ab-ba}=8(\kappa_{3}^{a})^{2}(\kappa_{3}^{b})^{2}+c,

where
c
does not have any term of the form
(\kappa_{3}^{a})^{2}(\kappa_{3}^{b})^{2}
.

Let us show how our graph methods help us to write the second order free cumulants of the product

.

Notation 6.1
.

Recall that we denote
\mathcal{O}:=\{1,\dots,2n+2m-1\}
and
\mathcal{E}:=\{2,\dots,2n+2m\}
. Given a permutation
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}(2n,2m)
we say that

(1)

\pi
is parity preserving if for every cycle
V\in\pi
either
V\subset\mathcal{O}
or
V\subset\mathcal{E}
. We denote the set of parity preserving permutations
S_{\mathcal{NC}}^{par}(2n,2m)
.

(2)

\pi
is a non-crossing pairing if every cycle has size
2
. We let
\mathcal{NC}_{2}(2n,2m)
to be the set of non-crossing pairings.

(3)

We denote by
\mathcal{NC}_{2}^{non-par}(2n,2m)
to be the subset of
\mathcal{NC}_{2}(n,m)
with no parity preserving cycles.

(4)

We let
\mathcal{NC}_{2}^{non-par}(n)
the set of all non-crossing pairings
\pi\in\mathcal{NC}_{2}(2n)
with no parity preserving cycles.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
n,m\geq 1
, and
\pi\in\mathcal{NC}_{2}^{non-par}(2n,2m)\cup\mathcal{NC}_{2}^{non-par}(2n)\times\mathcal{NC}_{2}^{non-par}(2m)
. Then
\gamma\pi
is parity preserving.

Proof.

The proof follows immediately from the fact that both
\pi
and
\gamma
map even numbers into odd numbers and viceversa.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
a,b
be two second order free random variables, then for every
n,m\geq 1

\displaystyle\kappa_{n,m}(ab)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{NC}_{2}^{non-par}(2n,2m)}\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\gamma\pi\\
W\subset\mathcal{O}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{|W|}^{a}\right)\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\gamma\pi\\
W\subset\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{|W|}^{b}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\gamma\pi\cap[2n]\\
V\in\gamma\pi\cap[2m]\\
U,V\subset\mathcal{O}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{|U|,|V|}(a)\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\gamma\pi\\
W\subset\mathcal{O}\\
W\neq U,V\end{subarray}}\kappa_{|W|}^{a}\right)\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\gamma\pi\\
W\subset\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{|W|}^{b}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\gamma\pi\cap[2n]\\
V\in\gamma\pi\cap[2m]\\
U,V\subset\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{|U|,|V|}(b)\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\gamma\pi\\
W\subset\mathcal{O}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{|W|}^{a}\right)\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\gamma\pi\\
W\subset\mathcal{E}\\
W\neq U,V\end{subarray}}\kappa_{|W|}^{b}\right)

where the second and third sums are over
\pi=\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\in\mathcal{NC}_{2}^{non-par}(2n)\times\mathcal{NC}_{2}^{non-par}(2m)
. Observe that the blocks of
\gamma\sigma
are completely contained in either
\mathcal{O}
or
\mathcal{E}
, thanks to Proposition
6.2
.

We can rephrase Lemma

in terms of graphs.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
a,b
be two second order free random variables, then for every

\displaystyle\kappa_{n,m}(ab)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}^{par}(2n,2m)\\
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}\text{ unicyclic}\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\pi\\

W\subset\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{|W|}^{b}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi=\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\\
\mathcal{G}_{\pi_{1}}\text{ tree}\\
\mathcal{G}_{\pi_{2}}\text{ tree}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\pi_{1}\\

U,V\subset\mathcal{O}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{|U|,|V|}(a)\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\pi\\

W\subset\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{|W|}^{b}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi=\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\\
\mathcal{G}_{\pi_{1}}\text{ tree}\\
\mathcal{G}_{\pi_{2}}\text{ tree}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\pi_{1}\\
V\in\pi_{2}\\
U,V\subset\mathcal{E}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{|U|,|V|}(b)\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\pi\\
W\subset\mathcal{O}\end{subarray}}\kappa_{|W|}^{a}\right)\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\pi\\
W\subset\mathcal{E}\\
W\neq U,V\end{subarray}}\kappa_{|W|}^{b}\right)

where the second and third sums are over
\pi=\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\in\mathcal{NC}^{par}(2n)\times\mathcal{NC}^{par}(2m)
.

Proof of Lemma
6.3
.

Notice that we can make use of Theorem
1.3
with the only difference that there is only one
\varepsilon\in\{1,*\}^{n+m}
given by
\varepsilon=(1,\dots,1)
because we only have
\kappa_{n,m}(ab,ab,\dots,ab)
. Thus, in the first sum in Equation (
14

\pi\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}(2n,2m)
that we consider must be
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}^{par}(2n,2m)
so that
\{A(\varepsilon),B(\varepsilon)\}=\{\mathcal{O},\mathcal{E}\}\geq\pi
. Hence, any cycle of
\gamma^{-1}\pi
alternates even and odd numbers. Since we require that
\pi^{-1}\gamma
separates even numbers then every cycle of
\pi^{-1}\gamma
has size
2
and it consist of an even and an odd number, this is
\rho=\pi^{-1}\gamma\in\mathcal{NC}_{2}^{non-par}(2n,2m)
. Conversely if
\rho=\pi^{-1}\gamma\in\mathcal{NC}_{2}^{non-par}(2n,2m)
then
\pi=\gamma\rho\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}^{par}(2n,2m)
and
\pi^{-1}\gamma
separates even. This mean we can re-index the sum over
\rho\in\mathcal{NC}_{2}^{non-par}(2n,2m)
and the permutation that corresponds to each
\rho
is
\pi=\gamma\rho
. This gives the first term in the formula. For the second term, we have
\pi\in\mathcal{NC}(2n)\times\mathcal{NC}(2m)
, again it must be
\pi=\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\in\mathcal{NC}^{par}(2n)\times\mathcal{NC}^{par}(2m)
. The same argument as before shows
\pi^{-1}\gamma
has only cycles of size

consisting of one odd and one even number. Thus
\rho=\pi^{-1}\gamma\in\mathcal{NC}_{2}^{non-par}(2n)\times\mathcal{NC}_{2}^{non-par}(2m)
. From here the same argument shows that we can index our sum in terms of
\rho
. Finally we choose one cycle from each
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
to make a block of
\mathcal{U}
. Since we require
\{A(\varepsilon),B(\varepsilon)\}=\{\mathcal{O},\mathcal{E}\}\geq\mathcal{U}
then we must choose two cycles with the same parity. There are two possible options, each corresponding to the second and third terms in the right hand side of the formula.
∎

Proof of Lemma
6.4
.

This proof is similar to the previous. The first sum corresponding to
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}(2n,2m)
must be such that
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}^{par}(2n,2m)
. The same argument as before shows
\#(\pi^{-1}\gamma)=n+m
and therefore
\#(\pi)=n+m
, thus
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
is a connected graph with the same number of edges and vertices, namely
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
consist of one simple cycle. Conversely if
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
is a cycle, then
\#(\pi)=n+m
from where
\#(\pi^{-1}\gamma)=n+m
. Since
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}^{par}(2n,2m)
, then any cycle of
\pi^{-1}\gamma
alternates even and odd numbers, which means that every cycle has at least size
2
and therefore
\pi^{-1}\gamma
has

cycles each of size
2
, thus
\pi^{-1}\gamma\in\mathcal{NC}_{2}^{non-par}(2n,2m)
. So we can rewrite the first sum in Lemma
6.3
in terms of the graphs
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
that have one cycle. For the second and third sums we proceed similarly. Now
\pi\in\mathcal{NC}^{par}(2n)\times\mathcal{NC}^{par}(2m)
so
\#(\pi)=n+m+2
, further
\#(\pi_{1})=n+1
because
\#(\pi_{1}^{-1}\gamma|[2n])=n
from where we conclude
\mathcal{G}_{\pi_{1}}
must be a tree as it has

n

\pi_{1}^{-1}\gamma|[2n]

\mathcal{G}_{\pi_{1}}
is a tree then

, hence

\pi_{1}^{-1}\gamma\in\mathcal{NC}_{2}^{non-par}(2n)
. The same applies to
\mathcal{G}_{\pi_{2}}
. Finally we can choose one cycle from each
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
that has the same parity, yielding the second and third terms in the formula.
∎

One of the advantages this approach with graphs is that it greatly simplifies the computations in some cases. For instance when we consider centered variables.

Corollary 6.5
.

Let
a,b
be two centered second order free variables, that is
\kappa_{1}(a)=\kappa_{1}(b)=0
. Then, if
n,m\geq 1
with
n\neq m
we get
\kappa_{n,m}(ab)=0
. Otherwise,

(24)

\kappa_{n,n}(ab)=n[\kappa_{2}(a)\kappa_{2}(b)]^{n}\qquad\text{for }n\geq 1.

Proof.

We first apply Lemma
6.4
, and then look at which terms vanish. Notice that if a graph
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
has a leave (meaning a vertex of degree
1
) then
\pi
has a block of size
1
, say
U
, and since the variables are centered, then
\kappa_{|U|}(a)=\kappa_{|U|}(b)=0
and the whole product corresponding to
\pi
vanishes. Thus we only keep the terms indexed by
\pi
such that
\mathcal{G}_{\pi}
has no leaves. Since the second and third sums are indexed by trees, that must have a leave, then all the terms vanish and we get 0.
On the other hand, in the first sum we must have a simple cycle. So every cycle of
\pi
must have size
2
. Further
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{NC}}^{par}(2n,2m)
and then as proved in the proof of Lemma
6.4
it must be
\pi^{-1}\gamma\in\mathcal{NC}_{2}^{non-par}(2n,2m)
, particularly
\pi^{-1}\gamma
separates even. Let
(2k,2s)
be a cycle of
\pi
then
\pi^{-1}\gamma
has a cycle of the form

(\gamma^{-1}(2k),2s,\pi^{-1}(\gamma(2s)),\cdots).

If
\pi^{-1}(\gamma(2s))\neq\gamma^{-1}(2k)
then
\pi^{-1}(\gamma(\pi^{-1}(\gamma(2s))))
is an even number distinct to
2s
which is not possible. So, it must be
\pi^{-1}(\gamma(2s))=\gamma^{-1}(2k)
, thus
(\gamma(2s),\gamma^{-1}(2k))
is a cycle of
\pi
. This means that given
(2k,2s)
a cycle of
\pi
all its other cycles are determined and hence the permutation
\pi
is completely determined. Moreover, it must be
n=m
and there are exactly
n
possible permutations
\pi
(such a permutations will appear latter on and will be called spoke diagrams). Finally, for each
\pi
we get
\kappa_{\pi}(a,b,\dots,a,b)=[\kappa_{2}(a)\kappa_{2}(b)]^{n}
, yielding the desired result.
∎

7.
Application to second order free semicircular variables

As an application of our results, we will use them with one of the most important distributions in free probability, the semicircular distribution. Let us recall that in a non-commutative probability space, an element
a\in\mathcal{A}
in our algebra has
semicircular distribution
if its first order cumulants are all
0
except
\kappa_{2}(a)=1
. Further, we say that
a
has
second order semicircular distribution
if all its first and second order cumulants are
0
except
\kappa_{2}=\kappa_{2,2}=1
. As a motivation, these variables appear naturally as the large
N
-limit of Wigner
N\times N
random matrices, see for instance
[
MMPS22
,
MG24
]
.

7.1.
Anti-commutator

Using two different approaches we will prove that if
a,b
are two second order free semicircular elements, the second order cumulants of its anti-commutator have a simple expression. First a direct argument using the Kreweras complement and the permutation
I\pi
studied in Proposition
4.7
. In the second approach we identify the set of non-crossing annular pairings that contribute to the sum. These pairing actually have a very simple description.

Proof of Proposition
1.9
.

Applying Theorem
1.3
, and using that all the cumulants of
a
and
b
vanish except for
\kappa_{2}^{a}=\kappa_{2,2}^{a}=\kappa_{2}^{b}=\kappa_{2,2}^{b}=1
, then we know a term in (
3
)
will vanish unless all the elements of the product correspond to one of those four cumulants. In other words, the partition permutations must be a pairing, namely all blocks have size
2
.

Let us focus in the first sum in (
3
).
The only terms surviving are when
\pi
is a pairing. Moreover, each surviving term is just a product of ones, so contributes 1 to the sum. Thus, for the first sum we just need to count the number of pairings
\pi
in
\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
. Since
\pi
is a non-crossing annular pairing, then
|\pi|=n+m
and
|Kr_{2n,2m}(\pi)|=2n+2m-n-m=n+m

separates even, this means that each of the

cycles of

must contain an even element. In particular, no cycle of
Kr_{2n,2m}(\pi)
has only odd elements. By Proposition

4.7
, this means that
I\pi

I\pi=C^{\text{out}}C^{\text{inn}}
consists only of two cycles. Since both
I
and
\pi
are pairings, one can easily check that the two cycles determine each other in the following sense:

(26)

C^{\text{out}}=(c_{1}c_{2}\dots c_{j})\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad C^{\text{inn}}=(I(c_{j})I(c_{j-1})\dots I(c_{1})).

Notice that since these are the only two cycles, this in particular means that both cycles of
I\pi
have the same size
j=n+m
. Using part 1 of Proposition

4.7
, we conclude that
n+m
must be even. In other words, if
n+m
is odd then there are no pairings
\pi
in
\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}
so the first sum in (
3
). Assuming that
n+m
is even, we notice that parts 2 and 3 of Proposition

4.7
provide a specific description of the two cycles.
C^{\text{out}}
contains the elements
2,4,\dots,2n
in that order, so by (
26
) we obtain that
C^{\text{inn}}
must contain
2n-1,2n-3,\dots,1
in that order. Similarly

contains

in order, so

must contain
2n+2m-1,2n+2m-3,\dots,2n+1

Thus, to construct

we have to interpolate the numbers
2,4,\dots,2n
(in that order) with the elements
2n+2m-1,2n+2m-3,\dots,2n+1
(in that order). To count in how many ways this is possible, let us fix
2
as the first number in the cycle. For the remaining

\binom{n+m-1}{m}
ways to choose which
m
positions are occupied by the odd numbers and which
n-1
positions are occupied by the even numbers. The actual position of each even number is now fixed, as we started with 2 and they must be in increasing order. Finally we have
m
ways to choose which is the first odd number appearing in the cycle, once this is settled, the remaining odd numbers should be in decreasing order. This means that there are

\mathcal{J}_{2n,2m}

\pi

when

satisfies that

\pi^{\prime}
and

is a pairing. Moreover, in order for

Remark 7.1
.

One can readily generalize the previous proof to allow the four (non-vanishing) cumulants be different from 1. In this case we get that if
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
are second order free semicircular variables, then the second order cumulants of their anti-commutator are

\kappa_{n,m}^{ab+ba}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}2\frac{(n+m-1)!}{(n-1)!(m-1)!}(\kappa_{2}^{a}\kappa_{2}^{b})^{\frac{n+m}{2}}&\text{if }n\text{ and }m\text{ are odd, }\\

We now provide an alternative approach where we identify precisely which non-crossing pairings are counted by the term

and through cycles of the form

or

such that given a through string the rest of through strings are determined. The non-crossing pairing obtained by removing the interval cycles is usually called a

and it emerges in other calculations such as the cumulants of the square of a second order semicircular variable
[
AM23
, Example 8.6]
and the concept of second order real freeness
[
Red14
]
.

Proposition 7.5
.

For any
n,m\geq 1
,

|\mathcal{NC}_{2}^{spoke}(2n,2m)|=\frac{(n+m-1)!}{(n-1)!(m-1)!}.

Proof.

Set
\gamma=\gamma
. Let
\pi
be the permutation with cycles
(2s,\gamma(2s))
for any
1\leq s\leq n+m
. Any permutation
\sigma
in
\mathcal{NC}_{2}^{spoke}(2n,2m)
can be obtained from
\pi
by turning some of its cycles into through strings. Note that if
(2s,2u)
is a through string of
\sigma
then
\gamma(2s)
and
\gamma(2u)
must lie in a through string. This means that we are allowed to turn any cycle of
\pi
into through strings to get
\sigma
. In the outer circle we have as many as
n
cycles of
\pi
while in the inner circle we have
m
. So if
\sigma
has
2k
through strings, the number of ways to choose the cycles of
\pi
is to make them into through strings of
\sigma
is
\binom{n}{k}\binom{m}{k}
. Once we have a choice we just need to pair any two elements (in distinct circles and with the same parity) to get a through string. By definition of
\mathcal{NC}_{2}^{spoke}(2n,2m)
the rest of through strings are determined. This can be done in

distinct ways. Let us assume
n\leq m
, hence

\displaystyle|\mathcal{NC}_{2}^{spoke}(2n,2m)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}k\binom{n}{k}\binom{m}{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(k+1)\binom{n}{k+1}\binom{m}{k+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k+1}\binom{m-1}{k}=m\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n}{n-1-k}\binom{m-1}{k}

The sum
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n}{n-1-k}\binom{m-1}{k}
counts the number of ways of choosing

, hence

Proof of Proposition

.

Recall that formulas for the commutator and anti-commutator have the same terms, what changes is the sign. Thus, we can follow the proof of Proposition
1.9
to check that
I\pi=C^{\text{out}}C^{\text{inn}}

must interpolate the numbers

is even. Now, we need to check the sign
s(\pi)
associated to each permutation constructed in this way. Turns out that the sign is always the same. Indeed, recall from the prof of Proposition
4.7
, that if the natural bipartite decomposition is
\pi=\pi^{\prime}\sqcup\pi^{\prime\prime}
, and we denote
A^{\prime}=\cup\pi^{\prime}
and

(29)

I\pi(A^{\prime})\subset A^{\prime\prime},\qquad\text{and}\qquad I\pi(A^{\prime\prime})\subset A^{\prime}.

In particular, since

by assumption. The elements of

at an odd position in the cycle (starting with 2) are in

19

\pi
