Enhancing foot-and-mouth disease virus thermostability with a VP1 substitution selected by host HSP60 inhibition
Authors: Mei Ren, Haiqian Zhou, Hu Dong, Jinen Wu, Jianing Wang, Jiaxi Yu, Huichen Guo, Shiqi Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFoot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV) exhibits greater temperature sensitivity compared to many other picornaviruses, which poses a substantial challenge for the stability of inactivated vaccines. Therefore, enhancing the thermal stability of the FMDV capsid is crucial. FMDV exists as a quasispecies with high mutation rates, providing a basis for selection under pressure. Our group previously discovered that heat shock protein HSP60 was recruited to support FMDV replication by promoting the assembly of the viral replication complex (RC). By applying the HSP60 inhibitor mizoribine, we selected FMDV variants with enhanced fitness under conditions of impaired HSP60 function. Subsequent sequencing of the selected populations and reverse genetics approaches identified a key amino acid substitution, T171P in the viral VP1 protein that confers resistance to the HSP60 inhibitor. Structural simulation predicted that the T171P substitution enhances the stability of the VP1 protein, which was further supported by experimental evidence demonstrating increased thermal stability of the whole viral capsid. A series of experiments, including virion stability assessment using the PaSTRy assay, quantification of intact particles following heat challenge, and evaluation of viral infectivity at elevated temperatures, demonstrated that the T171P-VP1 variant possesses enhanced thermostability compared to the wild-type (WT) virus. Furthermore, immunization experiments showed that the inactivated T171P-VP1 variant, after heat challenge, elicited a stronger neutralizing antibody response than the similarly treated WT virus. This study identifies a novel amino acid substitution that significantly enhances FMDV thermostability. This finding not only provides a viable strategy for improving the thermal stability of FMD vaccines but also opens new avenues for exploring the intricate mechanisms of host-virus interaction.Key points• A novel HSP60 inhibitor-based selection strategy identified the T171P-VP1 substitution conferring enhanced viral fitness.• The T171P substitution significantly improves FMDV capsid thermostability compared to the wild-type virus.• The inactivated T171P-VP1 variant induces stronger neutralizing antibody responses after heat challenge, offering a promising approach for thermostable FMD vaccine development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00253-026-13971-2.

Introduction

Foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV), a member of the Picornaviridae family, possesses a positive-sense single-stranded RNA genome (Domingo et al. 2002). The viral structural proteins VP0, VP1, and VP3 assemble into protomers, which further form pentamers and ultimately icosahedral capsids that package the newly synthesized viral RNA to form infectious progeny viruses (Belsham 2005; Jiang et al. 2014). FMDV infects cloven-hoofed animals including cattle, sheep, and pigs (Sutmoller and Casas 2002; Jamal and Belsham 2013; Stenfeldt et al. 2016). The resulting foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) spreads rapidly and causes substantial economic losses in the livestock industry through reduced productivity, trade restrictions on animal products, and increased epidemic prevention costs. Consequently, developing more effective FMD vaccines represents a critical research priority.

Heat shock proteins (HSPs) are molecular chaperones upregulated under various cellular stress conditions (Singh et al. 2024), where they facilitate protein folding, assembly, and transport. Among these chaperones, the 60-kDa heat shock protein (HSP60) facilitates the proper folding of newly synthesized polypeptides within mitochondria, thereby ensuring their functional integrity (Barrios et al. 1994; Caruso Bavisotto et al. 2020). Our previous work demonstrated that HSP60 participates in FMDV replication and translation processes, thereby influencing viral infectivity (Tang et al. 2022). We therefore hypothesize that inhibiting HSP60 function could select for FMDV variants capable of replicating independently of this host chaperone. Furthermore, we proposed that such HSP60-independent variants might exhibit altered biological characteristics compared to the wild-type (WT) virus. To test this hypothesis, we employed the HSP60 inhibitor mizoribine, which directly binds to HSP60 and inhibits both the chaperone activity of the HSP60-HSP10 complex (Itoh et al. 1999) and the ATPase activity of HSP60 (Meng et al. 2018).

In this study, we employed an HSP60 inhibitor to select thermostable FMDV variants and confirmed the functional role of specific amino acid substitutions using a reverse genetics system. Our findings provide novel insights into host-virus interactions and identify a specific VP1 substitution that significantly enhances the thermal stability of FMDV. This discovery offers a promising strategy for improving conventional FMD vaccines and informs the development of next-generation vaccines, including virus-like particles (VLPs).

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

According to the Animal Ethics Procedures and Guidelines of the Ministry of Science and Technology of the People’s Republic of China, all animal experiments were performed strictly following good animal practices. The study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences with permission number SCXK-GAN-2020-0002.

Cells, viruses, and plasmids

Baby hamster kidney-21 (BHK-21) cells were obtained from Lanzhou veterinary research institute and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 μg/mL) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere.

FMDV serotype O strain O/China/99 (GenBank accession no. AF506822.2) was obtained from the OIE/National Foot-and-Mouth Disease Reference Laboratory (Lanzhou, China). FMDV was propagated in BHK-21 cells, and viral titers were determined using a 50% tissue culture infective dose (TCID50) assay during selection and a plaque assay in other experiments (Bachrach et al. 1957). Virus antigens were purified through a 15 to 45% (weight per volume, wt/vol) sucrose density gradient and examined by negative-stain electron microscopy, as described previously (Dong et al. 2021, 2022).

The full‑length FMDV genome was inserted into the pcDNA3.1 vector and the resulting plasmid, designated pcFMDV, was synthesized by GenScript Biotech Corporation (Piscataway, NJ, USA). This plasmid was used to establish the reverse genetics system. To facilitate site‑directed mutagenesis, an intermediate subclone (pcFMDV-EN) was constructed by subcloning a fragment from pcFMDV into a separate plasmid vector using the EcoRV and NotI restriction sites.

Antibodies and reagents

Mouse anti-β-actin monoclonal antibody (mAb) was purchased from CWBIO (Beijing, China), while pig serum polyclonal antibody (pAb) against FMDV was prepared in our laboratory. Secondary antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) and fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Merck KGaA) (St. Louis, MO, USA). Lipofectamine 2000 and SYTO 9 green fluorescent nucleic acid dye were purchased from Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Mizoribine (NSC 289637) was obtained from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China) and dissolved in 0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Tragacanth gum powder for plaque assays was sourced from Wako Pure Chemical Industries (Osaka, Japan).

2 × Minimum Essential Medium (2 × MEM) and enhanced chemiluminescence detection reagent (ECL) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. The CellTiter 96 AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay reagent used in cell viability assays was obtained from Promega (Madison, WI, USA). RNAi Plus reagent and PrimeSTAR® HS DNA Polymerase were sourced from TaKaRa (Dalian, China). Binary ethylenimine (BEI) was purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China), while restriction enzymes were from New England Biolabs (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA). All primers were synthesized by Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Xi'an, China).

Cell viability assay

Cell viability under the application of the HSP60 inhibitor (mizoribine) was assessed using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. The experiments were performed as described previously (Teng et al. 2022).

Plaque purification

Plaque assays were conducted as described (Bachrach et al. 1957). Individual plaques were picked with sterile pipette tips, transferred into serum-free DMEM, and amplified by infecting fresh BHK-21 cells. This process was repeated three times to ensure clonal purity. Purified clonal viruses were aliquoted and stored at – 80 °C.

Total RNA was extracted from cells using RNAi Plus reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Reverse transcription was then performed to synthesize cDNA. Immunoblotting assay

Cells were lysed directly in sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) loading buffer. Immunoblotting was performed as previously described (Abdullah et al. 2021).

Viral genome sequencing and analysis

The viral coding sequences were amplified by PCR from cDNA using the primers listed in Table 1. The PCR product was gel-purified and subjected to Sanger sequencing. The nucleotide sequences were translated in silico, and the resulting amino acid sequences were aligned. Sequence alignments were analyzed using MegAlign (MegAlign, Madison, WI, USA). Amino acid substitutions were mapped onto the viral protein sequences (e.g., T171P-VP1 is a threonine-to-proline change at position 171 of VP1).

Virus rescue by reverse genetics

The infectious FMDV was rescued by transfecting the full-length FMDV genomic plasmid (under the control of a CMV (cauliflower mosaic virus) promoter) into BHK-21 cells using Lipofectamine 2000, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Transfection was performed as previously described (Abdullah et al. 2021). Specifically, the synthesized plasmid (pcFMDV) was transfected into BHK‑21 cells cultured in 6‑well plates at approximately 70% confluence. For each well, 2 µg of plasmid DNA and 4 µL of Lipofectamine 2000 were used in a total volume of 2 mL of DMEM per well, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. At 48 h post‑transfection, the cells were harvested, subjected to three cycles of freezing and thawing, and centrifuged (Lu et al. 2009). The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22‑μm filter to remove cell debris, and 100 μL of the filtrate was used to inoculate fresh BHK-21 monolayers (90% confluence) in 6-well plates. Cells were then incubated at 37 °C. At 8 h post-inoculation, cells were harvested. After sequence confirmation, the virus was passaged to the fifth generation for further validation (Perales et al. 2009). Mock-transfected cell lysates (cells transfected with empty pcDNA3.1 vector; GenScript Biotech Corporation, Piscataway, NJ, USA) processed in parallel served as negative controls.

Indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA)

The expression of FMDV structural proteins was detected by indirect immunofluorescence. The assay was performed as previously described (Wang et al. 2025).

Site-directed mutagenesis

Site-directed mutations were introduced into an intermediate plasmid (pcFMDV-EN) by PCR using PrimeSTAR® HS DNA Polymerase. Mutagenic primers were designed online using the QuikChange Primer Design website (https://www.agilent.com.cn/store/primerDesignProgram.jsp; accessed 8 April 2025) to ensure high specificity and an optimal melting temperature (Tm) (Table 2). The PCR products were treated with DpnI restriction enzyme to selectively digest the methylated parental plasmid template, thereby enriching for the newly synthesized mutated DNA. The nicked plasmid DNA was then transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells (Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Beijing, China). Positive clones were screened by colony PCR, and the desired nucleotide changes were verified by DNA sequencing (Tsingke Biotechnology). The verified site-mutated pcFMDV-EN were subsequently assembled into the full-length FMDV infectious clone using the in-fusion cloning, facilitated by the restriction endonuclease sites, BsiWI and StuI. The resultant full-length mutant plasmids were used to rescue infectious viruses. Structural modeling and analysis

All structural modeling and energy calculations were performed using YASARA software with the FoldX plugin (Vienna, Austria) (Schymkowitz et al. 2005; Van Durme et al. 2011; Krieger and Vriend 2014). The initial wild-type FMDV VP1 protein structure (PDB: 7DSS) was energy-minimized and repaired using the “RepairPDB” function in FoldX to correct steric clashes and optimize the hydrogen bonding network. The substitution was then introduced into the refined wild-type structure, followed by local energy minimization to relieve atomic clashes. The root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of the Cα atomic positions between the wild-type and mutant structures was calculated by after superposing their protein backbones, providing a quantitative measure of global structural change. The folding free energy (ΔG) was computed for both structures using the FoldX “Stability” function. The change in folding free energy (ΔΔG) was calculated as ΔG mutant – ΔG wild-type. Structural visualization, highlighting of the mutation site, and figure generation were performed using PyMOL Molecular Graphics System (Version 3.0, Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, USA).

Virus inactivation and purification

For virus inactivation, the viruses were treated with 0.01 M BEI at 28 °C. The inactivated virus was clarified by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris. The inactivated virus was purified using sucrose density gradient centrifugation (SDGC) with a 15–45% sucrose gradient. The purified virus was verified by immunoblotting and negative-stain electron microscopy, as described in our previous study (Dong et al. 2021).

Particle stability thermal-release assay (PaSTRy)

The particle stability thermal release assay (PaSTRy) was performed to assess the stability of FMDV virions (Walter et al. 2012; Kotecha et al. 2016). A reaction mixture of 50 μL containing 2 mg SDG-purified 146S particles and 5mM SYTO 9 green fluorescent nucleic acid dye was run under the temperature ramped from 25 to 95 °C in 0.5 °C increments with intervals of 10 s. The viral RNA would be released from capsids and detected by an increase in the fluorescence signal. The melting temperature was determined by identifying the minimum value of the negative first derivative of the fluorescence curve.

Heat resistance of inactivated virions

To evaluate the particle stability of the mutant virus, SDG-purified inactivated virions were continuously heat dissociated at 37 °C, and samples were collected every 12 h to detect the remaining intact viral particles by a liquid-phase blocking ELISA (LPBE) using the broadly neutralizing single-domain antibody (VHH) M8 (Harmsen et al. 2017, 2022; Li et al. 2025). Briefly, 96-well ELISA plates were coated with M8 VHH antibody at 4 °C overnight and then blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at 37 °C for 1 h. Serial dilutions of heat-treated virus samples were pre-incubated with pig anti-FMDV polyclonal antibody at 37 °C for 1 h. The mixture was then transferred to the M8-coated plates and incubated at 37 °C for an additional 1 h to allow binding of unneutralized intact virions to the immobilized M8. After washing, biotinylated M8 VHH antibody was added and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to detect captured virions, followed by HRP-conjugated streptavidin. Finally, the plates were developed with 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate for 15 min, the reaction was stopped with 2 M H₂SO₄, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm. The percentage of intact capsids was calculated based on a standard curve generated with known concentrations of intact FMDV particles, and the proportion of intact capsids relative to untreated controls was determined.

Mouse immunization and sample collection

Mouse experiments were performed in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines for animal welfare. All procedures, including immunization and blood collection, were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute (Permission No. SCXK-GAN-2020-0002). Female BALB/c mice were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute (Lanzhou, China). All mice were confirmed healthy and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12/12-h light/dark cycle, provided with standard rodent chow and sterile water ad libitum. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental and control groups (n = 6 per group). The sample size was determined based on preliminary data to minimize the number of animals used while ensuring scientific validity. Mice were immunized via intramuscular injection with PBS (negative control), BEI-inactivated WT FMDV, or BEI-inactivated mutant FMDV. Mild anesthesia or local analgesics would be applied where necessary to reduce pain. Following immunization, mice were monitored regularly for any signs of distress. Analgesics would be administered when needed. If pain persisted, animals would be humanely euthanized. Blood samples were collected at 0, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days post-vaccination (dpv) using gentle handling techniques to minimize stress. The sample volume was strictly controlled to avoid significant physiological impact. This experiment strictly adhered to the principles of animal welfare to ensure a balance between scientific research and ethical protection.

Quantification of FMDV-specific antibody

Serum levels of serotype O FMDV-specific antibodies were determined by ELISA as previously described (Dong et al. 2021).

Quantification of FMDV neutralizing antibody

Neutralizing antibody titers against serotype O FMDV in mouse sera were determined using a microplate neutralizing assay following established methods (Dong et al. 2021).

Multiple sequence alignment

The conservation of the amino acid position was assessed by multiple sequence alignment of 50 FMDV strains (including 41 serotype O strains and 9 strains representing all seven serotypes) sourced from GenBank. Sequence logos were generated using WebLogo (Crooks et al. 2004) to visualize positional conservation.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to assess the significance of differences among groups by Prism version 9.3.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05 for all tests. All error bars represent the standard deviation of the mean.

Results

Selection of FMDV strains resistant to Hsp60 inhibitor

The RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) of FMDV lacks proofreading activity, leading to a high mutation rate during viral replication (Belsham 2005; Belsham and Normann 2008; Nie et al. 2024). This high mutation rate generates a FMDV population, quasi-species, which consists of a series of closely related genetic variants rather than a single genotype (Domingo and Perales 2019). To select for viral variants capable of replicating under impaired HSP60 chaperone function, we serially passaged an FMDV quasispecies population in BHK-21 cells treated with the HSP60 inhibitor mizoribine, which is known to disrupt the chaperone activity of the HSP60-HSP10 complex (Itoh et al. 1999; Singh et al. 2020).

Our experimental strategy for selecting resistant variants is outlined in Fig. 1A. We first determined a non-cytotoxic concentration of mizoribine (1 mM) for BHK-21 cells using an MTT assay (Fig. 1B). To confirm the inhibitory effect of mizoribine on HSP60 function, we monitored the expression of Bcl-x(L), a downstream client protein whose folding depends on HSP60’s ATPase activity (Shan et al. 2003). The resistance of the selected strains to HSP60 inhibition was validated at multiple levels. Compared to WT virus, the selected strains exhibited significantly higher viral titers (Fig. 1F), higher viral protein expression (Fig. 1G), and maintained stable viral RNA levels following mizoribine treatment, whereas the WT virus showed a significant reduction in RNA level (Fig. 1H). Establishment of a reverse genetics system for FMDV

To functionally characterize the amino acid substitutions identified in the selected strains, we established a reverse genetics system. A synonymous nucleotide mutation was introduced into the 3D gene region of the full-length FMDV genome to serve as a genetic marker for distinguishing the rescued virus from the WT virus. Infectious virus (designated pFMDV) was rescued by transfecting the pcFMDV plasmid into BHK-21 cells at approximately 60% confluency. The rescued virus was serially passaged in BHK-21 cells to amplify the stock. We then validated the rescued pFMDV at passage 5. Indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) confirmed successful viral protein expression (Fig. 2A). RT-qPCR analysis showed no significant difference in viral RNA levels between pFMDV and the WT virus at passages 4 and 5 (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, the one-step growth kinetics of pFMDV were comparable to those of the WT virus (Fig. 2C). Finally, Sanger sequencing confirmed the presence of the engineered synonymous marker in the rescued pFMDV population (Fig. 2D). The T171P-VP1 variant exhibits a resistance to HSP60 inhibition

Following sequencing, we used the reverse genetics system to rescue viruses carrying each identified amino acid substitution. Plaque assays revealed that the T171P-VP1 variant maintained similar viral titers in the presence or absence of the HSP60 inhibitor, unlike the WT virus which showed a significant titer reduction under inhibitor treatment (Fig. 4A). We first assessed whether the substitution induced major structural perturbations. The root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) between the WT and T171P VP1 structures was 0.526 Å, indicating minimal overall structural change (Reva et al. 1998). We next examined the evolutionary conservation of this site. Multiple sequence alignment of VP1 from 41 serotype O FMDV strains revealed high conservation at position 171 (Fig. 5C; see Supplementary Fig. S3A for full alignment). Furthermore, analysis across all seven FMDV serotypes showed that position 171 consistently contains polar amino acids (T, E, D), while the T171P substitution introduces a nonpolar proline residue (Fig. 5D; complete alignment in Supplementary Fig. S3B).

Given that proline can enhance protein thermostability by reducing polypeptide chain flexibility and conformational entropy (Choi and Mayo 2006), we computed the folding free energy (ΔG) for both structures. The T171P substituted VP1 exhibited a lower ΔG (124.78 kcal/mol) compared to WT VP1 (130.93 kcal/mol), resulting in a ΔΔG of − 6.15 kcal/mol. (ΔΔG = ΔG mutated − ΔG wild-type). This decrease in free energy indicates enhanced structural stability, leading us to hypothesize that the T171P substitution might improve viral capsid stability, particularly thermotolerance.

We incubated the viruses for 30 min under different temperature conditions. Both the T171P-VP1 variant and the WT virus lost infectivity at 65 °C. In the PaSTRy assay for picornaviruses, upon heating of the virus, when the particles disassociate at a certain temperature, exposing the viral genome, the fluorescent probes, such as SYTO-9 in our study, would bind to the exposed genome and emit a strong fluorescent signal (Walter et al. 2012). To quantify intact virions after heat exposure, we used an ELISA-based method with VHH antibodies specifically recognizing 146S particles (Harmsen et al. 2017, 2022). Following incubation at 37 °C, the T171P-VP1 variant maintained a significantly higher proportion of intact particles than WT after 24 h (Fig. 6C). Although this difference diminished at later time points (36–48 h), the sustained initial stability further supports the enhanced thermotolerance of the T171P-VP1 variant.

Discussion

At present, the main strategy for controlling FMD is the use of vaccines, especially inactivated vaccines (Cao et al. 2016). These vaccines preserve the intact FMDV capsid, enabling the presentation of antigenic determinants (primarily on VP1) that trigger B cell differentiation and the production of neutralizing antibodies. These antibodies bind viral surface antigens, preventing attachment and entry into host cells, thereby neutralizing infectivity (Bachmann and Jennings 2010; Ren et al. 2024). However, a major limitation of current inactivated FMD vaccines is their thermal lability. Unlike other picornaviruses, the FMDV capsid is exceptionally heat-sensitive, readily dissociating from the immunogenic 146S intact virion into 12S pentamers, which are poor inducers of neutralizing antibodies (Cartwright et al. 1980; Harmsen et al. 2011; Caridi et al. 2017). This instability necessitates a stringent cold chain to maintain vaccine efficacy (Caridi et al. 2017). Our study addresses this critical issue by identifying a specific amino acid substitution, T171P in VP1, that significantly enhances the thermostability of the FMDV capsid.

Conventional approaches for generating thermostable FMDV variants have involved serial passage under elevated temperatures. In 1960, Bachrach et al. reported heat-resistant FMDV after 3–4 passages of heat screening, although the trait was lost subsequently, likely due to a mixed viral population (Bachrach et al. 1960). Cui et al. (2010) found that the expression of HSP60 mRNA of hemocytes from the Asiatic rice borer Chilo suppressalis increased significantly after the heat shock. Also, Lin et al. (2023) reported that under temperatures exceeding optimal conditions, HSP60 was required to stabilize a key protein in rice, ensuring its normal function. Our group previously found that HSP60 participates in the formation of the replication complex of FMDV (Tang et al. 2022). Therefore, we assumed that HSP60 inhibitors could be used to affect the replication process, thereby screening out strains with higher capsid stability from the viral quasispecies. This approach led to the identification of several variants (Fig. 1E). We propose that these variants, including T171P-VP1, were likely pre-existing within the FMDV quasispecies, suggesting that the HSP60 inhibitor did not induce new mutations but selectively enriched these pre-adapted minorities. This selective enrichment would be consistent with classical quasispecies dynamics (Domingo and Perales 2019).

After obtaining the amino acid substituted strain through a reverse genetics system (Fig. 3B–D), we verified the HSP60 inhibitor resistance through viral virulence, growth kinetics, and viral protein levels, identifying T171P-VP1 as a resistant variant (Fig. 4). Resistance was confirmed by the plaque reduction test. The T171P substitution on VP1 introduces a proline residue, which has been widely reported to enhance conformational stability in microbial proteins under high-temperature conditions through increased rigidity (Fleming and Littlechild 1997; Korkhin et al. 1999; Littlechild et al. 2013). Our structural simulation analysis confirmed that T171P substitution improved VP1, consistent with these early reports. Importantly, the threonine at VP1-171 is highly conserved among serotype O strains but is not conserved in other serotypes (Fig. 5C–D). Therefore, the stabilizing effect of the T171P substitution is likely serotype‑O‑specific, and extrapolation of this finding to non‑O serotypes requires further experimental validation.

In addition to the T171P-VP1 substitution, we also identified several amino acid changes in the non-structural protein 3 A in the HSP60 inhibitor-selected population (Table 3). However, the FMDV 3 A protein is primarily involved in viral replication complex formation and host range determination rather than directly modulating capsid stability (González-Magaldi et al. 2014; Sun et al. 2016; Rodríguez Pulido and Sáiz 2017). Capsid thermal stability is principally determined by residues within structural proteins (Mateo et al. 2008; Dong et al. 2021; Li et al. 2024). Therefore, our functional characterization focused on the T171P-VP1 substitution as the direct determinant of the observed thermostability enhancement.

The focus on a single substitution (T171P) is supported by previous reports demonstrating that a single amino acid change can suffice to enhance FMDV thermostability without compromising immunogenicity. For instance, Biswal et al. (2022) showed that a single VP2_Y98F substitution conferred significant thermostability and protective immunity in cattle, and Gao et al. (2021) found that a single substitution (Y2098F) was sufficient to achieve the desired enhancement. These findings support that focusing on a single key substitution is a valid strategy for thermostable vaccine development.

Further heat resistance assays, including viral titer determination, PaSTRy, and intact particle quantification, confirmed that the T171P-VP1 variant exhibits enhanced thermostability (Fig. 6A–C). Since the viral lysis threshold occurs between 50 and 60 °C (Kamolsiripichaiporn et al. 2007; Dong et al. 2021), our experimental design prioritized assessing stability near this critical threshold.

The long‑term stability data at 4 °C are ultimately required for vaccine development. However, the primary goal of this study was to identify a thermostability‑enhancing substitution using short‑term heat challenges, a strategy widely adopted in the field (Mateo et al. 2008; Gao et al. 2021). Accelerated stability testing based on the Arrhenius/Q₁₀ principle is an established method to predict long‑term storage stability without extended real‑time studies (Egan and Schofield 2009), and has been successfully applied to viral vaccines including rotavirus and FMDV (Kotecha et al. 2016; Pullagurla et al. 2022). Applying this principle, the superior performance of T171P‑VP1 at elevated temperatures (Fig. 6) strongly predicts improved stability during prolonged 4 °C storage. This correlation is further supported by Biswal et al. (2022), who demonstrated that a short‑term heat‑selected FMDV mutant maintained enhanced stability for up to eight months at 4 °C. Thus, our accelerated thermal stability data provide a robust scientific basis for predicting the long‑term stability of the T171P‑VP1 variant.

The T171 site is located near the GH loop (aa141-160) of VP1, which is the main neutralizing antigenic determinant of the virus (Li et al. 2024). Therefore, an animal experiment was conducted to evaluate the ability of heat-treated viral strains to induce immune responses in mice. This result indicated that the T171P mutation did not compromise antigenicity. While heat-treated inactivated WT induced significantly lower antibody responses compared to non-heat-treated WT, the T171P-VP1 variant showed no significant difference in specific or neutralizing antibody levels between heat-treated and non-heat-treated groups (Fig. 7B, C). Moreover, at 28 days post-vaccination, both specific and neutralizing antibody levels in the heat-treated T171P-VP1 group were significantly higher than those in the corresponding WT group (Fig. 7D, E).

Importantly, these findings demonstrate that the heat-resistant T171P-VP1 variant retains its ability to induce strong humoral immune responses after thermal exposure. This has practical implications for vaccine development. Introducing stabilizing mutations like T171P-VP1 into the viral capsid can enhance the thermal robustness of existing vaccines, improving their stability during storage and transportation and reducing dependence on cold chain logistics.

A potential limitation of the T171P-VP1 variant is its lower infectious titer compared to the WT virus. This observation aligns with previous reports in which capsid-stabilizing substitutions in picornaviruses sometimes reduce replication efficiency (Mateo et al. 2008; Dong et al. 2021). However, the modest reduction (with peak titers still reaching ∼104·5 TCID50/mL) does not preclude vaccine production, as such titers are routinely used for small-scale inactivation and formulation. More importantly, the key advantage of the T171P variant lies in its thermostability, which translates into better preservation of neutralizing epitopes under thermal stress. As shown in Fig. 7C, heat-challenged T171P virions induced significantly higher neutralizing antibody responses than the wild-type, demonstrating that the trade-off is acceptable in the context of vaccine development.

One important consideration regarding the T171P substitution is how increased capsid stability might influence the viral life cycle and immune recognition. Structurally, introducing a proline at VP1 position 171 likely stabilizes the local backbone by reducing the conformational entropy of the unfolded state, consistent with the observed 2 °C rise in Tm and greater retention of intact 146S particles after thermal challenge. In picornaviruses, capsid uncoating during entry requires expansion or disassembly (Bergelson and Coyne 2013), and mutations that increase rigidity might impair this process, potentially explaining the moderately reduced TCID₅₀ of the T171P variant. Regarding immune recognition, position 171 lies outside known major antigenic sites such as the GH loop (residues 133–160) (Kotecha et al. 2015; Li et al. 2024), and heat‑challenged T171P virions induced significantly higher neutralizing antibody titers than wild type (Fig. 7C), indicating that the mutation preserves—and under thermal stress better maintains—native antigenic conformation. We acknowledge that our structural conclusions rely on computational modeling. High‑resolution methods such as cryo‑EM or X‑ray crystallography would be required for atomic‑level validation. Nevertheless, multiple lines of functional evidence support that the T171P substitution enhances capsid thermostability. The T171P variant exhibited a 2 °C higher Tm in the PaSTRy assay and greater retention of intact 146S particles after heat challenge. Heat‑challenged T171P virions induced higher neutralizing antibody titers than wild type (Fig. 7C). The reduced TCID₅₀ is consistent with increased capsid rigidity affecting uncoating efficiency. The mutation remained genetically stable without compensatory mutations after extended passage. This functional evidence strongly indicates that T171P stabilizes the FMDV capsid without compromising its native antigenicity.

This study employed HSP60 inhibitor screening to isolate resistant FMDV variants. Using reverse genetics, we identified a key VP1‑T171P substitution that confers this resistance. Further characterization revealed that this substitution enhances capsid stability, thereby significantly improving virion thermostability. These findings offer a novel strategy for increasing the thermal resilience of FMD vaccines. Moreover, the T171P substitution can be incorporated into next‑generation vaccine platforms, such as virus‑like particles, to enhance their stability and support more effective FMD control.
