From quality indicator to quality measurement: design and implementation of an automated audit and feedback tool for chronic kidney disease in Belgian primary care
Authors: Justine Soetaert, Laurien Paredis, Bert Vaes, Ine Van den Wyngaert, Gijs Van Pottelbergh, Steve Van den Bulck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo design and implement an automated audit and feedback (A&F) tool for Belgian Electronic Medical Record (EMR) systems to assess and improve the quality of primary care for chronic kidney disease (CKD).Methods36 quality indicators (QIs) for primary CKD care were translated into conjugations and queries programmable within EMR systems. In the development phase, executable queries were constructed, taking into account technical constraints and the reliability of available data. The Intego database, a Belgian general practice-based morbidity registration network and A&F implementation laboratory, was used to test the feasibility and pilot the intervention. Visual feedback dashboards were designed using SAS Enterprise Guide and SAS Visual Analytics.ResultsA final set of 80 queries that could be built into EMR systems was developed, allowing for an automated audit. The design of visual feedback and follow-up queries to make the feedback actionable was described. During the selection process 16 of the 36 indicators were excluded and 10 were adapted for the audit and/or feedback due to technical limitations or unreliable data. The final feedback included 14 indicators presented to clinicians through interactive dashboards.DiscussionThis study describes the design of an automated A&F tool to improve the quality of primary CKD care in Belgium. Challenges included data accuracy within EMRs, the variability in data completeness and limitations in EMR query functionality. Future improvements in EMR systems and coding practices, such as the implementation of the Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine Clinical Terms (SNOMED CT) and improving feedback accessibility, could enhance the effectiveness of the tool.ConclusionThis study provides building blocks for implementing automated A&F interventions in Belgian EMR systems to improve primary CKD care quality. These building blocks could be applied internationally after validating the QIs.

Introduction

For more than two decades, the quality of healthcare has been an increasingly important topic in society. Since the publication of two major reports from the US Institute of Medicine, the need to implement systems to make healthcare safer and more efficient has become abundantly clear.1 2 Quality assessment is necessary to identify ‘blind spots’ in professional practice and healthcare delivery.3 However, evaluating and improving the quality of care for patients with chronic medical conditions and their comorbidities can be extremely challenging due to an ageing population and a high perceived workload, especially among general practitioners (GPs).4

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a common chronic medical condition in which GPs play a central role in early detection and follow-up.5 CKD, defined as structural or functional kidney damage, is associated with increased morbidity and mortality, even in the early stages.6 CKD also places a heavy burden on healthcare finances. Between 2017 and 2022 in Belgium, the National Institute for Health and Disability Insurance (NIHDI) reported, with 14.125 interventions per year, an average annual expenditure of €478 804 083 on dialysis, which represents 1.33% of the total expenses of the NIHDI.7 However, despite the high impact of CKD, the under-registration (defined as not having a diagnostic code in the electronic medical record (EMR)) of CKD in general practice is problematic. A previous study found that 68% of patients with CKD did not have a diagnostic code in their EMR.8 Taken together, these findings highlight the need for systems that could improve the quality of primary care for CKD and correct its coded registration in the GP’s EMR.5

A strategy to evaluate and improve the quality of care is an audit and feedback (A&F) intervention.9 In such an intervention, feedback on the performance of health professionals regarding a (chronic) condition is provided based on predetermined measurements. When data from EMRs is used, the audit and visualisation process can be automated, provided that key design features are considered.10 In general practice in Belgium, these automated A&F interventions have been used to gather data at the practice level.11 Furthermore, A&F implementation laboratories such as Intego, a Belgian general practice-based morbidity registration network and A&F implementation laboratory, could be useful as testing grounds to run pilots of automated A&F interventions.12 13

The first essential step in evaluating the quality of care for CKD is the development of a tool that enables such assessment. This paper describes the creation of an automated A&F intervention designed for that purpose. The process began with the development of quality indicators (QIs) that can be automatically extracted from GPs’ EMRs. QIs could be developed using the RAND-modified Delphi method14 and have been established for various chronic medical conditions, including CKD.15 The second prerequisite is the translation of QIs into queries that could be programmed by EMR vendors into their software. To this end, the audit needs to be conjugated into small building blocks that could be programmed into both imperative and declarative programming languages. Subsequently, feedback based on the results of these queries must be designed.

This study aimed to facilitate the transition from theory to practice by designing building blocks/queries for an automated A&F intervention for CKD that could be easily programmed into the EMR. In addition, the feasibility and technical implementation of automated queries within EMR systems were evaluated. Finally, actionable visual feedback for CKD care and follow-up queries were constructed to support quality improvement interventions.

Methods

Belgian context and data source

To contextualise the development of this A&F instrument, the Belgian primary care setting is briefly described. In Belgium, eight EMR software packages for general practice have been homologated by the federal government. The global medical record (GMR) is not a separate EMR type but an administrative record within it, linked to a financial incentive. It designates a GP or practice responsible for maintaining the patient’s EMR and coordinating chronic care, while patients remain free to consult other GPs. In 2023, 88% of the Belgian population had a GMR.7

The queries were developed and tested using Intego, a Belgian general practice-based morbidity registration network.16 The Intego database contains structured EMR data from 134 practices, covering approximately 5% of the Flemish population. Participating practices submit weekly longitudinal data extractions, which are integrated into the Intego database.

Audit and feedback tool

The A&F tool for general practice enables practices to conduct automated assessments at the practice level. The queries are designed to automatically identify and count patients with specific characteristics within a practice over a well-defined time frame. Maintaining a consistent time window across all participating practices is essential to ensure data comparability. Quality indicators for CKD

A previously developed set of 36 QIs that provided a comprehensive representation of the current quality of care for patients with CKD by GPs were the basis for this study.15 These indicators assess whether CKD has been documented in the patient’s problem list within the EMR and address various aspects of care, including screening, diagnosis, follow-up, treatment, medication management and patient safety. The QIs were translated into queries based on conjunctions.

Selection of indicators

The selection of QIs was mainly based on the availability of the required structured information in the EMR systems. Because the process is fully automated, only data elements stored in a structured or coded way within the EMR could be used. Information about the diagnosis (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD10), International Classification of Primary Care-second edition), drug prescriptions (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification) and lab results (Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes) were available in every software package because of their coded nature. Other information, such as clinical parameters and vaccinations were also available because of the way EMRs structured their data.

Transformation of indicators into queries

The queries consisted of logical conjunctions that automatically defined groups of patients based on the presence of certain characteristics and counted how many patients belonged to this group, in that practice in a certain time window. This was done using conjunctions with the keywords ‘and’, ‘or’ and ‘not’. Individualised searches were not feasible because the data could not be inspected at the individual level. Only patients who had their GMR in the practice were included.

Electronic medical records

Monthly meetings with software developers and industry representatives ensured alignment between project requirements and technical feasibility. These meetings assessed query viability and enabled developers to resolve issues, thereby promoting audit standardisation and consistent data collection across EMR systems.

Follow-up queries were developed to enhance the usefulness of the A&F tool for chronic disease management. These queries were not used to generate visual feedback but were made available within the GPs’ EMR systems to identify patients who did not meet the specific QIs. In practice, the follow-up queries were the inverse of the essential part of the conjunction. For example, patients with CKD who did not receive influenza vaccination.

Data quality and handling of missing or inconsistent data

Missing or inconsistent data primarily originated from incomplete coding or variations in EMR documentation practices. Three principles guided data handling to ensure data quality and comparability across practices. First, a strict structured-data requirement was applied: only information recorded as coded entries or stored in a clearly structured format within the EHR was eligible for extraction. Second, a uniform temporal window was defined for all the measures. Records outside this predefined time frame were automatically excluded to maintain consistency across practices. Third, during the implementation phase, automated screening procedures were introduced to detect impossible or contradictory values (eg, a higher number of patients with CKD with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) screening than the total number of patients with CKD). Any flagged anomalies were reviewed in collaboration with EMR vendors to ensure accuracy and resolve underlying data issues.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted in RStudio within the Healthdata environment, the central national health data platform in Belgium, where the Intego data are hosted. Healthstat, the reporting platform of Healthdata, is used to make reports available to GPs.18 Descriptive statistics were used to summarise CKD prevalence, comorbidities and CKD-related care and to support pilot testing within the Intego database.

Pilot testing

A pilot test was conducted to assess the feasibility of transforming QIs into conjunctions by extracting them from the Intego database. Queries were built in the same manner as implemented by EMR vendors to review the availability and registration of the necessary information. Descriptive statistics were used to examine the availability and stability of these conditions over time. Based on this evaluation, QIs were either included for audit and visual feedback, retained only for the audit (with the purpose of later inclusion in the feedback) or excluded.

Feedback development

Once the audit section was completed and the data were sent safely to Healthdata, feedback was developed. This process began with the creation of an outline based on the selected indicators and previous research.10 Next, initial drafts of the visual feedback were made and discussed with GPs for input. The final version was then developed into an automated, interactive format using SAS (V.8.3, SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA) on Healthstat servers.

In SAS Enterprise Guide, the dataset was transformed from a wide format (one row per practice) to a long format (one row per language, indicator and geographical benchmark), facilitating efficient feedback development and improving data security. SAS Visual Analytics (SAS VA) was used to develop interactive visual feedback.

Benchmarks and theory-informed design

In addition to the practice’s own performance, the achievable benchmark of care (ABC) was displayed in the visual feedback as a realistic and attainable performance target.19 The ABC method identified a ‘top performance’ benchmark for each indicator using the paired mean, that is, the average performance of the top 10% of practices, each representing at least 10% of the patient population.20 Feedback development was guided by the Clinical Performance Feedback Intervention Theory (CP-FIT), incorporating features such as biannual feedback, low cognitive load, benchmarking to peers and references to evidence-based guidelines to ensure a theory-informed design.21

Selection of the QIs

Selection

Several indicators could not be technically translated into extractable queries owing to limitations in the available data or query infrastructure. For others (7b, 7c, 9, 10, 11a–c, 12, 17 and 19), the operationalisation was adapted to enable automated extraction while preserving the original intent of each QI as closely as possible. Some parameters, such as smoking status, physical condition and body mass index, were only retrievable in some of the EMRs. During the selection process, 16 of the 36 indicators were excluded, and 10 were adapted for the audit and/or feedback due to technical limitations or unreliable data.

For example, for QI 8 (table 2), the percentage of patients with CKD who were vaccinated annually with an influenza vaccine, the following conversion was performed: Two queries were constructed: one for the denominator and one for the numerator. For the denominator, the general query defining the population of interest was used, where ‘all patients>18 y with GMR in the practice in the last 2 years and the diagnosis of CKD as a registered active care element in their problem list’ was identified.

Feedback

The feedback report consisted of 14 indicators, which were displayed to clinicians using an interactive dashboard. The dummy data were uploaded to the SAS VA server, where visual feedback was constructed using simple bar charts that included four key elements. The first element featured a bar representing the individual GP practice, colour-coded to indicate the practice’s performance relative to quartiles. The second element showed a bar representing the geographical benchmark, which could be customised by the individual receiving feedback. The third element displayed the average performance of the top 10% of practices based on the ABC. Finally, each indicator was given its own dedicated page within the report, with a link to the guidelines. Figure 3 provides an example of the feedback.

Once the actual data from the EMR vendors were collected and cleaned, they replaced the pilot phase dummy data. The final step was to publish the report on the secure Healthstat platform, allowing GPs to consult feedback specific to their practice. The audit will be repeated every 6 months and the visual feedback will be updated with the new data, while historical data can still be consulted.

Pilot

During pilot testing, the average CKD prevalence across all Intego practices was 32/1000 EMR patients, based on 440 322 patients from 134 practices.

In the Intego population 14 051 of the patients were diagnosed with CKD (ICD10 N18.9); 61.4% were older than 75 years; and 50.3% were female. Hypertension and diabetes seen in 7082 patients and diabetes in 3723 patients. On average, only 46% of patients meeting laboratory criteria for CKD had a coded diagnosis, suggesting the true number of patients with CKD in Intego exceeds 30 500.

The pilot test revealed that indicators 20 and 23 (examination without field code) and indicators 24–36 (lack of coded referral) could not be operationalised due to insufficient coded information. Additionally, while indicators 1c, 1d, 7a–7c, 10, 12, 17–19 and 22 could be transformed, they produced unreliable results, such as a lack of variability or a mean of zero across all practices. Consequently, these indicators were temporarily excluded from visual feedback.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study described the systematic development of queries and conjugations to build an automated A&F tool for CKD care in GPs’ EMR in Belgium. By doing so, this research demonstrated the design and implementation of a fully automated audit mechanism that could generate interactive feedback at practice level. The pilot phase confirmed the feasibility and technical viability of the automated audit, the accurate extraction of relevant indicators and the ability to produce interpretable visual feedback for practices. These findings provided the key components necessary for a scalable A&F intervention aimed at supporting a shift from reactive to proactive CKD management in primary care.

The A&F tool described in this paper differed from previous interventions through its fully automated process, interactive feedback and the incorporation of follow-up queries that translated audit results into actionable strategies. These design components preserved established determinants of effective feedback interventions, such as guideline linkage and repeated cycles, while introducing innovations intended to reduce clinician burden and improve usability.10 22 23 Automated audits could be scheduled regularly to ensure timely updates, and visual feedback could support the identification of areas for improvement. Furthermore, follow-up queries were designed to support GPs in executing actionable strategies for better patient care. However, whether these design advantages ultimately translate into behaviour change or improved patient outcomes remains hypothetical and requires empirical evaluation.

The nationwide implementation of an automated A&F tool in Belgium creates new opportunities to improve clinical practice and advance research on A&F. Previous studies have shown only modest effects of different feedback strategies, making it challenging to demonstrate their effectiveness.22 Combining a nationwide A&F tool with Intego, an established A&F implementation laboratory, provided a strong foundation for future research and further development of A&F interventions.12 13 Future studies should rigorously assess feedback uptake, contextual moderators and mechanisms of action.

During the pilot phase, CKD registration within EMRs was consistent with prior studies and was found to be low, with an average of 46% of the total number of patients meeting the diagnostic criteria.9 10 This under-registration impacted the interpretability of several QIs and introduced variability in others. Although A&F interventions have the potential to improve data completeness, further research is needed to confirm this.19 Anticipating improved CKD-related registration following the launch of this A&F initiative, some relevant queries were proactively included in the audit, such as the queries concerning the subclassification of CKD (nr. 6, 8, 10 and 12). This future-oriented design aligns with EMR vendors’ preference for simultaneous integration of all relevant queries, enabling efficient implementation and flexible feedback updates.

The feedback design incorporated several principles from the CP-FIT and supporting literature, including biannual feedback, low cognitive load, benchmarking to peers and references to evidence-based guidelines.10 22 24 By engaging in an A&F intervention grounded in multiple CP-FIT principles, GP practices can progress from goal setting to behaviour change and improved patient outcomes. Input from GPs gathered through a previously developed questionnaire will guide iterative refinement of the feedback interface and content.25 Future research will then focus on evaluating the effectiveness of the automated A&F tool by assessing intermediate outcomes such as improved CKD coding, increased adherence to recommended follow-up testing and changes in clinician behaviour, potentially through a stepped-wedge trial. However, the extent to which practices engage with feedback and translate it into clinical action remains unknown and represents an important area for future research. Clinician engagement will therefore be examined using platform log data and follow-up questionnaires to better understand uptake, user experiences and factors influencing integration into routine clinical practice.

Strengths and limitations

One of the primary advantages of this study is that the QIs are grounded in globally acknowledged frameworks, which means they could potentially be applied internationally once validated. The queries and conjugations developed can be modified for use in other countries to create or enhance similar automated A&F interventions. Nonetheless, validation is crucial to confirm their relevance in different settings, necessitating a comparison of healthcare standards, patient outcomes and regulatory frameworks. Due to the differences in EMR systems across countries, it is also important to locally reassess EMR functionalities. A significant drawback of automated A&F interventions is their reliance on structured data from EMRs. Some QIs, especially those related to medication dosages and specific time frames, presented technical difficulties in data extraction. However, working with EMR vendors led to practical solutions, such as simplifying queries and implementing a two-step process for most of the indicators. In addition, variability in coding practices and EMR documentation across practices may have contributed to missing or inconsistent data. Although strict data handling rules, uniform temporal windows and automated plausibility checks were applied to enhance comparability, these procedures may have resulted in the exclusion of some valid clinical information and could not fully eliminate residual heterogeneity between practices.

Currently, referral data cannot be consistently retrieved due to unstructured registration practices. However, the ongoing implementation of the Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine Clinical Terms (SNOMED CT) coding system in Belgian general practice may help to resolve this issue in the future.26 Currently, feedback is accessible only via the Healthstat platform, requiring account creation and repeated logins. The future integration of feedback directly into the EMR using a Single Sign-On feature is expected to significantly improve accessibility.27

Owing to privacy constraints, individual patient data could not be captured, necessitating a shift to group-level analysis. For instance, rather than tracking multiple eGFR measurements for individual patients, the system identified patient groups with at least one measurement and assessed whether a subset had a second measurement. While this approach reduced data granularity, it safeguarded patient privacy by avoiding the use of identifiable data.

Conclusion

This study provided the building blocks for programming an automated A&F intervention in an EMR system for CKD. This A&F intervention could enhance CKD primary care quality by improving the registration and increasing adherence to QIs. Both the audit process as well as the creation of the feedback were described. After receiving the feedback GPs should use follow-up queries to help them shift towards more proactive care.
