Differentially private scale testing via rank transformations and percentile modifications
Joshua Levine, Kelly Ramsay

Abstract
We develop a class of differentially private two-sample scale tests, called the rank-transformed percentile-modified Siegel–Tukey tests, or RPST tests. These RPST tests are inspired both by recent differentially private extensions of some common rank tests and some older modifications to non-private rank tests. We present the asymptotic distribution of the RPST test statistic under the null hypothesis, under a very general condition on the rank transformation. We also prove RPST tests are differentially private, and that their type I error does not exceed the given level. We uncover that the growth rate of the rank transformation presents a tradeoff between power and sensitivity. We do extensive simulations to investigate the effects of the tuning parameters and compare to a general private testing framework. Lastly, we show that our techniques can also be used to improve the differentially private signed-rank test. Key words and phrases: Differential privacy, robust statistics, rank testing, nonparametric statistics, scale testing.

One common testing problem, scale testing, has not yet been carefully studied.
To fill this gap, we develop a class of differentially private two-sample scale tests, called the
rank-transformed percentile-modified Siegel–Tukey tests
, or RPST tests.

These RPST tests are inspired both by recent differentially private extensions of some common rank tests

In particular,

Furthermore, our test incorporates rank transformations and percentile modifications, which allows us to increase the power of a “naive” private Siegel–Tukey test.
Percentile modifications were first introduced by

The rank transformation is inspired by the Van der Waerden test

Critically, we show that the transformation can actually reduce the sensitivity of the test statistic, and therefore reduce the additive noise required to ensure differential privacy.
At a high level, the proposed test works as follows: (i) Combine both samples into one, and rank all points in a center-outward manner. (ii) Modify these ranks by setting central ranks to 0. (iii) Apply a non-negative, increasing transformation
\psi
to the ranks. (iv) Sum the ranks and privatize the sum, privately estimate the asymptotic variance of the rank sum, and compare the private rank sum statistic to the appropriate null distribution.

Preliminaries

2.1
Problem of study

We first introduce the problem we study in this work.
Let
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
be the set of Borel probability distributions on
{\mathbb{R}}
. Going forward, we will identify members of
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
with their cumulative distribution functions (CDFs).
We consider two independent random samples from distributions
F\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
G\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
. The first one, which we will call “group 1”, or
\mathbf{X}_{n_{1}}
, contains the observations
X_{1},\dots,X_{n_{1}}\sim F
. The second one, which we will call “group 2”, or
\mathbf{Y}_{n_{2}}
, contains the observations
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n_{2}}\sim G
.
We will further assume that both
F
and
G
are members of some
location-scale family
of distributions, and both have the same location parameter.
That is, there is some “base” distribution
F_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
and location
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, which are fixed and unknown, and from which we can form the family of distributions given by
\mathcal{L}=\{F_{0}(\sigma(\cdot-\mu)\colon\sigma\geq 0\}\subset\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
. We then assume that
F
and
G
lie in
\mathcal{L}
. For example,
\mathcal{L}
may be the family of Normal distributions with mean
\mu
or the family of Cauchy distributions with median
\mu
.
Our goal is to develop a differentially private test for a difference in scale,
\sigma
, between
F
and
G
.
That is, we wish to test the hypothesis

H_{0}:F=G\quad\text{vs.}\quad H_{1}:\text{there exists }\theta>0,\ \theta\neq 1,\text{ such that }F(x)=G(\theta x)\ \forall x\in{\mathbb{R}},

(2.1)

in a differentially private manner.
Note that (
2.1
) implicitly assumes that
F
and
G
have the same location, e.g., mean or median.
In practice, one can normalize the samples by a private estimate of the mean or median, or if one assumes normality, this can be achieved by differencing within each sample.
For simplicity, we will also assume that
F
and
G
are absolutely continuous distributions.
Relaxing the assumption of absolute continuity is discussed later in Remark
3.4
.
These conditions can be summarized as follows:

Condition 1
.

We have that
X_{1},\dots,X_{n_{1}}\sim F
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n_{2}}\sim G
are two independent random samples for some
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
. Further, we have that
F,G\in\mathcal{L}
and
F,G
are absolutely continuous.

Sometimes it will be convenient to consider the combined sample. In this way, let
n=n_{1}+n_{2}
, and we let
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{n}
be the combined sample, where we label the points in non-decreasing order
Z_{1}\leq\ldots\leq Z_{n}
.

2.2
Siegel–Tukey test

Now that we understand the testing problem, we review the Siegel–Tukey test, a key ingredient in the proposed class of private scale tests.
To test the hypothesis (
2.1
) in the nonprivate setting,
Siegel and Tukey, 1960
devised the following procedure, which is known as the Siegel–Tukey test. We first arrange the data points in the combined sample from least to greatest. We then assign ranks to the ordered data points in the following manner:

1.

Assign rank 1 to the lowest data point.

2.

Assign rank 2 to the greatest data point, and assign rank 3 to the second-greatest data point.

3.

Assign rank 4 to the second-lowest data point, and assign rank 5 to the third-lowest data point.

4.

Continue alternating between the two highest unranked points and the two lowest unranked points in this manner until every data point has been assigned a rank.

The (non-private) test statistic is then computed as follows: the minimum of the sum of the ranks in group 1, say
\widehat{U}_{1}
and the sum of the ranks in group 2, say
\widehat{U}_{2}
is first computed.
The final test statistic is given by
\widehat{U}=\min(\widehat{U}_{1}-n_{1}(n_{1}+1)/2,\widehat{U}_{2}-n_{2}(n_{2}+1)/2)
, which has the same distribution under the null hypothesis as the Wilcoxon rank-sum statistic.
Later, we introduce a new test based on these principles, which avoids using the minimum of two statistics.

2.3
Percentile modification

It is helpful to review the percentile modification introduced by
Gastwirth, 1965
. This is a technique used to improve the asymptotic relative efficiency of nonparametric rank-based hypothesis tests. This technique entails ignoring either the most extreme or the most central ranks of a dataset when calculating a test statistic.
For instance, consider the Siegel–Tukey test. Given that both samples have the same mean, both samples will have some central observations. Therefore, both samples will have some larger (center-outward) ranks, and these will not be helpful in determining if there is a difference in dispersion between the two groups.
Therefore, given
0<q<1
, we can instead set the
{\lfloor nq\rfloor}
largest ranks to be equal to
n-{\lfloor nq\rfloor}
. This reduces unhelpful noise in the set of ranks, which improves the power of the test.
Indeed, Gastwirth applied this technique to the Ansari-Bradley-Freund Test
[
1
]
, another scale tests based on ranks which is very similar to the Siegel–Tukey Test. The result was an increase in the asymptotic relative efficiency and power for certain settings
q
.
Subsequent works have utilized the technique of percentile modification to improve the power of other hypothesis tests
[
6
,
19
]
. As mentioned previously, we utilize this technique in our proposed test.

2.4
Differential privacy

Next, we introduce the neccessary background on differential privacy
[
12
,
13
]
.
Define a dataset of size
n\in\mathbb{N}
to be a set of
n
real numbers, and let
\mathcal{D}_{n}
be the set of datasets of size
n
.
First, we say that a dataset
\mathbf{x}_{n}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
, is adjacent to another dataset
\mathbf{y}_{n}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
if
\mathbf{x}_{n}
and
\mathbf{y}_{n}
differ by exactly one point.
Let
\mathcal{A}_{n}
be the set of pairs of adjacent datasets of size
n
.
Next, let
H_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}
be a probability distribution over
{\mathbb{R}}
, which depends on
\mathbf{x}_{n}
.
That is,
H_{.}\colon\mathcal{D}_{n}\to\mathcal{M}_{1}({\mathbb{R}})
.
Assuming that
H_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}
is absolutely continuous, let
h_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}
be the associated density.
We can now define differential privacy.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that the quantity
\theta\sim H_{\mathbf{x}_{n}}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differentially private if for all
(\mathbf{y}_{n},\mathbf{z}_{n})\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, it holds that

h_{\mathbf{y}_{n}}(x)\leq e^{\epsilon}h_{\mathbf{z}_{n}}(x)+\delta.

Here,
\theta
is a differentially private quantity and the pair
(\epsilon,\delta)
represent the privacy budget.
The parameter
\epsilon>0
controls the level of privacy assured to each individual, and
\delta>0
is the probability of leakage, or, the probability that the privacy condition
h_{\mathbf{y}_{n}}(x)\leq e^{\epsilon}h_{\mathbf{z}_{n}}(x)
fails to hold.
Typically,
\epsilon
is a small constant
\approx 1
and
\delta=O(n^{-k})
for some positive integer
k
. Smaller values of each of
\epsilon
and
\delta
enforce stricter privacy guarantees.
Note that if
\delta=0
, the mechanism is said to be
\epsilon
-differentially private.
The following is a useful property for developing differentially private estimates.

Proposition 2.1

(
Basic Composition

[
10
]
)
.

Suppose that, for
i=1,2,\dots,k
, the quantity
\theta_{i}
is
(\epsilon_{i},\delta_{i})
-DP. Let
\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{k})
be a sequence of these algorithms. Then,
\theta
is
\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\epsilon_{i},\sum_{i=1}^{k}\delta_{i}\right)
-differentially private.

This allows us to quantify the privacy loss incurred when composing multiple separate algorithms.
Next, we introduce the simplest way to generate a differentially private statistic: the Laplace mechanism.
For a univariate statistic
T\colon\mathcal{D}_{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, define the global sensitivity of
T
as

\mathrm{GS}(T)=\sup_{(\mathbf{y}_{n},\mathbf{z}_{n})\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}|T(\mathbf{y}_{n})-T(\mathbf{z}_{n})|.

The Laplace mechanism is given as follows:

Proposition 2.2

(
Laplace Mechanism

[
11
]
)
.

Let
T\colon\mathcal{D}_{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be any statistic with
\mathrm{GS}(T)<\infty
.
If
Z
is a standard Laplace random variable, then

T(\mathbf{x}_{n})+\mathrm{GS}(T)Z/\epsilon

is
\epsilon
-differentially private.

Proposition
2.2
illustrates how correctly calibrated Laplace noise can be added to a statistic to ensure differential privacy.

2.5
Unknown group sizes in private rank testing

One issue in private, two-sample rank testing is that the group sizes must be known to execute the test.
That is, the asymptotic distribution of the test statistic under the null hypothesis depends on the group sizes.
However, the group sizes can potentially be sensitive information.
Therefore, we cannot use them directly to obtain the asymptotic reference distribution or to compute the test statistic.
In addition, it is not ideal to plug in a simple private estimate of the group sizes, as this can inflate the probability of type I error.

Couch et al., 2019
considered this problem when creating their private Mann-Whitney test, where it is essential to know the minimum group size.
They estimate the size of smallest group,
m
, such that with probability
1-\delta
, the estimate of
m
, say
m^{*}
, is guaranteed to be less than
m
.
This ensures that the type I error is bounded by
\alpha
, with probability
1-\delta
.
However, this comes at the cost of pure differential privacy.
Given
\epsilon_{m}>0
, the estimate is defined as follows

m^{*}=\max\left\{\left\lceil\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}+\frac{1}{\epsilon_{m}}Z+\frac{\ln(2\delta)}{\epsilon_{m}}\right\rceil,0\right\},

where
Z
is a standard Laplace random variable.

Couch et al., 2019
show that this algorithm is
(\epsilon_{m},\delta)
-differentially private and that
m^{*}<m
with probability
1-\delta
. We generalize this approach to the private version of the Siegel–Tukey test.

3
Private Siegel–Tukey tests

3.1
Definition

With the necessary background in hand, we can now introduce the RPST tests.
Briefly, the tests proceed as follows: (i) We first rank the data similarly to that of the original Siegel–Tukey test. (ii) We then apply a percentile modification and a transformation
\psi
to the ranks. (iii) We then compute a private rank-sum statistic based on these modified, transformed ranks. (iv) We conservatively and privately estimate the asymptotic variance of the private rank-sum statistic, which we use together with a central limit theorem to determine the
p
-value of the test.
The last step relies on a private estimate of
|n/2-n_{1}|
, after which we adapt the techniques of
Couch et al., 2019
previously mentioned.

In order to adapt the percentile modification to be used with the Siegel–Tukey test, we must modify the traditional Siegel–Tukey test statistic.
In the original Siegel–Tukey test, under the null hypothesis, we have that
\widehat{U}_{1}-n_{1}(n_{1}+1)/2
and
\widehat{U}_{2}-n_{2}(n_{2}+1)/2
have the same distribution.
If we were to naively replace the ranks in the calculation of the Siegel–Tukey test statistic with the rank-transformed, percentile-modified ranks, then this property would no longer hold in general.
That is, the quantity associated with group 1 would no longer be equal in distribution to the quantity in group 2 under the null hypothesis.

As a result, we propose the following, alternative ranking procedure.
As in the traditional percentile-modified tests, let
q
be the proportion of central data points we wish to set to 0, and let
Q={\lfloor nq\rfloor}
be then the number of points to set to 0.
We propose a new way to assign the ranks, which is from the extremes inward, starting at
n-Q
. That is, the lowest data point is assigned rank
n-Q
, the highest and second-highest point are assigned ranks
n-Q-1
and
n-Q-2
respectively, and so on until the positive ranks have been exhausted. The remaining unranked points, which are the
Q
most central points, are assigned rank 0.
Denote these ranks by
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}
, and without loss of generality, take the first
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n_{1}}
to be the ranks pertaining to group 1.
After computing these ranks, we apply an increasing, non-negative function
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\rightarrow\left[0,\infty\right)
to the ranks, where we require that
\psi(0)=0
.
Here,
\psi
is a
rank transformation
. We have found that rank transformations alter the asymptotic distribution and global sensitivity of the test statistic. Therefore, in some cases, it can increase the power of the test. An example of our proposed ranking procedure is given in Figure

Let
\delta_{i}=1
if

pertains to an observation in group 1, and 0 otherwise. We can now define the non-private, percentile modified, rank transformed Siegel–Tukey test statistic.

Definition 3.1
.

Given
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\rightarrow\left[0,\infty\right)
such that
\psi(0)=0
and
Q\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
, the non-private, percentile modified, rank transformed Siegel–Tukey test statistic is given by

U_{1}\coloneqq U_{1}(Q,\psi)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-Q}\delta_{i}\psi(i)-\frac{n_{1}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n-Q}\psi(i)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\psi(r_{i})-\mu_{1}.

The test statistic is simply the sum of the ranks in group 1, minus the expected value of the sum under the null hypothesis.

Remark 3.1
.

We lose no generality when we add the restriction
\psi(0)=0
.
To see this, first for any real function
h
, define
U_{1}^{\prime}(h)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}h(r_{i})-E\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}h(r_{i})\right).
Next, note that
U_{1}^{\prime}(Q,h)=U_{1}^{\prime}(Q,h+b)
for some
b\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Here, for a function
h
and
b\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
b+h
denotes the function
x\mapsto h(x)+b
.

One should note that critically, if we were to define
U_{1}
in terms of group 2, say
U_{2}
, we have that
U_{1}=-U_{2}
, so the test is unaffected by which group is labelled group 1.
To privatize the above test, we employ the Laplace mechanism after bounding the sensitivity of
U_{1}
.
Let
\bar{\psi}_{Q}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-Q}\psi(i)
.
Then, the private test statistic can be obtained as follows:

Definition 3.2
.

Given
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\rightarrow\left[0,\infty\right)
such that
\psi(0)=0
,
\epsilon_{U}>0
and
Q\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
, the private percentile modified, rank transformed Siegel–Tukey test statistic is given by

\displaystyle|\widetilde{U}_{1}|\coloneqq|\widetilde{U}_{1}(Q,\psi)|=|U_{1}+Z\cdot\mathrm{GS}^{*}(U_{1})/\epsilon_{U}|,

where
Z
is a standard Laplace random variable and

\mathrm{GS}(U_{1})\leq\mathrm{GS}^{*}(U_{1})=\max\left\{\psi(n-Q),\psi(n-Q)+\psi(n-Q-1)-\bar{\psi}_{Q}\right\}.

The proof of
\mathrm{GS}(U_{1})\leq\mathrm{GS}^{*}(U_{1})

\widetilde{U}_{1}

Let

\sigma^{2}(n_{1},n_{2},\psi,Q)=\frac{n_{1}}{n}\left(1-\frac{n_{1}}{n}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{n-Q}\psi^{2}(i)+2\frac{n_{1}}{n}\left(\frac{n_{1}-1}{n-1}-\frac{n_{1}}{n}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{n-Q-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n-Q}\psi(i)\psi(j).

We show later, see Theorem

, that under the null hypothesis, for many choices of

\displaystyle\widetilde{U}_{1}\mathrel{\leadsto}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}(n_{1},n_{2},\psi,Q)\right)\text{.}

Here,

denotes convergence in distribution and
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
denotes the normal distribution with mean
\mu
and standard deviation

. Therefore, if we knew the group sizes
n_{1},n_{2}
, we could compute a
p

\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}(n_{1},n_{2},\psi,Q)\right)
.

However, the group sizes are often subject to the constraint of differential privacy. Thus, we need a way to privately estimate the
p
-value, such that the probability of type I error does not increase.
For this test, we reject the null hypothesis when the test statistic is sufficiently large.
In that case, we must configure the algorithm to
overestimate
the standard deviation of the asymptotic normal distribution of
\widetilde{U}_{1}
. This is equivalent to underestimating the disparity between the two group sizes, given by
d_{1}=\left|n_{1}-n/2\right|=\left|n_{1}/2-n_{2}/2\right|
, see Lemma
A.3
.
Therefore, inspired by
Couch et al., 2019
, given
\delta,\epsilon_{d}>0
, we can use the following estimate of

.
Let
Z^{\prime}
be a standard Laplace random variable.

1.

Compute
\widetilde{d}_{1}\coloneqq d_{1}+Z^{\prime}/\epsilon_{d}
and, subsequently,
d_{1}^{*}\coloneqq\max\{{\lceil\widetilde{d}_{1}+\frac{\ln(2\delta)}{\epsilon_{d}}\rceil},0\}
.

2.

If
n
is odd and
d_{1}^{*}\neq 0
, subtract
\frac{1}{2}
from
d_{1}^{*}
. If
n
is odd and
d_{1}^{*}=0
, instead take
d_{1}^{*}=\frac{1}{2}
.

Letting
\widetilde{n}_{1}=n/2-d_{1}^{*}
and
\widetilde{n}_{2}=n-\widetilde{n}_{1}
, we can then use the normal distribution with variance
\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}\coloneqq\sigma^{2}(\widetilde{n}_{1},\widetilde{n}_{2},\psi,Q)
as the reference distribution to compute the

-value for our test.
Using a similar argument to the one used to prove Lemma A.7 of
[

]

, implying that we are not introducing excess type I error into the procedure by estimating the group sizes. See Lemma
D.1
for a formal statement.

3.2
Theory

We now present some theoretical results concerning the RPST tests.
The first result shows that the RPST tests are differentially private.

Theorem 3.2
.

For all
n_{1},n_{2}\geq 1
,
Q\in\{0\}\cup[n-1]
, strictly-increasing, non-negative functions
\psi:{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
, the pair
(\widetilde{U}_{1},\widetilde{\sigma}^{2})
are jointly
(\epsilon_{U}+\epsilon_{d},\delta)
-differentially private.

The proof Theorem
3.2
is given in Appendix
A
. Theorem
3.2
guarantees that all versions of the RPST test are differentially private.
Our next result concerns the asymptotic distribution of the test statistic under the null hypothesis.
For this result, we need a few conditions.

Condition 2
.

There exists
\lambda\in(0,1)
such that
\frac{n_{1}}{n}\rightarrow\lambda
as
n\to\infty
.

Condition
2
says that the groups sizes are bounded away from zero, or, one group size is not asymptotically dominating another. This is a relatively standard assumption in rank testing. We now introduce another condition.

Condition 3
.

Let
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a continuous, strictly increasing, positive, real-valued function on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, such that
\psi(n)=O(n^{k})
for any
k>0
and
\psi(0)=0
.

Condition
3
gives a sufficient condition on
\psi
for asymptotic normality of
U_{1}
under the null hypothesis.
Condition
3
is satisfied for increasing functions which pass through the origin, and do not grow faster than some polynomial.
For instance, Condition
3
is satisfied for
\psi(x)
being any of
x,\log(x+1)
or
x^{k}
.
Condition
3
can also be weakened, see Condition
4
in the appendix. However, the weaker condition is very opaque, whereas Condition
3
is very straightforward.
We now present our main theorem, which gives the distribution of the test statistic under the null hypothesis.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
Q\in\{0\}\cup[n-1]
be the number of central data points to truncate. Suppose that Conditions
1
–
3
hold, as well as
H_{0}
holds, as given in (
2.1
).
For all
\epsilon,\delta>0
, it holds that

\frac{\widetilde{U}_{1}}{\sigma(\widetilde{n}_{1},\widetilde{n}_{2},\psi,Q)}\mathrel{\leadsto}\mathcal{N}(0,1)\text{.}

The proof of this Theorem
3.3
is given in Appendix
A
.
Theorem
3.3
gives the limiting distribution of the test statistic, which yields that it suffices to use the distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}(\widetilde{n}_{1},\widetilde{n}_{2},\psi,Q))
as the reference distribution for computing the
p
-values.

Remark 3.4
.

[Relaxing the absolute continuity assumption]
If
F
and
G
are not absolutely continuous, then the data may contain repeated observations. In that case, a small amount of noise can be added to the data in order to obtain data without repeated observations. In that case, it is obvious that the global sensitivity of the test statistic remains the same. In addition, the asymptotic distribution under the null hypothesis will also remain unchanged, and so the test can be applied without changes. The only hiccup is to ensure that the magnitude of noise is small enough so that the order of the non-repeated observations stays the same, which can be chosen based on domain knowledge.

5
Extensions to the Wilcoxon signed rank test

The rank transformation and percentile modification can be applied to other rank-based testing procedures. As an example, we consider its application to the Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test. The Wilcoxon signed rank test applies to the setting where we observe
n
pairs of data, say
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
where
X_{1}\sim F
and
Y_{1}\sim G
, and we want to test now whether

H_{0}\colon E(X_{1})=E(Y_{1})\ vs.\ H_{1}\colon E(X_{1})\neq E(Y_{1}).

Note that
X_{i}
is not necessarily independent of
Y_{i}
, but for
i\neq j
,
(X_{i},Y_{i})
is independent of
(X_{j},Y_{j})
, and
(X_{i},Y_{i})\overset{d}{=}(X_{j},Y_{j})
.
In this case, the non-private test statistic is computed as follows. For each pair of observations, we compute the absolute difference
b_{i}=|Y_{i}-X_{i}|
and the sign
s_{i}=sign(Y_{i}-X_{i})
. Each observation gets a rank
r_{i}
, which is simply its linear rank amongst the
b_{i}
. We can then apply percentile modification and rank transformations to these ranks. The test statistic is then

W_{1}\coloneqq W_{1}(\psi,Q)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\cdot\psi((r_{i}-Q)\vee 0).

Following similar logic as
Couch et al., 2018
, we have that

Theorem 5.1
.

The global sensitivity of
W_{1}
is bounded by
2\psi(n-Q)
.

Therefore, we can define
\widetilde{W}_{1}=W_{1}+2Z\psi(n-Q)/\epsilon_{U}
as the test statistic.
We can call this the private rank transformed percentile modified signed rank test, or private RPSR test.
We also have the following theorem

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\psi:{\mathbb{N}}\,\cup\,\{0\}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be a strictly-increasing, non-negative function that is
O(n^{z})
for some
z>0
. Under
H_{0}
,
\widetilde{W}_{1}\mathrel{\leadsto}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sum_{i=1}^{n-Q}\psi^{2}(i)\right)
.

The proofs of both of these theorems can be seen in Appendix
B
.
We note that
Couch et al., 2018
introduced a private version of
W_{1}(h,0)
, where
h(x)=x
. We now compare the rank transformed, percentile modified version to theirs via a simulation study.
We consider the same
\psi,q,\epsilon,n
combinations as in the previous section. We consider normally and student
t_{3}
distributed data, and consider the effect size to be the difference in means between the paired data means.
As in the previous section, each combination of parameters was run 500 times.

In terms of the results, the empirical size was acceptable and comparable between all combinations of
\psi
and
q
, so we defer those results to the appendix. In fact, in the interest of space, we defer many of the results to the appendix. To illustrate our results, we simply present Table

n=100

For example, using
\psi(r)=\tan^{-1}(r)
with

\epsilon\leq 1

with
q=0.25
. Otherwise, the test of

with

6
Conclusion

We have investigated a new class of nonparametric, robust rank tests, called percentile modified rank transformed tests. We have introduced and studied differentially private versions of these tests. We have shown that these tests often perform better relative to existing tests. Some open questions remain, such as controlling the type I error under pure differential privacy, deriving asymptotic results under alternative hypotheses and private scale testing for multivariate data. Another interesting direction of future work is deriving the optimal transformation given a class of distributions.

Proofs

A.1
A useful structural theorem

We begin by stating a useful structural theorem.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
(these values need not be distinct). Let
S_{k}
be a random variable equal to the sum of
k
values drawn from
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
in a simple random sample without replacement. Then, we have that:

1.

{\mathbb{E}}(S_{k})=\frac{k}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
.

2.

\var(S_{k})=\frac{k}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}+2\frac{k(k-1)}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}-\left(\frac{k}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right)^{2}
.

Proof.

We begin with assertion
1
. Let
A=(A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{k})
be a random sample from
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
. For each
i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, let
\delta_{i}=\mathbf{1}\{x_{i}\in A\}
. Then,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(S_{k})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\delta_{i}\right)

(As
A_{i}\in\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
for all
i\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}(x_{i}\delta_{i})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(!)}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\,\frac{k}{n}

(Due to the properties of simple random samples without replacement)

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}.

This completes the proof of assertion
1
.

To prove assertion
2
, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(S_{k}^{2})=\frac{k}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}+2\frac{k(k-1)}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}\text{.}

We may write:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(S_{k}^{2})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}\right)^{2}\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}^{2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{k}A_{i}A_{j}\right).

To make further progress, we introduce the indicators
\delta_{ij}
, where

\displaystyle\delta_{ij}=\begin{cases}1\quad&\text{if $\{x_{i},x_{j}\}\subseteq A$}\\
0\quad&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\cdot

We then have that:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(S_{k}^{2})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}^{2}\right)+2\,{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{k}A_{i}A_{j}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}\delta_{i}\right)+2\,{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}\delta_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}\,{\mathbb{E}}(\delta_{i})+2\,\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}{\mathbb{E}}(\delta_{ij})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}\,\frac{\binom{n-1}{k-1}}{\binom{n}{k}}+2\,\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}\frac{\binom{n-2}{k-2}}{\binom{n}{k}}

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{r l}&\text{(As the expectation of an indicator of an event}\\
&\text{is the probability of that event occurring)}\\
\end{array}

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}+2\frac{k(k-1)}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}\text{.}

Assertion
2
then follows from the fact that
\var({X})={\mathbb{E}}(X^{2})-{\mathbb{E}}(X)^{2}
for any random variable
X
.
∎

A.2
Results justifying the private group sizes

The following two results ensure the validity (but not the privacy) of the algorithms we use to estimate the group sizes in the RPST tests.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
S_{k}
be defined as in Theorem
A.1
. We have that
\var(S_{k})=\var(S_{n-k})
.

Proof.

Let
A=(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
be a particular simple random sample without replacement from
(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
. Consider the set of elements
B=(b_{1},\dots,b_{n-k})
which are not present in
A
.
A
and
B
are related in the following two ways:

•

Since
\binom{n}{k}=\binom{n}{n-k}
, the number of possible samples of size
k
is equal to the number of possible samples of size
n-k
. Therefore, the probability of obtaining sample
A
from
k
draws without replacement is equal to the probability of obtaining sample
B
from
n-k
draws without replacement.

•

Since
A\cup B=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}\}
, we must have that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}b_{i}
.

Taken together, and noting that
A
is an arbitrary sample of size
k
, we have that
S_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}-S_{n-k}
. The desired result then follows from the fact that neither the addition of a constant nor negation affects the variance of a random variable.
∎

Next, we show that
\var(S_{k})
is a decreasing function of
|k-\frac{n}{2}|
, which justifies our method of computing the
p
-value.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
and
S_{k}
be defined as in Theorem
A.1
. Suppose that
x_{h}\geq 0
for all
h\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and that there exists
i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
such that
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
. Then,
V(k)=\var(S_{k})
is decreasing in
|k-\frac{n}{2}|
.

Proof.

Applying Theorem
A.1
, we have that:

\displaystyle V(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}^{2}+2\frac{k(k-1)}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}-\left(\frac{k}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\propto k\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}^{2}+2\frac{k^{2}-k}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}-\frac{\left(k\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right)^{2}}{n}.

We will extend the domain of
V
to
\left[0,n\right]
and take the first two derivatives with respect to
k
. Starting with the first derivative:

\displaystyle V^{\prime}(k)

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}^{2}+2\frac{2k-1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}-\frac{2k}{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}^{2}+2\frac{2k-1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}-\frac{2k}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}-\frac{4k}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}.

It can easily be verified that
V^{\prime}(\frac{n}{2})=0
, indicating the possible existence of a maximum or minimum there. We investigate further by taking the second derivative of
V
with respect of
k
, with the aim of showing that it is negative:

\displaystyle V^{\prime\prime}(k)

\displaystyle\propto\frac{4}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}-\frac{2}{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}-\frac{4}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{n^{2}-n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\propto\frac{2}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}-\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right)^{2}-\frac{n}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\propto\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right)^{2}-n\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}.

To show that the above expression is negative under our hypotheses, we employ the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.
We obtain:

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}}\,\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}1^{2}}\iff\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}}\,\sqrt{n}\iff\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq n\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}.

Therefore, the aforementioned expression is non-positive, and is equal to 0 if and only if
x_{1}=x_{2}=\dots=x_{n}
. This cannot be the case under our assumptions, and so
V^{\prime\prime}
is negative. Since
V^{\prime\prime}
is also a constant, we may draw the following conclusions:

1.

V
attains a maximum at
k=\frac{n}{2}
.

2.

V
is decreasing in
\left|k-\frac{n}{2}\right|
.

This is what we wanted to show.
∎

Lemma A.5

Region

\displaystyle b_{j}=\sum_{i:Z_{i}\in\mathbf{X}_{n_{1}},w_{i}\in A_{j}}(r_{i}-r^{\prime}_{i}),

Case 1: The Changed Row Retains its Group

By a similar argument, if it were the case that

Case 1b:

and

The removal of the row with raw rank

from database
\mathbf{x}
, which (by itself) decreases

by

U_{1}

Since

\displaystyle 0\leq||b_{2}+r(p,n)|\leq r(h,n)-r(1,n)=\psi(n)-\psi(1),

\displaystyle 0\leq||b_{3}+r(q,n)|\leq r(h,n)-r(n,n)=\psi(n)-\psi(2)\text{,}

Case 2a:

\displaystyle\mathrm{GS}(U_{1})\leq\left|-r(p,n)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi(i)+|b_{3}|-|b_{2}|\right|.

A.4
The limiting distribution under
H_{0}

We first consider what happens to the “Laplace” portion of the test statistic. That is, the term
Z\cdot\mathrm{GS}^{*}(U_{1})/\sigma(n_{1},n_{2},\psi,Q),
where

\mathrm{GS}^{*}(U_{1})=\max\left\{\psi(n-Q),\psi(n-Q)+\psi(n-Q-1)-\bar{\psi}_{Q}\right\}.

Lemma A.6
.

Suppose that Conditions
1
–
2
hold as well as
H_{0}
(as given in (
2.1
)) holds. Then, if
\psi
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, then

\frac{\mathrm{GS}^{*}(U_{1})}{\sigma(n_{1},n_{2},\psi,Q)}=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\lambda(1-\lambda)}}\right).

Proof.

We have that

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(n_{1},n_{2},\psi,Q)

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{1}}{n}\left(1-\frac{n_{1}}{n}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{n-Q}\psi^{2}(i)+2\frac{n_{1}}{n}\left(\frac{n_{1}-1}{n-1}-\frac{n_{1}}{n}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{n-Q-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n-Q}\psi(i)\psi(j).

Now,

\displaystyle\frac{n_{1}}{n}-\frac{n_{1}-1}{n-1}=\frac{n_{1}(n-1)-n(n_{1}-1)}{n(n-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{n-n_{1}}{n(n-1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(!)}}{{=}}\frac{n-\lambda n+O(n^{1/2})}{n(n-1)}=\frac{1-\lambda}{n-1}+O(n^{-3/2}),

Using this,

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(n_{1},n_{2},\psi,Q)

\displaystyle\approx\lambda(1-\lambda)\left(\left(1+\frac{1}{n-1}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{n-Q}\psi^{2}(i)-\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-Q}\sum_{j=1}^{n-Q}\psi(i)\psi(j)\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda(1-\lambda)\left(\left(1+\frac{1}{n-1}\right)n\bar{\psi}_{Q}^{2}-\frac{n^{2}}{n-1}(\bar{\psi}_{Q})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\approx n\lambda(1-\lambda)\left(\bar{\psi}_{Q}^{2}-(\bar{\psi}_{Q})^{2}\right).

In general, we have that

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(n_{1},n_{2},\psi,Q)

\displaystyle=n\lambda(1-\lambda)\left(\bar{\psi}_{Q}^{2}-(\bar{\psi}_{Q})^{2}\right)+R,

where
R
is a remainder of lower order.
Note that

\mathrm{GS}^{*}(U_{1})\to\psi(n-Q)+\psi(n-Q-1)-\bar{\psi}_{Q}.

Thus, we consider the quantity

\frac{\psi(n-Q)}{\sqrt{n\lambda(1-\lambda)\left(\bar{\psi}_{Q}^{2}-(\bar{\psi}_{Q})^{2}\right)}}.

Now, let

\displaystyle C(n,\psi)

\displaystyle=(\bar{\psi}_{Q}^{2}-(\bar{\psi}_{Q})^{2})/\psi(n-Q)^{2}

\displaystyle=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-Q}\psi(i)^{2}/\psi(n-Q)^{2}-\left(n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-Q}\psi(i)/\psi(n-Q)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq V_{n,1}-V_{n,2}^{2}.

Then, since
\psi
is continuous and monotonic, it is differentiable. We have by the mean value theorem there exists
n-Q\leq r\leq n-Q+1
,
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<p<1
so that for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

\displaystyle\psi(n-Q+1)-\psi(n-Q)=\psi^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle\implies 1-\frac{\psi(n-Q)}{\psi(n-Q+1)}=\frac{\psi^{\prime}(r)}{\psi(n-Q+1)}

\displaystyle\implies\frac{\psi(n-Q)}{\psi(n-Q+1)}\geq 1-\frac{\psi^{\prime}(r)}{\psi(n-Q+1)}\geq\sqrt{p}.

In addition,
a_{n}=n/(n+1)
is also greater than
\sqrt{p}
eventually.
Thus,

\displaystyle C(n+1,\psi)

\displaystyle=V_{n+1,1}-V_{n+1,2}^{2}

\displaystyle=a_{n}b_{n}V_{n,1}+(n+1)^{-1}-a_{n}^{2}b_{n}^{2}V_{n,2}^{2}-2a_{n}b_{n}V_{n,2}/n-1/(n+1)^{2}

\displaystyle\approx a_{n}b_{n}V_{n,1}-a_{n}^{2}b_{n}^{2}V_{n,2}^{2}

\displaystyle=a_{n}b_{n}C(n,\psi)+(a_{n}b_{n}-a_{n}^{2}b_{n}^{2})V_{n,2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq pC(n,\psi)\text{ eventually. }

We have that

\displaystyle C(n,\psi)

\displaystyle\geq p\sum_{i=n_{0}}^{n-1}C(i,\psi)+a_{n_{0}}b_{n_{0}}C(n_{0},\psi)+(a_{n_{0}}b_{n}-a_{n_{0}}^{2}b_{n_{0}}^{2})V_{n_{0},2}^{2}\geq(a_{n_{0}}b_{n}-a_{n_{0}}^{2}b_{n_{0}}^{2})V_{n_{0},2}^{2}.

Thus, there exists
0<c<1
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
c<C(n,\psi)<1
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.
∎

\frac{1}{2}
apiece. As well, for each
j\in\{1,2,\dots,n-Q\}
, define:

\displaystyle W_{j}=\begin{cases}\psi(j)\quad&\text{w.p. $\frac{1}{2}$}\\
-\psi(j)\quad&\text{w.p. $\frac{1}{2}$}\end{cases}\text{,}

where the
W_{j}
’s and the
W_{0,i}
’s are mutually independent. Notice that under
H_{0}
,

\displaystyle W_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sum_{i=0}^{Q}W_{0,i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-Q}W_{j}.

We have just re-expressed the test statistic in a manner that facilitates the application of the
Theorem C.1

(
Lyapunov Central Limit Theorem
)
.
. Before using this result, we need to compute the relevant moments of the
W_{0,i}
’s and the
W_{j}
’s. First, we observe that for all
i\in\{1,2,\dots,Q\}
, for all
j\in\{1,2,\dots,n-Q\}
and for all
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
:

1)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(W_{j}^{m})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0\quad&\text{if $m$ is odd}\\
\psi^{m}(j)\quad&\text{if $m$ is even}\end{cases}

(B.1)

2)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(|W_{j}|^{m})=\psi^{m}(j)

(B.2)

3)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(W_{0,i}^{m})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0\quad&\text{if $m$ is odd}\\
\psi^{m}(0)\quad&\text{if $m$ is even}\end{cases}

(B.3)

4)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(|W_{0,i}|^{m})=\psi^{m}(0)

(B.4)

5)

\displaystyle\var(W_{j})=\sigma^{2}_{j}=\psi^{2}(j)\text{.}

(B.5)

6)

\displaystyle\var(W_{0,i})=\sigma^{2}_{0,i}=\psi^{2}(0)\text{.}

(B.6)

To make further progress, we note that there must exist
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
and
\psi
is
\Omega(n^{\frac{\ell}{4}})
and
o(n^{\frac{\ell+1}{4}})
. Our strategy is then to bound above the growth rate of the numerator of the fraction present in Condition
C.1
and bound below the growth rate of the denominator. For reference, Condition
C.1
reads as follows in our context (due to the above equations):

\frac{Q|\psi(0)|^{2+\delta}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-Q}\left(|\psi(j)|^{2+\delta}\right)}{\left(Q\psi^{2}(0)+\sum_{j=1}^{n-Q}\psi^{2}(j)\right)^{1+\frac{\delta}{2}}}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as $n\rightarrow\infty$ for some $\delta>0$}.

(B.7)

Since
\psi
is non-negative, increasing and
o(n^{\frac{\ell+1}{4}})
, term
j
of the sum in the numerator is
o(j^{\frac{\ell+1}{2}+\delta\,\frac{\ell+1}{4}})
, so the sum as a whole is
o((n-Q)^{\frac{\ell+3}{2}+\delta\,\frac{\ell+1}{4}})
. The
Q|\psi(0)|^{2+\delta}
term does not affect this bound on the asymptotic growth rate of the sum as
Q\leq n
and
\psi
is strictly increasing. Meanwhile, the sum in the denominator is
\Omega((n-Q)^{\frac{\ell}{2}+1})
, so the denominator as a whole is
\Omega((n-Q)^{\frac{\ell+2}{2}+\delta\,\frac{\ell+2}{4}})
. Thus, if
\delta\geq 2
, the limit of the fraction at large as
n\rightarrow\infty
is indeed 0. This indicates that the hypotheses of the
Theorem C.1

(
Lyapunov Central Limit Theorem
)
.
are satisfied, which is what we needed to show. The asymptotic distribution of
W_{1}
then follows from the
Theorem C.1

(
Lyapunov Central Limit Theorem
)
.
and Theorem
A.1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Fix neighboring databases
\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{w}
and assume row
j
of the former can be changed to produce the latter. We may assume, without loss of generality, that
a_{j}^{{}^{\prime}}>a_{j}
. We will also divide the rows of
\mathbf{v}
into three regions:

1.

A_{1}
, consisting of the rows with ranks not exceeding
r_{j}
;

2.

A_{2}
, consisting of the rows with ranks exceeding
r_{j}
but with
a_{i}<a_{j}^{{}^{\prime}}
, where
a_{j}^{{}^{\prime}}
is the absolute difference of row
j
of database
\mathbf{w}
; and,

3.

A_{3}
, consisting of the rows with
a_{i}>a_{j}^{{}^{\prime}}
.

Before proceeding further, we will define some more notation. Let
\Delta j
be the change in the signed rank of row
j
when transitioning from
\mathbf{v}
to
\mathbf{w}
. In addition, for
i=1,2,3
, let
\Delta A_{i}
be the change in the sum of the signed ranks of all rows in region
A_{i}
upon making this transition. The change in the overall test statistic,
\Delta W_{1}
, is the sum of these two effects. This is because the ranks of the data points in
A_{1}
and
A_{3}
are unaffected by the transition between the neighbouring databases.

We notice that the only rows whose ranks differ between the databases are row
j
and the rows in region
A_{2}
. Let us first bound
\Delta j
. As
\psi
is increasing and
s_{j}
changes in the worst-case scenario (this is the same justification as in the proof of Theorem 4.1 of
[
7
]
), we have that:

\displaystyle\Delta j\leq\psi(n-Q)+\psi(\max\{c_{1}+1-Q,0\}).

Meanwhile, the ranks of each row in region
A_{2}
decrease by 1 apiece, while their signs remain unchanged. Thus,

\displaystyle\Delta A_{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i\in A_{2}}\psi(\max\{r_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}-Q,0\})-\psi(\max\{r_{i}-Q,0\})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in A_{2}}\left|\psi(\max\{r_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}-Q,0\})-\psi(\max\{r_{i}-Q,0\})\right|\quad\text{(by the triangle inequality)}

\displaystyle=(\psi(\max\{c_{1}+2-Q,0\})-\psi(\max\{c_{1}+1-Q,0\}))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+(\psi(\max\{c_{1}+3-Q,0\})-\psi(\max\{c_{1}+2-Q,0\}))

\displaystyle\phantom{=\,}+\dots+(\psi(\max\{c_{1}+c_{2}+1-Q,0\})-\psi(\max\{c_{1}+c_{2}-Q,0\}))

\displaystyle=\psi(\max\{c_{1}+c_{2}+1-Q,0\})-\psi(\max\{c_{1}+1-Q,0\})

\displaystyle\leq\psi(n-Q)-\psi(\max\{c_{1}+1-Q,0\}).

Then,

\displaystyle\Delta W_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\Delta j+\Delta A_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\psi(n-Q)+\psi(\max\{c_{1}+1-Q,0\})+\psi(n-Q)-\psi(\max\{c_{1}+1-Q,0\})

\displaystyle=2\psi(n-Q).

This is what we wanted to show.
∎

Appendix C
Useful auxiliary results

We now present a set of results which will be useful in our proofs pertaining to the asymptotic distributions of our test statistics.
Our theoretical results rely upon two central limit theorems.
The first of these results is the
Theorem C.1

(
Lyapunov Central Limit Theorem
)
.
, which generalizes the ‘ordinary’ Central Limit Theorem to sets of random variables which are not necessarily identically distributed. We adopt the formulation of this result from
Pratt and Gibbons, 2012
.

Theorem C.1

(
Lyapunov Central Limit Theorem
)
.

If
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
are independent, real-valued random variables with possibly different distributions, each having finite absolute moments of the order
2+\delta
for some
\delta>0
, and if

\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left(|X_{j}-\mu_{j}|^{2+\delta}\right)}{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}^{2}\right)^{1+\frac{\delta}{2}}}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as $n\rightarrow\infty$},

(C.1)

where
\mu_{j}={\mathbb{E}}(X_{j})
and
\sigma_{j}^{2}=\var(X_{j})
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}(X_{j}-\mu_{j})}{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}},

converges in distribution to the standard normal distribution.

The second result is due to
Wald and Wolfowitz, 1944
:

Theorem C.2

(
Wald-Wolfowitz Theorem
)
.

For a sequence
({y}_{n})
and for all
m,r\in{\mathbb{N}}
, define:

\displaystyle\mu_{r}(y_{n},m)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(y_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}y_{j}\right)^{r}.

Let the sequence
({a}_{n})
have the property that

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{r}(a_{n},m)}{\mu_{2}(a_{n},m)^{\frac{r}{2}}}=O(1),

(C.2)

for all
r>2
, and let the sequence
({d}_{n})
also satisfy this property. Let
X=\left(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m}\right)^{T}
be a random vector whose elements form a permutation of the first
m
terms of
({a}_{n})
with probability 1. Then, the random variable

\displaystyle L_{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{i}\,x_{i},

has the property that

\displaystyle\frac{L_{m}-{\mathbb{E}}(L_{m})}{\text{SD}(L_{m})}\mathrel{\leadsto}N(0,1)\text{.}

Appendix D
Correction of the Proof of Theorem A.8 of
[
8
]

In Section
3
, we asserted that there is a minor oversight in the proof of Theorem A.8 of
Couch et al., 2019
. However, using their techniques, and correcting the mistake, Lemma
D.1
below implies Theorem A.8 of
Couch et al., 2019
holds whenever

\displaystyle 1-{\Erf\left(\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}(n-1)}\right)}<\alpha<0.5\text{,}

where

{\Erf\left(x\right)}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}\exp(-t^{2})dt\,

is the error function.
This is restriction is essentially immaterial, for example, it holds for
n\geq 3
when
\alpha=0.05
, but does depend on
\alpha
.

The issue with the proof of Theorem A.8 of
Couch et al., 2019
is the following.
At one point in the proof,
[
8
]
seem to imply that if
X\sim N(\mu_{X},\sigma_{X}^{2})
and if
Y\sim N(\mu_{Y},\sigma_{Y}^{2})
with
\mu_{X}<\mu_{Y}
and
\sigma^{2}_{X}<\sigma^{2}_{Y}
, then the lower-tail critical values of
Y
are less than the corresponding critical values of
X
. In general, this is not true. To give a counterexample, the 1st percentile of
X\sim N(0,1)
(which is approximately -2.33) is less than the 1st percentile of
Y\sim N(-0.5,0.5^{2})
(which is approximately -1.66).

Another issue is that, in the Mann–Whitney test (as in the Siegel–Tukey Test), while
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
are both normally-distributed, the “final” test statistic
U
follows a different distribution (i.e., a half-normal distribution). Below, we prove a modified version of their Theorem A.8 which accounts for this.

In this work, we adopt a similar algorithm as that of
Couch et al., 2019
to underestimate the difference between the groups sizes with high probability. Lemma
D.1
below gives that underestimating the difference in the groups sizes does not increase the type I error of the Siegel–Tukey test.

Lemma D.1
.

Suppose that
\alpha
satisfies

\displaystyle 1-{\Erf\left(\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}(n-1)}\right)}<\alpha<0.5\text{.}

Let
\pi_{\alpha}(n_{1};n)
be the
100\alpha
th percentile of the limiting distribution of the private (or non-private) Siegel–Tukey test statistic under the null hypothesis when the group sizes are
n_{1}
and
n-n_{1}
, respectively. Then,
\pi_{\alpha}
is decreasing in
|\frac{n}{2}-n_{1}|
.

Proof.

Let
n_{2}=n-n_{1}
. We know that
U_{1}\mathrel{\leadsto}N(\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{2},\frac{n_{1}n_{2}(n+1)}{12})
. Let us consider
U
. By definition,

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}U_{1}\quad&\text{if $U_{1}\leq\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{2}$}\\
n_{1}n_{2}-U_{1}\quad&\text{if $U_{1}>\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{2}$.}\end{cases}

Thus,

\displaystyle U-\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}U_{1}-\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{2}\quad&\text{if $U_{1}\leq\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{2}$}\\
\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{2}-U_{1}\quad&\text{if $U_{1}>\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{2}$}\\
\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\left|U_{1}-\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{2}\right|\text{.}

Since

\displaystyle\left(U_{1}-\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{2}\right)\mathrel{\leadsto}N\left(0,\frac{n_{1}n_{2}(n+1)}{12}\right)

it is the case that

\displaystyle-\left|U_{1}-\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{2}\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\text{Half-Normal}\left(\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{n_{1}n_{2}(n+1)}{12}}\right)\text{.}

As such,
\pi_{\alpha}
, the
100\alpha
th percentile of
U
, is given by:

\displaystyle\pi_{\alpha}=\frac{m(n-m)}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{m(n-m)(n+1)}{12}}\sqrt{2}\,{\Erf^{-1}\left(1-\alpha\right)}

where
m=\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
. Extending
\pi_{\alpha}
to be continuous in
m
and taking the first derivative, we obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\pi_{\alpha}}{\partial m}=\left(\frac{n}{2}-m\right)\left(1-\frac{{\Erf^{-1}\left(1-\alpha\right)}}{\sqrt{6m(n-m)}}\right)\text{.}

We would like
\pi_{\alpha}
to be increasing among
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
for
0\leq m\leq\frac{n}{2}
. This would be the case if the following two conditions were met:

1.

\frac{\partial\pi_{\alpha}}{\partial m}
is positive for all
m\in\left[1,\frac{n}{2}\right)
.

2.

\pi_{\alpha}(0;n)<\pi_{\alpha}(1;n)
.

Let us start with item 1. We note that
\frac{n}{2}-m
is positive for all
m\in\left[1,\frac{n}{2}\right)
. Consequently,
\frac{\partial\pi_{\alpha}}{\partial m}
is positive whenever

\displaystyle\frac{{\Erf^{-1}\left(1-\alpha\right)}}{\sqrt{6m(n-m)}}<1\text{.}

We may also note that
m(n-m)
is increasing in
m
for
0<m<n/2
. Hence, the left-hand side of the above expression is maximized when
m
takes the lowest value in the interval under consideration, i.e., when
m=1
. By substituting
m=1
and rearranging, we find that item 1 holds whenever

•

n>\frac{\left[{\Erf^{-1}\left(1-\alpha\right)}\right]^{2}}{6}+1

or

•

\alpha>1-{\Erf\left(\sqrt{6(n-1)}\right)}

.

Let us move on to item 2, which, after substituting
m=0
and
m=1
into the expression for
\pi_{\alpha}
and setting the latter to be greater than or equal to the former, turns out to be equivalent to:

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{n-1}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{n-1}{6}}{\Erf^{-1}\left(1-\alpha\right)}.

This is in turn equivalent to either or both of the two following inequalities:

•

n>\frac{2}{3}\left[{\Erf^{-1}\left(1-\alpha\right)}\right]^{2}+1
,

•

\alpha>1-{\Erf\left(\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}(n-1)}\right)}
.

As long as these inequalities (which are stricter than the ones from before) hold, we have our desired result.
∎

The restrictions on
\alpha
and
n
are very mild and would be inconsequential for the purposes of virtually any study. To illustrate, in order to be able to use any
\alpha\geq 0.05
, we only require
n\geq 3
. Even the use of
\alpha=0.001
, which is low enough for a broad range of disciplines, only requires
n\geq 5
.
