Abstract
Berberine is a plant alkaloid with insulin-sensitizing properties. Recent meta-analyses have addressed glycemic
endpoints and components of the metabolic syndrome, but none has specifically synthesized its effect on insulin
resistance measured by the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance in non-diabetic populations. The
objective was to estimate that effect in non-diabetic adults. We searched PubMed for randomized controlled trials
comparing berberine, in any salt, formulation or dose, alone or as an add-on, with a non-berberine control or an active
comparator, in non-diabetic adults with insulin resistance or metabolic risk. The primary outcome was the mean
difference in the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance. A random-effects model was fitted by restricted
maximum likelihood, with subgroup and leave-one-out sensitivity analyses and risk-of-bias appraisal using the RoB 2
tool. Of 56 records, nine trials met eligibility and six, comprising 485 participants, provided extractable data.
Berberine reduced insulin resistance versus control by a mean difference of −0.95 (95% confidence interval −1.29 to
−0.61), with substantial heterogeneity. The effect was larger and homogeneous in polycystic ovary syndrome and
smaller in the metabolic and prediabetic subgroup. The prediction interval crossed the null. Five of six pooled trials
carried some concerns and the single low-risk trial, which was the largest, showed no effect. In conclusion, berberine
was associated with a reduction in insulin resistance, most consistently in polycystic ovary syndrome, but the pooled
effect was driven by smaller trials at higher risk of bias. Larger and methodologically rigorous trials are needed before
any clinical recommendation.
Keywords: Berberine; Insulin resistance; Prediabetic state; Polycystic ovary syndrome; Meta-analysis as topic.

1. Introduction
Insulin resistance is a central mechanism linking obesity, the metabolic syndrome, prediabetes, polycystic ovary
syndrome and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and it precedes the onset of type 2 diabetes (Tabák et al., 2012). Interventions
that improve insulin sensitivity before diabetes develops are therefore of clinical interest for prevention.
Berberine, an isoquinoline alkaloid extracted from plants such as Coptis chinensis and Berberis species, activates
AMP-activated protein kinase and has demonstrated glucose-lowering and lipid-lowering effects (Lee et al., 2006; Xu et al.,
2021). It has been proposed as a natural insulin sensitizer, and early randomized trials in type 2 diabetes reported reductions in
glycemia comparable to those of metformin (Yin et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2008).
Several meta-analyses have evaluated berberine for glycemic control and for components of the metabolic syndrome.
The most recent and comprehensive of these (Liu et al., 2025) pooled triglycerides, fasting plasma glucose, waist
circumference, lipids, body-mass index and the two-hour oral glucose tolerance test, but did not synthesize the homeostatic
model assessment of insulin resistance (Matthews et al., 1985). No meta-analysis has specifically quantified the effect of
berberine on insulin resistance restricted to non-diabetic adults, a prevention-oriented question distinct from the glycemiccontrol question studied in type 2 diabetes.
The objective was to estimate that effect in non-diabetic adults and to characterize its consistency across non-diabetic
populations.

2. Methodology
2.1 Design and eligibility criteria
This is a bibliographic study (Snyder, 2019), specifically classified as a systematic literature review with quantitative
synthesis. Following the classification of research approaches proposed by Gil (2026) and by Risemberg et al. (2026), the
study adopts a quantitative approach: the corpus was assembled through the systematic selection of randomized controlled

treatment of the data.
This review followed the PRISMA 2020 guideline (Page et al., 2021). Eligible studies were randomized controlled
trials, parallel or crossover, enrolling non-diabetic adults aged 18 years or over with insulin resistance or metabolic risk,
namely metabolic syndrome, prediabetes or impaired fasting glucose, polycystic ovary syndrome diagnosed by contemporary
criteria (Teede et al., 2024), or non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. Trials had to compare berberine, in any salt or formulation,
including dihydroberberine and phospholipid or phytosome preparations, at any dose, alone or as an add-on, against a nonberberine control such as placebo, no treatment, usual care or lifestyle, or against an active comparator such as metformin,
where berberine was the only component differing between the compared arms, and had to report the homeostatic model
assessment of insulin resistance.
Studies enrolling populations with established type 2 or type 1 diabetes as the target condition, non-randomized
designs, berberine given only within a fixed multi-component formulation, and studies without an extractable insulinresistance outcome were excluded.
2.2 Search and study selection
PubMed was searched from inception to 2024, combining terms for berberine, insulin resistance and metabolic risk
with a randomized-trial filter, restricted to human studies. Titles and abstracts were screened with the assistance of a large
language model; potentially eligible reports underwent independent full-text assessment. Reference lists of relevant reviews,
including a meta-analysis on berberine and metabolic-syndrome components (Liu et al., 2025), were checked for additional
trials.
2.3 Data extraction and risk of bias
Two reviewers independently extracted, for the berberine and control arms, the sample size and the homeostatic
model assessment of insulin resistance at baseline and at the end of the intervention, or the change from baseline where
reported, together with the population, the berberine formulation and dose, the duration of the intervention and the comparator.
Risk of bias was assessed with the Cochrane RoB 2 tool across five domains with an overall judgement (Sterne et al., 2019).
Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.
2.4 Statistical analysis
The primary outcome was the mean difference in the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance, calculated
as berberine minus control, so that negative values favour berberine. Endpoint values were pooled together with change-frombaseline values, which estimate the same intervention effect under randomization. Change scores were used where a baseline
imbalance was present (Rondanelli et al., 2023) or where only change was reported (Ming et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2015).
Where only a 95% confidence interval was reported, the standard error was derived from its width; where only
baseline and final values were available for a change analysis, the change-score standard deviation was imputed assuming a
pre-post correlation of 0.5. A random-effects model was fitted by restricted maximum likelihood using the metafor package
(Viechtbauer, 2010). Heterogeneity was quantified with the inconsistency statistic and with tau squared, a 95% prediction
interval was computed as recommended for random-effects syntheses (IntHout et al., 2016), and pre-specified subgroup
analyses, comparing polycystic ovary syndrome with metabolic and prediabetic populations, and leave-one-out sensitivity

2.5 Role of artificial intelligence
The literature search and the initial title and abstract screening were assisted by a large language model. All
subsequent steps, namely full-text eligibility, data extraction and risk-of-bias assessment, were conducted independently by the
two authors, who reconciled any differences by consensus and are responsible for the data reported.
2.6 Ethical considerations
This study is a systematic review based exclusively on aggregate data from previously published studies, without
access to individual participant data and without direct involvement of human subjects. It therefore did not require appraisal by
a Research Ethics Committee.

3. Results
3.1 Study selection
The search returned 56 records. At title and abstract screening, 22 records were excluded and 34 reports underwent
full-text assessment, of which 25 were excluded: no extractable insulin-resistance outcome (12), berberine not isolable from a
co-intervention (8), review or protocol (2), ineligible design (2), and diabetic population (1). Nine randomized controlled trials
were included in the systematic review. Six provided extractable data and were meta-analysed; three were included in the
narrative synthesis only, namely Pérez-Rubio et al. (2013), in which insulin sensitivity was reported as the Matsuda index, Pu
et al. (2021), available as an abstract only and without extractable per-arm data, and Nikseresht et al. (2024), in which the
outcome was reported only graphically. The selection process is summarized in Figure 1.

3.3 Effect of berberine on insulin resistance
Berberine significantly reduced the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance compared with control, with a
pooled mean difference of −0.95 (95% confidence interval −1.29 to −0.61; p < 0.001; six trials, 485 participants). Individual
estimates ranged from −1.30 to +0.16: Wei et al. (2012) −1.21; An et al. (2014) −1.30; Yan et al. (2015) −0.80; Panigrahi and
Mohanty (2023) −0.99; Rondanelli et al. (2023) −0.91; and Ming et al. (2021) +0.16. Heterogeneity was substantial, with an
inconsistency of 72% and tau squared of 0.10, and the 95% prediction interval, from −2.12 to +0.21, crossed the null,
indicating that a future trial could plausibly show no effect.
3.4 Subgroup and sensitivity analyses
The effect was concentrated in trials of polycystic ovary syndrome, with a mean difference of −1.27 (95% confidence
interval −1.52 to −1.01; two trials; inconsistency 0%), and was smaller and heterogeneous in the metabolic and prediabetic
subgroup, with a mean difference of −0.69 (95% confidence interval −1.22 to −0.16; four trials; inconsistency 63%). The
subgroup difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.093). In leave-one-out analysis the pooled estimate remained
between −0.84 and −1.08 and statistically significant throughout. Omitting the null trial (Ming et al., 2021) tightened the
estimate to −1.08 and reduced the inconsistency to 24%, identifying it as the principal source of heterogeneity.

3.6 Studies not pooled
Pérez-Rubio et al. (2013), in metabolic syndrome with 24 participants, reported improved insulin sensitivity by the
Matsuda index but did not report the homeostatic model assessment. Pu et al. (2021), in olanzapine-associated metabolic
disturbance with 132 participants, reported a significant reduction with berberine in the abstract, without extractable per-arm
data. Nikseresht et al. (2024), in prediabetic men in a six-arm design combining high-intensity interval training with or without
berberine and 54 participants, reported that berberine alone reduced insulin resistance versus untreated control but added no
benefit over exercise; values were presented only graphically. All three were directionally consistent with the pooled result but
could not contribute numeric data.
A currency check performed at revision confirmed that recent randomized trials of berberine in non-diabetic
populations, such as Koperska et al. (2025) in metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease, do not report the
homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance and therefore did not alter the pooled estimate.

4. Discussion
4.1 Main findings
In this updated meta-analysis restricted to non-diabetic adults, berberine was associated with a clinically meaningful
reduction in the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance, with a mean difference of −0.95, most consistently in
polycystic ovary syndrome, where two trials agreed closely with an inconsistency of 0%. This is compatible with the proposed
action of berberine as an insulin sensitizer mediated by AMP-activated protein kinase (Lee et al., 2006) and with the direction
of the three studies that could not be pooled.

4.2 Interpretation and the weight of the evidence
The result must be read against its internal structure. Five of the six pooled trials carried some concerns, and the large
reductions came predominantly from smaller trials. The single trial at low risk of bias, which was also the largest with 148
participants (Ming et al., 2021), found no effect and was the dominant source of heterogeneity. Removing it left the effect
intact, but its inclusion is precisely what a cautious reader should weigh. The prediction interval crossed the null, which means
that the interval within which the effect of a future comparable trial is expected to fall includes no benefit at all (IntHout et al.,

4.3 Relation to previous reviews
Prior meta-analyses of berberine addressed type 2 diabetes, glycemic traits or metabolic-syndrome components (Xu et
al., 2021; Yin et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2008). The most recent and comprehensive (Liu et al., 2025), which searched multiple
databases including Chinese-language sources, meta-analysed lipids, fasting glucose, anthropometry and the two-hour glucose
tolerance test, but did not pool the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance. The present review complements that
work by isolating the insulin-resistance question in a non-diabetic population, and by incorporating trials of polycystic ovary
syndrome, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease and prediabetes that a scope restricted to metabolic syndrome would exclude.
An overview of systematic reviews of berberine across health outcomes reached a comparable conclusion regarding
the fragility of the underlying evidence base, noting that most syntheses rest on small trials of limited methodological quality
(Shi et al., 2025).

4.4 Limitations
Only six trials with 485 participants contributed to the primary analysis, several were small, and heterogeneity was
substantial. The pooled populations, berberine formulations, comparators and durations differed, and endpoint and changefrom-baseline values were combined. Two variance inputs relied on assumptions, namely a conversion from confidence
interval to standard error (Yan et al., 2015) and a pre-post correlation of 0.5 (Rondanelli et al., 2023), and one influential trial
reported an unusually small standard deviation (Panigrahi & Mohanty, 2023); these are flagged for confirmation against
source. The unequal arm sizes in Ming et al. (2021), of 49 versus 99, reflect a four-arm design, in which berberine alone and
berberine combined with Bifidobacterium were each allocated 49 participants against a placebo arm of 99, and are therefore a
feature of the allocation rather than a reporting error. The search was limited to PubMed, so non-English trials reporting the
outcome, for example in Chinese databases, may have been missed, although cross-checking a multi-database review (Liu et
al., 2025) yielded no additional poolable trial. Publication bias could not be formally assessed with six studies (Egger et al.,
1997).

5. Conclusion
In non-diabetic adults, berberine was associated with a reduction in the homeostatic model assessment of insulin
resistance, most consistently in polycystic ovary syndrome. Because the effect was driven by smaller trials at higher risk of
bias while the only low-risk trial did not confirm it, and because the prediction interval crossed the null, the finding should be
regarded as promising but not definitive. Larger, adequately powered trials at low risk of bias reporting this outcome are
needed to establish whether berberine meaningfully improves insulin resistance before the onset of diabetes.
