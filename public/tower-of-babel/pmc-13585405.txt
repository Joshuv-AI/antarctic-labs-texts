Cirrhosis Impairs Reconstitution of the Circulating T‐Lymphocyte Compartment After Direct‐Acting Antiviral Therapy for Hepatitis C Virus Infection
Authors: Lorena Paule, Elisa Castillo, Leticia Muñoz, Javier Martínez, Carlos Fernández, Alejandro Miranda, Margaret Lario, Jorge Monserrat, Miguel Ángel Ortega, José Luis Calleja, Melchor Álvarez‐Mon, Agustín Albillos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDirect‐acting antivirals (DAAs) achieve sustained virological response (SVR) in over 95% of patients with chronic hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection. Although viral eradication allows partial immune restoration, it remains unclear whether established cirrhosis limits recovery of the circulating T lymphocyte compartment after SVR.MethodsWe performed a phenotypic and functional analysis of circulating T‐helper (Th) and T‐cytotoxic (Tc) lymphocytes in patients with chronic HCV infection without fibrosis (HCV‐NoF, n = 21) or with cirrhosis (HCV‐cirrhosis, n = 20), as well as in alcohol‐related cirrhosis (AL‐cirrhosis, n = 12) and healthy controls (HC, n = 12). HCV patients were evaluated at baseline and 14 weeks after SVR following DAA therapy. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were analysed by flow cytometry to quantify T lymphocyte subsets, cytokine production (IFNγ, IL‐17), and regulatory T lymphocytes (CD25high Tregs).ResultsHCV infection was associated with marked Tc lymphopenia and selective depletion of naïve Th lymphocytes, regardless of fibrosis stage. These alterations were exacerbated in cirrhosis, particularly in decompensated disease, and closely resembled those observed in AL‐cirrhosis. After SVR, HCV‐NoF patients showed normalization of Tc counts, redistribution of Tc subsets, and restoration of Th1/Th17 balance and IFNγ/IL‐17 profiles. In contrast, HCV‐cirrhosis patients exhibited minimal immune recovery, with persistent Th and Tc lymphopenia, sustained depletion of naïve and central memory T lymphocyte subsets, and ongoing inflammatory cytokine skewing. CD25high Treg frequencies increased after SVR in both HCV groups but remained below HC levels, with normalization of chemokine receptor expression occurring only in HCV‐NoF patients.DiscussionCirrhosis profoundly constrains immune reconstitution after HCV cure, limiting post‐treatment T‐cell recovery.

Introduction

Direct‐acting antivirals (DAAs) have transformed the management of hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection, achieving cure rates > 95% and thereby preventing progression of liver injury. Beyond viral eradication, etiological treatment of advanced chronic liver disease—including HCV—can be accompanied by fibrosis regression and improvement in portal hypertension and hepatic dysfunction, with downstream effects on systemic manifestations such as immune alterations [1, 2]. Chronic HCV infection is associated with substantial disturbances in adaptive immunity, including quantitative and qualitative changes in circulating T cells, reshaping of subset composition, and increased expression of activation/exhaustion markers (e.g., PD‐1, TIM‐3), which collectively contribute to impaired antiviral function and viral persistence [3, 4]. DAA‐induced viral clearance has enabled detailed investigation of immune reconstitution, with reports describing redistribution of memory subsets towards an effector phenotype and partial downregulation of inhibitory pathways on CD8+ T cells [5, 6, 7, 8]. However, whether such immune restoration occurs uniformly across the spectrum of disease—or is limited by established cirrhosis—remains insufficiently defined.

Cirrhosis itself, irrespective of aetiology, is characterized by immune dysfunction, encompassing prominent abnormalities in the T‐lymphocyte compartment. Patients with cirrhosis typically exhibit reduced CD4+ T‐cell counts and CD8+ T cells with an atypical, activated/exhausted phenotype (e.g., increased HLA‐DR and TIM‐3) together with impaired cytokine‐producing capacity, reflecting profound systemic immune dysregulation [9, 10, 11, 12]. In advanced HCV‐related liver disease, HCV‐driven immune perturbations are therefore likely to overlap with, and be amplified by, cirrhosis‐related mechanisms, potentially limiting immune recovery after viral cure.

The rapid and effective clearance of HCV with DAAs offers a unique opportunity to study immune alterations attributable to HCV from those driven by cirrhosis and to assess their reversibility according to fibrosis severity. Here, we performed a comprehensive phenotypic and functional analysis of the circulating T‐cell compartment in patients with chronic HCV infection at different stages of fibrosis, before and after sustained virological response (SVR), to determine whether cirrhosis constrains immune reconstitution following viral eradication.

Materials and Methods

Patient Selection and Study Design

Patients and healthy controls were recruited from the Department of Gastroenterology at Hospital Ramón y Cajal and Hospital Puerta de Hierro (Madrid, Spain) between 2015 and 2020. The study was approved by the ethic committees of the participating hospital and conducted in accordance with the principles of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion.

We included subjects with chronic liver disease due to HCV infection, both without fibrosis (HCV‐NoF, 21 patients, F0‐F1) and with cirrhosis (HCV‐cirrhosis, 20 patients, F4), as well as individuals with alcohol‐related cirrhosis (AL‐cirrhosis, 12 patients) and healthy controls (HC, 12 subjects). The diagnosis of chronic liver disease was established based on clinical, biochemical, and imaging findings, including liver stiffness measurement (LSM) by transient elastography (FibroScan VCTE). Absence of fibrosis (F0–F1) was defined by a LSM < 7.0 kPa, whereas cirrhosis was defined by an LSM > 15 kPa [13, 14]. Clinically significant portal hypertension was considered present based on LSM > 25 kPa or LSM 15–22 kPa with a platelet count < 110,000/μL. Within the cirrhosis patient groups, patients were classified as having compensated or decompensated cirrhosis based on the presence of complications, primarily ascites, at the time of inclusion. HCV infection was diagnosed by detectable serum HCV RNA, while alcohol‐related liver disease was diagnosed based on a documented history of daily alcohol intake exceeding 20 g in women and 40 g in men.

Participants were excluded if any of the following were present: age > 75 years; active alcohol consumption within the previous 3 months; acute decompensation of cirrhosis within the previous 3 months; recent (< 3 months) or ongoing antibiotic therapy; current treatment with immunomodulators or corticosteroids; HIV infection; hepatic or extrahepatic malignancy; body mass index > 30 kg/m2; diabetes mellitus.

At enrolment, all participants underwent a structured physical examination, comprehensive biochemical testing, and venous blood sampling for immunological analyses. A second blood sample was obtained 3 months after achieving sustained virological response (SVR) following treatment with direct‐acting antivirals (DAA) in HCV patients.

Sample Collection and Processing

Fresh venous blood was collected in both heparinized and serum‐separating gel tubes (Becton‐Dickinson Vacutainer System, Meylan Cedex, France). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from heparinized samples by Ficoll density gradient centrifugation (Lymphoprep, Nyegaard, Oslo, Norway). Serum was obtained from gel separator tubes after centrifugation and stored at −80°C until further analysis.

Flow Cytometry Analysis

Subpopulations of T‐helper (Th; CD3+CD4+) and T‐cytotoxic (Tc; CD3+CD8+) lymphocytes—Naïve (CD45RA+CD27+), central memory (CD45RA−CD27+), effector memory (CD45RA−CD27−), and terminal effector (CD45RA+CD27−)—were quantified in PBMCs stained with fluorochrome‐conjugated monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) listed in Supporting Information S1: Tabel S1. The viability of fresh PBMCs consistently exceeded 95%.

For intracellular cytokine profiling of Th and Tc cells, PBMCs were cultured for 6 h in complete medium and stimulated with phorbol myristate acetate (PMA), ionomycin, and brefeldin A. Following stimulation, surface staining was performed using mAbs listed in Supporting Information S1: Table S2. Cell viability was assessed with the Live/Dead Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain Kit. Cells were then processed with the FIX & PERM Cell Permeabilization Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Intracellular cytokines were stained using cytokine‐specific mAbs (Supporting Information S1: Table S3).

CD25high regulatory T lymphocytes (CD25high‐Tregs; CD4+CD25^high^FoxP3+) were analysed by surface staining with antibodies listed in Supporting Information S1: Table S4, followed by fixation/permeabilization and intracellular FoxP3 staining using the eBioscience Anti‐Human FoxP3 Staining Set, following the manufacture instructions.

Cells were acquired on a FACSAria flow cytometer operated with DiVa software (Becton‐Dickinson, San Jose, CA, USA). Data analysis was performed using FlowJo v4.6 and BD FACSDiva v8.0.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]). Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons between groups, and Wilcoxon signed‐rank test for within‐group comparisons. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 23 for Windows (IBM SPSS Statistics).

HCV Infection, Independently of the Presence of Cirrhosis, Is Associated With Profound Tc Lymphopenia

Total circulating T lymphocyte counts in HCV‐NoF patients were similar to those of HC (median difference < 20%). Patients with cirrhosis, whether HCV‐ or alcohol‐related, showed approximately a 2.7‐fold reduction in total T cells (p < 0.01; Figure 1 and Supporting Information S1: Table S5).

Despite preserved total T‐cell numbers in HCV‐NoF, Tc cells were reduced by ∼2.55‐fold compared with HC (p < 0.01), whereas Th‐cell counts remained within normal ranges. In contrast, both Th and Tc populations were significantly decreased in HCV‐ and AL‐cirrhosis (Th: ∼2.7–2.9‐fold decrease; Tc: 2.2–3.6‐fold decrease; p < 0.01) (Figure 1 and Supporting Information S1: Table S5). Stratification by disease severity showed that decompensated cirrhosis exhibited the most pronounced lymphopenia, with more than 3‐fold reductions in Th and Tc counts compared with HC (Supporting Information S1: Table S6). Across all patient groups, Tc lymphopenia was driven by significant reductions in the numbers and frequencies of naïve (∼6.5–11.7‐fold), central memory (∼11.3–14.4‐fold), and effector memory (∼8.4–17.7‐fold) subsets, while effector Tc cell numbers were largely preserved (Figure 2A–B).

The expression frequencies of Th lymphocyte subpopulations were broadly similar across patient groups compared with HC, although all patient groups showed significant reductions in the naïve Th compartment together with increases in the effector memory and terminal effector subsets (Figure 2D, Supporting Information S1: Table S5). In HCV‐NoF patients, this pattern reflects reduced naïve Th cells (2.2‐fold decrease, p < 0.01) along with compensatory expansion of effector subsets (effector memory: 2.3‐fold increase; terminal effector: 3.7‐fold increase) relative to HC (Figure 2C, Supporting Information S1: Table S5). In contrast, patients with cirrhosis exhibited marked Th lymphopenia, characterized by pronounced reductions in naïve Th (6.1–7‐fold decrease, p < 0.01) and central memory (2.4–3.2‐fold decrease, p < 0.01) populations, preventing any compensatory expansion of effector subsets (Figure 2C, Supporting Information S1: Table S5).

Both cirrhosis groups showed more severe T‐cell lymphopenia in decompensated patients, with reductions of varying magnitude depending on aetiology (2‐fold decrease in AL‐cirrhosis vs. 1.4‐fold in HCV‐cirrhosis) (Supporting Information S1: Table S6). Consistently, the Th and Tc compartments showed more pronounced depletions in decompensated patients compared with compensated patients and HC (Supporting Information S1: Table S6). In AL‐cirrhosis, the Th compartment and its subpopulations were more affected, whereas Tc depletion was milder. In contrast, HCV‐cirrhosis showed more severe lymphopenia across all Th and Tc subpopulations, reaching significance in total naïve and effector memory Tc cells and in overall Tc counts (p < 0.05) (Supporting Information S1: Table S6).

Recovery of the Circulating Tc Lymphocyte Compartment After HCV Cure Is Restricted to Patients Without Cirrhosis

DAA therapy induced a robust recovery of Tc cells in HCV‐NoF patients. Total Tc counts increased by ∼2.4‐fold (p < 0.01), reaching post‐SVR values comparable to HC. Tc subset redistribution after SVR was striking, with central memory Tc cells increasing ∼3.7‐fold (p < 0.01), effector memory cells increasing ∼3.8‐fold (p < 0.01), and terminal effector cells decreasing by ∼1.9‐fold, resulting in final values not significantly different from those of HC (Figure 2B). Notably, depletion of the naïve Th subset persisted after SVR in HCV‐NoF, accompanied by sustained homeostatic expansion of effector Th subsets compared with baseline (p < 0.05).

HCV‐cirrhosis patients showed no significant recovery in Th or Tc counts or subset composition after SVR. Post‐treatment values remained similar to AL‐cirrhosis and far below HC (Figures 1, 2). The Th compartment showed a non‐significant increase, accompanied by non‐significant rises across naïve, effector memory, and terminal effector subsets. By contrast, the Tc compartment displayed a modest but significant reduction after DAA treatment (1.3‐fold decrease, p < 0.01), driven by mild depletions across all Tc subsets, reaching significance in the central memory population (p < 0.05) (Figures 1, 2 and Supporting Information S1: Table S5).

As shown in Supporting Information S1: Table S6), no significant differences were observed in treatment response between compensated and decompensated HCV‐cirrhosis.

Cytokine‐Producing Tc and Th Subsets Normalize After SVR Only in HCV‐NoF

In Tc cells, HCV infection led to ∼40%–50% reductions in IFNγ expression, together with ∼3.5‐fold increases in IL‐17 expression at baseline. After SVR, IFNγ‐producing Tc cells normalized in both HCV‐NoF and HCV‐cirrhosis. IL‐17 expression decreased significantly (p < 0.01) in both groups following viral eradication but normalized only in HCV‐NoF (Supporting Information S1: Table S7)). In HCV‐cirrhosis, IL‐17 Tc expression remained elevated and comparable to AL‐cirrhosis, suggesting a cirrhosis‐driven IL‐17 phenotype independent of viral clearance (Figure 3B).

At baseline, Th1 and Th17 frequencies were elevated (1.3–1.6‐fold, p < 0.01) and reduced (∼60%, p < 0.01), respectively, in all patient groups (Figure 3A). DAA therapy restored both subsets in HCV‐NoF, but not in HCV‐cirrhosis (Figure 3A).

CD25high‐Tregs (CD4+CD25^high^FoxP3+) were significantly reduced (∼40%–70%) in all patient groups compared with HC. Discussion

In this study, we characterized alterations in Th and Tc subsets across HCV‐infected patients with and without fibrosis and evaluated their evolution after DAA‐induced cure. HCV infection was associated with Tc lymphopenia and selective depletion of naïve Th cells, accompanied by expansions of activated effector Th subsets. These disturbances were markedly exacerbated in cirrhosis, particularly in decompensated disease, where profound losses of naïve and central memory populations prevented compensatory effector expansion. Following SVR, normalization of Tc counts, Tc subset redistribution, and restoration of Th1/Th17 and IL‐17/IFNγ profiles occurred only in patients without fibrosis, whereas those with cirrhosis showed minimal immunological recovery, persistent Th and Tc depletion, and sustained inflammatory cytokine imbalances.

Our study shows that, in HCV‐infected patients, irrespective of cirrhosis, the circulating T‐lymphocyte compartment is profoundly disturbed, with reduced Tc counts driven by the loss of naïve, central memory, and effector memory subsets, together with a selective depletion of the naïve Th population. In patients with cirrhosis, these HCV‐related abnormalities of the Tc compartment are compounded by mechanisms intrinsic to cirrhosis itself, as evidenced by their greater severity in decompensated disease, their close similarity to the alterations observed in AL‐cirrhosis, and their minimal or absent reversibility after antiviral therapy, contrasting with the marked normalization seen in patients without fibrosis. It is therefore likely that, in the absence of fibrosis, the depletion of Tc subpopulations reflects an active antiviral response aimed at promoting viral clearance [15], whereas once cirrhosis is established, Tc depletion predominantly reflects cirrhosis‐associated immune dysfunction [10, 20].

The behaviour of the Th compartment in HCV‐infected patients deserves specific consideration. Across all groups, naïve Th cells were reduced with a reciprocal increase in effector populations, reflecting a typical Th response to viral or chronic inflammatory stimuli. In patients without fibrosis, homeostatic differentiation towards activated effector subsets preserves overall Th counts. However, the naïve Th deficit does not normalize after SVR, a pattern well described in acute and chronic viral infections and attributed to persistent thymic impairment and sustained homeostatic pressure towards memory differentiation [16, 17, 18]. The continued activation of the effector Th compartment after viral clearance may also reflect residual inflammatory signalling and cytokine‐mediated bystander activation [19, 20, 21]. In cirrhosis, this redistribution of Th subsets does not translate into an expanded effector Th response, likely because profound lymphopenia and cirrhosis‐associated immune dysfunction prevent effective compensation and preclude recovery of the Th compartment even after antiviral therapy. At the functional level, HCV‐infected patients showed reduced basal IFNγ expression in Tc lymphocytes compared with HC, which normalized in both HCV groups after viral eradication. This suggests that the altered IFNγ profile in Tc lymphocytes is mainly driven by HCV infection itself, potentially through viral interference with metabolic pathways involved in IFNγ synthesis and secretion [22, 23, 24] and/or induction of inhibitory mechanisms (e.g., PD‐1‐ or ICOS‐mediated signalling) that constrain Tc effector function [25, 26]. Similarly, and in line with previous reports [27], IL‐17 expression in Tc lymphocytes was increased at baseline in all HCV‐infected patients compared with HC. After DAA therapy, IL‐17 expression decreased in both HCV groups but normalized only in HCV‐NoF patients, while remaining elevated in HCV‐cirrhosis at levels comparable to AL‐cirrhosis. Taken together, these findings indicate that HCV infection promotes an IL‐17‐skewed Tc phenotype, whereas cirrhosis sustains this inflammatory imprint even after viral clearance. In inflammatory conditions, IL‐17 promotes recruitment and amplification of innate and adaptive immune responses. In consequence, persistent IL‐17 production by Tc lymphocytes may contribute to ongoing immune activation and Tc dysfunction within cirrhosis‐associated immune dysregulation [12].

In the Th lymphocyte compartment, both groups of HCV‐infected patients showed increased Th1 (IFNγ+) frequencies at baseline compared with HC. After DAA therapy, Th1 levels normalized in HCV‐NoF patients, whereas they remained elevated in HCV‐cirrhosis, at values comparable to AL‐cirrhosis, suggesting that HCV‐related inflammation together with the chronic inflammatory milieu of cirrhosis sustains a persistent Th1 response [25, 28]. Regarding Th17 cells, both HCV groups displayed a reduced proportion of IL‐17+ Th lymphocytes before treatment compared with HC, with values similar to AL‐cirrhosis. After viral eradication, Th17 frequencies increased and normalized only in HCV‐NoF patients, while remaining low in HCV‐cirrhosis. Th17 cells contribute to antiviral immunity by supporting Tc responses and exerting direct antiviral activity, and virus‐specific Th17 lymphocytes have been described in several viral infections (e.g., HIV and CMV) [28, 29, 30]. Thus, the persistently reduced Th17 compartment in cirrhotic patients may reflect impaired antiviral immune competence and may contribute to the limited immune recovery observed after SVR in this population.

An important finding of our study was that DAA therapy increased the frequency of high‐Tregs in HCV‐infected patients irrespective of cirrhosis status, although levels remained below those observed in HC. This pattern may reflect preferential trafficking of circulating high‐Tregs to inflamed tissues, consistent with reports describing an inverse association between circulating high‐Treg frequency and/or suppressive function and the severity of liver damage [31, 32, 33, 34]. Persistent reductions in circulating high‐Tregs may also contribute to the immune dysregulation underlying the increased risk of lymphoproliferative and monoclonal disorders reported in HCV‐infected patients, including after SVR [35, 36, 37, 38].

Overall, our findings indicate that, once cirrhosis is established, whether HCV‐ or alcohol‐related, patients exhibit a profound and persistent disruption of the circulating T‐cell compartment, including combined Tc and Th lymphopenia, marked depletion of naïve and central memory subsets, and a pro‐inflammatory functional skewing. T‐cell depletion and dysfunction in cirrhosis likely reflect a combined failure of immune renewal and peripheral maintenance, primarily driven by impaired thymopoiesis and further amplified by chronic inflammatory activation related to bacterial translocation‐associated immune activation, leading to activation‐induced apoptosis and a shift from naïve‐to‐memory T‐cell phenotypes, together with splenic sequestration in the context of portal hypertension [10, 15].These alterations show minimal or no reversibility after etiological treatment and track with disease severity, being most pronounced in decompensated cirrhosis, consistent with a cirrhosis‐driven and largely fixed immune dysfunction. In contrast, patients with HCV infection without fibrosis display a more selective phenotype, characterized by Tc lymphopenia with preserved overall Th counts through homeostatic effector redistribution, most of which normalizes after viral eradication, although the naïve Th deficit and some features of Th activation persist. Together, these results support the concept that the transition to cirrhosis marks a shift from predominantly virus‐driven, potentially reversible immune perturbations to a sustained immune dysregulation dominated by cirrhosis itself. Although our study focuses on peripheral blood T‐cell populations, these systemic alterations may partly mirror immune remodelling within the hepatic microenvironment, where chronic liver injury has been associated with accumulation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, enrichment of regulatory T cells, and activated or tissue‐resident T‐cell phenotypes [39, 40].

Key strengths of our study include the inclusion of a disease control group assessed after a defined abstinence period, the comprehensive profiling of the circulating T‐lymphocyte compartment rather than focussing exclusively on HCV‐specific T cells, and the functional assessment of T‐cell cytokine production. We acknowledge several limitations. First, the relatively short interval between completion of antiviral therapy and the second immune assessment is likely sufficient to capture the immediate effects of viral clearance on the immune system but may be insufficient to detect changes secondary to structural liver improvement after SVR, which would require longer follow‐up [1]. Nevertheless, the alterations observed in cirrhotic patients closely mirrored those seen in the alcohol‐related cirrhosis group, evaluated after a minimum abstinence period of 3 months, supporting an association with cirrhosis itself rather than with acute immunological effects of HCV eradication. Second, the relatively small sample size limits statistical power to detect differences between compensated and decompensated cirrhosis. Third, no formal method was used to exclude recent alcohol use and therefore, we cannot rule out a potential influence of recent, unreported alcohol consumption on our results.

Our data show that HCV infection is associated with marked perturbations of the circulating T‐cell compartment, which are largely reversible after DAA‐induced viral eradication only in patients without fibrosis. In contrast, once cirrhosis is established T‐cell lymphopenia, depletion of key Th/Tc subsets, and persistent functional skewing become largely fixed and track with disease severity, consistent with cirrhosis‐associated immune dysfunction. These findings support the concept that preventing progression to cirrhosis is critical to preserve immune plasticity and maximize immunological recovery after etiological therapy.
