Breathing ferroelectricity, flat-bands and multiple chiral phonons in van der Waals breathing kagome Nb3Cl8
Authors: Yilun Yu, Fengrui Sui, Beituo Liu, Shuai Yang, Changjun Li, Ruofan Li, Yufan Zheng, Rong Jin, Jiawen Dai, Heng Gao, Ruijuan Qi, Shujing Jia, Wei Ren, Junhao Chu, Fangyu Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Van der Waals layered Nb3X8 (X=Cl, Br, I) are predicted to integrate multiple intriguing physical properties, notably multiferroicity and flat-bands that are highly promising for novel-principle memories and superconductors. However, direct experimental validations such as the proposed breathing ferroelectricity are lacking. Even the electronic band structure concerning flat-bands still remains inconsistent. Here, we systematically explore the structure and electrical/optical properties through a multifaceted study of Nb3Cl8. We demonstrate that, with an intralayer breathing Kagome lattice and an alternating van der Waals gap between adjacent layers, Nb3Cl8 can exhibit breathing ferroelectricity originating from a breathing-in/out distortion of alternate triangles via a Kagome state. More strikingly, our optical characterizations disclose that Nb3Cl8 shows the exciton-like absorption peaks involving flat-bands in thin states but semiconductor-like absorption edge in thick states possibly due to both interlayer interaction and thickness-induced stronger photon absorption, whose energies match our theoretical calculations, and possesses strong optical activity and multiple chiral phonons. Temperature dependencies of phonon modes and flat-bands consistently reveal several temperature-induced electronic-phase or magnetic-related transitions. The coexistence of these interrelated properties in Nb3Cl8 not only offers a platform for exploring fundamental condensed matter physics but also holds great promise for developing multifunctional nanodevices.

Introduction

In the big data era, van der Waals (vdW) layered materials integrating optical, electrical, and magnetic properties are urgently desired for developing new-principle nanodevices with low consumption. The emerging vdW-layered breathing kagome lattices with corner-sharing alternate triangles forming a hexagon show unique electronic band structures, and are predicted to possess multiferroic1–3, chiral phonons4, and flat bands5–9. These suggest the potential to revolutionize the understanding of condensed matter physics, such as the interconnection among geometry10, topology11–13, ferroelectricity14, and magnetism15,16, thus garnering significant attention from researchers across various disciplines. As a group of vdW-layered breathing kagome lattices, Nb3X8 (X=Cl, Br, I) stands out as a captivating example that showcases an opened energy gap with extraordinary flat-bands2,17 due to the alternating triangles with spin-orbit coupling (SOC) and broken-time reversal symmetry9,18,19. As a representation, Nb3Cl8 exhibits better chemical stability17, and can be mechanically thinned to a monolayer because of the advantage of the lowest exfoliation energy in Nb3X820 while keeping the original atomic configuration21,22. This makes it a promising material platform for the study of breathing behavior tied physical properties from monolayer to bulk states23. For instance, Nb3Cl8 was predicted to possess direction-dependent anisotropic optical properties24, and the inherent polarization properties of light signals made it an ideal medium for material polarity detection25,26. However, systematical in-plane polarization-dependent results involving photon absorption or phonon scattering in Nb3X8 are inaccessible. Also, Nb3Cl8 as a Mott insulator has one unpaired electron occupying the 2a1 orbital on each Nb3-trimer27,28, leading to breathing-related magnetism including a temperature-dependent paramagnetic to nonmagnetic transition17, but how temperature influences the electronic band structure of Nb3X8 is pending. Although the angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) has confirmed the existence of Mott gap (EMott) in Nb3Cl8 and demonstrated unique flat-bands in few-layered Nb3Cl810,17,29,30, Nb3I810, and Nb3Br831, it cannot detect the band signal above the Fermi surface, and hardly accurately obtain the whole electronic band structure, including the true optical bandgap (Egap) and flat-bands in this kind of narrow-gap Mott insulator possessing strong intralayer electron correlation32,33. Even combining with first-principles calculations29,33,34, the inconsistency between theory and experiment still exists in Nb3Cl82,17,29,35,36. In particular, to experimentally evidence the breathing-related ferroelectricity in Nb3X82,37 remains a challenge, neither from the comprehensive electrical characterizations nor from the atomic configuration rearrangement regarding polarization switching under the external electric field, although theoretical predictions have argued the distortion of alternating triangles that can trigger a net out-of-plane (OOP) polarization in each layer. These issues hinder the understanding of intrinsic optical and multiferroic properties in this promising lattice2, thus adverse to practical developments of multifunctional nanodevices.

Here, we focus on the structure, optical, and electrical properties in vdW-layered Nb3Cl8. The atomic imaging confirms that Nb3Cl8 has a breathing kagome configuration with corner-sharing alternative triangles in each single layer and an alternating vdW-gap in the bulk state. At ground state, monolayer Nb3Cl8 can present spontaneous polarization, while the bilayer cannot due to its antipolar/antiferroelectric-like ground state. This can be roughly supported by the odd-even layer-dependent second harmonic generation (SHG). Our first-principles calculations further clarify that Nb3Cl8 can transform to a ferroelectric state under external electric field, presenting ferroelectricity. Consistently, our piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM) measurements reveal switchable local electromechanical responses in Nb3Cl8 flakes with different thicknesses down to a bilayer. It can be further validated from functional device structures, such as ferroelectric tunnel junction (FTJ) and ferroelectric field-effect transistor (FeFET), and especially from the atomic-level imaging of the specified polar state by in situ spherical aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy (Cs-STEM), which reflects that the ferroelectric polarization switching likely takes a unique pathway with the breathing distortion of alternative triangles via the intermediate paraelectric phase. Absorption spectra demonstrate that Nb3Cl8 in the bulk state has an optically allowed Egap ≈ 0.5 eV (room temperature), similar to that of classical narrow-gap semiconductors, but presents the atom-like (or exciton) absorption reflecting optical flat-bands when the thickness is reduced significantly, whose energies, including the optically-forbidden EMott (≈ 0.35 eV), are well consistent with our first-principles calculations. Polarized Raman results disclose strong optical activity and multiple chiral phonons in Nb3Cl8, suggesting in-plane (IP) circular-like vibration of Eg modes carrying pseudo-orbit angular moment (PAM)38. Temperature dependencies of flat bands and phonon modes display several noncontinuous critical points below room temperature, largely relevant to the electron-phase or magnetic-related transitions by referring to temperature-dependent resistance and magnetic susceptibility. These properties of unconventional ferroelectricity, multiple flat bands, abundant chiral phonons and even magnetism integrated in Nb3Cl8 suggest its promising applications in new-principle information storage, superconductor and phonon filter.

Breathing ferroelectricity in Nb3Cl8

As proposed above, in the ideal stacking sequence, each Nb3Cl8 monolayer carries a net OOP polarization associated with the breathing kagome distortion, while adjacent layers are arranged in an antipolar manner. Therefore, odd-layer flakes are expected to retain a nonzero net polarization, whereas even-layer flakes have nearly canceled macroscopic polarization and should be regarded as antiferroelectric-like in the zero-field ground state. Our first-principles calculations confirm this picture: the Nb3Cl8 monolayer exhibits a spontaneous polarization of ~0.53 pC/m39 with a switching barrier of ~0.7 eV per Nb atom (Fig. 2a); the bilayer has a zero net polarization in the pristine antipolar state, and can transform to a ferroelectric state with a slightly decreased switching barrier of ~0.6 eV per Nb atom. The ferroelectric bilayer has a polarization of ~0.55 pC/m (Fig. 2b). In bulk state, Nb3Cl8 can also transform to ferroelectric states with a similar transition barrier of ~0.6 eV, and exhibits a polarization of ~0.25 µC/cm2 (Fig. 2b). We then perform piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM) measurements (schematically shown in Fig. 2c) on Nb3Cl8 flakes covering a thickness range from 2L to ~63L, including both odd- and even-layers. As shown in Figs. 2d, e, and S2, each flake presents a ferroelectric-like amplitude hysteresis loop and 180° phase switching simultaneously. Fundamentally, PFM measures a local electromechanical response under the tip-induced OOP electric field. Such a local field may drive the antipolar arrangement in both even-layer and odd-layer Nb3Cl8 into a ferroelectric-like aligned configuration, demonstrating detectable hysteretic PFM loops (Figs. 2d, e, and S2). This can be roughly supported by thickness- (Fig. S3) and bias-dependent (Fig. S4) SHG, and is also consistent with our in situ biasing STEM observations shown in Fig. 2f–h, where the pristine antiferroelectric-like interlayer arrangement is modulated into two OOP (upward or downward) ferroelectric-like states under vertical electric fields. Notice that, by varying the AC (Fig. S5 and Supplementary Note S1) and DC (Fig. S6 and Supplementary Note S1) voltages, respectively, some extrinsic effects generating artificial ferroelectric-like PFM signals are excluded, such as the electrostatic interaction between the sample and probe, charge injection, and so on.

Furthermore, we fabricate a ~20 nm Nb3Cl8-based FTJ device (“Methods”), in which two asymmetric electrodes sandwich the Nb3Cl8 ferroelectric tunnel barrier, as schematically shown as insets in Fig. 2i. When the voltages sweep from negative to positive, the device presents an asymmetric current–voltage loop due to the polarization switching induced effective tunneling barrier height difference in Nb3Cl8, demonstrating two (high and low) resistance states (HRS and LRS) with a memory window of at least three orders of magnitude (Figs. 2i and S7). When the voltage range changes (e.g., with a step of increase/decrease), the current–voltage loop vertically (downward/upward) shifts. Consistently, the Nb3Cl8-based FeFET (Fig. 2j) shows distinguishable gate voltage-tuned hysteresis loops, which will vanish when the gate voltage decreases below the coercive field. Both functional devices show reversible and nonvolatile electrical modulation that is consistent with switchable OOP polar states in Nb3Cl8, i.e., the breathing-related ferroelectricity.

As illustrated in Fig. 1c, the polarization reversal in each layer of Nb3Cl8 can take place via the paraelectric state. We then try to snapshot this special state during the polarization switching via electron-beam illumination in the Cs-STEM system, as widely employed to trigger the polarization reversal in many ferroelectric materials, such as the hexagonal YMnO340 and newly emerging wurtzite ferroelectrics41 as well as recently-reported vdW γ-InSe42,43 and GeSe44. Interestingly, the proposed paraelectric state (Fig. S8a) in Nb3Cl8 can be clearly observed arising from the pristine antiferroelectric state (Fig. S8b) under the electron-beam illumination. It directly exhibits the Nb atoms moving in-plane (Fig. S8c), i.e., from the breathing-out/in Nb-Nb distance of ~2.41 Å/3.47 Å to the average of ~2.94 Å (Fig. S8d), resulting in the distorted NbCl6 octahedra evolving into symmetric NbCl6 ones together with the modulation of the vdW-gap from alternative in pristine state to uniform (~2.59 Å) in paraelectric state (Fig. S8c, e). This implies that (i) the switching between breathing-in and breathing-out patterns (i.e., polarization reversal) can occur via the proposed paraelectric state in Nb3Cl837; and (ii) the paraelectric state corresponding to a traditional (not breathing) kagome lattice with congruent Nb-Nb distances (intratrimer and intertrimer) presents an OOP spatial compression ratio of ~10% along the c direction. Notice that the electron-beam illumination-induced electric field is omnidirectional at STEM mode40. For Nb3Cl8, the adjacent layers will change oppositely due to the antipolar atomic arrangement, so only the paraelectric state can be successfully modulated and observed under electron-beam illumination.

Electronic band structures involving optical bandgap and flat bands

As well accepted17,29,36,45, the electronic structure of Nb3Cl8 is dominated by the Nb 4d orbital, with one unpaired electron occupying the 2a1 orbital on each Nb-trimer (Fig. S9), nominally leading to a half-filled metallic band constructed by a molecular orbital that is spatially distributed over the three Nb atoms in each Nb-trimer46. The strong electronic correlations convert this molecular orbital into a cluster Mott insulator, where electrons are delocalized inside the Nb-trimer without long-range transport. Recent calculations and ARPES results also suggest that Nb3Cl8 is a dimerized Mott-insulator rather than band-insulator29,31. Then, we investigate the electronic structure of Nb3Cl8 by transmission spectroscopy covering a wide waveband from 0.2 eV to 3 eV. As shown in Fig. 3a, thick Nb3Cl8 obviously shows a steep absorption edge, very similar to that of conventional narrow-gap semiconductors. The Tauc-plot procedure47,48 gives an optical gap of Egap ≈ 0.5 eV (Fig. 3a, inset), excluding the metallic-like property of Nb3Cl8. When the flakes are thinned significantly, the absorption edge begins to blue-shift, and multiple exciton-like absorption peaks appear above the absorption edge (Fig. 3a, inset; marked by Pi, i = 1, 2, 3 …; also see Fig. S10). Figure 3b summarizes the peak energy as a function of thickness covering two orders of magnitude. The Egap increases by ~100 meV as the thickness decreases from bulk-like to ~3 μm, from which an abrupt jump with a gap of ΔEg ≈ 200 meV can be observed due to the feature transiting from absorption edge to peaks. Then, all peaks (P1–P5) become more evident but with slightly different thickness-dependence, i.e., P1 and P2 almost keeping constant while P3–P5 showing a negative relationship. For the flake with a representative thickness of 1 μm, the peaks are at about 0.80 eV (half width = 0.31 eV), 1.15 eV (0.21 eV), 1.50 eV (0.14 eV), 1.65 eV (0.28 eV), and 1.90 eV (0.47 eV), as well as the higher 2.75 eV (0.67 eV) (also see Fig. S11). Considering the large illumination spot size of the focused incident light, which can cover the entire Brillouin zone, these sharp exciton-like absorption peaks physically define the separated narrow levels, allowing for the resonance absorption of incident photons, characterizing the weakly dispersing bands and featuring flat bands10,48. Temperature-dependent absorption spectra of the ~1 μm flake suggest the robustness of flat bands. As shown in Fig. 3c, the flat bands are always observable in the entire temperature range and show different temperature-dependent evolutions with several critical temperatures. For instance, (i) the lowest P1 and closest P2 are almost independent of temperature, either below or above ~115 K, at which a non-ignorable transition can be observed; and (ii) the high P3–P5 present a slight decrease first and then increase at the inflexion points of ~190 K for P3/P4 and possibly of ~270 K for P5. These specific temperature points may reflect temperature-induced multiple-phase modulations of carriers, as will also be observed and discussed below in other characterizations. We should point out that Gao et al. observed several separated energy levels in Nb3Cl8 via ARPES, combining with theoretical predictions29, by which they determined the highest valence band at ~0.7 eV below the Fermi level and assumed Egap ≈ 1.15 eV (exactly close to our P2 but not P1 energy), because no information of conduction band or subsequently Egap could be obtained via ARPES.

Thus, we calculate the electronic band structure of Nb3Cl8 with different thicknesses (from 1L to 4L, and bulk states) under different physical conditions (“Methods”). The calculations present that (i) there is a narrow electronic gap only by taking into account the spin-polarization, otherwise demonstrating a Fermi-level-crossed state (Fig. S12); (ii) the narrow gap exists within the whole thickness limit, and the overall frontier band structure of the bulk is similar to that of the monolayer (Fig. S13), similar to the reports33,36; and (iii) additional consideration of SOC has no evident change upon the electronic-band structure (Fig. S14). These indicate a weak thickness dependence of the dispersing property of the band structure, same as the case in Nb3I810. As representatively shown in Fig. 3d, the spin-resolved band structures (with or without considering the SOC effect) show multiple flat bands with the Fermi level sitting between the spin-split 2a1 orbitals with a spin-resolved gap of EMott ≈ 0.35 eV (shadowed area). The minimums of the unfilled (or conduction) bands are ~0.09 eV (M point with spin down), 0.52 eV (K point, spin up), 0.94 eV (K point, spin down) and 1.33 eV (M point, spin up), and the maximums of the filled (or valence) bands are about −0.07 eV (Γ point, spin up), −1.01 eV (M point, spin down), −1.16 eV (M point, spin up) and −1.71 eV (Γ point, spin down, not shown) for bulk Nb3Cl8. Subsequently, the spin-split 2a1 orbital closest to the Fermi level points to an optically-forbidden gap (EMott ≈ 0.35 eV). However, the spin up 2a1 can transit to the same spin 2e state with an optically-allowed gap of ~0.59 eV (corresponding to Γ-K transition with spin up; tentatively marked by P1 for simplicity), and the spin down 1e can transit to the same spin 2a1 state with an optically-allowed gap of ~1.11 eV (corresponding to M-M transition with spin down; marked by P2). Notice that the Nb3Cl8 monolayer shows slightly enhanced allowed gaps of 0.65 eV (spin-up) and 1.18 eV (spin-down), although the interlayer interaction was assumed to be weak and ignorable. Similarly, the other optically allowed transitions can be calculated in energetic sequence as ~1.39 eV (spin up; marked by P3), 1.68 eV (spin up; marked by P4), 1.96 eV (spin down; marked by P5) and potentially 2.49 eV (spin up; marked by P6). Evidently, these theoretical values marked by Pi (Fig. 3d) together with their widths are well consistent with our experimental results (Fig. 3b, c).

So, combining transmission spectroscopy with independent theoretical calculations, we demonstrate that Nb3Cl8 is a Mott insulator with a theoretical (optically inactive) gap of ~0.35 eV and an optically allowed gap of ~0.6 eV, as well as multiple optical flat-bands. The thickness-independent calculation results of the spin-resolved band structure, contrast to the experimental observation of a seeming thickness-induced band to Mott-insulating transition31, may suggest that the strong photon absorption obscures the narrow and separative optical transitions in thicker flakes, while the interlayer coupling and Coulomb interactions31 cannot be ignored because the absorption edge has evident blue-shifts as the thickness remarkably decreases. It is necessary to point out that for thin flakes (thickness less than 3 μm) there is a non-ignorable absorption peak at ~0.35 eV (see the blue curve in Fig. 3a), whose origination needs further exploration.

We then obtain the helicity-resolved CPR spectra of Nb3Cl8 with right-handed (σ+) or left-handed (σ−) polarized excitation/detection under four typical configurations (achiral σ+/σ+ and σ−/σ−, and chiral σ+/σ− and σ−/σ+; see “Methods”). As shown in Figs. 4b-ii, iii, and S16a, the two kinds of Raman modes exhibit distinct circular polarization dependencies. Namely, the Eg and A1g modes can only be observed under chiral and achiral configurations, respectively. Notice that under both configurations the σ+ and σ− excitations give the same CPR spectra. Thus, it evidences that the Eg modes are optically active to reverse the helicity of incident photons. Figure 4d displays the angle-dependent intensity distributions of the two CPR modes in polar coordinates. Sharply contrast to LPR results in Fig. 4c, while the A1g modes still keep the horizontally splayed shape, the Eg modes are changed from circle-shape to a vertically splayed shape, firmly confirming the exact reversal of the helicity of incident photons, pointing to the chiral phonon behavior of Eg modes. Figure S17 shows static representations of the calculated eigenvectors of the five chiral Eg phonon modes, and Movie S1 gives the corresponding −i and +i atomic motions. Figure S18 shows static representations of the calculated eigenvectors of the four achiral A1g phonon modes, with Movie S2 for the corresponding atomic motions. Furthermore, temperature-dependent CPR spectra are recorded from ~80 to 550 K (Fig. S16b). All modes show continuously red-shift without disappearance or appearance of new modes, suggesting no evident temperature-dependent structure transition in Nb3Cl8. However, the temperature-dependence of the intensity of all modes shows abnormal noncontinuous transition points, in particular the A1g modes, typically at about 115, 190, and 270 K (Fig. 4e). Notice that there were reports pointing to a structure phase transition46 at ~100 K (close to our ~115 K) between α-Nb3Cl8 (2L as a unit cell) and β-Nb3Cl8 (6L as a unit cell)23, but the results of Nb3Cl8 polycrystalline powders characterized by temperature-dependent XRD and magnetic measurements did not support this argument23. It is worth mentioning that the critical transition points observed in Fig. 4e are consistent with previous absorption results in Fig. 3c. So, it can be then inferred that the vdW-layered Mott insulator Nb3Cl8 is stable in a rather wide temperature range, but has a temperature-dependent photon/carrier-phonon coupling due to temperature-induced electronic state transitions, e.g., the spin reversal of the unpaired single electron carrying a local magnetic moment of S = 1/2 in Nb-trimer clusters of Nb3Cl817,29. Temperature-dependent magnetic susceptibility χ measurement on Nb3Cl8 single crystal supports this expectation. As widely reported17, an evident χ–T loop for Nb3Cl8 can be observed during the temperature sweeping. Both the IP and OOP χ–T loops are similar, except for enhanced χ-levels of the latter. The transition points at ~90 K (cooling), and ~120 K (heating), pointing to the dramatic decrease of χ-value can be observed, which has been attributed to magnetic phase transition between paramagnetism and nonmagnetism during the heating/cooling process23,45. This argues that the critical temperature of ~115 K observed in temperature-dependent Raman and absorption measurements may correspond to the spin state modulation of the unpaired single electron in Nb-trimers, although deeper investigations of magnetism in Nb3Cl8 are urgent. Additionally, since our temperature-dependent XRD characterizations do not show any obvious structure-related phase transition even at ~93 K (Fig. S19), the other critical temperatures like 190 and 270 K may be related to the photon-carrier or phonon-carrier interactions, but without structural or magnetic transitions of Nb3Cl8 (e.g., see the temperature-dependent resistance in Fig. S20).

Discussion

In summary, the vdW-layered Nb3Cl8 has a breathing kagome lattice with an alternative vdW gap among adjacent layers. It exhibits breathing-distortion-induced polar order with a clear layer-parity dependence. In the zero-field ground state, odd-layer flakes retain a nonzero net OOP spontaneous polarization, whereas even-layer flakes form an antipolar/antiferroelectric-like arrangement with nearly canceled macroscopic polarization. Under external electric fields, e.g., the PFM tip-induced local field, the antipolar configuration can be driven into a ferroelectric-like aligned state, giving rise to switchable local electromechanical responses. This establishes Nb3Cl8 as a breathing-kagome vdW system with coupled ferroelectric/ antiferroelectric-like polar order. The intrinsic absorption spectroscopy directly reveals the electronic band structure, including the optical Egap and flat bands. Thick Nb3Cl8 shows the optical property of conventional narrow-gap semiconductors exhibiting a steep absorption edge, which gives an optically allowed Egap of ~0.5 eV (room temperature). The flat bands can only be observed in significantly thinned flakes, e.g., less than ~3 μm, due to the thickness-induced strong absorption at high-energy side, of which the energies are well consistent with our calculated spin-resolved electronic band structures. The circularly polarized Raman spectroscopy demonstrates the IP optical activity and chiral phonons, suggesting the existence of PAM of these phonons, which can, on the one hand, break the intra/intervalley optically-forbidden transition, e.g., allowing the spin-resolved absorption transition of EMott ≈ 0.35 eV (chiral phonons carrying PAM breaking the spin-forbidden EMott), and also influences the transport and recombination of spin-resolved carriers, as reported elsewhere26,51–54, and on the other hand may induce the diamagnetism due to the orbit-like movement of these chiral phonons55,56. It is interesting to point out that the temperature has a significant effect on the modulations of the electronic band structure and, in particular, the spin state of the unpaired single electron in each Nb-trimer, e.g., at ~115 K, which can be confirmed by temperature-dependent chiral phonons and flat bands as well as magnetism.

Notice that, although previous studies have established important ingredients in Nb3Cl8 or other Nb3X8 compounds, including cluster-Mott physics, flat electronic bands observed by ARPES or predicted by theory, and theoretically proposed breathing-distortion-induced ferroelectricity. However, these ingredients are largely addressed separately. No experimental framework that connects the breathing kagome lattice to switchable polar response, optically allowed flat-band-related transitions, and chiral lattice vibration dynamics in the same Nb3Cl8 system is accessible. The purpose of the present work is therefore aiming to experimentally confirm and validate their coexistence in a single vdW breathing-kagome material platform.

Methods

Sample preparation

The Nb3Cl8 single crystal is provided by Nanjing MKNANO Tech. Co. Ltd. For optical (Raman and transmission) and PFM characterizations, Nb3Cl8 flakes with different thicknesses from bulk-like to 2L are prepared via mechanical exfoliation.

Device preparation and characterization

FTJ device preparation

The two-terminal Au/Nb3Cl8/Graphene/Au vertically stacked devices are fabricated by mechanical exfoliation and all-dry transfer stacking method, with the Au electrodes defining and depositing on the silicon wafer by using photolithography techniques.

FeFET device preparation

We used mechanical exfoliation to form Nb3Cl8-based devices on the degenerately doped Si substrate with a 50 nm SiO2 layer. Source and drain electrodes were patterned by photolithography. Cr (5 nm) and Au (35 nm) were then deposited by thermal evaporation.

Electrical characterizations of Nb3Cl8-based FTJ and FeFET devices are performed on a Keithley 4200A-SCS semiconductor parameter analyzer combined with a vacuumed probe station.

Structural characterization

We determined the elemental composition by the Ultim Extreme energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy coupled on a Zeiss Gemini 450 scanning electron microscope, and collected the X-ray diffraction patterns with a Bruker D8 Discover diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm). Cross-sectional TEM samples were prepared with a Helios G4 UX focused-ion-beam system. Ga-ion accelerating voltages from 2 to 30 kV were used. A JEOL Grand ARM300 microscope was used for atomic-resolution HAADF-STEM images. We recorded the elemental maps with two 158 mm2 silicon-drift detectors. A PicoFemto double-tilt holder was employed for the in situ biasing. Simulated HAADF-STEM images were generated with MacTampas.

PFM characterization

An Asylum Research Cypher scanning probe microscope was used for PFM measurements with the dual-amplitude resonance-tracking mode. Pt-coated AC240TM silicon cantilevers were used. Their nominal spring constant was 2.0 N/m. The contact-resonance frequency was approximately 220 kHz. The applied AC voltage ranged from 0.2 to 0.8 V. The DC sweep voltage ranged from 3 to 9 V. Each cantilever was 240 μm long and 40 μm wide. The nominal tip radius was 28 nm, and the nominal tip height was 14 μm.

Optical characterization

Raman spectra are obtained on a micro-Raman system with a back-scattering configuration (Renishaw inVia Reflex, UK) using a 532 nm laser as the excitation source. To avoid extrinsic effects, including excitation-induced heating on samples, the laser power density is set at ~102 W/cm2. Both excitation and scattered optical paths are equipped with linear polarizers. Controlling both polarizers can provide specified LPR configurations: parallel (//) and cross (⊥). The sample holder is in-plane rotatable. For circularly polarized Raman measurements, a linear polarizer followed by a quarter-wave plate is placed in the incident path to achieve right (σ+) or left (σ−) circular polarization light, while another set of optical elements (including a broadband quarter-wave plate, a broadband half-wave plate and a broadband linear polarizer) is placed in the scattering path. The σ+ (or σ−) circular polarization of the incident light is confirmed at the sample position. The σ−/σ+ polarized configurations are employed to check the helicity of scattering photons.

A Bruker Vertex 80 v Fourier-transform infrared spectrometer was employed for transmission measurement in the range of 0.4–15 μm. Different sources, beamsplitters and detectors were selected for different spectral regions. A high-vacuum cryogenic holder (RDK-101J, Sumitomo Heavy Industries) was used for measurements from 4 K to room temperature.

SHG measurements are performed using an infrared laser as the fundamental excitation. The SHG signal is collected in reflection geometry. The incident laser power is varied to confirm the intrinsic SHG signal. Angle-resolved SHG measurements are carried out by changing the incident polarization angle or rotating the sample. To explore the potential polarization switching in Nb3Cl8 flakes, the bias-dependent SHG signals are recorded on the vertical Nb3Cl8-based device with the lithography-fabricated transparent top electrode (indium tin oxide/ITO).

Computational details

First-principles calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)57,58. The (device studio)59 projector augmented wave (DS-PAW)60 method was employed to describe the electron-ion interaction, and the exchange-correlation functional was treated within the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) generalized gradient approximation (GGA)61,62. The plane-wave kinetic-energy cutoff was set to 700 eV. The atomic structures were fully relaxed until the residual force on each atom was less than 1.0 × 10−3 eV/Å. The convergence criterion for the self-consistent electronic iteration was set to 1.0 × 10−6 eV, while a tighter total-energy convergence criterion of 1.0 × 10−8 eV was used for single-point energy calculations. The SOC was included in the electronic band-structure calculations to make a comparison.

To systematically investigate thickness-dependent properties, monolayer/1L, bilayer/2L, trilayer/3L, tetralayer/4L, and bulk Nb3Cl8 were considered. For the 2D structures, a vacuum layer of 25 Å was introduced along the OOP direction to avoid artificial interactions between periodic images. The Monkhorst–Pack k-point meshes used for structural relaxation were 9 × 9 × 1 for the 2D systems and 8 × 8 × 6 for the bulk structures. For single-point calculations based on the optimized structures, denser k-point meshes of 12 × 12 × 1 and 12 × 12 × 10 were used for the 2D and bulk systems, respectively. vdW interactions were included in all calculations, using the Tkatchenko–Scheffler method with iterative Hirshfeld partitioning63–65.

To account for the strong electronic correlation of the Nb d orbitals, the GGA+U method66 was also employed to make a comparison.

The ferroelectric switching pathway was determined using the solid-state nudged elastic band (SSNEB) method67,68. Phonon frequencies and eigenvectors were calculated using the finite-displacement method as implemented in the VASP and PHONOPY69,70, with a 2 × 2 × 1 supercell adopted for the phonon calculations. Chiral phonon properties were further analyzed based on the calculated phonon eigenvectors. Crystal-structure models and static phonon-eigenvector illustrations were prepared using VESTA 371. The atomic-displacement animations in Movies S1 and S2 were generated from the calculated phonon eigenvectors.
