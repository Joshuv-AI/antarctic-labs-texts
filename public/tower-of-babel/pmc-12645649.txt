Mixed-methods psychosocial autopsy study of suicide in young and middle-aged individuals in the Netherlands
Authors: Elias Balt, Saskia Mérelle, Stefan Vrinzen, Merijn Eikelenboom, Amra Žerić, Dewi Wiggers, Renske Gilissen, Daan Creemers, Arne Popma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSociodemographic characteristics of suicide decedents are recorded in the Netherlands, through which we know who died by suicide, but not why. In this mixed-methods, informant-based psychosocial autopsy, we aimed to identify psychosocial characteristics and precipitating factors of suicide in young (aged 10–30) and middle-aged (40–70 years old) women and men and investigate differences on a group level.MethodsData were collected through a structured questionnaire and semistructured interviews with bereaved proxy informants. The questionnaire detailed sociodemographic and psychosocial characteristics of the suicide decedent. We conducted bivariate analyses to explore differences between women and men. Additionally, we employed the constant comparative method to investigate the perceptions of respondents about key contributing factors and proximal stressors in the months preceding the suicide, as expressed in the interviews. The findings from questionnaires detailing 428 suicide cases were integrated with themes reported in 64 interviews using statistics-by-theme.ResultsWe identified differences in psychosocial characteristics, proximal stressors and experiences with healthcare of young and middle-aged women and men, including a high prevalence of out-of-the-blue suicide in young men and sexual abuse in young women. Notably, middle-aged men showed no significantly higher rates for any of the considered risk factors in our comparisons. Furthermore, respondents reported the lowest number of perceived contributing factors in young men. Thematic patterns in the interviews concerning young decedents included disconnectedness, a silent struggle and a loss of hope and perspective. In middle-aged decedents, two patterns described a long-term struggle with episodic depression, and conversely a brief suicidal process following an event of loss. In middle-aged women, physical problems and pain were salient topics.ConclusionsMixed-methods psychosocial autopsy produces a unique empirical perspective on the diverse needs of individuals who died by suicide. We discuss practice implications and possible avenues for future research.Keywords: Mental Health, Preventive Psychiatry, Public Health

Introduction

With an annual global mortality rate of over 700 000 people, suicide is a major public health concern. Multilevel prevention may reduce the number of suicides.1 This requires knowledge of risk factors for suicide, insight into the suicidal process of individuals at risk, understanding their needs for care and support, and the collaborative efforts of stakeholders to translate scientific evidence into standards for treatment and prevention of suicidal thoughts and behaviours.1 Stakeholders can be an individual, group or organisation with an interest in suicide prevention, and the potential to influence our actions and aims in this regard.2 To stimulate multilevel prevention in the Netherlands, a National Agenda detailing a 5-year strategy (2021–2025) for suicide prevention has been formulated.3 This agenda fosters a collaborative approach with active participation of local and national stakeholders (ie, ProRail, Suicide Bereavement Care Coalition, mental healthcare institutions), to prevent suicide together through various programmes, educational material and interventions. Two demographic groups received special interest for prevention efforts in the National Agenda: youth and young adults (aged 10–30) and middle-aged individuals (aged 40–70). These age categories align with the 10-year strata used by Statistics Netherlands.

Approximately 300 people aged 10–30 years old die by suicide every year in the Netherlands. These are 2.7 per 100 000 adolescents aged 10–19 and 9.7 per 100 000 young adults aged 20–29.4 Suicide in young people has increased over the last decade in the Netherlands,5 as well as in the USA6 and in the UK.7 However, it is for a great part unclear what underlies the increase. Marcotte and Hansen8 propose that for young people aged under 25, an increase in the prevalence of depression can explain the increase in suicide mortality during the 2010s in the USA. In another study, Padmanathan et al9 found that income inequality is an important contributing factor for increasing suicide rates in young people in high-income countries. Experts additionally suggest that suicide rates in young people may be affected by new media screen time and social media use.10 Others remark on the increasing academic pressure,11 and even distress about global issues such as climate change,12 13 but this requires more empirical evidence.

A second priority group entails people of middle age. Suicide rates are high in this age range, with 14.6 suicides per 100 000 individuals in this age group compared with 10.6 per 100 000 in the overall population,5 especially in men. Four in every 10 suicides in the Netherlands occur in middle-aged men.4 Male-specific risk factors may contribute to this disparity.14 Additionally, men more often use lethal means to attempt suicide, compared with women.15 Studies reported relationship breakdowns, work-related and finance-related stress and substance abuse as risk factors that disproportionately affected men.16,18 However, the suicidal process of middle-aged men remains largely obscured, particularly the step from suicidal thoughts to action. Importantly, although men account for approximately 75% of suicide victims worldwide,19 they represented 46% of the average study population in a sample of 158 studies into suicidal thoughts and behaviours.20 Men less often engage in deliberate self-harm and suicide attempts. Taken together, this makes it difficult for researchers to study their suicidal processes. Additionally, there has been sparse research into suicide in middle-aged women, which stresses the need to better understand suicide in this age group.

Informant-based psychosocial autopsy, which involves a retrospective study of suicide deaths by obtaining information from bereaved individuals, is an internationally acclaimed method to record psychosocial and psychological characteristics of suicide decedents.21,26 Case–control psychological autopsies provide robust evidence concerning risk factors for suicide.21 23 27 28 Moreover, according to Pouliot and Leo,29 psychosocial autopsy can reveal factors contributing to suicide that epidemiological studies cannot, including a qualitative reconstruction of precipitating factors. Consequently, it may be one of the few ways to better understand why suicide rates are increasing in young people, elucidate the suicidal process in middle-aged men and delineate their needs for prevention and care. Our study aimed to investigate psychosocial and precipitating factors of suicides in the two previously identified risk groups, using data obtained in a mixed-methods psychosocial autopsy study.

Materials and methods

Study population and procedures

The current study concerns a mixed-methods observational psychosocial autopsy, using a structured questionnaire and a semistructured in-depth interview. The study population consisted of individuals living in the Netherlands who lost someone to suicide between 2020 and 2023. We included recent suicides and focused on key indicators to minimise the risk of recall bias. Inclusion criteria were that a person was a relative (eg, parent, child, partner), friend or other relation (eg, healthcare professional) of the decedent, was aged over 16 and spoke Dutch.

We recruited individuals through a brochure for bereaved families used by the Dutch national police and Victim Support, through a consortium of organisations for the interests of the bereaved, and via social media channels of 113 Suicide Prevention. Through a secured link, participants could read the information letter and start the online questionnaire. Questionnaires were completed in Qualtrics.

Participants could opt in for a follow-up interview. An interview coordinator called the informant and sent an information letter and the informed consent form for the interview. At least 1 week after the initial call, and on signing the informed consent form, the interview coordinator called back the respondent to proceed with participation. Individuals bereaved by suicide are at an increased risk of adverse mental health effects and suicidal behaviours.30 Respondents were, therefore, screened using the Suicidal Ideation Attribute Scale (SIDAS).31 A score of ≥21 was defined as an exclusion criterion for the interviews.

Participants who were not selected for an interview (eg, due to a high SIDAS score or for other reasons) received a personal explanation from the interview coordinator. When a high SIDAS was recorded or the coordinator otherwise perceived a need, the participants were advised to contact their general practitioner (GP), or call the 113 Suicide Prevention helpline, and were provided with further information about available services. This procedure was reviewed and approved by the ethics committee and was designed to safeguard participants while ensuring they had access to appropriate information and support throughout the study.

Representativeness of the study population

Online supplemental table S1 presents the study sample to illustrate the representativeness of the psychosocial autopsy sample of 217 youth and young adults and 211 middle-aged individuals compared with a sample of 879 youths and young adults and 2983 middle-aged individuals who died by suicide between 2021 and 2023 in the Netherlands as registered by Statistics Netherlands. Our sample was an adequate representation in terms of gender, education level, geographical dispersion, household composition and suicide method, but contained less people with a migration background in both youth and young adults and middle-aged people. Additionally, among middle-aged people, we observed that individuals with marital status ‘unmarried’ or ‘never married’ were underrepresented, and middle-aged people who were divorced were overrepresented, compared with national statistics data. This is presumably because our study format relies on proxy informants whereby partners are an important participant group. There were fewer cases who had a household situation ‘other’, which included hospitalisation, in both young and middle-aged cases.

Procedures

A questionnaire was developed, informed by an analysis of stakeholder perceptions and needs32 and a reasonable body of evidence for risk factors for suicide, including earlier psychological autopsy studies22,26 and a cross-sectional health monitor of the Dutch community health services.33 The questionnaire aimed to identify sociodemographic and psychosocial characteristics of the decedents. A range of questions featured the victim’s social relations, work, financial problems, education, migration background, physical health, mental health, religious background, gender identity, sexual orientation, digital media use, history of suicidal behaviours, suicide-related communication and care use. The questionnaire has been added as a supplement to this manuscript (online supplemental additional file 2).

Interviews were face-to-face and lasted approximately one to one-and-a-half hours. The interview aimed to identify precipitating factors and gain insight into the suicidal process. It was based on the earlier psychological autopsy studies of de Jaegere et al,28 Mérelle et al,25 and Balt et al.34 The instrument first invited a narrative account from the bereaved participant about key contributing factors for the suicide, after which events and behaviours leading up to the suicide were reconstructed. We recorded the subject’s social relations, behaviours, suicide-related communications and preparation of the suicide in the months preceding their death. The complete translated interview has been added as a supplement to this manuscript (online supplemental additional file 3 file 3). Interviewers were (retired) practitioners and psychologists with previous experiences in psychosocial autopsy studies or counselling in the helpline of 113 Suicide Prevention. Interviewers were trained in using the semistructured interview instrument by a trained expert in the psychosocial autopsy and a licensed clinical psychologist. We organised monthly meetings with the interview team, to discuss their experiences and reflect on the process.

Data analysis

Raw data, in the form of questionnaires and interview transcripts, were stored on a local server of our research site. The questionnaire data were analysed in R. Demographic characteristics (age, sex, migration background, region of the suicide, marital status and education level) of the study population were first compared with national registries of suicide decedents5 to establish the representativeness of the study sample. Prevalence rates, and corresponding confidence intervals, of psychosocial characteristics were computed separately for youth and young adult men and women (aged 10–30 years old), and middle-aged men and women (aged 40–70 years old). The 13 individuals (all aged 10–30) who were non-binary or had another gender identity were included in the overall proportions.

If a participant responded ‘no’ or ‘unknown’ to a preceding question, causing the respective follow-up question to be skipped, this was interpreted as a ‘no’ and included in the analysis. When a participant answered ‘unknown’ to the respective question itself, it was treated as a missing value and excluded from the prevalence analysis. Within age groups (youth and young adults, middle-aged), differences in the prevalence of psychosocial characteristics between men and women were investigated using χ² tests. The 13 people who were non-binary or had another gender identity were excluded from the χ² tests, as the group was too small to allow for meaningful statistical comparison. We did not formulate hypotheses for the χ² tests a priori because these tests were explorative in nature and mainly aimed at providing the frame of reference for our qualitative findings. To control for the family-wise error rate, reducing the risk of false positives, we employed the Holm-Bonferroni correction for the probability statistic. The Holm-Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons within each age group (youth and young adults, middle-aged) and domain (public health, mental health) separately. When <5 cases were registered, we did not present the data to prevent identification. In these instances, we also did not test for differences.

The interviews were transcribed verbatim. The interview transcripts were first deductively coded in collaboration with a 70b instruct LLAMA 3.1 Large language model (LLM),35 which entailed that a language model labelled fragments by means of predefined codes with corresponding definitions. The experts behind the widely acclaimed COREQ (consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research) are exploring acceptable and feasible use of LLMs in research.36 Our work pioneers alongside these developments. The LLM’s deductive coding was reviewed by two researchers independently (EB, SV), and further inductively coded in accordance with scientific standards for thematic qualitative analyses.37 The coding team met biweekly to reflect on their coding process, discuss emerging themes in the data and propose changes to the code list. A third researcher (SM) was involved to achieve consensus in the case of discord. The Constant Comparative Method38 was employed to investigate key themes in the different demographic subgroups. We aimed to include twelve cases for each of the demographic subgroups to achieve saturation.37 Findings from the questionnaire were integrated with the interview themes using the joint display of statistics-by-themes.39 We present the most important indicators together with themes from qualitative analyses that provide context to these statistics, along six topics in the aforementioned public and mental health domains (1) academic-related, work-related and socioeconomic distress; (2) substance use, addiction and physical problems; social relations, bullying and violence; (4) talking about suicide, taboo and help seeking; (5) mental health problems and (6) experiences and perceptions of healthcare use. Quotes are used to support and illustrate the findings. Quotes were translated from Dutch by a researcher with a Cambridge certificate in Advanced English at C2 level.

Patient and public involvement

Translating the findings from the psychosocial autopsy to recommendations for prevention was a key aim of this study, as informed by an analysis of stakeholder perceptions.32 Therefore, an independent expert committee consisting of fifteen experts with a multidisciplinary background, including persons with lived experience, was formed. An overview of the involved experts and their affiliation can be found in online supplemental additional file 4. The committee’s goals were to reflect on the research processes, interpret the outcomes and discuss the implications of the findings. We organised biannual meetings, with two additional digital meetings to review the findings of the study and discuss practice implications, in which stakeholders reflected on the outcomes in the context of their knowledge and experience. In this manuscript, we translated their recommendations into practice implications for suicide prevention.

Results

The results detail 428 individuals who died by suicide and include young women (n=85), young men (n=119), young people with non-binary or other gender identities (n=13), middle-aged women (n=51) and middle-aged men (n=160). The respondents were parents (n=103), friends (n=93), other family such as brothers and sisters (n=85), partners (n=75), children (n=43) or other relations (n=29), who mostly included ex-partners and/or family by-law. In addition, 64 in-depth interviews were analysed, namely young women (n=11), young men (n=15), young people with non-binary or other gender identities (n=2), middle-aged women (n=11) and middle-aged men (n=25).

Academic-related, work-related and socioeconomic distress

We identified no significant differences between men and women across academic or work-related factors. Notably, 71% of young women experienced school pressure, and perfectionism and academic pressure were salient topics in the interviews of young females. However, the perceived impact of broader school problems for the suicide (including dropping out, and problems with teachers and other students) was prominent in both young women (57%) and young men (41%).

Financial problems were frequent in middle-aged persons (ie, 20% had financial debts). The interviews showed a recurring theme describing the perceived professional and personal inadequacy, failure and shame in middle-aged men, which typically coincided with work problems.

Partner: He felt like he belonged [at work]. But then someone new came, and this is often the thing with interim professionals; they must leave their mark, because otherwise they never amounted anything. So, everything had to change, and [name] was no longer any good. And he thought: how can it be that I always delivered good work here, and they were happy with me, and suddenly nothing’s alright anymore and they are being so nasty to me. He really struggled with that.

Substance use, addiction and physical problems

According to the respondents, approximately 48% of the young decedents used soft drugs in the 6 months preceding the suicide, which included marihuana, hashish, laughing gas, shrooms or mild sedatives, and one fourth (25%) had used hard drugs, like ecstasy, methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA), cocaine, amphetamines, lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD), gamma-hydroxybutyric acid (GHB) or heroin. There were no significant differences in substance use or addiction between deceased young men and women. The interviews revealed that a subgroup of young people had ordered illegal substances online which included wrongfully acquired medication, designer drugs and substances that were sold as ‘scientific medication’, but were really modified illegal substances. Substance misuse among young decedents could be connected to difficulties they experienced in regulating their emotions. Many used substances to numb their feelings, calm themselves down or help with sleeping.

Young women experienced physical problems and disability more often than young men. In the interviews, these physical problems were not a prominent theme, but for young girls who had problems, these were typically gastrointestinal problems, including recurring stomach-ache and sensitive bowels, which respondents associated with stress, but also eating disorder and previous suicide attempt. Finally, many middle-aged women experienced a chronic physical condition (61%), pain (66%) and disability (66%). The interviews described how physical discomfort and pain affected their daily life. Notably, several middle-aged women used soft drugs to deal with their pain, which might explain the high proportion of soft drugs use in middle-aged women who died by suicide (27%).

Social relations, bullying and violence

Most adverse life events preceding the suicide were of an interpersonal nature, including the loss of a relationship, fights or even physical or sexual abuse. Significantly more women than men who died by suicide were the victim of sexual abuse. In the year before the suicide, 17% of young women were subjected to sexual violence. Physical violence was especially high in young women. Respondents described the devastating impact of such experiences on feelings of self-worth, and its contribution to traumatic symptoms and anxiety in young women.

Parent: She was used, or abused, by several men. […] From high school, gradually on. The first, which was in full view, we met him at a neighbourhood get-together when she was [age omitted, early adolescence] years old, and she fell in love with him, and it showed. But the boy was warned by his mom, he was [age omitted, late adolescence] at the time. Like, ‘she is way too young, you have to stay away from her.’ And in first instance, it looked like he did, but later it turned out he did not. […] And then it turned out he made videotapes.”

Loneliness was a recurring theme in the interviews and could take various forms, ranging from feelings of thwarted belonging, even when someone was part of a large friend group, to self-instigated social isolation and social anxiety. According to respondents, around three in ten young decedents (30%) had been bullied. In young, bullied women, bullying was consistently perceived as a key contributing factor to the suicide, and a frequent topic in the interviews, less so in young men.

Friend: She had some very tough teenage years. A lot of acne, and [she was] very insecure, she was bullied. So, I think that for her, this thing, her obsession, with [self-help] books in her search for happiness, may be because of that.

A hammer blow

Broken relationships in the year of the suicide were a common proximal stressor for suicide in middle-aged men, and the interviews corroborate this. A pattern was identified which described eight men (out of 25 of whom interviews were available) who had a limited history of mental health problems, but for whom an interpersonal adverse life event, generally an event of loss, caused severe psychological distress. These adverse life events, and the corresponding psychological distress, arose up to 2 years before the suicide. Psychiatric problems were not salient in the interviews; mild depression was mentioned in six of these men.

Partner: He was down. Like he had received a hammer blow. I have never seen him cry, but then he did. He was very upset. He sat there, with a pale face, on the couch, crying, like: ‘it’s all my fault, I failed to do what I had to.’ Since his father passed, I saw his flame slowly extinguish, a little.

By contrast, there was a group of middle-aged men—and some women—who had struggled for a long time, up to decades, with episodes of a depressive mood disorder. Respondents typically described a series of recurring episodes, in which these individuals were supported by their GP, a psychiatrist or therapist, of whom many had received antidepressant medication. Alcohol misuse and sleeping problems were commonly described in these episodes. However, they also experienced periods where they at least appeared to enjoy life.

A silent struggle

We defined the suicide as out-of-the-blue when the decedent did not receive treatment for mental health problems at the time of death, there were no known events of explicit suicide-related communication (eg, “I am thinking about killing myself”) and there was no known history of suicide attempts. Out-of-the-blue suicides were most common in young men (23%), and there was a stark contrast with young women (6%). This difference was not observed in middle-aged persons (16% in men vs 10% in women). A first impression of the accounts of respondents indicates these young men were ‘sensitive’, ‘loners’ or ‘dreamy’ from a young age, inclined to cater to the needs of others, which bereaved referred to as ‘people pleasers’, avoided conflicts and had strong feelings of perceived burdensomeness. Conversely, while respondents often noted how boys ‘did not talk’, many also found it challenging to recognise these problems.

Parent: I read the letter, and everything I felt was confirmed in that. He described… and it was so nice to hear [read] how he felt in his own words, why he distanced himself from everyone. The emptiness he felt. It was written so beautifully that his sister said: “See? He could talk after all.” And it made me think: yes, my boy, why didn’t you talk about this? But apparently, he couldn't talk about it in those words. Because that was his limitation too.

A network of peers with suicidal thoughts and behaviours

Significantly more young women (76%) than young men (29%) knew other people who were suicidal. This difference was not found in middle-aged men and women. Particularly young women appeared to shape their social network around suicidal thoughts and behaviours. Especially in clinical settings, they met peers with similar problems who they could relate to, and in time, this became their social network, affecting their perceptions of mental health and recovery.

Parent: She always brought home other children with… baggage. Children who, well, had something going on.

Interviewer: Do you mean vulnerable children?

Parent: vulnerable children, yes, she brought them home all the time. And she was also good with children who had, uhm, a disability, was always good with them, that was [name].

Around half of the young women (53%) and middle-aged women (49%) had expressed a wish for euthanasia, significantly more than men. Interviews concerning young women indicated that their wish for euthanasia was a manifestation of hopelessness after long care trajectories with little improvement.

Mental health problems

Over half of the suicide decedents (59%) had a reported diagnosed psychiatric disorder. Young women most often had a diagnosed disorder (81%), and comorbidity was common. To illustrate, the 64 young women with a diagnosis in our sample had a combined 220 diagnosed disorders (on average, 3.4), compared with 122 diagnoses in 54 young men (on average, 2.3). The reported psychiatric burden was very high in young women. Deliberate self-harm and suicide attempts were significantly more common in young women; 89% had deliberately harmed themselves once or more frequently, and 81% had engaged in one or multiple suicide attempts, compared with 42% and 46%, respectively, in young men. This difference was also found in middle-aged persons, but less pronounced.

Disconnectedness

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) had a strikingly high prevalence in young decedents, both in young men and women (23% of all young people in the sample, 41% of young people with a diagnosis). In the interviews, a pattern emerged which described the suicidal process of six youths and young adults with developmental disorders. What distinguished them was that they felt like they did not belong from a young age. They were described as loners, or socially withdrawn, and respondents described how they did not feel in touch with others. They felt different and detached. “He did not feel the warmth around him”, explains one parent. Three of them had spoken out that they did not want to live at a very young age (at 5, 6 years old).

Parent: I think he was [age omitted, early childhood] years when he first said… it was during his birthday party; we celebrated on a Sunday afternoon. There were many children, who also went into his room, with his things, and it was dreadful for him. He couldn’t handle it at all. […] he became very angry. Well, and then those children were gone, and he sat in his room. And I was with him for a bit, and he said: ‘mom, I wish I was dead.’

They were often described as rigid or normative and had difficulties putting their emotions into words. Disconnectedness was a consistent theme throughout their lives. Four of them started using substances to suppress their feelings. Finally, what stood out for this group was the increase in distress during transitions, from lower to higher education, and later when they became working age. They struggled to become self-sufficient adults, whereby they experienced a marked contrast with other young people.

First, 80% of young women and 78% of middle-aged women had received specialised mental healthcare in their lives. This was less in middle-aged men (62%) and especially young men (48%). Remarkably, we found many significant differences in indicators of care use between young men and women, while these differences were not found in middle-aged men and women. Barriers to healthcare use were salient in interviews about young men, which aligns with the findings concerning out-of-the-blue suicides and the use of informal support. At the time of the suicide, 41% of all decedents received treatment for mental health problems. In 77% of these cases, suicidality was known to be a focal point.

A loss of hope and perspective

Several decedents were declared ‘beyond treatment’ according to informants. This entailed 21% of young women, 19% of middle-aged women vs 5% and 13% of young and middle-aged men respectively. Importantly, the interviews revealed the major impact of having been told that there were no further treatment options available. It induced a loss of perspective and feelings of hopelessness.

Parent: She felt that practitioners and her network became increasingly powerless, and I think that a feeling emerged, as she wrote in her own words: ‘I’m too difficult.’ It’s the saddest thing… to realize that with her [age omitted, young adult] years of life, she became convinced that nobody could help her, save her. No person should have that feeling.

A pattern involving intensive healthcare use and its associated problems emerged in the interviews, which involved seven young women and one young man. They were all characterised by a negative self-image, a gradual deterioration of mental health problems from the transition to adolescence, increasing self-harm, suicide attempts, a long period of intensive mental healthcare use, multimorbidity of psychiatric problems and a loss of hope and perspective towards recovery.

Parent: she should have been sent to a ward, but no one wanted to have us. They did not dare to take on the accountability of someone with suicidal… so we were obliged to sit in a little room at the emergency department.

Finally, one in every five decedents (19%) was on a waitlist to receive treatment in specialised mental healthcare. For half of them, some form of care or treatment was already provided, which implies they were on an internal waiting list or listed for a different treatment.

Mental health problems and problems with social relations, such as a break-up, fight or divorce, were universally addressed. However, several factors had a profound perceived impact in subgroups, such as problems with healthcare in women, school problems in young people, physical health in middle-aged people, bullying in young women, and work and financial problems in middle-aged men. The perceived impact corresponded closely with the prevalences of indicators of that respective domain. Interestingly, however, the sum of contributing factors was much lower in young men than in other groups, suggesting that respondents found it more difficult to conceive factors for suicide in young men.

Discussion

This study reports findings from a retrospective, cross-sectional cohort detailing information about people who died by suicide in the Netherlands, collected through informant-based psychosocial autopsy. We described the prevalence of key characteristics, tested differences between men and women within two age groups of interest to our national suicide prevention strategy, and integrated these findings with qualitative outcomes, to better understand and address (unmet) needs for care and support in subgroups of people who died by suicide. The most prominent risk factors addressed in our psychosocial autopsy closely align with previous psychological autopsy studies, including psychiatric disorders, particularly depressive mood disorder28 40 and interpersonal adverse life events.41 The overall prevalence of psychiatric disorders among suicide decedents was approximately 60% in our study, which aligns with reviews of psychological autopsies by Milner et al42 and Favril et al,40 a recent case–control psychological autopsy study41 and a large cohort in the USA.43

Young men more often died ‘out-of-the-blue’ compared with young women. There is currently no agreed-on definition for out-of-the-blue suicide in scientific literature. The perceived unexpectedness of a suicide was not unequivocally informed by objectifiable parameters at all but may instead reflect reconciliation. We recommend that the field develops a proper conceptual framework for studying this poorly understood topic. Farewell notes indicated that young men who died out of the blue thought of themselves as a burden, which is a well-known psychological risk factor for suicide44 and this may pose a double risk when it hampers help seeking. Respondents further described how these young men did not believe healthcare would help them. To better cater to the needs of young men at risk of dying by suicide, it is important to investigate how we may change their perspectives towards care or develop new types of care that better align their needs. Female-dominated study samples in the research of lived experience pose an obstacle for this.45 Lastly, respondents tended to attribute a lot of agency to the young men in their accounts, often noting how they ‘did not open up’. While it is important to recognise the agency and resilience of young people, our interviews indicate that suicidal thoughts, through burdensomeness, undermine the agency of young men to seek help for themselves. This has been previously recognised in other contexts, and it is suggested that outreaching care could yield better outcomes.46 47

We found that 41% of young decedents with a diagnosis had ASD. People with ASD have an increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviours.48 49 For suicide prevention, it is vital that we then understand what this type of neurodiversity meant in the lives of young people who died by suicide. We identified a pattern in the interviews that related to young people, among whom mostly men, with a developmental disorder who died by suicide. A combination of themes emerged, including strong feelings of thwarted belongingness from a young age, disconnectedness, limited social support and a particular vulnerability during the transition to adulthood. Alcohol and substance use were mentioned to numb their feelings and conceived of as self-medication. This could at least partly explain the high prevalence of substance use in our sample compared with young people in the general population.50 A Dutch guideline to support young people with ASD and suicidal thoughts was recently developed,51 which incorporated preliminary findings from our work. It is furthermore important that practitioners who diagnose ASD in young people explicitly discuss and monitor suicidal thoughts. We furthermore encourage professionals to provide young people with ASD and their parents with tools to facilitate the transition to adulthood, for which promising international programmes are available.52,54

Young women who died by suicide had more often talked about their suicidality, discussed euthanasia and received care for mental health problems, which corresponds with findings that highlight gender differences in the communication about suicidal thoughts,55 the presence of self-harm and suicide attempts56 and mental health literacy and utilisation of healthcare services57—although for the latter the effect of education level was greater, which we did not correct for in our analyses. There was a very high disease burden in young women, with 81% having had a diagnosis for a disorder. Young women with any disorder on average had 3.4 different diagnoses at the time of their death and, critically, one disorder could be a contraindication for the treatment of another. The interviews revealed how many young women had received years of intensive care but did not recover. One in five young women was told at some point that no further treatment options were available, and this had significant impact on feelings of hope and perspective. Care professionals may remain involved in the process of finding appropriate care or support, inside or outside specialised mental healthcare settings. Half of the young women reported a wish to die by euthanasia. Finally, what stood out was how young women reshaped their network around suicidal thoughts and behaviours and how this may affect resilience. Three in four young women knew someone else who was suicidal, and interviews suggested they met these peers in mental healthcare, which could explain the difference with young men. This propensity to unify a disease with identity has received limited attention in literature but could hamper recovery and should be a focal point for professionals working with young women in mental healthcare.

Middle-aged men showed no significantly higher rates for any of the considered risk factors compared with middle-aged women. However, several topics were more salient in the interviews concerning men. Additionally, respondents more frequently reported work and financial problems to be a key contributing factor for suicide in middle-aged men. We cannot fully ascertain what underlies this, but there may be several explanations. It is conceivable that particular risk factors for suicide affect men differently56—a small number of risk factors may affect men more strongly. Alternatively, proxy informants could have rooted sociocultural perceptions about what affects women and men respectively. In the interviews of middle-aged men, we identified two thematic patterns that were primarily distinguished by long-term versus short-term psychological distress. The first pattern involved middle-aged males who died after a relatively short period of distress which followed a notable adverse life event, typically an experience of loss (eg, divorce). The men experienced feelings of perceived inadequacy and were anxious that one event of loss would cascade. This taps into earlier findings concerning the experiences of help-seeking middle-aged men, which show how situational stressors fractured their masculine identity.58 The other pattern entailed middle-aged men, and several women, with a depressive mood disorder, describing episodes of depression that sometimes recurred over decades, typically with long periods of relative stability. A Belgian case–control psychological autopsy28 recently found that episodic compared with persistent depressive mood disorder separated suicide cases from controls.

Nearly two-thirds of middle-aged women who died by suicide suffered from physical problems, disability and pain, and respondents perceived this to play a key role in their suicide. Nearly all physical health conditions are associated with suicide risk.59 Based on our data, middle-aged women may be especially vulnerable to this risk. Moreover, 25% of middle-aged women had used soft drugs in the 6 months before the suicide, and our interviews showed that they sometimes used drugs to cope with physical discomfort, disability or pain. Ahmedani et al60 note that most individuals who died by suicide received health services in primary care or general medical speciality settings. GPs should therefore be specifically aware of suicidality in older adult women who present with physical discomfort, disability or pain. Finally, high rates of sexual intimidation and sexual violence were reported in women who died by suicide, especially in young women. The devastating impact of sexual abuse became evident from the interviews, where respondents described diminished self-worth, traumatic symptoms and anxiety, which echoes qualitative themes described in previous research.61 This could also explain the significantly higher rates of post-traumatic stress disorder we identified among women of both age groups.

Limitations

The current study had several limitations. First, no control group was included. To contextualise the findings of this study and provide recommendations for prevention, we compared the prevalence of characteristics identified in this study to available figures in comparable demographic groups in the general population5 and other monitors50 and discussed our findings in relation to relevant and recent literature, but this comparison does not produce the quality of matched controls.22 23 27 We cannot ascertain that the identified prevalences are indicative of suicide decedents without matched controls. A follow-up study with matched GP controls would provide the opportunity to investigate protective factors on a large scale, which is unprecedented. Second, the data may be subject to recall bias, which has been stressed before to compromise the validity of psychological autopsy studies.29 Assink62 notes that the risk of recall bias is lower when it concerns an event with a strong negative emotion, which arguably includes suicide. A post hoc assessment indicated that the proportion of ‘I don’t know’ was highest in the ‘other’ respondent type (eg, far relatives) and was not associated with time between the suicide and participation when corrected for participant relation (onlinesupplemental additional files 5ab). We propose that the timeframe to conduct informant-based psychosocial autopsy studies should therefore be guided foremost by reasonable boundaries to respect the well-being of the bereaved respondents. Respondents are well able to answer questions about their loved ones across a broad range of topics. Our data further suggest that a direct relative (child, parent or partner) or a close friend of the decedent should always be included. If specific topics are studied, this perspective is preferably complemented with additional informants from a different social environment.63 For example, out-of-the-blue cases in our study are conceivably overestimated based on single accounts. Obviously, these conditions depend on the study aims and the type of data collected. For an in-depth qualitative reconstruction, experts recommended that the interviews take place earlier, ranging from 6 months to 3 years after the suicide,64 65 although exact recommendations differ, and argumentation is largely experiential and not guided by measurement. We conducted the interview up to three calendar years after the event. Finally, there may have been selection bias. Our recruitment relied on self-admission of proxy informants, which may deter people who experience guilt or other barriers to participate and cause underrepresentation of decedents with a smaller social network. We believe the latter resulted in a relatively high proportion of young suicide decedents and fewer elderly. For our analyses, we used stratified samples, and within-group representativeness was satisfactory on most indicators. However, there were relatively few people with a migration background. This challenge is widely acknowledged in participant research and has been a limitation of our earlier work as well,25 despite efforts to use inclusive language and working with local ambassadors.

Conclusions and implications

Mixed-methods psychosocial autopsy presents a unique empirical perspective through a large retrospective, cross-sectional cohort of individuals who died by suicide. We identified differences in the psychosocial characteristics of young and middle-aged men and women, and the interviews illustrated how these differences may constitute different needs for care and support. This research output is part of a national psychosocial autopsy which enables consistently monitoring and learning from suicide events. We believe such a learning approach has most merit when it also fosters a system to translate findings into recommendations, and recommendations into action. The basis for such a system has been installed, in the form of a multidisciplinary expert committee, but implementation of the practice implications discussed in this work requires the collaborative and persistent effort of stakeholders. In the future, we aim to expand our analyses with time trends and cluster analyses and strengthen our network of involved stakeholders for the purpose of valorisation.
