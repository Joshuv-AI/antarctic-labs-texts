Analysis of the learning curve for unilateral biportal endoscopic technique using CUSUM method on fresh frozen cadavers
Authors: Han Wang, Xinzhi Li, Bo Li, Junsheng Du, Zenan Meng, Wenyao Chen, Xiangyong Que, Chongyu Huang, Yi Li, Jie Wang, Abulimiti Mireadeli
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aims to determine the learning curve for lumbar discectomy and nerve root decompression using the unilateral biportal endoscopic (UBE) technique on fresh frozen cadavers through linear regression and cumulative sum (CUSUM) analyses.MethodsThe analysis was conducted on three spine surgeons without UBE experience. They performed lumbar discectomy using the unilateral endoscopic technique on fresh frozen cadavers. Each physician performed operations on 40 lumbar spine segments, recording the time it took each physician to complete a segment of lumbar discectomy by UBE. The learning curve was assessed by linear regression and CUSUM analysis using SPSS 27.0 software and GraphPad Prism 8.0 software.ResultsAccording to the CUSUM analysis of the learning curve, the number of segments required to complete the learning phase for the three surgeons was 20, 16, and 20, respectively. With the increased number of operated segments, the operation time for each lumbar vertebral segment gradually decreased and stabilized.ConclusionsIn our study, spine surgeons without experience in UBE require 20 segments of surgery to overcome the UBE learning curve. Additionally, fresh frozen cadavers can effectively simulate real surgeries. Whenever possible, we should reasonably use fresh frozen cadavers for early UBE training of doctors, thereby better protecting the health and safety of patients.Keywords: Spine surgery, Fresh frozen cadavers, Unilateral biportal endoscopy discectomy, Learning curve

Lumbar degenerative diseases refer to conditions caused by degenerative changes in the lumbar intervertebral discs, facet joints, ligaments, and other surrounding structures of the lumbar spine [1]. The main symptoms include lower back pain and leg pain. Lumbar disc herniation is the most common form of lumbar degenerative disease and is also one of the most common causes of lower back and lower limb pain. Severe lumbar disc herniation can even lead to nerve damage in the lower limbs, affecting individuals’ workability and quality of life [2]. For patients who do not respond to standard conservative treatments, surgery becomes the only treatment option. Since the first report of minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (MIS-TLIF) twenty years ago, followed by percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy (PELD), and in 2017, Heo introduced the concept of unilateral biportal endoscopic (UBE) technique and successfully applied it to lumbar fusion surgery. The field of spinal surgery has transformed from open to minimally invasive procedures [3]. Compared to traditional surgery, UBE surgery demonstrates superior outcomes regarding intraoperative radiation exposure, blood loss, and average hospital stay [4].

However, as a relatively new and immature technique using various arthroscopic instruments, the UBE technique poses a significant challenge for spinal surgeons during its early implementation [5]. For most spinal surgeons, ensuring surgical safety while maintaining clinical efficacy and maximizing patient benefits requires extensive practice and experience. Currently, research on the learning curve of the UBE technique is limited, and existing studies derive their learning curves directly from actual patients, which can compromise patients’ right to optimal treatment. Innovatively, we performed UBE technique operations on fresh frozen cadavers and used the cumulative sum (CUSUM) method to analyze the relationship between operation time and surgeon proficiency, determining the optimal number of repetitions during the UBE learning process. Additionally, we summarized critical points in the procedure to reduce the risk of complications, ensuring maximum patient safety and health.

Procedure

Surgical incision location and establishment of endoscopic viewing channel

Position the fresh frozen cadaver prone. Under fluoroscopy with a C-arm, identify the surgical incision site. The initial target point is located at the junction of the spinous process and the lamina, marked with a transverse line, and a line is drawn along the inner edge of the pedicle. The intersection of these lines is 1.5 cm above and below the viewing portal and working portal surface points. Six marking points are made on each side of the cadavers, totaling twelve, and syringe needles are inserted at these points. A vertical skin incision of about 1–2 cm is made, and graduated cannulas and dissectors are inserted to establish the channel. An endoscope is inserted into the viewing portal channel, and a nerve retractor or UBE half-tube is inserted into the corresponding working portal channel, with both ends converging at the lamina (Fig. 1).

Establishment of endoscopic viewing channel

Handling of the lamina and ligamentum flavum

Under the endoscope, a 90°plasma blade is used to treat the soft tissue in the interlaminar space. Once the lamina is exposed, a 4.0 drill and rongeur are used to handle the lower edge of the lamina. Some researchers use an ultrasonic bone scalpel to handle the lamina, improving surgical precision, reducing surgical risk, and easing lamina treatment’s difficulty. After handling the upper edge of the ligamentum flavum, distally detach it from the proximal attachment point. This part of the procedure is prone to bleeding, requiring hemostasis with a radiofrequency blade, and appropriate water pressure can effectively reduce intraoperative bleeding. The proximal lamina is treated up to the proximal attachment point of the ligamentum flavum; then the lower vertebral lamina is treated. This allows for the complete removal of the ligamentum flavum on the same side. Maintaining a clear surgical field and preventing electrical burns and infection is critical, necessitating continuous and unobstructed irrigation and appropriate water pressure. Studies have shown that the average water pressure in the intraoperative channel should be controlled at 16.99 ± 9.12 cmH2O (12.25 ± 6.71 mmHg), not exceeding 31.00 cmH2O (22.83 mmHg) [6]. Excessive water pressure is detrimental. A 30° endoscope during surgery can expand the surgical field and help observe small vessels and ligaments, reducing the risk of vascular and nerve damage [7] (Fig. 2、3).

Removal of the intervertebral disc

Handling of the intervertebral disc

Once the herniated nucleus pulposus is exposed under the endoscope, incise the posterior longitudinal ligament, remove the exposed nucleus pulposus, and protect the dural sac and nerve roots with a nerve retractor or half-tube. After completely removing the exposed intervertebral disc, the nerve roots should no longer be tense and appear relaxed under the endoscope. After thoroughly the herniated nucleus pulposus, the annulus fibrosus can be sutured, and local corticosteroids may be used (Fig. 4).

Statistical methods

SPSS 27.0 software was used for statistical analysis of the time data for each step performed by the operators. Measurement data, the time for each step, is expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Linear regression analysis of the total duration was performed using SPSS software, with a P-value < 0.05 indicating statistically significant differences.

CUSUM analysis was employed to assess each step and the overall learning curve for UBE surgery. The CUSUM analysis method is based on operation time, using the formula CUSUM=, in which represents each step’s operation time, and represents the corresponding step’s mean operation time. The CUSUM analysis results were plotted as scatter diagrams in GraphPad Prism 8.0 software, with function equations calculated through curve fitting. A P-value < 0.05 indicates successful fitting. The goodness of fit is determined by the R² value, with an R² closer to 1 indicating better fit. The peak value of the fitted curve slope was calculated to delineate learning stages.

Results

Surgeon 1

The average operation time for Surgeon 1 was 98.68 ± 17.24 min. The three steps took17.45 ± 4.48 min, 48.80 ± 8.30 min, and 32.43 ± 5.73 min, respectively. The logistic regression equation for the total operation time was y=-1.423x + 127.846 (R²=0.931). CUSUM analysis showed a peak at 20 cases, indicating that Surgeon 1 needs 20 cases to master the UBE surgical procedure (Fig. 5).

CUSUM learning curve for surgeon 1

Surgeon 2

The average operation time for Surgeon 2 was 76.43 ± 16.45 min. The three steps took 13.53 ± 3.76 min, 37.50 ± 7.51 min, and 25.40 ± 5.57 min, respectively. The logistic regression equation for the total operation time was y=-1.305x + 103.177 (R²=0.779). CUSUM analysis showed a peak at 16 cases, indicating that Surgeon 2 needs 16 cases to master the UBE surgical procedure. Compared to Surgeons 1 and 3, reaching the peak earlier suggests that Surgeon 2 may have a natural aptitude or better foundational skills in UBE operations (Fig. 6).

CUSUM learning curve for surgeon 2

Surgeon 3

The average operation time for Surgeon 3 was 85.75 ± 18.58 min. The three steps took 18.40 ± 4.95 min, 40.63 ± 8.14 min, and 26.73 ± 6.21 min, respectively. The logistic regression equation for the total operation time was y=-1.55x + 117.515 (R²=0.95). The peak was reached at 20 cases, indicating that Surgeon 3 needs 20 cases to master the UBE surgical procedure. Compared to Surgeon 1, the higher learning curve for Surgeon 3 indicates a more significant improvement with each case. The negative slopes of the logistic regression equations for all three surgeons and the excellent fit indicate minimal external information interference, suggesting accurate learning curves (Fig. 7).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to analyze the learning curve of spinal surgeons performing UBE surgery for the first time using the cumulative sum (CUSUM) method. The results show that spinal surgeons without prior UBE experience need about 20 spinal segments to achieve basic proficiency in this technique. The operation time for all three surgeons significantly decreased and stabilized with increasing cases, consistent with previous studies [8]. The height of the learning curve peak and the number of cases needed to reach the peak reflect the surgeon’s learning ability for new techniques. Simultaneously, when the slope of the curve changes from positive to negative, the corresponding number of segments at the peak of the learning curve represents the amount required for a doctor to progress from mastery to proficiency in the technique.

With the aging global population, lumbar degenerative diseases have become a significant health concern. As a rapidly developing technique, UBE has become an essential skill for spinal surgeons treating lumbar degenerative diseases. Compared to MIS-TLIF, both techniques reduce iatrogenic soft tissue damage, such as less trauma to paraspinal muscles, less blood loss, and faster postoperative recovery [9]. However, for deeper surgical incisions, the surgical field in MIS-TLIF could be more apparent, posing challenges for the surgeon. The tubular retractor used to pull the paraspinal muscles can affect local soft tissue ischemia and postoperative recovery [10–13]. In UBE surgery, most arthroscopic instruments can be used, lowering the threshold for surgical instruments. UBE employs two independent channels for operation and imaging, combining the advantages of the wide field of view and convenient operation of traditional open surgery with the minimal tissue damage of minimally invasive surgery. However, creating an artificial working space, unlike the natural space in arthroscopic surgery, demands high hand-eye coordination from the surgeon. Our study analyzes the learning curve for spinal surgeons performing UBE surgery for the first time and, through practice on fresh frozen cadavers, helps familiarize surgeons with UBE operations quickly, thereby shortening the UBE surgery learning curve.

Fresh frozen cadavers have many advantages compared to formalin-preserved cadavers [14]. Cadavers soaked in formalin exhibit stiffness and unrealistic characteristics, leading to the loss of surgical planes and spaces. Some small structures (e.g. nerves, blood vessels, etc.) are difficult to identify, and its strong irritating odour may lead to the operation of this adverse symptoms such as nasal congestion, itchy eyes, dizziness and malaise [15]. In contrast, fresh cadavers simulate actual surgical conditions more accurately. Fresh frozen cadavers not only lack a noticeable odor but also have significant advantages in terms of tissue delicacy and softness, making them more suitable for biomechanical testing and fine anatomical training post-simulation surgery [15]. This study demonstrates that operations on fresh cadaver specimens closely resemble real clinical surgeries, reducing the psychological burden on surgeons. All of this allows them to focus more on surgical procedures, and effectively learn surgical steps and key anatomical points of attention. Additionally, the study’s timeframe was concentrated, with all segment operations completed within two weeks, minimizing the influence of external information on the surgeons and resulting in more accurate learning curves.

Traditional learning of minimally invasive spinal surgery mainly involves using simulated surgical instruments or watching surgical videos. In this conventional training model, doctors find it difficult to intuitively experience the natural feeling of minimally invasive surgery, making it suitable only for introductory learning. To better simulate real surgical scenarios and shorten the UBE surgery learning curve, many surgical centers are exploring new training methods. With technological advancements, virtual reality training for UBE surgery has emerged [16]. However, such training requires high-end equipment and does not adequately simulate natural surgical environments. The spinal surgery department in our hospital, by analyzing the advantages and disadvantages of various surgeries and combining our disease expertise, has pioneered the use of fresh cadavers for UBE surgery training, leading the practice domestically.

In the early stages of our study, due to the extensive range of movement of the endoscope and operating instruments, operators often lost the visual field of the instruments or even entered the intervertebral space, injuring nerve roots or the dura mater. Research on the UBE surgery learning curve indicates that proficiency, reduced operation time, and minimized patient blood loss during lumbar laminectomy and spinal canal decompression are achieved after approximately 58 surgeries [17]. Additionally, studies show that the overall failure rate of UBE surgery is 6.11%, with a learning phase rate of 10.11% and a mastery phase rate of 2.78%. Surgeons require experience with at least 89 cases to achieve a stable success rate [18]. To further reduce the most common complications of UBE surgery—dural tears (2.23%), incomplete decompression (1.7%), and epidural hematoma (3.79%)—spinal surgeons need extensive learning and experience [19]. Our study suggests that after establishing the UBE learning curve, more targeted training is required to optimize each step of the UBE procedure, lower its operational threshold, and further promote the application of UBE technology in lumbar degenerative diseases, allowing more doctors to understand and even master this technique.

The methods for studying surgical learning curves can be categorized into four types: graphical, grouping, cumulative, and regression. These four methods can be combined, each with its own advantages and limitations. Among these methods, CUSUM is one of the most commonly used [20]. Since E.S. Page first applied CUSUM in manufacturing for product performance inspection in 1954, and de Leval first used CUSUM in medicine for analyzing 104 neonatal arterial switch surgeries in 1994, CUSUM has gradually entered the medical field. Most surgical procedures can now be analyzed for learning curves using this method [21, 22]. Van Ramshorst compared the learning curves of four “self-taught” and four “trained” surgeons performing laparoscopic distal pancreatectomy (LDP), finding that “trained” surgeons had shorter and smoother learning curves with better intraoperative and postoperative outcomes [23]. Bo Wang used the Da Vinci robotic system for 104 thyroidectomy cases. They concluded through CUSUM analysis that this surgical system helps reduce operation time and complication rates, thereby improving overall patient outcomes [24]. Li retrospectively analyzed 42 surgical cases using CUSUM and risk-adjusted CUSUM (RA-CUSUM) to delineate the learning curve for single-port laparoscopic surgery for endometrial cancer, identifying three phases: learning (5 cases), basic mastery (6–11 cases), and proficiency (11–42 cases) [25]. With an increased learning curve, operation time decreased rapidly, though there was no significant difference in surgical complications across the three phases. Among many CUSUM studies and applications, Sun cleverly combined finite element analysis with CUSUM to evaluate femoral stress distribution and deformation during intramedullary fixation, determining the safety range for drilling eccentricity [26]. This approach could provide new insights for future surgical safety zone research. Our study along with numerous previous studies, demonstrates that CUSUM analysis is an excellent statistical tool for quantitatively evaluating the learning curves of specific techniques in the medical field, accurately representing the time relationship between surgical case numbers and surgeon proficiency. Currently, most surgeries can be evaluated for their learning curves using the CUSUM method [27]. After analyzing the surgical learning curve in the early stages, it facilitates targeted training for doctors in later stages, enhancing surgical skills, further improving patient outcomes, reducing surgery time, and lowering complication rates.

In this study, we used fresh frozen cadavers for hands-on training in UBE technique and analysed the learning curve using the CUSUM method, which, although providing some reference for training in this technique, still has some limitations. Firstly, the cadaveric model, although representative of anatomical structure, lacks the physiological responses and feedback of living tissues and may not fully simulate the challenges of a real surgical environment. Surgical details such as the extent of laminectomy, the degree of articular eminence polishing, ligamentum flavum removal and the amount of intraoperative bleeding could not be effectively evaluated. In addition, we did not perform endoscopic lumbar fusion and unilateral laminotomy for bilateral decompression (ULBD) during the study. Secondly, the small sample size may affect the generalisability of the results. With twelve cadavers equally allocated to each surgeon and the number of operable segments per surgeon being forty, this study was forced to generate CUSUM curves only for surgeons within forty segments. Fortunately, however, more consistent proficiency and a complete learning curve was obtained at forty segments. Future studies should consider increasing the sample size to improve the reliability and extrapolation of the study. In addition, differences in surgeon experience were not fully controlled for. Differences in each surgeon’s prior knowledge base and hands-on skills affected the learning curve to varying degrees. Finally, although the CUSUM method provides a quantitative tool for learning curve analysis, it relies on assumptions and methods that may not fully reflect the multidimensional development of surgical skills, especially in complex surgical scenarios. Therefore, future studies must take these factors into account to further optimise the experimental design and validate the clinical applicability of the results.

With the rapid development of modern surgery, many new techniques and procedures are emerging rapidly. Today’s surgeons must excel in their clinical duties while also being educators and guides. With the burgeoning development of emerging technologies like UBE, its inherent advantages and ease of dissemination have made it an essential surgical technique for treating lumbar degenerative diseases. However, at this stage, many issues must be addressed in perfecting UBE technology, such as reasonable application indications and optimization of technical details. Whether UBE technology will play a pivotal role in the field of spinal endoscopy in the future depends not only on the advantages of the technology itself and historical opportunities but also on the mission and passion of today’s minimally invasive spine surgeons.

Conclusion

In this study, the learning curve of UBE technique in the practical operation of fresh frozen cadavers was systematically analysed using the CUSUM method, which allows a more in-depth study of the learning process of this technique. The learning curve showed a typical progressive development with the accumulation of surgical experience, and the CUSUM method of analysis was effective in reflecting the stages of improvement in operator skills. This result highlights the importance of systematic and quantitative assessment of different surgical techniques in clinical skills training. Further studies should focus on exploring the combined effects of different training strategies, operating environments and skill assessment methods to provide more scientific and accurate guidance for the training and clinical application of surgical techniques.
