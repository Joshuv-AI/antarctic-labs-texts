Masquelet technique combined with Ilizarov external fixation for the management of large femoral defects: A neglected technique
Authors: Ioannis S. Vasios, Konstantinos G. Makiev, Marios Tryfonidis, Efthymios Iliopoulos, Paraskevas Georgoulas, Konstantinos Tilkeridis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Large segmental femoral bone defects resulting from severe trauma, infection, or tumor resection present significant surgical challenges. Traditional methods like the Ilizarov bone transport technique and Masquelet technique are effective but have limitations. This study reports two cases of post-traumatic femoral bone defects successfully managed using a combination of the Masquelet technique and Ilizarov external fixation. The Masquelet technique, a two-stage process, involves the use of polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) cement to induce membrane formation, followed by bone grafting at the second stage. The Ilizarov external fixator, known for its stability and versatility, was used to provide more rigid fixation than the monolateral device and to help prevent complications such as infection and nonunion. In both cases, the patients sustained open, high-energy femoral fractures with extensive bone loss and associated soft tissue defects. The Masquelet technique was used to manage the bone defects and the Ilizarov external fixator was employed to ensure stability, allow early mobilization and provide the option of bone transport if necessary. Both patients achieved fracture union with satisfactory functional outcomes. The combination of these techniques effectively addresses the biological and mechanical challenges of large femoral defects, offering a promising solution when internal fixation is not feasible. This approach can be considered a valuable addition to the treatment armamentarium for complex femoral fractures, particularly in cases with infection risk or severe bone loss.

Introduction

Severe trauma, septic or aseptic non-union, infection (such as osteomyelitis) and tumor resection could lead to critical-sized long bone defects that demand surgical intervention. A multitude of methods has been proposed for the management of large segmental bone defects such as the Ilizarov bone transport technique, the implementation of huge quantities of autografts and allografts, vascularized bone grafts e.g., vascularized fibula, the usage of titanium cages, intramedullary lengthening devices or even the replacement of these defects with megaprostheses [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. There is no doubt that for many years the Ilizarov bone transport technique was the most popular among all the above-mentioned techniques. However, the Masquelet technique, also known as the induced membrane technique, has become increasingly popular over the last decades due to its simplicity, reliability and efficiency. Relatively few complications have been linked with this technique whereas several drawbacks of the rest of the techniques have been extensively documented in the current literature [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14]].

The Masquelet technique was firstly described in 1986 and since then, it belongs to our armamentarium for the treatment of cases with variable-sized bone defects. It is a two-staged procedure with high success rates for up to 25 cm bone defects. During the first stage, surgical debridement is performed in order to remove all damaged tissues (e.g., soft tissue necrosis, devitalized bone, infection, tumor) followed by filling the defect with polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) cement as a spacer. The implantation of the PMMA cement spacer into the defect promotes foreign body reaction that results in the formation of a membrane. In the second stage, which is performed usually 6 weeks after the first stage, the induced membrane is divided carefully, the PMMA cement spacer is removed, the defect is filled with bone graft, the induced membrane is preserved and closed carefully and finally, fracture stabilization is performed [10,11,[15], [16], [17], [18]].

On the other hand, the Ilizarov bone transport technique, which works on the principles of distraction osteogenesis, is considered the mainstream treatment strategy for large segmental bone defects. Despite the good outcomes reported in the literature, there are still concerns regarding its high complication rates [[19], [20], [21]]. However, since its first introduction in 1950s by Gavril Abramovich Ilizarov, it has been subjected to various modifications that are used today either isolated or combined with other methods such as nails, plates, wires and flaps [13,15,[21], [22], [23]].

In this study, we report two cases of post-traumatic large femoral bone defects that were managed with a combination of the Masquelet technique and the Ilizarov external fixation. To the best of our knowledge, there is only a small number of patients reported in the literature that were treated for segmental bone defects of the femur with this type of combined-technique treatment.

Materials & methods

Both patients were treated initially according to the principles of damage control orthopaedics (DCO) and in both cases the femoral fractures were stabilized with a monolateral external fixator. During the following stage, extensive surgical debridement of the wound and removal of all devitalized bone (until “paprika sign” was obvious) were performed and as a consequence, large defects were created. After careful evaluation, it was decided to implement the Masquelet technique along with the preservation of the monolateral external fixation (first stage). During the second stage, although not part of the Masquelet technique itself, the existing monolateral external device was replaced with an Ilizarov external fixator. This change was made to provide a more stable and rigid construct, as the Ilizarov frame offers superior mechanical stability compared to the monolateral system. The Ilizarov frame was applied prior to the removal of the monolateral fixator in order to avoid disrupting the induced membrane during the transition.

Despite the fact that locking compression plates and retrograde intramedullary nails are considered as the gold standard treatment for distal femoral fractures, none of these options could be implemented in these two patients. Both patients sustained open comminuted distal femoral fractures which were grossly contaminated and harbored the risk of infection. Additionally, the surgeon opted for this technique with the consideration that, in the event of failure of the Masquelet technique, it would allow for a seamless transition to bone transport for managing the bone defect.

First case

A 23-year-old male patient sustained open fractures of the right distal third of the femur and tibia following a high-energy motor vehicle accident. The injuries were classified as Gustilo-Anderson type 3C, indicating severe soft tissue damage and associated vascular injury. On initial assessment in the emergency department, the patient was found to have a femoral artery injury. Intravenous antibiotic therapy with a second-generation cephalosporin and an aminoglycoside was promptly administered, along with tetanus prophylaxis.

Following initial resuscitation and assessment, the patient was transferred to the operating theater for damage control surgery (Fig. 1A). Meticulous irrigation with normal saline and thorough debridement of the wounds were performed. In this case, the femur was intentionally shortened by approximately 3 cm, and the proximal fragment was impacted into the distal femoral condyles. This shortening strategy reduced the soft tissue tension, minimized the wound size, and eliminated the need for soft tissue flaps, while also improving construct stability. Although an Ilizarov frame was in place, which could have allowed for secondary limb lengthening, the patient declined any further surgical intervention for this purpose.

The Ilizarov frame (Fig. 3A) was removed 16 weeks later, after both clinical and radiographic evidence confirmed fracture union (Fig. 3B). Postoperatively, the patient developed contractures of the gastrocnemius-soleus complex, but range of motion (ROM) of the right knee was considered satisfactory at 0–90°. Second case

A 69-year-old female patient sustained multiple traumatic brain injuries as well as an open fracture, 3B according to Gustilo-Anderson classification, of the right distal third of the femur with extensive bone loss during a high-energy motor-vehicle accident. Intravenous antibiotics, 2nd generation cephalosporin and aminoglycoside, as well as tetanus vaccination were administered as soon as the patient arrived at the emergency department. After completion of the initial assessment, the patient was transferred to the operating theater for damage control surgery. Meticulous irrigation with normal saline solution as well as debridement of the wounds were performed. At the same time, the fracture was stabilized with a temporary monolateral external fixator, and afterwards, the patient was transferred for a short period of time to the ICU (Intensive Care Unit) due to the traumatic brain injuries. With regard to the femoral fracture, there was a bone defect measuring 8 cm, which was managed according to the staged Masquelet technique (Fig. 4) in combination with a circular Ilizarov frame (Fig. 5), as described in the previous case. During the first stage of the Masquelet technique, a cement spacer was placed and left in situ for 8 weeks. At the second stage, the spacer was removed and the defect was filled with a combination of autologous bone graft harvested from the iliac crest and allogenic bone grafts (fresh-frozen femoral head and demineralized bone matrix). The Ilizarov circular frame was subsequently removed 24 weeks later, once the fracture was fully healed (Fig. 6). Discussion

The femur, the strongest bone in the human body with one of the highest capacities to withstand stress forces, bears a fundamental role in patients' mobility [24]. Distal femoral fractures are quite rare fractures and represent almost 1 % of all fractures and 4–6 % of femoral fractures [25]. It belongs to the bones that have one of the highest incidences of nonunion with a mean value of 4–6 % and up to 13.9 % [25,26]. Nonunions burden patients and healthcare services worldwide and for this reason, efforts should be made to minimize their frequency and impact [12]. As a consequence, lot of studies have been conducted aiming to identify risk factors that may lead to fracture nonunion. These risk factors have been categorized as patient dependent or independent and as local or systematic [12,25,26]. Open fractures, high-energy trauma, comminuted fractures and fracture location are some of the established risk factors for a fracture nonunion [12,25,26].

Fracture healing can be achieved by two ways, either with primary or secondary bone healing. Primary healing, such as in ulnar fractures, is rare and demands absolute stability, compression and cortical contact. Secondary healing is the most common as it occurs in the vast majority of bone fractures. The “diamond concept”, introduced by Giannoudis et al. in 2007, describes in details the key elements that are essential for a successful fracture healing. Mechanical stability and biological environment along with adequate bone vascularity constitute the framework of an uncomplicated fracture healing [12,14,27,28].

There is no doubt that open fractures that are accompanied by soft-tissue loss pose significant challenges to the treating surgeons. The condition of soft tissues around the fracture sites, the level of vascularization and the high risk of infection are some of the factors that have to be considered for an optimal management [29,30]. Even though there have been various treatment modalities proposed, there is no standardized treatment protocol for these injuries until now. However, the so-called “orthoplastic collaboration” leads to superior outcomes as shown from recent studies [30,31].

In general, treatment approaches for long bone defects can be divided into two categories: prosthetic replacement and biological reconstruction. Biological reconstruction includes methods of either bone transport (e.g., Ilizarov external fixation) or bone grafting (e.g., the Masquelet technique). In complicated cases, such as in presence of infection, the eradication of the infection is of utmost significance for a successful result [15].

Over the last decades, the Masquelet technique has gained popularity in the restoration of large bone defects due its superb innate rationale [32,33]. Severe injuries that cause bone defects and soft tissue loss have impaired local vascularization and thus the healing potential decreases dramatically. Moreover, the healing environment is even more impaired in cases with open fractures and in the presence of infection [34]. Thus, the highly vascularized induced membrane may contribute to restoring local regenerative capacity. Numerous studies have shown its effectiveness in treating posttraumatic bone defects in various locations, including the hand, forearm, humerus, femur, tibia and foot. The Masquelet technique is particularly beneficial for managing infected bone defects, as the spacer helps address the infection by minimizing dead space, preventing the surrounding soft tissues to invade between the fracture surfaces, serving as a local antibiotic carrier and providing a certain level of fracture stability [34,35]. In this point, we must highlight that sufficient soft tissue coverage has to be achieved so that the bone and the cement spacer are not exposed [17,18,[36], [37], [38], [39]].

The second stage of the Masquelet technique takes place after a period of approximately 6–8 weeks. After careful incision of the induced membrane, the cement spacer is removed and the bone defect is filled with morselized cancellous autograft, most of the times harvested from the iliac crest, that is augmented with various types of allografts when needed. In the final step, the membrane is closed carefully and a stable and definite fixation is performed [10,21,34,36,40,41]. The induced membrane has a key role in this process since it provides vascular supply to the bone grafts and secretes growth factors e.g., BMP-2, VEGF and TGFβ [10,35,[41], [42], [43]]. Thus, preserving the membrane intact during the procedure is of utmost importance [29,32,41,42,44].

Regarding fracture stability, which is an inextricable part of the Masquelet technique, implant selection remains a controversial issue among orthopaedic surgeons. Fixation with locking plates and screws or with retrograde intramedullary nails is generally considered the treatment of choice for the definitive management of distal femoral fractures. The goals of these fixation methods are to provide sufficient stability at the fracture site, minimize the interfragmentary gap, and maintain interfragmentary strain below 10 % [45]. It is widely accepted that monolateral external fixators are not appropriate for the definitive treatment of distal femoral fractures, as they are associated with a higher incidence of complications such as pin tract infections, malunion, delayed union, nonunion, and knee joint stiffness. However, in cases where femoral fractures are deemed unsuitable for internal fixation, particularly in the presence of infection or extensive bone loss, the use of external fixation becomes unavoidable. In such situations, external fixators are often considered ideal, especially in patients with infected bone defects [16,21,46,47].

A wide variety of external fixation devices are now available, with the most commonly used being monolateral external fixators and circular external fixators (CEFs). Recent studies have demonstrated that CEFs, such as Ilizarov frames, provide superior mechanical stability and flexibility compared to monolateral devices. Monolateral fixators have been linked to a higher rate of residual complications, including infection, limb shortening, and limited capacity to correct multiplanar deformities, particularly when bone defects exceed the critical threshold of 6 cm. In contrast, CEFs allow more effective management of complex deformities and large bone defects, offering postoperative adjustability for gradual deformity correction without reopening the surgical site. Their ring-and-wire configuration promotes biological healing through controlled micromotion and can be applied away from infected or necrotic bone, making them ideal for cases of osteomyelitis, nonunion, and segmental bone loss [48,49].

Furthermore, the modular and patient-specific design of CEFs supports progressive adjustments during bone transport or lengthening procedures. Advances in lightweight materials have improved their stiffness-to-weight ratio, while the multiplanar configuration offers resistance to torsional and bending forces from multiple directions. Various techniques have also been proposed to enhance the rigidity of circular frames. For instance, Antoci et al. demonstrated that increasing the number of transfixion wires and optimizing the spacing between fixation levels significantly improves construct stiffness and overall biomechanical performance. Collectively, these biomechanical and clinical advantages – improved mechanical stability, lower infection rates, superior deformity correction, early mobilization, and adaptable implantation – have led to lower complication rates and improved outcomes in complex fractures and limb reconstruction [21,[47], [48], [49], [50], [51]].

The bone transport technique, which was firstly described by Ilizarov, is based on the principle of distraction osteogenesis and is traditionally considered the leading method for dealing with large bone defects. It has the potential to eliminate infection, counterpoise bone defects and provide the appropriate conditions for the progressive bone histogenesis [52]. Furthermore, it contributes to correction of deformities and limb-length discrepancies during the course of bone transport [53], a principle that is called the law of tension stress. Consequently, it can counteract the adverse impacts emerging from severe injuries. However, the bone transport technique is not without drawbacks. First of all, the prolonged utilization of an Ilizarov external fixation leads to joint stiffness as well as increased risk of pin tract infection. Moreover, the healing time is directly related to the size of the bone defect. As a consequence, the greater the defect, the longer it takes for the circular frame to be removed. Another important issue related to the technique is the possibility of docking site nonunion, which is one of the most reported complications [15,16,40].

The current study highlights the effectiveness of the Masquelet technique combined with the Ilizarov external fixation for the management of large defects of the femur. The study was based on the hypothesis that each technique has its own strengths and weaknesses, and their combination could maximize the favorable outcomes while minimizing the undesirable drawbacks of each. Taking all the above mentioned into account, the bone defects described were managed successfully with this combined treatment protocol. It is important to mention that the monolateral external fixation device was exchanged with the Ilizarov external fixator in order to provide a more stable construct having as an ultimate goal the protection of the induced membrane. Finally, the authors also support the notion that in case of failure of the Masquelet technique, the Ilizarov external fixator offers the flexibility to be transformed to a bone transport device and as such, an additional major procedure can be avoided.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates the successful management of large segmental femoral defects using a combination of the Masquelet technique and Ilizarov external fixation. Both techniques have individually proven effective in treating complex bone defects, but their combination offers several advantages. The Masquelet technique provides a biologically favorable environment through the formation of an induced membrane, which promotes bone healing and helps control infection, while the Ilizarov external fixator offers a more stable construct than a monolateral device, allowing for early mobilization and reducing the risk of mechanical failure.

By integrating the strengths of both methods, this combined approach addresses the limitations inherent in each technique. Although internal fixation, such as distal femoral locking plates or retrograde intramedullary nails, is the most commonly used method in combination with the Masquelet technique, external fixation provides a viable alternative in cases where internal fixation is not feasible. This includes situations with a high risk of infection or severe bone loss that prevents adequate distal fixation of the implants. Moreover, the Ilizarov frame offers the flexibility to be transformed to a bone transport device if the Masquelet technique alone is insufficient, potentially avoiding the need for additional major procedures.

Overall, the results of the two cases presented indicate that this combined treatment protocol can effectively restore bone continuity, promote healing and improve functional outcomes in patients with post-traumatic large femoral defects. This approach could be considered a valuable addition to the armamentarium for managing complex, high-risk fractures where both biological reconstruction and mechanical stability are crucial.
