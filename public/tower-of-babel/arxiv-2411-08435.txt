Dynamic Programming–Compatible Uncertainty Sets in Robust Markov Decision Processes
Julien Grand-Clément, Nian Si, Shengbo Wang

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate the compatibility of robust Markov Decision Processes (RMDPs) with dynamic programming under various assumptions on the uncertainty set, i.e., we investigate when one can solve an RMDP by solving a fixed point equation. We show that in all generality, s-rectangular and sa-rectangular uncertainty sets are the only models of uncertainty that are compatible with dynamic programming. Our analysis shows that existing non-rectangular models, including r-rectangularity, are only weakly compatible with dynamic programming, as they require the assumption that rewards do not depend on the next state. In this case, our results imply that one can always construct a rectangular uncertainty set that is equivalent, for both policy evaluation and optimization, to the dynamic programming-compatible non-rectangular model. This highlights a key limitation: dynamic-programming-compatible non-rectangular uncertainty sets, although practically relevant for uncertainty quantification, do not provide a genuinely distinct assessment of policy performance. Interestingly, our proof techniques rely on identifying a novel simultaneous solvability property, which we show is central to several important properties of RMDPs, including the existence of stationary optimal policies and dynamic programming-based formulations. The simultaneous solvability property enables a unified approach to studying all existing models of uncertainty, rectangular and non-rectangular alike.

Introduction

Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) are a framework for modeling sequential decision problems
[
26
]
, where the decision-maker optimizes for the total return obtained over a given horizon (potentially infinite). When the model parameters are known, finding an optimal policy can be done efficiently with value iteration, policy iteration, linear programming or gradient descent. However, it has been observed repeatedly that misestimating the model parameters may lead to severe deterioration of the performance of the computed policies
[
6
,
9
]
.
Robust
MDPs ameliorate this issue by considering a pessimistic formulation and optimizing for a policy that maximizes the worst-case return over the
uncertainty set
, which represents the set of all plausible scenarios for the realizations of the parameters’ values
[
18
,
25
,
34
]
.

In all generality, i.e. when no assumption is made on the uncertainty set, even the
policy evaluation
problem is computationally difficult, i.e., computing the worst-case of a stationary policy may be strongly NP-hard
[
34
,
9
]
, and optimal policies may have to be history-dependent
[
34
]
.
Therefore, an active line of research in the literature on robust MDPs focuses on finding
sufficient
conditions on the uncertainty set such that the corresponding RMDP problem is compatible with dynamic programming, i.e., can be solved by computing the fixed point of a contracting operator.
The seminal papers
[
18
,
25
]
prove that robust MDPs are compatible with dynamic programming under the
sa-rectangularity
assumption, which states that the uncertainty set is a Cartesian product over all pairs of state-action. This means that the transition probabilities out of every state-action pair can be chosen independently. The authors in
[
34
]
show that robust MDPs are compatible with dynamic programming under the more general assumption of
s-rectangularity
, where the uncertainty set is a Cartesian product over all states, i.e., the transition probabilities can be chosen independently across different states. The sa-rectangular and s-rectangular models of uncertainty are by far the most widely used in the literature, and non-rectangular models of uncertainty have been considered difficult to solve, but recent work has highlighted the existence of other interesting models of uncertainty.

In particular,
[
9
]
introduce the
r-rectangularity assumption
that generalizes sa-rectangularity and assumes that the transition probabilities are convex combinations of some underlying factors that can be chosen independently. The authors in
[
9
]
show that for the r-rectangular model of uncertainty, the policy evaluation problem is compatible with dynamic programming but only in a weak sense, i.e.,
when the rewards only depend on the current state-action pair
and not on the next state. Under the same assumption on the rewards,
[
10
]
show that stationary deterministic optimal policies exist and can be computed efficiently by dynamic programming for convex compact r-rectangular uncertainty sets.
In the case of finite horizon problems, k-rectangular models
[
24
]
require history-dependent optimal policies that can be computed efficiently via state augmentation.
Another recent model of uncertainty is the

(\xi,\eta)
-rectangular uncertainty set

[
17
]
, a low-rank model where the transitions depend on some underlying factors and some coefficients that may both vary (with constraints across factors). The authors in
[
17
]
study the computational complexity of the worst-case Bellman updates in this case, without focusing on the properties of optimal policies or the compatibility with dynamic programming.

In this paper, we will use the term
rectangular
models to designate s-rectangular or sa-rectangular models of uncertainty, and
non-rectangular
models for any other models of uncertainty, e.g. r-rectangular models. We note that this is the original meaning of the word
rectangularity
as introduced in
[
18
,
25
]
, which describe a set that is based on a Cartesian product over the set of states.

We note that the compatibility with dynamic programming, i.e. the existence of fixed point equations describing the optimal solutions, is central to virtually all current algorithms for solving robust MDPs. Dynamic programming is not an algorithm per se, but it enables the computation of optimal value functions via value iteration (corresponding to simply iterating the Bellman operator, as shown in the seminal papers
[
18
,
25
,
34
]
), policy iteration
[
15
]
, and even convex programming (via the contraction lemma, e.g.
[
25
]
or Lemma 3.1 in
[
11
]
). Similarly, the recent advances in policy mirror descent for robust MDPs
[
33
,
22
]
are based on dynamic programming equations for the worst-case value functions (e.g., Section 2 in
[
33
]
or Proposition 2.2 in
[
21
]
). We do not claim that solving a robust MDP problem is impossible when dynamic programming does not hold: one can resort to approximation methods in general
[
20
]
or design algorithms assuming some specific structure for the uncertainty sets, e.g. normed-based uncertainty
[
19
]
.

Overall, the picture is quite mixed regarding the compatibility of robust MDPs with dynamic programming under different models of uncertainty: several models are currently known, some leading to robust MDPs compatible with dynamic programming in all generality (s-rectangularity and sa-rectangularity), some leading to robust MDPs compatible with dynamic programming only under some assumptions on the rewards (r-rectangularity), some for which this question has not been studied (
(\xi,\eta)
-rectangularity), some for which this question has only been studied for finite horizon (k-rectangularity), and the proof techniques appear quite different across the papers introducing the different models without any obvious connections.
This raises the following research question:

XXXXX 1

(Research Question.)

What are the models of uncertainty for which robust MDPs are compatible with dynamic programming? Is there a unifying approach to studying the compatibility with dynamic programming of all existing uncertainty models?

Answering this research question is the main objective of this paper. Since policy evaluation plays a central role in robust MDPs, we will focus on obtaining
necessary and sufficient
conditions for the policy evaluation problem to be solvable by dynamic programming. If this is the case in all generality, we will say that the uncertainty set is
“compatible with dynamic programming”
; if this is the case when the rewards are independent of the next state, we will say that the uncertainty set is
“weakly compatible with dynamic programming”
.
To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first to focus on
necessary and sufficient
conditions, which means that we will be able to characterize
all
uncertainty sets compatible with dynamic programming, in contrast to prior work focusing on
sufficient
conditions and introducing a single model of uncertainty per paper, as in
[
18
,
25
]
(studying sa-rectangularity), in
[
34
]
(studying s-rectangularity) and in
[
9
,
10
]
(studying r-rectangularity).
Our
main contributions
are as follows.

Rectangular MDPs are the only models compatible with dynamic programming.

We show that in all generality, only s-rectangularity and sa-rectangularity are compatible with dynamic programming. Our proof techniques relate dynamic programming with the
simultaneous solvability property
of a collection of optimization problems over the uncertainty set. These optimization problems appear from the Bellman operator, which operates over the value functions as vectors indexed by the set of states, thereby involving a different optimization problem for each state of the MDP instance. This provides a unifying view on the underlying reasons behind the compatibility (or incompatibility) of uncertainty models with dynamic programming.

Weakly compatible RMDPs in some special cases.

We then study the weak compatibility of robust MDPs with dynamic programming, i.e. under the assumption that the rewards do not depend on the next state. We prove the equivalence between weak compatibility and a particular weaker form of the simultaneous solvability property. Our analysis shows that solving policy evaluation via dynamic programming has several implications, including the ability to find the best stationary policies and, when the uncertainty set is convex, to find optimal policies (among all policies), as well as an equivalence between RMDP instances with stationary and non-stationary adversaries. We then describe several new weakly compatible non-rectangular uncertainty sets not previously considered in the literature, a surprising result given that before our work, there was a paucity of weakly compatible uncertainty sets known (only three were known: sa-rectangularity, s-rectangularity, and r-rectangularity).

New perspectives on robust MDPs.

Overall, our results answer several important open questions in the literature on robust MDPs and shed new light on important aspects of RMDPs, that were previously poorly understood. We summarize some of our main takeaways here:

•

We emphasize the crucial role of rectangular models for robust MDPs, by proving that it is hopeless to search for other uncertainty models that can be solved via dynamic programming
in all generality
(Theorem
3.4
and Theorem
3.7
).

•

We show that the compatibility of policy evaluation with dynamic programming implies that we can efficiently compute optimal stationary policies (Theorem
4.4
), emphasizing that the gist of the difficulty in solving robust MDPs (a max-min problem) lies in solving the policy evaluation problem (the inner minimization problem). We also highlight that the existence of a stationary optimal policy is a consequence of strong duality (Lemma
4.9
) and provides a very general equivalence result for RMDPs with stationary and non-stationary adversaries (Theorem
4.13
).

•

In future work on robust MDPs, the (weak) compatibility of new models of uncertainty can be verified directly using our necessary and sufficient simultaneous solvability properties as in (
25
) (in all generality) and as in (
39
) (under the assumption that the rewards do not depend on the next states).
From a practical standpoint, these conditions are simple enough for new uncertainty sets to be (weakly) compatible “by design”, i.e. to be built purposely to satisfy (
25
) or (
39
).

•

Non-rectangular models were originally introduced to overcome the conservativeness of the rectangularity assumption. Crucially, we prove that policy evaluation over an important class of non-rectangular models of uncertainty (r-rectangularity) can actually be solved by solving policy evaluation
over their s-rectangular or sa-rectangular extensions
, i.e. over the smallest s-rectangular or sa-rectangular uncertainty sets containing the non-rectangular uncertainty set.
This shows that the only known non-rectangular models are, in fact,
equivalent to rectangular models
, which undermines the practical need for non-rectangular models and the fact that they may be more general than rectangular models.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. We provide important notations in the remainder of this section. In Section
2
we introduce some preliminaries on robust MDPs and several important models of uncertainty. In Section
3
we study the compatibility of uncertainty sets with dynamic programming in all generality. In Section
4
we study the weak compatibility of uncertainty sets (i.e., under some structural assumption on the rewards). Section
5
provides some discussion on the main takeaways of our paper.

2
Preliminaries on robust MDPs

A robust Markov Decision Process instance is given by the tuple
\mathcal{M}=\left(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\bm{r},\gamma,\bm{\mu},\mathcal{P}\right)
, where
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
are the finite sets of states and actions, with cardinality
S:={\sf Card}(\mathcal{S}),A:={\sf Card}(\mathcal{A})
. With a slight abuse of notation, we identify the set
\mathcal{S}
with
\{1,...,S\}
and the set
\mathcal{A}
with
\{1,...,A\}
. We note
\bm{r}=(r_{sas^{\prime}})\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times A\times S}
for the instantaneous reward obtained at each period. Without loss of generality, we assume that every action is admissible at every state. The scalar
\gamma\in[0,1)
is the
discount factor
, which models the diminishing importance of the rewards obtained over time, and the vector
\bm{\mu}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is the initial distribution over the set of states. The set
\mathcal{P}
is the uncertainty set, which models all the plausible realizations of the transition probabilities: it is a subset of the set of all transition probabilities, i.e.
\mathcal{P}\subset\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{S\times A}=\{\left(\bm{P}_{sa}\right)_{sa}\;|\;\bm{P}_{sa}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}),\forall\;(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\}
. The set of
histories
up to period
t\in\mathbb{N}
is defined as
\mathcal{H}_{t}:=\{(s_{0},a_{0},s_{1},a_{1},...,s_{t-1},a_{t-1},s_{t})\;|\;(s_{\tau},a_{\tau})\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A},\forall\;\tau\leq t-1,s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}\}=\left(\times_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\right)\times\mathcal{S}
and the set of history-dependent policies
\Pi_{\sf H}
is the set of maps from the set of all finite histories
\cup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{H}_{t}
to
\Delta(\mathcal{A})
.
Given a robust MDP instance, the goal of the decision-maker is to find an optimal policy, defined as a solution to the following optimization problem:

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\inf_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}

(1)

where
\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
is the value function associated with a pair of policy-transitions
(\pi,\bm{P})\in\Pi_{\sf H}\times\mathcal{P}
, whose component gives the expected cumulative discounted return obtained starting from any state:

v^{\pi,\bm{P}}_{s}=\mathbb{E}^{\pi,\bm{P}}\left[\left.\sum_{t=0}^{+\infty}\gamma^{t}r_{s_{t}a_{t}s_{t+1}}\;\right|\;s_{0}=s\right],\forall\;s\in\mathcal{S}.

(2)

Note that in (
1
), the transition probabilities do not change over time; we discuss the case of time-varying transition probabilities in Section
4.1.1
. We will also use the notation
R(\pi,\bm{P}):=\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}
for the expected discounted return.
The set of
stationary policies
that only depend on the current state is denoted by
\Pi_{\sf S}:=\Delta(\mathcal{A})^{S}
.
Note that when
(\pi,\bm{P})\in\Pi_{\sf S}\times\mathcal{P}
, the vector
\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}
is the unique fixed point of the contraction operator
T^{\pi}_{\bm{P}}:\mathbb{R}^{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{S}
, defined as, for
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
,

T^{\pi}_{\bm{P}}(\bm{v})_{s}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{sa}\bm{P}_{sa}^{\top}\left(\bm{r}_{sa}+\gamma\bm{v}\right),\forall\;s\in\mathcal{S}.

(3)

Solving the policy evaluation problem.

Following the terminology from
[
34
]
, the
policy evaluation
problem (
4
) consists in computing the worst-case value obtained by a stationary policy, i.e. in solving, for
\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}
, the optimization problem:

\inf_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}

(4)

Being able to efficiently solve the policy evaluation problem for all stationary policies is a desirable and important property for the practical use of a model of uncertainty. For instance,
[
9
]
use robust MDPs to evaluate the worst-case cost between two existing screening guidelines for colorectal cancer; for this purpose, it is sufficient to be able to solve (
4
). Solving the policy evaluation problem also arises as a subroutine in recently proposed gradient descent algorithms for robust MDPs, e.g. Algorithm 1 in
[
30
]
or Algorithm 1 in
[
21
]
.

The authors in
[
34
,
9
]
show that without any assumption on the set
\mathcal{P}
, solving (
4
) is NP-hard. In the
sa-rectangular
model of uncertainty
[
18
,
25
]
, the set
\mathcal{P}
can be written as a Cartesian product over all state-action pairs
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
:

\mathcal{P}=\times_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{P}_{sa}

where
\mathcal{P}_{sa}\subset\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is the set of marginals induced by
\mathcal{P}
when only considering the transition probabilities out of the pair
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
:

\mathcal{P}_{sa}:=\{\bm{p}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})\;|\;\exists\;\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P},\bm{P}_{sa}=\bm{p}\}.

(5)

The authors in
[
18
,
25
]
show that under the sa-rectangularity assumption, the optimization problem (
4
) can be solved efficiently when the set
\mathcal{P}
is compact, and that in this case we have

\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}=\bm{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{u}$}}}^{\pi}

(6)

where
\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{u}$}}}^{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
is the unique vector satisfying the following dynamic programming equations:

\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$u$}}}^{\pi}_{s}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{sa}\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{P}_{sa}^{\top}\left(\bm{r}_{sa}+\gamma\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{u}$}}}^{\pi}\right),\forall\;s\in\mathcal{S}.

Importantly, under the sa-rectangularity assumption, an optimal policy in (
1
) exists and can be chosen stationary and deterministic.

There are two generalizations of sa-rectangularity. In the case of
r-rectangularity

[
9
,
10
]
, the transition probabilities in
\mathcal{P}
are assumed to be convex combinations of the same underlying
r
factors for some integer
r\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e., there exists some
coefficients

\bm{u}_{sa}\in\Delta([r])
for
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, some
factors

\bm{w}^{i}\in\mathcal{W}^{i}
for
i\in[r]
, and some sets
\mathcal{W}^{i}\subset\Delta(\mathcal{S})
such that

\mathcal{P}=\left\{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}u^{i}_{sa}\bm{w}^{i}\right)_{sa}\;|\;(\bm{w}^{1},...,\bm{w}^{r})\in\mathcal{W}^{1}\times...\mathcal{W}^{r}\right\}.

(7)

Any sa-rectangular uncertainty set can be reformulated as an r-rectangular uncertainty set with
r=S\times A
(Proposition 2.1 in
[
10
]
). Under the additional assumption that the reward is independent of the next state:
r_{sas^{\prime}}=r_{sas^{\prime\prime}}
for all
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
and
s^{\prime},s^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
,
[
10
]
show that one can still solve the policy iteration problem by a fixed point argument and that an optimal policy in (
1
) can be chosen stationary and deterministic. In fact, we will show in Section
4
that for compact r-rectangular uncertainty sets, the dynamic programming equation (
6
) still holds, simplifying the approach taken in
[
9
,
10
]
.

A second generalization of sa-rectangularity is
s-rectangularity
, introduced in
[
34
]
. In this model,
\mathcal{P}
is equal to the Cartesian product of its marginals across each state
s
:

\mathcal{P}=\times_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{P}_{s}

with, for each
s\in\mathcal{S}
,

\mathcal{P}_{s}:=\left\{\left(\bm{p}_{a}\right)_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{A}\;|\;\exists\;\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P},\left(\bm{P}_{sa}\right)_{a\in\mathcal{A}}=\left(\bm{p}_{a}\right)_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\right\}.

(8)

For compact s-rectangular uncertainty sets,
[
34
]
prove that

\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}=\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{u}^{\pi}

(9)

where
\bm{u}^{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
is defined as the unique vector satisfying the following fixed point equations:

u^{\pi}_{s}=\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{sa}\bm{P}_{sa}^{\top}\left(\bm{r}_{sa}+\gamma\bm{u}^{\pi}\right),\forall\;s\in\mathcal{S}.

(10)

It is important to note that
[
34
]
show that (
9
) holds for s-rectangular uncertainty
without any structural assumption on the rewards
, in stark contrast to the case of r-rectangular uncertainty. We also note that
[
34
]
show the existence of a stationary optimal solution to the robust MDP problem for compact convex s-rectangular uncertainty, which can be computed efficiently via value iteration
[
34
]
, policy iteration
[
15
]
, or convex programming
[
11
]
.

The rectangular extensions of an uncertainty set.

Overall, we note that for all the widely-studied models of uncertainty, the policy evaluation (
4
) can be solved efficiently by computing the fixed point of a certain operator:

•

For compact sa-rectangular models and compact r-rectangular models (the latter requiring the additional assumption that the rewards are independent of the next state), one can solve the policy evaluation problem (
4
) by leveraging (
6
), i.e., by computing the fixed point
\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$u$}}}^{\pi}
of the contracting operator
\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$T$}}}^{\pi}:\mathbb{R}^{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{S}
, defined as, for
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
,

\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$T$}}}^{\pi}(\bm{v})_{s}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{sa}\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{P}_{sa}^{\top}\left(\bm{r}_{sa}+\gamma\bm{v}\right),\forall\;s\in\mathcal{S}.

(11)

•

For s-rectangular models, one can solve the policy evaluation problem (
4
) by leveraging (
9
), i.e., by computing the fixed point
\bm{u}^{\pi}
of the contracting operator
T^{\pi}:\mathbb{R}^{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{S}
, defined for
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
,

T^{\pi}(\bm{v})_{s}=\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{sa}\bm{P}_{sa}^{\top}\left(\bm{r}_{sa}+\gamma\bm{v}\right),\forall\;s\in\mathcal{S}.

(12)

We note that from the definition of the operators
T^{\pi}_{\bm{P}},\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$T$}}}^{\pi}
and
T^{\pi}
, we always have

\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{u}$}}}^{\pi}\leq\bm{u}^{\pi}\leq\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}},\forall\;(\pi,\bm{P})\in\Pi_{\sf S}\times\mathcal{P}.

(13)

Equation (
13
) follows from classical arguments based on the properties of the operators
T^{\pi},\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$T$}}}^{\pi}
and
T^{\pi}_{\bm{P}}
, see Appendix
B
for completeness. Since
\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$T$}}}^{\pi}
and
T^{\pi}
are always contractions, irrespective of the rectangularity properties of
\mathcal{P}
, their fixed point can be computed efficiently via fixed point iterations, among other algorithms (see more details in Section
4.1.1
and Appendix
C
).
We note that Equality (
6
) and Equality (
9
) are related to the recently introduced
dynamic programming principle
framework
[
31
]
, which relates the optimization problem (
1
) in the case of s-rectangular and sa-rectangular uncertainty sets, and dynamic programming formulations for stationary or history-dependent decision-maker and transition probabilities. Our analysis in this paper will focus specifically on the relation of the policy evaluation problem (
4
) and dynamic programming formulations, for general uncertainty sets.

Even though r-rectangular uncertainty sets may not be sa-rectangular, it is important to note that since (
6
) holds, we can solve policy evaluation problems over an r-rectangular uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}
by solving policy evaluation problems over its
sa-rectangular extension

\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}
, i.e. with
\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}
defined as

\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}=\times_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{P}_{sa}

for
\mathcal{P}_{sa}\subset\Delta(\mathcal{S})
as defined in (
5
). We can define the analogous notion of
s-rectangular extension
of an uncertainty set, as

\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}=\times_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{P}_{s}

(14)

with
\mathcal{P}_{s}
as defined in (
8
). Note that we always have
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}\subseteq\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}

with
\mathcal{P}=\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}
if and only if
\mathcal{P}
is s-rectangular, and

DP-Compatible uncertainty sets.

We focus on the class of uncertainty sets for which we can solve policy evaluation by dynamic programming. We will distinguish two notions of compatibility with dynamic programming, depending on whether the policy evaluation problem (
4
) can be solved via its s-rectangular extension (as in (
9
)) or via its sa-rectangular extension (as in (
6
)). Since the compatibility properties of policy evaluation will follow entirely from the properties of the uncertainty sets, with a slight abuse of language, we will say that the uncertainty sets themselves are
compatible
(with dynamic programming).

Definition 2.3

(DP-compatible Uncertainty Sets)

Let
\mathcal{P}
be an uncertainty set.

1.

The set
\mathcal{P}
is s-DP-compatible if Equation (
9
) holds for all policies
\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}
and for all choices of the rewards
\bm{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times A\times S}
, the initial distribution
\bm{\mu}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
and the discount factor
\gamma\in[0,1)
.

2.

The set
\mathcal{P}
is sa-DP-compatible if Equation (
6
) holds for all policies
\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}
and for all choices of the rewards
\bm{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times A\times S}
, the initial distribution
\bm{\mu}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
and the discount factor
\gamma\in[0,1)
.

In the rest of the paper, we will use the term
DP-compatibility
if we refer to s-DP-compatibility or sa-DP-compatibility indifferently.
Note that in Definition
2.3
, Equation (
6
) or Equation (
9
) must hold under all choices of the MDP parameters
(\bm{r},\bm{\mu},\gamma)
. Indeed, if one is willing to consider only specific values of the MDP parameters, several corner cases allow for very simple solutions for the policy evaluation problem, e.g. when the rewards are all zero (
\bm{r}=\bm{0}
), or when
\gamma=0
and the rewards do not depend on the next states. For a model of uncertainty to be useful in all applications, it is crucial that we can
always
solve the policy evaluation problem, not only in some special cases.
Equipped with the concept of compatible uncertainty sets, we can reformulate the main research question of this paper, outlined in the introduction:

{resq}

Can we fully characterize the class of uncertainty sets that are sa-DP-compatible? Can we fully characterize the class of uncertainty sets that are s-DP-compatible?

In the next section, we will provide a complete answer to this research question in the most general case (without any assumption of the rewards). We then treat the case of
weak DP-compatibility
in Section
4
, i.e., the case where the rewards are independent of the next state. By doing so, we also provide a unifying approach to all existing models of uncertainty in the literature.

The following proposition shows the relation that sa-DP-compatibility implies s-DP-compatibility, mirroring the fact that sa-rectangularity implies s-rectangularity.

Proposition 2.4

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a compact sa-DP-compatible uncertainty set. Then
\mathcal{P}
is s-DP-compatible and
\bm{u}^{\pi}=\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{u}$}}}^{\pi}
for any
\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}
.

Proof 2.5

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.
Let
\mathcal{P}
be compact and sa-DP-compatible. Let
\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}
and
\bm{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times A\times S},\gamma\in[0,1),\bm{\mu}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
.
We have

\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}=\bm{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{u}$}}}^{\pi}\leq\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{u}^{\pi}\leq\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}

where the equality comes from sa-DP-compatibility, and the inequalities come from (
13
). Therefore, all terms above are equal and
\mathcal{P}
is s-DP-compatible. Since
\bm{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{u}$}}}^{\pi}=\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{u}^{\pi}
for all
\bm{\mu}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
, we have
\bm{u}^{\pi}=\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{u}$}}}^{\pi}
.

\square

We conclude this section with the next remarks about other models of robust MDPs.

Remark 2.6

(RMDPs with other objective functions.)

This paper focuses on RMDPs with discounted return, i.e. on the case where the value function is defined as in (
2
). While this is the most widely studied RMDP model, it is worth highlighting that when the discount factor is close to
1
, computing an optimal policy for RMDPs with discounted returns may be difficult, since the complexity of the main algorithms (e.g. value iteration) scales as
O(1/(1-\gamma))

[
34
]
, leading to very long solving times when
\gamma
approaches 1. Other models of RMDPs, without discounting, include finite horizon MDPs (e.g. section 3 in
[
25
]
) or the case of average return where the value function is the gain, defined as the limit of the running-average of the rewards, which has seen recent progress
[
12
,
32
]
after the seminal work
[
29
]
. However, for average return RMDPs, a Bellman equation has been obtained only for unichain instances
[
32
]
. We leave studying our main research question (Research Question
2
) for average return RMDPs as an interesting future direction.

Remark 2.7

(RMDPs with optimistic objectives.)

There exists a variant of robust MDPs where the decision-maker optimizes for the best-case return over the set
\mathcal{P}
, i.e., where one wants to solve

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\sup_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}.

(15)

This problem is considered for bounded parameter MDPs
[
8
]
.
Variants of (
15
) with the average return and sa-rectangular uncertainty sets are studied in
[
29
]
and used as subroutines in extended value iteration
[
1
]
and KLUCRL
[
7
]
. The corresponding optimistic policy evaluation for (
15
) is to solve, for each
\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}
, the optimization program

\sup_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}

(16)

Because of the homogeneity of the expectation (appearing in the definition of the value functions as in (
2
)), it is clear that we can turn the optimistic policy evaluation problem (
16
) into a corresponding policy evaluation problem (
4
) by flipping the signs of the rewards
\bm{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times A\times S}
. Because of this, all of our results for the (classical) policy evaluation problem (
4
) also hold for the optimistic policy evaluation problem (
16
).

3
DP-compatible uncertainty sets

In this section, we show that, in general, the only DP-compatible models of uncertainty are the s-rectangular and the sa-rectangularity models of uncertainty. Our main results are based on reformulating DP-compatibility as an equivalent
simultaneous solvability property
, which unifies most of the existing approaches to studying dynamic programming for robust MDPs.
We start with our results for s-rectangular uncertainty and then provide the analogous results for sa-rectangular uncertainty.

s-DP-compatible uncertainty sets.

We start with the following theorem, which provides
necessary and sufficient
conditions for (
9

Theorem 3.1

Let
\mathcal{P}
be compact.
The following statements are equivalent.

1.

The set
\mathcal{P}
is s-DP-compatible:

\forall\;\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S},\forall\;\bm{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times A\times S},\forall\;\gamma\in[0,1),\forall\;\bm{\mu}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S}),\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}=\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{u}^{\pi}

\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}

Theorem
3.1
provides
necessary and sufficient
conditions for Equation (

\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{P}$}}}\in\mathcal{P}
such that
\bm{u}^{\pi}=\bm{v}^{\pi,\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{P}$}}}}
. Since the operator

involves a minimization over
\mathcal{P}

independently

, Equation (

(SSP), i.e. to the property that a feasible point in
\mathcal{P}
can be recovered as an optimal solution of multiple optimization problems, each of them involving the marginals of
\mathcal{P}
across different

, as formulated in (

\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}
) but where the objective function only depends on the transition probabilities
\bm{P}_{s}
out of a single state

Uncertainty sets satisfying the SSP property (

It is straightforward to note that compact s-rectangular uncertainty sets always satisfy (

).
However, if we allow the uncertainty set to be nonconvex, some non-rectangular uncertainty sets may satisfy (

). We provide a simple example below.

Example 3.3

(Non s-rectangular sets may satisfy (
19
))

Assume that
\mathcal{S}=\{1,2\}
and that there is only one action to choose (
\mathcal{A}=\{1\}
). Then any uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}
is a subset of
\{(p,1-p),(q,1-q)\;|\;p,q\in[0,1]\}
where
(p,1-p)
is the transition probabilities out of State
1
and
(q,1-q)
is the transition probabilities out of State
2
. For the sake of conciseness, we write
\bm{P}=(p,q)
for
\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}
. For instance
\bm{P}=(0,0)
corresponds to deterministic transitions
1\rightarrow 2
and
2\rightarrow 1
.
Consider the following finite uncertainty set:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}=\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1),(1/2,1/2)\}.

Clearly,
\mathcal{P}
is not convex since it is finite. It is also not s-rectangular, because
(1/2,1/2)\in\mathcal{P}
and
(0,0)\in\mathcal{P}
but
(0,1/2)\notin\mathcal{P}
.
However (

\mathcal{P}
, since the point
(1/2,1/2)
is irrelevant from an optimization standpoint - it is never the unique minimizer of a linear form over
\mathcal{P}
, and
\mathcal{P}\setminus\{(1/2,1/2)\}
is s-rectangular.

As highlighted in Example
3.3
, the SSP (

of
\mathcal{P}
, since it only involves minimization of linear objectives over
\mathcal{P}
. When
\mathcal{P}
is convex and compact, it is fully described by its set of extreme points
{\sf ext}(\mathcal{P})
, since in this case
\mathcal{P}={\sf conv}({\sf ext}(\mathcal{P}))
from the Krein-Milman theorem. Therefore, we can completely characterize the sets satisfying (

uncertainty sets. Our main theorem in this section shows that, in this case, the only s-DP-compatible uncertainty sets are s-rectangular.

Theorem 3.4

Assume that
\mathcal{P}
is convex and compact. Then

\eqref{eq:adv ssp rsas' - srec}\iff\mathcal{P}=\times_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{P}_{s}
,   for
\mathcal{P}_{s}
the marginals of

for state

, defined in (

We have already shown that we always have

is nonconvex).
Thus, the main difficulty in proving Theorem

is proving the other implication under the convexity and the compactness of the uncertainty set.

Proof 3.5

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.
We start by proving that
\times_{s\in\mathcal{S}}{\sf ext}(\mathcal{P}_{s})\subset\mathcal{P}.

Let
\left(\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{P}$}}}_{s}\right)_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\in\times_{s\in\mathcal{S}}{\sf ext}(\mathcal{P}_{s})
. We want to show that
\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{P}$}}}\in\mathcal{P}
.
From Straszewicz’s theorem
[
28
]
(see Theorem 18.6 in
[
27
]
), we know that for each

, there exists a sequence of exposed points
\left(\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{P}$}}}_{s}^{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and a sequence of vectors
\left(\bm{V}_{s}^{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{P}$}}}_{s}^{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{s}
,
\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{P}$}}}^{n}_{s}\rightarrow\hat{\bm{P}}_{s}
for

, and

\{\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{P}$}}}_{s}^{n}\}=\arg\min_{\bm{P}_{s}\in\mathcal{P}_{s}}\langle\bm{P}_{s},\bm{V}_{s}^{n}\rangle.

Let us consider
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We want to show that
\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{P}$}}}^{n}=\left(\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{P}$}}}_{s}^{n}\right)_{s\in\mathcal{S}}
is feasible in
\mathcal{P}
. From (
19
), we know that there exists
\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\bm{P}$}}}^{n}\in\mathcal{P}
such that

\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\bm{P}$}}}^{n}\in\cap_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\arg\min_{\bm{P}_{s}\in\mathcal{P}_{s}}\langle\bm{P}_{s},\bm{V}_{s}^{n}\rangle.

From the uniqueness of the minimizers in each of the optimization programs
\arg\min_{\bm{P}_{s}\in\mathcal{P}_{s}}\langle\bm{P}_{s},\bm{V}_{s}^{n}\rangle
for each
s\in\mathcal{S}
, we obtain that
\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\bm{P}$}}}_{s}^{n}=\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{P}$}}}_{s}^{n}
for each

, from which we conclude that
\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{P}$}}}^{n}=\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\bm{P}$}}}^{n}
and therefore that
\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{P}$}}}^{n}\in\mathcal{P}
.
Therefore, there exists a sequence
\left(\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{P}$}}}^{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{P}$}}}^{n}\in\mathcal{P}
for all

and

is compact, we conclude that
\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{P}$}}}\in\mathcal{P}
, which shows that
\times_{s\in\mathcal{S}}{\sf ext}(\mathcal{P}_{s})\subset\mathcal{P}
.
It is now straightforward to conclude since we have

\times_{s\in\mathcal{S}}{\sf ext}(\mathcal{P}_{s})\subset\mathcal{P}\Rightarrow{\sf conv}(\times_{s\in\mathcal{S}}{\sf ext}(\mathcal{P}_{s}))\subset{\sf conv}(\mathcal{P})\Rightarrow\times_{s\in\mathcal{S}}{\sf conv}({\sf ext}(\mathcal{P}_{s}))\subset\mathcal{P},

where the first implication follows from taking the convex hull on both sides of the first inclusion, and the second implication follows from the convexity of
\mathcal{P}
and from

for two sets

and

Since
\mathcal{P}
is convex and compact,

. Since it is always true that

Discussion.

Our results in this section provide a complete characterization of DP-compatible uncertainty sets, and show that only s-rectangular and sa-rectangular are DP-compatible
in all generality
, i.e., for all values of the MDP parameters. In retrospect, this result is natural, since the operator
T^{\pi}
and
\hat{T}^{\pi}
involves independent optimization programs over
\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}
for each
s\in\mathcal{S}
. This answers an important open question in the robust MDP literature, where new non-rectangular models of uncertainty are still being invented even in recent years, often without a clear understanding of the compatibility of these models with dynamic programming
[
17
]
. Our results show that it is hopeless to search for non-rectangular models that are compatible with dynamic programming
in all generality
. However, we will uncover several new non-rectangular models that are compatible with dynamic programming under some more restricted reward models in the next section.

We also provide a new understanding of the rectangularity property for robust MDPs. For the class of convex compact uncertainty sets, Theorem
3.4
and Theorem
3.7
show that s-rectangularity and sa-rectangularity can be interpreted as
simultaneous solvability properties
, which directly relates to the Bellman operators
T^{\pi}
and
\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$T$}}}^{\pi}
defined in Section
2
involving optimization problems over
\mathcal{P}
across different states and different state-action pairs. To the best of our knowledge, these reformulations of the rectangularity assumption are new, and they provide interesting insights on the underlying reason behind the DP-compatibility of s-rectangular and sa-rectangular models of uncertainty. We summarize the main takeaways of this section in Figure
1
.

4.1.1
Implications of weak s-DP-compatibility

The weak s-DP-compatibility has several important consequences.

Tractability of the policy evaluation problem.

When
\mathcal{P}
is weakly s-DP-compatible, solving
\displaystyle\inf_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}
, the policy evaluation problem over
\mathcal{P}
, boils down to solving the policy evaluation problem over
\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}
, the s-rectangular extension of
\mathcal{P}
:

\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}=\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}.

(26)

This is because, from Lemma
2.1
, we always have
\displaystyle\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{u}^{\pi}=\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}
, while weak s-DP-compatibility is exactly equivalent to
\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}=\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{u}^{\pi}
.

There is an extensive literature on solving the policy evaluation problem for s-rectangular models of uncertainty (e.g.
[
14
,
15
,
2
,
16
]
), and we refer to the seminal paper
[
34
]
for precise complexity statements (more precisely Section 3 in
[
34
]
). For the sake of completeness, we provide the description and complexity of the fixed point iteration algorithm (also called value iteration in our setting) in Appendix
C
.

Solving the max-min problem.

We show that under (
23
), one can efficiently compute the best policy in the class of stationary policies, i.e. one can efficiently solve

\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}}\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}

(27)

In particular, let us define the contracting operator
T:\mathbb{R}^{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{S}
as

T(\bm{v})_{s}:=\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}}\;T^{\pi}(\bm{v})_{s}=\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}}\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}T^{\pi}_{\bm{P}}(\bm{v})_{s},\forall\;\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{S},\forall\;s\in\mathcal{S}

(28)

and we define
\bm{u}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
as the unique fixed point of
T
:
\bm{u}^{\star}=T(\bm{u}^{\star})
. The operator
T
is the Bellman operator for the s-rectangular extension of
\mathcal{P}

[
34
]
. Note that since
\Pi_{\sf S}=\Delta(\mathcal{A})^{S}=\times_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\Delta(\mathcal{A})
and since the optimization problem for the s-th component of

only involves the s-th component of
\pi

\pi^{\star}\in\Pi_{\sf S}
such that
\bm{u}^{\star}=\bm{u}^{\pi^{\star}}

Theorem 4.4

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a compact uncertainty set and assume that (

\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}}\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}=\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{u}^{\star}.

Additionally, for any
\pi^{\star}\in\Pi_{\sf S}
such that
\bm{u}^{\star}=\bm{u}^{\pi^{\star}}
, we have

\pi^{\star}\in\arg\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}}\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}.

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\inf_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}=\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}}\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}.

Proof of Proposition

Recall that (

First, weak duality gives

\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{u}^{\star}\leq\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}.

To show the equality in the above equation, we show the existence of
\bm{P}^{\star}\in\mathcal{P}
such that

By definition of

u^{\star}_{s}=\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}}\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}T^{\pi}_{\bm{P}}(\bm{u}^{\star})_{s}.

Now recall that

\pi^{\star}\in\Pi_{\sf S}
such that for each

From the definition of

\cap_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\arg\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}T^{\pi^{\star}}_{\bm{P}}(\bm{u}^{\star})_{s}\neq\emptyset.

From the definition of the operator

is convex-concave (it is actually bilinear). Additionally, the sets

i.e., we have shown the existence of some

such that

The above equation can be interpreted as the Bellman equation for the MDP where the transition probabilities is
\bm{P}^{\star}
. Thus

\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{u}^{\star}=\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}^{\star}}\geq\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}

which concludes our proof.

\square

Remark 4.12

(The finite horizon case.)

We state all our results for the case of the infinite horizon, with values defined as in (
2
). It is straightforward to extend our results to the case of a finite horizon, i.e. to the case where the value functions are defined as

v^{\pi,\bm{P}}_{s}=\mathbb{E}^{\pi,\bm{P}}\left[\left.\sum_{t=0}^{H}\gamma^{t}r_{s_{t}a_{t}s_{t+1}}\;\right|\;s_{0}=s\right],\forall\;s\in\mathcal{S}

for some finite horizon
H\in\mathbb{N}
. To do so, one can consider a finite horizon MDP with copies of the state set at each epoch
t\in\{0,...,H\}
and consider that the states attained at period
H
are absorbing with a reward of
0
. Stationary policies and adversaries in this MDP interpreted as an infinite horizon decision problem corresponds to Markovian policies and Markovian adversaries in this MDP interpreted as a finite decision problem (without duplicating the state space). Therefore Theorem
4.8
shows that the existence of Markovian optimal policies in robust MDPs with finite horizon, when the uncertainty set is weakly s-DP-compatible.

We note that two important RMDP papers focus solely on the finite horizon case.
[
23
]
focus on a model called d-rectangularity, which exactly corresponds to r-rectangular uncertainty with a finite horizon. The authors in
[
24
]
consider a model called k-rectangularity, where conditioned on some transition probabilities out of any subset of states
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{S}
, the transition probabilities out of the other states in
\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
take at most
k
distinct values. For k-rectangular models, an optimal policy may be history-dependent, but one can compute it efficiently by augmenting the state space to keep track of the number of realizations of the values of the transition probabilities until the current period of decision. The results in
[
24
]
rely on the decision-maker observing the entire realizations of the transition values over a trajectory and the finiteness over the possible remaining transitions induced by the k-rectangularity property. These two properties are very particular to the finite horizon case, and for this reason, k-rectangular models are not part of the models of uncertainty covered by this paper.

History-dependent transitions are equivalent to stationary transitions.

We conclude this section by showing that stationary and
history-dependent
transition probabilities are equivalent under weak s-DP-compatibility, i.e., when (
23
) holds. History-dependent transitions correspond to transition probabilities that may change at different periods, and we follow
[
31
]
to define this notion in the RMDP setting. Formally, given an uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}\subset\Delta(\mathcal{S})^{A\times S}
(potentially non-rectangular),
the set
\mathcal{P}_{\sf H}
of
\mathcal{P}
-constrained history-dependent transition probabilities is defined as

\mathcal{P}_{\sf H}:=\{\left(\bm{P}_{t}\right)_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\;|\;\bm{P}_{t}(\cdot|s_{0},a_{0},\dots,a_{t-1},\cdot,\cdot)\in\mathcal{P},\;\forall(s_{0},a_{0},\dots,a_{t-1})\in(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A})^{t},\forall t\in\mathbb{N}\}.

(30)

The constraint

\bm{P}_{t}(\cdot|s_{0},a_{0},\dots,a_{t-1},\cdot,\cdot)\in\mathcal{P}

is interpreted as restricting the transition probabilities
\bm{P}_{t}
at period
t\in\mathbb{N}
to be chosen in
\mathcal{P}
but based only on the past state-action pairs up to period
t-1
:
(s_{0},a_{0},\ldots,a_{t-1})
, and not on the current state–action pair
(s_{t},a_{t})
.
We have the following theorem.

Theorem 4.13

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a compact convex uncertainty set and assume that weak s-DP-compatibility (
23
) holds. Let
\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}
. Then

\inf_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\sf H}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}=\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}.

(31)

Proof 4.14

Proof of Theorem
4.13
.
Assume that weak s-DP-compatibility holds. As highlighted in Equation (
26
), we know that
\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}=\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}
, with
\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}
the s-rectangular extension of
\mathcal{P}
, as defined in (
14
).
We then claim that

\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}=\inf_{\bm{P}\in\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}_{\sf H}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}},

(32)

where
\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}_{\sf H}
is the set of history-dependent transition probabilities over the s-rectangular extension
\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}
of the uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}
, with a definition analogous to (
30
):

\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}_{\sf H}:=\{\left(\bm{P}_{t}\right)_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\;|\;\bm{P}_{t}(\cdot|s_{0},a_{0},\dots,a_{t-1},\cdot,\cdot)\in\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}},\;\forall(s_{0},a_{0},\dots,a_{t-1})\in(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A})^{t},\forall t\in\mathbb{N}\}.

(33)

Equation (
32
) holds because
\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}
is s-rectangular, so that we actually also have

\displaystyle\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}_{\sf H}=\{\left(\bm{P}_{t}\right)_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\;|

\displaystyle\bm{P}_{t}(\cdot|s_{0},a_{0},\dots,a_{t-1},s_{t},\cdot)\in\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}_{s_{t}},\;\forall(s_{0},a_{0},\dots,a_{t-1})\in(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A})^{t},

(34)

\displaystyle\forall\;s_{t}\in\mathcal{S},\forall\;t\in\mathbb{N}\}

i.e., in
\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}_{\sf H}
we can assume that the transition probabilities are chosen after observing the state
s_{t}
.

The authors in
[
15
]
prove that we can interpret
\inf_{\bm{P}\in\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}_{\sf H}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}
as an adversarial MDP defined as follows (see Definition 4.1 in
[
15
]
): the set of states is
\mathcal{S}
and the set of actions available at each state
s\in\mathcal{S}
is
\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}_{s}
. The initial probability distribution is
\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
and the discount factor is
\gamma\in[0,1)
. The instantaneous reward obtained for choosing action
P_{s}\in\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}_{s}
in state
s\in\mathcal{S}
is
-\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{sa}P_{sa}^{\top}r_{sa}\in\mathbb{R}
. Recall that by convention MDPs are maximizing return, so we use a minus sign in the definition of the instantaneous rewards of the adversarial MDP to turn the minimization problem over
P
into a maximization problem over
P
:
\inf_{\bm{P}\in\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}_{\sf H}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}=\sup_{\bm{P}\in\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}_{\sf H}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}(-\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}})
, and
-\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}}
is the value function when choosing policy
P\in\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}_{\sf H}
in this adversarial MDP. The probability to transition from state
s
to state
s^{\prime}
after choosing action
P_{s}\in\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}_{s}
is
\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{sa}P_{sas^{\prime}}\in[0,1]
. Note that the set of states and the initial distribution of the adversarial MDP coincide with those of the original nominal MDP, but the set of actions available at each state is now compact (compared to finite action sets for the original nominal MDP).

Standard results for discounted MDPs with compact convex action sets, a finite state set, and continuous rewards and transitions show that in this adversarial MDP, an optimal policy may be chosen stationary (e.g., we are in the setup where Assumptions 6.0.1-6.0.4 of Chapter 6 in the classical textbook
[
26
]
hold, so Theorem 6.2.7 in
[
26
]
applies). A history-dependent policy in this adversarial MDP is an element in
\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}_{\sf H}
, and a stationary policy in this MDP is an element in
\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}
. Therefore, the existence of an optimal stationary policy in the adversarial MDP means that minimizing the discounted return
P\mapsto\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,P}
over
\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}_{\sf H}
(the set of history-dependent policies) yields the same objective value as minimizing the discounted return over
\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}
(the set of stationary policies).
This means that

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{P}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}=\min_{\bm{P}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{\sf H}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}
, i.e., that Equation (
32
) holds. We refer to Section 4 in
[
15
]
for more context on the interpretation of the policy evaluation problem as an adversarial MDP when the uncertainty set is s-rectangular.

Finally, since
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}
we also have
\mathcal{P}_{\sf H}\subseteq\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}_{\sf H}
, hence

\inf_{\bm{P}\in\mathbf{\widetilde{\text{$\mathcal{P}$}}}_{\sf H}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}\leq\inf_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\sf H}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}.

Overall we have shown that
\displaystyle\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}\leq\inf_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\sf H}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}
, while the reverse inequality is always true since
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\sf H}
(identifying elements in
\mathcal{P}
with elements in
\mathcal{P}_{\sf H}
that remain constant across time), from which we can conclude that
\displaystyle\inf_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\sf H}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}=\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}
.

\square

The existence of stationary optimal policies (for which we require convex uncertainty sets satisfying the weak SSP, as from Theorem
4.8
) implies the following theorem.

Theorem 4.15

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a compact convex uncertainty set and assume that weak s-DP-compatibility (
23
) holds. Then

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\inf_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\sf H}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}.

Proof 4.16

Proof of Theorem
4.15
.
We have

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}=\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}}\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}=\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}}\inf_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\sf H}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}\leq\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\inf_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\sf H}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}

where the first equality is from Corollary
4.6
, the second equality is from Theorem
4.13
, and the inequality is from
\Pi_{\sf S}\subset\Pi_{\sf H}
.
This implies that
\displaystyle\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\inf_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\sf H}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}
.

\square

The questions addressed in our last two theorems have been studied in several recent papers for the case of s-rectangular models

,

]
. Theorem
4.13
provides a generalization to any uncertainty set satisfying the weak SSP, and enables a concise and unifying proof.

4.1.2
Uncertainty sets satisfying the weak SSP

We now describe a large class of uncertainty sets satisfying weak DP-compatibility, generalizing all known weakly DP-compatible models of uncertainty (s-rectangular, sa-rectangular, r-rectangular models). Given the results in Theorem
4.2
, one can obtain the weak DP-compatibility (and all its consequences as listed in Section
4.1.1
) of a model of uncertainty “by design”, i.e. by ensuring that the weak SSP (
25
) holds.
In particular, we consider the class of uncertainty sets where we can write
\bm{P}_{sa}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}u^{i}_{sa}\bm{w}^{i}
but where both the factors
\bm{w}^{1},...,\bm{w}^{r}

and
the coefficients
\bm{u}_{s}=\left(u^{i}_{sa}\right)_{ia}
for
s\in\mathcal{S}
may vary and belong to sets that are Cartesian product.

Definition 4.17

(Generalized rectangularity)

Let
\mathcal{P}
be an uncertainty set. We say that
\mathcal{P}
is generalized rectangular if we can write

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=\left\{\left.\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}u^{i}_{sa}\bm{w}_{i}\right)_{sa}\right|(\bm{w}^{1},\ldots,\bm{w}^{r})\in\mathcal{W},(\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{S})\in\mathcal{U}\right\},

(35)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}

\displaystyle=\times_{i\in[r]}\mathcal{W}^{i},\mathcal{W}^{i}\subset\Delta(\mathcal{S})\forall\;i\in[r],

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle=\times_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{U}^{s},\mathcal{U}^{s}\subset\Delta([r])^{A}\forall\;s\in\mathcal{S}

for some compact sets
\mathcal{W}^{i}
for
i\in[r]
and some compact sets
\mathcal{U}^{s}
for
s\in\mathcal{S}
.

Weak s-DP-compatibility of generalized rectangular uncertainty.

We start by showing that generalized rectangular uncertainty sets satisfy the weak SSP.

Proposition 4.18

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a generalized rectangular uncertainty set. Then the weak SSP (
25
) holds.

Proof 4.19

Proof of Proposition
4.18
.
Let
s\in\mathcal{S},\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S},\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
. We have

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\langle\bm{P}_{s},\bm{\pi}_{s}\bm{V}^{\top}\rangle

\displaystyle=\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{sa}\sum_{i=1}^{r}u_{sa}^{i}\bm{w}^{i\top}\bm{V}

\displaystyle=\min_{\bm{u}_{s}\in\mathcal{U}^{s},(\bm{w}^{1},...,\bm{w}^{r})\in\mathcal{W}^{1}\times...\times\mathcal{W}^{r}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{sa}\sum_{i=1}^{r}u_{sa}^{i}\bm{w}^{i\top}\bm{V}

\displaystyle=\min_{\bm{u}_{s}\in\mathcal{U}^{s},(\bm{w}^{1},...,\bm{w}^{r})\in\mathcal{W}^{1}\times...\times\mathcal{W}^{r}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{sa}\bm{u}_{sa}^{\top}(\bm{w}^{i\top}\bm{V})_{i\in[r]}

\displaystyle=\min_{\bm{u}_{s}\in\mathcal{U}^{s}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{sa}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\bm{u}_{sa}^{\top}\left(\min_{\bm{w}^{i}\in\mathcal{W}^{i}}\bm{w}^{i\top}\bm{V}\right)_{i\in[r]}

Note that the inner optimization problem
\min_{\bm{w}^{i}\in\mathcal{W}^{i}}\bm{w}^{i\top}\bm{V}
is independent of
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
\bm{\pi}_{s}
, and it appears in each of the optimization problem
\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\langle\bm{P}_{s},\bm{\pi}_{s}\bm{V}^{\top}\rangle
across each
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Therefore, if we pick
\bm{w}^{i\star}\in\arg\min_{\bm{w}^{i}\in\mathcal{W}^{i}}\bm{w}^{i\top}\bm{V}
for each
i\in[r]
and then, for each
s\in\mathcal{S}
,

\bm{u}_{s}^{\star}\in\arg\min_{\bm{u}_{s}\in\mathcal{U}^{s}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{sa}\bm{u}_{sa}^{\top}\left(\bm{w}^{i\star\top}\bm{V}\right)_{i\in[r]}

we obtain that
\bm{P}^{\star}:=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}u_{sa}^{i\star}\bm{w}^{i\star}\right)_{sa}
is feasible:
\bm{P}^{\star}\in\mathcal{P}
, and that
\displaystyle\bm{P}^{\star}\in\cap_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\arg\min_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\langle\bm{P}_{s},\bm{\pi}_{s}\bm{V}^{\top}\rangle
.

\square

As a corollary, we directly obtain that all the important consequences of DP-compatibility highlighted in Section
4.1.1
hold for generalized rectangular models.

Special cases of generalized rectangularity.

As the name suggests, generalized rectangularity encompasses as special cases several well-known models of uncertainty.

Lemma 4.20

1.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be s-rectangular. Then
\mathcal{P}
is generalized rectangular.

2.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be r-rectangular. Then
\mathcal{P}
is generalized rectangular.

Proof 4.21

Proof of Lemma
4.20
.

1.

\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{S}

\mathcal{W}^{i}

\mathcal{S}_{1}
are s-rectangular and the transitions out of
\mathcal{S}_{2}
are r-rectangular. Since

has only two elements, this would mean that
\mathcal{P}
is s-rectangular or that
\mathcal{P}
is r-rectangular. However, neither of these two cases is possible.
Indeed,
\mathcal{P}
is not r-rectangular since

proved that an optimal robust policy must be randomized if starting in State

Additionally,

cannot be s-rectangular because the transitions out of the state-action pairs

Remark 4.27

(
(\xi,\eta)
-rectangularity)

The authors in
[
17
]
introduce the
(\xi,\eta)
-rectangularity models of uncertainty, which is a model where one can write
\bm{P}_{sa}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}u^{i}_{sa}\bm{w}^{i}
where both the coefficients
\bm{u}^{1},...,\bm{u}^{r}
and the factors
\bm{w}^{1},...,\bm{w}^{r}
can vary, but where there are constraints of the form

for some radii

and some nominal factors
\hat{\bm{w}}^{1},...,\hat{\bm{w}}^{r}
and nominal coefficients

(see section 3.2 in
[

). These constraints imply that
(\bm{w}^{1},...,\bm{w}^{r})
do not belong to a Cartesian product sets (and similarly for the coefficients), and therefore,
(\xi,\eta)

[

]
is very different from ours:
[
17
]
focus on the case of finite horizon objective, on uncertainty in the transitions and in the rewards, on policy gradient algorithms, and does not discuss the properties of optimal policies (e.g. history-dependent vs. stationary policies) for their model. Our technical setting is different (we focus on finite and infinite objective function, and on general compact sets
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{U}
), and most importantly, we focus on finding necessary and sufficient conditions for efficiently solving the policy evaluation problem and the relation between DP-compatibility and all the important consequences for robust MDPs (strong duality, existence of stationary policies, etc.).

Implications of weak sa-DP-compatibility.

We first note that following Proposition

An interesting subclass of models satisfying the weak SSP (

5
Discussion

We now discuss some of the most important results of our paper.

Properties of robust MDPs.

Our results highlight interesting connections between the important properties of robust MDPs.

In particular, we are the first to show that the weak DP-compatibility of the policy evaluation problem implies that we can efficiently solve the max-min problem, see Theorem
4.4
. This suggests that the main difficulty in solving robust MDPs lies in the ability to compute worst-case returns over
\mathcal{P}
for each policy
\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}
. Because of the Cartesian product structure of the set of stationary policies:
\Pi_{\sf S}=\times_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\Delta(\mathcal{A})
, it is possible to obtain a dynamic programming equation for solving the max-min problem, if we have a dynamic programming equation for the minimization problem, i.e. if
\mathcal{P}
is (weakly) DP-compatible. Interestingly, the main results for r-rectangular models were obtained in this order: the r-rectangular model was first introduced in
[
9
]
where the authors only show how to solve policy evaluation via dynamic programming, before
[
10
]
show how to solve the robust MDP (max-min) problem via dynamic programming.

Our key theorems yielding sufficient and necessary conditions for (weak) DP-compatibility highlight the main difficulty behind obtaining a dynamic programming equation for policy evaluation: this is possible
if and only if
one can solve several linear programs over
\mathcal{P}
simultaneously, a condition that we call
simultaneous solvability property
(SSP). At a high level, these conditions arise from the feasibility of the operators
T^{\pi}
and
\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$T$}}}^{\pi}
(which independently minimize over
\mathcal{P}
on each component) within the set of operators
\{T^{\pi,\bm{P}}\;|\;\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}\}
, in the precise sense that

(
25
)

\displaystyle\iff\forall\;\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{S},\forall\;\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S},T^{\pi}(\bm{V})\in\{T^{\pi,\bm{P}}(\bm{V})\;|\;\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}\}

(
39
)

\displaystyle\iff\forall\;\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{S},\forall\;\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S},\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$T$}}}^{\pi}(\bm{V})\in\{T^{\pi,\bm{P}}(\bm{V})\;|\;\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}\}

We note that the weak SSPs (
25
) and (
39
) could be reformulated in various ways, and we have done our best to provide a formulation of these conditions that is both readable and easily verifiable.

Finally, we also highlight explicitly that the existence of stationary optimal policies follows from strong duality, see Lemma
4.9
. We note that a similar approach is undertaken in
[
34
,
10
]
without emphasizing the key role of strong duality. Interestingly, strong duality follows from Sion’s minimax theorem for convex-concave saddle-point problems over convex compact sets,
even though robust MDPs are not convex-concave problems
. This is because the optimal value function
\bm{u}^{\star}
is the solution to the convex-concave saddle-point problems for each component of
T(\bm{u}^{\star})
, with
T
the Bellman operator as defined in (
28
). While some of the properties that we show in Section
4.1.1
were already known for some models of uncertainty (e.g. for r-rectangular models), the main contribution of this paper is to provide a unifying approach to the DP-compatibility of uncertainty models, as we describe next.

(Weakly) DP-compatible uncertainty models by design.

From a practical standpoint, our results suggest a change of paradigm in the design of uncertainty sets.
In particular, different models of uncertainty, such as s-rectangularity or r-rectangularity, have been introduced independently, each requiring a particular approach for showing their important properties, such as efficient algorithms for solving policy evaluation and the existence of stationary optimal policies. In contrast, based on Theorem
4.2
and Theorem
4.28
, the weak DP-compatibility of an uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}
can be verified easily and in a unified way, by verifying that the weak SSP conditions hold, which we show are
necessary and sufficient
for weak s-DP-compatibility and weak sa-DP-compatibility.

We also uncover a wealth of non-rectangular uncertainty sets that are weakly s-DP-compatible uncertainty sets ((s+r)-rectangular models, generalized r-rectangular models, generalized rectangular models).

It is likely that several other weakly DP-compatible models of uncertainty could be discovered since, based on our results, one can obtain the weak DP-compatibility of new uncertainty models “by design”, ensuring that they satisfy the weak SSP conditions. However, we believe that our main contribution lies in obtaining simple
necessary and sufficient
conditions for verifying the weak DP-compatibility of an uncertainty set, and that our results actually undermine the case for non-rectangular uncertainty models, as we discuss below.

The case for rectangular uncertainty sets.

Overall, our results from Section
3
show that in all generality (i.e. without any assumption on the rewards), only rectangular uncertainty sets are DP-compatible. Under an additional assumption (see Assumption
4
), there exists several non-rectangular uncertainty sets that may be weakly DP-compatible, but all these models of uncertainty sets are “equivalent” to rectangular models, since we proved that dynamic programming-based approaches always reduce to solving policy evaluation
over rectangular models
.
We emphasize that this is one of the key conceptual and practical contributions of our paper: we prove that we can find an optimal policy or evaluate the worst-case return of a policy for these
non-rectangular
models by
solving the same optimization problems over their s-rectangular or sa-rectangular extensions
.
This is true for all the non-rectangular models known in the literature, i.e., for the r-rectangularity model introduced in
[
9
,
10
]
, and for the new non-rectangular models studied in this paper. This undermines the narrative behind the introduction of non-rectangular uncertainty models, which were introduced to overcome the conservativeness of rectangular models.

Beyond dynamic programming.

As an important next direction of research for the RMDP community, we argue that the search for new models of uncertainty should focus on finding models that are not s-DP-compatible or sa-DP-compatible but for which we can still efficiently compute an optimal policy, i.e. to focus on finding models of uncertainty that do not rely on dynamic programming for their solutions. This represents a significant departure from the usual solution methods for robust MDPs. In particular, we have shown in Section
4.1.1
that the (weak) DP-compatibility of an uncertainty set has several consequences, including the existence of stationary optimal policies
that do not depend on the initial distribution
(Corollary
4.6
and Theorem
4.8
). This property is attractive from an optimization standpoint and follows from an optimal value function dominating the value function of any other policy (see Appendix
B
). However, in practice, it is conceivable that the decision-maker is willing to stick to stationary policies but would like to adapt their policies depending on where the decision process starts, i.e., depending on the initial distribution, and in fact, the existence itself of stationary optimal policies may also be dependent on the choice of initial distribution. We provide examples of non-rectangular uncertainty sets satisfying these properties in Appendix
G
, and we leave generalizing these examples for future work.

6
Conclusion

We show that the existence of dynamic programming-based approach for policy evaluation is equivalent to a property of the uncertainty sets that we term
simultaneous solvability property
(SSP). This provides a unifying approach to investigating the compatibility of various models of uncertainty with dynamic programming. Crucially, we prove that in general, only the s-rectangular and sa-rectangular models are compatible with dynamic programming.
Some non-rectangular models can be
weakly
DP-compatible, requiring the assumption that the rewards do not depend on the next state, and in this case weak compatibility is equivalent to a weaker form of SSP. However, even weakly DP-compatible non-rectangular models are equivalent to rectangular models in the sense that their worst-case and optimal value functions can be computed by solving worst-case and optimal value functions over their rectangular extensions. Our work opens interesting directions for future research, perhaps the most exciting being finding uncertainty models that do not rely on dynamic programming, thereby potentially identifying entirely novel approaches for solving robust MDPs.

Appendix B
Proofs for comparing value functions

In this appendix, we provide proof for the comparisons of the value functions
\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}},\bm{u}^{\pi}
and
\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{u}$}}}^{\pi}
. The proofs of the results in this section follow from classical arguments from the RMDP literature and we only include them for completeness. The gist of the proofs in this section relies on:

1.

All the operators in this paper being
order-preserving
, with an operator
F:\mathbb{R}^{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{S}
being order-preserving if
\bm{v}\leq\bm{w}\Rightarrow F(\bm{v})\leq F(\bm{w})
for any
\bm{v},\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
.

2.

All the operators in this paper being
contractions
, with an operator
F:\mathbb{R}^{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{S}
being a contraction (with factor
\gamma<1
) if
\|F(\bm{v})-F(\bm{w})\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma\|\bm{v}-\bm{w}\|_{\infty}
for any
\bm{v},\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
. A contraction
F
admits a unique fixed point, and which is the limit of
\left(F^{k}(\bm{v}_{0})\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
for any
\bm{v}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
.

Lemma B.1

Let
(\pi,\bm{P})\in\Pi_{\sf S}\times\mathcal{P}
. Then
\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{u}$}}}^{\pi}\leq\bm{u}^{\pi}\leq\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}
.

Proof B.2

Proof of Lemma
B.1
.
Let
s\in\mathcal{S}
. We have
\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}\geq T^{\pi}\left(\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}\right)
. Indeed,

\displaystyle v^{\pi,\bm{P}}_{s}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{sa}\bm{P}_{sa}^{\top}\left(\bm{r}_{sa}+\gamma\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}\right)\geq\min_{\tilde{\bm{P}}\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{sa}\tilde{\bm{P}}_{sa}^{\top}\left(\bm{r}_{sa}+\gamma\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}\right)=T^{\pi}(\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}})_{s}

where the first equality is from
\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}

.

. Solving for s-rectangular and sa-rectangular MDPs is a very well-studied problems; for the sake of completeness, we provide here some background on fixed point iterations algorithms. We refer to the monograph
[
4
]
for an extensive review of algorithms for computing the fixed point of contracting operators. We describe below the complexity of the value iteration algorithm for the setting studied in this paper.

The value iteration algorithm.

Consider the general problem of approximating the fixed point
\bm{v}_{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
of an operator
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, that is a contraction with factor
\gamma\in(0,1)
(we can assume
\gamma>0
here otherwise computing the fixed point is trivial). The complexity of computing the fixed point of
F
is controled by (a) the complexity of evaluating
F(\bm{v})
at a given point
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and (b) how close is the discount factor
\gamma\in[0,1)
to
1
. Indeed, consider the following

value iteration
algorithm:

\bm{v}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\bm{v}^{t+1}=F(\bm{v}^{t}),t\in\mathbb{N}.

(41)

From Theorem 6.3.1 in
[
26
]
, we know that the condition
\|\bm{v}^{t+1}-\bm{v}^{t}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon(1-\gamma)\gamma^{-1}
is sufficient to ensure that
\|\bm{v}^{t+1}-\bm{v}_{F}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
. Since
F
is a contraction, we know that
\|\bm{v}^{t+1}-\bm{v}^{t}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon(1-\gamma)\gamma^{-1}
will happen after at most
O\left((1-\gamma)^{-1}\log\left(\epsilon^{-1}(1-\gamma)^{-1}\right)\right)
iterations, each iteration
t\in\mathbb{N}
requiring to evaluate
F
at a given point
F(\bm{v}^{t})
. If we write
{\sf comp}(F)
the number of arithmetic operations for evaluating the function
F
at a given point, we note that the overall number of arithmetic operations to compute an
\epsilon
-approximation of the fixed point of
F
is given by
O\left({\sf comp}(F)(1-\gamma)^{-1}\log\left(\epsilon^{-1}(1-\gamma)^{-1}\right)\right)
.

The case of Bellman operators.

In the particular case of interest in this paper, the complexity of evaluating
T^{\pi}
directly relates to optimizing a linear function over
\mathcal{P}
, since this is the type of optimization problems that appear naturally in the component-wise definitions of
T^{\pi}
as in
12
). Optimizing a linear objective function can be done efficiently (in polynomial time) for a wide class of sets, including polytopes, ellipsoids, sets defined over the non-negative orthants and even sets involving semi-definite positive inequalities, see Section 4.6 in
[
3
]
. When the set
\mathcal{P}
is weakly sa-DP-compatible, we know that
\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{u}$}}}^{\pi}=\bm{u}^{\pi}
(see Section
4.2
), so the complexity of computing
\mathbf{\widehat{\text{$\bm{u}$}}}^{\pi}
boils down to the complexity of computing
\bm{u}^{\pi}
. The complexity of evaluating the operator
T
boils down to solving a saddle-point problem with a bilinear objective and convex compact decision sets
\Pi_{\sf S}
and
\mathcal{P}
, a well-studied optimization problems for which several efficient algorithms, e.g. section 5.5 and section 5.6 in
[
3
]
and
[
5
]
.

Proof D.1

Proof of Lemma

We have

\displaystyle\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\inf_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}\leq\inf_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf H}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}\leq\inf_{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\sf S}}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{v}^{\pi,\bm{P}}

\gamma=1/2

Proof E.2

Proof of Proposition
E.1
.
Recall that
\xi\mapsto R(\beta,\xi)
is always a strictly concave function for any
\beta\in[0,1]
.
In particular, if we fix
\beta\in[0,1]
we obtain that
\min_{\xi\in[0,1]}R(\beta,\xi)=\min\{R(\beta,0),R(\beta,1)\}=\frac{1}{16}\min\{\beta,1-\beta\}
from which we get that

\max_{\beta\in[0,1]}\min_{\xi\in[0,1]}R(\beta,\xi)=\max_{\beta\in[0,1]}\frac{1}{16}\min\{\beta,1-\beta\}=\frac{1}{32}

attained for
\beta=1/2
and
\xi\in\{0,1\}
.
Let us now fix
\xi\in[0,1]
. Because of the expression of
R(\beta,\xi)
, we see that
\max_{\beta\in[0,1]}R(\beta,\xi)
is attained at
\beta=1
if
\xi\geq 1/2
(in which case
\max_{\beta\in[0,1]}R(\beta,\xi)=\frac{1}{4}\cdot\xi\cdot\left(\frac{5}{4}-\xi\right)
), and attained at
\beta=0
if
\xi\leq 1/2
, in which
\max_{\beta\in[0,1]}R(\beta,\xi)=\frac{1}{4}\cdot(1-\xi)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{4}+\xi\right).
In both cases (
\xi\geq 1/2
and
\xi\leq 1/2
), the corresponding function
\xi\mapsto\max_{\beta\in[0,1]}R(\beta,\xi)
is concave, so we can find its minimum at one of the endpoint. In particular, we get

\displaystyle\min_{\xi\in[1/2,1]}\max_{\beta\in[0,1]}R(\beta,\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\cdot\min_{\xi\in[1/2,1]}\xi\cdot\left(\frac{5}{4}-\xi\right)=\frac{1}{4}\cdot\min\{\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{3}{4},\frac{1}{4}\}=\frac{1}{4}\cdot\min\{\frac{3}{8},\frac{1}{4}\}=\frac{1}{16}

\displaystyle\min_{\xi\in[0,1/2]}\max_{\beta\in[0,1]}R(\beta,\xi)

\displaystyle=\min_{\xi\in[0,1/2]}\frac{1}{4}\cdot(1-\xi)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{4}+\xi\right)=\min\frac{1}{4}\cdot\{\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{3}{4}\}=\frac{1}{16}.

Overall, we get that
\min_{\xi\in[0,1]}\max_{\beta\in[0,1]}R(\beta,\xi)=\frac{1}{16}
, which concludes the proof.
\square
