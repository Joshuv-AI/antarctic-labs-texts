Role of Neuroimaging Before Coronary Angiography in Patients With Out‐of‐Hospital Cardiac Arrest
Authors: Edmond Wong, Kathryn Yu, Davide Tomassoni, Timothy Scully, Timothy Yeung, Jennifer Cooke, David Tong, Jaya Chandrasekhar, Jason Nogic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPatients presenting following out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (OOHCA) often require emergency coronary angiography. Presentations can be associated with syncope or head‐trauma.AimsWe investigated the impact and utility of pre‐coronary angiography CT‐Brain (CT‐B) in patients following OOHCA on clinical outcomes.MethodsRetrospective analysis was undertaken at a large metropolitan hospital to identify patients. Patients following OOHCA who were transferred directly to the cardiac catheterization lab were included with patients stratified according to whether they received a CT‐B prior to coronary angiography or not.ResultsFollowing analysis, 235 patients met inclusion criteria. The CT‐B (n = 39) and non‐CT‐B (n = 196) groups were similar in baseline demographics with respect to age (64.1 vs. 63.3 years, p = 0.739), male sex (79.2% vs. 82.7%, p = 0.637), hypertension (48.7% vs. 49.2%, p = 0.953), and hypercholesterolemia (33.3% vs. 32.8%, p = 0.950) respectively. There was no difference in ECGs suggestive of cardiac ischemia (53.8% vs. 61.7%, p = 0.338). The CT‐B group were more likely to have fallen (89.7% vs. 50.5%, p < 0.001), and have documented head strike (61.5% vs. 8.2%, p < 0.001). Door‐to‐procedure time was significantly longer in the CT‐B group (105 vs. 53 min, p < 0.001). There was no difference in length of ICU stay (median 3 (IQR 1–6) vs. 3‐days (IQR 1–6), p = 0.44), or 30‐day mortality (41.0% vs. 29.5%, p = 0.159). Of those undergoing CT‐B, 17.9% had pathology identified, with only one patient (2.6%) having their angiogram deferred.ConclusionIn patients with OOHCA, pre‐angiography CT‐B has a low likelihood of identifying neuropathology and significantly increases DTP time.Keywords: cardiac arrest, CT‐Brain, door‐to‐procedure

Introduction

Out of hospital cardiac arrest (OOHCA) occurs in over 300,000 patients in the USA annually, and can be separated into shockable and non‐shockable causes [1]. This distinction is important because of the immediate treatment implications, furthermore patients presenting with shockable rhythms have improved prognosis [2]. Although other reasons may be present for ventricular arrhythmias, such as cardiomyopathy or ventricular scar, cardiac ischemia is an important reversible cause to identify as it is amenable to immediate treatment. These patients often require emergency angiography from both a diagnostic and therapeutic perspective. Coronary angiography currently carries a Class I recommendation for survivors of sudden cardiac arrest to investigate for coronary artery disease and often guides the need for myocardial revascularisation with percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) [3].

Patients presenting with OOHCA may experience syncope with concomitant head strike and are therefore at increased risk of intracranial pathology. Specifically, intracranial hemorrhage may impact the ability to safely perform coronary angiography as anticoagulants such as heparin are used as the standard of care in most diagnostic angiograms. If PCI is performed, higher doses of heparin are required with the potential need for potent intravenous glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitor, along with dual antiplatelet therapy post stent insertion. These agents increase bleeding risk and have the potential to worsen any intracranial pathology.

No guidelines currently exist for pre‐procedural neuroimaging in patients with survived OOHCA as there is a dearth of evidence. Although performing of a CT‐Brain (CT‐B) may be quick, the instability of these critically ill patients may result in a study that is more time consuming than is typical and may delay definitive cardiovascular care. Such delay poses significant implications on clinical outcomes, as studies have shown that for every reduction of door to balloon time by 30 min, there is a significant increase in 1‐year mortality rates [4]. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the utility of pre‐coronary angiography CT‐B and assess clinical outcomes in patients following OOHCA who received neuroimaging prior to their coronary intervention compared to those who did not.

Materials and Methods

A retrospective analysis was performed on all consecutive patients presenting following OOHCA with presumed cardiac cause to a large metropolitan hospital in Melbourne, Australia, between 2013 and 2024. Inclusion criteria were adult patients (> 18 years) presenting with OOHCA with documented and/or presumed ventricular arrhythmia who were transferred directly from the emergency department to the cardiac catheterization laboratory. Presumed ventricular arrhythmia was defined by discharge from an automated external defibrillator with documented syncope. Exclusion criteria were as follows; patients who had witnessed cardiac arrest whilst receiving medical care as these patients were unlikely to have had a head strike, patients with cardiac arrest unrelated to ventricular arrhythmia, such as pulseless electrical activity (PEA) and those who had non‐immediate angiography. During the study period, societal guidelines recommended considering immediate coronary angiography in all patients with OOHCA with sustained ventricular tachycardia and ventricular fibrillation [5].

Patients were stratified into those who had a pre‐angiography CT‐B, and those who did not. The primary outcome was door‐to‐procedure (DTP) time with secondary outcomes including new heart failure, inpatient stroke, 30‐day mortality, length of ICU stay, days intubated, and discharge status. DTP time was defined by arrival time in the emergency department, to start time of coronary angiogram. Door to procedure was chosen as the primary outcome as not all patients with cardiac arrest proceeded to percutaneous coronary intervention, and hence door‐to‐balloon time was not consistently available. The decision to perform CT‐B was assessed by the treating team, which may have involved emergency, cardiology and intensive care physicians. Further secondary outcomes included TIMI III flow rates and CT‐B dose (mSv).

Ethics approval was obtained by the institutional Office of Research and Ethics (reference number QA23‐077‐101437). Data was obtained from medical records from Eastern Health.

Statistical Analysis

Patient characteristics, baseline demographics and procedural characteristics were reported using mean and standard deviation, median and IQR, and frequency and proportions where appropriate. Categorical outcomes were described using frequency and proportions and were statistically tested using Chi‐squared tests or Fisher's Exact tests when data violated assumptions for the use of Chi‐squared tests. Normality of continuous outcomes was assessed visually using box plots and statistically using the Shapiro Wilks test. Given the non‐parametric nature of all continuous outcomes considered, median and interquartile ranges were used to describe, and the Mann‐Whitney‐U test was used to statistically tests differences between groups. Significant difference between groups at each outcome was determined at a threshold of p < 0.05 and STATA v.18 was used for data analysis.

Results

A total of 359 patients presented with out of hospital cardiac arrest over the study period, of whom 235 patients met the study inclusion criteria undergoing emergency coronary angiography. Of these patients, 39 (16.6%) had a pre‐angiography CT‐B, and 196 (83.4%) did not. Baseline patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. In brief, there was no difference in mean age between groups (64.1 yrs +/− 11.0 vs. 63.3 yrs +/− 12.6; p = 0.739) in the CT‐B and non‐CT groups respectively. Both groups were well balanced with regards to background history of hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, anticoagulant prescription and history of coronary artery disease. There was no difference in pre‐hospital arrest characteristics with no statistical difference in rates of witnessed arrests (79.4%, n = 31 vs. 76.0%, n = 149, p = 0.587), or time to return of spontaneous circulation (20 [IQR 10–30] vs. 20 min [IQR 12‐30], p = 0.652). Pre‐procedural biochemical markers of pH (7.11 vs. 7.11, p = 0.685), lactate (5.6 vs. 5.6 mmol/L, p = 0.731), bicarbonate (21 vs. 20 mmol/L, p = 0.0.530), creatinine (108 vs. 105 mmol/L, p = 0.981) and peak troponin levels (1040 vs. 923 ng/L, p = 0.875) were not significantly different between groups. Univariate regression showed that age and gender were associated with worse 30‐day mortality, however time from arrest to ROSC, ECG showing ischemia, shock or witnessed fall were not (Table 2). Multivariate analysis including variables of age, gender, time from arrest to ROSC, ECG showing ischemia, shock or witnessed fall were not associated with a worse 30‐day mortality (Table 2).

The CT‐B group had a smaller mean body mass index (BMI) (26.4 vs. 28.6 kg/m2, p = 0.017), were more likely to have fallen (89.7% vs. 50.5%, p < 0.001) and to have a witnessed head strike (61.5% vs. 8.2%, p < 0.001). Of those who had pre‐procedural CT‐B, 7 (17.9%) had an abnormality detected. These included 1 skull bone fracture, 2 subgaleal haematomas, 2 with evidence of cerebral edema and 1 with poor differentiation of gray and white matter. The patient who sustained a skull fracture was the only patient to have their angiogram deferred.

Procedural characteristics were similar between the groups with high rates of femoral access (89.7% vs. 84.1%, p = 0.618), and pre‐procedural intubation (78.9% vs. 75.0%, p = 0.604). As shown in Table 4, there was a significant increase in DTP time in the CT‐B group at 105 (IQR 56–150) vs. 53 (IQR 28–87) minutes (p < 0.001). Of those who subsequently underwent PCI (14, (35.9%) vs. 95, (48.5%), p = 0.290), there was no difference in DTB time (median 86.5 (IQR 67–122) vs. 68 (IQR 45–99) minutes), p = 0.159).

Discussion

In patients with unwitnessed arrests, or those with evidence or history of head strike, the desire to perform neuroimaging to exclude intracranial pathology pre‐procedure often results in delays to timely care with no current guidelines guiding decision making in this scenario. Our data, representing one of the largest studies of its kind, suggests that in an OOHCA population, pre coronary angiography CT‐B in patients with suspected or confirmed head strike led to a significant increase in DTP time. This delay in angiography time could not otherwise be explained by a more critically unwell patient population, as baseline characteristics and pre‐procedural biochemical markers were similar between groups. The delay in DTP did not adversely affect outcomes of 30‐ or 60‐day mortality, discharge from hospital, or length of ICU stay. Those who received CT‐B had a smaller mean BMI, possibly suggesting a frailer cohort.

Early revascularisation in AMI improves survival with consensus guidelines advocating for urgent early revascularisation within 90‐min of hospital arrival [6, 7]. In STEMI patients longer door to balloon time significantly increases mortality, with mortality rates of 3.0% for patients < 90‐min, compared to 7.4% for those > 150‐min [4]. In our study, performing a CT‐B prior to angiography resulted in an almost doubling of DTP time from 58 to 105 min. A median DTP time of 105‐min suggests the majority of this cohort had an unacceptably long delay to their procedure, extending beyond guideline recommendations. The increased time to revascularisation may expose patients to a vulnerable period which may worsen haemodynamic states, risk malignant arrhythmias and increase infarct size.

Patients presenting with OOHCA with a fall and/or possible head strike usually occur at ground level and are considered low energy falls that are not classified as dangerous falls [8]. We specifically analyzed patients at risk of head strike and excluded patients with a witnessed arrest whilst receiving medical care, such as with paramedic staff present. Patients included in our study who fell from standing height would mechanistically not be classified as having a high‐risk fall. In a cohort of 848 older, higher risk patients presenting with a fall and concomitantly prescribed antiplatelet or anticoagulants, the rates of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) from a ground level fall were 3.5% [9]. Rates of ICH in a younger or antiplatelet/anticoagulant naïve population would likely be lower. Other studies have shown a 2.2% incidence of intracerebral or subarachnoid hemorrhage in all patients following out of hospital cardiac arrest [10]. Within our study, angiography was deferred in one patient (2.5%) who sustained multiple cranial fractures identified on neuroimaging. The yield of significant pathology on neuroimaging even in this highly selected cohort was low, and the majority of findings did not change management. The most common findings in low incidence were that of cerebral edema, poor gray‐white matter differentiation and subgaleal haemtomas. For CTB to change management, it would need to identify a significant intracranial hemorrhage where heparinisation would be contra‐indicated, or neurosurgical intervention warranted, with none identified in our study.

Performing CT‐B in all comers is unlikely to detect significant pathology. Imaging should be targeted at those with the highest risk profile. Antiplatelet medications will increase the risk of ICH following fall, with Aspirin having higher risk than other P2Y12 inhibitors [11]. Cohort studies of older patient who have fallen have shown that seizure, GCS < 13, and focal neurological deficits increased the risk of ICH [12]. Patients with low conscious state are challenging to manage, and have an over 20x rate of mortality [13]. Low conscious state may be caused by a variety of factors such as anoxic brain injury, hypotension as well as ICH. Findings such as skull haematomas, pupil dilation, lacerations, displaced teeth and epistaxis are objective and can be assessed in a neurologically obtunded patient. Bleeding risk that is increased from a known bleeding diathesis or from medications should also lower clinician's threshold for considering CT‐B in this cohort. CT‐B should also be considered if the level of conscious state is low out of keeping with a short duration and success of the resuscitation management where normotension is achieved.

Given the need for intraprocedural use of heparin and often post procedural anti‐platelet therapy, there is a concern that performing angiography and/or stenting may extend intracranial hemorrhage. Rates of spontaneous ICH in non‐emergency PCI cases are low at 1.3% [14]. Currently there are no clear guidelines regarding the safety of coronary angiography or percutaneous coronary intervention following a hemorrhagic stroke. Therefore there is uncertainty toward PCI and DAPT in patients with ICH, especially in the absence of a readily available reversal agent for antiplatelet drugs [15]. To minimize the medication associated bleeding risks, a femoral approach can be undertaken to obviate the need for up‐front intraprocedural heparin, and in patients proceeding with PCI once a coronary lesion is identified, lower doses of heparin such as 40 IU/kg have been used safely (as compared to the usual 70‐100 IU/kg) [16]. Further, use of balloon angioplasty and drug coated balloons can minimize DAPT durations as compared to drug eluting stents [17].

Limitations

Our study was a retrospective, single center study. Therefore, the decision to perform a CT‐B may have been subject to the bias from the treating clinician and institutional culture. Other centers may have different thresholds for performing pre‐angiography imaging. Additionally, the COACT trial published in 2019 altered the threshold for which OOHCA patients received immediate angiography and given our trial analyzed patients between 2013 and 2023, this may have affected the threshold for which patients were investigated. Although the COACT trial showed no difference in delayed angiography with regards to survival at 90‐days, this does not apply to our cohort, as patients with active signs of ischemia were excluded from the COACT trial [18].

The number of patients in our CT‐B arm was limited. This was due to strict inclusion criteria where patients who had a witnessed arrest with the ambulance or emergency team were not included, as they would not have been exposed to a risk of head strike and therefore require a CT‐B. The majority of patients in our study were male (79.2% vs. 82.7%, p = 0.637), however this is similar proportion in large trials such as the CO‐ACT trial where 81.7% in the immediate angiography group were male [18].

Conclusion

In this retrospective analysis, pre‐coronary angiography CT‐B neuroimaging in patients presenting with OOHCA as a result of presumed or observed ventricular arrhythmia, significantly increases door to procedure time and showed low yield for identification of significant intracranial pathology that would necessitate delay of coronary angiography. Clinical outcomes in the two groups remained similar. Further prospective research assessing the most appropriate patients to pre‐select for pre‐procedural neuroimaging is required.
