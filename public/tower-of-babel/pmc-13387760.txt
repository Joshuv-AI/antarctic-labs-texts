Diagnosis, Risk Factors, and Management Principles of Fetal Premature Atrial Contractions
Authors: Ye Feng, Jianhua Niu, Yumei Wei, Huixia Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFetal premature atrial contractions (PACs) are the most common fetal arrhythmia, accounting for up to 90% of cases. Although predominantly benign and self-limiting, PACs carry a small but significant risk of adverse outcomes, including progression to supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), and heart failure. This review synthesizes current evidence on the epidemiology, etiology, and risk stratification of fetal PACs. We highlight the importance of M-mode and Doppler echocardiography in diagnosis and in distinguishing PACs from atrioventricular block (AVB). A risk-stratified management pathway is proposed: low-risk cases (isolated PACs, normal ventricular rate, no structural abnormalities) require weekly heart rate monitoring and routine obstetric care; high-risk cases—characterized by blocked PACs, bigeminy/trigeminy, frequent PACs, bursts of tachycardia, atrial septal aneurysm, heart failure, or associated congenital heart disease—warrant enhanced surveillance, multidisciplinary consultation, and consideration of antiarrhythmic therapy or timely delivery. Postnatal follow-up is essential to confirm resolution and manage late-onset SVT. Accurate diagnosis and risk-adapted intervention are critical to optimizing perinatal outcomes.

Introduction

Fetal arrhythmia, which complicates 1%–3% of pregnancies, includes irregular arrhythmia, tachyarrhythmia, and bradyarrhythmia.1234 Irregular arrhythmias include premature atrial contractions (PACs) and premature ventricular contractions (PVCs), with PACs far outnumbering PVCs (ratio approximately 10:1) and representing the vast majority (up to 90%) of fetal arrhythmias.1 This review synthesizes current evidence on the etiology, pathophysiological mechanisms, diagnostic methods, differential diagnosis, classification, and risk stratification of fetal PACs from recent cohort studies and meta-analyses. Furthermore, we propose evidence-based monitoring protocols and management strategies to optimize perinatal outcomes, underscoring the need for a multidisciplinary approach to the care of these fetuses.

Etiology

PACs typically have no definitive etiology and may result from immaturity of the fetal conduction system.5 Some PACs are triggered by mechanical stimulation of the left atrial wall due to a redundant foramen ovale or an atrial septal aneurysm,6 whereas others are associated with maternal stress, irregular uterine contractions, caffeine or stimulant intake, and smoking. A case study reported a 36-year-old pregnant woman whose fetus developed frequent PVCs and occasional PACs at 23 weeks of gestation. She had been regularly taking Unisom, which contained doxepin (for morning sickness), during pregnancy. Within one week of discontinuing Unisom, the fetal heart rhythm completely normalized. The study suggests that doxylamine may have proarrhythmic effects in utero, emphasizing that maternal drug exposure must be systematically investigated when assessing fetal arrhythmias, particularly regarding lipophilic first-generation H1 antihistamines capable of crossing the placenta.7

Diagnostic methods and differential diagnosis

M-mode echocardiography shows atrial and ventricular movements, typically using a four-chamber view to record atrial and ventricular contractions simultaneously. It is most useful in rhythm disturbances (e.g., ectopy, conduction block) and confirms the presence or absence of a V wave following the A wave. Doppler echocardiography records flow in the superior vena cava (SVC) and the ascending aorta (aAo); atrial contraction produces reversed SVC flow, and ventricular contraction results in forward aAo flow, corresponding to the onset of the P and QRS waves, respectively. This modality is most useful when the hemodynamics are compromised (e.g., regurgitation, filling abnormalities).8 Both modalities are the primary methods for diagnosing fetal PACs.

Both PAC and AVB are characterized by an irregular ventricular rhythm, dissociation between atrial and ventricular rates, and bradycardia, making differentiation challenging. However, distinguishing between the two is critical due to differences in clinical management and neonatal prognosis. The key distinction is that the atrial rhythm is irregular in blocked PAC but regular in AVB. PACs may occur suddenly in a fetus with an otherwise normal heart rate, whereas sudden-onset 2:1 AVB is rare.

Approximately 50% of AVB cases are associated with CHD and may result in fetal heart failure, hydrops, intrauterine death, or postnatal neurological impairment, with a high mortality rate. The untreated fetal mortality rate is 14%–34%, but with modern medical management, the perinatal survival rate reaches 95%.9 In cases of AVB with normal cardiac anatomy, maternal anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB antibodies should be tested, as maternal anti-Ro antibodies can induce fetal myocarditis, damage the conduction fibers, and cause endocardial fibroelastosis, leading to AVB. Prenatal administration of glucocorticoids (dexamethasone) combined with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) can improve myocardial inflammation but cannot reverse established CHB. After birth, if the ventricular rate is < 55 bpm or if there is associated cardiac dysfunction, permanent pacemaker implantation should be performed as early as possible in affected neonates.1011 A sustained low ventricular rate before delivery may require pregnancy termination, depending on gestational age.

From 2002 to 2012, 17 cases of sustained fetal bradycardia were diagnosed as blocked atrial bigeminy or 2:1 AVB at a tertiary hospital in Sweden. Sustained bradycardia was defined as a ventricular rate < 110 bpm for more than 50% of the examination time. Among 16 confirmed cases, six were 2:1 AVB and 10 were blocked atrial bigeminy. One case involved LQTS, with a prolonged myocardial refractory period mimicking 2:1 AVB. In addition, five AVB cases were positive for anti-Ro antibodies. Heart rate was higher in blocked PACs than in 2:1 AVB; however, the overlap between 65 and 75 bpm limits its diagnostic value.9

Pathophysiological mechanisms

The normal sequence of cardiac excitation conduction is as follows: sinoatrial node, internodal tracts, atrioventricular node, atrioventricular bundle, left and right bundle branches, and Purkinje fibers. However, in PACs, the heartbeat originates from ectopic foci in the left atrial appendage or atrial wall. These ectopic atrial impulses may be conducted to the ventricles in a 1:1 manner, conducted intermittently, or not conducted at all. Accordingly, PACs are classified as conductive, non-conductive, or combined conductive–non-conductive. They may also occur after every one or two sinus beats, resulting in patterns such as bigeminy or trigeminy. According to the frequency of occurrence, they are divided into occasional (< 5 times/min) and frequent (≥ 6 times/min) premature beats. When PACs are blocked and do not conduct to the ventricles, the ventricular rate becomes lower than the atrial rate, and may fall below 100 bpm, or even to 40–50 bpm; this is referred to as a blocked PAC. When PACs are intermittently non-conducted, episodes of bradycardia alternate with periods of normal heart rate. When PACs are alternately blocked at the atrioventricular node, they can cause bradycardia (heart rate < 110/min), a pattern known as blocked atrial bigeminy.4 PACs can vary from one type to another, with the heart rate fluctuating between 50 and 180 bpm. Although conducted PACs do not affect the ventricular rate, they can alter the sinoatrial node rhythm and, subsequently, reduce the sinus heart rate. Frequent premature beats reduce ventricular contractility and affect normal cardiac output.

Risk stratification of fetal PACs

Over 90% of PACs resolve prenatally with a favorable prognosis. Most PACs are not associated with structural heart defects and do not require treatment; however, a small proportion are associated with CHD, and fewer than 2% develop SVT, which carries a risk of heart failure and fetal death.7 Furthermore, the risk of SVT increases to 10%–14% when blocked atrial bigeminy is present.4

Previous studies have mainly focused on risk factors for tachyarrhythmias in fetuses with PAC due to its association with an increased risk of fetal heart failure and death. A retrospective cohort study analyzed fetuses diagnosed with PAC at two medical centers in Amsterdam between 2007 and 2022.8 Among 1439 cases, 24.1% (347/1439) resolved spontaneously before referral to the fetal medicine unit (FMU). In the remaining 939 cases with confirmed PAC, the overall incidence of adverse outcomes was 6.8% (64/939), including CHD, tachyarrhythmias, other arrhythmias, need for antiarrhythmic therapy, and death. CHD was diagnosed in 14 fetuses (1.5%, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.9–2.5), including eight cases diagnosed prenatally and six postnatally. The incidence of CHD was twice that of the baseline population (odds ratio (OR): 1.8, 95% CI: 1.0–3.3; P = 0.034). SVT was diagnosed prenatally in 25 fetuses (2.7% (25/939), 95% CI: 1.8–3.9), with six (24.0%) showing signs of heart failure and 14 (56.0%) receiving antiarrhythmic treatment. Postnatally, 12 newborns (1.3%, 95% CI: 0.7–2.2) were diagnosed with SVT; five cases had prenatal manifestations, while seven were diagnosed only after birth. Of the newborns diagnosed with SVT, seven cases were of supraventricular origin, and five were of ventricular origin; two neonates (16.7% (2/12)) exhibited signs of heart failure, and 11 (91.7% (11/12)) received antiarrhythmic treatment. In total, 3.4% (32/939) of PAC fetuses had tachyarrhythmias pre- and/or postnatally, with 25% (8/32) showing signs of heart failure and 71.9% (23/32) requiring treatment. Ten fetuses (1.06% (10/939)) developed bradycardia due to sustained blocked PACs. Risk factors for fetal tachycardia include PACs with short bursts of tachycardia, blocked PACs, PAC bigeminy, frequent PACs, evidence of heart failure, and atrial septal aneurysm. The median time to postnatal resolution of PACs was 1.5 months.

A systematic review assessed the risk of complications in fetuses with PAC,7 including CHD, prenatal SVT, heart failure, intrauterine or neonatal death, PAC bigeminy/trigeminy, and blocked PACs. The review included 19 cohort studies involving 2260 fetuses. Six studies reported the gestational age at PAC diagnosis, ranging from 28 to 33 weeks. Meta-analysis showed a pooled incidence of CHD of 2.8% (95% CI: 1.5–4.1), with a heterogeneity of 85%, which increased to 7.2% (95% CI: 3.5–10.9) in high-risk PAC fetuses and decreased to 0.9% (95% CI: 0–2.0) in low-risk PAC fetuses, compared to 0.6% in the general population. Among 108 PAC fetuses with CHD, 36 (33.3%) had isolated ventricular septal defects. Seven studies involving 547 fetuses identified atrial septal aneurysm in 61 (11.2%) cases. Advanced ultrasound is recommended upon PAC detection to confirm diagnosis and rule out CHD. Among 1685 fetuses in 13 studies, 22 (1.3%) were diagnosed with prenatal SVT, with a meta-analysis estimating an SVT incidence of 1.4% (95% CI: 0.6–3.4) in PAC fetuses. Cardiac failure was described in 16 fetuses (1.4%, 95% CI: 0.5–3.5), of which eight were CHD-related. Intrauterine fetal demise occurred in four fetuses (0.9%, 95% CI: 0.5–1.7) and was related to CHD in two cases. Twenty-four neonatal deaths were reported, which were mostly associated with cardiac or extracardiac anomalies; only one death resulted from tachycardia and heart failure. Furthermore, among 733 PAC fetuses in six studies, 44 (6.0%) developed bigeminy or trigeminy, and 42 (5.7%) had blocked PACs. Of 992 fetuses, PACs resolved spontaneously in 925 (93.2%), with resolution rates ranging from 66.0% to 100% across studies. Although complications are rare, PACs can lead to fetal death; therefore, weekly fetal heart rate monitoring is recommended to detect and manage complications promptly. A report from a tertiary hospital in China describes two cases of fetal PACs with high-risk features, including frequent blocked PACs and structural cardiac anomalies (e.g., atrial septal defect). Both neonates developed heart failure but achieved favorable outcomes after short-term supportive therapy without needing long-term antiarrhythmic me.12

Evidence-based monitoring protocols and management strategies

For the diagnosis and differential diagnosis of fetal arrhythmias, as well as the risk factor analysis and stratified management strategy for fetuses with PACs, see Fig. 1.

Clinical management pathway for fetuses with PACs. AVB: Atrioventricular block; CHD: Congenital heart disease; PAC: Premature atrial contractions; SSA: Sjögren’s syndrome antigen A SSB: Sjögren’s syndrome antigen B SVC: Superior vena cava; SVT: Supraventricular tachycardia.

Step 1: initial detection and comprehensive diagnosis

Confirm PAC diagnosis and type using fetal echocardiography: (1) M-mode: to assess the atrial (A) and ventricular (V) wall motion sequence and their relationship (A-V concordance vs. dissociation); (2) Doppler: (SVC/Ao) to analyze flow patterns corresponding to atrial systole and ventricular systole; (3) rule out structural heart disease: Perform a detailed fetal echocardiogram to exclude CHD; (4) classify PAC type (e.g., isolated, frequent, bigeminy/trigeminy, blocked PAC).

Step 2: risk stratification

Based on the diagnostic findings, stratify the fetus into one of two pathways:

A Low-risk PAC profile: (1) isolated or occasional PACs; (2) no structural heart disease; (3) normal ventricular rate.

B High-risk PAC profile: presence of any high-risk feature: (1) Blocked PAC/blocked atrial bigeminy; (2) frequent PACs (e.g., every 5–10 beats); (3) PACs with short bursts of tachycardia; (4) atrial septal aneurysm; (5) evidence of heart failure (hydrops); (6) associated structural CHD.

Step 3: management and monitoring pathway

A For low-risk fetuses: monitoring: (1) weekly fetal heart rate monitoring until PAC resolution is confirmed; (2) obstetric care: Routine prenatal care; (3) parental counseling: reassure about the excellent prognosis (> 90% resolve prenatally).

B For high-risk fetuses: initiate enhanced monitoring: (1) more frequent surveillance (e.g., bi-weekly or as determined by the fetal medicine specialist), including echocardiograms to assess for tachycardia or heart failure; (2) critical differential diagnosis: blocked PAC or AVB; (3) key differentiator: atrial rhythm; irregular atrial rhythm - > diagnosis of blocked PAC; (4) regular atrial rhythm with AV dissociation - > suggests AVB.

If AVB is suspected: (1) test maternal anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB antibodies; (2) consult pediatric cardiology for prognosis and postnatal management planning; (3) involve the following departments: Maternal–Fetal Medicine, Pediatric Cardiology, and Neonatology.

Discuss: (1) need for in-utero anti-arrhythmic therapy (if SVT develops); (2) optimal timing and mode of delivery; (3) postnatal resuscitation and care plan.

Step 4: postnatal follow-up

For all PAC cases: confirm resolution postnatally with echocardiography and/or cardiac monitoring.

For high-risk cases, especially those with SVT or CHD: require close pediatric cardiology follow-up and management.

For cases with unresolved PACs or a history of blocked atrial bigeminy: monitor for late-onset SVT in the neonatal period.

Conclusion

Most fetal arrhythmias are transient and part of normal cardiac development, requiring no intervention; however, approximately 10% are associated with fetal cardiovascular or neurological anomalies.1314 If not diagnosed and managed in time, severe fetal arrhythmias—especially those associated with structural heart disease—can result in heart failure, hydrops, or death.15 PACs are the most common fetal arrhythmia, which may alternate between different types and normal rhythm. The prognosis is generally favorable, but special attention is required in high-risk PAC cases, such as blocked PAC, bigeminy/trigeminy, frequent PACs (every 5–10 beats), coexistence of SVT, or atrial septal aneurysm. These fetuses are at risk of heart failure and require close monitoring and timely delivery. In the absence of risk factors, weekly heart rate monitoring until PAC resolution is recommended, with routine obstetric care sufficing.10 In high-risk cases, multidisciplinary consultation should be arranged to determine the optimal timing for delivery in collaboration with neonatologists and pediatric cardiologists. PACs must be differentiated from AVB, which may sound similar on auscultation but differ in management and prognosis. M-mode and Doppler echocardiography are essential for accurate diagnosis. Precise classification of fetal arrhythmias is key to formulating individualized treatment plans and improving prognosis.
