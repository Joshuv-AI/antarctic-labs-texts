Sentinel lymph node biopsy in vulvar cancer: current evidence, limitations, and future perspectives
Authors: Giorgio Bogani, Andreas Obermair, Antonia Testa, Giorgia Gargenese, Valentina Chiappa, Francesco Raspagliesi, Neville Hacker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vulvar cancer is a rare gynecologic malignancy in which regional lymph node status represents the most powerful prognostic factor and a key determinant of therapeutic strategy. For the past 60 years, bilateral inguinofemoral lymphadenectomy was the standard approach for staging and disease control. Although effective, this procedure was associated with substantial morbidity, including wound complications, chronic lower limb lymphedema, and long-term impairment of quality of life. In the past 20 years, sentinel lymph node (SLN) mapping has emerged as a less invasive alternative for selected patients with unifocal, clinically node-negative vulvar tumors less than 4 cm in diameter. Large prospective trials, such as GROINSS-V and GOG-173, demonstrated that sentinel node biopsy is feasible, safe, and significantly reduces postoperative morbidity while maintaining excellent oncologic outcomes in appropriately selected patients. Nevertheless, concerns persist regarding false-negative results, reported in up to 7%–10% of cases in some series, with anecdotal reports of rates up to 27%. Undetected nodal disease represents a missed opportunity for cure, and most patients with an isolated groin recurrence ultimately succumb to their disease. Therefore, the potential benefits of morbidity reduction must be carefully balanced against the risk of undertreatment. This review summarizes the historical evolution of groin management in vulvar cancer, critically appraises the current evidence supporting SLN mapping, and discusses its clinical limitations, patient preferences, and implications for practice. Emphasis is placed on surgical expertise, the importance of ultrastaging, structured surveillance, and ongoing prospective research to achieve the best survival with the lowest morbidity.

INTRODUCTION

Vulvar cancer is a rare malignancy, accounting for approximately 3%–5% of all gynecologic cancers [123]. Although uncommon, it affects both survival outcomes and quality of life. The incidence of vulvar cancer is highest in postmenopausal women; however, the incidence of HPV-associated vulvar cancer in younger women has been increasing at approximately twice the rate observed in older women [12]. Quality of life concerns, including psychological distress, sexual dysfunction, and treatment-related morbidity, highlight the importance of optimizing therapeutic strategies to balance oncologic outcomes with functional preservation [123].

Prognostic factors are pivotal in guiding treatment, with nodal status representing the most critical determinant of survival [123]. Historically, survival outcomes for vulvar cancer were poor, with 5-year survival rates of approximately 20%–25% [4]. A paradigm shift in management occurred with the adoption of en bloc radical vulvectomy, bilateral inguino-femoral lymphadenectomy and bilateral pelvic lymphadenectomy by Way [5] in 1966. Way reported an overall 5-year survival rate of 61%, firmly establishing appropriate management of the lymph nodes as the key prognostic factor [5]. However, this survival benefit was achieved at the cost of substantial treatment-related morbidity, including wound complications, prolonged hospitalization, chronic lower limb lymphedema, and long-term functional impairment. Subsequent studies have consistently shown that the presence of inguinal lymph node metastases reduces 5-year survival to approximately 40%–50%, compared with over 90% in node-negative patients [67]. Other significant prognostic factors include tumor size, depth of invasion, and margin status following surgery [123]. Current European Society of Gynaecological Oncology guidelines emphasize accurate staging and tailored management strategies that balance oncologic efficacy with morbidity reduction, building on these historical insights [1].

Over the past 3 decades, there has been a significant shift toward less invasive surgical techniques. Conservative vulvar resection has largely replaced radical vulvectomy for patients with unifocal tumors, as it provides equivalent oncologic outcomes while reducing psycho-sexual morbidity [678910].

EVOLUTION OF SURGICAL STRATEGIES TO REDUCE GROIN MORBIDITY IN VULVAR CANCER

Over time, several strategies have been developed with a view to reducing the morbidity associated with inguino-femoral and pelvic lymphadenectomy while preserving oncologic safety. The introduction of the separate incision approach to the groin dissections represented an important technical advance, as it allowed primary wound closure and shortened hospital stay. However, this modification did not address the long-term problem of lower limb lymphedema [11]. Elimination of routine pelvic lymphadenectomy followed studies from UCLA and Gateshead NHS Foundation Trust [1213]. Further efforts focused on reducing or avoiding full groin dissection.

The earliest approach was to omit nodal surgery altogether in patients with so-called “microinvasive” vulvar cancer (defined as cancers 2 cm or less in diameter with stromal invasion <5 mm [14], based on the assumption that nodal metastases would not occur in this setting. Unfortunately, this approach resulted in an unacceptably high rate of groin recurrences [15].

The second strategy was the performance of a “superficial” inguinal lymphadenectomy, as described by DiSaia et al. [8], which they regarded as a precursor of the sentinel node concept. The idea was to preserve the femoral nodes, in order to decrease the incidence of chronic lower limb lymphoedema, but a GOG study demonstrated that the initial drainage was sometimes to the femoral nodes [16] Similarly, the use of primary groin irradiation as an alternative to surgery proved inferior in terms of disease control [17]. Against this background, sentinel lymph node (SLN) biopsy emerged. When initially introduced, it was associated with a reduced rate of surgical morbidity compared with complete inguino-femoral dissection [1819]. In brief, SLN mapping in vulvar cancer relies on accurate identification of the first draining groin node from the primary tumor. The procedure is usually performed using a radiocolloid, blue dye, or a combination of tracers. Available evidence suggests that the combined technique provides the highest detection accuracy and the lowest false-negative rates, whereas the use of a single tracer may be associated with inferior performance. After removal, sentinel nodes should undergo detailed pathological assessment, including ultrastaging, in order to improve the detection of low-volume metastases that may be missed on routine examination [202122232425262728293031]. ISOLATED INGUINAL RECURRENCES AFTER SENTINEL NODE MAPPING

If the SLN is indeed the first site of metastatic spread, its negativity should indicate a minimal risk of involvement of the remaining regional lymph nodes However, several studies have reported higher false-negative rates than the 3% which was reported in the early experience [2021]. Importantly, the clinical consequences of a false-negative sentinel node appear to be particularly severe. It has been reported that up to 90% of patients who developed an isolated groin recurrence ultimately die of disease [22]. Long term clinical follow-up of the GROINSS-V I trial reported that among 259 patients with uni-focal disease and negative SLNs (median follow-up of 35 months), there were 6 false-negatives and all 6 patients subsequently died of disease [23].

In a systematic review and meta-analysis investigating outcomes after sentinel node biopsy, Covens et al. [20], reported a pooled false negative rate of 6.4% (4.4–8.8). Similarly, in another systematic review of 29 studies, including data on 1,779 patients, Meads et al. [21] observed a false-negative rate of 9%, and suggested the importance of a learning curve. Notably, all centers participating in the GROINSS-V trial were required to have documented successful experience of sentinel node mapping and inguino-femoral lymphadenectomy [18]. Other studies have also emphasized the relevance of the learning curve before adopting sentinel node mapping, even in low-risk patients [2425262728293031]. Technical factors also influence the accuracy of sentinel node mapping, such as the type of tracer used. A meta-analysis showed that combined use of radiocolloid and blue dye was associated with a false-negative rate of 6.6% (4.5–9.0) [20], whereas the use of blue dye or radiocolloid alone resulted in false-negative rates of 9.3% (range, 0–27.1), and 10.4% (range, 0.7–23.4), respectively [20].

PATIENTS PREFERENCE AND QUALITY OF LIFE

SLN biopsy has consistently been associated with lower treatment-related morbidity compared with complete inguino-femoral lymphadenectomy, particularly with regard to wound complications and chronic lower limb lymphedema. This reduction in morbidity has been widely regarded as one of the main advantages of the sentinel node approach.

On this basis, it has often been assumed that patients would be willing to accept a small decrease in survival in return for reduced long-term morbidity, particularly lifelong lower limb lymphedema. However, available evidence suggests that this assumption is largely unfounded [32].

de Hullu et al. [33], published data on a survey investigating patients and physicians’ thoughts on sentinel node mapping. Structured questionnaires were sent both to patients (99 questionnaires evaluable out of 117) and gynecologists (80 responders out of 100 invitations). Patients were long-term survivors who had been treated with an inguino-femoral lymphadenectomy. Among them, 40% had experienced one or more infections in the legs (cellulitis) and 47% had severe pain and/or chronic lower limb lymphedema [33]. Among these patients, 66% still preferred complete inguino-femoral lymphadenectomy rather than sentinel node biopsy if the false-negative rate was estimated to be 5%. In contrast, 60% of gynecologists would be willing to accept a 5%–20% false-negative rate [33].

In a subsequent survey, Oonk et al. [34], investigated quality of life in patients included in the GROINSS-V randomized trial. The study aimed to investigate quality of life after sentinel node mapping and inguino-femoral lymphadenectomy respectively, and to determine what advice these patients would offer their relatives if they were in a similar situation. Although sentinel node mapping was associated with lower complication rates than full groin dissection, no significant difference in overall quality of life was observed between the 2 groups. As stated by the authors: “Our present study does not support our original idea that a decrease in especially long-term morbidity also translates into an improved quality of life for vulvar cancer patients” [34].

Similarly, Farrell et al. [35], investigated quality of life following inguino-femoral lymphadenectomy and treatment preferences between sentinel node biopsy and lymphadenectomy among a cohort of 60 patients with vulvar cancer. Overall, 73% (n=43) of patients reported lymphoedema. Patients were asked to assume that the false negative rate for a sentinel node biopsy was 5%, and that virtually all patients with a false-negative sentinel node would die of disease. Assuming that the incidence of positive nodes was 20%, this would mean that the risk of dying would be 1 in 100. Fifty-three percent of patients said they would take no risk at all with their life, and another 10% said they would take a 1 in a million risk. Only 15% of patients were prepared to take the 1 in 100 risk required [35].

FUTURE DIRECTIONS AND EMERGING TECHNOLOGIES

1. Sentinel node biopsy in vulvar cancer >4 cm

The GroSNaPET-1 study was specifically designed to assess the reliability of SLN biopsy in clinically node-negative patients traditionally excluded from standard indications, including tumours larger than 4 cm, multifocal disease, prior vulvar surgery, unilateral node-positive disease, and recurrent tumours [36]. In this study, sentinel node biopsy was systematically followed by standard inguino-femoral lymphadenectomy, providing a robust reference standard and demonstrating high oncologic reliability even in these higher-risk settings (negative predictive value 100%). An updated analysis including approximately 110 selected groins has now been published, confirming the initial findings and further supporting the extension of eligibility criteria for SLN biopsy in vulvar cancer [37]. In parallel, the GroSNaPET-2 study has evaluated the same patient subgroups using a further de-escalation design, omitting inguino-femoral lymphadenectomy in the presence of a negative sentinel node biopsy and using groin-specific recurrence as the reference standard. Accrual has been completed and results are expected shortly [38]. If confirmed, these data, together with evidence from a recent Swedish prospective study extending sentinel node biopsy indications to tumours ≥4 cm and multifocal disease [39], may provide the basis for a safe expansion of sentinel node biopsy beyond currently accepted criteria in carefully selected patients.

2. Sentinel node biopsy following previous tumour excision

Several retrospective series and systematic reviews have independently explored the feasibility and oncologic safety of sentinel node biopsy in clinical scenarios traditionally considered unsuitable for the procedure. In particular, multiple studies have addressed sentinel node biopsy after prior tumour excision or at first local recurrence, using tracer injection at the surgical scar. These reports consistently show acceptable detection rates and inguinal outcomes comparable to those observed in patients undergoing sentinel node biopsy for intact primary tumours [404142]. A recent systematic review further confirmed that sentinel node biopsy following prior excision or scar injection is feasible, accurate, and not associated with worse inguinal outcomes, thereby opening the possibility of minimally invasive nodal staging in patients otherwise candidates for inguino-femoral lymphadenectomy [43]. Although heterogeneous and largely non-randomized, these data consistently suggest that tumour size, multifocality, or prior surgery should not be regarded as absolute contraindications, but rather as variables to be incorporated into a risk-adapted decision-making process.

3. Serial groin ultrasound to replace groin node surgery

An important recent development has been the introduction of serial groin ultrasonography [444546]. Pouwer et al. [44] initially reported its use for the follow-up of 76 patients with negative sentinel nodes. In a prospective study, all patients were scheduled to have 3-monthly follow-up visits consisting of physical examination combined with an ultrasonic examination of the groins by a radiologist. During a routine visit, 2 asymptomatic isolated groin recurrences were detected [44]. One of these nodes was clinically palpable. Both patients were treated by inguinofemoral lymphadenectomy and adjuvant radiotherapy, and both were alive without evidence of disease at 39 and 120 months after diagnosis, respectively [44].

It was suggested that the addition of serial groin ultrasonography for patients with negative sentinel nodes might ensure the oncological safety of this approach, and in our opinion, this approach should be investigated further in patients who meet the requirements for sentinel node biopsy.

Subsequently, Hacker et al. [45] reported a pilot study involving 32 patients in whom serial groin ultrasonography every 3 months was used in lieu of any groin dissection in the following circumstances: 1) 14 women with a unifocal stage 1B squamous cell carcinoma up to 20 mm in diameter. Three (21.4%) patients had a positive node detected on serial ultrasonography (at 4, 8, 11 months); 2) 10 patients with an ipsilateral squamous cell carcinoma of any size extending to within 1 cm either side of the midline. These patients were treated with ipsilateral inguino-femoral lymphadenectomy and ultrasonic surveillance of the contralateral groin; 3) 5 patients with multifocal invasive cancers, with the largest individual focus being 20 mm or less in diameter, and 4) 3 patients who refused or were considered unfit for groin surgery.

No patient in the last 3 categories had a positive node, but one patient had nodal dissection due to a false-positive result. Overall, this study showed an overall sensitivity for ultrasonic surveillance of 100% (95% confidence interval [CI]=44%–100%), with a specificity of 97% (95% CI=83%–99%). The positive predictive value was 75% (95% CI=31%–95%) and the negative predictive value was 100% (95% CI=88%–100%). In this study, 2 of the 3 positive nodes on ultrasound were not clinically palpable, and both these patients were free of disease at 3 and 8 years respectively after inguino-femoral lymphadenectomy and adjuvant radiation. The third patient had a clinically palpable node, and she died of disease at 6 months despite appropriate treatment [45].

In both these studies [4445], groin ultrasonography was performed every 3 months, and 2 of the 5 positive nodes detected were already clinically palpable. This suggests that the ultrasonography should be performed every 2 months, which will hopefully ensure that all positive nodes are not yet clinically palpable, and therefore likely to be cured with further treatment. The latest a groin node was detected was 11 months, which is relevant to the duration of ultrasonic surveillance [45].

An ongoing prospective, international, phase 3 randomized controlled clinical trial will further determine the role of serial groin ultrasonography in 480 patients with clinical stage 1B to 2 squamous cell carcinoma or adenocarcinoma of the vulva (ANVU trial; NCT06476639) [46]. To be eligible, patients will have to have a negative groin ultrasound. They will then be randomized 3:1 to serial high-resolution ultrasonic monitoring of both groins versus standard upfront groin lymph node dissection (inguino-femoral lymphadenectomy or sentinel node biopsy according to local clinical practice management guidelines) [46]. Patients in the ultrasonic surveillance group will have this performed in conjunction with clinical examinations every 2 months for 12 months and then every 3 months for another 6 months (total of 18 months). The primary end point will be death secondary to a groin node recurrence at 30 months. Important secondary objectives include Patient Reported Outcomes Measures and Health-Related Quality of Life, the incidence of lower limb lymphoedema, and cost and cost effectiveness of the 2 groups. Translational objectives will relate to the role of blood and tissue molecular biomarkers to accurately determine the burden of disease (positive lymph nodes) in vulvar cancer, assist with risk stratification and aid with disease surveillance.

This trial is expected to enroll patients from Australian and international sites from 2026. The value of ultrasound is also tested in another ongoing study (the RHINOCERUS study; NCT06906705) that integrates high-resolution and ultra-high-frequency groin ultrasonography with histopathological reference standards to identify imaging features associated with nodal metastatic burden. This trial aims to refine real-time risk stratification rather than replace sentinel node biopsy [47].

CONCLUSION

Identification of positive groin nodes remains essential in the curative management of patients with vulvar cancer. Failure to detect nodal metastatic disease represents a missed opportunity for cure and can have fatal consequences. Although sentinel node mapping reduces surgical morbidity and demonstrates relatively low false-negative rates in selected series, the clinical consequences of false-negative nodal assessment remain important and should continue to inform patient selection, counselling, and follow-up strategies. Studies have demonstrated that survival is the greatest priority for patients and many patients are not prepared to take risks with their life. For this reason, comprehensive preoperative counseling about the pros and cons of the different approaches to determine the groin node status is essential. Future prospective studies are planned to determine whether serial ultrasonic surveillance of the groins, and surgical management if a positive node is found, can replace any form of primary groin dissection in patients with a negative groin ultrasound at presentation. If this trial is positive, about 70% of patients will be spared the need for any type of groin dissection.
