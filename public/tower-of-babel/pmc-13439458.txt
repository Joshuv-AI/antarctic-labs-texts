Pre-trained knowledge transfer with application in multisystem inflammatory syndrome subphenotyping in children
Authors: Xiaokang Liu, Naimin Jing, Yiwen Lu, Jason H Moore, Christopher B Forrest, James Zou, Yong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C) is a severe post-acute sequela of SARS-CoV-2 infection in children and presents with highly heterogeneous signs and symptoms. Disentangling the clinical manifestations of MIS-C by characterizing its subphenotypes can help identify children at risk for severe outcomes and may help identify more targeted therapies. Characterizing subphenotypes usually requires large sample sizes and can often benefit from combining data from multiple sources. However, joint analysis often faces two major challenges: the prohibition of sharing patient-level data due to privacy concerns and between-study population clinical heterogeneity. To address both challenges, we propose a one-shot summary statistics-based framework to transfer knowledge of the shared subphenotypes pre-trained on large-scale source studies to a target study. Study-specific subphenotype mixing proportions are used to explain between-study heterogeneity. Both real-data guided simulation studies and an application to the MIS-C analysis demonstrate the benefits of our method.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Subtyping, a process to divide a broad and complex population into several specific and different subgroups, facilitates a more nuanced understanding of the population under study. Subtyping has a wide range of applications. For instance, in social sciences, it can be used to identify subgroups of individuals based on their attitudes towards issues such as climate disruption or vaccine hesitancy (e.g., COVID or HPV vaccines). This can help to better understand the underlying factors that influence these attitudes and inform interventions to address them. In biomedical studies, subtyping (also known as subphenotyping) is also commonly used to identify different variants or forms of complex syndromic diseases, so that healthcare providers can gain more insights into the diseases’ pathophysiological mechanisms, thereby providing tailored treatments to better suit individual patients1–3.

Multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C) is a post-acute hyperinflammatory disorder associated with SARS-CoV-2 infection4–6. As a rare disease, MIS-C occurs in about 1 out of every 3000 to 4000 children who had a SARS-CoV-2 infection, and has become even rarer since the start of the pandemic6. Children with MIS-C can present with highly heterogeneous signs and symptoms, involving multiple organ systems and overlapping with conditions of Kawasaki disease, acute COVID-19, toxic shock, and macrophage activation syndrome7. Further elucidation of MIS-C disease patterns is critical to improving its recognition and management.

A useful tool to dissect complex MIS-C manifestations is through subphenotyping with latent class analysis (LCA)8,9, where each MIS-C patient subpopulation (i.e., subphenotype) can be characterized by a specific pattern of MIS-C signs, symptoms, and organ system complications. However, as LCA models often involve many parameters, reliable estimation typically requires a relatively large sample size, which is difficult to obtain at a single institution for a rare disease such as MIS-C10. One alternative is to combine data from multiple data contributors; however, such an analysis faces two major challenges: prohibition of sharing patient-level data due to institutional and patient privacy concerns and study population heterogeneity. Federated or distributed LCA can help address these challenges11, but such approaches generally require coordinated iterative computation across all participating sites. In many real-world settings, however, a large source study has already been analyzed, and a new target site instead needs a practical way to reuse that source study knowledge without reopening a full multi-site estimation procedure. Our method is designed for this setting.

To address this problem, one promising strategy is pre-trained knowledge-guided transfer learning. Pre-trained models can effectively capture knowledge from large datasets and benefit downstream tasks with limited data via fine-tuning. This technique has been extensively applied in natural language processing and visual recognition12. Here, we exploit this idea to flexibly achieve information integration in subtyping tasks with data from multiple sources. Specifically, based on the assumption that source and target studies share the same set of latent classes and that between-study heterogeneity can be explained by study-specific class mixing proportions, we propose a one-shot summary statistics-based transfer framework, which we term “PreTrained-Subtyping”. PreTrained-Subtyping transfers estimates of the shared latent class parameters and their variances derived from a large source study to the target study to construct informative priors. The target study then combines these priors with its own data to update the shared latent class parameters while estimating target study-specific class mixing proportions to account for between-study heterogeneity. In this way, the method improves latent class estimation in a small target study, preserves privacy by avoiding patient-level data sharing, and avoids the need for a new iterative multi-site fitting procedure. Notably, this framework is designed for statistical transfer learning via Bayesian priors in LCA, rather than deep-learning parameter transfer.

In the MIS-C analysis presented in this manuscript, we identified MIS-C patients from 1,955,736 patients from 8 hospitals participating in PEDSnet13,14, and summarized symptoms, signs, health conditions, and laboratory values into eight binary organ-system involvement indicators to derive subphenotypes of MIS-C15. Pre-trained knowledge of MIS-C subphenotypes is made available through the analysis of electronic health records (EHR) from 7 pediatric medical centers (945 patients) participating in PEDSnet. This subphenotype information is then sent to and updated on another pediatric medical center (193 patients), also participating in PEDSnet, using our proposed PreTrained-Subtyping method. Via simulation studies with synthetic data and the application using real-world PEDSnet data, we verified that the PreTrained-Subtyping approach can effectively improve the estimation and prediction performance compared with local analysis using data from the target study only. It performed as well as pooled analysis using the centralized data. We also demonstrated the developmental nature of our approach, i.e., the pre-trained knowledge can be continuously refined as new data accumulate.

From a clinical perspective, PEDSnet provides one of the largest MIS-C cohorts described to date and is a powerful resource for detailed analyses of MIS-C subphenotypes. In particular, the dataset covers observations from the majority period of the pandemic. By using this dataset, our analyses provide the current knowledge of MIS-C subphenotypes, and we make this knowledge publicly available for future use. On the other hand, from a methodology perspective, although mainly illustrated by using the MIS-C study, our PreTrained-Subtyping method has a wide range of applications, particularly in situations where the study sample size is relatively small.

Results

Overall pipeline

The main goal of the method is to use reliable prior knowledge of the shared subphenotypes learned from a large-scale source study (e.g., the collaboration of 7 pediatric medical centers) to guide the identification and prediction of MIS-C subphenotypes in a target study with a limited sample size. The gold standard approach is to pool data from all studies and then apply LCA. However, in real-world settings, this may not be practical since patient-level data are commonly protected and institutions are often reluctant to share these data. Our approach, PreTrained-Subtyping, provides an alternative that requires only one-time transfer of the source study summary statistics to support target study subphenotype identification.

Figure 1 illustrates the two-step workflow of PreTrained-Subtyping. In Step 1, the source study fits the latent class model and obtains the estimates of the shared class-specific manifest-variable prevalences and their estimated variances. These source-derived summary statistics constitute the prior knowledge that are sent once to the target study. In Step 2, the target study uses this transferred information to construct informative beta priors for the shared latent class parameters and combines these priors with the target study likelihood to obtain updated estimates of the shared class-specific prevalences, while simultaneously estimating the target study-specific class mixing proportions to account for between-study heterogeneity. Thus, this framework enables source-to-target knowledge transfer without sharing patient-level data. See Methods for more details of the formulation of the approach.

Overview of the pipeline of the PreTrained-Subtyping method and its application in the MIS-C analysis.

PreTrained-Subtyping is a method that uses estimates obtained from a large-scale source study to improve the latent class model fitting in a small target study. It allows leveraging information from both the source study and the target study to jointly identify latent classes. The method has two steps. The first step is to obtain and transfer the LCA results fitted in a source study to the target study, including the estimated prevalence of manifest variables (shown by a heatmap using colors to represent prevalence), e.g., MIS-C manifestations of interest such as the presence of particular symptoms or organ system complications, and the corresponding variances. The second step is to incorporate this pre-trained knowledge into the model fitting using data in the target study, where the pre-trained knowledge gets updated and the target study-specific mixing proportions are estimated.

PreTrained-Subtyping improves estimation and prediction performance

The motivation behind our proposed PreTrained-Subtyping method is to improve the estimation and prediction accuracy for latent class models fitted with a small sample by incorporating pre-trained knowledge, i.e., estimates of the shared latent class parameters and their variances from a large-scale source study, and to update this knowledge with new data as well. Thus, our goal here is to demonstrate that PreTrained-Subtyping outperforms local analysis and achieves a good approximation to the pooled analysis in a variety of simulation settings. To accomplish this, we synthesized two datasets with the same latent classes but different class mixing proportions. One dataset, referred to as the source study, has a larger sample size and is used to generate pre-trained knowledge. Here we increase the source study sample size gradually from 1000 to 5000 to reflect an increased level of reliability of the pre-trained knowledge. The second dataset, known as the target study, has a smaller sample size 150. We compare the performance of three methods: local analysis using only the target dataset without any pre-trained knowledge, pooled analysis combining both datasets, and the PreTrained-Subtyping method.

The estimation parameters consist of two parts: the prevalence of binary manifest variables within each latent class and the mixing proportions of latent classes. Figure 2 displays the comparison of methods in terms of estimation accuracy. It shows that PreTrained-Subtyping achieved higher estimation accuracy than local analysis and performed similarly to pooled analysis across all simulation settings. The prediction is considered for both the class membership prediction and the manifest variable prediction for each patient (see details in Methods) and is measured by misclassification rate and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), respectively. Figure 3 shows that PreTrained-Subtyping had a higher prediction accuracy compared to local analysis and performed similarly to pooled analysis. Additionally, as the sample size of the source study increases, the improvement in estimation and prediction became more substantial. We have also confirmed that PreTrained-Subtyping improved the pre-trained knowledge by incorporating data from a new study; simulation settings can be found in Supplementary Tables 1-2, and results are available in Supplementary Fig. 1. These findings suggest that, as more source study data accumulate over time, the source-derived parameter estimates become more accurate, leading to greater improvement in estimation and prediction for smaller target studies.

Additionally, to evaluate robustness of PreTrained-Subtyping to violations of the shared-class assumption, we conducted a sensitivity analysis in which the class-specific manifest-variable prevalences were allowed to differ between the source and target studies. The results, reported in Supplementary Figs. 2–5, show that PreTrained-Subtyping remained beneficial under small to moderate shifts in class-specific manifest-variable prevalences, improving both estimation and prediction performance relative to local analysis. However, when the between-study shift became substantial, the transferred source information could become misaligned with the target study latent structure, and local analysis could become preferable. These findings clarify that the proposed method is reasonably robust to modest departures from the shared-class assumption, but its advantage diminishes when the source and target studies differ too strongly in their class definitions.

Multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C) study cohort description

The EHR data used in this study were extracted from the PEDSnet COVID-19 Database, which includes all healthcare encounters for children and adolescents under 21 years of age who were hospitalized with MIS-C from March 1, 2020, to September 30, 2022 (see more details in Method). This study was deemed not human subjects’ research by the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia’s Institutional Review Board and informed consent was waived. The target study includes 193 children from one member institution of PEDSnet and the source study contains 945 patients from 7 other member institutions that participated in the study earlier. Table 1 provides a summary of the two study cohorts. In the source study, the children were from 7 institutions with the largest site contributing 23.2% of patients and the smallest site contributing 3.2% of patients. Most children (41.6%) were between the ages of 5–11 years, with a predominance of males (61.8%) and non-Hispanic whites (40.6%). Measured using the Pediatric Medical Complexity Algorithm (PMCA)16, most (59.0%) children had no chronic conditions. 41.6% were admitted to the ICU, 18.0% were mechanically ventilated, and 1.6% died. The target study cohort has similar characteristics as the source study cohort.

For each MIS-C patient, we constructed eight binary organ-system involvement indicators: cardiac, gastrointestinal, hematological, neurologic, renal, respiratory, dermatologic and shock. We first coded detailed symptoms, conditions, and laboratory abnormalities as binary patient-level indicators based on any occurrence from 1 day prior to the entire duration of hospitalization. For each organ system, the corresponding indicator was then coded as 1 if any prespecified component item for that system was present during this window, and 0 otherwise. Thus, multiple codes or findings within the same organ system were aggregated using an “any-positive” rule. The model pre-trained on the source study carries reliable prior information on MIS-C subphenotypes

In the source study, a joint learning of the subphenotypes of MIS-C with eight manifest variables was conducted with 945 patients from 7 institutions using an iterative distributed LCA algorithm11. Based on log-likelihood value, information criteria including Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)17 and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)18, as well as the input from clinicians, three MIS-C subphenotypes were identified. Each of them was characterized by a unique vector of prevalence across the eight binary organ-system involvement indicator variables, i.e., a unique co-occurrence pattern of organ-system involvement for each subphenotype as shown in Supplementary Fig. 6. Class 1 represented severely ill patients with the most organ systems involved, including shock, cardiac, gastrointestinal, hematological, renal, and respiratory system. Class 2 presented moderately ill patients with 4 organ systems involved, including gastrointestinal, hematological, renal, and cardiac system. Class 3 represented patients with a mild form of MIS-C, and only hematological and renal organ systems were involved.

Based on the fitting results, 945 patients were each assigned probabilities of belonging to different latent classes based on their observed organ-system involvement. Each class was then further descriptively characterized by using detailed information about demographics, clinical outcomes (ICU, mechanical ventilation, death), diagnoses, laboratory values, medications, and duration of hospitalization stays. Note that these summaries were used to aid clinical interpretation rather than to provide independent validation of the class definitions. We conducted Cochran’s Q test19 at a significance level of 0.05 for each variable and the null hypothesis is no between-class heterogeneity. This fine-grained characterization of three subphenotypes revealed significant between-class differences in terms of various variables. Further details can be found in the Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. 7.

PreTrained-Subtyping improved prediction on organ-system involvement in the target study

Source study fitting results, i.e., the estimated prevalence and its variances, were regarded as prior information to aid in fitting the model on the target study with the PreTrained-Subtyping method. Figure 4 shows the three subphenotypes estimated by PreTrained-Subtyping, with the mixing proportions of Class 1, 2, and 3 being 44.8%, 17.8%, and 37.4%, respectively, in the target study.

Next we show that the PreTrained-Subtyping method outperformed local analysis in the target study. Due to the lack of the true manifest-variable prevalences, mixing proportions, and class memberships, we cannot calculate estimation error and class membership misclassification rate. Thus, we only compare the prediction accuracy of the manifest variables (i.e., eight organ-system involvement indicators) obtained using the fitting results returned by the local analysis and the PreTrained-Subtyping method, respectively. Each time we randomly divide the 193 patients in the target study cohort into a training set (129 patients) to fit the model, and a testing set (64 patients) to check the prediction performance. For each manifest variable, we use the remaining manifest variables together with the fitting results to make a prediction and calculate the AUC (see details in Method). Intuitively, this metric measures how well the latent class structure identified by a method captures the underlying co-occurrence pattern among manifest variables. This random splitting procedure is repeated 50 times, and the results are visualized in Fig. 5. Overall, for most of the eight manifest variables, PreTrained-Subtyping showed statistically significant improvements in prediction accuracy relative to local analysis based on the statistical testing results. The magnitude of improvement varied across organ-system variables, which is expected because manifest variable prediction depends on how strongly each variable is related to the shared co-occurrence structure captured by the latent classes and on how stable the corresponding estimate is when the model is fit using the limited target study data alone. Variables that are more strongly informed by the transferred latent class structure can benefit more from PreTrained-Subtyping, whereas variables that are already estimated relatively stable from the target study alone may show smaller gains.

To assess whether the observed benefit of PreTrained-Subtyping depends on a particular choice of target site, we conducted an additional leave-one-site-out validation by rotating the role of the target study across the PEDSnet sites with sufficient sample size. Six sites were eligible for this analysis, and two smaller sites (sample sizes are 64 and 30, respectively) were not used as target studies because their sample sizes were insufficient for repeated splitting into training and testing sets. For each eligible target site, we randomly assigned two-thirds of the patients to a training set and one-third to a testing set, repeated this procedure 50 times, and evaluated manifest variable prediction using the AUC averaged over all eight organ-system indicators. Across all six target-site scenarios, PreTrained-Subtyping achieved a statistically significant improvement in average AUC compared with local analysis, indicating that the observed gain is not driven by a single particularly favorable target site.

Characterizing three MIS-C subphenotypes using the entire PEDSnet MIS-C cohort and fine-grained variables

A comprehensive descriptive characterization of the identified three MIS-C subphenotypes was conducted for the entire PEDSnet MIS-C cohort based on both the source and target study information using fine-grained variables beyond the eight class-defining organ-system involvement indicators. Specifically, we sent the updated manifest-variable prevalence estimates obtained by PreTrained-Subtyping to the seven institutions in the source cohort to update the class membership prediction for each patient. Rather than classifying using the highest posterior probability, the posterior probabilities were used as weights to calculate weighted prevalence for each variable in the latent class characterization to reduce the impact of class misclassifications. We included demographic characteristics, clinical outcomes (ICU, mechanical ventilation, death), diagnoses, laboratory results, procedures, medications, and length of hospitalization stay in the characterization of latent classes. These summaries are intended to provide clinical context for the identified subphenotypes rather than used as independent validation of the latent class structure. We displayed the distributions of clinical indicator variables across latent classes using heatmaps of weighted prevalence (Fig. 6, Panels a–c). For continuous clinical variables, we calculated their sample mean, standard error, median and interquartile range across latent classes (Fig. 6, Panel d). Cochran’s Q test was conducted for all variables at a significance level of 0.05 and the null hypothesis is no between-class heterogeneity.

In all three classes, the majority of children were between the ages of 5 and 11, male, and non-Hispanic White. Specifically, Class 1 represented critically ill patients with a higher proportion of children having pre-existing medical complications and the highest rate of ICU admission among all classes. Children in this class experienced hypotension, shock, dehydration, cardiac dysfunction and various laboratory abnormalities including elevated d-dimer and troponin levels, lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia. They required treatment with vasopressors and fluids. Additionally, Class 1 had a higher occurrence of acute kidney injury and respiratory complications, including respiratory failure, pleural effusion, and the need for mechanical ventilation. Moreover, this class had the highest use of systemic steroids compared to the other classes. Class 2 represented an intermediate population, and its illness severity was lower than Class 1 but greater than Class 3. Children in this class displayed involvement of multiple organ systems, including dermatological manifestations such as rashes, gastrointestinal problems such as abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting, hematologic abnormalities like lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia, and elevated d-dimer, as well as renal dysfunction, cardiac dysfunction, and abnormal troponin levels. Most children in this class did not have pre-existing medical complexities, and it had the highest utilization of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory and antirheumatic medications. Class 3 consisted of a younger population experiencing a milder form of MIS-C. The number of systems involved in this class was fewer, primarily hematological and renal. Laboratory values indicated fluid and electrolyte imbalances, lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia, and elevated d-dimer levels. Most children in this class had no underlying medical complexity and this group had the highest rate of antihistamines for systemic use among all three classes. Among the three classes, there were significant differences between them for more than 80% of the variables considered.

Discussion

For handling highly complex syndromic diseases that involve various and heterogeneous conditions, such as MIS-C, the use of LCA to explore subphenotypes may help identify patients at risk for severe outcomes and facilitate more targeted therapies. However, fitting LCA requires a large sample size to ensure reliable estimates. To overcome this challenge, leveraging information from similar studies to aid in subphenotype identification in a study with a relatively small sample size can lead to more generalizable and robust research results, as well as refine existing knowledge. Therefore, we proposed a one-shot summary statistics-based framework, PreTrained-Subtyping, that transfers the knowledge of shared subphenotypes pre-trained in a large-scale source study to a target study to achieve joint learning.

The key assumption underlying PreTrained-Subtyping is a heterogeneous latent class model, where both the source study and the target study are assumed to share the same set of latent classes, with between-study heterogeneity explained by different class mixing proportions11. This assumption is crucial to avoid the class-matching issue that commonly arises when the definition of latent classes differs across studies, which could bring bias into the transfer learning procedure if ignored. On the other hand, the study-specific class mixing proportion can provide a reasonable explanation of the between-study population heterogeneity. As Fig. 1 shows, the information of the shared latent class estimated from the source study can be directly transferred to the target study as prior information to update the latent class estimates without requiring a class-matching step, and the target study-specific class mixing proportion is estimated to take account the potential differences between the two study populations. Throughout this procedure, only summary-level statistics are transferred once, and no patient-level data are shared.

To demonstrate the effectiveness of PreTrained-Subtyping, we conducted simulation studies to compare its estimation and prediction performance with the commonly used local analysis and the gold standard pooled analysis. We customized the simulation to mimic the MIS-C study setting, with a small sample size for the target study and simulated binary manifest variables with three latent classes. We also increased the source study size to see its impacts. The simulation results showed that PreTrained-Subtyping achieved higher estimation and prediction accuracy than the local analysis and performed similarly to the gold standard pooled analysis. This pattern is expected. First, since the source and target studies are assumed to share the same latent classes under our working model, the transferred source information remains highly relevant to the target study, allowing PreTrained-Subtyping to perform similarly to pooled analysis. Second, in the small target study, local analysis relies only on limited target study data and therefore produces less stable estimates of the latent class structure. In contrast, PreTrained-Subtyping incorporates source-derived prior information on the shared class-specific manifest-variable prevalences, which improves its performance in the target study. Notably, the improvement became more significant with an increasing source study size, indicating that, as the source study sample size increases, the source-derived prior information becomes more accurate, and the resulting improvement in target-study estimation and prediction becomes more pronounced. Moreover, by incorporating data from a new study, PreTrained-Subtyping also improves the pre-trained knowledge, and these updated estimates can serve as improved source information for future analyses.

PreTrained-Subtyping was applied to find subphenotypes of MIS-C, resulting in three clinically meaningful groups. Class 1 represented critically ill patients, with a higher proportion of children having underlying medical complexity and the highest rate of ICU admission among all classes. These children had the most organ systems involved, including shock, cardiac, gastrointestinal, hematological, renal, and respiratory system, and required treatment with vasopressors and fluids. Class 1 also had the highest usage of systemic steroids among all classes. Class 2 presented moderately ill patients with four organ systems involved, including gastrointestinal, hematological, renal, and cardiac system. Most children in this class had no underlying medical complexity, and this class had the highest use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory and antirheumatic medications. Class 3 represented patients with a mild form of MIS-C, and only hematological and renal organ systems were involved. Most children in this class had no underlying medical complexity and this group had the highest rate of systemic antihistamines use among all three classes. The identified MIS-C subphenotype structure can be used to predict the unknown manifest variable values, specifically to predict unknown organ-system involvement (see Fig. 5). In the MIS-C study, we also identified hospital-specific class mixing proportions, which are interpreted as the site-specific prevalence of shared subphenotypes under the working model. In practice, differences in mixing proportions may reflect variation in patient case mix, referral patterns, disease severity among hospitalized patients, ascertainment, coding practices, and local care patterns.

PreTrained-Subtyping is a powerful method for transferring knowledge from a source study to a target study to improve latent class analysis; however, it also has some limitations that need further investigation. First, the current framework does not directly address settings in which the latent class definitions (i.e., class-specific manifest-variable prevalences) themselves differ substantially across studies. Our sensitivity analysis (see Supplementary Material) demonstrates that our approach is robust to small or moderate shift of latent class definitions across studies, but the benefit of transfer can diminish if the latent classes differ substantially across studies. Second, the informative priors used in the target study analysis depend on accurate estimation of the source study class-specific manifest-variable prevalences and their associated variances. When the source study sample size is small and these quantities are poorly estimated, the resulting prior specification may be less reliable. Third, the number of classes is determined by the source study analysis, and in the case of diseases that change over time, new variants may give rise to new subphenotypes that would require modifications to the method. With comments from experts in certain fields, when necessary, the PreTrained-Subtyping method can be modified to allow adding new latent classes into the analysis by setting noninformative priors for the parameters of the new latent classes. Moreover, the current framework can only integrate knowledge from a single source study and the way to optimally leverage multiple sources of prior information remains to be explored. One solution is to use the meta-analysis technique to first combine subphenotype information from multiple sources. However, the class-matching issue will commonly arise here due to the lack of an optimal rule to find matched classes between source studies.

The current PreTrained-Subtyping is developed for binary manifest variables, the related methods for nominal, ordinal, and count data can be formulated similarly and will be our future work. Another promising direction of future work is to extend the framework to accommodate continuous variables, such as body mass index (BMI) and laboratory results, which are critical clinical indices broadly used in biomedical studies. What’s more, from a clinical point of view, in the current MIS-C study, the knowledge of MIS-C subphenotypes is derived from 1138 MIS-C patients identified in 8 hospitals participating in PEDSnet. To further update this knowledge by incorporating information from more MIS-C patients, as one of our future work, we plan to apply our approach to TriNetX, a global network of healthcare organizations covering EHR from more than 275 million patients in more than 120 healthcare organizations20.

From the source study, the pre-trained knowledge of LCA is obtained by finding parameters π and source study-specific class mixing proportions λ1=(λ11,…,λ1C) to maximize the source study log-likelihood function logL1Y1i,Z1i. By applying the EM algorithm, we can get the estimated πˆ=π1ˆ,…,πCˆ, and their related variances are estimated from the inverse of the information matrix evaluated at the estimated parameters following the standard large sample properties of maximum likelihood estimates. The poLCA function from the R package “poLCA” has implemented this estimation procedure, and the calculation details and function document can be found in22. These information can be then formulated as the prior density from a Bayesian perspective to aid fitting LCA on the target study23. Simulation study

Here we use synthetic data for methodological validation. The reason for using synthetic data is because in the real-data MIS-C application, the true latent class memberships, class-specific manifest-variable prevalences, and study-specific class mixing proportions are unknown. As a result, quantities such as estimation error and class-membership misclassification cannot be evaluated directly in the real data. In contrast, simulation allows us to generate source and target datasets from a known latent class model, so that the true parameter values and true latent classes are available for benchmarking. Our simulation design was chosen to reflect the key structural features of the MIS-C application, including a relatively large source study, a much smaller target study, shared latent classes across studies, and between-study heterogeneity represented through different class mixing proportions. We then evaluated method performance by comparing the estimated parameters with their true values and by assessing prediction accuracy for latent class membership and manifest variables on test data.

In this simulation study, we compare PreTrained-Subtyping with the local analysis, which applies the EM algorithm to only the target study’s dataset, and the pooled analysis, which applies the EM algorithm to the dataset pooled from both two studies. MIS-C cohort construction and variable definition

In PEDSnet COVID-19 Database, with the criteria including receiving a COVID-19 diagnosis or vaccination, having a COVID-19 test (PCR, serology, or antigen), or showing evidence of respiratory illness or post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 and having a visit at any of the eight PEDSnet institutions between March 2020 and September 2022, we identified 1,955,736 patients in total. We then narrowed the patient pool by limiting it to patients who had at least one diagnosis code for MIS-C assigned during an inpatient stay or one day before an inpatient stay, resulting in a total of 1280 patients. We combined hospitalizations if the discharge date of the first hospitalization was within 24 hours of the subsequent hospitalization admission date. We further excluded patients with a Kawasaki disease diagnosis and those who had missing admission or discharge dates, and the final MIS-C cohort included 1138 children.
