ABSTRACT
This multi-institutional randomized phase II clinical trial (NCT03414684) investigated the efficacy and safety of carboplatin plus nivolumab compared to carboplatin in metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (mTNBC). The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS) in a modified intention-to-treat (mITT) population of patients with chemotherapy-naïve (i.e., first-line) mTNBC. Secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), confirmed objective response rate, confirmed clinical benefit rate, time to and duration of confirmed objective response, safety, and tolerability. Clinical outcomes were evaluated in the PD-L1-positive subgroup ( ≥ 1% immune cells; SP142 clone) per central review. Biospecimens were collected for correlative analyses. 75 patients were enrolled and treated between 2/2018-9/2020. Among the mITT population (n = 62), median PFS was 4.2 months with carboplatin plus nivolumab vs 5.5 months with carboplatin. Median OS did not significantly differ between arms (16.8 vs 11.1 months, respectively). In PD-L1-positive mTNBC patients (n = 24), median PFS was 8.3 vs 4.7 months; median OS was 17.6 vs 10.7 months. Grade ≥3 adverse events occurred in 56.8% of patients in the combination arm and 65.8% in the carboplatin arm. Carboplatin plus nivolumab did not significantly improve PFS compared to carboplatin in patients overall; however, a trend toward improved outcomes was observed in PD-L1-positive mTNBC patients. High mutational burden, interferon-gamma signaling, early circulating tumor DNA reduction, low baseline serum thymidine kinase activity, and urea cycle dysregulation in the microbiome emerged as potential predictors of response to immune checkpoint inhibitors with platinum.

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is an aggressive subtype that accounts for approximately 10–15% of breast cancers1 and has a higher rate of distant recurrence within the first 3 years of diagnosis compared to other breast cancer subtypes2,3. With standard chemotherapy, median overall survival (OS) in patients with metastatic TNBC (mTNBC) ranges between 13–18 months4. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) against PD-1/L1 have yielded promising outcomes in combination with chemotherapy in patients with mTNBC. In the IMpassion130 trial, atezolizumab plus nab-paclitaxel resulted in improved progression-free survival (PFS) compared to placebo plus nab-paclitaxel in patients with mTNBC5. Although OS was not significantly different between the two groups, atezolizumab plus nab-paclitaxel resulted in a clinically meaningful improvement in OS among patients with PD-L1-positive tumors (defined as ≥ 1% of immune cells [IC] staining positive with the SP142 assay)6. In the KEYNOTE-355 trial, pembrolizumab plus investigator’s choice of chemotherapy (paclitaxel, nab-paclitaxel, or gemcitabine plus carboplatin) significantly improved PFS and OS compared to placebo plus chemotherapy in patients with treatment-naïve mTNBC with a PD-L1 combined positive score (CPS) ≥ 10 using the 22C3 assay, leading to the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval of pembrolizumab in this patient population7,8.

Carboplatin is a DNA-damaging agent with demonstrated clinical activity against mTNBC9,10. In pre-clinical models, platinum agents have been shown to upregulate MHC class I expression and promote the recruitment, proliferation, and cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T-cells11–13. In the phase II TONIC trial, patients with mTNBC were randomized to one of four different immune induction treatments (irradiation to a single lesion, low-dose cyclophosphamide, cisplatin, or doxorubicin) or to a control group with a two-week waiting period. All patients subsequently received single-agent nivolumab (PD-1 inhibitor). The objective response rate (ORR) in the induction cohort was 20%, with the highest rates seen in patients treated with doxorubicin (35%) or cisplatin (23%)14.

To further explore the potential of combining immune-priming chemotherapy with ICI, we conducted an open-label, randomized phase II trial of carboplatin with or without nivolumab in patients with mTNBC unselected by PD-L1 status. Clinical outcomes were also evaluated in the PD-L1-positive subgroup ( ≥ 1% IC; SP142 clone) per central review. Participants who were randomized to the control arm could crossover to receive nivolumab at the time of disease progression.

Results

Study population and treatment characteristics

A total of 78 patients were randomized between February 12, 2018 and September 23, 2020, of whom 75 started protocol therapy: 37 carboplatin plus nivolumab, 38 carboplatin (Supplementary Fig. 1). Patient characteristics are included in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1. Median age was 59.1 (range 25.4-75.8). Four participants (5.3%) had a known germline BRCA1 (n = 3) or BRCA2 (n = 1) mutation. In total, 7 patients (9.3%) had prior platinum (including 6 patients who received platinum-based neo-/adjuvant therapy for breast cancer and one additional patient who received platinum for a prior non-breast malignancy), and 13.3% of patients were diagnosed with de novo mTNBC. Thirteen patients (17.3%) had received prior chemotherapy for advanced disease; the most common agents were capecitabine (8.0%) and paclitaxel (6.7%). Central PD-L1 testing using the SP142 assay (metastatic/recurrent tumor, n = 52; primary tumor, n = 23) revealed that 37.3% of patients (14 patients in each treatment arm) had PD-L1-positive tumors. Patient characteristics were generally balanced between patients with PD-L1-positive and PD-L1-negative mTNBC, and only differed significantly for visceral involvement (PD-L1-positive, 10/24 [41.7%]; Among the 62 participants who had not received prior chemotherapy for mTNBC, comprising the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) population (Supplementary Table 2), 32 were assigned to treatment with carboplatin plus nivolumab and 30 to carboplatin. Two patients (3.2%) had known germline BRCA mutations (BRCA1, n = 1; BRCA2, n = 1), both randomized to the monotherapy arm, and 6 patients (9.7%) had prior platinum. PD-L1-positivity was centrally confirmed in 24 patients (38.7%), with 13 and 11 patients assigned to each treatment arm, respectively.

Efficacy in mITT Population

At a median follow-up of 27.3 months in the mITT population (n = 62), median PFS was 4.2 months (95% CI: 2.7-11.5) in the carboplatin plus nivolumab arm and 5.5 months (95% CI: 4.4-19.2) in the carboplatin arm (stratified hazard ratio [HR]: 0.95; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.49-1.84; one-sided stratified log-rank p = 0.44) (Fig. 1A; Table 2). In Cox regression models, elevated LDH, shorter disease-free interval (DFI) ( < 12 months vs ≥ 12 months/de novo), and presence of liver metastases were associated with worse PFS, of which LDH and DFI remained significant after adjustment in the multivariable model (Supplementary Tables 3, 4). Median OS in the mITT population was 16.8 months (95% CI: 10.4-NA) vs 11.1 months (95% CI: 8.2–24.4) for carboplatin with vs without nivolumab (stratified HR: 0.80; 95% CI: 0.43–1.48; one-sided stratified log-rank p = 0.24) (Table 2; Fig. 1B). The only factor significantly associated with shorter OS was DFI ( < 12 months vs ≥ 12 months/de novo metastatic [ref], unadjusted HR: 5.50; 95% CI: 2.27–13.30, p = 0.0002), which remained significant in the multivariable analysis (Supplementary Tables 5, 6).

The confirmed ORR was 25.0% (95% CI: 11.5%–43.4%) in patients receiving carboplatin plus nivolumab and 23.3% (95% CI: 9.9%–42.3%) with carboplatin (odds ratio [OR]: 1.09; 95% CI: 0.29-4.18; Fisher’s exact p = 1) (Table 2, Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. 2A). One confirmed complete response (CR) was observed in each arm. Median duration of confirmed response (DOR) was 19.3 months (95% CI: 16.8-NA) and 7.7 months (95% CI: 2.7-NA), respectively. Median time to confirmed objective response (TTOR) among responders was 3.1 months (95% CI: 2.56-NA) and 2.96 months (95% CI: 2.79-NA), respectively (Table 2).

Exploratory outcomes among the remaining patients who had received one prior chemotherapy regimen for metastatic disease (n = 13) are displayed in Fig. 1A and Supplementary Table 7.

Efficacy outcomes by PD-L1 status

Among the mITT population, central PD-L1 testing and pathologist assessment of TILs were performed mostly in metastatic tumor samples (n = 41, 66.1%), of which 24 were baseline research biopsies, and 17 were archival tumor samples collected in the metastatic setting (Supplementary Table 8). Among patients with PD-L1-positive tumors (n = 24), there was a trend toward improved PFS favoring the combination arm. Median PFS was 8.3 months (95% CI: 2.5-NA) with carboplatin plus nivolumab and 4.7 months (95% CI: 1.6-NA) with carboplatin (stratified HR: 0.54; 95% CI: 0.18-1.62; two-sided stratified log-rank p = 0.27) (Fig. 1A). For patients with PD-L1-negative tumors (n = 38), median PFS was 4.1 months (95% CI: 2.9–11.5) and 5.5 months (95% CI: 4.4-NA), respectively (stratified HR: 1.30; 95% CI: 0.56–2.98; two-sided stratified log-rank p = 0.53) (Supplementary Table 9).

In the PD-L1-positive subgroup, median OS was 17.6 months (95% CI: 4.9-NA) with the combination and 10.7 months (95% CI: 6.7-NA) with carboplatin (stratified HR: 0.75; 95% CI: 0.26-2.16; two-sided stratified log-rank p = 0.61) (Fig. 1B). Among those with PD-L1-negative tumors, median OS was 14.6 months (95% CI: 10.4-NA) and 11.5 months (95% CI: 8.2-NA), respectively (stratified HR: 0.82; 95% CI: 0.38–1.78; two-sided stratified log-rank p = 0.61). Similar response rates were observed between treatment arms by PD-L1 status (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. 2B).

An exploratory analysis of outcomes by PD-L1 using antibody clones 22C3 and 28-8 was also performed. PD-L1 positivity rate was 14.5% (9/62) with the 22C3 assay, and 74.2% (46/62) with the 28-8 assay (Supplementary Fig. 3). Overall agreement across the three assays was low (Light’s Kappa = 0.18). There was no significant difference in PFS or OS between treatment arms among patients with PD-L1-positive tumors whether using the 22C3 (carboplatin/nivolumab [n = 5], carboplatin [n = 4]; median PFS not reached vs 10.6 months [HR: 0.74; 95% CI: 0.10–5.34]; median OS, 16.8 vs 17.9 months [HR: 1.41; 95% CI: 0.23–8.53]) or 28-8 assay (carboplatin/nivolumab [n = 25], carboplatin [n = 21]; median PFS, 4.2 vs 10.6 months [HR: 1.29; 95% CI: 0.63–2.66]; median OS, 17.6 vs 17.9 months [HR: 1.04; 95% CI: 0.50–2.12]).

Stromal tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes

In the mITT population, pathologist-assessed stromal tumor-infiltrating lymphoctyes (sTILs) were ≥ 10% in 23 patients (37.1%), < 10% in 38 patients (61.3%), and not assessed in one patient (1.6%) due to insufficient tissue. A higher proportion of patients in the carboplatin alone arm had sTILs ≥ 10% (50%) compared to the carboplatin plus nivolumab arm (26%). No significant associations between sTILs and clinical outcomes were observed. Among patients with sTILs ≥ 10%, median PFS was 3.2 months (95% CI: 1.3-NA) with carboplatin plus nivolumab and 5.5 months (95% CI: 4.7-NA) with carboplatin (stratified HR: 2.68; 95% CI: 0.68-10.59; two-sided stratified log-rank p = 0.15). In patients with sTILs < 10%, median PFS was 7.7 months (95% CI: 2.9-26.0) vs 4.7 months (95% CI: 2.2-NA), respectively (stratified HR: 0.86; 95% CI: 0.34-2.21; two-sided stratified log-rank p = 0.76) (Supplementary Table 10). In the sTILs ≥ 10% subgroup, median OS was 14.3 months (95% CI: 4.0-NA) vs 13.5 months (95% CI: 8.7-NA), respectively (stratified HR: 1.18; 95% CI: 0.36–3.92; two-sided stratified log-rank p = 0.76). In patients with sTILs < 10%, median OS was 18.1 months (95% CI: 9.3-NA) vs 9.8 months (95% CI: 4.7-NA), respectively (stratified HR: 0.75; 95% CI: 0.33–1.72; two-sided stratified log-rank p = 0.50). The confirmed ORR in patients with sTILs ≥ 10% was 12.5% with carboplatin plus nivolumab and 33.3% with carboplatin (OR: 0.30; 95% CI: 0.01–3.63; Fisher’s exact p = 0.37) and, for sTILs < 10%, 30.4% vs 13.3% (OR: 2.77; 95% CI: 0.42–31.83; Fisher’s exact p = 0.27).

Safety and tolerability

All patients who received at least one dose of study drug were considered evaluable for toxicity (n = 75). Treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) grade 2 or higher occurred in 89.3% of patients who received carboplatin with or without nivolumab. The most common TRAEs included thrombocytopenia (53.3%), anemia (49.3%), neutropenia (44.0%), fatigue (33.3%), and nausea (16.0%) (Table 3 and Supplementary Table 11). Grade 3 or higher TRAEs were more common with carboplatin monotherapy (65.8%) than carboplatin plus nivolumab (56.8%), although this difference was not statistically significant (OR: 0.68; 95% CI: 0.27-1.74; Fisher’s exact p = 0.48). Grade 4 toxicity occurred in three patients (8.1%) who received carboplatin plus nivolumab (thrombocytopenia, n = 1; sepsis, n = 1; hyperbilirubinemia, n = 1) and two patients (5.3%) who received carboplatin (thrombocytopenia, n = 1; neutropenia, n = 1), with recovery of all events except hyperbilirubinemia (ongoing at last follow-up). Treatment-Related Adverse Events Occurring in ≥ 5% of the Overall Study Cohort

G, grade.

Immune-related adverse events, defined as TRAEs grade 2 or higher attributed to nivolumab (+/- carboplatin) by the investigator, occurred in 73% of patients who received carboplatin plus nivolumab (Supplementary Tables 12, 13), of which the most frequent toxicities included: fatigue (27.0%), anemia (18.9%), neutropenia (16.2%), leukopenia (16.2%), diarrhea (10.8%), and hypothyroidism (10.8%). Two grade 4 immune-related TRAEs were reported: thrombocytopenia (n = 1), hyperbilirubinemia (n = 1).

Dosing of carboplatin and/or nivolumab was held or delayed in 67.6% of patients in the combination arm compared to 65.8% in the monotherapy arm. Dose reduction of carboplatin occurred in 67.6% and 57.9% of patients in each treatment arm, respectively (Supplementary Table 14). Progressive disease was the most common reason for treatment discontinuation in both arms (carboplatin/nivolumab, 83.8%; carboplatin, 84.2%) (Supplementary Table 15). Eighteen participants assigned to receive carboplatin alone (18/38; 47.4%) crossed over at progression to nivolumab (n = 8) or nab-paclitaxel plus nivolumab (n = 10) (Supplementary Tables 16–22 and Supplementary Figs. 4, 5).

OncoPanel

Somatic targeted NGS results were available from 44 samples (Arm A, n = 21; Arm B, n = 23) (Supplementary Fig. 6A), including 13 primary and 31 metastatic tumors. The most frequently altered genes across all samples were: TP53 (86%), CDKN2A (18%), PIK3CA (18%), CDKN2B (16%), FGFR1 (14%), CEBPA (9%), PTEN (9%), AKT2 (7%), CCNE1 (7%), NOTCH1 (7%), RB1 (7%), and TET2 (7%). When restricting to oncogenic alterations, the mutational and copy number landscape did not significantly differ between treatment arms, by PD-L1 status, or by sTILs (Supplementary Fig. 6B–D). No significant associations between single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) or copy-number variations (CNVs) with PFS were observed in this cohort.

Median TMB was 7.6 mut/Mb (Arm A, 6.8 mut/Mb; Arm B, 7.6 mut/Mb). No significant differences in TMB were observed between responders and non-responders across all samples (p = 0.26), by treatment arm (Arm A, p = 0.53; Arm B, p = 0.46), or by PD-L1 status (PD-L1-positive, p = 0.20; PD-L1-negative, p = 0.92), although there was a trend toward higher TMB in responders in the PD-L1-positive subgroup (Supplementary Fig. 6E–G). TMB measured as a continuous (HR: 1.01; 95% CI: 0.96–1.07; p = 0.70) or categorical (low vs high [ref], HR: 0.94; 95% CI: 0.35–2.50; p = 0.90) variable was not significantly associated with PFS.

Cox proportional hazards models were fit to evaluate the association of gene alterations with survival, adjusting for prior platinum, age, and PD-L1 status (SP142). Following multiple hypothesis correction, no gene alterations were significantly associated with PFS or OS in either arm, although analyses were limited by small sample size (Supplementary Figs. 8, 9). CCND2 amplification exhibited a trend toward improved PFS in the carboplatin plus nivolumab arm (HR 0.04, unadjusted p-value = 0.03, FDR q-value = 0.39) but not in the carboplatin arm (HR 0.91, unadjusted p-value = 0.90, FDR q-value = 0.95), and patients with PIK3CA mutations had worse PFS with carboplatin (HR 10.70, unadjusted p-value = 0.03, FDR q-value = 0.39) but not in the combination arm (HR 0.21, unadjusted p-value = 0.34, FDR q-value = 0.77). Trends toward worse OS for BRCA2 deletion (HR 18.0, unadjusted p-value = 0.02, FDR q-value = 0.37) and better OS for MYC amplification (HR 0.1, unadjusted p-value = 0.02, FDR q-value = 0.37) were observed in the carboplatin arm, though not with the combination.

Cox models were also fit to evaluate the association of TMB with survival in each arm. TMB was tested as both a continuous and binary variable (with samples divided into TMB-high and TMB-low groups based on the median TMB in the overall cohort). In each of these models, higher TMB was significantly associated with improved PFS with carboplatin plus nivolumab (pcont = 0.040, pbinary = 0.008) but not with carboplatin alone (pcont = 0.609, pbinary = 0.514), aligning with the OncoPanel results (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. 10). Higher TMB was also associated with improved OS in the combination arm when analyzed as a binary variable (pcont = 0.103, pbinary = 0.039) but not in the carboplatin arm (pcont = 0.550, pbinary = 0.567).

In total, 74 bulk RNA-sequencing samples derived from 44 patients passed QC. Of these, 39 samples were collected at baseline prior to protocol therapy: carboplatin plus nivolumab, n = 22; carboplatin, n = 17. Within each arm, differential gene expression analysis was performed to determine upregulated or downregulated genes between responders (confirmed CR or partial response [PR]) and non-responders (stable disease [SD] or progressive disease [PD] as best overall response), while controlling for prior platinum, age, and PD-L1 status. GSEA pathway enrichment using the MSigDB Hallmark gene sets was performed based on the differential gene expression within each arm. Immune-related pathways such as the Hallmark Interferon Gamma Response and IL-6/JAK/STAT3 Signaling were significantly upregulated in responders compared to non-responders in the carboplatin plus nivolumab arm (FDR q-value < 0.05), but not in the carboplatin arm. Conversely, cell-cycle related pathways such as G2-M Checkpoint, E2F Targets, Mitotic Spindle, MYC Targets V1 and MYC Targets V2 were significantly upregulated in responders in the carboplatin arm (FDR q-value < 0.05), but not in the combination arm (Supplementary Fig. 7B, C).

To assess if immune-related signatures that were upregulated in responders vs non-responders were associated with pathologist-assessed TILs, we assessed transcriptional signature scores for Hallmark Interferon Gamma Response and IL-6/JAK/STAT3 Signaling in the baseline samples in each treatment arm, dichotomizing between high ( ≥ 10%) and low ( < 10%) subgroups of sTILs and iTILs. Overall, there was a trend toward higher transcriptional scores for both signatures in patients with high sTILs or iTILs, although this only reached statistical significance (p < 0.05) for IL-6/JAK/STAT3 Signaling and sTILs in the carboplatin plus nivolumab arm (Supplementary Fig. 11).

The distribution of TNBCtype-4 subtypes was: basal-like1 (BL1), 23.08% (9/39); basal-like 2 (BL2), 25.64% (10/39); mesenchymal (M); 23.08% (9/39); luminal androgen receptor (LAR), 25.64% (10/39); equivocal, 2.5% (1/39) (Supplementary Fig. 7D). TNBCtype-4 subtypes were not associated with PFS or OS (Supplementary Fig. 12). Within each subtype, PFS and OS did not significantly differ between treatment arms (Supplementary Fig. 7E), consistent with Lehmann et al.16. Among all patients who received nivolumab (with carboplatin in the first course of therapy, or alone or with nab-paclitaxel at cross-over), no significant association was found between TNBCtype-4 subtypes and the presence of Grade 2 or higher (p = 0.32) or Grade 3 or higher (p = 0.48) immune-related TRAEs.

ctDNA dynamics

Of 57 patients with available plasma, baseline samples (+/− C3D1 or end of treatment [EOT]) met assay QC metrics for downstream analysis in 42 patients (baseline, n = 42; C3D1, n = 40; EOT, n = 19): carboplatin/nivolumab, n = 25; carboplatin, n = 17. Patients with ctDNA not detected at baseline (n = 6) had a trend toward longer PFS (14.5 vs 7.7 months; HR: 0.73; p = 0.5) and OS (NR vs 17.9 months; HR: 0.16; p = 0.072) compared to those with ctDNA detected (n = 36). Of the 32 patients with paired baseline and C3D1 samples (21 Arm A, 11 Arm B), 10 had PD-L1-positive tumors, and 22 were PD-L1-negative. Of the 28 patients with ctDNA detected at baseline and a paired C3D1 sample, at C3D1 10 were not detected, 4 had a 90–99% reduction in ctDNA, and 4 had 50–89% reduction.

Across the cohort, patients with ≥ 90% reduction from baseline to C3D1, or baseline ctDNA not detected or detected but not quantifiable (ND/NQ) who remained ND at C3D1, had more favorable survival compared to those without reduction: median PFS, 11.6 vs 3.0 months (HR: 0.23; 95% CI: 0.08-0.71; p = 0.010); median OS, 17.3 vs 8.6 months (HR: 0.30; 95% CI: 0.13-0.71; p = 0.006). Similar results were found using a ≥ 50% cutoff for ctDNA reduction: median PFS, 8.4 vs 2.9 months (HR: 0.13; 95% CI: 0.03-0.52; p = 0.004); median OS, 17.3 vs 7.7 months (HR: 0.31; 95% CI: 0.13-0.73; p = 0.007) (Fig. 2C, D). When comparing outcomes in patients with and without ≥ 90% (or ≥ 50%) ctDNA reduction, excluding those with baseline ctDNA ND/NQ, ctDNA reduction remained significantly associated with improved PFS and OS (Supplementary Fig. 13). Similar trends were observed in both treatment arms, and in PD-L1-positive and PD-L1-negative groups. Ten of the 32 patients with paired baseline and C3D1 results had an increase in ctDNA. At C3D1, the confirmed objective response was: SD, n = 7; PD, n = 2; Non-CR/Non-PD, n = 1. None achieved CR or PR, and 6 developed PD within ≤ 3 months (Fig. 2E).

Among 20 patients with paired baseline, C3D1 and C5D1 results, 7 were ctDNA negative at C5D1, of whom 4 remained progression-free at 12 months from C5D1. Of 22 patients with paired baseline, C3D1 and any additional on-treatment result, 12 had an increase in ctDNA prior to progression (including 3 who were ctDNA negative at baseline), and 2 others had an increase at progression.

Among 16 patients with paired baseline and EOT plasma, genes with the most frequent newly emerging or lost (i.e., detected at baseline and not EOT) alterations were TP53 (emerging, n = 10; lost, n = 4) and NF1 (emerging, n = 3; lost, n = 3). Of the 15 TP53 emerging variants detected among 10 patients, 13 likely derived from clonal hematopoiesis. Alterations in TP53 (n = 11), PIK3CA (n = 4), DNMT3A (n = 4), and PTEN (n = 3) were frequently identified both at baseline and EOT.

Thymidine kinase 1 Activity (TKa)

Baseline and C3D1 samples were available for TKa analysis in 41 patients (carboplatin/nivolumab, n = 22; carboplatin, n = 19). Mean and median TKa values at baseline were 964 DuA and 461 DuA, respectively, with a range of 74 DuA to 9070 DuA, reflecting the heterogeneous nature of TNBC. Mean and median baseline TKa values were 1038.2 DuA and 338 DuA in the carboplatin/nivolumab arm, and 878.1 DuA and 486 DuA in the carboplatin arm. Median fold-change in TKa between baseline and C3D1 was 0.88 (1.01 and 0.61 for each treatment arm, respectively). Baseline TKa greater than or equal to the sample median was associated with worse PFS (median PFS 4.1 vs 18.0 months; unadjusted HR: 3.71; 95% CI: 1.54-8.93; p = 0.003), and OS (median OS 14.6 months vs NR; unadjusted HR: 2.73; 95% CI: 1.22-6.15; p = 0.015). These effects remained after adjustment for treatment arm, PD-L1 (SP142), prior platinum, LDH, and presence of liver metastases: adjusted PFS, HR: 5.39 (95% CI: 1.58–18.36; p = 0.007); adjusted OS, HR: 2.77 (95% CI: 1.04–7.38; p = 0.04). For PFS, the trend was more pronounced in the combination arm (interaction HR: 0.21; 95% CI: 0.04–1.05; p = 0.057) (Supplementary Fig. 14). There was no association between fold-change in TKa and PFS or OS, nor between baseline TKa and sTILs.

Microbiome

Among the 52 patients with a baseline sample (carboplatin plus nivolumab, n = 25; carboplatin, n = 27), median alpha diversity (Inverse Simpson) was 10.3 among all patients, and 10.8 and 9.1 in each treatment arm, respectively. Patient characteristics with a trend toward significant association with alpha diversity in the overall cohort included race (p = 0.01), ECOG performance status (p = 0.07), and LDH (p = 0.09), albeit confidence intervals were wide. Alpha diversity was not associated with PFS or OS in the overall cohort or by treatment arm (Supplementary Fig. 15A–C and Supplementary Tables 24, 25).

Analysis of unique species, pathways, and enzymes was performed in the overall cohort. A total of 161 unique species were represented in the sequenced samples, of which 130 had a sufficient proportion of non-zero values and were included in the analysis. No species were associated with PFS or OS after FDR correction. 359 unique pathways were represented in the sequenced samples; 355 had non-zero values and were analyzed. Of 31 pathways that were associated with PFS, only one (PWY-4984: urea cycle) remained significant after FDR correction. For this pathway, increasing activity was associated with improved PFS (continuous HR 0.53; 95% CI: 0.39-0.72; p = 4.66e-5; q = 0.017). Subsequent classification and regression tree (CART) analysis identified a relative abundance of 0.003 as the optimal cut point to differentiate PFS (HR: 0.34; 95% CI: 0.17–0.68) (Supplementary Fig. 15D). From further exploratory analysis by treatment arm, this effect was pronounced among patients treated with carboplatin (HR: 0.10; 95% CI: 0.02–0.48), but did not reach statistical significance among patients treated with carboplatin plus nivolumab (HR: 0.56; 95% CI: 0.22–1.42). A total of 34 pathways were associated with OS before adjusting for multiple testing, none of which remained significant after FDR correction.

All 1108 enzymes that were represented in the sequenced samples were explored. An association with PFS was detected for 115 enzymes; none remained significant after FDR correction, although there was a trend for Arginase (HR: 0.58; 95% CI: 0.44–0.76; p = 8.90e-5; q = 0.099). Of 173 enzymes associated with OS, one enzyme (N-substituted formamide deformylase) remained significant after FDR correction, and increasing relative abundance was significantly associated with poorer OS (HR: 2.09; 95% CI: 1.53-2.86; p = 4.28e-6, q = 0.005). Subsequent CART analysis identified a relative abundance of 6e-05 as the optimal cut point to differentiate OS (HR: 13.81; 95% CI 1.88-101.20) (Supplementary Fig. 15E).

4D Q-Plasia OncoReader™ (QPOR™)

Baseline WSI were available in 52 patients (26 in each treatment arm). In the overall cohort, higher baseline levels of the complex immune response signature CmbI, previously reported to be predictive of NAC response in TNBC17, were associated with worse PFS (HR for above vs below or at the sample median: 2.14; 95% CI: 1.05-4.37), however, this effect was not significant after FDR correction. Patients with CmbI ≤ − 0.033 had longer median PFS (6.0 vs. 4.8 months). No associations with PFS were observed in either arm separately. Higher baseline levels of the G1/S deregulation signatures AvG1SpI and MG1SpI were associated with better OS in the overall cohort (continuous HR [95% CI]: 0.23 [0.06-0.95] and 0.24 [0.07-0.83], respectively), but these effects also did not remain significant after FDR correction. Dichotomization of these biomarkers at the respective sample medians (2.56 for both signatures) showed longer median OS among patients with values > 2.56 (AvG1SpI: 18.1 vs 10.5 months; MG1SpI: 18.7 vs 10.5 months). No associations with OS were observed in either arm separately. Lastly, higher baseline levels of the G1/S deregulation signature MCG1SI were associated with higher odds of confirmed response in the combination arm (continuous OR: 2.27; 95% CI: 1.14–5.78), but did not retain significance after FDR correction. Confirmed ORR was 71.4% vs 28.6% for patients with MCG1S greater than the sample median (0.29) and less than the sample median, respectively. No associations with ORR were observed in the overall population.

Discussion

In this randomized phase II trial, the combination of carboplatin with nivolumab did not result in a significant improvement in survival compared to carboplatin alone in patients with mTNBC, unselected by PD-L1 status. Consistent with previously reported trials, a trend toward improved PFS favoring carboplatin plus nivolumab was observed in patients with PD-L1-positive tumors, as well as a trend toward improved OS, despite cross-over to ICI in approximately half of patients in the control arm of the mITT population. As anticipated, in the multivariable analysis, patients with prior exposure to platinum, elevated LDH, and DFI less than 12 months experienced worse PFS.

The study initially enrolled patients who had received 0-1 chemotherapy regimens in the metastatic setting, unselected by PD-L1 status. Eligibility criteria were amended in response to the evolving treatment landscape and approval of atezolizumab for PD-L1-positive mTNBC5, which was later voluntarily withdrawn due to the lack of benefit observed with atezolizumab plus paclitaxel in a confirmatory phase III trial18. In addition, another phase III study that randomized patients with recurrence within 12 months of adjuvant treatment to chemotherapy (carboplatin/gemcitabine or capecitabine) with atezolizumab or placebo did not meet its primary endpoint of OS in the PD-L1-positive population19.

Currently, pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy (nab-paclitaxel, paclitaxel, or carboplatin/gemcitabine) is the only FDA-approved ICI-based regimen for mTNBC, in patients with PD-L1-positive tumors, based on the significant improvement in median PFS (9.7 vs 5.6 months) and OS (23.0 vs 16.1 months) compared to placebo plus chemotherapy in KEYNOTE-3557,20. In subgroup analyses, the addition of pembrolizumab improved PFS in PD-L1-positive patients with de novo mTNBC (9.7 vs 5.3 months) or DFI ≥ 12 months (9.9 vs 6.6 months), although not in those with DFI < 12 months (7.5 vs 7.2 months). Similarly, patients who received pembrolizumab with a taxane appeared to derive greater PFS benefit (nab-paclitaxel: 9.9 vs 5.5. months; paclitaxel: 9.6 vs 3.6 months) than those who received the ICI with carboplatin/gemcitabine (8.0 vs 7.2 months), possibly due to differences in chemotherapy selection based on DFI and neo-/adjuvant regimen. In the mITT population of our study, 87.1% of participants presented with recurrent TNBC after DFI ≥ 12 months or de novo metastatic disease (79% of the KEYNOTE-355 cohort), and the median PFS in the control arm was similar to that reported in the phase III trial. Given the approval of pembrolizumab with a platinum-containing regimen, the decision was made to close the study to enrollment, thus resulting in a smaller sample size and insufficient power to detect clinically meaningful differences between treatment arms, particularly in the PD-L1-positive subgroup.

Beyond PD-L1 status, there has been a paucity of data from randomized trials in mTNBC to identify predictive biomarkers of response to the addition of ICIs to chemotherapy. In IMpassion130, intratumoral CD8 and sTILs were associated with PD-L1 status, but did not independently predict improved PFS or OS with atezolizumab plus nab-paclitaxel21. Here, using the same 10% cutoff to dichotomize pathologist-assessed tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), no significant differences in survival were noted between carboplatin with or without nivolumab in patients with high or low sTILs. In contrast, in the phase II TBCRC 043 trial in which patients with mTNBC after 0-1 prior regimens for advanced disease were randomized to carboplatin with or without atezolizumab, high intratumoral TILs and sTILs (defined as > 5%) in pre-treatment metastatic biopsies were significantly associated with improved PFS with the combination compared to carboplatin alone16. It is unclear if these discrepancies across studies are due to differences in quantitative TILs cutoffs, immune cell subtypes, biopsy site (i.e., primary vs metastatic tumor), or limited sample sizes.

Previous studies have suggested that high TMB may predict benefit from ICI combined with chemotherapy in mTNBC22,23. In TBCRC 043, patients with high TMB (defined as > 25 mut/Mb, derived from whole genome sequencing) appeared to derive greater benefit from carboplatin plus atezolizumab than those with low TMB (p = 0.055), while this trend was not observed in the carboplatin arm. Similarly, in our study, higher TMB (derived from WES) as a continuous or binary (above the median, > 3.87 mut/Mb) variable was associated with improved PFS with carboplatin plus nivolumab, and not with single-agent carboplatin. Importantly, GSEA from bulk RNA sequencing identified upregulation of immune-related pathways such as interferon-gamma response in responders compared to non-responders in the combination arm, but not in the carboplatin arm, providing supporting evidence that a T-cell inflamed environment at baseline is predictive of response to ICI24. Understanding if there are specific subsets of immune cells (e.g., activated memory CD4+ T-cells, effector cytotoxic CD8+ T-cells, B-cells, M1 macrophages) or tumor-immune cell interactions that may predict response to the addition of ICI to chemotherapy is key.

Several studies have demonstrated the prognostic value of ctDNA detection in the metastatic setting25. Early ctDNA reduction and clearance are associated with improved PFS with pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy/trastuzumab in metastatic esopaghogastric cancer26. There are scant data on ctDNA dynamics in randomized ICI trials for mTNBC. Here, early ctDNA reduction by 50% or 90% was associated with improved PFS with carboplatin with or without nivolumab. In addition, 14/22 (64%) of patients with plasma at baseline, C3D1 and an additional time point had a detectable increase in ctDNA prior to or at progression. Further studies are needed to prospectively evaluate the clinical implementation of liquid biopsies and the role of serial ctDNA monitoring for clinical decision-making in mTNBC.

Enrichment of proliferation genes and increased Ki-67 expression in basal-like TNBC suggest that this subtype may benefit from agents that preferentially target highly proliferative tumors (e.g., anti-mitotic, DNA-damaging agents). High proliferation gene expression signature scores have been associated with higher pCR rates to neoadjuvant platinum-based regimens in TNBC compared to those with low proliferation scores27. Current methods of assessing tumor proliferation (e.g., Ki67, gene expression profiling, etc.) require tissue sampling. Evaluation of TKa activity, which is strongly linked to the cell cycle, in blood is a less invasive method that also allows longitudinal monitoring of proliferation levels over time. High pre-treatment TKa, a surrogate marker of increased tumor burden, is an independent predictor of poor prognosis in metastatic breast cancer28,29. Here, lower baseline TKa values (below the median) were associated with longer PFS, with a more pronounced trend in the combination arm, consistent with previous reports in ICI-treated melanoma patients30. These findings suggest that patients with lower disease burden are more likely to benefit from an ICI-based regimen, although results should be interpreted with caution, given the limited sample sizes and need for prospective validation.

The intestinal microbiome plays an important role in modulating antitumor immunity. Initial ICI trials in melanoma showed that alpha diversity (variability of species within a sample) was significantly higher in responders compared to non-responders to anti-PD-1 therapy and associated with prolonged PFS31. Several studies across tumor types have reported specific bacterial taxa enriched in responders to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 blockade (e.g., Faecalibacterium from the Ruminococcaceae family of the Clostridiales order), which have been associated with a higher frequency of effector CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells in peripheral blood, in contrast to those at higher abundance in non-responders (e.g., Bacteroidales order) associated with increased regulatory T-cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells and weakened antigen presentation capacity31–33. Here, neither alpha diversity or unique species were associated with survival in the overall cohort or by treatment arm. However, increased activity of the PWY-4984 (urea cycle) pathway at baseline was associated with improved PFS regardless of treatment arm, with a similar trend observed for the enzyme arginase. Urea cycle activation, together with the absence of favorable bacteria with ureolytic capacity (e.g., Bifidobacterium), has been linked to colorectal tumorigenesis, possibly due to modulation of the immune microenvironment via accumulation of polyamines that shift macrophages toward a more immunosuppressive, pro-tumoral phenotype34. Despite a worse prognosis, urea cycle activation correlated with improved response to ICI in melanoma through increased pyrimidine synthesis, leading to a transversion mutational signature that generates hydrophobic neopeptides with increased immunogenicity, independent of mutational load35. Larger studies are needed to fully elucidate specific microbiome profiles associated with improved outcomes with ICI in breast cancer.

Limitations of our study include the premature closure to accrual, resulting in a smaller sample size and insufficient power to detect a significant difference in the primary endpoint of PFS. Given the sparse correlative data from randomized trials evaluating the addition of ICI to chemotherapy in mTNBC to date, we conducted a broad range of tissue, blood, and microbiome analyses to identify predictive biomarkers of response. Our findings require validation in larger, prospective cohorts. Future directives include assessment of the tumor and immune microenvironment via single-cell and spatial profiling of pre- and post-treatment biopsies to identify specific host-immune interactions associated with response or resistance to ICI.

In conclusion, to our knowledge, this is the first randomized trial comparing the activity of chemotherapy with or without the PD-1 inhibitor nivolumab in mTNBC. Although enrollment was halted early due to the rapidly evolving landscape of ICI, a trend toward improved PFS and OS was observed with the addition of nivolumab to carboplatin in patients with PD-L1-positive mTNBC. Correlative analyses identified high TMB, upregulation of interferon-gamma signaling, early ctDNA reduction, low baseline serum TKa, and dysregulation of the urea cycle in the host microbiome as potential predictors of response to ICI with platinum in this patient population.

Methods

Compliance with ethical standards

The study was conducted in accordance with the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice Standards and the Declaration of Helsinki, and approval was obtained from the Dana-Farber/Harvard Cancer Center (DF/HCC) Institutional Review Board (Dana-Farber Cancer Institute #17-512). Participants provided written informed consent prior to any protocol-specific procedures or assessments. Data were monitored throughout the study by the DF/HCC Data and Safety Monitoring Board. This trial was registered with the U.S. National Institutes of Health (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03414684).

Study design and patient characteristics

Eligible patients had histologically or cytologically confirmed invasive breast cancer with unresectable locally advanced or metastatic TNBC, defined as estrogen receptor (ER) < 1% and progesterone receptor (PR) < 1% by immunohistochemistry (IHC) and HER2-negative by ASCO/CAP guidelines36, per local review of the most recent tumor sample. Participants had measurable or evaluable disease by RECIST 1.1 criteria37.

Initially, participants who had received 0-1 prior lines of chemotherapy in the metastatic setting were eligible, and known PD-L1 status was not required prior to enrollment. Prior platinum or PARP inhibitor was allowed in the neo-/adjuvant setting if ≥ 12 months had elapsed since the end of adjuvant systemic therapy to metastatic recurrence. Prior anti-PD-1/-L1 therapy was not allowed. After the accelerated approval of atezolizumab plus nab-paclitaxel for PD-L1-positive, treatment-naïve mTNBC5, the protocol was amended to enroll only participants who had not received any chemotherapy in the metastatic setting. For neo-/adjuvant platinum or PARP inhibition, the timeframe was modified to ≥6 months from the end of adjuvant systemic therapy to metastatic recurrence. Known PD-L1 status (per local testing of a primary or metastatic sample with any PD-L1 antibody validated in a CLIA-certified environment) was required at registration for stratification purposes.

Study design and procedures

This was an investigator-initiated, multi-institutional (4 centers), open-label, randomized phase II trial to compare the efficacy of carboplatin with and without nivolumab in patients with previously untreated mTNBC. Participants were randomized 1:1 to receive carboplatin AUC 6 intravenously (IV) every 3 weeks in combination with nivolumab 360 mg IV every 3 weeks (Arm A) or carboplatin alone (Arm B). Treatment was continued until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity. Crossover in Arm B to receive nivolumab was allowed at disease progression. After the accelerated approval of atezolizumab plus nab-paclitaxel, the protocol was amended to allow cross-over to nab-paclitaxel (100 mg/m2 IV on Days 1, 8 and 15 of an every 28-day cycle) in combination with nivolumab (480 mg IV every 4 weeks).

Participants were stratified by prior platinum (yes vs no), germline BRCA status (wild-type vs mutant/unknown), and number of prior lines of therapy (0 vs 1). After the protocol amendment, the latter stratification factor was replaced by PD-L1 status (positive vs negative; PD-L1 positivity defined as staining of ≥ 1% of immune-infiltrating cells using SP142 Ventana). Tumor response assessments were performed at baseline, every 6 weeks for the first 24 weeks, and every 9 weeks thereafter. For participants who discontinued therapy for reasons other than progressive disease, tumor measurements were to continue every 6–12 weeks.

Statistical methods

The primary objective was to compare the efficacy of carboplatin plus nivolumab versus carboplatin in patients with mTNBC unselected by PD-L1 status who had not received prior chemotherapy for advanced disease. The primary endpoint of PFS was assessed among all patients who initiated protocol therapy according to the randomized treatment assignment. Secondary endpoints included OS, confirmed ORR, clinical benefit rate (CBR, defined as confirmed CR, PR, or SD for ≥ 24 weeks), DOR, TTOR, and toxicity (graded according to NCI CTCAE Version 4.0). Clinical outcomes were analyzed in the mITT and PD-L1-positive populations.

Considering a one-sided type I error of 0.05 and 85% power to detect a 2-month difference in median PFS at the time of the final analysis (HR: 0.60), 132 patients with previously untreated mTNBC would need to enroll on the trial. On November 13, 2020, the FDA granted accelerated approval to pembrolizumab with chemotherapy (including a platinum-based regimen) for locally recurrent unresectable or metastatic PD-L1-positive (22C3 CPS ≥ 10) TNBC, and the decision was made to close the study early to accrual.

PFS was defined as the time from study randomization to disease progression according to RECIST 1.1 or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Patients alive without disease progression were censored at the date of last disease evaluation. The primary comparison in PFS between treatment arms was performed by the stratified log-rank test, with prior exposure to platinum, germline BRCA1/2 status, and PD-L1 status as stratification factors. The primary tests of PFS and OS were one-sided with a significance level at 0.05; all other comparisons were two-sided. Kaplan-Meier estimates were calculated separately for patients allocated to each arm. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate treatment efficacy, adjusting for potential confounders. Additional details regarding the Statistical Plan are included in the protocol (Supplementary Information).

Correlative analyses

Biospecimen collection

Tumor tissue biopsies at baseline and after 3-6 weeks of treatment (i.e., between C2D1 and C3D1) were required if safely accessible, and optional at progression. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue from archival tumor samples was also collected, if available. Research blood was drawn at baseline, every 6 weeks (Day 1 of each cycle) for the first 24 weeks and then every 9 weeks, and at EOT. Stool samples were collected at baseline, on-treatment (after 3–6 weeks of treatment), and at progression (if best overall response was PR or CR).

PD-L1 Immunohistochemistry

Central PD-L1 IHC was performed on baseline research biopsy FFPE tissue (and/or archival FFPE if research biopsy tissue was not available) using the antibody clone SP142 (Ventana), and scored by a trained breast pathologist at the Brigham and Women’s Hospital (BWH) Pathology Core. PD-L1 positivity was defined as staining of ≥ 1% of immune-infiltrating cells. For the purposes of exploratory analyses, PD-L1 testing was also conducted with the antibody clones 28-8 and 22C3 (Leica Bond). For 28-8, PD-L1 positivity was defined as staining of ≥ 1% of tumor cells (TC); for 22C3, CPS scoring was calculated [(PD-L1-positive tumor cells + PD-L1-positive mononuclear inflammatory cells) / (total tumor cells) x 100], and PD-L1 positivity was defined as CPS ≥ 10.

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes

Pathologist-assessed TILs were scored from baseline research biopsy FFPE tissue (and/or archival FFPE if research biopsy tissue was not available) according to the recommendations of the International TILs Working Group38. FFPE tissue (5 µm section) was stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for quantitative assessment of stromal and intratumoral TILs by a trained breast pathologist. sTILs were dichotomized into < 10% vs ≥ 10% for stratified presentation of efficacy, and for Cox regression analyses to assess differences in PFS between groups.

OncoPanel

Archival FFPE metastatic tumor (or primary tumor if metastatic tissue was not available) specimens were analyzed using a targeted next-generation sequencing panel (OncoPanel) to detect SNVs, CNVs, and structural variants (SVs). Genomic testing was performed at the BWH Center for Advanced Molecular Diagnostics, a CLIA-certified laboratory environment, according to published methods39–41. Gene enrichment analyses were performed using Fisher’s exact test considering 239 genes common to all OncoPanel versions, and multiple testing correction was applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg method42. Correlation of TMB with binary variables was analyzed using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. For univariate correlation of clinical covariates with PFS, TMB was evaluated both as a continuous and binary variable (high TMB, ≥ 10 mutations/megabase (mut/Mb); low TMB, < 10 mut/Mb). For patients across the cohort and within each arm, correlation of the presence of a given CNV or SNV (with ≥ 5 events in the cohort) with PFS was tested using a Cox proportional hazards model, with prior platinum, age, and PD-L1 status as covariates.

Whole exome and transcriptome sequencing

Sample testing is detailed in the Supplementary Methods. Baseline metastatic tumor biopsies that passed QC metrics were included in the analysis. Associations between somatic genomic alterations or TMB with PFS were assessed using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models (two-sided Wald tests), adjusting for age, prior platinum, and PD-L1 status (SP142). Only CNVs known to be oncogenic and protein-altering (missense, nonsense, frameshift deletions/insertions, in-frame deletions, and splice site/region mutations) mutations were included in these tests. Tests were performed for gene alterations that occurred in ≥ 2 patients per arm, using Benjamini-Hochberg multiple hypothesis correction42.

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq243, correcting for age, prior platinum, and PD-L1 status. Subsequent pathway analysis was performed using GSEA44 with log2foldchange as the ranking metric. Samples were also categorized into transcriptomic subtypes according to the Lehmann 2016 revised TNBCtype4 (BL1, BL2, LAR, M)45. Associations of transcriptomic subtypes with PFS were tested using adjusted Cox proportional hazards models, and with immune-related toxicity using Fisher’s exact test.

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA)

Plasma samples were analyzed with the laboratory-developed test FoundationOne® Monitor (Foundation Medicine, Inc.) to assess ctDNA tumor fraction (TF) and genomic alterations46. HRs and 95% CIs were calculated with univariate Cox proportional hazard regression. Patients with progression prior to C3D1 (n = 2) were omitted from PFS analyses landmarked at C3D1. Prediction of tumor, germline, or clonal hematopoiesis (CH) origin of each variant from plasma was performed as described47. Variants were classified as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, or of unknown significance (VUS) following previously reported methods46,48.

Thymidine kinase 1 activity

TKa was analyzed retrospectively using the DiviTum® TKa assay (Biovica International, Uppsala, Sweden). TKa was determined using a refined ELISA-based method in serum samples according to the manufacturer’s instructions (www.biovica.com) and performed at Biovica’s CLIA-certified, CAP-accredited laboratory (San Diego, CA). Descriptive TKa analysis was performed, and fold-changes in TKa values between baseline and C3D1 were calculated. Associations between TKa and PFS, OS, or sTILs were investigated in the overall population and in each treatment arm, separately. Patients with both baseline and C3D1 TKa samples were included in the analysis, and PFS and OS were evaluated from the start of C3D1. TKa values were log-transformed for analysis.

Microbiome

Whole genome metagenomic sequencing was performed, and data processed for analysis of species, enzyme, and pathway taxonomic profiles. Associations between alpha diversity and patient characteristics were explored using univariable linear regression. Associations between alpha diversity and survival (PFS or OS) were explored using the Kaplan-Meier method and uni-/multivariable Cox regression models. In analyses of the species, enzyme, and pathway profiles, associations between log-transformed relative abundances and survival were explored using univariable Cox models. Taxa with greater than 80% zero values were excluded from analysis. CART methods49 were used to search for cut points in relative abundance that are potentially significant for PFS or OS, for each taxon. In Cox regression and CART analyses, inferences were made after FDR correction42.

4D Q-Plasia OncoReaderTM (QPORTM)

Digitized whole slide H&E images (WSI) of pre-treatment samples were provided to 4D Path Inc. for biomarker analyses using the 4D Q-Plasia OncoReader™ (QPOR™) platform. Images were uploaded to the QPOR™ cloud system for end-to-end automated computation of the numerical values for 18 different QPOR™ signature biomarkers for each patient (Supplementary Methods). The continuous biomarker values were assessed for association with PFS, OS, and confirmed ORR in each arm separately, as well as in the overall population, and adjusted for FDR. For biomarkers with statistical significance before FDR correction, alternative parameterizations were also explored (e.g., dichotomization at the sample median).
