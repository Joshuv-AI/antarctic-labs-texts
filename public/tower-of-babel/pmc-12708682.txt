Advancing animal behavior recognition with self-supervised pre-training on unlabeled data
Authors: Axiu Mao, Miaoyun Peng, Guikun Liu, Meilu Zhu, Kaiying Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeep learning-based animal activity recognition (AAR) achieves promising performance but remains constrained by its reliance on large labeled datasets. While pre-training offers a viable path toward reducing annotation dependency, existing approaches like transfer learning primarily utilize single-species labeled data, neglecting the substantial publicly available unlabeled data across species. To address this gap, this study introduces a contrastive learning based self-supervised learning framework that effectively leverages cross-species unlabeled data to mitigate annotation scarcity in AAR. The approach involves two stages: (1) Self-supervised pre-training of a patch time series Transformer (PatchTST) based encoder using cross-species unlabeled data with a time-frequency consistency objective to learn transferable motion representations; and (2) Fine-tuning on species-specific labeled data, where the pre-trained encoder serves as a feature extraction backbone in a novel classification model that integrates intra-axis local dynamics via PatchTST-based encoder and cross-axis global patterns via depth-wise separable convolution. Experimental results demonstrate that our approach significantly enhances recognition performance under limited labeled data, achieving improvements of 4.79% in accuracy and 4.57% in F1-score over a baseline trained from scratch, while also improving discrimination of semantically similar behaviors and maintaining robustness with reduced samples. This work establishes a new direction for scalable and label-efficient animal behavior monitoring.Keywords: Animal behavior, Time series, Self-supervised learning, Time-frequency consistency, Transformer modelSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Animal behavior serves as a vital indicator for evaluating both physiological and psychological well-being in animals1. Compared to conventional manual observation methods, the development of intelligent and fine-grained animal behavior monitoring systems can significantly reduce manpower and time investments while enhancing cost efficiency2. Currently, wearable sensors, such as accelerometers, gyroscopes, and Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS), have been widely used in animal behavior monitoring, owing to their compact, energy-efficient, and interference-resistant design with high-fidelity sensing. They are typically attached to different animal body parts like ears, neck, or legs to accurately capture movement signals, which are then input to models for feature extraction and classification of different animal behaviors (e.g., grazing, drinking).

Deep learning has emerged as a powerful computational approach in animal activity recognition (AAR) tasks, owing to its automated pattern recognition and feature extraction from complex datasets3,4. Literature has demonstrated the promising performance of deep learning models in distinguishing various animal activities2,5. For instance, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks can identify cow feeding and ruminating behaviors with up to 87% accuracy by analyzing triaxial acceleration data from nose-worn wearable devices6. Eerdekens et al. applied convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to behavior recognition of horses and dogs based on accelerometer data. Their research results consistently show that CNNs perform excellently, with an accuracy rate exceeding 97%1,7–9. Yin et al. proposed the CNN_LSTM_Attention model, which achieves 89.3% accuracy in classifying pig health status10.

Although deep learning-based AAR methods have made significant progress, their high performance often relies on large amounts of labeled data. However, due to the labor-intensive and time-consuming nature of labeling work, such data is often scarce, which directly impairs the model’s ability to learn features and generalization. To address the scarcity of labeled data, prevailing approaches in literature commonly employ three principal strategies, i.e., expanding labelled data via augmentation techniques11,12, leveraging unlabeled data via semi-supervised learning13 or active learning14, and pre-training models on extensive datasets via transfer learning15,16. Amongst, pre-training serves as a promising solution, obtaining high-quality model initialization by learning general representations from large datasets and thus significantly reducing reliance on labeled data17. For instance, Kleanthous et al. achieved precise identification of three sheep activities by developing a hybrid model that integrates CNNs with handcrafted time-frequency domain features and leveraging deep transfer learning16. This approach effectively tackles sensor heterogeneity and positional variance, demonstrating state-of-the-art performance with 98.55% accuracy on source data and 96.59% on target data. Nevertheless, existing approaches in AAR primarily depend on restricted labeled datasets from single species for pre-training, neglecting the substantial quantities of publicly available unlabeled data from different species. These unlabeled resources, while more readily accessible and rich in behavioral information, remain largely untapped in current research paradigms. Therefore, effectively leveraging such multi-species unlabeled data to mitigate annotation scarcity presents a critical yet under-explored challenge.

Self-supervised learning (SSL) offers an efficient paradigm that harnesses massive unlabeled data for model initialization, effectively mitigating the scarcity of labeled data in downstream tasks. Masked reconstruction18,19 and contrastive learning20,21 are the dominant SSL techniques. Following these paradigms, some time-series SSL methods have recently emerged22–28. Masked reconstruction trains models to recover randomly masked input segments, promoting learning of robust data representations29,30. However, such methods face inherent challenges with time-series data, where the core semantics of sensor signals like periodicity, trends, and peak-valley structures are intrinsically linked to temporal dynamics. Masking disrupts this temporal integrity, substantially complicating reconstruction and hindering the model’s ability to capture key data semantics31.

Conversely, contrastive learning has broader practical adoption. It trains models to identify similarities between positive sample pairs (e.g., augmented views of the same instance) while differentiating them from negative pairs (views from different instances). Standard methods like SimCLR32 and SimMTM31 adopt this framework. However, applying traditional augmentations inherited from the field of natural language processing and computer vision, including flipping, permutation, or cropping, to motion time-series remains challenging. These operations can invert trends, disrupt event causality, or damage peak-valley structures due to the critical dependence of signal semantics on temporal order and dynamics33, thereby compromising semantic consistency between original and augmented samples and undermining representation learning. To address this limitation, methods like Time-Frequency Consistency (TF-C)34, Mixing-up35, and FEI36 have shifted towards frequency-domain augmentation, including frequency masking, mixing, and amplitude perturbation, with empirical evidence confirming their efficacy in preserving semantic consistency for motion time-series data. Based on these considerations, contrastive learning with frequency-domain augmentation presents a more suitable approach for time-series data in AAR, effectively addressing semantic consistency challenges while preserving crucial behavioral patterns. Such a technique will be consequently adopted and validated in this work.

In this study, we introduce a contrastive learning-based SSL method designed to learn general feature representations from publicly available unlabeled datasets, under the assumption that representations learned from large-scale cross-species data can provide a superior initialization for species-specific downstream classification tasks, thereby effectively alleviating the performance degradation caused by scarce labeled data. The method follows a two-stage pipeline: (1) First, we employ the TF-C framework to conduct large-scale pre-training of a patch time series Transformer (PatchTST)37 based encoder on cross-species unlabeled data, optimizing time-frequency representations through consistency loss minimization. Herein, we assume the existence of generalizable motion patterns transferable across species during similar behaviors, based on established literature38–40 documenting behavioral similarities between species and our recent validation of cross-species model training feasibility41, thereby supporting model pre-training on cross-species unlabeled datasets. In addition, further assume that the temporal and frequency-domain representations of the same behavioral sample share consistent core semantics, which enables the use of the TF-C objective to minimize their representational distance in the feature space, thereby facilitating the learning of more robust and invariant features. (2) Then, the pre-trained encoder acts as a feature-extraction backbone within a newly designed classification model, which is fine-tuned on species-specific labeled data. This model incorporates both intra-axis feature extraction via PatchTST and cross-axis feature fusion via depth-wise separable convolution. We evaluate the generalization capability of the fine-tuned model using leave-one-subject-out validation and compare its performance against a baseline trained from scratch. Materials and methods

Overview

To address the challenge of limited labeled data in animal activity recognition, this study proposes an SSL framework based on contrastive learning that leverages publicly available cross-species unlabeled data. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the framework consists of two consecutive phases: (1) In the pre-training stage, a PatchTST-based encoder is trained using the TF-C framework on cross-species unlabeled data, where the model learns robust representations through three complementary supervision signals: time-domain contrastive learning, frequency-domain contrastive learning, and cross-domain temporal-spectral alignment, integrated via a joint loss function; (2) In the fine-tuning stage, the pre-trained encoder serves as a feature extraction backbone for the downstream classification model, which is subsequently optimized on target species-specific labeled data using a carefully designed architecture that incorporates depth-wise separable convolution for effective cross-axis feature fusion.

The method operates in two main stages: (1) Pre-training stage: a PatchTST-based time-domain encoder is trained via the Time-Frequency Consistency (TF-C) framework on cross-species unlabeled data, where the model learns invariant representations by minimizing a joint loss combining time-domain contrastive loss (), frequency-domain contrastive loss (), and cross-domain consistency loss (). (2) Fine-tuning stage: the pre-trained encoder serves as a feature extraction backbone in a classification model enhanced with depth-wise separable convolution, followed by fine-tuning on species-specific labeled data.

Model pre-training on cross-species unlabeled data

Existing contrastive learning research often employs temporal augmentations to generate multi-view data, but struggles to maintain semantic consistency between original and augmented samples34,42. Time-Frequency Consistency (TF-C)34 addresses this issue by performing augmentation in the frequency domain, thus effectively preserving semantic consistency by maintaining temporal-spectral correlations. Inspired by this, we introduce an SSL method based on the TF-C principle to pretrain a PatchTST-based encoder using cross-species unlabeled data. As shown in Fig. 1, the method incorporates three dedicated components to supervise feature learning from distinct viewpoints. The respective objectives of these components are: (a) to learn time-invariant features from the time-domain signal; (b) to reveal frequency-domain invariance by introducing perturbations to the spectral characteristics; and (c) to achieve cross-domain alignment between the temporal and spectral representations. A dedicated loss function is derived from each objective, and these losses are subsequently integrated into a composite loss function to jointly optimize the contrastive learning. Cross-species unlabeled dataset

This dataset aggregates publicly accessible accelerometer data from cattle, sheep, and horses, totaling 576,897 unlabeled samples12,16,44,45. Consistent with common practice in animal activity recognition2, only triaxial accelerometer data are utilized, as this modality has been widely validated as both prevalent and effective for the task. Initially, the dataset was fully labeled across 13 categories of common animal behaviors. To simulate an unlabeled environment for model pre-training, we subsequently removed all labels.

Species-specific labeled dataset

This dataset comprises 42,943 annotated samples collected from five goats46, encompassing five behavioral categories: standing (43.15%), grazing (35.35%), walking (20.19%), running (0.87%), and trotting (0.44%). A severe class imbalance is present, with an imbalance ratio of 98.05; the minority class, trotting, contains only 189 samples. This dataset is used for fine-tuning the pretrained model.

Implementation details

Data preprocessing

For the cross-species pre-training dataset, we applied a systematic preprocessing pipeline to ensure consistency across heterogeneous data sources. First, multiple publicly available datasets were cleaned and harmonized by standardizing their sampling rates. High-frequency recordings (e.g., 100 Hz) were down-sampled, while low-frequency data (e.g., 12.5 Hz) were up-sampled to 25 Hz via nonlinear interpolation. This rate was chosen based on previous studies indicating that 25 Hz provides a suitable balance between performance retention and computational efficiency2. To maintain uniformity across datasets, only triaxial acceleration data (common to all sources) were retained. The signals were segmented using a 2-second sliding window and normalized through z-score standardization, resulting in a final tensor of dimensions 576,897 × 50 × 3. The same preprocessing steps, including 2-second window segmentation and z-score normalization, were applied to the species-specific labeled dataset, yielding a tensor of size 42,943 × 50 × 3.

Experimental setting

This study evaluates the effectiveness of the proposed method by comparing a classification model initialized with pretrained parameters (our method) against one with random initialization (baseline). The objective is to assess whether cross-species pre-training improves behavior recognition performance for the target species. We employ accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, macro-average, and weighted-average as evaluation metrics to comprehensively assess model performance47,48. The macro-average metric computes the arithmetic mean of per-class scores, giving equal weight to each class, whereas the weighted-average accounts for class imbalance by weighting each class’s score by its sample size.

Similar to previous studies49–51, we employed the leave-one-subject-out validation method with three random repetitions to assess the generalization of the fine-tuned classification model. In each repetition, we randomly assigned one goat for testing, one for validation, and the remaining four for training, while ensuring that the testing and validation sets were derived from different goats across all three runs. The model was trained using a class-balanced cross-entropy loss function52, augmented with an L2 regularization term (weight decay = 0.01) to mitigate overfitting. Optimization was performed with the Adam optimizer at a learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁴. Each training run consisted of 100 epochs with a batch size of 32. The model achieving the highest validation accuracy was saved and evaluated on the test set. An early stopping strategy with a patience of 10 epochs was applied during training. All experiments were implemented in PyTorch and executed on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 GPU.

Analysis of our proposed classification model

In addition to the PatchTST-based feature extractor, our classification model incorporates the depth-wise separable convolution, which combines grouped 1D convolution with pointwise convolution. To evaluate their individual contributions to the final classification performance, we compared four configurations: (1) the model without depth-wise separable convolution, (2) with only grouped 1D convolution, (3) with only pointwise convolution, and (4) with both operations. Results in Table 3 show that each operation independently improves performance, confirming that both are critical for goat behavior classification. It is worth noting that the macro-average accuracy places greater emphasis on classification balance across different behavioral categories47. We observe that the inclusion of grouped 1D convolution significantly enhances the macro-average accuracy. This improvement can be attributed to the ability of 1D convolution applied along each sensor axis to capture local temporal dependencies within axis-specific signals. By extracting more fine-grained motion patterns, the model learns discriminative features that are particularly beneficial for recognizing behaviors with limited samples, thereby improving classification performance even for under-represented categories. The integration of pointwise convolution following the grouped 1D convolution further enhances overall model performance, yielding improvements of 0.05% in accuracy, 3.61% in F1-score, 7.88% in macro-average accuracy, and 0.49% in weighted-average accuracy compared to the model without these components. This gain can be attributed to the role of pointwise convolution in effectively combining cross-axis features extracted by the preceding grouped 1D convolutions, thereby enabling a more comprehensive local-to-global feature representation.

This work employs self-supervised learning to leverage large-scale unlabeled cross-species data, aiming to enhance robustness under limited labeled samples. To systematically assess classification performance with varying labeled data availability, we compared our method against the baseline using progressively reduced training subsets (75%, 50%, and 25% of the original labeled data) during the fine-tuning process. As illustrated in Fig. 8, the proposed approach consistently surpasses the baseline in both accuracy and F1-score across all data volumes. Limitations and implications

This work presents the first attempt to utilize a cross-species unlabeled dataset for self-supervised pre-training in AAR, demonstrating significant potential to mitigate annotation scarcity. However, the unlabeled data used in this work were derived from originally annotated datasets by removing labels, thus representing a simulated corpus rather than real-world unlabeled data. Theoretically, the presence or absence of labels in the pre-training data has a minimal impact on the TF-C based pre-training, as the method focuses solely on time-frequency consistency within a sample itself and does not use category labels. Nevertheless, empirical validation with genuinely unlabeled data remains essential to further confirm the practical efficacy of this self-supervised approach. Although the current pre-training data encompass multiple species and scenarios to simulate real-world variability, the dataset remains limited in scale and diversity since it was constructed from existing annotated sources. Therefore, future work will prioritize collecting larger-scale, truly unlabeled data from a wider range of animal species and authentic environments. Such expansion is essential to enable the training of a foundational model with strong generalization capabilities for animal behavior recognition. Moreover, this learning paradigm could be extended to other sensory modalities such as audio and vision, further alleviating the labeling bottleneck and promoting scalable intelligent monitoring in the field of precision livestock farming.

Furthermore, while the objective of our pre-training was to learn universal behavioral features across species, the model did not explicitly account for the fact that similar behaviors can manifest through distinct motion patterns in different animals. This oversight may limit the generalizability and discriminative power of the learned representations. To address this, future work should incorporate species-specific prior knowledge, such as taxonomic or morphological information, into the self-supervised learning framework. By guiding the model to disentangle species-invariant behavioral patterns from species-specific motion characteristics, we can enhance both the quality of pre-trained features and their transferability to downstream recognition tasks.

Conclusion

This study presents a self-supervised learning framework that effectively leverages cross-species unlabeled data to enhance AAR under limited annotation scenarios. The primary strength of this work lies in its two-stage design: self-supervised pre-training on cross-species unlabeled data using the Time-Frequency Consistency objective to learn transferable motion representations, followed by fine-tuning on species-specific labeled data, where depth-wise separable convolution enables effective local-to-global feature extraction by capturing both fine-grained temporal patterns and cross-axis dependencies. Experimental results demonstrate that our approach substantially enhances recognition performance under limited labeled data, achieving a 4.79% improvement in accuracy and a 4.57% increase in F1-score compared to the baseline trained from scratch. This performance gain validates the practical value of our method, showing consistent advantages across multiple aspects, including improved discrimination of semantically similar behaviors, enhanced classification balance across different behavioral categories, and maintained robustness with reduced training samples. However, the study has limitations, including the use of simulated rather than genuine unlabeled data and the lack of explicit modeling for species-specific motion characteristics, which may affect the generalizability of the learned features.

For future applications, this paradigm offers a viable pathway toward foundational animal behavior models that can adapt to new species or environments with minimal supervision. We recommend subsequent research to focus on constructing large-scale, truly unlabeled datasets collected in real-world settings and to explore the integration of multi-modal data or biological priors for improved representation learning. Such efforts would further advance scalable and accurate monitoring in precision livestock farming.​.
