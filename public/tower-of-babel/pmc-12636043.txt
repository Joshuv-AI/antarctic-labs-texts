Nasal Septal Perforation and Hereditary Hemorrhagic Telangiectasia: Evolution in Management
Authors: Stephen F. Bansberg, Trenton House, Devyani Lal, Michael J. Marino, Cullen M. Taylor, Amar Miglani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo review septal perforation management and outcomes in patients with hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT).MethodsCollection and presentation of patient demographic, perforation size, and prior perforation and HHT treatment data over a 20‐year period. Symptom and quality of life treatment outcomes were determined using the Nasal Obstruction Symptom Evaluation (NOSE)‐Perf scale and the 5‐Factor Glasgow Inventory (GBI‐5F), respectively, in patients treated since 2017.ResultsNine patients met study criteria. Mean (range) age was 64.4 (52–80) years and seven were biological males. Mean (range) perforation length and height were 1.9 (1.2–2.6) and 1.4 (1.0–1.8) cm. Four patients were treated with a customized septal button, one with posterior septal resection, and four with endonasal bilateral mucosal flap repair supported with an interposition graft. The button prostheses were well tolerated and the surgical closures successful at 8–60 months postoperatively. Subjective responses in the first three patients, and NOSE‐Perf with GBI‐5F scores in the latter six patients, demonstrated symptom and quality of life improvement for all patients. Perforation repair patients demonstrated substantial improvement in mean GBI‐5F subdomain scores for quality of life, self‐confidence, and social involvement and all have undergone sodium tetradecyl sclerotherapy injection postoperatively without re‐perforation.ConclusionSymptom and quality of life improvement can be achieved in HHT patients with a perforation using customized septal buttons, posterior septal resection, or bilateral flap surgical repair. Surgical closure combined with injection sclerotherapy is feasible with a low risk of re‐perforation and represents an effective treatment strategy in selected patients.Level of Evidence4.

Introduction

Hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT) is an autosomal dominant condition that affects blood vessel wall integrity to cause telangiectasias and arteriovenous malformations (AVM). These vascular lesions can involve mucocutaneous tissues, visceral organs, and the central nervous system [1]. Nearly all patients develop nasal mucosa telangiectasias that cause recurrent epistaxis, and bleeding episodes typically worsen over time [2, 3, 4]. Iron‐deficiency anemia, high‐output cardiac failure, and stroke can be associated complications. Rarely, epistaxis can become life‐threatening [4].

HHT patients who experience moderate to severe epistaxis typically require occasional to frequent nasal packing for control. Treatments directed toward individual telangiectasias include chemical and electrical cauterization, bipolar diathermy, argon plasma coagulation, coblation, submucosal radiofrequency ablation, varied laser treatments, and sodium tetradecyl sulfate (STS) sclerosing injection [2, 3, 4]. Bevacizumab administered intravenously, through topical spray, or by submucosal injection targets current telangiectasias and attempts to minimize their future development. Removal of the affected nasal lining with subsequent application of a split‐thickness skin graft (septodermoplasty) can effectively reduce epistaxis severity. These treatments result in varied degrees of septal mucosal injury that can progress to cartilage devascularization, necrosis, and perforation. Subsequent epistaxis management can traumatize the perforation margin, increasing both perforation size and the treatment challenge.

Crusting, obstruction/congestion, bleeding, whistling on inspiration, and drainage are common perforation symptoms [5, 6]. Treatment options for symptomatic perforations beyond nasal moisturization and lubrication include obturation with a button prosthesis, attempted surgical closure, and posterior septal resection. This study reviews the senior author's management of septal perforation in HHT patients. Patients with HHT pose additional treatment considerations and challenges due to the need for ongoing management of telangiectasias and epistaxis, as well as the destructive and potentially scarring effect from prior treatments on the septum. The 20‐year period of this study demonstrates an evolution to contemporary treatment options that utilize bilateral mucosal flap perforation repair combined with telangiectasia sclerotherapy.

Methods

This retrospective study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB 19‐001700). The study period included the preceding 20 years ending in February 2025. Patients with HHT per Curaçao criteria [7] with a septal perforation treated by the senior author (S.F.B.) were identified for review. Data on patient demographics, prior HHT treatments, perforation size, and perforation treatment with outcomes were extracted from the medical record and are presented as a case series. The Nasal Obstruction Symptom Evaluation (NOSE)‐Perf questionnaire and the 5‐Factor Glasgow Benefit Inventory (GBI‐5F) patient‐reported outcome measurement (PROM) questionnaires were administered to the six most recent patients.

Results

Ten patients with HHT who underwent perforation treatment were identified. One patient was lost to follow‐up 2 weeks following surgical repair and excluded from review. Of the nine study patients, the mean (range) age at presentation was 64.4 years (52–80) and seven were biological males. Mean (range) perforation length and height for all perforations were 1.9 cm (1.2–2.6) and 1.4 cm (1.0–1.8), respectively. Patient demographics, pertinent prior HHT treatment history, perforation size, and treatment with outcomes and NOSE‐Perf scores are noted in Tables 1 and 2. The GBI‐5F total and subdomain scores are documented in Table 3. Scores reflect improvement or worsening of quality of life with a range of +100 to −100. The total mean GBI‐5F score was 43.3 in patients with surgical perforation repair. Quality of life, self‐confidence, social involvement, support, and general health domain mean scores following repair were 70.8, 58.3, 54.2, 25.0, and 8.4, respectively. Posterior Septal Resection

Patient 1: A 55‐year‐old male presented after multiple laser treatments and septodermoplasty. The patient subsequently underwent three additional septodermoplasties with telangiectasia bipolar cauterization, complicated by incomplete skin graft take. A perforation developed within a field of scarred, excoriated, and telangiectatic mucosa. Patient quality of life was substantially impacted by continued epistaxis and obstructive nasal crusting. Subtotal septal resection was performed to encompass the large area of scarred and diseased mucosa, preserving 2 cm of septum anteriorly, superiorly, and posteriorly. Substantial symptom improvement was reported by the patient at 6 weeks, with minimal posterior margin crusting noted on exam. The patient continued to experience clinically insignificant bleeding and crusting that did not require medical intervention to the time of his death due to pancreatic cancer 20 months postresection.

Septal Button Prosthesis

Patient 3: A 77‐year‐old male presented with HHT complicated by esophageal telangiectasias, hepatic AV malformation, cardiomyopathy, anemia, and known septal perforation. The patient required 40 blood transfusions and frequent nasal self‐packing over the preceding 9 months. Nasal treatment history included multiple chemical cauterizations and laser procedures, septodermoplasty, and placement of a prefabricated septal button 2 years prior to presentation. Examination demonstrated a large perforation incompletely obturated by the button with the exposed posterior margin covered by crusting. Bevacizumab infusions provided short‐term improvement in epistaxis. Six months following presentation the patient underwent placement of a custom‐designed silicone button prosthesis, telangiectasia bipolar cauterization, and submucosal bevacizumab injections of the posterior septum and middle and inferior turbinates. At 1 year postoperatively the patient's hemoglobin had remained stable. The patient reported good button tolerance, significant improvement and stability in nasal symptoms and quality of life, and rare and easily managed epistaxis until his death due to cardiomyopathy 19 months postbutton application.

Septal Perforation Repair

Patient 5: A 63‐year‐old male presented with a history of pulmonary AVM embolization, multiple blood transfusions, recent episodes of severe epistaxis, obstructive sleep apnea managed with a positive airway pressure (PAP) nasal mask, and a known large perforation. Past treatments included multiple nasal cauterizations, laser treatments, submucosal bevacizumab injections, sclerotherapy, and left‐sided nasal airway closure. Nasal patency was re‐established during subsequent endoscopic sinus surgery. Bilateral external valve weakness and vestibular scarring were noted following nostril closure reversal. Endonasal perforation repair was attempted utilizing left‐sided superior and inferior bipedicled advancement flaps and an endoscopically developed right anterior ethmoidal artery (AEA) flap. The auricular donor site provided both perichondrium for the repair's interposition graft and cartilage for external valve grafts [8]. The patient reported marked improvement in symptoms including epistaxis, quality of life, and PAP tolerance following the successful repair. Six sclerotherapy injections have been performed over the 3.5‐year postoperative period with complete perforation closure maintained following the last treatment.

Patient 6: A 52‐year‐old male with a history of two prior laser procedures, multiple blood transfusions, daily intermittent epistaxis requiring frequent self‐packing, and a known septal perforation presented for perforation closure. Staged management was recommended and included three STS treatments prior to repair. Endonasal perforation closure was performed 8 weeks following the last STS injection utilizing bilateral mucosal advancement flaps supported with a temporalis fascia interposition graft. Successful closure, with improved crusting and bleeding, was noted 6 months postoperatively. Sclerotherapy was performed 4 months later. Twenty months postrepair the patient reported infrequent and insignificant bleeding, with complete perforation closure noted on exam.

Patient 8: A 59‐year‐old male with HHT and a distant history of septoplasty presented with increasingly severe epistaxis, crusting, congestion, drainage, and cough. A perforation developed following HHT treatments that included multiple bevacizumab infusions and telangiectasia cauterizations. Endonasal closure was attempted 6 weeks postsclerotherapy utilizing superior and inferior bipedicled flaps on the left side, an AEA flap on the right, and a temporalis fascia interposition graft. Multiple small telangiectasias were cauterized at the time of repair. At 6 months postoperatively the patient reported substantial improvement in epistaxis and quality of life, though problematic nasal congestion persisted that was greater on the left side. Complete perforation closure with restriction of the left nasal cavity due to the inferiorly advanced nasal swell body contained within the superior flap was noted. Direct submucosal excision of the swell body with concurrent STS injection was performed 2 months later to improve nasal obstruction [9].

Discussion

Nasal septal perforation is a heterogeneous condition with management based on multiple factors that include symptom burden, perforation size, risk for enlargement, condition of the septal mucosa and cartilage, concurrent functional and aesthetic rhinologic conditions, patient health and treatment preferences, and surgeon experience. We observed the perforation's posterior margin to be the primary source for bleeding and obstructive crusting in some HHT patients (Figure 1). Although the HHT patient does not typically manifest unhealthy inflammatory mucosa directly related to telangiectasias, prior interventions may cause septal mucosal scarring and loss of septal cartilage resulting in the obliteration of the surgical planes that could negatively impact mucosal elevation and flap development for an attempt at closure. Additionally, the mean 1.9 cm length by 1.4 cm height of the perforations in this study predicts an increased risk for a poor treatment outcome in some patients [10, 11, 12]. Septal button prostheses and posterior septal resection have been utilized to treat perforations regarded as “complex” in an attempt to improve symptoms.

Septal Button Prosthesis

Prefabricated septal buttons made of soft silicone elastomer have historically been promoted as a first‐line, conservative treatment for perforation bleeding, whistling, and crusting. Though button tolerance varies widely among patients, a meta‐analysis found level 4 evidence for positive treatment efficacy and safety [13]. Treatment success with prefabricated buttons tends to decrease with increasing perforation size [14, 15]. Custom‐designed button prostheses of soft silicone are precisely sized for larger perforations and contoured to accommodate adjacent septal irregularities attempting to improve fit and tolerance (Figure 2). Improved Sino‐Nasal Outcome Test‐22 scores have been reported following customized button application [16].

Two case reports, each with three perforated HHT patients, noted treatment success utilizing prefabricated silicone buttons [17, 18]. Creation of a septal perforation in an HHT patient to accept a silastic button, with subsequent symptom improvement, has been reported [18]. A button prosthesis offers a potential treatment advantage over the septodermoplasty procedure that creates a large, skin‐lined area on the anterior septum prone to dryness, desquamation, obstructive crusting, and malodor [18]. Furthermore, the flanges of either a prefabricated or customized button can be tailored for extended mucosal coverage beyond the perforation margin (Figure 2F). The HHT patient may be conditioned for button tolerance given the impact of recurrent severe epistaxis, and its management, on quality of life. Disadvantages of septal buttons include the continued need for moisturization/lubrication and progressive silicone corrosion that generally requires device replacement every 4–5 years. We have noted an increase in perforation size at the time of button replacement in some patients.

Study patients treated with a customized button had perforations that ranged from 1.4 to 2.6 cm length by 1.2 to 1.8 cm height. Patients 2 and 3 had failed prior treatment with a prefabricated button. These older unhealthy patients, as well as Patient 9, reported substantial improvement in epistaxis following treatment with a customized prosthesis and adjunctive HHT treatments. Notably, both early patients reported a history of substantial bleeding prior to button insertion and did not require further epistaxis treatment prior to their deaths 9 and 19 months postbutton placement. Not unexpectedly, Patient 7 with thrombocytopenia has experienced limited improvement in bleeding and posterior septal resection is under consideration.

Posterior Septal Resection

Posterior septal resection is a technically easy procedure to perform that attempts to reduce perforation symptoms [19]. Patients with larger or complex perforations, a large re‐perforation following repair, or chronically unhealthy nasal mucosa are candidates for posterior resection. In the first study patient, a septum with a large perforation situated within a field of mucosal scarring, excoriation, telangiectasias, and obstructive crusting was deemed irreparably damaged and sacrificed to achieve the goal of a less symptomatic, posteriorly extended defect [20]. Posterior resection in the HHT patient has a further theoretical advantage of reducing the amount of septal mucosa containing telangiectasias (Figure 3).

Lesnick et al. combined subtotal posterior septectomy and dermoplasty for nine severe transfusion‐dependent HHT patients with a perforation [21]. The split‐thickness skin graft was applied in a U‐shaped fashion across the inferior perforation margin and nasal floor bilaterally, then advanced superiorly to cover the inferior and middle turbinates. Long‐lasting quality of life improvement and significant reduction in blood transfusions were reported for all patients. Ichimura et al. described a dermoplasty procedure for HHT patients with an existing large perforation, with most patients experiencing reduced frequency and volume of bleeding [22, 23]. The posterior resection technique described by Lesnick, and applied to our first patient, did not completely remove the posterior septum. The procedure has since evolved to extend the resection into the choanae (Figure 3E,F) [21].

Septal Perforation Repair

Over the last 20 years there has been an increasing interest and growing confidence in the surgical closure of symptomatic perforations. Closure rates exceeding 90% have been reported using a variety of repair procedures [8, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29]. Reduction in nasal cavity humidity occurs following perforation and can exacerbate nasal mucosal desiccation, an important consideration for patients afflicted with telangiectasias [30, 31]. Improved nasal humidification following surgical closure has been documented through nasal airflow studies utilizing computational flow dynamics modeling [32]. Furthermore, perforation closure eliminates the symptomatic perforation margin. Most current closure procedures utilize physiologic nasal mucosal flaps, which present special consideration in the patient with a mucosal disease that will require future treatment interventions. The condition of the septum following a dermoplasty procedure would likely disqualify the patient from surgical repair consideration. Of note, septodermoplasty had not been used in the last six study patients prior to their presentation for evaluation and treatment, possibly indicating an evolving trend away from this procedure for HHT management. The author's 35‐year experience and success with perforation repair, combined with the absence of prior septodermoplasty, made perforation repair feasible in the later and healthier study patients. Though these patients' telangiectasias had been treated previously with varied treatments including cauterization, laser, coblation, mucosal bevacizumab injection, and STS, unhealthy mucosal changes potentially affecting healing were not detected at the time of examination or surgery.

The senior author's basic repair technique is performed endonasally and utilizes bilateral bipedicled mucosal advancement flaps supported with an autologous interposition graft [12, 25]. This procedure was performed in Patients 4 and 6. Longitudinally oriented left‐sided bipedicled flaps and a right AEA pedicled flap achieved intraoperative complete bilateral closure in Patients 5 and 8 (Figure 4) [12, 25, 29]. We have reported an increased risk of repair failure utilizing our standard technique for perforations 1.2 cm or greater in vertical height [12]. The AEA flap leverages the available large quantity of posterior septal and nasal floor mucosa to achieve complete, tension‐free closure for these larger perforations [33, 34]. The presence of telangiectasias did not increase surgical difficulty or intraoperative bleeding. Telangiectasia bipolar electrocautery was performed on the posterior septum and turbinates during both repairs. The postoperative course for all four patients was uneventful.

Perforation length ranged from 1.2 to 2.1 cm, and height 1.0 to 1.2 cm in the surgical cohort. Perforations exceeding 2 cm in length are generally classified as large and have a higher risk of repair failure [10, 11]. Patients were assessed for both closure and symptom outcomes 6–8 months postoperatively to allow for repair stabilization. Complete perforation closure was noted in all four patients with closure maintained at last follow‐up at 60, 42, 19, and 8 months. Future re‐perforation related to operative technique is unlikely as we have rarely noted failures beyond the fifth postoperative month [12].

Symptom outcomes were determined 5–10 months postoperatively utilizing the perforation‐specific NOSE‐Perf scale [6, 35]. Postoperative NOSE‐Perf scores documented significant symptom improvement surpassing the minimally clinically important difference (MCID) threshold for this instrument. Interestingly, the measured improvement in this small cohort is comparable to the scores obtained following our non‐HHT bilateral flap and interposition autologous graft repairs. We utilize the GBI‐5F questionnaire as a global quality of life assessment following treatment to complement the NOSE‐Perf symptom scale [36, 37]. This 15‐item instrument with five domain factors measures posttreatment change in daily activities and social interactions important to patient quality of life. The questionnaire is administered only once posttreatment. The mean scores in this study's surgical cohort exceeded the median scores in our prior study, most notably in the quality of life, self‐confidence, and social involvement domains (Table 3) [37]. These findings suggest an outsized quality of life benefit to patients selected for concurrent perforation closure with telangiectasia treatment compared to non‐HHT repairs.

STS Sclerotherapy

Repair durability against future treatments for the control of epistaxis is an important surgical repair consideration. The three‐layered bilateral flap and graft repair would, in theory, be the most durable of contemporary closure procedures when compared to unilateral flap and mucosal regeneration and migration repairs. STS sclerotherapy has become our primary treatment for telangiectasias [38]. Sclerosing treatments reduce the frequency and severity of epistaxis and are well‐tolerated with a low risk of complication, including perforation [39, 40, 41]. Injections are placed 1–2 mm submucosally using minimal syringe pressure to limit the sclerosing reaction to the superficial mucosal vessels (Figure 5). All four closure patients have undergone one to five treatments postoperatively without re‐perforation. Our early experience treating the HHT patient with a perforation suggests precise STS is well‐suited for telangiectasia treatment following bilateral mucosal flap repair.

The extent of posttreatment symptom and quality of life improvement noted by the patients in this review cannot be solely attributed to the perforation treatments presented, as individual telangiectasias were treated concurrent to customized prosthesis placement or surgical repair. The marked symptom improvement reported in the earlier three patients is based on the documentation of a subjective response, while the validated NOSE‐Perf and GBI‐5F questionnaires measured posttreatment symptomatic and quality‐of‐life improvements, respectively, in the later six patients. The GBI‐5F questionnaire is designed to be completed only following treatment, and not to be administered prior to intervention. One question on the 12‐question NOSE‐Perf scale assesses epistaxis and all patients evaluated with NOSE‐Perf demonstrated improvement in that item score following repair. Inclusion of a PROM specific to epistaxis would provide an additional measurement to corroborate patient‐reported improvement.

Conclusion

Effective symptom control and improved quality of life can be achieved in the HHT patient with a perforation utilizing multiple and complementary HHT and perforation treatments. These treatments may be administered concurrently or in a staged fashion. An individualized approach to the management of the HHT patient with a perforated septum is warranted. This series of nine patients demonstrated that custom‐designed septal button prostheses, posterior septal resection, and perforation repair can play a role in achieving a significant reduction in nasal symptoms. We are increasingly confident that the quality of life in select HHT patients with a septal perforation can be substantially improved and maintained utilizing an approach to management that leverages the benefits of bilateral mucosal flap closure combined with STS. Additional treatment experience with extended follow‐up may provide further information to improve outcomes while minimizing treatment interventions and risks for these challenging patients.
