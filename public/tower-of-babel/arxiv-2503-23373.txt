On the fundamental group of the regular part of Fujiki’s compact Kähler symplectic orbifolds
Shun Yamaguchi

Abstract
We calculate the fundamental group of the regular part of certain compact Kähler symplectic orbifolds constructed by Fujiki, called Fujiki’s examples. We determine which one is an irreducible symplectic orbifold among Fujiki’s examples. This answers a question posed in [ 9 ] .



1
Introduction

Symplectic orbifolds and related notions

First, we define symplectic orbifolds and related notions.
Here, we define an
orbifold
to be a normal complex space with only quotient singularities.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
X
be a compact Kähler orbifold and
\omega
a holomorphic 2-form defined on the regular part
X_{\mathrm{reg}}
.
We call the pair
(X,\omega)
a
symplectic orbifold
when
\omega
is non-degenerate at each point on
X_{\mathrm{reg}}
.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
(X,\omega)
be a symplectic orbifold.

(i)

(X,\omega)
is said to be
primitive
when
h^{2,0}(X)=1
.

(ii)

(X,\omega)
is said to be
irreducible
when
h^{2,0}(X)=1
and
X_{\mathrm{reg}}
is simply connected.

Irreducible symplectic orbifolds appear in the orbifold version of Beauville-Bogomolov decomposition theorem:

Theorem 1.3

(cf.
[
4
, Thm. 6.4]
)
.

Let
X
be a compact Kähler orbifold with trivial first Chern class.
Then there is a finite, étale in codimension one cover
\tilde{X}\to X
and a decomposition

\tilde{X}\cong T\times\prod_{i}Y_{i}\times\prod_{j}Z_{j}

where
T
is a complex torus,
Y_{i}
are irreducible Calabi-Yau orbifolds and
Z_{j}
are irreducible symplectic orbifolds.
(Here, a compact Kähler orbifold
Z
is said to be irreducible Calabi-Yau when
K_{Z}\sim 0
,
H^{0}(Z_{\mathrm{reg}},\Omega_{Z_{\mathrm{reg}}}^{p})=0
for every
0<p<\mathrm{dim}(Z)
, and
Z_{\mathrm{reg}}
is simply connected.)

An irreducible symplectic orbifold is always a primitively symplectic orbifold by the definition.
However, the converse is not always true. The following example is such a case in two dimension.

Example 1.4
.

Let
T=\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Lambda
be a 2-dimensional complex torus (where
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
is a lattice) and
\tau
an involution on
T
defined by
\tau(p)=-p
.
Then the variety
Y:=T/\langle\tau\rangle
(a singular Kummer surface) is a primitively simplectic orbifold and has 16 singularities.

Let
\Sigma
be an arbitrary subset of
\mathrm{Sing}(Y)
and
X
the blowup of
Y
only at the points in
\Sigma
.
Then
X
is also a primitively simplectic orbifold.
Let us think of
\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})
.
If
\Sigma=\emptyset
, we have
\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})\cong\Lambda\rtimes\langle\tau\rangle
and it is an infinite group.
If
\Sigma=\mathrm{Sing}(Y)
, then
X
is a Kummer surface and
\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})=\{1\}
.
Hence, the larger the subset
\Sigma
is, the smaller
\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})
is.
Indeed, we will prove the following claim in Example
3.2
.

Claim
.

Assume that
\Sigma
is nonempty.
Let
p\in\Sigma
be an arbitrary point.
Regard
\mathrm{Sing}(Y)
as a group isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{4}
whose identity is
p
and let
\langle\Sigma\rangle
be the subgroup of it generated by
\Sigma
.
Then

\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{4}/\langle\Sigma\rangle.

We briefly describe our method to prove Theorem
1.7
.
Firstly, we prove a general result.
Let
Z
be a simply connected complex manifold and
\mathcal{G}
a discrete subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(Z)
such that
Z/\mathcal{G}
is an orbifold.
Then for an arbitrary open subset
U\subset Z/\mathcal{G}
containing the regular part of
Z/\mathcal{G}
, we define a certain normal subgroup
N_{U}\subset\mathcal{G}
and prove that

\pi_{1}(U)\cong\mathcal{G}/N_{U}
(Theorem
3.1
).
Let us return to the setting of Theorem
1.7
.
By Proposition
2.1
, we can take a Zariski open subset
U
of
Y
in such a way that

(i)

U\supset Y_{\mathrm{reg}}
,

(ii)

X_{\mathrm{reg}}\supset f^{-1}(U)
,

(iii)

\mathrm{Codim}(X_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus f^{-1}(U),X_{\mathrm{reg}})\geq 2
.

Since
U
has only quotient singularities, we have

f_{*}:\pi_{1}(f^{-1}(U))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\pi_{1}(U).

On the other hand, we have an isomorphism

\pi_{1}(f^{-1}(U))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})

by
(iii)
.
Finally, by writing
Y
in the form
Z/\mathcal{G}
, we get

\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})\cong\pi_{1}(U)\cong\mathcal{G}/N_{U}

and then we calculate
\mathcal{G}/N_{U}
.
If
S
is a K3 surface, we simply set
Z=S\times S
and
\mathcal{G}=G
(Corollary
3.3
).
If
S=\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Lambda
is a complex torus (where
\Lambda
is a lattice), we set
Z=\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{2}
and
\mathcal{G}
a certain discrete group containing
\Lambda\times\Lambda
as a subgroup of finite index. In this case, the calculation becomes more complicated (Corollary
3.4
).

As is well-known,
\mathrm{Hilb}^{2}(S)
(where
S
is a K3 surface) or
\mathrm{Kum}^{2}(S)
(where
S
is a 2-dimensional complex torus) is simply connected (cf.
[

).
Our method also gives a new proof to this fact.

2
Fujiki’s examples

In this section, we describe the construction of Fujiki’s examples in further detail.
Let
S
be either a K3 surface or a 2-dimensional complex torus and
H
a finite abelian subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(S)
such that each element in
H
preserves the symplectic form on
S
.
If
S
is a complex torus, we also assume that
H
is not contained in the group of translations.
Then the possibilities of the isomorphism class of
H
as a group are classified as follows (see
[
5
, Prop. 12.5]
):

(1) The case
S
is a K3 surface.

(1.a)

(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{m},\quad(0\leq m\leq 4)
,

(1.b)

(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})^{m},\quad(1\leq m\leq 2)
,

(1.c)

(\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z})^{m},\quad(1\leq m\leq 2)
,

(1.d)

\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z},\quad(p=5,7,8)
,

(1.e)

\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z},
,

(1.f)

\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z},\quad(q=4,6)
.

(2) The case
S
is a complex torus.

(2.a)

(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{m}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z},\quad(0\leq m\leq 4)
,

(2.b)

(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})^{m}\times\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z},\quad(0\leq m\leq 2)
,

(2.c)

(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{m}\times\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z},\quad(0\leq m\leq 2)
,

(2.d)

\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}\;(\cong\{1\}\times\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z})
.

In the torus cases, the first factor indicates the translation part, and the second factor indicates the linear part.
If we regard the translation part as a subgroup of
S
, all of its elements are fixed by the action of the linear part because of the abelianness.

Define the action of
H
on
S\times S
by

\begin{array}[]{ll}h(x,y)=(hx,h^{-1}y)&\textup{($h\in H$, $(x,y)\in S\times S$ )}\end{array}

and the involution
\iota\in\mathrm{Aut}(S\times S)
by

\begin{array}[]{ll}\iota(x,y)=(y,x)&\textup{($(x,y)\in S\times S$ )}.\end{array}

Let
G
be the subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(S\times S)
generated by
H
and
\iota
.
Then for any
h\in H
and
(x,y)\in S\times S
,

\displaystyle\iota h\iota^{-1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\iota h\iota(x,y)

\displaystyle=\iota h(y,x)

\displaystyle=\iota(hy,h^{-1}x)

\displaystyle=(h^{-1}x,hy)

\displaystyle=h^{-1}(x,y).

Hence,
\iota h\iota^{-1}=h^{-1}\in H
, and so
G=H\rtimes\langle\iota\rangle
.

Let
Y=(S\times S)/G
and
f:X\to Y
a
\mathbb{Q}
-factorial terminalization of
Y
.
This exists by
[
3
, Corollary 1.4.3]
.
Except in the case of
(1.d)
,
X
is constructed explicitly in
[
5
, §7]
and it is proven that
X
has only quotient singularities.
Therefore,
X
is a symplectic orbifold except in the case of
(1.d)
.
Moreover,
[
5
, Lemma 13.4]
shows that
X
is a primitively symplectic except the case
(2.a)
.
Consequently, except in the cases of
(2.a)
and
(1.d)
,
we obtain a primitively symplectic orbifold
X
, which we call a Fujiki’s example.

Let us observe the singularities of
Y
, which are the images of fixed points on
S\times S
by the action of
G
.
Since
G=H\rtimes\langle\iota\rangle
, each element
g\in G\setminus\{1\}
is expressed in the form either
h
or
\iota h
for some
h\in H
.
In the case
g=\iota h
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Fix}_{S\times S}(\iota h)

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\in S\times S\mid(h^{-1}y,hx)=(x,y)\}

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\in S\times S\mid y=hx\}

\displaystyle=\{(x,hx)\in S\times S\mid x\in S\}.

Thus,
\mathrm{Fix}_{S\times S}(\iota h)\,(\cong S
) is a 2-dimensional locus on
S\times S
and its image by the natural projection
\pi:S\times S\to Y
is a 2-dimensional singlular locus of
Y
.
In the case
g=h
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Fix}_{S\times S}(h)

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\in S\times S\mid(hx,h^{-1}y)=(x,y)\}

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\in S\times S\mid hx=x,hy=y\}

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\in S\times S\mid x,y\in\mathrm{Fix}_{S}(h)\}.

Since
h\,(\neq 1)
is a symplectic automorphism on
S
, it has only isolated fixed points.
This implies that
\mathrm{Fix}_{S\times S}(h)
consists of finitely many points.
Let
p
be a point of
\mathrm{Fix}_{S\times S}(h)
.
There are two possibilities of the image
\pi(p)
.
The first one is an intersection point of 2-dimensional singular loci and the second one is a isolated singular point.
In summary,
Y
has 2-dimensional singular loci (which intersect each other) and isolated singlular points.

For later use, we prove the next proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

Define an open subset
U
of
Y
by

U=Y\setminus\pi\left(\bigcup_{h\in H\setminus\{1\}}\mathrm{Fix}_{S\times S}(h)\right).

Then
U
satisfies the following conditions:

(i)

U\supset Y_{\mathrm{reg}}
,

(ii)

X_{\mathrm{reg}}\supset f^{-1}(U)
,

(iii)

\mathrm{Codim}(X_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus f^{-1}(U),X_{\mathrm{reg}})\geq 2
.

Proof.

The open subset
U
is obtained by substructing isolated singular points and intersection points of 2-dimensional singular loci from
Y
.
Thus
(i)
is obvious.

Because

f|_{f^{-1}(U)}:f^{-1}(U)\to U

is a resolution of quotient singularities,
(ii)
also holds.

By
[
8
, Corollary 0.2]
,
\mathrm{dim}(f^{-1}(y))\leq 2
for any point
y\in Y
.
Since
Y\setminus U
consists of finitely many points,
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Codim}(X_{\mathrm{reg}}\setminus f^{-1}(U),X_{\mathrm{reg}})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Codim}(X_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap f^{-1}(Y\setminus U),X_{\mathrm{reg}})

\displaystyle\geq 2.

∎

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
U
be the open subset of
Y
defined in Proposition
2.1
.
Then

\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})\cong\pi_{1}(U).

Proof.

By
[
6
, Thm. 7.8]
, we have that

f_{*}:\pi_{1}(f^{-1}(U))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\pi_{1}(U).

Also, we see

\pi_{1}(f^{-1}(U))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})

by
(iii)
.
Therefore, the conclusion holds.
∎

3
Calculation of
\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})

In this section, we calculate
\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})
for Fujiki’s examples
X
.
First, we prove the following (general) theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
Z
be a simply connected complex manifold.
Let
\mathcal{G}
be a discrete subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(Z)
which satisfies the following conditions:

(i)

the action of
\mathcal{G}
on
Z
is properly discontiuous,

(ii)

the stabilizer group
\mathrm{Stab}(z)
is finite for all
z\in Z
,

(iii)

\mathrm{Codim}(\mathrm{Fix}(g),Z)\geq 2
for all
g\in\mathcal{G}\setminus\{1\}
.

Define
Y=Z/\mathcal{G}
and let
\pi:Z\to Y
be the natural surjection
(because of the conditions
(i)
and
(ii)
,
Y
is a complex orbifold).
Finally, let
U
be an arbitrary open subset of
Y
containing
Y_{\mathrm{reg}}
and define
N_{U}
to be the subgroup of
\mathcal{G}
generated by the set

\bigcup_{z\in\pi^{-1}(U)}\mathrm{Stab}(z).

Then
N_{U}
is a normal subgroup of
\mathcal{G}
and
\pi_{1}(U)\cong\mathcal{G}/N_{U}
.

Proof.

For all
g\in\mathcal{G}
,
z\in\pi^{-1}(U)
and
h\in\mathrm{Stab}(z)
,
hz\in\pi^{-1}(U)
and
ghg^{-1}\in\mathrm{Stab}(hz)
.
Hence,
N_{U}
is a normal subgroup of
\mathcal{G}
.

Let
p
be the projection
Z/N_{U}\to Y
.
Then
p^{-1}(U)\to U
is unramified.
Indeed, let
\bar{z}
be an arbitrary point in
p^{-1}(U)
and take a lift
z\in\pi^{-1}(U)
of
\bar{z}
.
The covering
p:p^{-1}(U)\to U
is a Galois covering whose Galois group equals to
\mathcal{G}/N_{U}
. The stabilizer group of
\mathrm{Stab}(\bar{z})\subset\mathcal{G}/N_{U}
is trivial because

\mathrm{Stab}(\bar{z})=\mathrm{Stab}(z)N_{U}/N_{U}=\{1\}

and this implies
p:p^{-1}(U)\to U
is unramified.

Moreover,
p^{-1}(U)
is simply connected.
To see this, let
q:V\to p^{-1}(U)
be the universal covering of
p^{-1}(U)
and assume that
q
is not the trivial covering.
Since the composition map

p\circ q:V\to p^{-1}(U)\to U

is also the universal covering of
U
, its restriction

p\circ q:(p\circ q)^{-1}(Y_{\mathrm{reg}})\to p^{-1}(Y_{\mathrm{reg}})\to Y_{\mathrm{reg}}

is an unramified Galois covering.
On the other hand, since
Z
is simply connected complex manifold and
\pi^{-1}(Y_{\mathrm{reg}}
) is the open subset of
Z
such that the (complex) codimension of its complement is at least two,
\pi^{-1}(Y_{\mathrm{reg}})
is also simply connected and
\pi^{-1}(Y_{\mathrm{reg}})
is the universal covering of
Y_{\mathrm{reg}}
.
Therefore,
\pi:\pi^{-1}(Y_{\mathrm{reg}})\to Y_{\mathrm{reg}}
factors as

\pi^{-1}(Y_{\mathrm{reg}})\to(p\circ q)^{-1}(Y_{\mathrm{reg}})\xrightarrow{q}p^{-1}(Y_{\mathrm{reg}})\xrightarrow{p}Y_{\mathrm{reg}}.

(1)

Let
N^{\prime}
be the normal subgroup of
\mathcal{G}
which corresponds to
(p\circ q)^{-1}(Y_{\mathrm{reg}})
.
The sequence (
1
) corresponds to the sequence of groups

1\subset N^{\prime}\subset N_{U}\subset\mathcal{G}.

Since
q
is not trivial covering,
N^{\prime}\subsetneq N_{U}
and hence, there exists a
z\in\pi^{-1}(U)
such that
\mathrm{Stab}(z)\not\subset N^{\prime}
.
If
z^{\prime}
is the image of
z
in
\pi^{-1}(U)/N^{\prime}=V
, then

\mathrm{Stab}(z^{\prime})(\subset\mathcal{G}/N^{\prime})=\mathrm{Stab}(z)N^{\prime}/N^{\prime}\neq\{1\}.

This contradicts the fact that
p\circ q:V\to p^{-1}(U)\to U
is unramified.
Therefore,
q
is the trivial covering and
p^{-1}(U)
is simply connected.

Consequently,
p:p^{-1}(U)\to U
is the universal covering.
From this fact and the definition of
p
, we deduce that

\pi_{1}(U)\cong\mathrm{Aut}(p^{-1}(U)/U)\cong\mathcal{G}/N_{U}.

∎

Example 3.2
.

As an example of the theorem, we calculate the fundamental group of the regular part of the primitively simplectic orbifold obtained by blowing up some of the singularities of a singular Kummer surface.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
be a 2-dimensional lattice and
T=\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Lambda
a 2-dimensional complex torus.
Let
\tau
be an involution
T
is defined by
\tau(p)=-p
and let
Y=T/\langle\tau\rangle
be the singular Kummer surface associated with the complex torus
T
.
Let
\mathrm{Sing}(Y)=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{16}\}
be the set of singular points of
Y
.
Fix a nonempty subset
\Sigma\subset\mathrm{Sing}(Y)
and let
X
be a surface obtained by blowing up
X
at the points in
\Sigma
.
We prove the following claim.

Claim
.

Let
p\in\Sigma
be an arbitrary point.
Regard
\mathrm{Sing}(Y)=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{16}\}
as a group isomorphic to
(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{4}
whose identity is
p
and let
\langle\Sigma\rangle
be the subgroup of it generated by
\Sigma
.
Then

\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{4}/\langle\Sigma\rangle.

Proof.

Let
f:X\to Y
be the blowup morphism and let
U
an open subset of
Y
obtained by getting rid of singular points not belonging to the set
\Sigma
:

\displaystyle U:=

\displaystyle Y\setminus(\mathrm{Sing}(Y)\setminus\Sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Y_{\mathrm{reg}}\sqcup\Sigma.

Then the restriction map

f|_{X_{\mathrm{reg}}}:X_{\mathrm{reg}}\to U

is a resolution of quotient singularities.
Therefore, by
[
6
, Thm. 7.8]
,

\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\pi_{1}(U).

Hence, we compute
\pi_{1}(U)
by applying Theorem
3.1
.
In order to do that, we write
Y
as a quotient of a simply connected complex manifold:

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=T/\langle\tau\rangle

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Lambda)/\langle\tau\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}^{2}/(\Lambda\rtimes\langle\tau\rangle),

where the action of
\tau
on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
is similarly defined by
\tau(x)=-x
for any
x\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
We apply Theorem
3.1
by setting
Z=\mathbb{C}^{2}
and
\mathcal{G}=\Lambda\rtimes\langle\tau\rangle
.
Then we get

\pi_{1}(U)\cong\mathcal{G}/N_{U}.

and we determine the normal subgroup
N_{U}
in the next lemma.

Lemma
.

Let
\pi:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to Y(=\mathbb{C}^{2}/\mathcal{G})
be the natural projection and

\Lambda^{\prime}
be the sublattice of
\Lambda
generated by the set

\left\{\lambda\in\Lambda\mid\pi\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\in\Sigma\right\}.

Then

N_{U}=\Lambda^{\prime}\rtimes\langle\tau\rangle.

Proof of Lemma.

For any
\lambda\in\Lambda
, let
t_{\lambda}\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{C}^{2})
be the translation map:

t_{\lambda}:\mathbb{C}^{2}\ni x\mapsto x+\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{2}.

Since
G=\Lambda\rtimes\langle\tau\rangle
, we can write each element of
G
in the form either
t_{\lambda}
or
t_{\lambda}\tau
.

Let us recall the definition of the normal subgroup
N_{U}\triangleleft\;G
in Theorem
3.1
:

N_{U}:=\left\langle\bigcup_{x\in\pi^{-1}(U)}\mathrm{Stab}(x)\right\rangle.

If
t_{\lambda}
belongs to
\mathrm{Stab}(x)
for some
x\in\pi^{-1}(U)
, then

\displaystyle t_{\lambda}(x)=x

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle x+\lambda=x

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\lambda=0.

Hence, any non-trivial translation element
t_{\lambda}
cannot be in
\mathrm{Stab}(x)
.
On the other hand, if
t_{\lambda}\tau\in\mathrm{Stab}(x)
for some
x\in\pi^{-1}(U)
, then

\displaystyle t_{\lambda}\tau(x)=x

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle-x+\lambda=x

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle x=\frac{\lambda}{2}.

Therefore, the image of
x
on
T
is a 2-torsion point, and so
\pi(x)
is a singlular point of
Y
.
Because
\pi(x)\in U=Y_{\mathrm{reg}}\sqcup\Sigma
, we deduce that
\pi\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)=\pi(x)\in\Sigma
.
Hence, for any
\lambda\in\Lambda
such that
\pi\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\in\Sigma
, we have

\displaystyle t_{\lambda}\tau\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)=-\frac{\lambda}{2}+\lambda=\frac{\lambda}{2}

and therefore

t_{\lambda}\tau\in\mathrm{Stab}\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\right).

Consequently, we get

\displaystyle N_{U}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\bigcup_{x\in\pi^{-1}(U)}\mathrm{Stab}(x)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left\{t_{\lambda}\tau\mid\pi\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\in\Sigma\right\}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\left\{t_{\lambda}\mid\pi\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\in\Sigma\right\}\right\rangle\rtimes\langle\tau\rangle

\displaystyle=\Lambda^{\prime}\rtimes\langle\tau\rangle.

∎

We return to the proof of the claim.
From the above, we have

\displaystyle\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})

\displaystyle\cong\pi_{1}(U)

\displaystyle\cong G/N_{U}

\displaystyle\cong(\Lambda\rtimes\langle\tau\rangle)/(\Lambda^{\prime}\rtimes\langle\tau\rangle)

\displaystyle\cong\Lambda/\Lambda^{\prime}

\displaystyle\cong(\Lambda/2\Lambda)/(\Lambda^{\prime}/2\Lambda)

\displaystyle\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{4}/\langle\Sigma\rangle.

∎

By means of Theorem
3.1
, we calculate
\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})
for Fujiki’s examples
X
.
Let

be either a K3 surface or a complex torus and

the finite abelian subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(S\times S)
which is used to construct

.
Let

be the subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(S\times S)
defined in Section

, where

Let

.
Then a Fujiki’s example

is obtained as a

We firstly deal with the cases where

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
X
be a Fujiki’s example obtained from a K3 surface
S
.
Then
\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})=\{1\}
.
Thus,
X
is an irreducible symplectic orbifold.

Proof.

Define an open subset
U
of
Y
as in the Proposition
2.1
.
Then by Corollary
2.2
, we have
\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})\cong\pi_{1}(U)
.

Therefore, it is enough to show that
\pi_{1}(U)=\{1\}
.
Let us apply Theorem
3.1
by setting
Z=S\times S
and
\mathcal{G}=G
.
Then
\pi(U)\cong G/N_{U}
holds.
We need to show that
G=N_{U}

Let
h\in H
be an arbitrary element.
Since

\mathrm{dim}\,\mathrm{Fix}_{S\times S}(\iota h)=\mathrm{dim}\,\pi(\mathrm{Fix}_{S\times S}(\iota h))=2

and

\mathrm{dim}\,Y\setminus U=\mathrm{dim}\,\pi\left(\bigcup_{h\in H\setminus\{1\}}\mathrm{Fix}_{S\times S}(h)\right)=0,

we can choose a point

so that

.
Hence,

\iota h\in\mathrm{Stab}(p)\subset N_{U}.

In particular, we have
\iota\in N_{U}
by setting

.
Therefore, for any

, we see that

h=\iota\cdot\iota h\in N_{U}.

As a result,

G=H\rtimes\langle\iota\rangle\subset N_{U}.

∎

Next, we calculate for the cases where
X
comes from a complex torus.
The same argument as above cannot be applied to these cases because
S\times S
is not simply connected when
S

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
X
be a Fujiki’s example obtained from a 2-dimensional complex torus
S
.
Then

\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})\cong\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})^{2-m}&\textup{(for the case \ref{t3})}\\
(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{2-m}&\textup{(for the case \ref{t4})}\\
\{1\}&\textup{(for the case \ref{t6})}\end{array}\right..

Proof.

Let us write
S=\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Lambda
where
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
is a lattice.
We choose the image of the origin of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
as a origin of
S
.
Let
U
an open subset of
Y
defined in the Proposition
2.1
.
Then by Corollary
2.2
, we have
\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})\cong\pi_{1}(U)
.
We calculate
\pi_{1}(U)
.

Since
H(\subset\mathrm{Aut}(S))
is abelian, we have the decomposition
H=H^{\prime}\times H_{0}
where
H^{\prime}
is the translation part and
H_{0}
is the linear part of
H
.
We can regard
H^{\prime}
as a finite subgroup of
S
whose elements are preserved by the action of
H_{0}
.
Then

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle\cong H\rtimes\langle\iota\rangle

\displaystyle\cong(H^{\prime}\times H_{0})\rtimes\langle\iota\rangle

\displaystyle\cong H^{\prime}\rtimes(H_{0}\rtimes\langle\iota\rangle).

This is the decomposition of
G
as a subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(S\times S)
;
H^{\prime}
is the translation part and
H_{0}\rtimes\langle\iota\rangle
is the linear part. The group
H^{\prime}
is regarded as a subgroup of the 4-dimensional torus
S\times S
via the inclusion

\displaystyle i:H^{\prime}\ni\gamma\mapsto(\gamma,-\gamma)\in S\times S.

Let
n
be the order of
H_{0}
(by the classification of
H
,
n\in\{3,4,6\}
and
H_{0}\cong\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
).
Then
H_{0}\rtimes\langle\iota\rangle
is isomorphic to the dihedral group
D_{n}
of order
2n
.
Therefore,
G\cong H^{\prime}\rtimes D_{n}
.
We have

\displaystyle(S\times S)/G

\displaystyle\cong(\mathbb{C}^{4}/\Lambda^{2})/G

\displaystyle\cong\mathbb{C}^{4}/(\Lambda^{2}\rtimes G)

\displaystyle\cong\mathbb{C}^{4}/(\Lambda^{2}\rtimes(H^{\prime}\rtimes D_{n}))

\displaystyle\cong\mathbb{C}^{4}/((\Lambda^{2}\rtimes H^{\prime})\rtimes D_{n}).

Since the action of
H^{\prime}
is translation,
H^{\prime}
and
\Lambda^{2}
commutes.
Hence,
\Lambda^{2}\rtimes H^{\prime}\cong\Lambda^{2}\times H^{\prime}
.
This group is an overlattice of
\Lambda^{2}
corresponds to the quotient torus
(S\times S)/H^{\prime}
.
We denote this overlattice by
\Gamma
.
Finally, we have

Y=(S\times S)/G\cong\mathbb{C}^{4}/(\Gamma\rtimes D_{n}).

We explain the action of
\Gamma\rtimes D_{n}
on
\mathbb{C}^{4}
.
The lattice
\Gamma
acts as translations.
Let
\sigma
be a generator of
H_{0}\cong\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
.
The group
H_{0}
originally acts on
S=\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Lambda
as the linear part of
H
.
Hence,
\sigma
naturally lifts to a linear automorphism of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
which preserves the lattice
\Lambda
.
Because
H
acts on
S\times S
by
h(x,y)=(hx,h^{-1}y)
,
the lifted linear action of
\sigma
on
\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{2}=\mathbb{C}^{4}
is also expressed as

\sigma(x,y)=(\sigma x,\sigma^{-1}y),\quad(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{2}.

Similarly, the involution
\iota\in\mathrm{Aut}(S\times S)
lifts to
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{C}^{4})
by the action

\iota(x,y)=(y,x),\quad(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{2}=\mathbb{C}^{4}.

These lifts
\sigma
and
\iota
generate
H_{0}\rtimes\langle\iota\rangle=D_{n}
,
and preserve the lattice
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{C}^{4}
.

Let
\pi:\mathbb{C}^{4}\to Y=\mathbb{C}^{4}/(\Gamma\rtimes D_{n})
be the natural projection.
Then by applying Theorem
3.1
(by setting
Z=\mathbb{C}^{4}
and
\mathcal{G}=\Gamma\rtimes D_{n}
), we have

\pi_{1}(U)\cong(\Gamma\rtimes D_{n})/N_{U},

where
N_{U}=\langle\bigcup_{p\in\pi^{-1}(U)}\mathrm{Stab}(p)\rangle
is the normal subgroup of
\Gamma\rtimes D_{n}
defined Theorem
3.1
.
Let us calculate
N_{U}
.
For each
k=0,\ldots,n-1
,
let

P_{k}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{2}=\mathbb{C}^{4}\mid y=-\sigma x\}.

Each
P_{k}
is a 2-plane in
\mathbb{C}^{4}
.
We set
\Gamma_{k}=\Gamma\cap P_{k}
and

\Gamma^{\prime}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Gamma_{k}\quad(\subset\Gamma)

We prove the following claim.

Claim
.

(i)

N_{U}=\Gamma^{\prime}\rtimes D_{n}
as a subgroup of
\Gamma\rtimes D_{n}
,

(ii)

\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma_{0}\oplus\Gamma_{1}
,

(iii)

Define
\phi:\mathbb{C}^{4}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{C}^{4}
by

\phi(x,y)=(x,\sigma y),\quad(x,y)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{2}=\mathbb{C}^{4}.

Then
\Gamma_{1}=\phi(\Gamma_{0})

Proof of Claim.

(i)

For each
\gamma\in\Gamma
, let
t_{\gamma}\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{C}^{4})
be the translation
p\mapsto p+\gamma
.
Then each element
g\in\Gamma\rtimes D_{n}
is expressed in the form either
t_{\gamma}\sigma^{k}
or
t_{\gamma}\iota\sigma^{k}
for some
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
k\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
.
Let us suppose that

t_{\gamma}\sigma^{k}\in\bigcup_{p\in\pi^{-1}(U)}\mathrm{Stab}(p).

Then for some
p\in\pi^{-1}(U)
, we have

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=t_{\gamma}\sigma^{k}(p)=\sigma^{k}p+\gamma.

Therefore,

\pi(p)=\sigma^{k}\pi(p).

Since this contradicts the definition of
U
,
any element in the form of
t_{\gamma}\sigma^{k}
dose not belong to
\bigcup_{p\in\pi^{-1}(U)}\mathrm{Stab}(p)
.
On the other hand, if

t_{\gamma}\iota\sigma^{k}\in\bigcup_{p\in\pi^{-1}(U)}\mathrm{Stab}(p),

then
\mathrm{Fix}_{\mathbb{C}^{4}}(t_{\gamma}\iota\sigma^{k})\neq\emptyset
.
Conversely, if
\mathrm{Fix}_{\mathbb{C}^{4}}(t_{\gamma}\iota\sigma^{k})\neq\emptyset
, then

\mathrm{Fix}_{\mathbb{C}^{4}}(t_{\gamma}\iota\sigma^{k})\setminus\bigcup_{\gamma\in\Gamma,k=0,\ldots,n-1}\mathrm{Fix}_{\mathbb{C}^{4}}(t_{\gamma}\sigma^{k})\neq\emptyset

because the first term is 2-dimensional locus and the second term is 0-dimensional locus.
Let
p
be a point of the above set.
Again by the definition of
U
, we have
\pi(p)\in U

and therefore,

t_{\gamma}\iota\sigma^{k}\in\bigcup_{p\in\pi^{-1}(U)}\mathrm{Stab}(p).

Consequently,

\bigcup_{p\in\pi^{-1}(U)}\mathrm{Stab}(p)=\{t_{\gamma}\iota\sigma^{k}\mid\mathrm{Fix}_{\mathbb{C}^{4}}(t_{\gamma}\iota\sigma^{k})\neq\emptyset\}.

Moreover, we claim that

\mathrm{Fix}_{\mathbb{C}^{4}}(t_{\gamma}\iota\sigma^{k})\neq\emptyset\Leftrightarrow\gamma\in\Gamma_{k}.

If
p\in\mathrm{Fix}_{\mathbb{C}^{4}}(t_{\gamma}\iota\sigma^{k})
,
then

\displaystyle\iota\sigma^{k}p+\gamma=p\Leftrightarrow\gamma=p-\iota\sigma^{k}p

and hence,

\displaystyle\iota\sigma^{k}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\iota\sigma^{k}(p-\iota\sigma^{k}p)

\displaystyle=\iota\sigma^{k}p-p

\displaystyle=\gamma.

This implies
\gamma\in P_{k}\cap\Gamma=\Gamma_{k}
. On the other hand, if
\gamma\in\Gamma_{k}
,
then

\displaystyle t_{\gamma}\iota\sigma^{k}\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=t_{\gamma}\left(-\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma}{2}+\gamma

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{2}

and so

\frac{\gamma}{2}\in\mathrm{Fix}_{\mathbb{C}^{4}}(t_{\gamma}\iota\sigma^{k})\neq\emptyset.

Finally, we have

\displaystyle N_{U}

\displaystyle=\langle\bigcup_{p\in\pi^{-1}(U)}\mathrm{Stab}(p)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\{t_{\gamma}\iota\sigma^{k}\mid\gamma\in\Gamma_{k},k=0.\ldots,n-1\}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\{t_{\gamma}\mid\gamma\in\bigcup_{k=0}^{n-1}\Gamma_{k}\}\cup D_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\{t_{\gamma}\mid\gamma\in\Gamma^{\prime}\}\cup D_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{\prime}\rtimes D_{n}\quad(\subset\Gamma\rtimes D_{n}).

(ii)

First, we show that
\Gamma_{k}\subset\Gamma_{0}+\Gamma_{1}
for all
k=0,\ldots,n-1
.
This implies
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma_{0}+\Gamma_{1}
.
We prove by induction on
k
. Let
\gamma=(x,y)\in\Gamma_{k}
.
Then
y=-\sigma^{k}x

and for arbitrary
l
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma^{l}\gamma

\displaystyle=\sigma^{l}(x,-\sigma^{k}x)

\displaystyle=(\sigma^{l}x,-\sigma^{k-l}x)

\displaystyle=(\sigma^{l}x,-\sigma^{k-2l}(\sigma^{l}x))

\displaystyle\in\Gamma_{k-2l}.

We also have

\displaystyle\gamma+\sigma^{l}\gamma

\displaystyle=(x+\sigma^{l}x,-\sigma^{k}x-\sigma^{k-l}x)

\displaystyle=(x+\sigma^{l}x,-\sigma^{k-l}(x+\sigma^{l}x))

\displaystyle\in\Gamma_{k-l}.

In the case where
k
is even, let
l=\frac{k}{2}
.
Then
\sigma^{l}\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}
and
\gamma+\sigma^{l}\gamma\in\Gamma_{l}
.
By the induction hypothesis,
\Gamma_{l}\subset\Gamma_{0}+\Gamma_{1}
.
Hence,

\gamma=-\sigma^{l}\gamma+(\gamma+\sigma^{l}\gamma)\in\Gamma_{0}+\Gamma_{1}

Similarly, in the case where
k
is odd, let
l=\frac{k-1}{2}
.
Then
\sigma^{l}\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}
and
\gamma+\sigma^{l}\gamma\in\Gamma_{l+1}\subset\Gamma_{0}+\Gamma_{1}
.
Hence,
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}+\Gamma_{1}
.
Therefore, we have proved that
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma_{0}+\Gamma_{1}
.
Since
\Gamma_{0}\cap\Gamma_{1}\subset P_{0}\cap P_{1}=\{0\}
,
we have
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma_{0}\oplus\Gamma_{1}
.

(iii)

Since
\Gamma
is preserved by
\phi
, we have

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}

\displaystyle=P_{1}\cap\Gamma

\displaystyle=\phi(P_{0})\cap\phi(\Gamma)

\displaystyle=\phi(P_{0}\cap\Gamma)

\displaystyle=\phi(\Gamma).

∎

By the claim, we have

\displaystyle\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})

\displaystyle\cong\pi(U)

\displaystyle\cong(\Gamma\rtimes D_{n})/(\Gamma^{\prime}\rtimes D_{n})

\displaystyle\cong\Gamma/\Gamma^{\prime}

\displaystyle\cong\Gamma/(\Gamma_{0}\oplus\Gamma_{1})

\displaystyle\cong\Gamma/(\Gamma_{0}\oplus\phi(\Gamma_{0}))

We calculate
\Gamma_{0}
and
\phi(\Gamma_{0})
.
Let us consider, for example, the case of
(2.c)
and
m=1
, i.e., the case where
H\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}
.
The calculations for the other cases are similar.

We can choose a basis

for the lattice

so that

\displaystyle\sigma(e_{1})=e_{2},\quad\sigma(e_{2})=-e_{1},\quad\sigma(e_{3})=e_{4},\quad\sigma(e_{4})=-e_{3}

(see the proof of
[
5
, Theorem 12,.7]
).
Let

.
Then

\displaystyle\sigma(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{-e_{2}+e_{1}}{2}

\displaystyle=f-e_{2}

\displaystyle\in f+\Lambda

and so the image of

in

is fixed by
\sigma
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that
H^{\prime}=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
(the translation part of

) is generated by this image. Since

is embedded into

by the inclusion

and

is the overlattice of

we can take

\displaystyle(f,-f),\;

\displaystyle(e_{2},0),\;(e_{3},0),\;(e_{4},0),\;

\displaystyle(0,e_{1}),\;

\displaystyle(0,e_{2}),\;(0,e_{3}),\;(0,e_{4})

as a basis for
\Gamma
.
Then we readily see that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}

\displaystyle=P_{0}\cap\Gamma

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Z}\langle\,(f,-f),\;(e_{2},-e_{2}),\;(e_{3},-e_{3}),\;(e_{4},-e_{4})\,\rangle.

and therefore,

\displaystyle\phi(\Gamma_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Z}\langle\,(f,-f+e_{2}),\;(e_{2},e_{1}),\;(e_{3},-e_{4}),\;(e_{4},e_{3})\,\rangle.

A direct computation shows that

\displaystyle\Gamma/(\Gamma_{0}\oplus\phi(\Gamma_{0}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{Z}\langle\,(f,-f),\;(e_{2},0),\;(0,e_{1}),\;(0,e_{2})\,\rangle}{\mathbb{Z}\langle\,(f,-f),\;(e_{2},-e_{2}),\;(f,-f+e_{2}),\;(e_{2},e_{1})\,\rangle}

\displaystyle\oplus\frac{\mathbb{Z}\langle\,(e_{3},0),\;(e_{4},0),\;(0,e_{3}),\;(0,e_{4})\,\rangle}{\mathbb{Z}\langle\,(e_{3},-e_{3}),\;(e_{4},-e_{4}),\;(e_{3},-e_{4}),\;(e_{4},e_{3})\,\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{1\}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}

As a result, we have
\pi_{1}(X_{\mathrm{reg}})\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
for the case of

and

.
∎
