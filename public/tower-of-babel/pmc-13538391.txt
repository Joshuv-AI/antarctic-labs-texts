Vitamin B5 supports anti-PD1 response in HER2-positive gastric cancer and enhances interaction between naïve B cells and T cells
Authors: Chaoye Wang, Jing Yang, Ming-Yu Lai, Jia-Chen Lu, Li-Qiong Yang, Zhi-Ying Wu, Hai-Yan Zhang, Fang Wang, Masami Yamamoto, Tetsuya Tsukamoto, Sachiyo Nomura, Feng-Hua Wang, Wen-Long Guan, Ying Jin, Rui-Hua Xu, Qi Zhao, Miao-Zhen Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The cellular mechanisms underlying HER2-positive gastric cancer (HER2+ GC) responses to immunochemotherapy remain underexplored. Here, we perform longitudinal single-cell RNA-sequencing with paired TCR/BCR profiling on HER2+ and HER2-negative gastric cancer (HER2− GC) pre- and post-treatment to find dynamic immune remodeling during. Pre-treatment HER2+ GC displays an immunogenic microenvironment enriched with tumor-reactive T and B cells compared to HER2− GC. Following immunochemotherapy (anti-PD-1, XELOX/FOLFOX and anti-HER2), HER2+ GC display clonal expansions of tumor-reactive T cells, increased memory B cell differentiation, enhanced naïve B cell recruitment, and enriched B-T cell interaction. Mechanistically, vitamin B5 promotes human T cell proliferation in coculture with naïve B cells. Notably, B5 biosynthesis is enriched in tumor-infiltrating naïve B cells in GC patient samples, and serum B5 concentration is elevated in the HER2+PD-L1+ subset. In vivo, vitamin B5 supplementation boosts anti-PD-1 responses in female mice bearing subcutaneous cancers. Our data thus demonstrate a critical function for naïve B cells in immunochemotherapy response in HER2+ GC, and also implicate vitamin B5 supplementation for potentiating immune checkpoint blockade.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide, claiming over 659,853 deaths in 20221,2. Notably, the overexpression of HER2 protein or the amplification of the ERBB2 gene has been associated with the development of gastric adenocarcinoma3. HER2 positivity rates in patients with metastatic GC range from 12–23%4–6, often indicating a poorer prognosis7–9.

The combination of anti-HER2 antibody trastuzumab with chemotherapy has been recommended as first-line therapy for patients with HER2+ gastric/gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma10,11. The addition of pembrolizumab to first-line trastuzumab and chemotherapy led to meaningfully longer progression-free survival and improvements in tumor response12, with the objective response rate (ORR) increasing from 51.9% to 74.4%13. Phase III trials have also explored the addition of anti-PD-1 inhibitors to first-line chemotherapy in patients with HER2− advanced gastric or gastroesophageal cancer14. Agents such as nivolumab, pembrolizumab, sintilimab, and tislelizumab have demonstrated an improvement in ORR by approximately 10% when combined with anti-PD-1 inhibitor15–18. Intriguingly, anti-PD-1 inhibitors seem to exert a more pronounced tumor regression effect in HER2+ GC compared to HER2− GC (ORR ~ 74% vs. ~50%).

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes are pivotal regulators of tumor growth and are key determinants of response to immunotherapy19,20. Among these, B cells contribute significantly to antitumor immunity by presenting antigens, secreting cytokines, and differentiating into antibody-producing plasma cells21–23. Studies in HER2+ breast cancer and gastric cancer have revealed an association between tumor-infiltrating B cells and improved patient survival24–26, suggesting that B cell-mediated immunity may play a key role in treatment response for HER2+ cancers. These findings imply that the tumor immune microenvironment in HER2+ GC likely engages in complex interactions with therapy, potentially influencing outcomes in response to combination treatments. Recent studies have begun to characterize the tumor microenvironment (TME) of HER2+ GC at a low resolution27,28. Nevertheless, a comprehensive understanding of the immune landscape in HER2-driven tumors is lacking. Moreover, survival analysis from the KEYNOTE-811 trial demonstrated that a survival benefit of pembrolizumab was only found in the subgroup of patients with HER2+ and PD-L1+ tumors29. The differential responses to immunotherapy observed in HER2+ gastric cancer warrant further mechanistic exploration of the biological drivers.

In this work, we prospectively gather tumor tissues of GC from both HER2+ and HER2− GC, prior to and 6 weeks following the administration of standard first-line therapy in conjunction with an anti-PD-1 inhibitor. Using single-cell sequencing, we characterize the TIME of HER2+ GC and assess the impact of trastuzumab, combined with the anti-PD-1 inhibitor sintilimab and chemotherapy, on the cellular composition. Consequently, we compare the TIME between PD-L1+ and PD-L1− within HER2+ GC to identify potential strategies for improving immunotherapy efficacy in patients with PD-L1− GC. Our findings elucidate the unique microenvironmental landscape of HER2+ GC and highlight the pivotal role of B-cell-mediated immunity. The insights gained from this study hold significant clinical promise for developing more potent therapeutic strategies and personalized approaches for HER2+ GC immunotherapy.

Results

Immune dynamics atlas in HER2+ GC and study design

To profile the TIME dynamics in HER2+ GC, we prospectively enrolled seven treatment-naïve patients as a discovery cohort, including 4 HER2+ and 3 HER2−. Data from three HER2− patients were derived from our prior research project30. EBV+ and dMMR/MSI-H GC were excluded from this study since these patients had distinct TIME30–32. To confirm that observed immunological alterations were primarily driven by HER2 status rather than other genomic alterations, we performed targeted panel sequencing on the three patients with HER2+ GC. We specifically focused on mutations known to modulate the TIME or influence progression-free survival. Critically, oncogenic mutations in the PIK3CA, ARID1A and KRAS have been reported to be associated with an inflamed microenvironment and immunotherapy response in gastric cancer33–35. In our datasets, the absence of these mutations in HER2+ GC suggests that HER2 status is the predominant driver of the observed remodeling in the TIME (Supplementary Data 1). All participants received 2 cycles of immunochemotherapy (anti-PD-1 inhibitor + XELOX/FOLFOX regimens). For HER2+ GC, anti-HER2 therapy was additionally administered. After evaluating the treatment efficacy according to RECIST v1.1 criteria, two patients with HER2− GC were considered to have a partial response (PR), and one had stable disease (SD). Of the patients with HER2+ GC, one got a clinical complete response (cCR), one got PR, one had progressive disease (PD), and one had SD after treatment (Fig. 1a). We were unable to obtain post-treatment tissue for this patient with PD since the patient declined a repeat gastroscopy. sc(RNA + TCR + BCR)-seq were performed on tumor infiltrating CD45+ immune cells sorted from paired pre- and post-treatment endoscopic biopsies (discovery cohort). In addition, bulk RNA-seq was conducted on baseline tumor tissues from 31 treatment-naïve patients with HER2+ GC and 10 patients with HER2+ GC who had received anti-PD-1 therapy. Peripheral blood samples were also analyzed from 19 patients with HER2+ GC and 10 patients with HER2− GC. For validation, we also integrated publicly available datasets, including the three scRNA-seq cohorts, three bulk RNA-seq cohorts and a bulk proteomic dataset. To further characterize the spatial distribution and functional states of tumor-infiltrating immune cells, we performed multiplexed immunohistochemistry (mIHC) and conducted complementary in vivo and in vitro experiments (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Data 1).

Following rigorous quality control, we obtained scRNA profiles from 95,248 immune cells, encompassing six major lineages: T cells, NK cells, B cells, plasma cells, myeloid cells, and mast cells (Fig. 1b). Each major cell lineage was defined by canonical markers and specific expression genes (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Data 2). A distinct separation between HER2+ and HER2− groups was observed (Level 1). Additionally, both HER2 subsets underwent cellular shifts following treatment (Level 2). These findings suggest that HER2+ tumors exhibit a unique immune profile compared to HER2−, which undergoes further changes post-treatment (Supplementary Fig. 1a). We then observed dynamic shifts in immune cell abundance across different HER2 statuses and treatment stages. Notably, B cells and myeloid cells exhibited strong associations with HER2 status: B cell infiltration was elevated in HER2+ GC and further increased with treatment (Supplementary Fig. 1b), whereas myeloid cells displayed an inverse trend (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). Additionally, T cell infiltration was consistently higher in HER2+ GC regardless of treatment phase, while plasma cells were enriched in HER2− GC (Fig. 1e). A high-resolution analysis of immune cell distributions was performed across the defined patient subgroups. The results also showed that myeloid cells were enriched in HER2− GC (macrophages, neutrophils, DCs and monocytes), while T cells (CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells and gdT) and B cells (naïve B cells and memory B cells) were enriched in HER2+ GC (Supplementary Fig. 1e). These findings suggest that HER2+ GC is characterized by enhanced lymphocyte infiltration, particularly B and T cells. Treatment-induced adaptive immune changes may contribute to immunotherapy responsiveness for HER2+ GC.

Differentiation of tumor-reactive T cells is increased in HER2+ GC

Clonal T-cell populations are pivotal mediators of anti-tumor immunity36,37. Through whole transcriptome analysis of clonal T cells in both treatment-naïve HER2+ and HER2− GC, we identified significant enrichment in HER2+ tumors for cytokines (CXCL13, CCL4 and CCL3), effector T cell markers (CD27, GZMK, GZMB and GNLY) and T cell exhaustion genes (TIGIT and LAG3). In contrast, HER2− tumor displayed upregulation of inhibitory killer cell lectin-like receptors (KLRC1 and KLRB1), associated with CD8+ T cells suppression38–40 (Fig. 2a). Pathway analysis revealed further divergence: HER2+ tumors exhibited heightened activity in lymphocyte migration, T cell activation/differentiation, cytotoxic effector functions and IFN-γ response compared to HER2− tumors (Fig. 2b). To systematically evaluate the impact of HER2 status on T cells, we established a high-resolution atlas of T-cell subsets, identifying 7 CD4+ T cells, 6 CD8+ T cells and 3 unconventional T cell populations (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). Functional annotation revealed distinct subsets, including naïve (CD4.c01 and CD8.c08), cytotoxic (CD8.c09) and two CXCL13-expressing populations (CD4.c03 and CD8.c11) (Fig. 2d). The CD4.c03 subset exhibited a T follicular helper (Tfh) phenotype with elevated IL-21 production (Supplementary Fig. 2c), while CD8.c11 displayed exhaustion markers (Fig. 2e, f). Notably, these CXCL13+ T cells showed greater clonal expansion in HER2+ GC (Supplementary Fig. 2d, e) and have been previously linked to tumor reactivity and immunotherapy response in other malignancies41–43.

Comparative analysis of T-cell subset distribution between HER2+ and HER2− tumors at baseline demonstrated that HER2− GC were enriched for quiescent populations (naïve: CD4.c01, CD8.c08; memory-like: CD4.c02, CD8.c10). In contrast, HER2+ GC harbored higher frequencies of cytotoxic (CD4.c07, CD8.c09), interferon-responsive (CD8.c12), and tumor-reactive T cells (CD4.c03, CD8.c11) (Fig. 2g, h). To delineate HER2+ GC-specific T-cell dynamics, we uncovered two CD8+ T-cell differentiation trajectories: (1) TnCCR7 → TrmZNF683 and (2) TnCCR7 → TcGZMK → TcCXCL13+PDCD1+ (Fig. 2i). The latter trajectory, predominant in HER2+ cases, correlated with increased clonality and tumor-specificity (Fig. 2j, k), suggesting HER2 status directs tumor-reactive T-cell lineage development. Supporting this, bulk RNA-seq data from the SYSUCC HER2+ GC cohort and TCGA-STAD cohort confirmed positive associations between HER2 overexpression and tumor-reactive subsets (CD4.c03.TfhCXCL13 and CD8.c11.TcCXCL13+PDCD1+) (Fig. 2l, m). Public scRNA-seq data also validated higher infiltration of tumor-reactive T cells in the HER2-high group (IHC = 2+/3+) (Supplementary Fig. 2f, g). Collectively, HER2− GC is characterized by an inactive T-cell microenvironment, whereas HER2+ tumors foster T-cell activation and clonal expansion of tumor-reactive populations. This pre-existing tumor-reactive T-cell repertoire may underlie the enhanced responsiveness to combination therapies observed in HER2+ GC.

HER2+ GCs expand tumor-reactive effector T cell repertoires by clonal expansion after combination therapy

Longitudinal dynamic analysis of clonal T cell repertoires revealed HER2+ GC maintained preferential expansion of Path 2, whereas HER2− GC predominantly exhibited Path 1-driven clonal expansion (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). To trace the origins of post-treatment T cell expansion, we developed a TCR tracking computational framework according to previous studies (applied to clones with TCR abundance > 1 per sample)36,37, categorizing clones as: (i) Pre-New (detected only pre-treatment); (ii) Post-New (treatment-induced emergent clones); (iii) Pre-Exist and Post-Exist (persistent clones). This approach precisely delineates therapy-induced T cell repertoire remodeling, revealing responding clones and their expansion sources (Supplementary Fig. 3c). In Path 2, Post-New clones expanded significantly versus baseline, suggesting peripheral T cell recruitment, while Pre-Exist and Post-Exist clones sustained robust expansion (Supplementary Fig. 3d). These findings demonstrate that HER2+ GC undergo clonal revival post-treatment, with reconstituted T cell repertoires arising from two distinct sources: maintenance of pre-existing anti-tumor clones and infiltration of new T cell populations mediating clonal replacement. Additionally, treatment reactivated Trm cells, shifting their differentiation towards Path 2 (Supplementary Fig. 3e). mIHC validation confirmed elevated abundances of CD8+ T, CD8+GZMK+ T, CD8+CXCL13+ T, and CD8+CXCL13+GZMK+ T cells in HER2+ GC post-treatment, supporting therapy-induced expansion of tumor-reactive effector T cells (Supplementary Fig. 3f, g). Collectively, the enhanced response of HER2+ GC to combination therapy stems from the multifaceted expansion of anti-tumor T-cell clones.

Combination therapy reduces the abundance of exhausted B cells in HER2+ GC

Our single-cell analysis revealed a strong association between HER2 status and B cell infiltration (Fig. 1d, e), with HER2+ GC exhibiting higher clonal B cell expansion (Supplementary Fig. 4a). These findings were validated across three bulk sequencing cohorts (Fig. 3a–c) and three scRNA-seq cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 4b). These datasets consistently demonstrate a critical role for B cells in the HER2+ microenvironment. We constructed a detailed B cell atlas, identifying 3 naïve B cell states (N = 4384), 6 memory B cell states (N = 7027) and 1 intermediate differentiation state (N = 160) (Fig. 3d). Of note, we evaluated the transcriptional profile of B cells against a validated gene set associated with B cell exhaustion44,45. Our scoring analysis revealed that Bm.c09(ITGAX) exhibits a significantly higher exhaustion score compared to all other B cell lineages, suggesting a distinct state of functional impairment (Supplementary Fig. 4c). Consistent with this exhausted profile, Bm.c09(ITGAX) specifically expressed several genes linked to B cell dysfunction: FCGR2B, an inhibitory low‑affinity FcγR that can block downstream BCR signaling46,47; FCRL4, a receptor for IgA, when co‑expressed with FCGR2B, can synergistically suppress B cell activation48,49; and ENTPD1, which promotes adenosine generation and thereby inhibits immune activation50,51 (Supplementary Fig. 4d). In contrast, TNFRSF13B+ Bm cells showed an activated mature signature characterized by CD27, TNFRSF13B, and AIM2 expression52,53 (Fig. 3e, f). We further evaluated functional activation by measuring somatic hypermutation (SHM) frequency in BCR. Relative to the activated mature Bm.c05(TNFRSF13B), Bm.c09(ITGAX) showed a significantly lower somatic hypermutation frequency (Supplementary Fig. 4e). Collectively, these results confirm that Bm.c09(ITGAX) exhibits an exhausted phenotype, aligning with previously reported immunosuppressive Bm populations44,54. To assess the impact of treatment on B cell differentiation dynamics, we identified two distinct developmental trajectories: one leading to mature activated TNFRSF13B+ Bm cells (naïve B → TNFSF9+ Bm → TNFRSF13B+ Bm) and the other toward exhausted ITGAX+ Bm cells (naïve B → TNF+ Bm → S100A10+ Bm → ITGAX+ Bm). This pattern was corroborated by Slingshot and monocle3 algorithms (Fig. 3h). BCR clonal analysis revealed that combination therapy in HER2+ GC enhanced clonal expansion toward mature activated TNFRSF13B+ Bm cells and suppressed differentiation toward exhausted ITGAX+ Bm cells (Fig. 3i). Pre-treatment, ITGAX+ Bm cells shared BCR clones with various B cells in the microenvironment, but post-treatment, this clonal sharing was lost, indicating therapy-driven suppression of exhausted B cell differentiation and promotion of mature activated memory B cell generation (Fig. 3j). A similar reduction in ITGAX+ Bm clonality sharing was observed in HER2− GC post-treatment (Supplementary Fig. 4g), suggesting treatment mediated alleviation of ITGAX+ Bm driven immunosuppression. mIHC confirmed therapy-induced B cell subset redistribution, aligning with single-cell data (Fig. 3k, l). Spatial analysis revealed exhausted ITGAX+ Bm cells proximal to HER2+ tumor cells at baseline (P = 4.51e-06), while CD27+ activated B cells migrated closer to tumor cells post-treatment (P = 4.37e-06) (Fig. 3m). Together, our data demonstrate that combination therapy reprograms memory B cells toward an activated state.

Vitamin B5 enhances the Tfh-stimulatory capacity of naïve B cells

Beyond reshaping B cell differentiation dynamics in HER2+ GC, combination therapy also induced substantial naïve B cell infiltration. Notably, this therapy-driven recruitment was specific to HER2+ GC, suggesting a unique functional role in treatment response (Fig. 3g). Bulk transcriptome analyses also validated a significant increase in naïve B cells and Tfh cells after treatment (Supplementary Fig. 5a). To assess the immune remodeling effect independent of HER2 targeted blockade, we analyzed an in-house single-cell cohort of locally advanced HER2+ GC treated with neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (chemotherapy + anti-PD-1) without HER2 targeted blockade. Both single-cell data and the bulk transcriptome revealed a significant increase in naïve B cells and Tfh cells after treatment, consistent with the findings from our primary cohort (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Cell-cell interaction analysis revealed enhanced T cell-B cell communication post-treatment (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Specifically, interactions between exhausted ITGAX+ Bm and T cells weakened, while engagement between naïve B cells and CD4+ Tfh cells and CD8+ GZMK+ Tc cells strengthened (Fig. 4c). This aligns with known mechanisms where naïve B cells, acting as antigen-presenting cells (APC), activate Tfh cells, which subsequently enhance CD8+ T cell responses via IL-21 secretion55,56. The Tfh-naïve B cell axis exhibited significant activation of multiple co-stimulatory ligand-receptor pairs (Fig. 4d). Tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) are key sites for T–B cell interactions and are closely associated with responses to immunotherapy57–59. To investigate this within our datasets, we evaluated the enrichment of two independent TLS-associated gene signatures across various B cell lineages60,61. Our analysis revealed that naïve B cells exhibited significantly higher TLS enrichment scores compared with other B cell subsets (Supplementary Fig. 5d). Consistent with this, BCL6 and CD38 were upregulated in the naïve B cell clusters. Both genes are commonly used markers of TLS-associated B cells62–65. We also observed an enrichment of CD83 in the naïve B cell clusters. Specifically, CD83 marks the light zone of germinal centers, the site of T–B interactions66,67 (Supplementary Fig. 5e). Collectively, our data demonstrate that these naïve B cells express key TLS hallmarks and possess light-zone features, supporting their capacity to engage in T cell interactions. Spatial mIHC validation also confirmed co-localization of naïve B cells, Tfh, and CD8+ GZMK+ Tc cells post-treatment (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 5f). Together, these findings illustrate how therapy-induced naïve B cells enhance T cell-mediated antitumor immunity through APC functions.

Given the critical role of naïve B cells in the therapeutic response for HER2+ GC, we explored the molecular mechanisms underlying their unique functions. Considering established links between metabolic heterogeneity and B cell functional diversification54,68, we performed unsupervised clustering of B cells using 85 metabolism-associated genes from the KEGG database. This revealed distinct metabolic states separating naïve B cells, memory B cells, and intermediate differentiation states, highlighting metabolism as a key phenotypic determinant (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). Notably, naïve B cells specifically enriched pantothenate and CoA (vitamin B5) biosynthesis pathways (P = 7.36e-09) (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Fig. 6c). Using CD4+ T cell-naïve B cells coculture systems, vitamin B5 supplementation significantly enhanced CD4+ T cell proliferation (Fig. 4g). However, vitamin B5 alone had minimal impact on CD4+ T cells in monoculture systems (Fig. 4h). Furthermore, vitamin B5 altered CD4+ T cell polarization in cocultures systems, increasing the frequencies of both IFN-γ+ and IL-21+ subsets (Fig. 4i, j). We then assessed the CD4+ T cell activation across a vitamin B5 concentration gradient. Vitamin B5 failed to induce CD4+ T cell activation in the absence of naïve B cells, regardless of the concentration used. In addition, the basal pantothenate levels in standard RPMI medium were insufficient to trigger significant CD4+ T cell responses. A marked enhancement in the generation of CD4+IFN-γ+ T cells and CD4+IL-21+ T cells was observed only when the medium was additionally supplemented with 1 mM vitamin B5 (Supplementary Fig. 6d–g). Collectively, these findings indicate that vitamin B5 promotes the differentiation of CD4+IFN-γ+ T cells and CD4+IL-21+ T cells in a concentration-dependent manner, and this effect is also dependent on the presence of naïve B cells.

We further examined the metabolic status of B vitamins across different B cell subsets using single-cell data. The vitamin B5 metabolic pathway was uniquely enriched in all three naïve B cell subsets (Supplementary Fig. 7a). When various B vitamins were added to co-cultures of CD4⁺ T cells and naïve B cells, vitamin B5 alone significantly increased the production of CD4⁺IFN-γ⁺ and CD4⁺IL-21⁺ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 7b, c), whereas other B vitamins did not induce a similar effect (Supplementary Fig. 7d–m). These results established vitamin B5 as a critical regulator of naïve B cell-mediated T cell activation.

We also examined the proliferation of CD4+IFN-γ+ and CD4+IL-21+ T cells when co-cultured with different B cell subsets. Our results showed that, regardless of whether vitamin B5 was added, memory B cells and plasma cells failed to effectively promote the proliferation of CD4+IFN-γ+ and CD4+IL-21+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 8a–d). Our results indicate that naïve B cells possess a stronger ability to activate CD4 + T cells compared to memory B cells and plasma cells. We also performed mIHC on paired HER2+ GC biopsies collected pre and post chemo-immunotherapy (Supplementary Fig. 8e). The infiltration of Tfh, naïve B cells, memory B cells, and plasma cells were quantified. The results indicated a significant increase in the proportions of Tfh cells, naïve B cells, and plasma cells within the TIME following treatment (Supplementary Fig. 8f). In addition, by analyzing the proximity of Tfh cells to each B cell subset, we found that the frequency of Tfh cells neighboring naïve B cells increased significantly post-treatment, whereas no such trend was observed for memory B cells or plasma cells (Supplementary Fig. 8g). Our results indicate that naïve B cells possess a stronger ability to activate CD4+ T cells compared to memory B cells and plasma cells.

To explore the effects of vitamin B5 on T cells and B cells, we utilized YTN16 subcutaneous tumor models in C57BL/6 mice and then treated them with vitamin B5 or IgG (n = 3 mice/group). We then investigated the effects of vitamin B5 on the TME. The YTN16 subcutaneous tumor models in C57BL/6 mice were established, which were subsequently treated with vitamin B5 or IgG to investigate their effects on the TME. CD45+ immune cells were isolated via fluorescence-activated cell sorting and subjected to single-cell RNA sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 10d). Discussion

HER2 status is a critical prognostic marker in GC7–9. Clinical studies demonstrated this subtype exhibits distinct responses to chemoimmunotherapy and target therapy13, underscoring the need to investigate its unique immunological landscape. Using pre-/post-treatment datasets and integrated TCR/BCR-seq, we comprehensively compared the TIME between HER2+ and HER2− GC, as well as their differential responses to combination therapy.

We found that HER2+ GC exhibits a higher abundance of CD4+ and CD8+ tumor-reactive T cells, characterized by elevated CXCL13 expression, a feature linked to improved responses following immunotherapy36,42,74. These results suggest that HER2+ GC elicits a stronger anti-tumor immune response than HER2− GC (Fig. 7a, b). While the activation mechanism remains unclear, previous studies indicated HER2 protein suppresses the cGAS-STING pathway, a key innate immune regulator27,75. Our scRNA-seq data further revealed pronounced myeloid cell depletion in HER2+ GC, indicating T cell responses are unlikely to be myeloid-driven. Notably, B cell infiltration was significantly higher in HER2+ GC and further increased post- treatment. Given that tumor-infiltrating B cells associate with better survival in HER2+ breast cancer24, we investigated their role in HER2+ GC. We observed an enrichment of naïve B cells in HER2+ GC. Notably, we identified vitamin B5 as a key metabolite supporting naïve B cell-mediated T cell activation. Vitamin B5 supplementation enhances naïve B cell-mediated activation of both Tfh and CD4+IFN-γ+ T cells, which in turn stimulated CD8+ T cells via IL-21 and IFN-γ56,76–78. Previous studies have identified a complex relationship between vitamin B5 and immune responses. Mechanistically, vitamin B5 promotes the differentiation of Tc22 cells by upregulating oxidative phosphorylation within CD8+ T cells. This metabolic shift significantly bolsters anti-tumor immune activity79. Beyond direct T-cell modulation, vitamin B5 facilitates immunogenic cell death by suppressing the expression of GOLPH3L. This process intensifies T-cell-mediated anti-tumor effect80. In addition, the pathogenic activity of mouse Th17 cells depends on increased GLUT3-mediated glycolysis, coupled with mitochondrial oxidation of pyruvate to citrate. This process supports ACLY-driven acetyl-CoA production and subsequent histone acetylation81. These studies suggest that vitamin B5 can positively regulate inflammatory responses in tissues. However, its mechanism in B cell antigen presentation remains unclear, warranting further investigation.

Survival analysis of the KEYNOTE-811 trial identified PD-L1 expression as a critical stratifying factor for immunotherapy response in HER2+ GC12. Based on the importance of naïve B cells as APCs, we detected naïve B cells and vitamin B5 deficiency in HER2+PDL1− GC, prompting us to propose vitamin B5 supplementation to sensitize these tumors to immunotherapy. This rationale rests on two considerations: 1) firstly, the interaction between B cells and Tfh cells involves mutual activation. Tfh cells receive antigen signals from B cells, while B cells obtain co-stimulatory signals that promote their survival82,83. In our study, we demonstrated that vitamin B5 supplementation can enhance the ability of naïve B cells to support Tfh cell activation. 2) Tfh cells exhibit substantial PD-1 expression, and anti-PD-1 therapy enhances their activation84,85. Combining vitamin B5 with an anti-PD-1 inhibitor thus dual-activates Tfh cells: via enhanced antigen presentation and checkpoint blockade. Our experimental results from murine models confirmed this combination elevates naïve B cell and Tfh levels in HER2+PDL1− GC, amplifying anti-tumor immunity. The low toxicity and wide availability of vitamin B5 make it an ideal candidate for therapy (Fig. 6c).

The role of Vitamin B5 in the TIME appears to be complex, exhibiting a context-dependent impact across various tumor models. While our findings underscore vitamin B5 as a metabolic engine for naïve B cells, existing literature suggests a dual nature of this metabolite. For instance, in certain c-MYC-driven tumor models, tumor cells upregulate the vitamin B5 transporter SLC5A6 to outcompete immune cells for vitamin B5 uptake, thereby fueling accelerated tumor progression86. Furthermore, Vitamin B5 metabolism may also promote the differentiation of immunomodulatory cells, such as regulatory T cells and Th2 cells87. Such complexity highlights the necessity of carefully considering the metabolic competition between malignant cells and other cellular compartments within the TME when implementing Vitamin B5-based interventions.

In addition, we identified two divergent B cell trajectories in tumors based on BCR clonotype sharing and pseudotime modeling: 1) from naïve B cells to TNFRSF13B+ Bm cells; 2) from naïve B cells to ITGAX+ Bm cells. These distinct trajectories gave rise to functionally heterogeneous B cell subsets. Trajectory 2, enriched for ITGAX+ Bm cells, potently suppressed T cell activation via adenosine generation88, consistent with pan-cancer B cell studies54. Importantly, combination therapy in HER2+ GC significantly reduced the fraction of ITGAX+ Bm cells, revealing the effect of treatment on modulating B cell differentiation.

Despite the shared prevalence of HER2 overexpression in multiple solid tumors, like breast cancer and lung cancer, the immune alterations vary significantly between different cancer types. In HER2+ breast cancer, clinical evidence demonstrated that a robust baseline presence of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes is a strong predictor of achieving a pathologic complete response (pCR) following standard-of-care treatments, especially the proportions of B cells and CD4+ T cells at baseline, which correspond precisely to the cell categories of naïve B cells and Tfh89. Emerging data suggest that B-cell-related subpopulations serve as independent and superior biomarkers for predicting pCR in HER2+ breast cancer24,25. However, a randomized phase III IMpassion050 trial demonstrated that the addition of immunotherapy did not yield significant clinical benefits for patients with HER2+ breast cancer90. Recent mechanistic insights provide a metabolic explanation for this resistance. Specifically, CNS-enriched N-acetyltransferase 8-like (NAT8L) and its metabolite N-acetylaspartate (NAA) are overexpressed in resistant tumors. NAT8L impairs anti-tumor immunity by inhibiting cytotoxicity of natural killer cells and CD8+ T cells via NAA91. In HER2+ lung cancer, unlike breast and gastric cancer, HER2+ lung cancer is mostly characterized by mutations rather than amplifications92,93. Recent studies have shown that HER2 amplification has an inflamed immune microenvironment compared to HER2 mutation, indicating that HER2+ lung cancer has complex characteristics94,95. Overall, these studies suggest that the microenvironment and HER2 status (mutation or amplification) vary greatly among different HER2+ tumors. These mechanisms warrant further investigation within the field of gastric cancer.

In summary, our study delineates the immune microenvironment of both HER2+ and HER2− GC and their dynamic change under therapeutic perturbation. HER2+ GC-enriched B cells and tumor-reactive T cells, and combination therapy skews lymphocyte differentiation toward effector T cells and mature activation B cells. Post-treatment, naïve B cells act as APCs to activate both Tfh and CD4+IFN-γ+ T cells, which then bolster effector CD8+ T cell responses via IL-21 and IFN-γ secretion. Notably, vitamin B5 potentiates the ability of naïve B cells to activate CD4+ T cells, suggesting its supplementation could enhance immunotherapy efficacy in HER2+PD-L1− GC. Further prospective clinical trials are necessary to verify the effects of vitamin B5 in HER2+PD-L1− GC.

A limitation of this study is the restricted availability of paired pre- and post-treatment tumor specimens (n = 6), due to the relatively low incidence of HER2+ GC and the voluntary collection of biopsies in advanced-stage disease. To address this, we implemented multi-region biopsy sampling and enriched CD45+ immune cells using flow cytometry, enabling comprehensive analysis of the TIME. Furthermore, our findings were supported by validation in public datasets and in vivo models. Future larger prospective studies will be essential to assess whether vitamin B5 metabolism influences progression-free survival and OS in HER2+PD-L1− GC within the combination setting and to further clarify its role in naïve B cell function.

Methods

Patients and sample collection

This study was conducted in compliance with all applicable ethical regulations and approved by the Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University (B2020-335-01). Written informed consent has been obtained from all participants. Primary tumor tissue biopsies were collected by endoscopy at baseline (<2 weeks prior to treatment) and at 6 weeks post-treatment initiation. The anti-PD-1 used is Sintilimab, and the anti-HER2 used is Trastuzumab. In one HER2+ GC, only the pre-treatment sample was available due to a loss of visit, and the remaining 6 patients had paired samples. The study population included 5 male and 2 female patients. Sex was determined based on clinical records. The detailed patient information is provided in Supplementary Data 1.

Sample processing

Single-cell RNA-sequencing, TCR-sequencing and BCR-sequencing were performed on both human tumor biopsies and mouse tumor tissues. For human samples, tumor CD45+ cells were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). The fresh tumor samples were dissociated by a gentleMACS™ octo dissociator with heaters (Miltenyi Biotec) using a human tumor dissociation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat#130-095-929) for 40 min. For mouse samples, YTN16 hHER2-OE subcutaneous tumors from two treatment groups (IgG and vitamin B5) were collected and dissociated using a mouse tumor dissociation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat#130-096-730). Tumor CD45+ cells were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting FACS. The dissociated cells were subsequently passed through a 100-μm strainer and centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min. Following removal of the supernatant, the collected cells were resuspended with red blood cell lysis (Biolegend, Cat#420301) and incubated 4–5 min. After washing twice, the cell pellets were resuspended in MACS buffer (Miltenyi, Cat#130-091-221). The concentration of single-cell suspensions was adjusted to 5–10 × 106/mL. A total of 12,000–20,000 cells were utilized for 10X Chromium Single-cell sequencing according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. All subsequent procedures were conducted following standard protocols provided by the manufacturer. The purified libraries were sequenced using an Illumina HiSeq X Ten sequencer, generating 150-base pair (bp) paired-end reads.

Library preparation and sequencing for Bulk RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), followed by quality assessment with a NanoDropTM One/OneC spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 4200 TapeStation system (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). For library preparation,1 μg of total RNA per sample was processed using the Vazyme VAHTS Total RNA-seq (H/M/R) Library Prep Kit for Illumina was used to prepare RNA sequencing libraries. In brief, RNA underwent rRNA depletion and fragmentation, synthesis of double-strand cDNA, dA-Tailing, adapter ligation, purification and size selection of adapter-ligated DNA and library amplification. The concentration of the constructed library was measured by Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The library molar concentration was measured by QuantStudio 3 qPCR. Agilent 4200 (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used to examine the distribution of segments in the library. RNA-seq was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform (San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’ s instructions.

Animal experiments

Given the current lack of well-established immunocompetent HER2+ gastric cancer mouse models, we employed two murine cell lines kindly provided by Prof. Masami Yamamoto, Prof. Tetsuya Tsukamoto and Prof. Ruihua Xu: YTN16 (murine gastric cancer) and CT26 (murine colorectal carcinoma). YTN16 served as the primary model given its gastric origin, while microsatellite-stable (MSS) CT26, which exhibits low responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors, was used as a complementary system to evaluate combination immunotherapies based on previous literature96–98. Both cell lines were engineered to overexpress human HER2 (hHER2-OE) via lentiviral transduction. Briefly, HER2-expressing lentivirus was packaged using pLenti-CMV-Homo-ERBB2-GFP-Puro vector (MiaoLingBio, China, see Supplementary Data 5 for sequencing validation), with the empty vector serving as a control. Transfection was performed following the manufacturer’s protocol using polybrene (8 μg/mL) as a transduction enhancer.

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center and conducted in accordance with NIH guidelines. Given that this study focuses on complex immune interactions and the TME in HER2+ gastric cancer, only female mice were used to avoid aggression-related stress in group-housed males, which can confound immunological readouts. Female C57BL/6 mice and female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old, GemPharmatech Co., Ltd) were housed under SPF conditions with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, at 22 ± 2 °C and 50 ± 10% relative humidity. Food and water were provided ad libitum. After randomization, mice from different treatment groups were housed separately. Individual mice were identified by ear punch codes. Mice received subcutaneous inoculation of 5 × 106 YTN16 hHER2-OE cells and 1 × 106 CT26 hHER2-OE cells in the right flank. When tumor volume reached ~100 mm3, mice were randomized into: PBS control, Anti-PD-1 monotherapy (5 mg/kg, i.p., once/week for YTN16 model and twice/week for CT26 model), Pantothenic acid monotherapy (100 mg/kg, i.p., daily for YTN16 model and 5 mg/per, i.p., daily for CT26 model), Combination therapy. Tumor dimensions were measured every 3 days using digital calipers, with volume calculated as (length × width2)/2. The IACUC stipulated that tumor burden must not exceed 10% of the animal’s body weight or a mean tumor diameter of 20 mm, neither limit was exceeded during the study. Excised tumors were immediately preserved in MACS Tissue Storage Solution (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat#130-100-08) on ice for same-day flow cytometry analysis. Detailed antibody information is provided in Supplementary Data 3.

Western blots

Total cellular protein in cell lysates and proteins in the prestained Protein Ladder (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were respectively separated by 10% SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and then transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Cat#ST025) in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 for 1 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated with different primary antibodies. For details, see Supplementary Data 3. Next, the membranes were washed and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies. The proteins were visualized using electrochemiluminescence (ECL) western blotting reagent (NcmECL Ultra, Cat#P10060). The uncropped and unprocessed scans of blots are presented in Source Data.

Preparation of single-cell suspensions from tumors

For isolation of Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, freshly excised murine tumor tissues were immediately minced in ice-cold PBS containing 1% FBS, followed by enzymatic digestion using a tumor dissociation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat#130-096-730) with the gentleMACS Octo Dissociator (Program 37C_TDK_1) at 37 °C for 60 min. Splenic tissue was processed by mechanical grinding. Cell suspensions were filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer (BD Falcon, Cat#352350), treated with ACK lysing buffer (eBioscience, Cat#00-4333-57) for 5 min to remove erythrocytes, washed twice, and finally resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS with 1% FBS). All procedures were performed under strict 4 °C conditions. Single-cell suspensions were adjusted to 1 × 107 cells/mL for subsequent flow cytometric analysis.

Isolation of mononuclear cells from peripheral blood

Peripheral blood samples were collected from 10 healthy donors. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated via Ficoll-Hypaque density gradient centrifugation, washed twice with PBS containing 2 mM EDTA, and resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Cat#A5256701). Naïve B cells (CD19+IgD+CD27−; Miltenyi Biotec, #130-091-150) and CD4+ T cells (CD3+CD4+; Miltenyi Biotec, Cat#130-045-101) were subsequently purified from the PBMCs using a MACS magnetic sorting system (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat#130-042-401) under strict sterile conditions. All isolated cell populations were immediately used for subsequent co-culture experiments.

T cell proliferation assays

Following magnetic bead isolation, CD4⁺ T cells were labeled with carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE; Becton Dickinson, Cat#565082) to track proliferation. Briefly, purified CD4⁺ T cells were washed thoroughly with PBS to remove serum proteins and resuspended at 1–3 × 107 cells/mL in pre-warmed PBS. Cells were incubated with 2.5 μM CFSE for 10–15 min at 37 °C with gentle agitation to maintain suspension, followed by quenching with 9 volumes of ice-cold PBS containing 10% FBS. After two additional washes in complete RPMI-1640 medium, cells were ready for downstream applications, with CFSE fluorescence detectable in the FITC channel.

In vitro co-culture system

To investigate the immunomodulatory effects of pantothenate, we established co-culture systems using CD4⁺ T cells and naïve B cells from both human healthy donors and mouse spleens. For human experiments, PBMCs were isolated from healthy donors, and CD4⁺ T cells and naïve B cells were purified by magnetic-activated cell sorting. For mouse experiments, naïve CD4⁺ T cells and naïve B cells were isolated from the spleens of C57BL/6 mice using FACS. In both systems, CFSE-labeled CD4⁺ T cells and naïve B cells were co-cultured at a 1:1 ratio (1 × 105 cells each) in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol. Cells were plated in 96-well round-bottom plates (Corning, Cat#3799) and stimulated with 5 μg/mL anti-human CD3/CD28 antibodies (eBioscience, Cat#11131D), or with mouse CD3/CD28 activation magnetic beads (PinpoRNA, Cat#AM2001)). After 24 h, cultures were maintained in IL-2-containing medium (100 IU/mL; PeproTech, Cat#AF-200-02 for human, or Cat#212-12 for mouse) for 3–5 days, with pantothenate added to treatment groups. For healthy donors, four experimental conditions were included: activated CD4⁺ T cells alone, pantothenate-treated activated CD4⁺ T cells, co-culture system and a pantothenate-treated co-culture system. Complete antibody information is provided in Supplementary Data 3.

Flow cytometric analysis

T and B cells isolated from murine tumor tissues, splenic samples, and in vitro culture systems were subjected to comprehensive immunophenotyping. Peripheral blood leukocytes, tissue-infiltrating leukocytes, and cultured T cells were first stimulated for 5 h at 37 °C with Cell Activation Cocktail containing Brefeldin A (eBioscience, Cat#00-4975-03). Following stimulation, cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated surface markers for 30 min at 4 °C, then fixed and permeabilized using BD Cytofix/Cytoperm solution (Cat#554714) for intracellular marker detection. Flow cytometric analysis was conducted using a CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer (Beckman Colter) located at the Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center. Acquired data were analyzed with FlowJo software (v.10.8.1; BD Biosciences, Supplementary Fig. 11). Complete antibody information, including clone numbers, fluorophore conjugates, and commercial sources, is provided in Supplementary Data 3.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Serum vitamin B5 concentrations were quantified in the following serum samples using species-specific ELISA kits (human: MEIMIAN Cat#MM-50588H1; murine: MEIMIAN Cat#MM-47560M1) according to the manufacturer’s protocols: (1) peripheral blood samples from HER2(+/−) and PD-L1(+/−) GC, and (2) mouse serum collected in sterile endotoxin-free tubes following natural coagulation and centrifugation. All assays included duplicate measurements with appropriate quality controls, including standard curves and internal controls for each plate.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry

Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) staining was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor sections from 6 patients with HER2+ GC (pre- and post-targeted therapy + chemotherapy + immunotherapy) using the Opal 7-color kit (Cat# 102C4100100, Panovue, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, slides were deparaffinized and subjected to antigen retrieval in pH 6 or pH 9 epitope retrieval buffer at 95 °C for 15 min. After 30 min of blocking, primary antibodies were applied and incubated at room temperature for 60 min or overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, Ms & Rb polymer-HRP was applied for 30 min, followed by a 15-min incubation with fluorophore-conjugated tyramide to generate target-specific signals. Primary HRP complexes were stripped with epitope retrieval buffer (95 °C, 15 min) before proceeding to the next target. After completing all staining cycles, slides were mounted with DAPI-containing antifade medium and coverslipped. Primary antibodies were sourced from Abcam (Waltham, MA, USA), Cell Signaling Technology (CST; Danvers, MA, USA), or Abcepta (Suzhou, China). Detailed antibody information is provided in Supplementary Data 3. Selected mIHC-stained regions were imaged using the Vectra Polaris imaging system (AKOYA Biosciences), followed by spectral unmixing and cellular segmentation via the CytoNuclear algorithm v2.0 in HALO AI (Indica Labs). To investigate intercellular interaction dynamics, we employed Nearest Neighbor Analysis and Infiltration Analysis within HALO AI to quantify spatial distance relationships between distinct cellular phenotypes defined by marker combinations.

Single-cell RNA-seq data processing

Cleaned sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) and quantified using Cellranger software (version 3.0, 10X Genomics Inc.). Three output matrices, namely, the barcodes, number of detected genes, and count matrices, were used for downstream analysis by the Seurat package (version 4.0.4)99 in R (version 4.1.0). Cells with less than 800 features, >10% mitochondrial UMI counts, or genes detected in <3 cells were filtered out. DoubletFinder (version 2.0.3)100 was used to remove potential doublets. The expected doublet rate of each sequencing library was set according to the respective cell numbers. After quality control, we normalized the expression matrix based on UMI counts with the LogNormalize function, with the scale factor set to 10000.

Dimension reduction and unsupervised clustering

Single-cell data underwent dimension reduction and unsupervised clustering via the standard Seurat workflow. We selected the top 2000 variable genes using “vst”, then scaled the data with the “ScaleData” function. To reduce noise in the dataset, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed with 30 components through the “RunPCA” function. Batch effects were eliminated using the Harmony algorithm based on patient-level information with default parameters. For visualization, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was applied to further reduce dimensionality using the “RunUMAP” function with default settings. A shared nearest neighbor (SNN) graph was constructed based on expression profiles, and unsupervised graph-based clustering was performed using the original Louvain method. Initial clustering was done with a resolution parameter of 0.7. The identification of signature genes for each cluster was performed using the “FindAllMarkers” function, and the p-value was calculated using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. We characterized major cell lineages with high expression of CD2 and CD3D (T cells), FGFBP2 and FCGR3A (NK cells), MS4A1 and CD19 (B cells), SDC1 and TNFRSF17 (plasma cells), TPSAB1 and CPA3 (mast cells), and CD14 and S100A8 (myeloid cells). Notably, we removed EpCAM+ cells and COL1A1+ cells, which were epithelial cells and fibroblast cells, to ensure that we captured immune cells as the cell populations. To further characterize the subsets of major lineages, a second round of dimension reduction and unsupervised clustering was performed following the same strategy.

Cell heterogeneity and subset identification

T cells within a cluster that was distinguished by elevated expression of CD4 or CD8 were defined as CD4+ T cells or CD8+ T cells. To characterize the diversity of unconventional T cells, we defined subsets based on lineage-specific transcriptomic markers. Specifically, gdT cells were identified by their expression of gamma and delta chains, while Mucosal-Associated Invariant T cells (MAIT) were delineated via TRBV6-1. Proliferating T cells (Tpro) were further distinguished by the co-expression of the mitotic markers STMN1 and MKI67. Within the conventional T cell compartment, we utilized the expression of CCR7 and TCF7 in conjunction with CD4 or CD8 to isolate naive T populations (Tn). Among the CD4+ lineage, IL7R cells were categorized as memory T cells (Tm), whereas specialized helper subsets were identified by distinct functional signatures: IL21, CXCL13, and PDCD1 for follicular helper T cells (Tfh); IL17A for Th17 cells; and CX3CR1 for terminally differentiated effector memory cells (Temra). Furthermore, the T regulatory (Treg) lineage was defined by FOXP3 expression and stratified into CCL20+ or TNFRSF9+ subpopulations to reflect distinct activation states. Classification of the CD8+ compartment focused on cytotoxic and exhausted phenotypes. Effector CD8+ T cells (Tc) were identified by GZMK expression. A distinct subpopulation characterized by the co-expression of cytotoxic molecules alongside exhaustion and recruitment markers, specifically PDCD1 and CXCL13, was identified as CD8+CXCL13+PDCD1+ Tc. Tissue-resident memory populations (Trm) were characterized by the residency-associated transcription factor ZNF683. Finally, we identified two specialized clusters: an interferon-stimulated (ISG + ) population marked by ISG15, and a natural killer T NKT population defined by the expression of killer-cell immunoglobulin-like receptors.

We characterized B cell subpopulations based on a hierarchical expression of lineage-defining markers and functional states. Naïve B cells (Bn) were identified by the co-expression of IGHD. Within this compartment, we resolved distinct subsets: TCL1A+ Bn, characterized by TCL1A enrichment; IL4R+ Bn, characterized by IL4R enrichment, and GBP4+ Bn, defined by the interferon-stimulated gene GBP4. To capture the transition toward a memory phenotype, we utilized EBI3 to identify an intermediate state bridging naïve and memory populations. The memory B cell (Bm) compartment was primarily defined by CD27 expression. We further used TNFRSF13B to identify mature activated Bm, TNFSF9 to identify TNFSF9+ Bm, S100A10 to identify S100A10+ Bm, TNF to identify TNF+ Bm, ITGAX and ENTPD1 to identify exhausted Bm, and the interferon-stimulated gene IFIT1 to identify IFIT1+ Bm. For the plasma cell compartment, we utilized the expression of IGHA and IGHG to distinguish between IgA+ and IgG+ plasma cells, respectively.

Myeloid cell populations within the TME are characterized by distinct surface markers and transcriptional profiles. Monocyte subsets are primarily distinguished by the differential expression of CD14 and CD16. Within the dendritic cell (DC) compartment, lineage-specific markers facilitate precise identification: CLEC9A defines the cDC1 lineage, while CD1C serves as a hallmark for cDC2. Specialized DC states are further delineated by LILRA4 for plasmacytoid DC (pDC) and the co-expression of LAMP3 and CD274 for regulatory or migratory DC phenotypes. In addition, neutrophils were identified by CXCR2 expression. A diverse macrophage population defined by the canonical markers CD68 and CD163. Beyond these general identifiers, macrophages exhibit significant functional heterogeneity. Specialized subclusters were classified based on the enriched expression of CXCL3, THBS1, TREM2, VEGFA, and ISG15, reflecting their varied roles in inflammation, angiogenesis, lipid metabolism, and interferon signaling.

Single-cell TCR/BCR data analysis

The Cellranger software (version 3.0, 10X Genomics Inc.) provided by the 10X Genomics platform was utilized to align the data to the human or mouse VDJ reference genome (GRCh38 or GRCm38). To minimize noise, only assembled chains that met the criteria of being highly confident, full-length, and having a valid barcode were aligned and retained.

In TCR analysis, cells with > 1 TCR count were classified as clonal T cells. The BCR analysis followed a similar approach. For reliable and robust categorization of T cells cloning dynamics, we identified 3 clonal modes of T cells. The vanishment mode refers to the presence of clonal T cells only in the pre-treatment stage. The emergence mode denotes the presence of clonal T cells solely in the post-treatment stage. The persistence mode indicates the continuous presence of clonal T cells throughout the treatment period.

For the assembled IgH sequence of each B cell, the C, V, D, J gene and CDR3 sequence were next annotated from the Change-O toolkit (version 1.3.4), with the alignment performed by IgBLAST (version 1.22.0). The SHM level of each B cell was quantified as the point mutation rate in the IGHV sequence using the shazam (version 1.3.0) package.

Pathway enrichment and functional annotation

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was performed using ClusterProfiler (version 4.3.0)102, with differentially expressed genes (adjusted p-value < 0.05) identified via the “FindAllMarkers” function as input. We subsequently utilized the “AddModuleScore” function from the Seurat package (version 4.0.4) to calculate the gene signature scores associated with T cells and B cells (Supplementary Data 4).

Cell-cell interaction analysis

We assessed the interaction between naïve B cells and Tfh using CellPhoneDB (version 5.0.0)103 to infer potential communications among known ligand‒receptor (L-R) pairs based on single-cell expression profiles. We randomly sampled 1000 cells from each cellular state for analysis and conducted 1000 iterations.

Cell trajectory analysis

We utilized Monocle3 (version 1.0.0)104 and Slingshot (version 2.6.0)105 algorithms to evaluate the developmental trajectories of T cells and B cells. The “reduce_dimension” function in Monocle3 was utilized as a standalone tool for unsupervised clustering. We utilized the “learn_graph” function to construct cell trajectories, followed by visualization of trajectories and pseudotime using “plot_cells”. Immune checkpoint expression patterns across pseudotime were analyzed with the “plot_genes_in_pseudotime” function. All parameters were left at their default values.

Statistics and reproducibility

GraphPad Prism version 8.0.2, as well as R version 4.1.0, were used for statistical analysis. The comparison of independent groups was performed using a two-sided Student’s t-test or two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Kruskal-Wallis test were used for more than three conditions of a single factor. The Spearman correlation coefficient was used for the correlation data. The P-values are computed using algorithm AS 89106. No statistical method was used to predetermine the sample size. For animal experiments, mice were randomized to treatment groups. All flow cytometry and YTN16 hHER2-OE in vivo experiments were performed independently three times with similar results, while CT26 hHER2-OE in vivo studies and Western blot assays were conducted in two independent experiments. IHC and clinical mIHC data were validated across multiple patient samples to ensure reproducibility. No other data was excluded from the analysis.
