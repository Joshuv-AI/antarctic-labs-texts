Nursing students’ experiences with VR-based simulation in caring for patients with heart failure: a qualitative study
Authors: Soomin An, Youn-Joo Um
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart failure is a complex and dynamic clinical condition that requires continuous assessment, timely intervention, and re-evaluation in response to fluctuating patient status. Opportunities for nursing students to actively engage in such high-risk and rapidly changing situations during clinical practicum are often limited. Virtual reality (VR) simulation offers an immersive and interactive learning environment in which students can experience symptom progression and make nursing decisions within a safe yet realistic context. Given these characteristics, VR simulation is particularly well suited for heart failure nursing education.MethodsThis qualitative study employed Colaizzi’s phenomenological method to explore the lived experiences of nursing students. The final analysis included reflection journals from 34 fourth-year nursing students. Data were collected through reflection journals written immediately after the instructor-led debriefing to capture immediate experiences and perceived meanings. Two qualitative researchers independently analyzed the data and reached consensus on themes following Colaizzi’s procedure. The study adhered to COREQ guidelines.ResultsFive theme clusters emerged: (1) transitioning from technical practice to clinical presence, (2) clinical reasoning as situated action within a shared virtual space, (3) communicating within the reality of virtual accountability, (4) reorienting nursing through relational presence, and (5) navigating the boundaries of virtual and physical reality.ConclusionsVR-based simulation provided nursing students with multidimensional learning experiences extending beyond technical skill acquisition to include clinical reasoning, professional responsibility, communication, and patient-centered care. This study contributes qualitative evidence that elucidates how VR-based simulation supports integrated clinical reasoning and professional identity formation in heart failure nursing education, supporting its role as a complementary component within blended nursing education approaches.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04773-y.Keywords: Virtual reality, Nursing education, Heart failure, Simulation-based learning, Clinical reasoning, Qualitative research

Introduction

Heart failure is a major global health problem affecting approximately 26 million adults worldwide, and its prevalence continues to increase across most regions [1]. This increase is multifactorial, reflecting population aging, improved survival following cardiovascular advances, and the growing complexity of comorbid disease management [2].

In response to this growing burden, nurses play an integrative role within the multidisciplinary healthcare team, frequently serving as the primary point of continuous patient monitoring and education [3]. While patient safety in heart failure care is a systemic outcome involving medication regimens and interprofessional coordination, enhancing the clinical competence of nursing students through high-quality education may contribute to reducing preventable errors within these complex systems [4]. As large numbers of newly graduated nurses enter the workforce each year, nursing education faces the ongoing challenge of equipping students with the knowledge, skills, and clinical judgment required to provide safe and competent care.

However, traditional lecture-based teaching approaches often have limitations in facilitating the transfer of theoretical knowledge to complex clinical practice. Nursing students also have limited opportunities to directly experience high-risk clinical situations related to heart failure care, and even when such opportunities arise, their involvement in patient care is often restricted due to patient safety concerns [5]. Given that patient deterioration and emergencies may occur during heart failure management, it is essential for nursing students to develop realistic clinical response capabilities and the ability to make timely and appropriate nursing decisions in acute situations [6].

To address these educational challenges, innovative teaching strategies that provide realistic yet safe learning environments are required. VR-based simulation has emerged as a promising educational technology that allows nursing students to engage in immersive clinical experiences beyond the limitations of traditional education and training [7]. VR technology provides a platform in which students can learn and apply clinical knowledge in a controlled and risk-free environment [8]. Previous studies have reported that VR-based learning increases students’ interest in nursing, enhances interaction with simulated patients, and contributes to improvements in educational quality [9].

VR is broadly defined as a computer-generated immersive environment that enables users to interact with three-dimensional virtual objects and scenarios, supporting presence and engagement [10, 11]. From a theoretical perspective, VR-based simulation aligns closely with Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory, which conceptualizes learning as a continuous process grounded in experience [12]. VR-based simulation may facilitate the experiential learning cycle by providing concrete experiences, supporting reflection, and allowing active experimentation in repeated simulated practice [13].

Previous research has demonstrated that VR-based simulation is effective in improving cardiopulmonary knowledge, spatial understanding of cardiac anatomy, and clinical performance in cardiovascular care [14, 15]. Compared with traditional two-dimensional learning methods, VR-based education has been shown to enhance training effectiveness and improve monitoring and assessment of cardiac and pulmonary functions [16]. Moreover, immersive VR has been associated with fewer errors and improved learning efficiency in procedural training contexts [17].

Despite the growing incorporation of VR into nursing education programs [8, 18], VR-based learning remains a novel experience for many nursing students. Existing studies exploring nursing students’ experiences with VR have primarily focused on specific clinical contexts, such as airway management [19] or technical nursing skills education [20]. Furthermore, systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggest that VR is currently more effective as a complementary educational tool rather than a standalone teaching approach, highlighting the need for further investigation [21]. Although many studies have evaluated learning outcomes associated with VR-based education, few have explored nursing students’ experiential learning processes related to caring for patients with heart failure.

Accordingly, this study aimed to explore nursing students’ experiences of participating in a VR-based nursing education program for patients with heart failure. The guiding research question was: How do nursing students with no prior experience using VR perceive and experience VR-based simulation learning for nursing care of patients with heart failure?

Methods

Research design

This study employed a qualitative research design using Colaizzi’s phenomenological method to gain an in-depth understanding of nursing students’ experiences of participating in VR-based simulation education for nursing care of patients with heart failure [22]. The study was conducted at a university in South Korea between October 4 and December 16, 2024. Reporting of this study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines (Supplementary Materials 1) [23].

Participants

Participants were fourth-year nursing students who completed a VR-based simulation program focused on nursing care for patients with heart failure. Convenience sampling was used to recruit students who were available and willing to participate after completion of the program. A total of 64 students participated in the simulation, and 60 voluntarily agreed to participate in the study and provided written informed consent.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) first-time participation in a VR-based heart failure simulation class, (2) voluntary agreement to participate, and (3) completion of the VR-based simulation program. The exclusion criterion was non-completion of the program or failure of the final evaluation because the study aimed to explore experiences following achievement of the minimum learning objectives. However, no participants were excluded on this basis.

VR simulation, reflection journal writing, and instructor-led debriefing were implemented as part of routine educational practices within the senior nursing curriculum. Participation in these educational activities was required as part of the course, whereas participation in the research component was entirely voluntary. Students were assured that their decision regarding research participation would not affect their grades or course evaluation.

All students completed a reflection journal immediately after the heart failure VR scenario session and instructor-led debriefing as part of the class activities. After completion of the educational session, students were informed about the study and invited to provide voluntary consent for the research use of their reflection journals. A total of 60 students agreed to participate and provided written informed consent.

Reflection journals from consenting participants (n = 60) were randomly ordered and analyzed iteratively. Data saturation was reached at journal 32, and two additional journals were analyzed to confirm saturation, resulting in a final analytic sample of 34 journals. Data collection

Data were collected between October 4 and December 16, 2024. All students completed the reflection journal immediately after the heart failure VR scenario and instructor-led debriefing as part of the routine simulation class activities. Reflection journals served as the primary data source to explore the essence of nursing students’ experiences in a VR-based heart failure simulation. Although semi-structured interviews or focus groups are commonly used in phenomenological research, reflection journals were selected to capture participants’ lived experiences with greater immediacy while minimizing researcher influence. Unlike interviews, where a researcher’s probing questions might inadvertently direct or limit a participant’s narrative, reflection journals allow students to structure and articulate their experiences in their own words, thereby reducing potential interviewer influence during data generation [24]. This approach is particularly advantageous in VR research, as it enables the immediate recording of psychological presence and clinical reasoning processes before they are distorted by retrospective recall or post-hoc rationalization [25]. Furthermore, narratives generated without interviewer prompting allow participants to express their experiences in their own terms. This approach aligns with phenomenological principles by reducing researcher pre-structuring during data generation [26]. Consequently, the use of reflection journals served as a valid and rigorous tool to identify the essential structures of the phenomenon without external distortion. Although follow-up interviews may provide additional depth, the immediacy and self-directed nature of reflection journals were considered appropriate for capturing participants’ first-person experiential descriptions immediately after immersion in the VR scenario. Participants completed a brief demographic questionnaire and were provided with written instructions on how to complete the reflection journal.

The reflection journal guide used in this study was specifically developed by the researchers for this project. The full English version of the guide is provided as a supplementary file (Supplementary Materials 1). The questions were as follows:

What did you learn when you first encountered the VR simulation for patients with heart failure?

What did you learn when you first encountered the VR simulation for patients with heart failure?

What aspects of the VR simulation for heart failure nursing did you find most beneficial?

What aspects of the VR simulation for heart failure nursing did you find most beneficial?

What aspects of the VR simulation were challenging or disappointing?

What aspects of the VR simulation were challenging or disappointing?

These questions were intended to elicit in-depth reflections on participants’ lived experiences of VR-based simulation in heart failure nursing. Reflection journals were written once, immediately after the debriefing session following the VR simulation training. Participants were provided with a quiet classroom environment and asked to freely describe their experiences on approximately one page of A4 paper. The average time required to complete the reflection journal was approximately 30 min (range: 15–50 min). Reflection journals were collected immediately after completion and anonymized prior to analysis. Reflection journals were selected as the primary data source because they allow participants to express their thoughts, feelings, and attitudes with minimal researcher intervention and facilitate reflective learning [27].

VR-based simulation program

The VR-based simulation program was designed to enhance nursing students’ competencies in caring for patients with heart failure. Each class consisted of approximately 20 students divided into teams of four to five. Each team was provided with one wireless VR headset and a monitoring system, and classroom desks were arranged to facilitate discussion and teamwork.

The simulation was conducted using a commercially available medical education platform (Medicrew VR; Medibase Co., Ltd., Seoul, South Korea) delivered through a fully immersive head-mounted display (Meta Quest 2; Meta Platforms, Inc., Menlo Park, CA, USA). The system provided an interactive, scenario-based 3D clinical environment in which learners selected and performed nursing interventions for patients with heart failure—such as patient assessment, oxygen therapy, and monitoring—from menu-based options while following a predefined procedural sequence according to the clinical scenario design.

Students participated in the simulation using a team-rotation format. In each round, one student actively performed the VR scenario while the remaining team members observed the performance via real-time screen mirroring on a 2D monitor (live viewing rather than a recorded video). Observers followed patient data, anticipated interventions, and discussed clinical decisions.

Each student individually performed the patient-care scenario three times using three modes: basic, practice, and assessment. The basic mode supported adaptation to the VR environment and understanding of fundamental patient assessment. The practice mode simulated clinical nursing care for patients with heart failure, in which learners performed sequential nursing actions in response to changes in the patient’s physiological status. Students verified patient identity, assessed symptoms, measured vital signs, and connected monitoring devices. When respiratory distress occurred, learners positioned the patient in a semi-Fowler’s position (sitting up the head of the bed), initiated oxygen therapy via nasal cannula, and titrated the oxygen flow rate according to the patient’s condition. When deterioration was detected, students reported the patient status using SBAR, performed a 12-lead ECG, inserted an intravenous catheter, and administered prescribed medications, including scheduled furosemide, followed by reassessment. For persistent conduction abnormalities, learners prepared and applied transcutaneous pacing using a defibrillator, including correct pad placement and mode selection. All interventions required continuous reassessment and completion of nursing documentation. In the assessment mode, students independently managed the same clinical scenario without peer assistance. Performance was evaluated using a structured checklist focusing on completeness and accuracy of assessment, prioritization and timeliness of interventions, appropriateness of clinical judgment across the assessment–intervention–reassessment cycle, and clarity and accuracy of communication and documentation. After completion of all three modes, a structured team-based debriefing session was conducted. The system recorded each performance for debriefing review. During debriefing, participants reviewed the recordings (their own and those of peers within the same team), compared actions with clinical expectations, and reflected on clinical reasoning and decision-making processes with instructor guidance.

The program was delivered over four weeks, with one 120-minute session per week (four sessions in total). Each individual scenario required approximately 10–15 min to complete. Data analysis

Demographic data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations. Qualitative data were manually coded and analyzed using Colaizzi’s phenomenological method [22]. Microsoft Excel was used to assist with data organization and coding management. First, researchers read the reflection journals sequentially in the randomized order to gain an overall understanding of participants’ experiences. Second, significant statements related to VR-based heart failure nursing experiences were identified and extracted. Third, meanings were formulated from these significant statements and expressed in the researchers’ own words. Fourth, formulated meanings were organized into theme clusters based on similarities and differences. Fifth, an exhaustive description of the phenomenon was developed, and the fundamental structure of the experience was identified. Data saturation was monitored during analysis and was reached after the 32nd journal, with two additional journals analyzed to confirm saturation.

To enhance credibility, member checking was conducted with two participants to confirm that the interpretations reflected their experiences. In addition, two nursing professors with expertise in qualitative research reviewed the analysis process and findings.

Ethical considerations

This study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Dongyang University (IRB approval number: 1041495-202312-HR-04-01; Approval date: December 15, 2023). All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion of their reflection journals in the research analysis. Participants were informed that their participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. Anonymity and confidentiality were strictly maintained, and all data were stored securely.

Rigor of the study

The rigor of the study was ensured based on the criteria of credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability proposed by Guba and Lincoln [28].

Credibility was enhanced through repeated reading of reflection journals, participant validation of findings, and peer debriefing with qualitative research experts. Transferability was supported by providing detailed descriptions of participants, the educational context, and the VR intervention. Dependability was ensured by adhering strictly to Colaizzi’s analytic procedure and maintaining an audit trail of analytic decisions. Confirmability was strengthened through reflexive discussions among researchers to identify and minimize potential biases related to VR-based education. Participation was voluntary and unrelated to program grades, and the program instructor was not involved in participant recruitment or data collection.

Findings from Colaizzi’s phenomenological analysis of VR-based simulation experiences

Together, the five theme clusters describe a progressive experiential structure of participation, through which students progressed from immersion in the clinical situation to awareness of responsibility, relational engagement, and the limits of the virtual environment.

Theme cluster 1: Transitioning from technical practice to clinical presence

Theme Cluster 1 describes how participants first encountered the situation as an immediately responsive clinical reality. Actions and patient reactions were experienced as inseparable, and participants shifted from performing practiced skills to being present within an unfolding clinical encounter.

Real-time clinical immersion via patient reciprocity

The boundary of this subtheme lies in how participants experienced the patient as a responsive presence rather than a predefined scenario. Unlike mannequin-based simulations, where feedback is often delayed, the VR system provided instantaneous visual and auditory responses. Participants perceived their actions and the patient’s condition as unfolding together within the same moment, creating a sense of being situated inside the clinical situation.

Unlike practicing with a mannequin, the patient responded right away to what I did, which made it feel like I was really providing nursing care. (Participant 10)

After I applied oxygen, the patient said they felt more comfortable, and that made my actions feel real. (Participant 28)

What stayed with me the most was how the patient’s condition changed immediately after I performed an intervention. (Participant 24)

Experiencing responsibility as situated involvement

The boundary of this subtheme is the shift from performing a task to being personally implicated in the patient’s situation. Participants did not simply think they should act correctly; they felt that what they did or failed to do mattered to the patient in that moment. Responsibility emerged as a lived involvement with the patient’s condition rather than as an abstract professional expectation.

When the patient kept saying they felt dizzy, it made me feel like I was truly responsible for this patient. (Participant 16)

I felt pressured because it seemed that if I did not perform correctly, it would immediately affect the patient. (Participant 9)

It no longer felt like practice; I felt that I had to take responsibility and provide proper nursing care. (Participant 25)

Experiencing irreversible time and reorganizing attention

This subtheme is distinguished by participants experiencing time as continuously progressing and not pausable. Rather than following memorized steps, they described having to attend selectively to what mattered most as the patient’s condition evolved. Fundamental care was experienced as stabilizing action within a situation that was already moving forward.

Because time was limited, I kept thinking about what I should do first. (Participant 16)

In trying to act quickly, I realized that I could overlook basic steps, such as properly checking the patient. (Participant 4)

I physically felt that the fundamentals were more important than speed. (Participant 22)

Theme cluster 2: Clinical reasoning as situated action within a shared virtual space

Theme Cluster 2 captures how reasoning was experienced not as stepwise decision-making but as continuous coordination while remaining within the evolving situation. Participants acted while perceiving, and understanding emerged through ongoing involvement rather than detached analysis.

Remaining within a continuously transforming situation

The boundary of this subtheme lies in experiencing care as occurring inside a single, seamlessly evolving virtual reality rather than across separate, disjointed steps. Unlike 2D-screen simulations where scenes often switch abruptly, the 360-degree spatial continuity of VR allowed participants to perceive the patient’s condition as unfolding in real-time. Each intervention immediately altered the virtual surroundings, so the situation did not “end” but re-presented itself as a new clinical state within the same immersive field. Nursing was experienced as “staying within” an ongoing environment where students remained continuously engaged with the patient’s changing condition.

It wasn’t just about doing one skill; assessment, intervention, and checking the patient again were all connected. (Participant 6)

After doing something, I had to look at the patient again and think about what to do next. (Participant 18)

I realized that nursing care keeps going in a cycle, not step by step and then ending. (Participant 27)

Being drawn between simultaneous clinical cues in a 360° perceptual field

This subtheme is distinguished by how participants’ attention was continuously pulled across multiple elements appearing simultaneously within their spherical field of perception. Within the Head-Mounted Display (HMD), the patient’s facial expressions, the rhythmic beeps of the monitor, and physiological signs were encountered as a unified sensory whole. Understanding emerged from the need to physically shift one’s gaze and focus between these competing cues while the situation continued to change. This produced a sense of embodied presence, as students had to actively navigate the data-rich environment rather than passively observing a flat screen.

I had to look at both the ECG and the patient’s symptoms to decide which intervention should come first. (Participant 2)

I realized that interventions are not always done in the same order, but depend on the patient’s condition at that moment. I was able to accurately understand the process and how to use an oxygen flow meter when using nasal O2. (Participant 31)

Even after seeing the ECG, I wasn’t confident in my interpretation, which made me realize how much more I need to study. (Participant 8)

Encountering equipment as immediate possibilities for action within reach

The boundary of this subtheme is the experience of equipment appearing not as abstract icons or instructor-led demonstrations, but as directly graspable means of acting within the virtual space. Participants described the high-risk devices as being “within reach,” allowing for a sense of agency that is often denied to them in real clinical placements. Handling equipment was experienced as a first-person participation in the same virtual room as the patient, where the physical (tracked) movement of their hands and the resulting change in the patient’s status were felt as a single, powerful loop of cause and effect.

In actual clinical practice, students usually just watch, but here I could actually operate the equipment myself. (Participant 23)

I had never handled devices like this before, so being able to try them in the simulation was very meaningful. (Participant 16)

It felt valuable to practice using equipment that we normally can’t touch because it’s considered high risk. (Participant 24)

Theme cluster 3: Communicating within the reality of virtual accountability

Theme Cluster 3 explains how communication became part of the event itself rather than a description of it. Reporting and documentation were experienced as actions that shaped what followed, giving rise to a sense of responsibility for the unfolding situation.

Reconstructing clinical reasoning through structured reporting in the VR interface

The boundary of this subtheme lies in the cognitive translation of an immersive experience into a formal electronic report within the VR system. Unlike 2D-screen simulations where clinical data is often presented as pre-organized text, the VR environment requires students to extract meaningful data from their 360-degree observations and input them into the system’s reporting interface (e.g., SBAR). This process was experienced not as a simple clerical task, but as a real-time cognitive reconstruction, where students had to stabilize their thoughts and prioritize information while still “situated” inside the virtual clinical scenario.

I knew about SBAR, but when I actually had to speak, my thoughts felt disorganized. (Participant 26)

The most difficult part was deciding which information to select and communicate. (Participant 5)

I felt that I needed more practice in organizing my thoughts before speaking. (Participant 10)

Experiencing the weight of accountability within the virtual reporting system

This subtheme is distinguished by the visceral pressure of submitting clinical data that feels final. Within the VR interface, once a report is “sent” or “documented,” it is perceived as an official communication within the simulated professional hierarchy. The boundary here is the shift from “completing a worksheet” to “owning a clinical action.” Participants felt that their input in the virtual system was non-retractable and would directly influence the patient’s next clinical outcome, creating a heightened sense of professional accountability unique to the immersive environment.

I felt pressured because if I said something wrong, it could affect the patient right away. (Participant 11)

It wasn’t just about speaking; I felt responsible for what I was communicating. (Participant 26)

I realized that communication is something nurses have to be accountable for, not just a formality. (Participant 21)

Encountering the complexity of documenting within the immersive context

The boundary of this subtheme is the perceptual challenge of filtering vast sensory data into a concise record. After being “inside” the VR situation, students struggled to translate their first-person lived experiences into formal nursing notes. Unlike case studies where the data is already organized for the student, VR documentation requires a high level of retrospective professional judgment—filtering out the noise of the immersive environment to identify the “clinically significant” essence of the case.

Writing the nursing notes was harder than I expected because I didn’t know how detailed I should be. (Participant 25)

It was difficult to decide what was important enough to include in the record. (Participant 30)

I realized that documenting nursing care properly requires a lot of judgment, not just writing things down. (Participant 34)

Theme cluster 4: Reorienting nursing through relational presence

Theme Cluster 4 illustrates how continued involvement shifted attention from performing tasks to recognizing others within the situation. Nursing came to be experienced as relational presence with patients and peers rather than individual technical performance.

Repositioning oneself through shared situational reflection

The boundary of this subtheme lies in experiencing one’s performance as a revisitable event within the exact same virtual situation rather than observing a separate case. Because the VR scenario provides a consistent 360-degree environment, watching a peer did not feel like encountering a new clinical example but like returning to the same unfolding spatial reality from a different standpoint. Participants described recognizing how alternative actions within that identical virtual space could lead to different developments, which shifted their perspective from acting within the moment to understanding their place within a broader relational context.

Watching my peers made me realize what I had missed in my own performance. (Participant 17)

Seeing how others performed made me feel that I could do better next time. (Participant 28)

I learned a lot by watching my peers—things I wouldn’t have noticed if I had practiced alone. (Participant 6)

Encountering the patient as a responsive other

This subtheme is distinguished by a shift from performing procedures to attending to the patient as a present Other within the immersive field. Participants described initially concentrating on the technical interface, but as the sensory immersion of the HMD persisted, they began noticing the patient’s ongoing expressions and reactions as a continuous presence. The patient was experienced as a responsive person rather than merely a clinical case. This recognition, intensified by the spatial proximity of VR, reoriented their understanding of nursing from technical execution toward relational involvement.

I was so focused on the next skill that I regretted not listening to the patient until the end. (Participant 12)

I realized that I should have paid more attention to the patient’s responses first. (Participant 29)

I felt that respecting the patient is the most important aspect of nursing. (Participant 14)

Theme cluster 5: Navigating the boundaries of virtual and physical reality

Theme Cluster 5 reveals moments when involvement was interrupted and participants became aware of themselves as separate from the situation. Through these disruptions, the limits of the experiential involvement were recognized.

Being pulled out of the situation by bodily awareness

The boundary of this subtheme lies in moments when participants became aware of their physical bodies while engaged in the situation. Rather than remaining fully within the unfolding clinical context, discomfort and awkward movements redirected attention toward themselves, interrupting their involvement. These experiences were described as shifts between being in the situation and being aware of standing outside it, requiring participants to repeatedly regain their focus on the patient.

I wasn’t used to the controls, so it took time just to figure out how to move or do things. (Participant 29)

I felt dizzy at times, which made it hard to stay focused on the nursing tasks. (Participant 18)

Because I was struggling with the controls, I couldn’t fully concentrate on the situation itself. (Participant 32)

Questioning skill authenticity due to lack of tactile feedback and procedural automation

This subtheme is distinguished by performing actions that visually appeared complete but lacked bodily confirmation. Participants described uncertainty when actions produced visible results yet did not feel physically grounded. The absence of resistance made it difficult to trust whether they were truly performing care, creating tension between what was seen and what was felt. As a result, they sometimes relied on procedural guidance rather than their own sense of performing the action.

I performed the IV, but I couldn’t feel anything, so I wasn’t sure if I was actually doing it correctly. (Participant 12)

Since the steps appeared in order, it felt like I was just following instructions rather than really thinking. (Participant 17)

When I tried to hold the thermometer with the virtual hand, it didn’t recognize it well, which was frustrating. (Participant 8)

Discussion

This study suggests that nursing students experienced VR-based simulation as a multilayered learning process extending beyond technical skill practice, including the formation of professional responsibility under tension, enhancement of clinical reasoning, recognition of communication as a core nursing competency, expansion of patient-centered attitudes, and adaptation to technology-mediated care. These findings highlight the potential of VR-based simulation to function not merely as a substitute for clinical practicum but as a complementary educational strategy supporting professional nurse development.

VR-based simulation has gained attention as an educational approach that enhances learner immersion and presence, thereby offering learning experiences that approximate elements of real clinical practice. Immersive VR environments increase cognitive and emotional engagement, which may contribute to the development of clinical thinking and a sense of responsibility [10]. Participation in a scenario-based clinical situation positioned learners as active agents required to make judgments and respond to patient changes. This form of agency can also occur in mannequin- or actor-based simulation. However, in the immersive VR environment, continuous responsiveness, perceptual immediacy, and the immersive continuity of the VR environment appeared to shape how participants experienced ongoing involvement in the situation shaped how this agency was experienced, making actions feel immediately consequential to the patient’s condition.

From a theoretical perspective, simulation-based learning is well suited for heart failure nursing education because heart failure is a complex and dynamic condition requiring continuous assessment, prioritization, and timely intervention. Such environments support experiential and situated learning by placing learners in clinical contexts where they must respond to evolving patient conditions rather than practice isolated skills.

Within this broader simulation framework, immersive VR does not introduce a different learning mechanism but shapes the immediacy and continuity with which the situation is encountered, presenting symptom changes, alarms, and treatment responses within a persistent environment. Because heart failure care involves rapid transitions between stability and deterioration, encountering these changes as an ongoing situation may support the organization of clinical reasoning and anticipatory judgment [29]. Similarly, simulation across modalities can represent nursing care as a continuous cycle of assessment, intervention, and re-evaluation [30]; the present findings therefore describe how this process was experienced within the virtual context rather than demonstrating modality-specific effectiveness.

Previous experimental research, including the randomized controlled trial by Padilha et al. [29], has reported improvements in knowledge retention and clinical decision-making following virtual simulation compared with traditional instructional approaches. Similar findings have been reported across subsequent randomized trials and systematic reviews, including improvements in learning outcomes, clinical reasoning, confidence, and learner satisfaction [31–33].

The present findings complement this outcome-based evidence by clarifying how learners organized their actions within the situation. Participants did not describe learning as memorization of procedures but as clinical reasoning enacted within an unfolding clinical situation in which perception and decision-making occurred simultaneously. From this perspective, previously reported improvements may be understood in relation to learners experiencing themselves as participants in a clinical event rather than observers of a case.

In the present study, professional responsibility emerged while participants responded to patient changes under situational tension. Although responsibility has been reported in various simulation modalities, the form it took in this study was shaped by the immediacy and continuity of the virtual environment. Participants experienced the patient’s condition as changing in direct response to their actions, making inaction or delay perceptually consequential. Rather than pausing to deliberate, they remained within the unfolding situation while attending to multiple cues simultaneously, creating accountability grounded in real-time involvement rather than retrospective evaluation.

Simulation studies using mannequin-based or case-based formats have shown improvements in clinical reasoning, competence, and learner attitudes [34–36], often supported by stepwise progression and reflective pauses. In contrast, participants in the present study described responsibility arising from the difficulty of stepping outside the situation, as the virtual environment continuously responded to their actions. Clinical reasoning therefore appeared as ongoing coordination within a persistent situation rather than sequential decision-making.

Our findings further suggest how understanding of the nursing process develops during virtual simulation. By allowing learners to experience and correct errors in a safe environment, virtual simulation provides a context in which problem-solving and clinical reasoning become organized within the situation [32]. In the randomized controlled trial by Padilha et al. [29], improved performance was associated with integration of assessment, intervention, and evaluation. The present findings indicate that this integration may be experienced as maintaining involvement in an evolving patient situation, where each action required immediate reassessment rather than completion of a discrete step. Repetition may also have contributed to the learning experiences identified in this study, as participants completed each scenario through progressive basic, practice, and assessment modes. Previous simulation literature suggests that repeated and deliberate practice may support the development of clinical reasoning, confidence, and self-efficacy through iterative engagement and error correction [31–33]. In particular, repeated immersive simulation has been reported to provide greater perceived learning benefits than single standalone simulation experiences. The present findings extend this perspective by suggesting that staged repetition within immersive VR may support learners’ transition from environmental adaptation to increasingly independent clinical judgment and responsibility. Accordingly, some aspects of the reported experiential meanings may reflect not only immersion itself but also the structured repetition embedded within the educational design.

Consistently, participants described heart failure nursing care as a coordinated progression of assessment, intervention, and re-evaluation rather than isolated tasks.

Unlike case-based or observational simulation, participants acted within a continuously responding environment in which their actions immediately altered the patient’s condition. This interpretation does not imply that immersive VR is superior to other forms of simulation. Mannequin-based or screen-based simulations may support similar educational goals, such as clinical reasoning and situated learning. Rather, the present findings indicate that immersive VR shapes how these processes are experienced by positioning learners within a continuously responsive environment. The results therefore describe a difference in experiential structure rather than comparative effectiveness across modalities. Notably, participants were able to directly operate high-risk medical equipment through VR-based simulation—equipment that is typically inaccessible during clinical practicums or skills laboratory sessions. This opportunity allowed students to move beyond observation-based learning and engage as active performers of nursing care. Such experiences may help address structural limitations of observation-centered clinical education by enabling learners to integrate judgment and action in complex clinical situations [30, 32]. In addition, participants’ awareness of their own limitations in knowledge and judgment prompted recognition of the need for further learning, suggesting that the VR-based simulation program may promote self-regulated learning and metacognitive development [37].

VR-based simulation also foregrounded non-technical competencies, such as communication and teamwork, as essential components of nursing practice. Simulation-based education has been shown to improve structured communication, situational awareness, and collaborative skills [38]. In particular, structured communication tools such as SBAR play a critical role in clinical judgment and patient safety [39]. In this study, participants experienced communication tasks as both challenging and burdensome, yet came to recognize communication as a professional responsibility inseparable from nursing performance.

Furthermore, peer observation and debriefing following VR-based simulation functioned as important mechanisms for deepening learning. Structured debriefing supports reflection on performance and fosters clinical reasoning and patient-centered thinking [40]. Observing peers’ performances and exchanging feedback encouraged reflective comparison and professional growth, indicating that VR-based education can function not only as an individual learning experience but also as a relational and social learning space [30, 41].

At the same time, this study revealed clear limitations of VR-based simulation alongside its educational potential. Participants reported that difficulties in system operation, lack of tactile feedback, and technical constraints interfered with immersion and skill execution. These findings are consistent with previous research indicating that excessive cognitive load and complex controls may hinder learning in immersive environments [42]. Such limitations suggest that VR-based simulation may be less suitable for learning objectives requiring precise tactile mastery of psychomotor skills. Conversely, VR demonstrates particular strengths in cognitive and affective learning domains—such as clinical judgment, prioritization, responsibility awareness, and professional communication—highlighting the importance of aligning VR use with appropriate educational goals [29, 38].

Therefore, VR-based simulation should not be designed to fully replace clinical practicum, but rather to function as a complementary educational strategy supported by sufficient pre-orientation, stepwise implementation, and stable technical support [30, 43].

The findings of this study have several implications for nursing education and practice. VR simulation may be useful not merely for procedural skill training but as a learning environment that supports clinical judgment and responsibility in response to evolving patient conditions. Educational design may therefore benefit from incorporating dynamic patient changes, time pressure, and communication demands rather than fixed task repetition. Providing staged difficulty and opportunities for repeated exposure to high-risk interventions may help learners engage with situations that are difficult to experience directly in clinical settings. Sensory cues in the virtual environment (e.g., alarms and symptom changes) may promote reflection on professional responsibility and can be supported through structured pre-briefing and debriefing.

In addition, the findings suggest directions for future research. This study highlights the importance of evaluating VR learning beyond knowledge outcomes by examining cognitive and affective processes occurring during participation. Future research may integrate reflective accounts with simulation log data and physiological responses to examine the relationship between presence and the development of clinical reasoning. Longitudinal studies are also needed to determine how repeated exposure to VR influences clinical judgment and professional role formation over time.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. The use of reflection journals may involve selective disclosure and social desirability bias, and the absence of interviews limited opportunities for real-time probing of meanings. No new experiential meanings emerged after the 32nd journal in the randomized analysis sequence, and two additional journals were analyzed to confirm thematic completeness (final n = 34). Because not all collected journals (n = 60) were included in the analysis, saturation should be interpreted as bounded to the analyzed dataset. Participants were recruited from a single course cohort within one institution, sharing similar curricular exposure and prior simulation experience; therefore, the experiential meanings identified may differ in other learner populations or educational contexts. In addition, exposure to VR was limited to the defined training schedule, and the findings may primarily reflect early-stage engagement with immersive simulation. Experiential meanings may evolve with repeated or prolonged exposure. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as context-dependent rather than generalizable characteristics of VR-based learning.

Conclusion

This study suggests that nursing students experienced VR-based simulation as participation in an unfolding clinical situation rather than isolated skill performance, through which clinical reasoning, responsibility, communication, and patient-centered understanding were integrated in heart failure care. Although VR-based simulation cannot replace hands-on clinical training, it may function as a complementary educational approach that supports active engagement in complex clinical situations when aligned with appropriate learning objectives.
