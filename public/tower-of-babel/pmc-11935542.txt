Nonadherence to Preventive Medications After Ischemic Stroke: Opportunities to Target Interventions by Sex, Race, and Ethnicity
Authors: Maria Daniela Zambrano Espinoza, Tracy E Madsen
Published: 2024

Approximately 7.6% to 11.6% of patients experience a recurrent stroke within a year of the first event, 1 , 2 with population studies revealing important disparities due to evidence of higher rates in women, 3 , 4 , 5 racial and ethnic minorities, 6 , 7 , 8 and uninsured individuals. Medication, including antiplatelets, anticoagulants, and statins are the cornerstone of secondary stroke prevention, 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 with much of the reduction of stroke recurrence attributed to availability and consistent use of the medication.

In this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), Chen et al 14 report findings from a study investigating sex differences in nonadherence to medications for secondary stroke prevention in the BASIC (Brain Attack Surveillance in Corpus Christi) Project. BASIC is a population‐based study from a biethnic population (in whom the majority of adults are either US‐born Mexican American or non‐Hispanic White individuals) from Nueces County, Texas. Investigators included patients aged ≥45 years who were identified to have a first‐time stroke between 2008 and 2019 and were able to complete an outcome interview at 90 days. The Adherence eValuation After Ischemic Stroke Registry 15 methods were used to assess nonadherence to anticoagulants, antiplatelets, cholesterol‐lowering agents, and antihypertensives at the 90‐day visit as well as an overall measure of medication nonadherence, which incorporated nonadherence to any of the 4 medication classes.

Prevalence ratios of nonadherence comparing women to men were explored in 8 different models looking at the potential effects of comorbidities as well as social, lifestyle, and health system–related factors. Due to the potential for shared factors contributing to both participation in the follow‐up visit and medication nonadherence, inverse probability weighting was used so that the analytic sample (those who completed both the baseline and 90‐day follow‐up visit) reflected the full BASIC population who lived to at least 90 days after a stroke.

After excluding patients who were discharged to a nursing home, were not prescribed medication for secondary stroke prevention, or did not complete the outcome interview, a total of 1324 individuals were included in the analysis. The nonadherence rate in this population was 11.5%, with women exhibiting a higher rate of 13% (compared with men who had a nonadherence rate of 10.1%). Using a series of regression models with various groups of potential confounders (demographics, social factors, health‐system factors [eg, access to a primary care physician], lifestyle factors, prestroke measures of illness, and factors specific to the treatment of the stroke), authors identified which of these variables changed the prevalence ratio by at least 10%. These variables were then labeled as key confounders and were included in a fully adjusted model. In the fully adjusted model, key sex differences were revealed: women had higher prevalence ratios (PRs) of noncompliance with cholesterol‐lowering agents (PR, 1.80 [95% CI, 1.12–2.84]) and antiplatelet medications (PR, 1.53 [95% CI, 1.03–2.34]). Notably, race and ethnicity modified these findings; Mexican American women were 3 times more likely to report nonadherence to cholesterol‐lowering drugs than Mexican American men at 90 days after the event. Interestingly, sex was not associated with nonadherence to antihypertensive drugs (PR, 1.31 [95% CI, 0.82–2.09]). For the outcome of overall nonadherence, while partially adjusted models demonstrated a statistically significant sex difference in nonadherence (with women having higher rates of nonadherence compared with men), the PR for overall nonadherence in the final model did not meet statistical significance (PR, 1.34 [95% CI, 0.95–1.90]).

This study possesses several strengths. It includes a biethnic population, with a substantial representation of Mexican American individuals, the largest Hispanic group in the United States. Additionally, incident strokes were identified through surveillance across all 7 acute care hospitals in Nueces County, and diagnoses were confirmed by a vascular neurologist before participants were invited to participate. The outcomes of medication nonadherence were assessed using the Morisky scale, which is available in Spanish and has been validated in minority populations. In addition, the use of inverse probability weighting to mitigate the potential for selection bias due to a large proportion of the original cohort of BASIC participants who did not complete the 90‐day outcome visit is important to note.

The greatest strength of the study lies in its approach to the identification of potential key confounders. Investigators separated potential confounders in 7 groups: patient‐related, social, lifestyle, comorbidities, health system, and prestroke‐ and stroke‐related factors. Adjusted and unadjusted analysis were conducted using 8 different models, each adjusted for 1 of the groups of variables so that authors could then calculate the percentage change in the ß estimates from unadjusted to adjusted models to quantify the confounding effect of each individual factor. Factors that altered ß estimates by >10% were deemed to be key contributors to the observed sex differences and were included in the final model. Authors also thoughtfully incorporated both positive and negative confounders of the female‐to‐male prevalence ratios, allowing them to identify both factors that attenuated or accentuated the unadjusted estimates. Differentiating positive from negative confounders in the association between sex and medication nonadherence leads to the ability to identify subgroups of the population who may benefit from targeted interventions. For example, access to a primary care physician was higher among women and served as a negative confounder, as demonstrated by the increase in the effect estimate for nonadherence when primary care physician access was included in the model. As a result, we can speculate that men without regular access to primary care are a subgroup of the population for which targeted interventions may be important. Conversely, obesity was a significant positive confounder in the study, pointing to women with overweight or obesity as a population who may benefit from targeted intervention. Finally, the detailed attention to positive and negative confounding emphasizes an important methodological point in the study of sex differences: that understanding the role of confounders is necessary even when the primary unadjusted association is not statistically significant.

The study also had some key limitations. For example, 1 major limitation of the study is the exclusion of patients who were not prescribed medication for secondary prevention following the index event. This is concerning, given the consistent evidence that Latinx patients receive statins and antiplatelet agents at lower rates after a myocardial infarction or stroke. 16 Additional limitations include the self‐report nature of the outcomes. Further, the study did not collect information on the reasons for medication nonadherence, leaving the factors contributing to medication discontinuation unclear. Detailed information on this issue could shed light on the potential for higher rates of medication side effects among Mexican American women, as well as the impact of access to medications on adherence. Access is a crucial area of investigation, as previous studies have shown that statin compliance improves with changes in insurance coverage among Hispanic populations. 17

With respect to other limitations, it is important to note that while the study focused on biological sex (presumably due to the availability and nature of the available data in BASIC), many of the constructs that contribute to health behaviors, such as whether patients are serving in caregiving roles, are more closely linked to the construct of gender. Such data on gender roles, caregiver status, and other more in‐depth data on social constructs could be investigated further using focus groups or qualitative methodologies.

Authors do, however, speculate about some other potential contributors to the sex differences in adherence to secondary stroke prevention medications, specifically statins. As the authors point out, there are known relatively common side effects to statins including muscular pain. Further, some data suggest there are sex differences in efficacy of statins for the secondary prevention of stroke and that women may be at higher risk of statin toxicity due to factors such as lower body mass, hormone interactions, and other medications that affect the metabolism of statins. 18 , 19 Major knowledge gaps in this area still remain. Because of the central role that statins play in secondary stroke prevention, further research should be conducted to better understand how women experience the side effects of statins, sex difference in the biological and pharmacokinetic properties of statins, and potential alternative therapies if statins cannot be tolerated.

There has been a significant decline in stroke death in recent years. Risk factor modification, particularly blood pressure and cholesterol optimization, have been linked to a reduction in stroke rates and stroke recurrence. This important study suggests potential factors associated with the disparities observed in stroke outcomes in women. The sex differences in medication nonadherence evidenced in this study could potentially explain the disproportionately higher rates of death and disability among women after stroke, especially those from minoritized populations.

Identifying the main drivers for sex differences in nonadherence could inform the development of more effective, targeted preventive strategies. The current study also points to other subgroups who may benefit from targeted interventions around medication adherence after stroke such as Mexican American women specifically, women with obesity, men who smoke or use moderate or heavy alcohol, and men without regular access to primary care. Medication adherence interventions for each of these groups should address both sex‐ and gender‐specific barriers to adherence at the patient level as well as other types of barriers that exist including those to access, education, and inequities built into our health care system. Improving medication adherence after stroke has the potential to decrease recurrence and reduce the overall population‐level disease burden due to stroke, making future work in this area particularly critical.
