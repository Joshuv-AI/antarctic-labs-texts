Dose-Response Association Between Parental Gaming Time and Internet Gaming Disorder of Adolescents and Parents: A Longitudinal Dyadic Study
Authors: Qian Li, Kylie Kai-yi Chan, Samuel Yeung-Shan Wong, Anise Man Sze Wu, Xue Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParental gaming has become increasingly common, yet its dose–response relationship with parental and adolescent Internet gaming disorder (IGD) remains unclear.MethodsA one-year, two-wave longitudinal study was conducted among parent–child dyads in Hong Kong. Adolescents completed anonymous questionnaires in classrooms, and parents completed online surveys. Generalized additive models (R version 4.1.3) were used to assess non-linear associations between parental gaming time and parental IGD symptoms/adolescent IGD, with the nadir defined as a “minimum-risk threshold”. When no non-zero “minimum-risk threshold” was identified, parental gaming time was categorized into three groups (ie, non-gamers, below and over the selected cutoff) stepwise starting from the available smallest non-zero value (0.5 hours/day (h/d)). Mixed regression models were used to identify a “low-risk gaming level” where gaming above the cutoff showed a significantly higher risk than both lower categories. Gender-specific subgroup analyses were conducted.ResultsA total of 712 parent–child dyads (50.4% girls; 80.8% mothers) were included (follow-up rate: 55.8%). Parental gaming time showed a monotonic positive association with parental IGD symptoms and an inverted U-shaped association with wide CIs at parental gaming times above 4 h/d with adolescent IGD, without showing a non-zero “minimum-risk threshold”. Compared with non-gamers and gaming for 0.5 h/d, parents gaming exceeding 0.5 h/d was associated with higher parental IGD symptoms (>0.5 h/d vs non-gamers: b (95% CI) = 2.409 (2.280, 2.538); >0.5 h/d vs 0.5 h/d: 1.787 (1.603, 1.972)) and adolescent IGD (OR (95% CI)=11.124 (6.658, 18.584); 5.119 (2.684, 9.765)). Similar findings were observed in fathers, mothers, boys and girls.ConclusionWhile no non-zero “minimum-risk threshold” was identified, limiting gaming to 0.5 h/d or less may be considered a pragmatic “low-risk gaming level” for parents. It preserves parents’ enjoyment of gaming while maintaining a relatively low IGD risk across two generations. It is worth noting that the observed findings may primarily reflect female caregivers’ gaming behaviors and may not be generalizable to male caregivers.Keywords: internet gaming disorder, parent-child dyads, threshold, longitudinal

Introduction

With the increasing accessibility and popularity of Internet games, problematic gaming behaviors have become increasingly common.1 The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) has included Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) as a condition warranting further research.2 According to the DSM-5, individuals with at least five of the nine symptoms (ie, preoccupation, withdrawal, tolerance, unsuccessful control, giving up other activities, continuation despite problems, deception, escape, and negative consequences) in the past 12 months are classified as IGD cases. Adolescent IGD has emerged as a significant public health concern. Globally, the pooled prevalence of adolescent IGD was 8.8%, with a higher rate in males (15.4%) than in females (6.4%).3 The situation is particularly serious among adolescents in Hong Kong, with the prevalence ranging from 12.7% to 14.9%.4–6 Furthermore, Internet gaming has become increasingly popular among middle-aged and older adults, including parents. Globally, over 75% of adults aged 25 to 54 years played video games in 2025.7 In Hong Kong, over 50% of parents engaged in Internet gaming.8 However, no empirical study has been found on the prevalence of parental IGD.

Emerging evidence suggests that the association between gaming time and IGD may be non-linear. Prior studies3 have indicated that among adolescents and young adults, excessive gaming time increased IGD risks, whereas moderate gaming time (eg, 5 hours/week or less)9 did not exhibit such negative impacts. Instead, moderate gaming has been associated with potential benefits, such as enhancing prosocial behaviors10 and improving problem-solving skills.11 These findings underscore the double-edged nature of Internet gaming and suggest that, rather than complete restriction, identifying a pragmatic gaming-time threshold that balances enjoyment and addiction risk may be more appropriate in this digital era. Such a recommendation (≤5 hours/week) has been proposed for adolescents;9 however, no studies have examined this potential non-linear association in middle-aged adults, including parents, and no such gaming time-related recommendations have been proposed. Identifying a pragmatic gaming-time recommendation for adults, particularly for parents, is important, as gaming has become increasingly prevalent among them. This may not only help reduce their own risk of IGD but also promote a healthier family gaming environment and inform family-based IGD intervention strategies.

Parents play a critical role in shaping adolescents’ behavioral development, including gaming behaviors.12 The relationship between parental gaming time and adolescent IGD may also be non-linear: On one hand, according to the Social Learning Theory,13 children may acquire gaming behaviors through observation and imitation of their parents, potentially increasing the risk of IGD. In addition, according to the Family Systems Theory,14 family is an organized system in which behaviors of family members are interdependent. Parental gaming may influence their child’s gaming behavior through parental gaming attitudes and supervision, parent–child relationship, and co-gaming practices.12,15 On the other hand, under certain conditions, parental gaming may exert beneficial influences. For instance, Sarah et al16 reported that parent–child co-playing Internet games was associated with lower adolescent aggression and stronger parent–child connectedness, both of which have been identified as protective factors against adolescent IGD.12,15,17

Despite these mixed effects, no studies have examined the potential non-linear relationship between parental gaming time and adolescent IGD. Existing evidence is limited to several cross-sectional studies that reported positive associations between parental gaming frequency and adolescent IGD.18–20 However, these studies relied on gaming frequency rather than gaming time in hours, with the former one providing less accurate measures and limiting the ability to examine the potential non-linear associations. Only one cross-sectional South Korean study (N = 199 mothers) assessed maternal gaming time and found no significant differences between adolescents with and without IGD.21 This study relied exclusively on maternal self-reports, which might be susceptible to social desirability bias,19 and limit the validity of the findings. To date, no dyadic studies have investigated the relationship between parental gaming time and adolescent IGD.

Given the increasing prevalence of gaming engagement among parents, and its potential mixed influences on adolescents and themselves, examining the non-linear relationship between them is warranted to inform family-based IGD intervention strategies. Accordingly, this study aimed to (1) examine the non-linear and threshold-dependent associations between parental gaming time and parental/adolescent IGD using a representative parent–child dyadic sample in Hong Kong; (2) identify a non-zero, pragmatic gaming-time threshold for parents; and (3) explore gender-specific thresholds for fathers, mothers, boys, and girls. Conducting this research in Hong Kong is important given its special sociocultural context: High parental expectations regarding academic achievement, intense academic pressure on adolescents, and widespread access to digital technologies may contribute to excessive gaming and IGD in both parents and adolescents. Identifying a pragmatic gaming threshold for parents has important public health implications. It could help parents self-monitor their gaming behaviors, enable healthcare professionals to identify families at elevated risk for IGD, and provide an empirical basis for developing family-based IGD intervention strategies.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This is a one-year, two-wave longitudinal dyadic study implemented in Hong Kong from 2022 to 2024. Secondary school students in public schools and one of their parents (the primary caregiver) were invited. Inclusion criteria for parent–child dyads are: 1) Secondary school students in grades 1–3 in public schools; 2) Both willing to participate in this study; 3) Both speak and read Chinese; 4) Living together; 5) The child’s main caregiver is father or mother. Exclusion criteria include: (1) either the parent or the child is currently receiving treatment for a mental disorder; and (2) either has an intellectual disability that prevents independent completion of the questionnaire. Secondary school students in grades 4–6 were excluded because they needed to prepare for public examinations and were difficult to follow up with after graduation.

Procedures

Stratified random sampling was used to recruit participants. Among the 18 districts in Hong Kong, eight districts were randomly selected, and one secondary school was randomly selected from each district. Schools that agreed to participate submitted application forms, and replacement schools were recruited in cases of refusal. With the assistance of teachers and social workers, the research team distributed leaflets and consent forms to parents. As a token of appreciation, parents received supermarket electronic vouchers, and adolescents were given bookmarks. Adolescents completed a structured, anonymous, self-administered questionnaire in classroom settings without the presence of teachers. Parents completed an online questionnaire based on the Qualtrics platform through WhatsApp or through a telephone interview conducted by trained research staff. In total, 1,277 dyads completed the baseline survey (T1), with 712 dyads completing the follow-up survey (T2) (follow-up rate: 55.8%). Among the dyads lost to follow-up, eight adolescents had transferred to other schools, while the remaining parents declined to participate in the follow-up survey (Supplementary Figure 1).

Measures

Parent-Reported measures

Parental IGD Symptoms

The DSM-5 IGD symptoms checklist2 was employed to assess parental IGD symptoms, which has been validated among Chinese adults.22 The total score ranges from 0 to 9, with higher scores indicating more severe IGD symptoms. Participants with a cutoff of five or more were considered as IGD cases.23 In the current parent sample, Cronbach’s α was 0.74 at baseline and 0.72 at follow-up.

Parental IG Time

Parents reported their gaming time on computers, consoles, tablets, or smartphones over the past six months. Response options include “did not play”, 0.5 hours/day (h/d), 1 h/d, 2 h/d, 3 h/d, 4 h/d, 5 h/d, and 6 h/d or more.

Covariates and Background Variables

Potential covariates were collected, including depression measured by the two-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-2)24 and other Internet-related activities (browsing news and information, online chatting, online shopping, and online entertainment (eg, watching movies or listening to music) per day over the past six months. Background variables, including age, gender, employment, marriage, socioeconomic status, educational level, and mental health service history, were collected.

Adolescent-Reported Measures

Adolescent IGD Symptoms

The same DSM-5 IGD symptoms checklist was used to assess adolescent IGD symptoms.2 Adolescents scoring five or more were classified as IGD cases. The Chinese version of the DSM-5 IGD checklist has been validated in Chinese adolescents and demonstrated good psychometric properties.4,25 In the present study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.75 at baseline and 0.73 at follow-up.

Covariates and Background Variables

Potential covariates, including depression measured by the nine-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9),26 anxiety measured by the seven-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7),27 and parent–child relationship measured by the Relationship subscale of the 27-item Family Environment Scale,28 were collected. These scales demonstrated good reliability in the current sample at both baseline and follow-up, with Cronbach’s α ranging from 0.87 to 0.94. Adolescent IG time and time on other Internet-related activities were assessed using the same items as those reported by parents.

Background variables, including age, gender, school ranking, living arrangement, siblings’ gaming engagement and history of mental health service, were collected.

Statistical Analyses

Generalized additive models (GAMs) by the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) were first applied to examine potential non-linear associations between parental gaming time and adolescent IGD. As the prevalence of parental IGD was very low (1.1% at T1 and 0.8% at T2), to ensure model robustness, the total score of parental IGD symptoms was used instead in GAMs. Following previous research, the nadir of the curve was defined as a “minimum-risk threshold”.29 If no non-zero “minimum-risk threshold” was identified, a stepwise approach would be employed to identify an alternative “low-risk gaming level”.

Specifically, parental gaming time was categorized into three groups (ie, non-gamers, below and over the cutoff) from the smallest non-zero cutoff available in this study (0.5 h/d). Generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) and linear mixed models (LMMs) were used to examine the longitudinal associations of parental gaming time with adolescent IGD and parental IGD symptoms, respectively. Cluster effects within schools and repeated measurements were adjusted for. Significant background variables identified in univariable models were adjusted for in multivariable analyses. Post‑hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Tukey–Kramer adjustment. If parental gaming time >0.5 h/d showed a significantly higher risk than that of both non-gamers and 0.5 h/d, then 0.5 h/d was considered as a “low-risk gaming level”. Otherwise, a larger cutoff value would be used to categorize parental gaming time and repeat the same analyses until a defined “low-risk gaming level” was identified. Chi-square tests were used to further compare the prevalence and incidence of adolescent and parental IGD across the three groups. If the p-value of the overall chi-square test was less than 0.05, post-hoc multiple comparisons would be further conducted with Holm-Bonferroni correction. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore gender-specific thresholds among mothers, fathers, boys, and girls.

Sensitivity analysis by inverse probability weighting (IPW) was conducted to adjust for potential selection bias due to biased loss to follow-up. Weights were calculated based on background variables that significantly differed between participants who completed the 12-month follow-up and those who dropped out. Multiple imputations (MI) by chained equations (N of imputed datasets =10) were implemented to reduce the potential biases caused by missing data. To assess the adequacy of the current sample, sensitivity power analyses were conducted for tested GLMM and LMM based on 1000 Monte Carlo simulations.30 The SIMR package30 in R was used to perform all power analyses. The power of all models in the total parents, fathers, mothers, total adolescents, boys, and girls was over 95% (Supplementary Table 1).

All analyses were performed using R version 4.1.3. Two-tailed p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethics Consideration

All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from both adolescents and their parents. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of the corresponding author’s institution at The Chinese University of Hong Kong (Reference: SBRE-19-451).

Results

The mean (SD) age of parents (81.5% mothers) was 45.41 (5.41) years. Most parents (85.4%) played games for one hour or less per day, with an average gaming duration of 0.59 (0.88) h/d. Among adolescents (49.6% female), the mean (SD) age was 12.28 (1.35) years, and the mean gaming time was 1.78 (1.64) h/d (Table 1). Significant differences between participants who completed follow-up and those who dropped out were observed in adolescents’ school banding, age, gender, living arrangement, time spent on other Internet-related activities, and siblings’ gaming engagement (Supplementary Table 2).

Subgroup analyses showed consistent results for mothers and fathers (Table 2). These findings were robust in sensitivity analyses using IPW (Supplementary Table 4) and MI (Supplementary Table 5 and Supplementary Figure 2).

The Threshold of Parental Gaming Time for Adolescent IGD

Figure 2 illustrates an inverted U-shaped association between parental gaming time and adolescent IGD, with risk peaking at approximately 1.5 h/d for boys and 2 h/d for girls. Although risk appeared to decline beyond these levels, the extremely wide CIs precluded a definitive conclusion. After controlling for significant background factors (Supplementary Table 6), parental gaming exceeding 0.5 h/d was associated with a higher adolescent IGD risk compared with non-gaming parents (OR (95% CI) = 11.124 (6.658–18.584)) and parents gaming for 0.5 h/d (5.119 (2.684–9.765)). Subgroup analyses showed similar patterns among girls, whereas among boys, parental gaming for 0.5 h/d did not significantly increase boys’ IGD compared to those with non-gaming parents (Table 3). Results were consistent in IPW (Supplementary Table 7) and MI analyses (Supplementary Table 8 and Supplementary Figure 3).

Differences in Prevalence and Incidence of Parental and Adolescent IGD Among Groups by the Identified Gaming Level of Parental Gaming Time

As shown in Table 3, adolescents whose parents gamed for more than 0.5 h/d had significantly higher IGD prevalence at T1 (33.5%) and T2 (29.0%) than those whose parents were non-gamers or gamed for 0.5 h/d (both p < 0.001). The number needed to be exposed for harm (NNH)31 was five, indicating that every five adolescents with parental gaming exceeding 0.5 h/d was associated with one additional adolescent IGD case, compared to those with parental gaming time of 0.5 h/d. Among parents, gaming for more than 0.5 h/d was associated with higher IGD prevalence at T1 (3.5%) compared with non-gamers. No significant differences in IGD incidence were observed among the three groups.

Discussion

This study is the first to examine the non-linear relationship between parental gaming time and parental IGD symptoms/adolescent IGD. We found that parental gaming time was monotonically associated with greater severity of parental IGD symptoms and exhibited an inverted U-shaped association with adolescent IGD, with wide CIs at parental gaming times above 4 h/d. We did not identify a “non-zero safe threshold”; instead, our findings suggest a pragmatic “low-risk gaming level” of parental gaming time within 0.5 h/d based on categorical comparison. Compared with gaming more than 0.5 h/d, gaming 0.5 h/d or less was associated with lower risks for both parental IGD symptoms and adolescent IGD, although risks remained slightly higher than those of non-gaming parents. Based on the observed associations, compared to gaming over 0.5 h/d, every five parents whose gaming time was within 0.5 h/d was associated with one fewer case of adolescent IGD.

Approximately half of parents in this sample (48.2%) reported gaming engagement, which was lower than estimates for the general adult population in Hong Kong (83% among adults aged 25–34 years and 72% among those aged 35–44 years).32 This discrepancy is plausible given that our sample comprised primary caregivers, who may have less discretionary time due to household and childcare responsibilities. The predominance of mother samples (81.5%) in this study may have further contributed to the underestimation of overall parental gaming engagement. Despite widespread parental gaming, parents appeared less susceptible to IGD than adolescents. This disparity may be partially explained by adolescents’ immature neurodevelopment, which may increase their vulnerability to external influences and addictive behaviors.33

Contrary to previous findings that moderate gaming time among adolescents did not increase their IGD risk,9 our study did not identify such a “safe threshold” of parental gaming for adolescents. Rather, any parental gaming engagement may signal an increased likelihood of adolescent IGD. This pattern suggests that parental modeling effects may depend more on the presence of gaming behavior than on its duration. Adolescents observing parental gaming may normalize gaming within the family, reduce perceived harms, and weaken the legitimacy of parental restrictions. Evidence from other addictive behaviors supports this interpretation. For example, children with smoking parents were found to tend to perceive smoking as less harmful than those with non-smoking parents.34 In addition, the observed association may also be partly explained by other unmeasured family-level factors, such as more permissive household media norms, lower levels of parental monitoring, and shared genetic susceptibility between parents and adolescents.12,15 These findings suggest that parental gaming behaviors, in addition to adolescents’ own gaming behaviors, may warrant greater attention in family-based IGD intervention strategies.

Notably, the risk of adolescent IGD increased rapidly with parental gaming time up to 1.5–2 h/d, followed by a plateau. This “diminishing marginal effect” (ie, successive increases in an exposure produce progressively smaller incremental effects on an outcome) was also observed in other family studies, such as excessive parental involvement in education not consistently improving children’s school performance.35 The plateau may suggest that once adolescents’ perception of gaming as a normative behavior has been fully activated, further increases in parental gaming time may not introduce additional risks to adolescents’ IGD. Although the IGD risk appeared to decline after the plateau, the extremely wide CIs, due to the small number of parents gaming for 4 h/d or more (N=10), may lead to instability of estimates in this range and therefore should be interpreted with caution.

This study further proposed a pragmatic “low-risk gaming level” of parental gaming time at 0.5 h/d through categorical comparisons. Based on the NNH, every five parents whose gaming time was within, rather than above, 0.5 h/d was associated with one fewer adolescent IGD case. However, the current study did not examine whether the proposed gaming level varied according to parents’ gaming motivations, game genres, or co-playing behaviors with their children. Future studies should investigate these potential moderators and conduct subgroup analyses to evaluate the robustness and generalizability of the proposed gaming level. The large ORs should be interpreted cautiously. They may be caused by the near-zero prevalence of adolescent IGD in the non-gaming reference group and residual confounding, such as shared genetic vulnerability, family media rules, parental monitoring, and gaming motivations.7,15,36 Reverse causation (ie, adolescents’ gaming influencing parental gaming) may also have contributed to the observed large ORs. Parental gaming time may partly represent a marker of adolescents’ previous gaming propensity, leading to an overestimation of its association with adolescent IGD. Future longitudinal studies should simultaneously examine reciprocal associations between them using structural equation modelling, such as the Actor-Partner Interdependence Model.37

Gender-specific analyses revealed that compared with having non-gaming parents, parental gaming for 0.5 h/d increased the risk of IGD among girls but not boys, which suggests greater tolerance of parental gaming among boys. One possible explanation is that girls may place greater emphasis on emotional support and connectedness within the family,38 which may make them more sensitive to perceive parental unavailability and emotional neglect associated with gaming. Such negative perceptions may encourage compensatory engagement in online gaming for social and emotional fulfilment. Given that over 80% of parents in the present study were mothers (81.5%), the observed findings may primarily reflect female caregivers’ gaming behaviors and may not be generalizable to male caregivers. Future studies with more balanced samples of fathers and mothers are needed to determine whether these associations remain stable.

Implications

Current public health guidance primarily emphasizes limiting children’s screen time.39 Our findings suggest that parental gaming behavior was closely related to their child’s IGD. The “low-risk gaming level” of 0.5 h/d identified through categorical comparison may serve as a preliminary reference for identifying parental gaming patterns associated with lower adolescent IGD risk. Schools and community organizations could consider incorporating information on the observed association between parental gaming and adolescent IGD into family-based gaming education. Similarly, clinicians and counselors working with adolescents affected by problematic gaming may consider parental gaming behavior as a relevant family-context factor when discussing gaming patterns with families. Given the observational nature of this study, these implications should be interpreted cautiously. Future longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to validate the observed associations and gaming levels in other populations and cultural settings.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, potential confounders, such as genetic predisposition, cultural factors in Hong Kong (eg, high academic pressure for adolescents and high academic expectations for parents), and family media rules, were not measured or controlled for. These potential confounders may have led to overestimation of the observed associations between parental gaming time and adolescent IGD. Besides, potential moderators, such as parental gaming contexts (eg, co-playing vs solitary), gaming genres and motivations, were not measured, which may moderate both the magnitude and direction of the associations with adolescent IGD. Second, parental gaming time was measured using a single item with fixed response options ranging from “did not play” to “6 h/d or more”. Therefore, the gaming level of 0.5 h/d was constrained by the predefined response categories and reflected a categorical comparison. It was not a discovered threshold from continuous gaming time, and a lower threshold cannot be ruled out. In addition, the specific effect beyond six hours remains unexplored. Future studies using continuous or diary-based measures of gaming time are needed to validate and refine the gaming level. Third, the small number of parents in the longer gaming-time categories may have reduced the precision and stability of the estimates for GAM and GLMM. Fourth, the attrition rate was relatively high (44.2%). Although the IPW sensitivity analyses adjusted for observed differences between participants who were lost to follow-up and those who completed follow-up, they could not account for unmeasured factors. For example, families with greater concerns about gaming may have been more likely to retain, potentially leading to an overestimation of the observed associations. Finally, only the primary caregiver per family was invited to participate in this study, with a disproportionately higher proportion of mothers. This gender imbalance may have amplified the observed association between parental gaming time and girls’ IGD. Future studies are recommended to recruit more balanced samples of fathers and mothers, ideally, including both parents to capture the full spectrum of parental gaming influences.

Conclusions

Although no non-zero “minimum-risk threshold” was identified, gaming for 0.5 h/d or less may be considered a pragmatic “low-risk gaming level” that preserves parental enjoyment while keeping IGD risk at a relatively low level, particularly for adolescents. Given the observational design, the unmeasured potential confounders, and the categorical measurement of gaming time, these findings should be interpreted with caution. It is worth noting that the observed findings may primarily reflect female caregivers’ gaming behaviors and may not be generalizable to male caregivers. Future longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to validate the findings in other populations and settings.
