Whole Body Phase Angle as a Promising Marker of Disease Severity in Facioscapulohumeral Muscular Dystrophy
Authors: Oscar Crisafulli, Renato Baptista, Andi Nuredini, Matteo Fortunati, Luca Grattarola, Rossella Tupler, Anna Odone, Giuseppe D'Antona
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsTo investigate its potential role as a marker of disease severity in facioscapulohumeral dystrophy (FSHD), this study examined the association between whole‐body phase angle (PhA) and clinically assessed severity in FSHD patients.MethodsPatients were evaluated for PhA using bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) and for disease severity using the FSHD clinical score. In a subgroup, physical activity level (PAL) was assessed using a questionnaire. Pearson correlations were performed in the total cohort and PAL‐assessed subgroup, stratified by sex and clinical phenotype (category A and non‐A) according to the Comprehensive Clinical Evaluation Form. Multivariate regression analyses were conducted adjusting for age, body mass index, and PAL. Due to the small sample, correlation was not performed in PAL‐assessed non‐A patients, while multivariate regression was not performed in non‐A patients and PAL‐assessed stratified subgroups.ResultsSeventy‐two patients (mean age 39.94 ± 17.52 years; 27 females and 45 males; 58 category A and 14 non‐A) were enrolled. The PAL‐assessed subgroup comprised 38 patients (mean age 41.39 ± 14.98 years; 16 females and 22 males; 31 category A and 7 non‐A). Pearson analyses revealed that PhA was inversely associated with clinical score in all groups (r –0.63 to –0.84, p 0.02 to < 0.001). Multivariate regression confirmed PhA as the sole independent predictor across all groups (β = –0.64 to –0.93, all p < 0.001).DiscussionThese results support the potential role of PhA as a marker of disease severity in FSHD. Longitudinal studies are warranted to determine its sensitivity for monitoring disease progression over time.Keywords: bioelectrical impedance analysis, body composition, clinical score, disease severity, facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy, phase angle

Introduction

Facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy (FSHD) causes progressive muscle loss accompanied by an increase in adipose tissue [1]. These body composition changes lead to muscle weakness and functional limitations [2]. Functional‐based clinical scales have been developed to provide numerical measures of disease severity [3, 4]. However, these assessments may be influenced by patient cooperation and learning effects [5]. Moreover, given the slow disease progression, clinical scales may lack sensitivity to detect subtle changes over time and reliably capture treatment effects in clinical trials [5]. Parameters obtained from body composition assessments, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), ultrasound, and dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry (DXA), have shown good correlation with clinical assessment scales, supporting their role in disease evaluation [5]. However, routine clinical use and follow‐up can be limited by cost and access (MRI), inter‐device variability (DXA), and operator dependency (ultrasound) [6]. The potential utility of bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA)‐derived Phase Angle (PhA), a raw parameter reflecting whole body tissues' resistance and reactance [7], remains unexplored in FSHD. Although PhA has been associated with functional measures [8, 9] and muscle quality [10] in sarcopenic cohorts, and with disease severity in myotonic dystrophy Type 1 [11], in FSHD it is only known to distinguish between patients and healthy controls [12, 13], while its association with clinical scales has not been investigated. The identification of an association between PhA and clinical assessment scores could provide a novel, accessible, and cost‐effective marker of disease severity with potential applicability in routine practice. To address this, the present report analyzes the association between PhA and disease severity in a cohort of FSHD patients, accounting for potential confounding factors that may influence this relationship.

Methods

Participants and Procedures

The study was conducted at the CRIAMS–Sport Medicine Centre, University of Pavia (Voghera, Italy), approved by the Lombardy Territorial Ethics Committee 6 (protocol no. 0006176/24), and performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or, in the case of minors, from their parents or legal guardians. Patients, identified and recruited among those regularly followed at our center, were enrolled if they had a clinical and genetic diagnosis of FSHD and were registered in the Italian National FSHD Registry [14]. Exclusion criteria included comorbid cardiovascular, pulmonary, orthopedic, or neuromuscular disorders other than FSHD. Patients were classified according to the Comprehensive Clinical Evaluation Form (CCEF) into categories A (typical phenotype), B (incomplete phenotype), C (asymptomatic), or D (complex/uncommon phenotype) [15]. In a single session, patients underwent assessments of disease severity and PhA. In a subgroup, physical activity level (PAL) was also evaluated.

Disease severity was quantified using the FSHD clinical score [4], corresponding to Section 2 of the CCEF [15], ranging from 0 (no motor impairment) to 15 (severe functional impairment). The assessment was performed by a neurologist with extensive experience in the clinical evaluation of FSHD patients (AN). PhA was assessed by BIA as previously described [16]. Briefly, a single‐frequency (50 kHz) impedance analyzer was used (BIA 101 BIVA PRO, Akern, Florence, Italy) under standardized conditions. Two adhesive electrodes (Biatrodes, Akern Srl, Florence, Italy) were applied to the dorsum of the hand and foot on the same body side. A low‐amplitude alternating current was passed through the body, providing whole‐body measurements of resistance (R) and reactance (Xc), from which PhA (°) was calculated as arctangent (Xc/R) × 180°/π. Finally, PAL was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) and expressed as metabolic equivalents of task minutes per week (METs‐min/week), a validated approach previously applied in FSHD [16, 17].

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 31.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY). Associations between PhA and clinical score were initially examined using Pearson's correlation coefficients, with 95% confidence intervals (CI) calculated via Fisher's r‐to‐z transformation and back‐transformed to the r scale. Analyses were performed in the full sample and stratified by sex as a potential confounder, given previously reported sex‐related differences in PhA values [18]. A secondary analysis stratified participants into category A (typical clinical phenotype) and non‐A category (B and D categories; atypical clinical phenotypes combined due to limited sample sizes). This exploratory stratification was based on the hypothesis that distinct FSHD phenotypes may exhibit different patterns of muscle involvement, potentially affecting both disease severity and body composition, and influencing the PhA–clinical score association. The same analytical approach was applied to the subgroup with PAL assessment, except for non‐A patients, who were excluded due to limited sample size. To assess the independent association between PhA and clinical score, multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted with clinical score as the dependent variable. The number of covariates included in each regression model was determined a priori based on sample size considerations, using a conservative target of approximately 10 observations per predictor variable to reduce the risk of overfitting, ensuring stability and interpretability of the estimates [19]. Model fit was evaluated using R 2 and adjusted R 2. Regression results are reported as unstandardized coefficients (B), standardized coefficients (β), p‐values, and 95% CI. Collinearity diagnostics indicated acceptable multicollinearity (maximum variance inflation factor = 2.1), supporting the stability and interpretability of the estimates [20]. The primary model included all participants and was adjusted for potential confounders, including age [21] and body mass index (BMI) [22]. Additional analyses were conducted stratifying by sex and restricting the sample to patients in category A, as the number of non‐A participants was insufficient to support reliable modeling. In a subsample of participants, a secondary regression model incorporated PAL as an additional covariate [23]. These analyses were performed only in the overall PAL subgroup as sex and phenotype stratification were not feasible due to limited sample size. Statistical significance was defined as α = 0.05 (two‐tailed).

Discussion

Overall, the results indicate that whole‐body PhA is inversely and independently associated with FSHD clinical score. Previous studies using electrical impedance myography (EIM) have demonstrated that localized impedance parameters provide quantitative information on muscle composition and function [24, 25, 26] and are sensitive to disease progression in FSHD [27]. While EIM assesses impedance at the level of individual muscles, whole‐body BIA provides a global assessment of body impedance, from which PhA is derived. Our findings further support the role of impedance analysis in FSHD by showing that whole‐body PhA is significantly associated with clinically assessed disease severity. Whether such parameter is similarly sensitive to disease progression, as previously demonstrated for EIM‐derived measures [27], warrants investigation in longitudinal studies. Given the descriptive and observational nature of this study, a mechanistic explanation cannot be provided. However, it can be hypothesized that progressive muscle wasting and fibro‐fatty replacement [1] may modify the electrical properties of body tissues, contributing to associated alterations in PhA. Nevertheless, our findings seem in line with previous investigations in which PhA was associated with functional capacity in various populations, including sarcopenic older adults [28], stroke patients [29], and hemodialysis patients [30]. Future studies are needed to elucidate the physiological mechanisms underlying this association.

This study has some limitations. Its cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference and limits interpretation as indicative of clinical validity. Multivariable models in females and PAL subgroups (Table 2) should be considered exploratory due to sample size constraints. The association between PhA and disease severity was assessed exclusively using the FSHD clinical score. Future studies should include objective functional assessments, such as muscle strength, to further explore the clinical relevance of PhA. Additionally, PAL was assessed via a self‐reported questionnaire, which may be subject to recall bias.

In conclusion, whole‐body PhA is inversely associated with disease severity, independently of sex, age, BMI, and PAL. These findings support its role as a promising marker of disease severity in FSHD, with potential application in routine clinical monitoring. Longitudinal studies are needed to establish its sensitivity and effectiveness in tracking disease progression over time.
