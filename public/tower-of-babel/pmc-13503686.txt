Predictive value of aortic propagation velocity for cardiovascular events in asymptomatic individuals with elevated ASCVD risk
Authors: Leila Bigdelu, Mostafa Ahmadi, Bahram Shahri, Mohammad Ali Yaghoubi, Mona Kabiri, Faeze Keihanian, Morvarid Mirzaeian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prediction of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) is a challenge that cardiologists have always faced all the times. Aortic propagation velocity (APV) is an echocardiography index that can be helpful with this regard; however, few studies have addressed the condition. This prospective cohort study was conducted at the outpatient clinic of Imam Reza and Quaem Hospital in Mashhad, Iran. It was conducted on cases with ASCVD score of > 10. All the cases underwent echocardiography and APV was measured for them. The cases were classified into two groups including normal APV (> 56 cm/s) and abnormal APV (≤ 56 cm/s). They were followed for a duration of 1 year and the rate of CVD was compared between the two groups. Moreover, ROC curve analysis was used for determining a cut-off. The primary endpoint was a composite of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE): non-fatal MI, non-fatal stroke, coronary revascularization, or cardiovascular death. A total of 60 cases, including 37 (61.7%) males and 23 (38.3%) females, were enrolled. After 1 year of follow-up, 7 cases developed MACE: 6 in the abnormal APV group and 1 in the normal APV group (p = 0.039). Logistic regression analysis showed a significant relationship between APV and MACE development (OR = 2.10 per 10 cm/s decrease in APV; 95% CI 1.116–3.932; p = 0.02). ROC curve analysis proposed a cut-off of 49.75 cm/s with a sensitivity of 85.7% and specificity of 60% (AUC = 0.710; 95% CI 0.585–0.835; p = 0.072). Seven cases developed MACE (non-fatal MI, stroke, revascularization, or CV death). APV showed a trend toward prognostic value for MACE, suggesting it may serve as a potential adjunctive marker in this high-risk population; however, larger studies are needed to confirm these findings.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) remain a leading cause of mortality worldwide, accounting for more than 19 million deaths globally in 20211,2. In the United States, CVD was responsible for approximately 697,000 deaths in 2020, representing one in every five deaths3,4. Similarly, CVDs constitute the foremost cause of death in Iran, accounting for about 45% of all deaths5,6. In 2021, CVDs accounted for nearly 30% of total deaths and contributed to 15% of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in Iran, highlighting a significant health and economic burden6. It is also reported that CVDs poses an economic burden of 6700 billion IRR in Iran. part of this high burden is due to the premature death and loss of years of productivity7. This burden is compounded by risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and unhealthy lifestyle behaviors prevalent in different regions8.

Risk stratification tools such as the Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease (ASCVD) risk score are valuable in identifying individuals at elevated risk for cardiovascular events. However, the ASCVD risk score has limitations, including possible overestimation of risk in certain populations, prompting ongoing research into novel predictive markers to enhance risk assessment9–11. Echocardiography, a safe and non-invasive modality, offers potential for such advances by assessing cardiac structure and function12,13.

With this regard, this tool can be used as a diagnostic, prognostic, and even screening method. Aortic propagation velocity (APV) is an echocardiography index investigated for this reason14. Aortic propagation velocity (APV), measured via color M-mode echocardiography, is a promising marker of arterial stiffness15. While prior studies have explored its diagnostic role in symptomatic patients with coronary artery disease16,17, its prognostic utility in asymptomatic, high-risk individuals—precisely those where the ASCVD score has limitations—remains underexplored. This study aims to prospectively evaluate the predictive value of APV for incident cardiovascular events in asymptomatic patients with an ASCVD risk score > 10.

Material and methods

Study population and design

This prospective cohort study utilized data from the Mashhad Echocardiography (M-ECHO) Registry (Ethical Code: IR.MUMS.REC.1403.052), which systematically collects data from patients referred to the echocardiography departments of Imam Reza and Quaem Hospitals in Mashhad. Data for the present analysis were prospectively collected within this registry framework from eligible asymptomatic adults with an ASCVD risk score > 10 who attended the outpatient clinic of Imam Reza and Quaem Hospital.

Included participants had an ASCVD score > 10 and provided consent for a 1-year follow-up. Volunteers were excluded for any of the following: valvular stenosis or insufficiency, cardiomyopathies, atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, or other arrhythmias; history of myocardial infarction; congenital heart diseases; bundle branch block on electrocardiography; aortic dilation > 40 mm; serum creatinine ≥ 2 mg/dl or need for dialysis; or poor echocardiography image quality.

Data gathering

Demographic data including age and gender were extracted. Moreover, underlying diseases including diabetes, hypertension, and obesity were recorded. Also, social history (smoking), statin usage, and aspirin usage were assessed. Furthermore, systolic and diastolic pressures along with laboratory data including LDL, HDL, TG, and total cholesterol were measured.

The primary endpoint was a composite of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), including: (1) non-fatal myocardial infarction (defined according to the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction); (2) non-fatal stroke (defined as a focal neurological deficit of cerebrovascular origin lasting > 24 h with imaging confirmation); (3) coronary revascularization (percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary artery bypass grafting); and (4) cardiovascular death. All events were prospectively ascertained and adjudicated by an independent clinical events committee blinded to the APV measurements. In addition to participant self-report and end-of-follow-up contact, hospital records and electronic health registries were reviewed to ensure complete event capture and minimize under-ascertainment.

Echocardiography

All the patients underwent routine standard echocardiography by an expert echocardiography fellowship, during electrocardiography and blood pressure tracking. To measure aortic diameters, the proximal descending thoracic aorta was visualized from the suprasternal window. The systolic and diastolic diameters of the descending aorta were then measured 3 cm distal to the left subclavian artery origin using M-mode. The systolic diameter was measured, when the valve was open, while the diastolic diameter was measured according to the QRS peak. The data were measured during 3 cardiac cycles and the final data were the mean of the recorded measurements. Moreover, M-Mode color Doppler echocardiography from proximal part of descending aorta at suprasternal window was performed. While the Doppler curser was parallel with the main flow of descending aorta, Nyquist limit was reduced 30 to 50 cm/s and Aortic propagation flow was demonstrated by switching to M-Mode and the slope was measured by analyzing at the Sweep rate of 200 mm/s. The value of APV was calculated by dividing the distance by the time during flow emission by measuring the velocity gradient by an echocardiography machine, and the final value was the average of at least three consecutive calculations. Examinations were performed using an echocardiographic ultrasound system equipped with a X5-1 probe (Philips Medical System, Andover, MA).

For a priori grouping based on existing literature, participants were classified as having abnormal APV if APV ≤ 56 cm/s and normal APV if APV > 56 cm/s18. The volunteers were followed for a duration of 1 year and were asked to contact the researcher, when they had a CVD. Moreover, the researcher contacted the cases to assess the CVD occurrence at the end of follow up. The two groups were compared regarding the occurrence of CVD. Subsequently, a data-driven ROC analysis was performed to identify an optimal prognostic cut-off value for this specific cohort; this exploratory cut-off (49.75 cm/s) requires external validation before clinical application.

Ethics

Those who were volunteer were asked for written informed consent. Moreover, the cases were free to discontinue the study when they want to do so. All the steps of the study were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by ethics committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences.

Discussion

Our study prospectively evaluated APV in asymptomatic, high-risk individuals. We found that a lower APV was significantly associated with a higher incidence of MACE over 1 year, although the ROC analysis did not reach statistical significance, suggesting the need for cautious interpretation and validation in larger cohorts.

Echocardiography can be a useful method, as has no adverse radiation and can provide structural and functional data about the heart19. APV is an index that has been investigated in case of having prognostic value for CVD20. We focused on the utility of the APV for predicting CVD risk in asymptomatic patients with ASCVD score of more than 10. Our results demonstrated that although the ASCVD score showed no significant difference between normal and abnormal APV groups, the occurrence of CVD in abnormal APV group was significantly higher. Moreover, logistic regression showed that there was a significant relationship between APV and CVD occurrence. The best propose cut-off regarding predicting CVD was 49.75 cm/s with a sensitivity of 85.7% and specificity of 60%.

The optimal APV cut-off value of 49.75 cm/s for predicting cardiovascular events in our asymptomatic, high-risk cohort aligns with the range of values reported in prior studies investigating APV for coronary artery disease (CAD) detection. Our value sits within the spectrum of proposed thresholds, which vary from 41 to 56 cm/s depending on the study population and endpoint15–17. This variation is expected, as cut-offs are inherently cohort-specific; for instance, the lower threshold of 41 cm/s reported by Güneş et al.17 was derived in a symptomatic population referred for angiography, whereas our higher-risk asymptomatic cohort may manifest arterial stiffness at a less advanced, but still prognostic, stage. Importantly, the consistent direction of association across studies—where lower APV signifies higher risk—reinforces the biological plausibility of our finding. While the study by Arı et al.21 reported no correlation with classical stiffness parameters, its divergent results may be explained by differences in methodology or patient selection. The preponderance of evidence supports APV as a marker of vascular pathology.

Physiologically, a lower APV directly reflects increased aortic stiffness and impaired elastic function, akin to the well-established metric of aortic pulse wave velocity (PWV)22,23. Stiffening of the aorta leads to faster, not slower, propagation of the foot of the pressure wave (high PWV), but it impairs the energy-dependent flow wave propagation measured by APV. This dysfunctional state results in elevated central systolic pressure, increased left ventricular afterload, and reduced coronary perfusion pressure—key mechanisms linking aortic stiffness to adverse cardiovascular events24. Therefore, our derived cut-off, while requiring external validation in a similar asymptomatic cohort, is grounded in both empirical data from comparable studies and a sound pathophysiological framework.

In fact, it seems that APV can be an easy and useful marker of CVD. However, more studies are needed to reach a consensus regarding the proposed cut-off. These findings suggest that along the vascular aging and atherosclerosis progression, the vascular elasticity lowers. This arterial stiffness has relationship with risk factors like cigarette smoking, hypertension, obesity, diabetes, lipid profile changes, and age25,26. Arterial affect the structure and functionality of vascular system and therefore, APV as a marker of vascular function and structure can be helpful27.

Our findings suggest APV may serve as a useful adjunct to the ASCVD score for risk stratification. Incorporating a simple, non-invasive echocardiographic measure like APV could help refine risk assessment, particularly in intermediate/high-risk asymptomatic individuals, potentially identifying those who would benefit from more intensive monitoring or earlier preventive therapy.

One of the strengths of our study was the fact that we studied all CVD cases and did not focus on only CAD. The other positive point of our investigation was the study of asymptomatic cases with ASCVD score more than ten. Other studies in the literature mainly focused on the patients that needed angiography. Our study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size (n = 60) limits the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. The post hoc power analysis indicated a power of 72%, which is below the conventional 80% threshold. This increases the risk of a Type II error. Therefore, our results should be considered preliminary and require validation in larger, multi-center prospective studies. Our study was limited to a follow-up time of only 1 year and higher follow up time may yield more concise results. Secondly, the 1-year follow-up period is relatively short for the natural history of atherosclerosis. A longer observation time would be necessary to capture a more robust number of hard clinical endpoints. Third, the echocardiographic measurement of APV, while standardized, is operator-dependent and requires good image quality, which may limit its widespread reproducibility.

Conclusion

In this prospective cohort of asymptomatic high-risk individuals, lower aortic propagation velocity (APV) showed a significant association with incident cardiovascular events, although the ROC analysis did not meet conventional significance, suggesting potential as an adjunctive prognostic marker that requires further validation. The derived cut-off of 49.75 cm/s requires external validation. Larger, multi-center studies with longer follow-up are needed to confirm these findings and establish the clinical utility of APV in routine risk stratification.
