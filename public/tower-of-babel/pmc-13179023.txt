Microvascular Toe-to-thumb Transfer After High-voltage Electrical Burn: A Case Series and a Narrative Review
Authors: Dana Bregman, Eloise Stanton, Peggy Ebner, Anna C. Howell, Saeed Mohammed, Milan Stevanovic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Thumb amputation due to high-voltage electrical injury represents a unique challenge to the reconstructive microsurgeon due to the uncertain reliability of recipient vessels. The zone of injury in these particular burns is often difficult to elucidate from physical examination, and the ideal time for reconstruction has not yet been well characterized. We present 3 cases of successful microsurgical reconstruction in 2 patients via toe-to-thumb transfer following electrical injury resulting in thumb amputation.Methods:Retrospective review of a single surgeon’s experience identified 2 patients who sustained electrical injuries resulting in thumb amputation (1 bilateral and 1 unilateral), with subsequent successful reconstruction via free toe-to-thumb transfer.Results:Both patients had successful thumb reconstruction after microvascular toe-to-thumb transfer. Both patients regained sensibility, motion, and sufficient pinch strength to use their reconstructed thumbs in everyday activities, with minimal donor-site morbidity.Conclusions:Microsurgical reconstruction of the thumb following amputation as a result of electrical burn can be particularly challenging due to extensive soft-tissue injury. We report 3 cases of successful thumb reconstruction using this technique with good functional outcomes. Microsurgical reconstruction of the thumb should be considered even in patients who have experienced thumb amputation as a result of electrical injury.

Takeaways

Question: Are high-voltage electrical burns, which often result in complex thumb amputations with vessel viability and reconstruction timing, a contraindication to microsurgical toe-to-thumb transfer, and what strategies optimize reconstruction timing and recipient vessel selection?

Findings: Through a detailed case series and literature review, we demonstrate that toe-to-thumb transfer can be safely performed after electrical burn injury when recipient vessels are carefully evaluated. Successful reconstruction restored grip and fine motor function without flap loss.

Meaning: Electrical burns are not a contraindication to microsurgical thumb reconstruction when managed with staged planning and meticulous vessel assessment.

INTRODUCTION

High-voltage electrical injuries can cause devastating harm, including severe morbidity and mortality.1 The severity of an electrical burn depends on amperage, tissue resistance, current type and magnitude, and duration of exposure to the electrical source. Complications of electrical burns include compartment syndrome, rhabdomyolysis, and cardiac arrhythmias. Approximately 400 patients in the United States die every year due to complications from electrical burns.2 Because the cutaneous manifestations of electrical burn may not be as outwardly apparent as a thermal burn, prompt clinical management, and oftentimes surgical treatment, can be life- and limb-saving. During the acute management of patients who sustain electrical burns, functional outcomes are often overlooked, to save both life and limb, and simple coverage of wounds is the priority in the acute phase.

Patients who sustain electrical burns to the hands are particularly vulnerable to long-term functional disability. The hand is among the most commonly involved anatomical structures in burns.3 Because electrical current flows through tissues with the lowest resistance to current, extremities, with high muscular content, are particularly at risk of electrical burn injury. Injury may lead to compartment syndrome, which requires expeditious surgical release and appropriate debridement of necrotic tissues.

Electrical injury of the hands and its surgical treatment can leave patients with poor functional outcomes. When the thumb is lost, patients are considered to be 40% disabled.4 Microvascular toe transfer for thumb reconstruction has been shown to be a reliable method of reconstruction and allows improved function in patients who have lost their thumb in traumatic, oncological, and congenital settings.4–7 More recent series have extended these principles to electrical injuries, emphasizing that reconstruction should proceed once the wound and soft tissues have fully stabilized rather than at a fixed time after injury.8–10 The purpose of the present article is to discuss 2 cases, 1 of which was bilateral, in which the thumb was successfully reconstructed with a microvascular toe transfer after sustaining high-voltage electrical injury, resulting in life-altering improvement of function in both patients.

PATIENTS/METHODS

Both patients sustained electrical injuries and were taken immediately to the Los Angeles General Medical Center. One patient had a unilateral thumb amputation, and the other patient had bilateral thumb amputation. Institutional review board approval was obtained for retrospective data collection on both patients. Reconstruction was undertaken after completion of all necessary debridements, with no ongoing tissue demarcation; absence of clinical or laboratory evidence of infection; a stable, supple, and fully grafted or healed soft-tissue envelope; and resolution of the acute systemic inflammatory phase. The patients underwent preoperative computed tomography angiography imaging and Doppler assessments to map both donor and recipient vessels. Intraoperative Doppler was implemented to confirm adequate blood flow in our anastomoses.

SURGICAL PROCEDURE

In both patients, free great toe transfer was performed under general anesthesia. A tourniquet was applied to the upper extremity, and the recipient site was explored to confirm adequate inflow before proceeding. Dense scar tissue was encountered; however, in each case, a healthy-appearing princeps pollicis artery was identified proximally within the anatomical snuffbox, allowing for preservation of the radial artery. Two dorsal veins and sensory nerves were also identified and marked. The tourniquet was then released to confirm adequate perfusion before hand debridement and preparation for transfer.

Intraoperatively, a “healthy-appearing” recipient vessel was defined by brisk bleeding at the transection edge, a smooth and shiny intimal surface, palpable or Doppler-detectable proximal pulsatility, pliable vessel walls without sclerosis or discoloration, and sustained distal perfusion after tourniquet release. When vessel quality was uncertain, dissection was extended proximally until these criteria were met. Anastomoses were performed several centimeters proximal to the visible burn margin, consistent with prior literature.11–13

Donor-site preparation included preoperative computed tomography angiography, which demonstrated normal trifurcation of the popliteal artery. The first dorsal metatarsal artery and superficial veins were mapped using Doppler ultrasonography. With a thigh tourniquet in place, the great toe was harvested in standard fashion: the artery and veins were dissected, sensory nerves were isolated, and tendons were divided. The bone was transected at the level of the proximal phalanx or metatarsophalangeal joint, depending on the length required to match the reconstructed thumb. Closure was achieved with a split-thickness skin graft and bolster dressing.

The flap was transferred to the hand, with bony fixation using 2 crossing 0.045-inch Kirschner wires. Flexor tendon repair was performed using 4-0 nonabsorbable monofilament with a 4-strand cruciate technique plus 6-0 epitendinous suture; the extensor tendon was repaired with 4-0 nonabsorbable monofilament. Arterial anastomosis was performed with 9-0 interrupted sutures, venous anastomosis with 10-0 interrupted sutures, and neurorrhaphy with two 9-0 epineural sutures reinforced with fibrin glue.

Skin closure of the reconstructed thumb was completed with simple interrupted sutures, and grafts were applied to the remaining open wounds. A bulky above-elbow dressing was applied with the elbow flexed and wrist extended. Donor sites were splinted in neutral dorsiflexion. Postoperatively, patients were monitored in the intensive care unit with hourly flap checks for 48 hours. Aspirin at 81 mg daily was started on postoperative day 1. Bolster dressings were removed at 1 week, and patients remained non-weight-bearing for 2 weeks, first in splints and later in ankle foot orthosis boots. K-wires were removed after radiographic evidence of union at 4–6 weeks, after which occupational therapy was initiated.

Laterality of toe harvest was individualized based on donor-foot CTA findings, desired tendon vector alignment and thumb opposition, and patient functional priorities, including athletic foot dominance. Ipsilateral transfers were chosen when they optimized alignment and functional symmetry.

RESULTS

Case 1

A 56-year-old, otherwise healthy, right-hand dominant man sustained a high-voltage electrical injury to his left hand, resulting in amputation of the thumb through the metacarpophalangeal joint (MCPJ) and index finger. After serial debridement, his defect demonstrated full-thickness skin loss over the thenar eminence extending from the distal aspect of the first metacarpal to the volar proximal aspect of the base of the first metacarpal. The patient expressed an interest in thumb reconstruction; however, given the lack of stable soft-tissue coverage, he was indicated for a staged procedure. His initial procedure following definitive debridement was a pedicled groin flap. The ipsilateral groin was used, and the flap was designed as previously described.14,15 After 3 weeks, the flap was divided at its base and inset. The donor site was closed primarily, and the patient was monitored regularly in clinic during the following 8 weeks for healing and soft-tissue stabilization. The groin flap was chosen specifically to provide a thin, pliable, and reliably perfused soft-tissue envelope in the setting of an evolving electrical injury, while preserving upper extremity vessels for subsequent microsurgical reconstruction.

After that time, his soft tissues were determined to be supple enough to permit transfer of the right great toe to reconstruct the left thumb. Free toe transfer was performed as described earlier, and the patient had an uneventful postoperative course. He remained in the hospital for 3 days for postoperative monitoring and was otherwise managed as an outpatient between his initial burn care and delayed microsurgical reconstruction.

His donor site and recipient site healed without complication, and he had an excellent aesthetic outcome of the toe transfer. After a period of rehabilitation, he was able to perform key pinch and demonstrated good thumb opposition. His 2-point discrimination (2PD) was 10 mm on the ulnar and radial aspects of his thumb. At 18 months of follow-up, these functional and sensory outcomes remained stable, with no late donor-site morbidity.

Case 2

A 41-year-old right-hand dominant butcher sustained a high-voltage injury involving 3.5% total body surface area: bilateral hands (2% fourth-degree burns), anterior chest, and mid-central back. He underwent emergent hand fasciotomies, followed by staged debridements that included bilateral thumb amputations at the MCPJ and multiple finger amputations. Wounds were closed with split-thickness skin grafts, and he was discharged.

Despite therapy, he developed contractures requiring first webspace release and a first dorsal metacarpal artery flap. Ten months after injury, once his soft tissues had stabilized and he requested right thumb reconstruction, ipsilateral great toe transfer was performed as described earlier. Postoperative recovery was uncomplicated, but partial donor-site graft loss required repeat grafting 6 weeks later. At 15 months, he had good thumb motion and opposition, with 2PD of 10 mm proximal and 12 mm distal to the interphalangeal joint on the ulnar side, and protective sensation radially.

Initially, he deferred left thumb reconstruction due to concerns about soccer performance after toe harvest and instead underwent metacarpal distraction lengthening, gaining 2 cm. However, he found the reconstructed right thumb far more functional and subsequently elected left great toe transfer at 21 months postinjury. Toe narrowing and nail plate removal were performed during this procedure. Healing was uncomplicated apart from partial donor-site dehiscence, managed with staged grafting.

At 4-year follow-up, he demonstrated key pinch and effective bilateral opposition, with both donor and recipient sites having healed with satisfactory cosmesis (Figs. 1–6). DISCUSSION

Thumb reconstruction via free toe transfer has been extensively described in the literature in multiple reports.16–22 Functional outcomes have demonstrated that thumb reconstruction can allow patients to have excellent functional and aesthetic outcomes. However, there is a paucity of literature that specifically discusses free toe transfer to an electrically burned hand.

Given concerns about the microvascular recipients in the hand and thumb, it is important to delineate the safety of this procedure. Studies have detailed the successful transfer of free flaps containing only soft tissue to the upper extremity and hand for wound closure in patients who have sustained electrical burns. Zhao et al23 described their experience covering electrical burn wounds to the hands and feet with free single-perforator anterolateral thigh flaps. The authors waited until after 1 or 2 debridements of the wound. At the time of free flap reconstruction, they identified inflow via an artery that was at least 3 cm away from the wound margin. They also comment that the artery, in the location where it was planned to perform microanastomosis, was found to have pulsatility and a shiny, healthy-appearing endothelium. All flaps survived in the case series. Another study demonstrating the feasibility of free flap reconstruction after electrical burns details the authors’ experience with lateral tarsal artery flap reconstruction of electrical injuries to the fingers.24 The series of 14 digits in 10 patients discusses anastomosis to the proximal digital artery and vein and reports successful flap transfer in all digits. Finally, Saint-Cyr and Daigle25 documented their experience with electrical burn reconstruction of soft-tissue defects and compared this group to matched controls who underwent skin grafting alone.24 Of the 14 patients who sustained electrical injury, 15 flaps were performed, including lateral arm (6), latissimus dorsi (4), rectus abdominis (1), temporoparietal fascia (2), tensor fascia data (1), and scapular (3) flaps. Twelve of these patients (85.7%) went on to have successful limb salvage.

The transfer of a contralateral upper extremity digit to the electrically burned thumb has also been described in the literature. Giot et al26 described a patient who sustained high-voltage electrical injury resulting in 52% total body surface area that involved the torso and upper extremities and resulted in total left brachial plexopathy and a paralyzed left hand. Further, the patient lost the right thumb at a level just proximal to the MCPJ. He underwent skin grafting for wound closure. In a delayed fashion, a decision was made to proceed with the transfer of the left small finger to the right thumb site. The patient was amenable to this reconstruction and was not willing to transfer his paralyzed left thumb to the right hand for aesthetic reasons. During their procedure, the authors first explored the proximal radial artery and median nerve at the wrist, and then prepared the recipient site for microanastomosis at the anatomical snuffbox. Next, the patient’s left small finger proximal phalanx was transected and transferred to the right thumb site; the small finger proximal interphalangeal joint was to function as the thumb MCPJ, and the small finger distal interphalangeal joint was used to function as the thumb interphalangeal joint.26

Importantly, 3 articles described the transfer of toes to the electrically burned thumb for reconstruction. Kempny et al27 described a great toe wraparound flap that included a small portion of the distal phalanx of the great toe for transfer. The authors did not describe in detail their surgical approach or dissection of the recipient site, but did note that the flap remained alive and well perfused. At 5.5 years after transfer, the patient had poor 2PD (15 mm dynamic, 25 mm static) and no interphalangeal motion, but good grip strength. Yoon and Jones10 published a series that includes a description of free toe transfers to burned digits in pediatric patients. Of the 4 patients included in the study, 2 sustained electrical burns. One 14-year-old male patient required a right second toe transfer for reconstruction of the left thumb. The second patient, a 16-year-old boy, required bilateral great toe transfers for thumb reconstruction. Unfortunately, the report does not give operative details for either of these cases. Finally, the largest series of patients who underwent toe tissue transfer for reconstruction of electrically burned digits includes 15 patients.28 Four of these patients sustained their injury via low current electricity, and the remaining 11 sustained their injury via high current electricity. Seven patients underwent second toe transfer, 4 underwent webspace transfer, 2 underwent free vascularized joint transfer, and 2 underwent toe pulp transfer. All transferred flaps survived with 8–36 months of postoperative follow–up, and patients who underwent toe transfer achieved an average static 2PD of 9.5 mm.

Recent literature has further clarified the optimal timing and technical considerations for microsurgical reconstruction in electrical burn injuries. Several studies have demonstrated that early application of microsurgical flaps—typically within 2–4 weeks of injury—can be safe and effective, provided that meticulous debridement is performed and microvascular anastomosis is carried out in healthy tissue well outside the acute zone of injury. In a 2-institution case series, early microsurgical reconstruction (mean 24 d postinjury) resulted in good flap viability and functional preservation, with all patients avoiding amputation and achieving satisfactory outcomes.29 Similarly, other reports have shown that, with careful intraoperative assessment, early free tissue transfer can be performed with flap survival rates comparable to those in nonburn trauma, provided that the recipient vessels are healthy and the wound bed is adequately prepared.30,31

Recent large cohort studies have identified key predictors of amputation in electrical injuries, including the development of compartment syndrome, rhabdomyolysis, renal failure, and sepsis. These factors should be considered in surgical planning, as they are associated with a higher risk of limb loss. Importantly, the timing of flap-based reconstruction remains critical: although early decompression and serial necrosectomy are essential, delayed-early or secondary reconstruction (after the acute inflammatory phase) is associated with lower flap failure rates, particularly in high-voltage injuries.8,32

Technical advances in flap selection and microsurgical technique have expanded reconstructive options for severe electrical injuries. Free anterolateral thigh flaps, single-perforator flaps, and combined chimeric flaps have all been successfully used for limb salvage in high-voltage injuries, with high rates of flap survival and functional restoration.23,33 These approaches allow for tailored reconstruction based on defect size, location, and the condition of recipient vessels. Furthermore, systematic reviews and large series confirm that vascularized toe transfers—whether for traumatic, congenital, or burn-related thumb loss—consistently achieve high success rates and functional outcomes, with individualized surgical planning recommended to optimize both function and aesthetics.34,35 In summary, the most recent evidence supports the safety and efficacy of microsurgical toe-to-thumb transfer for thumb reconstruction after high-voltage electrical injury, in both adult and pediatric populations.8,10,23,32,33,36–38

Overall, outcomes reported in this case series, specifically in patients who experienced electrical burn injuries, are consistent with those in the published literature on toe-to-thumb transfer more generally. Flap survival in the present series was 100%, which compares favorably with survival rates of 94.7%–100% reported in recent large series.29,35,37,39 Two-point discrimination in this series ranged from 10 to 12 mm, which falls within the published range of 6–15 mm for toe-to-thumb transfers and is comparable to the 14.6 mm average reported by Le et al40 in their series of great toe transfers for proximal thumb reconstruction.29,35,37,39 Del Piñal et al41 reported 2PD of 7–11 mm in partial hallux transfers, whereas Kempny et al42 observed poorer discrimination (15–25 mm) using the twisted-toe technique.40 Grip strength in this series aligns with published data, with Le et al40 reporting grip ranging from 15 to 86 lb in patients achieving opposition. All patients achieved opposition and key pinch, consistent with studies showing that 80%–100% of toe-to-thumb transfers restore these critical functions.29,40,43 Donor-site morbidity was minimal, with only partial graft loss requiring secondary grafting in 1 case, which compares favorably to the literature reporting gait impairment in 23.7% of cases and donor-site complications ranging from mild (47.2%) to major (11.3%). The individualized timing approach is supported by recent evidence emphasizing biological readiness over fixed time intervals following electrical injury.40–42,44

We advocate exploring the recipient site before making any surgical incisions on the foot before toe transfer. This ensures that there is good inflow to allow for flap survival and mitigates the risk of compromising the toe in the setting of an unpredictable zone of injury at the recipient site. Similarly, we advocate for the delay of the free toe transfer until the wound is stable and the patient is outside of the acute inflammatory process that occurs during any burn. Finally, this study demonstrates that an electrical burn should not necessarily be seen as a contraindication to free toe-to-thumb transfer.

CONCLUSIONS

The present article adds to the existing literature supporting that free toe transfer for thumb reconstruction in electrically burned hands is safe, feasible, and can lead to excellent patient outcomes.
