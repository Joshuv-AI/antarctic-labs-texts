Ethnobotanical knowledge of Tharu people living nearby Koshi River and Koshi Tappu Wildlife Reserve (KTWR), eastern Nepal
Authors: Durga Kutal, Shreehari Bhattarai, Dipak Khadka, Sandesh Koirala, Rajesh Tamang, Bishal Ghimire, Ripu Kunwar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMany indigenous and ethnic communities have been displaced from their ancestral homelands and disconnected from their traditional dependencies on forests and rivers as a result of protected area management of Nepal. Ongoing changes in land use, socio-economy and culture pose additional threats to their livelihoods and knowledge systems. This study aims to document the traditional knowledge and ethnobotanical practices of the Tharu ethnic group living in and around the Koshi River and Koshi Tappu Wildlife Reserve (KTWR) in order to assess whether their culture has been influenced by the recent changes, including interventions associated with KTWR management.MethodData were collected from 119 Tharu participants residing in the Jhapa, Morang, and Sunsari districts of Koshi Province. Quantitative ethnobotanical indices were computed to assess the significance and consensus of plant use within the community. Interactions of the Tharu with rivers, forests, and protected areas were also examined.ResultsA total of 2,426 use reports were recorded, documenting 113 useful plant species across 13 use categories. Ficus religiosa emerged as the most frequently cited species with 180 use reports, followed by Ficus benghalensis (156), Piper betle (130), Ocimum sanctum (119), and Mangifera indica (95). All these species were terrestrial and primarily agroforestry-based. Geographically, the highest number of use reports per person (33.16) was recorded in Jhapa district, which is located farthest from both the Koshi River and KTWR.ConclusionThe findings of this study not only document the rich ethnobotanical heritage of the Tharu people but also reveal the vulnerability of this knowledge system to modern pressure and protected area management. The concentration of knowledge among elder healers, the reduced species utilization near KTWR, and the pressures of modernization urge an immediate need for deliberate revitalization of ethnobotanical knowledge. Viable strategies include systematic documentation and intergenerational transmission, community-based biodiversity conservation, and strengthening and restoring traditional cultural practices.Keywords: Ethnoecological knowledge, Protected area, Conservation, Tharu, Koshi River

Introduction

Several studies underscore the vital role of local communities in preserving Indigenous Traditional Knowledge (ITK) and ensuring the sustainable use of valuable plant resources. This has become increasingly urgent as ITK faces growing threats from rapid development, outmigration, modernization, land use changes, and the overexploitation of forest products driven by market demand [27, 42]. In Nepal, traditional ethnobotanical knowledge is particularly vulnerable due to the combined effects of socio-cultural transformation, land use change and climate change [11, 28]. The erosion of this knowledge is further accelerated by weak intergenerational transmission, declining interest among younger generations, and lack of systematic effort in documentation and registration (Widen et al., 2020) [50]. Furthermore, ITK is jeopardized by protected area (PA) management which displaces the indigenous people and relatively restricts their access to the forests and river resources within PA boundaries [24]. Among the 142 caste and ethnic groups in Nepal, the Tharu ethnic group has been particularly affected by such displacement [37, 40].

The Tharu is recognized as one of the indigenous groups native to Nepal’s Tarai region. According to the 2021 national census [4], the Tharu population stands at 1,807,124, accounting for 6.2% of the country’s total population [4]. The term Tharu is believed to derive from two words: “Tha” meaning Tarai, and “Ru” meaning permanent inhabitant, together referring to the original or indigenous people of the Tarai plains. Often referred to as the “Sons of the Forest” [3], the Tharu played a pivotal role in transforming the once-malarial and marshy wetlands of the Tarai into one of Nepal’s most fertile agricultural zones [6]. The Tharu people have resided in Nepal Tarai region for centuries, with historical evidence of their settlement dating back to the 5th − 7th centuries [31]. The Tharu group is internally diverse, comprising several sub-groups - including the Kathariya, Dagaura, Rana, Sonaha, Danuwar, Chitwaniya, and Kochila (also spelled Koshila)- distinguished by subtle variations in dialect and cultural expression. The Kochila Tharu, native to eastern Nepal, is primarily concentrated in the Saptari and Siraha districts, where they are often referred to as the Saptariya Tharu. Krauskopff, [30] noted that the ethnonym Koshila or Kochila may be linked to the Koshi River, along whose banks these groups historically resided.

Until the 1950 s, Nepal’s eastern Tarai was covered by forest with a sparse population consisting mainly of Tharus, who were believed to have strong resistance to malaria. The western Tarai remained similarly densely forested with scattered Tharu settlements until 1963 [36]. Considered indigenous to the Tarai region, the Tharu are believed to be descendants of the population that once lived along the banks of the Koshi and Mechi Rivers (Chatterji, [5]). Their subclans such as the Kochila, which includes the Morangya Tharu, Lamputchwa Tharu and Saptariya Tharu - are thought to be historically associated with settlements across the Koshi River. As these accounts confirm, the Tharu have inhabited the Nepal Tarai for generations, not merely as residents but as active agents who transformed the landscape, converting malarial marshes and forests into fertile agricultural lands [6] while nurturing grasslands through cattle grazing.

Despite their deep-rooted connection to forests and rivers, research on their access to these resources and the indigenous knowledge they possessed remains limited particularly in eastern Nepal. This study aims to fill that gap by documenting the traditional knowledge of the Tharu – a historically significant indigenous ethnic group - residing in the vicinity of Koshi River and Koshi-Tappu Wildlife Reserve (KTWR), specifically in the districts of Jhapa, Morang, and Sunsari. Given the Tharu’s dependence on forests and rivers, we assess whether their cultural practices remain tied to the Koshi River or have been influenced by the management introduced by the KTWR, one of the protected areas of Nepal Tarai. Moreover, we compare the knowledge across geographic areas and the levels of PA management. We hypothesize that ethnobotanical knowledge varies with geographic location, and socio-demographic attributes such as age, gender, education level, and occupation.

Materials and methods

Study sites and ethnic group

We conducted an ethnobotanical study across 15 different locations of three districts; Gauradaha, Saniarjun, Buddhashanti, Mechinagar, Sharnamati, Gaurigunj from Jhapa; Pathari Sanischare, Jhorahaat, Kanchanbari from Morang; and Khanar, Duhabi, Shivanagar, Chatara, Jhumka from Sunsari. To be precise, the sites occur between 26.49 and 26.95 N Latitude, 87.05 and 87.884 E Longitude and 85 and 210 m altitude (Fig. 1). Mechi and Koshi rivers are major rivers of the province. Koshi Tappu Wildlife Reserve, the second most-oldest protected area of Nepal, lies along the Koshi River, comprising floodplain areas of Sunsari and Saptari districts.

Map showing land use pattern and study sites in Koshi province, eastern Nepal

According to CBS [4], the Tharu people made up 4.22% of the total population in Koshi Province, which has 4,961,412 residents. Within the province, Morang and Sunsari districts contributed significantly, with Tharu populations comprising 11.22% and 5.78% of their respective district totals, together accounting for 3.44% of the provincial population. Sunsari district has the largest Tharu population 104,072, followed by Morang 66,403 and Jhapa 11,380 (Table 1). Based on their origin, the Tharu community is categorized into two groups: the Morangiya Tharu, native to Morang, and the Saptariya Tharu, who migrated from Saptari. The Morangiya Tharu are predominantly found in Morang District, especially in areas such as Jorahat, Biratnagar, Netachowk, and Tetariya. The Saptariya Tharu are dispersed across various regions of Morang, Sunsari, and Jhapa districts - for instance, in Morang (Pathari, Sanischare, Baahuni), Sunsari (Chatahara, Jhumka, Itahari), and Jhapa (Gaurigunj, Sharnamati in Jahapa Rural Municipality, and Dhulabari) (Fig. 1). They speak Kochila Tharu language which Glott code is koch1247 (https://glottolog.org/glottolog/language). They follow Hindu religion.

Field visit and data collection

Between April and May 2024, we conducted three field visits - first in Jhapa, followed by Sunsari, and finally in Morang. Each field visit lasted approximately 15 days on average. Across the three districts, a total of 11 focus group discussions were held, and 36 key informant interviews were conducted with Tharu Guruwas, Guraw, and Vrapas - traditional Tharu healers known for treating illnesses through their distinctive three-pronged medicinal system using medicinal herbs, chanting mantras and physiotherapy. A total of 119 individuals - including Tharu traditional healers (7) as well as farmers and plant collectors (74) and traders and others (38) - participated in the survey, with 45 from Jhapa, 26 from Morang, and 48 from Sunsari. There were 59 female and 60 male participants with ages ranging from 17 to 93 years. Most participants belonged to clan Chaudhary (94) followed by Tharu (13), Majhi (9) and Rajbanshi (3). Rajbanshi is the ethnic minority with only 0.42% of total population and native to Jhapa and Morang districts, residing along the Koshi River (Table 1).

After identifying an initial traditional healer or plant collector/trader, snowball sampling was employed [51] to trace and recruit additional peer participants. We followed the Code of Ethics of the International Society of Ethnobiology (http://ethnobiology.net/code-of-ethics/) and obtained informed consent from each participant in accordance with the principles of free, prior, and informed consent. All respondents were informed of the study’s objectives, invited to participate voluntarily, and assured of their anonymity as well as their right to withdraw at any stage. The plant specimens were collected, carefully studied and identified with the help of [45, 46] and deposited at the herbarium of Faculty of Forestry, Agriculture and Forestry University. For the recent nomenclature, Plants of the World Online (POWO) (https://powo.science.kew.org/) and [46] were taken as a reference material.

Data analysis

Free listing and ranking

Free listing was conducted to compile a comprehensive inventory of useful plants and their parts as utilized by the Tharu community across 13 distinct use categories - including cultural, eco-friendly, and ethnic uses; contributions to household economy; associations with taboos; applications in agricultural tools; oil, spice, and spinach plants; as well as religious, ritual, tonic, medicinal, and wild edible fruit uses (adapted from [14, 32, 35]). The variety and usefulness of plant parts or products identified by each respondent were quantified and used as a scoring metric to assess and compare levels of traditional knowledge among participants.

Ethnobotanical indices

To examine the relationship between socioeconomic attributes and use reports, we initially applied regression analysis, Student’s t-test, ANOVA, Kruskal-Walli’s test, post-hoc Tukey’s test, and post-hoc Dunn’s test. We then used a Generalized Linear Model (GLM) to assess the individual effect of each attribute on the utilization of useful species.

Results

Livelihood and culture

Tharu have inhabited Tarai for generations and are regarded as the region’s pioneers, having transformed its marshy, malaria-prone forests into fertile agricultural land. They cultivate rice, lentils, wheat, mustard, and maize, while also gathering forest products like wild fruits, vegetables, medicinal plants, and building materials; in addition, they hunt wild animals and fish in the rivers and oxbow lakes. The Tharu’s way of life is deeply connected to the forests and rivers. They are rich in ethnobotanical knowledge since they have long been collecting food, vegetables, edible plants and medicinal plants for their livelihood for centuries. Collected plants are also meant to rituals, socio-economy and culture.

Use values at species level (FL, UV and ICF)

A number of species exhibited low or statistically insignificant FL and UV values (Fig. 3). Among the 113 useful species, seven were identified as the most valuable based on their high use value (UV) and popularity within the community (FL). Among the seven species, Ficus religiosa, F. benghalensis, Ocimum sanctum, and Piper betle showed the highest values compared to the others. Ficus religiosa was identified as the most widely recognized and utilized species within the community (UV = 1.6; Fig. 3), reflecting its significant cultural, medicinal, and religious importance. Ficus benghalensis was recognized for its broad canopy and medicinal properties, while Ocimum sanctum was revered in Hindu culture and widely used in Tharu rituals and folklore. Piper betle was a commonly used medicinal plant for treating the common cold. The other three species Mangifera indica, Azadirachta indica, and Iresine herbstii were popular despite having relatively lower use values.

The Informant Consensus Factor (ICF) analysis highlights the degree of agreement among community members regarding the use of specific plant species (Fig. 4). The highest consensus (ICF = 0.95) was recorded for eco-friendly plant species such as Ficus religiosa and Ficus benghalensis, reflecting strong communal recognition of their role in promoting environmental sustainability. This was closely followed by spice plants like Piper longum and Mentha species (ICF = 0.94), and by non-timber forest products (NTFPs) such as Piper betle and Bambusa species, which are vital to household economies (ICF = 0.93). Among medicinal use categories, the highest levels of consensus were associated with treatments for respiratory ailments (e.g., cold and cough), skin conditions, and inflammatory issues such as cut and wounds, indicating well-established traditional knowledge in these areas.

Factors influencing plant utilization

Variation in ethnobotanical knowledge was observed across different sites, subclans (e.g., Morangya), and other hierarchical levels, with significant differences also evident at both individual and household scales. A positive association was found between age and the number of use reports (β = 0.093), indicating that older respondents tended to report a higher number of plant uses (Fig. 5). This relationship was statistically significant (p = 0.0352; F-statistic = 4.541). Additionally, a Student’s t-test revealed a significant gender-based difference in use reports within the Tharu community (p = 0.0033; t = 2.99; df = 117), with a 95% confidence interval for the true difference in means ranging from 1.506 to 7.356, suggesting meaningful variation in plant use knowledge between men and women.

Association of plant use reports and socio-economic variables (a) age; (b) gender; (c) education level; (d) occupation with use reports

A one-way ANOVA was conducted to assess differences in use reports among the three districts within the Tharu community. The results revealed a statistically significant variation in use reports across districts (p = 0.00023; F-statistic = 9.006). To further examine pairwise differences, Tukey’s post hoc test was performed. The test indicated significant differences between Jhapa and Morang (p-adjusted = 0.0108) and between Jhapa and Sunsari (p-adjusted = 0.0003). However, no significant difference was observed between Sunsari and Morang (p-adjusted = 0.8915), with the confidence interval including zero, suggesting comparable use report levels between these two districts.

Respondents with primary education reported a moderate range of plant uses (11–25), while those with higher education showed a broader range (11–29), suggesting a potential link between education and knowledge diversity. However, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.181), indicating that education of the respondents alone does not explain variation in ethnobotanical knowledge within the Tharu community.

Healers typically possess the highest level of ethnobotanical knowledge, with use reports (UR) ranging from 46 to 53. In contrast, individuals engaged in agriculture reported the lowest range of use reports (UR 11–25). Since the data did not meet the assumptions of normality, a Kruskal-Wallis test was conducted, revealing a significant difference in use reports among occupational groups (χ² = 26.754, df = 5, p = 6.369 × 10⁻⁵). To determine which specific groups differed, a post hoc analysis using Dunn’s test was performed. The results indicated statistically significant differences between the following pairs: (i) Agriculture vs. Healer (p-adjusted = 0.00003), (ii) Business vs. Healer (p-adjusted = 0.02), (iii) Healer vs. Labour (p-adjusted = 0.0002), (iv) Healer vs. Others (p-adjusted = 0.027), and (v) Healer vs. Service (p-adjusted = 0.043). No significant differences were found among the other occupational groups.

Socio-economic factors and plant utilization

The generalized linear model revealed varying degrees of influence of socioeconomic factors on plant selection and utilization (Table 5). Among these, occupation emerged as a significant determinant: individuals identified as healers reported substantially higher use reports (coefficient = 32.14, SE = 2.04, p < 2 × 10⁻¹⁶), highlighting their deep engagement with ethnobotanical knowledge. These results indicate that occupation - particularly the role of a healer—is the most influential factor in shaping plant use patterns within the Tharu community, whereas gender, age, and education level had comparatively minimal effects.

Discussion

Usefulness of species

This study reveals the community’s profound connection with local plant species, highlighting a rich repository of traditional knowledge and deeply embedded cultural practices. A total of 113 plant species were documented, yielding 2,426 use reports across 13 distinct use categories and illustrating the multifaceted roles of plants in medicine, culture, religion, and cuisine within a community. Among the documented species, Ficus religiosa emerges as the most culturally and functionally significant, with 180 use reports, a high Use Value (UV) of 1.6, and an exceptional Fidelity Level (FL) of 0.97. These values reflect near-universal consensus among informants, affirming the species’ central role in cultural, medicinal, and religious life. Other notably valued species include F. benghalensis, O. sanctum, and P. betle, the latter being particularly associated with warming the body and protecting against the cold. These findings suggest that the Tharu not only utilize these species but also actively nurture them for their cultural and functional significance. The prominent use of F. religiosa, F. benghalensis and P. betle aligns with the findings of Gachhadar [19], Chaudhary and Rai [7], Chaudhary et al., [8], Chaudhary et al., [11], Das et al., [17], and Khanal et al., [29], which similarly report the high cultural and medicinal value attributed to Ficus species among Tharu communities.

Ocimum sanctum, a species commonly cultivated in Hindu households and characterized by high informant consensus, is extensively used in traditional medicine, further illustrating the community’s reliance on local plants for healthcare. This pattern of dependence is reinforced by a substantial body of research; studies by Chaudhary and Rai [7], Dangol and Gurung [16], and Manandhar [39] collectively affirm the enduring significance of medicinal plants and the central role of Tharu traditional medicine in local healthcare systems. Among the species with notable cross-study consensus is A. indica, whose leaves are widely used for skin care across Tharu groups, as documented by Chaudhary and Rai [7], Chaudhary et al., ([8], [11]), Das et al., [17], and Gachhadar [19]. Similarly, M. indica emerges as a highly valued species, providing timber, edible fruit, and ritual materials—a multifaceted significance consistently reported across studies [7, 8, 11, 19, 21]. These examples underscore how certain species, through their diverse and culturally embedded uses, achieve widespread recognition and continued relevance within Tharu communities.

Distribution of ethnobotanical knowledge

Elder Tharu healers, known as Guruwas or Dhami, serve as vital custodians of traditional ethnobotanical knowledge. They recorded the highest number of use reports, averaging 48.86 per person, which reflects their thorough understanding of medicinal plants and familiarity with associated species (Yadav & Chaudhary, [10]; [16]). A statistically significant association between age and use reports (p = 0.002) further confirms that older individuals possess greater experience and skills in identifying and utilizing useful plant species [2, 21]. This finding suggests the critical role of age in shaping the depth and continuity of traditional plant use within the Tharu community, highlighting a dynamic relationship between cultural heritage, ecological knowledge, and generational experience.

The large number of use reports (UR = 2,426) in relation to the documented species pool (113 species) shows intensive multipurpose use indicating that many species serve multiple functions within the community. This trend has been consistently reported in Tharu communities in Chitwan [16], Nawalparasi [10], Kailali [29], and eastern Nepal [6, 7, 19]. Species with the highest Use Value (UV) and Fidelity Level (FL) include Ficus religiosa (UV = 1.6; FL = 89.92%), Ficus benghalensis (UV = 1.37; FL = 82.35%), Ocimum sanctum (UV = 1.05; FL = 69.75%), and Piper betle (UV = 1.15; FL = 61.35%). These closely correlate with priority species found in earlier Tharu ethnobotanical studies [16, 47]. Similar high-fidelity species were noted by Chaudhary et al., [9] in maternal and child healthcare, pointing out that high FL values reflect culturally accepted and specific uses instead of random plant selection.

High Informant Consensus Factor (ICF) values for environmental use (ICF = 0.95), spices (0.94), and non-timber forest products (0.93) are similar to or surpass consensus levels found in earlier studies involving Tharu and related groups [10]. This statistical agreement suggests that plant use knowledge has been validated across generations. However, unlike past studies that focused on riverine areas and reported high consensus for wetland and grassland species [16], this current study notes higher consensus for non-aquatic and agroforestry species. This indicates a shift in use regimes rather than a decline in knowledge.

Regression and multivariate analyses support the trends shown in earlier literature. The positive relationship between age and use reports (β = 0.093, p = 0.035) aligns with findings by Chaudhary and Rai [7] and Khanal et al., [29], confirming that age predicts the depth of ethnobotanical knowledge. The generalized linear model identified occupation—especially being a healer—as the strongest factor influencing plant use (β = 32.14, p < 2 × 10⁻¹⁶). This is consistent with earlier observations that ethnobotanical knowledge is increasingly held by specialists (Kunwar & Bussmann, 2009) [34]. In contrast, gender and education level did not show significant effects, reflecting the findings of Chaudhary et al., [8] and Gachhadar [19]. There is significant variation in use reports at the district level (ANOVA, p = 0.00023), with Jhapa showing the highest UR per person (2.37) and the Sunsari has the lowest (0.98). This contrasts with previous findings from areas near protected sites like Chitwan and Nawalpur, where higher forest reliance was reported [10, 16]. This inverse pattern suggests that being close to protected areas like KTWR may limit the diversity of ethnobotanical practices, redefining rather than diminishing traditional knowledge systems. This pattern is consistent with the off-farm and off-forest livelihood options promoted by KTWR management.

The ethnobotanical selection and use of plant resources reveal significant locality-based variation, despite strong cultural similarities across the study regions. A marked variation in plant use is evident across Jhapa, Morang, and Sunsari, with Jhapa (Saptariya Tharu) recording the highest average use report of 33.16 per person. This elevated level of utilization likely stems from the district’s heterogeneous resource users. Jhapa demonstrates the highest number of use reports across multiple categories, reflecting the current dependence of Tharu more on non-aquatic and agroforestry plant species. The district is also home to over 100 ethnic groups, collectively using around 200 medicinal plant species for ethnomedical purposes [33] and sharing diverse knowledge amongst them. Interestingly, Jhapa also has the smallest Tharu population among the three districts studied, only 11,380 individuals according to the 2021 census making it an ethnically diverse region that fosters a mosaic of traditional knowledge systems [21]. The richness of ethnobotanical knowledge in Jhapa may also be influenced by factors such as infrastructural development, cross-border interactions, and tourism, which expose local communities, including the Tharu, to a broader spectrum of plant uses and knowledge systems [27].

Tharu community and culture

As development progressed, the Kochila Tharu gradually migrated westward, a movement facilitated by extensive deforestation and new settlement opportunities in western Nepal during and after the mid 1960 s [36]. The movement was intensified by the government-sponsored resettlement programs and widespread unregulated migration which led to increased settlement in the Tarai and the subsequent displacement of many Tharu communities [49]. However, their migration within Nepal was often constrained by the country’s three major rivers namely the Koshi, Gandaki, and Karnali – which acted as natural barriers. Consequently, the cultural and sociological identity of the Tharu has been profoundly shaped by the natural landscape, including rivers, forests, and agriculture and grasslands [30]. River- and forest-based livelihoods remain common among Tharu communities to this day [12].

Moreover, the establishment of the Koshi-Tappu Wildlife Reserve (KTWR) in 1978 resulted in restricted access to forests and rivers and relocation of approximately 12,000 local people, disproportionately affected the culture of indigenous Tharu people [18]. Access to the large tall grasses such as Saccharum spp (used for thatching roofs), Phragmites spp (used for fencing and walling houses) and Typha spp (used for weaving mats), and the mixed forests of Acacia-Dalbergia and Bombax species were limited [15]. A similar displacement of Tharu communities followed the establishment of the Chitwan National Park in Chitwan in 1974 [40]. As a result, numerous Tharu communities lost their ancestral lands, traditional customs came under threat, and the once-dense forests and river systems began to degrade [23]. In an effort to mitigate the conflict and grievance, the KTWR introduced various interventions and incentives encouraging the Tharu people to adopt off-farm- and off-forest-based livelihood options. Yet, as a direct result, the traditional practices of collecting and using natural products for cultural and subsistence needs have gradually faded. Today, factors such as human outmigration, land abandonment, urban expansion, population pressure, and unsustainable resource exploitation further complicate the sustenance of ethnobotanical knowledge [20, 47, 48]. The greatest risk is not merely the loss of individual practices but also a gradual erosion of an entire knowledge system that has sustained both the local livelihood and the unique biodiversity of the Koshi floodplain for centuries. As evidence, the least use reports and useful species per person were reported by Saptariya Tharu in Sunsari (the closest district to the KTWR). Revitalizing the Tharu and their traditional wisdom is not just an act of cultural preservation; it is an invaluable asset for the future conservation of rivers, forests, and protected areas alike. Viable strategies include systematic documentation and intergenerational transmission, community-based biodiversity conservation, and strengthening and restoring traditional cultural practices.

Compared to other regions, the eastern side of the Koshi River has easier access to infrastructure, roads, and public services. This accessibility may paradoxically both enable knowledge sharing through exposure to diverse groups and threaten traditional practices through accelerated modernization. As a result, the Tharu group in this region referred to as Purbiya Tharu appear to be more adaptive and culturally assimilated due to ongoing modernization and socio-economic transformation. Amidst changes, use of Typha leaves for weaving mats and household items, Trapa quadrispinosa as an edible food source, and Tamilnadia uliginosa in traditional medicine remains common, supporting local economies and cultural practices [17, 21]. This reflects a rich understanding of wild fruits and vegetables derived from local ecosystems, particularly wetlands and swamps [11, 38, 41]. The Tharu’s ability to utilize a wide array of locally available species—along with their integration of non-native plants such as Ageratum conyzoides, Persea americana, and Aloe vera—demonstrates remarkable adaptive capacity. Similar patterns of extensive non-native plant use have been documented in KTWR [21] by Saptariya Tharu. However, the sustainability of these cultural and ethnobotanical traditions is increasingly challenged by restricted access to KTWR, changed vegetation (risk posed by non-native species), knowledge concentrated among the elderly people, disinterest of younger generation [20], and lack of systematic documentation of traditional knowledge [1].

Implications for bioprospecting and conservation

The high use values, fidelity levels, and informant consensus factors recorded in this study demonstrate that Tharu ethnobotanical knowledge is adaptive, nature-friendly and empirically validated. However, the strong link between knowledge, age, and healer occupation points to the increasing concentration of knowledge among elderly specialists. This highlights the need for urgent documentation and passing knowledge down to younger generations. Species with consistently high use value, fidelity levels, and informant consensus factors, such as Ficus religiosa, Ficus benghalensis, Ocimum sanctum, and Piper betle, should be prioritized for conservation as the species are frequently reported by studies as important. These species are culturally important and used sustainably, especially in agroforestry and community forestry systems. Additionally, high-consensus medicinal and spice species are good candidates for bioprospecting. Their consistent validation by informants offers a strong and ethical foundation for studying their chemical and medicinal properties, as long as fair profit-sharing agreements are in place.

Conclusions

This study reveals the deep relationship between the Tharu ethnic group and their surrounding plant biodiversity, documenting the cultural and ethnobotanical significance of 113 plant species. Among these, Ficus religiosa, Ficus benghalensis, Piper longum, Piper betle, and Ocimum sanctum emerged as the most important, reflecting their central role in the Tharu’s daily life, particularly in cultural, religious, and healing practices. These terrestrial and agroforestry-based species exhibited the highest plant use values, fidelity levels and use reports. Across the three districts studied, Jhapa - located farthest from both the KTWR and Koshi River - recorded the highest average number of use reports per person (33.16). The frequent use of non-aquatic and agroforestry-based plant species in both Jhapa and Sunsari indicates a shift away from riverine and wetland resources. The Tharu’s capacity to utilize diverse locally available species including non-native plants such as Ageratum conyzoides, Persea americana, and Aloe vera, demonstrates remarkable adaptability. However, the increased reliance on non-native species, partly driven by the interventions introduced by the protected area management system, may pose serious risks to biodiversity and traditional knowledge. Furthermore, the sustainability of these cultural and ethnobotanical traditions is threatened by knowledge concentrated among the elderly people, declining youth interest towards ITK, restricted access to KTWR, and lack of systematic documentation. Viable revitalization strategies include systematic documentation and intergenerational knowledge transmission, community-based biodiversity conservation, and restoration of traditional cultural practices.
