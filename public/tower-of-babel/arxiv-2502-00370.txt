Modelling car-following dynamics with stochastic input-state-output port-Hamiltonian systems
Julia Ackermann 1 , Matthias Ehrhardt 1 , Thomas Kruse 1 and Antoine Tordeux 2

Abstract
In this contribution, we introduce a general class of car-following models with an input-state-output port-Hamiltonian structure. We derive stability conditions and long-term behavior of the finite system with periodic boundaries and quadratic interaction potential by spectral analysis and using asymptotic properties of multivariate Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes. The uncontrolled dynamics exhibit instability and random collective behavior under stochastic perturbations. By implementing an open-loop speed control, the system stabilizes and weakly converges to Gaussian limit distributions. The convergence is unconditional for constant speed control. However, a stability condition arises for the closed-loop system where the speed control acts as a dynamic feedback depending on the distance ahead. The results are illustrated by numerical simulations. Interestingly, only the closed-loop system is able to reproduce, at least transiently, realistic stop-and-go behavior that can be resolved using the Hamiltonian component of the model.

Introduction

Port-Hamiltonian systems (PHS) have become a fundamental framework for modeling and analyzing complex physical systems
due to their ability to incorporate energy-based structures and dissipation phenomena.
This mathematical paradigm has been widely applied in domains such as robotics, electrical circuits, and thermodynamics, where energy conservation and exchange are central to system dynamics
[
1
]
.
Inspired by recent results of Knorn et al.
[
2
]
and Matei et al.
[
3
]
,
we have formulated a general class of stochastic car-following models with an input-state-output port-Hamiltonian structure
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
.
This port-Hamiltonian formulation introduces a new modeling paradigm for the analysis of car-following dynamics.

In this work, we present a novel class of stochastic car-following models formulated within the input-state-output port-Hamiltonian framework.
Our approach builds on earlier studies, particularly the car-following dynamics explored by Bando et al.
[
7
]
and Jiang et al.
[
8
]
based on
optimal velocity functions
.
However, in contrast to classical approaches where the interaction is totally asymmetric, the port-Hamiltonian car-following models include both vehicles in front and behind in the interaction.
By leveraging the PHS formulation, we systematically incorporate noise and external control inputs into the car-following dynamics,
thereby enabling a comprehensive analysis of both uncontrolled and controlled traffic systems.
This extension provides new insights into stability properties and emergent behaviors such as stop-and-go waves,
which are currently observed in real-world traffic and in experiments
[
9
,
10
]
.

The proposed models do not claim to provide a more realistic description of driver behavior.
However, they extend previous research by introducing a rigorous mathematical formulation of car-following dynamics as multivariate stochastic differential equations with periodic boundary conditions.
Notably, the Hamiltonian framework enables us to analyze the asymptotic behavior of these systems,
leveraging spectral theory and properties of multivariate Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) processes.
The stochastic nature of the model captures realistic fluctuations in driver behavior,
such as delays and variability in acceleration, which are critical for reproducing observed traffic phenomena.

In uncontrolled systems, the absence of active stabilization mechanisms leads to instability and collective random behavior characterized by the divergence of the ensemble’s mean velocity.
By contrast, open-loop control stabilizes the system, ensuring weak convergence to Gaussian limit distributions under constant speed control.
Moreover, closed-loop systems, where the speed control dynamically depends on the distance to the preceding vehicle,
exhibit richer behaviors, including transient stop-and-go patterns.
These observations align with empirical findings and highlight the robustness of the PHS-based approach to modeling complex traffic dynamics.

Our study is closely related to recent advancements in adaptive cruise control systems and their mathematical underpinnings
[
11
,
12
]
.
We show that the Hamiltonian structure not only simplifies the analysis of stability conditions but also enables the systematic design of control laws to mitigate traffic instabilities.
In particular, we derive exact stability criteria for closed-loop systems, demonstrating that increasing the Hamiltonian component or control parameters improves system stability.
These results are complemented by numerical simulations, which illustrate key behaviors and validate the theoretical predictions.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we introduce the stochastic port-Hamiltonian car-following model and outline its mathematical formulation.
Section
3
analyzes the asymptotic behavior of uncontrolled, open-loop, and closed-loop systems, with a focus on stability and convergence properties.
Numerical simulations in Section
4
validate the theoretical findings and provide insights into transient and long-term behaviors.

2
Stochastic port-Hamiltonian car-following model

2.1
Notations

Consider
N
vehicles on a segment of length
L
with periodic boundaries.
The positions of the
N
vehicles at time
t\geq 0
are denoted by
q_{1}(t),\dots,q_{N}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
while their speeds are the variables
p_{1}(t),\dots,p_{N}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
.
Assume that the vehicles are initially ordered by their indices, i.e.,

0\leq q_{1}(0)<q_{2}(0)<\ldots<q_{N}(0)\leq L
.
The distances and the speed differences to the preceding vehicle are given by

\Delta q_{n}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}q_{n+1}(t)-q_{n}(t),&&n<N,\\
L+q_{1}(t)-q_{N}(t),&&n=N,\end{array}\right.

(1)

and

\Delta p_{n}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}p_{n+1}(t)-p_{n}(t),&&n<N,\\
p_{1}(t)-p_{N}(t),&&n=N,\end{array}\right.

(2)

respectively.

2.2
Car-following model

A general symmetric car-following model in one dimension is given by

\left\{\begin{aligned} ~\mathrm{d}q_{n}(t)&=p_{n}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,\\[1.42262pt]
~\mathrm{d}p_{n}(t)&=\Bigl[\gamma\bigl[u_{n}(t)-p_{n}(t)\bigr]+\beta\bigl[\Delta p_{n}(t)-\Delta p_{n-1}(t)\bigr]\\[-1.42262pt]
&\qquad\quad+\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\bigl(\Delta q_{n}(t)\bigr)-\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\bigl(\Delta q_{n-1}(t)\bigr)\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\,\mathrm{d}W_{n}(t),\end{aligned}\right.

(3)

where

•

u_{n}
is the input component that acts as a speed control with relaxation rate
\gamma\geq 0
.
It is an external control parameter for open-loop systems, or can be state-dependent and acts as a feedback control for closed-loop systems.

•

\beta\geq 0
is the speed alignment rate with the nearest neighbors and corresponds to the dissipative part of the PHS.

•

\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
is the derivative of a distance-based (isotropic) convex interaction potential
\mathcal{U}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R},[0,\infty))
, which allows collisions to be avoided.
It provides the potential energy in the Hamiltonian and, together with the kinematic relations, the skew-symmetric structure of the PHS.

•

W=\bigl(W_{n}\bigr)_{n=1}^{N}\colon[0,\infty)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
is an
N
-dimensional standard Brownian motion on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
that models noise in the dynamics with volatility
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
.

2.3
Port-Hamiltonian formulation

With
z=(\Delta q,p)^{\top}
, the port-Hamiltonian formulation is given by the stochastic input-state-output system

\mathrm{d}z(t)=\bigl(J-R\bigr)\nabla\mathcal{H}\bigl(z(t)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+U\bigl(z(t)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\Sigma\,\mathrm{d}W(t),

(4)

where the Hamiltonian operator is given by

\mathcal{H}\binom{\Delta q}{p}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}p_{n}^{\top}p_{n}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{U}(\Delta q_{n}),

(5)

and where

A=\begin{bmatrix}-1&1&\\[-2.84526pt]
&\ddots&\ddots\\[0.0pt]
1&&-1~\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},\quad J=\begin{bmatrix}0&A\\
-A^{\top}&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 2N}\;\text{skew symmetric},

U(z)=\bigl[0,\gamma u(z)\bigr]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N},\quad R=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&\beta A^{\top}A+\gamma I_{N}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 2N}\;\text{positive semidefinite},

u\colon\mathbb{R}^{2N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the input control,

\Sigma=\bigl[0,\sigma I_{N}\bigr]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times N}
is the noise volatility.

The study focuses on the control terms
\gamma
and
u
, which seem to play a dominant role in the dynamics.
We successively consider the uncontrolled case where
\gamma=0
before considering the cases
\gamma>0
where, in an open-loop framework,

u_{n}
is constant and equal for all the agents and where, in a closed-loop framework,

u_{n}
is a feedback controller that depends linearly on the gap between the vehicles.

3
Long-term behavior

3.1
Uncontrolled system

Without control, i.e., when
\gamma=0
, the system is Hamiltonian dissipative

\mathrm{d}z(t)=\bigl(J-R\bigr)\nabla\mathcal{H}\bigl(z(t)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\Sigma\,\mathrm{d}W(t).

(6)

The noise plays the role of the inputs in the port-Hamiltonian formulation.
In this case, the stochastic system does not converge to a limiting distribution
[
4
]
.
In fact, thanks to the telescopic form of the model and the periodic boundaries,
we can quickly observe that the ensemble’s mean velocity

\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.1pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-1.00006pt$p$\kern-1.00006pt}}}\,(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}p_{n}(t),\quad t\in[0,\infty),

(7)

is a diverging Brownian motion for any potential
\mathcal{U}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R},[0,\infty))
.
For
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
, we consider in the sequel the quadratic distance-based potential
\mathcal{U}(x)=(\alpha x)^{2}/2
.
The Hamiltonian and its gradient then are

\mathcal{H}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\|p\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\|\Delta q\|^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla\mathcal{H}(z)=\bigl[\alpha^{2}\Delta q,p\bigr]^{\top},

while the system is the multidimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck stochastic process

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}z(t)=Bz(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\Sigma\,\mathrm{d}W(t),

(8)

where

B=\begin{bmatrix}0&A\\
-\alpha^{2}A^{\top}&-\beta A^{\top}A\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 2N}\quad\text{and}\quad\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\sigma I_{N}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times N}.

(9)

Each block
N\times N
of
B
is circulant and we have for
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
the determinant

|B-\lambda I_{2N}|=|C|\quad\text{with}\quad C=\lambda^{2}I_{N}+\lambda\beta A^{\top}A+\alpha^{2}A^{\top}A.

(10)

The matrix
C\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is also circulant. Since the determinant of a circulant matrix

C=\begin{bmatrix}c_{0}&c_{N-1}&\ldots&c_{1}\\
c_{1}&c_{0}&\ldots&c_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
c_{N-1}&c_{N-2}&\ldots&c_{0}\end{bmatrix}

is the product

|C|=\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}\bigl[c_{0}+c_{N-1}\omega^{j}+c_{N-2}\omega^{2j}+\dots+c_{1}\omega^{(N-1)j}\bigr],

where
\omega=\exp\big({2\pi\mathrm{i}/N}\big)
,
the characteristic equation of the matrix
B
is

\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}\bigl[\lambda^{2}+\lambda\beta\mu_{j}+\alpha^{2}\mu_{j}\bigr]=0,\quad\text{with}\quad\mu_{j}=2-2\cos\biggl(\frac{2\pi j}{N}\biggr)\in[0,4].

(11)

From this we can directly compute the eigenvalues of
B
.
The real parts of the
2(N-1)
eigenvalues for
j=1,\ldots,N-1
are non-positive.
However, the two zero eigenvalues for
j=0
make the process
(z(t))_{t\geq 0}
diverging
[
4
]
.

The deviation from the ensemble’s mean velocity

p_{n}(t)-\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.1pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-1.00006pt$p$\kern-1.00006pt}}}\,(t)=\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{N}\Bigr)p_{n}(t)-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\neq n}p_{k}(t),\qquad t\geq 0,\quad n=1,\ldots,N,

(12)

gives rise to
the
\mathbb{R}^{2N}
-valued process
(x(t))_{t\geq 0}
given by
x(t)=\bigl(\Delta q(t),Mp(t)\bigr)^{\top}

where
M\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is the deviation from the ensemble’s mean velocity metric. It
follows the dynamics

\mathrm{d}x(t)=Bx(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\sigma M\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}W(t).

(13)

Here, the noise averaging
\sigma M
causes the deviation process
(x(t))_{t\geq 0}
to converge
[
4
]
.
In summary, the mean speed of the uncontrolled vehicles diverges as a Brownian motion. Interestingly, however, the variance of the vehicle speeds converges.

3.2
Open-loop system

With a constant input speed control, i.e.,
\gamma>0
and
u_{n}\equiv x\in\mathbb{R}
for all
n\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, the system is a stochastic input-state-output port-Hamiltonian system.
In contrast to the previous model without control, the ensemble’s mean velocity (
7
) converges.
In fact, thanks to the model’s telescopic form and periodic boundaries, the mean velocity follows an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process for any potential function
\mathcal{U}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R},[0,\infty))
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.1pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-1.00006pt$p$\kern-1.00006pt}}}\,(t)=\gamma\bigl(x-\,\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.1pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-1.00006pt$p$\kern-1.00006pt}}}\,(t)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{\sigma}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathrm{d}W_{n}(t),\quad t\in[0,\infty).

(14)

In addition, the ensemble’s mean velocity (
7
) relaxes to
x
for the deterministic system with
\sigma=0
.

We consider the dynamics of the difference to the controlled velocity
x
to deal with a linear system

\tilde{p}_{n}(t)=p_{n}(t)-x,\quad t\in[0,\infty),\;n\in\{1,\ldots,N\}.

(15)

The process
(\Delta q,\tilde{p})^{\top}
with the quadratic potential
\mathcal{U}(x)=(\alpha x)^{2}/2
is the multidimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process (
8
) where

B=\begin{bmatrix}0&A\\
-\alpha^{2}A^{\top}&-\beta A^{\top}A-\gamma I_{N}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 2N}\quad\text{and}\quad\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\sigma I_{N}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times N}.

(16)

Each block
N\times N
of
B
is circulant, and we obtain the characteristic equation of the open-loop system

\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}\bigl[\lambda^{2}+\lambda\bigl(\beta\mu_{j}+\gamma\bigr)+\alpha^{2}\mu_{j}\bigr]=0,\quad\mu_{j}=2-2\cos\left(2\pi j/N\right)\in[0,4].

(17)

From this we can directly compute the eigenvalues of
B
.
The system is truncated due to the periodic boundaries (the sum of the distances is systematically
L
at all times) and one eigenvalue for
j=0
is zero.
However, all the remaining eigenvalues
\lambda
have
negative
real parts.
This makes the open-loop system unconditionally stable and convergent
[
5
]
.

3.3
Closed-loop system

More realistic models, related to optimal speed models in traffic engineering
[
7
,
8
]
,
have a feedback control

u_{n}\equiv f(\Delta q_{n})
with
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})

where the control speed depends on the distance ahead.
They can describe complex instability phenomena such as stop-and-go waves
[
9
,
6
]
.
In addition, such a modeling component allows to include a constant time gap parameter
T>0
for linear functions
f
, which is the following strategy observed in real traffic, recommended by international standards
[
11
]
, and used to design adaptive cruise control systems
[
12
]
.
The state-dependent input control plays the role of a feedback in the dynamics.
However, in contrast to the constant speed control
[
5
]
,
the system is no longer unconditionally stable.
The stochastic car-following model is

\left\{\begin{aligned} ~\mathrm{d}q_{n}(t)&=p_{n}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,\\[1.42262pt]
~\mathrm{d}p_{n}(t)&=\Bigl[{\gamma\bigl[f\bigl(\Delta q_{n}(t)\bigr)-p_{n}(t)\bigr]}+{\beta\bigl[\Delta p_{n}(t)-\Delta p_{n-1}(t)\bigr]}\\[-2.84526pt]
&\qquad\quad+{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\bigl(\Delta q_{n}(t)\bigr)-\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\bigl(\Delta q_{n-1}(t)\bigr)}\Bigr]\mathrm{d}t+{\sigma\,\mathrm{d}W_{n}(t)}.\end{aligned}\right.

(18)

Consider the quadratic interaction potential
\mathcal{U}(x)=(\alpha x)^{2}/2
, the linear feedback
f(x)=(x-\ell)/T
, with
T>0
the time gap and
\ell\geq 0
the vehicle size, and the perturbed system with

\tilde{p}_{n}(t)=p_{n}(t)+\ell/T,\quad t\in[0,\infty),~~n\in\{1,\ldots,N\}.

Then the closed-loop system is the multidimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process (
8
)
where

B=\begin{bmatrix}0&A\\
\frac{\gamma}{T}I_{N}-\alpha^{2}A^{\top}&-\beta A^{\top}A-\gamma I_{N}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 2N}\quad\text{and}\quad\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\sigma I_{N}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times N}.

(19)

Here again, each block
N\times N
of
B
is circulant and we obtain the characteristic equation

\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}\bigl[\lambda^{2}+\lambda(\beta\mu_{j}+\gamma)+\alpha^{2}\mu_{j}+\frac{\gamma}{T}(1-\omega^{j})\bigr]=0,\quad~~\left|\begin{array}[]{l}\mu_{j}=2-2\cos\left(2\pi j/N\right),\\[2.84526pt]
\omega=\exp(2\pi\mathrm{i}/N).\end{array}\right.

(20)

From this we can directly compute the eigenvalues of
B
:
\lambda_{0,0}=0
and
\lambda_{0,1}=-\gamma
for
j=0
and

\lambda_{j,k}=\frac{1}{2}\bigg[-(\beta\mu_{j}+\gamma)+(-1)^{k}\sqrt{(\beta\mu_{j}+\gamma)^{2}-4\Bigr(\alpha^{2}\mu_{j}+\frac{\gamma}{T}(1-\omega^{j})\Bigr)}\,\bigg]

(21)

for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,N-1\}
and
k\in\{0,1\}
.

For simplicity, we introduce

c_{j}=\cos(2\pi j/N)
,

s_{j}=\sin(2\pi j/N)
and

\left|~\begin{aligned} \kappa_{j}&=2\beta(1-c_{j})+\gamma,\\
\eta_{j}&=0,\end{aligned}\right.\qquad\left|~\begin{aligned} \nu_{j}&=(1-c_{j})\bigl(\textstyle\frac{\gamma}{T}+2\alpha^{2}\bigr),\\
\rho_{j}&=-\textstyle\frac{\gamma}{T}s_{j}.\end{aligned}\right.

(22)

Recall that the roots of the 2nd order polynomial
\lambda_{j}^{2}+(\kappa_{j}+\mathrm{i}\eta_{j})\lambda_{j}+\nu_{j}+\mathrm{i}\rho_{j}=0
satisfy
\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_{j})<0
if and only if
[
13
, Th. 3.2]

\kappa_{j}>0\quad\text{and}\quad\det\begin{bmatrix}\kappa_{j}&0&-\rho_{j}\\
1&\nu_{j}&-\eta_{j}\\
0&\rho_{j}&\kappa_{j}\end{bmatrix}=\kappa_{j}\left(\nu_{j}\kappa_{j}+\rho_{j}\eta_{j}\right)-\rho_{j}^{2}>0.

(23)

We obtain the exact stability conditions

\displaystyle\gamma>0,\quad\bigl(2\beta(1-c_{j})+\gamma\bigr)^{2}\,\Bigl(\frac{\gamma}{T}+2\alpha^{2}\Bigr)-\Bigl(\frac{\gamma}{T}\Bigr)^{2}\bigl(1+c_{j}\bigr)>0,\quad j\in\{1,\ldots,N-1\},

(24)

which simplify to the sufficient conditions

\gamma>0,\quad~~\gamma T+2(\alpha T)^{2}>2.

(25)

Increasing the Hamiltonian component
\alpha
, the time gap
T
, or the control rate
\gamma
improves the stability.
In addition, the speed alignment parameter has only a small effect.
