On a connection between total positivity and Bernoulli stopping problems
Zakaria Derbazi

Abstract
Consider a discrete-time optimal selection problem where one observes a sequence of independent Bernoulli trials and receives a nonnegative reward upon stopping on a success. The objective is to find a single-choice strategy that maximises the expected payoff. These Bernoulli stopping problems are characterised by two key properties: (i) a recurrence relation connecting the reward sequence to the continuation payoff sequence, and (ii) the total positivity of the Markov chain embedded in success epochs of the trials. The recurrence is fundamental in proving the optimality of the myopic strategy under unimodal continuation payoff sequence. On the other hand, the total positivity ensures that the expectation of a quasi-unimodal function of the chain remains quasi-unimodal with respect to the initial state. In particular, if the number of successes is finite almost surely, the quasi-unimodality of the reward sequence is sufficient for the myopic rule to be optimal. Illustrative examples are given in various last-success settings.

Our goal in this work is to revisit the fundamental question of optimality of the myopic strategy, particularly in the infinite-horizon setting with quasi-unimodal payoffs. A quasi-unimodal sequence is one that may or may not attain its supremum and becomes unimodal once it is truncated. This investigation is motivated in part by analysing the intrinsic Markovian structure of Bernoulli stopping problems and extending existing results to the case of infinite number of trials. In addition, we aim to study the properties of the transition operator that links stopping rewards to continuation payoffs. This connection is explored through the theory of total positivity, which has found successful applications in many areas of mathematics

When the property

Transformations involving sign-regular matrices are characterised by the

To verify a unimodality-preserving property in Markov processes with a stochastic monotone transition operator,

combined the theory of total positivity with the properties of stochastically monotone matrices—first introduced by
[

]
and later generalised by
[

Even in the countably finite case, the method hinges on stochastic monotonicity of the transition matrix. Consequently, an alternative approach is needed to determine when a stochastic matrix preserves (quasi-)unimodality. It turns out that
\textrm{TP}_{3}
stochastic matrices preserve quasi-unimodality unconditionally.

The structure of the paper is as follows: Section 2 introduces key properties of unimodal sequences and shows that transformations induced by

\textrm{TP}_{3}
stochastic matrices preserve unimodality for finite sequences and quasi-unimodality for infinite sequences. This result finds natural application in the classical embedding of success epochs of independent Bernoulli trials in a Markov chain. Specifically, it is shown that the expectation of unimodal functions of the chain is quasi-unimodal in the initial state. Since the continuation payoff sequence is a mapping of the stopping reward sequence via the transition matrix of this chain,
Section 3 builds on the previous findings to derive a recurrence relation linking continuation payoffs and stopping rewards. It then establishes a sufficient condition for the optimality of the myopic strategy, based on the unimodality of the continuation payoffs or the quasi-unimodality of the stopping rewards. Finally, Section 4 applies these results to generalised last-success problems, providing new and simpler proofs of the myopic rule’s optimality.

Transformations Preserving Quasi-Unimodality

First, we introduce several key concepts used throughout this paper.

Definition 2.1

(Minor sign)
.

Given a sign-regular matrix, its minor sign of order
k\geq 1
is the number
\varepsilon_{k}\in\{-1,1\}
satisfying
\varepsilon_{k}d_{k}\geq 0
, where

denotes any determinant of order

Definition 2.2

(Number of sign changes)
.

The number of sign changes in a finite or infinite sequence
u\coloneqq(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,)
is defined by

S(u)\coloneqq S(u^{\prime}_{1},u^{\prime}_{2},\ldots)=\sum_{i\geq 1}\left\lvert\sgn(u^{\prime}_{i+1})-\sgn(u^{\prime}_{i})\right\rvert,\qquad S(0,0,\ldots,0)=0,

where
u^{\prime}_{1},u^{\prime}_{2},\ldots
is the sequence

with all zero terms removed.

Definition 2.3

(Unimodal sequence)
.

A real sequence
u\coloneqq(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots)
supported on the lattice of positive integers is said to be

unimodal
or modal of order one, if there exists an
interval of modes

[k^{-},k^{+}]\subset\mathbb{N}
such that

\begin{cases}u_{j}\leq u_{j+1}&~\text{all }~j<k^{-},\\
u_{j}=u_{j+1}&~\text{all }~k^{-}\leq j<k^{+},\\
u_{j}\geq u_{j+1}&~\text{all }~j\geq k^{+}\end{cases}.

(3)

A more practical characterisation of unimodality is given next.

Lemma 2.4

(
[
5
]
)
.

A sequence
u:I\subseteq\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
of two or more elements is unimodal iff

(i)

For every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
u-\lambda
has at most two sign changes and whenever two sign changes occur, their pattern is
-,+,-
.

(ii)

u
attains its supremum.

Condition (ii), which always holds for finite sequence, implies

possesses a nonincreasing tail. That is, there exists an integer

such that

for all

. When

, we set

.

Many infinite sequences—such as the infinite nondecreasing sequences

—do not attain a maximum. We refer to such sequences as

Definition 2.5

(Quasi-unimodal sequence)
.

A real sequence on
\mathbb{N}
is quasi-unimodal if it satisfies condition (i) of Lemma
2.4
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Every unimodal real sequence is quasi-unimodal, and every truncated quasi-unimodal real sequence is unimodal.

Proof.

The first assertion is immediate from the definitions. The second follows from the fact that any finite sequence attains its supremum.

Consider a Bernoulli stopping problem with nonnegative stopping reward and continuation payoff sequences
f
and
g
, respectively. Assume the hypotheses of Theorem
3.1
hold. A sufficient condition for the myopic strategy to be optimal is that the ratio
g/f
(or equivalently, the difference
g-f
) is nonincreasing.

Proof.

At stage
k\in\mathbb{N}
, if
f_{k}=0
, then stopping is not optimal, since we can do at least as well by continuing one more step. Consequently, we define both
g_{k}/f_{k}
and
g_{k}-f_{k}
to be
+\infty
when
f_{k}=0
even if
g_{k}=0
. The myopic policy calls for stopping at stage
k
if
g_{k}-f_{k}\leq 0
, which is equivalent to
g_{k}/f_{k}\leq 1
. Define the events
B_{k}\coloneqq\{X_{k}=1\text{ and }g_{k}-f_{k}\leq 0\}=\{X_{k}=1\text{ and }g_{k}/f_{k}\leq 1\}.
Recall that the problem is monotone
[
3
]
if
B_{1}\subset B_{2}\subset B_{3}\subset\cdots\text{ a.s.}

By assumption,
g_{k}/f_{k}\leq g_{k+1}/f_{k+1}
for all
k
, so
g_{k+1}/f_{k+1}\leq 1
implies
g_{k}/f_{k}\leq 1
. Likewise, since
g_{k}-f_{k}\leq g_{k+1}-f_{k+1}
holds for all
k
, the condition
g_{k+1}\leq f_{k+1}
implies
g_{k}\leq f_{k}
.
Thus, the sequence
(B_{k})
is absorbing, and the myopic strategy is optimal.
∎

Theorem 1 in
[
9
]
is a special case of the following corollary.

Corollary 3.4
.

Suppose there exists a scalar
w\in[0,1)
and a real sequence
h\coloneqq(h_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
g=h+wf
. A sufficient condition for the myopic strategy to be optimal is that the ratio
h/f
is nonincreasing.

Proof.

If
w\geq 1
, stopping is never optimal since
f<g
, justifying the constraint on
w
. The result follows immediately from Theorem
3.3
, since
\Delta\displaystyle\frac{g}{f}=\Delta\displaystyle\frac{h}{f}
, where
\Delta
is the difference operator.
∎

3.3
Approximation of the infinite-horizon problem

Suppose there exists an optimal rule
\tau
with optimal value
V^{*}
. Define its truncated form by
\tau(n)=\min\{\tau,n\}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
with value
V^{*}_{n}
. One approach to tackle the infinite-horizon problem is to solve its truncated version with
n
trials then show that
\tau=\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau(n)
and
V^{*}=\lim_{n\to\infty}V^{*}_{n}
. The topic is treated extensively in Chapter 5 of Ferguson’s monograph
[
8
]
. This section provides details specific to the Bernoulli stopping case and discusses the conditions under which the approximation approach is valid.

We begin by verifying a uniform integrability condition, which will play a central role in the proofs.

Definition 3.5

(Uniform integrability
[
3
,
8
]
)
.

Let
T\coloneqq(T_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be a sequence of real random variables. If either of the following holds:

1.

\lim_{a\to\infty}\sup\mathbb{E}\left[\1\{\left\lvert T_{n}\right\rvert>a\}\right]=0,

2.

\sup\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert T_{n}\right\rvert\right]<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\1\{A_{n}\}\left\lvert T_{n}\right\rvert\right]\to 0
as
n\to 0
, for any
A_{n}
such that
\mathbb{P}(A_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
,

then
T
is
uniformly integrable
.

Define the sequence of random variables
T\coloneqq(T_{k})
, where

T_{k}\coloneqq\sup_{j\geq k}\{Y_{j}-Y_{k}\},

(9)

and, as before,
Y_{k}=X_{k}f_{k}
is the random payoff for stopping at trial

.

Lemma 3.6
.

If
\sum_{k}p_{k}<\infty
or
\sum_{k}\left\lvert f_{k}\right\rvert<\infty
then

is uniformly integrable.

Proof.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{E}\left[\1(\left\lvert T_{n}\right\rvert>a)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\1(\sup_{n}\left\lvert Y_{n}\right\rvert>a)\right]\leq{\mathbb{E}\left[{\sup_{n}\left\lvert Y_{n}\right\rvert}\right]}/{a}
by Markov inequality. By (
8
),
\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{n}\left\lvert Y_{n}\right\rvert\right]<C\min(\sum_{k}p_{k},\sum_{k}\left\lvert f_{k}\right\rvert)
for some constant

. Hence,
\mathbb{E}\left[\1(\left\lvert T_{n}\right\rvert>a)\right]\to 0
as

, proving the assertion.
∎

Lemma 3.7

(Lemma 1 in Chapter 5,
[
8
]
)
.

Suppose
T
is a uniformly integrable sequence. If
(A_{n})
is any sequence of events such that
\mathbb{P}(A_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, then as
n\to\infty
,
\mathbb{E}\left[\1\{A_{n}\}\left\lvert T_{n}\right\rvert\right]\to 0
.

Theorem 3.8
.

Suppose
f
is nonnegative and
f_{\infty}=0
.
If
\sum_{k}p_{k}<\infty
or
\sum_{k}{f_{k}}<\infty
, then as
n\to\infty
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}V^{*}_{n}\to V^{*}
.

Proof.

By hypothesis
f\geq 0
and
f_{\infty}=0
, therefore the random reward sequence
Y
is nonnegative and

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\infty}\right]=0
, respectively. By Theorem
3.1
, (
A1
) and (
A2
) are satisfied and hence an optimal rule exists. We denote this rule
\tau
, with
\tau(n)=\min\{\tau,n\}
for

being its truncated form. Then

\displaystyle 0\leq V^{*}-V^{*}_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\tau}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\tau(n)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\1\{n<\tau<\infty\}\left(Y_{\tau}-Y_{n}\right)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\1\{\tau=\infty\}\left(Y_{\infty}-Y_{n}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\1\{n<\tau<\infty\}\left\lvert T_{n}\right\rvert\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\infty}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\1\{n<\tau<\infty\}\left\lvert T_{n}\right\rvert\right]

Since

defined in (
9

, then
as

,
\mathbb{P}(\1\{n<\tau<\infty\})=0
. Invoking Lemma

∎

This result demonstrates only convergence of the optimal value; it does not ensure that the myopic strategy remains optimal in the infinite-horizon setting. The next theorem and its corollary furnish this guarantee.

Theorem 3.9

(Theorem 2, Chapter 5 in
[
8
]
)
.

Suppose (
A1
) and (
A2
) are satisfied and suppose the problem is monotone. If
V^{*}_{n}\to V^{*}
as

, then the myopic strategy is optimal.

Corollary 3.10
.

Suppose
f
is nonnegative and
f_{\infty}=0
.
Assume the hypothesis of Theorem
3.8
is satisfied.
If the stopping problem is monotone then the myopic strategy is optimal.

Proof.

The assertion follows from Theorem
3.9
since
\lim_{n\to\infty}V^{*}_{n}\to V^{*}
holds by Theorem
3.8
.
∎

This result is unsurprising, but monotonicity remains essential for proving optimality of the myopic rule and for passing to the infinite-horizon limit. In the next section, we show that this condition is ensured by quasi-unimodal stopping rewards or unimodal continuation payoffs.

3.4
Unimodality of the Continuation Payoff Sequence

Ferguson’s theorem provides a sufficient condition for optimality without imposing restrictions on the monotonicity patterns of the reward or payoff sequences. However, it does not exploit the structural relationship between the properties of
f
and
g
inherent in Bernoulli stopping problems. In the classical last-success problem,
[
1
]
demonstrated that monotonicity follows implicitly from the unimodality of
g
. Moreover,
[
7
]
argued that the unimodality of
g
, within the framework of best-or-worst choice problems and the bivariate secretary problem, ensures the optimality of the myopic policy. Despite these insights, the direct connection between
f
and
g
was not utilised. In what follows, we show how unimodal continuation payoffs guarantee the optimality of the myopic strategy.

Theorem 3.11
.

Consider the Bernoulli stopping problem in which success epochs are governed by a Markov chain with transition matrix (
5
). If the continuation payoff is unimodal, then the stopping problem is monotone.

Proof.

For any set
E\subset\{1,2,\ldots,\infty\}
, there exists a strategy that stops immediately when the Markov chain
M
enters
E
. Conditional that
M
ever entering
E
, the first visited site
\min\{k\in E:X_{k}=1\}
is independent of the path of
M
prior to hitting
E
. By definition,
g_{k-1}
is the expected payoff of the strategy associated with the stopping set
E_{k}:=\{k,k+1,\ldots,\infty\}
, given by

g_{k-1}=\sum_{j\geq k}P(k-1,j)f_{j}.

(10)

By monotonicity, once the chain enters
E_{k}
, it cannot leave. The first site visited in
E_{k}
is either
k
or an element of
E_{k+1}
, leading to the recursion

g_{k-1}=p_{k}f_{k}+(1-p_{k})g_{k},

(11)

which implies

g_{k-1}\geq g_{k}\Longleftrightarrow f_{k}\geq g_{k}.

(12)

Because
g
is unimodal, this equivalence ensures the existence of a threshold
k_{*}
such that
f_{k}\geq g_{k}
for all
k\geq k_{*}
. By the proof of Theorem
3.3
, the sets
(A_{k})
, where
A_{k}\coloneqq\{X_{k}=1\text{ and }g_{k}-f_{k}\leq 0\},
are absorbing. Consequently, the problem is monotone, and the myopic strategy is optimal.
∎

Corollary 3.12
.

The myopic strategy is a threshold strategy, where the optimal value equals the mode of the continuation payoff and the optimal threshold corresponds to the index of that mode.

Proof.

Let
E_{k}:=\{k,k+1,\ldots,\infty\}
and suppose the mode interval of
g
is
[k_{-},k_{+}]
. Using (
12
), the optimal stopping set is given by

B\coloneqq\{j\in\mathbb{N}:~X_{j}=1\text{ and }f_{j}-g_{j}\geq 0\}=\{j\in E_{k_{-}}:X_{j}=1\}

(13)

By monotonicity, the myopic rule coincides with the first visit to
B
, namely

\min\{j\geq k_{-}:X_{j}=1\}
. But this defines a threshold strategy with threshold
k_{-}
and expected payoff
g_{k_{-}}
, completing the proof.
∎

3.5
Quasi-Unimodality of the Stopping Payoff Sequence

In the context of the relatively best-choice problem with a fixed number of trials, Lemma 2 in
[
10
]
established via backward induction that the unimodality of the stopping payoff
f
secures the optimality of the myopic strategy. Since this proof is independent of the success profile, it remains valid for Bernoulli stopping problems. The authors also demonstrated that the unimodality of
f
ensures the optimality of the myopic strategy in the discounted duration model (see p. 44 in
[
10
]
). Because the monotone case of optimal stopping driven by the (quasi-)unimodality of
f
has not been extensively explored, we extend this result to Bernoulli stopping problems.

The following elementary lemma will be useful in proving our main result,

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
\alpha\coloneqq(\alpha_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a bounded sequence of nonnegative numbers. If
\alpha_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
, then
\alpha
attains its maximum.

Proof.

Let
M\coloneqq\sup\alpha
. Clearly,
M\in[0,\infty)
since
a
is nonnegative and bounded by assumption. Because
\alpha_{k}\to 0
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\alpha_{k}<M/2
for all
k>N
. This implies that
\sup\alpha=\sup\bigl\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{N}\bigr\}
. Since this set is finite, its supremum coincides with the maximum. Thus,
\alpha
attains its maximum.
∎

Theorem 3.14
.

Consider the Bernoulli stopping problem with nonnegative and bounded stopping reward
f
, where
f_{\infty}=0
. Assume
\sum_{k}p_{k}<\infty
, where
p_{k}\in(0,1)
. If
f
is quasi-unimodal, then the stopping problem is monotone.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.11
,
g=Pf
is quasi-unimodal. To prove the unimodality of
g
, is it sufficient to show that it attains its supremum. Let
M\coloneqq\sup g
. Clearly,
0\leq M=\sup(Pf)\leq\sup f<\infty
. If
M=0
, then
g=0
and the proof is complete. Suppose
M>0
. For
i\in S
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}g_{i}=\lim_{i\to\infty}\sum_{j=i+1}^{\infty}P(i,j)f_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{j\geq i+1}f_{j}\left(\lim_{i\to\infty}\sum_{j=i+1}^{\infty}P(i,j)\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{j\geq i+1}f_{j}\left(\lim_{i\to\infty}\sum_{j=i+1}^{\infty}p_{j}\prod_{k=i+1}^{j-1}(1-p_{k})\right)\to 0

Since
g
is nonnegative, invoking Lemma
3.13
completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.15
.

Assume
n\in\mathbb{N}
. If
f
is quasi-unimodal, then the
n
-step continuation payoff
P^{n}f
is unimodal.

Proof.

Obvious by induction on
n
.
∎

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{N}
be
N\geq 1
arbitrary positive integers. If
f
is nonincreasing on
S\setminus\{\infty\}
, then the payoff
(P^{m_{1}}+P^{m_{2}}+\cdots+P^{m_{N}})f
is unimodal.

Proof.

Fix
N\geq 1
positive integers
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{N}
and let
g^{(i)}=P^{i}f
for
i\in\{m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{N}\}
. By the previous corollary, each
g^{(i)}
is unimodal. Hence,
h\coloneqq(P^{m_{1}}++P^{m_{2}}+\cdots+P^{m_{N}})f=g^{(m_{1})}+\cdots+g^{(m_{N})}
attains its maximum. It remains to show that
h
possesses at most two sign changes. Since
f
is nonincreasing on
S\setminus\{\infty\}
, the VD property of
P
implies that each
g^{(i)}
is nonincreasing. It follows that
h
is nonincreasing on
S\setminus\{\infty\}
. Because
h
is nonnegative, it remains quasi-unimodal on
S
, which is the desired result.
∎

4
Applications

Consider the last-success problem, where the objective is to select any of the
\{\ell
th,
(\ell+1)
th,
\dots
,
m
th
\}
last successes in
n<\infty
independent Bernoulli trials
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}
, with
\ell\leq m

[
18
]
. We extend this problem to the case of an infinite number of trials. The optimisation problem seeks the rule satisfying

\displaystyle\tau^{*}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\argmax_{\tau\in{\cal T}}~\mathbb{P}\left[\ell\leq\sum_{j\geq\tau}X_{j}\leq m\right].

(14)

Without loss of generality, assume that
p_{k}\in[0,1)
for all
k\geq 1
, where
p_{k}
is the success probability of the
k
th trial. If
\sum_{k}p_{k}=\infty
, then by the Borel-Cantelli lemma,
\sum_{k}X_{k}=\infty
a.s. and no optimal rule exists. For the remainder of this section, we assume
\sum_{k}p_{k}<\infty
.

For fixed
n<\infty
, the number of successes among trials
k,k+1,\cdots,n
is a random variable
following a Poisson-binomial distribution with probability-generating function

z\mapsto\prod_{j=k}^{n}(1-p_{j}+zp_{j}).

Define the odds ratio
r_{k}\coloneqq{p_{k}}/{(1-p_{k})}
. A straightforward computation yields the probability of zero successes and the probability of
m
successes from stage
k
onward:

\displaystyle s_{0}(k,n)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\prod_{j=k}^{n}(1-p_{j}),

\displaystyle s_{m}(k,n)

\displaystyle\coloneqq s_{0}(k,n)e_{m}(k,n),

where
e_{m}(k,n)
is an elementary symmetric polynomial

e_{m}(k,n)\coloneqq\sum_{k\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{m}\leq n}r_{i_{1}}\cdots r_{i_{m}},~~~e_{0}(k,n)=1.

For a given
\ell\in[1,m]
, independence of the trials implies that the probability of observing
j\in[\ell,m]
successes from trial
k
onward is

\displaystyle u_{m}(k,n;\ell)\coloneqq\sum_{j=\ell}^{m}s_{j}(k,n).

(15)

From (
14
), it is clear that maximising the probability of selecting a success amongst the
\ell\text{th},(\ell+1)\text{th},\ldots,m\text{th}
last successes is equivalent to maximising
u_{m}(\cdot,n,\,\ell)
. For an infinite number of trials, we drop the second argument of
s_{m}
,
u_{m}
,
e_{m}
and define
s_{0}(\infty)=0
. Moreover, if
\sum_{k}p_{k}<\infty
then
e_{m}(k)
converges for all
k
since
e_{m}(1)\leq e^{m}_{1}(1)<\infty
.

The next proposition is key to the solution of various last-success problems in infinite-horizon.

Proposition 4.1
.

Assume
\sum_{k}p_{k}<\infty
. For any
m<n\in\mathbb{N}
, the sequences
(s_{m}(1),s_{m}(2),\ldots)
and
(u_{m}(1;\ell),u_{m}(2;\ell),\ldots)
are unimodal.

Proof.

Fix
m<n
and introduce the sequences
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots)
,
b\coloneqq(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots)
and ,
c\coloneqq(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots)
, where

a_{k}\coloneqq s_{0}(k)
,
b_{k}=s_{m}(k)
and
c_{k}=u_{m}(k;\ell)
. These sequences satisfy
b=P^{m}a
,
and
c=P^{\ell}a+\cdots+P^{m}a
, where
P
is the transition matrix in (
5
). Since
a
is nondecreasing, it is quasi-unimodal; hence
b
is unimodal by application of Corollary
3.15
. Similarly,
c
is unimodal by application of Lemma
3.16
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
I\coloneqq\{m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{N}\}
be a arbitrary collection of positive integers, where
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Define

\displaystyle v(k;I)\coloneqq\sum_{j\in I}s_{j}(k),\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

(16)

If
\sum_{k}p_{k}<\infty
, then
v
is unimodal.

Proof.

The Lemma is proved in much the same way as Proposition
4.1
. Given an arbitrary set
I
with
N\geq 1
positive integers
m_{1},\ldots m_{N}
, introduce the sequences
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots)
and

, where
a_{k}\coloneqq s_{0}(k)
, and
d_{k}=v(k;I)
. From the definition of

, we have
d=P^{m_{1}}a+\cdots+P^{m_{N}}a
. Since

is quasi-unimodal, the assertion is proved by application of Lemma

.
∎

4.1
Selecting the
m

The optimisation task in this last-success problem is defined in (

) with
\ell=m
.
We generalise Theorem 2 in
[

]
to the infinite-horizon setting as follows.

Theorem 4.3
.

Consider the

\sum_{k}p_{k}<\infty
and that zero payoff is awarded for never stopping. Then an optimal rule exists, and the optimal strategy is to stop at the first success, if any, occurring on or after index

\displaystyle k^{*}\coloneqq\min\{j\in\mathbb{N}:e_{m}(j)\leq e_{m-1}(j)\}.

s_{m}(k^{*})

Define the sequences

These sequences are the payoffs for stopping at stage

with respectively

successes and

Remark 4.5
.
