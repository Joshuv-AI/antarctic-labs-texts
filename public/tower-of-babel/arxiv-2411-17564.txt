The tempered finite element method
Antoine Quiriny, Václav Kučera, Jonathan Lambrechts, Nicolas Moës, Jean-François Remacle

Abstract




Abstract

In this paper, we propose a new approach – the Tempered Finite Element Method (TFEM) – that extends the Finite Element Method (FEM) to classes of meshes that include zero-measure or nearly degenerate elements for which standard FEM approaches do not allow convergence. First, we review why the maximum angle condition
[
1
]
is not necessary for FEM convergence and what are the real limitations in terms of meshes. Next, we propose a simple modification of the classical FEM for elliptic problems that provably allows convergence for a wider class of meshes including bands of caps that cause locking of the solution in standard FEM formulations. The proposed method is trivial to implement in an existing FEM code and can be theoretically analyzed. We prove that in the case of exactly zero-measure elements it corresponds to mortaring. We show numerically and theoretically that what we propose is functional and sound. The remainder of the paper is devoted to extensions of the TFEM method to linear elasticity, mortaring of non-conforming meshes, high-order elements, and advection.

Since the early 1970s, the finite element method has been used extensively in computational physics and engineering.
The fact that there exists a structure of proof for finite element algorithms

Then, the currently prominent method of analysis, finite elements, requires a mesh, which is an alternative (discrete) representation of a geometry.

There are many partial results concerning such quantities as vertex and dihedral angles, projected circumradii, etc.

In the paper

The interface is handed over from layers of nodes to new layers of nodes across elements which can be rather thin in the direction of the front propagation. These elements may even possibly form bands of caps.

This is the motivation for this paper:
be able from an X–

to compute easily with bands of caps.
This requires a more robust finite element approach that we have named
the
tempered finite element method
.
This name is inspired by an analogy with metallurgy. The problem with finite elements on a band of caps is that the numerical solution undergoes a locking phenomenon which prevents proper functioning of the method and convergence. In a material-science analogy, after discretization the ‘material’ becomes extremely hard on a band of caps and the numerical solution can no longer conform to the exact solution. The method we propose alleviates this by locally ‘softening’ the material on the band. In Poisson’s problem this will correspond to locally decreasing the diffusion parameter in a mesh-dependent way. This allows the numerical solution to conform to the continuous problem. This process is analogous to that of tempering in metallurgy. Steel can be made very hard by heating it to a high temperature and then rapidly cooling it in water or oil. This process is called
quenching
and it makes the steel extremely hard, but also very brittle.
Tempering
is then performed, when the steel is slowly reheated to an intermediate temperature and slowly cooled, allowing the internal stresses of the material to be relieved. This makes the alloy more flexible and ductile, while preserving most of the hardness. This is analogous to our approach in the FEM, where we decrease the diffusion coefficient (‘soften’) to increase flexibility, while preserving other desired qualities (e.g. good approximation properties). For this reason, we call the method
tempered finite elements (TFEM)
.

The paper is structured as follows.
First we perform a simple numerical experiment on a manufactured Laplacian to show that, using a simple numerical trick, we are able to recover finite element convergence. The TFEM solution on a mesh containing a band of caps is compared with the finite element solution on a regular mesh. Section 3 is dedicated to a rigorous mathematical analysis, confirming the numerical experiment. We demonstrate that the TFEM convergence has the same rate as the one of the traditional finite element method on regular meshes. In the next section, we present extensions to the TFEM method and its usefulness in various situations: linear elasticity, non-conformal mesh mortaring, high-order elements, and advection.

3
Theory behind the TFEM scheme

In the previous section we showed that a simple numerical trick allowed us to recover the convergence rate of the finite element method on meshes with a band of caps. In this section we demonstrate this convergence for the choice of
J_{\text{min}}\sim h^{d+1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. To do so, we need to consider two cases: when the band of caps is not exactly degenerate but tends to a zero width, and the exactly degenerated case. For the first case, we do a classic finite element error analysis where we separate the error made on the band and the rest of the domain and we estimate and balance these carefully. In the second case for exactly degenerate bands of caps, we demonstrate that our scheme corresponds to an under-integrated mortar scheme with penalization of the jump across the band. In order to make this jump converge to zero as the mesh is refined, we obtain an expression the choice of
J_{\text{min}}
.

In order to simplify the analysis, we consider the case of homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions (
u_{D}=0
). Considering a nonhomogeneous Dirichlet condition would only add a level of technicalities unrelated to the TFEM method itself (Dirichlet lift, error of the interpolation of
u_{D}
on the boundary, etc.) which would make the analysis less clear and readable. In the notation of Section
2.1
we therefore have
V_{h}=V_{h}^{0}=X_{h}\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
in the following analysis.

3.1
TFEM for a band of arbitrarily small but non-zero caps

By applying the circumradius estimate of the Lagrange interpolation error and using the scaling of the band, we are able to bound the error
|u-\tilde{u}_{h}|_{H^{1}}
in the
H^{1}
semi-norm. This requires a careful decomposition and balancing of the error. The main difficulty is that the method (
12
) is not consistent in the sense that we are essentially solving a modified continuous problem (with diffusion coefficient
d(x)
instead of 1), hence we lack so-called Galerkin orthogonality. Nevertheless this obstacle can be overcome. The analysis requires three main working tools/lemmas.

Lemma 1

(Circumradius estimate
[
9
]
)
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an arbitrary triangle. Let
u\in W^{2,\infty}(K)
and let
\Pi_{K}u
be the linear Lagrange interpolation of
u
on
K
. Then there exists a constant
C_{c}
independent of
u
and
K
such that

|u-\Pi_{K}u|_{W^{1,\infty}(K)}\leq C_{c}R_{K}|u|_{W^{2,\infty}(K)}\leq C_{c}\frac{h^{2}}{\bar{h}}|u|_{W^{2,\infty}(K)}.

(13)

where
R_{K}
is the circumradius of the
K
triangle.

Lemma 2

(Maximum-angle condition
[
1
]
)
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a triangle satisfying the maximum angle condition:
\alpha_{K}\leq\alpha_{0}<\pi
for some fixed
\alpha_{0}
. Let
u\in H^{2}(K)
and let
\Pi_{K}u
be the linear Lagrange interpolation of
u
on
K
. Then there exists a constant
C_{I}
independent of
u
(but depending on the maximum angle
\alpha_{0}
) such that

|u-\Pi_{K}u|_{H^{1}(K)}\leq C_{I}h|u|_{H^{2}(K)}.

(14)

Lemma 3

(Young’s inequality)
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and
r>0
. Then

ab\leq\frac{a^{2}}{2r}+\frac{rb^{2}}{2}.

(15)

Our main theoretical result is the TFEM error estimate (
16
) with a general value of
D
. We will then optimize this estimate with respect to
D
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
u\in W^{2,\infty}(\Omega)
. Let
0<D\leq 1
in (
11
). Then

\begin{split}&|u-\tilde{u}_{h}|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}+D|u-\tilde{u}_{h}|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathcal{B})}\\
&\quad\leq 4C_{I}^{2}h^{2}|u|^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}+\frac{4}{D}|\mathcal{B}||u|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}^{2}+6D|\mathcal{B}|C_{c}^{2}\frac{h^{4}}{\bar{h}^{2}}|u|^{2}_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}.\end{split}

(16)

Proof.

We subtract (
12
) and (
6
) with a test function
v=v_{h}\in V_{h}
:

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdotp\nabla v_{h}\,{\rm d}x-\int_{\Omega}d(x)\nabla\tilde{u}_{h}\cdotp\nabla v_{h}\,{\rm d}x=0.

(17)

We define
\xi_{h}=\Pi_{h}u-\tilde{u}_{h}\in V_{h}
and set
v_{h}=\xi_{h}
in (
17
):

\int_{\Omega}\Big(\nabla u-d(x)\nabla\Pi_{h}u+d(x)\nabla\Pi_{h}u-d(x)\nabla\tilde{u}_{h}\Big)\cdotp\nabla\xi_{h}\,{\rm d}x=0,

where we have introduced the terms
\pm d(x)\nabla\Pi_{h}u
. Rearranging gives us

\int_{\Omega}d(x)|\nabla\xi_{h}|^{2}\,{\rm d}x=-\int_{\Omega}\Big(\nabla u-d(x)\nabla\Pi_{h}u\Big)\cdotp\nabla\xi_{h}\,{\rm d}x.

We split the integrals over
\mathcal{B}
and
\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B}
and take (
11
) into account:
d(x)=1
over
\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B}
and
d(x)=D
over
\mathcal{B}
. This gives us:

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B}}|\nabla\xi_{h}|^{2}\,{\rm d}x+D\int_{\mathcal{B}}|\nabla\xi_{h}|^{2}\,{\rm d}x\\
&\quad=-\underbrace{\int_{\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B}}\Big(\nabla u-\nabla\Pi_{h}u\Big)\cdotp\nabla\xi_{h}\,{\rm d}x}_{(\star)}-\underbrace{\int_{\mathcal{B}}\Big(\nabla u-D\nabla\Pi_{h}u\Big)\cdotp\nabla\xi_{h}\,{\rm d}x}_{(\star\star)}.\end{split}

(18)

Estimating
(\star)
is standard, because we have ‘nice’ elements on
\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B}
:

\begin{split}\big|(\star)\big|&\leq|u-\Pi_{h}u|_{H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}|\xi_{h}|_{H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}|u-\Pi_{h}u|_{H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}|\xi_{h}|_{H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}^{2}\\
&\leq\tfrac{1}{2}C_{I}^{2}h^{2}|u|_{H^{2}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}|\xi_{h}|_{H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}^{2},\end{split}

(19)

where we have used Young’s inequality (
15
) with
r=1
and then the Maximum Angle Condition from lemma
1
. We note that the last term in (
19
) will be ‘hidden’ under the corresponding left-hand side term of (
18
).

To estimate
(\star\star)
, we proceed more carefully since this integral is evaluated on the band. First, we have

(\star\star)=\underbrace{\int_{\mathcal{B}}\Big(\nabla u-D\nabla u\Big)\cdotp\nabla\xi_{h}\,{\rm d}x}_{(A)}+\underbrace{\int_{\mathcal{B}}\Big(D\nabla u-D\nabla\Pi_{h}u\Big)\cdotp\nabla\xi_{h}\,{\rm d}x}_{(B)},

(20)

where we have introduced
\pm D\nabla u
. Since
D<1
, we can estimate
(A)
:

\begin{split}\big|(A)\big|&=(1-D)\bigg|\int_{\mathcal{B}}\nabla u\cdotp\nabla\xi_{h}\,{\rm d}x\bigg|\leq\int_{\mathcal{B}}\big|\nabla u\big|\big|\nabla\xi_{h}\big|\,{\rm d}x\\
&\leq\frac{1}{D}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\big|\nabla u\big|^{2}\,{\rm d}x+\frac{D}{4}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\big|\nabla\xi_{h}\big|^{2}\,{\rm d}x\\
&\leq\frac{1}{D}|\mathcal{B}||u|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}^{2}+\frac{D}{4}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\big|\nabla\xi_{h}\big|^{2}\,{\rm d}x\end{split}

(21)

where we have used Young’s inequality (
15
) with
r=\tfrac{D}{2}
and taken the maximum of the first derivative on the band
|u|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}
to obtain an upper bound. Again, the last term in (
21
) will be ‘hidden’ under the corresponding left-hand side term of (
18
).

Finally, it remains to estimate term
(B)
from (
20
):

\begin{split}\big|(B)\big|&\leq D\int_{\mathcal{B}}\big|\nabla u-\nabla\Pi_{h}u\big|\big|\nabla\xi_{h}\big|\,{\rm d}x\\
&\leq D\int_{\mathcal{B}}\big|\nabla u-\nabla\Pi_{h}u\big|^{2}\,{\rm d}x+\frac{D}{4}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\big|\nabla\xi_{h}\big|^{2}\,{\rm d}x\\
&\leq D|\mathcal{B}||u-\Pi_{h}u|^{2}_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}+\frac{D}{4}|\xi_{h}|_{H^{1}(\mathcal{B})}^{2}\\
&\leq D|\mathcal{B}|C_{c}^{2}\bigg(\frac{h^{2}}{\bar{h}}\bigg)^{2}|u|^{2}_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}+\frac{D}{4}|\xi_{h}|_{H^{1}(\mathcal{B})}^{2}\end{split}

(22)

where we have used Young’s inequality (
15
) with
r=\tfrac{1}{2}
and the circumradius estimate (
13
) in which the maximum of the second derivative on the band,
|u|_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}
, appears. As usual, the last term in (
22
) will be ‘hidden’ under the left-hand side of (
18
).

Combining estimates (
18
), (
19
), and (
20
)–(
22
) gives us

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}|\xi_{h}|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}+\frac{D}{2}|\xi_{h}|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathcal{B})}\\
&\quad\leq\frac{1}{2}C_{I}^{2}h^{2}|u|_{H^{2}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}^{2}+\frac{1}{D}|\mathcal{B}||u|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}^{2}+D|\mathcal{B}|C_{c}^{2}\frac{h^{4}}{\bar{h}^{2}}|u|^{2}_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}.\end{split}

(23)

This gives us an estimate for
\xi_{h}=\Pi_{h}u-\tilde{u}_{h}
. Now we estimate the error
u-\tilde{u}_{h}
using the fact that
u-\tilde{u}_{h}=(u-\Pi_{h}u)+\xi_{h}
:

\begin{split}&|u-\tilde{u}_{h}|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}+D|u-\tilde{u}_{h}|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathcal{B})}\\
&\leq\big(|u-\Pi_{h}u|_{H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}+|\xi_{h}|_{H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}\big)^{2}+D\big(|u-\Pi_{h}u|_{H^{1}(\mathcal{B})}+|\xi_{h}|_{H^{1}(\mathcal{B})}\big)^{2}\\
&\leq 2|u-\Pi_{h}u|_{H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}^{2}+2|\xi_{h}|_{H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}^{2}+2D|u-\Pi_{h}u|_{H^{1}(\mathcal{B})}^{2}+2D|\xi_{h}|_{H^{1}(\mathcal{B})}^{2},\end{split}

(24)

where we have used the fact that
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
. The
\xi_{h}
-terms in (
24
) are estimated using (
23
). The remaining terms have already been dealt with in (
19
) and (
22
), respectively:

|u-\Pi_{h}u|_{H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}^{2}\leq C_{I}^{2}h^{2}|u|^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})},

(25)

since
\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B}
contains only ‘nice’ elements, and

D|u-\Pi_{h}u|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathcal{B})}\leq D|\mathcal{B}||u-\Pi_{h}u|^{2}_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}\leq D|\mathcal{B}|C_{c}^{2}\frac{h^{4}}{\bar{h}^{2}}|u|^{2}_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathcal{B})},

(26)

where we took the maximum of the gradient of
u-\Pi_{h}u
over the band
\mathcal{B}
and used the circumradius estimate from lemma
1
.
Using (
23
), (
25
), and (
26
) in (
24
) gives us the error estimate

\begin{split}&|u-\tilde{u}_{h}|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}+D|u-\tilde{u}_{h}|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathcal{B})}\\
&\leq 4C_{I}^{2}h^{2}|u|^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}+\frac{4}{D}|\mathcal{B}||u|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}^{2}+6D|\mathcal{B}|C_{c}^{2}\frac{h^{4}}{\bar{h}^{2}}|u|^{2}_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathcal{B})},\end{split}

which is our desired estimate.
∎

Remark 1
.

We note that for
D=1
(i.e. standard FEM), the second right-hand side term in (
16
) vanishes, since this comes from the consistency term
(A)
which is zero in this case, cf. (
21
). On the other hand, the last right-hand side term in (
16
) is
O(h^{2})
if and only if
\bar{h}\geq Ch^{2}
for some constant
C
. This is due to the fact that
|\mathcal{B}|\frac{h^{4}}{\bar{h}^{2}}=\bar{h}L\frac{h^{4}}{\bar{h}^{2}}=L\frac{h^{2}}{\bar{h}}h^{2}
. This means that if
\bar{h}\geq Ch^{2}
then standard finite elements have optimal
O(h)
convergence in the
H^{1}(\Omega)
-seminorm.

Now we will optimize the value of
D
in the case of a band of caps. The value of
D
appears both in the numerator and the denominator of the error estimate of Theorem
4
. The following lemma optimizes the value of such a function:

Lemma 5
.

Let
a,b>0
and

g(x)=\frac{a}{x}+bx.

(27)

Then
g
attains its minimum at a unique
x_{\min}
, where

x_{\min}=\sqrt{\frac{a}{b}},\quad g(x_{\min})=2\sqrt{ab}.

(28)

Proof.

We have
g^{\prime}(x)=b-a/x^{2}
which is zero for
x=\sqrt{a/b}
. Then
g(\sqrt{a/b})=a\sqrt{b/a}+b\sqrt{a/b}=2\sqrt{ab}
.
∎

We can now apply the result of this lemma to the error estimate of Theorem
4
. This gives us an optimal choice for
D
:

Theorem 6
.

Let
u\in W^{2,\infty}(\Omega)
. Let
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
contain a band of caps of length
L\sim 1
and width
\bar{h}
. In (
11
), set

D=\min\left(\frac{\bar{h}}{h^{2}}\frac{|u|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}}{|u|_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{3C_{c}}}\,,\,\,1\right),

(29)

then the tempered FEM solution
\tilde{u}_{h}
satisfies

\begin{split}&|u-\tilde{u}_{h}|_{H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}+\sqrt{D}|u-\tilde{u}_{h}|_{H^{1}(\mathcal{B})}\leq C(u,L)h,\end{split}

(30)

where

\begin{split}C(u,L)=2\sqrt{2}C_{I}|u|_{H^{2}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}+2\sqrt{2}\sqrt[4]{6}\sqrt{C_{c}L}\sqrt{|u|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}|u|_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}}\end{split}

(31)

is a constant independent of
\bar{h},h
.

Proof.

We optimize the last two terms in (
16
) with respect to
D
. To this end, we denote them as

g(D)=\frac{4}{D}|\mathcal{B}||u|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}^{2}+6D|\mathcal{B}|C_{c}^{2}\frac{h^{4}}{\bar{h}^{2}}|u|^{2}_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathcal{B})},

which is a function of the form (
27
) with respect to
D
. The application of Lemma
5
gives us the optimal choice of
D
:

D_{\mathrm{opt}}=\sqrt{\frac{4|\mathcal{B}||u|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}^{2}}{6|\mathcal{B}|C_{c}^{2}\frac{h^{4}}{\bar{h}^{2}}|u|^{2}_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}}}=\frac{\bar{h}}{h^{2}}\frac{|u|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}}{|u|_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{3C_{c}}},

which is (
29
). Moreover,

\begin{split}g(D_{\mathrm{opt}})&=2\sqrt{4|\mathcal{B}||u|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}^{2}6|\mathcal{B}|C_{c}^{2}\frac{h^{4}}{\bar{h}^{2}}|u|^{2}_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}}\\
&=4\sqrt{6}C_{c}|\mathcal{B}|\frac{h^{2}}{\bar{h}}|u|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}|u|_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}\\
&\leq 4\sqrt{6}C_{c}Lh^{2}|u|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}|u|_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathcal{B})},\end{split}

since
|\mathcal{B}|\leq\bar{h}L
(inequality here since
\mathcal{B}
is not a rectangle). With the choice
D=D_{\mathrm{opt}}
, estimate (
16
) therefore becomes

\begin{split}&|u-\tilde{u}_{h}|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}+D|u-\tilde{u}_{h}|^{2}_{H^{1}(\mathcal{B})}\\
&\quad\leq 4C_{I}^{2}h^{2}|u|^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}+4\sqrt{6}C_{c}Lh^{2}|u|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}|u|_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}.\end{split}

(32)

Now we wish to take the square root of (
32
). This is done by considering the equivalence of norms in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which for
a,b,c,d\geq 0
gives us

a^{2}+b^{2}\leq c^{2}+d^{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a+b\leq\sqrt{2}(c+d).

(33)

Applying this to (
32
) gives

\begin{split}&|u-\tilde{u}_{h}|_{H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}+\sqrt{D}|u-\tilde{u}_{h}|_{H^{1}(\mathcal{B})}\\
&\quad\leq 2\sqrt{2}C_{I}h|u|_{H^{2}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B})}+2\sqrt{2}\sqrt[4]{6}\sqrt{C_{c}L}h\sqrt{|u|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}|u|_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}}.\end{split}

This is the desired estimate (
30
) with the constant
C(u,L)
defined by (
31
). Since
D
needs to be smaller than 1, we take the minimum between
D_{\mathrm{opt}}
and
1
.
∎

We note that this choice of
D_{\mathrm{opt}}
can be transformed back to a choice of
J_{\text{min}}
by using (
10
) and the definition of
d(x)
from (
11
):

\displaystyle J_{\text{min,opt}}

\displaystyle=\frac{J}{D_{\text{opt}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{h\bar{h}}{2}\,\frac{h^{2}}{\bar{h}}\,\frac{|u|_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}}{|u|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}}\,\sqrt{\frac{3C_{c}}{2}}

\displaystyle=h^{3}\frac{|u|_{W^{2,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}}{|u|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{B})}}\,\sqrt{\frac{3C_{c}}{8}}

(34)

Thus we have proved that the error converges in 2D when we choose
J_{\text{min}}\sim h^{3}
as we can see in the convergences plots of the numerical experiment in Figure
6
.

Contrary to what one might think, the choice of
J_{\text{min}}
is independent of the length
L
. This corresponds well to what was observed during the numerical experiments in section
2.5
. The error estimate provided by the theorem
6
predicts an error that varies as
\sim\sqrt{L}
when
J_{\text{min}}
is chosen optimally. In the numerical experiment we observed this dependence in
\sim\sqrt{L}
when
J_{\text{min}}
was chosen too small (locking), as we can see in figure
10
.

Remarks:

•

The expression for
D
in (
29
) guarantees that
D\leq 1
. One might ask what exactly happens when the expression in the first argument of the minimum is greater than 1. In this case
\bar{h}\geq Ch^{2}
, which means that standard finite elements (i.e.
D=1
) have optimal
O(h)
convergence rate (cf. Remark
1
), and therefore no tempering is necessary in the finite element method, i.e.
D=1
is the best choice in this case. This justifies the fact that the diffusion coefficient is always decreased in TFEM, never increased.

•

We note that the very regular structure of elements in the band
\mathcal{B}
as presented in Figure
2
is not needed in the analysis, the band is only assumed to be somehow composed of ‘degenerating’ elements.

•

There need not be a singe band of caps, there can be several layers of these bands next to each other or several separate bands of caps in the domain
\Omega
. The only condition for the analysis to remain the same is that their number does not grow as
h\to 0
.

•

Poincaré’s inequality immediately gives us the estimate

|u-\tilde{u}_{h}|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(u,L)h

(35)

for some constant
C(u,L)
independent of
h
. This can be easily seen by applying Poincaré’s inequality to (
30
) on
\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B}
and a simple continuity argument on
\mathcal{B}
. This
L^{2}
-error estimate is subomptimal. However, we were unable to prove optimal
L^{2}
-error estimates, as the standard Aubin-Nitsche duality technique runs into technical obstacles. Nevertheless numerical experiments indicate that the method has optimal convergence rates in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

•

The constant
C_{c}
from Lemma
1
is very close to 1. The paper
[
12
]
gives the value
2\sqrt{2}+2
, which can most likely be improved. In the end, the factor
\sqrt{\frac{2}{3C_{c}}}
from (
29
) can simply be neglected for simplicity.

Extension to 3D

The results of Theorem
6
can be straightforwardly extended to 3D. In this case, the thin band
\mathcal{B}
with width
\bar{h}
and length
L
becomes a thin slab
\mathcal{S}
of width
\bar{h}
and area
\mathcal{A}
of its base. We assume that the slab
is partitioned into degenerating tetrahedra. The situation in 3D is much more complicated than 2D, as there are 9 basic types of degenerate tetrahedra, cf.
[
10
]
. However what they have in common is that at least one of their four altitudes is always small (similarly to one of the altitudes of a degenerate triangle). We assume the partition of
S
into tetrahedra is such that the minimal altitude of each tetrahedron is
\bar{h}
and its longest edge is
h
. Then the proof of Theorem
6
can be trivially extended to 3D provided we have an estimate for the error of linear Lagrange interpolation on a tetrahedron
T
of the same form as in Lemma
1
. We then obtain an
O(h)
estimate of the TFEM error in the
H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\mathcal{S})
seminorm provided
D\sim\frac{\bar{h}}{h^{2}}
, exactly as in Theorem
6
. We note that in 3D, this choice corresponds to
J_{\text{min}}\sim h^{4}
, which agrees with the numerical experiments from Figure
7
.

The necessary interpolation error estimate analogous to Lemma
1
can be easily obtained from the estimate of Baidakova,
[
3
]
, as in the following lemma.

Lemma 7

(Interpolation error estimate in 3D)
.

Let
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an arbitrary tetrahedron with longest edge length
h
and minimal altitude
\bar{h}
. Let
u\in W^{2,\infty}(T)
and let
\Pi_{T}u
be the linear Lagrange interpolation of
u
on
T
. Then there exists a constant
C_{c}
independent of
u
and
T
such that

|u-\Pi_{T}u|_{W^{1,\infty}(T)}\leq C_{c}\frac{h^{2}}{\bar{h}}|u|_{W^{2,\infty}(T)}.

(36)

Proof.

Denote the faces of
T
as
T_{i},i=1,\ldots,4
. Let
R_{i}
be the radius of the
circle circumscribed about
T_{i}
, and let
\varphi_{ij}
be the angle between faces
T_{i},T_{j}
. Let
R=\max_{i}R_{i}
and
\sin\varphi=\max_{ij}\sin\varphi_{ij}
. Then in
[
3
]
it is proven that for some constant
C
,

|u-\Pi_{T}u|_{W^{1,\infty}(T)}\leq C\frac{R}{\sin\varphi}|u|_{W^{2,\infty}(T)}.

(37)

Now we estimate the factor
\frac{R}{\sin\varphi}
. Let
R=R_{i}
for some
i
. Then the corresponding face
T_{i}
is a triangle with vertices
A,B,C
. Assume that
AB
is the longest edge. The law of sines then states that
2R_{i}=|AC||AB|/h_{c}
, where
h_{c}
is the altitude in ABC corresponding to vertex
C
. Moreover, let
\bar{h}_{c}
be the height of the tetrahedron
T
corresponding to the apex
C
. Then trivially,
\sin\varphi_{ij}=\bar{h}_{c}/h_{c}
, combining these expressions, we get

\frac{R}{\sin\varphi}\leq\frac{R}{\sin\varphi_{ij}}=\frac{|AC||AB|/(2h_{c})}{\bar{h}_{c}/h_{c}}=\frac{|AC||AB|}{2\bar{h}_{c}}\leq\frac{h^{2}}{2\bar{h}},

(38)

since
\bar{h}\leq\bar{h}_{c}
. Substituting (
38
) into (
37
) gives the desired estimate.
∎

3.2
Zero-measure elements – TFEM and Mortaring

In §
3.1
, we proved a sufficient condition for the convergence of the tempered finite element method for any band of degenerate elements with nonzero width. It is important to realize that this proof
is not valid if the band width is exactly zero.
It is however valid
when the band degenerates to zero width whatever the rate.
In order to generalize the proof of section 3.1 to the case where
\bar{h}=0
(truly or up to machine precision), we show that our approach is equivalent to a mortar method penalizing the jump of the solution on the band.

Figure 14
:
Degeneration of a band of caps
\mathcal{B}
to a line
\Gamma
as the width of the band
\bar{h}
goes to zero.

Again we assume a regular structure of the band of caps
\mathcal{B}
with length
L
and width
\bar{h}
. The goal of this section is to formulate the limiting scheme of (
12
) for
\bar{h}=0
, which we will interpret as the limit for
\bar{h}\to 0^{+}
.

As
\bar{h}\to 0^{+}
, the band
\mathcal{B}
degenerates to a line
\Gamma
and the resulting triangulation
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{\Gamma}
is not conforming on
\Gamma
, cf. Figure
14
. We will also call
\Gamma
a zero-measure band. We will label the elements in the band as
K_{i},i=1,\ldots,N
, ordered from one end of
\mathcal{B}
to the other. For an element
K_{i}\in\mathcal{B}
, let
x_{i}
denote its maximum angle vertex and
p_{i}
be the foot of the altitude corresponding to
x_{i}
, cf. Figure
14
. We also label the endpoints of
\Gamma
as
x_{0}
and
x_{N+1}
,respectively. As we send
\bar{h}\to 0^{+}
and
\mathcal{B}
degenerates to a line
\Gamma
, each
x_{i}
merges with its corresponding
p_{i}
. We note that each
x_{i}
is the vertex of a triangle adjacent to
\mathcal{B}
. Let
n_{\Gamma}
be the unit normal to
\Gamma
(arbitrarily oriented). We can then define the two one-sided limits of a function
f:\Omega\setminus\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
at
x\in\Gamma
:

f^{+}(x)=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}f(x+tn_{\Gamma}),\quad f^{-}(x)=\lim_{t\to 0^{-}}f(x+tn_{\Gamma})

(39)

and the jump

[f]_{x}=f^{-}(x)-f^{+}(x),

(40)

where we will sometimes omit the subscript
x
for simplicity.

On
\mathcal{B}
we will need to split the gradient of a function into two parts:
\nabla_{\!\!\perp}u
will be the component of
\nabla u
in the direction perpendicular to
\Gamma
, i.e.
\nabla_{\!\!\perp}u=(\nabla u\cdotp n_{\Gamma})n_{\Gamma}
, while
\nabla_{\!\!{\scriptscriptstyle\parallel}}u
denotes the component of
\nabla u
in the direction parallel to
\Gamma
. Using this notation, we can rewrite the scheme (
12
), i.e.

\int_{\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B}}\nabla\tilde{u}_{h}\cdotp\nabla v_{h}\,{\rm d}x+D\int_{\mathcal{B}}\nabla\tilde{u}_{h}\cdotp\nabla v_{h}\,{\rm d}x=(f,v_{h}),\quad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}.

(41)

On
\mathcal{B}
we write

\nabla\tilde{u}_{h}\cdotp\nabla v_{h}=(\nabla_{\!\!\perp}\tilde{u}_{h}+\nabla_{\!\!{\scriptscriptstyle\parallel}}\tilde{u}_{h})\cdot(\nabla_{\!\!\perp}v_{h}+\nabla_{\!\!{\scriptscriptstyle\parallel}}v_{h})=\nabla_{\!\!\perp}\tilde{u}_{h}\cdotp\nabla_{\!\!\perp}v_{h}+\nabla_{\!\!{\scriptscriptstyle\parallel}}\tilde{u}_{h}\cdotp\nabla_{\!\!{\scriptscriptstyle\parallel}}v_{h},

since
\nabla_{\!\!\perp}\tilde{u}_{h}\cdotp\nabla_{\!\!{\scriptscriptstyle\parallel}}v_{h}=\nabla_{\!\!{\scriptscriptstyle\parallel}}\tilde{u}_{h}\cdotp\nabla_{\!\!\perp}v_{h}=0
by definition. We can therefore write (
41
) as

\int_{\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B}}\nabla\tilde{u}_{h}\cdotp\nabla v_{h}\,{\rm d}x+D\int_{\mathcal{B}}\nabla_{\!\!\perp}\tilde{u}_{h}\cdotp\nabla_{\!\!\perp}v_{h}+\nabla_{\!\!{\scriptscriptstyle\parallel}}\tilde{u}_{h}\cdotp\nabla_{\!\!{\scriptscriptstyle\parallel}}v_{h}\,{\rm d}x=(f,v_{h}).

(42)

Now we derive the form of (
42
) for
\bar{h}=0
by sending
\bar{h}\to 0^{+}
. Eventually, we will have
D\sim\bar{h}/h^{2}
, where the numerator
\bar{h}
will play an important role in the derivation. For this reason, we write
D(\bar{h},h)=\bar{h}\widetilde{D}(h)
and it will be important to choose
\widetilde{D}
appropriately in the scheme.

Lemma 8
.

Let
D=\bar{h}\widetilde{D}
. Then the tempered FEM scheme (
12
) for
\bar{h}=0
is equivalent to the formulation

\int_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}\nabla\tilde{u}_{h}\cdotp\nabla v_{h}\,{\rm d}x+\widetilde{D}\frac{h}{2}\sum_{x_{i}\in\Gamma}[\tilde{u}_{h}]_{x_{i}}[v_{h}]_{x_{i}}=(f,v_{h}),\quad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}^{\Gamma},

(43)

where

V_{h}^{\Gamma}=\{v_{h}\in C(\Omega\setminus\Gamma):\ v_{h}|_{K}\in P^{1}(K)\,\forall K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h},v_{h}|_{\partial\Omega}=0\}

is the set of globally continuous piecewise linear functions discontinuous on
\Gamma
.

Remark 2
.

The scheme (
43
) is similar to the nonconsistent penalization method of Babuška, cf.
[
2
]
,

\int_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}\nabla u_{h}\cdotp\nabla v_{h}\,{\rm d}x+\widetilde{D}\int_{\Gamma}[u_{h}][v_{h}]\,{\rm d}S=(f,v_{h}),\quad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}^{\Gamma},

(44)

due to the fact that

\int_{\Gamma}[u_{h}][v_{h}]\,{\rm d}S\approx\frac{h}{2}\sum_{x_{i}\in\Gamma}[u_{h}]_{x_{i}}[v_{h}]_{x_{i}}

(45)

is a
composite trapezoidal quadrature
approximation on the partition given by the nodes
x_{i}\in\Gamma
, which has step size
h/2
. We note that the endpoints
x_{0},x_{N+1}
of
\Gamma
do not come into play, since
[v_{h}]_{x_{0}}=[v_{h}]_{x_{N+1}}=0
for all
v_{h}\in V_{h}^{\Gamma}
. Also note that (
45
) is an approximation, not an equality, since
[u_{h}][v_{h}]
is a piecewise quadratic function and composite trapezoidal quadratures integrate exactly only piecewise linear functions.

Proof of Lemma
8
.

Consider the scheme (
12
) in the form (
42
). Let
\varphi_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,M
be the standard ‘tent’ basis functions of
V_{h}
corresponding to individual vertices of
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
. We write
\tilde{u}_{h}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}U_{j}\varphi_{j}(x)
, where
U_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
, and set
v_{h}:=\varphi_{k}
. Then, (
42
) becomes

\sum_{j=1}^{M}U_{j}\int_{\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B}}\nabla\varphi_{j}\cdotp\nabla\varphi_{k}\,{\rm d}x+\sum_{j=1}^{M}U_{j}\bar{h}\widetilde{D}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\nabla_{\!\!\perp}\varphi_{j}\cdotp\nabla_{\!\!\perp}\varphi_{k}+\nabla_{\!\!{\scriptscriptstyle\parallel}}\varphi_{j}\cdotp\nabla_{\!\!{\scriptscriptstyle\parallel}}\varphi_{k}\,{\rm d}x=(f,\varphi_{k}).

(46)

Now consider that for all
j
, it holds
|\nabla_{\!\!{\scriptscriptstyle\parallel}}\varphi_{j}|\leq 1/h
on each
K\in\mathcal{B}
and that
|\mathcal{B}|\leq L\bar{h}
. Therefore, for fixed
h
,

\Big|\bar{h}\widetilde{D}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\nabla_{\!\!{\scriptscriptstyle\parallel}}\varphi_{j}\cdotp\nabla_{\!\!{\scriptscriptstyle\parallel}}\varphi_{k}\,{\rm d}x\Big|\leq\bar{h}^{2}L\widetilde{D}h^{-2}\to 0,\text{ as }\bar{h}\to 0^{+},

therefore these terms ‘disappear’ from the limiting system of linear equation (
46
) for
\bar{h}=0
.

On the other hand, on an element
K_{i}\in\mathcal{B}
with apex
x_{i}
we have

\nabla_{\!\!\perp}\varphi_{j}=\pm\frac{\varphi_{j}(x_{i})-\varphi_{j}(p_{i})}{\bar{h}}n_{\Gamma},

for all
j
(the sign depends on the orientation of
K
with respect to
n_{\Gamma}
). Therefore (since
|K|=\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{h}h
for all
K\in\mathcal{B}
),

\begin{split}&\bar{h}\widetilde{D}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\nabla_{\!\!\perp}\varphi_{j}\cdotp\nabla_{\!\!\perp}\varphi_{k}\,{\rm d}x=\bar{h}\widetilde{D}\sum_{K_{i}\in\mathcal{B}}\frac{1}{2}\bar{h}h\frac{\varphi_{j}(x_{i})-\varphi_{j}(p_{i})}{\bar{h}}\frac{\varphi_{k}(x_{i})-\varphi_{k}(p_{i})}{\bar{h}}\\
&\ =\widetilde{D}\frac{h}{2}\sum_{K_{i}\in\mathcal{B}}\big(\varphi_{j}(x_{i})-\varphi_{j}(p_{i})\big)\big(\varphi_{k}(x_{i})-\varphi_{k}(p_{i})\big)\longrightarrow\widetilde{D}\frac{h}{2}\sum_{x_{i}\in\Gamma}[\varphi_{j}]_{x_{i}}[\varphi_{k}]_{x_{i}},\end{split}

for
\bar{h}\to 0^{+}
.

Altogether, the limiting formulation of (
46
) for
\bar{h}=0
is

\sum_{j=1}^{M}U_{j}\int_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}\nabla\varphi_{j}\cdotp\nabla\varphi_{k}\,{\rm d}x+\sum_{j=1}^{M}U_{j}\widetilde{D}\frac{h}{2}\sum_{x_{i}\in\Gamma}[\varphi_{j}]_{x_{i}}[\varphi_{k}]_{x_{i}}=(f,\varphi_{k}),

which is equivalent to (
43
).

∎

Now that we have a limiting formulation for
\bar{h}=0
, the TFEM scheme can be analyzed. The main result is Theorem
13
, which basically states that if
\widetilde{D}\sim\frac{1}{h^{2}}
, then

|u-\tilde{u}_{h}|_{H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\Gamma)}+\sqrt{\widetilde{D}}\big\|[u-\tilde{u}_{h}]\big\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\leq C(u,L)h,

(47)

as expected. In order not to break the flow of the paper, we move the theorem and its proof to Appendix
B
. We note that similarly as in (
34
), the choice
\widetilde{D}\sim\frac{1}{h^{2}}
corresponds to simply taking
J_{\text{min}}\sim h^{3}
in the code.

4
Extensions

So far we’ve worked with a scalar Laplacian operator.
In this section we discuss possible extensions. The first one is to
consider linear elasticity. The second extension deals with the potential use of the TFEM for the mortaring of non-conformal meshes, even when there is a large difference in mesh size. Third, we show that high-order elements adapt well to our approach. With a slightly modified choice of
J_{\text{min}}
, the TFEM ensures the proper convergence rate of high-order elements. The fourth and last extension studies the TFEM
capabilities for advection type equations.

4.1
Extension to linear elasticity

The deformation
\mathbf{u}
of a solid under mechanical loads is defined by the system of partial differential equations:

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{\sigma}(\epsilon(\mathbf{u}))

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}\hskip 56.9055pt\text{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{D}\hskip 48.36958pt\text{on }\Gamma_{D},

\displaystyle\mathbf{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{t}_{N}\hskip 51.21504pt\text{on }\Gamma_{N},

\mathbf{\sigma}
is the stress tensor,
\mathbf{f}
body forces, and
\mathbf{t}_{N}
normal stresses applied on the part
\Gamma_{N}

of the boundary. The complementary of the boundary
\Gamma_{D}
is where Dirichlet boundary conditions
\mathbf{u}_{D}
are imposed.
The infinitesimal strain tensor
\epsilon

is given by
\mathbf{\epsilon}(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}^{T}\right)
.

The relationship between the stress tensor and the infinitesimal strain tensor is assumed linear (Hooke’s model) :

\mathbf{\sigma}=\mathbf{C}:\mathbf{\epsilon},

where
\mathbf{C}
is the elasticity tensor for plane strain in our case.

5
Conclusion

In this work we have developed a new method called the Tempered Finite Element Method (TFEM), which increases the flexibility of finite elements with respect to meshing. The method is simple to implement within any finite element code: it simply consists to avoiding division by (almost) zero in the evaluation of FEM matrix entries. We have shown through numerical experiments that on meshes with degenerate elements the method provides an error close to the one of finite elements on regular meshes and that it converges at the same rate. We have also proven this convergence theoretically for Poisson’s problem and linear elements. This method is however not only useful for solving a Laplacian. It works just as well for linear elasticity, advection, and high-order elements and advection. For exactly zero-measure elements, we show that TFEM corresponds to non-conforming mesh mortaring. We are particularly interested in solving on meshes containing a so-called band of caps. This structure mimics a finely resolved one-dimensional interface in the two-dimensional domain. The TFEM approach is able to completely resolve the locking phenomenon that standard finite elements undergo in this situation.

A crucial point of the scheme is the choice of a parameter called
J_{\text{min}}
, which is the minimal value of element Jacobians that we are willing to tolerate in denominators when evaluating FEM matrix entries. Our numerical experiments and the mathematical theory have identified the dependence of
J_{\text{min}}
on
h
, the mesh size. However, the optimal practical choice of
C
in the expression
J_{\text{min}}=Ch^{d+1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
remains to be determined. The mathematical study of the scheme provides a theoretically optimal choice of
C
. However, we have observed numerically that it was not always the best possible in practice. Nevertheless, the sensitivity analysis shows that the method is robust as the choice of
C
could vary by at least an order of magnitude around the minimum without being too far from the classical finite element error. Although the choice of the optimal
C
should be the subject of further research, the method can already be used. We show this by performing 100 tests on random functions consisting of a sum of linear, quadratic, sine and cosine functions of random amplitude and frequency, and a Gaussian function of random amplitude and position. The domains considered were rectangles of random sizes containing a band of caps of variable length and we considered the simple choice
J_{\text{min}}=h^{3}
, i.e.
C=1
. The relative errors in the
L^{2}
norm and in the
H^{1}
semi-norm are shown in Figure
23
. It can be seen that, despite the simple choice of
C=1
, the error of the TFEM method is close to the error of the FEM method on a regular mesh. In the
H^{1}
semi-norm, both errors are almost identical.

The TFEM method could save considerable time and effort in mesh generation by reducing the mesh quality required to perform finite element analysis.

Now we analyze the limiting scheme (

•

The scheme is not consistent and we must deal with a consistency error.

•

As noted in Remark
2

First, we detail the equation that the exact solution
u

\Omega\setminus\Gamma
, if we use
v_{h}\in V_{h}^{\Gamma}
as a test function.

Lemma 9
.

The exact solution
u
of (

\int_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}\nabla u\cdotp\nabla v_{h}\,{\rm d}x-\int_{\Gamma}\nabla u\cdotp n_{\Gamma}[v_{h}]\,{\rm d}S+\widetilde{D}\frac{h}{2}\sum_{x_{i}\in\Gamma}[u]_{x_{i}}[v_{h}]_{x_{i}}=(f,v_{h}),

for all

Proof.

We multiply problem (
5
) by
v_{h}

\Omega\setminus\Gamma
and apply Green’s theorem. Since

is in general discontinuous on
\Gamma
, we obtain

\int_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}\nabla u\cdotp\nabla v_{h}\,{\rm d}x-\int_{\Gamma}\nabla u\cdotp n_{\Gamma}[v_{h}]\,{\rm d}S=(f,v_{h}).

(54)

Since
[u]=0
on
\Gamma
, we can add the last left-hand side term in (

) to the formulation as it is equal to zero.
∎

Now we address the second problem.

Lemma 10
.

Let
f:\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
be a piecewise linear function on the partition
x_{i},i=0,\ldots,N+1,
such that
f(x_{0})=f(x_{N+1})=0
. Then

\int_{\Gamma}f^{2}\,{\rm d}S\leq\frac{h}{2}\sum_{x_{i}\in\Gamma}f^{2}(x_{i}).

(55)

Proof.

For simplicity, we can consider
\Gamma
to be the one-dimensional interval
[0,L]
with corresponding nodes
x_{i}
. We consider two neighboring points
x_{i},x_{i+1}
of the partition and estimate the integral over this segment of
\Gamma
, which has length

. Since
f
is linear on this segment, we can write
f(x)=f(x_{i})\phi_{i}(x)+f(x_{i+1})\phi_{i+1}(x)
, where
\phi_{i}(x)=\frac{2}{h}(x_{i+1}-x)
and
\phi_{i+1}(x)=\frac{2}{h}(x-x_{i})
are the standard Lagrange basis functions in 1D, for which the necessary integrals can be easily evaluated. Therefore

\begin{split}&\int_{x_{i}}^{x_{i+1}}f^{2}(x)\,{\rm d}x\\
&=\int_{x_{i}}^{x_{i+1}}f^{2}(x_{i})\phi_{i}^{2}(x)+2f(x_{i})f(x_{i+1})\phi_{i}(x)\phi_{i+1}(x)+f^{2}(x_{i+1})\phi^{2}_{i+1}(x)\,{\rm d}x\\
&=\frac{h}{6}f^{2}(x_{i})+2\frac{h}{12}f(x_{i})f(x_{i+1})+\frac{h}{6}f^{2}(x_{i+1})\leq\frac{h}{4}f^{2}(x_{i})+\frac{h}{4}f^{2}(x_{i+1}),\end{split}

(56)

since
2ab\leq a^{2}+b^{2}
. Summing (
56
) over all

and taking into account
f(x_{0})=f(x_{N+1})=0
gives us (
55
).
∎

We will eventually apply Lemma

to the function

. For this it is important to realize that
[\xi_{h}]
is a piecewise linear function on the partition given by the nodes

. Indeed,

is linear on the intersection, i.e. on the segment between

, since

is continuous at the endpoints of
\Gamma

Next, we need an estimate of the interpolation error on

Lemma 11
.

\displaystyle\frac{h}{2}\sum_{x_{i}\in\Gamma}[u-\Pi_{h}u]_{x_{i}}^{2}

(70)

where we have used inequality (
55
) with
f=[\xi_{h}]
. The
\xi_{h}

(71)

\begin{split}&|u-\tilde{u}_{h}|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega\setminus\Gamma)}+\widetilde{D}\frac{h}{2}\sum_{x_{i}\in\Gamma}[u-\tilde{u}_{h}]_{x_{i}}^{2}\\

(72)

which is our desired estimate.
