Overexpression of rttA leads to activation of the fumicycline biosynthesis cluster including the O-acetyltransferase encoding fccF
Authors: Lukas Birštonas, Sophie Tröger-Görler, Ingo Bauer, Birte Mertens, Henrick Schweder, Klaus Faserl, Axel A. Brakhage, Fabio Gsaller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe opportunistic mold Aspergillus fumigatus, a WHO-priority fungal pathogen, causes life-threatening invasive infections in immunocompromised patients. A major antifungal drug target is the ergosterol biosynthesis pathway, primarily regulated by the transcription factors SrbA and AtrR. Recently, we identified RttA as an additional regulator of sterol homeostasis that mediates the activation of the sterol C24-methyltransferase-encoding gene erg6. Here, we demonstrate that overexpression of rttA leads to elevated production of the secondary metabolite fumicycline via induction of fccR, coding for the principal transcriptional activator of fumicycline biosynthesis. Lack of fccR blocked activation of fumicycline biosynthetic genes (fccA-fccE) during rttA overexpression, highlighting the indispensable role of FccR for activation of this cluster. In contrast, rttA inactivation did not abolish fumicycline production during fccR overexpression or during co-cultivation with Streptomyces rapamycinicus. In addition to fccA-fccE, our findings reveal a crucial role of FccR for the upregulation of a gene located adjacent to the fumicycline biosynthesis cluster, here termed fccF. Deletion of fccF severely diminished the conversion of fumicycline B into fumicycline C, uncovering FccF as fumicycline B O-acetyltransferase. Intriguingly, during fccR overexpression and resulting fumicycline overproduction, inactivation of fccF caused growth defects, likely due to precursor toxicity. Together, these findings broaden our understanding of fungal secondary metabolite regulation and reveal a novel role of RttA in the activation of fccR encoding the cluster-specific regulator that activates fumicycline production, including FccF, the terminal acetyltransferase in the pathway.IMPORTANCEAspergillus fumigatus is a major opportunistic fungal pathogen that causes severe infections in immunocompromised individuals and represents a significant global health threat. Understanding the regulatory networks controlling fungal metabolism is important for identifying potential therapeutic vulnerabilities. Here, we uncover a previously unrecognized role for the sterol homeostasis regulator RttA in activating fumicycline production through induction of the pathway-specific transcription factor FccR. We further show that FccR controls expression of an additional biosynthetic gene, fccF, and identify FccF as the terminal O-acetyltransferase required for conversion of fumicycline B to fumicycline C. Disruption of this final biosynthetic step impairs fungal growth during metabolite overproduction, indicating that accumulation of fumicycline B or related intermediates can negatively affect fungal growth. Together, these findings expand our understanding of fungal secondary metabolite regulation and provide new insights into the organization and function of the fumicycline biosynthetic pathway.

OBSERVATION

Recent estimates indicate that fungal infections are linked to more than 3.8 million human deaths annually, far exceeding previous estimates of 1.5–2.0 million cases of fatality per year (1–3). Aspergillosis, a severe disease caused by species of the genus Aspergillus, accounts for more than 2 million of these deaths (3). Among Aspergillus species, the human pathogenic mold Aspergillus fumigatus is the primary causative agent of aspergillosis and has been designated by the World Health Organization (WHO) as one of the four critical priority fungal pathogens (4).

Detrimental effects of aspergillosis result from a combination of physical invasion and disruption of the host’s immune system and homeostasis (5). A. fumigatus has the capacity to produce an array of secondary metabolites such as siderophores, gliotoxin, and DHN-melanin that contribute to the success of infection (6–8). Genes associated with such metabolic pathways are frequently co-localized within so-called secondary metabolite gene clusters (SMCs), which are typically located in transcriptionally silent genomic regions (9, 10). Some fungal SMCs are known to be activated in response to specific environmental stresses or upon exposure to particular molecules or surrounding bacteria (11–13). One of the SMCs shown to be induced upon co-cultivation of A. fumigatus with the bacterial species Streptomyces rapamycinicus is the fumicycline (neosartoricin) biosynthetic cluster (Fig. 1A), which was first discovered in Neosartorya fischeri and A. fumigatus during genome mining for polycyclic prenyltransferases (14, 15). The induced compounds exhibited only moderate activity against this bacterial species (15). In addition to co-cultivation with bacteria, upregulation of this cluster was observed in A. fumigatus during murine lung infection and in an fphB phytochrome gene deletion mutant (16, 17). In recent work, the arginoketide azalomycin F, a bacterial metabolite produced and excreted by Streptomyces iranensis, was shown to activate the cluster (12). In previous work, antiproliferative activity in vitro against anti-CD3/CD28-activated murine spleenic T cells was detected, implying an immunosuppressive role of fumicycline C (14). A clear inhibitory potential of fumicycline C against Gram-positive and -negative bacteria, yeast species Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Candida albicans, A549 lung epithelial cells, and RAW 264.7 macrophage cells could not be identified (14).

Fumicycline biosynthesis involves the consecutive action of the enzymes FccA-FccD (Fig. 1B), which leads to the generation of fumicycline B. In a final step of the pathway, fumicycline B undergoes acetylation to form fumicycline C. Alternatively, dehydration of fumicycline B through the loss of one water molecule leads to the formation of fumicycline A (14, 15). FccR represents the principal transcriptional activator of the fumicycline biosynthetic cluster, and its encoding gene is located within the cluster itself. It has been demonstrated that its overexpression increases the expression of all fumicycline biosynthetic genes within the cluster (14, 15). In this study, we demonstrate that genes of the fumicycline biosynthetic cluster can also be activated by overexpressing rttA, a previously characterized transcription factor involved in the maintenance of sterol homeostasis and azoleresistance (18, 19). Moreover, we identify a previously uncharacterized gene adjacent to the fumicycline biosynthetic gene cluster and demonstrate its role in fumicycline biosynthesis. During overexpression of rttA, we observed unusual yellow pigmentation in both the mycelium and the surrounding medium (Fig. 1C). Hence, we suspected upregulation of a biosynthetic pathway in this mutant responsible for overproduction of a specific metabolite. To elucidate corresponding differentially expressed genes linked to a biosynthetic cluster, we performed RNAseq analysis comparing transcriptional profiles of wild type (WT) and rttAPxylP (mutant carrying an additional xylose-inducible rttA copy) grown in the presence of 1% xylose. Among the genes that showed the highest differential expression in rttAPxylP versus WT, we detected all the genes of the fumicycline SMC (Fig. 1D). Therefore, we suspected that the observed yellow pigmentation results from overproduction of fumicyclines, which would be in agreement with previous work reporting this phenotype (14). To investigate whether rttA overexpression could be responsible for upregulation of the individual biosynthetic genes inside the cluster directly, independent of fccR, we deleted fccR in rttAPxylP. The resulting strain rttAPxylPΔfccR exhibited no yellow pigmentation during inducing conditions (Fig. 1C), indicating that overexpression of rttA leads to induction of fccR, which in turn activates the fumicycline SMC. To exclude a direct contribution of RttA to fumicycline production during fccR overexpression, we generated the reciprocal mutant fccRPxylPΔrttA. There was no observable difference in the yellow pigmentation comparing fccRPxylPΔrttA with fccRPxylP (Fig. 1C). In addition to the phenotypic analysis, we monitored the expression of the fumicycline SMC genes using northern blot analysis (Fig. 2A). During inducing conditions, the fumicycline biosynthetic genes fccA-fccE were upregulated in rttAPxylP, fccRPxylP, and fccRPxylPΔrttA but not rttAPxylPΔfccR. Notably, compared with rttA, upregulation of fccR led to a substantially higher expression of the genes. Using LC-MS analysis, we detected a high abundance of fumicyclines in the strains with elevated transcript levels of fccA-fccE (Fig. 2B). The lack of fccA-fccE expression and abolished metabolite production in rttAPxylPΔfccR further confirms the indispensable role of FccR for activation of the fumicycline SMC. In addition to xylose-induced artificial activation of the cluster through rttA and fccR overexpression, we generated single deletion mutants ΔrttA and ΔfccR to investigate the contribution of these genes to cluster activation during co-cultivation with S. rapamycinicus. Consistent with a dispensable role of RttA, neither expression of fumicycline cluster genes nor production of fumicyclines was diminished during co-cultivation with ∆rttA compared with WT (Fig. 2C through E). To date, the mechanism underlying fccR induction during rttA overexpression remains to be identified. Although speculative, rttA overexpression may perturb sterol homeostasis and thereby alter membrane composition or membrane-associated stress signaling, indirectly elevating fccR, which results in the activation of fumicycline biosynthetic genes. Consistent with a membrane-stress linked route to fccR activation, amphotericin B, which targets the fungal membrane by binding ergosterol and forming pores, induces expression of eneA, encoding a transcription factor involved in the expression of fumicycline biosynthetic genes (20). Future work will test whether RttA directly activates fccR via promoter binding or regulates it indirectly, and will aim to elucidate the triggers that lead to fccR activation via RttA.

In addition to fccA-fccE, a gene located directly downstream of the fumicycline SMC (AFUB_086740) (Fig. 1A), here designated fccF, was strongly upregulated in rttAPxylP and fccRPxylP during inducing conditions (Fig. 1D and Fig. 2A through C). Using the translated FccF protein sequence as input for the InterPro (21) classification tool, FccF was annotated as a putative “plant and fungal acyltransferase.” Domain architecture analysis predicted a chloramphenicol acetyltransferase-like domain, while family classification assigned the protein to the trichothecene 3-O-acetyltransferase family (IPR050317). Based on this, we hypothesized that FccF is responsible for the acetylation of fumicycline B to fumicycline C. Prior to this study, the gene and corresponding enzyme catalyzing this reaction had not been identified. To investigate the potential involvement of FccF in this reaction, we deleted the gene encoding FccF in the fccRPxylP background (strain fccRPxylPΔfccF) and detected fumicycline levels in the supernatants of the mutant strains using LC-MS (Fig. 2B). Comparison of fccRPxylP with fccRPxylPΔfccF showed that production of fumicycline C was almost abolished, accompanied by increased levels of fumicycline B. This is consistent with the predicted role of FccF as the O-acetyltransferase catalyzing the conversion of fumicycline B to fumicycline C. Moreover, fumicycline A levels were elevated in the absence of FccF, which may result from increased availability of substrate for dehydration. Similar results were observed during co-cultivation of ∆fccF with S. rapamycinicus (Fig. 2D). These findings confirm the role of FccF as O-acetyltransferase responsible for acetylation of fumicycline B. Interestingly, radial growth as well as microtiter-based microscopic analysis revealed a growth impairment of fccRPxylPΔfccF compared with fccRPxylP (Fig. 2F). Notably, microscopic analysis showed more pronounced growth differences between the mutants at later time points (10 h vs 20 h), which may result from accumulation of fumicycline B and/or fumicycline A over time (Fig. 2F).

Together, these data demonstrate that overexpression of rttA upregulates fccR, which in turn activates the fumicycline SMC, including fccF, here characterized as encoding the O-acetyltransferase catalyzing the conversion of fumicycline B to fumicycline C. Disruption of this step exacerbates growth defects associated with fumicycline production, likely due to markedly reduced fumicycline C levels and consequent accumulation of its precursor fumicycline B and/or fumicycline A.
