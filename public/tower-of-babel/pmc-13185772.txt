Modeling ventricular tachycardia in genetic long QT syndrome using engineered cardiac tissues
Authors: Richard Z Zhuang, Georgia Doufexi, Marc Yehya, Margaretha AJ Morsink, Xianghai Liao, Manu Ben-Johny, Gordana Vunjak-Novakovic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCongenital long QT syndrome (LQTS) predisposes patients to malignant ventricular tachycardias, yet existing in vitro models do not fully capture the clinical arrhythmic phenotypes that determine patient outcomes. We report the development of an engineered platform with mature, fully hiPSC-derived human cardiac tissues that reproduces genotype-specific ventricular tachycardia under physiologically relevant triggers. Using patient-derived and corrected hiPSCs, we engineered electromechanically matured cardiac tissues to model LQT8 and LQT2 syndromes. LQT8 tissues exhibited spontaneous early afterdepolarizations and sustained tachycardia, including monomorphic, polymorphic, and torsades de pointes-like waveforms. In contrast, LQT2 tissues remained stable under constant pacing but developed arrhythmias during dynamic rate acceleration, mirroring clinical trigger-dependent arrhythmogenicity. Frequency-domain analysis and activation mapping revealed re-entrant dynamics consistent with ventricular tachycardia-like activity. By unifying cellular-level electrophysiology with tissue-scale ECG-like arrhythmic activity, this platform enables physiologically inspired investigation of arrhythmogenic mechanisms in inherited arrhythmia syndromes.Keywords: Cardiac tissue engineering, Maturation, Long QT syndrome, Arrhythmia

NOMENCLATURE

Introduction

Heart disease remains the global leading cause of mortality [1,2]. Congenital long QT syndromes (LQTS) collectively affect 1 in 2500 people and represent a major class of cardiac disorders that heighten the risk of ventricular tachycardia (VT) that can lead to sudden cardiac death [3]. Numerous subtypes of LQTS have been identified and are distinguished by differences in disease severity, prevalence, and triggers of arrhythmia [4]. In general, gain- or loss-of-function mutations affecting cardiac ion channels alter the balance of inward and outward currents, which prolongs repolarization. These effects increase cellular susceptibility to early afterdepolarizations (EADs) that can trigger arrhythmic activity [5].

While the causal mutations and direct electrophysiological consequences of LQTS are well-characterized, sophisticated in vitro tissue models are needed to advance therapeutic development. Current clinical management of LQTS relies on pharmacological β-adrenergic blockade to reduce the risk of adrenergically mediated arrhythmias, lifestyle modifications to avoid arrhythmic triggers, and, in severe cases, left cardiac sympathetic denervation or implantation of cardioverter-defibrillators [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. However, these interventions are fundamentally palliative and do not address the underlying electrophysiological defects. In addition, the clinical utility of ion channel-targeted pharmacotherapies remains limited by variable efficacy across LQTS genotype variants and risk of pro-arrhythmic effects, highlighting the need for models that can resolve arrhythmic mechanisms within physiologically relevant contexts [[9], [10], [11], [12]].

LQTS-associated VT is a multiscale phenomenon that arises from (i) drug- or mutation-induced repolarization instability, (ii) tissue-scale architecture and electrophysiology to support re-entry, and (iii) physiological stressors that trigger arrhythmogenic vulnerability. In preclinical studies of congenital LQTS, cellular and animal models have laid the foundation for mechanistic insights into mutation-specific perturbations of cardiac repolarization [[13], [14], [15]]. The advent of human-induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC) technology has enabled interrogation of disease mechanisms within genetically defined human systems, thereby overcoming key species-specific limitations inherent to animal models [16]. Patient-derived hiPSCs have facilitated the dissection of LQTS mechanisms with direct correlation to patient outcomes [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. Additionally, gene-editing technologies such as CRISPR-Cas9 can identify mutation-specific phenotypes by eliminating confounding variations of genetic background [21]. These innovations have yielded insights into how distinct genetic variants perturb repolarization and contribute to LQTS pathogenesis at the molecular and cellular levels [18,20,[22], [23], [24], [25]]. A central challenge in modeling cardiac arrhythmias is that these phenomena require a tissue substrate with sufficient structural organization, cellular coupling, and coordinated conduction to support emergent arrhythmic activity. Accordingly, engineered cardiac tissues have been used to visualize spiral wavefronts using high-resolution mapping modalities, demonstrating that engineered myocardium can support re-entrant excitation at the tissue scale [[26], [27], [28]]. We combined hydrogel scaffolding, fully hiPSC-derived cellular composition, and electromechanical maturation to produce a human engineered myocardial tissue substrate capable of supporting the emergence of LQTS-associated arrhythmic dynamics.

To better recapitulate the principal axes that govern arrhythmia emergence in LQTS, we adopted a design strategy that integrates genetic control, functional maturation, and electrical pacing. (i) Fully hiPSC-derived tissue construction is crucial for patient-specific disease modeling, as it ensures a shared genetic background across all constituent cell types [20,29]; (ii) Maturation of engineered cardiac tissues establishes adult-like excitability, conduction, and repolarization dynamics and enables more faithful recapitulation of adult disease phenotypes [[30], [31], [32]]; and (iii) Electrical pacing provides controlled conditions that mimic physiological stressors. To that end, we implemented our electromechanical stimulation protocol to mature fully-hiPSC-derived engineered cardiac tissues towards an adult-like phenotype. By combining this platform with CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing, we established a patient-specific model of LQTS. We applied electrical pacing to interrogate two mechanistically distinct forms of LQTS: Types 2 and 8 (Fig. 1).

Experimental overview of LQTS patient-on-a-chip. Patient-derived hiPSCs and CRISPR-corrected control pseudo-wild-type (ΨWT) hiPSCs were differentiated into cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts that were encapsulated in a fibrin hydrogel stretched between two elastic pillars and matured by electromechanical conditioning to generate LQT and control cardiac tissues. Membrane potential dyes and fluorescence imaging were used to visualize the dynamics of action potential propagation.

While arising from distinct molecular defects, both LQTS subtypes share the clinical presentation of prolonged repolarization and susceptibility to VT, particularly torsades de pointes (TdP) [33]. LQT8, also known as Timothy syndrome, is a rare but highly malignant disorder caused by gain-of-function mutations in the CACNA1C gene, which encodes the L-type Ca2+ channel. These mutations impair voltage-dependent inactivation, leading to excessive and prolonged Ca2+ influx. Clinically, Timothy syndrome is associated with a high incidence of sudden cardiac death early in life, deeming it one of the most severe examples of channelopathy-driven arrhythmogenesis [34]. LQT2, in contrast, is one of the most prevalent forms of congenital LQTS. Loss-of-function mutations in KCNH2, which encodes the rapid delayed rectifier potassium current, impair ventricular repolarization. Clinically, LQT2 is characterized by arrhythmic events triggered by rapid increases in heart rate or sympathetic drive, often in response to sudden auditory stimuli or emotional stress [19,35]. Despite a generally milder phenotype than LQT8, LQT2 accounts for a substantial burden of syncope and sudden cardiac death among inherited arrhythmia syndromes owing to its high prevalence. Together, LQT8 and LQT2 provide complementary disease contexts, spanning a spectrum from a robust, readily detectable arrhythmic phenotype to a more subtle but prevalent disorder.

We developed a platform to resolve LQTS phenotypes, spanning from cellular electrophysiology to emergent tissue-level arrhythmias. In addition to recapitulating the hallmark cellular abnormalities of LQTS, including prolonged action potential duration and EAD formation, these tissue models exhibit re-entrant dynamics and sustained VT-like activity under physiological conditions. The emergent electrical patterns directly link genetic perturbations to clinically observed arrhythmic behaviors, providing a mechanistically grounded basis for in vitro modeling of inherited arrhythmias and a tool for therapeutic development.

Results

Modeling of LQT8 in hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes and engineered cardiac tissues

We first focused on LQT8, a high-penetrance disease, to establish and benchmark our tissue model and characterize the associated arrhythmic phenotypes. To this end, we evaluated how tissue architecture and maturation influence the in vitro manifestation of LQTS phenotypes. We employed patient-derived hiPSCs carrying the CACNA1C gain-of-function mutation, along with their isogenic pseudo-wild-type (ΨWT) controls [22]. To interrogate the geometric axis, we investigated LQT8 phenotypes across cardiomyocyte two-dimensional monolayers and three-dimensional engineered tissues, assessing how structural organization influences repolarization stability (Fig. 2A–E). hiPSCs were differentiated into cardiomyocytes (iCMs) and cardiac fibroblasts (iCFs) (Fig. S1A–C). Membrane potential dyes were used for fluorescent recordings in both systems. Consistent with canonical L-type calcium channel dysfunction, prolongation of both action potential duration (APD80) and repolarization time (Repolarization50) was observed in LQT8 relative to ΨWT controls (Fig. 2A and B). To determine whether these phenotypes arise from intrinsic mutation-associated defects in L-type calcium channel gating, we performed patch clamp recordings under voltage-clamp conditions. Compared to ΨWT controls, calcium current (ICa) in LQT8 cardiomyocytes exhibited diminished inactivation, manifesting as an increase in the pedestal current (Fig. 2C). To further assess changes in voltage-dependent inactivation (VDI), Ba2+ was substituted as the charge carrier (IBa), and the resulting Ba2+ current also demonstrated an increase in the pedestal current amplitude. Quantifying the fraction of peak current remaining following 300 ms of depolarization (r300) demonstrated a reduction in VDI, confirming L-type channel dysfunction associated with the CACNA1C mutation (Fig. 2D) [22,36].

Next, we examined the manifestation of LQTS-associated phenotypes in tissue preparations and found that action potential prolongation was preserved (Fig. 2E). However, as in previous in vitro studies, arrhythmic behavior was not observed under these conditions in either monolayers or nascent engineered tissues [22]. We then interrogated whether tissue maturation would enable the emergence of arrhythmic phenotypes. We applied our previously established electrical ramp stimulation protocol to drive tissue maturation [30]. Stimulated tissues showed significant structural, functional, and transcriptional maturation compared to nascent (unstimulated) controls (Fig. S2). Peak-aligned traces revealed that matured LQT8 tissues displayed accelerated depolarization kinetics but accentuated mutation-related delay in late-phase repolarization, including increased Repolarization50 and decay constant (τ) compared to nascent LQT8 tissues (Fig. 2F and G).

Only electromechanically matured tissues displayed LQT8 phenotypes beyond action potential prolongation, exhibiting pro-arrhythmic activity in the form of EADs (Fig. 2H and I). Notably, a subset of tissues exhibited arrhythmia-like waveform distortions resembling tachycardic events (Fig. 2I) with spontaneous rhythm reaching up to 167 beats per minute (bpm). These features were absent in ΨWT controls and nascent LQT8 tissues (Fig. 2H & S3). The emergence of pronounced arrhythmic signatures in the electrically matured tissue model underscores the importance of functional maturation in replicating disease-relevant phenotypes, highlighting the utility of in vitro tissue platforms for mechanistic studies of VT in LQTS. Consistent with these findings, acute caffeine stimulation revealed an enhanced relative calcium transient amplitude in matured tissues, suggesting increased sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium reserve that may contribute to their heightened arrhythmogenic susceptibility (Fig. S2I).

Engineered LQT8 cardiac tissues recapitulate key features of clinical ventricular tachycardia

Examination of arrhythmia-like traces from LQT8 tissues revealed waveform morphologies resembling those of clinical VT ECG recordings [37,38]. While optical membrane potential traces are not direct ECG measurements, waveform morphology and spectral features permit mechanistically relevant comparisons. Arrhythmic action potential recordings were characterized by rapid, repetitive depolarization cycles and a loss of the discrete depolarization-repolarization morphology observed during normal conduction, consistent with pathological ventricular arrhythmias. Visual assessment of all arrhythmic traces revealed waveform geometries resembling the two principal ECG-defined VT phenotypes: monomorphic ventricular tachycardia and polymorphic ventricular tachycardia. Monomorphic VT-like traces from engineered tissues displayed uniform cycle lengths and consistent depolarization amplitudes, indicating a stable and repetitive excitation pattern (Fig. 3A). In contrast, polymorphic VT-like traces from engineered tissues exhibited marked variability in cycle amplitudes (Fig. 3B).

Representative arrhythmic traces of LQT8 tissues (left) and clinical ECGs [37,38] of ventricular tachycardia (right). A) Monomorphic ventricular tachycardia. B) Polymorphic ventricular tachycardia. C) Torsades de pointes.

Notably, a subset of these arrhythmic events closely resembles the clinical ECG waveforms of TdP, the hallmark arrhythmia of congenital LQTS (Fig. 3C). These traces displayed a gradual twisting of the signal axis, characterized by cyclical shifts in depolarization amplitude over successive beats, producing a waxing-and-waning envelope pattern [39]. The ability of this platform to recapitulate a spectrum of clinical VT-like phenotypes enables patient-specific probing of the emergent mechanisms of arrhythmogenicity.

Frequency-domain analysis of LQT8 engineered tissue and clinical waveforms

To demonstrate that tissue arrhythmic activity exhibits electrophysiological features of VT, we quantified beat-to-beat interval and amplitude variability of arrhythmic patterns. Implantable cardioverter-defibrillators discriminate arrhythmic waveforms using the electrical signal, such as by assessing beat-to-beat interval variability: Low-variability rhythms are categorized as VT, whereas highly irregular, rapidly fluctuating intervals denote ventricular fibrillation (VF) [40]. Applying an analogous principle, we found that the traces exhibit markedly uniform beat-to-beat intervals, with only modest deviations around the minimum of the TdP envelope, and greater variability in polymorphic episodes (Fig. 4A). VT and VF can both present as high-frequency, irregular electrical activity, making them difficult to discriminate based on heart rate alone. However, they differ in the temporal stability of their cycle lengths. Clinically, VT occurs at rapid ventricular rates (≥100 bpm) [33] but typically maintains relatively stable cycle lengths, with the standard deviation of instantaneous beat rate reported to remain below ∼30 bpm [40]. All engineered tissue traces fell well below this threshold, providing evidence that the observed rhythms align with tachycardic behavior (Fig. 4B).

Frequency-domain analysis of LQT8 arrhythmia waveforms. A) Beat-to-beat interval of monomorphic, polymorphic, and torsades de pointes-like traces. B) VT-VF discrimination criterion based on beat rate standard deviation. Comparison of Welch's power spectral density of arrhythmic trace waveforms of LQT8 engineered tissues (top) to clinical ECGs (bottom), demonstrating C) monomorphic ventricular tachycardia, D) polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, and E) torsades de pointes.

To distinguish the three distinct waveform phenotypes, Welch's power spectral density (PSD) analysis was performed on non-arrhythmic, monomorphic VT, polymorphic VT, and TdP arrhythmia traces of LQT8 cardiac tissues and corresponding arrhythmic clinical ECGs to quantify their frequency components. The representative PSD profile of non-arrhythmic traces exhibited a dominant fundamental peak accompanied by a secondary high-frequency harmonic reflecting the sharp depolarization-repolarization features of the canonical action potential waveform (Fig. S4). In contrast, the representative PSD profile of monomorphic VT traces similarly exhibited a dominant fundamental peak but a markedly reduced high-frequency harmonic, consistent with loss of canonical action-potential morphology (Fig. 4C). This spectral pattern reflects a highly periodic rhythm, indicative of stable excitation mechanisms, such as those generated by fixed re-entry circuits.

The PSD profile of representative polymorphic VT waveforms showed a similar dominant frequency peak and small secondary high-frequency harmonic, accompanied by a secondary low-frequency component (Fig. 4D). While the dominant frequency suggests preservation of an underlying rapid tachycardic drive, the additional low-frequency component reflects beat-to-beat variability in cycle amplitude and timing, consistent with the heterogeneous activation dynamics that define polymorphic activity. This spectral pattern reflects a loss of temporal regularity and reduced phase coherence, features compatible with disorganized activation or meandering re-entrant activity.

The representative TdP traces, most indicative of LQTS-induced arrhythmic activity, exhibited a characteristic, dominant single-frequency peak accompanied by small symmetrical flanking side peaks (Fig. 4E). As a specific subtype of polymorphic VT, TdP also displayed low-frequency spectral power indicative of temporal modulation. However, the low-frequency peak was significantly smaller, indicating that the amplitude modulation is more regular and periodic, corresponding to the envelope frequency that generates the characteristic waxing-and-waning amplitude pattern of TdP.

Characterization of arrhythmia in engineered LQT8 cardiac tissues

To further understand the mechanisms underlying arrhythmic activity in tissues, activation maps were analyzed during non-arrhythmic and arrhythmic events. Non-arrhythmic depolarization exhibited uniform, linearly propagating conduction across the tissue, indicative of coordinated electrical coupling and synchronous activation (Fig. 5A). In contrast, arrhythmic tissues demonstrated re-entrant spiral wave propagation patterns (Fig. 5B & Video S1).

Traces of tissue membrane potentials revealed a pronounced shift in depolarization-repolarization morphology in arrhythmic events compared with those exhibiting linear conduction (Fig. 5C). In non-arrhythmic tissues, peaks occurred earlier in the cycle, reflecting the characteristic four-phase action potential morphology. In contrast, the asynchronous activation observed during arrhythmic events obscures this depolarization waveform. As such, arrhythmic and non-arrhythmic events could be quantitatively distinguished by their average cycle length-normalized relative peak position.

Clinically, polymorphic and monomorphic VT waveforms are visually differentiated based on the uniformity of QRS amplitude and morphology across successive beats, criteria that parallel the amplitude variation observed in our tissue models. Representative monomorphic and polymorphic tissue traces exhibited distinct beat-to-beat differences in peak amplitude (Fig. 5D). Statistical analysis confirmed a significant difference in amplitude variance between the two groups, with a threshold at approximately 10−3 (Fig. 5E). Additionally, successive mapping of rotor anchor positions in representative monomorphic and polymorphic VT traces revealed distinct spatiotemporal dynamics: monomorphic events exhibited clustered, fixed rotor anchors, whereas polymorphic events displayed continuously shifting anchor points indicative of meandering re-entrant circuits (Fig. 5F-G & S5).

To evaluate pharmacological modulation of arrhythmic susceptibility in engineered tissues, we examined the effects of both pro- and anti-arrhythmic interventions. Isoproterenol was used to activate β-adrenergic signaling and test whether heightened sympathetic stimulation precipitates arrhythmic behavior in LQT8 tissues. Tissue exposure to isoproterenol resulted in a small but statistically significant reduction in APD80 and Repolarization50 when paced at the same frequency (Fig. S6A). Tissues that previously did not exhibit electrical instability developed prominent EADs following drug administration (Fig. S6B). We also observed the occasional development of multiple EADs within a single beat, a phenotype that was not observed in any tissues under baseline conditions (Fig. S6C).

Verapamil, an L-type calcium channel blocker, has been extensively explored as a potential therapeutic option for Timothy syndrome. By attenuating calcium influx, verapamil reduces the QT interval and alleviates re-entrant vulnerability, although its clinical efficacy remains limited [12,41]. In tissues, we found that verapamil treatment significantly attenuated LQT8 tissue APD80 and Repolarization50 (Fig. S6D and E). Overall, we did not observe a significant reduction in EAD formation or arrhythmic activity, consistent with previous studies [42]. Although verapamil terminated sustained arrhythmic activity in one instance, indicating that calcium-channel blockade can disrupt LQT8-associated re-entrant activity, we did not observe a significant benefit across tissues (Fig. S6F).

Generation and electrophysiological assessment of LQT2 & ΨWT iCMs and engineered cardiac tissues

Having established that electromechanically matured engineered cardiac tissues can resolve severe, spontaneous arrhythmogenic phenotypes in LQT8, we asked whether they could expose the arrhythmogenic vulnerability characteristic of LQT2, a more prevalent but phenotypically milder form of genetic LQTS associated with less overt arrhythmic behavior. To do so, we sourced de-identified hiPSCs from an 18-year-old female LQT2 patient with a clinical history of prolonged QTc and intermittent TdP. Genetic testing revealed a heterozygous pathogenic KCNH2 mutation at c.1714G > A [43].

We employed CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing with homology-directed repair to correct the pathogenic variant (Fig. 6A & S7). The CRISPR-corrected hiPSC line (ΨWT) maintained normal karyotype (Fig. 6B) and expression of pluripotency markers (Fig. 6C). LQT2 and ΨWT control hiPSCs both robustly differentiated into iCMs and iCFs (Fig. 6D & S1D-E). LQT2 cardiomyocytes exhibited a characteristically prolonged action potential morphology, reflected by a significant increase in APD80 relative to ΨWT controls (Fig. 6E). To determine whether these phenotypes arise from mutation-associated defects, we performed patch clamp recordings under voltage-clamp conditions and measured IKr currents as the E−4031 sensitive component of the K+ current. Compared to ΨWT controls, LQT2 cardiomyocytes exhibited decreased tail current density (pA/pF) following depolarization at a range of test pulse potentials (Fig. 6F and G).

Engineered cardiac tissues derived from LQT2 hiPSCs similarly demonstrated a significant increase in APD80 and Repolarization50 relative to ΨWT controls, confirming that the disease-associated electrophysiological abnormalities were preserved at the tissue level (Fig. 6H and I). Importantly, spontaneous EADs were also detected in LQT2 tissues, reflecting the presence of pathological repolarization instabilities characteristic of LQTS (Fig. 6J).

Dynamic electrical pacing induces arrhythmic events in engineered LQT2 cardiac tissues

Unlike the LQT8 tissues, LQT2 tissues did not exhibit spontaneous arrhythmic events under baseline conditions or during constant stimulation. This distinction is consistent with LQTS pathology: LQT8 patients often exhibit spontaneous ventricular arrhythmias, whereas LQT2 episodes are typically triggered by sudden increases in heart rate and sympathetic activation. To recapitulate these triggers in vitro, we applied electrical pacing protocols to determine how dynamic changes in beat frequency could reveal the latent arrhythmogenic susceptibility of LQT2 [44]. Simple low- or high-frequency electrical pacing failed to trigger arrhythmias, suggesting that constant high-frequency pacing was insufficient to unmask arrhythmogenic electrical instability (Fig. 7A and B). Arrhythmia-like events in engineered LQT2 tissues emerged only when pacing protocols incorporated a ramped increase in stimulation frequency (Fig. 7C, D, & S8A). ΨWT tissues, subjected to identical pacing protocols, exhibited no arrhythmic behavior, confirming that these responses are dependent on the LQT2 mutation (Fig. S8B). Notably, while sustained elevations in heart rate can precipitate arrhythmias in other forms of congenital LQTS, arrhythmogenesis in KCNH2-mutant myocardium depends on rapid temporal acceleration rather than elevated heart rate alone, underscoring the importance of dynamic pacing conditions for recapitulating the physiological triggers of LQT2 arrhythmogenesis [44].

Having established the conditions required to interrogate LQT2-associated arrhythmogenic vulnerability in vitro, we next examined the resulting waveform geometries. LQT2 tissues exhibited apparent TdP-like activity, closely resembling the clinical ECGs of patients with KCNH2 dysfunction (Fig. 7E). Consistent with our previous observations, arrhythmic events also conformed to monomorphic VT or polymorphic VT-like ECG patterns (Fig. 7F).

Characterization of pacing-induced arrhythmogenesis in engineered LQT2 cardiac tissues

By evaluating action potential recordings before and after frequency-ramp pacing, we found that LQT2 tissues can be robustly paced at 2 Hz, with no spontaneous arrhythmic activity (Fig. 8A). Following frequency-ramp pacing, tissues diverged into either arrhythmic or non-arrhythmic electrophysiological outcomes (Fig. 8B & S8A). ΨWT tissues showed similar baseline activity and remained non-arrhythmic following the same frequency-ramp pacing protocol (Fig. 8C & S8B). Across all conditions, arrhythmic activity was observed exclusively in engineered LQT2 cardiac tissues subjected to ramped pacing but not in LQT2 tissues exposed to constant-frequency stimulation or ΨWT controls (Fig. 8D). We also found that adrenergic stimulation via isoproterenol further increased the incidence of arrhythmic events in LQT2 tissues, suggesting a sensitizing effect of adrenergic stimulation on repolarization stability (Fig. 8D).

Examination of the traces revealed that all arrhythmic events were initiated during the frequency ramp, rather than during baseline or post-ramp pacing (Fig. 8E). Additionally, non-arrhythmic LQT2 tissues exhibited transient, triggered arrhythmia-like activity during the ramp phase that did not progress to prolonged arrhythmias (Fig. 8E). This behavior is consistent with clinical reports in LQTS, where arrhythmic episodes may manifest as brief, self-terminating ventricular ectopy but can also progress to prolonged or malignant arrhythmias [45,46]. In contrast, ΨWT tissues exhibited no evidence of irregular electrical activity under tested conditions, remaining electrically stable throughout the pacing protocol (Fig. 8F).

Consistent with prior observations in our LQT8 model, LQT2 tissues similarly exhibited marked changes in action potential morphology during arrhythmic episodes (Fig. 8G) and spiral re-entry (Video S2). Representative monomorphic VT and polymorphic VT traces displayed pronounced temporal variability and substantially different relative amplitudes across the duration of the recordings (Fig. 8H), with polymorphic events accounting for 73.7% of arrhythmic episodes and the remainder were monomorphic.

Effects of arrhythmic activity in engineered LQT2 cardiac tissues

To assess the functional consequences of arrhythmic activity, we compared the peak amplitude of action potential signals during paced normal rhythm and arrhythmic episodes within the same tissue (Fig. 9A). The arrhythmic traces exhibited markedly reduced amplitude, indicating less synchronous depolarization across the tissue. This electrical disorganization was also evident in contractile recordings, where characteristic periodic force peaks became indistinct during arrhythmic episodes (Fig. 9B). The loss of discernible force transients indicates a profound decline in coordinated contractile force, as expected under conditions of spatially uncoordinated electrical activation.

We found that ramp pacing elicited three distinct electrophysiological outcomes across engineered tissues: (i) non-arrhythmic responses that ceased beating upon termination of stimulation, as expected; (ii) transient arrhythmias that promptly terminated once electrical pacing stopped; and (iii) sustained arrhythmias that persisted well beyond the pacing period, indicating self-propagating electrical instability and the emergence of autonomous re-entrant activity (Fig. 9C). Overall, 52.6% of arrhythmic events were transient; the remaining 47.4% were sustained (Fig. 9D).

Finally, to evaluate whether the observed arrhythmic activity exhibited physiological behavior, we applied a high-voltage single biphasic electrical pulse to tissues exhibiting sustained arrhythmia, analogous to clinical electrical cardioversion. Following this intervention, we observed a brief sequence of post-shock transition beats, after which the arrhythmic activity was terminated (Fig. 9E). Consistent with clinical outcomes, we found that sustained arrhythmias in engineered tissues recapitulate physiological responses to electrical cardioversion.

Discussion

We established a mature patient-specific fully-hiPSC-derived LQTS model capable of recapitulating phenotypic behaviors from cellular-level repolarization instability to tissue-level arrhythmic activity. To address the need for high-fidelity models that faithfully reproduce clinically observed disease phenotypes, our platform enables reproduction of the key features of VT under genotype-appropriate physiological triggers. By combining patient-derived and CRISPR-corrected hiPSC lines with electromechanically matured engineered human cardiac tissues and physiological pacing protocols, this approach advances beyond monolayer and single-cell assays that primarily reveal APD prolongation and EAD formation. We demonstrate the ability to generate monomorphic VT, polymorphic VT, and TdP-like waveforms with characteristic spectral signatures, physiological arrhythmia initiation mechanisms, loss of coordinated contraction during arrhythmic activity, and physiological responses to electrical cardioversion.

Our model recapitulates a mutation-dependent process that precipitates clinical arrhythmic phenotypes associated with physiological tissue architecture and triggers. In both LQT8 and LQT2 tissues, the absence of arrhythmias in ΨWT tissues under identical pacing conditions further confirms that the re-entrant dynamics arise from mutation-specific electrophysiological defects rather than from artifacts of stimulation or tissue architecture. Specifically, spontaneous arrhythmogenicity in LQT8 and frequency-ramped pacing-induced arrhythmogenicity for LQT2 mirror the clinical conditions under which VT is provoked in each. This is particularly important for the study of LQTS subtypes associated with mutation-specific arrhythmia trigger profiles. Arrhythmia recordings in our model directly parallel clinical ECG diagnostic criteria, demonstrating the capability to bridge the current experimental gap between cellular electrophysiology and the manifestation of LQTS observed in patients.

A central conceptual shift we intended to introduce by this work is the unification of traditional in vitro readouts with clinically recognized disease phenotypes that define patient outcomes. Conventional platforms that solely quantify cellular abnormalities are necessary but insufficient to explain the lethal arrhythmic events observed in LQTS. Mechanistically targeted interventions that correct APD or suppress isolated EADs developed in simple systems often fail to prevent VT or hemodynamic collapse in patients. While re-entry can be observed in several engineered models, the mere presence of a spiral wave does not equate to the ECG waveforms that define VT, which are used to diagnose and risk-stratify arrhythmias. Without an in vitro platform that exhibits the higher-order dynamics, it is impossible to determine whether candidate therapies merely normalize cellular biomarkers or genuinely prevent arrhythmogenic activity responsible for syncope or sudden cardiac death.

In prior efforts to model LQTS and arrhythmias using hiPSC-derived systems, the vast majority of studies have focused on resolving mutation-specific electrophysiological abnormalities at the cellular level, such as prolonged action potential duration and early afterdepolarizations [[17], [18], [19],47,48]. However, simple two-dimensional models lack the complexity of tissue-level disease phenotypes and often fail to recapitulate re-entrant activity that underlies associated arrhythmias. While re-entrant activity has been previously observed in engineered tissues, thus far, arrhythmic behavior has only been achieved under non-physiological contexts such as supraphysiological pro-arrhythmic environments or at baseline without direct physiological triggers [18,26,28]. TdP-like field potential traces and spiral waves have been visualized in engineered cardiac tissues under conditions of exaggerated stromal content along with supraphysiologic pharmacological induction using arrhythmogenic hERG channel blocker E−4031 [26]. Likewise, spiral waves have been visualized in engineered atrial tissues composed of otherwise healthy cardiomyocytes [28]. However, in this context, re-entrant activity is not a physiological manifestation of underlying disease, but rather a consequence of the engineered model system. Additionally, LQTS-associated arrhythmic activity is often induced through pharmacological amplification of an already sensitized genetic background [27]. Although isogenic engineered LQT2 tissues have previously been reported, the study primarily focused on mutation-associated mechanisms and functional perturbations, rather than resolving higher-level arrhythmic phenotypes [20]. Together, these limitations highlight the need for human engineered cardiac tissue models that do more than reproduce isolated electrophysiological defects or supraphysiological provocation of arrhythmias. Our study addresses this gap by demonstrating that genetically defined LQTS substrates can give rise to clinically relevant arrhythmic behavior at the tissue level within an advanced human engineered tissue platform.

However, our model also has several limitations. First, while the engineered tissues incorporate architectural anisotropy and multicellularity, they do not fully capture the physiological integration of intrinsic pacemaking and autonomic regulation present in the human heart. While electrical stimulation provides precise control of depolarization that can mimic physiological pacing regimens, it is mechanistically different from sinoatrial node pacing. Incorporating mechanisms that approximate cellular pacemaking and autonomic drive will be essential for future efforts to reproduce the complete physiological sequence of arrhythmic phenomena.

In addition, TdP-like activity within the platform can transition into monomorphic VT through stabilization of re-entrant dynamics, representing a consequential limitation of the system (Fig. S9). More faithful modeling of LQT-associated arrhythmogenesis in engineered tissues will therefore require strategies that suppress rotor-anchoring-permissive tissue interfaces and prevent premature stabilization of re-entrant dynamics, thereby preserving TdP-like behavior.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that functionally mature fully hiPSC-derived engineered human cardiac tissue can reproduce the genotype-specific mechanisms, trigger-dependent arrhythmogenicity, and clinically recognizable arrhythmic patterns arising from congenital LQTS. By integrating patient-specific hiPSC lines, precise genome editing, biomimetic myocardial architecture, and physiologically grounded pacing paradigms, this system bridges the long-standing divide between molecular electrophysiology and tissue-level arrhythmia behavior. As preclinical safety testing increasingly shifts toward human-derived in vitro platforms, such models are needed to enable mechanistically grounded and clinically relevant prediction of arrhythmia liability that conventional reductionist assays cannot achieve [[49], [50], [51]]. Our ability to elicit ECG-like VT activity, re-entrant circuit dynamics, arrhythmia-induced contractile failure, and cardioversion responsiveness within a controlled, fully isogenic context establishes a foundation for more clinically relevant predictive modeling of patient-specific arrhythmia susceptibility and therapeutic discovery.

Looking ahead, the next generation of in vitro models should also capture the later stages of arrhythmia progression, including VF and hemodynamic collapse [52]. Clinically, TdP progresses to VF when wavefront fragmentation generates multiple self-sustaining re-entrant wavelets [53]. However, the small size and structural simplicity of engineered tissues constrain wavelet proliferation, limiting their ability to support the entirely disorganized dynamics intrinsic to true VF. Sudden cardiac death arises not solely from electrical instability but also from the cessation of effective ventricular output and collapse of coronary perfusion. Although the platform resolves arrhythmia-induced contractile failure, incorporating vascular networks and contraction-coupled flow will be essential for recapitulating the complete electromechanical cascade that culminates in hemodynamic failure during lethal arrhythmias. As such, progress in vascular integration is vital to advancing the physiological fidelity of in vitro arrhythmia models [54].

Methods

hiPSC culture. hiPSC lines were obtained through material transfer agreements from B. Conklin, Gladstone Institute (Healthy, WTC-11 line), M. Yazawa, Columbia University (LQT8, TS1-E3-5 & isogenic control), and Greenstone Biosciences Inc. (LQT2, L2177). hiPSCs were maintained in mTeSR Plus medium (Stem Cell Technologies) with 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco) on Matrigel-coated plates. Cells were passaged every 3–4 days using 0.5 mM EDTA (Invitrogen) and treated with 5 μM Y-27632 (Tocris) for 24 h.

Generation of CRISPR-corrected hiPSC lines. hiPSCs were pre-treated with 5 μM Y-27632 for 2 h prior to nucleofection. Cells were dissociated with 0.5 mM EDTA, and 1 × 106 cells were mixed into a reaction mix containing 3 μL sgRNA (50 μM; Alt-R sgRNA, IDT), 3 μL HDR donor sequence (500 ng/μL; Alt-R Donor Oligos, IDT), 2 μL Cas9 protein (62 μM; Alt-R S.p. HiFi Cas9 Nuclease V3, IDT), 74 μL P3 primary cell buffer, and 18 μL Supplement in a 100 μL cuvette (Lonza V4XP-3024). Cells were electroporated in a 4D-Nucleofector Core Unit (Lonza) using program CA137. After nucleofection, the cells were resuspended in mTeSR Plus medium supplemented with 10% CloneR2 (StemCell Technologies) and 50 nM Alt-R HDR Enhancer v2 (IDT), and then seeded into Matrigel-coated 6-well plates. mTeSR Plus medium was refreshed after 24 h. 3 days after recovery, edited cells were dissociated, and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) was performed using a Sony MA900, depositing single cells into Matrigel-coated 96-well plates containing mTeSR Plus medium and 10% CloneR2. mTeSR Plus medium was refreshed after 72 h. After an additional 7 days of growth, the colonies were passaged into duplicate 24-well plates for parallel clonal expansion and genotyping. Karyotyping was conducted by the Clinical Cytogenetics Laboratory at Columbia University.

Genotyping of hiPSCs. Genomic DNA was isolated by adding 100 μL of Lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 50 mM KCl, 2.5 mM EDTA, 0.5% v/v NP-40, 0.5% Tween-20) supplemented with 10 μL/mL Proteinase K (20 mg/mL; Thermo Scientific) to each well of a 24-well plate containing hiPSCs, followed by 30 min incubation at 60 °C. Following cell lysis, 100 μL of 100% ethanol containing 150 mM NaCl was added to each well. The plate was then centrifuged at 3000×g for 20 min, and the supernatant was decanted. The DNA pellet was then washed twice by adding 100 μL of 70% ethanol, centrifuging at 3000×g for 10 min, and decanting the supernatant. The samples were then air-dried and resuspended in 50 μL nuclease-free water. 1 μL of isolated DNA was used per PCR reaction with Platinum II Hot-start Green PCR Mastermix 2 × (Invitrogen). Sanger sequencing analysis was performed by GENEWIZ (Azenta Life Sciences).

Cardiomyocyte differentiation, proliferation, and culture. Cardiomyocytes were differentiated from hiPSCs based on the protocol of Burridge et al., with modifications [55]. 48 h prior to differentiation (Day −2), hiPSCs were replated in Matrigel-coated 6-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 106 cells per well in mTeSR Plus medium with 5 μM Y-27632. The medium was refreshed with mTeSR Plus the following day. At the start of differentiation (Day 0), the medium was switched to 3 mL per well of chemically defined medium containing RPMI1640 (Gibco), human recombinant albumin (500 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich), L-ascorbic acid (213 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (CDM3), supplemented with 6-8 μM CHIR99021 (Tocris). On day 1 of differentiation, medium was refreshed with CDM3. On day 2 of differentiation, medium was refreshed with CDM3 with 2 μM Wnt-C59 (Tocris). On days 4, 6, and 8, medium was refreshed with CDM3. On days 10–12 post-differentiation, metabolic selection was performed by switching to starvation medium containing RPMI1640 without glucose (Gibco) supplemented with 5 mM sodium-L-lactate (Sigma-Aldrich). On day 12, beating cardiomyocytes were switched to RPMI 1640 supplemented with B27 (ThermoFisher) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. On day 14, cardiomyocytes were expanded according to the protocol of Buikema et al., with modifications [56]. Cardiomyocytes were dissociated with 1 mL per well of 200 U/mL collagenase type II (Worthington Biochem) in Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (Gibco) for 1 h and plated in a Matrigel-coated T150 flask in proliferation medium containing RPMI1640, B27 supplement, and 2 μM CHIR99021–supplemented with 5 μM Y-27632 at 4 × 105 cells/cm2. Medium was refreshed with proliferation medium after 24 h and every other day thereafter. Every 7 days of proliferation, cardiomyocytes were dissociated using TrypLE Select Enzyme (Gibco) and passaged at a 1:3 ratio in proliferation medium supplemented with 5 μM Y-27632 for 24 h, up to passage 4. 24 h prior to tissue fabrication or monolayer cell seeding, cardiomyocytes were switched back to RPMI1640 with B27 medium. Cardiomyocyte differentiations with >95% Troponin-T positivity determined by flow cytometry were used for all experiments.

Cardiac fibroblast derivation and culture. Cardiac fibroblasts were differentiated from hiPSCs based on the protocol of Zhang et al., with modifications [57]. On day 5 of cardiomyocyte differentiation, one well of a 6-well plate was dissociated using 1 mL of Accutase (Innovative Cell Technologies) and plated in a Matrigel-coated 6-well plate at 1:24 in 2 mL per well of Advanced DMEM/GlutaMAX (Gibco) medium with 1% penicillin-streptomycin supplemented with 5 μM CHIR99021, 2 μM retinoic acid, 5 μM Y-27632, and 1% fetal bovine serum. After 24 h (day 6), the medium was refreshed with advanced DMEM/Glutamax medium supplemented with 5 μM CHIR99021 and 2 μM retinoic acid. On day 8, the medium was refreshed with DMEM/GlutaMAX. On day 10, cells were dissociated using Accutase and passaged 1:6 in a Matrigel-coated 6-well plate in DMEM/GlutaMAX medium supplemented with 2 μM SB431542 (SelleckChem). The cells were refreshed with the same medium every other day. On day 14, cells were dissociated using Accutase and plated in a Matrigel-coated 6-well plate in FGM3 medium (Promocell) supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 20 ng/mL FGF2 (Peprotech), and 10 μM SB431542 at a density of 1 × 105 cells/cm2. The cells were refreshed with the same medium after 48 h. On day 18, cells were passaged at 1 × 105 cells/cm2. After 48 h (day 20), medium was refreshed with FGM3 medium (Promocell) supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin and 10 μM SB431542. Upon confluency, cardiac fibroblasts were dissociated using TrypLE Select Enzyme and passaged in Matrigel-coated T150 flasks and maintained in FGM3 medium with 10 μM SB431542 up to passage 5.

Fabrication of tissue culture platform. The cardiac tissue platform was generated using our previously developed protocols [30]. The platform consists of three components: elastomeric pillars for tissue attachment, molds for tissue fabrication, and a stimulation platform (Fig. S10A). Tissue fabrication molds were custom-milled from polycarbonate using a computer numerical control (CNC) milling machine. Pillar supports were cast from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, Dow Corning Sylgard 184) and anchored onto polycarbonate support structures. First, polycarbonate supports were CNC-milled and inserted into custom Delrin (polyoxymethylene) molds. PDMS (10:1 base-to-curing agent ratio) was mixed and poured into the molds, degassed in a vacuum chamber for 1 h, and then cured in an oven at 60 °C overnight. Tissue stimulation platforms were milled from polysulfone. Carbon electrodes (Ohio Carbon Blank) were fitted into slots machined on both sides of the stimulation well. Electrodes were connected to a Grass S88X stimulator via platinum wires (Surepure Chemetals) for electrical pacing.

Generation and maintenance of engineered cardiac tissues. Cardiac tissues were generated using our previously developed protocol [30], with some modifications as specified here. Briefly, 24 h prior to tissue fabrication, tissue molds were coated with 2% Pluronic F-127 (Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS overnight at 37 °C. Immediately prior to tissue fabrication, Pluronic was removed, and pillar supports were fitted onto the molds for tissue casting. Cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts were dissociated with 1 × TrypLE Select enzyme. Cells were combined at a 3:1 ratio (75% iCMs and 25% iCFs) and resuspended in 5 mg/mL fibrinogen (Sigma-Aldrich) such that 90 μL of the mixture contained 4 × 106 cells. For each tissue, 90 μL of cell suspension was mixed with 10 μL of 25 U/mL thrombin and deposited within the mold. After 30 min, tissues were gently demolded and transferred to stimulation platforms in tissue culture medium (RPMI1640 + B27, 213 μg/mL L-ascorbic acid, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin) supplemented with 10% v/v FBS, 5 μM Y-27632, and 3 mg/mL 6-aminocaproic acid (Sigma-Aldrich). After 24 h, the media was refreshed and then changed every other day thereafter: 40 mL tissue culture medium with 3 mg/mL 6-aminocaproic acid for the first 7 days, followed by tissue culture medium supplemented with AlbuMAX (1% w/v, ThermoFisher) for days 7–28. For electromechanically matured conditions, electrical stimulation was administered as 1 ms biphasic square-wave pulses at 3.5 V using a Grass S88X stimulator. Electrical stimulation was initiated on day 7 at 2 Hz and increased by 0.33 Hz per day until reaching 6 Hz until day 21. Tissues were then stimulated at 2 Hz until day 28. All arrhythmia recordings were performed on day 28 (Fig. S10B).

Measurement of membrane potential. Membrane potential was measured using the FluoVolt fast voltage-sensitive fluorescent dye (ThermoFisher). For preparation of the dye loading buffer, FluoVolt (1:1000) was mixed with PowerLoad (1:100, ThermoFisher), then diluted in pre-warmed Tyrode's buffer (Sigma Aldrich). Engineered cardiac tissue or cardiomyocyte monolayers were incubated in dye-loading buffer for 30 min at 37 °C, protected from light, then washed twice with dye-free Tyrode's buffer. Samples were equilibrated for an additional 30 min in fresh Tyrode's buffer before imaging. Fluorescence imaging was performed on an Olympus IX83 inverted fluorescence microscope equipped with an Andor Zyla high-speed sCMOS camera. Excitation was set at 480 ± 20 nm, and emission light was collected at 525 ± 25 nm using standard filter sets. Video sequences were acquired under temperature- and carbon dioxide-controlled conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). For all electrically paced recordings, a Grass S88X stimulator (2 ms monophasic square wave at 5.0 V) was triggered via an Arduino Uno microcontroller connected to a computer-controlled TTL output. Fluorescence signals were background-subtracted and normalized using custom Python scripts. Waveform analysis was performed on spatially averaged traces. For LQT8 tissues, spontaneous arrhythmic activity was recorded without electrical pacing; tissues that did not exhibit spontaneous arrhythmias and remained quiescent were electrically paced at 2 Hz for action potential evaluation. LQT2 tissue arrhythmias were captured under dynamically controlled pacing. The pacing regimen involved 5 s with no pacing, 10 s of 2 Hz constant pacing, followed by 2–4 Hz pacing ramped at 0.1 Hz increase per pulse (not included in the non-ramp condition), then 8 s of 4 Hz pacing, finally 5 s of no pacing.

Measurement of Caffeine-stimulated Calcium Transients. Calcium transients were measured using the Fluo-4 AM fluorescence-based calcium indicator (ThermoFisher). For preparation of the dye loading buffer, Fluo-4 (2 μM) dye loading and imaging were performed as described above for FluoVolt. Baseline calcium transients were first recorded under 1 Hz electrical pacing, after which pacing was discontinued, and caffeine (5 mM, ThermoFisher) was acutely administered to assess sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium release capacity. Fluorescence signals were normalized to the 1 Hz baseline amplitude.

Whole cell electrophysiology. Whole-cell voltage-clamp recordings for hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes were collected at room temperature using an Axopatch 200B amplifier (Axon Instruments). Glass pipettes (MTW 150-F4, World Precision Instruments) were pulled with a horizontal puller (P97; Sutter Instruments Co.) and fire-polished (Microforge; Narishige), resulting in 1–3 MΩ resistances, before series resistance compensation of 70%. For recording CaV1.2 currents from LQT8 and ΨWT hiPSC CMs, internal pipette solution contained 135 mM CsMeSO3, 5 mM CsCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, 4 mM MgATP, 10 mM HEPES, and 10 mM BAPTA, adjusted to 290–295 mOsm with CsMeSO3 and pH 7.4 with CsOH. The extracellular bath solution contained 140 mM TEA-MeSO3, 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), and either 40 mM CaCl2 or BaCl2, adjusted to 300 mOsm with TEA-MeSO3. For VDI measurements, we used a family of test pulses from −50 to +50 mV with repetition intervals of 20–120 s from a holding potential of −80 mV. Custom MATLAB (Mathworks) software was used to determine peak current and fraction of peak current remaining after 300 ms (r300) of depolarization. Data were sampled at 50 kHz and low-pass-filtered at 2 kHz.

For recording IKr currents, an internal solution containing 110 mM KCl, 5 mM K2ATP, 11 mM EGTA, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM CaCl2 and 1 mM MgCl2 (pH adjusted to 7.3 with KOH) was used. The extracellular solution contained: 132 mM NaCl, 4.8 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 1.2 mM MgCl2, 10 mM HEPES and 5 mM glucose (pH adjusted to 7.4 with NaOH). Voltage protocols were applied both before and after bath perfusion of 2 μM E−4031, a selective IKr blocker. IKr currents were determined by subtracting the total current in the presence of E−4031 from the baseline current. To measure voltage-dependence, membrane currents were elicited by a family of test pulses of 3 s duration from −50 to +30 mV in 10 mV intervals from a holding potential of −40 mV. Tail currents were then measured during a subsequent 6 s step to −40 mV. The peak tail current amplitudes were normalized to cell capacitance. Data were sampled at 10 kHz and low-pass-filtered at 1 kHz.

Flow cytometry. The differentiation efficiency of iCMs was determined by Troponin T staining using flow cytometry. Dissociated single cells were pelleted, fixed in 4% PFA for 15 min at room temperature, and washed twice with PBS. Permeabilization was performed using 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS, followed by incubation with an Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated troponin-T antibody (Cat no. 565744, BD Biosciences) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After 30 min of staining, the cells were washed three times with PBS and analyzed using a NovoCyte Quanteon cell analyzer.

Immunofluorescence. Samples were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma) in PBS for 15 min at room temperature for monolayers and overnight at 4 °C for tissues, and subsequently washed three times with PBS for 5 min each. Samples were then permeabilized in 0.25% v/v Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min at room temperature, followed by incubation in blocking buffer (5% v/v FBS in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature. They were then incubated overnight at 4 °C in primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer. Cardiomyocytes were stained with Troponin-T (1:400, Abcam, cat. no. ab45932) and α-actinin (1:100, Sigma, cat. no. A7811). Cardiac fibroblasts were stained with Vimentin (1:1000, Abcam, cat. no. ab24525) and GATA4 (1:100, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, cat. no. sc-25310). hiPSCs were stained with OCT4 (1:500, abcam, ab19857), SOX2 (1:500, abcam, ab97959), and NANOG (1:500, abcam, ab21624). Samples were then washed three times in washing buffer (0.025% v/v Triton X-100 in PBS) for 5 min each. Secondary antibodies were diluted in blocking buffer, and the samples were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 2 h. Samples were washed three times in washing buffer and counterstained using DAPI (1 μg/mL in DPBS, BD Pharmingen). Samples were imaged with NIS-Elements on a Nikon Ti2 Scanning Confocal Microscope using a 20 × objective or a 40 × /1.3 Plan Fluor oil-immersion lens, denoised, and analyzed in ImageJ (Fiji).

RT-qPCR. Lysis of engineered cardiac tissues was performed using 300 μL of RNA lysis buffer and stainless-steel beads to mechanically disrupt the tissues in the Mini-BeadbeaterTM (Biospec Products) and stored on ice. mRNA was then extracted from tissue lysate using the RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen, cat. no. 74106) according to the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA samples were obtained from mRNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription kit (Applied Biosystems, cat. no. 4368813) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Reverse transcriptase quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed with the Fast SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, cat. no. 4385612) according to manufacturer's instructions in a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) thermocycler using 40 cycles of two-step PCR protocol of 95 °C melting for 3 s and 60 °C annealing and extension for 30 s, prior to an initial hot start at 95 °C for 20 s. Primer oligo sequences (5′-3′) used were SCN5A: FWD AGCTGGCTGATGTGATGGTC & REV CACTTGTGCCTTAGGTTGCC, CACNA1C: FWD TGATTCCAACGCCACCAATTC & REV GAGGAGTCCATAGGCGATTACT, KCNH2: FWD CAACCTGGGCGACCAGATAG & REV GGTGTTGGGAGAGACGTTGC, and GAPDH: FWD GAGTCAACGGATTTGGTCGT & REV GACAAGCTTCCCGTTCTCAG.

Western blot. Protein samples were obtained using Pierce IP Lysis Buffer (ThermoFisher, Cat no. #87787) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (ThermoFisher, Cat no. #78442) from nascent and matured engineered cardiac tissues. Lysis was performed using magnetic beads to mechanically disrupt the tissues in the Mini-BeadbeaterTM (Biospec Products) and kept on ice for 30 min. Cell debris was removed by centrifuging the lysate at 16000 g for 10 min and the total protein content was quantified using Pierce BCA kit. Lysates were mixed with 4x Laemmli buffer and boiled at 95 °C for 5 min and 25 μg total protein was loaded in a 4-20% Tris-glycine gel (ThermoFisher, Cat no. #XP04205BOX). The gels were transferred onto 0.2 μm nitrocellulose membrane, and blocked in 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST. Incubation with primary antibodies was done overnight at 4 °C in 1% BSA in TBST. The following primary antibodies were used: Cav1.2 (1:250, Invitrogen, Cat no. MA5-27717), hERG (1:200, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat no. AB5908), and HRP-conjugated β-Actin (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technologies, #5125). The next day, the membranes were washed and blotted with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000, Cell Signaling Technologies, Cat no. #7076 for Anti-mouse IgG and #7074 for Anti-rabbit IgG) in milk for 1 h at room temperature. Western blot membranes were imaged on a Licor Odyssey Fc system, after incubation with enhanced chemiluminescent (ECL) substrate (SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate, ThermoFisher), and the band intensities were quantified in the ImageStudio Lite Software (v5.2).

Statistical Analysis. Statistical significance was determined using an unpaired, two-tailed Welch's t-test or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test using GraphPad Prism 10.6 (GraphPad Software). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
