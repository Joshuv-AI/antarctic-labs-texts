Well-posedness of the motion of a rigid body immersed in a compressible inviscid fluid
Frédéric Rousset, Pei Su

Abstract
. We consider a rigid body freely moving in a compressible inviscid fluid within a bounded domain Ω ⊂ ℝ 3 {\Omega}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3} . The fluid is thereby governed by the non necessarily isentropic compressible Euler equations, while the rigid body obeys the conservation of linear and angular momentum. This forms a coupled system comprising an ODE and the initial boundary value problem (IBVP) of a hyperbolic system with characteristic boundary in a moving domain, where the fluid velocity matches the solid velocity along the normal direction of the solid boundary. We establish the existence of a unique local classical solution to this coupled system. To construct the solution, we first perform a change of variables to reformulate the problem in a fixed spatial domain, and then analyze an approximate system with a non-characteristic boundary. For this nonlinear approximate system, we use the better regularity for the trace of the pressure on the boundary to contruct a solution by a fixed-point argument in which the fluid motion and the solid motion are updated in successive steps. We are then able to derive estimates independent of the regularization parameter and to pass to the limit by a strong compactness arguments.

1.
Introduction

We study the interaction between a compressible inviscid fluid and a rigid body, which is completely immersed in the fluid. The fluid is described by the three dimensional non necessarily isentropic
compressible Euler equations
. The motion of the rigid body obeys the conservation of linear and angular momentum. We are interested in establishing the local well-posedness of this fluid-body coupled system in bounded domains. The approach we are using can be extended to an arbitrary number of solids in a straightforward way. We shall now precisely state the problem.

1.2.
Main result

We recall that we have defined
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

To quantify the regularity of solutions in a time-space domain

Remark 1.2
.

We obtain only a short-time existence result. As with all fluid–rigid body systems, one restriction is that collision between the solid and the boundary of the fluid domain is not allowed. Nevertheless, in addition, here the time interval must be chosen sufficiently small to prevent the formation of singularities in the fluid. Indeed, since the fluid is governed by the compressible Euler system, we cannot, in general, expect a global classical solution up to the time of collision with the boundary. By using the finite speed of propagation property of the Euler equations, it is easy to see that the motion of the solid cannot prevent shock formation in the fluid. For instance,
one can take initial data that leads to shock formation and place the solid in a large region
of constant pressure and zero velocity, so that at the time of singularity the solid will be still
immerged in a region
of constant pressure and zero velocity and thus is still not moving.

Remark 1.3
.

For the sake of simplicity, we just treat one rigid body in this statement. The results in Theorem
1.1
can be directly extended to the system with many bodies, as long as there is also no collision between the bodies.
To do so, it suffices to adapt the cut-off function in the change of variables. The details can be found in Remark
2.2
.

The first difficulty in the analysis is to find a suitable change of coordinates that reformulates the fluid-solid system
(
1.1
)
–
(
1.5
)
in a fixed spatial domain. On the one hand,
for inviscid fluids, along the solid boundary the tangential component of the fluid velocity is in general different from that of the solid. Consequently, the use of a Lagrangian map derived from the fluid’s Lagrangian coordinates does not yield a time-independent domain, since the solid continues to move with nonzero tangential velocity (except in the very special case where the solid is a sphere).
On the other hand, the choice of the frame moving with the solid for performing the change of variables, i.e. taking
x=Q^{\intercal}(t)(y-h(t))
, is not suited to fluid domains with boundaries as this makes the exterior boundary to be moving in the transformed frame. Moreover, this approach is also not well-suited to configurations involving multiple rigid bodies.
To overcome these issues, we introduce an artificial velocity field that smoothly transitions between the solid velocity on the solid boundary and the zero velocity on the exterior boundary of the fluid domain. The flow map associated with this vector field is then used to define the change of variables. More details are presented in Section
2
.

After reformulating the problem on a fixed spatial domain, we can derive the high-order a-priori estimates for the coupled model, using tools similar to those developed for the compressible Euler system in
[
24
]
. Based on the conservation of total energy in the coupled system, we first obtain high-order energy estimates for tangential and time derivatives.
To recover normal derivatives, we can use the transport equation solved by the entropy and the vorticity on the one-hand
and the fluid system itself to convert time and tangential derivatives into normal derivatives for the acoustic components close to the boundary
on the other hand.
The main difficulty is then the construction of a solution.
A first attempt would be to use an iterative approach, where assuming that
the solid motion is given, we solve the fluid system and then using in particular the obtained pressure we update the motion
of the solid by using (
1.2
), (
1.3
). Nevertheless, this scheme yields a loss of derivative at each step due to the lack
of control of the trace of the pressure on the boundary.
We shall thus first use a non-characteristic approximation, in the sense that the fluid part of the coupled system
is modified through the addition of small transport terms to make the boundary non-characteristic. The resulting approximate system allows for control of the trace of the pressure on the boundary and thus it can be solved eventually using the iterative scheme described above. We can then derive uniform estimates with respect to the small perturbation parameter by adapting the strategy of
[
24
]
above.
Finally, we obtain the existence of solution for the original fluid–solid coupled system by passing to the limit using strong compactness arguments.
Note that the method of non-characteristic approximations has been adopted for instance in
[
24
]
(see also
[
13
]
for a more recent utilisation) to construct solutions to the compressible Euler system in a bounded domain. This technique provides an effective way to develop existence theories for nonlinear hyperbolic systems, while circumventing the delicate analysis of linear characteristic hyperbolic IBVP.

3.2.
Proof of Theorem
3.1

We are now in a position to give the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
We shall use a fixed-point argument.
To set up the fixed-point procedure, we introduce the following space:

(3.21)

\mathcal{K}_{T}:=\begin{Bmatrix}\Theta=(L,W)\in C^{m+1}([0,T])\left|{\hbox{\hskip 1.0pt}}\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta(0)=\mathcal{I}_{\Theta}^{k,{\varepsilon}}(U_{0}^{n},\Theta_{0},F^{k}),k\leqslant m\right.\end{Bmatrix}.

For
\Theta=(L,W)\in\mathcal{K}_{T}
we introduce the map
\Lambda(\Theta)
as below:

(3.22)

\Lambda(\Theta):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &l_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(L\times W+\frac{1}{m}\int_{\partial\mathcal{S}(0)}\overline{p}_{\Theta}^{\varepsilon}n_{0}{\rm d}\hbox{\hskip 0.5pt}\Gamma\right){\rm d}\hbox{\hskip 0.5pt}\sigma,\\
&\omega_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(W\times(J_{0}^{-1}W)+J_{0}^{-1}\int_{\partial\mathcal{S}(0)}x\times\overline{p}_{\Theta}^{\varepsilon}{\hbox{\hskip 1.0pt}}n_{0}{\rm d}\hbox{\hskip 0.5pt}\Gamma\right){\rm d}\hbox{\hskip 0.5pt}\sigma,\end{aligned}\right.

where
U^{\varepsilon}_{\Theta}
is the solution of (
3.9
) for a given
\Theta
which is provided by Proposition
3.3
.

Our main task in the following is to show that the map
\Lambda
possesses a fixed point in the space
\mathcal{K}_{T}
.

Let
B_{T,R}^{\mathcal{K}}
be the ball defined as

B_{T,R}^{\mathcal{K}}:=\left\{\Theta\in\mathcal{K}_{T}~\text{with}~\|\Theta\|_{C^{m+1}([0,T])}\leqslant R\right\},

for some
R
large enough and
T
small enough. We shall always consider

smaller than

given by Proposition
3.3
.

Proposition 3.4
.

There exists a time
T>0
and
R>0
, such that the map
\Lambda
in
(
3.22
)
is well-defined on
B_{T,R}^{\mathcal{K}}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\Lambda:B_{T,R}^{\mathcal{K}}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow B_{T,R}^{\mathcal{K}}

\displaystyle\Theta

\displaystyle\mapsto\Lambda(\Theta).

Moreover,
\Lambda
has a fixed point in
B_{T,R}^{\mathcal{K}}.

Proof.

We first prove that the map
\Lambda
sends the ball
B_{R}^{\mathcal{K}}
into itself for

R
sufficiently large and
T
sufficiently small.
To do this, we need to show that
\Lambda(\Theta)\in C^{m+1}[0,T]
and that it satisfies the compatibility conditions in
(
3.21
)
.

We first note that
\Lambda(\Theta)
verifies the compatibility condition in
(
3.21
)
.
This follows directly from the definition of
\mathcal{I}_{\Theta}^{k,{\varepsilon}}(U_{0}^{n},\Theta_{0},F^{k})
.

Now we study the mapping property of
\Lambda(\Theta)
.
Note that from Proposition
3.3
, for a given
R>0
there exists
T_{0}
such that for every
\Theta\in\mathcal{B}_{T,R}^{\mathcal{K}}
,
for
T\leqslant T_{0}
there exists a unique

U^{\varepsilon}_{\Theta}\in X^{m+1}_{T}
solution of (
3.9
)
and (
3.12
) implies that

\|U_{\Theta}^{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{m+1}((0,T)\times\partial\mathcal{F}(0))}\leqslant M_{\varepsilon}.

From the the definition of
\Lambda(\Theta)
, we clearly get that

\Lambda(\Theta)\in C^{m+1}([0,T])
and that

\|\Lambda(\Theta)\|_{C^{m+1}[0,T]}\leqslant C_{0}+CT^{1\over 2}M_{\varepsilon},

where
C_{0}
only depends on the initial data and

is harmless.
Indeed, we estimate

\displaystyle\|\Lambda(\Theta)\|_{C^{m+1}[0,T]}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left|\begin{bmatrix}l_{0}\\
\omega_{0}\end{bmatrix}\right|+T^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}L\\
W\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{C^{m+1}[0,T]}\|W\|_{C^{m+1}[0,T]}

\displaystyle\quad+\|n_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\partial_{\mathcal{S}}(0))}\max_{|\beta|\leqslant m+1}\int_{0}^{T}\bigl(\|\partial_{t}^{\beta}\overline{p}_{\Theta}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\mathcal{S}(0))}+\|\partial_{t}^{|\beta|-1}\overline{p}_{\Theta}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\mathcal{S}(0))}\bigr){\rm d}\hbox{\hskip 0.5pt}t

\displaystyle\lesssim|\Theta_{0}|+T^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\Theta\|_{C^{m+1}[0,T]}^{2}+c(n_{0})T^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\overline{p}_{\Theta}^{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{m+1}((0,T)\times\partial\mathcal{S}(0)))}

\displaystyle\lesssim|\Theta_{0}|+T^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{2}+c(n_{0})T^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\overline{p}_{\Theta}^{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{m+1}((0,T)\times\partial\mathcal{S}(0))}

\displaystyle\lesssim|\Theta_{0}|+T^{1\over 2}R^{2}+T^{\frac{1}{2}}M_{\varepsilon},

where

depending on

,
\|U_{0}^{n}\|_{H^{m+1}}
and
\|F\|_{H^{m+1}((0,T)\times\mathcal{F}(0))}
is introduced in

3.12
)
.

Consequently, by choosing

large enough and then

sufficiently small, we obtain that

\|\Lambda(\Theta)\|_{C^{m+1}[0,T]}\leqslant R.

Note that by using estimates similar to the ones above, we also obtain that

(3.23)

|\partial_{t}^{m+1}\Lambda(\Theta)(t)-\partial_{t}^{m+1}\Lambda(\Theta)(s)|\leqslant C_{0}+(t-s)^{1\over 2}C(R)\qquad\forall{\hbox{\hskip 1.0pt}}s,\,t,\,0\leqslant s\leqslant t\leqslant T.

We are now in a position to prove the existence of a fixed point.
We pick
\Theta^{0}\in\mathcal{B}_{T,R}^{\mathcal{K}}
and consider the iteration

(3.24)

\Theta^{n+1}=\Lambda(\Theta^{n})\qquad\forall{\hbox{\hskip 1.0pt}}n\geqslant 0.

Based on the choice of

, for every

Moreover, from (
3.23
), we also get that

According to the the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem, this yields that up to a subsequence,
\Theta^{n}
converges to
\Theta
in
C^{m+1}[0,T]
.
To conclude for a fixed point from (

converges in any topology weaker than

This will be a direct consequence of the following Lemma:

Lemma 3.5

With the same assumptions as in Proposition
3.3
, and for given
\Theta_{1}
,
\Theta_{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{T,R}^{\mathcal{K}}

Before, giving the proof of this Lemma, we shall finish the proof of Proposition

.
Using Lemma

and the definition of
\Lambda
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\|\Lambda(\Theta_{1})-\Lambda(\Theta_{2})\|_{C^{0}([0,T])}

\displaystyle\leqslant T\|\Theta_{1}-\Theta_{2}\|_{C^{0}[0,T]}(\|\Theta_{1}\|_{C^{0}[0,T]}+\|\Theta_{2}\|_{C^{0}[0,T]})+T^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\overline{p}_{L_{1}}^{\varepsilon}-\overline{p}_{L_{2}}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times\partial\mathcal{S}(0))}\|n_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\mathcal{S}(0))}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2RT\|\Theta_{1}-\Theta_{2}\|_{C^{0}[0,T}+C_{\varepsilon}[M_{{\varepsilon}},R]T\|\Theta_{1}-\Theta_{2}\|_{C^{m}[0,T]}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{\varepsilon}[M_{{\varepsilon}},R]T\|\Theta_{1}-\Theta_{2}\|_{C^{m}[0,T]}.

\int_{\partial\mathcal{S}(0)}Z^{\beta}\overline{p}^{\varepsilon}(Z^{\beta}(\overline{\omega}^{\varepsilon}\cdot(x\times n_{0}))){\rm d}\hbox{\hskip 0.5pt}\Gamma=\frac{m}{2}\frac{{\rm d}\hbox{\hskip 0.5pt}}{{\rm d}\hbox{\hskip 0.5pt}t}|\partial_{t}^{\beta_{0}}\overline{l}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\rm d}\hbox{\hskip 0.5pt}}{{\rm d}\hbox{\hskip 0.5pt}t}J_{0}\partial_{t}^{\beta_{0}}\overline{\omega}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial_{t}^{\beta_{0}}\overline{\omega}^{\varepsilon}\\
\quad-{\hbox{\hskip 1.0pt}}\sum_{|\gamma_{0}|\neq 0,|\sigma_{0}+\gamma_{0}|\leqslant m}\left(m(\partial_{t}^{\sigma_{0}}\overline{l}^{\varepsilon}\times\partial_{t}^{\gamma_{0}}\overline{\omega}^{\varepsilon})\cdot\partial_{t}^{\beta_{0}}\overline{l}^{\varepsilon}+((J_{0}\partial_{t}^{\gamma_{0}}\overline{\omega}^{\varepsilon})\times\partial_{t}^{\sigma_{0}}\overline{\omega}^{\varepsilon})\cdot\partial_{t}^{\beta_{0}}\overline{\omega}^{\varepsilon}\right).

Moreover, we have the estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{|\gamma_{0}|\neq 0,|\sigma_{0}+\gamma_{0}|\leqslant m}\left(m(\partial_{t}^{\sigma_{0}}\overline{l}^{\varepsilon}\times\partial_{t}^{\gamma_{0}}\overline{\omega}^{\varepsilon})\cdot\partial_{t}^{\beta_{0}}\overline{l}^{\varepsilon}+((J_{0}\partial_{t}^{\gamma_{0}}\overline{\omega}^{\varepsilon})\times\partial_{t}^{\sigma_{0}}\overline{\omega}^{\varepsilon})\cdot\partial_{t}^{\beta_{0}}\overline{\omega}^{\varepsilon}\right)

that

The proof is complete.
∎

4.2.
Vorticity estimate

From the estimates in Proposition
4.1
, only the estimates of normal derivatives of
U^{\varepsilon}
close to the boundary are missing. Since the boundary becomes characteristic when
{\varepsilon}
tends
to zero, in order to get uniform estimates, we cannot use the system (

) to express normal derivatives in terms of conormal ones
close to the boundary for all the components of

which rely

Here, since we have in the velocity equation

we consider a modified vorticity in order to avoid the appearance of second order derivatives of the pressure.
We thus instead take the curl of the product

\text{Curl}{\hbox{\hskip 1.0pt}}(M\overline{u}^{\varepsilon})

This is actually a good choice because of the behavior of the matrix

(4.4)

with

(4.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}[\Theta^{\varepsilon},U^{\varepsilon}]:

\displaystyle+{\varepsilon}``\nabla(M\overline{u}^{\varepsilon})\cdot\nabla((\eta^{\varepsilon}M)^{-1}\nu)"+{\varepsilon}\text{Curl}{\hbox{\hskip 1.0pt}}\left((\eta^{\varepsilon}M)^{-1}\overline{u}^{\varepsilon}\nabla M\cdot\nu\right)+(\eta^{\varepsilon})^{-2}\nabla\eta^{\varepsilon}\times\nabla\overline{p}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle+\text{Curl}{\hbox{\hskip 1.0pt}}\left(M\left(\nabla\mathcal{J}{\hbox{\hskip 1.0pt}}\mathcal{J}^{-1}\overline{u}^{\varepsilon}\cdot(\overline{u}-\overline{u}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\varepsilon})+\partial_{t}\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}^{-1}\overline{u}^{\varepsilon}\right)+{\varepsilon}(\eta^{{\varepsilon}})^{-1}F_{u}\right).

\text{Curl}{\hbox{\hskip 1.0pt}}(u\cdot\nabla)v=(u\cdot\nabla)\text{Curl}{\hbox{\hskip 1.0pt}}v+``\nabla v\cdot\nabla u".

)

(

)
