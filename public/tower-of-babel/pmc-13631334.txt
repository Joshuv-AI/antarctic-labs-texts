End-to-end deep learning methods for genetic risk prediction of schizophrenia
Authors: Nora Verplaetse, Yves Moreau, Daniele Raimondi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Schizophrenia is a highly heritable psychiatric disorder with a complex genetic basis. While recent GWAS and whole exome sequencing studies have identified numerous risk loci, existing clinical prediction models rely primarily on linear assumptions, limiting their ability to capture complex, nonlinear genetic effects such as epistasis. In this study, we apply end-to-end genome interpretation neural network models to predict schizophrenia risk using whole exome sequencing data in a cohort of 6,135 cases and 6,245 controls. We show that nonlinear neural networks significantly outperform conventional additive models when sufficient sample size is available. These findings support the fact that high-order genetic interactions between alleles and variants should be considered by clinical and quantitative genetics models. To investigate the decision process our models follow, we integrate explainable AI techniques and biological priors into our models, using them to identify predictive genes and pathways. Our approach recovers both known schizophrenia risk genes and recommends BASP1 as a potential understudied schizophrenia gene involved in neuronal development.

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a complex disease which is defined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders as the following collection of symptoms: positive or psychotic symptoms (e.g., delusions and hallucinations), negative symptoms (e.g., impaired motivation, reduction of speech and social involvement), and cognitive impairment1–3. Although its global prevalence is only 0.28%, it contributes significantly to the global burden of disease in terms of disability2. The interplay between genetic and environmental factors that gives rise to the constellation of symptoms defining schizophrenia, remains poorly understood3.

The disease typically manifests as a psychotic episode in late adolescence or early adulthood, making at that time point the differential diagnosis with other mental disorders linked to psychosis, such as Bipolar Disorder (BD), Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), alcohol induced psychosis, difficult1,3. The often chronic course of the disease, together with a broad spectrum of comorbidities going from mental disorders to cardiovascular and metabolic diseases, linked to lifestyle factors such as smoking and poor diet and exercise, as well as medication side effects, causes schizophrenia to pose a massive burden on the life quality of the patient, their family, the health care system and society in general1,2. An important decrease in life expectancy of 10 to 20 years is observed, mostly attributed to suicide and cardiovascular disorders1,2. Antipsychotic medications remain the cornerstone of treatment, primarily addressing positive symptoms, though their use is often accompanied by significant side effects. On top of this, treatment response remains highly variable and difficult to predict3.

Improving our understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying schizophrenia would help identify much-needed therapeutic targets and improve patient stratification for more personalized treatment approaches3. A large Danish study involving 31,524 twin pairs estimates the heritability of schizophrenia to be 79%4, with general estimates ranging between 60 and 80%5. The most recent genome-wide association study reports common variant associations at 287 distinct loci through the analysis of 76,755 schizophrenia patients and 243,649 controls5. Fine-mapping these variants revealed involvement of genes expressed in excitatory and inhibitory neurons of the central nervous system, related to synaptic organization, differentiation and transmission5. However, the identified associated common variants explain only 24% of the genetic liability to schizophrenia in the European population2. Further studies on Whole Exome Sequences (WES) have also implicated rare variants and insertions and deletions1–3.

Although the practical application of these genetics results remains limited, they might lead to viable treatments in the future. For example, one study demonstrates that adding genetic information in the form of a Polygenic Risk Score (PRS) to clinical and historical variables (e.g., neurocognitive function, language patterns, social functioning, stressful events and trauma history), improved the individual risk prediction of psychosis3,6.

The genetic risk factors identified in schizophrenia show substantial overlap with other mental disorders such as BD, MDD, Obsessive Compulsive Disorder (OCD), Anorexia Nervosa and Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)1,2. This genetic pleiotropy, combined with the clinical heterogeneity of schizophrenia in terms of symptoms, disease course and treatment response, challenges current diagnostic classifications and underscores the need for a deeper understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms to refine these diagnostic boundaries3.

In our previous publications, we proposed a paradigm of tailor-made Genome Interpretation (GI) Neural Networks (NNs) models for the WES-based prediction of phenotypes from observed genotypes3,7–11, challenging the status quo of the statistical methods used in clinical and quantiative genetics. Here, interpreting the genome means modeling the relationship between genotype and phenotype, aiming at understanding the functional role of genomic regions and how they contribute to the observed traits. GI is a fundamental goal of biology and a major bioinformatics challenge on which several efforts, encompassing the latest AI developments, are currently devoted12,13. Our previous works show that our NN GI methods can be used to predict quantitative traits in plants directly from Whole Genome Sequences (WGS)7,9 and disease risk in humans from WES, where we focused on Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)3,10,11,14,15.

In particular, in ref. 10, we showed that once a sufficiently large sample size is reached, nonlinear NN architectures can outperform conventional linear additive methods used in clinical genetics, such as PRS16, thanks to the NNs ability to model epistatic interactions between genes and variants. Although nonlinear interactions are prevalent in molecular biology17–19, the vast majority of conventional quantitative genetics models focus on detecting linear/additive patterns by design18. Despite this limitation, additive models such as Polygenic Risk Scores (PRS), have been highly successful in identifying many associated variants and remain a decent baseline for predicting complex traits, often outperforming more complex methods in practice.

Our hypothesis is that, with the increased sample size of future cohorts, including epistasis in GI models will likely be the key to unlock a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying many non-trivial oligo- to polygenic traits. Given enough data, advanced GI NN models can indeed allocate more degrees of freedom to approximate the complexity of the interactions that lead to polygenic traits. This not only can lead to better genetic risk prediction (higher accuracy) but could also enable a more profound understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms, going beyond the assumption that additivity is the only inheritance mechanism that should be modeled3,17,18.

In the case of schizophrenia, a better predictive tool could help the difficult differential diagnosis that needs to be done when a patient presents their first psychotic episode. Moreover, a better understanding of the molecular mechanisms behind it could help untangle the heterogeneity of schizophrenia-associated phenotypes, as well as predicting the disease course and response to therapy, potentially leading to better-founded diagnostic boundaries.

In this article, we apply our WES-based GI NN architectures for disease risk prediction to the discrimination of schizophrenia cases vs controls. To do so, we train and test our model on a cohort containing 6,135 patients with schizophrenia and 6,245 healthy controls20, showing that our approach outperforms conventional linear models used in clinical genetics.

In addition, since the purported “interpretability” of linear models is often cited the main reason for their widespread use in genetics, we also apply the latest eXplainable AI (XAI) methods to investigate the decision process that leads our models towards their predictions. We show that, although nonlinear, our NNs can be used to identify pathways, genes and variants that are likely nonlinearly involved with schizophrenia. We show that several of the genes with the highest predictive potential have been identified in past schizophrenia studies, confirming that our models learned biologically relevant patterns. On the other hand, since our models are not limited by the detection of purely additively relevant genes and variants, we show that our approach could also identify previously overlooked potential candidate genes, such as the neuronal development gene BASP1 (Brain Abundant Membrane Attached Signal Protein 1).

Results

Encoding WES data into NN-understandable vectors

In this paper we train and test several NN architectures belonging to the end-to-end Genome Interpretation (GI) paradigm we envision, comparing strategies for the binary case-control prediction of 12,380 samples from the Sweden schizophrenia project20. By end-to-end, we mean that the model directly maps “raw" genomic data to the phenotype without manual feature engineering, jointly learning representations and prediction within a single computation stream.

The first step in predicting any kind of phenotype from WES data, is to find an optimal way to encode the partially structured high-dimensional WES samples (1,811,204 variants present in our dataset) into NN-understandable feature vectors. In previous works, we proposed a gene-centric7,8,10 and two variant-level WES encodings11. The gene-centric encoding was first prototyped on a smaller IBD dataset8 and WGS plant dataset7,9 and we later confirmed its usefulness in a larger IBD cohort10,11. We call it gene-centric because we count the number of different variant types mapped on each gene, thus reducing the dimensionality of the data and encoding them into a feature vector by selecting the gene as the main semantic unit. While very efficient in summarizing WES data, the main drawback of this encoding is that the individual effect of each variant is lost, and a kind of mutational damage for each gene is passed as input to the NN3.

To avoid this limitation, we propose two variant-centric feature encodings11, in which we represent the exome as a list of mutations and a 2-dimensional image respectively (see “Methods” for more details). In this way, the model could theoretically learn the specific effect of each individual variant, if needed, and this encoding should in principle be more nuanced than the gene-centric. For some phenotypes, the presence or absence of specific variants is indeed key for their prediction, with the replacement of glutamic acid by valine at position 6 in the β-globin protein in sickle-cell anemia being one of the most striking examples21.

Among these two variant-centric encodings, the mutation list encoding represents each gene with a list of feature vectors describing their functional effects (see “Methods”). This list varies in length for each sample depending on the number of sequenced variants. The second variant-centric approach encodes every position in the genome as a pixel in a two-dimensional image by folding the genome sequence on the fractal pattern of a Hilbert Curve (HC) (CNNHC), as shown in Fig. 2 (see “Methods” for more details). We choose to map WES variants on HCs due to their unique properties. HCs are a type of fractal space-filling curves that map a sequence on a N-dimensional space ensuring that points that are close in the 1-D sequence (variants) will be close also in the N-D space created by the HC22. Regardless of the specific feature encoding, to avoid potential confounding from sex and population structure, we excluded sex chromosomes from the input data, confirmed sex balance between cases and controls, and assessed population heterogeneity using principal component analysis (PCA) as well as inferring genetic ancestries by clustering samples in the 1000 Genomes project PCA space (see “Methods” and Supplementary Note 1).

NNlogreg directly connects the gene neurons to the output, limiting model expressivity with an inability of capturing interactions between genes3,10, in contrast to NNsmalldense and NNlargedense that have a fully connected hidden layer between the genes and the output with 5 and 50 hidden neurons respectively (see also Fig. 4B).

In a first attempt to lower the number of parameters of the dense NNs, while preserving the ability to model nonlinear interactions between genes, we replaced the fully connected layer in NNsmalldense and NNlargedense with a sparsely connected layer (see Fig. 4C). To introduce at the same time an inductive bias carrying a degree of biological meaning into our models, we induced this sparsity by connecting each gene with hidden layers representing pathways in which the input genes are known to interact3,10. This allowed us to reduce the overall number of parameters in the NNs by exploiting the small-worldness of biological networks. Depending on the databases we used to retrieve these gene-pathways interactions (KEGG23 or consensusPathDB24), we built the NNbiosparseKEGG and NNbiosparseCPATHDB models. The hidden layer in these models represents biological pathways from KEGG23 or consensusPathDB24 respectively, and connections are only made between gene-pathway pairs known in the databases, as illustrated in Fig. 4C. The incorporation of biological prior knowledge into a NN architecture has been also explored in recent studies on complex phenotype prediction25,26. This line of work builds on earlier approaches such as the study by Ma et al., which introduced hierarchical biological structures into NNs design, called “visible NNs”, for predicting yeast cellular growth27.

Reducing the actual number of parameters is a way to modulate the complexity of the models. On the other hand, the true complexity is a function of several other parameters, including the strength of regularization. NNDO is a dense NN model to which a very high regularization, in the form of a Dropout with p = 0.95, has been applied. This means that, during training, each connection has 95% probability to be discarded during each forward pass in the training.

Among the models based on variant-centric encodings, the Graph NNs (GCNmutlist) and the Sparse Convolutional Neural Net (CNNHC) are the best performing, while the Transformer-based architecture (TNNmutlist) trails behind probably due to the fact that these architectures are known to require very high amounts of data to excel.

Nonlinear neural networks consistently outperform linear models

Figure 3 shows that all proposed nonlinear NN GI architectures outperform the linear baseline (Penalized Linear), which takes all the 1,811,204 variants as input. While our hypothesis is that by increasing the sample size available during training, nonlinear models should outperform linear ones, in this specific case, this effect is too striking to be due solely to epistatic effects.

To investigate the excessively low performance of the linear baseline model, we trained and tested several ML models of different complexities on top of the same SNP array-like input, without observing any significant performance improvement (see. Supplementary Table 1). These underwhelming results did not improve even after trying different MAF thresholds, in an attempt to reduce the potentially detrimental effects of sparse input features (cfr. Supplementary Table 2). We also compared the baseline results with the performance obtained by several published PRS (see Supplementary Note 2). The main issue with this approach is that PRS are usually computed from GWAS studies spanning the entire genome, while the dataset used in our study encompasses only the exome. Therefore, only about 2.06–2.62% of PRS variants overlap with our WES data. As expected, the PRS predictive performances were modest (0.513-0.565 ROC AUC range), even when adding rare gene burden scores, reflecting that the majority of schizophrenia GWAS signal likely arises from non-coding regions not captured by WES (cfr. Supplementary Note 2).

Comparing these results based on the SNP array-like input features with the NNlinear model, which is equally unable to model epistasis, we can conclude that the underwhelming performance of the linear baseline and all the other ML methods tested (see Supplementary Table 1) is due to the fact that this conventional feature encoding does not seem to be suitable for this prediction task on this dataset.

Given the behavior of the SNP array-like feature encoding, to determine the extent of the role that being able to model epistasis plays in predicting schizophrenia cases from controls, independently of the way in which the input variants are encoded, in the rest of the paper we will focus on the NNlinear model as a new reference baseline for a purely additive, epistasis unaware, model. NNlinear has the same architecture as NNlogreg, but all its activations are replaced by identity functions, making it mathematically equivalent to a purely linear model built on the gene-centric encoding10. Figure 3 shows that the performance of NNlinear is significantly higher than the linear baseline on top of the SNP array-like input and more in line with what we expect from a linear model from literature and published PRS scores28,29. This empirically indicates that in the case of schizophrenia, using the gene-centric encoding leads to better predictions than the conventional SNP array-like vector of variants, given that performances remain low across models of different complexity on top of this SNP array-like input (see also Supplementary Table 1). This phenomenon is specific to this schizophrenia cohort, because in our previous work on IBD, the Penalized Linear model outperformed the NNlinear3,11. Aggregation at the gene level appears more informative for schizophrenia, indicating that molecular mechanisms may act primarily at the gene rather than the variant level.

The difference between NNlinear and all the other GI NN models in Fig. 3, shows that adding nonlinearity in the models is beneficial for the prediction of schizophrenia, given the same feature encoding. This can be considered an empirical evidence for the fact that schizophrenia inheritance mechanisms might include epistatic contributions.

The topology determines the type of epistasis a network can detect

As we shown in our previous work3,10, the architecture of NNs can directly determine its expressivity, namely the complexity of the patterns they can model. For example, the expressivity of NNlogreg is still quite limited, since it can model nonlinear interactions between types of variants within each gene (if they are consistent across genes), but it cannot model epistasis between genes (see Fig. 4).

Besides NNlinear and NNlogreg, all the other models are able to detect at least certain types of epistatic patterns between genes. The NN model with the lowest number of parameters, NNbiosparseKEGG, has a peculiar type of biologically-induced sparsity (see Fig. 4) that makes it able to detect only positive/negative epistasis3,10. NNsmalldense, which is the non-sparse model with the lowest number of parameters, is also the overall best-performing model. Its architecture allows it to model any kind of nonlinearities between genes, including the most extreme form of epistasis, called reciprocal sign10,19.

Figure 3 shows that variant-level encodings are slightly underperforming compared to the gene-centric ones. The best performing HC NN model is a sparse two-dimensional Convolutional NN (2D CNN) that takes as input a one-channel image in which each pixel represents the zygosity the corresponding variant (0, 1, or 2). Adding additional channels providing functional contextualization of each variant, for instance by adding variant-effect prediction scores30, did not improve the performance (see Supplementary Table 3).

To further investigate the benefit of using this 2D sparse CNN on top of WES data encoded as 2D HC, we tested it against a more conventional 1D CNN with similar architecture on top of WES data arranged as a 1D string of variants. The 1D CNN performed significantly worse (test ROC AUC 0.538, see Supplementary Note 3). The advantage of the proposed 2D sparse convolutional architecture over conventional 1D CNNs can be explained from both biological and computational perspectives. From a modeling standpoint, in standard 1D CNN architectures applied to genomic sequences, convolutional filters primarily capture local patterns within linkage disequilibrium blocks, and modeling long-range dependencies typically requires increased network depth or larger receptive fields. In contrast, the Hilbert Curve-based 2D representation is designed to preserve local genomic neighborhood relationships while simultaneously bringing distant genomic regions into closer spatial proximity. This spatial folding converts certain long-range genomic dependencies into shorter distances in 2D space, enabling the network to capture broader genomic context with fewer convolutional layers and smaller receptive fields. In addition, the sparse 2D representations and convolutions are particularly memory efficient, and require significantly less storage and computation since only the individually observed variants are actually instantiated in memory.

The curse of dimensionality causes sample-size dependence

In the context of WES or SNP-array cohorts, researches often deal with huge feature spaces (tens or hundreds of thousands of variants), but relatively modest dataset sizes, in particular in the earliest cohorts available. This data underdetermination gives rise to the curse of dimensionality, where data points become insufficient to densely cover it and the risk of overfitting grows dramatically for expressive models. Under these conditions, simpler linear additive models, such as penalized linear regressors, often perform best, not because they capture the underlying biological complexity better, but because their lower expressivity protects them from overfitting when data is scarce3.

This phenomenon can also be thought in terms of bias-variance tradeoff: linear models have high bias (they assume additive relations between inputs) but low variance (they don’t overfit easily), making them robust in small-sample regimes. In contrast, nonlinear models like NNs are highly expressive (low bias), but can suffer from high variance (overfitting) if data is insufficient. As sample size increases, however, variance decreases, and the expressivity of nonlinear models becomes a strength, since it allows them to model complex genetic interactions that linear models inherently miss3.

To investigate at which sample size nonlinear models start gaining an advantage overlinear ones, in Fig. 5 we artificially sub-sampled the dataset, investigating the robustness of NNlinear, the best performing NN model (NNsmalldense), and the Penalized Regressor under different sample size conditions. Figure 5 shows that increasing the sample size available for training improves performance and drastically reduces variance. The Penalized Linear model benefits the least from the increased sample size, likely due to its low expressivity (high bias) and particularly unsuitable feature encoding for this specific task. Opening the Neural Network black boxes with eXplainable AI methods

In clinical genetics, prediction performance can not be the sole metric to choose one model over the others. Model explainability is essential to ensure that models learn meaningful patterns rather than relying on unintended shortcuts31,32, which is especially critical in a clinical context. Additionally, it will provide insights into the biological mechanisms underlying schizophrenia.

One of the reasons why we introduced biologically meaningful sparsity in our NN architectures is the fact that they provide a more intuitive way to reach explainability compared to conventional fully connected NNs, due to the biological meaning that is pre-assigned to each neuron (see Fig. 4C), which represents either genes or pathways3,10,25. In these biologically meaningful sparse architectures, the relevance of a particular biological entity for the prediction of schizophrenia can be obtained just by inspecting which neurons are activated during the forward pass (see “Methods”).

Although the precise mechanisms underlying schizophrenia remain unclear, it is generally believed to be driven by neurodevelopmental abnormalities, neurotransmitter imbalances and immune dysregulation, leading to disruptions in the brain neural circuitry and function1.

The most relevant pathway for NNbiosparseKEGG predictions involves the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction3. This finding is in line with the growing body of evidence suggesting that immune dysregulation plays a crucial role in schizophrenia. Elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines have been observed in serum and cerebrospinal fluid samples of patients with schizophrenia33. The predictive importance of the KEGG ribosome pathway in the model is attributed to mitochondrial ribosome proteins, as the top predictive genes in this pathway all encode mitochondrial ribosomal proteins. Schizophrenia has been associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, and several mitochondrial ribosome subunits have been shown to be down-regulated in brain and serum samples of schizophrenia patients34. Mitochondrial dysfunction can impair cellular energy production and lead to the excessive generation of reactive oxygen species, resulting in neuronal damage and inflammation. Additionally, mitochondria are involved in synaptic activity and the growth of excitatory cells, such as neurons3,34.

The third most relevant pathway relates to herpes simplex virus 1, a neurotropic virus that can replicate in the brain. Multiple studies have linked herpes simplex virus 1 to cognitive deficits and structural abnormalities in the brains of individuals with schizophrenia3,35.

The Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK-STAT) signaling pathway, which mediates the effects of pro-inflammatory cytokines, ranks fourth in predictive importance. This pathway is known to be involved in neuroinflammation, a key feature of schizophrenia. Numerous genes within this pathway have previously been implicated in schizophrenia through GWAS3,36.

The fifth pathway concerns human T-cell leukemia virus 1 infection3. While no direct link has been established between human T-cell leukemia virus 1 and schizophrenia, the two share common characteristics, including immune dysregulation with chronic inflammation and upregulated cytokines, and activation of microglial cells33. As such, it is not surprising that the model assigns high predictive importance to this pathway. When multiple pathways share many genes, the model may not necessarily select the pathway that is most biologically meaningful to us10. This is because NNbiosparseKEGG distinguishes pathway neurons solely based on the genes they are connected to, and is indifferent to the specific names we assign to the pathways. This observation is further supported by the fact that the human T-cell leukemia virus 1 infection KEGG pathway is also significantly enriched in one of the four largest schizophrenia GWAS gene clusters we will discuss in the next paragraph (p value 1.170E − 4)3.

Discussion

In this study, we propose and compare several end-to-end Genome Interpretation (GI) NN architectures for the WES-based case-control discrimination of schizophrenia. These GI NN models belong a paradigm of quantitative and clinical genetics methods we propose, with the goal of going beyond the conventional additive/linear statistical models that dominate the field3. Conventional approaches, such as PRS16, have been the backbone of clinical genetics for decades, but with the advent of modern sequencing technologies and larger cohorts, their strict modeling assumptions, limited to additive inheritance and designed for SNP arrays, are shifting from a strength to a burden, restricting their ability to capture complex biological and inheritance mechanisms and preventing the direct use of WES data.

The GI NN architectures we propose, on the other hand, are able to detect nonlinear inheritance patterns between variants and genes, and are thus able to model epistatic interactions between them3. Additionally, thanks to the flexibility of the NN architectures, our approaches follow an end-to-end modeling philosophy, which takes as input the WES data with as little pre-processing as possible. Our end-to-end philosophy relates to representation learning, where the task of extracting features from the input data is delegated to the model. This approach is inherently data agnostic, in the sense that it imposes fewer a priori assumptions on the structure and relevance of the input features, increasing the likelihood of the discovery of complex patterns and more profound insights in GI. The effectiveness of end-to-end learning is increasingly recognized across the broader ML/AI community, as demonstrated by large language models like GPT and protein structure predictors such as AlphaFold, which achieve state-of-the-art performance precisely by learning directly from raw, high-dimensional data3.

In this study, we compare three different ways to encode WES data in NN understandable formats, organizing them into gene-centric and variant-centric feature encodings. While the variant-centric encoding should theoretically convey the maximum amount of information, being a transliteration of the variant observed in the input VCF files, our results show that aggregating the variant at the gene level, providing the model with an aggregate summary of the mutational damage carried by each gene, is generally slightly better in terms of predictions, as shown in Fig. 3. This could be another way in which the curse of dimensionality manifests itself, and future studies on even larges samples sizes will be necessary to test whether variant-centric encodings do not improve performance after enough data are available to the model3.

This paper further confirms the positive results we obtained in our previous application of these encodings and GI NN models for the case-control discrimination of IBD3,10,11, in terms of the gap between conventional linear models and the NN methods we propose. While the absolute values of the predictive performances are lower for schizophrenia than for IBD, this is not unexpected given the differences in heritability, underlying genetic mechanisms and involvement of environmental factors that characterize these two different phenotypes.

Although several polygenic risk scores (PRS) have been developed to estimate schizophrenia risk from GWAS data, we could not directly compare our GI NN models to these PRS. This is because only a small proportion of the variants used in published PRS are present in our exome sequencing cohort (2.06%–2.62%), resulting in very limited predictive performance. Consistent with this, baseline models of varying complexity applied to the raw SNP-array like representation also yielded similarly low performance.

To provide context, we considered results reported in the literature, where a median ROC AUC of 0.644 was achieved for schizophrenia PRS in a cohort of 5554 cases and 6299 controls28, consistently with earlier findings from the schizophrenia Working Group of the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium29. These values are comparable to the performance of our best linear baseline model (NNlinear). The authors also report an increase in performance of the nonlinear Support Vector Machine classifier when more samples are included, but not for PRS. This is in line with the notion that complex ML methods can benefit from larger sample sizes, and indeed we empirically witness this trend in our cohort as well (see Fig. 5)3.

Our results suggest that increasing model complexity alone does not substantially improve predictive performance, as no model achieves competitive results when applied directly to the raw SNP representation. Instead, performance gains are primarily driven by (i) the aggregation of variant-level data into biologically meaningful feature representations, and (ii) the choice of NN architecture suited to these representations. For the schizophrenia phenotype, aggregation at the gene level appears more informative in this cohort, indicating that molecular mechanisms may act primarily at the gene rather than the variant level.

The bias-variance trade-off explains why properly sizing the model with respect to the sample size is key. The results in Fig. 3 show that NNsmalldense achieves the best performance among all models tested, despite being one of the smallest nonlinear architectures in the benchmark. This highlights that larger models are not necessarily better, particularly in the high-dimensional, low sample size settings typical of genetic data3. In these settings, we are challenged by the curse of dimensionality, where sparsely populated high-dimensional input spaces make it difficult to learn complex patterns without very large amounts of data. Maintaining a moderate number of trainable parameters is therefore crucial for avoiding overfitting and achieving optimal generalization. This observation reflects a fundamental aspect of the bias-variance tradeoff: overly complex models tend to have low bias but high variance, which can hurt performance when data is scarce, while smaller models may generalize better by balancing the two. These findings underscore the importance of addressing the issue of data underdetermination (i.e., the p ≫ n regime, where the number of genetic variants far exceeds the number of samples), and suggest that identifying an appropriate model complexity or effective n/p ratio is key to maximizing predictive performance for a given phenotype and cohort size3.

Besides the sheer number of parameters, also factors such as the network topology, the strength of regularization, the presence of dropouts, and the type of feature encoding play a role in moving the model up or down in the bias-variance spectrum. For example, from Fig. 3 we can see, perhaps counter-intuitively, that coarse-grained gene-centric representation outperforms the variant-level representations, although the latter is more informative. We hypothesize that this observation might be again due to the data underdetermination issue, and that larger datasets will enable more complex encodings and models to be more competitive by shifting the n/p ratio to allow the exploitation of more fine grained variant-level representations by more complex NN architectures3.

In particular, the HC representation is of interest since it encodes every position in the genome and is therefore easily extended to other genetic dataset formats such as Whole Genome Sequences, enabling a truly end-to-end approach maximally maintaining all variant-level information in the input3.

As a second objective, this study aims to move beyond the Obscurum per Obscurius paradigm, towards interpretable nonlinear Genome Interpretation models3. After evaluating their performance, we select two of the best performing models, and we use Explainable AI (XAI) techniques to investigate their decision process. We focus on the two top-performing models that differ in the extent of biological knowledge incorporated into their architecture. NNsmalldense mainly relies on gene-level concepts, while NNbiosparseKEGG, which integrates pathway-level concepts in addition to gene concepts, thanks to the KEGG23-induced sparsity of its hidden layer (see Fig. 4C).

The goal of this investigation is twofold. The first is to verify the biological meaningfulness of the learned patterns, avoiding as much as possible Clever Hans models based on spurious correlations32. The second is to gain insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying schizophrenia, ideally discovering some epistatic relations between genes, to which univariate GWAS or PRS are totally oblivious. In the case of NNbiosparseKEGG, the biology-inspired sparsity pattern, which links biological units such as genes and pathways to each neuron, allows for a straightforward and intuitive interpretation compared to the approaches that are necessary on conventional black-box-like fully connected NNs3,32,44,45.

For both models, about half of the top predictive genes are known schizophrenia-associated genes from the PGS catalog36. This confirms the biological relevance of the patterns learned by the models.

The remaining predictive genes exhibit known interactions with GWAS genes in the STRING database37, with one exception: ZNF600, a predictive gene in the NNbiosparseKEGG model. While ZNF600 itself has no direct association with schizophrenia, the herpes simplex pathway through which it exerts its influence on the model’s predictions is potentially linked to the disorder3,35.

This highlights the added value of incorporating pathway-level information for uncovering meaningful biological insights. However, while ranking known schizophrenia genes validates the model’s interpretative value, the discovery of not yet known genes is arguably more valuable, as these may reveal biological mechanisms or therapeutic targets. For example, BASP1 (Brain Abundant Membrane Attached Signal Protein 1), identified as the second most predictive gene after the already known gene YJEFN3, shows potential as it is involved in neuronal development3. Further literature search on this gene, reveals an increased expression in the prefrontal cortex of a small cohort of schizophrenia patients46.

GI models like the ones we propose are able to provide insights into molecular mechanisms3. These insights, and the models’ predictions, could be the building block to provide a framework for AI-guided diagnosis and patient stratification in the future. This could be of particular interest for psychiatric disorders, which are defined as syndromes, meaning a cluster of symptoms. The large clinical heterogeneity of schizophrenia in terms of predominant symptoms, disease course and therapy response, shows how broad the symptomatic disease definition actually is1. At the same time, the genetic pleiotropy with large overlap between genetic risk factors in multiple psychiatric diseases, seems to be suggesting a too small disease definition1,2. It is clear that the current categorical notions of classification do not suffice and that a deeper molecular understanding would greatly improve diagnostic boundaries. Research already showed that PRS can be used to dissect the heterogeneity of schizophrenia, showing for example, higher scores associated with a chronic disease course2. This is a first indication that genetics can help to better define psychiatric disease entities, and our framework would provide a next step in achieving such goals. The resulting signals should be seen as hypothesis-generating indications of potentially relevant genes and pathways, which would require further validation using independent datasets and experimental validation3.

A major challenge in applying complex Machine Learning models to GI, despite their potential, is the “curse of dimensionality” discussed above. Collecting sufficiently large homogeneous genetic datasets remains difficult due to factors such as low phenotype incidence, sequencing costs, batch effects, and population structure. In addition, heterogeneity in patient populations and phenotype definitions, particularly for symptom-based disorders such as schizophrenia, may dilute or confound the predictive signal. To balance dataset size with homogeneity, we selected the Swedish schizophrenia whole-exome sequencing dataset, which provides cases defined by a consistent and strict phenotype definition, and includes only individuals with both parents born in Scandinavia to reduce population stratification3,20. We further performed extensive analyses to assess data homogeneity (e.g., sex balance and genetic ancestry; see Supplementary Note 1).

We acknowledge that for certain diseases, using exome sequencing (WES) data, which covers only approximately 2% of the genome and does not capture potentially relevant non-coding elements, can constitute a limitation. This might be the case for schizophrenia, since the poor performance of PRS and linear model on the SNP array-like feature encoding can indicate that non-coding regions likely have an important role in the development of this disease.

On the other hand, our choice to use WES data was motivated by the availability of sufficiently large cohorts required to prototype our NN architectures. Moreover, WES data is theoretically able to identify any form of coding variant (and not only common SNPs), increasing the chances of identifying truly causative variants. The present study is meant as a methodological investigation of the effectiveness of nonlinear models for GI in complex diseases, rather than a definitive genetic risk model for schizophrenia.

Importantly, the representations and architectures proposed in this work are not restricted to exome data and can be extended to genome-wide sequencing data3. As larger whole-genome sequencing datasets become increasingly available through initiatives such as the UK Biobank and other population sequencing efforts, future work will enable evaluation of these approaches using more comprehensive genomic information, including non-coding regulatory regions.

Finally, we acknowledge the need for external validation of our genome interpretation neural network models on independent datasets to assess robustness and generalization ability. Although replication in schizophrenia cohorts was not possible within the scope of the present study, we note that similar modeling approaches have previously been applied to other complex diseases, such as inflammatory bowel disease, where comparable architectures yielded consistent results across datasets 10,11. This suggests that the modeling framework is not specific to a single dataset.

Methods

Dataset

In this paper we used the data from the Sweden-schizophrenia Population-Based Case-Control Exome Sequencing project (dbGaP phs000473.v2.p2), consisting of 12,380 Whole Exome Sequences (WES) for 6,135 schizophrenia patients and 6,245 healthy controls20. Samples used for data analysis were provided by the Swedish Cohort Collection supported by the NIMH grant R01MH077139, the Sylvan C. Herman Foundation, the Stanley Medical Research Institute and The Swedish Research Council (grants 2009-4959 and 2011-4659). Support for the exome sequencing was provided by the NIMH Grand Opportunity grant RCMH089905, the Sylvan C. Herman Foundation, a grant from the Stanley Medical Research Institute and multiple gifts to the Stanley Center for Psychiatric Research at the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard3.

The cases are individuals aged between 18 and 65, who have been hospitalized 2 or more times with schizophrenia discharge diagnosis on the Swedish Hospital Discharge Register, who are alive and born in Sweden or another Nordic country with both parents born in Sweden. Controls were age-matched individuals that never have been hospitalized with a discharge diagnosis of schizophrenia in the Inpatient Hospitalization register, who are alive with both parents born in Sweden. Related individuals were excluded. For the cases, 55.71% of the samples are male, for the controls, 50.95%. Genetic ancestries were inferred by clustering samples in the 1000 Genomes project PCA space, with the large majority being predicted as European (98.83% of the cases and 98.35% of the controls, see Supplementary Note 1). The WES data is provided as a Variant Calling Format (VCF) file listing the observed variants (total of 1,811,204 variants after excluding sex chromosomes)3.

Whole exome sequence encoding and neural network architectures

Here we summarize the 3 feature encodings we use to transform the WES data (in the form of VCF files) into NN-readable feature vectors. Each encoding requires a specific NN architecture to process it, which we describe below.

We describe one gene-level and two variant-level encodings. The variant representations have a higher resolution and thereby keep more information on the individual variants. However, they come with additional challenges concerning dimensionality and variable input size.

The gene-centric encoding

We originally proposed this encoding in our previous work3,7,8,15. It is called gene-centric because it uses the gene as the smallest conceptual unit to reduce dimensionality (from 1,811,204 variants to 21,625 genes). After functional annotation of the input VCFs using Annovar47, this encoding aggregates the variants on the genes by counting for each gene all the variants of the following variant types: UTR3, UTR5, splicing, upstream, downstream, intronic, intronic ncRNA, exonic ncRNA, splicing ncRNA, exonic non-frameshift insertion, exonic frameshift insertion, exonic non-frameshift deletion, exonic frameshift deletion, exonic stoploss, exonic stopgain, exonic startloss and exonic nonsynonymous.

The dataset contains 21,625 genes in total, so the resulting gene-centric input has the dimension of (12380, 21625, 17). To avoid numerical issues, we standardize the counts of each variant type across all genes as the final processing step. We also tried to enrich this encoding with various kinds of functional annotation, for example by adding gene scores (the Haploinsufficiency Index (pHI)48, the Gene Recessiveness Index (pRec)49, the Gene Damage Index (GDI)50 and the Residual Variation Intolerance Score (RVIS)51, resulting in input with dimension (12380, 21625, 21)) and variant effect predictor scores to contextualize the likely functional impact of each specific variant (low, mid and high category for M-CAP52 and DEOGEN230, resulting in input with dimension (12380, 21625, 27)).

The mutation list encoding

This encoding represents each sample as a list of variants with respect to the reference genome, with each variant described by a feature vector containing: the gene it belongs to, plus some additional features providing information on the functional impact of the variant and the affected gene. The functional relevance of the gene is described by the Haploinsufficiency Index (pHI)48, the Gene Recessiveness Index (pRec)49, the Gene Damage Index (GDI)50 and the Residual Variation Intolerance Score (RVIS)51). The functional impact of the variant is described by variant-effect prediction scores (metaSVM53, M-CAP52, VEST454, DEOGEN230 and PhyloP3055). We retrieved those scores from dbNFSP v.42a56. Each sample is thus encoded by a (10, NVARIANTS) matrix of variable size with NVARIANTS determined by the total number of exonic variants in this particular sample3.

Neural network architectures for the mutation list encoding

We tested two architectures we designed to take as input the mutation list encoding3,11. The major challenge in this case is that each sample is described by a list of different size. For this reason, we opted for a biologically inspired Graph Convolutional Neural network (GCNmutlist)57 and a Transformer58 based biologically sparsified model (TNNmutlist), which can deal with this kind of inputs.

The initial step is identical in both architectures, with each variant vector undergoing identical processing, as illustrated in Fig. 1B. The 11 exonic variant classes (e.g., the functional gene element in which the variant is positioned) are encoded using a 5-dimensional trainable embedding. This embedding is passed through a dedicated NN module composed of two fully connected layers with 20 and 5 hidden neurons, with LayerNorm and Tanh activation. In parallel, gene-specific features are processed using a separate neural network module with the same architecture: two fully connected layers with 20 and 5 hidden units, followed by LayerNorm and Tanh. Variant-specific features are handled by a third dedicated module, also comprising two fully connected layers but with 20 and 10 hidden neurons, respectively. The outputs of these three components (variant class embedding, gene-specific module, and variant-specific module) are concatenated to yield a 20-dimensional vector per variant (top of Fig. 1B). Across all variants in a sample, this forms a matrix of shape (20, NVARIANTS).

The biologically inspired Graph Convolutional Neural Network

GCNmutlist, shown in Fig. 1A, is designed as GCN layers operating on hierarchical biological graphs, linking variants to genes, or genes to pathways3. By providing for each sample a sample-specific variant-gene adjacency matrix, encoded as PyTorch sparse COO tensors to minimize memory and compute requirements, the model manages the variable size of the inputs. The GCNmutlist that worked best for this dataset only uses one GCN layer, aggregating variant vectors at the gene level. A shared gene module, comprising of a fully connected hidden layer of 25 hidden neurons followed by LayerNorm and Tanh activation, that is subsequently connected to 1 output neuron, further processes the obtained gene vectors and compresses those vectors into a single value per gene. These are passed to a final classification module with one hidden layer of 280 neurons. A full schematic is shown in Fig. 1A.

A Transformer architecture for Genome Interpretation

The second architecture addressing the variable input size leverages the Transformer NN3,58,59 (TNNmutlist, Fig. 1C). For every 20-dimensional variant vector obtained by the first processing procedure described above, a 10-dimensional key (K) and value (V) vector are produced, using two separate NN modules with two hidden layers of 20 and 10 neurons and Tanh activation (green boxes in Fig. 1C). A separate set of randomly initialized gene embeddings (blue boxes in Fig. 1C) is processed by an NN module with 2 hidden layer of 20 and 10 neurons respectively with Tanh activation, to generate 21, 625 10-dimensional query (Q) vectors. Following the original attention architecture, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{softmax}}}(Q\times {K}^{T})\times V$$\end{document}softmax(Q×KT)×V is computed. To make this computation scalable to our high-dimensional input, we introduce a biologically sparsified version that only computes this product of Q and K vectors for the variant-gene pairs of variants belonging to a particular gene. This information is again provided as a sample-specific variant-gene adjacency matrix. In this biologically sparsified version, only the variants that are mapped to the gene are used to reweigh the value vectors that are summed to produce the end-product gene vectors of the layer. These gene vectors are subsequently processed by a shared gene module consisting of two fully connected layers with 25 and 1 hidden neurons and Tanh activation. Finally, a classification layer connects this layer with 21,625 neurons directly to the output neuron. A schematic of this transformer-based model is shown in Fig. 1C.

Representing genomes as fractal images

The second variant-level encoding we propose uses Hilbert space-filling fractal curves60 to represent WES as two-dimensional images of size 216 × 216 = 65, 536 × 65, 536 3,11. A Hilbert Curve (HC) provides a method for mapping a linear sequence onto a two-dimensional image by traversing every pixel exactly once in a recursive, meander-like pattern that divides the space into quadrants, as shown in Fig. 2. This mapping preserves locality, meaning that elements close together in the original sequence remain spatially proximate in the resulting image. Following a similar approach to that proposed in ref. 22, we use HCs to encode genomic positions as pixels using a fold level of N = 16. These large images are too large to be processed by conventional Convolutional NNs, and we therefore used the sparse CNN implementation provided in the Spatially Sparse Convolution library (Spconv)61 to keep computations feasible. Details on the different CNN architectures that we tested are provided in Supplementary Fig. 4.

Baseline models

The baseline models used for comparison were taken from the scikit-learn library62 (SGDClassifier with log-loss, RandomForestClassifier, kernel_approximation.Nystroem). Given the high dimensional and highly sparse feature input space (1,811,204 variants with 97.4% zero-valued over the samples), we implemented the gradient boosting classifier with the LightGBM algorithm due to its native support for sparse input matrices, histogram-based feature binning, and favorable memory and runtime scaling in ultra-high-dimensional settings63. The PRS summary statistics were obtained from the Polygenic Score Catalog36. The ten first principal components were added to the scores. For rare gene burden scores, we summed variants with minor allele frequency < 0.1%. Protein-truncating variants (PTV) included variants causing a stop gain, frameshift variants, splice acceptor and splice donor variants. Damaging missense variants were defined as missense variants with CADD > 20. We tested this for different gene sets (all genes, GWAS genes and genes with probability of Loss-of-function Intolerance (pLI) > 0.9).

Implementation and validation

All NNs have been implemented with PyTorch64 and are trained with the Adam optimizer and the binary cross-entropy loss function. Weight decay and dropout layers were used as regularization techniques. Batch size was 128 for the gene-centric models and GCNmutlist, but only 32 for the Hilbert curve encoding and 8 for TNNmutlist because of the memory size.

Learning rate for optimal convergence was 0.001 for gene-centric models, 1e − 5 for GCNmutlist, TNNmutlist and CNNHC. The linear baseline is a logistic regression with L2-penalty, implemented using log-loss with the SGDClassifier from the scikit-learn library62. Since this model has 1,811,204 parameters, it needs heavy regularization (α = 1, 000, 000). We chose not to use nested cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning. Instead, we applied a coarse-grained search over a minimal set of values to keep computational load feasible while minimizing the risk of overfitting and data leakage. The hyperparameters evaluated for each model are provided in Supplementary Table 7.

To verify that our approach was indeed immune to information leakage between training and test sets, we conducted an additional nested cross-validation experiment using Optuna65 for hyperparameter optimization. This procedure employed a nested three-fold cross-validation run. The prediction performances from this experiment are indicated as stars in Supplementary Fig. 5. These results were always equal to or higher than the performances obtained without hyperparameter optimization, confirming that the lack of nested hyperparameter tuning in Fig. 3 did not introduce data leakage.

To assess variability in model performance, we used 10 repetitions of stratified three-fold cross-validation. In each repetition, the dataset was randomly split into three folds while preserving the case-control ratio. Two folds were used for training and one fold for testing, so that every sample served as test data exactly once per repetition. To ensure fair comparison across models, the same random seeds (0–9) were applied for all models in each repetition.

Performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC AUC) on the held-out test folds. We report the mean and standard deviation of ROC AUC across all repetitions. The source code is freely available at (https://github.com/NoraVerplaetse/schizophreniagi/66).

STRING network analysis

We collected schizophrenia GWAS genes from the PGC catalog36. The resulting set contained 1,883 genes that were present in our dataset. We performed a network analysis for the GWAS genes using STRING v.12.037 with the edges being defined as those having gene-gene interaction score > 0.4. Subsequently, we used the GLay Community Clustering38 with default parameters to form clusters (Cytoscape v.3.10.167). We performed a gene enrichment analysis using Gene Ontology Biological Process68 and Reactome pathways69 for the clusters and show the top three most significant ones. Gene Ontology Biological Process and Reactome pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the Enrichment Table app in Cytoscape v.3.10.167, which queries the g:Profiler g:GOSt web service70. Statistical significance was assessed using a one-tailed Fisher’s exact test for over-representation. P values were corrected for multiple testing using the default g:SCS procedure. For the set of top ten predictive model genes of NNbiosparseKEGG and of NNsmalldense, connections with the clusters were calculated separately using STRING (gene-gene interaction score > 0.4)3.

Variability of top predictive genes

To quantify the consistency of gene selection across model runs, we computed pairwise Jaccard similarities between the sets of top predictive genes (top 10 and top 100) obtained from ten independent runs of NNbiosparseKEGG. To evaluate whether the observed overlap exceeded random expectation, we performed a permutation-based analysis. For each run, gene sets of equal size were randomly sampled without replacement from the full set of genes. These random gene sets were used to compute pairwise Jaccard similarities, and this procedure was repeated 1000 times to generate a null distribution of overlap under random selection. The observed mean Jaccard similarity was compared to this null distribution. Additionally, we performed a cluster analysis for the genes using scikit-learn agglomerative clustering based on (1) the Pearson correlation and (2) the cosine similarity between the summed gene vectors. The same procedure of calculating Jaccard similarity as described above was performed on top of the cluster labels of the top predictive genes.
