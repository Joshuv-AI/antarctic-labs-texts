Mechanical problem solving by plush-crested jays: are tools special after all?
Authors: Jimena Lois-Milevicich, Lauriane Rat-Fischer, María Alicia de la Colina, Raúl Orencio Gómez, Juan Carlos Reboreda, Alex Kacelnik
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Tool use is taxonomically associated with high behavioural flexibility and innovativeness, and its prevalence is greater in primates and some bird species. This association, however, is not known to be causally determinant of tool-related competence since flexibility and innovativeness are often observed in the absence of tool use and vice versa. For this reason, it is interesting to explore whether animals that can be loosely categorized as outstanding, or ‘intelligent’ physical problem solvers, are also remarkable using tools innovatively, rather than tool use presenting special constraints. We investigate this problem using plush-crested jays (Cyanocorax chrysops), a corvid new to cognitive research that shows highly flexible and inquisitive behaviour in the wild and has not been reported to use tools. We tested jays in two tasks of apparent similar manipulative complexity and incentive, one involving a tool (T) and the other not (NT). In the NT task birds had to open a box with a transparent lid blocked by a latch to get a reward, whereas in the T task, they had to use a rake to pull out the reward from the box. Eight out of nine subjects succeeded in the NT task, whereas none of them learned to solve the T task. This is consistent with tool use involving dedicated competencies, rather than just high problem-solving proficiency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10071-024-01922-1.

Introduction

The ability to solve novel mechanical problems has attracted attention from different disciplines, since it allows for the investigation of the cognitive capabilities in non-human animals (Gruber et al. 2019) and establish parallelisms with the development of intelligence in humans (Whiten et al. 1996; Seed et al. 2011; Beck et al. 2016; Rat-Fischer et al. 2016). One trait that is frequently associated with human evolution and intelligence is the use and manufacturing of tools (Ambrose 2010; Fragaszy and Mangalam 2018). Besides humans and other primates, few mammal and bird taxa regularly display it (Shumaker et al. 2011; Auersperg et al. 2012; Lambert et al. 2015; Rutz et al. 2016; Goodman et al. 2018; Colbourne et al. 2021; Jacobs and Osvath 2023). Although it is often assumed that tool use requires a relatively high level of cognitive sophistication, the trait may also involve specific adaptations to ecology, social context, and motivation (i.e., a dedicated competence), rather than just general cognitive capacity (Kacelnik 2009; Shettleworth 2010; Ibáñez de Aldecoa et al. 2024). Teschke et al. (2013) addressed this issue very sharply, by asking whether species that are remarkable for their tool use are also exceptional in other forms of physical problem solving. In brief, they compared three species of corvids and two species of Galapagos finches and found no correlation between intense tool use in the wild and performance in two arbitrary learning tasks, one of them demanding physical manipulation. On the contrary, some species that are known to be exceptionally innovative in general problems but are not known to routinely use tools in natural ecological circumstances, are, under further research effort, being found also to have the capability to use tools when appropriate. Goffin cockatoos fall in this category as they are well documented for inquisitive, innovative, and overall intelligent behaviour, including individual or social discovery of tool use in captivity (Auersperg et al. 2012, 2014; Osuna-Mascaró et al. 2023), but until recently, they were not among the species known to routinely employ tool use in the wild. This is changing, as O’Hara et al. (2021) reported that they do show extremely sophisticated tool use in their natural habitat, thus leaving the issue of how dependent tool use is on dedicated adaptations still wide open.

Overall, and even in the absence of clearly demarcated metrics for intelligence, avian species are widely accepted as useful models to understand cognition in general, and their cognitive traits are target of intense comparative cognitive research (e.g., Emery 2006; Clayton and Emery 2015). Among birds, corvids and parrots have consistently been shown to have highly sophisticated cognitive skills (reviewed in Emery and Clayton 2004; Güntürkün and Bugnyar 2016; Lambert et al. 2019; Bastos and Taylor 2020; Zorina et al. 2022). In particular, the presence of tool use in only some of the species questions the extent to which this ability is best treated as an expression of general cognitive flexibility and innovativeness rather than as a trait in itself, such that some species may perform highly in one aspect but not in the other. Thus, comparing performance in tool-based and non-tool-based tasks within a species may help to understand whether tool-related behaviour is limited by a general cognitive capacity or by ecologically driven dedicated mechanisms.

The New World jays are a monophyletic assemblage of seven genera and 38 corvid species (Fernando et al. 2017; Clements et al. 2022), endemic to the Americas (Bonaccorso and Peterson 2007). The group has radiated successfully in tropical and subtropical regions and has served as a model system for numerous behavioural studies (e.g., Olson et al. 1995; Bond et al. 2007; Goto et al. 2014; Wei et al. 2014; Hofmann et al. 2016; Logan et al. 2016; Manrique et al. 2017; McCune et al. 2019; Clary et al. 2020; Harvey and Black 2021; Vernouillet et al. 2021), some of which have addressed complex problem solving or reported tool use. For instance, Hofmann et al. (2016) studied the rules western scrub-jays (Aphelocoma californica) use to solve different string-pulling tasks and Jones and Kamil (1973) observed that northern blue jays (Cyanocitta cristata) tore pieces of newspaper and used them to rake in food pellets that were out of reach. Similarly, there is some work on problem solving and tool use in Cyanocorax jays. Gayou (1982) observed that two individual green jays (Cyanocorax yncas) used twigs to remove insects from under bark and Manrique et al. (2017) showed that green jays solved a string-pulling task.

We report a within-subject comparison of problem solving and tool use performance in plush-crested jays (Cyanocorax chrysops), a New World jay new to behavioural research that shows flexible (Uejima et al. 2012) and inquisitive behaviour in the wild (JLM personal observations). The species is widely distributed in tropical forests and woodlands of South America. Its members live in groups of up to 10–12 individuals, are cooperative breeders, and have a complex vocal repertoire (Uejima et al. 2012; Rosa et al. 2016; Anjos et al. 2019) but have not been reported to use tools. We used two tasks designed to present an apparent similar motor difficulty and incentive, but where one involved a simple tool action, and the other did not. The non-tool task consisted of a box with a transparent lid blocked by a latch that birds had to unblock to get a reward, whereas the tool task involved manipulating an external object (a rake) to extract the same reward from a box. These tasks would allow comparison between the need or not to use an object as a tool. They have been used previously to compare Corvus species (C. moneduloides, a tool user and C. monedula, not a routine tool user) and to compare human infants that differ in play style (Rat-Fischer et al. 2016, 2024).

Methods

Subjects

We used nine plush-crested jays, eight adults (two females and six males), and one juvenile male who was 4 months of age at the beginning of the experiments (details in Table 1). They were sexed molecularly, using 2550F/2718R primer set (Lois-Milevicich et al. 2021). Birds were housed in the zoo Bioparque Temaikèn, Buenos Aires, Argentina (32° 21’ 50’’ S, 58° 48’ 10’’ W).

Outside experimental periods, the birds were housed in a large exhibit aviary. For the experiments, individuals were housed individually in one of two cages of 1.5 m x 0.8 m x 1 m (length x width x height) placed inside a contiguous aviary measuring 5.3 m x 2.5 m x 4 m (length x width x height). These cages were visually but not acoustically isolated from the exhibit aviary and each other. We conducted the non-tool (NT) experiments first (June to July 2018) and the tool-use (T) experiments second (September to December 2018). We opted for this tandem within-subject design because we did not know the time required to solve the NT and the T tasks a priori and if we would have access to the birds to test the two tasks. So, we decided to start with one of them (i.e., NT task) and, as the subjects succeeded in that one, we continued with the other task (i.e., T task). We consider that, although the experimental design was not counterbalanced, this sequence did not affect our conclusions as the subjects were equal or more expert with the procedures when they were tested on the T task. We reasoned that if despite this advantage, they showed significantly better performance in the NT task, it would be more justified to conclude that using tools calls for a dedicated tool-related competence. At the beginning of the NT task, bird VV suffered a minor injury, so it was recaptured, and began the experiments after recovery. This was the only subject that had difficulties with the NT task (see Results).

Apparatus

For the NT task, we used a fibreboard box (15 cm width x 25 cm depth x 5 cm height) with a transparent plastic lid, whose front half could be lifted to access the content. The front part of the lid could be either blocked or free, depending on the position of two lateral latches (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Material 1). For the T task, we used a similar box (only 2 cm high), but with the transparent lid permanently fixed and a gap at the box’s front. A wooden T-shaped rake measuring 16 cm x 3 cm (length x width) was placed into the box with its handle sticking out (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Material 2).

Experimental procedures

Experiments took place in two one-hour daily sessions, for the NT task starting at 1030 and 1300 h, and for the T task at 1000 and 1130 h, the difference being due to constraints in the operation of the host zoo. Birds had food ad libitum between 1130 and 1200 h and between 1400 and 1800 h (NT task), or between 1230 and 0900 h (T task). Water was only removed during the tests. The food reward was half a larval stage of Zophoba morio, which was not part of the subjects’ usual diet (boiled egg, raw chicken, commercial balanced food), due to their high-fat content. The jays were always highly motivated to obtain and consume these larvae.

Each session consisted of five trials, each 8 min in length, with a 5 min intertrial interval (ITI). Before the start of each session, we divided the cage in two with a wooden partition. During the ITI, the subject was retained on the right side, while the experimental box was set on the left. Each trial started by allowing the bird access to the left side. The total number of sessions experienced by each individual was determined according to welfare considerations (avoiding long runs of unsuccessful sessions) and the consistency of birds’ success (reaching a completion criterion as described later). Overall, it ranged between eight and 28 for the NT task and between eight and 14 for the T task. After completion, birds returned to the exhibit aviary.

Both tasks had a training and a testing phase. Training familiarised the subjects with each task’s ancillary actions and motivated them towards the task, while the critical required behavioural innovations were introduced in the testing phase. In the NT task, during training, neither latch blocked the lid, so the bird could lift it to obtain the food reward. We scaffolded acquisition by starting with the lid partly opened, reducing the aperture until the birds reliably extracted the reward from a fully closed position. If a bird failed to lift the lid during the five trials of a training session, it was presented with the box opened. A trial was considered successful if the bird obtained the reward. The criterion to finish training was to succeed in at least four of the five trials during two consecutive sessions. During the testing phase, one of the latches blocked the lid, and the bird had to rotate it to access the reward. The latch that blocked the lid was counter-balanced in semi-random order between trials (no more than three trials with the same blocking latch per session). The criterion to finish the test phase was to succeed in four of five trials during three consecutive sessions. All birds reached criterion, except for one (VV) that did not show signs of progress and whose testing was interrupted after 19 sessions. If on a given trial a bird did not interact with the apparatus at all (i.e., no touches during the eight minutes), the trial was dismissed and a ‘motivating’ trial with the lid unblocked was interspersed.

For the T task, the training phase started with the reward attached to the rake, so that the bird only had to pull the rake out. This stage was closer to a string-pulling retrieval than to a tool use task. If the bird failed to obtain the reward, the rake with the attached reward was placed on top of the box for one trial. Once the birds pulled out the (reward-attached) rake in at least four of five trials during two consecutive sessions, testing started. During testing, the reward was moved 3 cm to one side of the rake (right or left, counter-balanced in semi-random order between trials which meant that no more than three trials per session could have the same displacement direction), which had to be appropriately manipulated to retrieve the reward, thus fitting the conventional definition of tool use. A trial was considered unsuccessful if the bird did not obtain the reward during the specified time. If a bird pulled the rake out without the reward, it was allowed to continue manipulating the rake (i.e., touching, moving) until the trial finished. Testing ended for all birds after 30 unsuccessful trials (except for one bird that retrieved the reward once during the second testing session, the third trial, see Supplementary Material 3).

We video-recorded training and test trials in both tasks using a micro camera placed on top of the experimental box connected to a PVR recorder (PV-500 ECO, LawMate Int., TW).

Data analysis

For the test phase of the NT task, we recorded the number of sessions to reach criterion and the number of successful trials per session (0 to 5) for the first and last sessions. For these two sessions we analysed the videos and recorded if the first latch chosen was the one blocking the lid (correct) or the other one (incorrect), the latencies to touch the correct latch and to obtain the reward (if the bird did not succeed in getting the reward or did not touch the correct latch in the whole trial, the maximum possible value of latency = 480 s was assigned), and the number of times the bird touched the correct and incorrect latches. We only considered beak contacts as touches. For each trial of these two sessions, we calculated a choice index as the difference between the number of times the bird touched the correct and the incorrect latches divided by the total number of times the bird touched the latches (Index = [Correct – Incorrect] / [Correct + Incorrect], ranging theoretically from − 1 to 1). We followed a univariate approach for describing each of the variables mentioned and a multivariate approach using five variables (see Statistical analysis) for describing global patterns in the results. Results are reported as median ± SE; interquartile. The sample size for all the NT task results is eight. To perform an inter-rater reliability (IRR) test we analysed a randomly selected subset of the videos (15%) by a second coder who was naïve to the session treatment (i.e., first or last).

For the T task, we report for each individual if it showed spontaneous use of the rake which means that it pulled the rake out from the first trial of training (with no prior contact with the tool), the number of training sessions and the number of successful trials during the six testing sessions.

Statistical analysis

For the NT task, we performed a principal component analysis (PCA) using the following standardized dependent variables: number of sessions to reach criterion (V1); number of trials at which the bird touched the correct latch first (0 to 5) (V2); mean latencies to obtain the reward for (V3) and to touch the correct latch (V4), and the mean choice index (V5). This multivariate analysis aims to capture information from multiple variables and, although the variables are not totally independent, integrate the resulting information in orthogonal components that offer a richer descriptive picture of each subject’s behaviour. Each individual can be visualized in a two-dimensional plot (biplot) where patterns of similarity may arise (Abdi and Williams 2010). For biplot visualization, we used the R package factoextra (Kassambara and Mundt 2020).

To detect if the behavioural strategy to solve the task changed as the birds accumulated experience, we assessed the significance of differences between the first and last sessions by applying a permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) using the adonis function implemented in R package vegan (Oksanen et al. 2019). We used Euclidean distances and bootstrapped the samples 999 times. Similar multivariate dispersion for each group assumption of PERMANOVA was checked by calculating a permutation test for homogeneity of multivariate dispersions (pseudo-F1,14 = 0.50, P = 0.53).

To compare the variances of the first and last sessions, we quantified disparity from the Euclidean distance matrix bootstrapping 1000 times the samples and calculating the distance to the centroid of each group. This analysis allows us to discriminate if the birds changed their similarity in the values, they obtained in each variable during the last in comparison to the first session. This would indicate if they were converging or not to a similar solving strategy, rather than converging to a success rate through different approaches. Statistical significance among groups was assessed by the Welch Two Sample t-test using test.dispRity function. All disparity analyses were done using R package dispRity (Guillerme 2018).

For the IRR test we calculated the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) using the function icc from R package irr (Gamer et al. 2019). The ICC obtained was 0.91 (F51,52 = 21.2, P < 0.001, 95%-Confidence Interval for ICC Population Values: 0.85 < ICC < 0.95).

We performed multivariate analysis and IRR test on R v. 4.0.2 (R Core Team 2020).

For statistical purposes, we quantified differences in the number of successful trials between the first six testing sessions of the two tasks using a paired t-test, excluding subject VV because of its special circumstances (its inclusion would make no difference because it failed in both tasks and the conclusions drawn from the tests remained the same). Normality assumption of the difference between the two variables was checked with a Shapiro-Wilks Normality test (W = 0.98, P = 0.86).

Results

Did the birds learn the non-tool task?

Eight of the nine birds reached the criterion, taking a median of 7.5 sessions (Table 2). To quantify how behaviour changed with experience, we compared the first and last sessions. In this period, the number and range of successful trials per session, varied from 4 ± 0.6; 1–3 to 5 ± 0; 5–5 (Fig. 2A) and the latency to obtain the reward from 198.9 ± 45.3; 158.9–387 s to 63.6 ± 7.9; 42.6–73.8 s (Fig. 2B).

What did the birds learn during the non-tool task?

If improved performance depended on increased sensitivity to the physical structure of the task, the birds would have learned to discriminate between the correct (i.e., the one blocking the lid) and incorrect latch as they became more competent. We sought evidence for this in three ways: (1) proportion of trials in which they touched the correct latch first, (2) latency to touch the correct latch, and (3) proportion of correct latch touches relative to all latch touches. Comparing the first and last sessions, the proportion of trials in which they touched the correct latch first changed from 3 ± 0.4; 3–4 to 2 ± 0.4; 2–3 (random expectation = 2.5) (Fig. 2C), the latency to touch the correct latch decreased from 142.7 ± 47.7; 26–243.2 s to 31.6 ± 46.6; 20–100.8 s (Fig. 2D), and the choice index (which captures all latch touches in each trial rather than just the first ones) changed from 0.5 ± 0.1; 0.4–0.9 to 0.3 ± 0.1; 0.2–0.6 (Fig. 2E). Thus, there is no evidence of the birds detecting and exploiting the mechanical operation of the latches regarding the blocking of the lid.

Did the birds converge to a common solving pattern?

The multivariate analysis aimed to capture information from multiple variables to generate a descriptive picture of each subject’s behaviour and detect if birds were converging or not to a similar solving strategy rather than succeeding through different approaches. The PCA accounted for 69.8% of the total variance in the first two principal components (Fig. 3). Positive values of the PC1 (44.56%) mainly indicated shorter latencies (V3 and V4) and fewer sessions to reach criterion (V1), but also lower choice index (V5), whereas negative values indicated the opposite. Positive values of the PC2 (25.24%) depicted a greater number of trials at which the bird touched the correct latch first (V2), longer latencies to obtain the reward (V3) and higher values of choice index (V5), but also fewer sessions to reach criterion (V1) and shorter latencies to touch the correct latch (V4). The biplot defined by PC1 and PC2 depicted first and last sessions occupying different regions, with a differentiation in PC2 (as the hulls were more separated in this axis), whereas differences along PC1 were less obvious. Individual changes between the first and last sessions were also evident (see left inset in Fig. 3), indicating individual improvements in latency times in six out of eight cases. The little overlap between first and last sessions in the biplot (Fig. 3A) was also reflected in the results of the PERMANOVA (pseudo-F1,14 = 3.38, P = 0.017). Differences between first and last sessions were also denoted in a decrease in the distance to the centroid of each hull (disparity) between sessions, which indicates that individuals became more similar to each other in solving the task than they were at the beginning of the test phase (Fig. 3 right inset, Welch Two Sample t-test, t1779.6 = 62.1, P < 0.001). The results of the multivariate approach suggest that birds changed their ways to solve the task between sessions, converging to progressively similar behaviour, but does not identify the details of their asymptotic strategy.

We found no conspicuous side bias in the birds. The individual who failed to reach criterion in this task was the one who had to start after injury recovery, hence was probably impaired by extra-experimental factors.

Did the birds learn the tool task?

The training phase of the T task was promptly solved by all subjects. In the testing phase, that is when the rake had to be actively aimed at the reward, however, none of the birds succeeded in 30 trials, except for one individual who retrieved the reward in one trial (during the second testing session, the third trial; Table 2, Supplementary Material 3). We tested this task after the NT task, so individuals were already accustomed to the experimental protocols and were more likely to interact with the apparatus. We would have expected this design to provide some advantages in solving the T versus the NT task, but it did not. Comparing both tasks’ first six testing sessions, yields significantly greater success in the NT than the T task (successful trials in the NT task: 13 ± 2.2; 9–15; T task: 0 ± 0.1; 0–0; t7 = 5.91, P < 0.001).

Discussion

We tested plush-crested jays in two tasks of apparently similar motor complexity and incentive, one involving appropriate manipulation of an external free object, according to most definitions of tool use (T) and the other not (NT). In the NT task, most of the birds (eight out of nine) succeeded, while in the T task, none did except for one single retrieval. The results in the NT task show that these birds were cognitively competent enough to acquire effective behaviour when facing a novel mechanical problem, which, to all appearances, was not much simpler than our contrasting tool-dependent task but resulting in almost total failure. With only one task representing each category of problem, conclusions must, by necessity, be limited and wait for additional experimentation, but this should not impede reflecting on the potential implications. A first interpretation is that the difference in performance may have been caused by artefactual differences in protocol, especially because we could not, for external reasons, have identical deprivation schedules. We consider this extremely unlikely because the rewards we offered were extremely appetitive, and in both tasks the birds tried very actively to reach them. Indeed, all birds were eager to pull out the rake as soon as the trial started and eat the reward whenever they obtained it, pointing to more interesting explanations for the difference. Also, we consider unlikely that the different performance between NT and T tasks was the result of differences in the number of test sessions, as individuals were significantly more successful in the NT than the T task from the beginning of the test sessions. Considering that when exposed to the T task the subjects were more experienced than when facing the NT task, the difference in performance is striking. The difference in the experienced difficulty for the birds was obvious from start to the extent that testing in the T task was interrupted after six sessions, as none of the birds showed a qualitative improvement. Given the observed lack of progress and the extreme difference in success rate between tasks (successful individuals in each task: NT 8/9; T 0/9) we consider the sample size in terms of number of individuals and extent of testing sufficient. Further, the failure in the T task does not seem attributable to motoric difficulties since they were able to grasp and move the rake but did not seem to be able to aim it appropriately to make contact with the reward. However, it remains desirable to extend the number of tasks of both kinds, since the difference in competence may be influenced by the kind of tool use required.

Our results are consistent with the view that, at least in some species, tool use is a dedicated competence (Kacelnik 2009) that requires an inherited predisposition that facilitates the emergence of tool-related precursor behaviours (Kenward et al. 2006), rather than being just an outcome of general intelligence or learning proclivity. That view is also supported by studies that show similar performance of closely related tool-user and non-tool-user species in different NT tasks (Teschke et al. 2011, 2013; Kabadayi et al. 2016; Rat-Fischer et al. 2016). Moreover, Goffin cockatoos perform outstandingly in mechanical problem-solving problem tasks (Auersperg et al. 2013) and seem also capable of innovative tool use both in laboratory (Auersperg et al. 2012, 2014; Osuna-Mascaró et al. 2023) and free-living conditions (O’Hara et al. 2021). In other words, among species that are strikingly innovative, some appear to be able to project their abilities across tasks that depend or not on the use of tools. New Caledonian crows are another interesting case: they possess a heritable propensity for tool use (Hunt 1996; Kenward et al. 2005, 2006, 2011), but there is no evidence suggesting that they are cognitively more competent than other closely related corvids (Teschke et al. 2013; Kabadayi et al. 2016; Rat-Fischer et al. 2016). Clearly, the relation between tool-related behaviour and general intelligence still needs considerable experimentation and a great deal of thinking.

Returning to our results, we sought to identify how behaviour changed with experience using a multivariate approach that focused on several candidate variables. This analysis showed that the hulls around performance from the first to the last sessions had a little overlap, indicating that overall behaviour differed in many respects. Regarding the content of learning, we were surprised to discover that the birds continued touching both latches (correct and incorrect) similarly, indicating that although they became faster, they did not become more precise in their allocation of effort. This last result matters, because it goes against our expectation that the birds would acquire some ‘understanding’ of the physical relations between objects, perhaps learning to detect the mechanical operation of the blocking configuration and exploiting it. This lack of sensitivity to the blocking relation was evidenced when occasionally the birds moved the correct latch ‘incompletely’, so the lid was still blocked. In those cases, they switched to the incorrect latch and often switched back and forth until both were completely placed away from the lid, rather than taking a small step to complete the rotation of the correct latch. Overall, our results are consistent with the birds becoming competent by a rich form of reinforcement learning, rather than by ‘figuring out’ that there was a blocking relation between latch and lid. The multivariate analysis also showed that the disparity between individuals was reduced with learning, as if they were converging to some successful behavioural pattern, thereby becoming more similar in the way they solve the task.

One reason all subjects failed in the T task was that they persisted in pulling the rake out without performing the lateral displacements, that were mandatory to succeed. Interestingly, in human infants, this task becomes more difficult as the distance between the reward and the rake increases (Rat-Fischer et al. 2012). The gap between the tool and the reward seems to increase the difficulty of controlling an interaction between two objects as is characteristic of tool use. This difficulty is absent when just pulling a rake out without promoting such an interaction as in string-pulling tasks. Although reinforcement learning may play a central role in all cases, these more mechanically and kinematically demanding tasks can help to differentiate tool-use acquisition from conventional manipulanda used in the analysis of associative learning, such as pressing a lever or pecking at a key (Shumaker et al. 2011).

Fox et al. (1999) presented a series of hypotheses to explain the emergence of tool use, one of which states that this ability is favoured in unstable environments, where there is a fluctuation in the availability of resources. Unfortunately, not enough is known of this barely studied neotropical species.

Finally, we agree with von Bayern et al. (2020, p. 2738) who stated: “If unraveling human cognitive evolution and its link to flexible, creative tool use is the goal, it may be more fruitful to shift our focus from tool use per se to behavioural flexibility and creativity in general”, but if our goal is unravelling how cognition evolves in its extraordinary diversity across different organisms, tool use may still have a special place to play.
