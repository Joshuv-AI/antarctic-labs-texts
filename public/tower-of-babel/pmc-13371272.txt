Outcomes and evolving surgical trends in inguinal hernia repair following prior urologic surgery: a 15-year single-center retrospective cohort study
Authors: Hyo Seon Ryu, Eun Hae Cho, Ji-Seon Kim, Jung-Myun Kwak, Jin Kim, Se-Jin Baek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePrevious urologic surgeries, including prostatectomy and cystectomy, are associated with an increased risk of inguinal hernia and may complicate subsequent hernia repair due to adhesions and altered pelvic anatomy. However, evidence regarding the optimal surgical approach and outcomes in this population remains limited and inconsistent. This study aimed to investigate the outcomes of inguinal hernia surgery in patients with and without a history of urologic surgery and to examine evolving surgical trends over time.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study included adult male patients (≥ 18 years) who underwent primary inguinal hernia repair between January 2010 and December 2024. Patients were categorized according to a history of prior urologic surgery involving retropubic dissection. The primary outcome was the postoperative complication rate, and the secondary outcomes was hernia recurrence. Multivariate regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with outcomes.ResultsAmong 927 patients included in the analysis (826 without and 101 with prior urologic surgery), there were no significant differences in postoperative complication rates between groups (22.8% vs. 24.0%, p = 0.805), nor recurrence rates (1.0% vs. 4.2%, p = 0.186). However, operative time was significantly longer in the urologic group (87.0 vs. 68.4 min, p < 0.001). Multivariable analysis showed that prior urologic surgery was independently associated with longer operative time but not with increased postoperative complications or recurrence. Open repair was more common in the urologic group (51.5%), while the totally extraperitoneal approach (TEP) was rarely used (2.0%). Over time, the transabdominal preperitoneal approach (TAPP) became the predominant approach in this population (46.5%).ConclusionInguinal hernia repair in patients with a history of urologic surgery is safe and effective, with postoperative outcomes comparable to those in patients without such a history. Despite the increased technical complexity and longer operative times, minimally invasive approaches can be performed reliably in experienced centers.

Introduction

Inguinal hernia is a common surgical condition, particularly among older males, and its incidence is projected to increase with an aging population. Retropubic urologic surgeries are significant risk factors for the development of inguinal hernias [1, 2]. Although the incidence of inguinal hernia in the general population is < 1% [3–5], patients who have undergone prostatectomy have much higher rates, ranging from 7% to > 24% [1, 6–8]. This increased risk is attributed to the disruption of the transversalis fascia, endopelvic fascia, and preperitoneal tissue planes during pelvic dissection, which may weaken the myopectineal orifice. In addition, fibrosis of Hesselbach’s ligament, which originates from the lower margin of the transversalis fascia and anchors to the lacunar ligament, may pull the internal ring anteromedially, thereby weakening its support and predisposing it to indirect inguinal hernia formation [9].

Although the association between urologic surgery and inguinal hernia is well documented, evidence to guide optimal surgical strategies for hernia repair in this subset of patients is limited. Adhesions, fibrosis in the space of Retzius, and anatomical distortions pose technical challenges during hernia repair. Consequently, the appropriateness of minimally invasive surgery in this population is unclear, because technical difficulties may lead to a higher risk of complications or recurrence. Because inguinal hernia repair is generally considered a routine procedure with high expectations for rapid recovery and durable outcomes, avoiding postoperative complications and recurrence is particularly important in these technically challenging cases.

Minimally invasive hernia repair has been widely adopted because of its benefits in reducing early postoperative pain, faster return to work and daily activities, and fewer postoperative complications compared to Lichtenstein tension-free repair [10]. However, studies assessing the outcomes of minimally invasive repair in patients with prior urologic surgeries have shown inconsistent results. Some studies suggest outcomes equivalent to those of open repair [11, 12], whereas others report increased operative times or higher complication rates [13, 14]. Moreover, there is no consensus on the best approach for technically challenging cases.

Given the limited and inconsistent evidence regarding surgical outcomes in patients with prior urologic surgery, a clearer understanding of the safety and feasibility of different surgical approaches is needed. Therefore, this study aimed to compare postoperative outcomes of inguinal hernia repair between patients with and without prior urologic surgery and to evaluate temporal changes in surgical strategy over a 15-year period. We hypothesized that although prior urologic surgery would be associated with increased technical complexity, reflected by longer operative time, it would not result in higher rates of postoperative complications or recurrence.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective observational cohort study was conducted in a single tertiary care center. This study included patients who underwent primary inguinal hernia repair between January 2010 and December 2024. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Korea University Anam Hospital (IRB No. 2025AN0315), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design.

Patient selection

Adult male patients aged ≥18 years who underwent inguinal hernia repair were eligible for inclusion in the study. We excluded patients who had undergone recurrent inguinal hernia surgeries, prior contralateral hernia repair, emergency surgeries involving bowel resection, or those combined with other procedures. Patients were categorized into two groups: those with a history of urologic surgery involving retropubic dissection (e.g., radical prostatectomy or cystectomy) and those without any such surgical history (Fig. 1). All procedures were performed by board-certified surgeons specializing in abdominal wall and colorectal surgery at a tertiary academic center. The choice of surgical approach was determined by the attending surgeon. All procedures were performed electively using a tension-free mesh repair technique. Perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis and venous thromboembolism prevention were administered according to institutional protocols. Postoperative management followed institutional protocols. Patients were typically discharged after short hospital stays.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the rate of postoperative complications, including wound infection, seroma or hematoma formation, urinary retention, and chronic pain. Chronic pain was defined as pain in the inguinal region that persisted beyond three months after surgery. The complication severity was graded using the Clavien–Dindo classification [15]. Recurrence was defined as a clinically or radiologically confirmed hernia at the same site during the follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations when they followed a normal distribution based on the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test results and were compared using the Student’s t-test. Categorical variables are reported as numbers and frequencies and were analyzed using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate whether a history of urologic surgery was associated with postoperative complications and hernia recurrence. Predictors affecting the operative time were analyzed using linear regression analysis. To assess temporal trends in the surgical approach, stacked bar charts were used to visualize the changes over time. Logistic regression analysis with the year as a continuous variable was conducted to determine whether these changes were statistically significant. To reduce potential confounding by indication related to surgical approach selection, we performed sensitivity analysis restricted to patients who underwent minimally invasive surgery. All statistical analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.5.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

The urologic group was significantly older than the primary group (68.9 ± 7.6 vs. 63.5 ± 15.9 years, p < 0.001), while the body mass index (BMI) did not differ significantly between the groups (24.0 ± 2.6 vs. 23.6 ± 3.5 kg/m², p = 0.266). Regarding comorbidities, the prevalence of renal failure was significantly lower in the urologic group (0% vs. 5.1%, p = 0.039), while benign prostatic hyperplasia was significantly more common in the primary group (8.8% vs. 1.0%, p = 0.011). Other comorbidities did not differ significantly between the groups.

In the urologic group, 83.2% of the patients underwent prostatectomy, and 16.8% underwent cystectomy. Most urologic surgeries were performed using a robotic approach (89.1%).

The hernia laterality was not significantly different between the groups, and unilateral hernias were predominant in both groups (primary group: 91.0%, urologic group: 93.1%, p = 0.621). Indirect hernias were significantly more common in the urologic group (94.1% vs. 80.9%, p = 0.002).

The surgical approach differed markedly between the groups. While most patients in the primary group underwent laparoscopic repair (86.9%), open repair was more frequently performed in the urologic group (51.5%, p < 0.001). Additionally, the totally extraperitoneal approach (TEP) was rarely used in the urologic group (2.0%) but was commonly used in the primary group (52.2%, p < 0.001). The robotic approach was used similarly in both groups (5.9% vs. 3.2%). Anticoagulant use did not differ significantly between the groups (20.8% vs. 27.0%, p = 0.224).

Trends in surgical approach over time stratified by prior urologic surgery history

The temporal distribution of the surgical methods in patients with a history of urologic surgery is shown in Fig. 2a. Until the late 2010s, the Lichtenstein repair was the predominant approach used in this group. Although TEP was briefly attempted from 2016 to 2018, its use was limited and eventually discontinued. In contrast, the transabdominal preperitoneal approach (TAPP) has increasingly replaced open surgery in recent years, becoming the predominant method after 2020 and comprising nearly all cases by 2024.

Operative time and surgical approach

Table 2 summarizes the perioperative outcomes of the two groups. The operative time was significantly longer in the urologic group than in the primary group (87.0 ± 47.7 vs. 68.4 ± 33.6 min, p < 0.001). Intraoperative events

Intraoperative vessel injury or bleeding occurred more frequently in the urologic group (2.0% vs. 0.1%, p = 0.031). The conversion rates to other surgical methods were similar between the groups (1.8% each, p = 0.051).

Postoperative complications

The overall postoperative complications were not significantly different (22.8% vs. 24.0%, p = 0.051). The most frequent complications were seromas (12.9% vs. 8.7%, p = 0.237) and hematomas (6.9% vs. 5.9%, p = 0.470). In the primary group, other complications included pneumonia (n = 3), ileus (n = 1), epididymitis (n = 2), testicular atrophy (n = 1), hydrocele (n = 1), immediate recurrence (n = 3), and incisional hernia (n = 5). In the urologic group, one patient developed postoperative fever due to atelectasis. The chronic pain rates were also comparable, occurring infrequently in both groups (1.0% vs. 2.7%, p = 0.495). The Clavien-Dindo classification showed significant differences, mainly due to grade Ⅲb and Ⅳa complications occurring only in the primary group (p < 0.001).

Length of hospital stay

The hospital stay duration did not significantly differ between groups (urologic vs. primary: 1.6 ± 0.9 vs. 1.8 ± 2.4 days; p = 0.094).

Recurrence

Recurrence rates did not differ significantly between the groups (urologic vs. primary: 1.0% vs. 4.2%; p = 0.186).

Univariate and multivariate analyses of risk factors for postoperative complications and recurrence (n = 927)

Multivariate analysis revealed that an older age (OR: 1.018, 95% CI: 1.005–1.032, p = 0.005), hepatic disease (OR: 2.931, 95% CI: 1.472–5.835, p = 0.002), and longer operative time (OR: 1.006, 95% CI 1.001–1.011, p = 0.027) were independently associated with a higher risk of postoperative complications. Although bilateral hernia, anticoagulant use, and the TEP approach compared with TAPP approach were significant in the univariate analysis, they did not remain significant in the multivariate model.

For hernia recurrence, multivariate analysis identified bilateral hernia (OR: 4.397, 95% CI: 1.643–11.003, p = 0.002) and the TEP approach compared with TAPP (OR: 4.338, 95% CI: 1.811–12.167, p = 0.002) as independent predictors. A higher BMI showed borderline significance (OR: 1.089, 95% CI: 1.020–1.226, p = 0.059). A history of urologic surgery was not significantly associated with postoperative complications or recurrence.

Table 5 summarizes the results of the linear regression analyses and identifies the factors associated with the operative time. In the univariate analysis, a younger age, higher BMI, prior history of urologic surgery, and surgical approach were significantly associated with the operative time. In the multivariate model, a younger age (β = -0.678, p < 0.001), prior urologic surgery (β = 18.003, p < 0.001) remained independent predictors of a longer operative time. Compared to TAPP, the TEP approach was associated with a shorter operative time (β = -20.923, p < 0.001), whereas open repair was associated with longer operative time (β = 17.724, p < 0.001). Robotic repair was associated with a longer operative time than the laparoscopic approach (β = 30.322, p < 0.001).

Discussion

In this study, we found that a history of major urologic surgery, such as prostatectomy or cystectomy, influenced the inguinal hernia repair approach and operative complexity but did not adversely affect surgical outcomes. Patients with prior urologic surgery were more likely to have undergone open hernia repair in the earlier years of our series, whereas patients without a history of urologic surgery embraced laparoscopic repair as the dominant approach from the outset. However, these patterns evolved considerably over time. In the urologic group, open repair was the predominant approach until the end of the last decade. Since 2020, it had been almost completely supplanted by the TAPP laparoscopic technique. We rarely employed the TEP approach in the urologic cohort, which reflects a clear preference for TAPP in patients with prior pelvic surgery. In contrast, laparoscopic surgery was the preferred approach in patients without prior urologic surgery. TEP was the most commonly used technique for primary inguinal hernias during the initial years of the study; however, in recent years, there has been a notable shift toward TAPP in this group. Despite these differences in practice patterns, our results showed that a previous urologic surgery did not predispose patients to higher rates of postoperative complications or hernia recurrence.

A key observation of our study was that the only clear disadvantage associated with prior urologic surgery was a prolonged operative time. Extensive scarring and an altered anatomy from prior pelvic surgeries make dissection more laborious and can naturally lengthen the procedure. In TAPP procedures, adhesions within the Retzius space and along the medial umbilical ligament may complicate dissection and reperitonealization, thereby prolonging operative time. This finding is consistent with other reports that have documented an increased operative time in such cases [12–14]. Although TEP might theoretically be even more challenging in this context due to the need for medial preperitoneal space development, the number of TEP cases in the urologic group was very small. Moreover, it is likely that TEP was attempted only in carefully selected patients with relatively limited adhesions, introducing a potential selection bias. Therefore, the apparent lack of prolonged operative time in the TEP subgroup should be interpreted cautiously.

Importantly, a longer operative time did not translate into higher rates of major complications or recurrence. In the sensitivity analysis restricted to minimally invasive surgery, overall complication rates remained comparable. However, minor complications such as seroma and Grade Ⅰ events were more frequently observed in the urologic group. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that inguinal hernia repair after prostatectomy to be safe and feasible, with outcomes equivalent to those in patients without prior pelvic surgery [12, 16, 17].

Notably, studies have reported that patients with prior major urologic pelvic surgery have an increased risk of postoperative complications after inguinal hernia repair [11]. A meta-analysis reported significantly higher rates of postoperative complications (3.7% vs. 1.9%, p = 0.004) and seroma (1.6% vs. 0.9%, p < 0.001) in patients who underwent prostatectomy than in controls [13]. The variability in the findings across different studies is likely due to multiple factors. Many previous studies had relatively small sample sizes, limiting the generalizability of their conclusions. In addition, the extent of adhesions and anatomical alterations can vary depending on the specific type and technique of the preceding urologic surgery, thus affecting the technical complexity of the hernia repair. Therefore, the differences in outcomes between patients with and without prior urologic surgery may largely reflect the increased surgical difficulty. Surgical outcomes in this population are likely to vary considerably, depending on the surgeon’s experience and proficiency in complex settings.

Regarding the recurrence rate, there was concern that scar tissue or pelvic floor changes from surgeries such as radical prostatectomy could lead to suboptimal hernia repair and increase the risk of recurrence. We found no significant differences in recurrence between patients with and those without prior urologic procedures. This finding aligns with previous studies indicating that the long-term efficacy of inguinal hernia repair is not compromised by a history of pelvic surgery [13–14, 16–17]. In our cohort, TEP was predominantly performed in patients with primary hernias, whereas TAPP was more frequently selected in patients with prior urologic surgery. In the multivariable model, TEP was associated with a higher odds or recurrence compared with TAPP. Although this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the non-randomized nature of approach selection, it suggests that TAPP may achieve durable repair even in anatomically challenging cases rather than being disadvantaged by case complexity.

Our study also highlighted the evolution of surgical decision-making over time. Initially, many surgeons approached inguinal hernias after urologic surgery as special high-risk cases best managed with open repair, owing to their anatomical complexity. The fear of encountering obliterated tissue planes and risking organ injury led to caution regarding laparoscopy in this subgroup. However, as evidence accumulated and minimally invasive skills became more widespread, the practice patterns changed. By the late 2010s, surgeons at our center increasingly offered laparoscopic TAPP, even to patients with a history of urologic surgery, a trend that mirrored the broader acceptance of laparoscopic techniques. This shift coincided with improvements in laparoscopic technology and the advent of robotic platforms, which further facilitated minimally invasive surgery in complex cases. Our data showed that TAPP became the predominant approach after 2019 in patients with a history of urologic surgery, essentially replacing open repair by 2020. The driving rationale for this transition is the well-known benefits of minimally invasive hernia repair, such as reduced postoperative pain, faster recovery, and lower complication rates [18, 19]. Growing experience indicates that these advantages can be achieved even in patients with prior pelvic operations, provided the procedure is performed safely [9, 12, 20]. In the early study period, open repair remained the preferred approach because prior pelvic surgery was widely regarded as a contraindication to minimally invasive repair. TEP was attempted in a small number of patients but was progressively abandoned after the mid-2010s because dense fibrosis in the medial preperitoneal space frequently made dissection difficult. As experience accumulated, surgeons increasingly favored TAPP because it allowed safer lateral access and greater operative flexibility in these complex cases.

In our series, the TAPP approach was clearly favored over TEP in patients with prior urologic surgery, and this preference was rooted in technical considerations. TEP repair requires the creation of a preperitoneal space from the medial side, where adhesions from previous pelvic surgeries are often the densest. In contrast, TAPP allows entry into the hernia plane from the lateral side, which is generally less affected by scarring, making dissection technically easier. Additionally, TAPP provides a wider field of view and greater operative flexibility, which is advantageous for managing intraoperative challenges, such as bleeding or adherent hernia sacs. These factors likely explain why we used TAPP almost exclusively in the urologic surgery group. A meta-analysis evaluating minimally invasive hernia repair in patients with prior prostatectomy also reported that TAPP is more commonly performed than TEP in this subgroup and that TAPP is associated with significantly lower rates of seroma formation, conversion to open surgery, and chronic postoperative pain than TEP [20]. Nevertheless, TEP remains a viable option in selected cases. Previous studies have reported favorable outcomes with TEP, even in patients with prior pelvic surgery [16, 21]. The choice between TAPP and TEP can be individualized, based on the expertise of the surgeon. In our practice, given the challenges of scar tissue in the space of Retzius, we have found that TAPP offers clear advantages, which explains why TEP is rarely utilized in the urologic group.

This study had several limitations. First, this retrospective observational study was conducted at a single center, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Although the number of patients with prior urologic surgery was relatively high compared to that of other studies, the overall statistical power remained limited. Moreover, given the non-randomized design, selection bias and confounding by surgical indication cannot be entirely excluded. Although a sensitivity analysis restricted to minimally invasive cases was performed to mitigate differences in surgical approach, residual confounding may still have influenced the observed associations. Second, recurrence may have been underreported because routine long-term follow-up is not typically performed after inguinal hernia repair, and predefined surveillance intervals were not uniformly applied. Consequently, recurrence was analyzed as an observed clinical event rather than as a time-to-event outcome. However, as our institution is a tertiary referral center, many surgical patients continue to receive care for other conditions, and all patients in the urologic group were regularly followed up by the urology department, minimizing the likelihood of complete loss to follow-up. Nevertheless, the absence of standardized long-term follow-up may have resulted in underestimation of late recurrences, and the reported rates should therefore be interpreted with caution. Lastly, since routine postoperative ultrasonography was implemented for laparoscopic hernia repair in 2024, the detection rate of minor complications such as seroma or hematoma may have increased in recent cases, potentially introducing a detection bias. Therefore, caution should be exercised when interpreting complication-related outcomes.

In summary, our findings indicate that prior urologic pelvic surgery adds technical complexity to inguinal hernia repair but does not compromise patient outcomes. With appropriate surgical planning and techniques, patients with a history of major urologic procedures can achieve postoperative results equivalent to those in patients without prior pelvic surgery. The evolution of our surgical practice from a predominance of open repairs in the urologic group in earlier years to the near-universal adoption of TAPP by the end of the study period highlights the growing confidence and capability of surgeons and the ongoing refinement of minimally invasive techniques in inguinal hernia surgery. Nevertheless, the optimal approach for each patient should be individualized. Our results support the notion that prior pelvic surgery should not be viewed as a contraindication to a minimally invasive approach with careful surgical techniques and judicious intraoperative decision-making. Even patients with complex surgical histories can undergo safe hernia repair with low complication and recurrence rates. Further prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings and better define the optimal surgical strategies for inguinal hernia repair in this unique patient population.

Conclusion

Inguinal hernia repair in patients with a history of major urologic surgery is safe and feasible, with outcomes comparable to those in patients without a history of pelvic surgery. Although these patients often require more complex dissections and longer operative times, minimally invasive approaches can be performed effectively with low recurrence and complication rates. With careful surgical planning and appropriate technique selection, excellent outcomes can be achieved, even in this challenging subgroup.
