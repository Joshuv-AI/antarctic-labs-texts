Joule expansion of a quantum gas
Christopher J. Ho ∗,†, Simon M. Fischer ∗, Gevorg Martirosyan, Sebastian J. Morris, Jiří Etrych, Christoph Eigen, Zoran Hadzibabic

Abstract
We revisit the classic Joule-expansion experiments, now with a quantum-degenerate atomic Bose gas. In contrast to the classical-gas experiments, where no temperature change was measured, here we observe and quantitatively explain both cooling and heating effects, which arise, respectively, due to quantum statistics and inter-particle interactions.



The Joule expansion of a gas, first performed by Gay-Lussac in 1807
[
3
]
and then independently by Joule in 1845
[
4
]

[
5

While the original experiments were celebrated for measuring no change in the gas temperature, it was later understood that this expansion is isothermal only for ideal (non-interacting) gases, and should lead to cooling or heating for gases with, respectively, attractive or repulsive interactions
[
6
,
7
]

We now turn to experiments with an interacting quantum gas, where the total energy per particle, conserved during the Joule expansion, is

E=E_{\rm k}+E_{\rm int}\,,

I.1
Calculations for imperfect box trap

The walls of optical box traps
[
11
]
, created by focused laser beams, are not infinitely steep, and the resulting deviation from a perfect box potential is, for the purposes of thermodynamic calculations, captured well by an isotropic power-law potential,
U(r)=U_{0}(r/r_{0})^{p}
, where
p\gg 1
and
r_{0}
defines the effective box size. In our setup
[
30
]
, we typically achieve
p\approx 20
for low-temperature (
T\ll T_{\rm{c}}
) gases, but to study clouds at temperatures approaching
T_{\rm{c}}
we increase the trapping-laser power, which reduces the exponent
p
. We experimentally assess that
p\approx 10
by studying the shape of the momentum distribution in a degenerate non-condensed gas
[
10
,
13
]
.

In the semi-classical approximation, the distribution function for the ideal Bose gas in such a potential is

f_{\rm B}(k,r)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,g_{0}\left(ze^{-\frac{\hbar^{2}k^{2}}{2mk_{\rm{B}}\!T}-\frac{U(r)}{k_{\rm{B}}\!T}}\right)\,,

(S1)

and integrating over
r
gives the momentum distribution:

f_{\textrm{B}}(k)=\frac{V}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,g_{3/p}\left(ze^{-\frac{\hbar^{2}k^{2}}{2mk_{\rm{B}}\!T}}\right)\,,

(S2)

where

V\equiv\frac{3}{p}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{p}\right)\,\left(\frac{k_{\rm{B}}\!T}{U_{0}}\right)^{3/p}\,\frac{4\pi}{3}r_{0}^{3}\,,

(S3)

and
\Gamma
is the Gamma function; note that
V\rightarrow(4\pi/3)\,r_{0}^{3}
for
p\rightarrow\infty
. Integrating
f_{\textrm{B}}(k)
over
k
gives the total number of non-condensed atoms:

N^{\prime}=\frac{V}{\lambda^{3}}\,g_{\alpha}(z)\,,

(S4)

with
\alpha=3/2+3/p
. Also note that
V/\lambda^{3}\propto T^{\alpha}
. The critical temperature for condensation is then given by
z=0
and
N^{\prime}=N
, where
N
is the total particle number, so
T_{\rm{c}}\propto N^{1/\alpha}
.

Similarly, starting from Eq. (
S2
) and integrating for the kinetic energy per particle:

\dfrac{E_{\rm k}}{k_{\rm{B}}\!T}=\begin{cases}\frac{3}{2}\frac{\zeta(\alpha+1)}{\zeta(\alpha)}\left(\frac{T}{T_{\rm{c}}}\right)^{\alpha}&for $T\leq T_{\rm{c}}$\\
\frac{3}{2}\frac{g_{\alpha+1}(z)}{g_{\alpha}(z)}&for $T>T_{\rm{c}}$\end{cases}\,.

(S5)

If the gas is partially condensed both before and after the expansion,

\frac{T_{\rm{f}}}{T}=\left(\frac{V}{V_{\rm{f}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+1}},

(S6)

and more generally we numerically solve Eqs. (
S4
) and (
S5
) to get
T_{\rm{f}}
.
In Fig.
S1
we show the results of our calculations for a larger range of
T/T_{\rm{c}}
than covered in the experiments, for both
p=10
and a perfect box potential (
p\rightarrow\infty
).
