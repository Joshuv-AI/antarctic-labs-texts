Abstract
. In this paper, we study the absorption and fixation times for evolutionary processes on graphs, under different updating rules. While in Moran process a single neighbour is randomly chosen to be replaced, in proliferation processes other neighbours can be replaced using Bernoulli or binomial draws depending on 0 < p ≤ 1 0<p\leq 1 . There is a critical value p c p_{c} such that the proliferation is advantageous or disadvantageous in terms of fixation probability depending on whether p > p c p>p_{c} or p < p c p<p_{c} . We clarify the role of symmetries for computing the fixation time in Moran process. We show that the Maruyama-Kimura symmetry depend on the graph structure induced in each state, implying asymmetry for all graphs except cliques and cycles. There is a fitness value, not necessarily 1 1 , beyond which the fixation time decreases monotonically. We apply Harris’ graphical method to prove that the fixation time decreases monotonically depending on p p . Thus there exists another value p t p_{t} for which the proliferation is advantageous or disadvantageous in terms of time. However, at the critical level p = p c p=p_{c} , the proliferation is highly advantageous when r → + ∞ r\to+\infty .



1.
Introduction

Proliferation models were introduced in
[
1
]
to more realistically describe the propagation of mutant individuals in a finite population arranged on a graph. Resident individuals evolve like in the structured version
[
2
]
of the classical Moran process
[
3
]
, that is, if the individual randomly chosen for reproduction is a resident, then its clone replaces a single neighbour randomly chosen for death. However, if the individual chosen for reproduction is a mutant, we distinguish two types of draw:
Bernoulli
, where all or none of the neighbours become mutants depending on a certain probability
p
; or
binomial
, where the number of neighbours chosen for death follow a binomial distribution with probabilistic parameter
p
. Both processes were
used to model the spread of prion diseases and the proliferation of stem cells that produce multiple daughter cells, see
[
1
]
and references therein.

Although it may seem surprising, proliferation is not always to the advantage of mutants. The main result of
[
1
]
states that, whatever the underlying population structure, there always exists a critical value
p_{c}
(depending on the graph structure) such that the fixation probability for the proliferation process is equal to the fixation probability for the corresponding Moran process. If
p>p_{c}
, proliferation is advantageous, but if
p<p_{c}
it is disadvantageous. Symbolic computation for a small population shows that this critical value is highly correlated to the mean degree of the graph so that proliferation is more advantageous in cliques than in star graphs. By randomly updating of the proliferation parameter, fluctuating waves in the population of proliferating mutant individuals (similar to the
parasitaemia waves
exhibited in
[
4
]
) can be also created, replacing fixation or extinction by a quasi-stationary state.

In this paper, we are interested in the absorption and fixation times. The
mean absorption time
is the mean number of steps needed to reach one of the two absorbing states of the process, namely fixation or extinction of mutants, starting from any mutant or any set with the same number of mutants. The
mean fixation time
is the mean time required for any mutant (or any set with the same number of mutants) to take over the whole population, assuming that this will happen.

Similarly to fixation probability, definitions and derivations of these times for Birth-Death processes on (infinite) homogeneous populations can be found in
[
5
, Chapter 4, Section 7]
. A subsequent description of finite-state continuous-time Markov chains
[
5
, Chapter 4, Section 8]
has later been used by Ashcroft et al.
[
6
]
and independently by Hathcock and Strogatz
[
7
]
to study the distribution of mean fixation times in a finite homogeneous population. A full analytic computation has been given by Antal et al.
[
8
]
in the homogeneous case, whereas computations for the Moran process on complete, cycle and star graphs have been derived in
[
9
,
10
]
.

Maruyama
[
11
]
and Maruyama and Kimura
[
12
]
were the first to observe a symmetry between the fixation time of a set of
i
mutants and the extinction time of the complementary set of
N-i
residents in a homogeneous population. This symmetry was later rediscovered by Ashcroft et al.
[
6
]
and also
[
13
]
in average. More recently Hathcock and Strogatz
[
7
]
derived that the fixation time distributions of mutants of fitness
r
and
1/r
are identical in this case.
We will obtain both symmetries from another kind of symmetry in a clique, which we call
G-symmetry
, and we will use it to study the monotonicity of the fixation time of any mutant in the Moran process on any graph.
Although one would expect this time to decrease if the mutant is advantageous and his advantage increases, we will see that this is not strictly true.

Likewise, it could be expected that proliferation would reduce the mean fixation time, at least if the fixation probability is identical for both processes.
Here we will indeed prove that, in the critical regime, proliferation of mutant individuals reduces the mean absorption and fixation times, at least for some large classes of graphs.
However, we will also show the existence of a new critical probability
p_{t}
determining the transition from the regime where proliferation does not reduce the fixation time to the regime where proliferation reduces the fixation time.

More precisely, in Section
3
,
we explicitly compute absorption and fixation times for complete, cycle and star graphs of order
N
for Bernoulli proliferation, allowing to compare these times to the corresponding times for Moran process on each graph. The critical case where the proliferation is neither advantageous nor disadvantageous is particularly interesting. In all studied cases, this critical rate (Moran times/proliferation times, at
p=p_{c}
) is strictly greater than one for any fitness value
r>0
. The limit of the critical rate coincides for both, absorption and fixation times, as
r\to+\infty
, but depending on the type of graph, this limit is equal to:

•

Complete graph:
(N-1)H_{N-1}
where
H_{N-1}
is the
(N-1)
-harmonic number,

•

Cycle graph:
\frac{\textstyle 2N(N-1)}{\textstyle(N+1)(N-2)}
,

•

Star graph:

\frac{\textstyle N}{\textstyle 3N-2}\big[(N-1)\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}H_{i}\big].

Analytical expressions and rate limits are completed with the exact computation of times and rates for all graphs of order
6
included in Supplementary Material S4. Both approaches confirm that mean absorption and fixation proliferation times are lower for star and complete graphs, with cycles being the worst performing graphs.

In Section
4
, we also give complete derivations of mean absorption times for binomial proliferating and non-proliferating individuals on a complete graph. Absorption and fixation times and rates are graphically described for all graphs of order
6
and also for some additional orders, see Supplementary Material S4-S5.

Finally, in Sections
5
and
6
, we demonstrate the existence of a second critical value
p_{t}
such that the fixation time rate is greater or less than 1 depending on whether
p
is greater or less than
p_{t}
. This imply the existence of three different regimes when we compare Bernoulli or binomial proliferation with the classical Moran process: chance and time disadvantageous, chance or time disadvantageous, and finally chance and time advantageous.

2.
Proliferation models on evolutionary graphs

Proliferation models
on finite graphs
G=(V,E)
has been introduced in
[
1
]
. All such graphs will be assumed connected and undirected. As for the Moran model
[
3
]
and its structured version
[
2
]
, see also
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
, we assume:

•

A finite population is arranged on the vertex
set
V
of cardinal
|V|=N
.

•

There exist two types of individuals/particles/alleles/cells:
mutant/resident
,
type-1/type-2
,
advantageous/disadvantageous
or
mutant/wild-type
.

•

Mutant individuals have a relative fitness
r>0
with respect to resident individuals.

•

The population evolves when clones of a mutant or resident individual occupying a vertex
v
replace individuals, called
neighbours
, occupying vertices
w
linked to
v
by an element of the edge set
E
.

To define the evolutionary process, we suppose that, at a given time

n
, there are
i
mutants and
N-i
residents occupying
V
. Let
S_{n}\subset V
be the set of vertices occupied by mutants at this time. A vertex
v
is randomly
chosen with probability
p_{v}=\frac{r}{w_{i}}
if
v\in S_{n}
and
p_{v}=\frac{1}{w_{i}}
if
v\notin S_{n}
, where
w_{i}=ri+N-i
is the total reproductive weight of all individuals at this state.
As in the Moran process, if the vertex

v
is occupied by a resident then we randomly choose a neighbour
w
of
v
and put a resident on the vertex
w
.
If
v
is occupied by a mutant, under the classical
model, a neighbour
w
is also chosen in an equiprobable way to put a mutant. However, in
[
1
]
, we considered two types of clonal proliferation, both associated to a parameter
p\in[0,1]
which regulates its intensity:

i)

Bernoulli proliferation
: with probability
p
all the
neighbours of vertex
v
(whose number
d(v)
is the
degree of
v
) become occupied by mutants, and with probability
1-p
nothing happens.

ii)

Binomial proliferation
: each neighbour of
v
is occupied by a mutant with probability
p
independently. Thus the number of potentially occupied neighbours of
v
follows a binomial distribution with parameters
d(v)
and
p
.

The
fixation probability
is the basic quantity for the stochastic analysis of the proliferation studied in
[
1
]
.
For the Moran process on a graph
G=(V,E)
, given a set of vertices
S\subset V
occupied by mutants, the
fixation probability

\Phi^{M}_{S}(r)=\mathbb{P}\,[\,\exists n\geq 0:S_{n}=V\mid S_{0}=S\,]

is the solution of a system of
2^{N}
linear equations with boundary conditions

\Phi^{M}_{\emptyset}(r)=0
and
\Phi^{M}_{V}(r)=1
. Here
S_{n}
denotes the set of vertices occupied by mutants at time
n\geq 0
. As
G
is undirected, such a linear
system admits a unique solution
[
21
]
.
The
(mean) fixation probability

is given by

\Phi^{M}_{1}(r)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{v\in V}\Phi^{M}_{\{v\}}(r),

being a rational function on the fitness
r
. Similarly, we denote by
\Phi^{B}_{1}(r,p)
and

\Phi^{b}_{1}(r,p)
the mean
fixation probability for Bernoulli and binomial proliferation on

G
with fitness
r
and proliferation parameter
p
respectively. In
[
1
]
, we compared the fixation probabilities for proliferating and non-proliferating processes obtaining the following result:

Theorem 2.1

(
[
1
]
)
.

For any undirected
graph
G
, there is a unique pair of
critical values
p_{c}^{B}=p_{c}^{B}(r)
and
p_{c}^{b}=p_{c}^{b}(r)
depending on the
fitness
r>0
such that

\Phi^{B}_{1}(r,p_{c}^{B})=\Phi^{M}_{1}(r)\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi^{b}_{1}(r,p_{c}^{b})=\Phi^{M}_{1}(r).

Thus, for every fitness value
r>0
, there is a transition between two
different regimes where the proliferation is
advantageous
for mutants if
p>p_{c}^{\beta}(r)
, but
disadvantageous
if
p<p_{c}^{\beta}(r)
, with
\beta=b,B
. As explained in
[
1
]
, this fact alters the balance between amplifiers, suppressors and isothermal graphs in the Moran
process
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
]
.

Here we study other fundamental quantities in the stochastic analysis of the evolution in a finite population: the
mean absorption time
and the
mean fixation time
.
Denoting again
S_{n}
the set of vertices occupied by mutants at time
n\geq 0
,
for each subset
S
of
V
, the
absorption time
is the random variable

\tau^{\beta}_{S}=\min\{\;n\geq 0\;|\,S_{n}=\emptyset\text{ or }S_{n}=V\,\}

conditioned to
S_{0}=S
. Its expectation is given as solution of a system of linear equations, although this is now non-homogeneous. With a slight abuse of notation, we denote both the random variable and its expectation by the same symbol. The
mean absorption time

\tau^{\beta}_{i}
is the average of the expected times
\tau^{\beta}_{S}
associated to the subsets
S
having
i
elements.
In the next sections, we will consider the equations in some specific cases, although a precise general description is given in Supplementary Material S1.
The
fixation time

T^{\beta}_{S}
is the expectation of the random variable
\tau^{\beta}_{S}
, but conditioned to the fixation of mutants (see also Supplementary Material S1). The
mean fixation time

T^{\beta}_{i}
is again the average of
T^{\beta}_{S}
when
|S|=i
.
For convenience, we will usually omit the term mean for the times
\tau^{\beta}_{i}
and
T^{\beta}_{i}
.

For comparing the absorption and fixation times
\tau^{\beta}_{1}
and
T^{\beta}_{1}
of a single proliferating mutant (in average) to the absorption and fixation times
\tau^{M}_{1}
and
T^{M}_{1}
of a single non-proliferating mutant in the usual Moran process, we introduce the
absorption
and
fixation rates

\rho^{\beta}(r,p)=\frac{\tau^{M}_{1}(r)}{\tau^{\beta}_{1}(r,p)}\quad\text{and}\quad\emph{\textsf{P\hskip 0.44446pt}}^{\beta}(r,p)=\frac{T^{M}_{1}(r)}{T^{\beta}_{1}(r,p)}.

We will be especially interested in the case
p=p_{c}^{\beta}(r)
where proliferation does not imply an advantage or a disadvantage in which respect to the fixation probability, see Sections
3
and
4
. The indices
M
and
\beta
will be suppress when the type of process is clear. At the limit when
r\to+\infty
, we can observe the following properties (which will be proved in Supplementary Material S2):

Proposition 2.1
.

For any graph
G
, for any state
S\neq\emptyset,V
, and for any
p>0
, we have:

\lim_{r\to+\infty}\Phi_{S}^{M}(r)=\lim_{r\to+\infty}\Phi_{S}^{\beta}(r,p)=1,

where
\beta\in\{b,B\}
. We also have:

\lim_{r\to+\infty}\tau_{S}^{M}(r)=\lim_{r\to+\infty}T_{S}^{M}(r)\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{r\to+\infty}\tau_{S}^{\beta}(r,p)=\lim_{r\to+\infty}T_{S}^{\beta}(r,p).

These quantities will be later interpreted as the fixation time of the so-called
red process
associated to the Moran process or the proliferation processes, see Section
5
and Supplementary Material S3.

As we will see in Section

, the symmetry of expected fixation times is only apparent, unless the graph is a clique or a cycle. The following theorem details the general situation:

Theorem 2.2
.

For any graph
G

decreases on
[r,+\infty)

For proliferation processes, we will prove the following monotonicity result:

Theorem 2.3
.

For any graph
G
, for any fitness value
r>0
, and for any vertex

, we have that

T^{\beta}_{\{v\}}(r,p^{\prime})\geq T^{\beta}_{\{v\}}(r,p)

.

From this result, we will deduce the existence of a second phase transition

Theorem 2.4
.

For any graph

and for any fitness value

, there exists a proliferation parameter
p^{\beta}_{t}=p^{\beta}_{t}(r)
such that

\emph{\textsf{P\hskip 0.44446pt}}^{\beta}(r,p)\leq 1
if

and

\emph{\textsf{P\hskip 0.44446pt}}^{\beta}(r,p)\geq 1

p\geq p^{\beta}_{t}

Absorption and fixation times for Bernoulli proliferation

\displaystyle\begin{split}\emph{\textsf{P\hskip 0.44446pt}}(r,p)=\frac{T_{1}^{M}}{T_{1}~}&=(1+rp)\frac{N-1}{r+N-1}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\frac{\Phi^{M}_{j}}{\Phi^{M}_{1}}\frac{rj+N-j}{rj(N-j)}\frac{r^{N}-r^{j}}{r^{N}-1\phantom{o}}\Big)\\
&=(1+rp)\frac{N-1}{r+N-1}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\Phi^{M}_{j}\frac{rj+N-j}{rj(N-j)}\frac{r^{N}-r^{j}\phantom{m}}{r^{N}-r^{N-1}}\Big)\\
&=(1+rp)\frac{N-1}{r+N-1}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\frac{r^{N}(r^{j}-1)}{r^{j}(r^{N}-1)}\frac{rj+N-j}{rj(N-j)}\frac{r^{N}-r^{j}\phantom{m}}{r^{N}-r^{N-1}}\Big),\end{split}

and hence the
critical fixation time rate
is given by

(3.15)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\emph{\textsf{P\hskip 0.44446pt}}(r,p_{c})&=\frac{r^{N-1}+\cdots+r+1}{r^{N-2}+\cdots+r+1}\frac{N-1}{r+N-1}\big(\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\frac{\Phi^{M}_{j}}{\Phi^{M}_{1}}\frac{rj+N-j}{rj(N-j)}\frac{r^{N}-r^{j}}{r^{N}-1}\big)\\
&=\frac{r^{N-1}+\cdots+r+1}{r^{N-2}+\cdots+r+1}\frac{N-1}{r+N-1}\big(\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\frac{r^{N}(r^{j}-1)}{r^{j}(r^{N}-1)}\frac{rj+N-j}{rj(N-j)}\frac{r^{N}-r^{j}\phantom{m}}{r^{N}-r^{N-1}}\big).\end{split}

Observe that for
r=1
we get
\rho(1,p_{c})=H_{N-1}
and
\emph{\textsf{P\hskip 0.44446pt}}(1,p_{c})=N-1
. Finally, comparing (
3.15
) with (
3.9
), as the rate
\Phi^{M}_{j}/\Phi^{M}_{1}
converges to
1
when
r\to+\infty
(or applying directly Proposition
2.1
), we deduce immediately the following result:

Proposition 3.2
.

The fixation time for a single mutant individual in the Moran process on a complete graph approximates
(N-1)H_{N-1}
times the fixation time for a single proliferating mutant individual when
p=p_{c}
and
r\to+\infty
, that is,

\lim_{r\to+\infty}\emph{\textsf{P\hskip 0.44446pt}}(r,p_{c})=(N-1)H_{N-1}
. ∎

Propositions
3.1
and
3.2
can be graphically visualised in Supplementary Material S5 for some values of
N
.

3.3.
Absorption time for Bernoulli proliferation on cycles

For a cycle, we must distinguish when the starting state has one element or more than one element. In the first case, by symmetry, we can assume that the starting state is
S=\{1\}
. It may evolve to states
with 0 and 3 elements as in Figure
3(a)
(or also remains at the same state).
The non-trivial transition probabilities
are given by

\displaystyle p_{1,0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{w_{1}}

\displaystyle p_{1,3}

\displaystyle=\frac{rp}{w_{1}}

\displaystyle p_{1,1}

\displaystyle=1-(p_{1,0}+p_{1,3}),

where
w_{1}=r+N-1
. As depicted in Figure
3(b)
, the first equation for the absorption time is given by

(3.16)

\displaystyle\tau_{1}=p_{1,3}\tau_{3}+(1-p_{1,0}-p_{1,3})\tau_{1}+1

as
\tau_{0}=0
, and then

(3.17)

\tau_{3}=\frac{1+rp}{rp}\tau_{1}-\frac{w_{1}}{rp}.

(a)

The initial state

\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{\scriptstyle 0}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{\scriptstyle 1}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{\scriptstyle 2}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{\scriptstyle 3}
\textstyle{\dots}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{\bullet}
\textstyle{\scriptstyle N}

(b)

The reduced state space

Figure 3.
Bernoulli proliferation on a cycle:

3(a)
the initial state
S=\{1\}
,

3(b)
the reduced state space.

In general, as in the usual Moran model, all individuals of the same type are contiguous. Thus, for
i=2,3,\ldots,N-1
, the transition probabilities are given by:

(3.18)

\displaystyle\begin{split}p_{i,i-1}&=\frac{\textstyle 1}{\textstyle w_{i}}\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
p_{i,i+1}&=\frac{\textstyle 2rp}{\textstyle w_{i}}\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
p_{i,i}&=1-(p_{i,i-1}+p_{i,i+1}),\end{split}

where
w_{i}=ri+N-i
.
The corresponding absorption times are given by the equations:

(3.19)

\displaystyle\tau_{i}

\displaystyle=p_{i,i-1}\tau_{i-1}+p_{i,i+1}\tau_{i+1}+(1-p_{i,i-1}-p_{i,i+1})\tau_{i}+1.

As usually (see
[
10
]
and
[
26
]
), we can solve this system by replacing

\delta_{i}=\tau_{i}-\tau_{i-1}

to obtain

(3.20)

\displaystyle\delta_{i+1}=\frac{1}{2rp}\delta_{i}-\frac{w_{i}}{2rp}

for
i\geq 2
. More generally, we have

(3.21)

\displaystyle\delta_{i+1}=\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i-1}\delta_{2}-\sum_{j=2}^{i}\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i-j+1}w_{j}

for
i=2,3,\ldots,N-1
. Then

(3.22)

\displaystyle 0=\tau_{N}-\tau_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}=\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i}\Big)\delta_{2}-\sum_{i=2}^{N-1}\sum_{j=2}^{i}\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i-j+1}w_{j}+\delta_{1}.

Combining

\tau_{2}=\frac{1}{1+2rp}\tau_{1}+\frac{2rp}{1+2rp}\tau_{3}+\frac{w_{2}}{1+2rp}

with (
3.17
), we obtain

\tau_{2}=\Big(1+\frac{2}{1+2rp}\Big)\tau_{1}-\frac{2w_{1}}{1+2rp}+\frac{w_{2}}{1+2rp}

and hence

\delta_{2}=\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}=\frac{2}{1+2rp}\tau_{1}+\frac{w_{2}-2w_{1}}{1+2rp}=\frac{2}{1+2rp}\tau_{1}-\frac{N}{1+2rp}.

Therefore (
3.22
) becomes:

(3.23)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Big(1+\frac{2}{1+2rp}\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i}\Big)\tau_{1}&=\sum_{i=2}^{N-1}\sum_{j=2}^{i}\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i-j+1}w_{j}\\
&+\frac{N}{1+2rp}\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i}\end{split}

and finally we get:

(3.24)

\tau_{1}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{N-1}\sum_{j=2}^{i}\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i-j+1}w_{j}+\frac{N}{1+2rp}\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i}}{\displaystyle 1+\frac{2}{1+2rp}\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i}}.

Like for complete graphs, this value can be compared with the value of the absorption time for one single mutant in the Moran process on the cycle:

(3.25)

\displaystyle\tau_{1}^{M}=r^{N-1}\frac{\displaystyle r-1}{\displaystyle r^{N}-1}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\frac{w_{j}}{r^{i-j+1}}\Big)=\frac{\displaystyle r^{N-1}}{r^{N-1}+\cdots+r+1}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\frac{w_{j}}{r^{i-j+1}}\Big).

As is shown in
[
1
]
, the critical value
p_{c}
is the only solution of the equation

(3.26)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{1+2rp}\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\big(\frac{1}{r}\big)^{i}.

Then the absorption time rate at
p=p_{c}
becomes, after some simple calculations:

(3.27)

\displaystyle\rho(r,p_{c})=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\big(\frac{1}{r}\big)^{i-j+1}\big(rj+N-j\big)}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{N-1}\sum_{j=2}^{i}\big(\frac{1}{2rp_{c}}\big)^{i-j+1}\big(rj+N-j\big)+\frac{N}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\big(\frac{1}{r}\big)^{i}}.

Observe that, for
r=1
and
p_{c}=\frac{1}{2}
, we get:

\rho(1,p_{c})=\frac{N\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}i}{N\sum_{i=2}^{N-1}(i-1)+\frac{N}{2}(N-1)}=\frac{N}{N-1}.

When
r\rightarrow+\infty
,
the situation is now somewhat different from that described in Proposition
3.1
:

Proposition 3.3
.

The absorption time rate for a single mutant individual on a cycle graph satisfies the condition:

\lim_{r\to+\infty}\rho(r,p_{c})=\frac{2N(N-1)}{(N+1)(N-2)}.

Proof.

From equations (
3.24
) and (
3.25
), we deduce
\tau_{1}^{M}/\tau^{1}
is the product of the following three factors:

(3.28)

1+\frac{2}{1+2rp}\frac{(2rp)^{N-2}+\cdots+2rp+1}{(2rp)^{N-2}},

(3.29)

\frac{r^{N-1}}{r^{N-1}+\cdots+r+1},

(3.30)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\phantom{O}&\phantom{=}\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\frac{w_{j}}{r^{i-j+1}}}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{N-1}\sum_{j=2}^{i}\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i-j+1}w_{j}+\frac{N}{1+2rp}\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i}}\\
&=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{N-1}\sum_{j=2}^{i}\frac{rj+N-j}{r^{i-j+1}}+\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\frac{r+N-1}{r^{i}}}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{N-1}\sum_{j=2}^{i}\frac{rj+N-j}{(2rp)^{i-j+1}}+\frac{N}{1+2rp}\frac{(2rp)^{N-2}+\cdots+2rp+1}{(2rp)^{N-2}}}\\
&=\frac{\displaystyle D(r)+\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\frac{r+N-1}{r^{i}}}{\displaystyle D(2rp)+\frac{N}{1+2rp}\frac{(2rp)^{N-2}+\cdots+2p+1}{(2rp)^{N-2}}}.\end{split}

The two first factors (
3.28
) and (
3.29
) converge to
1
as
r\to+\infty
. For the third factor (
3.30
), we have:

\lim_{r\to+\infty}D(r)=\frac{(N+1)(N-2)}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{r\to+\infty}D(2rp)=\frac{1}{2p}\frac{(N+1)(N-2)}{2},

so this factor converges to

\frac{2pN(N-1)}{(N+1)(N-2)}.

Therefore we have:

\lim_{r\to+\infty}\rho(r,p)=\frac{2pN(N-1)}{(N+1)(N-2)}.

Finally, as
p_{c}
converges to
1
as
r\to+\infty
, we deduce:

\hskip 97.56493pt\lim_{r\to+\infty}\rho(r,p_{c})=\frac{2N(N-1)}{(N+1)(N-2)}.\hskip 135.14455pt\qed

Remark 2
.

From (
3.25
), we also deduce that
\lim_{r\to+\infty}\tau_{1}^{M}=\lim_{r\to+\infty}T_{1}^{M}=\frac{N(N-1)}{2}
.

3.4.
Fixation time for Bernoulli proliferation on cycles

As before, first we write the equation for the fixation time of one single mutant:

(3.31)

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}T_{1}

\displaystyle=p_{1,0}\Phi_{0}T_{0}+p_{1,3}\Phi_{3}T_{3}+(1-p_{1,0}-p_{1,3})\Phi_{1}T_{1}+\Phi_{1}

\displaystyle=p_{1,3}\Phi_{3}T_{3}+(1-p_{1,0}-p_{1,3})\Phi_{1}T_{1}+\Phi_{1}

as
\Phi_{0}T_{0}=0
. We deduce:

(3.32)

\displaystyle T_{3}=\frac{\Phi_{1}}{\Phi_{3}}\big(\frac{1+rp}{rp}T_{1}-\frac{w_{1}}{rp}\big).

In general, to solve the equation

(3.33)

\displaystyle\Phi_{i}T_{i}

\displaystyle=p_{i,i-1}\Phi_{i-1}T_{i-1}+p_{i,i+1}\Phi_{i+1}T_{i+1}+(1-p_{i,i-1}-p_{i,i+1})\Phi_{i}T_{i}+\Phi_{i}

for
2\leq i\leq N-1
, we proceed in two steps:

(i) First, we replace
z_{i}=\Phi_{i}T_{i}
to obtain:

(3.34)

\displaystyle z_{i}

\displaystyle=p_{i,i-1}z_{i-1}+p_{i,i+1}z_{i+1}+(1-p_{i,i-1}-p_{i,i+1})z_{i}+\Phi_{i}.

(ii) Second, we replace
\delta_{i}=z_{i}-z_{i-1}
to get:

(3.35)

\delta_{i+1}=\frac{1}{2rp}\delta_{i}-\frac{\Phi_{i}}{p_{i,i+1}}=\frac{1}{2rp}\delta_{i}-\frac{w_{i}\Phi_{i}}{2rp}.

As before, we deduce:

(3.36)

\displaystyle 0=z_{N}-z_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}=\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i}\Big)\delta_{2}-\sum_{i=2}^{N-1}\sum_{j=2}^{i}\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i-j+1}w_{j}\Phi_{j}+\delta_{1}.

Combining again

z_{2}=\frac{1}{1+2rp}z_{1}+\frac{2rp}{1+2rp}z_{3}+\frac{w_{2}\Phi_{2}}{1+2rp}

with (
3.32
), we obtain

z_{2}=\big(1+\frac{2}{1+2rp}\big)z_{1}-\frac{2w_{1}\Phi_{1}}{1+2rp}+\frac{w_{2}\Phi_{2}}{1+2rp}

and hence

\delta_{2}=z_{2}-z_{1}=\frac{2}{1+2rp}z_{1}+\frac{w_{2}\Phi_{2}-2w_{1}\Phi_{1}}{1+2rp}.

Therefore (
3.36
) becomes:

(3.37)

\Big(1+\frac{2}{1+2rp}\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i}\Big)z_{1}=\sum_{i=2}^{N-1}\sum_{j=2}^{i}\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i-j+1}w_{j}\Phi_{j}+\frac{2w_{1}\Phi_{1}-w_{2}\Phi_{2}}{1+2rp}\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i}

and finally we get:

(3.38)

T_{1}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{N-1}\sum_{j=2}^{i}\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i-j+1}w_{j}\frac{\Phi_{j}}{\Phi_{1}}+\frac{2w_{1}-w_{2}\frac{\Phi_{2}}{\Phi_{1}}}{1+2rp}\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i}}{\displaystyle 1+\frac{2}{1+2rp}\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\big(\frac{1}{2rp}\big)^{i}}.

Using a similar argument, the identity

0=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}=\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\big(\frac{1}{r}\big)^{i}\Big)\delta_{1}-\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\big(\frac{1}{r}\big)^{i-j+1}w_{j}\Phi^{M}_{j},

implies that the fixation time for one single non-proliferating mutant is given by

(3.39)

T_{1}^{M}=\frac{\delta_{1}}{\Phi^{M}_{1}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\frac{w_{j}}{r^{i-j+1}}\Phi^{M}_{j}.

For
r=1
and
p_{c}=\frac{1}{2}
, the critical fixation rate results (after using
w_{i}=N
and
\Phi^{M}_{i}=\Phi_{i}=\frac{i}{N}
, for all
1\leq i\leq N-1
, and some simple algebra):

\emph{\textsf{P\hskip 0.44446pt}}(1,p_{c})=\frac{N(N+1)}{(N-2)(N+3)}.

The same argument used before allows us to state the following result:

Proposition 3.4
.

The fixation time rate for a single mutant individual on a cycle graph satisfies the condition:

\lim_{r\to+\infty}\rho(r,p_{c})=\frac{2N(N-1)}{(N+1)(N-2)}.

Proposition 3.5
.

The absorption time for a single mutant individual on a star graph satisfies the condition:

\lim_{r\to+\infty}\rho(r,p_{c})=\frac{N}{3N-2}\big[(N-1)\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}H_{i}\big].

Proof.

To compute

\lim_{r\to+\infty}\rho(r,p)
,
we will compute independently the limits of
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{1}^{M}
using
equations (
3.43
) and (
3.47
). First,

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to+\infty}\tau_{1}=\frac{1}{N}\Big(\frac{1}{p}+(N-1)^{2}\frac{2p+p}{(N-1)p^{2}}\Big)=\frac{3N-2}{Np}.

Secondly, we compute the limit of each summand in (
3.47
). Since

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to+\infty}E(i)=\frac{N-1}{N-i}i

and

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to+\infty}\frac{D(i)}{D(1)}=\lim_{r\to+\infty}\frac{\displaystyle 1+\frac{N-1}{N-1+r}\sum_{j=i}^{N-2}\Big(\frac{N-1+r}{(N-1)r+1}\Big)^{j-i+1}\Big(\frac{1}{r}\Big)^{j-i+1}}{\displaystyle 1+\frac{N-1}{N-1+r}\sum_{j=1}^{N-2}\Big(\frac{N-1+r}{(N-1)r+1}\Big)^{j}\Big(\frac{1}{r}\Big)^{j}}=1,

the first summand converges to

\sum_{i=2}^{N-1}\frac{N-1}{N-i}i=(N-1)\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\frac{i}{N-i}-1=(N-1)\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}H_{i}-1,

where

\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}H_{i}=NH_{N-1}-(N-1)=N(H_{N-1}-1)+1
.
The second one is equal to
1/N

Remember finally that
\lim_{r\rightarrow+\infty}p_{c}(r)=1
.
∎

\tau_{i}=p_{i,i-1}\tau_{i-1}+p_{i,i}\tau_{i}+\cdots+p_{i,N}\tau_{N}+1,

We write

p_{i,i-1}\Delta_{i-1,i}=p_{i,i+1}\Delta_{i,i+1}+\cdots+p_{i,N}\Delta_{i,N}-1.

As

\Delta_{i,j}=\Delta_{j-1,j}+\Delta_{j-2,j-1}+\cdots+\Delta_{i,i+1}

we can reorder the terms of (

\displaystyle p_{i,i-1}\delta_{i}=~

\displaystyle p_{i,i+1}\delta_{i+1}+

\displaystyle p_{i,i+2}\delta_{i+1}+p_{i,i+2}\delta_{i+2}+

\displaystyle p_{i,i+3}\delta_{i+1}+p_{i,i+3}\delta_{i+2}+p_{i,i+3}\delta_{i+3}+\cdots

\displaystyle p_{i,N}\phantom{x}\delta_{i+1}+p_{i,N}\phantom{x}\delta_{i+2}+p_{i,N}\phantom{x}\delta_{i+3}+\cdots+p_{i,N}\delta_{N}-1,

where
\delta_{i}=\Delta_{i-1,i}
and
\delta_{j}=\Delta_{j-1,j}
for
j\geq i+1
.
Hence, defining

w_{i,j}=\frac{1}{p_{i,i-1}}\sum_{k=j}^{N}p_{i,k},

we reduce (
4.1
) to the linear system

(4.3)

\delta_{i}=w_{i,i+1}\delta_{i+1}+w_{i,i+2}\delta_{i+2}+\cdots+w_{i,N-1}\delta_{N-1}+w_{i,N}\delta_{N}-\frac{1}{p_{i,i-1}},

where
i=1,2,\ldots,N-1
. This system can be solved by backward substitution.
Firstly, we take

\delta_{N-1}=w_{N-1,N}\delta_{N}-\frac{1}{p_{N-1,N-2}}.

Substituting in the expression for
\delta_{N-2}
, we get:

\displaystyle\delta_{N-2}

\displaystyle=w_{N-2,N-1}\delta_{N-1}+w_{N-2,N}\delta_{N}-\frac{1}{p_{N-2,N-3}}

\displaystyle=\bigl(w_{N-2,N-1}w_{N-1,N}+w_{N-2,N}\bigr)\delta_{N}-\frac{w_{N-2,N-1}}{p_{N-1,N-2}}-\frac{1}{p_{N-2,N-3}},

and continue recursively.
Notice all possible strictly increasing sequences of numbers from
N-2
to

N
appears in the term involving
\delta_{N}
, while those from
N-2
to

N-1

(with
w_{N-2,N-2}=1
)
appears in the constant terms. In general, let

, with the convention

for all

, and denote

.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tau_{1}\;=\;-\delta_{1}\;&=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{p_{j,j-1}}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{1,j}}W(s)\\

Also recall

The aim of this section is to prove Theorem
2.2

\rm P^{M}

for the proliferation processes
\rm P^{\beta}
with

. Let
\rm P
be any process
\rm P^{M}
or
\rm P^{\beta}
defined on a connected undirected graph

with fitness

and proliferation parameter

\rm P
identifies to the random walk on the state space
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{P}(V)
with the graph structure given by the transition matrix
P

A more complete description can be seen in Supplementary Material S1 and S2.

5.3.
Monotonicity in
r

A consequence of G-symmetry for complete and cycle graphs is the fact that the maximum fixation time corresponds to the neutral drift
r=1
. The two previous examples, line and octahedron, show that this symmetry can partially persist, but it is globally broken (except in the two cases just mentioned). This implies a shift in the fitness value at which the maximum fixation time is reached, showing that the symmetry in Figure
1(b)
is only apparent.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

For any graph
G
of order
N
, there are only a finite number of states in which the G-symmetry on
\mathcal{K}_{N}
can be broken. Therefore, the fitness value at which the maximum fixation time is reached can only be shifted finitely many times.
For each vertex
v\in V
, as a rational function has finitely many local maxima, there is a fitness value
r(v)>0
such that the expected fixation time

T^{M}_{\{v\}}(r)
decrease for
r\geq r(v)
.
∎

The same argument applies for the mean fixation time
T_{1}^{M}
(as convex combination of functions
T^{M}_{\{v\}}
) and the mean absorption time
\tau_{1}^{M}
(as convex combination of the mean fixation and extinction times according to Supplementary Material S1). Obviously our argument does not allow us to say whether the maximum shifts to the right or left, nor the amplitude of the shift. For example, in the line
L_{3}
, the maximum is reached at
r=1.4253
, whereas
r=0.9997
in the octahedron. In fact, for the graphs of order
6
, the fitness value where the maximum is reached ranges between
r=0.9991
and
r=1.2559
.

5.4.
Graphical model

To prove Theorem
2.3
about the mononicity of expected fixation time depending on the proliferation parameter, we now consider the proliferation process
\rm P=\rm P^{\beta}
with
\beta=B,b
. Let us recall that Moran and proliferation processes can be obtained from a graphical representation
[
1
]
, which is inspired on Harris’ representation for contact processes
[
30
]
(see also
[
31
]
). This realisation is done on the set
G\times[0,+\infty)
and we start by taking two independent Poisson distributions

\Pi_{\varepsilon}(v)=\{\tau^{\varepsilon}_{i}(v):i\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}\}

on
[0,+\infty)
, with rate
r
for
\varepsilon=1
and rate
1
for
\varepsilon=2
. Each distribution determines a set of coloured marks

\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}=\{(v,\tau)\in V\times[0,+\infty):\tau\in\Pi_{\varepsilon}(v)\},

red for
\varepsilon=1
and blue for
\varepsilon=2
. Next we add two random sets of red and blue arrows. For any
(v,\tau)\in V\times[0,+\infty)
, we denote
A(v,\tau)\subset E\times\{v\}
the set of edges with origin
v
. The set
\mathcal{A}_{1}
of red arrows is defined as follows:

1)

For the Moran process, an element of
A(v,\tau)
is chosen at random if
(v,\tau)\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
.

2)

For Bernoulli proliferation, we have
\mathcal{A}_{1}(v,\tau)=A(v,\tau)
with probability
p
and
\mathcal{A}_{1}(v,\tau)=\emptyset
with probability
1-p
.

3)

For binomial proliferation, each element of
\mathcal{A}_{1}(v,\tau)
is chosen independently at random in
A(v,\tau)
with probability
p
.

To define
\mathcal{A}_{2}
, similarly to that happens in the Moran process, a unique element of
A(v,\tau)
is chosen at random if
(v,\tau)\in\mathcal{M}_{2}
.

From this graphical model we obtain a continuous-time Markov chain
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in[0,+\infty)}
which will realise the discrete-time Markov chain
\{S_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
provided by
\rm P
.
From now on, we will not distinguish between the expected absorption and fixation times of both types of processes, since they differ only in a factor that does not affect the inequalities that interest us. In fact, it will be convenient to consider only continuous-time processes, even if the results were stated for discrete-time processes. If
\rm P=\rm P^{\beta}
, we will write
\{X_{t}^{\beta}\}
if we need to specify it.
Ignoring blue marks and arrows, we obtain another continuous-time Markov chain, which we will denote by
\{\bar{X}_{t}\}
and call
red process
. Up to scale, this process is uniquely determined by the underlying graph.

Processes with the same fitness, but associated to different proliferation parameters
0<p^{\prime}<p
can be coupled taking the same random set of red and blue marks, but randomly deleting red arrows (see Supplementary Material S3 for details). Obviously, if the overall processes
\{X_{t}\}
and
\{X^{\prime}_{t}\}
are coupled, then the red processes
\{\bar{X}_{t}\}
and
\{\bar{X}^{\prime}_{t}\}
are also coupled.

For the red process, under Moran updating (1) and conditioning to an initial state
\bar{X}_{0}=S_{0}=\{v\}
, we define the first sojourn time as

\bar{t}_{1}=\inf\{t\in[0,+\infty):\bar{X}_{t}\neq\bar{X}_{0}\}

Recursively, if
\bar{t}_{n}<+\infty
, we define

\bar{t}_{n+1}=\inf\{t\in[\bar{t}_{n},+\infty):\bar{X}_{t}\neq\bar{X}_{\bar{t}_{n}}\}.

All sojourn times

\bar{s}_{n}=\bar{t}_{n}-\bar{t}_{n-1}

follow exponential distributions, but the parameters depend on the states
S_{n-1}
. Given the random variable

\bar{\mathbf{n}}=\sup\{n\geq 1:\bar{t}_{n}<+\infty\},

the expected fixation time
T_{S_{0}}
is the expectation of the random variable

(5.3)

\bar{t}_{\bar{\mathbf{n}}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bar{t}_{n}\mathds{1}_{\{\bar{\mathbf{n}}=n\}},

where
\mathds{1}_{\{\bar{\mathbf{n}}=n\}}
is the characteristic function of the event
\{\bar{\mathbf{n}}=n\}
.
We have already described the
mean fixation time
\bar{T}_{1}
of the (neutral) red process for complete, cycle and star graphs, as it coincides with the limit
\lim_{r\to+\infty}\,T_{1}^{M}
analytically computed in Remarks
1
,
2
, and
3
. As observed in the Supplementary Material S3, if the sojourn times
\bar{s}_{n}
are identically distributed, then Wald’s first lemma implies that:

(5.4)

\bar{T}_{S_{0}}=\mathbb{E}[\bar{t}_{\bar{\mathbf{n}}}]=\mathbb{E}[\bar{t}_{1}]\,\mathbb{E}[\bar{\mathbf{n}}].

More generally, returning to the overall process
\{X_{t}\}
, we can also define the first sojourn time

t_{1}=\inf\{t\in[0,+\infty):X_{t}\neq X_{0}\},

and recursively

t_{n+1}=\inf\{t\in[t_{n},+\infty):X_{t}\neq X_{t_{n}}\}

if
t_{n}<+\infty
. The conditional distribution of
t_{1}
given by
X_{0}=S_{0}=\{v\}
is exponential with parameter
\lambda(S_{0})
. Similarly

s_{n}=t_{n}-t_{n-1}

follows an exponential distribution of parameter
\lambda(S_{n-1})
when we conditioned to the state
S_{n-1}=X_{t_{n-1}}
in
\mathcal{S}
. Thus, if
\{S_{n}\}
denote the embedded jump chain associated to
\{X_{t}\}
and if we conditioned to
X_{0}=S_{0},\dots,X_{t_{n-1}}=S_{n-1}
, the variable

t_{n}=s_{1}+\dots+s_{n}

has a hypoexponential distribution with parameters
\lambda(S_{0}),\dots,\lambda(S_{n-1})
. This description should be compared to that of
[
5
, Section 4.8]
). Defining

\mathbf{n}=\sup\{n\geq 1:t_{n}<+\infty\},

then (
5.3
) becomes:

t_{\mathbf{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}t_{n}\mathds{1}_{\{\mathbf{n}=n\}}

and (
5.4
) can be reformulated as follows (see again Supplementary Material S3): for any state
S_{0}=\{v\}
, if the sojourn times
s_{n}
are identically distributed, then the expected fixation time is given by

(5.5)

T_{S_{0}}=\mathbb{E}[t_{\mathbf{n}}|\mathcal{F}_{S_{0}}]=\mathbb{E}[t_{1}|\mathcal{F}_{S_{0}}]\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{n}|\mathcal{F}_{S_{0}}],

where
\mathcal{F}_{S_{0}}
is the fixation event. Nevertheless, this approach to obtain monotonicity in
r
and
p
depends strongly on the hypothesis about the identical distribution of sojourn times.
Furthermore, even under this constraint, it is not possible to prove monotonicity in
r
, not even for the expected value of the first sojourn time
\mathbb{E}[\bar{t}_{1}]
or
\mathbb{E}[t_{1}|\mathcal{F}_{S_{0}}]
.

5.5.
Monotonicity in
p

Let
\rm P^{\beta}
be the proliferation process on
G
where
\beta=B,b
. As before under Moran updating, we can now define random variables
t_{1}^{\beta}
,
t_{n}^{\beta}
and
t_{\mathbf{n}}^{\beta}
associated to the continuous-time Markov chain
\{X_{t}^{\beta}\}
under
\beta
proliferation updating. The random variables associated to the corresponding red process will be denoted
\bar{t}_{1}^{\beta}
,
\bar{t}_{n}^{\beta}
and
\bar{t}_{\bar{\mathbf{n}}}^{\beta}
. As before, we choose a vertex
v
and take
S_{0}=\{v\}
as initial state.

Given any fitness value
r>0
, if the proliferation parameters
0<p^{\prime}\leq p
, then

(5.6)

\bar{t}_{n}^{\beta}(p^{\prime})\geq\bar{t}_{n}^{\beta}(p)

for any pair of coupled trajectories.
Therefore

(5.7)

\mathbb{E}[\bar{t}_{n}^{\beta}(p^{\prime})]\geq\mathbb{E}[\bar{t}_{n}^{\beta}(p)],

and then

(5.8)

\mathbb{E}[\bar{t}_{\bar{\mathbf{n}}^{\prime}}^{\beta}(p^{\prime})]\geq\mathbb{E}[\bar{t}_{\bar{\mathbf{n}}}^{\beta}(p)].

In the overall process, the blue marks and arrows modify the value of probabilities and times, but they do not modify the inequality after conditioning.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Indeed, replacing the red process by the overall process and translating the conditioning by using fixation probabilities (as we describe in Supplementary Material S2), we deduce:

(5.9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}T_{S_{0}}^{\beta}(r,p^{\prime})&=\frac{\mathbb{E}[t^{\beta}_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}(p^{\prime})]}{\Phi_{S_{0}}^{\beta}(r,p^{\prime})}\\
&\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}[t^{\beta}_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}(p^{\prime})]}{\Phi_{S_{0}}^{\beta}(r,p)}\\
&\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}[t^{\beta}_{\mathbf{n}}]}{\Phi_{S_{0}}^{\beta}(r,p)}=T_{S_{0}}^{\beta}(r,p),\end{split}

proving the result.
∎

6.
Critical proliferation

In this section, we shall prove Theorem
2.4
, that is, the existence of a second critical value of the proliferation parameter
p^{\beta}_{t}
such that
\beta
proliferation is advantageous in term of fixation time if
p\geq p^{\beta}_{t}
and disadvantatgeous if
p\leq p^{\beta}_{t}
. The proof will be immediately derived from three lemmas. The first lemma is a corollary of Theorem
2.3
:

Lemma 6.1
.

For any graph
G
and any fitness value
r>0
, the mean fixation rate
\emph{\textsf{P\hskip 0.44446pt}}^{\beta}(r,p)
is monotonically increasing in
p
.

Proof.

The mean fixation rate

\emph{\textsf{P\hskip 0.44446pt}}^{\beta}(r,p)=\frac{T^{M}_{1}(r)}{T^{\beta}_{1}(r,p)}

is obviously monotonically increasing in
p
since the mean fixation time
T^{\beta}_{1}(r,p)
is monotonically decreasing in
p
according to Theorem
2.3
.
∎

From the previous remark, we know that both limits

\lim_{p\to 0}T^{\beta}_{1}(r,p)\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{p\to 0}\emph{\textsf{P\hskip 0.44446pt}}^{\beta}(r,p)

always exist.

Lemma 6.2
.

For any graph
G
and any fitness value
r>0
,

\lim_{p\to 0}\emph{\textsf{P\hskip 0.44446pt}}^{\beta}(r,p)<1
.

Proof.

For each vertex
v\in V
, there exists
p>0
small enough to assure that the expected value of the sojourn time in the initial state
S_{0}=\{v\}
(which depends on
p
when
\beta=B
, but also on
d(v)
when
\beta=b
) is greater than the mean fixation time
T^{M}_{1}(r)
.
As
V
is finite, we deduce that all expected fixation times
T^{\beta}_{\{v\}}(r,p)>T^{M}_{1}(r)
for
p
small enough. It follows

\lim_{p\to 0}T^{\beta}_{1}(r,p)>T^{M}_{1}(r),

and then

\lim_{p\to 0}\emph{\textsf{P\hskip 0.44446pt}}^{\beta}(r,p)<1,

as we wanted.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

If
G\neq K_{2}
, then
\emph{\textsf{P\hskip 0.44446pt}}^{\beta}(r,1)\geq 1

Proof.

First, observe that the Moran process
\rm P^{M}
and the proliferation process
\rm P^{\beta}
can be coupled if
p=1
. Furthermore, if
G\neq K_{2}
, the number of red arrows must be
\geq 2
for some vertex
v\in V
.
This implies

T^{\beta}_{\{v\}}(r,1)\leq T^{M}_{\{v\}}(r)

and
then

\emph{\textsf{P\hskip 0.44446pt}}^{\beta}(r,1)=\frac{T^{M}_{1}(r)}{T^{\beta}_{1}(r,1)}\geq 1,

as we asserted.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

For any graph
G\neq K_{2}
and any fitness value
r>0
, the fixation time rate

\emph{\textsf{P\hskip 0.44446pt}}^{\beta}(r,p)<1
for

p
small enough from Lemma
6.2
and
\emph{\textsf{P\hskip 0.44446pt}}^{\beta}(r,1)\geq 1
from Lemma
6.3
. From Lemma
6.1
, there exists a unique proliferation value
p_{t}=p^{\beta}_{t}(r)\in(0,1]
such that

\emph{\textsf{P\hskip 0.44446pt}}^{\beta}(r,p_{t})=1
. For
p<p_{t}
, the rate
\emph{\textsf{P\hskip 0.44446pt}}^{\beta}(r,p_{t})<1
and the proliferation is disadvantageous, and for
p>p_{t}
the rate
\emph{\textsf{P\hskip 0.44446pt}}^{\beta}(r,p_{t})>1
and the proliferation is advantageous.
∎

7.
Conclusion

Proliferation processes
were introduced in
[
1
]
as a realistic method for increasing the fixation probability of mutant individuals in populations structured on graphs. However, somewhat surprisingly, we found that there always is a value of the proliferation parameter depending on the graph structure in which the fixation probability was the same. For lower values, proliferation is disadvantageous and only for higher values is advantageous. However, it could be expected that the occupation of neighbouring nodes following Bernoulli or binomial draws would always be advantageous in terms of (mean) fixation time.

This idea was initially supported by the analytical computation of mean absorption and fixation times for Bernoulli proliferation on complete, cycle and star graphs in Section
3
and binomial proliferation on complete graphs in Section
4
. We have applied methods similar to those used in
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
and
[
10
]
to solve the equations formulated from the transition matrix of the Moran process. Exact computations of mean fixation times for all graphs of order 6 (included in Supplementary Material) raised two questions about this process: first the apparent symmetry between the mean fixation time for mutants with fitness
r
and
1/r
and second the monotonicity of the mean fixation time when
r>1
. As observed by Hathcock and Strogatz in
[
7
]
, this symmetry can be derived from Maruyama-Kimura symmetry, introduced in
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
and rediscovered in
[
8
]
,
[
6
]
and
[
13
]
. We derive this symmetry from some symmetry of the process, which we has called
G-symmetry
, only observed in cliques and cycles. As a consequence, the maximum fixation time for these graphs is reached when
r=1
, but the breaking of the G-symmetry implies a shift to the right or the left. Our method does not allow us to compute the displacement, nor predict its direction. We only know that the mean fixation time is monotonically decreasing from this value. It would be interesting to find another method that explains this shift and, more precisely, why the values found by numerical methods for regular graphs of order 6 are less than 1.

Contrary to the initial idea, supported by analytical calculations for cliques, cycles and stars, we have proven that there is a second critical value
p_{t}
such that proliferation is no longer advantageous for
p\leq p_{t}
. As in
[
1
]
, we have resorted to a graphical model based on the Harris’ graphical method
[
30
]
and reduced the analysis to a ”no-backward” process that we have called
red process
. This method has allowed us to demonstrate that the mean fixation time decreases monotonically when the proliferation parameter increases, providing the second critical value, but does not explain
the entire dynamics of the process. An open question is the existence of graph structures where proliferation is advantageous in probability and disadvantageous in time in the intermediate regime. Another issue to be studied in the future is the relation between the fixation time for the (neutral) red process and the cover time for the SRW on the underlying graph.

Our main contribution in this work has been to clarify the role of symmetries of the Moran process for computing fixation time in a population structured on a graph. Unlike what happens with the fixation probability, the Maruyama-Kimura symmetry and the symmetry for mutants with fitness
r
and
1/r
depends on the graph structure induced on each states, justifying
some asymmetry in all graphs except cliques and cycles. The application of Harris’ graphical method to the study of Moran and proliferation processes is promising, but we have only used it to study the dependence of the mean fixation time on the proliferation parameter.
It is possible that the combination of these methods with others, whether classical (such as those used by Durrett, Grimmett and their co-authors
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
in the study of contact processes) or more recent (such as the martingale method proposed by Monk and van Schaik in
[
36
]
and
[
37
]
), will allow progress in the study of the absorption and fixation times of the Moran process.
