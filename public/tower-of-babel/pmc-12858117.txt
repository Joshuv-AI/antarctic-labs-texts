Assessing internal disaster preparedness at Security Forces Hospital, Riyadh
Authors: Alotaibi M. Wael, Abdullah Aedh Alotaibi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Hospital safety is very important, particularly in preparing for internal disasters such as fires, power outages, and chemical exposures, which can significantly disrupt hospital functions and endanger patients and staff.Study Objective:This study aimed to assess the readiness of the Security Forces Hospital in Riyadh to effectively manage internal disasters.Materials and Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted to evaluate hospital staff awareness, perception, knowledge, and preparedness regarding internal disasters. Data were collected through a structured survey. The study analyzed associations between awareness levels, work experience, and participation in disaster training programs.Results:Results showed that there is a generally high level of awareness, but gaps remained in disaster response training and knowledge of emergency protocols. Arabic-speaking respondents showed lower awareness compared to English speakers, while employees with less than five years of experience were more aware of internal disasters but lacked knowledge of the hospital’s emergency plan, with limited planning courses and evacuation drills.Conclusion:To enhance disaster preparedness, regular evacuation drills, structured training programs, and multilingual educational materials should be implemented. Standardizing emergency information on staff ID cards, improving accessibility to disaster protocols, and increasing awareness campaigns will strengthen overall readiness.

Introduction

Hospital safety is a critical priority, ensuring the well-being of patients and staff. Internal disasters, such as fires, power outages, and chemical exposures, originate within the facility itself and can severely impact hospital operations. Given that hospitals must function continuously to provide care, maintaining a high level of preparedness for internal disasters is essential to safeguard lives.

According to a study, hospital preparedness for internal disasters is crucial for mitigating risks.[1] However, research indicates that hospitals in Saudi Arabia struggle with disaster preparedness, particularly in staff education and training.[2]

Existing laws with regard to establishing institutional safety committee/s

The Joint Commission International accredits facilities based on safety standards, including emergency and disaster management, one of seven key programs in facility management.[3] Disaster planning involves three phases: pre-disaster, disaster, and post-disaster. Hospitals must fulfill six key elements: (1) develop and maintain disaster management program; (2) identify major internal and external disaster risks based on location; (3) assess the impact on care and services; (4) conduct annual program tests; (5) perform debriefing after each test; and (6) implement necessary improvements.[34]

Examples of internal disasters

Internal disasters occur within hospital walls due to human error or uncontrollable factors. Fire incidents are among the most severe, with nearly 5700 medical facility fires reported in the United States from 2012 to 2014, causing deaths, injuries, and annual losses of $5 million.[5] In India, 19 hospital fires were recorded primarily due to electrical short-circuits since 2011, with five hospitals experiencing repeated incidents.[6] Making robust emergency plans and fire suppression systems is essential.[7] Power outages and chemical exposure pose another major risk, disrupting critical equipment. Hospitals cannot function without electricity, as seen in Venezuela during the pandemic, where outages worsened healthcare crises.[8] A chemical incident in Gu-mi City, Korea, killed five people, highlighting the importance of inter-agency cooperation in disaster response.[19] Cyanide exposure can cause pulmonary damage and death.[10]

Effective fire safety management is crucial to protect hospital resources and human life.[11] Given the fatal risks of burns, hospitals must prioritize fire safety. Additionally, disaster management responsibility lies with hospital management and government authorities to ensure preparedness and response.[12]

Advantages of hospital preparedness plan for internal disasters

An effective hospital preparedness plan is crucial for institutional benefits. It enhances staff knowledge, confidence, and competency in managing internal disasters.[13] Integrating disaster preparedness into education and training ensures healthcare professionals are well-equipped to assess, plan, implement, and evaluate disaster response.[14] Hospitals require specific evacuation strategies, particularly for vulnerable patients.[15] Proper protocols for hazards must be in place to shut off medical gases, enhance staff awareness, and ensure automatic door closures to contain smoke and fumes,[16] and emergency lighting systems should activate immediately during power loss.[17]

Awareness of internal disasters among healthcare personnel

Mass casualty incidents, such as Hurricane Katrina (2005), the Japan earthquake and tsunami (2011), and the swine flu pandemic (2009), have reshaped healthcare workers’ attitudes toward disaster awareness and preparedness.[1819]

Despite its importance, disaster preparedness is rarely included in medical curricula. This gap in education contributes to low levels of awareness among healthcare personnel.[20] Studies have consistently reported poor knowledge of disaster preparedness among healthcare workers, emphasizing the need for structured training programs.[1821] Hospitals have a duty to provide ongoing education and training for their staff.[22] Increased awareness allows healthcare personnel to confidently and effectively respond to internal disasters.[23] Given that hospitals play a crucial role in disaster response, administrators must develop risk-based, tailor-fit internal disaster plans for their facilities.[1822]

Hospitals in the Middle East and many other regions suffer from a severe lack of structured training programs and drills.[24] Studies indicate that healthcare facilities are inadequately prepared for disaster management. Despite ongoing efforts to improve hospital staff training for disaster response, a significant gap persists.[24] Furthermore, many medical training programs do not adequately address the specific risks associated with internal disasters.[2]

The World Health Organization identifies key vulnerability factors that affect hospitals during emergencies, including patients, staff, medical equipment and facilities, fundamental services (communication, electricity, waste management, water, and sanitation), and hospital buildings. Structural failures, such as hospital collapses, pose significant risks to human life and financial resources.[25] A well-prepared hospital disaster plan could significantly reduce losses, as seen in past building collapses like the Surfside condominium collapse in Florida.[26]

Importance of disaster units in hospitals

Hospitals are critical infrastructure, especially during disasters. Ensuring hospital safety is essential for protecting patients, staff, and health infrastructure investments.[27] Disaster units within hospitals play a key role in disaster management. These units conduct rapid damage assessments, issue disaster warnings, implement preparedness exercises, and provide immediate assistance and a long-term recovery plan to maintain human life and health system stability.[2829] Regular engagement in disaster planning and evaluation enables hospitals to refine their response strategies through immediate and reflective feedback.[29]

Aims and research question formulation

Research on hospital preparedness for internal disasters in Saudi Arabia exists, but specific studies on Riyadh remain limited. A study found that government hospitals in Riyadh had unsatisfactory disaster preparedness in four key areas: chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear.[30] Disaster management education is crucial due to the increasing frequency of deadly disasters. Another study emphasized that implementing a structured disaster management curriculum enhances medical students’ knowledge of disaster medicine, covering emergency medicine, public health, and disaster management.[31]

As Security Forces Hospital (SFH) serves security forces personnel and their families,[32] this study assesses SFH personnel’s ability to handle internal disasters and to evaluate awareness levels among SFH employees with research questions that include how familiar are SFH employees with internal disaster management?, Do workers understand and follow evacuation procedures?, What factors contribute to a lack of preparedness?, Why is a strong internal disaster plan essential?, What measures can improve SFH’s disaster response?

This study provides a critical framework for enhancing internal disaster preparedness within hospital settings, offering direct benefits to both healthcare practitioners and patients. For practitioners, it highlights key areas of vulnerability, such as low participation in drills and limited communication with disaster committees, allowing hospital management to prioritize training. This, in turn, improves staff confidence, decision-making, and response efficiency during emergencies. For patients, a well-prepared hospital ensures timely evacuations, minimized disruptions to care, and reduced risk of harm during internal disasters.

Materials and Methods

This study was conducted at SFH, a military hospital, participants involving medical staff (physicians, pharmacists, paramedics, nurses) and non-medical employees (receptionists, human resources personnel). Participants are included if they are over the age of 18 years, employed at SFH for more than 12 months, registered in the human resources department, and can read or write in English or Arabic. A convenience sample was obtained through hospital email and WhatsApp, where participants completed the questions assessing demographics and internal disaster preparedness. The study was conducted between May and June 2021, following Institutional Review Board of Security Force Hospital Program—IRB approval in April 2021 (Ref No.: H-01-R-069). Of the 980 SFH personnel contacted, only 16% responded as given in suppl. table in Figure 1.

Pie chart showing the distribution of respondents based on sex

The questionnaire, comprising 23 questions, was developed through a literature review and expert consultation. Email was the primary mode of communication, with FaceTime and Zoom for follow-ups. Excel was used for data analysis and visualization. The research followed a structured procedure: identifying participants, distributing a questionnaire via email, analyzing responses using statistical methods, and interpreting results. To ensure validity and reliability, participants’ identities were kept confidential, no interviews were recorded, and expert consultation helped verify responses, particularly in the knowledge and awareness section.

Nurses constituted the largest group (35.95%), while other respondents included physicians, computed tomography technologists, security, engineers, and HR staff. Knowledge

Only 62% correctly identified an internal disaster, with physicians (93.75%) and security personnel (91.67%) scoring highest, followed by pharmacists (88.89%) and EMTs (83.33%), while nurses had the lowest (9.09%). 93% of respondents knew how to use a fire extinguisher, with EMTs and security personnel (100%) most proficient. Awareness of emergency codes was low (19% by occupation, 21% by department), with nurses (29%) and obstetrics staff (55.56%) scoring highest. Code Red was the most recognized (77.78% by occupation, 75.16% by department) while Code Silver was the least (28.76% by occupation, 30.72% by department). Only 29.41% knew the correct evacuation steps, and about one-third knew the emergency hotline, as shown in Tables 2-4 and Figures 7–10.

Perception

The perception of the importance of having a disaster management unit and preparedness for specific incidents was generally positive. Respondents believed the hospital was best prepared for power outages (88.89% by occupation, 92.16% b department) in which nurse, pharmacist, EMT, and security scored above 90% but physician has the lowest perception of 25%, followed by fire (86.27% by occupation, 81.7% by department) and chemical spills (72% by occupation, 70.59% by department). Most (83% by occupation, 73.20% by department) supported a dedicated disaster unit, though a small percentage (1.96%) viewed it as less important, as in Tables 2-4 and Figures 7–10.

Preparedness

Only 19.61% of all participants reported having taken a disaster planning course, and 45.75% participated in evacuation drills, with the highest representation seen among “Security” (58.33%) and EMT (33.33%). Nurses had a notably low participation rate (1.82%) in disaster planning courses; however, they demonstrated high awareness in evacuating non-ambulatory patients (89.09%) and participation in evacuation drills (45.45%). In contrast, physicians reported moderate participation in drills (31.25%) and knowledge of patient evacuation (56.25%) but showed low engagement with disaster committees (6.25%). Overall, engagement with disaster committees was limited, with only 20.26% of all participants reporting any communication, indicating a potential gap in organizational coordination, as shown in Tables 2-4 and Figures 7–10.

Discussion

This study found that staff at Security Forces Hospital in Riyadh had a moderate level of awareness and knowledge about internal disasters. However, their understanding varied depending on their job and department. For example, while nurses showed good awareness of general emergencies, they were sometimes unsure about their exact responsibilities and the correct protocols. This is similar to what Al-Thobaity and colleagues found: nurses in military hospitals were more prepared than those in government hospitals, but overall disaster knowledge was still low and often came only from participating in drills.[33] Another study showed that emergency doctors and nurses could answer basic questions but didn’t feel very confident in disaster response, with 98% saying they needed more training. In our study, many staff members understood the basic emergency steps, but fewer knew the more detailed procedures, such as using the hospital’s command system or managing utilities during a disaster. This is similar to findings from another study, that while many staff knew the basics, like fire evacuation (85.8%), fewer understood specific systems like the incident command system (49.9%).[34] In short, most staff knew the simple actions but were not confident in handling complex emergency situations.

Other studies support these findings. A national survey showed that disaster preparedness across Saudi hospitals was about 70%, but the weakest area was staff skills and training.[35] This matches what we found at SFH, while there are written plans, many staff had little experience with actual drills. Many hospitals in Saudi Arabia lack enough disaster drills and proper training.[37] In our emergency department, only about half the staff had ever taken part in a real drill. In another Riyadh study, 81% of emergency staff said their hospital had drills,[34] but those may have been general drills, not focused on internal disasters. This matters because practice is essential, and simulation training has been shown to improve knowledge and disaster response. Two recent reviews found that disaster simulations help people learn better and remember more, meaning hospitals without regular drills may not be truly ready for emergencies.[36]

Participation in drills was especially low among non-medical staff, such as administration and security, and among staff in departments outside the emergency unit. These teams are just as important in internal disasters, but often get overlooked. Al-Thobaity’s research also showed that disaster preparedness was different between military and civilian nurses,[33] and in our military hospital, coordination across departments was not always strong. Another big issue was communication. Many of SFH’s staff are foreigners, and studies show that over half of the nurses in Saudi public hospitals are non-Saudis who mostly speak English.[38] Research suggests that having clear, bilingual communication and good teamwork is essential.[38] In one study from Mecca, nurses reported that poor communication between doctors, nurses, managers, and support staff was a major problem.[37] We saw similar concerns at SFH without shared language protocols and team exercises; important instructions could be missed during real emergencies like fires or chemical leaks.

Lastly, differences between departments and roles stood out. Like in Al-Dulijand’s study, where staff had higher awareness of earthquake procedures in areas more likely to experience them, we found that emergency department staff who are used to dealing with crises knew more about disaster plans than those working in regular wards. Non-medical staff, like housekeeping and office workers, had lower awareness of how to deal with spills or evacuate during emergencies. This kind of uneven awareness is worrying because internal disasters can happen in any part of the hospital. It shows the need for department-based training. International reviews show that internal disasters often affect high-risk areas like ICUs or operating rooms the most.[38] In the Netherlands, power and information and communication technology failures hit emergency and surgical departments hardest.[38] Based on this, SFH should make sure that these high-risk departments regularly hold drills for fire, chemical, and power-related emergencies.

Limitations

There are several limitations. First, the response rate was very low, with only 16% participation, which may introduce non-response bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. The single-site, cross-sectional design of the study provides only a snapshot of internal disaster preparedness at the hospital, making it difficult to infer causality or apply the results to other hospitals or regions. The reliance on self-reported data further introduces potential recall. Furthermore, the observed disparities in awareness between Arabic and English speakers suggest that language and cultural barriers may have influenced the responses. Finally, the study’s focus on perceived awareness and preparedness, rather than on actual performance, limits the ability to evaluate the true effectiveness of the hospital’s disaster response capabilities.

Conclusion

This study addressed familiarity with internal disasters and evacuation processes, revealing gaps in awareness and perception. Some respondents questioned the importance of a disaster unit, which could undermine evacuation plans if not addressed. Robust awareness campaigns and periodic training are essential, with assessment tools to evaluate their impact.

Making disaster-related documents accessible to all employees, regardless of language, can enhance comprehension. Additionally, using proportional allocation in sampling would improve data accuracy, strengthening policy-making efforts to enhance knowledge, awareness, perception, and preparedness for internal disasters.

Future research should focus on developing standardized internal disaster training programs for challenges of healthcare settings in Saudi Arabia. Longitudinal studies can be conducted to assess the long-term impact of such programs on preparedness levels. Additionally, simulation-based learning and multilingual training modules could help address gaps identified among staff with diverse backgrounds and language proficiencies.
