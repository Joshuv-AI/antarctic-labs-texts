Algebraic representatives of the ratios


Abstract
In [ 10 ] we introduced even polynomials Ξ n , Λ n ∈ ℚ ⁡ [ x ] \Xi_{n},\Lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}[x] arising from integral representations of β ⁡ ( 2 ​ n ) / π 2 ​ n − 1 \beta(2n)/\pi^{2n-1} and ζ ⁡ ( 2 ​ n + 1 ) / π 2 ​ n \zeta(2n+1)/\pi^{2n} . Notations.

Throughout this manuscript, we adopt the following conventions. The set
\mathbb{N}_{0}
denotes the set of all non-negative integers, while
\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of strictly positive integers. The symbol
\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{m}{k}
denotes the Eulerian numbers of type
A
. The quantities
B_{2n}
and
E_{2n}
denote, respectively, the Bernoulli numbers and the Euler (secant) numbers. Finally,
\displaystyle\left\langle{n\atop k}\right\rangle^{\!B}
denotes the Eulerian numbers of type

Introduction

Euler’s celebrated evaluation

\zeta(2)=\dfrac{\pi^{2}}{6}

and, more generally, his formula for the even zeta values

\zeta(2n)=(-1)^{n+1}\frac{B_{2n}(2\pi)^{2n}}{2(2n)!}

stand among the classical milestones of analytic number theory. In striking contrast, no analogous closed formula is known for the odd values
\zeta(2n+1)
. A complementary phenomenon occurs for Dirichlet’s beta function

\beta(s)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}}{(2m+1)^{s}},

whose special values at odd arguments admit explicit evaluations in terms of Euler numbers, while the even values
\beta(2n)
remain mysterious. The arithmetic nature of the normalized ratios

\dfrac{\zeta(2n+1)}{\pi^{2n+1}},\quad\dfrac{\beta(2n)}{\pi^{2n}}

continues to pose deep and largely unresolved questions.

In the earlier work
[
10
]
, various integral representations for the normalized values
\dfrac{\zeta(2n+1)}{\pi^{2n}}
and

\dfrac{\beta(2n)}{\pi^{2n-1}}
were developed within a unified analytic framework involving logarithmic, trigonometric and hyperbolic kernels. In particular, it was shown that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exist even polynomials
\Xi_{n},\Lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
such that

\frac{\beta(2n)}{\pi^{2n-1}}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x\,\Xi_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,\operatorname{arctanh}(x)}\,dx,\qquad\frac{\zeta(2n+1)}{\pi^{2n}}=\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{x\,\Lambda_{n}(x)}{\operatorname{arctanh}(x)}\,dx,

and several initial examples of these polynomials were explicitly computed. These constructions emerged naturally from families of Malmsten-type integrals and from rational expressions of polylogarithms of negative order in terms of Eulerian numbers of type A and type B.

The purpose of the present article is to undertake a systematic study of these polynomials. While
[
10
]
established their existence and provided illustrative low-degree cases, here we derive explicit closed formulae for their coefficients, prove structural properties valid for all
n
, and investigate their zero distributions and asymptotic behavior.

More precisely, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we construct explicit even polynomials
\Xi_{n}
and
\Lambda_{n}
of degree
2n-2
expressed through finite sums involving Eulerian numbers. Starting from rational representations of negative-index polylogarithms and performing suitable changes of variables and integrations by parts, we obtain integral identities linking these polynomials directly to
\beta(2n)
and
\zeta(2n+1)
. This approach replaces the nested binomial structures appearing in earlier formulations by compact polynomial expressions.

Beyond their analytic origin, the families
(\Xi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(\Lambda_{n})_{n\geq 1}
exhibit remarkable algebraic features. We determine their leading coefficients and boundary values, establish sign properties outside the interval
[-1,1]
, and show that all real zeros lie in this interval. For
n\geq 2
we prove that all zeros are real and simple and that consecutive polynomials display an interlacing pattern. As consequences, the associated coefficient sequences are log-concave (hence unimodal), and the extremal zeros admit precise asymptotic control via endpoint ratio estimates.

In this way, the present work complements and deepens the analytic perspective developed in
[
10
]
. It reveals an unexpected bridge between special values of Dirichlet series, Eulerian combinatorics, and the theory of real-rooted polynomials, thereby enriching the structural understanding of the normalized quantities

\zeta(2n+1)/\pi^{2n}
and
\beta(2n)/\pi^{2n-1}
.

§1. Mathematical motives

A key ingredient underlying our work is the very simple identity

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x^{2m}}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\,B\!\left(m+\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\,\frac{\Gamma\!\left(m+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma(m+1)}=\frac{\pi}{2}\,\frac{(2m-1)!!}{(2m)!!},

valid for every
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Here
n!!
denotes the double factorial, i.e.

(2m-1)!!=1\cdot 3\cdot 5\cdots(2m-1),\qquad(2m)!!=2\cdot 4\cdot 6\cdots(2m),

with the usual conventions
(-1)!!=0!!=1
. The identity itself follows by the substitution
t=x^{2}
, which transforms the integral into

\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}t^{m-\frac{1}{2}}(1-t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,dt=\frac{1}{2}\,B\!\left(m+\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right),

and then by using the relation between the Beta and Gamma functions together with the standard formulas expressing
\Gamma(m+\tfrac{1}{2})/\Gamma(m+1)
in terms of double factorials. As a direct consequence, the quantity
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{P_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx
is always a rational multiple of
\pi
when
P_{n}
means an even polynomial, that is, a polynomial of the kind :

a_{n}\,x^{2n}+a_{n-1}\,x^{2n-2}+a_{n-2}\,x^{2n-4}+\ldots+a_{1}\,x^{2}+a_{0}\quad\text{where}\quad\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}\\
a_{1}\\
\vdots\\
a_{n}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n+1}.

(1)

In
[
10
]
we proved the existence of polynomials
\Xi_{n}(x)
and
\Lambda_{n}(x)
of the kind (
1
) such that the following representations hold:

\frac{\beta(2n)}{\pi^{2n-1}}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x\,\Xi_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,\operatorname{arctanh}x}\,dx,\qquad\frac{\zeta(2n+1)}{\pi^{2n}}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x\,\Lambda_{n}(x)}{\operatorname{arctanh}x}\,dx.

(2)

We deduce that the integrals

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\Lambda_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\Xi_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx

are always rational multiples of
\pi
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In
proposition

4.14
we even prove that these quantities are strictly positive. That is, there exist
(p_{n}^{A},q_{n}^{A}),(p_{n}^{B},q_{n}^{B})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
satisfying

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\Lambda_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx=\frac{p_{n}^{A}}{q_{n}^{A}}\pi\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\Xi_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx=\frac{p_{n}^{B}}{q_{n}^{B}}\pi.

(3)

The uniform convergence of these polynomials to
0
on
(0,1)
established in
theorem

4.10
implies that

\frac{p_{n}^{A}}{q_{n}^{A}}\to 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{p_{n}^{B}}{q_{n}^{B}}\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Now assuming that
\dfrac{\beta(2n)}{\pi^{2n}}
and
\dfrac{\zeta(2n+1)}{\pi^{2n+1}}
are rational reads

\dfrac{\beta(2n)}{\pi^{2n-1}}=\frac{s_{n}^{B}}{t_{n}^{B}}\pi\qquad\text{and}\qquad\dfrac{\zeta(2n+1)}{\pi^{2n}}=\frac{s_{n}^{A}}{t_{n}^{A}}\pi.

with some positive integers
s_{n}^{A},s_{n}^{B},t_{n}^{A}
and
t_{n}^{B}
. Using (
3
) yields

\dfrac{\beta(2n)}{\pi^{2n-1}}=\frac{s_{n}^{B}\,q_{n}^{B}}{t_{n}^{B}\,p_{n}^{B}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\Xi_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx\qquad\text{and}\qquad\dfrac{\zeta(2n+1)}{\pi^{2n}}=\frac{s_{n}^{A}\,q_{n}^{A}}{t_{n}^{A}\,p_{n}^{A}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\Lambda_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx.

Going back to (
2
) gives

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x\,\Xi_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,\operatorname{arctanh}x}\,dx=\frac{s_{n}^{B}\,q_{n}^{B}}{t_{n}^{B}\,p_{n}^{B}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\Xi_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx\quad\text{,}\quad\int_{0}^{1}\frac{x\,\Lambda_{n}(x)}{\operatorname{arctanh}x}\,dx=\frac{s_{n}^{A}\,q_{n}^{A}}{t_{n}^{A}\,p_{n}^{A}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\Lambda_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx.

This is equivalent to

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\Xi_{n}(x)\left(\frac{x\,}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\,\operatorname{arctanh}x}-\frac{s_{n}^{B}\,q_{n}^{B}}{t_{n}^{B}\,p_{n}^{B}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\right)\,dx=0\\
\vskip 8.5359pt\\
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\Lambda_{n}(x)\left(\frac{x\,}{\operatorname{arctanh}x}\,-\frac{s_{n}^{A}\,q_{n}^{A}}{t_{n}^{A}\,p_{n}^{A}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\right)\,dx=0.\end{cases}

These last equalities may serve as a natural starting point for a more
robust approach to proving the irrationality of the ratios

\beta(2n)/\pi^{2n}
and
\zeta(2n+1)/\pi^{2n+1}
. In particular, a
detailed analysis of the structural and analytic properties of the
polynomials involved is likely to yield further insight into this
problem. The present article is devoted to the investigation of the
structure of these polynomials and of several closely related properties.

§2. Preliminaries

Lemma 2.1
.

As
n\to\infty
one has

\bigl((2n-1)!\bigr)^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}\longrightarrow 0.\qquad\text{,}\qquad\frac{4}{\bigl(2(2n)!\bigr)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}}\longrightarrow 0.

Proof.

We use the standard Stirling-type lower bound: there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all integers
m\geq N
,

m!\ \geq\ \left(\frac{m}{e}\right)^{m}.

(4)

(For instance, this follows from Stirling’s formula
m!\sim\sqrt{2\pi m}\,(m/e)^{m}
.)

Applying (
4
) with
m=2n-1
(for
n
large enough) yields

(2n-1)!\ \geq\ \left(\frac{2n-1}{e}\right)^{2n-1}.

Taking
(n-1)
-th roots we obtain

\bigl((2n-1)!\bigr)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\ \geq\ \left(\frac{2n-1}{e}\right)^{\frac{2n-1}{n-1}}=\left(\frac{2n-1}{e}\right)^{2+\frac{1}{n-1}}.

Since
(2n-1)/e\to\infty
and
2+\frac{1}{n-1}\to 2
, the right-hand side diverges to
+\infty
, hence

\bigl((2n-1)!\bigr)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\to\infty
. Taking reciprocals gives

\bigl((2n-1)!\bigr)^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}\to 0
.

For the second claim, note that

\bigl(2(2n)!\bigr)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}=2^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\,\bigl((2n)!\bigr)^{\frac{1}{n-1}},\qquad\text{with}\qquad 2^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\to 1.

Applying (
4
) with
m=2n
gives

(2n)!\ \geq\ \left(\frac{2n}{e}\right)^{2n},

hence

\bigl((2n)!\bigr)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\ \geq\ \left(\frac{2n}{e}\right)^{\frac{2n}{n-1}}=\left(\frac{2n}{e}\right)^{2+\frac{2}{n-1}}\longrightarrow\infty.

Therefore
\bigl(2(2n)!\bigr)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\to\infty
and taking reciprocals yields

\dfrac{4}{\bigl(2(2n)!\bigr)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}}\to 0
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
0\leq k\leq n-1
. Define

E_{n,k}(x):=(1-x^{2})^{k}\Big((1+x)^{2n-2k-1}-(1-x)^{2n-2k-1}\Big).

Then
E_{n,k}
is an odd polynomial of degree
2n-1
and admits the representation

E_{n,k}(x)=2x\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}\binom{k}{i}\binom{2n-2k-1}{\,2t-2i+1\,}\right)x^{2t},

where, throughout, binomial coefficients are understood to vanish whenever the lower index is negative or exceeds the upper index.

Proof.

Set

P_{k}(x):=(1-x^{2})^{k},\qquad Q_{n,k}(x):=(1+x)^{2n-2k-1}-(1-x)^{2n-2k-1}.

By the binomial theorem, these polynomials admit the finite expansions

P_{k}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}(-1)^{i}x^{2i},\qquad Q_{n,k}(x)=2\sum_{r=0}^{n-k-1}\binom{2n-2k-1}{2r+1}x^{2r+1}.

We now adopt the standard convention that

\displaystyle\binom{a}{b}=0\quad\text{whenever}\quad b<0\ \text{or}\ b>a,

which allows us to rewrite these finite sums as sums over
\mathbb{N}_{0}
without changing their values:

P_{k}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\binom{k}{i}(-1)^{i}x^{2i},\qquad Q_{n,k}(x)=2\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\binom{2n-2k-1}{2r+1}x^{2r+1}.

Evaluating both series at
\sqrt{x}
yields

P_{k}(\sqrt{x})=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\binom{k}{i}(-1)^{i}x^{i},\qquad Q_{n,k}(\sqrt{x})=2\sqrt{x}\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\binom{2n-2k-1}{2r+1}x^{r}.

Forming the Cauchy product gives

P_{k}(\sqrt{x})Q_{n,k}(\sqrt{x})=2\sqrt{x}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{t}(-1)^{i}\binom{k}{i}\binom{2n-2k-1}{2(t-i)+1}\right)x^{t}.

Since
\displaystyle\binom{k}{i}=0
for
i>k
, the inner sum may be truncated at
i=k
.
Moreover,
\displaystyle\binom{2n-2k-1}{2(t-i)+1}=0
whenever
t>n-1
, hence the outer sum truncates at
t=n-1
.
Therefore,

P_{k}(\sqrt{x})Q_{n,k}(\sqrt{x})=2\sqrt{x}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}\binom{k}{i}\binom{2n-2k-1}{2(t-i)+1}\right)x^{t}.

Replacing
x
by
x^{2}
yields the asserted representation for
E_{n,k}(x)
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
|z|<1
. Then

\frac{\Im\!\bigl(\operatorname{Li}_{-m}(iz)\bigr)}{z}=\frac{1}{(1+z^{2})^{m+1}}\sum_{r=0}^{m}\left\langle{m\atop r}\right\rangle^{\!B}\,(-z^{2})^{r}.

Proof.

We begin with the power-series definition of the polylogarithm (valid for
|z|<1
):

\operatorname{Li}_{-m}(w)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{m}w^{k}.

With
w=iz
we obtain

\operatorname{Li}_{-m}(iz)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{m}i^{k}z^{k}.

Taking imaginary parts and using the cycle
i^{k}\in\{1,i,-1,-i\}
gives

\Im\!\bigl(\operatorname{Li}_{-m}(iz)\bigr)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\Bigl((4j+1)^{m}z^{4j+1}-(4j+3)^{m}z^{4j+3}\Bigr).

Dividing by
z
yields

\frac{\Im\!\bigl(\operatorname{Li}_{-m}(iz)\bigr)}{z}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(4j+1)^{m}z^{4j}-\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(4j+3)^{m}z^{4j+2}.

Now set
x:=-z^{2}
. Since
z^{4j}=x^{2j}
and
z^{4j+2}=(-z^{2})^{2j+1}=x^{2j+1}
, we obtain the single series

\frac{\Im\!\bigl(\operatorname{Li}_{-m}(iz)\bigr)}{z}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(4j+1)^{m}x^{2j}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(4j+3)^{m}x^{2j+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(2k+1)^{m}x^{k},\qquad x=-z^{2}

(5)

Next we invoke the Worpitzky-type identity for Eulerian numbers of type
B
(Brenti
[
2
]
; Bagno–Garber–Novick
[
1
]
),
which states that for every
k\geq 0
,

\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\binom{m+k-\ell}{m}\left\langle{m\atop\ell}\right\rangle^{\!B}=(2k+1)^{m}.

(6)

Multiply (
6
) by
x^{k}
and sum over
k\geq 0
. Interchanging the order of summation gives

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(2k+1)^{m}x^{k}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\left\langle{m\atop\ell}\right\rangle^{\!B}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{m+k-\ell}{m}x^{k}.

In the inner sum, substitute
k=\ell+j
(so
j\geq 0
) to obtain

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{m+k-\ell}{m}x^{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\binom{m+j}{m}x^{j+\ell}=x^{\ell}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\binom{m+j}{m}x^{j}.

By the general binomial series expansion
[
8
]
,

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\binom{m+j}{m}x^{j}=\frac{1}{(1-x)^{m+1}},\qquad|x|<1.

Hence,

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(2k+1)^{m}x^{k}=\frac{1}{(1-x)^{m+1}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\left\langle{m\atop\ell}\right\rangle^{\!B}x^{\ell},\qquad|x|<1.

(7)

Finally, combining (
5
) and (
7
) with
x=-z^{2}
yields

\frac{\Im\!\bigl(\operatorname{Li}_{-m}(iz)\bigr)}{z}=\frac{1}{(1-(-z^{2}))^{m+1}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\left\langle{m\atop\ell}\right\rangle^{\!B}(-z^{2})^{\ell}=\frac{1}{(1+z^{2})^{m+1}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\left\langle{m\atop\ell}\right\rangle^{\!B}(-z^{2})^{\ell},

which proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Big\langle{2n-1\atop k}\Big\rangle^{B}(-1)^{k}(2n-2k-1)=-2^{\,2n-2}\,E_{2n},\\
\hskip 2.84544pt\\
\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Big\langle{2n\atop k}\Big\rangle(-1)^{k}(2n-2k-1)=-\,\dfrac{2^{\,2n+1}\bigl(2^{2n+2}-1\bigr)}{2n+2}\,B_{2n+2}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Define the coefficients

\begin{cases}C^{(B)}_{n,m}:=\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2m+1)!}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Big\langle{2n-1\atop k}\Big\rangle^{B}(-1)^{k}(2n-2k-1)^{2m+1},\\
\hskip 2.84544pt\\
C^{(A)}_{n,m}:=\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2m+1)!}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Big\langle{2n\atop k}\Big\rangle(-1)^{k}(2n-2k-1)^{2m+1}.\end{cases}

Let

F^{(B)}_{n}(x):=\sum_{m\geq 0}C^{(B)}_{n,m}\,x^{2m+1},\qquad F^{(A)}_{n}(x):=\sum_{m\geq 0}C^{(A)}_{n,m}\,x^{2m+1}

be the associated odd generating functions. We prove the claim by evaluating
C^{(A)}_{n,0}
and
C^{(B)}_{n,0}
. Substituting the definitions of the coefficients into the odd generating functions and using

\displaystyle\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{(ax)^{2m+1}}{(2m+1)!}=\sinh(ax)
, we obtain

\begin{cases}F^{(B)}_{n}(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Big\langle{2n-1\atop k}\Big\rangle^{B}(-1)^{k}\,\sinh\!\big((2n-2k-1)x\big),\\[10.0pt]
F^{(A)}_{n}(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Big\langle{2n\atop k}\Big\rangle(-1)^{k}\,\sinh\!\big((2n-2k-1)x\big).\end{cases}

Writing
\sinh(y)=\frac{e^{y}-e^{-y}}{2}
and splitting the sums, we obtain

\begin{cases}F^{(B)}_{n}(x)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,e^{(2n-1)x}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Big\langle{2n-1\atop k}\Big\rangle^{B}(-1)^{k}e^{-2kx}-\frac{1}{2}\,e^{-(2n-1)x}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Big\langle{2n-1\atop k}\Big\rangle^{B}(-1)^{k}e^{2kx},\\[12.0pt]
F^{(A)}_{n}(x)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,e^{(2n-1)x}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Big\langle{2n\atop k}\Big\rangle(-1)^{k}e^{-2kx}-\frac{1}{2}\,e^{-(2n-1)x}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Big\langle{2n\atop k}\Big\rangle(-1)^{k}e^{2kx}.\end{cases}

Reindexing only the second sums by
k\mapsto 2n-1-k
so that they run from
k=n
to
2n-1
, we obtain

\begin{cases}F^{(B)}_{n}(x)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,e^{(2n-1)x}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Big\langle{2n-1\atop k}\Big\rangle^{B}(-1)^{k}e^{-2kx}+\frac{1}{2}\,e^{(2n-1)x}\sum_{k=n}^{2n-1}\Big\langle{2n-1\atop 2n-1-k}\Big\rangle^{B}(-1)^{k}e^{-2kx},\\[12.0pt]
F^{(A)}_{n}(x)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,e^{(2n-1)x}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Big\langle{2n\atop k}\Big\rangle(-1)^{k}e^{-2kx}+\frac{1}{2}\,e^{(2n-1)x}\sum_{k=n}^{2n-1}\Big\langle{2n\atop 2n-1-k}\Big\rangle(-1)^{k}e^{-2kx}.\end{cases}

Recalling the symmetry relations for Eulerian numbers
[
5
]
,

\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{k}=\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{N-1-k}\quad\text{(Typ $A$)},\qquad\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{k}^{B}=\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{N}{N-k}^{B}\quad\text{(Typ $B$)},

we may identify the reindexed coefficients and merge the two sums into a single sum:

\begin{cases}F^{(B)}_{n}(x)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,e^{(2n-1)x}\sum_{k=0}^{2n-1}\Big\langle{2n-1\atop k}\Big\rangle^{B}(-1)^{k}e^{-2kx},\\[12.0pt]
F^{(A)}_{n}(x)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,e^{(2n-1)x}\sum_{k=0}^{2n-1}\Big\langle{2n\atop k}\Big\rangle(-1)^{k}e^{-2kx}.\end{cases}

\text{Using}\qquad\frac{\Im\!\bigl(\operatorname{Li}_{-m}(iz)\bigr)}{z}=\frac{1}{(1+z^{2})^{m+1}}\sum_{r=0}^{m}\Bigl\langle{m\atop r}\Bigr\rangle^{B}(-z^{2})^{r},\qquad\operatorname{Li}_{-m}(z)=\frac{z}{(1-z)^{m+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\Bigl\langle{m\atop k}\Bigr\rangle z^{k},

(proven in
[
12
]
and
lemma

2.3
) substitute
m=2n-1
with
z=e^{-x}
for the
B
-case, and
m=2n
with
z=-e^{-2x}
for the
A
-case. After inserting the resulting sums into the definitions of
F_{n}^{(B)}
and
F_{n}^{(A)}
, and simplifying with
1+e^{-2x}=2e^{-x}\cosh x
, we obtain

\begin{cases}F_{n}^{(B)}(x)=2^{\,2n-1}\,\cosh^{2n}(x)\,\Im\!\Bigl(\operatorname{Li}_{-(2n-1)}\!\bigl(i\,e^{-x}\bigr)\Bigr),\\[6.0pt]
F_{n}^{(A)}(x)=-\,2^{\,2n}\,\cosh^{2n+1}(x)\,\operatorname{Li}_{-2n}\!\bigl(-e^{-2x}\bigr).\end{cases}

We recall that, for
x>0
(so that
|e^{-x}|<1
), the polylogarithm admits the absolutely convergent series

\operatorname{Li}_{-k}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{k}\,z^{j}\qquad(k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\ |z|<1).

Applying this to the arguments occurring in
F_{n}^{(B)}
and
F_{n}^{(A)}
yields the series representations

\operatorname{Li}_{-(2n-1)}\!\bigl(ie^{-x}\bigr)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{2n-1}\,i^{\,j}\,e^{-jx},\qquad\operatorname{Li}_{-2n}\!\bigl(-e^{-2x}\bigr)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{2n}\,(-1)^{j}\,e^{-2jx}.

Hence,

\begin{cases}F_{n}^{(B)}(x)=2^{\,2n-1}\,\cosh^{2n}(x)\,\Im\!\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{2n-1}\,i^{\,j}\,e^{-jx}\right),\\
\vskip 2.84544pt\\
F_{n}^{(A)}(x)=-\,2^{\,2n}\,\cosh^{2n+1}(x)\,\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{2n}\,(-1)^{j}\,e^{-2jx}\right).\end{cases}

Since

F^{(B)}_{n}(x)=\sum_{m\geq 0}C^{(B)}_{n,m}\,x^{2m+1},\qquad F^{(A)}_{n}(x)=\sum_{m\geq 0}C^{(A)}_{n,m}\,x^{2m+1},

the linear coefficients satisfy
C^{(B)}_{n,0}=\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dx}F^{(B)}_{n}(x)\right|_{x=0},\qquad C^{(A)}_{n,0}=\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dx}F^{(A)}_{n}(x)\right|_{x=0}.

In terms of the above series, this can be written (structurally) as

\begin{cases}C^{(B)}_{n,0}=2^{\,2n-1}\,\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dx}\Biggl[\cosh^{2n}(x)\,\Im\!\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{2n-1}\,i^{\,j}\,e^{-jx}\Bigr)\Biggr]\right|_{x=0},\\
\vskip 2.84544pt\\
C^{(A)}_{n,0}=-\,2^{\,2n}\,\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dx}\Biggl[\cosh^{2n+1}(x)\,\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{2n}\,(-1)^{j}\,e^{-2jx}\Biggr]\right|_{x=0}.\end{cases}

We differentiate termwise. Using that
\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\cosh^{k}(x)\big|_{x=0}=0
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we obtain

\begin{cases}C^{(B)}_{n,0}=2^{\,2n-1}\,\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dx}\Im\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{2n-1}\,i^{\,j}\,e^{-jx}\right)\right|_{x=0}=-\,2^{\,2n-1}\,\Im\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{2n}\,i^{\,j}\right),\\
\vskip 2.84544pt\\
C^{(A)}_{n,0}=-\,2^{\,2n}\,\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dx}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j^{2n}\,(-1)^{j}\,e^{-2jx}\right)\right|_{x=0}=2^{\,2n+1}\,\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}\,j^{2n+1}.\end{cases}

Finally, evaluating the imaginary part via the cycle
i^{j}\in\{1,i,-1,-i\}
gives the purely real series forms

\begin{cases}C^{(B)}_{n,0}=2^{\,2n-1}\,\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Bigl((4k+3)^{2n}-(4k+1)^{2n}\Bigr),\\
\vskip 2.84544pt\\
C^{(A)}_{n,0}=2^{\,2n+1}\,\displaystyle\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(2m)^{2n+1}-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2m+1)^{2n+1}\right).\end{cases}

We rewrite the two series in terms of classical Dirichlet series (understood via analytic continuation at negative
integers). For the type
B
case, observe that

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Bigl((4k+3)^{2n}-(4k+1)^{2n}\Bigr)=-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}(2k+1)^{2n}.

Recalling the Dirichlet beta function

\beta(s):=\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(2k+1)^{s}},

we obtain

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Bigl((4k+3)^{2n}-(4k+1)^{2n}\Bigr)=-\beta(-2n),

and hence

C^{(B)}_{n,0}=-2^{\,2n-1}\,\beta(-2n).

For the type
A
case, introduce the Dirichlet eta function
\eta(s):=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j-1}}{j^{s}}.

Then

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}j^{2n+1}=-\eta\bigl(-(2n+1)\bigr),

so that

C^{(A)}_{n,0}=-2^{\,2n+1}\,\eta\bigl(-(2n+1)\bigr).

Using
\eta(s)=(1-2^{1-s})\zeta(s)
, we may equivalently write

C^{(A)}_{n,0}=-2^{\,2n+1}\bigl(1-2^{2n+2}\bigr)\,\zeta\bigl(-(2n+1)\bigr).

In summary,

\begin{cases}C^{(B)}_{n,0}=-2^{\,2n-1}\,\beta(-2n),\\
\vskip 2.84544pt\\
C^{(A)}_{n,0}=-2^{\,2n+1}\bigl(1-2^{2n+2}\bigr)\,\zeta\bigl(-(2n+1)\bigr).\end{cases}

We evaluate the special values via analytic continuation.
Recall that

\zeta(-m)=-\frac{B_{m+1}}{m+1}\qquad(m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}),

where
B_{k}
denotes the Bernoulli numbers, and that the Dirichlet beta function satisfies

\beta(-2n)=\frac{E_{2n}}{2}\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}),

where
E_{2n}
are the Euler (secant) numbers. Therefore,

\begin{cases}C^{(B)}_{n,0}=-2^{\,2n-2}\,E_{2n},\\[10.0pt]
\hskip 2.84544pt\\
C^{(A)}_{n,0}=-\,\dfrac{2^{\,2n+1}\bigl(2^{2n+2}-1\bigr)}{2n+2}\,B_{2n+2}.\end{cases}

This concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem 2.5

(Smallest zeros from endpoint ratios at a left endpoint
a
)
.

Let
(P_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of real polynomials.
Fix a real number
a
. Assume that:

1.

\deg P_{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\+\infty
,

2.

P_{n}
has only real zeros, counted with multiplicity, all contained in the open interval
(a,+\infty)
.
Denote them by:

a<r_{n,1}\leq r_{n,2}\leq\cdots\leq r_{n,\deg P_{n}}.

3.

Writing
\operatorname{lc}(P_{n})
for the leading coefficient of
P_{n}
, the
normalized endpoint ratio
satisfies

\left|\frac{P_{n}(a)}{\operatorname{lc}(P_{n})}\right|^{1/\deg P_{n}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Then the smallest zero of
P_{n}
converges to
a
; more precisely,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}r_{n,1}=a.

Moreover, for every
n
one has the quantitative bound :

\displaystyle r_{n,1}-a\leq\left|\frac{P_{n}(a)}{\operatorname{lc}(P_{n})}\right|^{1/\deg P_{n}}.

Proof.

Since all zeros are real and lie in
(a,+\infty)
, we may factor

P_{n}(x)=\operatorname{lc}(P_{n})\prod_{j=1}^{\deg P_{n}}(x-r_{n,j}).

Evaluating at
x=a
gives

\frac{P_{n}(a)}{\operatorname{lc}(P_{n})}=\prod_{j=1}^{\deg P_{n}}(a-r_{n,j})=(-1)^{\deg P_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{\deg P_{n}}(r_{n,j}-a).

Taking absolute values,

\left|\frac{P_{n}(a)}{\operatorname{lc}(P_{n})}\right|=\prod_{j=1}^{\deg P_{n}}(r_{n,j}-a).

All factors are positive since
a<r_{n,j}\quad\forall j\in\left\{1,2,\cdots,\deg P_{n}\right\}
, hence

\prod_{j=1}^{\deg P_{n}}(r_{n,j}-a)\ \geq\ \bigl(\min_{1\leq j\leq\deg P_{n}}(r_{n,j}-a)\bigr)^{\deg P_{n}}=(r_{n,1}-a)^{\deg P_{n}}.

Therefore,

r_{n,1}-a\leq\left(\prod_{j=1}^{\deg P_{n}}(r_{n,j}-a)\right)^{1/\deg P_{n}}=\left|\frac{P_{n}(a)}{\operatorname{lc}(P_{n})}\right|^{1/\deg P_{n}}.

By assumption the right-hand side tends to
0
, hence
r_{n,1}\to a
.
The displayed inequality also yields the quantitative bound.
∎

Theorem 2.6

(Largest zeros from endpoint ratios)
.

Let
(P_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of real polynomials. Fix an arbitrary real number
a
.
Assume that:

1.

\deg P_{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\+\infty

2.

P_{n}
has only real zeros, counted with multiplicity, all contained in the open interval
(-\infty,a)
.
Denote them by:

r_{n,1}\leq r_{n,2}\leq\cdots\leq r_{n,\deg P_{n}}<a.

3.

Writing
\operatorname{lc}(P_{n})
for the leading coefficient of
P_{n}
, the
normalized endpoint ratio
satisfies

\left|\frac{P_{n}(a)}{\operatorname{lc}(P_{n})}\right|^{1/\deg P_{n}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Then the largest zero of
P_{n}
converges to
a
; more precisely,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}r_{n,\deg P_{n}}=a.

Moreover, for every
n
one has the quantitative bound:

\displaystyle a-r_{n,\deg P_{n}}\leq\left|\frac{P_{n}(a)}{\operatorname{lc}(P_{n})}\right|^{1/\deg P_{n}}.

Proof.

Since all zeros lie in
(-\infty,a)
, the polynomial
P_{n}
admits the factorization

P_{n}(x)=\operatorname{lc}(P_{n})\prod_{j=1}^{\deg P_{n}}(x-r_{n,j}).

Evaluating at
x=a
yields

\frac{P_{n}(a)}{\operatorname{lc}(P_{n})}=\prod_{j=1}^{\deg P_{n}}(a-r_{n,j}).

All factors are positive since
r_{n,j}<a\quad\forall j\in\left\{1,2,\cdots,\deg P_{n}\right\}
. Hence

\prod_{j=1}^{\deg P_{n}}(a-r_{n,j})\;\geq\;\bigl(\min_{1\leq j\leq\deg P_{n}}(a-r_{n,j})\bigr)^{\deg P_{n}}=(a-r_{n,\deg P_{n}})^{\deg P_{n}},

and therefore

a-r_{n,\deg P_{n}}\leq\left(\prod_{j=1}^{\deg P_{n}}(a-r_{n,j})\right)^{1/\deg P_{n}}=\left|\frac{P_{n}(a)}{\operatorname{lc}(P_{n})}\right|^{1/\deg P_{n}}.

By assumption, the right-hand side tends to
0
, so
a-r_{n,\deg P_{n}}\to 0
, i.e.
r_{n,\deg P_{n}}\to a
. The displayed inequalities also prove the stated quantitative bounds. This completes the proof.
∎

(8)

Proposition 3.2

(Expression depending on Eulerian polynomials)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}

We first rewrite
\Xi_{n}

E_{n,k}\!\left(\frac{1-x}{1+x}\right)=\frac{2^{2n-1}}{(1+x)^{2n-1}}\,x^{k}\bigl(1-x^{2n-2k-1}\bigr).

This gives

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\Xi_{n}\!\left(\frac{1-x}{1+x}\right)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{2n}(2n-1)!\,(1-x)(1+x)^{2n-2}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left\langle{2n-1\atop k}\right\rangle^{B}(-1)^{k}x^{k}\bigl(1-x^{2n-2k-1}\bigr),\\[11.99998pt]
\displaystyle\Lambda_{n}\!\left(\frac{1-x}{1+x}\right)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}2^{2n-1}}{(2^{2n+1}-1)(2n)!\,(1-x)(1+x)^{2n-2}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left\langle{2n\atop k}\right\rangle(-1)^{k}x^{k}\bigl(1-x^{2n-2k-1}\bigr).\end{cases}

Using

x^{k}\bigl(1-x^{2n-2k-1}\bigr)=x^{k}-x^{2n-1-k},

we split each finite sum into two parts and then re-index the second half
via
j=2n-1-k
, exactly as in the proof of
theorem

3.1
.
Employing the Eulerian symmetries

\left\langle{m\atop r}\right\rangle=\left\langle{m\atop m-1-r}\right\rangle,\qquad\left\langle{m\atop r}\right\rangle^{B}=\left\langle{m\atop m-r}\right\rangle^{B},

we obtain the compact form

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\Xi_{n}\!\left(\frac{1-x}{1+x}\right)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{2n}(2n-1)!\,(1-x)(1+x)^{2n-2}}\sum_{k=0}^{2n-1}\left\langle{2n-1\atop k}\right\rangle^{B}(-1)^{k}x^{k},\\[11.99998pt]
\displaystyle\Lambda_{n}\!\left(\frac{1-x}{1+x}\right)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}2^{2n-1}}{(2^{2n+1}-1)(2n)!\,(1-x)(1+x)^{2n-2}}\sum_{k=0}^{2n-1}\left\langle{2n\atop k}\right\rangle(-1)^{k}x^{k}.\end{cases}

By the definition of the Eulerian polynomials, this becomes

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\Xi_{n}\!\left(\frac{1-x}{1+x}\right)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{2n}(2n-1)!\,(1-x)(1+x)^{2n-2}}\,B_{2n-1}(-x),\\[11.99998pt]
\displaystyle\Lambda_{n}\!\left(\frac{1-x}{1+x}\right)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}2^{2n-1}}{(2^{2n+1}-1)(2n)!\,(1-x)(1+x)^{2n-2}}\,A_{2n}(-x).\end{cases}

Substituting
x\to(1-x)/(1+x)
, we arrive at

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\Xi_{n}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{4n-1}(2n-1)!}\,\frac{(1+x)^{2n-1}}{x}\,B_{2n-1}\!\left(-\,\dfrac{1-x}{1+x}\right),\\[11.99998pt]
\displaystyle\Lambda_{n}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{(2^{2n+1}-1)(2n)!}\,\frac{(1+x)^{2n-1}}{x}\,A_{2n}\!\left(-\,\dfrac{1-x}{1+x}\right).\end{cases}

This completes the proof.
∎

§4. Algebraic and analytic structure of the polynomials

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and define
\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,t}
and
\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,t}
as in
theorem

3.1
. The leading coefficients of the even polynomials
\Xi_{n},\Lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
satisfy

[x^{2n-2}]\,\Xi_{n}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{2n}},\qquad[x^{2n-2}]\,\Lambda_{n}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{2n+1}-1}.

Equivalently,

\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,n-1}=2^{2n-2}(2n-1)!,\qquad\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,n-1}=\frac{(2n)!}{2}.

Proof.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
0\leq k\leq n-1
.
Recall from
lemma

2.2
that

E_{n,k}(x)=(1-x^{2})^{k}\Bigl((1+x)^{2n-2k-1}-(1-x)^{2n-2k-1}\Bigr),

and that
E_{n,k}
is an odd polynomial of degree
2n-1
admitting the representation

E_{n,k}(x)=2x\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}\binom{k}{i}\binom{2n-2k-1}{2t-2i+1}\right)x^{2t}.

On the one hand, the leading term of
(1-x^{2})^{k}
is
(-1)^{k}x^{2k}
, while the leading term of

(1+x)^{2n-2k-1}-(1-x)^{2n-2k-1}
is
2x^{2n-2k-1}
. Hence

[x^{2n-1}]\,E_{n,k}(x)=2(-1)^{k}.

On the other hand, in the above series representation the monomial
x^{2n-1}
arises only from the index

t=n-1
, so that

[x^{2n-1}]\,E_{n,k}(x)=2\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}\binom{k}{i}\binom{2n-2k-1}{2n-2i-1}.

Comparing the two expressions yields the identity

\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}\binom{k}{i}\binom{2n-2k-1}{2n-2i-1}=(-1)^{k}.

(17)

Now set
t=n-1
in the definitions of
\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,t}
and
\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,t}
from

theorem

3.1
and apply (
17
). This gives

\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,n-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left\langle\begin{matrix}2n-1\\
k\end{matrix}\right\rangle^{B}(-1)^{k}\cdot(-1)^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left\langle\begin{matrix}2n-1\\
k\end{matrix}\right\rangle^{B},

and similarly

\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,n-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left\langle\begin{matrix}2n\\
k\end{matrix}\right\rangle.

Using the palindromicity (symmetry) of Eulerian numbers (type
A
and type
B
)
[
5
]
,

\left\langle\begin{matrix}m\\
k\end{matrix}\right\rangle=\left\langle\begin{matrix}m\\
m-1-k\end{matrix}\right\rangle,\qquad\left\langle\begin{matrix}m\\
k\end{matrix}\right\rangle^{B}=\left\langle\begin{matrix}m\\
m-k\end{matrix}\right\rangle^{B},

the partial sums up to the midpoint equal one half of the total sums. Since

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left\langle\begin{matrix}n\\
k\end{matrix}\right\rangle=n!,\qquad\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left\langle\begin{matrix}n\\
k\end{matrix}\right\rangle^{B}=2^{n}n!,

we obtain

\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,n-1}=\frac{(2n)!}{2},\qquad\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,n-1}=2^{2n-2}(2n-1)!.

Finally, taking the coefficient of
x^{2n-2}
in the explicit expressions for
\Xi_{n}
and
\Lambda_{n}

from
theorem

3.1
yields

[x^{2n-2}]\,\Xi_{n}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{4n-2}(2n-1)!}\,\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,n-1}=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{2n}},

and

[x^{2n-2}]\,\Lambda_{n}(x)=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{(2^{2n+1}-1)(2n)!}\,\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,n-1}=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{2n+1}-1},

as claimed.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the polynomials
\Xi_{n}
and
\Lambda_{n}
from
theorem

3.1

have degree exactly
2n-2
.

Proof.

By
theorem

3.1
, both
\Xi_{n}
and
\Lambda_{n}
are even polynomials of degree at most
2n-2
.
Moreover, by
proposition

4.1
their leading coefficients are

∎

Proposition 4.3

(Values at
x=0
)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

\Xi_{n}(0)=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2^{2n}(2n-1)!}\,E_{2n},\qquad\Lambda_{n}(0)=\frac{(-1)^{n}\,2^{2n+2}\bigl(2^{2n+2}-1\bigr)}{(2^{2n+1}-1)(2n+2)(2n)!}\,B_{2n+2},

where
E_{2n}
are the Euler (secant) numbers and
B_{2n+2}
are the Bernoulli numbers.

Proof.

Evaluating (
8
) at
x=0
leaves only the term
t=0
, hence

\Xi_{n}(0)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{4n-2}(2n-1)!}\,\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,0},\qquad\Lambda_{n}(0)=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{(2^{2n+1}-1)(2n)!}\,\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,0}.

By definition, for
t=0
we have

\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,0}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left\langle{2n-1\atop k}\right\rangle^{B}(-1)^{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}\binom{k}{i}\binom{2n-2k-1}{\,1-2i\,},

\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,0}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left\langle{2n\atop k}\right\rangle(-1)^{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}\binom{k}{i}\binom{2n-2k-1}{\,1-2i\,}.

Since
\binom{2n-2k-1}{\,1-2i\,}=0
for all
i\geq 1
(negative lower index), only the term
i=0
contributes, and thus

\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}\binom{k}{i}\binom{2n-2k-1}{\,1-2i\,}=\binom{2n-2k-1}{1}=2n-2k-1.

Therefore,

\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,0}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left\langle{2n-1\atop k}\right\rangle^{B}(-1)^{k}\,(2n-2k-1),\qquad\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,0}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left\langle{2n\atop k}\right\rangle(-1)^{k}\,(2n-2k-1).

By
lemma

2.4
, these alternating Eulerian sums evaluate to

\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,0}=-2^{2n-2}E_{2n},\qquad\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,0}=-\frac{2^{2n+1}\bigl(2^{2n+2}-1\bigr)}{2n+2}\,B_{2n+2}.

Substituting into the expressions for
\Xi_{n}(0)
and
\Lambda_{n}(0)
yields

\Xi_{n}(0)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{4n-2}(2n-1)!}\,\bigl(-2^{2n-2}E_{2n}\bigr)=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2^{2n}(2n-1)!}\,E_{2n},

and

\Lambda_{n}(0)=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{(2^{2n+1}-1)(2n)!}\,\left(-\frac{2^{2n+1}\bigl(2^{2n+2}-1\bigr)}{2n+2}\,B_{2n+2}\right)=\frac{(-1)^{n}\,2^{2n+2}\bigl(2^{2n+2}-1\bigr)}{(2^{2n+1}-1)(2n+2)(2n)!}\,B_{2n+2}.

This proves the claim.
∎

Proposition 4.4

(Values at
x=1
)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

\Xi_{n}(1)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{2n}(2n-1)!},\qquad\Lambda_{n}(1)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}\,2^{2n-1}}{(2^{2n+1}-1)(2n)!}.

Proof.

Evaluating (
8
) at
x=1
gives

\Xi_{n}(1)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{4n-2}(2n-1)!}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,t},\qquad\Lambda_{n}(1)=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{(2^{2n+1}-1)(2n)!}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,t}.

To evaluate the sums over
t
, we use
lemma

2.2
. Indeed,
lemma

2.2
states that for

0\leq k\leq n-1
the odd polynomial

E_{n,k}(x):=(1-x^{2})^{k}\Big((1+x)^{2n-2k-1}-(1-x)^{2n-2k-1}\Big)

admits the representation

E_{n,k}(x)=2x\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}\binom{k}{i}\binom{2n-2k-1}{\,2t-2i+1\,}\right)x^{2t}.

Setting
x=1
and noting that

E_{n,k}(1)=(1-1^{2})^{k}\Big(2^{\,2n-2k-1}-0\Big)=\begin{cases}2^{\,2n-1},&k=0,\\
0,&1\leq k\leq n-1,\end{cases}

we obtain

\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}\binom{k}{i}\binom{2n-2k-1}{\,2t-2i+1\,}\right)=\frac{E_{n,k}(1)}{2}.

Consequently,

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,t}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left\langle{2n-1\atop k}\right\rangle^{B}(-1)^{k}\cdot\frac{E_{n,k}(1)}{2}=\left\langle{2n-1\atop 0}\right\rangle^{B}\cdot\frac{2^{2n-1}}{2}=2^{2n-2},\\
\vskip 2.84544pt\\
\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,t}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left\langle{2n\atop k}\right\rangle(-1)^{k}\cdot\frac{E_{n,k}(1)}{2}=\left\langle{2n\atop 0}\right\rangle\cdot\frac{2^{2n-1}}{2}=2^{2n-2}.\end{cases}

Substituting these evaluations into the formulas for
\Xi_{n}(1)
and
\Lambda_{n}(1)
yields

\begin{cases}\Xi_{n}(1)=\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{4n-2}(2n-1)!}\,2^{2n-2}=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{2n}(2n-1)!},\\
\vskip 2.84544pt\\
\Lambda_{n}(1)=\displaystyle\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{(2^{2n+1}-1)(2n)!}\,2^{2n-2}=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}\,2^{2n-1}}{(2^{2n+1}-1)(2n)!}.\end{cases}

This proves the claim.
∎

Theorem 4.5

(No real zeros for
x>1
)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\Xi_{n},\Lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
be the even polynomials defined in
theorem

3.1
. Then, for every
x>1
,

(-1)^{n+1}\Xi_{n}(x)>0\qquad\text{and}\qquad(-1)^{n+1}\Lambda_{n}(x)>0.

In particular,
\Xi_{n}(x)\neq 0
and
\Lambda_{n}(x)\neq 0
for all
x>1
.

Proof.

Fix
x>1
and recall from
theorem

3.1
that

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\Xi_{n}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{4n-2}(2n-1)!}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,t}\,x^{2t},\\
\vskip 2.84544pt\\
\displaystyle\Lambda_{n}(x)=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{(2^{2n+1}-1)(2n)!}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,t}\,x^{2t}.\end{cases}

(18)

For
0\leq k\leq n-1
set
M:=2n-2k-1
and define

E_{n,k}(x):=(1-x^{2})^{k}\Big((1+x)^{M}-(1-x)^{M}\Big).

(19)

By
lemma

2.2
we have

E_{n,k}(x)=2x\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}\binom{k}{i}\binom{M}{\,2t-2i+1\,}\right)x^{2t}.

(20)

Dividing by
2x
yields

\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}\binom{k}{i}\binom{M}{\,2t-2i+1\,}\right)x^{2t}=\frac{E_{n,k}(x)}{2x}.

(21)

Using the definition of
\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,t}
from
theorem

3.1
and exchanging finite
sums, (
21
) implies

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,t}\,x^{2t}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left\langle{2n-1\atop k}\right\rangle^{B}(-1)^{k}\,\frac{E_{n,k}(x)}{2x}\\
\vskip 2.84544pt\\
\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,t}\,x^{2t}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left\langle{2n\atop k}\right\rangle(-1)^{k}\,\frac{E_{n,k}(x)}{2x}.\end{cases}

(22)

We now show that each summand on the right-hand sides of (
22
) is strictly
positive for
x>1
. Since
x>1
, we have
1-x^{2}<0
and thus

(1-x^{2})^{k}=(-1)^{k}(x^{2}-1)^{k}.

(23)

Moreover,
1+x>0
and
1-x<0
, and since
M
is odd we have
(1-x)^{M}<0
. Therefore,

(1+x)^{M}-(1-x)^{M}=(1+x)^{M}+|1-x|^{M}>0.

(24)

Combining (
19
), (
23
), and (
24
) yields

(-1)^{k}E_{n,k}(x)=(x^{2}-1)^{k}\Big((1+x)^{M}-(1-x)^{M}\Big)>0.

(25)

Since
\frac{1}{2x}>0
for
x>1
and all Eulerian numbers of type
A
and type
B
are positive, it follows from
(
22
) and (
25
) that

\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,t}\,x^{2t}>0,\qquad\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,t}\,x^{2t}>0\qquad(x>1).

(26)

Finally, inserting (
26
) into (
18
) (whose prefactors have
positive absolute value) gives

(-1)^{n+1}\Xi_{n}(x)>0,\qquad(-1)^{n+1}\Lambda_{n}(x)>0\qquad(x>1),

which completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 4.6
.

Under the assumptions of

4.5
, the polynomials
\Xi_{n}
and
\Lambda_{n}
have no real zeros

. In particular, they have no real zeros with

shows that

.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
n\geq 2
. All zeros of
\Lambda_{n}(x)
and
\Xi_{n}(x)
are real, simple and interlace with those of
\Lambda_{n+1}(x)
and
\Xi_{n+1}(x)
respectively.

Proof.

By
proposition

3.2
we have, with

u=\dfrac{1-x}{1+x},

explicit representations

\Lambda_{n}(x)=c_{n}\,\frac{(1+x)^{2n-1}}{x}\,A_{2n}(-u),\qquad\Xi_{n}(x)=d_{n}\,\frac{(1+x)^{2n-1}}{x}\,B_{2n-1}(-u),

for some nonzero constants
c_{n},d_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}
. It is classical that the Eulerian
polynomials
A_{n}(t)
have only real zeros

[
4
,
6
,
9
]
,
and that the type B Eulerian polynomials
B_{n}(t)
have only real zeros as well

[
2
,
9
]
. Chak-On Chow

[
3
]
moreover proved once again that all these zeros are

simple
and
negative
. Consequently,
A_{2n}(-u)
and
B_{2n-1}(-u)

have only real and simple zeros in the variable
u
.

Palindromicity implies
A_{2n}(-1)=0
and
B_{2n-1}(-1)=0
, hence
u=1
is a zero
in both cases. Therefore the quotients

\widetilde{A}_{2n}(u):=\frac{A_{2n}(-u)}{1-u},\qquad\widetilde{B}_{2n-1}(u):=\frac{B_{2n-1}(-u)}{1-u}

are polynomials, and dividing by
1-u
removes only the real zero
u=1
and does
not create nonreal zeros. Using
1-u=1-\dfrac{1-x}{1+x}=\dfrac{2x}{1+x}
, we may
rewrite the above formulas as

\Lambda_{n}(x)=\tilde{c}_{n}\,(1+x)^{2n-2}\,\widetilde{A}_{2n}(u),\qquad\Xi_{n}(x)=\tilde{d}_{n}\,(1+x)^{2n-2}\,\widetilde{B}_{2n-1}(u),

for suitable nonzero constants
\tilde{c}_{n},\tilde{d}_{n}
. The factor

(1+x)^{2n-2}
has only the real zero
x=-1
. In
proposition

4.3
we
computed the values of
\Lambda_{n}
and
\Xi_{n}
and in particular showed that
neither polynomial ever vanishes at
x=-1
. Thus the possible zero
x=-1

coming from
(1+x)^{2n-2}
does not occur, and all zeros of
\Lambda_{n}
and

\Xi_{n}
arise from the factors
\widetilde{A}_{2n}(u)
and
\widetilde{B}_{2n-1}(u)
.

Solving
u=\dfrac{1-x}{1+x}
for
x
gives
x=\dfrac{1-u}{1+u}
. The map

u\mapsto x
is a strictly decreasing homeomorphism

\mathbb{R}\setminus\{-1\}\to\mathbb{R}\setminus\{-1\}
, so real zeros

u\neq-1
correspond bijectively to real zeros
x
, and multiplicities are
preserved. Since all zeros of
\widetilde{A}_{2n}
and
\widetilde{B}_{2n-1}
are
real and simple, all zeros of
\Lambda_{n}
and
\Xi_{n}
are real and simple as
well.

Chow
[
3
]
also proved that the Eulerian
polynomials of type A and type B have the
interlacing property
: the zeros
of
A_{2n}(t)
and
A_{2n+2}(t)
interlace, and the same holds for

B_{2n-1}(t)
and
B_{2n+1}(t)
. The same is therefore true for

A_{2n}(-u),A_{2n+2}(-u)
and for
B_{2n-1}(-u),B_{2n+1}(-u)
, and removing the
common simple zero
u=1
from each pair (passing to

\widetilde{A}_{2n},\widetilde{A}_{2n+2}
and

\widetilde{B}_{2n-1},\widetilde{B}_{2n+1}
) preserves interlacing. Since the
transform
u\leftrightarrow x=\dfrac{1-u}{1+u}
is strictly monotone, it
preserves the relative order of real zeros, and hence the interlacing property
is transported from the Eulerian polynomials to
\Lambda_{n},\Lambda_{n+1}
and
to
\Xi_{n},\Xi_{n+1}
. This shows that all zeros of
\Lambda_{n}
and
\Xi_{n}
are
real, simple, and interlace as claimed.
∎

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The polynomials
\Lambda_{n}(\sqrt{x})
and
\Xi_{n}(\sqrt{x})
have alternating signs.

Proof.

Define the associated (adapted) polynomials

so that
\Lambda_{n}(x)=\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}(x^{2})
and

\Xi_{n}(x)=\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}(x^{2})
.

Since
\Lambda_{n}
and
\Xi_{n}
are even and real-rooted, their zeros occur in
pairs
\pm\alpha
(with multiplicity). Hence the zeros of

\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}
and
\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}
are exactly the squares of the
nonzero zeros of
\Lambda_{n}
and
\Xi_{n}
, respectively, and are therefore real
and nonnegative. Moreover, by
proposition

4.3
we have

\Lambda_{n}(0)\neq 0
and
\Xi_{n}(0)\neq 0
, so
0
is not a zero of either
polynomial. Consequently, all zeros of
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}
and

\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}
are in fact strictly positive.

Therefore we may write

\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}(y)=a_{n-1}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(y-\rho_{j}),\qquad\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}(y)=b_{n-1}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(y-\sigma_{j}),

with
\rho_{j}>0
and
\sigma_{j}>0
. Expanding yields

\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}(y)=a_{n-1}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{n-1-t}\,e_{n-1-t}(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{n-1})\,y^{t},

\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}(y)=b_{n-1}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{n-1-t}\,e_{n-1-t}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n-1})\,y^{t},

where
e_{k}
denotes the
k
-th elementary symmetric polynomial
[
11
]
. Since

\rho_{j},\sigma_{j}>0
, all these elementary symmetric polynomials are strictly
positive, which gives

(-1)^{n-1-t}\,\frac{a_{t}}{a_{n-1}}>0,\qquad(-1)^{n-1-t}\,\frac{b_{t}}{b_{n-1}}>0\quad(t=0,1,\dots,n-1).

This is precisely the alternating-sign property for the coefficients of

\Lambda_{n}
and
\Xi_{n}
.
∎

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
\Xi_{n}
and
\Lambda_{n}
be the even polynomials defined in

theorem

3.1
, i.e.

\Xi_{n}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{4n-2}(2n-1)!}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,t}\,x^{2t},\qquad\Lambda_{n}(x)=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{(2^{2n+1}-1)(2n)!}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,t}\,x^{2t}.

Then the sequences
\bigl(|\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,t}|\bigr)_{t=0}^{n-1},\qquad\bigl(|\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,t}|\bigr)_{t=0}^{n-1}
are log-concave and hence unimodal.

Proof.

By
theorem

4.7
, the polynomials
\Xi_{n}
and
\Lambda_{n}

have only real, simple zeros and are even. Moreover,

\Xi_{n}(0)\neq 0
and
\Lambda_{n}(0)\neq 0
by
proposition

4.3
.
Hence the zeros of
\Xi_{n}
and
\Lambda_{n}
occur in symmetric pairs

\pm\alpha_{1},\dots,\pm\alpha_{n-1}
with
\alpha_{j}>0
.

Define the adapted polynomials

\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}(y):=\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,t}\,y^{t},\qquad\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}(y):=\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,t}\,y^{t}.

Then

\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}(y)=\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,n-1}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(y-\alpha_{j}^{2}),\qquad\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}(y)=\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,n-1}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(y-\alpha_{j}^{2}),

and therefore all zeros of
\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}
and
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}

are real and strictly positive. Expanding the products yields

\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}(y)=\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,n-1}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{\,n-1-t}\,e_{n-1-t}(\alpha_{1}^{2},\dots,\alpha_{n-1}^{2})\,y^{t},

and analogously for
\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}
.
Consequently,

|\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,t}|=|\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,n-1}|\,e_{n-1-t}(\alpha_{1}^{2},\dots,\alpha_{n-1}^{2}),\qquad|\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,t}|=|\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,n-1}|\,e_{n-1-t}(\alpha_{1}^{2},\dots,\alpha_{n-1}^{2}).

Since the elementary symmetric polynomials of positive arguments form a
log-concave sequence by Newton’s inequalities (equivalently, Maclaurin’s
inequalities), it follows that the sequences

(|\mathcal{C}^{(A)}_{n,t}|)_{t=0}^{n-1}
and

(|\mathcal{C}^{(B)}_{n,t}|)_{t=0}^{n-1}
are log-concave.
Log-concavity implies unimodality, which completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
n\geq 2
and let the even polynomials
\Xi_{n},\Lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
be
defined as in
theorem

3.1
. Then, as
n\to\infty
,

\Xi_{n}\to 0\quad\text{uniformly on }(0,1),\qquad\Lambda_{n}\to 0\quad\text{uniformly on }(0,1).

Proof.

By
theorem

4.7
, all zeros of
\Xi_{n}
and
\Lambda_{n}
are
real. By
corollary

4.6
, these zeros are in fact contained in

(-1,1)
, and by
proposition

4.3
neither polynomial vanishes at

x=0
. Since
\Xi_{n}
and
\Lambda_{n}
are even, their zeros therefore occur in
symmetric pairs
\pm\alpha
with
0<\alpha<1
.

Define the adapted polynomials

\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}(y):=\Xi_{n}(\sqrt{y}),\qquad\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}(y):=\Lambda_{n}(\sqrt{y}),\qquad 0<y<1,

so that
\Xi_{n}(x)=\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}(x^{2})
and
\Lambda_{n}(x)=\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}(x^{2})
.
The zeros of
\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}
and
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}
are the squares
of the positive zeros of
\Xi_{n}
and
\Lambda_{n}
, hence they are all strictly
contained in
(0,1)
. Therefore we can factor

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}(y)=\ell^{(B)}_{n}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(y-\rho^{(B)}_{n,j}),\\[10.00002pt]
\displaystyle\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}(y)=\ell^{(A)}_{n}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(y-\rho^{(A)}_{n,j}),\end{cases}\qquad\text{with }0<\rho^{(B)}_{n,j},\rho^{(A)}_{n,j}<1,

where
\ell^{(B)}_{n}
and
\ell^{(A)}_{n}
denote the leading coefficients of

\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}
and
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}
, respectively (equivalently,
the coefficients of
x^{2n-2}
in
\Xi_{n}
and
\Lambda_{n}
).

For any
y\in[0,1]
and any
\rho\in(0,1)
we have
|y-\rho|\leq 1
. Hence

\begin{cases}\displaystyle|\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}(y)|=|\ell^{(B)}_{n}|\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}|y-\rho^{(B)}_{n,j}|\leq|\ell^{(B)}_{n}|,\\[11.99998pt]
\displaystyle|\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}(y)|=|\ell^{(A)}_{n}|\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}|y-\rho^{(A)}_{n,j}|\leq|\ell^{(A)}_{n}|.\end{cases}

Taking suprema and using
\Xi_{n}(x)=\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}(x^{2})
,

\Lambda_{n}(x)=\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}(x^{2})
yields

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sup_{x\in(0,1)}|\Xi_{n}(x)|=\sup_{y\in(0,1)}|\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}(y)|\leq|\ell^{(B)}_{n}|,\\[11.99998pt]
\displaystyle\sup_{x\in(0,1)}|\Lambda_{n}(x)|=\sup_{y\in(0,1)}|\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}(y)|\leq|\ell^{(A)}_{n}|.\end{cases}

Finally, by
proposition

4.1
we have

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\ell^{(B)}_{n}=[x^{2n-2}]\Xi_{n}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{2n}},\\[11.99998pt]
\displaystyle\ell^{(A)}_{n}=[x^{2n-2}]\Lambda_{n}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{2n+1}-1},\end{cases}

and both tend to
0
as
n\to\infty
. Therefore

\sup_{x\in(0,1)}|\Xi_{n}(x)|\to 0
and
\sup_{x\in(0,1)}|\Lambda_{n}(x)|\to 0
,
i.e.
\Xi_{n}\to 0
and
\Lambda_{n}\to 0
uniformly on
(0,1)
.
∎

Theorem 4.11

(Largest zeros of
\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}
and
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}

.

Let
\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}(y):=\Xi_{n}(\sqrt{y})
and

\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}(y):=\Lambda_{n}(\sqrt{y})
.
Then the largest zero of
\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}
and the largest zero of
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}

both converge to

.

We verify the hypotheses of

theorem

2.6
.
By
theorem

4.7
and
corollary

4.6
,
all zeros of
\Xi_{n}
and
\Lambda_{n}

(-1,1)
.

\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}
and

propositions

4.1
and
4.4

\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}(1),\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}(1),\operatorname{lc}(\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n})

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
(\Lambda_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be the family of polynomials defined as in
theorem

3.1
, and set

\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}(x):=\Lambda_{n}(\sqrt{x}).

For each
n
let
0<\alpha_{n}<1
denote the smallest positive zero of
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}
. Then

\alpha_{n}\longrightarrow 0\qquad(n\to\infty).

Proof.

The polynomial
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}
does not fulfill all requirements of
theorem

2.5
. We provide instead another long proof and split the argument therefore into three steps.

Step 1: Reduction to zeros of Eulerian polynomials.

From the explicit computation in
proposition

3.2
, we may conclude the following identity: there exist nonzero constants
C_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
such that for every
x\in(0,1)

\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}(x)=C_{n}\,\frac{(1+\sqrt{x})^{2n-1}}{\sqrt{x}}\,A_{2n}\!\left(-\,\frac{1-\sqrt{x}}{1+\sqrt{x}}\right),

(27)

where
A_{2n}
is the Eulerian polynomial of type
A
of degree
2n-1
.

For convenience, write

u(x):=\frac{1-\sqrt{x}}{1+\sqrt{x}},\qquad r=-u(x)=-\,\frac{1-\sqrt{x}}{1+\sqrt{x}.}

Then
x\in(0,1)
implies
\sqrt{x}\in(0,1)
and therefore
u(x)\in(0,1)
and

r\in(-1,0).

Conversely, if
r\in(-1,0)
we can solve for
x
:

r=-\,\frac{1-\sqrt{x}}{1+\sqrt{x}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sqrt{x}=\frac{1+r}{1-r}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=\phi(r):=\left(\frac{1+r}{1-r}\right)^{2},

and one checks that
\phi
is a strictly increasing bijection

\displaystyle\phi:(-1,0)\longrightarrow(0,1).

In particular, on
(0,1)
the prefactor

\displaystyle C_{n}\,\frac{(1+\sqrt{x})^{2n-1}}{\sqrt{x}}

never vanishes, so (
27
) shows that

\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}(x)=0\ \ \text{for some }x\in(0,1)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A_{2n}(r)=0\ \ \text{for }r=-u(x)\in(-1,0).

(28)

Equivalently, the map
r\mapsto x=\phi(r)
gives a bijection between

\{\text{zeros of $A_{2n}$ in $(-1,0)$}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\{\text{zeros of $\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}$ in $(0,1)$}\}.

Let us fix
n
and denote by

r_{n,1}<r_{n,2}<\dots<r_{n,m_{n}}<0

the zeros of
A_{2n}
that lie in
(-1,0)
(in increasing order).
By the discussion above, the corresponding zeros of
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}
in
(0,1)
are

x_{n,j}:=\phi(r_{n,j})\in(0,1),\qquad j=1,\dots,m_{n},

and the smallest positive zero
\alpha_{n}
satisfies

\alpha_{n}=\min_{1\leq j\leq m_{n}}x_{n,j}=\min_{1\leq j\leq m_{n}}\phi(r_{n,j})=\phi\!\left(\min_{1\leq j\leq m_{n}}r_{n,j}\right)=\phi(\rho_{n}),

(29)

where
\rho_{n}:=\min\{r\in(-1,0):A_{2n}(r)=0\}
is the leftmost zero of
A_{2n}
in
(-1,0)
.

Thus, to prove
\alpha_{n}\to 0
it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\rho_{n}\longrightarrow-1.

Step 2: Zeros of Eulerian polynomials accumulate at
-1
.

Let
\xi_{m,1},\dots,\xi_{m,m-1}
denote the zeros of
A_{m}
(all real and negative).
Following Melotti
[
7
]
, define the empirical measure

\mu_{m}:=\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\delta_{-\xi_{m,k}}

on
(0,\infty)
(so we look at the positive numbers
-\xi_{m,k}
).

A recent result of Melotti states that the sequence
(\mu_{m})_{m\geq 2}
converges weakly to a probability measure
\mu
on
(0,\infty)
, which is the distribution of
e^{C}
where
C
is a standard Cauchy random variable. In particular, the support of
\mu
is the entire interval
(0,\infty)
and the point
1
belongs to the interior of the support; see Theorem 1.1 in
[
7
]
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and put

I_{\varepsilon}:=(1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)\subset(0,\infty).

Since
1
is in the interior of the support of
\mu
, we have
\mu(I_{\varepsilon})>0
.

By weak convergence
\mu_{m}\Rightarrow\mu
and the Portmanteau theorem, it follows that

\liminf_{m\to\infty}\mu_{m}(I_{\varepsilon})\ \geq\ \mu(I_{\varepsilon})\ >\ 0.

Hence there exists an integer
M(\varepsilon)
such that for all
m\geq M(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\mu_{m}(I_{\varepsilon})>0.

Unpacking the definition of
\mu_{m}
, this means that for each
m\geq M(\varepsilon)
there exists an index
k=k(m)
such that

-\xi_{m,k(m)}\in(1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\xi_{m,k(m)}\in(-1-\varepsilon,-1+\varepsilon).

We now specialise to
m=2n
(even indices) and choose, for each sufficiently large
n
, a root

r_{n}\in(-1-\varepsilon,-1+\varepsilon)

of
A_{2n}
. In particular, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
N(\varepsilon)
such that

n\geq N(\varepsilon)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|r_{n}+1|<\varepsilon.

(30)

Equivalently,
r_{n}\to-1
as
n\to\infty
.

Finally, note that each
r_{n}
is one of the zeros
r_{n,j}
of
A_{2n}
in
(-1,0)
, so by definition of
\rho_{n}
we have

\rho_{n}\leq r_{n}\quad\text{for all large $n$.}

Combining this with (
30
) and the obvious bound
\rho_{n}\geq-1
yields

-1\leq\rho_{n}\leq r_{n}\longrightarrow-1,

and hence

\rho_{n}\longrightarrow-1\qquad(n\to\infty).

(31)

Step 3: Back to
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{n}
.

Recall that
\phi:(-1,0)\to(0,1)
is given by

\phi(r)=\left(\frac{1+r}{1-r}\right)^{2}.

A short computation shows that
\phi
is continuous and strictly increasing, with

\lim_{r\downarrow-1}\phi(r)=0,\qquad\lim_{r\uparrow 0}\phi(r)=1.

By (
29
) we have
\alpha_{n}=\phi(\rho_{n})
and by (
31
) we know that
\rho_{n}\to-1
.
By continuity of
\phi
at
-1
we obtain

\alpha_{n}=\phi(\rho_{n})\longrightarrow\phi(-1)=0.

This completes the proof.
∎

Conjecture 4.13
.

Let
(\Lambda_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be the family of polynomials defined as in
theorem

3.1
, and set

\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}(x):=\Xi_{n}(\sqrt{x}).

For each
n
let
0<\beta_{n}<1
denote the smallest positive zero of
\widetilde{\Xi}_{n}
. Then

\beta_{n}\longrightarrow 0\qquad(n\to\infty).

Proposition 4.14
.

For every integer
n\geq 1
one has

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\Lambda_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx>0,\qquad\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\Xi_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx>0.

Proof.

By using the functions defined in
proposition

3.2
, we may write

\Lambda_{n}(x)=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{(2^{2n+1}-1)(2n)!}\,F_{n}(x),\qquad\Xi_{n}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^{4n-2}(2n-1)!}\,G_{n}(x),

where

F_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{2x}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Big\langle{2n\atop k}\Big\rangle(-1)^{k}E_{n,k}(x),\qquad G_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{2x}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Big\langle{2n-1\atop k}\Big\rangle^{B}(-1)^{k}E_{n,k}(x).

Since the prefactors

\displaystyle\frac{2}{(2^{2n+1}-1)(2n)!},\qquad\frac{1}{2^{4n-2}(2n-1)!}

are positive, it suffices to prove

(-1)^{n+1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{F_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx>0,\qquad(-1)^{n+1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{G_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx>0.

Set

\displaystyle u=\frac{1-x}{1+x},\qquad x=\frac{1-u}{1+u}.

Then
x\in(0,1)
corresponds to
u\in(0,1)
, and one computes

1+x=\frac{2}{1+u},\qquad\sqrt{1-x^{2}}=\frac{2\sqrt{u}}{1+u},\qquad\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}=\frac{du}{(1+u)\sqrt{u}}.

By
proposition

3.2
we have

F_{n}(x)=(1+x)^{2n-2}\frac{A_{2n}(-u)}{1-u},\qquad G_{n}(x)=(1+x)^{2n-2}\frac{B_{2n-1}(-u)}{1-u}.

Substituting the above identities yields

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{F_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx=2^{2n-2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{A_{2n}(-u)}{(1-u)(1+u)^{2n-1}}\,\frac{du}{\sqrt{u}},

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{G_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx=2^{2n-2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{B_{2n-1}(-u)}{(1-u)(1+u)^{2n-1}}\,\frac{du}{\sqrt{u}}.

We now use the classical Eulerian generating function identities (valid for
|t|<1
):

\frac{A_{m}(t)}{(1-t)^{m+1}}=\sum_{r\geq 0}r^{m}t^{r},\qquad\frac{B_{m}(t)}{(1-t)^{m+1}}=\sum_{r\geq 0}(2r+1)^{m}t^{r}.

Setting
t=-u
with
u\in(0,1)
gives

\frac{A_{2n}(-u)}{(1+u)^{2n+1}}=\sum_{r\geq 0}(-1)^{r}r^{2n}u^{r},\qquad\frac{B_{2n-1}(-u)}{(1+u)^{2n}}=\sum_{r\geq 0}(-1)^{r}(2r+1)^{2n-1}u^{r}.

Since

\frac{1}{(1-u)(1+u)^{2n-1}}=\frac{(1+u)^{2}}{1-u}\cdot\frac{1}{(1+u)^{2n+1}},

we obtain

\frac{A_{2n}(-u)}{(1-u)(1+u)^{2n-1}}=\frac{(1+u)^{2}}{1-u}\sum_{r\geq 0}(-1)^{r}r^{2n}u^{r}.

Moreover,

\frac{(1+u)^{2}}{1-u}=(1+2u+u^{2})\sum_{m\geq 0}u^{m}=\sum_{m\geq 0}b_{m}u^{m},

where
b_{0}=1
,
b_{1}=3
, and
b_{m}=4
for
m\geq 2
; in particular
b_{m}>0
for all
m
.

Multiplying the two absolutely convergent power series and separating even and odd indices in the alternating sum, one obtains a power series representation

(-1)^{n+1}\frac{A_{2n}(-u)}{(1-u)(1+u)^{2n-1}}=\sum_{q\geq 0}\alpha_{n,q}u^{q},\qquad\alpha_{n,q}>0\quad(q\geq 0).

An entirely analogous argument yields

(-1)^{n+1}\frac{B_{2n-1}(-u)}{(1-u)(1+u)^{2n-1}}=\sum_{q\geq 0}\beta_{n,q}u^{q},\qquad\beta_{n,q}>0\quad(q\geq 0).

Absolute convergence for
u\in(0,1)
justifies termwise multiplication and rearrangement.

Consequently,

(-1)^{n+1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{F_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx=2^{2n-2}\sum_{q\geq 0}\alpha_{n,q}\int_{0}^{1}u^{q-\frac{1}{2}}\,du.

Since

\int_{0}^{1}u^{q-\frac{1}{2}}\,du=\frac{1}{q+\tfrac{1}{2}}>0

and all
\alpha_{n,q}
are strictly positive, the series is strictly positive. The same reasoning applies to
G_{n}
.

Finally, multiplying by the positive prefactors in the definitions of
\Lambda_{n}
and
\Xi_{n}
gives

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\Lambda_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx>0,\qquad\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\Xi_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\,dx>0.

∎
