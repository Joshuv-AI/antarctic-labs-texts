Diagnostic performance of the Sepsis qPCR MX-30® panel compared with conventional blood culture in pediatric febrile neutropenia: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Fatma Burçin Kurtipek, Ayça Koca Yozgat, Elif Gökçek, Dilek Kaçar, Bedia Dinç, Neşe Yaralı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFebrile neutropenia (FN) is a frequent and potentially life-threatening complication in patients with hematologic malignancies. Prompt and accurate identification of bloodstream pathogens is essential to optimize antimicrobial therapy and improve outcomes. Although conventional blood culture (BC) remains the diagnostic gold standard, its limitations have led to increased interest in rapid molecular diagnostics such as real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR).ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the Sepsis qPCR MX-30® panel in comparison to conventional blood culture in detecting causative pathogens during febrile neutropenia episodes in immunocompromised pediatric patients.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 251 febrile neutropenia episodes in 80 children with hematologic disorders between February 2023 and November 2024. Blood samples for both BC and the Sepsis qPCR MX-30® panel were collected concurrently. The performance metrics of the qPCR panel were assessed by calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), with BC as the reference.ResultsOf the 251 episodes, the qPCR panel detected pathogens in 54 episodes (21.5%), while BC confirmed infection in 45 episodes (17.9%). Overall accuracy was 68.5% (172/251 episodes with agreement on presence or absence of infection). Among the 10 episodes positive by both methods, pathogen-level concordance (same organism detected) was observed in 7 episodes (70%). The calculated sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of the qPCR panel were 22.2%, 78.6%, 18.5%, and 82.2%, respectively. Discordant results were frequent: 44 qPCR-positive cases were BC-negative, and 35 BC-positive cases were not detected by qPCR.Notably, the panel enabled early detection of several antimicrobial resistance genes and fungal pathogens not identified by culture.ConclusionThe Sepsis qPCR MX-30® panel offers a faster turnaround time than conventional blood cultures and may serve as a complementary diagnostic tool for early pathogen detection in febrile neutropenic children. However, due to its limited sensitivity and PPV, it cannot replace BC in clinical practice. Further studies are warranted to optimize assay design and clinical interpretation, especially in the context of PCR-positive but culture-negative findings.Clinical trialNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13352-0.

Introduction

In febrile neutropenic patients, such as those with leukemia, early and accurate diagnosis of sepsis, combined with the prompt initiation of appropriate therapy, is critical for survival [1]. Rapid microbiological identification not only enables timely and targeted antimicrobial treatment but also helps to prevent the emergence of resistant pathogens that may result from the empirical use of broad-spectrum antibiotics [2]. Although conventional blood culture remains the gold standard for identifying the causative pathogen and performing antimicrobial susceptibility testing, it has several limitations. These include the prolonged time required for pathogen growth, frequent false-negative results in patients already receiving antimicrobial therapy, and the inability to detect fastidious organisms [3]. Such limitations may delay the initiation of adequate antimicrobial treatment, potentially leading to increased mortality rates [4, 5].

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) is a molecular technique that enables the real-time detection and quantification of PCR products generated during each amplification cycle [6]. It allows for the precise quantification of gene expression levels [7]. Unlike conventional culture methods, RT-qPCR are not affected by the bacteriostatic effects of prior antimicrobial therapy, which can inhibit or delay bacterial growth [8–12]. Several adult studies have demonstrated that RT-qPCR performed on whole blood provides rapid and reliable identification of causative pathogens for the early diagnosis of sepsis [9–12].

This study aimed to compare the detection of causative microorganisms during febrile neutropenia episodes using both conventional blood cultures and the Sepsis qPCR MX-30® panel, and to assess the diagnostic performance of the qPCR panel in immunocompromised pediatric patients with hematologic disorder.

Methods

This retrospective single center study included patients under the age of 18 who were diagnosed with hematological diseases between February 2023 and November 2024 at the Department of Pediatric Hematology and Oncology, Ankara Bilkent City Hospital, a tertiary referral center. Patient data including age, diagnosis, and prior antibiotic use were obtained from electronic medical records.

During the study period, blood cultures and the Sepsis qPCR MX-30® panel were collected concurrently from the same central venous catheter or peripheral vein at the onset of febrile neutropenia episodes, or in cases of persistent fever in hospitalized patients. All patients with leukemia were undergoing chemotherapy as part of a standardized treatment protocol. Patients who underwent hematopoetic stem cell transplantation were not included. Febrile neutropenia (FN) was defined as a single oral temperature of ≥ 38.3 °C or a sustained temperature of ≥ 38.0 °C for more than one hour, in the presence of an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) < 500 cells/µL, or an expected decline to < 500 cells/µL within 48 h. For patients with multiple FN episodes, each episode was analyzed separately. A new episode was defined when a patient developed fever again after being afebrile for at least 72 h. When multiple blood cultures were obtained during a single febrile neutropenia episode, corresponding Sepsis qPCR MX-30® panel samples were collected simultaneously with each blood culture draw. For episodes requiring repeated sampling, both blood culture and PCR testing were performed concurrently at each time point. The reference standard (gold standard) for diagnostic performance analysis was defined as any positive blood culture result from the same febrile episode, regardless of whether it was detected in the first, second, or subsequent blood culture sample.

Standard prophylaxis consisted of trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for Pneumocystis jirovecii and voriconazole for fungal prophylaxis in high-risk leukemia patients. Empirical therapy for FN followed institutional guidelines and included anti-pseudomonal agents such as piperacillin-tazobactam, cefepime, or sulbactam/cefoperazone.

The Sepsis qPCR MX-30® panel (Genmar, Istanbul, Turkey) was used for the detection of bloodstream infections. This test is based on real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) technology and can detect up to 30 bacterial and fungal pathogens simultaneously from whole blood samples within 4–6 h. The detection limit is approximately 10³ CFU/mL. Pathogen identification was performed through automatic DNA extraction followed by amplification using target-specific primers. The analysis was conducted on the MX-30® device (Genmar, Turkey) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Results were interpreted using integrated software that reports pathogen load based on cycle threshold (Ct) values. The kit is CE-IVD certified Conventional blood cultures were processed using the automated BACTEC FX system (BD Diagnostics). Positive cultures were subcultured and identified using MALDI-TOF MS (Bruker Biotyper, Germany), and rapid resistance gene characterization was performed when necessary using the BCID2 panel (BioFire Diagnostics, Salt Lake City, UT, USA). Blood culture isolates considered likely contaminants (e.g., coagulase-negative staphylococci, Corynebacterium spp., Bacillus spp.) were excluded unless recovered from ≥ 2 independent samples or deemed clinically significant. For each febrile neutropenia episode, one blood sample was collected for both blood culture and PCR testing at the time of fever onset. If repeat sampling was required during the same episode (e.g., for suspected contaminant confirmation), all cultures and their corresponding PCR results from that episode were included in the analysis, with the episode classified as positive if any sample was positive by either method.The reference standard (gold standard) was defined as any positive blood culture from the same episode. Missing or indeterminate PCR or culture results were excluded from diagnostic accuracy analyses.

The Sepsis qPCR MX-30® panel is capable of detecting broad range of pathogens and resistance markers, including:

Gram-positive bacteria

Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), Staphylococcus spp., Enterococcus faecium (E. faecium), E. faecalis, Streptococcus pneumoniae (S. pneumoniae), Streptococcus. spp., Listeria monocytogenes (L. monocytogenes).

Gram-negative bacteria

Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa), Escherichia coli (E. coli), Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae), K. oxytoca, Acinetobacter baumannii (A. baumannii), Haemophilus influenzae (H. influenzae), Pseudomonas spp. (P.spp.), Enterobacteriaceae spp., Neisseria meningitidis (N. meningitidis), Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (S. maltophilia),

Fungi

Candida glabrata (C. glabrata), C. tropicalis, C. krusei, C. albicans, and C. parapsilosis.

Antibiotic resistance markers

Vancomycin resistance (VanA, VanB), carbapenem resistance (OXA-48, KPC, NDM, VIM, IMP), and methicillin resistance (mecA + C).

For statistical analysis, diagnostic accuracy indices, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), overall accuracy, likelihood ratios (LR⁺ and LR⁻), and Cohen’s kappa (κ) were calculated using conventional blood culture as the reference standard. Calculations were performed with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The association and differences between PCR-based and culture-based pathogen detection were evaluated using the Pearson chi-square test, with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Pathogen-level concordance was defined as detection of the same organism by both PCR and blood culture in the same episode. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp. Released 2019. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0. Armonk, NY).

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ankara Bilkent City Hospital (Approval No: E2-23-5323). As this was a retrospective study using routinely collected clinical specimens, no additional informed consent was required. Parental consent for diagnostic testing was obtained at the time of hospital admission. The study did not receive any external or industry funding; all PCR test kits were procured by the institution.

Results

A total of 251 febrile neutropenia (FN) episodes were analyzed in 80 children, with 60 patients contributing more than one FN episode (range: 1–10 episodes per patient). The mean age was 9.4 ± 5.1 years, with 45 females (56.3%). The most common diagnosis was pre-B acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) (n = 53, 66.3%), followed by acute myeloid leukemia (AML) (n = 11, 13.8%), T-cell ALL (n = 8, 10.0%), aplastic anemia (AA) (n = 4), mixed-phenotype ALL (n = 3), and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) (n = 1).

The Sepsis qPCR MX-30® panel detected pathogens in 54 of 251 FN episodes (21.5%), whereas conventional blood culture (BC) was positive in 45 episodes (17.9%). Ten episodes were positive by both methods, 44 were PCR-positive but culture-negative, and 35 were culture-positive but PCR-negative; 162 episodes were negative by both methods. Among the 10 episodes that were positive by both PCR and blood culture, pathogen-level concordance (detection of the same organism by both methods) was observed in 7 episodes (70%). These included 5 episodes with Klebsiella pneumoniae, 1 with Escherichia coli, and 1 with Pseudomonas aeruginosa. In the remaining 3 episodes, both methods detected pathogens but identified different organisms, highlighting the complexity of interpreting molecular and culture-based results in the context of polymicrobial infections or detection of different organisms at low bacterial loads.

Using blood culture as the reference standard, the qPCR panel demonstrated a sensitivity of 22.2% (95% CI: 12.5–36.3), specificity of 78.6% (95% CI: 72.5–83.7), positive predictive value of 18.5% (95% CI: 10.4–30.8), and negative predictive value of 82.2% (95% CI: 76.3–86.9), with an overall accuracy of 68.5% (95% CI: 62.5–74.0) (Table 1). Discussion

Despite advances in antimicrobial therapies and supportive care, sepsis and bacteremia continue to pose serious threats in FN patients, underscoring the need for rapid diagnostic tools and early intervention strategies. Blood culture remains the gold standard for identifying causative pathogens in sepsis and bacteremia [13–17]. In automated blood culture systems, most clinically significant organisms yield a positive signal within 12–36 h, though cultures are typically incubated for up to five days to detect slow-growing organisms such as Cutibacterium acnes, Candida spp., and variant Streptococcus strains [16, 17]. However, prolonged turnaround times limit timely decision-making, leading to increased interest in molecular diagnostics. Among these, PCR-based assays are the most widely implemented, offering results within hours rather than days [18]. While culture provides antimicrobial susceptibility data, rapid molecular testing can be particularly valuable in Gram-negative infections, where early pathogen identification may enable timely initiation or de-escalation of therapy and potentially reduce mortality.

The present study evaluated the diagnostic performance of the Sepsis qPCR MX-30® panel compared with conventional blood culture for detecting bloodstream infections in children with FN. Although molecular panels like the MX-30® can identify pathogens directly from blood within hours, their diagnostic accuracy and clinical impact in immunocompromised pediatric populations remain under investigation. To date, there is a notable paucity of clinical studies assessing the MX-30® panel, particularly in children with hematologic malignancies. Our findings contribute novel data showing that the MX-30® panel demonstrated an NPV of 82.2% and a PPV of 18.5%, highlighting both the potential and limitations of molecular detection in this setting. While this NPV may provide some reassurance when negative, it is not sufficiently high to reliably exclude infection in this vulnerable population. A false-negative rate of approximately 18% poses unacceptable risk for missed diagnoses in immunocompromised patients. Therefore, negative PCR results must be interpreted in conjunction with clinical presentation and blood culture findings, and should not independently guide decisions regarding antibiotic de-escalation. Comparable findings have been reported in adult cohorts using similar PCR-based assays. A study analyzing 809 blood samples from 636 adult patients found 132 samples (16.3%) positive for one or more relevant microorganisms, with the test demonstrating a sensitivity of 29% and specificity of 95% [10]. Likewise, Miringu et al. (2024) reported a multiplex PCR achieving 100% sensitivity and 98% specificity for Gram-negative bacteria in pediatric septicemia [19]. Overall, the PCR panel showed low sensitivity and PPV, indicating that it cannot replace blood culture as a standalone diagnostic tool. While the PCR panel identified a greater number of E. coli and K. pneumoniae cases compared to culture, overall concordance remained modest. Our results, while showing lower PPV, align with these studies in supporting the value of rapid molecular testing as an adjunctive tool when interpreted alongside clinical and conventional culture data. Similar outcomes have been reported for other rapid molecular assays such as SeptiFast and BioFire FilmArray, which demonstrated modest sensitivity but meaningful reductions in time to pathogen identification [20, 21].

In our study, discordant results were common, with 44 PCR-positive but culture-negative episodes. Several factors may explain this, including low pathogen burden, intermittent bacteremia, prior antimicrobial exposure, or detection of non-viable microbial DNA (DNAemia) [22, 23]. Conversely, 35 culture-positive but PCR-negative cases likely reflected bacterial loads below the assay’s detection threshold, a recognized limitation of rapid molecular tests, particularly in FN where circulating bacterial concentrations are extremely low [20, 24]. Similar trends have been observed in next-generation sequencing studies, where molecular methods improved pathogen detection but did not fully eliminate false negatives at low bacterial densities [9, 20, 24]. An important finding was that among episodes positive by both methods, only 70% (7/10) showed true pathogen-level concordance with the same organism detected by both PCR and culture. In the remaining 3 episodes, different organisms were identified, which could reflect true polymicrobial bacteremia, detection of transient DNAemia by PCR while culture captured a different viable pathogen, or discordant detection thresholds between methods. This underscores that agreement on infection presence does not guarantee agreement on pathogen identity, which has important implications for targeted antimicrobial therapy decisions. These findings emphasize that molecular assays should complement, not replace, conventional blood culture in the diagnostic workflow [25].

The MX-30® panel successfully detected methicillin resistance (mecA) in Staphylococcus aureus isolates with full concordance between genotypic and phenotypic results, demonstrating the potential added value of molecular resistance markers. However, PCR-based assays cannot provide comprehensive susceptibility profiles, detect all resistance mechanisms, or reliably identify polymicrobial infections; thus, molecular diagnostics currently serve as adjuncts rather than alternatives to culture. The detection of resistance genes by the MX-30® panel enabled early recognition of multidrug-resistant pathogens, which has important implications for timely antimicrobial adjustment. Our analysis showed a statistically significant difference in method concordance based on Gram status (χ²=5.69, p = 0.017), suggesting a tendency of the sepsis panel to detect Gram-positive bacteria with higher sensitivity but lower specificity for Gram-negative pathogens. Additionally, episodes with prior antibiotic exposure demonstrated lower concordance between PCR and blood culture results, which may reflect reduced culture positivity from antimicrobial pressure while PCR continues to detect circulating microbial DNA. This illustrates how antimicrobial exposure can decrease culture yield while molecular methods may still detect DNAemia, representing a known limitation in retrospective FN cohorts where treatment is often initiated before diagnostic sampling.

Recent evidence across infectious disease settings reinforces the complementary role of molecular diagnostics alongside culture. Meta-analyses indicate that PCR shortens the time to organism identification but exhibits variable diagnostic accuracy across patient subgroups [20]. In pediatric oncology populations, rapid PCR testing has been associated with earlier antimicrobial optimization without reducing the need for culture confirmation [23]. Similarly, antimicrobial stewardship programs integrating PCR panels improved targeted therapy adjustments within 24 h, yet still relied on culture for definitive diagnosis and susceptibility testing [9]. These data suggest that PCR primarily detects DNAemia, which may not always reflect active infection [25].

This study has several important limitations that warrant consideration. First, the retrospective design with convenience sampling may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of our findings to other pediatric hematology-oncology populations. Our study population consisted of patients who had already undergone Sepsis qPCR MX-30® panel testing as part of routine clinical practice, which may not represent all febrile neutropenia episodes at our institution. Second, we did not systematically assess whether clinical management decisions were influenced by PCR-positive but culture-negative results. Specifically, we could not determine whether antimicrobial therapy was prolonged, modified, or escalated based solely on PCR findings, or whether such decisions improved patient outcomes. This lack of prospective documentation of clinical decision-making limits our ability to evaluate the clinical impact and practical utility of discordant PCR results. Future prospective studies should systematically track how clinicians interpret and act upon PCR-positive, culture-negative results to better understand the real-world value of molecular diagnostics in this setting. Third, the reliance on blood culture as the sole reference method for evaluating the MX-30® panel represents another limitation. Incorporating an additional molecular assay or composite reference standard could have strengthened the analysis by helping resolve discordant results. Nevertheless, our findings support growing evidence that PCR-based diagnostics can substantially accelerate pathogen identification in high-risk pediatric patients. Importantly, results should always be interpreted in conjunction with clinical presentation and conventional culture findings to prevent overestimation of infection and unnecessary antimicrobial escalation.

In conclusion, while blood culture remains indispensable for its reliability, accessibility, and role in guiding antimicrobial therapy, the Sepsis qPCR MX-30® panel represents a promising rapid adjunctive tool that may complement conventional diagnostics when used appropriately. However, with an NPV of 82.2% and a false-negative rate of approximately 18%, negative PCR results are not sufficient to exclude infection or guide antibiotic de-escalation independently in this vulnerable population. The panel’s optimal use lies in providing rapid preliminary pathogen and resistance gene detection to inform initial empirical therapy choices, while awaiting definitive blood culture results. Future prospective multicenter studies are warranted to define its clinical utility, evaluate cost-effectiveness, and develop evidence-based interpretation frameworks for PCR-positive but culture-negative results. Ultimately, the MX-30® panel and blood culture should be viewed as complementary rather than interchangeable diagnostic modalities, with molecular results always interpreted in the context of clinical presentation and conventional culture findings.
