Distribution Matching for Graph Quantification under Structural Covariate Shift
Clemens Damke (✉), Eyke Hüllermeier

Abstract
Graphs are commonly used in machine learning to model relationships between instances. Consider the task of predicting the political preferences of users in a social network; to solve this task one should consider, both, the features of each individual user and the relationships between them. However, oftentimes one is not interested in the label of a single instance but rather in the distribution of labels over a set of instances; e.g., when predicting the political preferences of users, the overall prevalence of a given opinion might be of higher interest than the opinion of a specific person. This label prevalence estimation task is commonly referred to as quantification learning . Current quantification learning methods for tabular data are typically based on the so-called prior probability shift assumption which states that the label-conditional instance distributions should remain equal across the training and test data. In the graph setting, prior probability shift generally does not hold if the shift between training and test data is structural, i.e., if the training data comes from a different region of the graph than the test data. To address such structural shifts, an importance sampling variant of the popular adjusted count quantification approach has previously been proposed. In this work, we extend the idea of structural importance sampling to the state-of-the-art KDEy quantification approach. We show that our proposed method adapts to structural shifts and outperforms standard quantification approaches.



1
Introduction

Quantification learning
refers to the task of estimating the distribution of labels
\mathcal{Y}
over a set of instances
\mathcal{X}

[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.
More specifically, one is given a set of labeled training instances
\mathcal{D}_{L}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
drawn from a distribution
P
and a set of unlabeled test instances
\mathcal{X}_{U}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
drawn from a distribution
Q
for which the label distribution
Q(Y)
is to be estimated.
For example, the problem of predicting the prevalence of different opinions in a given population of people can be seen as a quantification learning task.
Here, the training data consists of a sample of people with known opinions, while the test data consists of a second sample of people with unknown opinions for which the opinion prevalences should be estimated.

A naïve way to solve a quantification problem is to use a standard classification model to label all test instances
\mathcal{X}_{U}
.
The relative frequencies of the predicted labels could then be used as an estimate of
Q(Y)
.
Given a perfect classifier, this so-called
Classify & Count
strategy would indeed yield a perfect estimate of the test label distribution.
This is, however, an unrealistic assumption, leading to the question of whether an imperfect classifier can still provide good quantification results.

Forman 2005
showed that the simple Classify & Count approach can lead to poor quantification results if the classifier is biased.
To understand why, note that the goal of a classifier is to minimize the number of classification errors, i.e., the sum of false positive and false negative predictions (
\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{FN}
) in the binary case.
In contrast, the goal of a quantifier is to minimize
\left|\mathrm{FP}-\mathrm{FN}\right|
; if
\mathrm{FP}=\mathrm{FN}
, the missing positive predictions are perfectly compensated for by the missing negative predictions, leading to a perfect quantification result.
This implies that even a poor classifier (in terms of misclassifications) can provide good quantification results and vice versa.
Therefore, quantification should be treated as a distinct task from classification
[
5
]
.

Suppose the training and test data are drawn from the same distribution (
P=Q
).
In that case, the quantification task is trivially solved using the label distribution of the sampled training data
\mathcal{D}_{L}
as an unbiased estimate of the label distribution of the test data.
The quantification learning problem gets more challenging when the training and test data are drawn from different distributions, i.e., in the presence of so-called
distribution shift
.
In this case, the distribution of training labels
P(Y)
is not necessarily a good estimate of the test label distribution
Q(Y)
.
In the extreme case, if the two sampling distributions are entirely unrelated and the training data thus is uninformative about the test data, the quantification task becomes intractable without other prior assumptions.

Therefore, one typically assumes at least some kind of relation between the training and test data.
The most commonly assumed type of shift is called
prior probability shift

[
23
,
14
]
, which states that the class-conditional instance distributions remain unchanged between
P
and
Q
.
This assumption is made, among others, by the well-known
Adjusted Classify & Count
quantification method
[
8
]
and by
distribution matching
methods, such as the
Mixture Models
approach
[
8
]
, HDy
[
15
]
, DyS
[
19
]
or KDEy
[
25
]
.

While prior probability shift is a reasonable assumption in many domains, there are problems where it does not hold.
If the training and test data are drawn from different regions of the instance space, the prior probability shift assumption may not be satisfied.
In this case,
P
and
Q
may instead be related by
covariate shift

[
23
]
, which states that the distributions of the instances can differ, while the instance-conditional label distributions remain unchanged.

González et al. 2024
and
Tasche 2022
show, both empirically and theoretically, that the standard Adjusted Classify & Count and distribution matching approaches are not robust to covariate shift.

One domain where covariate shift arises naturally is
node label quantification
in graph data.
Here, instances are nodes in a graph which are connected by edges indicating some notion of relatedness.
Consider the opinion quantification problem mentioned earlier, where people can be naturally represented as nodes in a social network with edges indicating social relations (coworkers, friendships, or familial relations).
The structural information contained in such graph representations has been used with great success by
graph neural network
models to solve node classification tasks
[
16
]
.
However, there has been little work on
graph quantification learning
.

Milli et al. 2015
and
Tang et al. 2010
have proposed simple graph quantification learning methods based on community detection algorithms which do not make use of current predictive graph models.
Recently,
Damke and Hüllermeier 2025
proposed the first classifier-based quantification method for graph data.
They extend Adjusted Classify & Count to account for structural covariate shift by introducing the kernel-based
structural importance sampling
method.
Adjusted Classify & Count with structural importance sampling is shown to give unbiased estimates of
Q(Y)
under covariate shift.
However, state-of-the-art distribution matching methods, such as KDEy, generally tend to outperform Adjusted Classify & Count methods under prior probability shift
[
25
]
.
Translating the practical advantages of distribution matching to the covariate shift setting via structural importance sampling is therefore desirable.

In this work, we extend structural importance sampling to the distribution matching framework and adapt KDEy to structural covariate shift and show that this combination outperforms previously proposed quantification approaches.
To this end, we begin with a brief introduction to quantification learning in general and distribution matching methods in particular (
Section

2
).

Section

3
then describes the structural importance sampling method and how it can be used in the distribution matching framework.
In
Section

4
, we evaluate the proposed method on a set of benchmark datasets under different types of distribution shift and compare it to other quantification approaches.
Finally, we conclude with a brief outlook in
Section

5
.

2
An Overview of Quantification Learning

In the literature on quantification learning, one typically distinguishes between
aggregative
and
non-aggregative
approaches
[
5
]
.
Aggregative methods are based on a standard classification model that is trained on labeled training instances.
The predictions of this model on the test data are aggregated to estimate the test label distribution.
Non-aggregative methods, in contrast, are directly trained to predict label prevalences given a set of instances.
Here, we focus on aggregative methods; the extension of non-aggregative quantification methods to covariate shift is left for future work.
First, we fix some notation and formally define the quantification learning setting.

2.1
Notation and Problem Setting

As described in the introduction, let
\mathcal{X}
denote the instance space and
\mathcal{Y}=\{1,\dots,K\}
the (finite) label space.
Let
P
and
Q
be probability measures on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
representing the training and test data distributions, respectively.
Let
X
and
Y
denote
random variable
s that project the joint instance-label space to the instance and label spaces, respectively.
In quantification learning, we are given a labeled sample
\mathcal{D}_{L}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
drawn from
P
and an unlabeled sample
\mathcal{X}_{U}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
drawn from
Q(X)=Q\circ X^{-1}
.
The goal of quantification learning is to estimate
Q(Y)
from
\mathcal{D}_{L}
and
\mathcal{X}_{U}
.
The two measures
P
and
Q
are assumed to be related by some kind of distribution shift
[
23
]
:

(i)

In
prior probability shift
(PPS),
P(Y)
and
Q(Y)
might differ but the label-conditional instance distributions remain equal, i.e.,
P(X\mid Y)=Q(X\mid Y)
.

(ii)

In
covariate shift
, the distributions of the instances
P(X)
and
Q(X)
differ, while the conditional label distributions remain unchanged, i.e.,
P(Y\mid X)=Q(Y\mid X)
.

(iii)

In
concept shift
, it is the conditional label distributions
P(Y\mid X)
and
Q(Y\mid X)
that change while the marginal instance distributions
P(X)
and
Q(X)
remain equal.

Figure 1:
Overview of the three typically considered types of distribution shift.

See
Fig.

1
for an overview.
Depending on the problem domain, different types of distribution shift can occur
[
23
]
.
For example, prior probability shift might arise in the context of epidemiological studies where the task is to estimate the prevalence of a disease in a population.
Here, the training data might be collected via a case-control study where the percentage of healthy and infected people is fixed by design, while the test data is collected from a random sample of the population.
In contrast, the opinion estimation problem mentioned earlier might be subject to covariate shift if the training data is collected in a different region of the population than the test data.
Last, concept shift occurs if the meaning of labels change between training and test data, e.g., whether a newspaper article is about a local or world news depends on the location of the reader/newspaper.

Assuming prior probability shift, the training and test distributions are related by
P(X\mid Y)=Q(X\mid Y)
.
Consequently, for any measurable mapping
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}
, we have
P(Z\mid Y)=Q(Z\mid Y)
where
Z=\phi(X)

[
18
]
.
This allows us to factorize
Q(Z)
as follows:

\displaystyle Q(Z)=\sum_{i=1}^{K}Q(Z\mid Y=i)Q(Y=i)=\sum_{i=1}^{K}P(Z\mid Y=i)Q(Y=i)

(1)

Given
\mathcal{D}_{L}
and
\mathcal{X}_{U}
, both,
Q(Z)
and
P(Z\mid Y)
can (in principle) be estimated, resulting in a system of equations which can be solved for the desired test label distribution
Q(Y)
.
This idea forms the basis for many quantification learning methods, including Adjusted Classify & Count
[
8
,
10
,
2
]
and the family of distribution matching methods
[
7
,
3
,
25
]
.
The main difference between those methods lies in how the mapping
\phi
is chosen and how the system of equations is solved.
One common approach is to define
\phi
in terms of a hard classifier
h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
or a probabilistic classifier
h_{s}:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta_{K}
, where
\Delta_{K}
is the unit
(K-1)
-simplex.
Let
\hat{Y}=h(X)
denote the predicted label and
\hat{S}_{i}=h_{s}(X)_{i}
be the predicted probability of label
i
.

For
\phi=h
,
Eq.

1
turns into Adjusted Classify & Count, where
P(\hat{Y}\mid Y)
is simply the confusion matrix of
h
.

Tasche 2017
shows that this results in an unbiased estimate of
Q(Y)
under prior probability shift.
Similarly, using a confusion matrix derived from
h_{s}
leads to the
Probabilistic Adjusted Classify & Count
approach
[
1
]
, while the unadjusted pendant of Classify & Count is referred to as
Probabilistic Classify & Count
.
Next, we will describe the distribution matching framework.

2.2
Histogram-based DM Approaches

The first distribution matching quantification method, simply refered to as
Mixture Models
(MM), was proposed by
Forman 2005
.
Mixture Models is designed for binary quantification problems, i.e.,
\mathcal{Y}=\{\oplus,\ominus\}
, and uses
\phi=h_{s}
, where
h_{s}:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
is a soft binary classifier.

Equation

1
then becomes

\displaystyle Q(\hat{S})={\underbrace{P(\hat{S}\mid Y=\oplus)}_{P_{\oplus}}\underbrace{Q(Y=\oplus)}_{\alpha}}+{\underbrace{P(\hat{S}\mid Y=\ominus)}_{P_{\ominus}}\underbrace{Q(Y=\ominus)}_{1-\alpha}}\ .

Here, the distribution of predicted probabilities
Q(\hat{S})
is modeled as a mixture of the class-conditional predicted probability distributions
P_{\oplus},P_{\ominus}
.

Forman 2005
estimates those distributions via discrete
cumulative distribution function
s
\hat{p}_{\oplus},\hat{p}_{\ominus}
predicted by
h_{s}
on
\mathcal{D}_{L}
.
Analogously, an estimate
\hat{q}
of
Q(\hat{S})
is obtained by computing the discrete cumulative distribution function using
\mathcal{X}_{U}
.
The mixture weight
\alpha
is then determined by solving the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\alpha^{*}=\argmin_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\ell\left(\alpha\cdot\hat{p}_{\oplus}+(1-\alpha)\cdot\hat{p}_{\ominus},\hat{q}\right)\ ,

(2)

where
\ell
is a loss function measuring the discrepancy between the mixture cumulative distribution function and
\hat{q}
.

Forman 2005
proposes two different loss functions: PP-Area, which is equivalent to minimizing the L1-norm between the two cumulative distribution functions
[
7
]
, and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic.

González-Castro et al. 2013
extend Mixture Models by proposing a variant that estimates the
probability density function
s of
P_{\oplus}
,
P_{\ominus}
and
Q(\hat{S})
via normalized histograms
\hat{p}_{\oplus},\hat{p}_{\ominus},\hat{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{b}
instead of discrete cumulative distribution functions estimates; here,
b\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is the number of bins.
Additionally, they suggest using the
Hellinger distance
as a loss function
\ell
between probability density functions:

\displaystyle\mathrm{HD}(p,q)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{q}\|_{2}

(3)

This variant of Mixture Models is referred to as HDy.
Note that, both, Mixture Models and HDy can only be applied to binary quantification problems.
Extensions of HDy to the multi-class regime have been proposed by
Firat 2016
and
Bunse 2022b
.
If
K>2
, one can compute a class-conditional histogram
\hat{p}_{j,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b}
of
P(\hat{S}_{j}\mid Y=i)
for each pair of classes
j,i
and one histogram
\hat{q}_{j}
of
Q(\hat{S}_{j})
for each
i\in\mathcal{Y}
.
To obtain a representation of
P(\hat{S}\mid Y=i)
, one can then combine the class-conditional histograms by concatenating and renormalizing them to obtain a single histogram
\hat{p}_{i}=\frac{1}{K}\left(\hat{p}_{1,i},\dots,\hat{p}_{K,i}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{bK}

[
7
]
, or by directly averaging them, i.e.,
\hat{p}_{i}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\hat{p}_{j,i}

[
3
]
.

Moreo et al. 2025
show that both of these histogram aggregation variants are theoretically flawed, since neither of them produces a proper estimate of the divergence between probability density functions.
The problem of those multi-class extensions of HDy is that the per-class histograms
\hat{p}_{j,i}
and
\hat{q}_{j}
are unable to capture inter-class information.

2.3
Kernel Density Estimation-based DM

To address the shortcomings of histogram-based distribution matching methods,
Moreo et al. 2025
propose the KDEy quantification approach.
Unlike Mixture Models and HDy, KDEy does not represent
Q(\hat{S})
and
P(\hat{S}\mid Y)
by decomposing them into class-wise histograms but instead uses
kernel density estimation
to model the probability density functions of the predicted probabilities as
Gaussian mixture model
s.
More specifically, let
q(\hat{s})
be the probability density function of
Q(\hat{S})
and
p(\hat{s}\mid i)
be the probability density function of
P(\hat{S}\mid Y=i)
, with
s=(s_{1},\dots,s_{K})\in\Delta_{K}
being a vector of predicted label probabilities.
Using kernel density estimation, we can estimate those probability density functions as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{q}(\hat{s})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}_{U}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{U}}k(\hat{s},h_{s}(x))\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{p}(\hat{s}\mid i)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{L}^{i}|}\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{L}^{i}}k(\hat{s},h_{s}(x))\ ,

(4)

where
k:\Delta_{K}\times\Delta_{K}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a kernel function and
\mathcal{D}_{L}^{i}\coloneqq\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{L}\mid y=i\}
.
In KDEy, the kernel
k
is chosen to be a Gaussian kernel with bandwidth
\sigma
:

\displaystyle k(\hat{s},\hat{s}^{\prime})\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{K}}\sigma^{K}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\hat{s}-\hat{s}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\ .

(5)

The bandwidth
\sigma
is treated as a model hyperparameter.
Using the linearity of the Radon-Nikodym derivative
1
1

1

Note that
q(\hat{s})=\frac{dQ(\hat{S})}{d\mu}
and
p(\hat{s}\mid i)=\frac{dP(\hat{S}\mid Y=i)}{d\mu}
, with the Lebesgue measure
\mu
.
we can plug our probability density function estimates into
Eq.

1
to obtain the following equation:

\displaystyle\hat{q}(\hat{s})=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\hat{p}(\hat{s}\mid i)q(i)\ ,

(6)

where both sides of the equation are Gaussian mixture models and
q\in\Delta_{K}
is the vector of label prevalences in the test distribution which we are looking for.
Analogous to the histogram-based distribution matching approaches, we can now solve for
q
by minimizing a divergence
\ell
between the left-hand side and the right-hand side of
Eq.

6
:

\displaystyle\hat{q}=\argmin_{q\in\Delta_{K}}\ell\left(\hat{q}(\hat{s}),\sum_{i=1}^{K}q_{i}\cdot\hat{p}(\hat{s}\mid i)\right)\ .

(7)

Different choices of
\ell
are possible here, e.g., Hellinger distance (see
Eq.

3
), L2, Cauchy-Schwarz, Jensen-Shannon or
Kullback-Leibler divergence
.
Since
\ell
has to be computed for continuous distributions which, depending on the divergence, can be computationally intractable,
Moreo et al. 2025
suggest a number of divergence-dependent optimization strategies.
For the Cauchy-Schwarz divergence, they derive a closed-form solution to
Eq.

7
.
For the Hellinger distance, Jensen-Shannon and L2 divergences, they propose a Monte Carlo approximation approach.
For the Kullback-Leibler divergence, they show that
Eq.

7
reduces to

\displaystyle\hat{q}=\argmin_{q\in\Delta_{K}}-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{U}}\log\sum_{i=1}^{K}q_{i}\cdot\hat{p}(h_{s}(x)\mid i)\ ,

(8)

which can be solved using standard (gradient-based) constrained optimization techniques.
In their experiments, the different variants of KDEy generally outperform the histogram-based distribution matching methods, with the Hellinger distance- and Kullback-Leibler divergence-based variants performing particularly strongly.

3
DM under Structural Covariate Shift

The distribution matching approaches for quantification described in the previous section are all based on the prior probability shift assumption.
However, as discussed before, this assumption is not always justified in practice.
When dealing with graph data, covariate shifts are of particular interest.
If the training data is collected from a different region of the graph than the test data, there is so-called
structural covariate shift
between
P
and
Q
.

Damke and Hüllermeier 2025
propose an extension of Adjusted Classify & Count to account for such structural covariate shifts via so-called
structural importance sampling
(SIS).
We will now show how this idea can be extended to the distribution matching framework.

To motivate our approach, consider the following example:

Figure

2
shows a simple graph consisting of three vertex clusters, each corresponding to one label
\mathcal{Y}=\{{\color[rgb]{0.7031,0.207,0.207}\textsf{{A}}},{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.7031,0.207}\textsf{{B}}},{\color[rgb]{0.207,0.3711,0.7031}\textsf{{C}}}\}
.

Figure 2:
Illustration of the advantage of structural importance sampling in the distribution matching framework under structural covariate shift.

Most vertices in each cluster have the matching label; there are, however, a few outliers incident to the edges between clusters.
Assume that the training data
\mathcal{D}_{L}
is sampled uniformly at random from all three clusters and that the test data
\mathcal{X}_{U}
comes only from the topmost cluster
A
(indicated by the large nodes), i.e., there is covariate shift between both samples.
A probabilistic classifier
h_{s}
has high confidence on inlier vertices and low confidence for outliers.
The simplex plots on the left of
Fig.

2
show the probability density functions of
P(\hat{S}\mid Y)
for all three labels
\mathcal{Y}
.
The standard KDEy method described in
Section

2.3
tries to find an optimal mixture of those probability density functions to match the target probability density function
Q(\hat{S})
of the test data (shown in black) by minimizing a divergence
\ell
between the mixture probability density function and
Q(\hat{S})
.
Since outliers are rare for each class, the probability density functions
P(\hat{S}\mid Y)
are concentrated around the high confidence regions of each class.

\mathcal{X}_{U}
, on the other hand, contains many outliers, causing the test probability density function
Q(\hat{S})
to be less concentrated.
In this case, the optimal mixture found by KDEy will put all weight on
P(\hat{S}\mid Y={\color[rgb]{0.7031,0.207,0.207}\textsf{{A}}})
, resulting in a heavily biased estimate of
Q(Y)
.

The problem is that the global probability density functions
P(\hat{S}\mid Y)
are not representative of the local (shifted) probability density functions
Q(\hat{S}\mid Y)
.
Instances with label
B
and
C
within cluster
A
will receive low confidence scores, as shown in the simplex plots on the right side of
Fig.

2
.
By combining these estimates of
Q(\hat{S}\mid Y)
, as opposed to
P(\hat{S}\mid Y)
, one can find a probability density function mixture that better matches
Q(\hat{Y})
resulting in a better predicted label distribution.
The core idea behind structural importance sampling is to use the graph structure to obtain such estimates.
We will now describe structural importance sampling more formally and then adapt it to the distribution matching framework.

3.1
Structural Importance Sampling for ACC

As described in
Section

2.1
, both, Adjusted Classify & Count and distribution matching methods are based on the factorization in
Eq.

1
.
Under covariate shift, this factorization does not hold, since
Q(Z\mid Y)
is unknown.
If
\phi=h
, i.e.,
Z=\hat{Y}=h(X)
, we can rewrite
Q(\hat{Y}\mid Y)
via importance sampling
[
4
]
:

\displaystyle Q(\hat{Y}=j\mid Y=i)

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathds{1}[h(x)=j]dQ(X=x\mid Y=i)

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathds{1}[h(x)=j]\underbrace{\frac{q_{X|Y}(x\mid i)}{p_{X|Y}(x\mid i)}}_{=\rho_{X|Y}(x\mid i)}dP(X=x\mid Y=i)

(9)

Here,
q_{X|Y}
and
p_{X|Y}
denote the conditional probability density functions of
Q
and
P
, and
\rho_{X|Y}
denotes the ratio between those densities.
Under (structural) covariate shift, we know that
p_{Y|X}=q_{Y|X}
, which allows us to rewrite
\rho_{X|Y}
:

\displaystyle\rho_{X|Y}(x\mid y)

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{X|Y}(x\mid y)}{p_{X|Y}(x\mid y)}=\frac{q_{Y|X}(y\mid x)q_{X}(x)p_{Y}(y)}{p_{Y|X}(y\mid x)p_{X}(x)q_{Y}(y)}=\rho_{X}(x)\cdot\rho_{Y}(y)^{-1}

(10)

Plugging this into
Eq.

9
gives us

\displaystyle Q(\hat{Y}=j\mid Y=i)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{Y}(i)^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathds{1}[h(x)=j]\rho_{X}(x)dP(X=x\mid Y=i)}{\rho_{Y}(i)^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\rho_{X}(x)dP(X=x\mid Y=i)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P(X\mid Y=i)}[\mathds{1}[\hat{Y}=j]\rho_{X}(X)]}{\mathbb{E}_{P(X\mid Y=i)}[\rho_{X}(X)]}\ .

(11)

To compute
\rho_{X}=\frac{q_{X}}{p_{X}}
,
Damke and Hüllermeier 2025
propose to use kernel density estimation.
Since we are given samples
\mathcal{D}_{L}
from
P
and samples
\mathcal{X}_{U}
from
Q(X)
,
q_{X}
and
p_{X}
can be estimated as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}_{U}|}\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{U}}\kappa(x,x^{\prime})\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{p}_{X}(x)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{L}|}\sum_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}_{L}}\kappa(x,x^{\prime})\ ,

(12)

where
\kappa:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a suitable instance kernel function.
Without any domain assumptions, choosing
\kappa
appropriately is difficult.

However, in the context of graph data where instances
x
are vertices, assuming structural covariate shift, the probability of sampling a vertex
x
from
P
or
Q
should depend on how close
x
is to the training or test vertices, respectively.

Damke and Hüllermeier 2025
suggest that the appropriate notion of “closeness” in a graph depends on the nature of the covariate shift.
For example, if the data is sampled via random walks, a
personalized page-rank
kernel
[
27
]
is appropriate:

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathrm{PPR}}(x_{i},x_{j})=\Pi_{i,j},\quad\text{where}\quad\Pi={\left(\alpha\mathbf{I}+(1-\alpha)\bar{\mathbf{A}}\right)}^{L}\ .

(13)

Here,
\bar{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{-1}
is the normalized adjacency matrix of the graph,
\alpha\in(0,1)
is a teleportation parameter and
L
is the number of steps in the random walk.
If the vertex sampling process is based on the shortest path lengths between vertices, a
shortest path
kernel can be used, e.g.,

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathrm{SP}}(x_{i},x_{j})=\exp\left(-\lambda\cdot d_{\mathrm{SP}}(x_{i},x_{j})\right),

(14)

where
d_{\mathrm{SP}}(x_{i},x_{j})
is the length of the shortest path between
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
and
\lambda
is a scaling parameter.
To summarize,
k
should be chosen based on available knowledge about the quantification problem at hand to reflect the nature of the shift as closely as possible.

3.2
SIS in the DM Framework

In Adjusted Classify & Count, the instance mapping
\phi
reduces an instance
X
to a single label prediction
\hat{Y}
, discarding much, potentially valuable, information.
As discussed in
Section

2
, distribution matching methods instead consider the distribution of predicted probability vectors
\hat{S}=h_{s}(X)
.
To apply structural importance sampling in the distribution matching framework, we can rewrite
q(\hat{s}\mid y)
:

\displaystyle q(\hat{s}\mid y)

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}q(\hat{s}\mid x)dQ(X=x\mid Y=y)

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p(\hat{s}\mid x)\underbrace{\frac{q_{X|Y}(x\mid y)}{p_{X|Y}(x\mid y)}}_{=\rho_{X|Y}(x\mid y)}dP(X=x\mid Y=y)

(15)

Analogous to structural importance sampling for Adjusted Classify & Count, we can use
Eq.

10
for the following replacement:

\displaystyle q(\hat{s}\mid Y=i)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{P(X\mid Y=y)}[p(\hat{s}\mid X)\rho_{X}(X)]}{\mathbb{E}_{P(X\mid Y=y)}[\rho_{X}(X)]}\ .

(16)

Given
\mathcal{D}_{L}
and
\mathcal{X}_{U}
we can compute an estimate
\hat{\rho}_{X}(x)
via
Eq.

12
.
Additionally,
q(\hat{s}\mid Y=i)
can be estimated via

\displaystyle\hat{q}(\hat{s}\mid y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{L}^{i}}\hat{\rho}_{X}(x)}\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{L}^{i}}p(\hat{s}\mid x)\cdot\hat{\rho}_{X}(x)

(17)

Since
p(\hat{s}\mid x)=\delta[\hat{s}=h_{s}(x)]
is a Dirac delta, i.e., the density is zero everywhere except at
\hat{s}=h_{s}(x)
, this estimate is not particularly useful given a finite sample
\mathcal{D}_{L}
.
To account for this, we replace the Dirac delta with a Gaussian kernel
k:\Delta_{K}\times\Delta_{K}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and obtain

\displaystyle\hat{q}(\hat{s}\mid y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{L}^{i}}\hat{\rho}_{X}(x)}\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{L}^{i}}k(\hat{s},h_{s}(x))\cdot\hat{\rho}_{X}(x)\ .

(18)

The result is a weighted version of KDEy (cf.
Eq.

4
), where the instance weights
\hat{\rho}_{X}(x)
are themselves estimated via kernel density estimation using a vertex kernel
\kappa
.
By reweighting the labeled samples
\mathcal{D}_{L}
via structural importance sampling, KDEy can be applied to quantification problems with structural covariate shift.

We compare our approach against Probabilistic Classify & Count, Probabilistic Adjusted Classify & Count, Probabilistic Adjusted Classify & Count with structural importance sampling and standard KDEy without structural importance sampling.

Quantification Metrics

To compare the quality of a label distribution estimate
\hat{q}
against the ground-truth label distribution
q

Absolute error
(AE) and
relative absolute error
(RAE):

\displaystyle\mathrm{AE}(q,\hat{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}|q_{i}-\hat{q}_{i}|

\displaystyle\mathrm{RAE}(q,\hat{q})=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{|q_{i}-\hat{q}_{i}|}{q_{i}}

(19)

While absolute error penalized all errors equally, relative absolute error
[
15
]
penalizes errors on rare labels more heavily.

Distribution Shift

To evaulate the behavior of the quantifiers under distribution shift, we synthetically introduce shifts to the test partitions of the datasets, while the training data is sampled uniformly at random from the training split.
We consider the following types of distribution shift:

1.

3.

Structural covariate shift via breadth-first search: Analogous to the personalized page-rank setting, we also evaluate structural covariate shift by sampling 100 nodes via breadth-first search instead of random walks.

Influence of the Classifier

Unsurprisingly, the choice of classifier has a significant impact on the quantification performance.
Even though, a good classifier
h
is not required by quantification learning to obtain an unbiased estimate of the label prevalences, the quality of this estimate is still correlated with the classifier’s accuracy.
Overall, the structure-unaware Multilayer Perceptron classifier thus performs worst while approximate personalized propagation of neural predictions performs best.

Influence of the Type of Covariate Shift

The
\kappa_{\lambda}
used in our experiments is based on the assumption that the distribution shift is induced by sampling localized random walks.
In the random walk covariate setting, this assumption is satisfied, while in the breadth-first search setting, the personalized page-rank kernel is, at least in theory, not appropriate.
Since the test vertices are selected by breadth-first search based on their distance to some start vertex, a SP-based kernel, as in
Eq.

14
, seems plausible here.
However, our results show that a perfect match between the structural importance sampling kernel and the underlying distribution shift is not necessary.
In fact, the personalized page-rank kernel performs well even in the breadth-first search setting, clearly outperforming all other quantifiers, while the shortest path kernel performs comparatively poorly.

Influence of
\kappa
on SIS

Note that the performance of structural importance sampling strongly depends on the choice of the kernel
\kappa
.
For
\lambda=1
, the personalized page-rank kernel performs poorly on all datasets except Amazon Computers.

Figure

3
shows that decreasing
\lambda
slightly to
0.9
already improves the performance significantly, further decreasing
\lambda
then has little to no effect.
This illustrates an important tradeoff to consider when using structural importance sampling:
By making
\kappa
more aggressive, in the sense that little to no weight is assigned to distant vertices, one can in-principle improve the performance of structural importance sampling by reducing the influence of irrelevant or misleading vertices from different regions of the graph.
However, if too many vertices are excluded, the effective sample size for the estimate
\hat{q}(\hat{s}\mid y)
is reduced, making it more noisy.

The dataset-dependent optimal
\lambda
value differences can be explained by different connectivity patterns in the datasets.
For example, while the CiteSeer dataset consists of multiple disconnected components, the Amazon Computers dataset mostly consists of a single large connected component (excluding a few disconnected outlier vertices).
If all vertices in a structurally shifted test set are sampled from a single (small) connected component, the personalized page-rank kernel with
\lambda=1
will assign zero weight to all training vertices that are not in the same component, resulting in noisy estimates based on only a few vertices.
For less connected datasets, where sampling a test set from a small component is more likely, it is therefore often beneficial to assign at least some weight even to disconnected vertices, which is achieved by using a
\lambda<1
.
For a well-connected dataset, such as Amazon Computers, this is not necessary.

To summarize, we have seen that KDEy combined with structural importance sampling and the personalized page-rank kernel perform very well across different datasets and shift types, corroborating that structural importance sampling is able to effectively account for (structural) covariate shift given an appropriate kernel.

5
Conclusion

We proposed a novel approach to quantification under structural covariate shift extending structural importance sampling from Adjusted Classify & Count to the KDEy quantification method.
We showed the effectiveness of this approach on a set of benchmark datasets with different types of distribution shift.
For future work, it would be interesting to investigate whether structural importance sampling can also be applied outside of the graph domain, e.g., in the context of timeseries data or geospatial data, where covariate shifts might occur in time or space.
Second, a more thorough analysis of the influence of the choice of the kernel
\kappa
on the quantification performance is needed, especially since the choice of
\kappa
is crucial for the performance of structural importance sampling.
Third, in this work we focused on the combination of structural importance sampling and KDEy, since KDEy is a state-of-the-art quantification method within the distribution matching framework.
Making other quantification learning methods that assume prior probability shift applicable to covariate shifts would be another avenue for future work.
More specifically, investigating the combination of non-aggregative quantification approaches
[
28
]
would be interesting.
