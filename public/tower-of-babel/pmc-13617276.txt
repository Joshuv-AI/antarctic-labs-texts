The refinement of repeated exact time-to-event modeling: methodological refinements and implementation in NONMEM® —part I
Authors: Jiyoung Seo, Hyeong-Seok Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundParametric time-to-event (TTE) modeling is widely used in clinical trials to evaluate treatment response, patient prognosis, and drug-related adverse events (AEs). As follow-up assessments increasingly capture recurrent AEs, there is a growing need for analytical approaches that extend beyond traditional single-event frameworks. However, commonly used TTE models often assume independence between recurrent events, limiting their ability to represent inter-event dependence observed in real-world clinical data.MethodsWe mathematically examined and reconstructed the existing NONMEM® (version 7.5) control code used for parametric repeated-event analyses and evaluated the statistical foundations of commonly applied parametric and nonparametric approaches, including Kaplan–Meier–based methods. Based on these analyses, we identified methodological gaps and proposed an improved probabilistic framework that more rigorously represents recurrent event dynamics while allowing for multiple dependent event occurrences. The proposed and existing methods were compared through simulation studies under diverse event-generation scenarios, and practical guidance for model selection was developed.ResultsThree approaches were evaluated: two conventional parametric methods (methods 1, 2) and a newly proposed method (method 3). Overall, when the hazard function remained constant, all three methods produced similar results; however, clear differences in performance emerged depending on hazard dynamics and event density. When the hazard decreased gradually and events were relatively evenly distributed, method 2 demonstrated the closest alignment with nonparametric estimates, whereas when the hazard declined rapidly and events were concentrated in the early phase, method 3 showed a relatively better fit. In addition, because nonparametric estimators inherently involve uncertainty, discrepancies between parametric and nonparametric approaches do not necessarily indicate model misspecification, underscoring the need for careful interpretation in light of data characteristics and underlying hazard structures.ConclusionThese findings highlight the importance of selecting analytical methods according to data characteristics and suggest that complementary use of both widely used and newly proposed methods can improve the accuracy of recurrent event modeling. The proposed guidelines are expected to facilitate more systematic and reliable interpretation of recurrent AE data frequently encountered in clinical trials and real-world clinical settings, ultimately contributing to more robust evaluations of drug efficacy and safety as well as optimization of dosing strategies.

Introduction

Parametric time-to-event (TTE) modeling is widely used in analyses of clinical trial data to evaluate treatment responses, patient prognoses, and drug-related adverse events (AEs) (Collett, 2015; Lim, 2021; Bonate, 2011). As post-treatment follow-up assessments for the recurrence of AEs increase, there is a growing need for methodologies that can be extended beyond single-event analyses to accommodate recurrent events (Cook, 2007; Amorim and Cai, 2015; Ghosh and Lin, 2000; Rauch et al., 2018).

Currently, the TTE framework used for survival analysis in clinical trials was originally developed for single event analyses (Collett, 2015). Therefore, when a single TTE framework is applied to repeated TTE data, conventional approaches typically treat each event as independent (Cook, 2007). Hence, in such conventional approaches based on a single TTE framework, the likelihood of repeated events is computed as the product of individual event probabilities, assuming that the intervals between successive events follow independent single-event processes (Kaplan and Meier, 1958).

However, in real repeated event processes, events often exhibit dependence on previous events (Wang and Chang, 1999). Therefore, such dependence structures can be more appropriately described using conditional probability formulations (Andersen et al., 1993; Fleming and Harrington, 2011). Additionally, even when conditional probabilities are introduced, careful specification is required. If only one of the survival and hazard functions used in parametric methods is defined conditionally, the model may not adequately account for the possibility of multiple events occurring after the previous event and may implicitly assume that only a single event can occur in each interval. In such cases, the probability structure of the repeated event process is not rigorously defined, which may result in a likelihood that is not probabilistically valid.

To better understand these issues, this study conducted a mathematical investigation of the NONMEM® control stream used for parametric repeated time-to-event modeling implemented in NONMEM® (Version 7.5). The NONMEM control stream is a script-based model specification used in NONMEM, a standard software package for nonlinear mixed-effects modeling, to define model structures, parameters, statistical models, and estimation procedures.

In this study, we evaluated two conventional parametric approaches (methods 1 and 2) widely used in repeated time-to-event modeling. Both approaches treat the time interval between successive events as a single time-to-event process and apply this formulation repeatedly; however, they differ in the choice of time scale and in how dependence between events is handled. Method 1 uses gap time as the time scale and does not account for inter-event dependence, whereas method 2 uses calendar time and defines the survival function based on conditional probabilities, thereby partially reflecting dependence between events. To address these limitations, we propose a refined parametric approach (method 3) that uses calendar time and defines both survival and hazard functions within a conditional probability framework. To this end, we reconstructed the equations used in conventional parametric approaches and analyzed them from a probabilistic perspective, compared the likelihood definitions across the three approaches, and evaluated their relationships. Furthermore, we compared the three approaches in terms of probabilistic structure and practical implementation and provided practical guidance for selecting appropriate methods under different scenarios.

For the purposes of this study, we consider two types of repeated time-to-event (TTE) data: exact event times, where the precise timing of each event is recorded, and interval-censored events, where events are known to occur within specific time windows. While other data structures such as terminal events or competing risks also exist, these require different modeling frameworks (e.g., joint models or competing risk models) and are therefore not considered in this study. This paper focuses on parametric repeated time-to-event models with exact event times, while methodological approaches for interval-censored events will be presented separately in Part II.

Methods

Non-parametric analysis

Kaplan–Meier framework for recurrent event data

To compare parametric approaches for recurrent events, we first considered a nonparametric approach (Kaplan–Meier (KM) estimator) as a reference framework for evaluation.

The mathematical expressions derived for the single-event scenario are summarized in Supplementary Files 1, 2 (Collett, 2015; Kalbfleisch and Prentice, 2002; Kleinbaum and Klein, 2012). The hazard and cumulative hazard functions of commonly used parametric survival models (exponential, Weibull, and log-logistic) are in Supplementary File 3 (Collett, 2015; Lim, 2021).

The KM estimator is a nonparametric method traditionally used to analyze single-event TTE data. The KM estimator assumes that the number of subjects not experiencing an event within the risk set at each event time follows a binomial distribution, with risk sets redefined at each event time point and initially defined as the total number of subjects at a baseline time point (typically t0=0 ). A summary table of the KM estimator applied to a single TTE dataset is provided in Supplementary File 4 (Collett, 2015; Kaplan and Meier, 1958; Nelson, 1982; D’Arrigo et al., 2021; Greenwood, 1926).

In the analysis of repeated events with exact event times, a widely adopted nonparametric approach extends the KM framework originally developed for the single TTE analysis (Nelson, 1982). Each interval, defined between a previous event time and the subsequent event time, is reset using the previous event time as the baseline origin, with the occurrence of an event within that interval modeled as a separate binomial trial (Kaplan and Meier, 1958; Lawless, 2003; Therneau, 2023a; Therneau, 2023b; Therneau and Grambsch, 2000).

Accordingly, the probabilities of each event occurrence and non-occurrence within an interval sum to 1, and because the probability of event occurrence after the previous event time is calculated conditionally and multiplied across intervals, the event contribution from each interval is represented by a conditional probability. Although these intervals are not fully independent, the event contributions from each interval are treated as conditionally independent (Cook, 2007; Andersen et al., 1993; Therneau, 2023a; Therneau, 2023b; Therneau and Grambsch, 2000). The overall probability is then constructed as the product of these conditional probabilities, yielding the product-limit estimator of the survival function. As this does not constitute an exact joint distribution, this estimator should not be interpreted as a probability curve representing the exact joint distribution (Cook, 2007; Andersen et al., 1993; Therneau, 2023a; Therneau, 2023b; Therneau and Grambsch, 2000).

From a survival perspective, the probability of survival up to the previous event is treated as a realized constant and incorporated as a conditional component. Thus, because the survival probability up to the current time point is divided by that up to the prior time point, the resulting function can be regarded as conditional survival (Cook, 2007; Wang and Chang, 1999; Andersen et al., 1993; Lawless, 2003). In contrast, for the cumulative hazard, the baseline is reset to the preceding event time, effectively treating the hazard accumulated before that point as zero, and recalculating the cumulative hazard up to the current event time. Consequently, the cumulative hazard until the previous event is not retained. Thus, the KM method does not capture the conditional hazard but instead represents the event hazard in an unconditional form (Cook, 2007; Wang and Chang, 1999; Andersen et al., 1993; Lawless, 2003). Hence, the KM estimator resembles a conditional probability but does not constitute a true conditional distribution that reflects event-to-event dependence. In settings where such a dependence is present, the resetting mechanism fails to capture cumulative risk from earlier events, potentially leading to the underprediction or distortion of overall hazard dynamics (Cook, 2007; Wang and Chang, 1999; Andersen et al., 1993; Lawless, 2003).

Nonetheless, because of its simplicity and its ability to effectively capture overall trends in repeated-event processes, this nonparametric framework remains one of the most widely applied approaches in practice, particularly for visualization and model qualification using KM-type plots (Wang and Chang, 1999; Therneau, 2023a; Therneau, 2023b; Therneau and Grambsch, 2000). In the KM-based framework for repeated exact-time events, each event is treated as a distinct observation and ordered sequentially in time, irrespective of subject identifiers. This sequential arrangement defines the time intervals, with each interval representing the duration between successive events. Therefore, rather than estimating the conditional probability of an event occurring given the previous event for a specific individual, this approach focuses solely on the chronological occurrence of events across the total population, including all individuals (Cook, 2007; Wang and Chang, 1999; Andersen et al., 1993; Therneau, 2023a; Therneau, 2023b; Therneau and Grambsch, 2000).

Parametric extensions corresponding to the KM recurrent event framework

In the nonparametric (KM-based) recurrent-event framework, the time origin is reset at the beginning of each inter-event interval, and event occurrence after the previous event time ti is modeled conditionally. Under this structure, ti is treated as an observed value (realized constant), and the random variable is defined only for the next event time ti+1 (Cook, 2007; Wang and Chang, 1999; Andersen et al., 1993; Lawless, 2003). This conditional probability structure was also used to extend single-event parametric models to recurrent event settings. As shown in Table 1, the two approaches begin to diverge conceptually in the definition and interpretation of the survival function. In the non-parametric (KM-based) framework, the survival function ( S^ ), conditional cumulative probability ( F^ ), and conditional probability ( f^ ) are not restricted to a specific event such as the first event but instead represent probability structures that reflect the aggregate effect of multiple events that may occur after the previous event time t i. Consequently, these quantities can be interpreted as the probability that no event occurs in the interval (i.e., survival probability ( S^ )) and, complementarily, as the cumulative probability that at least one event occurs (i.e., conditional cumulative probability ( F^ )), thereby reflecting the possibility of multiple event occurrences after the previous event time t i.

In contrast, when single-event parametric models are extended to recurrent events using the same framework, although the possibility of multiple events after the previous event time ti is still allowed, the definitions of the survival function (S) and conditional cumulative probability (F) are interpreted differently. Specifically, the probability structure in each interval is interpreted in terms of the time to the first event occurring after t i, rather than the aggregate occurrence of multiple events.

This difference in the interpretation of the survival function (S) ultimately leads to a more substantial difference in the definition of the conditional probability density function (f). Unlike the survival function (S) or conditional cumulative probability function (F), the conditional probability density function (f) is defined under the assumption that only a single event occurs after the previous event time ti . Therefore, when only one event occurs after ti , the conditional probability density (f) is defined for the occurrence of that event at time ti+1 . This differs from the non-parametric approach, which allows the possibility of multiple events after ti .

In summary, although single-event parametric models can be extended to recurrent event settings using the same framework applied in the KM-based approach, conceptual differences between the two approaches remain. Nevertheless, the parametric formulation presented in Table 1 can be regarded as the parametric approach most closely aligned with the KM estimates.

In addition, we compare these methods with the KM estimates and identify which parametric methods yield function structures similar to those shown in Table 1.

Likelihood function

In parametric models for recurrent events, the conditional probability density function for each event, fk , constitutes a component of the likelihood function used in maximum likelihood estimation implemented in NONMEM®. Parameter estimation is performed by maximizing this likelihood function (Bauer et al., 2022).

The likelihood function for recurrent event data differs fundamentally from that for single-event data, primarily due to the dependence structure between successive events (Cook, 2007). In recurrent event data, the likelihood is expressed as the product of conditional probability density functions ( fk for successive event occurrences, where dependence between events is handled through conditioning on the previous event time. However, the specific formulation and modeling of this conditional structure differ across the methods presented later (methods 1, 2, and 3), and this constitutes the main difference between the parametric approaches.

Theoretical background and mathematical verification of parametric method

In this study, the mathematical formulations of methods 1, 2 were constructed based on the control stream code implemented in NONMEM® (Version 7.5), and the corresponding code is provided in Supplementary Files 7, 8 (Bauer et al., 2022).

We focused primarily on scenarios with decreasing hazard patterns and constant hazard conditions were also considered. In contrast, increasing hazard patterns are relatively uncommon in repeated event analyses and were therefore not included within the scope of this study (Amorim and Cai, 2015; Rauch et al., 2018).

Method 1

Time scale

In method 1, the conditional survival and hazard functions are not defined directly using the actual event times; instead, they are defined based on the time between successive events (gap time) (Cook, 2007; Kalbfleisch and Prentice, 2002).

Conditional survival and hazard functions

When the gap time scale is applied to the survival framework, the conditional survival and hazard functions in method 1 are defined as functions of the elapsed time ( Δti=ti+1−ti ) since the previous event, ti . In this framework, the time origin is reset at each event time, and the previous event time, ti is treated as a new time origin t0 , at which the survival probability is equal to one. Consequently, the conditional survival function can be expressed in the same form as the unconditional survival function defined on the gap time scale, Δti (Cook, 2007; Kalbfleisch and Prentice, 2002). The same time redefinition applies to the hazard function. As a result, the conditional hazard function simplifies to the unconditional instantaneous hazard function evaluated on the gap time scale, Δti .

Accordingly, the conditional survival and hazard functions can be written as, Sti+1∣ti=PT>Δti∣T>t0=SΔti hti+1∣ti=limδt→0PΔti≤T<Δti+δtT>t0|T≥Δti|T>t0δt=hΔti (3)

When time-shifted intervals are used instead of actual event times, only the initial segment of the underlying parametric survival function is repeatedly applied to each recurrent event. As a result, the survival and hazard functions defined in Equation 3 may differ from those defined on the absolute time scale. This modeling framework can therefore be interpreted as a renewal process, in which the event-generating process restarts after each event (Cook, 2007; Kalbfleisch and Prentice, 2002).

Here, fΔti presented in Equation 4 is the derivative of the cumulative distribution function FΔti , where FΔti represents the cumulative probability that the first event occurs by time Δti . Therefore, fΔti represents the probability density that a single event occurs at time Δti under the assumption that only one event occurs after the reset time origin t0 (Cook, 2007; Kalbfleisch and Prentice, 2002). Thus, in method 1, the conditional probability density function fk defined in the recurrent event framework is equivalent to the probability density function f of a single-event model defined in Supplementary File 1, applied directly on the gap time scale.

Because the probability density function for each event in method 1 does not depend on previous events, the probability structure of earlier events does not influence the distribution of subsequent events, and the previous event time only serves to reset the time origin. Consequently, inter-event times (gap times) are assumed to be independent and identically distributed (Cook, 2007; Kalbfleisch and Prentice, 2002).

As a result, the conditional probability density function fkti+1|ti derived in Equation 4 does not explicitly account for the dependency structure inherent in recurrent events. For this reason, method 1 may not fully reflect the conditional event occurrence process following previous events and may additionally differ from the non-parametric KM-based approach, which accounts for the probability of multiple event occurrences after the previous event time.

Conceptually, method 1 relies on a probabilistic structure that differs fundamentally from the KM-based recurrent event analysis. The KM approach estimates event occurrence probabilities based on aggregated event counts and corresponding risk sets at each event time across the entire population, without assuming a parametric form (Kaplan and Meier, 1958; Wang and Chang, 1999). In contrast, method 1 models event occurrence times at the individual level using probability density functions defined for each subject, with parameters estimated by processing each subject’s event history sequentially over time and updating the likelihood contribution at each event record. The resulting parameter estimates are obtained by aggregating these individual-level likelihood contributions across all subjects (Cook, 2007; Andersen et al., 1993).

This individual-level modeling framework contrasts with the KM approach, which operates at the population level by directly capturing event occurrence probabilities from observed data at each event time. Consequently, the probability structure reflecting multiple event occurrences after the previous event time, as captured by the KM method, may not be fully represented in method 1. As a result, method 1-based models may underestimate event occurrence probabilities relative to KM-based nonparametric estimates under certain conditions (Amorim and Cai, 2015; Andersen et al., 1993).

The two methods also differ in their interpretation of the event occurrence process. The KM approach assumes an event count process in which multiple events may occur after the previous event time, whereas method 1 assumes a renewal process structure in which only the next event after the previous event is modeled, based on the assumption that only one event occurs after each previous event, with the time origin reset to t0 following each event (Cook, 2007; Kalbfleisch and Prentice, 2002).

The probabilistic dependency structures also differ between the two approaches. In the KM method, dependence between event occurrences is partially retained through the survival structure up to the previous event time, resulting in a conditional independence structure. In contrast, method 1 assumes renewal independence, where inter-event times (gap times) are independent and the probability structure of previous events or event history does not influence future event distributions (Cook, 2007; Kalbfleisch and Prentice, 2002).

These differences in probabilistic structure may lead to discrepancies between non-parametric KM estimates and simulation-based results obtained from parametric models using method 1, such as visual predictive checks (VPCs) (Cook, 2007; Amorim and Cai, 2015).

Special case: equivalence between method 1 and KM estimates

In general, method 1 differs conceptually from the KM-based approach. However, when the hazard function is constant over time, the time-shift transformation does not alter the hazard value, and the two methods yield equivalent results.

In particular, under an exponential model with constant hazard, the hazard calculated using the gap time is identical to the hazard evaluated at the actual event time. Consequently, the survival and hazard functions obtained from method 1 coincide with the corresponding conditional quantities defined on the actual event time scale. A detailed proof of this equivalence is provided in Supplementary File 10 (Kalbfleisch and Prentice, 2002).

Therefore, although method 1 can produce results similar to those of the KM approach in the special case of constant hazard, differences may arise when the hazard varies over time due to the different probabilistic structures of the two methods. This discrepancy may lead to differences between simulation-based visual predictive checks (VPCs) from method 1 and KM-based non-parametric estimates. To address this limitation, an alternative approach is introduced in the following section (method 2).

Method 2

Time scale

Unlike method 1, method 2 does not apply a time shift when defining the conditional survival and hazard functions; instead, it uses calendar time based on the actual event times (Cook, 2007; Andersen et al., 1993; Fleming and Harrington, 2011).

Conditional survival and hazard functions

In method 1, the previous event time ti is reset as a new time origin t0 , which effectively removes the probabilistic structure prior to the previous event. In contrast, method 2 maintains the previous event time ti as the conditioning time and directly incorporates survival information up to that time into the conditional probability structure (Andersen et al., 1993; Fleming and Harrington, 2011). In this setting, the survival probability up to ti is treated as a realized quantity rather than a random variable and thus serves as conditioning information for defining the distribution of subsequent event times. Consequently, the current event time ti+1 is defined under a conditional probability structure given survival up to the previous event time ti (Cook, 2007; Andersen et al., 1993; Fleming and Harrington, 2011).

Within this conditional framework, the joint survival distribution can be decomposed by conditioning on the previous event time ti (Cook, 2007; Kalbfleisch and Prentice, 2002; Lavielle, 2014). The conditional survival function is therefore defined as Sti+1∣ti=PT>ti+1∣T>ti=Sti,ti+1 (5)

The conditional survival function defined in Equation 5 can be interpreted as the survival function for a single event conditional on the previous event time ti and thus represents a conditional form of the first-event survival function. This formulation therefore reflects a probabilistic structure that preserves the dependency inherent in recurrent event processes.

The conditional hazard function can be defined in a manner analogous to the conditional survival function by treating the cumulative hazard up to the previous event time ti as a realized quantity. In general, the hazard function ht is defined as the derivative of the cumulative hazard function Ht (Cook, 2007; Kalbfleisch and Prentice, 2002). Accordingly, the conditional hazard function hti+1∣ti can be expressed as the derivative of the conditional cumulative hazard function Hti+1∣ti (Cook, 2007; Andersen et al., 1993; Fleming and Harrington, 2011; Aalen et al., 2008). Since the conditional cumulative hazard Hti+1∣ti represents the cumulative hazard accumulated over the interval ti,ti+1 (Supplementary File 11), the conditional hazard function corresponds to the derivative of the cumulative hazard over that interval (Andersen et al., 1993; Fleming and Harrington, 2011; Kleinbaum and Klein, 2012; Rudin, 1976; Apostol, 1974).

In method 2, sequential conditioning is applied in the probabilistic formulation, and therefore the cumulative hazard up to the previous event time ti is first treated as a random variable during the derivation. However, once the previous event time has been realized, the cumulative hazard up to ti becomes a fixed quantity and is subsequently treated as constant when differentiation is performed at the current event time ti+1 . As a result, the cumulative hazard term prior to the previous event is eliminated in the differentiation process, and the conditional hazard function is simplified to the instantaneous hazard at the current event time ti+1 (Andersen et al., 1993; Fleming and Harrington, 2011). hti+1|ti=H′ti+1|ti=H′ti,ti+1=ddtHt−Htit=ti+1=hti+1 (6)

Consequently, although the conditional survival function reflects the cumulative survival structure up to the previous event time ti , the conditional hazard function is expressed as the instantaneous hazard at the current time ti+1 (as defined in Equation 6), and thus the carryover effect of cumulative hazard is not preserved at the hazard level. In other words, dependency between previous events is reflected in the survival probability structure but not reflected at the instantaneous hazard level.

Conditional density function

In method 2, the conditional probability density fkti+1∣ti is defined based on a sequential conditional probability structure according to the event order. The survival probability and cumulative hazard up to the previous event time ti are treated as random variables during the derivation but become fixed quantities once the previous event time ti has been observed. Therefore, the conditional cumulative distribution function Ft∣ti for the current event time ti+1 is continuous and differentiable with respect to t . Differentiation with respect to t followed by evaluation at t=ti+1 yields the conditional probability density function fkti+1∣ti (Andersen et al., 1993; Fleming and Harrington, 2011; Aalen et al., 2008). fkti+1∣ti=Sti+1∣ti·hti+1=ddtPT≤t∣T>ti∣t=ti+1=ddtFt∣ti∣t=ti+1=fti+1∣ti (7)

The conditional probability density fkti+1∣ti defined over the interval ti,ti+1 in Equation 7 is structurally equivalent to the probability density function ft∣t0 for a single time-to-event model defined in Supplementary File 1 (Collett, 2015; Kalbfleisch and Prentice, 2002; Kleinbaum and Klein, 2012). The difference between the two formulations lies in the treatment of the reference time. In Supplementary File 1, the reference time t0 is treated as a realized constant and the survival probability is set to St0=1 , whereas in method 2 the reference time ti is treated as a random variable during the probabilistic formulation but, once the event time has been observed, the realized survival value Sti is treated as a constant and incorporated into the conditional probability structure (Collett, 2015; Kalbfleisch and Prentice, 2002; Kleinbaum and Klein, 2012). Accordingly, the conditional density in method 2 may be interpreted as the corresponding form of the single-event density defined in Supplementary File 1.

In method 2, the probability density for each event depends partially on the previous event time ti . Survival information up to the previous event time ti is retained as conditioning information within the conditional framework, whereas the probability update at the current event time ti+1 is determined by the instantaneous hazard at that time. Consequently, the time between successive events is defined under a conditional structure given the previous event time ti and is assumed to follow the same functional form of conditional distribution (Cook, 2007; Andersen et al., 1993; Fleming and Harrington, 2011).

However, under this updating structure, potential cumulative effects from previous events (carryover effects) are not directly reflected at the hazard level; rather, such effects are incorporated indirectly through the conditional survival structure (Cook, 2007; Andersen et al., 1993).

The probability density function defined in method 2 leads to a likelihood structure that is structurally similar to the nonparametric approach obtained by extending the Kaplan–Meier estimator to recurrent event settings, as presented in Table 1. In method 2, the likelihood is constructed sequentially through conditional probabilities for successive event occurrences, which makes it closely related to KM-based approaches. However, despite this structural similarity, the conditional density fti+1∣ti^ in the nonparametric approach and the conditional density fkti+1∣ti in method 2 are generally not mathematically identical. The differences between these two formulations are discussed in the following section (Cook, 2007; Kaplan and Meier, 1958; Wang and Chang, 1999).

The exact definitions of the RTTE functions in method 2, along with their detailed mathematical derivations, are presented in Supplementary File 9.

Unlike method 1, method 2 exhibits a probabilistic dependence structure that is conceptually similar to the Kaplan–Meier (KM)-based approach for recurrent events. Both approaches follow a conditional independence structure in which partial dependence between event occurrences is introduced through the survival function up to the previous event time ti . Under this framework, the conditional survival function, conditional hazard, and conditional probability density function in method 2 are conceptually similar to the probabilistic structure used in KM-based parametric extensions for recurrent events. Therefore, method 2 can be interpreted as a parametric counterpart to the KM-based recurrent event model presented in Table 1, with the key difference that the conditional survival structure is modeled using a parametric hazard function rather than a nonparametric product-limit estimator (Cook, 2007; Kaplan and Meier, 1958; Wang and Chang, 1999; Andersen et al., 1993; Kalbfleisch and Prentice, 2002).

The difference in the probabilistic dependence structure arises from how the event process after a previous event is interpreted. In nonparametric KM-based approaches, the conditional probability structure is defined for the entire process in which multiple events may occur after a previous event (Cook, 2007; Andersen et al., 1993). In contrast, for each conditional likelihood unit, method 2 defines the conditional distribution only for the time to the next event following the previous event (Collett, 2015; Kleinbaum and Klein, 2012; Lawless, 2003).

In addition, similar to method 1, method 2 adopts an individual-level modeling framework. Therefore, some differences may arise compared to KM estimates, which directly estimate the probability at the population level. These differences can be interpreted as resulting from the structural differences between parametric model-based estimation and nonparametric estimation methods.

Nevertheless, despite these differences, in most cases the KM-based nonparametric estimates and the model-based simulation results obtained using method 2 (e.g., visual predictive checks) show similar overall behavior.

Special case: equivalence between method 2 and KM estimates

Although the nonparametric KM estimates and method 2 are not mathematically identical, the differences between the two approaches become negligible when the hazard function varies smoothly over time. Under such conditions, the KM-based nonparametric estimates and the model-based simulation results obtained using method 2 are often nearly indistinguishable. In many clinical situations, hazard functions tend to be relatively high at early times and gradually decrease over time rather than changing abruptly. Therefore, in most practical applications, method 2 can be appropriately considered a parametric counterpart to the KM estimates.

However, when the hazard decreases rapidly over time, for example, in situations where strong dependence exists between events or early events occur in clusters, method 2 may underestimate event occurrences. As discussed previously, this arises from structural differences between method 2 and the nonparametric estimation approach.

As a result, in parametric approaches, hazard increments are effectively updated sequentially along each subject’s event history, with likelihood contributions evaluated row by row within each subject. Consequently, the incorporation of repeated events follows an individual-level, time-ordered structure. In contrast, the KM-based nonparametric approach aggregates events across all subjects within the entire time frame and reflects their occurrence order at the population level. As a result, in parametric approaches the hazard increments tend to be estimated in an averaged form at the individual level. Consequently, when events are clustered in the early observation period and the hazard changes rapidly, these differences may become more pronounced. In addition, method 2 implicitly assumes that only one event occurs after a previous event, which may lead to additional smoothing of the early hazard increments (Cook, 2007; Kaplan and Meier, 1958; Wang and Chang, 1999; Andersen et al., 1993).

This limitation may become more pronounced when steep changes in the hazard need to be described using relatively inflexible parametric models, particularly in exponential models with covariates, such as those incorporating drug concentration, exposure metrics (e.g., area under the curve [AUC]), or non-drug effects. As a result, the estimates obtained from method 2 may differ from nonparametric estimates. Detailed mathematical derivations related to this behavior are provided in Supplementary File 12 (Kleinbaum and Klein, 2012; Lawless, 2003; OpenStax, 2022; Conrad, 2010).

Therefore, method 2 can still be widely applied in clinical situations where the hazard changes gradually over time. However, when the hazard changes rapidly or when strong dependence exists between events, alternative approaches may be required. Accordingly, method 3 is proposed as a new approach that more explicitly accounts for dependence between events.

Method 3

Method 3, proposed in this paper, is designed to more explicitly capture risk by reflecting event-to-event dependence, particularly in settings where events occur densely in the early phase. In this approach, both the time scale and the survival function are applied consistently with method 2.

Notably, the primary distinction between methods 2 and 3 lies in the definition of the conditional hazard.

Conditional hazard function

In the sequential framework of method 2, although the approach is designed to sequentially compute the cumulative hazard between two consecutive events in order to preserve the risk at the previous event time within the conditional structure, the differentiation step—where the hazard is defined as the derivative of the cumulative hazard—effectively removes the component reflecting the previous event.

To address this limitation, instead of evaluating the two event times separately, we define the conditional hazard by jointly considering both time points and by characterizing the change in cumulative hazard over the interval between them. When both time points are treated simultaneously as two possible realizations of event-time random variables that share the same underlying properties, the interpretation of H′ti ti+1 extends beyond the instantaneous rate of change of Ht at ti+1 . Instead, the interpretation represents the overall rate of change in the cumulative hazard over the interval ( ti,ti+1 . Accordingly, the conditional hazard is not defined as the instantaneous risk at a specific time point, but instead as the interval-averaged hazard, obtained by dividing the increment in cumulative hazard over the interval by its length.

Mathematically, this interpretation corresponds to the average rate of change of the cumulative hazard between ti and ti+1 . Therefore, the conditional hazard in method 3 can be defined as follows: hti+1∣ti=1ti+1−ti∫t0t2hu du−∫t0t1hu dut1=ti,t2=ti+1=∫t0ti+1hudu−∫t0tihuduti+1−ti (8) where t1 and t2 are treated as two realized values of event-time random variables associated with ht .

Under a counting process framework, the increment in cumulative hazard over an interval ti, ti+1 corresponds to the expected number of events occurring within that interval. Based on this relationship, the conditional hazard in method 3 can be interpreted as an interval-averaged event intensity. The detailed derivations are provided in Supplementary File 13 (Andersen et al., 1993; Fleming and Harrington, 2011; Aalen et al., 2008; Singer and Willett, 2003).

According to the mean value theorem, the mean rate of change of a continuous and differentiable function Ht across an interval equals the slope of the tangent line at some point within that interval (Conrad, 2010). Therefore, if Ht is concave, corresponding to a decreasing hazard function ht , the mean rate of change will necessarily exceed the tangent slope hti+1 at the later endpoint, ti+1 (Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004). This provides a natural explanation for the amplification of dependent risk, as the contribution of risk at the previous event time is carried forward and reflected in the current event risk.

In method 2, the conditional hazard is defined as the instantaneous hazard at a single time point, whereas in method 3 it is defined as an interval-averaged event intensity based on the increment of cumulative hazard over the interval. In method 3, the cumulative hazard up to the previous event time is explicitly incorporated into the definition of the hazard between successive events. As a result, the accumulated risk from prior events is naturally propagated to subsequent event risk, allowing event-to-event dependence to be more effectively captured through changes in cumulative hazard.

Conditional probability density function

Unlike methods 1 and 2, method 3 defines the conditional hazard based on an interval formulation. Accordingly, ti and ti+1 are treated as realizations of the same underlying continuous event-time random variable T . This formulation allows method 3 to more effectively capture the sequential dependence inherent in recurrent events.

Under this framework, the conditional probability density function fkti+1∣ti can be expressed as the product of the conditional survival function and the interval-averaged event intensity: fkti+1∣ti=Sti+1∣ti·hti+1∣ti=Sti+1Sti·Hti+1−Htiti+1−ti (9)

In Equation 9, the conditional density is not defined as an instantaneous hazard of event occurrence at a specific time point, but rather as a quantity based on the interval event intensity defined over the interval between two event times. This formulation avoids reliance on the first-event distribution and, unlike method 2—which assumes, for each conditional likelihood unit, that a single event occurs after ti —allows for the possibility of multiple events occurring after ti .

Nevertheless, method 3 does not yield a mathematically identical form to the nonparametric KM estimator. Although conceptually consistent with the KM conditional survival structure in accounting for multiple events, method 3 defines the conditional hazard using an interval-averaged intensity, which leads to differences from KM-based estimates. These differences are further discussed in the subsequent section.

Furthermore, similar to methods 1 and 2, method 3 is a parametric, model-based approach that updates risk sequentially at the individual level following each event, whereas KM estimates are derived nonparametrically at the population level. This difference in the level of inference introduces structural discrepancies between the two approaches. Special cases: equivalence between method 3 and KM estimates

As hazard functions most commonly exhibit a decreasing trend, we focus on this setting and further categorize it into two scenarios: one in which the hazard changes smoothly and another in which it changes abruptly (Amorim and Cai, 2015; Rauch et al., 2018).

This study provides mathematical derivations for quantifying the differences between the probability density function fti+1|ti^ of the KM method and fkti+1|ti in methods 2 and 3 under conditions of smoothly varying versus rapidly changing hazard functions. Full details are presented in Supplementary File 14 (Kleinbaum and Klein, 2012; Lawless, 2003; OpenStax, 2022; Conrad, 2010; Miller, 2016). Notably, when the hazard varies smoothly, the discrepancy between fkti+1|ti in method 2 and fti+1|ti^ in the KM method is smaller. In contrast, when the hazard exhibits steep or abrupt changes, the discrepancy between fkti+1|ti in method 3 and fti+1|ti^ in the KM method becomes smaller.

Accordingly, method 2 may be less appropriate in settings where events occur frequently, when the hazard changes rapidly following initial exposure, or when prior events exert a strong influence on subsequent risk (e.g., drug-induced adverse events), leading to pronounced event-to-event dependence. In such cases, the discrepancy between the KM-based results-reflecting cumulative population-level risk and the parametric individual-level estimates fkti+1|ti obtained from method 2 may become more evident, partly due to the averaging inherent in individual-level updating. Conversely, method 3 may help reduce these discrepancies by explicitly incorporating a cumulative prior-event risk into the conditional hazard structure, thereby amplifying conditional cumulative risk at the individual level while aligning more closely with the KM-based cumulative risk at the population level. In these settings, the divergence between KM curves and method 3 often remains smaller than that observed with method 2.

These discrepancies may become more pronounced in scenarios where events are densely concentrated in the early period and the hazard changes rapidly, particularly when inflexible parametric models are used (e.g., exponential models incorporating covariates such as drug concentration, cumulative drug exposure (area under the curve, AUC), or non-drug effects). Given the diversity of clinical scenarios, method 3 does not universally provide a better fit than method 2. However, when hazards vary sharply and events are heavily concentrated in the early phase, especially in exponential models with multiple covariates, method 2 may tend to underestimate the hazard relative to KM estimates. In such cases, method 3 may serve as a useful alternative.

Comparisons of VPC between method 2 and method 3 in base models without covariates

To examine how methods 2 and 3 differ across time intervals under a base model without covariates (i.e., the exponential, Weibull, and log-logistic models defined in Supplementary File 3), this study analyzes the mathematical differences in the definition of the conditional hazard for each method.

The difference between methods 2 and 3 can be understood through a Taylor series expansion of the cumulative hazard function Ht at the previous event time ti . Under this expansion, the conditional hazard in method 3 can be expressed as the unconditional hazard in method 2 plus a residual term. When the inter-event interval Δt is small, this residual is dominated by the leading term and its sign is determined by the curvature of Ht , i.e., the second derivative H″t . Specifically, when Ht is concave (decreasing hazard), the residual becomes positive, whereas when Ht is convex (increasing hazard), the residual becomes negative. The detailed mathematical derivation of this relationship, based on a Taylor series expansion of the cumulative hazard function, is provided in Supplementary File 17 (OpenStax, 2022; Miller, 2016).

Consequently, during the early observation period with short event intervals, a decreasing hazard produces positive residuals, leading method 3 to overpredict relative to method 2. Conversely, when the hazard is increasing, the residuals are negative, causing method 3 to underpredict. In the mid-to-late stages of observation, where event intervals are longer and hazard changes are negligible, the residuals converge toward zero, and the difference between methods 2 and 3 becomes minimal.

When interpreted relative to KM estimates, under a base model without covariates (with a decreasing hazard), scenarios characterized by early clustering of events and rapidly changing hazards may lead to a pronounced difference between methods 2 and 3, with method 3 tending to yield higher estimates than method 2, potentially resulting in different levels of agreement with the KM estimates. As events become less frequent and inter-event intervals increase over time, the discrepancies between KM estimates and both methods 2 and 3 tend to converge, resulting in similar levels of agreement.

Comparison of likelihood values among methods 1, 2, 3

The likelihood values derived from the three methods differ and can be distinguished according to how each defines the conditional hazard. Detailed equations and explanations of these likelihood formulations are provided in Supplementary File 18 (Collett, 2015; Cook, 2007; Rudin, 1976; Pawitan, 2001).

The likelihood values obtained from methods 1, 3, and 2 were consistently smaller in that order across the examples described in the following sections. A detailed mathematical exposition of this comparison is presented in Supplementary File 19 (OpenStax, 2022; Conrad, 2010; Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004; Miller, 2016; LibreTexts, 2023; Rosen, 2012; Knopp, 1951).

Together, these comparisons demonstrate that under a decreasing hazard (i.e., a concave cumulative hazard function), with events densely concentrated in the early period, the likelihood values follow the order: method 1 > method 3 > method 2, while the objective function values follow the reverse order: method 1 < method 3 < method 2 (Collett, 2015; Cook, 2007; Rudin, 1976; Pawitan, 2001).

Therefore, in cases of decreasing or constant hazard, the observed differences in objective function values primarily reflect variations in mathematical computation methods rather than differences in model performance. Accordingly, we did not evaluate the comparative explanatory power of the three methods based on the magnitude of the objective function, because the relative magnitude of log-likelihood or objective function values is meaningful only when the compared models share an identical likelihood formulation and are in a nested relationship (Collett, 2015; Venables and Ripley, 2002).

Model fitting

In this study, methods 1, 2, and 3 were independently applied to two datasets presented in Table 9. All model fitting was performed using NONMEM® (version 7.5) with the conditional Laplace method (METHOD = COND LAPLACE) (Bauer et al., 2022). For the rat recurrence dataset, three parametric distributions—exponential, Weibull, and log-logistic—were implemented for each method, resulting in a total of nine fitted models. The mathematical formulations and parameter definitions for each model are provided in Supplementary File 3 (Collett, 2015; Lim, 2021). For the jittered AE dataset, two analysis strategies were considered. First, base models without covariates (i.e., the exponential, Weibull, and log-logistic models defined in Supplementary File 3) were fitted across all methods. Second, extended models incorporating covariates (e.g., drug concentration and AUC) were developed based on the exponential model and applied to each method. Nonparametric analysis was conducted using Kaplan–Meier (KM) estimation implemented in R (version 4.4.1). Within each method, model selection was based on the minimization of the objective function value and stable convergence, with additional consideration of visual predictive check (VPC) results. Direct comparison of objective function values across methods was not performed, as the models were not nested (Collett, 2015; Venables and Ripley, 2002).

Simulation dataset

Simulation datasets were constructed to preserve the structure of the original data, including the number of individuals, observation periods, and covariate distributions. Evaluation time grids were defined, with rat data collected at 2-day intervals up to 122 days, and AE data recorded at 15-h intervals.

Simulation implementation in NONMEM®

Simulations were performed using a Monte Carlo approach based on the estimated hazard structures. For each model and method, 100 replicated datasets (n = 265 per replicate) were generated with fixed parameter estimates. Event occurrences were generated sequentially over time for each individual, reflecting the temporal structure following previous events and thereby preserving event-history structure specified by each method. Detailed mathematical formulations and the implementation algorithm used for event generation are provided in Supplementary File 22.

Simulation-based evaluation

For each simulated dataset, survival functions and cumulative event probabilities were computed and compared with Kaplan–Meier estimates derived from the observed data. Model performance was primarily evaluated using visual predictive checks (VPC), in which simulated percentiles (5th, 50th, and 95th) were compared against observed KM curves. For each method, the model that demonstrated the closest agreement with the KM-based patterns was selected as the best-fitting model (Bonate, 2011; Kaplan and Meier, 1958).

Therefore, in applied settings, AE analyses often assume a constant hazard over the observation period and incorporate covariates, such as drug concentration or AUC to improve explanatory power (Collett, 2015; Bonate, 2011).

It showed slight underprediction during the initial steep interval (∼0.5 weeks), after which the simulated curve aligned closely with the median of the KM estimates. These results likely reflect the greater flexibility of method 3 in representing the conditional hazard. Specifically, by approximating the conditional hazard as the average rate of change in the cumulative hazard, method 3 was able to more effectively capture the underlying event dynamics, particularly in the early phase where events are highly clustered.

Discussion

Summary of probabilistic structures across methods

In this study, we systematically examined and reformulated the probabilistic structures underlying three parametric approaches (methods 1–3) for repeated time-to-event (TTE) analysis and evaluated their characteristics through comparison with nonparametric Kaplan–Meier (KM) estimates (Cook, 2007; Amorim and Cai, 2015).

Method 1 defines the survival and hazard functions independently based on gap time, treating repeated events as a renewal process and thus not accounting for the influence of prior events. Method 2 preserves calendar time and incorporates partial dependence between events through a conditional survival framework; however, the hazard remains defined as an instantaneous quantity, limiting its ability to reflect accumulated risk. In contrast, the proposed method 3 defines the conditional hazard as the rate of change in cumulative hazard over event intervals, enabling a coherent formulation of both survival and hazard within a conditional probabilistic framework and providing a more consistent representation of inter-event dependence (Cook, 2007; Andersen et al., 1993; Fleming and Harrington, 2011; Kalbfleisch and Prentice, 2002).

Bridging structural differences between KM-based and parametric hazard representations

Parametric modeling estimates parameters at the individual level by sequentially updating likelihood contributions based on each subject’s event history, whereas the KM approach estimates event probabilities at the population level using aggregated event counts within each time interval. Consequently, these two approaches differ fundamentally in how event information is accumulated and represented (Kaplan and Meier, 1958; Wang and Chang, 1999).

In parametric approaches, hazard increments are effectively averaged over individual-level event time distributions, which may smooth out the sharp and localized increases in risk observed in KM curves. This discrepancy becomes more pronounced under decreasing hazard patterns with early event clustering, where steep cumulative hazard trajectories are observed (Cook, 2007; Andersen et al., 1993).

In this context, the proposed method 3 adopts an interval-averaged conditional hazard corresponding to the expected number of events within each interval from a counting process perspective. This formulation is mathematically aligned with the way nonparametric approaches implicitly average hazard within intervals. As a result, method 3 can preserve individual-level parametric modeling while more faithfully reproducing population-level KM curves, as demonstrated by improved agreement in visual predictive checks (VPCs) (Andersen et al., 1993; Fleming and Harrington, 2011).

Mathematical basis and compensatory role of method 3 in complex and relatively inflexible parametric models

In parametric analyses, model structures can become complex with the inclusion of covariates yet may still struggle to adequately capture the steepness of KM curves; in particular, exponential models with covariates (e.g., concentration and AUC) may have inherent structural limitations in representing the curvature of the cumulative hazard function. The proposed method 3 addresses this limitation by defining the conditional hazard as an interval-averaged quantity. This formulation allows repeated events to be interpreted as a counting process with time-varying intensity, linking the hazard within each interval to the expected number of events (Andersen et al., 1993; Fleming and Harrington, 2011).

In particular, pharmacokinetic covariates (e.g., concentration and AUC) typically change rapidly immediately after dosing, further accentuating the concavity of the cumulative hazard function in early phases. As a result, the residual term (derived from the Taylor series expansion in Section 2.5 and Supplementary File 17) becomes positive, leading method 3 to yield systematically larger hazard estimates than method 2.

Consequently, method 3 is better able to capture the dynamic features of the data, particularly the steep early-phase increases observed in KM curves. Therefore, method 3 can help address the limitations of covariate-based exponential models in capturing complex and rapidly changing hazard patterns and may serve as a useful alternative not only in such settings but also in more complex parametric models where steep KM trajectories are not adequately reproduced (Bonate, 2011).

Practical considerations and limitations in applying method 3

The proposed method 3 may exhibit overprediction in certain intervals under decreasing hazard patterns, which can be interpreted as a structural consequence of its cumulative hazard-based conditional formulation. In particular, when events are densely clustered in the early phase, this effect may be amplified in combination with highly flexible parametric models, leading to an overall increase in the estimated cumulative risk. This behavior reflects an inherent trade-off associated with the structural properties of method 3. Accordingly, model selection should be guided by the underlying hazard patterns and event distribution, with an approach that not only selects an appropriate model but also incorporates complementary methods to address the limitations of each modeling framework.

In addition, method 3 defines the conditional hazard using the time interval ( Δt ) between successive events. As a result, numerical instability may arise in the presence of tied events, where multiple events occur at the same time point. To address this issue, a small-time jittering strategy(0.1h) was applied in this study, which enabled stable parameter estimation and convergence within the NONMEM® framework. These findings suggest that, with appropriate preprocessing, the advantages of method 3 can be effectively utilized in real-world clinical datasets. Nevertheless, further work is warranted to develop more robust numerical stabilization approaches for broader applicability (Bauer et al., 2022).

Uncertainty and structural limitations of KM-based estimates

An important but often overlooked consideration in the evaluation of repeated event models is that the Kaplan–Meier (KM) estimator, which is widely used as a reference in visual predictive checks (VPCs), inherently involves both statistical uncertainty and structural limitations. As described in Section 2.1, KM-based extensions for recurrent events reset the time origin at each event and construct product-limit estimates by approximating inter-event intervals as independent binomial processes. This formulation does not fully capture the underlying probabilistic structure of recurrent event processes, particularly the dependence between successive events (Kaplan and Meier, 1958; Wang and Chang, 1999).

Moreover, while KM estimates may be relatively stable in early intervals with a large number of events, their uncertainty increases substantially in later intervals as the risk set diminishes, leading to wider confidence intervals. Therefore, discrepancies between parametric model predictions and KM estimates should not be interpreted solely as evidence of model misspecification but rather should be considered in light of both the structural characteristics of the data and the inherent limitations of the KM estimator (Kaplan and Meier, 1958; Wang and Chang, 1999).

Practical guidance for method selection based on data characteristics

The findings of this study suggest that no single method is universally optimal for recurrent event analysis; rather, method selection should be guided by the underlying data characteristics. While all three approaches demonstrated comparable predictive performance under relatively stable hazard conditions, real-world clinical data often exhibit complex, time-varying hazard dynamics.

When considering decreasing hazard patterns, the scenarios can be broadly categorized as follows.

In scenarios where events are sparse and the hazard changes gradually over time, method 2 may provide a reasonable approximation to nonparametric estimates while offering computational efficiency. In contrast, when early events are clustered—such as immediately following drug administration—or when strong dependence exists between events, resulting in rapidly changing hazard functions, models with sufficient inherent flexibility (e.g., log-logistic or Weibull base models) may still allow method 2 to perform reasonably well; however, underestimation may occur in such settings. In rapidly changing hazard settings, method 3, with its greater flexibility, can provide a more appropriate framework by better capturing these dynamic hazard patterns.

This advantage becomes more pronounced in structurally complex models that incorporate multiple covariates but remain limited in flexibility—such as exponential models with covariates—where method 3 can more effectively accommodate complex, time-varying hazard dynamics and yield improved agreement with nonparametric KM-based estimates. Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, a systematic sensitivity analysis was not conducted to quantitatively compare differences among the three methods across diverse data scenarios. In particular, factors such as event density, the magnitude of inter-individual variability, and the proportion of right censoring may substantially influence model performance. Our research was based on two datasets—a rat tumor recurrence dataset and a jittered AE dataset—and comparisons were primarily performed using VPCs. As such, the findings may be specific to the decreasing hazard patterns and structural characteristics shown in these datasets. Future research should incorporate simulation-based sensitivity analyses by varying the key factors such as event density, observation period, hazard dynamics, and covariate structures. Such analyses provide a more rigorous basis for refining the method selection guidelines presented in Table 8.

Moreover, external validation using datasets from multiple therapeutic areas and diverse event patterns encountered in real-world clinical settings can be performed to assess the generalizability of method 3. In doing so, datasets with scenarios in which recurrent events are accompanied by terminal events or competing risks should also be included since these occur in real-world clinical settings. In such settings, joint modeling frameworks are required to account for the dependence between recurrent and terminal events, and similarly, distinct modeling approaches are needed to describe competing risk scenarios. Such modeling frameworks may be further developed to improve generalizability (Ghosh and Lin, 2000; Rauch et al., 2018).

Furthermore, the present study primarily focused on decreasing hazard patterns, and systematic evaluation under increasing or more complex hazard dynamics was not conducted. Although decreasing hazards are common in many clinical contexts, scenarios with increasing or non-monotonic hazard patterns may arise, and the relative performance of the proposed methods under such conditions remains to be fully characterized. Such complex data structures fall outside the scope of the current study and should be addressed in future research to extend the applicability of the proposed methods (Ghosh and Lin, 2000).

Lastly, the present study (Part I) only focuses on recurrent event analyses with exact event times within the NONMEM® framework, while interval-censored events will be addressed separately in Part II. In practical applications, recurrent event data in NONMEM® are typically analyzed using exact event times, interval-censored events, right-censored observations, and time scales based on either gap time or calendar time. However, under alternative modeling frameworks, other data structures may also arise, including left censoring, mixed censoring, or irregular follow-up schedules with varying observation intervals. These more complex data configurations were not considered in the present study and should be considered in future research to evaluate the applicability and performance of the proposed methods under these alternative data structures.

Conclusion

Recurrent time-to-event (RTTE) analysis has been increasingly applied across diverse therapeutic areas, including hemophilia, hereditary angioedema, and pharmacovigilance (Wong et al., 2025; Garcia et al., 2025; Goulooze et al., 2026), underscoring the growing importance of selecting appropriate modeling frameworks for such data. In conclusion, the findings of this study highlight that no single method is universally optimal for recurrent time-to-event modeling, and that method selection should be guided by the underlying hazard patterns and data characteristics. Under constant hazard conditions, methods 1–3 yield equivalent results and can be used interchangeably. In contrast, under decreasing hazard patterns, the choice between methods becomes more consequential. When events are sparse and hazard changes are gradual, method 2 provides a practical and computationally efficient approach with good agreement to nonparametric estimates.

In settings characterized by early event clustering and rapidly changing hazard dynamics—particularly when parametric models lack sufficient flexibility to capture such patterns—method 3 emerges as an appropriate approach, as it more effectively captures steep cumulative hazard trajectories. For example, when covariate-augmented exponential models fail to adequately represent the temporal shape of the hazard, the interval-based conditional hazard formulation of method 3 provides a preferred modeling strategy. Accordingly, an integrated modeling framework that combines appropriate method selection with complementary methodological approaches is recommended to better capture the complexity of recurrent event data in clinical applications.
