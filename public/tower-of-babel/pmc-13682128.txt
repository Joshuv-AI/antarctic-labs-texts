Exome-wide association study reveals common and rare coding variants shaping chronic pain in 327,642 UK biobank participants
Authors: Xiang Ao, Goodarz Kolifarhood, Marc Parisien, Andrey Bortsov, Audrey V Grant, Luda Diatchenko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic pain is a major global health burden with a substantial but incompletely understood genetic basis. Although genome-wide association studies have identified multiple common-variant loci for pain-related traits, the contribution of rare coding variants to chronic pain susceptibility, biological mechanisms, and shared architecture across pain phenotypes remains unclear.MethodsWe performed an exome-wide association study across ten chronic pain phenotypes using whole-exome sequencing data from 327,642 European participants in the UK Biobank. Variant-level and gene-level association analyses were conducted to identify common and rare coding signals. Downstream analyses included conditional analysis, statistical fine-mapping, validation in non-European populations, replication in independent cohorts, cross-trait meta-analysis, colocalization, pathway and tissue enrichment, single-cell enrichment, summary-data-based Mendelian randomization, and estimation of heritability and genetic correlation from common and rare variants.ResultsWe identified 286 significant variant-level associations arising from 235 unique variants mapped to 117 genes, including 13 novel lead variants and 44 independent lead signals. Gene-level analyses identified ten significant genes driven predominantly by rare functional variants: ADAMTSL5, ANKRD12, ARID5A, DPP7, GMCL1, HCK, KIF20B, SLC13A1, UBR2, and ZNF558. Functional analyses implicated pathways related to nervous system development, axonogenesis, and synaptic organization, with enrichment in brain tissues and hTRPM8-expressing dorsal root ganglion neurons. Mendelian randomization analyses highlighted convergent regulatory effects across neural and immune-related tissues. Heritability analyses showed that rare coding variants explained a modest proportion of variance relative to common variants but exhibited larger average effects in the most damaging functional classes, particularly high-confidence loss-of-function variants. Genetic correlation analyses revealed shared genetic components among chronic pain conditions and positive correlations with selected psychiatric and nervous system disorders.ConclusionsThese findings provide a comprehensive map of coding variation underlying chronic pain and show that both common and rare coding variants contribute to its genetic architecture. Rare coding variants explain only a limited fraction of heritability but offer strong mechanistic insight by implicating discrete genes and pathways related to neuronal development, neuroimmune signaling, and tissue homeostasis. This work advances understanding of chronic pain biology and provides a foundation for future multi-ancestry studies and functional validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13073-026-01732-9.Keywords: Chronic pain, Exome-wide association study, Whole-exome sequencing, Rare coding variants, UK Biobank, Genetic architecture, Heritability, Genetic correlation, Pain genetics

Background

Chronic pain is a major global health burden, affecting 20–30% of adults worldwide [1, 2] and often comorbid with mental health problems, such as depression and anxiety [3, 4]. Twin studies estimate its heritability at 27–59% [5], highlighting a substantial genetic component. Understanding this genetic basis is crucial for precision pain management and treatment.

Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) on common variants have mapped numerous genetic loci for chronic pain, including 18 for chronic low back pain [6], 23 for chronic overlapping pain [7], 39 for multisite pain [8], 99 across 17 pain traits [9], and 125 linked to pain intensity [10]. Yet, common variants explained less than 20% of heritability, presenting a heritability gap (i.e., ‘missing heritability’ [11]) when contrasted with twin study estimates. Rare coding variants, which can disrupt protein function and exert a large functional effect, remain poorly studied and represent a promising source of this missing heritability.

Our prior rare variant study identified SLC13A1 as a gene associated with chronic back pain and multisite pain [12], providing early evidence that rare protein-altering variants contribute to pain susceptibility. Building on this, we leveraged 327,642 UKB exomes to perform a comprehensive exome-wide association study (ExWAS) across ten chronic pain phenotypes (Fig. 1). Our objectives were threefold: (i) identify and quantify the association of common and rare coding variants through variant- and gene-level analyses, (ii) translate these associations into biological meaning by integrative tissue, pathway, and cell-type enrichment, (iii) characterize the genetic architecture of chronic pain and assess genetic correlations with mental and nervous system disorders.

Methods

Study population

We analyzed whole-exome sequencing (WES) data from the UK Biobank (UKB, application number 20802), a prospective cohort of ~ 500,000 participants aged 40–69 years at recruitment (2006–2010) from across the United Kingdom. Ethical approval was granted by the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Ancestry assignment

Participants from four ancestry groups were included in this study. European participants were defined as UK Biobank individuals who self-reported as White British (UK Biobank field 21000) and were confirmed to have genetically inferred European ancestry via principal components analysis [13]. The three non-European subsets were inferred using UK Biobank genotyping array data and the combined Human Genome Diversity Project and 1000 Genomes Project (HGDP + 1kGP) reference panel, following the ancestry inference pipeline described by Koenig et al. [14]. Briefly, autosomal variants shared between the UK Biobank and HGDP + 1kGP were used to compute principal components in the combined dataset. The first six principal components were used as features to train a random forest classifier on reference individuals with known continental ancestry labels. Assignment to African, Central/South Asian, and East Asian genetically defined ancestry groups was restricted to individuals for whom this was concordant with self-reported ethnicity (UK Biobank field 21000), namely Black or Black British, Asian or Asian British, and Chinese, respectively. The four groups are hereafter referred to as European, African, Central/South Asian, and Chinese.

Chronic pain definition

In accordance with the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) definition of chronic pain [15] and the logic of our previous study [12], we identified cases based on participants’ responses to the baseline pain questionnaire regarding the presence of pain lasting for at least three months. In order to represent a wide spectrum of chronic pain conditions and enable powerful exome-wide association tests [16], eight self-reported chronic pain phenotypes were considered: seven pain traits at specific body sites (headache (acronym cHD), facial pain (cFA), neck or shoulder pain (cNS), stomach or abdominal pain (cSA), back pain (cBK), hip pain (cHP), and knee pain (cKN)) and one general pain (cGN) all over the body. As expected, substantial comorbidity across pain sites was observed, with overlapping case proportions ranging from 1.6% (between chronic facial pain and chronic knee pain) to 24.6% (between chronic back pain and chronic neck or shoulder pain) (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Nearly half of all pain cases reported multisite pain involvement, a pattern that has been reported previously and may reflect broader, system-level factors influencing pain perception (Supplementary Table 1) [7, 8, 17]. To capture the spectrum from localized to systemic pain etiologies, we further defined two composite pain phenotypes: chronic lone pain (cLP) and chronic overlapping pain (cOP). Participants who reported pain at a single body site were defined as chronic lone pain cases, while participants who reported pain at two or more body sites were chronic overlapping pain cases (Supplementary Fig. 1 C). The control group included participants who did not report pain at any of the listed body sites. Across the ten phenotypes, case numbers ranged from 3,377 for chronic facial pain to 90,414 for chronic lone pain, compared with 157,583 pain-free controls (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 1).

To further validate our findings in the clinical populations, we adopted a definition of clinically diagnosed pain conditions [18]. In brief, the definition was based on electronic health records, including the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9) diagnostic codes, ICD-10 codes, and death records. We defined clinical headache (ICD10 codes G43, G44, R51), facial pain (G50.0, G50.1), neck or shoulder pain (M54.2, M25.51), stomach or abdominal pain (R10.0, R10.1, R10.3, R10.4), back pain (M54), hip pain (M25.55), knee pain (M25.56) and general pain (M79.1, M79.7).

Together, this comprehensive phenotypic framework enabled the systematic dissection of genetic basis across distinct and overlapping chronic pain presentations, encompassing both localized and generalized characteristics within a well-documented population cohort.

Definition of mental and nervous system disorders

Mental and nervous system disorders were commonly reported comorbid with chronic pain [3, 4], they were defined according to the ICD-10 codes, as recorded in the UK Biobank hospital inpatient (UKB field 41270). The ICD-10 codes from F00 to G47 were used to define each disorder, detailed description of the disorders and ICD10 codes as well as the numbers is presented in Supplementary Table 2. In brief, participants were classified as cases if they had at least one recorded diagnosis corresponding to the relevant ICD-10 code. Controls of mental or nervous system disorders were defined as individuals without any recorded diagnosis of mental disorders or nervous system disorders, respectively.

Exome sequencing and quality control

To explore the contribution of coding variants to chronic pain, we used the in BGEN format whole-exome sequencing data (UKB field ID 23159) from the original quality functionally equivalent (OQFE) protocol, aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome, from the UKB. Details of sample preparation, sequencing and initial processing have been described previously [19, 20]. Variants with a call rate below 90% or a Hardy–Weinberg P-value less than 1 × 10–12 were excluded from downstream analyses.

Variant annotation

Rare variants (minor allele frequency (MAF) < 1%) were annotated using the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) release 109 [21] based on the GRCh38 human genome assembly and categorized according to their predicted functional consequences in canonical transcripts. Following our previous definition [12], rare loss-of-function (LoF) variants were those that: 1) were annotated by VEP as stop_lost, start_lost, splice_donor_variant, frameshift_variant, splice_acceptor_variant, or stop_gained; and 2) were not predicted to be a benign by PolyPhen [22], or Sorting Intolerant From Tolerant (SIFT) [23], or Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) [24] (i.e., PolyPhen < 0.15, SIFT > 0.05 or CADD < 15). Furthermore, LOFTEE [25] annotations were applied to distinguish high-confidence (HC_LoF) from low-confidence LoF variants.

Rare missense variants were identified by the missense_variant label in VEP and further evaluated using functional predictions from dbnSFP database (v4.4a) [26], which compiles functional prediction scores from 41 prediction algorithms, covering over 80 millions non-synonymous single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). A deleterious missense variant (del_mis) was defined as one for which more than one-fifth of non-null predictions were classified as “damaging”, “high”, or had a rank score greater than 0.9. A more permissive threshold, i.e., more than one-twentieth of non-null predictions, was used to identify possible deleterious missense variants (posD_mis). Rare synonymous variants were defined by the synonymous_variant annotation in VEP. The distribution of coding variants categories is summarized in Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2,3.

Whole-exome burden estimation of functional rare variant groups

To estimate the overall burden of rare coding variants across functional categories, we quantified the total number of variants per individual within each functional rare variant group and evaluated their association with chronic pain using linear regression models. The odds ratio (OR) and corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) were derived from the regression coefficients, and P-values were computed using Wald tests. To ensure model calibration, analyses were repeated with synonymous variant burden as a neutral baseline control.

This analysis provided a global estimate of the cumulative genetic load conferred by different classes of rare coding variants, independent of gene-level aggregation. By contrasting protein-altering and neutral variants, the approach allowed us to determine which functional variant classes exert measurable genome-wide effects on chronic pain risk and to prioritize categories with the greatest contribution to downstream gene-level discovery.

Genome-wide and exome-wide association study

Variant-level and gene-level association tests for common and rare variants were performed using SAIGE-GENE + (version 1.1.6) [27]. Variants with a minor allele count (MAC) greater than 20 were retained for variant-level association analyses, while rare variants with MAF < 0.01 were also aggregated at the gene level according to their functional annotations derived from VEP.

Gene-level association tests were conducted across three MAF thresholds (0 < MAF < 0.0001, 0 < MAF < 0.001, and 0 < MAF < 0.01) within each functional rare variant group (HC_LoF, del_mis, and posD_mis) as well as a damaging group (HC_LoF and del_mis) and a group of all functional variants (HC_LoF, del_mis, and posD_mis). The SKAT-O test implemented in SAIGE-GENE + was used to evaluate gene-level associations.

Genome-wide significance thresholds were set per phenotype at P < 5 × 10–8 for variant-level tests and P < 2.6 × 10–6 for gene-level tests, the latter corresponding to Bonferroni correction for approximately 19,000 protein-coding genes. Genome-wide association analyses were adjusted for age, age squared, sex, genotyping array, the first 40 genetic principal components, and dummy-coded recruitment sites. Exome-wide association analyses were adjusted for age, age squared, sex, the first 40 genetic principal components, and dummy-coded flow cells.

Variant-level and gene-level GWAS summary statistics were downloaded from AstraZeneca PheWAS Portal based on the union phenotypes of clinically diagnosed pain [18].

Conditional analysis of variant-level ExWAS results

To disentangle correlated association signals arising from local linkage disequilibrium (LD) and to identify statistically independent loci driving the observed associations, conditional analyses were conducted using the GCTA-COJO framework (v1.94.1) [28]. A stepwise model selection procedure (--cojo-slct) was applied to the exome-wide summary statistics of the ten pain phenotypes, using a genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) within a ± 1 Mb window centered on each lead variant. To ensure accurate LD estimation, the analyses employed an ancestry-matched LD reference panel constructed from 10,000 unrelated European participants from the UKB WES dataset.

Independent variants were defined as those retained in the final joint model output (*.jma.cojo) with conditional P < 5 × 10⁻⁸ and pairwise LD (r2 < 0.1) among the selected signals. To minimize spurious model selection due to sparse LD, genome-wide significant rare variants (MAF < 1%) were excluded from conditional analysis and retained as independent associations, as these variants exhibited negligible correlation with nearby variants in the reference panel.

Statistical significance of per phenotype replication of the identified independent lead variants in non-European populations was set for P < 0.05/(3 populations x n independent lead variants) in each chronic pain condition.

LOTO analysis of chronic overlapping pain signals

To determine whether the lead signals associated with chronic overlapping pain were attributable to specific component of pain traits, we conducted leave-one-trait-out (LOTO) sensitivity analyses. In each LOTO iteration, one site-specific pain trait was removed from the chronic overlapping pain case definition, and ExWAS was repeated using the modified phenotype while retaining the same covariates and analytical framework as in the primary analysis. The resulting association statistics for the lead chronic overlapping pain variants were compared across iterations to evaluate the extent to which each signal depended on individual component traits.

Novelty assessment of lead variants

To determine whether the independent lead variants identified in the variant-level ExWAS were reported previously associated with pain or pain-related phenotypes, a systematic evaluation was conducted using the LDtrait function from the LDlinkR package (v1.4.0; accessed October 14, 2025) [29, 30]. For each index variant, we queried the GWAS Catalog for previously reported associations and manually annotated traits containing pain-related keywords (e.g., analgesic, arthralgia, bowel dysfunction, dysmenorrhea, dysuria, fibromyalgia, headache, irritable bowel syndrome, mastodynia, metatarsalgia, migraine, myalgia, neuralgia, neuropathy, nociception, pain, sciatica, stomachache, toothache).

To account for linkage disequilibrium, proxy variants were identified using the British in England and Scotland (GBR) population from the 1000 Genomes Project (GRCh38), applying an r2 threshold of 0.6 and a ± 500 kb window around each index variant. Both index and proxy variants were then cross-referenced against the GWAS Catalog. Associations were classified as pain-related if the corresponding trait descriptions included one or more relevant keywords.

Variants with no direct or LD-proxy evidence for pain or related phenotypes were designated as novel in the context of pain genetics.

Replication of lead variant-pain associations in AllofUS, FinnGEN, MVP, and CLSA

Replication analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness and generalizability of lead variant-pain associations identified in the UKB variant-level ExWAS. Four large, independent cohorts were used for replication: the All of Us Research Program (AllofUS) [31], the FinnGen Study [32], the Million Veteran Program (MVP) [33], and the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (CLSA) [34]. The AllofUS cohort comprises over 400,000 participants from across the United States, with linked whole-genome sequencing and electronic health records (EHRs), representing diverse ancestral backgrounds. The FinnGen Study integrates nationwide Finnish health registry data with genotype information from approximately 500,000 participants, predominantly of Finnish ancestry. The MVP study includes around 650,000 U.S. veterans with genome-wide genotyping, comprehensive EHRs, and longitudinal follow-up; only participants of European ancestry were included for comparability. The CLSA cohort is a national cohort of over 50,000 middle-aged and older adults from across Canada, integrating genetic, clinical, and lifestyle data with a particular emphasis on aging-related phenotypes.

In the AllofUS cohort, replication analyses were conducted across multiple chronic pain phenotypes, including chronic headache, chronic facial pain, chronic neck pain, chronic abdominal pain, chronic back pain, chronic hip pain, chronic knee pain, chronic fibromyalgia. In addition, composite traits, i.e., chronic lone pain and chronic overlapping pain, were also included for replication. Pain phenotypes were derived using the All of Us OMOP-structured clinical datasets. For example, chronic headache was defined as having at least two clinical records (diagnoses) of headache for the same participant occurring at least 90 days apart. All other phenotypes were defined using analogous criteria. Controls were defined as individuals who reported zero pain in the past 7 days in the survey and who did not meet the corresponding case definition. All analyses were adjusted for age, sex, and 10 principal components (PCs). Only self-reported European American participants were included. For the gene-based burden tests, variants with minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.01 were included.

In FinnGen, GWAS summary statistics were retrieved for headache (G6_HEADACHE), low back pain (M13_LOWBACKPAIN), and fibromyalgia (M13_FIBROMYALGIA), corresponding to the UKB pain traits of headache, back pain and general pain. For the MVP cohort, summary data from genetically inferred ancestry (GIA) were retrieved for migraine (Phe_340), back pain (Phe_760), and abdominal pain (Phe_785), corresponding to UKB headache, back pain and stomach and abdominal pain. In the CLSA cohort, three chronic pain phenotypes were available for replication: migraine, knee pain, and back pain. Detailed traits description in the CLSA cohort can be referred to [35].

Replication was evaluated for each lead variant-pain pair within corresponding phenotypes from participants of European ancestry. To account for local LD, proxy variants were identified using the GBR population from the 1000 Genomes Project (GRCh38), applying an r2 threshold of 0.6 within a ± 500 kb window around each lead variant.

A locus-pain pair was considered replicated if either the lead variant or a proxy exhibited a significance of P < 0.05/n (n = the total number of lead variants tested in a pain condition) with a consistent direction of effect relative to the discovery analysis.

Meta-analysis

Meta-analyses were performed for both variant-level and gene-level genome-wide significant signals across the eight self-reported chronic pain phenotypes, i.e., headache, facial pain, neck or shoulder pain, stomach or abdominal pain, back pain, hip pain, knee pain, and general pain. The analyses followed an inverse-variance weighted fixed-effects model as implemented in the METAL [36], which combines effect estimates across traits while accounting for sample overlap and differences in standard error. For gene-level associations, beta coefficients and standard errors of burden tests were used as input statistics. To assess the extent to which genome-wide significant gene-level signals were driven by single pain phenotype, we conducted leave-one-trait-out (LOTO) meta-analyses. For each significant gene, the meta-analysis was repeated after excluding the pain that generated the significant signal.

Association was considered robust if its LOTO meta-analysis remained genome-wide significance (P < 2.6 × 10–6). Otherwise, the association was flagged as potentially pain-specific association.

ExWAS fine-mapping

To identify putative causal variants for pain from association loci, fine-mapping was performed using the susieR package (v0.12.35) [37, 38], which leveraged ExWAS summary statistics together with in-sample WES genotypes. Fine-mapping regions were defined as a ± 500 kb window centered on each lead ExWAS variant (P < 5 × 10–8), with overlapping regions merged to form a single locus. For each locus, the Sum of Single Effects (SuSiE) algorithm was run under default parameters, allowing for up to 10 independent 95% credible sets per region to capture multiple potentially independent causal signals. When multiple credible sets were detected within a locus, credible sets representing weaker association signals (higher P values) and exhibiting high LD (r2 > 0.8) with other credible sets were excluded to avoid redundant representation of the same association.

A credible set was considered an independent primary hit if it contained a variant with P < 5 × 10−8 and represented the strongest local signal. Secondary signals within the same region were retained only if they met genome-wide significance and exhibited log Bayes factor (BF) > 2, indicating meaningful improvement in model fit relative to a single-signal model. This BF filtering was essential to mitigate spurious multi-signal outputs occasionally produced by SuSiE when additional signals provide negligible explanatory power [32].

Genetic overlaps between lead SNP and gene loci were calculated using bedtool's version 2.17 intersect function [39], with Ensembl’s release 103 genes on GRCh38 coordinates [40].

Multi-trait colocalization analysis

To identify potential pleiotropic causal variants influencing multiple chronic pain disorders, we performed multi-trait colocalization analyses using HyPrColoc package (v0.0.2) [41]. This Bayesian algorithm tests for the presence of a causal variant across multiple traits under the assumption of a single causal variant per locus, while efficiently evaluating all possible trait combinations.

At each of the 235 pain-related variants, multi-trait colocalization was performed across the ten pain phenotypes that showed at least one genome-wide significant association within a ± 500 kb window of the lead variant. Overlapped regions were merged to define unique genomic loci. Analyses were performed using default variant-specific regional and alignment priors, as implemented in HyPrColoc.

Evidence for colocalization was determined based on posterior probability (PP) ≥ 0.25, following the empirical threshold recommended by the developers of HyPrColoc, which balances sensitivity and specificity by maximizing the true positive rate while minimizing false discoveries.

LOVO analysis and conditional analysis

To assess whether associations identified in gene-level ExWAS were driven predominantly by single variants or reflected aggregated effects of multiple rare variants, we performed a leave-one-variant-out (LOVO) sensitivity analysis. For each significant gene, all constituent rare variants were sequentially excluded, and the collapsing SKAT-O test was recalculated after each exclusion. Genes in which the association signal was substantially attenuated (i.e., the P values increased markedly) upon removal of a single variant were considered to be driven primarily by that variant, suggesting a single-variant effect underlying the gene-level association. Conversely, genes maintaining robust significance (i.e., minimal change in P value across exclusions) were interpreted as reflecting polygenic contributions from multiple rare coding variants within the locus.

To further access whether nearby variant-level ExWAS signals influenced the gene-level associations, we perform conditional analysis within a ± 500 kb window around each significant gene. Among the ten significant genes, only DPP7 gene was proximal to genome-wide significant variants. We reran the gene-level analysis for DPP7 by sequentially adding the neighboring significant variants as covariate to the model. The conditional P values were then compared to the unadjusted result to determine whether the gene-level association persisted after accounting for the nearby variant effects.

Enrichment analysis

To gain functional insights into the biological mechanisms underlying the identified genes, we conducted enrichment analyses across multiple annotation resources, including Gene ontology (GO) pathway [42], genotype-tissue expression from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) v8 [43], and DrugBank database [44]. GO pathway and DrugBank enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler R package [45] with default parameters, enabling systematic assessment of overrepresented biological processes and drug target classes among the associated genes.

Tissue-specific expression enrichment was evaluated using the GENE2FUNC module implemented in FUMA [46], based on GTEx v8 expression profiles across 54 human tissue types. This analysis tested whether the genes implicated in the ExWAS exhibited significant upregulation in specific tissues compared to background expression levels.

For all analyses, significance thresholds were defined at false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05, and only pathways or tissue types passing this criterion were considered significantly enriched.

Cell type enrichment

To explore the cellular contexts underlying genetic associations with chronic pain, we performed cell-type enrichment analysis using the enricher function from the clusterProfiler R package. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets were compiled from three human tissue sources most relevant to pain biology: blood (hBlood) [47], brain (hBrain) [48], and dorsal root ganglion (hDRG) [49]. Within the DRG dataset, neurons exhibiting similar functional characteristics were clustered together to enhance signal detection across related neuronal subtypes.

Gene expression matrices were processed and normalized using the NormalizeData function from the Seurat R package (v5.2.1) [50]. Marker genes for each cell type were identified with Seurat’s FindAllMarkers function, defining robust, cell type-specific gene expression signatures. These marker gene sets were subsequently used as reference annotations for the enrichment analysis.

Cell-type enrichment was then evaluated to determine whether genes implicated in the ExWAS were disproportionately higher expressed in specific cell populations across the analyzed tissues.

Summary-data-based Mendelian Randomization (SMR)

To investigate putative causal relationships between gene expression and chronic pain traits, we conducted summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) analysis using UKB ExWAS summary statistics. SMR was implemented using the SMR software (version 1.4.0) [51], which integrates association summary statistics and cis-expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) data to evaluate whether the association between gene expression and a phenotype is consistent with a shared causal variant rather than distinct but correlated signals. Cis-eQTL summary statistics were obtained from GTEx v8 [43], eQTLGen [52], and eQTLBrainMeta [53], focusing on immune- and nervous system–related tissues, relevant to pain biology. For each gene, the top cis-eQTL variant was used as the instrumental variable. To distinguish causal effects from confounding due to LD, we applied the heterogeneity in dependent instruments (HEIDI) test, implemented within SMR, using the GBR population from the 1000 Genomes Project as the LD reference (± 1 Mb window around the probe). Genes showing significant heterogeneity (P_HEIDI < 0.01) were excluded, as this pattern indicates that the observed association likely reflects linkage rather than a shared causal variant.

Associations were considered significant if they met the Bonferroni-corrected SMR threshold (P_SMR < 2.5 × 10⁻⁶) and passed the HEIDI test (P_HEIDI > 0.05) where available. Genes meeting both criteria were prioritized as putative causal candidates influencing chronic pain susceptibility through modulation of gene expression.

Heritability estimation and genetic correlation

To quantify the genetic contribution of coding variants to chronic pain and assess the shared genetic architecture among them, we estimated both heritability (h2) and genetic correlation (rg) using complementary approaches tailored for common and rare variants. Significant threshold for genetic correlation was set to P < 0.05.

For common variants, we applied linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC), [54, 55] using the baseline model LD scores derived from HapMap3 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) of the International HapMap Project phase 3 reference panel. LDSC estimates were computed using GWAS summary statistics from the same cohort, providing measures of SNP-based heritability and pairwise genetic correlations between chronic pain traits.

For rare variants, we estimated burden heritability and genetic correlation using burden heritability regression (BHR) [56], which models gene-based burden test statistics while accounting for the sparse allele frequency spectrum. Analyses were performed for each functional group and each order of allele frequency using default parameters. Heritability estimate for each group was aggregated from all allele frequency and were reported on the observed scale.

Estimate of heritability fraction explained by significant signals

To quantify the proportion of total heritability attributable to genome-wide significant variants and genes, we employed both heritability estimation from summary statistics (HESS) [57] and BHR [56] frameworks. HESS is a statistical method that estimates SNP-based heritability and local genetic covariance directly from association summary statistics while accounting for LD structure. We applied HESS (v0.5.4-beta) to quantify the contribution of genome-wide significant loci to the total SNP-based heritability of chronic pain disorders. The genome was first partitioned into approximately independent LD blocks (∼100 Kb to 25 Mb each) following established boundaries [58]. For each block, per-locus heritability was computed using the GBR population from the 1000 Genomes Project as the external LD reference panel. Overall heritability was summed up from the LD blocks and applied to compute local heritability fraction. LD blocks containing genome-wide significant associations were collected for their local heritability and fraction.

For rare variant–driven associations, the fraction of heritability explained by significant genes was estimated using BHR, leveraging outputs from the gene-based burden heritability estimation procedure.

Results

Overview of study population and phenotypes

We conducted a large-scale exome-wide association study leveraging whole-exome sequencing data from the UK Biobank (application number 20802), which recruited approximately half a million participants. Given the predominance of individuals of White British/European ancestry in the UKB cohort and the statistical power requirements of ExWAS, our primary analyses were restricted to participants of European ancestry, with validation of the main findings in non-European populations. After applying stringent quality control filters, a total of 327,642 European individuals were retained for downstream analyses, with a median age of 59 years at recruitment and 54.8% (n = 179,564) being female. In addition, 7,082 African, 8,446 Central/South Asian, and 1,430 Chinese participants were retained for validation analyses. Ten chronic pain phenotypes were evaluated in the study: headache, facial pain, neck or shoulder pain, stomach or abdominal pain, back pain, hip pain, and knee pain, general pain, lone pain and overlapping pain [7] (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 1).

Exome-wide variant-level analysis identified 286 associations

We performed variant-level ExWAS to study the contribution of coding variants in the ten chronic pain phenotypes using SAIGE-GENE + [27], Across all analyses, we identified a total of 286 genome-wide significant variant-pain associations (P < 5 × 10–8), originating from 235 unique variants mapped to 117 genes (Fig. 2a and b, Supplementary Table 4 and 5). Among these, 67 variants were associated with reduced risk (protective) and 168 with increased risk of chronic pain. The largest number of associations was observed for chronic headache (n = 143), whereas no significant associations were detected for chronic facial pain (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Table 5). As expected, we detected fewer rare variants (minor allele frequency (MAF) < 1%) than common variants, but they exhibited larger effect sizes compared to common variants (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 4–13).

To identify independent association signals potentially masked by linkage disequilibrium (LD), we conducted conditional analyses using GCTA-COJO [28]. In total, we identified 59 independent lead variant-pain associations, representing 44 unique lead variants (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Table 6). For chronic headache, the number of signals decreased from 143 to 15 following conditional analysis, reflecting high linkage disequilibrium among variants on chromosome 17 (Supplementary Fig. 14). To evaluate cross-ancestry generalizability of these lead variant-pain associations, we performed ExWAS in non-European UK Biobank populations. Of the 44 lead variants, seven variants were not present in any of the non-European populations (Supplementary Table 6). Of these 37 lead variants, 33 showed consistent direction of effect in at least one non-European population. At nominal significance (P < 0.05), ten variants replicated in at least one non-European population. After Bonferroni correction for the number of populations and the number of lead variants in each pain, we observed replication of one lead variant rs1431196 for chronic overlapping pain, in the Central/South Asian population with a consistent direction of effect (P = 4.1 × 10–4, Supplementary Table 6). Formal replication rates below Bonferroni-corrected thresholds are expected given substantially smaller non-European sample sizes and marked allele frequency differences between European and non-European populations. Linear regression of effect estimates between European and non-European populations showed generally low concordance, with only limited correlation observed in the Central/South Asian population (Fig. 3a-c). These results suggest that some coding-variant associations may be shared across ancestries, but broader evaluation will require substantially larger multi-ancestry sequencing datasets.

To evaluate the consistency of self-reported pain signals with clinically defined phenotypes, we compared effect sizes for lead ExWAS variants against corresponding estimates of clinical diagnosis-based pain analyses from AstraZeneca PheWAS Portal [18]. Linear regression revealed a significant correlation between the two sets of effect estimates (Fig. 3d), indicating that the associations identified from self-reported pain were broadly consistent with those obtained from clinically ascertained pain phenotypes. Among the 59 lead variant–pain associations, 14 were also detected using clinical pain phenotypes. All 14 exhibited concordant effect directions, with slightly larger effect-size magnitudes in the clinical pain analyses (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Table 7).

To determine whether the lead variants associated with chronic overlapping pain were driven by site-specific phenotypes, we conducted leave-one-trait-out ExWAS analyses by sequentially excluding each of the seven site-specific pain traits from the chronic overlapping pain definition. Exclusion of chronic musculoskeletal pain traits, including chronic neck/shoulder pain, chronic back pain, chronic hip pain, and chronic knee pain, resulted in loss of significance for nearly all chronic overlapping pain-associated signals, except for variant rs197393 (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Table 8). Together, these results suggest that the chronic overlapping pain associations were primarily driven by multiple musculoskeletal pain conditions but not by any particular one disproportionately, thus supporting pleiotropy.

Of the 44 lead variants, 13 had not been previously reported in the GWAS Catalog as associated with pain or pain-related phenotypes (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Table 9). Notably, one of the novel lead variants was the rs79447161 variant from chronic headache, located within an intronic region of the MAPT gene, which encodes the microtubule-associated protein tau and is known to be associated with several neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease, frontotemporal dementia, and Parkinson’s disease [59]. Intriguingly, this same variant also showed significance in our variant-level ExWAS on Parkinson’s disease (P = 7.3 × 10–9).

Replication analysis was conducted for each chronic pain condition in four independent cohorts. Of all the lead variant-pain pairs, we confirmed 12, 8, 6, and 3 associations (consistent effect direction and P < 0.05/number of lead variants in each chronic pain) in the All of US Research Program (AllofUS), FinnGen, Million Veteran Program (MVP), and Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (CLSA), respectively (Fig. 3f, Supplementary Table 10). Statistical fine-mapping with susieR [37, 38] identified at least one 95% credible set (CS) at all 59 loci, and 39 loci resolved to a single-variant credible set, providing high-resolution evidence for putative causal alleles (Supplementary Fig. 15–21, Supplementary Table 11).

Among the 235 pain-related variants, 197 were unique to a single trait, where 38 were shared across multiple pain phenotypes. Meta-analysis between them revealed that 225 of the 235 variants remained significant after multiple testing correction (P < 0.05/235), indicating pleiotropic effects across chronic pain (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Table 12). Subsequent multi-trait colocalization analysis identified 25 potential pleiotropic variants for chronic pain, including a notable signal on chromosome 15 (rs11073964), coding for missense variant of VPS33B protein. This variant shows colocalization across eight pain traits (except chronic facial pain and general pain), suggesting a shared regulatory mechanism underlying generalized chronic pain (Fig. 3g, Supplementary Fig. 22–46, Supplementary Table 13).

Despite the large number of associations, only three of the 286 signals originated from rare variants, underscoring the underrepresentation of rare coding variant in variant-level ExWAS results. To further delineate their contribution, we subsequently performed gene-level ExWAS focusing on functional rare variants.

Exome-wide gene-level analysis identified 10 associations

To assess the aggregate impact of rare coding variants on chronic pain, we conducted whole-exome burden analyses across functional categories of rare and common variants, which help prioritize the categories that potentially contribute to pain susceptibility. By computing whole-exome burden for different variant categories, including high-confidence loss-of-function (HC_LoF), deleterious missense (del_mis), possible deleterious missense (posD_mis), neutral (synonymous) and common variants, we found that rare del_mis variants affected nine chronic pain disorders (all except chronic facial pain), followed by posD_mis and HC_LoF variants, whereas synonymous and common variants exhibited minimal effects (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Table 14). We further observed heterogeneous effects across MAF bins, where lower MAF variants had larger impact on pain. The heterogeneity in effect size by functional consequences and MAF suggested that rare coding variants disproportionately shape the genetic architecture of pain and require rigorous analytic handling (Fig. 4b).

To increase the statistical power and biological interpretability and decrease bias from effect size heterogeneity, we next performed gene-level ExWAS with aggregation of rare variants by functional consequences and MAF bins. We assigned rare variants to six functional groups, i.e., group HC_LoF, del_mis, posD_mis, synonymous, damaging (a blended group of HC_LoF and del_mis variants), and functional (a blended group of HC_LoF, del_mis, and posD_mis variants). We conducted association tests on these groups individually across three different MAF bins (maximum MAF = 0.01%, 0.1% and 1%). These analyses identified ten genes, i.e., ADAMTSL5, ANKRD12, ARID5A, DPP7, GMCL1, HCK, KIF20B, SLC13A1, UBR2, and ZNF558, that reached a per phenotype exome-wide significance (P < 2.6 × 10–6), predominantly driven by HC_LoF and del_mis variants (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Table 15). Generalizability analysis in non-European populations didn’t report any significant results (P < 0.05, Supplementary Table 15).

Leave-one-variant-out (LOVO) analyses confirmed that these associations were not driven by single rare variants or by nearby significant common variants from the variant-level ExWAS (Supplementary Fig. 47 and 48), indicating polygenic contributions from multiple rare coding variants. Among the ten significant genes, eight (excluding HCK and SLC13A1) represented novel pain-associated loci not previously reported in the GWAS Catalog, whereas two genes, i.e., HCK and SLC13A1, were previously reported for pain [60, 61]. Except for ADAMTSL5 and GMCL1, all the other genes conferred increased pain risk.

None of the ten genes were shared among pain phenotypes, underscoring the phenotype-specific role of rare variants in chronic pain. Meta- and leave-one-trait-out (LOTO) meta-analyses further revealed that six genes (ARID5A, GMCL1, HCK, KIF20B, SLC13A1, and ZNF558) were unique to site-specific or widespread pain traits, whereas four genes (UBR2, ADAMTSL5, ANKRD12, and DPP7) exhibited broader, cross-phenotype effects (Supplementary Fig. 49, Supplementary Table 16). For instance, while SLC13A1 reached genome-wide significance in the meta-analysis, its signal dissipated in the LOTO meta-analysis, indicating that its association was driven primarily by chronic back pain (Fig. 4d). Conversely, UBR2 remained genome-wide significant in both analyses, highlighting a robust, pleiotropic contribution to chronic pain (Fig. 4e).

Importantly, none of these ten genes overlapped with those identified from the variant-level ExWAS, emphasizing the complementary and additive role of rare coding variants in shaping the genetic architecture of chronic pain beyond the effects of common variants.

Biological insights from the identified genes

To explore the biological implications of the genes identified from both variant-level and gene-level ExWAS, we conducted a series of functional analyses. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that the significant genes were predominantly involved in regulatory pathways related to nervous system development (6 out of 12) and synaptic organization (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Table 17). Consistently, tissue-specific enrichment analysis using expression data of 54 tissues from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) atlas identified enrichment in 11 tissues, including eight brain regions, underscoring the fundamental role of the nervous system in chronic pain pathogenesis (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Table 18). Enrichment analysis in DrugBank further implicated tetrahydrofolic acid (THF) as a potential pharmacological modulator of pain biology (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Table 19).

To investigate cellular specificity, we examined gene expression across human single-cell datasets from the brain (hBrain) [48], dorsal root ganglia (hDRG) [49], and peripheral blood (hBlood) [62]. In human brain, pain-associated genes showed the strongest but non-significant enrichment in oligodendrocyte (Oligo_50) and medial ganglionic eminence interneurons (cell MGE_249) (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Table 20). In human DRG, significant enrichment was observed in hTRPM8 neurons, specialized in processing thermal stimuli (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Table 21). In blood immune cells, expression enrichment was strongest but again non-significant in memory and immature B cells (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Table 22).

Summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) analysis further revealed convergent transcriptomic signals in both nervous system and immune-related tissues across all chronic pain traits (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Table 23). A prominent cross trait locus on chromosome 3p21, centered on RBM6, showed consistent causal effects across chronic neck or shoulder pain, chronic knee pain, and chronic overlapping pain, with expression effects extending across brain, peripheral nerve, muscle, blood, and immune tissues. Nervous system–specific effects included DCC for chronic back pain and chronic overlapping pain, and two genes on chromosome 20, i.e., GDF5 and UQCC1, linked to chronic knee pain. In contrast, immune-related expression effects were particularly evident for chronic knee pain, whereas chronic headache was uniquely associated with immune expression of TJP2 and CFDP1, suggesting distinct immunogenetic mechanisms among pain subtypes. For several loci, the heterogeneity in dependent instruments (HEIDI) test could not be performed due to limited SNP coverage within the ± 1 Mb of the top eQTL, precluding formal exclusion of LD-based confounding; thus, these results should be interpreted with caution. Nonetheless, the convergent SMR signals, particularly those implicating RBM6, suggest potential shared molecular mechanisms across chronic pain phenotypes, integrating nervous system regulation and immune activation as central pathways in chronic pain susceptibility.

Together, these results indicate that the biological signals identified by ExWAS are rooted to the brain and broader nervous system (Fig. 5d). Pathway enrichment highlighted processes related to nervous system development, axonogenesis, and synaptic organization, while tissue-level analyses showed predominant enrichment across multiple brain regions, pointing to central nervous system. Additional signals were observed in peripheral sensory neurons in DRG and immune-related contexts, the overall pattern points to the nervous system as the principal biological framework underlying the coding-variant associations with chronic pain.

Genetic architecture of chronic pain

We characterized the genetic architecture of chronic pain by quantifying genetic heritability () and correlation () from rare variants. We applied burden heritability regression (BHR) [56] to rare variants (, ) and linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) [54, 55] to common variants (, ), respectively. We observed modest heritability estimates from rare coding variants (mean = 0.0098), with the highest estimate in chronic facial pain ( = 0.014) and the lowest in chronic lone pain ( = 0.005). In contrast, common variants contributed to moderate heritability (mean = 0.073) ranged from 0.009 in chronic facial pain to 0.12 in chronic overlapping pain (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Table 24 and 25). We found positive genetic correlations among pain traits for both rare and common variants, with rare variants exhibiting moderate correlations (mean = 0.37), whereas common variants contributed a much stronger effect (mean = 0.91) (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Table 26). While genetic correlations from common variants were consistently significant across pain traits, rare variant correlations were less robust, particularly for chronic general pain, which showed a significant link only with chronic back pain, suggesting a distinct genetic architecture for chronic general pain at the rare variant level. These findings suggest that although different in relative contribution, both common and rare variants significantly impact chronic pain development, composing a polygenic underpinning of chronic pain.

We further assessed the genetic contribution of each rare variant group to pain. In general, we observed modest but detectable contributions from each group, with the strongest effects observed from the group “functional”, followed by the group “damaging” (Fig. 6c, Supplementary Table 24, Supplementary Fig. 50), highlighting the importance of protein-altering variants to pain. At the locus level, significant genes and variants accounted for a modest fraction of total heritability. Significant genes from gene-level ExWAS explained up to 5% of the rare variant heritability in chronic headache (Fig. 6d, Supplementary Table 27), whereas all significant ExWAS variants collectively accounted for as much as 9% of total heritability in chronic headache (Fig. 6e, Supplementary Table 28), with the remaining heritability distributed across many polygenic risk variants of individually small effect. Although coding variants conferred a limited contribution to pain heritability, these results reflect a concentration of rare variant heritability in a small number of genes and a broadly distributed common variant heritability across a larger set of loci.

We next compared effect sizes across variant classes to contrast the contribution of significant common and rare coding signals. By comparing common lead variants with rare functional variants from the ten genes identified by ExWAS, rare HC_LoF variants showed the largest mean effect sizes, followed by common lead variants, whereas deleterious missense and possible deleterious missense variants had more modest average effects. Synonymous variants showed the weakest effects overall. (Fig. 6f, Supplementary Table 29) Thus, although rare coding variants explain a smaller share of heritability, the most disruptive functional classes can carry larger per-variant effects than common lead signals.

Shared genetic architecture with mental and nervous system disorders

Given the high clinical comorbidity between chronic pain and mental and nervous system disorders [3, 4], we explored their shared genetic architecture for coding variants. Using 14 mental disorders and six nervous system diseases (Supplementary Table 2), we first assessed whether genome-wide significant loci were shared across traits. Of the 44 pain-associated lead variants, three also showed genome-wide significant association with mental or nervous system disorders (P < 5 × 10–8). Specifically, two variants, rs3874801 and rs79447161, mapping to gene LRRC37A3 and MAPT, respectively, were shared between chronic headache and Parkinson's disease (PD) (P = 2.5 × 10–10 and P = 1.1 × 10–8), and one variant, rs111675168, mapping to HLA-DRB3/5, was shared between chronic general pain and multiple sclerosis (MS) (P = 9.7 × 10–17). These genes had previously not been reported in relation to pain, but with Parkinson’s disease or multiple sclerosis [59, 63]. We didn’t find shared genes between them from gene-level ExWAS.

We then evaluated the genetic correlation () between chronic pain and mental and nervous system disorders at both common and rare variant level. At common variant level, mental and nervous system disorders mainly showed positive correlations with chronic pain traits (195 out of 200, mean = 0.42, Fig. 7a, Supplementary Table 30), with the most significant correlation between chronic overlapping pain and depressive episode (= 0.73, P = 7.6 × 10–27). High positive correlations were found in anxiety- and depression-related disorders, including depressive episode (DPE, mean = 0.71), major depressive disorder (MDD, mean = 0.71), major anxiety disorder (MAD, mean = 0.72), and other anxiety disorders (OAD, mean = 0.70). Positive correlations were consistently detected for sleep disorder (SD), transient cerebral ischaemic attacks (TCIA), and substance use disorders, including alcohol use disorder (AUD) and tobacco use disorder (TUD). At rare variant level, we identified fewer positive correlations, and at a lower strength (152 out of 200, mean = 0.23, Fig. 7b, Supplementary Table 30), and was correlated with common variant correlations (Pearson’s r = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.04–0.31, P = 0.0009). The most significant correlation was found between chronic overlapping pain and tobacco use disorder ( = 0.87, P = 4.3 × 10–3). While many of the significant common variant correlations were reflected at the rare variant level, especially for tobacco use disorder (TUD), there was a distinct profile in reaction to severe stress disorders (RSS), where seven significant rare variants correlations were reported compared to two from common variants.

Discussion

By coupling discovery analyses in European populations with extensive cross-ancestry validation, replication in independent cohorts, and clinical evaluation, our study delineates the contributions of both common and rare coding variation to chronic pain. These findings underscore the utility of whole-exome sequencing for uncovering novel risk loci for pain and other complex traits. Consistent with prior pain-related GWAS reports [7–10], common variants exhibited widespread, pleiotropic associations across pain phenotypes, suggesting a highly polygenic basis involving shared biological pathways. However, rare coding variants, though fewer in number, exerted larger effect sizes and their corresponding genes were largely non-overlapping with genes corresponding to common variant loci, indicating that they capture an independent and complementary dimension of genetic risk. This pattern mirrors trends seen in other complex traits, including neuropsychiatric diseases [64], heart failure [65], and clonal haematopoiesis [66], where the specific genetic loci identified through the study of rare coding variants were mostly different from those found using common variant analysis. Such divergence likely arises from differences in allele frequency, effect size, and analytical methods between common and rare variants. Besides, common variants often tag loci through linkage disequilibrium and may not directly perturb protein function, whereas rare functional variants can exert more direct consequences on protein structure or activity. Therefore, each variant group captures distinct aspects of the genetic architecture of complex traits. Overall, these findings from coding variants underscore the unique value of WES in uncovering the molecular underpinnings underlying complex traits that may remain invisible to traditional GWAS.

Our study identified several previously unreported coding variants and genes associated with chronic pain, providing mechanistic insights beyond prior GWAS findings. We identified 13 independent variants and eight genes that had not been previously linked to pain. Notably, rs79447161 in MAPT was associated with both chronic headache and Parkinson’s disease. Given that this variant lies within the MAPT gene, which encodes a key factor in neurodegenerative processes [59], i.e., microtubule-associated protein tau, its dual association suggests that tau-related neuronal dysfunction may contribute not only to neurodegeneration but also to pain perception and susceptibility to headache, highlighting a possible molecular intersection between chronic pain and neurodegenerative pathways. Another key finding, ARID5A, stabilizes pro-inflammatory mRNAs (e.g., IL6, STAT3, IL-17–responsive genes) [67, 68], providing a direct route from immune activation to pain chronification via cytokine signaling dysregulation, aligned with extensive evidence for immune–pain crosstalk [69, 70]. These discoveries expand the genetic landscape of chronic pain and highlight promising targets for functional studies and therapeutic development.

The gene-level ExWAS signals suggest that rare coding variation contributing to chronic pain is not dispersed randomly across genes, but converges on a limited number of biologically coherent pathways. Genes such as ANKRD12, ZNF558 and KIF20B implicate transcriptional control and neuronal development [71–73]; ARID5A, HCK, DPP7 and UBR2 highlight immune regulation and neuroimmune interactions [68, 74–76]; and SLC13A1 and ADAMTSL5 are consistent with sulfate homeostasis and extracellular-matrix biology, respectively, while the relevance of GMCL1 to pain remains less clear [77, 78]. Although these annotations do not establish direct roles for each gene in nociception, they fit the broader pattern from our enrichment analyses, which pointed to nervous-system development, axonogenesis, synaptic organization, brain tissues and hTRPM8 dorsal root ganglion neurons, together supporting a cautious model in which rare coding variants may influence chronic pain through partially overlapping effects on neuronal organization, neuroimmune signaling, and tissue homeostasis (Fig. 5d).

When we tested the identified variants in non-European populations, we observed only limited replication, which nevertheless should be interpreted with caution. Because the primary ExWAS was conducted in the much larger European cohort, the smaller African, Central/South Asian, and Chinese validation samples had substantially reduced power, particularly for rare coding variants. Differences in allele frequencies, linkage disequilibrium patterns, and ancestry-specific genetic architecture may further limit cross-population replication, which becomes especially noticeable for rare variants that can be more characteristic of a specific population with increasing geographic isolation [79–82]. From this perspective, Central/South Asian population shows the closest affinity to European populations among the African and the Chinese population [14, 83], and this is where we found modest but significant concordance and replicated lead lead variant rs1431196 (Fig. 3b). In addition, variation in demographic characteristics, environmental exposures, and healthcare access may contribute to phenotype heterogeneity across groups. Thus, low replication does not necessarily indicate absence of association in non-European populations, but rather highlights the need for larger, more diverse sequencing studies with harmonized phenotypes to assess the generalizability of these findings. Gene-level replication across ancestries would require full discovery analyses in adequately powered non-European populations, rather than the variant-level cross-ancestry replication feasible here. Thus, the All of Us study, which demonstrated that distinct loss-of-function variants in the same gene can produce comparable biological consequences across ancestries, is an example of such a resource, but thorough pain phenotyping is an added requirement [84].

On the other hand, the robustness of our identified lead ExWAS signals was supported by the high concordance between self-reported and clinically diagnosed pain results, suggesting that they capture genuine pain-related genetic effects rather than artifacts of questionnaire-based phenotyping. The slightly larger effect sizes observed in the clinical pain analyses are consistent with greater clinical specificity and reduced phenotypic noise in medically ascertained pain definitions. At the same time, the significant correlation between the two sets of effect estimates indicates that self-reported pain, despite its greater heterogeneity, is a valid and well-powered phenotype for genetic discovery, whereas clinically diagnosed pain provides complementary clinical validation.

Our fine-mapping analyses provided additional resolution at associated loci. For a subset of loci, credible sets resolved to a single variant, strengthening causal inference. Yet in many cases, credible sets remained larger, reflecting limitations of resolution imposed by linkage disequilibrium and sample size. Moreover, the SuSiE framework assumes accurate linkage disequilibrium estimates that may not always hold, underscoring that fine-mapping results should be interpreted probabilistically and ideally corroborated by functional assays [85]. Similarly, our multi-trait colocalization provided evidence of shared genetic signals across multiple pain phenotypes at several loci. Notably, some common variants showed pleiotropic effects across diverse pain sites, whereas rare gene-level signals appeared more phenotype-specific. This contrast suggests that while some mechanisms may confer broad pain susceptibility, others may underlie site-specific or trait-specific risk.

Consistent with prior pain studies, our functional analyses provided convergent evidence that the genetic architecture of chronic pain is rooted in nervous system development, neuronal signaling, and immune modulation. Pathway enrichment highlighted some previously discovered processes that are involved in the development and plasticity of neural circuits, such as axonogenesis [7, 86], neurogenesis [8, 9, 87], and synaptic organization [8]. Maladaptive neuroplasticity has been implicated in the transition from acute to chronic pain [87, 88], collectively illustrated the significance of connectivity and plasticity in neural pain circuits to chronic pain pathogenesis [87, 89]. Tissue-specific enrichment further demonstrated that pain-associated genes were preferentially expressed in brain regions such as the amygdala, cerebral cortex, hippocampus, and basal ganglia, underscoring the pivotal role of central nervous system circuits in pain perception and maintenance [90, 91].

Single-cell expression analyses additionally pinpointed TRPM8 sensory neurons in the DRG, which are key sensory neurons mediating cold nociception [49] and major contributors to cold allodynia and neuropathic pain [92]. Beyond detecting cool temperatures, TRPM8 afferents regulate sensory gain and interact with inflammatory and neuroimmune signals, positioning them as modulators of chronic pain states [93]. The enrichment of pain-associated genes in this specific neuronal subtype therefore suggests that genetic susceptibility may act in part by altering excitability or signaling in peripheral sensory neurons that gate nociceptive input. This provides a mechanistic link between our identified coding variants, peripheral neural pathways, and clinically relevant features of chronic pain such as cold hypersensitivity.

SMR analysis identified genes whose expression was potentially causally linked to chronic pain, with shared regulatory signals, most notably at RBM6, across neural and immune tissues. These findings suggest that inherited genetic variation may influence chronic pain primarily through nervous system pathways, while immune regulation introduces an additional layer of genetic contributors. Together, these results support a neuroimmune perspective in which genetic effects on neuronal function interact with immune-related processes [94–97]. The convergence across central, peripheral, and immune pathways is consistent with the view that chronic pain is a system-level condition shaped by both genetic architecture and environmentally responsive biological pathways [94, 95].

DrugBank enrichment linked chronic pain to tetrahydrofolate (THF), suggesting potential for therapeutic prioritization and drug repurposing. THF supports neuroplasticity through neurogenesis, neurotransmitter synthesis, epigenetic regulation [98], and is essential for the hematopoiesis and peripheral nerve function [99]. Enrichment in neural circuits and peripheral TRPM8-expressing DRG neurons indicates THF-dependent pathways may modulate peripheral neuroplasticity and influence neuroimmune interactions relevant to pain chronification. However, these associations require cautious interpretation and mechanistic validation. Still, the intersection between genetic pathways and existing drug targets highlights a promising avenue for translational exploration with individualized treatment, especially because THF deficiency was already associated with peripheral neuropathy with some indication for treatment efficacy [100].

Heritability and genetic correlation analyses further revealed that chronic pain is underpinned by a dual genetic architecture, where common variants contribute broadly to polygenic risk while rare coding variants exert concentrated effects on specific genes. In terms of heritability, we observed that rare coding variants explained a modest heritability (~ 1% on average), whereas common variants accounted for a substantially larger component (~ 7%) spread across the whole genome. This modest rare-variant heritability is expected for exome-based analyses, which capture only ~ 2% of the human genome. Accordingly, the missing heritability of chronic pain is not fully explained by rare coding variants in our data, and likely remains distributed across rare non-coding regulatory variants, structural variants, gene–gene and gene–environment interactions, and residual phenotypic heterogeneity [11]. Nonetheless, relative to the number of variants contributing to heritability, rare coding variants appear enriched for biological impact, in agreement with previous studies of complex human phenotypes [101]. Notably, the rare-variant heritability presented an oligogenic architecture in which a few genes carry the cumulative effects. In contrast, common coding variants showed a broadly polygenic pattern, with the effects encompassing far more variants. Together, these findings show that rare variants pinpoint discrete pain genes with high biological impact, whereas common variants contribute widespread polygenic pain risk, underscoring why rare coding variants, despite their smaller aggregate heritability, offer disproportionately strong mechanistic insight into chronic pain biology.

Furthermore, our genetic correlation analyses on rare variants reflected previous GWAS findings by demonstrating the shared liability between chronic pain and mental and nervous system disorders [8–10]. Using BHR, we show that protein-altering rare variants also exhibit strong positive correlations with depression, anxiety, and tobacco use disorder, particularly reaction to severe stress disorders, whereas correlations with neurodegenerative diseases, including multiple sclerosis, Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s diseases, remain minimal. This indicates that the genetic overlap with psychiatric traits reflects a core feature of pain biology rather than an incidental consequence of polygenicity. The convergence of rare and common variant architectures on the similar set of psychiatric traits suggests that inherited vulnerability in affective, stress-responsive, and arousal-related pathways contributes to pain chronification. In particular, the disproportionally high rare-variant correlation with reaction to severe stress disorders highlights stress reactivity, threat detection, and emotional memory formation as potential mechanistic bridges linking pain and psychiatric vulnerability.

Despite the large sample size and integrative analytic framework, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the study was conducted primarily in white British/European participants from the UKB and the generalizability of genetic associations to other ancestries is limited; future efforts in large multi-ancestry exome and genome sequencing cohort will be essential to assess population-specific effects. Second, although independent replication was performed where suitable external datasets were available, not all identified associations were replicated. This may reflect limited statistical power in the replication cohorts, particularly for variants with modest effects, lower allele frequencies, or phenotype-specific associations, as well as differences in phenotype definition (e.g., self-reported pain versus electronic health records-based pain), case ascertainment, and sample composition. Therefore, non-replication should be interpreted cautiously and may not necessarily indicate a lack of true association. Third, the definition of self-reported chronic pain in our study may limit its clinical application. Compared with ICD codes-based pain definition, self-reported pain is more susceptible to recall bias, subjective interpretation, and phenotypic heterogeneity, increasing the risk of diluted genetic signals and reducing clinical specificity. Fourth, while rare variant analyses benefited from the large WES dataset, statistical power remains limited for ultra-rare alleles and gene-by-environment interactions, leading to potential underestimation of their contribution. Fifth, tissue and single-cell enrichment analyses rely on reference expression data from mostly healthy donors, which may not capture disease-relevant expression dynamics. Finally, although our integrative analyses provide biological context for the identified associations, validation of these findings will require replication in independent and clinically refined cohorts, broader multi-ancestry sequencing studies, and functional investigation of prioritized variants and genes in cellular and animal models, to confirm biological mechanisms and translate findings into therapeutic insights.

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive map to date of coding variation underlying chronic pain susceptibility. Our analyses reveal this susceptibility arises from the combined effects of common and rare coding variants, converging on neurodevelopmental and neuroimmune pathways, with expression signals spanning central nervous system regions and peripheral sensory neurons. Notably, strong genetic overlap with psychiatric disorders, particularly post-traumatic stress disorder, suggests shared stress-response and emotional processing pathways. These findings position chronic pain as a system-level disorder shaped by integrated neuronal and immune mechanisms, providing a foundation for precision diagnostics and targeted therapeutics.

Conclusions

In summary, this study provides a comprehensive exome-wide view of chronic pain and shows that both common and rare coding variants contribute to its genetic architecture. While common variants underlie broadly distributed polygenic risk, rare coding variants act through more concentrated effects on specific genes and offer greater mechanistic resolution. The associated signals converge on pathways related to neuronal development, neuroimmune regulation, and tissue homeostasis, supporting chronic pain as a system-level disorder shaped by interacting neural and immune mechanisms. Together, these findings expand the genetic landscape of chronic pain and provide a foundation for future functional studies, multi-ancestry validation, and more biologically informed approaches to pain research.
