A Relativity-Based Framework for Statistical Testing Guided by the Independence of Ancillary Statistics: Methodology and Nonparametric Illustrations
Albert Vexler, Douglas Landsittel

Abstract
Summary A test statistic is typically constructed to discriminate effectively between competing hypotheses. In contrast, we propose and examine a framework that shifts attention to ancillary statistics—quantities whose distributions remain invariant under the tested hypotheses. Rather than directly optimizing discriminatory power, the proposed approach seeks to construct test statistics that exhibit relative independence from ancillary structures. We show that reducing the dependence between a test statistic and a vector of ancillary statistics can yield the most powerful (MP) decision-making procedure. We establish a Basu-type independence result and show that certain forms of MP test statistics characterize the underlying distribution. These principles are analyzed through decision-theoretic arguments and illustrated via modifications of classical nonparametric procedures, including the Shapiro–Wilk, Anderson–Darling, and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests, as well as a test for the center of symmetry. Simulation studies confirm the practical advantages of the proposed method. The framework is simple to implement and provides a theoretically grounded strategy for enhancing the power of statistical tests in practice.



Keywords:

Ancillary statistics, Characterization, Basu’s theorem, Most powerful tests, Nonparametric tests, Distance correlation, Goodness-of-fit

1
Introduction

The development of decision-making mechanisms lies at the heart of statistical theory and underpins a wide range of inferential procedures in practice.
This paper explores a strategy for constructing, improving, and comparing test statistics, emphasizing performance gains achieved through structural modifications guided by the principle of independence from ancillary statistics.

To illustrate the ideas of this paper, consider a simple example. Suppose we observe a sample of
n
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) data points
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
with variance
1
, and our goal is to test the hypothesis
H_{0}:\mathrm{E}(X_{1})=0
versus
H_{1}:\mathrm{E}(X_{1})>0
. A natural choice of test statistic is based on the sample mean
\bar{X}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}/n
, which discriminates between the hypotheses via the rule: we reject
H_{0}
if
n^{0.5}\bar{X}_{n}>C
, where
C
is a test threshold.
The law of large numbers and the central limit theorem justify this choice by ensuring that
\bar{X}_{n}
converges to its expectation and has asymptotically distinct distributions under
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
. This reflects a classical strategy: selecting a statistic with strong discriminatory power.

Alternatively, within a relativity-based framework, one may focus on constructing or modifying test statistics to be (exactly or approximately) independent of certain ancillary quantities—statistics whose distributions are invariant (or approximately invariant) under the null and alternative. For instance, continuing with the example above, the sample variance,

S^{2}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\bar{X}_{n})^{2}/n,
is ancillary in this context. By seeking a test statistic that is independent of
S_{n}^{2}
, one can develop or refine procedures that exhibit improved performance.
More broadly, this idea can guide both the construction of new test statistics and the modification of existing ones, by reducing their dependence on a relevant set of ancillary statistics.
For example, modifying the sample mean to reduce its dependence on the sample variance can yield a more powerful test when the distribution of
X_{1}
is skewed
(
Vexler and
Hutson 2024
)
. It is worth noting that, when
X_{1}
follows a normal distribution, the statistic
\bar{X}_{n}
is independent of the sample variance
S^{2}_{n}
, and can represent a most powerful test statistic in the Neyman–Pearson sense.

That is, by identifying a collection of statistics with no discriminatory ability—denote this set by
A
—one can construct a function of the
data that is independent of
A
, and thereby obtain a statistic that is highly efficient for testing the hypotheses of interest.

In this context, an intriguing question arises: how should the set
A
be chosen to ensure maximal testing efficiency? This leads to several related inquiries. For example, can we define
A
so that a statistic independent of
A
yields a most powerful test? In practice, if several ancillary statistics
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
are available, which among them should be used to guide the transformation of a test statistic? Should
A
be selected to characterize the data distributions under the null and alternative hypotheses, or chosen as a maximal ancillary statistic in some formal sense? These questions are examined in this research, but many relevant aspects remain open for further investigation in future research.

Recently,
Vexler and
Hutson 2024
showed that transforming a test statistic
T

to achieve independence from a relevant ancillary statistic
A
can yield a new statistic
T_{N}

with greater power than the original
T
. In this paper, we associate the term “Most Powerful (MP) Test” with ancillarity by defining a class of relevant ancillary statistics such that independence between a test statistic and this class plays a central role in achieving efficient test performance.

We adopt a theoretical formalism in which, under the considered decision-making structure, an MP mechanism exists—for example, the Neyman–Pearson likelihood ratio test. In many practical settings, however, an MP test may be unavailable. In such cases, we interpret the term “MP” as a theoretical ideal: a target that can be closely approached by approximating the underlying assumptions and structural principles discussed in the methodological sections of this paper. As an application, we consider nonparametric tests for normality that exemplify this perspective.

Ancillarity underpins many foundational statistical concepts
(
Lehmann and
Scholz 1992
;
Ghosh
et al. 2010
)
. Perhaps one of the most widely recognized results in the study of ancillary statistics is Basu’s theorem
(
Ghosh 2002
)
. In simple terms, Basu’s theorem states that if one has a complete sufficient statistic—containing all the information about an unknown parameter—and an ancillary statistic—containing no information about the parameter—then these two statistics are independent.

It appears that we are particularly interested
in a type of conversion of Basu’s result. However, it is important to note that a direct or naïve reversal of the classical Basu theorem—e.g., concluding sufficiency from independence—is generally false
(
Kagan and
Malinovsky 2013
)
.

This paper deals with an extension of Basu’s theorem and its conversion-type results to decision-making fields. We propose and examine the relativity-based framework, showing relevant examples. A consequence of this perspective is characterizations of the normal and negative exponential distributions. In analogy to these results, we refer the reader to
Pfanzagl 1968
for an example of characterizing the one-parameter exponential family through the existence of MP test statistics.

We emphasize the practical value of the proposed methodology in goodness-of-fit testing. Here, the application of the proposed theoretical method is simple, clear, and efficient.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the necessary notation and preliminaries. Section
3
develops a decision-theoretic approach that motivates the construction of test statistics with reduced dependence on ancillary structures and establishes conditions under which such procedures are MP. Moreover, a result in the spirit of Basu’s theorem is reformulated and proved within a decision-theoretic context. The theoretical implications are illustrated through Examples
3.1
-
3.3
.
We also demonstrate that certain forms of MP test statistics implicitly characterize the underlying data distribution.
Section
4
presents nonparametric applications, including ancillary-guided modifications of classical tests for normality and a new test for the center of symmetry. Section
5
concludes with a discussion of methodological implications and potential directions for future research. Proofs of the main results are given in the Appendix, and additional technical derivations, along with several auxiliary remarks, are provided in the online Supplement.

2
Some notation and preliminaries.

Without loss of generality and to simplify the exposition of the main objectives of this paper, we introduce the following formal notation.

Assume we observe data denoted by
D
, and our goal is to test a null hypothesis
H_{0}
against an alternative
H_{1}
. Let
T=T(D)
be a real-valued test statistic constructed from
D
, such that the hypothesis
H_{0}
is rejected when
T(D)>C
, for some fixed threshold
C
. We denote the probability and expectation under
H_{k}
by
\Pr_{k}
and
\mathrm{E}_{k}
, respectively, for
k\in\{0,1\}
.

Throughout this paper, we assume that the distributions
\Pr_{0}
and
\Pr_{1}
of
D
are absolutely continuous with respect to a
\sigma
-finite measure
\tau
, defined over a class
\Upsilon
of measurable subsets of a space
\mathbb{X}
, in which
D
takes values. Then, there exist nonnegative generalized density functions
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
with respect to
\tau
such that for all
\upsilon\in\Upsilon
and
k\in\{0,1\}
,

{\Pr}_{k}(D\in\upsilon)=\int_{\upsilon}f_{k}(x)\,d\tau(x).

Note that
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
need not belong to the same parametric family of distributions.

Define
f^{T}_{0}
and
f^{T}_{1}
as the probability density functions of the statistic
T(D)
under
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
, respectively. Then, for each
k\in\{0,1\}
and any threshold
t\in\mathbb{R}^{1}
,

{\Pr}_{k}\{T(D)\leq t\}=\int_{\mathbb{X}}I\{T(x)\leq t\}f_{k}(x)\,d\tau(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}f^{T}_{k}(u)\,du,

where
I(\cdot)
denotes the indicator function.

In this setting, we assume that there exists some most powerful (MP) test statistic, denoted by
\Lambda(D)
.
For example, in many situations involving simple hypotheses, the Neyman–Pearson Lemma implies that the likelihood ratio
\Lambda(D)=f_{1}(D)/f_{0}(D)
defines the MP test statistic, assuming
f_{0}(x)>0
and
f_{1}(x)>0
for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
.
In the multivariate case,
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
are interpreted as joint densities with respect to a dominating measure
\tau
; see
Lehmann 1950
.

A statistic
A=A(D)
is said to be
ancillary
if its distribution is invariant under the hypotheses, i.e.,
f^{A}_{0}(u)=f^{A}_{1}(u)
for all
u
in the support of
A
. Thus,
A
cannot be used to discriminate between
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
.

Let
f_{k}^{T_{1},T_{2}}(u,v)
denote the joint density of two statistics
T_{1}(D)
and
T_{2}(D)
under
H_{k}
, and let
f_{k}^{T_{1}\mid T_{2}}(u,v)
denote the conditional density of
T_{1}(D)
given
T_{2}(D)=v
under
H_{k}
. If
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are independent under
H_{k}
, then, for all
u\in\mathrm{supp}(T_{1})
and
v\in\mathrm{supp}(T_{2})
,
f_{k}^{T_{1},T_{2}}(u,v)=f_{k}^{T_{1}}(u)\,f_{k}^{T_{2}}(v)
.

For conceptual simplicity, it will be assumed that in cases where a researcher plans to employ a test statistic
S(D)=L(\Lambda(D))
, where
L(u)
is a monotonically increasing function and
\Lambda(D)
is MP, the test statistic in use is
T(D)=W(S(D))
, with
W=L^{-1}
. In such cases, we treat
T(D)
as the MP test statistic.

We now restate a
strengthened version of a result from
Vexler and
Hutson 2024
, whose proof is revisited and streamlined in the present paper for completeness.

Let
A=A(D)
be a statistic satisfying
f_{1}^{A}=f_{0}^{A}
; that is,
A
is ancillary. Suppose the test statistic
T=T(D)
can be written as a function of two components,
T_{N}
and
A
, via
T=\psi(T_{N},A)
, where
\psi
is a bivariate function. Assume further that
T_{N}
and
A
are independent under both
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
. The following result holds.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
T_{N}
be a statistic such that there exists a strictly increasing function
L(u)>0
for which
f_{1}^{L(T_{N})}(u)=uf_{0}^{L(T_{N})}(u)
, for all
u
. Then the test that rejects
H_{0}
for large values of
T_{N}
dominates the test based on
T
in terms of power.

Proof.

See Appendix.
∎

This proposition illustrates that modifying a test statistic to be independent of an ancillary statistic can improve the power of the original test.

Remark 2.1
.

In connection with the condition
f_{1}^{L(T_{N})}(u)=uf_{0}^{L(T_{N})}(u)
, for all
u
, in Proposition
2.1
, we observe the following.
Assume we have a test statistic
Y=Y(D)
, and the likelihood ratio
L^{Y}(u)=f^{Y}_{1}(u)/f^{Y}_{0}(u)
is a strictly increasing function with an inverse function
W(u)
. In this case,
Y
can be transformed to the form
Y_{N}=L^{Y}(Y)
, and

f^{Y_{N}}_{k}(u)=\frac{d}{du}{\Pr}_{k}\{L^{Y}(Y)\leq u\}=\frac{d}{du}{\Pr}_{k}\{Y\leq W(u)\}=f^{Y}_{k}(W(u))\frac{d}{du}W(u),\quad k\in\{0,1\}.

This implies

0<f^{Y_{N}}_{1}\left(u\right)/f^{Y_{N}}_{0}\left(u\right)\,=\,f_{1}^{Y}\left(W(u)\right)/f_{0}^{Y}\left(W(u)\right)\,=\,L^{Y}(W(u))\,=\,u.

Proposition
2.1
may alternatively be derived under the assumption that the identity

f^{L(T_{N})}_{1}(u)=uf^{L(T_{N})}_{0}(u)
holds at
u=L(T_{N})
only (see the Appendix for details).

For example, according to
Hájek
et al. 1967
, for location parameter testing problems,
L^{Y}(Y)
can be monotonic if and only if
f^{Y}_{0}
and
f^{Y}_{1}
are strongly unimodal
(
Hájek
et al. 1967
, pp. 32–33)
.

3
Converse-type results on ancillarity and test optimality: examples and characterizations

In this section, Proposition
3.1
addresses the natural question arising from Proposition
2.1
: what constitutes an optimal choice of the ancillary statistic

A
to ensure that the modified test statistic becomes most powerful (MP). Proposition
3.2
presents a result analogous to Basu’s theorem, reformulated and proven within a decision-theoretic context. Proposition
3.3
further illustrates how the proposed theoretical principles can be used to characterize the distribution of the data. The proofs of the propositions are provided in the Appendix. Examples
3.1
-
3.3
involving tests for location and scale are provided to illustrate the theoretical framework.
Additional insights and implications of the theoretical results are discussed in Remark
3.1
and in the Supplementary Material (see Remarks S1.1 and S1.2).

Assume that
V=V(D)
denotes a set of ancillary statistics with a joint density satisfying

f^{V}_{1}(v)=f^{V}_{0}(v)
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let the mapping
D\mapsto\left(T_{N},V\right)
be injective with a measurable inverse.
Assume
T_{N}
is a test statistic that is independent of
V
under
H_{k}
,
k\in\{0,1\}
, and that there exists a strictly monotonic function
L(u)>0
such that
f^{L(T_{N})}_{1}(u)=u\,f^{L(T_{N})}_{0}(u).
Then
T_{N}
is MP.

Informally, Proposition
3.1
states that if a test statistic
T_{N}
is independent of a set of ancillary statistics
V
, and the pair
(T_{N},V)
suffices to reconstruct the underlying data, then the test based on
T_{N}
is MP.

Remark 3.1
.

Proposition
3.1
is plausibly related in spirit to Lemma 1 in
Kagan 2002
, though formulated, proved, and employed here in a decision-theoretic context.

An auxiliary result concerning data transformations and ancillary statistics is provided in the supplementary material (see Remark S1.1).

To state a result in the spirit of Basu’s theorem, we formulate the following proposition.

Proposition 3.2
.

Assume the test statistic
T_{N}
is complete under
H_{k}
, for
k=0
and/or
k=1
.
If
T_{N}
is MP, then
T_{N}
is independent of
V
under both
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
.

Example 3.1

(Tests of the location in symmetric models)
.

Assume we observe i.i.d. data points
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
that provide
D=\left\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\right\}
.
Let
X_{1}
have a symmetric distribution with characteristic function
\phi_{X}(t)\neq 0
, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{1}
. Define
\bar{X}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}/n
, and
\sigma^{2}=\text{var}(X_{1})
. We consider the problem of testing
H_{0}:

\mu=0
against
H_{1}:

\mu>0
, where
\mu
is the center of symmetry such that
f^{X_{1}}(\mu+u)=f^{X_{1}}(\mu-u)
for every
u\in\mathbb{R}^{1}
, and where
f^{X_{1}}
denotes the unimodal density function of
X_{1}
. A commonly used test statistic in this setting is
T=n^{0.5}\bar{X}_{n}/\sigma
.

According to
Behboodian 1990
and
Hamedani and
Volkmer 2003
,
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are symmetric if and only if
X_{j}-\bar{X}_{n}
is symmetric for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Given
X_{j}-\bar{X}_{n}
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and
T
, we can reconstruct
D
, since
X_{j}=X_{j}-\bar{X}_{n}+\sigma T/n^{0.5}
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Define a linear combination
Z_{n}=a_{1}(X_{1}-\bar{X}_{n})+\ldots+a_{n}(X_{n}-\bar{X}_{n})
, where
a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{1}
,
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. We have
Z_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\left(a_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}/n\right)
, and thus

\mathrm{cov}\left(\bar{X}_{n},Z_{n}\right)=\mathrm{E}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\mu)^{2}\left(a_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}/n\right)=0
.

By virtue of the Cramér–Wold theorem (i.e., since any linear combination of the components of the vector
\mathbf{V}=\left[X_{1}-\bar{X}_{n},\ldots,X_{n}-\bar{X}_{n}\right]^{\top}
is uncorrelated with
T
), it follows that
T
and

\mathbf{V}
are independent when
X_{1}
is normally distributed, where the operator
⊤
denotes transposition. The ratio
L(u)=f_{1}^{T}(u)/f_{0}^{T}(u)
is exponential in
u
, due to the normality of
T
under both hypotheses. This satisfies the condition
f_{1}^{L(T)}(u)=uf_{0}^{L(T)}(u)
required by Proposition 3.1. For a technical illustration of this principle, see Example
3.2
.
Thus, the conditions of Proposition
3.1
are satisfied.

We can also note that if
X_{1}
is normally distributed and
\sigma
is known, then
T
is a complete statistic
(
Lehmann and
Casella 2006
, see Theorem 6.22)
.

Building on Example
3.1
, we present a result that can characterize the normal distribution. Suppose the vector
\mathbf{V}
is ancillary with respect to the location parameter
\mu
; see, for example,
Welsh 2011
for discussion. This condition can also be treated in terms of the characteristic function of
\mathbf{V}
, denoted by

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathbf{V}}\left(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\exp\left\{\mathrm{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}\left(X_{j}-\bar{X}_{n}\right)\right\}\,=\,\mathrm{E}\exp\left\{\mathrm{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}X_{j}-\mathrm{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}/n\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\exp\left\{\mathrm{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}\left(t_{j}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}t_{k}/n\right)\right\}\,=\,\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi_{X}\left(t_{j}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}t_{k}/n\right),

where
\mathrm{i}^{2}=-1
,
t_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{1}
for
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and
\phi_{X}
, the characteristic function of
X_{1}
, is assumed to satisfy
\phi_{X}(\mu+t)=\phi_{X}(\mu-t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{1}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. random variables such that
X_{1}
has a symmetric unimodal distribution about
\mu
and satisfies
\mathrm{E}X_{1}^{2}<\infty
. Suppose that the sample mean
\bar{X}_{n}
is a complete statistic under either
H_{0}
or
H_{1}
. Then,
X_{1}
follows a normal distribution if and only if
T=n^{0.5}\bar{X}_{n}/\sigma
is the MP test statistic for the location parameter.

It is worth noting that, in a broad class of models, statistics based on
\bar{X}_{n}
may serve as MP test statistics. For instance, consider testing the null hypothesis
H_{0}
that
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. observations from a density function
f_{0}
, against the alternative
H_{1}:

X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. from
f_{1}(u)=\exp\left(\theta u+\gamma\right)f_{0}(u)
, where
\theta
and
\gamma
are unknown; see
Vexler and
Hutson 2018
.

An additional characterization of the distribution via the form of an MP test is presented in Example
3.2
(see also
Pfanzagl 1968
in this context).

For testing the hypotheses considered in Example
3.1
, the statistics commonly used in practice are
T=n^{0.5}\bar{X}_{n}/\sigma
and the Wilcoxon signed rank test statistic.
Proposition
3.3
raises a concern regarding the comparative performance of these procedures: the
t
-test may be superior to the Wilcoxon test when the underlying distribution deviates from normality; see
Vexler and
Yu 2018
for discussion in this context.

Rosenblatt and
Benjamini 2013
showed that the Wilcoxon signed rank test is more efficient than the
T
-based test under the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}\colon 0<\mu\leq 1
, where
X_{1}\sim(1-\mu)N(0,1)+\mu N(\theta,\sigma^{2})
and the parameters
\theta
and
\sigma
are known.
Furthermore,
Shiraishi 1986
proved that the asymptotic power of the Wilcoxon signed rank test equals that of the MP test under the local alternative
H_{1}\colon X_{1}\sim(1-\mu)G(x)+\mu\{G(x)\}^{2}
, where
\mu=\delta/n^{0.5}
,
G(x)
is a known logistic distribution, and
\delta
is fixed.
Shiraishi 1986
also concluded that the numerical values of the asymptotic relative efficiency of the Wilcoxon test with respect to the
T
-based test show no loss, even under general contaminated alternatives.

Example 3.2

(Tests of the location in an asymmetric exponential model)
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
be a sample from the negative exponential distribution

F_{e}(x)=\left[1-\exp\left\{-(x-\mu)/\sigma\right\}\right]I(x>\mu),

with location parameter
\mu
and scale parameter
\sigma>0
. Consider testing hypotheses concerning
\mu
. Let
X_{(1)}\leq X_{(2)}\leq\cdots\leq X_{(n)}
denote the order statistics based on
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
.
Define the vector
\mathbf{V}=[V_{1},\ldots,V_{n-1}]^{\top}
, where
V_{i}=X_{(i+1)}-X_{(i)}
for
i\in[1,\ldots,n-1]
. The vector
\mathbf{V}
is ancillary
(
Buehler 1982
)
, since

\displaystyle\Pr\left\{\bigcap_{i=1}^{n-1}\left\{V_{i}<v_{i}\right\}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{x_{1}<\ldots<x_{n}}I\left\{\bigcap_{i=1}^{n-1}\left\{x_{i+1}-x_{i}<v_{i}\right\}\right\}n!\prod_{i=1}^{n}f^{X_{1}}(x_{i})\,dx_{1}\ldots dx_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{z_{1}<\ldots<z_{n}}I\left\{\bigcap_{i=1}^{n-1}\left\{z_{i+1}-z_{i}<v_{i}\right\}\right\}n!\prod_{i=1}^{n}f^{X_{1}}(z_{i}+\mu)\,dz_{1}\ldots dz_{n},

where
f^{X_{1}}
is the density function of
X_{1}
. Given
X_{(1)}
and
\mathbf{V}
, the original sample can be reconstructed via the recursion:

X_{(2)}=V_{1}+X_{(1)},\,X_{(3)}=V_{2}+X_{(2)},\ldots,\,X_{(n)}=V_{n-1}+X_{(n-1)}.

Ferguson 1967
established the independence between the statistic
T=X_{(1)}
and the vector
\mathbf{V}
. Assume, for simplicity, that when testing the location parameter
\mu
, we consider the null hypothesis
H_{0}:\mu=\mu_{0}
, and let
\mu_{1}>\mu_{0}
be a possible alternative value. The ratio

L(u)=f^{T}_{1}(u)/f^{T}_{0}(u)=e^{(\mu_{0}-\mu_{1})/\sigma}\,h(u)^{n-1},\quad\text{for }u>\mu_{1}>\mu_{0},

with

h(u)=\frac{1-e^{-(u-\mu_{1})/\sigma}}{1-e^{-(u-\mu_{0})/\sigma}},\quad h^{\prime}(u)=\frac{e^{-(u-\mu_{1})/\sigma}-e^{-(u-\mu_{0})/\sigma}}{\left(1-e^{-(u-\mu_{0})/\sigma}\right)^{2}}>0.

Thus,
L(u)
is strictly increasing. In a manner similar to Remark
2.1
, we may consider the monotonic transformation
T_{N}=L(T)
as a theoretical device, which satisfies
f^{T_{N}}_{1}(u)=uf^{T_{N}}_{0}(u)
.
Then, we conclude that the conditions of Proposition
3.1
hold, and therefore
T
is an MP test statistic.

Now suppose
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. observations from a continuous distribution. Redefine the ancillary vector

\mathbf{V}=[X_{(2)}-X_{(1)},X_{(3)}-X_{(1)},\ldots,X_{(n)}-X_{(1)}]^{\top}.

According to
Govindarajulu 1966
,
T
and
\mathbf{V}
are independent if and only if
X_{1}
follows the negative exponential distribution
F_{e}(x)
. Thus, if
T=X_{(1)}
is an MP test statistic for the location, then
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
must follow the negative exponential distribution. This yields a characterization of the negative exponential distribution via the form of the MP test statistic in this decision-making problem.

Example 3.3

(Tests of the scale (rate) of a gamma distribution)
.

Let observations
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. from a gamma distribution with the scale parameter
\theta
(the rate parameter equals
1/\theta
) and the shape parameter
\kappa
. The random variables
X_{i}/\bar{X}_{n}=(\theta Y_{i})/(\theta Z)=Y_{i}/Z
, where
Y_{i}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\kappa,1)
and
Z\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(n\kappa,1/n)
,
i\in[1,\ldots,n]
. The distribution of the vector
\mathbf{V}=[X_{1}/\bar{X}_{n},\ldots,X_{n}/\bar{X}_{n}]^{\top}
is Dirichlet with parameter vector
[\kappa,\ldots,\kappa]^{\top}
, and it does not depend on
\theta
.
According to
Lin and
Stoyanov 2022
, the statistics
\bar{X}_{n}
and
\mathbf{V}
are independent.
When
T=\bar{X}_{n}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(n\kappa,\theta/n)
, the analysis of the ratio
L(u)=f^{T}_{1}(u)/f^{T}_{0}(u)
is the same as in Example 2.1.
Therefore, in this setting,
\bar{X}_{n}
serves as an MP test statistic for the scale parameter
\theta
.

In relation to Proposition
3.1
, a discussion on using distance correlation to compare test statistics with respect to their dependence on ancillary vectors is provided in the Supplementary Material (see Remark S1.2).

4
Distribution-free applications

This section presents simple examples that demonstrate the proposed technique in a transparent and practically applicable manner.
The following analysis examines ways to adapt existing test mechanisms using the framework developed in Section
3
.

Section
4.1
explores a simple modification of classical tests for normality. The example is intended to be clear and self-contained, and it captures elements that may generalize to broader contexts of goodness-of-fit testing.

Section
4.2
addresses the distance covariance as a measure of dependence between a test statistic and a corresponding ancillary vector. Section
4.3
examines the role of distance correlation in the construction and evaluation of composite test statistics. In particular, it develops an ancillary-guided approach for optimally combining two test statistics, leveraging their respective strengths while minimizing dependence on ancillary structure. These ideas are applied to nonparametric testing for the center of symmetry.

4.1
A straightforward approach to improving goodness-of-fit tests

We consider the problem of testing for normality based on a sample
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
.
Let the underlying distribution of
X_{i}
,
i\in[1,\ldots,n]
, be denoted by
F(u)
, which is assumed to be unknown.

According to Proposition
3.1
, the vector

\mathbf{V}\,=\,\left[F\left(|X_{1}|\right)-F\left(-|X_{1}|\right),\ldots,F\left(|X_{n}|\right)-F\left(-|X_{n}|\right)\right]^{\top}

serves as an example of ancillary statistics that can be particularly effective for constructing a test of normality, assuming
F(u)
were known, since
\Pr\left(|X_{1}|\leq u\right)=F(u)-F(-u)
.
Using the empirical estimator of
\Pr\left(|X_{1}|\leq u\right)
based on

|X_{1}|,\dots,|X_{n}|
, we approximate
\mathbf{V}
by
\mathbf{\hat{V}}=n^{-1}\left[R_{1}^{+},\ldots,R^{+}_{n}\right]^{\top}
,
where
R^{+}_{i}
denotes the rank of
|X_{i}|
,
i\in[1,\dots,n]
.

Define the order statistics
|X|_{(1)},\ldots,|X|_{(n)}
based on
|X_{1}|,\dots,|X_{n}|
.
We invoke the following result: for any symmetric
F
, the random vectors
\mathbf{\hat{V}}
,
[\mathrm{sign}(X_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{sign}(X_{n})]^{\top}
, and

[|X|_{(1)},\ldots,|X|_{(n)}]^{\top}
are mutually independent
(
Hájek
et al. 1967
, p. 40)
.

This independence, along with Proposition
3.1
, motivates the development of tests for normality against symmetric alternatives based on the observations

\mathrm{sign}(X_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{sign}(X_{n}),|X|_{(1)},\ldots,|X|_{(n)}.

For example, the Anderson-Darling test (AD), the Cramér–von Mises test (CvM), and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (KS) involve the empirical distribution function of

X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
. We propose modifications to these tests
based on the following transformation: define
Z_{i}=n(X_{i}-\bar{X}_{n})/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(X_{i}-\bar{X}_{n}\right)^{2}
, with
\bar{X}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}/n
, and
apply the AD, CvM and KS test strategies based on
|Z|_{(1)},\ldots,|Z|_{(n)}
for assessing the null hypothesis
\Pr(|Z|<u)=2\Phi(x)-1
, where

\Phi(u)=(2\pi)^{-0.5}\int_{-\infty}^{u}\exp(-t^{2}/2)dt
, resulting in the modified versions of the tests, denoted as mAD, mCvM, and mKS, respectively.
Regarding the equivalence between the statements ”
Z\sim\Phi(u)
” and ”
|Z|\sim 2\Phi(u)-1
” under the assumption that
Z
is symmetrically distributed,
we refer the reader to
Vexler 2020
.

The suggested approach above uses an empirical approximation of the theoretical principle shown in Proposition
3.1
.
Its efficiency should be evaluated thoroughly, for example, through extensive Monte Carlo simulations.
In this regard, we note that our attempts to modify the Shapiro–Wilk test, for example, by incorporating the expectations of
|X|_{(i)},i\geq 1,
or employing L-estimators based on
|X|_{(1)},\ldots,|X|_{(n)}
in the SW manner, did not result in noticeable improvements.
Motivated by empirical evaluations, the modification (mSW) of the SW test that we suggest is as follows: consider the data
D=\big\{-|Z|_{(1)},\ldots,-|Z|_{(n)}
,
|Z|_{(1)},\ldots,|Z|_{(n)}\big\}
,
and compute the classic SW test statistic based on
D
. Under

H_{0}
, the mSW statistic is distributed independently of the parameters of the null distribution of the observations.

To evaluate the extent of power loss when the proposed modifications are applied to asymmetric alternatives, we include the Spiegelhalter test (S) for comparison, as it is specifically constructed for testing normality against symmetric alternatives
(
Spiegelhalter 1977
)
.

.

4.2
Asymptotic dependence and center symmetry testing

4.2.1
Dependence between the ancillary vector and the test statistic

Assume that data
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are independent copies of the underlying data
D
; that is,
D_{1}
,
D_{2}
, and
D
are i.i.d. We define
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{V}(D)=\left[v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\right]^{\top}
,
\mathbf{V_{1}}=\mathbf{V}(D_{1})=\left[v_{11},\ldots,v_{1n}\right]^{\top}
, and
\mathbf{V_{2}}=\mathbf{V}(D_{2})=\left[v_{21},\ldots,v_{2n}\right]^{\top}
as the vectors, each consisting of the
n
corresponding ancillary statistics
v_{ki}
, for
i\in\left[1,\ldots,n\right]
and
k\in\{1,2\}
.
Denote the test statistic by
T
. Let the mapping
D\mapsto\left(T(D),\mathbf{V}\right)
be injective (with a measurable inverse).

According to
Székely
et al. 2007
, when

\mathrm{E}\left\{|T|+\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}v_{j}^{2}\right)^{0.5}\right\}<\infty,

the distance covariance

\displaystyle 0\,\leq\,\mathrm{dCov}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma\left(n/2+3/2\right)}{\pi^{n/2+3/2}}\int_{{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}}}\frac{\left|\mathrm{E}e^{\text{i}tT+\text{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}s_{j}v_{j}}-\mathrm{E}e^{\text{i}tT}\mathrm{E}e^{\text{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}s_{j}v_{j}}\right|^{2}}{t^{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}s_{j}^{2}\right)^{n/2+0.5}}dtds_{1}\ldots ds_{n}

equals zero if and only if
T
and
\mathbf{V}
are independent, where
\text{i}^{2}=-1
.

Define

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}(D,D_{k})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(v_{i}-v_{ki}\right)^{2}-\varkappa,\,\,\varkappa=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}2\mathrm{var}(v_{i}),\,\,k\in\{[1,2\}.

We then obtain the following evaluation of the dependence between
T(D)
and
\mathbf{V}(D)
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
b_{1}=1
,
b_{2}=-2
, and
R=\sum_{k=1}^{2}b_{k}\left|T(D)-T(D_{k})\right|+\mathrm{E}\left|T(D)-T(D_{1})\right|
.
Then, the distance covariance

\mathrm{dCov}
satisfies the following relationships:

\displaystyle 0\,\leq\,\mathrm{dCov}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[\left|T(D)-T(D_{1})\right|\sum_{k=1}^{2}b_{k}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(v_{j}-v_{kj}\right)^{2}\right\}^{0.5}\right]

(1)

\displaystyle+\,\mathrm{E}\left|T(D)-T(D_{1})\right|\mathrm{E}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(v_{j}-v_{1j}\right)^{2}\right\}^{0.5}\,\,\,=\,\,\,\mathrm{E}\left[\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(v_{j}-v_{1j}\right)^{2}\right\}^{0.5}R\right].\quad

If, in addition, we assume that
\mathrm{E}\left(T\right)^{2}<\infty
and
\mathrm{E}|\Delta_{n}(D,D_{1})|^{6}<\infty
, then
\mathrm{dCov}
satisfies the expression:

\displaystyle\mathrm{dCov}\,=\,n^{0.5}\mathrm{E}\left[\left\{\frac{\Delta_{n}(D,D_{1})}{2\varkappa^{0.5}}-\frac{\Delta_{n}(D,D_{1})^{2}}{8\varkappa^{3/2}}\right\}R\right]+n^{0.5}\,O\left(\left(\mathrm{E}|\Delta_{n}(D,D_{1})|^{6}\mathrm{E}\left|T(D)-T(D_{1})\right|^{2}\right)^{0.5}\right),\qquad

as
n\to\infty
.

The proof is provided in the supplementary material.

Remark 4.2
.

For various scenarios, the evaluation of
\mathrm{dCov}
, as as obtained in Proposition
4.1
, can be simplified by expressing

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left\{\Delta_{n}(D,D_{1})R\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{E}\left[\left\{\left(v_{i}-v_{1i}\right)^{2}-\varkappa\right\}R\right],

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left\{\Delta_{n}(D,D_{1})^{2}R\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{E}\left[\left\{\left(v_{i}-v_{1i}\right)^{2}-\varkappa\right\}\left\{\left(v_{j}-v_{1j}\right)^{2}-\varkappa\right\}R\right].

This formulation can enable one to exploit structural similarities among the summands; see Section
4.3
. For example, depending on the test statistic and data structure, it may be shown that

\mathrm{E}\left\{\left(v_{i}-v_{1i}\right)^{2}\,R\right\}=\mathrm{E}\left\{\left(v_{i}-v_{1j}\right)^{2}\,R\right\}
, for all
i
and
j
.

For further relevant technical evaluations, including asymptotic results and illustrative calculations, see Remarks S1.3–S1.5 in the Supplementary Material.

Consider scenarios where the bivariate vectors

[T(D)-T(D_{1}),n^{0.5}\Delta_{n}(D,D_{1})]^{\top}
and
[T(D)-T(D_{2}),n^{0.5}\Delta_{n}(D,D_{1})]^{\top}

are asymptotically normally distributed, for example, by applying the multivariate central limit theorem
(
Bentkus 2005
)
. In such cases, a Lyapunov-type bound in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and symmetry-based arguments may yield

n^{0.5}\mathrm{E}\left\{\left|T(D)-T(D_{1})\right|\Delta_{n}(D,D_{k})\right\}=o(1)
,
k\in\{1,2\}\}
,
as
n\to\infty
. According to Proposition
4.1
and to Remarks S1.3–S1.5 in the Supplementary Material, this can imply that the test statistic
T
and the ancillary vector
\mathbf{V}
are asymptotically independent.
Assume further that
T
and
\mathbf{V}
can reconstruct
D
, so that the mapping
D\mapsto(T,\mathbf{V})
is injective with a measurable inverse. These conditions may be satisfied by a class of candidate test statistics
T
, each paired with a corresponding ancillary vector
\mathbf{V}
. In this case, Proposition
3.1
may be employed to compare such statistics according to the rate at which asymptotic independence from ancillary structure is attained.

4.3
Test for the center of symmetry

Motivation.

In health-related research, the treatment effect is typically defined as the difference between post- and pre-treatment measurements.

In many health studies, assuming symmetry in outcome changes is reasonable, particularly when a treatment is expected to have a consistent impact across individuals, resulting in a symmetrically distributed shift in outcomes.
For example, when evaluating a medication intended to lower cholesterol, it is reasonable to expect that patients will experience reductions in cholesterol levels that result in a symmetric shift of the outcome distribution. From a statistical perspective, one may assume that improvements are measured as differences between paired observations drawn from the same distribution family, adjusted for location, and that these differences then follow a symmetric distribution.

Assuming symmetry simplifies statistical procedures, particularly those testing shifts in central tendency such as the mean or median. In paired data settings, interest typically lies in whether the distribution of within-pair differences is symmetric about zero.

To this end, we consider i.i.d. observations
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
drawn from an unknown, symmetric, unimodal, and absolutely continuous distribution
F
with finite variance
\sigma^{2}
. Following
Bickel and
Lehmann 2012
, the natural location parameter for
F
, denoted by
\nu
, corresponds to its center of symmetry.
Our objective is to test the hypothesis:

H_{0}:\nu=0\quad\text{versus}\quad H_{1}:\nu>0.

To address this issue, we consider the simple t- and sign statistics
T_{t}=n^{0.5}\,\bar{X}_{n}/S_{n}
and
T_{s}=2n^{-0.5}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(I(X_{i}>0)-0.5)
, where
S^{2}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\bar{X}_{n})^{2}/(n-1)
.

Our results from an illustrative power simulation study are presented below. Power simulations were conducted with a sample size of
n=100
, where data were generated under the following symmetric scenarios:
(1).
X_{1}\sim N(0.25,1)
;
(2).
X_{1}=y_{1}/y_{2}-y_{3}/y_{4}+0.25
, where
y_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,4\}
, are independent,
y_{1},y_{3}\sim\mathrm{Exp(3)}
, and
y_{2},y_{4}\sim\chi_{3}^{2}
;
(3).
X_{1}=\exp(y_{1})-\exp(y_{2})+0.9
, where
y_{i}
,
i\in[1,2]
, are independent,
y_{1},y_{2}\sim N(0.1,2^{2})
.
The tests
T_{t}
and
T_{s}
were performed with
0.05
critical values, and Monte Carlo power estimates were based on
10,000
replications.

In Case (1), the tests based on
T_{t}
and
T_{s}
yield the empirical estimates of the power as
0.809
and
0.608
, respectively, with
T_{t}
being the most powerful in this scenario. In Cases (2) and (3), the Monte Carlo power of the
T_{t}
-based test is considerably lower, at
0.149
(Case (2)) and
0.088
(Case (3)), compared to that of the
T_{s}
-based test, which achieves power levels of
0.999
and
0.928
, respectively. This indicates a significant advantage of the
T_{s}
test in Cases (2) and (3).
The sign test is asymptotically MP for the double exponential distribution
(
Hoeffding and
Rosenblatt 1955
)
.

These considerations motivate the combination of the statistics
T_{t}
and
T_{s}
in the simple form

T_{c}=(T_{t}+aT_{s})/c,

where
c^{2}
is the sample estimator of

\mathrm{var}(T_{t}+aT_{s})
, where, for large values of
n
,

\mathrm{var}(T_{t}+aT_{s})\approx 1-4a\mathrm{E}\left\{(X_{1}-\nu)I(X_{1}<0)\right\}/\sigma+4a^{2}p(1-p),

with
\nu=\mathrm{med}(X_{1})=\mathrm{E}X_{1}
,
p=\Pr\{X_{1}<0\}
.

To simplify explanations in this study, we assume without loss of generality that the combined test takes the theoretical form

T_{c}=c^{-1}\left\{n^{0.5}\bar{X}_{n}/\sigma+2an^{-0.5}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(I(X_{i}>0)-0.5\right)\right\},

where

c^{2}=1-4a\mathrm{E}\left\{(X_{1}-\nu)I(X_{1}<0)\right\}/\sigma+4a^{2}p(1-p).

Approximately optimal combined test.
As in Example
3.1
, the relevant vector of ancillary statistics is given by

\mathbf{V}=[X_{1}-\bar{X}_{n},\ldots,X_{n}-\bar{X}_{n}]^{\top}.

Our goal is to determine the values of
a
that reduce the dependence between
\mathbf{V}
and
T_{c}
.
To achieve this, one could consider different dependence measures between the random vector
\mathbf{V}
and the statistic
T_{c}
, potentially leading to an accurate identification of such values of

a
. In this section, to simplify the analysis, we adopt a straightforward rationale based on the following observation.
We analyze the closeness of the joint characteristic function

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\exp\left\{\mathrm{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}s_{j}\left(X_{j}-\bar{X}_{n}\right)+\mathrm{i}s\left(T_{c}-\mathrm{E}T_{c}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\,=\,\mathrm{E}\exp\left\{\mathrm{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(X_{j}-\nu)\left(s_{j}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}s_{k}/n\right)+\mathrm{i}s\left(T_{c}-\mathrm{E}T_{c}\right)\right\}

to the product of the corresponding characteristic functions:

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\exp\left\{\mathrm{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}s_{j}\left(X_{j}-\bar{X}_{n}\right)\right\}\,\mathrm{E}\exp\left\{\mathrm{i}s\left(T_{c}-\mathrm{E}T_{c}\right)\right\},

where
\mathrm{i}^{2}=-1
, and
s,s_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{1}
,
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Within this framework, the Cramér–Wold theorem asserts that
\mathbf{V}
and
T_{c}
are independent, if
\mathbf{\tilde{V}}=[X_{1}-\nu,\ldots,X_{n}-\nu]^{\top}
and
T_{c}
are independent.
Then, we approximate the values of
a
that minimize the dependence measure
\mathrm{dCov}
, which quantifies the dependence between
T_{c}
and
\mathbf{\tilde{V}}
.

Approximation to the distance covariance between
T_{c}
and
\mathbf{\tilde{V}}
.

According to Section
4.2.1
, we consider

\Delta_{n}(D,D_{1})=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left((X_{i}-X_{1i})^{2}-\varkappa\right),\,\,\,\varkappa=2\sigma^{2},

where

5
Conclusion

This paper has introduced a relativity-based perspective for constructing, comparing, and improving test statistics by exploiting approximate independence from ancillary structures.
Within a decision-theoretic framework, we have shown that reducing the dependence between a test statistic and a vector of ancillary statistics can yield MP procedures under suitable conditions. Both direct and converse results in the spirit of Basu’s theorem were established, offering a structural characterization of MP test statistics through their independence from ancillary quantities. We also demonstrated that certain forms of MP test statistics implicitly characterize the data distribution.

The practical utility of the proposed approach was illustrated through simple examples and two nonparametric applications.
Specifically, we developed ancillary-guided modifications of classical normality tests and proposed a new test for the center of symmetry based on an optimal combination of statistics using distance correlation.
These procedures are easy to implement and demonstrate strong efficiency.
Simulation studies confirm that the modified tests consistently improve power, particularly under symmetric alternatives.

Several directions merit further investigation. Extending the methodology to multiple testing, high-dimensional inference, and regression settings may reveal broader connections between ancillarity and the efficiency of statistical tests. Alternative dependence measures, such as mutual information or maximal correlation, may also yield new perspectives on test construction. Data-driven approaches for selecting ancillary-guided transformations—potentially involving resampling or empirical likelihood—offer promising opportunities for future work.

Proof.

Consider the elementary inequality: for all real values

and
C
,

(B-C)\left\{I(B\geq C)-\delta\right\}\geq 0,

where

.
Let
\delta=I(T\geq C^{T})
denote the rejection rule for a test based on the statistic
T
at threshold
C^{T}
; that is, we reject
H_{0}
when
\delta=1
. Taking
B=L(T_{N})
, we obtain:

\mathrm{E}_{0}\left\{(L(T_{N})-C)I(L(T_{N})\geq C)\right\}\geq\mathrm{E}_{0}\left\{(L(T_{N})-C)I(T\geq C^{T})\right\}.

Now fix thresholds
C
and
C^{T}
such that the type I error rates are equal:
{\Pr}_{0}\left\{(L(T_{N})\geq C\right\}={\Pr}_{0}(T\geq C^{T})
. It follows that:

\mathrm{E}_{0}\left[I(L(T_{N})\geq C)L(T_{N})\right]\geq\mathrm{E}_{0}\left[I(T\geq C^{T})L(T_{N})\right].

Since the densities satisfy the identity

, the left-hand side becomes:

\displaystyle\int I(u\geq C)uf_{0}^{L(T_{N})}(u)\,du=\int I(u\geq C)f_{1}^{L(T_{N})}(u)\,du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\Pr}_{1}\left\{L(T_{N})\geq C\right\}.

To compute the right-hand side, recall that
T=\psi(T_{N},A)
, and use the independence of
T_{N}
and
A
under
H_{0}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{0}\left[I(T\geq C^{T})L(T_{N})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{0}\left[I(\psi(T_{N},A)\geq C^{T})L(T_{N})\right]

\displaystyle=\iint I(\psi(W(u),a)\geq C^{T})uf_{0}^{L(T_{N}),A}(u,a)\,du\,da

\displaystyle=\iint I(\psi(W(u),a)\geq C^{T})uf_{0}^{L(T_{N})}(u)\,f_{0}^{A}(a)\,du\,da

\displaystyle=\iint I(\psi(W(u),a)\geq C^{T})f_{1}^{L(T_{N})}(u)\,f_{0}^{A}(a)\,du\,da

where
