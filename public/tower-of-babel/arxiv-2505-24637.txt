Abstract
Consider a one-to-one two-sided matching market with workers on one side and single-position firms on the other, and suppose that the largest individually rational matching contains n n pairs. We show that the number of workers employed and positions filled in every stable matching is bounded from below by ⌈ n 2 ⌉ \lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil and we characterise the class of preferences that attain the bound.



1
Introduction

Our starting point in this paper is a one-to-one two-sided matching market, à la
Gale and Shapley 1962
, with ‘‘workers’’ on one side and single-position ‘‘firms’’ on the other.
1
1

1

While we employ labour market terminology (see the classic papers of
Crawford and Knoer 1981
and
Kelso and Crawford 1982
), the set up is applied widely and we could have labelled the two sides “students” and “colleges”, or “men” and “women”, etc.
Roth 2015
is a popular book that details many environments to which the framework applies.

The only assumption we make on preferences is that the largest individually rational matching is of size
n
.
That is, there exists an assignment in which each of the
n
matched workers prefers their position to being unemployed and each of the
n
firms that has its post filled prefers its employee over leaving the position empty.
Given this, we ask: what fraction of workers are assured of employment in equilibrium?

Our first result is that the number of those employed in equilibrium can never go below
50\%
of what is attainable.
In other words, every stable matching is at least half the size of a maximum matching:

Theorem 1
.

Fix a one-to-one two-sided matching market with an individually rational matching of size
n
.
Then, every stable matching has at least
\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil
pairs.

Formulating the stable matching problem using undirected graphs facilitates a short proof.
2
2

2

This formulation appears in, for example, Chapter 1 of the
Echenique et al. 2023
edited volume “Online and Matching-Based Market Design” (
Echenique et al. 2023
).

Fix a bipartite graph,
G=(W,F;E)
, where
W
and
F
are the set of workers and the set of firms respectively, and the edge set,
E
, represents the acceptable pairs (i.e., for each edge, both parties prefer to be matched to each other over being unmatched).
Finally, assume that each vertex has a strict preference ranking over each of its neighbours.

Proof.

Let
\mu_{\max}
denote a maximum (possibly non-stable) matching of size
n
.
Let
\mu^{*}
denote a stable matching, and suppose, towards a contradiction, that
|\mu^{*}|<\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil
.
Since the inequality is strict, there exists an edge in
\mu_{\max}
with neither end-point in
\mu^{*}
.
But because any such edge corresponds to an acceptable pair, there is some acceptable pair, call it
(w,f)
, where both parties are unmatched in
\mu^{*}
.
This implies that
\mu^{*}
is not individually rational, since we could add the edge
(w,f)
, and therefore contradicts that
\mu^{*}
is stable.
∎

While succinct, the proof of Theorem
1
is not particularly informative.
Indeed, two natural questions remain unanswered.
Is the bound tight?
And in the event that it is, what is the structure of preferences that attain it?

Theorem
2
confirms that the answer to the first question is “yes” and it also resolves the second.
All preferences that attain the bound share a property: amongst those participants that do not appear in an individually rational matching, and therefore also not in any stable one, at least one must find the other unacceptable.
If not, then there would be an acceptable pair amongst them which would increase the size of a feasible assignment.
One natural (sub)class of preferences that attain the bound possess this property and also have a feature that could be termed “agreement on those at the top”.
That is, every workers that appears in a stable matching ranks the firms that do not appear in a stable matching below the firms that do (with a similar statement for firms).

It is worth emphasising that Theorem
1
does not guarantee that the equilibrium
employment rate
– the number of workers with jobs as a percentage of the total labour force – must be at least 50%.
Far from it.
Indeed, our characterisation illustrates how any two-sided market could, in theory, be extended indefinitely so that the equilibrium employment rate can be taken arbitrarily close to zero even when there are unemployed workers and unfilled jobs out there.
As mentioned above, this kind of market failure will maintain as long as either unemployed workers prefer unemployment over any of the empty vacancies or the opposite (or both).

Section
2
defines the environment and fixes notation.
Despite having stated Theorem
1
in the language of undirected graphs, going forward we change tack and adopt the directed graph (digraph) formulation of the stable matching problem due to
Maffray 1992
.
Our reason for settling on this approach is that it provides a nice visual aid to illustrate preferences that attain the bound.
In Section
3
, we characterise this class using digraphs.

2
Preliminaries

The set up is the original one-to-one two-sided matching model of
Gale and Shapley 1962
enriched in two ways: (i) there need not be the same number of participants on each side of the market, and (ii) there may be those who prefer to be unmatched over being paired with some participants.

Let
W
denote the finite set of
workers
and let
F
denote the finite set of single-position
firms
.
The sets
W
and
F
are disjoint.
Each market participant has a complete, transitive, and strict preference ordering over some nonempty subset of those on the other side of the market.

A
matching
is a one-to-one mapping
\mu
from
W\cup F
to itself, such that:
\mu(w)=f
if and only if
\mu(f)=w
, in which case we will say that
w
is
matched
to
f
; if
\mu(w)
is not in
F
, then
\mu(w)=w
, in which case
w
is
unmatched
; if
\mu(f)
is not in
W
, then
\mu(f)=f
, in which case
f
is said to be
unmatched
.

A matching is
individually rational
if no participant prefers to be unmatched over who they are matched to.
We say that the pair
(w,f)
in
W\times F
is
acceptable
if
w
and
f
prefer each other over being unmatched, and we assume that every market participant belongs to at least one acceptable pair.
A matching
\mu
is
stable
if it is individually rational and there is no
blocking pair:
an acceptable pair
(w,f)
such that worker
w
is either unmatched or prefers firm
f
to
\mu(w)
and firm
f
is either unmatched or prefers
w
to
\mu(f)
.

Given a matching market, we define the associated
matching digraph
,
D=\big(V,A)
, where the vertex set
V
is the collection of acceptable pairs and the arc set
A
is defined as follows.
3
3

3

The digraph terminology that we employ is the same as that in
Gutin et al. 2023
.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}A_{W}&:=&\{(w,f_{i})(w,f_{j})\;|\;w\text{ prefers }f_{j}\text{ to }f_{i}\}\\
A_{F}&:=&\{(w_{i},f)(w_{j},f)\;|\;f\text{ prefers }w_{j}\text{ to }w_{i}\}\\
A&:=&A_{W}\cup A_{F}\\
\end{array}

A
matching
appears in
D
as an independent set of vertices (i.e., no two vertices contain the same worker or the same firm).
A
stable matching
appears in
D
as a matching such that for every vertex
(w,f)\in V
, either
(w,f)
belongs to the matching or
(w,f)
has an out-neighbour that does.

A matching is
maximal
if it is not a subset of any other matching.
A
maximum
matching is at least as large as every other matching.
It is almost immediate that a stable matching is maximal (since otherwise there must be an acceptable pair
(w,f)
with both
w
and
f
unmatched).
At its core, our motivating question – see the opening paragraph – concerns the potential difference in size between a (maximal) stable matching and a maximum one.

3
The characterisation

Let
n
be an integer greater than or equal to 2.
We now define a class of matching markets that is denoted by
{\cal G}_{n}
.
(We will show that
{\cal G}_{n}
is precisely the class of markets that contain a stable matching of size
n
.)

Definition 1

(The class of two-sided markets,
{\cal G}_{n}
)
.

Let
P
be any matching market with a set of
n
workers,
W
, and a set of
n
firms,
F
, that contains a stable matching,
\mu
, of size
n
.
Add any number of new workers
W^{\prime}
and any number of new firms
F^{\prime}
.
Now, for any
(w,f)\in\mu
and
(w^{\prime},f)
where
w^{\prime}
is in
W^{\prime}
, we add an arc from
(w^{\prime},f)
to
(w,f)
(that is,
f
prefers
w
over
w^{\prime}
).
And for any
(w,f)\in\mu
and
(w,f^{\prime})
where
f^{\prime}
is in
F^{\prime}
, we add an arc from
(w,f^{\prime})
to
(w,f)
(that is,
w
prefers
f
over
f^{\prime}
).
Finally, there are no vertices
(w^{\prime},f^{\prime})
where
w^{\prime}\in W^{\prime}
and
f^{\prime}\in F^{\prime}
, and all preferences that are not specified by
P
or the rules above can be made arbitrarily.

Now, consider the class of matching markets generated by the above procedure for all markets with
n
workers,
n
firms, and contains a stable matching of size
n
.
This defines the class
{\cal G}_{n}
.

Remark.

In Definition
1
, we assumed a market with
n
workers,
n
firms, and a stable matching of size
n
.
This assumption is without loss of generality because the
normal form
of any matching market is always balanced (i.e., contains the same number of workers and firms) and contains only those participants that are matched in every stable matching.
The normal form can be arrived at by running the
iterated deletion of unattractive alternatives

(
Balinski and Ratier 1997
;
Gutin et al. 2023
)
, a reduction procedure that strips a matching market of all participants that do not appear in any stable matching.
4
4

4

Knowledge of the normal form strengthens the rural hospitals theorem
(
McVitie and Wilson 1970
;
Roth 1984
;
Roth 1986
)
since it also identifies many pairings that cannot be part of any stable matching amongst those that are always matched.

Preferences of this nature possess a

We now show that the class
{\cal G}_{n}
defined in Definition
1
precisely characterises all matching markets that contain a stable matching of size
n
.

Lemma 1
.

A stable matching problem,
R
, contains a stable matching of size
n
if and only if
R\in{\cal G}_{n}
.

Proof.

Let
R
be any two-sided matching market that contains a stable matching,
\mu
, of size
n
.
Let
W_{R}
denote the workers in
R
and let
F_{R}
denote the firms in
R
.
Let
W_{\mu}
be the workers and
F_{\mu}
the firms that are matched in
\mu
.
Let
W^{\prime}=W_{R}\setminus W_{\mu}
and let
F^{\prime}=F_{R}\setminus W_{\mu}
.
Finally, denote by
D_{R}=\big(V(D_{R}),A(D_{R})\big)
the digraph constructed from market
R
.

First we show that for every
(w,f)\in\mu
and every
f^{\prime}\in F^{\prime}
, either we have that
(w,f^{\prime})\not\in V(D_{R})
or we have that
(w,f^{\prime})(w,f)\in A(D_{R})
.
For the sake of contradiction, assume that
(w,f^{\prime})\in V(D_{R})
and
(w,f)(w,f^{\prime})\in A(D_{R})
.
However, this implies that
(w,f^{\prime})
is a blocking pair for
\mu
(as
w
prefers
f^{\prime}
over
f
and
f^{\prime}
is not matched with any worker).
So, for every
(w,f)\in\mu
and every
f^{\prime}\in F^{\prime}
, we either have
(w,f^{\prime})\not\in V(D_{R})
or
(w,f^{\prime})(w,f)\in A(D_{R})
.
An analagous argument shows that for every
(w,f)\in\mu
and every
w^{\prime}\in W^{\prime}
, we have either
(w^{\prime},f)\not\in V(D_{R})
or
(w^{\prime},f)(w,f)\in A(D_{R})
.

Clearly
(w^{\prime},f^{\prime})\not\in V(D_{R})
for all
w^{\prime}\in W^{\prime}
and
f^{\prime}\in F^{\prime}
, because such a vertex would be a blocking pair for
\mu
, a contradiction to
\mu
being a stable matching in
R
. Therefore
R\in{\cal G}_{n}
.

We now show that every matching market in
{\cal G}_{n}
has a stable matching of size
n
.
Let
R\in{\cal G}_{n}
be arbitrary.
Let
\mu
be the stable matching of
P
, which had size
n
, when we constructed
R
.
Since no pair in
P
is a blocking pair for
\mu
and no pair including a worker or firm not in
P
is a blocking pair for
\mu
, it must be that
\mu
is a stable matching for
R
.
Therefore,
R
has a stable matching of size
n
as desired.
∎

Theorem 2

A matching market,

\lceil n/2\rceil

R\in{\cal F}_{n}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
1
, we note that every market in
{\cal F}_{n}
contains a stable matching of size
\lceil n/2\rceil
.
And by construction, all markets in
{\cal F}_{n}
also contain a matching of size
n
.

Now let
R
be any market with a stable matching of size
\lceil n/2\rceil

n

By Lemma

R\in{\cal G}_{\lceil n/2\rceil}

∎

We conclude by describing a natural class of preferences that satisfy the criteria.
This class could be described as having “agreement at the top”.
By this we mean the following.
Let
W^{*}
and
F^{*}
be the set of workers and the set of firms that appear in a market’s
normal form
and hence in every stable matching.
And suppose that all pairs in
W^{*}\times F^{*}
are acceptable.
Now, if a worker
w
in
W^{*}
deems a newly added firm
f
as acceptable, then
w
also ranks
f
below all firms in
F^{*}
.
A similar but opposite statement holds for newly added workers and how firms in
F^{*}
rank them.

To give an example, let us extend the market
P

To generate the market
