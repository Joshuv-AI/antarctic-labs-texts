Compositional architecting by additive manufacturing enables hardenable titanium alloys
Authors: Yaorui Ma, Yongchun Zou, Zhenghua Huang, Jiyang Yan, Di Wu, Chongrui Wang, Jianye Liu, Jichen Jin, Yuteng Gui, Kaihua Hu, Quanqing Zeng, Chain-Tsuan Liu, Yunzhi Wang, Tianlong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Titanium alloys (Ti-alloys) often suffer from limited strain-hardening capacity (typically <250 MPa), hindering broader adoption in demanding structural applications. This study reports a Ti-alloy with ultrahigh strain-hardening capability, enabled by a unique three-dimensional compositional architecture obtained by in-situ alloying via additive manufacturing (AM) using a mixture of Mo and Ti-6Al-4V (Ti64) powders. In particular, the 3D interconnected compositional waves generate three distinct microstructural regions in as-built components: α′ martensite in low-Mo regions, metastable β phase in medium-Mo regions, and stable β phase in high-Mo regions. Each region activates unique deformation mechanisms – detwinning/retwinning, stress-induced martensitic transformation, and multiple systems-involved slipping, respectively – which together contribute to a synergistic enhancement in strain-hardening. The mechanical contrast among different regions generates heterogeneous deformation-induced (HDI) stresses, prompting a progressive, stepwise increase in hardening rate. This microstructural architecture enables an exceptional hardening increment of ~557 MPa (exceedingly twice the conventional limit in Ti-alloys (<250 MPa)), along with a remarkable combination of tensile strength (1236 MPa) and ductility (uniform elongation:11.5%). This work presents a powerful AM-enabled strategy for harnessing bulk compositional modulations for designing next-generation strain-hardenable Ti-alloys.

Introduction

For many critical applications, such as aerospace structural components, automotive crash components, orthopedic implants and stents, armor and protective structures, etc., high strain-hardening capacity is not just beneficial; they are essential for ensuring performance, safety, and manufacturability (e.g., in high-precision forming processes). Titanium alloys (Ti-alloys) exhibit many of the desired properties for these advanced applications. However, their widespread adoption has been hindered by limited strain-hardening ability and a tendency toward strain softening, typically capping the hardening increment, Δσ = σUTS − σy, at ~100 MPa1–3, where σUTS is the ultimate tensile strength, and σy is the yield stress.

Efforts to enhance strain hardening in Ti-alloys have focused on exploring the activation of various deformation modes, including non-basal slip1,2, deformation twinning (DT)3,4, and stress-induced martensitic transformation/transition (SIMT)5,6, by leveraging twinning-induced plasticity (TWIP)7 and transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP)8–10 effects. In practical critical applications, DT serves as a fundamental plastic deformation mechanism that produces {112}〈111〉11 or {332}〈113〉11–13 mechanical twinning to accommodate plastic deformation within metastable β-Ti grains. Similarly, SIMT induces the formation of hexagonal or orthorhombic martensitic phases under external stress, enabling large plastic strain accommodation. Both DT and SIMT contribute to extensive plastic strain tolerance, alleviate localized stress concentration at crack tips14–16, and refine the effective grain size under continuous loading. This continuously refining microstructure hinders dislocation motion and yields a dynamic Hall–Petch effect17, establishing the fundamental basis for excellent uniform plasticity in metastable alloys. By tailoring the phase stability18 of metastable β phases through alloying element engineering4, DT and SIMT can be readily activated in metastable titanium alloys to develop TWIP and TRIP titanium alloys.

These mechanisms offer improved ductility but generally fall short in strengthening and strain hardening, with most TRIP/TWIP-enabled Ti alloys exhibiting low yield strength and strain-hardening increments below 250 MPa. Their stress-strain curves often feature extended plateaus, reflecting limited strain hardening during plastic flow.

In metastable β-Ti-alloys, the critical stresses required to activate DT, SIMT, or complex slip modes are governed by the stability of the β phase19,20. This opens an opportunity: by spatially modulating β-phase stability, it may be possible to orchestrate distinct deformation mechanisms across different regions and delay strain localization, enabling sustained strain hardening. This concept departs from conventional TRIP/TWIP Ti alloys, in which the β phase is chemically homogeneous, and deformation is typically accompanied by a stress plateau with little strain hardening8.

Here, by in-situ alloying Ti-6Al-4V (Ti64) with Mo, a potent β-stabilizer, we leverage powder blending and Laser-Powder Bed Fusion (L-PBF) to design a microscale-compositionally modulated (microCM) Ti64-6Mo (wt.%) alloy. The composition modulation eventually generates a phase-constitution modulation, thus leading to different mechanical responses upon loading. Regions of different phase constituents interact by heterogeneous-deformation-induced (HDI) stresses and bring the microCM Ti64-6Mo alloy a record-high hardening increment of ~557 MPa, and a uniform elongation of 11.5%. This work establishes an additive manufacturing-enabled approach for engineering bulk compositional heterogeneity to achieve superior strain-hardening behavior in next-generation Ti alloys.

Results

MicroCM microstructure and mechanical behavior

Mo is a strong β stabilizer in Ti alloys, which decreases the martensite transformation start temperature (Ms) by 49 K per weight percent21. Therefore, the concentration gradient of Mo leads to an Ms gradient, which in turn results in a spatial variation in phase constitution. During printing, which has an ultrahigh cooling rate22, a cooling-induced β → α′ transition takes place in the low-Mo region (marked by the light cyan zone in Fig. 1c), but not the medium-Mo and high-Mo regions (moderate cyan zone and dark cyan zone), where the β phases are stable down to ambient temperature as metastable β phase and stable β phase, respectively. Eventually, such a microscale concentration modulation (microCM) of Mo leads to a unique phase-constitution gradient consisting of cooling-induced α′ martensite in the low-Mo swirls and metastable and stable β phases in the medium- and high-Mo swirls, as verified by the XRD spectrum in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Within these distinct regions of low-, medium-, and high-Mo content, different deformation modes - detwinning/retwining, SIMT, and dislocation slip involving multiple slip systems, respectively - are activated upon loading. Co-deformation of these regions generates heterogeneous-deformation-induced (HDI) stresses, notably back stresses, which drive a progressive, stepwise strain-hardening response. This provides the microCM Ti64-6Mo alloy with an ultrahigh strain-hardening capacity. As shown in Fig. 1d, the yield stress (σy) is 679 ± 23 MPa, the ultimate tensile stress (σUTS) is 1236 ± 16 MPa, thus leading to a record-high strain-hardening increment of ~557 MPa (Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation, based on over three independent measurements). As shown in Fig. 1e, this high strain-hardening capacity almost surpassed all Ti23–39, Al alloys40–44, steels45–49, and Mg alloys50 while maintaining a considerable specific ultimate tensile stress. This high strain-hardening capacity enables the alloy to achieve an exceptionally low yield ratio of ~0.55, which is significantly lower than most conventional structural materials, while maintaining a competitive specific yield strength (Fig. 1d, g). A lower yield ratio is highly desirable in engineering applications, as it enhances overload resistance, promotes uniform stress distribution, delays strain localization, and prevents premature failure, which is critical for structural safety and energy absorption under extreme conditions (e.g., seismic/impact loads). This combination of a low yield ratio and high specific strength defies the conventional trade-off in Ti alloys that are often perceived as inherently high-yield-ratio materials (>0.9). Benefit from the unparalleled strain-hardening capacity, the current microCM Ti64-6Mo alloy was uniformly strain hardened during deformation and thus possesses an excellent uniform elongation of 11.5 ± 0.5% (Fig. 1d). Compared to typical alloys fabricated by both conventional23,24 and AM methods25–36, the current alloy shows a record high combination of specific ultimate tensile strength (σUTS) (surpassing even ultrahigh-strength steels23–39) and uniform elongation (εu) (Fig. 1g).

After deformation, the medium-Mo regions undergo stress-induced martensite transformation (SIMT), as evidenced by a reduction in the β-phase fraction (Fig. 3h, i). This SIMT is further confirmed by XRD analysis (Fig. 3j), where the intensity of the β-phase peak decreases after deformation. The resulting martensite lamellae are aligned parallel to one another within the β matrix, with lamellar spacing ranging from 50 to 100 nm (Fig. 3k). The morphology shown in Fig. 3k is representative of the SIM microstructures observed within regions containing 8–13 wt.% Mo (see Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Table. 4). The transformation is arrested at the boundary of compositional swirls with higher Mo concentrations; for example, in one case, a β-phase domain of ~700 nm wide remains at the grain center, where the local Mo content reaches 13.6 wt.%. In the Ti64-6Mo alloy exhibiting pronounced Mo concentration modulation, the products of the stress-induced phase transformation of the metastable β phase evolve with increasing Mo content, and consequently with enhanced phase stability. As the Mo content rises, the stress-induced transformation yields hexagonal α′ phase, orthorhombic α″ phase, and minor traces of the ω phase. A detailed analysis of these phase transformation products is provided in Supplementary Note 6.

Thus, in this microCM Ti64-6Mo system, the metastable β phase occupies the intermediate regions between the low- and high-Mo zones, with a monotonic Mo concentration gradient from 8 to 13 wt.%. Within this range, the critical stress required for SIMT increases with Mo content (Fig. 3n)19 due to enhanced β-phase stability. During the early stages of deformation, SIMT initiates in the less-stable medium-Mo region. As strain increases, the α′/β interface advances into the high-Mo region, halting once the local stress is insufficient to drive further transformations of the increasingly stable β phase. This progressive activation of SIMT allows the transformation front to move through the Mo concentration gradient under rising applied stress. As a result, SIMT is not simultaneous but spatially and temporally distributed, contributing to continuous strain hardening. This behavior is illustrated in Fig. 3o and stands in contrast to SIMT in chemically homogeneous metastable Ti alloys, which typically exhibit stress plateaus during transformation8. This microscale Mo concentration modulation produces a spatially heterogeneous β stability that governs strain partitioning through SIMT under monotonic loading. Such transformation-mediated hardening and three-dimensional phase connectivity may also suppress cyclic strain localization and delay fatigue crack initiation.

Interestingly, a new α′/β orientation relationship (OR) distinct from the conventional Burgers orientation relationship (BOR) is observed in the current SIM structure (Fig. 3m). According to BOR, the closed-packed plane of the BCC β phase aligns with the basal plane of the HCP α′ phase, i.e., (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$01\bar{1}$$\end{document}011¯)β//(0001)α′. However, in this newly identified OR, there is a ~6° deviation between the {110}β plane and the (0001)α′ basal plane. This results in a novel parallelism between (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10\bar{1}1$$\end{document}101¯1)α′ and (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10\bar{1}$$\end{document}101¯)β. Under the conventional BOR, dislocations could readily transmit across the α′/β interface—from {101}β to {0001}α′—due to favorable geometrical compatibility, thereby contributing minimally to strain hardening37,57. This is attributed to the dominance of basal slip in the stress-induced α′ (a = 0.289 nm, c = 0.451 nm, with a c/a ratio of 1.56). In contrast, the new OR introduces a less favorable interface geometry, requiring higher stress for dislocation transmission. This impedes easy slip transfer across the interface and either suppresses basal slip or activates harder non-basal slip modes in α′, thereby contributing to a significantly enhanced strain-hardening response.

During deformation, dislocation slip is the dominant deformation mechanism in the high-Mo, stable β-phase regions. At locations with Mo concentration ~13.7 wt.%, the deformation microstructure exhibits high-density slip traces (Fig. 4e), which are indexed by the [110]β diffraction pattern as belonging to the {112} family, with an intersection angle about 70.6° (marked in Fig. 4e). This indicates that {112} cross-slip governs the deformation mechanism of the residual β phase in this Mo concentration range.

As the Mo concentration increases further to ~33.3 wt.% (Fig. 4f), the deformation mechanism evolves. The corresponding BF image reveals dense dislocation arrays and parallel slip traces. These slip bands terminate at GBs, forming zigzag patterns along the GBs—clearly visible in the HAADF image in Fig. 4g—indicating the presence of significant local plastic strains. Diffraction analysis along the [111]β zone axis (Fig. 4g) identifies the active slip system as (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\bar{1}0$$\end{document}11¯0). The absence of intersecting slip traces suggests that {110} planar slip becomes the predominant deformation mechanism at higher Mo concentrations (~33.3 wt.%).

The presence of high-density slip bands observed in Fig. 4f, g indicates that slip localization is effectively suppressed, which is notable, as slip localization is a known precursor to premature cracking58. Therefore, in these stable β-phase regions, the deformation mode transitions smoothly from {112} cross-slip at ~13 wt.% Mo to {110} planar slip at higher Mo concentrations, contributing to more uniform plastic deformation.

Discussion

The excellent mechanical properties and strong strain-hardening capability show great reproducibility across different 3D printing platforms. Even with a large processing variation (volumetric energy density from 60 to 100 J/mm³, 67%), mechanical property fluctuations remain within 3% (Supplementary Fig. 3), and no significant microstructural changes are observed (Supplementary Fig. 4). The commercial Ti-6Al-4V powder used in this study contains 0.13 wt.% oxygen, uniformly distributed in the feedstock powder. No oxygen segregation was detected in the as-built microstructure. Thus, the uniformly distributed oxygen at this content level serves mainly to suppress the β to α′ phase transformation and as an interstitial solid-solution strengthener, without causing embrittlement or altering the transformation and deformation pathways. In contrast, the rapid-solidification-induced Mo gradient produces pronounced spatial variations in β stability and martensitic propensity, directly governing the heterogeneous phase distribution and deformation mechanisms. From the medium- to high-Mo regions (8~13 wt.% to >13 wt.%), the deformation mechanism transitions from SIMT to dislocation slip, governed by the relative critical shear stresses required to activate each mechanism (Fig. 4h). During deformation, SIMT initiates in the metastable β phase regions (Fig. 4e, i), then progressively propagates toward the more stable β phase regions, but halts where the critical stress for dislocation slip becomes lower than that for SIMT3. Upon further loading, the stable β phase (Mo concentration >13 wt.%) undergoes plastic deformation primarily via dislocation slip, as shown in Fig. 4e and j.

Notably, the concentration gradient within the high-Mo regions also induces variation in slip behavior, with different slip systems activated in adjacent areas depending on the local Mo concentration (e.g., Fig. 4e vs. 4f). In the SIMT regions, the lamellar thickness of the stress-induced α′ martensite and the residual β phase is remarkably fine—only 50–100 nm (Fig. 4e)—in stark contrast to the micron-scale martensite structure typically observed in conventional alloys39,59. These high-density, nanoscale martensite lamellae significantly strengthen the SIMT regions relative to the surrounding stable β-phase matrix60. This microstructural heterogeneity gives rise to mechanical incompatibility between the harder SIMT zones and the softer β-phase regions, resulting in strain partitioning. The associated plastic misfit generates a back stress61,62 within the soft domains (indicated by the cyan arrow in Fig. 4j), which enhances their work hardening and, over time, contributes to the activation of dislocation-mediated plasticity within the SIMT regions themselves. This synergistic interplay between martensitic transformation and dislocation slip is central to the exceptional strain-hardening capability of the microCM alloy.

To summarize, this study demonstrates the design of a highly strain-hardenable titanium alloy with a microscale compositional modulation (microCM) architecture via partial homogenization during in situ alloying in L-PBF. The resulting microCM Ti64-6Mo (wt.%) alloy exhibits three distinct microstructural regions: (I) Low-Mo regions (<7 wt.%): form cooling-induced martensite, (Ⅱ) Medium-Mo regions (8–13 wt.%): contain metastable β phase, and (Ⅲ) High-Mo regions (>13 wt.%): consist of stable β phase. In this work, we used Ti64-6Mo as the model alloy to demonstrate a pathway for designing Ti alloys with great strain-hardening. This strategy endows the as-printed microCM alloy an exceptional strain-hardening rate of 5.8 GPa and a strain-hardening increment of 557 MPa—more than doubling the upper limit of conventional Ti alloys (typically lower than 250 MPa) with a remarkable combination of tensile strength (1236 MPa) and uniform elongation (11.5%). Since higher strength is critical to broaden industrial applications, we further explored pathways to enhance this property. Preliminary experiments reveal that even minor adjustments to printing parameters or a rapid post-build heat treatment can substantially raise the yield strength (by over 37%) and tensile strength (by over 10%), without compromising the strain hardenability (see Supplementary Note 9 for details). This innovative alloy design strategy sets a paradigm for developing highly hardenable titanium alloys and other structural materials through controlled micro-compositional architectures enabled by additive manufacturing.

Methods

Materials

Commercial Ti-6Al-4V (Ti64) and Mo powders are provided by AP&C with diameters between 15 and 45 μm for additive manufacturing. The powder composition in weight percent of Ti64 is Ti (balance), Al (6.3%), V (4.2%), Fe (0.18%), O (0.13%), H (0.0031%), C (0.03%), and Ni (0.03%). The Mo powder is 99.99% pure.

Laser-powder bed fusion

Before the 3D printing process, Ti-6Al-4V (Ti64) and molybdenum (Mo) powders were blended in a mechanical mixer for a duration of four hours. Selective Laser Melting (SLM) was subsequently conducted using an EOS M290 printer equipped with a Yb-fiber laser in an argon atmosphere and maintaining the minimum oxygen level of 0.1 vol%. The used machine provides a maximum laser power of 400 W with a laser spot size of around 100 μm. The printing parameters were set as follows: laser power at 240 W, scan speed at 1000 mm/s, hatch spacing at 100 μm, and layer thickness at 40 μm, resulting in an energy density of 60 J/mm³. The base temperature was maintained at 35 °C. An S-shaped scanning strategy was employed, with the scanning direction between adjacent layers rotating 90 degrees clockwise. The relative density of the as-printed alloy is over 99.9%.

Mechanical tensile test

The tensile specimens were fabricated directly using the laser-powder bed fusion technique, featuring dimensions of a gauge length of 32 mm, a width of 6 mm, and a total length of 84 mm. The as-printed specimens were subsequently sliced to a thickness of 1.5 mm. Prior to the tensile test, the samples underwent mechanical polishing to achieve a mirror-like surface finish using silicon carbide (SiC) paper with a 5000# grit. Following polishing, the specimens were cleaned with distilled water and acetone to remove any residual contaminants. Room temperature tensile tests were conducted using a Material Testing System (MTS) tensile testing machine, which was equipped with a 25 mm extensometer (MTS MODEL 632.24F-50). The tests were performed at a strain rate of 10−3 s−1. To ensure reproducibility, at least three tensile samples were tested, and the average values were reported. The yield strength was determined using the 0.2% offset plastic strain method. The true stress-strain curve was derived by the formulas: εT = ln(1+εΕ); σT = σΕ⋅(1 + ϵΕ). The work-hardening rate was derived by differentiating the true tensile stress over the true tensile strain by the equation of θ = dσT/dεT.

Scanning electron microscopy and analysis

The sample in this paper is analyzed under a scanning electron microscope (SEM) to get mesoscale microstructure characterization, an electron probe X-ray micro-analyzer (EPMA) to obtain chemical composition, and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) to get phase identification.

The sample prepared for SEM, EPMA, and EBSD was subsequently subjected to mechanical grinding and polished using silicon carbide (SiC) paper and silicon dioxide (SiO₂) suspension to achieve a mirror-like surface finish. Following this initial polishing process, an electrolytic polishing step was conducted on the observed surface using a polishing solution consisting of nitric acid (HNO₃) and methanol in a 1:3 volume ratio. The polishing was performed at a voltage of 22 V, with a power density maintained below 0.5 W/cm³. The duration of the polishing process was set at 20 s, and the polishing was conducted at a temperature of −20 °C.

The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observations were conducted using a JEOL JSM-IT800 SEM in backscattered electron (BSE) mode, with an accelerating voltage of 30 kV. The chemical distribution was analyzed using the wavelength dispersive spectrometer (WDS) technique integrated into the electron probe micro-analyzer (EPMA) on a JEOL JSM 7100 SEM. For the EPMA, the accelerating voltage was set to 20 kV, with a dwell time of 10 ms for each analysis point. Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) data was obtained using an Oxford Instruments Symmetry 2 EBSD detector mounted on a JEOL 7800 SEM. The parameters for the EBSD analysis included an accelerating voltage of 20 kV, a probe current of 13 nA, a step size of 0.1 µm, a working distance of 5 mm, and a sample pre-tilt angle of 70°. The EBSD data were subsequently analyzed using Aztec crystal software.

X-ray diffraction acquisition

The XRD spectra were collected from the polished sample surface (10 mm × 10 mm) by a Rigaku SmartLab X-ray diffraction (XRD) instrumented by Cu-Kα radiation (wavelength, 0.15406 nm) operated at 40 kV and 40 mA. The diffraction patterns were recorded over a 2θ range from 20° to 100° at a scanning speed of 2°/min.

Transmission electron microscopy

To prepare the transmission electron microscopy thin foil specimens, the thin pieces were sliced from the bulk samples using a spark cutting machine. The thin pieces were ground to a thickness of less than 30 μm. Then the 3-mm-diameter disks were punched from these thin slices and cleaned by an ultrasonic cleaning machine in alcohol solution to remove potential organic contaminations. After that, an ion milling system (Gatan, PIPS, 695) was used to thin the 3-mm disks to electron transparency. FEI TEM Talos F200x was utilized to obtain the TEM bright-field image, dark-field image, and selective area diffraction pattern (SADP). TEM bright field images were captured with the exposure time from 0.2 to 0.5 s, depending on the actual thickness of the site. To ensure the accuracy of the integrated intensity of the diffraction spots, all the SADP images were obtained with an exposure time of 0.5 s. For the TEM dark field image, 10 s was used. The STEM atomic resolution images were obtained from a double Cs-corrected JEOL Grand Arm300F TEM operated at 300 kV. A 15 mrad convergence semi-angle was used to achieve a resolution better than 0.12 nm.

Thermodynamic calculation

Thermodynamic calculations were conducted to derive the pseudo-binary phase diagram for the Ti64-6Mo alloy and assess its constitutional supercooling properties. The calculations utilized Thermo-Calc 3.1 software in conjunction with the Ti database (TTTI3).

Statistics and reproducibility

In this paper, no statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Mechanical tests, microstructure observations, and other characterizations were performed on a minimum of three independent metal samples. Experimental data are reported as mean ± standard deviation based on at least three independent measurements. No data was excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized. The Investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. Repeated tests were conducted to verify the stability and reproducibility of results.
