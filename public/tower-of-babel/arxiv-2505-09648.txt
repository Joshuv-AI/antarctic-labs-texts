Density Ternary Goldbach for primes in a fixed residue class
Ali Alsetri

Abstract
. We prove that if A A is a subset of those primes which are congruent to 1 ( mod 3 ) 1\pmod{3} such that the relative density of A A in this residue class is larger than 1 2 , \frac{1}{2}, then every sufficiently large odd integer n n which satisfies n ≡ 0 ( mod 3 ) n\equiv 0\pmod{3} can be written as a sum of three primes from A . A. Moreover the threshold of 1 2 \frac{1}{2} for the relative density is best possible.



1.
Introduction

This article falls under the general theme of representations of integers as sums of primes. The famous binary Goldbach conjecture states that every even integer
n\geq 4
can be written as the sum of two primes. While the binary Goldbach problem is still out of reach, the ternary Goldbach problem which studies representations of odd integers as sums of three primes has now been solved. Vinogradov
[
5
]
showed in
1937
that every sufficiently large odd integer is a sum of three primes, and in
2015
Helfgott
[
2
]
showed this holds for every odd integer
n\geq 7.

There has recently been an effort to obtain density versions of the aforementioned Goldbach-type results. In
2005
Green
[
1
]
established a version of Roth’s theorem for the primes; he proved that any subset of the primes with positive relative density contains a three term arithmetic progression. In this paper he developed what has become known as a transference principle, which allows one to transfer certain results which hold for dense subsets in the integers to relatively dense subsets of the primes. Variants of Green’s transference principle have been developed to allow for density versions of Goldbach-type problems. Given a subset
A\subset\mathbb{P}
of the primes define its lower density in the primes to be

\underline{\delta_{\mathbb{P}}}(A)=\liminf_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|A\cap\{1,2,\dots,N\}|}{|\mathbb{P}\cap\{1,2,\dots,N\}|}.

Li and Pan
[
3
]
developed a variant of Green’s transference principle suitable for ternary Goldbach-type problems and used it to prove a density version of Vinogradov’s theorem. Namely they showed that if
A,B,C\subset\mathbb{P}
such that
\underline{\delta_{\mathbb{P}}}(A)+\underline{\delta_{\mathbb{P}}}(B)+\underline{\delta_{\mathbb{P}}}(C)>2,
then every sufficiently large odd integer
n
can be written as a sum
n=a+b+c
with
a\in A,b\in B
and
c\in C.
Shao
[
4
]
, using Li and Pan’s variant of the transference principle, improved their result in the case when the three primes belong to the same subset of the primes. He proved that if
A\subset\mathbb{P}
such that
\underline{\delta_{\mathbb{P}}}(A)>\frac{5}{8}
then every sufficiently large odd integer
n
can be written as a sum
n=a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}
with
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in A.
Moreover he showed that the threshold
\frac{5}{8}
was sharp.

In this paper we prove a density version of a variant of Vinogradov’s theorem in which the primes in our subset belong to the residue class
1\pmod{3}.
Sums of three such primes must be multiples of
3
, and so we seek to represent every sufficiently large odd integer
n
such that
n\equiv 0\pmod{3}
as a sum of three primes from our subset. We will now precisely state our main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{1,3}
denote the primes congruent to
1
modulo
3
, and let
A\subset\mathcal{P}_{1,3}
satisfy
\underline{\delta}(A)>\frac{1}{2}
where
\underline{\delta}(A)=\liminf_{N\rightarrow\infty}{\frac{|A\cap[1,N]|}{|\mathcal{P}_{1,3}\cap[1,N]|}}
. Then
A+A+A
contains every sufficiently large odd integer
n
which satisfies
n\equiv 0\pmod{3}
.

Our strategy in this paper follows the strategy adopted by Shao
[
4
]
. To facilitate his use of the transference principle he develops a local result, Proposition
1.4
in
[
4
]
, which is the key technical tool in his paper. It implies that if
m
is an odd squarefree integer and
A\subset\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{*}
, then
A+A+A
contains all residue classes modulo
m
provided
|A|>\frac{5}{8}\phi(m).
This is Corollary
1.5
in
[
4
]
, it follows immediately from the more quantitative Proposition
1.4
preceding it. We will develop an analog of his local result which will allow us to deduce our main theorem using an appropriate variant of the transference principle. We will now state our local result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
m
be an odd squarefree integer which satisfies
m\equiv 0\pmod{3}.
Let
f:\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{*}\rightarrow[0,1]
be a function supported on those reduced residue classes which are
1\pmod{3}
which satisfies

\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{*}}f(a)>\frac{\varphi(m)}{4}.

Then for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
for which
x\equiv 0\pmod{3}
, there exist
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{*}
such that

f(a_{i})>0,f(a_{1})+f(a_{2})+f(a_{3})>\frac{3}{2}.

This result is sharp. Let
A=\{1,7\}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{15}^{*}
, then the residue class
12\pmod{15}\notin A+A+A.
Therefore the corresponding function
f:\mathbb{Z}_{15}^{*}\rightarrow[0,1]
which is defined by

f(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x\in A\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

has its average
\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{15}^{*}}f(x)=\frac{\phi(15)}{4}
but the conclusion of the theorem fails upon taking
x=12\pmod{15}.

We prove our local result by following the strategy used by Shao
[
4
]
. Shao develops Lemmas
2.1
,
2.2
and Propositions
3.1
,
3.2
and uses these to deduce his local result. Accordingly we will need versions of these lemmas and propositions which are suitable for our setting.

This article is organized as follows. In Section
2
we prove some key lemmas which are needed for the proof of our local result
1.2
. In Section
3
we prove our local result
1.2
which we deduce from the two propositions
3.1
and
3.2
. Then in Section
4
we include the variant of the transference principle which we need in the form used by Shao in
[
4
]
. Finally we prove our main result in Section
5
using our local result and the transference principle.

2.

Lemma 2.1
.

For even
n\geq 6
, let
a_{0}\geq a_{1}\geq\dots\geq a_{n-1}
be a decreasing sequence of numbers in
[0,1]
. Let
A
denote their average
A=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_{j}
. Suppose that for all triples
(i,j,k)
with
0\leq i,j,k\leq n-1
and with
i+j+k\geq n
we have

a_{i}a_{j}+a_{j}a_{k}+a_{k}a_{i}\leq\frac{1}{2}(a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}).

Then the average value
A
is bounded by
\frac{1}{2}.

Proof.

Using the transformation
x_{i}=4a_{i}-1
, we have a new decreasing sequence
x_{0}\geq x_{1}\geq\dots\geq x_{n-1}
in
[-1,3]
. Let
X
denote the average value
X=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}x_{j}
, and observe that it suffices to show
X\leq 1.
Moreover for all triples
(i,j,k)
with
0\leq i,j,k\leq n-1
and with
i+j+k\geq n
we have

(2.1)

x_{i}x_{j}+x_{j}x_{k}+x_{k}x_{i}\leq 3.

We first treat the case
n=6
. We want to show

x_{0}+x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}+x_{4}+x_{5}\leq 6.

We may assume that
x_{0}>1
, since otherwise
X\leq x_{0}\leq 1
and we are done. Hence
x_{0}+x_{i}>0
for each
i=0,\dots,n-1.
Observe we have the following inequalities, using the relations associated to the triples of indices.

\displaystyle x_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\min(1,\frac{3-x_{0}x_{4}}{x_{0}+x_{4}})\quad\textrm{using}\quad(2,2,2),(0,2,4).

\displaystyle x_{3}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{3+x_{0}^{2}}-x_{0}\quad\textrm{using}\quad(0,3,3).

\displaystyle x_{5}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3-x_{0}x_{1}}{x_{0}+x_{1}}\quad\textrm{using}\quad(0,1,5).

Hence letting
x=x_{0},y=x_{1},z=x_{4}
it suffices to show
g(x,y,z)\leq 6
where

g(x,y,z)=y+z+\min(1,\frac{3-xz}{x+z})+\frac{3-xy}{x+y}+(3+x^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}},

for

belonging to
[-1,3]
. Observe additionally that
z
satisfies

z\leq\min((3+x^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}-x,\frac{3-y^{2}}{2y}),

due to the triples
(0,4,4),(1,1,4).

Now we split into cases based on the range of values taken by
x,y,z
. Note without loss of generality we may assume
x\geq 1
and
y\geq 0.6.
Observe on each of the following regions we can bound
g(x,y,z)
above by the corresponding expressions involving
x
and
y
.

\displaystyle g(x,y,z)

\displaystyle\leq f_{1}(x,y)=1+2y+\sqrt{3+x^{2}}+\frac{3-xy}{x+y},\quad 1\leq x\leq 2,0.6\leq y\leq 1.

\displaystyle g(x,y,z)

\displaystyle\leq f_{2}(x,y)=1+y+2\sqrt{3+x^{2}}-x+\frac{3-xy}{x+y},\quad 2\leq x\leq 3,0.6\leq y\leq 1.

\displaystyle g(x,y,z)

\displaystyle\leq f_{3}(x,y)=1+y+\sqrt{3+x^{2}}+\frac{3-xy}{x+y}+\frac{3-y^{2}}{2y},\quad 1\leq x\leq 2,1\leq y\leq x.

\displaystyle g(x,y,z)

\displaystyle\leq f_{4}(x,y)=1+y+2\sqrt{3+x^{2}}-x+\frac{3-xy}{x+y},\quad 2\leq x\leq 2.5,1\leq y\leq 1.7.

\displaystyle g(x,y,z)

\displaystyle\leq f_{5}(x,y)=1+y+\sqrt{3+x^{2}}+\frac{3-xy}{x+y}+\frac{3-y^{2}}{2y},\quad 2\leq x\leq 2.5,1.7\leq y\leq x.

\displaystyle g(x,y,z)

\displaystyle\leq f_{6}(x,y)=1+y+\sqrt{3+x^{2}}+\frac{3-xy}{x+y}+\frac{3-y^{2}}{2y},\quad 2.5\leq x\leq 3,1.4\leq y\leq x.

\displaystyle g(x,y,z)

\displaystyle\leq f_{7}(x,y)=y+\sqrt{3+x^{2}}+\frac{3-xy}{x+y}+1.4,\quad 2.5\leq x\leq 3,1\leq y\leq 1.4,z\leq 0.4.

\displaystyle g(x,y,z)

\displaystyle\leq f_{8}(x,y)=y+2\sqrt{3+x^{2}}-x+\frac{3-xy}{x+y}+\frac{3-x(0.4)}{x+0.4},\quad 2.5\leq x\leq 3,1\leq y\leq 1.4,0.4\leq z.

One may check by hand that for each
1\leq i\leq 8
,

on the corresponding region by showing there is no critical point in the region and maximizing along the boundary. Alternatively one may use, say, Wolfram Alpha and verify that
f_{i}(x,y)\leq 6
on the corresponding region for

Hence we are done with the

The remaining

in

]

Let
n=2m
for
m\geq 4
and define

S_{0}=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}x_{i}\quad\textrm{and}\quad S_{1}=\sum_{i=m}^{n-1}x_{i}.

Assume
S_{0}+S_{1}>2m
for a contradiction. Consider the quantity
S_{0}^{2}+2S_{0}S_{1}
; the terms in this sum can be grouped into expressions of the form
x_{i}x_{j}+x_{j}x_{k}+x_{k}x_{i}
with

, and
m\leq k\leq 2m-1
such that

Observe that there are
m^{2}
such expressions since for each pair
0\leq i,j\leq m-1

such that

Therefore

S_{0}^{2}+2S_{0}S_{1}=\sum_{i+j+k\equiv 0\pmod{m}}x_{i}x_{j}+x_{j}x_{k}+x_{k}x_{i}.

Moreover every term, except for the term corresponding to
(i,j,k)=(0,0,m)
, has its indices sum to at least
n
. Applying (
2.1
), we find that

(2.2)

S_{0}^{2}+2S_{0}S_{1}\leq 3(m^{2}-1)+x_{0}^{2}+2x_{0}x_{m}\leq 3m^{2}+(x_{0}^{2}-x_{m}^{2})

where the final inequality follows since
x_{m}^{2}+2x_{0}x_{m}\leq 3
due to the triple
(m,m,0)
and the corresponding relation (
2.1
).

Now observe that relation (
2.1
) corresponding to the triple
(m-1,m-1,m-1)
yields
x_{m-1}^{2}\leq 1.
Hence
x_{m-1}\leq 1
and so
S_{0}\leq 3m-2.
Combining this with our assumption that
S_{0}+S_{1}>2m
it follows that
S_{1}>-m+2.

First suppose
S_{1}\leq 0
, and so

4m^{2}<(S_{0}+S_{1})^{2}\leq(S_{0}^{2}+2S_{0}S_{1})+S_{1}^{2}\leq 3m^{2}+9+(m-2)^{2},

where we used
x_{0}^{2}-x_{m}^{2}\leq 9
. This implies

which is a contradiction for

Now let
S_{1}>0.
It follows that

Hence

4m^{2}<(S_{0}+S_{1})^{2}\leq 3m^{2}+(x_{0}^{2}-x_{m}^{2})+m^{2}x_{m}^{2},

by (
2.2
).

Rearranging we obtain

(2.3)

x_{0}^{2}>(m^{2}-1)(1-x_{m}^{2})+1.

Using the relation (
2.1
) corresponding to the triple
(0,m,m)
we have the inequality

x_{0}x_{m}\leq\frac{3-x_{m}^{2}}{2}\leq 1+\frac{1-x_{m}^{2}}{2}.

Squaring we obtain

(x_{0}x_{m})^{2}\leq 1+(1-x_{m}^{2})+\frac{(1-x_{m}^{2})^{2}}{4}.

Combining this with the lower bound (

), and using

) corresponding to the triple

, we obtain the inequality

Hence for

,

Therefore by (

), we obtain

x_{0}^{2}>(m^{2}-1)(1-\frac{9}{4m^{2}-3})+1.

which contradicts

for all

.
∎

We now state an asymmetric variant of Lemma
2.1
which is needed for the proof of Proposition
3.1
. The statement is identical to Lemma 2.2 in

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
n\geq 10
be an even integer and
\{a_{i}\}
,
\{b_{i}\}
,
\{c_{i}\}
with
0\leq i\leq n-1
decreasing sequences in
[0,1]
. Let
A,B,C
be the averages of the sequences
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i}
respectively. If for all
(i,j,k)
with
i+j+k\geq n
we have

a_{i}b_{j}+b_{j}c_{k}+c_{k}a_{i}\leq\frac{1}{2}(a_{i}+b_{j}+c_{k}),

then
AB+BC+CA\leq\frac{1}{2}(A+B+C)

Proof.

As before we use the change of variables

x_{i}=4a_{i}-1,\quad y_{i}=4b_{i}-1\quad\textrm{and}\quad z_{i}=4c_{i}-1,

for
0\leq i\leq n-1.
This results in decreasing sequences
\{x_{i}\},\{y_{i}\},\{z_{i}\}
in
[-1,3]
, denote their averages by
X,Y,Z
respectively. For each triple of indices
(i,j,k)
such that
i+j+k\geq n
we have

(2.4)

x_{i}y_{j}+y_{j}z_{k}+z_{k}x_{i}\leq 3.

It suffices to show
XY+YZ+ZX\leq 3.
Let
n=2m
and define

X_{0}=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}x_{i},X_{1}=\sum_{i=m}^{n-1}x_{i},Y_{0}=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}y_{i},Y_{1}=\sum_{i=m}^{n-1}y_{i},Z_{0}=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}z_{i},Z_{1}=\sum_{i=m}^{n-1}z_{i}.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the set of all triples
(i,j,k)
with
0\leq i,j\leq m-1
,
m\leq k\leq n-1
and such that
i+j+k\equiv 0\pmod{m}.

We now sum the relation (
2.4
) over all triples
(i,j,k)\in\mathcal{M}
except for
(0,0,m)
which yields the inequality

\sum_{(i,j,k)\in\mathcal{M}}x_{i}y_{j}+y_{j}z_{k}+z_{k}x_{i}-(x_{0}y_{0}+y_{0}z_{m}+z_{m}x_{0})\leq 3(m^{2}-1).

Observe the sum can be expressed as
X_{0}Y_{0}+Y_{0}Z_{1}+Z_{1}X_{0}
and so we have the inequality

X_{0}Y_{0}+Y_{0}Z_{1}+Z_{1}X_{0}\leq 3(m^{2}-1)+(x_{0}y_{0}+y_{0}z_{m}+z_{m}x_{0}).

By symmetry we also have the inequalities

X_{0}Y_{1}+Y_{1}Z_{0}+Z_{0}X_{0}\leq 3(m^{2}-1)+(x_{0}y_{m}+y_{m}z_{0}+z_{0}x_{0}),

and

X_{1}Y_{0}+Y_{0}Z_{0}+Z_{0}X_{1}\leq 3(m^{2}-1)+(x_{m}y_{0}+y_{0}z_{0}+z_{0}x_{m}).

Combining these inequalities we have

n^{2}(XY+YZ+ZX)=(X_{0}+X_{1})(Y_{0}+Y_{1})+(Y_{0}+Y_{1})(Z_{0}+Z_{1})\\
+(Z_{0}+Z_{1})(X_{0}+X_{1})\leq 9(m^{2}-1)+U-W+V,

where

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=(x_{0}+x_{m})(y_{0}+y_{m})+(y_{0}+y_{m})(z_{0}+z_{m})+(z_{0}+z_{m})(x_{0}+x_{m}),

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=X_{1}Y_{1}+Y_{1}Z_{1}+Z_{1}X_{1},

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=(x_{m}y_{m}+y_{m}z_{m}+z_{m}x_{m}).

Hence our goal becomes to show

(2.5)

U+V-W\leq 3m^{2}+9.

Now observe that summing condition (
2.4
) over all triples
(i,j,k)
such that
m\leq i,j,k\leq n-1
and
i+j+k\equiv 0\pmod{m}
, except for
(m,m,m)
, yields the inequality

X_{1}Y_{1}+Y_{1}Z_{1}+Z_{1}X_{1}-(x_{m}y_{m}+y_{m}z_{m}+z_{m}x_{m})\leq 3(m^{2}-1),

and so

V-W\leq 3(m^{2}-1).

Now let

r=x_{0}+x_{m},\quad s=y_{0}+y_{m}\quad\textrm{and}\quad t=z_{0}+z_{m}

and so
U=rs+st+tr.
Observe
r,s,t\in[-2,6]
. Now suppose one of
\{r+s,s+t,t+s\}
, say
r+s
, is negative. Then

U=rs+t(r+s)\leq rs+(-2)(r+s)=(r-2)(s-2)-4\leq(-4)(-4)-4=12.

Therefore

U+V-W\leq 3m^{2}+9,

as desired. Hence we may assume
r+s,s+t,t+s
are nonnegative and consequently that
U
is an increasing function of

We will now reduce to four cases based upon the parities of
X_{1},Y_{1},Z_{1}.
First, observe that

x_{m}-(m-1)\leq X_{1}\leq mx_{m},\quad y_{m}-(m-1)\leq Y_{1}\leq my_{m}\quad\textrm{and}\quad z_{m}-(m-1)\leq Z_{1}\leq mz_{m}.

Case 1

X_{1},Y_{1},Z_{1}<0.

In this case

is a decreasing function of

.

V\leq(x_{m}-(m-1))(y_{m}-(m-1))+(y_{m}-(m-1))(z_{m}-(m-1))+(z_{m}-(m-1))(x_{m}-(m-1)),

V\leq 3(m-1)^{2}+W-2(m-1)(x_{m}+y_{m}+z_{m}).

Since

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle\leq(3+x_{m})(3+y_{m})+(3+y_{m})(3+z_{m})+(3+z_{m})(3+x_{m})

V\leq m^{2}(x_{m}y_{m}+y_{m}z_{m}+z_{m}x_{m})=m^{2}W

) we have the bound

Subtracting

from both sides, we obtain

(2.8)

W+x_{0}(y_{m}+z_{m})+y_{0}(x_{m}+z_{m})+z_{0}(x_{m}+y_{m})\leq 9.

Now suppose that
x_{m}+y_{m},y_{m}+z_{m},z_{m}+x_{m}\geq 0.6.
This implies that

x_{0}+y_{0}-10(x_{m}+y_{m})\leq 6-10(0.6)=0,

and so
(x_{0}+y_{0}-10(x_{m}+y_{m}))(z_{0}-z_{m})\leq 0.
Expanding we obtain

(x_{0}z_{0}+y_{0}z_{0})+10(x_{m}z_{m}+y_{m}z_{m})\leq x_{0}z_{m}+y_{0}z_{m}+10(x_{m}z_{0}+y_{m}z_{0}).

By symmetry we also have the inequalities

(x_{0}y_{0}+z_{0}y_{0})+10(x_{m}y_{m}+z_{m}y_{m})\leq x_{0}y_{m}+z_{0}y_{m}+10(x_{m}y_{0}+z_{m}y_{0}),

and

(z_{0}x_{0}+y_{0}x_{0})+10(z_{m}x_{m}+y_{m}x_{m})\leq z_{0}x_{m}+y_{0}x_{m}+10(z_{m}x_{0}+y_{m}x_{0}).

Adding these inequalities we obtain

2(x_{0}y_{0}+y_{0}z_{0}+z_{0}x_{0})+20W\leq 11(z_{0}(x_{m}+y_{m})+y_{0}(x_{m}+z_{m})+x_{0}(y_{m}+z_{m})).

Inserting the bound (
2.8
), we obtain

2(x_{0}y_{0}+y_{0}z_{0}+z_{0}x_{0})+20W\leq 11(9-W),

and so we have

(2.9)

2(x_{0}y_{0}+y_{0}z_{0}+z_{0}x_{0})+31W\leq 99.

Therefore, using (
2.6
), (
2.6
) and (
2.9
), we obtain

\displaystyle U+V-W

\displaystyle\leq 9+(x_{0}y_{0}+y_{0}z_{0}+z_{0}x_{0})+m^{2}W-W

\displaystyle\leq 9+\frac{1}{2}(99-31W)+m^{2}W-W

\displaystyle\leq 49.5+W(m^{2}-16.5)

\displaystyle\leq 3m^{2},

Where the last inequality holds since
W\leq 3
, and
m\geq 5.

Finally we suppose at least one of
x_{m}+y_{m},y_{m}+z_{m},z_{m}+x_{m}
is less than
0.6
, say
x_{m}+y_{m}<0.6.
Since
x_{m}+y_{m}\geq 0
, we have
x_{m}y_{m}<(\frac{0.6}{2})^{2}
. Observe

W=z_{m}(x_{m}+y_{m})+x_{m}y_{m}\leq 3(0.6)+(\frac{0.6}{2})^{2}<2.

Hence, using this bound on
W
, (
2.7
) and (
2.6
),

U+V-W\leq 9+(x_{0}y_{0}+y_{0}z_{0}+z_{0}x_{0})+2(m^{2}-1)\leq 36+2(m^{2}-1)\leq 3m^{2}+9

for
m\geq 5.

∎

3.
Proof of local result

In this section we will prove our local result Theorem
1.2
. It is our key tool which will make the transference principle argument work, and it will follow from Propositions
3.1
and
3.2
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
m
be a squarefree integer which satisfies
(m,30)=1.
Let
f:\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{*}\rightarrow[0,1]
satisfy

\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{*}}f(a)>\frac{\varphi(m)}{2}.

Then for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
there exists
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{*}
with
x=a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}
such that

f(a_{1})f(a_{2})+f(a_{2})f(a_{3})+f(a_{3})f(a_{1})>\frac{1}{2}(f(a_{1})+f(a_{2})+f(a_{3})).

Proof.

See Proposition
3.1
in Shao
[
4
]
. The proof is identical upon swapping the occurrences of
\frac{5}{8}
with
\frac{1}{2}
.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}:\mathbb{Z}_{15}^{*}\rightarrow[0,1]
be functions such that
f_{i}(x)=0
for all
x\equiv 2\pmod{3}
,
i=1,2,3
. Let

F_{i}=\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{15}^{*}}f_{i}(a),

and suppose that

(3.1)

F_{1}F_{2}+F_{2}F_{3}+F_{3}F_{1}>2(F_{1}+F_{2}+F_{3}).

Then for any
x\equiv 0\pmod{3}
there exist
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}_{15}^{*}
with
x=a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}
such that

f_{i}(a_{i})>0,f_{1}(a_{1})+f_{2}(a_{2})+f_{3}(a_{3})>\frac{3}{2}.

Proof.

Let
A_{i}
be the support of
f_{i}
, and so
A_{i}=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{15}^{*}:f_{i}(x)>0\}\subset\{1,4,7,13\}.
Let
n_{i}=|A_{i}|
, without loss of generality we may assume that
n_{1}\geq n_{2}\geq n_{3}.
Since
n_{i}\geq F_{i}
, we have that

(3.2)

n_{1}n_{2}+n_{2}n_{3}+n_{3}n_{1}>2(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}).

Let
M
be the set of all possible
(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
satisfying (
3.2
), with
n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}.
Fix some
(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})\in M.
For
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, write

A_{i}=\{x_{i1},x_{i2},\dots,x_{in_{i}}\}\quad\textrm{and}\quad y_{ij}=f_{i}(x_{ij}),

for
1\leq j\leq n_{i}.
Without loss of generality assume that
y_{i1}\geq y_{i2}\geq\dots\geq y_{in_{i}}.

Let
J
be the set of all triples
(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})
satisfying

(3.3)

j_{1}+j_{2}+j_{3}>6,\quad 1\leq j_{i}\leq n_{i}.

Suppose
y_{1j_{1}}+y_{2j_{2}}+y_{3j_{3}}>\frac{3}{2}
for some
(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})\in J
. The three sets
A_{i}=\{x_{i1},\dots,x_{ij_{i}}\}
for
i=1,2,3
are subsets of

\mathbb{Z}_{15}^{*}\cap\{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{15}:x\equiv 1\pmod{3}\}.

Making use of the isomorphism
\mathbb{Z}_{15}^{*}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{3}^{*}\times\mathbb{Z}_{5}^{*}
we can view
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{5}^{*}
. It follows by Cauchy-Davenport-Chowla that

|A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3}|\geq\min(5,|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|+|A_{3}|-2)\geq 5,

where the last inequality follows since
|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|+|A_{3}|=j_{1}+j_{2}+j_{3}>6
by (
3.3
). Therefore viewing
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}
as subsets of

\mathbb{Z}_{15}^{*}\cap\{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{15}:x\equiv 1\pmod{3}\},

it follows that

A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3}=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{15}:x\equiv 0\pmod{3}\}.

Hence for any
x\equiv 0\pmod{3}
, we can write
x\equiv a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}\pmod{15}
with
a_{i}\in A_{i},
such that

f(a_{i})\geq y_{ij_{i}}>0,\quad f(a_{1})+f(a_{2})+f(a_{3})\geq y_{1j_{1}}+y_{2j_{2}}+y_{3j_{3}}>\frac{3}{2}.

We now suppose for a contradiction that

y_{1j_{1}}+y_{2j_{2}}+y_{3j_{3}}\leq\frac{3}{2}

for each triple
(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})\in J.
We can set up the following optimization problem with variables
y_{ij}\in[0,1]
for
1\leq j\leq n_{i}
and constraints given by
y_{1j_{1}}+y_{2j_{2}}+y_{3j_{3}}\leq\frac{3}{2}
for all
(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3})\in J.
Our objective is to maximize the sum of all variables:

(3.4)

S=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}y_{ij}=F_{1}+F_{2}+F_{3}.

The constraints and the objective function in this optimization problem are all linear, and the maximum of
S
can be found using a linear programming algorithm. Our conclusion is the following.

F_{1}+F_{2}+F_{3}\leq\begin{cases}6.5&(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})=(4,4,1)\\
6.2&(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})=(4,4,2)\\
6&\textrm{otherwise}\par\end{cases}

Suppose
F_{1}+F_{2}+F_{3}\leq 6.
Applying Cauchy-Schwarz it follows that

(3.5)

F_{1}F_{2}+F_{2}F_{3}+F_{3}F_{1}\leq\frac{1}{3}(F_{1}+F_{2}+F_{3})^{2}\leq 2(F_{1}+F_{2}+F_{3}),

which contradicts the condition (
3.1
). We will now show the other possibilities for the triple
(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
also lead to a contradiction.

For notational convenience, we will write

T(x,y,z)=xy+yz+zx-2(x+y+z).

By the condition (
3.1
) it follows that
T(F_{1},F_{2},F_{3})>0
and that
T(x,y,z)
is an increasing function of
x,y,z
in the range
x\geq F_{1}
,
y\geq F_{2}
,
z\geq F_{3}
.

Now suppose
(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})=(4,4,2)
. If
F_{3}\leq 1,
it follows that

T(F_{1},F_{2},F_{3})\leq T(2.6,2.6,1)<0.

Hence we may assume
F_{3}\geq 1,
solving the optimization problem as before but with this additional constraint, we have

F_{1}+F_{2}+F_{3}\leq 6.

This is a contradiction due to inequality (
3.5
).

Finally suppose
(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})=(4,4,1),
then

F_{1}+F_{2}+F_{3}\leq 6.5.

Consider the case where
F_{3}\leq 0.5,
then

T(F_{1},F_{2},F_{3})\leq T(3,3,0.5)<0.

Hence we may assume
F_{3}\geq 0.5,
including this as an additional constraint and solving the optimization problem as before we obtain

F_{1}+F_{2}+F_{3}\leq 6.

This is a contradiction due to inequality (
3.5
).
∎

We now prove our local result by an induction argument
1.2
.

Proof.

Let
m
be an odd square free integer divisible by
15
. Let
m=15m^{\prime}
where
(m^{\prime},30)=1.
Identifying
\mathbb{Z}_{m}\equiv\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{\prime}\times\mathbb{Z}_{15}
, let
(u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}
with
v\equiv 0\pmod{3}
where
u\in\mathbb{Z}_{m^{\prime}},v\in\mathbb{Z}_{15}
. Define

f^{\prime}(x)=\frac{2}{\phi(15)}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}_{15}^{*}}f(x,y)

for
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{m^{\prime}}^{*}
. Observe the sum
\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{m^{\prime}}^{*}}f^{\prime}(x)>\frac{\phi(m^{\prime})}{2}
so we may use Proposition
3.1
to conclude that there exists
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}_{m^{\prime}}^{*}
with
u=a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}
such that

(3.6)

f^{\prime}(a_{1})f^{\prime}(a_{2})+f^{\prime}(a_{2})f^{\prime}(a_{3})+f^{\prime}(a_{3})f^{\prime}(a_{1})>\frac{1}{2}(f^{\prime}(a_{1})+f^{\prime}(a_{2})+f^{\prime}(a_{3})).

Define
f_{i}:\mathbb{Z}_{15}^{*}\rightarrow[0,1]
for
i=1,2,3
by

f_{i}(y)=f(a_{i},y).

The condition (
3.6
) satisfied by the
f^{\prime}(a_{i})
allows the use of Proposition
3.2
to conclude that there exists
b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}_{15}^{*}
with
v=b_{1}+b_{2}+b_{3},
such that

f_{i}(b_{i})>0\quad\textrm{and}\quad f_{1}(b_{1})+f_{2}(b_{2})+f_{3}(b_{3})>\frac{3}{2}.

Therefore we have
(u,v)=(a_{1},b_{1})+(a_{2},b_{2})+(a_{3},b_{3})
with

f(a_{i},b_{i})>0\quad\textrm{and}\quad f(a_{1},b_{1})+f(a_{2},b_{2})+f(a_{3},b_{3})>\frac{3}{2},

as desired.

∎

4.
Transference principle

In this section we state the variant of the transference principle which we will use in the next section to deduce our main result. We will record the statement as it is in Proposition 4.1 in
[
4
]
.

Let
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
be the cyclic group of integers modulo
N.
. Define the Fourier transform of a function
f:\mathbb{Z}_{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
by

\widehat{f}(r)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}f(n)e_{N}(rn),\quad r\in\mathbb{Z}_{N},

where
e_{N}(rn)=\exp(2\pi rn/N).

For two functions
f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{Z}_{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, their convolution
f_{1}*f_{2}
is defined by

f_{1}*f_{2}(n)=\sum_{n_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}f_{1}(n_{1})f_{2}(n-n_{1}),\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
N
be a sufficiently large prime. Suppose that
\nu_{i}:\mathbb{Z}_{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
f_{i}:\mathbb{Z}_{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}(i=1,2,3)
are functions satisfying the majorization condition

(4.1)

0\leq f_{i}(n)\leq\nu_{i}(n),

and the mean condition

(4.2)

\min(\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\delta_{3},\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}+\delta_{3}-1)\geq\delta

for some
\delta>0,
where the
\delta_{i}=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}f_{i}(x).
Suppose that
\nu_{i}
and
f_{i}
also satisfy the pseudorandomness conditions

(4.3)

|\hat{\nu}_{i}(r)-\delta_{r,0}|\leq\eta

for all
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, where
\delta_{r,0}
is the Kronecker delta, and

(4.4)

\|\hat{f}_{i}\|_{q}=\Big(\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}|\hat{f}_{i}(r)|^{q}\Big)^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq M

for some
2<q<3
and
\eta,M>0.
Then for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{N},
we have

(4.5)

\sum_{y,z\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}f_{1}(y)f_{2}(z)f_{3}(x-y-z)\geq\frac{c(\delta)}{N}

for some constant
c(\delta)>0
depending only on
\delta,
provided that
\eta=\eta(\delta,M,q)
is small enough.

Proof.

See Proposition 4.1 in
[
4
]
.
∎

5.
Ternary Goldbach for dense subsets of primes in a fixed residue class

In this section we prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof.

Let
n
be a sufficiently large odd integer such that
n\equiv 0\pmod{3}
. Let
A\subset\mathcal{P}_{1,3}
be a subset satisfying
\underline{\delta}(A)>1/2
. Then there exists a positive constant
\delta>0
such that

(5.1)

|A\cap[1,N]|>(\frac{1}{2}+\delta)\frac{N}{2\log N},

for all sufficiently large positive integers
N.

Set
W=\prod_{p\leq z}p
, where
z=z(\delta)
is a constant sufficiently large in terms of
\delta
.
For each reduced residue class
b\pmod{W}
, define

f(b)=\max(\frac{\phi(W)3}{2n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in A\cap[1,\frac{2n}{3}]\\
x\equiv b\pmod{W}\end{subarray}}\log x-\frac{\delta}{8},0).

Note the support of
f
is contained in those reduced residue classes congruent to
1\pmod{3}
and
0\leq f(b)\leq 1
for all
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{W}^{*}.
Moreover by the prime number theorem in arithmetic progressions, (
5.1
) implies

\sum_{b\in\mathbb{Z}_{W}^{*}}f(b)>\frac{\phi(W)}{4}.

Let
m=W/2
, and since
\mathbb{Z}_{W}^{*}
can be identified with
\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{*}
we may consider
f
as a function
f:\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{*}\rightarrow[0,1]
satisfying

\sum_{b\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{*}}f(b)>\frac{\phi(m)}{4}.

Hence we may apply our local result
1.2
to conclude there exist
b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}_{W}^{*}
such that
n\equiv b_{1}+b_{2}+b_{3}\pmod{m}
and

(5.2)

f(b_{i})>0,\quad f(b_{1})+f(b_{2})+f(b_{3})>\frac{3}{2}.

Since
n
is odd,
n\equiv b_{1}+b_{2}+b_{3}\pmod{2}
and therefore
n\equiv b_{1}+b_{2}+b_{3}\pmod{W}.
Now define

A_{i}=\{\frac{x-b_{i}}{W}:x\in A\cap[1,\frac{2n}{3}],x\equiv b_{i}\pmod{W}\},

and so it suffices to show that

\frac{n-b_{1}-b_{2}-b_{3}}{W}\in A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3}.

Let
\kappa>0
be sufficiently small, and choose a prime

N\in\Big[\frac{(1+\kappa)n}{W},\frac{(1+2\kappa)n}{W}\Big].

By our choice of
N
it suffices to show that
\frac{n-b_{1}-b_{2}-b_{3}}{W}\in A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3}
when the sets
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}
are considered subsets of
\mathbb{Z}_{N}.

For
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, define
f_{i},\nu_{i}:\mathbb{Z}_{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
(naturally identifying
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
with
\{1,2,\cdots,N\}
) by

\nu_{i}(n)=\begin{cases}\frac{\phi(W)}{WN}\log(Wn+b_{i})&\text{if}\ Wn+b_{i}\in\mathcal{P},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

and

f_{i}(n)=\nu_{i}(n)1_{A_{i}}(n).

We will show that
f_{i},\nu_{i}
satisfy the assumptions of Proposition
4.1
. Clearly
0\leq f_{i}(n)\leq\nu_{i}(n)
for every
n
and so (
4.1
) holds trivially. Now we verify the mean value estimate (
4.4
) on
\|\widehat{f_{i}}\|_{p}.

Lemma 5.1

(Mean value estimate)
.

Suppose that
p>2
. Then there is an absolute constant
C(p)>0
such that
\|\widehat{f_{i}}\|_{p}\leq C(p).

Proof.

See Lemma 6.6 in
[
1
]
with exponent
p=3
(say)
∎

Now we verify the the Fourier decay property (
4.3
) of
\nu_{i}
.

Lemma 5.2

(Fourier decay property)
.

Suppose that
N
and
z
are sufficiently large. Then

\sup_{r\neq 0}|\hat{\nu_{i}}(r)|\leq\frac{2\log\log z}{z}.

Proof.

See Lemma 6.2 in
[
1
]
.
∎

This establishes (
4.3
) when
r\neq 0
. The case when
r=0
follows from the prime number theorem.

Now we verify the requirement (
4.2
) on the averages of
f_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
. Observe that

\delta_{i}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}f_{i}(n)=\frac{\phi(W)}{NW}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in A\cap[1,\frac{2n}{3}]\\
