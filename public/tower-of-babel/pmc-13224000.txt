Evaluating the implementation of self-management skill readiness assessment for adolescents and young adults with spina bifida
Authors: Megan Gallagher, Rhonda G Cady
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpina bifida (SB) is a complex condition affecting brain, bowel, bladder, cognitive, motor, and sensory functions. Evidence shows that adolescents and young adults (AYAs) with SB who master self-management of their complex condition experience better health outcomes. Assessment and support of AYA self-management using tools like the Transition Readiness Assessment Questionnaire Spina Bifida (TRAQ-SB) is recommended by the Spina Bifida Association.MethodsAn explanatory sequential mixed-methods evaluation grounded in the RE-AIM framework investigated reach, effectiveness, adoption, and implementation of the TRAQ-SB into a multi-disciplinary specialty SB clinic. Medical record chart review quantified RE-AIM metrics and informed design of the qualitative strand, focus group sessions for three distinct ‘users’ of the TRAQ-SB: AYAs with SB, their parents, and their clinicians.FindingsSelf-management skill acquisition is a positive step towards AYA independence, but various factors impact this move. While most AYA with SB completed the TRAQ-SB, only 25% of clinicians documented discussion of the tool to inform the AYA plan of care. This indicates inadequate clinician adoption of the TRAQ-SB. Parents of AYA with SB that depend on others for self-care desire a different assessment and indicate reduced effectiveness of the TRAQ-SB. AYA with SB want the ‘gentleness and tenderness’ of the pediatric care system to extend to the adult care system.ConclusionsOur evaluation provided insight into who completed the TRAQ-SB, how it was or was not used by clinicians, and the impact of internal and external environmental factors, providing support for self-management readiness assessment of AYAs with SB.Keywords: Healthcare transition, Young adults, Spina bifida, Adolescents, Transition readiness

Introduction

Spina bifida (SB) is a neural tube defect in which the spine does not close properly during fetal development and is characterized by an extrusion of cerebrospinal fluid and meninges through the spinal opening.1 Categorized as a developmental disability, SB affects brain, bowel, bladder, cognitive, motor, and sensory functions.2 Medical and surgical advances have increased the life expectancy of persons living with complex conditions,3 including SB,4, 5 into adulthood. Subsequently, adolescents with SB must shift from pediatric health providers and systems to adult health providers and systems, and this shift is defined as healthcare transition (HCT).6

Successful HCT is an ongoing process that supports adolescents and young adults (AYAs) and when assistance is needed, their caregivers (such as parents, siblings, or partners), to prepare for transfer and integration into the adult healthcare model.6 Successful HCT matters for all AYAs, and is especially critical for AYA with SB, who must master self-management of their complex health condition.7 Research indicates that AYAs with SB who fail to establish care with adult providers have higher emergency department visits and adverse health outcomes.8 These poor health outcomes often come from a lack of self-management skills needed to administer medications and schedule follow-up appointments.9, 10 This is compounded by the impact of SB on executive and cognitive function, and the bowel, bladder, and skin co-morbidities common with SB.7 Conversely, AYA with SB who effectively self-manage have better health outcomes.11 This evidence indicates the importance of assessing AYA self-management skills and assisting AYA in acquiring these skills as needed.

The Six Core Elements of HCT™12 (Fig. 1) are an evidence-informed set of tools and processes designed to guide and monitor the successful transition of AYAs from pediatric to adult healthcare.6, 12 The Six Core Elements of HCT™12 are the foundation for HCT guidelines developed by the Spina Bifida Association (SBA)13 and the SB HCT Toolkit published by the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP).14 Element Three of the Six Core Elements of HCT™12 is “Transition Readiness” and includes tools and processes to assess AYA mastery of essential health-related self-management skills that include understanding how to manage health conditions, having an emergency plan, filling prescriptions, and discussing health conditions with a new provider.12 Commonly referred to as transition readiness assessments (TRAs), these tools are designed to help AYAs identify self-management skills they have not mastered and to help clinicians develop a plan of care that supports AYAs’ acquisition of these skills.12

Six Core Elements of Health Care Transition™.12.

An evidence-based SB-specific TRA is the Transition Readiness Assessment Questionnaire Spina Bifida (TRAQ-SB).15, 16 The 12-item tool was designed to help AYA with SB develop mastery of SB-specific self-management skills and includes monitoring shunt function, maintaining intact skin, and ensuring proper bowel and bladder function.16 The TRAQ-SB uses a 5-point Likert response set using a Stages of Change Framework, scale of 1–5, with 1 being “No, I do not know” and 5 being “Yes, I always do this.” It is a validated tool with strong internal reliability and criterion validity15, 16 and is recommended by the SBA HCT guidelines13 for regular AYA self-management skill assessment. Over time, the progress of AYA can be assessed, and new self-management skills are introduced and reinforced.

Following AAP and SBA HCT guidelines, in 2022, Gillette Children's (Gillette) initiated collection and use of the self-management skill assessment tool TRAQ-SB,16 from AYA with SB, to support Element Three, Transition Readiness.12 Implementing new tools and processes into busy clinical workflows requires an implementation science lens17 to ensure uptake of the evidence-based tool and supporting processes, and to promote sustainability of use over time.18 This paper describes an explanatory sequential mixed methods study evaluating the implementation of evidence-based tools and processes to support Element Three of the Six Core Elements of HCT,™12 Transition Readiness, for AYAs with SB. The study was grounded in the RE-AIM framework of reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, and maintenance.19, 20 The perspective of all ‘users’ of these tools and processes: AYAs with SB, parents of AYAs with SB, and clinicians working with AYAs with SB, were explored. Our findings are reported below.

Methods

The study was approved by Gillette Children’s Institutional Review Board.

Setting and participants

The setting for this study is Gillette Children's (Gillette), an urban, midwestern, independent, not-for-profit, specialty health system. Gillette provides pediatric and adult specialty care for persons living with brain, bone, and movement conditions that start in childhood. These conditions include, but are not limited to, acquired brain injury, cerebral palsy, epilepsy, genetic conditions, hydrocephalus, muscular dystrophy, Rett syndrome, and spina bifida. Persons living across Minnesota (MN) and surrounding border states travel to Gillette for their specialty care.

Lifespan specialty care for persons with SB is provided in Gillette Children’s Pediatric and Adult SB Coordinated Clinics. The Pediatric SB Coordinated Clinic sees children from birth to approximately 18 years old, and available specialties include neurosurgery, nutrition, orthopedics, physical medicine and rehabilitation, physical therapy, therapeutic recreation, and urology. Transition to the Adult SB Coordinated Clinic typically begins at age 18 and is done in collaboration with AYAs, their family, and pediatric SB specialists. The Adult SB Coordinated Clinic sees adults from 18 years onward, and available specialties include neurosurgery, physical medicine and rehabilitation, physical therapy, therapeutic recreation, and urology. An exception to the 18 year age is 16 – 17-year-old youth with SB who are ‘new to Gillette’ and have not established care in any Gillette pediatric clinic. These youth are seen in the Adult SB Coordinated Clinic to establish a lifelong connection with adult SB specialists.

Both the Pediatric and Adult SB Coordinated Clinics are certified as Spina Bifida Association Clinical Care Partners (SBA CCP).21 Certified partners provide clinical care grounded in the evidence-based SBA Guidelines for the Care of People with Spina Bifida22 and include HCT guidelines.13 Initial use of the SBA recommended TRAQ-SB16 began in December 2022, with AYA age 15–25, seen in Gillette Children’s SB Coordinated Clinics and participating in the National Spina Bifida Patient Registry (NSBPR). A description of the NSBPR is provided elsewhere.23, 24 The NSBPR clinical research coordinator (CRC) provided a paper copy of the TRAQ-SB16 to AYA, and when appropriate, their parents. Upon completion of the TRAQ-SB,16 the CRC offered a brief explanation of the tool and a paper copy, to all SB Coordinated Clinic providers seeing the AYA. Several providers consistently used the TRAQ-SB16 to inform the visit plan of care.

In late 2023, Gillette initiated a pediatric to adult healthcare transition initiative (PAHTI). A team of clinicians, persons with lived experience, outcome measurement and process improvement experts adapted the Six Core Elements of HCT™12 to align with Gillette Children’s care delivery model. The PAHTI implementation plan used our organization-specific process improvement method adapted from the Model for Improvement.25

AYA aged 15–25 years were identified as the target population for PAHTI tools and processes. This age range is older than recommended by the Six Core Elements of HCT™12 and selected to align with Gillette Children’s internal HCT policy. SB providers that previously used the TRAQ-SB16 helped the PAHTI team develop standardized, electronic processes for AYA/parent completion of the TRAQ-SB16 and incorporation into the AYA electronic medical record (EMR), and standardized workflows to integrate the completed tool into the work of clinic providers.

The same SB provider champions advocated for initial implementation of the PAHTI tools and processes into the SB Coordinated Clinics. In October 2024, the SB provider champions conducted multiple in-person and virtual educational meetings with SB Coordinated Clinic staff and described the new HCT tools and workflow processes that would be implemented beginning November 2024. Similar information was shared on the Gillette Children’s internal website.

The primary participants for this study are AYAs aged 15 – 25 and seen in Gillette Children’s Pediatric or Adult SB Coordinated Clinic between December 2022 and July 2025.

Conceptual design

We used an explanatory sequential mixed methods design (Fig. 2) to evaluate the implementation of Element Three of the Six Core Elements of HCT,™12 Transition Readiness, for AYA with SB. Transition readiness is defined as the tools and processes that support assessment and acquisition of health-related self-management skill tools and processes. Our evaluation used the RE-AIM20 metrics of reach, effectiveness, adoption and implementation. Reach20 describes the population intended for self-management skill assessment and includes AYA with SB seen in Gillette Children’s SB Coordinated Clinics. Effectiveness20 describes the benefit of self-management skill assessment and is measured by the mean score of the TRAQ-SB16 responses. Adoption20 describes ‘system level uptake’ or use of the newly implemented self-management skill assessment tools and processes by clinicians.20 Implementation describes ‘system level consistency’ of using the same tools and processes to inform the AYA plan of care.20

Conceptual Model.

Quantitative and qualitative strands were equally prioritized and directly interacted. The quantitative strand reviewed the medical record of all primary participants to quantify the RE-AIM20 metrics. Findings from the quantitative strand informed design of the qualitative strand and included development of a focus group guide for three district groups of participants: AYAs with SB, parents of AYAs with SB, and clinicians working with AYAs with SB. Merging and interpretation of the quantitative and qualitative strands provided a robust evaluation of the reach, effectiveness, adoption and implementation of the tools and processes that support self-management skill ‘readiness.’

Data collection

The data collection timeframe consists of two periods. The ‘Pre-PAHTI’ period is from December 2022 – October 2024 and is limited to AYA that completed the TRAQ-SB16 on paper and as a component of their NSBPR enrollment. The ‘Post-PAHTI’ period is from November 2024 – July 2025. During this period ALL AYA seen in the SB Coordinated Clinics were given the opportunity to complete the TRAQ-SB16 electronically.

Data collection tools (excluding the TRAQ-SB16) for the quantitative and qualitative strands were developed by a team of research experts (A, B) and clinical and lived experience experts from Gillette Children’s PAHTI team. During the quantitative strand, a single researcher (A) reviewed the EMR documentation of all primary participants to capture the: TRAQ-SB16 responses, if completed; and documentation in the clinic note or after visit summary indicating use of the TRAQ-SB16 responses to inform the AYA’s plan of care. All data were stored in a secure REDCap database26, 27 hosted by Gillette.

The qualitative strand used virtual focus groups to further explore and contextualize findings from the quantitative medical record review. A focus group is conducted with a small group, generally 4–9 participants with similar characteristics, to better understand how the group perceives and experiences an issue, idea, or service.28 The similar characteristic of our participants was based on their affiliation to Gillette Children’s SB Coordinated Clinics and resulted in three focus group cohorts: 1) AYAs with SB or ‘patient’; 2) Parents of AYAs with SB or ‘parent of patient’; and 3) clinicians working in the SB Coordinated Clinic or ‘clinician’ (Fig. 2). The shared experience of our participants involved completing or using the TRAQ-SB self-management skill assessment tool.

Focus groups can be conducted in-person or virtually and both formats have shown comparable quantities of data.29, 30 Virtual focus groups have the added benefit of wider geographical reach and increased representation.31 A focus group is guided by a trained facilitator28 and was selected over individual interviews for two reasons. First, the iterative nature of a focus group enhances group dynamics and can elicit a broader range of ideas than individual interviews.28, 29 Second, focus groups are more likely to generate disclosure of sensitive or personal information, especially when conducted virtually.29

Development of the focus group guide was conducted through an iterative process with the previously described experts. Focus group questions (Appendix 1) were informed by findings from the quantitative strand and explored the effectiveness, adoption and implementation of the transition readiness assessment (TRAQ-SB16) employed to support Element Three of the Six Core Elements of HCT,™12 Transition Readiness. A separate focus group was conducted with each cohort (Fig. 2), resulting in three focus groups. The same questions were used for the AYA and parent of AYA focus groups. Wording of the clinician focus group questions differed slightly, and question #3 of the AYA/Parent group, asking ‘what was missing in the clinic visit’, was omitted for the clinician group.

The AYA and parent focus groups were scheduled for September 2025 and the clinician focus group scheduled for October 2025. A single email invitation was sent to each cohort of potential participants, based on their affiliation to Gillette Children’s SB Coordinated Clinics: 1) 25 AYAs with SB; 2) 23 parents of AYAs with SB; and 3) 15 clinicians working with AYAs with SB. Potential participants self-selected to register for a focus group and provided demographic and contact information. An email was sent confirming a person’s registration and providing the virtual focus group link.

Each of the three focus group sessions were conducted over Zoom (version 6.6.6, San Jose, CA, Zoom Video Communications), used closed captioning for accessibility, and lasted 75 min. One investigator (A) facilitated each session, and the second investigator (B) took notes. AYAs and parents of AYAs were offered a $40 gift card as compensation for their time and lived experience expertise. Clinicians were not provided with any compensation. Sessions were audio transcribed for data analysis.

Data analysis

Descriptive analysis of the quantitative data was conducted for the Pre-PAHTI and Post-PAHTI periods. Reach was measured as the number of AYAs completing the TRAQ-SB, divided by the number of AYAs eligible to complete the TRAQ-SB, and was calculated only for the Post-PAHTI period. Effectiveness was defined as the TRAQ-SB score and followed the developer guidelines of averaging item scores to determine a mean score.15, 16 Adoption and implementation were defined as clinician documentation of using the TRAQ-SB responses to inform the AYA plan of care. Findings from the quantitative analysis informed and were integrated into the development of the focus group guide.

Thematic analysis32 of the qualitative focus group transcripts used an iterative and inductive approach to code and develop themes. Transcript data was coded manually and separately by two analysts (A, B), and no a priori themes or theoretical framework were used. Codes emerging from the data were discussed iteratively, and a consensus was reached on discrepancies. Refinement of overlapping codes was achieved, and final codes were organized into themes and subthemes. Verbatim excerpts from the focus group transcripts were selected for illustration of findings.

Mixed methods interpretation consisted of assessing how quantitative results and qualitative findings addressed the reach, effectiveness, adoption and implementation of the new tools and processes that support Element Three of the Six Core Elements of HCT, ™12 for AYAs with SB seen at Gillette.

Connecting data sources

Multiple factors to facilitate clinician adoption and implementation of the TRAQ-SB were introduced between the Pre- and Post-PAHTI periods. These included automation of TRAQ-SB16 collection, inclusion of the completed tool in the EMR, and clinician education about the new tool. Despite these factors, there was minimal change in the ‘documented uptake’ of clinician use of the TRAQ-SB tool to inform the plan of care. We expected the TRAQ-SB scores associated with clinician documentation of educational actions would fall into the not knowing how to do self-management (1) or wanting to learn self-management (2) range. Although our sample was small, this did not occur and the mean TRAQ-SB scores varied from wanting to learn self-management (2) to full mastery of self-management (5). These unexpected findings informed development of the qualitative strand to better understand the effectiveness, adoption and implementation of self-management skill assessment for AYA with SB.

Clinician Focus Group Themes and Subthemes.

Theme: stepping towards independence

AYAs with SB, parents of AYA that can direct their own self-care, and clinicians perceived that completing and using the TRAQ-SB16 helped them realize that self-management is more than knowing how to do something; it requires doing the self-management tasks and understanding why the tasks are important [1.1]. AYA participants varied in their trajectory of learning self-management skill, with older AYAs viewing the tool as helpful to “their younger self” and teen AYAs viewing the tool as “helping me understand my symptoms better, myself better, so that I’m actually becoming more independent” [1.2]. Parents of AYAs that can direct their own self-care described how the process “opened a new layer of conversation [with their AYA]” and helped them realize the need for “letting go” and having their AYA “start taking more control” [1.3]. Clinicians working with AYAs felt the completed tool triggered conversations where they realized the youth was less experienced or knowledgeable about self-management skill than initially assessed [3.1]. These findings provide context for the higher than expected TRAQ-SB16 scores.

AYAs with the ability to manage their care, their parents of AYAs, and their clinicians, endorsed discussing self-management readiness early (beginning at age 13), as suggested in the literature, and continuing these discussions into early adulthood [1.2; 3.2]. However, as described by the AYAs and parent of AYAs participants, none had this experience; their first self-management skill assessment occurred at age 17 or older. The older age of the AYAs first assessment reflects the very recent implementation of self-management skill assessment tools and processes into the SB Coordinated Clinics workflow and could explain the high TRAQ-SB16 scores.

A final subtheme in stepping towards independence and expressed by AYA, is keeping the gentleness in healthcare [1.4], regardless of age. AYA with SB are subject to multiple and often painful procedures and “keeping the gentleness and tenderness when you’re doing procedures, labs, or different things that might be invasive.…. like it might seem childish a little bit, but I know a lot of people who are terrified” and helps the move toward independence. This finding was not captured in the quantitative findings and reflects the impact of organizational culture on implementation success.

Theme: delaying movement toward independence

All participants identified situations that delay or hinder AYA movement towards self-management and independence, but these impediments varied based on the focus group participants’ role (AYA, parent, clinician). All AYAs and parents described feeling unwanted or invisible [2.1] when seeing new adult clinicians that are not affiliated with the SB Coordinated Clinics. One parent explained the reaction of these new adult clinicians in general, “…They look at you with glazed-over eyes, like, what? And they're so not ready to, like, jump in.” AYAs expressed the importance of all clinicians talking to them, not their parents, “meeting new doctors, they immediately talked to my mom. So, then that can be hard for me to almost get a question in.” AYA also noted that these experiences extend to feelings of not being listened to [2.2] by clinicians, “like, I know my body, and this is an issue, and it's… been, uh…pushed off basically” and “it's a matter of, you know, who's gonna actually listen.” These findings were not reflected in the quantitative findings and highlight how external environmental factors can negatively impact AYAs move toward independence and self-management skill ‘readiness.’

Two of the parent participants have AYA that are dependent on others for self-care and have a different viewpoint of their AYAs independence. They described how their AYA benefited from medical and surgical advances that increased the longevity of children with complex conditions like SB. These parents described different ‘transition readiness’ support needs [2.3] that included help assessing and understanding the “next level of independent living and what does that look like?” for their AYA. They also felt frustrated with the limited opportunities for their AYA, “So you fix the medical part, but then there's this social… economic part that hasn't caught up with it.” This finding indicates a lack of effectiveness and usefulness of the TRAQ-SB16 for this group of AYA with SB.

Clinicians noted that AYA perception of self-management ability may differ from the clinicians' perception [4.1]: “SB youth have trends of misperceiving what they are able to do or are actively doing so have doubts about the answers.” They also noted a lack of AYA knowledge about their condition, especially for persons transferring their adult care to Gillette from another pediatric system: “these new patients …. I’m like OK, so tell me what you know about your shunt, or your hydrocephalus or whatever. And they're like, I don't know…… so I have start with their fetal anatomy.” These findings could explain the discrepancy between high TRAQ-SB16 scores and clinician documentation that included education to increase AYA self-management skill.

Last was acknowledgment by several clinician participants of their lack of awareness about Gillette Children’s newly implemented self-management skill assessment tools and processes [4.2]. “Just kind of getting people (clinicians) more familiar with the resources and really having this work go hand in hand with the other” is needed. This finding could explain the lack of ‘uptake’ of the TRAQ-SB16 found in the quantitative strand. This is an important and consequential finding and indicates that our strategy of ‘didactic clinician education’ prior to implementation was not successful. Clinicians also expressed the need for medical record “… flagging notable findings or answers that the parent or the patient give” to help support their workflow and discussions. This finding provides insight into how EMR technology could support adoption and implementation of the new tools and processes.

Discussion

A key component of implementing evidence-based tools and processes into a clinical environment is evaluating the impact through an implementation science lens.17 This is critical because while evidence suggests these tools and processes will improve health outcomes, implementation science has shown that those outcomes only happen if the persons we intended to use the tools and processes actually use them.18 We conducted a mixed-methods study to evaluate implementation of tools and processes to support self-management skill assessment and acquisition, defined as ‘transition readiness’ in the Six Core Elements of HCT,™12 in outpatient specialty clinics serving AYA with SB. Grounded in the RE-AIM framework of reach, effectiveness, adoption and implementation,20 our findings highlight the importance of considering all ‘users’ of transition readiness tools and processes.

We learned that the reach of self-management skill assessment using the evidence-based TRAQ-SB16 must be evaluated for both AYAs and their clinicians. Completion of the TRAQ-SB16 by AYA is the first component of reach and similar to current HCT guidelines on self-management skill ‘readiness’,12, 14 we found that AYAs with the ability to manage their care, and their parents, endorse the value of early and on-going self-management skill assessment. Helping SB clinicians to understand why the TRAQ-SB16 responses should inform the clinic visit is the second component of reach. Our didactic education strategy for clinicians did not achieve this goal and reflects an absence of adult learning principles.33

We learned that the effectiveness of self-management skill assessment using the evidence-based TRAQ-SB16 is more than a score. It facilitates critical discussions between AYAs with SB, their parents and their clinicians, that enhance the AYAs move toward independence. For AYA with the ability to direct or provide their own care, this finding provides an important ‘why should I use the TRAQ-SB’ and should be incorporated into future clinician education. For this group of AYA we also learned that self-management skill assessment should focus on whether an AYA performs the skills and understands why the skills are important instead of assessing knowing how to do the skill. This subtle but important difference requires further investigation.

Both AYA and clinician participants acknowledged the importance of AYAs understanding self-management of their health conditions and our findings add to the body of knowledge7, 8, 9, 10, 11 regarding the importance of assessing the self-management skill of AYAs with SB. We found that AYA can misestimate their self-management knowledge and this finding has been previously described from the perspective of AYA executive function.11 Our work adds a new finding to the body of evidence of the potential for clinician ‘misestimation’ of AYA ability and the need for objective assessment and discussion to mitigate potential bias.

The adoption of self-management skill assessment using the evidence-based TRAQ-SB was driven by SBA HCT Guidelines.13 This adoption strategy aligns with recent investigation of HCT processes at SBA CCP clinics across the United States. Self-management was identified as a critical dimension of HCT success by 70% of these clinics and 65% reported using the TRAQ-SB16 for self-management skill assessment.34 Equally important is acknowledging that AYA with SB have varying levels of physical and cognitive ability which can impact their capacity for self-care and self-management. This variation requires use of the toolkits specifically developed for AYA with intellectual and developmental disabilities and found on MovingToAdultHealthCare.org.35

We learned that the implementation or ‘uptake’ of self-management skill assessment using the evidence-based TRAQ-SB16 is affected by internal/organizational and external factors. Understanding these factors is a necessary component of implementation science and underscores the multidimensional aspects of HCT success advanced by Hopson, et.al.34 Internally, clinician ‘uptake’ of the TRAQ-SB16 was low and did not change when paper tools and processes were replaced with EMR-based tools and processes. This finding amplifies the importance of EMR tools and processes that unobtrusively but effectively notify clinicians of new patient-related information.36 The low reach of clinician education discovered during the clinician focus group further explains this finding. Last, ‘implementation uptake’ is impacted by external factors. We learned that AYA with SB want the ‘gentleness and tenderness’ of the pediatric care system to extend to the adult care system when explaining and conducting invasive procedures. This finding is important and should be investigated in future HCT work. All AYA participants conveyed experiences where they felt ‘invisible’ or ‘not listened too’ by clinicians. This finding is well-reported in the literature.6, 8 These findings directly reflect how external factors can hinder AYA sustainability of self-management skill development.

Limitations

All participants were recruited from a single health system (Gillette) and limited to AYAs with SB who received care in Gillette Children’s Pediatric or Adult SB Coordinated Clinic. Participants from other health systems could yield different results. We defined clinician ‘uptake’ or use as documented use of the TRAQ-SB16 to inform the AYA plan of care, in a clinic note. AYA and clinician discussions that were not documented could not be reviewed. Direct observation of AYA and clinician interactions could yield different ‘uptake’ results. The AYA and parent focus group sessions were limited to English-speaking persons with the shared experience of completing a TRAQ-SB16 self-management readiness assessment and conducted at the start of school year. Conducting focus groups at a different time of year, with non-English speaking participants or AYA that did complete the TRAQ-SB could yield a larger group of participants and different results. The focus groups were conducted virtually to allow participation by persons unable to travel to an in-person event. Data collected during in-person focus groups could yield different results.

Conclusion and next steps

We implemented an evidence-based SB-specific transition readiness assessment, the TRAQ-SB,16 to support Element Three of the Six Core Elements of HCT,™12 ‘Transition Readiness.’ Six months later, we used the implementation science RE-AIM framework37 to evaluate the impact reach, effectiveness, adoption and implementation of the TRAQ-SB16 using mixed methods. Our retrospective application of RE-AIM provided valuable insight into who used the new tool, how the tool was used or not used, and revealed the impact of internal and external environment factors. While our findings informed changes to future PAHTI implementations at Gillette, we encourage others to incorporate RE-AIM early and proactively during the program planning38 that accompanies implementation of the Six Core Elements of HCT.™12 Doing so will foster identification of contextual internal and external factors that support and hinder the uptake and system sustainability of the tools and processes needed to facilitate a safe, planned and purposeful move for AYA with SB into adult care.
