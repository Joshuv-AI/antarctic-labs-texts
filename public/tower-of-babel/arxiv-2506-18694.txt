Shifted HSS preconditioners for the indefinite Helmholtz equation
C. J. Cotter, J. Hope-Collins and K. Knook

Abstract
We provide a preconditioning approach for the indefinite Helmholtz equation discretised using finite elements, based upon a Hermitian Skew-Hermitian Splitting (HSS) iteration applied to the shifted operator, and prove that the preconditioner is k k - and mesh-robust when 𝒪 ⁡ ( k ) \mathcal{O}(k) HSS iterations are performed. The HSS iterations involve solving a shifted operator that is suitable for approximation by multigrid using standard smoothers and transfer operators, leading to a fully scalable algorithm. We argue that the algorithm converges in 𝒪 ⁡ ( k ) \mathcal{O}(k) wallclock time when within the range of scalability of the multigrid. We provide numerical results verifying our proofs and demonstrating this claim. This establishes a scalable 𝒪 ⁡ ( k ) \mathcal{O}(k) method using multigrid with entirely standard components.

2
Primal and mixed formulations

In this section we briefly introduce the variational formulations that
we will address. We consider two
formulations that lead to closely related preconditioning

Starting from (
1

“primal” formulation is obtained by multiplying by a test function
and integrating by parts, and restricting solution and test function
to the subspace

H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)=\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega):v=0\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega_{0}\}.

(2)

The primal formulation thus seeks
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

b_{\delta}(u,v)=G_{u}[v],\quad\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

(3)

where

\displaystyle b_{\delta}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(-\delta+ik)^{2}uv^{*}+\nabla u\cdot\nabla v^{*}\diff x-(-\delta+ik)\int_{\Gamma}uv^{*}\diff S,

(4)

\displaystyle G_{u}[v]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}fv^{*}\diff x,

(5)

and where
∗

To form the finite element discretisation we can choose a finite
element space
Q_{h}\subset H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

element space. To be concrete, we choose a triangular/tetrahedral
mesh with continuous piecewise polynomials of degree
k
, which we
denote
\text{CG}_{k}
, and select the subspace of functions that satisfy the
boundary condition on
\partial\Omega_{0}
. Then the finite element
discretisation becomes: find
u\in Q_{h}
, such that

b_{\delta}(u,v)=G[v],\quad\forall v\in Q_{h}.

(6)

The “mixed” formulation we consider here proceeds from the first order system

(\delta-ik)\sigma-\nabla u=0,\quad-\nabla\cdot\sigma+(\delta-ik)u=\frac{f}{\delta-ik},

(7)

(\sigma,u)\in(L^{2}(\Omega))^{d}\times H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

\displaystyle a_{\delta}((v,\tau),(u,\sigma))=F_{\sigma}[\tau]+F_{u}[v],\quad\forall(v,\tau)\in(L^{2}(\Omega))^{d}\times H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

(8)

where

\displaystyle a_{\delta}((v,\tau),(u,\sigma))=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\delta-ik)\sigma\cdot\tau^{*}-\nabla u\cdot\tau^{*}\diff x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}\sigma\cdot\nabla v^{*}+(\delta-ik)uv^{*}\diff x\int_{\Gamma}uv^{*}\diff S,

\displaystyle F_{u}[v]=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{fv^{*}}{\delta-ik}\diff x,

(10)

\displaystyle F_{\sigma}[\tau]=

\displaystyle 0,

(11)

and where

is the spatial dimension
2

2

also chosen a formulation
(\sigma,u)\in H(\mbox{div};\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)

The conditions for well posedness and quasi-optimality of (

3
HSS preconditioning for the shifted system

In this section we define a stationary iterative method for the
shifted (
i.e.

\delta>0
) mixed and primal systems. The
stationary method can be used as a preconditioner, or a solver in its
own right. We motivate this by formulating an HSS iteration using
operators on the finite element space, before reformulating as a
standard variational formulation that demonstrates how to implement
the iteration in practice, before analysing the convergence.

3.1
Mixed formulation

First we consider the shifted mixed system (
12
),
We define the operator
S_{\mxd}:V_{h}\times Q_{h}\to V_{h}\times Q_{h}
,

\langle S_{\mxd}(\sigma,u),(\tau,v)\rangle_{H_{\mxd}}=-ik\langle\tau,\sigma\rangle-ik\langle v,u\rangle+\langle\nabla v,\sigma\rangle-\langle\tau,\nabla u\rangle,

(13)

via the
H_{\mxd}

\langle(\sigma,u),(\tau,v)\rangle_{H_{\mxd}}=\delta\langle\sigma,\tau\rangle+\delta\langle u,v\rangle+\llangle u,v\rrangle,

(14)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the usual
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product
for scalar complex valued functions defined (with similar definition for vector fields) as

\langle u,v\rangle_{\Omega}=\int_{\Omega}uv^{*}\diff x,

(15)

and
\llangle\cdot,\cdot\rrangle
denotes the
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)

inner product for functions restricted to the boundary

\partial\Omega

is skew-Hermitian in the

inner product. We also

inner product is only an inner product for

Thus, our shifted system (
6

\underbrace{(I+S_{\mxd})}_{A}\underbrace{(\sigma,u)}_{U}=f,

(16)

where
f
is the Riesz representer of
F
in the
{H_{\mxd}}
inner product.
Since we have a splitting
A=I+S_{\mxd}
and

is clearly Hermitian,
we can write a two-step stationary iterative method based on the Hermitian/skew-Hermitian splitting (HSS) for this system
as

(\gamma+1)U_{n+1/2}=(\gamma I-S_{\mxd})U_{n}+f,\quad(\gamma I+S_{\mxd})U_{n+1}=(\gamma-1)U_{n+1/2}+f.

(17)

with
\gamma>0
. Eliminating
U_{n+1/2}
gives

(\gamma I+S_{\mxd})U_{n+1}=\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}(\gamma I-S_{\mxd})U_{n}+\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}f.

(18)

The introduction of the
{H_{\mxd}}
inner product is equivalent to formulating
a preconditioned HSS method
[
10
]

using the Hermitian part of the matrix as the preconditioner.

The optimal value of
\gamma
for HSS iteration with the Hermitian
part being the identity is
\gamma=1
, which converges in 1 iteration,
but this is just equivalent to solving the original problem
(
12
), and we have not gained anything. Instead,
in this article we choose
\gamma=k
. In each iteration, we have to
solve the shifted operator
kI+S_{\mxd}
which is more suited to
multigrid than
I+S_{\mxd}
; this is more clearly seen by inspecting
the equivalent variational formulation below.

Definition 1
(
\gamma=k
HSS iteration in mixed form).

The
\gamma=k
HSS iteration for the system (
12
)
produces a sequence
(\sigma_{0},u_{0}),(\sigma_{1},u_{1}),\ldots
, defined
iteratively for
n>=0
by finding
(\sigma_{n+1},u_{n+1})

in
V_{h}\times Q_{h}
such that

\displaystyle k\langle\tau,(\delta-i)\sigma_{n+1}\rangle-\langle\tau,\nabla u_{n+1}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k-1}{k+1}\left(\langle\tau,k(\delta+i)\sigma_{n}\rangle+\langle\tau,\nabla u_{n}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle quad+\frac{2k}{k+1}F_{\sigma}[\tau],\quad\forall\tau\in V_{h},

(19)

\displaystyle k\langle v,(\delta-i)u_{n+1}\rangle+\langle\nabla v,\sigma_{n+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+{k}\llangle v,u_{n+1}\rrangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{k-1}{k+1}\left(k\langle v,(\delta+i)u_{n}\rangle-\langle\nabla v,\sigma_{n}\rangle+{k}\llangle v,u_{n}\rrangle\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{2k}{k+1}F_{u}[v],\quad\forall v\in Q_{h}.

(20)

To solve this system, we make use of the embedding property to
deduce from (
1
) that

k(\delta-i)\sigma_{n+1}-\nabla u_{n+1}=\frac{k-1}{k+1}k(\delta+i)\sigma_{n}+\nabla u_{n}=\frac{2k}{k+1}\sigma_{0},

(21)

in the
L^{2}
sense,
where

\langle\tau,\sigma_{0}\rangle=F_{\sigma}[\tau],\quad\forall\tau\in V_{h}.

(22)

Using (
21
) to eliminate
\sigma_{n+1}

from (
1
) leads to a system of the form

k^{2}(\delta-i)^{2}\langle v,u_{n+1}\rangle+\langle\nabla v,\nabla u_{n+1}\rangle+k^{2}(\delta-i)\llangle v,u_{n+1}\rrangle=\tilde{F}_{u}[v],\forall v\in V_{h},

(23)

which is a primal shifted Helmholtz system with the imaginary shift

-2i\delta k
replaced by
-2i\delta k^{2}
. This shift is the minimum
necessary scaling with
k
for efficient inversion by multigrid as
discussed in
[
11
]
. The only difference
in our present formulation is that the boundary term is scaled
differently. Presently, we have not analysed multigrid applied
to this modification but our numerical experiments demonstrate that
this shifting is sufficient to obtain
k
independent multigrid
convergence.

Having understood the benefits of the choice
\gamma=k
, we can
analyse the convergence of our preconditioned HSS scheme (
1
). In a scalable implementation, the iteration is solved
approximately using a multigrid preconditioned Krylov method. However,
here we concentrate on the case where the iteration is solved exactly.

Proposition 2
(HSS convergence for the shifted mixed system).

The mixed preconditioned HSS scheme (
1
-
1
)
has the error contraction rate
bound

\|e_{n+1}\|_{H_{\mxd}}\leq\frac{1-1/k}{1+1/k}\|e_{n}\|_{H_{\mxd}},

(24)

where

\|e\|_{H_{\mxd}}^{2}=\langle e,e\rangle_{H_{\mxd}}.

(25)

Proof.

(Follows standard HSS arguments.)
Writing
e_{n}=U_{n}-U_{*}
, where
U_{*}
is the exact solution,
the error equation is

e_{n+1}=\frac{k-1}{k+1}(kI+S_{\mxd})^{-1}(kI-S_{\mxd})e_{n}.

(26)

Then,

\|e_{n+1}\|_{H_{\mxd}}=\frac{1-1/k}{1+1/k}\|(I+S_{\mxd}/k)^{-1}(I-S_{\mxd}/k)e_{n}\|_{H_{\mxd}}\leq\frac{1-1/k}{1+1/k}\|e_{n}\|_{H_{\mxd}},

(27)

since
(I+S_{\mxd}/k)^{-1}(I-S_{\mxd}/k)
is the Cayley
transform of
S_{\mxd}/k
, which is skew Hermitian in the
{H_{\mxd}}
norm, and
hence is
{H_{\mxd}}
norm preserving.

It is useful to batch the HSS iterations in groups of
m\lceil k\rceil
(for integer
m>0

k

Corollary 3
.

If we take
m\lceil k\rceil
iterations of the HSS iteration (
1
-
1

\|e_{m\lceil k\rceil}\|_{H_{\mxd}}\leq c\|e_{0}\|_{H_{\mxd}},

(28)

k

Proof.

The previous result gives

\|e_{m\lceil k\rceil}\|_{H_{\mxd}}\leq\left(\frac{1-1/k}{1+1/k}\right)^{m\lceil k\rceil}\|e_{0}\|_{H_{\mxd}}.

(29)

Using the Bernoulli estimate

0<\left(\frac{1-1/k}{1+1/k}\right)^{m\lceil k\rceil}\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{m\lceil k\rceil}\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{\lceil k\rceil}\right)^{m\lceil k\rceil}\leq\exp(-m)<1,\\

We conclude that the HSS iteration will converge to a chosen tolerance

3.2
Primal formulation

Now, we move on to consider the primal shifted problem (
6
).
Multiplication by
i
gives

\int_{\Omega}(i(\delta^{2}-k^{2})+2\delta k)uv^{*}+i\nabla u\cdot\nabla v^{*}\diff x+(\delta i+k)\int_{\Gamma}uv^{*}\diff S=i\int_{\Omega}fv^{*}\diff x,\quad\forall v.

(33)

Then, we define
S_{\prm}
such that

\langle v,S_{\prm}u\rangle_{H_{\prm}}=i(\delta^{2}-k^{2})\langle v,u\rangle+i\langle\nabla v,\nabla u\rangle+\delta i\llangle v,u\rrangle,\quad\forall u,v,

where

\langle v,u\rangle_{H_{\prm}}=2\delta k\langle v,u\rangle+k\llangle v,u\rrangle.

(35)

Our problem becomes

(I+S_{\prm})u=\hat{f},

(36)

where

\langle v,\hat{f}\rangle_{H_{\prm}}=i\langle v,f\rangle.

Proceeding as for the mixed formulation, an HSS iteration with

(kI+S_{\prm})u_{n+1}=\frac{k-1}{k+1}(kI-S_{\prm})u_{n}+\frac{2k}{k+1}\hat{f}.

Rewriting this in the usual form, we get

\displaystyle\left(2\delta k^{2}+i(\delta^{2}-k^{2})\right)\langle v,u_{n+1}\rangle+(k^{2}+\delta i)\llangle v,u_{n+1}\rrangle+i\langle\nabla v,\nabla u_{n+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{k-1}{k+1}\left(\left(2\delta k^{2}-i(\delta^{2}-k^{2})\right)\langle v,u_{n}\rangle+(k^{2}-\delta i)\llangle v,u_{n}\rrangle-i\langle\nabla v,\nabla u_{n}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{2k}{k+1}i\langle v,f\rangle,\quad\forall v.

(39)

Finally, we divide by

\displaystyle-\hat{\delta}^{2}\langle v,u_{0}\rangle+2\hat{\delta}ki\langle v,u_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle-\hat{\delta}\llangle v,u_{0}\rrangle,\quad\forall v\in Q_{h}.

(100)

Recall that

\|u\|^{2}_{{H_{\prm}}}:=2\hat{\delta}k\|u\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+k\|u\|^{2}_{0,\partial\Omega}.

(101)

We will derive bounds for
\|u\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}
and
\|u\|^{2}_{0,\partial\Omega}
. To derive the bound for
\|u\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}
we apply (
52
) to obtain

\|u\|^{2}_{1,k,\Omega}\lesssim\hat{\delta}^{2}k^{2}\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\hat{\delta}^{2}\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{0,\partial\Omega},

(102)

assuming that
\hat{\delta}\leq k
. Now we multiply this expression by
\frac{\hat{\delta}}{k}
to obtain

\displaystyle\hat{\delta}k\|u\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}

\displaystyle\lesssim\hat{\delta}^{2}\left(\hat{\delta}k\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\frac{\hat{\delta}}{k}\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{0,\partial\Omega}\right),

(103)

\displaystyle\lesssim\hat{\delta}^{2}\left(\hat{\delta}k\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\frac{\hat{\delta}^{2}}{k^{3}}\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{0,\partial\Omega}+k\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{0,\partial\Omega}\right),

(104)

\displaystyle\lesssim\hat{\delta}^{2}\left(\hat{\delta}k\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+k\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{0,\partial\Omega}\right),

(105)

\displaystyle\lesssim\hat{\delta}^{2}\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{{H_{\prm}}},

(106)

where we have used Young’s inequality in the second line. To derive the bound for
\|u\|^{2}_{0,\partial\Omega}
, we substitute
v=u
in (
4
) and take negative imaginary parts

\displaystyle 2\hat{\delta}k\|u\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+k\|u\|^{2}_{0,\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=-2\hat{\delta}k\langle u,u_{0}\rangle,

(107)

\displaystyle\leq 2\hat{\delta}k\|u_{0}\|_{0,\Omega}\|u\|_{0,\Omega},

(108)

\displaystyle\leq k\hat{\delta}^{2}\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+k\|u\|^{2}_{0,\Omega},

(109)

where we have used Schwarz’s and Young’s inequality in the second and third line respectively. Thus, using the bound for
\|u\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}
we obtain

\displaystyle k\|u\|^{2}_{0,\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle\lesssim k\hat{\delta}^{2}\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\hat{\delta}\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{{H_{\prm}}},

(110)

\displaystyle\lesssim\hat{\delta}\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{{H_{\prm}}}+\hat{\delta}\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{{H_{\prm}}}\lesssim\hat{\delta}\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{{H_{\prm}}}.

(111)

So

\displaystyle\|u\|^{2}_{{H_{\prm}}}

\displaystyle:=2\hat{\delta}k\|u\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+k\|u\|^{2}_{0,\partial\Omega},

(112)

\displaystyle\lesssim\hat{\delta}^{2}\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{{H_{\prm}}}+\hat{\delta}\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{{H_{\prm}}}\lesssim\hat{\delta}\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{{H_{\prm}}}.

(113)

Finally we may write

\|B_{\hat{\delta}}^{-1}B_{0}-I\|_{H_{\prm}}=\sup_{0\neq u_{0}\in Q_{h}}\frac{\|(B_{\hat{\delta}}^{-1}B_{0}-I)u_{0}\|_{H_{\prm}}}{\|u_{0}\|_{H_{\prm}}}=\sup_{0\neq u_{0}Q_{h}}\frac{\|u\|_{H_{\prm}}}{\|u_{0}\|_{H_{\prm}}}\leq C\hat{\delta}^{1/2},

(114)

for some constant
C>0
independent of
k
and
\hat{\delta}
. Hence, if we
choose
\hat{\delta}
sufficiently small, we have
C\hat{\delta}^{1/2}<1
, as
required.

,

5.3

convergence of the fully scalable algorithm when the linear

The multigrid setup is very similar between the primal and the mixed formulations.

per level for all further results with this formulation. In the mixed formulation,
the
\mathcal{O}(k)
shifted operator is obtained
after
eliminating the
vector-valued field, so multigrid is applied to a scalar-valued field of the same

Finally, we examine the algorithm’s behaviour in a high performance
computing setting, focussing on the uniform source problem with the
primal formation, with larger
k
. In these experiments we kept

\theta=1
, and varied the resolution constant
c_{0}\in(0.5,0.85,1)

larger numbers of cores per node. The computations were done on
ARCHER2, the UK national supercomputer
[

]
. ARCHER2
has 128 cores per node, however we used 64 cores per node in this
computation since we are otherwise limited by memory bandwidth due
to the low arithmetic intensity of Jacobi smoothers.

We use a brief performance model to judge the parallel scaling.
The total number of DoFs scales as

DoF=\mathcal{O}((k^{3/2})^{2})=\mathcal{O}(k^{3})
. For standard
multigrid components, the communication load per processor is
independent of the wavenumber so we can use
P=\mathcal{O}(k^{3})

processors and expect very good scaling from the multigrid algorithm.
The work per HSS iteration scales with the DoF count because the number
of multigrid cycles is fixed, so if the number of HSS iterations
is
\mathcal{O}(k)
then the total work is
W=\mathcal{O}(k^{4})
.
Therefore, we expect the wallclock time to scale linearly with the
wavenumber
T=W/P=\mathcal{O}(k)
.

9
; we see that our
observations in previous experiments are extended to higher
k

values, up to
k=1024
in the largest case. Once again the HSS
convergence bounds are tight, and we see further evidence of the
convergence rate for the outer iterations approaching a stable
value for larger
k
.
Finally, we show timings in Table

10
and in Figure

.
We see very robust linear wallclock time scaling with
k
, as we hoped,
especially if we account for the variation in the number of DOFs per core.
In Figure

of processors. We see that the throughput is almost constant, indicating
that the multigrid algorithm scales almost perfectly in this regime. This
is due not only to the use of standard transfer operators and smoothers,
but also to the fact that we do not need a direct solver on the coarsest
grid for robust iteration counts, which is often a major culprit in the
loss of scaling for multigrid algorithms at large processor counts.

Summary and outlook

In this article we introduced and analysed a preconditioner for the
indefinite Helmholtz equation based on HSS iteration applied to a

\mathcal{O}(1)
shifted operator. The HSS iteration requires the
solution of another
\mathcal{O}(k)
shifted operator which is known
to be suitable for multigrid with standard components. We proved and
demonstrated numerically that when
\mathcal{O}(k)
HSS inner iterations
are used in the preconditioner,
\mathcal{O}(1)
GMRES outer iterations are
required, i.e. the number of GMRES iterations is independent of
k

and the mesh resolution. We then demonstrated numerically that when
the inverse
\mathcal{O}(k)
shifted operator is replaced by a
multigrid cycle using standard components, the robustness still holds.
Thus, we can rely upon standard multigrid parallel scalability for the
solver, with the only serial component being the
k
inner iterations.
Therefore we expect
\mathcal{O}(k)
wallclock times within the range
of optimal scalability of the multigrid for a given mesh resolution,
which we demonstrated in practice up to high wavenumbers.

In future work we will extend our analysis and computational
experiments to higher-order discretisations, H(div)-
L_{2}
mixed formulations, as well as harmonic
elastodynamics and harmonic Maxwell equations.
