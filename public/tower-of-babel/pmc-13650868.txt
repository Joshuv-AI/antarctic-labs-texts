Authors’ Response
Authors: Viswanathan Mohan, Coimbatore Subramanian Shanthi Rani, Ganesan Uma Sankari, Rajendra Pradeepa, Ranjit Unnikrishnan, Thangarajan Rahulashankiruthiyayan, Kumaradas Gini Venisha, Loganathan Geetha, Ulagamadesan Venkatesan, Ranjit Mohan Anjana
Published: 2026

We thank the authors of the letter for their thoughtful critique1 of our study on the incidence of diabetes complications.2 We appreciate the opportunity to address these points and provide additional context to strengthen the interpretation of our findings.

As rightly pointed out, our cohort consisted of 679 patients with type 2 diabetes screened at baseline for diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) using biothesiometry, excluding those with pre-existing neuropathy. We would like to highlight that among the 427 patients who developed incident DPN (62.8% over 9 y), 16.2% had diabetic retinopathy (DR), 26.5% had diabetic kidney disease (eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73m2 and/or albuminuria ≥30 µg/mg), 31.8% had systemic hypertension and 59.9% had low HDL cholesterol at baseline. We agree that overlap between microvascular complications exists; however, we adjusted for risk factors like systolic blood pressure and cholesterol in relative risk analysis, confirming the independent effects of modifiable factors. In the same Chennai Urban Rural Epidemiology Study (CURES), Pradeepa et al3 had earlier reported the overlap of microvascular complications of diabetes (retinopathy, overt nephropathy, and neuropathy) at baseline among the 1,608 individuals with diabetes, in that report only 1.1% had all three microvascular complications, while DR and overt nephropathy were present in 23.9% and 7% of the subjects with DPN.

We agree that smoking, alcohol, obesity, and vitamin D/B12 deficiencies are key modifiable risks for DPN. Smoking and alcohol data collected via self-report were seen in 56.1 and 46.1% of male participants, respectively. Data on smoking and alcohol use were unavailable for women, possibly reflecting cultural influences that contribute to the underreporting of these behaviours. Vitamin D/B12 and C-peptide were not measured in this study due to resource constraints, but future prospective studies from our centre plan to address this.

We thank the authors for highlighting our statement on DPN incidence in women. We agree that Dieleman et al4 noted higher neuropathic pain incidence in women. However, Mehta et al5 specifically assessed peripheral neuropathy incidence and risk factors in Kenyan HIV-infected adults on antiretroviral therapy, reporting a gender-based comparison specific to peripheral neuropathy (Women: 17.7 vs. Men: 1.9 per 100 person-years). We agree that the inclusion of the study on HIV/antiretroviral-associated incidence of neuropathy constitutes a limitation. However, longitudinal studies assessing the incidence of DPN in South Asians, stratified by gender, are scarce, thereby limiting direct comparisons.

We believe that these clarifications do not alter our conclusions but rather enhance their generalizability.
