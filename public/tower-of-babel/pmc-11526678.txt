Exploring paternal postpartum depression and contributing factors in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Gemeda Wakgari Kitil, Mustefa Adem Hussen, Samuel Ejeta Chibsa, Alex Ayenew Chereka
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPaternal postpartum depression (PPD) is a significant yet often neglected mental health issue affecting fathers during the postpartum period. While maternal postpartum depression is extensively studied, the psychological challenges faced by new fathers, particularly in resource-limited settings like Ethiopia, receive considerably less attention. Paternal postpartum depression not only impacts fathers’ well-being but also affects the health and development of their children and their relationships with partners. Understanding the prevalence of paternal postpartum depression and its underlying causes in Ethiopia is essential for developing effective healthcare policies and tailored support programs for new fathers. The goal of this meta-analysis and systematic review is to compile the information currently available regarding the prevalence of and contributing factors to postpartum depression in Ethiopian fathers.MethodsThis study adhered to the PRISMA guidelines and focused on research from Ethiopia. A comprehensive search was performed across multiple databases, including Google, Google Scholar, PubMed, Web of Science, and Medline. Data were systematically collected using a structured checklist and analyzed with STATA version 11. To assess heterogeneity, the Cochrane Q test and I² statistic were applied. Publication bias was also checked using Egger’s regression analysis, a funnel plot, and Begg’s test.ResultsFive studies with a total of 2,055 participants were included in the meta-analysis. The pooled prevalence of paternal postpartum depression in Ethiopia was 20.86% (95% CI: 16.43–25.29). Significant factors associated with paternal postpartum depression included low family income (OR = 3.04, 95% CI: 1.46–6.32), substance use (OR = 2.96, 95% CI: 1.63–5.37), poor social support (OR = 4.28, 95% CI: 2.53–7.23), unplanned pregnancy (OR = 3.42, 95% CI: 2.24–5.24), and infant sleep problems (OR = 4.78, 95% CI: 2.35–9.73). Heterogeneity was high (I² = 97.9%, P < 0.05). A subgroup analysis was conducted to better understand the variations among the primary studies.ConclusionThe study reveals a significant prevalence of paternal postpartum depression in Ethiopia, highlighting key risk factors such as low family income, substance use, poor social support, unplanned pregnancies, and infant sleep difficulties. These findings emphasize the need for routine depression screening for fathers, improved social support programs, education on infant sleep management, and further research to develop targeted prevention and treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-024-06206-z.

Introduction

Paternal postpartum depression (PPD), also referred to as paternal postnatal depression, is an under-recognized mental health condition affecting fathers in the weeks and months following childbirth [1–3]. PPD typically emerges within four weeks postpartum and can persist for up to a year, manifesting through symptoms such as fatigue, disrupted sleep, changes in appetite, crying spells, and emotional distress [3]. Additionally, fathers with PPD may experience suicidal thoughts, violent behavior, irritability, low motivation, increased substance use, poor concentration, withdrawal from relationships, and significant changes in work habits, either overworking or underworking [4, 5].

The global prevalence of PPD is estimated to vary from 4 to 25%, influenced by regional and socio-economic differences [6, 7]. In low-income countries like Ethiopia, where mental health services are limited and cultural stigmas around depression persist, the prevalence of PPD is notably higher, ranging from 17 to 29.37% [8, 9]. Despite the considerable number of men experiencing depression following the delivery of their babies, this condition has largely remained underreported and unacknowledged. As a result, its detrimental effects on fathers and their families often go unnoticed and untreated [10].

Paternal postpartum depression not only affects fathers’ mental health but also has significant implications for their partners and children [11]. The consequences of PPD can be far-reaching, leading to persistent depression in fathers, strained relationships with partners, and challenges in parenting. Furthermore, it can impede bonding with the infant and contribute to developmental issues that may affect children from infancy through adolescence [12, 13].

Various factors have been identified as contributing to PPD, including maternal depression, unemployment, low socioeconomic status, a prior history of depression, strained marital relationships, insufficient social support, substance use, limited education, adverse pregnancy outcomes, maternal postpartum distress, loneliness, infant sleep disturbances, and unplanned pregnancies [8, 9, 14–17].

Addressing paternal postpartum depression requires a clear understanding of the risk and protective factors, including sociodemographic, behavioral, psychosocial, and pregnancy-related elements. These factors vary across different contexts and affect the manifestation of depression in fathers [18, 19]. Mental health is now integrated into the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) agenda, aiming to address mental health challenges globally across high- and low-income countries [20]. In the updated SDGs, the United Nations has formally recognized mental health as a global development priority, with an ambitious 15-year plan to tackle these challenges. Ethiopia’s Health Sector Transformation Plan 2 also aims to reduce the burden of non-communicable diseases, which may include mental health initiatives [21, 22].

Although several studies have examined paternal postpartum depression in Ethiopia, a comprehensive national estimate and consistent identification of key factors remain unavailable. Research findings are often inconsistent and vary across regions, highlighting the need for a more unified analysis. The objective of this meta-analysis and systematic review is to compile available data on the incidence and risk factors of paternal postpartum depression in Ethiopia. By identifying key determinants and prevalence rates, the study seeks to raise awareness of the condition and inform policies and interventions that support fathers’ mental health within the postpartum timeframe, ultimately improving family and societal well-being.

Methods and materials

Source of information and search strategy

To identify relevant studies for our analysis, we conducted a thorough search using two key databases: PROSPERO and the Database of Abstracts of Reviews of Effects (DARE), both accessible through the UCSF Library. This search was designed to gather a broad range of studies relevant to our research objectives. We carefully developed our search strategy following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [23], which ensure a systematic and transparent review process, thereby improving the reliability and reproducibility of our findings. Our study aims to explore factors influencing paternal Postnatal depression and its contributing factors in Ethiopia. This involves a detailed review of existing literature to identify trends, correlations, and gaps in current knowledge. The studies identified through our search were meticulously reviewed and analyzed to extract relevant data (S1 Table).

To perform a comprehensive literature review, we systematically searched several online databases, including Google, Google Scholar, Scopus, Medline, and PubMed, from May 2, 2024, to August 2, 2024. Our search strategy utilized a combination of keywords, free-text queries, and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) to ensure a thorough and relevant selection of studies.

We used Boolean operators to combine various terms related to “prevalence,” “magnitude,” “paternal,” “male partners,” “associated factors,” “determinants,” “postpartum depression,” and “paternal postpartum depression” with terms specifically associated with male partners. The structured search criteria were as follows: (“prevalence” OR “magnitude” OR “associated factors” OR “determinants”) AND (“postpartum depression” OR “paternal postpartum depression”) AND (“postpartum depression among paternal partners” OR “postpartum depression among male partners” OR “associated factors of postpartum depression among male partners in Ethiopia”). This systematic approach ensured a thorough and structured examination of the literature within the specified period.

We employed a rigorous approach by systematically reviewing the titles, abstracts, and full texts of selected studies. To ensure data accuracy, we adhered to the guidelines established by the Joanna Briggs Institute [24]. Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers—GWK and AAC—with any discrepancies resolved through collaborative discussions to maintain the reliability of our results.

Eligibility criteria

Our systematic review aimed to synthesize research on paternal postpartum depression and contributing factors in Ethiopia. We included all observational studies, regardless of their publication status, to ensure a comprehensive overview. To maintain the breadth and relevance of our review, we did not restrict studies based on their publication date. As a result, our review encompasses articles published up to September 5, 2024. To ensure our findings were accurate and relevant, we set strict criteria for including studies. We only included studies that specifically focused on Paternal Postpartum Depression and Its Associated Factors in Ethiopia. We excluded studies from journals that did not have abstracts or full texts to ensure a thorough review. This was done to keep the review’s quality high.

Our review aimed to look at cross-sectional studies related to postpartum depression’s prevalence and contributing factors among male partners in Ethiopia. We only considered full-text articles in English from peer-reviewed journals. We excluded studies that were not cross-sectional, written in other languages, as part of national surveys, conference reports, case studies, expert opinions, editorials, letters, reviews, or commentaries.

This meta-analysis and systematic review adhered to the CoCoPop criteria. The population of interest (Co) consists of fathers in Ethiopia during the postpartum period (Po). The condition (C) under investigation is paternal postpartum depression, while the context (Co) includes various factors associated with this condition, such as socio-demographic variables, mental health history, and family dynamics. The study aims to identify the key determinants of paternal postpartum depression and to provide a comprehensive understanding of how these factors impact fathers throughout their postpartum experience.

Data extraction

The quality of the selected studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) cross-sectional study quality checklist (JBI) [24]. Data from the studies were compiled by two researchers (AAC and GWK) into a spreadsheet and then verified for accuracy by a third researcher (SEC). One of the authors (GWK) used Microsoft Excel to extract key information following a predefined checklist.

Data synthesis and analysis

The data synthesis began by extracting relevant information into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet, which was then imported into STATA version 11 for detailed analysis. In the first step, search results from different databases were merged, and EndNote version 20.0 was used to detect and remove duplicates. After that, study titles and abstracts were reviewed to filter out irrelevant ones. A detailed review of the full-text versions of the remaining studies was then carried out for final analysis. Key findings were summarized and presented using forest plots, tables, and figures. A 95% confidence interval (CI) was applied to a random-effects model to estimate the overall prevalence of postpartum depression in fathers and the contributing factors in Ethiopia.

We used odds ratios with a 95% CI to evaluate the relationship between postpartum depression in fathers and its contributing factors. Due to the variability among the studies, we adopted a random-effects model in the meta-analysis, which provided a more broad understanding of the pooled data.

To evaluate the possibility of publication bias, we conducted Egger’s regression test and examined a funnel plot. Additionally, we used Cochran’s Q test and I² statistics to evaluate differences in the reported prevalence and associated factors across studies. Statistical significance was set at a p-value of less than 0.05 in Cochran’s Q test. The I² statistic measured heterogeneity, where 0% indicated no heterogeneity, 25% low, 50% moderate, and 75% or more indicated substantial heterogeneity.

Outcome measurement

Paternal Postpartum Depression (PPD) refers to depressive symptoms experienced by fathers following the birth of a child, typically during the initial year following childbirth. These symptoms were identified using standardized screening tools such as the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) or the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), with depression defined by a score above the recommended cut-off point for each tool.

In the first phase of our study, we determined the prevalence of Paternal Postpartum Depression and its associated factors across different studies conducted in Ethiopia. Data collection involved using a checklist to gather details such as study design, authors, sample size, study location, publication year, and the number of fathers who participated.

In the second phase, we analyzed factors associated with Paternal Postpartum Depression, such as socio-demographic, psychological, and environmental variables. Two-by-two tables were used to extract data, and the log odds ratio (OR) was calculated from the original studies. Any disagreements between reviewers (GWK and AAC) were resolved through the discussion of two authors who screened the data (SEC and MAH). For studies with missing data, we contacted the authors via email for additional information.

Quality assessment and appraisal

To assess the quality of the studies and identify potential biases and variations in research findings, we used a standardized assessment tool. The quality check was conducted by four independent reviewers. We employed the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), specifically designed to assess bias in observational studies, to address methodological concerns. Only studies that achieved a score of seven or higher on the modified NOS scale were included (S2 Table ).

Consent to publish and ethical approval

This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines with precision, emphasizing thoroughness and meticulous attention to detail. To address potential conflicts of interest and ensure fair representation, the included studies were subject to stringent review and approval. As a result, we have determined that ethical approval is not required, and the timing of participant recruitment and access to medical records are no longer pertinent concerns.

Public and patient involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the study’s design, data analysis, or interpretation, nor will they be informed of the outcomes.

Results

Study selections

We began by gathering 100 articles from various databases relevant to our research topic. After removing 20 duplicate articles to ensure data uniqueness, we examined the remaining articles’ titles and abstracts. This process led to the exclusion of 50 papers that did not meet our inclusion criteria. We then reviewed the full texts of the remaining 30 articles to evaluate their relevance and quality in depth. Ultimately, we selected five studies that met all our stringent inclusion criteria for the meta-analysis and systematic review, ensuring a rigorous and focused analysis of the evidence (Fig. 1).

Characteristics included in the reviewed research

Data from five studies carried out in Ethiopia were examined by our thorough meta-analysis and systematic review, each providing significant insights into Paternal Postpartum Depression and Its Associated Factors in Ethiopia. The combined sample size across all studies was 2,055 fathers, with participant numbers ranging from 287 to 634 per study. Notably, the study conducted in the Amhara region had the largest sample size with 634 participants [25], while the study from Addis Ababa in the capital city of Ethiopia had the smallest sample size with 287 participants [16]. All studies used a cross-sectional design for data collection.

Geographically, these studies were distributed across various regions of Ethiopia. Specifically, three studies were conducted in the Oromia region [9, 16, 17], one in the Amhara region [25], and one in the southern region [8] (Table 1).

Pooled prevalence of paternal postpartum depression in Ethiopia

Our meta-analysis revealed that the pooled prevalence of paternal postpartum depression in Ethiopia is 20.86% (95% CI: 16.43–25.29). Among the studies included, the highest prevalence was reported in Mattu Town, Oromia Region, at 29.37% (95% CI: 28.0–30.74) [9], while the lowest prevalence was found in the Wolaita Zone, Southern Ethiopia, at 17.0% (95% CI: 15.51–18.49) [8]. The analysis indicated significant heterogeneity among the studies, with a p-value of 0.00 and an I² value of 97.9%. Due to this substantial heterogeneity, a subgroup analysis was necessary to better understand and interpret the variations among the primary studies (Fig. 2).

Publication bias

We evaluated publication bias through Egger’s regression test and a visual inspection of the funnel plot. While the funnel plot initially appeared asymmetrical, the result of Egger’s test (P = 0.312) was not statistically significant. Consequently, our analysis found no significant indication of publication bias in the included studies, a conclusion further supported by Egger’s test outcome (Fig. 3).

Contributing factors to postpartum depression among fathers in Ethiopia

The purpose of our meta-analysis was to identify the variables affecting paternal postpartum depression (PPD) in Ethiopia by synthesizing data from five studies. We employed the command metan logor selogor, xlab(0.1, 1, 10) label(namevar = authors) by(factors) random texts(180) eform, which allowed for a comprehensive assessment of the cumulative effects of odds ratios.

Our analysis revealed key factors associated with paternal postpartum depression, highlighting the unique challenges fathers face during the postpartum period. The significant factors included low family income, substance use, unplanned pregnancy, poor social support, and infant sleep problems, all of which contribute to the psychological strain experienced by fathers.

Low family income (OR = 3.04, 95% CI: 1.46–6.32) was a significant predictor, as financial stress can heighten anxiety and feelings of inadequacy in fathers. Substance use (OR = 2.96, 95% CI: 1.63–5.37) was also associated with PPD, indicating that fathers may resort to unhealthy coping mechanisms under stress, further exacerbating their mental health.

Poor social support (OR = 4.28, 95% CI: 2.53–7.23) emerged as another major factor, with fathers lacking emotional and practical support being more vulnerable to depression. Strengthening social networks may help mitigate this risk. Unplanned pregnancy (OR = 3.42, 95% CI: 2.24–5.24) was linked to heightened anxiety and depression, as the unexpected nature of parenthood often leaves fathers feeling unprepared.

Lastly, infant sleep problems (OR = 4.78, 95% CI: 2.35–9.73) were highly associated with PPD. Sleep deprivation caused by infant sleep issues leads to fatigue and emotional distress in fathers, worsening depressive symptoms (Fig. 6).

Discussion

This meta-analysis offers a comprehensive examination of paternal postpartum depression in Ethiopia, shedding light on key factors that contribute to its prevalence. Our analysis determined that the pooled prevalence of paternal postpartum depression in Ethiopia is 20.86% (95% CI: 16.43–25.29), indicating that one in five fathers may experience depressive symptoms during the postpartum period.

The findings from our study are consistent with results from several international studies. For instance, similar prevalence rates have been reported in India (24.06%), Nigeria (17.8%), Japan (17.0%), and Saudi Arabia (16.7%) [7, 26–28], suggesting that paternal postpartum depression is a global issue affecting fathers across various cultural and socio-economic contexts.

However, our prevalence estimate is notably higher than that study reported in China, where the prevalence was 7.5% [29]. The higher prevalence of paternal postpartum depression can be attributed to several factors. Limited healthcare infrastructure and socioeconomic stressors reduce access to and utilization of mental health services, worsening depressive symptoms among fathers. Furthermore, cultural stigma surrounding mental health, societal pressures, and inadequate support systems contribute to increased stress and exacerbate mental health issues.

On the other hand, our study finding is lower than that reported in studies from Uganda and Ireland, where the prevalence was found to be higher, at 28% [2, 30]. These variations may be attributable to differences in study methodologies, healthcare systems, cultural perceptions of mental health, and the availability of paternal support services, which could influence the mental well-being of fathers during the postpartum period.

Additionally, we conducted a subgroup analysis to explore sources of heterogeneity in the prevalence of paternal postpartum depression. One key factor contributing to heterogeneity was the sample size. Studies with a sample size of 423 or more exhibited a higher pooled prevalence and greater variability (I² = 98.8%, p = 0.000). This suggests that larger studies may capture a more diverse population and a broader range of experiences, thereby increasing variability in the prevalence estimates. The subgroup analysis emphasizes the importance of considering sample size when interpreting prevalence data on paternal postpartum depression.

Geographic regions also played a significant role in the variation of prevalence rates. The highest pooled prevalence was observed in studies conducted in the Oromia region, where the rate of paternal postpartum depression reached 22.76% (95% CI: 16.14–29.38), accompanied by a high degree of heterogeneity (I² = 98.4%, p = 0.0001). Regional differences may be explained by disparities in socioeconomic conditions, healthcare infrastructure, access to mental health services, and cultural perceptions of mental health. Oromia, being a populous region, faces unique stressors that may exacerbate mental health challenges for fathers during the postpartum period.

Low family income was identified as a significant predictor of paternal postpartum depression, with fathers from low-income households being three times more likely to develop depression compared to those from higher-income families (OR = 3.04, 95% CI: 1.46–6.32). This result is consistent with findings from Nigeria [26]. The increased risk may be attributed to the economic strain and financial stress associated with meeting the family’s needs, including the baby’s essentials. This financial anxiety, combined with limited access to mental health resources and support, exacerbates fathers’ mental well-being. Additionally, job insecurity and reduced social support in low-income households further contribute to stress and isolation, heightening the risk of suffering from depression in the postpartum period.

Poor social support also emerged as a significant factor associated with paternal postpartum depression, with fathers lacking adequate support being over four times more likely to experience depression compared to those with strong social networks (OR = 4.28, 95% CI: 2.53–7.23). This finding aligns with a systematic review on paternal postnatal depression conducted in China, which emphasizes the critical role of social support in safeguarding fathers’ mental health [31]. Social support during the postnatal period comes from various sources, including medical staff, family members, and peers, each playing a distinct role. Medical staff can provide professional guidance and reassurance, reducing anxiety associated with a lack of knowledge and skills in caring for the mother and infant. Family support, through emotional understanding and practical assistance, helps fathers manage their new responsibilities, offering emotional relief and promoting positive coping mechanisms. Peer support is equally important, as it offers fathers a shared space to exchange experiences and receive validation, reducing feelings of isolation. Without adequate support from these networks, the compounded responsibilities of supporting a partner and caring for a newborn can lead to significant stress, ultimately contributing to the onset of paternal postpartum depression.

Similarly, a strong correlation was found between unplanned pregnancy and postpartum depression in fathers. Partners who experienced unplanned pregnancies were three times more likely to suffer from depression compared to those with planned pregnancies (OR = 3.42, 95% CI: 2.24–5.24). This finding is consistent with a study conducted in Nigeria, which also highlighted the impact of unplanned pregnancies on fathers’ mental health [26]. This may be due to the emotional stress and anxiety caused by unplanned pregnancies, as well as financial concerns and relationship strains.

Furthermore, substance use was linked to paternal postpartum depression. Partners who used substances like alcohol or khat during the postnatal period were three times more likely to experience depression compared to their counterparts (OR = 2.96, 95% CI: 1.63–5.37). This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Japan, which also identified substance use as a contributing factor to paternal mental health issues [32]. This is due to Substance use that might exacerbate paternal postpartum depression by impairing emotional regulation and increasing stress and fatigue. It also creates additional stressors and hinders effective coping and support-seeking, with stigma potentially preventing fathers from seeking help.

Lastly, partners whose infants experienced sleep issues had a considerably higher probability of suffering from paternal postpartum depression. These partners were nearly five times more likely to be depressed compared to those whose infants did not have sleeping problems (OR = 4.78, 95% CI: 2.35–9.73). This finding aligns with the results observed in research conducted in China and Ireland [2, 33]. This heightened risk can be attributed to the considerable stress and fatigue that parents experience when dealing with infants who have sleeping issues. Sleep disturbances in infants often lead to increased parental stress and reduced sleep quality for fathers, which can exacerbate mental health challenges and contribute to the onset of postpartum depression.

Strengths and limitations

This meta-analysis has several notable strengths. It offers a comprehensive overview by incorporating a substantial number of studies, which provides a robust estimate of the prevalence of postpartum depression among fathers in Ethiopia. The study’s multifactorial approach effectively identifies and examines significant factors associated with postpartum depression among fathers, including socioeconomic, behavioral, and familial influences. The research also utilized rigorous methodologies for data extraction, study selection, and statistical analysis, thereby enhancing the reliability of the findings. Furthermore, the collaborative effort of multiple authors ensured thorough review and expertise across various aspects of the research.

However, there are also limitations to consider. The number of studies included was limited due to the relatively small number of studies available in Ethiopia, which may affect the comprehensiveness of the analysis. Additionally, some studies relied on self-reported data, potentially introducing biases or inaccuracies in the assessment of paternal postpartum depression. Lastly, there is a potential risk of publication bias, as studies with notable results are more likely to be published, which could distort the overall prevalence estimates.

Conclusion and recommendation

Our comprehensive meta-analysis reveals that paternal postpartum depression is prevalent in Ethiopia, with a concerning pooled rate of 20.86%. The study identified several critical factors associated with this condition: low family income, substance use, unplanned pregnancy, poor social support, and infant sleep problems. These factors contribute to increased risk and highlight the complex interplay of economic, social, and familial influences on paternal mental health.

To address these issues, targeted financial assistance and employment support programs should be implemented to alleviate economic pressures on families. Prevention and intervention programs tailored for new fathers, including access to counseling and support services, are essential for managing substance use. Additionally, supporting initiatives that build and enhance social networks for fathers, such as peer support groups and community-based programs, can provide crucial emotional and practical support.

Education and counseling resources should be offered to help fathers navigate the challenges of unplanned pregnancies. Furthermore, providing parenting classes and resources focused on managing infant sleep issues can help fathers reduce the stress associated with these disturbances.
