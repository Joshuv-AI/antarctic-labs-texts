Association between platelet-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio and cognitive function in older americans: insights from a cross-sectional study
Authors: Tianyi Wang, Ruwen Zheng, Siqi Zhang, Hongyu Qin, Hong Jin, Yubo Teng, Shuai Ma, Miao Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The main aim of this study was to explore the correlation between the platelet/high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (PHR) and cognitive function in elderly individuals from the United States. This investigation leveraged data encompassing 2299 participants, all aged 60 years and above, from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey conducted from 2011 to 2014. Inclusion criteria were based on the availability of complete datasets for PHR and cognitive function assessments. The analytical approach incorporated multivariate logistic regression to discern the association between PHR and cognitive function. Additionally, the study employed restricted cubic splines (RCS) to explore potential non-linear relationships and subgroup analyses to identify variations in the observed associations across different demographic and clinical subgroups. In the fully adjusted model, an increment of 10 units in PHR was associated with a decline of 0.014 in cognitive scores (β=-0.014, 95% CI: -0.025, -0.002; P < 0.05). Compared to the lowest quartile, participants in the highest quartile exhibited a 38.4% increased prevalence of cognitive impairment per one-unit increase in PHR (OR = 1.384, 95% CI: 1.012, 1.893; P < 0.05). Subgroup analysis revealed consistent results regarding the relationship between PHR and cognitive impairment across all subgroups. A non-linear relationship between PHR and cognitive impairment was observed using RCS, indicating that an increase in PHR above 111.49 significantly elevated the incidence of cognitive impairment (P < 0.05). Our study demonstrates that a higher PHR is associated with a greater risk of cognitive decline in an older U.S. population, and although further validation is needed, this warrants consideration in clinical assessments and interventions.

Introduction

Cognitive decline, a hallmark of aging, involves the gradual deterioration of cognitive functions, including memory, attention, language, and decision-making1. While cognitive decline is a common aspect of aging, it is important to distinguish between normal age-related changes and those driven by neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD). As individuals age, the risk of cognitive disorders—such as mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia—rises significantly2. With global demographic shifts toward aging populations, dementia prevalence is expected to reach 150 million cases by 20503–5. Identifying modifiable risk factors is therefore essential for developing effective prevention strategies. Neurodegenerative diseases, particularly AD, are driven by pathological mechanisms, including amyloid-beta (Aβ) aggregation and tau protein hyperphosphorylation, leading to neuroinflammation and accelerated cognitive deficits beyond normal aging6,7. Cognitive decline in these contexts results from both aging-related and disease-specific processes, making it crucial to differentiate between them for targeted interventions8. While normal age-related cognitive changes are generally irreversible, addressing modifiable risk factors could delay or prevent the onset of dementia and cognitive impairment9. For instance, improving cardiovascular fitness and controlling inflammation have demonstrated potential in slowing cognitive decline and neurodegenerative progression10–13.

Platelet (PLT), small cell fragments produced by megakaryocytes in the bone marrow, perform functions beyond hemostasis, such as mediating inflammation, promoting wound healing, and regulating blood flow14,15. Emerging research highlights their role in immune responses, particularly their contribution to inflammatory cascades and disruption of the blood-brain barrier—critical steps in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases16. High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), a key cardiovascular biomarker, protects against atherosclerosis by promoting cholesterol efflux and providing antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects17,18. Furthermore, HDL-C plays a vital role in maintaining cognitive health by preserving vascular integrity necessary for brain function, with evidence linking higher HDL-C levels to improved cognitive performance and a reduced risk of neurodegenerative conditions17,19.

Building on these individual roles, the platelet-to-HDL-C ratio (PHR) has emerged as a novel biomarker that reflects both inflammatory and pro-thrombotic activity and is associated with the severity of metabolic syndrome20,21. It is hypothesized that PHR could provide a more integrated measure of the inflammatory and pro-thrombotic processes that contribute to both cardiovascular and neurodegenerative diseases. Given the influence of both PLT and HDL-C on brain function, PHR may represent a promising indicator for assessing cognitive impairment risk. However, the biological mechanisms underlying this relationship are still not well understood, and few studies have explored its connection to cognitive function. Thus, this study aims to investigate the association between PHR and cognitive impairment in older adults, with a specific focus on its potential implications in neurodegenerative diseases.

Materials and methods

Date source and participants

The study data were derived from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a nationwide health and nutrition survey program conducted jointly by the National Institutes of Health and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. NHANES is specifically created to evaluate the health status and nutritional intake of the U.S. population, providing a scientific basis for formulating public health policies and health interventions. The research protocol of the NHANES database has been approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Institutional Review Board, and all participants have provided informed consent; the NHANES data are publicly accessible and can be directly used for analysis20,21. For more detailed information about NHANES, please visit the website (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/about_nhanes.htm).

For this study, we included 3,632 participants aged 60 and above who had undergone cognitive function assessment from the NHANES database between 2011 and 2014. Participants with incomplete cognitive function assessments, missing PLT, HDL-C, or other covariate data were eliminated, leaving a total sample size of 2299.

Cognitive function assessment

The study included participants whose cognitive functioning was established by the word learning and recall modules from the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s Disease (CERAD), the Animal Fluency Test (AFT), and the Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST). The CERAD consists of three consecutive word learning trials and one delayed word recall after AFT and DSST. The word learning and delayed memory modules of CERAD can monitor the cognitive functions of the patients, and they play an essential role in the monitoring and early screening of AD, which is conducive to early diagnosis and intervention of AD22,23. The AFT is a widely cited neuropsychological tool used across diverse populations, including those with AD and MCI, to assess verbal fluency, cognitive flexibility, and executive function by evaluating a subject’s cognitive status through the task of listing as many animal names as possible within a one-minute timeframe24,25. DSST assesses multiple cognitive collaterals, including processing speed, attention, and executive function, by measuring how quickly and accurately an individual matches numbers to symbols within a limited time frame, and the results can help physicians and researchers identify and monitor the progression of cognitive impairment. The DSST can detect cognitive impairments in multiple affected domains, and people with AD generally exhibit subpar performance on mental tasks that require intricate information processing, such as the DSST26,27.

In alignment with previous studies28,29, this investigation employed a composite “Cognitive score” to quantify participants’ cognitive performance. This score integrated the results from the CERAD, AFT, and DSST assessments, thereby mitigating the discrepancies that might arise from different cognitive scales and controlling for extreme values to prevent biases due to floor or ceiling effects in cognitive evaluations. Assessment of PH

PHR = PLT/HDL-C. The participants’ PLT and HDL-C biochemical characteristics were acquired from blood samples collected at the mobile screening centre.

Covariates

The covariates included in this study include gender (male and female), age, race (Mexican American, other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, and other races), educational level (below high school, high school or GED and above high school), poverty-income ratio(PIR), body mass index (BMI) (normal: <25 kg/m2, overweight: 25 to < 30 kg/m2, obese: ≥30 kg/m2), alcohol use, smoking status. A medical history of hypertension, diabetes, or stroke was determined based on whether or not the individual received a diagnosis from a doctor or other healthcare provider. Alcohol consumption status was categorized into three grades: (1) Never: <12 drinks in lifetime; (2) Former: ≥12 drinks in lifetime, but no drinks in last year; (3) Now: ≥12 drinks in last year. Smoking status was categorized into two classes: (1) Never: no more than 100 cigarettes in a lifetime; (2) Now: more than 100 cigarettes in a lifetime.

Statistical analysis

The participants’ initial characteristics were evaluated using chi-square tests and Student’s t-tests. Categorical data were presented as numbers (%) and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation. The study employed multifactor logistic regression to evaluate the association between PHR, PHR quartiles, and Cognitive scores, as well as the relationship between PHR, PHR quartiles, and cognitive impairment. In order to exclude confounding factors from interfering with the study results, three adjusted models were developed. Model 1 was a basic model without any adjustments. Model 2 included adjustments for the variables of gender, age, and race of the participants. Model 3 included adjustments for a more comprehensive set of variables: age, gender, race, education level, PIR, alcohol usage, smoking status, BMI, hypertension, stroke, and diabetes.

The dose-response association between PHR and cognitive impairment was examined using a Restricted Cubic Spline (RCS), and inflection points in the RCS images were assessed by threshold effect analysis. The interaction between PHR and cognitive impairment was tested by stratifying by age, gender, smoking status, drinking status, whether stroke, diabetes, or hypertension, and forest plots were used to reflect the final results. The analyses were conducted using EmpowerStats (version: 4.1) and R software (version: 4.3.3). The association between PHR and cognitive function scores was assessed using β and 95% confidence interval (CI). The relationship between PHR and cognitive impairment was evaluated using Odds ratio (OR) and 95% CI. A statistically significant difference was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

As shown in Table 1, the differences in all variables included in this study were statistically significant (P < 0.05) across the four populations, except for stroke. The Q4 population mainly consisted of low-income Non-Hispanic White males aged < 70 years and had lower cognitive scores and a higher prevalence of cognitive impairment in the Q4 population compared to the Q1 population.

Relationship between PHR and cognitive

The relationships between PHR and cognitive scores and PHR and cognitive impairment were described by multifactor logistic regression; Model 1 represented the unadjusted model, Model 2 represented the partially adjusted model, and Model 3 represented the fully adjusted model. The results of Model 3, fully adjusted for confounders, showed that there was a significant negative correlation between PHR and cognitive scores (β=-0.014; 95% CI: -0.025, -0.002; P < 0.05), for every 10-unit increase in cognitive scores there was a 0.014 decrease in cognitive scores, and for every 1-unit increase in PHR in the Q4 population, compared to Q1, a consequent decrease in cognitive scores of − 0.35 (β=-0.35; 95% CI: -0.579, -0.120; P < 0.01). Whereas there was no significant correlation between PHR and cognitive impairment (OR = 1.012; 95% CI: 0.996, 1.028; P > 0.05), there was a 38.4% increase in the chance of suffering from cognitive impairment for every 1-unit increase in PHR in the Q4 population compared to that of Q1 (OR = 1.384; 95% CI: 1.012, 1.893; P < 0.05), indicating that a rise in PHR by itself does not reliably indicate a heightened likelihood of cognitive decline, and that only if a specific threshold of PHR is surpassed does its growth indicate an increased risk of cognitive impairment.See Table 2 for details.Further details are presented in Table 2.

Based on fully adjusted for confounders, the RCS analysis showed a nonlinear relationship between PHR and cognitive impairment (P for nonlinear = 0.06) (Fig. 2). The threshold analysis revealed that the inflexion point of the RCS was 111.49. Above this inflexion point, there was an elevated risk of cognitive impairment with PHR, as indicated by an OR of 1.012 (95% CI: 1.000, 1.024; P < 0.001) (Table 3). However, in the case of PHR < 111.49, the results of RCS analysis and threshold analysis indicated that there was no significant negative correlation between PHR and cognitive impairment (OR = 0.990, 95% CI: 0.979–1.001; P > 0.05) (Fig. 2; Table 3).

Subgroup analysis

To assess the reliability of the association between PHR and cognitive impairment, we analyzed the association according to age, gender, drinking habits, smoking status, hypertension, diabetes, and stroke, performed subgroup analyses, and assessed whether there was a significant interaction between the different subgroups. The results showed (see Fig. 3 for details) that all subgroups showed a positive but not significant association between PHR and cognitive impairment (P > 0.05), and no significant interaction was observed in all subgroups (P for interaction > 0.05).

Discussion

This study of 2,299 Americans aged 60 and older was designed to explore the relationship between the PHR and cognitive ability. The results revealed a significant inverse correlation between PHR and cognitive scores, along with a “U-shaped” relationship between PHR and cognitive impairment. This non-linear association likely explains the lack of a strong positive correlation in multivariate logistic regression, despite the marginal association observed (OR = 1.384, 95% CI: 1.012–1.893; P < 0.05). While this association was statistically significant, the confidence interval lower bound of 1.01 indicates that the relationship is marginal and requires cautious interpretation. This suggests that PHR as an independent biomarker for cognitive impairment may be limited, with its influence becoming more apparent only after surpassing a certain threshold. Further studies with larger sample sizes and more robust model validation are required to confirm this relationship and clarify the underlying mechanisms. RCS analysis showed that when PHR exceeded 111.49, the likelihood of cognitive impairment increased significantly. These findings suggest that elevated PHR, particularly above 111.49, is linked to lower cognitive scores and a higher risk of cognitive impairment. This association may be due to higher platelet counts and lower HDL-C levels, both of which are known to promote vascular inflammation and neurodegenerative processes that contribute to cognitive decline.

PLT play a key role in repairing vascular damage and modulating inflammatory pathways, and due to their role in thrombosis, immune response regulation, and vascular homeostasis, PLT are thought to be implicated in the pathogenesis of several diseases including atherosclerosis, cancer, and stroke32. Additionally, PLT counts and activation levels have been found to affect cognitive function, with increased PLT counts often associated with heightened PLT activation. Excessive PLT activation may lead to vascular inflammation, carotid artery disease, excessive release of Aβ, multiple microemboli or infarcts, all of which are known contributors to cognitive impairment33. Excessive PLT activation can lead to vascular inflammation, carotid artery disease, excessive release of Aβ, multiple microemboli or infarcts, and ultimately may lead to cognitive impairment34,35. A recent study using resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) observed that PLT levels negatively impact cognitive function in patients with MCI, showing a negative correlation between PLT count and cognitive performance, particularly affecting executive functions in the left precuneus region36. As platelets are a primary source of peripheral blood Aβ, increased PLT count and activity may enhance cerebral Aβ deposition, worsening cognitive impairment and promoting the onset and progression of AD37,38. Other neurodegenerative conditions have noted similar associations between elevated PLT counts and cognitive decline39. These observations suggest that platelets play a significant role in the genesis and progression of cognitive impairment. Further research in this domain could elucidate the underlying mechanisms and offer novel insights for the early diagnosis and intervention of cognitive disorders.

HDL-C exerts multifaceted roles within the central nervous system, extending beyond reverse cholesterol transport to include antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, endothelial function promotion, antithrombotic, and immune function modulation activities, all of which are crucial for maintaining cognitive function40. Higher levels of HDL-C have been consistently linked to better cognitive performance in older individuals, while lower HDL levels increase the risk of future cognitive decline, memory deterioration, and neurodegenerative conditions41. Furthermore, low HDL-C levels are significantly correlated with reduced total white matter volume and hippocampal volume, increased cognitive impairment, and a heightened likelihood of MCI in the elderly42. A prospective study indicates that having greater levels of HDL-C in middle age is linked to a lower risk of developing MCI and dementia later in life, therefore supporting the idea that HDL-C has a protective effect on cognitive function43. In the context of AD, HDL-C is implicated in the clearance of cerebral Aβ and the reduction of amyloid plaque formation associated with AD, with higher HDL-C levels potentially reducing the risk of AD44. Additionally, high HDL-C levels may facilitate cholesterol efflux from neurons, reduce neuroinflammation, and protect neurons from oxidative stress, which are likely key mechanisms in its protective role in cognitive function45. This study focuses on the relationship between PHR and cognition, with results suggesting that low HDL levels may exacerbate cognitive impairment, consistent with the findings of the studies mentioned earlier.

While the present study suggests that elevated PHR may impair cognition, it does not imply that higher levels of HDL-C and lower PLT counts are invariably beneficial. There is evidence suggesting that excessively high HDL-C levels may disrupt cholesterol transport and metabolism, potentially affecting neuronal function and contributing to cognitive decline46. Elevated levels of HDL-C are linked to a higher likelihood of acquiring AD and any dementia in individuals47. Studies have shown that long-term increases in HDL-C are associated with the risk of developing cognitive decline and memory loss, and that there is an inverted U-shaped relationship between HDL-C levels and cognition, with either too high or too low HDL-C levels impairing cognition48–50. This suggests that excessively high HDL-C levels, likewise, impair cognition. Although high PLT counts impair cognition, low PLT counts are associated with coagulation disorders, spontaneous bleeding, etc. PLT promotes brain homeostasis in physiological states51, PLT is the largest peripheral store of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), and low levels of BDNF have been associated with poorer cognitive performance, whereas high levels of BDNF play a role in the prevention of the neurological syndromes52–54.

The findings of this study emphasize the possibility of PHR as a potential biomarker for assessing the risk of cognitive impairment, especially when PHR levels are high. However, it is important to acknowledge that the association found between PHR and cognitive impairment is marginal, as indicated by the lower bound of the confidence interval (CI = 1.012). This suggests that PHR as a standalone biomarker may not be sufficient for predicting cognitive impairment with high accuracy, and further research is needed to validate these findings in larger and more diverse populations. The results of the stratified analysis also confirmed that there was a positive association between PHR and cognitive impairment in both unused populations, especially in the stroke population, which may be due to the fact that both high PLT count and low HDL-C are risk factors for cardiovascular disease34,55. Future studies should adopt longitudinal designs, expand sample populations, and explore the interactions between PHR, platelet count, and HDL-C, as well as their specific impact on cognitive function. Including additional biomarkers and environmental factors will provide a more comprehensive understanding of cognitive decline. These efforts will contribute to more effective strategies for the prevention and treatment of cognitive impairment and establish a stronger theoretical foundation for PHR as a biomarker of cognitive function, revealing its underlying biological pathways.

Conclusions

Although this study provides preliminary evidence that PHR may be associated with cognitive impairment risk, the observed relationship was marginal, with a confidence interval lower bound close to 1.01. This suggests that PHR may not be a strong standalone predictor of cognitive impairment, and further validation through prospective longitudinal studies is necessary to establish causality. Additionally, because the sample was limited to the older American population, future research should include more diverse populations with different ethnic, regional, and cultural backgrounds to enhance the generalizability and applicability of the findings. Expanding the scope of research could also help clarify the biological mechanisms underlying the relationship between PHR and cognitive function.
