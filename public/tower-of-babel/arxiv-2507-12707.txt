Splittable Spanning Trees and Balanced Forests in Dense Random Graphs
David Gillman, Jacob Platnick, Dana Randall

Abstract
. Weighted equitable partitioning of a graph G G has been of interest lately due to several applications, including redistricting, network algorithms, and image decomposition. Equitably partitioning a graph according to the spanning-tree metric, which assigns a weight to each component equal to its number of spanning trees, and overall weight equal to the product of the weights of its components, has been of mathematical and practical interest because it typically favors partitions with more compact pieces. An appealing algorithm suggested by Charikar et al. is to sample a random spanning tree and remove k − 1 k-1 edges, producing a random forest. If the components of the forest form a balanced partition, the partition is equitable under an easily computed acceptance probability. Cannon et al. recently showed that spanning trees on grid graphs and grid-like graphs on n n vertices are splittable into k k equal sized pieces with probability at least n − 2 ​ k n^{-2k} , leading to the first rigorous sampling algorithm for a class of graphs. We present complementary results showing that spanning trees on dense random graphs also have inverse polynomial probability of being splittable, specifically with probability at least n ( − k / 2 ) n^{(-k/2)} for both the G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p) and G ⁡ ( n , m ) G(n,m) models, giving another class of graphs where equitable partitions can be efficiently sampled exactly. Interestingly, G ⁡ ( n , m ) G(n,m) is the easier case, and we infer the property for random graphs from G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p) indirectly. These proofs also guarantee that the up-down walk for almost uniformly sampling forests without restrictions on the sizes will produce a balanced forest with inverse polynomial probability, giving another provably efficient randomized method for generating equitable partitions. Further, we show that problems with the well-studied ReCom algorithm for equitable partitioning are more extensive than previously known, even in special cases that were believed to be more promising. We present a family of graphs where the Markov chain fails to be irreducible when it must keep the components perfectly equitable; yet when the chain is allowed an imbalance of just one vertex between components, the rejection sampling step may take exponential time. Thus, ReCom, which combines just two components at each step, can suffer from splittability as much as the direct algorithm that chooses a spanning tree and removes k − 1 k-1 edges at once. This is true even when the graph satisfies desirable properties that have been conjectured to be sufficient for fast sampling, such as Hamiltonicity, having low degree, or being an induced subgraph of the triangular lattice.

Introduction

Weighted, equitable partitions of a graph have garnered a lot of interest lately, especially in the context of political redistricting.
Given an integer

Notice that sampling partitions according to the spanning tree weight is equivalent to sampling balanced forests, where each connected component of the forest has exactly

In the context of redistricting, random weighted equitable partitions are sampled to form an ensemble against which proposed maps can be tested for political gerrymandering.
Each vertex of the graph represents a small indivisible region, or precinct, and the components of a partition represent the districts from which representatives are elected.

Algorithms for sampling balanced forests:
The most widely used algorithm for sampling balanced forests is ReCom (for recombination)

For simplicity of exposition, we refer to this group of algorithms simply as “ReCom,” although our results rely on any of the modified versions which have provably correct stationary distributions

These have been particularly popular among mathematicians assisting politicians and lawyers by establishing a baseline against which they can identify outliers. The algorithms all use Markov chains that walk on balanced forests (or weighted partitions) by iteratively joining two adjacent pieces and then splitting this joint region into two equal sized pieces with the correct conditional probabilities. When a tree cannot be equitably split by removing a single edge, the move is rejected and the Markov chain proceeds to propose a new recombination move.

For this approach to be efficient and reliable, it is necessary that the recombination move connects the state space of conditions, the chain is rapidly mixing, and subgraphs of
G
are

with at least an inverse polynomial probability. Unfortunately, it has been shown that there are potentially issues with each of these three requirements.
The chain is not guaranteed to mix quickly, and indeed there exist graphs where it mixes slowly (double cycle graphs)

. Worse, the state space may not even be connected; a cycle with
3n

3

[

]
. More strikingly,
the problem of determining connectivity of the state space under ReCom moves is PSPACE-complete, even for planar graphs
[
3
]
. This means that when running this algorithm on a given graph, the user has no way of knowing if the results are close to stationarity, and so we cannot trust this result for the purpose of sampling without additional proofs about some restricted classes of graphs.

Natural modifications to ReCom have been proposed to increase its connectivity and potentially its mixing rate by allowing some slack on the piece sizes. However, even with this allowance, it still has only been shown to be connected in special cases, such as the double cycle graph
[

]
, a triangular region of the triangular lattice with slack at most 1
[

, and Hamiltonian graphs with a significantly larger linear-sized slack
[

. For most graphs little is known about the probability of a random spanning tree being “splittable” into two (or more) equal sized pieces.

Alternative approaches for directly generating balanced forests also have been proposed, relying on a random spanning tree’s

Definition 1.1
.

A tree is
k

k-1

k

The simplest alternative sampling algorithm generates uniform spanning trees on all of

(using, for example, the loop erased random walk algorithm of Wilson
[

]
) and checks whether each tree is
k
-splittable, which can be done in efficiently if

is constant. Note that any splittable tree has a unique set of

edges whose removal leaves a balanced forest, so rejection sampling will generate such forests uniformly. If the tree is not splittable, it is resampled. This sampling algorithm produces samples from exactly the right distribution, but the running time depends on the rejection rate. A second algorithm uses a Markov chain to generate samples from approximately the right distribution, also using rejection sampling.

Approximate Markov chain algorithms also exist in this setting, using the up-down walk
[
11
]
to efficiently sample random forests with exactly
k
components but with no restriction on the sizes of the components. This chain has a
O(n^{2}\log(n))
mixing time on all graphs and is known to be ergodic. Balanced forests will be generated almost uniformly and all others will be rejected and resampled, so the algorithm will be fast if the rejection rate is not too large. Note that the probability that a random tree is splittable is polynomially related to the likelihood a random forest is balanced (see Lemma
2.2
), so either both or neither of these algorithms will be efficient.

Charikar et al.
[

conjectured that many graphs are splittable with inverse polynomial probability, including rectangular regions of

Recently, Cannon, Pegden and Tucker-Foltz

However, planarity is critical to their argument because they rely on the dual of the loop erased random walk of Wilson to bound the likelihood of specific families of forests being generated to show that these already occur with at least
1/n^{2}
probability, suppressing dependencies on

Little is known about the splittability of any other classes of graphs.

Next, to examine the mixing time of ReCom on the complete graph
K_{kn}

We therefore see that the mixing time of ReCom on this graph is proportional to the inverse probability that a random tree is splittable.

Let

Theorem 3

Let

Note that Cannon et al.

These are the first results showing any dense graphs are splittable with large enough probability to use rejection sampling, as needed by the simple algorithm, the up-down walk, and ReCom.
Interestingly, the results on
G(n,m)
are more straightforward to prove. We then derive the matching results for

What makes analysis of the splittability of dense graphs especially challenging, and even somewhat surprising, is that spanning trees that appear with higher probability than others are
less likely
to be splittable.
While any two spanning trees are equally likely to appear in a random graph, one tree may be more likely to appear in graphs that contain fewer spanning trees, thus making it more likely to be selected at random. For example, a star with three leaves is more likely than a path of three edges to be selected as a random spanning tree of a random graph on four vertices. Alon et al.
[
4
]
have shown that the diameter of random spanning trees of dense random graphs grow as
\sqrt{n}
, as was previously shown by Szekeres
[
17
]
for the complete graph. Trees with smaller diameter would seem less likely to be splittable than those with large diameter, so inverse polynomial bounds on the splittability of uniform spanning trees on dense graphs is more suprising than on sparse graphs such as the grid, where the diameter of a uniform spanning tree is linear.

Another difficulty is that balanced forests tend not to be the mode of a histogram showing the probabilities of splits of various sizes, even when
k=2
. In fact, when on the complete graph, balanced splits contain the fewest forests out of all possible partition sizes, as shown in Figure
2
. This means that standard techniques showing that this distribution is log-concave or unimodal will certainly fail and the calculations need to be more precise.

Instead of such combinatorial techniques,which are less likely to apply here, we use precise calculations to determine the probability of splittability on the complete graph. For random graphs, we use conditional probability on the number of edges to analyze settings similar to the complete graph, create precise calculations there, and use our results about the complete graph to draw conclusions about the probability of splittability in more general random graphs.

Lemma 2.3

For any graph

(u_{2},v)

, so by induction we get

∎

Consider two adjacent vertices

As the graph can only be partitioned along the Hamiltonian cycle, there are only

To show that the balanced partitions of the graph have exponentially less total weight than the almost balanced partitions with slack at most 1, we need only show that there exists an almost balanced partition with exponentially higher spanning tree weight than any individual balanced partition.

Now consider one of the two smaller ladders, for example, the one colored blue in Figure

Splittability of Dense Graphs

As Section

4.1.

We now show that in the complete graph a polynomial fraction of spanning trees are splittable.
This is equivalent to showing that a polynomial fraction of partitions of the complete graph are balanced (Lemma

In the next subsection we will extend this result to dense random graphs.
We state and prove this result first because we will use it in the proof of the stronger result.

Recall Theorem
2
, which says:

Theorem
2
.

Let
N=nk
.
The probability that a random spanning tree of
K_{N}
is
k
-splittable is
C_{k}n^{-\frac{k-1}{2}}
, where
C_{k}
depends only on
k
.

Proof.

The number of spanning trees in
K_{N}
is
N^{N-2}
. Each splittable spanning tree can be split uniquely into balanced pieces (Lemma
2.1
); therefore, each one is uniquely defined by the partition it creates, the spanning trees on the
K_{n}
s in those partitions, and the
k-1
edges between partitions.
There are
{(kn)!}/{(n!)^{k}k!}
such partitions.
The edges between the partitions form a spanning tree on
k-1
vertices, with each edge having
n^{2}
possibilities.
Then, by Stirling’s formula
[
1
]
, the probability a tree is splittable is

\displaystyle\frac{(n^{n-2})^{k}\frac{(n^{2})^{k-1}k^{k-2}}{k!}\frac{(kn)!}{(n!)^{k}}}{{(kn)}^{kn-2}}=

\displaystyle(1+O(\frac{1}{n}))^{k}(1+O(\frac{1}{k}))(1+O(\frac{1}{kn}))n^{-\frac{k-1}{2}}(\frac{e}{\sqrt{2\pi}})^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+o(k))n^{-\frac{k-1}{2}}(\frac{e}{\sqrt{2\pi}})^{k}.

If we set
C_{k}=(1+o(k))(\frac{e}{\sqrt{2\pi}})^{k}
, the theorem follows.
∎

We note that our bound for the probability that a random spanning tree is
k
-splittable on the compete graph is
\Omega(1/n^{(k-1)/2})
, better than the best known bound of
\Omega(n^{2-2k})
on the grid.
However, Algorithm
1
, which generates a
k
-partition from a spanning tree, uses rejection sampling twice; once when determining if a spanning tree is
k
-splittable, and once to account for the fact that each
k
-balanced partition may be generated by many different spanning trees. This second step of rejection sampling is maximally inefficient in the complete graph, with an acceptance probability of
1/(k^{k-2}n^{2k-2})
.

4.2.
Dense Random Graphs

We now show that in a dense random graph a polynomial fraction of spanning trees are splittable. This is equivalent to showing that a polynomial fraction of partitions of a dense random graph are balanced (Lemma
2.2
), which implies that we can find a balanced partition of a dense random graph in polynomial time by generating random spanning trees and using rejection sampling.

While each labeled tree is equally likely to appear in a random graph, it is not true that they are equally likely to be selected as a random spanning tree of a random graph. The probability that any given tree is selected as a random spanning tree is proportional to
\mathbf{E}[1/\tau(G)\,|\,T\in G]
, which varies for different trees.
Trees that occur in graphs that contain many other trees are less likely to be selected.

For example, a star with three leaves is more likely than a path of three edges to be selected as a spanning tree of a random graph on four vertices. We can see that adding
1
edge to a path of
4
vertices can create a graph with
3
or
4
spanning trees, while adding
1
to a star graph always creates a graph with
3
spanning trees. Since adding
2
or more edges always creates
K_{4}
or
K_{4}
minus one edge, we see that random graphs containing a given star graph have fewer spanning trees than those containing given path graph, so stars appear more often in random spanning tres of random graphs on
4
vertices than they do in random labeled trees on
4
vertices. Based on numerical results, this same trend persists into star and path graphs on
n
vertices.

Recall Theorem
3
, which says:

Theorem
3
.

Let
N=nk
. Let
G
be a random connected graph in either the
G(N,p)
model or the
G(N,m)

m=p\binom{N}{2},p=\Omega(1/\log n)

C_{k}n^{-\frac{k}{2}-O(1)}

We first prove that a random spanning tree in a connected graph in the

when

.
We then use that result to prove the corresponding result for the

model
when

In the statements of both theorems we fix

,

We note that in the

Theorem 4
.

Let
N=nk
.
Let
G

m=p\binom{N}{2}
edges on
N

Let
T
be a random spanning tree of

If
m=\Omega(\binom{N}{2}/\log n)

is

C_{k}n^{-\frac{k}{2}-O(1)}

C_{k}n^{\frac{1-k}{2}}pe^{-(2+o(1))/p}

Proof.

Let

Let

For a fixed

\displaystyle\frac{\tau_{k}(G)}{\tau(G)}\geq\frac{\tau_{k}(G)}{\tau_{max}(m,N)}

For random

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{\tau_{k}(G)}{\tau(G)}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\tau_{max}(m,N)}\mathbf{E}[\tau_{k}(G)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau_{max}(m,N)}\sum_{T~k\mathrm{-splittable}}\mathbf{P}[T\in G]

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{k}}{\tau_{max}(m,N)}N^{N-2}n^{\frac{1-k}{2}}\mathbf{P}[T\in G],

from Theorem

Grimmett

\displaystyle\frac{C_{k}}{(pN)^{N-2}}N^{N-2}n^{\frac{1-k}{2}}\mathbf{P}[T\in G]=\frac{C_{k}}{p^{N-2}}~n^{\frac{1-k}{2}}~\mathbf{P}[T\in G],

5.
Conclusions and Open Problems

We have shown that random spanning trees of the complete graph are polynomially likely to be splittable, and that the same holds for random spanning trees of dense random graphs. These allow polynomial time sampling techniques to guarantee an inverse polynomial probability of sampling a valid balanced partition.

It remains unknown whether the ratio
\tau(G)/\tau_{k}(G)
is bounded by some polynomial for many classes of random graphs. It is noteworthy that for small enough
p
, specifically
p=O(n^{-2})
, the probability that a random tree from a random graph conditioned on connectivity is
k
-splittable is inverse polynomial for a trivial reason - the expected number of edges is constant, so the number of spanning trees is bounded by a constant in
G(n,m)
, and bounded by a constant in sufficiently high probability in
G(n,p)
; therefore for every tree
T
, the expectation
\mathbf{E}[1/\tau(G)\,|\,T\in G]
is bounded below by some inverse polynomial. We conjecture that polynomial probability of splittability will hold for all values of
p
above the connectivity threshold.

We also observe a link between random spanning trees of random graphs and minimum spanning trees with random weights in the complete graph. We conjecture that random spanning trees of random graphs will have smaller diameter than random spanning trees of the complete graph, as this occurs on random minimum spanning trees
[
2
]

and in our empirical results on random graphs.
