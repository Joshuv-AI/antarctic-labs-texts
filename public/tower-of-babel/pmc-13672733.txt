Synthesis of selenium particles using Durio zibethinus seed extract
Authors: Linh Doan, Quynh N Le, Quynh LK Ong, Khanh G Huynh, Ngoc HY Le, Anh T K Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe average particle sizes of selenium particles (SeP) synthesized using Durio zibethinus seed extract (DZSE) and Durio zibethinus husk extract (DZHE) were 40.1 ± 11.4 nm and 47.7 ± 13.6 nm, respectively. The antimicrobial efficacy of SeP was assessed against Staphylococcus aureus (SA), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA), Salmonella enterica (SE), and Escherichia coli (Ec). SeP/DZE achieved 50% growth inhibition (MIC50) against SA, PA, SE, and Ec with a dilution percentage of 25%, indicating the ability to demonstrate antimicrobial activity. The green synthesis of SeP by using Durio zibethinus offered an eco-friendly approach, leveraging the reducing and stabilizing agents naturally present in plant extracts. The research demonstrated the potential of agricultural waste, such as Durio zibethinus husks and seeds, in nanotechnology for developing effective antimicrobial agents.Keywords: Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Salmonella enterica, Escherichia coli, Durian, Green synthesis

Introduction

Water plays a crucial role in both production and the daily activities of every individual. However, as society continues to develop, people are increasingly damaging the environment, especially water sources [1]. Every year, the amount of waste from factories and household waste discharged into rivers and oceans increases, and oil spills at sea also occur more frequently, making water sources increasingly polluted [2]. Wastewater discharged from many sources has caused a large amount of pollution that could be harmful to humans [3, 4]. The presence of microorganisms in water significantly contributes to water pollution, leading to various health issues in humans and contributing to the predicted water crisis in 2025 [5, 6]. Water contamination can originate from multiple sources, with hospitals being a common site where gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (SA), gram-negative Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA), and gram-negative Salmonella enterica (SE) bacteria are prevalent [5, 7–20]. Moreover, the presence of Escherichia coli (Ec) was also included as an important bacterial species in this study. E. coli is the representative species of the genus Escherichia, which mainly consists of motile, gram-negative bacilli belonging to the Enterobacteriaceae family and the Escherichia tribe [21]. E. coli can be easily isolated from clinical samples using general or selective media at 37 °C under aerobic conditions [21]. Especially, it is widely found in the large intestines of humans and warm-blooded animals as a facultative anaerobe. Pathogenic strains causing diarrhea have developed through horizontal gene transfer, acquiring specific traits that enhance their survival in hosts. Therefore, these diarrheagenic strains, known as diarrheagenic E. coli, vary in their colonization sites, virulence mechanisms, and the ensuing clinical symptoms, and are classified into different types, such as enteropathogenic E. coli, enterohemorrhagic E. coli (Shiga toxin-producing), enteroaggregative E. coli, enterotoxigenic E. coli, and enteroinvasive E. coli [22]. In addition, E. coli is serotyped according to its O (somatic), H (flagellar), and K (capsular) surface antigens using the modified Kauffmann scheme [23]. In individuals who are debilitated or immunosuppressed, or when gastrointestinal barriers are compromised, even normally harmless strains of E. coli can lead to infections [21]. Furthermore, even healthy individuals could be vulnerable to infections caused by certain highly adapted E. coli clones, which have evolved to cause a wide range of human diseases [21].

While antibiotics are effective in eliminating these bacteria, the overuse of antibiotics has given rise to antimicrobial resistance (AMR), posing a new threat to public health [13–15]. Consequently, there is a growing focus on researching antimicrobial substances that can combat these pathogens without leading to AMR.

Researchers worldwide have discovered that certain polymers can inhibit the growth of microorganisms and even destroy them, which is one solution to this problem. Chitosan (CS), polyethylene glycol (PEG), polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), CS/PVA, PEG, PVP, CS/PEG, CS/PVP/PEG/PVA in various physical forms (e.g., films, coatings, aqueous, solids, gels, beads,…) have recently been found to be effective in inhibiting SA, PA, and SE [24–44]. Metal oxide particles (e.g., silver, iron oxide, calcium oxide, zinc oxide, copper oxide, etc.) can also be used as antibacterial agents in conjunction with polymers [45–56]. Metal oxides (e.g., iron oxide/PVA, cobalt ferrites (CoFe2O4)/PEG/PVP, zinc oxide (ZnO), polymeric-ZnO, copper oxide (CuO), nickel oxide (NiO), polymeric-CuO, polymeric-NiO, CoFe2O4/polymers-blend, graphitic carbon nitride/polymers-blend, Fe3O4/Se/Ag/polymers-blend, ZnO/polymers-blend, etc.) can also be mixed with polymers to generate antibacterial composites [17, 18, 57–76]. However, out of all metal or metal oxide nanoparticles, silver nanoparticles are among the most well-studied and common antibacterial agents [77]. In contrast, selenium nanoparticles, which can be used as antibacterial and anticancer agents, are still shrouded in mystery [78].

Briefly, the production and stabilization of selenium particles and selenium composites can be achieved through a range of techniques, encompassing both conventional methods such as chemical reduction using ascorbic acid (i.e. vitamin C), sonochemical synthesis, hydrothermal synthesis, and physical evaporation, as well as innovative approaches such as green synthesis involving bacteria, fungi, plant extracts, and more. These selenium-based materials have shown potential as antibacterial agents effective against SA, SE, and PA [79–86]. Moreover, as a trace element, selenium is essential for appropriate immune system function, plays a role in redox process regulation and iodine metabolism, and has anti-inflammatory and antiviral properties [20, 24, 25]. Selenium additionally serves as an antioxidant enzyme cofactor (glutathione peroxidase and thioredoxin reductase), protecting the human body from reactive oxygen species (ROS) [26].

Selenium particles (SeP) can be synthesized through biological, physical, and chemical methods [87, 88]. Each offers unique benefits and drawbacks. For biological methods, a potential drawback of this approach is that genetically manipulating eukaryotic organisms to overexpress specific enzymes involved in nanomaterial synthesis is considerably more challenging compared to prokaryotes [89]. These methods form heterogeneous populations of metalloid-nanomaterials in the same system. Additionally, synthesis procedures based on physical methods are not cost-effective to perform due to the complexity of the entire operating system [90, 91]. Another drawback of physical methods is the generation of SeP with various sizes, which is generally caused by a splashing phenomenon due to the laser irradiation of Se0 atoms deposited onto the substrates [90, 91]. Consequently, broad SeP distributions (polydispersity) are usually produced [92]. Chemical method reduction involves using specific chemical compounds to reduce an element, its salts (sodium selenite – Na2SeO3), or other compounds. The particle size is managed through the use of surfactants or growth-terminating agents like polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) or folic acid [93]. This process results in a stable colloidal solution of selenium particles (SeP), which are sought after for various applications [83]. The microwave method is one of the chemical methods used to synthesize SeP. It is known for its rapid, effortless, and cost-effective nature and has become a popular green synthesis technique [83]. It provides high yields and uniform heating by directly interacting with molecules, making it more efficient than conventional methods [83]. Despite these advantages, the use of microwave energy for synthesizing SeP is scarcely documented in the literature. Another chemical method is the hydrothermal method. Shi et al. pioneered the use of cellulose nanocrystals to reduce Na2SeO3, resulting in the eco-friendly and convenient synthesis of SeP with sizes ranging from 10 to 20 nm [94]. However, a notable disadvantage of this method is the production of excessively small particles. Over the past two decades, chemical methods using green synthesis of metal nanoparticles have attracted significant research interest. Although SeP offer numerous advantages over organic and inorganic selenium compounds, their poor cellular uptake remains challenging [83]. Therefore, synthesizing SeP through green methods to enhance biocompatibility and stability is highly desirable. Traditional methods rely on chemical reducing and stabilizing agents, which may be toxic and limit their biological applications [83]. In contrast, green agents derived from plant extracts and microorganisms are considered superior due to their cost-effectiveness and non-toxic nature [95]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that these green extracts can effectively reduce and stabilize nanoparticles, with a substantial body of literature supporting the green synthesis of SeP using plant extracts (e.g., Artemisia dracunculus [96], Urtica dioica [97]), which also yields high-efficiency antimicrobial activities. Ideally, extracts should come from plants or agricultural waste products to reduce the negative impact on the environment.

Out of all agricultural waste, roughly 70–85% of a durian fruit (Durio zibethinus L.), a major tropical fuit crop in Southeast Asia often hailed as the “King of Fruits”, is discarded as waste (including the seeds), which could pose an environmental problem if not disposed of properly [98–100]. Durio zibethinus, or durians, are produced on a large scale in the food industry, and handling them after being discarded can generate a significant amount of waste. Every year, the amount of durian husks (white part) discarded worldwide is estimated to be hundreds of thousands of tons [98]. For example, in Malaysia, one of the largest durian-producing countries, the amount of durian husks (white part) discarded annually is about 376,000 tons [98]. In fact, 5–15% of a durian is composed of seeds, while 55–66% is made up of the husk and seeds in a durian, which means the consumable portion of the durian constitutes merely 15–30% of its total edible mass [99]. Therefore, effectively managing the vast quantity of discarded seeds and husks (white portion) of durian presents a significant challenge because of issues related to waste disposal, environmental pollution, and potential risks to public health if not handled efficiently [98]. Research on durian has experienced a substantial increase in recent years, particularly in the area of metabolic profiling. Moreover, the fruit pulp, the main organ of interest, is abundant in bioactive compounds and serves as a potent source of vitamins, including thiamin (vitamin B1), riboflavin (vitamin B2), A, C, and E, as well as minerals such as calcium, phosphorus, potassium, and iron [101, 102]. Thus, research and development aimed at utilizing durian husk waste (white part) and seeds presents an intriguing area of study for Southeast Asian countries that export agricultural produce [103]. Given that durian peels are predominantly composed of carbohydrates [103, 104], they naturally contain polysaccharides that can serve as both reducing and stabilizing agents in the synthesis of nanoparticles, obviating the need for additional chemical reducing agents [103]. This reaction occurs in an aqueous solution, making the process environmentally benign [103]. Consequently, Chutrakulwong et al. demonstrated that the efficient synthesis of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) using durian peel extract was successful [103]. Therefore, the primary objective of this study is to utilize identical reducing agents for the purpose of synthesizing sodium selenite (Na2SeO3) to SeP. This process aims to contribute to the advancement of knowledge in nanoparticle production through the utilization of natural resources, particularly durian seeds with husks (white portion), as a more environmentally friendly and sustainable alternative compared to the conventional use of synthetic chemicals typically employed in traditional methodologies [98]. By exploring this approach, this research seeks to establish a novel approach towards the creation of SeP, thereby potentially offering a more efficient and eco-conscious method for nanoparticle synthesis.

Hence, to create a novel antibacterial agent against SA, PA, and SE, selenium particles (SeP) were synthesized from Durio zibethinus husks and seed extracts (DZHE and DZSE, respectively), and their antimicrobial activity against SA, SE, PA and Ec was studied.

Experimental methods

Materials

Sodium selenite (Na2SeO3) was obtained from Weng Jiang Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shaoguan, China). Ethanol (99.7%) was purchased from Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd. (Shantou, China). Staphylococcus aureus strain ATCC 29,523, Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922, and Salmonella enterica ATCC 14,028 were obtained from the School of Biotechnology, International University – Vietnam National University (Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam). Pseudomonas aeruginosa strain ATCC 9027 was provided by the Research Center for Infectious Disease, International University – Vietnam National University (Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam). All materials were used as obtained.

Methods

The green synthesis process of selenium seeds (SeP/DZE) using extract from Durio zibethinus peel/rice as a reducing agent and stabilizer, along with steps to evaluate antibacterial activity against test bacterial strains, is described in general in Fig. 1.

Experimental methods for the green synthesis of selenium particles (SeP/DZE) using Durio zibethinus extract and their antimicrobial activity evaluation

Durio zibethinus extract (DZE)

Fresh Durio zibethinus husks (DZH) and seeds (DZS) were collected (10°52’07.3"N 106°43’43.5"E), properly cleaned with distilled water (DI), washed with ethanol (~ 10% v/v), and cut into small pieces. The white parts of the husks and seeds were dried at 70 °C overnight. Approximately 10 g of the chopped and dried white parts of the Durio zibethinus husks or seeds were weighed and placed in a 250 mL Duran bottle containing 200 mL DI water, stirred thoroughly, and heated at 80 °C for 120 min. The extracted liquid was filtered through a 200–Mesh filter and collected in a 250 mL Duran bottle.

Synthesis of selenium particles (SeP/DZE)

This procedure was repeated three times. Initially, Na2SeO3 solutions of several concentrations were prepared by combining 5mL of DI water with 0.025, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 g of Na2SeO3. Subsequently, 5 mL of the Na2SeO3 solution was introduced into 60 mL of husk extract (DZHE) and seed extract (DZSE), and the resulting mixture was agitated at 60 °C for 390 and 355 min, respectively. To determine the complete conversion of Se2+ to Se0, the absorbance of SeP/DZE (at various Na2SeO3 concentrations) was measured at a wavelength of 200–700 nm using UV–VIS spectroscopy [105].

Determining the inhibition percentage of SeP/DZE

This experiment was conducted three times. The bacteria (SA, PA, SE, and Ec) and incubation technique utilized in this experiment were unchanged from previous studies [32]. The minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) against SA, PA, and SE was assessed by utilizing 9.5 L of SeP/DZE, following the procedures and equipment described in the referenced publication [32]. The 9.5 L of SeP/DZE was dried at 100 °C for 72 h using the Bluepard DHG-9075 A (China, Shanghai). Subsequently, 0.15 g of the dried solid was mixed with 60 mL of deionized water for analysis of particle morphology, functional groups, and composition.

Results and discussion

Conversion of Se2+ to Se0

Through the measurement of absorbance at 315–350 nm (using UV-VIS spectrometer, Hach, DR6000, CO, USA), the maximum mass of Na2SeO3 required for 100% conversion of Se2+ to Se0 can be determined, as illustrated in Fig. 2 [105–109].

(a) Maximum absorbance at 315–350 nm vs. mass of Na2SeO3 of DZHE. b UV-VIS spectra of SeP/M8 at different doses of Na2SeO3 of DZHE

During the experimental procedure, the color changes were clearly observable as the samples transitioned from light yellow to light pink, and eventually to dark pink over a period of time. The color of the solution could be directly correlated to the concentration of the nanoparticles formed, which was reflected in the absorption in the visible range [110, 111]. The red color of the reaction mixture solution indicated the presence of Se particles resulting from the reduction of Se ions, their conversion to Se0, and the subsequent formation of Se particles [112]. The variations in color were directly influenced by the concentration of Na2SeO3 introduced. The final result displayed two distinct trend lines merging to create a slope on the graph in Fig. 2a. Furthermore, the absorbance increased from 200 nm to 700 nm proportionally with the quantity of Na2SeO3. However, a shift in the slope occurred when the amount of Na2SeO3 reached 0.1 g per 60 mL of Durio zibethinus husks extract (DZHE). This indicated that the maximum mass of Na2SeO3 was necessary for the complete conversion of Se2+ to Se0, ranging from 0.1 g to the origin (0,0). This process was similar to the conversion of Ag+ to Ag0 as described in the literature [113].

As illustrated in Fig. 2b, the experiment was conducted over around 6.5 h. The graph’s data revealed that the absorbance value increased significantly with the initial time and began to flatten out at the end of the time period. This observation might indicate that the reaction was approaching the equilibrium state around the 6.5-hour mark. The progression towards equilibrium suggested a balance in the formation and transformation of Se particles, which might potentially stabilize the reaction dynamics at the specific time point.

Similarly, the same phenomenon happens to seeds. In detail, according to Fig. 3, the equilibrium time and the maximum mass of Na2SeO3 for 60mL of seed extract were 390 min and 0.05 g, respectively.

(a) Max absorbance at 315–350 nm vs. mass of Na2SeO3 of DZSE. b UV-VIS spectra of SeP/M8 at various concentrations of Na2SeO3 of DZSE

Characterization

A field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, Hitachi SU8000, Tokyo, Japan), a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR, Tensor 27, Bruker, MA, USA), X-ray crystallography (XRD, Bruker, D8Advance ECO A25-X1-9C2Z11G7F0, Karisruhe, Germany), and energy - dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (JEOL JED-2300, Tokyo, Japan) were used to examine the morphology, functional group, elemental composition, and crystallographic structure of the synthesized materials.

FE-SEM

The morphology of SeP/DZE is shown in Figure 4.

FE-SEM images of SeP synthesized using (a, b) DZHE, (c, d) DZSE

As depicted in Fig. 4a and b, the SeP/DZHE particles retained a spherical morphology structure, with individual particles being clearly distinguishable and showing a relatively consistent size distribution. Although these particles were uniform in size, a slight tendency toward aggregation was observed, which was a typical phenomenon observed for particles. In comparison, Fig. 4c and d do not exhibit any obvious differences from Fig. 2a and b. However, the selenium particles synthesized using DZSE appeared to be more evenly dispersed across the surface, with a more homogeneous distribution and less noticeable clustering. Hence, the FE-SEM analysis confirmed that the selenium particles were in the nanometer range, implying the production of selenium particles.

As shown in Fig. 5a, the size distribution of SeP synthesized using DZHE exhibited an average size of 47.7 ± 13.6 nm due to a slight tendency toward aggregation observed in FE-SEM images of DZHE. In contrast, with less noticeable clustering, the measure 40.1 ± 11.4 nm indicated the smaller size distribution of SeP synthesized using DZSE compared to DZHE. Since the size of the particles is within the range of 1–100 nm, these particles can be considered to be nanoparticles [114].

XRD analysis

As shown in Fig. 7, the XRD analysis of SeP/DZE was illustrated.

XRD spectra of SeP synthesized using (a) DZHE, (b) DZSE

As illustrated in Fig. 7, the XRD patterns of both DZSE- and DZHE-mediated SeP did not exhibit distinct diffraction peaks corresponding to crystalline trigonal selenium. Instead, a broad diffraction halo centered at approximately 20–30° (2θ) was observed, indicating the predominantly amorphous nature of the samples. The absence of characteristic reflections suggests that selenium is mainly present in an amorphous state and/or as highly dispersed ultra-small nanodomains below the detection limit of XRD.

According to the elemental composition analysis (Table 1), EDS confirmed the presence of selenium with contents of 6.61 ± 0.14 wt% in DZSE and 4.92 ± 1.08 wt% in DZHE. However, no characteristic crystalline selenium peaks were observed in the XRD patterns. Although selenium was clearly detected, a content of 4–6 wt% may still be relatively low for phase identification by XRD, particularly when the selenium phase is amorphous or poorly crystalline and embedded within a predominantly amorphous organic matrix. Under such conditions, diffraction signals can be significantly weakened or masked, resulting in the absence of well-defined reflections. This observation suggests that selenium nanoparticles are present in an amorphous or highly dispersed state, consistent with previous reports [120].

The similar diffraction profiles obtained for DZSE and DZHE further indicate that selenium incorporation did not induce long-range crystalline ordering within the matrix. While FE-SEM images revealed clearly defined nanoparticles with DZHE and DZSE average sizes of 47.7 ± 13.6 nm and 40.1 ± 11.4 nm, respectively, XRD reflects crystallographic order rather than particle diameter; therefore, particles of this size may still appear amorphous or poorly crystalline in XRD analysis.

Considering that Na2SeO3 was the sole selenium precursor introduced during synthesis, together with the consistent EDS confirmation of selenium presence and the visible nanoparticles in FE-SEM images, it is reasonable to conclude that the observed particles correspond to selenium nanoparticles despite the absence of distinct crystalline reflections in XRD.

From the antibacterial experiments, the MIC50 values exhibited variability among all bacterial species at the same 25% dilution percentage. However, the MIC90 revealed significant differences in the efficacy of the DZHE and DZSE samples for each bacterial species. Both PA and SE demonstrated MIC90 for DZHE at a dilution of 50%, indicating substantial inhibition. In contrast, Ec required a higher concentration, with both DZHE and DZSE achieving MIC90 only at a dilution of 100%. Notably, SA did not achieve MIC90 with either DZHE or DZSE at any tested dilution level, suggesting that SA is ineffective against both samples. Besides that, the antibacterial activity of DZHE was slightly more effective than that of DZSE, and it was safe to consume, as SE was the most affected by DZE.

Before exploring antibacterial processes, we must first comprehend the distinction between gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria. Gram-negative bacteria were well-known for having a bilayer cell wall with a negative charge that has an inner and outer layer [121]. This wall was composed of lipopolysaccharides on the outside and peptidoglycan on the inside [121]. In contrast, gram-positive bacteria had a thicker cell wall and a lower negative charge than gram-negative bacteria [121]. While they were generally more susceptible to antibiotics compared to gram-negative bacteria, some gram-positive bacteria had also developed resistance mechanisms, such as the production of enzymes that could inactivate antibiotics [122]. Metal oxides were shown to have antibacterial effects using the following mechanisms: (1) ion release, (2) ROS (reactive oxygen species), (3) membrane interactions, (4) inhibiting biofilm development, and (5) biofilm eradication [123]. Therefore, it was postulated that selenium particles in SeP/DZE display resistance due to the aforementioned processes.

Additionally, given that the initial stock concentration was 0.15 g in 60 mL (i.e., 0.0025 g/mL), the corresponding concentrations after 25%, 50%, and 100% dilution - assuming homogeneous particle dispersion - are 0.000625 g/mL, 0.00125 g/mL, and 0.0025 g/mL, respectively.

Conclusion

For SeP/DZHE, 100% conversion of Se2+ to Se0 requires a mass of Na2SeO3 of 0.1 g. Additionally, the particles in SeP/DZE have an average size of 47.7 ± 13.6 nm. Along with that, in the case of SeP/DZSE, to achieve 100% conversion of Se2+ to Se0, the mass of Na2SeO3, and the average size of the particles were determined to be 0.05 g and 40.1 ± 11.4 nm. Hence, SeP/DZHE, and SeP/DZSE inhibited 50% bacterial growth (MIC50) in Staphylococcus aureus (SA), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA), Salmonella enterica (SE), and Escherichia coli (Ec) with dilution percentages of 25% (or 0.000625 g/mL) for these two samples, DZHE and DZSE. Therefore, it was discovered that both of the composites DZSE and DZHE exhibited antibacterial activity against gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria. Overall, the synthesis of p particles using Durio zibethinus husk and seed extracts presented the potential ability for antimicrobial activities and the utilization of agricultural waste. Especially, future studies could investigate this green synthesis process and widen its applications in biomedical and environmental fields.
