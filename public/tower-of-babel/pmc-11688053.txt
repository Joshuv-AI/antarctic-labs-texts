Association between pathological scar and hypertension: A two-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Xiyang Li, Bo Jiang, Chong Yao, Site Li, Yuzhi Zuo, Hong Yan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Observational studies have linked pathological scars to hypertension; however, the causality remains ambiguous. In this study, we aimed to explore this issue using Mendelian randomization (MR). We obtained genome-wide association study data for hypertrophic scar and hypertension from the IEU Open genome-wide association study project [hypertension (containing 9851,867 SNPs, observation group of 124,227, and control group of 337,653), hypertrophic scars (containing 16,380,443 SNPs with a sample mass of 207,482), and keloids (containing 24,197,210 SNPs and sample volume of 481,912)]. The inverse variance weighted method and MR-Egger test were used, followed by a sensitivity analysis. With hypertension as the exposure and hypertrophic scar as the outcome, we obtained the IVW analysis results (OR = 0.264, 95% CI = 0.098–0.709, P = .008) and the MR-Egger test results (OR = 0.036, 95% CI = 0.002–0.544, P = .017); for keloid as the outcome, the results of IVW analysis showed OR = 0.592, 95% CI = 0.293–1.195, P = .143. Contrary to the findings of observational studies, our results revealed hypertension as a protective factor against hypertrophic scarring, and was unrelated to keloids.

1. Introduction

Pathological scars can usually be classified into 2 types, namely hypertrophic scars and keloids.[1] They are characterized by fibroblast hyperproliferation and collagen deposition from fibroblasts.[2,3] In recent years, many studies have indicated that pathological scars are caused by chronic inflammation of the skin and are associated with a variety of circulating immune cells and cell factors.[4,5] Pathological scars not only give the patient an unpleasant appearance but are also accompanied by itching, pain, and other discomfort, causing the patient to suffer both physically and mentally. Although several treatments have been proposed till date, including surgical removal, laser treatment, radiation therapy, and injection therapy,[3] no single method is sufficient for solving all the problems related to pathological scars. Therefore, treatment of pathological scars remains a complex and challenging clinical problem.

Hypertension is a global issue that endangers people’s health.[6] Besides genetic predisposition, the etiology of hypertension involves a complex interplay of environmental and pathophysiological factors that affect multiple systems.[7] It is one of the most important risk factors for ischemic heart disease, stroke, chronic kidney disease, and dementia.[8–11] In fact, it affects all aspects of physiological health of the human body.

Previous studies had reported a certain relationship between hypertension and pathological scars (hypertrophic scars and keloids)[12]; however, it has not been a precise conclusion and needs further studies. Mendelian randomization (MR) may be an effective method to address this issue.

MR is an important tool that uses genetic variation as an instrumental variable (IV) to infer the causal effects of exposure factors on the outcome.[13] Similar to randomized control experiments, MR utilizes the principle of random distribution of parental genes in the offspring during meiosis, gametogenesis, and conception, and is generally less prone to confounding and reverse causation, which can impair the interpretation of results from conventional observational studies.[14] Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) reported the first robust genetic associations with common diseases and can enable MR to be very informative.[15]

In this study, we used GWAS data to conduct a two-way MR analysis to determine whether hypertension is associated with pathological scars and, if so, to provide important evidence of the causal role of hypertension in pathological scars.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

This was a two-sample bidirectional MR study. To ensure that the causal inference derived from the MR analysis is valid, the IV had to meet the following three core assumptions: the relevance assumption, which implies that the genetic variation should be closely related to the exposure phenotype; the independence assumption, which indicates that the IV should not be associated with the mixture; and the exclusion restriction assumption, which implies that the effect ends only through exposure.[16,17]

MR analysis was conducted in two directions, namely high blood pressure as an exposure (to evaluate whether people with hypertension are more susceptible to reproductive scars); and high blood pressure as a result (to assess whether patients with pathological scars are more prone to hypertension).

2.2. Data sources

The entire study population was European, including both males and females.

A GWAS of hypertension, hypertrophic scars, and keloids was conducted through the IEU Open GWAS project; hypertension (ukb-b-14177) had 9851,867 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), an observation group of 124,227, and a control group of 337,653; hypertrophic scar (finn-b-L12_HYPETROPHICSCAR) had 16,380,443 SNPs with a sample mass of 207,482; and keloid (ebi-a-GCST90018874) had 24,197,210 SNPs and a sample volume of 481,912.

The current study involved a reanalysis of previously collected and published data; therefore, it did not require additional ethical approval.

2.3. SNPs

Significantly related SNPs, as the IVs, were filtered from hypertension GWAS data, and the threshold for obtaining IV was set to P < 5 × 10−8. Furthermore, to ensure the independence of each IV, we conducted the linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping process; the linkage disequilibrium coefficient (r2) was set to 0.001, width of the linkage disequilibrium was set to 10,000 kb, and SNPs that did not exist in the result and those returned were removed.

2.4. Mendelian randomization

The principal use of inverse variance weighted (IVW) method (the most effective method compared to others[18]), to verify the causal relationship between exposure (hypertension) and outcome (hypertrophic scar and keloid), was supplemented with the MR-Egger method.[19,20] SNP was used as an IV; P < .05 in the result indicated a potential and statistically significant causal relationship in the MR analysis.

2.5. Sensitivity analysis

We used a variety of methods for sensitivity analysis.

1) The Cochran’s Q test assessed the heterogeneity of the selected SNPs; the resulting P-value > 0.05 indicated an absence of heterogeneity and did not affect the interpretation of the result, and vice versa.

2) An MR-Egger regression intercept was used to examine the horizontal pleiotropy of the SNPs.[21] If the intercept in the MR-Egger regression was close to zero and not statistically significant, the MR analysis had no significant horizontal multiplicity. The “leave-one-out” analysis was then conducted by removing each SNP individually to assess whether the variation could drive the association between exposure and outcome variable; if the result after removal of the SNP was small, the removal was considered to not have a nonspecific effect on the effect estimate.

2) An MR-Egger regression intercept was used to examine the horizontal pleiotropy of the SNPs.[21] If the intercept in the MR-Egger regression was close to zero and not statistically significant, the MR analysis had no significant horizontal multiplicity. The “leave-one-out” analysis was then conducted by removing each SNP individually to assess whether the variation could drive the association between exposure and outcome variable; if the result after removal of the SNP was small, the removal was considered to not have a nonspecific effect on the effect estimate.

Finally, funnel and forest plots were generated to visualize the results of sensitivity analysis.[22]

All statistical analyses were performed using MR and Two Sample MR packets in R software version 4.3.2.

3. Results

For high blood pressure as the exposure, and hypertrophic scars and keloids as the outcome, a total of 232 hypertension-related SNPs were screened (Table 1), and after data harmonization, 230 SNPs were obtained. Through MR analysis, we found hypertension as a protective factor for hypertrophic scar (Table 2); IVW analysis showed OR = 0.264, 95% CI = 0.098–0.709, P = .008 and MR-Egger results showed OR = 0.036, 95% CI = 0.002–0.544, P = .017. Hypertension had no apparent correlation with keloids, the results of IVW analysis showed OR = 0.592, 95% CI = 0.293–1.195, P = .143.

4. Discussion

The results of MR analysis in this study suggested that hypertension is a protective factor for hypertrophic scars and is not associated with keloids; this is contrary to the previous reports that suggested that hypertension is a possible risk factor for pathological scars. Previous studies reported that high blood pressure may be a risk factor for hypertrophic scars and keloids.[23–25] Proper blood pressure control with medication has been reported to improve pathological scars.[26]

There are several possible reasons for this bias, contrary to previous studies. Due to the methodological limitations of traditional observational research, such as confounders, reverse causation, and information bias, the relevancy may be biased.[27,28] Observational studies, such as cross-sectional studies, include the possibility of reverse causation. Lack of data, such as sequential hypertension data, circulatory inflammatory factor levels, local skin stress, and physical factors of friction, may affect the observed association. Confounders, such as obesity, namely diet, smoking, and psychological stress, may further lead to faulty associations.[29,30]

Hypertension may affect pathological scars by influencing the blood supply to skin, microcirculation, fibroblast synthesis, and inflammatory responses. Immediately after an injury, there is a little vascular damage, breaking, or bleeding, which initiates a process to stop the bleeding.[31] After hemostasis, cell proliferation, and remodeling, wound healing results in scar formation. The scars may lead to adverse outcomes, such as skin scars potentially developing into hypertrophic scars or keloids, and scar defects after cesarean sections, easily leading to cesarean scar pregnancy (CSP).[32] Hypertrophic scars and keloids are thought to be caused by poor wound healing following a trauma.[3] People with hypertension have high blood pressure in the arteries.[33] Thus, the skin wound has a good blood supply during the period of acute injury. Blood-rich wounds typically heal faster and are less likely to develop into pathological scars.[34] Sestrin levels in patients with hypertension are significantly elevated[35] and affect multiple signaling pathways, including AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK).[36] The actin-cable-type of embryonic wound healing, a rapid and scar-free repair process, requires the activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK).[37] Patients with hypertension have microcirculatory restructuring, which presents as anatomical sparseness of the capillary structure.[38,39] Controlling the number of local capillaries is another treatment for pathological scars.[40] Meanwhile, patients with diabetes mellitus (DM) combined with hypertension have a negative correlation with substance P levels in the peripheral blood,[41] and substance P participates in hypertrophic scar formation.[42] Pathological scars are characterized by the excessive proliferation of fibroblasts and collagen deposition. Research has shown that the expression of p53 in keloids and hypertrophic scars is significantly higher than in normal skin, and inhibition of the p53/Sp1 signaling pathway can reduce collagen accumulation and prevent the formation of post-GFS vesicular scars.[43] Hypertension may alter collagen synthesis and degradation between fibroblasts and/or endothelial cells through autocrine and paracrine signaling, including signaling pathways involving angiotensin-converting enzyme.[44] In an Ang II-induced hypertension model, inhibition of the TLR4/NF-κB pathway and activation of the NLRC4 inflammasome reduced renal injury, inflammatory responses, and renal fibrosis.[45] Hypertension can lead to increased collagen deposition in cardiac fibroblasts (CFs), thereby promoting myocardial fibrosis.[46] The level of Alu methylation varies in the skin fibroblasts obtained from patients with different blood pressure conditions, which may be related to hypertension.[47]

Hypertrophic scar is a chronic inflammation of the skin,[5] and is associated with circulating cytokines.[30] Hypertension is considered a chronic inflammation of the endocrine system of blood vessels.[48] In this study, MR revealed a genetic relationship between the two. Although both are inflammatory diseases, the inflammation involves many complex aspects of inflammatory cells, inflammatory factors, and signal pathways.[49] Most existing studies have dealt with observations and discussions of hypertension and keloids, and very few conclusions have been drawn regarding hypertrophic scars and hypertension. However, studies on inflammation and hypertrophic scarring are not comprehensive. The currently available information is insufficient to determine whether the two are linked by inflammation. Whether hypertension affects hypertrophic scarring through inflammatory processes and, if so, what are the possible specific mechanisms remain unclear. However, further clinical and basic research would be required to confirm the association.

Previous studies have shown that individuals with darker skin are more prone to pathological scars[50] and that hypertension has a promoting effect.[51] Contrary to the conclusions drawn in this study, we speculated that the correlation between hypertension and pathological scars may differ across ethnic groups. In the database used, European ancestry was dominant, and this study only analyzed the European population; therefore, the results are still credible.

This study has several advantages. First, we used genetic variation as the IV of exposure to explore the two-way causal association between hypertension and pathological scars, unlike traditional retrospective studies, which are less susceptible to the influence of confounders and reverse causation. Second, we conducted heterogeneity and pleiotropy tests to ensure consistency and reliability of the results.

However, this study had some limitations as well. First, since all the data were obtained from people of European descent, the results did not represent a genuine random population sample and may not necessarily apply to other races. Furthermore, the sample volume of the GWAS data is not large enough at present, and further research using more GWAS data would be required in future. Additionally, the effects of different classifications and groupings of hypertension on pathological scars were not investigated.

In summary, in this study, we revealed (from a genetic perspective) that hypertension may be a protective factor against reproductive scarring in Europe, with no apparent correlation with keloids. This could serve as a reference for comprehensive clinical treatment of hypertrophic scars in future, and could help make a better choice for the appropriate maintenance of blood pressure and avoidance of low blood pressure during the treatment process. More comprehensive clinical studies would be required to validate the relationship between hypertension and pathological scars.
