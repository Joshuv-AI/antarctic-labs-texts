Advected carbon younger than the sediment fuels microbial metabolism in a pumped deep aquifer
Authors: Brian J. Mailloux, Kazi Matin Ahmed, Afsana Akter, Sarah Blau, Benjamin Bostick, Bruce A. Buchholz, Imtiaz Choudhury, Tyler Ellis, Charles Harvey, Mahfuzur Khan, Madeleine Killough, Maggie C. Y. Lau Vetter, Holly Michael, Rajib Mozumder, Katharine Scanlan, Greg Slater, Elizabeth Trembath-Reichert, Alexander van Geen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ever deeper wells are drilled worldwide to pump potable groundwater. Recent studies argue that overpumping compresses clays and releases reactive dissolved organic carbon (DOC), which in turn drives a series of microbial reactions that affect groundwater potability including arsenic concentrations. Here, we use a novel method to measure the radiocarbon ages of microbial RNA to determine the source of reactive or metabolizable DOC and argue against the compression of clays as the sole source of carbon. We show that microbial RNA (5,230; 5,550; 6,250 yr; n = 3 wells), DOC (280-10,800 yr; n = 13), and methane (modern to 6,240 yr; n = 3), from an overpumped deep aquifer in Bangladesh are much younger than the overlying clay layers deposited during the Pleistocene over 12,000 years ago. Mass-balance indicates that at least half of the carbon incorporated into RNA has to come from reactive DOC or methane that is advected downward via vertical recharge. This metabolism of advected organic carbon could have implications for the quality of water pumped from deep aquifers.

Introduction

Shallow aquifers are often depleted, contaminated, or affected by saltwater intrusion, requiring drinking water to be pumped from deep aquifers1–3. Altering the hydrology of deep aquifers enhances two processes that could introduce reactive carbon and fuel redox processes that degrade water quality, including the release of geogenic arsenic: (a) increased downward flow, which could bring reactive carbon into deep aquifers4, and (b) the compression of clays, which could release reactive carbon directly into deep aquifers5. Indirect evidence suggests either pathway could be important, but neither has been directly documented.

Whether advected from a shallow aquifer or released from a clay, reactive DOC will by definition be metabolized by microbes near their source in the presence of abundant electron acceptors such as oxygen, nitrate, iron oxides, and sulfate6. In shallow aquifers, these DOC-fueled biogeochemical transformations can both benefit and endanger groundwater quality by, for instance, consuming fertilizer-derived nitrate and limiting uranium and chromium solubility7,8, or conversely by releasing iron (Fe), manganese, and especially arsenic that continues to poison over 100 million people drinking untreated groundwater across the world9. Larger DOC molecules are more likely to adsorb, remaining near their source, and are therefore protected from degradation10–12, leaving non-reactive DOC that will be transported13. When deeper wells are drilled for drinking water, the untested assumption is that deep aquifers are protected by either the consumption or adsorption of DOC in shallow aquifers, and that problems associated with shallow DOC can be avoided even as demand increases.

Radiocarbon (14C) dating of inorganic carbon from deep aquifers around the world shows that at depths of 100–1000 m, most groundwater was recharged at the surface by infiltration at least 12,000 years ago14. The shorter-lived tracer tritium (3H), produced by atmospheric testing of nuclear bombs and incorporated into water molecules, traces more recent recharge starting in the 1950s and is typically only detected at depths of <100 m. However, tritium is occasionally found at depths greater than 100 m, particularly in heavily pumped aquifers15–17. Overpumping can drawdown more recent recharge, but it is not clear if it can also impact reactive DOC.

Overpumping can also lead to the compaction of low-permeability clay-rich units. This compaction will cause the release of DOC and/or other constituents, including arsenic, from the clays to the overlying and underlying aquifers. Utilization of carbon expunged from clays has been shown to occur18, and respiration of DOC near clay layers has been shown to produce zones of high groundwater arsenic19,20. High arsenic in deep aquifers has been shown to occur beneath clay layers around the world, including the Mekong Delta in Vietnam21, the Central Valley of California22, and the Bengal Basin in Bangladesh5. It is not known if the clays are the source of DOC, arsenic, or both.

The present study sheds new light on organic carbon dynamics in deep aquifers by quantifying the 14C content of DOC and bacterial RNA in groundwater. RNA is a rapidly recycled component of cellular carbon that records both the 14C content of carbon used by the microbe and the identity of that microbe. Because sediment and advected young carbon have widely different 14C content or ages, this method provides direct evidence for the source of advected and metabolized carbon. Expressing the results in terms of ages, if the carbon in RNA is significantly older than 12,000 years, it would indicate that clays or sediment are the primary source of carbon. An RNA age younger than the sediment indicates the presence of advected DOC. RNA was chosen for analysis over DNA as RNA degrades more easily and is thought to better represent active or recently active microbes23. To date, only a few studies have attempted RNA radiocarbon dating because it is challenging, as it requires over 20,000 liters of water to obtain the required quantities of RNA, and it has caveats related to RNA preservation and extraction24. We developed a novel workflow to address these complications and use it to quantify the fraction of young carbon in DOC and RNA in a deep aquifer that is heavily pumped for the municipal supply of Dhaka, the capital of Bangladesh with a population of 23 million (Fig. 1). Our previous work already established that advected, young DOC controls water quality in shallow aquifers25–27. Results and discussion

Setting

Araihazar upazila, one of about 500 sub-districts in Bangladesh, is a rapidly developing densely populated rural to peri-urban area where the composition of groundwater has been mapped with exceptional detail as part of a two-decade study of the health effects of drinking well water containing arsenic (Fig. 1A)28–30. Within Dhaka itself, the groundwater level in the deep aquifer has declined to more than 80 m below the surface and the resulting cone of depression has expanded 20 km to the east and into Araihazar (Fig. 1A)31–33.

The deep aquifer is threatened in this region by the intrusion of shallow water containing reactive DOC because of the potential for it to cause arsenic contamination. Extensive sampling (>50,000 samples) reveals groundwater arsenic contamination is widespread within the shallow Holocene (<12,000 yr-old) aquifer in Araihazar34, with occasional contamination also observed in vertical clusters within the deep, older aquifer (Fig. 2). A discontinuous low permeability clay/silt paleosol separates the shallow and deep aquifers and is evident by the band of low well density at 30–40 meters in the SE that outcrops to the NW (Fig. 2A). Compiling all the available 3H data from Araihazar, we infer that the fraction of modern groundwater (i.e., groundwater recharged after 1953) decreases with depth, as does the fraction of samples with detectable 3H (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. S5). Importantly, 3H was detected in 6 out of 12 wells at depths of 100–150 m, one sample was detected at a depth of 152.4 m out of 4 wells from 150 to 200 m, and no tritium was detected in the 13 wells below 200 m. This indicates that a small fraction of groundwater in pre-Holocene aquifers deeper than 100 m is most likely modern. Moreover, groundwater flow modeling indicates that a component of young groundwater, on the order of centuries old, could reach the deeper aquifer via flow paths through clay layers32, complicating the radiocarbon dating of groundwater35 that uses Dissolved Inorganic Carbon (DIC)36. Given the presence of clay-rich layers throughout the site (Fig. 2), young tracers, and extensive municipal pumping, dating of RNA will help constrain the sources of reactive DOC in the pre-Holocene aquifer >100 m deep.

RNA radiocarbon

The radiocarbon content of RNA extracted from five wells up to 244 m deep reveals the extent to which shallow organic carbon is transported to and metabolized in deep (>100 m) pre-Holocene aquifers (Supplementary Table S1). The analysis of radiocarbon in RNA requires multi-day filtering of microbes from groundwater onto a 0.1 μm filter, followed by extraction and purification of the RNA for radiocarbon dating24. The data, therefore, represent a bulk average of the carbon utilized by the viable or potentially active23 microbes in the aquifer surrounding a well screen.

The radiocarbon age of microbial RNA from the shallow Holocene aquifer at 19.4 m is 1435 ± 35 yr. Duplicate RNA ages from the shallow pre-Holocene aquifer at 51 m are within error at 980 ± 60 and 880 ± 190 yr (Supplementary Fig. S2). The newly-obtained RNA radiocarbon ages are therefore reproducible and comparable to previous radiocarbon analyses of PLFA and DNA from shallow aquifers in the area (Fig. 3A, B). The ages of the three RNA samples from the pre-Holocene aquifer >100 m deep are 6250 ± 45 yr at 201 m (well DPHE Roynadi K), 5230 ± 70 yr at 238 m (well 24502), and 5550 ± 40 yr at 244 m (well 42401) (Table 1, Figs. 3 and 4). Although sediments were not available for dating at the two deeper intervals, they are very unlikely to be younger. The uncorrected radiocarbon age of dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) for the same three deep wells averages 10,750 ± 1390 yr. The DOC ages at the three wells were younger and were 2000 ± 230 yr at 201 m (well DPHE Roynadi K), 290 ± 750 and 545 ± 150 yr at 238 m (well 24502), and 4510 ± 290 yr at 244 m (well 42401) (Table 1, Figs. 3 and 4). DOC and methane radiocarbon

DOC was radiocarbon dated for 11 samples from 10 additional pre-Holocene wells deeper than 100 m without RNA data (for a total of 15 samples from 13 wells). The DOC ages ranged from 280 to 10,750 years with a median of 2730 years (Figs. 3 and 4). In the pre-Holocene aquifer deeper than 100 m not a single groundwater sample contained DOC older than the uncorrected DIC or of an age similar to that of the sediment. Based on the new data, we hypothesize that DOC advection into the pre-Holocene aquifer to depths >100 m is ubiquitous and that this could be one source of reactive carbon utilized by the microbial community.

Methane, in addition to DOC, can act as a carbon source in reduced environments37 and has recently been recognized to mobilize arsenic through reductive dissolution of Fe oxides38,39. A distinguishing feature of methane is that, unlike DOC, it does not adsorb to aquifer sediment and therefore will be transported at the rate of groundwater flow40. Methane is often present in Bangladesh groundwater4,20,41and concentrations were high enough to enable radiocarbon dating from 10 wells.

In the shallow Holocene aquifer and the shallow pre–Holocene aquifer, the methane ages are similar to both the DIC and DOC (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S3). In the deep pre-Holocene aquifer, 6 samples were collected from 3 wells where enough methane was present for dating. The methane ages range from modern to 5800 yr and is similar to that of DOC (Fig. 3). Given the small sample size and spread of ages there is no statistical difference in methane radiocarbon ages between the aquifers (Fig. 3). The oldest methane samples are found in the pre-Holocene aquifer deeper than 100 m, but at least one modern value is present in all aquifers. In the deep pre-Holocene aquifer the radiocarbon of methane with a Δ14C of +185‰, contains a signature from atmospheric testing of nuclear weapons and had to be formed from carbon present in the atmosphere between ~1960 and 1990. Metagenomic and metatranscriptomic analysis of the DNA and RNA shows that methane cycling is probably occurring within the deep aquifer (Supplementary Table S3, Supplementary Fig. S7). Given the molecular data and radiocarbon signature of methane we hypothesize that methane could therefore also be a source of reactive carbon utilized by the microbial community in pre-Holocene aquifers >100 m.

Given the difference in the radiocarbon signatures of the DIC and the methane in the pre-Holocene aquifer >100 m, the DIC or DOC from clays could not be a source of carbon for the methane. Therefore, methane in the pre-Holocene aquifer deeper than 100 m represents either utilization of advected DOC or utilization of DOC and/or CO2 in the shallow aquifer followed by transport of methane from the shallow aquifer into the deep aquifer. Eight of the nine samples with both δ13C of DIC and CH4 have an αδ13CCO2-CH4 greater than 1.06 indicating reduction of CO2, but acetoclastic methanogenesis cannot be excluded (Supplementary Fig. S6). Taken together, the data indicate that the methane must be produced from either DOC that is transported into the deep pre-Holocene aquifer >100 m or produced in the shallow aquifer and advected into the deep aquifer. For either mechanism, both vertical recharge and advection are required.

To interpret the new observations, we constructed a simple conservative mixing model to constrain the various possible sources of reactive carbon for the RNA in the pre-Holocene aquifer >100 m. We assume DIC is not an important source of carbon for microbial metabolisms. Genes associated with autotrophy are present but in low concentrations in the metatranscriptome (Supplementary Fig. S7) and including DIC as it is older than the RNA only increases the importance of advection. The mixing model, therefore, represents RNA as a mixture sourced from a combination of sedimentary organic carbon, which would include carbon from clay layers (fraction SOC) and either DOC or methane (fraction advected). If the RNA is from a mixture of SOC and advected carbon and the fraction advected decreases (fraction SOC increases), the age of the fraction advected needs to become younger to balance the fixed SOC endmember to reproduce the observed RNA ages (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. S8). The model shows that within 25% of the median age for DOC and methane, the RNA contains between 62 and 95% advected carbon (Fig. 4). At mixtures with less than 40% advected carbon (greater than 60% sedimentary carbon), the required radiocarbon signature of the advected carbon becomes unrealistically modern (Fig. 4). Regardless of whether deep aquifer microbes use purely advected carbon or a mixture of advected and sedimentary sourced carbon, the data show that advected carbon is a significant fraction of microbial RNA and carbon from clays or the sediment is not the sole or even a dominant source of carbon for the RNA.

On the basis of the new RNA radiocarbon data, we argue that methane and/or DOC are advected into the pre-Holocene aquifer >100 m deep and either one or both sustain over half of the microbial metabolism in this reducing aquifer. The findings imply some level of protection of sedimentary carbon and is consistent with large river deltas being carbon sinks over geologic time-scales42,43. The radiocarbon ages of the DIC in pre-Holocene aquifer >100 m deep, both uncorrected and corrected for carbonate dissolution, are closest to the RNA ages but older than the DOC, and CH4 ages (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S3). Radiocarbon DIC ages, widely used as an indicator of aquifer recharge14,16, evidently don’t reflect flowpaths and mixing that are relevant to carbon metabolism in these deep aquifers. Methane is conservatively transported in groundwater40 but is not suitable for estimating groundwater age given that it is both produced and consumed in situ. In low organic carbon, carbonate and sandstone aquifers, DOC has been proposed as an alternative measure of aquifer recharge44,45 but the underlying hypothesis of conservative transport does not seem to apply to deep aquifers of the Bengal Basin. The wide range of radiocarbon ages measured across tracers is consistent with a complex reactive flow system. This flow system recharging the pre-Holocene aquifer >100 m deep could be occurring through both conductive strata32 along with, possibly, flow within and along well casings46. Independent of the pathway, this groundwater flow supplies advected carbon to deep aquifers that is incorporated into RNA.

Well-water arsenic data from our study area suggest that deep advection of reactive carbon could have implications for water quality. The cross-section of arsenic concentrations highlight vertical clusters of elevated concentrations penetrating the pre-Holocene aquifer (Fig. 2). Some of these patterns have been tracked over time and linked to deep pumping for the municipal water supply of nearby Dhaka19,20,32. The new RNA data indicate that reductive dissolution of Fe oxides, and therefore release of arsenic, in pre-Holocene aquifers is attributable at least in part to advected carbon and not solely sedimentary organic carbon from the compression of clays. To what extent this mechanism applies to other deep aquifers including deltaic regions that are not overpumped is presently not known yet critical for gauging the vulnerability of drinking water supplies.

Methods

RNA radiocarbon

Five wells were sampled for radiocarbon of RNA (Supplementary Table S1). Groundwater was filtered at 1–5 gpm onto a 0.1-µm nylon 10-inch filter cartridge (Filtersource, Hamburg, NY). The filters were immediately frozen, shipped on dry ice, and stored at −80 °C until processing. The complete RNA extraction and purification procedure is presented in Mailloux et al.24. Briefly, the filters are subsectioned, extracted in a CTAB-based buffer and purified using phenol:chloroform and the DNA and RNA are separated with the LiCl. The DNA is used for metagenomic analysis and the RNA is used for metatranscriptomic analysis and radiocarbon dating47. Radiocarbon analyses of RNA and DNA were done at the Center for Accelerator Mass Spectrometry at Lawrence Livermore National Lab.

Methane radiocarbon

Headspace samples were collected for radiocarbon dating of methane by degassing groundwater with a spraying device within a glass flow-through vessel at a rate of ~1 L/min for 10–30 min between December 2016 and January 2018. Headspace samples were transferred with a 50 mL syringe to IsoBag® gas bags (Isotech Laboratories, Inc., Champaign, IL). Within a month of collection, headspace samples were analyzed and the subset containing 1–50% methane was processed by Isotech for radiocarbon analysis by accelerator mass spectrometry.

Other radiocarbon analyses

Groundwater samples for radiocarbon (14C/12C) and carbon stable isotopic (13C/12C) measurements in DIC and DOC were collected using a submersible pump in 125 mL or larger glass bottles with Polyseal caps. Samples for DIC radiocarbon were immediately fixed with 0.1 mL of saturated mercuric chloride solution. Samples for DOC radiocarbon were stabilized by acidifying to 0.1% hydrochloric acid. All carbon isotopic measurements were performed at the National Ocean Science Accelerator Mass-Spectrometer (NOSAMS) facility at the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution following standard protocols and we utilize the data as reported48. Some of the tritium, DIC and DOC radiocarbon measurements were previously reported19,20,36,49.

Groundwater arsenic and well testing

The set of almost 50,000 well tests for Arsenic from Araihazar was obtained in 2012-13 using the ITS Econo-Quick field kit. The kit overestimates Arsenic concentrations by a factor of two around 50 ug/L on average, but very effectively distinguishes samples containing ≤10 and ≥100 ug/L Arsenic34,50. Leak tests were performed on one of the three wells sampled for RNA and no leak was found (Supplementary Fig. S1).
