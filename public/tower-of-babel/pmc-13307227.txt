Combined Arthroscopic Acetabular Labral Reconstruction With Dermal Allograft Hip Capsular Reconstruction
Authors: Jacob Waldron, Justin Harrington, Raymond Kenney, Brian Giordano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHip instability after arthroscopy is a rare but challenging complication which can result from iatrogenic capsular deficiency and labral insufficiency. When accompanied by acetabular undercoverage, this combination compromises both the static and dynamic stabilizers of the hip joint, predisposing patients to pain, dysfunction, and progressive joint degeneration. Open reconstruction of these structures can be morbid, particularly in cases requiring a staged periacetabular osteotomy. This report describes an all‐arthroscopic technique for simultaneous acetabular labral and hip capsular reconstruction in a patient with recurrent instability after prior arthroscopy. The labrum is reconstructed with a posterior tibialis tendon allograft, whereas a dermal allograft is used to restore the deficient anterior capsule using knotless suture anchor fixation. The technique restores both the suction seal and capsular integrity before a subsequent staged retroverting periacetabular osteotomy, allowing sequential management of soft‐tissue and osseous pathology while minimizing morbidity. This combined arthroscopic approach provides a reproducible, minimally invasive method to address soft‐tissue instability in patients with labral and capsular insufficiency, optimizing conditions for a later corrective periacetabular osteotomy and potentially delaying or preventing degenerative progression in young, active patients.

Hip instability after hip arthroscopy represents a rare but serious complication, particularly in young, active patients. Injudicious capsular management has been implicated as a potential cause of iatrogenic instability, leading to further pain and dysfunction. 1 , 2 , 3 When compounded by labral insufficiency, both the suction seal and static restraint of the joint are affected, predisposing patients to repeated instability events, pain, and progressive joint degeneration. 4 Revision arthroscopy with labral repair or reconstruction may not reliably restore stability in these complex cases, potentially necessitating more aggressive containment options such as periacetabular osteotomy (PAO). Open surgery carries the risk of increased complications especially if planning for a staged PAO. 5 Arthroscopic techniques that address both capsular and labral insufficiency simultaneously provide an opportunity to restore soft tissue stability and function while minimizing potential complications prior to undergoing a PAO. We describe an arthroscopic technique for addressing capsular and labral insufficiency in a patient with recurrent iatrogenic instability prior to undergoing a staged PAO.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Patient Positioning and Initial Portal Sites

The patient is positioned supine on a postless hip distraction table with the feet in well‐padded boots (Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN). After sterile draping, the procedure is initiated by creating anterolateral and midanterior (MA) portals.

Diagnostic Arthroscopy

Diagnostic arthroscopy is first completed to assess the status of the capsule, labrum, and cartilage. Absence of viable capsule at the anterior aspect of the hip joint is visualized (Figure 1). The cartilage of the acetabulum and femoral head are then inspected, with evidence of grade I and II chondral wear noted in the acetabulum at zones 2, 3, and 4 and in the posterior superior femoral head. The acetabular labrum was noted to be completely deficient from 1 o'clock to 4 o'clock and irreparably degenerative and ossified from 9 to 1 o'clock.

Arthroscopic view of the right hip viewing through the anterolateral portal showing deficiency of the anterior capsule and labrum, with direct view of the iliopsoas tendon sheath (labeled *). In this image, proximal is left and distal is right.

Loose Body Removal, Rim Preparation, Debridement, and Additional Portal Creation

The loose body noted on the computed tomography scan from prior dislocations was then identified medial to the psoas notch. With the combination of a radiofrequency wand and mechanical shaver, the loose body is freed from surrounding scar tissue and retrieved with an arthroscopic grasper (Figures 2 and 3).

CT scan of the right hemipelvis and acetabulum with 3D reconstruction showing a bony loose body (blue circle) from recurrent dislocations adjacent to the anterior medial acetabulum at the psoas notch. In this image, anterior is to the right of the image and posterior is to the left. (3D, 3‐dimensional; CT, computed tomography.)

Intraoperative arthroscopic image of the right hip viewing through the anterolateral portal showing removal of the loose body (as depicted in Figure 2, again represented here with a blue circle) with an arthroscopic grasper. In this image, proximal is to the top left corner and distal is to the bottom right corner.

Pathologic capsulolabral adhesions are debrided to facilitate rim preparation and visualization. The recess between the intact remnant anterior superior capsular stump and anterior acetabular rim is fully exposed and prepared with a 5.0 mm burr for a double row of suture anchors to be used for both the capsular and labral reconstruction. The joint is then thoroughly debrided of all anchors and suture material from the prior surgery.

Next, posterolateral, direct anterior, and distal anterolateral accessory portals are created to assist with graft passage, suture management, and anchor placement (Figure 4).

External view of the right hip with all arthroscopic portals used for the procedure as labelled. In this image, the patient is supine with the proximal being to the left of the image and the distal being to the right of the image. (DALA, distal anterolateral accessory.)

Revision Femoroplasty, Measurement of Capsular Defect, and Preparation of Capsular Graft

Traction is released and attention is turned to the femoral head neck junction where there is noted evidence of incomplete femoroplasty. With the use of a radiofrequency wand and 5.0 mm burr, a revision femoroplasty is performed under multiplanar fluoroscopic guidance. During the revision femoroplasty, the assistant prepares the labral reconstruction allograft. In this case, we used a 5 mm posterior tibialis tendon allograft (Figure 5). The tendon is prepared with a looped suture (Fiber Loop, Arthrex, Naples, FL) at one end to create a running looped traction stitch which will be used to shuttle the graft out of the posterolateral portal and assist with graft tension.

Posterior tibial tendon allograft (outlined by red rectangle) used for labral reconstruction with a looped suture through 1 of the free ends (labeled with solid black arrow). The looped suture end is later shuttled thorough a posterolateral portal and used for tensioning of the graft as it is sequentially secured from anterior to posterior.

Once the revision femoroplasty is complete, the area of the capsular defect is measured with the use of a flexible arthroscopic measurement probe (Arthrex, Naples, FL) (Figure 6).

Intraoperative arthroscopic image of the right hip viewing through the anterolateral portal showing use of an arthroscopic measurement probe (labeled *) on the exposed area of the proximal femoral neck to measure the dimensions of capsular deficiency.

A 2‐mm‐thick dermal allograft (ArthroFLEX, Arthrex, Naples, FL) is prepared for capsular reconstruction. The graft is cut to the appropriate size in accordance with the previously measured capsular defect, which measured 4 × 4 cm (Figure 7).

Dermal allograft tissue for capsular reconstruction is marked with a sterile surgical marker (as depicted with blue dots) at the appropriate dimensions which were previously measured arthroscopically (as shown in Figure 6). The graft is then trimmed to the desired size by cutting between the blue dots with a scalpel or sterile scissors.

Prepare Anchor Points and Place Sutures for Labral and Capsular Reconstruction

The operative extremity is again placed into traction to prepare for the labral reconstruction. First, two 3.0 mm knotless suture anchors (SutureTak, Arthrex, Naples, FL) are placed in close proximity on the acetabular rim directly anterior to the transverse acetabular ligament. These 2 adjacent anchors allow for secure fixation of the anterior free edge of the graft and immediate backup fixation. Next, a third anchor is placed posteriorly adjacent to the remaining healthy posterior labral tissue.

Next, three 2.4 mm knotless suture anchors (SutureTak, Arthrex, Naples, FL) are placed into a second row along the acetabular rim to serve as anchor points for the capsular reconstruction and shuttled out of the anterior portal. These suture anchors are placed at anterior inferior, central, and superior locations just medial/ proximal to the row of anchors that will be used for the labral reconstruction (Figure 8).

Sawbones model depicting the right acetabulum and pelvis. In this image, left is proximal and right is distal. Red circles and arrows depict our location for the three 2.4 mm SutureTak suture anchors used for capsular reconstruction. Blue circles and arrows depict our location for the 3 initial 3.0 mm SutureTak suture anchors used for the anterior and posterior labral reconstruction attachment points.

Alternatively, 1.8 mm all suture anchors (FiberTak, Arthrex, Naples, FL) may be used which feature a similar knotless mechanism but with a smaller footprint.

Labral Reconstruction

With the above preparatory work completed, we next proceeded to labral reconstruction. The anteroinferior most anchor sutures are retrieved out of the MA portal cannula, and the repair stitch is then passed through the anterior leading edge of the labral allograft using a free needle. The opposite free end of the graft is shuttled into the MA portal and retrieved out of the posterolateral portal using the traction sutures previously placed. The anterior edge of the graft is then pulled into the joint with a combination of the “knotless‐pull‐through” mechanism of the suture and countertraction using the free suture ends from the posterolateral portal. The repair suture from the next anterior inferior labral anchor is then passed around the graft and secured with the knotless mechanism. With the graft now well fixated anteriorly, subsequent suture anchors are placed at approximately 1 cm increments working anterior to posterior until the labral graft is fully secured to the acetabular rim. A probe is used to reduce the graft to the acetabular rim before final tightening of each suture anchor. Finally, the preplaced posteroinferior most anchor is used to secure the graft at its last point directly adjacent to the native healthy labrum. The graft is amputated with the use of a radiofrequency wand once the labral reconstruction is complete.

Traction is then removed and restoration of the suction seal of the labrum against the femoral head is confirmed with direct visualization (Figure 9). Appropriate hip reduction and congruency is confirmed with fluoroscopy.

Arthroscopic image viewing through the midanterior portal following revision femoroplasty after traction is let down and the hip joint is reduced, showing satisfactory return of the suction seal and a functioning labral reconstruction. The completed labral reconstruction is noted with the 5 * labels. In this image, proximal is superior in the image, distal is inferior, medial is right, and lateral is left.

Capsular Reconstruction

Before proceeding with capsular reconstruction, it is crucial to gain adequate pericapsular exposure to assess the extent of capsular attenuation or retraction and develop robust capsular leaflets. The repair suture of each of the 3 capsular anchors previously placed into the acetabular rim are retrieved sequentially through the MA portal cannula and separated externally in the direction of their corresponding location to ensure they do not become tangled.

The cannula is then removed and each repair suture is passed through the dermal allograft with a Scorpion suture shuttling device (Arthrex, Naples, FL) in a horizontal mattress configuration with care taken to pass the sutures with the same spacing of the capsular acetabular anchors. Each repair stitch is then sequentially passed through the knotless mechanism to their respective anchors and provisionally tightened to bring the medial edge of the graft close to the MA portal. A luggage tag suture is placed at the distal end of the graft to facilitate positioning and tensioning once the graft is passed into the capsular space. An arthroscopic portal skid (Arthrex, Naples, FL) is then introduced into the MA portal to facilitate graft passage (Figure 10).

The graft is carefully rolled and introduced into the joint by sequentially tensioning each knotless suture anchor in a pull‐through manner, while simultaneously guiding the folded graft into the capsular space with a tissue grasper. Once inside the joint, the graft is gently unfolded using a blunt arthroscopic grasper, and countertraction on the distal luggage‐tag suture is used to aid in precise positioning within the capsule (Figure 11). Once in position, final tightening of each suture anchor is performed, with the final position as depicted on the sawbones model shown in Figure 12.

A blunt suture grasper (labeled with a blue oval) can be seen above the graft which was used to help unfold and position the graft in the joint.

The distal free end of the graft is then repaired to the distal stump of remaining native capsule with nonabsorbable suture using a variety of arthroscopic capsule closure devices including a shoulder Scorpion, CapsuleStitch suture passer, and CapsuleClose Scorpion (Arthrex, Naples, FL).

Periacetabular Osteotomy

Following arthroscopic intervention, the patient subsequently underwent a staged retroverting PAO 3 weeks later using standard osteotomy sites in the ilium, ischium and pubis as first popularized and described by Ganz. 6 In this patient's case, the primary bony deficiency involved undercoverage of the anterior wall of the acetabulum, with recurrent anterior instability. A retroverting PAO was performed to restore anterior coverage of the femoral head and prevent recurrent anterior instability (Figures 13 and 14, Video 1, and also Figure 15 for an overview of the technique).

DISCUSSION

Recurrent instability after failed hip arthroscopy is often multifactorial, involving capsular deficiency, labral insufficiency, and potential underlying osseous abnormalities such as acetabular undercoverage. 1 , 7 Management is technically demanding and requires careful preoperative planning. Although open capsular reconstruction has been described in patients requiring staged PAO, limiting additional open dissection may reduce blood loss, heterotopic ossification, and scar formation. 5 , 8

Repetitive shear forces in unstable hips can accelerate labral degeneration and leave insufficient tissue for repair. 4 , 9 Labral reconstruction restores the suction seal critical for hip stability. 9 , 10 Various graft options have been reported for labral and capsular reconstruction, with arthroscopic approaches showing reliable restoration of stability. 2 , 3 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Both segmental and circumferential labral reconstructions have shown favorable outcomes. 9 , 15 , 16 Performing both reconstructions arthroscopically minimizes morbidity, avoids staged surgery, and enables precise graft tensioning. Advantages and Disadvantages

In the setting of acetabular undercoverage, soft‐tissue procedures alone may be insufficient to restore native hip stability. Reorientation pelvic osteotomy can restore coverage, and reduce translational shear forces, which may exert a protective influence on labral repair or reconstruction. 17 Both staged and combined arthroscopy and PAO have shown durable functional improvements and mid to long‐term survivorship. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23

A staged strategy with arthroscopic labral and capsular reconstruction followed by PAO offers young patients with recurrent instability and preserved cartilage the best opportunity for durable stability and joint preservation, potentially delaying or preventing arthroplasty in this complex, yet potentially salvageable, population.
