Effects of external radiation dose on pediatric peripheral blood components after the Great East Japan Earthquake: Fukushima Health Management Survey
Authors: Koichi Hashimoto, Hironori Nakano, Akira Sakai, Michio Shimabukuro, Junichiro James Kazama, Tetsuya Ohira, Tetsuo Ishikawa, Masanori Nagao, Kanako Okazaki, Shiho Sato, Seiji Yasumura, Hitoshi Ohto, Mitsuaki Hosoya, Hayato Go
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Great East Japan Earthquake (GEJE) caused an accident at the Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant, resulting in widespread dispersion of radioactive materials across the Fukushima Prefecture. We aimed to analyze the effects of external radiation exposure after the GEJE on peripheral blood components in children living in the evacuation area. We analyzed 27,844 children aged ≤ 15 years, including residents of evacuation areas and infants aged < 1 year born to parents residing in these areas. All participants underwent comprehensive health checks between 2011 and 2017, with available data on effective radiation dose due to external exposure (EDEE) during the first 4 months post-GEJE. Participants were categorized by age as: <1, 1–4, 5–10, and 11–15 years. The mean (standard deviation) of EDEE was 0.8 (0.7) mSv, ranging from 0.1 to 8.8 mSv in males and 0.1–8.9 mSv in females. When stratified into groups with mean EDEE < 1 mSv and ≥ 1 mSv, peripheral blood parameters remained within reference intervals, and no significant changes over time were observed across age groups. Additionally, the upper and lower 5% of white blood cell counts did not correlate with EDEE, including neutrophils and lymphocytes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47787-x.

Introduction

Following the Great East Japan Earthquake (GEJE) that occurred on March 11, 2011, the Fukushima Health Management Survey (FHMS) was initiated in Fukushima Prefecture to monitor the health of its residents1. Additionally, because of the widespread dispersion of radioactive substances across Fukushima Prefecture following the accident at the Tokyo Electric Power Company’s Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant (FDNPP), the Basic Survey was conducted as one component of the FHMS to assess the radiation exposure status of Fukushima residents. Following the disaster, evacuation areas were designated by the national government. The Comprehensive Health Check (CHC) was conducted for all residents of the evacuation areas at the time of the GEJE, including their children. During the CHC, children aged 0–6 years had their height and weight measured, and blood cell counts were measured only in those who consented to participate. Children aged 7–15 years had their height and weight measured and underwent blood tests for blood cell counts and blood biochemical tests, if desired1.

From 2011 to 2018, the authors reported changes in peripheral blood cell components in children aged ≤ 15 years residing in the evacuation area at the time of the GEJE and post-disaster, as well as in children born to residents of the evacuation area2. Although the effects of the effective radiation dose due to external exposure (EDEE) were not examined in previous studies, there was no significant impact on children’s peripheral blood, including radiation exposure from the FDNPP accident3. In an age- and sex-adjusted model further adjusted for evacuation status and lifestyle-related factors in 54,087 individuals aged 16–84 years, no association was found between EDEE and the incidence of thrombocytopenia, lymphocytopenia, or neutropenia from 2011 to 20174. Additionally, no effect of EDEE on peripheral blood cell counts or white blood cell (WBC) fractions was observed among 16–18-year-old residents of evacuation areas from 2011 to 20174. Furthermore, EDEE due to FDNPP is not associated with congenital anomalies, low birth weight, small for gestational age, or preterm birth5. However, the United Nations Scientific Committee on the Effects of Atomic Radiation (UNSCEAR) stated in its report submitted to the United Nations General Assembly on the levels and effects of radiation exposure from the FDNPP accident that because of the low estimated doses to the child and young adult populations, excess cases of leukemia attributable to radiation exposure are generally not discernible. Nevertheless, the report also noted that a possibility of discernible risk exists among those aged ≤ 5 years at the time of the accident6. Therefore, we aimed to analyze the effects of EDEE after the GEJE on peripheral blood components in children.

Methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fukushima Medical University (approval number: 1319). Informed consent for CHC data collection was obtained from the parents (legal guardians) of all children aged ≤ 15 years who participated in this study. Details of the CHCs have been described in a previous study. This study was performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration and the Japanese Ethical Guidelines; the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects.

Study population

The study population consisted of residents aged ≤ 15 years who lived in municipalities, including evacuation areas (study areas), during the period from the occurrence of the nuclear accident to the evacuation (study period); underwent voluntary CHCs, including blood tests; participated in the Basic Survey; and had EDEE data.

The participants in the AGE_0 group were infants aged < 1 year residing in the study area or were born to parents residing in the study area during the study period. Some participants may have moved from other areas to the study area during the study period; however, no such information was available, owing to there is a possibility of short-term relocation, and because residency data are based on individual applications, the available data does not cover these detailed movements. Evacuation areas were initially defined based on distance from the nuclear power plant and were later revised by incorporating measured radiation dose levels. The evacuation areas included Tamura City, Minamisoma City, Kawamata Town, Hirono Town, Naraha Town, Tomioka Town, Kawauchi Village, Okuma Town, Futaba Town, Namie Town, Katsurao Village, Iitate Village, and part of Date City.

Estimation of external exposure doses

External radiation exposure was estimated as EDEE for individuals registered as residents of Fukushima Prefecture from March 11, 2011, to July 1, 2011. Details of the EDEE estimations are provided separately7. Briefly, a self-administered questionnaire was distributed to the residents, asking them to record their activities (such as time spent indoors and outdoors and timing of movements) during the 4 months post-GEJE on March 11, 2011, and return the completed questionnaire. The respondents’ behavioral records were digitized, and individual EDEE values were calculated using a computer program that overlaid the behavioral records with daily ambient dose-rate maps. The daily ambient dose-rate maps were constructed based on two types of data: hourly effective dose rate maps based on simulation and radiation monitoring data8. The former type was used for constructing the dose-rate maps for the period from March 11 to March 14, 2011, when the monitoring data were not available. The latter type was used for constructing the maps for the rest of dose estimation period (i.e., from March 15 to July 11). The monitoring data on ambient dose rates were taken with hand-held equipment by governmental or prefectural staff. The ambient dose rates were not necessarily measured in a consistent manner, because no preparations had been made for such nuclear accident. In general, however, it seemed that ambient dose rates were supposed to be measured at around 1 m height from the ground using NaI (Tl) survey meters.

Evaluation items

The children were classified into age groups based on their age at the time of the CHC survey, according to previous studies. To ensure sufficient sample sizes for analysis, children aged < 5 years were grouped, regardless of sex. Additionally, based on the average and median EDEE values of the study population, the subjects were classified into two groups according to their individual exposure doses: <1 mSv and ≥ 1 mSv. We analyzed temporal changes in peripheral blood WBC and hemoglobin (Hb) values and platelet (PLT) counts from 2011 to 2017. Each year was defined as a fiscal year from April to March. WBC counts and fractions were examined, and Hb values were used as indicators of anemia and polycythemia. Anemia was defined as an Hb level of ≤ 12 g/dL and polycythemia as an Hb level of ≥ 18 g/dL for both sexes. WBC counts of ≥ 11,100/µL and ≤ 2,900/µL were considered high and low, respectively. PLT counts of ≥ 450,000/µL and ≤ 89,000/µL were considered high and low, respectively. The reference intervals for pediatric test values vary depending on age9–12; therefore, they were divided into four age groups: <1 year (AGE_0), 1–4 years (AGE_1–4), 5–10 years (AGE_ 5–10), and 11–15 years (AGE_11–15). The mean values of the EDEE for the lower or upper 5% and the central 90% of WBC counts, PLT counts, and Hb values for each age group in each year were compared.

Reference values

The mean values and reference intervals used for each age group were based on peer-reviewed papers and textbooks9–12. The mean values and reference intervals for peripheral blood in pediatric patients change with age. Because there were no reference intervals for specific age groups in this study, we adopted the reference intervals of the age groups closest to the study age groups.

Statistical analysis

Clinical laboratory values by year, sex, and age group are presented as mean values and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) of the distribution of clinical laboratory values (2.5th and 97.5th percentiles). Differences in the mean values between the EDEE groups were analyzed using an unpaired t-test. Differences in proportions were analyzed using a generalized linear mixed model, with 2011 as the control. However, since the CHC survey began in January 2012, FY2011 was conducted from January to the end of March 2012. The mean EDEE values in the lower or upper 5% of WBC count, PLT count, and Hb value were compared with those in the central 90% of these blood cell components and analyzed using an unpaired t-test. Furthermore, to account for multiple testing, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Adjustments were performed separately for each comparison type (low vs. middle and high vs. middle). All statistical analyses were performed using SAS Version 9.3 (SAS Institute). Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Number of participants

This study included 27,844 participants. The number of people with EDEE values who underwent CHCs at least once between 2011 and 2017 was 8559, of whom 7523 (87.9%) underwent CHC in 2011. Among children who underwent blood tests, the percentage of children with available EDEE data was 42–45% in the first four years but decreased to 33.8% in 2017 (Table 1).

EDEE of the participants

In 2011, EDEE was < 1 mSv, ≥ 1 mSv, < 2 mSv, < 3 mSv, and < 5 mSv in 69.6%, 30.4%, 84.3%, 98.1%, and 99.7% of the total population aged 0–15 years, respectively. EDEE was < 2 mSv, < 3 mSv, and < 5 mSv in 94.9%, 98.1%, and 99.5% of those aged < 5 years, respectively. Among those aged ≥ 5 years, 94.1%, 98.1%, and 99.7% had EDEE of < 2 mSv, < 3 mSv, and < 5 mSv, respectively (Fig. 1).

WBC counts over time

Regarding total WBC counts, in the AGE_1–4 group, the mean values for WBC counts were slightly lower in the ≥ 1 mSv group than in the < 1 mSv group in 2012 (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 1). However, there were no age groups with significant differences in mean values between the < 1 mSv and ≥ 1 mSv in the age groups up to 4 years (Supplementary Table 2). In the AGE_5–10 group, the mean values for WBC counts were slightly lower in the ≥ 1 mSv group than in the < 1 mSv group, in 2012 for males, and in 2016 and 2017 for females. Additionally, in the AGE_11–15 groups, females in the ≥ 1 mSv group had slightly lower average values than those in the < 1 mSv group in 2012. However, all the average and 95th percentile values of each test were within the reference interval (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1).

Next, we analyzed the changes in the percentages of high and low WBC counts over time using 2011 as the baseline. In the AGE_1–4 group, the percentage of high WBC counts decreased in the < 1 mSv group in 2015 and in the ≥ 1 mSv group in 2012; however, there was no increase in the percentage of low WBC counts. In the AGE_5–10 group, the percentage of high WBC counts decreased in 2016 and 2017 among males in the < 1 mSv group, and no increase in the percentage of low WBC counts was observed. In the same age group, among females, the percentage of high WBC counts decreased in the < 1 mSv group from 2012 to 2017 and in the ≥ 1 mSv group in 2016; however, no increase was observed in the percentage of low WBC counts. In the AGE_11–15 group, the percentage of high WBC counts increased among females in the < 1 mSv group in 2013, whereas no such increase was observed among males. Furthermore, among females in the ≥ 1 mSv group, the percentage of low WBC counts decreased in 2016, whereas no increase in the percentage of high WBC counts was observed (Table 3).

Discussion

External and internal radiation exposure in Fukushima

As of March 31, 2024, the response rate for the behavioral records used to estimate EDEE in the basic survey of the FHMS studying all residents of Fukushima Prefecture was approximately 28%13. As of June 30, 2014, the average EDEE value for all ages (421,394 residents, excluding radiation workers) was 0.8 mSv, with a maximum value of 25 mSv. Additionally, 91.6% of residents aged 0–9 years and 94.0% of those aged 10–19 years had EDEE values of < 2 mSv. Furthermore, 99.61% of those aged 0–9 years and 99.63% of those aged 10–19 years had EDEE values of < 3 mSv, and 99.97% of those aged 0–9 years and 99.95% of those aged 10–19 years had EDEE values of < 5 mSv. The maximum dose over the 4 months was within the range of 10–11 mSv for the 0–9 and 10–19-year age groups14.

As part of the FHMS, internal effective dose due to Cs-134 and Cs-137 was measured using whole-body counters from July 11, 2011, to January 31, 2012, for 9,927 residents in the warning and planned evacuation areas. Details of the measurements are provided elsewhere15. In short, two types of whole-body counters were used for the measurements: one with stand-up geometry, and the other with chair geometry. Each type has two large-sized NaI(Tl) detectors with steel shielding of 10 cm thickness for the stand-up geometry or with lead shielding of 5 cm thickness for the chair geometry. Counting time per person was 2 min (stand-up type) or 3 min (chair-type). Of these, 6,799 were under 17 years of age. The estimated internal effective dose from Cs-134 and Cs-137 for 99.7% of those under 17 years of age was < 1 mSv, with a median value of 0.02 mSv for those aged 13–17 years15.

Generally, radiation exposure in daily life includes external exposure to natural radiation from space and the earth and internal exposure to natural radioactive substances such as radon progeny in air and potassium-40 in food16. Combining external and internal exposures, the annual global average is reportedly 2.4 mSv, and the average in Japan is 2.1 mSv. By extrapolating the EDEE value for the 4 months in the Basic Survey after GEJE to an annual dose, the first-year municipality-average EDEEs were estimated to be < 2 mSv for most municipalities in non-evacuated areas7. The maximum municipality-average effective dose (sum of internal and external doses) in areas outside the evacuation area in Fukushima Prefecture over a long period (10 years) following the FDNPP accident was estimated to be 14 mSv for infants aged 1 year, 12 mSv for children aged 10 years, and 11 mSv for adults17. Here, the municipality-average effective dose included internal effective dose from iodine as well as cesium. The settlement-average effective dose shown below is the same. As for the evacuated areas, the maximum settlement-average effective doses for evacuees in the first year after the FDNPP accident, as estimated by UNSCEAR, were 7.8 mSv for infants, 6.5 mSv for 10-year-olds, and 5.5 mSv for adults. The evacuees moved outside of the evacuation areas within several months after the accident. Consequently, when comparing the doses for a long period (more than 1 year), the internal and external effective doses due to the FDNPP accident were almost equivalent to or less than the typical effective doses due to natural radiation in Japan and worldwide. The 2008 UNSCEAR report stated that for low-dose exposure not exceeding 100 mSv per year, no adverse health effects on humans, including cancer, have been confirmed18. However, the UNSCEAR 2020/2021 report expressed concerns about the possibility of risk among individuals who were ≤ 5 years old at the time of the FDNPP accident6. Although lymphocytes are known to be highly radiosensitive among blood cell components, this characteristic primarily reflects responses to acute or high-dose radiation exposure; the threshold for lymphocyte reduction is 0.5 Gy (500 mSv)20, and lymphocyte counts decrease rapidly after radiation exposure but recover unless exposure persists at the same level20. In the context of chronic low-dose exposure, such as that examined in the present study, direct cytotoxic effects on circulating lymphocytes are not expected. Instead, potential hematologic effects are more appropriately considered in relation to radiation-induced damage to hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells upstream of mature blood cell lineages. Furthermore, radiation-induced bone marrow stem cell damage has been implicated in the development of myelodysplastic syndrome several years after exposure, as reported in atomic bomb survivors in Nagasaki22. However, estimated effective doses among evacuees in the Fukushima Prefecture were substantially lower than those experienced by atomic bomb survivors. In line with these considerations, our study did not identify a sustained or clinically meaningful reduction in the lymphocyte counts among children exposed to chronic low-dose radiation after the FDNPP accident, including those aged ≤ 5 years at the time of exposure, within the dose ranges assessed.

Relationship between external radiation dose and pediatric peripheral blood components

In this analysis, based on the average (0.8mSv) and median (0.6mSv) EDEE values, the cutoff value was set slightly higher at 1 mSv, and the subjects were classified into two groups according to their individual exposure doses: <1 mSv and ≥ 1 mSv. Under these conditions, the analysis revealed differences in the mean values of blood cell components between the EDEE groups in some analysis groups. However, these differences were minimal, and the average values and 95% CI for each test value were almost within the reference interval. Additionally, no significant changes in annual cell count trends were observed across the dose groups. Furthermore, there were a few groups with significant differences in the mean EDEE values between the lower or upper 5% and the middle 90% of total WBC, neutrophil, lymphocyte, and PLT counts and Hb levels by unpaired t-test. However, after FDR adjustment, the number of groups showing significant differences decreased, and the mean EDEE of all groups with significant differences was lower than the EDEE of the central 90%.

Health effects of changes in living environment

Although individual radiation exposure doses were not incorporated into the analyses, previous studies have reported health effects related to evacuation-associated changes in the living environment. After the Great East Japan Earthquake and the subsequent nuclear power plant accident, residents of the Fukushima Prefecture were forced into unanticipated evacuation, resulting in marked changes in the living environment, lifestyle, and dietary habits, together with long-term psychosocial stress and anxiety related to radiation exposure.

Analyses based on CHC data have shown that evacuation is associated with increased risks of obesity23, hypertension24,25, diabetes26,27, dyslipidemia28, metabolic syndrome29, kidney disease30, liver dysfunction31, polycythemia32,33, and hyperuricemia34,35 in adults. Although evidence in children is limited, but studies have reported a high prevalence of obesity and hyperlipidemia among children aged 7–15 years living in evacuation areas in 2011, with subsequent improvement over time36; moreover, an increase in overweight after the disaster was observed in separate analyses of health checkup data in early childhood (aged 1–3 years)37. However, evacuation-related increases in polycythemia observed in adults were not observed in children3.

Strengths and limitations

The most distinctive strength of this study is that it took into account post-GEJE radiation exposure doses, in addition to those reported previously. On the other hand, this study has some limitations. The behavioral survey conducted for estimating EDEE in the basic survey studying all residents of Fukushima Prefecture and the CHCs for residents in evacuation areas was voluntary, suggesting that the analysis population may be biased. Furthermore, the condition and health status of the participants at the time of blood collection were unknown, and the measured values obtained from the CHC may have contained unadjusted biases.

In conclusion, among residents aged ≤ 15 years, including aged ≤ 5 years as UNSCEAR expressed concern, in the evacuation area who underwent CHC with peripheral blood tests, between 2011 and 2017 and had EDDE data, peripheral blood test results were within the reference range in all age groups, even when EDDE was considered, and no significant changes over time were observed. However, the living environment and lifestyle following evacuation due to the GEJE have various effects on health; therefore, long-term attention is important.
