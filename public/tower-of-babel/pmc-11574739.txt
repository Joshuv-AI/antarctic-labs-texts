Development of a Tagmentation‐Based Next‐Generation Sequencing Clinical Assay as an Alternative to Capillary Electrophoresis‐Based Sequencing
Authors: Wei Cheng David Kuek, Chean Nee Chai, Wei Ming Jason Tham, Alvin Yu Jin Ng, Dilys Shi Ning Lau, Janice Yen Qi Loo, Dan Thu Van, Joanna Kia Min Tan, Chun Kiat Lee, Benedict Yan, Tim Hon Man Chan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNext‐generation sequencing (NGS) technology enables sample multiplexing for interrogation of multiple regions of interest (ROI). Leveraging this, together with access to affordable NGS platforms, we explored the practicality of moving capillary electrophoresis (CE), noncapillary electrophoresis and single‐gene testing to NGS. In this work, we evaluated the iSeq 100's capacity to validate 89 samples at once.MethodsGenomic DNA was extracted from 89 archival samples of varying specimen types. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was done with in house primers, library preparation with the Nextera XT Library Preparation Kit and cleaning up with paramagnetic beads. The sequencing was performed on one Illumina iSeq 100 flow cell.ResultsWith our workflow, 88 out of 89 samples were accurately sequenced with variant alleles identified. One sample of the 88 samples was initially discordant because the primers used were in a heterozygous deletion region. Upon redesigning of primers, the sample proved concordant.ConclusionsThe iSeq‐Nextera workflow proved accurate. However, variant allele frequencys generated by the Nextera are not precise.

Introduction

Capillary electrophoresis (CE)‐based sequencing (also known as Sanger sequencing) has been the mainstay technology for sequencing‐based diagnostic molecular tests since the early 2000s (Schrijver et al. 2005). Since the early 2010s, next‐generation sequencing (NGS) technologies have gradually permeated the clinical diagnostic laboratory landscape (Hernan et al. 2012).

Like most clinical molecular laboratories sited within a tertiary government hospital, our laboratory has been offering predominantly CE‐based‐sequencing tests for over a decade. Our existing CE‐based‐sequencing tests include constitutional/hereditary genetic testing (such as thalassemia genotyping), cancer‐associated somatic variant testing (such as CEBPA (OMIM: 116897) testing in acute myeloid leukemia), and somatic variant testing in noncancer conditions (such as UBA1 (OMIM: 314370) sequencing in VEXAS [vacuoles, E1 enzyme, X‐linked, autoinflammatory, somatic] syndrome).

Besides CE‐based sequencing tests, our laboratory has also been performing non‐CE‐based sequencing tests (typically for hotspot mutational testing) such as the MYD88 (OMIM: 602170) p.Leu265Pro variant for Waldenstrom's macroglobulinemia (Yu et al. 2018). We have also been performing panel‐based NGS testing for somatic variant detection for myeloid neoplasms. Not infrequently, clinicians have requested for only single‐gene testing (such as TP53 (OMIM:191170) only) testing for the genes within this panel.

With the arrival of affordable NGS platforms, we have explored the feasibility of migrating our existing CE‐ and non‐CE‐based sequencing/genotyping tests to NGS‐based testing and also evaluated the performance of single‐gene tests. In this study, we report the early results of our validation study using the iSeq 100.

DNA/RNA Extraction and Library Preparation

DNA extraction was performed using Qiagen Gentra PureGene kit. For BCR‐ABL1 kinase domain NGS, RNA was extracted using Maxwell RSC simplyRNA Blood Kit from Promega, and reverse transcription was performed using SuperScript VILO cDNA synthesis kit from Invitrogen.

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) of the regions of interest was performed using in‐house designed primers except for BCR‐ABL1 kinase domain. The ABL1 kinase domain from the fusion BCR‐ABL1 allele was selectively amplified as described previously (Soverini et al. 2020).

Libraries were generated using the Nextera XT DNA Sample Preparation kit (Illumina, San Diego, California, USA) as described previously (Ng, Kosmo, and Lee 2018). Briefly, PCR products were quantified by Qubit dsDNA HS assay using Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, California, USA). Tagmentation was then performed by adding one nanogram of PCR products (0.2 ng/μL) to the Nextera XT transposase at 55°C for 5 min followed by adding 5 μL of Neutralise Tagment Buffer for 5 min to neutralize the reaction. The tagmented DNA was then amplified via a 12‐cycle PCR protocol, which adds adapters required for cluster establishment.

Postamplification clean‐up was performed using 30 μL of AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Australia). The automated method was applied using Tecan Evo NGS (Männedorf, Switzerland) following the manufacturer's instructions.

Bioinformatics Analysis

Raw‐sequencing base call files (BCL) generated from the iSeq 100 sequencer were demultiplexed and converted to FASTQ using bcl2fastq2 (Illumina). For each sample, the FASTQ files were aligned to the human reference (GRCh37/hg19) using BWA‐MEM2 v2.2.1 to generate the BAM files (Vasimuddin et al. 2019). Pisces variant caller was used to identify germline and somatic variants including low‐frequency variants (Dunn et al. 2019).

The VCF files for each sample in the iSeq run were merged together into a single VCF file to reduce the compute time needed for multiple samples. The variants were annotated via ANNOVAR, VEP, SnpEff, Transvar, and CancerVar (Wang, Li, and Hakonarson 2010; McLaren et al. 2016; Cingolani et al. 2012; Zhou et al. 2015; Li et al. 2022). Variants were also annotated by several population databases, including SG10k, gnomAD, and genomeAsia (Wu et al. 2019; Karczewski et al. 2020; GenomeAsia100K Consortium 2019). Transcripts for each gene were selected via the NCBI's RefseqSelect database.

Nonsplice site intronic, UTRs, upstream, downstream, and synonymous variants were filtered off from the variant list. Variants that had strand bias (SB), had low quality (QUAL < 100), or not PASS variants in VCF were filtered off. Variants with known “Benign/Likely Benign” classifications in the Clinvar database (accessed March 20, 2022) were removed (e.g., TP53 p.Pro72Arg). Variants that were common, seen in population, or multiple times in samples were removed (e.g., CEBPA p.His195_Pro196dup). Variants that had known associations with disease or were a known pathogenic reference allele on hg19 were retained (e.g., F2 UTR variant rs1799963 and F5 hg19 pathogenic reference rs6025). The IGV screenshots of the filtered variant list were automatically generated by the IGV snapshot automator (Kelly and Hall 2023).

To determine the coverage of each sample, the number of bases that met a threshold of 1×, 100×, 250×, and > 1000× within a given target sequence of a primer pair were calculated by Mosdepth (Pedersen and Quinlan 2018). The proportion/percentage of the coverage in each threshold window allowed for the detection of any sample or primer pair that had low or no coverage. For validation against the existing methodology/platform, a 2% variant allele frequency (VAF) threshold was used for somatic variant calling.

Results

Description About Quality Metrics

In general, the majority of samples had a coverage of at least 100× in at least 90% of bases. Cases that did not attain at least 100× coverage in 90% of bases included five JAK2 exon 12 samples (38, 39, 40, 43, 44), and one CEBPA sample (64). For these six JAK2 exon 12 samples, the percentage of bases that attained at least 100× coverage ranged from 81% to 89%. For the CEBPA sample, only 70% of bases attained at least 100× coverage.

For this validation study, no hard coverage filtering criterion was implemented.

Concordance Between Expected and Observed Results

Of the 89 samples, there was one sample for which no data were generated. We surmise that this was possibly due to a failure of the sequencing reaction to take place. Of the remaining 88 samples, 87 (98.9%) were concordant, and 1 sample was initially discordant, for the expected and observed results (Figure 1).

Flow chart for concordance between expected and observed results.

For the one discordant sample where the expected result was a p.His538_Lys539delinsGlnLeu variant in JAK2, further investigation revealed the presence of a previously unknown deletion that affected the primer‐binding site of the forward primer that had been designed by our laboratory during assay development for the iSeq 100 JAK2 assay (Figure 2). Re‐designing the primers enabled the detection of the expected JAK2 p.His538_Lys539delinsGlnLeu variant.

Discussion

The iSeq 100 is an NGS platform with generally similar sequencing chemistry to existing paired‐end short‐read sequencing platforms where complementary metal‐oxide‐semiconductor (CMOS) technology is combined with sequencing by synthesis (SBS) chemistry. However, the particular sequencing chemistry is unique to the iSeq100 because of its ability to perform image capture with only one fluorescent dye, thus contributing to the platform's small physical footprint. Using only one dye, only the adenine and the thymine are labeled when the first image is taken. By removing the label on adenine, labeling the cytosine in an intermediate chemistry step while the guanine remains unlabeled, a second image is taken. This enables the different nucleotides to be distinguished by their emission patterns (Illumina, iSeq 100 System 2023). Analysis of the two images allows the algorithm to determine the appropriate base call.

Because of the unique sequencing chemistry, it is important in our opinion for clinical laboratories to carefully validate the platform before clinical deployment. In the literature to date, there are groups that have published their validation experience on the iSeq 100. The iSeq 100 has been validated by and currently used in conjunction with a Cancer Hotspot Panel at the Wellington Regional Genetics Laboratory, Wellington Hospital, whereas the Eye Research Institute is working to targeted sequencing of seven Familial Exudative Vitreo‐Retinopathy (FEVR)‐Linked Genes with the sequencer (Wilson et al. 2020; Cicerone et al. 2022; Bruzek et al. 2020). However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no literature yet on the validation of the iSeq 100 with the Nextera XT library preparation workflow.

By combining the iSeq 100 with the Nextera XT library preparation workflow, customization is possible throughout the entire NGS process from the region of interest, library preparation method, cascade testing, and many more to perform different tests based on different needs (Bastida et al. 2016; Mohamed et al. 2023). We were able to complete the run being presented with varying number of samples for different genes, totaling 89 samples (Table 1).

The entire process took 3 days. Using this run as an example, if the 89 samples are done with CE‐based sequencing, it will not be efficient, as it will take much longer and more expensive to sequence (Table 2).

Compared to CE‐based sequencing, NGS using the iSeq 100 enables the detection of low VAF variants (e.g., somatic mosaic variants, somatic variants in cancer, or heteroplasmic variants in mitochondriopathies) that are below the limit of detection of CE‐based sequencing. However, Nextera tends to have lower VAF as compared to amplicon‐based sequencing and pyrosequencing, perhaps due to several reasons such as the algorithm of the bioinformatics analysis pipelines being used for variant calling, differing read lengths, the contrasting library preparation method and GC‐content‐dependent coverage bias (Segerman et al. 2022; Tyler et al. 2016).

Ascertaining the BCR‐ABL1 coverage initially posed an interesting challenge and surfaced the high‐level question of reference transcriptome alignment, as opposed to reference genome alignment (Morillon and Gautheret 2019). In the end, coverage of the BCR‐ABL1 cDNA sequences on the exons were done to be able to identify the isoforms.

The Nextera XT workflow, being limited to DNA use, uses transposons to fragment and add adapter sequences to the end of the input DNA simultaneously. The resulting random fragmentation can cause lower coverage uniformity (Jennings et al. 2017). The transposon tends to prefer certain regions based on the GC content contributes as well (Segerman et al. 2022). There is also a discussion to be made of the need for the use of no‐template control in the runs. Since there is nothing to sequence, it could be deemed as a waste of reagents, but it could also be included as a way to prevent carryover or any contaminants (Oliver, Hart, and Klee 2015).

Bioinformatics analysis requires much knowledge of code, pipeline creation, variant filtering, variant annotation, and clinical reporting (Figure 3). It is not perfect and has many known factors that affect the readings. The hg19, for one, does not completely cover the entire human genome which in turn will affect variant calling (Domchek and Weber 2008). The many complex factors in variant calling interpretation is something to take into account as well, from the database to use, the method of annotation for the variants, the population of the samples, its impact to protein or its mechanisms of disease to its location and predicted effect, there are many pitfalls of bioinformatics to bear in mind when performing this task (Alkan, Coe, and Eichler 2011; Knoppers et al. 2016).

At the time of submission, automation is not yet possible, with much of the process requiring expertise from the Bioinformatician, Laboratory Scientist, Clinicians, and data protection policies in the clinical laboratory. CE‐based‐sequencing faces the same issue, typically using Gene Mapper and Sequencing Analysis Software. These programs are not integrated with laboratory information systems (LIS) and often require preparing, conditioning of the capillary, and maintenance of the machine.

We present a case where the observed result was not concordant with the intended result. Including different types of samples in the run, a JAK2 sample was expected to have the p.His538_Lys539delinsGlnLeu variant but no mutations were initially detected. To investigate, PCR and Sanger sequencing were performed only to produce a short PCR product, which was attributed to the primer binding site being located at a heterozygous deletion region. As such, a new set of primers was designed to maintain coverage but at different primer binding sites. The p.His538_Lys539delinsGlnLeu variant was detected in the following run. Incidentally, there has been a need to redesign the primers regardless since longer amplicons are needed for the Nextera library preparation compared to Sanger sequencing.

On the other hand, Sanger sequencing has its advantages as well. Unlike the iSeq100, it has no need for batching to maximize cost efficiency since running Sanger sequencing is not too costly. Combining that with a laboratory that does not have too high an overall sequencing workload will enable faster turnaround time as compared to the iSeq100.

Our approach of using the Nextera‐iSeq workflow developed in our study proved accurate, utilizing differing sample types, and supporting the possibility of moving our existing CE and non‐CE‐based‐sequencing/genotyping tests to NGS‐based testing. The VAFs generated by the Nextera workflow are not precise, as also seen by germline variants not having a VAF close to 50%.
