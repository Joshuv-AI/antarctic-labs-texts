An App‐Based Proton Pump Inhibitor Discontinuation Tool: A Multicentre, Open‐Label, Randomised Superiority Trial
Authors: Lieke M Koggel, Thomas Timmers, Marten A Lantinga, Rudolf B Kool, Cornelis Kramers, Noortje van Herwaarden, Peter D Siersema, SUPPORT study consortium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundProton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are frequently used without long‐term indication. Discontinuation remains challenging because of rebound symptoms. Whether digital guidance tools improve discontinuation is unknown. We evaluated whether an app improves PPI discontinuation.MethodsIn this open‐label adaptive randomised controlled superiority trial, adults using daily PPI for ≥ 4 weeks without chronic PPI indication, according to the Dutch College of General Practitioners guideline, were recruited from outpatient clinics of Internal Medicine, Gastroenterology, Rheumatology and Nephrology departments at three Dutch hospitals. Patients were excluded if their physician deemed PPI use necessary beyond guideline recommendations, after prior upper gastrointestinal surgery, in case of inability to understand the study, unavailability of a smartphone/tablet, or limited life expectancy. Patients were randomly assigned (1:1), stratified by department and initial indication, to receive information on PPI discontinuation through an app (intervention) or brochure (control). Primary outcome was PPI discontinuation at 2 months follow‐up, defined as self‐declared intake of ≤ 1 tablet in the preceding 14 days. Sample‐size was revised earlier than prespecified based on interim data. The trial is registered as closed to accrual at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05348252).ResultsBetween May 15, 2022, and March 31, 2023, 174 patients were randomized (86 intervention, 88 control). Nine and two patients, respectively, did not receive the allocated intervention. Of the 163 participants (mean age 54 y/o [SD 11], 76 males [47%]), 66/77 (86%) discontinued PPIs in the intervention group versus 55/86 (64%) in the control group (RR 1.34, 95% CI 1.11–1.61, p = 0.002). Adverse events occurred in 18/121 (15%) patients who discontinued PPI use and in 6/42 (14%) who did not. Only 4/121 (3%) and 1/42 (2%), respectively, were potentially attributable to PPI discontinuation.ConclusionAn app‐based intervention significantly improves discontinuation of inappropriate PPI use compared with an information brochure and could therefore be considered for PPI deprescribing initiatives.

Introduction

Globally, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) rank prominently as one of the most frequently prescribed drugs [1]. Although indications for chronic PPI use are limited to severe reflux oesophagitis, Barrett's oesophagus, Zollinger–Ellison syndrome and as chronic gastroprotection, 40%–70% of PPIs are used for indications that are not according to guidelines [2, 3, 4, 5]. This inappropriate use may unnecessarily harm patients by inducing adverse drug reactions (ADRs) or drug‐drug interactions. Despite limited evidence for causal relationships, increasing evidence suggests that long‐term PPI use may lead to severe ADRs, including deficiencies of magnesium, Clostridium difficile colitis and renal disease [6]. Furthermore, excessive PPI prescriptions contribute to avoidable healthcare costs [7]. Given the clinical and economic consequences described above, it is important to prevent and discontinue inappropriate PPI use.

PPI discontinuation in current clinical practice is coordinated by treating physicians that assess the possibility of PPI discontinuation and inform patients sufficiently about discontinuation strategies including expected difficulties (by means of a consult, brochure or website). Sometimes, a follow‐up consult is scheduled for evaluation. One of the factors that complicates discontinuation of PPI use, among others, is the rebound of acid‐related symptoms [8]. Despite the development of discontinuation strategies, including tapering schedules, and guidelines advocating discontinuation of inappropriate PPI use, no improvement in discontinuation rates has been noticed and inappropriate long‐term PPI use remains a problem [5, 9, 10].

Studies conducted in settings other than PPI deprescribing programmes have shown that offering timely information through e‐health applications improves several outcomes such as clinical outcomes, patient knowledge, treatment adherence and patient satisfaction [11]. The most favourable effects have been demonstrated in interventions with a short duration (weeks‐months) and frequent interaction (≥ 1 push notification(s)/week) [11]. The process of discontinuing PPIs meets these characteristics. To our knowledge, no digital interventions currently exist that specifically guide patients through the tapering or discontinuation of pharmacotherapy, let alone PPI discontinuation. Having demonstrated effectiveness in delivering timely, personalized information across different clinical programmes, the Patient Journey App (Interactive Studios BV, ’s‐Hertogenbosch, the Netherlands) holds promise for implementation in PPI deprescribing care [12, 13]. We hypothesize that timely and interactive guidance on discontinuing inappropriate PPI use through the Patient Journey App will improve the overall discontinuation rate compared to an information brochure delivering all information at once by providing information at moments of highest relevance during tapering, reducing uncertainty and fear of rebound symptoms, reinforcing progress, and supporting patient capability and confidence throughout the discontinuation process.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This group sequential adaptive open‐label randomized controlled superiority trial with sample size adaptation enrolled patients between May 15, 2022, and March 31, 2023, from the outpatient clinic of the departments of Internal Medicine, Gastroenterology, Rheumatology and Nephrology from two community hospitals (Canisius Wilhelmina Hospital and Sint Maartenskliniek) and one university hospital (Radboud university medical centre), all located in Nijmegen, The Netherlands. Study data were collected and stored using Castor EDC (Ciwit BV, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) and OnlinePROMS (Interactive Studios BV, 's‐Hertogenbosch, The Netherlands). The Medical Research Ethics Committee (MREC Máxima Medical Centre, reference N21.079) exempted the study from the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act as the study did not involve withholding care nor was any additional, non‐standard or interventional care provided—the study solely examined how information was communicated. The local study committees of participating centres approved the study protocol. The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guidelines were followed. The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (registration number NCT05348252, registration date 05‐04–2022) [14]. The trial protocol including the statistical analysis plan can be found in Supporting Information S1. After the trial commenced, the protocol was amended to revise the sample size, differing from the originally prespecified plan, as described below. Participants received no compensation or were offered any incentive for participation. Apart from patient involvement in the development of the app as mentioned below, there was no patient or public involvement in the study procedures.

Participants

Patients aged 18–70 years were eligible for inclusion in case of self‐reported daily PPI use for ≥ 4 weeks without a chronic PPI indication, according to the 2013 guideline ‘Upper gastrointestinal symptoms’ by the Dutch College of General Practitioners, at the moment of screening or in the next three years (e.g., patients without chronic PPI indication during screening that needed chronic ulcer prophylaxis based on age and drug use after their next birthday, were not invited) [5]. Chronic PPI indications for patients aged < 70 years according to the Dutch College of General Practitioners guideline consist of (1) Barrett's oesophagus, (2) reflux oesophagitis grade C/D, (3) Zollinger–Ellison syndrome, (4) peptic ulcer disease in medical history and high‐risk medication use and (5) non‐selective non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drug (NSAID) use and at least two other risk factors including age 60–70 years, severe rheumatoid arthritis, heart failure or diabetes mellitus, use of high‐dose non‐selective NSAID and other high‐risk medication (Supporting Information S1). On demand high‐risk medication use was considered relevant in case of using it for one‐third of the time or more (i.e., ≥ 1 tablet/capsule every 3 days). Exclusion criteria were PPI indication according to their treating physician outside the guideline indications, prior upper gastrointestinal surgery, inability to understand the study, unavailability of a smartphone/tablet and limited life expectancy.

Potential participants were identified by means of an electronic health record (EHR) overview based on the eligibility criteria, after which patient records where manually screened and treating physicians were asked for approval. Agreements were made with several departments to exclude patients with specialty‐specific PPI indications, regardless of the Dutch College of General Practitioners guideline (see Supporting Information S2), such as patients with systemic sclerosis. After approval of treating physicians, patients were invited through an opt‐out letter. In case of no response, patients were personally contacted 2 weeks later. All participants had direct contact with a physician before enrolment as part of eligibility confirmation and informed consent, during which the rationale for discontinuing inappropriate PPI use was explained. After signing informed consent, their general practitioner (GP) and pharmacist were informed and offered the option to object against participation. A detailed description of the recruitment process can be found in Supporting Information S2.

Study Procedures

Sequentially numbered patients were randomized (1:1) using randomly selected variable block sizes of two and four blocks with stratification for department and initial PPI indication (upper gastrointestinal symptoms or ulcer prophylaxis) using the digital randomization system of Castor EDC. Blinding was not possible for patients or investigators/outcome assessors due to the nature of the intervention.

Patients allocated to the control group received an electronic information brochure (Supporting Information S1) by email offering all information at once. Information was based on the ‘Upper gastrointestinal symptoms’ section of Thuisarts.nl, a Dutch website from the Dutch College of General Practitioners with healthcare information for patients [15]. It includes background information on PPIs in general, indications for PPI use, possible adverse drug reactions, how to discontinue PPI use, possible rebound effects after PPI discontinuation and instructions on how to manage these.

Patients allocated to the intervention group received the same information but in a different modality. Instead of exposing patients to all this information at once, patients in the intervention group received the information as brief messages during the course of several months, timed to the patient's tapering regimen—through the Patient Journey App (Interactive Studios BV, 's‐Hertogenbosch, the Netherlands) using push notifications and supporting videos that were specifically developed for this study (screenshots and overview of application can be found in Supporting Information S3) [16]. The app is adaptive, meaning that the guidance was dependent on the chosen tapering regimen and personalized based on patient‐reported outcomes, such as how they were feeling and whether they were satisfied with the current tapering regimen. Patients received informative messages at intervals ranging from one to 5 days, depending on the timing within the schedule. Furthermore, they were reminded of tapering steps and could directly request contact with the research physician in case of rebound symptoms or wish to switch to a different tapering regimen. For the development of the app, a literature search was performed on evidence and advice on discontinuation regimens and rebound symptoms followed by interviews with gastroenterologists, GPs and patients regarding their experiences, preferences and tips for successful PPI discontinuation. Based on this information, a communication expert developed short and understandable text messages for the application. In addition, short videos of two medical doctors (LK and PS) who provided concise explanations about the process, background information and tips were recorded and implemented. The application was tested by all authors for completeness, comprehensibility, correctness of process flow and ease of use.

Patients in the intervention group could choose between three tapering regimes of which potential (dis)advantages were explained: (1) directly stop PPI use, (2) taper PPI dose by 50% until the lowest dose possible every week or (3) every 2 weeks. In contrast, patients in the control group were all advised to taper their PPI use by 50% every week, based on the Dutch College of General Practitioners guideline [5]. The control group was not offered alternative options because of the limited insight into the actual tapering process, as they received a brochure only without further interaction afterwards. When the lowest possible dose was already used, direct discontinuation of the PPI was advised in both groups, as tapering was not possible. Both groups could contact the research physician in case of questions or (rebound) symptoms.

Follow‐up started with the initiation of the PPI tapering regime (e.g., if pantoprazole 40 mg was used, follow‐up started at the first day of pantoprazole 20 mg) and consisted of monthly digital questionnaires which continued monthly until the last patient had reached a follow‐up of 2 months—whether or not PPI tapering was achieved. Follow‐up questionnaires were identical for both study groups, except for an application usability questionnaire, which was administered only in the intervention group. To prevent missing data, patients who did not complete the questionnaires were contacted by telephone.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients who discontinued PPI use, measured after 2 months after the start of their discontinuation programme. The 2‐month time point was selected because rebound symptoms are expected to be most pronounced during the first weeks after PPI discontinuation [17]. PPI discontinuation was defined as self‐declared intake of ≤ 1 tablet/capsule in the previous 14 days. Following successful discontinuation, re‐initiation of PPI use was defined as the self‐declared intake of > 1 tablet/capsule in the previous 14 days during two consecutive follow‐up measurements (or during one follow‐up measurement in case no further follow‐up data was available). In case of unsuccessful discontinuation, the decrease in PPI use between baseline and 2 months follow‐up was measured as the proportion of the Defined Daily Dose (DDD). DDD was defined by the World Health Organization (omeprazole 20 mg/day, pantoprazole 40 mg/day, lansoprazole 30 mg/day, rabeprazole 20 mg/day, esomeprazole 30 mg/day). Furthermore, we determined variables which could potentially be associated with successful PPI discontinuation including general patient characteristics (age, gender), patient characteristics associated with reflux symptoms (BMI), fears associated with PPI use or discontinuation (fear of ADRs and fear of discontinuing PPI), PPI characteristics associated with rebound/reflux symptoms (indication, duration of use and DDD) and concomitant drug use associated with PPI indications such as ulcer prophylaxis (non‐selective NSAIDs, coxibs and low‐dose aspirins).

Secondary outcomes included (serious) adverse events ((S)AEs). As the intervention solely involved a change in communication strategy, no (S)AEs were anticipated due to the intervention. However, (S)AEs potentially related to PPI discontinuation were monitored. (S)AEs were systematically assessed at 2‐month follow‐up based on self‐reported physician visits including specification of the underlying reason. AEs were defined as any medical occurrence that was not necessarily related to the study. Serious adverse events (SAEs) were defined as AEs resulting in death, being life‐threatening, requiring hospitalization, causing statistically significant disability, or that could have resulted in such outcomes if not for timely intervention. AEs were considered related to PPI discontinuation if they involved (potential) rebound symptoms, reflux or peptic ulcer disease. We additionally assessed the presence of rebound symptoms after successful discontinuation, defined as an increase in upper gastrointestinal symptom severity between baseline and 2 months as measured by the validated Patient Assessment of Gastrointestinal Symptom Severity Score (PAGI‐SYM) questionnaire [18]. PAGI‐SYM consists of 20 items and six subscales covering heartburn/regurgitation, fullness/early satiety, nausea/vomiting, bloating, upper abdominal pain and lower abdominal pain. All scales range from zero to five. A minimal difference in the mean of 0.3 was considered clinically relevant, based on the minimal clinically important difference defined by the questionnaire developers [19]. We assessed the start of new medication for upper gastrointestinal symptoms other than PPIs within the first 2 months and the difference in self‐management behaviour as measured by the validated short Patient Activation Measure (PAM‐13) questionnaire at baseline and at 2 months [20]. PAM‐13 consists of 13 items on a 4‐point Likert scale. Item scores are summed up to a raw sum score resulting in theoretical values between 13 and 52, which are then transformed to a standardized metric ranging from 0 to 100. The most prevalent (1%–10%) ADRs according to the Medicines Evaluation Board (Dutch Medication Registration Authority) were compared between baseline and 2‐month follow‐up [21]. In the intervention group, usability of the application was measured at 4 months by the validated System Usability Scale (SUS), which was interpreted according to a 5‐point adjective scale and transformed to a standardized score ranging from 0 to 100, with mean scores > 50.9 interpreted as sufficient, > 71.4 as good, and > 85.5 as excellent [22].

Sample Size

To estimate the effect size, literature on other PPI discontinuation guidance strategies was used. Initially, for the intervention group, a slightly lower effect (50%) was expected than the 83% PPI discontinuation rate that was seen after a previously performed intensive face‐to‐face coaching strategy, as our study only offers digital guidance [23]. For the control group, a somewhat higher PPI discontinuation rate (40%) was anticipated than previously described by sending information leaflets without personal contact with a healthcare worker (24%), because our participants were all motivated to discontinue PPI use [24]. Based on a two‐stage design with a power of 84.5% at a two‐sided significance level of 0.0294 (Pocock boundaries p‐value), a sample size of 450 patients (225 in each group) was needed to detect an increase in PPI discontinuation from 40% to 55% [25]. If the interim analysis did not show statistically significant results, the study would be expanded to 902 patients (451 in each group) to detect an increase in the discontinuation rate from 40% to 50% (power 80%, alpha 0.0294).

As inclusions progressed slower than expected, mainly due to treating physicians’ preference to continue PPI use despite the absence of a chronic indication according to the guideline and patients' strong wish to continue PPI use because of (fear of) dyspepsia, the sample size calculation was revised on 03‐03‐2023 based on the data available for the first 100 patients. Only the group allocation and primary outcome were used to determine the revised sample size (PPI discontinuation rate control group 65% vs. intervention group 87%). To correct for repeated testing, a critical p‐value of 0.0294 and power of 84.5% were used [25]. Accordingly, a minimum of 152 patients were needed for the final analysis. The analysis was performed by LK and TT, supervised by a statistical consultant affiliated with a different department within Radboudumc who was not involved in the trial, and approved by all authors and the MREC. Details on the sample size revision can be found in Supporting Information S2.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis plan was pre‐defined in the research protocol (Supporting Information S1). Means with standard deviations and medians with interquartile ranges were used for normal distributed data and non‐normal distributed data, respectively. The primary outcome (proportion of PPI discontinuation) was analysed using the χ2 test rather than logistic regression as described in the protocol, as randomisation was stratified and no additional covariate adjustment was required. The same method was applied for the post hoc subgroup analysis. Re‐initiation of PPI use was measured using a Kaplan‐Meier and presented as mean and 95% confidence interval (CI). The decrease in DDD was compared using the Wilcoxon's rank test. Variables associated with successful PPI discontinuation were identified using multivariable logistic regression. Variables with a p‐value < 0.2 in the univariable analysis were included in the multivariable analysis. A backwards model was used to stepwise eliminate the variables with the highest p‐value until all variables had a p‐value < 0.05. Differences in mean scores of questionnaires were tested with the paired or independent samples t‐test, whereas start of new medication was analysed with the χ2 test. Analyses were performed using complete cases only. Participants with missing outcome data were excluded from the respective analyses. Analyses were performed by LK and TT with IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). All tests were 2‐sided and considering the final analysis to be the second of two analyses with α spending at the first (revised sample size) analysis, a p‐value < 0.0294 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Patients and Follow‐Up

A total of 2658 patients were identified by the EHR report, of which 1057 met the inclusion criteria after manual patient chart screening (Figure 1). Approval of treating physicians was obtained for 736 patients. A total of 308 patients were excluded based on PPI indications according to their treating physician outside the guidelines. Following patients' invitation and direct patient contact, 576 patients were deemed eligible. Of these, 86/576 (15%) were not motivated to discontinue PPI therapy with (n = 72) or without (n = 14) underlying dyspepsia. The exact reason for exclusion was not known for the 66 patients who chose direct opt‐out. Due to completion of the study, 99 patients were eventually not invited to participate. In total, 174 patients were randomized to the intervention group (n = 86) or control group (n = 88) of whom nine and two patients, respectively, did not receive the allocated intervention. As these patients dropped out of the study before any intervention or follow‐up was started, the per protocol analysis therefore equals the intention‐to‐treat analysis. To our knowledge, none of the patients received relevant concomitant interventions during the study period.

Patient selection.

Mean age was 54 years (SD 11) and 76 participants (47%) were male. Most patients were included from the Rheumatology department (n = 62, 38%). Initial PPI indications were mainly related to ulcer prophylaxis (n = 117, 72%). The daily dose at baseline was clustered around a DDD equal to 1, resulting in a median daily dose of 1.0 DDD (IQR 1.0‐1.0) with only 10% using a DDD < 1.0% and 20% using a DDD > 1.0. PPIs were used for a median of 8 years (IQR 3‐15). Baseline characteristics were similar between groups, except for a slightly higher fear of ADRs in the control group and a slightly higher fear of PPI discontinuation in the intervention group (Table 1).

Re‐initiation of PPI use after successful discontinuation at 2‐month follow‐up was seen in 19% (n = 23/121) (Supporting Information S4: eFigure 1), with no statistically significant difference in re‐initiation of PPI use between the intervention group (n = 11/66, 17%) and control group (n = 12/55, 22%, p = 0.586).

In the unsuccessful discontinued group, daily PPI dose decreased with a median DDD of −0.5 (IQR ‐0.8‐0.0, p < 0.001). No statistically significant difference was seen in DDD decrease between the intervention group (−0.5, IQR ‐1.0‐0.0) and control group (−0.5, IQR ‐0.7‐0.0, p = 0.233).

Regarding variables associated with successful PPI discontinuation, only PPI indication (ulcer prophylaxis vs. gastric symptoms) was found to be statistically significantly associated with successful discontinuation (OR 2.88, 95% CI 1.37‐6.05) (Supporting Information S4: eTable 1). No differences were seen in potential ADRs of PPI use between baseline and 2‐month follow‐up (Supporting Information S4: eTable 2).

Adverse Events

AEs occurred in 15% (n = 18/121) of patients who successfully discontinued PPI use, and in 14% (n = 6/42) of patients who did not successfully discontinue PPI use. No SAEs were reported. AEs potentially attributable to PPI discontinuation were observed in 3% (n = 4/121) and 2% (n = 1/42) of patients who successfully and unsuccessfully discontinued PPI use, respectively. These events included coughing (n = 3), abdominal pain (n = 1) and chest pain (n = 1).

Rebound Symptoms and Treatment

Patients who successfully discontinued PPI use showed a mean increase in upper gastrointestinal symptom severity of 0.16 (95% CI 0.05‐0.27, p = 0.004), which is considered to be not clinically relevant (Supporting Information S4: eTable 3). Only the subdomain heartburn/regurgitation showed a clinically relevant increase of 0.39 (95% CI 0.24‐0.55, p < 0.001).

No difference in the start of new medication for upper gastrointestinal symptoms was seen between the successful (n = 21/121, 17%) and unsuccessful discontinued group (n = 8/42, 19%) (RR 1.10, 95% CI 0.53–2.29, p = 0.805). However, despite the success of discontinuation, alternative medication was started less often in the intervention group (n = 8/77, 10%) compared to the control group (n = 21/86, 24%) (RR 0.43, 95% CI 0.20‐0.90, p = 0.019). Antacids were the most frequently started alternative medication for upper gastrointestinal symptoms (Supporting Information S4: eTable 4).

Self‐Management Behaviour

No statistically significant differences in change of self‐management behaviour between baseline and 2‐month follow‐up were measured in the intervention group (mean PAM‐13 increase −0.5, 95% CI −4.0–2.9) versus control group (mean PAM‐13 increase 3.3, 95% CI 0.2–6.3, p = 0.099) nor in the successful discontinued group (mean PAM‐13 increase 2.4, 95% CI 0.1–4.7) versus unsuccessful discontinued group (mean PAM‐13 increase −0.9, 95% CI −6.9–5.1, p = 0.214) (Supporting Information S4: eTable 5).

Use of the Application

Median application use was 161 days (IQR 79‐273) with a median use frequency of 25 times (IQR 16‐34) per individual (Supporting Information S4: eTable 6). The usability score of the application was available for 83% of patients (n = 64/77) who rated the application with a mean SUS of 72 (SD 13), which classifies it as ‘good’ usability [26].

Discussion

This open‐label, multicentre, multidisciplinary randomised superiority trial demonstrated that a patient‐centred smartphone app delivering timely and interactive information statistically significantly improved the discontinuation rate of inappropriate PPI use compared with an information brochure (86% vs. 64%). The app also reduced the use of alternative medication for upper gastrointestinal symptoms, such as antacids (10% vs. 24%). Although a moderate increase in reflux symptoms was observed after successful discontinuation, only about 20% re‐started PPI therapy during a median follow‐up of 8 months, with no difference between the groups. The patients who could not completely discontinue PPI use showed a dose decrease in 50% in both groups.

Prior studies have shown PPI discontinuation rates ranging from 17% to 83%, likely influenced by the method and intensity of guidance, and targeted patient population [23, 24, 27, 28]. Although no exact comparison could be made as this is the first study to discontinue drug use with support of an app, PPI discontinuation in the app‐assisted group is relatively high compared to previous studies. This is in line with the results of other app‐based medical programmes offering timely information, such as postoperative care after knee replacement [12, 13]. The control group also showed a relatively high PPI discontinuation rate, especially when compared to a previous study that used a similar design as our control group (64% vs. 24%) [24]. This can be explained by the fact that all invited patients in our study had direct contact with a physician before the start of the study, during which they received information about the study's rationale and potential risks as part of the informed consent process, in contrast to the study mentioned above, in which patients themselves had to actively contact their physician if they considered PPI discontinuation. Furthermore, ulcer prophylaxis was the dominant initial PPI indication (> 70%), suggesting that a large proportion of the study population was asymptomatic and therefore more likely to successfully discontinue PPI use.

The moderate increase in heartburn/regurgitation symptoms after successful PPI discontinuation suggests the occurrence of rebound symptoms, which is known to occur in 44% of healthy patients who discontinue PPI use [29]. These symptoms most likely resulted in the 19% PPI relapse rate after successful discontinuation, as rebound symptoms can persist beyond 2 months, specifically after long‐term PPI use [30]. Still, even when taking re‐initiation of PPI use into account, the PPI discontinuation rate for both groups combined remains high (n = 98/163, 60%). This is likely explained by the use of tapering regimes, which are known to prevent rebound symptoms thereby increasing PPI discontinuation rates, combined with the focus on lifestyle interventions [31].

Only a small number of patients was not motivated to discontinue PPI therapy. Although this is thought to be an underestimation as the reason for exclusion was not known for all patients, it is still expected to be a minority, which is in line with a previous qualitative study indicating that patients are quite receptive to cease PPIs [32]. Nevertheless, we encountered some challenges in the selection process based on the lack of clarity on PPI indications, outdated PPI indications and caution among physicians regarding PPI withdrawal. The latter is thought to have led to the relatively high number of patients with continued PPIs prescribed by physicians outside the current guidelines.

The randomized, multicentre design involving multiple specialties increases the generalizability of results across different outpatient populations. Furthermore, the use of validated questionnaires (PAGI‐SYM and PAM‐13) along with stratified randomization strengthened the internal validity of the trial. Another strength of this study is the absence of loss to follow‐up, likely resulting from both the motivated patient population and the structured study design. In the intervention group, frequent attention through the app likely enhanced adherence and responsiveness. In both groups, follow‐up completeness was further ensured by systematically contacting participants when questionnaires were not completed. Finally, the inclusion process was executed consistently and thoroughly, leading to a detailed insight into the selection of participants.

Nonetheless, several limitations must also be acknowledged. The most important limitation is the sample size adaptation mid‐trial. This could have led to an overestimation of the effect, as was shown in a meta‐analysis of randomized controlled trials that were prematurely terminated [33]. However, measures were taken to reduce the risk of early stopping at interim analysis via a Pocock methodology resulting in a stricter p‐value to minimize this risk, and consultation of a statistician who was neither involved in the study nor affiliated with the department. The revised sample size and a non‐prespecified interim analysis may also limit generalisability, as effect estimates derived from a smaller population may not fully reflect outcomes in broader clinical settings. A sensitivity analysis was performed at the time of reducing the sample size to understand the risk of bias due to early stopping using an upward revised interim analysis count in the used Pocock methodology to adjust the critical value tested against. Using this approach, early stopping would have been acceptable if this methodology was utilized at the initiation of the trial. Consequently, the risk of bias due to early stopping was considered minimal. Details are provided in the Supporting Information S2. Another limitation is the difference in tapering regimes between the groups, including the option for patients in the intervention group to choose their tapering strategy. This choice may have increased patient involvement and motivation, which are known to support behaviour change and may have contributed to the observed effect. In addition, using tapering regimens that differed from the standard regimen advised to the control group could have led to an overestimation of the discontinuation rate. However, sensitivity analysis confirmed that the tapering regimen chosen did not account for the observed difference in PPI discontinuation rate between the groups. The high discontinuation rates in both arms may raise concerns about potential bias. First, the primary outcome was based solely on self‐reported PPI use without external validation. The self‐reported outcomes are subject to recall and social desirability bias, which may be differential between study groups. In particular, patients in the intervention arm received more frequent contact and reminders, leading to greater engagement, which may have increased the likelihood of reporting successful PPI discontinuation. Second, selection bias cannot be fully excluded, given the study design and substantial number of initial exclusions. However, exclusions were primarily made due to ineligibility that became apparent only after clarifying details with the patients, owing to outdated or missing information in the EHRs. Therefore, the final study population is considered representative of PPI users that are eligible and motivated for discontinuation. It should be noted, however, that a proportion of patients was excluded due to lack of motivation, often related to dyspeptic symptoms, suggesting that the overall discontinuation rate observed in this study may be an overestimation of what could also be expected in a broader population of PPI users without a guideline‐based indication. Although both above‐mentioned potential biases might have influenced the external validity by an overestimation of the overall proportion of successful discontinuation, it is unlikely that they have led to biased estimations of between group differences. In addition, the lack of data on sociodemographic variables such as ethnicity limits assessment of generalizability and the open‐label design introduces a risk of bias, potentially resulting in overestimation of the outcome. Lastly, for a minority of participants, follow‐up data was only available for a shorter period as the study was terminated when the last patient reached 2‐month follow‐up. Even so, the median follow‐up data comprised a total of 8 months. Although a follow‐up of several years would be ideally to reliably report on long‐term sustainability of PPI discontinuation, the current follow‐up is thought to be sufficient to estimate the PPI relapse risk as most relapses are expected to occur shortly after discontinuation [34].

Given the success of the application in PPI discontinuation, we advocate wider implementation of a digital PPI discontinuation intervention using timely guidance to decrease inappropriate PPI use and thereby reduce potential risks of chronic PPI use. Compared to intensive face‐to‐face guidance in PPI discontinuation, which also shows high success rates, an additional advantage of the application is the potential to decrease costs and save valuable time of healthcare providers as guidance is provided via an automated digital delivery channel and has the potential to replace 1‐3 physician consultations per patient [23]. The feasibility and potential scalability of the intervention is supported by the fact that the Patient Journey App is already in use in other clinical settings [16]. Although no formal cost‐effectiveness analysis was performed, the app requires only one‐time development/implementation costs and minimal costs per additional patient, whereas brochure‐based strategies rely on repeated personnel time and printing/distribution costs. These characteristics suggest potential economic advantages. It can be discussed whether this project should be performed in primary or secondary care, which depends on the local organization of healthcare. Tasks not covered by the application are patient screening, prescribing of tapering‐down doses and consultation for rebound symptoms that are not responding to the (lifestyle) tips as proposed by the application. Apart from PPI discontinuation guidance, the success of this study should stimulate initiating deprescribing studies with timely app‐based information for chronic medication use that often is deemed of low‐value such as benzodiazepines, opioids and antidepressants.

In conclusion, an app‐based intervention providing timely education improves PPI discontinuation rates compared with a one‐time information brochure. Following discontinuation, a moderate increase in reflux symptoms was observed. Further research is needed to assess long‐term outcomes and large‐scale implementation across different healthcare settings.
