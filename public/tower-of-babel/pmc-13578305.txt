HIF1α-KLF4 signaling dictates extra-erythrocyte expression of hemoglobin conferring sorafenib resistance in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Bo Zhang, Feichang Liu, Xinyue Gao, Yichao Zhu, Tong Chen, Jiahui Hao, Yuexian Wei, Zhuoran Sun, Ruigang Yang, Yalan Yang, He Ren, Yutong Li, Chunxiao Yu, Tianyi Ren, Yinuo Huang, Hong Jiang, Gerry Melino, Li Ma, Hongyan Huang, Qiang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer cells must deal with excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) to survive severe hypoxia and anticancer therapy; however, anti-ROS mechanisms other than the well-known NFE2L2/NRF2 signaling pathway are poorly recognized. Here, we report a ROS scavenging mechanism mediated by HIF1α-KLF4-induced hemoglobin extraerythrocytically expressed in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). We found that the ROS pathway was aberrantly activated in HCC and was associated with hemoglobin upregulation, which independently predicts poor outcomes. Network analysis further identified heme binding as the top ROS-associated functional module, suggesting a previously unrecognized role of hemoglobin in maintaining redox homeostasis in HCC. Hemoglobin expression in cancer cells is transcriptionally controlled by HIF1α via KLF4 but not HIF2α or the recently identified KDM5A-KLF1 signaling. The upregulated hemoglobin counteracts the detrimental effects of oxidative stress by scavenging ROS, promoting sorafenib resistance, which could be effectively reversed by interfering with hemoglobin expression, leading to tumor suppression. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments consistently supported the functional importance of hemoglobin in regulating oxidative stress adaptation and therapeutic response. Overall, a previously unrecognized mechanism was identified for cancer cell survival under oxidative stress, where HIF1α-KLF4 signaling induces hemoglobin to scavenge ROS produced during hypoxia and anticancer therapy, providing a promising target of synthetic lethality for cancer therapeutics.Subject terms: Cancer, Cancer

Introduction

With rapid and uncontrolled proliferation, solid tumors frequently experience local hypoxia in the tumor microenvironment caused by relatively insufficient angiogenesis and oxygen supply.1 In fact, multiple studies have indicated that hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) tissues are subjected to extreme hypoxia,2–4 which helps cancer cells within the tumor mass enhance angiogenesis,5,6 malignancy,7 and drug resistance.8,9 These gain-of-function effects of hypoxia require activation of a cascade of signal transduction that, primarily via hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), regulates a network of gene expression to cope with the detrimental effects associated with hypoxia, such as oxidative stress caused by excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS).10,11 A similar strategy is frequently adopted by cancer cells to counteract therapy, leading to compromised therapeutic effects or even complete drug resistance.

Recent progress in redox signaling has identified the NF-E2 p45-related factor 2 (NRF2) pathway as a prominent regulator of the oxidative response in multiple contexts, including cancers.12,13 Under homeostatic conditions, the level of NRF2, encoded by the nuclear factor erythroid-derived 2-like 2 (NFE2L2) gene, is kept at a low level by several E3 ubiquitin ligases, such as Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1), which constitutively targets NRF2 for proteasomal degradation. Upon oxidative stress, KEAP1 is inactivated by the modification of reactive cysteines, leading to NRF2 accumulation and translocation into the nucleus to drive the expression of antioxidative enzymes, such as NQO1 and GPX4, to protect cells against oxidative stress.14 In the meantime, evidence exists for anti-ROS mechanisms other than NRF2 signaling,15,16 accommodating the diversity and complexity of biological contexts. However, this group of non-NRF2 redox systems is poorly elucidated.

Hemoglobin is a specialized protein that is prominently expressed in erythrocytes, where it functions as a carrier of oxygen. In the shortage of oxygen supply, the expression of hemoglobin has also been detected in nonerythrocyte cells, such as macrophages,17 retinal pigment epithelium,18 and endothelial cells,19 although its functions remain to be further investigated in a context-dependent manner. In our recent study, hemoglobin was discovered within chondrocytes.20 The upregulation of hemoglobin in chondrocytes was responsive to hypoxia and dependent on KDM5A-KLF1 signaling rather than the HIF1/2α pathway. The deletion of hemoglobin in chondrocytes resulted in severe local hypoxia and extensive cell death in the central region of the cartilaginous tissue, which could be attributed to the absence of hemoglobin-controlled oxygen supply under hypoxic conditions.20,21 These studies indicate that hemoglobin outside erythrocytes carries tissue-specific functions and regulatory mechanisms.

In this study, we report in HCC the identification of a previously unrecognized anti-ROS mechanism, where HIF-1α, via KLF4, transcriptionally upregulated the expression of hemoglobin, a classical erythrocyte molecule, which plays a critical role in hypoxia adaptation. Mechanistically, hemoglobin protects HCC cells from apoptotic death by scavenging excessive ROS to ameliorate oxidative stress. Importantly, interfering with hemoglobin expression effectively sensitized cancer cells to sorafenib, an FDA-approved drug for HCC.22 Thus, our work identified a non-NRF2 redox mechanism and uncovered an essential role of hemoglobin in HCC, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.

Results

Activation of the ROS pathway in HCC with a poor outcome

To explore the major aberrations that affect patient outcomes in HCC, we analyzed the expression profile of human HCC samples from the public TCGA database with reference gene sets collected from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). The scores of 50 cancer-related hallmarks in each sample were quantitated (Fig. 1a). Among them, six hallmarks were significantly associated with patient survival by univariate Cox regression analysis23 (Supplementary Fig. 1a), and three were further confirmed to be independent prognostic factors by LASSO regression and multivariate Cox regression analyses (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). The ROS pathway, apart from the PI3K–AKT–mTOR pathway and coagulation pathway, was scored as the most influential risk factor (Fig. 1c).

To further investigate the functional implications of ROS-related genes, a scale-free coexpression network was constructed with the ROS pathway ssGSEA Z-scores and transcriptome data by the WGCNA algorithm.24 A total of 41 gene modules were generated by the soft threshold power of β = 9 (scale-free R2 = 0.98) (Supplementary Fig. 1d–g, Supplementary Table 1). Among these, the yellow module, containing 647 genes, showed a strong correlation with ROS scores (r = 0.48, p = 4e-07) and was designated the “ROS-related module” (Supplementary Fig. 1h). Then, pathway enrichment analysis was performed to confirm the representativeness of the ROS module. The results showed that these genes were significantly enriched in ROS-related pathways such as “regulation of superoxide anion generation” and “superoxide anion generation” (Supplementary Fig. 1i). Further intersection of the ROS-related module genes with the genes differentially expressed in cancer tissues over the noncancerous tissues produced a set of 38 signature genes (Supplementary Fig. 1j), which divided the enrolled patients into two clusters by unsupervised clustering with an optimal k of 2 (Supplementary Fig. 1k, l). As shown in Fig. 1d, the ROS ssGSEA score was significantly higher in cluster 2, and the HCC patients in cluster 2 exhibited a significantly shorter overall survival time than those in cluster 1 (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Table 2).

Hemoglobin expression correlates with the ROS pathway

Gene Ontology analysis identified a group of molecular-function terms known to be associated with ROS pathway activation, such as “positive regulation of lymphocyte activation” and “lymphocyte differentiation” (Supplementary Fig. 2a–c). Interestingly, among the ROS-associated functional modules, “heme binding” was identified as the top-ranking module (Fig. 1f). Correspondingly, the expression of Hba and Hbb, two heme-binding subunits of hemoglobin, was detected in HCC tissues and was significantly higher in ROS-high cluster 2 (Fig. 1g). Remarkably, HCC patients with higher levels of either Hba or Hbb displayed significantly shorter overall survival (Fig. 1h, i). To validate the RNA sequencing results at the protein level, immunofluorescence staining was performed on a tissue microarray (TMA) consisting of cancer tissues from human HCC with antibodies against HBA and HBB (Fig. 1j). Consistently, higher expression of either HBA or HBB was associated with shorter overall survival, as shown by Kaplan–Meier analysis (Fig. 1k, l, Supplementary Table 3). Notably, while these associations were clear, multivariate analysis suggested that the prognostic utility of HBA/HBB may be context-dependent (Supplementary Fig. 3), prompting us to further investigate its functional role in HCC pathogenesis.

Hemoglobin is expressed in malignant HCC cells

To circumvent potential confounding effects of hemoglobin expression in other cell types, such as the previously reported influence in vascular endothelial cells, we leveraged the advantages of single-cell sequencing by performing cluster annotation on cells isolated from 9 independent HCC patients. This systematic classification revealed 8 major cell populations (Fig. 2a, b). Subsequent analysis demonstrated that although low levels of hemoglobin-positive cells were detected in the populations of B cells, T cells, CAFs, TAMs, TECs, and unclassified populations (Fig. 2c, d), hemoglobin expression was predominantly enriched in malignant cells and HPC‑like cells (Fig. 2d, e). The malignant identity of the HBA/HBB-expressing cluster was confirmed by copy number variation analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Critically, this population did not coexpress canonical erythroid markers (Supplementary Fig. 4b–d), and the signal persisted after rigorous doublet removal (Supplementary Fig. 4e), effectively excluding contamination from red blood cells. Notably, the proportion of hemoglobin-positive malignant cells varied across individual patients (Supplementary Fig. 4f), indicating intertumor heterogeneity. This spatial distribution pattern was further validated using independent HCC patient-derived pathological specimens (Fig. 2f, g, Supplementary Fig. 4g), confirming the malignant cell-specific expression profile of hemoglobin.

HIF1α-dependent upregulation of hemoglobin in HCC cells

The unexpected extraerythrocytic expression of hemoglobin in HCC cells suggests a regulatory mechanism different from that in erythrocytes. To unveil the regulatory mechanism, we first performed ssGSEA with ROS-related pathways. Although NRF2 is a canonical ROS regulator,14,25 ssGSEA revealed a weak association between the NRF2 pathway and ROS activity in HCC (Fig. 3a–c), a finding corroborated by only a modest increase in intracellular ROS upon NRF2 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Moreover, NRF2 knockdown did not alter hemoglobin expression (Supplementary Fig. 5b–d). In contrast, HIF1α signaling emerged as the top‑ranked candidate, with GSEA confirming significant enrichment of hypoxia signaling in the ROS‑high cluster (Fig. 3d) and a positive correlation between the ROS score and HIF1α target genes (Supplementary Fig. 5e). Since this hypoxia‑enriched cluster exhibited elevated hemoglobin expression (Fig. 1g), we hypothesized that hypoxia might directly upregulate hemoglobin. Indeed, both Hba and Hbb were induced under hypoxia in most liver cancer cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 5f), where hypoxia signaling was intact, as indicated by the inducible expression of VEGF, a hypoxia-responsive gene.26 The regulation was also confirmed at the protein level by Western blot in Hep3B 2.1-7, SNU 387, and Huh 7 cells (Fig. 3e), where HIF1α was also upregulated coincidently. HIFs turned out to be involved in hemoglobin upregulation given that roxadustat (FG-4592), a stabilizer of HIFs,27 effectively upregulated hemoglobin expression and HIF1/2 protein levels (Fig. 3f, g). Specifically, HIF1α, but not HIF2α, is likely to be the major mediator of hypoxia signaling because the expression of hemoglobin and VEGF was only robustly induced by ectopically expressed HIF1α, but not HIF2α, in Hep3B cells exposed to hypoxia (Fig. 3i, j, Supplementary Fig. 6a), whereas little elevation was detected in Hep3B cells even exposed to hypoxia for 12 hours (Fig. 3h, Supplementary Fig. 6a). Consistently, depletion of HIF1α, but not HIF2α, rendered cells lose response to hypoxia-induced upregulation of hemoglobin and VEGF (Fig. 3k, l, Supplementary Fig. 6b) in Hep3B and Huh 7 cells. Collectively, these data indicate that hypoxia upregulates hemoglobin through HIF1α.

Hemoglobin expression is independent of KDM3A and KLF1

To delineate the mechanisms governing HIF1α-dependent hemoglobin regulation, we conducted chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-Seq) targeting HIF1α and HIF2α in HCC cells (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). Notably, no significant HIF1α/2α binding peaks were detected within hemoglobin gene loci (HBA1, HBA2, HBB) or their putative transcriptional regulatory elements (Supplementary Fig. 6e). Subsequent functional annotation of HIF1α/2α-bound genes identified 7,476 HIF1α-specific transcriptional targets (Supplementary Fig. 7a), which were distinct from HIF2α-associated genes, while revealing divergent pathway enrichment profiles between the two isoforms (Supplementary Fig. 7b–e). Among the top 10 most enriched HIF1α-exclusive targets (ranked by peak significance), KDM3A was prioritized for functional validation (Supplementary Fig. 7f) given that it is a JmjC domain-containing demethylase sharing homology with the hemoglobin regulator KDM5A20 and was previously linked to KLF2/5 transcriptional activation and oncogenic progression.28–30 However, despite hypoxia-inducible KDM3A upregulation (Supplementary Fig. 7g), ectopic KDM3A overexpression under normoxia (20% O₂) or hypoxia (1% O₂) failed to modulate hemoglobin transcription (Supplementary Fig. 7h, i). Notably, although ectopic overexpression of KLF1, a transcription factor driving hemoglobin expression in chondrocytes,20 robustly induced hemoglobin expression (Supplementary Fig. 7j, k), endogenous KLF1 protein was marginally detected, indicating low expression, in HCC cells (Supplementary Fig. 7l). Consistently, quantitative RT-PCR analysis demonstrated no hypoxia-responsive upregulation of KLF1 mRNA (Supplementary Fig. 7m). In summary, these data suggest the existence of a novel regulatory mechanism, other than the established KDM3A and KLF1 pathways, for hemoglobin expression in HCC.

HIF1-KLF4-dependent expression of hemoglobin in HCC cells

Given the critical regulatory functions of the Krüppel-like factor (KLF) family in erythroid maturation and hemoglobin expression,31,32 coupled with their potential compensatory mechanisms in transcriptional networks,33 we systematically interrogated the correlation between KLF gene expression profiles and hemoglobin transcripts. Multivariate regression analysis identified KLF2, KLF4, and KLF10 as positively correlated with hemoglobin expression, alongside the established regulator KLF1 (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). Subsequent differential expression analysis of HIF1α-dependent transcriptional targets, employing fold-change thresholds (log2FC > 4) and Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P values (FDR < 0.01), revealed statistically significant enrichment of KLF4 and KLF5 within the hypoxia-responsive gene signature (Fig. 4c). Functional validation through ectopic overexpression demonstrated that among the candidate KLFs (KLF2, KLF4, KLF5, KLF10), only KLF4 robustly augmented hemoglobin transcription (Fig. 4d, e, Supplementary Fig. 8c). Consequently, we focused on KLF4 and confirmed that its depletion markedly suppressed hemoglobin expression (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Fig. 6b). Notably, ChIP‑qPCR confirmed the direct binding of KLF4 to key regulatory regions of both the HBA and HBB loci (Supplementary Fig. 8d–f). Mechanistic interrogation revealed that HIF1α knockdown specifically abrogated hypoxia-induced KLF4 upregulation (Fig. 4g), suggesting HIF1α-dependent transcriptional control. Genomic interrogation via Homer-based motif (Fig. 4h) identified ten potential binding sites within the KLF4 locus (Fig. 4i, Supplementary Fig. 8g), with spatial concordance between predicted motifs and empirical ChIP-seq peaks. Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR) validated three functional HIF1α binding elements: one within the proximal promoter region and two localized to the second exon (Fig. 4j). Dual-luciferase reporter assays further demonstrated that the HIF1α binding site within the proximal promoter was critical for driving KLF4 expression under hypoxia (Supplementary Fig. 8h–i), confirming its functional importance. In agreement with a role of KLF4 in hemoglobin regulation, KLF4 is also positively correlated with the ROS signaling pathway (Supplementary Fig. 5e). Collectively, these data establish HIF1α-KLF4 as a hypoxia-responsive transcriptional axis governing hemoglobin expression in HCC cells.

Hemoglobin reduces intracellular ROS levels

Next, we explored the effects of hemoglobin on intracellular ROS levels in cancer cells. First, the cells were stained with the fluorescence probe DCFH-DA to detect ROS levels.34 Flow cytometry (FCM) analysis indicated that depletion of the two hemoglobin subunits, either alone or in combination, increased the number of DCF (DCFH oxidation)-positive cells, which was further potentiated under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 5a–d, Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). In contrast, overexpression of hemoglobin significantly increased the number of DCF-negative cells (Fig. 5e–g, Supplementary Fig. 9c, d). These results suggest that hemoglobin negatively regulates cellular ROS levels. Consistent results were obtained using the mitochondrial superoxide probe MitoSOX Red and the general ROS probe dihydroethidium (DHE) (Supplementary Fig. 10). Moreover, the ROS-reducing effect of hemoglobin was heme dependent, as inhibition of heme synthesis with succinylacetone (SA) attenuated this function (Supplementary Fig. 11a–c). Notably, the magnitude of ROS elevation upon hemoglobin knockdown was comparable to that observed after knockdown of canonical antioxidant enzymes such as catalase (CAT) or GPX4 (Supplementary Fig. 11d–f), underscoring its significant role in redox homeostasis. To demonstrate the regulation of ROS levels by hemoglobin more intuitively, HBA and HBB fused with fluorescent protein mCherry were overexpressed in Hep3B cells. Confocal microscopy imaging showed that the cells expressing HBA or HBB exhibited markedly lower DCF intensity than the adjacent control cells, indicating reduced ROS levels (Fig. 5h–i). This effect was likely a result of ROS scavenging directly by hemoglobin, as incubation with purified hemoglobin protein in vitro resulted in dramatic ROS reduction (Fig. 5j–k). Thus, these data demonstrate that hemoglobin is proficient in reducing intracellular ROS levels as a scavenger, which is consistent with the proposal that globin proteins such as cytoglobin may function as a type of catalytic dioxygenase to scavenge ROS.35,36

Hemoglobin protects cells from oxidative stress–induced death

Since excessive ROS levels strongly induce programmed cell death by ferroptosis,25 apoptosis,37 and necroptosis,38 thereby compromising tumor growth, we hypothesized that hemoglobin may inhibit cell death. To test this idea, a CCK8 assay was performed, indicating that hemoglobin depletion significantly suppressed cancer cell growth, which was further enhanced by hypoxia (Fig. 6a, b, Supplementary Fig. 12a–c) and rescued by the ROS scavengers edaravone39 (Fig. 6c–f) and acetylcysteine (NAC) (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b, 13a, b). Importantly, while ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) and necrostatin-1 (Nec-1), inhibitors of ferroptosis and necroptosis, respectively, had little rescue effects, the apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK (Z-VAD)40 and caspase3 (CASP3) knockdown effectively rescued cells from growth suppression induced by hemoglobin depletion (Fig. 6c, d, Supplementary Fig. 12d, 13c). These results suggest that hemoglobin is required for cancer cells to survive oxidative stress-induced apoptosis by hypoxia. Consistent with this notion, Pearson analysis indicated that hemoglobin ranks as the top antioxidant in negative correlation with a set of cell death programs, including apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 13d), and both ROS scavengers and apoptosis inhibitor/CASP3 knockdown significantly inhibited apoptosis of hemoglobin-depleted cells under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 6e–g, Supplementary Fig. 12e–i, 13e, f). Cell growth was not rescued by the autophagy inhibitor 3-methyladenine (3-MA) (Fig. 6c, d), which is consistent with the fact that autophagy is required for HCC cell survival.7,41 Similarly, the ROS scavenger amifostine failed to restore growth. This is explained by our dose-response analysis, which revealed that Amifostine itself exerts a growth-inhibitory effect under hypoxia at the concentration required for effective ROS scavenging (Supplementary Fig. 13g). This inherent cytostatic activity aligns with previous reports of its anti-proliferative effects.42

Hemoglobin inhibition breaches sorafenib resistance

Sorafenib is a tyrosine kinase inhibitor that has been approved by the FDA as the first-line drug for advanced HCC since 2008.43 However, its clinical efficacy is modest,43 primarily due to the development of drug resistance associated with hypoxia adaptation.44 Therefore, we wondered whether hemoglobin may be a feasible target for sensitizing HCC cells to sorafenib treatment. We analyzed the expression of hemoglobin in clinical samples of HCC patients with different sorafenib responses. The results showed that hemoglobin expression was significantly higher in sorafenib-nonresponder patients than in responder patients (Fig. 7a). As expected, the IC50 of sorafenib was profoundly reduced by nearly half upon hemoglobin depletion (Fig. 7b, Supplementary Fig. 14a). This effect could be ascribed, at least largely, to the sharply increased ROS levels (Fig. 7c, Supplementary Fig. 15a) and subsequent apoptosis (Fig. 7d–e, Supplementary Figs. 14b, c, 15b) given that either scavenging ROS by edaravone and NAC or blocking apoptosis by Z-VAD/CASP3 KD effectively restored the growth of hemoglobin-depleted cells from sorafenib-induced suppression (Fig. 7d, e, Supplementary Fig. 14d-g, 15c). A similar sensitization effect was observed with lenvatinib, another multikinase inhibitor, where hemoglobin knockdown also lowered the IC50 and potentiated drug‑induced ROS accumulation and apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 15d–f). Notably, while indicators of ferroptosis, such as lipid peroxidation, were elevated slightly (Supplementary Fig. 16a–c), the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 provided only minimal protection (Supplementary Fig. 16d), indicating that apoptosis is the predominant death mode. We further examined the impact on key signaling pathways and found that hemoglobin knockdown did not alter sorafenib-induced AKT phosphorylation but markedly enhanced ERK1/2 (MAPK) activation (Supplementary Fig. 16e), suggesting engagement of a distinct stress-response axis and that sensitization is unlikely to be achieved through synergistic inhibition of sorafenib’s canonical kinase targets. Accordingly, hemoglobin depletion significantly potentiated the suppression of tumor growth by sorafenib both in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 7f–g, Supplementary Figs. 16f–g, 17, 18). Thus, these data indicate that hemoglobin may be a potential synthetic lethality target for sorafenib treatment of HCC.

Discussion

Although excessive ROS levels are detrimental to cancer cells, accumulating evidence implicates ROS in multiple processes of tumorigenesis. A moderate level of intracellular ROS is beneficial to the development and progression of tumors.45,46 Consistent with this hypothesis, we identified activation of the ROS pathway as an independent risk factor for poor prognosis in patients with HCC. However, excessive ROS may induce cancer cell death via ferroptosis,25 apoptosis,37 or necroptosis.38 Therefore, fine-tuning of intracellular ROS levels is critical for tumor growth; nevertheless, the underlying mechanisms are not yet clear. While the NRF2 pathway is a well-established master regulator of the antioxidant response, anti-ROS mechanisms operating independently of NRF2 remain poorly characterized. In this study, we addressed this question by identifying a potential negative feedback loop between hypoxia and ROS, where HIF1α-mediated upregulation of hemoglobin is crucial for avoiding excessive ROS and subsequent oxidative stress, thereby protecting HCC cancer cells from apoptotic death and promoting tumor growth.

The extra-erythrocytic expression of hemoglobin has been reported in macrophages, retinal pigment epithelium, and endothelial cells,17–19 and we recently demonstrated that hemoglobin in chondrocytes is regulated by KDM5A-KLF1 signaling and functions in oxygen delivery.20 In contrast, we found that hemoglobin expression in HCC is independent of KDM5A and KLF1, but is instead driven by a HIF1α-KLF4 transcriptional axis. Notably, HIFs have not previously been shown to directly regulate adult hemoglobin, and the identification of KLF4 may represent a key link between hypoxia sensing and hemoglobin transcription. KLF4 has been found to play important roles in tumorigenesis, progression, and metastasis across various cancers,47–49 but its impact on tumor drug resistance has rarely been reported. Our discovery of a KLF4-mediated mechanism of drug resistance in liver cancer therefore expands the known functional repertoire of this transcription factor. Furthermore, the observation that KLF4 overexpression upregulates HBB exclusively under hypoxia while HBA is induced in a hypoxia-independent manner (Fig. 4e) suggests that additional cofactors or epigenetic modifiers may fine-tune subunit-specific expression, a question that warrants further mechanistic investigation.

Our finding that elevated hemoglobin expression correlates with poor prognosis may appear paradoxical given its ROS-scavenging function. However, this can be reconciled by considering hemoglobin as an adaptive survival factor rather than an oncogenic driver. This can be explained by viewing hemoglobin not as a driver of malignancy but as an adaptive component that sustains tumor cell survival under chronic oxidative stress. Aggressive tumors often operate in a high‑ROS state that promotes protumorigenic signaling while constantly risking the exceedance of a lethal threshold.50,51 Maintaining ROS within a pro‑survival “Goldilocks zone” is a key adaptive challenge for cancer cells.37 Our findings demonstrate that the HIF1α-KLF4‑hemoglobin axis may function as a cytoprotective brake, scavenging excess ROS to prevent apoptosis and thereby may secure this survival window. Consequently, high hemoglobin expression reflects successful tumor adaptation to a high-stress microenvironment, correlating with a more aggressive phenotype and therapy resistance. This framework also explains why targeting hemoglobin disrupts this balance and synergizes with ROS‑inducing therapies such as sorafenib and lenvatinib. The identification of the hemoglobin-ROS buffering mechanism shows promising potential in the treatment of HCC, particularly in combination with chemotherapeutic drugs, such as sorafenib, which utilize ROS to kill cancer cells. Critically, our functional experiments demonstrated that hemoglobin depletion potently enhanced the tumor suppressive effect of sorafenib (Fig. 7f, g), which was reversed by ROS scavenging by NAC, consistent with previous studies showing that ROS scavengers could promote drug resistance in HCC.9,52 Intriguingly, Xu et al. developed a hemoglobin–sorafenib conjugate to supply oxygen and iron for ferroptosis induction53; in contrast, we found that hemoglobin knockdown primarily induces apoptosis rather than ferroptosis, highlighting the context‑dependent role of hemoglobin in cell death modalities. This discrepancy suggests that endogenous hemoglobin may preferentially channel ROS detoxification toward inhibiting apoptosis, a hypothesis that merits further exploration. Additionally, we observed that hemoglobin knockdown did not alter sorafenib-induced AKT phosphorylation but markedly enhanced ERK1/2 activation, indicating that sensitization is unlikely to be achieved through synergistic inhibition of sorafenib’s canonical kinase targets but rather through engagement of a distinct stress-response pathway.

From a therapeutic standpoint, the HIF1α‑KLF4‑hemoglobin axis may represent a synthetic lethality target worth exploring. While HIF1α inhibitors exist, targeting the downstream hemoglobin node could provide an alternative strategy to disrupt redox adaptation in HCC cells that rely on this pathway. Moreover, molecular subtyping of HCC by hemoglobin expression may help identify patients who would benefit most from ROS-inducing therapies such as sorafenib or lenvatinib. The broader relevance of hemoglobin in oncology is supported by its detectable expression across multiple cancers (Supplementary Fig. 19), coupled with a notably low frequency of genetic alterations (e.g., ~5%, Supplementary Fig. 20a–c). This low mutational burden implies that hemoglobin’s oncogenic functions are likely driven by transcriptional and post‑translational regulation, as evidenced by the HIF1α‑KLF4 pathway described here and the coordinated downregulation of multiple PTM sites observed in HCC (Supplementary Fig. 20d). Future studies should explore whether similar hemoglobin‑dependent redox adaptation occurs in other malignancies and investigate the structural basis of hemoglobin’s ROS‑scavenging activity in the cytoplasmic environment of non‑erythroid cells.

In summary, we have identified a hepatocellular carcinoma-specific regulatory mechanism for hemoglobin expression through a HIF1α‑KLF4 transcriptional axis. This extra‑erythrocytic hemoglobin functions as an ROS scavenger to protect cancer cells from oxidative stress‑induced apoptosis, conferring resistance to sorafenib. Our findings not only reveal a previously unrecognized anti‑ROS mechanism independent of NRF2 but also establish hemoglobin as a potential therapeutic target for overcoming drug resistance in HCC. This finding adds to the growing recognition of context-dependent, extra‑erythrocytic hemoglobin functions. Collectively, our work not only identifies a targetable axis of redox adaptation in HCC but also provides a framework for investigating the noncanonical roles of hemoglobin in other malignancies.

Materials and Methods

Ethic statements

All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Institute of Bioengineering, Academy of Military Medical Sciences under approval number IACUC-SWGCYJS-2025-013 and were conducted in accordance with institutional and national guidelines for laboratory animal welfare.

The use of human hepatocellular carcinoma tissue microarrays (TMAs) was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Outdo Biotech Company (approval number: SHYJS-CP-1701002). All patient samples were obtained with written informed consent, and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cell lines

All cell lines were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% carbon dioxide (CO2), with cell media and supplementation as recommended by each cell line’s supplier. QGY-7703, SMMC-7721 and BEL-7402 cells were obtained from Ningbo Mingzhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd. HepG2, PLC/PRF/5, Huh7, Hep3B 2.1-7, SNU182 and SNU387 cells were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. All cell lines were tested for mycoplasma using the MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza) and had passage numbers of ≤10.

Mice

Male NCG mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). The animals were housed in individually ventilated polycarbonate cages under controlled environmental conditions (temperature: 20–26 °C/68–79 °F; humidity: 30–70%) with a 12-hour light/dark cycle. The facility maintained 100% HEPA-filtered air with regular air changes. Mice had ad libitum access to autoclaved rodent diet and reverse osmosis-purified municipal water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Academy of Military Medical Sciences and conducted in accordance with institutional and national guidelines for laboratory animal welfare. Animal health was monitored daily through clinical observation, with weekly assessments of body weight, food intake, and water intake.

Tissue microarray (TMA) staining and data analysis

These tissue microarrays were purchased from Shanghai Outdo Biotech Company (Cat No.: HLivH180Su11; OD-CT-DgLiv03-003) and contained 94/61 samples from HCC patients. These tissue chips were stained by tyramide signal amplification (TSA) technology. Details were described previously. A total of 107 tissue samples were qualified for analysis. After staining, images were captured by a Nikon Eclipse-TI inverted microscope. Regions of the parenchyma were identified, and the mean intensities of the hemoglobin signal were calculated automatically. The optimal cutoff value was calculated to divide patients into two groups for survival analysis.

Survival analysis

The correlation between the expression of genes and overall survival was identified using the R package “survival”. The optimal cutoff value for low expression versus high expression was automatically determined by the best cutoff model using the R package “survminer” (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/survminer/index.html). The log-rank test was used to calculate the p-value (p < 0.05), and the Kaplan–Meier plot of OS was depicted as well.

Single-cell RNA-Seq analysis and quality control

To validate the cell type-specific expression of hemoglobin (Hb) and rigorously exclude potential contamination, we reanalyzed a public single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset (GSE125449) from hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) patients. Cell type annotation was performed based on the expression of canonical lineage-specific markers as defined in the original publication. The expression profiles of hemoglobin genes (HBA1, HBA2, HBB) alongside these markers were visualized using dot plots. To control for ambient RNA contamination, immune cells (T and B cells) within the same samples served as internal negative controls, and the distribution of Hb gene expression was compared between malignant and immune cell populations using violin plots.

Cell culture and constructs

Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco, #11965092) was used to culture the QGY-7703, SMMC-7721, BEL-7402, HepG2, PLC/PRF/5 and Huh 7 cell lines. Hep3B 2.1-7 cells were cultured with minimum essential medium (MEM) supplemented with essential amino acids (complete medium, Procell, #CM-0102). SNU182 and SNU387 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 (RPMI-1640, Gibco, #11875119). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, #1248028). pBABE-HBA and pBABE-HBB were constructed by inserting the synthesized human HBA/HBB ORF into the pBABE or pBABE-mCherry vector by EcoR I and BamH I sites. Hep3B 2.1-7 was purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wu Han, China). Expression vectors for HIF1ɑ (#G113888) and HIF2ɑ (#G102862) were purchased from You Bio Corp. (Chang Sha, China). KLF2, KLF4, KLF5, and KLF10 overexpression vectors were purchased from Youbo Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

RNA interference

siRNAs were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Before siRNA transfection, cells (3 × 105/well) were seeded in 6-well plates. After overnight culture, the cells were transfected with 50 nM siRNA by Lipofectamine® RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, #13778-150). Six hours later, the cells were cultured with fresh media. The sequences of the siRNAs are described in the Supplementary Methods.

Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, #15596026) 48 h after siRNA transfection. TransScript® One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, #AT311-02) was used to convert total RNA into cDNA. cDNA was analyzed through quantitative PCR (qPCR) performed by SYBR Green Real-time PCR Master Mix (TOYOBO, #QPK-201) according to the instructions. NCBI Primer-BLAST has confirmed that primer pairs span at least two exons. Sequences of primer pairs as described in the Supplementary Methods. The PCR program was as follows: 34–38 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, 60 °C for 10 s and 72 °C for 15 s followed by initial denaturing (95 °C for 30 s). Melting curves were measured immediately after PCR amplification. Three independent biological replicates were performed for all analyses in this work. Fold changes among samples were calculated by the 2 − ΔΔCt method. HPRT was used as the reference gene.

Single-cell transcriptome data analysis

Gene expression files at the single-cell resolution for pancancer were obtained from the TISCH database (LIHC_GSE125449_aPDL1aCTLA4), and heatmaps of gene expression landscapes were constructed using the pheatmap package. Euclidean distance was used as a metric, and Ward’s method of minimum variance hierarchical clustering facilitated the identification of patterns and trends in the data, aiding in the recognition of conserved gene expression sources. Uniform manifold approximation and projection were used to visualize the gene expression data. The Nebulosa package was used to estimate weighted nuclear density, which allows for the recovery of lost gene signals by incorporating similarities between cells, allowing for the convolution of cell features and thus better visualizing single-cell data. To evaluate the differences in the expression of the HBA/HBB gene in different cell types, the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test was used. First, the expression level of the HBA/HBB gene in each cell type was calculated. The Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test was then used to assess significant differences in expression levels between different cell types. The cells expressing HBA/HBB were identified, then the cells were divided into positive and negative groups according to their expression levels, and then the proportion of each cell subpopulation in the two groups was calculated to observe which cell types contributed to HBA/HBB expression.

Correlation analysis between KLF family members and HBA/HBB

The gene expression profiles of HCC patients from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) were analyzed. We calculated the Pearson correlation between KLF family members and HBA/HBB expression levels.

ChIP-seq and ChIP-qPCR

Hep3B cells (5 × 10⁶) were seeded in 10 cm culture dishes and allowed to adhere overnight under standard culture conditions. Subsequently, the cells were subjected to hypoxia (1% O₂) for 20 h. Following hypoxia treatment, the cells were fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min at room temperature. The fixation was quenched by incubating with glycine (final concentration: 0.125 M) for 5 min, followed by three washes with ice-cold PBS. The fixed cell pellets were either stored at −80 °C for future use or immediately processed for chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) experiments. ChIP-seq libraries were constructed and sequenced by Wuhan Kangce Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). For ChIP-qPCR validation, chromatin immunoprecipitation was performed using the ChIP-IT® High Sensitivity Kit (Active Motif, Cat. No. 53040) strictly following the manufacturer’s protocol. Immunoprecipitated DNA was analyzed by quantitative PCR with target-specific primers.

Immunostaining and immunoblotting

Hep3B cells (3 × 105) were first cultured in 6-well glass bottom plates overnight followed by siRNA transfection. After 24 h, the cells were treated with hypoxia or reagents for 48 h. Cultured cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min, treated with 0.15% Triton X-100 for 3 min and washed with PBS. Nonspecific protein binding sites were blocked with 5% BSA for 30 min, followed by incubation with primary antibodies in humidified boxes for 12–16 h. After being washed with PBS for 5 min four times, the cells were incubated with secondary antibodies for 30 min and then washed with PBS for 5 min four times. Finally, the cells were sealed with fluorescent mounting medium with DAPI (ZSGB-BIO, #ZLI-9557). For the tissue microarray, tissues were deparaffinized at 75°C followed by a standard xylene-deparaffin procedure. After antigen retrieval by a microwave in EDTA antigen retrieval buffer (pH 8.0), the tissues were treated with procedures used in cell staining. For ROS detection, ROS reaction liquid was diluted in fresh culture media at 1:1000, and the original media was replaced and incubated for 1 h, followed by washing with PBS 2 times. An Ultraview Vox confocal system (Perkin Elmer) was used to capture images and analyze the data. Western blotting was performed following standard procedures.

The primary antibodies used were as follows: anti-HBA (Santa Cruz, sc-514378), anti-HBB (Santa Cruz, sc-21757), anti-HIF1α (CST, #36169), anti-HIF2α (CST, #7096), anti-KLF1 (Abalonal, A10581), anti-caspase-3 (CST, #9662), anti-cleaved caspase-3 (CST, #9661), anti-β-actin (Sigma, A5441), and anti-β-Tubulin (CST, #2146).

Flow cytometry

Apoptosis was assessed using an Agilent NovoCyte flow cytometer. Cells were stained with Annexin V-APC/Annexin V-FITC and PI. For automated compensation, single-stained controls were prepared: unstained cells, PI-only stained (using heat-fixed cells), and Annexin V-APC-only stained (using camptothecin-induced apoptotic cells). These control samples were acquired, and their data files were loaded into the respective slots within the Agilent NovoExpress software autocompensation module. The software automatically calculated the compensation matrix and applied it to all experimental samples. Data analysis was performed using NovoExpress software.

For ROS measurement, cells were incubated with a diluted ROS detection reagent for 1 h and analyzed via the BL1/FITC channel.

Cell viability detected by CCK8 assay

Hep3B cells (3 × 103) were maintained in 96-well plates for 12 h followed by siRNA transfection. After 12 h, baseline cells were measured, and then the cells were treated with hypoxia or reagents for 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h.

Measurement of cell viability using the CCK8 assay Cell viability was determined by the CCK8 assay following the manufacturer’s manual. The data were normalized using baselines. In addition, we used the STAR cell counter to read the number of live cells. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, APExBIO, K1018).

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and superoxide anion scavenging

H2O2 was treated with PBS or hemoglobin. The initial H2O2 concentration was 1 μM, and the cells were coincubated with hemoglobin at 0.125 g/L for 6 h at 37 °C. Then, the hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) concentration was detected by a titanium oxysulfate (TiOSO4) colorimetric assay.

The superoxide anion (O2−) scavenging activity of hemoglobin was assessed using DCFH-DA. The initial H2O2 concentration was 5 μM, and the cells were coincubated with hemoglobin at 0.125 g/L for 6 h at 37 °C. The superoxide anion (O2−) concentration was detected by DCFH-DA using a fluorescence microplate reader.

Bioinformatic analysis of hemoglobin expression in treatment response

Gene expression data from the public dataset GSE109211 were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. The raw or normalized expression matrix and the corresponding sample metadata were retrieved using the GEOquery R package. Samples were categorized into treatment responders and nonresponders based on the clinical response criteria provided within the dataset annotation.

Subsequently, the expression levels of hemoglobin subunit genes were extracted from the expression matrix. Differential expression analysis between the Responder and Nonresponder groups was performed using the limma R package. The statistical significance of the differential expression was assessed using a moderated t-test, and the resulting p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). A corrected p-value (adj.P. Val) < 0.05 and an absolute log2-fold change (logFC) > 0.5 were set as the thresholds to define statistically significant differential expression.

All statistical analyses and visualizations (such as box plots or violin plots) were generated using R software (version 4.5.1) and relevant packages, including ggplot2 and ggpubr.

Xenograft experiment

Male NOD-scid IL2R gamma null (NCG) mice (6 weeks old) were purchased from Gempharmatech Co., Ltd. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Academy of Military Medical Science.

To establish xenograft models, Hep3B cells (3 × 10⁷) in 100 μL serum-free DMEM were subcutaneously injected into the left armpit of each mouse. Three days post-inoculation, mice received intratumoral injections of Cholesterol-/2’-O-Methyl-modified siRNA (1 OD per mouse) every three days. Two days after the first siRNA injection, mice were administered vehicle or drugs (sorafenib, 30 mg/kg; edaravone, 20 mg/kg; NAC, 300 mg/kg; Z-VAD, 1 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection every other day. All compounds were dissolved in a vehicle of 2% DMSO, 40% PEG300, 5% Tween-80, and 53% saline.

Tumor volume was monitored every two days and calculated using the formula: Volume = (width² × length × π)/6. Mice were sacrificed on day 12, and tumors were harvested for subsequent Western blot and immunofluorescence analysis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All assays were carried out in triplicate or more. Data are expressed as the means with standard deviations (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM). P-values were calculated using two-tailed Student’s t test from Excel or GraphPad Prism software, and P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Data from public databases and TMA were analyzed by RStudio_0.98 software.
