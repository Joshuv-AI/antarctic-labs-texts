Heterologous vaccination with a next-generation vaccinia virus and Ad26 induces Ebola-specific immunity in macaques
Authors: Aneesh Vijayan, Tamara Cooper, Marijn van der Neut Kolfschoten, Gary Heinemann, Mirjam Van Kampen, Satish Boedhoe, Harry van Diepen, Jan Serroyen, John Hayball, Leanne Hobbs, Anne Collins, Jerome Custers, Roland Zahn, Ronald Vogels
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDifferent ways of combining virus vector-based vaccines can impact the effectiveness of immune responses against specific antigens. In this study, we explored the effects of different immunization schedules on immune responses elicited by viral vector-based vaccines in monkeys, with a focus on the heterologous regimens with a next-generation attenuated vaccinia virus, Sementis Copenhagen vector (SCV), and human adenovirus serotype 26 (Ad26) expressing the Ebola glycoprotein (EBOV-GP). Our findings highlight that the order of administration of these vaccines plays a pivotal role in modulating cellular immune responses. We observed that initiating immunization with Ad26.EBOV followed by SCV.EBOV, significantly enhanced the magnitude and quality of antigen-specific memory CD4+ T-cell responses when compared to a homologous Ad26.EBOV regimen. Importantly, the binding antibody titers induced by this heterologous approach were similar to that elicited by a homologous Ad26.EBOV regimen, although we observed a transiently elevated neutralizing antibody titers at the peak of the response for the SCV.EBOV/Ad26.EBOV regimen. Our results suggest that a heterologous vaccination strategy combining Ad26.EBOV and SCV.EBOV could be an alternative to the currently used Ad26/MVA platform for Ebola vaccination, potentially mitigating some of the manufacturing challenges associated with Modified Vaccinia virus Ankara (MVA). The observed benefits of the heterologous regimen, particularly its capacity to enhance T-cell responses, reinforce its potential as a promising alternative to homologous regimens that warrants further investigation.Subject terms: Biotechnology, Diseases, Immunology, Microbiology

Introduction

T-cell responses in acute viral infections have been illustrated to be effective in reducing disease severity and, in some instances, can lead to complete protection1–3. Therefore, strategies to improve the quality and the magnitude of T cell-mediated responses via vaccination are an area of active research. Potent T-cell responses elicited by vaccination with viruses modified to carry heterologous genes of interest has shown to be a promising approach4–6. These viral vectors possess mechanisms for efficient cell entry and protein expression and stimulate innate immunity, through pattern recognition receptor signaling, to support robust antigen presentation and adaptive responses. Additional modifications, such as deleting genes encoding immunomodulatory proteins or that aid viral replication, can attenuate these viral vectors to be safe or/and replication incompetent7–9.

Among prominent recombinant viral vector vaccine platforms, adenoviral (Ad) and vaccinia virus (VACV) vectors are the most extensively studied, with several of these candidates advanced into clinical trials and some being licensed for use in humans10–12. While standalone regimens based on Ad or VACV vectors have shown to be immunogenic, heterologous regimens using Ad/VACV combinations have often demonstrated higher immunogenicity in pre-clinical models as well as in the clinic11,13–16. However, strategies are being explored to further optimize the Ad/VACV heterologous regimens to enhance T cell responses. These include, but are not limited to the evaluation of novel vectors, immunization intervals, dose levels, and alternative routes of immunization17. A prominent strategy is the sequence in which the viral vectors are administered in context of a heterologous 2-dose regimen. Previously, we have shown in humans that Adenovirus 26 (Ad26.EBOV) induced immunity can be boosted with Modified Vaccinia virus Ankara (MVA-BN-Filo), a well characterized vaccinia virus vector, both expressing the glycoprotein (GP) of Ebola virus (EBOV). An in-depth immunogenicity assessment demonstrated a tendency for higher humoral and cellular response with a regimen of Ad26.EBOV followed by MVA-BN-Filo compared to giving MVA-BN-Filo as a first dose18.

In this study, we examined the effects of an alternative heterologous regimen consisting of Ad26 and Sementis Copenhagen vector virus (SCV) expressing EBOV-GP on immunogenicity. SCV is a novel next-generation viral platform derived from the Copenhagen strain of vaccinia virus19. Previous studies have shown that immunizing Rhesus macaques with SCV encoding immunogenic structural proteins from Chikungunya and Zika viruses potentially protects against viral challenge20. Importantly, compared to MVA-based vaccines, which require chicken embryo fibroblasts (CEF) for production, SCV is manufactured using a modified version of Chinese Hamster Ovary (CHO) cells, offering significant manufacturing advantages19.

Our study showed that the heterologous regimen of Ad26.EBOV/SCV.EBOV induced a greater number of polyfunctional memory CD4+ T cells compared to the SCV.EBOV/Ad26.EBOV or Ad26.EBOV/Ad26.EBOV homologous regimens. While the sequence of viral vector administration did not impact EBOV-GP specific binding antibody responses, the SCV.EBOV/Ad26.EBOV regimen showed transiently higher peak neutralizing titers. Overall, these findings suggest that a heterologous regimen starting with Ad26 followed by SCV is an effective strategy to enhance the quality of cellular responses, particularly polyfunctional memory CD4+ T cells, in non-human primates.

Results

Vector design and in vitro characterization

SCV.EBOV was constructed by insertion of the EBOV-GP coding sequence under control of a synthetic early/late promoter21 between the J2R and J3R ORFs of an SCV with existing deletions of D13L, B7R/B8R, A39R, and C3L (Fig. 1A). Deletions and EBOV-GP insertion were verified by PCR (Fig. 1B). Expression of EBOV-GP was confirmed by immunostaining plaques of SCV.EBOV with a monoclonal anti EBOV-GP antibody (Fig. 1C). Construction and production of vaccine stocks were performed in the suspension CHO-derived cell line engineered for manufacturing replication defective SCV vaccines19. Vaccine material was purified through a sucrose cushion, quantified by plaque assay, and tested for bacteria, fungi, mycoplasma, and endotoxin contamination. No microbial growth was detected in inoculated test mediums, mycoplasma was not detected by PCR, and endotoxin was less than 5 EU/mL.

SCV.EBOV elicited a dose-dependent increase in EBOV-GP specific antibodies in mice

To confirm immunogenicity of SCV.EBOV, BALB/c mice (n = 5/group) were vaccinated by intra-muscular administration. Three groups of mice were given two doses, 28 days apart, at dose levels of 105, 106, and 107 PFUs each. An additional group received a single dose of 107 PFUs of SCV.EBOV while the control group was immunized with 107 PFUs of SCV without any transgene (SCV.empty) (Fig. 2A). The EBOV-GP protein encoded by SCV is homologous to the one used in the licensed Ad26.EBOV Ebola vaccine18. Within two weeks of a single dose, a dose-dependent increase in EBOV-GP specific IgG antibodies was observed, with geometric mean titers of 15,908 for 105 PFU, 370,995 for 106 PFU, and 434,791 for 107 PFU of SCV.EBOV. At day 28, animals were boosted with a second homologous dose of SCV.EBOV, which boosted EBOV-GP binding antibodies at day 42 dose dependently by a factor of 124, 14, and 9.6, while no increase was seen in animals given a single dose of SCV.EBOV (Fig. 2B). No antigen-specific T cell responses, above background, were detected by IFNγ ELISpot after SCV.EBOV vaccination in mice (data not shown). These findings demonstrate that SCV.EBOV elicits a dose-dependent increase in levels of antigen-specific humoral responses in mice.

A boost with SCV.EBOV increased antigen specific cellular response in Ad26.EBOV primed non-human primates

Studies have shown that using a combination of different vaccine modalities (heterologous regimen) to deliver the antigen can enhance immunogenicity compared to using the same vaccine modality (homologous regimen)22–24. In this study, we aimed to evaluate immunogenicity of a heterologous regimen of SCV.EBOV and Ad26.EBOV vaccines compared to a homologous regimen of Ad26.EBOV vaccine in Cynomolgus macaques. The cohort consisted of 17 animals aged 5–6 years, which were allocated into three groups, each containing 5–6 animals. Two groups received a two-dose heterologous immunization regimen using SCV.EBOV (5 × 107 PFUs) and Ad26.EBOV (5 × 1010 VPs) viral vectors, wherein the order of immunization varied (Ad26.EBOV-SCV.EBOV or SCV.EBOV-Ad26.EBOV). The comparator group received a two-dose homologous regimen of the Ad26.EBOV (5 × 1010 VPs) vaccine (Fig. 3A). The vaccines were administered in an 8-week interval between each dose.

To assess cellular responses, first, an IFNγ ELISpot assay was employed. All animals receiving Ad26.EBOV as a first dose immunization responded in the first 8 weeks compared to baseline (Geometric mean response; GMR = 185 SFU/106 PBMCs; 95%CI 135–254). In contrast, a single dose of SCV.EBOV immunization did not result in a measurable response in the first 8 weeks (Fig. 3B), which is consistent with studies involving other recombinant vaccinia viral vectors and has been demonstrated to be dependent on the antigen25–27.

Three weeks after the second dose, all animals showed a significant increase in cellular responses compared to pre-second dose levels, regardless of the vaccine used. However, differences were observed between the study groups at this timepoint. The group that received the heterologous regimen of Ad26.EBOV followed by SCV.EBOV had a 3.5-fold higher response (GMR = 1653 SFU/106 PBMCs; 95% CI 827-3308) compared to the SCV.EBOV - Ad26.EBOV regimen (GMR = 479 SFU/106 PBMCs; 95%CI 267-859; p = 0.0076) and the Ad26.EBOV-Ad26.EBOV regimen (GMR = 464 SFU/106 PBMCs; 95%CI 203-1060; p = 0.0062). Although the response contracted up to week 24, it remained stable thereafter in the 7-month follow-up period in all study groups. Differences between the groups remained statistically significant for up to 4 months (Ad26.EBOV-Ad26.EBOV vs Ad26.EBOV-SCV.EBOV; p = 0.0166 and Ad26.EBOV-SCV.EBOV vs SCV.EBOV-Ad26.EBOV; p = 0.0211) after the second dose, thereafter differences were not statistically significant.

A heterologous Ad26.EBOV/SCV.EBOV regimen enhanced the magnitude and polyfunctionality of memory CD4+ T-cells

Analyzing subsets of antigen-specific T cells that produce multiple effector cytokines and other functional markers with intracellular cytokine staining (ICS) has become essential for studying the immune responses against viruses, as it is indicative of T cell potency28. We assessed the polyfunctionality of memory T cells specific to this antigen was assessed and quantified using COMPASS (Combinatorial Polyfunctionality analysis of Antigen-Specific T-cell Subsets)29,30.

The antigen specific expression of four functions (IFNγ, TNFα, IL2, and CD107a) was measured in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells using ICS. A significant increase in total antigen specific memory CD4+ and CD8+ T cells after the 2nd dose compared to pre-immunization levels was observed in both heterologous regimens: Ad26.EBOV/SCV.EBOV (CD4+ response; p = 0.00009; CD8+ response; p = 0.059) and SCV.EBOV/Ad26.EBOV (CD4+ response; p = 0.024; CD8+ response; p = 0.006), although no significant differences between different vaccine regimens at week 11 could be detect due to the high variation (Fig. 4A, B).

Next, COMPASS was used to analyze cell subsets based on the combination of the individual functions in the EBOV-GP specific memory T cell pool. This analysis reduced the subsets into single scores that reflect the posterior probabilities of an antigen-specific response. No significant increase in functionality scores (FS) for either CD4+ or CD8+ T cells was observed up to week 8 after a single dose of either Ad26.EBOV or SCV.EBOV (Figs. 4C, D and S1A). However, a significant increase in CD4+ FS was seen at week 11 following the second vaccination in all groups, regardless of the vaccine regimen. Notably, the heterologous regimen using Ad26.ZEBOV/ SCV.EBOV had a 1.5-fold higher CD4+ FS compared to the SCV.EBOV Ad26.EBOV (p = 0.0120) and Ad26.ZEBOV/ Ad26.EBOV (p = 0.0264) regimens. There were no significant differences observed in CD8+ FS between regimens at week 11 (Figs. 4D and S1B).

The polyfunctionality score (PFS), which considers the degree of functionality of different subsets, favoring higher order combinations of functions, increased after a single dose of Ad26.EBOV and SCV.EBOV for CD4+ T cells, but not for memory CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4E, F). Like the CD4+ FS, the heterologous regimen of Ad26.EBOV followed by SCV.EBOV resulted in a 2-fold increase in PFS at week 11, compared to SCV.EBOV/Ad26.EBOV (p = 0.0028) and Ad26.ZEBOV/Ad26.EBOV (p = 0.0009) regimens. The main contributors to the PFS for CD4+ T cells in the Ad26.ZEBOV/SCV.EBOV regimen at week 11 was primarily defined by the following phenotypes: CD107a+TNFα+IFNγ+IL2+, CD107a+TNFα+IFNγ+ and IFNγ+TNFα+ (Fig. S2).

The functionality of the EBOV-GP antibodies was assessed with Zaire-Makona pseudo-virus neutralization assay (psVNA). Discussion

In both animal models and humans, heterologous as well as homologous immunization with viral vaccine vectors can be effective. However, different strategies can enhance the immune responses generated by viral vectors like MVA or Adenovirus. In this study, we investigated the potential of SCV.EBOV, a newly developed vaccinia viral vector, in combination with Ad26.EBOV, to elicit specific responses against EBOV-GP in non-human primates. Our findings demonstrate that the order in which viral vectors are administered impacts the magnitude and quality of the cellular immune response in NHPs. Specifically, a heterologous regimen of Ad26.EBOV followed by SCV.EBOV resulted in a higher frequency of polyfunctional memory CD4+ T-cells compared to the reverse heterologous regimen or the homologous 2-dose Ad26.EBOV regimen. In contrast, all regimens induced a high magnitude of EBOV-GP specific humoral responses. These results contribute to the understanding of strategies to enhance T-cell responses while maintaining humoral responses when utilizing viral vectors in heterologous immunization.

Many prior investigations exploring these approaches have primarily focused on MVA, a third generation VACV that has been generated via serial passaging in chicken embryo fibroblast (CEF) cells, resulting in random mutations and deletions that rendered the virus host-range restricted and attenuated. Production of MVA in primary CEF cells is a labour-intensive process that does not allow manufacturing scaling or speed, and has an inherent risk of availability of CEF in pandemic settings. While recent advances in continuous avian cell line technology, such as AGE1.CR.pIX and EB66, have been developed, the current licensed MVA based vaccines still rely on CEF as manufacturing platform. SCV is a fourth generation VACV, engineered to be replication deficient through the single targeted deletion of D13L, a gene essential for virus assembly. The SCV technology is designed to capitalize on the potential of VACV as a safe, immunogenic, and stable vector with capacity for large transgene loads; and to address the issues of manufacturing scale and speed by using a proprietary, recombinant Chinese Hamster Ovary (CHO) manufacturing cell line. Furthermore, while the method used here to purify SCV employs traditional sucrose gradient centrifugation to produce research grade material, downstream purification technologies are being developed and will be integrated to provide a robust, scalable process for future clinical manufacturing. Here, we have shown in preclinical models that SCV may be an alternative boosting vector for Ad26 in the context of a vaccine against Ebola virus disease.

Correlations between antigen-specific polyfunctional T-cells and positive clinical outcomes have been reported in convalescent individuals following viral infections or vaccination3,28,31. Comparisons between Ebola survivors and non-survivors have shown that EBOV-specific T-cells and continuous T-cell cytokine expression are associated with disease survival32,33. Additionally, the association of EBOV-specific CD4+ T-cell dependent antibodies with protection has been demonstrated in preclinical Ebola challenge models34,35. We did not observe significant differences in the magnitude of CD4+ or CD8+ T-cell responses in the ICS assay upon comparing regimens at 3 weeks post 2nd dose. However, qualitative differences in T-cells were observed. While the regimens tested in this study elicited polyfunctional CD8+ T-cells, they were not significantly different from each other. In contrast, the heterologous Ad26.EBOV/SCV.EBOV regimen induced higher levels of polyfunctional CD4+ T-cells compared to other regimens. These polyfunctional CD4+ T-cells produced combinations of Th1 cytokines (IFNγ, TNFα, and IL2) along with CD107a+, a degranulation marker often associated with cytotoxic CD8+ T-cells. Notably, the induction of protective cytotoxic CD4+ T-cells positive for CD107a or perforins, during acute viral infections or vaccination, has been reported36–39. Importantly, vaccine-induced CD107a+CD4+ T-cells exhibit resistance to Simian Immunodeficiency Virus (SIV) infection40, suggesting that heterologous prime-boost strategies capable of eliciting these responses may have broader applications in HIV vaccine development. Future research is required to confirm the potential of the Ad26/SCV regimen to induce polyfunctional CD107a+CD4+ T-cells in other species and humans, and extend to other potential targets. A drawback of this study was the lack of assessment regarding the persistence of these polyfunctional T-cells beyond the peak of the response. Since there is temporal diversity in the polyfunctionality of T-cells, it is necessary to conduct further studies to monitor these T-cells over the long term, understand its anamnestic response, and evaluate their influence on efficacy41,42.

SCV.EBOV did not elicit detectable T cell responses when used as a prime in NHPs in our study. This is consistent with published observations for other poxvirus-vectored vaccines such as MVA and NYVAC, though this phenomenon appears to be antigen-dependent. For instance, MVA expressing the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein elicited statistically significant T cell responses after a single dose in NHPs, and MVA expressing Mycobacterium-derived antigens induced modest T cell responses in humans after single-dose vaccination43,44. While statistical significance was limited by small group sizes and inter-individual variability typical of outbred animals in our study, the Ad26.EBOV/SCV.EBOV regimen showed a consistent trend toward higher magnitude of EBOV-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses at the peak of the response post-boost compared to alternate regimens. This is reflected concordantly in both ELISpot and ICS assays, suggesting that Ad26 priming may optimize the quality and magnitude of the cellular immune response followed by SCV boosting.

The dynamics of the EBOV-GP antibody response did not show any differences between regimens, which aligns with findings from other studies. For instance, in humans, the long-term humoral response induced by the Ad26.EBOV and MVA-BN-Filo vaccines were found to be independent of the order of immunization18. Similarly, in our study, binding antibodies were not significantly different between regimens at any time point post second dose. We observed significantly higher psVNA responses elicited by the SCV.EBOV/Ad26.EBOV compared to the reverse regimen at the peak of the humoral response. Clinical data show that the peak psVNA titer with an MVA/Ad26 regimen was higher than the reverse regimen, while the durability of the response did not differ between the regimens. These findings indicate that while the SCV.EBOV/Ad26.EBOV regimen may transiently enhance neutralizing antibody responses compared to Ad26.EBOV/SCV.EBOV, the durability of humoral immunity appears comparable between regimens. Therefore, future studies with longitudinal assessment of neutralizing antibody response beyond the peak response timepoint would be important to determine whether the observed differences in peak psVNA titers persist or converge over time between regimens. Additionally, in previous studies, we demonstrated that the survival probability of NHPs immunized with Ad26.EBOV/MVA-BN-Filo, followed by Ebola virus challenge, was correlated with the presence of EBOV-GP binding antibodies. Immuno-bridging studies between preclinical models and humans further confirmed the protective role of EBOV-GP antibodies, serving as a foundation for the licensing of Ebola vaccines. Consequently, challenge experiments designed to assess the efficacy of the vaccine regimens tested in this study could provide further evidence of the clinical potential of the Ad26 and SCV regimen.

While a direct head-to-head comparison with MVA-BN-Filo would provide additional immunogenicity benchmarking, several factors precluded this comparison in the current study. MVA-BN-Filo expresses glycoproteins from three filoviruses (EBOV, SUDV, and MARV) and nucleoprotein from Taï Forest virus, thereby providing broader filovirus coverage compared to SCV.EBOV, which expresses only the EBOV Mayinga glycoprotein. These differences in construct design would confound direct immunogenicity comparisons. However, the extensive published literature on Ad26.ZEBOV/MVA-BN-Filo heterologous vaccination provides substantial benchmarking context confirming the potency of SCV.EBOV is in the range of MVA-BN-Filo11,45,46. In summary, our results indicate that priming with Ad26.EBOV followed by SCV.EBOV can positively influence the functionality of CD4+ T-cells targeting the antigen compared to a homologous Ad26.EBOV regimen. Additionally, our study suggests that the order in which the immunization regimens are given does not significantly influence the binding antibody responses, and peak psVNA response is significantly higher in SCV.EBOV/Ad26.EBOV regimen. These findings provide evidence for using a combination of Ad26 and a new vaccinia virus vector, SCV, in a heterologous regimen and further explore if this could be an alternative regimen to use as a preventive Ebola virus vaccine.

Methods

Ethics statement

The mouse study at the University of South Australia was conducted in accordance with the Australian code for the care and use of animals of scientific purposes. These studies were approved by the University of South Australia Animal Ethics Committee. The NHP study was conducted at the Alpha Genesis test facility and was approved by the IACUC committee of Alpha Genesis (IACUC#20-29).

Construction and characterization of viral vectors

Ad26.EBOV (Janssen Vaccines and Prevention) is a monovalent, recombinant, replication-incompetent, Ad26-vectored vaccine encoding the EBOV Mayinga GP.

To construct SCV.EBOV, the same EBOV GP was inserted between the J2R and J3R ORFs of SCV.empty (vaccinia Copenhagen strain (VACV-COP) previously modified to delete D13L, A39R, B7R/B8R and C3L genes) by homologous recombination and a transient dominant zeocin selection process described elsewhere6,47,48. Vaccine stocks were prepared by infection of recombinant D13 and CP77 expressing cells derived from CHO-S as previously described19,49. Harvested cells were lysed in 1% Tween80 in 10 mM Tris, pH 8, and the clarified supernatant was layered onto a 36% sucrose cushion and centrifuged at 3200 × g for 18 h at 10 °C. The virus pellet was resuspended in 10 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 8, and stored at −80 °C. The virus titer was determined by plaque assay using a modified D13 expressing 143b cell line (human osteosarcoma). Bioburden was tested by direct inoculation of fluid thioglycolate and soybean-casein digest broths (Millipore). Endotoxin was tested using the ToxinSensor™ Gel Clot Endotoxin Assay kit (GenScript) and mycoplasma with a PCR detection kit (SouthernBiotech).

For PCR analysis, DNA was extracted from vaccine stocks (NucleoSpin Tissue kit; Macherey-Nagel, Germany). PCR was performed using KAPA HiFi polymerase (Roche) with primer pairs for D13L (5′- gtgagtaccctggatacgaaataaa-3′ and 5′-aaccatctacagtatacgtttatattaaaactccgtttga-3′), EBOV GP (5′-aggtggtgaactatgagg-3′ and 5′-tgtccaccagttatcgtt-3′), and flanking the intergenic J2R/J3R EBOV GP insertion site (5′-ttccttttctaaacgattgggtgagga-3′ and 5′-tacataaacggtttatctaacgacacaacatcc-3′). Products were run on 1% agarose gels with RedSafe™ nucleic acid stain (iNtRON Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s directions. The J2R/J3R region containing EBOV GP was also verified by Sanger sequencing at the Australian Genome Research Facility (Adelaide, Australia).

To detect production of EBOV GP, monolayers of infected modified D13 expressing 143b cell line were fixed using 1:1 methanol/acetone solution and air dried. Immunostaining was performed with anti-Ebola surface glycoprotein [KZ52] (Absolute antibody product Ab006690-1.1) and peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse IgG antibody (Merck product A9044) diluted 1:1000 in 1% skim milk powder in phosphate buffered saline (PBS). Washes were performed with PBS, and 3,3′,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) Liquid Substrate System for Membranes (Merck) was used for colorimetric detection.

Animals and immunization

In the mouse study, groups of 5 female BALB/c mice aged 6-8 weeks old were administered vaccines through intramuscular injection in a total volume of 50 mcL per hind leg (100 mcL per mouse). The study consisted of 2-dose homologous regimen groups, in which each group was immunized with different amounts of SCV.EBOV : group 1 received 105 PFUs, group 2 received 106 PFUs, and group 3 received 107 PFUs. These doses were given on day 0 and again on day 28. In addition, there was a single dose regimen group (group 4) that received 107 PFUs of the vaccine. The control group was given two doses of 107 PFUs of SCV without any transgene (SCV.empty) on days 0 and 28. Throughout the study, serum samples were collected from the mice to assess antibody levels.

In the NHP study, healthy cynomolgus macaques aged 5–6 years old (n = 5–6/group), of mixed gender, were chosen. The animals in the heterologous regimen group were given Ad26.EBOV (5 × 1010 VPs) or SCV.EBOV (5×107 PFUs) at week 0, and then vice-versa in the eighth week. The homologous immunization group received two doses of Ad26.EBOV at the same dose-level and interval. The vaccines were administered intramuscularly with a volume of 500mcL into the deltoid muscle. Throughout the study, serum and whole blood samples were collected at scheduled timepoints. Animals were sedated with either ketamine hydrochloride (10–20 mg/kg) IM or tiletamine- zolazepam (5–8 mg/kg) IM for all procedures. The test item formulation injection sites were monitored closely. The hair at the site of injection was clipped at least two days prior to immunization. The injection site was marked with indelible ink and scored according to the Draize Grading scale. Blood was collected from the femoral vein using a 22 G 1-inch needle, vacutainer sheath, and collection tube. Following venipuncture, manual compression of the vein was maintained until haemostasis is achieved. Blood sampling was based on weight of the animals and time points but never exceeded the maximum volume as set forth by IACUC.

NHP IFNγ ELISpot

For non-human primate studies (NHPs), EDTA-anticoagulated whole blood was shipped overnight at ambient temperature to Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated using Ficoll-Paque and counted using a Guava EasyCyte Plus. The assay was performed in triplicate, with 200,000 cells per stimulation condition. Multiscreen 96-well PVDF-membrane plates were coated with anti-human IFNγ (B27; BD Pharmingen). The plates were then washed three times with PBS containing 0.25% Tween 20 and blocked for 2 h with RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS. Freshly isolated cells were stimulated with 158 overlapping 15-mer peptide pool (JPT Technologies) (11-amino acid overlaps) of the EBOV-GP protein (at a concentration of 1 mcg/mL) to detect antigen-specific responses, while medium stimulation was used to measure background response. As a positive control, cells were stimulated with phytohemagglutinin-M (PHA-M). After incubation for 18 h at 37 °C, plates were washed nine times with PBS containing Tween-20, followed by a single rinse with distilled water. Subsequently, they were incubated for 2 h at room temperature with 2 mcg/mL of biotinylated rabbit anti-human IFNγ antibody (U-CyTech Biosciences). After six additional washes with PBS-Tween, plates were incubated for 1.5 h with 1:500 dilution of streptavidin–alkaline phosphatase (Southern Biotechnology). Plates were then washed five times with PBS-Tween and three times with PBS. Plates were developed with bromochloroindolyl phosphate–nitro blue tetra-zolium (BCIP-NBT) substrate, and the reaction was terminated by rinsing with tap water. Plates were air-dried and analyzed using an ELISpot reader with dedicated analysis software (Cellular Technology Limited). A response was considered positive according to the assay positivity criteria if the background-subtracted values were greater than 100 SFU/106 cells and exceeded two times the medium response for each animal. Any values below the threshold of assay positivity were set to 100 SFU in the figures and for statistical analysis.

NHP multiparameter flow cytometry

Cryopreserved PBMC were thawed and allowed to rest at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment for 4 h. Afterward, PBMC were incubated for 6 h in the presence of: RPMI with 10% fetal bovine serum (unstimulated), Staphylococcus enterotoxin B (SEB) as a positive control, or peptide pool spanning the entire EBOV-GP protein. All cultures had a protein transport inhibitor called monensin (GolgiStop; Becton, Dickinson and Company) and 1 μg/ml of anti-CD49d (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Cat# 340976). The cultured cells were then stained with a cell viability marker and predetermined quantities of antibodies (BD Pharmingen) against various markers such as CD3-R718 (clone SP34.2; Cat#566955), CD4-BV711 (clone L200; Cat#563913), CD8-FITC (clone SK1; Cat#347313), CD45-BV786 (clone D058-1283; Cat#563861), CD95-PE.CF594 (clone DX2; Cat#562395), CD28-PE.Cy7 (clone 28.2; Cat#25-0289-42 from Life Technologies), CD107a-BV421 (clone H4A3; Cat#562623), TNFα-BV650 (clone Mab11; Cat#563418), IFNγ-BUV395 (clone B27; Cat#563563), IL2-APC (clone MQ1-17H12; Cat#554567). Samples were only included if they had at least 1000 viable CD4+ or CD8+ T cells. Gating was performed as described previously50. Memory T cell subsets were defined based on CD95 and CD28 expression as follows: naïve T cells (CD95−CD28+), central memory T cells (CD95+CD28+), and effector memory T cells (CD95+CD28−). Finally, the samples were analyzed using an LSR II instrument (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ) with FlowJo v10.8.1 software.

The antigen-specific T-cells generated by the vaccination regimen were evaluated using an R package called COMPASS (Combinatorial Polyfunctionality Analysis of Antigen-Specific T-cell Subsets), as described29. The model provides a posterior probability of specificity for each cell subset and each sample, which can be used to profile a subject’s immune response to external stimuli such as infection or vaccination. COMPASS provides two novel scores: the functionality score (FS) and the polyfunctionality (PFS) scores. These scores summarize, as a single number for each subject, the posterior probabilities of Ag-specific response across cell subsets. FS is defined as the proportion of antigen-specific subsets detected among all possible combinations. PFS is similar, but it weighs the different subsets by their degree of functionality, with more weightage for higher degrees of functionality.

ELISA

The EBOV-GP ELISA for NHP serum was performed at TBRI (San Antonio, USA). The method was described previously51. Briefly, Maxisorp 96-well plates (Nunc-Immuno) were coated with 10 mcg/mL of Galanthus nivalis lectin (GNA; Sigma-Aldrich) diluted in PBS at 4 °C overnight. After incubation, the solution was discarded, and the plates were blocked with 200mcL of PBS containing 10% FBS at room temperature for 90 min. Plates were washed twice manually with 200 mcL of PBS containing 0.2% Tween-20 (PBS-T). Supernatants containing recombinant filovirus glycoprotein (GP) were added and incubated for 90 min at RT, followed by three manual washes with PBS-T. NHP serum samples were serially diluted in 4-fold steps in sample buffer (PBS containing 0.2% Tween-20 and 1% FBS), starting at a 1:50 dilution, using round-bottom polypropylene plates. Subsequently, 100 mcL of each diluted sample was transferred to the ELISA plates and incubated for 90 min at RT. Plates were then washed six times with PBS-T. Bound IgG was detected using horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-human IgG (H + L) (Millipore, USA) diluted 1:5000 in sample buffer and incubated for 1 h at RT. Plates were washed six times with PBS-T prior to the addition of o-phenylenediamine (OPD; Sigma-Aldrich), followed by incubation in the dark for 10 min. The reaction was stopped, and absorbance was measured at 492 nm. Half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) values were determined using a four-parameter logistic curve fit. Results were normalized against a filovirus GP strain-specific reference serum and expressed as ELISA units (EU)/mL.

Zaire-Makona psVNA

The serum samples were analyzed at Monogram Biosciences in San Francisco, California for neutralizing antibodies using a previously described method45. In summary, a replication defective retroviral vector containing a luciferase gene was co-transfected into human embryonic kidney 293 cell cultures along with an expression vector containing the envelope glycoprotein sequences of the Ebola virus (specifically the EBOV Zaire Makona strain from 2014). Pseudovirus stocks were collected and incubated with serum samples at various dilutions for 18 h. The mixture was then used to infect HEK 293 cell cultures. The ability of antibodies in the serum to neutralize the infectivity of the Ebola virus was assessed by measuring luciferase activity approximately 72 h after viral incubation, comparing it to a control infection using a pseudotyped virus with the envelope from murine leukemia virus (aMLV). The neutralization titers were expressed as the reciprocal of the serum dilution that inhibited virus infection by 50%.

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons between different immunization regimens were conducted using various tests. EBOV-GP specific binding antibodies were assessed by ELISA, NAb titers were measured by psVNA, and cellular responses were assessed by IFN-γ ELISpot and multiparameter ICS assays. The immunization regimens were compared to each other post-second dosing (inter-group analysis) or between timepoints within the same group (intra-group analysis). The statistical tests performed to evaluate these comparisons were the two-sample t-test or a paired t-test for the ANOVA model, or in the case of censored values, a z-test from the Tobit model. To account for multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied. The statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, US). All statistical tests were conducted with a two-sided approach at an overall significance level of α = 0.05.
