Barriers and facilitators of deceased organ donation among Pakistanis living globally: a systematic review
Authors: Britzer Paul Vincent, Zeeshan Ghaffar, Asmaa Al-Abdulghani, Ifza Taaruf, Grace Idam, Gurch Randhawa
Published: 2025

Introduction

Most individuals with end-stage organ failure require a transplant which offers a good quality of life compared with other supportive treatments, like dialysis, for instance.1 Also, transplant is the most cost-effective option to treat end-stage organ failure.2 While all of this evidence proposes transplant over other supportive treatment as the best option,3 there is a substantial global disparity between the number of individuals willing to donate organs and the number of patients requiring organ transplants.4 This gap is even wider in countries with the highest hypertension and diabetes, such as in South Asia, which, if not managed, leads to end-stage organ failure and ultimately requires organ transplantation.5,8

Pakistan is one of those South Asian countries with the highest diabetes and hypertension,9 10 the leading cause of end-stage renal disease. Chronic kidney disease has been ranked as the 10th leading cause of death in 2020 which is expected to rise to the fifth leading cause of mortality in 2040 and the low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are expected to bear 70% of this burden.11 In addition to this, the prevalence of hepatitis C in Pakistan is 4.8% making it the second largest worldwide,12 where chronic hepatitis ends up in cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, requiring liver transplantation. Also, the demand for liver transplants is expected to rise by 23% in 20 years.13 With such a higher demand for organs for transplant in a region like Pakistan, there is a significant shortage of organs for transplant.14

Since the start of organ transplantation in Pakistan, it has been highly reliant on living donation compared with deceased organ donation, thereby limiting the ability to reach self-sufficiency in organ donation and transplantation.15 Based on a recently published paper,15 the exact statistics of the number of deaths due to the lack of transplants are not available. However, in Pakistan, with around 241.5 million population, estimated figures show that the annual need for various organs in Pakistan is: kidneys 25 000, livers 100 000, hearts 7000, pancreas 2000 and lungs 2000.16 17 Hence, to address such a demand for organs for transplant, in 2007, an ordinance (a temporary law) was promulgated by the President of Pakistan to allow the practice of deceased organ donation in Pakistan following this, the Transplantation of Human Organ and Tissues Act was enacted in 2010.18 However, despite efforts, a huge gap continues to exist.

There is a large Pakistani diaspora around the world,19,21 and studies have shown that when individuals migrate to another country, they often carry their cultural and belief systems with them.22 23 In the UK, organ donation continues to be lower among the South Asian population.24 25 A qualitative study has shown how Pakistanis carry out their culture and belief systems, influencing organ donation behaviour while living in the UK.26 Therefore, there is a need to understand what evidence suggests are the barriers to deceased organ donation from the perspectives of the public, not only in Pakistan but the whole Pakistani diaspora who live around the world. Hence, this systematic review aimed to understand the barriers and facilitators towards deceased organ donation among Pakistanis, not only living in Pakistan but globally.

Methods

Protocol and registration

The protocol of this systematic review has been registered with PROSPERO (CRD42022346343).27

Search strategy

The search strategy was developed using key concepts and MeSH terms in alignment with the PCC framework (Population—Pakistanis living globally; Concept—deceased organ donation; Context—barriers and facilitators). The search terms were informed by previously published reviews on organ donation2223 28,30 and were adapted to suit the needs of the current review in collaboration with a library specialist (online supplemental file 1). Studies were retrieved from databases including CINAHL, Medline with full text, Global Health and PsycINFO via EBSCO, Scopus via Elsevier, Web of Science via Clarivate and PubMed via the US National Library of Medicine, National Institutes of Health.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The studies included in this systematic review were selected based on specific criteria. They focused on male and female participants aged 18 and above, as this is the age at which individuals in Pakistan can independently choose to register as organ donors. The review considered studies conducted among Pakistanis both within the country and globally, across various settings such as educational institutions, religious venues, hospitals and workplaces. Only studies employing cross-sectional or qualitative methods were included, and the scope was limited to deceased organ donation rather than living donation, specifically focusing on solid organs. Studies published only in English between 1 January 1995 and 31 July 2024 were considered.

Data screening

The 11 944 retrieved studies were initially exported to Rayyan (https://www.rayyan.qcri.org), where duplicates were removed before undergoing title, abstract and full-text screening. The number of studies was then split into two halves, where the first half was screened in a double-blind manner by two reviewers (AA-A and GI) and the second half was screened in a double-blind manner by two reviewers (ZG and IT). In cases of disagreement between the reviewers, a fourth reviewer (BPV) assessed the studies to reach a consensus. The reasons for excluding several studies during the full-text screening are detailed in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flowchart (figure 1). After completing the step-by-step screening process, a total of 26 studies were included in the review (table 1).

Quality assessment

All included studies were assessed for quality using the Joanna Briggs Institute tool31 (tables 2 and 3), which has been employed in numerous other systematic reviews, including those focused on organ donation.22 23 Separate tools were used to evaluate cross-sectional and qualitative studies. Four reviewers (AA-A, GI, ZG and IT) conducted the initial quality appraisal, which was then reviewed by a fifth reviewer (BPV). There were no disagreements between the reviewers regarding the quality assessment of any study. No studies were excluded based on their quality. However, the quality appraisal for each study is provided (tables2 3) to allow readers to make their judgement on the credibility of the findings.

Data synthesis

The narrative synthesis method outlined by Popay et al was employed to synthesise the data extracted from both quantitative and qualitative studies.32 This method facilitates the integration of findings from both types of studies (quantitative and qualitative) within systematic reviews. It involves four key steps: (1) systematic searching and quality appraisal, (2) grouping and clustering of the included studies, (3) developing a textual summary for each study and (4) assessment and interpretation. Once the screening process was completed (figure 1), all included studies underwent quality appraisal (tables2 3). This approach has been widely used in other systematic reviews on organ donation.22 23 33

Numerical findings from all cross-sectional studies were organised under relevant headings, while the findings sections of the qualitative studies were coded using NVivo V.11 (Lumivero, 2015, NVivo V.11, https://lumivero.com/). The coded findings were then examined in relation to the quantitative data. To gain a comprehensive understanding of the interrelationships across the studies, factors such as the study topic, type, methodology, findings, research settings and studied population were considered. The full syntheses of both the quantitative and qualitative studies were thoroughly reviewed to capture the collective evidence that either supported or refuted key themes.

Results

A total of 26 studies were included in the review, comprising 3 conducted in the UK and 23 in Pakistan, with a combined sample of approximately 7326 participants. The studies included were undertaken across a range of settings such as universities, public spaces and hospitals. The majority employed a quantitative research design (n=20), while six adopted a qualitative approach. Based on the narrative synthesis,32 the findings of the review are presented in five themes, namely: (1) knowledge of deceased organ donation, (2) willingness toward deceased organ donation, (3) collective decision-making overriding individual’s preferences, (4) religious uncertainty and its impact on deceased organ donation and (5) trust and the healthcare systems.

Knowledge of deceased organ donation

Most participants were aware of the term ‘organ donation’ and its potential to save lives,34,43 yet fewer had adequate knowledge despite general awareness.4143,48 Awareness of organ donation legislation correlated positively with knowledge,49 and participants largely supported legal frameworks to ensure ethical practice.41 43 46 However, most were unaware of existing laws governing organ donation in Pakistan34 39 44 45 50 51 and the registration process or official donor registry,36 49 50 52 with the latter cited as a major reason for not registering.52

The majority of the participants favoured living donation compared with deceased organ donation.35 47 51 One of the reasons was the lack of understanding of brain death. This was lower among the general public than healthcare professionals or hospital patients.41 43 50 53 However, the kidney and eye were the most recognised transplantable organs and awareness of their donation influenced willingness to donate.3536 42,44 46

Mass media, particularly television and social media, were the main source of information,2634,37 39 42 43 45 while encounters with donor families, religious leaders or healthcare professionals were rare. Public figures in social media had a significant impact on awareness, as reflected in a participant’s statement: “I only knew that one can donate his one kidney to another person, after the death of [name of a famous person], a debate was going on regarding his decision of donating his organs … I was surprised initially …. but I liked the idea”.50

Exposure to personal stories/conversations with organ donation was uncommon,3537 41 43 45,47 yet knowing someone who had pledged, received or donated an organ had a positive influence.38 50 One participant shared: “One of my friends has already signed the donor card. He discussed with me on the importance of this donation, and because of him, I am already interested to donate my organs”.50 Also, limited visibility and unclear religious perspectives further hindered awareness, highlighting the need for education.26 35 39 46 52 55 56 However, overall, knowledge correlated positively with willingness to donate.38 52

Willingness towards deceased organ donation

The primary reason most participants chose to donate organs was to save lives and help others.3435 40 46,49 57 Organ donation was seen as morally right,50 53 with some viewing posthumous donation positively.50 52 One participant noted, “It would be morally right for me if I donate my organs after death”,50 while another opposed it, believing, “Our life is a gift to us by Allah … On the day of judgment, we will need to answer about our bodies”.50 Hence, while most were willing to donate,37 38 42 43 45 47 48 50 53 57 58 this was not always the case.39,4149

Most participants had no preference for recipients,40 42 though some prioritised young people,44 family members3435 37 42,44 46 47 49 or those sharing their religious beliefs.51 However, while family and people sharing the same religion were prioritised, among those unwilling to donate,34 44 fear of family reaction and religious uncertainty were the key reasons significantly influencing decisions unfavourably.43 48 49

While monetary benefits were positively accepted by some participants, only a small proportion considered them a factor that would influence them to donate.34 35 40 43 46 47 Some are willing to buy organs for loved ones in distress,42 51 one participant stated, “If someone is giving an organ for free then I will accept, and if the donor demands money, then I will give it”.36 Many who initially supported buying later preferred a deceased donor, believing it caused no harm and brought spiritual rewards.51 More participants were willing to receive than donate organs,26 39 41 43 55 57 often due to desperation.55 Empathy was seen as a motivating factor, with one participant stating, “We should think about the illness of other people … and then only we will think about donating organs”.50

Pakistanis in the UK often shape their views based on experiences in Pakistan, where organ donation is linked to backlash and trafficking concerns. One participant highlighted this by saying, “In Pakistan, if someone’s got an organ donation, then something dodgy happened at some point”.26 However, to tackle this, the majority believed raising awareness,52 increasing donor-family interactions,39 expanding donor numbers,52 implementing stricter laws41 43 46 49 and allowing out-of-family donations could reduce trafficking.36 52 Self-identity and empathy also played a role,58 with a favourable attitude increasing willingness.54 However, the major lack of awareness within the community negatively impacted attitudes and willingness,34 36 38 43 46 47 50 58 and actual donor registration remained low.38 40 52

Collective decision-making overriding individual’s preferences

Although there is a correlation between willingness to discuss organ donation and willingness to donate,52 58 most study participants had not engaged in any conversations about the subject and were apprehensive about discussing it with family members.26 35 41 52 A key challenge cited was the anticipated resistance from parents when presenting the topic, especially when it involves talking about one’s death. One participant commented: “Organ donation is a good idea, but I think …. my parents will dislike my decision. Also, it is difficult to talk about death issues with the family”.50

While most participants personally viewed organ donation as a noble act, many preferred to honour their family’s wishes/preferences.50 55 57 One participant shared: “I highly acknowledge the decision and the opinion of my parents. Even though I feel it is a great cause to save another person’s life, yet I am afraid … I’ll not consider being an organ donor after my death as it will go against my parents”.50 This preference extended not just to conversations and registration, but also to consent. Participants often felt that family members should have the ultimate authority to grant consent.26 41 46 48 51 In another study, many participants stressed the importance of family consent alongside the donor’s approval. While some interviewees used the English term ‘family’, many opted for Urdu expressions such as ‘ghar walay’ (household members) or ‘khandan’ (extended family), indicating the broader family unit.51

Those who prioritised family consent over the donor’s wishes emphasised the family’s role as the ‘waris’ (successor) of the deceased, a role which bestows sacred and moral responsibilities concerning the body. Conversely, participants who prioritised the donor’s wishes referred to the religious-legal concept of ‘wasiyat’ (will) and the family’s moral duty to respect it.51

Religious uncertainty and its impact on deceased organ donation

Although knowledge about organ donation was higher among Muslims, their attitudes and practices were less favourable compared with other religious groups.38 Religious views played a significant role in the decision-making process regarding organ donation, often taking precedence over all other considerations.26 41 42 49 While most participants who were unaware of their religious leaders’ views personally believed that organ donation was a noble act that should be promoted and supported,51 the majority still felt that their religion opposed it.41 One participant who was uncertain expressed: “I do want to sign the card, but I feel … my religion prohibits me. That’s why I am reluctant”.50

The proportion of participants who believed organ donation is not allowed in Islam was nearly equal to those who believed it is permitted.34,3744 46 57 Participants cited several religious reasons for not becoming donors or granting consent, including perceived religious prohibition, lack of visibility on the religious standpoint, the belief that the body belongs to God, and concerns about burial timing.26 36 43 51 Worries about the disfigurement of the body following organ donation were also common.3537,39 41 50 51 53 55 One participant explained: “I want the integrity of the body after death. After death, on the day of judgment when I will wake up, I will have no organs. Also, I am not comfortable when I think of cutting a dead body and taking organs after death. A dead body should be in rest after death”.50

The uncertainty surrounding the views of religious leaders acted as a barrier, preventing participants from fully supporting their positive stance on organ donation.40 41 45 49 51 55 Many participants who were unsure of their religion’s stance were also reluctant to share their personal opinions.51 Overall, while awareness of religious leaders’ favourable views on organ donation positively influenced participants’ willingness to donate, the limited visibility of these views negatively impacted their willingness.39 44 47 52 58

Trust and the healthcare systems

Participants had trust issues with hospitals,26 36 41 46 which negatively affected their willingness to donate organs. One participant stated: “I do not trust the hospitals … if I want to donate organs … If there is a reliable organisation which can assure me that my organ will not be wasted or sold in the black market, then I will donate my organs to that organisation”.36 Trust issues with doctors stemmed from perceptions that care for critically ill patients might be compromised due to doctors prioritising organ recovery, potentially withdrawing mechanical support prematurely. There were also concerns about doctors engaging in the illicit procurement of organs for profit.41 Additionally, a significant proportion of participants expressed concerns about the misuse of donated organs.35 46 47 57

Due to public distrust of healthcare professionals, doctors and nurses were reluctant to seek consent from families, fearing that it could harm their relationship with the potential donor’s relatives.41 53 However, most members of the public felt that doctors should properly educate families and allow them to make informed decisions.47 Healthcare professionals emphasised the importance of establishing an ethics committee to oversee organ donation practices, which they believed could improve public trust.52 They also highlighted the need for sufficient training to enable them to conduct these conversations without damaging trust with the families.41 Moreover, increasing the number of government transplant centres was suggested as a means to make transplants more accessible and affordable, thereby enhancing trust among the public.36

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review to examine the barriers and facilitators to deceased organ donation among Pakistanis living globally. The review highlights that although the majority of individuals are aware of organ donation, there remains a significant gap between awareness and the proportion of those who are willing or registered as donors. This indicates that, while knowledge is linked to willingness and practice, other factors play a crucial role in decision-making. These factors include collective decision-making structures, family influence, religious beliefs and trust in the healthcare system. These findings align with similar reviews conducted among Indian and Muslim populations globally.22 23 Hence, this review confirms that the Pakistani community tends to favour a collective decision-making approach, where decisions are often made within the family or extended family unit and are influenced by religious beliefs, family dynamics and trust in healthcare institutions.

While the majority of participants identified mass media and social media as their primary sources of information on organ donation,2634,37 39 42 43 45 a significant gap exists in the literature regarding the Pakistani population. Specifically, there is limited understanding of the role of media content, the nature of the information the public receives, how it is perceived, the effectiveness of media interventions and messaging strategies, and which engagement methods are most effective in increasing organ donation rates in Pakistan. Such studies predominantly originate from Western countries or regions with higher organ donation rates.59,61 However, even in these regions, there is minimal research on how these strategies perform among ethnic minority groups or whether they are effective in reaching these populations.62 63 For example, our review revealed that individuals were more willing to receive a transplant than to donate.26 39 41 43 55 57 One study in the review even suggested that using reciprocity could be an effective strategy to improve the public’s willingness towards organ donation.50 Yet, there is a clear lack of evidence to inform targeted messaging or intervention strategies within the Pakistani context, leaving a critical gap in understanding and intervention.

Family plays a crucial role in decision-making within this population, which largely adheres to a collective decision-making culture,50 55 57 where not only immediate family members but also extended relatives are involved, making the process more complex.51 Within this collective culture, one study identified that participants who prioritised family consent over the donor’s wishes highlighted the family’s role as the waris (successor) of the deceased, a position that carries sacred and moral responsibilities concerning the body. On the other hand, those who prioritised the donor’s wishes referred to the religious-legal concept of wasiyat (will), emphasising the family’s moral duty to respect the donor’s decisions. In countries with soft opt-out or opt-in systems, studies of this nature are vital in identifying the factors that lead a family to overrule the donor’s wishes.51 They also help to understand what might prevent individuals from initiating these conversations, as we see in the aforementioned study, where once the family is aware of the deceased’s wishes, it becomes a moral obligation to respect those wishes.11 However, there is a need for further research to explore how these dynamics influence organ donation decisions and how effective current policies are in addressing these cultural nuances.

Pakistan has a Muslim-majority population,64 and most of the population considers religious views to guide their decision on organ donation. This finding is aligned with a recent systematic review of organ donation among Muslims living globally.22 Lack of visibility and uncertainty on the views of the religious leaders were all seen to negatively override the personal favourable views toward deceased organ donation.40 41 45 49 51 55 Will this just benefit from engaging with religious leaders regionally as in the UK, for instance, or should this engagement and consensus be done at a global level are questions that need to be addressed by further work.

Trust was another layer of construct that influenced the public views toward organ donation, often referring to thoughts of organ trafficking and not saving the patient but prioritising organ harvesting.26 35 36 41 46 47 57 One of the views of the participants was to increase the number of government hospitals to perform transplants which could be affordable for all, thereby increasing the trust in hospitals toward organ donation not being a commercial treatment option affordable only for the rich.15 Also, making the whole system more transparent was proposed.36 Hence, in such a context where decision-making is collective, considering various levels of the society in which these individuals live, a whole system approach is most needed. The whole systems approach emphasises the need for more than just educational awareness programmes. It advocates for a comprehensive strategy that includes active and positive media engagement, involvement of religious and community leaders, increased visibility of donor and recipient families, more detailed data to understand organ donation behaviours across different ethnic groups, and culturally sensitive interventions.65 Therefore, adopting a whole systems approach that acknowledges the collectivist nature of decision-making within this population could significantly contribute to improving organ donation rates.

In this systematic review, we included both quantitative and qualitative data, making it a more comprehensive search of literature to address its aim. More than one reviewer was involved in all the stages of the review, making it more credible and trustworthy. A key methodological issue with studies conducted outside of Pakistan is that the findings from the Pakistani population were often combined with those from other groups, such as South Asians or Bangladeshis.24 However, other research shows that these groups are not entirely homogeneous, even though they do share some similarities.26 Hence, we did not include those studies. With the Pakistani diaspora around the world, it is important to understand if their barriers are the same or if they differ based on the region they live in or based on the generation. This would help in identifying if the interventions should be modified based on their region or if they should stay the same.

This review identified that most of the studies included have taken the same aim and approach, leading to repetition of the same studies leading to confirmation of the previous evidence and having little scope to identify further gaps in research in this particular area. Similar to the studies undertaken in other parts of the world, diverse research is required in these regions to improve the organ donation system and organ donation rates to reach self-sufficiency. Experimental studies that aimed to improve awareness or the behaviour towards organ donation were excluded since it was beyond the scope of the current review, which is one of the limitations of this review.

Conclusion

This review highlights that decisions regarding deceased organ donation in Pakistani communities are typically collective, influenced by family dynamics, religious beliefs and trust in the healthcare system. However, further research is needed to expand on these findings and inform effective evidence-based practices. Current studies tend to replicate existing evidence rather than uncover new gaps that could enhance organ donor registration and consent rates in Pakistan. Future research should also encompass the perspectives of other key stakeholders, including healthcare professionals, religious leaders, social media influencers, as well as families of deceased organ donors and transplant recipients. However, findings from this systematic review indicate that strategies should adopt a whole-systems approach, engaging the interconnected healthcare, legal, social and cultural domains to enhance organ donation and transplantation outcomes. Such an approach underscores the importance of collaboration among policymakers, healthcare professionals, communities and the media, while also recognising the critical roles of families, religion and trust in shaping donation practices. The effectiveness of these strategies will ultimately depend on the diversity of future research within this population.
