A Four‐Center Study on the Activity of Aztreonam and Aztreonam‐Avibactam Combination Against Clinical Isolates of Gram‐Negative Bacilli Producing Representatives of Ambler Class A, B, C or D β‐Lactamases
Authors: Gizem İnce Ceviz, Hasan Cenk Mirza, Aylin Üsküdar Güçlü, Özlem Öğüç Şanlı, Özlem Kurt Azap, Banu Sancak, Elvan Hortaç İştar, Ahmet Başustaoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe aimed to investigate the activity of aztreonam and aztreonam‐avibactam combination against gram‐negative bacilli producing different types of β‐lactamases. Activity of aztreonam and aztreonam‐avibactam against 166 isolates (33 AmpC‐producing, 33 ESBL‐producing, 66 carbapenemase‐producing Enterobacterales, 34 metallo‐β‐lactamase‐producing P. aeruginosa ) from four centers in Türkiye was determined using gradient diffusion method. Aztreonam susceptibility rates among AmpC‐producing, ESBL‐producing and carbapenemase‐producing Enterobacterales were 75.8%, 3% and 6.1%, respectively. All AmpC‐producing or ESBL‐producing Enterobacterales were susceptible to aztreonam‐avibactam. ESBL‐producing K. pneumoniae had significantly higher aztreonam‐avibactam MICs compared with ESBL‐producing E. coli . K. pneumoniae carrying bla CTX‐M + bla SHV + bla TEM had significantly higher aztreonam‐avibactam MICs when compared to those carrying only bla CTX‐M. Aztreonam‐avibactam susceptibility rate among carbapenemase‐producing K. pneumoniae (100%) was significantly higher than that observed for carbapenemase‐producing E. coli (65%). Metallo‐β‐lactamase‐producing P. aeruginosa had significantly higher aztreonam‐avibactam MICs (MIC50/90: 16/96 mg/L) when compared to metallo‐β‐lactamase‐producing Enterobacterales (MIC50/90: 0.19/8 mg/L). Aztreonam‐avibactam may be an option for treatment of infections caused by ESBL‐producing or AmpC‐producing K. pneumoniae , E. coli and carbapenemase‐producing K. pneumoniae . However, aztreonam‐avibactam resistance may be encountered in a considerable portion of carbapenem‐resistant E. coli isolates. High aztreonam‐avibactam MIC values suggest limited in vitro activity of aztreonam‐avibactam against MBL‐producing P. aeruginosa isolates in this collection.Keywords: aztreonam, aztreonam‐avibactam, E. coli , K. pneumoniae , P. aeruginosa

Introduction

Aztreonam, unlike other β‐lactams, is stable to hydrolysis by metallo‐β‐lactamases (MBLs). However, MBL‐producing Enterobacterales often co‐produce other β‐lactamases (e.g., ESBL, AmpC) that inactivate aztreonam [1]. Avibactam, a non‐β‐lactam β‐lactamase inhibitor, protects β‐lactams from hydrolysis by Ambler class A (e.g., ESBL), C (e.g., AmpC) and some class D β‐lactamases [2]. When combined with avibactam, aztreonam shows activity against gram‐negative bacteria producing serine β‐lactamases or MBLs, or both. In 2024, aztreonam‐avibactam combination was approved by European Medicines Agency (EMA) for the treatment of complicated intra‐abdominal and urinary infections, hospital‐acquired pneumonia and infections due to aerobic gram‐negative bacteria in adults with limited treatment options [3]. In 2025, aztreonam‐avibactam in combination with metronidazole was approved by US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of complicated intra‐abdominal infections [4].

Our objective was to evaluate the in vitro activity of aztreonam and aztreonam‐avibactam against gram negative bacilli ( E. coli , K. pneumoniae and P. aeruginosa ) isolated in four centers in Türkiye, which carry β‐lactamases belong to Ambler class A (i.e., ESBL, KPC), class B (i.e., NDM, IMP, VIM), class C (i.e., AmpC) or class D (i.e., OXA‐48‐like β‐lactamases). We also aimed to investigate the molecular mechanisms of resistance by whole‐genome sequencing (WGS) analysis in aztreonam‐avibactam‐resistant isolates of Enterobacterales and to test the in vitro activity of ceftazidime/aztreonam‐avibactam combination in these isolates.

Materials and Methods

This study was approved by Başkent University Institutional Review Board (project no: DA24/11) and supported by Başkent University Research Fund.

Bacterial Isolates

A total of 166 clinical isolates including 33 AmpC‐producing Enterobacterales (30 E. coli , 3 K. pneumoniae ), 33 ESBL‐producing Enterobacterales [22 carrying bla CTX‐M (13 E. coli , 9 K. pneumoniae ), 11 carrying bla CTX‐M + bla SHV + bla TEM (4 E. coli, 7 K. pneumoniae )], 66 carbapenemase‐producing Enterobacterales [28 carrying bla OXA‐48‐like (9 E. coli , 19 K. pneumoniae ), 19 carrying bla NDM (5 E. coli, 14 K. pneumoniae ), 13 carrying bla OXA‐48‐like + bla NDM (5 E. coli , 8 K. pneumoniae ), 4 carrying bla KPC (1 E. coli , 3 K. pneumoniae ), 1 carrying bla IMP ( K. pneumoniae ) and 1 carrying bla VIM ( K. pneumoniae )] and 34 MBL‐producing P. aeruginosa (20 carrying bla IMP, 5 carrying bla VIM, 9 carrying bla IMP + bla VIM) were included in our study. Strains were isolated in Başkent University Adana Medical and Research Center [Center‐1 (n = 73)], Başkent University Ankara Hospital [Center‐2 (n = 41)], Hacettepe University Faculty of Medicine‐Department of Medical Microbiology [Center‐3 (n = 15)] and Ankara Etlik City Hospital [Center‐4 (n = 37)] from urine (n = 71), blood (n = 52), respiratory specimens (n = 22), wound (n = 13), drain fluid (n = 5), abdominal fluid (n = 2) and tissue biopsy (n = 2) between May 2015 and June 2025. Only one isolate per patient was included in the study.

Detection of AmpC Production

To detect AmpC production in Enterobacterales, the method proposed by Polsfuss et al. was used [5]. Briefly, potential AmpC‐producers were detected using cefoxitin disks (30 μg, Bioanalyse, Türkiye). Isolates with cefoxitin inhibition zone diameters of ≤ 18 mm were subjected to AmpC confirmation testing using disks containing “cefoxitin (30 μg)” or “cefoxitin plus cloxacillin (30/200 μg, Bioanalyse, Türkiye)”. If the cefoxitin‐cloxacillin inhibition zone diameter was ≥ 4 mm larger than that of cefoxitin, AmpC production was confirmed [5]. All the AmpC‐producing isolates were also subjected to ESBL testing using the combination disk method as described by CLSI [6]. Isolates co‐producing AmpC and ESBL enzymes were excluded.

Detection of ESBL Production and ESBL Genes

For ESBL testing, the combination disk method recommended by CLSI was used [6]. Briefly, disks containing cefotaxime (30 μg), ceftazidime (30 μg), cefotaxime‐clavulanate (30/10 μg), and ceftazidime‐clavulanate (30/10 μg) (Bioanalyse, Türkiye) were placed on an inoculated Mueller–Hinton agar and incubated for 18 h at 35°C. If the inhibition zone diameter for one of the cephalosporin‐clavulanate disks was ≥ 5 mm larger than that observed for the corresponding cephalosporin disk, the isolate was considered positive for ESBL production. All the ESBL‐producing Enterobacterales isolates were also screened using a cefoxitin disk to rule out AmpC co‐production. ESBL‐producing isolates with cefoxitin inhibition zone diameters of ≤ 18 mm were excluded. In phenotypically confirmed ESBL‐producing isolates, ESBL genes (bla CTX‐M, bla SHV, and bla TEM) were detected using conventional PCR. The PCR protocol was performed as previously described by Mirza et al. [7]. Positive control strains included E. coli ATCC 35218 (bla TEM), E. coli CCUG 62975 (bla CTX‐M), and K. pneumoniae ATCC 700603 (bla SHV).

Detection of Carbapenem Resistance and Carbapenemase Genes

The K. pneumoniae and E. coli isolates which were resistant to meropenem, imipenem or ertapenem by disk diffusion method according to EUCAST breakpoints [8] were screened for bla OXA‐48‐like and bla NDM, the two most commonly encountered carbapenemase genes in Türkiye. The PCR protocol was performed as previously described by Poirel et al. [9]. K. pneumoniae CDC529 (bla NDM‐1) and K. pneumoniae NCTC 13442 (bla OXA‐48) were used as positive controls.

To increase the number of carbapenem‐resistant Enterobacterales strains and diversity of carbapenemase genes, isolates with previously confirmed carbapenemase genes from center‐2 (two bla KPC‐positive isolates) and center‐3 (11 bla NDM‐positive, two bla KPC‐positive, one bla IMP‐positive, and one bla VIM‐positive isolate) were also included in the study. Thus, the study was performed using a selected isolate collection rather than a prevalence‐based isolate collection.

The P. aeruginosa isolates that were resistant to imipenem or meropenem by disk diffusion method according to EUCAST breakpoints [8] were screened for MBL production using the imipenem‐EDTA disk method. Briefly, disks containing 10 μg of imipenem alone and in combination with 930 μg (5 μL) of 0.5 M EDTA were placed on an inoculated Mueller–Hinton agar and incubated for 18 h at 35°C. An increase of ≥ 7 mm in inhibition zone diameter of the EDTA‐containing imipenem disk compared to that of the imipenem disk alone was considered positive for MBL production [10]. In phenotypically confirmed MBL‐producing P. aeruginosa isolates, commonly encountered MBL genes (bla IMP and bla VIM) were detected using conventional PCR. The PCR protocol was adapted from the study conducted by Zowawi et al. [11]. E. coli NCTC 13476 (bla IMP) and K. pneumoniae NCTC 13440 (bla VIM) were included as positive controls.

Aztreonam and Aztreonam‐Avibactam Susceptibility Testing

MICs of aztreonam and aztreonam‐avibactam were determined by gradient diffusion method using aztreonam and aztreonam‐avibactam MIC test strips (Liofilchem, Roseto degli Abruzzi, Italy). For Enterobacterales and P. aeruginosa isolates, aztreonam MICs were interpreted using EUCAST breakpoint tables (version 16.0) [8]. For Enterobacterales, aztreonam‐avibactam MICs were interpreted using EUCAST breakpoint tables (susceptible ≤ 4 mg/L, resistant > 4 mg/L) as well as CLSI‐defined breakpoints (susceptible ≤ 4 mg/L, intermediate 8 mg/L, resistant ≥ 16 mg/L) [6, 8]. Currently, there are no aztreonam‐avibactam MIC breakpoints for P. aeruginosa . For antimicrobial susceptibility testing, E. coli ATCC 25922 was used as a quality control strain. Quality control results were within the acceptable ranges, as reported in EUCAST documents.

Assessment of Interactions Between Aztreonam‐Avibactam and Ceftazidime

In aztreonam‐avibactam‐resistant Enterobacterales isolates, activity of ceftazidime/aztreonam‐avibactam combination and interactions between ceftazidime and aztreonam‐avibactam were determined using gradient diffusion method, as described by White et al. [12]. Briefly, aztreonam‐avibactam and ceftazidime MIC test strips (Liofilchem, Roseto degli Abruzzi, Italy) were crossed at a 90° angle at their predetermined MIC value on inoculated Mueller Hinton agar. When the MIC value exceeded the concentration range on one or both of the MIC test strips, the strips were crossed at the highest concentration present on the respective MIC test strip. After incubation for 18 h at 35°C, the MIC of each antimicrobial in the combination was interpreted as the value at which the inhibition zone intersects the MIC test strip.

Interactions between antimicrobials were interpreted using the fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) that was calculated as follows: MIC of ceftazidime in combination/MIC of ceftazidime alone + MIC of aztreonam‐avibactam in combination/MIC of aztreonam‐avibactam alone. If the MIC value exceeded the concentration range on the strip for any antimicrobial, the FICI was calculated using a MIC one two‐fold dilution above the highest concentration on the MIC test strip [13]. “Synergy” was defined as FICI ≤ 0.5, “additivity” was defined as FICI > 0.5–1, “indifference” was defined as FICI > 1–4, and antagonism was defined as FICI > 4 [14, 15].

Whole‐Genome Sequencing and Analyses

To understand the molecular drivers of aztreonam‐avibactam resistance, all aztreonam‐avibactam‐resistant isolates were subjected to WGS. Whole‐genome sequencing of an aztreonam‐avibactam‐susceptible isolate was also performed for comparison. Total genomic DNA was extracted from isolates using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany). WGS libraries were prepared using Twist and sequenced on a DNBSEQ‐T7 platform (MGI Tech, China), generating 2 × 150 bp paired‐end reads. The quality of the reads was assessed using FastQC v0.12.1 and MultiQC v1.35. Genomes were assembled de novo with SPAdes v4.3.0, and assembly quality was evaluated using QUAST v5.3.0. Multilocus sequence typing was performed using the Achtman seven‐gene scheme. Acquired antimicrobial resistance genes, including β‐lactamases, were detected using NCBI's AMRFinderPlus v4.2.7, with independent confirmation against the ResFinder database via ABRicate v1.4.0. Amino acid alterations in penicillin‐binding protein 3 (PBP3) and the outer membrane proteins OmpC and OmpF, which are not covered by curated AMRFinderPlus/ResFinder panels, were identified by aligning each assembly against the E. coli K‐12 MG1655 reference proteins. Efflux pump genes and associated antibiotics were characterized using RGI v6.0.8 with the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD). Genetic relatedness among resistant isolates was assessed by core‐genome single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis. The reads were aligned against the E. coli ATCC 25922 reference genome (GCF_000401755.1) with Snippy v4.6.0. Recombinant regions were removed using Gubbins v3.4, and a maximum‐likelihood phylogeny was inferred with IQ‐TREE v3.1.3.

Statistical Analysis

The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare aztreonam and aztreonam‐avibactam MICs between different subsets of bacteria. The Fisher–Freeman–Halton Exact test was used to compare aztreonam and aztreonam‐avibactam susceptibility rates between different subsets within Enterobacterales. p values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Among Enterobacterales, the highest aztreonam susceptibility rate was observed in AmpC‐producing isolates (75.8%), which was significantly higher than the rates for ESBL‐producing (3%) (p < 0.001) and carbapenemase‐producing (6.1%) (p < 0.001) isolates. Isolates with aztreonam MIC of ≥ 256 mg/L accounted for 40.9% of carbapenemase‐producing Enterobacterales. However, none of the AmpC or ESBL‐producing isolates had aztreonam MIC of ≥ 256 mg/L. Among carbapenemase‐producing Enterobacterales, isolates carrying bla OXA‐48‐like+bla NDM had significantly higher aztreonam MICs (MIC50/90: ≥ 256/≥ 256 mg/L) than those carrying only bla OXA‐48‐like (MIC50/90: 48/≥ 256 mg/L) (p = 0.026). However, no significant difference was observed between aztreonam MICs for isolates carrying bla OXA‐48‐like + bla NDM and isolates carrying only bla NDM (MIC50/90: 64/≥ 256 mg/L) (p = 0.127) (Table 2). No significant difference was observed between aztreonam MICs for isolates carrying only bla OXA‐48‐like and isolates carrying only bla NDM (p = 0.351). Also, no significant difference was observed between aztreonam MICs for MBL‐producing P. aeruginosa (MIC50/90: 24/≥ 256 mg/L) and MBL‐producing Enterobacterales (MIC50/90: 64/≥ 256 mg/L) (p = 0.618).

Aztreonam and aztreonam‐avibactam susceptibilities of AmpC‐producing, ESBL‐producing, and carbapenemase‐producing Enterobacterales isolates.

MIC50 and MIC90 values were not calculated due to the small number of isolates.

All AmpC‐producing or ESBL‐producing Enterobacterales isolates were susceptible to aztreonam‐avibactam using EUCAST or CLSI breakpoints. Also, no significant difference was observed between aztreonam‐avibactam MICs for AmpC‐producing (MIC50/90: 0.047/0.094 mg/L) and ESBL‐producing (MIC50/90: 0.047/0.125 mg/L) isolates (p = 0.99). Although aztreonam‐avibactam was highly active against all ESBL‐producing isolates, ESBL‐producing K. pneumoniae had statistically significantly higher aztreonam‐avibactam MICs (MIC50/90: 0.064/0.125 mg/L) when compared to ESBL‐producing E. coli (MIC50/90: 0.023/0.064 mg/L) (p = 0.004). No significant difference was observed between aztreonam‐avibactam MICs for E. coli carrying three (bla CTX‐M + bla SHV + bla TEM) or one (bla CTX‐M) ESBL gene (p = 0.296). However, aztreonam‐avibactam MICs for K. pneumoniae carrying three ESBL genes (MIC50/90: 0.064/0.19 mg/L) were statistically significantly higher than those for K. pneumoniae carrying one ESBL gene (MIC50/90: 0.047/0.125 mg/L) (p = 0.046) (Table 2).

Although all carbapenemase‐producing K. pneumoniae isolates were susceptible to aztreonam‐avibactam, only 13 (65%) of 20 carbapenemase‐producing E. coli isolates were susceptible to aztreonam‐avibactam according to both EUCAST and CLSI breakpoints. The difference between aztreonam‐avibactam susceptibility rates among carbapenemase‐producing K. pneumoniae (100%) and E. coli (65%) was statistically significant (p < 0.001). Seven carbapenemase‐producing E. coli isolates were resistant to aztreonam‐avibactam using EUCAST breakpoints. The isolates which were found to be resistant to aztreonam‐avibactam using gradient diffusion method produced inhibition zone diameters between 21 and 24 mm using aztreonam‐avibactam disks (Liofilchem, Roseto degli Abruzzi, Italy) and were categorized also resistant according to EUCAST breakpoints by disk diffusion testing. According to CLSI breakpoints, five of these seven isolates were found intermediate and two were found resistant to aztreonam‐avibactam by gradient diffusion method. Among the seven aztreonam‐avibactam resistant isolates (using EUCAST breakpoints), five were isolated in Center‐1 and two were isolated in Center‐2. Three isolates carried bla NDM, two carried bla OXA‐48‐like, and two carried both bla OXA‐48‐like and bla NDM genes. To understand the molecular mechanisms responsible for aztreonam‐avibactam resistance, all the seven aztreonam‐avibactam‐resistant isolates were subjected to WGS. In addition, one randomly selected aztreonam‐avibactam‐susceptible E. coli isolate was subjected to WGS for comparison. All resistant isolates showed amino acid alterations in PBP3. Four isolates carried YRIN insertion, and two carried YRIK insertion after amino acid 333. One isolate carried TIPY insertion after amino acid 330. The susceptible isolate (E‐KAR19) did not show any amino acid alterations in PBP3. Besides carbapenemase enzymes, all the aztreonam‐avibactam‐resistant isolates carried acquired AmpC (one carried DHA‐1, three carried CMY‐42, two carried CMY‐145) and/or ESBL (two carried CTX‐M‐15) enzymes. Three isolates also carried TEM‐1 and two also carried OXA‐1 enzymes (Table 3). Except a frameshift mutation in ompF of isolate “E‐KAR17”, no major porin loss or inactivation of efflux pump regulators was observed in aztreonam‐avibactam‐resistant isolates. All the resistant isolates carried core efflux system components.

For all the seven aztreonam‐avibactam‐resistant Enterobacterales isolates, interactions between ceftazidime and aztreonam‐avibactam were investigated. Indifference was observed in five (71.4%) of seven isolates, whereas synergy was observed between aztreonam‐avibactam and ceftazidime in two (28.6%) isolates. All the five isolates for which aztreonam‐avibactam and ceftazidime showed indifferent effect were positive for bla NDM. Both the two isolates for which aztreonam‐avibactam and ceftazidime exhibited synergy were negative for bla NDM (Table 3).

According to core‐genome SNP analysis, the seven aztreonam‐avibactam‐resistant isolates did not represent a clonal spread but comprised at least three distinct lineages. Three isolates (E‐KAR3, E‐KAR11, and E‐KAR17) differed by 52–173 SNPs, indicating a close clonal relationship. Three isolates (E‐KAR4, YE‐KAR44, and YE‐KAR6) differed by 246–419 SNPs. Isolate YE‐KAR 7 was more distantly related to both groups (> 11,000 SNPs).

Overall, no significant difference was observed between the aztreonam‐avibactam susceptibility rates in isolates carrying bla OXA‐48‐like+bla NDM and isolates carrying only bla OXA‐48‐like (84.6% and 92.9%, respectively; p = 0.579). However, aztreonam‐avibactam MICs for isolates carrying bla OXA‐48‐like+bla NDM (MIC50/90: 0.38/8 mg/L) were significantly higher than those for isolates carrying only bla OXA‐48‐like (MIC50/90: 0.19/3 mg/L) (p = 0.045). No significant difference was observed between the aztreonam‐avibactam susceptibility rates in bla OXA‐48‐like+bla NDM carrying and only bla NDM carrying Enterobacterales isolates (84.6% and 84.2%, respectively; p = 1.000). Similarly, no significant difference (p = 0.108) was observed between aztreonam‐avibactam MICs for isolates carrying bla OXA‐48‐like+bla NDM and isolates carrying only bla NDM (MIC50/90: 0.19/12 mg/L). When aztreonam‐avibactam MICs for isolates carrying only bla OXA‐48‐like were compared to those for isolates carrying only bla NDM, no significant difference was observed (p = 1.000). The Enterobacterales isolates carrying bla KPC (n = 4), bla IMP (n = 1) or bla VIM (n = 1) were susceptible to aztreonam‐avibactam (Table 2).

MBL‐producing P. aeruginosa had significantly (p < 0.001) higher aztreonam‐avibactam MICs (MIC50/90: 16/96 mg/L) when compared to MBL‐producing Enterobacterales (MIC50/90: 0.19/8 mg/L).

P. aeruginosa isolates carrying only bla IMP had significantly higher aztreonam MICs (MIC50/90: ≥ 256/≥ 256 mg/L) when compared to those carrying bla IMP + bla VIM (MIC50/90: 16/24 mg/L) (p < 0.001) or carrying only bla VIM (p = 0.003). Similarly, isolates carrying only bla IMP had significantly higher aztreonam‐avibactam MICs (MIC50/90: 48/128 mg/L) when compared to those carrying bla IMP + bla VIM (MIC50/90: 12/16 mg/L) (p < 0.001) or carrying only bla VIM (p = 0.006) (Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, we tested the in vitro activity of aztreonam and aztreonam‐avibactam against gram‐negative bacteria producing different types of β‐lactamases. To obtain a more diverse bacterial collection, we included isolates from four different centers that produce β‐lactamases representing all the four molecular classes (A, B, C, and D).

Among Enterobacterales, aztreonam susceptibility rate in AmpC‐producing isolates (75.8%) was significantly higher than that observed for ESBL‐producing (3%) and carbapenemase‐producing (6.1%) isolates. Although AmpC enzymes can hydrolyse aztreonam, aztreonam is a weak substrate for these enzymes [16]. Similar to our study, Li et al. found higher aztreonam MICs in Enterobacteriaceae strains producing ESBL when compared to those producing “Class C cephalosporinase” [17]. In the study of Li et al., 2 (8%) of 25 Enterobacteriaceae strains producing ESBL had aztreonam MICs ≤ 1 mg/L, whereas 3 (17.6%) of 17 Enterobacteriaceae strains producing “Class C cephalosporinase” had aztreonam MICs ≤ 1 mg/L (the susceptibility breakpoint according to EUCAST). However, the aztreonam susceptibility rate in “Class C cephalosporinase” producing strains (17.6%) was considerably lower when compared to our study.

In the current study, all aztreonam‐resistant AmpC‐producing or ESBL‐producing isolates were susceptible to aztreonam‐avibactam. Although very low aztreonam‐avibactam MICs were observed in all ESBL‐producing Enterobacterales, aztreonam‐avibactam MICs for ESBL‐producing K. pneumoniae were significantly higher than those for ESBL‐producing E. coli . Also, aztreonam‐avibactam MICs for K. pneumoniae carrying three ESBL genes (bla CTX‐M + bla SHV + bla TEM) were significantly higher than those for K. pneumoniae carrying one ESBL gene (bla CTX‐M), a finding which was not observed among ESBL‐producing E. coli isolates. This finding is of questionable significance because of the small sample size, and studies containing a larger number of isolates may clarify this issue. Aztreonam‐avibactam may be slightly more active against K. pneumoniae isolates producing only CTX‐M enzyme when compared to those producing CTX‐M + SHV + TEM enzymes.

Aztreonam is stable to hydrolysis by OXA‐48 and MBL enzymes [18]. However, vast majority of the OXA‐48‐like and/or MBL‐producing Enterobacterales isolates in our study had high aztreonam MICs. For most of these isolates, addition of avibactam to aztreonam resulted in a significant reduction in MICs. This finding was likely due to co‐production of other β‐lactamase enzymes (e.g., ESBL) which hydrolyse aztreonam and were inhibited by avibactam. Although all carbapenemase‐producing Enterobacterales isolates were also subjected to ESBL testing using combination disk method and isolates co‐producing ESBL by this method were excluded, carbapenemase enzymes may have masked the presence of ESBL in a considerable portion of our study isolates [19]. Similar to our study, previous studies reported high aztreonam MICs in MBL‐producing Enterobacterales [20, 21]. In the study conducted by Wei et al., none of the MBL‐producing Enterobacterales was inhibited by aztreonam at 1 mg/L (the susceptibility breakpoint according to EUCAST) [20]. Addition of avibactam significantly reduced aztreonam MICs. Most of the MBL‐producing Enterobacterales isolates were found to co‐harbor ESBL and/or AmpC genes [20]. Niu et al. also reported high aztreonam MICs (MIC50 and MIC90: > 128 mg/L) in MBL‐producing K. pneumoniae isolates. Addition of avibactam reduced the MIC50 value to ≤ 0.25 and MIC90 value to 1 mg/L [21].

Aztreonam‐avibactam was generally reported to have high activity against K. pneumoniae isolates, even against the carbapenem‐resistant strains co‐producing KPC and NDM enzymes. In the study conducted by Li et al., all the KPC and NDM co‐producing K. pneumoniae isolates (n = 71) were found to be susceptible to aztreonam‐avibactam [22]. However, it has also been reported that some amino acid alterations in KPC enzyme can increase aztreonam hydrolysis while reducing the inhibitory effect of avibactam on KPC [23]. In our study, all the KPC‐producing strains had low aztreonam‐avibactam MICs (MIC range: 0.047–0.38 mg/L). Although we did not investigate the structures of KPC enzyme in our study, low aztreonam‐avibactam MICs in KPC‐producing isolates may indicate the lack of amino acid substitutions associated with increased aztreonam‐avibactam MICs.

Although aztreonam‐avibactam resistance was not observed among carbapenemase‐producing isolates of K. pneumoniae , seven (35%) of 20 carbapenemase‐producing E. coli were resistant to aztreonam‐avibactam according to EUCAST breakpoints. In large multicenter studies, consistent with the findings of our study, E. coli was reported as the predominant species among aztreonam‐avibactam resistant Enterobacterales isolates [24, 25]. In the study conducted by Wise et al., 99.8% of 24,937 Enterobacterales isolates had aztreonam‐avibactam MICs of ≤ 8 mg/L, and 38 (62.3%) of 61 isolates with aztreonam‐avibactam MICs of > 8 mg/L were E. coli [24]. However, the researchers used aztreonam susceptibility breakpoint of 8 mg/L instead of the recommended breakpoint (4 mg/L) by EUCAST. Thus, aztreonam‐avibactam susceptibility rates may be lower than indicated. In the study conducted by Sader et al., aztreonam‐avibactam susceptibility rate among 30,074 Enterobacterales isolates was higher than 99%, and E. coli represented 72.7% of aztreonam‐avibactam resistant Enterobacterales. Interestingly, most of the aztreonam‐avibactam resistant E.coli isolates (12 out of 16) were from Türkiye [25]. In the same study, all the aztreonam‐avibactam resistant E. coli isolates had four amino acid insertions (YRIK or YRIN) in PBP3, the primary target of aztreonam. In addition, most isolates carried CMY (AmpC‐type β‐lactamase) usually with CTX‐M and/or OXA‐type β‐lactamases.

In our study, the overall aztreonam‐avibactam susceptibility rate among Enterobacterales isolates was 94.7% which is lower than abovementioned studies. The aztreonam‐avibactam resistant E. coli isolates were isolated in two centers located in two different cities (Ankara and Adana) of Türkiye. Three isolates carried bla NDM, two carried bla OXA‐48‐like, and two carried bla OXA‐48‐like+bla NDM genes. Although addition of avibactam resulted in some reduction (2–43 fold) in aztreonam MICs, the decrease was not sufficient to categorize these isolates as susceptible to aztreonam‐avibactam. Aztreonam‐avibactam resistance in E. coli was reported to be related mainly to amino acid alterations (YRIK or YRIN insertion) in PBP3 plus the presence of CMY β‐lactamase and/or ESBL [1, 25, 26]. Consistent with previous reports, all aztreonam‐avibactam‐resistant E. coli isolates in our study carried four amino acid insertions in PBP3 (YRIN in four, YRIK in two, TIPY in one isolate) and besides carbapenemases, they produced AmpC β‐lactamase (CMY‐42 in three, CMY‐145 in two, DHA‐1 in one isolate) and/or ESBL (CTX‐M‐15 in two isolates). Interestingly, one of the resistant isolates carried TIPY insertion in PBP3 and produced NDM‐5, OXA‐181 (OXA‐48‐like carbapenemase), DHA‐1 (an AmpC type β‐lactamase) and TEM‐1 enzymes. The TIPY insertion was previously identified as an unusual alteration observed in PBP 3 of an E. coli strain with increased aztreonam‐avibactam MIC (8 mg/L). This strain was reported from the USA and additionally carried KPC‐2 and TEM‐1 enzymes [27]. In the light of findings of current and previous study [27], TIPY insertion in PBP3 of E. coli may also be considered as a factor that plays a role in the development of aztreonam‐avibactam resistance.

In aztreonam‐avibactam‐resistant E. coli isolates, we investigated the activity of the ceftazidime/aztreonam‐avibactam combination and interactions between ceftazidime and aztreonam‐avibactam. All the aztreonam‐avibactam‐resistant isolates had high ceftazidime MICs (> 256 mg/L). In isolates carrying bla NDM, aztreonam‐avibactam and ceftazidime exhibited indifferent effect and MICs in the combination did not differ significantly compared to MICs of these agents alone. This was likely due to the combination of factors including alterations in PBP3 (which is the main target both for aztreonam and ceftazidime), the production of NDM (which hydrolyzes ceftazidime and is not inhibited by avibactam), and the production of AmpC β‐lactamase. Unlike bla NDM‐positive isolates, aztreonam‐avibactam and ceftazidime exhibited synergy against bla NDM‐negative isolates even though they carried amino acid insertions in PBP3. Similar to our findings, Simner et al. reported an NDM‐producing E. coli isolate resistant to the ceftazidime‐avibactam and aztreonam combination. Besides NDM, this isolate carried four amino acid insertions in PBP3 and bla CMY (an AmpC β‐lactamase) gene [28]. Senchyna et al. reported a fatal bloodstream infection caused by NDM‐producing E. coli with PBP3 insertions which did not respond to treatment with the aztreonam/ceftazidime‐avibactam combination followed by cefiderocol [29]. Based on our findings, ceftazidime might not improve the activity of aztreonam‐avibactam against NDM plus “AmpC or ESBL” producing E. coli strains with PBP3 insertions.

When compared to MBL‐producing Enterobacterales, MBL‐producing P. aeruginosa had significantly higher aztreonam‐avibactam MICs. Previous studies also reported higher aztreonam‐avibactam MICs in P. aeruginosa when compared to Enterobacterales isolates [2, 30]. Karlowsky et al. tested the in vitro activity of aztreonam‐avibactam against Enterobacteriaceae and P. aeruginosa isolated from 40 countries. The MIC90 value of aztreonam‐avibactam against MBL‐positive P. aeruginosa and MBL‐positive Enterobacteriaceae was 32 and 1 μg/mL, respectively [2]. In a multicenter study from the US, aztreonam‐avibactam inhibited 78% and 100% of P. aeruginosa and Enterobacterales isolated from ICU patients at ≤ 8 mg/L, respectively [30].

In P. aeruginosa isolates, addition of avibactam to aztreonam led to a very limited reduction in MIC50 values (from 24 to 16 mg/L). Similar findings were also reported in previous studies [2] and suggest the presence of non‐enzymatic mechanisms causing aztreonam resistance. PBP3 variations and increased expression of MexAB‐OprM efflux pump were reported to play a role in aztreonam resistance in P. aeruginosa [31]. Interestingly, aztreonam and aztreonam‐avibactam MICs for isolates carrying only bla IMP were significantly higher than those for isolates carrying bla IMP + bla VIM or carrying only bla VIM. In light of our findings, the mechanisms causing higher levels of aztreonam and aztreonam‐avibactam resistance may be found more frequently in P. aeruginosa isolates carrying only bla IMP when compared to those carrying bla IMP + bla VIM or only bla VIM. However, further research is required to support this hypothesis.

Our study has some limitations. Since we could not obtain aztreonam and avibactam in powder form, we used gradient diffusion instead of the reference broth microdilution method for aztreonam and aztreonam‐avibactam susceptibility testing. However, aztreonam‐avibactam MIC test strips are among the US FDA‐cleared strips produced by Liofilchem. Against 686 isolates of Enterobacterales, the US. FDA have found an essential agreement of 96.7% and categorical agreement of 97.8% between aztreonam‐avibactam strips and the reference broth microdilution method [32]. Second, we investigated not all but the most commonly encountered MBL genes (bla IMP and bla VIM) in P. aeruginosa isolates. Also, we could not perform gene cloning assays to introduce individual resistance genes into strains with an identical genetic background, which would eliminate confounding variables and allow clearer evaluation of the sole impact of each β‐lactamase on antibiotic susceptibility. Last, we included only one isolate of Enterobacterales carrying bla IMP and one carrying bla VIM. Increasing the number of Enterobacterales isolates carrying bla IMP or bla VIM would provide more reliable information regarding the susceptibility profile of isolates carrying these genes. Even so, the findings of our study may provide insight into the activity of aztreonam‐avibactam against commonly encountered gram‐negative bacilli.

Conclusion

In conclusion, aztreonam‐avibactam showed high in vitro activity against ESBL‐producing, AmpC‐producing isolates of K. pneumoniae , E. coli , and carbapenemase‐producing isolates of K. pneumoniae . Aztreonam in combination with avibactam may be an option for the treatment of infections caused by these microorganisms. However, aztreonam‐avibactam resistance may be encountered in a considerable portion of carbapenem‐resistant E. coli isolates. Although there are no established aztreonam‐avibactam MIC breakpoints for P. aeruginosa , high MICs detected in most P. aeruginosa isolates and the very limited reduction in aztreonam MICs after the addition of avibactam suggest limited in vitro activity of aztreonam‐avibactam against MBL‐producing P. aeruginosa isolates in this collection.
