A study on the effective utilization of ultrafine fly ash and silica fume content in high-performance concrete through an experimental approach
Authors: Arava Lekhya, N. Senthil Kumar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Conventional cement production is a major source of CO2 emissions. As a result, there is an increasing emphasis on finding sustainable alternatives for cement and their appropriate proportion in concrete. This investigation explores the optimization of supplementary cementitious materials (SCM) like ultrafine fly ash (UFFA) and silica fume (SF) content in high-performance concrete (HPC). The study includes the production of binary and ternary concrete mixes by replacing Portland cement with UFFA and SF at 0, 5, 10, and 15 %. A total of 16 mixes were prepared and evaluated in three stages. The first stage involved assessing the mechanical properties using compressive strength test and non-destructive test (NDT) results at various intervals. The second stage included durability tests, such as water absorption, volume of voids, and water permeability tests. In the third stage, characterization studies like XRD, TGA, and FTIR were conducted on the finalized mixes at 28 and 90 day intervals to find the optimum mix. The NDT findings revealed that all HPC mixes had superior quality, with a velocity of more than 5 km/s. From the test results, the ternary mix U10S15 exhibited superior compressive strengths of 104.28 MPa at 90 days of curing. The durability test results also demonstrated that the blend U10S15 showed a lower water absorption of 1.26 %, indicating a 44.9 % reduction in water absorption with extended curing. The FTIR and TGA analysis of HPC mixes demonstrated that blending the optimal amounts of UFFA and SF results in a dense microstructure. The mixture U10S15 exhibits a considerable peak shift from 950 to 980 cm⁻1. XRD peaks confirmed the presence of extra hydration peaks in blended specimens at 28° of 2θ. The optimized HPC mix containing 10 % UFFA and 15 % SF (U10S15) is appropriate for water-retaining structures due to its high strength, lower permeability, and higher particle packing effect.

Introduction

Concrete is one of the most significant construction materials, ranking the second most utilized globally after water. It plays a vital role in building the infrastructure essential for social and economic progress [1]. High-performance concrete is one of the preferred materials for high-rise buildings due to its workability, strength, and impermeability. It also allows for reduced cross-sections in structural elements, which increases useable space. HPC is prevalent in marine environments because of its low permeability and resilience. Its mechanical strength, durability, and cost-effectiveness make it challenging to replace with alternative materials in numerous construction projects. Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) is the primary binder in concrete; nevertheless, OPC manufacturing is the primary source of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, contributing to global warming and climate change. Around 8 %–10 % of the world's annual emissions of CO2 are generated during the burning of Portland clinker. Some industrial or agricultural wastes have been successfully used in conventional concrete and HPC production as supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) to reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and enhance the economic and environmental sustainability of cement production [[2], [3], [4]]. Incorporating these SCMs, like silica fume, fly ash, rice husk ash, ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS), metakaolin, sugar cane bagasse ash, etc., into concrete improves mechanical and durability properties up to a certain extent [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. Additionally, the properties of multi-component cement are more favorable due to the synergistic effect and the interaction of different mineral admixtures than cement containing only one mineral admixture [9]. Superplasticizers, the other most helpful chemical admixtures developed in the late 1970s, allowed for significant reductions in the water/binder ratio in HPC, leading to the discovery that lower ratios resulted in numerous improved concrete characteristics [10].

One of the most widely used pozzolanas in developing HPC is silica fume (SF) or micro silica, which acts as a highly reactive pozzolanic material. It is a fine amorphous type of silicon dioxide formed while manufacturing silicon and ferrosilicon alloys with an average diameter of 0.1 μm. It is one hundred times smaller than typical cement particles. The ultra-fine size of SF particles works as a micro-filler, occupying spaces between cement particles and reducing porosity in the bulk matrix. This decreases permeability and enhances the strength and durability of the HPC because its oxides (SiO2) react with the primary hydrates of Portland cement by consuming calcium hydroxides (CH), resulting in the formation of secondary hydrates (C-S-H and C-A-S-H) [11]. Previous studies have shown the optimal silica fume replacement for attaining a maximum 28-day compressive strength is between 10 % and 15 % [[12], [13], [14]].

Fly ash (FA) has long been the most accepted pozzolanic material for substituting cement in concrete. In typical applications, replacing up to 20 % of the cementitious material mass with fly ash can enhance concrete's physical and mechanical properties [8,15,16]. Utilizing fly ash, a by-product of industrial processes, has appeared to address sustainability concerns in construction. Fly ash particles exhibit slow pozzolanic characteristics due to their larger particle size, similar to cement particles [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. From the literature, it was observed that increasing the degree of fineness of fly ash through grinding improves reactivity to some extent but eventually increases water demand due to the increased surface area of ground particles [21]. To improve the reactivity of fly ash, a novel ultrafine fly ash (UFFA) was produced by mechanically separating the ultrafine fractions of Class F fly ash to make fly ash highly reactive. At a low replacement level of 5 %–15 % by cement weight, it shows improvement in the strength of the concrete and decreases permeability compared to regular fly ash concretes. With UFFA, a faster hydration reaction can be achieved at earlier ages than with regular class-F fly ash. Also, the smaller particles are good at densifying the microstructure and improving particle packing to enhance concrete density. Previous studies show that the optimum UFFA dosage ranges between 8 % and 12 % of the total binder content [22,23]. The optimal replacement level of 10 % resulted in higher early strength and workability using fine fly ash as a mineral additive, resulting in high-strength and high-performance concrete, as evidenced by its positive effects on water penetration [24]. Concrete containing 10 % UFFA displayed higher compressive strength, reduced sorptivity, superior chloride resistance, lowered autogenous shrinkage, and increased resistance to restrained cracking [25,26]. Adding 8 % UFFA to ordinary concrete increases compressive strength by 55 % after 90 days, reduces water sorptivity by 50 % and 45 % after 28 and 90 days, and reduces permeable voids by 15–20 % when compared to Portland cement concrete [[27], [28], [29]]. R. Sugranez, J. I. conducted a study on fly ash particles ranging from 3 to 30 μm. The experiment found that fly ash particles in the 0–3 μm range significantly increased cement strength at one day, while those in the 3–25 μm range increased cement strength at 90 days. Fly ash particles in both ranges had similar effects on cement strength at 28 days. The addition of fly ash with particle sizes ranging from 25 to 30 μm had no impact on the cement's strength increment after 28 days [30].

The primary benefit of SF is that it improves aggregate-cement paste interaction. Its ultra-fine size of particles boosts water demand. As a result, SF is combined with a superplasticizer to manage the workability. However, some disadvantages of utilizing SF in HPC include decreased plasticity during manufacture and increased shrinkage during curing [11]. Higher replacement levels of SF in concrete mixtures resulted in the need for greater dosages of superplasticizers. Concrete mixtures consisting of silica fume did not exhibit an increase in compressive strength after 90 days [13]. So, to improve the efficiency of SF concrete and to mitigate the detrimental impacts of using FA in concrete structures, a hybrid mixture of two or three pozzolanic additives is commonly utilized. UFFA is another preferable pozzolanic material used in HPC than typical FA. The ternary mix combining SF with UFFA offers a promising alternative to binary mixes due to its synergistic effect. Using ternary mixes with SF and UFFA as supplementary materials increases short and long-term strength and greater resistance to chloride penetration in concrete [31]. Msand is becoming increasingly popular as an alternative to river sand generated by crushing rock deposits. It has an irregular surface texture than naturally formed aggregates. Excess fines in Msand contribute to higher powder content. Its increasing water consumption raises concerns about its suitability for use in HPC [32]. However, limited information exists on the microstructure and non-destructive testing (NDT) of M80 grade and above high-performance concrete with ternary cementitious composites containing SF and UFFA. It was often revealed that employing large volumes of SCMs reduced both initial and long-term strengths [33].

Although ultrafine fly ash and silica fume can significantly improve the strength and durability of high-performance concrete, their use is restricted by factors such as the potential workability issues associated with their extremely fine particle size, which might considerably decrease the workability of the concrete mixture, necessitating the incorporation of extra superplasticizers to achieve the appropriate flow and hence raising the overall cost of the mix. Due to their broad range of particle sizes of UFFA (1 μm–25 μm) and SF (0.1 μm–1.0 μm), these SCMs participate in hydration reactions at early ages, and the reaction is continuous for a long time for complete hydration. From the literature and the observations based on this study, it was observed that SF and UFFA are giving better results, with up to 15 % of each material and a total replacement of 30 % to cement. The larger dosages of these materials will lead to more paste content in the mix, leading to a reduction in strength due to the “wall effect” and “loosening effects”. The excessive incorporation of ultrafine fly ash or silica fume may adversely affect concrete's characteristics, potentially leading to shrinkage problems. The quality of UFFA and SF may fluctuate based on their source, thereby affecting the performance of the concrete mixture. Both components may be more costly than regular Portland cement, elevating the concrete mixture's total expense. Although UFFA and SF are seen as sustainable alternatives, the processing and transportation of substantial amounts may raise environmental issues.

Finding the optimum dosage of supplementary cementitious materials (SP) is essential in producing high-performance concrete. The low initial SP dose did not effectively disperse all cementitious material (CM). Before the segregation limit, higher SP dosages resulted in greater dispersion effects. The highest wet packing density (WPD) was achieved when all CMs were evenly distributed by SP right before the concrete reached the segregation limit [34,35]. Increasing the packing density of cementitious materials reduces the demand for water to fill gaps, allowing excess water to be used to improve concrete workability. To produce HPC with a low W/CM ratio and good workability, it is necessary to maximize the packing density of the concrete's granular structure by appropriately selecting its constituents. The fineness of a supplemental cementitious material influences its efficacy in filling voids and enhances packing. A broader range of particles often results in increased packing density [36].

This work investigates the optimum dosage of SCMs to produce M80-grade HPC. Sixteen concrete mixes were prepared with 5 %, 10 %, and 15 % replacement levels of each material, and a maximum of up to 30 % was replaced with cement with a constant w/b ratio of 0.28. The binary and ternary mixes were examined for compressive strength development from an early age of one day to 90 days. Ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) and rebound hammer test were performed in non-destructive testing to assess the quality of the HPC, and their correlation with compressive strength was derived concerning its 90-day strength. To understand the effect of supplementary cementitious materials in HPC, durability tests like water absorption, volume of voids, and water permeability tests are performed and compared with controlled concrete. Characterization studies like XRD, TGA, and FTIR analysis were performed to understand the microstructure of the obtained mixes at the ages of 28 and 90 days.

Tests on hardened concrete

Mechanical tests

Compressive strength test

The specimens for all the mixes were prepared and cured in laboratory conditions. The compressive strength was determined using a digital compressive testing machine shown in Fig. 4(d) with a loading capacity of 3000 kN, following IS: 516–1959 standards [42]. The load is applied gradually at a rate of about 2.6 ±0.2kN/sec until the specimen loses its capability to resist the increasing load. Three cubes were tested for each mix, and the average of their strength readings was taken. The compressive strength of the cube specimens was measured at the ages of 1, 3, 7, 28, 56, and 90 days to understand the rate of strength gain with time. The mixing and testing equipment's used in the present work to find the mechanical properties of HPC are presented in Fig. 4.

Ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) test

For conducting the UPV test, the specimen surfaces were adequately cleaned to remove any residue or imperfection that could affect the results. Cube specimens of 100 × 100 × 100 mm were utilized. Three specimens are tested for each mix using the Portable ultrasonic non-destructive digital testing machine at the age of 90 days. An average of three readings were taken perpendicular to the concrete's casting direction. The test was conducted on the specimens before testing for their compressive strength following IS 516 part-5 specifications [43] was shown in Fig. 4(b).

Rebound hammer (RH) test

The concrete surface hardness was measured using a digital Schmidt rebound hammer shown in Fig. 4(c). The rebound number readings were taken by holding the rebound hammer at right angles to the surface of the 100 × 100 × 100 mm concrete specimens at 90 days of age. The average of the rebound number (RN) values measured at twelve separate points was chosen as a representative value for each specimen. The test was performed on 48 specimens as per IS 516 guidelines [44].

Durability tests

Water absorption and volume of permeable pore spaces

The water absorption test for concrete specimens was performed on 100 × 100 × 100 mm specimens at 28 and 90 days of curing. Three cubes from each mix were tested, and the average values were recorded. As per ASTM C642-97 guidelines [45], the test was performed as mentioned below. At first, the specimen's initial weight was taken (I1). Dry the specimens in an oven at 100–110 °C for 24 h. Then, allow each specimen to cool in dry air to room temperature (20–25 °C) before determining its mass. Consider the specimen is dry if the second mass nearly matches the first, indicating relatively dry, as shown in Fig. 5(a). Repeat drying for an additional 24 h until the difference between consecutive results is less than 0.5 % was obtained. The reduced oven-dried weight was taken once the specimen reached a constant weight (I2). After oven drying, the specimens were submerged in water at standard temperature for 48 h, and weight was noted (I3). Then, the submerged and saturated cube specimens were boiled at 100 °C for 5 h in a water bath, as shown in Fig. 5(b). After cooling down to room temperature, their weights were taken as (I4). Then, the apparent weight of the boiled specimens was taken in water under submerged conditions (I5).Fig. 5Water absorption testing of HPC. a) oven drying and b) water bath for boiling of specimens.Fig. 5

Water permeability test

To determine the pore network formed by excess water added during the early hardening period, 150 × 150 × 150 mm3 specimens were prepared. The test was performed on the six selected mixes to understand their resistance toward water penetration. For each blend, three specimens were tested as per IS 516 [46] guidelines at 28 and 90 days. The test was performed using water permeability testing equipment with a water pressure of 500 ± 50 Kpa for 72 ± 2 h. To know the water penetration depth of each specimen, the specimens were split into two halves using the compressive testing machine, and the corresponding water depths were measured using vernier callipers.

Characterization studies

X-ray diffraction analysis

After performing compressive strength test, the middle fragments from the core of the specimen, excluding coarse aggregates, were collected. These samples were finely ground and sieved through a 90 μm sieve to produce a fine powder and dried in the oven to stop hydration taken to perform the XRD test. The mineralogy of hydrated cementitious samples (crystalline phases) was studied using the XRD test. The test was conducted using a D8 advanced Bruker machine with Cu-Kα radiation (k = 1.5406 A). The concrete samples were analyzed with a 0.01° step size and the Bragg's 2θ range of 10°–90°. The phases were then examined using high score plus software.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential thermal analysis (DTA) measure mass and heat differences in a material as a function of temperature in controlled conditions. TGA gives the weight loss percentage, whereas DTA gives the thermal decomposition of different phases of powdered samples. The samples of approximately 50 mg were analyzed from 0 °C to 800 °C using the TGA SDT Q600 instrument at a heating rate of 20 °C/min in a nitrogen atmosphere. TGA was performed on concrete samples after 28 and 90 days of curing for CC and optimum concrete mixtures. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR spectroscopy is an efficient and straightforward method for identifying functional groups present in concrete and comparing them with the reference bands. The FTIR analysis was carried out using a Thermo Nicolet iS50 with an inbuilt ATR model to improve the surface sensitivity of samples. The infrared spectra were performed on the selected HPC mixes at 28 and 90 days curing periods, with a mid-IR range of 400–4000 cm−1 wave spectrum.

Results and discussion

The test data was analyzed to investigate the effect of supplementary cementitious materials and curing time on concrete strength and durability. Additionally, the ultrasonic pulse velocity and surface resistance measurements are used to assess the quality of the concrete. Each factor's influence was clarified by examining the effects of the selected mixes on the concrete's microstructure.

Mechanical strength

Compressive strength variation

The obtained compressive strength test results of HPC mixes aged 1, 3, 7, 28, 56, and 90 days are shown in Fig. 6. The UFFA and SF are spherical in nature, which reduces interconnected pores and provides better packing in the mix. The WPD was larger for specimens with SF due to its finer size and spherical nature, resulting in a better “filling effect” than UFFA alone in the mix [7,48]When finer pozzolans (UFFA and SF) are mixed with aggregate to replace certain quantities of cement to obtain a specific slump, an additional SP is required. Compressive strength variations of HPC mixes concerning age.

The results were compared with the controlled mix results to varying concrete ages. The one-day compressive strength of all the mixes was between 31 and 43 MPa. The mix with no replacement (CC) showed a lower strength of 31.4 MPa, and the mix with 5 % ultrafine fly ash replacement (S0U5) showed a higher strength of 43 MPa due to its finer size particles, leading to initial reactivity. On three days, an increase in strength of 4–12 % was observed. The mix U0S15 gained a maximum of 12.3 % increase in strength at three days and 24.3 % at seven days. The 28-day test results showed that both binary and ternary mixes showed a higher strength gain from 7 to 28 days. The strength of the concrete improves with decreasing particle size. This effect becomes more pronounced at initial stages with low water-to-cement ratios. The mixes S0U10, U15S10, and U10S15 showed a higher increment in compressive strength of 34.74 %, 29.84 %, and 29.3 %, respectively, at the age of 28 days. At 56 days, the strength increment was observed between 3 and 15 %. The mixes U0S5, U10S5, and U15S5 showed a 15 % increase in compressive strength from 28 to 56 days of curing period. From 56 to 90 days, only a 3–11 % increase in strength was observed. The CC mix showed only 3.15 %, and the mix U5S10 showed an 11 % increase in compressive strength.

However, some mixes show significant increments in compressive strength at some point in time, but it may not lead to higher compressive strength. For example, the mix U10S15 and U15S10 showed an increment in the strength of 29.3 % and 29.42 %, respectively, but the obtained strength of U10S15 and U15S10 was 86.17 MPa and 77.56 MPa, respectively, at 28 days. The 90-day test results revealed a significant improvement in compressive strength compared to CC due to the slow pozzolanic reaction of UFFA and SF [50]. In binary mixes, S0U10 and U0S15 showed higher compressive strength results at 28 and 90 days. Whereas in ternary mixes, the mix with U10 combination U10S5, U10S10, and U10S15 showed higher compressive strength results than U5 and U15 combinations at later ages. In the U5 mix, U5S5 and U15 mix U15S15 showed higher strength results at 28 and 90 days. The mix U10S15 showed higher compressive strength results than the remaining mixes, showing an 11.9 % improvement in strength compared to the controlled mix. A low UFFA and SF replacement ratio (≤30 %) in high-performance concrete (HPC) allows finer particles to fill gaps between coarser particles, enhancing wet packing density (WPD). The excess UFFA and SF particles in the mix will disturb typical particle packing due to the “wall effect.” As the replacement ratio increases further, the spaces between particles become larger than the initial voids, leading to a reduction in the WPD. Incorporating excess finer particles in the HPC system has a ‘loosening effect’ rather than a ‘filling effect,’ resulting in lower WPD [7]. This is because the increased packing density would decrease the voids content, which in turn would reduce the water demand and the quantity of water required to fill the voids [36].

In all the HPC mixes, the maximum size of aggregates was restricted to 12.5 mm because there is an inverse relationship between the size of aggregates and the ultrasound wave velocity. The key reason is that a larger aggregate size will result in a higher local water-cement ratio in the transition zone. This, in turn, will cause a higher capillary void count in the transition zone, lowering the ultrasonic velocity into the concrete. The Pore volume and ultrasonic wave velocity in concrete also have an inverse relationship with extended curing periods. The volume of capillary pores in concrete decreases over time. This is due to the degree of hydration of cement with UFFA and SF, which depends on the duration of hydration, as well as other curing factors, such as temperature and humidity, which were constant in this study [52].Fig. 8Relationship between UPV and fc of HPC mixes at 90 days.Fig. 8

Durability tests

Based on the compressive strength and NDT results, binary mixes U10S0 and U0S15 and ternary mixes U10S10, U10S15, and U15U15 mixes showed superior results than other mixes. The selected mixes were subjected to durability tests like water absorption and water permeability tests, and the obtained results were compared with the controlled concrete (CC) results.

Water absorption test by immersion

The obtained water absorption test results of the HPC mix at the age of 28 and 90 days are found using Eqn (1) are shown in Fig. 11. There was a decrease in the water absorption rate seen in 90-day cured specimens due to the continuation of the hydration process even after 28 days. Conventional concrete shows the highest water absorption rate at 28 and 90 days. CC shows a 17 % decrease in water absorption with time. In the case of binary mixes, UFFA with 10 % showed a 28 % and SF with 15 % showed a 20.75 % decrease in absorption. The mix U10S10 and U15S15 showed a 20 % and 23.95 % reduction in water absorption with time. The water absorption rate primarily depends on the voids and their connection to the concrete. Although the interfacial transition zone (ITZ) comprises 1/3 to 1/2 of the hardened cement paste volume in concrete, it has a distinct microstructure from the paste within the composite. The location of the first microcracks in concrete may have a major impact on its permeability and water absorption [4,55]. The volume of voids present in the HPC concrete mixes at the age of 28 and 90 days are calculated using Eqn (2) and represented in Fig. 12. Due to more voids in concrete, a higher water absorption percentage was absorbed in the controlled concrete. In all the other mixes, there is a decrease in the volume of pores absorbed at a 90-day curing period than 28 days. A 17.3 % decrease in voids was observed in CC. In binary mixes, U10S0 showed a 33 % reduction in voids; in the U0S15 mix, only 13.1 % was observed. In ternary HPC mixes, U10S15 showed the least volume of voids with a 22.3 % reduction with time. In contrast, the U10S10 and U15S15 mixes showed 8.57 % and 11 % reduction in pores concerning with time. Therefore, it is evidenced that with increasing the fineness of fly ash, the rate of reaction in concrete was increased, which further leads to a decrease in pores in concrete, which makes concrete more resistant to water and chemical penetrations. Also, the SF and UFFA combination showed better durability over time. The mix U10S15 showed higher strength and greater resistance to water absorption in HPC. The increased packing density of CM can enhance concrete workability by reducing voids and water demand, increasing the surplus water ratio. However, the fineness and shape of cementitious materials can affect the concrete's workability. The spherical shape of UFFA and SF will improve flow through the ball-bearing effect; SF's higher fineness increases surface area, reduces water film thickness, and reduces flow [36]. The study showed that UFFA and SF react with Ca (OH)2 to form C-S-H gel, enhancing concrete microstructure. Since C-S-H gel is insoluble, it improves concrete water resistance by filling spaces between solid particles. Introducing UFFA and SF in concrete was more effective in lowering Ca (OH)2 content due to its ultra-fine spherical form and high amorphous silicon dioxide content. Adding these effective CMs was more effective in enhancing concrete compressive strength and water penetration resistance [7].Fig. 12The volume of permeable pore spaces in HPC mixes.Fig. 12

In binary mixes, UFFA with 10 % showed greater resistance to water penetration than the SF mix, and it showed 24 % higher resistance at 90 days [56] due to its greater pozzolanic reactivity, and better packing of this combination showed higher durability properties. Optimizing the wet packing density (WPD) of concrete with suitable pozzolanic ingredients may significantly boost strength and segregation resistance while reducing water permeability and incorporating UFFA and SF into high-performance concrete results in impermeable concrete that resists the penetration of detrimental ions, extending the concrete's service life [7].Fig. 13Water penetration depth in HPC mixes.Fig. 13

Adding SF and UFFA improves the performance of calcite crystals in controlling the microstructure of hydrated cement composites.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermogravimetric analysis on concrete gives a quantitative measurement of hydration products with temperature. The obtained TGA curves of the hydrated HPC samples after 28 days and 90 days of curing are shown in Fig. 15. The mass loss of HPC samples was measured at a heating rate from ambient temperature to 800 °C at an increment of 20 °C per minute. The first two endothermic peaks correspond to free water loss within pores and dehydration of some hydrated compounds like C-S-H and ettringite. Castelotte [61] discovered that ettringite decomposes around 90 °C, whereas C-S-H gel dehydrates between 150 °C and 400 °C. The endothermal peaks observed between 150 °C and 220 °C might be ascribed to the presence of calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) and calcium aluminate hydrates (C-A-H). The next endothermic peak witnessed between 400 °C and 500 °C is associated with portlandite (CH) disintegration. The peak between 500 °C and 800 °C indicates carbonate phase decomposition. The water loss peak was observed at a temperature up to 450 °C due to the breakdown of portlandite [62,63].Fig. 15TGA curves of HPC mixes at 28 and 90 days.Fig. 15

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectra were performed to detect the functional groups in the selected HPC mixes. The test was conducted on the chosen mixes CC, U10S15, and U15S15, which were obtained based on higher durability criteria. This FTIR technique is employed to detect inorganic molecules such as Si-O, aluminosilicate (Si-O-Al), -OH, H-O-H (water), C-O (carbonate), and S-O (sulfate or gypsum) found in hydrated cement. The significant bands in hardened cement composites include calcium carbonate (871, 1792–2516 cm−1), carbonate (CO32−) (1390–1475 cm−1), strength-giving compounds or Al-Si compounds such as C–S–H (950–1055), water or hydroxyl molecules (1607, 3400–3650 cm−1), and sulfate (600–680, 1080-1100 cm−1). The presence of AlO4 and SiO4 groups can be seen in the range 1200–950 cm−1 [65]. Some factors that might alter the spectra bands include variations in molar mass, water-to-binder ratio, pH, temperature, Ca/Si, and Si/Al ratio used during binder production. FTIR links bond properties to mineral properties to support X-ray diffraction (XRD) with additional details. It is also helpful in characterizing bonds in hydroxyl and aluminosilicate-based compounds. Linear or cross-link chain products could also distinguish between crystalline and amorphous materials [66].

The FTIR spectra show a broad peak ranging from 2900 to 3635 cm−1 in UFFA and SF HPC mixes at 28 days, indicating Ca(OH) presence, whereas in the reference mixture, no peak was observed in Fig. 19. This broad peak was due to the formation of the H-O-H hydrogen bond and asymmetric stretching of the -OH molecules. In U10S15 and U15S15, the smooth surface of SF and UFFA particles did not observe much water for hydration even at 28 days, and the peak vanished at 90 days due to complete hydration, similar to the CC mix. This was also confirmed from TGA test results that in the mixes U10S15 and U15S15, at the age of 28 days, The loss of water due to free water and bound water was very high between 30 °C to 400 °C whereas it became minimal compared to reference mix at the age of 90 days. The FTIR peaks between 900 and 1100 cm−1 correspond to vibrations of the Si-O bond in the C-S-H phase, also identified by XRD analysis at 21° and 29.45°. Blended samples containing UFFA and SF exhibit higher relative intensity of the C-S-H than control samples. The hydration of C3S and C2S causes a decrease in the intensity of peaks at 1970-2200 cm−1 with curing time in UFFA and SF concrete mixes and is shown in Fig. 20. Because of C-O stretching, the bands detected around 1400 cm−1 and 970 cm−1 shift to the lower wave number when silica content increases. Moreover, ettringite, mono-sulfate, and C-S-H bands are observed at 980-1180 cm−1 and move to the lower side in the U15S15 sample [67]. The existence of C3S and C3A in concrete mixtures in the FTIR spectra can be confirmed by searching for bands at 528 and 626 cm−1. Because of C-O stretching, the bands noticed around 1400 and 970 cm−1 show a shift towards the smaller wave number when silica content increases [16]. The mix U10S15 and U15S15 show shifting in the FTIR band at 970 cm−1 toward the lower wave number. This shifting of the bands indicates that the hydration reaction has enabled the C-S-H phase in the selected HPC mixes.Fig. 19FTIR spectroscopic curves for CC, U10S15, and U15S15 mixes.Fig. 19Fig. 20FTIR spectroscopic curves in closer view.Fig. 20

Conclusions

The present study developed a novel High-Performance Concrete by incorporating ultrafine fly ash and silica fume into a conventional concrete mix at various proportions with a w/b ratio of 0.28. Over 300 specimens were tested to evaluate the optimum blend based on its strength, durability, and microstructural characteristics. The following are significant findings from this study:1.The binary HPC mixes containing 10 % UFFA and 15 % SF replacement exhibited superior compressive strengths, reaching 99.8 MPa and 98.32 MPa, respectively. These mixes also demonstrated excellent quality with an ultrasonic pulse velocity of 5.23 and 5.19 km/s and surface resistance with a rebound number of 57.5 and 57.3, respectively.2.In ternary HPC mixes, the mix U10S15 exhibited a significant improvement in compressive strengths of around 12 % at 90 days due to the extended pozzolanic reaction of SF and UFFA. It showed excellent concrete quality with an ultrasonic pulse velocity of 5.28 km/s and surface resistance with a rebound number of 60.22, respectively.3.The mix U10S15 showed lower water absorption of 1.26 %, which shows a 44.9 % reduction in water absorption over time when compared to controlled concrete due to its pore refinement at a lower level and improved wet packing density.4.The water permeability of the mix U10S15 showed a 48.3 % reduction in water penetration due to its pore refining effect. It also showed a 35 % reduction in the percentage of voids when compared with controlled concrete at 90 days.5.TGA confirms the formation of additional hydration compounds like calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) and calcium aluminum hydrate (C-A-H) by consuming a more considerable amount of portlandite (CH), and Ca (CO)3 was observed in the ternary mixes. XRD peaks also supported the existence of additional hydration peaks in blended specimens at 28° of 2θ. A decrease in CH peaks in the U10S15 mix resulted in the formation of additional CSH gel.6.The FTIR analysis on HPC mixes confirmed that adding the optimum dosages of UFFA and SF forms a dense microstructure. The U10S15 and U15S15 mixes show a significant peak shift from 950 to 980 cm⁻1. This shift indicates higher C-S-H gel formation, characterized by a lower calcium-silicate ratio.7.The obtained optimum U10S15 HPC mix has revealed superior strength and durability properties compared to all other mixes due to its effective participation in the hydration reaction. This makes the concrete more dense due to improved wet packing density at the micro level (filling effect). The obtained mix can be effectively used in prestressed concrete structures, high-rise buildings, etc. Due to its greater resistance to water penetration, the optimized mix can be effectively utilized in water-retaining and marine structures.
