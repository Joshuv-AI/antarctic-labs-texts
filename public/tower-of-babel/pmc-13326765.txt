Development of Chimeric Antigen Receptor‐Modified NK Cells for the Immunotherapy of Adult T‐Cell Leukemia/Lymphoma
Authors: Yuichiro Uchida, Seigo Tateo, Masahiro Wakao, Hiroyuki Shinchi, Hayate Sawayama, Akira Fukumoto, Makoto Yoshimitsu, Yasuo Suda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAdult T‐cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATL) is an aggressive hematological malignancy with a poor prognosis and limited therapeutic options. ATL cells exhibit aberrant surface glycosylation patterns resulting from dysregulated glycosyltransferase expression, representing a potentially tumor‐specific therapeutic target. We previously isolated an ATL‐specific single‐chain variable fragment (scFv), S1TSCFR3‐1, that selectively binds to ATL cell surface glycans. In this study, we engineered natural killer (NK) cells to express a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) incorporating S1TSCFR3‐1 and evaluated their therapeutic potential against ATL. In vitro, CAR‐NK cells exhibited dose‐dependent cytotoxicity against ATL cell lines, mediated primarily through enhanced granzyme B production, whereas no augmentation of cytotoxic activity was observed against non‐ATL cell lines. S1TSCFR3‐1 binding was detected in PBMCs from 3 of 7 ATL patients, and CAR‐NK cells demonstrated significant cytotoxicity against PBMCs from a binding‐positive patient. In a xenograft mouse model, administration of irradiated CAR‐NK cells partially suppressed tumor burden as evidenced by reduced serum hsIL‐2R levels, although survival benefit did not reach statistical significance. These findings suggest that CAR‐NK cell therapy targeting ATL‐associated aberrant glycan epitopes represents a potentially valuable therapeutic approach warranting further investigation.

Introduction

Adult T‐cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATL) is a highly aggressive hematological malignancy caused by human T‐cell leukemia virus type 1 (HTLV‐1), which targets CD4‐positive T lymphocytes [1]. Four‐year survival rates depend on clinical subtype: acute type (11.4%–16.8%), lymphoma type (16.2%–19.6%), chronic type (35.6%–37.4%), and smoldering type (51.9%–59.8%) [2, 3]. The acute and lymphoma types are particularly aggressive, with poor prognoses [4]. Primary treatment for these forms is combination chemotherapy [4, 5]. However, ATL cells exhibit substantial genetic heterogeneity, leading to high chemotherapy resistance and frequent recurrence with median overall survival of only 6–13 months. Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo‐HSCT) is another option, but response rates are limited to approximately 30%, and long‐term disease control remains challenging due to high relapse rates and transplant‐related morbidity [4, 6]. Targeted therapies such as mogamulizumab, a defucosylated anti‐CCR4 monoclonal antibody, have demonstrated clinical activity in relapsed or refractory ATL; however, overall response rates remain modest and median overall survival after treatment initiation is as short as 5.5 months in real‐world settings, indicating that durable remissions are rarely achieved [7, 8]. Moreover, loss of CCR4 expression has been identified as a resistance mechanism following mogamulizumab treatment, highlighting the limitations of single‐antigen targeting strategies in a disease characterized by clonal evolution and antigen escape [9]. These challenges highlight the critical need for developing new therapeutic strategies.

Cellular immunotherapy has shown promise for various malignant neoplasms, including hematologic malignancies and multiple myeloma. In particular, CAR‐T and CAR‐NK cell‐based therapies have yielded encouraging results in multiple clinical trials [10]. In these approaches, T and NK cells serve as effector cells capable of directly lysing tumor cells [11]. However, in the context of T‐cell malignancies such as ATL, CAR T‐cell therapy faces unique obstacles. Because malignant T cells often share surface antigens with therapeutic CAR T cells, expression of the target antigen on effector cells leads to fratricide, thereby limiting their in vivo expansion and impairing tumoricidal activity [12]. Additionally, shared antigen expression between malignant and non‐malignant T cells complicates the harvesting of sufficient autologous T cells, as malignant cells frequently contaminate the leukapheresis product, compromising both the quality and safety of the manufactured CAR T‐cell product [12]. To overcome these inherent limitations—including restricted in vivo cell expansion and the inability to bypass tumor‐derived inhibitory signals—chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) technology continues to be refined as a more robust therapeutic strategy [13].

Building on CAR T‐cell therapy success in hematological malignancies, we aimed to develop new cellular immunotherapy for ATL. To address CAR T‐cell therapy limitations and create safer treatment for ATL patients, we focused on using NK cells as the cellular platform. NK cells have gained attention as potential off‐the‐shelf therapeutic agents due to their distinct biological characteristics. Unlike T cells, NK cells from different donors or cell lines pose a much lower risk of causing GVHD. Furthermore, NK cells primarily release noninflammatory cytokines upon activation, significantly lowering the risk of cytokine release syndrome, a severe side effect commonly linked to CAR T‐cell therapy [14, 15].

Alterations in cell surface glycosylation patterns are widely recognized as a hallmark of cellular transformation and have been considered potential biomarkers for cancer diagnosis [16]. Aberrant glycosylation arising from dysregulated glycosyltransferase expression during malignant transformation results in tumor‐associated glycan structures that are absent or minimally expressed on normal tissues, making them attractive targets for antigen‐specific immunotherapy [17]. Our previous study successfully isolated a single‐chain variable fragment (scFv), named S1TSCFR3‐1, which specifically binds to cell surface glycans on the ATL cell line S1T [18]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that CAR‐NK cells expressing this ATL‐specific scFv would offer a safer and more effective immunotherapy for ATL. To test this, we engineered CAR‐NK cells with S1TSCFR3‐1 as the targeting moiety and evaluated their potential as a novel ATL treatment.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

Cell culture media and reagents are detailed in the Supporting Information. NK‐92‐MI cells [19] and derived CAR‐NK cells were cultured in complete MEMα (MEMα supplemented with 12.5% FBS and 1% PS) at 37°C, 5% CO2. S1T, Su9T01, MT‐2, and MOLT‐4 cells were maintained in complete RPMI‐1640 (RPMI‐1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 0.5% PS, and 1% L‐glutamine) with HEPES under the same conditions. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee for Life Science and Medical Research Involving Human Subjects at Kagoshima University. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their family members before sample collection.

Preparation of CAR‐NK Cells

NK cells (1 × 106) were transduced with CAR‐encoding lentivirus (VectorBuilder, Yokohama, Japan) at 5 × 107 or 1.5 × 108 TU/mL (50 μL) and incubated at 37°C, 5% CO₂ for 3 days, followed by sequential expansion in 24‐well plates, T‐25, and T‐75 flasks as detailed in the Supporting Information. EGFP‐positive cells were sorted using a cell sorter and cultured in complete MEMα. S1TSCFR3‐1 was prepared as previously described [19]. CAR‐10‐35‐NK (Japanese Patent Application No. 2022‐165505) was used as a negative control. NK‐92‐MI cells were γ‐ray irradiated (10 Gy) in 10 mL of culture media using a cobalt‐60 source (MDS Nordion, GC‐220, Washington, D.C., USA) prior to in vivo administration.

Western Blot Analysis of CAR Expression

NK cells and CAR‐NK cells (2 × 107) were harvested, washed twice with PBS, and lysed in RIPA buffer for 30 min on ice. After centrifugation at 10000 × g for 10 min, protein concentrations were determined by Bradford assay. Cell lysates (2.7 mg/mL) were mixed with 2‐ME‐containing sample buffer, heated at 100°C for 10 min, and separated on a 12.5% SDS‐PAGE gel. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with 5% skim milk in PBS/T, and incubated with anti‐CD247 antibody (1:1000) followed by HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000). Bands were visualized using Chemi‐Lumi One.

Analysis of CAR Expression by Flow Cytometry

NK cells or CAR‐NK cells were resuspended in PBS at 1 × 106 cells/mL and incubated with anti‐Myc‐tag antibody (1:500 in FACS buffer) for 30 min at 4°C, followed by CF633‐conjugated anti‐mouse IgG antibody (1:1000 in PBS) for 30 min at 4°C. Cells were washed with PBS and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Calcein and PI Staining of Cell Lines

NK cells were washed with PBS and resuspended at 1 × 106 cells/45 μL in Calcein AM solution (1:1500 in PBS). After incubation for 10 min at 37°C in 5% CO2, cells were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in complete RPMI‐1640 at 1 × 106 cells/mL. Dead cells were identified by staining with propidium iodide (PI) solution immediately prior to flow cytometric analysis.

Generation of ATL Transplant Model Mice

NOG mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from the Central Institute for Experimental Medicine and Life Science (Kawasaki, Japan). ATL models were established by injecting 1 × 107 S1T cells suspended in PBS (100 μL) into mouse tail veins, followed by a 10‐day engraftment period. Serum hsIL‐2R levels were measured using a Human CD25/IL‐2R alpha Quantikine ELISA Kit (R&D Systems, Minnesota, USA) as a surrogate marker of tumor burden. This study was approved (VM23036) by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Kagoshima University.

Results

Design and Expression Verification of CAR Construct in NK Cells

The CAR construct was designed based on the framework developed by You et al. [20]. The structure of the CAR was arranged from the N‐terminus in the following order: CD8α, S1TSCFR3‐1, Myc‐tag, CD28 hinge domain, CD28 transmembrane domain, 4‐1BB signaling domain, and CD3ζ signaling domain (Figure 1A,B). EGFP, controlled by the CMV promoter, was incorporated as a transduction marker (Figure 1A,B). The complete domain expression sequence was designed for integration into a lentiviral vector.

Following the design, NK cells were transduced with lentiviral vectors at 1:10 or 1:30 ratios for 3 days. Flow cytometric analysis revealed EGFP‐positive cells at 1.5% (1:10) and 2.5% (1:30) ratios (Figure S1A–C). EGFP‐positive cells were subsequently sorted and expanded, achieving approximately 100% purity of CAR‐NK cells (Figure S1D,E).

CAR protein expression was verified using Western blotting and flow cytometry. Western blot analysis with purified anti‐CD247 (TCRζ, CD3ζ) antibody showed a band around 60 kDa in CAR‐NK cell lysates, while no band was observed in NK cell lysates. The band at 60 kDa likely reflects glycosylation of the CAR protein domains during biosynthesis. These results confirmed expression of the complete CAR construct (Figure 1C).

Flow cytometry analysis using anti‐Myc‐tag monoclonal antibody showed positive staining in CAR‐NK cells but not in NK cells (Figure 1D–G), confirming surface expression of the scFv portion of the CAR construct.

In Vitro Cell‐Killing Activity of CAR‐NK Cells Against ATL Cell Lines

The cytotoxic activity of CAR‐NK cells was evaluated by flow cytometry using ATL cell lines (S1T, Su9T01, MT‐2) and non‐ATL cell lines (MOLT‐4, Jurkat, HL60) as targets. NK, CAR‐NK, and CAR‐10‐35‐NK cells were co‐cultured with target cells at effector‐to‐target (E:T) ratios of 1:10, 1:5, and 1:1 for 24 h.

When co‐cultured with ATL cell lines, CAR‐NK cells demonstrated dose‐dependent cytotoxicity achieving substantial target cell killing at the highest E:T ratio, whereas unmodified NK cells showed no significant cytotoxic activity (Figure 2A–C). These results suggest that CAR introduction enhanced the targeting and cytotoxic activity of NK cells against ATL cells.

In vitro cell‐killing activity of CAR‐NK cells against ATL cell lines. Cell viability of (A) S1T, (B) Su9T01, (C) MT‐2, (D) MOLT‐4, (E) Jurkat, and (F) HL60 cells following 24‐h co‐culture with NK cells, CAR‐NK cells, or CAR‐10‐35‐NK cells at the indicated effector‐to‐target (E:T) ratios. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from three independent experiments (n = 3). Statistical significance was determined by Student's t‐test. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01.

When non‐ATL cell lines (MOLT‐4, Jurkat, and HL60) were used as target cells, NK cells tended to show comparable or slightly higher cytotoxic activity than CAR‐NK cells (Figure 2D–F). CAR‐NK cells did not exhibit enhanced killing against these non‐ATL cell lines, suggesting that the introduced CAR did not augment cytotoxicity toward CAR‐antigen‐negative targets.

Analysis of Cytokines and Cytotoxic Factors Produced by CAR‐NK Cells

We examined whether CAR‐NK cell cytotoxicity against ATL cells was due to CAR‐mediated activation following antigen recognition. Since CAR produces IFN‐γ through CD3ζ‐mediated signaling upon antigen recognition [21], we measured IFN‐γ production. NK or CAR‐NK cells were co‐cultured with ATL cell lines at a 1:1 ratio for 24 h. Co‐culturing CAR‐NK cells with ATL cell lines led to higher IFN‐γ production compared to unmodified NK cells (Figure 3A–E). In co‐cultures with MT‐2 cells, high levels of IFN‐γ were observed in both NK and CAR‐NK cell co‐cultures with no significant difference between groups. IFN‐γ production was not detected in co‐cultures with MOLT‐4 cells. These findings suggest that CAR‐NK cell cytotoxic activity against ATL cell lines is triggered by CAR‐mediated antigen recognition.

NK cells are known to induce target cell death through the release of granzyme and perforin [22]. To determine if CAR‐NK cells produce granzyme B and perforin upon ATL cell recognition, NK or CAR‐NK cells were co‐cultured with S1T, Su9T01, MT‐2, and MOLT‐4 cells at a 1:1 ratio for 24 h, and supernatant levels were measured by ELISA. Granzyme B production was significantly higher in CAR‐NK cell co‐cultures compared to NK cells when ATL cell lines were used as targets (Figure 3F–H). Co‐cultures with MOLT‐4 cells showed higher granzyme B production in NK cell co‐cultures than in CAR‐NK cell co‐cultures (Figure 3I). Perforin production was higher in NK cell co‐cultures than in CAR‐NK cell co‐cultures across all target cell lines tested (Figure 3J–M). These findings suggest that CAR‐NK cells primarily induce ATL cell death through enhanced granzyme B production.

Analysis of CAR‐NK Cell Cytotoxicity Against PBMCs Derived From ATL Patients

To assess whether CAR‐NK cells exhibit cytotoxicity against ATL cells derived from patients, we first evaluated the binding ability of scFv against PBMCs from seven ATL patients. These PBMCs contain both ATL and non‐ATL cells. To identify the ATL cells within the patient‐derived PBMCs, we used antibodies against SynCAM, a known marker for ATL cells [23], and analyzed the binding of S1TSCFR3‐1 to ATL cells via flow cytometry. Healthy donor‐derived PBMCs were used as control cells. Binding analysis of PBMCs from ATL patients showed that S1TSCFR3‐1 bound to SynCAM‐positive cells in some patients (#1, #4, #6), but not in others (#2, #3, #5, #7) (Figure 4A,B). No SynCAM‐positive cells or S1TSCFR3‐1 binding was detected in healthy donor PBMCs (Figure 4A,B). Representative flow cytometry dot plots illustrating the distribution of SynCAM‐positive and S1TSCFR3‐1‐binding cell populations are shown in Figure 4A, and the proportions of double‐positive cells across all donors are summarized in Figure 4B.

Co‐culture with NK cells resulted in higher viability compared to PBMCs cultured alone. In contrast, CAR‐NK cells reduced the viability of ATL patient‐derived PBMCs by approximately 35% compared to NK cells (Figure 4C). No cytotoxicity was observed when CAR‐NK cells were co‐cultured with healthy donor PBMCs (Figure 4D). These results suggest that our CAR‐NK cells selectively target and kill ATL cells within PBMCs from ATL patients, mediated by the targeting ability of S1TSCFR3‐1.

The clinical characteristics of the seven ATL patients whose PBMCs were used in the ex vivo experiments are summarized in Table 1. The cohort included three patients with acute‐type ATL and four with chronic‐type ATL; however, all four chronic‐type patients had an unfavorable prognosis. Four patients received mLSG15 combination chemotherapy, with best responses of progressive disease (PD), stable disease (SD), complete response (CR), and partial response (PR), respectively; three received mogamulizumab‐containing CHOP (Moga‐CHOP) therapy, achieving CR, CR, and PR, respectively. Therapeutic Efficacy of CAR‐NK Cell Therapy in ATL Xenograft Mouse Models

The therapeutic efficacy of CAR‐NK cells against ATL cells was tested in ATL xenograft mouse models. To establish S1T cell engraftment, 1 × 107 S1T cells were injected into the mice via the tail vein. After 10 days, CAR‐NK cells were administered through the tail vein at different doses: 0.2 × 106 cells (low dose), 5 × 106 cells (high dose), and 1 × 107 cells of irradiation‐inactivated CAR‐NK cells (10 Gy γ‐radiation) (high dose (IR)). Serum hsIL‐2R was measured as a surrogate marker of ATL tumor burden [24]. On day 18, mice treated with high dose IR CAR‐NK cells exhibited significantly lower serum hsIL‐2R levels compared to the untreated group (p < 0.01, two‐tailed unpaired Student's t‐test; Figure 5A). Group sizes were n = 5 per treated group and n = 4 for the untreated group, the latter reduced to minimize the number of experimental animals. Serum LDH levels were also measured as an additional marker of tissue damage; however, no significant differences were observed between the treated and untreated groups (data not shown). These findings indicate that CAR‐NK cells exerted antitumor activity against S1T cells in vivo.

Ten days after tumor inoculation, CAR‐NK cells were administered intravenously at the following doses: Low dose (0.2 × 106 cells), high dose (5 × 106 cells), or high dose with 10 Gy γ‐irradiation inactivation (high dose IR; 1 × 107 cells). Statistical comparison was performed between the untreated (n = 4) and high dose IR CAR‐NK cell–treated (n = 5) groups using the log‐rank test (p = 0.258; HR = 2.03, 95% CI: 0.51–8.11).

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed a trend toward improved survival in the high dose IR CAR‐NK cell–treated group compared to the untreated group, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (log‐rank test: p = 0.258; HR = 2.03, 95% CI: 0.51–8.11; Figure 5B). Mice in the low‐dose and high‐dose CAR‐NK‐ or NK‐treated groups died within approximately 4–5 weeks of CAR‐NK or NK cell administration, prior to the development of lymph node tumors, due to multi‐organ failure predominantly involving hepatic tumor formation, likely attributable to NK cell administration. Macroscopic examination of mice at the experimental endpoint revealed tumor formation in multiple lymph nodes in a subset of mice from both the untreated and high‐dose IR CAR‐NK‐treated groups. These results collectively suggest that CAR‐NK cells possess in vivo antitumor activity, though further optimization is warranted.

Discussion

In this study, we generated CAR‐NK cells incorporating a glycan‐targeting scFv, S1TSCFR3‐1, and demonstrated selective in vitro cytotoxicity against ATL cell lines. When co‐cultured with ATL cell lines (S1T, Su9T01, and MT‐2), CAR‐NK cells demonstrated dose‐dependent cytotoxicity achieving substantial target cell killing, whereas unmodified NK cells showed no significant cytotoxic activity against the same targets. In contrast, when non‐ATL cell lines (MOLT‐4, Jurkat, and HL60) were used as targets, CAR‐NK cells did not exhibit enhanced killing compared with unmodified NK cells. Notably, MOLT‐4 cells have been reported to be inherently sensitive to NK‐mediated lysis [25], which likely accounts for the comparable cytotoxic activity observed between NK and CAR‐NK cells against this line, confirming that the augmented cytotoxicity was not directed toward CAR‐antigen‐negative cells. These results indicate that the introduced CAR specifically enhanced NK cell cytotoxicity against ATL cells and establish proof‐of‐concept for CAR‐NK therapy targeting ATL‐associated aberrant glycan epitopes recognized by S1TSCFR3‐1.

Mechanistic analyses indicated that CAR‐NK cells primarily mediate cytotoxicity through granzyme B rather than perforin. Co‐culture with ATL cell lines significantly increased granzyme B production in CAR‐NK cells compared with unmodified NK cells, whereas perforin levels were comparable between the two groups. NK cells are known to induce target cell death by producing granzyme B and perforin during cytotoxic responses [26]. Recent studies have demonstrated that granzyme B can function through both perforin‐dependent and ‐independent pathways [27] and the observed differential expression is consistent with this emerging evidence [28]. CAR‐NK cells co‐cultured with ATL cell lines also produced higher levels of IFN‐γ than unmodified NK cells, with the exception of MT‐2 cells. Since NK cells are known to be activated by IFN‐γ and subsequently produce IFN‐γ themselves, it is plausible that IFN‐γ produced by MT‐2 cells activated the NK cells independently of CAR signaling in this experiment. Collectively, these results suggest that CAR‐NK cells recognize ATL targets via the scFv and induce cell death through granzyme B‐predominant cytotoxic activity.

Analysis of primary patient material revealed that S1TSCFR3‐1 bound to SynCAM‐positive cells in 3 of 7 ATL patient PBMC samples (#1, #4, #6), while no binding was detected in healthy donor lymphocytes. CAR‐NK cells demonstrated significant cytotoxic activity against PBMCs from patient #1, in whom scFv binding was confirmed. However, the observation that scFv binding was absent in 4 of 7 patients tested is an important limitation. Glycosylation alterations can arise from genetic origins or result from dysregulation of glycosylation biosynthesis mechanisms in response to pathological conditions like inflammation, infection, and malignant transformation [29]. Aberrant O‐glycosylation is frequently observed in cancer, correlating with tumor aggressiveness and adverse clinical outcomes [30]. These observations may reflect differences in cell surface glycan expression patterns associated with disease subtype and stage. Indeed, recent whole genome sequencing has demonstrated greater mutational diversity in aggressive‐type ATL [31], which may contribute to variations in surface glycan expression across disease stages. These findings underscore the need for larger, well‐characterized patient cohorts to define the prevalence and predictors of S1TSCFR3‐1 binding across ATL subtypes.

The modest increase in PBMC viability observed upon co‐culture with unmodified NK cells in the ATL patient sample (#1) warrants consideration. NK‐92‐MI cells spontaneously produce IL‐2 to support their proliferation [32], and since healthy donor PBMCs contain fewer IL‐2‐sensitive cells, their viability was unaffected. However, human IL‐2 is also known to enhance the viability of ATL cells [33], and consequently IL‐2 production by NK‐92‐MI cells may have influenced the survival of ATL cells within the PBMC population.

In the ATL xenograft model, transplantation of S1T cells into NOG mice resulted in progressive elevation of serum hsIL‐2R, a validated surrogate marker of ATL tumor burden [34]. Administration of γ‐irradiated CAR‐NK cells attenuated the rise in blood hsIL‐2R concentrations compared with controls, suggesting partial suppression of tumor expansion. However, survival analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant benefit of CAR‐NK cell treatment, a finding that tempers the interpretation of the hsIL‐2R data and underscores the limitations of the current in vivo model. The use of γ‐irradiated CAR‐NK cells was necessitated by xenograft‐related immune complications: our preliminary studies showed that NOG mice were adversely affected by viable CAR‐NK and NK cells, whereas NOD‐SCID mice rejected the human cells. Therefore, we employed high numbers of γ‐ray irradiated CAR‐NK cells, an approach commonly used in preclinical CAR‐NK cell experiments [35]. Irradiation eliminates the proliferative and persistent activity of the infused cells, fundamentally limiting their in vivo antitumor potential. Future studies should explore alternative immunodeficient mouse models or humanized platforms that permit the use of non‐irradiated CAR‐NK cells.

A critical consideration in selecting NK cells over T cells as the CAR platform for ATL relates to the unique challenges of autologous T‐cell manufacturing in this disease. ATL patients typically have received multiple lines of prior chemotherapy, resulting in profound lymphopenia that can reduce the number and functional fitness of collectible T cells [36]. In the specific context of ATL, this problem is further compounded by the fact that malignant T cells circulate in the peripheral blood and can contaminate the leukapheresis product, creating a risk of inadvertently engineering CAR constructs into leukemic cells [36, 37], and T cells recovered from heavily pretreated patients often demonstrate impaired proliferative capacity and rapid exhaustion [36]. In contrast, CAR‐NK cells circumvent these issues: NK cells do not share the T‐cell antigens targeted by the CAR, substantially reducing the risk of fratricide; CAR‐NK cells have emerged as a promising platform for allogeneic, off‐the‐shelf immunotherapy owing to their favorable safety profile, including a lower risk of graft‐versus‐host disease, cytokine release syndrome, and immune effector cell‐associated neurotoxicity syndrome [38]; and NK cells intrinsically express activating receptors such as NKG2D, NKp30, and NKp46, which recognize stress‐induced ligands frequently upregulated on malignant cells, providing an additional CAR‐independent mechanism of tumor recognition [39]. The NK‐92‐MI cell line represents a well‐characterized and continuously expandable platform that enables standardized and scalable manufacturing independent of patient‐derived starting material [13]. NK‐92‐MI‐derived CAR‐NK cells can be expanded to large numbers under GMP conditions from a single master cell bank and cryopreserved for immediate availability—an important logistical advantage for patients with rapidly progressive ATL. From a regulatory standpoint, NK‐92‐MI‐derived cell therapies require γ‐irradiation prior to infusion to prevent uncontrolled proliferation in vivo, a step already implemented in the present study.

Several limitations of the present study must be acknowledged. First, the patient cohort for primary sample analyses was limited in size and incompletely characterized with respect to ATL subtype, disease history, and prior therapy, which constrained the assessment of predictive biomarkers for scFv binding. Second, the potential for S1TSCFR3‐1 to recognize glycan epitopes on normal tissues represents an important safety consideration for clinical translation. Although surface plasmon resonance analysis has previously characterized the binding specificity of S1TSCFR3‐1 to its target glycan structure [18], systematic evaluation of cross‐reactivity with normal tissue antigens has not yet been performed, and off‐target binding cannot be excluded. Future preclinical studies should therefore assess scFv binding to normal peripheral blood cell populations by flow cytometry, followed by histochemical staining of a diverse normal tissue panel. These analyses will be essential to establish the safety profile of S1TSCFR3‐1‐modified CAR‐NK cells prior to any clinical application. Third, long‐term cytotoxicity and serial killing capacity of CAR‐NK cells were not assessed in the present study. As NK‐92‐MI cells caused death in mice within approximately 4–5 weeks without irradiation, γ‐irradiation is required prior to administration, which inherently limits their proliferative capacity and in vivo persistence. This constraint represents a fundamental challenge for conducting long‐term efficacy studies using this platform. Fourth, irradiation of CAR‐NK cells in the xenograft model eliminated their proliferative capacity, limiting the translational relevance of the in vivo findings; future studies should therefore prioritize the development of improved xenograft models that accommodate irradiated off‐the‐shelf CAR‐NK cells while enabling more robust assessment of antitumor efficacy. Fifth, NOG mice, while valuable for studying hematological malignancy pathogenesis and evaluating therapeutic efficacy, do not recapitulate the human immune microenvironment, and more sophisticated humanized models will be needed to fully evaluate CAR‐NK cell function in vivo.

This study provides initial proof‐of‐concept for target‐specific cytotoxicity of S1TSCFR3‐1–modified CAR‐NK cells against ATL in vitro; however, no survival benefit was observed in vivo, and thus a definitive efficacy signal has not yet been established. Accordingly, comprehensive validation using diverse cell lines, patient samples, and expanded experimental conditions will be required not as a step toward clinical translation, but to determine whether this approach has sufficient biological potential to warrant further preclinical development. Future efforts will include creation of an NK‐92‐MI cell line library expressing scFvs targeting diverse surface glycans, facilitating personalized immunotherapy strategies. Such studies will clarify the feasibility of glycan‐targeted CAR‐NK approaches in ATL and related hematological malignancies.
