Minoxidil and Prostaglandin Analogs for Chemotherapy-Induced Alopecia: A Scoping Review
Authors: Yohei Iimura, Tomoya Yokota, Hironobu Hashimoto, Seiichiro Kuroda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChemotherapy-induced alopecia (CIA) is common, distressing, and markedly impairs patients’ quality of life. Although scalp cooling is the only approved preventive strategy, its effectiveness and accessibility remain limited. Therefore, alternative pharmacologic approaches, such as minoxidil and prostaglandin analogs, have been explored.MethodsThis scoping review aimed to explore the available evidence on minoxidil’s efficacy and safety and the potential role of prostaglandin analogs in alopecia. PubMed, Cochrane Library, Medical Online, and Ichushi-Web were comprehensively searched from inception to February 2026. Studies evaluating minoxidil in CIA and prostaglandin analogs in any alopecia type were included. Case reports, case series, and non-human studies were excluded.ResultsSeven studies were included. Five studies evaluated minoxidil in CIA, including two randomized controlled trials and three retrospective studies on prophylactic and therapeutic use, respectively. Prophylactic topical minoxidil did not markedly prevent CIA but was associated with earlier hair regrowth. In contrast, therapeutic use—particularly of oral minoxidil—improved hair density and treatment response. No serious adverse events were reported, although cardiovascular effects remain a concern with oral formulations. Two randomized controlled trials evaluated prostaglandin analogs in non-chemotherapy-related alopecia and showed markedly improved hair density and regrowth. However, no study directly assessed their efficacy in CIA.ConclusionsMinoxidil may be beneficial for hair regrowth in CIA but shows limited preventive efficacy. Prostaglandin analogs are promising but untested in this setting. Future studies should evaluate their role, particularly in combination with minoxidil, to address the current evidence gap and improve outcomes for patients with CIA.Keywords: Chemotherapy-induced alopecia, Minoxidil / prostaglandin analogs, Hair regrowth: hair loss, Scoping review

Introduction

Chemotherapy-induced alopecia (CIA) is one of the most common and distressing adverse events associated with anticancer treatment. Several classes of cytotoxic agents, including topoisomerase inhibitors, microtubule inhibitors, alkylating agents, and antimetabolites, have been frequently associated with hair loss [1]. Although CIA does not manifest as physical symptoms, such as nausea or pain, its psychological impact is substantial and should not be underestimated. Indeed, alopecia has been identified as one of the most distressing side effects experienced by patients undergoing chemotherapy [2]. The burden of CIA is particularly pronounced in female patients, as many strongly alopecia-inducing chemotherapeutic agents are commonly used in cancers such as breast and gynecologic malignancies. As such, hair loss can markedly impair quality of life and negatively affect body image and self-esteem in this population [3, 4].

Despite extensive research efforts, scalp cooling remains the only intervention approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for the prevention of CIA [5, 6]. However, its effectiveness is limited, and the high cost and logistical challenges associated with cooling systems have restricted its widespread implementation. Therefore, there is a need for alternative, more accessible preventive strategies. Topical pharmacologic interventions represent a potentially convenient and cost-effective approach. Minoxidil, in both topical and oral formulations, has been investigated in several clinical studies for CIA prevention or treatment [7–9]. However, the available evidence has been obtained from relatively small-scale studies, and ongoing clinical trials are currently evaluating its efficacy in this setting [10, 11]. Notably, minoxidil is associated with cardiovascular adverse events, including palpitations and tachycardia, particularly with oral administration [12, 13]. On the other hand, while topical formulations are generally considered safer, their effectiveness as monotherapies for CIA remains unclear.

Prostaglandin analogs have also emerged as potential therapeutic candidates for hair growth. They are thought to promote hair follicle activity by enhancing blood flow and stimulating the hair growth cycle within the follicle. The potential role of prostaglandin supplementation in preventing CIA has been suggested [14]. Moreover, the efficacy of prostaglandin analogs in the treatment of eyelash alopecia in patients with cancer has already been reported [15]. However, their effectiveness in the management of scalp CIA has not been well established. Therefore, we conducted a scoping review to comprehensively map the available evidence on the efficacy and safety of minoxidil, as well as to explore the potential role of prostaglandin analogs in the prevention and management of CIA.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).

Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed/Cochrane Library/Medical Online/Ichushi-Web to identify relevant studies evaluating the efficacy and safety of minoxidil and prostaglandin analogs for alopecia. The search covered all records from database inception to February 2026. The following keywords were used in combination: “minoxidil,” “prostaglandin,” “alopecia,” “hair loss,” and “chemotherapy.” These terms were adapted as appropriate for each database. Two authors, YI and SK, independently conducted an initial review of the titles and abstracts of all the articles extracted. This was followed by a full-text review, or second screening, based on the established inclusion and exclusion criteria. The authors were permitted to independently search for articles manually and include them in the list if they satisfied all the inclusion criteria.

Eligibility Criteria

Eligibility criteria were defined separately for minoxidil and prostaglandin analogs owing to differences in evidence availability.

Minoxidil

Studies were included if they (1) involved patients with CIA; (2) evaluated minoxidil as an intervention; and (3) reported outcomes related to hair loss prevention, hair regrowth, or safety.

Prostaglandin Analogs

Given the limited availability of studies specifically addressing CIA, studies evaluating prostaglandin analogs in non-chemotherapy-related alopecia were also included to broadly assess the potential role of these agents in hair growth and follicular stimulation. Studies were included if they (1) evaluated prostaglandin analogs as an intervention for any type of alopecia and (2) reported outcomes related to hair growth or regrowth.

General Exclusion Criteria

Case reports, case series, and non-human studies were excluded.

Study Selection

Randomized controlled trials and observational studies were included in this review. Titles and abstracts were screened to identify potentially eligible studies, followed by a full-text review for final inclusion.

Data Extraction

Data were extracted and charted using a standardized form. The following information was collected: study design, sample size, population characteristics, cancer type and chemotherapy regimen (if applicable), intervention details (e.g., formulation and dosage), alopecia-related outcomes, and reported adverse events.

Data Synthesis

Given the anticipated heterogeneity in study populations, interventions, and outcome measures, a quantitative synthesis was not performed. Instead, findings were summarized descriptively. Particular attention was paid to differences in evidence between chemotherapy- and non-chemotherapy-related settings.

Results

Study Selection and Characteristics

Overall, seven studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in this scoping review. Among these, five studies evaluated minoxidil in patients with CIA, while two studies investigated prostaglandin analogs in individuals with non-chemotherapy-related alopecia. Among the five minoxidil studies, two were randomized controlled trials evaluating prophylactic use, and three were retrospective observational studies assessing therapeutic (reactive) use after alopecia onset. The prostaglandin analog studies consisted of randomized controlled trials in patients with androgenetic alopecia and alopecia areata (Fig. 1).

Prophylactic Use

Two randomized controlled trials evaluated the prophylactic use of topical minoxidil in patients receiving chemotherapy. Rodriguez et al. reported that topical minoxidil 2% did not significantly reduce the incidence of severe alopecia compared to placebo (88% vs. 92%) [7]. Similarly, although Duvic et al. reported no preventive effect against alopecia development, minoxidil significantly shortened the time to hair regrowth (86.7 days vs. 136.9 days, p = 0.03) [16]. Overall, the prophylactic use of topical minoxidil did not afford a clear benefit in preventing CIA but may have contributed to earlier hair regrowth.

Therapeutic (Reactive) Use

Three retrospective studies evaluated minoxidil for the treatment of established alopecia. Kuo et al. reported that oral minoxidil (1.25 mg daily) markedly improved hair density in both frontal and occipital regions [8]. Similarly, Kang et al. reported improved treatment response rates with low-dose oral minoxidil, either alone or in combination with topical minoxidil [17]. Although Zaminski et al. did not conduct a comparative study, their findings suggest that low-dose minoxidil has a restorative effect on CIA [18]. These findings suggest that minoxidil may be effective in promoting hair regrowth after the onset of CIA, particularly when administered orally or in combination regimens.

Safety

No serious adverse events were reported in the included studies. However, given the known association of oral minoxidil with cardiovascular adverse events, careful monitoring may be warranted in clinical practice.

Two randomized controlled trials evaluated prostaglandin analogs in the context of non-chemotherapy-related alopecia [19, 20]. In a study of patients with androgenetic alopecia, topical latanoprost use considerably increased hair density compared to that at baseline and that associated with placebo use at 24 weeks. Similarly, in patients with alopecia areata, the use of latanoprost in combination with topical corticosteroids markedly improved hair density and regrowth compared to control treatment. Although these findings suggest that prostaglandin analogs may promote hair growth, no studies directly evaluated their efficacy in CIA management.

Evidence Mapping

The available evidence shows a clear imbalance between minoxidil and prostaglandin analogs in the context of CIA. While minoxidil has been directly evaluated in populations with CIA, albeit in a limited number of studies, evidence for prostaglandin analogs is restricted to non-chemotherapy-related alopecia. Furthermore, the effects of minoxidil appear to differ by treatment timing, with limited benefit in prevention but more consistent efficacy in promoting hair regrowth after alopecia onset (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This scoping review summarized the available evidence on minoxidil and prostaglandin analogs used for alopecia management, with a particular focus on CIA. Overall, the evidence base remains limited. Minoxidil has been directly evaluated in the context of CIA, although only in a small number of studies, whereas prostaglandin analogs have not yet been investigated in individuals with CIA. Notably, minoxidil showed limited efficacy in the prevention of CIA but showed more consistent benefits in promoting hair regrowth after alopecia onset. In contrast, prostaglandin analogs have shown efficacy in non-chemotherapy-related alopecia, suggesting a potential role in hair growth that is yet to be explored in the CIA.

Mechanisms of Chemotherapy-Induced Alopecia

Table 2Clinical studies investigating the efficacy of prostaglandin agentsCIA is primarily caused by the cytotoxic effects of anticancer agents on rapidly proliferating hair matrix keratinocytes within the hair bulb [21]. Because hair follicles are highly vascularized, they are particularly susceptible to systemic chemotherapy exposure [22]. Consequently, agents such as topoisomerase inhibitors, microtubule inhibitors, alkylating agents, and antimetabolites are frequently associated with alopecia [1]. Scalp cooling systems are thought to prevent CIA by inducing vasoconstriction, thereby reducing drug delivery to hair follicles and decreasing follicular metabolic activity [5]. This highlights the central role of follicular blood flow and cellular proliferation in the pathogenesis of CIA.

Minoxidil: Efficacy, Limitations, and Safety Considerations

Minoxidil is metabolized to its active form, minoxidil sulfate, by sulfotransferase enzymes expressed in the outer root sheath of hair follicles [23]. The active metabolite is believed to promote vasodilation and enhance microcirculation through the activation of ATP-sensitive potassium channels in vascular smooth muscle and dermal papilla cells [24]. Additional mechanisms may include upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor signaling and increased prostaglandin synthesis [23]. From a hair cycle perspective, minoxidil is thought to shorten the telogen phase and accelerate the transition to the anagen phase, while also prolonging the anagen duration, thereby increasing hair length and thickness [25].

These mechanisms may explain why minoxidil appears to be more effective in promoting hair regrowth than preventing CIA. Since chemotherapy-induced damage occurs at the level of rapidly dividing matrix cells, minoxidil may have a limited ability to prevent initial follicular injury. However, it may facilitate the recovery of follicular function once the cytotoxic insult has subsided.

From a safety perspective, although no serious adverse events were reported in the included studies, oral minoxidil is known to be associated with cardiovascular effects, such as tachycardia and fluid retention [12, 13]. According to a recent review on the safety of low-dose oral minoxidil, no significant changes in blood pressure were observed; however, there was a statistically significant increase in heart rate, specifically in terms of minimum and average values [26]. Therefore, careful patient selection and monitoring are warranted, particularly when considering systemic administration.

Prostaglandin Analogs: Potential and Evidence Gap

Prostaglandin analogs represent a promising but underexplored therapeutic option for alopecia management. These agents are thought to increase hair growth through multiple mechanisms, including modulation of the hair cycle, increased follicular blood flow, and stimulation of dermal papilla activity. Prostaglandin supplementation has been proposed as a potential strategy for preventing alopecia [14]. In the present review, prostaglandin analogs showed efficacy in increasing hair density and promoting regrowth in non-chemotherapy-related alopecia, including androgenetic alopecia and alopecia areata. However, no studies have directly evaluated their use in the context of CIA.

This represents a clear translational gap between mechanistic plausibility and clinical evidence. Given that CIA shares certain pathophysiological features with other forms of alopecia, particularly disruption of the hair growth cycle, prostaglandin analogs may have therapeutic potential in this setting. Future clinical studies are needed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of these agents in patients undergoing chemotherapy. Evidence from mouse models indicates that prostaglandins are effective against alopecia triggered by multiple chemotherapeutic agents or radiation [27–29]. These findings suggest that co-administration with minoxidil might be a viable strategy to optimize clinical outcomes.

Potential for Combination Therapy

The distinct mechanisms of action of minoxidil and prostaglandin analogs suggest that combination therapy may offer synergistic benefits. Minoxidil primarily enhances microcirculation and promotes anagen phase transition, whereas prostaglandin analogs may directly stimulate follicular activity and hair growth signaling pathways. Furthermore, minoxidil has been reported to increase prostaglandin synthesis, suggesting potential biological interplay between these pathways. Mechanisms of action of minoxidil and prostaglandin analogs in the prevention and treatment of chemotherapy-induced alopecia

In the context of CIA, where both vascular and proliferative pathways are disrupted by chemotherapy, a multimodal pharmacologic approach may be particularly advantageous. However, no clinical studies to date have evaluated combination therapy with minoxidil and prostaglandin analogs in this population. This represents an important area for future research.

Chemotherapeutic agents damage highly proliferative hair matrix keratinocytes within the hair bulb, leading to hair loss. Minoxidil is converted to its active form, minoxidil sulfate, by sulfotransferase enzymes in the outer root sheath. It activates ATP-sensitive potassium channels in vascular smooth muscle and dermal papilla cells, resulting in vasodilation and enhanced microcirculation. In addition, minoxidil may shorten the telogen phase and promote transition to the anagen phase, thereby enhancing hair growth and thickness. Secondary mechanisms include the upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor signaling and prostaglandin synthesis.

Prostaglandin analogs promote hair growth by enhancing prostaglandin signaling within the hair follicle, leading to increased perifollicular blood flow and stimulation of hair matrix cells. These agents may contribute to the maintenance of the anagen phase and improvement of hair density. Together, these mechanisms suggest that both minoxidil and prostaglandin analogs may mitigate chemotherapy-induced alopecia by improving the follicular microenvironment and promoting hair regrowth.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the number of available studies, particularly those on CIA, was limited. Second, heterogeneity in study design, patient populations, and outcome measures precluded quantitative synthesis. Third, prostaglandin analogs were evaluated only in the context of non-chemotherapy-related alopecia, limiting the direct applicability of these findings to CIA.

Conclusions

The current evidence suggests that minoxidil may be beneficial for promoting hair regrowth in CIA but has limited efficacy in prevention. Prostaglandin analogs have demonstrated efficacy in other forms of alopecia but remain unstudied in the CIA setting. Future research should focus on well-designed clinical trials to evaluate both minoxidil and prostaglandin analogs—individually and in combination—in patients undergoing chemotherapy.
