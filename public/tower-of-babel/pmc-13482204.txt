3-Year Outcomes of Low-Dose vs High-Dose Drug-Coated Balloons in Femoropopliteal Artery Disease
Authors: Tatsuya Nakama, Mitsuyoshi Takahara, Yo Iwata, Kenji Suzuki, Kazuki Tobita, Naoki Hayakawa, Kazunori Horie, Shinsuke Mori, Kotaro Obunai, Takao Ohki, PROSPECT MONSTER Study Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn the PROSPECT MONSTER study, LD-DCB demonstrated comparable 1-year outcomes to HD-DCB in a real-world population; however, long-term comparative data remain limited.ObjectivesTo compare 3-year outcomes of a second-generation low-dose drug-coated balloon (LD-DCB; Ranger) vs a first-generation high-dose drug-coated balloon (HD-DCB; IN.PACT) in symptomatic femoropopliteal artery (FPA) disease.MethodsThis prospective, multicenter, study included 581 patients undergoing endovascular therapy with LD-DCB (n = 370) or HD-DCB (n = 211) for FPA disease. Median follow-up was 19.9 (IQR: 9.4-37.7) months. Primary outcomes were 3-year primary patency and freedom from clinically driven target lesion revascularization, assessed after propensity score matching. Secondary outcomes included major adverse limb events, limb salvage, and overall survival.ResultsPropensity score matching in an approximate 2:1 ratio extracted 163 matched sets (358 and 163 patients in LD-DCB and HD-DCB, respectively) with no significant baseline differences. The median follow-up among matched participants was 19.9 (9.2-37.9) months. At 3 years, primary patency (60.1% [95% CI: 53.1%-67.9%] vs 48.4% [39.3%-59.7%]; HR: 1.02 [0.60-1.73], P = 0.93) and freedom from clinically driven target lesion revascularization (71.0% [64.7%-77.9%] vs 59.9% [50.8%-70.7%]; hazard ratio: 1.02 [0.58-1.81], P = 0.94) were similar between groups. Secondary outcomes were comparable. No baseline characteristics showed a significant interaction with 3-year restenosis.ConclusionsIn a real-world population with complex FPA disease, second-generation LD-DCB demonstrated 3-year clinical outcomes comparable to those of first-generation HD-DCB. These findings support the use of second-generation LD-DCB as a reasonable treatment option and suggest that comparable efficacy may be achieved despite substantially lower paclitaxel exposure.

Drug-coated balloons (DCBs) have become an established treatment strategy for femoropopliteal artery (FPA) disease by enabling effective endovascular revascularization without leaving a permanent implant, the so-called “leave nothing behind” strategy.1 Over the past decade, clinical studies have demonstrated favorable outcomes with DCB therapy, supporting their widespread adoption.2 However, as evidence has accumulated, the relationship between paclitaxel dose and long-term clinical benefit has become less straightforward.

IN.PACT (Medtronic), a first-generation high-dose DCB (HD-DCB) delivering 3.5 μg/mm2 of paclitaxel, has established robust evidence as an effective antirestenotic therapy.3 In contrast, first-generation low-dose DCBs (LD-DCBs), particularly those using a polysorbate–sorbitol excipient, have often been associated with inferior efficacy, leading to a preference for HD-DCB.4,5 In contrast to these earlier platforms, newer excipient technologies have been developed to improve drug transfer efficiency, potentially enabling comparable efficacy with a lower drug dose. Ranger (Boston Scientific) is a second-generation LD-DCB delivering 2.0 μg/mm2 of paclitaxel with an acetyl tributyl citrate excipient, designed to optimize drug transfer while minimizing drug loss.6 Consistent with this concept, the randomized COMPARE trial demonstrated comparable mid-term outcomes between LD-DCB and HD-DCB.7,8

Although a clear dose–response relationship between paclitaxel exposure and long-term safety has not been established,9,10 concerns regarding paclitaxel exposure persist. Limus-based technologies have emerged as a potential alternative; however, long-term outcome data and direct comparative evidence with paclitaxel-based devices remain limited.11,12 In this context, reducing paclitaxel exposure while maintaining efficacy may represent a reasonable therapeutic strategy. However, randomized trial populations do not fully reflect daily clinical practice, where FPA interventions frequently involve complex clinical and lesion characteristics. In such settings, long-term comparative data between LD-DCB and HD-DCB remain limited.

The PROSPECT MONSTER study was designed to address this gap by prospectively comparing second-generation LD-DCB with first-generation HD-DCB in a multicenter real-world cohort. The 1-year analysis demonstrated comparable efficacy and safety despite substantial clinical and lesion complexity.13 The present study aimed to evaluate 3-year outcomes and determine whether this equivalence is maintained in real-world practice.

Methods

Study design, patient population, and eligibility

PROSPECT MONSTER is a multicenter prospective study comprising 7 institutions. Detailed study design and patient and lesion characteristics have been published previously.13 In summary, 581 consecutive patients who underwent endovascular therapy (EVT) using either LD-DCB (Ranger) or HD-DCB (IN.PACT) for FPA lesions were enrolled. The study protocol was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committees of each participating institution. Informed consent was obtained from the participants or their families if that was not possible.

Key inclusion criteria included symptomatic FPA disease (Rutherford classes 2-5). Patients undergoing daily hemodialysis, those with chronic limb-threatening ischemia (Rutherford classes 4-5), and those who underwent EVT of the mid (P2) or distal (P3) popliteal arteries were also eligible for enrollment. There were no limitations according to the lesion length and vessel diameter. The main exclusion criteria were the absence of atherosclerotic disease and Rutherford category of 0 (asymptomatic) or 6. Endovascular procedure

EVT was performed according to the standard practice of each operator. Lesion preparation with balloon angioplasty was mandatory before DCB use. Lesion preparation generally reflected contemporary Japanese practice and typically involved intravascular ultrasound (IVUS)-guided vessel sizing whenever available, followed by noncompliant balloon dilatation. In heavily calcified lesions, scoring or cutting balloons were often used before high-pressure dilatation at the operator's discretion. No atherectomy devices or intravascular lithotripsy balloons were used because these devices were not available in Japan during the study period. If lesion preparation failed because of severe recoil or flow-limiting severe dissection requiring scaffold implantation, the patient was excluded from this study. After successful lesion preparation, DCBs were applied.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the 3-year primary patency and freedom from clinically driven target lesion revascularization (CD-TLR) rates. Loss of patency was defined as a peak systolic velocity ratio of ≥2.4 determined using duplex ultrasound or ≥50% restenosis on follow-up with computed tomography or angiography. Patency follow-up was performed according to the recommendations of the Society of Vascular Surgery report: every 3 months during the first year and every 6 months or annually thereafter.14 The secondary outcomes included 3-year freedom from major adverse limb events (MALE), defined as a composite of CD-TLR and major amputation, limb salvage, and all-cause mortality.

Statistical analysis

Data on baseline characteristics are presented as the mean ± SD for continuous variables and as frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables unless otherwise indicated. P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. Differences in baseline characteristics between the groups were crudely tested using Welch’s t test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for discrete variables. When clinical outcomes were compared between the LD-DCB and HD-DCB groups, propensity score matching was performed to minimize the intergroup differences in baseline characteristics. The propensity scores were calculated using a logistic regression model. The following explanatory variables were included in the model: sex, age, smoking, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, medications, Rutherford classification, ankle-brachial index, history of revascularization, lesion location, reference vessel diameter, lesion length, chronic total occlusion, % diameter stenosis, Peripheral Artery Calcium Scoring System classification, IVUS use, predilatation balloon insertion, severity of dissection after predilatation, and below-the-knee runoff.

The present dataset contained missing data for chronic kidney disease and ankle brachial index. The missing data patterns are summarized in Supplemental Tables A and B. During the model development, dummy variable adjustment was adopted to handle missing data. Briefly, we created a new dummy variable indicating that a variable of interest was missing and entered it into the model together with the variable of interest where missing data were imputed by the mean value of nonmissing cases. We additionally performed a sensitivity analysis with complete case data.

Matching was performed utilizing the logit of the propensity score and applying a caliper of 0.2 SD of the logit of the propensity score. To maximize the statistical power to detect intergroup prognostic differences, we matched as many samples as possible from the LD-DCB group to each one in the HD-DCB group. After matching, intergroup differences were analyzed with stratification by matched set, and weighted descriptive statistics were reported. The balance in baseline characteristics was assessed using standardized differences. The rates of time-to-events were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and intergroup differences were analyzed using Cox proportional hazards regression models stratified by matched sets (ie, conditional Cox models). In the models, the intercenter heterogeneity was entered as the random effects by incorporating random intercepts. The interaction effect of baseline characteristics on the association of LD-DCB vs HD-DCB with restenosis risk was also analyzed using Cox proportional hazards regression models stratified by matched pairs where the intercenter heterogeneity was entered as random effects by incorporating random intercepts. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.1.1 (R Development Core Team).

Results

Baseline characteristics

The PROSPECT MONSTER study enrolled 581 consecutive patients with symptomatic FPA disease treated with LD-DCB (Ranger; n = 370) or HD-DCB (IN.PACT; n = 211) between April 2021 and March 2022 at 7 sites in Japan. The baseline characteristics and primary efficacy and safety results at 12 months have been published previously.13 During a median follow-up of 19.9 months (IQR: 9.4-37.7 months), restenosis was observed in 199 patients. Propensity score matching extracted 163 matched sets (358 patients in the LD-DCB group and 163 patients in the HD-DCB group), with no remarkable intergroup differences in baseline characteristics (Table 1). The median and interquartile range of follow-up time for all matched participants were 19.9 (9.2-37.9) months. In the overall cohort, IVUS was used in 73.5% (272/370) of lesions treated with LD-DCB and 66.8% (141/211) of lesions treated with HD-DCB (P = 0.11). After propensity score matching, IVUS utilization was similar between the LD-DCB and HD-DCB groups (70.5% [115.0/163] vs 68.7% [112.0/163]). Cutting/scoring balloons were used less frequently in the LD-DCB group than in the HD-DCB group in the overall cohort (28.4% [105/370] vs 37.9% [80/211], P = 0.023). However, after propensity score matching, the frequency of cutting/scoring balloon use was comparable between the LD-DCB and HD-DCB groups (34.6% [56.4/163] vs 38.7% [63.0/163]). After matching, the cohort had a mean age of approximately 74 years; 70% were male (LD-DCB = 69.2% [112.9/163], HD-DCB = 69.9% [114.0/163]), 70% had diabetes (LD-DCB = 71.6% [116.8/163], HD-DCB = 71.2% [116.0/163]), and approximately one-third were on dialysis (LD-DCB = 35.1% [56.1/163], HD-DCB = 33.5% [54.0/163]). Chronic limb-threatening ischemia was present in about 38% of patients (LD-DCB = 38.0% [61.8/163], HD-DCB = 38.0% [62/163]). Popliteal artery involvement was observed in approximately one-third of cases (LD-DCB = 35.0% [57.2/163], HD-DCB = 31.3% [51.0/163]). The mean lesion length was approximately 18 cm, and severe calcification (Peripheral Artery Calcium Scoring System grade 4) was present in about 30% (LD-DCB = 29.4% [47.8/163], HD-DCB = 27.6% [45.0/163]). Outcome measures

The 3-year primary patency rates were 60.1% (95% confidence interval, 53.1% to 67.9%) in the LD-DCB group and 48.4% (39.3% to 59.7%) in the HD-DCB group and were comparable between groups (hazard ratio [HR]: 1.02 [0.60-1.73] P = 0.93) (Central Illustration, Figure 2). Freedom from CD-TLR was also comparable between the LD-DCB and HD-DCB groups (71.0% [64.7% to 77.9%] vs 59.9% [50.8% to 70.7%]; HR: 1.02 [0.58-1.81], P = 0.94) (Central Illustration, Figure 3). Sensitivity analysis (complete case analysis)

In the complete case analysis, propensity score matching extracted 147 matched sets (321 patients in the LD-DCB group and 147 patients in the HD-DCB group), with no remarkable intergroup differences in baseline characteristics (Supplemental Table D). There were no significant intergroup differences in the incident risk of restenosis (HR: 1.02; 95% CI: 0.59-1.77; P = 0.95), CD-TLR (HR: 1.02; 95% CI: 0.57-1.84; P = 0.95), major amputation (HR: 0.42; 95% CI: 0.11-1.58; P = 0.20), MALE (HR: 0.95; 95% CI: 0.54-1.66; P = 0.85), and mortality (HR: 0.68; 95% CI: 0.36-1.30; P = 0.25) (Supplemental Figures A to E). No baseline characteristics had any interaction effect on the association between DCB type and restenosis risk (P for interaction >0.05 for all) (Supplemental Figure F).

Discussion

In this prospective multicenter real-world study, second-generation LD-DCB demonstrated 3-year clinical outcomes comparable to those of first-generation HD-DCB in patients with complex FPA disease. Primary patency, freedom from CD-TLR, freedom from MALE, limb salvage, and overall survival were similar between groups. Furthermore, no significant interactions were observed across a broad range of clinical and lesion characteristics, including lesion complexity. These findings suggest that the comparative effectiveness of LD-DCB and HD-DCB was maintained across a spectrum of lesion morphologies frequently encountered in contemporary clinical practice. Our findings are consistent with those of the randomized COMPARE trial, which demonstrated comparable efficacy between LD-DCB and HD-DCB through 2 years, with durable results reported through 5 years.7,8 Compared with COMPARE, however, the present study included a broader real-world population with more heterogeneous clinical and lesion characteristics. Despite these differences, the overall consistency of outcomes between studies supports the reproducibility of comparable efficacy with second-generation LD-DCB in routine clinical practice.

Historically, paclitaxel dose density has been considered a major determinant of DCB efficacy, largely based on the inferior performance of first-generation low-dose platforms and the widespread adoption of HD-DCBs as the clinical standard.3, 4, 5 The principal finding of the present study is that comparable long-term outcomes were achieved despite substantially lower paclitaxel exposure. This observation suggests that increasing drug dose alone may not be necessary to maintain clinical effectiveness. One potential explanation is the improvement in drug-delivery efficiency achieved with second-generation DCB technology. The acetyl tributyl citrate–based coating used in the Ranger DCB has been shown experimentally to reduce drug loss, minimize distal embolization, and enhance vessel-wall drug transfer.6 These pharmacokinetic advantages may have contributed to the comparable outcomes observed in the present study. Importantly, the consistency of findings between the randomized COMPARE trial and the present real-world PROSPECT MONSTER study suggests that factors beyond paclitaxel dose alone contribute substantially to DCB efficacy. Taken together, these studies support the concept that improvements in coating formulation and drug-delivery efficiency may allow comparable clinical effectiveness to be achieved despite lower drug loading.

The clinical implications of these findings may be particularly relevant given ongoing discussions regarding cumulative paclitaxel exposure. Although subsequent studies have largely mitigated concerns regarding excess mortality associated with paclitaxel-coated devices,9,10 minimizing drug exposure while maintaining efficacy remains an attractive treatment concept. In this regard, the present study supports the feasibility of achieving comparable long-term outcomes with a LD-DCB platform. Limus-based DCB technologies have emerged as potential alternatives to paclitaxel-coated devices, partly in response to persistent concerns regarding long-term paclitaxel exposure. Although the available clinical evidence remains limited, early studies have demonstrated efficacy comparable to that of paclitaxel-coated DCBs, and emerging mid-term data suggest durable clinical performance.11,12 These developments raise the possibility that alternative drug platforms may further reduce concerns regarding drug safety while maintaining clinical effectiveness. At the same time, our findings suggest another potential approach: reducing paclitaxel exposure through improvements in drug-delivery efficiency. Future studies directly comparing contemporary low-dose paclitaxel DCBs with limus-based DCBs, together with longer-term follow-up data, will be important to determine the optimal balance between efficacy and safety.

Another notable finding was the very low rate of bailout stenting compared with previous Western studies, including COMPARE.7,8 In addition, IVUS was used in approximately two-thirds of procedures. These characteristics likely reflect contemporary Japanese endovascular practice and may have contributed to procedural optimization. At the same time, these differences should be considered when extrapolating our findings to healthcare systems with different procedural strategies.

Although no statistically significant differences were observed between treatment groups, the achievement of comparable outcomes despite substantially lower paclitaxel exposure supports further evaluation of LD-DCB technology. Given the observational design of the present study, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and warrant confirmation in future clinical trials.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a nonrandomized observational study, and residual confounding cannot be excluded despite propensity score matching. Second, device selection was left to the discretion of the treating physician. Third, the compared devices differed not only in paclitaxel dose but also in coating formulation and drug-delivery technology, making it difficult to isolate the independent effect of drug dose.

Fourth, the study population was limited to Japan, where procedural strategies such as frequent IVUS use and low bailout stenting rates may differ from those in other countries, potentially limiting generalizability. Fifth, the relatively limited sample size after propensity score matching and incomplete long-term follow-up may have reduced statistical power to detect modest but clinically meaningful differences between groups; therefore, the possibility of a type II error cannot be excluded. Sixth, competing risks of all-cause deaths were not accounted for when assessing risks of the primary outcome measure. The standard approach to account for the competing risk is to use the Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model to directly model the cumulative incidence function. However, we employed a mixed-effects Cox model to account for intercenter heterogeneity by incorporating random intercepts. To our knowledge, there is currently no widely established or readily available statistical framework that allows the simultaneous incorporation of both random effects (ie, mixed-effects structure) and the Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model in a coherent and well-validated manner. Longer-term follow-up will be important to further evaluate the durability and safety of these findings.

Conclusions

In a real-world population with complex FPA disease, second-generation LD-DCB demonstrated 3-year clinical outcomes comparable to those of first-generation HD-DCB, with consistent results across clinical and lesion subgroups.
