Microinterventional in-sensor computing system for real-time metabolic health assessment
Authors: Peidi Fan, Haitao Zhang, Xiaoyu Su, Xuewei Yang, Ying Liu, Shuangfeng Pan, Xunjia Li, Yibin Ying, Yuxiang Pan, Jianfeng Ping
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microneedle biosensors enable dynamic monitoring of interstitial fluid biomarkers, but remain constrained by sensing interface susceptibility to motion artifacts and the prohibitive energy consumption of wireless cloud-based processing. Here, we report a bio-inspired, self-anchoring microinterventional in-sensor computing system. By leveraging a starfish-inspired suction cup-microneedle self-anchoring mechanism, the system effectively counteracts microneedle extrusion, attenuating signal fluctuations by 38-fold and enhancing signal intensity by up to 5.49-fold compared to conventional planar devices. Crucially, the high-fidelity data acquisition reduces the computational burden, enabling the deployment of a lightweight algorithm (43 KB) on a coin-sized embedded circuit, achieving 98.68% diagnostic accuracy and a 120-h battery life via local closed-loop feedback. Validation in a porcine model confirmed the system’s capability to capture continuous biochemical dynamics. This co-design of a robust biomimetic interface and lightweight deep learning paves the way for next-generation wearables capable of performing high-fidelity, on-chip metabolic risk stratification in dynamic daily settings.

Introduction

Microneedle-based microinterventional biosensors enable minimally invasive and painless epidermal penetration, providing precise access to physiologically relevant biomarkers in deeper skin layers1,2, due to their micron-scale diameters, excellent mechanical strength, and high aspect ratios3–5. Capitalizing on this efficient skin-interfacing capability, electrochemical microneedle sensors deliver dynamic and accurate monitoring of interstitial fluid (ISF) analytes6,7. However, seamless integration of these rigid interfaces with the curvilinear, dynamic topography of skin remains a formidable challenge (Fig. 1a). Skin is characterized by hair coverage, wrinkles, and irregular contours, exhibiting continuous deformation during physical movement8. Such topographical complexity frequently destabilizes the bioelectronic interface, manifesting as micro-displacements and partial microneedle extrusion. These mechanical failures create insulating voids that substantially increase contact impedance and degrade signal fidelity9–11. Crucially, shear forces and vibrations exacerbate these issues, generating substantial motion artifacts and accelerating device failure12–15. To enable real-time metabolic assessment for proactive health management, the integration of wearable biosensors with machine learning algorithms has emerged as a pivotal strategy to decipher the complex physiological dynamics. Driven by the critical need to minimize communication latency and energy consumption, modern wearable architectures are increasingly pivoting toward the edge-computing paradigm. While edge intelligence generally improves efficiency by executing inference tasks locally16,17, in-sensor computing takes this a step further by integrating processing capabilities directly within the sensor, eliminating wireless data transmission redundancy entirely. However, the stringent resource constraints inherent to in-sensor computing architectures preclude the execution of complex denoising algorithms to recover physiological signals from data distorted by motion artifacts18. Since lightweight models are inherently susceptible to input noise, the deployment of edge intelligence imposes an even more stringent requirement for high-fidelity signal acquisition.

In this work, we report a stably microinterventional in-sensor computing system (MiSCS) that resolves this conflict through a synergy between bio-inspired mechanics and efficient edge AI. Inspired by the starfish’s distinctive predatory behaviors, we develop a hybrid suction cup-microneedle interface that achieves mechanical self-anchoring with the skin, maintaining a stable electrochemical connection even during vigorous movement. We validate this system in the context of ketogenic metabolic health monitoring. This scenario demands the simultaneous tracking of glucose (Glu), β-hydroxybutyrate (HB), and pH under rapid metabolic shifts and physical exertion (Supplementary Note 1). By securing high-fidelity raw data at the physical source, we minimize the reliance on computationally intensive denoising, thereby enabling the deployment of a lightweight Dynamic Routing Residual Network (DRResNet) directly on a low-power microcontroller (MCU). In vivo validation in a porcine model confirms that this symbiotic approach, where mechanical stability empowers computational efficiency, achieves high accuracy with millisecond-level latency, establishing a robust framework for next-generation wearable health monitoring.

Results

Design and workflow of the MiSCS

The pentaradially symmetrical structure and ambulacral tube feet equipped with integrated suction cups confer omnidirectional mobility on starfish. The contraction-extension cycles of these tube feet generate negative pressure, enabling the suction cup to maintain stable adhesion to prey surfaces. Crucially, this anchoring mechanism mechanically stabilizes the central oral opening, preventing its extrusion or displacement during dynamic movement. This stability on dynamically deforming interfaces provides a bio-inspired blueprint for designing high-fidelity sensing interfaces (Fig. 1b). Therefore, we developed a starfish-inspired microinterventional biosensing system featuring a five-arm pentaradial topology, which minimizes mechanical coupling between arms, enhancing multidirectional flexibility and improving overall mechanical compliance19. We integrated peripheral suction cups with a central microneedle array to construct a suction-reinforced self-anchoring structure. Mechanically, the peripheral suction force counterbalances upward extrusion caused by skin deformation. This layout effectively decouples the central sensing interface from peripheral strain, ensuring robust microneedle retention. By mitigating microneedle displacement and the associated fluctuations in electrochemical contact impedance, this biomimetic extrusion-resistant design preserves a stable biosensing interface and significantly enhances signal integrity and stability (Fig. 1c).

To translate this bio-inspired mechanical stability into a practical wearable platform, we adopted a high-density integration strategy. Consequently, the MiSCS (total mass: 4.5 g; diameter: 5 cm) implements a vertically integrated seven-layer architecture (Fig. 1d). This stack comprises: (i) A biocompatible flexible patch equipped with a micro-suction cup array; (ii) A transdermal microneedle biosensing array; (iii) A holder that anchors the biosensing array to the suction cup patch; (iv) A connector that ensures precise alignment between the microneedle biosensing array and the printed circuit board (PCB); (v) A coin-cell battery; (vi) A PCB incorporating modules for signal acquisition, transmission, in-sensor computing, and LED alerts; (vii) A soft Agilus30 silicone cap that encapsulates the internal components while providing high optical transparency for LED-based visual feedback.

This integrated platform enables the high-fidelity, continuous tracking of HB, Glu, and pH dynamics. To fully exploit the persistent data streams provided by the stable bio-interface while addressing the energy bottlenecks of wireless transmission, we established the in-sensor computing paradigm. By deploying the optimized lightweight DRResNet algorithm directly on the STM32 MCU, the system performs local metabolic risk stratification with minimal power consumption. Coupled with an integrated LED-based optical feedback mechanism, this architecture realizes an ultrafast, low-power, and autonomous workflow for closed-loop ketogenic health sensing, analysis, and feedback (Fig. 1e).

Bio-inspired self-anchoring mechanism for stable microinterventional interfacing

The dynamically stable bioelectronic interface is established through the self-anchoring microneedle module, which serves as the foundational layer of the MiSCS (Fig. 2a). This module integrates three functional components: suction cup patch, microneedle, and holder. Utilizing a five-arm pentaradial topology, the flexible substrate enables radially distributed suction cups to surround the central microneedle array (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). These suction cups provide robust skin adhesion via negative pressure generated by elastic recovery following initial compression. Furthermore, the suction cups effectively resist detachment by establishing anti-peeling pressure differentials during tensile deformation (Supplementary Fig. 3). To mitigate vacuum leakage potentially caused by skin hair, the inner surface of the suction cups was functionalized with a tannic acid-polyethylene glycol (TAPE) composite coating, thereby enhancing interfacial sealing and adhesion (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5)20. The stability of the microinterventional interface is governed by the interaction between the microneedles and the viscoelastic dermis, where tissue deformation dictates the force balance during insertion and retention. As illustrated in Fig. 2b (i), the microneedle insertion process comprises two phases. During the insertion phase, microneedle insertion force (Finsertion) overcomes skin resistance force (Fskin) and friction force (Ff) to drive penetration. The Fskin arises from the rigid resistance of the stratum corneum and the elastic deformation of the dermal matrix. As collagen fibers progressively straighten and stretch, Fskin increases proportionally with tissue deformation21,22. The Ff acts at the interface between the needle sidewall and surrounding tissue, arising from viscoelastic damping and contact friction. In the retraction phase, the elastic recovery of the skin, driven by the reorganization and recoil of collagen fibers, generates a restoring force (Fskin’). In conventional flat patches, this force promotes partial extrusion of the microneedle, leaving only Ff to resist external perturbation. During subsequent in vivo monitoring, skin deformation caused by movement and hair interference might further exacerbate microneedle extrusion.

The microneedle module operates via a self-anchoring mechanism that actively compensates for skin recoil, thereby maintaining deeper and more stable microneedle embedding at equilibrium (Fig. 2b (ii), and Supplementary Note 2). Specifically, this design exploits the skin’s intrinsic Fskin’, which typically causes microneedle extrusion in flat patches, to trigger localized negative pressure within the suction chambers. This restorative-driven anchoring establishes a dynamic balance between the self-generated holding force (Fhold) and Fskin’, transforming a destabilizing force into a stabilizing one, thereby effectively suppressing microneedle displacement (Fig. 2c (i)). The flat patch exhibits large stress fluctuations during the retraction phase, indicating rapid extrusion. In contrast, the self-anchoring microneedle module shows a gradual stress decay that stabilizes over time, suggesting that negative pressure generated by the suction cups buffers the Fskin’ (Fig. 2c (ii) and Supplementary Fig. 7). Upon skin recoil, the partial expansion of the chamber reduces tensile stress along the inner cup wall, generating localized negative pressure that counteracts skin retraction and re-establishes mechanical equilibrium (Fig. 2c (iii)). Further experimental validation using polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) skin simulant and ex vivo porcine skin confirmed mitigation of microneedle displacement by the self-anchoring module compared to flat patches, aligning well with simulation predictions (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Movie 1). During the retraction phase, the self-anchoring microneedle module exhibited a reduced terminal displacement, confirming that the microneedle tips were retained at a greater insertion depth. Notably, the derivative of the reaction force with respect to displacement (dF/dx) was higher for the self-anchoring module. This signifies that the self-anchoring mechanism facilitates a more rapid re-establishment of mechanical equilibrium against the skin’s restorative forces during extrusion (Fig. 2e, f).

Furthermore, considering the complex topography of biological surfaces in practical applications, the adaptability to biological surfaces was systematically evaluated under controlled curvature variations. Finite element analysis (FEA) and surface adaptability tests demonstrated that the bio-inspired pentaradial design reduces the compressive load required for conformal contact by 25.9% compared to disk-shaped patches (Supplementary Fig. 9). Crucially, this structural compliance is integral to the self-anchoring module. By minimizing the substrate’s elastic rebound, the topology ensures that peripheral suction cups maintain a hermetic seal against curvilinear contours, thereby maximizing the generation of negative pressure for stable anchoring. This design also facilitates homogeneous strain distribution, mitigating stress concentration and mechanical failure risk at the microneedle junctions (Fig. 2g). On-body validation confirmed that this topology synergizes with the self-anchoring mechanism to achieve seamless adhesion during vigorous motion, yielding an adhesive strength 5.6-fold higher than commercial medical tape (Fig. 2h, i and Supplementary Movie 2).

Multi-parametric biosensing performance of the microneedle array

The multiplexed microneedle array was fabricated via magnetron sputtering of gold onto a micromachined poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) substrate, integrating working electrodes (WE) for HB, Glu, and pH alongside a common reference electrode (RE) and a counter electrode (CE) (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 10). To augment the electroactive surface area and interfacial adhesion, the gold microneedle surfaces were functionalized with gold nanoparticles (AuNPs). This modification strategy significantly enhances signal sensitivity while improving mechanical resistance to coating delamination during skin insertion23. Following functionalization, the sensors were systematically characterized in vitro (Supplementary Notes 3, 4, and 5). The amperometric HB and Glu sensors both exhibited dual-range linear responses, catering to both physiological and pathological concentrations. Specifically, the HB sensor demonstrated sensitivities of 0.02058 and 0.04306 μA mM−1 over the 0–3 mM (R2 = 0.9971) and 3–4.5 mM (R2 = 0.9997) ranges, respectively (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Figs. 11 and 12). Similarly, the Glu sensor, which incorporates a stabilizing Prussian blue-nickel hexacyanoferrate (PB-NiHCF) bilayer architecture, showed sensitivities of 0.4112 and 0.06032 μA mM−1 across the 0–1.5 mM (R2 = 0.9974) and 1.5–5 mM (R2 = 0.9928) ranges (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Figs. 13–16). Reliable operation within complex biological matrices represents a pivotal performance benchmark for microneedle-based biosensors. Our array demonstrated exceptional selectivity, with negligible cross-interference when challenged by a suite of physiologically relevant interferents in PBS and artificial ISF (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 20). This high selectivity, complemented by excellent inter-batch reproducibility across all sensors (RSDs <5.8%, n = 5), ensures accurate and reliable simultaneous measurements (Supplementary Fig. 21).

Long-term operational and storage stability were rigorously validated to meet the demands of continuous monitoring. During 24 h of continuous operation, the HB and Glu sensors maintained stable currents, while the pH sensor displayed a minimal potential drift of less than 0.043 mV h−1 (Supplementary Fig. 22). Furthermore, after 14 d of storage, the HB, Glu, and pH sensors retained 89.94 ± 1.49%, 94.78 ± 9.11%, and 84.21 ± 1.60% of their initial responses, respectively (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Figs. 23 and 24).

To ensure mechanical durability and biocompatibility during practical application, the microneedle’s structural design and material properties were validated to facilitate minimally invasive, painless, yet reliable skin penetration (Supplementary Figs. 25 and 26). The sensing interface was protected by a bio-degradable silk fibroin-hyaluronic acid (SF-HA) composite coating, which effectively shielded the sensing layers during insertion and dissolved rapidly upon contact with ISF (Supplementary Figs. 27–30 and Supplementary Table 1). Comprehensive testing confirmed the structural integrity and resistance of the microneedles under repetitive wear-and-tear (Supplementary Fig. 31 and Fig. 3g). Crucially, the microneedles demonstrated unwavering mechanical and electrochemical integrity even after 50 repeated skin insertion cycles and a 24-h indwelling period in porcine skin (Fig. 3h, i). Collectively, this robust mechanical design, coupled with high selectivity and prolonged stability, validates the system’s potential for reliable, practical continuous monitoring (Supplementary Table 2).

We further evaluated the sensor’s robustness against dynamic mechanical perturbations by subjecting the porcine skin to diverse deformation profiles, including planar, concave, and convex profiles (Supplementary Movie 4). For amperometric sensors (HB and Glu), the MiSCS maintained intimate interfacial contact, yielding signal responses 5.49-fold (HB) and 1.42-fold (Glu) higher than those of planar devices (Fig. 4c, d (i, ii)). For the potentiometric pH sensor, the MiSCS demonstrated superior stability across varying skin topologies. The standard deviation of the sensor signal (0.03938) was significantly lower than that of the planar group (0.75337). Notably, maximum signal fluctuations were constrained to <7.9 mV, representing a 38-fold reduction relative to the substantial fluctuations (> 300 mV) observed in planar devices (Fig. 4d (iii)). These results underscore the superior reliability of the MiSCS in dynamic biological environments, a direct consequence of its mechanically adaptive design that effectively neutralizes the squeeze-out effect.

To evaluate the dynamic tracking capability and fidelity of the MiSCS, we conducted a 4-h real-time monitoring of acute metabolic perturbations (Fig. 4e). Following prolonged fasting, baseline Glu levels were physiologically low, while HB levels were elevated due to fasting-induced lipolysis. Upon Glu injection, Glu levels peaked at 27.15 mM and returned to a steady state within 60 min due to metabolic clearance. Crucially, the system captured the dynamic interaction of these biomarkers, where the Glu surge suppressed lipolysis, triggering a corresponding reduction in HB concentration. Simultaneously, the accumulation of ketone bodies caused a general downward trend in pH. The Glu and HB sensor data mirrored blood Glu and ketone dynamics, with expected time lags consistent with ISF-blood diffusion kinetics, as reported in previous studies (Fig. 4f)24.

To validate the universality of the MiSCS, we extended our evaluation to distinct porcine breeds, including Bama minipigs and Large White pigs. The system exhibited consistent performance independent of breed, with no statistically significant differences in detection errors observed between the two groups (Fig. 4g). Across all three distinct phases of the ketogenic diet (Supplementary Table 3), MiSCS-derived measurements showed strong concordance with standard reference values. Linear regression analysis confirmed high correlation between sensor readouts and reference values (HB: R2 = 0.9690, Glu: R2 = 0.9461, pH: R2 = 0.9424) (Fig. 4h). These findings confirm that the MiSCS effectively captures persistent and continuous biochemical dynamics, providing a high-fidelity data foundation for real-time metabolic health assessment.

In-sensor computing architecture for real-time ketogenic metabolic health assessment

To transition from raw biochemical monitoring to precise health assessment, we simultaneously tracked three biochemical markers (HB, Glu, and pH) (Supplementary Figs. 33–35) and three physiological parameters (heart rate, oxygen saturation, and respiratory rate), utilizing these multimodal data streams to generate composite risk labels (Supplementary Figs. 36–39). Representative sensor monitoring curves, overlaid with these risk classifications, are illustrated across standard and ketogenic diet phases in Fig. 5a. Additionally, a chord diagram visualizes the strong correlations between the individual parameters and the final metabolic risk labels (Fig. 5b). Under the standard diet, the subjects maintained a “healthy”, Glu-dominant metabolic state with negligible HB levels. Upon transition to the ketogenic phase, the animals exhibited sustained elevated HB and reduced Glu concentrations. Notably, pH levels demonstrated an inverse correlation with HB concentration. To enable real-time assessment of ketogenic health risks during the metabolic transition phase, the custom-developed DRResNet deep learning algorithm was employed for ketosis risk stratification (Fig. 5c). Constructed as an optimized variant of the ResNet architecture (Supplementary Note 6), DRResNet utilizes a dynamic routing mechanism to adaptively control feature propagation based on importance. This architecture enabled the model to achieve an accuracy of 96.02% on the validation dataset (Fig. 5d). Remarkably, the DRResNet architecture requires only 0.3616% of the parameters used by a standard ResNet while maintaining comparable accuracy (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Table 4). In comparative experiments, DRResNet outperformed other state-of-the-art architectures, including representative lightweight models (Supplementary Figs. 40–42 and Supplementary Note 7).

To demystify the model’s decision-making process, we employed Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) to assess the individual sensor feature contributions (Fig. 5f). The global feature importance analysis identified pH and Glu as the dominant predictors driving model inference (Supplementary Fig. 43). To further evaluate the model’s generalization capability, we conducted transfer learning experiments by fine-tuning a model pre-trained on five pigs using data from a new, unseen individual (Fig. 5g). While training from scratch on the new subject yielded a baseline accuracy of 90.05%, fine-tuning the pre-trained model with only 30 min of new data boosted accuracy to 95.14% (Supplementary Fig. 44). This highlights the system’s robust transferability and its capacity for rapid adaptation to new individuals by leveraging pre-existing knowledge27.

To facilitate low-power, long-duration monitoring suitable for edge intelligence, we developed a lightweight binary classification model based on DRResNet. Optimization for deployment involved post-training quantization, converting model parameters from 32-bit floating points to 8-bit integers. This process compressed the model size from 70 KB to 43 KB, achieving an optimal balance between computational performance and hardware constraints while maintaining a high accuracy of 98.68% in distinguishing between healthy and abnormal states. Consequently, the model was successfully deployed directly onto a resource-constrained STM32L151 MCU (128 KB Flash, 32 KB SRAM), utilizing only 7.77 KB of RAM for runtime inference.

The final hardware deployment follows a sensor-MCU-indicator co-design architecture (Supplementary Fig. 45). To minimize power consumption, the system operates on a duty cycle, capturing a 15 s signal window every 15 min. Crucially, all data processing is executed entirely via local on-chip inference, eliminating the latency and privacy risks associated with cloud transmission, and significantly reducing communication energy costs28. Diagnostic outcomes are visualized via an LED module, where a steady light indicates a healthy state and a blinking light indicates an abnormal state (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Movie 5). With an inference latency of less than 10 ms and an average current draw of 1.0188 mA per cycle, the MiSCS sustains a battery life exceeding 120 h on a standard coin cell (Supplementary Fig. 46). Ultimately, this highly integrated sensing paradigm proves robust for practical daily activities, including eating, exercising, and sleeping, demonstrating significant potential for home healthcare and out-of-hospital patient management (Supplementary Fig. 47).

Discussion

In summary, we have developed and demonstrated the application of MiSCS, a sensing-computing integrated system for real-time metabolic health assessment. The starfish-inspired pentaradial architecture, coupled with a self-anchoring microneedle module, effectively mitigates motion artifacts arising from skin deformation-induced extrusion. This structural compliance constrained pH signal fluctuations to within 7.9 mV, representing a 38-fold reduction compared to conventional planar devices. Furthermore, the HB and Glu sensors exhibited 5.49-fold and 1.42-fold signal enhancements, respectively. In vivo validation in porcine models confirmed the system’s robust multi-parametric sensing performance and long-term biocompatibility under dynamic physiological conditions.

By synergistically integrating advanced data-centric edge intelligence, the MiSCS establishes a paradigm for decentralized metabolic management. The proposed DRResNet architecture, featuring a dynamic routing mechanism, adaptively prioritizes biomarker significance, thereby enhancing classification accuracy while reducing computational complexity. This lightweight model implementation facilitates a fully autonomous, end-to-end workflow incorporating seamless sensing, integrated computing, and local feedback. By executing on-chip inference directly on the onboard MCU, the system eliminates the energy costs and privacy risks associated with cloud-based transmission. Collectively, these capabilities ensure stable operation during dynamic daily routines, thereby offering a promising technological foundation for future home healthcare and decentralized disease management.

Methods

Materials

H2SO4, HCl, HAuCl4, Na2HPO4, NaH2PO4, H2O2, NADH, HB, and Glu were purchased from Aladdin. NaCl, KCl, CaCl2, MgCl2, FeCl3, potassium ferricyanide (III), potassium ferrocyanide (IV), ascorbic acid (AA), lactic acid (LA), uric acid (UA), acetaminophen (AP), acetoacetate (AcAc), glucose oxidase (GOx), and NAD⁺ were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Polyvinyl chloride (PVC), hyaluronic acid, aniline, glutaraldehyde, polyvinyl butyral resin (PVB), methanol, ethanol, Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.4), PBS (pH 7.4), polyethylene glycol (PEG), NiCl2, and tannic acid (TA) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Thionine (THI) was purchased from Med Chem Express. Medical tapes were purchased from 3 M (468 MP). PDMS (SYLGARD 184) was purchased from Dow Corning. β-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase (HBD) was purchased from Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. 5% Nafion solution was purchased from DuPont Co., Ltd. Water soluble silk fibroin was purchased from EFL-Tech Co., Ltd. Conductive silver paste was purchased from Luxianzi Technology Co., Ltd.

Fabrication of the MiSCS

Fabrication of microneedle array

The microneedle array was designed using SolidWorks and comprised five individual microneedle electrode blocks. Each block featured six conical microneedles with a height of 1 mm, a base diameter of 300 μm, and a center-to-center pitch of 700 μm. The fabrication of the microneedle array was conducted by precision micromachining from a poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) substrate. Subsequently, a 15 nm Cr adhesion layer and a 200 nm Au layer were sequentially deposited onto the microneedle surfaces using magnetron sputtering.

Fabrication of suction cup patch

A custom mold for the suction cup array was fabricated using polyurethane (PU), with structural parameters including an inner diameter of 2 mm, sidewall thickness of 0.2 mm, height of 1 mm, and a center-to-center spacing of 4 mm between adjacent cups along each arm. The mold was ultrasonically cleaned with deionized water and dried in a 60 °C oven for 1 h. A PDMS precursor and curing agent were mixed at 20:1 (w/w) ratio, degassed under vacuum (−0.1 MPa, 15 min), and poured into the mold. The mold was then placed on a 70 °C hotplate for 3 h to obtain a flexible PDMS substrate with biomimetic suction cup structure. TA (50 wt%) and PEG (4000, 50 wt%) were mixed at a volume ratio of 671:329 (v/v) and subjected to high-speed centrifugation (17500 × g, 3 min) to obtain the TAPE composite coating material, which was uniformly applied to the inner walls of the PDMS suction cups to form a biomimetic adhesion-enhancing layer.

Assembly of the MiSCS

The PDMS substrate was patterned into a five-armed star shaped topology using a laser cutting system guided by pre-defined alignment marks. The exposed solder joints of the microneedle sensors were bonded to single-pin headers using conductive silver adhesive and aligned with the connector. Multilayer circuit interconnections were established via a PCB, housing the signal acquisition and processing modules. Finally, the entire assembly was encapsulated within a soft Agilus30 silicone cap to ensure structural integrity and environmental protection.

Mechanical penetration tests of microneedles

The mechanical strength of microneedles was measured by a texture analyzer. In the compression test, the microneedle array was fixed at the center of the stage, and a flat-headed stainless steel cylindrical probe (diameter = 5 mm) was used to apply downward vertical pressure at a constant speed (0.01 mm s−1). To evaluate skin penetration capability, the insertion process was simulated using fresh porcine skin. The microneedles were adhered to the probe of the texture analyzer and vertically inserted into the skin fixed on the platform at a constant speed (0.01 mm s−1). The load and displacement were sampled every 5 ms to generate characteristic force-displacement curves.

Adhesion strength tests of suction cup patch

Adhesion strength of the patches was evaluated through shear and peel tests performed on fresh porcine skin. For the T-peel test, the skin was fixed on the top stage while the patch was on the bottom stage. Only the handle part of the suction cup patch bonded to the vertical table surface, with the suction cups faced and bonded to the skin. Then, the patch was pulled away in the vertical direction at a speed of 3 mm min−1 until it completely separated from the skin. For the shear test, the skin was fixed on one side of the vertical stage while the patch was on the opposite stage. Then, the patch was raised to shear away from the skin at a speed of 3 mm min−1.

Functionalization and electrochemical characterization of the biosensing array

Electrode surface activation

The gold microneedle electrodes were electrochemically pretreated in 0.1 M H2SO4 by cyclic voltammetry (CV) from 0.2 to 1.2 V at a rate of 1 V s−1 for 40 cycles, to remove surface contaminants and activate the electrodes.

Working electrode preparation

Following activation, AuNPs were electrodeposited onto the WE at −0.22 V for 200 s in a solution of 1.2 mg/mL HAuCl4, 0.1 M NaCl, and 1.5 wt% HCl. To prepare the two types of enzymatic sensors, redox mediators were electrochemically deposited onto the Glu and HB electrodes to reduce detection potential and minimize interference from other electroactive species. Before drop-casting the enzyme cocktail, the electrodes were treated with plasma cleaning for 5 min to obtain a hydrophilic surface, improving enzyme contact.

For the Glu electrodes, a PB mediator layer was deposited using CV (−0.15 to 0.3 V, 20 cycles, 20 mV s−1) in a solution containing 2.5 mM FeCl3, 100 mM KCl, 2.5 mM K3Fe(CN)6, and 100 mM HCl. The layer was then stabilized in 0.1 M KCl/HCl with CV (−0.2 to 0.5 V, 5 cycles, 50 mV s−1). A NiHCF protection layer was then deposited by CV (0–0.8 V, 50 cycles, 100 mV s−1) in a solution containing 0.5 mM NiCl2, 0.5 mM K3Fe(CN)6, 100 mM KCl, and 100 mM HCl. A 5 μL mixture of GOx (30 KU/mL in 0.1 M PBS) and glutaraldehyde (2% in deionized water) was prepared at a 1:1 (v/v) and drop-cast onto the WE29. A Nafion layer was then uniformly applied as an anti-interference layer.

For the HB electrodes, a polythionine (pTHI) layer was deposited by CV (−0.2 to 0.8 V, 20 cycles, 50 mV s−1) in 1 mM THI (0.1 M PBS). A 4 μL mixture of NAD+ (0.25 M) and HBD (500 U/mL) in 1 M Tris-HCl buffer was prepared at a 1:1 (v/v) and drop-cast onto the WE. 1 μL of glutaraldehyde (2% in deionized water) was added for cross-linking. Finally, 1 μL of PVC solution was cast onto the electrode as an anti-interference layer.

For the pH electrodes, a polyaniline (PANI) layer was deposited using CV (-0.2 to 1 V, 30 cycles, 100 mV s−1) in 0.1 M aniline (1 M HCl).

Reference electrode preparation

To prepare the Ag/AgCl RE, the microneedle array electrodes were coated with conductive silver paste and cured at 60 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, a 0.1 M FeCl3 solution was applied to the Ag surface for 30 s to chlorinate the Ag layer and form an AgCl layer. Finally, a solution containing 79.1 mg PVB and 50 mg NaCl dissolved in 1 mL methanol was drop-cast onto the Au/Ag/AgCl electrode and left to dry overnight30.

Protective coating

A degradable protective coating was uniformly applied to the WE, RE, and CE by drop-casting an aqueous solution containing 5 wt% water soluble silk fibroin and 5 wt% hyaluronic acid (1:1 volume ratio). The sensors were subsequently dried and stored at 4 °C.

In vitro electrochemical characterization and optimization

The electrochemical performance of the MiSCS was systematically characterized using a suite of techniques.

Sensitivity

The enzymatic sensors were characterized using chronoamperometry (CA). For the Glu sensors, the response current was recorded sequentially at a bias voltage of 0.6 V as Glu concentration increased from 0 to 5 mM in PBS. For the HB sensors, the response current was recorded sequentially at a bias voltage of 0.3 V as HB concentration increased from 0 to 4.5 mM in PBS. The pH sensors were characterized using OCP measurements. Buffered solutions (pH 5–9) were prepared using Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4 and calibrated with a pH meter. The electrode potential was measured against the RE across varying pH levels. To test the pH influence on PB-NiHCF-based Glu electrode, Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4 buffers were prepared and calibrated containing 0–2 mM H2O2.

Selectivity

To verify the anti-interference capability of the enzymatic sensors, the current response was monitored during the sequential addition of 5 mM LA, 1 mM UA, 1 mM AP, 5 mM HB, and 3 mM Glu to PBS. For the pH sensor, potential stability was tested by adding 1 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, 4 mM KCl, and 20 mM NaCl solutions to Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4 buffers. Cross-sensitivity was rigorously quantified in artificial ISF, prepared following established protocols31. The artificial ISF was sequentially spiked with physiologically relevant interferents: 1 mM AA, 5 mM LA, 1 mM UA, 1 mM AP, 2 mM AcAc, 1 mM tryptophan, 1 mM histidine, and a mixture of ions (1 mM NH4Cl, 1 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, 4 mM KCl, and 20 mM NaCl). Finally, the target analytes (3 mM HB, 5 mM Glu, and 0.01 mM HCl) were introduced to verify the signal fidelity in complex matrices.

Reproducibility

Inter-batch reproducibility was evaluated using five independent sensors under identical conditions. Glu sensor: 1 mM Glu in PBS, HB sensor: 1 mM HB in PBS, pH sensor: 0.1 M PBS at pH 7.4.

Stability

Sensor stability was evaluated through three distinct tests. First, operational stability and signal drift were assessed by continuously monitoring the sensor’s real-time response over a 24-h period while immersed in artificial ISF. Furthermore, the biofouling resistance of the enzymatic biosensor was quantitatively assessed through comparative CA pre- and post-immersion (72 h) in artificial ISF (pH 7.4). Finally, storage stability was evaluated by storing the sensors at 4 °C and recording their electrochemical response daily over a 14-day period.

In vivo biocompatibility assessment

The in vivo biocompatibility of the microneedle patch was evaluated over a continuous 72-h period. Three rats were assigned to distinct experimental conditions: (1) Positive Control Group, implanted with a stainless-steel hypodermic needle; (2) Experimental Group, treated with the microneedle patch; and (3) Negative Control Group, covered with a medical adhesive bandage.

To assess the systemic immune response, blood samples were collected from the tail vein at six specific time points: 0 (pre-implantation), 1, 4, 8, 24, and 72 h post-implantation. The blood samples were centrifuged to isolate the serum. The concentrations of key inflammatory biomarkers, including Interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and C-reactive protein (CRP), were quantified using commercial Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) kits, strictly following the manufacturer’s protocols.

At the endpoint of the 72 h study, the implanted devices or materials were carefully removed. The rats were monitored for a 30-min recovery interval post-removal before being euthanized. Skin tissues (1 × 1 cm2 with a thickness of 2–3 mm) were excised and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Subsequently, the tissue sections were processed for immunohistochemical (IHC) staining to evaluate the local expression levels of the inflammatory cytokines Interleukin-6 (IL-6) and IL-1β.

On-body evaluation of the MiSCS

All animal experiments were reviewed, approved, and supervised by ZJU-Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Review Committee (approval number: ZJU20250685). The animals (three Bama minipigs and three Large White pigs) were housed in climate-controlled facilities and fed once daily at 8:00 a.m. Following anesthesia induction and depilation, the MiSCS was securely fixed on the animal’s neck. A multiparameter monitor was used to continuously track three physiological parameters, including respiratory rate, blood oxygen saturation, and heart rate throughout the experiment.

Short-term intervention

For real-time response validation, an acute Glu fluctuation model was induced in one Bama minipig (Subject ID 1) via 50% Glu injections at 0 and 90 min. Sensor readings and auricular venous blood samples were collected every 30 min over a 4-h period for comparative analysis.

Long-term dietary intervention

To evaluate the system’s performance across varying metabolic states, all subjects underwent a three-phase dietary intervention: (i) Non-ketogenic state, continuous feeding of maintenance feed. (ii) Short-term ketogenic state, ketogenic feed intervention for 3 d. (iii) Long-term ketogenic state, ketogenic feed continuous intervention for 14 d. Detailed compositions and energy contribution ratios of the maintenance and ketogenic diets are provided in the Supplementary Table 3. For each state, data acquisition was performed for 30 min both before and after a 2-h fasting period. Blood samples (2 mL) were collected from the auricular vein every 30 min.

Data validation and accuracy quantification

Serum was separated from collected blood samples to quantify Glu, HB concentrations, and blood pH. The HB, Glu, and pH levels detected via sensors were compared to the actual values for quantifying the detection accuracies.

AI algorithms for health state assessment

Risk classification assessment and dataset construction

The dataset was constructed using sensor data collected from the long-term intervention described above. The MiSCS operated at a sampling frequency of 10 Hz, accumulating 64,800 s of data for model training. The physiological and biochemical parameter ranges under normal and pathological ketotic conditions were systematically investigated, specifically evaluating three biochemical markers (HB, Glu, and pH) and three physiological parameters (heart rate, blood oxygen saturation, and respiratory rate). To align with the 5-s sampling rate of the physiological monitor, sensor data within each 5-s interval served as a single input instance, and the data point at the 5-s mark was designated for labeling. Each parameter was categorized into three levels based on reference ranges: normal, mild deviation, and severe deviation. A composite risk level was determined by integrating these statuses: a “Healthy” label was assigned when all indicators were normal, a mild deviation flag indicated “Low-risk”, two mild deviation flags or one severe deviation flag indicated “Moderate-risk”, and any more severe combinations were classified as “High-risk”. The final labeled dataset was partitioned into training and validation sets at an 8:2 ratio32. During the model training process, only the data from 5 participants were used (Subject ID 1, 2, 3, 5, 6).

Model selection for ketogenic health condition classification

All models were implemented in Python and developed on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 GPU. Three-channel sensor signals served as inputs, with output labels assigned according to the classification criteria described previously. Using this framework, we constructed multi-class classification models and evaluated them based on average accuracy and computational complexity. Our comparative analysis included six representative baseline models (MLP, GRU, LSTM, AlexNet, ResNet, and Transformer) as well as three representative lightweight architectures (ShuffleNet33, SqueezeNet34, and MobileNet35). Furthermore, the generalization capability was evaluated through transfer learning, where the pre-trained DRResNet was fine-tuned using data from an unseen subject (Subject ID 4).

Model lightweighting and local deployment

To address power and computational constraints in local deployment, we optimized the DRResNet architecture into a lightweight model for edge computing. The model output was simplified to a binary classification, distinguishing exclusively between “healthy” and “abnormal” states. The pre-trained model was exported to ONNX format and dynamically quantized from 32-bit floating-point to 8-bit integers, ensuring minimal precision loss. The quantized model was then converted into efficient, self-contained C code without dynamic memory allocation to enable inference on resource-constrained devices. Using the GCC toolchain, the code was compiled for the STM32L151 MCU. The integrated system processes real-time sensor data directly on-chip, providing immediate visual feedback via local LED indicators.

Ethics

Every experiment involving animals, human participants, or clinical samples has been carried out following a protocol approved by an ethical commission. For the human validation portion of this study, data were collected from a single female participant (n = 1, age 24). The participant gave informed written consent.

Data analysis

Data are presented as the means ± SDs. Data from experiments were analyzed with Origin 2018. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for comparisons among multiple groups (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001). Model construction and FEA were performed using SolidWorks 2018 and ABAQUS, respectively. Detailed physical settings, model parameters, and the governing equations are provided in the Supplementary Materials.
