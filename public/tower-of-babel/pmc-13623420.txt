Beyond diagnostic accuracy: evaluating the clinical utility of artificial intelligence for suspicious lesion triage
Authors: Nethmi Chandrasekara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Nirula et al. reports pooled sensitivity and specificity of 89% and 82%, respectively, for artificial intelligence (AI)-based differentiation between benign and malignant skin lesions. This highlights the diagnostic potential of these tools. However, diagnostic accuracy alone does not establish clinical utility; a tool that performs well in retrospective validation may not meaningfully alter referral patterns, reduce waiting times or improve healthcare efficiency in practice. As we move closer to the implementation of AI technologies in dermatology services, future studies should evaluate implementation outcomes – including referral avoidance, biopsy rates and healthcare utilization – alongside traditional accuracy metrics.

Dear Editor, Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly proposed as a solution to the rising demand for dermatology services, particularly in the assessment and triage of skin lesions. In this context, the systematic review by Nirula et al.1 provides a timely evaluation of the performance of AI tools in distinguishing benign from malignant skin lesions. The authors report pooled sensitivity and specificity estimates of 89% and 82%, respectively, for distinguishing between benign and malignant lesions, highlighting the potential of AI to improve lesion assessment and reduce unnecessary procedures. Although these findings are encouraging, the next challenge for the field is not to demonstrate further improvements in diagnostic accuracy, but to determine whether AI can deliver meaningful clinical utility as a triage tool within real-world care pathways.

Ongoing demand for skin cancer assessment continues to place substantial pressure on dermatology services. Consequently, the greatest opportunity for AI may not lie solely in classifying lesions more accurately, but in supporting the efficient allocation of dermatology resources. A tool capable of reliably identifying benign lesions has the theoretical potential to support referral management, the prioritization of urgent cases, and the reassurance of patients who are at low risk. Such applications may reduce unnecessary specialist appointments, while simultaneously preserving timely access for patients requiring in-person assessment. A recent report from the British Association of Dermatologists2 has similarly highlighted the potential of AI to alleviate service pressures, while emphasizing that successful implementation will depend on robust validation and integration within existing clinical pathways. Notably, the 2025 National Institute for Health and Care Excellence early value assessment of AI technologies for skin lesion triage3 concluded that the Deep Ensemble for Recognition of Malignancy AI tool shows a signal of effectiveness, recommending conditional use subject to further evidence generation to establish real-world benefit, thereby underscoring that regulatory endorsement itself does not yet constitute evidence of clinical efficacy.

Despite this, evidence of diagnostic performance alone does not establish clinical utility. A model with high sensitivity and specificity may still have a limited impact on patient care if it does not meaningfully alter referral patterns, reduce waiting times or improve healthcare efficiency. Keane and Topol4 have described the gap between algorithmic performance and real-world clinical benefit as the ‘AI chasm’, arguing that validation of an algorithm’s accuracy is not equivalent to demonstrating clinical efficacy. As AI technologies move closer to implementation, prospective evaluation within routine clinical pathways will be essential to determine whether these tools can safely improve service delivery.

Encouragingly, recent evidence suggests that the field is beginning to move beyond retrospective performance assessment. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective studies5 found that AI-assisted melanoma diagnosis achieved diagnostic performance comparable to that of dermatologists, while highlighting the need for larger, methodologically robust studies evaluating effectiveness in real-world clinical settings. Similarly, recent reviews6,7 have emphasized that future progress in dermatological AI will depend not only on improvements in diagnostic accuracy, but also on demonstrating clinical utility, implementation feasibility and benefits to healthcare delivery.

Therefore, future studies should evaluate implementation outcomes alongside traditional accuracy metrics. Measures such as referral avoidance, diagnostic delay, biopsy rates, patient satisfaction, clinician confidence and healthcare utilization may provide a more meaningful assessment of whether AI-enabled triage systems deliver value in clinical practice. These are domains that established reporting frameworks, such as DECIDE-AI,8 recommend for evaluation in early-stage clinical studies of AI decision support. Importantly, AI-assisted triage should be viewed as a mechanism to augment rather than replace clinical decision-making. The most successful implementation strategies are likely to involve AI supporting clinicians within established pathways, particularly in primary care and teledermatology settings where rapid risk stratification may help direct patients towards the most appropriate level of care.

The review by Nirula et al.1 provides encouraging evidence that AI can distinguish benign from malignant lesions with reasonable accuracy. As the field progresses, demonstrating improvements in clinical workflow, service efficiency and patient outcomes may prove to be as important as further gains in diagnostic performance. Future implementation studies will determine whether AI can fulfil its promise as a safe and effective triage tool for increasingly pressured dermatology services.
