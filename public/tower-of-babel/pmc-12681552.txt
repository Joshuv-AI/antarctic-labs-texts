Exploring brain activation during a buttoning task in adults: A functional near infrared spectroscopy investigation
Authors: Niloufar Benam, Courtney Pollock, Jill G Zwicker, Shannon B Lim, Kaya Yoshida, Julia Schmidt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe ability to complete activities of daily living (ADLs) is an important part of daily life and can promote well-being and independence. There is currently limited knowledge of brain activity during ADLs (e.g. dressing tasks). Previous studies explored brain activity during dressing using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI); however, the supine position during fMRI is not a natural dressing posture and may impact findings. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) is a promising method of data collection as it can investigate brain activity in a natural state (sitting) during dressing. In this study, to understand brain activity during buttoning in unimpaired adults, twenty participants (25–65 years) completed an upper extremity task of buttoning in three 20 s repetitions with 15 s rest in between each activity block. Brain activation patterns were recorded using fNIRS over the prefrontal, premotor, supplementary motor, sensorimotor, and posterior parietal cortices. Compared to the resting period, significantly higher activation during the activity block was observed in all recorded regions but the posterior parietal cortex. Understanding brain activity in unimpaired adults during the performance of activities of daily living is a critical first-step for investigating brain activation in different clinical populations.Keywords: Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), Activities of daily life, Dressing task, Healthy adults, Cerebral hemodynamics

Introduction

Activities of daily living (ADLs) consist of tasks such as maintaining personal hygiene, dressing, toileting, transferring, and eating, among many others (Edemekong et al., 2022). Being able to complete ADLs and self-care activities independently can lead to a sense of well-being and is often personally meaningful for individuals (Mlinac and Feng, 2016). Decreased ability to complete activities of daily living can affect one's quality of life and confidence for individuals that value independence in their daily lives (Mlinac and Feng, 2016; Robinson-Smith et al., 2000).

Different brain imaging technologies have been used to investigate brain activation patterns when completing ADLs such as dressing. One widely used neuroimaging tool for research in this area is functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). However, fMRI does not allow for brain activation patterns to be recorded in a natural state, as individuals have to be in a supine position during an imaging session. This is far from the natural state of many ADL activities such as dressing. fNIRS is an alternate neuroimaging tool that overcomes the limitations of MRI, as it is portable, non-invasive, silent, and has a relatively low sensitivity to motion artifacts (Pinti et al., 2020). Being portable, fNIRS can allow for brain activation levels to be measured in many different positions such as sitting, standing, and walking. In addition, fNIRS has a relatively high temporal resolution on the level of 0.01 s (Wilcox and Biondi, 2015). For these reasons, fNIRS is an important neuroimaging tool that can provide information on cortical brain activity while an individual is completing ADLs in a natural posture.

Previous research has explored brain activity during upper extremity movements in unimpaired adults using fNIRS (Yang et al., 2020). A previous study explored brain activation patterns of unimpaired adults during finger extensions and shoulder movement tasks using fNIRS in order to understand mechanisms of recovery after different neurological disorders (Yang et al., 2020). Their study illustrated bilateral brain activation during both tasks of finger extension and shoulder movement with a higher activation in the contralateral compared to ipsilateral primary motor cortex (Yang et al., 2020). However, this study does not directly involve an ADL task that incorporates coordinating movement patterns within multiple joints in the upper extremity simultaneously. In fact, there is limited research investigating brain activation patterns in unimpaired adults during ADLs using fNIRS. In fact, most fNIRS studies use controlled repetitive tasks such as unimanual and bimanual reaching, standardized manual dexterity assessment tools to explore brain activity (Li et al., 2024; Nishiyori et al., 2016). This study intends to expand this by using an ecologically valid ADL task.

The objective of this study was to explore cortical activity of the brain during a specific bimanual ADL task – buttoning –in unimpaired adults using fNIRS. These data will provide foundational understanding of cortical brain activity in unimpaired adults performing an ADL task without restriction of movement. Study findings will provide clinically important information that can serve as points of reference when studying brain activity in individuals with neurological injuries such as traumatic brain injury (TBI).

Methods

Study design

An observational study design was used for this study. This design allows for observing and gaining information on brain activation patterns while completing a buttoning task in unimpaired adults. Ethics approval was completed by the University of British Columbia (certificate #H22-00456). Informed consent was obtained from each participant both written and verbally. The participants were given a $30 honorarium for their time and participation.

Participants

Recruitment

Participants with no previous history of impairments were recruited using convenience sampling from the community.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants needed to understand English to be included in our study in order to understand and follow instructions during assessment and data collection. Participants were at least 25 years old to increase the chances of the prefrontal cortex being fully developed and no more than 65 years old to reduce risk of participants experiencing age-related changes in brain health (Arain et al., 2013), (Tyndall et al., 2018). Participants were excluded if they had a prior brain injury as well as any injury that impaired their ability to complete a buttoning task.

Demographic data

Information on age, sex, and handedness (self-report) were collected from the participants before starting with data collection.

Clinical measure of hand dexterity

The Purdue Pegboard Test (PPT) assesses dexterity in hand movement and was used to measure the upper extremity function (Rule et al., 2021). This test has been shown to have a high reliability and validity in previous studies (Rule et al., 2021).

Experimental protocol

Before starting data collection, the participants' head circumference was measured (including nasion to inion and the periauricular points) in order to fit an appropriately sized head cap (EASYCAP GmbH, Germany). If a participant had a head circumference in between the available head cap sizes, the smaller head cap size was used to ensure proper fit. The fNIRS optodes were situated on the cap prior to the arrival of the participants in order to decrease the length of data collection session and participant fatigue. Upon the arrival of the participants, the Cz position (10/20 international) (Jurcak et al., 2007) was measured by finding the intersection of the distance between the nasion-inion and periauricular points. The cap was then placed on the participant's head while visually inspecting an appropriate position with respect to the Cz position. A top and side view of a participant wearing the fNIRS cap is shown in Fig. 1 in the supplementary material. All participants were asked to wear an extra-large shirt (provided) with five buttons and to sit on a chair with a backrest. The fNIRS data collection device covered one of the buttons, leaving four buttons for the activity. The buttons on the shirt were on the right side with the button slits on the left side, making buttoning this shirt biased for right handed participants. The buttons were 1 cm in diameter.

Top and side view of a participant wearing the fNIRS cap.

Participants performed buttoning and unbuttoning of four buttons on a dress shirt over three 20-s activity blocks. They were instructed to button and unbutton the dress shirt from top to bottom as many times and as accurately and efficiently as possible. The researcher provided cues to start buttoning and to stop buttoning by saying “button” and “rest” respectively. All participants were instructed to refrain from making unnecessary movements and were asked not to talk during the activity and the resting state. Each activity block was repeated three times with 15-s breaks in between each block. There was also a 15-s rest block before the first and after the last activity period. Out of these 15-s rest periods immediately prior to each buttoning trial, 7-s were used as baseline for making comparisons to the brain activation levels during the activity block. The rest period between each activity block allowed fNIRS signals to return to a resting state prior to the start of the next activity block. To maintain consistency in brain activation patterns, participants were asked to maintain a downward head posture during the entire data collection session. To do so they were asked to focus on a red circle placed directly under their view during the rest period. The data collection process is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Data collection process for completing three activity blocks of buttoning.

fNIRS system and optode array design

We used a portable and wireless continuous wave fNIRS device (NIRSport2, NIRx Medical Technology, Germany) with LED optodes and wavelengths of 760 and 850 nm which allow for the measurement of changes in the concentration of both oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbO and HbR, respectively). This system measured cortical activity at a sampling rate of 4.3597 Hz. A total of 16 sources and 15 long separation detectors (approximately 3 cm apart from the source) and 8 short separation detectors (approximately 8 mm apart from the source) were used. This configuration resulted in a total of 54 channels with 46 long and 8 short separation source-detector pairs. The long separation channels measured changes in hemoglobin concentrations within the superficial layers, scalp, skull, and the brain cortex. The short channels measured noise within the superficial layers which we removed from the raw data. The optode design (geometry) used in this study is illustrated in Fig. 3.

The red circles indicate sources while the blue circles illustrate the detectors. The blue outer circles indicate the position of the short separation detectors. The purple lines indicate long separation channels between sources and detector pairs. Brain regions corresponding to these channels are illustrated in Table A1 in the supplementary material. Primary outcome measures

The primary outcomes of the study were changes in the concentration of HbO and HbR within the cerebral cortex due to the task onset. Oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) has a higher absorption for wavelengths of NIR light greater than 800 nm and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR) has a higher absorption for wavelengths of NIR light lower than 800 nm (Pinti et al., 2020). This difference in absorptions allows for measurement of changes in the concentration of HbO and HbR within the cerebral cortex (Pinti et al., 2020). An increase in HbO and a decrease in HbR in a brain region can signify increased brain activity (Pinti et al., 2020).

Data processing

After data collection, the fNIRS data were preprocessed using the open-source software, Homer3 (Huppert et al., 2009). All Homer3 functions are noted within square brackets. To begin preprocessing, all noisy channels were first removed [hmrR_PruneChannels: dRange = 5e-4 to 1e+00, SNRtresh = 3.0, SDrange = 0–45]. The signal was then turned into optical density [hmrR_Intensity2OD]. This was followed by the detection of motion artifacts using 0.5 s time windows to find whether the signal exceeded 20 standard deviations above the mean signal for every channel or illustrated a change in signal greater than 5 times in amplitude. [hmrR_MotionArtifactByChannel: tMotion = 0.5, tMask = 1.0, STDEVthresh = 20.0, AMPthresh = 5.0]. This was followed by motion correction using a wavelet transformation (Hocke et al., 2018) with a 1.5 interquartile range [hmrR_MotionCorrectWavelet: iqr = 1.50 ]. A low pass filter was then applied to the data [hpf = 0.0, lpf = 0.15] and the data were converted to hemoglobin concentration using the modified Beer-Lambert equation [hmrR_OD2Conc: ppf = 6.0, 6.0] (Kocsis et al., 2006; Sassaroli and Fantini, 2004). The hemodynamic response function (HRF) was then estimated using a general linear model (GLM) with an ordinary least squares method (Ye et al., 2009). Rather than assuming a fixed canonical shape, the HRF was modelled as a series of consecutive Gaussian basis functions (0.5 s width, 0.5 s step), allowing a flexible representation of the hemodynamic response (Yücel et al., 2021). To remove superficial noise from the signal, the data were regressed out from the short separation channel that had the highest correlation to each channel (Gagnon et al., 2012). Drifts within the signal were corrected using a 3rd order polynomial correction [hmrR_GLM: trange = −10.0 25.0, glmSolveMethod = 1, idxBasis = 1, paramsBasis = 0.5 0.5, rhoSD ss Thresh = 15.0, flagNuisanceRMethod = 1, driftOrder = 3, c Vector = 0].

A custom MATLAB script (Matlab2017b, Mathworks, RRID:SCR_001622) was used to further process the data. First, baseline correction was applied to the data using the average from 7-s prior to the onset of the buttoning task. For each participant, data were separated into two segments: rest (−7 to 0 s) and task (3–20 s). The average amplitude of activation change during these segments was used for comparison between the segments. The first 3 s after the onset of the stimulus was not used for analysis due to the delay in the hemodynamic response function which has been reported to be < 2 s after stimulus onset (Taylor et al., 2022). The wavelet correction method has been used by a previous study exploring brain activity during hand and shoulder movement (Yeo et al., 2013) and has also been supported for use by comprehensive comparison studies, investigating different motion correction algorithms (Hocke et al., 2018). After completing wavelet motion correction, major spikes were removed from the data without making any other major changes in the rest of the data.

Probabilistic localization

Spatial location of optodes was collected for eight participants. In order to localize the specific 3D locations of the optodes for each participant, a 3D digitizer (Polhemus Patriot, USA) and the software PHOEBE (Pollonini et al., 2016) was used. A 3D custom printed interface between the digitizing stylus and the optode holder was used. The 3D coordinate data were imported into AtlasViewer (Aasted et al., 2015). The data were then used to project the channels to the Colin27 atlas brain. AtlasViewer then provided the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) coordinates as well as Automated Anatomical Labeling Projections, which were then translated into Brodmann areas. The Brodmann area that was in majority among the eight participants was applied to each channel (as shown in Table A1 in Supplementary Material). From the participants with digitization data collected using PHOEBE, some channels had no digitization data collected. As a result, a MATLAB embedded toolbox called fNIRS Optodes’ Location Decider (fOLD) was used for localizing such channels. Brodmann areas in the majority within the eight participants with available digitization data were applied to participants for whom no digitization data was collected.

Statistical analysis

To investigate brain activity during buttoning, paired t-tests were used to find the difference in average hemodynamic response function between baseline (7 s immediately before the start of the task) and the task (3–20 s after the onset of the task). Baseline compared to task period were paired for each of the four brain regions of interest including the prefrontal cortex, premotor cortex and supplementary motor area, sensorimotor cortex, and posterior parietal cortex. The IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 28.0.1.1 (14), RRID: SCR_002865) was used for completing the paired t-test analysis. All of the paired t-tests were completed with an alpha of 0.05.

Results

Demographics summary

Brain activation data was collected from 24 unimpaired adults. However, 4 participants had brain activation data impacted significantly by noise and hence could not be used to retrieve useful information. As such, data from 20 participants were included in the analysis. These participants had an age range of 25–65 years (mean age: 29.6, 7 males, 17 females). All participants were right-handed and scored within the normal range of the sum of the scores for the right, left, and both hands on the Purdue Pegboard Test (PPT). Discussion

Our study is the first to investigate cortical activity of the brain in unimpaired adults during shirt buttoning, a common ADL task. Findings from our study demonstrate that performing a shirt buttoning task resulted in significantly increased cortical activity in the prefrontal cortex, premotor cortex and supplementary motor area, as well as the sensorimotor cortex when compared to the rest condition. The increase in prefrontal cortex activity may have been due to increased cognitive load within the task of buttoning, as it was an unfamiliar shirt for participants, with no practice period prior to the task. As participants had very limited time to complete the buttoning task (60 s in total during the study), the task was likely novel to them throughout the study. A previous study indicated significantly higher activity within the prefrontal cortex as well as the premotor cortex during the initial movements in a finger tapping task (Wu et al., 2004). Furthermore, prefrontal cortex has an important role for motor function during selection and planning of the movement (Kantak et al., 2012) which are needed for the task of buttoning. Furthermore, participants were instructed to button the shirt as fast as they can which may have added to the cognitive load and the observed increase in prefrontal cortex activity.

We found an increase in cortical activation within the premotor cortex and supplementary area. This could be due to sequential movement of buttoning, which is aligned with a previous study that found premotor cortex and supplementary motor area to have important function for the preparation and performance of sequential movements (Dayan and Cohen, 2011). The activity of buttoning involves creating a grasping shape with hands prior to the task such that the fingers are positioned properly around the buttons. This hand posture transformation requires a neuronal network that involves the premotor cortex (Kantak et al., 2012). This is further supported by a previous study where the creation of a virtual lesion using repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation disrupted individuals’ ability to form grasping shapes with fingers (Davare et al., 2006). The supplementary motor area is also important for the preparation and initiation of sequential motor activities, such that lesions in this area can lead to a decrease in the ability to self-initiate sequential movements (Ohbayashi, 2021). Furthermore, a study exploring brain activity during a bimanual pinch force task illustrated an increase in supplementary motor area and premotor cortex activity (Karabanov et al., 2023). The task in this study is similar to buttoning where different levels of force have to be applied to the buttons in order to complete the task.

The increase in brain activity in the sensorimotor cortex, comprised of the primary motor and primary somatosensory cortices, aligns with a previous study which explored brain activity during bimanual tasks of symmetric and parallel wrist flexions and extensions (Goble et al., 2010). This study found increased activity bilaterally in the sensorimotor cortex in both older and younger adults (Goble et al., 2010). Similarly, a recent study exploring brain activity during opening and closing movements of the hand found increased contralateral hemisphere activation in the sensorimotor cortex (Bonnal et al., 2023).

When observing channels from all participants, some cases of double peaks were observed. This has been observed in the HbO hemodynamic response function (HRF) curve for functional studies related to the motor cortex, especially for those involving a 20 s activity period (Kashou et al., 2016). This was not present for all participants as some only presented one peak. Some studies have also illustrated an HRF response a few seconds before the onset of the stimulus. This early onset of the HRF response has been explained to be due to the mental preparation that participants may have had prior to the start of the task.

This study involved several limitations. First, our participants were all right-handed and may have had different brain activation patterns compared to left-handed individuals, as handedness can impact the upper limb motor control (Schaffer and Sainburg, 2017). Future studies could address this issue by involving an equal representation of both left-handed and right-handed participants. Furthermore, we recruited participants using convenience sampling, with many younger participants (mean age = 29.6) which may limit the generalizability of these results to all unimpaired adults. Furthermore, the fNIRS equipment may not have been consistently collecting accurate data in participants with different hair textures, as having darker hair, darker skin pigmentation, or higher hair root density may obstruct light and reduce optical contact with the scalp (Orihuela-Espina et al., 2010). This may have limited the diversity of participants included in the data analysis. In addition, despite completing measurements and having different cap sizes, it is still possible for the cap to not fully fit an individuals’ head due to their head shape or their hair thickness (Yang et al., 2020). Furthermore, having the same inter stimulus time intervals, may have led to participants anticipating the task onset and the observed early HRF response within the results. In addition, this study did not include a repetitive motor task in addition to the ADL task of buttoning to provide a point of comparison as this study was an exploratory study. Furthermore, this study included three activity blocks to prevent repetition suppression effects that may be possible after repeated presentation of the task (Bouchon et al., 2015).

Future research can take this study a step further by exploring brain activation patterns in populations with neurological injuries such as during upper extremity and other ADL tasks. Furthermore, comparison studies can explore differences in brain activation between unimpaired adults and individuals with a TBI. Consideration will need to be made for neurological populations that may elicit seizures and tremors that might interfere with fNIRS data collection. As such, future research may devise an ADL task that is suitable for a neurological population. Furthermore, future studies may include a repetitive task in addition to the ADL task as a point of comparison.

Conclusions

This study showed increased cortical brain activity related to the ADL task of buttoning using fNIRS in unimpaired adults within the prefrontal, premotor, supplementary motor, and sensorimotor cortices. This new understanding of brain activation patterns in unimpaired adults can provide important knowledge for future studies investigating brain activity in individuals with neurological injuries such as TBI. Through the use of a multi-channel fNIRS system, we were able to collect data from bilateral parietal and frontal cortices. This can guide the selection of channels and regions of studies in future studies. fNIRS allows for more dynamic movement, a faster brain activity measurement and is less time consuming, compared to the gold standard of fMRI. Findings from this study can be beneficial for clinical application and the use of fNIRS with clinical populations in future studies.
