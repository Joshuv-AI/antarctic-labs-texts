Development and psychometric evaluation of a multimedia quality assessment tool for health education and promotion through mixed methods
Authors: Saeideh Rastjoo, Amin Salehi-Abargouei, Mahsa Khodayarian, Reyhane Sefidkar, Zohreh Rahaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHealth strategies primarily aim to improve public health, a goal increasingly supported by multimedia resources that enhance learning and audience satisfaction. Despite their significant influence on health literacy, quality standards for producing and disseminating such multimedia materials remain under-researched. This study aimed to develop and validate a comprehensive tool for assessing the quality of multimedia resources in health education and promotion. This methodological study employed a two-phase explanatory sequential mixed-methods approach. It began with a scoping review to evaluate the initial item pool, followed by a qualitative phase utilizing semi-structured interviews with 22 health, media, and communication professionals to generate additional items. The subsequent quantitative phase rigorously assessed the instrument’s validity and reliability using McDonald’s omega (ω) and the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC). Combining findings from the scoping review and qualitative phase (which covered content quality, aesthetics, communication, and effectiveness) resulted in 131 initial items. After rigorous validation, this pool was reduced to a final 64-item questionnaire featuring 10 factors, including text/image/sound quality, layout, interactivity, behavior focus, and credibility. The tool demonstrated excellent reliability, with an overall McDonald’s omega of 0.95 and an ICC between raters of 0.86. The developed 64-item questionnaire exhibits acceptable validity and reliability, establishing it as a useful, comprehensive instrument that empowers specialists and stakeholders to enhance the design and assessment of multimedia health education resources, ultimately contributing to improved community health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43955-1.Keywords: Multimedia, Health Education, Health Promotion, PsychometricsSubject terms: Education, Health care

Introduction

Health education, a vital component of health promotion, provides informed learning opportunities and communication strategies aimed at enhancing health literacy by increasing knowledge and developing individual and community life skills1. Understanding the dynamics of communication between sender and receiver, along with the medium employed, constitutes crucial tools for health promoters. This can be achieved through a variety of traditional (print) and modern (multimedia) channels2. Multimedia integration of text, static or dynamic visuals, and sound enhances learning compared to traditional text-only delivery3. By employing the dual coding of information—both visual and auditory—multimedia facilitates easier comprehension, higher engagement, and improved recall than standard written materials4.

Various studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of multimedia use in the outcomes of health education and promotion program interventions5–10. Furthermore, some health researchers comparing multimedia with written materials in their interventions found that multimedia interventions were more effective in increasing individuals’ awareness and attitudes11–15.

Despite the benefits of multimedia educational programs, traditional teaching methods like lectures and written materials such as leaflets and pamphlets persist. While these may offer a quick, short-term impact, their effect tends to be temporary, whereas multimedia interventions are more likely to foster long-lasting changes in attitudes and behavior16,17.

Recognizing the importance of multimedia interventions, the quality of their content and audiovisual features is crucial for behavior change in health education and promotion, necessitating an analytical and critical perspective on educational media. Researchers define “media literacy” as the practical mastery of information and communication technology, including educational media, combined with a critical approach to them18. High-quality educational media fosters audience media literacy, enabling accurate content understanding and informed decision-making19. This is crucial because the way audiences receive and evaluate information—particularly high-quality, evidence-based information—directly impacts their ability to make informed choices20. Furthermore, high-quality educational media, assessed through its content, visuals, and audio, promotes effective health communication, leading to improved health literacy, behavior change, and greater public participation in health education and promotion programs21.

Therefore, meticulous planning is essential for the preparation, production, and distribution of effective, standardized media, along with a standard criterion for media evaluation to ensure impactful education. For written educational materials, tools like the Suitability Assessment of Materials (SAM), the Cloze Procedure for readability, and various other methods exist22.

However, limited evidence exists regarding the evaluation of educational multimedia quality. Some studies have solely assessed specific aspects of multimedia content, such as textual features like difficulty level, comprehensibility, organization, design, and layout23,24. Alternatively, some research has focused solely on evaluating educational videos within health education programs25,26. Theoretically, the development of this instrument is anchored in the Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (CTML), specifically drawing on the dual-channel and active processing principles27, and the principles of Health Communication Excellence28. This integrated framework moves beyond mere content evaluation by focusing on how the structural and technical quality of a multimedia resource (e.g., visual hierarchy, auditory clarity, and interactivity) manages cognitive load and enhances the delivery of health messages. Therefore, the present study aims to identify the key dimensions and components influencing multimedia quality by reviewing domestic and international literature and surveying experts. By generating an item pool and assessing its validity and reliability, this research seeks to provide a standardized framework for health communication quality that integrates pedagogical principles with modern media production standards to enhance educational effectiveness and behavioral change facilitation.

Methods

Study design

This study employed an exploratory sequential mixed-methods design to develop and validate a comprehensive instrument for evaluating the quality of health education multimedia. The study was conducted in two distinct phases: First, a qualitative phase comprising a scoping review followed by semi-structured expert interviews was performed to identify quality indicators and develop an initial item pool. The scoping review findings directly informed the interview guide to address existing gaps in the literature. Second, the quantitative phase utilized the results from the first phase to perform item reduction and rigorous psychometric evaluation (validity and reliability testing) of the final instrument.

Phase 1: Qualitative phase (Item Generation)

The item generation process involved three systematic steps: (a) conducting a scoping review to map existing evidence; (b) performing semi-structured interviews with experts to capture practical and specialized indicators; and (c) integrating the findings from both the literature and interviews to develop the initial item pool.

Step 1: A scoping review

Information sources and search strategy

To identify relevant studies on the quality assessment of health education multimedia, a comprehensive search was conducted across electronic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, and ISI Web of Science, from their respective inception to November 11, 2023. The search employed keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms such as ‘assessment’, ‘evaluation’, ‘quality indicators’, ‘multimedia’, ‘audiovisual media’, ‘health education’, and ‘health promotion’, combined effectively using Boolean operators (AND/OR). Following the initial screening, a supplementary search was performed on Google Scholar, and the reference lists of identified articles were manually reviewed to ensure no relevant sources were overlooked. No start-date restrictions were applied to ensure a comprehensive mapping of the literature. The complete search strings for each database are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Eligibility criteria

To achieve a comprehensive mapping of foundational concepts, no temporal or language restrictions were imposed, encompassing all available records from the databases’ inception up to the aforementioned search date. Regarding non-English records, titles and abstracts were screened (using translation tools when necessary) to assess their relevance; however, these studies were excluded during the screening phase as they either did not meet the specific inclusion criteria or lacked sufficient technical details for data extraction. Studies were eligible if they focused on the design and evaluation of multimedia content (audio, text, images, and video) within health education and promotion contexts. All peer-reviewed study designs were considered. To minimize selection bias, for any study whose full text was not immediately available, we conducted extensive searches through institutional library resources and contacted the corresponding authors. Ultimately, studies where the full text remained inaccessible despite these exhaustive retrieval efforts were excluded. Furthermore, studies primarily focusing on the effectiveness of websites or applications were excluded from this review.

Study selection

The screening process commenced after a comprehensive search across all databases and the removal of duplicate records. Two independent researchers then conducted a three-phase screening of the identified studies. This involved reviewing the titles, abstracts, and subsequently, the full texts of the articles. Agreement between the two researchers was mandatory at each stage for an article to be included. In cases of disagreement, a third researcher was consulted to reach a final decision. The systematic search process was facilitated using EndNote (version 8).

Data charting and synthesis of results

To ensure transparent reporting of the included evidence, the PRISMA-ScR framework29 was utilized. A standardized data extraction form was developed to capture relevant information, including author(s), year of publication, country of origin, study design, target audience, multimedia type, and key findings related to quality indicators. This process was performed by two independent reviewers to ensure the accuracy and consistency of the extracted data. While a scoping review does not mandate quality appraisal, the reviewers assessed the relevance of each study to the research question to ensure a robust mapping of the evidence. Ultimately, 35 relevant articles were analyzed. A content analysis approach was employed to categorize the findings into themes, subthemes, and codes. This synthesis provided a comprehensive overview of quality indicators, which served as the preliminary framework and guiding foundation for assessing health education and promotion multimedia. To further refine and complete this framework based on practical expertise, these findings were subsequently integrated with the results of the qualitative phase (expert interviews) in the next step of the study.

Step 2: Qualitative study

The next step in the research involved conducting interviews with experts to incorporate further items and finalize the item pool. For the qualitative phase of this study, semi-structured individual interviews were conducted with a diverse group of 22 participants. This group was purposefully selected to include 9 media specialists, 7 health education specialists, and 6 communication specialists, ensuring a broad range of perspectives.

Data collection continued until saturation was achieved, indicating that no new substantive information was emerging from subsequent interviews. Participants were chosen based on their established expertise in media and multimedia production, health communication and education strategies, and demonstrated skills in the design and evaluation of audio-visual media and educational multimedia, as well as their experience in utilizing multimedia for educational purposes.

At the beginning of each interview, the researcher clearly outlined the study’s objectives, which focused on identifying essential indicators that contribute to the enhanced quality of educational multimedia and its subsequent influence on audience behavior within the health context. The interview guide was informed by the specific gaps identified during the scoping review phase concerning quality indicators for health education multimedia. To ensure content validity and comprehensiveness, these findings were integrated with a targeted review of specialized literature. Subsequently, the interview questions were refined through iterative consultations with the research team.

The interviews commenced with a broad, open-ended question: “What standard and quality features should educational media within the health field possess to maximize its impact on audience behavior?” Following this initial question, subsequent inquiries were dynamically formulated based on the participants’ immediate responses, employing a co-construction approach. This iterative technique involved developing follow-up questions that directly stemmed from the concepts and themes emerging in the participants’ initial answers, thereby shaping the semi-structured nature of the interview process.

The specific topics addressed in the interview questions centered on identifying key indicators and quality standards for educational multimedia in the health domain. These included inquiries regarding the criteria considered during the design and evaluation phases of educational multimedia development, as well as the essential characteristics that each component of educational multimedia—namely sound, text, and image—should possess to ensure effectiveness. Throughout the interviews, additional probing questions were introduced as needed to ensure comprehensive exploration of the research objectives. Participants were consistently encouraged to elaborate on their answers and to provide specific examples to facilitate a deeper and more nuanced understanding of their perspectives. The duration of the interviews ranged from 45 to 60 min, with an average length of approximately 50 min. To best accommodate the schedules of the participants, the majority of interviews were conveniently conducted at their respective workplaces. However, for a few experts with scheduling constraints or geographical distance, online interview sessions were successfully arranged.

Data analysis and rigor

Data analysis was performed concurrently with data collection using the conventional content analysis approach28. All interviews were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using MAXQDA (version 20). The coding process was primarily conducted by the first author, who followed an inductive approach by reading the transcripts multiple times to achieve immersion and identify primary meaning units. Through open coding, initial codes were extracted and subsequently organized into sub-categories and main categories. To ensure the objectivity of the findings and minimize individual bias, the emerging codes and categories were independently reviewed and verified by the research team through regular consensus-building and peer-debriefing sessions.

To ensure the trustworthiness of the qualitative findings, Guba and Lincoln’s criteria30 were strictly followed. Credibility was established through member checking, where a summary of the extracted codes was shared with a subset of participants to verify the accuracy of the interpretations. Dependability and confirmability were addressed through peer debriefing and maintaining a detailed audit trail of all analytical decisions. Furthermore, maximum variation sampling was employed to enhance the transferability of the results by including experts from diverse professional backgrounds.

Step 3: Incorporation of steps 1 and 2

In the third phase, the extracted indicators from both the scoping review and the qualitative study were integrated to form a comprehensive item pool. To ensure transparency, a side-by-side comparison table was first developed to map the findings from the scoping review against the themes emerged from the interviews.

The process of refining the item pool was guided by two specific criteria:1 Conceptual Integration: redundant or overlapping items from both phases were merged into single, comprehensive statements to ensure thematic unity; and2 Objectivity and Measurability: items that could not be objectively assessed by an evaluator or were based on unpredictable audience post-viewing behaviors (e.g., “The audience shares the multimedia with others”) were eliminated. This ensured that the final tool focuses strictly on observable quality indicators of the multimedia content itself.

Following the removal of duplicates and the merging of similar items, the preliminary instrument comprised 131 items. These items were then subjected to a formal expert panel review (the research team), where each item’s wording was refined to ensure it was declarative and measurable. This process led to the removal or modification of 30 additional items, resulting in a refined questionnaire of 101 items. These items were organized into thirteen main categories: text quality, image quality, sound quality, layout, attractiveness, artistic and technical features, audience analysis, title characteristics, communicativeness, interactivity, focus on behavior, utility, and credibility. Each item utilized a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “completely adhered to” to “not adhered to at all ,” and these items were further examined for their psychometric properties in the subsequent phase.

Phase 2: Preliminary quantitative phase (item reduction and psychometric evaluation)

Following the qualitative phase, the preliminary quantitative phase commenced with the assessment of content and face validity. Although cognitive interviews are a common pre-testing method, we utilized an expert-based content validity process (CVR and CVI) combined with qualitative feedback from the experts. This approach was chosen because it allows for a rigorous, systematic quantification of item relevance and clarity by specialists who are deeply familiar with both health education and multimedia technical standards, ensuring the tool’s professional accuracy before broader application.

To ensure the instrument’s face and content validity, a multidisciplinary panel of ten experts was recruited, consisting of five specialists in health education and promotion and five experts in educational media and multimedia production. The inclusion criteria for the panelists were:1 holding a Master’s or PhD degree in a relevant discipline;2 possessing a minimum of five years of professional experience in the design or evaluation of educational materials; and3 having a documented research track record in health promotion or multimedia development.

Face validity

To establish how relevant the questionnaire appeared, ten specialists with expertise in health education and media evaluated its components and how they connected. This qualitative assessment of face validity involved examining the clarity and suitability of individual questions, considering how easy they were to answer, their readability, grammar, and writing style regarding potential ambiguity, difficulty level, and overall appropriateness. The research team reviewed the wording of the questions and integrated the experts’ feedback. For a quantitative measure of face validity, the item impact technique was employed. Here, participants rated each question on a five-point Likert scale, from five (very important) to one (not at all important). The impact score was determined by multiplying the frequency of responses by the importance rating (Impact Score = Frequency (%) × Importance). A score exceeding 1.5 was deemed satisfactory31. Consequently, no questions were eliminated at this point.

Content validity

Content validity was assessed both qualitatively and quantitatively. Initially, a panel of ten experts in health education and media reviewed the items for grammar, clarity, and appropriateness, leading to minor rephrasing to eliminate ambiguity. Quantitatively, the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) and Content Validity Index (CVI) were employed. Following Lawshe’s method32, experts categorized items as ‘essential,’ ‘useful,’ or ‘not necessary,’ with a minimum threshold of 0.6 for retention. For the CVI, experts rated each item on relevance, clarity, and simplicity using a 4-point Likert scale33; items scoring above 0.79 were considered acceptable34. Additionally, to account for chance agreement, Cohen’s Kappa (κ)* was calculated based on Polit’s recommendations. Only items demonstrating a kappa value of 0.74 or higher (interpreted as excellent agreement) were retained in the final tool.

Construct validity

The instrument follows a hierarchical formative–reflective structure35. At the lower level, items within each of the ten dimensions (e.g., text, image, and sound quality) are reflective, meaning they are conceptually inter-related indicators of that specific dimension. However, at the higher level, the overall ‘multimedia quality’ is a formative construct; it is ‘formed’ by these ten independent dimensions rather than being an effect of them. For example, while ‘high-quality audio’ and ‘clear text’ both contribute to the total quality score, they are independent; a video can have excellent audio but poor text quality, yet both are essential for the final assessment. Consequently, traditional global factor analysis was precluded, as it could lead to the exclusion of theoretically vital but independent dimensions. Instead, construct validity was established through content and domain exhaustiveness, ensured by a scoping review and qualitative interviews with 22 experts until theoretical saturation was reached.

Tool reliability

Reliability was evaluated through two complementary approaches: internal consistency and inter-rater reliability (IRR). Internal consistency was assessed at the dimensional level using McDonald’s Omega36–38, as items within each dimension are reflective and conceptually related. While an overall omega was computed for descriptive purposes, it was not used as a global reliability index due to the tool’s formative structure.

Furthermore, internal reliability was confirmed through a rigorous review by 15 experts in health and media. For external reliability, the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) served as the primary indicator at the formative (global) level, reflecting agreement between independent raters. Following the guidelines of Koo and Li (2016), a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement was employed39. To ensure technical saturation, two independent raters evaluated five multimedia-compliant health video clips, purposefully selected to cover diverse formats (e.g., animation and real-life footage) and all 64 items of the tool. This sampling approach effectively managed rater fatigue while maintaining high measurement precision.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shahid Sadoughi University of Medical Sciences, Yazd, Iran (Ethical Code: IR.SSU.SPH.REC.1402.047). For the qualitative phase involving expert interviews, verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants and was specifically approved by the Ethics Committee. This consent covered both participation in the study and the audio recording of the interviews. All participants were informed about the study’s objectives, the confidentiality of their data, and their right to withdraw at any stage.

Results

Qualitative phase (Item generation)

In the first phase of the study, which included a scoping review and qualitative interviews, we extracted the primary codes, sub-themes, and categories. To maintain clarity and comply with word count limitations, the detailed results of both sections are provided in Supplementary Information (Tables S2 and S3). These findings directly informed the initial item pool, ensuring a transparent ‘audit trail’ from raw data to the operationalized instrument. Based on this foundation, the results of each phase are summarized below:

Step 1: Scoping review: A total of 8,681 records were initially retrieved from PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus. After removing 3,075 duplicates, 5,606 titles were screened, of which 5,265 were excluded. Of the remaining 341 abstracts, 231 unrelated records were removed, leaving 110 articles for full-text assessment. Following this review, 80 papers were excluded due to lack of accessibility or failure to meet inclusion criteria. By incorporating five additional studies identified through reference screening and Google Scholar, a final sample of 35 relevant articles was included for analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1).

A comprehensive qualitative content analysis of these 35 articles yielded 105 codes, 15 sub-themes, and three primary themes: content quality, design quality, and effectiveness in evaluating the quality of health education multimedia. Content quality encompassed the sub-themes of clarity, credibility, realism, and cultural appropriateness. Design quality focused on aspects such as audio and image quality, layout, aesthetic appeal, highlighting, and the generation of positive excitement. Effectiveness factors included the sub-themes of encouragement of participation, behavior-focused approach, time management, utility, and user-friendliness of the media. (Detailed evidence is presented in Supplementary Table S2).

Step 2: Qualitative study: This qualitative study utilized conventional content analysis to explore key indicators in the design and evaluation of health education multimedia. A purposive sampling strategy was employed to recruit 22 experts from the fields of health education, media, and communication.

The findings from this qualitative investigation and expert interviews identified 109 codes, which were subsequently organized into four primary categories and 14 associated sub-themes relevant to the design and evaluation of health education multimedia. Specifically, the Content Quality category encompassed the sub-themes of text quality, image quality, and audio quality, while the Aesthetics domain included layout, attractiveness, artistic-technical features, and user-friendliness. Furthermore, Communication Factors comprised audience analysis, title features, character-audience connection, and educational strategies. Finally, Effectiveness Factors were defined by the sub-themes of utility, emotional impact, and credibility. (The complete coding framework is available in Supplementary Table S3).

Step 3: Incorporation of steps 1 and 2: Following the systematic integration of findings from the scoping review and qualitative interviews, the research team held several consensus meetings to refine the initial item pool. Applying the predefined criteria of “Conceptual Integration” and “Objectivity/Measurability” (as detailed in the Methods section), redundant items were removed and similar concepts were merged. This rigorous refinement process reduced the preliminary 131-item list to a final pool of 101 items, categorized into thirteen main domains. These items, designed to be strictly observable and declarative, were subsequently subjected to the psychometric evaluation phase.

Results of the second phase of the study

Face and content validities

During the qualitative face validity assessment, eight items were revised to correct minor spelling errors, and no items were removed. Subsequently, following the calculation of the impact score, 101 items demonstrating an impact score of 1.5 or higher were deemed suitable and retained for further analysis in the quantitative face validity stage.

To assess qualitative content validity, some items were rephrased by adding, reducing, or replacing certain words with more common and understandable terms, which clarified ambiguous items. Items with similar meanings were removed, and some were merged due to overlap. Based on the feedback from 10 experts, 8 items were integrated into others. In the quantitative content validity stage, 28 items with a CVR lower than 0.6 were removed. Subsequently, the content validity index (CVI) for relevance, clarity, and simplicity was calculated. Only one item, with a relevance CVI of 0.7 and a Kappa coefficient of 0.4, was removed. Following these systematic exclusions based on expert consensus, the initial pool was refined to a final set of 64 items across 10 domains (see Table 1 and its accompanying Note for details). Based on the mean CVI scores of all items, the overall CVI for the instrument was estimated at 0.93 for relevance, 0.92 for clarity, and 0.90 for simplicity, with 0.9 being the acceptable standard34.

Tool reliability results

The internal consistency of the individual dimensions of the Multimedia Quality Assessment Tool was satisfactory, with McDonald’s omega values for all ten dimensions exceeding the commonly accepted threshold, indicating acceptable intra-dimensional coherence. For completeness, the overall McDonald’s omega for the full instrument was 0.95; however, given the formative nature of the higher-order construct, this value was not interpreted as evidence of overall internal consistency.

Inter-rater reliability was assessed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC). The overall ICC for the instrument was 0.86, indicating high agreement between raters. This result provides primary evidence for the reliability of the instrument at the global (formative) level (Table 2).

The final tool

The final instrument is a 64-item, professional-level tool specifically designed for the evaluation of non-interactive health education multimedia, such as educational videos and animations. It is structured for use by professional evaluators (specialists in health education and media) within quality assurance and systematic material review contexts. The tool is organized into ten primary domains: text quality, image quality, sound quality, layout, artistic and technical features, audience analysis, communicability, interactivity, focus on behavior, and credibility. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “fully adhered”4 to “not adhered” (0).The scoring system and applicability thresholds were established based on the consensus of experts in the fields of health and media.

The cutoff point of the tool

To standardize the interpretation of results from the 64-item, 5-point Likert scale questionnaire (scoring 0 to 4), the raw total score was converted into a Quality Percentage. This percentage reflects the multimedia’s conformity to the maximum possible quality standard based only on the items that were applicable (not “N/A”). For classification, this percentage score range was divided into three distinct qualitative categories using a mathematical logic approach of equal interval division: Low Quality (cut-off: 0% to 33%) indicates substantial weaknesses and necessity for major revisions; Medium Quality (cut-off: 34% to 66%) suggests acceptable quality with areas for targeted improvement; and High Quality (cut-off: 67% to 100%) signifies excellent assessment, indicating the multimedia effectively meets quality standards across content, design, and technical features.

Discussion

The presented research aimed to design and validate a comprehensive instrument for assessing the quality of multimedia in health education and promotion. The psychometric results, particularly the strong inter-rater agreement and the satisfactory internal consistency observed at the dimensional level, attest to the robustness and practical utility of this tool. Not only does it address a critical gap in the research literature, but it also demonstrates significant structural and methodological advantages compared to existing instruments.

While established tools like the Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool (PEMAT)40 and DISCERN41, as confirmed by existing reviews42,43, often overlook technical characteristics such as audio-visual quality and layout aesthetics, our instrument addresses this gap by recognizing that effective health communication emerges from the synergy between technical, educational, and aesthetic features. By anchoring the tool in the Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (CTML)27, we posit that incorporating technical features—such as auditory clarity and visual signaling—is a cognitive necessity for managing mental load rather than a mere aesthetic choice. Unlike the Audiovisual Production Assessment Scale (APAS)23, which is primarily bound to specific behavioral modeling, our instrument provides a holistic communication-centric framework situating it within the broader literature of Health Communication Quality28.This shift is operationalized through a Formative Construct structure (35),where dimensions such as audio quality and interactivity are viewed as constitutive causes—rather than effects—of the overall quality. This aligns with a systems approach where the ‘medium’ is inseparable from the ‘message’27, ensuring that all essential dimensions of the multimedia environment are represented through domain exhaustiveness and rigorous content validity.

Another key distinction lies in the rigorous, hybrid validation process. Unlike studies that rely solely on expert consensus44 or theoretical extraction45, we integrated specialized literature with expert consultation to ensure both operational feasibility and technical robustness. A significant competitive advantage of this study is its dual-stage psychometric filtration. By applying more stringent quantitative cut-off criteria for content validity compared to tools like PEMAT40 and providing greater methodological transparency than instruments such as PEVAT46, we ensured the elimination of low-quality items. This high-precision approach enhances the tool’s reliability, offering a more robust framework for assessing multimedia quality in health promotion28.

With respect to reliability, satisfactory internal consistency was observed at the dimensional level, consistent with the reflective nature of the items within each domain. For completeness, a high overall McDonald’s omega was also obtained; however, given the formative nature of the higher-order construct, this value was interpreted with caution and not considered primary evidence of overall reliability. More importantly, strong inter-rater agreement was achieved, as indicated by a high Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC). This level of agreement reflects the clarity and objectivity of the indicators and the inclusion of explicit technical dimensions, enabling consistent application of the instrument by independent evaluators. Notably, the observed inter-rater agreement exceeds that reported for existing assessment tools, highlighting the practical robustness and superior precision of the present instrument in ensuring objective quality evaluations23,40.

Finally, several methodological considerations should be noted. This instrument was specifically engineered to establish a rigorous, professional benchmark for assessing non-interactive health education multimedia, such as educational videos and animations. By focusing on professional contexts—including expert-led quality assurance and systematic program reviews—the tool provides a level of technical and pedagogical scrutiny that may not directly generalize to interactive platforms like websites or applications. The 64-item length was a deliberate design choice to ensure high diagnostic granularity across ten specialized domains. This comprehensive structure is optimized for expert evaluators rather than lay audiences, acting as a stringent quality filter before public dissemination. While the observed intra-dimensional precision supports the instrument’s coherence, it also provides a foundation for developing a short-form version in future studies using Item Response Theory (IRT) to enhance efficiency. Lastly, as the current version prioritizes professional standards, future research should explore integrating these expert-derived benchmarks with end-user perspectives to ensure that educational materials are not only technically sound but also audience-resonant.

Conclusion

Overall, the developed tool provides a comprehensive framework for evaluating non-interactive health education multimedia. It functions as a structured benchmark for professional quality assurance and systematic material review. By ensuring technical and pedagogical rigor during the production phase, the instrument acts as a stringent quality filter. Future integration with end-user perspectives may further enhance its applicability and real-world relevance. Ultimately, the systematic evaluation of multimedia through such specialized tools represents an essential step toward advancing public health and strengthening health literacy through the delivery of reliable, high-quality educational content.
