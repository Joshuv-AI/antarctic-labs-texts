Angiotensin II Receptor Signaling in the Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal Axis and Spleen After Spinal Cord Injury Depends on the Sympathetic Innervation Integrity
Authors: Erika Kellerova, Jana Snopkova, Erika Hvozdikova, Jaroslav Pavel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpinal cord injury (SCI) disrupts the integrated neuroendocrine network linking the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, and sympathetic nervous system, resulting in endocrine and immune dysfunction. However, the effects of SCI on angiotensin II receptor signaling remain unclear. This study investigated temporal and lesion-dependent changes in angiotensin II type 1 (AT1) and type 2 (AT2) receptor expression following SCI in rats. Low-thoracic SCI (Th9 compression, 40 g/15 min) was evaluated over 28 days, while acute responses to partial (Th9) versus near-complete (Th1) sympathoadrenal denervation were compared. Receptor expression was assessed in the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus, pituitary, adrenal gland, and spleen, and correlated with hormonal and morphological alterations. Surgical stress alone activated systemic angiotensin II signaling. Low-thoracic SCI induced time- and tissue-specific changes in angiotensin receptor expression independent of circulating angiotensin II levels, indicating adaptive remodeling of HPA axis sensitivity. SCI caused transient adrenal enlargement, zona fasciculata hypertrophy, dysregulated HPA axis activity, angiotensin II-independent aldosterone production, and altered catecholamine secretion. Splenic AT1 receptor expression showed early upregulation followed by compensatory downregulation, consistent with dynamic neuroimmune modulation. In contrast, high-thoracic SCI markedly reduced receptor expression across central and peripheral HPA axis components and the spleen, accompanied by decreased corticotropin-releasing hormone, aldosterone, and norepinephrine, stable adrenocorticotropic hormone, and elevated corticosterone and epinephrine. These findings demonstrate that the extent of sympathetic disruption critically determines neuroendocrine–immune responses after SCI and identify sympathetic innervation as a key regulator of angiotensin receptor balance and stress-axis homeostasis following spinal cord trauma.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Angiotensin II, AT1 receptor, AT2 receptor, Spinal cord injury, HPA axis, Sympathoadrenal innervation

Introduction

The function of adrenal gland is regulated by a tightly controlled neuroendocrine system involving the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), nervous system inputs, and local molecular pathways that together coordinate hormone production to maintain homeostasis, respond to stress, and regulate metabolism, blood pressure, and immune response (Herman et al. 2016; Charmandari et al. 2005; Correa et al. 2022). The sympathoadrenal system is a fundamental component of the rapid stress response, integrating the sympathetic nervous system with adrenal medullary function to coordinate a “fight-or-flight” reaction. Preganglionic sympathetic neurons innervating the adrenal gland in the rat are primarily located in the intermediolateral horn of the spinal cord, extending roughly from Th1 to L1 segments (Kesse et al. 1988). However, traumatic spinal cord injury (SCI) can significantly disrupt the descending autonomic innervation of the adrenal gland, profoundly impacting hormonal output and consequently immune function. The degree and type of autonomic dysfunction depend on the spinal level and completeness of the spinal cord lesion (Hou and Rabchevsky 2014). Thus, the finely tuned hormonal and immune balance regulated by the sympathoadrenal system after spinal cord trauma is significantly weakened.

Angiotensin II, a key peptide hormone of the RAAS, best known for its role in regulating blood pressure and fluid balance, also plays a fundamental role in the neuroendocrine stress response through modulating the HPA axis. There is evidence for a bidirectional relationship between angiotensin II and the HPA axis, especially under pathological conditions (Kakehi et al. 2023). Although both major angiotensin II receptors have been reported, predominantly AT1 receptors are present throughout the HPA axis, including the hypothalamus (notably the paraventricular nucleus), anterior pituitary, and adrenal cortex (Leong et al. 2002). Through activation of AT1, angiotensin II stimulates corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) secretion (Saavedra 2012), enhances adrenal glucocorticoid synthesis (Carey 2017; Saavedra and Armando 2018), and amplifies sympathetic outflow mechanisms (Feng et al. 2020) that collectively can contribute to immunosuppression and neuroimmune dysregulation following SCI (Lucin et al. 2007). The AT2 receptor is highly expressed in the adrenal medulla (Saavedra and Armando 2018), where it can influence catecholamine secretion in response to angiotensin II (Harada et al. 2010). The presence of the AT2 receptor mRNA in other HPA axis tissues was detected, but their functional role in modulating the axis is less prominent. SCI can disrupt the balance of the RAAS, favoring a more proinflammatory profile and increasing the activity of the angiotensin II/AT1 receptor axis (Zhang et al. 2025).

The spleen plays a critical role in the immune response and serves as a significant source of inflammatory cells that can influence spinal cord pathology and systemic immune function after SCI. Angiotensin II is a potent immunomodulator in the spleen, influencing lymphocyte activation, cytokine production, and monocyte mobilization through AT1 receptor signaling (Sánchez-Lemus et al. 2009). The AT1 receptors are predominantly expressed in the red pulp of the spleen, where a large reservoir of monocytes and macrophages resides. Activation of AT1 receptors on splenic monocytes by angiotensin II promotes their recruitment out of the spleen into circulation, contributing to inflammation in distant tissues (Wang et al. 2017). This finding underlies the spleen´s important role in angiotensin II-driven inflammation and immune regulation.

The exact role of RAAS, especially angiotensin II signaling, in post-traumatic autonomic dysregulation remains not fully elucidated. Understanding the detrimental consequences of sympathoadrenal denervation is critical for the management of SCI patients and for preventing life-threatening complications. In our experimental studies, we investigated the kinetics of expression of major angiotensin II receptors within the neuroendocrine HPA axis and spleen during long-term survival, as well as the impact of partial and near-complete adrenal denervation after severe SCI. Observed changes have been correlated with alterations in stress hormone levels.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Animals and Design

Adult female Wistar rats (3 months old; 250–320 g) were housed individually under standard conditions (20–22 °C, 40–60% humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. All procedures complied with Directive 2010/63/EU and were approved by the State Veterinary and Food Administration of the Slovak Republic (approvals 1066/19-221-3 and 3421/2022-220).

The study was conducted in two sequential phases. Phase I assessed angiotensin II type 1 and 2 receptor expression in HPA axis structures and spleen over 28 days following severe SCI at the Th9 segment. The Th9 spinal level was selected for optimal technical feasibility, animal welfare, and clinical relevance (Corleto et al. 2015; Nourbakhsh et al. 2024). Animals were assigned to intact control or SCI groups, with corresponding sham-operated controls (laminectomy only), and evaluated at 1, 3, 7, 14, 21, or 28 days post-injury. Phase II examined the effects of near-complete versus partial sympathoadrenal denervation following SCI. The experimental animals were assigned to intact control, sham-operated control, and SCI at higher (Th1) and lower (Th9) thoracic levels. The survival was limited to 3 days post-injury (dpi) due to compromised animal welfare and high risk of life-threatening complications associated with the Th1 lesion.

Sample size for the primary outcome (AT1/AT2 receptor expression across the HPA axis) was determined based on an expected effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.8), corresponding to six animals per group and providing 80% power to detect group differences at p < 0.05. For secondary outcomes, group sizes were determined based on tissue availability.

Spinal Cord Lesion

All surgical procedures were performed under sterile, minimally invasive conditions. Animals were weighed and anesthetized with isoflurane (1–1.5%; Alivira Laboratorios Karizoo, Spain). Core body temperature was maintained at 37 °C using a rectal probe and a homothermic heating pad (ATC1000, WPU, USA). The surgical area was shaved and disinfected with Betadine (EGIS Pharmaceuticals, Hungary).

A midline incision was made, paraspinal muscles were retracted, and a dorsal laminectomy was performed. The vertebral column was stabilized in a stereotaxic frame, and spinal cord injury (SCI) was induced by compression as previously described (Fedorova et al. 2022; Fedorova and Pavel 2019; Hamamoto et al. 2007; Van Gorp et al. 2013; Yu et al. 2013). A 40-g weight was applied for 15 min at Th9 or Th1 using a custom-made ABS-M30i impactor (uPrint SE, Stratasys, USA). Sham-operated animals underwent a laminectomy only. Wounds were closed with sterile silk (USP 4/0, Chirana, CR), and the animals were provided with postoperative care: Amoksiklav (12.5 mg/kg, s.c., every 24 h for 3 days) (Sandoz Pharmaceutical, Germany), Novasul (500 mg/kg for 24 h, i.m.) (RichterPharma, Austria), sterile saline solution (5 mL, s.c.) (Bieffe Medital S.P.S., Italy).

SCI resulted in immediate hindlimb paralysis, followed by partial spontaneous recovery, with rapid improvement during the first 14 days and slower progression thereafter (Fedorova et al. 2022). Uncoordinated stepping, characterized by an inability to bear weight and the paw placement primarily on the rear portion of the hind limbs, was observed at the end of long-term survival. Bladder dysfunction occurred after SCI, and manual bladder expression was performed at least twice daily until reflex voiding returned.

Tissue Processing

Tissue processing varied depending on the downstream analysis. For histology, animals were deeply anesthetized with an intraperitoneal overdose of Exagon (RichterPharma, Austria) and transcardially perfused with saline (pH 7.9), followed by 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). Adrenal glands were dissected, post-fixed overnight at 4 °C, and cryoprotected in graded sucrose solutions (20% and 30%) until saturation. For biochemical analyses (angiotensin II receptor binding and ELISA), animals were euthanized by decapitation. The adrenal glands, brain, pituitary, and spleen were rapidly collected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Blood was collected into heparinized tubes, immediately centrifuged, and plasma was stored under identical conditions.

Histological Analysis and Stereological Volume Estimation

Adrenal gland volume and volumes of individual cortical zones and medulla were estimated using the Cavalieri stereological method (Gundersen and Jensen 1987). Cryoprotected glands were embedded in M-1 matrix, cryosectioned at 25 μm, and mounted on SuperFrost Plus slides. Sections were selected by systematic random sampling (every sixth section; 150 μm interval), yielding 18–21 sections per gland, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin using a standard protocol. Slides were digitized at 20× magnification (Aperio AT2, Leica Biosystems).

Angiotensin II Receptor Binding

Angiotensin II receptor binding was performed as previously described (Tsutsumi and Saavedra 1991). Tissue samples were cryosectioned at 16 μm, thaw-mounted on SuperFrost Plus slides, dried, and stored at − 80 °C until use. Sections were preincubated for 15 min at 22 °C in sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) containing NaCl, MgCl₂, EDTA, bacitracin, and protease-free bovine serum albumin, followed by a 120-min incubation with 0.5 nM [¹²⁵I]-Sar¹-Ile⁸-Angiotensin II (2176 Ci/mmol). Total and nonspecific binding were determined in the absence or presence of 5 µM unlabeled angiotensin II. Selective AT₁ and AT₂ receptor binding was assessed using 10 µM losartan or 10 µM PD123319, respectively, and calculated as the binding displaced by each antagonist. Sections were washed in ice-cold Tris-HCl buffer, briefly rinsed in distilled water, air-dried, and exposed to Kodak Biomax MR film together with ¹⁴C microscales. Films were developed using standard procedures.

ELISA

Angiotensin II was analyzed using an enzyme immunoassay kit (EIA) (RAB0010-1KT, Merck KGaA, Germany) from blood plasma according to the manufacturer´s protocol. Results were calculated using four-parameter logistic regression in SigmaPlot. Plasma samples were also used for the determination of adrenocorticotropic hormone (MBS2022156, MyBioSource, Inc., CA, USA), aldosterone (MBS731388, MyBioSource, Inc., CA, USA), and epinephrine (MBS732836, MyBioSource, Inc., CA, USA) by quantitative competitive assay, while quantitative sandwich ELISA was used for the analysis of corticotropine releasing hormone (MBS2508462, MyBioSource, Inc., CA, USA) and norepinephrine (MBS164034, MyBioSource Inc., CA, USA) according to the attached manual. Adrenal gland tissue homogenate was used for analysis of corticosterone (MBS163556, MyBioSource, Inc., CA, USA) according to the manufacturer´s instructions. Determination was based on a four-parameter logistic curve, and calculations were performed using MyAssays software. Processing of the reference sample served as quality control.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.01 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). One-way ANOVA followed by Sidak’s multiple comparisons test was used to compare individual experimental groups with intact controls at each time point. Two-way ANOVA was used for comparisons between SCI and sham-operated control groups across time, followed by Sidak’s multiple comparisons test. Exact p-values are reported, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Morphological Changes in the Adrenal Gland Following Lower-Thoracic Injury

Severe SCI induced transient changes in adrenal gland morphology, characterized by reduced color intensity at 3–7 dpi, which normalized by day 14 onwards (Fig. 1A). Quantitative analysis revealed an enlargement of the adrenal gland during the first 7 dpi, returning to control values thereafter (Fig. 1B), which was paralleled by increased adrenal weight at 1 (p = 0.0320), 3 (p = 0.0018), and 7 dpi (p = 0.0066) (Fig. 1C) (one-way ANOVA: F(6,60) = 4.772, p = 0.0005). No differences were observed between the intact and sham-operated controls (one-way ANOVA: F(6,61) = 0.5190, p = 0.7917), indicating that the surgical procedure alone did not account for these changes. Morphometric analysis identified hypertrophy of the zona fasciculata as the primary contributor (Fig. 2), with significant increases at 1 (p = 0.0066), 3 (p < 0.0001), and 7 dpi (p = 0.0020) (one-way ANOVA: F(6,37) = 10.53, p < 0.0001).

Post-traumatic Circulating Angiotensin II and Its Receptor Expression Within the HPA Axis

Circulating angiotensin II, the endogenous ligand for AT1 and AT2 receptors, was measured over 28 days in SCI and sham-operated rats (Fig. 3). Levels increased significantly in both SCI (one-way ANOVA: F(6,33) = 7.614, p < 0.0001) and sham-operated groups (one-way ANOVA: F(6,33) = 5.489, p = 0.0005), with elevations at 1 (SCI: p = 0.0008; sham: p = 0.0085) and 3 dpi (SCI: p < 0.0001; sham: p = 0.0005) compared with intact controls. No differences were observed between SCI and sham groups, indicating that the surgical procedure itself drives the transient increase in circulating angiotensin II.

AT1 receptors in the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus are primarily located on corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) neurons of the parvicellular subdivision, indicating a potential direct influence on the HPA axis regulation (Aguilera et al. 1995; Wang et al. 2016). SCI induced a significant increase in receptor expression at 3 dpi (p < 0.0001) compared with intact controls (Fig. 4A) (one-way ANOVA: F(6,48) = 9.607, p < 0.0001). Two-way ANOVA confirmed significant effects of time (F(5,79) = 8.140, p < 0.0001), group (F(1,79) = 19.58, p < 0.0001), and interaction (F(5,79) = 5.858, p = 0.0001), with differences between SCI and sham-operated groups at 1 dpi (p = 0.0047) and 3 dpi (p < 0.0001). No differences were observed between sham-operated and intact controls (F(6,45) = 1.534, p = 0.1891).

In the pituitary gland, AT1 receptors are predominantly expressed in the anterior lobe (adenohypophysis), particularly on adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)-producing corticotrophs (Coiro et al. 1998). In sham-operated control groups, receptor expression remained within the range of intact controls, except for an initial increase at 1 day (p = 0.0247; one-way ANOVA: F(6,31) = 3.998, p = 0.0045) (Fig. 4B), indicating an acute surgical effect. Following SCI, receptor expression normalized at 1 dpi, increased at 3 dpi (p = 0.0001; one-way ANOVA: F(6,30) = 7.98, p < 0.0001), and subsequently declined below control levels. Two-way ANOVA revealed significant effects of time (F(5,48) = 4.355, p = 0.0024) and interaction (F(5,48) = 6.777, p < 0.0001), but not group (F(1,48) = 3.301, p = 0.0755), with a difference between SCI and sham groups observed only at 3 dpi (p < 0.0001).

Consistent with previous findings (Belloni et al. 1998), autoradiography showed that AT1 receptor expression in the adrenal gland is confined almost to the zona glomerulosa (Fig. 4C). A time-dependent, sigmoidal pattern of receptor expression was observed following both surgery and SCI. In sham-operated controls, receptor levels remained unchanged up to 3 dpi, increased at 7 and 14 dpi (p < 0.0001 and p = 0.0009; one-way ANOVA: F(6,48) = 29,75, p < 0.0001), and then gradually returned toward baseline. Following SCI, receptor expression decreased at 1 dpi (p = 0.0063), increased at 7 dpi (p = 0.0001), and remained elevated at 14 dpi (p = 0.0348) (one-way ANOVA: F(6,52) = 11.36, p < 0.0001). Two-way ANOVA revealed significant effects of time (F(5,75) = 34.22, p < 0.0001), group (F(1,75) = 9.521, p = 0.0028), and their interaction (F(5,75) = 2.565, p = 0.0338), with a difference between SCI and sham groups at 7 dpi (p = 0.0011).

Angiotensin II receptor-binding assay showed that AT2 receptors are scarce in the zona glomerulosa but highly expressed in adrenal medulla chromaffin cells, where they regulate catecholamine secretion (Wang et al. 2002). In both SCI and sham-operated animals, AT2 receptor expression was generally upregulated over time, except at 1 and 21 dpi (Fig. 4D). One-way ANOVA confirmed significant changes in SCI (F(6,54) = 12.29, p < 0.0001) and sham groups (F(6,54) = 7.789, p < 0.0001), with increases at 1 dpi (p = 0.0244), 7 dpi (p = 0.0043), 14 dpi (p < 0.0001), and 28 dpi (p = 0.0022) in SCI animals, and at 3 dpi (p = 0.0035), 7 dpi (p = 0.0002), 14 dpi (p = 0.0015) and 28 dpi (p < 0.0001) in sham-operated animals. Two-way ANOVA showed significant effects of time (F(5,80) = 12.04, p < 0.0001) and group (F(1,80) = 5.724, p = 0.0191), without interaction (F(5,80) = 1.855, p = 0.1116). SCI animals exhibited reduced AT2 receptor expression compared with shams only at 1 dpi (p = 0.0195).

Neuroendocrine Response to SCI

The HPA axis is tightly regulated under physiological conditions, with activation characterized by increased CRH release from the hypothalamic PVN, followed by elevated ACTH and glucocorticoid secretion. Both surgery and SCI induced a transient increase in CRH at 3 dpi (SCI: p < 0.0001; sham: p = 0.0050) (Fig. 5A), with significant effects in SCI (one-way ANOVA: F(6,33) = 4.890, p = 0.0011) and sham-operated groups (one-way ANOVA: F(6,34) = 4.833, p = 0.0012), and no changes at later time points. Thereafter, CRH levels declined slightly below control values in sham animals and showed mild elevation in SCI animals.

As CRH release is the primary driver of ACTH secretion, plasma ACTH dynamics were expected to follow this pattern. Notably, ACTH levels did not fully reflect hypothalamic CRH changes. ACTH in sham-operated controls was significantly different from that of intact animals (Fig. 5B) (one-way ANOVA: F(6,35) = 5.232, p = 0.0006). Compared with the sham-operated control group, SCI at the Th9 segmental level induced a marked, time-dependent increase in plasma ACTH at 3, 14, 21, and 28 dpi (p = 0.0020, p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001, and p < 0.0001, respectively) (one-way ANOVA: F(6,37) = 22.23, p < 0.0001). Two-way ANOVA showed significant effects of time (F(5,58) = 22.33, p < 0.0001), group (F(1,58) = 105.5, p < 0.0001), and interaction (F(5,58) = 6.241, p = 0.0001). ACTH primarily stimulates the adrenal cortex to secrete glucocorticoids, completing the HPA axis response to stress. Despite elevated ACTH, corticosterone levels remained near control values (Fig. 5C), indicating a disruption of normal adrenal responsiveness following SCI.

Aldosterone is synthesized in the zona glomerulosa, a region with high AT1 receptor expression (Fig. 4C), and its production is primarily regulated by angiotensin II and extracellular potassium (Ekman et al. 2025). While surgery had no effect on aldosterone levels (one-way ANOVA: F(6,38) = 1.204, p = 0.3252), SCI induced a significant increase (one-way ANOVA: F(6,35) = 11.06, p < 0.0001), with elevations at 3, 7, and 14 dpi (p < 0.0001), as well as at 21 and 28 dpi (p = 0.0214 and p = 0.0046) (Fig. 5D). Notably, this increase did not correspond to changes in circulating angiotensin II (Fig. 3) or AT1 receptor expression in zona glomerulosa (Fig. 4C), suggesting the impaired angiotensin II-dependent regulation of aldosterone following partial sympathoadrenal denervation after SCI at the Th9 level.

Catecholamines (norepinephrine and epinephrine) are primarily produced by chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla under the control of acetylcholine released from preganglionic sympathetic nerves (Guérineau 2020). Except for the acute phase (1 dpi), norepinephrine levels were modestly increased in sham-operated controls, with a significant elevation at 21 dpi (one-way ANOVA: F(6,35) = 3.553, p = 0.0075), while SCI had no significant effect (one-way ANOVA: F (6,30) = 1.594, p = 0.1834) (Fig. 5E). In contrast, epinephrine levels were markedly elevated in sham animals up to 14 dpi (one-way ANOVA: F (6,33) = 11.84, p < 0.0001), with increases at 1 dpi (p < 0.0001), 3 dpi (p = 0.0077), and at 7 and 14 dpi (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 5F). This response was significantly attenuated following SCI. Compared to the intact control, its values were significantly elevated in SCI animals at 7 dpi (p = 0.0014), 14 dpi (p = 0.0102), 21 dpi (p = 0.0241), and 28 dpi (p = 0.0046) (one-way ANOVA: F(6,35) = 7.198, p < 0.0001).

Post-traumatic AT1 Receptor Expression in Spleen and Its Weight Alterations

The AT1 receptor expression in the splenic red pulp remained largely unchanged in sham-operated animals, except for a decrease at 28 dpi (p = 0.0453) (one-way ANOVA: F(6,39) = 2.698, p = 0.0274) (Fig. 6A). In contrast, SCI induced significant changes (one-way ANOVA: F(6,49) = 36.29, p < 0.0001), with an increase at 1 dpi (p < 0.0001) and a reduction at 7 dpi (p = 0.0111). Two-way ANOVA showed significant effects of time (F(5,69) = 27.03, p < 0.0001) and interaction (F(5,69) = 10.92, p < 0.0001), with a difference between SCI and sham-operated animals at 1 dpi (p < 0.0001). The sympathetic nervous system regulates not only splenic immune functions but also influences the spleen´s structure and microenvironment, which can be reflected in changes in spleen weight. Spleen weight was normalized per kilogram of body weight and expressed as spleen index (Wang et al. 2021). SCI induced a marked decrease in spleen index at 1 dpi (p < 0.0001), followed by recovery at 7–14 dpi and a subsequent decline below control levels at 28 dpi (p = 0.0064) (Fig. 6B) (one-way ANOVA: F(6,49) = 36.29, p < 0.0001). In contrast, sham-operated animals showed no substantial changes (one-way ANOVA: F(6,50) = 2.344, p = 0.0451). Two-way ANOVA revealed significant effects of time (F(5,99) = 7.364, p < 0.0001), group (F(1,99) = 20.52, p < 0.0001), and interaction (F(5,99) = 2.678, p = 0.0259), with differences between SCI and sham-operated animals at 1 dpi (p = 0.0164) and 28 dpi (p = 0.0004).

AT1 Receptor Expression Following the Spinal Cord Lesion at Higher- vs Lower-Thoracic Level

As mentioned previously, the adrenal glands receive preganglionic sympathetic inputs from the Th1-L1 spinal segments (Kesse et al. 1988). Depending on the spinal level of injury, the compression model of severe SCI determines the degree of adrenal denervation: low-thoracic injuries (e.g., Th9) partially preserve innervation, whereas high-thoracic injuries (e.g., Th1) result in near-complete disconnection. Accordingly, the receptor expression varied with the injury level. Partial denervation (Th9 lesion) caused a marked upregulation of AT1 receptors above sham-control levels in central components of the HPA axis, including the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus and pituitary gland (Fig. 7A, B). AT1 receptor expression increased in the Th9 SCI group (one-way ANOVA: PVN: F(3,25) = 13.07, p < 0.0001; pituitary gland: F(3,20) = 12.35, p < 0.0001), with differences compared with sham-operated (PVN: p = 0.0003; pituitary gland: p = 0.0001) and Th1 SCI groups (PVN: p = 0.0240; pituitary gland: p = 0.0017). No differences were observed in peripheral components of the HPA axis (Fig. 7C, D), or in the red pulp of the spleen (Fig. 7E, one-way ANOVA: zona glomerulosa: F(3,31) = 9.251, p = 0.0002; adrenal medulla: F(3,33) = 14.42, p < 0.0001; spleen: F(3,27) = 9.250, p = 0.0002), with no differences between Th9 SCI and sham-operated groups (all p > 0.05). Near-complete sympathoadrenal denervation (Th1 lesion) resulted in a significant reduction of AT1 receptor expression compared with both the sham-operated group (p < 0.0001) and the Th9 SCI group (p = 0.0080) in the adrenal medulla (Fig. 7D). The AT1 receptors were almost abolished in the zona glomerulosa compared with the intact control (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 7C) and markedly downregulated in the red pulp of the spleen, with differences between the Th1 SCI group and the intact control (p = 0.0003), as well as between the Th1 and Th9 SCI groups (p = 0.0006) (Fig. 7E). Collectively, these results suggest that the expression of angiotensin II receptors in these structures depends on the extent of sympathetic innervation.

Hormonal Changes Following the Spinal Cord Lesion at Higher- vs Lower-Thoracic Level

Hormone concentrations varied depending on the spinal level of injury. One-way ANOVA revealed significant differences in CRH (F(3,24) = 11.24, p < 0.0001) and norepinephrine levels (F(3,24) = 4.811, p = 0.0092); however, no differences were observed between Th9 SCI and sham-operated control groups (Fig. 8A, E). A significant effect was also detected for corticosterone (F(3,24) = 3.859, p = 0.0219), with no differences between Th9 SCI and sham-operated controls (Fig. 8C). In contrast, epinephrine levels were significantly affected (F(3,24) = 25.26, p < 0.0001), with a marked decrease in the Th9 SCI group to values comparable to those of intact controls (Fig. 8F). One-way ANOVA further revealed significant changes in ACTH (F(3,28) = 16.76, p < 0.0001) and aldosterone levels (F(3,27) = 12.07, p < 0.0001). ACTH increased in the Th1 SCI group compared with intact controls (p = 0.0158) and sham-operated animals (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 8B). Similarly, aldosterone levels were significantly elevated, with a difference detected between Th1 and Th9 SCI groups (p = 0.0008) (Fig. 8D). By comparing the level of injury, lower CRH (p = 0.0178), aldosterone (p = 0.0008), and norepinephrine (p = 0.0271) levels were observed in the Th1 SCI group compared with the Th9 SCI group (Fig. 8A, D, E). In contrast, ACTH levels did not differ between Th1 and Th9 groups, while corticosterone (p = 0.0150) and epinephrine (p = 0.0381) were significantly increased following Th1 SCI (Fig. 8B, C, F).

Discussion

The bidirectional interplay between the nervous, endocrine, and immune systems is fundamental for maintaining systemic homeostasis. SCI disrupts this tightly coordinated communication network, resulting in profound neuroendocrine and immune alterations that extend beyond the injury site. The extent of sympathetic disruption caused by SCI critically influences neuroendocrine-immune regulation and functional outcomes. In this study, we demonstrate that the level of SCI, whether resulting in partial (Th9 lesion) or near-complete (Th1 lesion) sympathoadrenal denervation, exerts a localized impact on angiotensin II receptor expression and neuroendocrine responses following trauma. These results highlight the essential role of sympathetic innervation integrity in modulating neuroimmune-endocrine plasticity after injury.

Transient discoloration and enlargement of the adrenal gland within the first post-injury week belong among the most apparent alterations observed following severe SCI at the lower-thoracic level. Observed changes associated with partial denervation likely reflect structural and functional alterations in the adrenal gland due to disrupted sympathetic nervous system inputs. This disruption can result in: (1) altered blood flow or perfusion in the adrenal gland, leading to changes in tissue color (discoloration), (2) modifications in cellular composition, including stress-induced damage or remodeling of adrenal cortex and medulla zones, (3) impaired production or release of critical adrenal hormones needed for stress response and homeostasis. Histological and morphometric analyses revealed that glandular enlargement was associated with hypertrophy of the zona fasciculata, the cortical region primarily responsible for glucocorticoid production (Vinson 2016). Reduced sympathetic innervation may impair normal adrenal hormone regulation, leading to activation of the HPA axis and increased ACTH release, as shown in Fig. 5A, B. ACTH promotes growth and enlargement (hypertrophy) of zona fasciculata cells, thereby enhancing glucocorticoid production to meet the body´s demand under chronic stress (Ulrich-Lai et al. 2006). Thus, hypertrophy, as an adaptive plasticity response, may reflect the adrenal gland´s ongoing attempt to maintain cortisol output despite altered neural and hormonal signals after the injury. This interpretation is supported by our finding showing that adrenal corticosterone levels remained nearly unchanged (Fig. 5C), likely due to compensatory mechanisms centered on ACTH signaling, even though rapid glucocorticoid modulation by sympathetic tone is markedly impaired. Importantly, these adrenal morphological changes resolved by day 14, indicating that such an adrenal response was transient and phase-specific, confined primarily to the early post-injury period.

Angiotensin II, through AT1 receptors, acts directly on CRH-producing neurons in the PVN (Wang et al. 2016) and ACTH-secreting corticotrophs in the pituitary (Coiro et al. 1998) to regulate stress axis output. Although both SCI and sham-operated animals showed an early increase in circulating angiotensin II, no differences between the two groups were observed. In contrast, significant time-dependent alterations in receptor expression profiles within the HPA axis and the spleen were observed in both experimental groups. This indicates that the surgical procedure itself is stressful enough to trigger systemic stress response (via angiotensin II release), and SCI doesn´t further change systemic angiotensin II release beyond that early phase, but it reshapes the sensitivity and the adaptability of the HPA axis by altering the receptor expression in a time- and tissue-specific manner. Our data suggest that SCI increases the central sensitivity of the HPA axis to angiotensin II, not necessarily due to elevated levels of the peptide itself, but rather through enhanced receptor expression, which may amplify neuroendocrine responsivity to stress (Saavedra et al. 2011).

Paradoxically, ACTH levels remained markedly elevated until the end of survival period (Fig. 5B), despite the absence of changes in the AT1 receptor density in the PVN of the hypothalamus and their gradual decline under physiological values in the pituitary gland after significant elevation at the early phase of the injury (Fig. 4A, B) were observed. There are several possible explanations: (1) The PVN, as a central component of HPA axis control, is shielded from direct circulating angiotensin II (Fragas and Davanzo 2021), so its receptor levels do not fluctuate strongly with transient systemic changes in angiotensin II. (2) Early surge of circulating angiotensin II after SCI may overstimulate pituitary AT1 receptors, causing their downregulation/desensitization over time, since the pituitary gland is directly exposed to blood angiotensin II. Meanwhile, PVN can still be indirectly influenced by angiotensin II via the circumventricular organs and local RAS activity (Zhang et al. 2002), but its receptor expression remains relatively stable because it is driven more by local neuroendocrine signaling than by systemic spikes.

The primary mineralocorticoid hormone aldosterone, typically driven by angiotensin II via AT1 receptors in the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex, remained persistently elevated from 3 dpi until the end of survival period. This pattern does not fully correspond to AT1 receptor expression or plasma angiotensin II concentration profile, suggesting that aldosterone secretion is regulated by mechanisms beyond classical angiotensin II-AT1 signaling. In addition to angiotensin II, two other potent regulators of aldosterone biosynthesis are serum potassium levels and ACTH (Bollag 2014). Our findings suggest that persistently increased ACTH during the post-injury period could be responsible for chronic aldosterone elevation. However, this explanation seems unlikely because adrenal glomerulosa cells (both in vivo and in vitro) can increase aldosterone production in response to ACTH only transiently since prolonged ACTH exposure tends to desensitize the glomerulosa cells (Hattangady et al. 2012). Although extracellular potassium and angiotensin II are powerful independent regulators of aldosterone production and secretion (Bollag 2014; Williams 2005), the potency of these regulators of aldosterone secretion depends on their interplay. Angiotensin II is less effective at stimulating sustained aldosterone production at extremely low potassium levels, whereas elevated potassium levels enhance angiotensin II-induced aldosterone synthesis (Chen et al. 1999). Potassium may therefore act as a fine-tuning mechanism for aldosterone production, allowing precise regulation of sodium balance, blood volume, and blood pressure in both acute and chronic settings (Rainey et al. 2009). Further experiments are needed to determine whether chronic aldosterone elevation after SCI is driven by increased potassium, a disrupted interaction between extracellular potassium and angiotensin II, or a combination of neuroendocrine, renal, and cardiovascular factors. Prevention of aldosterone elevation observed after near-complete sympathoadrenal denervation (Th1 lesion, Fig. 8D) suggests that aberrant sympathetic outflow below the lesion, altering adrenal responsiveness even in the absence of elevated angiotensin II, may represent a primary mechanism underlying chronically elevated aldosterone.

The adrenal medulla is the main source of epinephrine (~ 80%) and, to a lesser extent, norepinephrine (~ 20%) released into systemic circulation in response to stressful situations (De Nardi et al. 2017). Enhanced catecholamine synthesis in the adrenal medulla, together with a marked increase in norepinephrine release to the bloodstream, are hallmarks of stress response (Saavedra and Armando 2018). Notably, the pronounced increase in epinephrine levels observed over 14 days in sham-operated animals (Fig. 5F) corresponds well with the time of adrenal gland discoloration following SCI at the lower-thoracic level (Fig. 1A). This suggests that descending supraspinal control of the adrenal medulla is essential for the physiological increase in epinephrine secretion in response to surgical stress. Under normal conditions, the adrenal gland appears reddish-brown due to its high vascularization and catecholamine content (especially epinephrine). Our findings demonstrate that impaired epinephrine synthesis in chromaffin cells may be another factor contributing to the transient post-traumatic discoloration of the adrenal gland. Quantitative measurement of the receptor binding sites revealed no impact of partial sympathoadrenal denervation on the AT2 receptor expression in adrenal medulla (Fig. 4D). It has been shown that AT1 receptors play the dominant role in angiotensin II-induced epinephrine release from adrenal medulla, whereas AT2 receptors contribute more modestly by modulating catecholamine synthesis and local release (Hano et al. 1994; Mazzocchi et al. 1998). As previously described (Israel et al. 1995), angiotensin II receptor binding assay revealed very high expression of AT2 receptors (~ 95%) and sparse expression of AT1 receptors (~ 5%) in the adrenal medulla. Although this method has advantages, it also has limitations. In particular, due to its sensitivity limitations for detecting low-density receptor populations, we could not reliably assess changes in AT1 receptor expression.

The spleen serves as a critical hub for pathogen detection and initiation of immune responses, coordinating both rapid innate defenses and adaptive immunity, while also maintaining immunoregulatory functions through local and neural pathways (Noble et al. 2018). In rats, splenic autonomic innervation arises primarily from the Th3 to Th12 spinal segments (Cano et al. 2001). The SCI disrupts these autonomic pathways in a level-dependent manner, leading to immune dysfunction. Angiotensin II regulates splenic immune activity primarily via AT1 receptor-mediated effects on T cells, monocytes, and macrophages, affecting cell activation, cytokine production, trafficking, and possibly spleen hemodynamics and volume (Tsutsumi et al. 1992; Wang et al. 2017). Following low-thoracic (Th9) injury, an acute increase in splenic AT1 receptor expression at 1 dpi likely reflects an immediate pro-inflammatory and immune-activating response driven by elevated angiotensin II levels. The subsequent reduction below baseline may represent a compensatory mechanism aimed at preventing prolonged or excessive inflammation, thereby protecting against chronic immune overactivation or splenic dysfunction. These findings suggest that splenic autonomic denervation after SCI alters the balance of neuroimmune signaling, modulating the integration of AT1 receptor-mediated angiotensin II activity with sympathetic nervous system inputs. Such adaptation may help limit sympathetic hyperactivation-induced immune suppression after high-thoracic injury but has only modest effects after low-thoracic injury, where sympathetic splenic innervation is less affected. Consistent with this interpretation, markedly reduced AT1 receptor binding in the spleen (Fig. 7E) and elevated corticosterone release (Fig. 8C) observed after high-thoracic injury (Th1 lesion) provide further evidence that the level of injury critically influences splenic neuroimmune regulation. Slight reduction in spleen index after low-thoracic (Th9) injury likely reflects subtle changes in splenocyte distribution or retention rather than overt splenic atrophy, as sympathetic innervation of the spleen remains largely preserved at this injury level.

SCI typically combines severe acute stress with neurogenic disruption of autonomic control. High-thoracic (Th1) lesion causes greater impairment of descending supraspinal regulation of sympathetic preganglionic neurons that innervate the adrenal gland and many abdominal/visceral targets than low-thoracic (Th9) injury (Kesse et al. 1988). Consequently, high-thoracic SCI in the acute phase (3 dpi) elicits distinct neuroendocrine and adrenal responses, reflecting differences in sympathetic denervation patterns, HPA axis feedback, and compensatory receptor-regulation mechanisms.

Upregulation of AT1 receptors in the hypothalamic PVN and pituitary gland is strongly linked with acute stress response, as angiotensin II, acting through AT1 receptors on CRH neurons, facilitates activation of the HPA axis (Aguilera et al. 1995; Leong et al. 2002). Our results showed that a similar receptor expression pattern was observed even after the Th9 lesion, in which much of the descending sympathetic innervation to the adrenal gland and other organs remains intact, although partially disrupted (Kesse et al. 1988). However, extensive sympathetic denervation following a high-level lesion suppressed this upregulation, resulting instead in AT1 receptor downregulation and a blunted HPA axis response. The direction of AT1 regulation (upregulation vs. downregulation) may reflect the balance between systemic stress activation and neurogenic disconnection. Decreased CRH likely reflects strong negative feedback from elevated circulating corticosterone and epinephrine or altered PVN output, while stable ACTH levels suggest preserved pituitary responsiveness - possibly masked by alternative regulatory mechanisms (e.g. inflammatory cytokines, vasopressin) or disrupted feedback pathways (Lucin et al. 2007). The increased corticosterone levels after Th1 injury corroborate previous findings emphasizing the pivotal role of sympathetic-adrenal reflexes in mediating SCI-induced immunodeficiency following high-level SCI (Pruss et al. 2017).

The prevented increase in aldosterone partially corresponds to the drastic downregulation of AT1 receptors in the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex (Fig. 7C), making it almost unresponsive to circulating angiotensin II. It is likely that other potent regulators beyond classical angiotensin II-AT1 signaling play an important role in this context, as aldosterone levels declined only to baseline. These findings clearly indicate that sympathetic innervation is essential for maintaining AT1 receptor expression in the adrenal cortex.

Although the HPA axis primarily regulates the rat adrenal cortex, activation of the adrenal medulla is mainly under direct neural control via the sympathetic nervous system, particularly via preganglionic fibers from the splanchnic nerves, which stimulate chromaffin cells to release catecholamines into the bloodstream (Kesse et al. 1988). Near-complete sympathetic denervation induced by a Th1 lesion resulted in an acute fall in circulating norepinephrine, a biomarker of systemic sympathetic nerve activity rather than adrenal function (Goldstein et al. 1983). Contrary, plasma epinephrine reflects adrenal medullary activity (Goldstein et al. 2003). While plasma epinephrine levels remained preserved after a Th9 lesion, animals with a Th1 lesion exhibited significantly elevated epinephrine, possibly due to spinal reflex activation, local sympathetic overactivity, or dysregulated surges – although levels remained lower than those in sham-operated animals exhibiting a strong generalized stress response. Additionally, systemic inflammatory cytokines can sensitize the adrenal medulla (Bunn et al. 2012).

AT1 receptors in the CNS are thought to mediate sympatho-excitation, whereas AT2 receptor activation inhibits sympathetic tone (Gao and Zucker 2011). Prominent alterations in AT2 receptor expression in adrenal medulla suggest that the stable epinephrine level after partial injury may be maintained by enhanced AT2 receptor-mediated neuroregulatory mechanisms. Conversely, the pronounced increase in epinephrine observed after extensive adrenal denervation likely results from the loss of this AT2 receptor modulation, leading to disinhibited and potentially maladaptive adrenal medullary catecholamine secretion (Jezova et al. 2003).

Conclusions

In summary, our research demonstrates that the integrity of sympathetic innervation is essential for maintaining normal angiotensin II expression across both central (hypothalamic-pituitary) and peripheral (adrenal) components of the HPA axis, as well as within the spleen. Disruption of sympathetic pathways after SCI causes cascading alterations in angiotensin II receptor expression and neuroendocrine regulation that depend on injury level (reflecting degree of sympathetic denervation), post-injury timing (acute vs. chronic phases), and the impairment of interconnected feedback loops between the RAAS, HPA axis, and adrenal gland.

The degree of sympathetic disruption following SCI critically determines the pattern of HPA activation, adrenal responsiveness, and the angiotensin II receptor adaptation, with low-thoracic lesions preserving partial compensatory capacity and high-thoracic lesions causing more profound neuroendocrine failures. These findings enhance our understanding of how SCI disrupts the integrated regulation of the nervous, endocrine, and immune systems, which are essential for maintaining systemic homeostasis. They also underscore the need for individualized therapeutic approaches to manage endocrine and immune complications after spinal trauma, emphasizing the importance of sympathetic integrity and post-injury timing.
