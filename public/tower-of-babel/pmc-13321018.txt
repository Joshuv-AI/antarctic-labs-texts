Return to work and sports after posterior latissimus dorsi transfer for posterior superior irreparable rotator cuff tears
Authors: Chang Hee Baek, Bo Taek Kim, Jung Gon Kim, Chaemoon Lim, Seung Jin Kim, Luis Alfredo Miranda, Jean Kany
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPosterior superior irreparable rotator cuff tears (PSIRCTs) cause substantial functional limitations, particularly in individuals performing physically demanding work or participating in sports. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the rate and timing of return to work (RTW) and return to sports (RTS) following posterior latissimus dorsi (LD) transfer in patients with PSIRCTs and to identify factors associated with successful return. It was hypothesized that patients would demonstrate high RTW and RTS rates.MethodsThis retrospective study included patients who underwent posterior LD transfer for PSIRCTs between April 2012 and June 2020. RTW and RTS rates, timing, and levels of return were assessed using structured questionnaires. Clinical outcomes, including visual analog scale, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons, Constant, and Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation scores, as well as active range of motion (ROM), were evaluated pre-operatively and post-operatively. Multivariable analysis was used to identify factors associated with RTW and RTS. Patients who had undergone revision surgery, were lost to follow-up, or had unavailable clinical or radiological data were excluded.ResultsAfter excluding 15 patients, 83 patients were included (mean age 61.9 ± 5.3 years; mean follow-up 65.3 ± 13.9 months). Pre-operative occupational demand was classified according to the Dictionary of Occupational Titles criteria as light, medium, or heavy work based on physical workload requirements. Overall, 85.5% of patients returned to work, including 56.6% at their previous work level and 28.9% at a reduced level, while 14.4% did not return. In subgroup analysis, patients with lighter workloads demonstrated higher RTW rates (95.2% vs. 92.3% vs. 75.0%) and shorter return times (4.1 ± 1.1 vs. 4.1 ± 1.2 vs. 5.6 ± 1.6 months) compared with those performing heavy labor. The overall RTS rate was 89.7%, with 72.1% achieving complete RTS and 27.8% partial RTS. On multivariable analysis, higher work intensity and tendon retear were significantly associated with RTW, whereas tendon retear was the only significant predictor of RTS.ConclusionPosterior LD transfer for PSIRCTs was associated with high rates of RTW and RTS among patients without major post-operative failure. Higher occupational demand was associated with delayed and less complete RTW, while tendon integrity was associated with both RTW and RTS. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because of retrospective design, self-reported activity outcomes, limited subgroup sizes, and exclusion of patients requiring revision surgery or conversion to reverse shoulder arthroplasty.Keywords: Latissimus dorsi, Posterior superior rotator cuff, Tendon transfer, Irreparable rotator cuff, Rotator cuff tear, Return to sports, Return to work

Posterior superior irreparable rotator cuff tears (PSIRCTs) can lead to significant impairment of shoulder function and limitations in activities of daily living.5,20 These deficits are particularly impactful for individuals engaged in physically demanding occupations or those who participate in recreational or competitive sports. Conservative management often fails to restore sufficient shoulder strength and mobility in such cases, making surgical intervention necessary.21,27 Posterior latissimus dorsi (LD) transfer has been used as one of the surgical options for patients with PSIRCTs, aiming to restore external rotation, enhance shoulder biomechanics, and relieve pain.1,7,13,18,24,26 Previous studies have reported that posterior LD transfer provides satisfactory improvements in shoulder function and patient-reported outcomes.1,3,6,7,12,13,18,22, 23, 24,26 However, most existing literature has primarily focused on pain reduction and functional recovery, with limited investigation into patients' ability to return to work (RTW) and return to sports (RTS).

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the rate and timing of RTW and RTS after posterior LD transfer in patients with PSIRCTs. The secondary objective is to assess post-operative clinical outcomes, including pain, shoulder function, and range of motion (ROM), and to identify factors associated with successful RTW and RTS. It was hypothesized that patients undergoing posterior LD transfer would demonstrate favorable RTW and RTS rates comparable to those previously reported following lower trapezius tendon (LTT) transfer for PSIRCTs, with most patients returning to occupational and sports activities within approximately 4 to 6 months post-operatively.15

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective cohort study included patients who underwent posterior LD transfer for PSIRCTs at a single institution between April 2012 and June 2020, with a minimum follow-up duration of two years. The surgical indications were as follows: (1) persistent shoulder pain and functional limitations despite conservative management, (2) irreparable tears of the supraspinatus and infraspinatus tendons with retraction extending to the glenoid level, (3) advanced fatty infiltration of the supraspinatus and infraspinatus muscles (Goutallier9 grade 3 or 4), (4) intraoperative confirmation that the retracted tendons could not be mobilized to their anatomic footprint, (5) an intact or reparable subscapularis (Lafosse17 grade ≤ III), and (6) absence or minimal glenohumeral arthritis (Hamada10 grade ≤2). Contraindication included patients with advanced glenohumeral arthritis (Hamada10 grade >2) or an irreparable subscapularis tear (Lafosse10 grade > III). The exclusion criteria included any revision tendon transfers following a failed LD transfer, conversion to reverse total shoulder arthroplasty after LD transfer, post-operative infection, loss to follow-up, or incomplete clinical or radiological data. Retirement status and discontinuation of sports participation were determined based on patient self-report obtained through structured questionnaires at final follow-up and confirmed through medical record review. Patients who had retired due to advanced age or discontinued sports participation for reasons unrelated to shoulder symptoms were excluded from the RTW and RTS analyses.

Surgical procedure

All surgeries were performed by a single senior surgeon (C.H.B.) using an arthroscopic-assisted posterior LD transfer technique previously described in the literature.1 Patients were positioned in the lateral decubitus position under general anesthesia. The operation commenced with a diagnostic arthroscopy to evaluate intra-articular structures, including the glenohumeral joint and the long head of the biceps (LHBs) tendon. Within the study cohort, 57 patients had an intact LHB, while 10 had complete ruptures with distal retraction. Either tenotomy (n = 11) or tenodesis (n = 5) was performed in cases of partial LHB tears. Concomitant subscapularis pathology was addressed according to the Lafosse17 classification: type I lesions (n = 5) were treated with débridement, type II lesion (n = 12) were repaired with a single-row construct, and type III lesion (n = 2) were repaired with double-row fixation. Posterior LD transfer was indicated when supraspinatus and infraspinatus tears were deemed irreparable and could not be mobilized to their anatomic footprint despite thorough surgical release.

Through a posterior axillary approach made along its anterior border, with the shoulder flexed, internally rotated, and abducted, the LD tendon was harvested as a soft-tissue tendon-only graft without harvesting a bone block from the humeral insertion. The tendon was carefully identified, isolated, and mobilized while ensuring protection of the radial nerve and anterior humeral circumflex vessels. Proximal-to-distal blunt dissection was carried out to achieve sufficient excursion for the tendon transfer. The free end of the tendon was reinforced with nonabsorbable traction suture in a continuous locking fashion to facilitate handling and secure fixation. Arthroscopic preparation of the humeral footprint involved meticulous débridement and decortication of the greater tuberosity to expose bleeding cancellous bone and promote tendon-to-bone integration. The harvested LD tendon was then passed under the deltoid through the interval between the posterior deltoid and external rotators. Fixation was performed using a double-row suture bridge configuration: 2 triple-loaded anchors were inserted medially, with mattress sutures passed through the LD tendon, followed by lateral fixation with 3 4 knotless anchors to achieve broad tendon compression and maximize contact across the prepared footprint.

Post-operative rehabilitation

After surgery, the operated arm was immobilized in a 30° abduction brace with the shoulder in neutral rotation for four weeks. During this period, patients were instructed to perform gentle self-assisted passive ROM exercises to maintain joint mobility and prevent stiffness. Between post-operative weeks 4 and 12, the rehabilitation protocol progressed to active-assisted and active ROM exercises. From three months onward, a structured strengthening program was progressed to enhance dynamic stability and optimize shoulder function.

Outcome measures

The RTW was defined as the patient's ability to resume pre-injury occupational activities either completely or partially. A complete return was defined as resuming the same or a higher level of work compared to the preinjury state, whereas a partial return indicated a return at a lower level of occupational activity. Failure to return was classified as no return. The duration of RTW was recorded as the period from the date of surgery to the first resumption of work. Pre-operative occupational demand was classified according to physical intensity: light work (exerting up to 20 lb of force occasionally, up to 10 lb frequently, or a negligible amount constantly), medium work (exerting 20-50 lb occasionally, 10-25 lb frequently, or up to 10 lb constantly, exceeding light work demands), and heavy work (exerting 50-100 lb occasionally, 25-50 lb frequently, or 10-20 lb constantly).2 RTS was defined similarly, with activities categorized as shoulder-related sports (eg, tennis, badminton) or nonshoulder sports (eg, soccer, running) or no sports. The time to RTS was recorded from surgery to the first participation in sports activities. At the final follow-up, patients completed a structured questionnaire regarding occupation, sports participation, RTW or RTS status (complete, partial, or no return), time to return, and any task modifications required upon resumption. For patients unable to attend outpatient clinics, the survey was administered via telephone with 2 separate contact attempts; nonresponders were excluded from the analysis. Among 10 patients contacted for telephone follow-up, 7 completed the survey, whereas 3 patients did not respond despite repeated contact attempts and were excluded from the final analysis. RTW and RTS status were assessed using structured questionnaires administered by an experienced clinical research coordinator blinded to the study objectives. The timing and decision regarding RTW and RTS were based on patient self-reported determination obtained through the questionnaires.

Demographic variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, hypertension), smoking status, dominant arm involvement, and prior rotator cuff surgery. The dominant arm was defined as the arm primarily used by the patient for daily activities and was not necessarily the operative side. Pre-operative rotator cuff pathology was assessed using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and graded according to fatty infiltration of the subscapularis, supraspinatus, infraspinatus, and teres minor muscles. Clinical outcomes were assessed using validated measures, including the visual analog scale for pain, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score, Constant Score, Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation, and active ROM (forward elevation, abduction, external rotation, and internal rotation). Radiographic evaluation included acromion-humeral distance (AHD) and Hamada classification. Tendon integrity was evaluated using post-operative MRI obtained at final follow-up in all patients regardless of symptom status. Retear was defined as loss of continuity of the transferred tendon at the humeral footprint on MRI.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons of outcomes among work intensity groups or sports participation categories were performed using one-way analysis of variance or chi-square tests, as appropriate. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with RTW and RTS. Statistical significance was set at P < .05, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons.

Results

Patient characteristics

After excluding 15 patients, including revision surgery following LD transfer (n = 2), conversion to reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (n = 4), post-operative infection (n = 2), loss to follow-up (n = 4), and unavailable clinical or radiological data due to retirement or unemployment for reasons unrelated to shoulder function (n = 3), a total of 83 patients were included in the final analysis. The mean age was 61.9 ± 5.3 years, with a mean follow-up duration of 65.3 ± 13.9 months. Most patients were male (66.2%), and 75.9% had involvement of the dominant arm. Patients had worked for a mean of 34.5 ± 5.5 years, averaged 47.1 ± 4.7 work hours per week, and engaged in sports for 8.8 ± 2.9 hours per week (Tables I and II).

Return to work and sports

Among the 83 patients included in the final analysis for RTW, 85.5% of patients returned to work, including 56.6% with complete return and 28.9% with partial return, while 14.4% did not return. The mean time to RTW was 4.1 ± 1.1 months for the light work group and 5.1 ± 1.6 months for the heavy work group. Work level showed a clear gradient of RTW outcomes: light work had the highest rate of complete RTW (85.7%), followed by medium work (76.9%), and heavy work (25.0%), with heavy work also showing the highest proportion of no return (25.0%). Patients in the heavy work group required a significantly longer period to return (5.6 ± 1.6 months) compared with light and medium work groups.

Among the 68 patients eligible for RTS analysis, 60 patients (82.2%) participated in shoulder-related sports and 8 patients (9.6%) participated in nonshoulder sports, while 8 patients (18.1%) did not participate in sports. The overall RTS rate was 89.7%, with 72.1% achieving complete RTS and 27.8% partial RTS. Patients engaged in shoulder-related sports showed a higher complete RTS rate (66.6%) than those in nonshoulder sports (50.0%), but there were no significant differences between the 2 groups (Table III).

Clinical outcomes

All clinical outcome scores improved significantly across all work-intensity groups (P < .001 for all within-group comparisons). Mean visual analog scale pain scores significantly decreased in each group, and post-operative values differed among the work-level categories. ASES, Constant, and Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation scores also demonstrated substantial post-operative improvements in all groups, although no significant between-group differences were observed. Active ROM improved in forward elevation, abduction, and external rotation (all P < .001), whereas internal rotation showed no significant change. Radiographic evaluation demonstrated a post-operative decrease in AHD and progression in Hamada grade across all groups, with changes being significantly greater in the heavy-work group compared with the light- and medium-work groups (Table IV). A total of 12 retears (14.5%) were identified on post-operative MRI evaluation, including 3 patients in the light-work group (14.3%), 2 patients in the medium-work group (7.7%), and 7 patients in the heavy-work group (19.4%).

Factors associated with return to work and sports

Multivariable analysis identified work level as a significant predictor of RTW, with higher work intensity associated with lower odds of RTW (odds ratio [OR] = 0.614-0.819, P < .001). Retear was also independently associated with reduced likelihood of both RTW (OR = 0.614-0.819, P < .001) and RTS (OR = 0.712-0.870, P < .001). Concomitant subscapularis repair was negatively associated with RTW (P = .019). Other demographic and clinical variables, including age, sex, BMI, comorbidities, smoking status, and dominant arm involvement, were not significantly associated with RTW or RTS (Table V).

Discussion

This study assessed RTW and RTS outcomes in patients undergoing posterior LD transfer for PSIRCTs. Among the 83 patients included, 85.5% returned to work, with 56.6% achieving complete return and 28.9% a partial return, while 14.4% did not return. Patients with lighter work demands returned at higher rates and in a shorter time, with 95.2% returning to work at an average of 4.1 ± 1.1 months, compared with 92.3% at 4.1 ± 1.2 months in the medium-work group and 75.0% at 5.6 ± 1.6 months in the heavy-work group. Regarding sports, 89.7% of patients resumed activities, with 72.1% achieving complete return and 27.8% partial return. Higher work intensity and tendon retear were significantly associated with RTW, whereas tendon integrity was the sole significant predictor of RTS. Clinically, all work-level groups demonstrated significant improvements in pain and functional outcomes with no significant differences between the different work-intensity groups. Radiographic assessment indicated minor progression of arthritic changes, particularly in the heavy-work group.

In the current study, the rates of RTW and RTS following posterior LD transfer observed in this study are encouraging and consistent with prior evidence demonstrating that tendon transfers effectively restore function in patients with PSIRCTs. Although no previous studies have specifically evaluated RTW or RTS outcomes after posterior LD transfer for PSIRCTs, meaningful comparisons can be drawn from both related shoulder tendon transfer procedures and other clinical contexts. For example, Ghosh et al8 reported that 21 of 24 adults (88%) who underwent isolated LD transfer for traumatic brachial plexus injuries were able to RTW at final follow-up, with 13 resuming their pre-injury occupations. While this population differs from patients with irreparable rotator cuff tears, their findings reinforce the broader concept that LD transfer reliably restores useful shoulder function and supports meaningful return to daily and occupational activities. Kim et al15 reported RTW and RTS rates of 93.6% (63.1% completely, 36.9% partially) and 90.7% (70.5% completely, 29.5% partially), respectively, following LTT transfer for PSIRCTs, with mean return times of approximately 5-6 months. Similarly, Mihata et al19 demonstrated that arthroscopic superior capsular reconstruction (SCR) for PSIRCTs enabled 100% of patients to return to recreational or competitive sports and 94% to resume physical work, with significant improvements in post-operative ASES scores and ROM. The findings of the present study—while showing RTW and RTS rates lower than those reported for LTT and SCR—indicate that posterior LD transfer remains an effective and clinically reliable option for restoring function and facilitating return to occupational and recreational activities. Interestingly, patients participating in shoulder-related sports demonstrated a higher complete RTS rate compared with those participating in nonshoulder sports. This finding may reflect greater motivation for shoulder-specific recovery and rehabilitation adherence among patients involved in shoulder-demanding activities. However, interpretation should be made cautiously because the non-shoulder sports subgroup was relatively small, which may limit the reliability of subgroup comparisons. Also, these comparisons were intended to provide clinical context for the reported RTW and RTS outcomes and should not be interpreted as evidence of relative effectiveness among posterior LD transfer, LTT transfer, and SCR, as patient populations, indications, and surgical characteristics may substantially differ between procedures.

In the current study, patients with lighter occupational workloads returned more quickly and more consistently to their preinjury levels, whereas those engaged in heavy labor experienced longer recovery times and higher rates of partial or nonreturn. This trend is consistent with the findings of Haunschild et al,11 who reported that only 62% of patients returned to their previous work after rotator cuff repair, with markedly lower rates among individuals performing physically demanding labor. Similarly, Ting et al25 identified preinjury work intensity as a major determinant of RTW, noting that patients with light workloads were up to 11 times more likely to resume preinjury-level work compared with those in strenuous occupations. In line with these data, the present study similarly demonstrated that work intensity was significantly associated with RTW, indicating that although posterior LD transfer can effectively restore functional capacity for daily and moderate work demands, patients engaged in heavy labor may experience delayed or incomplete return.

The finding of this study showed that retear was independently associated with a lower likelihood of both RTW (OR = 0.614-0.819, P < .001) and RTS (OR = 0.712-0.870, P < .001). This finding aligns with the study by Koh et al,16 which reported that post-operative retears following rotator cuff repair significantly impaired the recovery of supraspinatus and external rotation strength, ultimately hindering patients' ability to resume previous activity levels. In contrast, those with intact repairs achieved higher return rates and faster functional recovery. Similarly, Chen et al4 demonstrated that early post-operative shoulder activity increased the risk of retears after rotator cuff repair, and that structural failure was closely linked to poorer function in active patients, whereas sedentary patients with retears often maintained functional gains comparable to those with intact tendons. Nonetheless, beyond tendon integrity, several pre-operative factors, including gender, workload, chronicity, tear size, and cholesterol level, have also been identified in recent systematic reviews as potential determinants of both the rate and timing of RTW or RTS following rotator cuff surgery.14 However, in the current study, other demographic and clinical factors, including age, sex, BMI, comorbidities, smoking status, and dominant arm involvement, were not significantly associated with either RTW or RTS. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as the relatively small sample size in certain subgroups and the limited number of events may have reduced the statistical power of the subgroup comparisons and multivariable regression analyses.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces inherent selection bias and limits control over potential confounding variables. Second, RTW and RTS assessments were based on patient self-report via surveys and telephone interviews, which may be subject to recall bias. Furthermore, occupational workload intensity and sports participation levels were self-reported and were not independently verified using objective performance measures or external records. Third, the relatively small sample size in certain occupational and sports subgroups may reduce statistical power, particularly for multivariable analyses. Furthermore, the relatively small number of nonreturn events compared with the number of analyzed variables may have increased the risk of model overfitting and reduced the stability of the multivariable regression analyses. Fourth, some patients returned to work earlier than recommended due to economic necessity, potentially affecting recovery outcomes. Fifth, RTS rates may be overestimated, as the majority of patients participated in recreational rather than professional sports. Sixth, while tendon integrity was assessed using MRI, objective measures of muscle strength or dynamic shoulder function were not evaluated, limiting insight into functional performance beyond patient-reported outcomes. Seventh, occupational workload classification was based on patient-reported job demands categorized according to Dictionary of Occupational Titles criteria, which may not fully reflect the variability of physical demands within individual occupations. Eighth, patients who underwent revision tendon transfer, conversion to reverse total shoulder arthroplasty, or experienced post-operative infection were excluded from the final analysis. Because these cases represent clinically important treatment failures, their exclusion may have introduced survivorship bias and potentially overestimated post-operative RTW/RTS rates and clinical outcomes. In addition, validated activity-level instruments such as the Tegner Activity Scale were not collected, limiting the precision of activity-demand assessment. Ninth, sports participation was categorized only as shoulder-related or nonshoulder–related activities without use of a validated sports activity grading system, which may limit the precision of activity-level assessment. Finally, although the follow-up period was sufficient to assess early RTW and RTS, it may not fully capture the long-term durability of functional recovery or progression of glenohumeral arthropathy.

Conclusion

Posterior LD transfer for PSIRCTs was associated with high rates of RTW and RTS among patients without major post-operative failure. Higher occupational demand was associated with delayed and less complete RTW, while tendon integrity was associated with both RTW and RTS. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because of retrospective design, self-reported activity outcomes, limited subgroup sizes, and exclusion of patients requiring revision surgery or conversion to reverse shoulder arthroplasty.
