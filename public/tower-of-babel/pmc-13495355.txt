Integrating single-cell and bulk RNA data to explore the prognostic significance of LIM domain family genes in colorectal cancer and unveil their immune landscape
Authors: Xinyu Zhao, Yili Shao, Huang Huang, Yumin Guo, Junkai Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•This study revealed that cells with high LIM scores were predominantly fibroblasts.•A risk model constructed from nine LIM domain family signature genes (CRYAB, RGS16, CSRP2, RCAN2, TPM1, EGFL7, PLS3, PDGFRA, and PLAU) accurately predicted the prognosis of CRC patients.•Erlotinib, fulvestrant, EPZ5676, and AZD1208 were identified as potential therapeutic agents for patients in the low-LIMRS group, whereas vinblastine, docetaxel, paclitaxel, and vinorelbine may be more suitable for high-LIMRS group patients.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Lim domain family, SCRNA-seq, bulk RNA-seq, prognosis

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most common malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally[1]. Its incidence continues to rise steadily, particularly in developing countries[2]. Current CRC treatment strategies—including radical surgery, adjuvant chemotherapy, and/or radiotherapy—are guided by disease stage and clinicopathological features[3]. Despite therapeutic advances, most patients face poor prognoses due to metastasis, recurrence, and drug resistance, with nearly half surviving less than five years[[4], [5], [6]]. Thus, novel prognostic models and therapies are urgently needed to improve clinical outcomes.

The LIM domain family comprises specialized tandem zinc finger structures rich in cysteine and histidine residues, serving as modular protein-binding interfaces[7]. Accumulating evidence highlights their roles in cytoskeletal organization, tissue-specific gene expression, cell adhesion, motility, and signal transduction[[8], [9], [10], [11]]. Notably, LIM domain proteins are emerging as pivotal molecules in human cancers. Studies emphasize the importance of PDZ-LIM family proteins (PDLIMs) in tumorigenesis[12]. For instance, PDLIM1 interacts with the E-cadherin/β-catenin complex to stabilize its structure, thereby suppressing β-catenin transcriptional activity and inhibiting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in highly metastatic CRC cells[13]. ABLIM1, another LIM domain protein, is reportedly overexpressed in CRC patients and associated with shorter recurrence-free survival, promoting CRC progression via IκBα ubiquitination-mediated NF-κB pathway activation[14]. These findings position the LIM domain family as a potential therapeutic target in CRC, though their prognostic roles remain incompletely elucidated.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) enables transcriptomic profiling at cellular resolution, uncovering immune cell diversity and unraveling the complexity of the tumor immune microenvironment[[15], [16], [17]]. Integrating scRNA-seq data with clinicopathological information may reveal innovative prognostic biomarkers[18]. Here, we combined scRNA-seq, bulk RNA-seq, and WGCNA to identify prognostic LIM domain family genes for risk modeling. We further examined these genes' expression patterns across cell types, constructed a robust nomogram incorporating risk scores and clinical variables, and explored correlations between risk scores and immune infiltration, biological functions, and drug sensitivity. Collectively, this study provided potential prognostic biomarkers for CRC therapy.

Materials and methods

Data download

CRC dataset (n = 701), comprising 650 cancer samples and 51 normal samples, was downloaded from TCGA (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). GSE29261 and GSE17536 datasets were obtained from GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) as external validation cohorts. Samples with survival time >30 days and complete survival information were included for prognostic validation. In addition, 13 cancer samples from the scRNA-seq dataset GSE166555 were included for single-cell analysis. It should be noted that the TCGA cohort contains RNA-seq data, whereas GSE29261 and GSE17536 consist of microarray data. Each cohort was processed separately according to the characteristics of its respective platform, and raw expression matrices from different platforms were not directly combined. Accordingly, batch effect correction was not performed between the training and validation cohorts.

The LIM domain family gene set, consisting of 47 genes (Table S1), was identified by reviewing relevant literature[19].

scRNA-seq data processing

scRNA-seq data (GSE166555) were analyzed using the "Seurat" R package[20]. Gene expression was normalized using the NormalizeData and ScaleData functions, and the top 2000 highly variable genes were identified by using FindVariableFeatures function. To mitigate batch effects, the Harmony algorithm was applied for integration and correction. Cell clustering was performed using a shared nearest neighbor (SNN)-based clustering algorithm (resolution = 0.6), and the structures of resulting cell clusters were visualized through UMAP dimensionality reduction.

Cell type and marker gene identification

Cell annotation was performed using the "SingleR" package[21] with two reference datasets: HumanPrimaryCellAtlasData and BlueprintEncodeData. The results were further refined using CellMarker (http://xteam.xbio.top/CellMarker/) and literature-based annotations[22]. Bubble plots were generated to visualize the expression levels of common marker genes.

AUCell scoring based on LIM domain family genes

The intersection of genes expressed in the scRNA-seq dataset and the LIM domain family gene set yielded 42 genes. Using the "AUCell" R package[23], all cells were scored based on these 42 genes. A threshold (AUC = 0.088) calculated by AUCell_exploreThresholds was applied to classify cells into high- and low-LIM score groups. It should be noted that the AUC in this study refers to the area under the curve of gene set activity calculated by AUCell based on the ranking of intracellular gene expression values. The cutoff AUC value of 0.088 was not manually predefined according to cell type distribution; instead, it was automatically identified by the AUCell_exploreThresholds function after evaluating candidate thresholds against the overall distribution of AUCell scores across all cells. This data-driven automatic threshold minimizes subjective bias introduced by manual cutoff selection. Cells with an AUC score above 0.088 were classified into the high LIM score group, and all remaining cells were assigned to the low LIM score group.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two groups were identified using the FindMarkers function with the following parameters: min.pct = 0.01, |log2FC| > 0.585, and adjusted p-value < 0.05 (other parameters set as default).

WGCNA construction and key module identification

ssGSEA algorithm was used to compute scores for the 42 LIM domain family genes in TCGA-CRC dataset. "WGCNA" R package[24] was employed to identify LIM-related gene modules. Genes were clustered into modules based on topological overlap matrix (TOM) dissimilarity. WGCNA parameters included a soft threshold of 7 and a minimum module size of 30 genes. The module most significantly correlated with LIM domain family gene scores (p < 0.05) was selected, and its genes were intersected with DEGs from the high- and low-LIM score groups to obtain overlapping genes.

Prognostic model construction and evaluation

In TCGA training set, LASSO regression analysis was performed using "glmnet"[25] R package to screen for CRC prognostic biomarkers from the overlapping genes. The LASSO Cox regression model was fitted with 10-fold cross-validation to identify the optimal penalty parameter λ. In this study, lambda.min = 0.02,129,074 was selected as the final parameter, and genes with non-zero regression coefficients under this λ value were included in the subsequent multivariate Cox regression analysis. Patients in the TCGA training set and GEO validation set (GSE29261) were stratified into high- and low-LIMRS groups based on the median LIMRS. Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis was conducted using the "survival" R package. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted using the "timeROC"[27] R package, and the area under the curve (AUC) was computed to evaluate the model’s predictive accuracy. GSE29261 and GSE17536 datasets were used as independent validation cohorts to evaluate the predictive performance of the model.

Fibroblast subpopulation identification and pseudotime analysis

We analyzed expression patterns of model genes across cell types and observed significant expression in fibroblasts. Fibroblasts were extracted using the subset function and reprocessed following the steps in Section 1.2. Fibroblast subpopulations were identified using FindClusters at a resolution of 0.4.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed using the "monocle"[28] R package to examine the distribution of fibroblast subpopulations along the pseudotime axis and the dynamic expression changes of model genes.

Prognostic nomogram construction and validation

A prognostic nomogram integrating risk scores and clinical factors (age, gender, TNM stage, and tumor stage) was developed. Predictive performance was assessed using ROC curves, decision curve analysis (DCA), and calibration curves. Clinical covariates were predefined based on their well-established clinical significance in CRC prognostic evaluation and data completeness within the current cohort, including variables such as age, sex, and pathological staging, rather than being screened solely according to the statistical significance of univariate analyses. Subsequently, the LIMRS and the clinical variables were jointly incorporated into a multivariate Cox regression model to assess the independent prognostic value of the LIMRS.

Tumor microenvironment (TME) and immunotherapy response analysis

This study employed the ssGSEA algorithm to calculate immune cell infiltration levels and immune function activity scores in high- and low-LIMRS groups. Immune cell infiltration data for the TCGA-CRC dataset were obtained from the TIMER online platform (https://timer.cistrome.org/), including computational results from the EPIC, XCELL, and MCPCOUNTER algorithms. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF) from these data were subjected to univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses to evaluate their prognostic value along with riskScore. Additionally, the CMScaller package was used for CMS subtyping of TCGA samples, and samples with FDR < 0.05 were selected to analyze the association between CMS subtypes and prognosis. Box plots were utilized to compare the expression differences of immune checkpoint genes and HLA-related genes across different LIMRS groups. The Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) score was applied to predict the immune therapy response of each group. To further validate the predictive performance of immunotherapy response, the Immunophenotypescore (IPS) was retrieved from the TCIA database in this study. The IPS comprehensively evaluates tumor immunogenicity based on indicators including antigen presentation, effector cells, immunosuppressive cells, and immune checkpoints. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare IPS differences between the high and low LIMRS subgroups. A higher IPS indicates enhanced tumor immunogenicity and potentially superior responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy.

Biological function analysis

DEGs between high- and low-LIMRS groups were identified using wilcox.test (stats R package)[29] with thresholds of FDR < 0.05 and |log(FC)| > 0.585. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses were performed on DEGs, and results were visualized using the "enrichplot"[30] and "GOplot"[31] R packages.

Drug sensitivity prediction

Chemotherapy sensitivity analysis was conducted using "oncoPredict" R package and Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) dataset[32]. Drug sensitivity scores were predicted for high- and low-LIMRS groups in CRC dataset, with GDSC data serving as the training set. Differences in drug sensitivity between groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon test. The Imputed Sensitivity Score in this study is an IC50-related drug response score predicted by oncoPredict using GDSC drug response datasets. A lower score corresponds to a smaller predicted IC50 value and relatively higher drug sensitivity, whereas a higher score suggests relatively weaker drug sensitivity or a greater tendency toward drug resistance. This score was only utilized to compare relative predictive differences across distinct LIMRS subgroups and does not reflect the actual clinical drug response of patients.

Cell culture

The human colon cancer cell line HCT116 was purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (catalog number: H1–0401, OriCell, China). Human colon cancer cell line HT29 was purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (catalog number: TCHu103, China). HCT116 cells were cultured in McCoy’s 5A medium (catalog number: 11,965,118, Thermo Fisher, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; catalog number: 16,600,082, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS; catalog number: C0222, Beyotime, China). HT29 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; catalog number: 11,965,118, Thermo Fisher, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (catalog number: 16,600,082, Gibco, USA) and 1% PS (catalog number: C0222, Beyotime, China). All cells were maintained in a constant temperature incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

Cell transfection

HCT116 cells were transfected with si-RGS16 (RiboBio, China) and negative control (si-NC, RiboBio, China) using the Lipofectamine 2000 kit (catalog number: 11,668,019, Thermo Fisher, USA). Transfected cells were harvested after 24 h for subsequent experiments. Similarly, si-RGS16 (RiboBio, China) and negative control si-NC (RiboBio, China) were transfected into HT29 cells using the Lipofectamine 2000 transfection kit (catalog number: 11,668,019, Thermo Fisher, USA) following the transfection procedure described above. Cells were harvested 24 h post-transfection for subsequent experiments.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using Trizol reagent (15596018CN, Thermo Fisher, USA), and RNA concentration and purity were measured with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. The RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using HiScript III RT SuperMix (R323–01, Vazyme, China). Finally, qRT-PCR amplification was performed on a Step-One Plus real-time PCR system using the SYBR Green qPCR kit (CL132–01, Vazyme, China). Western blot (WB)

Total cellular protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, China). Protein concentration was quantified with the BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0012, Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated via 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, USA). The PVDF membranes were blocked with PBS blocking buffer containing 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation with the following primary antibodies at 4 °C: anti-RGS16 antibody (1:1000, PA5–92,131, Thermo Fisher, USA), anti-phospho-EGFR (Tyr1068) antibody (1:1000, MA5–42,450, Thermo Fisher, USA), anti-EGFR antibody (1:1000, 700,994, Thermo Fisher, USA), anti-phospho-ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204) antibody (1:1000, MA5–15,173, Thermo Fisher, USA), anti-ERK1/2 antibody (1:1000, MA5–15,134, Thermo Fisher, USA), and anti-GAPDH antibody (1:50,000, MA5–35,235, Thermo Fisher, USA). After washing with PBST, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (1:10,000, 31,460, Thermo Fisher, USA) was added and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit (P0018, Beyotime, China), and the gray values of protein bands were quantitatively analyzed with ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

Colony formation assay

HCT116 or HT29 cells in the logarithmic growth phase from each group were trypsinized and resuspended to prepare a single-cell suspension. After cell counting, the cells were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 500 cells per well, with 2 mL of complete medium added to each well. The cells were then cultured undisturbed in a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator, with the medium refreshed every three days. When visible cell colonies had formed, the medium was discarded, and the cells were gently washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. Excess stain was gently rinsed off with running water, and the plates were air-dried at room temperature. Finally, colonies containing >50 cells were counted. For the Erlotinib treatment group, referring to previous literature[33], HCT116 or HT29 cells were treated with 20 μM Erlotinib (catalog number: S1023, Selleck Chemicals, USA) for 48 h. The drug was dissolved and prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), while the control group was treated with an equal volume of DMSO.

Flow cytometry

Cell apoptosis in HCT116 cells from each group was detected using an Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (560,931, BD Biosciences, USA). First, cells from each treatment group were washed with PBS to prepare a single-cell suspension. Subsequently, 500 μL of the cell suspension was mixed with 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 μL of PI, followed by incubation in the dark for 15 min. Finally, the apoptosis rate of these cells was measured using a FACScan flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). Twenty-four hours after transfection with si-NC or si-RGS16, HCT116 and HT29 cells were treated with 20 μM Erlotinib (catalog number: S1023, Selleck Chemicals, USA) for 48 h prior to apoptosis assay according to experimental grouping requirements, whereas the control group was treated with an equal volume of DMSO.

Data analysis and statistical methods

Bioinformatics analysis in this study was performed using R software (version 4.1.1), while experimental data analysis was assisted by Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism 8.0. The specific R packages and versions used included: Seurat (v4.0.1), SingleR (v4.5), AUCell (v1.20.2), WGCNA (v1.73), glmnet (v4.1.7), survival (v3.5.5), timeROC (v0.4), monocle (v2.28.0), oncoPredict (v0.2), stats (v4.1.1), enrichplot (v1.14.2), and GOplot (v1.0.2). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Group comparisons were conducted using Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test based on data distribution characteristics, while multi-group comparisons were performed using two-way ANOVA. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test, and correlation analysis was performed using Spearman’s method. Patients were divided into high- and low-risk groups based on the median LIMRS. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method and log-rank test, with hazard ratios calculated by Cox proportional hazards regression models, and multivariate Cox regression was used to identify independent prognostic factors. Patients with survival times <30 days were excluded from the survival analysis. All statistical tests were two-sided, with significance levels marked as follows: ns, P > 0.05; *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001; ****, P < 0.0001.

Results

scRNA-seq reveals cellular composition and heterogeneity in the CRC tumor microenvironment

Following processing of scRNA-seq dataset (GSE166555), we obtained 37,712 cells from 13 CRC patients, which were clustered into 20 distinct clusters (Fig. 1A). These clusters were annotated into 8 major cell types based on canonical marker genes: B cells, endothelial cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, macrophages, mast cells, monocytes, and T cells (Fig. 1B). Expression patterns of marker genes for each cell type were visualized via a bubble plot (Fig. 1C). Notably, interpatient heterogeneity in cell type proportions was observed, with epithelial cells, T cells, and B cells constituting the dominant populations, followed by fibroblasts and monocytes (Figs. 1D, E).

Intersection of scRNA-seq-expressed genes with 47 LIM domain family genes yielded 42 expressed LIM genes (Fig. 2A). Violin and bar plots demonstrated that high-LIM-score cells were predominantly fibroblasts, followed by endothelial and epithelial cells (Figs. 2D, E).

Based on the median LIMRS, CRC patients were categorized into high-LIMRS and low-LIMRS groups. Kaplan-Meier survival curves demonstrated that patients in the low-LIMRS group had significantly prolonged overall survival compared to those in the high-LIMRS group (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 5A). Similar results were observed in the GSE29261 and GSE17536 validation cohorts (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5B-C). The predictive performance of the model was further evaluated using ROC curves. In the TCGA-CRC cohort, the AUC values for 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival were 0.71, 0.70, and 0.71, respectively (Fig. 5D). In the GSE29261 validation cohort, the corresponding AUC values were 0.87, 0.77, and 0.74 (Fig. 5E), while in the GSE17536 validation cohort, the AUC values were 0.75, 0.65, and 0.66 (Fig. 5F). To further quantify the predictive performance of the LIMRS model in external validation cohorts, we calculated the concordance index (C-index). The C-index values of LIMRS were 0.6532 in the GSE29261 cohort and 0.6744 in the GSE17536 cohort, indicating moderate prognostic discrimination in independent datasets. Calibration curves and Brier score results indicated that the model exhibited good calibration and predictive accuracy in the validation cohorts (Supplementary Figure S1A-D). Nested cross-validation results further supported the robust predictive performance of the model (Supplementary Figure S1E). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses confirmed that LIMRS remained an independent prognostic predictor after adjusting for tumor purity and cancer-associated fibroblast scores (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5G-H). After further combined stratification of LIMRS and CMS subtypes, the high-LIMRS/CMS1–3, high-LIMRS/CMS4, low-LIMRS/CMS1–3, and low-LIMRS/CMS4 subgroups consisted of 153, 120, 199, and 75 patients, respectively. There was a statistically significant difference in overall survival among the four groups (p < 0.0001, Fig. 5I). This finding suggests that LIMRS and CMS classification possess combined prognostic stratification value, though it does not indicate that LIMRS yields significant prognostic discrimination within each individual CMS subtype.

Heterogeneous expression and differentiation dynamics of LIM model genes in fibroblast subpopulations

scRNA-seq analysis revealed that model genes were highly expressed in fibroblasts (all 9 genes), endothelial cells (CSRP2, TPM1, EGFL7, PLS3), epithelial cells (TPM1), and macrophages (PLAU) (Figs. 6A, B). Therefore, since all nine model genes were expressed in fibroblasts and high-LIM-score cells were also predominantly fibroblasts, we analyzed fibroblasts by clustering them into 10 distinct subsets (Fig. 6C). UMAP plots and violin plots illustrated the expression patterns of the nine model genes across these 10 fibroblast clusters (Figs. 6D, E). We employed pseudotime trajectory analysis to investigate the differentiation dynamics of fibroblasts. The results revealed a leftward differentiation trajectory over time, with cluster 0 representing the early stage of differentiation, while clusters 2, 3, and 6 were associated with the late stage (Fig. 6F). Dynamic expression patterns were observed: CSRP2, PLAU, PLS3, RCAN2, RGS16, and TPM1 increased along pseudotime, while CRYAB and PDGFRA declined; EGFL7 showed no clear trend (Fig. 6G).

Immune microenvironment analysis

To systematically evaluate the differences in immune characteristics of the tumor microenvironment between high- and low-risk groups, we conducted a comprehensive analysis across four dimensions: immune checkpoint expression, HLA-related gene transcription, immune functional activity, and immunotherapy response. The immune checkpoint gene expression profile revealed that CD40LG and ICOS were significantly highly expressed in the low-LIMRS group, while PDCD1 showed higher expression in the high-LIMRS group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 8A). Regarding HLA-related genes, compared to the low-LIMRS group, the expression levels of HLA-DRB1, HLA-DQB2, HLA-DPB1, and HLA-DMA were significantly higher in the high-LIMRS group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 8B). Immune-related functional analysis indicated that the Type II IFN Response score was significantly higher in the high-LIMRS group (FDR < 0.05) (Fig. 8C). Although differences were observed in other cell lines, these failed to reach statistical significance. Additionally, the high-LIMRS group was associated with significantly higher TIDE scores (FDR < 0.05) (Fig. 8D). To further evaluate potential immunotherapy responsiveness, IPS scores were compared between the high-LIMRS and low-LIMRS subgroups. The low-LIMRS group exhibited markedly higher IPS values than the high-LIMRS group, indicating that low-risk patients possessed stronger tumor immunogenicity and a greater likelihood of responding to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy (Fig. 8E). This result was consistent with TIDE-based predictions and further corroborated the correlation between LIMRS and the immunotherapy-relevant tumor immune microenvironment.

957 DEGs were identified between LIMRS groups in TCGA dataset (Fig. 9A). GO analysis linked these to adenylate cyclase-modulating G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathway, neuronal cell body, and cytokine activity (p < 0.05; Fig. 9B). Impact of RGS16 on drug sensitivity in CRC cells

RGS16, as a member of the RGS protein family, plays a critical role in G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) signaling pathways by promoting GTP hydrolysis of Gα subunits, thereby precisely regulating downstream signal transduction processes[34]. Recent studies have shown that GPCR signaling is widely involved in tumorigenesis and progression, making RGS proteins an emerging target for cancer therapy[35]. Computational predictions from molecular docking revealed that Erlotinib may form a potential binding conformation within the structure of RGS16, suggesting a possible interaction between the two molecules (Fig. 11A). It should be highlighted that these computational results are only used to propose a hypothesis of potential interaction and cannot verify the direct physical binding between RGS16 and Erlotinib.

E: Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry evaluating apoptosis in the indicated treatment groups. To further investigate the relationship between RGS16 and Erlotinib sensitivity, we conducted subsequent cellular experiments. First, we successfully established an RGS16 knockdown model in HCT116 and HT29 cells, confirming efficient knockdown (Fig. 11B, Supplementary Figure S2A). WB was performed to further verify the knockdown efficiency at the protein level. The results demonstrated that RGS16 protein expression was markedly reduced in both HCT116 and HT29 cells transfected with si-RGS16 compared with the si-NC control group (Fig. 11C, Supplementary Figure S2B), indicating effective knockdown of RGS16. After Erlotinib treatment, colony formation assays showed that both the si-NC + Erlotinib group and the si-RGS16 group exhibited significant inhibition of cell proliferation (P < 0.0001), indicating that RGS16 is a key factor in maintaining CRC cell proliferation and that Erlotinib monotherapy also effectively suppresses proliferation. Notably, compared to the si-RGS16 group, the combination of si-RGS16 and Erlotinib did not produce an additive effect (P > 0.05), suggesting that the efficacy of Erlotinib depends on RGS16 expression levels (Fig. 11D). Flow cytometry further revealed that either RGS16 knockdown or Erlotinib monotherapy significantly induced apoptosis (P < 0.001), but their combination did not further enhance the apoptotic effect (P > 0.05) (Fig. 11E). These results collectively demonstrate that RGS16 not only promotes the malignant progression of CRC but also serves as a key regulator of tumor cell sensitivity to specific targeted drugs.‌ To further verify the generalizability of the above findings, key experiments were replicated in HT29, another CRC cell line with a distinct genetic background (BRAF-mutant). Consistent with the results obtained in HCT116 cells, RGS16 knockdown also markedly suppressed proliferation and induced apoptosis in HT29 cells. Moreover, the combined treatment of si-RGS16 and Erlotinib failed to exhibit additional synergistic effects compared with treatment with si-RGS16 alone or Erlotinib alone (p > 0.05) (Supplementary Figure S2C–D), demonstrating that the dependence of Erlotinib efficacy on RGS16 expression is not cell-line specific.

To further validate the proposed regulatory hypothesis of the GPCR-MAPK signaling axis, WB was utilized to detect the phosphorylation levels of EGFR and ERK1/2 before and after Erlotinib treatment. The results showed that Erlotinib treatment significantly reduced the phosphorylation levels of EGFR (Tyr1068) and ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204) in si-NC cells, while the total protein levels of EGFR and ERK1/2 remained largely unchanged. Notably, the basal phosphorylation levels of EGFR and ERK1/2 were significantly lower in si-RGS16 cells relative to the si-NC group, and no further reduction in phosphorylation was observed following Erlotinib administration (Fig. 11F, Supplementary Figure S2E). Collectively, these data suggest that RGS16 knockdown may attenuate the therapeutic efficacy of Erlotinib via suppressing the activity of the MAPK signaling pathway.

Discussion

CRC ranks among the most prevalent malignant gastrointestinal tumors globally, with high morbidity and mortality rates[36]. Accumulating evidence highlights the indispensable roles of LIM domain family genes in tumorigenesis, progression, and host immunity[[37], [38], [39]]. While prior studies have focused on individual genes, the prognostic implications of LIM domain family genes in CRC remain incomprehensive. To address this, our study integrated scRNA-seq, bulk RNA-seq data, and WGCNA to identify nine signature genes and establish a robust risk model with strong predictive capability. Furthermore, we explored the associations between LIMRS, TME, and drug sensitivity. Collectively, this research provides a more effective foundation for developing CRC therapeutic strategies.

Our investigation initially identified eight cell types based on scRNA-seq data. Subsequent intersection of single-cell data genes with LIM domain family genes enabled AUCell scoring across all cells. Remarkably, cells in the high LIM score group were predominantly enriched in fibroblasts. TME comprises not only tumor cells but also diverse stromal and immune cells[40]. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), the primary stromal component, secrete growth factors, inflammatory ligands, and extracellular matrix proteins to promote malignant proliferation, drug resistance, and immune evasion[41,42]. A prior study has demonstrated LIM domain family gene expression in CAFs, facilitating cancer progression[43]. Gullotti et al. [44]. revealed that FHL2 expression in peritumoral fibroblasts may enhance lymphatic metastasis of colon cancer cells. Further intersecting 826 DEGs from high/low LIM score cell subpopulations with 1812 WGCNA-screened genes allowed the construction of a 9-gene prognostic model via LASSO and multivariate Cox regression analyses. These genes—CRYAB, RGS16, CSRP2, RCAN2, TPM1, EGFL7, PLS3, PDGFRA, and PLAU—were confirmed to be highly expressed in fibroblasts, suggesting that LIM domain family genes predominantly function through fibroblast-related mechanisms in tumor development. Cells with high LIM scores were predominantly enriched in fibroblasts, indicating that LIM-associated transcriptional programs are tightly linked to stromal compartments and fibroblast states in CRC. LIM-domain proteins are extensively involved in cytoskeleton organization, cell adhesion, and mechanosensing; accordingly, elevated LIM activity may reflect fibroblast activation and stromal remodeling[45]. Activated fibroblasts modulate growth factor signaling and extracellular matrix remodeling to regulate tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and therapeutic response[46]. Single-cell and spatial omics studies further reveal profound phenotypic and functional heterogeneity among CAFs[47]. In CRC, subsets of CAFs can suppress effector T cell function via NECTIN2-dependent signaling, thereby mediating immune microenvironment remodeling[48]. Thus, the expression patterns and pseudotemporal dynamics of the nine model genes across distinct fibroblast subpopulations likely recapitulate dynamic heterogeneity in fibroblast states. Pseudotime trajectory analysis uncovered pronounced dynamic transitions along fibroblast lineages, with multiple LIMRS model genes exhibiting stage-specific expression profiles. These observations imply that LIM-related genes participate in fibroblast state switching and tumor stroma remodeling. Clusters 2, 3, and 6 at the late pseudotemporal stages may represent more activated or functionally remodeled fibroblast populations. Nevertheless, this study lacks systematic annotation of high-LIM-score fibroblasts using canonical CAF markers, functional gene signatures, or spatial localization approaches, precluding their direct classification as a specific CAF subtype at present. Future integrative analyses incorporating CAF subtype signature scores, cell-cell communication profiling, spatial transcriptomics, and immunofluorescence co-localization will help definitively characterize the identity of these cells and delineate their functional roles within the tumor microenvironment.

In our model, TPM1, PDGFRA, and PLAU were identified as protective factors (HR < 1), while the remaining genes acted as risk factors (HR > 1). Emerging studies suggest these genes influence CRC via distinct mechanisms. CRYAB drives CRC progression by repressing TRIM55-mediated β-catenin ubiquitination and degradation[49]. RGS16 hinders JNK/P38-mediated apoptosis in CRC cells by interfering with TRAF6 recruitment via TAB2/TAK1[50]. CSRP2 inhibits Rac1 activation by blocking p130Cas phosphorylation, thereby activating the Hippo pathway and suppressing ERK and PAK/LIMK/cortactin signaling, ultimately restraining CRC EMT and metastasis[51]. Discrepancies in these findings may stem from sample inconsistency and CRC’s heterogeneity. USP7-mediated JUND inhibition suppresses RCAN2 transcription while activating NFATC1, enhancing stemness in CRC[52]. TPM1, a tumor suppressor downregulated in CRC[53], is transcriptionally repressed by LINC01116 through EZH2-driven promoter methylation, thereby promoting proliferation and angiogenesis[54]. EGFL7 regulates invasion and apoptosis via PI3K/AKT pathway[55]. PLS3 induces EMT through TGF-β signaling, conferring invasive capabilities[56]. PDGFRA also emerges as a potential therapeutic target and predictive biomarker for regorafenib response in young CRC patients[57]. Targeting these genes may thus represent promising strategies to improve CRC treatment efficacy.

CRC exhibits a highly heterogeneous immune microenvironment, with significant differences in the types and proportions of infiltrating immune cells[58]. This study systematically reveals notable differences in the tumor microenvironment between high- and low-LIMRS groups, providing critical insights into the prognostic predictive mechanism of LIMRS. In terms of immune regulation mechanisms, the high expression of CD40LG and ICOS in the low-risk group suggests a more pronounced T-cell activation state[59,60]. CD40LG serves as a key co-stimulatory signal for B-cell activation, while ICOS is involved in maintaining T follicular helper cell function. The synergistic high expression of these two molecules may form the immunological basis for the favorable prognosis in this subgroup[61,62]. In contrast, the significant upregulation of PDCD1 in the high-risk group reflects aggravated T-cell exhaustion[63], providing a plausible explanation for the poorer prognosis observed in this subgroup. Additionally, the overall enhanced expression of HLA class II genes in the high-risk group may be associated with adaptive changes during tumor immunoediting. Under sustained immune pressure, tumor cells may upregulate HLA class II molecule expression to mediate immune escape[64]. At the functional level, the enhanced Type II IFN Response signaling in the high-risk group may reflect a complex dynamic balance within the immune microenvironment. Mechanistically, persistent IFN-γ signaling can both activate anti-tumor immune responses and promote immune escape by inducing the expression of various immune checkpoint molecules[[65], [66], [67]]. This dual regulatory effect may explain the paradoxical immune characteristics observed in the high-risk group. Particularly noteworthy, predictions based on the TIDE algorithm indicate that the high-risk group may exhibit lower sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy. In addition to TIDE analysis, IPS was further utilized in this study to evaluate potential immunotherapy responsiveness. The low-LIMRS subgroup exhibited significantly elevated IPS values, suggesting stronger tumor immunogenicity and an increased probability of favorable responses to immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy in this cohort. Consistent with the predictive trend derived from TIDE, this result provides supplementary computational evidence supporting the association between LIMRS and immunotherapy-related signatures. Nevertheless, both IPS and TIDE are algorithm-based predictive tools and cannot be regarded as direct clinical evidence of ICI treatment benefit. Further validation in CRC cohorts with authentic ICI therapeutic outcomes is therefore warranted.

Functional enrichment analysis implicates calcium signaling in modulating tumor immune microenvironment. Genetic polymorphisms in calcium signaling pathway components reportedly influence CRC pathogenesis via TME regulation[68]. Drug sensitivity studies further highlight chemoresistance-related pathways. α-hederin activates Ca²⁺/MAPK signaling via GPCRs to induce CRC cell apoptosis[69]. Our data demonstrated that low-LIMRS patients exhibited heightened sensitivity to Erlotinib, Fulvestrant, EPZ5676, and AZD1208, whereas high-LIMRS patients displayed resistance. Conversely, high-LIMRS patients showed increased sensitivity to Vinblastine, Docetaxel, Paclitaxel, and Vinorelbine. These drugs target specific pathways: Erlotinib suppresses TrkB-dependent ERK signaling to reduce apoptosis resistance, EMT, migration, and invasion[70], while Paclitaxel enhances apoptosis via MAPK inhibition[71]. Computational molecular docking predictions in this study suggested a potential interaction between RGS16 and Erlotinib. Nevertheless, molecular docking is a computational simulation relying on protein structures and scoring functions, and its outcomes only reflect theoretical potential binding capacity rather than evidence of direct physical interaction between the two molecules. Although cellular phenotypic assays and detection of EGFR–ERK-associated proteins supported a functional correlation between RGS16 expression levels and Erlotinib responsiveness, these results still fail to confirm direct binding of Erlotinib to RGS16. Follow-up experiments including surface plasmon resonance, microscale thermophoresis, isothermal titration calorimetry, or intracellular target binding assays are required to further verify their direct binding and binding affinity. Further cellular experiments confirmed that both RGS16 knockdown and Erlotinib monotherapy significantly inhibited HCT116 cell proliferation and induced apoptosis, but the combined treatment did not produce an additive effect. This important finding indicates that the efficacy of Erlotinib may depend on the expression level of RGS16, suggesting that both may act on the same signaling pathway or share common downstream effector mechanisms. Research by Shen et al. [50]. demonstrated that RGS16 can inhibit apoptosis in CRC cells by interfering with the TRAF6-TAB2-TAK1-JNK/p38 MAPK signaling pathway. Based on the results of this study, we hypothesize that RGS16 may influence Erlotinib drug sensitivity by regulating the GPCR-MAPK signaling axis. Specifically, as a key regulator of G protein signaling, the expression level of RGS16 may affect the activation state of the MAPK signaling pathway, thereby altering the cellular response to Erlotinib. When RGS16 expression is inhibited, the MAPK signaling pathway may already be in a relatively suppressed state, making it difficult for additional Erlotinib treatment to produce further therapeutic effects. This mechanistic hypothesis provides a new perspective for understanding the role of RGS16 in targeted therapy for CRC and also points the way for further in-depth research.

This study systematically constructed and validated a CRC prognosis model based on LIM domain family genes using scRNA-seq data. Through bioinformatics analysis and independent cohort validation, the robustness of this model in prognosis stratification was confirmed. We emphasize that at this stage, the model mainly serves as a retrospective research tool and an important foundation for future exploratory analysis. Its core value lies in providing new research directions and methodological references for the functional study of LIM domain family genes in CRC. From a potential clinical translational perspective, LIMRS could serve as a candidate adjunct to conventional clinicopathological staging to assist prognostic stratification of patients with CRC. It enables discrimination of patients with divergent survival risks within identical clinical stages and provides references for individualized surveillance strategies and clinical trial enrollment. However, this study did not incorporate LIMRS alongside established molecular biomarkers such as MSI status and KRAS mutation status into a unified predictive model for head-to-head comparison; accordingly, we cannot confirm whether LIMRS yields incremental prognostic value independent of these biomarkers. Future analyses should compare the base model incorporating clinical factors plus MSI and KRAS status against an extended model integrated with LIMRS, with Harrell’s C-index, time-dependent AUC, and decision curve analysis adopted to quantify the supplementary prognostic information conferred by LIMRS. Despite the aforementioned promising translational potentials of LIMRS, several limitations of the present study remain to be acknowledged. First, although tumor purity and cancer-associated fibroblast scores were considered in the statistical analyses, the potential contribution of stromal-cell-derived signals to the LIMRS remains to be further clarified. Future studies using immunofluorescence co-localization, cell sorting, and protein-level validation may help determine the cellular specificity of the model genes. Second, the scRNA-seq analysis was based on samples from only 13 patients, which may not fully capture the interpatient heterogeneity of the CRC tumor microenvironment. Integration of additional publicly available single-cell datasets and validation in independent cohorts are therefore warranted. Third, differences between RNA-seq and microarray platforms may affect the reproducibility of the model. Although the datasets were processed separately and used as independent training and validation cohorts, residual cross-platform technical variation cannot be completely excluded. Fourth, the predicted subgroup-specific drug sensitivities were derived from computational analyses based on GDSC cell-line data and should be regarded as exploratory findings rather than evidence for clinical treatment selection. These predictions require further validation using patient-derived organoids, animal models, and clinical cohorts with documented treatment responses. Finally, the model has not yet been evaluated in prospective clinical cohorts. Its predictive performance and potential utility in treatment decision-making should be assessed in well-designed multicenter studies using standardized sample collection, testing procedures, and complete clinical follow-up data. Future research should also further investigate the biological functions of key LIM-related genes and accumulate prospective evidence to support the clinical translation of LIMRS.
