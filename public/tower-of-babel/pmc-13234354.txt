Digital documentation and virtual representation of built heritage using terrestrial LiDAR
Authors: Divya Priya Balasubramani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Indus Valley Civilization, one of the oldest and most diverse heritages, must be meticulously preserved. We aim to utilize modern techniques and tools to conserve these extensive built heritage sites for the benefit of society and future generations. Accurate digitization through non-invasive and long-range imaging tools is essential for preserving and showcasing India’s heritage on the global platform. These advanced methods capture intricate details, ensuring precise documentation of geometric, structural, and architectural elements, thereby maintaining their historical value. In this study, we propose a novel approach to document a 125-year-old Indo-Saracenic building complex in the Madras High Court campus using advanced reality capture techniques, such as close-range photogrammetry and terrestrial laser scanning surveys. The three-dimensional model developed from these hybrid techniques enables as-built documentation and precise measurement. They also support the extraction of 2D drawings, facilitating the planning and execution of restoration works. Furthermore, the developed model is integrated with virtual reality techniques to create an immersive and walk-through model. The outcome of our work is an application that offers a virtual and interactive environment with digital datasets supporting the conservation, restoration, and management of built heritage structures and also promotes digital heritage tourism in the near future.

Introduction

The Indian subcontinent is filled with a variety of heritage structural wonders that are standing as a testament to India’s rich cultural history. The need for the conservation and protection of heritage sites and buildings is well understood by the experts and stakeholders and so the Government of India has taken various initiatives and issued guidelines for the conservation of heritage sites. The first step in any conservation project is to collect the information and assess its physical condition before any action or intervention that causes the change in the objects/built environment. Moreover, the originality and functionality of this built heritage are threatened by various factors such as environmental agencies, natural hazards, fire, vandalism, urban development, and aging.These threats are exacerbated by several acute, practical challenges inherent to the Indian heritage documentation landscape, including restricted physical access to protected structures, safety constraints in assessing deteriorated buildings, and a frequent absence of reliable archival drawings or engineering records. Furthermore, the task is complicated by occlusions and data incompleteness caused by dense urban surroundings, which hinder comprehensive data capture.

Hence, methodical documentation of these heritage marvels through digitization is very crucial for the preservation of their historical value in the long-term interest of society. Heritage documentation is a continuous process enabling maintenance, monitoring and providing information for conservation and management. Pertaining to their different architectural styles, most heritage buildings have complex (non-parametric) geometries which make their digital documentation a very time-consuming process when done through conventional techniques. In case of historic buildings, the accurate measurement of their geometrical elements, characteristic features, quantification of structural configurations, extracting the overall building plan, identification of damages, micro cracks and other details is an crucial issue in their process of restoration and conservation. In this regard, the use of digital technologies in digitization, preservation, and extraction of semantic information is essential for heritage conservation and management. In addition, these data and information will serve as a digital record with improved visual perception as a memory to future generations for understanding and reconstruction process if required.

Nowadays, the most effective way to document versatile heritage sites spread over large areas is to use non-invasive imaging tools like 3D terrestrial laser scanning (TLS) and close-range photogrammetry to achieve accuracy and precision. Terrestrial LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) also referred to as terrestrial laser scanners (TLS) allows for the capture of high-accuracy point clouds overlaid with color imagery, providing both geometric and surface texture data. It is one of the most cutting-edge technologies available today used for 3D data collection of complex heritage sites/objects along with images providing both geometric and surface texture of the recorded features.However, a distinct research gap persists: while TLS and photogrammetry are established globally, comprehensive, accuracy-assessed digital documentation—particularly for endangered Indo-Saracenic architecture in India—remains underrepresented. There is an urgent need for rapid, accurate workflows tailored for large, complex structures vulnerable to decay, often before formal conservation plans are formulated. Compounding this is the limited application of immersive digital visualization tools for conservation planning within the Indian context.

This study addresses this gap and aims to digitally preserve and virtually present heritage monuments by reconstructing photo-realistic 3D models. Our contribution is a context-specific, end-to-end methodology that integrates high-resolution TLS and photographic documentation into a streamlined workflow. This workflow is designed to overcome the stated challenges, resulting in both actionable engineering drawings for conservation and public-facing immersive models for engagement The development of a virtual and interactive environment by integrating Virtual Reality (VR) with digital datasets in the form of 3D models, images, and maps not only supports documentation and maintenance but also enables digital exhibitions and virtual tours to promote digital heritage tourism.

Related work

The handbook of conservation of heritage buildings published by the Central Public Works Department (CPWD), Government of India defines ‘Conservation’ as all the processes of looking after a place to retain its historical and/or architectural and/or aesthetic and/or cultural significance1. To achieve comprehensive heritage conservation demands the utilization of cutting-edge tools and methodologies to effectively store, share, and oversee information pertaining to the historical, current, and prospective conditions of heritage structures becomes essential. Due to advent of digital workflows, 3D data collection methods based on laser scanners leading to development of realistic 3D virtual models of heritage buildings has become popular in the past two decades2–7.

Incase of large cultural heritage sites, terrestrial laser scanners have been deployed for complex 3D documentation to obtain high resolution digital models with accurate topographic plan including details of each structure and elements which will serve as a based for conservation and site monitoring8. These systems are mounted on a tripod and use reflected laser signals to generate millions of dense, discrete and high precision point cloud data. The point cloud data bears the coordinate information with respect to scanner position on the ground describing the measured geometry of the surrounding environment. In-order to cover large and complex heritage sites, the scanners has to be placed at multiple locations i.e. at selected stations.TLS quickly acquires the geometry of a large heritage sites due to its high operation speed, mobility, and accessibility. This Ground-based LIDAR technology captures the exterior and interior of the building producing datasets for digital documentation, digital conservation, management, damage detection and restoration works. Crucially, these accurate 3D models also form the essential foundation for advanced public engagement applications, including virtual reality, digital tourism, education, and information dissemination.The evolution of these digital assets from static reconstructions to platforms for immersive experience is a key trend, as highlighted in reviews of Extended Reality (XR) for cultural heritage, which chart the progression towards interactive and immersive public engagement9. In particular, comparative studies of Augmented Reality (AR) platforms demonstrate the practical potential for transforming such 3D models into accessible mobile applications for historic narrative and on-site interpretation10–12.

In-spite of high accuracy with increased level of details (LOD), deployment of TLS for large heritage sites is relatively ineffective due to its high operational time and tedious data processing. To overcome the challenges and to avoid the possible occlusions, nowadays TLS is being integrated with various technologies like Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs), Mobile Laser Scanners (MLS) and Satellite data for improved visualization and data acquisition13–16. Recent HBIM developments emphasize accuracy assessment between point clouds and modeled geometries17–19. Integration of TLS and photogrammetry has been widely applied for heritage documentation, offering hybrid models combining high metric fidelity with semantic enrichment20. These studies provide a foundation upon which the present work adapts and validates a context-specific workflow for Indo-Saracenic heritage.

Chennai, the capital of Tamil Nadu in South India, is renowned for its heritage buildings showcasing the distinctive Indo-Saracenic architectural style. The city, with its rich historical tapestry and colonial legacy, boasts an array of architectural marvels ranging from traditional Dravidian structures to grand colonial-era edifices. This study aims to comprehensively document the intricate Indo-Saracenic architecture, capturing its as-built condition, including structural elements, geometric details, and the extent of damages, defects, and decay. Employing advanced reality capture (RC) techniques such as photogrammetry and terrestrial laser scanning surveys, we will acquire precise data essential for developing a highly detailed 3D model. This model will facilitate interactive walkthroughs in an virtual reality environment, enabling precise marking and measurement. Furthermore, the extracted data will include detailed plans and sectional drawings, photographs of specific elements, and condition mapping that highlights the building’s deterioration. This information will be invaluable for supporting restoration and maintenance efforts.

Study area

The study was conducted within the premises of a 125-year-old Indo-Saracenic building complex located on NSC Bose Road, Broadway, Chennai, within the Madras High Court campus. This distinguished heritage building called old law college, designed by Henry Irwin, the then consulting architect, is renowned for its architectural significance and cultural importance with many distinguished alumni. It boasts a unique hexagonal design with six facades and magnificent domes at the entrance, enhancing its aesthetic appeal (Fig. 1). Functioning as a law college since 1899, the building is recognized as a Grade I heritage structure by the Chennai Metropolitan Development Authority.

This two-storied building covers an area approximately 2836 m2 and features four classrooms on each floor, oriented in all directions. The floors are interconnected by grand stairs, and the roof is adorned with 20 domes of varying sizes, showcasing the primary characteristics of Indo-Saracenic architecture. Despite its historical and architectural value, recent metro rail projects have inflicted significant damage, rendering the building unsafe and necessitating the relocation of the law college to a new campus. Since 2015, the building has been sealed and covered to prevent entry, remaining unused in preparation for necessary repair and restoration.

Methodology

In heritage buildings, the facades including materials used, their joints, types of windows and doors, balconies, stonework, woodwork, and other ornamental features—are crucial in reflecting the unique characteristics of each historic structure. In India, the lack of proper information and engineering details for most heritage buildings poses a significant challenge for their repair, restoration, and maintenance. While, TLS and photogrammetry are established techniques, their integrated deployment for the comprehensive digital documentation of endangered Indo-Saracenic architecture, a style particularly vulnerable to urban development in India, remains underrepresented in the literature. Although standard commercial tools were adopted, this study addresses this gap by presenting a context-specific holistic documentation workflow (Fig. 2) optimized for inaccessible, protected, and at risk heritage building, that is, the Old Law College in Chennai that had not been digitally documented at this level of detail before. This approach aims to provide comprehensive engineering data and semantic information to support restoration and management efforts. The documentation process begins with data collection, followed by point cloud data processing and analysis. Subsequently, this is followed by the production of results and the extraction of detailed information. The integration of TLS and photographic data ensured geometric completeness, while visualization in an immersive environment enabled virtual site inspection for restoration planning.

Techniques, equipment and software used

Terrestrial Laser Scanning also known as topographic LiDAR, is a ground-based active remote sensing technology extensively utilized in documentation, geodesy, photogrammetry, and various engineering applications. This system comprises a laser rangefinder that generates laser pulses and scans the surrounding environment by deflecting through oscillating and rotating mirrors. The reflected laser beam, received by the instrument, contains XYZ coordinate information of numerous points on the reflected surface, resulting in point cloud data. These points are referenced such that their origin aligns with the instrument center. The position and elevation information are precisely measured based on survey principles. However, the measurement accuracy is influenced by the intensity of the reflected laser pulses.

In this study, laser scanning was conducted using a Leica BLK360 (Fig. 3), a portable and compatible scanner weighing 1 kg, with dimensions of 165 mm in height and 100 mm in diameter. The complete setup, including the device, iPad, batteries, cables, and the bag, is lightweight and travel friendly, with a total weight of just 1.5 kg. The system operates with an 830 nm wavelength laser and features a field of view of 360° horizontally and 300° vertically around a fixed scan station. The Leica BLK360 scanner is capable of covering a maximum range of 60 m, capturing data at a frequency of 360,000 points per second. It offers an accuracy of 4 mm at a 10-m distance and 7 mm at a 20-m distance21. This 3D scanner is equipped with an integrated spherical imaging system that captures the built environment virtually using ‘time of flight dimension principle’, producing full-color panoramic images overlaid on a high-accuracy point cloud in standard, high, and fast resolutions.

A critical methodological constraint was the inability to employ Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) photogrammetry due to strict regulatory and security restrictions over the Madras High Court campus, a sensitive government and judicial complex. To compensate for the lack of aerial data, which would have been optimal for roof documentation, the geometry of roofs and domes was captured using the terrestrial scanner from interior upper floors and accessible terraces, supplemented by long-focal-length photographic documentation from adjacent structures. This adaptive approach ensured comprehensive coverage. To acquire the laser data in the field, Leica Cyclone Field 360 software is used. For data cleaning and creating an AR-supported walkthrough model, Cyclone Register 360 is employed. Additionally, a professional Nikon D850 45.7MP Digital SLR Camera was used for capturing close-range images. Autodesk Revit 2020 software is used for building the 3D models.

Close-range photographic documentation

The field documentation process commenced with capturing close-range images of the old law college building in the field. This photographic documentation spanned five days, with the primary objective of capturing the distinctive Indo-Saracenic architectural features of the building in its current state.A high-resolution Nikon D850 DSLR camera (45.7 MP) paired with a NIKKOR 24-70mm f/2.8E ED VR lens was employed to ensure the capture of fine-grained details critical for a qualitative assessment.

Images were acquired in RAW format with carefully selected parameters to optimize data quality for archival and analytical purposes. An aperture range of f/8 to f/11 was used to maximize the depth of field, ensuring sharpness across intricate architectural elements. To preserve image clarity, the ISO was maintained between 100 and 400, minimizing digital noise. Shutter speed was dynamically adjusted according to ambient lighting conditions to achieve correct exposure. Critically, a deliberate forward overlap of approximately 60-80 percentage was maintained between consecutive shots throughout the campaign.

The fundamental intent of this photographic survey was to document the architectural details, structural elements, the extent of damages and defects, and to provide source imagery for texture mapping and filling occlusions in the Terrestrial Laser Scanning (TLS) data.These images were primarily used for qualitative condition assessment rather than for generating an independent photogrammetric model. Consequently, this approach generated a thorough visual record, capturing the building’s unique architectural details while simultaneously identifying areas requiring attention (as shown in Figs. 4, 5, 6 and 7)

Terrestrial laser scanning survey

To scan the entire building, the TLS survey has to be carried out at multiple scan stations covering both exterior and interior (see Fig. 8). Each scan station captures the surrounding environment within the TLS system’s maximum range. Successive scan stations are strategically selected to ensure sufficient overlap with the preceding scanned area, facilitating accurate registration through the Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm. The number and placement of scan stations depend on the scanner’s field of view, potential obstructions, nature of the scanned surfaces and the building’s complexity. Therefore, careful planning is essential prior to the field survey to achieve optimal point cloud data coverage. Point-cloud data processing

The raw data collected using a terrestrial laser scanner (TLS) consists of a discrete set of data points representing the reflected surfaces in 3D coordinates, commonly referred to as ”point cloud data”. This raw point cloud data, initially stored in the scanner system, is subsequently transferred to a computer system for further processing. In this project, Leica Cyclone software was utilized for post-processing the millions of point cloud data points. For each scan, the point cloud data collected by the TLS was referenced to the instrument’s center, i.e., the scan station, thereby georeferencing it in a local coordinate system. The first step in processing involves establishing the spatial relationship between scan stations (pair by pair) through a process known as ”registration”. Registration can be performed using two approaches: target-based registration and surface-based registration. Target-based registration aligns scans based on common targets between them. While this method offers high accuracy, it is impractical for heritage documentation due to several reasons:The vast area and structural complexity of heritage buildings make it challenging to place the numerous targets required to link the scan stations.The target area must be scanned at high resolution, significantly increasing the time required for each scan and making the data collection process time-consuming.Placing targets on high walls, inaccessible areas, and in open environments adds to the complexity.In this study, the point cloud registration utilized a surface-based methodology through a proprietary variant of the Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm within Leica Register 360. The process began with a coarse alignment of scan stations via visual inspection. Following this initialization, the algorithm automatically detected common planar surfaces and progressively refined their alignment by iteratively minimizing distances between corresponding points, linking individual scans into comprehensive bundles (Fig. 11). To ensure global accuracy and mitigate cumulative error, a final optimization pass was applied to the entire dataset, with a mandated minimum 40% scan overlap further safeguarding geometric integrity. This iterative refinement continued until all convergence residuals fell within strict instrumental tolerances, guaranteeing a reliable and precisely aligned final model. This procedure was repeated for each set of scans, linking them to create a comprehensive dataset bundle. Following this, an Iterative Closest Point (ICP) based registration was applied to the entire dataset, optimizing the bundles and minimizing the alignment error (Fig. 11).

The deployment of a geodetic control network with physical targets was precluded for this project due to the building’s sealed status and overriding safety concerns, which restricted site access. Consequently, a local coordinate system was established using the southwest corner of the structure as a reference point. In the absence of physical targets, which provide the most rigorous error estimation, the registration process relied on the software’s surface-based Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm. The geometric integrity of the resulting point cloud was rigorously validated through iterative alignment checks for each scan pair. This methodology achieved a high degree of internal consistency, with a mean registration error of 4.2 mm, maintained below the project threshold of 6 mm22. This level of precision is consistent with the scanner’s specified accuracy and was deemed fully sufficient for the purposes of conservation planning and structural modeling ensuring geometric consistency across the dataset even in absence of an external control network23. Due to the high data volume, it is advantageous to create separate sub point cloud datasets i.e. bundles for processing and linking them together at the final stage.

In addition to the registration process, the datasets must be cleaned of noise, which includes irrelevant objects and reflections from mirrors or shiny materials that are not pertinent to documenting the built heritage. This project addresses obstructions such as trees, people, cables, cars, and other unwanted materials by utilizing fencing tools and smooth surface tool options, as illustrated in Figs. 12 and 13. Reflections from windows, mirrors, or any reflective surfaces are identified using the limit box and by viewing the point cloud data based on their reflected intensities, which helps in delineating and removing these unwanted reflections (Fig. 14). The registration and cleaning of scans can be performed on individual scans, sets of scans, bundles, or the entire point cloud dataset, depending on the volume of noise to be cleaned (as shown in Fig. 15). Cleaning the entire dataset reduces the cleaning time, as it allows for the removal of noise from multiple scans simultaneously; however, this approach may result in the loss of individual scan files.

For scans conducted in outdoor environments, moving objects such as vehicles and people can be detected and removed using the Detect Moving Object filter (Fig. 16). Although some artifacts in scans can be removed automatically, the majority of the cleaning process remains manual, tedious, and time-consuming. In this project, the registration and noise cleaning processes were performed for a total of 330 scans (Fig. 17), taking nearly seven days to complete. To export the processed data into software’s like Autodesk Revit, CAD etc for further modelling a specific co-ordinate system is created corresponding to the left corner of the building. Since all scan stations were positioned at ground level, the entire roof area could not be covered, resulting in missing data for certain parts of the building facades. Fortunately, the use of close-range photographs enabled us to obtain the necessary data for these areas.

3D reconstruction of heritage structures

Creating accurate 3D models representing architectural and structural elements is essential for the restoration and management of heritage buildings. These models provide crucial support for professionals and stakeholders in their decision-making processes. In this work, Autodesk Revit 2020 is used as it is utilized due to its capability to compile millions of data points and relevant images from the site, resulting in highly detailed 3D models.The process begins with exporting the registered and cleaned 3D point cloud dataset from Leica Cyclone software into Revit. This dataset is then aligned with a pre-defined coordinate system to ensure accuracy.

The modeling process starts with defining the levels of each floor. Since panoramic images are obtained alongside the point cloud data, RGB colors are assigned to the data to enhance realism.The next step involves defining the building’s facades in a simplified manner. Following this, the building walls are modeled, and floors are defined. Once the walls are constructed, visibility settings are adjusted to allow the addition of doors and windows through the point cloud. Openings in the verandah and walls are subsequently added. Architectural components are carefully selected and fitted into the model to match the heritage building. Railings and steps are then added to the staircase, followed by the construction of ceilings. This process is repeated for each floor, ensuring consistency and accuracy. Finally, roofs are added, and all the domes in the terrace area are modeled.

Following model generation, A deviation analysis was subsequently conducted to validate the geometric accuracy of the generated Revit model. This was achieved using ReCap Pro’s surface comparison tool, which performed a continuous visual overlay and direct comparison between the model and the registered point cloud, supplemented by high-resolution panoramic imagery.The analysis, which quantified the differences across primary structural elements, yielded a mean deviation range of 8–12 mm. This result falls within the established acceptable tolerances for Heritage Building Information Modeling (HBIM) confirming that the model possesses the geometric reliability required for informed conservation and restoration planning17,18 . Once the 3D models are complete, textures for the different facades are prepared using close-range photographs and panoramic images. This comprehensive approach ensures that the final 3D model is a precise and realistic representation of the heritage building, aiding in its preservation and management.

This immersive platform transcends basic visualization, offering a virtual inspection and decision-making support tool for distributed stakeholders. Conservation architects can conduct remote condition audits, structural engineers can perform preliminary assessments, and project managers can plan interventions within a spatially accurate context, thereby reducing the need for potentially hazardous physical access during the planning stages.

The present approach for the digital documentation of heritage buildings involves advanced 3D high-definition surveying techniques such as terrestrial laser scanning, close-range photogrammetry, and 3D modeling. This approach is designed to support the conservation, restoration, and management of built heritage structures.The key metrics for this data acquisition and processing workflow are summarized in Table 1 .While the workflow encounters operational challenges typical of complex heritage sites such as occlusions from permanent obstructions, restricted access to fragile areas, extensive data volumes, and high computational demands, these limitations were effectively managed within the adopted methodology. Geometric fidelity was quantitatively assessed through internal consistency measures, with registration accuracy evaluated using residuals from bundle adjustment and scan alignment, and model deviation analyzed by comparing the meshed model with the registered point cloud. The results demonstrate alignment and modeling accuracy within ±5 mm, meeting the tolerances required for detailed condition assessment, architectural documentation, and quantity estimation in conservation projects. Although external control validation was constrained by site conditions, the internal accuracy assessment provides a reliable and robust foundation for engineering-grade heritage documentation and long-term information management.

These methodological challenges and accuracy outcomes find resonance in recent international studies employing TLS and HBIM for heritage documentation. The achieved sub-centimeter accuracy is consistent with benchmarks set by similar projects on complex facades, confirming the suitability of the workflow for conservation. Compared to studies at sites with fewer access restrictions, the present approach demonstrates a tailored strategy for navigating high-risk or partially inaccessible environments, prioritizing optimal scanner placement to mitigate occlusions. Furthermore, while many documented workflows culminate in HBIM or static models, this study explicitly extends the pipeline to include immersive visualization as a direct tool for stakeholder engagement and virtual inspection, a step less frequently elaborated in technical literature.

The process involves capturing survey data using state-of-the-art laser scanning and close-range photography, ranging from general to detailed scales, and integrating this data with quantitative and qualitative heritage information. This integration explores new possibilities for managing cultural heritage data through digital platforms. The developed 3D models and 2D drawings from the high-definition survey data support planning, navigation, documentation, and preservation of cultural heritage.

Furthermore, the application facilitates measurements and quantitative estimation, offering an efficient method for assessing pre- and post-restoration works, thus supporting engineers and contractors. The developed photo-realistic virtual reality (VR) walk through models create an immersive and interactive environment. These models support the addition of geotagged images and markups, enabling project sharing and collaboration among stakeholders.These walk-through models, enriched with as-built heritage data and information, will attract not only experts such as architects, engineers, heritage restorers, and historians but also non-experts like tourists, students, and the general public.

It is important to note that this immersive walkthrough model, developed using the proprietary Leica TruView platform, is accessible via project-based sharing links. In accordance with heritage protection and security protocols, public access is restricted; however, the model is available for academic and professional evaluation upon reasonable request, as detailed in the Data Availability statement.

Conclusions

The documentation and modelling of built heritage sites is often constrained by detailed mapping due to extensive area and complexity of the structures. In this study, we presented a novel approach that integrates the latest digital technologies, including 3D surveys using laser scanners, close-range photogrammetry, 3D modeling, and Virtual reality (VR) assisted walk-through models. This case study demonstrates the practical effectiveness of a tailored, hybrid TLS-photogrammetry approach yielding robust outcomes for heritage at risk.It successfully overcame significant site-specific challenges, such as restricted access and the absence of archival drawings, to generate a multi-purpose digital dataset from a single survey campaign. This integrated methodology efficiently produced both high-accuracy engineering outputs (2D drawings, 3D models for condition assessment) and public engagement tools (immersive walkthroughs), thereby addressing the dual needs of conservation professionals and the public.

A critical reflection on the methodology acknowledges that the absence of a geodetic control network presented a limitation. However, this constraint was a deliberate trade-off, balanced against the imperative for rapid data acquisition, a common challenge in emergency heritage recording. The reliance on robust internal registration metrics provided a defensible substitute, demonstrating a level of precision within the instrument’s tolerance that was sufficient for comprehensive documentation. Ultimately, this approach effectively serves the primary objective of raising awareness and reinforcing the value of preserving such heritage structures. The developed 3D models provide accurate engineering information, detailing structural elements and architectural characteristics of the built heritage. These models can be integrated into virtual environments to create virtual reality experiences, which facilitate the dissemination of information to non-expert users and the interested public, thereby enhancing cultural tourism and digital access to cultural heritage.

The resulting assets—from precise point clouds and annotated 3D models to immersive VR environments—establish a foundational digital record. This will directly support the long-term maintenance, monitoring, and management of the heritage building, aiding stakeholders in informed decision-making for conservation, restoration, and management projects.

Looking forward, this work opens several clear avenues for future research and development. Subsequent efforts could focus on: (a) integrating the 3D model with sensor data within a Historic Building Information Modelling (HBIM) framework to create a dynamic 4D (space + time) management system for structural health monitoring; (b) developing a simplified, web-based or mobile Augmented Reality (AR) viewer derived from this model to enhance on-site interpretation and visitor experience; and (c) exploring automated point cloud segmentation algorithms to increase the efficiency of modelling complex, repetitive architectural elements. Ultimately, this integrated approach not only achieves the primary objective of creating a detailed archival record but also reinforces the value of digital preservation and provides a replicable framework for safeguarding other at-risk heritage structures.
