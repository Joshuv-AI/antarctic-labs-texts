Advanced carbon dot- and quantum dot-based sensors for lead ion detection: a comprehensive review
Authors: Neelam Sharma, Suman Swami, Ved Prakash Verma, Rahul Shrivastava
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lead ion contamination in water sources poses severe threats to the environment and human health owing to its high toxicity, long-term persistence and nonbiodegradability. Natural events as well as diverse industrial and unavoidable human activities such as battery manufacturing, metal plating, use of cosmetics, recycling of electronic waste, consumer products, volcanoes, and corrosion of service pipes are some common sources of lead ion contamination. It has been well established that exposure to any ionic form of lead causes severe systemic toxicity and carries carcinogenic risks. Therefore, the development of potent innovative technologies or methodologies for detecting toxic lead ions is necessary for the protection of human health and environmental monitoring. Recent developments have highlighted the advancement of innovative analytical methodologies for detecting lead ions, focusing on enhanced sensitivity, rapid detection, accuracy and sustainability. The emergence of carbon-based nanomaterials, particularly carbon dots, carbon quantum dots and graphene quantum dots, has established them as promising candidates for designing efficient sensing materials for the detection of lead ions because of their biocompatibility, superior photoluminescence, tuneable surface chemistry, low detection limits and high sensitivity. Along with these carbon-based nanomaterials, quantum dots have also been established as benchmark semiconductor nanomaterials for sensing applications because of their distinct electro-optical properties arising from quantum confinement effects. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the current developments in carbon dots, carbon quantum dots, graphene quantum dots, quantum dots and their composites as zero-dimensional nanosensors for the detection of Pb2+ ions, focusing on their functionalization techniques, synthesis methods, sensing mechanisms and analytical capabilities. Special attention is placed on “turn-on/turn-off” systems, ratiometric sensors and hybrid nanocomposites to enhance stability and selectivity for lead ion detection, highlighting their crucial role in safeguarding the environment and public health.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of urbanization, industrialization and agricultural activities significantly improves the quality of life and provides necessary facilities for human beings. Despite several advantages of rapid industrialization and urbanization, the continuous discharge of harmful toxins and effluents from industries, agricultural activities and domestic waste without any pretreatment poses severe risks to human health and other living organisms.1,2 Heavy metal ions (HMIs) are major pollutants in these harmful toxins and effluents released from industry.3 Contamination of soil and drinking water with toxic heavy metals has become a significant and urgent problem that needs to be addressed because of the toxicity, nonbiodegradability and persistence of HMIs in soil and groundwater resources, posing severe threats to ecosystems and human health even at low concentrations.4 The carelessness and negligence in the management of wastewater disposal are the main contributing factors to heavy metal ion pollution, which eventually contaminates drinking water and enters the food chain.5 More importantly, heavy metal ions have the ability to alter the structures of several proteins, which can cause serious health hazards. Arsenic, lead and mercury are among the most toxic metals to most living organisms because of their poor degradability, toxicity, long-term persistence, carcinogenicity and high tendency of bioaccumulation in living organisms.6 These ions pose several hazardous risks to humans even at very low concentrations, including irreversible brain impairment, development impairments in adolescents, neurological and cardiovascular disorders, and sometimes mental retardation.7–9 Pollution by lead ions is considered one of the most serious global environmental issues affecting human health.10,11 Diverse industrial activities like metal plating, battery manufacturing, and oil refining, and the extensive use of cosmetics, as well as consumer products, like paints, electronics, pigments, petrol, alloys, ceramics, glass, and toys, along with natural events such as volcanoes and weathering, are common sources of lead ion pollution in environmental matrices (soil, water and air).12–16 Lead ions enter the human body through ingestion, inhalation and dermal adsorption and accumulate in the body over a lifetime, inducing serious health risks.17 It has been documented that any form of lead ions has highly carcinogenic and toxicological effects on the human body, which not only harms hematopoiesis, neuron, the immune system, the digestive system, the kidneys, and other organs but also causes changes in the number of functional cells, resulting in major health hazards, because lead ions are capable of forming covalent/coordination bonds with bio-ligands containing sulfur, oxygen and nitrogen functional groups.18,19 The formation of these bonds disrupts most non-covalent interactions (i.e. hydrogen bonds) and alters protein structures, inhibiting enzyme activity and impairing nucleic acid function and thereby posing a major health risk, especially for children. Considering the detrimental effects of lead ions, the maximum permissible concentration of lead ions in drinking water is limited to 10.0 µg L−1 by the World Health Organization.20 Considering these hazardous effects, the detection and removal of lead ions with high sensitivity are necessary for waste management, water safety and environmental protection. Although conventional analytical techniques, such as ICP-AES, AAS, ICP-MS and electrochemical techniques, are available for lead ion detection with high accuracy and sensitivity, most of these techniques/methods are associated with several drawbacks such as use of expensive instrumentation, high cost, difficult sample pretreatment process, time-consuming analysis and requirement of trained operators.21–25 All the limitations associated with conventional methods have encouraged scientists to investigate and develop more effective alternatives for monitoring lead ions in drinking water and environment.26 To fulfill the demand for rapid, selective, highly sensitive, field-applicable and miniaturized detection techniques for lead ions in diverse media, numerous analytical techniques based on colorimetry, fluorescence, electrochemical, SERS, and SPR have been explored.27,28 Among them, owing to several benefits such as high sensitivity, straightforward operation, lower detection limits, cost effectiveness and good accuracy, fluorescence and electrochemical detection approaches have emerged as simple and advantageous alternatives.29–31 Therefore, in recent years, tremendous progress has been made in the development of environmentally friendly fluorescent materials that exhibit outstanding sensitivity and selectivity for the detection of Pb2+ ions.32,33 Fluorescent nanomaterials have drawn lot of interest in the scientific community since these materials offer advantages like high adsorption capacity, large surface area, small size, quantum confinement effects, facile surface functionalization, surface reactivity and sensitivity.20,34 In designing fluorescent materials for sensing applications, a wide range of sensing materials including metallic organic frameworks, metallic nanoclusters, metal or metal oxide nanoparticles, quantum dots (QDs) and carbon-based nanomaterials, such as carbon dots (CDs) carbon quantum dots (CQDs), graphene and graphene quantum dots (GQDs), have been explored in the last few decades. Among the various types of nanomaterials, quantum dots (QDs) and carbon-based nanomaterials have emerged as promising nanomaterials for the detection of toxic analytes because of their remarkable characteristics including mechanical strength, tunable optical properties, thermal stability, superior biocompatibility and non-toxicity.35 Carbon dots (CDs) are important members of the family of carbon-based nanomaterials and are considered promising candidates for the development of efficient nanosensors for various sensing applications.36 Zero-dimensional carbon dots show distinct benefits over other nano and bulk materials, primarily because of their nanoscale dimensions of less than 10 nm, relatively superior fluorescence properties, higher biocompatibility, excellent photostability and precisely tunable photoluminescent properties.37,38 The selectivity of carbon dots can be easily fine-tuned according to the requirements of the target analytes because of the presence of intrinsic hydroxyl, carboxyl and epoxy functional groups that can be easily modified.45 Moreover, surface functional groups containing oxygen and nitrogen atoms impart water solubility to carbon dots, thereby enhancing their applicability primarily in biomedical, analytical and photoelectronic fields.39 For instance, strong lead thiol interactions and effective fluorescence quenching processes have enabled thiol-functionalized carbon dot-derived hybrid systems (e.g., CD/Au–MOF composite films) to exhibit ultra-trace detection capabilities at parts-per-trillion levels.40 Similarly, sub-nanomolar detection limits (as low as ∼0.14 nM) have been achieved for biomass-derived CDs, indicating their promise as affordable and sustainable sensing platforms.41,42 In addition to their sensitivity, these systems work exceptionally well in real sample analysis, including water matrices, with little interference from competing ions. Collectively, these developments show how CD-based nanosensors are moving away from traditional detection methods and toward mechanistically informed design and application-oriented optimization, highlighting their increasing importance in analytical chemistry and environmental monitoring.43–48 Overall, the remarkable photobleaching resistance, photostability, versatile sensitivity exceptional optical properties and high quantum yield of carbon dots place them at the forefront of diverse sensing applications, including biosensing, bioimaging, gene and drug delivery, and environmental monitoring, as depicted in Fig. 1.49–54

Potential applications of carbon dots (CDs) and carbon quantum dots (CQDs).

The viability of employing carbon dots in sensing applications for the detection of environmental contaminants, such as hazardous cations, small biomolecules, anions, and organic pollutants, has been extensively explored in a wide range of review articles.55 In contrast, a comprehensive review of the literature revealed that the potential of carbon dot- (CD) and quantum dot (QD)-derived nanomaterials for sensing lead ions remains unexplored despite the extraordinary development of carbon dot- and quantum dot (QD)-derived fluorescent nanomaterials for sensing lead ions observed in the last few years. Considering these facts, the compilation of reported nanosensors derived from zero-dimensional carbon dots and quantum dots in a comprehensive review focusing on lead ion detection is an important requirement for the thorough investigation of the potential of carbon dots and quantum dots in lead ion sensing. Therefore, the present review article explores the synthetic methodologies, characteristics, fundamental sensing mechanisms and latest advancements in this rapidly evolving research area.

Carbon dots, quantum dots and their classification

In nanotechnology, carbon-based nanoparticles represent a special category, exhibiting unique physicochemical properties and playing noteworthy roles in modern advanced nanotechnologies. Their specific photophysical properties and facile synthesis procedures are frequently utilized in several important fields such as chemistry, materials science, electronics, mechanics, biomedicine, sensors, solar energy storage, and additives.56 Carbon dots (CDs) are subsets of carbon nanomaterials having ultrafine diameters of less than 10 nm, making them attractive options for a variety of applications in diverse fields, especially in sensor applications due to their distinctive fluorescent properties, biocompatibility and multifunctionality.57 Moreover, the presence of several carboxyl and hydroxyl functional groups on the surface of carbon dots enables their easy and straightforward functionalization with a variety of chemical species, making them suitable for sensing applications in aqueous media. The types and quantities of oxygen-containing functional groups vary according to the synthesis route employed for the fabrication of carbon dots. Carbon dots were first identified in 2004 during the separation and filtration of single-walled carbon nanotubes.58 In 2006, carbon dots were produced using simpler methods, but their low quantum yields resulted in limited applications. To improve the practical applications of carbon dots, high quantum efficiency is an essential requirement. In recent years, the majority of limitations associated with carbon dots have been resolved and several alternative synthetic methods for carbon dots/modified carbon dots have been introduced with outstanding performance in various fields.59 Carbon dots are generally classified into three categories according to their molecular size and synthetic method: (i) carbon quantum dots (CQDs), (ii) graphene quantum dots (GQDs), and (iii) carbonized polymer dots (CPDs). Carbon quantum dots (CQDs) were obtained during the work-up procedure of single-walled carbon nanotubes, and they had a particle size of 10.0 nm and exhibited superior photostability, biocompatibility and quantum efficiency, making them suitable for drug delivery, bioimaging and sensor applications.60,61 Graphene quantum dots (GQDs) are made up of mono- or multi-layer nano-sized graphite and their surface functional groups or interlayer defects are anisotropic with lateral dimensions greater than their height. The size of the π-conjugated domains and surface/edge structures primarily determine their optical properties.62,63,66 In contrast to GQDs, carbonized polymer dots (CPDs) and carbon quantum dots (CQDs) have a spherical core that is connected to surface groups. The spherical core of CQDs shows multiple-layer graphite structures. Their photoluminescence (PL) emission properties are mainly dominated by size-dependent quantum confinement effects and intrinsic-state luminescence.60 Additionally, their multi-wave excitation and electrochemical properties are helpful in improving sensing applications.64 All these properties of CQDs establish these materials as potential candidates for designing efficient nanosensor materials for application in lead ion sensing.65 Carbonized polymer dots (CPDs) are hybrid nanostructures made of polymer chain shells and aggregated/cross-linked carbon cores. Their optical characteristics are primarily dominated by their cross-linking structure and molecular state. Similarly, semiconductor nanocrystals are known as inorganic quantum dots (QDs) having sizes ranging from 1 to 100 nm. Quantum dots are also considered as promising sensor material because of their high quantum yield and photostability. The fluorescent properties are dependent on the size of quantum dots. These specific properties enable quantum dots to be used for the highly selective, sensitive, real-time and accurate detection of lead ion in aqueous media and biological samples.67

General synthetic methods for carbon dots (CDs)

The fluorescence properties, photostability, biocompatibility and quantum yield of carbon dots mainly depend on the carbon source, synthetic methods, size and purification methods. Carbon dots can be synthesized either top-down or bottom-up approaches. In the top-down synthetic process, carbon dots are prepared from the decomposition of large carbon structures into nanoparticles. Electrochemical methods and laser ablation are the most utilized and well-known top-down synthesis processes for the fabrication of carbon dots. In the bottom-up synthetic process, carbon dots are prepared from molecular precursors via pyrolysis upon heating. Ultrasound- and microwave-assisted methods are commonly utilized in this approach for the synthesis of carbon dots, as depicted in Fig. 2. The synthesis of carbon dots utilizing the bottom-up approach generally provides an amorphous carbon core, several doping sites and surface functional groups. The properties of carbon dots are controlled by temperature, microwave power, precursor type and reaction time. Recently, natural precursors, such as plant materials, fruit juices, leaves, food items, and waste biomass, have been used for the synthesis of carbon dots, and the surface of the synthesized nanomaterials has been further modified via doping or functionalization with polymeric or organic molecules to improve their sensing capability, especially for lead ions.68,69

Mechanisms of metal ion sensing by carbon dot sensors

Carbon dots (CDs) have several applications ranging from biomedical applications, such as gene delivery, drug delivery and bioimaging probes, to other versatile applications in photovoltaic cells, photocatalysis, chemiluminescence, fluorescent inks, electrochemiluminescence, light-emitting diodes, optical sensors, etc. In recent years, CDs have been applied in the development of precise sensor systems with limits of detection in the nanomolar or even picomolar range.70,71 This section aims to briefly discuss the mechanisms of metal ion sensing using different types of CD sensors. Although numerous hypotheses exist, the precise mechanism of metal ion sensing by carbon dots (CDs) remains a subject of debate among chemists. Some proposed mechanisms include coordination/complexation, ion binding and/or aggregation, the inner filter effect (IFE) and light-induced electron transfer (LET). Most carbon dot sensors operate based on fluorescence quenching or the prevention of quenching triggered by the addition of metal ions. These functional groups have the ability to form coordination complexes with metal ions by donating their lone pairs of electrons to metal ions, thereby quenching the fluorescence of CDs. The fluorescence emission quenching might be due to charge-transfer processes. The quenching mechanisms of CD sensors for detecting metal ions include static quenching, dynamic quenching, fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET), the inner-filter effect (IFE), photoinduced electron transfer (PET), coordination/complexation, light-induced electron transfer (LET), and ion binding and/or aggregation.72,73

Static and dynamic mechanisms of CDs

Static quenching in CDs arises from interactions between a quencher and CDs, which result in the formation of a nonfluorescent ground-state complex. The generation of a ground-state complex during static quenching alters the absorption spectrum of CDs. In static quenching, elevated temperatures may destabilize the ground-state complex, thereby diminishing the overall quenching effect.74 In contrast, dynamic quenching refers to the quenching mechanism in which collisions between CDs and a quencher facilitate the return of excited-state CDs to their ground state via charge or energy-transfer mechanisms. Unlike static quenching, dynamic quenching does not change the absorption spectrum of CDs and only impacts the excited state, while an increase in temperature results in an enhancement of the dynamic quenching effect. Furthermore, the fluorescence lifetime of CDs is altered by the presence of a quencher compared to that in its absence.

Photoinduced electron transfer (PET) quenching mechanism of CDs

In the photoinduced electron transfer (PET) process, electron transfer occurs between an electron donor and an electron acceptor, which leads to the formation of a complex between them that can return to the ground state without photon emission. In oxidative photoinduced electron transfer (PET), CDs behave as an electron donor and donate electrons to an electron receptor, whereas in reductive PET, CDs behave as an electron receptor and receive electron from a donor. The energy gap between the highest occupied molecular orbitals (HOMO) of CDs and lowest unoccupied molecular orbitals (LUMO) of the quencher is main driving force for reductive electron transfer.74,75

Fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) mechanism of CDs

Fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) is a radiationless energy transfer process involving long-range dipole–dipole coupling, in which energy is transferred from a donor fluorophore with high energy (i.e., in an excited state) to an acceptor with lower energy when they are in close proximity (typically 1–10 nm), leading to an enhancement of the fluorescence of the acceptor and quenching the fluorescence of the donor.76 Initially, the emission of carbon dots (CDs) is suppressed by the presence of a quencher in close proximity. However, the introduction of the analyte competitively displaces the quenching dye, leading to recovery of the CD fluorescence.77

Inner-filter effect (IFE) mechanism of CDs

In carbon dot sensors based on ICT, PET, FRET or electronic energy transfer (EET), a precise distance must be maintained between the fluorophore and the acceptor (quencher) to facilitate effective energy or electron transfer. In some cases, this requirement can lead to complicated detection procedures and time-consuming operations. An alternative to the mechanisms discussed above is the use of sensors based on the inner-filter effect (IFE) mechanism, which results from the overlap of the emission spectrum of CDs with the absorption spectrum of a quencher.78 In fact, IFE is not a genuine quenching effect but rather results from the attenuation of an excitation beam or the reabsorption of emitted radiation by additional absorbing species or by an extra fluorophore in the solution. Although IFE causes a decrease in signal intensity without lifetime decay, it is often misclassified as quenching. Instead, it is the result of a secondary absorber attenuating the excitation or emission light. Thus, the absorption response leads to exponential changes in fluorescence intensity. Moreover, IFE does not require covalent linking, energy or electron transfer, surface functionalization, complex probe synthesis, etc. Additionally, the inner-filter effect (IFE) can occur despite the spatial separation between the absorber and emitter being greater than 10 nm.79

Aggregation-induced enhanced emission (AIEE) mechanism

In the aggregation-induced enhanced emission (AIEE) process, non-luminescent molecules in solution are induced to exhibit stronger emission upon aggregation.74 The aggregation-induced emission (AIE) phenomenon relies on the restricted molecular motion of molecules in the aggregated state, which suppresses nonradiative energy dissipation and significantly enhances fluorescence efficiency.80,81 If freely rotating groups in an organic fluorophore rotate in solution, the excited-state energy is dissipated instead of being emitted as light. When these molecules aggregate, their rotation is constrained, resulting in enhanced fluorescence.

Light-induced electron-transfer (LET) mechanism

In the light-induced electron-transfer (LET) process, electrons from CD sensors are transferred to metal ions, leading to the formation of an excited-state complex. This excited-state complex then relaxes to the ground state without emitting any photons. In transition metals, where d-orbitals are responsible for bonding, the LUMO of the receptor (CDs) lies at a lower energy level than that of the fluorophore.82

Carbon dot- (CD) and carbon quantum dot (CQD)-derived sensors for the detection of lead ions

The recent development of carbon dots (CDs) as potential fluorescent nanomaterials for lead ion detection is attributed to their unique and favorable properties, including aqueous solubility, environmentally friendly nature, minimal toxicity, chemical stability, compatibility with biological systems, and robust optical properties, such as broad emission spectra, that enhance their sensing efficacy.83–86 However, carbon dot-derived fluorescence sensors often exhibit limited sensitivity and selectivity toward specific target analytes, mainly due to insufficient surface functionalization. These limitations can be addressed via strategic surface modifications through heteroatom doping and suitable functionalization, which produce novel carbon dots possessing enhanced fluorescence efficiency, improved adsorption capabilities, and an increased number of active sites, thereby enhancing their sensing performance toward lead ions.

Carbon dot (CD)-based fluorescence sensors for the detection of lead ions

A carbon dot-based fluorescent biosensor (BNCD/TbMOF@GR5 DNAzyme) was innovatively developed to achieve the accurate quantification of lead ions in water using a DNAzyme system (Fig. 3). The developed nanosensor consists of two major parts: (a) a nitrogen- and boron-doped carbon dot carboxyl-functionalized terbium metal–organic framework serving as the fluorescent signaling unit, and (b) a quencher-modified catalytic NH3-GR5 DNAzyme as the biosensing unit. The reported nanosensor demonstrated weak fluorescent intensity due to the overlapping emissions between BNCDs@Tb-MOF and the NH3-GR5 DNAzyme. However, lead ions activated the catalytic core of the DNAzyme strand, resulting in the cleavage of the quencher-tagged substrate strand, which restored the fluorescence emission of BNCDs/Tb-MOF. The notable change in the fluorescence of the biosensor (BNCD/TbMOF@GR5 DNAzyme) was represented as an “ON”/“OFF” fluorescence switch, with the fluorescence being activated in the presence of Pb2+ ions and deactivated in their absence. The reported sensor exhibited a low LOD of 5.97 nM over a wide linear range (1 to 1000 nM).87

Further, a fast and versatile method was reported for the synthesis of an FRET ratiometric fluorescent probe via the electrostatic assembly of negatively charged gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) and amino-carbon dots (N-CDs). The N-CDs with a large number of amine groups on their surface served as an energy donor, whereas glutathione-capped AuNCs served as an energy acceptor. Finally, AuNCs and N-CDs were connected via electrostatic interactions in water without the use of any other linkers. This report claimed that electrostatic interactions are more practical and convenient for generating FRET probes than the covalent bonding approach and provide preferable detection outcomes for dual targets. This study described a simple method for the preparation of inexpensive FRET probes that enable accurate ratiometric quantification and visual semi-quantitative measurement of Pb2+ ions with a detection limit of 5.0 × 103 nM.88 Further, another dual-emission carbon dot-based ratiometric probe was fabricated for the sensitive determination of lead ions. The reported sensor exhibits dual emission peaks at 477 and 651 nm upon excitation at a single wavelength of 391 nm. The fabricated carbon dots detect lead ions because of the ability of lead ions to quench the fluorescence of carbon dots at 651 nm while maintaining their fluorescence at 471 nm constant. The mechanistic investigation by FT-IR, UV-visible and fluorescence lifetime studies revealed that the fluorescence at 651 nm was quenched by Pb2+ ions due to the formation of nonfluorescent metal chelates between Pb2+ ions and various functional groups, such as carboxylate and amino groups, on surface of the CDs (Scheme 1). The dual-emission sensor demonstrated excellent selectivity toward Pb2+ ions with a detection limit of 55 nM.89

Magnetic and photoluminescence (PL) cellulose acetate nanofibers/Fe/carbon dots were synthesized using electrospinning, sonochemical, ball-milling and hydrothermal processes for Pb2+ ion detection. During the formation of the sensing material, iron nanoparticles were produced by the ball-milling method, whereas cellulose acetate nanofibers were synthesized via an electrospinning process (Fig. 4). The photoluminescent carbon dots were fabricated from ethylene diamine and lemon extract through the hydrothermal process. The iron nanoparticles were introduced into the polymeric fibers, and carbon dots were coated on the fibers via a sonochemical-assisted hydrothermal method to produce the magnetic and photoluminescence (PL) cellulose acetate nanofibers/Fe/carbon dots. The iron nanoparticles embedded within the fibres were responsible for the magnetic properties, whereas the carbon dots adsorbed on the surface were responsible for the photoluminescence. The detection studies showed that the fluorescence intensity was significantly reduced upon interaction with Pb2+ ions, as the d orbitals of Pb2+ ions accept electrons from the activated/excited carbon dots and form a complex that reduces the fluorescence intensity of the carbon dots. The developed sensor efficiently detects toxic Pb2+ ions in industrial wastewater. Additionally, its magnetic filler enables easy recovery of the sensor, eliminating potential environmental hazards associated with the use of nanomaterials.90

A novel synergistic catalytic-amplifying fluorescence technique was developed for Pb2+ ion detection. Carbon dots (CDs) and gold-doped carbon dots (CDAu) were quickly fabricated via a microwave irradiation process using HAuCl4 as a doping agent and glucose as a carbon source. Both the DNAzyme and CDAu of the Pb2+-aptamer-heme complex catalyze the fluorescence indicator reaction between H2O2 and 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) to generate oxidized TMB (TMBox), which exhibits a fluorescent peak at 404 nm. The aptamer adsorbs onto the surface of CDAu to produce a CDAu-Apt conjugate, which inhibits the catalytic reaction and reduces the fluorescence intensity. The Pb2+ target interacted with the aptamer to create a stable G-quadruplex complex, resulting in the release of CDAu, while the G-quadruplex reacted with heme to create the G-quadruplex/heme DNAzyme. The DNAzyme and free CDAu acted synergistically to produce TMBox, and fluorescence was significantly improved.91 Liu et al. developed novel carbon dots (CDs) using polyacrylamide and sodium citrate through a quick, simple and inexpensive hydrothermal method and further used them as a fluorescent probe for the sensitive detection of Pb2+ ions and pyrophosphate (PPi), as shown in Scheme 2. The author reported that Pb2+ ions effectively chelated with the synthesized carbon dots, forming CDs/Pb2+ complexes. The formation of complexes between the surface of the carbon dots and Pb2+ ions led to the quenching of the fluorescence signal. Interestingly, the addition of pyrophosphate (PPi) restored the fluorescence because PPi has a greater binding preference for Pb2+ ions than the carbon dots. The reported probe provided high sensitivity with low limits of detection for Pb2+ (4.6 nM) and PPi (54 nM). Moreover, the reported probe was successfully evaluated using real water matrices and human urine. Experimental data confirmed that the “off–on” fluorescent probe exhibits a rapid response with outstanding selectivity, sensitivity, stability and reproducibility.92

A bovine serum albumin-capped boron-doped carbon dot (BSA-capped BCD) probe was successfully fabricated by Sahu et al. The synthesized BSA-capped BCDs have great potential for use in biochemical sensing because of the superior biocompatibility of bovine serum albumin. It was reported that the fluorescence signal of the BCA-capped BCDs was significantly quenched upon interaction with Pb2+ ions (Scheme 3). A linear relationship was observed between the decrease in fluorescence intensity and Pb2+ ion concentration over the range of 1 ppb to 10 ppb, with a limit of detection of 0.08 ppb.93

In a similar fashion, Wang et al. developed unmodified carbon dots (CDs) as eco-friendly fluorescent probes for the detection of Pb2+ ions and pyrophosphate (PPi) in an aqueous medium (Scheme 4). The average diameter of the synthesized yellow-green-coloured carbon dots was found to be 50 nm, and their fluorescence intensity was sensitive to pH. It was found that the fluorescence of the carbon dots was quenched by Pb2+ ions, and the resultant CD-Pb2+ complex was found to be sensitive to pyrophosphate. Pyrophosphate (PPi) has the ability to coordinate with Pb2+, restoring the fluorescence intensity of the CDs. Excellent linearity for detecting Pb2+ ions was attained over a concentration range of 5.0 × 102 to 1.1 × 104 nM. Moreover, the binding constant was determined to be 5.0 × 104 L mol−1 with a detection limit of 2.0 nM. Additionally, the detection limit for PPi was reported to be 5.0 × 102 nM.94

Further, the synthesis of boron-doped carbon nanodots (B-CNDs) was reported by Wang et al. via a hydrothermal method using boric acid and ascorbic acid as precursors. The reported carbon nanodots (BCNDs) possessed an irregular shape with a diameter of 10 nm and exhibited a maximum emission at 440 nm. The blue fluorescence of BCNDs was significantly quenched upon the addition of Pb2+ ions because of charge transfer between the COO−/–OH groups or bidentate OH donor groups on the surface of the carbon dots and Pb2+ ions. The results were utilized to establish a fluorometric assay for quantifying Pb2+ ions. Further, a linear relationship was observed between the concentration of Pb2+ ions and the decrease in fluorescence intensity over the range of 25 nM to 250 µM. It was reported that interactions with pyrophosphate (PPi) restored the fluorescence of the Pb2+-quenched B-CNDs as a result of the chelation between the Pb2+ ions and pyrophosphate (Scheme 5).95

Further, two simple, facile and environmentally friendly nanosensors were constructed, which utilized dynamic quenching mechanisms and aggregation-induced emission enhancement (AIEE) respectively. The undoped carbon dots (u-CDs) and nitrogen and sulphur co-doped carbon dots (N, S-CDs) were produced via a quick one-step process at high temperatures without the use of any solvent. The synthesized carbon dots showed excellent stability and higher water solubility. In contrast to N, S-CDs, which exhibit excitation-independent photoluminescence behavior, u-CDs exhibit excitation-dependent photoluminescence behavior. To elucidate various photoluminescence mechanisms, various photophysical properties, such as fluorescence quantum yield, radiative and non-radiative deactivation channels and average lifetimes, were carefully investigated. In contrast to u-CDS, N, S-CDs demonstrated superior PL intensity and photostability due to heteroatom doping, which increased the number of available active sites and generated synergistic effects, leading to radiative recombination of trapped electron–holes pairs. As surface passivation on CDs increases, non-radiative self-trapped electron states are eliminated through the removal of specific surface functional groups, consequently altering their photoluminescence (PL) properties. Thus, N, S-CDs demonstrated excitation-independent PL emission owing to their high surface passivation induced by heteroatom doping in comparison with u-CDs. To elucidate various photoluminescence mechanisms, various photophysical properties, such as fluorescence quantum yield, radiative and non-radiative deactivation channels and average lifetime, were carefully examined. It was found that exposing both CDs to Pb2+ ions induced opposite fluorescence responses. For example, photoluminescent enhancement in u-CDs was recorded (turn-on) due to aggregation-induced emission enhancement (AIEE), whereas fluorescence quenching in N, S-CDs was observed (turn-off) because of a dynamic quenching mechanism. In this phenomenon, hydroxyl and carboxyl groups present on the surface of u-CDs behave as recognition sites for the detection of Pb2+ ions. These functional groups on the surface of u-CDs form coordination complexes with Pb2+ ions, which leads to the formation of an aggregated state. The intramolecular movements become restricted in the aggregated state, resulting in a reduction of nonradiative decay and enhance radiative decay, leading to an increase in the PL intensity of u-CDs based on AIEE. Conversely, the decrease in the PL intensity of N, S-CDs was attributed to dynamic quenching because of charge transfer occurring between Pb2+ ions and the surface functional groups present on the CDs in the excited state. Further, the limits of detection for N, S-CDs and u-CDs towards the sensing of lead ions were found to be 7.0 nM and 30.0 nM, respectively. The resultant CDs were also utilized for the detection of Pb2+ ions in real sample analysis.96 The microwave-assisted approach was used to swiftly fabricate N, S co-doped carbon dots (N, S-CDs) from thiourea and tri-ammonium citrate. The N, S-CDs demonstrated a quantum yield of 11.5%. Moreover, the synthesized N, S-CDs were employed as a sensitive probe for Pb2+ ion detection in food and water samples. The relative fluorescence intensity of N, S-CDs was proportional to the Pb2+ ion concentration over the range of 0.029–2.40, with a detection limit of 9.2 nM.97 Further, for the selective determination of Pb2+ ions, a green-emitting carbon dot-based ratiometric fluorescent probe was developed by pyrolyzing m-aminophenol (m-AP) as a precursor in the presence of phosphoric acid and dimethylformamide. The surface of the synthesized carbon dots was modified with glutathione (GSH), which exhibits high affinity for Pb2+ ions. The chemical interaction of lead ions with the GSH ligands on the surfaces of neighboring carbon dots causes the GSH-modified carbon dots to aggregate, which results in the formation of a new well-separated emission peak at 692 nm. The developed probe exhibits two distinct emission peaks at 522 mm and 692 nm that respond in opposite directions to Pb2+ ions, resulting in a clear visible fluorescence colour change with the steady addition of Pb2+ ions. It is observed that during Pb2+ ion sensing, sulfur lone-pair electrons of the surface GSH groups on the CDs participate in complex formation with Pb2+ ions, inhibiting the photoinduced electron transfer (PET) process. The reported probe was ideal for determining determination of Pb2+ ions over a linear concentration range of 10–700 nM with a detection limit of 2.7 nM (Scheme 6).98

Another rapid neutralization-heat technique was applied for the synthesis of nitrogen-doped carbon dots (N-CDs). In this synthesis process, the dopamine precursor was converted into N-CDs within a very short time (∼2 min) by utilizing the heat generated from the neutralization reaction between phosphoric acid and ethylenediamine. The developed N-CDs were used as a “turn-on” fluorescence sensing probe toward Pb2+ ions, exhibiting high sensitivity and selectivity and a broad linear detection range of 0–50 µM. Its capacity for trace-level Pb2+ monitoring was demonstrated by its LOD of 0.92 µM. The synthesis procedure involved dehydration reactions with ethylenediamine and self-polymerization of dopamine, resulting in the formation of N-CDs bearing polydopamine-like functional groups. These amino and hydroxyl groups exhibited a strong binding affinity for Pb2+ ions and played a crucial role in the distinctive “OFF–ON” fluorescence sensing behavior. The fluorescence enhancement was explained by the inhibition of the photo-induced electron transfer (PET) process upon interaction with Pb2+ ions. DFT and TD-DFT calculations revealed that hydroxyl groups served as the major coordination sites for Pb2+ binding. The formation of the N-CDs–Pb2+ complex reduced the HOMO–LUMO energy gap, facilitating electronic transitions and restoring the fluorescence intensity.100

A dual-emission fluorescence sensor (N-CDs/R-CDs@ZIF-8) was fabricated via the introduction of red carbon dots (R-CDs) and nitrogen-doped carbon dots (N-CDs) into the ZIF-8 matrix through a simple stirring process for the selective detection of Pb2+ ions (Scheme 8). The synthesized composite material retained the optical properties and adsorption capacity of its precursors N-CDs, R-CDs and ZIF-8 while maintaining its fluorescent integrity and structural stability under harsh conditions. The N-CDs were fabricated from urea and citric acid, whereas red fluorescent carbon dots (RCDs) were produced from spinach as a green raw material. The resulting synthesized composite material not only combined the distinct advantages of a ratiometric fluorescent probe but also utilized the robust adsorption capacity of ZIF to capture the target analyte, thereby boosting detection sensitivity. The resulting composite, N-CDs/R-CDs@ZIF-8, exhibited fluorescence quenching of N-CDs upon interaction with Pb2+ ions, whereas the fluorescence of R-CDs remained intact. The Pb2+ ion-induced fluorescence quenching of N-CDs/R-CDs@ZIF-8 could result from the collapse of the ZIF framework caused by Pb2+ ion coordination with imidazole groups and from the quenching of N-CDs via coordination with nitrogen-containing groups. Initially, Pb2+ ions were adsorbed by ZIF, and the subsequent concentration of Pb2+ ions led to fluorescence quenching of N-CDs and decomposition of the ZIF framework through coordination interactions. Overall, the developed composite material demonstrated structural stability and excellent luminescence under extreme environmental conditions and could employed for the selective and sensitive determination of Pb2+ ions, with a linear range of 0.05–50 µM and a detection limit of 4.78 nM.101

Solid-state detection platforms are desirable for the creation of reusable sensors to support public health measures, such as the detection of contaminants in drinking water. In this regard, a bioinspired metal–organic framework-based material was developed by imitating metal–protein interactions in biological systems to achieve highly selective and sensitive detection of Pb2+ ions using fluorescence sensing. The HSC/Au(x)/UiO-66 materials with varying Au contents were developed by decorating the surface of octahedral-shaped HS-NH-UiO-66 crystals with gold nanoparticles, followed by coating Au(x)/UiO-66 with thiol-functionalized CDs, as shown in Scheme 9. The films of the resulting HS-C/Au(x)/UiO-66 powder showed a noticeable change in fluorescence intensity upon exposure to sub-10 ppb levels of Pb2+ ions in water and maintained their performance over five cycles. Moreover, it is noteworthy that, by adjusting the Au content, the fluorescence response of HS-C/Au(x)/UiO-66 towards Pb2+ ions could be tuned from emission quenching to fluorescence enhancement. Interestingly, a shift in the emission wavelength maximum of HS-C/Au(x)/UiO-66 was observed with increasing Au content because of the interaction of Au nanoparticles with incident light, generating oscillating dipoles at their LSPR (localized surface plasmon resonance) wavelength, which coincides with the excitation wavelength of CDs. The excited CDs act as electric dipoles, and their emissions can be tuned by coupling with Au nanoparticles. The changes in the electric field distribution around the excited CDs induced by Au nanoparticles lead to a bathochromic shift in emission. Further, the fluorescence emission intensity of the resulting films declined with a higher Au content, attributable to FRET (fluorescence resonance energy transfer). In FRET, energy transfer occurs from CDs (donor) to Au (acceptor) through dipole–dipole interactions in the excited state of CDs. The overlap between the absorption spectra of Au nanoparticles and the Au(x)/UiO-66 film and the emission spectrum of the HS-C/Au(x)/UiO-66 films was attributed to the possibility of FRET between HS-CDs (fluorophore) and Au(x)/UiO-66 (acceptor). Further, additional fluorescence quenching during Pb2+ ion sensing was explained by the formation of a nonfluorescent complex between Pb2+ ions and the surface thiol groups of HS-C/Au(x)/UiO-66 in the ground state, leading to fluorescence quenching of the films after excitation, as illustrated in Scheme 10. The solid-state sensor performance of the HS-C/Au(x)/UiO-66 film demonstrates its potential for use in reusable sensing devices to protect the public from Pb2+ ion pollution in drinking water.102

Natural biomass-derived carbon dot (CD)-based fluorescence sensors for the detection of lead ions

To date, various methodologies have been employed for the synthesis of carbon dots (CDs), such as hydrothermal treatment, microwave-assisted synthesis, pyrolysis, arc discharge, acidic oxidation, ball-milling, electrochemical synthesis, combustion and laser ablation.

However, the environmental concerns associated with these synthetic methodologies have not been adequately addressed. The reliance on conventional chemicals and complex instrumentation limits their practical use in wastewater treatment by increasing financial burdens and environmental risks. Moreover, the utilization of conventional organic solvents, such as DMSO, DMF, DCC, and DMAP, has further hindered their adoption in large-scale wastewater treatment operations. Therefore, the sustainable, biomass-mediated synthesis of carbon dots (CDs) has emerged as a more sustainable and environmentally friendly alternative to conventional physicochemical methods. Various types of natural biomass, such as tea extract, rice straw, apple juice, bamboo leaves, vegetable waste, orange juice, garlic, honey, shrimp, and aloe vera, have been used as carbon sources to fabricate CDs. Synthesizing carbon dots (CDs) from natural biomass avoids the use of toxic and costly chemicals and eliminates the need for complicated post-treatment processes. For instance, Singh et al. constructed carbon dots (R-CDs) and biochar (R-BC) from rice straw using a hydrothermal process. The carbon dots (R-CDs) exhibited outstanding selectivity and sensitivity for the detection of Pb2+ ions in polluted water. This approach helps reduce agricultural waste by developing multi-functional materials from waste resources. The dual use of R-BC as an adsorbent and R-CDs as a sensor demonstrates the potential of rice straw as a useful resource for environmental rehabilitation projects. Furthermore, this study demonstrated the exceptional reproducibility and stability of Pb2+ ion detection in terms of efficiency and reliability. R-CDs exhibited a broad linear range and a low detection limit of 110 nM, demonstrating higher sensitivity than many Pb2+ detection sensors already in use (Fig. 7).108

Schematic of chelation between R-CDs and Pb2+ ions through the formation of coordination bonds between the lone-pair electrons on the surface functional groups and electron-deficient Pb2+.

In an interesting report, two novel ratiometric fluorescent nanosensors were successfully fabricated for the rapid, selective and sensitive determination of Pb2+ ions in river water. The extracts of bamboo leaves were used to generate two different types of multi-emissive nanohybrids via an economical and environmentally friendly solvothermal treatment. This method is advantageous since it avoids the incorporation of other fluorescent nanomaterials and post-modification of CDs, which are commonly used in many previous ratiometric sensors based on CDs. The as-prepared dual CD nanohybrids detected Pb2+ ions with a sub-nanomolar detection limit of 0.14 nM, which is significantly lower than the safe limit for lead in drinking water recommended by the WHO (Fig. 8).109

Schematic of the solvothermal synthesis of dual- and three-emission CD nanohybrids using bamboo leaf extracts under neutral conditions, respectively, for the ratiometric fluorescent sensing of Pb2+ ions.

A straightforward thermal process was successfully used to convert pearl millet seeds into value-added carbon dots. Pearl millet is a major food crop grown extensively worldwide. Pearl millet seeds were converted into highly luminous carbon dots using water as the reaction medium. The specific optical characteristics of the synthesized carbon dots made them effective for sensitive and selective Pb2+ ion detection, with a detection limit of 0.18 nM, in contaminated water using UV-visible, fluorescence and colorimetric methods. This interesting outcome might be due to the complexation between Pb2+ ions and the p-nitrophenol carbon dot (PNP@CD) framework. The hydroxyl groups of PNP have a strong propensity to interact with the amine and carboxyl groups of carbon dots. This strategy offers good biocompatibility of the carbon dots and an environmentally friendly, cost-effective methodology (Fig. 9).110

In this methodology, additional chemical functionalization of the carbon dots was not required, thereby providing a simple and cost-effective approach. A straightforward, economical and one-step chemical oxidation process was utilized to fabricate fluorescent carbon dots using C. dichotoma fruits as a natural biowaste source of carbon (Scheme 13). Carbon quantum dot (CQD)-derived fluorescence sensors for the detection of lead ions

Metal nanoclusters have been widely utilized to construct nanosensors for the detection of Pb2+ ions with high sensitivity owing to their specific photoluminescent characteristics, such as size-tunable emissions, high quantum yields, high stability and broad absorption spectra. Copper nanoclusters (CuNCs) are inexpensive and show aggregation-induced emission enhancement (AIEE) properties, and thus can be used for the fabrication of fluorescent probes for Pb2+ ion detection. Considering the aggregation-induced emission enhancement properties of CuNCs, a ratiometric fluorescent probe, CuNC-N-CQD nanohybrid, was synthesized from nitrogen-doped carbon quantum dots (N-CQDs) and copper nanoclusters (CuNCs) for Pb2+ ion detection in Porphyra samples (Scheme 18). In this fluorescent probe, CuNCs provide an emission response via the AIEE effect due to the interactions between CuNCs and Pb2+ ions, whereas a self-calibration signal was provided by the NCQDs, whose fluorescence intensity was unaffected by Pb2+ ions. The authors reported that, under the optimal reaction conditions, the nanohybrid exhibited high sensitivity for lead ions over a concentration range of 0.010–2.5 ppm with a detection limit of 0.0031 ppm.119

Fluorescent sulfur and nitrogen-doped carbon quantum dots (N–S CQDs) were fabricated via a facile hydrothermal procedure for the sensitive detection of Pb2+ ions, as shown in Scheme 19. The doping of nitrogen and sulfur significantly reduced the band gap of the CQDs and induced a redshift in the emission peak of the CQDs to the visible region at 540 nm upon excitation at 460 nm. The interactions of the N–S CQDs with Pb2+ ions resulted in fluorescence quenching because Pb2+ ions formed coordination structures with the sulfhydryl and carboxyl groups on the surface of the CQDs. The CQDs demonstrated excellent anti-interference capability under the optimized conditions. Carbon quantum dot (CQD) and graphene quantum dot (GQD)-derived sensors for the electrochemical detection of lead ions

Electrochemical sensors are widely explored devices that detect target species by measuring the electrical current generated during their oxidation or reduction. The ability of electrochemical sensors to selectively detect target species relies fundamentally on electrode materials and their immobilized receptors. In electrochemical sensors, the responses obtained from the interactions of the target analyte with electrode materials are converted into a measurable signal representing the analyte concentration. The advantages of electrochemical sensors include a fast response time, the ability to miniaturize and fabricate them in a cost-effective manner, a simple measurement procedure, the potential for portable measurement instrumentation, and sufficient sensitivity and selectivity. A carbon dot-derived hybrid electrochemical sensor was constructed from graphene oxide (GO) and nitrogen-doped carbon quantum dots (NCQDs) via anodic stripping voltammetry for Pb2+ ion detection (Scheme 26). In this NCQDs-GO hybrid, both GO and NCQDs have abundant functional groups and large/highly electroactive surface areas, which provide active sites for the effective adsorption of heavy metal ions via electrostatic interactions, thereby increasing sensitivity. The N-doped carbon quantum dots (NCQDs) were homogeneously spread on the surface of GO and played a critical role in re-oxidizing metals to ions for stripping analysis. Graphene oxide served as a support for loading NCQDs and increasing the rate of electron transfer and enhance the conductivity of the hybrid. By combining the significant advantages of both NCQDs and GO, along with their synergistic effects, the reported NCQDs-GO hybrid exhibited outstanding electrochemical performance for the detection of Pb2+ ions with good selectivity, excellent reproducibility and a wide linear range.138

Quantum dot-based nanosensors for the detection of lead ions

Quantum dots have emerged as promising semiconductor nanomaterials for a variety of applications, especially in sensing applications, because of their unique electro-optical properties arising from quantum confinement effects. Features such as large extinction coefficients, exceptional photostability, high quantum yields, prolonged fluorescence lifetimes and broad absorption spectra make quantum dots attractive fluorophores for the fabrication of fluorescent sensing probes. Furthermore, facile surface modifications, such as polymer-shell embedding, micelle incorporation, ligand exchange, and surface silanization, have broadened their applicability as nanosensor materials for the detection of toxic analytes. In this context, an interesting “turn-on” fluorescent probe was constructed for Pb2+ ion detection by mixing graphene oxide sheets with aptamer-functionalized CdSe/ZnS quantum dots (QDs), producing a GO/aptamer-QD ensemble, as shown in Fig. 15. The emission signal of the GO/aptamer-QD ensemble was significantly quenched because the aptamer-QDs were absorbed onto the surface of GO via π-π interactions, which brought the QDs into intimate contact with the GO sheets. This facilitated energy transfer from the quantum dots to the GO sheets, which effectively “turned off” the fluorescence emission of the quantum dots. The GO/aptamer–QD ensemble behaved as a “turn-on” fluorescence sensor for Pb2+ ion detection. The interaction of Pb2+ ions with the aptamer led to changes in its conformation, resulting in the formation of a G-quadruplex/Pb2+ complex. The formation of the G-quadruplex/Pb2+ complex induced the detachment of the QDs from the graphene oxide sheets, which resulted in the “turn-on” fluorescence of the QDs. Moreover, the GO/aptamer-QD ensemble demonstrated good selectivity for Pb2+ ions, with a detection limit of 0.09 nM.144

Schematic of the operating principle of the “turn-on” fluorescence sensor for the detection of Pb2+ ions.

Further, molybdenum disulfide (MoS2)-functionalized quantum dots (QDs) were synthesized via a hydrothermal process. Absorption studies revealed that the absorption intensity of the MoS2 QDs was linearly dependent on the lead ion concentration, whereas their fluorescence intensity was strongly quenched after interaction with Pb2+ ions. Absorption followed a linear S–V relationship in the presence of Pb2+ ions over a broad dynamic concentration range, with a limit of detection of 5 × 104 nM (Scheme 30). The functionalized MoS2 QDs enabled the effective and selective sensing of Pb2+ ions through significant changes in their optical properties. The linear increase in absorption intensity confirmed the formation of a stable complex between Pb2+ ions and the surface-functionalized MoS2 QDs. Simultaneously, the progressive decrease in PL lifetime and photoluminescence (PL) intensity followed the Stern–Volmer relationship, indicating that diffusional quenching is the dominant sensing mechanism. The decrease in PL lifetime and intensity with increasing Pb2+ ion concentration indicates nonradiative electron–hole recombination or electron transfer from the excited QDs to metal ions. The electron-deficient Pb2+ ions interact strongly with the nitrogen-containing functional groups on the MoS2 QD surface, facilitating electron transfer from the excited QDs to Pb2+ ions. This process enhances non-radiative electron–hole recombination, resulting in efficient fluorescence quenching. Moreover, the absence of any significant red shift in the absorption or PL spectra suggests that cation-exchange processes are negligible, confirming that electron-transfer-induced quenching is the primary mechanism.145

Schematic of the (a) synthesis of functionalized MoS2 QDs via a facile hydrothermal process and (b) PL turn-off sensing of Pb2+ ions by MoS2 QDs.

Further, a highly fluorescent MoS2 QD probe was fabricated using (NH4)2MoS4 and GSH as sulfur and molybdenum sources, respectively, through a one-pot hydrothermal process. In the reported methodology, GSH was also utilized as a passivation/capping agent to remove surface defects from the MoS2 QDs, thereby enhancing their fluorescence responses, as well as a reductant to reduce (NH4)2MoS4. The surface-functionalized MoS2 QDs were employed as a fluorescent probe for Pb2+ ion detection. The detection limit of Pb2+ ions was determined to be 0.056 × 103 nM, which was below than acceptable limit recommended by the United States Environmental Protection Agency. The GSH-functionalized MoS2 quantum dots (QDs) detected Pb2+ ions through a nonradiative electron-transfer mechanism, where Pb2+ ions chelated with surface carboxyl, amino, and thiol groups, leading to fluorescence quenching. Strong interactions between these functional groups and Pb2+ ions provided excellent selectivity over other competing metal ions. The sensor exhibited favorable photophysical properties, including good stability over a wide pH range, a high quantum yield (20.4%), and a low LOD of 56 nM over the linear detection range of 5–60 µM.146 Another orthorhombic AIS QD system (AgInS2 quantum dots) was synthesized using an eco-friendly water-phase approach, with glutathione (GSH) as a stabilizing agent. The spherical particles of the prepared AIS QDs showed good water dispersibility and excellent linear correlation between the concentration of Pb2+ ions and the relative fluorescence quenching of the AIS QDs over the range of 9–530 nM (Fig. 16). The recovery ranged between 92.2% and 102.6% and a detection limit of 4.0 nM was reported. The AIS QD sensor detected Pb2+ ions through a dynamic fluorescence quenching mechanism involving light-induced electron transfer from excited QDs to Pb2+ ions. Strong interactions between Pb2+ ions and surface hydroxyl, carboxyl and amino groups enhance the ’stability and selectivity of the sensor. Furthermore, it showed good selectivity toward Pb2+ ions over most competing metal ions. However, a relatively long response time (∼110 min) and potential interference from certain metal ions remain important limitations. Future improvements should focus on faster sensing, improved anti-interference capability, and validation in complex real-world samples.147

Further, a novel concept was demonstrated for the visual detection of Pb2+ ions in water. A dual-color-based fluorescent nanohybrid material was fabricated from green-emissive gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) and red-emissive quantum dots (QDs) embedded in silica nanoparticles for Pb2+ ion detection based on a color change from green to yellow (Scheme 31). In this methodology, highly green-emissive gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) were prepared using mercaptopropionic acid as a ligand and glutathione as a stabilizing agent. The fluorescence intensity of AuNCs was specifically sensitive to Pb2+ ions. The authors observed that the red fluorescence signal was insensitive to Pb2+ ions and served as an internal reference, while the green fluorescence was selectively quenched by Pb2+ ions. The two emissions responded differently, resulting in a continuous color change from green to yellow that could be seen by the naked eye. The reported method exhibited improved selectivity for visual detection compared with AuNC-based probes. The reported ratiometric probe was practically applied to both quantitative analysis and qualitative recognition based on fluorescence ratios and color-change signatures. This approach exhibited low interference and good selectivity, with a detection limit of 3.5 nM, which is substantially lower than the permitted threshold of lead in drinking water (72 nM). Finally, the reported ratiometric fluorescent probe was used to create a PVA film for rapid visual measurement of lead ions in complex real water samples, with a detection limit of 1 × 105 nM.148

A new polyindole (Pin)/CuInS (CIS)/ZnS nanocomposite quantum dot (QD) sensor was developed by in situ polymerization of polyindole (Pin) with CIS/ZnS QDs, as illustrated in Scheme 32. The photoluminescence spectrum of the Pin/CIS/ZnS QD nanocomposite showed a quenched peak for the CIS/ZnS QDs, whereas the fluorescence intensity of the polyindole (Pin) peak was enhanced. The fluorescent Pin/CIS/ZnS QD nanocomposite probe was used to detect Pb2+ ion concentrations ranging from 5 to 50 ppb. The strong interactions between CIS/ZnS QDs and Pin enabled the detection of Pb2+ ions with high sensitivity, a detection limit of 4.48 ppb and a dynamic linear range of 5 to 50 ppb. The Pin/CIS/ZnS QD sensor exhibited strong affinity for Pb2+ ions owing to the presence of nitrogen- and sulfur-containing ligands rather than oxygen-containing ligands, which facilitated selective interactions with lead ions. The fluorescence quenching sensing mechanism (turn-off) was primarily attributed to photo-induced electron transfer and possible ion-exchange processes involving Pb2+ ions, resulting in significant photoluminescence quenching. A linear response was observed over a Pb2+ ion concentration range of 5–50 ppb, demonstrating effective sensing performance. However, the sensing process was relatively slow, requiring approximately 90 min to reach equilibrium, which may limit rapid on-site detection. Further studies are needed to clarify the exact quenching mechanism and improve response kinetics for practical environmental monitoring applications.149

A selective and sensitive fluorescence technique was designed for lead ion detection in water using l-glutathione (GSH)-modified graphitic carbon nitride quantum dots (GSH@g-C3N4 QDs), as shown in Scheme 33. In the reported carbon dots, GSH was bound to the g-C3N4 QDs via a carbodiimide-activated coupling process, and trisodium citrate and urea were used as precursors for the synthesis of g-C3N4 QDs at a low temperature. The reported fluorescent GSH@g-C3N4 QDs demonstrated resistance to changes in ionic strength and good photostability. The passivated GSH@g-C3N4 QDs generated a significant blue emission peak at 420 nm when excited at 340 nm. Further, the addition of Pb2+ ions to an aqueous solution of GSH@g-C3N4 QDs resulted in fluorescence quenching, which was attributed to the formation of a complex. Furthermore, it was reported that the carbon dots exhibited a limit of detection of 0.025 µM over a linear range of 0.01 to 0.1 µM. The lifetime decay analysis revealed a static quenching process, which was confirmed by XPS analysis, indicating that Pb2+ ions interact with the carboxylic and thiol groups of the GSH@g-C3N4 QDs.150

Blue ZnSe quantum dots were synthesized by refluxing an aqueous zinc ion solution and NaHSe in DMF, with N-acetyl cysteine (NAC) acting as a capping agent. The blue-emitting NAC-ZnSe QDs without any dopant were used for the first time to detect traces of Pb2+ ions in water, fruit juices, and biological fluids. The ZnSe QDs exhibited remarkable selectivity toward Pb2+ ions, showing rapid and complete fluorescence quenching compared with other tested metal ions. Although Hg2+ ions also induced fluorescence quenching, the response was significantly slower, enabling clear discrimination between Hg2+ and Pb2+ ions. Interference studies further demonstrated that the presence of other metal ions had a negligible influence on Pb2+ detection, confirming the high specificity of the sensing system. These findings highlight the potential of ZnSe QDs as sensitive, selective and reliable fluorescent probes for Pb2+ ion monitoring in complex aqueous environments. A time-dependent fluorescence study of ZnSe QDs in the presence of Pb2+ ions showed that the addition of Pb2+ ions quenched the fluorescence of the QDs within 90 s.151

A straightforward hydrothermal technique was described for the fabrication of photoluminescent carbon quantum dots for the detection of harmful Pb2+ ions. The fabricated quantum dots exhibited high photostability, low toxicity, and considerable photoluminescence capabilities, along with good water solubility. These quantum dots were successfully used as a sensing probe for the determination of Pb2+ ions, with a detection limit of 60 nmol L−1, which is suitable for practical applications. The synthesized quantum dots demonstrated outstanding selectivity for Pb2+ ions with minimal interference from other metal ions in aqueous solution. The fluorescence quenching mechanism was primarily attributed to an electron-transfer process resulting from the strong complexation between the hydroxyl, amine, and carboxyl functional groups on the surface of the QDs and Pb2+ ions. The strong binding affinity between the nitrogen-containing functionalities on the QD surfaces and the vacant d-orbitals of Pb2+ ions compared with those of other metal ions further increased the selectivity. Time-resolved photoluminescence studies of QDs revealed almost unchanged fluorescence lifetimes before and after Pb2+ ion addition, confirming that a predominantly static quenching process occurred through the fluorescence quenching mechanism. These findings demonstrate the potential of functionalized carbon dots as highly selective and efficient fluorescent probes for the detection of lead ions in environmental samples.152 A summary of quantum dot-based sensors, doping agents, functionalization groups and detection limits is presented in Table 7.

Conclusion

Carbon dot (CD) and quantum dot (QD)-derived sensors are promising materials for the detection of lead ions with high selectivity and ultra-low detection limits. Therefore, these zero-dimensional sensing materials have attracted considerable attention because of their specific characteristics, such as tunable optical properties, photochemical stability, water solubility, biocompatibility, facile synthesis procedures, surface modification, and the potential to determine toxic heavy-metal ions at trace levels. This review highlights recent advancements in CDs, CQDs, GQDs and QD-based sensors and their key features, advantages, and sensing mechanisms for detecting hazardous lead ions. Moreover, this review emphasizes the development of doped CDs, CQDs, GQDs and QDs and their composites with metal nanoclusters, ion-exchange membranes, metal nanoparticles, natural polymer films and coatings, which not only improve selectivity but also enhance detection performance. Furthermore, this review addresses the fabrication of CDs, CQDs, and GQDs from natural and agricultural waste biomass and evaluates their sensing performance for lead ions in detail, demonstrating their potential for environmental and health-related safety applications. Although these sensors have made significant advancements, there are still significant obstacles that restrict their practical use. For example, inadequate knowledge of photoluminescence mechanisms, a lack of standardized and reproducible synthesis techniques and insufficient detection selectivity restrict their viability for hazardous metal detection. Furthermore, issues related to long-term stability, scalability, low sensitivity, and on-site applicability persist in practical applications beyond laboratory conditions. To increase selectivity and sensitivity, future studies should focus on mechanism-driven design, accurate surface functionalization and heteroatom doping. Furthermore, the translation of these nanosensors into useful technologies/tools for environmental monitoring will depend on integrating CDs into portable sensing platforms and developing standardized evaluation procedures with real-sample validation.
