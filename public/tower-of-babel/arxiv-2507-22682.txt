Conjecture on Maximal Sublattices of Finite Semidistributive Lattices and Beyond
K. Adaricheva 1, A. Mata 2, S. Silberger 3, A. Zamojska-Dzienio 4

Abstract
. We study maximal sublattices of finite semidistributive lattices via their complements. We focus on the conjecture that such complements are always intervals, which is known to be true for bounded lattices. Since the class of semidistributive lattices is the intersection of classes of join- and meet-semidistributive lattices, we study also complements for these classes, and in particular convex geometries of convex dimension 2, which is a subclass of join-semidistributive lattices. In the latter case, we describe the complements of maximal sublattices completely, as well as the procedure of finding all complements of maximal sublattices.

1.1.
Background and motivation

A proper sublattice
\mathop{\mathcal{S}}
of a lattice
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}
is said to be
maximal
if for every

, the sublattice generated by
\mathop{\mathcal{S}}\cup\{x\}
is all of

. Maximal sublattices of finite distributive lattices were fully described in

\left[a,b\right]=\{x\in L\ :\ a\leq x\leq b\}
with

being a unique meet-irreducible one in the interval.

In
[

it was first shown that the complement of a proper
(0,1)-
sublattice of a lattice of finite length (in particular, for a finite one) contains an interval
\left[a,b\right]
with
a
being a join-irreducible element and
b
being a meet-irreducible one. We are interested in the complements of maximal sublattices (which are always
(0,1)-
sublattices).

In this paper we focus on finite lattices and for them the interrelationships between classes we are interested in are shown on Figure

This suggests the following question.

Question 1
.

For which classes of lattices the complements of maximal lattices are intervals?

The class of semidistributive lattices is the extension of the class of bounded lattices, and

[
2

In this paper we start a systematic study of this hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1
.

The complements of maximal sublattices of (finite) SD lattices are also intervals.

First, we will briefly recall some basic notions necessary to understand the formulation of problems which we investigate here.

A join-semidistributive (SD
∨
) lattice is a lattice
\left<\mathop{\mathcal{L}},\vee,\wedge\right>
in which the following quasi-identity holds:

x\vee y=x\vee z\implies x\vee(y\wedge z)=x\vee y

for all
x,y,z\in\mathop{\mathcal{L}}
. Meet-semidistributive lattices (SD
∧

A lattice is said to be
semidistributive
(SD) if it is both join- and meet-semidistributive.

The examples of

lattices shown in Figure

demonstrates that complements of maximal sublattices may have arbitrary number of maximal elements. All these examples come from a special subclass of join-semidsitributive lattices known as

A

Due to results of

This motivates the following hypothesis for the class of convex geometries and, more generally, join-semidistributive lattices.

Hypothesis 2
.

The complement of a maximal sublattice of a (finite) CG, or, more generally, lattices satisfying SD
∨
, is the union of intervals with a common minimal element.

Immediately, one has also the dual formulation that the complement of a maximal sublattice of a (finite) SD
∧
lattice is the union of intervals with a common maximal element.

If the Hypothesis
2

∨

If

that lies on chain

is the meet of elements from the generating chains, for each

and for each

There are a few facts about convex geometries (see

]
) that we will be using. Suppose
\left<X,\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\right>
is a convex geometry with

and generating chains

,

C_{n}

(a)

\mathop{\mathcal{G}}
is SD

(b)

\mathop{\mathcal{G}}

(c)

If

(d)

All meet-irreducible elements of

are on generating chains of
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}

(e)

For all

.

(15)

As discussed in
[

,
the
glued sum
of lattices
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}_{2}
is the lattice
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}
formed by equating the maximal element of
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}_{1}
with the minimal element of
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}_{2}
, considering every element
in
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}_{1}

\mathop{\mathcal{L}}_{2}

\mathop{\mathcal{L}}

to every other element of the lattice, can be
decomposed
into smaller components that are glued-summed together that point. We’ll refer to a lattice as

In this section we note observations about complements of maximal sublattices in general, independent of restrictions we have on the initial lattice
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}

Suppose

of
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}

Observation 3.1
.

The complement of a maximal sublattice of any lattice will be contained entirely within an indecomposable component of that lattice. In particular, if
x
is comparable to every element of
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathop{\mathcal{S}}
sublattice of
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}
, then both
\mathop{\mathcal{S}}\cup\uparrow x
and
\mathop{\mathcal{S}}\cup\downarrow x
are sublattices and
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}
and so if
\mathop{\mathcal{S}}
is maximal, its complement muste be entirely contained in either
\uparrow x
or
\downarrow x
.

Proof.

Let
\mathop{\mathcal{S}}
be any sublattice of
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}
, with complement
\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
. We show that
\mathop{\mathcal{S}}\cup\downarrow x
is a sublattice of
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}
. Fix
u,v\in\mathop{\mathcal{S}}\cup\downarrow x
. If both
u
and
v
are in
\mathop{\mathcal{S}}
, then
u\vee v\in\mathop{\mathcal{S}}
and
u\wedge v\in\mathop{\mathcal{S}}
. If both
u
and
v
are in
\downarrow x
, then
u\vee v\in\downarrow x
and
u\wedge v\in\downarrow x
. Suppose
u\in\mathop{\mathcal{S}}\setminus\downarrow x
and
v\in\downarrow x
. Then
u\in\uparrow x
since
x
is comparable to everything and so
v\leq x\leq u
. That is,
u\wedge v=v\in\downarrow x
and
u\vee v=u\in\mathop{\mathcal{S}}
. In all cases,
u\vee v\in\mathop{\mathcal{S}}\cup\uparrow x
and
u\wedge v\in\mathop{\mathcal{S}}\cup\uparrow x
and thus
\mathop{\mathcal{S}}\cup\uparrow x
is a sublattice of
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}
. An analogous argument shows that
\mathop{\mathcal{S}}\cup\uparrow x
is a sublattice of
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}
.

∎

In particular, if the lattice has a unique atom then the complement of any maximal sublattice lies entirely in the up set of that atom and if it has a unique coatom, then the complement would like entirely within the down set of that coatom. Thus, we may assume all the lattices we are working with have at least two atoms and at least two coatoms.

Observation 3.2
.

If
\mathop{\mathcal{M}}
is a maximal sublattice of lattice
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}
whose complement

has more than one element, then no element of the complement is doubly irreducible for if

is doubly irreducible,
\{a\}
is itself the complement of a sublattice (and hence of a maximal sublattice).

Observation 3.3
.

If
\mathop{\mathcal{S}}
is a

sublattice of lattice
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}
and

Observation 3.4
.

If
\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
is the complement of a maximal sublattice
\mathop{\mathcal{M}}
of some lattice
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}
, then
\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
cannot be partitioned into two nonempty sets

and

is comparable to any element of

Proof.

Suppose
\mathop{\mathcal{M}}
is a maximal sublattice of
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}

\mathop{\mathcal{C}}=\mathop{\mathcal{L}}\setminus\mathop{\mathcal{M}}
, and
\mathop{\mathcal{C}}=C_{1}\cup C_{2}

and both

and

are nonempty. Fix

. If either

\mathop{\mathcal{M}}\cup C_{2}
is a sublattice of
\mathop{\mathcal{L}}
which contains
\mathop{\mathcal{M}}
, contradicting the fact that
\mathop{\mathcal{M}}
is maximal.

∎

Observation 3.5
.

Suppose
\mathop{\mathcal{M}}
is a maximal sublattice of some lattice

∎

(a)

(b)

4.
SD
∨
and SD
∧
Lattices

Here we summarize our results for SD
∨
and SD
∧
lattices. We remind you of our hypothesis involving SD
∨
and SD
∧
lattices: the complements of maximal sublattices of SD
∨
lattices are unions of intervals with the same lower bound and hence have a unique minimal element and dually, the complements of maximal sublattices of SD
∧
lattices are unions of intervals having the same upper bound and hence they have a unique maximal element. Example
2
from Section
6
shows that we cannot expect complements of maximal sublattices of SD
∨
and SD
∧
lattices to be intervals, in general. It is an example showing how we can find convex geometries with

and complements of maximal sublattices with
n
maximal elements. Since all convex geometries are SD
∨
, this shows you can have arbitrarily many maximal elements of the complement of a maximal sublattice of an SD
∨
lattice. Dually, one can have arbitrarily many minimal elements of a complement of a maximal sublattice of an SD
∧
lattice.

Recall Definition
2.1
(

) of
canonical joinand
and
canonical meetand
. For our analysis of SD
∨
and SD
∧
lattices the concepts of

canonical joinands and meetands will be useful.

Definition 4.1
.

Given
\mathop{\mathcal{C}}=\mathop{\mathcal{L}}\setminus S

of an SD lattice

. Then if

a

(or

. Similarly, if

(or

) of

with respect to
\mathop{\mathcal{C}}

. (When

Lemma 4.2
.

Let

∎

Theorem 4.4

Theorem 4.5

\mathop{\mathcal{L}}

Suppose

Now we want to show that

X_{1}
and
X_{2}
such that the orderings that determine both

and

have every element of

Lemma 6.3
.

Suppose
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}=\left<X,\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\right>
is a convex geometry of
cdim=2
generated by chains
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
. Then each of the following is true.

(1)

For every
x\in X
,
(x)=C_{1}(x)\wedge C_{2}(x)=M_{1}(x)\wedge M_{2}(x)=c_{1}\wedge c_{2}
for each
c_{i}\in[(x),M_{i}(x)]
.

(2)

For each
x\in X
,
[(x),M_{1}(x)]\cap[(x),M_{2}(x)]=\{(x)\}
and so
[(x),C_{1}(x)]\cap[(x),C_{2}(x)]=\{(x)\}
, as well.

(3)

Let
C_{1}(x)\prec C_{1}(x_{1})
and
C_{2}(x)\prec C_{2}(x_{2})
. If
x_{1}\in C_{2}(x)
or
x_{1}=x_{2}
, then
C_{1}(x)=M_{1}(x)
.

(4)

For any
a\in\mathop{\mathcal{G}}
which is not the minimal element of
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}
,
C_{i}(a)=C_{i}(j)
for some
j\in X
with
(j)\leq a
.

(5)

For any
x\in X
,
[\emptyset,C_{i}(x)]=[(x),C_{i}(x)]\dot{\cup}[\emptyset,C_{i}(p)]
, where
C_{i}(p)\prec C_{i}(x)
.

(6)

Suppose
C_{i}(x)=X
for some
x\in X
. Then
(x)\in C_{i^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Fix a convex geometry
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}
with
cdim=2
, generating chains
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, and base set
X
.

1
.

Fix
x\in X
. We know that
(x)=m_{1}\wedge m_{2}
for some meet irreducible
m_{i}\in C_{i}
. But then,
x\in m_{i}
and thus,
m_{i}\geq M_{i}(x)\geq C_{i}(x)
for each
i
. Thus,
(x)=m_{1}\wedge m_{2}\geq M_{1}(x)\wedge M_{2}(x)\geq C_{1}(x)\wedge C_{2}(x)
. But also,
x\in C_{i}(x)
and hence
x\in C_{1}(x)\wedge C_{2}(x)
. That is,
(x)\leq C_{1}(x)\wedge C_{2}(x)
as well. Hence
(x)=C_{1}(x)\wedge C_{2}(x)=M_{1}(x)\wedge M_{2}(x)
. Further, if
c_{i}\in[(x),M_{i}(x)]
, then
(x)\leq c_{1}\wedge c_{2}\leq M_{1}(x)\wedge M_{2}(x)=(x)
.

2

Let
a\in[(x),M_{1}(x)]\cap[(x),M_{2}(x)]
. Then
a\in[(x),M_{2}(x)]
and
a\leq M_{1}(x)
. Thus, by part (
1
),
(x)=a\cap M_{1}(x)=a
.

3

Denote
a=C_{1}(x)
. It is enough to show that
C_{2}(a)\geq C_{1}(x_{1})=a\cup\{x_{1}\}
, i.e.,
C_{1}(x_{1})=a^{*}
, a unique cover of
a
. Apparently,
x\in C_{2}(a)
, therefore,
x_{1}\in C_{2}(x)
implies
x_{1}\in C_{2}(a)
. Now assume
x_{1}=x_{2}
and denote
b=C_{2}(x)
. If
a=b
, then
a,a\cup\{x_{1}\}
are in both chains, thus,
a
is meet irreducible. So assume that
a\not=b
. Then
C_{2}(a)>b
, therefore,
x_{1}\in C_{2}(a)
, which is needed.

4

Let
j\in X
by the greatest element of
a
in chain
C_{i}
. Then
C_{i}(a)=C_{i}(j)
by the definition. Moreover,
(j)=C_{i}(j)\wedge C_{i^{\prime}}(j)
, which proves
(j)\leq C_{i}(a)
.

5
.

Suppose
x\in X
and without loss of generality, say
C_{1}(p)\prec C_{1}(x)
. Fix
u\in[\emptyset,C_{1}(x)]
. If
u\geq(x)
, then
u\in[(x),C_{1}(x)]
. So suppose
u\ngeq(x)
. We know
u=C_{1}(u)\wedge C_{2}(u)
. Clearly
C_{1}(u)\leq C_{1}(x)
.
By (
4
) above, since
(x)\nleq u
, we know
C_{1}(u)\neq C_{1}(x)
. Thus,
C_{1}(u)\leq C_{1}(p)
. That is,
u\in[\emptyset,C_{1}(p)]
.
In either case,
u\in[\emptyset,C_{1}(p)]\cup[(x),C_{1}(x)]
and so
[\emptyset,C_{1}(x)]\subseteq[\emptyset,C_{1}(p)]\cup[(x),C_{1}(x)]
.

Now, suppose
v\in[\emptyset,C_{1}(p)]
. Then
v\leq C_{1}(p)
and so
x\notin v
since
x\notin C_{1}(p)
. That is,
(x)\nleq v
and so
v\notin[(x),C_{1}(x)]
. Thus,
[\emptyset,C_{1}(p)]
and
[(x),C_{1}(x)]
are disjoint.

6
.

Suppose without loss of generality that
C_{2}(x)=X
. Then
(x)=C_{1}(x)\wedge C_{2}(x)=C_{1}(x)
.

∎

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}=\left<X,\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\right>
be a convex geometry with
cdim=2
generated by chains
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
with trivial intersection and suppose
C_{i}(j)\prec C_{i}(x_{i})
for each
i\in\{1,2\}
.

(1)

Suppose
x_{1}=x_{2}
.

(a)

If
(x_{1})
is in neither chain, then
(j)
is in neither chain, and
[(j),C_{1}(j)]\cup[(j),C_{2}(j)]
is the complement of a sublattice of
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}
.

(b)

If
(x_{1})\in C_{i}
for one of the

, then
(j)=C_{i}(j)
is also in
C_{i}
and
[(j),C_{i^{\prime}}(j)]
is the complement of a sublattice of
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}
.

(2)

If
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
and
x_{i}\in C_{i^{\prime}}(j)
, then
[(j),C_{i}(j)]
is the complement of a sublattice of
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}
.

(3)

In all other cases
[(j),C_{i^{\prime}}(j)]
is not the complement of a sublattice.

We note that if item (1a) holds above, then necessarily
C_{1}(j)=M_{1}(j)
and
C_{2}(j)=M_{2}(j)
and if item (1b) or (2) holds, then necessarily
C_{i}(j)=M_{i}(j)
since the maximal elements of complements of sublattices are meet irreducible.

Proof.

Assume the hypothesis. We first show that a subset outside of the complement is closed under
\vee
. Note that in all cases it is sufficient to show that if
u,v\notin[(j),C_{i}(j)]
, then
u\vee v\notin[(j),C_{i}(j)]
. Take
u,v\in\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\setminus[(j),C_{i}(j)]
. If
u\not\leq C_{i}(j)
, then
u\vee v\not\leq C_{i}(j)
.

So now let
u,v\leq C_{i}(j)
but
(j)\not\leq u,v
. If
C_{i}(p)\prec C_{i}(j)

then by Lemma
6.3
(
5
) we know
u,v\leq C_{i}(p)
and hence
u\vee v\leq C_{i}(p)
and thus is not in
[(j),C_{i}(j)]
. That is, the complement of
[(j),C_{i}(j)]
is closed under join for all
j\in X
. Thus to establish items (1) and (2) of the lemma we need only show the complement of the appropriate interval or union of intervals is also closed under meet and determine whether or not
(j)
is on a generating chain.

(1)

Suppose
x_{1}=x_{2}=x
. Note that
(j)=C_{1}(j)\wedge C_{2}(j)\leq C_{1}(x)\wedge C_{2}(x)=(x)
and so
(j)<(x)
. But also, by semimodularity and since
x\notin C_{i}(j)
,
C_{i}(j)\wedge(x)\prec C_{i}(x)\wedge(x)=(x)
. But then
(x)_{*}\in[(j),C_{i}(j)]
for each
i
and so, by Lemma
6.3
(
2
),
(x)_{*}=(j)
.

Fix
w>(j)
such that
w\notin[(j),C_{1}(j)]\cup[(j),C_{2}(j)]
.
Then
w=C_{1}(w)\cap C_{2}(w)
. Since
w\nleq C_{i}(j)
,
C_{i}(w)>C_{i}(j)
and thus
C_{i}(w)\geq C_{i}(x)
for each
i
. But then
x_{1}\in C_{i}(w)
for each
i
and so
x\in(w)
. Thus,
x
would be in the meet of any pair of elements greater than
(j)
that are not in
[(j),C_{1}(j)]\cup[(j),C_{2}(j)]
and so the complement of
[(j),C_{1}(j)]\cup[(j),C_{2}(j)]
is closed under the meet. Therefore, the complement of
[(j),C_{1}(j)]\cup[(j),C_{2}(j)]
is a sublattice.

(a)

Suppose that
(x)\notin C_{i}
. Then
(x)\neq C_{i}(x)
and so
(x)<C_{i}(x)
. But then
(j)<(x)<C_{i}(x)
and hence
(j)\nprec C_{i}(x)
. That is,
(j)\neq C_{i}(j)
and so
(j)
is not on chain
C_{i}
, either.

Thus, if we assume
(x)
is not in
C_{1}
or
C_{2}
, then
(j)
is in neither chain, as well.

(b)

Without loss of generality, suppose
(x)\in C_{1}
. Then
(x_{1})=C_{1}(x)\succ C_{1}(j)\geq(j)
and
(x)\succ(j)
. Thus it must be the case that
(j)=C_{1}(j)
; that is, that
(j)
is on chain
C_{1}
.

We know that
[(j),C_{1}(j)]\cup[(j),C_{2}(j)]
is the complement of a sublattice, but in this case
[(j),C_{1}(j)]=\{(j)\}
and so
[(j),C_{1}(j)]\cup[(j),C_{2}(j)]=[(j),C_{2}(j)]
is the complement of a sublattice.

(2)

Suppose
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
and without loss of generality that
x_{1}\in C_{2}(j)
. Then
C_{2}(x_{2})<C_{2}(j)
.

Say
u>(j)
and
u\notin[(j),C_{1}(j)]
. We know
u=C_{1}(u)\wedge C_{2}(u)
. Since
u\nleq C_{1}(j)
,
C_{1}(u)>C_{1}(j)>C_{1}(x_{1})

and hence
x_{1}\in C_{1}(u)
. Also, since
(j)<u\leq C_{2}(u)
, we know
C_{2}(x_{1})<C_{2}(j)\leq C_{2}(u)
. So,
x_{1}\in C_{2}(u)
as well. That is,
x_{1}\in C_{1}(u)\wedge C_{2}(u)=u
. Hence, the meet of any two elements bigger than
(j)
but not in
[(j),C_{1}(j)]
will contain
x_{1}
and, hence, will also not be in
[(j),C_{1}(j)]
. Therefore, the complement of
[(j),C_{1}(j)]
is a sublattice.

(3)

Suppose
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
and, without loss of generality, that
x_{1}\notin C_{2}(j)
. We need to show that
[(j),C_{1}(j)]
is not the complement of a sublattice.

Since
x_{1}\notin C_{2}(j)
,
C_{2}(j)<C_{2}(x_{1})
and since
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
and
C_{2}(j)\prec C_{2}(x_{2})
, this implies
C_{2}(x_{2})<C_{2}(x_{1})
. Then
x_{1}\notin C_{2}(x_{2})
and hence
x_{1}\notin(x_{1})\cap C_{2}(x_{2})
. So,
(x_{1})\cap C_{2}(x_{2})<(x_{1})
. However, it is the case that
j\in C_{2}(x_{1})
and so
j\in C_{1}(x_{1})\cap C_{2}(x_{1})=(x_{1})
and
j\in C_{2}(x_{2})
. That is,
(j)\leq(x_{1})\wedge C_{2}(x_{2})<(x_{1})
.

By lower semi-modularity, since
C_{1}(j)\prec C_{1}(x_{1})
, either
C_{1}(j)\wedge(x_{1})=(x_{1})
or
C_{1}(j)\wedge(x_{1})\prec(x_{1})
. Since
C_{1}(j)\ngeq(x_{1})
, it must be the case that
C_{1}(j)\wedge(x_{1})=(x_{1})_{*}\prec(x_{1})
. But then, since
(x_{1})\wedge C_{2}(x_{2})<(x_{1})
,
we obtain
(j)\leq(x_{1})\wedge C_{2}(x_{2})\leq(x_{1})_{*}=C_{1}(j)\wedge(x_{1})\leq C_{1}(j)
. That is,
(x_{1})\wedge C_{2}(x_{2})\in[(j),C_{1}(j)]
, while

(x_{1}),C_{2}(x_{2})\not\leq C_{1}(j)
.
Therefore,
[(j),C_{1}(j)]
is not the complement of a sublattice.

∎

Lemma 6.5
.

Suppose
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}=\left<X,\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\right>
is a convex geometry of
cdim=2
generated by chains
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, with trivial intersection, and that
\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
is the complement of a sublattice
\mathop{\mathcal{S}}
of
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}
. Let
(j)\in\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
be a maximal join irreducible element of
\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
.

(1)

Suppose that for some
x\in X
,
C_{i}(j)\prec C_{i}(x)
for each
i=1,2
. Then
[(j),C_{1}(j)]\cup[(j),C_{2}(j)]\subseteq\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
.

(2)

If
C_{i}(j)\prec C_{i}(x_{i})
for some
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
, then either
x_{1}\in C_{2}(j)
or
x_{2}\in C_{1}(j)
.

(3)

Suppose that
C_{i}(j)\prec C_{i}(x_{i})
for some
x_{1}\neq x_{2}

[(j),C_{i}(j)]\subseteq\mathop{\mathcal{C}}

[(j),C_{i^{\prime}}(j)]\subseteq\mathop{\mathcal{C}}

(4)

If
C_{i}(j)\prec C_{i}(x_{i})
for some

, then for at least one of

\mathop{\mathcal{C}}

[(j),C_{i}(j)]

Proof.

Assume the hypothesis.

(
1

Suppose that for some
x\in X
,
C_{i}(j)\prec C_{i}(x)
for each
i=1,2
.
We know
(j)=C_{1}(j)\wedge C_{2}(j)\leq C_{1}(x)\wedge C_{2}(x)=(x)
and since
(j)\neq(x)
, this implies
(j)<(x)
and hence by our choice of
(j)
,
(x)\in\mathop{\mathcal{S}}
. Also, by semimodularity, since

but
x\in(x)
,
C_{i}(j)\wedge(x)\prec(x)
. But,
j\in C_{i}(j)\wedge(x)=(x)_{*}
and so
(x)_{*}\in[(j),C_{i}(j)]
for each
i
, which means that
(j)=(x)_{*}
by Lemma
6.3
(
2
). Furthermore, since
(j)=(x)_{*}
and
(x)\notin[(j),C_{i}(j)]
for either

, for each

and so

. That is,
[(j),C_{1}(j)]\cup[(j),C_{2}(j)]\subseteq\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
.

(
2
)

Suppose that
C_{i}(j)\prec C_{i}(x_{i})
for some
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
. Suppose by way of contradiction that
x_{1}\not\in C_{2}(j)
and
x_{2}\not\in C_{1}(j)
. Then
C_{2}(j)<C_{2}(x_{1})
. Therefore,
(x_{1})=C_{1}(x_{1})\cap C_{2}(x_{1})>C_{1}(j)\cap C_{2}(j)=(j)
and so by our choice of
j
,
(x_{1})\in\mathop{\mathcal{S}}
.
Since
C_{1}(j)\prec C_{1}(x_{1})
, by lower semimodularity we know

. That is,
C_{1}(j)\wedge C_{2}(x_{1})=(x_{1})_{*}
. But we know then, since
(j)<(x_{1})
,
(j)\leq(x_{1})_{*}\leq C_{1}(j)
; that is,
(x_{1})_{*}\in[(j),C_{1}(j)]
. Analogously,
(x_{2})\in\mathop{\mathcal{S}}
and
(x_{2})_{*}\in[(j),C_{2}(j)]
and
(x_{2})\in\mathop{\mathcal{S}}
. But then, since

and elements
(x_{1})
and
(x_{2})

.

Thus, either

x_{2}\in C_{1}(j)
.

(
3
)

Suppose that
C_{i}(j)\prec C_{i}(x_{i})
and

. Assume without loss of generality that
x_{1}\in C_{2}(j)
. By Lemma

(

.

x_{2}\notin C_{1}(j)
. Then,
C_{1}(j)\prec C_{1}(x_{1})<C_{1}(x_{2})
and

(x_{2})=C_{1}(x_{2})\wedge C_{2}(x_{2})\geq C_{1}(j)\wedge C_{2}(j)=(j)

(x_{2})\neq(j)
. Hence, by our choice of

,
(x_{2})\in\mathop{\mathcal{S}}
. By lower semimodularity, since

C_{1}(x_{2})\wedge C_{2}(j)\prec C_{1}(x_{2})\wedge C_{2}(x_{2})=(x_{2})

Thus, if

.

(

)

Suppose

) either
x_{1}\in C_{2}(j)
or

. Without loss of generality, suppose that

),

is the complement of a sublattice. By Lemma

(

) either
[(j),C_{1}(j)]\subseteq\mathop{\mathcal{S}}

x_{2}\in C_{1}(j)

),
[(j),C_{2}(j)]
is the complement of a sublattice.

Note that in each case, there is an

such that

and
[(j),C_{i}(j)]\subseteq\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
.

∎

We finally prove Theorem
6.1
.

Proof.

(of Theorem
6.1
)
Let
\mathop{\mathcal{G}}=\left<X,\mathop{\mathcal{G}}\right>
be a convex geometry with
cdim=2
and generating chains
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
. In light of Observation
3.1
from Section
3
we may assume
C_{1}
ad
C_{2}
have trivial intersection. Let
\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
be the complement of some maximal sublattice
\mathop{\mathcal{M}}
.
\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
has at least one minimal element, which must be join irreducible. Thus, we can choose a maximal join irreducible element
(j)
in
\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
.

(1)

Suppose that, without loss of generality
C_{2}(j)=X
. Then
(j)=M_{1}(j)\wedge C_{2}(j)=M_{1}(j)
. That is
(j)
is doubly irreducible and hence
\mathop{\mathcal{C}}=\{(j)\}
. We know

and so there is an

with
(j)=C_{1}(j)\prec C_{1}(x)

C_{1}(j)
is meet irreducible,
(j)=C_{1}(j)\prec C_{1}(x)\leq C_{2}(j)
.
That is,
[(j),C_{1}(j)]=\{(j)\}
is a complement of type
1
of Theorem
6.1
.

(2)

Suppose that
C_{i}(j)\neq X
for either
i
. Then for some
x_{i}\in X
,
C_{i}(j)\prec C_{1}(x_{i})
. We have two cases:
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
or
x_{1}=x_{2}
.

(a)

Suppose
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
. Then by Lemma
6.5
(
4
) for some

we have
C_{i^{\prime}}(x_{i})<C_{i^{\prime}}(j)
,
[(j),C_{i}(j)]\subseteq\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
and
[(j),C_{i}(j)]
is the complement of a sublattice. Since
\mathop{\mathcal{M}}
is a maximal sublattice, it must be the case that
[(j),C_{i}(j)]=\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
.

Notice that in this case, since

, we know
x_{i}\notin(j)
and
x_{i}\in C_{i^{\prime}}(j)
and thus
(j)\neq C_{i^{\prime}}(j)
. Hence,
(j)
is not on chain
C_{i^{\prime}}
.
Thus in this case
\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
is a complement of type (
1
) of Theorem
6.1
.

(b)

Suppose
x_{1}=x_{2}
. Then by Lemma
6.5
(
1
) we have one of the two following possibilities:

(i)

(x_{1})
is on neither chain, in which case neither is
(j)
and
[(j),C_{1}(j)]\cup[(j),C_{2}(j)]\subseteq\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
and is itself the complement of a sublattice. Thus, since
\mathop{\mathcal{M}}
is maximal,
\mathop{\mathcal{C}}=[(j),C_{1}(j)]\cup[(j),C_{2}(j)]
. In this case
\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
is a complement of type (
2
) of Theorem
6.1
.

(ii)

(x_{1})
is on
C_{i}
for one
i
, in which case
(j)
is also on
C_{i}
and
[(j),C_{i^{\prime}}(j)]\subseteq\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
. But then
\mathop{\mathcal{C}}=[(j),C_{i^{\prime}}(j)]
since
\mathop{\mathcal{M}}
is maximal. Thus
\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
is a complement of type
3
of Theorem
6.1
.

∎

Note that Hypotheses
2
and
3
for CGs with
cdim=2
follow from Theorem
6.1
immediately, as does an affirmative answer for Question
2
. In fact, we also have Corollary
6.6
, below.

Corollary 6.6
.

For every element
a
in the complement of a maximal sublattice
\mathop{\mathcal{M}}
of a CG with
cdim=2
, there is an
m\in\mathop{\mathcal{M}}
with
m\prec a
.

Proof.

We just have to note that the only join irreducible element of an interval
[(j),C_{i}(j)]
is
(j)
. Indeed, if
(j)\leq(s)\leq C_{i}(j)
, then
C_{i}(s)=C_{i}(j)
as
C_{i}(j)
must necessarily be the first element of
C_{i}
bigger than or equal to
(s)
. Thus
s=j
.

∎

This suggests the following hypothesis in convex geometries.

Hypothesis 4
.

For every element
x\in\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
of convex geometry
(X,\mathop{\mathcal{G}})
, where
\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
is the complement of a maximal sublattice
\mathop{\mathcal{M}}
, there exists
m\prec x
with
m\in\mathop{\mathcal{M}}
. Moreover,
[0,m]\subseteq\mathop{\mathcal{M}}
.

Lemma
6.7
tells us if Hypothesis
4
is correct, then Hypothesis
3
is correct for CGs.

Lemma 6.7
.

If Hypothesis
4
is correct, then minimal complement
\mathop{\mathcal{C}}
must be convex.

Proof.

Suppose
c_{1}<m<c_{2}
with
m\in\mathop{\mathcal{M}}
,
c_{1},c_{2}\in C
. By the hypothesis, there exists
m_{1}\prec c_{2}
,
m_{1}\in\mathop{\mathcal{M}}
. Then
m\leq m_{1}
, otherwise,
m\vee m_{1}=c_{2}\in\mathop{\mathcal{M}}
. But then
c_{1}\in[0,m_{1}]
, a contradiction.
∎

Note that the hypothesis fails in bounded lattices, as Figure
4
demonstrates.

Proposition 6.10
.

Algorithm
6.8
runs in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time with respect to
n=|X|
.

Proof.

Let us notice that the representation of the input to the algorithm as presented in
6.2
it is of the size
n
. We would like to answer the question regarding complexity relating to the size of input. In the loop, the algorithm processes each element of
X
. For each element
j
, where
j=1,2,\dots n
, we need to establish the following objects:

•

C_{1}(j)=\{1,2,\dots,n\}

•

C_{2}(j)=\{\phi(1),\phi(2),\dots,\phi(\phi^{-1}(j))\}

•

(j)=C_{1}(j)\cap C_{2}(j)
.

All of this may be computed at the preprocessing stage of the algorithm implementation, going from the bottom element of the chain up to the top one. Each of this computation complexity is
\mathcal{O}(n)
for all elements in
X
. Hence the cost of preprocessing is
\mathcal{O}(n)
.

Now, let us list the types of comparisons which are made within the algorithm, for some
j,k\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
:

(1)

Does
(j)=C_{1}(j)
?

(2)

Does
(j)=C_{2}(j)
?

(3)

Does
j+1\in C_{2}(j)
?

(4)

Does
\phi^{-1}(j)\not=n
?

(5)

Does
j+1=\phi(\phi^{-1}(j)+1)
?

(6)

Does
\phi(\phi^{-1}(j)+1)\in C_{1}(j)
?

The complexity of each step is
\mathcal{O}(1)
assuming the preprocessing has been carried out. Since every step is of
\mathcal{O}(1)
time complexity and these steps are performed for each
a\in X
then the algorithm main part’s complexity is
\mathcal{O}(n)
.

Having the preprocessing at time cost of
\mathcal{O}(n)
and the main part of the algorithm with the same complexity, we obtain that presented algorithm time complexity is
\mathcal{O}(n)
.

∎

Future Work

At the time of submission of this work, we have preliminary results on the structure of the Frattini sublattice (the intersection of maximal sublattices of a lattice) obtained for convex geometries of
cdim=2
