Trust Through Others’ Eyes: An Experiment on How Vicarious Health Care Experiences Shape System Trust
Authors: SILVIA CANNAS, MARIA CUCCINIELLO
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Policy Points Negative vicarious health care experiences circulating within communities can significantly erode systemic trust, while positive experiences can help rebuild it.Health systems should actively monitor the narratives circulating within distinct community networks, treating patterns of negative shared experiences as early signals of eroding institutional trust that warrant a proactive organizational response.Investing in the quality of patient–provider relationships could be a concrete lever for institutional trust resilience: strong provider relationships buffer individuals—including those in historically marginalized communities—against the corrosive effects of negative systemic narratives circulating in their networks.Health systems should invest in creating structured opportunities for patients to share their positive care experiences, ensuring that these voices reach community networks where trust has been hardest to build and where authentic accounts of respectful, equitable care are currently underrepresented. ContextTrust in the US health care system has declined substantially in recent years, threatening patient engagement, care outcomes, and health policy effectiveness. While prior research confirms that direct personal experiences with providers shape trust, questions remain about the influence of vicarious experiences—the health care stories shared by family members, friends, and community networks. This gap is particularly consequential in the United States, where structural inequalities have created distinct collective memories and expectations that circulate within racial and ethnic communities. This study addresses this gap by examining how the emotional valence (positive or negative) and racial identity of the narrators influence trust in the US health care system, with attention to variation by respondents’ race and baseline trust in their own provider.MethodsWe conducted a 2×2 between‐subjects online survey experiment with 1,042 US adults. The sample was demographically representative by age, education, and gender, and evenly divided between non‐Hispanic White and non‐Hispanic Black participants. Respondents were randomly assigned to eight brief vignettes that varied in two dimensions: (1) emotional valence (predominantly positive vs. predominantly negative) and (2) narrators’ racial identity (majority in‐group vs. majority out‐group).FindingsWe find that vicarious experiences significantly affect trust in the US health care system—but not uniformly. Non‐Hispanic White participants were not affected by in‐group experiences and responded to out‐group accounts, while the contrary held for non‐Hispanic Blacks. Trust in one's own doctor moderated these effects: strong patient–provider relationships buffered individuals from the impact of systemic stories, whereas low provider trust heightened susceptibility to narrative influence.ConclusionsThese findings underscore the role of collective narratives in shaping systemic trust and highlight new avenues for its strengthening among diverse populations.

Public trust in health care is eroding—and it is doing so worldwide. After COVID‐19 exposed deep fault lines in public health infrastructures and politicized the very act of seeking care, rebuilding public trust in health institutions has become one of the defining challenges of the post‐pandemic era. 1 This decline in trust is not confined to health care; data from the Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development 2 and the Edelman Trust Barometer 3 report that trust in governments, media, and public institutions more broadly has deteriorated across most societies, with health care trust increasingly caught in the crossfire of this wider crisis.

The United States reflects this pattern with particular acuity. Cross‐national evidence from the 2021 International Social Survey Programme shows that low public confidence in health care systems is widespread (Appendix Figure A1), though the United States ranks among the higher‐concern countries, closer in profile to Russia, Poland, and South Africa. Confirming this alarming scenario, a 2023 Gallup national survey found that only 34% of Americans expressed strong confidence in the medical system, down ten points from 2021. 4 This erosion of trust threatens patient engagement, care outcomes, and the success of health policies. 5 , 6 , 7

While research confirms that direct personal experiences with providers shape trust, 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 questions remain about the influence of vicarious experiences—health care encounters of family members, friends, and community members that individuals live through indirectly, as if they were partially their own. 12 How do individuals use new information about the ways health care systems treat others? Does this information get integrated into their system of beliefs and expectations to shape their trust judgements? These questions, we contend, deserve attention. Yet despite growing concerns about system‐level trust, policy discussions rarely address whether and how these secondhand narratives might influence public trust in health care institutions. 13 , 14

Trust in health care has been conceptualized as a multidimensional construct—encompassing competence, honesty, fidelity, and fairness 15 , 16 —though scholars differ on which dimensions apply beyond the patient–provider relationship to more impersonal, systemic judgments. Our study focuses on this systemic dimension of trust: the global evaluative assessment of whether the health care system as a whole can be relied upon, which is the form of trust most likely to be shaped by circulating social narratives.

Secondhand experiences may be particularly consequential in the United States, which provides a theoretically distinctive setting for studying the mechanisms through which systemic trust is formed and transmitted. People possess multiple social identities, but not all are equally salient or accessible in a given context. Research demonstrates the speed and automaticity with which racial group membership is encoded when perceiving others, 17 , 18 and sociological theory has long recognized race as a “master status”—a characteristic that dominates social perception regardless of other attributes. 19 Race functions as a chronically accessible identity in US society, 20 , 21 and its salience is especially heightened in health care settings. The well‐documented history of structural racism in US health care institutions—including the systematic medical mistreatment of Black Americans and its enduring legacy 22 , 23 , 24 —has created distinct collective memories and expectations that circulate within racial and ethnic communities. 25 , 26 , 27 This constellation of features motivates our country selection.

In this study, we conduct a between‐subjects 2×2 randomized survey experiment exploring how the emotional valence (predominantly positive or negative) and source (majority in‐group or out‐group) of shared health care stories affect trust in the US health care system. We define “valence” as the emotional tone of a health care experience—whether it is predominantly positive or negative. A story with positive valence might describe respectful treatment or high‐quality care, while a story with negative valence might describe being dismissed, mistreated, or misdiagnosed.

While prior (largely descriptive) research on policing suggests that bad or good vicarious experiences can shape public attitudes in a predictable way, 28 the role of these narratives in health care remains understudied. Building on this line of inquiry, we test whether exposure to vicarious experiences that are in great part negative leads to lower levels of health care system trust compared to positive experiences. Trust is in itself a generalized expectancy, 29 and vicarious experiences may act as prompts to revise it. Exposure to negative narratives may lead individuals to lower their assessment of the system's reliability, while positive ones may support an upward revision—particularly among those with limited direct experience or whose confidence in the system has already been eroded.

But whether this updating process is set in motion may itself depend on who delivers the information and how relevant it feels to the receiver. Social identity theory suggests that people may attend more carefully to narratives from members of their own race, 30 , 31 perceiving in‐group sources as more credible and their experiences as more personally diagnostic of their own likely treatment. 32 , 33 This leads us to expect that vicarious health care experiences will, at least in part, exert stronger effects on systemic trust when they come from racial in‐group members.

Yet in‐group identification may also shape which information is perceived as relevant given the expectations brought to the encounter. The circulation of vicarious experiences provides people with information that reveals how the system treats members of social groups they do not belong to, expanding the informational basis on which trust judgments are built. For non‐Hispanic Whites, for instance, receiving information on how the system treats members of society who have historically been mistreated by health institutions may provide a stronger test of system trustworthiness than hearing about in‐group experiences. Interestingly, Expectancy Violation Theory (EVT) 34 , 35 proposes that information disconfirming prior expectations attracts heightened attention and exerts disproportionate influence on attitudes—suggesting that the same narrative may carry different weight depending on whether it confirms or violates what a person already anticipates.

We contend that in‐group credibility and expectancy‐based mechanisms are not mutually exclusive; one's social position simultaneously shapes which sources feel credible, which expectations one brings to the encounter, and which information feels surprising or consequential. Together, these considerations lead us to expect that the racial identity of the narrator shapes the effects of vicarious experiences differently for White and Black Americans—and motivates our empirical exploration of these asymmetries. 36 We also investigate a further moderating factor: whether strong trust in one's personal doctor might buffer against broader trust erosion triggered by negative systemic narratives.

This research meets recent scholarly concerns about oversimplification of the relationship between race and health care trust. Taylor and colleagues argue that the literature has problematically treated racial identity as a direct predictor of trust rather than examining the experiences and mechanisms—such as discrimination, oppression, and structural racism—that shape trust formation among racially marginalized communities. 37 By focusing on vicarious experiences as a mechanism through which information about systemic inequality circulates—and by examining whether in‐group narratives carry particular weight—our study addresses these concerns.

By determining whether and how vicarious experiences shape public trust in health care—especially across racially diverse communities—this research offers actionable insights for health communicators, system leaders, and policymakers. If vicarious experiences prove influential, as we hypothesize, these findings could inform novel, evidence‐based approaches to addressing the trust crisis currently challenging American health care.

Study Data and Methods

Study Setting

We conducted an online survey experiment with US adults recruited through Qualtrics, a firm specializing in online social research. Our respondents matched the US demographic profile in terms of age, education, and gender. Racially, the sample was evenly divided between non‐Hispanic Whites and non‐Hispanic Blacks. To qualify, participants had to be US citizens aged 18 or older. Qualtrics handled the invitations, researchers did not offer extra compensation, and participation was voluntary. Data were collected as part of a broader project that received ethics approval from the Bocconi Research Ethics Committee on November 29, 2021.

We administered the experiment to 1,042 individuals. The sample size was defined based on a power calculation conducted using G*Power. We calculated that a total sample size of 725 participants was required to achieve sufficient statistical power (Appendix Section 2). We intentionally oversampled to increase the precision of estimates, ensure adequate power for detecting potential interaction effects, and enable subgroup analyses.

Study Design

We implemented a 2×2 between‐subjects online survey experiment in which we manipulated (1) the valence of the reported experiences with health care (predominantly positive vs. predominantly negative) and (2) the sources of the experiences (majority in‐group vs. majority out‐group).

Respondents were shown a realistic web page from CRoPP (Appendix Figure A2), a (fictitious) nonpartisan research institute “known for reliable data and rigorous analysis.” The page displayed results from a mixed‐methods study in which CRoPP researchers would have asked US citizens not only to rate their satisfaction with the health care system but also to provide personal anecdotes explaining their ratings. Our respondents viewed eight concise paragraphs (Section 3.b), each recounting a different health care experience, accompanied by photos and initials of fabricated sources. These stories were presented within a box that cited the organization's name to make the treatment appear more realistic (Appendix Figures A3, A4, A5, A6).

To examine the impact of source characteristics on trust, we manipulated the racial diversity of the authors whose experiences were shown to participants. In the majority in‐group condition, participants were presented with experiences from six individuals of their own race and two from a different race. Conversely, in the majority out‐group condition, the configuration was reversed. We utilized photos purchased from iStock, representing various age groups and genders, to visually signify the race of each author.

Several precautionary steps were taken to minimize the risk of uncontrolled visual confounds. All images were standardized to the same size to eliminate variation in visual salience. Facial expressions were deliberately neutral to minimize differential warmth perceptions—an approach informed by research showing that positive affect is associated with higher wealth and social power, 38 , 39 meaning that expressive photos could inadvertently signal socioeconomic status (SES) differences. 40 To control for gender effects on trustworthiness perceptions, 41 exactly half of the photos in each condition depicted women and half depicted men. Finally, photos were selected to ensure age equivalence across treatment arms, so that no condition was systematically skewed toward younger or older faces.

For valence, we varied the content of experiences, keeping word lengths consistent between positive and negative stories. Given the multidimensional nature of trust, the narratives presented covered competence, communication, honesty, confidentiality, and fairness. 16 The predominantly positive condition showed participants six positive and two negative experiences, with the inverse in predominantly negative condition.

This 6:2 ratio was a design choice grounded in ecological validity: In real‐world social networks, vicarious health care experiences rarely arrive as uniformly positive or negative streams but rather as a predominantly valenced flow of stories—mostly good or mostly bad—with occasional counterexamples. Our design mirrors how trust‐relevant narratives are actually encountered and processed in everyday life and follows an established convention in the vicarious experience literature. Sparks and Browning, 42 in their experimental study of online reviews and trust, explicitly operationalized valence as predominance, mixing majority‐valenced reviews with a minority of opposing ones, justifying this choice on the grounds that an entirely positive or negative set “would seem unrealistic.” 42 Experiences were also racially tailored. For instance, Black participants in a majority in‐group, predominantly positive condition read six positive experiences from Black sources and two negative experiences from White sources.

The vignettes were developed based on academic and non‐academic sources, 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 tested in a pilot study for valence and source identification, and refined based on feedback to ensure clarity and effectiveness.

Randomization

Participants were randomly assigned to one of four treatment arms within their racial block (Appendix Tables A1 and A2): (1) majority in‐group—predominantly positive, (2) majority in‐group—predominantly negative, (3) majority out‐group—predominantly positive, or (4) majority out‐group—predominantly negative.

Outcome and Moderating Variables

Our primary variable of interest was trust in the health care system, measured with a single‐item question asking, “In general, how much do you trust the health care system as a whole?” 52 Participants were asked to respond on a scale of one to ten, where one meant “Do not trust at all” and ten meant “Trust completely.”

We operationalized systemic trust as a global evaluative judgment—the overall assessment of whether the health care system as a whole can be relied upon. Although trust has been conceptualized as a multidimensional construct, 15 , 16 available multi‐item batteries measuring systemic trust were developed and validated in health care systems structurally different from the US context, 53 , 54 and range from 17‐38 items—a burden that would risk measurement fatigue and bias between treatment arms when administered post‐treatment in an experimental design. 55 , 56 We therefore relied on a single‐item measure drawn from NORC national surveys, 57 an approach consistent with how institutional trust is operationalized in major national and cross‐national data collections, including the European Social Survey, the World Values Survey, and a recent Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development health care survey. 2

Before treatment, and after a set of sociodemographic questions, we collected data on trust in respondents’ own doctors using a single‐item measure. The question asked was, “We are interested in knowing more about how you feel about your family or primary care doctor. On a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), how much do you agree with the following statement? All in all, I have complete trust in my doctor.” 15 We intended to use this measure to assess its potential moderation of the experimental factors.

Analysis

We employed ordinary least squares (OLS) regression with robust standard errors to assess the effects of the experimental treatments on trust in the health care system. Gender, age, education, income, and marital status were included as control variables. The analysis was first conducted on the full sample, followed by models that included race as an interaction term. In addition, we estimated regressions to explore whether trust in one's family or primary care doctor moderated treatment effects, both in the overall sample and within each racial subgroup.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, participants were informed at the outset that the study investigated how social interactions influence health care attitudes and were notified that some deception would be involved. Although ethically necessary, this disclosure may have influenced participants’ responses, particularly among individuals from communities historically subjected to unethical medical experimentation, potentially reducing their willingness to express candid views. We therefore emphasize the importance of transparency, ethical reflexivity, and thorough debriefing in future studies addressing trust within populations marked by historical inequity. Second, the design of the vignettes (e.g., six positive and two negative stories, or vice versa) may have revealed the experimental manipulation to some participants, potentially prompting socially desirable answers. Third, while our sample was quota‐balanced to match the US population on gender, age, and education, reliance on an online research panel may limit generalizability, as panel participants may differ systematically from the broader population. Also, while our exploratory moderation analyses by interpersonal trust and race yielded suggestive patterns, their interpretability is constrained by small subgroup sizes—particularly where multiple interaction terms are considered simultaneously.

Although several steps were taken during photo selection to minimize uncontrolled visual variation, stock photo sets introduce an inherent degree of uncontrolled variability in facial and social cues that cannot be fully eliminated. A post‐hoc validation study provided reassurance that our findings are not attributable to spurious differences in perceived warmth, competence, and SES across racial conditions. Nevertheless, future research building on this work should draw from standardized, pre‐validated face databases—such as the Chicago Face Database 58 —to enable tighter experimental control over visual stimuli from the outset.

In addition, our 6:2 story ratio, while grounded in ecological validity, did not allow us to fully isolate the role of narrative sentiment from that of exposure intensity in producing the overall main effect of valence. Future designs should systematically vary this ratio to disentangle these potential contributions.

Finally, while our experimental design provides strong internal validity, the online vignette format inevitably limits ecological realism. Reading short, text‐based narratives differs from how people typically encounter and discuss health care experiences in everyday social contexts. Nevertheless, the use of a large, demographically representative sample enhances the generalizability of our results, offering credible insights into the mechanisms linking vicarious experience and system trust.

Experimental Results

When considering the overall sample (Appendix Section 8), our findings suggest that it is the valence of the shared experiences—not their source—that most strongly influences trust. Participants reported significantly higher levels of trust in response to exposure to positive vicarious experiences (in‐group: mean = 6.442; 95% CI, 6.190‐6.695; out‐group: mean = 6.681; 95% CI, 6.434‐6.928) compared to negative experiences (in‐group: mean = 5.621; 95% CI, 5.317‐5.925; out‐group: mean = 5.784; 95% CI, 5.515‐6.053). These patterns held irrespective of whether participants were assigned to the majority in‐group or majority out‐group condition.

However, we wondered if White and Black respondents differed in how they were affected by new information about their system of health care (Appendix Section 9).

Interacting race with the two experimental factors revealed distinct patterns in how in‐group and out‐group vicarious experiences affected Black and White respondents’ trust in the health system (Figure 1). For White participants, the valence of out‐group narratives significantly shaped systemic trust: Exposure to predominantly positive vicarious experiences lived by (a majority of) Black individuals led to significantly higher trust in the health system (mean = 6.74; 95% CI, 6.41‐7.08) than exposure to out‐group's negative experiences (mean = 5.50; 95% CI, 5.13‐5.87). In contrast, the valence of in‐group narratives did not significantly differentiate trust levels among White respondents: Those exposed to predominantly positive experiences reported similar trust to those exposed to predominantly negative ones (positive: mean = 6.13; 95% CI, 5.78‐6.49; negative: mean = 5.64; 95% CI, 5.21‐6.07).

For Black participants, it was the valence of in‐group narratives that significantly differentiated trust: Exposure to predominantly positive experiences from (a majority of) Black narrators was associated with significantly higher trust (mean = 6.73; 95% CI, 6.37‐7.10) than exposure to predominantly negative in‐group experiences (mean = 5.62; 95% CI, 5.18‐6.05). Notably, we found no difference between trust levels among respondents exposed to predominantly positive (mean = 6.62; 95% CI, 6.25‐6.99) or negative (mean = 6.09; 95% CI, 5.69‐6.49) out‐group experiences.

We were curious to understand whether pretreatment trust in respondents’ own doctors moderated treatment effects (Appendix Section 10). Due to the substantial negative skew in the distribution of this variable (Appendix Figure A8), we dichotomized it at the median value. Responses equal to or greater than the median (4) were categorized as indicating high trust, while responses below the median (ranging from 1‐3) were categorized as low trust in one's doctor.

First, we looked into the overall sample and found that trust in the health care system was significantly lower across all conditions among respondents who reported low trust in their own doctor. For participants with high trust in their doctor, exposure to positive or negative experiences resulted in nonsignificant differences in trust in the health care system. This pattern held across both the in‐group condition (positive: mean = 6.948; 95% CI, 6.659‐7.237; negative: mean = 6.296; 95% CI, 5.928‐6.664) and the out‐group condition (positive: mean = 7.067; 95% CI, 6.785‐7.349; negative: mean = 6.449; 95% CI, 6.124‐6.773). In contrast, among participants with low trust in their doctor, the valence of the experience had a strong effect (Cohen's d ≈ 0.46). Those exposed to predominantly positive stories reported significantly higher levels of trust (in‐group: mean = 5.384; 95% CI, 4.948‐5.819; out‐group: mean = 5.859; 95% CI, 5.410‐6.308) compared to those who read predominantly negative stories (in‐group: mean = 4.306; 95% CI, 3.870‐4.743; out‐group: mean = 4.817, 95% CI, 4.414‐5.221).

Exploratorily, we looked within each racial group (Figures 2 and 3). Non‐Hispanic White respondents in the majority in‐group conditions consistently reported lower trust in the health care system if trust in their doctor was low compared to those with high trust, regardless of whether the experiences they read about were predominantly positive (low: mean = 5.11; 95% CI, 4.46‐5.75; high: mean = 6.76; 95% CI, 6.35‐7.12) or negative (low: mean = 4.39; 95% CI, 3.75‐5.04). In contrast, in the out‐group conditions, the pattern shifts: Among participants with low baseline trust in their doctor, those exposed to predominantly positive experiences reported significantly higher levels of trust in the health care system than those exposed to negative ones (positive: mean = 6.28; 95% CI, 5.62‐6.94; negative: mean = 4.46; 95% CI, 3.93‐5.02). Notably, these individuals reach systemic trust levels comparable to the high‐trust‐in‐doctor participants under the same (positive out‐group) condition (mean = 6.18; 95% CI, 5.67‐6.70). This suggests that exposure to positive, out‐group vicarious experiences may be particularly powerful in restoring or enhancing trust in the system of health care, even among those with skepticism toward individual providers.2, 3

Even non‐Hispanic Black respondents with low preexisting trust in their doctor reported lower levels of trust in the health care system compared to their high‐trust counterparts, regardless of the experimental condition to which they were assigned. Importantly, among low trust in doctor individuals, exposure to the positive in‐group condition was associated with significantly higher trust in the health care system (mean = 5.66; 95% CI, 5.08‐6.24,) than exposure to the negative in‐group condition (mean = 4.29; 95% CI, 3.61‐4.80). This suggests that, for individuals with low baseline trust, exposure to affirming experiences from racially concordant sources may have a meaningful impact on their trust in the system of health care. This pattern did not hold in the out‐group condition; among low‐trust participants, levels of trust remained relatively similar whether the out‐group experiences described were predominantly positive or negative.

Discussion

This study illuminates how vicarious experiences—health care encounters of family members, friends, and community members lived through indirectly as if partially one's own—shape trust in the US health care system. Through a between‐subjects survey experiment, we examined how the emotional tone (positive or negative) and racial identity of the narrator influence systemic trust, with particular attention to how these effects vary by respondents’ race and baseline trust in their own health care providers.

Our findings reveal that the effects of these two factors are not uniform across respondents. Overall, the emotional valence of shared experiences emerged as the dominant influence on systemic trust. However, this overall pattern concealed important racial asymmetries that became visible when race was examined as a moderating factor.

For non‐Hispanic White participants, the difference in trust between positive and negative experiences was significant only when narratives came from racial out‐group members; exposure to in‐group narratives produced no significant difference in systemic trust regardless of valence. For non‐Hispanic Black participants, the pattern was reversed: A significant difference between positive and negative experiences emerged only in the in‐group condition, whereas exposure to out‐group narratives from White narrators had no significant effect on trust. These findings suggest that the racial identity of a narrator shapes how health care narratives are perceived and internalized, likely reflecting broader dynamics of social identification and lived experience.

The asymmetric patterns we observe across racial groups are consistent with several potential theoretical mechanisms. Social identity theory suggests that group membership shapes which information individuals perceive as personally relevant and credible. People are more attentive to, and more likely to internalize, experiences from those who share their social identity, as in‐group accounts are seen as more directly informative about what they themselves can expect from the system. Under this interpretation, narratives shared by Black individuals may have been especially consequential for Black respondents because they provided information about how the health care system treats people in their own social position. However, the response pattern among White participants, who reacted more strongly to narratives from Black narrators, suggests that a simple in‐group credibility explanation cannot fully account for the results. Alternative cognitive mechanisms may explain the asymmetric responses observed across groups. In particular, individuals’ prior expectations about how the health care system treats different racial groups may have shaped which narratives were perceived as diagnostic for evaluating institutional trustworthiness.

White and Black Americans enter health care encounters with historically and structurally distinct expectations. For White participants, who are less likely to anticipate discriminatory treatment, experiences shared by fellow White individuals may simply confirm what they already expect about how the system operates, rendering them less informative for updating trust judgments. Narratives describing the experiences of Black individuals may instead function as a stronger test of whether the system can be trusted broadly. This interpretation is consistent with the EVT, which holds that information contradicting prior expectations tends to attract greater attention and exert stronger persuasive influence. 34 , 35 Positive experiences reported by Black narrators may therefore have had a greater impact on White participants because they challenged expectations about racial disparities in health care. Our experimental design does not allow us to adjudicate among these potential mechanisms. Future research should measure baseline expectations or manipulate expectancy independently of narrator identity to clarify the psychological mechanisms involved.

We also explored whether individuals’ trust in their own doctor moderates these effects. Indeed, high trust in a personal provider emerged as a protective factor: Participants who reported strong relationships with their doctors showed similar levels of systemic trust, regardless of the stories they encountered. Among individuals with low provider trust, however, the valence of the vicarious experiences mattered greatly—in both negative and positive directions. Notably, White participants with low provider trust who were exposed to positive stories from Black narrators reported markedly higher systemic trust than those exposed to negative ones. This pattern suggests that vicarious exposure to inclusive, respectful care may be particularly consequential for institutional trust among individuals whose personal health care relationships are already fragile, though additional research is needed to assess the durability of such effects.

These findings underscore the importance of measuring and understanding trust at multiple levels within the health care system. As Merenstein and colleagues showed in their comprehensive review, trust in primary care clinicians can be reliably assessed and represents a distinct yet interconnected construct from broader institutional trust. 59 Our results suggest that future research should examine how these two dimensions of trust, interpersonal trust in one's own provider and system‐level trust in health care institutions, interact dynamically, particularly as patients gain experiences that may reinforce or challenge their beliefs about the health care system as a whole.

This study, one of the few experimental investigations of trust dynamics in health care, 37 carries important implications for health care communication and trust‐building. In an era where social narratives travel rapidly through personal networks and digital platforms, secondhand accounts of care—especially those involving racial in‐group members—may profoundly influence public trust. Because our experimental treatment involved brief exposure to narratives in a controlled online setting, further research is needed to examine whether similar mechanisms operate in sustained, real‐world communication environments.

With this in mind, we offer three implications for health system practice and policy, while acknowledging that their effectiveness and scalability in real‐world settings remain open empirical questions.

First, health systems should consider actively monitoring the narratives circulating within distinct community networks, treating patterns of negative shared experiences as early signals of eroding institutional trust that warrant a proactive organizational response. Future research should explore whether community listening infrastructures—partnerships with faith organizations, local advocacy groups, and community health workers—may provide health systems with ongoing intelligence about how their performance is being experienced and transmitted at the community level.

Second, health systems should consider investing in the quality of patient–provider relationships as a lever for institutional trust resilience. Our findings suggest that strong provider relationships buffered individuals—including those in historically marginalized communities—against the corrosive effects of negative systemic narratives, and future research should examine whether relational investment at the clinical level can function as a frontline defense against systemic trust erosion in real‐world settings.

Third, health systems may want to structure opportunities for patients to share their positive care experiences, ensuring that these voices reach community networks where trust has been hardest to build and where authentic accounts of respectful, equitable care are currently underrepresented. Future research should examine whether removing barriers that currently prevent positive experiences from circulating as widely as negative ones can influence institutional trust.

Beyond health equity, rebuilding trust in underserved communities carries potential returns for the long‐term sustainability of health care financing. Higher trust is associated with earlier engagement with care, greater treatment adherence, and reduced reliance on emergency services—all of which reduce friction costs and contribute to system efficiency.

If our findings were to be confirmed, they would raise important questions for future research regarding how collective narratives influence institutional trust. The limitations of our study point to directions for future research. For instance, our experiment focused on race as the primary identity dimension shaping responses to vicarious narratives, and we provide theoretical justification for this choice. Future research should nonetheless examine how other group memberships—including socioeconomic status, political identity, or gender—shape the effects of vicarious experiences on institutional trust, particularly in contexts where these identities may be more diagnostically relevant.

Taken together, our findings suggest that trust in health care systems is shaped not only by direct personal experiences but also by socially transmitted narratives about how others are treated. Institutional trust therefore appears to emerge from a combination of interpersonal encounters and shared interpretations of how health care systems treat different social groups.

More broadly, these results contribute to ongoing debates about how health institutions earn and maintain public legitimacy. Rather than treating trust as a purely attitudinal outcome, our findings highlight the importance of institutional trustworthiness, demonstrated through equitable treatment, transparency, and accountability, as the foundation upon which public trust ultimately rests. Moving toward more trustworthy health care systems therefore requires attention not only to interpersonal care experiences but also to the broader institutional conditions that shape them. Policymakers and health care leaders should prioritize structural reforms that reduce racial disparities in care, invest in community engagement infrastructures, and ensure that patients’ voices—especially those from marginalized groups—inform institutional practices.

Conclusion

Policies aimed at addressing structural inequalities and strengthening patient–provider relationships are essential to sustaining trust in health care systems. Yet, restoring trust requires more than improving interpersonal encounters—it demands that institutions demonstrate their trustworthiness through transparent, equitable, and accountable practices. Health systems must, therefore, treat trust as a core performance indicator, investing in the organizational capacities that sustain fairness, openness, and responsiveness.

Beyond the clinical encounter, community narratives and vicarious experiences form a powerful social infrastructure through which perceptions of legitimacy are transmitted. By engaging directly with these narrative ecosystems, acknowledging harms, amplifying positive examples of care, and co‐producing reforms with affected communities, health care organizations can begin to rebuild collective confidence. In doing so, they move from merely seeking the public's trust toward earning it, fostering institutions that are not only trusted but demonstrably worthy of trust.
