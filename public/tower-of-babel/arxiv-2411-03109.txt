pTSE-T: Presentation Target Speaker Extraction using Unaligned Text Cues
Ziyang Jiang, Xinyuan Qian, Jiahe Lei, Zexu Pan, Wei Xue, Xu-cheng Yin

Abstract
TSE ( TSE ) aims to extract the clean speech of the target speaker in an audio mixture, thus eliminating irrelevant background noise and speech. While prior work has explored various auxiliary cues including pre-recorded speech, visual information (e.g., lip motions and gestures), and spatial information, the acquisition and selection of such strong cues are infeasible in many practical scenarios. Unlike all existing work, in this paper, we condition the TSE algorithm on semantic cues extracted from limited and unaligned text content, such as condensed points from a presentation slide. This method is particularly useful in scenarios like meetings, poster sessions, or lecture presentations, where acquiring other cues in real-time is challenging. To this end, we design two different networks. Specifically, our proposed TPE ( TPE ) fuses audio features with content-based semantic cues to facilitate time-frequency mask generation to filter out extraneous noise, while another proposal, namely TSR ( TSR ), employs the contrastive learning technique to associate blindly separated speech signals with semantic cues. The experimental results show the efficacy in accurately identifying the target speaker by utilizing semantic cues derived from limited and unaligned text, resulting in SI-SDRi of 12.16 dB, SDRi of 12.66 dB, PESQi of 0.830 and STOIi of 0.150, respectively. Dataset and source code will be publicly available. Project demo page: https://slideTSE.github.io/ .



Introduction

Speech communication is inherently the most natural and predominant mode of interaction among humans. However, the efficacy of computational models in processing speech-related tasks, including but not limited to
ASR
(
ASR
)
(
Yue et al. 2019
)
, speaker localization
(
Qian et al. 2021
)
,
ASD
(
ASD
)
(
Tao et al. 2021
)
, and
SER
(
SER
)
(
Pan et al. 2020
)
, is significantly compromised in scenarios with overlapping speakers and severe background noise. This motivates researchers to investigate different ways to extract high-quality target speaker’s speech.

The ‘
cocktail party problem
’
(
Cherry 1953
)
is defined as a common social situation in which multiple conversations occur simultaneously under ambient noise. Such an environment presents a challenge to interpersonal communication due to the potential interference from other speakers’ voices.
In this case, the human auditory system has an extraordinary ability to selectively attend to the speech of a target speaker while effectively suppressing irrelevant acoustic interference, i.e., selective auditory attention.

Illustration of our proposed pTSE-T task which extracts the presenter’s speech from the audio mixture (including interfering speaker’s speech and background noise) using unaligned text from the visual presentation slide.

The cocktail party problem can be approached by extracting individual sound sources from mixed audio signals. In this case, speech separation
(
Hershey et al. 2016
;
Liu and Wang 2019
;
Luo and Mesgarani 2019
;
Kolbæk et al. 2017
;
Zeghidour and Grangier 2021
)
serves as a solution that separates a mixed signal into individual speech streams, given prior knowledge of the total speaker number. However, separated speech signals do not have an association with speaker identities, which often suffers from the ambigous ID permutation problem. However, with an unknown speaker number, the speech separation performance degrades.

Differently, by mimicking humans’ intrinsic attentive listening ability,
TSE
does not require prior knowledge of the exact speaker number. Instead, it uses speaker-specific reference cues to accurately isolate the desired voice. For example, a pre-registered speech signal
(
Wang et al. 2019
;
Ge et al. 2020a
;
Xu et al. 2020
)
is used primarily as a cue to extract ID-consistent speech.
Moreover, co-speech gestures
(
Pan, Qian, and Li 2022
)
and lip movements
(
Pan et al. 2020
)
from synchronized video recordings, or even live location
(
Ge et al. 2022
)
can also be used as the auxiliary visual cues.
However, acquiring such strong cues either requires a pre-registration process or high-quality visual conditions, e.g., the speaker mostly moves inside the camera’s field of view with visible frontal face, continuous camera tracking, which may not always be available.

In this work, we target the meeting or presentation scenario where multiple individuals speak simultaneously and strong cues such as live recording or voice pre-registration are not available.
In these typical scenarios, speakers use visual support tools, such as PowerPoint slides or posters, to ensure that the information conveyed is easily understood with enhanced clarity and impact.
The text contents, i.e. the key points or summaries on the presentation slides, offer limited but concise semantic cues which are intrinsically related to the speaker’s spoken content.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous work has used such a text as the condition for
TSE
. Although challenging, it is useful in many scenarios. Therefore, in this paper, we propose a new task, namely
pTSET
(
pTSET
), that uses the text on the slide as a cue for speaker extraction.
Our key contributions can be summarized as follows.

1.

We are the first proposal of the
pTSET
task, as illustrated in Fig.
1
, which extracts the presenter’s clean speech from the audio mixture given the unaligned semantic text cues obtained from the presentation slide.

2.

To facilitate this new
pTSET
task, we design and annotate an innovative data corpus, which includes temporal-synchronized speech and presentation slides. In addition, we also provide the
ASR
transcripts and the text descriptions extracted from slides using an open-source
OCR
(
OCR
) toolkit.

3.

We propose two novel networks i.e.
TPE
and DPRNN-TSR. Specifically,
TPE
fuses the text prompts with audio features for
TSE
, achieving correct extraction rates of 96.46% and 95.26% on the MMSpeech-2mix and -3mix datasets, respectively. DPRNN-TSR employs contrastive learning to explore the intra-modal correlation between paired text prompts and speech data,
achieving correct matching rates of 86.28% and 76.70%, respectively.

Related work

TSE
extracts the clean speech of a target speaker from an audio mixture of multiple speakers and interference noise using speaker-related cues. Conventional cues include spatial information indicating the direction of the target speaker, speaker’s lip and gesture movements, various texts, or pre-recorded voiceprints
(
Zmolikova et al. 2023
)
. In the following section, we conduct a comprehensive review on the similarities and differences among prior significant works.

Audio Cues

Using audio as a cue is the most intuitive and potentially the most versatile way for
TSE
.
For example, in a personal use scenario, a user may explore his historical voice as a cue to extract his own voice from audio mixtures.
In long recording scenarios, e.g., meetings, individual clean audio clips can also be extracted when the pre-registered human voice is available.
Many existing works are built on audio cues, e.g., SpeakerBeam
(
Žmolíková et al. 2019
)
, VoiceFilter
(
Wang et al. 2019
)
, SpEx
(
Xu et al. 2020
)
, SpEx+
(
Ge et al. 2020b
)
, SpEx++
(
Ge et al. 2021
)
and X-Sepformer
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
.
These approaches capitalize on simplicity during the recording process while other
TSE
works rely on spatial or visual cues often require multiple microphones or auxiliary close-view cameras.
However, the performance of audio-based
TSE
may also be limited because discriminating a speaker through his voice characteristics can be erroneous due to the substantial inter- and intra-speaker variabilities
(
Zmolikova et al. 2023
)
. For example, a speaker’s voice characteristics are likely to be affected due to factors such as age, physical condition, varying noisy recording environments, and so on.

Spatial Cues

Spatial cues refer to the relative position of a speaker that is obtained through a microphone array strategically positioned at defined angles.
The advent of beamforming technology leads to the exploration of spatial cues on
TSE

(
Gannot et al. 2017
;
Flanagan et al. 1985
;
Janskỳ et al. 2020
)
,
which has achieved effective results
(
Gu et al. 2019
;
Heitkaemper et al. 2019
)
.
Typically, spatial cues yield optimal results in scenarios where the speaker remains stationary. With a moving speaker, the
TSE
performance can be compromised.
Moreover, the acquisition of these spatial cues, requiring the use of multiple microphones, presents a significant challenge due to its complexity, making it less practical for many real-world applications.

Visual Cues

Visual cues typically consist of images captured from real-time video recordings, focusing mainly on facial expressions
(
Chung et al. 2020
)
, upper body motions
(
Pan, Qian, and Li 2022
;
Gan et al. 2020
)
and lip movements
(
Wu et al. 2019
;
Pan et al. 2021
;
Pan, Ge, and Li 2022
)
.
However, visual cues are susceptible to obstructions in the video frame and varying changes in illumination.
Furthermore, their efficacy is highly dependent on the ability to capture detailed and high-quality images, which may not be feasible during periods such as the pandemic, where masks are worn or high-resolution cameras are not equipped.
Moreover, to ensure the effective capture of the speaker’s frontal facial expressions or upper body movements, the camera must be adeptly and continuously oriented towards them.
This setup is effective for stationary speakers, but requires ongoing camera recalibration and tracking with frequent movements.

Text Cues

In Text-to-Speech (TTS) tasks, text is aligned with speech at various levels.
Precise alignment ensures that speech rhythm, intonation, and pauses correspond with the text structure, enhancing comprehension. However, the condensed textual content on slides lacks explicit alignment with the speaker’s speech, posing unique challenges for using text as cue in
TSE
.

Text cues, which include any extraction-related information, such as the content of the speaker’s speech or other descriptive content specifying the sound of the target.
Text cues enhance contextual understanding, enabling separation models to more precisely extract target speech, reducing the reliance on audio cues for single-voice extraction.
Some works have explored using text to extract the corresponding sounds
(
Liu et al. 2022
;
Kilgour et al. 2022
;
Ma et al. 2024
)
. However, they only target at nature sounds, e.g., musical instruments and animal sounds while not dealing with multiple simultaneous speakers.
Consequently, the utilization of text as a medium for extracting the target speaker’s speech remains under explored.

According to our previous discussion, spatial and visual cues lack universality due to their dependency on additional supporting devices.
In particular, audio cues require pre-recording of the target speaker, which is inconvenient and can raise significant privacy concerns.
Furthermore, in multi-speaker scenarios, the effectiveness of previous works using text cues is compromised as it tends to focus on distinguishing distinctive sounds only. Therefore, our proposed task eliminates the need for any supplementary equipment or specific recording samples, relying solely on text prompt for
TSE
, thus offering greater universality and practicality.

Proposed Methods

We denote
s(\tau)
as the target speech, and
\alpha_{i}
as the scalar variable used to adjust the
SDR
(
SDR
) for interfering speech
v_{i}(\tau)
, where
i\in\{1,2,...,I\}
,
I
denotes the number of interfering speakers. And
\tau\in\{1,2,...,T\}
denotes the time index. The mixed speech
x(\tau)
is defined as,

x(\tau)=s(\tau)+\sum_{i=1}^{I}\alpha_{i}v_{i}(\tau)

(1)

TSE
aims to extract
\hat{s}(\tau)
that approximates
s(\tau)
from
x(\tau)
.

In this paper, we propose two networks: the
TPE
network and the
DPRNN
(
DPRNN
) with text-speech recognition (DPRNN-TSR) network. Specifically, the
TPE
network is applicable in any scenario, while the DPRNN-TSR network is particularly effective in scenarios with a known speaker number.

TPE Network

Figure 2:
The structure of our proposed
TPE
network. The input consists of mixed speech waveform
x(\tau)
and pre-processed text prompts
p_{t}
, which are separately processed by a speech encoder and a text encoder to obtain
X(t)
and
P_{text}
. Subsequently, within the fusion layer, we apply
\text{FiLM}(\cdot)
for hierarchically fusion of features from both modalities to obtain
F(t)
. The fused features are then processed by a mask estimator to generate an estimated mask
M(t)
, which is element-wise multiplied with
X(t)
and passed through the speech decoder to obtain the predicted speech waveform
\hat{s}(\tau)
of the target speaker.

The
TPE
network consists of five components, as shown in Fig.
2
:
1)
The speech encoder that transforms mixed speech waveforms
x(\tau)
into mixed speech embedding
X(t)
.
2)
A text encoder that encodes the text prompts of the slides into text embedding
P_{text}
.
3)
A fusion layer that fuses
X(t)
and
P_{text}
to obtain the fused features
F(t)
.
4)
A mask estimator that takes the fused features
F(t)
as input and estimates a mask, which is then multiplied element-wise by
X(t)
to generate a masked mixed speech embedding.
5)
A speech decoder that receives the embedding of the masked mixed speech to provide the extracted target speech
\hat{s}(\tau)
.

1) Speech encoder:
The speech encoder consists of a 1-D convolution followed by a
ReLU
activation function. It transforms the speech waveform
x(\tau)
into a spectral feature embedding
X(t)
in the latent space,

X(t)=ReLU(conv1D(x(\tau),1,D,L))\in{\mathbb{R}^{D\times T}}

(2)

where
conv1D(\cdot)
is with the input and output dimensions of
1
and
D
, respectively, kernel size of
L
, and stride of
L/2
. The output speech embedding
X(t)
is with dimension
D
, where
t\in\{1,2,...,T\}
, and
T=\frac{2(T_{seq}-L)+1}{L}+1
,
T_{seq}
is the sequence length.

2) Text encoder:
We utilize the pretrained Contrastive Language-Audio Pre-training (CLAP) as the text encoder
(
Wu et al. 2023
)
. CLAP is a pre-trained model using the contrastive learning strategy on multimodal data, enhancing their ability to understand the relationships between text and audio. All parameters of the CLAP model are frozen when training the proposed
TPE
network.
We denotes
p_{t}
as the text prompt of any length,

P_{text}=f_{text}(p_{t})\in{\mathbb{R}^{D}}

(3)

where
f_{text}(\cdot)
denotes the text encoder and
P_{text}
is the D-dimensional text embedding and
p_{t}
is the text prompt.

3) Fusion layer:
In the fusion layer, the speech embedding is first subjected to a layer normalization and then hierarchically fused with the text embedding. More precisely, speech embedding is element-wise linear modulated (FiLM
(
Perez et al. 2018
)
), conditioned by the text. Then, the normalized tensor is passed through a 1-D convolution. At the same time, the text embedding undergoes a down-sample operation to modulate lower-level speech embedding. Subsequently, we obtain the fused feature as,

F(t)=LN(conv1D(FiLM(X(t),P_{text}),D,B,L))\in{\mathbb{R}^{B\times T}}

(4)

where
B=D/2
and
LN
signifies layer normalization. This process repeats N times, with N being a hyperparameter.

Figure 3:

The structure of our proposed
TSR
.
The waveforms
speech_{i}
are separated from mixed speech using DPRNN, and the
text_{i}
are their corresponding text prompt. These inputs are passed through encoders and adapters to extract latent features
X_{s}
and
X_{t}
, respectively.
Next, the residual attention matrices
M_{s}
and
M_{t}
are generated via a cross-attention mechanism. During training, clean speech waveforms are used as inputs, whereas for inference, the inputs are the separation results from DPRNN.

4) Mask estimator:
The mask estimator takes fused feature
F(t)
as input to estimate a mask to filter out other interferences.
We adopted the DPRNN structure
(
Luo, Chen, and Yoshioka 2020
)
, which excels in modeling long-term temporal dependencies by using combined recurrent layers with dilated convolutions, facilitating efficient information propagation.
It includes a segmentation layer, R-repeated DPRNN blocks, and an aggregation layer.

Subsequently,
F(t)
is fed into a segmentation layer, dividing it into segments using a window size of
K
and a hop size of
K/2
. To ensure the minimum total input length,
K
is chosen approximately as
K\approx\sqrt{2T_{seq}}
. All chunks are then concatenated to form a 3-D tensor, denoted as
W\in{\mathbb{R}^{B\times K\times P_{c}}}
, where
P_{c}
is the chunk number. After segmentation,
W
is processed through the R-repeated DPRNN blocks with non-shared network weights. Each block consists of a sequence of intra- and inter-block processing with residual connections, which involves a bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BLSTM)
followed by linear layers.

The aggregation layer reverses the transformation applied to
W
from its segmented state back to the shape of
F(t)\in{\mathbb{R}^{B\times T}}
. This process is followed by a series of activation layers to obtain the estimated mask
M(t)
.

5) Speech decoder:
The speech decoder performs element-wise multiplication between the estimated mask
M(t)
and the speech encoder output
X(t)
to generate the masked speech embedding,

\hat{S}(t)=M(t)\odot X(t)\in{\mathbb{R}^{D\times T}}

(5)

\hat{S}(t)
can be transformed to the estimated target speech waveform
\hat{s}(\tau)
by performing an overlap-and-add operation.

We applied the negative
SI-SDR
(
SI-SDR
) as the loss function,

\mathcal{L}_{TPE}=-20\mathop{\log_{10}}\left(\frac{\lVert\frac{<\hat{s},s>s}{\lVert s\rVert^{2}}\rVert}{\lVert\hat{s}-\frac{<\hat{s},s>s}{\lVert s\rVert^{2}}\rVert}\right)

(6)

For brevity, we use
\hat{s}
and
s
to represent
\hat{s}(\tau)
and
s(\tau)
.

DPRNN-TSR Network

Given the excellent speech separation performance of DPRNN in the absence of any cues, we propose a two-stage DPRNN-TSR network. In the first stage,
given the prior speaker number, DPRNN produces randomly permuted speech waveforms which
lack fixed ID-correspondence.
To solve this issue, in the second stage, a
TSR
network is designed to identify the speech that best matches the target text prompt, thus selecting the target speech.

As shown in Fig.
3
,

speech_{i}
waveforms from DPRNN are fed into
TSR
to select the best matched target speech, given the text prompt
text_{i}
, where
i\in\{1,2,...,I\}

Specifically, the network is trained using a contrastive learning technique, where positive samples consist of paired text prompt and target speech, while negative samples are unpaired text prompt and interference speech.
The pretrained CLAP model
(
Ma et al. 2024
)
is used to extract latent representations of text prompt and speech separately. Then, these representations are mapped to hidden spaces via separate adapters, which consist of a series of fully connected layers to obtain text and speech embeddings.
The final layer maps these adapted representations to obtain speech and text embeddings.

Subsequently, using a cross-attention mechanism, we compute two attention matrices: one with speech embedding as Query and text embedding as Key and Value, and another with the roles reversed. The sum of these matrices, combined with speech embedding via skip connection, forms a residual attention matrix. Finally, by multiplying the residual attention matrices for text and speech, we derive logits that indicate the similarity between the embeddings. The residual attention matrices of text and speech are formulated as

M_{o}=softmax\left(\frac{Q_{t}K_{s}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)V_{s}+softmax\left(\frac{Q_{s}K_{t}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)V_{t}

(7)

M_{t}=X_{t}+M_{o}

(8)

M_{s}=X_{s}+M_{o}

(9)

where
d
denotes the dimension of
Q
,
K
and
V
.
X
denotes the output of adapters where the subscript indicate the text or speech modality.

We use the Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss function to optimize the
TSR
network,

\mathcal{L}_{TSR}=-\left(y\log(\hat{y})+(1-y)\log(1-\hat{y})\right)

(10)

where
y\in\{0,1\}
.
\hat{y}=softmax\left(M_{t}\cdot M_{s}^{\intercal}\right)\in[0,1]
represents the probability output of the
TSR
network: if the input text prompt and speech are predicted to be paired,
\hat{y}
achieves one; otherwise, it achieves zero.
(\cdot)^{\intercal}
denotes transpose.

MMSpeech Dataset

We design, collect and annotate a new dataset, namely MMSpeech, which includes synchronized audio-visual streams in the academic talking scenarios. The original videos in the MMSpeech dataset have a total duration of 12.5 hours, sampled at 32 kHz, with varying video resolutions. When using the audio from these videos, we downsampled them to 16 kHz. MMSpeech provides both video-aligned OCR data, speech data and its
ASR
transcripts, which are completely qualified to cover the
TSE
with slide text.

The MMSpeech dataset is collected from INTERSPEECH 2020 recordings
1
1

1

INTERSPEECH 2020: http://www.interspeech2020.org
. The multi-modal information is re-organized using the following procedure (Fig.
4
):

ASR:
We employ Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) to identify the beginning and end of each sentence. We utilize whisper-large-v3 pretrained model
2
2

2

https://huggingface.co/openai/whisper-large-v3
to obtain precise timestamps of each sentence, which are then used to segment the audio into speech clips.

Text pre-processing:
To extract textual content from visual slides, we leverage Paddle-OCR
3
3

3

Paddle-OCR: https://github.com/PaddlePaddle/PaddleOCR
, which has been widely adopted due to its exceptional performance, for text recognition on images. Subsequently, using the timestamps obtained from Whisper
(
Radford et al. 2023
)
for the start of each speech clip, we apply OCR to the corresponding frames of the video to obtain raw text recognition results.
We also record the width and height of detected bounding boxes. If this width or height is less than 1% of the video resolution, we discard them to remove irrelevant information.

Text post-processing:

Following the extraction of raw text recognition results, we employ Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 model
4
4

4

Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2: https://huggingface.co/mistralai/Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2
to refine and structure the raw text recognition output into a text prompt of natural language style.

We created the MMSpeech-2mix and -3mix datasets whose element consists of two and three randomly mixed speech waveforms.
Furthermore, we partitioned the dataset into training, validation, and test sets with sizes of 40,000, 5,000, and 1,000 samples, respectively.
It contains a total of 59 speakers, with 50 used for the training set, 5 for the validation set, and 4 for the test set. Thus, the MMSpeech dataset has no speaker ID overlap.

To further validate the performance of our model in real-world conference and speech scenarios, we also created the MMSpeech-2mix-noisy and MMSpeech-3mix-noisy datasets. We utilized the WHAM! dataset
(
Wichern et al. 2019
)
to incorporate noise, with an
SDR
between -3dB and 3dB.
Apart from the noise addition, there are no differences between the MMSpeech-2(3)mix and MMSpeech-2(3)mix-noisy datasets.

Experiments and Results

Experimental setup

For the DPRNN block of
TPE
, we set the in_channels
D
to 256, out_channels
B
to 64, hidden_channels to 128, kernel_size to 40, the number of BLSTM layers
R
to 6, windows size
K
to 80,
N
in the fusion layer to 2.

In the DPRNN-TSR network, the DPRNN follows the original implementation
(
Luo, Chen, and Yoshioka 2020
)
, with the exception of setting the kernel size to 40. Within the
TSR
architecture, both the speech and text adapter have input and output dimensions of 512, and a hidden size of 2048. The dimensions of Query, Key, Value matrices in the cross-attention module are all set to 512, with head set to 1.

Evaluation metrics

SI-SDRi
(
SI-SDRi
) and
SDRi
(
SDRi
) are used to measure extraction quality. We also report the Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality Improvement (PESQi) and the Short-Time Objective Intelligibility Improvement (STOIi) to measure perceptual quality and intelligibility. The improvements are calculated relative to the mixed speech.
Moreover, we define the
Accuracy
(Acc) of
TSE
as the ratio of correctly extracted speech utterances to the total number of test utterances. A higher value indicates better performance across all metrics (
\uparrow
).

Training

For the
TPE
network training, we utilize the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of
5\times 10^{-4}
. Additionally, we apply a learning rate reduction strategy where the rate is halved if the best validation loss does not decrease for two consecutive epochs. Furthermore, training is terminated if the best validation loss (BVL) does not decrease for 10 consecutive epochs. The minimum allowable learning rate is set to
1\times 10^{-8}
, after which no further reduction occurs. The MMSpeech-2mix and -3mix datasets are used.

For the DPRNN network training, we use the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of
10^{-3}
. We also apply the same BVL strategy as
TPE
. The MMSpeech-2(3)mix and MMSpeech-2(3)mix-noisy datasets are used.

For the TSR network training, we use the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of
10^{-4}
and a weight decay of
1\times 10^{-4}
. The MMSpeech-2mix and -3mix datasets are used.

Results

Target Speaker Extraction

We perform all experiments in the MMSpeech-2 (3) mix and MMSpeech-2 (3) mix-noisy datasets.

indicate that the DPRNN network achieves the best performance across all metrics, i.e.

Conclusion

In this work, we explore the use of unaligned text cues for target speaker extraction.
Our proposal stands out from previous works that have relied on various speaker cues
which often face challenges in practical applications due to their complexity and intrusive nature.
In particular, we proposed two networks, namely
TPE
and DPRNN-TSR.
Both networks excel in using text prompts for
TSE
.
Empirical results demonstrate the efficacy of our proposed methods. Specifically, the
TPE
network maintains a leading edge in all performance metrics in all scenarios.
Furthermore, by making the dataset and source code publicly available, we aim to encourage further research and development in
TSE
.
