Portal and hepatic vein(s) embolization as a preparation for major hepatectomy: Enough is enough or more is better?
Authors: Daniel Azoulay
Published: 2025

Despite being technically resectable, liver tumour resection can still be contraindicated when the future remnant liver (FRL) is too small due to the inherent risk of severe postoperative liver failure, the leading cause of early postoperative mortality. In this setting, preoperative portal vein embolization (PVE), usually of the right branch, has been developed to induce ipsilateral atrophy and contralateral compensatory hypertrophy of the FRL, preventing postoperative liver failure. The rationale for this approach relies on the experimental and clinical experience of more than a century (1-5). In the field of PVE, the advantage of segment 4 portal branches embolization to PVE has not demonstrated clinical advantage over PVE before right extended hepatectomy (6). Portal delivery of hematopoietic stem cells to the FRL, concomitant with PVE remains under investigation (7). Currently, provided optimally performed i.e., by ipsilateral approach, with N-butyl-cyanoacrylate mixed with iodized oil (8) and deployment of a central vascular plug (9) (which also minimizes the risk of non-target embolization of the FRL), the mean technical success rate (feasibility) of PVE is >99% and the clinical failure rate (failure to induce sufficient hypertrophy of the FRL to allow resection) is <4%. Lower clinical success rates are logically observed in series with a high proportion of patients with underlying liver injury and above all with cirrhosis, known to alter the regeneration process (10).

In 2009, Hwang et al. (11) reported the preliminary results from a series of 12 patients who underwent preoperative sequential PVE and hepatic vein [HPVE, also called liver venous deprivation (LVD) or biembolization] as preparation for major hepatectomy when the FRL volume remained <40% of the total liver volume after PVE. Technical success rate was 91.7% [erroneous hepatic vein embolization (HVE) occurred in one patient] and clinical success rate was 75%.

In 2016, to shorten the delay to proceed for surgery, Guiu et al. (12) proposed the so-called LVD technique that combines both PVE and HVE during a single procedure that still needs two percutaneous approaches.

In this journal, Boubaddi et al. (13) evaluated the feasibility, safety and efficacy of LVD. Between 2016 and 2023, LVD was performed in patients with liver malignancies planned for right sided major hepatectomy with an FLR ≤25% of total liver volume for a healthy liver or <30% for a diseased liver. FLR volumetry was assessed before and 4 weeks after the procedure. The retrospective study included 192 patients recruited in 7 centers during a 7-year period. The technical success rate of LVD was 100% and severe complication post-LVD occurred in 2.6% of patients. Clinical success rate was 83.8%: right sided major hepatectomy could be performed, 40 days after LVD on 161/192 patients. Hepatectomy could not be performed in 31 (16.2%) patients, mostly because of tumor progression (28/31, 90.3%). Following hepatectomy, severe morbidity (Clavien-Dindo grade ≥ IIIA) and 90-day mortality occurred in 21.1% (34/161) and 4.3% (7/161) of patients, respectively.

The results and messages of this study concur with numerous previous single center studies and three systematic reviews (14-16). LVD is a safe, reproducible, efficient technique that induces rapid major FLR growth and might increase liver resectability. Incidentally, it is worth remembering here that liver resection is too often considered as an objective, whereas it is simply a means (the best one when feasible for most liver tumors) to achieve the real objective, namely the patient long-term survival. Beside the limits of the study, acknowledged by the authors themselves, it must also be added that while such a multicenter study allows quick enrolment, the extreme imbalances in enrolment across sites (not clarified in the study) may challenge the power and sensitivity of the results (17).

More importantly, as with the entire current available knowledge, the study leaves us with many unanswered questions, including major ones addressed separately (though often overlapping) in the following sections.

First, should we switch from PVE to LVD for all patients with insufficient FRL? Probably not due to (I) the excellent current results of optimally performed PVE, and (II) the additional morbidity of HVE, allegedly low but still potentially severe. Rather than “one size fits all technique”, it seems reasonable to proceed for HVE after clinical failure of PVE (see below for the subsequent prolonged delay to resection). Nevertheless and intuitively, there might be a place for simultaneous PVE and HVE in some settings where the importance of the volume and the function of the FRL is exacerbated including perihilar cancer (18) (at a higher risk of cholangitis), planed combined hepatectomy and pancreaticoduodenectomy, and complex liver surgery under hypothermic perfusion of the liver.

Second, whenever the sequential approach is chosen, should we consider tumor progression (in the FRL or outside the liver) during the additional delay a friend or a foe? One can reasonably consider in case of tumor progression in such a short delay between PVE and HVE occurs that surgery is not the best treatment option.

Third, as compared to PVE (19), is there with biembolization (sequential or simultaneous) an increased risk of tumor growth, particularly in the FRL or outside the liver due the increased boost of tumor proliferation leading to oncologically futile surgery i.e., early tumor recurrence following surgery. This question is as fundamental as unanswered so far.

Fourth, what is the place of biembolization amongst the different options to tackle a too small FRL: two stage hepatectomy and ALPPS, radioembolization, combined TACE, pre-, per-, and postoperative conditionings including portal vein (flow/pressure) modulations to site a few?

The above raised questions remain unanswered by the recent report of the DRAGON1 study (20). This prospective, single-arm, international, multicenter trial evaluated the safety and efficacy of PVE/HVE simultaneous or within 48 hours as a preparation for major liver resection for colorectal liver metastases in patients with a small FLR. In total, 102 patients were included from 43 centers in a 2.5-year period. Of 96 patients undergoing PVE/HVE, no post-embolization mortality occurred, though major complications were reported in 2 patients (2%). Clinical success rate i.e., resection was completed in 86 patients (86/96=90%), with seven patients (7/86=8%) dying within 90 days. Liver failure occurred in one fifth of patients (19/86, 22%).

In brief, the debate over adding HVE (simultaneously or sequentially) to PVE continues. Like often for any innovation, like for the ALPPS (Associating Liver Partition and Portal vein Ligation for Staged Hepatectomy) procedure for example, the pendulum has swung too far towards biembolization, “the new kid on the block” of liver augmentation. This pendulum should stabilize with the results of the ongoing multicenter phase II, prospective and randomized study comparing simultaneous portal and HVE to PVE for hypertrophy of the FRL before major hepatectomy for colorectal liver metastases (21). Identifying indisputable indications for venous deprivation that translate into clinically meaningful patient-oriented endpoints will have a major influence on the overall acceptance and application of the technique.

Meanwhile, we are left with the question of whether enough is enough with PVE or more is better with biembolization.
