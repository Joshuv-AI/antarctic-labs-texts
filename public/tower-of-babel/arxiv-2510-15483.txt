Online Policy Learning via a Self-Normalized Maximal Inequality Thanks: This paper is currently under review.
Samuel Girard, Aurélien Bibaut, Houssam Zenati

Abstract
Adaptive experiments produce dependent data that break i.i.d. assumptions that underlie classical concentration bounds and invalidate standard learning guarantees. In this paper, we develop a self-normalized maximal inequality for martingale empirical processes. Building on this, we first propose an adaptive sample-variance penalization procedure which balances empirical loss and sample variance, valid for general dependent data. Next, this allows us to derive a new variance-regularized pessimistic off-policy learning objective, for which we establish excess-risk guarantees. Subsequently we show that, when combined with sequential updates and under standard complexity and margin conditions, the resulting estimator achieves fast convergence rates in both parametric and nonparametric regimes, improving over the usual 1 / n 1/\sqrt{n} baseline. We complement our theoretical findings with numerical simulations that illustrate the practical gains of our approach.

INTRODUCTION

Online policy learning lies at the heart of modern interactive systems such as recommendation engines
(
Li et al., 2010a
)
, clinical trials
(
Villar et al., 2015
)
, and adaptive tutoring
(
Lan et al., 2014
)
. In these settings, policies are updated sequentially as new data arrive, and the logged trajectories are inherently dependent. While this adaptivity enables efficient exploration
(
Lattimore and Szepesvari, 2019
)
, it breaks the i.i.d. assumptions underpinning classical learning theory
(
van der Vaart, 1998
;
Hall and Heyde, 1980
;
Bach, 2024
)
. As a result, standard empirical risk minimization
(
Vapnik and Chervonenkis, 1971
)
or counterfactual policy learning
(
Bottou et al., 2013
)
can severely underestimate risk, leading to unstable updates and poorly performing policies.

Alongside these challenges, advances in policy learning have been driven by a pessimistic learning principle, whereby the learner minimizes a deliberately conservative estimate of risk
(
Jin et al., 2021
;
Thomas et al., 2015
)
. In offline settings with i.i.d. logged data
(
Strehl et al., 2010
;
Dudik et al., 2011
)
, this principle has been instantiated through sample variance penalization (SVP)
(
Maurer and Pontil, 2009
)
, which augments empirical risk minimization with a penalty on the empirical variance of the losses. By down-weighting policies with unstable estimates, SVP provides variance-adaptive guarantees and has become a cornerstone of the so-called
counterfactual risk minimization
(CRM)
(
Swaminathan and Joachims, 2015b
)
, where exploding importance weights otherwise lead to biased or overly optimistic evaluation
(
Owen, 2013
)
.In this setting, two policies with similar empirical means may have vastly different variances, since policies that put weight on rarely logged actions suffer from unstable importance weights. SVP explicitly penalizes such high-variance policies, thereby favoring those that are both effective and statistically reliable.

While these pessimistic principles have yielded strong guarantees in the i.i.d. setting, extending them to adaptively collected data remains challenging. First, adaptivity induces dependencies that break the empirical-Bernstein tools
(
Boucheron et al., 2013
;
de la Peña et al., 2009
)
underlying SVP
(
Maurer and Pontil, 2009
)
and invalidate standard uniform convergence arguments. Second, existing extensions either restrict attention to importance-weighted estimators for offline learning
(
Kallus et al., 2021
;
Zhan et al., 2021
;
Jin et al., 2025
)
or rely on artificial sampling schemes, such as the doubling trick in sequential counterfactual risk minimization (SCRM)
(
Zenati et al., 2023
)
, where only the last exponentially larger batch is used. These restrictions make such approaches poorly suited for online policy learning, where policies must be refined continuously using all available data. This highlights the need for concentration tools and learning principles that remain valid under adaptive, sequential data collection.

In this paper, we introduce a variance-regularized pessimistic principle for learning from adaptively collected data. Our contributions are threefold: (i) we establish a new self-normalized maximal inequality for martingale empirical processes and use it to extend sample variance penalization beyond the i.i.d. regime; (ii) we apply this framework to policy learning with adaptively collected bandit feedback, deriving variance-adaptive excess-risk guarantees; and (iii) we develop an online algorithm that reuses all past data with regret guarantees that interpolate smoothly between parametric and nonparametric regimes, yielding fast rates. Empirically, our methods consistently improve stability and performance over existing approaches, especially in settings with limited exploration or high adaptivity. Our paper provides the first framework that unifies Bernstein-style variance penalization with adaptive, sequential policy updates.

1.1
Martingale Framework and Notation

We study a sequential setup with dependent data. Let
(Z_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
be random variables taking values in
\mathcal{Z}
, adapted to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t=0}^{T}
with
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{t})
.
For a class
\mathcal{F}
and
f\in\mathcal{F}
, let
\ell_{t}(f):\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
be a predictable (i.e.
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable) loss function. For brevity we write
\ell_{t}(f):=\ell_{t}(f)(Z_{t})
. The (conditional) risk of
f
is

R_{T}(f)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\ell_{t}(f)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right],

(1)

and the empirical risk is
\hat{R}_{T}(f)=\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(f)
.
We define the optimal hypothesis
f^{*}\in\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}}R_{T}(f)
.

The martingale increments are
\Delta_{t}(f)=\ell_{t}(f)-\mathbb{E}[\ell_{t}(f)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]
,
and their average

M_{T}(f)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Delta_{t}(f)=\hat{R}_{T}(f)-R_{T}(f),

(2)

is the deviation of the empirical risk from its conditional expectation.
Since our learning algorithm selects a data-dependent hypothesis from
\mathcal{F}
, we must ensure that
\hat{R}_{T}(f)
is a reliable proxy for
R_{T}(f)
uniformly for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
. This requires bounding the
maximal
deviation over the entire function class,
\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}M_{T}(f)
. Inequalities that control such suprema are known as maximal inequalities, and we will leverage variance-adaptive versions to achieve our results.

The empirical and conditional variances are

\displaystyle\widehat{V}_{T}(\ell(f))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(f)^{2}-\hat{R}_{T}(f)^{2},

(3)

\displaystyle V_{T}(\ell(f))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\ell_{t}(f)^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]-R_{T}(f)^{2}.

(4)

We denote
\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)=\sqrt{\widehat{V}_{T}(\ell(f))}
and
\sigma_{T}(f)=\sqrt{V_{T}(\ell(f))}
.

RELATED WORK

Pessimistic learning via sample variance penalization (SVP) augments ERM with a variance-based regularizer and can yield fast excess-risk rates for i.i.d. bounded losses; see
(
Maurer and Pontil, 2009
)
and applications in
(
Shivaswamy and Jebara, 2010
)
. Variance-dependent analyses for broader function classes (covering “slow/fast” regimes) further develop this viewpoint in the i.i.d. setting
(
Xu and Zeevi, 2020
)
.

In parallel, self-normalized concentration controls deviations relative to data-dependent variance proxies: classical results include Freedman’s martingale inequality
(
Freedman, 1975a
)
and the theory of self-normalized processes
(
de la Peña et al., 2009
)
. Our maximal inequality follows this tradition: for each
f
, the deviation
M_{T}(f)
is scaled by the conditional variance
\sigma_{T}(f)
or its empirical version
\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)
, yielding a martingale analogue of empirical Bernstein bounds (cf.
(
Waudby-Smith and Ramdas, 2022
;
Waudby-Smith et al., 2024
)
), and—unlike approaches tailored specifically to importance-weighted estimators
(
Bibaut et al., 2021
;
Jin et al., 2025
)
—applies to general bounded martingale empirical processes.

In offline policy learning from logged bandit data, CRM
(
Swaminathan and Joachims, 2015b
)
introduces SVP into policy learning but is highly sensitive to heavy-tailed importance weights, often mitigated by clipping at the cost of bias. Extensions to adaptive collection include localized bounds for importance-weighted ERM under exploration decay
(
Kallus et al., 2021
)
and policy learning from adaptive logs
(
Zhan et al., 2021
)
, both largely tied to importance sampling (IS) and doubly robust estimators. Sequential CRM (SCRM)
(
Zenati et al., 2023
)
explores pessimism under adaptivity but relies on a doubling trick that learns only from the last, exponentially larger batch, limiting practicality for continuous updates. A complementary PAC-Bayesian line, culminating in logarithmic smoothing for adaptive off-policy learning
(
Haddouche and Sakhi, 2025
)
, derives time-uniform pessimistic guarantees using supermartingale tools and KL-based complexity control. While sharing the high-level pessimism principle, this framework is structurally different: PAC-Bayes bounds are expressed through KL regularization and importance-weighted estimators, whereas we develop a self-normalized inequality for general bounded martingale processes and a variance-regularized SVP objective driven by conditional variance and sequential entropy. Closest in spirit,
Jin et al., 2025
propose a pessimistic rule with oracle-style adaptivity to overlap via a self-normalized inequality specialized to inverse-propensity estimators. By contrast, our approach yields a variance-regularized objective that is not tied to an IS martingale structure and is directly compatible with true online updates without artificial batching schemes.

A large body of work in
online contextual bandits
studies policy learning under structural assumptions on the reward function. In the
linear case
, algorithms such as LinUCB
(
Li et al., 2010b
)
, Thompson sampling
(
Agrawal and Goyal, 2014
)
, and balanced linear variants
(
Dimakopoulou et al., 2018
)
achieve regret bounds of order
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
, with balancing techniques reducing bias and improving robustness. Beyond parametric models,
nonparametric contextual bandits
attain regret rates driven by smoothness and margin conditions. Early work
(
Yang and Zhu, 2002
;
Rigollet and Zeevi, 2010
)
established minimax rates under Hölder and margin assumptions; subsequent contributions studied metric and Lipschitz settings
(
Slivkins, 2009
;
Auer and Ortner, 2007
;
Bubeck et al., 2011
)
, and
(
Guan and Jiang, 2018
)
developed optimal algorithms under Hölder smoothness in finite dimensions. These approaches typically achieve
\tilde{O}(T^{(d+1)/(d+2)})
regret when the context dimension is
d
. Our framework differs fundamentally: rather than assuming linearity, smoothness, or RKHS embeddings
(
Valko et al., 2013
;
Zenati et al., 2022
)
, it relies only on boundedness, with complexity captured by the
bracketing-entropy exponent
p
. This yields regret guarantees interpolating between the parametric rate
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
when
p=0
and nonparametric rates
\tilde{O}(T^{1-2/(2+p)})
for
p>0
, generalizing smoothness-based rates by replacing the context dimension with entropy. Finally, unlike online
reinforcement learning
methods for long-horizon stateful problems
(
Jaksch et al., 2010
;
Azar et al., 2017
;
Jiang et al., 2017
)
, our setting is that of
contextual bandits
, where the main challenge is to design concentration and learning principles robust to adaptivity.

3
SELF-NORMALIZED MAXIMAL INEQUALITY AND VARIANCE PENALIZED LEARNING

Building on the martingale framework from subsection
1.1
, this section develops our main theoretical results. We proceed in two steps. First, in subsection
3.1
, we establish a novel self-normalized maximal inequality for martingale empirical processes. This is our main technical tool, providing a high-probability bound on risk estimation error that adapts to the empirical conditional variance and the sequential complexity of the function class. Second, in subsection
3.2
, we use this inequality to construct and analyze a practical learning algorithm, which we call Adaptive Sample Variance Penalization (ASVP). By augmenting the empirical risk with a penalty term derived from our bound, this method yields an estimator with strong excess risk guarantees, effectively translating our theoretical inequality into a concrete learning principle.

3.1
Self-Normalized Maximal Inequality

For the remaining, we impose a standard bounded–envelope condition to control martingale increments, enabling self-normalized (Freedman/Bernstein) bounds and sequential chaining via bracketing entropy
(
Freedman, 1975b
;
de la Peña et al., 2009
)
.

We denote by
\mathcal{L}_{T}(\mathcal{F}):=\bigl\{(\ell_{1}(f),\dots,\ell_{T}(f)):f\in\mathcal{F}\bigr\}
the collection of predictable loss sequences generated by functions in
\mathcal{F}
.

Assumption 3.1

(Bounded envelope)
.

\max_{t\in[T]}\ \sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\ |\ell_{t}(f)|\leq B\quad\text{a.s.}

For maximal inequalities over processes indexed by a function class, we will use the sequential bracketing entropy as a notion of sequential complexity.

For any predictable sequence
\zeta_{1:T}
of functions
\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
define the pseudonorm

\rho_{T}(\zeta_{1:T}):=\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{t}(Z_{t})^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\right)^{1/2}.

A collection of pairs
\{(\lambda^{k}_{1:T},\nu^{k}_{1:T}):k\in[N]\}
is an
(\varepsilon,\rho_{T})
-sequential bracketing of
\mathcal{L}_{T}(\mathcal{F})
if:

1.

for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
, there exists
k
such that
\lambda_{t}^{k}\leq\ell_{t}(f)\leq\nu_{t}^{k}
for all
t
, and

2.

\rho_{T}(\nu^{k}_{1:T}-\lambda^{k}_{1:T})\leq\varepsilon
.

We denote the minimal such cardinality by
\mathcal{N}_{[]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal{L}_{T}(\mathcal{F}),\rho_{T})
. We will make the following assumption on the entropy measure.

Assumption 3.2

(Entropy on
\mathcal{L}_{T}(\mathcal{F})
)
.

There exists
p>0
such that, for any
\varepsilon>0
and
T\geq 1
,

\log N_{[]}\!\bigl(\varepsilon,\mathcal{L}_{T}(\mathcal{F}),\rho_{T}\bigr)\;\lesssim\;\varepsilon^{-p}.

The exponent
p
quantifies sequential complexity:
p=0
corresponds to parametric (log-entropy) classes,
0<p<2
yields sharper variance-adaptive rates, while larger
p
reflects heavier complexity and slower nonparametric rates
(
van der Vaart and Wellner, 1996
)
. We now present the self-normalized martingale empirical process martingale inequality at the cornerstone of our theoretical analysis.

Theorem 3.3

(Self-normalized maximal inequality)
.

Under Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
, For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with prob.
\geq 1-\delta
,
for all
\ell(f)\in\mathcal{L}_{T}(\mathcal{F})
, either

\sigma_{T}(f)\lesssim T^{-1/(2+p)}
, or

\displaystyle M_{T}(\ell(f))

\displaystyle\lesssim\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\,\sqrt{\log\!\Big(\tfrac{\log(T^{1/(2+p)}B^{2})}{\delta}\Big)}\,\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{\,1-p/2}

(5)

\displaystyle+\;\frac{1}{T^{2/(2+p)}}\log\!\Big(\tfrac{\log(T^{1/(2+p)}B^{2})}{\delta}\Big)+\;\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{-p}}{T}.

The details of the proof are in Appendix
B
.

Importantly, our inequality is a sequential, variance-adaptive analogue of the i.i.d. empirical Bernstein bound (Theorem 6 of
Maurer and Pontil, 2009
):
(i) the leading term has the same
variance-normalized
shape, now with the
empirical conditional
standard deviation
\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)
under adaptively collected data;
(ii) the complexity enters through the sequential bracketing exponent
p
, yielding the localized rate
T^{-2/(2+p)}
(up to logs).

When
p=0
(parametric/log-entropy classes), the bound reduces to the classical empirical-Bernstein shape
\tilde{O}(\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)/\sqrt{T})
(up to iterated logs), directly mirroring their Theorem 6 but in the
martingale
setting with data dependence.

3.2
Adaptive Sample Variance Penalization

SVP trades off empirical risk and data-driven variability by penalizing the (sample) variance of the loss.
In i.i.d. problems this yields bounds that adapt to the variance of the optimal predictor and can achieve fast rates when that variance is small. Our contribution transports this principle to sequential, adaptively collected data by replacing the i.i.d. sample variance with the
empirical conditional
variance and by controlling complexity via sequential entropy. We define our
adaptive sample variance penalization
(ASVP) learning objective for
\lambda>0
as:

\hat{f}_{T}^{\lambda}=\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\{\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(f)\;+\;\lambda\left(\tfrac{\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{\,1-p/2}}{\sqrt{T}}+\tfrac{\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{-p}}{T}\right)\right\}.

(ASVP)

Remark 3.4
.

The rule depends on the complexity index
p
. However, in applications with parametric policies, we can set
p=0
, yielding a penalty
of order
\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)/\sqrt{T}
, faithful to the original SVP.

Using the previous Theorem
3.3
, we are able to show the following important result.

Theorem 3.5

(ASVP Excess Risk)
.

Under Assumptions
3.1
–
3.2
, fix
\delta\in(0,1)
and set

\lambda=C_{1}\sqrt{\log\!\big(\log(T^{1/(2+p)}B^{2})/\delta\big)}
.
For any optimal hypothesis
f^{\star}\in\mathcal{F}
, the ASVP estimator
\hat{f}_{T}^{\lambda}

satisfies, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

R_{T}(\hat{f}_{T}^{\lambda})-R_{T}(f^{\star})\;=\;\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\sigma_{T}(f^{\star})^{1-\tfrac{p}{2}}}{\sqrt{T}}\;+\;T^{-\tfrac{2}{2+p}}\right),

where the
\widetilde{O}(\cdot)
hides factors polylogarithmic in
T
,
1/\delta
, and
B
.

We provide a detailed proof in Appendix
B
.

Notably, minimizing the variance-penalized objective (
ASVP
) with
\lambda
chosen as in Theorem
3.5
yields a comparator-dependent excess risk bound that matches the spirit of Theorem 15 in
(
Maurer and Pontil, 2009
)
: for
p=0
we obtain

\tilde{O}\!\big(\sigma_{T}(f^{\star})/\sqrt{T}\big)+\tilde{O}(1/T)
, the same
SVP rate shape
but now valid for
adaptive
(non-i.i.d.) data and with conditional variances.
If
\sigma_{T}(f^{\star})=0
, our result similarly exhibits the fast
\,\tilde{O}(1/T)
behavior (up to logs), paralleling their zero-variance case.

4
POLICY LEARNING FROM ADAPTIVELY COLLECTED DATA

In this section, we will apply our general previous results shown to policy learning with adaptively collected data.

4.1
Policy Evaluation and Learning Setting

We consider sequential data
\overline{Z}_{T}=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{T})
, where each observation
Z_{t}=(X_{t},A_{t},Y_{t},\varpi_{t}(A_{t}|X_{t}))
. Here
X_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
is a context drawn i.i.d. from a distribution
P_{X}
,
A_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
is an action sampled
according to a logging policy
\varpi_{t}(\cdot\mid X_{t})
, and
Y_{t}
is the observed outcome
drawn from a conditional distribution
P_{Y}(\cdot\mid X_{t},A_{t})
.
We assume
P_{X}
and
P_{Y}
are stationary, while the logging policies
\varpi_{t}
may

adapt
to the past:
\varpi_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
–measurable with respect to

\mathcal{F}_{t-1}=\sigma(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{t-1})
. Note that when
\mathcal{A}
is discrete,
\varpi_{t}(\cdot\mid X_{t})
defines a probability distribution, whereas for continuous
\mathcal{A}
it denotes a probability density.

A policy
\pi
maps each context
x
to a distribution

\pi(\cdot\mid x)
over actions. Let
\Pi
be the class of candidate policies. For notational purposes, for
\pi\in\Pi
, we will abbreviate the joint distributional
P_{\pi}=P_{Y\mid X,A}\times\pi\times P_{X}
. For a policy
\pi\in\Pi
, we define the
reference risk
as

R^{\star}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{P_{\pi}}\big[y\big],

(6)

where the loss
y
depends both on the context and the action.
The goal in our setting is to find the best-in-class policy

\pi^{\star}\in\arg\min_{\pi\in\Pi}R^{\star}(\pi).

(7)

Because data are generated by the adaptive sequence
(\varpi_{t})
rather than
\pi
, we rely
on importance weighting. The canonical unbiased estimator of
R^{\star}(\pi)
uses inverse propensity scoring (IPS)
(
Horvitz and Thompson, 1952
)
and writes:

\widehat{R}_{T}(\pi)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\pi(A_{t}\mid X_{t})}{\varpi_{t}(A_{t}\mid X_{t})}\,Y_{t},

(8)

Importantly, this setting refines the global framework introduced in Section
1.1
, when we write:
\ell_{t}(\pi)(Z_{t})=\tfrac{\pi(A_{t}\mid X_{t})}{\varpi_{t}(A_{t}\mid X_{t})}\,Y_{t}
,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\ell_{t}(\pi)(Z_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{P_{\varpi_{t}}}\!\left[\tfrac{\pi(A_{t}\mid X_{t})}{\varpi_{t}(A_{t}\mid X_{t})}\,Y_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{P_{\pi}}[Y]=R^{*}(\pi)
.

4.2
Variance-Penalized Objective for Off-Policy Learning

Our goal now is to design an off-policy learning procedure. Following
Zenati et al., 2023
, we use an IPS estimator with
implicit exploration

(
Neu, 2015
)
. For a candidate policy
\pi\in\Pi
and a sequence of observations
\bar{Z}_{T}
, the implicit exploration (IX) estimator is:

\widehat{R}_{T}^{\text{IX}}(\ell_{\varpi}(\pi))=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(\pi)(Z_{t}),

where

\ell_{t}(\pi)(Z_{t})=\frac{\pi(A_{t}\mid X_{t})}{\varpi_{t}(A_{t}\mid X_{t})+\alpha\pi(A_{t}\mid X_{t})}Y_{t}.

and the variance penalty as
\widehat{V}_{T}^{\text{IX}}(\ell(\pi))
with the formula from Equation (
3
). Here
\alpha>0
behaves like a clipping parameter that lower-bounds the effective propensity and yields a stabilized ratio (akin to implicit exploration). When
\alpha\to 0
it reduces to standard IPS.

Building upon (
ASVP
), we define a novel
adaptive sample variance penalization policy learning
method. In particular, our learning objective is, for
\lambda>0
:

\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{T}(\pi,\lambda,\varpi)=\widehat{R}^{\text{IX}}_{T}(\ell_{\varpi}(\pi))+\lambda\sqrt{\frac{\widehat{V}^{\text{IX}}_{T}(\ell_{\varpi}(\pi))}{T}}

(ASVP-PL)

4.3
Excess Risk Analysis

We now derive the excess risk of our
off-policy
estimator for
any logged dataset collected adaptively
. We use the assumption below that has been adopted in the offline policy learning literature
(
Swaminathan and Joachims, 2015a
;
Kallus and Zhou, 2018
)
:

Assumption 4.1

(Bounded importance weights)
.

For any policies
\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi
and any
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
,
we assume
\frac{\pi(a\mid x)}{\pi^{\prime}(a\mid x)}\leq W,\quad\text{for some }W>0
.

We now state the excess risk associated to (
ASVP-PL
).

Proposition 4.2

(ASVP-PL Excess Risk Bound)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{t},a_{t},y_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
be a dataset collected by a sequence of policies
(\varpi_{t})_{t\leq T}
.
Then, under Assumptions
4.1
and
3.2
, a minimizer
\hat{\pi}_{T}^{\lambda}\in\arg\min_{\pi\in\Pi}\{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{T}(\pi,\lambda,\varpi_{1:T})\}

satisfies, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

R^{*}(\hat{\pi}_{T})-R^{*}(\pi^{*})\;=\;\tilde{O}\left(\frac{W^{\tfrac{p+1}{2}}}{T^{\,1-\tfrac{p}{4}}}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nu_{t}^{2}\right)^{\tfrac{2-p}{4}}\;+\;\frac{W^{2}}{T^{\tfrac{2}{2+p}}}\right),

where

\nu_{t}^{2}\;=\;\operatorname{Var}_{x,\varpi_{t}}\!\left(\frac{\pi^{*}(a\mid x)}{\varpi_{t}(a\mid x)}\right),

and the
\tilde{O}(\cdot)
hides factors polylogarithmic in
T
,
1/\delta
, and
W
.

We provide a detailed proof in Appendix
C
.

5
ONLINE SVP POLICY LEARNING

While the previous section provided a learning objective and an excess risk bound for any offline logged propensities
\{\varpi_{t}\}_{t=1\dots T}
, we will focus here on developing an online algorithm, i.e. a sequence of optimized policies
\{\pi_{t}\}_{t=1\dots T}
that minimizes the cumulative excess risk, or expected regret.

The expected regret over
T
rounds is the sum of the expected excess risks of the optimized policies
\{\pi_{t}\}_{t=1\dots T}
:

\text{Regret}_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}(R^{\star}(\pi_{t})-R^{\star}(\pi^{\star}))

(9)

Minimizing this regret corresponds to learning the optimal policy
\pi^{\star}
as quickly as possible. To do so, we propose a sequential design where we deploy at each round the policy learned from previously learned policies using the objective of (
ASVP-PL
):

\pi_{t+1}\in\arg\min_{\pi\in\Pi}\{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{t}(\pi,\lambda_{t},\pi_{1:t})\}

(OSVP-PL)

where
\pi_{1:t}=\{\pi_{s}\}_{s=1\dots t}
is the predictable sequence of deployed policies until time
t
. We decide to call our learning procedure
online sample variance penalization policy learning
(OSVP-PL).

Though our setting consider online updates, its definition and analysis trivially extends to batch online updates. To this effect, we compare our online policy learning procedure to SCRM
(
Zenati et al., 2023
)
. Our estimator instead does not need to do a highly unrealistic doubling trick for theoretical guarantees, and is able to use all past data instead of the previous round only. This advance allows to improve learning with more stable learning updates, as illustrated qualitatively in Example
5.1
below.

\pi_{\theta}=\mathcal{N}(\theta,\sigma^{2})

Now, prior to establishing our regret analysis, we introduce a policy Hölderian error bound condition as stated in
(

Condition 5.2

(Policy Hölderian Error Bound,
(
Zenati et al., 2023
)
)
.

There exist constants
\gamma>0
and
\beta>0
such that for any policy
\pi\in\Pi
, there exists an optimal policy
\pi^{\star}\in\arg\min_{\pi\in\Pi}R^{\star}(\pi)
satisfying:

\gamma\,\,\text{Var}_{\pi,x}\left(\frac{\pi^{\star}(a\mid x)}{\pi(a\mid x)}\right)\leq(R^{\star}(\pi)-R^{\star}(\pi^{\star}))^{\beta}.

Intuitively, this condition connects to the Hölderian error bound
(
Bolte et al., 2007
)
which relates the distance to the optimum with the objective gap. It generalizes strong convexity
\beta=1
and boundedness

, and for
\beta\in[0,1]

(
Łojasiewicz, 1963
;
Łojasiewicz, 1993

, which has been used to obtain fast rates in online learning with restart strategies
(
Gaillard and Wintenberger, 2018
)
.

While this condition has been proved in
(
Zenati et al., 2023

to hold for certain parametric exponential families, we will state an assumption under which this condition will hold, as proven in Appendix
C

Assumption 5.3

(Margin condition)
.

Let
|\mathcal{A}|<\infty
and
\mu(x,a)=\E[Y\mid X=x,A=a]
.
Define
\mu^{*}(x)=\min_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(x,a)
and a minimizer
a^{*}(x)
.
For some
\nu\in[0,\infty]
, assume that for all

,

\Pr\!\left(\min_{a\neq a^{*}(X)}\mu(X,a)-\mu^{*}(X)\leq Mu\right)^{1/\nu}\lesssim u,

This condition bounds the probability that a suboptimal action lies too close to the optimal one.
Originally introduced in binary classification
(
Mammen and Tsybakov, 1999
)
, it has since been extended to multi-armed contextual bandits
(
Hu et al., 2021
;
Hu et al., 2020
)
,
where it plays a key role in achieving fast convergence rates.
Intuitively, it controls the density of the arm gap near zero: for smooth
\mu
and continuous covariates
X
, it typically holds with
\nu=1
, while for discrete
X
it often degenerates to
\nu=\infty
.

Discussion.

The rate reveals a clear margin–complexity tradeoff: increasing
\beta
(stronger separation) accelerates learning until it reaches the class–complexity ceiling determined by
p
. For
\beta=0
one recovers
R_{T}=\widetilde{O}(T^{1/2})
, the classical
\sqrt{T}
behavior where overlap/variance dominates and the rate is independent of
p
. At
\beta=\tfrac{1}{2}
the bound becomes
R_{T}=\widetilde{O}\!\big(T^{\frac{2+p}{6+p}}\big)

\sqrt{T}
for any
p<2
and improves as

(e.g.,
p=1

\beta=1
the margin no longer tightens the bound and the rate saturates at

p=0

p=1

p=2
returns to

. In summary,
\beta
continuously improves the exponent on
[0,1)

\frac{p}{2+p}
; thus, in nonparametric regimes, further acceleration requires reducing
p
via stronger smoothness or structural assumptions.

7
DISCUSSION

Our paper introduced three complimentary algorithmic contributions: (i) the Adaptive Sample-Variance Penalization (ASVP) principle, extending classical SVP to dependent data via new self-normalized martingale inequalities; (ii) ASVP-PL, which leverages this principle for policy learning with provable guarantees; and (iii) OSVP-PL, an online learning algorithm that reuses all past data to further strengthen robustness under limited exploration. Our analysis combined maximal inequalities with variance-sensitive generalization bounds, yielding tools of independent interest for sequential learning. Empirically, the proposed methods consistently improve stability and performance over existing baselines, particularly when exploration is scarce or adaptivity exacerbates variance. While our guarantees rely on boundedness and margin assumptions, the framework opens the way to broader extensions—such as reinforcement learning with long horizons, and adaptive recommendation—where variance-aware methods and systematic data reuse are especially promising.

From this we can derive Lemma

in subsection

This lemma is essential in subsection

In subsection

B.1
Preliminary Results

We first set again notations for our setting, for empirical and conditional variances, and state a maximal inequality with sequential bracketing; see
van Handel, 2010
for sequential bracketing.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}=\sigma(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{t-1})
. For a function class
\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{L}_{T}:=\{\ell_{1:T}(f):f\in\mathcal{F}\}
denote predictable processes with

\ell_{t}(f)
being
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
–measurable. And define the martingale deviation

M_{T}(\ell(f)):=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Big(\ell_{t}(f)(Z_{t})-\E[\ell_{t}(f)(Z_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\Big).

We assume the envelope condition on the predictable loss process:

\max_{t\in[T]}\ \sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\ \sup_{Z\in\mathcal{Z}}|\ell_{t}(f)(Z)|\leq B\quad\text{a.s.}

(10)

For notations purpose we will write
\ell_{t}(f)(Z_{t})=\ell_{t}(f)
in the rest of the proof.

Here we present the maximal inequality from

( Theorem A.4) used to derive Theorem

.

Theorem B.1

(Theorem bound; cf.
van Handel, 2010
)
.

Suppose (
10
) holds and let
r>0
. Define the localized class

\mathcal{L}_{T}(r)\ :=\ \big\{\ell_{1:T}(f)\in\mathcal{L}_{T}:\ \rho_{T}\big(\ell_{1:T}(f)\big)\leq r\big\}.

Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{T}(r)}\ M_{T}(\ell)\ \lesssim\ \psi_{T}\big(r,\delta;\mathcal{L}_{T},B\big).

With
\psi_{T}(r,\delta;\mathcal{L}_{T},B)
defined as

\psi_{T}(r,\delta;\mathcal{L}_{T},B):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}J(r,\mathcal{L}_{T},\rho_{T})+\frac{B}{T}\log N_{[]}(r,\mathcal{L}_{T},\rho_{T})+r\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{T}}+\frac{B\log(1/\delta)}{T}.

(11)

B.2
Peeling: from fixed-radius control to True Conditional Variance

In this Appendix we aim at turning the fixed-radius bound of
Theorem

B.1
into a
variance-adaptive
inequality that depends on the (unknown) true conditional variance of each
f
by using a peeling technique (Lemma
B.3
).

Remark B.2
.

Centering and moment-of-order-2 equivalence.
Let
\bar{\ell}_{t}(f):=\ell_{t}(f)-\E[\ell_{t}(f)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]
. Note first that the martingale deviation is unchanged by centering:

M_{T}(\ell(f))=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(\ell_{t}(f)-\E[\ell_{t}(f)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\bigr)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bar{\ell}_{t}(f)=M_{T}(\bar{\ell}(f)).

Shifts by predictable processes do not affect bracketing, so the centered class
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{T}
has the same sequential complexity (same
N_{[]}
and
J
) as
\mathcal{L}_{T}
. Moreover, the localization radius for the centered process is exactly the square root of the true conditional variance:

\rho_{T}(\bar{\ell}(f))^{2}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\E\!\big[\bar{\ell}_{t}(f)^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big]=V_{T}(\ell(f)).

Hence we may work with second moments without loss: centering leaves both the deviation and the complexity unchanged while identifying the radius with
\sqrt{V_{T}(\ell(f))}
. In what follows we write
\sigma_{T}(f):=\rho_{T}(\ell_{1:T}(f))
, therefore after centering
\sigma_{T}(f)=\sqrt{V_{T}(\ell(f))}
.

Lemma B.3

(Localized convergence by peeling)
.

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
and
r\in[0,B]
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{\ell_{1:T}\in\mathcal{L}_{T}(r)}M_{T}(\ell_{1:T}(f))\leq\psi_{T}(r;\delta,\mathcal{L}_{T}).

Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for any
\ell(f)\in\mathcal{L}_{T}
, either

\sigma_{T}(f)\leq T^{-\frac{1}{2+p}}
, or

M_{T}(\ell(f))\leq\psi_{T}\!\left(2\sigma_{T}(f);\,\delta\left(\log\!\big(T^{\frac{1}{2+p}}B^{2}\big)\right)^{-1},\,\mathcal{L}_{T}\right).

Proof.

We apply a peeling argument. Let
r_{k}=2^{k}r_{0}
for

and
r_{0}=T^{-\frac{1}{2+p}}

\delta_{k}=\delta/(\log_{2}(2B^{2}/r_{0}))

simultaneously with probability at least

. For any

\sigma_{T}(f)>r_{0}
, there is a

r_{k}<\sigma_{T}(f)\leq r_{k+1}
. Then

M_{T}(\ell(f))\leq\psi_{T}(r_{k+1};\delta_{k},\mathcal{L}_{T})\leq\psi_{T}(2\sigma_{T}(f);\delta\left(\log(T^{{\frac{1}{2+p}}}B^{2})\right)^{-1},\mathcal{L}_{T}).\qed

B.3
From True to Empirical Variance

We now transition from the variance–localized maximal inequality of
Lemma

B.3
, which depends on the
true
conditional variance, to a version formulated in terms of the
empirical
variance. To this end, we invoke on the one hand a concavity result from Lemma 2.1 of
van der Vaart and Wellner, 2010
, and on the other hand apply lemma
B.3
to the squared process. Then, combining these two ingredients yields corollary
B.6
.

We start by presenting a key lemma from
van der Vaart and Wellner, 2010
which is an observation made on concave functions.
We state here the lemma independently before invoking any probabilistic bounds.

Lemma B.4

(Lemma 2.1 of
van der Vaart and Wellner, 2010
)
.

Let
J:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a concave, non-decreasing function with
J(0)=0
.
If
z^{2}\leq A^{2}+B^{2}J(z^{r})
for some
r\in(0,2)
and
A,B>0
, then

J(z)\;\lesssim\;J(A)\,\Bigl[\,1+J\!\bigl(A^{r}\bigr)\Bigl(\frac{B}{A}\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr]^{\!1/(2-r)}.

Next we control the difference between the true and empirical second moments uniformly over the class by applying the variance-localized inequality to the squared process
\ell^{2}(f)
.

Lemma B.5
.

For any
r\geq 0\text{ and }\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for any
f\in\mathcal{F}
under the event
\{T^{\frac{-1}{2+p}}\leq\sigma_{T}(f)\leq B\}
,

\sigma_{T}(f)^{2}-\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}J(\sigma_{T}(f),\mathcal{L}_{T},\rho_{T}).

Proof.

We apply the peeling argument of Lemma
B.3
to the class of functions
\ell^{2}(f)
which is still bounded by
B^{2}
and has the same bracketing entropy as
\ell(f)
. We have:

M_{T}(\ell^{2}(f))=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Big(\ell_{t}(f)^{2}-\mathbb{E}[\ell_{t}(f)^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\Big)=\widehat{V}_{T}(\ell(f))-V_{T}(\ell(f)).

Applying Lemma
B.3
to
\ell^{2}(f)
, we get that for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for any
f\in\mathcal{F}
, with
\sigma_{T}(f)\geq T^{-\frac{1}{2+p}}
,

|\widehat{V}_{T}(\ell(f))-V_{T}(\ell(f))|\lesssim\psi_{T}(2\sigma_{T}(f);\delta(\log(T^{\frac{1}{2+p}}B^{2}))^{-1},\mathcal{L}_{T}).

The result follows from the definition of
\psi_{T}
and the fact that
\sigma_{T}(f)\leq B
.
∎

Transfer to the empirical radius.

We now combine the variance comparison with the concavity device to express the entropy term at the true radius
\sigma_{T}(f)
in terms of the empirical radius
\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)
.

Corollary B.6

(Local entropy transfer from the second moment)
.

Work on the high–probability event of Lemma
B.5
.
Then for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
with
\sigma_{T}(f)>T^{-\frac{1}{2+p}}
,

J\!\bigl(\sigma_{T}(f),\mathcal{L}_{T},\rho_{T}\bigr)\;\lesssim\;J\!\bigl(\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f),\mathcal{L}_{T},\rho_{T}\bigr)\;+\;\frac{J^{2}\!\bigl(\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f),\mathcal{L}_{T},\rho_{T}\bigr)}{\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{2}\,\sqrt{T}}.

Proof.

On the event of Lemma
B.5
we have, for each
f
,

\sigma_{T}(f)^{2}\leq\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{2}+T^{-1/2}\,J\!\bigl(\sigma_{T}(f),\mathcal{L}_{T},\rho_{T}\bigr).

Apply Lemma
B.4
(Lemma 2.1 of
van der Vaart and Wellner, 2010
) with

z=\sigma_{T}(f)
,
A=\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)
,
B^{2}=T^{-1/2}
and
r=1\in(0,2)
,
using that
J
is nondecreasing, concave and
J(0)=0
. This yields

J(z)\;\lesssim\;J\!\bigl(\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)\bigr)+\frac{J^{2}\!\bigl(\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)\bigr)}{\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{2}\sqrt{T}},

which is the stated bound. The small-variance case

\sigma_{T}(f)\leq T^{-\frac{1}{2+p}}
is covered by Lemma
B.3
.
∎

B.4
Self-Normalized Maximal Inequality and Excess Risk

In this subsection, we derive the two of our main theoretical results of our paper, starting with a novel self-normalized inequality:

Theorem
3.3

(Self-normalized maximal inequality)
.

Under Assumption
3.2

\exists C_{1},C_{2}>0
s.t
for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for any
\ell(f)\in\mathcal{L}_{T}
, either

\sigma_{T}(f)\leq T^{-\frac{1}{2+p}}
, or

M_{T}(\ell(f))\lesssim\frac{C_{1}\sqrt{\log\{\log\!\big(T^{\frac{1}{2+p}}B^{2}\big)\frac{2}{\delta}\}}}{\sqrt{T}}\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{1-\frac{p}{2}}+\frac{BC_{2}\log\{\log\!\big(T^{\frac{1}{2+p}}B^{2}\big)\frac{2}{\delta}\}}{T^{\frac{2}{2+p}}}+\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{-p}}{T}

Proof.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
and set

\delta^{\prime}\;:=\;\frac{\delta}{\log\!\big(T^{\frac{1}{2+p}}B^{2}\big)}\,\quad\text{and }L_{T}=\log\{\log\!\big(T^{\frac{1}{2+p}}B^{2}\big)\frac{2}{\delta}\}

By Lemma
B.3
, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
, for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
either

\sigma_{T}(f)\leq T^{-\frac{1}{2+p}}
or

M_{T}(\ell(f))\;\leq\;\psi_{T}\!\Big(2\sigma_{T}(f);\tfrac{\delta^{\prime}}{2},\mathcal{L}_{T}\Big).

(12)

Step 1: Replace
J(\sigma_{T}(f))
by
J(\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f))
.

On the event of Lemma
B.5
with confidence
\delta/2
(union–bound compatible with the peeling above), corollary
B.6
gives us for any
f
, with
\sigma_{T}(f)>T^{-\frac{1}{2+p}}
,

J\!\bigl(\sigma_{T}(f),\mathcal{L}_{T},\rho_{T}\bigr)\;\lesssim\;J\!\bigl(\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f),\mathcal{L}_{T},\rho_{T}\bigr)\;+\;\frac{J^{2}\!\bigl(\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f),\mathcal{L}_{T},\rho_{T}\bigr)}{\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{2}\sqrt{T}}.

(13)

Hence we have that:

\displaystyle\psi_{T}\!\Big(2\sigma_{T}(f);\tfrac{\delta^{\prime}}{2},\mathcal{L}_{T}\Big)\;\lesssim\;

\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\!\left(J\!\bigl(\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f),\mathcal{L}_{T},\rho_{T}\bigr)+\frac{J^{2}\!\bigl(\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f),\mathcal{L}_{T},\rho_{T}\bigr)}{\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{2}\sqrt{T}}\right)}_{\text{(I)}}\;+\;\underbrace{\frac{B\,L_{T}}{T^{\frac{2}{2+p}}}}_{\text{(II)}}

\displaystyle\;+\;\underbrace{\left(\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)+\frac{J\!\bigl(\sigma_{T}(f),\mathcal{L}_{T},\rho_{T}\bigr)}{\sigma_{T}(f)\sqrt{T}}\right)\!\sqrt{\frac{L_{T}}{T}}}_{\text{(III)}}.

(14)

Step 2: Bound the three blocks.

Block (I).
Using
J(r)\lesssim r^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}
with Assumption
3.2
,

\text{(I)}\;\lesssim\;\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}}{\sqrt{T}}\;+\;\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{-p}}{T}.

Block (II).
This is already at the target rate:
\text{(II)}=\tilde{O}\!\big(T^{-\frac{2}{2+p}}\big)
.

Block (III).
Split and bound each term:

\text{(III)}=\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)\sqrt{\frac{L_{T}}{T}}+\frac{J(\sigma_{T}(f))}{\sigma_{T}(f)}\frac{\sqrt{L_{T}}}{T}.

For the second piece, using
J(r)\lesssim r^{1-\frac{p}{2}}
and the branch
\sigma_{T}(f)>T^{-\frac{1}{2+p}}
,

\frac{J(\sigma_{T}(f))}{\sigma_{T}(f)}\frac{\sqrt{L_{T}}}{T}\;\lesssim\;\sigma_{T}(f)^{-\frac{p}{2}}\,\frac{\sqrt{L_{T}}}{T}\;\leq\;T^{\frac{p}{2(2+p)}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{L_{T}}}{T}\;=\;\tilde{O}\!\Big(T^{-\frac{2}{2+p}}\Big).

For the first piece, simply use the envelope bound
\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)\leq B
:

\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)\sqrt{\frac{L_{T}}{T}}=\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}\,\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{\frac{p}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{L_{T}}{T}}\;\leq\;B^{\frac{p}{2}}\,\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{L_{T}}{T}}.

Thus

\text{(III)}\;\lesssim\;\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}}{\sqrt{T}}\sqrt{L_{T}}\;+\;\tilde{O}\!\Big(T^{-\frac{2}{2+p}}\Big).

Step 3: Collect terms and rename constants.

Combining (I)–(III) inside (
14
) and the peeling branch (
12
), and union–bounding the two high–probability events, we obtain that with probability at least
1-\delta
, for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
either
\sigma_{T}(f)\leq T^{-\frac{1}{2+p}}
or

M_{T}(\ell(f))\;\lesssim\;\frac{\sqrt{L_{T}}}{\sqrt{T}}\;\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}\;+\;\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{-p}}{T}\;+\;\frac{B\,L_{T}}{T^{\frac{2}{2+p}}}.

Absorbing absolute constants into
C_{1},C_{2}
and recalling
L_{T}=\log\{\log(T^{\frac{1}{2+p}}B^{2})\,2/\delta\}
gives the stated inequality.
∎

Deriving the excess risk:

From this maximal inequality, we now derive the learning principle (
ASVP
).
We present the proof for its associated excess-risk.

Theorem
3.5

(ASVP Excess Risk)
.

Under Assumptions
3.1
–
3.2
, fix
\delta\in(0,1)
and set

\lambda=C_{1}\sqrt{\log\!\big(\log(T^{1/(2+p)}B^{2})/\delta\big)}
.
For any optimal hypothesis
f^{\star}\in\mathcal{F}
, the ASVP estimator
\hat{f}_{T}^{\lambda}

satisfies, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

R_{T}(\hat{f}_{T}^{\lambda})-R_{T}(f^{\star})\;\lesssim\;\frac{\sigma_{T}(f^{\star})^{1-\tfrac{p}{2}}}{\sqrt{T}}\;+\;T^{-\tfrac{2}{2+p}},

where the hidden factors are at most polylogarithmic in
T
,
1/\delta
, and
B
.

Proof.

Define the empirical and predictable risks

\hat{R}_{T}(f):=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(f),\qquad R_{T}(f):=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\!\big[\ell_{t}(f)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big],

so that
M_{T}(\ell(f))=\hat{R}_{T}(f)-R_{T}(f)
. Fix

\lambda\;=\;C_{1}\sqrt{\log\!\Big(\frac{\log(T^{\frac{1}{2+p}}B^{2})}{\delta}\Big)},\qquad L_{T}:=\log\!\Big\{\log\!\big(T^{\frac{1}{2+p}}B^{2}\big)\,\frac{2}{\delta}\Big\}.

Let the ASVP estimator be any minimizer

\hat{f}_{T}^{\lambda}\in\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\bigg\{\hat{R}_{T}(f)+\lambda\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\,\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}+\frac{1}{T}\,\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{-p}\right)\bigg\}.

Step 1: Uniform deviation event

By Theorem
3.3
(applied to
\ell
and to
-\ell
) together with a union bound and
Lemma
B.5
, there exists an event
\mathcal{E}
of probability at least
1-\delta
such that,

simultaneously for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
,
either
\sigma_{T}(f)\leq T^{-\frac{1}{2+p}}
or

R_{T}(f)\;\leq\;\hat{R}_{T}(f)+\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{T}}\,\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}+\frac{\lambda}{T}\,\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{-p}+\frac{BC_{2}\,L_{T}}{T^{\frac{2}{2+p}}},

(15)

and, symmetrically,

\hat{R}_{T}(f)\;\leq\;R_{T}(f)+\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{T}}\,\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}+\frac{\lambda}{T}\,\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f)^{-p}+\frac{BC_{2}\,L_{T}}{T^{\frac{2}{2+p}}}.

(16)

Step 2 : oracle inequality.

On
\mathcal{E}
, apply (
15
) with
f=\hat{f}_{T}^{\lambda}
:

R_{T}(\hat{f}_{T}^{\lambda})\;\leq\;\hat{R}_{T}(\hat{f}_{T}^{\lambda})+\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{T}}\hat{\sigma}_{T}(\hat{f}_{T}^{\lambda})^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}+\frac{\lambda}{T}\hat{\sigma}_{T}(\hat{f}_{T}^{\lambda})^{-p}+\frac{BC_{2}\,L_{T}}{T^{\frac{2}{2+p}}}.

By optimality of
\hat{f}_{T}^{\lambda}
,

\hat{R}_{T}(\hat{f}_{T}^{\lambda})+\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{T}}\hat{\sigma}_{T}(\hat{f}_{T}^{\lambda})^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}+\frac{\lambda}{T}\hat{\sigma}_{T}(\hat{f}_{T}^{\lambda})^{-p}\;\leq\;\hat{R}_{T}(f^{\star})+\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{T}}\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f^{\star})^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}+\frac{\lambda}{T}\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f^{\star})^{-p}.

Apply (
16
) with
f=f^{\star}
to bound
\hat{R}_{T}(f^{\star})
from above. Combining the three displays yields

R_{T}(\hat{f}_{T}^{\lambda})-R_{T}(f^{\star})\;\leq\;2\,\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{T}}\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f^{\star})^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}\;+\;\frac{2\lambda}{T}\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f^{\star})^{-p}\;+\;\frac{2BC_{2}\,L_{T}}{T^{\frac{2}{2+p}}}.

(17)

Step 3 (Replace
\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f^{\star})
by
\sigma_{T}(f^{\star})
in (
17
)).

Write
\sigma:=\sigma_{T}(f^{\star})
and
\hat{\sigma}:=\hat{\sigma}_{T}(f^{\star})
for brevity.

(a) Control the gap
|\sigma-\hat{\sigma}|
.

Lemma
B.5
and the entropy condition
J(r)\lesssim r^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}
give

\big|\sigma^{2}-\hat{\sigma}^{2}\big|\;\lesssim\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\,J(\sigma)\;\lesssim\;\frac{\sigma^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}}{\sqrt{T}}.

Hence

|\sigma-\hat{\sigma}|=\frac{\big|\sigma^{2}-\hat{\sigma}^{2}\big|}{\sigma+\hat{\sigma}}\;\leq\;\frac{\big|\sigma^{2}-\hat{\sigma}^{2}\big|}{\sigma}\;\lesssim\;\frac{\sigma^{-\frac{p}{2}}}{\sqrt{T}}.

On the branch
\sigma>T^{-\frac{1}{2+p}}
this implies the uniform bound

|\sigma-\hat{\sigma}|\;\lesssim\;T^{-\frac{1}{2+p}}.

(18)

(b) Push (
18
) through the map
x\mapsto x^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}
.

With
\alpha:=1-\frac{p}{2}\in(0,1]
, concavity yields
(x+y)^{\alpha}\leq x^{\alpha}+y^{\alpha}
for
x,y\geq 0
. Using
\hat{\sigma}=\sigma+(\hat{\sigma}-\sigma)
and (
18
),

\hat{\sigma}^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}=(\sigma+|\hat{\sigma}-\sigma|)^{\alpha}\;\leq\;\sigma^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}+|\hat{\sigma}-\sigma|^{\alpha}\;\lesssim\;\sigma^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}+\big(T^{-\tfrac{1}{2+p}}\big)^{\alpha}\;\leq\;\sigma^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}+T^{-\tfrac{1}{2+p}}.

Therefore

\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{T}}\;\hat{\sigma}^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}\;\lesssim\;\frac{\sigma^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}}{\sqrt{T}}\;+\;\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{T}}\,T^{-\frac{1}{2+p}}\;=\;\frac{\sigma^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}}{\sqrt{T}}\;+\;\tilde{O}\!\Big(T^{-\frac{2}{2+p}}\Big).

(19)

(c) Control
\hat{\sigma}^{-p}
by a two-case guard.

From (
18
),
\hat{\sigma}\geq\sigma-|\sigma-\hat{\sigma}|
with
|\sigma-\hat{\sigma}|\lesssim T^{-\tfrac{1}{2+p}}
.

•

If
|\sigma-\hat{\sigma}|\leq\sigma/2
, then
\hat{\sigma}\geq\sigma/2
and
\hat{\sigma}^{-p}\leq 2^{p}\,\sigma^{-p}
.

•

Otherwise
|\sigma-\hat{\sigma}|>\sigma/2
, which forces
\sigma\lesssim T^{-\tfrac{1}{2+p}}
. In that threshold regime, the contribution
\frac{1}{T}\hat{\sigma}^{-p}
is absorbed by
T^{-\tfrac{2}{2+p}}
(see Step 4 below).

In both sub-cases we may write

\frac{1}{T}\,\hat{\sigma}^{-p}\;\lesssim\;\frac{1}{T}\,\sigma^{-p}\;+\;T^{-\frac{2}{2+p}}.

(20)

Step 4 (Variance disjunction of
f^{\star}
).

If
\sigma\leq T^{-\frac{1}{2+p}}
, then

\frac{\sigma^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}}{\sqrt{T}}=\frac{\big(T^{-\tfrac{1}{2+p}}\big)^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}}{\sqrt{T}}=T^{-\frac{2}{2+p}},\qquad\frac{1}{T}\,\sigma^{-p}\leq\frac{1}{T}\,T^{\frac{p}{2+p}}=T^{-\frac{2}{2+p}}.

If instead
\sigma>T^{-\frac{1}{2+p}}
, then (
19
) and (
20
) give

\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{T}}\hat{\sigma}^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}+\frac{1}{T}\hat{\sigma}^{-p}\;\lesssim\;\frac{\sigma^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}}{\sqrt{T}}\;+\;T^{-\frac{2}{2+p}}.

Step 5 (Conclusion).

Insert the preceding bounds into (
17
) and use that the remaining

\frac{BC_{2}\,L_{T}}{T^{\frac{2}{2+p}}}
terms are of order
T^{-\frac{2}{2+p}}
(up to polylogs). Hence, on
\mathcal{E}
,

R_{T}(\hat{f}_{T}^{\lambda})-R_{T}(f^{\star})\;\lesssim\;\frac{\sigma_{T}(f^{\star})^{\,1-\frac{p}{2}}}{\sqrt{T}}\;+\;T^{-\frac{2}{2+p}},

with hidden constants absolute and hidden factors at most polylogarithmic in
T
,
1/\delta
, and
B
.
∎

C.1
Analysis of ASVP-PL Excess Risk Bound

Here we provide the proof for the excess risk bound:

Proposition
4.2

(ASVP-PL Excess Risk Bound)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{t},a_{t},y_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
be generated by a sequence of logging policies
(\varpi_{t})_{t\leq T}
with
a_{t}\sim\varpi_{t}(\cdot\mid x_{t})
.
Under Assumptions
4.1
and
3.2
, any minimizer

\hat{\pi}_{T}^{\lambda}\in\arg\min_{\pi\in\Pi}\ \hat{\mathcal{L}}_{T}(\pi,\lambda,\varpi_{1:T})

satisfies, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

R^{*}(\hat{\pi}^{\lambda}_{T})-R^{*}(\pi^{*})\;\lesssim\;\frac{W^{\tfrac{p+1}{2}}}{T^{\,1-\tfrac{p}{4}}}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nu_{t}^{2}\right)^{\tfrac{2-p}{4}}\;+\;\frac{W^{2}}{T^{\tfrac{2}{2+p}}},

where
\nu_{t}^{2}=\Var_{x,\varpi_{t}}\!\big(\frac{\pi^{*}(a\mid x)}{\varpi_{t}(a\mid x)}\big)
and the hidden factors are polylogarithmic in
T
,
1/\delta
, and
W
.

Proof.

We apply Theorem
3.5
to the predictable loss process of the form:

\mathcal{L}_{T}=\Bigl\{\ell_{1:T}(\pi)=\Bigl(\frac{1}{W}+\frac{1}{W}\,y\Bigl(\frac{\pi(a\mid x)}{\varpi_{t}(a\mid x)+\alpha\pi(a\mid x)}-1\Bigr)\Bigr)_{t=1}^{T}:\pi\in\Pi\Bigr\}.

Then with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\mathbb{E}[\ell_{t}(\hat{\pi}_{\lambda})|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]-\mathbb{E}[\ell_{t}(\pi^{*})|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\right)\;\lesssim\;\frac{\sigma_{T}(\pi^{*})^{1-\frac{p}{2}}}{\sqrt{T}}\;+\;T^{-\frac{2}{2+p}},

(21)

where
\sigma_{T}(\pi^{*})^{2}=V_{T}(\ell(\pi^{*}))
.
We denote this difference as

\Delta_{T}^{*}:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\mathbb{E}[\ell_{t}(\hat{\pi}_{\lambda})|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]-\mathbb{E}[\ell_{t}(\pi^{*})|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\right)\quad\text{and}\quad U_{T}^{*}:=\frac{\sigma_{T}(\pi^{*})^{1-\frac{p}{2}}}{\sqrt{T}}+T^{-\frac{2}{2+p}},

so that
\Delta_{T}^{*}\leq U_{T}^{*}
.

Step 1 (Relate
\Delta_{T}^{*}
to the policy risk).

From the definition of
\ell_{t}(f)
,

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}[\ell_{t}(\pi)|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]=\frac{1}{W}+\frac{1}{W}\,L_{T}^{\alpha}(\pi),\qquad L_{T}^{\alpha}(\pi):=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\varpi_{t}}\!\left[y\Bigl(\frac{\pi(a\mid x)}{\varpi_{t}(a\mid x)+\alpha\pi(a\mid x)}-1\Bigr)\right].

Hence

\Delta_{T}^{*}=\frac{1}{W}\,\bigl(L_{T}^{\alpha}(\hat{\pi}_{\lambda})-L_{T}^{\alpha}(\pi^{*})\bigr).

Step 2 (Lower bound
\Delta_{T}^{*}
).

L_{T}^{\alpha}(\pi^{*})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\varpi_{t}}\!\left[y\frac{\pi^{*}(a\mid x)}{\varpi_{t}(a\mid x)+\alpha\pi^{*}(a\mid x)}\right]-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R^{*}(\varpi_{t}),

We now lower-bound
\Delta_{T}^{*}
by upper-bounding
L_{T}^{\alpha}(\pi^{*})
.

To simplify notation, throughout this proof we omit the dependence on
(a,x)

and write
\varpi_{t}
for
\varpi_{t}(a\mid x)
and
f
for
f(a\mid x)
.
Applying the inequality
(1+x)^{-1}\geq 1-x
for
x\geq 0
gives

\displaystyle\E_{\varpi_{t},y}\!\left[y\,\frac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}+\alpha\pi^{*}}\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{\varpi_{t},y}\!\left[y\,\frac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}}\,\frac{1}{1+\alpha\pi^{*}/\varpi_{t}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\E_{\varpi_{t},y}\!\left[y\,\frac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}}\,(1-\alpha\pi^{*}/\varpi_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{\varpi_{t},y}\!\left[y\,\frac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}}\right]-\alpha\,\E_{\varpi_{t},y}\!\left[y\,\Big(\frac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}}\Big)^{2}\right].

(22)

By the bounded-weight assumption
\frac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}}\leq W

and
y\in[-1,0]
, the last term in (
22
) satisfies

-\alpha\,\E_{\varpi_{t},y}\!\left[y\,\Big(\frac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}}\Big)^{2}\right]\;\leq\;\alpha\,W^{2},

so that

\E_{\varpi_{t},y}\!\left[y\,\frac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}+\alpha\pi^{*}}\right]\;\leq\;\E_{\varpi_{t},y}\!\left[y\,\frac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}}\right]+\alpha W^{2}\;=\;R^{*}(\pi^{*})+\alpha W^{2}.

(23)

Substituting this bound into the definition of
L_{T}^{\alpha}(\pi^{*})
gives

L_{T}^{\alpha}(\pi^{*})\;\leq\;R^{*}(\pi^{*})+\alpha W^{2}-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R^{*}(\varpi_{t}).

(24)

which gives

L_{T}^{\alpha}(\hat{\pi}_{\lambda})-L_{T}^{\alpha}(\pi^{*})\;\geq\;L(\hat{\pi}_{\lambda})-L(\pi^{*})-\alpha W^{2}.

We also have that

L_{T}^{\alpha}(\hat{\pi}_{\lambda})\geq R^{*}_{T}(\hat{\pi}_{\lambda})-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R^{*}(\varpi_{t}).

Therefore,

\Delta_{T}^{*}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{W}\,\bigl(R^{*}(\hat{\pi}_{\lambda})-R^{*}(\pi^{*})-\alpha W^{2}\bigr).

(25)

Step 3 (Combine inequalities).

Combining (
21
) and (
25
) yields

\frac{1}{W}\,\bigl(R^{*}(\hat{\pi}_{\lambda})-R^{*}(\pi^{*})-\alpha W^{2}\bigr)\;\lesssim\;\frac{\sigma_{T}(\pi^{*})^{1-\frac{p}{2}}}{\sqrt{T}}\;+\;T^{-\frac{2}{2+p}}.

Multiplying by
W
gives

R^{*}(\hat{\pi}_{\lambda})-R^{*}(\pi^{*})\;\lesssim\;W\,\frac{\sigma_{T}(\pi^{*})^{1-\frac{p}{2}}}{\sqrt{T}}\;+\;\frac{W}{T^{\frac{2}{2+p}}}\;+\;\alpha W^{2}.

(26)

Step 4 (Bounding the variance term).

By definition and for each
t\in[T]
,

V_{T}(\ell(\pi^{*}))=\frac{1}{W^{2}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\varpi_{t}}\!\Big[\big(y\big(\tfrac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}+\alpha\pi^{*}}-1\big)\big)^{2}\Big]-\Big(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\varpi_{t}}\!\big[y\big(\tfrac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}+\alpha\pi^{*}}-1\big)\big]\Big)^{\!2}\Bigg].

Dropping the negative term and using
|y|\leq 1
gives

V_{T}(\ell(\pi^{*}))\leq\frac{1}{W^{2}T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\varpi_{t}}\!\Big[\Big(\tfrac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}+\alpha\pi^{*}}-1\Big)^{2}\Big].

Applying
(x+y)^{2}\leq 2x^{2}+2y^{2}
and adding–subtracting the mean, we obtain

V_{T}(\ell(\pi^{*}))\;\leq\;\frac{2}{W^{2}T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Var_{\varpi_{t}}\!\Big(\tfrac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}+\alpha\pi^{*}}\Big)+\frac{2}{W^{2}T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Big(\E_{\varpi_{t}}\!\Big[\tfrac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}+\alpha\pi^{*}}\Big]-1\Big)^{2}.

(27)

The first (variance) term satisfies

\Var_{\varpi_{t}}\!\Big(\tfrac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}+\alpha\pi^{*}}\Big)\leq\Var_{x,\varpi_{t}}\!\Big(\tfrac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}}\Big)=\nu_{t}^{2}.

For the second term, using the factorization

\frac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}+\alpha\pi^{*}}=\frac{\pi^{*}/\varpi_{t}}{1+\alpha\,\pi^{*}/\varpi_{t}}

and the inequality
(1+x)^{-1}\geq 1-x
for
x\geq 0
, we have

-\alpha\,\E_{x,\varpi_{t}}\!\Big[\!\Big(\tfrac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}}\Big)^{2}\!\Big]\leq\E_{x,\varpi_{t}}\!\Big[\tfrac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}+\alpha\pi^{*}}\Big]-1\leq 0,

which implies

\Big(\E_{x,\varpi_{t}}\!\Big[\tfrac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}+\alpha\pi^{*}}\Big]-1\Big)^{2}\leq\alpha^{2}\,\E_{x,\varpi_{t}}\!\Big[\!\Big(\tfrac{\pi^{*}}{\varpi_{t}}\Big)^{2}\!\Big]\leq\alpha^{2}W^{2}.

Averaging over
t=1,\dots,T
in (
27
) yields

V_{T}(\ell(\pi^{*}))\;\leq\;\frac{2}{W^{2}}\Bigg(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nu_{t}^{2}+\alpha^{2}W^{2}\Bigg).

Step 5 (Final substitution).

Plugging the above result into (
26
) gives

W\,\frac{\sigma_{T}(\pi^{*})^{1-\frac{p}{2}}}{\sqrt{T}}\;\lesssim\;\frac{W^{\frac{p+1}{2}}}{T^{\,1-\frac{p}{4}}}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nu_{t}^{2}\right)^{\frac{2-p}{4}}.

Setting
\alpha=1/T
and absorbing polylogarithmic terms, we obtain

R^{*}(\hat{\pi}_{\lambda})-R^{*}(\pi^{*})\;\lesssim\;\frac{W^{\tfrac{p+1}{2}}}{T^{\,1-\tfrac{p}{4}}}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nu_{t}^{2}\right)^{\tfrac{2-p}{4}}\;+\;\frac{W^{2}}{T^{\tfrac{2}{2+p}}}.

∎

C.2
Conditions for acceleration

In this subsection, we present the conditions necessary to obtain a fast rate, and prove the implication between the margin condition
5.3
and the Hölderian error bound condition
5.2
under a mild policy overlap assumption
C.1
(Theorem :
C.2
)

Here we propose an assumption that suffices to prove Theorem
C.2
while being lighter than assumption
4.1
.

Assumption C.1

(Policy overlap)
.

There exists
\beta>0
such that
\pi(a^{*}(x)\mid x)\geq\beta
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

Theorem C.2

(Variance–excess risk Hölder bound)
.

Under Assumptions
5.3
and
C.1
there exist
M^{\prime}>0
and

\omega=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\nu}{\nu+1},&\nu\in(0,\infty),\\[4.0pt]
1,&\nu=\infty,\\
0,&\nu=0,\end{cases}

such that

\Var_{\pi}\!\left(\frac{\pi^{*}}{\pi}\right)\ \leq\ M^{\prime}\,\big(R^{*}(\pi)-R^{*}(\pi^{*})\big)^{\omega}.

For
\nu\in(0,\infty)
, one may take
M^{\prime}=\frac{K}{\beta}
with
K
depending only on the implicit constant in Assumption
5.3
and on
M,\nu
.

Proof.

1) From variance to probability

Since
\pi^{*}(a\mid x)=\mathbbm{1}\{a=a^{*}(x)\}
,

\E_{\pi}\!\left[\frac{\pi^{*}}{\pi}\right]=\int\frac{\pi^{*}(a\mid x)}{\pi(a\mid x)}\,\pi(a\mid x)\,da\,dp_{X}(x)=\int\pi^{*}(a\mid x)\,da\,dp_{X}(x)=1.

Hence

\Var_{\pi}\!\left(\frac{\pi^{*}}{\pi}\right)=\E_{\pi}\!\left[\Big(\tfrac{\pi^{*}}{\pi}\Big)^{2}\right]-1=\E\!\left[\frac{1}{\pi(a^{*}(X)\mid X)}\right]-1.

For any
p\in(0,1]
,
p^{-1}=1+(1-p)/p
. Using Assumption
C.1
,

\frac{1}{\pi(a^{*}(x)\mid x)}=1+\frac{1-\pi(a^{*}(x)\mid x)}{\pi(a^{*}(x)\mid x)}\leq 1+\frac{1-\pi(a^{*}(x)\mid x)}{\beta}.

Taking expectations yields

\Var_{\pi}\!\left(\frac{\pi^{*}}{\pi}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\,\E[1-\pi(a^{*}(X)\mid X)]=\frac{1}{\beta}\,\Pr(A\neq A^{*}).

(28)

2) From error probability to excess risk (using Assumption
5.3
).

Let
\Delta_{\min}(x):=\min_{a\neq a^{*}(x)}\{\mu(x,a)-\mu^{*}(x)\}\geq 0
. Then

R^{*}(\pi)-R^{*}(\pi^{*})=\E\big[\mu(X,A)-\mu(X,A^{*})\big]\geq\E\big[\Delta_{\min}(X)\,\mathbbm{1}\{A\neq A^{*}\}\big].

Fix
u\geq 0
. Split on
\{\Delta_{\min}(X)>Mu\}
:

\E\big[\Delta_{\min}(X)\,\mathbbm{1}\{A\neq A^{*}\}\big]\geq\E\big[\Delta_{\min}(X)\,\mathbbm{1}\{A\neq A^{*},\,\Delta_{\min}(X)>Mu\}\big]\geq Mu\ \Pr\big(A\neq A^{*},\,\Delta_{\min}(X)>Mu\big).

Therefore,

R^{*}(\pi)-R^{*}(\pi^{*})\ \geq\ Mu\Big(\Pr(A\neq A^{*})-\Pr(\Delta_{\min}(X)\leq Mu)\Big).

(29)

Assumption
5.3
states that there exists a constant
c>0
(implicit in
\lesssim
) such that

\Pr(\Delta_{\min}(X)\leq Mu)\ \leq\ (cu)^{\nu}\qquad\text{for all }u\geq 0,

(with the conventions specified in the assumption for
\nu=0
or
\nu=\infty
). Plugging this into (
29
) gives, for all
u\geq 0
,

R^{*}(\pi)-R^{*}(\pi^{*})\ \geq\ Mu\Big(\Pr(A\neq A^{*})-(cu)^{\nu}\Big).

For
\nu\in(0,\infty)
, maximize the RHS over
u>0
. Define
p:=\Pr(A\neq A^{*})
and

\phi(u):=Mu\,p-Mc^{\nu}u^{\nu+1}.

Then
\phi^{\prime}(u)=Mp-Mc^{\nu}(\nu+1)u^{\nu}=0
yields

u^{*}=\left(\frac{p}{(\nu+1)c^{\nu}}\right)^{\!1/\nu}.

Substituting
u^{*}
back,

R^{*}(\pi)-R^{*}(\pi^{*})\ \geq\ \phi(u^{*})=Mu^{*}\,\frac{\nu}{\nu+1}\,p=\frac{\nu M}{(\nu+1)^{(\nu+1)/\nu}c}\,p^{1+1/\nu}.

Equivalently,

\Pr(A\neq A^{*})\ \leq\ K\,\big(R^{*}(\pi)-R^{*}(\pi^{*})\big)^{\nu/(\nu+1)},\qquad K:=\left(\frac{(\nu+1)^{(\nu+1)/\nu}c}{\nu M}\right)^{\!\nu/(\nu+1)}.

(30)

For
\nu=\infty
, Assumption
5.3
asserts a uniform gap (no small margins), so

\Pr(\Delta_{\min}(X)\leq Mu_{0})=0
for some
u_{0}>0
, and (
29
) with
u=u_{0}
yields a linear bound (exponent
\omega=1
). For
\nu=0
, the condition is vacuous and (
30
) becomes trivial (exponent
\omega=0
).

3) Conclusion.

Combine (
28
) and (
30
):

\Var_{\pi}\!\left(\frac{\pi^{*}}{\pi}\right)\ \leq\ \frac{1}{\beta}\,\Pr(A\neq A^{*})\ \leq\ \frac{K}{\beta}\,\big(R^{*}(\pi)-R^{*}(\pi^{*})\big)^{\nu/(\nu+1)}.

Thus (
C.2
) holds with
M^{\prime}=\frac{K}{\beta}
and
\omega=\nu/(\nu+1)
for
\nu\in(0,\infty)
, and with the indicated limits for
\nu\in\{0,\infty\}
.
∎

Appendix D
REGRET ANALYSIS

In this section, we will provide the proof for our regret bound (Theorem
5.4
), we will surprisingly use results from the differential equation literature. We start by recalling the Bihari-Lasalle inequality (
LaSalle, 1949
;
Bihari, 1956
), which is a non-linear generalization of Grönwall’s lemma.
From this inequality, several work has been done to adapt it to a discrete setting
Cheung and Ren, 2006
;
Yang, 1983
which we will use accordingly.

Theorem D.1

(Bihari–LaSalle inequality)
.

Let
t\mapsto u(t)\geq 0
be continuous on
[t_{0},T]
. Let
a,b:[t_{0},T]\to[0,\infty)

be integrable functions and let
\varphi:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
be continuous and nondecreasing.
Assume that for all
t\in[t_{0},T]
,

u(t)\;\leq\;u(t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}a(s)\,ds\;+\;\int_{t_{0}}^{t}b(s)\,\varphi\big(u(s)\big)\,ds.

Define

\Phi(x):=\displaystyle\int_{x_{*}}^{x}\frac{ds}{\varphi(s)}

for some
x_{*}>0
.
Then

\Phi\big(u(t)\big)\;\leq\;\Phi\big(u(t_{0})\big)+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}a(s)\,ds+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}b(s)\,ds,

and, since
\Phi
is increasing, this implies

u(t)\leq\Phi^{-1}\!\Big(\Phi(u(t_{0}))+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}a(s)+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}b(s)\Big).

We will now prove the regret of OSVP-PL.

Theorem
5.4

(OSVP-PL Regret Bound)
.

Under Assumptions
4.1
and
3.2
, for
0<\beta\leq 1
associated to assumed Condition
5.2
, the cumulative regret of (
OSVP-PL
) satisfies w.p.
1-\delta

\text{Regret}_{T}\;=\;\widetilde{O}\!\left(T^{\,\max\!\left\{\frac{2-\beta(2-p)}{\,4-\beta(2-p)}\;,\;\frac{p}{2+p}\right\}}\right).

Proof.

Let
r_{t}:=R^{*}(\pi_{t})-R^{*}(\pi^{*})\geq 0
be the instantaneous regret and

R_{t}:=\sum_{s=1}^{t}r_{s}
the cumulative regret.
In OSVP-PL, the policy
\pi_{t+1}
is obtained by solving the ASVP problem on the first
t
samples.
Applying the proposition
4.2
with
T=t
and
\hat{\pi}_{T}=\pi_{t+1}
yields, w.p.
\geq 1-\delta
,

r_{t+1}\;\lesssim\;t^{-(1-\frac{p}{4})}\Big(\sum_{s=1}^{t}\nu_{s}^{2}\Big)^{\frac{2-p}{4}}\;+\;t^{-\frac{2}{2+p}}\qquad(t\geq 1).

(31)

Use Jensen concave to retrieve explicitly the regret:

By the Hölderian error bound (Condition
5.2
),
there exists
M>0
such that
\nu_{t}^{2}\leq M\,r_{t}^{\,\beta}
.
Since
x\mapsto x^{\beta}
is concave on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
for
\beta\in(0,1]
, Jensen’s inequality gives

\sum_{s=1}^{t}\nu_{s}^{2}\;\leq\;M\sum_{s=1}^{t}r_{s}^{\,\beta}\;\leq\;M\,t^{1-\beta}\Big(\sum_{s=1}^{t}r_{s}\Big)^{\beta}\;=\;M\,t^{1-\beta}\,R_{t}^{\,\beta}.

Plugging this into (
31
) we obtain

r_{t+1}\;\lesssim\;t^{-\alpha}\,R_{t}^{\,\kappa}\;+\;t^{-\frac{2}{2+p}},\qquad\alpha:=1-\frac{p}{4}-\frac{(1-\beta)(2-p)}{4},\quad\kappa:=\frac{\beta(2-p)}{4}.

(32)

Discrete Bihari inequality

Consider
u(t):=R_{t}^{\,1-\kappa}
(so
R_{t}=u(t)^{1/(1-\kappa)}
).
Since
x\mapsto x^{1-\kappa}
is concave,
u(t+1)-u(t)\leq(1-\kappa)\,R_{t}^{-\kappa}\,(R_{t+1}-R_{t})=(1-\kappa)\,R_{t}^{-\kappa}r_{t+1}
.
Using (
32
),

u(t+1)\;\leq\;u(t)\;+\;C_{1}\,t^{-\alpha}\;+\;C_{2}\,t^{-\frac{2}{2+p}}\,u(t)^{-\frac{\kappa}{1-\kappa}},

which is exactly the one–dimensional discrete Bihari form in
Cheung and Ren, 2006
(see Theorem 2.2)

u(t+1)\leq u(t)+a_{t}+b_{t}\,\varphi(u(t))
with

a_{t}=C_{1}t^{-\alpha}
,
b_{t}=C_{2}t^{-\frac{2}{2+p}}
, and
\varphi(u)=u^{-\kappa/(1-\kappa)}
.

\Phi\big(u(t)\big)\;\leq\;\Phi\big(u(t_{0})\big)+\sum_{s=t_{0}}^{t-1}a_{s}+\sum_{s=t_{0}}^{t-1}b_{s},\qquad\Phi^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{\varphi(z)}=z^{\frac{\kappa}{1-\kappa}}.

Inverting
\Phi
and using
\sum_{s\leq t}s^{-\eta}\asymp t^{1-\eta}
for
\eta<1
gives (up to polylogs)

R_{t}\;=\;u(t)^{\frac{1}{1-\kappa}}\;\lesssim\;t^{\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\kappa}}\quad\text{and}\quad R_{t}\;\lesssim\;t^{\frac{1-\frac{2}{2+p}}{1-\kappa}}.

The second branch is looser than the direct summation of the additive term in (
32
), which yields

R_{t}\lesssim\sum_{s\leq t}s^{-\frac{2}{2+p}}\asymp t^{\frac{p}{2+p}}
.
Thus we retain the sharper additive exponent
p/(2+p)
.

Compute the first exponent:

\frac{1-\alpha}{1-\kappa}\;=\;\frac{\frac{p}{4}+\frac{(1-\beta)(2-p)}{4}}{1-\frac{\beta(2-p)}{4}}\;=\;\frac{2-\beta(2-p)}{4-\beta(2-p)}.

Therefore,

R_{t}\;=\;\widetilde{O}\!\left(t^{\,\max\!\left\{\frac{2-\beta(2-p)}{\,4-\beta(2-p)}\;,\;\frac{p}{2+p}\right\}}\right),

which is the stated regret rate.
∎

Remark D.2

(Continuous heuristic intuition)
.

There is also a very intuitive way to see why our regret should be of this rate. Indeed we can look at the excess risk
R(t+1)-R(t)=r(t+1)
for round
t+1
as the instantaneous rate of change
R^{\prime}(t)
of our regret when
t
goes toward infinity, we have :

R^{\prime}(t)\;\lesssim\;t^{-\alpha}\,R(t)^{\,\beta},

ignore constants and logarithmic factors and separate variables:

R(t)^{-\beta}\,dR(t)\;\lesssim\;t^{-\alpha}\,dt.

Integrating from a fixed
t_{0}
to
t
gives

\int_{R(t_{0})}^{R(t)}R^{-\beta}\,dR\;\lesssim\;\int_{t_{0}}^{t}s^{-\alpha}\,ds\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{R(t)^{\,1-\beta}-R(t_{0})^{\,1-\beta}}{1-\beta}\;\lesssim\;\frac{t^{\,1-\alpha}-t_{0}^{\,1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}.

Dropping lower-order constants yields

R(t)^{\,1-\beta}\;\lesssim\;t^{\,1-\alpha}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad R(t)\;\lesssim\;t^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\,1-\beta}}.

Which is the derived regret for the proper
\alpha
and
\beta
.

Advertising.

(noise
0.05
), yielding two user groups
g\in\{0,1\}
.
Each user has a latent responsiveness (target)
p_{x}\geq 0
drawn from a group–specific Gaussian:

p_{x}\mid g=0\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{0},\,\sigma_{p}^{2}),\quad p_{x}\mid g=1\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{1},\,\sigma_{p}^{2}),\qquad(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})=(3,1),\ \ \sigma_{p}=0.5,

followed by an absolute value to enforce nonnegativity (
p_{x}:=|p_{x}|
).
Given a bid
a\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the one-step
reward
is

r(p_{x},a)=\max\!\Bigg(\,\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{a}{p_{x}},&a<p_{x},\\[3.0pt]
1+\tfrac{1}{2}(p_{x}-a),&a\geq p_{x},\end{cases}\ ,\ -0.1\Bigg),

and the learning loss used in experiments is the negated reward,
\ell(a,p_{x})=-r(p_{x},a)
.
The logging policy is Gaussian,

a\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\ell},\sigma_{\ell}^{2}),\qquad\mu_{\ell}=2.0,\ \ \sigma_{\ell}=0.3,

with propensities computed from the exact Normal density.

E.3.2
Multiclass contextual bandit (environment & policy)

Here we give a quick description on how we convert a multiclass classification data to contextual bandit data

Environment.
Contexts
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, actions
\mathcal{A}=\{1,\dots,K\}
, labels
y\in\mathcal{A}
. Data are loaded via

load_dataset
with a
75/25
train/test split.

For synthetic sets we parse

‘‘synthetic_$n_features_$n_classes_$n_informative[_flip_y][_class_sep]’’

and call
make_classification
(10k samples, no redundant features, default

flip_y
=0.01,
class_sep
=1.0). The environment serves batches by slicing

(X_{\text{train}},y_{\text{train}})
. Loss is 0–1:

\ell(a,y)=\mathbf{1}\{a\neq y\}.

Logging policy.

\pi_{0}
is multinomial logistic regression (lbfgs,
L_{2}
,
C{=}1
,
max_iter
=10000) trained on the first
10\%
of
X_{\text{train}}
. For a batch
X
, it outputs class probabilities
P\in[0,1]^{n\times K}
, samples
A_{i}\sim\text{Cat}(P_{i,\cdot})
, and returns propensities
w_{i}=P_{i,A_{i}}
.

Learning policy.

\pi_{\theta}
is linear–softmax: logits
=XW
with
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times K}
, probabilities via softmax, actions sampled by inverse-CDF; propensities
\pi_{\theta}(A\mid X)
returned exactly. Greedy online evaluation uses
\arg\max_{a}
logits and 0–1 loss on the test split.

E.5
Exploration benefits

We study robustness to insufficient exploration and poor initialization in the Gaussian–quadratic environment (Example
5.1
) under three sequential designs:
CRM
, which learns from
all
collected batches but redeploys the
same
fixed logging policy at every round (no adaptive exploration);
SCRM

(
Zenati et al., 2023
)
, which redeploys an updated policy after each batch but trains only on the most recent data; and OSVP-PL which learns from
all
past data and redeploys at every round the newly learned policy.
Experiments proceed in a total of
M=15
rollouts, with rollout
m
collecting
2^{m}n_{0}
samples (with
n_{0}=100
), average over
n_{\text{seeds}}=10
seeds. At round
t
within a deployment, the logger samples
a_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t},\sigma_{t})
and records its density for exact importance weighting. The logging mean
\mu_{t}
is fixed within a deployment by the currently deployed policy and is updated only between deployments at redeployment time; the exploration level is held constant within each run (
\sigma_{t}\equiv\sigma_{0}
). Outcomes follow
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta^{\ast},\sigma_{y}^{2})
, and the per–round loss is quadratic,
\ell(a,Y)=c_{2}(a-Y)^{2}-\alpha
(minimizing loss aligns
a
with
Y
).

Final loss vs. initial misalignment (Fig.
4(a)
).

We vary the initial distance between the starting parameter and the optimum as

\delta_{0}=\lVert\theta_{0}-\theta^{\ast}\rVert\in\{0.1,\,0.3,\,0.6,\,1.0,\,1.5,\,2.0\}.

At each setting of
\delta_{0}
, we simulate
m=10
sequential deployments (rollouts), each collecting
T_{b}
interactions.
The logging policy at round
t
samples
a_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t},\,\sigma_{t})
, with exploration fixed across rounds
(
\sigma_{t}\equiv\sigma_{0}
) and mean
\mu_{t}
updated according to the policy learned at the previous deployment.
Thus, both SCRM and OSVP-PL adaptively redeploy new policies, while CRM keeps the same fixed logger. As
\delta_{0}
grows,
CRM
degrades rapidly since its fixed logger fails to explore;
SCRM
remains moderately stable thanks to redeployments, but uses only the last batch;
OSVP-PL
stays robust by learning from
all
past data and exploiting variance-adaptive penalization.

Final loss vs. logging variance (Fig.
4(b)
).

We now vary the initial exploration level

\sigma_{0}\in\{0.3,\,0.4,\,0.5,\,0.6,\,0.7,\,0.8,\,0.9\},

and let the logging policy become increasingly greedy across deployments according to the multiplicative decay

\sigma_{m}=(0.9)^{m}\,\sigma_{0},\qquad m=0,1,\dots,9.

The initial parameter
\theta_{0}
is fixed for all runs.
As exploration decreases, the support of the logged data narrows, leading to unstable importance weights.

CRM
quickly becomes unreliable under low variance;
SCRM
mitigates instability by redeploying policies at each batch, yet remains sensitive to small support;
OSVP-PL
achieves consistently lower loss by aggregating all past data and downweighting high-variance estimates via its empirical-variance penalty (Appendix
B
).
