Global trends, health inequalities, and long-term projections concerning liver cancer mortality attributable to nonalcoholic steatohepatitis between 1990 and 2050
Authors: Yujiao Deng, Zixuan Xing, Jingyue Tan, Jian Zu, Yue Zhang, Xiaomeng Cui, Chongyu Zhang, Qiaoman Fei, Dou Qu, Zhanpeng Yang, Yajing Bo, Enrui Xie, Yunyu Zhao, Xinyuan He, Xiao Yuan, Chenyang Qiao, Kairuo Wang, Xu Gao, Si Yang, Yuan Wang, Fanpu Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsNonalcoholic steatohepatitis is an important and increasing contributor to liver cancer. This study investigated mortality trends in liver cancer attributable to nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (LCN) over recent decades and projected to 2050.MethodsSpatiotemporal trends in age-standardized mortality rates (ASMRs) for LCN were evaluated using a Bayesian model. The Bayesian Age–Period–Cohort model was used to predict the ASMR and mortality in 2022–2050. Temporal trends were analyzed by calculating the average annual percent change (AAPC) in ASMRs. Health inequalities, frontier analysis, and decomposition analysis were applied.ResultsBetween 1990 and 2021, the ASMR for LCN trended upwards worldwide (AAPC 0.82). The Americas had the most rapid growth in ASMR for LCN (AAPC 2.03), whereas the African region had the highest ASMR for LCN. By 2050, India (ASMR 0.76) and Indonesia (ASMR 0.50) are predicted to be among the most populous countries facing the highest ASMR burden. Health inequalities associated with a lower sociodemographic index value have worsened over time: the concentration index was −0.24 in 2021, and the slope index of inequality is projected to decline sharply from −0.33 in 2021 to −0.82 by 2050. Decomposition analysis showed that the increase in LCN mortality was mainly attributable to changes in epidemiology and age structure. The mortality burden of smoking and high fasting plasma glucose-related LCN has continued to rise over the last three decades.ConclusionGlobal LCN mortality is escalating, particularly in low-sociodemographic and low-income regions. The expanding mortality burden associated with smoking and high fasting plasma glucose underscores the importance of lifestyle and metabolic risk factors in shaping future trajectories and the need for targeted prevention strategies in high-burden regions.

Introduction

Liver cancer (LC) is one of the most prevalent and deadly malignancies worldwide, with a burden that is continuing to rise annually.1 Nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) has become an increasingly significant contributor to the burden of LC2 and is the most common reason for liver transplantation in women in United States.3 This trend is particularly evident with the rise in obesity rates and growing prevalence of diabetes, making these nonviral etiologies more prominent in the development of LC.4

NASH has seen a marked rise in global prevalence.5 As a multisystem metabolic disease, NASH is closely related to extrahepatic organ diseases such as diabetes mellitus,6 cardiovascular disease,7 and chronic kidney disease.8 The incidence of LC attributable to NASH (LCN) has surged globally, especially in regions with high rates of obesity, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome and varies by country,9,10 posing a growing public health threat.

Unlike LC caused by viral hepatitis, LCN presents more insidiously and currently has fewer treatment options available.11 Strategies for prevention and treatment of LCN remain in the early stages of exploration.12,13 Moreover, NASH is strongly influenced by lifestyle, diet, and environmental factors, making the incidence of these types of LC highly responsive to socioeconomic and health behavior changes.14 Consequently, studying the trends and mortality burden of LCN and predicting changes in the future are essential for the development of effective public health policies and prevention strategies. However, there is still a lack of systematic analyses regarding the mortality and regional imbalance of LCN, particularly with respect to future projections.

The aim of this study was to close this gap by focusing on the LCN mortality trends over recent decades and projected to 2050, analyzing their differences globally and across regions, and providing forward-looking insights to guide global health interventions and formulation of policy.

Materials and methods

Data source and population

This study analyzed the LCN mortality burden across 204 countries and territories during 1990–2021 with projections to 2050 using comprehensive data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 database. The dataset included age-standardized mortality rates (ASMRs), death counts, population demographics stratified by sex, and socioeconomic indicators. Projections to 2050 were generated using validated modeling frameworks. The analysis spanned six World Health Organization (WHO) regions (Africa, Americas, Eastern Mediterranean, Europe, South-East Asia, and Western Pacific) and four World Bank income categories (low, lower-middle, upper-middle, and high income). Sociodemographic index (SDI) quintiles (low, low-middle, middle, high-middle, and high) and health system performance levels (minimal, low, moderate, advanced, and optimal) were incorporated to evaluate disparities in LCN mortality. We also assessed the associations of smoking as a behavioral risk factor and high fasting plasma glucose (HFPG) as a metabolic factor with LCN mortality.

Statistical analyses and data sources

The Bayesian Age–Period–Cohort model is a statistical approach that builds upon the standard Age–Period–Cohort model by incorporating Bayesian inference techniques. This model allows simultaneous analysis of age, period, and cohort effects in time-series data, offering a more robust framework for understanding mortality trends. To ensure the robustness of the long-term projections, uncertainty propagation was executed using the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation framework in R, which quantifies 95% uncertainty intervals by providing accurate deterministic approximations of posterior marginal distributions. Second-order random walk priors were systematically assigned to the age, period, and cohort effects. This choice was validated by sensitivity analyses demonstrating that alternative first-order random walk priors yielded horizontally rigid extrapolations that failed to capture information on dynamic trends. The adoption of second-order random walk priors not only aligns with standard forecasting paradigms for large-scale cancer mortality but also reflects the gradual, cumulative pathological mechanisms driving LCN as a chronic metabolic disease. The Age–Period–Cohort value and average annual percent change (AAPC) were calculated using the Joinpoint regression model, which identifies points where a significant change in a trend occurs.

We applied the Bayesian spatiotemporal model to analyze the spatial distribution and temporal trends of ASMR for LCN between 1990 and 2021. To better assess the demographic and economic impact on the LCN mortality burden across countries and territories, we used the standard methods of health equity analysis recommended by the WHO, including both absolute (slope index of inequality) and relative (concentration index) inequalities,15 to conduct an in-depth analysis of cross-country inequalities in LC mortality related to the SDI and to determine its severity and evolution over time. We performed our study using frontier analysis methods to measure the level of efficiency and potential of countries or territories to reduce the ASMR for LC. Based on the predicted ASMR value for LCN in 2050 for 204 countries or territories, the SDI was used as an indicator of the national development level. Furthermore, by using data envelopment analysis and the free disposal hull method, the gap between the predicted ASMR values of LC in each country or territory in 2050 and the corresponding expected ASMR values based on the predicted SDI values of that region was evaluated.

The standardization and decomposition method developed by Das Gupta was used to decompose the global and regional differences in LCN deaths during 1990–2021 and 2022–2050 by age structure, population growth, and epidemiological changes to explain the differences in LCN mortality. These models were implemented using R (version 4.3.3, https://www.r-project.org/, the details of the models are presented in the Supplementary Methods).

Results

Temporal and sex-specific trends in the ASMR and AAPC for LCN across regions in 1990–2050

Globally, the ASMR for LCN showed a sustained upward trajectory between 1990 and 2021, with an AAPC of 0.82 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.63–1.02). The Western Pacific was the sole exception among WHO regions, showing a stable trend in ASMR (AAPC: −0.1, 95% CI: −0.48–0.28), while the ASMR in all other regions increased during this period (Fig. 1A). Notably, the African region consistently recorded the highest ASMR for the total population and for both sexes, whereas the European region replaced the Americas as the region with the lowest ASMR. The Americas also experienced the most rapid growth in ASMR over the 32-year time span (AAPC: 2.03, 95% CI: 1.76–2.30). Analysis of the long-term data (1990–2050) revealed a linear global rise in LCN-related ASMR at an annual rate of 0.004 (Fig. 1B). Deviations from the overall trend highlighted divergent trajectories for various countries: China and India are projected to face accelerated increases in ASMR, whereas growth rates in Indonesia, Brazil, and United States are expected to be slower (Fig. 1C). These patterns underscore the influence of regional demographic and epidemiological dynamics on future mortality burdens.

LCN mortality burden according to the SDI, income, and health system, 1990–2021

The LCN mortality burden varied according to the SDI, health system, income level, and region. Specifically, the middle SDI quintile consistently experienced the highest number of LCN deaths, whereas the low SDI quintile consistently had the highest ASMR. Among men in 2021, the middle SDI quintile had the highest ASMR for LCN (Table 2). In 2021, the Americas had the highest ASMR for LCN, whereas Europe had the lowest. Areas with advanced healthcare systems had the lowest ASMR for LCN, whereas those with minimal healthcare systems had the highest ASMR. In terms of World Bank income levels, upper middle-income areas consistently had the highest number of LCN deaths. Spatiotemporal trends in LCN mortality in 204 countries and territories, 1990–2050

In terms of spatiotemporal trends in LCN mortality, significant geographic differences were observed in the ASMR. Table S1 shows the LCN ASMRs for 204 countries and territories in 2021; the ASMRs were 0.51, 0.47, 0.48, 0.41, and 0.17 for China, United States, Indonesia, India, and Brazil, respectively. The five most populous countries were selected to ensure representativeness of global demographic distribution and to facilitate comparison across major population centers. In 2050, India will have the highest ASMR of 0.76 among the five most populous countries, followed by Indonesia (0.50), United States (0.43), China (0.23), and Brazil (0.15). To highlight global temporal trends, we identified countries with the most significant changes in AAPC during the observed and projected periods (Table S1). During the observation period (1990–2021), the ASMR in Australia showed the most rapid growth (AAPC: 4.43, 95% CI: 3.94–4.93), while that in Mauritius showed the greatest decrease (AAPC: −4.49, 95% CI: −10.42–1.82). For the entire projected period (1990–2050), the trends diverged, with the ASMR expected to increase most rapidly in Norway (AAPC: 4.40, 95% CI: 3.78–5.02) and to decrease most rapidly in Singapore (AAPC: −3.32, 95% CI: −3.75 to −2.89). By 2050, it is projected that Gambia will have the highest ASMR for LCN. The global AAPC in LCN mortality during 1990–2050 is shown in Table S1.

The changes in mortality are described by the difference in α+S [i] between the periods 1990–2021 and 2022–2050. During 1990–2021, the ASMR was increased in 26.73% of countries and territories and decreased in 73.27%. However, 27.72% of countries and territories will have an increased ASMR in the next three decades. Changes in ASMR increased most rapidly in Gambia and decreased most rapidly in the Republic of Korea (Table S2).

The smoking-related ASMR for LCN increased significantly in the Eastern Mediterranean region among men (percent change 1.02, 95% uncertainty interval 0.36–1.79), the Americas (percent change 0.64 in men and 0.49 in women), and European region (percent change in AAPC 0.23 for men and 0.4 in women) (Fig. 4B–F). Smoking-related and HFPG-related deaths from LCN in the Western Pacific region showed a fluctuating upward trend (percent change 1.00 and 2.11, respectively; Fig. 4G). Smoking-related LCN deaths and ASMRs were higher among men than among women in all WHO regions. Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the global, regional, and national LCN mortality burden between 1990 and 2050. Unlike previous studies that focused primarily on temporal trends, our study integrates spatiotemporal modeling, assessment of health inequality, frontier efficiency analysis, and decomposition of demographic drivers. The findings reveal the increasing global mortality burden and substantial regional disparities in LCN, highlighting the important roles of socioeconomic status, healthcare infrastructure, and metabolic risk factors in shaping trends in LCN mortality. Between 1990 and 2021, the global ASMR for LCN showed an increasing trend, with the Americas experiencing the most rapid growth, while Africa had the highest ASMR throughout. With the exception of the Western Pacific region, ASMR increased in all five WHO regions. The rise in LCN mortality was driven mainly by changes in epidemiology and age structure. The mortality burden of smoking-related and HFPG-related LCN has increased steadily over the past 32 years. The ASMR for LCN is expected to increase significantly in China and India after 2022.

There is an urgent need to address the increasing disease burden of LCN. NASH is associated with metabolic syndromes, including obesity, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia.16,17 Mortality increases with increasing numbers of patients with metabolic syndrome. Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is associated with liver tumors and a variety of extrahepatic tumors through a variety of potential mechanisms, including metabolic dysfunction, intestinal dysbiosis, chronic inflammation, and dysregulated release of hepatic factor.18 We observed that LCN mortality showed a persistent upward trend globally, except in the Western Pacific, which might be driven by the rising prevalence of obesity, metabolic syndrome, and sedentary lifestyles.19,20 These trends are particularly pronounced in high-income regions such as North America and Europe, reflecting the transition in epidemiology of LC from viral to nonviral causes.21,22 The middle SDI quintile always had the highest number of LCN-related deaths, and the low SDI quintile and African region always had the highest ASMR for LCN, in addition to low-SDI areas, which is consistent with a previous report.23 The most rapid growth of ASMR was observed in the Americas region. Analysis of the Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients found that NASH is the most rapidly growing indication for liver transplantation in United States.24 Patients with NASH are less likely to be screened for LC than for viral hepatitis and are most likely to die while waiting for liver transplantation.25 Therefore, we must continue our efforts to find accurate noninvasive diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers, develop effective therapies for patients with NASH, and devise preventive strategies for high-risk populations to reduce mortality.26 Data from a 16-center cohort in United States, Europe, and Asia suggest that vibration-controlled transient elastography scores-based diagnosis is important for assessment of the severity of MASLD and its prognosis.27,28 Therefore, noninvasive testing should be used instead of liver biopsy for risk stratification when managing and monitoring patients with MASLD to improve their prognosis.

Global health inequities in the LCN mortality burden need to be balanced.29 From a policy perspective, frontier analysis revealed significant variability in the efficiency of national health systems for reduction of LC mortality. While some countries, such as Brazil, have achieved near-optimal reductions in ASMR through robust public health interventions,30 others, particularly low-income and middle-income regions, continue to face challenges with high mortality rates.31 According to our projections, the ASMR for LCN in China and India has been rising sharply since 2022, mirroring the increasing mortality burden of NASH in developing countries. Regions such as the Americas and the Eastern Mediterranean have shown rapid increases in the ASMR for LCN, likely reflecting shifts in lifestyle, urbanization, and limited preventive measures.2,32 However, the African region has always had the highest ASMR for LCN, highlighting the critical role of access to healthcare in the management of LCN. For example, regions with the weakest healthcare systems report a higher ASMR for LCN, underscoring the urgent need to allocate resources to improve healthcare services and treatment options in these areas.33 Furthermore, our decomposition analysis highlighted the pivotal role of demographic factors, such as aging populations, in driving LC-related mortality. By 2050, age structure is expected to play a dominant role in increases in mortality. One study showed that lipid turnover in adipose tissue decreases with age regardless of weight loss or gain 34 and that this decline is often associated with metabolic disorders such as insulin resistance and dyslipidemia. Given these differences, regionally tailored strategies are needed to improve prevention, early diagnosis and treatment, and the long-term management of NASH-related LC.35 Our statistical projections extending to 2050 carry inherent uncertainties regarding future clinical breakthroughs. The epidemiology of metabolic liver disease is evolving rapidly with the clinical scaling of GLP-1 receptor agonists and newly approved MASH-specific therapies (e.g., resmetirom), as well as tightening public health policies targeting obesity. Given that frameworks driven by historical data cannot fully anticipate the long-term impact of these emerging therapies, our models may over-project the future mortality burden, necessitating continuous updates with further accumulation of real-world data.

The risk factors for NASH include alcohol consumption, genetic susceptibility, epigenetics,5 diabetes,36 and microorganisms in the gut.37 We found that the smoking-related and HFPG-related LCN mortality burden has increased over the past few decades, indicating that more effective measures are needed to limit tobacco use and more resources should be allocated to managing people with high blood sugar, especially women in the African and Eastern Mediterranean regions. Notably, the global prevalence of non-alcohol-associated fatty liver/steatohepatitis in patients with type 2 diabetes is 55.5%, which is twice that of the general population, and has the highest prevalence in Europe.6 NASH also significantly increases the incidence of multiple extrahepatic complications, including diabetes, cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and certain extrahepatic malignancies.18,38 Therefore, it is important to design effective treatments for metabolic syndrome and cancer screening programs for patients with NASH. Mechanistically, chronic hyperglycemia exacerbates hepatic insulin resistance, promoting inflammation, oxidative stress, and fibrosis, which are key pathways in NASH-related hepatocarcinogenesis.39,40 Smoking accelerates fibrosis through increased oxidative damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, and release of inflammatory cytokines.41,42 Clinical studies have confirmed a direct correlation between high glucose levels and an increased incidence of LC among patients with NASH.43 Similarly, chronic smoking significantly elevates the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma in individuals with underlying metabolic disorders.41 Notably, associations between behavioral/metabolic risk factors and LCN mortality were derived from population-level GBD estimates; therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as causal relationships at the individual level because of the possibility of ecological fallacy.

This study had several limitations. First, although the data on age groups, years, and regions were extensive, they did not cover all regions of the world. Therefore, data from regions with poor healthcare facilities and limited ability to collect data, such as low-income countries, should be viewed with caution. Second, while Bayesian spatiotemporal and decomposition models provide robust projections, inherent uncertainties in long-term forecasting must be considered, especially considering potential shifts in global health behaviors or policy implementations. Third, since GBD only provides estimated data on the causes of LCN, this study is unable to analyze the coexistence of other causes. Fourth, the findings of our frontier analysis must be interpreted cautiously. Although several low-resource countries did not show any effective differences, suggesting that they have fully leveraged their resources at the statistical level, this pattern may reflect systemic underdiagnosis, underreporting, or incomplete cancer registration networks rather than optimal healthcare performance. Consequently, data-sparse regions require an improved surveillance infrastructure to validate these epidemiological boundaries. Fifth, we acknowledge the ongoing transition from NAFLD/NASH to MASLD/MASH nomenclature in the hepatology field. Although GBD 2021 still uses the traditional terminology, future epidemiological studies will need to consider this updated classification to ensure consistency and improved clinical relevance. Finally, emerging therapies for NASH may alter future trends, which were not fully accounted for in this study. In view of the limitations of GBD data, all risk factors for LCN could not be considered in our prediction model or in the analytical methods used. In the future, prediction models and decomposition analysis should include as many risk factors related to metabolic dysfunction, visceral obesity, and genetics as possible. Future research should focus on validating these projections with updated datasets and further exploration of the interactions among the demographic, genetic, metabolic, and behavioral factors contributing to LCN.

Conclusions

This study analyzed the global, regional, and national LCN mortality burden and revealed temporal and spatial trends and regional imbalances. The global ASMR of LCN has continued to increase over the past three decades. The global LCN mortality burden is expected to continue to rise, with significant disparities across regions and sexes. Governments must prioritize the development of targeted public health strategies to reduce the mortality burden, particularly in high-risk regions.
