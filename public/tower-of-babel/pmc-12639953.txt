FLASH: innovative integrated enzymatic-fluorescent labeling for automated muscle fiber typing, metabolic and morphometric analysis
Authors: Maxime Di Gallo, Thomas Guilbert, Doriane Pereira, Zoé Cepella, Raphaël Braud-Mussi, Edgar Jauliac, Gaspard Macaux, Florian Alexis Britto, Thierry Launay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSkeletal muscle is a dynamic tissue capable of structural and metabolic remodeling in response to physiological and pathological stimuli. These adaptations are central to understanding the mechanisms underlying conditions such as genetic myopathies, cancer, aging, and recovery from injury. Muscle fiber characterization—assessing fiber type, size, and metabolic profile—is essential for such studies. However, conventional histological methods often rely on serial tissue sections and multiple staining protocols, which are time-consuming, require significant biological material, and introduce methodological bias.MethodsWe developed FLASH (Fluorescence-based Labeling for Assessing Skeletal muscle Histology), a novel methodology combining enzymatic (SDH or GPDH) and quadruple fluorescent labeling (Laminin, MYH4, MYH2, MYH7) on a single muscle section. The resulting images were analyzed using a custom macro in Fiji/ImageJ, integrating the Cellpose segmentation algorithm. This automated pipeline detects individual muscle fibers, quantifies their cross-sectional area (CSA), identifies fiber types based on myosin isoform expression, and measures enzymatic staining intensity. Batch analysis was implemented to process entire image folders automatically. Validation was performed by comparing automated fiber detection with expert manual segmentation using correlation analysis and Bland-Altman plots.ResultsThe FLASH method allowed simultaneous assessment of both contractile and metabolic properties within individual fibers on the same section, removing the need for serial cuts. The automated image analysis achieved high accuracy in fiber detection (r > 0.95 compared to manual annotation) and produced consistent CSA and fiber-type quantification, even under suboptimal staining conditions. The macro enabled significant time savings by automating the complete analysis workflow, including ROI generation and Excel data export for each image.ConclusionsFLASH provides an efficient and robust tool for high-throughput skeletal muscle histology. By combining enzymatic and fluorescent co-labeling with machine learning-based image analysis, this method improves reproductibility, reduces experimental complexity, and minimizes user bias. FLASH is particularly well-suited for large-scale or longitudinal studies investigating muscle adaptation in health and disease.

Introduction

We are currently in a period of lightning technological evolution, and this is enabling us to imagine and design tools that will be helpful in terms of the precision and reproducibility of scientific research. Nowadays, it is of crucial importance to develop innovative tools to facilitate technical research work and improve the accuracy, reproducibility and rigor of scientific productions [1]. Muscle fiber classification in biomedical research similarly adheres to this principle. In fact, skeletal muscle is a highly adaptable organ modifying its structure under different conditions [2, 3]. It is now widely acknowledged that these changes may contribute to the development of pathologies or act as a co-morbidity factor in certain chronic diseases such as cancer, genetic myopathy, spinal cord injury or sepsis [3]. In other hand, muscle metabolic and structural adaptation also occurs in response to physical activity [2, 4]. Indeed, the role of fiber types (I, IIa, IIx, IIb) in muscular pathologies, sports performance and aging is a step in scientific knowledge that still needs a great deal of further investigation. The development of knowledge on this subject requires the ability to correctly quantify the different types of fibers, determine their size, their proportion in a muscle, as well as the metabolism used predominantly. That notably requires myosin heavy chain isoforms labeling and detection of Succinate DeHydrogenase (SDH) and/or Glycerol-3-Phosphate-Dehydrogenase (GPDH) enzymatic activity giving information on fibers contractile and metabolic functions [5, 6].

To determine fiber myosin expression and metabolism in muscle section, efficient methods have been developed [7, 8]. However, these are classically performed in separated muscle sections increasing experimental time and biological material consumption. Moreover, the analysis in serial cuts to associate myosin expression with energetic metabolism could drive to an increase of bias and methodological errors. As a result, the development of techniques that allow the detection of both myosin isoform and fiber metabolism within the same muscle fiber on the same muscle section are of paramount importance. Thus, the development of fluorescent labeling techniques combined with the analysis of metabolic enzyme activities on the same tissue section within the same muscle cell constitutes an indispensable tool in muscle biology. In this study, we initially developed a technique for fluorescent multi-labeling of structural molecules, coupled with colorimetric enzymatic labeling of SDH or GPDH activity. This technique has been tested on skeletal muscle section from C57Bl6N and SIX1 inducible muscle mice (KO HSA-Cre-ERT2 mice) which are known to develop oxidative muscle fibers [9]. Secondly, the analysis of images using processing softwares like ImageJ [10] is time consuming and also a source of error even if great progress has been made recently with the design of MuscleJ2 [7] or Truefad [8]. Notably, in our experience, the Cellpose algorithm [11] provides a considerably improved detection performance compared to most commercially available tools. Therefore, we considered developing a tool based on Cellpose segmentation to accelerate image processing and enhance the accuracy of automated fiber analysis. To further minimize analysis time and maximize fiber detection precision, we designed an automated pipeline that extracts multiple features from each fiber, enabling robust and accurate classification. To achieve this, we used a homemade ImageJ/FiJi (Fiji Is Just ImageJ) macro (FLASH: Fluorescence-based Labeling for Assessing Skeletal muscle Histology) that integrates the Cellpose segmentation algorithm [11], based on machine learning, to segment muscle fibers even when their contour labeling seem not visually perfect. Our method permitted to highly performant fibers detection and automatically quantified CSA, fluorescence intensity (myosin isoform expression) or GPDH/SDH staining for all fibers present in section. Moreover, FLASH was also developed to automatically analyse a batch of images in a selected folder, drastically reducing the person hours needed for the processing analysis.

Methods

Animals

Analyses were performed on mixed phenotype muscle (plantaris) of both wild type mice and muscle specific and inducible SIX1 KO mice (Six1flox/flox:: HSA-Cre-ERT2, developed in our laboratory) displaying an increase of slow oxidative fibers. 1 mg/day of Tamoxifen (Tam, MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA, USA) was administered intraperitoneally for 5 days to induce specific deletion of Six1 in skeletal muscle. Muscles from these mice were harvested and analyzed 1 month after the first tamoxifen injection to allow sufficient time for the fiber type phenotype to be established. Wild-type mice served as the control group. The mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation, and the hind leg muscles were carefully dissected and embedded in OCT compound. Subsequently, the tissue samples were rapidly frozen by immersion in isopentane cooled with liquid nitrogen and then stored at −80 °C until cryosections. Animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the European guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the institutional ethic committee and French Ministry of Research (n°2022121514506347 and n°201809041512432, agreement n°C75-14-02).

Image analysis

Image analysis was performed using the FLASH macro implemented in Fiji software [12] with a complete installation guide available at [GitHub - IMAG-IC/FLASH_FiJi_Macro]. Both MacOS and Windows computers were utilized for the analysis. It should be noted that Fiji requires a system with Java 8 available in order to run. To automatize the analysis, installation of Cellpose wrapper for Fiji, provided to the scientific community by the BioImagning and Optics Platform of EPFL, is necessary [GitHub - BIOP/ijl-utilities-wrappers].

FLASH macro validation

Six1 deletion in skeletal muscle results in a fiber type transition from fast glycolytic to slow oxidative allowing us to compare and validate quantification method on fibers CSA, metabolic phenotype and myosin expression [13]. Comparisons were made in various ways. Firstly, we assessed whether the automatic fiber detection system could identify a sufficient number of muscle fibers across the section, with a wide enough range of fiber sizes to avoid missing regenerating fibers (Fig. 3). Next, we compared the quantification of fiber types and their diameters using the FLASH macro on two different computers operated by two independent users. Finally, we compared the macro-based quantification results with those obtained through manual quantification and famous available softwares, again conducted independently by two investigators (Fig. 4).

Statistics

All statistical analyses were done using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0 GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). To examine the significance of observed differences between groups, we performed a two-way ANOVA, which allows the assessment of both main and interaction effects of two independent factors. Prior to analysis, data were tested for normality to ensure that the assumptions of ANOVA were satisfied. All statistical analyses were conducted using a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Data preparation

To analyze muscle images accurately with FLASH macro, it is imperative to (I) work on a copy of raw data to avoid their modification or loss, (II) ensure that each fiber type is assigned to the same channel across all images during channel merging, and (III) ensure that only the specific region intended for analysis is present in the image, or more precisely in the laminin channel. This precaution is necessary as the analysis software will otherwise take into account all visible elements. Consequently, image preparation is crucial, including any necessary cropping using tools such as the “Clear” and “Clear Outside” FiJi functions, to isolate and highlight the muscle or the area of interest. No specific minimum or maximum cropping size is required for optimal FLASH performance; however, sufficient image resolution must be maintained to preserve analysis quality and avoid excessive pixelation that could compromise fiber detection accuracy. This isolation can be carried out only on the laminin channel, since segmentation of the fibers takes place there (Fig. 1B). Be careful not to save the image by leaving a selection on it (such as a small drawn circle), otherwise the segmentation will take place on this selection and not on the whole section as intended in the process.

Conceptual description

FLASH’s architecture is based on an approach divided into several distinct modules, which are designed to offer a complete and automated muscle fibers classification solution. The workflow is organized into five main modules, each corresponding to a distinct stage of the analysis. The first module involves the configuration of parameters, which is performed only once at the beginning of the analysis and not repeated for subsequent images. Key configuration parameters include the average fiber diameter in pixels, which is automatically and autonomously calibrated by default. However, this value can be manually set if the user wishes to specifically target small or large fibers, for example. Additionally, the fiber types present in the analyzed sections must be defined by their corresponding channel numbers. Channels that are not used or fiber types that are absent from the staining should be assigned a value of 0 (e.g., if no MyHC IIx channel is present, the IIx channel should be set to 0). Following this, the workflow enters an iterative image analysis loop. This loop begins with the segmentation of individual fibers from the laminin channel, as defined during the initial configuration. Next, each segmented fiber is classified using an adaptive thresholding method. The results are then visualized as overlays on the original source image, allowing for qualitative assessment. Finally, quantitative statistical data are exported to the source folder before proceeding to the next image in the dataset. This process repeats until all prepared images and their respective fibers have been analyzed (Fig. 1 C).

The first module manages the configuration of general parameters in the form of an intuitive graphical interface. This is based primarily on the showEnhancedDialog() function, which enables comprehensive customization of all analysis parameters, including channel selection, mean diameter measurement, and the method used to assess fiber size. An optional checkbox allows access to advanced settings prior to validation, primarily intended for users seeking optimization. These parameters include fine-tuning of Cellpose sensitivity, exclusion thresholds for small and large structures, an adaptive thresholding factor for classification, as well as filtering options and GPU-related performance settings (Fig. 1 C).

The second module relates to the segmentation of the section into different muscle fibers. This component is central and decisive because it uses an innovative segmentation approach combining several phases. Initial fiber detection by the FiJi Cellpose wrapper (configurable via the configureCellpose() function) is based on laminin membrane labeling. The first involves image pre-processing with the application of directional filters and local contrast adjustment on the laminin channel. Downstream processing involves the use of functions such as getPlatformSafePath() and testGPUAvailability() to optimize segmentation performance and analysis time, along with cleaning and filtering algorithms to eliminate potential membrane-marking artifacts that could interfere with accurate segmentation. Once this has been done, segmentation by Cellpose can be correctly performed using the cyto3 model with the diameter specified by the user and optimized for the detection of muscle contours. The masks are then post-processed using morphological algorithms to close small discontinuities, eliminate aberrant structures and separate adjacent fibers using controlled watersheds, thanks to the MorphoLibJ plugin. Automatic elimination of objects according to criteria of size, circularity and relative position, indicated by the user in the advanced parameters, can also be implemented (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Overview and illustration of the FLASH analytical modules

Overview and illustration of the FLASH analytical modules

Once segmented into ROIs (Regions Of Interest) representing the different fibers in the section, the third module is dedicated to morphological analysis. Here, for each fiber detected, the system performs exhaustive measurements of morphological parameters such as area, perimeter, circularity, solidity, eccentricity and aspect ratio. The system also performs intensity measurements per channel, analyzing signal homogeneity and detecting peripheral staining to ensure that observed signals are not due to contamination from adjacent fibers. Here, for each ROIs, an homogeneity score is calculated, incorporating the coefficient of variation (CV = σ/µ), skewness, kurtosis (a measure of distribution sharpness), and the deviation between the mean and median. This scoring system is designed to penalize highly heterogeneous fibers, which may correspond to non-specific aggregates, while prioritizing those with consistent and homogeneous staining. This module implements advanced statistical functions such as calculateMean(), calculateStd(), calculateMedian(), calculateRatios() and specialized functions such as detectBimodalDistribution(), analyzeTexture() or detectPeripheralStaining(), which provide detailed knowledge of individual fiber information in order to optimize their classification (Fig. 2).

This leads to the fourth module, which is the innovative heart of FLASH.ijm, enabling muscle fiber classification to be estimated. Based on all fibers data, classification can be carried out using a sophisticated multi-criteria algorithm. The first step is to primarily classify the ROIs, i.e., to determine the main ROI type based on normalized intensities and homogeneity factors. The main determinant of a fiber’s positivity is the calculation of adaptive thresholds based on grayscale values for all fiber types, whether identified via immunofluorescence staining or enzymatic labeling. Here the enhancedAdaptiveThreshold() algorithm dynamically determines the optimal thresholds based on the statistical distribution of intensities, the bimodality analysis of the distributions and the textural characteristics of each of the fibers. It has a penalty and advantage system in the thresholding calculation that rebalances the BaseFactor. This variable is the number that acts as final threshold, which receives penalizing and advantageous multiplications throughout the various functions, ending with a final number that characterizes the discrimination or the inclusion of the fiber in this classification according to the individual characteristics of the ROI, based on the morphological analysis carried out in the third module (Fig. 2).

A second phase then consists of detecting hybrid fibers using weighted scores and biological rules to identify fibers co-expressing multiple myosin isoforms. This module includes a number of specialized functions, such as classifyHybridFiber(), which can detect fibers expressing several myosin isoforms and indicate that they are hybrids for both myosin isoforms or for one myosin isoform and a metabolic marker. Finally, the calculateHomogeneity() function is used to analyze the signal distribution within each fiber and adapt its classification accordingly, with a homogeneity Score factor that fluctuates as a function of penalty and advantage depending on the homogeneity of the signal, making it possible to discriminate between artefactual markings that could cause the classification to be incorrectly estimated (Fig. 2).

Finally, once the classification is determined, the final phase can be activated by generating several outputs. The fifth and final module allows to create a classification display directly on the source image with a colored overlay of the fibers according to their type. FLASH respects the original colors of the immunostaining channels and, if it does not find them, will define them itself with adjustable overlay transparency in the advanced settings. This overlay is useful to see immediately the accuracy of the classification estimate as well as potential misclassified fibers which are therefore easy to correct in the spreadsheet file. Speaking of which, there is also a data export in.csv spreadsheet format that contains all the measurements and summary statistics by fiber type, which are automatically adjusted. That is to say that by modifying an ROI in its classification column, from type IIa to IIb for example, the statistical table is automatically modified, both in terms of the number of IIa and IIb, as well as their size and proportion (Fig. 2).

Macro dependencies

The FLASH macro requires several essential dependencies. The first is FiJi. The ImageJ macro language is the only one used in this fiber type estimation system, enabling optimal integration by blending seamlessly into the FiJi environment without the need for external frameworks such as Python or other coding languages. Among the essential plugins is MorphoLibJ [14], required for advanced morphological operations, used in particular for controlled watershed segmentation, mathematical morphology operations and geodesic fiber analysis. The other plugin required is Cellpose3.1, which includes a dedicated conda environment for fiber segmentation. Tutorial

The complete installation guide is available at [GitHub - IMAG-IC/FLASH_FiJi_Macro]. On first use, Cellpose will automatically download its pre-trained templates. Then, the first step is to prepare the images to be analyzed (Fig. 1B). To illustrate this tutorial, we have chosen an [x, y, c] dimension image with the type IIb fibers on channel 1, laminin on channel 2, the type I fibers on channel 3, the type IIa fibers on channel 4, and the enzymatic label (SDH or GPDH depending on the section) on channel 5. If your SDH labeling was acquired via brightfield microscopy, invert the channel’s lookup table (LUT) in Fiji using Image > Color > Invert LUT to ensure positive labeling corresponds to stained fibers, because brightfield images typically display a white background, which Fiji interprets as high gray-level intensity. Without inversion, the software misattributes signal intensity to the background rather than the target fibers.

Once this image preparation step is done as mentioned before (Fig. 1B), the macro can be run. The analysis begins by filling in the various initial parameters on the user interface, which offers two levels of configuration:

Concerning the basic configuration, the user defines parameters such as the total number of channels in the image, here 5, the laminin channel that will be used for fiber segmentation, here number 2, the size measurement method, here Cross Sectional Area, the specific channels for each fiber type (Type I, IIa, IIx, IIb, and other customizable channels - in this case, we have used SDH), as well as the auto-calibrate diameter of the fibers, checked here.

An optional checkbox gives access to advanced settings such as fine-tuning of Cellpose sensitivity, exclusion thresholds for small and large structures, an adaptive thresholding factor for classification, as well as filtering options and GPU-related performance settings, if you want to refine your analysis.

Once all the desired parameters have been entered and the “OK” button clicked, the user must select the folder containing the well-prepared images in.TIF format. These images should have consistent channel correspondences for each fiber type and include only the laminin staining within the region of interest. Upon folder selection, the FLASH software will automatically process each image sequentially, performing the analysis and saving the results in the original folder. The output files will be named by appending identifiers to the original image filenames, ensuring traceability without requiring further user intervention. The process concludes with a confirmation message indicating successful completion.

Results

We sought to validate the accuracy of FLASH by demonstrating its capability to detect a wide range of fiber sizes, thereby enabling the inclusion of a comprehensive spectrum of fiber diameters (Fig. 3.A). However, segmentation becomes increasingly challenging and less accurate under pathological conditions such as denervated muscle with severe atrophy and fibrosis, where accuracy dropped to around 80% (data not shown). To further assess the algorithm’s performance, we compared FLASH-derived results with well-established data obtained from the skeletal muscle-specific SIX1 knockout (KO) mouse model, which exhibits a known shift towards a slower muscle phenotype following deletion in soleus muscle [9]. In agreement with this study, our analysis in plantaris muscle also revealed a fiber transition from glycolytic fiber to oxidative ones (7.3% decrease in the proportion of type IIb fibers and a 7.8% reduction in type IIx fibers, accompanied by an increase of 10% in type IIa fibers and notably a 5.1% increase in type I fibers (Fig. 3.C)). Although the muscle analyzed here is not the same as in Sakakibara et al., study, the overall trend aligns with the previously reported shift towards a slower phenotype, albeit with a less pronounced effect, thus supporting the ability of FLASH to capture biologically relevant changes in fiber type composition.

To further validate our approach, we compared the results obtained with FLASH to those from manual counting. We extracted the number and size of type I, IIa, IIx, and IIb fibers, as well as their colocalization with SDH and GPDH metabolic labeling, from 7 to 5 sections respectively of the plantaris muscle, which expresses all fiber types. Comparisons between manual counts and automated quantifications by FLASH revealed no significant differences regarding fiber type proportions—including hybrid fibers identified by colocalization of SDH or GPDH labeling—and the average fiber size measurements (Fig. 4E–J). These findings strongly support the high reliability of FLASH for accurate estimation of muscle fiber size and composition.Fig. 4Manual vs. FLASH automated counting : accuracy comparison with available software tools

Manual vs. FLASH automated counting : accuracy comparison with available software tools

To demonstrate its utility as a novel quantification tool, we further benchmarked FLASH against available software capable of similar analyses, namely MuscleJ2 and TrueFAD, using the same seven SDH-stained sections. We extracted fiber type proportions and the total number of fibers detected by each software and compared these metrics with manual counts. Regarding type IIb fibers, MuscleJ2 showed no significant difference compared to manual counting, whereas TrueFAD systematically overestimated their proportion by an average of 12.8%. For type IIx fibers, only MuscleJ2 deviated significantly, overestimating their proportion by 20.2% relative to manual quantification. Both MuscleJ2 and TrueFAD underestimated type IIa fiber proportions by 21% and 11.5%, respectively. Type I fibers, which are scarce in the plantaris, showed no significant differences across all methods. Importantly, since SDH colocalization analysis with fiber types is not supported in these software packages, the counts for these hybrid fibers were effectively zero.

Finally, in terms of overall fiber detection sensitivity, FLASH missed on average only 3.5% of fibers per section, whereas MuscleJ2 and TrueFAD failed to detect 18.5% and 24.3% of fibers on average, respectively. These discrepancies further highlight the limitations of these tools in accurately assessing fiber type proportions as reported above (Fig. 4E).

Discussion

The application developed in this work provides an effective link between the detection power of cellpose software and the image processing capabilities of ImageJ. Changes in fiber typology and specific alterations in their phenotype (including size) reflect muscle adaptation in response to physiological stimuli or pathophysiology [3]. Studying these variations is an essential step in understanding the mechanisms involved in these adaptations. Although the determination of the fiber composition on the entire muscle section area, the manual measurement of fiber typology, energy metabolism and CSA on sections is tedious and error-prone. It requires serial sections, numerous fluorescent and enzymatic markings, and channel-by-channel image analysis [15]. Furthermore, analysis is also highly dependent on the quality of the staining.

Enormous progress has been made with the advent of several software packages capable of automating fiber counting and size measurement (MuscleJ2 [7]), or improving fiber detection to limit errors using a machine learning-based detection system (TRUEFAD [8]),. MuscleJ2 also enables specific analyses of regeneration and vascularization. We propose a method associated with a new macro that offers numerous advantages. The FLASH method initially combines enzymatic labeling (SDH or GPDH) with quadruple fluorescent labeling (Laminin, MYH4, MYH2, MYH7). This first step avoids the need for serial sections to specify fiber energy metabolism in function of myosin isoform expression. This reduces the number of error-prone steps and refines counting, while cutting down on experimental time. The “custom channel” feature used here for the enzymatic stainings SDH and GPDH can readily be extended to other enzymatic markers, such as Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) staining, to determine the proportion of glycogen stored within each muscle fiber type, or to markers like Oil Red O for quantifying intramyocellular triglycerides. Furthermore, while FLASH has been applied to skeletal muscle cross-sections in the present study, this approach is readily adaptable to other segmented tissues, such as cardiomyocytes, provided that a membrane-labeling stain is used to delineate individual fibers. In a second step, the macro automates all the other steps for a complete image file to be processed including: detection of fibers, measurement of their CSA and typology, production of a ROI and a summary excel file per image (containing number of each fiber types and their CSA), chaining with the next image in the file. The macro uses Fiji’s cellpose plug-in, based on machine learning, to better detect fibers [11]. The level of detection is similar to that achieved by several experts on independent machines. Indeed, we show in this paper no difference in the counting of our Six1 mutant typology using Myosin Heavy Chain staining as well as Succinate DeHydrogenase between our application and the manual counting by two different operators. Moreover, importantly, thanks to machine learning, detection remains identical even with poor laminin labeling. Our macro thus enables reliable measurements even under sub-optimal experimental conditions, and considerable time-saving through automation and image batch.

The main limitation of our tool is that it can make errors in identifying the fiber type if the marking is not homogeneous (areas darker than others for example in the overall slide). In the first case it will tend to consider that the positive fibers for a channel will be negative if it is in a dark area. In the second case it will tend to “hallucinate” positive fibers especially if the positive marking is close to the background noise. The level of detection is similar to that achieved by several experts on independent machines.
