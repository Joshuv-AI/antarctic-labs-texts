Assessing the predictive power of nutritional indices on all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in hemodialysis patients: a longitudinal study
Authors: Qian Wang, Zengchun Ye, Jialing Rao, Wenbo Zhao, Jianqiang Liu, Yanru Chen, Juncheng Xiang, Xin Zhen, Jiawen Li, Yunfang Zhang, Xianrui Dou, Zhaoyong Hu, Jun Zhang, Hui Peng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and HypothesisThe prognostic value of nutritional scoring tools in assessing the relationship between nutritional status and prognosis in hemodialysis patients is unclear. This multicenter retrospective cohort study compared the Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI), Controlling Nutritional Status scores (CONUT), and Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) for predictive accuracy of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, especially the impact of dynamic changes over time on prognosis.MethodsHemodialysis patients from four hospitals were included. Laboratory data and nutrition scores were collected at the initiation of dialysis, and at 6th, 12th, and 18th months after dialysis initiation. A joint model analyzed the relationship between dynamic nutritional scores and prognosis. Predictive values were assessed using the area under the curve (AUC).ResultsThe study included 863 patients with a median follow-up of 37 months. During the follow-up, 23.8% of patients died, with 14% attributed to cardiovascular causes. Malnourished patients demonstrated higher risks for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Dynamic changes in PNI and GNRI scores were significantly associated with reduced all-cause and cardiovascular mortality risks. Precisely, longitudinal increases in PNI and GNRI scores corresponded to a 4% and 3% reduction in all-cause (PNI: HR, 0.96; 95% CI, 0.95–0.98; GNRI: HR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.96–0.98) and cardiovascular mortality risk (PNI: HR, 0.96; 95% CI, 0.94–0.98; GNRI: HR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.95–0.98) respectively, with longer dialysis duration. Changes in CONUT scores were not significantly associated with either all-cause or cardiovascular mortality. The AUCs of the three joint models indicated that the GNRI score (0.893) possessed higher predictive accuracy for all-cause mortality compared to PNI (0.832) and CONUT (0.852). Similar trends were observed for cardiovascular mortality.ConclusionNutritional scores and their dynamic changes are intimately associated with mortality risk in hemodialysis patients. Compared to PNI and CONUT, the baseline GNRI and its post-dialysis variations demonstrate a superior predictive capability for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in these patients.Keywords: objective nutritional scores, hemodialysis, mortality, prognostic value

Introduction

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is a significant global health challenge characterized by the gradual decline of kidney function. With the prevalence of CKD on the rise, it is estimated to affect approximately 9.1% of the global population [1]. As one of the principal renal replacement therapies, hemodialysis plays a critical role for patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD). However, patients undergoing hemodialysis often face numerous challenges, including cardiovascular diseases [2], infections [3], and notably, malnutrition [4], which collectively contribute to morbidity and mortality.

Malnutrition is particularly prevalent among hemodialysis patients and significantly contributes to the increased risk of cardiovascular events and mortality [4]. This condition adversely impacts patients’ quality of life and outcomes [5–7]. Early detection and accurate assessment of malnutrition are essential for determining appropriate clinical interventions and predicting patient prognosis. The insidious nature of malnutrition adds complexity to its management. Early stages often lack overt symptoms, making detection challenging. Furthermore, when symptoms are mild, malnutrition may not receive the necessary attention for intervention. Consequently, a reliable assessment of nutritional status is imperative for effective management.

Various indices have been employed to evaluate hemodialysis patients’ nutritional status, but no gold standard exists. Among the indices, the Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) [8] and the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) [9] are widely used for malnutrition screening. These indices have demonstrated a strong association with patient prognosis [10–14]. Moreover, recent studies suggest that the CONUT score may also help assess nutritional status related to prognosis in dialysis patients [15,16]. The three scores necessitate collecting certain information, such as the patient’s height, weight, serum albumin, total cholesterol, and peripheral blood lymphocyte count. These indicators are objective and straightforward to obtain to evaluate patients’ nutritional condition, and their scores are precisely determined using precise formulas. This information is more easily accessible for hemodialysis patients who receive routine blood testing. Multidisciplinary processes, including body, body composition, biochemical, functional, dietary, subjective evaluations and judgments, are necessary for other nutrition scoring tools like MIS, SGA and NRS2002. In addition to requiring qualified staff for the assessment, each evaluation entails a substantial time and workforce commitment. So, we select these three scoring systems for comparison, and some literature compares these three scores to MIS, SGA, and others, demonstrating that they are not inferior in diagnosing malnutrition [9,17,18].

However, existing research in this domain has limitations. First, previous studies have included patients with varied dialysis durations, which can affect the nutritional status and, consequently, the accuracy of prognostic assessments. Additionally, most of these studies have been cross-sectional, lacking longitudinal follow-up data. This absence of temporal data limits the understanding of how changes in nutritional status over time relate to the prognosis of hemodialysis patients.

To address these gaps, this multicenter retrospective cohort study aims to longitudinally assess the nutritional status of hemodialysis patients using PNI, GNRI, and CONUT scores. We will examine the relationship between these scores and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality by utilizing data collected at the initiation of dialysis and at 6th, 12th, and 18th months thereafter. Our objective is to discern which of these nutritional indices most effectively predicts patient prognosis and to deepen the understanding of the role of nutritional assessment in managing hemodialysis patients. This study aims to inform clinical practice in managing malnutrition among hemodialysis patients by investigating the temporal changes in nutritional scores and their correlation with clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study was a multicenter, retrospective cohort investigation conducted between January 2010 and June 2022, involving four outpatient dialysis centers in Guangdong Province, China, including the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, Foshan Shunde New Rongqi Hospital, the People’s Hospital of Huadu District, and Shunde Hospital of Southern Medical University. The inclusion criteria encompassed patients with a definitive diagnosis of chronic kidney disease stage 5d who had undergone hemodialysis for at least 4 months. Exclusion criteria included incomplete medical records, absence of essential baseline data (such as height, weight, serum albumin, total cholesterol, peripheral blood lymphocyte count), or lack of follow-up records (Figure 1).

Flow chart.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, China (registration number: II2023-154-01), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective study design and the analysis of anonymized patient data.

Study design and outcomes of interest

Endpoints were ascertained through the hemodialysis management system. The primary endpoint was all-cause mortality, while the secondary endpoint encompassed cardiovascular mortality, which included fatalities resulting from heart failure, myocardial infarction, fatal arrhythmia, sudden death, cerebral hemorrhage, and cerebral infarction.

Three objective nutrition scores

Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI): The PNI was computed using the formula Points = ALB (g/L) + 5 * LYM (10^9/L) [8]. The PNI score has been utilized since its release in 1980 to evaluate patients’ nutritional status in cardiovascular illnesses [19,20], cancer [21–23], and surgical patients [24]. In several articles, categorization is based on the median of the PNI score. Nevertheless, the GNRI and CONUT are not categorized according to the median. The cutoff values for the PNI score in this work are mainly established regarding pertinent literature. This paper utilizes 38 as a crucial number to distinguish between various nutritional statuses, primarily based on some significant publications in kidney disease [25] and cardiovascular disease [19,20]. A score exceeding 38 was considered normal, whereas scores equal to or below 38 indicated malnutrition. A higher score signified better nutritional status.

Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI): The GNRI was computed employing the equation formulated by Bouillanne et al. for geriatric patients as follows: Points = (1.489 * ALB g/L) + 41.7 * (current body weight/ideal body weight) [9]. Since its publication in 2005, The GNRI has clear criteria for classifying nutritional status. The GNRI formula replaces the ideal weight in the NRI formula with the usual weight calculated from the Lorentz formula [9]. The article indicates that the GNRI cutoff values were determined according to weight losses of 5% or 10% and abnormal albumin concentrations of 38, 35, and 30 g/L. From these GNRI values, we defined 4 grades of nutrition-related risk: A score >98 was considered normal; scores of 92 to 98, 82 to 91, and <82 reflect mild, moderate, and severe malnutrition, respectively [9,26]. Combined with the data characteristics of this paper, the overall patients were divided into two groups: normal nutrition and malnutrition. Controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT): CONUT was initially devised as an objective tool for screening hospital patients for nutritional status. It is derived by calculating scores based on three parameters: serum albumin, total cholesterol, and peripheral blood lymphocyte count [17]. CONUT is a nutritional scoring tool developed by J. Ignacio de Ulíbarri in 2005. The authors have placed scores according to the information published and the heuristic knowledge obtained from long experience. The albumin has double the rating than cholesterol and lymphocytes, as it provides more “weight” as an undernutrition indicator. A score of 0 to 1 is considered normal; scores of 2 to 4, 5 to 8, and 9 to 12 reflect mild, moderate, and severe malnutrition, respectively [17]. To divide into two groups uniformly, a score exceeding 5 was regarded as malnutrition, whereas scores equal to or below 5 indicated normal. Contrary to PNI and GNRI, a higher CONUT score indicates worse nutritional status.

The nutritional status of newly initiated hemodialysis patients was assessed using the three nutritional scores, and patients were categorized into two groups: a normal nutrition group and a malnutrition group (Table 1).

Data collection

Baseline data was defined as the data collected before the initiation of dialysis. Trained research staff collected the baseline and follow-up data on patient characteristics and laboratory tests using the hemodialysis management software from the four hospitals. The collected characteristics included age, sex, etiology, height, and weight. The laboratory variables encompassed lymphocyte count, hemoglobin, serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), uric acid, intact parathyroid hormone (iPTH), calcium, phosphate, albumin, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, triglycerides, serum iron, total iron-binding capacity (TIBC), and serum ferritin.

Statistical analysis

All continuous variables were initially assessed for normality of distribution using the Shapiro-Wilks test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed data are expressed as median with interquartile range (25th to 75th percentiles). Independent Student’s t-test and Mann-Whitney test were employed to compare groups for normally and non-normally distributed variables, respectively. Categorical data are presented as n (%), and the chi-square test was used to compare proportions between groups. Time-to-event data were graphically depicted using Kaplan–Meier curves, and log-rank tests were employed to compare survival between groups. For PNI and GNRI scores, linear mixed model (LMM) analyses, considering random intercepts and random slopes, were used to analyze the relationships between repeated measurements of PNI and GNRI over time and the longitudinal associations among them. For CONUT scores, which have a non-normal distribution, a generalized linear mixed model was used as a longitudinal sub-model. A Cox regression model served as a survival sub-model, and the joint model was constructed by integrating the longitudinal sub-model with the survival sub-model using joint Model Bayes. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to assess and compare the discriminative capacities of the three malnutrition indexes in predicting all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality. All p-values were two-sided, and values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.2.2.

Results

Baseline patient characteristics

The study included 863 patients for analysis, with baseline demographic and clinical characteristics detailed in Table 2. The patients had a median age of 60, with men comprising the majority at 63.3% (546 cases). Notably, during a median follow-up period of 37 months, there were 205 deaths, accounting for 23.8% of the participants. Of these, 121 deaths (14% of the total patients) were due to cardiovascular causes, underscoring the high cardiovascular risk prevalent in maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) patients. It is vital to recognize this risk for targeted interventions and management. Additionally, kidney transplantation was undergone by 30 patients (3.48%), and 110 patients (12.7%) were lost to follow-up. Importantly, as dialysis duration progressed, the nutritional status of MHD patients exhibited signs of stabilization, characterized by a decreasing proportion of patients in the malnutrition group (Figure S1), which indicates an improvement in nutritional status (Figure S2). This improvement in nutritional status over time is significant, as it suggests the potential for dietary interventions and management to impact patient outcomes positively. In conclusion, the study reveals that MHD patients are predominantly male, have a high risk of cardiovascular mortality, and tend to experience an improvement in nutritional status as the duration of dialysis extends.

Associations of the three nutritional screening tools with study outcomes

Regardless of the assessment tool used, with nutritional status as assessed by any of the three scores (PNI, GNRI, and CONUT), patients classified in the malnutrition group were observed to be older and displayed lower levels of crucial biochemical markers, including albumin, hemoglobin, calcium, uric acid, serum iron, TIBC, serum creatinine, and triglycerides (p < 0.05) (Table 3). The decline in these markers indicates their compromised nutritional status. It can have significant clinical implications, such as an increased vulnerability to infections and a diminished capacity to recover from illness. To better understand the relationship between the nutritional status and prognosis of patients with different dialysis vintages in the early stage of dialysis. Based on the critical values of three nutritional scoring systems (PNI 38, GNRI 98, CONUT 5), the dialysis population was divided into two groups: nutritionally normal and malnourished, at four-time points: pre-dialysis, 6th month, 12th month, and 18th month of dialysis. Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival curves were employed to compare the two groups’ risks of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality. The Kaplan–Meier survival curves were used to compare patient outcomes concerning the three scores. Intriguingly, no substantial difference was detected in the risk of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality between the normal nutrition and malnutrition groups in the pre-dialysis period (p > 0.05) (Figure S3). However, a striking pattern emerged as dialysis progressed to the 6th, 12th, and 18th months. The malnutrition group experienced significantly higher rates of both all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality compared to the normal group (p < 0.05) (Figure 2, and Figures S4 and S5). This suggests that the adverse effects of malnutrition on patient outcomes become more pronounced as patients continue undergoing dialysis, and this underlines the criticality of early nutritional interventions. The specific impacts of the three malnutrition indices on all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality, after adjustment for confounders, are elucidated in Table S1 and the multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional regression analysis, adjusted by age, sex, etiology, hemoglobin, serum creatinine, BUN, uric acid, iPTH, calcium, phosphate, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, cholesterol, triglycerides, BMI, serum iron, TIBC, serum ferritin. They first refer to a large amount of literature on the factors influencing the mortality rate of hemodialysis patients and some clinical experiences. Univariate Cox regression was used to assess the association between clinical characteristics and mortality of MHD patients. Independent predictors were assessed by multivariate Cox regression (entry criterion: p < 0.10). We mainly focused on demonstrating the influence of three nutritional scores on outcomes. Since all three scoring systems include albumin, the three scores cannot be included in the multivariable COX analysis simultaneously with other variables. In summation, patients identified as malnourished by any of the three nutritional screening tools were typically older and demonstrated substantially lower levels of essential biochemical markers. More importantly, these malnourished patients faced significantly heightened risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality as dialysis duration extended. These findings accentuate the gravity of malnutrition as a modifiable risk factor and the necessity for vigilant nutritional assessment and timely intervention in hemodialysis patients to improve survival outcomes.

Joint model analyses

A joint model was developed using the PNI score as a longitudinal indicator. In the longitudinal sub-model, the effect of observation time on PNI was statistically significant; as observation time increased, PNI gradually increased (β = 0.2674, p < 0.001). In the survival sub-model, patient age and primary etiology showed statistically significant differences (p < 0.05). Each year, an increase in age was associated with a 1.05-fold increase in the risk of mortality (HR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.04–1.07). Compared to non-diabetic patients, diabetic patients had an 11.33-fold increased risk of mortality (HR = 11.33, 95% CI: 1.79–60.13). Additionally, the association in the joint model was statistically significant, indicating that for each unit increase in PNI longitudinally, the mortality risk decreased by 4% (HR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.95–0.98). The GNRI joint model indicated that as observation time increased, GNRI also gradually increased (β = 0.3018, p < 0.001). With each unit increase in GNRI longitudinally, the mortality risk decreased by 3% (HR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.96–0.98). For the CONUT score, the longitudinal model suggested that the covariates did not predict CONUT values, making the joint effect model less significant. The area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) for the GNRI in the same time window (54th to 60th month) in the joint model was 0.893, which was more significant than that for the PNI and CONUT joint models (Table 4). The PNI joint model revealed that with each unit increase in PNI longitudinally, the risk of cardiovascular mortality decreased by 4% (HR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–0.98). The GNRI joint model showed that with each unit increase in GNRI longitudinally, the risk of cardiovascular mortality decreased by 3% (HR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.95–0.98). There was no significant change in the risk of cardiovascular mortality with the longitudinal increase of CONUT (HR = 1.04, 95% CI: 0.99–1.09). The AUC of the GNRI in the same time window (54th to 60th month) joint model was 0.880, more significant than that of the PNI joint model (Table 5). In summary, the joint model analyses revealed that increases in the PNI and GNRI scores over time were associated with reduced risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among patients undergoing hemodialysis. Notably, the GNRI joint model demonstrated a higher predictive accuracy for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, as evidenced by a larger area under the ROC curve than the PNI joint model. In contrast, the CONUT score did not show predictive value in this context. These findings suggest that the GNRI and PNI could be valuable tools for monitoring and predicting the outcomes of patients on hemodialysis.

Discussion

This study longitudinally assessed the nutritional status of patients undergoing hemodialysis at the commencement of dialysis and at the 6th, 12th, and 18th months post-dialysis initiation, employing three widely used objective nutritional scores, namely PNI, GNRI, and CONUT. Moreover, the study examined the association between nutritional status and patient prognosis. The findings revealed that PNI and GNRI scores were significant predictors of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in these patients. Notably, as indicated by the three scores at the onset of dialysis, malnutrition did not show a substantial association with the risk of mortality in patients. Furthermore, the joint model analysis suggested that the GNRI score was a more potent predictor of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality among hemodialysis patients than the PNI and CONUT scores.

Numerous studies have identified PNI, CONUT, and GNRI scores as significant predictors of mortality in hemodialysis patients. A recent historical cohort study involving 101,616 hemodialysis patients examined the relationship between PNI and mortality. It was observed that a higher PNI was associated with lower all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality, and PNI was a superior predictor of mortality compared to serum albumin levels and total lymphocyte count, particularly for patients under 65 years [27]. The GNRI score has also gained traction as a prevalent screening tool for malnutrition among dialysis patients. Several studies have validated GNRI as a notable predictor of morbidity and mortality and have found correlations with various nutrition-related markers [12,13,28–30]. For instance, a recent prospective cohort study encompassing 3,536 Japanese hemodialysis patients established that a lower GNRI was an independent risk factor for infection-related mortality and all-cause mortality. However, lower GNRI levels predicted all-cause mortality but not cardiovascular diseases [31]. In contrast, another study deduced that lower GNRI levels correlated with an elevated risk of cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality [18]. The studies mentioned above included patients with varying dialysis durations, ranging from newly initiated to over 10 years. The duration of hemodialysis, a significant confounding factor affecting nutritional status, can substantially influence the predictive accuracy of nutritional scores. According to our study, the objective nutritional scores of patients at the commencement of dialysis did not predict all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. However, patients diagnosed with malnutrition based on nutritional scores at the 6th, 12th, and 18th months of dialysis exhibited an elevated risk of mortality, and the nutritional scores were more predictive at these stages. This discrepancy may be attributed to several acute issues at the onset of hemodialysis, such as anemia, heart failure, and infections, alongside fluctuating nutritional and inflammatory indicators like serum albumin, lymphocytes, cholesterol, and body weight. With continued hemodialysis and pharmacological intervention, patients’ nutritional status and biochemical indexes stabilize, enabling the objective nutritional scores to more accurately reflect the nutritional status and predict cardiovascular and all-cause mortality. Consequently, nutritional scores based on indicators at the onset of hemodialysis may not effectively predict long-term all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality in hemodialysis patients.

Another study encompassing 311 newly initiated hemodialysis patients investigated the association between the CONUT score and the mortality risk. The findings revealed that the CONUT score was a powerful predictor of all-cause mortality for patients initiating hemodialysis [16]. Additionally, the CONUT score significantly predicted mortality from infectious diseases but did not indicate mortality from CVD [16]. These observations are largely consistent with the findings of the current study. However, the present study included a larger sample size. It assessed the CONUT score at the 6th, 12th, and 18th months post-dialysis initiation through longitudinal follow-up, which likely results in more reliable conclusions regarding the role of the CONUT score in predicting the prognosis of hemodialysis patients. Consequently, the clinical application of objective nutritional scores in predicting the prognosis of hemodialysis patients must consider that these scores may not apply to recently commenced hemodialysis patients.

This study’s patients underwent longitudinal follow-up, and nutrition-related data were collected regularly. Longitudinal and survival data are often interrelated; analyzing them separately may lead to biased results. The joint model is a statistical approach that addresses longitudinal and survival data. Leveraging the correlation between these two data types enhances the efficiency and accuracy of parameter estimation, compensates for biases due to missing or truncated longitudinal data, and offers valid inference [32–34]. We conducted regular follow-ups after the initiation of hemodialysis for the patients, collected data at various time points, and computed their nutritional scores, which were then analyzed using a joint model.

The results from the longitudinal sub-model indicated that with an increase in the duration of hemodialysis, there was a statistically significant rise in PNI and GNRI scores in patients (p < 0.05). This suggests a progressive improvement in the nutritional status of patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis over the 18 months following dialysis initiation. Moreover, the longitudinal increase in PNI and GNRI scores was associated with reduced risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Conversely, the longitudinal model suggested that covariates do not predict CONUT values, rendering the analysis of the joint effect model less significant. This could be due to the differences in calculation methods for the CONUT score compared to PNI and GNRI, which might result in CONUT changes being less apparent as the duration of dialysis extends. Thus, when aiming to evaluate patients’ nutritional changes dynamically, CONUT may not be the appropriate choice. Further, this study compared the predictive abilities of the three nutrition scores (PNI, GNRI, and CONUT) for all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality in hemodialysis patients. Among the three scores, GNRI emerged as a more robust predictor of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality compared to PNI and CONUT. This aligns with the findings of a previous study by Chen et al. which compared the efficacy of various nutritional screening tools (including MIS, GNRI, and OSND) in predicting patient prognosis among 1,025 hemodialysis patients with varying durations of hemodialysis. In that study, all three nutrition scores were significantly associated with increased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. A comparative analysis of the predictive power of these scores using C-statistics revealed that GNRI was at least as accurate as MIS in assessing patient mortality risk, and both were superior to OSND [18]. In summary, our study reinforces the utility of GNRI scores as a valuable tool for predicting all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in hemodialysis patients.

Remarkably, this study demonstrated that diabetic individuals face a 10 to 20-fold increased risk of cardiovascular mortality compared to non-diabetic patients. Diabetes, a metabolic disorder, is closely linked to dietary nutritional status. Diabetic patients often suffer from gastrointestinal autonomic nerve dysfunction, which affects appetite and leads to diminished intestinal protein absorption, elevating protein-energy wasting. The presence of chronic high proteinuria and excessive protein loss, coupled with an undue emphasis on low-phosphorus diets, contribute to hypoproteinemia. Additionally, insulin deficiency or resistance hampers muscle protein synthesis. Moreover, oxidative stress and a sustained micro-inflammatory state accelerate muscle protein breakdown, which can further aggravate malnutrition [35,36]. Consequently, it is crucial to meticulously monitor the nutritional status of diabetic hemodialysis patients and implement timely nutritional interventions, such as the inclusion of oral dietary supplements, to improve their prognosis.

Our study boasts strengths such as consistent longitudinal tracking from the onset of dialysis and assessments of nutritional status at six-month intervals, minimizing the potential confounding effects of varying hemodialysis durations. Furthermore, we employed a joint model to elucidate the relationship between patient dietary status trends and their prognosis over time, effectively capturing interdependencies between variables and diminishing biases. However, limitations persist. Being a multicenter retrospective cohort with an extended follow-up, the study was constrained to available data. It could not fully account for detailed aspects like patients’ medication histories, dietary intakes, and skinfold thickness. Additionally, our nutritional scores were not compared to the MIS scores recommended by K/DOQI, and the joint model was primarily centered on primary associations, neglecting supplementary variables. This was a retrospective study conducted mainly in Foshan and Guangzhou. The relatively small number of patients included in the research and the limited collection of baseline data, which was not adjusted for all confounders, limit the generalizability of the study. In addition, data collection was mainly focused on the pre-dialysis period and follow-up visits at months 6, 12, and 18; therefore, the overall changes in nutritional status of patients with longer years of dialysis could not be effectively analyzed due to patient loss of visits or lack of follow-up blood records.

In conclusion, this study emphasizes the importance of dynamic assessment of nutritional status in predicting all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in hemodialysis patients. Notably, GNRI and its subsequent changes post-dialysis exhibit a superior predictive ability for mortality in those patients. The higher risk observed in hemodialysis patients with diabetes calls for close monitoring of nutritional status and timely intervention. Future research should focus on prospective studies with comprehensive data and evaluation of the effects of dietary interventions.
