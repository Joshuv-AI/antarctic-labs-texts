Differences in skin cancer-related beliefs and psychological distress among adults living in Florida who do and do not report a sunburn
Authors: Nashira I Brown, Ana Paula S Rodrigues, Steven K Sutton, Marilyn Stern, Susan T Vadaparampil, Peter A Kanetsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective: To determine a parsimonious set of skin cancer-related beliefs and distress predictors for having at least one sunburn over the past year. Sun exposure and sun protection behaviors were also assessed as predictors of sunburn.Methods: In this cross-sectional analysis of 1172 adults reporting at most modest sun sensitivity, data were collected between September 2015 and September 2018. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify predictors of sunburn based on skin cancer-related beliefs, distress, sun exposure, and sun protection behaviors.Results: Higher levels of melanoma worry (aOR = 1.35, 95% CI [1.05, 1.75]), higher comparative chance of getting melanoma (aOR = 1.38, 95% CI [1.13, 1.69]), and reduced levels of response efficacy for skin cancer prevention activities (aOR = 0.76, 95% CI [0.61, 0.94]) were independent predictors of sunburn status after adjustment for covariates, as were higher levels of weekend sun exposure (aOR = 1.20, 95% CI [1.09, 1.32]) and more frequent outdoor intentional tanning (aOR = 1.50, 95% CI [1.28, 1.75)]).Conclusions: Melanoma worry, perceived risk, and response efficacy were independent predictors of sunburn status, as was sun exposure and intentional tanning. However, several key skin cancer-related beliefs and psychological distress did not predict burn status. Future work should examine social and cultural factors to guide intervention design.Keywords: Melanoma, Skin cancer, Behavioral prevention research, Epidemiology, Sunburn

Introduction

Melanoma is the major cause of deaths from skin cancer and greatly contributes to increasing health and economic burden (American Cancer Society, 2026; Zhou et al., 2025). Although most melanomas are preventable through modifiable behaviors such as wearing sun-protective clothing and using sunscreen (Hung et al., 2022), these behaviors are not well-practiced, underscoring the need for effective melanoma prevention interventions (Gordon et al., 2022).

Addressing behavioral motivations and barriers can inform prevention strategies, and the Protection Motivation Theory (PMT) has guided behavioral and observational research on skin cancer protective behaviors (Khani Jeihooni et al., 2022; Maleki et al., 2023; Prentice-Dunn et al., 2009). PMT posits that prevention behaviors are influenced by an individual's perceived disease severity, personal vulnerability (susceptibility), beliefs about the effectiveness of behaviors in reducing disease risk (response efficacy), and confidence in undertaking prevention behaviors (self-efficacy) (Rogers, 1975). These psychosocial constructs underpin behaviors and can vary across diverse population subgroups. Although other behavior theories have informed sunburn and skin cancer prevention research, PMT also has been widely used in skin cancer research (Azzarello et al., 2006; Maleki et al., 2023; McClendon and Prentice-Dunn, 2001), and it is well-aligned with research involving individualized risk information (Estebsari et al., 2023; Lacson et al., 2021). PMT also provides a practical framework for understanding and motivating skin protective behaviors by integrating threat (severity, susceptibility) and coping appraisal (response efficacy, self-efficacy).

Individuals with less sun-sensitive skin, e.g., those who report a lower tendency to sunburn or a greater ability to tan, tend to engage in more skin cancer risk behaviors (Coups et al., 2008; Kim et al., 2020), and burning can occur among tanners. In 2020, U.S. adults engaged in significantly more sun-protective behaviors compared to ten years prior, yet the prevalence of sun avoidance declined significantly over the same period (McKenzie et al., 2023). Research on other influential psychosocial factors, such as skin cancer-related beliefs and distress remains limited, and understanding these factors could inform future melanoma prevention efforts.

This secondary data analysis examined skin cancer-related beliefs and psychological distress as predictors of sunburning over the past year among individuals who reported at most modest sun sensitivity. We also assessed sun exposure and sun protection behaviors as predictors of sunburn status. We analyzed baseline data from a completed intervention study that was guided by PMT and examined whether a precision prevention approach to delivering skin cancer prevention information could promote behaviors associated with melanoma risk reduction and early detection (Lacson et al., 2021).

Methods

Study design and population

Participants were recruited from two university-based primary care clinics in Tampa, Florida, US, between September 2015 and September 2018. Patients reporting i) White, non-Hispanic identity, ii) no personal history of melanoma, iii) no skin examination over the past year, and iv) at most, modest sun sensitivity upon screening were eligible for participation. As previously reported (Lacson et al., 2021), modest sun sensitivity was defined as having naturally dark hair color at age 18 and at least three of the following: i) dark eye color, ii) good ability to tan in response to repeated summer sunlight without protection, iii) low proclivity to burn in response to first summer midday sunlight for an hour without protection, and iv) limited freckles on the face at the end of summer. These criteria excluded participants reporting sun-sensitive skin corresponding to Fitzpatrick skin type I. Beginning December 2016, eye color was removed from eligibility, and participants were thereafter required to have dark hair color along with two of the remaining three characteristics. Those eligible and interested signed informed consent and completed a self-administered baseline questionnaire. This study was approved by the University of South Florida Institutional Review Board.

Measures

Sociodemographic characteristics

The baseline questionnaire assessed biological sex, age, education level, relationship status, and family history of melanoma, keratinocyte cancers, and other (non-skin) cancers. Health literacy was measured using a single item assessing confidence in filling out medical forms by oneself (Chew et al., 2004). Health numeracy was measured using a single item assessing the ease in understanding medical statistics (Chen and Feeley, 2014).

Skin cancer-related beliefs and psychological distress

PMT constructs included perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, self-efficacy, and response efficacy (Azzarello et al., 2006). Perceived susceptibility was assessed using a comparative measure, with response options ranging from well below average to well above average regarding chance of getting melanoma compared to others like you, and an absolute measure with options of unlikely, likely, or no idea regarding getting melanoma. Perceived severity assessed the seriousness of developing melanoma, self-efficacy reflected capability of performing melanoma protection behaviors and response efficacy represented the level of importance attributed to melanoma protection behaviors in reducing the risk of melanoma and improving the chances of finding melanoma at an early stage (Rimal and Real, 2003). Scales were scored by averaging responses to seven items using a 4-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater severity or efficacy.

Worry about melanoma was assessed using an adapted version of the Lerman Cancer Worry Scale (Aspinwall et al., 2014; Lerman et al., 1994), and recent worry (rarely or never, sometimes, often, all the time) and recent concern (not at all concerned, a bit concerned, concerned, very concerned) over the past two weeks were assessed. The 15-item Impact of Event Scale assessed distress caused by intrusive and avoidant cognitions and behaviors regarding risk of melanoma using a 4-point scale (0 = not at all, 1 = rarely, 3 = sometimes, 5 = often) (Horowitz et al., 1979). Responses were summed (range: 0–75) with higher scores reflecting greater distress.

Sun exposure and sun protection behaviors

Participants reported on (i) the number of red or painful sunburns that lasted a day or more in the past year; (ii) their average number of hours (≤ ½, > ½ to 1, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6) spent outdoors between 10 am and 4 pm on weekdays and weekend days; and (iii) the frequency (never, rarely, sometimes, often, always) of five sun protection behaviors (sunscreen use, wearing a shirt with sleeves, wearing a hat, staying under an umbrella or seeking shade, and wearing sunglasses) when outside on a warm sunny day over the past year. We created an aggregate score (0–5) of number of sun-protective behaviors practiced often or always. Intentional indoor tanning was measured as the number of times an indoor tanning device was used. Intentional outdoor tanning was frequency of time spent in the sun to achieve a tanning response (never, rarely, sometimes, often, always) (Glanz et al., 2008).

Statistical analysis

Univariate analyses evaluated differences in demographics and study-related variables by sunburn status (none versus at least one in the past year). Chi-square, Mantel-Haenszel chi-square, or t-tests were used, as appropriate, to determine statistically significant differences between the two groups; variables with p ≤ 0.10 were included as covariates in models evaluating associations with sunburn status. Logistic regression evaluated the individual associations of (i) skin cancer-related beliefs and distress and (ii) sun exposure and sun protection behaviors with sunburn status. Then, multivariable logistic regression estimated independent associations (full models), followed by backward selection with a p < 0.05 threshold for model retention to determine a parsimonious set of significant predictors (reduced models). Estimated adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) are reported. Associations of measures of skin cancer-related beliefs and distress and sun exposure and sun protection behaviors with number of sunburns (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5+) were estimated by Spearman correlation coefficients. Correlation p-values were adjusted for false discovery, and corresponding q-values are reported (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995). Analyses were performed with SAS 9.4 software using PROC LOGISTIC and PROC CORR.

Results

Participants

Of the 7690 patients approached, 1704 were eligible, and 1172 (68.8%) consented and completed a baseline questionnaire (1134 were subsequently randomized). Table 1 summarizes participant characteristics. The sample was mostly partnered (61.9%), college educated (61.6%), reported a family history of (non-skin) cancers (61.9%), and had high health literacy (67.8%). Overall, 524 participants (45%) reported having at least one sunburn (M = 2.2) over the past year. Participants reporting a sunburn tended to be younger (p < 0.01), single or never married (p < 0.01), male (p = 0.07), and completed the baseline questionnaire in summer or fall (p = 0.05) compared to participants not reporting a sunburn. These four variables were included as covariates in all regression models.

Skin cancer-related beliefs and psychological distress

Four variables had positive associations with sunburn status when evaluated individually: melanoma worry (aOR = 1.57, 95% CI [1.23, 2.00]), perceived comparative chance of getting melanoma (aOR = 1.58, 95% CI [(1.34, 1.87)], perceived absolute belief of getting melanoma (aOR = 2.72, 95% CI [1.80, 4.10]), and having no idea of the likelihood of getting melanoma (aOR = 1.52, 95% CI [1.15, 2.00]) (Table 3). In the multivariable model including all prospective predictors, melanoma worry (aOR = 1.38, 95% CI [1.05, 1.82]), perceived comparative chance of getting melanoma (aOR = 1.38, 95% CI [1.13, 1.69]), perceived absolute likelihood of getting melanoma (aOR = 1.88, 95% CI [1.15, 3.07]), and response efficacy (aOR = 0.76, 95% CI [0.60, 0.96]) predicted sunburn status. Following backward stepwise procedures, melanoma worry (aOR = 1.35, 95% CI [1.05, 1.75]), comparative chance of getting melanoma (aOR = 1.53, 95% CI [1.29, 1.83]), and response efficacy (aOR = 0.76, 95% CI [0.61, 0.94]) remained predictors of sunburns (Table 3).

Sun exposure and sun protection behaviors

Higher levels of weekday (aOR = 1.15, 95% CI [1.04, 1.27]) and weekend (aOR = 1.27, 95% CI [1.17, 1.38]) sun exposure, more frequent outdoor intentional tanning (aOR = 1.58, 95% CI [1.37, 1.82]), seeking shade or using and umbrella often or always when outside (aOR = 0.76, 95% CI [0.57, 0.99]), and wearing a shirt with sleeves often or always when outside (aOR = 0.66, 95% CI [0.50, 0.86]) were individually associated with sunburn status (Table 5). Higher levels of weekend sun exposure (aOR = 1.20, 95% CI [1.09, 1.32]) and more frequent outdoor intentional tanning (aOR = 1.50, 95% CI [1.28, 1.75]) remained predictive of sunburn status in both the multivariable model including all prospective predictors and the final model following backward stepwise procedures (Table 5).

Discussion

Among individuals reporting at most modest sun sensitivity, higher melanoma-related worry, greater perceived comparative risk of developing melanoma, and lower response efficacy were independent predictors of having a sunburn in the past year. More frequent intentional tanning and higher weekend sun exposure also independently predicted sunburns. These findings suggest that individuals who are less sun sensitive engage in high-risk sun behaviors despite reporting awareness of risk and having experienced sunburn. Targeted risk-communication strategies may be needed to correct misperceptions, strengthen efficacy beliefs, and promote consistent sun protection within this population.

Limited research has compared psychosocial factors between individuals who sunburn and those who do not. Among U.S. undergraduate women, those reporting four or more lifetime sunburns perceived themselves as more susceptible to skin cancer and reported less control over intentional sunbathing and sun protection behaviors (Heckman et al., 2012). However, an Australian study found no differences in worry, perceived risk, or perceived severity of skin cancer among individuals reporting a sunburn in the last year (Venning et al., 2020). Inconsistencies across studies in beliefs and psychological distress may be attributable to variations in measurement methodologies, cultural factors, and the temporal proximity of sunburn to risk appraisal.

Increasing number of sunburns was correlated with greater comparative risk perceptions, more cancer worry, lower self-efficacy and response efficacy, more frequent intentional tanning, greater weekend sun exposure, and fewer sun protection behaviors. However, these correlations were attenuated among participants who reported at least one sunburn. Thus, skin cancer-related beliefs and psychological distress, as well as sun exposure and sun protection behaviors, likely more heavily impact receiving an initial sunburn rather than repeated burns, the latter perhaps more closely tied to repeated exposure patterns.

PMT proposes that threat appraisal (severity and perceived susceptibility) and coping appraisal (response efficacy and self-efficacy) contribute to protection motivation, with coping appraisal acting as a key determinant in whether perceived threat translates into adaptive behavior intention (Rogers, 1975). In the current study, experiencing sunburn was associated with increased perceived susceptibility and reduced response efficacy, consistent with prior research (Ch'ng and Glendon, 2014). This pattern suggests that heightened risk perceptions and affective responses are more strongly related to sunburn status than perceived capability to perform protective behaviors, highlighting the importance of addressing coping appraisal components in future interventions. Social and cultural influences such as perceived greater benefits of tanning (Heckman et al., 2012) and perceived attractiveness of a tan (Yoo and Lee, 2019) also are associated with intentions and behaviors, further suggesting the need to assess social and cultural influences to inform future skin cancer intervention.

Consistent with previous studies, participants who burned were younger (Holman et al., 2018; McKenzie et al., 2023), single or never married (Braun et al., 2021) and male (Hall et al., 2003; McKenzie et al., 2023), and the report of burning was associated with season of survey completion (Hall et al., 2003). Participants who experienced a sunburn in the past year also reported higher levels of weekday sun exposure, weekend sun exposure, and outdoor intentional tanning. The association between sun exposure and sunburn is well-documented with studies reporting a higher incidence of sunburn among those who spend more time outdoors and participate in outdoor intentional tanning (Bowers et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2020). Given the prevalence of these behaviors among individuals reporting a sunburn, future interventions should prioritize addressing these specific behaviors.

Overall, more sunburns over the past year were associated with practicing fewer sun protection behaviors. Individuals with at least one sunburn were less likely to wear a shirt with sleeves or shade themselves when outside, although these comparisons did not remain significant in the reduced model. These findings differ somewhat from those among a representative sample of U.S. adults, which included individuals self-identifying as sun-sensitive, that showed a small absolute association with sun avoidance behaviors—but none with wearing protective clothes—and lower likelihood of experiencing sunburn (Holman et al., 2018).

Previous analyses of National Health Interview Survey data from 2010, 2015 and 2020 have shown variability in the relationship between sun protection behaviors and sunburn (Holman et al., 2014; Holman et al., 2019; Holman et al., 2018; McKenzie et al., 2023). In 2010, sun avoidance behaviors were significantly associated with a lower sunburn prevalence (Holman et al., 2018). However, in 2015, sunburn was not associated with regular use of sun avoidance strategies (Holman et al., 2019) or sun protection behaviors (Holman et al., 2014). Trends from 2010 to 2020 showed significant increases in several sun-protective behaviors and sunburn avoidance. Adults in 2020 had significantly higher odds of avoiding sunburn compared to those in 2010 (McKenzie et al., 2023). These contradictory findings suggest that while sun-protective behaviors have become more common, their effectiveness in reducing sunburn risk may vary across populations and study periods.

A strength of the current study was the focus on differences in skin cancer-related beliefs and psychological distress among sun-resistant adults by burn status. However, several limitations should be noted, including potential misclassification of self-reported measures and specifically underreporting of sunburns and favorable reporting of sun-protective behaviors arising from social desirability; and the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish temporality. The focus on only non-Hispanic White individuals with at most modest sun sensitivity may introduce spectrum bias, thus limiting the generalizability of findings to more diverse populations. Lastly, changes in public awareness, sun-protection messaging, and prevention practices since the conduct of the parent study (2015–2018) may limit the applicability of these findings to current populations.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate behavioral interventions targeting sun risk behaviors, especially among adults who sunburn, are warranted, and highlight priority areas for future melanoma prevention studies. Participants who burned reported higher melanoma worry, greater perceived risk, less response efficacy, and were less likely to practice sun protection behaviors. However, burn status was not independently associated with multiple measured skin-cancer related beliefs and distress variables, including perceived disease severity and self-efficacy, suggesting the need to examine additional determinants of sun-exposure behavior.

Future work should assess social and cultural factors that influence sun-exposure among individuals with low to modest sun sensitivity, including tanning norms, beliefs linking tanned skin with attractiveness or health, and occupational or recreational exposure. These factors may be particularly relevant for young adults, athletes, and outdoor workers, who may not self-identify as at risk for melanoma despite engaging in high-UV behaviors. Interventions should prioritize correcting comparative risk misperceptions and strengthening response efficacy, with behavioral targets including avoiding peak UV hours, reducing intentional tanning, and consistent sunscreen use. Delivery strategies may include workplace-based programs for outdoor workers and social media–based messaging for young adults and athletes, with reinforcement during high-risk periods like weekends and summer months.
