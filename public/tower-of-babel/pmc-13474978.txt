Feasibility and acceptability of a rural palliative care intervention for patients with heart failure (RuPAL-HF)
Authors: Francesca X Piccolo, Eric C Anderson, Hanna Gutow, Claire M Johnson, Michelle D Nevers, Susan DeSanto-Madeya, Rebecca N Hutchinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople with heart failure (HF) have unmet palliative care (PC) needs, particularly in rural areas. Collaborative care models may meet these needs by connecting primary care teams with urban palliative care specialists. We aimed to evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of the Rural Palliative Care for Patients with Heart Failure (RuPAL-HF) intervention.MethodsWe recruited patients with HF from a rural Maine primary care clinic. A complex care team embedded in primary care was trained in PC. Specialty PC clinicians supported the team through weekly collaborative care meetings, providing coaching and case-specific guidance. Specialty palliative care clinicians also provided tele-PC when needed. Feasibility was assessed via recruitment, referral-to-completion rates, completion of advance care planning and surveys as well as collaborative care meeting attendance. Acceptability was assessed using qualitative methods through semi-structured interviews with patients, clinicians, and complex care team members.ResultsThe intervention was feasible and acceptable to patients, clinicians, and complex care team members. 31 patients were approached, 13 enrolled, and 12 (92.3%) completed all intervention components. All patients completed a serious illness conversation with the complex care team and 11 (84.6%) with the specialty PC physician. 12 patients (92.3%) completed an advance directive and 8 (61.5%) a Portable Medical Orders Form. Patients reported that goals-of-care conversations were not overly emotionally challenging, and they valued the telehealth option. All care team members attended trainings, completed surveys, and participated in ≥90% of collaborative care meetings. Complex care team members reported increased confidence and skills after trainings.ConclusionThe RuPAL-HF intervention was feasible and acceptable for patients, clinicians, and complex care team members. Telehealth appeared feasible as a mechanism to support access to specialty PC. Further research is needed to evaluate this intervention in other rural sites, care teams, and health conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02169-y.Keywords: Palliative care, Rural health, Heart failure, Collaborative care model, Complex care team, Feasibility, Acceptability

Introduction

Palliative care (PC) is beneficial for patients living with heart failure (HF); however, patients in rural areas often lack adequate access to PC services. HF is a serious illness with high symptom burden which is directly associated with decreased quality of life [1–3]. Although guidelines for HF management recommend annual goals-of-care conversations pertaining to prognosis and advance care planning, many patients report not having these discussions [4, 5]. Prior studies have demonstrated that PC may improve prognostic understanding [6, 7], end-of-life care [8, 9], and quality of life [10–13]. However, given the shortage of specialty PC providers in rural areas, many patients living in rural areas with HF cannot access specialty PC [14]. Additionally, cultural factors of rural life, such as a strong value on independence and a distrust of external or urban healthcare may reduce acceptance of seeing specialty PC [9, 15–18]. Emerging modalities for delivering care, such as nurse-led collaborative care interventions and telehealth, may create opportunities to expand access to PC.

Collaborative care models rely on specially trained primary care nurses to deliver specialty care to patients [12, 13, 19–21]. Collaborative care models have been successfully used to improve outcomes for rural patients living with depression [22–24], including an intervention that showed improved depression for HF patients [21]. One collaborative PC intervention found no improvement in HF-specific outcomes [1]. This suggests it may be necessary to include direct specialty PC, possibly using telehealth, to address patients’ needs. There is increasing evidence that telehealth is an effective way to deliver specialty PC [25–27]. A recent randomized controlled trial demonstrated equivalent outcomes between patients who had in-person PC compared to those who had tele-PC [28]. Based on these findings, we incorporated the option for tele-PC to allow patients in rural areas to have consults with specialty PC clinicians in urban centers when needed.

Our study evaluated the feasibility and acceptability of a collaborative care model in a rural primary care clinic: Rural Palliative Care for Patients with Heart Failure (RuPAL-HF). The intervention was designed using stakeholder feedback [29] and utilized an interprofessional “complex care” team embedded in the primary care clinic to deliver primary PC. The team included a bachelor’s level social worker, health coaches, and registered nurse. The intervention also incorporated telehealth visits with specialty PC as needed. The aim of this study was to evaluate the acceptability and feasibility of the RuPAL-HF intervention with the long-term goal of expanding this intervention to other rural locations.

Methods

Patient participants

Patient participants were adults diagnosed with HF. Patients were eligible if they (1) had a diagnosis of HF and (2) had 1 or more hospitalizations and/or emergency room visits for HF in the past year. Severity of heart failure was classified using American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association stages. Stage A includes asymptomatic patients with risk factors for heart failure. Stage B includes asymptomatic patients with one of the following: evidence of structural heart disease, elevated filling pressures, or elevated cardiac biomarkers plus risk factors. Stage C includes patients with structural heart disease and current or prior symptoms. Stage D includes patients with severe symptoms of HF that limit daily life and result in recurrent hospitalizations [30]. The study was conducted at a rural healthcare facility in Norway, Maine, and patients lived in the surrounding area.

Recruitment procedures

Patient referrals came from (1) the patient’s cardiologist, primary care physician (PCP), or the complex care team, or (2) through targeted recruitment. In the first method, the patient’s cardiologist, PCP, or complex care team member sent the patient’s name to the research team who then confirmed whether the patient was eligible through chart review. In the second method designed to maximize recruitment, a research coordinator reviewed all patients with upcoming appointments with primary care or cardiology to determine eligibility. If eligible, the research coordinator contacted the cardiologist and/or PCP to obtain permission to approach the patient about participating. If the PCP and/or cardiologist agreed, the patient was approached and given more information about the study. Encrypted, secure messaging integrated into EHR was utilized between the research team and cardiologist and/or PCP for patient data privacy.

Informed consent

After learning about the study, patients provided informed written consent either in-person or remotely. The project adhered to the declaration of Helsinski and was approved by the MaineHealth Institute for Research Institutional Review Board.

Intervention design

Community-based participatory methods

This project utilized community-based participatory methods. We formed an advisory board composed of a patient representative (a patient living in the community with HF), physicians (HF cardiologist and internal medicine PCP), primary care leadership, leadership of complex care teams across the health system, as well as practice leadership of the rural clinic. This board met quarterly with the research team to share information and solicit feedback about study design, recruitment, and any other challenges.

Complex care team

The complex care team is a care management team comprised of social workers, health coaches, and nurses who see patients with a high burden of illness. The team connects patients with community resources for health barriers related to social determinants of health. They also provide disease and medication education.

Training sessions

Training for the complex care team occurred in three sessions focused on: “Introducing PC”, “HF Education”, and “Serious Illness Conversation”. These trainings were delivered by PC and HF expert clinicians. We used role-playing with simulated patients for training in introducing PC and having a serious illness conversation. We also developed and utilized a manual that was adapted from a prior primary PC nursing-delivered intervention [31].

Patient intervention

The complex care team met with patients on a weekly or bi-weekly basis for approximately three months, or until the patient had their PC needs met. The complex care team explored and addressed psychosocial, spiritual, and symptom management needs. The team used a serious illness conversations guide to explore the patient’s values and preferences [32, 33].When the team felt it would be helpful, the complex care team would facilitate a telehealth visit with PC; the patient would be located either in their home if they had broadband or in the PCP office.

Collaborative care meetings

Weekly collaborative care team meetings were attended by specialty PC clinicians (a PC nurse, social worker, chaplain, and physician) and the complex care team. During these meetings the team discussed patients who were seen during the prior week by the complex care team. The team reviewed identified symptoms as well as any goals-of-care conversations. The specialty PC team provided coaching and advice on managing symptoms, helping the patient and/or family understand prognosis and clarifying goals of care. Recommendations from the PC team were communicated with the primary care team through notes in the electronic medical record as well as direct connection with primary care provider. During collaborative care meetings, the team would determine if the patient needed to meet directly with a PC clinician over telehealth (Fig. 1: Model of Intervention.)

Evaluation

We evaluated the feasibility and acceptability of RuPAL-HF intervention from March 2023 to June 2024. Feasibility was assessed via recruitment and referral-to-completion rates, documented serious illness conversations, completion of advance care planning documents (Advance Directive, Portable Medical Orders Form), completion of study related surveys, and number of collaborative care meetings and attendance. Acceptability was assessed via qualitative semi-structured interviews with patients, complex care team members, and clinicians after the completion of the study. Patients were asked about their experience with the intervention, suggested improvements, whether the conversations were overly emotionally difficult, and their telehealth experience. The complex care team members were asked about their experience with the intervention, conversations, and document completion. Primary care clinicians were asked about their perspectives on the collaborative care model (see supplemental materials for interview guides).

Patient outcomes were assessed quantitatively via pre- and post-surveys. Surveys asked about patients’ healthcare understanding (such as perceptions of their healthcare team [32] and purpose of their medical care [34], emotional symptoms (such as depression and anxiety scores and quality of life) [35], and physical symptoms [36]. Post-intervention surveys and interviews were conducted over the phone 1–2 weeks after each patient completed the intervention. Patient health status and healthcare utilization (such as intensive care unit [ICU], hospitalization, and ambulatory admissions) were assessed via chart review of electronic health records.

Data analysis

Quantitative

R version 4.2.1 was used for quantitative analysis. Simple descriptive statistics were performed for quantitative variables as the study sample size was too small for hypothesis testing [37]. To assess rurality for this study, we utilized the Rural-Urban Commuting Area Codes (RUCA) from the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) [38]. The RUCA codes were used to create a four-category classification system using the New England Rural Health Roundtable system [39]. R code for analysis is available online and data is available by reasonable request to the corresponding author.

Qualitative

MAXQDA 24.7.0: Semi-structured interviews were transcribed via Rev.com, a platform for recording and transcribing audio. Three researchers (FXP, ECA, CMJ) coded the patient, clinician, and collaborative care team interviews in MAXQDA 24.7.0. To avoid bias, each researcher reviewed and coded the interviews independently and later met together to discuss discrepancies which were resolved by consensus after discussion [40–42]. Quotations below were edited for readability, where applicable.

Results

Patient characteristics

Thirteen participants enrolled, and the majority identified as female (n = 10, 76.9%) and Caucasian (n = 12, 92.3%) (Table 1: Patient demographics). 10 patients were 70 years of age or older (77%). In terms of comorbidities, 11 (84.6%) patients had diabetes, 10 (76.9%) had kidney disease, and 12 (92.3%) had hyperlipidemia. Of the four stages of HF, nine (69%) participants were in stage C, while four (31%) participants were in stage D [42]. When asked about their self-assessed quality of life (physical, emotional, social, spiritual, and financial) over the past two days, on a scale of 1–10 (1 = Very Bad, 10 = Excellent), patients reported an average of 6.6. All patients lived in rural areas, three (23.1%) lived in large rural areas while 10 (76.9%) lived in small and isolated rural areas.

Feasibility

Two hundred fifty-six patients were screened for eligibility for this intervention and 58 met eligibility criteria (Fig. 2: Consort Diagram). 27 patients were not contacted by the research team because the PCP/cardiologist did not recommend contacting the patient (n = 14) or the patient declined the opportunity (n = 13) to learn more about the study. The remaining 31 patients were approached by the research team to learn about the study. Of the 31 that were approached, four were identified by their PCP/cardiologist, eight by the complex care team, and 19 by the research team. Of the 13 patients who enrolled, 12 (92.3%) completed all study activities, while one participant completed the intervention activities but died prior to completing the final survey.

All (100%) complex care team members attended the three trainings for the intervention. There was a transition in the nurse position for the team, where one member’s role was filled in by another nurse. Collaborative care meetings typically lasted one hour and occurred weekly from March 13, 2023 to August 5, 2024. The complex care nurse attended 52 (96%) meetings, one health coach attended 54 (100%) meetings, the second health coach attended 51 (94%) meetings, and the social worker attended 49 (90%) meetings.

Acceptability

Patient acceptability

Eleven participated in qualitative interviews (11/13, 85%), which revealed patients felt the goals-of-care conversations could be emotionally difficult but were not overly challenging. Some patients noted that it was difficult to talk about death and future uncertainty around this topic. At the same time, most patients felt that these conversations were valuable. One patient stated: “I think [the intervention] is going to be very helpful to other people, … who don’t know what to expect and who are frightened and left alone at home. It’s a very hard thing to be left out in the woods by yourself.” (Table 2: Qualitative Themes Table, Patient 4).

Patient Tele-PC acceptability

Of the 13 patients, 9 patients met with a palliative care clinician over telehealth (69%). These patients reported that the use of telehealth was a positive component of the intervention and the option to participate virtually made it easier. For example, one patient shared: “I think the computer’s easier [than in person] because I don’t have the means to get places all the time. So it’s easier when I could just sit there, I have my phone on and I could get the same treatment as I would if I had to travel some.” (Table 2: Qualitative Themes Table, Patient 10). On the other hand, some patients identified barriers to utilizing telehealth such as technology literacy and broadband access.

Complex care team acceptability

All four of the participating complex care team members (100%) reported that the collaborative care model and trainings were acceptable. Post-training survey data suggest the trainings increased complex care team confidence and skills essential in PC conversations (Fig. 5: Pre, Mid, and Post Intervention Training Surveys). The most notable increases in confidence were found in “introducing PC”, “responding to patient questions about PC”, “eliciting patient goals and values”, “responding to patient hesitations”, “responding to patient emotions”, and “discussing prognosis”. When asked if the complex care team member would recommend the trainings to others, on a scale of 1–5 (1 = No, 5 = Absolutely), team members reported an average of 4.9. When asked if the training improved their communication skills from each of the sessions, team members reported an average of 4.9 (on the same scale). “Role-playing” and “communication skills” were cited by the complex care team as the trainings’ strengths, while “further role-playing situations” and “more trainings” were noted commonly for areas for improvement.

Complex care team members reported finding patient discussions daunting at the beginning of the intervention but reported growing confidence throughout the intervention. One complex care team member said: “Now I feel confident to handle the unknowns as they come at me, whereas initially the thought of it was really scary” (Table 2, Qualitative Themes Table, Complex Care Team Member 1). The PC chaplain particularly noted growth in the complex care team: “I liked watching the providers who were involved. I believe many of them were nurses or social workers. I enjoyed watching them grow in their confidence as they reached out to people and had serious illness conversations with them.” (Table 2: Qualitative Themes Table, PC Chaplain).

Participating clinicians who were interviewed (n = 5) found the collaborative care meetings valuable. One clinician reported: “I liked how collaborative it was, how much everybody participated, shared their information, had input from other resources, be it both the [rural] crew as well as the [urban] crew. I thought that was a really good use of everyone’s abilities and knowledge to help people who are a little bit more new to PC get going” (Table 2, Qualitative Themes Table, Clinician 5).

Exploratory patient outcomes

Three of 13 (23.1%) patients entered hospice, with a length of stay of approximately 7–50 days from enrollment to death, and nine of 12 (75%) patients decreased their hospital and ambulatory visits 6 months after enrollment compared to 6 months before enrollment (Fig. 3B: Patient Admissions and Healthcare Utilization). All (100%) patients reported that they felt “somewhat” to “completely” heard and understood by their providers and complex care team, and 91% reported their providers understood what was important to them in their life (Fig. 3C: Perceptions of Healthcare Team). When asked about disease understanding prior to the intervention, two (15.3%) patients understood that treatment would not cure their HF; post-intervention, when asked the same question, this number increased to five (38.5%) patients, suggesting a possible increase in prognostic understanding (Fig. 3D: Purpose of Medical Care).

Patient-reported feelings of depression and worry by their family and friends appear to worsen pre intervention to post; however, worry about their illness appeared fairly similar (Fig. 4A: Emotional Concerns). Patients reported that they were more bothered by emotional problems and felt less at peace pre to post (Fig. 4B: Emotional Uncertainty). On average, nausea decreased slightly for patients, while shortness of breath increased (Fig. 4C: Patient Symptoms).

Discussion

Overall, we found that the RuPAL-HF collaborative care PC intervention was feasible and acceptable in the rural community primary care practice studied. This is supported by the following: (1) patients, primary care clinicians, and the complex care team found the intervention to be a positive experience; (2) we were able to effectively train complex care team members, and their self-rated skills improved after training and weekly coaching; (3) the novel telehealth component was effectively integrated into the intervention; and (4) all patients had a documented serious illness conversations and many patients completed advance care planning documentation.

Patient, primary care clinicians, and complex care team perspectives

The intervention was positively received and feasible for patients, as demonstrated by patients consenting and completing the study components. The intervention was also acceptable to patients as reflected in the qualitative interviews which found patients valued having the collaborative care team involved in their care. Previous research shows that patients may feel anxiety in having end-of-life care discussions with their care team [44]. However, our study suggests that goals-of-care conversations, though difficult, were not overly emotionally unpleasant and patients found them beneficial.

Clinicians and the complex care team reported in interviews that the intervention was acceptable as well, particularly access to specialty PC for coaching and direct patient consultations. The complex care team shared that they would recommend the trainings to others and felt that they were adequate in preparation for patient conversations.

Training and collaborative care meetings

We found it was feasible to train a complex care team embedded within a primary care practice and have them attend weekly collaborative care meetings for the duration of the intervention. The didactic training for complex care team members focused on HF management and prognosis; PC language and best practices; and serious illness conversations to effectively deliver PC for patients with HF [45]. After training in our study, complex care team members had increased confidence in: introducing PC; responding to emotional challenges; and completing advance care documentation with patients. The ongoing weekly case-specific coaching and guidance for complex care team members was also positively received. Notably, we found that self-rated skills improved after the training and confidence further increased throughout the intervention. This is consistent with prior studies indicating an importance of multiple trainings as well as ongoing coaching [46–48]. Attendance at collaborative care meetings was high (90–100%) and demonstrates that meetings are feasible for members to attend, despite busy schedules. The success of our trainings and high attendance contrasts with concerns others have raised regarding the time commitment required for multi-session training [49].

Telehealth

The incorporation of tele-PC is one component that sets RuPAL-HF apart from other collaborative PC interventions, such as the CASA trial [21]. While the CASA trial found no significant improvements in the primary outcome of patient-reported HF status, the integration of telehealth for specialty PC visits when needed may be a missing link to improve patient outcomes. Tele-PC has become increasingly utilized and seems to improve access to care and be as effective as in-person visits [28]. However, tele-PC is still relatively new and little research has incorporated it into collaborative care models to increase access to PC specialist when needed. Patients appreciated having access to specialty PC without needing to travel. Notably, telehealth introduces new barriers as well, such as access to broadband internet in rural areas [50]. We addressed this barrier by having patients connect to the tele-PC visit from their local primary care offices. This allowed them to connect using the clinic’s broadband and the complex care team could assist with using the computer or tablet. Interestingly, some participants in our study reported that tele-PC visits eased anxiety of face-to-face conversations suggesting tele-PC might be better than in-person for some patients. In addition to providing direct patient care through tele-PC, the inclusion of telehealth in our intervention also allowed the PC specialist to model skills that the complex care team could then use in future encounters.

Document completion

We observed high completion of patient advance care documents: 92.3% completion rate of advance directives, 100% completion of serious illness conversations with complex care team, 84.6% completion of serious illness conversations with PC physician, and 61.5% completion of Portable Medical Orders Form. This high completion rate is consistent with a randomized controlled trial which found advance care planning and documentation of end-of-life care goals significantly increased when a health worker assisted cancer patients [51]. Additionally, our results suggest that ACP document completion is possible even in remote areas where workforce numbers are significantly reduced as our team included health coaches with minimal education who assisted patients. POLST forms were completed virtually by the PC physician with assistance from the health coach or primary care nurse facilitating the visit.

Exploratory patient outcomes

While the goal of this project was not to test whether the RuPAL-HF intervention improved patient outcomes, some interesting preliminary trends emerged. After the intervention, we found that there were possible improvements in patients understanding their HF diagnosis and prognosis. This is consistent with other preliminary findings that patients who receive social worker-aided PC had improved prognostic understanding that aligned with their physician’s assessments [52]. There was a trend that patient overall depression, emotional problems, and feelings of peace worsened over time in the present study, which could be consistent with patients’ declining health. However, patients were not more anxious or worried about their illness or treatment, which may suggest that the intervention helps patients cope with their serious and progressive illness.

Limitations

This study had a small sample size (n = 13), limiting our ability to conduct hypothesis testing on patient outcomes. As such, findings from our study should be reviewed as promising but would need to be further studied in a larger group of patients to fully evaluate impact of the intervention. The patient sample lacked ethnic and racial diversity which limits generalizability to other populations. Additionally, this intervention took place at a single rural primary care site where there were high pre-existing understanding and enthusiasm for PC, which may not be true for all rural locations. In order to be recruited, patients had to be actively engaged with their primary care team and the PCP had to provide permission prior to contacting the patient, which may introduce a selection bias.

Future directions

A large randomized controlled trial is necessary to investigate whether the RuPAL-HF collaborative care intervention improves patient outcomes, such as quality of life. Given the mixed findings of other collaborative PC interventions [21], it will be important to test and understand what elements of collaborative PC improve patient outcomes and which do not. Additionally, this intervention was conducted within a rural setting, suggesting that it may be applicable to similar geographic locations; however, application in remote rural settings is unclear. Further research is warranted to test this intervention across other rural locations, care teams, and health conditions.

Conclusions

This study found that a collaborative PC intervention (RuPAL-HF) integrated in a rural primary care practice was feasible and acceptable to clinicians, complex care team members, and patients with HF. This intervention used education trainings, weekly collaborative care meetings, and coaching from specialty PC to support complex care team members in delivering PC. Future work is needed to test whether this intervention improves health outcomes for patients with HF in rural areas.
