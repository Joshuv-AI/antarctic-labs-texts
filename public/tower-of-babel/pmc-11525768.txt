Letter to the Editor. Coil embolization complications linked to aneurysmal inflammation
Authors: Alexander Sirakov, Svetozar Matanov, Georgi Vladev, Stanimir Sirakov, Kristina Sirakova
Published: 2024

TO THE EDITOR: It is always a pleasure to read well-written publications, especially when they describe a relatively rare but fearsome complication seen in our daily practice. The article by Okuyama et al.1 describes the case of a young patient who experienced significant visual dysfunction after coil embolization of a previously clipped and recurrent paraclinoid aneurysm (Okuyama T, Kurisu K, Osanai T, et al. Visual dysfunction and neurodegeneration caused by severe inflammatory optic neuropathy after coil embolization of a paraclinoid aneurysm: illustrative case. J Neurosurg Case Lessons. 2024;7[20]:CASE24143).

We would like to share our thoughts based on similar experiences that have allowed us to improve our medical protocols. We agree with the authors that paraclinoid aneurysms pose the danger of posttreatment neurological complications; however, this can be said of every intradural aneurysm. Another crucial point to mention is that such occurrences are more frequent in larger aneurysms than the ones discussed by the authors.1 Aneurysm size has been linked to posttreatment focal and perifocal inflammation and is associated with the most fearsome complication: delayed rupture.2, 3

We disagree with the authors that this clinical exacerbation and its mechanisms are well known. Frankly, little is known about the specific conditions that may trigger the uncontrollable inflammation of the aneurysm.4, 5 This inflammation can overwhelm the biological defense mechanisms of the vessel wall and the applied steroidal therapy, as demonstrated in the study by Okuyama et al.6

We must also turn our attention to the very nature of the aneurysm. Is it possible that the recurrence of the clipped aneurysm created a predisposed setting for the described complication to occur? It has been suggested that the mechanism of aneurysm recurrence involves the impairment of endothelial and smooth muscle cell proliferation and the presence of inflammatory cell infiltration within the growing aneurysm wall.7 Considering this and the fact that the infiltration of inflammatory cells is the basic and expected reaction after coil implantation therapy, could it be a mishap or something that can be avoided with endoluminal stent therapy?

Once the complication occurs, the question that matters is how one should manage it. Steroids have been used ever since the first similar cases were documented. We would like to clarify that the steroidal medications and the applied dosages used by Okuyama et al. are indeed on point.1 Based on our experience, we prefer more potent corticosteroids like dexamethasone.8 This drug reduces inflammation, subdues the immune system, and is significantly more potent than prednisolone. Like the authors, whenever we anticipate similar complications, steroidal therapy is initiated 3 days prior. We mandate the therapy for 2 weeks posttreatment, with a gradual dose decrease by 2 mg weekly.

Although corticosteroids are essential in the modulation of inflammation, another biological axis has been left untouched in the presented case. We consider nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs a key component to avoid similar complications. Reduction of inflammatory responses is expected once cyclooxygenase (COX)-1 and COX-2 inhibition has been obtained. In our protocol, we strictly adhere to a diclofenac dose of 150 mg/day, as it has a wider literature base attesting to its efficacy and tolerability, as well as being placid with regard to the modulation of thrombogenicity. Due to its high lipid solubility, diclofenac can cross the blood-brain barrier with an action directed principally toward COX-2.9

Needless to say, this commentary could potentially serve as an upgrade to the current authors’ protocol or a source for further discussion. We highly advocate the use of our protocol with extreme caution because of the fact that the risk/benefit ratio of this extensive medical therapy is controversial. It may prevent rupture but increases the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding and other organ complications. Complementary proton-pump inhibitors should also be considered in this context.
