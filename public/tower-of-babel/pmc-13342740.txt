Transforming growth factor alpha polymorphism and cleft lip and palate in North Indian Population
Authors: Juhi Yadav, Pradeep Raghav, Raj K. Mishra, Divya N. Upadhyaya, Veerendra Prasad, Chandra P. Chaturvedi, Kritanjali Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:One of the most common congenital malformations, with widespread racial and regional variation, is an orofacial cleft. Cleft lip and palate (CLP) are a congenital anomaly that results in incomplete or non-fusion of the lip and/or palate during the embryonic development of the child. Many studies support that the occurrence of CLP is attributed to a combination of factors of environment and genes. Earlier studies had studied many candidate genes for CLP in different populations but not all are significant to every population. Transforming growth factor alpha (TGFA) genes had been proposed as the candidate gene associated with CLP due to its involvement role in palatal process fusion during palate formation.Aim and Objectives:In this study, we studied the TGFA gene and its association with any type of orofacial cleft in the North Indian Population.Methods:An observational case-control study was conducted on 50 patients presenting with oral clefts in hospitals, who were categorized as Cases and 50 controls were selected that were free from any defect. Controls were coming to the hospital for some other type of treatment. Cases were evaluated for various phenotypes of clefts. Blood samples of each case and control were collected to extract genomic deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) and to further analyze the TGFA polymorphism in those cases and controls with Polymerase chain Reaction-Restriction fragment length polymorphism (PCR-RFLP). SPSS 23.0 version was employed for statistical analysis. The genotypic frequencies of TGFA gene variants were compared between cases and controls using appropriate statistics.Results:The results for gene polymorphism showed a significant association between TGFA gene polymorphisms and CLP in the North Indian population.Conclusion:If we can identify any trait beforehand that is somehow linked with cleft lip and palate, we can help parents and children. This study provides evidence for an association between TGFA gene polymorphism and an increased risk of CLP in non-syndromic patients in the North Indian population. We need more future research to understand the underlying mechanisms that link TGFA gene variants to CLP and the gene-environment interactions.

INTRODUCTION

Cleft lip (CL) and cleft palate (CP) remain the most common morphological defect that affects the Oro-facial structures of humans and remains a topic of considerable interest in etiology and pathogenesis. Due to the huge burden on mankind in terms of function and esthetical implications that have led to extensive research in terms of etiology and intervention strategies.[1]

Literature shows the incidence rate of oral clefts to be highest in Asians, followed by Caucasians, and lowest in Africans.[2] The etiopathogenesis concept of clefts is a heterogeneous category of craniofacial defects due to the interplay of both environmental and genetic factors.[3] Cleft lip and palate (CLP) is one of the most common congenital anomalies that affects the orofacial region.[4] It is characterized by the incomplete fusion or non-fusion of the lip and/or palate during embryonic development, leading to functional, aesthetic, and psychosocial challenges for affected individuals. CLP is a complex disorder influenced by a combination of genetic and environmental factors.[5]

Among the many environmental variables investigated in this are: maternal usage of tobacco, alcohol or substance usage, folic acid deficiency in the diet, maternal obesity and gestational diabetes, exposure to teratogenic drugs, and environmental contaminants like smoke due to the cooking or passive smoking.

Forecasting CLP might be considered a significant step forward toward prevention and facilitates early access to genetic services. The geneticist can determine the extent, to which genetic factors are involved in the etiology of CL and CP in each problem based on good family history and provide the parents with a prognosis for the recurrence of the condition in future siblings, as well as the potential for affected offspring arising from already affected children through genetic counseling.

Many candidate genes have been studied to date in various populations but not all genes are significant in all populations. Genes such as MTHFR, transforming Growth Factor Alpha (TGFA), IRF6, TGFB are some that have gained considerable attention due to their roles in folate metabolism, deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) synthesis, and methylation processes and palatal epithelium fusion, regulation delivery of E Catherin to the plasma membrane, regulation of cell cycle by blocking progress through G1 phase, respectively.

Several authors tried to investigate the potential association between TGFA gene polymorphisms and the risk of CLP, but the findings have been inconsistent, likely due to the population-specific genetic variations.[67] The North Indian population, with its unique genetic background and distinct cultural and environmental factors, remains understudied in the context of TGFA gene variants and their association with CLP.

TGFA gene is located at chromosome 2 at a location of 2P13 and contains six exons and spans 80 Kb of genomic DNA. TGFA is a gene that gives instructions for making a protein that is involved in cell growth and development. TGFA gene is a part of the epidermal growth factor family and the protein product is a signaling molecule that plays a role in various cellular processes that include cell proliferation, differentiation, and migration of cells.[8] TGFA is a gene that encodes a protein that is, involved in cell growth and differentiation and any alteration in that gene could lead to the occurrence of CLP. In this context of CLP development, the TGFA gene has been studied because of its potential involvement in facial development. CLP are congenital conditions where there is an incomplete fusion of the upper lip and or the roof of the palate during early fetal development.

For genetic association of the TGFA gene in non-syndromic CLP many studies have been done till now. Few studies showed significant association, but others did not show the same results.

Understanding the genetic factors involved in CLP is essential for many reasons. Importantly, it can contribute to our knowledge of the developmental processes and molecular mechanisms that can explain CLP pathogenesis. Then, it can help in identifying individuals, who are genetically susceptible to CLP according to family history, enabling targeted preventive measures and genetic counseling before conceiving. Furthermore, such knowledge can have implications for personalized treatment approaches, as all parents must know beforehand what to expect and how to manage the condition preoperatively and postoperatively. The findings from this study may also have broader implications for genetic research on CLP and related craniofacial anomalies. They can contribute to the growing body of evidence on the genetic basis of CLP and pave the way for future investigations into the interactions between the TGFA gene variants and other genetic or environmental factors that may modulate CLP risk.

Therefore, the present study aims to examine the association between the TGFA gene polymorphism in non-syndromic CLP patients from the North Indian population. By focusing on this specific population, we can gain insights into the genetic aspects of the CLP and potentially identify markers that may contribute to risk assessment, prevention, and treatment strategies.

METHODOLOGY

Design and settings: A case-control observational hospital-based study was conducted to assess the association of the TGFA gene polymorphism and CLP. The blood sample collection for the study was conducted for a duration of 5 months from January 2022 to May 2022. The study was granted ethical consent from the Ethical clearance was obtained reference number - 110 ECM 11A/P10, Dated- 22/12/21. Random sampling was performed to collect samples. Consent of all participants was obtained from both the cases and the controls, but in the case of the child patient, assent forms were filled by the guardian/parent or caretaker, who accompanied the child. Each participant’s information confidentiality was maintained during the study.

Screening of cases and controls

Cases: Patients with CLP attending the Outdoor patient department were recruited by two plastic surgeons to rule out any syndromic case of CLP and only non-syndromic subjects made the case group.

Controls: People without a history of CLP or any other craniofacial deformities served as controls.

Inclusion Criteria: All non-syndromic CLP patients formed the case group and their diagnosis was made after thorough clinical evaluations that included physical examination and review of records by two plastic surgeons.

Exclusion criteria: Individuals with Syndromic forms of CLP (e.g., eye, brain, limb anomalies, and cardiac defects) genetic diseases, or syndromic instances were excluded.

Sample size calculation

The sample size of the present study was based on effect size (i.e., mean difference). Expecting at least a mean difference of 0.50 in the TGF-α gene with non-syndromic CL with or without CP in the North Indian population, considering 5% margin of error (i.e., Type I error), 95% power (i.e., type II error) and 1:1 ratio (N1/N2), we have to recruit minimum 100 subjects for two groups or 50 for one group, evaluated using G*Power 3.1.9.4 software.

Sociodemographic variables

A written Schedule was filled out by two experts for each case and control eliciting demographic details of their parents such as cleft details, family income, marital status (consanguineous/nonconsanguineous), education, occupation, residence, religion, cooking medium, history of illness during pregnancy, history of smoking/alcohol during pregnancy. The findings of sociodemographic variables were that the education status of most of the parents was below 8th standard and most of them were economically poor as compared to the control group. Marriages were nonconsanguineous in most of the CLP cases parents, as well as in the control group. The relevant information was that in most of the CLP cases, parents used Chula (smoke) as a cooking medium and were less educated and occupation was low grade whereas the control group were well off and used cooking gas and had better occupations.

Elaborate details on clefts were recorded. Clefts were categorized separately as CL, CP, a combination of both CLP, and Clefts of the soft palate. The location of these clefts was also recorded based on unilateral, bilateral, or midline existence. The severity and type of cleft were recorded based on Kernahan’s classification.

Blood sample collection: Blood collection was conducted under strict aseptic conditions. About 3 ml of venous blood was collected from the median cubital vein through venepuncture via a disposable syringe and then was transferred to a vacutainer tube that contained K3 ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid. All the Collected blood samples were immediately stored at 4 degrees centigrade to avoid any hemolysis of RBCs for a maximum of 40 days so we could get a good quantity of DNA.

Genomic DNA extraction from blood: We used Qiagen kits, to extract genomic DNA from collected blood samples as per the guidelines of the company. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) analysis were used to check if any polymorphism existed in the region that is studied of the gene. Quality control measures were implemented to ensure accurate and reliable genotyping results. The ratio of sample absorbance at 260 nm and 280 nm was used to assess the purity of DNA (normal value, ≈1.8). A lower 260:280 ratio may indicate the presence of protein, phenol, and other contaminants (absorbance at 280 nm). To check if nucleic acid was free from salts, we used a ratio of sample absorbance at 260 nm and 230 nm (normal range, 1.8–2.2). A lower 260:230 ratio may indicate the presence of salts (absorbance at 230 nm).

Primers: They are essential components for polymerase chain reaction to get a good quantity of DNA of a particular region to study the Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) if it existed in that region of the gene. Primer Design: Specific primers are designed to target the regions of interest within the TGFA gene that contain the desired SNPs. The primers are designed to specifically bind to the complementary sequences flanking the SNP sites. BamH1 was the restriction enzyme used for digestion at the SNP site. Evaluation of outcome: In the context of this study, “heterozygous,” “wild-type,” and “Homozygous” are used to describe different genetic variants or genotypes.

For the present study, only one variant of the TGFA gene was studied and the TGFA gene is located on 2P13 on the second chromosome and consists of six exons. In this study, we have studied SNP located at exon 6.

Heterozygous: Heterozygous refers to a genetic condition where an individual carries two different alleles (variants) of a particular gene at a specific locus. In the TGFA gene, three bands will be seen in the heterozygous genotype.

Wild-type: The term “wild-type” refers to the most common or prevalent form of a gene or allele in a population. In the case of the TGFA gene, digestion with BamH1 and yielding two bands confirms the absence of SNP.

Homozygous mutant: The term “mutant” refers to a genetic variant that deviates from the wild-type allele. Nondigestion with BamH1 in the TGFA gene indicates the presence of SNP.

The genotyping analysis of the TGFA gene in the study of CLP in our study involved determining the presence or absence of specific genotypes, including heterozygous, wild-type, and homozygous genotypes. By comparing the frequencies of these genotypes between the CLP cases and control individuals, we tried to evaluate the potential association between specific TGFA genotypes and the risk of CLP in the studied population.

Statistical analysis

The current study investigated the association between the TGFA gene and the occurrence of CLP in non-syndromic patients in the North Indian population. Genotyping analyses were performed to assess the distribution of TGFA gene polymorphisms in both CLP cases and control individuals. TGFA genotypes (H, M, and W) were summarized in number (n) and percentage (%) and compared by the Chi-square (χ2) test. The genotypes were in Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE). A two-tailed (α =2) P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The analysis was performed on SPSS software (IBM SPSS Statistics 21).

DISCUSSION

The present study investigated the association between the TGFA gene and the occurrence of cleft lip and palate in non-syndromic patients within the North Indian population. The findings provide valuable insights into the genetic factors that TGFA can be a genetic marker for the North Indian Population and can contribute to the craniofacial anomaly this finding can have important implications for the understanding, its etiology, and potential preventive strategies.

The TGFA gene is located at the 2P13 and consists of six exons that span 80 KB of genomic DNA. The most common gene polymorphisms are Rsa1, Taq1 in intron 5, and BamH1 in exon 6 that have been studied. Dixon MJ et al.[9] saw that the TGFA gene is involved in inducing mitogenic response by binding to and simultaneously stimulating the tyrosine kinase activity of EGF receptors. At the time of craniofacial development, the TGFA gene is expressed at the medial edge epithelium of the fusing palatal shelves.[1011]

During palatal cultures, the TGFA gene increases the extracellular matrix and movement of mesenchymal cells and gives strength to the fused palate.[1213] Machida J et al.[14] supported the genetic support of the TGFA gene mutations and said five variants in the reserved segment may represent rare causes for CLP. Vanderas AP et al.[15] said that the TGFA gene encodes a growth factor that binds the epidermal growth factor receptor, which basically activates the signaling pathway for cell proliferation, differentiation, and development.

The results of our study revealed a significant association between the TGFA genes and CLP in the North Indian population. However certain articles do not support our claim that the TGFA gene plays a role in the causation of CLP. Qian JF et al.[16] studied BamH1 and Rsa1 SNPs and found that Rsa1 SNP was found statistically significant but not BamH1. Holder SE et al.[17] saw a significant association in Taq1 but no association seen in BamH1 and Rsa1.

Lidral et al. in 1998[18] studied candidate genes TGFA, BCL3, DLX2, MSX1, and TGFB3 and screened for Linkage Disequilibrium with either CLP or Cleft palate only (CPO) in the Caucasian population, in both the case, the control and nuclear family-based approaches and found out no significant association for the TGFA gene. Souza LT et al.[8] 2012 studied the TGFA gene and the environmental factors and found no association with the TGFA genes in the NSCLP population and said that tobacco or alcohol exposure during pregnancy was related to CL/P. Shen Y et al.[19] 2009 studied the IRF6 gene and TGFA gene in the population of Asian ancestry and found the contribution of the TRF6 gene in CLP with or without CP but no evidence was found for the TGFA gene in CLP. Martinelli M et al.[20] 2009 studied epidermal growth factor receptors that actively participate in palate development and saw that there is a lack of association between polymorphisms of epidermal growth factor receptors and non-syndromic CL with or without CP. Ehlers Bertoja A et al.[21] 2008 studied the TGFA gene in 140 Non syndromic Cleft Lip and Palate (NSCLP) patients and 142 controls and saw that there was no association with NSCLP in the sample from Rio Grande Do Sul.

However, some studies reported the same results as our study. Lu Y et al.[22] 2013 studied the TGFA gene and IRF6 gene in 236 unrelated pts, 185 mothers, and 154 fathers, including 128 complete trios, and 400 controls, and saw a significant association between the TGFA gene and IRF6 gene and NSCLP. Machida J et al.[14] 1999 studied TGFA gene mutations in 250 individuals with NSCLP or NS-CPO by conformational analysis of the coding sequence and splice junctions. Five variants represent clefting in humans and support the role of TGFA. Ardinger HH et al.[23] 1989 reported the role of TGFA in NSCLP and studied 80 subjects and 102 controls. A significant association was reported between two RFLPs at the TGFA gene and the occurrence of clefting. So, the study suggested that either the gene TGFA or any DNA sequence contributed to the development of a portion of CL/P. Letra A et al.[24] 2012 studied TGFA genes and IRF6 genes and found that the IRF6 gene and TGFA gene contribute to the subsets of C/P with specific dental anomalies. The potential IRF6-TGFA interaction may account for as much as 1% to 10% of CL/P cases. The IRF6 knock-out model performed by Letra et al. supported the evidence of IRF6-TGFA interaction found in humans. Derelli Tufekci E et al.[25] 2018 studied 70 NSCLP and 85 controls from Turkey and found a significant difference between TGFA gene frequencies in controls and occurrence of non-syndromic CL (P = .029), NSCLP (P = .024) and Non-syndromic NSCL/NSCLP (P = .036). Lu XC et al.[26] In 2014 did a systematic review and Meta-analysis and found a positive association between the TGFA gene and NSCLP. Desai AL et al.[27] 2014 studied 25 CLP patients and 25 controls and saw that the TGFA gene is significantly associated with NSCLP. Ebadifar A. et al.[28] 2015 studied 105 Iranian subjects and 218 controls and found TGFA gene was significantly associated with NSCLP. So many studies throughout the world in different populations supported the role of the TGFA gene in the causation of NSCLP and supported our claim that the TGFA gene is a genetic marker in NSCLP cases.

Limitations of the study

Certain limitations of our study are that the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to the entire North Indian population. Ethnicity of the study population due to the absence of any systematic information about the mother’s race. The increase in mixed-racial marriages makes it difficult to assign it to one population. Further studies with larger sample sizes should be taken up to confirm these findings and try to generalize the results for this population.

CONCLUSION

Our study provides evidence of an association between the TGFA gene and the occurrence of CLP in non-syndromic patients within the North Indian population. The findings of the study contribute to our understanding of the genetic basis of this craniofacial anomaly. Future research is needed to validate our results to investigate gene-environment interactions also and explore the underlying molecular mechanisms, involved in the development of the CLP. A better understanding of the candidate genes, involved in this condition can help doctors in early detection, intervention, and improved outcomes for the affected individuals and their families.

Significance of the study

The geneticist can assess from a good family history that candidate genes are involved in the occurrence of CLP for that population. An understanding of the genetic predisposition of Oral clefts provides a geneticist for determining the recurrence of these in future siblings as the firstborn and second born from cleft parents have a very high probability of getting a cleft of any type.

Future prospects of the study

The findings of this study should not be taken independently but rather be compared with candidate genes and other risk factors to increase the quality of evidence. A study could be planned with a larger sample size to conclude the results and with the help of extracted DNA, Single nucleotide polymorphisms of various candidate genes can be diagnosed for any occurrence of CLP.
