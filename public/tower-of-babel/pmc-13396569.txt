IL-HS: a deep inception-LSTM architecture for enhanced lithological mapping using EnMAP hyperspectral remote sensing data
Authors: Younes Khandouch, Soufiane Hajaj, Abderrazak El Harti, Amin Beiranvand Pour, Ahmed Laamrani, Jamal-Eddine Ouzemou, Aymen Flah, Nejib Ghazouani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate lithological mapping in semi-arid and geologically complex regions is crucial for advancing geoscientific understanding and guiding mineral exploration, yet it remains a technically challenging task. Here, we introduce the Inception-LSTM Hyperspectral Mapper (IL-HS), a deep learning framework designed to enhance lithological classification from hyperspectral satellite imagery. The model integrates InceptionV2 for multi-scale spatial feature extraction with a bidirectional long short-term memory (Bi-LSTM) module to capture sequential spectral information. Using EnMAP hyperspectral data over the Kerdous inlier, Anti-Atlas, Morocco, IL-HS achieved an overall accuracy (OA) of 98.05% across twenty-six lithological units, substantially outperforming state-of-the-art models, e.g., support vector machines (OA = 91.17%) and 3D convolutional neural networks (OA = 94.61%). The proposed model demonstrated strong class-wise performance, including perfect recall for copper and manganese-bearing formations in the Proterozoic and infra-Cambrian basement-cover border mineralizations, and reliably distinguished spectrally similar formations such as multi-age carbonates and volcanic units. These results show that IL-HS effectively mitigates spectral redundancy and mixing artifacts in heterogeneous extended age and altered terrains. Our findings pinpoint IL-HS as a robust and scalable approach for hyperspectral lithological classification and mapping using hyperspectral satellite data, with broad potential for applications in geoscientific research, mineral resource assessment, and sustainable exploration research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52520-9.Keywords: Lithological mapping, Hyperspectral satellite imagery, EnMAP satellite data, Deep learning, Deep inception architecture, Long short-term memory (LSTM)Subject terms: Environmental sciences, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Lithological mapping and the characterization of Earth’s surface materials are essential for many geoscientific applications. These include mineral prospectivity analysis, environmental monitoring, land-use planning, and geological hazard assessment1. Hyperspectral remote sensing has emerged as a powerful approach in this domain. Its fine spectral resolution allows the detection of subtle mineralogical and lithological variations. This is particularly effective at regional scales2–4. These capabilities have significantly improved our ability to identify and classify different lithological units. As a result, they support both fundamental research and resource exploration5. However, accurate lithological mapping remains difficult in semi-arid regions with complex geological settings. Spectral mixing, sparse vegetation, and weathering effects often obscure diagnostic signatures, complicating unit separation and introducing uncertainty6. These challenges can mask surface expressions of key geological processes, including those linked to mineralization7. Although traditional techniques have been effectively used to achieve this, they often fall short when dealing with complex and nonlinear remote sensing datasets, such as hyperspectral data. Overcoming these limitations requires advanced analytical methods capable of handling spectral redundancy and extracting reliable information from heterogeneous terrains.

To this end, a variety of sophisticated algorithms like Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) have been developed and used to process hyperspectral data efficiently and solve the aforementioned issues5,8–11. Furthermore, hyperspectral data is high-dimensional and cannot be effectively processed by baseline methods to reveal and capture hidden relationships12. Consequently, recent research has shown the benefits of machine learning-based techniques for processing hyperspectral data to perform lithological mapping and identify intricate spatial and temporal patterns13–15. Additionally, these data-driven methods may identify high-dimensional patterns, such as noisy, sparse, and complex relationships16,17. Among the most notable ML approaches are Support Vector Machines (SVM)18–20 and Random Forest (RF)21. Due to several challenges, including the curse of dimensionality, such approaches are shallow and limited in their ability to identify deep features linked to intricate and significant processes from hyperspectral data, particularly when working with high-dimensional datasets. Because of their superior feature representation and excellent performance, deep learning-based frameworks have thus been adopted for image processing22,23.

In the field of hyperspectral imagery classification, recent developments in deep learning have shown encouraging outcomes24–27. For instance, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) have demonstrated efficacy in autonomously extracting spatial information from hyperspectral images28,29. Furthermore, spectral information can be successively captured by recurrent architectures such as the Long Short-Term Memory module LSTM30. By combining spectral and spatial information, hybrid models like CNN-LSTM networks have further enhanced performance31.

Although there has been significant progress, current hyperspectral lithological mapping methods still have important methodological flaws. The capacity of classical machine learning techniques to capture geological structure is limited by their reliance on spectral vectors and lack of explicit spatial modeling17. Although CNNs based models, such as 1D and 2D CNN, are effective in learning local spatial–spectral patterns but commonly ignore the inherent sequential continuity of hyperspectral data by treating spectral bands as unordered dimensions26. While 3D CNN maintain the volumetric structure of hyperspectral cubes, their high parameter count frequently causes overfitting, especially in large-scale geological settings with few labeled data32. The majority of current hybrid models based on the combination of CNN and recurrent neural networks architectures (RNN), rely on single-scale convolutional front-ends, which limits their capacity to jointly capture multi-scale spatial features and long-range spectral dependencies necessary for distinguishing spectrally similar lithological units in complex terrains33.

Consequently, there is a deficiency of frameworks that can handle parameters efficiently and combine multi-scale 3D spectral–spatial feature extraction with bidirectional spectral sequence modeling in one architecture. In hyperspectral lithological mapping, it is crucial to integrate both hierarchical spatial context and global spectral continuity at the same time, as subtle spectral variations, geological heterogeneity, and mixed-pixel effects require simultaneous modeling. Furthermore, because most studies focus on a small number of lithological classes or employ idealized datasets2, there are a few trustworthy frameworks for large-scale, real-world mapping and the implementation of complicated hybrid deep architectures34,35.

To address these limitations, this study proposes the Inception–LSTM Hyperspectral Mapper (IL-HS), a hybrid deep learning architecture specifically designed for large-scale hyperspectral lithological mapping. The proposed framework combines a redesigned 3D Inception V2 module as a front-end for hierarchical multi-scale spectrally spatially extracted features with a Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTM) network for explicit spectral sequence modeling36. By constraining the number of branches, the redesigned 3D Inception V2 module reduces overfitting and improves generalization robustness, while preserving efficient multi-scale spatial–spectral feature extraction. Long-range spectral dependencies can be accurately modelled using the Bi-LSTM back-end, which processes hyperspectral bands as ordered sequences in both forward and backward directions, and enables robust discrimination of lithological units with subtle spectral differences. Compared to conventional 3D CNN/RNN hybrids that use fixed-scale convolutional features36. IL-HS explicitly integrates multi-scale spatial fusion before sequence modeling. Unlike transformer-based and Vision Transformer (ViT) approaches, which frequently necessitate large labeled datasets37. IL-HS provides a data-efficient and physically interpretable alternative that is more appropriate for real-world geological mapping scenarios using satellite hyperspectral data like EnMAP.

The IL-HS framework is designed to hierarchically learn both local and global spectral-spatial patterns, enabling precise discrimination of lithological units with close to very distinct spectral properties. By leveraging the EnMAP satellite’s high-resolution hyperspectral data, our approach explicitly models the intricate relationships between bands while mitigating noise and redundancy through optimized Inception modules. This work’s main contributions are threefold: Developing a hybrid Inception-LSTM architecture specifically designed for hyperspectral lithological classification. A comprehensive evaluation of a challenging semi-arid region with 26 litho-units spanning diverse geological ages. Benchmarking against state-of-the-art models to demonstrate superior accuracy and generalizability.

Material

Study area

The research area is situated in the Anti-Atlas belt of Morocco, at the northern West African Craton. This belt forms a wide anticline, characterized by numerous Precambrian inliers (e.g., the Bou Azzer and Kerdous inlier), where Proterozoic formations outcrop. Ediacaran-Paleozoic cover sequences cover these inliers in the western Anti-Atlas38. The current investigated area crosses the defunct Idikel mine region in the eastern Kerdous inlier (Fig. 1). The inlier reveals a paleoproterozoic basement unit as a polymetamorphic complex39,40. The Paleoproterozoic is represented by the blastomylonites (XIξ, luXII2), schists, mica-schists, and the orthogneisses of Jbel (mountain) Ouiharen (Xoε). The Tasserhirt Plateau calc-alkaline granite (XIm) is one of numerous Paleoproterozoic granitoids that cover these layers. The Neoproterozoic succession in the study area is characterized by quartzites (XII2q), rhyolitic volcanic rocks and ignimbrites (XIIIm), as well as Tafraout granites (XII3γ). It also comprises volcano-sedimentary deposits (XIIIS1), mainly represented by greso-pelitic layers (XIIIS1e) and conglomerate facies (XIIIS1cg). Doleritic occurrences (XII2) are predominantly observed crosscutting the quartzite units (Fig. 1). The Adoudou Formation marks the Lower Cambrian, which can be differentiated into a basal sequence of schist and sandstone (Ad11a) “série de base”, limestones and dolomites (Ad11b) “série inferieure”, and an overlying lower-series of dolomite and calcareous rocks (Ad12). It merits emphasis that the succession from the lower Cambrian shows the two Taroudant and Tata extended groups41, which included, for the first (Taroudant), the Adoudou carbonates-dominated. Whereas, Tata group is widely developed in the study northern part (Fouanou syncline), formed by schists and carbonates-dominated formations, developed between the inliers of Igherm et de Kerdous (Fig. 2). From a metallogenic standpoint, elucidating the pronounced lithological heterogeneity-reflected in the exceptionally high number of mapped units (26 classes), and their petrological attributes is indispensable for advancing mineral exploration within the study area, a domain characterized by noteworthy copper and manganese occurrences4,42. The investigated sector has been recurrently targeted in remote sensing–oriented research endeavors devoted to refined geological discrimination, thereby yielding critical perspectives on Neoproterozoic–Cambrian metallogenesis, principally copper mineralization, within the western Anti-Atlas province4,10,43,44.

Hyperspectral satellite data

In this study, we used an EnMAP hyperspectral imagery covering the investigated area. The EnMAP satellite mission was launched on April 1st, 202245. It is an imaging push broom system with two sensors recording data at 420 to 2450 nm wavelength range. The sensor is characterized by a 30 km swath width, 30 m spatial resolution, and 6.5–10 nm spectral resolution for the VNIR and the SWIR, respectively. The sensor provides a notable signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)46,47. The used scene was atmospherically and geometrically corrected (Level 2 A), obtained with 0% cloud cover, and is georeferenced in the Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) zone 29 N coordinate system with the WGS84 datum (https://www.enmap.org/data_access/, accessed on 2 February 2023). The (1780.22–1967.66 nm) regions were excluded, along with water absorption bands at 130 to 140 (1330.85–1495.89 nm) and 166 to 174 (1780.22–2032.7 nm), and anomalous band 171 (2005.08 nm). For subsequent analyses, only 204 spectral bands were retained and processed. Other preprocessing steps applied to the EnMAP imagery included the use of a sparse vegetation mask based on an NDVI threshold of 0.20531 selected to avoid masking an excessive portion of the study area. Following vegetation masking, a gap-filling step (triangulation) was performed to account for the spatial discontinuities introduced by the masked pixels and to maintain continuous spatial coverage of the image. In the present study, this procedure did not rely on interpolation or spectral value estimation, but rather consisted of preserving a consistent analysis domain after masking so that subsequent classification could be carried out on the valid image regions without introducing artificial spectral information48. Due to the spatial distribution of the masked zones, the full extent of Class 24 (XIIδ2, Xδ), corresponding to dolerites, could not be entirely mapped in this work; therefore, its results were excluded from the geological map update. Overall, the study adopts a lithological classification framework comprising 26 distinct units to represent the diversity of rock types present in the investigated region.

Labeled data

To categorize the different rock types in the study area, we use a lithological classification scheme with 26 classes. Based on the geological map of Tafraout, each class is given a distinct code for identification. The geological map of Tafraout, created by meticulous field mapping and petrographic validation, served as the basis for the ground truth dataset. The map was recreated by digitizing and rasterizing it to match the spatial resolution and coordinate reference system of the EnMAP imagery. The original geological legend was used to assign a class label to each lithological polygon. Although the geological map is a reliable reference, labeling is still uncertain, especially around lithological boundaries and areas that have been affected by weathering, sedimentary cover, or structural complexity. Pixels located at lithological contacts often represent mixed spectral responses and were retained in the dataset to preserve realistic mapping conditions. However, their presence is expected to lead to increased classification ambiguity and lowered accuracy in boundary zones. When interpreting classification results, it’s important to keep in mind that pixel-based hyperspectral lithological mapping is limited by uncertainty.

Notably, the labeled data or ground truth (GT) data make up 1.08% of the total pixels of the study area encompassing the EnMAP scene (Fig. 3). To decrease spatial autocorrelation effects, reference samples were chosen with spatial dispersion throughout each lithological unit, preventing close clustering of neighboring pixels. The sampling strategy is designed to minimize the optimistic bias that can arise from the spatial proximity between training and testing samples, in accordance with the best practices applied in recent hyperspectral classification studies.

Spectral characteristics

The spectral signatures extracted from the hyperspectral data across the study area, covering 26 different lithologic classes, show nuanced variations indicative of the different lithofacies (Fig. 4). These classes correspond to lithological units and related mineral changes that have been previously identified through hyperspectral studies and petrographic analyses4. The analysis reveals that there are distinct spectral responses across the different zones of the study area (Fig. 4), especially in the southern sector and eastern border of the Kerdous inlier, which show a good development of hydrothermal alteration. In particular, the spectral signatures display distinctive absorption characteristics that are associated with important alterations in mineral groups, such as Al–OH, Mg–Fe–OH/CO3, and Fe3+/Fe2+, which are signs of hydrothermal processes. Al–OH absorption near 2200 nm (class 25) and Mg–Fe–OH/CO₃ absorption around 2336 nm (classes 13 and 15) are two of the most important absorption features of the mapped litho-units. These diagnostic characteristics are depicted in satellite and airborne-derived imaging spectroscopy data datasets covering the eastern Kerdous region4,43.

Even though these diagnostic spectral features exist, there is a strong spectral similarity among various lithological units, particularly those with carbonate-rich formations and schist-dominated units. The spectral overlap in the SWIR region could lead to potential confusion among classes like Ad11b, Ad12b, Ki1a, and Twd. Such ambiguity is associated with shared mineralogical assemblages dominated by carbonates and clay, as well as mixed-pixel effects and intra-class mineralogical variability, which is commonly challenging in geologically complex terrains hyperspectral lithological mapping.

Methodology and material

This study proposes a comprehensive and robust framework for hyperspectral image (HSI) classification that integrates conventional machine learning algorithms with advanced deep learning techniques. Figure 5 illustrates the overall workflow of the proposed methodology.

Workflow of the proposed hyperspectral image classification methodology.

Data acquisition

The first step of the methodology involves acquiring hyperspectral imagery over the studied area from the EnMAP L2A data; for more details, refer to49. In order to guarantee the quality and reliability of the HSI dataset, a preprocessing procedure is performed to eliminate bad bands; bands impacted by noise, atmospheric interference, or sensor anomalies.

Data processing

Following data acquisition and preprocessing, dimensionality reduction was performed to address the inherent high dimensionality of hyperspectral data, which can introduce redundancy and increase computational complexity. In this study, Principal Component Analysis PCA is employed as a feature extraction technique to reduce the spectral dimensions while preserving the most significant variance within the data50. Following that, an SVM classifier was trained with an increasing number of PCs, and the resulting overall accuracy (OA) was systematically assessed to discover the appropriate number of components. The first maximum of OA occurred at 40 PCs, which was chosen as the ideal dimension. This step effectively compresses the hyperspectral data, facilitating more efficient and accurate classification. This approach achieves an optimal balance by eliminating redundant information while still maintaining high predictive accuracy. Furthermore, the retention of fewer principal components significantly reduces computational complexity and accelerates both model training and inference, which is a crucial aspect for deep learning frameworks. The supplementary materials include (Fig. D.1), which clearly illustrates that incorporating more than 40 principal components does not lead to additional improvement, thereby proving that the chosen dimensionality is both optimal and computationally efficient. The resulting dataset was split into training and testing subsets. The training set was used to build and optimize the classification models, while the test set served as an independent dataset for performance evaluation. Due to the unequal distribution of samples across lithological classes, in this investigation, no explicit class weighting or data augmentation strategies were used during training, while respecting the natural distribution of litho-classes (Data augmentation and oversampling techniques were intentionally avoided to prevent the introduction of artificial spatial dependencies between training and testing samples, which could otherwise lead to overly optimistic performance estimates.). Instead, class imbalance was addressed by carefully selecting samples, using a consistent train/test split across all evaluated models, and employing macro-average evaluation metrics that provide a balanced assessment of classification performance across all classes.

Classification

The classification of the preprocessed hyperspectral data is the main emphasis of the methodology. K-fold cross-validation (CV) is used to train and optimize classification models to ensure the resilience and reliability of the classification results51. This assessment technique divides the training dataset into k equal subsets, or folds, using each fold once as a validation set while the remaining k-1 folds as a training set. This procedure is carried out k times, and the performance metrics are averaged across all folds. The ability of k-fold CV to mitigate overfitting and minimize the bias associated with a single train-test split is its main benefit. K-fold CV optimizes data use by guaranteeing that each data point is utilized for both training and validation. Additionally, it helps to identify possible performance variance by offering insights into the model’s stability and resilience across various data divisions. This evaluation strategy supports the selection of hyperparameters and model architectures with increased confidence in their ability to generalize to unseen data. During model training in this work, a 5-fold CV approach (K = 5) is used. The proposed IL-HS architecture (Inception-V2/Bi-LSTM) was extensively benchmarked against a diverse set of state-of-the-art canonical and hybrid models, including SVM, 3D CNN, HybridCNN, 3D CNN/RNN, ViT, TCN, Inception V1, Inception V2, Inception V1/GRU, Inception V1/LSTM, and Inception V2/GRU.

Support vector machine

Support Vector Machine (SVM) is a classical machine learning baseline classifier18–20, has been used extensively because of its ability to solve a wide range of classification and regression problems in a variety of fields, including hyperspectral image processing52,53. To separate data points belonging to various classes, this technique builds a hyperplane or a collection of hyperplanes in a high-dimensional feature space. Its fundamental idea is maximizing the margin between the separating hyperplane and the closest training data points of any class, known as support vectors. SVMs have shown excellent performance in hyperspectral image classification, especially when using Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernels. Their capacity to implicitly transfer input information into a higher-dimensional space, which aids in addressing the non-linear separability frequently seen in spectral signatures, is primarily responsible for their efficiency5. But because SVM is categorized as a spectral-based classifier, it doesn’t take into account the spatial context of nearby samples, which is crucial for identifying local structure in hyperspectral imagery. Furthermore, compared to more modern deep learning techniques that incorporate both spectral and spatial information, SVMs are less able to capture the intricate and hierarchical feature representations contained in hyperspectral data.

Convolutional neural network

Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) is a type of deep feed-forward artificial neural network55. CNN architectures are primarily composed of convolutional layers, non-linear activation functions, and pooling layers. In convolutional layers, learnable filters (kernels) perform convolution operations over the input data, effectively computing dot products between localized regions (receptive fields) and the filters to extract hierarchical feature representations. During training, the filter parameters are iteratively optimized to capture the most discriminative features. Non-linear activation functions introduce non-linearity into the network, enabling it to model complex relationships in the data. Pooling layers, typically using operations such as max or average pooling, down sample feature maps by summarizing local regions, thereby reducing spatial dimensionality and enhancing translational invariance. These operations collectively enable CNNs to effectively learn spatial hierarchies and complex structures in high-dimensional and multivariate data. CNNs are generally categorized into 1D, 2D, or 3D architectures, depending on the dimensionality of the convolutional and pooling operations applied.

2D CNN

2D Convolutional Neural Networks (2D CNN) are intended to handle data arranged in two spatial dimensions. In the context of hyperspectral imagery, a 2D CNN processes multi-band data by treating each spectral band as a separate input channel, or by using reduced spectral dimension data such as the PCA-derived new bands, which are then fed as channels into the network.

3D CNN

3D CNN is specifically made to handle hyperspectral data in its native volumetric form56. 3D-CNNs carry out convolution operations across the spatial (width and height) and spectral (band) dimensions, in contrast to 1D and 2D CNN, which either process spectral signatures independently or consider spectral bands as stacked channels. This preserves the inherent three-dimensional structure of hyperspectral image cubes and allows for the direct extraction of joint spatial–spectral features. In order to describe intricate inter-band interactions and spatial textures, the network uses 3D kernel filters that span spectral bands while also capturing spatial context. In hyperspectral image classification, where precise material identification depends on the integration of spectral and spatial information, this hybrid representation is especially helpful. In applications like lithological mapping, where minor spectral variations and spatial patterns are crucial for accurate interpretation, 3D-CNNs’ capacity to work with the volumetric structure of hyperspectral data offers a major benefit17.

Hybrid 2D CNN/3D CNN (HybridCNN) for Joint Spectral and Spatial Feature Learning: Hybrid 2D/3D CNN architectures have been designed to address the constraints of pure 2D CNN or 3D CNN while leveraging their respective strengths32. These models are designed primarily for HSI analysis, with the goal of extracting joint spectral and spatial characteristics effectively. A typical hybrid architecture has a two-stage or modular design:

3D Convolutional Front-end: The initial layers are made up of 3D convolutional blocks. These layers use small 3D kernels (e.g., , where is the spectral depth) to conduct volumetric convolution. This stage extracts low-level spectral and spatial characteristics directly from the raw HSI cube. It learns to encode the intrinsic correlations between adjacent spectral bands in a given spatial neighborhood, resulting in compact and discriminative spectral-spatial feature maps.

2D Convolutional Backend: The feature maps generated by the 3D convolutional step are then reshaped or aggregated. These are fed into a stack of 2D convolutional layers. This stage focuses on learning high-level spatial abstractions and patterns using spectrally enhanced feature maps. The 2D convolutions only act on spatial dimensions, allowing the network to create sophisticated spatial hierarchies and global contextual understanding more efficiently than if it continued with 3D operations.

The output of the final 2D convolutional layers is usually flattened and coupled to one or more fully connected layers for final classification. The hybrid approach has several advantages: it combines 3D CNN explicit spectrum modeling capacity with the powerful and efficient spatial hierarchical learning of 2D CNN. This produces more discriminative features than a model that merely performs 2D operations on spectrally reduced data. The hybrid model’s parameter efficiency helps to reduce overfitting, which is a major concern when dealing with high-dimensional HSI data and typically limited labeled training sets.

Inception-based model

Inception V1, commonly known as GoogleNet, is a pioneering deep learning architecture developed by Google researchers and described in the paper “Going Deeper with Convolutions"57. The name “Inception” refers to the architectural notion of merging several processing channels within a single module to represent the inception of ideas. The network was created to enhance both computational performance and representational strength in deep CNNs. Despite architectures that stack layers sequentially, Inception V1 has a modular design based on the Inception module (Fig. 6a), which carries out numerous concurrent convolution operations in addition to max-pooling on the same input, in contrast to conventional designs that build layers sequentially. The network can capture spatial characteristics at many scales inside a single layer by concatenating the resultant feature maps along the channel axis. through effectively capturing both fine and coarse patterns and adaptively learning hierarchical spatial representations.

Architecture of Inception module; (a) Inception V1, (b) Inception V2, (c) Redesigned Inception V1 and (d) Redesigned Inception V2.

Inception V2 builds on the underlying architecture of Inception V1 by introducing a series of structural modifications aimed at improving both classification accuracy and computing efficiency58. The factorization of larger convolutional filters is one of the major advancements. While maintaining the network’s ability to represent intricate spatial patterns, this breakdown drastically lowers the number of trainable parameters and processing overhead (Fig. 6b). Furthermore, InceptionV2 includes batch normalization, a regularization technique that standardizes the activations in each layer. This feature allows for faster convergence during training, stabilizes the learning process, and minimizes sensitivity to weight initialization. Inception V2 provides a more reliable and scalable framework for navigating the complexities of high-resolution spectral data.

The Inception module’s capacity to extract multi-scale spectral-spatial information makes it ideal for complex image analysis applications such as hyperspectral image classification. This makes it a great design for applications that require both accuracy and scalability. In the context of hyperspectral image classification, the architecture is modified to analyze hyperspectral data in volumetric form with a 3D-CNN layer, allowing for the learning of detailed and discriminative spectral-spatial characteristics at different scales. (Fig. 6c) and (Fig. 6d) show the redesigned architectures for 3D Inception V1 and 3D Inception V2, respectively. For the Inception V2 variation, in the training process, after much trial and error, the number of branches is limited to two in order to reduce overfitting and increase accuracy.

Bidirectional recurrent neural network

The Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) is a type of neural network that is specifically built to model sequential data by using temporal connections between successive inputs. Unlike feedforward networks, recurrent neural networks have feedback connections, which allow information from earlier time steps to influence the current output. Bidirectional RNN architectures (Fig. 7) have shown superior performance in modeling sequential data due to their ability to capture both forward and backward temporal dependencies59. Unlike standard unidirectional RNNs, which process sequences in a single direction, bidirectional models have two parallel recurrent layers: one processes the input sequence forward in time, and the other backward. The outputs of these two layers are typically combined, allowing the model to access all contextual information at each time step. The essential premise of Bidirectional Recurrent Neural Networks (Bi-RNN) is the dual-path processing of sequential information. Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTM) networks develop long-term dependencies using memory cells and gating approaches, whereas Bidirectional Gated Recurrent Units (Bi-GRU) combine specialized gates with fewer parameters36.

In the context of hyperspectral image analysis, such bidirectional architectures are particularly beneficial for spectral modeling. Hyperspectral data consist of hundreds of contiguous spectral bands, where the relationships between bands are not strictly unidirectional. By leveraging both past and future spectral context, Bidirectional networks enhance the modeling of spectral dependencies, leading to improved classification accuracy and robustness. When combined with deep convolutional architecture, these models form a powerful hybrid architecture capable of learning rich spectral–spatial representations from high-dimensional hyperspectral data.

Hybrid 3D CNN/RNN architecture

A hybrid 3D CNN/RNN architecture can be designed to take advantage of the complementary strengths of spatial feature extraction and sequential spectral modeling33. In this approach, the front-end spatial feature extractor is a convolutional neural network. This CNN component transforms the raw hyperspectral cube into hierarchical spatial representations and, in the case of a 3D CNN, initial joint spectral-spatial characteristics. The resulting high-level feature maps are vectorized or spatially pooled to provide a series of spectral descriptors. This sequence is fed into a Bidirectional Recurrent Neural Network (Bi-RNN), which serves as the backend spectral modeler. The Bi-RNN captures long-range contextual dependencies and detailed spectral correlations across bands by processing the sequence both forward and backward. This synergistic combination enables the CNN to concentrate on finding local spatial patterns and textures, while the RNN explicitly represents the continuous spectral signature as a sequential phenomenon. The architecture concludes by concatenating the final hidden states or aggregating the Bi-RNN outputs, which are then routed to a fully connected layer for classification. This integrated approach provides a powerful mechanism for learning rich spectral-spatial representations, typically resulting in higher classification accuracy by using both the spatial topology of the image and the temporal-like structure of the spectrum dimension.

Temporal convolutional networks

Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN) offer a stable and efficient alternative to recurrent architectures for this task. A TCN is formed using a sequence of one-dimensional dilated causal convolutions grouped in residual blocks61. An exponentially expanding receptive field is made possible by the employment of exponentially growing dilation factors (e.g., 1, 2, 4, 8, etc.), which enable the network to collect long-range relationships across hundreds of spectral bands from a deep but compact architecture. Important benefits include stable gradients made possible by residual connections, intrinsic parallelizability during training (since convolutions are non-sequential), and the lack of vanishing/exploding gradient issues typical with RNN. Applying a TCN to hyperspectral data results in highly discriminative spectral representations by processing each pixel’s spectral signature as an input sequence and learning hierarchical filters that recognize discriminative spectral features and their contextual relationships throughout the whole wavelength range.

Vision transformers

By utilizing transformers’ global self-attention mechanism, Vision Transformers (ViT) offer a radically innovative, convolution-free method for hyperspectral imagery processing37. The input hyperspectral cube is first partitioned into a succession of patches. To preserve spatial information, each patch is flattened, linearly projected onto an embedding space, and enhanced with positional encodings. This sequence of patch embeddings is then put into a typical Transformer encoder, comprising alternating multi-head self-attention and multilayer perceptron blocks. By calculating pairwise interactions between each patch in the sequence, the self-attention mechanism enables the model to explicitly represent global dependencies throughout the whole spatial and spectral breadth of the image. This sequence of patch embeddings is then fed into a standard Transformer encoder, composed of alternating multi-head self-attention and multilayer perceptron blocks. The self-attention mechanism computes pairwise interactions between all patches in the sequence, allowing the model to directly model global dependencies across the entire spatial and spectral extent of the image. This enables the ViT to integrate information from distant spectral bands and diverse geographical locations concurrently, generating a holistic representation that captures complex, non-local spectral-spatial interactions. ViT has shown a surprising ability to learn strong, globally-informed features for pixel-wise categorization, while frequently needing large amounts of data for training.

Proposed architecture

The Inception-LSTM Hyperspectral Mapper (IL-HS) is a revolutionary deep learning architecture developed exclusively for comprehensive hyperspectral lithological mapping. It combines the synergistic benefits of enhanced 3D Inception V2 with the sequential modeling capabilities of bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTM) networks (Fig. 8). In this hybrid framework, the Inception V2 component serves as a powerful front-end, hierarchically extracting multi-scale spectral-spatial features from the hyperspectral imagery. The factorized convolutional and batch normalization within Inception V2 enable efficient learning of robust local and global patterns across the spectral bands. The output feature representations from the Inception V2 modules are then fed into an LSTM network. The LSTM, as presented, is capable of modeling long-range sequential dependencies. This capability enables it to model and capture the intricate relationships and contextual information distributed across the numerous contiguous spectral bands of hyperspectral data. This sequential processing by the Bi-LSTM, together with multi-scale characteristics obtained by 3D Inception V2, enables the IL-HS to effectively distinguish lithological units with small spectral differences. The model’s integration of spatial and spectral contextual information enables it to overcome common issues in hyperspectral lithological mapping, such as spectral mixing and geological complexity. Similarly, the Inception V2/GRU model combines the revised 3D Inception V2 module with GRU layers, achieving a balance between architectural enhancements and efficient recurrent modeling. GRU is a computationally efficient alternative to LSTM, requiring fewer parameters and faster convergence while efficiently capturing sequential spectral relationships. In addition, using 3D Inception V1 variations with recurrent units is investigated. Inception V1/GRU and Inception V1/LSTM architectures use the Inception module to extract hierarchical spatial-spectral information, which is then fed into Bi-GRU and Bi-LSTM layers, respectively. As a result, these hybrid architectures leverage the complementary qualities of Inception modules and recurrent units to capture complicated spatial patterns and spectral correlations. This integration solves critical issues in hyperspectral lithological mapping, including spectral mixing and geological variability, resulting in improved classification accuracy and robustness.

Discussion

Modeling approach: straight benchmark

The superior performance of the IL-HS model, evidenced by a 4.36% increase in F1-score compared to the baseline Inception V2 (Table 6), highlights the efficacy of incorporating sequential modeling via LSTM. Unlike 3D CNN architectures that treat hyperspectral data as static 3D volumes, the IL-HS approach models spectral bands as temporally ordered sequences. This is particularly advantageous for capturing cross-band dependencies, enabling the model to mimic the interpretative approach of geologists who analyze reflectance curves across spectral regions. This design is especially valuable for discriminating lithological units (Fig. 3). The IL-HS model demonstrates high classification performance, achieving over 98% user’s accuracy (UA) and producer’s accuracy (PA) for approximately 80% of the classes (Table 7). These results are consistent with ground-truth data from field surveys, particularly for dominant lithological units such as the XIm granitoids. The model’s strong generalization across these prevalent classes validates its suitability for geospatial geological mapping in lithologically complex terrains (due to the topology and the weathering process). Nevertheless, misclassifications remain in regions where post-formational weathering modifies spectral responses or limited extent classes. Although the suggested IL-HS model has high training accuracy. Model generalization was then tested utilizing the accuracy of the testing dataset, rather than the accuracy of the training dataset alone. Confusion matrices using test can be found in supplemental materials, as well as the class-wise accuracy metrics, which show that the model performs well across the 26 lithological units, demonstrating its capacity to generalize across a wide range of data. To further mitigate overfitting concerns associated with model complexity, multiple regularization strategies were implemented, including dropout layers through the deep network, early stopping based on validation loss, and architectural simplification by restricting the number of Inception branches. Furthermore, the observed differences in classification accuracy across lithological units are geologically consistent, with higher performance for spatially extensive and spectrally distinct formations and lower accuracy for narrow or highly mixed units.

In direct ablation, for SVM, the IL-HS framework significantly reduces spectral-spatial noise (Fig. 13), owing to its integrated modeling of both spatial features and spectral sequence continuity. Unlike pixel-wise SVM classifiers that lack spatial awareness, IL-HS leverages convolutional features in tandem with LSTM encoding, allowing for contextual learning within local geological structures. Compared to 3D CNN architectures, IL-HS offers a favorable classification accuracy. The Inception-LSTM hybrid achieves comparable or superior predictive performance, indicating a more parameter-efficient design. Furthermore, improvements over Inception V1 can be attributed to architectural enhancements in Inception V2, notably the incorporation of batch normalization, which contributes to more stable training dynamics. This is reflected in the narrower confidence intervals across cross-validation folds (Table 6), demonstrating improved model reliability.

The IL-HS model demonstrates superior lithological classification efficacy, evidenced by a 4.36% F1-score increase over Inception V2 (Table 3) and > 98% accuracy for ~ 80% of units (Table 7), validating its capacity to emulate geologists’ spectral-curve interpretation via LSTM-based sequential modeling. The reported accuracy is reasonable when considering EnMAP HSI capability and the appropriate labeling of studied area geology. Although the sensor’s spatial resolution of 30 m introduces mixing effects, many prominent lithological units form spatially coherent bodies that stretch far beyond the pixel scale, allowing diagnostic spectral signatures to be successfully caught. As a result, the observed performance reflects true spectral-lithological separability rather than an artifact of model complexity.

Although the proposed framework demonstrates robust classification performance, the 30-meter ground sampling distance (GSD) of EnMAP imagery imposes limitations on the delineation of fine-scale geological features. Narrow dykes, such as those classified under XIIδ2, are particularly susceptible to omission or spectral mixing due to their sub-pixel width. This underscores the need for multi-scale data fusion approaches. Integration with high-resolution drone-acquired hyperspectral imagery (< 10 cm GSD) could significantly enhance the discrimination of such features by providing finer spatial detail without sacrificing spectral fidelity. Future work should explore hierarchical fusion strategies, where drone data enhances EnMAP-based predictions in critical zones, particularly where geological structures exhibit sub-pixel heterogeneity. However, the model’s operational deployment remains restricted by domain-wide constraints such as coarse spatial resolution, spectrum ambiguities caused by surface processes, and class imbalance. Given these restrictions, future research should focus on multi-scale data fusion; the development of these advanced algorithms will contribute to addressing class imbalance. Addressing these issues is critical to improving the model’s resilience, transferability, and overall usability for real-world exploration scenarios.

A careful preprocessing of the HSI data was essential to minimize non-geological spectral interference and ensure that the extracted features reliably represent lithological variability. In the preprocessing, a vegetation masking approach was used to reduce spectral contamination from low vegetation cover, which has been shown to interfere with lithological classification in semi-arid conditions. The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), was used to identify vegetation pixels, which were then masked with a conservative threshold (NDVI > 0.205). This threshold was chosen to efficiently reduce vegetative pixels while retaining exposed rock surfaces, preventing severe data loss in sparsely vegetated geological units. This method assures that the obtained spectral signatures mostly represent lithological information rather than biogenic spectral contributions. Following vegetation masking, a gap-filling approach was used to rebuild missing pixel values while maintaining spatial consistency throughout the picture. Given the spatially fragmented distribution of masked pixels, gap filling was carried out, ensuring that reconstructed values were spectrally compatible with the surrounding lithological units. This procedure was necessary to prevent false discontinuities in the input data, which would otherwise impede convolutional feature extraction and spatial-spectral learning inside the classification models. However, vegetation-related spectral effects are primarily associated with Class 24. Owing to the inherent spectral–spatial characteristics of this class (see “Hyperspectral satellite data” section), it is considered less suitable for direct inclusion in subsequent geological map refinement, compared with the remaining areas, which provide more robust and interpretable geological information.

Contribution to current research on HSI-based classification

Previous surveys and comparative studies have shown that hybrid deep architectures perform better for HSI classification due to their capacity to exploit both spectral and spatial information64. These hybrid approach, which frequently combine CNN with RNN module or transformers, to effectively capture spatial and spectral data, resulting in higher classification accuracy. In the context of our work, the suggested model adopts this effective hybrid technique, utilizing a hybrid architecture built to solve the intrinsic problems of HSI data in lithological classifications. The proposed model uses an Inception V2 module to extract multi-scale spatial features and a Bi-LSTM module to for spectral sequences. This design is consistent with the literature, which shows that such integrations excel at dealing with high-dimensional data, spectral variability, and complex spatial-spectral relationships, particularly in difficult environments such as semi-arid, geologically complex regions65. The model’s ability to distinguish between spectrally similar lithologies and reduce spectral mixing problems demonstrates the benefits of architecturally addressing data dependencies. The performance of the proposed model was assessed on a difficult geological landscape with 26 lithological classes. In reference to state-of-the-art research in HSI classification, with the meta transfer learning-based architecture65. While their method produces competitive results on widely used benchmarks (Indian Pines: 72.60% OA, University of Pavia: 86.02% OA, and Salinas: 90.97% OA). These results are obtained under rather advantageous settings, specifically clean and balanced datasets with few labeled classes. Our approach maintains stronger performance even when applied to more challenging and heterogeneous data scenarios. The IL-HS model minimizes computing cost while enhancing generalization, particularly across classes with high spectral similarity (rock units). Significant class imbalance is a major difficulty in geological remote sensing. The suggested model addresses this issue directly, obtaining more than 95% accuracy in 23 of the 26 classes. This excellent performance also applies to economically important but difficult-to-discriminate minority classes, such as copper-bearing units66.

Challenges and future perspectives

Despite its high classification accuracy, the actual implementation of the IL-HS model is subject to a number of well-recognized constraints of hyperspectral lithological mapping. These limits are principally caused by sensor spatial resolution, class imbalance, mixed-pixel effects, surface weathering processes, and spectral ambiguity between lithological units, which are inherent issues in hyperspectral mineral/lithological mapping2,67,68. First, the use of EnMAP data at a 30 m spatial resolution limits the detection of fine-scale geological features, leading to unavoidable sub-pixel mixing and reduced representation of narrow lithological units, particularly in geologically varied and complex terrains. Second, mixed-pixel effects are a significant source of uncertainty, particularly at lithological boundaries and inside heterogeneous units. Pixels with various lithologies provide composite spectral responses, reducing class separability. Third, processes such as weathering and metamorphic overprinting can introduce spectral ambiguities by modifying primary lithological signatures through the development of secondary minerals (e.g., clay.), thereby increasing spectral similarity among otherwise distinct classes. Overall. Metamorphism-induced changes in optical characteristics, combined with the exclusion of blastomylonite units (due to their spectral convergence with adjacent lithologies in EnMAP false-color imagery) as well as the sensor’s 30 m limited GSD, make it difficult to delineate some fine-scale geological features (e.g., sub-pixel dykes). To overcome these problems, future research should take a multifaceted approach. The integration of satellite hyperspectral observations with high-resolution airborne or drone photography (< 5 cm GSD) is crucial for resolving sub-pixel features and overcoming present spatial limits. Hierarchical fusion designs are particularly promising for reducing spectral-spatial ambiguities in transitional zones while increasing robustness across diverse terrains.

To overcome class imbalance, advanced data augmentation approaches (e.g., generative models) and class-sensitive learning algorithms (e.g., weighted loss functions) are still required. Beyond class imbalance, a lack of labeled training pixels remains a major hurdle for deep learning in hyperspectral lithological mapping. Parallel efforts in well-log-based lithofacies identification have shown that semi-supervised learning can efficiently use large amounts of unlabeled data to increase prediction accuracy when labeled samples are limited69. Adopting semi-supervised or self-supervised frameworks for EnMAP data could greatly decrease the need for extensive ground-truth annotations while retaining high classification fidelity across lithological units with similar spectra. Furthermore, the development of strong transfer learning frameworks will be critical for boosting cross-terrain generalization. Integrating supplementary geographical datasets, such as LiDAR-derived topography, Digital Elevation Models (DEMs), and geophysical surveys, might provide additional contextual cues to help resolve spectral ambiguity. In terms of model transferability, the proposed IL-HS framework is expected to operate well in various geological contexts with comparable spectral separability, adequate geographic continuity of lithological units, and appropriate training samples. However, its performance may suffer in areas characterized by severe sub-pixel variability, strong surface change, or vastly diverse mineral assemblages. As a result, cross-site validation and domain-adaptive transfer learning algorithms are critical next steps for ensuring reliable deployment across varied geological provinces. Explainable AI and uncertainty quantification are critical operational components that enable end-user interpretability, trust, and decision-making reliability. To provide resilience, rigorous validation across a wide range of climatic variables, geological locations, and temporal scenarios is essential. Ultimately, by employing parameter-efficient models with steady performance (narrower confidence intervals), future frameworks can bring mineral exploration and geological mapping toward large-scale implementation.

Broader recommendations

The most critical challenge that researchers in the remote sensing field must address lies in the adaptation of advanced model architectures to HSI data. In recent years, many innovative model architectures have been developed to address complicated challenges in a variety of disciplines. However, direct application to HSI data frequently introduces fundamental problems such as overfitting and underfitting, resulting in poor generalization accuracy. As a result, research should focus on adapting and redesigning these structures, as well as fine-tuning their hyperparameters, to guarantee that they are better suited to the particular properties of HSI data.

Conclusions

Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed IL-HS model significantly outperforms existing methods in complex and varied geological settings, achieving superior precision in both boundary delineation and the identification of minority classes. This study highlights the promise of multi-scale, sequence-aware deep learning approaches for advancing remote sensing applications within geosciences. The IL-HS architecture effectively combines Inception V2’s multi-scale spatial feature extraction with Bi-LSTM based spectral sequence modeling, resulting in an improved overall classification accuracy of 98.05% and a Kappa coefficient of 97.94% across 26 lithological classes. This hybrid framework markedly outperforms conventional approaches, including standalone 3D-CNN (94.61%) and traditional machine learning methods such as SVM (91.17%), thereby validating its enhanced capacity for hyperspectral lithological mapping. By leveraging factorized 3D convolutions alongside batch normalization, IL-HS maintains strong discriminative capability for spectrally similar lithological units, for example, distinguishing dolerites from basalts with a precision of 97.8%. Moreover, the Bi-LSTM component effectively mitigates spectral mixing artifacts, which are prevalent in semi-arid terrains. The model’s robust per-class performance (exceeding 95% accuracy for 23 out of 26 lithological units) enables the detailed mapping of Proterozoic–Cambrian formations within the Kerdous inlier, directly supporting mineral exploration initiatives. Notably, the model achieves 97.44% and 100.00% PA and UA, respectively, for copper-bearing Adoudou schists “série de base”, underscoring its utility for targeted resource assessments. In summary, this work emphasizes the strategic benefits of combining sequential spectrum modeling with parameter-efficient structures. The proposed IL-HS model merges multi-scale spatial feature extraction with spectral sequence modeling, resulting in cutting-edge classification performance. Its versatility across lithological classes, particularly important minority units, demonstrates its potential as a reliable tool for geological interpretation and mineral prospecting. Furthermore, the model’s adaptability to multi-platform data streams makes it ideal for large-scale lithological mapping in semi-arid areas, as well as for seamless integration with new Earth observation systems and quick mineral resource assessment procedures. The performance of the IL-HS model supports and expands on recent advances in hyperspectral analysis. This paper makes numerous significant methodological contributions to the discipline, demonstrating the known advantage of deep learning methodologies. Our findings support and improve the current trend toward hybrid architectures by effectively integrating Inception V2 with a Bi-LSTM module, a combination that is effective for capturing complicated geo-spectral relationships.
