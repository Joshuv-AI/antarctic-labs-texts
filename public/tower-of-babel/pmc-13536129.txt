Geographical accessibility to hospital care amid armed conflict in Myanmar: a population-weighted analysis of compounding health inequality, 2021–2024
Authors: Hein Thu, Kaung Khant Tin, Aung Thura Htoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionArmed conflict disrupts health systems, but whether it compounds pre-existing geographical barriers to hospital care has not been quantified. We aimed to estimate population-weighted travel time to public hospital care across Myanmar’s 330 townships; test whether conflict following the February 2021 coup concentrated in populations with the worst baseline access, and whether attacks on health facilities followed the same pattern; and measure the cumulative person-year burden of simultaneous poor access and intense conflict.MethodsWe conducted a longitudinal ecological analysis of 330 townships over 16 quarters from 2021 to 2024. Travel time from each populated square kilometre to the nearest of 68 general hospitals, representing the surgical-care tier, was estimated using the pre-coup facility registry, OpenStreetMap road data and WorldPop 2020 population estimates. Townships were classified by population-weighted median travel time. Quarterly conflict intensity was calculated from 35 647 Armed Conflict Location and Event Data Project battle and explosion or remote-violence events; 655 Safeguarding Health in Conflict Coalition-reported facility attacks were analysed separately. The concentration index assessed whether conflict events, fatalities and facility attacks were disproportionately concentrated among populations with worse baseline access. Double-burden exposure (poor or very poor access combined with high or extreme conflict) was accumulated in person-years. Because access was held at pre-coup baseline, estimates should be read as optimistic bounds.ResultsBefore the coup, 5.8 million people (12%) lived over 2 hours from a general hospital. Double-burden exposure grew from 0.4 million (Q1 2021) to 3.1 million (Q1 2024). Conflict was significantly concentrated in worse-access populations: concentration index 0.126 (95% CI 0.053 to 0.199) for events, 0.169 (95% CI 0.078 to 0.263) for fatalities. Facility attacks showed no significant concentration (0.049, 95% CI −0.041 to 0.145), as remote townships lack targetable facilities. An estimated 8.4 million person-years of double-burden exposure accumulated over 2021–2024, rising roughly eightfold quarterly, from about 100 000 (Q1 2021) to 780 000 (Q1 2024).ConclusionConflict in Myanmar has compounded pre-existing geographical inequality in hospital access, with populations furthest from surgical care bearing a disproportionate burden. The concentration curve framework used is transferable to other conflict-affected settings.

Introduction

Armed conflict poses severe and well-documented threats to health systems, disrupting care through destruction of infrastructure, displacement of health workers, interruption of supply chains and creation of movement barriers that prevent patients from reaching hospitals.1 2 The consequences extend well beyond direct casualties: populations deprived of surgical care, emergency obstetrics and chronic disease management accumulate unmet health needs that persist long after active fighting subsides.3 4 The erosion of functional hospital capacity under conflict conditions represents a form of structural violence that distributes its consequences unevenly across geography, displacing the burden of inaccessibility onto populations already marginalised by distance, poverty and ethnic minority status.5 Since the 2021 military coup, Myanmar has experienced one of the highest rates of attacks on healthcare globally, with more than 1000 documented incidents affecting health facilities and workers.6

A critical but underexamined dimension of conflict-related health system disruption is its interaction with pre-existing geographical barriers to care. In many low- and middle-income countries, specialist hospital services are concentrated in urban centres while rural and border populations face travel times that render referral-level care effectively inaccessible even in peacetime.7 8 Conflict degrades accessibility through at least two distinct mechanisms: physical disruption, including facility destruction, occupation and road impassability, and functional disruption through health worker attrition and supply chain collapse.9 These mechanisms can operate independently or compound one another, yet their joint effect on population-level accessibility has rarely been quantified.10 Whether this compounding occurs systematically, or whether conflict and poor access are geographically independent, has significant implications for humanitarian response and health system recovery planning.

Health equity research has developed rigorous tools for quantifying the concentration of health burdens across population gradients. The concentration curve and concentration index, originally developed to measure income-related health inequalities,11 12 provide a standardised measure of whether a given outcome is disproportionately concentrated among disadvantaged populations. Despite wide application in health economics,13 these tools have not been used to measure whether armed conflict is disproportionately concentrated in populations with worse health service access. Geographical accessibility represents a theoretically distinct dimension of equity from wealth or education.14 A population may be financially able to seek care and sufficiently informed to recognise the need, yet still remain effectively excluded by travel time alone. This barrier may be particularly intensified by conflict, which can simultaneously reduce road passability and facility functionality.15 A recent study of primary healthcare access in four conflict-affected countries used concentration indices to examine educational and wealth disparities in access but did not examine whether conflict itself was distributed inequitably relative to geographical access.16 Evidence from multicountry analyses confirms that conflict countries carry substantially higher burdens of health inequality among their most disadvantaged populations.17 Rigorous measurement of these disparities requires road network based travel time modelling rather than Euclidean distance or administrative catchment ratios, as these approaches systematically underestimate access barriers in areas with sparse or damaged road infrastructure, which are common in conflict affected settings.

Myanmar provides a critical setting for this analysis. A lower-middle-income country of approximately 54 million people (census estimate; WorldPop 2020 raster total: 48.1 million),18 Myanmar entered the February 2021 military coup with deep geographical inequalities in hospital distribution. These inequalities stem from decades of centralised governance, predominantly under military rule from 1962 to 2011, which concentrated health infrastructure in lowland urban centres and neglected highland, border and ethnic minority regions.19 20 Pre-coup analyses document entrenched subnational disparities in essential health services across Myanmar’s states and regions,21 yet population-level estimates of travel-time-based accessibility to facility care remain limited, with existing studies predominantly restricted to primary care or specific disease programmes rather than the broader hospital referral network.22 23 Even during the period of partial civilian government (2016–2021), the military retained control of key ministries and 25% of parliamentary seats, and the structural geography of hospital distribution remained largely unchanged.24

Since the coup, armed conflict has spread across the majority of Myanmar’s states and regions, expanding from historically contested ethnic border areas in Chin, Kachin, Karen and Shan states into previously stable lowland regions including Sagaing, Magway and Mandalay, areas that held comparatively better pre-existing hospital access, creating new and qualitatively distinct patterns of compound deprivation.25 Following the coup, tens of thousands of health workers joined the Civil Disobedience Movement (CDM), refusing to work under military authority in an act of principled professional resistance.26 27 The junta’s response, arrests, revocation of medical licences and violence against striking health workers, converted that refusal into a lasting collapse of public-sector staffing, leaving district and township hospitals critically understaffed or functionally closed for extended periods.28 Many CDM health workers have continued to provide care through ethnic health organisations, National Unity Government-affiliated and community-based systems, often at considerable personal risk.25 29 At the same time, hospitals have been attacked, occupied and destroyed6 30 while roads have also become dangerous or impassable.31 The compounding of infrastructure damage, staff flight and active insecurity has created access deficits that extend far beyond the direct footprint of individual attack events, affecting catchment populations in surrounding townships who depend on the same facilities for referral-level care.32 In non-government-controlled areas, ethnic health organisations provide parallel facility-based care that partially compensates for the collapse of the public system, though this parallel infrastructure falls outside the scope of the present analysis, which measures access to the pre-coup public hospital network, the only network designed to provide universal geographic coverage.33 The rationale for this scoping decision and a robustness analysis addressing its consequences are presented as the sensitivity analyses. Yet no study has quantified the pre-coup access deficit at the population level, tracked how conflict has interacted with this deficit over time or measured the cumulative burden of compound deprivation across Myanmar’s townships.

This study addresses these gaps with three objectives: first, to produce population-weighted national estimates of geographical accessibility to public hospital care in Myanmar from 2021 to 2024 across three tiers of service; second, to quantify whether armed conflict has been disproportionately concentrated in populations with the worst pre-existing hospital access and whether physical attacks on health facilities followed the same pattern, using concentration curves and indices; and third, to measure the cumulative person-years of simultaneous poor access and high-intensity conflict, identifying the highest-priority townships for humanitarian health response.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a longitudinal ecological analysis of Myanmar’s 330 townships across 16 calendar quarters from Q1 2021 to Q4 2024. Baseline hospital accessibility was derived from pre-coup infrastructure and population data and treated as time-invariant, reflecting the assumption that the structural geography of public hospital distribution was established prior to the February 2021 coup and did not meaningfully change in the interval before conflict onset. Two interpretive consequences of this design should be stated at the outset. First, because the road network and facility layer are held at their pre-coup state, the modelled travel times do not capture conflict-related road damage, checkpoint delays, curfews or the danger of travel itself. Realised access during 2021–2024 was therefore likely to have been progressively worse than modelled, particularly in the most contested townships. All accessibility estimates in this paper should accordingly be read as optimistic upper bounds on true access, or equivalently, conservative lower bounds on the access deficit. Second, the population surface also predates post-coup displacement, a limitation of uncertain direction. The direction of each known bias is set out in the Limitations. Conflict intensity was measured at the township-quarter level. The primary unit of analysis was therefore the township-quarter, yielding 5280 observations. This study constitutes a secondary analysis of publicly available, de-identified, aggregate-level data. Patients and the public were not directly involved in the design, conduct or reporting. The analytical framework was designed with input from humanitarian practitioners working in Myanmar. Formal ethical review was not required.

Measures

Six publicly available datasets were integrated (online supplemental material 1).

Hospital facilities

Locations of 1130 public hospitals as of 2020 were obtained from the Myanmar Information Management Unit (MIMU) pre-coup facility registry.34 Facilities were classified into three analytical tiers: Tier 1 (all facilities, n=1130), Tier 2 (township and general hospitals, n=295) and Tier 3 (general hospitals only, n=68). Tier 3 facilities are designated to provide surgery, specialist inpatient care and emergency obstetrics and constituted the primary focus of analysis.20 Public hospital services are officially provided free of charge,35 although out-of-pocket costs for medicines and supplies persist,24 so geographical access remains the dominant modelled barrier to care; private hospitals exist but remain unaffordable for the majority. The outcome measured throughout is therefore access to the pre-coup public hospital network, the only network designed to provide universal geographical coverage and the denominator against which post-conflict recovery will be planned. Facilities operated by ethnic health organisations (EHOs), the National Unity Government (NUG) or private providers were not included in the facility layer, for three reasons: no comprehensive geocoded registry of such facilities exists in the public domain; publishing facility locations would create documented targeting risks in a conflict in which health facilities are attacked6; and we are aware of no published evidence that any EHO or NUG facility inside Myanmar provides the full Tier-3 package of comprehensive surgery, specialist inpatient care and emergency obstetrical care.33 The robustness of the concentration findings to this exclusion is tested in a dedicated, assumption-free leave-out analysis and its direction of bias is recorded in the Limitations.

Administrative boundaries

Township polygons (n=330) covering all 18 states and regions were obtained from MIMU (V.9.4).36

Armed conflict events

Conflict event data were obtained from the Armed Conflict Location and Event Data Project (ACLED).37 ACLED classifies political violence into six event types: battles, explosions/remote violence, violence against civilians, protests, riots and strategic developments.38 We included the two event types constituting armed conflict: battles (subevent types—armed clash, government regains territory, non-state actor overtakes territory) and explosions/remote violence (subevent types—chemical weapon, air/drone strike, suicide bomb, shelling/artillery/missile attack, remote explosive/landmine/IED (improvised explosive device) and grenade) as these capture the armed confrontation environment that disrupts transport networks and facility function; all events in these two types were retained regardless of the intended target. The remaining four event types were excluded. Each event is recorded with a date and location and was assigned to its township; because ACLED codes incidents separately by date and location, a battle lasting several days or spanning several localities appears as multiple events, whereas fatalities are recorded once, on the date they occur. The retained dataset comprised 35 647 events and 67 562 recorded fatalities.

Population

Gridded population estimates at 100 m resolution were obtained from WorldPop 2020 unconstrained data for Myanmar,39 resampled to a 1 km grid using population-preserving areal summation, yielding a national total of 48.1 million. WorldPop constructs its gridded population estimates from the 2014 census frame, disaggregated using satellite-derived covariates. By contrast, World Bank and United Nations estimates for Myanmar, approximately 53–54 million in 2021, are top-down national demographic projections based on different adjustment assumptions, so the figures are not directly comparable. We used WorldPop because the analysis required the spatial distribution of population across a 1 km grid, with all reported estimates population-weighted, rather than a national population total.

Health facility attacks

Facility-level attack data were obtained from the Safeguarding Health in Conflict Coalition (SHCC) for 2021–2024.6 Of 1509 raw events, 795 facility-impact events were retained after excluding 714 worker-only incidents, most of which involved arrests of health workers participating in the CDM. Physical attacks, defined as destruction, damage, forceful entry or occupation, were identified as the primary indicator of facility-level disruption, yielding 655 events with at least one recorded physical attack type. Three events falling outside township boundaries were assigned to the nearest township by minimum Euclidean distance. Because a single event may involve more than one physical attack type (for example, damage and occupation), these 655 events comprise 713 attack-type instances; township-level cumulative counts used in the prioritisation analysis sum attack-type instances.

Road network

Road data were obtained from the OpenStreetMap extract for Myanmar (Geofabrik, March 2026),40 comprising 37 607 classified road segments spanning motorway through tertiary classifications, including link variants. The analysis uses only this major-road skeleton, whose topology in Myanmar was substantially complete in OpenStreetMap before 2021 and changes slowly; post-coup additions to OpenStreetMap Myanmar have been predominantly mapping detail in residential and track classes not used here. No OpenStreetMap vintage records conflict damage: the map describes where roads exist, not whether they are passable, so an extract from any year embodies the same passability assumption. Travel times computed on this network assume pre-coup passability and are optimistic.

Analysis

All analyses were conducted at the township level (n=330) using Python. To address the three study objectives, five sequential analytical steps were undertaken: geographical accessibility estimation; conflict intensity measurement; equity analysis using concentration curves and indices applied to conflict events, fatalities and physical facility attacks; double burden quantification expressed as cumulative person-years; and priority township identification using two complementary classifications.

Geographical accessibility estimation

To quantify the physical barrier to hospital care facing each township’s population, we modelled travel time to the nearest public hospital within each tier using a friction-surface approach.41 A friction surface was constructed by rasterising the OpenStreetMap road network onto a 1 km grid, with travel speeds assigned by road class: motorway 80 km/hour, trunk 70 km/hour, primary 60 km/hour, secondary 45 km/hour and tertiary 30 km/hour, with link variants assigned proportionally lower speeds ranging from 25 to 60 km/hour. Off-road pixels were assigned a walking speed of 5 km/hour, reflecting movement across terrain without classified road access. Minimum travel times from every populated pixel to the nearest hospital within each tier were computed simultaneously using a minimum-cost-path algorithm.42 This approach captures the full within-township heterogeneity of access rather than approximating accessibility from a centroid and is preferred over Euclidean distance methods in settings with sparse or irregular road networks.43

For each township, the population-weighted median travel time was calculated as the primary summary measure of accessibility, defined as the 50th percentile of the within-township population distribution. This statistic weights travel time by where people actually live rather than treating all land area equally, and is robust to outlier pixels at township peripheries. Townships were assigned to one of four accessibility categories: Good (<60 min), Moderate (60–120 min), Poor (120–240 min) and Very Poor (≥240 min), corresponding to established benchmarks for emergency and referral care access.7 44 Tier-3 accessibility was the primary outcome of interest given the clinical significance of surgical and specialist care.

Conflict intensity measurement

To characterise the armed confrontation environment at the township-quarter level, a composite conflict intensity score was computed as the quarter’s raw score (n_events+2 × n_fatalities) plus one-half of the previous quarter’s raw score, a single-lag carryover; contributions do not compound beyond one quarter. A composite measure was used in place of event counts alone because fatality data capture the severity of armed confrontation independent of event recording practices. The 2×fatality weight and the 0.5 single-quarter carryover are our own analytical choices, informed by the battle-deaths literature, which treats battle deaths as a more direct indicator of conflict severity than event counts,45 46 and by the empirically documented persistence of conflict across periods.47 48 Their consequences are quantified in a parameter sensitivity analysis spanning fatality weights 1–3, carryover variants and classification cutpoints. Scores were classified using fixed a priori thresholds: Nil (0), Low (1–9), Moderate (10–29), High (30–99), Extreme (≥100), consistent with the methodological tradition of fixed-cutpoint conflict classification established by the Uppsala Conflict Data Program (UCDP) Armed Conflict Dataset and applied in recent health-conflict studies.49 50 The concentration analyses described below rank townships by travel time and use raw event and fatality counts; they are independent of the intensity score and of all its parameters by construction.

Equity analysis: concentration curves and concentration index

To test whether armed conflict has been disproportionately borne by populations with the worst pre-existing hospital access, we adapted the concentration curve and concentration index framework from health economics.11 12 Townships were ranked by population-weighted Tier-3 travel time from best to worst access. Concentration curves were constructed by plotting the cumulative population share against the cumulative share of each conflict outcome, including total ACLED events, total fatalities and total SHCC physical attacks. Under the null hypothesis that conflict is proportional to population regardless of access, the curve follows the 45° diagonal. A curve below the diagonal indicates disproportionate concentration in worse-access populations.

The concentration index was computed as C=1 − 2 × (area under the concentration curve), with positive values denoting greater conflict burden in populations with worse access.11 95% CIs were derived by non-parametric bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations. To examine whether the relationship between geographical access and conflict burden has intensified, attenuated or remained stable over the study period, the concentration index was computed separately for each of the 16 quarters and plotted as a time series.

Double burden quantification and cumulative person-years

To measure the scale of compound deprivation, the co-occurrence of poor hospital access and active conflict at the population level, we defined a double burden classification and derived a person-years metric that captures both the size of affected populations and the duration of their exposure. A township-quarter was classified as double burden if it simultaneously exhibited Poor or Very Poor Tier-3 accessibility (population-weighted median travel time ≥120 min) and High or Extreme conflict intensity, thresholds chosen to identify populations facing substantive rather than marginal deprivation on both dimensions. The double burden population for each quarter was estimated as the sum of residential populations across all qualifying townships.

For each qualifying township-quarter, the resident population contributed 0.25 person-years of compound deprivation exposure; these were summed across all 16 quarters to produce national, state and township-level estimates of cumulative burden. Three related measures are reported separately: the quarterly double-burden population, a stock representing the number of people living in qualifying townships in a given quarter; quarterly person-years added, a flow equal to 0.25 person-years for each resident of a qualifying township-quarter; and cumulative person-years, the sum of this flow across all quarters. Because person-years capture exposure already experienced, the cumulative total cannot decline: a later reduction in the double-burden population slows further accumulation but does not erase the burden accrued in earlier quarters. This metric integrates population size and temporal persistence into a single interpretable quantity, enabling direct comparison across administrative units of varying size and conflict trajectories.

Priority township identification

To translate the aggregate burden estimates into operationally actionable priorities, we identified townships satisfying two compound-deprivation criteria: Poor or Very Poor Tier-3 accessibility and at least one quarter of High or Extreme conflict intensity, and ranked them by cumulative person-years of compound deprivation; this ranking identifies the populations for whom access-assurance interventions matter most. Within it, a facility-protection subset is indicated: townships additionally recording three or more cumulative SHCC physical attack instances against health facilities, whose facility networks have sustained documented physical damage and where protection instruments apply. Every township that is either among the 15 highest-burden or in the facility-protection subset is ranked by person-years, with subset membership marked. The attack criterion is not used as a burden ranking: consistent with the facility-attack analysis below, it systematically excludes remote high-burden townships that have no facility to attack.

Sensitivity analyses

Three sensitivity analyses accompanied the primary results. First, we varied the conflict-intensity score across 14 specifications, changing the fatality weight (1, 2 or 3), temporal carryover (none, single-lag as published or recursive) and High/Extreme cutpoints (five variants), and recomputed all burden estimates. Second, we repeated the concentration analyses using Tier-1 and Tier-2 accessibility rankings instead of Tier-3. Third, we tested whether the concentration findings depended on areas where excluding parallel providers might distort the access measure by removing those areas from the sample entirely, first excluding all townships in Sagaing, Chin and Kachin, where ethnic health organisation and NUG-affiliated provision is most extensively documented,33 and second, excluding the 15 worst-case townships. This leave-out design is assumption-free by construction because it removes contested observations rather than imputing hypothetical facilities, and therefore requires no assumptions about parallel-provider location, functionality or capacity.

Results

Baseline geographical accessibility to hospital care

Before the coup, access to any public hospital (Tier 1) was relatively widespread, with 93.8% of Myanmar’s 48.1 million people living within 60 min of a facility. Access to general hospitals (the facilities capable of surgery, specialist inpatient care and emergency obstetrics) was substantially more restricted. An estimated 27.6 million people (57%) had Good Tier-3 access, 14.8 million (31%) Moderate, 4.9 million (10%) Poor and 0.8 million (2%) Very Poor, with 5.8 million people (12%) living more than 2 hours from the nearest general hospital. The progressive degradation in accessibility from Tier 1 through Tier 3 is illustrated in online supplemental materials 2 and 3.

Marked subnational variation was evident across states and regions. Nay Pyi Taw (100% Good) and Yangon (92.5% Good) reflected their dense urban hospital networks; no township in Chin fell within the Good access category. Chin hosts three general hospitals: in Hakha, Falam and Mindat, yet even these hosting townships recorded population-weighted medians of 75, 99 and 102 min, respectively, reflecting a population widely dispersed across mountainous terrain with limited road connectivity. Rakhine (39% Poor), Shan North (34% Poor) and Sagaing (25% Poor or Very Poor) each had substantial populations beyond the 2-hour threshold. These deficits preceded the coup and reflect decades of centralised health infrastructure investment concentrated in lowland urban centres (table 1, figure 1).

Temporal trends in compound deprivation

The access-conflict population matrices illustrate the redistribution of exposure over time. In Q1 2021, 22.6 million people had Good access and no conflict exposure; by Q4 2024, only 6.7 million remained in that cell (online supplemental material 4)

The population simultaneously exposed to Poor or Very Poor Tier-3 access and High or Extreme conflict intensity grew from 400 000 (0.8%) in Q1 2021 to a peak of 3.14 million (6.5%) in Q1 2024, before stabilising at 2.36 million (4.9%) by Q4 2024. Three temporal phases were evident. In the first phase (2021), the double burden expanded rapidly as armed resistance emerged in highland regions, particularly Sagaing and Chin, which already had poor baseline access. In the second phase (2022–2023), conflict in these areas became chronic without expanding substantially into new regions. The third phase followed Operation 1027—a coordinated offensive named for its launch date of 27 October 2023, mounted by the Three Brotherhood Alliance (the Myanmar National Democratic Alliance Army, the Ta’ang National Liberation Army and the Arakan Army), which began in northern Shan State, resumed in a second phase from June 2024 and produced the largest territorial shifts of the post-coup conflict,51 52 bringing sharp escalation as intense fighting spread into Rakhine and Shan North, both characterised by large populations and poor pre-existing access (figure 2, online supplemental materials 5 and 6).

Equity distribution of conflict burden by access level

Concentration curve analysis demonstrated that armed conflict was significantly and disproportionately concentrated in populations with the worst pre-existing hospital access: C=0.126 (95% CI 0.053 to 0.199) for conflict events and 0.169 (95% CI 0.078 to 0.263) for fatalities (figure 3). In concrete terms, the worst-access 60% of the population absorbed 72.3% of conflict fatalities (70.0% of events). ACLED’s documented tendency to underreport in remote, access-restricted areas would bias these indices downward, while the exclusion of parallel non-state providers biases modelled access downward in high-conflict states; the leave-out analysis shows the concentration findings do not depend on the areas where the latter bias could operate, and the direction of all known biases is set out in the Limitations. The concentration of conflict in worse-access populations holds at every facility tier, and is stronger when townships are ranked by Tier-1 or Tier-2 access (fatality concentration index 0.228 and 0.225, respectively), so the Tier-3 estimates reported here are the most conservative of the three. It also survives deletion of the most contested areas: excluding all townships of Sagaing, Chin and Kachin, removing 41% of recorded fatalities, left the estimates essentially unchanged (C=0.135, 95% CI 0.049 to 0.215, for events; 0.155, 95% CI 0.041 to 0.272, for fatalities), and excluding the 15 worst-case townships attenuated but did not eliminate the concentration (0.097, 95% CI 0.024 to 0.174; and 0.132, 95% CI 0.036 to 0.235, respectively).

Quarterly values of the concentration index are noisier than the cumulative estimates because they rely on single-quarter denominators. Per-quarter bootstrap CIs, based on 1000 township-resampling iterations, are shown in online supplemental material 7. In Q1 2021, the concentration index for fatalities was high at 0.395 (95% CI 0.082 to 0.612), as initial armed clashes were concentrated in Kachin and peripheral Sagaing townships.53 It declined through mid-2021 as post-coup violence spread to urban crackdowns in comparatively well-served cities. From 2022 onward, as conflict shifted more decisively toward armed resistance in highland areas, the concentration index rose and remained positive in every quarter, with a mean of 0.124 for events and 0.194 for fatalities across all 16 quarters. The sharpest movement occurred in Q3 2024, when the concentration index for fatalities fell to 0.020 from 0.078 in Q2 2024, before rebounding to 0.233 (95% CI 0.100 to 0.359) in Q4 2024. The Q3 2024 CI included zero (95% CI −0.129 to 0.212), indicating that the magnitude of the decline was imprecisely estimated. Notably, the event concentration index did not decline in the same quarter, remaining at 0.191 (95% CI 0.084 to 0.290), suggesting that only the geographical concentration of lethality changed.

This pattern is consistent with a temporary shift in the geography of lethal violence rather than a data artefact. In Q3 2024, recorded fatalities were dominated by the Arakan Army’s coastal Rakhine offensive.54 Maungdaw, with 815 recorded fatalities, and Thandwe, with 766, were both classified as Good-access townships and together accounted for 27.6% of national fatalities that quarter. The Maungdaw total included events involving reported mass civilian deaths among Rohingya communities during the offensive, as recorded by ACLED. Renewed fighting in northern Shan State, particularly in Kyaukme, classified as Good access and Lashio, classified as Moderate access, also contributed.51 Because a small number of mass-fatality events occurred in better-served townships, the fatality gradient temporarily flattened. In Q4 2024, the main centres of lethal violence shifted back toward poorly served townships, including Gwa, Ann, Hakha and Hpapun, and the concentration index rebounded. From late 2023 onward, conflict expanded into better-served areas in addition to, rather than instead of, the persistently affected remote townships. This broader geographical spread explains why the quarterly series became more volatile while the cumulative concentration indices remained positive and significant.

Cumulative burden of compound deprivation

An estimated 8.38 million person-years of simultaneous poor access and high-intensity conflict accumulated between Q1 2021 and Q4 2024. The quarterly rate of accumulation grew from approximately 100 000 person-years in Q1 2021 to 780 000 in Q1 2024, representing an eightfold increase driven by the expansion of conflict into populous, poorly served regions (online supplemental material 8)

Sagaing Region accounted for the largest share at 2.82 million person-years (34%), followed by Kachin (1.54 million, 18%), Kayin (0.94 million, 11%), Mon (0.80 million, 10%), Rakhine (0.70 million, 8%) and Chin (0.50 million, 6%), collectively accounting for 87% of the national total. At the township level, Hpakant (Kachin) recorded the highest cumulative burden at 1.45 million person-years across all 16 quarters. Hpakant alone accounts for 94% of Kachin State’s total burden. It also illustrates the value of population weighting: although its geographical centroid lies 348 min from the nearest general hospital, its population is concentrated along the road network, resulting in a population-weighted median travel time of 174 min. This remains within the Poor-access category but is roughly half the centroid-based estimate. Full state and township breakdowns are provided in online supplemental material 9.

Geographical and structural variation in health facility attacks

Physical attacks on health facilities showed no significant concentration by access level: C=0.049 (95% CI −0.041 to 0.145), substantially lower than the indices for conflict events or fatalities and not statistically significant. This pattern is consistent with a structural feature of the network. Of the 64 townships classified as Poor or Very Poor access, 62 (96.9%) contain no Tier-3 facility within their boundaries, including all 12 Very Poor townships and 50 of the 52 Poor townships. These 62 townships account for 5.7 of the 5.8 million people (99.0%) living in the Poor or Very Poor access categories. The two exceptions, Hkamti in Sagaing and Matman in Shan North, each contain a general hospital but still have population-weighted median travel times above 120 min because their populations are widely dispersed. By comparison, 36% of Good-access townships contain at least one Tier-3 facility. Hospital attacks therefore cannot concentrate in townships where no hospital is present. The absence of concentration in facility attacks was consistent across all three facility tiers (online supplemental material 10).

Subnational and township-level prioritisation

37 townships met the compound-deprivation criteria of Poor or Very Poor access and at least one quarter of High or Extreme conflict. Table 2 presents the 21 townships that were either among the 15 highest-burden townships or part of the facility-protection subset, ranked by cumulative person-years. The highest burdens were recorded in Hpakant (Kachin, 1.45 million person-years, in double burden for all 16 quarters), Kyainseikgyi (Kayin, 0.80 million, 13 quarters), Ye (Mon, 0.80 million, 14 quarters), Homalin (Sagaing, Very Poor access, 0.63 million, 10 quarters) and Kawlin (Sagaing, 0.49 million, 14 quarters). Together, these townships span nine states and regions across Myanmar’s periphery, showing that the compound crisis is nationally distributed rather than confined to a single region. Temporal persistence also varied in operationally important ways. Hpakant remained in double burden throughout all 16 quarters, whereas the Rakhine townships entered the category episodically, with Mrauk-U and Minbya each affected in 5 of 16 quarters, suggesting that chronic and episodic compound deprivation require different response strategies. The facility-protection subset comprises the 15 townships that also recorded at least three documented physical attack instances, and its membership was unchanged across all 14 conflict-intensity parameter specifications. Six high-burden townships fell outside this subset because they recorded fewer than three documented attacks. Homalin alone accumulated 626 000 person-years yet records only one documented facility attack, consistent with the facility-attack analysis showing that townships with little facility infrastructure record few facility attacks. Smaller chronically affected townships also remain visible through the quarters-in-burden measure. Hpapun (Kayin), for example, was in double burden in all 16 quarters but ranked lower on cumulative person-years because of its small population, not because its deprivation was less severe (table 2; online supplemental materials 11 and 12).

Discussion

This study provides the first population-weighted estimates of geographical accessibility to public hospital care in Myanmar and the first application of concentration curve analysis to quantify whether armed conflict compounds pre-existing health access inequality. Three principal findings emerged from this analysis. Throughout, we distinguish between what the data show, namely where recorded conflict fell relative to pre-coup access, and the mechanisms that ecological data cannot determine.

The 5.8 million people living more than 2 hours from a general hospital before the coup were concentrated in highland, border and ethnic minority regions—Chin, Rakhine and Shan States, where health infrastructure investment has historically been lowest. This structural geography is the legacy of decades of centralised governance, predominantly under military rule since 1962, which concentrated hospitals in Yangon, Mandalay and Nay Pyi Taw while peripheral regions were neglected. The access deficit is not a consequence of the current conflict but a pre-existing condition that conflict has exposed and compounded. This has a direct implication for recovery: even a complete cessation of hostilities would leave millions without realistic access to surgical or specialist care. Health system reconstruction must address the structural deficit, not merely restore the pre-coup status quo.

Our analysis measures access only to MIMU-registered public hospitals. In conflict-affected areas, ethnic health organisations, including the Karen Department of Health and Welfare, the health department of the Kachin Independence Organization, the Back Pack Health Worker Team and the Burma Medical Association, provide much of the day-to-day care for large populations. They operate village-tract health centres, mobile teams and a limited number of fixed clinics and secondary-level facilities under exceptionally difficult conditions.33 Since the coup, the National Unity Government has also established mobile clinics, telemedicine services and temporary hospitals.29 This care is real and, at the primary-care level, is often the only care available, with many services delivered by CDM health workers at considerable personal risk. However, these parallel systems do not provide comprehensive surgical, specialist inpatient and emergency obstetric capacity equivalent to a general hospital. The Karen Department of Health and Welfare, among the most established and best-documented EHO systems, reports fixed facilities reaching only a small secondary-care level within a predominantly primary-care network serving over 350 000 people,55 while Mae Tao Clinic, the best-known border facility, is located in Mae Sot, Thailand, and refers complex surgical cases to Thai hospitals.56

Our focus on Tier-3 access should therefore not be interpreted as suggesting that the crisis is confined to hospital care. The post-coup collapse in staffing, supplies and security has also severely affected Tier-1 and Tier-2 services, contributing to documented crises in routine immunisation,21 maternal and child health and continuity of care for cancer, diabetes, hypertension and kidney disease.32 We focus on Tier-3 because surgical, specialist and comprehensive emergency obstetric care is the highest-threshold, most spatially concentrated and least substitutable layer of the system. The Tier-3 deficit measured here should therefore be understood as a sentinel indicator of the minimum extent of the crisis, rather than a complete measure of its overall impact.

Rather than imputing hypothetical facilities in contested areas, which would require unverifiable assumptions about their location, functionality and capacity, we tested whether our findings depended on those areas. In our leave-out robustness analysis, excluding all townships in Sagaing, Chin and Kachin left the fatality concentration index essentially unchanged (0.155 vs 0.169 in the full sample), and excluding the 15 worst-case townships attenuated but did not eliminate it. The concentration of conflict among populations with worse access is therefore not driven by the townships or states where excluding parallel providers could most distort the access measure. The access deficit measured here is specific to the pre-coup public hospital network, the only network designed for universal geographical coverage, and should be interpreted alongside, rather than as a substitute for, the parallel systems that partially address the primary-care gap.

The significant concentration index for fatalities (0.169, 95% CI 0.078 to 0.263) demonstrates that the most lethal violence has fallen disproportionately on the populations furthest from hospital care. This finding does not imply that conflict deliberately targets medically underserved communities. Rather, it reflects multiple converging dynamics that produce the same statistical signature. In ethnic minority states along Myanmar’s periphery—Kachin, Kayin, Chin, Rakhine, Shan, both the deficit in health infrastructure and the presence of armed opposition groups are long-standing features of regions that have been at the margins of central state governance for decades. The overlap between poor access and armed conflict here reflects shared historical marginalisation rather than independent geographical coincidence.

Topography should also be considered among these converging dynamics. Rugged terrain can provide cover, defensibility and sanctuary that favour insurgency, a relationship well established in the civil-war literature,57 and much of Myanmar’s contested upland periphery fits this pattern. We are more cautious, however, about treating topography as a common cause of both conflict and poor hospital access. Myanmar’s hospital geography primarily reflects decades of centralised investment that concentrated infrastructure in lowland urban centres while neglecting border and ethnic-minority regions.19 20 Mountainous areas are underserved, but so are accessible areas that successive governments did not prioritise. Sagaing Region is the clearest counterexample: although it contributed 34% of national person-years of compound deprivation, many of its most affected townships lie in lowland dry-zone terrain. We therefore treat terrain as one of several plausible influences on conflict geography rather than as a shared cause of both conditions. The concentration index does not distinguish among these mechanisms; it measures where the burden fell, not why, and the resulting compound deprivation is the same under any causal explanation.

In predominantly Bamar regions such as Sagaing, post-coup resistance forces established their operational bases in rural terrain that offered strategic advantage such as cover, distance from military garrisons, proximity to ethnic armed organisations, which is also terrain that is inherently difficult to serve with hospital infrastructure. As conflict expanded geographically, particularly following Operation 1027 (October 2023),51 it progressively engulfed regions in Rakhine and Shan North whose poor access reflected geographical remoteness. Regardless of the underlying mechanism, the consequence for affected populations is the same: simultaneous exposure to armed conflict and structural exclusion from hospital care. The concentration index quantifies this compounding with a single, standardised measure that can be tracked over time and compared across settings.

The access–conflict matrices reveal an additional development that the aggregate index, by construction, summarises but does not show directly: by Q4 2024, approximately 4.2 million people lived in townships with Good modelled access but Extreme conflict intensity (online supplemental material 4). This does not contradict the concentration finding, because the concentration index measures the distribution of conflict across the full access gradient and remained positive in every quarter. Instead, it indicates the emergence of a second front in the compound crisis. During 2021–2023, high-intensity conflict was concentrated mainly in the historically underserved periphery. The growth of the Good access and Extreme conflict cell from mid-2024 coincided with the second phase of Operation 1027, which brought extreme violence into comparatively well-served towns. The Three Brotherhood Alliance captured Kyaukme, Hsipaw, and, on 3 August 2024, Lashio, the largest town in northern Shan State and the seat of the military’s Northeastern Regional Command. Fighting also intensified around towns in Mandalay Region, while the Arakan Army’s offensive in Rakhine culminated in the capture of Ann, headquarters of the Western Command, on 20 December 2024.52 58 Two implications follow. First, the compound burden broadened rather than shifted: violence expanded into better-served areas while remaining disproportionately concentrated, per capita, in poorly served populations in every quarter. Second, ‘Good’ modelled access in these newly contested townships may be largely nominal. A general hospital located within a besieged, occupied or depopulated town may be geographically close but functionally unreachable. The fixed pre-coup access layer therefore likely overstates realised access most severely in these settings, reinforcing the optimistic-bound interpretation described in the Methods.

The eightfold increase in the quarterly rate of person-year accumulation from 100 000 in Q1 2021 to 780 000 in Q1 2024 is a warning about the trajectory of this crisis. If conflict intensity remains at current levels, another 2–3 million person-years of compound deprivation will accumulate annually. The health consequences including excess maternal mortality from lack of obstetrical care, preventable trauma deaths, progression of chronic conditions, will continue to materialise long after any ceasefire, because health system capacity cannot be rebuilt overnight.3 Myanmar’s path to health system recovery will require sustained investment in the most underserved regions whether delivered through ethnic health organisations, NUG-affiliated services, or international humanitarian actors.59

The contrast between the significant concentration of conflict burden, with concentration indices of 0.126 for events and 0.169 for fatalities, and the absence of significant concentration for physical facility attacks, with an index of 0.049, rests on one clear structural fact: 62 of the 64 townships with Poor or Very Poor Tier-3 access contain no Tier-3 facility within their boundaries. At least two mechanisms could produce this flat attack curve, and our data cannot fully distinguish between them. The first is target availability, because attacks on hospitals require hospitals to be present and therefore tend to cluster in semi-urban townships where facilities exist. The second is differential documentation, since the SHCC dataset depends on reporting networks and the worst-access townships are also those most affected by internet shutdowns and limited monitoring presence, meaning attacks on smaller facilities may go unrecorded. Although SHCC source events include violence against both health personnel and facilities, our indicator was restricted to physical facility impacts, including destruction, damage, forced entry and occupation, and the completeness of this subset still depends on where documentation is possible. The operational conclusion does not depend on which mechanism dominates. Facility-protection instruments, including documentation, international humanitarian law advocacy, accountability and deconfliction, primarily engage townships where facilities and reporting networks exist, but cannot by themselves reach populations bearing the greatest compound burden, who often live in townships with no Tier-3 facility to protect and the weakest documentation of what happens to the services they do have. Ensuring access for these populations requires a different toolkit, including mobile surgical and emergency obstetrical capacity, task-shifted community-based care, pre-positioned supplies, patient transport and referral pathways and support for ethnic and community-based health organisations operating across front lines, where the binding constraint is negotiated humanitarian access rather than accountability. Humanitarian actors that allocate resources according to documented attacks alone may therefore direct protection efforts toward better-served and better-documented townships while overlooking where unmet need accumulates. Table 2 reflects this distinction by ranking all priority townships according to compound-deprivation burden while separately marking the subset where the facility-protection agenda applies.

The application of concentration curves and indices to quantify whether conflict burden is distributed inequitably relative to health access is, to our knowledge, novel. A recent study used concentration indices to examine wealth and educational disparities in primary healthcare access across four conflict-affected countries, but ranked populations by socioeconomic status rather than by geographical access, and measured access disparities rather than conflict distribution.16 We took a different approach, ranking populations by health access disadvantage and measuring whether conflict itself is concentrated among the most disadvantaged. This framework is transferable to conflict settings where a credible pre-conflict facility baseline is available alongside road, population and conflict-event data. Three of the four required inputs, conflict events from ACLED or UCDP, gridded population from WorldPop and road networks from OpenStreetMap, are available for nearly all active conflicts. The main constraint is the quality and recency of the facility baseline. Ukraine maintained comprehensive national facility registries before the 2022 invasion, including the eHealth registry of healthcare institutions operational since 201860 61; Sudan’s facility network has been documented through WHO Health Resources and Services Availability Monitoring System (HeRAMS) assessments62 and a pre-war master facility list9; the Democratic Republic of the Congo’s District Health Information Software 2 (DHIS2)-based national health information system provides facility inventories across its 519 health zones63 64; and WHO and Ministry of Health facility lists are available for the occupied Palestinian territory.65 66 As in Myanmar, however, substantial care in each setting is also delivered by non-state, community and humanitarian providers. The scoping and sensitivity approach used here would therefore need to be applied separately in each context by defining the measured construct as access to the pre-conflict state network and explicitly assessing the consequences of excluding parallel providers.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, travel times were computed using a road network and facility layer fixed at their pre-coup state, so they do not capture conflict-related road damage, checkpoint delays, curfews or the danger of travel itself. Our friction-surface simplifications, including roads rasterised using an inclusive all-touched rule and a uniform 5 km/hour off-road walking speed, may further flatten travel times. Modelled access therefore likely overstates true access, increasingly over 2021–2024 and most strongly in contested townships. Second, functional degradation is not captured. The staffing collapse following repression of CDM health workers, extended facility closures, supply disruptions and avoidance of military-controlled facilities mean that a facility counted as present was often not fully usable. Third, ACLED under-reports events in the most remote and access-restricted areas, which would bias the concentration index downward. Fourth, the WorldPop 2020 total is lower than UN and World Bank population estimates, reducing the absolute person-year totals, although the relative distribution, and hence the concentration index, is largely unaffected. Population denominators are fixed at WorldPop 2020 and therefore predate the internal displacement of an estimated 3.5 million people by December 2024,31 as well as substantial emigration. Because displacement has been multidirectional and incompletely measured,67 68 movement toward poorly served areas would cause underestimation, whereas movement toward better-served areas or out of the country would have the opposite effect. We therefore treat this bias as having an unknown direction, with concentration estimates affected through population weights and absolute person-year totals interpreted as conditional on the 2020 population distribution.

Two biases run in the opposite direction. The exclusion of parallel EHO and NUG providers may overstate the access deficit where they operate, although the leave-out analysis in the sensitivity analyses shows that the concentration findings do not depend on those areas. Incomplete OpenStreetMap coverage of minor rural roads may also overstate travel times in peripheral townships, although this is mitigated by our use of population-weighted medians. Two further biases cannot be signed: population displacement, given the multidirectional nature of documented flows and SHCC documentation coverage, which is weakest where modelled access is worst. Overall, the identifiable biases predominantly point toward understatement of the compound burden, although two run in the opposite direction and two cannot be signed.

Conclusion

Armed conflict in Myanmar since the February 2021 military coup has not fallen randomly across the population. It has been significantly and persistently concentrated in the communities that were already furthest from hospital care, a compounding driven by converging dynamics of historical peripheral neglect and the strategic geography of armed resistance. The resulting burden, 8.4 million person-years of simultaneous poor access and high-intensity conflict accumulating at an accelerating rate, represents a humanitarian crisis whose health consequences will persist for years beyond any cessation of hostilities. The 21 priority townships identified here span nine states and regions and warrant immediate prioritisation for humanitarian health intervention. They are ranked by cumulative compound-deprivation burden, with the 15 townships whose facility networks sustained documented physical damage separately indicated. The health consequences of this compound deprivation will outlast the conflict itself, accumulating in the form of unmet surgical need, preventable maternal deaths and undertreated chronic disease across communities already structurally isolated from care. Beyond Myanmar, the concentration curve and person-year approach demonstrated here provides a transferable analytical template for quantifying where conflict and geographical inequity converge, and for directing humanitarian health resources toward those who bear the greatest cumulative burden.
