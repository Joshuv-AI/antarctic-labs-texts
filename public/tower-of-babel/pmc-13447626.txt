A real-world, bottom-up cost of illness analysis of type 2 diabetes mellitus from a tertiary hospital in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Saleh Alyahya, Saif Alhawwashi, Saad Saeed Alqhatani, Mohammed Ayoob Asseri, Afaf Alshehri, Najla Saleh, Khalid Alshahrani, Amjad Alqahtani, Abdulmohsen Yahya Asseri, Ali Alshahrani, Bander Albalkhi, Abdullah Mohammed Al Shahrani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a major contributor to global morbidity, mortality, and healthcare expenditure. In the context of Saudi Arabia, it is concerningly notable that the prevalence of T2DM is particularly high and rising. Existing cost estimates have previously relied rely on aggregate methodologies, potentially an approach that limits their usefulness for hospital-level decision-making. Accordingly, the primary goal in the current study was to estimate the direct medical costs of T2DM using a real-world, bottom-up micro-costing approach. We carried out a retrospective, cross-sectional cost-of-illness study using electronic medical records from a tertiary hospital in Saudi Arabia. The data collection happened between January and December, 2024. We included adult patients with T2DM, and we calculated direct healthcare costs from the hospital payer perspective, including outpatient care, hospitalizations, laboratory investigations, and medications. In data analysis, we used descriptive statistics, one-way ANOVA, and log-transformed ordinary least squares regression in order to assess cost differences and identify predictors of total cost. A total of 431 patients participated and findings showed that the mean annual cost per patient was SAR 15,396.24 (4,103 USD), and a total cost of SAR 6,635,779.57 (1,768,209 USD). Medications (52.9%) and laboratory tests (40.6%) were the main cost drivers, and higher costs were significantly associated with elevated glycated hemoglobin levels (β = 0.067, p < 0.001), longer disease duration (β = 0.019, p = 0.003), and greater number of comorbidities (β = 0.070, p < 0.001). Going from the above findings, we concluded that T2DM imposes a substantial economic burden on tertiary healthcare systems, importantly indicating that improving glycemic control and managing comorbidities may reduce costs and enhance healthcare efficiency.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Cost of illness, Micro-costing, Healthcare costs, Glycemic control, Comorbidities, Saudi Arabia

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a major global public health challenge and a leading contributor to morbidity, mortality, and healthcare expenditure worldwide (AlHarbi et al. 2024). Characterized by chronic hyperglycemia which results from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both, DM disrupts carbohydrate, fat, and protein metabolism (Alhowaish 2013). Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is the most prevalent form, accounting for approximately 90–95% of all diagnosed diabetes cases globally (Alhowaish 2013). An important aspect to note here is that T2DM is largely preventable and modifiable, essentially with key risk factors including obesity, physical inactivity, poor diet, and aging populations which are factors that when put together make T2DM a critical target for public health interventions and cost-containment strategies (Alhowaish 2013).

The global prevalence of T2DM has increased substantially over recent decades, particularly in developing and middle-income countries. Estimates from the World Health Organization (WHO) and the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) indicate that nearly 500 million adults aged 20–79 years are currently living with diabetes, with projections rising to approximately 648 million by 2040 (Alhowaish 2013). This escalation represents a growing strain on healthcare systems worldwide. In Saudi Arabia, the burden of T2DM is especially pronounced (Mokdad et al., 2015). A systematic review estimated a national prevalence of approximately 32.8%, with projections reaching 40.37% by 2025 and 45.36% by 2030 (Banerji and Dunn 2013). These figures place Saudi Arabia among the countries with the highest diabetes prevalence globally, driven by rapid urbanization, sedentary lifestyles, unhealthy dietary patterns, rising obesity rates, and genetic susceptibility (Banerji and Dunn 2013). The rapid epidemiological transition in Saudi Arabia has further served to accelerated this very trend with lifestyle modernization contributing to earlier disease onset and increased long-term healthcare utilization.

T2DM is frequently accompanied by multiple comorbidities, defined here as the presence of two or more diseases occurring together with another disease, in this case type two diabetes which is the primary condition of focus (American Diabetes Association 2018). These comorbidities of type two diabetes include but are not limited to hypertension, ischemic heart disease, chronic kidney disease, hyperlipidaemia, cerebrovascular disease, and other metabolic disorders, which substantially increase disease complexity and healthcare costs (American Diabetes Association 2018). The presence of these comorbidities is associated with higher healthcare utilization, increased hospitalization rates, and greater pharmacotherapy as well as diagnostic demands (American Diabetes Association 2018). In an notable way, this clustering of conditions not only complicates clinical management but in essence, also makes it necessary for multidisciplinary care models which on the overall, further intensify resource utilization and total treatment costs.

The economic burden of diabetes and its complications is considerable. In 2011, diabetes-related healthcare expenditure accounted for approximately 11% of global healthcare spending, with projected costs reaching USD 595 billion by 2030 (Fan 2017). In Saudi Arabia, the financial burden has intensified over the past two decades due to rising prevalence and expanded healthcare utilization, importantly pointing to the need for efficient resource allocation and evidence-based cost-containment strategies (Fan 2017). Importantly, a substantial proportion of diabetes-related expenditure is attributable to preventable complications which is an aspect that indicates the economic value of early detection and effective disease management (Fan 2017).

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) represents not only a major clinical challenge but also a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems. While international benchmarks, such as those reported by the American Diabetes Association, fundamentally highlighting the multi-billion-dollar cost of diabetes care, localized estimates are essential for informing health system planning, particularly in regions experiencing rapid epidemiological transition, such as Saudi Arabia.

Despite a growing body of Saudi-based literature examining the economic burden of T2DM, important methodological limitations persist. Most previous studies have relied on top-down approaches using national expenditure aggregates, insurance claims data, or modeled assumptions that may obscure the complexity of hospital-level resource utilization (Fan 2017; Kanan et al. 2024). While such approaches provide broad national estimates, they often lack the granularity required to reflect contemporary clinical practice or to support operational decision-making at the hospital level. Furthermore, the substantial lack of patient-level granularity in these studies serves to inevitably limit the ability to identify key cost drivers and prioritize high-impact clinical and policy interventions.

Aggregate-based costing methods often overlook differences in drug use, lab monitoring, outpatient care, and inpatient patterns that significantly affect healthcare costs in practice. Additionally, heterogeneity in costing methodologies and reliance on outdated data limit the applicability of prior estimates for current budgeting, formulary management, and resource allocation decisions within Saudi healthcare institutions.

To address these gaps, this study employs a bottom-up, micro-costing approach, considered a gold-standard method for high-precision economic evaluation and recommended by Drummond et al. and the World Health Organization. Using real-world, patient-level data from a major tertiary hospital in Saudi Arabia, this analysis provides a detailed disaggregation of direct medical costs associated with T2DM, including acute care (hospitalizations and emergency visits), maintenance care (outpatient consultations and laboratory investigations), and pharmacotherapy.

By capturing actual healthcare resource utilization rather than relying on modeled assumptions, this study offers a contemporary and granular estimate of T2DM-related costs from the hospital payer perspective. The findings aim to support formulary committees, hospital administrators, and policymakers as they navigate the Saudi Health Sector Transformation Program, ensuring that resource allocation decisions are both economically efficient and aligned with real-world clinical needs.

Study design and population

This retrospective cross-sectional, prevalence-based, single-center cost-of-illness (COI) study was conducted at Armed Forces Hospital Southern Region (AFHSR) in Khamis Mushait to assess the economic burden of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Electronic medical records (EMRs) were used to identify all patients diagnosed with T2DM, aged 18 years and above, based on the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD-10). The study followed the hospital payer perspective to estimate direct medical costs over one year, covering the period from January 2024 to December 2024. A micro-costing, bottom-up approach was applied to quantify the healthcare resources consumed for diabetes management at the individual patient level. More particularly, we employed a bottom-up micro-costing, whereby each unit of healthcare resource utilization including but not limited to visits, tests, medications, was individually identified, quantified, and multiplied by its respective unit cost. Exclusion criteria have been applied to patients with Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) and Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM), as well as individuals diagnosed with diabetes or who had begun antidiabetic treatment less than one year before the study. Patients included were those with at least one year of continuous follow-up data to ensure complete cost capture.

Data collection

Patient demographics and disease characteristics were extracted from EMRs, including medical record number (MRN), gender, age, and duration of disease. The number of comorbidities was recorded. Healthcare utilization data included outpatient visits, emergency room (ER) visits, hospitalizations, intensive care unit (ICU) admissions, operating room (OR) procedures, and other services such as foot care and annual eye exams.

Laboratory investigations included fasting blood sugar, glycated hemoglobin (A1C), lipid profile, renal function tests, liver function tests, vitamin B12 levels, uric acid, serum creatinine, complete blood count (CBC), coagulation profile, thyroid function tests, bone profile, and vitamin D levels. Medication usage was assessed, including various antidiabetic therapies, and other medications for comorbid conditions.

In accordance with hospital payer perspective, cost estimation included direct healthcare costs associated with diabetes management, covering hospitalization, outpatient visits, laboratory tests, procedures, and medications. Costing data was obtained from AFHSR’s financial department. The total direct cost per patient was calculated by multiplying unit costs by the quantity of resources used. To improve generalizability, weighted averages were applied, cost were presented in Saudi Riyal. Prior to the data collection, the methods and procedures for this study were reviewed and approved by the Local Research Ethics Committee (LREC) under the Reference Number H-06-KM-001, Approval Number 830.

Statistical analysis

Data description

Patient demographics, clinical characteristics, and healthcare cost components were summarized using descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations (SD), and frequency distributions. The distribution of total healthcare costs was assessed for normality using histograms and skewness statistics, revealing a positively skewed distribution. To address this, a log transformation was applied to the cost variable prior to regression analysis. In addition, this Log transformation was useful for addressing right-skewness commonly observed in healthcare cost data and to meet regression model assumptions. Model assumptions including multicollinearity, homoscedasticity, and normality of residuals, were assessed prior to final model interpretation. Categorical variables were dummy coded prior to regression analysis, and records with missing key cost or clinical variables were excluded from the analysis.

Cost comparisons

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare average healthcare costs across clinically relevant subgroups, including A1C levels, duration of diabetes, and number of comorbidities. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Cost predictors

A log-transformed ordinary least squares (OLS) regression model was used to identify independent predictors of total healthcare cost. The model included age, gender, A1C levels, duration of disease, and number of comorbidities. Regression results are presented as beta coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p-values. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 431 patients were included in the study, with a mean age of 63.69 years (SD = 15.04) and a mean A1c of 7.9 (SD = 1.47). The cohort consisted of 269 females (62%) and 162 males (38%). Most patients had an A1c greater than 7, with 288 patients (66.8%) falling into this category, while 143 patients (33.2%) had an A1c less than 7. The mean number of comorbidities was 12.54 (SD = 4.23), with 145 patients (33.7%) having diabetes for 5 years or less, and 286 patients (66.3%) having diabetes for more than 5 years. Noting the high mean number of comorbidities was high (M = 12.54, SD = 4.23) which suggested a right-skewed distribution with potential outliers, we further carried out sensitivity analyses using winsorized counts capped at 10 and also categorized comorbidity burden (0–2, 3–5, 6–9, ≥ 10), as well as log-transformed counts. Results were materially unchanged across specifications, an indication that the findings were robust to alternative handling of comorbidity burden (Table 1).

Cost drivers in diabetes management

To investigate the determinants of total healthcare costs in patients with diabetes, we conducted a log-transformed ordinary least squares (OLS) regression analysis. This modeling approach was chosen due to the positively skewed distribution of cost data, which is common in healthcare expenditure studies. The model explained approximately 13.6% of the variance in the log-transformed costs (R2 = 0.136), indicating a moderate fit.

The analysis identified glycated hemoglobin (A1C) levels, duration of disease, and the number of comorbidities as significant predictors of higher healthcare costs. Specifically, each one-unit increase in A1C was associated with a 6.9% increase in total healthcare costs (β = 0.067, p < 0.001), emphasizing the financial implications of poor glycemic control. Likewise, a longer duration of diabetes was significantly associated with greater costs; each additional year since diagnosis corresponded to a 1.9% increase in cost (β = 0.019, p = 0.003). Additionally, the number of comorbidities emerged as a strong cost driver, with each added comorbid condition contributing to a 7.2% increase in healthcare expenditures (β = 0.070, p < 0.001).

In contrast, patient age and gender were not significantly associated with variations in total cost (p = 0.418 and p = 0.507, respectively), suggesting that clinical characteristics may play a more prominent role than demographic factors in explaining cost differences within this population.

Discussion

Summary of main findings

This study provides a comprehensive, real-world assessment of the direct medical costs associated with managing type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) at a major tertiary care hospital in Saudi Arabia using a bottom-up, micro-costing approach. The average annual direct medical cost was estimated at SAR 15,396.24 per patient, highlighting the substantial economic burden imposed by T2DM on hospital payers. Pharmacotherapy emerged as the dominant cost component, accounting for 52.9% of total expenditures, followed by diagnostic laboratory services (40.6%). These findings underscore the resource-intensive nature of diabetes management and provide a granular depiction of healthcare utilization that aggregate, top-down models often fail to capture.

While the overall pattern of cost concentration in medications and monitoring aligns with global evidence indicating that diabetes-related complications and long-term disease management drive healthcare spending, notable differences were observed when compared with earlier Saudi-based studies. In particular, the higher per-patient costs observed in this study relative to historical national estimates likely reflect methodological differences, inflation, evolving clinical practice, and increased use of newer antidiabetic therapies. Unlike top-down national estimates that provide a broad overview, the bottom-up approach used in this study captures actual hospital-level resource utilization, offering more precise and decision-relevant cost estimates for tertiary care settings (Fan 2017; Kanan et al. 2024; Meo 2016).

Interpretation of key cost drivers

A key contribution of this study is the identification of clinical factors associated with higher healthcare costs among patients with T2DM. Poor glycemic control emerged as a major cost driver, with patients exhibiting uncontrolled diabetes (A1C ≥ 7%) incurring substantially higher annual costs (SAR 17,212.06) compared with those achieving better glycemic control (SAR 11,739.21). This finding is consistent with previous research demonstrating that elevated A1C levels are associated with increased healthcare utilization, higher rates of complications, and greater expenditure (Sicras-Mainar et al. 2014; Stratton 2000).

The economic importance of glycemic control is well established in the literature. The UK Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS 35) demonstrated that each 1% reduction in HbA1c is associated with a 21% decrease in diabetes-related endpoints and a 37% reduction in microvascular complications (Tran et al. 2022). These clinical benefits translate into meaningful economic savings, reinforcing the value of early and sustained glycemic management not only as a clinical priority but also as a cost-containment strategy.

Disease duration was also a significant determinant of cost. Patients with diabetes for more than five years incurred approximately 20% higher healthcare costs than those with a shorter disease duration. This finding reflects the progressive nature of T2DM, whereby cumulative metabolic deterioration over time leads to more complex clinical presentations, greater medication requirements, and intensified monitoring and specialist care (Zhang et al. 2010). Further, diagnostic laboratory services accounted for more than 40% of total direct medical costs, indicating a substantial resource burden associated with routine monitoring of patients with type 2 diabetes (Kanan et al. 2024). This high utilization, as earlier indicated, is reflective of the repeated ordering of tests such as glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), renal function panels, and lipid profiles among others, some of which were performed more frequently than recommended by clinical guidelines. Accordingly, these are results indicating an opportunity for laboratory stewardship interventions including adherence to evidence-based testing intervals, implementation of electronic decision-support tools, and periodic audit-and-feedback to clinicians. Notably, optimizing test utilization could reduce unnecessary expenditures while maintaining the quality and safety of diabetes care.

In addition, multimorbidity substantially amplified healthcare expenditures. Each additional comorbid condition was associated with a 7.2% increase in total costs, consistent with international evidence demonstrating that coexisting conditions such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular disease markedly increase healthcare utilization among individuals with diabetes (Zhuo et al. 2013). These findings emphasize the economic consequences of multimorbidity and the importance of integrated, patient-centered care models.

Comparison with previous literature

When compared with earlier national estimates, the per-patient cost identified in this study is notably higher. For example, Alhowaish (Fan 2017) reported an average annual cost of SAR 9,506 per patient based on data from 1992 to 2010. The lower historical estimates likely reflect differences in costing methodology, inflation, expanded access to healthcare services, and the increasing adoption of newer, and often more expensive, antidiabetic medications. More recent longitudinal work by AlHarbi et al. (Saydah et al. 2004) estimated lifetime costs ranging from SAR 201,377 to SAR 437,371 depending on complication severity, underscoring the substantial long-term economic burden associated with T2DM progression.

Internationally, the average per-patient cost observed in this study remains lower than that reported in high-income countries such as the United States, where the American Diabetes Association estimated annual medical expenditures of approximately USD 9,601 (around SAR 36,000) per patient with diagnosed diabetes in 2017 (Newman et al. 2021). These differences likely reflect variation in healthcare financing systems, pricing regulations, service delivery models, and the payer perspective adopted in this analysis, which excluded indirect costs such as productivity loss. In addition, an important factor directly impacting the increase in cost burden is medication adherence as high medication adherence directly links to short-term pharmacy costs due to higher, consistent expenditure on medication (Rohatgi et al. 2021).

Policy and clinical implications

The findings of this study carry important implications for healthcare policy and clinical practice in Saudi Arabia. The predominance of medication and laboratory costs highlights the need for strategic formulary management and ongoing evaluation of the cost-effectiveness of newer antidiabetic agents, including DPP-4 inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors, and GLP-1 receptor agonists (Ng et al. 2014). While these therapies may offer clinical benefits, their economic impact must be carefully balanced against budgetary constraints.

Moreover, the strong association between poor glycemic control and higher healthcare costs supports a “spending to save” approach, whereby investment in early therapeutic intensification, patient education, lifestyle interventions, and routine monitoring may reduce the downstream costs associated with complications and hospitalizations. These insights are particularly relevant within the context of Saudi Vision 2030 and the Health Sector Transformation Program, which emphasize value-based care, efficiency, and sustainability. Targeting high-impact areas such as complication prevention and optimized outpatient management may yield both clinical and fiscal benefits.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the use of a bottom-up, micro-costing methodology based on real-world electronic medical record data, which provides a level of precision and internal validity that modeled, aggregate approaches cannot achieve. This enhances the relevance of the findings for hospital administrators and formulary decision-makers.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analysis was confined to direct medical costs from the hospital payer perspective and did not account for indirect costs such as productivity loss, caregiver burden, or reduced quality of life, likely resulting in an underestimation of the total societal burden of T2DM. Second, as a single-center study conducted in a military-affiliated tertiary hospital, generalizability to primary care, rural, or private-sector settings may be limited. Despite these constraints, the use of comprehensive patient-level data strengthens the study’s applicability to similar tertiary care contexts across Saudi Arabia.

Conclusion

This bottom-up, real-world cost-of-illness analysis demonstrates that T2DM imposes a substantial economic burden on tertiary-care hospitals in Saudi Arabia, driven primarily by pharmacotherapy, laboratory monitoring, poor glycemic control, longer disease duration, and multimorbidity. The current study provides a fundamental real-world, bottom-up micro-costing estimates of T2DM management at the hospital level in Saudi Arabia and as such addresses important gaps in existing aggregate-based evidence. The findings highlight the economic value of early intervention, optimized glycemic management, and integrated care strategies aimed at preventing complications. Further, the outcomes of this study consistently indicate that costs are concentrated in pharmacotherapy and monitoring, and are significantly amplified by poor glycemic control, longer disease duration, and multimorbidity. Strengthening outpatient management and evidence-based formulary planning is therefore not only a clinical necessity but also a fiscal imperative for the long-term sustainability of Saudi Arabia’s healthcare system. Ultimately, we conclude that from a health system perspective, prioritizing early glycemic control, optimizing outpatient management, and implementing integrated care models holds an immense potential reduce downstream complications and associated costs.
