Quest for particles production in the plane gravitational wave spacetime
Nail Khusnutdinov

Abstract
Massless vector and scalar Green’s functions are obtained in closed form for a plane gravitational wave spacetime using three independent methods: direct solution of the Green-function equation and construction via the DeWitt and Hadamard recursive schemes. The results coincide exactly with the DeWitt–Schwinger expansion, indicating no massless particles production in this background. Contrasting interpretations, based on Bogolyubov coefficient calculations predicting massless particle creation, are also addressed.

Introduction

The problem of particle production was first addressed in the early development of quantum electrodynamics. One of the most notable predictions in this context was made by J. Schwinger
[
1
]
, who demonstrated that a strong external electric field can induce particle creation from the vacuum – a phenomenon now known as the
Schwinger effect
. In this scenario, the vacuum becomes unstable under the influence of the external field
[
2
]
, leading to the creation of particle–antiparticle pairs. Gravitational fields can likewise produce particles, a possibility first predicted in the early 1960s
[
3
,
4
]
(see also the historical account by L. Parker
[
5
]
). The most famous example of particle production by a gravitational field is Hawking radiation
[
6
,
7
]
. Comprehensive discussions of particle creation in gravitational backgrounds can now be found in several monographs
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
.

Various methods have been proposed to compute the vacuum expectation value of the energy–momentum tensor. The most widely used is the point-splitting method
[
8
]
, in which the energy–momentum tensor is expressed as the action of a two–point differential operator on the renormalized Green’s function, followed by the coincidence limit. Renormalization in this approach consists of subtracting from the Green’s function its DeWitt–Schwinger adiabatic expansion. Another common technique is the Pauli–Villars subtraction method
[
13
]
, in which each Green’s function in a Feynman diagram is renormalized by subtracting analogous functions with large regulator masses, which are taken to infinity at the end of the calculation (see also the modified Pauli–Villars method in Ref.
[
14
]
).

The Bogolyubov method (see, e.g.,
[
10
,
2
]
) is likewise based on the concept of an unstable vacuum. The fields are assumed to be free in the infinitely distant past and future, but with different vacuum states – commonly referred to as the
in
and
out
vacua. The annihilation operators that annihilate the
in
vacuum do not, in general, annihilate the
out
vacuum. A linear transformation between the annihilation and creation operators of the
in
and
out
vacua involves two parameters, known as the Bogolyubov coefficients. One of these coefficients characterizes scattering, while the other determines the density of created particles. In principle, the Bogolyubov method and the vacuum expectation value approach should yield identical results; however, this equivalence does not hold in the case of a plane gravitational wave background.

The interaction between gravitational waves (GWs) and electromagnetic waves (EMWs) has a long history. Within classical physics, the conversion of weak GWs to EMWs – and vice versa – in the presence of external electric or magnetic fields was first considered by Gertsenshtein in 1962
[
15
]
and later in Refs.
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
. In the framework of quantum gravity in the weak–field approximation
[
19
,
20
]
, the cross sections of such processes have been calculated (see, e.g.,
[
21
]
); the effect is proportional to the square of the external electric or magnetic field strength. The conversion of weak GWs to EMWs in the absence of external electromagnetic fields has been investigated in Refs.
[
22
,
23
]
, with the corresponding cross section scaling as
(G/c^{3})^{2}
, where
G
is the gravitational constant and
c
is the speed of light.

The Green function for a scalar field in a plane gravitational-wave spacetime was first derived by Gibbons
[
24
]
, who demonstrated that it coincides exactly with its DeWitt–Schwinger expansion. This result implies that plane gravitational waves do not induce scalar particle production. In contrast, Refs.
[
25

26
,
27
]
present a different scenario for massless scalar fields, suggesting that particle production may occur when the particle momenta are exactly aligned with the propagation direction of the gravitational wave. This claim is based on the analysis of the Bogolyubov coefficient
\beta
, which quantifies the particle number density. The Bogolyubov approach, originally developed by Garriga and Verdaguer
[
28
]
, yields
\beta=0
for massive particles, confirming the results of Ref.
[
24
]
. However, Refs.
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
argue that in the massless case,
\beta
can be nonzero when the gravitational-wave and particle momenta are precisely collinear. Specifically,
\beta
is proportional to
\delta(k_{v}+l_{v})
, where
k_{v}=\omega-k_{x}
and
l_{v}=\omega^{\prime}-l_{x}
are the
v
-components of the wave vectors of the
in
and
out
scalar modes
1
1

1

\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
are frequencies of
in
and
out
scalar waves, correspondingly, and the direction of propagation of the gravitational wave is the axis
x

. For massive particles,
k_{v}+l_{v}>0
, ensuring
\beta=0
; for massless particles, the sum can vanish, allowing
\beta\neq 0

[
8

9

In this work, we compute the Green function for the vector (Maxwell) field using three complementary methods: direct integration of the Maxwell equations, the DeWitt–Schwinger expansion, and the Hadamard elementary solution. We show that the retarded Green function for the vector potential has support not only on the past light cone but also within its interior. By contrast, the Green function of the physical electromagnetic field (the field-strength tensor) is supported strictly on the past light cone. This property, in the context of the Huygens principle, was previously noted in Ref.
[
29
]
for fields of (0,1,2)-form type, and for the Maxwell field in Ref.
[
30

in the context of self-force calculations.

3
The Green functions

3.1
The scalar Green functions

We first consider the scalar Green function satisfying the equation

(\Box-m^{2})D=-\frac{\delta^{(4)}(\mathrm{x}-\mathrm{x}^{\prime})}{\sqrt{-\textsl{g}}},

(13)

where
\Box=L^{-2}\partial_{\mu}\left(L^{2}\textsl{g}^{\mu\tau}\partial_{\tau}\right)
is the Laplace–Beltrami operator. Using the set of functions
[
24
]

\phi_{k}=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}L}e^{\mathrm{i}k_{\mu}\mathrm{x}^{\mu}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2k_{v}}k_{i}k_{j}\int^{u}\gamma^{ij}\mathrm{d}u},

(14)

which solve the eigenvalue problem

(\Box-m^{2})\phi_{k}=(2k_{v}k_{u}-m^{2})\phi_{k},

(15)

we obtain the scalar Green function

D=-\int\frac{\phi_{k}(\mathrm{x})\phi^{*}_{k}(\mathrm{x}^{\prime})}{2k_{v}k_{u}-m^{2}}\mathrm{d}^{4}k.

(16)

The set of functions (
14
) forms a complete and orthogonal basis:

\displaystyle\int L^{2}\mathrm{d}^{4}\mathrm{x}\phi_{k}(\mathrm{x})\phi_{k^{\prime}}(\mathrm{x})

\displaystyle=\delta^{(4)}(k-k^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{4}k\phi_{k}(\mathrm{x})\phi_{k}(\mathrm{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta^{(4)}(\mathrm{x}-\mathrm{x}^{\prime})}{L^{2}}.

Expression(
16

In other words, one must first select the variable with respect to which the causal structure – future or past – is defined. The natural choice is the coordinate
u
, associated with the covariantly constant Killing vector
\bm{\xi}_{1}
.

With this choice, the retarded and advanced Green functions are given by

D_{\mathrm{ret}/\mathrm{adv}}=-\int\frac{\phi_{k}(\mathrm{x})\phi^{*}_{k}(\mathrm{x}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}^{4}k}{2k_{v}(k_{u}\mp\mathrm{i}0)-m^{2}}.

(17)

The Feynman Green function is obtained in the usual way by adding a negative imaginary part to the mass term

D_{\mathrm{F}}=-\int\frac{\phi_{k}(\mathrm{x})\phi^{*}_{k}(\mathrm{x}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}^{4}k}{2k_{v}k_{u}-m^{2}+\mathrm{i}0}.

(18)

Employing the prescription
k_{v}\to k_{v}-\mathrm{i}0\sgn(\delta u)
for convergence,

\int\mathrm{d}^{2}ke^{\mathrm{i}k_{i}\delta\mathrm{x}^{i}+\frac{\mathrm{i}k_{i}k_{l}p^{il}}{2k_{v}}}=\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}k_{v}\sgn(\delta u)}{\sqrt{\det p^{il}}}e^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}k_{v}}{2}p_{il}\delta\mathrm{x}^{i}\delta\mathrm{x}^{l}},

D_{\mathrm{ret}/\mathrm{adv}}=\frac{\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}}{4\pi}\theta(\pm\delta u)\delta(\sigma),D_{\mathrm{F}}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{8\pi^{2}}\frac{\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\sigma+\mathrm{i}0}.

(20)

[
24
]
. As expected,
\re D_{\mathrm{F}}=\overline{D}=(D_{\mathrm{ret}}+D_{\mathrm{adv}})/2

D^{(1)}=2\im D_{\mathrm{F}}=\frac{\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{\mathcal{P}}{\sigma}.

(21)

The same expressions can be obtained via the series expansion method of DeWitt

\overline{D}=\frac{\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}}{8\pi}\delta(\sigma)+\frac{\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}}{8\pi}\theta(-\sigma)\left(a^{(1)}-\frac{\sigma}{2}a^{(2)}+\ldots\right),

are solutions of the chain

na^{(n)}+\sigma^{;\alpha}a^{(n)}=\Delta^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Delta^{\frac{1}{2}}a^{(n-1)})_{;\alpha}^{\ \ ;\alpha},

where the biscalars

4
Discussion and conclusion

In the preceding sections, we have rederived the scalar Green function first obtained in Ref.
[
24
]
and computed the massless vector Green function. Three distinct computational methods were employed: (i) direct solution of the differential equation for the Green function using the complete set of eigenfunctions, (ii) construction via the DeWitt recursive scheme, and (iii) construction via the Hadamard recursive scheme. In all cases considered, the resulting Green functions coincide with their DeWitt–Schwinger form.

Within the point-splitting approach, the vacuum expectation value of the renormalized energy–momentum tensor (EMT) for the electromagnetic and massless scalar fields may be obtained through the procedure

\displaystyle\langle T_{\mu}^{\ \nu}\rangle^{\mathrm{ren}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{x^{\prime}\to x}\left[\nabla^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{\alpha}G^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ren}}{}_{\mu}^{\ \nu^{\prime}}-\nabla_{\mu}^{\alpha^{\prime}}G^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ren}}{}_{\alpha}^{\ \nu^{\prime}}-\nabla_{\alpha}^{\nu^{\prime}}G^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ren}}{}_{\mu}^{\ \alpha^{\prime}}+\nabla_{\mu}^{\nu^{\prime}}G^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ren}}{}_{\alpha}^{\ \alpha^{\prime}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\tfrac{1}{2}\textsl{g}_{\mu}^{\ \nu^{\prime}}\left(\nabla_{\alpha}^{\ \alpha^{\prime}}G^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ren}}{}_{\beta}^{\ \beta^{\prime}}-\nabla_{\alpha}^{\ \beta^{\prime}}G^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ren}}{}_{\beta}^{\ \alpha^{\prime}}\right)\right],

\displaystyle\langle T_{\mu}^{\ \nu}\rangle^{\mathrm{ren}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{x^{\prime}\to x}\left[(1-2\xi)\nabla_{\mu\nu^{\prime}}+\textsl{g}_{\mu\nu^{\prime}}\left(2\xi-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\textsl{g}^{\alpha\beta^{\prime}}\nabla_{\alpha\beta^{\prime}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\xi\left(\textsl{g}_{\mu\rho^{\prime}}\nabla^{\rho^{\prime}}_{\ \nu^{\prime}}+\textsl{g}_{\nu^{\prime}\sigma}\nabla^{\sigma}_{\ \mu}\right)\right]G^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ren}},

(54)

respectively. Here,
\xi
denotes the non–minimal coupling, while
G^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ren}}{}_{\nu}^{\ \nu^{\prime}}
and
G^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ren}}
represent the renormalized Hadamard elementary solutions. The renormalization consists of subtracting the first three terms of the DeWitt–Schwinger expansion (up to the second heat-kernel coefficient). Consequently,
G^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ren}}{}_{\nu}^{\ \nu^{\prime}}=0
and
G^{(1)}_{\mathrm{ren}}=0
, and the vacuum expectation value of the EMT vanishes exactly, implying the absence of particle production for both scalar and vector fields in a plane gravitational wave background. For scalar particles, this result was first obtained by Gibbons
[
24
]
and later confirmed by Garriga and Verdaguer
[
28
]
using the Pauli–Villars renormalization prescription.

In general, a Green function is defined only up to a solution of the associated homogeneous equation, with the homogeneous part determined by the boundary conditions. A well-known example is the electrostatic potential of a point charge at rest in a Schwarzschild spacetime: Copson
[
42
]
obtained the potential (the three-dimensional Green function) almost a century ago; however, as noted by Linet
[
43
]
, its asymptotic form does not match the Coulomb potential far from the black hole, as it should. The correct behavior is recovered by adding an appropriate homogeneous solution. In the present case, the fields are free, so no additional homogeneous solution is required, and consequently there is no mechanism to describe particle production.

Consider now a flux of photons propagating along the
x
-direction. Since
a_{2}=0
, there is no conformal anomaly
[
8
]
, and the trace of the renormalized EMT vanishes. The EMT therefore shares the same properties as that of an electromagnetic plane wave propagating in the
x
-direction. It is well known
[
44
]
that this tensor possesses only three nonzero components:
T^{tt}_{\mathrm{ren}}=T^{tx}_{\mathrm{ren}}=T^{xx}_{\mathrm{ren}}=W
, where
W=W(u)
is the energy density. In null coordinates
(u,v)
, the EMT has a single nonzero component,
T_{uu}=2W(u)
.

At the same time, Refs.
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
report that massless particle production occurs within the framework of the Bogolyubov coefficient method. In this picture, the flux of created particles is strongly aligned – both in direction and velocity – with the propagation of the gravitational wave.

This conclusion is based on the observation by Garriga and Verdaguer
[
28
]
that the Bogolyubov coefficients satisfy
\beta\sim\delta(k_{v}+l_{v})
and
\alpha\sim\delta(k_{v}-l_{v})
, where
k_{v}
and
l_{v}
are the
v
-components of the
in
and
out
wave vectors of the scalar field, respectively. For a gravitational wave "sandwich"
u_{1}<u_{2}<0
, the matrix
\mathbf{P}
after passage of the GW packet is given by
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{A}+u\mathbf{B}
, and, as shown in Ref.
[
28
]
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{k,l}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta(k_{v}-l_{v})}{4\pi\mathrm{i}k_{v}\sqrt{|\det\mathbf{B}|}}e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4k_{v}}\mathbf{J}^{T}\left(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{B}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{J}},

\displaystyle\beta_{k,l}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta(k_{v}+l_{v})}{4\pi\mathrm{i}k_{v}\sqrt{|\det\mathbf{B}|}}e^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4k_{v}}\mathbf{I}^{T}\left(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{B}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{I}},

(55)

where
J_{j}=l_{i}A^{i}_{j}-k_{j}
and
I_{j}=l_{i}A^{i}_{j}+k_{j}
. These relations follow directly from the existence of the Killing isotropic vector
\bm{\xi}_{1}=\partial_{v}
. For massive particles,
k_{v}+l_{v}>0
, and thus
\beta=0
, implying no particle production. For massless particles propagating in the direction of the GW,
k_{i}=l_{i}=0
, so that
k_{v}=l_{v}=0
. In this case, the arguments of the delta functions in
\beta
and
\alpha
vanish, leading to the conclusion that particle production occurs.

This results in an apparent contradiction between the two approaches to particle production calculations. The standard method based on the Green-function formalism
[
8
]
predicts no particle creation, whereas the Bogolyubov approach suggests a flux of photons in the direction of the GW. The aim of this paper is to draw the attention of the physics community to this unresolved issue.

\Lambda_{2}^{3}=-\Lambda_{3}^{2}=u_{v}\left(\frac{\gamma_{yz}\dot{\gamma}_{yy}-\dot{\gamma}_{yz}\gamma_{yy}}{2\gamma_{yy}L^{2}}-\dot{\psi}\right).

\displaystyle\delta\omega_{(1)}=0,\ \delta\omega_{(2)}=\omega_{(1)}\delta\upsilon_{(2)},\ \delta\omega_{(3)}=\omega_{(1)}\delta\upsilon_{(3)},

\displaystyle\delta\omega_{(0)}=\delta\upsilon_{(a)}\omega^{(a)}(u^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2}\delta\upsilon_{(a)}\delta\upsilon^{(a)}\omega_{(1)},

(61)

where
\delta\upsilon_{(a)}=\upsilon_{\mu}\delta\lambda^{\mu}_{(a)}
and

.

Now, we represent the solution in the form
\omega_{(a)}(u)=\textsl{g}_{(a)(b)}\omega^{(b)}(u^{\prime})

\displaystyle\omega_{(0)}(u)

\displaystyle=-\omega^{(1)}(u^{\prime})+\delta\upsilon_{(a)}\omega^{(a)}(u^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}\delta\upsilon_{(a)}\delta\upsilon^{(a)}\omega^{(0)},

\displaystyle\omega_{(2)}(u)

\displaystyle=\omega^{(2)}(u^{\prime})-\delta\upsilon_{(2)}\omega^{(0)},

\displaystyle\omega_{(3)}(u)

\displaystyle=\omega^{(3)}(u^{\prime})-\delta\upsilon_{(3)}\omega^{(0)},

\displaystyle\omega_{(1)}(u)

\displaystyle=-\omega^{(0)}(u^{\prime}).

(62)

From these relations we obtain bivector of parallel transport along geodesic line (

u_{i}^{0}/u_{v}^{0}=-p_{ij}\delta x^{j}
