Model-independent observational constraints with fast radio bursts
1,2 Lázaro L. Sales, 2 Klecio E. L. de Farias, 2 Amilcar R. Queiroz, 2,3 João R. L. Santos, 2 Rafael A. Batista, 4 Ana R. M. Oliveira, 2 Lucas F. Santana, 5 Carlos A. Wuensche, 5,6,7 Thyrso Villela, 8 Jordany Vieira

Abstract
In this study, we use well-localized fast radio bursts (FRBs) to constrain cosmological parameters through two model-independent approaches: the reconstruction of the Hubble parameter H ⁡ ( z ) H(z) with an artificial neural network and cosmography. By integrating FRB data with supernovae (SNe), BAO from DESI DR2, and cosmic chronometers (CC), we derive constraints on the Hubble constant ( H 0 H_{0} ), the deceleration parameter ( q 0 q_{0} ), and the jerk parameter ( j 0 j_{0} ). For the reconstruction method, our MCMC analysis with FRB-only provides H 0 = 69.9 ± 5.8 ​ km ​ s − 1 ​ Mpc − 1 H_{0}=69.9\pm 5.8\,\text{km}\,\text{s}^{-1}\,\text{Mpc}^{-1} , corresponding to a precision of ∼ 8 % \sim 8\% . A joint analysis with FRB+SNe+(BAO+BBN+CMB) gives H 0 = 68.85 − 0.48 + 0.47 ​ km ​ s − 1 ​ Mpc − 1 H_{0}=68.85_{-0.48}^{+0.47}\,\text{km}\,\text{s}^{-1}\,\text{Mpc}^{-1} , reaching a precision below 1 % 1\% . The cosmographic approach with FRBs alone provides H 0 = 65.83 − 4.87 + 3.77 ​ km ​ s − 1 ​ Mpc − 1 H_{0}=65.83_{-4.87}^{+3.77}\,\text{km}\,\text{s}^{-1}\,\text{Mpc}^{-1} , q 0 = − 0.45 − 0.31 + 0.26 q_{0}=-0.45_{-0.31}^{+0.26} , and j 0 = 1.17 − 0.58 + 0.70 j_{0}=1.17_{-0.58}^{+0.70} with a precision for the Hubble constant of ∼ 6 % \sim 6\% . In addition, the BAO+BBN+CMB dataset yields H 0 = 65.20 − 1.28 + 1.29 ​ km ​ s − 1 ​ Mpc − 1 H_{0}=65.20_{-1.28}^{+1.29}\,\text{km}\,\text{s}^{-1}\,\text{Mpc}^{-1} , q 0 = − 0.29 ± 0.07 q_{0}=-0.29\pm 0.07 , and j 0 = 0.58 − 0.04 + 0.03 j_{0}=0.58_{-0.04}^{+0.03} , indicating a precision of ∼ 2 % \sim 2\% for the Hubble constant. Combining the FRB, SNe, BAO+BBN+CMB, and CC data sets provides tighter constraints, for example, H 0 = 67.88 − 0.53 + 0.52 ​ km ​ s − 1 ​ Mpc − 1 H_{0}=67.88_{-0.53}^{+0.52}\,\text{km}\,\text{s}^{-1}\,\text{Mpc}^{-1} , q 0 = − 0.42 − 0.03 + 0.02 q_{0}=-0.42_{-0.03}^{+0.02} , and j 0 = 0.56 ± 0.02 j_{0}=0.56\pm 0.02 . In particular, these findings provide a statistically significant indication of deviation from the Λ \Lambda CDM prediction of j 0 = 1 j_{0}=1 , suggesting possible departures from standard cosmology at a confidence level 1 ​ σ 1\sigma . They also confirm a model-independent accelerated expansion ( q 0 < 0 q_{0}<0 ), demonstrating the potential of FRBs as valuable cosmological probes.

Introduction

On July 24, 2001, the Parkes radio telescope detected a very bright and unidentified radio signal in the
1.4\,\mathrm{GHz}
survey of the Magellanic Clouds. The burst had a significant flux of
30\pm 10\,\mathrm{Jy}
, with
<5\,\mathrm{ms}
duration and was reported by Lorimer and collaborators in 2007
Lorimer:2007qn
. These astronomical events are known as Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs), and their population has been increasing, reaching about 1,000 events at the time of writing
1
1

1

. One of the key properties of FRBs is their dispersion measure (DM), which refers to the time delay of the FRB signal across different radio frequency channels. This delay occurs because of interactions with free electrons along the signal’s path, causing lower-frequency wave fronts to arrive later than higher-frequency ones. By analyzing this time delay, we can estimate the distance of the FRB and assess the matter content along the line of sight. Because FRBs exhibit an extremely high dispersion that surpasses the maximum contribution from the Milky Way, they are classified as extragalactic events.

The classification of FRBs can be divided into repeaters (periodic or not) and non-repeaters, the latter corresponding to a single detected pulse. The majority of FRBs detected across different surveys are non-repeating. Despite numerous efforts to identify their origin, the sources of these phenomena remain unclear. However, repeating signals may be attributed to neutron stars with strong magnetic fields, such as pulsars
Cordes:2015fua
and magnetars
Popov:2007uv
;
Pen:2015ema
. However, since most FRBs are extragalactic in origin, identifying the host galaxy of a detected FRB may help constrain cosmological models
Kalita:2024xae
and estimate the fraction of baryonic mass in the intergalactic medium (IGM)
Munoz:2018mll
;
Walters:2019cie
;
Qiang:2020vta
.

FRBs with well-defined distances offer a powerful tool for studying open problems in astrophysics and cosmology, such as the Hubble tension
Kalita:2024xae
. The investigation of Hubble tension can be addressed through various methods and theories
di2021realm
.

In this work, motivated by the Hubble tension and recent indications of deviations from the standard cosmological model reported by the DESI collaboration, we explore two distinct approaches for estimating the Hubble parameter,
H(z)
, with the goal of reducing reliance on any specific cosmological model.

The first approach utilizes an artificial neural network (ANN), which is structured in layers that transform input signals into output signals. We employ the method developed by Wang et al.

, known as the Reconstruct Function with ANN (

The second model-independent approach employed in this work is the kinematic model, also known as cosmography
Gao:2014iva
;
Gao:2023izj

;

;

H_{0}

q_{0}

j_{0}

H_{0}

II
Fast radio bursts

Fast radio bursts are intense, millisecond-duration radio transients of extragalactic origin, first discovered serendipitously in 2007. They are characterized by their high flux densities (typically
0.5
–
100
Jy), very short durations (a few milliseconds or less), and large DM that often exceed the expected Galactic contribution along the line of sight. These features suggest that FRBs originate at cosmological distances, with inferred luminosities reaching up to
\sim 10^{43}
erg s
-1
. Their emission is coherent and spans a broad frequency range, from
\sim 400
MHz up to
\sim 8
GHz, although the precise spectral behavior often deviates from a simple power law and can be highly variable across bursts. The observed FRB population includes both one-off events, possibly of a cataclysmic nature, and repeating sources, implying diverse progenitor classes or emission mechanisms. In particular, the repeating FRB 121102 shows extreme rotation measures and complex spectral and temporal substructure, suggesting a dense and magnetized local environment
michilli2018extreme
.

The main observational properties of FRBs include significant frequency-dependent dispersion, temporal scattering, and in some cases, scintillation and strong Faraday rotation, all of which offer important diagnostics of the intervening medium. Pulse widths range from sub-milliseconds to tens of milliseconds and often exhibit asymmetric temporal profiles due to multi-path propagation effects. The spectral indices are poorly constrained due to limited bandwidth and uncertain source localization within the telescope beam. Some FRBs are highly polarized, with rotation measures exceeding
10^{5}
rad m
-2
, indicating extreme magneto-ionic environments. The diversity of pulse shapes, spectral variability, and propagation effects complicates the classification and interpretation of FRBs but also provides an opportunity to use them as probes of the interstellar and intergalactic media. Their high brightness temperature (
T_{B}\sim 10^{35}-10^{37}
K) and short durations require coherent emission processes, distinguishing them from incoherent synchrotron sources. As the FRB detection rate increases with new wide-field radio facilities, statistical analyses of their population properties will become a powerful cosmological and astrophysical tool. For a review of the properties of FRBs, interested readers can refer to Refs.
petroff2019fast
;
cordes2019fast

The DM is crucial for constraining cosmological models using FRB data. It accounts for contributions from various components, such as the host galaxy (where the FRB is supposedly located), the intergalactic medium and the interstellar medium of our galaxy. The total DM contribution, from the source to the observer, is calculated using the following expression
Petroff:2014taa
:

{\rm DM}_{\mathrm{obs}}(z)={\rm DM}_{\mathrm{MW}}+{\rm DM}_{\mathrm{IGM}}+\frac{{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}}{1+z}\;,

(1)

where the contribution of Milky Way reads as
{\rm DM}_{\rm MW}={\rm DM}_{\rm MW,ISM}+{\rm DM}_{\rm MW,halo}
with DM
{}_{\textrm{MW,ISM}}
being the galactic interstellar medium
(\sim 10^{0}-10^{3}\,{\rm pc\ cm^{-3}})
and DM
{}_{\textrm{MW,halo}}
the galactic halo. DM
{}_{\textrm{IGM}}
denotes the intergalactic medium
(\sim 10^{2}-10^{3}\,{\rm pc\ cm^{-3}})
and DM
{}_{\textrm{host}}
the host galaxy interstellar medium
(\sim 10^{0}-10^{3}\,{\rm pc\ cm^{-3}})
. The Milky Way halo contribution DM
{}_{\textrm{MW,halo}}
is not well defined, and the expectation values lie between
50-100\,{\rm pc\ cm^{-3}}
. The factor
(1+z)
in (
1
) is due to cosmic dilation.

When a FRB pulse approaches Earth, it interacts with all the components of Eq.
1
, resulting in dispersion and a time delay between the arrival of different frequencies in the observed signal. This delay is characterized by
Petroff:2019tty

\Delta t\propto\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{\rm lo}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\nu_{\rm hi}^{2}}\right){\rm DM}\;,

(2)

where
\nu_{\rm lo}
and
\nu_{\rm hi}
stand for the lower and higher frequencies of the emitted signal. The DM parameter relates to the column density of free electrons (
n_{e}
) along the line of sight (
l
) to the FRB (see, e.g.,
Petroff:2019tty
), so that

{\rm DM}=\int\frac{n_{e}(z)dl}{1+z}\;.

(3)

To use FRB data as a cosmological probe, we must account for the observed extragalactic DM, which is determined by removing the Milky Way contribution from the observed DM, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\rm DM}_{\rm ext}(z)

\displaystyle\equiv{\rm DM}_{\mathrm{obs}}(z)-{\rm DM}_{\mathrm{MW}}

\displaystyle={\rm DM}_{\mathrm{IGM}}+\frac{{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}}{1+z}\;.

(4)

The DM contribution from the intergalactic medium arises from the integrated column density of free electrons along the line of sight. While the cosmic web’s large-scale structure introduces inhomogeneities, cosmological hydrodynamical simulations, such as IllustrisTNG, have demonstrated that the distribution of DM
IGM
at a given redshift can often be approximated by a Gaussian profile, especially at higher redshifts (e.g.,
z\gtrsim 1
) where the IGM is more homogeneous
Zhang:2020xoc
;
Batten:2020pdh
. This approximation is supported by studies indicating that, at these redshifts, the variance in DM
IGM
is primarily due to the number of intervening structures along different sightlines, which, when numerous, lead to a distribution that approaches Gaussianity due to the central limit theorem
hilhorst2009central
. Moreover, observational analyses have found that the scatter in DM
IGM
around its mean relation with redshift is consistent with a normal distribution, further justifying this modeling choice
McQuinn:2013tmc
;
bhandari2021probing
. The present study assumes a Gaussian distribution for DM
IGM
as follows:

P_{\rm IGM}(\mathrm{DM}_{\mathrm{IGM}}|z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{IGM}}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\mathrm{DM}_{\mathrm{IGM}}(z)-\langle\mathrm{DM}_{\mathrm{IGM}}(z)\rangle}{\sigma_{\mathrm{IGM}}}\right)^{2}\right]~,

(5)

where
\mathrm{DM}_{\mathrm{IGM}}=\mathrm{DM}_{\mathrm{ext}}-\mathrm{DM}_{\mathrm{host}}/(1+z)
and the variance related to the DM
IGM

McQuinn:2013tmc

\sigma_{\rm IGM}^{2}(z)=c\left[\frac{\chi_{\rm e}\Omega_{\rm b}\rho_{\rm cr}}{\mu_{\rm e}m_{\rm p}}\right]^{2}\int^{z}_{0}{\rm d}z\frac{(1+z)^{2}}{H(z)}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}kkP_{\rm e}(k,z)~,

(6)

The average DM for the DM

Conversely, the DM contribution from the host galaxy, denoted as

This contribution is influenced by several factors, including the galaxy’s morphology, inclination, star formation rate, and the FRB’s location within the host. III
Theoretical data analysis framework

In this section, we present the theoretical and mathematical tools to be used in our FRB analysis. We shall apply two methods: the first is a reconstruction method using the Artificial Neural Network (ANN), which allows us to reconstruct functions from observational data. We chose cosmography as the second method. It uses a Taylor series expansion for the scale factor, enabling an investigation of the Universe’s acceleration status through a cosmological model-independent description. We describe the two methods below.

III.1
Reconstruction method

Based on the cosmic chronometer data, it is possible to create a function that depicts the Hubble parameter
H(z)
. As previously mentioned, this reconstruction method employs an ANN, which simulates biological neurons and is organized into layers where input signals are transformed into output signals. This code was developed by Wang et al.
Wang:2019vxv
, who referred to it as the Reconstruct Function with ANN (
ReFANN
2
2

2

https://github.com/Guo-Jian-Wang/refann.git
).

The
ReFANN
aims to construct a function that relates the redshift and Hubble parameter data, where one neuron is responsible for providing an associated Hubble parameter to a specific redshift. Therefore, by reconstructing a dataset, the
ReFANN
produces the best-approximated function to generate the dataset points.
The connection between the CC redshift and the respective value for the Hubble parameter is made by a series of hidden layers, where most neurons are present. Each layer receives a vector from the previous layer and performs a linear transformation followed by a nonlinear activation. The activation function used in the present work is the exponential linear unit (ELU)
clevert2015fast
, which has a positive hyperparameter. The hyperparameter controls the values at which the ELU saturates for negative net inputs. In the current network model, we set it to 1. Moreover, these neurons depend on the hyperparameter values (weights and biases) obtained during the ANN training. Such neurons are represented by vectors whose details can be found in Ref.
Wang:2019vxv
.

The optimization of hyperparameters from observational redshift data occurs through a loss function, which measures the difference between the predicted and observed values of the Hubble parameter. As the ANN is trained, the loss function is minimized, and consequently, the hyperparameters are optimized based on the datasets used. The simplest way to evaluate the difference between the predicted and observed values is using the loss function
Dialektopoulos:2023dhb

\mathrm{L}1=\sum_{i}\left|H_{\mathrm{obs}}\left(z_{i}\right)-H_{\mathrm{pred}}\left(z_{i}\right)\right|,

(10)

where
H_{\mathrm{obs}}
and
H_{\mathrm{pred}}
are the observed and ANN predicted values, respectively.

The construction of the approximated function is based on the association of the redshift
z
with the Hubble parameter, considering the uncertainty from the
H(z)
data. The
ReFANN
configuration used here follows the values presented in the original work
Wang:2019vxv
, where
0.01
was chosen for the learning rate, which decreases as the number of interactions increases. Moreover, the number of interactions is set as
3\times 10^{4}
, where this number is enough to find the maximum reduction in the loss function. The number of hidden layers is chosen to minimize the risk that is defined according to the following statistical relation
Wasserman:2001ng
:

risk

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{Bias}_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\text{ Variance}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[H\left(z_{i}\right)-\bar{H}\left(z_{i}\right)\right]^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma^{2}\left(H\left(z_{i}\right)\right)\,,

(11)

where
\bar{H}\left(z_{i}\right)
is the fiducial model and
N
is the number of points related to the
H\left(z_{i}\right)
data. Furthermore, we worked with one hidden layer, since it presents a minimal risk value when associated with 4096 neurons. Fig.

III.2
Cosmography

An alternative approach to investigate the evolution of the Universe is the kinematic model, also called cosmography
Gao:2014iva
;
Gao:2023izj
. In this method, only the evolution of the expansion from direct measurements of the kinematic parameters is considered, meaning that we do not need to worry about the composition of the Universe. This approach has the advantage of being model-independent since the parameters are obtained directly from observational data. To obtain the cosmographic parameters, we start with the Hubble parameter
H(t)
, expressed as

H(t)=\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\,,

(12)

where
a(t)
is the scale factor that describes the expansion of the Universe as a function of time. By performing a Taylor expansion around the scale factor, we get

a(t)=a_{0}\left\{1+H_{0}\left(t-t_{0}\right)-\frac{1}{2}q_{0}H_{0}^{2}\left(t-t_{0}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{3!}j_{0}H_{0}^{3}\left(t-t_{0}\right)^{3}+\mathcal{O}((t-t_{0})^{4})\right\}\,,

(13)

where
q_{0}
and
j_{0}
are the deceleration and the jerk cosmographic parameters. These parameters are defined as

q_{0}=-\frac{\ddot{a}(t_{0})}{a(t_{0})H_{0}^{2}}~~~~{\rm and}~~~~j_{0}=\frac{\dddot{a}(t_{0})}{a(t_{0})H_{0}^{3}}\,.

(14)

Here,
q_{0}
stands for the behavior of cosmic expansion; a positive value means a slowed-down expansion and a negative one indicates an accelerated expansion. While
q_{0}
describes whether the Universe is accelerating or decelerating,
j_{0}
captures the rate of change of the acceleration in the Universe’s expansion. In the realm of
\Lambda
CDM model, where the accelerated expansion is driven by the cosmological constant,
j_{0}
is equal to 1. On the other hand, deviations of
j_{0}=1
suggest non-standard cosmologies, potentially involving dynamic dark energy
Enkhili:2024dil
, modified gravity
Myrzakulov:2023ohc
, or other phenomena
Demianski:2016dsa
.

The Hubble parameter can be written in terms of the above parameters as follows:

H(z)=H_{0}\left[1+(1+q_{0})z+\frac{1}{2}\left(j_{0}-q_{0}^{2}\right)z^{2}+\mathcal{O}(z^{3})\right]\,.

(15)

The dependence on the luminosity distance
d_{L}(z)
of the Hubble parameter is reflected in a description of this quantity in terms of
q_{0}
and
j_{0}
(see details in
Visser:2003vq
), whose form is

d_{\rm L}(z)=\frac{c}{H_{0}}\left[z+\frac{1-q_{0}}{2}z^{2}+\frac{1}{6}\left(3q_{0}^{2}+j_{0}+q_{0}-1\right)z^{3}+\mathcal{O}(z^{4})\right]\,.

(16)

To address FRB physics in the context of cosmography, we need to know how the DM of the intergalactic medium is affected by the
q_{0}
and
j_{0}
cosmographic parameters. The
{\rm DM}_{\rm IGM}
from (
7
) can be approximated by

\langle\mathrm{DM}_{\mathrm{IGM}}(z)\rangle=10^{4}Ax_{\rm e}\Omega_{\rm b}h^{2}\left(\frac{1}{H_{0}}\left[z-\frac{q_{0}z^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{6}\left(4+6q_{0}+3q_{0}^{2}-j_{0}\right)z^{3}+\mathcal{O}(z^{4})\right]\right)~.

(17)

Furthermore, to include Baryon Acoustic Oscillations (BAO) in our analysis within the framework of cosmography, the relevant equations are

D_{\rm M}(z)=\frac{c}{H_{0}}\left\{z-\frac{(1+q_{0})z^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{3}\left[(1+q_{0})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(j_{0}-q_{0}^{2})\right]z^{3}+\mathcal{O}(z^{4})\right\},

(18)

which is the transverse comoving distance for a flat Universe, and the Hubble distance

D_{\rm H}(z)=\frac{c}{H_{0}}\left\{1-(1+q_{0})z+\left[(1+q_{0})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(j_{0}-q_{0}^{2})\right]z^{2}+\mathcal{O}(z^{3})\right\}~.

(19)

Since inferred distances are relative to the sound horizon (
r_{\rm d}
), the directly constrained quantities are the ratios
D_{\rm M}/r_{\rm d}
,
D_{\rm H}/r_{\rm d}
and
D_{\rm V}/r_{\rm d}
, with
D_{\rm V}=[zD_{\rm M}^{2}(z)D_{\rm H}(z)]^{1/3}
being the angle-averaged distance. We also assume the same formula for the sound horizon adopted by DESI
brieden2023tale
;
DESI:2025zgx
,

r_{\rm d}=147.05\,{\rm Mpc}\left(\frac{\Omega_{\rm b}h^{2}}{0.02236}\right)^{-0.13}\left(\frac{\Omega_{\rm m}h^{2}}{0.1432}\right)^{-0.23}\left(\frac{N_{\rm eff}}{3.04}\right)^{-0.1}~.

(20)

In our study, we employ the best-fit values from Planck 2018 for the cosmological parameters, i.e.,
\Omega_{\rm m}h^{2}=0.1424
and
N_{\rm eff}=2.99
, where
\Omega_{m}
is the matter density,
\Omega_{b}
is the baryon density,
h
is the Hubble parameter and
N_{eff}
is the number of neutrino families
aghanim2020planck
.
To break the degeneracy between
H_{0}
and
r_{\rm d}
inherent in uncalibrated BAO measurements, we impose a Gaussian prior on the baryon density,
\Omega_{\rm b}h^{2}=0.02218\pm 0.00055
, as determined by Big Bang Nucleosynthesis (BBN)
schoneberg20242024
. This allows for a consistent estimation of
H_{0}
without requiring an external calibration of
r_{\rm d}
.

Although additional cosmographic parameters, such as
s_{0}
and
l_{0}
, are included in
\mathcal{O}(z^{n})
, we will focus solely on
H_{0}
,
q_{0}
, and
j_{0}
. The cosmographic approach exhibits linear behavior at low redshifts; however, at higher redshifts, parameters derived from higher-order terms of the expansion become increasingly significant. Nevertheless, due to the mathematical limitations of the Taylor expansion in terms of redshift
z
, which has a convergence radius of at most
|z|=1

cattoen2007hubble
, we restrict our analysis to data with
z<1
by applying a redshift mask to all observational samples here considered. This ensures that the series expansion remains valid within its domain of convergence. Furthermore, we truncate the expansion at second order—i.e., including terms up to the jerk parameter
j_{0}
—to balance precision and reliability. Adding higher-order terms may improve the accuracy of the approximation, but such a procedure introduces substantial degeneracy among the parameters, thereby undermining the stability and interpretability of the inference. We aim to preserve physical coherence and statistical robustness in our cosmographic analysis by limiting the expansion to the second order.

V
Methods

V.1
Likelihoods and Bayesian inference

In this subsection, we present the statistical approaches adopted in our analysis. We aim to constrain the cosmological parameters using ANN and cosmography. By employing these methods, we strive to alleviate the dependence on a specific cosmological model, avoiding issues related to degeneracy between the model’s parameters. This study explores how the reconstruction and cosmography can constrain cosmological parameters using FRB, SNe, BAO, and CC data, and their combinations. For the likelihood function of FRBs, we utilize the following probability density function:

P(\mathrm{DM}_{\mathrm{ext}}\mid z)=\int_{0}^{\mathrm{DM}_{\mathrm{ext}}}P_{\rm IGM}(\mathrm{DM}_{\mathrm{IGM}}|z)P_{\rm host}(\mathrm{DM}_{\mathrm{host}}|z)d\mathrm{DM}_{\mathrm{host}}~,

(21)

where the distributions
P_{\rm IGM}(\mathrm{DM}_{\mathrm{IGM}}|z)
and
P_{\rm host}(\mathrm{DM}_{\mathrm{host}}|z)
are given by Eqs. (
5
) and (
9
), respectively. For CC data, we apply the Gaussian likelihood

\log\mathcal{L}(H_{i},z_{i})\propto-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\left(\frac{H_{i}^{\rm obs}-H^{\rm th}(z_{i})}{\sigma_{i}}\right)^{2}\;.

(22)

Here,
H_{i}^{\rm obs}
represents the observational data and
H^{\rm th}
the cosmological model under consideration, given by Eq. (
15
) in the realm of cosmography.

In the case of SNe data, the likelihood function reads as

\log\mathcal{L}(\mu_{i},z_{i})\propto-\frac{1}{2}(\mu_{\rm th}(z_{i})-\mu_{\rm obs})^{T}\mathbf{C}^{-1}(\mu_{\rm th}(z_{i})-\mu_{\rm obs})\;,

(23)

where
\mathbf{C}
is the covariance matrix,
\mu_{\rm obs}
is the observed distance modulus from the SNe data, and
\mu_{\rm th}
is the theoretical distance modulus, given by

\mu(z)=m_{b}-M_{b}=5\log_{10}\left(\frac{d_{\rm L}(z)}{1\;\text{Mpc}}\right)+25\;,

(24)

with
m_{b}
and
M_{b}
being, respectively, the apparent magnitude and absolute magnitude, and
d_{\rm L}
is the distance luminosity for cosmography Eq. (
16
). Moreover, the formula of the distance luminosity for a general model is

d_{\rm L}(z)=c(1+z)\int_{0}^{z}\frac{dz^{\prime}}{H(z^{\prime})}~.

(25)

For BAO data, the likelihood function is such that

\log\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{y}_{i},z_{i})\propto-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{y}_{\rm th}(z_{i})-\mathbf{y}_{\rm obs})^{T}\mathbf{C}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}_{\rm th}(z_{i})-\mathbf{y}_{\rm obs})\;,

(26)

where
\mathbf{y}_{\rm obs}
is the set of observed BAO quantities at different redshifts,
\mathbf{C}
is the covariance matrix provided by the survey, and
\mathbf{y}_{\rm th}(z_{i})
are the corresponding theoretical predictions for the observables
D_{M}(z)/r_{d}
,
D_{H}(z)/r_{d}
, and
D_{V}(z)/r_{d}
, computed either using cosmography or a specific cosmological model. In the
\Lambda
CDM case,
H(z)=H_{0}\sqrt{\Omega_{m}(1+z)^{3}+1-\Omega_{m}}
and

\displaystyle D_{M}(z)

\displaystyle=c\int_{0}^{z}\frac{dz^{\prime}}{H(z^{\prime})}\;,

(27)

\displaystyle D_{H}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{H(z)}\;,

(28)

all normalized by the sound horizon scale
r_{\rm d}
. For the cosmographic approach, the Hubble parameter
H(z)
is given by Eq. (
15
), from which
D_{M}(z)
,
D_{H}(z)
, and
D_{V}(z)
can be computed accordingly.

Since all events are independent, the combined likelihood of the sample is simply the product of the individual likelihoods:

\mathcal{L}_{\rm total}=\prod_{i}\mathcal{L}_{i}\;\;\;\;\mathrm{or}\;\;\;\;\log\mathcal{L}_{\rm total}=\sum_{i}\log\mathcal{L}_{i}\;.

(29)

So far, we have presented the setup of the likelihoods of individual samples. However, we must combine their likelihoods to include a joint analysis for two or more samples. For instance, for an analysis with FRB, CC, BAO, and SNe data, the joint likelihood is written as

\mathcal{L}_{\rm joint}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm FRB}\times\mathcal{L}_{\rm CC}\times\mathcal{L}_{\rm BAO}\times\mathcal{L}_{\rm SNe}\;.

(30)

Our approach uses Bayesian inference to update our understanding of model parameters based on prior information. This process refers to the dataset
D
and a probabilistic model for the given distribution
\mathcal{L}(D|\theta)
, known as the likelihood function
John1970
;
Smith1987
, conditioned on knowledge of the set of adjusted parameters
\theta
. The prior distribution,
\mathcal{P}(\theta)
, quantifies our knowledge of
\theta
. These functions are linked through Bayes’ theorem:

P(\theta|D,\mathcal{M})=\frac{\mathcal{L}(D|\theta,\mathcal{M})\cdot\mathcal{P}(\theta|\mathcal{M})}{\mathcal{E}(D|\mathcal{M})}~.

(31)

To identify which model best quantifies the data, we need to determine the Bayesian evidence, denoted as
\mathcal{E}(D|\mathcal{M})
, associated with each theoretical model (
\mathcal{M}
) under consideration. To achieve this, we must integrate the likelihood function over the parameter space, as shown below:

\mathcal{E}(D|\mathcal{M})=\int\mathcal{L}(D|\theta,\mathcal{M})\mathcal{P}(\theta|\mathcal{M})d\theta~.

(32)

In this study, we derived posterior probability distributions and the Bayesian evidence with the nested sampling Monte Carlo algorithm MLFriends
buchner2016statistical
;
buchner2019collaborative
using the
UltraNest
5
5

5

https://johannesbuchner.github.io/UltraNest/
package
buchner2021ultranest
. Bayesian posterior distributions, along with means, confidence intervals, and contour plots, are computed using the
GetDist
6
6

6

https://getdist.readthedocs.io/en/latest/#
package
lewis2019getdist
. This tool employs improved adaptive kernel density estimation techniques, which account for boundary effects and smoothing biases, to generate accurate marginalized densities and visualizations of the parameter space.

VIII
Conclusion

Fast radio bursts offer a novel avenue for estimating cosmological parameters. In this work, we harness their potential through two model-independent approaches: the reconstruct function with an artificial neural network (
ReFANN
) and cosmography. The
ReFANN
method utilizes an artificial neural network to reconstruct the Hubble parameter
H(z)
from observational data, specifically CC, by mapping redshift to Hubble parameter values through hidden layers with an exponential linear unit (ELU) activation function. Cosmography, on the other hand, employs a Taylor series expansion of the scale factor to derive kinematic parameters (
H_{0}
,
q_{0}
,
j_{0}
) directly from observational data, thereby bypassing assumptions about the universe’s composition. These methods mitigate model dependencies, leveraging datasets including 116 localized FRBs, 1701 SNe Ia light curves, DESI DR2 BAO measurements, and 35 CC data points to achieve robust constraints. The integration of these probes, combined through Bayesian inference with likelihood functions tailored to each dataset, enhances the precision of our results and addresses degeneracies inherent in individual probes.

Our findings demonstrate that FRBs alone can constrain the Hubble constant with precisions of
\sim 8\%
and
\sim 6\%
using the reconstruction and cosmographic methods, respectively. The reconstruction approach gives
H_{0}=69.9\pm 5.8\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}
, which is compatible with both Planck and SH0ES values within
1\sigma
, while the cosmographic estimate
H_{0}=65.83^{+3.77}_{-4.87}\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}
aligns more closely with the early-Universe inference from Planck. In the cosmographic framework, the FRB-only analysis also yields
q_{0}=-0.45^{+0.26}_{-0.31}
, confirming an accelerated expansion, and
j_{0}=1.17^{+0.70}_{-0.58}
, consistent with the
\Lambda
CDM prediction at
1\sigma
. A joint analysis combining FRB, SNe, BAO+BBN+CMB, and CC datasets yields substantially tighter constraints:
H_{0}=67.88^{+0.52}_{-0.53}\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}
,
q_{0}=-0.42^{+0.02}_{-0.03}
, and
j_{0}=0.56\pm 0.02
, the latter providing a statistically significant indication of deviation from the
\Lambda
CDM value
j_{0}=1
, suggesting potential non-standard cosmologies.

These results underscore the growing potential of FRBs as cosmological observables, particularly when combined with other probes. The relatively low tension between FRB-inferred
H_{0}
and both early- and late-Universe estimates highlights their utility in cross-validating and possibly alleviating the current Hubble tension. Moreover, our work examines the contribution of the host galaxy DM, recovering well-constrained log-normal parameters with significantly reduced uncertainties compared to previous studies.

Looking ahead, the ongoing increase in the number and precision of well-localized FRBs, supported by upcoming surveys and new instruments, will be crucial to improving the statistical significance of our constraints. Although current limitations in sample size present challenges, such as the low precision in the measurement obtained for
H_{0}
compared to those from SH0ES and Planck experiments, the demonstrated complementarity between FRBs and other probes opens up a promising path for precision cosmology. As larger and more accurate FRB datasets become available, by the already existent radiotelescopes and the new generation as shown in Ref.
dosSantos:2023ipw
, we expect significant advancements in our ability to constrain not only the Hubble constant but also higher-order cosmographic parameters. This could provide deeper insights into the nature of cosmic acceleration and explore possible extensions beyond the standard
\Lambda
CDM model.
