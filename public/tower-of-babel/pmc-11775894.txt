Interventional Echocardiography-Guided Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Interscallop Repair of the Mitral Valve
Authors: Ashtyn Chamberland, Trevor Dowd, Richelle Wildey, Firas Zahr, Kevin Wei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Transcatheter edge-to-edge repair of the mitral valve, Interscallop repair of the mitral valve, 3D intracardiac echocardiography, 3D transesophageal echocardiography, Multiplanar reconstruction imaging

Introduction

In the usual transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) of the mitral valve (MV), a device (MitraClip, Abbott Vascular; or Pascal, Edwards Lifesciences Corp.) is positioned perpendicular to the coaptation line between the anterior and posterior MV leaflets, and then a portion of the 2 leaflets is connected together with the device. In this unique case, however, mitral regurgitation (MR) was due to a deep interscallop defect located between the middle (P2) and medial (P3) scallops of the posterior MV leaflet and the flail segment did not face the coaptation line. Repair of this MV required an “interscallop repair” using a TEER device. Implantation of this device across the P2 and P3 scallops to close the interscallop defect would not have been possible without the use of three-dimensional (3D) intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) or transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) with multiplanar reconstruction (MPR) imaging.

Case Presentation

The patient is an 84-year-old white man with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the anterior MV leaflet causing left ventricular (LV) outflow tract obstruction as seen by transthoracic echocardiography years earlier. Other medical conditions included coronary artery disease, stage III chronic kidney disease, systemic hypertension, and pulmonary hypertension (pulmonary vascular resistance of 4 Woods units). More recently, the patient started to develop worsening shortness of breath and was hospitalized twice for decompensated heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiography showed SAM and severe MR. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy therapy was switched from metoprolol and disopyramide to mavacamten, which resolved the SAM and LV outflow tract obstruction, but severe MR was still present. A subsequent TEE then revealed severe eccentric primary MR due to a flail posterior MV leaflet with a torn chord (Figure 1, Video 1).

The patient was considered to be a high-risk candidate for surgery given their age and multiple comorbidities at multidisciplinary valve conference, prompting the valve team to favor an interscallop repair of the MV using a TEER device with 3D ICE, 3D TEE, and MPR imaging guidance.

During the TEER procedure, the atrial septum was punctured inferiorly and posteriorly. The inferior position helped to prevent an anterior to posterior trajectory of the device toward the MV, and a posterior position provided adequate height above the MV annulus. A 3D ICE probe was then advanced across the intra-atrial septum. Three-dimensional ICE and 3D ICE MPR images obtained from the left atrium confirmed the presence of a P3 flail with a deep interscallop defect between the P2 and P3 scallops, nearly identical to the images obtained with 3D TEE (Figure 4, Video 4). The use of ICE imaging during the case was beneficial whenever the guide or delivery apparatus of the TEER device shadowed or obscured the device on TEE. We could rapidly switch between TEE and ICE to confirm device location and orientation whenever required. Figure 4 and Video 4 also demonstrate why biplane imaging could not be used to guide the interscallop repair for this patient. The “green” MPR plane is the commissural view of the MV (equivalent to the 60° TEE view shown in Figure 1 and Video 1). The “red” MPR plane is orthogonal to the green MPR plane, which provides a long-axis view of the MV and is the usual “grasping” view used during a TEER procedure. In this case, it did not show the edges of the interscallop defect that needed to be captured by the device. In fact, no biplane images would have provided a true grasping view for the interscallop defect.

The TEER device was positioned over the interscallop defect of the posterior MV leaflet using 3D TEE and MPR imaging (Figure 5, Video 5). The red MPR plane was oriented parallel to the open arms of the device to become the “grasping” view for the P2 and P3 scallops of the MV (Figure 5, Video 5).

The red MPR image (B) therefore shows the device with its open arms in the left atrium above the P2 (right leaflet) and P3 (left leaflet) scallops of the MV.

After the device was closed, biplane images of the MV (commissural view on the left and long-axis view on the right) were obtained using 3D TEE (Figure 6, Video 6). These are the views that are usually used to guide an MV TEER procedure. In this case, however, these views completely fail to demonstrate the leaflet edges, which were captured by the device, but they do show that the device did not oppose the posterior and anterior MV leaflet edges.

Three-dimensional TEE using biplane imaging during diastole. The commissural view (60°) on the left shows the device is on the P3 scallop of the MV, with a free lateral (P1) scallop. The orthogonal long-axis view (150°) on the right shows the device is on the posterior leaflet of the MV, with a free anterior leaflet. Biplane imaging cannot demonstrate the leaflet edges that the device has captured.

The position of the TEER device after it was released was evaluated using 3D TEE MPR imaging (Figure 7, Video 7). The device was shown to capture both the P2 and P3 scallops of the posterior MV leaflet, confirming successful “bridging” of the interscallop defect (Figure 7, Video 7).

The blue MPR plane (C), which is the z axis or en face view of the MV, and the 3D en face view of the MV (D) show an open MV in this diastolic frame. The device is attached to the medial aspect of the posterior MV leaflet, between the P2 and P3 scallops. The red MPR plane (red line in panelsC and D) is parallel to the device, and the red MPR image (B) demonstrates that both P2 and P3 scallops are captured within the closed device. The 3D image shows that the interscallop defect has been successfully “bridged” by the device.

At the conclusion of the case, TEE showed mild residual MR with a normal transmitral mean pressure gradient and normal E-wave velocity, with forward systolic flow in the right upper pulmonary vein (Figure 8, Video 8).

Midesophageal 2D TEE and color-flow Doppler views of the MV at 60° (commissural view) showing mild residual MR at the conclusion of the procedure (A). Continuous-wave Doppler of the MV shows a peak E-wave velocity of 0.75 m/sec and mean pressure gradient of 1 mm Hg (B). Pulsed-wave Doppler of the right upper pulmonary vein shows forward systolic flows (C, arrows).

At 1 week postprocedure, the patient reported much less shortness of breath and more energy. At 4 months postprocedure, the patient was exercising daily, doing resistance strength training, and riding a stationary bike without any cardiorespiratory symptoms and was classified as New York Heart Association functional class I by their outpatient cardiologist.

Discussion

This case report demonstrates a successful MV TEER of an interscallop defect in a patient with severe degenerative mitral regurgitation (DMR). Chronic MR may be a primary (degenerative) abnormality of the MV apparatus, or secondary (functional) to a ventricular or atrial abnormality. The natural history of volume overload from DMR is LV dysfunction, heart failure and eventually death. The outcome of patients with severe DMR can be improved by early surgical repair,1 but many patients are at high risk for surgical intervention. Favorable outcomes from MV TEER have been reported in these patients. Mitral valve TEER is now a class 2a recommendation in symptomatic patients with severe DMR who are at high or prohibitive surgical risk.2

In the usual TEER procedure, the device is inserted via the femoral vein into the atria and positioned perpendicular to the coaptation line between the anterior and posterior MV leaflets, and then a portion of the 2 leaflets is connected together with the device. In the trials that led to transcatheter device approval, the vast majority of devices (>82%) were implanted across the central portion of the middle scallops of the anterior and posterior leaflets (A2/P2), which is a relatively chordae-free zone. A TEER of noncentral DMR originating from the medial or lateral commissures is more challenging due to the risk of device entanglement in chordae, less available space for device maneuvering, and more difficult imaging. Despite these barriers, procedural success of TEER in noncentral MR (found in 38% of patients with primary MR) was reported to be similar to patients with central MR in experienced centers.3 More recent reports have highlighted the important role of imaging using 3D TEE,4 3D MPR,5 or the “stitch artefact technique”6 to guide these challenging TEER cases.

In our patient, the pathology was further complicated by a deep interscallop defect between the P2 and P3 scallops of the posterior MV leaflet, and the flail segment did not face the coaptation line.

In the surgical literature, Carpentier7 described a method for closing gaps and indentations using a “magic stitch” as an adjunctive technique during an MV repair, and other surgical techniques including leaflet resection and folding, neochordal placement, chordal foldoplasty, ring annuloplasty, and suture closures have all been used, sometimes in combination, to address prominent cleft-like indentations or interscallop defects of the MV.8 These techniques have been reported to produce durable reductions in MR with preservation of MV area.8 Since our patient was not amenable to a surgical repair, we decided to attempt an interscallop repair using a TEER approach. Other groups have described the use of 2 devices on both sides of a “pseudo cleft” to create 2 “bridges” between the anterior and posterior MV leaflets, leaving the cleft itself as a third MV orifice.9 The use of a device to close a cleft as an adjunct to an MV TEER procedure has also been described.10 Seo11 described 3 different TEER grasping strategies for commissure prolapse using a single device where two-dimensional (2D) imaging and the stitch artefact technique were used to define the optimum strategy a priori. As demonstrated in this report, an appropriate grasping view was obtainable by 3D MPR.

One of our main concerns regarding a TEER approach, however, was that a reduction in posterior MV leaflet area and restriction of leaflet mobility caused by this repair could impair coaptation between the anterior and posterior leaflets, resulting in a new source of MR. In our patient, however, the P3 was markedly redundant, which allowed for adequate A3/P3 coaptation despite posterior leaflet plication, resulting in only mild residual MR postprocedure.

As detailed above, echocardiography is an essential part of the TEER procedure and is required to evaluate MV morphology preprocedure, to define the views that will be used to determine proper orientation of the device and the “grasping view” that will be used to ensure capture of the leaflets within the device, to guide safe transeptal puncture, and to evaluate residual MR after device closure. Although 2D and 3D TEE12 as well as ICE13 have been used to successfully guide TEER procedures, the off-axis views required to guide an interscallop repair as shown in this case report would not have been possible without the use of real-time MPR imaging.14 With MPR software, the “live” 3D dataset can be “sliced” to create 3 individual 2D orthogonal planes. In our case, the blue MPR plane (the z axis or “en face view”) demonstrated the orientation of the device relative to the valve leaflets, and the red MPR plane was oriented parallel to the device arms and in line with the P2 and P3 scallops (Videos 5 and 7) to show the 2 edges of the interscallop defect that needed to be opposed. The red MPR plane was effectively the “grasping view” that showed the medial aspect of P2 and the opposing flail lateral edge of P3. These views would not have been obtainable with traditional 2D TEE, real-time 3D TEE, or biplane imaging. Multiplanar reconstruction also allows issues of parallax commonly associated with 3D echocardiography to be minimized.15

Interscallop indentation closure, although effective surgically, is still novel with a TEER approach. There is still uncertainty about the lasting effectiveness and durability of this treatment. Long-term monitoring of MV area, MR, and the integrity of the repaired leaflet will be important. Furthermore, results from isolated case reports limit the generalizability of the findings, underscoring the need for a larger sample size to validate the efficacy and safety of this technique in diverse patient populations.

Conclusion

Advances in 3D ICE and TEE, and in particular the use of MPR imaging, allow the delineation of all varieties of valvular pathology. This case demonstrates how MPR imaging can clarify MV pathology much more accurately than traditional 2D or biplane images. The ability of MPR imaging to delineate off-axis slices of a valve can facilitate novel transcatheter repairs of the MV.
