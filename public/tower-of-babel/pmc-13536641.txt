Population-adapted Nolla method for dental age estimation in Northwestern Romanian children
Authors: Ligia Ioana Moga, Raluca Iurcov, Abel Emanuel Moca, Andrei Csep, Dan Slăvescu, Raluca Dima, Ligia Luminița Vaida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesAccurate dental age (DA) estimation is essential in pediatric dentistry, orthodontic treatment planning, and forensic practice; however, the applicability of international reference standards varies across populations. This study aimed to evaluate the accuracy of a population-adapted Nolla method for dental age estimation in children from northwestern Romania and to compare its performance with the original approach.Materials and methodsA retrospective analysis was performed on 860 digital panoramic radiographs of children aged 3–15 years. Dental development was assessed using the Nolla staging system, and a third-degree polynomial regression model was developed to estimate chronological age (CA) based on the total Nolla score. Model performance was evaluated using coefficient of determination (R²), five-fold cross-validation, and non-parametric statistical tests.ResultsThe original Nolla method underestimated CA by approximately 1.5 years. The population-specific model showed strong predictive performance (R² = 0.846; cross-validated R² = 0.84) and close agreement between estimated DA and CA (mean difference 0.000 ± 0.976 years; p = 0.983). Accuracy was stable across most age groups, although larger deviations were observed after 12 years. Girls showed slightly more advanced dental maturation than boys, but differences were clinically small.ConclusionsThe proposed population-calibrated equation eliminates systematic bias and provides a reliable tool for dental age estimation in children from northwestern Romania, although external validation is recommended.

Introduction

Estimating dental age (DA) plays an important role in Pediatric Dentistry, Orthodontic treatment planning, and forensic identification, and is also relevant in several medical fields, including Endocrinology and growth assessment [1, 2]. Although chronological age (CA) represents the conventional reference, biological indicators of maturation, such as skeletal and dental development, are considered more reliable markers of individual growth and developmental status [3, 4].

Biological maturation can be evaluated using skeletal or dental indicators. Skeletal age assessment methods, including the Cervical Vertebral Maturation (CVM) approach, are widely used in Orthodontics; however, skeletal development is known to be influenced by environmental and systemic factors [5, 6]. In contrast, dental development follows a relatively stable and genetically controlled pattern, showing a stronger correlation with CA and lower susceptibility to external influences [3, 4].

Dental maturation is characterized by a gradual and cumulative process that can be evaluated radiographically based on tooth mineralization stages. Several radiographic methods have been developed for DA estimation, among which the systems proposed by Demirjian and Nolla are the most widely used [2, 3]. These methods rely on the identification of developmental stages of permanent teeth and their conversion into age estimates using population-based reference standards [7]. Panoramic radiography is commonly used for this purpose, as it allows simultaneous visualization of multiple teeth at different stages of development [7, 8].

The Demirjian method, introduced in 1973, classifies dental development into eight stages (A–H) based on a French-Canadian population [3]. The Nolla method, developed earlier from a sample of Michigan children, describes ten sequential stages of tooth formation (1–10) [9]. Although the Nolla system has been widely applied in different populations, numerous studies have reported variations in its accuracy, highlighting the need for population-specific validation and calibration [10–12].

Population differences in growth patterns may lead to systematic errors when international standards are applied without local adjustment. In Romania, previous studies have shown that the Demirjian method tends to overestimate DA, whereas the Nolla method generally underestimates DA, particularly at older ages [13, 14]. These findings emphasize the need for locally adapted conversion models to improve the accuracy of DA estimation in Romanian children.

Therefore, the aim of the present study was to evaluate the accuracy and applicability of a population-adapted Nolla method for estimating dental age in a cohort of children from northwestern Romania. Specifically, the objectives were:

to assess the agreement between DA estimated using the adapted method and CA;to evaluate the reliability of the adapted model across age groups and between sexes;to compare the performance of the adapted method with that of the original Nolla approach.

Materials and methods

Sample selection

This retrospective study included digital panoramic radiographs obtained from a pediatric population in Oradea, northwestern Romania, between 1 August 2024 and 30 September 2024. The radiographs originated from a single dental clinic and had been taken for diagnostic purposes related to pediatric or orthodontic treatment, not for research.

All images were acquired using a Soredex Cranex Novus panoramic system (Soredex, Milwaukee, WI, USA). Panoramic radiography was performed only when clinically indicated following routine examination. Each radiograph was associated with the patient’s date of birth and the date of image acquisition. Images were stored in JPEG format.

A total of 945 panoramic radiographs were initially screened. The inclusion criteria were:

patients aged between 3 and 15 years;panoramic radiographs obtained for diagnostic purposes;availability of complete information regarding date of birth and date of radiographic examination.

After applying these criteria, 85 radiographs were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 860 images.

For analysis, the sample was stratified into 13 one-year age groups (e.g., 3.0–3.9 years, 4.0–4.9 years) to allow detailed comparison between CA and DA.

The sample of 860 panoramic radiographs included in this study is identical to that used in our previously published evaluation of the original Nolla method in the same population [14]. The present study extends that work by developing and validating a population-specific regression model for dental age estimation.

Assessment of chronological and dental age

CA was calculated as the difference between the date of birth and the date of radiographic examination, expressed in decimal years. DA was evaluated using the Nolla method, which assesses the developmental stages of the seven permanent teeth on the left side of the maxilla and mandible, excluding the third molar. Tooth development was classified into ten discrete whole-number stages (1–10), without the use of intermediate subdivisions, following the original scoring criteria described by Nolla (1960) [9]. Each stage was assigned as an integer value, ranging from the presence of a crypt (Stage 1) to complete root formation with apical closure (Stage 10). When applicable, Stage 0 (absence of a crypt) was recorded [9].

The developmental stages of the evaluated teeth were summed to obtain the total Nolla score. Dental age was subsequently estimated using a third-degree (cubic) polynomial regression model, with the Nolla score as the independent variable.

All radiographic assessments were performed by a single trained examiner to ensure methodological consistency.

A randomly selected subset of 10% of the radiographs (n = 86) was re-evaluated after a two-week interval by the same examiner to assess intra-observer reliability. Agreement was quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), which indicated excellent reproducibility (ICC = 0.93).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 and Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA).

The distribution of quantitative variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, depending on distribution.

Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Due to non-normal distribution, comparisons between independent groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test or the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc analysis when appropriate. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare chronological age and dental age. Associations between variables were evaluated using Spearman’s correlation coefficient.

A cubic polynomial regression model was constructed to estimate chronological age based on the Nolla score. Model performance was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R²), five-fold cross-validation, and assessment of residual normality and homoscedasticity. Error-based performance metrics, including mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean square error (RMSE), were computed for the pooled sample and separately by sex. Agreement between chronological age and model-derived dental age was further assessed using Bland–Altman analysis. Five-fold cross-validation was performed using a stratified random splitting algorithm (StratifiedKFold, scikit-learn library, Python), with age group used as the stratification variable to ensure balanced representation across all folds. The resulting age-group distribution across the five folds is presented in Table 4. This approach minimizes the risk of systematic imbalances between training and test sets due to unequal age-group representation.

The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Sample characteristics and performance of the original nolla method

The present analysis was performed on the same cohort of 860 panoramic radiographs previously used to evaluate the accuracy of the original Nolla method in children from northwestern Romania [14]. The sample included 356 boys (41.4%) and 504 girls (58.6%), with a mean chronological age of 9.95 ± 2.48 years (median: 9.9 years; range: 3–15 years).

As previously reported, the original Nolla method showed a systematic underestimation of chronological age in this population, with a mean dental age of 8.43 ± 2.13 years compared with the mean chronological age of the sample, corresponding to an average discrepancy of approximately 1.5 years. These findings indicated the need for population-specific calibration of the method [14].

Development of the population-specific regression model

Based on the systematic underestimation observed with the original Nolla method, a population-specific regression model was developed using the total Nolla score as a predictor of chronological age. A third-degree (cubic) polynomial regression analysis demonstrated a strong association between the Nolla score and chronological age (p < 0.001), with a coefficient of determination of R² = 0.846.

Model performance and stability were further evaluated using five-fold cross-validation, which confirmed a high predictive capacity (mean R² = 0.8398, SD = 0.0118). Residual analysis showed an approximately normal distribution and homoscedasticity, supporting the adequacy of the model.

Accuracy of the adapted nolla model

The data presented in Table 2 show the CA and DA estimated using both the original and the adapted Nolla methods. Consistent with previous findings, the original method underestimated chronological age, whereas the distribution of dental age calculated using the adapted model was closely aligned with the chronological age of the children included in the study. The comparison between chronological age and dental age estimated using the adapted method showed no statistically significant differences (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p = 0.983), suggesting no significant systematic difference at the group level between chronological age and the newly estimated dental age. The statistical non-significance of the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was complemented by error-based metrics and Bland–Altman analysis, which provide a more comprehensive assessment of clinical agreement (see Model Agreement and Error-Based Performance Metrics). The adapted model produced a mean dental age (9.94 ± 2.28 years) that closely matched the mean chronological age of the sample, while the original method underestimated age (8.43 ± 2.13 years). The mean difference between chronological age and dental age was 0.000 ± 0.976 years, confirming the elimination of the systematic bias observed with the original method.

Model agreement and error-based performance metrics

The five-fold cross-validation was performed using a randomized splitting algorithm, achieving a balanced age-group distribution across all folds, as presented in Table 4. Error-based performance metrics for the pooled sample and by sex are presented in Table 5. The model demonstrated good overall accuracy, with a MAE of 0.76 years and RMSE of 0.98 years for the pooled sample. Slightly better predictive accuracy was observed in males (MAE = 0.69 years, RMSE = 0.93 years) compared with females (MAE = 0.74 years, RMSE = 0.94 years). The close agreement between full-model and cross-validated metrics (pooled MAE: 0.76 vs. 0.77 years; pooled RMSE: 0.98 vs. 0.99 years) confirms model stability and argues against overfitting. In the subgroup of children older than 12 years, a higher MAE was observed (pooled: 0.85 years), consistent with the reduced discriminative capacity of the Nolla staging system at advanced stages of dental maturation (Tables 4 and 5).

Discussion

DA estimation is influenced by population characteristics, sex, and age range, particularly during pubertal maturation [15]. In the present study, a population-calibrated cubic regression model based on the Nolla score showed high predictive performance (R² ≈ 0.846; stable after five-fold cross-validation, R² ≈ 0.84) and an almost zero overall difference between chronological age (CA) and model-derived dental age (DA) (Wilcoxon test, p ≈ 0.983), suggesting an overall absence of systematic difference between CA and DA at the sample level. These results reinforce the widely recognized principle that local calibration improves the performance of dental age estimation methods when applied to different populations.

Most comparative studies report that the Demirjian method tends to overestimate DA, whereas the Nolla method frequently underestimates CA in both sexes [16]. A consistent finding in the literature is that the use of non-local reference standards for Nolla staging introduces systematic bias. Studies conducted in Spain, for example, reported significant underestimation with the Nolla method in different population samples, while Demirjian and Willems methods tended to overestimate CA [17, 18]. Similar patterns have been described in Turkish populations, although the magnitude and direction of the error varied according to age, sex, and region [19, 20]. Nevertheless, several studies have reported higher accuracy for the Nolla method compared with Demirjian in certain populations, and a systematic review and meta-analysis concluded that Nolla may provide overall better accuracy regardless of sex [21].

In Romania, previous research based on large samples from northwestern regions demonstrated that the original Nolla method systematically underestimated CA, with larger discrepancies at older ages and among girls, highlighting the need for population-specific calibration [14]. Similar observations were reported in a sample from Iași, further indicating that international reference standards may not be directly transferable across populations [22].

Within this context, the strong agreement achieved by the cubic regression model suggests that it effectively functions as a population-specific conversion from Nolla score to chronological age, substantially reducing the bias observed with the conventional approach. Age-group analysis showed relatively small and stable CA–DA differences up to approximately 11.9 years, with larger deviations observed after the age of 12 years.

This age-related pattern is consistent with previous reports indicating reduced accuracy during adolescence. Pubertal maturation is associated with increased biological variability and heterogeneous growth patterns, which may accentuate population differences and decrease the precision of age estimation methods. Similar age-dependent effects have been described in studies from Turkey and Iran, where accuracy varied across age groups and sometimes required age- or sex-specific adjustments [20, 23]. Importantly, the increased estimation error observed after the age of 12 years should not be interpreted as a sign of model instability, but rather as a reflection of a well-recognized biological phenomenon inherent to the Nolla staging system. As dental mineralization approaches its biological ceiling, most reference teeth reach Stage 10 (complete root formation with apical closure) during adolescence, producing an asymptotic plateau effect in the total Nolla score [10–12]. This reduction in the discriminative capacity of the score at advanced stages of maturation inevitably limits the precision of age estimation in older adolescents, regardless of the regression model applied. The subgroup analysis confirmed this pattern, showing a higher MAE in children older than 12 years (MAE = 0.85 years) compared with the overall sample (MAE = 0.76 years).

From a clinical perspective, this finding is particularly relevant, as late mixed dentition and early permanent dentition coincide with key orthodontic decision periods. Although the proposed model reduces overall error, adolescence remains the developmental stage where larger individual deviations may occur.

Sex-related differences were also observed, with girls showing slightly more advanced dental maturation than boys. This finding is consistent with established developmental patterns and with previous studies reporting earlier maturation in females and sex-related differences in estimation error when generalized standards are applied. Greater Nolla underestimation in girls has been reported in other populations [19], and Romanian data have similarly indicated larger discrepancies among female subjects [23]. In addition, previous research has demonstrated a significant positive correlation between dental age and skeletal maturity, with female subjects presenting higher bone age values than males, supporting the concept of earlier overall biological maturation in girls [24].

Although the present study focused on the Nolla method and its population-specific calibration, the broader literature frequently compares its performance with other approaches such as Demirjian, Willems, and Cameriere. Most comparative studies report method-dependent errors, supporting the concept that the optimal method may vary according to population characteristics. For example, a study from the Central Black Sea region found the Cameriere method to be the most accurate, followed by Nolla and Willems [20]. Similarly, studies in northeastern China proposed locally derived models based on Nolla staging, further demonstrating that population-specific recalibration improves accuracy and clinical applicability [25].

These findings underline an important practical implication: even when dental staging is reliable, the conversion of developmental stages into chronological age should be validated and, when necessary, adapted for the target population. The cubic model developed in the present study represents such a population-specific adjustment. Although a strong correlation between CA and model-derived DA was observed (Spearman r ≈ 0.93), correlation alone provides limited information, as DA is mathematically derived from the Nolla score. Model performance is better reflected by cross-validated R² values, residual analysis, and the clinical magnitude of estimation error.

Overall, the results confirm that the conventional Nolla conversion may introduce systematic bias when applied without local calibration, that this bias can be substantially reduced through population-specific regression, and that age and sex remain important modifiers of accuracy. The proposed equation, supported by internal validation, may therefore represent a clinically useful tool for dental age estimation in children from northwestern Romania. Future research should include external validation on independent cohorts and evaluation of inter- and intra-observer reliability for Nolla staging.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The sample was obtained from a single dental clinic in northwestern Romania, which may limit the applicability of the findings to the broader Romanian pediatric population. In addition, the retrospective design and the use of clinically indicated radiographs may have introduced selection bias. Although the model showed strong performance with internal cross-validation, external validation on an independent dataset remains an important limitation of the present study. The population-specific equation was developed and validated exclusively on a single-center sample from northwestern Romania, and its generalizability to other Romanian regions or broader populations cannot be assumed without further testing. External validation on independent Romanian samples, ideally including samples from multiple geographic regions and clinical settings, represents an essential next step to confirm the robustness and transferability of the proposed equation before its wider clinical application. Finally, dental staging was performed by a single examiner; despite excellent intra-observer reliability, inter-observer variability was not assessed.

Conclusions

The population-calibrated cubic regression model based on the Nolla score provided accurate estimation of CA in children from northwestern Romania, eliminating the systematic underestimation associated with the conventional Nolla conversion. The model showed high predictive performance and stable internal validation, with good agreement between dental age and chronological age across most age groups. Larger deviations were observed after the age of 12 years, and girls showed slightly more advanced dental maturation than boys. These findings support the clinical applicability of the proposed equation for pediatric and orthodontic assessment in the studied population, while external validation on independent Romanian samples is recommended.
