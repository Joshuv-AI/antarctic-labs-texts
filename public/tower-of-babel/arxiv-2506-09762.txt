Parallel computations for Metropolis Markov chains with Picard maps
Sebastiano Grazzi, Giacomo Zanella

Abstract
We develop parallel algorithms for simulating zeroth-order (aka gradient-free) Metropolis Markov chains based on the Picard map. For Random Walk Metropolis Markov chains targeting log-concave distributions π \pi on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , our algorithm generates samples close to π \pi in 𝒪 ⁡ ( d ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d}) parallel iterations with 𝒪 ⁡ ( d ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d}) processors, therefore speeding up the convergence of the corresponding sequential implementation by a factor d \sqrt{d} . Furthermore, a modification of our algorithm generates samples from an approximate measure π ϵ \pi_{\epsilon} in 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathcal{O}(1) parallel iterations and 𝒪 ⁡ ( d ) \mathcal{O}(d) processors. We empirically assess the performance of the proposed algorithms in high-dimensional regression problems and an epidemic model where the gradient is unavailable. Our algorithms are straightforward to implement and may constitute a useful tool for practitioners seeking to sample from a prescribed distribution π \pi using only point-wise evaluations of log ⁡ π \log\pi and parallel computing.



Key words—
Parallel computing, Metropolis-Hastings, sampling, log-concavity, Gradient-free, Zeroth-order

Introduction

1.1
Picard map for parallel-in-time Markov chain simulation

Consider a discrete-time Markov chain
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
on
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined as

\displaystyle X_{i+1}

\displaystyle=X_{i}+f(X_{i},W_{i})\,,

\displaystyle i=0,1,\dots

(1)

for some initial position
X_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
, measurable function
f\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{W}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and random innovations
W_{0},W_{1},\dots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\nu
on
\mathcal{W}
.
The canonical way to simulate the first
K
steps of the chain,
X=(X_{0},\dots,X_{K})
, is to go through the recursion in (
1
) for
i=0,1,\dots,K-1
.
This procedure requires
K
sequential calls to the function
f
.
Alternatively, one can define
X
through the set of equations

\displaystyle X_{i+1}

\displaystyle=X_{0}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{i}f(X_{\ell},W_{\ell})\,,

\displaystyle i=0,1,\dots\,.

Following this representation, one can define the Picard map
(named after the celebrated
Picard–Lindelöf theorem
),
\Phi\colon\mathcal{X}^{K+1}\times\mathcal{W}^{K}\mapsto\mathcal{X}^{K+1}
, which takes as input a trajectory
X\in\mathcal{X}^{K+1}
and the innovations
W=(W_{0},\dots,W_{K-1})\in\mathcal{W}^{K}
, and returns a new trajectory
X^{\prime}=(X^{\prime}_{0},\dots,X^{\prime}_{K})=\Phi(X,W)
defined as

\displaystyle X_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{i}(X,W)=\begin{cases}X_{0}&i=0\\
X_{0}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{i-1}f(X_{\ell},W_{\ell})&0<i\leq K\,.\end{cases}

(2)

Given
W\in\mathcal{W}^{K}
, the map
\Phi
is deterministic and its fixed point, i.e. the trajectory
X\in\mathcal{X}^{K+1}
satisfying
X=\Phi(X,W)
, is unique and coincides with the solution to (
1
).

This motivates computing
X
as the limit of the recursion

X^{(j)}=\Phi(X^{(j-1)},W)
for
j=1,2,\dots
, with initialization
X^{(0)}\in\mathcal{X}^{K+1}
. See Figure
1
for an illustration. A canonical choice for initialization is the constant trajectory
X^{(0)}=(X_{0},\dots,X_{0})\in\mathcal{X}^{K+1}
.
The computational advantage of the Picard recursion over the classical one defined in (
1
) is that the
K
calls to the function
f
in (
2
) can be executed in parallel.
This can be useful when computing
f
is expensive.

The Picard recursion is guaranteed to converge to its fixed point in at most
K
iterations because, by construction,
X^{(j)}
coincides with the solution of (
1
) in the first
j
coordinates.
However, since the sequential recursion in (
1
) also requires
K
iterations to compute
X
, relevant applications of the Picard recursion are those where
X^{(j)}
gets close (or exactly equal) to its fixed point for
j\ll K
iterations, trading the use of parallel computing for a shorter run-time.

(x_{1},x_{2})
-plane of the Picard recursion
X^{(j)}
for
K=1000
and
j=1,2,10,13

1.2
Zeroth-order Markov chain Monte Carlo

Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods are the computational workhorse of many scientific areas, including Bayesian Statistics. These methods consist of simulating a Markov chain
(X_{0},X_{1},\dots)
whose limiting measure coincides with a probability distribution of interest
\pi
, such as a Bayesian posterior. Then, ergodic averages
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}g(X_{i})

can be used to estimate expectations
\int g(x)\pi(\mathrm{d}x)
for
any integrable function
g
, see e.g.
Roberts and Rosenthal 2004
. We are interested in the simulation of zeroth-order (aka gradient-free, derivative-free) Markov chains, which only require point-wise evaluations of
\log\pi
(up to an additive unknown constant).
These methods are commonly used when gradient information is unavailable, for either practical, computational, or mathematical reasons
(cf.
Conn et al. 2009
, Chapter 1)
.
This may occur for example in Bayesian applications with black-box likelihood evaluations (e.g. in form of proprietary code, code written by domain experts, or models requiring complex numerical solvers) or when the gradient is not well-defined such as in models with censored data (e.g. the
SIR model considered in Section
6
), in Pseudo-Marginal MCMC
(
Andrieu and Roberts 2009
)
and in Approximate Bayes Computation (ABC) MCMC setting
(
Marjoram et al. 2003
)
.
See e.g.
Conn et al. 2009
and other references on zeroth-order optimization for more discussion about applications of gradient-free methods.

Our objective is to leverage the use of
K>1
parallel processors for reducing the
parallel round complexity
of this class of algorithms, defined as the number of point-wise evaluations of
\log\pi
per parallel processor in order to obtain samples from
\pi
. Under standard log-concavity assumptions on
\pi
, the best known complexity result w.r.t. the number of dimensions
d
for gradient-free sequential algorithms (i.e.
K=1
) is
\mathcal{O}(d)
evaluations per sample, and it is achieved by classical Random Walk Metropolis
1
1

1

Throughout the paper, the
\mathcal{O}
notation ignores constants and logarithmic terms

(
Dwivedi et al. 2019
;
Andrieu et al. 2024
)
. Note that this complexity matches the one of gradient-free convex optimization
(
Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
)
.

1.3
Main results

Corollary
2
shows that,
given a log-concave target density
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
K=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d})
processors, our proposed
Online Picard algorithm
applied to a Random Walk Metropolis Markov chain (initialized from a so-called feasible start) generates samples from a measure close to
\pi
in total variation distance after
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d})
parallel iterations. Thus, the Online Picard algorithm accelerates zeroth-order sequential algorithms by a factor of
\sqrt{d}
with
K=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d})
parallel processors, achieving optimal speedup (i.e. linear in
K
up to logarithmic factors) for
K
up to
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d})
. On the contrary, for larger values of
K
, we observe, both theoretically and empirically, a sharp decrease in relative gain.
Motivated by this bottleneck and leveraging insights from the analysis of the
Online Picard algorithm,
we also propose an
Approximate Picard algorithm
which generates samples from an approximate measure
\pi_{\epsilon}
after
\mathcal{O}(1)
parallel iterations with
K=\mathcal{O}(d)
processors. The error of the invariant measure
\pi_{\epsilon}
relative to
\pi
is assessed through numerical simulations, while a rigorous analysis lies beyond the scope of this work.
Section
4
provides convergence results also for Picard algorithms applied to Metropolis within Gibbs Markov chains, which we empirically observe to perform better.
The empirical performance observed in Section
6
closely matches the theory developed and shows substantial speedups (up to over 100 times) relative to the corresponding sequential algorithms, with highest efficiency obtained by Metropolis within Gibbs schemes. All proofs are provided in the appendix.

These results, combined with the simplicity of our algorithms, offer practitioners promising directions to parallelize computations for Bayesian problems with black-box, expensive models and no access to gradient information.

1.4
Related literature on parallel sampling and Picard maps

Previous attempts to parallelize computations for (zeroth-order) MCMC include running multiple independent chains
(
Rosenthal 2000
)
, which is straightforward to implement but does not reduce the burn-in or convergence period, or using
pre-fetching

(
Brockwell 2006
)
and
multiple-try

(
Frenkel and Smit 1996
;
Liu et al. 2000
)
algorithms, which however achieve complexity gains of only
\mathcal{O}(\log(K))
in typical zeroth-order settings
(
Pozza and Zanella 2024
)
.
The past year has seen a growing interest in parallel sampling based on Picard recursions applied to first-order (i.e. gradient-based) Markov chains for both Monte Carlo methods
(
Yu and Dalalyana 2024
;
Anari et al. 2024
)
and generative models
(
Shih et al. 2023
;
Zhou and Sugiyama 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Gupta et al. 2024
)
.
However, existing analyses focus on the (so-called unadjusted) setting where the function
f
in (
1
) is smooth with respect to their first argument, which is not the case for Metropolis-Hastings algorithms – see (
8
) below. Our work avoids the classical smoothness conditions on
f
and instead focuses on Metropolis-type Markov chains, where
f
is piecewise constant with respect to its first argument. This setting leads to a substantially different analysis as well as convergence results for the induced Picard map, as highlighted in Section
2
and Section
3.4
below.

2
Picard algorithms with piecewise constant map
\Phi

We consider Markov chains that satisfy the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

For every
W\in\mathcal{W}^{K}
, the Picard map
X\mapsto\Phi(X,W)
is constant in a neighborhood of its fixed point.

Assumption
1
is satisfied by standard zeroth order Metropolis-Hastings kernels described in Section
3.2
, which are our main motivation. Other relevant examples satisfying this assumption include Markov chains defined on discrete spaces.
Assumption
1
has many implications. For example, it implies that the fixed point of
\Phi
can be reached
exactly
after
j<K
parallel iterations.
When the fixed point is reached, the output of the Picard recursion matches that of the sequential counterpart, with no additional bias induced by the Picard recursion.
This is in contrast to classical applications of Picard recursions, where stopping the Picard recursion before
K
parallel iterations introduces bias and requires tuning of an additional error parameter.

2.1
Online Picard algorithm

Assume we seek to simulate
N
steps of the Markov chain defined in (
1
),
(X_{0},\dots,X_{N})
, using
K\leq N
independent processors.
In the classical Picard algorithm,
one updates the first
K
steps,
(X_{1},\dots,X_{K})
, with the Picard recursion until a stopping criterion is met, then proceeds with the next
K
ones,
(X_{K+1},\dots,X_{2K})
, and so on until reaching
X_{N}
.
Under Assumption
1
, instead, one can monitor the coordinates that have already reached their fixed point while the algorithm runs, and avoid wasting computational power in updating them further, directly allocating available processors to subsequent coordinates.
This leads to the following
Online Picard algorithm
:
given a sequence of noise
W=(W_{0},W_{1},\dots)\in\mathcal{W}^{\infty}
and initialization
X^{(0)}\in\mathcal{X}^{\infty}
, define the
j
-th parallel recursion of the Online Picard algorithm mapping
X^{(j)}\in\mathcal{X}^{\infty}
to
X^{(j+1)}\in\mathcal{X}^{\infty}

as

\displaystyle X_{i}^{(j+1)}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}X_{i}^{(j)}&i\leq L^{(j)},\\
\Phi_{i-L^{(j)}}\left(X_{L^{(j)}:U^{(j)}}^{(j)},W_{L^{(j)}:U^{(j)}-1}\right)&L^{(j)}<i\leq U^{(j)}\,,\\
X_{U^{(j)}}^{(j+1)}&i>U^{(j)},\end{cases}

(3)

where

L^{(0)}=0
,
U^{(j)}=L^{(j)}+K
and

\displaystyle L^{(j+1)}

\displaystyle=\sup\{i\leq U^{(j)}\colon f(X^{(j)}_{\ell},W_{\ell})=f(X^{(j+1)}_{\ell},W_{\ell})\text{ for }0\leq\ell<i\}

\displaystyle=\sup\{i\leq U^{(j)}\colon f(X_{\ell},W_{\ell})=f(X^{(j)}_{\ell},W_{\ell})\text{ for }0\leq\ell<i\}

\displaystyle=\sup\{i\leq U^{(j)}\colon X_{\ell}=X^{(j)}_{\ell}\text{ for }0\leq\ell\leq i\},\hskip 18.49988pt\hbox{for }j\geq 0\,,

(4)

where the equivalence of the formulations above follows from (
2
).

The Online Picard algorithm applies the Picard map to the indices
i\in\{L^{(j)},L^{(j)}+1,\dots,U^{(j)}\}
, where
L^{(j)}
is an increasing sequence which monitors the position of the last index
i
such that
X^{(j)}_{0:i}
has reached its fixed point.
The algorithm is then stopped as soon as
L^{(j)}\geq N
.
While (
3
) is formally defined as a map on
\mathcal{X}^{\infty}
, the algorithm in practice only operates on
K
coordinates of the Markov chain, as shown by Proposition
1
below and by the pseudocode provided in Appendix
C
.
As for the Picard recursion in (
2
), the online recursion in (
3
) requires
U^{(j)}-L^{(j)}=K
calls of the function
f
that can be computed in parallel.

The Online Picard algorithm is always more efficient than the classical Picard algorithm which applies sequentially the Picard recursion of (
2
) until convergence of each subsequent block
\{X_{i}\colon\ell K<i\leq(\ell+1)K\}
for
\ell=0,1,\dots
, since it avoids the vacuous updates of
X^{(j)}_{i}
for
i\leq L^{(j)}
. See Figure
2
for an illustration.

K

(j,i)

K=4
,
N=3K
. Yellow boundary line in correspondence of

.

2.2
Markov structure of Online Picard algorithms

Let
(X^{(j)},W^{(j)},L^{(j)})_{j=0,1,\dots}
be the sequence of random variables produced by the Online Picard recursion of (
3
)-(
4
).
Define
\bar{X}^{(j)}=X^{(j)}_{L^{(j)}:U^{(j)}}
and
\bar{W}^{(j)}=W_{L^{(j)}:U^{(j)}-1}
.

Proposition 1
.

The sequence
(\bar{X}^{(j)},\bar{W}^{(j)})_{j=0,1,\dots}

is a time-homogeneous Markov chain on
(\mathcal{X}^{K+1}\times\mathcal{W}^{K})
.

Proof.

The sequence
(\bar{X}^{(j)},\bar{W}^{(j)})_{j=0,1,\dots}
can be described by the recursion

\displaystyle\bar{X}^{(j+1)}_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\Phi_{G^{(j)}+i}(\bar{X}^{(j)},\bar{W}^{(j)})&i\leq K-G^{(j)}\,,\\
\Phi_{K}(\bar{X}^{(j)}\,,\bar{W}^{(j)})&i>K-G^{(j)}\,,\\
\end{cases}

\displaystyle i=0,1,\dots,K\,,

(5)

\displaystyle\bar{W}^{(j+1)}_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\bar{W}^{(j)}_{G^{(j)}+i}&i\leq K-G^{(j)}-1\,,\\
\tilde{W}_{i,j}&i>K-G^{(j)}-1\,,\end{cases}

\displaystyle i=0,1,\dots,K-1\,,

(6)

where
\tilde{W}_{i,j},\,i,j\geq 0,
are i.i.d. random variables with distribution
\nu
on
\mathcal{W}
,

G^{(j)}=G(\bar{X}^{(j)},\bar{W}^{(j)})=\sup\{i\leq K\colon\Phi_{\ell}(\bar{X}^{(j)},\bar{W}^{(j)})=\bar{X}^{(j)}_{\ell}\text{ for }0\leq\ell\leq i\}\,,

(7)

and (
5
)-(
6
) define the one-step Markov transition kernel of
(\bar{X}^{(j)},\bar{W}^{(j)})_{j=0,1,\dots}
.
∎

Proposition
1
sheds lights to the underlying (Markov) structure of Online Picard recursion. It can also be used for a memory-wise and computationally efficient implementation of the algorithm that iterates over a vector of length
K
.
In the next section, we study the distribution of the random variables
(L^{(j)})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
for popular zeroth-order Markov chains under log-concavity, in order to derive parallel round complexity results for the Online Picard algorithm.

3
Complexity of Picard algorithms for zeroth-order Monte Carlo

3.1
Assumption on the target

We study convergence of the Picard map under the following assumption on
\pi
.

Assumption 2

(Smooth and Hessian Lipschitz target)
.

The target distribution
\pi
has a Lebesgue density in
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
proportional to
\exp(-V)
where
V
is a twice-differentiable,
L
-smooth and
(\gamma L^{3/2}d^{1/2})
-strong Hessian-Lipschitz for some
\gamma\geq 0
, meaning that the Hessian of
V
satisfies

\displaystyle-L\mathrm{I}_{d}

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\nabla^{2}V(x)\preccurlyeq L\mathrm{I}_{d}\,,

\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle-M\|y-x\|

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\nabla^{2}V(y)-\nabla^{2}V(x)\preccurlyeq M\|y-x\|\,,

\displaystyle x,\,y\in\mathcal{X}

for some
M
with
\mathrm{Tr}(M)\leq\gamma L^{3/2}d^{1/2}
, where
A\preccurlyeq B
if
A-B
is positive semi-definite for two symmetric matrices
A,B
.

The strong Hessian-Lipschitz condition implies the more classical Hessian-Lipschitz condition,
\|\nabla^{2}V(y)-\nabla^{2}V(x)\|\leq m^{\star}\|y-x\|
, where
m^{\star}=\lambda_{max}(M)
, while it is weaker than assuming a bounded Frobenius norm of the third derivative, as previously used for analysis of MCMC algorithms
(
Chen and Gatmiry 2023
, cf. )
. Note also that the analysis of the convergence of
\Phi
does not require strong convexity of
V
.

3.2
Metropolis-Hastings kernels

In this work, we focus on two popular zeroth-order Metropolis-Hasting Markov chains:
Random Walk Metropolis (RWM)

(
Metropolis et al. 1953
)
and
Metropolis within Gibbs (MwG)

(
Geman and Geman 1984
)
.
Both satisfy Assumption
1
and can be simulated with the Online Picard algorithm.
We start with the RWM case and defer the MwG one to Section
4
.

Assumption 3

(RWM)
.

X_{0},X_{1},\dots
is a RWM Markov chain on
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
with step-size
\xi=h/(\sqrt{Ld})
for some
h>0
, i.e. it follows (
1
) with
W=(Z,U)
,
U\sim\mathrm{Unif}([0,1])
uniformly distributed,
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,h^{2}I_{d}/(Ld))
independently of
U
,

\displaystyle f(x,W)

\displaystyle=ZB(x,U,Z)\hskip 9.24994pt\hbox{with }B(x,u,z)=\mathbf{1}\left(\pi(x+z)/\pi(x)\geq u\right)

(8)

and
\mathbf{1}(\cdot)
being the indicator function.

By (
4
) and (
8
) combined we obtain

\displaystyle L^{(j+1)}

\displaystyle=\sup\{i\leq U^{(j)}\colon B(X_{\ell},U_{\ell},Z_{\ell})=B(X^{(j)}_{\ell},U_{\ell},Z_{\ell})\text{ for }0\leq\ell<i\}\,,

\displaystyle j\geq 0\,,

which means that, in the RWM case, the Picard convergence is entirely dependent on the probability of ‘correctly guessing’ the
\{0,1\}
-valued random variables

B(X_{i},U_{i},Z_{i})
.

3.3
Main results

Let
(X_{i})_{i=0,1,\dots}
and
(X^{(j)}_{i})_{i,j=0,1,2,\dots}
be as in (
1
) and (
3
), respectively.

Theorem 1

(Probability of an incorrect guess)
.

Under Assumptions
2
-
3
, for all
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
,
w_{0}\in\mathcal{W}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(f(X^{(j)}_{i},W_{i})\neq f(X_{i},W_{i})\mid X_{0}=x_{0},W_{0}=w_{0})

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}\frac{i}{d}+\delta(d)+2^{-j}

(9)

for all
0\leq i\leq\min(d,K)
and
j\in\{0,1,\dots\}
, with
c_{0}=15h^{4}(\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}+\frac{h\gamma}{2})^{2}
,

\delta(d)=\frac{5}{3}\exp(-3d/2)
. The probability in (
9
) is with respect to the randomness of
(W_{1},W_{2},\dots)
.

Theorem
1
essentially states that, after a logarithmic number of Picard recursions, the probability that the Picard map correctly predicts the
i
th step is
\mathcal{O}(i/d)
.

Remark 1
.

By Theorem
1
, the probability of correctly guessing individual increments can be controlled up to
i=\mathcal{O}(d)
.
Assuming (
9
) to be tight, this also suggests that increasing the number of parallel processors beyond
K=\mathcal{O}(d)
provides little gains in terms of speeding-up convergence of the Picard recursion, as numerically observed in Section
6
.

Remark 2
.

The upper bound of Theorem
1
becomes vacuous for
i>d
.
This is consistent with the fact that the mixing time of RWM under Assumptions
2
is
\mathcal{O}(d)
. Indeed, if
X_{0}
and
X_{i}
are approximately independent, then the prediction of
B(X_{i},U_{i},Z_{i})
with
B(X_{0},U_{i},Z_{i})
is uninformative.

By the union bound, Theorem
1
implies that, with high probability, the Online Picard algorithm makes
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d})
consecutive correct guesses after
\mathcal{O}(\log(d))
calls of the Picard map when using
K=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d})
processors, as shown below.

Corollary 1
.

Under Assumptions
2
-
3
, for all
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X},\,w_{0}\in\mathcal{W}
,
\epsilon>0

K\leq\sqrt{\epsilon^{2}d/(2c_{0})}

as in Theorem

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(L^{(j)}\geq K\mid X_{0}=x_{0},W_{0}=w_{0})\geq 1-\epsilon\,.

Notice the validity of the bound in Corollary

X_{0}
and innovation
W_{0}
.
This allows to prove the next main theorem, where we bound with high probability the number of parallel iterations required by the Online Picard algorithm to simulate a given number of steps of RWM with

Theorem 2
.

Suppose Assumptions
2
-
3
holds with

\delta>0
,

\mathbb{P}(L^{(j)}>n)\geq 1-\delta

By Theorem

Combining Theorem

Corollary 2

(Parallel round complexity of Online Picard algorithm)

Suppose Assumptions

Corollary
2
implies that the Online Picard algorithm with
K=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d})
achieves a Parallel round complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d})
.

3.4
Contraction of the Picard map: comparison between first- and zeroth-order case

In this section we compare the contraction of the Picard maps applied to RWM to the one obtained in the Unadjusted Langevin algorithm (ULA) case
(
Anari et al. 2024
)
.
The comparison illustrates how the first-order unadjusted case, where the map
x\mapsto f(x,W)
is smooth, differs from the zeroth-order case of RWM, where
x\mapsto f(x,W)
is piecewise constant.

Denote by
\Phi^{\mathrm{ULA}}\colon\mathcal{X}^{K+1}\times\mathcal{W}^{K}\mapsto\mathcal{X}^{K+1}
the Picard map applied to the ULA Markov chain, defined as in (
1
)
with

f^{\mathrm{ULA}}(X,W)=-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}\nabla V(X)+\xi W,\hskip 9.24994ptW\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})

for a tuning parameter
\xi>0
.
Under Assumption
2
, for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}^{K+1},
with
x_{0}=y_{0}
, all
w\in\mathcal{W}^{K}
, and for all
i\leq K
, we have

\displaystyle\|\Phi_{i}^{\mathrm{ULA}}(x,w)-\Phi_{i}^{\mathrm{ULA}}(y,w))\|

\displaystyle=\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}\|\sum_{\ell=0}^{i-1}(\nabla V(x_{\ell})-\nabla V(y_{\ell}))\|\leq\frac{\xi^{2}L}{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{i-1}\|x_{\ell}-y_{\ell}\|,

while Lemmas
5
-
6
in the supplement material imply that, under Assumptions
2
-
3
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\Phi^{\mathrm{RWM}}_{i}(x,W)-\Phi^{\mathrm{RWM}}_{i}(y,W)\|]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\|\Phi^{\mathrm{RWM}}_{i}(x,W)-\Phi^{\mathrm{RWM}}_{i}(y,W)\|^{2}]}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{15\xi^{2}d\sum_{\ell=1}^{i-1}\mathbb{P}(f(x_{\ell},W_{\ell})\neq f(y_{\ell},W_{\ell}))+\delta(d)}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{15\xi^{3}Ldc_{1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{i-1}\|x_{\ell}-y_{\ell}\|+\frac{1}{\xi Lc_{1}}\delta(d)}

(10)

where
W\sim\nu^{\otimes K}
, with marginals as in (
8
),
c_{1}=(\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}+\frac{\gamma\xi\sqrt{Ld}}{2})
and
\delta(d)
as in Theorem
1
.
Note that the contraction of the Picard map occurs pointwise (for each innovation
w
) for ULA and in expectation for RWM. More crucially, the RWM contraction has a slower rate, mainly due to the the square root function in (
10
).

3.5
Behavior in the tails

The convergence bounds shown in Section
3.3
apply to any initialization
X_{0}
.
Here we show that the convergence is faster when the chain is in the tails of the distribution.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose Assumptions
2
-
3
hold,
\ell I_{d}\preccurlyeq\nabla^{2}V
and
X^{(0)}_{\ell}=x_{0}
for all
\ell\geq 0
.
Then, for all
j\geq 0
and
K>0
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{|x_{0}|\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(L^{(j)}=jK\right)=1.

Proposition
2
implies that, as the initial point goes further in tails, the convergence of the Picard map becomes faster (up to converging in one iteration).
This is partly related to classical scaling limits obtained in the transient phase for RWM
(
Christensen et al. 2005
)
, which show that that RWM in the tails has a deterministic behavior. In our context, Proposition
2
suggests that the Picard map converges faster in the transient phase, which we do observe in the numerical simulations of Section
6.3
.

4
Metropolis within Gibbs

We now extend our convergence results to Metropolis within Gibbs (MwG) Markov chains.
Our main motivation to consider MwG is that it displays better empirical performances in Section
6
.

4.1
Metropolis within Gibbs kernels

For a given orthonormal basis
\{o_{0},o_{1},\dots,o_{d-1}\}
spanning
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(e.g. the standard basis), the (deterministic-scan)
Metropolis within Gibbs (MwG)
chain on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

(
Geman and Geman 1984
)
performs iteratively a one-dimensional Metropolis-Hasting update in the subspace spanned by
o_{i}
, for
i=0,\dots,d-1
.
The corresponding chain
(X_{i})_{i=0,1,\dots}
can be treated as a time-inhomogeneous Markov chain, where every block of
d
coordinates of the Markov chain is iteratively defined as

\displaystyle X_{i+1}

\displaystyle=X_{i}+f_{i}(X_{i},W_{i}),

\displaystyle\text{ for }i=0,1,\dots,d-1,

with innovations
W_{i}=(U_{i},\,Z_{i})
, where
Z_{0},\dots,Z_{d-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\nu
are one dimensional symmetric random variables,

U_{0},\dots,U_{d-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Unif}([0,1])
, independently of
Z_{0},\dots,Z_{d-1}
and

\displaystyle f_{i}(x,(U,Z))

\displaystyle=B(x,U,o_{i}Z)o_{i}Z\,,

\displaystyle\text{ for }i=0,1,\dots,d-1,

(11)

with
B
as in (
8
).

4.2
Analysis

If we denote by
\mathrm{Haar}(d)
the Haar measure on the orthogonal group in dimension

, our theoretical results hold with the following assumption.

Assumption 4
.

X_{0},X_{1},\dots
is a MwG chain on
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
(o_{0},o_{1},\dots,o_{d-1})\sim\mathrm{Haar}(d)
and

,

and

, where
\mathrm{Ber}(p)
and

are respectively the Bernoulli distribution with parameter

and the Chi distribution with

degrees of freedom.

Theorem 3
.

Under Assumptions
2
and
4
,

•

the statement of Theorem

holds with

and
\delta(d)
replaced by

•

the statement of Theorem
2
holds with

,
c_{0}
as above;

•

the statement of Proposition

below holds with

Theorem

shows that the analysis of the Picard map derived in Section

can be extended to MwG. However, for MwG, it is not possible to derive the parallel round complexity (as in Corollary

The next proposition shows that the Online Picard algorithm, when applied to MwG for isotropic Gaussian targets, achieves optimal speedup equal to

Proposition 3

Suppose Assumption

and

5
Approximate Picard

By Theorem
1
the probability of correctly guessing each individual increment for the Picard map remains small up to
i=\mathcal{O}(d)
steps. However, when accumulating all these small probabilities across
i
, the first incorrect guess happens much earlier, at
i=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d})
, see Corollary
1
.
This creates a bottleneck and does not allow us to leverage effectively more than
K=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d})
parallel processors, see Theorem
2
.

This motivated us to propose an Approximate Online Picard algorithm, which can leverage effectively up to
K=\mathcal{O}(d)
processors, but whose output does not match anymore the output of the recursion in (
1
) and for which the limiting distribution of the resulting Markov chain has a small bias relative to
\pi
.

5.1
Approximate Online Picard algorithm

By (
4
), each iteration of the Online Picard algorithm advances up to the position of the first error of the Picard recursion. Here we relax this condition by tolerating a fixed (and small) percentage
r\in[0,1]
of mistakes.
We thus define the
Approximate Online Picard algorithm
exactly as in (
3
) and (
4
), but replacing
(L^{(j)},U^{(j)})_{j=0}^{\infty}
with
(L_{r}^{(j)},U_{r}^{(j)})_{j=0}^{\infty}
defined as
L^{(0)}_{r}=0
,
U_{r}^{(j)}=L_{r}^{(j)}+K
and

\displaystyle L_{r}^{(j+1)}

\displaystyle=\sup\{L_{r}^{(j)}<i\leq U_{r}^{(j)}\colon\mathcal{A}_{\ell}^{(j)}\leq r\text{ for all }L_{r}^{(j)}<\ell\leq i\}

\displaystyle j\geq 0\,,

where

\mathcal{A}_{\ell}^{(j)}=\frac{|\{s\in\{L_{r}^{(j)},L_{r}^{(j)}+1,\dots,\ell-1\}\colon f(X^{(j+1)}_{s},W_{s})\neq f(X^{(j)}_{s},W_{s})\}|}{\ell-L_{r}^{(j)}}

is the proportion of mistakes made by the
j
-th Picard recursion in
\{L_{r}^{(j)}+1,\dots,\ell\}
, see also the pseudocode in Appendix
C
.
Here
r
acts as a tolerance parameter which controls the approximation error, and the case
r=0
coincides with the (exact) Online Picard algorithm.
By construction
L_{r}^{(j)}
is non-decreasing w.r.t.
r
, and thus increasing
r
reduces the runtime.
Interestingly, for any
r>0
, the Approximate Online Picard algorithm is able to exploit up to
K=\mathcal{O}(d)
parallel processors effectively, as shown in the following Proposition.

Proposition 4
.

Under Assumptions
2
-
3
, for all
\epsilon>0
,
r\in(0,1]
,
d\geq\log(5/(\epsilon r))
,
j\geq 1.5\log(3/(\epsilon r))
and
K\leq\epsilon rd/(3c_{0})
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\mathbf{1}\left(f(X_{i}^{(j)},W_{i})\neq f(X_{i},W_{i})\right)\leq r\right)>1-\epsilon.

Proposition
4
shows that the fraction of incorrect guesses can be controlled with high probability up to
K=\mathcal{O}(d)
processors and with only
j=\mathcal{O}(1)
parallel iterations.
This suggests that the Approximate Online Picard algorithm with
r>0
and
K=\mathcal{O}(d)
reduces the parallel run-time of the Online Picard algorithm by a factor
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d})
, thus converging in
j=\mathcal{O}(1)
parallel iterations, as numerically verified in Section
6
.

However, for
r>0
the output no longer matches exactly the output of the sequential algorithm.
Intuitively, we expect the error introduced to be small for small values of
r
, since the increments are coherent with the ones of the original RwM at least
(1-r)
-fraction of the times, so that, the Markov chain at those steps follows the correct transition kernel in (
1
).
Also, the size of the other increments is bounded by the size of
Z
, which has Gaussian tails.
The error of the Approximate Picard algorithm on the invariant distribution is numerically assessed in Section
6
for tolerance levels
r
ranging from 0% to 20%, while a rigorous analysis of the error introduced by taking
r>0
is outside the scope of this work.

6
Numerical simulations

We assess numerically the performance of the (Approximate) Online Picard algorithm applied to RWM and MwG Markov chains for high-dimensional regression models and the SIR epidemic model. The former simulations allow to test performances systematically and assess
the tightness of the bounds developed in Section
3
. The latter experiment illustrates the breadth of our results in a more sophisticated Bayesian problem where the posterior density is not log-concave and is only piecewise continuous. In this setting, state-of-the-art gradient-based methods such as Langevin and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
(
Roberts and Tweedie 1996a
;
Neal et al. 2011
)
cannot be directly used and zeroth-order (gradient-free) methods become a natural choice.

6.1
Algorithmic specification and performance metrics

In all numerical simulations, we set
\xi=c^{\mathrm{(RWM)}}/\sqrt{d}
for RWM,
and innovation
\tilde{Z}=\mathcal{N}(0,c^{\mathrm{(MwG)}})
for MwG (with standard basis). The stepsizes
c^{\mathrm{(RWM)}}
and
c^{\mathrm{(MwG)}}
are tuned to obtain an average Metropolis acceptance-rejection of approximately 23.4% and 40%, respectively, as prescribed by the literature
(
Gelman et al. 1997
;
Gelman et al. 1996
)
.
The code to reproduce the simulations is available at
https://github.com/SebaGraz/ParallelMH.git
.

We define the empirical speedup relative to sequential implementations as the average number of sequential steps simulated per parallel round, i.e.

\hat{G}=L^{(J)}/J\,,

where
J
is the total number of iterations of the (Approximate) Online Picard algorithm.
We can interpret
\hat{G}
as the multiplicative speedup obtained by parallel computing when ignoring
overheads due to parallel execution.

We evaluate the bias introduced with the Approximate Online Picard algorithms by computing the standardized moments:

\mathcal{M}_{r}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{(\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{i}-\mu_{i}(\pi))^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}(\pi)}},\hskip 18.49988pt\mathcal{E}_{r}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{(r)}-\sigma_{i}(\pi)}{\sigma_{i}(\pi)}\right)^{2}}

(12)

where

is the sample mean and
\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{(r)}
is the sample standard deviation of the
i
th element estimated through the Markov chain
X_{0:N}
simulated with the Approximate Picard algorithm with tolerance level
r
while
\mu_{i}(\pi)
and
\sigma_{i}(\pi)
are the mean and standard deviation of the
i

6.4
SIR epidemic model

Let
\{Y(t)\in\{S,I,R\}^{M},t\geq 0\}
be an infection process in a population of size
M
. Each coordinate
Y_{i}(t)
takes values

\displaystyle y_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}S&\text{if $i$ is \emph{susceptible} at time $t$}\\
I&\text{if $i$ is \emph{infected} at time $t$}\\
R&\text{if $i$ is \emph{removed} at time $t$}\\
\end{cases},

\displaystyle i=1,2,\dots,M.

Each coordinate process
t\to Y_{i}(t)
is allowed to change state in the following direction:
S\to I\to R
. In particular the coordinate process changes its state from
S
to
I
according to an inhomogeneous Poisson process with rate
t\to\beta_{0}\mathcal{I}_{t}
where
\beta_{0}>0
and
\mathcal{I}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}(y_{i}(t)=I)
is the total number of infected individuals at time
t
. The transition from
I
to
R
of each individual occurs according to an exponential random variable with rate equal to
\gamma_{0}>0
. The parameters
\beta_{0}
and
\gamma_{0}
are fundamental as they jointly determine the
reproduction number

R_{0}=M\beta_{0}/\gamma_{0}
. See
Baily 1975
for a thorough mathematical description of this model and its extensions.

Let
d\leq M
be the total number of individuals that have been infected during the epidemic. We assume that the epidemic has originated with 1 initial infected individual and has been observed until the epidemic is extincted, that is, after the last infected infected individual has been removed.

As it is common for this class of models, we do not observe the infection times (i.e. when the coordinate process changes from
S\to I
) and we only observe the times where infected individuals change their state from
I
to
R
. Hereafter, we denote by
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the
latent
space of infection times and by
x^{\circ}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
x_{i}\leq x^{\circ}_{i},\,1\leq i\leq d
, the
observed
removed times of the infected population. We assume that
\gamma_{0},\beta_{0}
are unknown. The likelihood for
(\gamma_{0},\beta_{0})
becomes tractable only when the infection times of each infected individual are known and is given by

\exp\left(-\beta A(x,x^{\circ})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq k(x)\end{subarray}}^{d}\log(\beta\mathcal{I}_{x_{i}-})\right)\\
\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\gamma\exp(-\gamma(x^{\circ}_{i}-x_{i}))\mathbf{1}(x_{i}\leq x^{\circ}_{i})\right).

Here
k=\arg\min(x)
is the label of the first infected individual,
t-=\lim_{s\uparrow t}s
the left-hand limit and

A(x,x^{\circ})=\int_{x_{k}}^{T}\mathcal{I}_{t}\mathcal{S}_{t}\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left((M-d)(x_{j}^{\circ}-x_{j})+\sum_{i=1}^{d}(x_{j}^{\circ}\wedge x_{i}-x_{j}\wedge x_{i})\right),

(13)

with
T=\max(x^{\circ}_{1},x^{\circ}_{2},\dots,x^{\circ}_{d})
and
\mathcal{S}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}(y_{i}(t)=S)
, is the total number of person-to-person units of infectious pressure exerted during the course of the epidemic, see
(
Neal and Roberts 2005
, Section 5)
for details of the likelihood and posterior measure in the set-up considered here.

We set two independent Gamma priors for
\gamma
and
\beta
with prior parameters
(\nu_{\beta},\lambda_{\beta})
and
(\nu_{\gamma},\lambda_{\gamma})
respectively. We then integrate
\gamma,\beta
out the posterior and get the marginal posterior density of the latent space of infection time:

\pi(x)\propto\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq k\end{subarray}}^{d}\mathcal{I}_{x_{i}-}\right)\left(\lambda_{\beta}+A(x,x^{\circ})\right)^{-(d+\nu_{\beta}-1)}\\
\left(\lambda_{\gamma}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}(x_{i}^{\circ}-x_{i})\right)^{-(d+\nu_{\gamma})}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mathbf{1}(x_{i}\leq x^{\circ}_{i})\right).

(14)

This posterior density is only piecewise continuous, with regions of discontinuity in correspondence to changes of the order of the combined vector of infection and removal times
(x,x^{\circ})
. Once
x
is simulated, we can obtain posterior samples of
\gamma
and
\beta
generating them from the conditional distributions

\gamma\mid x\sim\Gamma(d+\nu_{\gamma},\,1/(\lambda_{\gamma}+B(x,x^{\circ})),\hskip 18.49988pt\beta\mid x\sim\Gamma(d+\nu_{\beta}-1,\,1/(\lambda_{\beta}+A(x,x^{\circ}))

(15)

where
B(x,x^{\circ})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(x^{\circ}_{i}-x_{i})
and
A(x,x^{\circ})
as in (
13
).

We simulate synthetic data and adopt the same experimental setting of
Neal and Roberts 2005
, that is we set

M=200
,
\beta=0.001
and
\gamma=0.15
. With this setting, the reproduction number is equal to
R=4/3
. The forward model generates
d=98
infected individuals before the epidemic was extincted.

We set weak uninformative priors for
\beta
and
\gamma
with parameters
\lambda_{\beta}=\lambda_{\beta}=0.001
and
\nu_{\beta}=\nu_{\beta}=1
and run the Online-Picard applied to both RMW and MwG with target density as in (
14
) and for
N=10^{6}
steps and with
K=\lfloor\sqrt{d}\rfloor=9

7
Extensions

We outline here some extensions of this work, which are left for future research:

•

The Online Picard recursion (
3
) sets
X^{(j+1)}_{i}
for
i\geq U^{(j)}
to a constant value equal to the last coordinate of the Picard map
\Phi
. While this choice is simple and reasonable, it does not exploit the intrinsic dynamics of the underlying Markov chain and the noise variables beyond the time index
U^{(j)}
. More elaborate recursions are possible, combining Picard maps with inexpensive predictive functions that estimate the values of the Markov chain at those steps where the Picard map is not been applied yet. An example of such schemes is provided by the (stochastic) Parareal algorithm
(
Lions et al. 2001
;
Pentland et al. 2022
)
.

•

By analyzing the family of approximate measures
\pi_{r}
for
r>0
in Section
5.1
, it may be possible to establish the parallel round complexity of the Approximate Online Picard algorithm. Our numerical results suggest that this algorithm can achieve a parallel round complexity of order
\mathcal{O}(1)
using
\mathcal{O}(d)
parallel processors.

•

The proof of Proposition
1
shows that

L^{(j+1)}=L^{(j)}+G^{(j)}

with
G^{(j)}=G(\bar{X}^{(j)},\bar{W}^{(j)})
and
G
a deterministic function.
Assuming that
(\bar{X}^{(j)},\bar{W}^{(j)})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
has a unique invariant measure
\rho
, then we can interpret
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}(G(\bar{X},\bar{W}))
as the
average speedup
relative to the sequential algorithm in stationarity and
N/\mathbb{E}_{\rho}(G(\bar{X},\bar{W}))
is the expected number of parallel iterations of the Online Picard algorithm for simulating the Markov chain
X_{0:N}
.
This suggest that trying to characterize explicitly the invariant distribution
\rho
might be a promising way to improve the analysis of the Online Picard algorithm, e.g. going beyond classical log-concavity or obtaining more explicit expressions.

Background lemmas

We first state some background lemmas that will be helpful to streamline the proofs of the main results.

Lemma 1
.

Let
X\sim\mathcal{E}(\lambda)
, where
\mathcal{E}(\lambda)
denotes the exponential distribution with rate
\lambda
.
Then for any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
we have
\mathbb{P}(X\in[a\wedge b,a\vee b])\leq\mathbb{P}(X\leq a\vee b-a\wedge b)
, where
a\wedge b
and
a\vee b
denote, respectively, the minimum and maximum between
a
and
b
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, let
a\leq b
. For the cases
a,b\leq 0
and
a\leq 0,b\geq 0
, Lemma
1
is trivial, while for
a,b\geq 0
, it
follows from the fact that the density function of an exponential random variable is non-increasing.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
with
\sigma\geq 0
.
Then, for every
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\mathbb{E}[|\langle v,Z\rangle|]=\|v\|\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\sigma.

Proof.

By rotational invariance of
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
, we have
\mathbb{E}[|\langle v,Z\rangle|=\|v\|\mathbb{E}[|Z_{1}|]
,
where
|Z_{1}|
follows a one-dimensional folded normal distribution and has mean
\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\sigma
.
∎

Lemma 3
.

Let
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
and
M
be a
d\times d
random matrix independent of
Z
. Then

\mathbb{E}[Z^{\top}MZ]=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{E}[M])
.

Proof.

\mathbb{E}[Z^{\top}MZ]=\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}Z_{j}]\mathbb{E}[M_{i,j}]=\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[M_{i,i}]=\sigma^{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{E}[M])
.
∎

Lemma 4
.

For any non-negative random variable
Z
and constant
c>0
, we have

\mathbb{E}[Z\mathbf{1}(Z>c)]=\int_{c}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(Z\geq u)]\mathrm{d}u+c\mathbb{P}(Z>c).

Proof.

Follows from

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z\mathbf{1}(Z>c)]-c\mathbb{P}(Z>c)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\int_{0}^{Z}\mathbf{1}(u>c)\mathrm{d}u]=\mathbb{E}[\int_{c}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}(u<Z)\mathrm{d}u]

\displaystyle=\int_{c}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}(u<Z)\mathrm{d}u]=\int_{c}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(Z\geq u)\mathrm{d}u\,,

where we used Fubini-Tonelli’s Theorem in the third equality.
∎

Lemma 9
.
