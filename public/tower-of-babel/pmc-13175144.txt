Effect of Donepezil on Dimensions of Patient-Reported Cognitive Impairment in Breast Cancer Survivors: Secondary Analysis of the NCORP REMEMBER Trial (WF-97116)
Authors: Alayna E. Ernster, Emily V. Dressler, W. Mark Brown, Glenn J. Lesser, Kathryn E. Weaver, Vinay K. Gudena, William Fusselman, Heidi D. Klepin, Edward G. Shaw, Lynne I. Wagner, Stephen R. Rapp
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PURPOSECancer-related cognitive impairment (CRCI) is a prevalent and persistent problem among many breast cancer survivors (BCS). Results from the randomized, placebo-controlled REMEMBER clinical trial (WF-97116) showed no improvement in cognitive test performance among BCS 1-5 years after chemotherapy after 24 weeks of daily donepezil compared with placebo. Here we present results for an important secondary outcome, patient-reported cognitive impairment, and explore the correlates of CRCI.PATIENTS AND METHODSWomen age ≥18 years with a history of breast cancer who completed ≥4 cycles of adjuvant cytotoxic chemotherapy 1-5 years before enrollment and self-reported CRCI were eligible. We enrolled participants through the Wake Forest National Cancer Institute Community Oncology Research Program (NCORP) Research Base and randomly assigned participants to a 24-week course of once daily donepezil (single 5 mg dose titrated to 10 mg after 6 weeks) or placebo. We administered the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy-Cognitive Function (FACT-Cog), a patient-reported outcome measure, at baseline and weeks 12, 24 (end of intervention), and 36 (wash-out). Mixed-effects repeated-measures analysis of covariance models assessed treatment differences in four FACT-Cog subscales. Additionally, demographic, clinical, and cognitive performance correlates of CRCI severity at baseline were examined.RESULTSA total of 276 patients (mean age, 57.1; standard deviation, 10.5) from 87 NCORP sites were randomly assigned to donepezil (n = 140) or placebo (n = 136). There were no statistically significant treatment differences on the four CRCI outcomes: perceived cognitive impairments, cognitive abilities, impact on quality of life, and observations by others at 24 weeks. CRCI severity at baseline was associated with demographic variables, cognitive performance, and comorbidities, but not cancer-related variables.CONCLUSIONThere were no discernible reductions in patient-reported CRCI attributable to donepezil, consistent with results for cognitive performance. CRCI severity appears to be multidimensional in nature, suggesting multifactorial prevention and treatment approaches might be beneficial.

INTRODUCTION

Following the receipt of adjuvant or neoadjuvant chemotherapy, many breast cancer survivors (BCS) experience cancer-related cognitive impairment (CRCI) that can persist for months to years.1-3 Although prevalence estimates vary, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis indicated that one in three BCS experience clinically significant CRCI4 and as many as 61% exhibit CRCI at 1 year after treatment.5 CRCI varies in severity and commonly affects patients’ levels of functioning and quality of life (QOL).

CRCI among BCS likely is due to multiple factors including cancer severity, treatments (surgery, chemotherapy, and/or endocrine therapy), comorbidities, hormonal changes, genetic factors, age, and stress. A full understanding of the specific mechanisms underlying CRCI remains unclear.4 As such, finding efficacious treatments for CRCI has been elusive. Repurposing cognitive-enhancing medications shown to be useful in noncancer patient populations is one approach to treating CRCI in cancer survivors. One such drug is donepezil hydrochloride, an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor that has proven effective in treating cognitive impairment among individuals with Alzheimer’s dementia,6 vascular dementia,7 Parkinson’s disease,8 traumatic brain injury,9 and primary brain tumors.10

The REMEMBER study was a phase III randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial that assessed the efficacy of donepezil to treat CRCI among BCS 1-5 years after chemotherapy and has been previously described.11 Participants’ cognitive performance was directly assessed with a battery of neurocognitive tests, and their reported cognitive function was assessed with the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy–Cognition Version 3 (FACT-Cog).12 Initial results showed that after 24 weeks of daily treatment, there was no difference in neurocognitive test performance between BCS randomly assigned to donepezil or placebo nor was there a difference in the overall FACTCog score.11 However, just as cognitive performance includes distinct domains (eg, memory, language, processing speed, executive functions), so patient-reported CRCI references distinct domains of functioning, which are the focus of this report. Patient-reported CRCI is quite possibly the more important outcome to survivors because it captures patients’ experiences directly within the context of the cognitive demands encountered in their daily lives and therefore yields valuable unique information complementary to neurocognitive test performance. In this study, we examine four specific domains of patient-reported CRCI measured with the FACT-Cog v3: perceived cognitive impairments, perceived cognitive abilities, effects on QOL, and observations by others. Additionally, we explored how the severity of patient-reported CRCI is related to cancer treatment, neurocognitive performance, and clinical and demographic factors. Better phenotyping of CRCI may ultimately lead to more effective preventive and treatment approaches.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Sample

Female adult (age ≥18 years) BCS who received ≥4 cycles of adjuvant/neoadjuvant cytotoxic chemotherapy 1-5 years before enrollment with no evidence of suspected recurrent or metastatic disease were eligible to participate. Potentially eligible participants must have exhibited an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) score ≤2 and responded Yes to two questions: “Since you received chemotherapy have you had difficulty with thinking or remembering as well as you did before your diagnosis? For example, have you had difficulty thinking clearly, remembering things, finding words, or doing more than one mental thing at a time?” and “Do you still have some difficulty?” To further ensure that we enrolled BCS with CRCI, we administered a single learning trial of the Hopkins Verbal Learning Test–Revised (HVLT-R) and required a score below the mean for a noncancer normative group13 and below the median from our pilot study,14 which in both cases was 7. The HVLT-R form was different from the one used in the study. We enrolled participants at 87 community oncology practices affiliated with the Wake Forest National Cancer Institute Community Oncology Research Program (NCORP) Research Base (UG1CA189824). Patients were allowed to be on trastuzumab or other human epidermal growth factor receptor 2–directed therapies along with ongoing hormonal therapy for breast cancer if they remained on the same agent at least 3 months before enrollment and continued for the duration of the study (9 months). Antidepressant, anxiolytic, sleep, and narcotic medications were allowed if the patient had been on the medication for at least 12 weeks with a stable dose ≥4 weeks, although previous use of these medications must have stopped ≥4 weeks before enrollment. Survivors were excluded if they (1) received succinylcholine/acetylcholinesterase inhibitors or cognitive enhancers (donepezil, galantamine, rivastigmine, tacrine, memantine, methylphenidate, dextroamphetamine) 4 weeks before enrollment; (2) had a history of dementia, Alzheimer’s disease, multi-infarct dementia, or clinically significant cerebrovascular events; (3) were diagnosed with a medical condition known to affect cognition (eg, traumatic brain injury, multiple sclerosis, acute severe fatigue, chronic fatigue syndrome, fibromyalgia, severe depression, seizure disorder); and/or (4) were currently pregnant. A complete description of inclusion and exclusion criteria can be found in Rapp et al.11

Design

Participants who met eligibility criteria were stratified by age (<50, 50-59, 60-69, ≥70 years) and randomly assigned with variable block sizes 1:1 allocation to donepezil or placebo. Patients randomly assigned to donepezil self-administered a single daily 5-mg dose for 6 weeks (weeks 0-6) and if tolerated, a single daily 10-mg dose for 18 weeks (weeks 7-24). Treatment assignment was blinded to all participants, investigators, research staff, clinicians, and statisticians. RxCrossroads by McKesson held unblinded assignment and distributed donepezil or placebo. Certified site staff administered measures in a standardized manner before random assignment (baseline) and 12 weeks, 24 weeks (treatment termination), and 36 weeks (wash-out) after random assignment.

Measures

Patient-Reported Measures

Patient-reported CRCI was assessed with the validated FACT-Cog.12 The FACT-Cog includes four subscales: perceived cognitive impairments (PCI, n = 20 items, score range, 0-80), perceived cognitive abilities (PCA, n = 9 items, score range, 0-36), comments from others (CFO, n = 4 items, score range, 0-16), and impact on QOL (n = 4 items, score range, 0-16). All items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 4 describing frequency (PCI, CFO) or severity (PCA, QOL), and higher scores reflect better cognitive functioning. A total FACT-Cog score is the sum of the subscales with a range of 0-148. We also measured fatigue with the 7-item Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Fatigue scale (range, 7-35)15 and sleep disturbance with the 8-item PROMIS Sleep Disturbance Scale (range, 8-40).16 For PROMIS measures, higher scores indicate more of the construct being measured (ie, more fatigue, more sleep disturbance). Depressive symptoms were measured with the Patient Health Questionnaire–9 (PHQ-9) scale (range, 0-27), with higher scores reflecting greater depression symptom severity.17

Cognitive Tests

Verbal learning and memory were assessed with the HVLT-R18 which includes scores for immediate recall, delayed recall, and recognition memory. Attention and executive function were measured with the Trail Making Test (TMT) Part A and Part B, respectively.19 Verbal fluency was assessed with the Controlled Oral Word Association (COWA) test sum of words generated for three letters.20 Working memory was measured with the Digit Span Test–Backward (score range, 0-14).21 Processing speed was measured with the total Digit Symbol-Coding test.21 The Shipley Institute of Living Scale–Version 2 Vocabulary subtest (SILS-V)22 was used to estimate premorbid cognitive function (score range, 0-40). Higher scores indicate better functioning for all cognitive tests, except TMT.

Additional Variables

At baseline, demographics factors (age, BMI, menopausal status, marital status, education, ethnicity, race, and job) and cancer-related factors (chemotherapy regimen, cancer stage, current hormonal therapy) were also assessed with standardized questions.

Analyses

We determined sample size to detect a treatment difference at 90% power for the primary outcome of the REMEMBER trial (ie, memory test performance on the HVLT-R immediate recall scores).11 We tested treatment differences for each FACT-Cog subscale via mixed-effects repeated-measures analysis of covariance (RMANCOVA) models with model covariates of treatment, time (12, 24, and 36 weeks), time by treatment interaction, baseline outcome level, age stratification, and an unstructured covariance matrix to account for within-participant correlation over time. We report least squares means and 95% CIs, stratified by treatment and time, for the difference between treatment arms at each time. Post hoc sensitivity analyses included baseline education levels, fatigue, sleep disturbance, and depression, as well as separate analyses considering treatment interaction with endocrine therapy, and menopausal status was assessed in RMANCOVA models defined above.

We conducted analyses with the intention-to-treat population, regardless of intervention or amount received, and defined statistical significance as P < .05. All tests were two-sided, and the presented P values are unadjusted. We defined study adherence by the number of pills consumed throughout the trial divided by the ideal number that should have been consumed, calculated via self-report pill diaries.

To examine the relationship between patient-reported CRCI severity and cognitive test performance, demographic and clinical factors, and comorbidities of fatigue, sleep disturbance, and depression, we trichotomized the FACT-Cog v3 PCI subscale scores at baseline. Kruskal-Wallis and chi-square tests for differences were used for continuous and categorical variables, respectively.

The protocol was approved by the National Cancer Institute (NCI) Central Institutional Review Board, and participants signed informed consent before enrollment (Clinical-Trials.gov identifier: NCT02822573).

RESULTS

Study Population

As reported previously,11 we screened 530 BCS for enrollment, of which 342 (81.5%) were eligible for participation and 276 (52.1%) were randomly assigned (Fig 1). There were no significant differences in dropout rates between done-pezil (21.4%) and placebo (14.0%) throughout the study (P = .11). The mean age was 57.1 years (standard deviation [SD], 10.5). Participants were predominantly White (93.8%), non-Hispanic (93.8%), peri- or postmenopausal (92.0%), diagnosed with stage I (29.0%) or II (50.4%) local breast cancer, and currently taking hormonal therapy (69.6%). Nearly half (45.7%) were treated with anthracycline-based chemotherapy. The average time since chemotherapy completion was 29.6 months (SD, 14.2; Table 1). Adherence based on returned diaries was 93% and 95% for the done-pezil and placebo groups, respectively. Adherence defined as missing <20% of total expected doses, and conservatively counted as missed when a diary was not returned, was not statistically different across groups (59.1% donepezil, 67.7% placebo, P = .15). Postrandomization baseline scores differed between donepezil and placebo groups on the following FACT-Cog subscales: perceived cognitive impairment, comments from others, and perceived cognitive abilities—all of which indicated less CRCI in the placebo group at baseline (Table 2).

Treatment Effects

At the end of treatment (week 24), there were no statistically significant group differences on any FACT-Cog subscales after adjusting for baseline outcome scores, age groups, time point, and treatment group (Table 3). Both groups improved on all FACT-Cog subscales at weeks 12, 24, and 36 (Appendix Table A1). Post hoc analyses controlled for baseline education levels, sleep, fatigue, and depression. Separate analyses considering treatment interaction with endocrine therapy and menopausal status also did not demonstrate significant differences on FACT-Cog subscale scores across treatment groups.

Correlates of CRCI Severity

Trichotomizing the baseline PCI scores yielded three levels of patient-reported CRCI severity—higher (n = 89; range, 0-23), medium (n = 90; range, 24-38), and lower (n = 89; range, 39-68). Severity groups differed significantly by age, education, and marital status (P < .05) but not employment status (P = .11). Groups also differed significantly on age at diagnosis (P = .006), but not time since cancer treatment, current use of hormone therapy, menopausal status, or chemotherapy regimen (P > .05). On cognitive test performance, groups significantly differed on the estimated premorbid educational achievement (SILS-V, P = .03), executive function (TMT-Part B, P = .02), and verbal fluency (COWA, P = .03), but not memory (HVLT-R parameters), attention/concentration (Digit Span–Backward), or processing speed (Digit Symbol) tests (P > .05). There were also significant group differences on fatigue (P < .0001), sleep disturbance (P < .0001), and depressive severity (P < .0001; Table 4). BCS reporting more severe CRCI were slightly older, more likely to be married, diagnosed at a younger age, less formally educated, and had a lower estimated premorbid cognitive level. They also performed more poorly on the cognitive tests measuring executive function (TMT-Part B) and verbal fluency (COWA). More severely impaired BCS also reported more fatigue, sleep disturbance, and depressive symptom severity compared with survivors reporting less CRCI at baseline.

DISCUSSION

It is important to assess both survivors’ cognitive performance and experienced cognitive functioning because each provides unique information. PRO-assessed CRCI provides valuable insights into patients’ everyday experiences with CRCI within the context of the diverse demands encountered in daily life, the need for compensatory strategies, and the overall impact of cognitive function on patients’ functionality, well-being, and QOL.

This study sought to determine whether random assignment to a 24-week course of daily donepezil improved four distinct dimensions of patient-reported CRCI compared with placebo among BCS 1-5 years after chemotherapy. Changes in patient-reported cognitive impairments, cognitive abilities, QOL, and observations by others were not different across treatment groups at 24 weeks (end of treatment). These results parallel our previously reported results with neurocognitive performance outcomes,11 strengthening the conclusion that donepezil offers no discernible benefit as a treatment for CRCI to BCS 1-5 years following chemotherapy.

Several questions are raised by our results. First, was the CRCI reported at baseline severe enough to expect a treatment effect? Our sample had a mean FACT-Cog PCI subscale score of 31.3 well below (worse than) a previously reported cut point of 60 used to identify cases of CRCI in BCS.23 Second, what might have caused the improvement in CRCI scores over time independent of treatment assignment? An expectancy effect engendered when enrolling in a clinical study24 or regression to the mean resulting from enrolling BCS with low memory scores25 may partly explain why we saw improvement. Inclusion of a control group prevented misattributing the improvement to donepezil. The third question relates to the nature of self-reported CRCI, namely, what clinical and nonclinical factors are associated with CRCI severity that might lead to better phenotyping and inform the development of effective treatment approaches? When we examined PCI severity scores at baseline, an interesting variety of factors differentiated the groups. Sociodemographic factors including age, education, and a marker of educational achievement differed by severity, hinting that social determinants of health may contribute to CRCI. Somewhat surprisingly, other than age at diagnosis, cancer-related clinical factors like chemotherapy regimen, use of hormonal therapy, or menopausal status were unrelated to severity. As expected, cognitive performance differences in several domains (executive functioning and verbal fluency) were associated with self-reported CRCI severity, which is consistent with previous research26-28 Finally, markers of general well-being and comorbidities including sleep disturbance, fatigue, and depression were robustly and positively associated with patient-reported CRCI severity. Taken together, this suggests that CRCI may be more multifaceted than currently conceptualized, thus requiring multicomponent interventions that address cognitive functioning both directly and indirectly. Common comorbidities like fatigue, sleep disturbance, and depression are candidate targets for treating CRCI in addition to physical activity, cognitive rehabilitation, meditation, and cognitive behavioral therapy which have produced positive results.29-34 Whether these diverse treatments are offered sequentially in a stepped fashion or concurrently could be the focus of future research. Memantine, a neurotransmitter modulator, showed benefit in preventing CRCI in patients with brain tumor receiving brain radiotherapy35 and patients with breast cancer receiving chemotherapy.36 Additional research is also needed to more fully phenotype CRCI. Frailty, psychological resilience, coping style, medical comorbidities, and stress could also contribute to CRCI. This could identify possible subtypes which might lead to better matching of treatment(s) to patient. A deepened understanding of the underlying mechanisms of CRCI, including genetic and epigenetic factors, is also needed.

Limitations of this study include the predominately non-Hispanic White sample which limits generalizability. We did not have variables related to treatment history and other health, medical, or genetic factors which may be important in understanding the mechanisms of CRCI37 and in identifying subtypes. Despite random assignment, we reported baseline difference on the FACT-Cog subscales. By controlling for baseline FACT-Cog scores in our analyses, we have mitigated this potential problem. Perhaps had we intervened closer in time to the chemotherapy treatment we might have seen a treatment effect, although all participants reported clinically meaningful CRCI at enrollment, an average of 2 years after chemotherapy ended. Study strengths include enrollment of BCS from the geographically diverse network of community-based cancer treatment sites affiliated with the Wake Forest NCI Community Oncology Research Program Research Base and the use of well-validated, patient-reported outcomes measures recommended for the study of CRCI.38

In conclusion, a daily dose of donepezil for 24 weeks did not benefit patient-reported perceived cognitive impairments or abilities, their QOL, or observations by others of their functioning among BCS 1-5 years following chemotherapy. The severity of reported CRCI is likely associated with diverse factors, suggesting the possible benefit of multicomponent treatment approaches.
