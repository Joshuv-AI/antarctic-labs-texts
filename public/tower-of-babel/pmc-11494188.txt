Measurement and analysis of condylar morphology and thickness of the roof of the glenoid fossa in patients with unilateral second molar scissor bite
Authors: Gang Wang, Yu Feng, Jin-Qiao Tang, Jing-Jing Zhang, Xiao-Qin Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective The objective of this study was to measure and analyze the joint space, condylar morphology, and thickness of the roof of the glenoid fossa (RGF) in patients with unilateral second molar scissor bite using cone beam CT (CBCT). Methods A total of 80 patients were included in this study. Forty patients with a normal posterior occlusal relationship, who attended the Department of Orthodontics at the First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University from December 2021 to August 2023, were selected as the control group. The experimental group consisted of 40 patients with unilateral second molar scissor bite during the same period. All patients underwent CBCT scanning, and the resulting images were analyzed using the NNT viewer software in multiplanar reconstruction (MPR). Axial, coronal, and sagittal adjustments were made for each patient. Results In the control group, the right and left joint spaces (PS, SS, AS), the height of the upper part of the condyle, and the depth of the glenoid fossa did not show statistically significant differences (P > 0.05). When comparing bilateral condylar morphology and joint space in the experimental group, it was found that the posterior joint space on the scissor bite side was greater than that on the normal occlusion side, while the anterior joint space on the scissor bite side was smaller than that on the normal occlusal side. Additionally, the height of the upper condyle and the depth of the glenoid fossa on the scissor bite side were greater than that on the normal occlusal side, with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05). However, there were no statistically significant differences in SS, internal and external joint diameter, as well as anterior and posterior diameters (P > 0.05). The comparison of the minimum thickness of the roof of the glenoid fossa in both the sagittal and coronal directions between the patients in the experimental group and the control group showed no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05). Furthermore, the asymmetry index of the supracondylar height in the control group was significantly smaller than that in the experimental group, displaying a statistically significant difference (P < 0.05). The differences in the asymmetry indices of the anterior and posterior diameters of the condyle, as well as the inner and outer diameters, were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). When analyzing the position of the condyle in the sagittal direction of the glenoid fossa in the patients of the control group, it was observed that the majority were in the anterior and medial positions, with only 10% and 5% being in the posterior position. However, the analysis of the condyle position in the sagittal direction of the glenoid fossa in the experimental group revealed statistically significant differences (P < 0.05), with the condyle position on the scissor bite side being more anterior. Conclusion Unilateral second molar scissor bite can result in anterior displacement of the condyle, greater height of the condyle superiorly as well as the depth of the glenoid fossa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-76216-0.Keywords: Scissor bite, Roof of the glenoid fossa, CondyleSubject terms: Anatomy, Medical research

Introduction

A crossbite is is the inconsistency of the buccolingual relationship between the upper and lower teeth. Scissor bite is common in orthodontic treatment1.This condition is typically observed unilaterally or bilaterally2. Common etiological factors contributing to scissor bite include premature loss of deciduous teeth and abnormal positioning of permanent dental embryos. Crossbite can be divided into buccal crossbite and scissor bite.The term buccal crossbite refers to the buccal cusps of the lower teeth occlude buccal to the buccal cusps of the upper teeth. Scissor bite refers to the condition when the buccal cusps of the lower teeth occlude lingual to the lingual cusps of the upper teeth.Cross bite malocclusion can have a skeletal or dental component or combination of both.Scissor bite is more commonly encountered in clinical practice3. Lombardo4 discovered a higher prevalence of scissor bite and crowding in the permanent dentition as compared to the deciduous dentition. Scissor bite is a rare occlusal relationship where the lingual surfaces of the upper posterior teeth and the buccal surfaces of the lower posterior teeth exhibit varying degrees of abrasion during oral examination. This condition restricts the patient’s lateral occlusion, with a prevalence rate of 0.3 − 1.5%5,6. It is more likely to occur in women and on the right side7, but the mechanism is not clear.The imbalance in masticatory forces on both sides of the scissor bite may result in the patient developing a unilateral chewing habit, potentially leading to temporomandibular disorders(TMDs).

TMDs are highly prevalent in the population8. According to Macrì M9, 23.23% of patients with scissor bite were found to have TMDs, indicating a correlation between scissor bite and TMDs. Barrera-Mora10 conducted a comprehensive study suggesting that unilateral scissor bite should be regarded as a risk factor for TMDs. In a scissor bite relationship, the abnormal buccolingual position of the posterior teeth hinders the transmission of occlusal forces along their long axes11. Consequently, this condition not only leads to occlusal trauma but also compromises periodontal tissues and disrupts the balance of muscle dynamics in the related groups12. Scholars have previously highlighted the correlation between scissor bite and TMDs. However, they failed to address its impact on the structure of the temporomandibular joint(TMJ), as well as its effects on morphology. Therefore, further research is required to investigate these aspects.Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) is a standard pre-orthodontic treatment examination that offers enhanced visualization of condylar morphology and the thickness of the roof glenoid fossa (RGF) from a three-dimensional perspective13. This study employed CBCT to measure and analyze condylar morphology and RGF thickness in patients exhibiting scissor bite involving the second molars. The findings offer a valuable theoretical foundation for clinical diagnosis and treatment.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

The imaging data for this study were sourced from the Department of Orthodontics at the First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University between October 1st 2022 and September 1st 2023. The experimental group comprised 40 patients (24 males and 16 females), aged 14–18 years, with unilateral second molar scissor bite and intraoral manifestations. The average age of this group was 16.8 years. The control group consisted of forty patients (18 males and 22 females) aged 14–18 years, with a mean age of 16.3 years, who had normal overdenture and overlay relationship of their posterior teeth and attended the clinic within the same period. This experiment was reviewed by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University. Ethics No.KYLL-2023-106.

Inclusion criteria14: (1) permanent dentition (excluding the third molar) with no missing teeth; (2) absence of moderate or severe crowding in the teeth; (3) Angle Class I with neutral bilateral first molar relationship; (4) healthy periodontal tissue; (5) normal development of the maxillofacial region, absence of significant maxillofacial asymmetry, and no history of maxillofacial trauma; (6) no history of localized pain or popping in the TMJ, and normal range of jaw opening; (7) no previous orthodontic treatment or condylar hypertrophy.

Data collection

The same CBCT machine (NewTom VG10048S, Verona, Italy) was used in all patients before orthodontic treatment, and the same technician in the Department of Stomatological Imaging of the First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University performed the photography. The parameters: voltage 110 kV, scanning time 3.6 s, scanning field 15 cm × 15 cm, minimum axial thickness 0.3 mm. During the CBCT scanning, the patients were asked to maintain an upright sitting posture and a natural relaxed state, with the Frankfort Horizontal (FH) plane parallel to the floor, and the intraoral occlusion in the Intercuspal position. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University (Ethics No. KYLL-2023-106). All participants provided written informed consent, and consent was obtained from a legal guardian for participants who were younger than 16 years of age.

Measurement and analysis

Measurement parameters, as referenced in15,16, included the following: sagittal posterior joint space (PS), superior joint space (SS), anterior joint space (AS) (as shown in Fig. 1): the sagittal direction was used as the vertical line of the orbital ear plane and the tangent line of the anteroposterior convex point of the condyle, and then the vertical line of the tangent line crossed the glenoid fossa, and the distance of the vertical line was measured; internal and external diameters (W1): oblique position parallel to the long axis of the condyle; anterior and posterior diameters (W2) (as shown in Fig. 2) : oblique position perpendicular to the long axis of condyle. Additionally, the measurement of the minimum distance in the coronal direction (shown in Fig. 3) and sagittal direction (shown in Fig. 4) was performed the shortest distance from the upper boundary of the glenoid fossa to the middle cranial fossa is the thinnest thickness of the RGF; Superior height of the condyle(shown in Fig. 4): the vertical distance between the condyle apex Co and the horizontal line past the final condyle point; glenoid fossa depth (shown in Fig. 6): vertical distance from the apex of the sagittal glenoid fossa to the line connecting the lowermost point of the articular tubercle to the lowest point of the external auditory canal.Comparison of the minimum thickness of RGF between the two groups: the experimental group was the scissor bite side, and the control group was the average thickness on both sides. The condylar asymmetry index, calculated as (left side - right side) / (left side + right side) × 100%17, and the joint gap index, calculated as (PS − AS) / (PS + AS) × 100, were used to classify the sagittal position of the condyle in the glenoid fossa as anterior, medial, or posterior. Specifically, a condyle with an LR value greater than 12 indicated an anterior position in the glenoid fossa, while an LR value between − 12 and 12 indicated a medial position. Conversely, an LR value smaller than − 12 indicated a posterior position.

Statistical analysis

The measurements were acquired by the participant themselves, with each measurement conducted three times, two weeks apart. The average value was then calculated. The measurement data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 statistical software, and the results were presented as “mean ± standard deviation”. Inter-group comparisons were performed using the independent sample t-test, while intra-group comparisons utilized the paired-sample t-test. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of Joint Space and Condylar morphology between two groups of patients

In the control group, there were no statistically significant differences observed between the right and left sides in terms of the joint space (PS, SS, AS), the height of the upper part of the condyle, and the depth of the glenoid fossa (P > 0.05). However, in the experimental group, the comparison of bilateral condylar morphology and joint space showed that PS was greater on the scissor bite side compared to the normal occlusal side, AS was smaller on the scissor bite side compared to the normal occlusal side, and the height of the upper part of the condyle and the depth of the glenoid fossa were greater on the scissor bite side compared to the normal occlusal side. These differences were statistically significant (P < 0.05). On the other hand, the differences in SS, intra-extra, and anterior-posterior diameters were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Discussion

TMJ, as one of the most complex joints in human body18, is composed of five parts: glenoid fossa, condylar process, articular disc, articular capsule around the joint and articular ligament.It is the only movable joint in the craniomaxillofacial region, located in front of the bilateral tragus, and performs very important functions in the human body, such as breathing, chewing, swallowing and speaking19. The position and shape of the condyle can constantly change due to tooth wear and changes in occlusal relationship. Moreno20 proposed that most patients’ oral and maxillary systems would accept and adapt to changes in occlusal relationship, but a small number of patients would suffer from TMDs due to changes in occlusal relationship. The condyle process as one of the primary growth centers of the mandible, and it contributes to both sagittal and coronal growth21. Changes in condylar morphology during growth and development can result in mandibular asymmetry and, in severe cases, maxillofacial asymmetry.Additionally, the condyle transmits occlusal forces and can undergo adaptive modification due to articular stress. Its position is closely associated with occlusal relationships and different types of malocclusions. A stable and correct condylar position is essential for normal oral function.

With the progress of society, CBCT has been used more widely. As the preferred method for the diagnosis of TMDs, it can accurately observe the bone structure of TMJ from multiple levels, and has the advantages of short acquisition time, low radiation dose and high sensitivity22.Gomes23suggested that CBCT could be considered a reliable tool for assessing condylar morphology. Based on a previous study, Honda24found consistent measured values of RGF (minimum thickness of the roof of the glenoid fossa) using both a Digimatic micrometer and a 3DX image tool. This led to the conclusion that 3D imaging is a valid method for measuring the bone thickness of the RGF. These studies support the validity of CBCT for evaluating the joint and surrounding hard tissues.

In this study, there was no statistically significant difference in the measurement of joint space and condylar morphology between the two sides of the control group (P > 0.05). This suggests that in individuals with normal occlusion, the condyles were typically positioned in the center of the glenoid fossa, and the morphology of both condyles was relatively symmetrical. However, in the experimental group, it was observed that the anterior space (AS) on the scissor bite side was smaller compared to the normal occlusal side (P < 0.05),the posterior space on the scissor bite side was smaller than that on the normal occlusal side (P < 0.05), indicating anterior displacement of the condyles. Furthermore, in the experimental group, it was determined that the height of the condyle (h) and the depth of the glenoid fossa (D) were greater on the scissor bite side compared to the normal occlusal side (P < 0.05), revealing asymmetry between the condyles of the scissor bite and normal occlusal sides. These differences in height and depth may be linked to adaptive remodeling of the condyle and glenoid fossa25. Contrary to the experimental group, no statistically significant differences were found in the control group for internal and external condylar diameters, as well as anterior and posterior diameters of the condyles (P > 0.05), indicating that the condyles in the control group were mostly symmetrical. In the experimental group, there was no significant difference in condylar inner diameter, outer diameter and anteroposterior diameter (P > 0.05), but there was significant difference in the anterior and posterior joint space (P > 0.05), which may be related to the anterior and posterior hyperplasia of the condyle on the shearing side, that is, the adaptive change of condyle. Wang et al. studied RGF minimum thickness in 128 patients with TMJ symptoms and found no statistical difference between gender and RGF minimum thickness26. In this study, there was no significant difference in the minimum thickness of RGF in the sagittal and coronal directions between the control group and the experimental group (P > 0.05), indicating that the minimum thickness of RGF was not affected by scissor bite.The examination of condylar morphology symmetry in both groups indicated that the asymmetry indices of internal and external diameters, as well as anterior and posterior diameters, did not display statistical significance (P > 0.05). However, significant statistical differences were found in the asymmetry indices of the heights of the supracondylar joints (P < 0.05), highlighting a significant asymmetry in the heights of the supracondylar joints in the experimental group. In the control group, the condyles were primarily located in the anterior and medial positions of the glenoid fossa on both sides, while on the scissor bite side of the experimental group, the condyles were mainly positioned anteriorly, and this difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05). These findings support the idea that unilateral second molar scissor bite represents an incorrect occlusal relationship, which can potentially contribute to joint diseases and facial asymmetry over a prolonged period of time27. Guo28 et al. found that the morphology of the condyle would constantly change due to the extension of the time of scissor bite, which would lead to the increase of the asymmetry index of the condyle. The results of this study are basically consistent with those of the former.

Scissor bite is common in the permanent dentition, and adolescence is a critical period for the eruption of the second molars and the establishment of occlusion, which lasts for a relatively short period of time and is the beginning of unilateral posterior orthoclasticism, so measurement and analysis of the condylar morphology and position of the patients in this period is important to explore the early effects of scissor bite. The patients selected for this experiment were 14 years of age or older, at which time the patients had completed establishing occlusion. Scissor bite can cause masticatory system function disorder, even can cause TMDs, even if the patients have no subjective symptoms, but there are still a potential danger, it can lead to a 3–6 times increased risk of anterior disc displacement of the TMJ29. Lopatienė30showed that long-term unilateral chewing can lead to abnormal dynamic balance of the mandibular muscles, which makes the mandible gradually skew and rotate to the normal occlusal side, resulting in asymmetrical development on both sides of the mandible, resulting in skeletal deformities. Carlsson et al.31. found a correlation between unilateral second molar orthodontia and temporomandibular joint disorder in a 20-year study of 402 patients (r = 0.34,p < 0.01). Wang32 found through numerous studies that occlusal dysfunction leads to TMDs, with posterior crossbite and scissor bite accounting for as much as 23% of the cases. Based on the above scholars’ studies, Li33conducted a deeper study on the condylar morphology of patients with scissor bite. She measured the condylar volume and surface area of patients with unilateral second molar scissor bite and found that the volume and surface area of the condyle on the scissor bite side was larger than that on the normal occlusal side, indicating that positive occlusion had caused condylar hyperplasia on the same side. The relationship between scissor bite and TMDs is still controversial. Some studies have shown that scissor bite may induce TMDs or even osteoarthrosis34,35, and some studies have shown that there is no specific relationship between occlusion and TMDs36, the future should increase the sample size for a deeper research.

The TMJ disc is located between the condyle and the glenoid fossa and corresponds to the morphology of both, and as a biconcave soft tissue, it needs to be visualised by means of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)37. Giozet38pointed out through his study that in the closed mouth position most of the patients with symptoms of articular disc displacement showed a semiconvex or biconvex morphology, however, in the open mouth position the articular disc reverted back to its initial biconcave morphology, suggesting that the position of the condyles affects the morphology of the disc. Thilander39concluded from his study that unilateral posterior orthoclavicular occlusion increases the risk of TMDs as well as the risk of anterior displacement of the articular disc. The above studies by many scholars have shown that a stable disc-condylar relationship is a prerequisite for the healthy exercise of oral function in patients.

In this study, only the condyle and the surrounding hard tissues were measured and analysed by CBCT, and the TMJ disc, which is an important component of the TMJ, was not observed and analysed. In the future, the patient’s imaging data should be improved in order to study the position relation between the articular disc and the condyle further, so as to achieve a more systematic understanding of the hazards of scissor bite of the teeth.

Conclusion

Scissor bite leads to a change in the position of the condyle in the glenoid fossa, which is more anterior to the normal occlusal side, with an increase in the height of the supracondylar process and the depth of the glenoid fossa, and should be corrected at an early stage in order to prevent TMDs and facial asymmetry. Scissor bite has essentially no effect on the minimum thickness of the RGF.CBCT can provide a reliable basis for the diagnosis of scissor bite deformity and orthodontic treatment.
