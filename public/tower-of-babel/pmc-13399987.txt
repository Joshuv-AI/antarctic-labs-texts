Epigenetic Reactivation of SOCS-3 and Inhibition of ERK1/2 Underlie Thymoquinone-Induced Apoptosis in a Chronic Myeloid Leukemia Xenograft Model
Authors: Futoon Abedrabbu Al-Rawashde, Moath Alqaraleh, Diya Hasan, Abdullrahman Elsayed, Nur Anis Ashiela mohd Amiludin, Fouad Saleih Resq Al-Suede, Hamid Ali Nagi Al-Jamal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis research aimed to assess thymoquinone (TQ)’s potential to demethylate and re-express the suppressor of cytokine signaling-3 (SOCS-3) in the chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) mice model.Subject and MethodsA nude mouse model of CML was established, and the apoptotic influence of TQ was identified. TQ’s impact on the target gene expressions was examined using real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), and SOCS-3 methylation was assessed by pyrosequencing. TQ’s effects on the extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1 (ERK1) and ERK2 phosphorylation were assessed using Jess Simple Western analysis. Molecular docking of TQ’s interaction with the anti-apoptotic proteins was assessed.ResultsTQ exerted a marked hypomethylating influence on SOCS-3 and reduced ERK1/2 phosphorylation level in K562-xenograft tumors. TQ also significantly upregulated the apoptosis-related cysteine peptidase (Caspase-3), BCL-2-interacting mediator of cell death (Bim), p53-upregulated modulator of apoptosis (PUMA), and other apoptotic genes and downregulated B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL-2), BCL2-like 2 (BCL-w), survivin, and X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis (XIAP) in K562 cells. TQ also exhibited marked inhibitory properties on BCL-2-related gene expressed in fetal liver (BFL-1), B-cell lymphoma-extra large (BCL-xL), myeloid cell leukemia-1 (MCL-1), and BCL-2 proteins. This led to growth inhibition and apoptosis of the K562-tumour xenograft.ConclusionThese results indicate that TQ enhances CML cells’ apoptosis by hypomethylating and re-expressing SOCS-3, inhibiting ERK1/2, and modulating the apoptosis-regulator gene expression and by showing consistent interaction with most of the anti-apoptotic proteins, highlighting its potential as a scaffold for designing novel inhibitors to combat CML cells.

Introduction

The pathogenesis of chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) is mainly initiated by the breakpoint cluster region-Abelson (BCR-ABL) [1, 2]. The BCR-ABL demonstrates constitutive protein kinase activities, aiding in excessive stimulation of the downstream signaling, that includes the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling, triggering unregulated proliferation and apoptosis of the myeloid cells [1, 3, 4].

Epigenetic silencing of the tumor suppressor genes (TSGs) exerts a crucial role in the leukemogenesis of CML [1, 5]. The suppressor of cytokine signaling-3 (SOCS-3) is a TSG that negatively regulates various proliferative pathways, including MAPK signaling, that ultimately regulate cell survival and the apoptosis process [5, 6]. Hypermethylation induces SOCS-3 epigenetic silencing, which triggers unregulated stimulation of MAPK signaling, leading to increased proliferation of the CML cell [5]. The epigenetic modulating agents that restore SOCS-3 expression have demonstrated a considerable inhibition of leukemia cell survival [5, 7]. However, the conventional DNA hypomethylating agents have insufficient response rates [7]. Therefore, it is imperative to develop new therapies that effectively alter the dysregulated epigenetic mechanisms. Two families of anti-apoptotic proteins control the apoptosis process: BCL-2 (B-cell lymphoma-2) and IAPs (inhibitor of apoptosis) family members [8]. XIAP (X-linked IAP) and survivin belong to the IAP family and control apoptosis. Proteins of the BCL-2 family are classified into two groups: the anti-apoptotic members, which include B-cell lymphoma-extra large (BCL-xL), BCL2-like 2 (BCL-w), BCL-2, induced myeloid leukemia cell differentiation protein (MCL-1), and BCL-2-related gene expressed in fetal liver (BFL-1), and the pro-apoptotic members, like BCL-2 homologous antagonist/killer (Bak), BCL2-associated X protein (Bax), BCL-2-interacting mediator of cell death (Bim), BCL2 modifying factor (Bmf), p53-upregulated modulator of apoptosis (PUMA), BCL-2-associated agonist of cell death (Bad), and BH3-interacting-domain death agonist (Bid) [8, 9]. The extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1 (ERK1) and ERK2 are components of the MAPKs family, which are inhibited by SOCS3 [6, 10]. ERK kinase mediates inhibition of the apoptosis mechanism, which involves suppression of the proapoptotic factors such as Bim, Bad, and Bmf and stimulation of the anti-apoptotic proteins [10]. In addition, ERK signaling controls the expression of survivin, MCL-1, BCL-2, and BCL-xL. ERK1 and ERK2 are constitutively activated by BCR-ABL kinase activity. Thus, it is essential to target ERK signaling to enhance the targeted therapies of CML.

Molecular docking is a widely utilized in silico methodology for analyzing ligands-proteins chemical interactions. It is applied to develop novel inhibitors for the anti-apoptotic proteins and investigate the probable mechanisms behind the actions of newly designed molecules and existing drugs for cancer treatments [3, 5]. Inhibitors of anti-apoptotic proteins provide a promising anticancer therapeutic approach [9]. Thus, the identification of new anti-apoptotic protein inhibitors from natural sources is an effective strategy to enhance cancer cell apoptosis.

Natural phytochemicals provide efficient alternative therapies for cancer [1, 3, 11]. One of the primary phytochemicals found in the seeds of Nigella sativa is thymoquinone (TQ) [1, 3, 11]. TQ has demonstrated anticancer effects by modifying a variety of cellular pathways important for the development of cancer [1–3, 11, 12]. Although TQ’s anticancer effects have been clarified in recent years, the effects of TQ against leukemia and the mechanism driving these effects are still not entirely elucidated.

The aim of this study was to assess TQ’s apoptotic activities in K562 CML cells and mice models of CML. Therefore, the impacts of TQ on SOCS-3 expression and methylation status were assessed. TQ’s influences on the phosphorylation and protein states of ERK1/2, along with the expressions of ERK1 and ERK2 genes, were also evaluated. In addition, TQ’s impacts on the expressions of the pro-apoptotic genes caspase-3, caspase-9, Bim, Bax, Bid, Bad, Bak, PUMA, and Bmf, and the expressions of genes associated with anti-apoptosis, BCL-2, BCL-w, BCL-xL, MCL-1, BFL-1, survivin, and XIAP, along with their influence on apoptosis and cell division in K562-tumor xenograft, were investigated. The molecular docking of TQ, edaravone, obatoclax, venetoclax, and paclitaxel to BCL-2, BFL-1, BCL-xL, and MCL-1 was also examined.

Materials and Methods

Cell Cultures

The cell line of human CML (K562; expressing BCR-ABL) was ordered through the American Type Cultures Collection (ATCC, USA). For the preparation of a complete RPMI culturing media, RPMI was added to one percent penicillin, combined with streptomycin and ten percent fetal bovine serum. The cells were cultured in the complete media and preserved at 37°C in a moist environment inside a carbon dioxide-supplemented incubator (5%). Once the cells attained an adequate confluence, they were utilized for treatments with TQ.

TQ and Imatinib Treatment

TQ with a purity level of >98%, imatinib with a purity of >98%, polysorbate 80 (Tween 80), and dimethyl sulfoxide (2%) were ordered through Sigma-Aldrich (Sigma Aldrich Corp., Louis, USA). For K562 cells treatment, dimethyl sulfoxide was utilized for dissolving TQ, and 27 mm stock solutions were prepared. Following aliquoting, the stock solutions were preserved for treatments at −80°C. Working solutions of 11 µm were prepared before the treatment of the cells. For mouse treatment, polysorbate 80 (Tween 80) was diluted with distilled water (DW) to prepare 0.5% (v/v) Tween 80. TQ was then freshly prepared by dissolving in 0.5% Tween 80 for intraperitoneal injection. Imatinib was dissolved fresh by DW to make a 20 mg/kg concentration to apply intraperitoneal injection to the positive control group of the mice model of CML.

Determination of the Optimal Tolerated Dose of TQ in Nude Mice

Athymic NCR Nu/Nu nude mice weighing 25 ± 2 g and aged 4 to 6 weeks were available from the Eman Biodiscoveries company. Specific cages free of pathogens, comprising high-efficacy particle air filters, were used for mice housing. Mice were provided with sterile water, food, and bedding under standardized 12/12 h light and dark cycles at 24°C room temperature and sixty percent level of moisture. The sterile bedding was replaced every 2 weeks. All experiments were conducted in compliance with the ethical guidelines and with the approval of the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of EMAN Research Ltd., Malaysia/Animal Ethics Approval/31 May 2021/Ref. No.: ethic. 112119A11910621.

To determine the optimal tolerated dose of TQ in nude mice, forty-eight nude mice between the ages of 6 and 8 weeks were sorted at random into eight groups (with 6 mice in each group). One group was used as a control negative group and was injected with 0.2 mL of DW. Groups 2 through 8 received injections of TQ at concentrations of 1 mg/kg, 5 mg/kg, 10 mg/kg, 20 mg/kg, 30 mg/kg, 50 mg/kg, and 100 mg/kg, respectively. The administration schedule was an intraperitoneal injection of one dose per day.

Establishment of the Tumor Xenograft in the Nude Mice

The nude mice between the ages of 6 and 8 weeks were implanted ectopically into the right flank with K562 cells (6 × 106 cells/mL). Following a week of tumor development, mice were sorted at random into four distinct groups (6 mice each). The determined sample size (n = 6 for each group) complies with the xenograft research standards and was sufficient to identify significant variations in the tumor growth and related molecular targets. The negative control group (group 1) was injected with 0.2 mL DW. Group 2 and Group 3 received treatment of TQ at 1 mg/kg and 5 mg/kg dosages, respectively. Group 4 (group of positive control) received 20 mg/kg imatinib. Mice treatments were performed intraperitoneally once a day (wt/wt) for 3 weeks. The body weights and tumor sizes were reported before injecting the treatments and twice a week during the treatment period. The dimensions of the tumors were measured using the Digital Vernier Calliper (TESA ShopCal, Swiss) from three angles: depth, width, and length. The tumor sizes were determined based on the following equation: tumor volume (mm3) = (L × D × W)/2, where L stands for length, D for depth, and W for width. Upon experiment completion, CO2 was used to euthanize the mice before the cervical dislocations. Xenograft tumors were removed and fixed in four percent paraformaldehyde until the performance of histopathological examination.

Histology

Four percent paraformaldehyde was utilized to fix the excised K562 tumors for 24 h. Paraffin was then used to embed the fixed tumors, and the paraffin blocks were made. Five-micron tissue sections were then cut using a microtome (AccuCutHSRMTM, SAKURA). Staining with hematoxylin and eosin was then applied to the prepared tissue sections and examined for vascularization, tissue morphology, necrosis, and apoptosis.

Pyrosequencing Study

The DNA was extracted from K562-xenograft tumors excised from the negative untreated control and TQ-treated mice groups (1 mg/kg and 5 mg/kg) utilizing a DNA purification kit (from Promega, USA) based on the producer’s directions. The extracted DNA’s concentrations and purities were checked utilizing a Nanodrop-Photometer (Implen, USA). Bisulfite treatment was applied to the obtained DNA (2 µg) using Qiagen EpiTect’s Bisulfite Kit (Qiagen, Germany) based on the producer’s directions. The EpiTect’s PCR Control DNA Sets (Qiagen, Germany) were utilized as controls. After bisulfite treatment, a pyrosequencing assay was applied to quantify the levels of methylation for the SOCS-3 promoter region. Within the designated CpG island, three CpG sites were evaluated. The sequences of the primer set utilized in pyrosequencing and PCR amplification, and the investigated promoter region, were addressed in our previously released publication [5]. The PCR reactions and pyrosequencing analysis were performed according to our prior publication [5].

Protein Expressions Assessment

The cell lysates from the K562-xenograft tumors excised from the negative untreated control and TQ-treated mice groups (1 mg/kg and 5 mg/kg) were prepared with radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer provided with phosphatase inhibitors and protease inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific) based on the producer’s directions. Measurement of the protein levels was conducted utilizing the Bradford assay.

The Protein Simple Jess technique (Jess; Protein Simple, USA) was used to estimate the amount of ERK1/2 and p-ERK1/2 proteins in the K562-xenograft tumors excised from the untreated and TQ-treated mice (1 mg/kg and 5 mg/kg). In brief, the cell lysates (1 mg/mL) were prepared utilizing a sample buffer. The master mix, composed of fluorescent standard, 40 mm dithiothreitol, and sample buffer, was mixed with each cell lysate sample. To denature the cell lysates, the mixtures were heated for 5 min at 95°C into each relevant well of the plate, 3 µL of the denatured protein samples were added to 10 µL chemiluminescent substrate, 10 µL protein normalization solution, 10 µL primary antibody, and 10 µL anti-mouse secondary antibodies (horse radish peroxidase-conjugated). For each evaluation, a 12–230 kDa biotinylated ladder cartridge was included. After that, the capillaries and the plate were inserted inside the machine to start the automated proteins electrophoresis, automated proteins blocking, automated proteins incubation with the antibodies, and determination of protein intensity signals. Total protein within capillaries was normalized to account for variant loading of the protein. The normalizing substance eliminated housekeeping proteins that might lead to inconsistent and unreliable protein intensities while also enabling the detection of total proteins through bindings to the amine groups via biomolecules. Therefore, control loading is not necessary for Jess’ technique. Analysis of chemiluminescent reactions was performed using Compass software (ProteinSimple). The corrected areas of chemiluminescence peaks were used to assess the relative protein intensities. The primary antibodies, anti-ERK1/2 (Catalog No.: GZ-AF1576, R&D System, Minneapolis, USA) and anti-phospho-ERK1/2 (Catalog No.: GZ-AF1018, R&D System, Minneapolis, USA) were used in the current assay.

RNA Extractions and Conversion to cDNA

For 72 h, K562 cells received exposure to 11 µm doses of TQ. Applying the guidelines that the manufacturer has supplied, samples of RNA were attained by RNA kit from Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA). The GoTaq®-2-Steps RT-qPCR (Promega, USA) kit was then employed to convert RNA (100 nanograms) to cDNA, considering the manufacturer’s directions. The quantities and purity levels of RNA were assessed utilizing a Nanodrop photometer.

RT-qPCR for Gene Expression Assessment

The SYBR Green-based Go-Taq two-Steps RT-qPCR kit reagents (from Promega, Madison, USA) were used to evaluate the transcript levels of targeted genes according to our previously published report [1]. Triplicates of each PCR reaction were run. Calculations of the relative mRNA expression values were implemented after normalizing the values with the GAPDH gene by applying the comparative CT (2−ΔΔCq) method. Molecular Docking

Target Protein Preparation

The crystal structure of the anti-apoptotic proteins: BCL-2-like protein 1 (BCL2L1/BCL-xL) (PDB: 6VWC – UniProtID: Q07817), BCL-2 (PDB: 8HOG – UniProtID: P10415), BCL-2-related protein A1 (BCL2A1/BFL-1) (PDB: 9FKY – UniProtID: Q16548), and MCL-1 (PDB: 5IF4 – UniProtID: Q07820) were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. Protein preparation was performed using AutoDock Tools (ADT) v1.5.6 [3, 5]. All crystallographic water molecules were removed except those explicitly coordinated within the binding pocket (defined as within 4.0 Å of the co-crystallized ligand). Polar hydrogens were added to all protein structures at pH 7.4 using ADT (full name already given) (highlighted in gray). Kollman united-atom partial charges were assigned to all protein atoms using ADT.

Ligand Preparation

TQ (PubChem: 10281) and the current treatment molecules, edaravone (PubChem: 4021), paclitaxel (PubChem: 36314), obatoclax (PubChem ID: 11404337), and venetoclax (PubChem ID: 49846579) [20–23] were prepared. The three-dimensional structures were retrieved from PubChem. Ligands were processed at physiological pH (7.4) using AutoDock Tools, and Gasteiger-Marsili partial charges were calculated iteratively (106 cycles) until convergence (<0.001 charge unit change).

Grid Box Definition and Validation

Grid boxes were centered on the co-crystallized ligand binding site (BH3 binding groove) for each target protein. The grid center (x, y, z) (Å) was (2.594, −4.2745, 11.8035) for BCL-xL, (−7.6225, 4.6905, 20.452) for BCL-2, (−10.8055, 10.8025, 13.4995) for BFL-1, and (−3.374, −7.5755, 17.4525) for MCL-1. Self-docking validation was performed for each target to verify grid box parameters; ligands were re-docked into their respective proteins using identical grid parameters, and root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) between crystallographic poses and top-ranked docked poses was calculated using PyMOL [3, 11].

Molecular Docking Protocol

Docking calculations were performed using AutoDock Vina v1.2.5 [3, 5, 11] on Ubuntu 24.04 LTS with the following search parameters: exhaustiveness: 16, number of binding modes: 9 (default), CPU cores: 16, and CPU threads: 32.

Statistical Analyses

To analyze the data, SPSS (v25) was utilized. The repeated measures of ANOVA analyses with Bonferroni corrections were implemented for tumor weight, body weight, tumor volume, methylation study, and protein analyses. Wilcoxon’s signed-rank test was employed for gene expression results. The tests showed statistical significance at p values <0.05, 0.01, and 0.001.

Results

Optimal Tolerated Dose of TQ in Nude Mice

To explore the antileukemic abilities of TQ, tolerable doses of TQ were first identified in nude mice and further used for in vivo administration. One dose of TQ per day was intraperitoneally administered for 4 weeks at the doses of 1 mg/kg, 5 mg/kg, 10 mg/kg, 20 mg/kg, 30 mg/kg, 50 mg/kg, and 100 mg/kg. Mice that received 0.2 mL of DW were considered the control. The results showed that TQ caused 100% mortality in mice injected with 100 mg/kg on the first day of injection, 100% mortality in mice injected with 50 mg/kg and 30 mg/kg on the second day of injection, 70% mortality in mice exposed to 20 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg following a week of injection, and no mortalities were identified in mice that were given 5 mg/kg and 1 mg/kg and in the mice control group. Based on these findings, the mice received one daily injection of 1 mg/kg and 5 mg/kg for the treatment of mice.

TQ Promotes Cell Apoptosis in Leukemia Tumor Xenografts

TQ was administered once a day for 3 weeks in the tumor-bearing nude mice. The findings proved that the tumor size decreased in a dose-related pattern. Figure 1a–d shows the tumor growth, tumor sizes, tumor weights, and body weights in the respective tested groups. TQ dose of 1 mg/kg induced 50.3 ± 4% (p < 0.001) inhibition in tumor growth, and 5 mg/kg dosage of TQ caused an 84 ± 2% decrease in the growth of tumors (p < 0.001), comparable to the negative control. However, the reference drug imatinib induced 72.4 ± 2% (p < 0.001) inhibition in the growth of tumors, comparable to the negative control (Fig. 1b). As revealed in Figure 2, the eosin and hematoxylin-stained tumor sections from the negative control mice revealed rounded and compact shapes of the viable cells of the tumor (T), with a characterized dense distribution of the cells and blood vessels (BV) abundance, while tumor sections from mice that received imatinib illustrated a marked reduction in the viable cells of the tumor with an elevation in the necrotic tumor cells (N). Tumor sections of mice given 1 mg/kg and from mice that were given 5 mg/kg of TQ indicated a remarkable decline in the viable cells of the tumor, increased necrotic tumor cells, and a noticeable decline in the blood vessels relative to the negative control group.

TQ Modulates the Methylation and Expression Levels of SOCS-3 in K562-Xenograft Tumors

Pyrosequencing assays were carried out to examine the impacts of TQ on DNA methylation of SOCS-3 promoter in xenograft tumors excised from the negative untreated control and TQ-treated mice groups (1 mg/kg and 5 mg/kg). Three CpG sites were examined in the SOCS-3 promoter for the DNA methylation status evaluation. The pyrosequencing findings identified a marked decline in the CpG sites methylation states in the promoter of SOCS-3 (14.4%) in K562-xenograft tumors excised from mice treated with 1 mg/kg compared to 19.6% in K562-xenograft tumors excised from the negative untreated control (p < 0.05) (Table 2; Fig. 3). Furthermore, the findings reflected a substantial decline of the CpG site methylation levels at the SOCS-3 promoter (4.0%) in K562-xenograft tumors excised from mice receiving 5 mg/kg relative to 19.6% in K562-xenograft tumors excised from the negative untreated control (p < 0.001) (Table 2; Fig. 3).

TQ Inhibits ERK1/2 in K562-Xenograft Tumors

TQ’s capability to modulate the phosphorylation states and the levels of protein and gene expressions of ERK1/2 was assessed in K562-xenograft tumors excised from the untreated and TQ-treated mice (1 mg/kg and 5 mg/kg), applying the Jess Simple Western technique. The administration of TQ induced a marked inhibition of ERK1/2 by reducing the phosphorylation levels of p-ERK1/2 after 1 mg/kg treatment (p < 0.01) and 5 mg/kg treatment (p < 0.001) against the untreated mice (Fig. 4a, b). Moreover, TQ reduced the levels of ERK1/2 protein after 1 mg/kg treatment (p < 0.05) and 5 mg/kg treatment (p < 0.01) related to the untreated group (Fig. 4a, b). The findings also illustrated that TQ markedly lowered ERK1 mRNA expression to 2.6-fold (with a p < 0.01) and 7.7-fold (with a p < 0.001) after 1 mg/kg treatment and 5 mg/kg treatment, respectively, relative to the negative untreated control (Fig. 4c). In addition, TQ markedly lowered ERK2 mRNA expression to 3.7-fold (with a p < 0.01) and 12.5-fold (with a p < 0.001) after 1 mg/kg treatment and 5 mg/kg treatment, respectively, compared to the untreated group (Fig. 4c).

TQ Modifies the Apoptosis-Regulator Gene Expressions

The expression values of caspase-3, caspase-9, Bim, Bax, Bid, Bad, Bak, PUMA, Bmf, BCL-2, BFL-1, BCL-xL, MCL-1, BCL-w, XIAP, and survivin were investigated using RT-qPCR in K562 cells. Upon 72 h treatments of 21 μm TQ, the analysis stated a notable rise in caspase-3, caspase-9, Bim, Bid, Bax, Bad, Bak, PUMA, and Bmf mRNA values by 12.3-fold (with a p < 0.001), 9.3-fold (with a p < 0.001), 6.2-fold (with a p < 0.001), 9.7-fold (with a p < 0.001), 14.1-fold (with a p < 0.001), 7.6-fold (with a p < 0.001), 13.1-fold (with a p < 0.001), 6.3-fold (with a p < 0.001), and 6.5-fold (with a p < 0.001), accordingly in comparison with 0 μm treatments (Fig. 5a). The analysis also revealed a notable reduction in BCL-2, BCL-xL, BCL-w, MCL-1, BFL-1, XIAP, and survivin mRNA values by 20-fold (with a p < 0.001), 23.8-fold (with a p < 0.001), 10.4-fold (with a p < 0.001), 5.3-fold (with a p < 0.001), 14.3-fold (with a p < 0.001), 38.6-fold (with a p < 0.001), and 18.2-fold (with a p < 0.001), accordingly upon 72 h treatments of 21 μm TQ as compared with treatments of 0 μm TQ (Fig. 5b).

Binding affinities (kcal/mol) of TQ and four marketed drugs with the target proteins

For BCL-xL, venetoclax exhibited the highest binding affinity (−10.64 kcal/mol) through a critical interaction with Arg139, which is necessary for sustaining protein inhibition (Fig. 8; Table 4). Additionally, paclitaxel and obatoclax showed lower affinities of −6.81 and −6.37 kcal/mol, respectively, by interacting with the side residue of the essential functional Arg139 residue at Gly138. Moreover, paclitaxel and obatoclax showed another interaction along the far side of the pocket with Tyr195, which stabilizes further interaction and compensates for the lack of bulk groups present in venetoclax that establish lipophilic interactive drug fixation. However, this compensation is not enough to result in a higher inhibition of BCL-xL compared to venetoclax. As for TQ, it had a decent interaction with Gly138, which blocks the way for any contact of signaling molecules with Arg139. Yet TQ binds with BCL-xL with a calculated affinity of −6.19 kcal/mol, which accounts for being relatively as efficient as obatoclax and edaravone. Indeed, its lack of bulk group still disqualifies it from competing with the effective inhibition of venetoclax (Fig. 8; Table 4).

Almost similar results were obtained with BCL-2 since it has an active site with similar preferences as BCL-xL. With a dual binding to Tyr202 and Asp103 and vigorous lipophilic interactions, venetoclax showed a stabilized inhibition of BCL-2, exhibiting −8.94 kcal/mol binding affinity. On the other hand, edaravone and obatoclax were found to form no additional bonds than the essential interaction with Asp103 with affinities of −5.12 kcal/mol and −6.76 kcal/mol, respectively. Mostly, they showed lower affinities than venetoclax due to the lack of a heavy lipophilic chain to accommodate the lipophilic region of the protein. The lipophilic chain present in paclitaxel plays an important role in its binding. However, despite forming a double hydrogen bond with Asp103 and being further stabilized by hydrogen bonds with Gly145 and Tyr202, paclitaxel showed a binding affinity (−6.20 kcal/mol) similar to that of edaravone and obatoclax. This relatively modest affinity is primarily due to paclitaxel’s extensive hydrophilic branching, which, unlike venetoclax, hinders its ability to form strong lipophilic interactions with the lipophilic region of the binding pocket. As for TQ, it showed similar binding characteristics to edaravone with a single hydrogen bond to Asp103, which, however, is sufficient to maintain an inhibitory action on BCL2 (Fig. 8; Table 4).

BFL-1 interactions with the investigated molecules were found to be highly similar in affinity. However, an exception to this similarity was paclitaxel, whose affinity, depending on the thiol side chain of Cys55, was found to be very weak at −2.04 kcal/mol. Edaravone, obatoclax, venetoclax, and TQ demonstrated relatively close binding affinities of about −5.5 to −5.95 kcal/mol, all through a common interaction determinant at Cys55 for inhibition. Interestingly, TQ demonstrated a very competitive affinity compared to venetoclax; despite the extra hydrogen bond that venetoclax forms with Cys55, which is not established with TQ (Fig. 8; Table 4).

At MCL-1, venetoclax still illustrates the highest inhibition represented by −8.2 kcal/mol binding energy, with a strong interaction with Arg263, an important residue in the BH3 binding site, mediated by three hydrogen bonds. Also, venetoclax, due to its lipophilic backbone, could pursue a uniform pocket occupation with enhanced potency maintained by an additional hydrogen bond with Val253. In contrast, paclitaxel although could not achieve as good pocket occupation due to the hydrophilic branches of its backbone, yet it maintained dual interactions with Arg263 and Val253. On the other hand, obatoclax did not show any potential inhibition on MCL-1. TQ and edaravone were observed to occupy the hydrophilic bond with Arg263. Despite their relatively low binding affinities against MCL-1 (−4.88 kcal/mol for both TQ and edaravone), TQ effectively blocks the active site, thereby inhibiting the protein. Also, thanks to the mediator water molecules around Val253, it is suggested that in vivo, TQ is expected to show higher than −4.88 kcal/mol affinity (Fig. 8; Table 4).

Discussion

It has been reported TQ has antitumor properties in several cancers [1, 3, 11]. However, a thorough investigation of the potential molecular changes that result in the antileukemia properties of TQ is still needed. In this study, the impact of antileukemic properties of TQ has been examined. This has been done by investigating the impact of TQ on the expression and methylation status of SOCS-3, phosphorylation of ERK1/2 protein, expressions of the pro-apoptotic and the anti-apoptotic genes, along with their influences on apoptosis and cell division in K562-tumor xenografts. Moreover, molecular docking analysis of TQ to the active sites of anti-apoptotic proteins has also been assessed.

The therapeutic properties of natural compounds against cancer have been studied extensively in some previous reports [1, 3, 11]. TQ was shown to prevent the growth of tumor cells of acute myeloid leukemia in vivo [24]. The results of this study have also shown the in vivo apoptotic effect of TQs on CML tumor in a mouse model of CML. In addition, our data have shown that TQ produced a marked dose-related reduction of K562 tumor cells growth. TQ induced 50.3% and 84% inhibition of the growth of the K562 tumor at dosages of 1 mg/kg and 5 mg/kg, respectively (Fig. 1a–c). The histological findings of the K562 tumor implied that TQ considerably lowered the number of blood vessels, resulting in a substantial reduction of the viable cells (Fig. 2).

SOCS-3 is a TSG that suppresses tumor cells’ proliferation and metastasis [5]. It has been reported that SOCS-3 is epigenetically silenced in malignant hematological conditions such as leukemia [5, 6]. Interestingly, in our previous studies, TQ lowered the methylation state of SHP-1 and SOCS-3 in MV4-11 AML cells [5], reduced the methylation state of SHP-1 in K562 cells [1], and promoted the expressions of SOCS-1, SHP-1, and SOCS-3 that contributed to induction of apoptosis in MV4-11 AML cells [5], illustrating its effectiveness as a candidate for targeting the epigenetic changes against leukemia. In this study, we have examined SOCS-3’s methylation status in K562-xenograft tumors to reveal further TQ’s hypomethylating activities. The results reported in this study have shown that TQ triggered hypomethylation of SOCS-3 gene (14.4%) and consequently enhanced its expression in xenograft tumors excised from mice receiving 1 mg/kg relative to 19.6% in xenograft tumors excised from the negative untreated control (Table 2; Fig. 3). These findings also indicated that TQ induced a more significant hypomethylating influence on SOCS-3 (4.0%) in xenograft tumors excised from mice exposed to 5 mg/kg as compared to 19.6% in xenograft tumors excised from the negative untreated control (Table 2; Fig. 3). Our results are in line with a previous report, which had shown that TQ reduced DNA methylation, and, therefore, contributed to cell growth inhibition of MV4-11 cells [24]. Furthermore, it has also been reported that TQ elevated the TWIST1 oncogene methylation and reduced its expression, which caused an inhibition of breast cancer cells’ growth [25].

Constitutive ERK1/2 activation has a significant function in CML leukemogenesis [26]. Therefore, inhibition of activated ERK1/2 proteins is thought to provide a promising treatment option for CML. In this study, we evaluated the influence of TQ on ERK1/2 phosphorylation and expression in K562-xenograft tumors. The results reported in our study have shown that TQ made a marked reduction in ERK1/2 phosphorylation and protein level and also caused a significant downregulation of ERK1 and ERK2 genes in K562-xenograft tumors excised from mice after 1 mg/kg and 5 mg/kg treatments (Fig. 4). These findings indicated that ERK signaling inhibition caused by TQ was correlated with the enhancement of apoptosis in CML tumor xenografts. Considering the role of ERK1/2 as an essential upstream regulator of various anti-apoptotic genes, the inhibition of ERK1/2 at the phosphorylation and protein levels represents a molecular basis for the expression modulation of downstream apoptosis-related genes that was observed in our study. These results are supported by the findings of Zhao et al. [26], which reported that ERK and p-ERK were reduced in K562 cells as a result of treatment with the phytochemical compound, asperuloside.

The disruption of the apoptosis process is a key mechanism that promotes drug resistance by cancer cells [4, 27]. Several researchers reported that low mRNA expression of proapoptotic genes favors inhibition of apoptosis in leukemic cells [27, 28]. Thus, the apoptotic genes are a key molecular target to enhance the apoptotic events and restrain cancer cell proliferation. The results reported in our study further elucidated TQ’s apoptotic impacts on CML cells. We have studied the impact of TQ on caspase-3, Bax, caspase-9, Bim, Bid, Bad, Bak, PUMA, and Bmf expressions in K562 cells. Our analyses verified that TQ elevated caspase-3, Bax, caspase-9, Bim, Bid, Bad, Bak, PUMA, and Bmf gene expressions, as demonstrated in Figure 5a. According to Ashour et al. [27], caspase‐8, caspase‐9, caspase‐3, and caspase‐7 mRNA values were elevated in Daoy medulloblastoma cells after TQ incubation. Moreover, Adinew et al. [28] have documented an upregulation of the mRNA values of Bax, Bad, Apaf1, and Bik in the MDA-MB-468 cells of breast cancer and an upregulation of caspases-3 and caspase-9 in the MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells.

To further investigate TQ’s apoptotic activities in CML, we examined its effect on the expression of BCL-2, BCL-w, BCL-xL, BFL-1, MCL-1, XIAP, and survivin in K562 cells. As illustrated in Figure 5b, our results demonstrated the downregulation of these anti-apoptotic genes after TQ treatment. Similar results have been reported in a previous study, which demonstrated that TQ downregulated the BCL-2 anti-apoptotic gene in Daoy medulloblastoma cells [27]. Furthermore, Adinew et al. [28] reported the downregulation of mRNA levels of the MCL-1 gene in MDA-MB-231 cells of breast cancer. Additionally, it was also reported that XIAP mRNA levels were reduced in Daoy medulloblastoma cells after TQ incubation [27]. Our findings have demonstrated that TQ-induced modulation of apoptosis-regulator genes in K562 cells was accompanied by improved apoptosis in K562-tumor tissues. Interestingly, these findings further support our previously published data identifying apoptosis induction by TQ in the MV4-11 cells of AML and K562 cells via flow cytometry analysis [1, 5].

Various inhibitors of BCL-2, BFL-1, BCL-xL, and MCL-1 have been developed [20–23]. Direct inhibitions of proteins that belong to the BCL-2 family proved clinical therapeutic benefits in cancer treatment [21]. However, the approach to target a single protein in the BCL-2 group of proteins is changing because of the compensating effects of other members of the BCL-2 family of proteins. Thus, developing a potent multi-target inhibitor of BCL-2 proteins needs extensive investigations. In our study, molecular docking of TQ to BCL-2, BFL-1, BCL-xL, and MCL-1 was assessed. The nature of the target pockets prefers a moderately bulky ligand for prolonged inhibition. However, despite these preferences of the active sites of the target proteins, TQ was found to establish a reliable interaction with the BCL-xL, BCL2, BFL-1, and MCL-1 (Fig. 8; Table 4). The inhibitory properties of TQ, especially its consistent interaction with most of the anti-apoptotic proteins, enhance its potential as a scaffold for designing novel inhibitors. Most importantly, TQ showed very good flexibility by interacting with residues crucial for binding in the BH3 domain, thus enhancing its potential as a potent anticancer agent. These interactions are suggested to extend to further proteins in the BCL-2 family as most of them share the same active domain BH3. Consistent with our findings, Saffari_Chaleshtori et al. [29] revealed that TQ exhibited apoptotic properties in PC3 cells of prostate cancer, and their molecular docking analysis revealed that TQ demonstrated stable interactions with BCL-2, MCL-1, and BCL-xL. In addition, Natarajan Sathishkumar et al. [30] revealed that ginsenosides, a natural product, showed potent inhibitory effects on MCL-1, BCL-xL, and BCL-2 with proper affinities. However, it is essential to emphasize the need for additional validation experiments for molecular docking results since they provide predictive inhibition of the studied proteins.

Taken together, TQ enhanced apoptosis of K562-tumor cells through its marked hypomethylating influence on SOCS-3, inhibition of activated ERK1/2 and p-ERK1/2 proteins, downregulation of ERK1 and ERK2 genes, modification of the apoptosis-regulator genes’ expressions, and showed consistent interaction with most of the anti-apoptotic proteins, enhancing its potential as a scaffold for designing novel inhibitors to combat CML cells. Although these results demonstrate significant mechanistic insights, the translational implications of these findings require further clinical studies to be established.

A limitation of our study is the lack of information on pharmacokinetics, which limits evaluation of bioavailability, systemic exposure, and dose-response relationships. To better identify the therapeutic value and translational application of TQ, further investigations incorporating pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic assessments are needed.

Conclusion

The antileukemic influences of TQ were examined on mice models of CML and K562 CML cells. TQ improved cell apoptosis and restricted the cell growth of the K562-tumor xenograft. TQ-enhanced apoptosis could be a consequence of its hypomethylating influence on SOCS-3, inhibition of activated ERK1/2 and p-ERK1/2 proteins, downregulation of ERK1 and ERK2 genes, modulation of the apoptosis-regulator genes’ expressions by upregulating caspase-3, caspase-9, Bim, Bid, Bax, Bad, Bak, Bmf, and PUMA pro-apoptotic genes expression, and downregulating BCL-2, BCL-xL, BCL-w, MCL-1, BFL-1, XIAP, and survivin anti-apoptotic genes expression. These anti-leukemia activities can also be a consequence of TQ-mediated inhibition of the protein activities of BFL-1, BCL-xL, MCL-1, and BCL-2. These insights suggest TQ as an efficient multi-target inhibitor of various anti-apoptotic members belonging to the BCL-2 family and provide novel perspectives in the stimulation of the apoptotic mechanism in CML cells. Ultimately, these results present a preclinical demonstration of the biological activity of TQ; however, further investigations are needed to determine its therapeutic effectiveness and clinical relevance.
