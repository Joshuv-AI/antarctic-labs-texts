Early postoperative pain and quality of life with different modalities of mesh fixation in laparoscopic trans-abdominal pre-peritoneal repair of inguinal hernia: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Ahmed Eid Aziz, Ahmed H Khalil, Ahmed Abdelnasser, Ahmed Maher Abd Elmonim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCompared with open inguinal hernia repair, laparoscopic approaches, including Transabdominal Preperitoneal (TAPP) and Totally Extraperitoneal (TEP), improve recovery and reduce pain. However, optimal mesh fixation remains unclear due to limited high-quality comparative evidence. We aimed to evaluate early postoperative pain and quality of life with different mesh fixation modalities (glue, tacks, and sutures) in laparoscopic TAPP inguinal hernia repair.MethodsA single-center, prospective, randomized controlled trial was conducted on 75 eligible adult patients with primary inguinal hernia scheduled for elective TAPP repair. Patients were randomized in a 1:1:1 ratio to glue (n = 25), suture (n = 25), or tack (n = 25) fixation. Primary outcomes were postoperative pain intensity (visual analogue scale, VAS) and hernia-specific quality of life (Carolinas Comfort Scale, CCS) assessed at postoperative day 1 1, 3, and 6 months. Secondary outcomes included operative time, length of hospital stay, time to return to work, complications, recurrence, and procedural cost.ResultsCompared with the glue and suture fixation groups, the tack fixation group had substantially higher pain levels on the first day after the operation and at one month (P < 0.001). Over the 3rd and 6th months, VAS scores were low and comparable across all groups, with no significant differences. The glue group had significantly lower mesh sensation and total CCS scores at all time points compared to the suture and tack groups (P < 0.001).ConclusionsIn the early postoperative period following laparoscopic TAPP inguinal hernia repair, mesh fixation with surgical glue results in superior pain control, better quality of life, and faster return to work compared to sutures and tacks, without increasing early complication rates.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT07401082 (Registered February 6, 2026; retrospectively registered after completion of enrollment).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03801-7.Keywords: Glue, Inguinal hernia, Mesh fixation, Post-operative pain, Quality of life, Sutures, Tackers, TAPP

Background

Surgeons are increasingly using the less-invasive laparoscopic transabdominal preperitoneal (TAPP) repair for inguinal hernia repair. The reason is reduced post-surgical discomfort. Plus, compared with open Lichtenstein mesh repair, there are fewer complications after the procedure and less time spent in the hospital. The result is a shift toward laparoscopic TAPP repair, which allows patients to resume their regular exercise regimens sooner [1].

Open hernia repairs have made use of mesh fixations such as glue or self-gripping mesh, whereas laparoscopic hernia repairs have made use of metallic tack, absorbable tack, glue, suture, self-gripping mesh, or even non-fixation procedures [2].

Surgeon and patient satisfaction have been enhanced by promising postoperative outcomes from alternative mesh fixation procedures using tissue adhesives. Research on alternatives to traditional suture or tack fixation, such as mesh fixation with fibrin glue or cyanoacrylate tissue adhesive, has shown outcomes comparable to or even superior to those of traditional fixation. There is still some skepticism about these hernia mesh-fixing techniques [3].

Although previous studies have compared individual fixation methods (e.g., glue vs. tacks, sutures vs. tacks), direct, three-armed randomized comparisons within the standardized TAPP procedure are limited. High-level evidence is needed to guide surgeons in selecting a fixation technique that optimizes the balance between durability, patient comfort, recovery, and cost.

Therefore, this prospective randomized controlled trial was designed to comprehensively evaluate and compare the three mesh fixation modalities (glue, sutures, and tacks), assessing postoperative pain and quality of life as primary outcomes and operative details, recovery, complications, and cost as secondary outcomes.

Methods

This single-center, prospective, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial was conducted at the General Surgery Department, Cairo University Hospitals, from September 2024 to October 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Cairo University (Approval No: MS-358-2024) and retrospectively registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07401082) on February 6, 2026, following completion of patient enrollment but before data analysis. All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

This randomized controlled trial was conducted in accordance with the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) 2010 Statement. The CONSORT checklist is provided as Additional File 1.

Participants

Eligible patients were adults aged 21–60 years with a primary, unilateral, or bilateral inguinal hernia, scheduled for elective laparoscopic TAPP repair. Hernia type was classified intraoperatively according to the European Hernia Society (EHS) classification (direct, indirect, femoral, or combined). Key exclusion criteria included recurrent or complicated hernias (e.g., incarcerated, strangulated, or with obstruction), contraindications to laparoscopy or general anesthesia, American Society of Anesthesiologists class ≥ III, chronic pain syndromes, regular use of strong analgesics, known allergies to mesh or fixation materials (assessed by patient history of prior reactions to surgical implants or adhesives), and inability to complete follow-up.

Randomization and blinding

Seventy-five eligible participants were randomly allocated into one of three intervention arms (glue, sutures, or tacks) in a 1:1:1 ratio. The randomization sequence was computer-generated. Allocation was concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Due to the nature of the interventions, blinding of the operating surgeon was not feasible. However, outcome assessors and the statistician performing data analysis were blinded to group assignment.

Perioperative care

All patients underwent complete history taking, clinical examination, radiological assessment (ultrasound showing inguinal hernia, content, and defect, with exclusion of intra-abdominal pathology), and laboratory tests (complete blood cells, coagulation profile, kidney function, and liver function).

Surgical procedure

All patients underwent a standardized laparoscopic TAPP procedure under general anesthesia. Laparoscopic access was achieved using a three-port technique. A 10-mm supraumbilical camera port was placed using an open (Hasson) technique. Two 5-mm working ports were placed in the midclavicular line at the level of the umbilicus bilaterally. Port sites were infiltrated with 10 mL of 0.5% bupivacaine before incision for local anesthesia.

After creating the preperitoneal space, a lightweight macroporous polypropylene mesh (Parietene™, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) was placed over the myopectineal orifice. For unilateral hernias, a 10 × 15 cm mesh was used; for bilateral hernias, two meshes of 10 × 15 cm each (one per side) were employed. The 10 × 15 cm mesh has a weight of approximately 46 g/m² and a pore size of 2 × 2.4 mm. The same mesh type was used consistently across all study groups.

The Parietene™ mesh has a macroporous structure (large pore size), which is optimal for tissue adhesive fixation, as cyanoacrylate-based glues require adequate mesh porosity to allow adhesive penetration and interlocking. Conversely, for the tack fixation group, the large pore mesh allows for adequate tissue incorporation while minimizing the risk of nerve entrapment, as helical titanium tacks can be placed selectively through the mesh pores without compressing the mesh against underlying structures.

Intraoperatively, the hernia defect size was measured using a calibrated laparoscopic grasper. The maximum transverse diameter was recorded in centimeters, and defects were classified as small (< 2 cm), medium (2–4 cm), or large (> 4 cm).

Fixation was performed according to the assigned group: Glue Group [Fixation using a cyanoacrylate-based surgical adhesive (Glubran-2®, GEM, Viareggio, Italy) applied in small drops at the mesh corners via a dedicated applicator (GlueTack). The glue was allowed to set for 30 s before mesh manipulation. Suture Group [Fixation using two interrupted non-absorbable polypropylene sutures (Prolene® 2 − 0, Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA). One suture was placed at the medial corner of the mesh, securing it to Cooper’s ligament and the pubic tubercle region. The second suture was placed at the lateral corner, securing the mesh to the iliopubic tract. Sutures were placed using an intracorporeal knot-tying technique with a laparoscopic needle holder, and Tack Group [Fixation using helical titanium tacks (ProTack® 5-mm, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA). A standardized fixation pattern was employed: 5–7 tacks were placed around the mesh periphery at approximately 2–3 cm intervals, with specific avoidance of the “triangle of doom” and the “triangle of pain”.

All other procedural steps were identical across groups. Peritoneal closure was performed using a running absorbable suture (Vicryl 2 − 0, Ethicon) continuously from the medial to lateral aspect, ensuring complete coverage of the mesh. Operative time in minutes from skin incision to closure was recorded.

All port sites (both 10-mm and 5-mm) were closed at the fascial level using Vicryl 2 − 0 suture. Skin incisions were closed with subcuticular absorbable sutures (Monocryl 4 − 0) and covered with sterile adhesive strips.

Postoperative care

The three groups received the same postoperative care. Oral fluids were allowed for two hours postoperatively, and a normal diet was resumed once tolerated. Routine analgesia in the form of parenteral paracetamol every 8 h on the first day, then oral tablets on demand, was prescribed.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were postoperative pain intensity (visual analogue scale, VAS), quality of life (Carolinas Comfort Scale total score), and mesh sensation (CCS sensation subscore). Secondary outcomes were operative time (minutes), length of hospital stay (days), time to return to work (days), direct material cost of the fixation device, and incidence of complications and recurrence.

Assessment of patients’ postoperative pain using a 10-point visual analogue scale (VAS) (0 = no pain, 10 = worst imaginable pain) was performed on postoperative day 1 and at subsequent follow-up visits at 1, 3, and 6 months. Assessment of quality of life and mesh-related symptoms was done at 1, 3, and 6 months after surgery using the Carolinas Comfort Scale (CCS), a validated hernia-specific outcome measure [4]. The CCS consists of 23 items evaluating pain, mesh sensation, and movement limitation during daily activities, each scored from 0 (no symptoms) to 5 (disabling symptoms), yielding a total score range of 0–115. Higher scores indicate worse postoperative comfort and quality of life.

Hernia recurrence, seroma or hematoma formation, wound/superficial infection, mesh/deep infection, scrotal edema, or any other complications were recorded during follow-up visits if present.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) based on the primary outcome of early postoperative pain (VAS at 24 h). Data from a previous three-arm randomized trial by Al-Bendary et al. [5], which compared glue, tack, and suture fixation in laparoscopic TAPP inguinal hernia repair, were used. In that study, mean VAS scores (± SD) were 3.00 ± 0.667 in the glue group, 3.60 ± 0.843 in the tack group, and 4.40 ± 1.075 in the suture group, yielding an effect size (Cohen’s f) of 0.653. For a three-group fixed-effects one-way ANOVA with α = 0.05 and power = 0.90, the required total sample size was 33 patients (11 per group). Accounting for a 15% dropout rate, the target sample size was rounded to 40 patients. To further ensure adequate power for secondary outcomes and post-hoc comparisons, we enrolled 75 patients (25 per group).

Statistical analysis

Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for data coding and entry. Number of instances and relative frequencies (percentages) were used to summarize data for categorical variables, while median and interquartile range were used for non-normally distributed quantitative variables. Normally distributed quantitative variables were described using the mean and standard deviation. ANOVA with post hoc multiple-comparison tests was used for normally distributed quantitative variables in group comparisons; for non-normally distributed quantitative variables, the nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test and Mann-Whitney test were employed [6]. The Chi-square χ2) test was used to compare categorical data. When the anticipated frequency was less than 5, exact tests were utilized instead. Statistical significance was determined by p-values that were less than 0.05 [7].

Results

Eighty-four patients were assessed for eligibility; nine patients did not meet the criteria. Following exclusion, 75 patients remained and were randomized equally (25 per group) by the computer-generated sequence. All assigned patients were monitored, and their data were statistically analyzed (Fig. 1).

Groups with mesh fixation experienced significantly different levels of pain in the early postoperative period. Compared with the Glue and Sutures groups, the Tacks group reported greater pain on Day 1 (median 3, interquartile range (IQR) 3–4) than the other groups (median 2, IQR 2–2; P < 0.001). At 1 month, pain remained slightly higher in the Tacks group (median 1, IQR 1–1) versus the other groups (median 0, IQR 0–1; P < 0.001). However, there were no significant differences in 3 months (P = 0.809) or 6 months (P = 1.000), with all groups reporting minimal pain (median 0). This indicates that early postoperative pain is influenced by the type of mesh fixation, but long-term pain outcomes are comparable across groups (Table 2).

Discussion

Laparoscopic TAPP repair is a preferred minimally invasive approach, associated with less pain and faster recovery than the open technique [8]. Mesh fixation is a critical determinant of postoperative outcomes in TAPP. Mechanical fixation (tacks/sutures), while effective, may increase pain due to tissue penetration. Atraumatic adhesive fixation (e.g., surgical glue) offers a promising alternative to enhance early recovery without compromising durability [9]. Direct comparative evidence to optimally balance efficacy, patient comfort, and cost among these techniques remains limited.

In this study, postoperative pain was evaluated using VAS on Day 1 and at 1, 3, and 6 months post-surgery. On day 1, both the glue and suture groups reported a median VAS score of 2.00, while the tacks group reported a higher median score of 3.00, indicating more intense immediate postoperative pain with tack fixation. At one month, pain persisted predominantly in the tack group (median 1.00), whereas the glue and suture groups both reported minimal pain (median 0.00). By 3 and 6 months, VAS scores in all groups had decreased to 0.00, suggesting resolution of pain over time.

A randomized, double-blinded, controlled clinical trial by Tolver et al. [10] evaluated 112 patients with inguinal hernias who underwent TAPP with fibrin glue and tacks to fix the mesh. There were one hundred patients who could be analyzed. On the first day, there was a statistically significant difference in the amount of discomfort experienced when coughing between the fibrin (n = 50) and tacks (n = 50) groups [median 23 (range 0–80) vs. 35 (2–100) mm] (p = 0.020). Moreover, day 1 scores and all cumulated scores of pain during rest, discomfort, and fatigue were significantly lower in the fibrin group compared with the tacks group (all p-values ≤ 0.02).

A prospective randomized controlled trial by Aziz et al. [11] discovered that after 6 and 24 h, the average pain level in the group with tack fixation was 4.88 ± 0.887 and 5.29 ± 0.777, respectively. The average pain level in the suture fixation group was 3.43 ± 0.962 at 6 h and 4.11 ± 0.703 at 24 h. At both 6-hour and 24-hour periods, the suture fixation group experienced considerably reduced mean pain compared to the tack fixation group (P < 0.001). Summing up, compared to tack fixation, suture fixation of mesh results in reduced postoperative discomfort on the first day.

In this study, mesh sensation, a key component of postoperative quality of life, was evaluated using the CCS’s mesh-specific domains. At one month, the glue group exhibited the lowest mean sensation score (5.20 ± 1.29), followed by the suture group (6.60 ± 0.82), and the tack group (7.08 ± 0.81), suggesting greater discomfort associated with tacks. This trend persisted at three months (glue: 3.68 ± 0.95; sutures: 5.24 ± 0.66; tacks: 5.32 ± 1.03) and at six months (glue: 2.32 ± 0.63; sutures: 3.36 ± 0.81; tacks: 3.68 ± 0.80), indicating that glue fixation led to significantly less mesh-related discomfort over time. The difference in mesh sensation likely reflects the absence of penetrating foreign material (tacks or sutures) in the glue group, reducing local tissue irritation and foreign body reaction [3, 9].

This is consistent with the findings of Tolver et al. [10], who used fibrin glue versus tacks for mesh fixation during TAPP. After 1 month, the fibrin group had a much lower incidence of foreign-body experience (p = 0.006). Conversely, a prospective randomized controlled study by Shetiwy et al. [12] observed a statistically significant difference between the groups in VAS score on the first day (P = 0.018), with the tissue adhesive group reporting significantly less discomfort, but no significant difference in mesh sensation (p = 0.233).

In this study, quality of life was assessed using CCS at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively. Patients in the glue group consistently reported better outcomes, with significantly lower mean CCS total scores across all time points. At one month, the glue group had a mean CCS score of 11.28, compared to 14.48 in the suture group and 15.56 in the tack group. This pattern remained at 3 months (glue: 7.08; sutures: 10.80; tacks: 10.88) and 6 months (glue: 4.44; sutures: 6.92; tacks: 7.52).

The findings of this study align with and extend the evidence from large national hernia registries. The Danish Hernia Database analysed 49,029 TAPP repairs over 12 years (mean follow-up 5.76 years) and reported a reoperation rate of 3.6% for recurrence. Tack fixation, the most common method, had the highest 5-year reoperation rate (5.3%). Compared to tacks, glue fixation (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 0.28, 95% CI 0.23–0.38) and self-fixating meshes (aHR 0.24, 95% CI 0.18–0.32) were associated with significantly lower reoperation risks. No fixation also showed a lower risk (aHR 0.55, 95% CI 0.39–0.71), whereas clips and sutures did not differ significantly from tacks [13]. The Swedish Hernia Registry prospectively evaluated 29,109 laparoscopic repairs, with 20,455 patients (70%) responding to a chronic postoperative inguinal pain (CPIP) questionnaire at one year. CPIP was reported by 28% of respondents. Non-fixated standard polypropylene mesh was associated with the lowest CPIP risk and was also the least expensive option, leading the authors to recommend this combination [14].

In a cohort study by Fortelny et al. [15] performed surgeries on a series of non-selected individuals suffering from inguinal hernias, using fibrin sealant to secure the mesh. Based on statistically significant changes across the board in physical health-related measures (p = 0.0010), the original SF-36 study showed that the physical health component summary measure improved by a very substantial amount. There was no significant difference between the pre- and post-operative values of the mental health-related scale ‘social functioning’ (SOCIAL), which ranged from 77.3 ± 23.7 to 95.5 ± 8.4. After one year, there was a substantial decrease in the VAS (P < 0.05).

Conversely, a multicenter randomized clinical trial by Petro et al. [16] evaluated the use of absorbable tacks (n = 133) with mesh fixation using N-Butyl-2-Cyanoacrylate glue (n = 131) in laparoscopic hernia repair, which involved 264 patients. At various points throughout the study, participants’ quality of life was evaluated using the Carolinas Comfort Scale (CCS). There were no significant differences between the groups at one week and one month postoperatively (median: 12 for both groups; p = 0.92; month 1 median: 2 for glue vs. 1 for tacks). At three months, both groups had a median CCS score of 0 (p = 0.08). By six months, a statistically significant difference emerged (median 0 for glue [IQR: 0] vs. 0 for tacks [IQR: 1]; p = 0.01), though this difference was minimal and not deemed clinically relevant, nor sustained at any further time points.

Compared with sutures and tacks, adhesive fixation in laparoscopic TAPP inguinal hernia repair improves postoperative quality of life and reduces early postoperative discomfort. This supports the increasing shift toward atraumatic fixation techniques in minimally invasive hernia surgery to enhance patient-centered outcomes [16].

In this study, the mean operative time was 100.04 ± 25.81 min in the glue group, 107.16 ± 23.21 min in the suture group, and 93.64 ± 15.00 min in the tack group. Although the differences were not statistically significant, the tacks group demonstrated the shortest operative time, aligning with the general perception that mechanical fixation methods are quicker to perform.

A prospective comparative cohort study by Emad et al. [17] investigated the relative merits of fibrin glue and mechanical mesh fixation for laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair. Fifty patients were randomly assigned to two groups in the study: one group received fibrin glue, and the other received mechanical fixation. The average operating time for group A is 90.8 min, with a standard deviation of 22.7, whereas the average operating time for group B is 88.6 min, with a standard deviation of 22. Although the tacker group required a little more time for surgery on average than the fibrin group, this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.7294).

A retrospective study by Ferrarese et al. [18] evaluated the effectiveness of stapler fixation in laparoscopic treatment of transabdominal inguinal hernias. The subjects in the trial were divided into two groups: one that received fibrin glue (the “Glue group”) and another that used traditional tacks. Though not statistically significant, the glue group required more time for the operation (mean = 80 min) than the tacks group (mean 75 min). In summary, while our study observed shorter operative times with tack fixation, the differences were not statistically significant. The literature presents mixed findings: some studies report significant differences favoring mechanical fixation methods, while others find none. This indicates that operative time may be influenced by multiple factors beyond the fixation technique alone.​.

In this study, the mean hospital stay was 1.04 ± 0.20 days for both the glue and suture fixation groups and 1.08 ± 0.28 days for the tacks group, with no statistically significant difference among the groups (P = 0.775). These findings suggest that the choice of mesh fixation method, whether glue, sutures, or tacks, does not significantly influence the length of postoperative hospitalization.

A retrospective study by Ferrarese et al. [18] contrasted stapler fixation with fibrin glue for laparoscopic TAPP. There was no statistically significant difference in the average number of days spent in the hospital following surgery: 1.3 for the glue group and 1.5 for the tacks group.

In this study, the mean return-to-work duration was significantly shorter in the glue group (5.56 ± 1.04 days) than in the suture (8.08 ± 1.04 days) and tacks (9.60 ± 0.96 days) groups, with a statistically significant difference (P < 0.001). However, a retrospective study by Ferrarese et al. [18] determined that the average number of days it took to get back to work was 3.8 for the glue group and 4.1 for the tacks group. Time to return to work was not significantly different between the two groups. Consistent with previous research, our findings support the idea that glue fixation allows for a more rapid recovery and return to work after surgery, most likely due to less postoperative pain. However, some studies highlight that return-to-work times may be influenced by factors beyond the choice of mesh fixation, such as patient demographics and work-related requirements.

In this study, the direct financial cost of mesh fixation differed significantly among the groups (P < 0.001). Sutures were associated with the lowest cost range ($30–60), glue with a moderate cost range ($200–300), and tacks with the highest cost range ($350–500), with all patients in each group falling within their respective cost category. These findings demonstrate a marked cost disparity, with tacks representing the greatest financial burden, followed by glue, while sutures remained the most economical option.

Recurrence rates at 6 months were 4.0% in the glue group, 4.0% in the suture group, and 8.0% in the tack group, with no significant difference between groups (P = 1.000). These rates are comparable to early recurrence rates reported in other randomized trials comparing fixation techniques [10, 11]. Factors such as inclusion of bilateral hernias and larger defects may contribute, but importantly, the fixation technique did not influence early recurrence. Longer follow-up is needed to assess late recurrences.

There was no statistically significant difference in total complication rates between the groups with mesh fixation in this investigation. This is consistent with the randomized clinical trial by Shetiwy et al. [12] that involved one hundred patients with inguinal hernias. Two groups of patients were randomly assigned to participate. There was no significant difference between the two groups, while 10% of the glue group and 4% of the tacks group had seroma (p = 0.436). Additionally, group II had one hematoma case, whereas group I had none (p = 1.0). In conclusion, our study indicates that the mesh fixation method does not significantly affect the overall complication rates, including wound infection, seroma, and hematoma formation. These findings are consistent with the broader literature, which generally supports the idea that the fixation technique does not substantially influence postoperative complications.

Limitations

First, the relatively small sample size (75 patients) may limit the ability to detect small differences between groups, especially in secondary outcomes such as complications and recurrence rates. Second, the follow-up duration of 6 months, while sufficient to evaluate early postoperative outcomes, is insufficient to capture long-term recurrence rates (which typically manifest beyond 1–2 years) or the evolution of chronic pain and mesh sensation. Third, the retrospective registration of the trial is a limitation, though all outcomes were prespecified in the ethics-approved protocol. Fourth, this was a single-center study, which may limit generalizability. Finally, blinding of the operating surgeon was not feasible, though outcome assessors and statistician were blinded.

Conclusions

In the early postoperative period following laparoscopic TAPP inguinal hernia repair, mesh fixation with surgical glue results in superior pain control, better quality of life, and faster return to work compared to sutures and tacks, without increasing early complication rates. Glue fixation also presents a cost-effective alternative to tacks, with no significant differences in early recurrence rates or operative time.

The choice of mesh fixation method does not significantly affect overall complication rates, including wound infection, seroma, and hematoma formation. This finding is consistent with the broader literature.

These results point to glue fixation as a potential best alternative in terms of cost-effectiveness and clinical outcomes for the early postoperative period. Longer follow-up is needed to confirm the durability of these findings.
