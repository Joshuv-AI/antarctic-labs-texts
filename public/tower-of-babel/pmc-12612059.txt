Physiological indicators drive climate-smart and sustainable AWD irrigation in rice systems
Authors: Bittawat Wichaidist, Amornrat Intrman, Pavit Junhom, Songsak Puttrawutichai, Chawakorn Rewtragulpaibul, Chaisri Suksaroj
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Efficient water management is crucial for modern rice cultivation, particularly in the context of climate change and limited water resources. To optimize irrigation, this study evaluated the physiological responses of rice to water stress. From January to April 2022, a randomized complete block design (RCBD) was employed to evaluate physiological traits associated with water regulation in rice plants. Key photosynthetic metrics, including stomatal conductance (gsw), leaf temperature (Tleaf), fluorescence (Fs), electron transport rate (ETR), and photosystem II efficiency (ΦPSII), as well as stress markers such as malondialdehyde (MDA) content and osmolality, were monitored. Results showed that gsw shifts preceded visible stress symptoms, making them reliable early indicators of irrigation need. In the second experiment, water-use efficiency, plant stress, and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions were measured from October 2022 to February 2023 to refine alternative wetting and drying (AWD) methods for better water-use efficiency. Using gsw thresholds, the modified AWD (mAWD) outperformed conventional AWD (cAWD) in terms of irrigation water productivity (WPI) and consumptive water footprint (WFconsumption) without inducing stress conditions in rice. WPI for continuous flooding (CF), cAWD, mAWD was 1.16, 2.72 and 3.92 kg m−3, while WFconsumption was 1,079.93, 584.90 and 517.57 m3 t−1, respectively. Additionally, mAWD reduced yield-scaled GHG emissions, enhancing environmental benefits. This study underscores the value of physiological monitoring, especially gsw, to advance AWD irrigation for sustainable rice production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23184-8.

Introduction

Rice serves as a staple food for the majority of the global population, particularly in Asian countries, where it plays a critical role in ensuring food security. On average, approximately 480 million metric tons of milled rice are produced annually. However, rice production places a significant strain on water resources. Agriculture accounts for over 85% of the world’s water consumption, and irrigation alone consumes about 35% of that total—more than half of which is utilized for rice cultivation1–3. Paddy rice, which is favored for its high yield, is produced in far greater quantities than upland rice. However, this method is highly water-intensive, requiring at least twice as much water as other staple grains4. In Thailand, for instance, rice farming occupies over 11 million hectares of land as of 2022, contributing significantly to the country’s economy with an export value exceeding 160 billion Thai baht5.

For paddy rice cultivation, continuous flooding (CF) is the most widely adopted irrigation method due to its practicality and simplicity. Despite its prevalence, CF has relatively low water-use efficiency. Studies have shown that producing just 1 kg of rice using this method requires between 1 to 3 cubic meters of water1,3,6. This immense water demand highlights the resource-intensive nature of rice cultivation. However, the challenges associated with water availability are exacerbated by global warming and shifting climate patterns, which have led to increasingly unpredictable rainfall. Irregular rainfall distribution has resulted in prolonged dry spells and severe droughts, particularly in regions already vulnerable to water scarcity7,8. Such conditions have created significant disruptions in agricultural irrigation systems, particularly in years where rainfall falls below average levels. The situation has been further aggravated by the ongoing impacts of climate change, which are expected to intensify water-related challenges in the future. In many rice-producing regions, the current irrigation systems are struggling to meet water demands, especially during drought years when natural water sources are insufficient to support agricultural needs9. To balance the demands of both humans and the environment and move closer to long-term sustainable development goals by making optimal use of limited water resources, effective irrigation is necessary10. Given these challenges, improving irrigation technologies and water management strategies for rice cultivation has become a critical priority.

To ensure sustainable rice production in the face of changing climatic conditions, developing innovative irrigation methods that enhance water-use efficiency (WUE) is essential. Traditional irrigation practices, such as continuous flooding (CF), have proven to be inefficient, consuming vast amounts of water and contributing to the depletion of resources. In contrast, alternative methods, such as alternate wetting and drying (AWD), are emerging as viable solutions to improve water management in rice cultivation. AWD is a well-documented and cost-effective irrigation technique capable of reducing water usage by up to 57% compared to CF, without compromising rice yields11–13. AWD enhances water-use efficiency by periodically allowing the paddy fields to dry between irrigation events, significantly lowering water consumption in the production system. This approach not only optimizes water use but also increases water productivity, making it a critical tool for sustainable agriculture in water-scarce regions12,14. As global water resources become increasingly limited, integrating AWD and other water-saving technologies with climate-adaptive agricultural practices can mitigate water stress and build resilience against the adverse effects of climate change.

Furthermore, global trends underscore the urgent need to minimize greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, emphasizing the importance of AWD as a long-term irrigation strategy. AWD provides substantial agronomic and environmental advantages in addition to water conservation, notably lowering the emission of methane, which is an important contributor of climate change caused by rice production. By intermittently aerating the soil, methane emissions from anaerobic decomposition are significantly minimized15–17. Moreover, AWD enhances the uptake of essential nutrients, such as nitrogen and phosphorus, promoting better crop nutrition and yield quality. Additionally, this method reduces the accumulation of toxic pollutants such as mercury (Hg) and arsenic (As) in rice grains, thereby improving food safety and consumer health9,12. Notably, previous investigations have reported no adverse effects on rice yield or overall performance under suitable AWD practices. This suggests that AWD can be implemented without compromising productivity, provided it is managed appropriately. However, the transition to AWD requires careful evaluation of stress factors that may arise during water-saving practices. Water shortage can trigger hormone signalling and lead to reduced stomatal conductance, lower rates of photosynthesis, increased spikelet sterility, diminished biomass, obstructed assimilate translocation, and ultimately cause a decrease in yield production18–20. Therefore, further research and in-depth analysis of key criteria—such as physiology, stresses, and water availability—are essential to refine AWD protocols for diverse agro-ecological conditions.

Water-saving irrigation techniques, such as AWD, have proven beneficial in reducing water use while maintaining rice productivity. However, a previous study did not account for physiological stress signs, which may represent opportunities for improvement14. As a functional link between a plant and the environment, plant physiology is essential for photosynthesis, growth, and stress resilience21. Consequently, it can be determined that the development of AWD necessitates meticulous tracking backed by research emphasizing the physiological and biochemical indicators in determining plant stress. The efficiency and environmental adaptability of AWD might be increased by integrating physiological indicators into irrigation management22–25. By integrating plant physiological characteristics into irrigation decision-making processes, this work seeks to advance the AWD irrigation technique. This novel strategy attempts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions, maximize water use efficiency, and avoid negative impacts on rice plant. Therefore, the goal of this study is to enhance the AWD technique by incorporating physiological data into irrigation decision-making. This approach aims to optimize water-use efficiency, reduce greenhouse gas emissions, and ensure there are no adverse effects on plant physiology or productivity. By integrating precise irrigation strategies, the study seeks to enhance water management while contributing to sustainable rice production and mitigating environmental impacts.

Methods

Experimental design and treatments

This study was conducted at the Thailand Rice Science Institute (TRSI) greenhouse, located in Mueang District, Suphan-Buri Province, Thailand (14.48041°, 100.08941°) (Fig. 1), using the rice cultivar ‘RD41’. The experiment was conducted approximately ten meters above sea level. The experimental greenhouse was not a controlled environmental facility. The weather data collected during the experiment showed a temperature range of 18.6 °C to 39.4 °C, a relative humidity range of 34.3% to 98.9%, and daily solar radiation values ranging from 65,475 to 419,388 W·m−2·min−1.. The experiments were designed to investigate the effects of various irrigation treatments on plant physiology, with the aim of enhancing water management strategies for rice cultivation. Phase 1: plant physiological study for improving irrigation decisions

The study utilized a randomized complete block design (RCBD) to analyze essential physiological data related to water balance in rice plants from January to April 2022. For the experiment, pots with a diameter of 12 inches were used. The study compared rice plants under well-watered conditions (control, CK) with two drought stress treatments (Fig. 2). Water stress was induced by withholding irrigation until specific visual and soil moisture criteria were reached, as defined by leaf rolling intensity26.Fig. 2Rice cultivar ‘RD41’ (a) control (CK), (b) moderate drought (MD) and (c) severe drought (SD).

Rice cultivar ‘RD41’ (a) control (CK), (b) moderate drought (MD) and (c) severe drought (SD).

Moderate drought (MD): This treatment was characterized by V-shaped folded leaves, indicating a moderate level of water stress. Soil moisture was monitored using tensiometers, with readings averaging approximately -40 kPa at a 15 cm soil depth.

Severe drought (SD): In this treatment, O-shaped rolled leaves, where the leaf margins touch, indicated severe drought stress. Tensiometer readings at 15 cm soil depth averaged around -80 kPa.

For both drought treatments, plants were maintained under stress conditions without irrigation until the specified leaf rolling and tensiometer criteria were reached. After achieving the desired stress levels, irrigation resumed following the continuous flooding (CF) method until harvest. Photosynthetic pigment contents, photosynthetic-related characteristics, MDA content, and osmolality were analyzed. Data were collected in 5 replicates to ensure statistical reliability.

Phase 2: enhancement of alternate wetting and drying (AWD)

To evaluate and enhance alternate wetting and drying (AWD) techniques for improving water-use efficiency, a randomized complete block design (RCBD) was implemented from October 2022 to February 2023. This study utilized plot size of 200 cm in length, 100 cm in width, and 70 cm in height to ensure ample space for thorough examination and observation. The study included three irrigation treatments to examine their effects on plant growth and physiological responses:

Continuous flooding (CF): This traditional irrigation method served as the control. Fields were initially flooded to a water depth of 5 cm during the seedling stage. Water levels were then maintained between 5–10 cm throughout the entire rice-growing period.

Conventional alternate wetting and drying (cAWD): This method closely resembled CF; however, the water level was periodically allowed to drop to 15 cm below the soil surface between the seedling and tillering stages.

Modified alternate wetting and drying (mAWD): This advanced AWD method introduced a physiological criterion—stomatal conductance (gsw)—to determine irrigation timing. Irrigation was applied when gsw dropped significantly below the values observed in the well-watered control plants. This approach aimed to improve irrigation precision by responding to the plant’s physiological water status rather than relying solely on soil moisture.

Plant growth and development data were rigorously collected in five replicates for each treatment to ensure statistical reliability. MDA content, osmolality, water productivity, water footprint and GHG emission were analyzed. This experimental design enabled a detailed comparison of the effects of traditional and alternative irrigation on plant physiology, water balance and overall growth.

Analytical method

Subsequently, after the collection of rice leaf samples for the measurements of photosynthetic pigment content, malondialdehyde content, and osmolality, the samples were processed immediately for the corresponding analyses.

Photosynthetic pigments content

The photosynthetic pigment content, including chlorophyll a (Chla), chlorophyll b (Chlb), and carotenoids, was meticulously measured to assess the plant’s physiological status under varying irrigation conditions27. To perform the analysis, 0.5 g of flag leaf tissue was carefully weighed and subsequently immersed in 10 ml of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The samples were incubated in complete darkness for 24 h to ensure efficient extraction of pigments. Following the extraction period, the absorbance of the pigment solution was measured using a spectrophotometer at specific wavelengths: 664 nm (A664), 648 nm (A648), and 470 nm (A470)14. Photosynthetic related characteristics

In this study, physiological data related to key processes such as photosynthesis and plant stress were carefully analyzed to refine irrigation decisions under the alternate wetting and drying (AWD) system. Parameters, including stomatal conductance (gsw), chlorophyll fluorescence (Fs), quantum yield of photosystem II (ΦPSII), and the electron transport rate (ETR), were measured and evaluated. These critical indicators of plant performance were assessed using the LI-600 porometer/fluorometer21. By monitoring these physiological responses, we aimed to gain a deeper understanding of how plants react to varying water availability, enabling us to fine-tune AWD practices to optimize water-use efficiency without causing undue stress to the crop.

Malondialdehyde content

The amount of malondialdehyde (MDA), which reflects the severity of lipid peroxidation caused by reactive oxygen species (ROS), is a crucial biomarker of drought-induced oxidative stress in plants28. The determination of malondialdehyde (MDA) was performed following the method29. To begin, 1 g of fresh rice leaf tissue was carefully weighed and homogenized in 5 mL of a 0.1% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) solution. The homogenized samples were subjected to centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C to separate the supernatant from the leaf debris. Following centrifugation, 1 mL of the resulting supernatant was mixed with 3 mL of a solution containing 0.5% thiobarbituric acid (TBA) dissolved in 20% TCA. The mixture was then heated in a 95° C water bath for 30 min to allow the reaction between MDA and TBA to proceed, forming a pink-colored complex known as MDA-TBA adducts, which absorb light at a specific wavelength. After the heating step, the reaction was immediately stopped by placing the samples in an ice bath to cool. The cooled samples were then centrifuged again at 10,000 rpm for 10 min to clarify the solution and remove any precipitates. The absorbance of the solution was measured spectrophotometrically at 532 nm to determine the concentration of MDA. A correction for non-specific turbidity was made by measuring absorbance at 600 nm. Osmolality

The analysis of osmoprotectant accumulation in plants, a crucial physiological response to low soil water potential, provides valuable insight into their ability to adjust osmotically and maintain water absorption under water-limiting conditions. Osmoprotectants, such as soluble sugars, amino acids (e.g., proline), and other small organic molecules, help plants balance cellular osmolality, enabling them to retain turgor pressure and avoid dehydration when exposed to water stress30. In this study, the osmolality of fully inflated leaves was evaluated using the following technique. Before analysis, the samples were stabilized by storing the cellular components at -20 °C for 24 h. The osmolality was measured using a vapor pressure osmometer31.

Gas sampling and analysis

Given that rice cultivation contributes significantly to agricultural emissions, especially methane (CH4) and nitrous oxide (N2O), which are potent greenhouse gases, it is imperative to investigate on GHG emissions in rice production15,35. The closed chamber method was used to evaluate the emissions of methane (CH4) and nitrous oxide (N2O). The transparent acrylic chamber with a volume of 216 litre base (60 cm × 60 cm × 60 cm) and also 216 L top was used for gases collection. To maintain hermetic conditions during sampling, the chamber top was placed over the base and sealed with water. While a thermometer recorded the inside temperature for emission calculations, a battery-operated fan made sure that the air was mixed evenly. A 50-mL airtight syringe was used to collect gas samples every week at 9:00 a.m. for 12 weeks. During a 30 min interval, samples were collected at 0, 5, 10, 20, and 30 min then immediately transferred to 30-mL evacuated glass vials sealed with butyl rubber septa for analysis35. A Thermo Scientific Trace 1310 gas chromatograph installed with an electron capture detector (ECD) for N2O and a flame ionization detector (FID) for CH4 was used to measure the amounts of these two substances.

Phase 2: enhancement of alternate wetting and drying (AWD)

Water efficient assessment

Water consumption plays a crucial role in agriculture, particularly in rice cultivation, where efficient water use is vital for sustainable production and effective resource management. An analysis of water consumption for rice cultivar ‘RD41’ revealed the following data under three distinct water management regimes: continuous flooding (CF), conventional alternate wetting and drying (cAWD), and modified alternate wetting and drying (mAWD). The observed water consumption values were 0.45 ± 0.02 m3 m−2 for CF, 0.19 ± 0.02 m3 m−2 for cAWD, and 0.16 ± 0.01 m3 m−2 for mAWD (Fig. 6A). The results clearly indicate that both AWD methods resulted in significantly lower water consumption compared to the CF method, highlighting their potential for enhancing water-use efficiency in rice production. Interestingly, despite the differences in water usage, all treatments produced comparable yields, with values of 0.519 ± 0.039 kg m−2 for CF, 0.503 ± 0.049 kg m−2 for cAWD, and 0.511 ± 0.035 kg m−2 for mAWD (Fig. 6B). When evaluating irrigation water productivity (WPI), a measure of partial productivity based on the volume of irrigation water utilized (Tuong et al. 2005), it becomes evident that the WPI for rice under both AWD techniques were significantly higher than that of CF. The WPI values recorded for CF, cAWD, and mAWD were 1.16 ± 0.11, 2.72 ± 0.16, and 3.29 ± 0.27 kg m−3, respectively (Fig. 6C). Moreover, the consumptive water footprint (WFconsumption), which serves as an indicator of water productivity by encompassing both direct and indirect water use, reveals a similar trend. The WFconsumption values for rice under CF, cAWD, and mAWD were 1,079.93 ± 97.29, 585.00 ± 35.81, and 517.57 ± 39.69 m3 ton−1, respectively (Fig. 6D). In addition, a comparison between the two types of AWD in this study revealed notable significant differences in water consumption and WPI. These findings highlight the importance of integrating plant physiological data to enhance the AWD technique, ultimately contributing to improved water-use efficiency and promoting sustainable practices in rice production. This underscores the potential of mAWD as a more effective management strategy in optimizing water resources while maintaining crop yield.

This result aligns with experimental studies on the water use efficiency of the AWD technique in rice cultivation. According to the study, appropriately used AWD dramatically reduced water usage without compromising rice yield, thereby decreasing the water footprint and increasing water productivity40–42.

Discussion

Water deficit is one of the most critical constraints for rice production, especially in rainfed conditions. As a semi-aquatic species, rice requires careful water management to balance water availability and avoid drought stress. It is crucial to base irrigation decisions on plant physiological data to increase water-use efficiency (WUE) and prevent rice from suffering from drought9,30,44,45. By monitoring key physiological responses, such as photosynthesis and stress indicators, irrigation systems can be optimized to prevent water stress, close knowledge gaps, and develop more efficient, adaptive irrigation techniques for sustainable rice production. Ultimately, these initiatives contribute to sustainable rice production by optimizing water use while maintaining plant health and ensuring yield stability. The effects of water deficiency stress on plant physiology have been the subject of several investigations, with a particular emphasis on photosynthetic traits. Crop productivity is largely determined by photosynthetic components, with photosynthetic pigments serving as important markers of water availability and their impact on yield production22,46.

Examining the additional photosynthetic characteristics that respond to water deficit stress reveals substantial interactions between water availability and plant physiology. Prominent physiological parameters that are affected by water status consist of stomatal conductance (gsw), leaf temperature (Tleaf), chlorophyll fluorescence (Fs), electron transport rate (ETR), quantum yield of photosystem II (ΦPsII), osmolality and malondialdehyde (MDA) content21. Stomatal conductance and net photosynthetic rate often exhibit a linear relationship; changes in gsw have a direct impact on net photosynthesis levels. In general, gsw and transpiration rates are greatly impacted by leaf water status, especially during water stress when there is often a high link between gsw and leaf water potential23,47. In addition, plants under drought stress demonstrated greater leaf temperatures at both the vegetative and generative growth stages than plants with sufficient water availability48,49. Furthermore, chlorophyll fluorescence intensity (Fs) normally serves as an indicator of a plant’s response to environmental stress and can provide insights into growth and yield capacity. Under water deficit conditions, fluorescence kinetics change due to variations in the ratios of photochemical reactions to energy-dependent quenching, which affect electron transport (ETR) and quantum yield (ΦPsII) during photosynthesis50,51. The examination reveals that plant physiological responses, particularly photosynthetic characteristics, are closely linked to water deficit conditions. Understanding these relationships is crucial for optimizing irrigation practices to enhance the resilience and productivity of rice crops exposed to varying environmental stresses. According to the general response of plants to stressful environments24, all physiological and stress parameters of rice under both levels of drought significantly differed from those of the control group (CK). These fluctuations in the plant’s traits or activities are a direct result of stress conditions.

Furthermore, the effect of dehydration on physiological indicators associated with stress, including osmolality and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, was examined. By disrupting internal processes and promoting the production of excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), inadequate water availability can impede plant growth and induce oxidative stress. MDA, a byproduct of lipid peroxidation, is closely correlated with the severity of stress in plants. As such, it serves as a reliable marker of oxidative stress52,53. Additionally, as a defense mechanism for osmoregulation, plants typically accumulate organic solutes in their cytoplasm in response to water deficiency54. Therefore, osmolality serves as a valuable indicator for assessing the water status of plants due to its role in maintaining cellular integrity and hydration. According to the study, a severe water deficit significantly increased levels of osmolality and MDA in rice plants. The sensitivity of these physiological markers to water availability and their value in evaluating plant stress responses are highlighted by these increases, which represent increased oxidative and osmotic stress under drought circumstances.

The study on the enhancement of alternate wetting and drying (AWD) emphasizes the importance of water consumption in rice cultivation and the efficiency methods to enhance water-use efficiency. Without compromising rice yield, both conventional AWD (cAWD) and modified AWD (mAWD) significantly reduced water consumption compared to continuous flooding (CF). In addition, irrigation water production (WPI) was noticeably higher under both AWD systems, especially mAWD, suggesting more efficient irrigation. AWD treatments also had a lower consumptive water footprint (WFconsumption), which further supports their benefits in sustainable water management. In terms of water usage and WPI, mAWD significantly exceeded cAWD, highlighting its potential as a better method for maximizing water use in rice cultivation. In rice production, where water management is crucial for optimal development and yield, water shortages are a major concern. To effectively assess drought stress and evaluate the effectiveness of modified irrigation practices, two parameters—malondialdehyde (MDA) and osmolality—are commonly chosen for investigation. MDA serves as a crucial biomarker for oxidative stress in plants, indicating the extent of cellular damage caused by lipid peroxidation when reactive oxygen species accumulate under drought conditions. Therefore, the evaluation of MDA levels can impair plant metabolism under stressful conditions, making it essential for determining the impact of irrigation modifications55,56. During the assessment of irrigation systems, increased MDA levels indicate significant oxidative stress that may hinder plant development and metabolism. Conversely, osmolality indicates the concentration of solutes in plant tissues and how well the plant can maintain turgor pressure when facing a water shortage. A higher osmolality indicates higher adaptations, indicating the adaptation to water-limited environments25,57. According to the experimental findings, the MDA content and osmolality of both AWD techniques were identical to those of the CF, suggesting that the mAWD was still at a suitable level and did not stress rice as a result of water scarcity.

Furthermore, greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions are significantly impacted by water management in rice cultivation. Common irrigation techniques like CF provide anaerobic conditions that encourage the decomposition of organic matter, which results in significant emissions of methane, a potent greenhouse gas that contributes to global warming15,16. This emphasizes how urgent it is to investigate irrigation techniques that lessen their negative effects on the ecosystem. Both AWD approaches have been demonstrated to greatly reduce methane emissions, even though they may produce slightly higher nitrous oxide emissions. The overall global warming potential (GWP) is considerably lower with AWD systems, due to methane makes up a substantially larger portion of total GHG emissions. In addition, by increasing nitrogen uptake and decreasing belowground carbon input, optimizing photosynthate allocation to the grain can help minimize paddy N2O emissions. This highlights prospects to produce more rice with fewer emissions. On top of that, a climate-smart approach that allows for increased yields and improved nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) without promoting N2O emissions is provided by combining urea deep placement with AWD irrigation in a ground cover rice production system17,58,59. This phenomenon highlights the importance of investigating GHG emissions associated with water management in rice production. Understanding these emissions allows us to find possibilities to optimize water consumption, reduce GHG emissions, and implement more sustainable irrigation systems.

Finally, in the enhancement of AWD, the application of gsw monitoring in mAWD irrigation showed the improvement of water productivity compared to cAWD. In addition, it was discovered that mAWD reduced yield-scaled GHG emissions and improved water productivity in comparison to cAWD without influencing rice stress conditions. Thus, it can be concluded that mAWD is a potential strategy for making rice cultivation more ecologically friendly and resource-efficient. The transformation of AWD to mAWD may reduce water use by more than 50% compared to CF and 15% compared to cAWD. This may increase water management capability in the Chao Phraya and Mae Klong basins by 9.2 billion m3 compared to CF and 3.0 billion m3 compared to cAWD, respectively.

Conclusion

In the context of modern rice cultivation, where water scarcity and climate change pose significant challenges, efficient irrigation management has become indispensable for sustaining crop productivity and environmental health. This study highlights the critical role of physiological monitoring, particularly stomatal conductance (gsw), as a sensitive and early indicator of water stress, enabling proactive irrigation decisions before visible damage occurs.

By integrating plant physiological data into the alternate wetting and drying (AWD) irrigation strategy, the modified AWD (mAWD) system demonstrated superior performance in optimizing water use. The mAWD approach significantly reduced water consumption by more than 50% compared to continuous flooding (CF) and achieved higher water productivity than conventional AWD (cAWD), all while maintaining rice yield and minimizing plant stress. Moreover, mAWD effectively lowered yield-scaled greenhouse gas emissions, especially methane, thereby contributing to climate change mitigation and aligning with sustainable agricultural development goals.

This research highlights the transformative potential of integrating plant physiological insights with irrigation management to enhance resource efficiency, mitigate environmental impact, and ensure the resilience of rice production systems. The adoption of mAWD represents a promising, science-driven pathway toward sustainable, climate-smart agriculture that balances water conservation with food security in water-limited regions.
