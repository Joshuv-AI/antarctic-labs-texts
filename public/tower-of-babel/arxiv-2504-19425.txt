Fibrewise compactifications and generalised limits in commutative and noncommutative topology
Alexander Mundey

Abstract
. We introduce fibrewise compactifications in both the setting of locally compact Hausdorff spaces and continuous maps, and the parallel setting of C ∗ C^{*} -algebras and nondegenerate multiplier-valued ∗ * -homomorphisms. In both situations, we use fibrewise compactifications to define regulated limits. In the topological setting, regulated limits extend classical inverse limits so that the resulting limit space remains locally compact; examples include the path spaces of directed graphs. In the operator-algebraic setting, regulated limits realise a direct-limit construction for multiplier-valued ∗ * -homomorphisms; examples include the cores of relative Cuntz–Pimsner algebras.

1.
Introduction

Locally compact Hausdorff spaces are a mainstay of modern mathematics and are often considered to be “nice” topological spaces, but the category of locally compact Hausdorff spaces and continuous maps has limitations. Notably, the category is not closed under taking inverse limits. The purpose of this article is to provide a solution to this problem, along with the analogous problem for
C^{*}
-algebras.

The inverse limit
\varprojlim(X_{i},f_{i})
of

is Hausdorff, but in general it is not locally compact. This can be remedied by insisting that the maps

are
proper

[
8
, Theorem 3.7.13]
; that is,
f_{i}^{-1}(K)
is compact for all compact
K\subseteq X_{i}

. In practice, one may not always have the luxury of proper maps.

A related situation occurs in the setting of
C^{*}
-algebras. Given a sequence of

-algebras and

-homomorphisms

we can form the direct limit

. Gelfand duality implies that if the

are commutative, and the maps

take an approximate identity of

to one of

for which the

In this article, we provide a remedy the problems occurring in both situations, dealing with the topological setting in
Section

2
and the
C^{*}
-algebraic setting in
Section

3
. We do so by introducing
fibrewise compactifications
in both situations. Fibrewise compactifications allow us to extend a continuous map to a proper continuous map, or a morphism between
C^{*}
-algebras to a proper morphism in the sense of
[
7
]
. We use fibrewise compactifications to construct more general notions of limits called
regulated limits
.

In the topological setting, the idea of fibrewise compactifications dates back to Whyburn’s unified space of a continuous map
[
30
,
31
]
. The theory has since been further developed and refined
[
1
,
4
,
13
]
.
The idea is to start with a continuous map
f\colon X\to Y
and “compactify its fibres” to extend
f
to a continuous proper map
\widetilde{f}\colon\widetilde{X}\to Y
, whose domain
\widetilde{X}
is a new locally compact Hausdorff space containing
X
as an open subspace.
The original approach of
Whyburn was to “glue”
Y
to the fibres
\{f^{-1}(y)\}_{y\in Y}
of
f
“at infinity”.
The existing literature on fibrewise compactifications assumes that
f\colon X\to Y
is surjective, so we formulate a more general definition (see
Definition

2.1
) to handle non-surjective maps. We call a fibrewise compactification in which
\widetilde{f}
is also surjective a
perfection
since the maps
\widetilde{f}
are perfect (continuous, proper, and surjective).

Inductively applying fibrewise compactifications to
1
, yields a new sequence

(3)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{X}_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{X}_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{X}_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{f}_{0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{f}_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{f}_{2}}

of locally compact Hausdorff spaces,
connected by proper continuous maps.
Regulated limits in this setting are a class of limits of the form
\varprojlim(\widetilde{X}_{i},\widetilde{f}_{i})
that depend on a suitable sequence of open sets
U_{i}\subseteq X_{i}
. Regulated limits are locally compact and Hausdorff, and the original limit
\varprojlim(X_{i},f_{i})
continuously embeds in
\varprojlim(\widetilde{X}_{i},\widetilde{f}_{i})
(see
Corollary

2.40
).

Different choices of fibrewise compactification result in different regulated limits. We highlight three constructions that are somewhat canonical. The first construction is based on Whyburn’s unified space, which we call the
unified limit

\unilim(X_{i},f_{i})
. The second construction
\minlim(X_{i},f_{i})
is the smallest regulated limit of
(X_{i},f_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, and the third
\perlim(X_{i},f_{i})
is the smallest regulated limit in which the universal projections
\perlim(X_{i},f_{i})\to\widetilde{X}_{i}
are surjective.

Examples of regulated limits—and part of the author’s motivation for introducing them (cf.
[
22
, Chapter 3]
)—comes from directed-graph
C^{*}
-algebras. The

and

\partial E
of a directed (or topological) graph

are locally compact Hausdorff spaces that contain the space of one-sided infinite paths

of the graph (see
[
5
,
28
,
33
]
). In
Example

2.37
we show that

can be realised as a regulated limit of the form
\unilim(X_{i},f_{i})
, and in
Example

2.43
we show that
\partial E
can be realised as a regulated limit of the form
\perlim(X_{i},f_{i})
. Regulated limits provide a systematic way of studying these spaces.

In the
C^{*}
-algebraic setting, we extend the noncommutative topology paradigm to fibrewise compactifications and regulated limits. A noncommutative fibrewise compactification (see

) of a morphism

containing

In

-algebras

The noncommutative analogous of Whyburn’s unified spaces,

-algebras, and we show in

that every split extension of

The ideals defined by Pimsner

We use our noncommutative fibrewise compactifications to develop a notion of regulated limit for sequences of morphisms
(\varphi_{i}\colon A_{i}\to\mathcal{M}(A_{i+1}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

2.
The topological setting

Recall that a map
f\colon X\to Y
between topological spaces is
proper
if
f^{-1}(K)
is compact for all compact
K\subseteq Y
. In particular, the
fibres

f^{-1}(y)
of
f
are compact for all
y\in Y
.
We say that
f
is
perfect
if it is continuous, closed, surjective, and for every
y\in Y
the fibre
f^{-1}(y)
is compact in
X
. If
Y
is locally compact and Hausdorff, then perfect maps coincide with continuous proper surjections. Almost every topological space encountered in this paper is locally compact and Hausdorff, so we never need to worry about the distinction between perfect maps and continuous proper surjections.

Perfect maps respect local compactness; the continuous image of a locally compact space under a perfect map is again locally compact
[
20
]
. We recall that a subset of a topological space is
precompact
if it has compact closure. Locally compact Hausdorff spaces admit bases consisting of precompact open sets
[
8
, Theorem 3.2.2]
.

2.1.
Fibrewise compactifications and perfections

Given a continuous map
f\colon X\to Y
we define

(4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\pr(f)\coloneqq\bigcup\{W\mid W\subseteq Y&\text{ is a }\text{precompact open set}\\
&\text{ such that }f^{-1}{(\overline{W})}\text{ is compact}\}.\\
\end{split}

The set
\pr(f)
is open in
Y
. In
Lemma

2.19
we show that
\pr(f)
is the largest open subset
U
of
Y
such that the restriction
f|_{f^{-1}(U)}\colon f^{-1}(U)\to U
is proper, but we do not require this fact for now.

We are interested in the process of taking a continuous map and extending it to either a proper or perfect map.
We capture this idea with the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a continuous map between locally compact Hausdorff spaces. A
fibrewise compactification
of
f
is a pair
(Z,g)
consisting of a locally compact Hausdorff space
Z
and a continuous proper map
g\colon Z\to Y
such that

(F1)

there is an open inclusion
\iota_{X}\colon X\to Z
such that
g\circ\iota_{X}=f
; and

(F2)

the restriction of
g
to
{Z\setminus\overline{\iota_{X}(X)}}
is a homeomorphism onto a subset of
\pr(f)
that is closed in the subspace topology of
\pr(f)
.

If
g
is also surjective, then
g
is perfect, and we call
(Z,g)
a
perfection
of
f
. If
\overline{\iota_{X}(X)}=Z
, then we call
(Z,g)
a
strict fibrewise compactification
. We say that fibrewise compactifications
(Z,g)
and
(Z^{\prime},g^{\prime})
of
f
are
isomorphic
if there is a homeomorphism
h\colon Z\to Z^{\prime}
such that
g^{\prime}=h\circ g
and
h|_{X}=\id_{X}
.

Condition
(F1)
says that
g
is a proper map extending
f
to a larger domain,
while condition
(F2)
ensures that the extension is “not too large”. The inclusion, in
Z
, of points that lie outside of
\overline{\iota_{X}(X)}
is sometimes necessary to ensure surjectivity of
g
.

The notion of a “fibrewise compactification” has appeared in the literature previously (see
[
13
, §8]
and
[
1
]
). In these definitions it is assumed that

(a)

the map
f\colon X\to Y
is surjective, and

(b)

\overline{\iota_{X}(X)}
is dense in
Z
.

If
f\colon X\to Y
is surjective, then a strict fibrewise compactification of
f
corresponds to a fibrewise compactification of
f
in the sense of
[
1
,
13
]
.
If
f\colon X\to Y
is not surjective, then things are more subtle. As the following lemma shows, points on the boundary of
f(X)
can act as an obstruction to properness of
f
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose that
f\colon X\to Y

can be identified with the topological subspace of

\big((-\infty,0]\times\{0\}\times\{0\}\big)\cup S^{2}\cup\big([1,\infty)\times\{0\}\times\{0\}\big)

consisting of two infinite rays attached to the sphere

, and

can be identified with the projection onto the first component. The closed embedding of

in

can be identified with the subspace

\big((-\infty,0]\times\{0\}\times\{0\}\big)\cup\big\{(x,y,0)\in S^{2}\colon y\geq 0\big\}\cup\big([1,\infty)\times\{0\}\times\{0\}\big).

The minimal fibrewise compactification consists of the sphere

and the projection onto the first component.

For the remainder of this section we are exclusively concerned with sub-fibrewise compactifications of the unified space; however larger fibrewise compactifications do exist. If

is surjective then it is shown in
[

, Remarks 1.3]
that there is an analogous fibrewise compactification to the Stone–Čech compactification.

Remark 2.17
.

We outline the construction of a “large” fibrewise compactification. For each locally compact Hausdorff space
X
, let
\beta X
denote its Stone–Čech compactification. Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a continuous map between locally compact Hausdorff spaces and consider the extension
\overline{f}\colon X\to\beta\overline{f(X)}
. By the universal property of the Stone–Čech compactification,
\overline{f}
extends to a unique continuous map
\beta{\overline{f}}\colon\beta X\to\beta\overline{f(X)}
such that
(\beta\overline{f})|_{X}=\overline{f}
. Let
Z=(\beta\overline{f})^{-1}(\overline{f(X)})
and let
g
be the composition
Z\overset{\beta\overline{f}}{\to}\overline{f(X)}\hookrightarrow Y
.

Since
\overline{f(X)}
is open in
\beta\overline{f(X)}
,
Z
is open in
\beta X
. Since
X
is open in
\beta X
and
X\subseteq Z
,
X
is open in
Z
. Moreover,
g|_{X}=f
. If
K\subseteq Y
is compact, then
g^{-1}(K)=(\beta\overline{f})^{-1}(K\cap\overline{f(X)})
is compact in

, so
g
is proper. Since
X
is dense in
\beta X
it is also dense in
Z
, and so
(Z,g)
is a strict fibrewise compactification of
f
.

By
[
11
, Theorem 10.13]
, the space

is the largest subspace of
\beta X
for which

has a continuous proper extension into
Y
. Thus, we suspect that
(Z,g)
is universal among strict fibrewise compactifications, but we leave this question and its correct formulation open.

2.3.
Sub-fibrewise compactifications of the unified space

Although the unified space construction yields a perfection of
f\colon X\to Y
, it is by no means efficient. There are typically many sub-fibrewise compactifications and sub-perfections. To classify these we introduce the following notion.

Definition 2.18
.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a continuous map between locally compact Hausdorff spaces. An open set

is said to be
f
-proper
if the restriction
{\left.\kern-1.2ptf\vphantom{|}\right|_{f^{-1}(U)}}\colon f^{-1}(U)\to U
is proper, and

-perfect
if the restriction is perfect.

Given

we consider the open set

\per(f)\coloneqq\pr(f)\cap\intt(\overline{f(X)}).

Lemma 2.19

(cf.
[

, Remark 1.7]
and
[

Let

(a)

-proper if and only if

-proper subset of

-perfect subset of

Proof.

First suppose that

has a precompact open neighbourhood

Since

If

. Hence,

is
f
-proper.
Conversely, suppose that

is
f
-proper and fix

is a locally compact Hausdorff space, there is a precompact open neighbourhood

of

. Then

is compact.

Now suppose that

is open and fix

is
f
-perfect.
Conversely, suppose that

is
f
-perfect. Then

If

Let

2.4.
Composing sub-fibrewise compactifications

Let
X,Y
, and
Z
be locally compact Hausdorff spaces and suppose that
f\colon X\to Y
and
g\colon Y\to Z
are continuous. There are at least three ways that the unified space construction interacts with the composition of
f
and
g
.

The simplest approach is to take the unified space
({X}\uplus_{g\circ f}{Z},{(g\circ f)}_{\uplus})
of
g\circ f
. This loses information about the intermediate space
Y
, making it undesirable when we introduce regulated limits in
Section

2.5
.

The second approach is to first form the unified space
({Y}\uplus_{g}{Z},{g}_{\uplus})
of
g
. Considering the induced map
\iota_{Y}\circ f\colon X\to{Y}\uplus_{g}{Z}
, we form
({X}\uplus_{\iota_{Y}\circ f}{({Y}\uplus_{g}{Z})},{(\iota_{Y}\circ f)}_{\uplus})
.
The second construction is distinct from the first: as sets
{X}\uplus_{g\circ f}{Z}=X\sqcup Z
, while
{X}\uplus_{\iota_{Y}\circ f}{({Y}\uplus_{g}{Z})}=X\sqcup Y\sqcup Z
. So the second construction remembers the intermediate space
Y
.
The composition
{g}_{\uplus}\circ{(\iota_{Y}\circ f)}_{\uplus}
is perfect.

The third approach is to first form the unified space
({X}\uplus_{f}{Y},{f}_{\uplus})
of
f
. Considering the composition
g\circ{f}_{\uplus}\colon{X}\uplus_{f}{Y}\to Z
, we form
({({X}\uplus_{f}{Y})}\uplus_{g\circ{f}_{\uplus}}{Z},{(g\circ{f}_{\uplus})}_{\uplus})
. As sets
{({X}\uplus_{f}{Y})}\uplus_{g\circ{f}_{\uplus}}{Z}=X\sqcup Y\sqcup Z
, like in the second construction.

Remark 2.29
.

Despite the unified space (and its sub-fibrewise compactifications) displaying an associativity-like property, it is not clear to the author whether any of the constructions can be made functorial.
An interesting observation is that the unified space (and other sub-fibrewise compactifications) can be used to define a category whose objects are locally compact Hausdorff spaces that differ from the usual category with continuous maps.

Let
X
and
Y
be locally compact Hausdorff spaces. Suppose that
(i_{A},A,p_{A})
is a triple consisting of a locally compact Hausdorff space
A
, an open inclusion
i_{A}\colon X\to A
, and a continuous proper map
p_{A}\colon A\to Y
. We say that another such triple
(i_{A^{\prime}},A^{\prime},p_{A^{\prime}})
is isomorphic to
(i_{A},A,p_{A})
if there is a homeomorphism
h\colon A\to A^{\prime}
such that
i_{A}\circ h=i_{A^{\prime}}
and
p_{A}\circ h=p_{A^{\prime}}
.

Let
{\mathbf{LCH}}
be the category of locally compact Hausdorff spaces with continuous maps.
We define a new category
{{\mathbf{LCH}}}_{\uplus}
whose objects are also locally compact Hausdorff spaces. A morphism
X\to Y
in
{{\mathbf{LCH}}}_{\uplus}
is (an isomorphism class of) a triple
(i_{A},A,p_{A})
, which we denote by
[i_{A},A,p_{A}]
.
Composition of morphisms is defined using the unified space construction: if
[i_{A},A,p_{A}]\colon X\to Y
and
[i_{B},B,p_{B}]\colon Y\to Z
are morphisms in
{{\mathbf{LCH}}}_{\uplus}
, then
[i_{B},B,p_{B}]\circ[i_{A},A,p_{A}]

is defined to be

[\iota_{A}\circ i_{A},{A}\uplus_{i_{B}\circ p_{A}}{B},p_{B}\circ{(i_{B}\circ p_{A})}_{\uplus}]
. By
Lemma

2.27
, this composition is associative, so
{{\mathbf{LCH}}}_{\uplus}
is a category.

The map taking a morphism
f\colon X\to Y
in
{\mathbf{LCH}}
to
[\iota_{X},{X}\uplus_{f}{Y},{f}_{\uplus}]
in
{{\mathbf{LCH}}}_{\uplus}
is not functorial since if
g\colon Y\to Z
is another continuous map then typically
{X}\uplus_{g\circ f}{Z}\not\cong{X}\uplus_{f}{{Y}\uplus_{g}{Z}}
.

2.5.
Locally compactifying inverse limits

Let
(X_{i},f_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be an
inverse sequence
of topological spaces
X_{i}
and continuous maps
f_{i}\colon X_{i+1}\to X_{i}
. Its
inverse limit

\varprojlim(X_{i},f_{i})
is the unique space (up to homeomorphism) with continuous maps
\pi_{k}\colon\varprojlim(X_{i},f_{i})\to X_{k}
satisfying
\pi_{k}\circ f_{k}=\pi_{k+1}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, that is universal: if
Z
is another topological space with continuous maps
g_{k}\colon Z\to X_{k}
satisfying
g_{k}\circ f_{k}=g_{k+1}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then there is a unique continuous map
g\colon Z\to\varprojlim(X_{i},f_{i})
such that
f_{k}\circ g=g_{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

By
[
8
, Proposition 2.5.1]
, the inverse limit
\varprojlim(X_{i},f_{i})
may be described explicitly as the closed subspace
\{(x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mid x_{i}\in X_{i},\,f_{i}(x_{i+1})=x_{i}\}
of the product
\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}X_{i}
. The maps
\pi_{k}\colon\varprojlim(X_{i},f_{i})\to X_{k}
are the coordinate projections, and the collection
\{\pi_{k}^{-1}(U)\colon k\in\mathbb{N},\,U\in\tau_{X_{k}}\}
constitutes a base for the topology on
\varprojlim(X_{i},f_{i})
.
We record the following well-known result.

Lemma 2.30
.

Let
(X_{i},f_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(Y_{i},g_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be inverse sequences of topological spaces and continuous maps. Let
\pi_{k}\colon\varprojlim(X_{i},f_{i})\to X_{k}
and
\tau_{k}\colon\varprojlim(Y_{i},g_{i})\to Y_{k}
be the universal projections. If for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there are continuous maps
\alpha_{k}\colon X_{k}\to Y_{k}
satisfying
g_{k}\circ\alpha_{k+1}=\alpha_{k}\circ f_{k}
, then there is a unique continuous map
\alpha\colon\varprojlim(X_{i},f_{i})\to\varprojlim(Y_{i},g_{i})
satisfying
\alpha_{k}\circ\pi_{k}=\tau_{k}\circ\alpha
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, if each
\alpha_{k}
is injective, then so is
\alpha
.

Proof.

The first statement follows directly from the universal property of
\varprojlim(Y_{i},g_{i})
applied to the maps
\alpha_{i}\circ\pi_{i}\colon\varprojlim(X_{i},g_{i})\to Y_{i}
. To see that
\alpha
is injective, suppose that
\alpha(x)=\alpha(x^{\prime})
for some
x,x^{\prime}\in\varprojlim(X_{i},f_{i})
. Then
\alpha_{k}\circ\pi_{k}(x)=\tau_{k}\circ\alpha(x)=\tau_{k}\circ\alpha(x^{\prime})=\alpha_{k}\circ\pi_{k}(x^{\prime})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Since each
\alpha_{k}
is injective,
\pi_{k}(x)=\pi_{k}(x^{\prime})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Since the
\pi_{k}
are coordinate projections in
\prod_{i\in\mathbb{N}}X_{i}
, we have
x=x^{\prime}
.
∎

If each
X_{i}
is locally compact and Hausdorff, then
\varprojlim(X_{i},f_{i})
is Hausdorff, but it is not locally compact unless the maps
f_{i}
are proper
[
8
, Theorem 3.7.13]
.
Using the unified space construction we produce a new inverse limit
\varprojlim(\widetilde{X}_{i},\widetilde{f}_{i})
in which the maps
\widetilde{f}_{i}
are perfect extensions of the
f_{i}
.

Definition 2.31
.

Let
(X_{i},f_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be an inverse sequence of locally compact Hausdorff spaces with continuous maps
f_{i}\colon X_{i+1}\to X_{i}
.
Let
\widetilde{X}_{0}\coloneqq X_{0}
. For each

inductively define

\widetilde{X}_{i}\coloneqq{X_{i}}\uplus_{\iota_{X_{i-1}}\circ f_{i-1}}{\widetilde{X}_{i-1}}

and let
\widetilde{f}_{i}\colon\widetilde{X}_{i+1}\to\widetilde{X}_{i}
be the perfect map given by

\widetilde{f}_{i}(x)={(\iota_{X_{i}}\circ f_{i})}_{\uplus}(x)=\begin{cases}f_{i}(x)&\text{if }x\in X_{i+1},\\
x&\text{if }x\in\widetilde{X}_{i}.\end{cases}

We call the space
\unilim(X_{i},f_{i})\coloneqq\varprojlim(\widetilde{X}_{i},\widetilde{f}_{i})
the
unified limit
of
(X_{i},f_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
.

As sets we have
\widetilde{X}_{i}=\bigsqcup_{k=0}^{i}X_{k}
, however the topology on

is quite nontrivial. We extend the notation established in

2.4
and write

\widetilde{X}_{i}\eqqcolon{X_{i}}\uplus_{f_{i-1}}{{X_{i-1}}\uplus_{f_{i-2}}{{\cdots}\uplus_{f_{0}}{X_{0}}}}.

The next result follows inductively from
Lemma

2.27
.

Lemma 2.32
.

Let
(X_{i},f_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be an inverse sequence of locally compact Hausdorff spaces and continuous maps. Fix

. Let
f_{p,q}\colon{X_{p+q}}\uplus_{f_{p+q-1}}{{\cdots}\uplus_{f_{q+1}}{X_{q+1}}}\to\widetilde{X}_{q}

Then

is open in

is closed in
\widetilde{X}_{p+q}
.

The unified limit

can be thought of as a “local compactification” of the limit

.

Proposition 2.33
.

Let
(X_{i},f_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be an inverse sequence of locally compact Hausdorff spaces and continuous maps. The unified limit
\unilim({X}_{i},{f}_{i})
is a locally compact Hausdorff space. Moreover,

(a)

the universal projections
\widetilde{\pi}_{k}\colon\unilim({X}_{i},{f}_{i})\to\widetilde{X}_{k}
are perfect;

(b)

the sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(U_{0},\ldots,U_{m};K_{0},\ldots,K_{n})\coloneqq\big(\widetilde{\pi}_{0}^{-1}(U_{0})

\displaystyle\cap\cdots\cap\widetilde{\pi}_{m}^{-1}(U_{m})\big)

\displaystyle\setminus\big(\widetilde{\pi}_{0}^{-1}(K_{0})\cup\cdots\cup\widetilde{\pi}_{n}^{-1}(K_{n})\big)

with

;

the inclusions
\iota_{X_{k}}\colon X_{k}\hookrightarrow\widetilde{X}_{k}

(d)

is second countable, then

Proof.

By construction,
(\widetilde{X}_{i},\widetilde{f}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is an inverse sequence of locally compact Hausdorff spaces with perfect maps. By
[
8
, Theorem 3.7.13]
, the limit
\varprojlim(\widetilde{X}_{i},\widetilde{f}_{i})
is a locally compact Hausdorff space in the initial topology induced by the

are proper. The

\widetilde{f}_{k}
is surjective.
That the sets in part

form a base follows inductively from the base

By

2.30

One application of the unified limit is in extending a countably infinite product of locally compact spaces (which is typically not locally compact) to a locally compact space.

.

Let
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of locally compact Hausdorff spaces.
Let

Example 2.35
.

Suppose that
X_{i}=\mathbb{Z}
for all

. Then
\mathbb{Z}^{\infty}\coloneqq\prod_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{Z}

and

consists of all infinite sequences that start with the finite sequence

.

we write

for its

of sequences

if, for every

such that

for all

and

.

As a set, we identify the unified limit
\varprojlim^{\uplus}(\prod_{i=0}^{m}\mathbb{Z},p_{m})

\mathbb{Z}^{\infty}\sqcup\bigsqcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, the collection of all finite and infinite sequences in

. Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, and fix a finite set
F\subseteq\bigsqcup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{Z}^{i}
of finite sequences. Let

\mathcal{Z}(n,F)\coloneqq\Big\{a\in\mathbb{Z}^{\infty}\sqcup\bigsqcup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{Z}^{i}\mid(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})=n\text{ and }(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i})\notin F\text{ for all }i\in\mathbb{N}\Big\}.

Adapting
Proposition

2.33

(b)
to this setting, a base for the topology on
\varprojlim^{\uplus}(\prod_{i=0}^{m}\mathbb{Z},p_{m})
is given by the sets

.

Fix a finite sequence

3.
The
C^{*}
-algebraic setting

In this section, we move from classical point-set topology to noncommutative
C^{*}
-algebras. Briefly, a
C^{*}
-algebra is a norm-closed
*
-subalgebra of the bounded linear operators on some complex Hilbert space. We refer the reader to
[
2
,
6
,
23
]
for a standard introduction to
C^{*}
-algebras.

By Gelfand duality, every
commutative

C^{*}
-algebra
A
is isomorphic to
C_{0}(X)
, the algebra of continuous
\mathbb{C}
-valued functions vanishing at infinity on some locally compact Hausdorff space
X
.
The space
X
is compact if and only if
A
is unital (has a multiplicative identity). In fact, Gelfand duality provides an equivalence of categories between the category of compact Hausdorff spaces with continuous maps, and the category of unital commutative
C^{*}
-algebras with unital
*
-homomorphisms. A continuous map
f\colon X\to Y
is dual to the
*
-homomorphism
f^{*}\colon C(Y)\to C(X)
given by
f^{*}(a)=a\circ f
. As such, noncommutative unital
C^{*}
-algebras can be thought of as “noncommutative compact Hausdorff spaces”. Results about the category of compact Hausdorff spaces and continuous maps often have analogous in the category of
noncommutative
unital
C^{*}
-algebras and
*
-homomorphisms. This is referred to as the
noncommutative topology
paradigm.

In the noncompact setting things are more subtle, requiring us to introduce some additional terminology.
By an ideal in
C^{*}
-algebra we mean a norm-closed 2-sided ideal. Ideals in
C_{0}(X)
are isomorphic to
C_{0}(U)
for some open set
U\subseteq X
, and the quotient
C_{0}(X)/C_{0}(U)
is isomorphic to
C_{0}(X\setminus U)
, with the quotient map
C_{0}(X)\twoheadrightarrow C_{0}(X\setminus U)
given by restriction of functions to
X\setminus U
.
If
I
is an ideal in a
C^{*}
-algebra
A
then the
annihilator
of
I
is the ideal of
A
given by

(10)

I^{\perp}=\{a\in A\colon ab=0\text{ for all }b\in I\}.

An ideal
I\trianglelefteq A
is
essential
if
I^{\perp}=\{0\}
.
If
U\subseteq X
is open, then
C_{0}(U)^{\perp}\cong C_{0}(X\setminus\overline{U})
, so
C_{0}(U)\trianglelefteq C_{0}(X)
is essential if and only if
U
is dense in
X
.

The
multiplier algebra

\mathcal{M}(A)
of a
C^{*}
-algebra
A
is the largest unital
C^{*}
-algebra containing
A
as an essential ideal (see
[
19
, Ch. 2]
). The
strict topology
on
\mathcal{M}(A)
is generated by the seminorms
t\mapsto\|ta\|
and
t\mapsto\|t^{*}a\|
for all
a\in A
. For a commutative
C^{*}
-algebra
C_{0}(X)
there are isomorphisms
\mathcal{M}(C_{0}(X))\cong C_{b}(X)\cong C(\beta X)
, where
C_{b}(X)
is the continuous bounded functions on
X
, and
\beta X
is the Stone–Čech compactification of
X
. We usually identify
\mathcal{M}(C_{0}(X))
with
C_{b}(X)
and
C(\beta X)
.

Let
B
be another
C^{*}
-algebra.
Following
[
19
]
, we call a
*
-homomorphism
\varphi\colon A\to\mathcal{M}(B)
a
morphism
from
A
to
B
if
\varphi(A)B\coloneqq{\spaan}\{\varphi(a)b\colon a\in A,b\in B\}
is norm-dense in
B
. We write
\Mor(A,B)
for the collection of all morphisms from
A
to
B
. By
[
19
, Proposition 2.5]
, a
*
-homomorphism
\varphi\colon A\to\mathcal{M}(B)
belongs to
\Mor(A,B)
if and only if for some approximate unit
(a_{\lambda})
of
A
, we have
\varphi(a_{\lambda})\to 1_{\mathcal{M}(B)}
in the strict topology on
\mathcal{M}(B)
. If
I\trianglelefteq A
, then a morphism
\varphi\in\Mor(I,B)
extends to a unique morphism
\overline{\varphi}\in\Mor(A,B)
satisfying
\overline{\varphi}(a)b=\lim_{\lambda}\varphi(au_{\lambda})b
for all
a\in A
,
b\in B
, and approximate identities
(u_{\lambda})
for
I

[
19
, Proposition 2.1]
. If
\varphi\in\Mor(A,B)
, then the extension
\overline{\varphi}\colon\mathcal{M}(A)\to\mathcal{M}(B)
, induced by
A\trianglelefteq\mathcal{M}(A)
, is unital.

Following
[
7
]
, we say that a morphism
\varphi\in\Mor(A,B)
is
proper
if
\varphi(A)\subseteq B
.
We denote the collection of proper morphisms from
A
to
B
by
\Morp(A,B)
.
By post-compositing with the inclusion
B\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}(B)
, a
*
-homomorphism
\varphi\colon A\to B
corresponds to a
*
-homomorphism
A\to\mathcal{M}(B)
whose image is contained in
B
. Thus, a
*
-homomorphism
\varphi\colon A\to B

corresponds to a proper morphism in
\Morp(A,B)
if and only if for any approximate identity
(a_{\lambda})
for
A
, the net
(\varphi(a_{\lambda}))
is an approximate identity for
B
. We identify
\Morp(A,B)
with such
*
-homomorphisms.

It is important to recognise that if
\varphi\colon A\to\mathcal{M}(B)
belongs to
\Mor(A,B)
, then it is not usually the case that
\varphi\in\Morp(A,\mathcal{M}(B))
; as this requires
\overline{\varphi(A)\mathcal{M}(B)}=\mathcal{M}(B)
(see
Remark

3.7
below).
In the literature there is some confusion; the maps that we call “morphisms” and “proper morphisms” are both referred to a
nondegenerate
*
-homomorphisms
, despite their subtle distinction. We refrain from using the term “nondegenerate” to avoid confusion, except when referring to representations of
C^{*}
-algebras on Hilbert spaces and Hilbert modules.

Both morphisms and proper morphisms can be used to define categories whose objects are
C^{*}
-algebras. If
\phi\in\Mor(A,B)
and
\psi\in\Mor(B,C)
, then the composition of
\psi
with
\phi
is defined to be
\overline{\psi}\circ\phi\in\Mor(A,C)
, where
\overline{\psi}\colon\mathcal{M}(B)\to\mathcal{M}(C)
is the extension induced by the ideal
B\trianglelefteq\mathcal{M}(B)
.
The category of
C^{*}
-algebras and proper morphisms is a wide subcategory of the category of
C^{*}
-algebras and morphisms.
Composition of proper morphisms corresponds to the usual composition of
*
-homomorphisms.

If
f\colon X\to Y
is a continuous map between locally compact Hausdorff spaces, and
a\in C_{0}(Y)
, then
f^{*}(a)\coloneqq a\circ f
is continuous and bounded, but does not necessarily vanish at infinity. We instead get a
*
-homomorphism
f^{*}\colon C_{0}(Y)\to C_{b}(X)\cong\mathcal{M}(C_{0}(X))
such that
f^{*}(C_{0}(Y))C_{0}(X)
is dense in
C_{0}(X)
. That is,
f^{*}\in\Mor(C_{0}(Y),C_{0}(X))
. In fact, there is an equivalence of categories between the category of locally compact Hausdorff spaces and continuous maps and the category of commutative
C^{*}
-algebras with morphisms
[
12
,
32
]
.
As such, the “correct” category of
noncommutative
locally compact Hausdorff spaces is the category of
C^{*}
-algebras with morphisms.

If
f\colon X\to Y
is also assumed to be proper, then the map
f^{*}
instead takes values in
C_{0}(X)
. The equivalence of categories descends to one between the category of locally compact Hausdorff spaces with proper continuous maps and the category of commutative
C^{*}
-algebras with proper morphisms. The category of
C^{*}
-algebras and proper morphisms is the noncommutative analogue of the category of locally compact Hausdorff spaces and proper continuous maps.

3.1.
Noncommutative fibrewise compactifications and perfections

For our noncommutative analogue of fibrewise compactifications our starting datum is a morphism
\varphi\in\Mor(A,B)
. The idea is that we want to modify the codomain of
\varphi
to attain a proper morphism.

In
[
25
, Definition 3.8]
, Pimsner introduced the following ideal.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
\varphi\in\Mor(A,B)
. We call the ideal

\pim(\varphi)\coloneqq\varphi^{-1}(B)

of
A
the
Pimsner ideal of
\varphi
.

The Pimsner ideal of
\varphi\in\Mor(A,B)
is the largest ideal
J
of
A
such that
\varphi|_{J}\in\Morp(J,B)
.
For commutative
C^{*}
-algebras, the Pimsner ideal is related to the open set
\pr(f)
of
4
.

The following result translates the notions of
f
-proper and
f
-perfect sets in topological spaces to ideals in
C^{*}
-algebras. In the language of topological graphs the result is known to experts.

Lemma 3.5

(cf.
[
16
]
)
.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a continuous map between locally compact Hausdorff spaces and let
f^{*}\colon C_{0}(Y)\to C_{b}(X)
be the
*
-homomorphism given by
f^{*}(a)=a\circ f
.
An open set
U\subseteq Y
is
f
-proper if and only if
f^{*}(C_{0}(U))\subseteq C_{0}(X)
, and
f
-perfect if and only if
U
is
f
-proper and
f^{*}|_{C_{0}(U)}
is injective. In particular,
\pim(f^{*})=C_{0}(\pr(f))
.

Proof.

First suppose that
U\subseteq Y
is
f
-proper. Identify
C_{0}(U)
with the ideal of functions in
C_{0}(Y)
that are identically zero on
Y\setminus U
. Since
U
is
f
-proper, for any compactly supported
a\in C_{c}(U)
the function
f^{*}(a)
is compactly supported. So
f^{*}(C_{0}(U))\subseteq C_{0}(X)
. If
a\in\ker(f^{*})\cap C_{0}(U)
, then
f^{*}(a)(x)=a(f(x))=0
for all
x\in U
. So, if
U
is also
f
-perfect, then
a=0
. That is,
f^{*}|_{C_{0}(U)}
is injective.

For the converse, suppose that
U\subseteq Y
is open but not
f
-proper. Since
f|_{f^{-1}(U)}
is not proper, there exists a compact set
K\subseteq U
such that
f^{-1}(K)
is not compact. Take a precompact open set
V\subseteq U
such that
K\subseteq V\subseteq\overline{V}\subseteq U
. Since
\overline{V}
is compact, it is normal, so the Tietze Extension Theorem gives a compactly supported function
a\in C_{c}(U)
such that
a(y)=1
for all
y\in K
, and
a(y)=0
for all
y\in U\setminus\overline{V}
. So,
f^{*}(a)(x)=1
for all
x\in f^{-1}(K)
. Now fix a compact set
K_{0}\subseteq X
. Since
f^{-1}(K)
is closed and not compact, there exists
x_{K_{0}}\in f^{-1}(K)\setminus K_{0}
. As
f^{*}(a)(x_{K_{0}})=1
the function
f^{*}(a)
does not vanish at infinity, so
f^{*}(C_{0}(U))\not\subseteq C_{0}(X)
.

Now suppose
f|_{f^{-1}(U)}
does not surject onto
U
. Then there exists
y_{0}\in U\setminus f(X)
. If
y_{0}\in\overline{f(X)}\setminus f(X)
, then
Lemma

2.2
implies that
f|_{f^{-1}(U)}
is not proper, so we can assume that
y_{0}\in U\setminus\overline{f(X)}
. Take a precompact neighbourhood
V
of
y_{0}
such that
\overline{V}\subseteq U\setminus\overline{f(X)}
. Using Tietze’s Extension Theorem on
\overline{V}
, there exists
a\in C_{c}(U)
such that
a(y_{0})=1
and
a(y)=0
for all
y\in U\setminus\overline{V}
. Then
f^{*}(a)=0
, so
f^{*}
is not injective.

The final statement follows from the definition of
\pim(f^{*})
and the fact that
\pr(f)
is the maximal
f
-proper set.
∎

Lemma

3.5
indicates the importance of the Pimsner ideal in our formulation of noncommutative fibrewise compactifications.

If
I
is an ideal in a
C^{*}
-algebra

, then there is an induced
*
-homomorphism
\alpha_{I}\colon A\to\mathcal{M}(I)
satisfying
\alpha(a)b=ab
for all

and

. Since

is an ideal
\overline{\alpha_{I}(A)I}=I

. Moreover,

restricts to an isomorphism between the copies of

in

and in

.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
A
and
B
be
C^{*}
-algebras and let
\varphi\in\Mor(A,B)
. A
(noncommutative) fibrewise compactification
of
\varphi
is a pair
(C,\psi)
consisting of a
C^{*}
-algebra
C
and
\psi\in\Morp(A,C)
such that

(NF1)

the algebra
B
is isomorphic to an ideal of
C
and if
\alpha_{C}\in\Mor(C,B)
is the induced morphism, then
\alpha_{C}\circ\psi=\varphi
; and

(NF2)

the image of the
*
-homomorphism
\overline{\psi}\colon\pim(\varphi)\to C/B
, given by
\overline{\psi}(a)=\psi(a)+B
, contains
\{c+B\colon c\in B^{\perp}\trianglelefteq C\}
.

If
\psi
is also injective, then we call

a
(noncommutative) perfection
. If
B
is essential in

, then we call
(C,\psi)
a
strict fibrewise compactification
.

Remark 3.7
.

Given
\varphi\in\Mor(A,B)
it is tempting to think that
(\mathcal{M}(B),\varphi)
is a fibrewise compactification of
\varphi
. Indeed,
\alpha_{\mathcal{M}(B)}=\id_{\mathcal{M}(B)}
and
B
is an essential ideal in
\mathcal{M}(B)
.
However, for
\varphi\in\Mor(A,B)
we typically have
\overline{\varphi(A)\mathcal{M}(B)}\neq\mathcal{M}(B)
, so
\varphi\notin\Morp(A,\mathcal{M}(B))
.
For example, suppose that

is nonunital and
\varphi\colon B\to\mathcal{M}(B)
is the inclusion of

as a proper ideal. Then,
\overline{\varphi(B)\mathcal{M}(B)}=B\neq\mathcal{M}(B)
.

Remark 3.8
.

To check that
(C,\psi)
is a
strict
fibrewise compactification we only need to verify that
(NF1)
holds and that
B^{\perp}=\{0\}
, since
(NF2)
holds automatically.

We record the following characterisation of the annihilator of an ideal.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
C
be a
C^{*}
-algebra, let
B\trianglelefteq C
, and let
\alpha_{C}\in\Mor(C,B)
be the induced morphism. Then
\ker(\alpha_{C})=B^{\perp}
.

Proof.

Let
B^{\prime}
denote the canonical copy of
B
in
\mathcal{M}(B)
and note that
\alpha_{C}
restricts to an isomorphism from
B
to
B^{\prime}
.
Fix
c\in\ker(\alpha_{C})
. For all
b\in B
we have
cb=\alpha_{C}^{-1}(\alpha_{C}(cb))=0
, and similarly
bc=0
, so
c\in B^{\perp}
. Now suppose that
c\in B^{\perp}
. Then for all
b^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}
we have
\alpha_{C}(c)b^{\prime}=\alpha_{C}(c\alpha_{C}^{-1}(b^{\prime}))=0
, so
c\in\ker(\alpha_{C})
.
∎

The next lemma shows that the condition
(NF2)
can be strengthened to an equality.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
A
and
B
be
C^{*}
-algebras and let
\varphi\in\Mor(A,B)
. Suppose that
(C,\psi)
is a fibrewise compactification of
\varphi
. Then

(a)

\overline{\psi}(\pim(\varphi))=\{c+B\colon c\in B^{\perp}\}
; and

(b)

\pim(\varphi)/\psi^{-1}(B)\cong B^{\perp}
.

Proof.

(a)
Let
q\colon C\to C/B
be the quotient map. Since
B\cap B^{\perp}=\{0\}
, the algebra
q(B^{\perp})=\{c+B\colon c\in B^{\perp}\}
is isomorphic to
B^{\perp}
. By
(NF2)
we have
\overline{\psi}(\pim(\varphi))\supseteq q(B^{\perp})
. Fix
a\in\pim(\varphi)
. By
(NF1)
we have
\alpha_{C}\circ\psi(a)=\phi(a)\in B\subseteq\mathcal{M}(B)
. Hence, there exists
b\in B
such that
\alpha_{C}(b-\psi(a))=0
. Since
\ker(\alpha_{C})=B^{\perp}
, there exists
b^{\prime}\in B^{\perp}
such that
\psi(a)=b+b^{\prime}\in B+B^{\perp}
. It follows that
\overline{\psi}(a)=b^{\prime}+B\in q(B^{\perp})
, so
\overline{\psi}(\pim(\varphi))=q(B^{\perp})
.

(b)
Observe that
\ker(\overline{\psi})=\psi^{-1}(B)\trianglelefteq\pim(\varphi)
. Since
\overline{\psi}
maps onto
q(B^{\perp})
, it induces an isomorphism
\pim(\varphi)/\psi^{-1}(B)\cong B^{\perp}
.
∎

Noncommutative fibrewise compactifications directly generalise fibrewise compactifications of continuous maps between locally compact Hausdorff spaces.
To see this we require the following lemma, known to experts.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
I
be an ideal of a
C^{*}
-algebra
A
.

(a)

The image of
I^{\perp}
in
A/I
is an essential ideal.

(b)

If
I
and
A/I
are commutative, then so is
A
.

(c)

If
I
and
I^{\perp}
are commutative, then so is
A
.

Proof.

(a)
Let
q\colon A\to A/I
denote the quotient map. If
a\in A
is such that
q(aI^{\perp})=0
, then
aI^{\perp}\subseteq I
, but this can only happen if
a=0
. So,
q(I^{\perp})
is essential in
A/I
.

(b)
Let
I
and
A/I
be commutative. Suppose for contradiction that
a,b\in A
are such that
[a,b]\neq 0
. Since
A/I
is commutative,
q([a,b])=0
, so
[a,b]\in I
. Fix an irreducible representation
\pi\colon I\to\mathbb{C}
such that
\pi([a,b])\neq 0
. Then
\pi
extends to an irreducible representation
\widetilde{\pi}\colon A\to\mathbb{C}
by
[
6
, Lemma I.9.14]
and
0\neq\widetilde{\pi}([a,b])=[\widetilde{\pi}(a),\widetilde{\pi}(b)]
, contradicting the commutativity of
\mathbb{C}
.

(c)
Suppose that
I
and
I^{\perp}
are commutative. Part
(a)
and the universal property of the multiplier algebra implies that
A/I
is a subalgebra of the commutative algebra
\mathcal{M}(q(I^{\perp}))
. So,
A/I
is commutative, and by
(b)
, so is
A
.
∎

Recall that the spectrum
\widehat{A}
of a commutative
C^{*}
-algebra
A
consists of all nonzero
*
-homomorphisms
A\to\mathbb{C}
with the weak* topology. If
B
is another commutative
C^{*}
-algebra and
\varphi\in\Mor(A,B)
, then Gelfand duality
[
12
, Theorem 2]
yields a continuous map
\varphi_{*}\colon\widehat{B}\to\widehat{A}
such that
(\varphi_{*})^{*}=\varphi
. The morphism
\varphi
is proper if and only if
\varphi_{*}
is proper.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a continuous map between locally compact Hausdorff spaces. If
(Z,g)
is a fibrewise compactification of
f
, then
(C_{0}(Z),g^{*})
is a fibrewise compactification of
f^{*}\in\Mor(C_{0}(Y),C_{0}(X))
. Conversely, if
(C,\psi)
is a fibrewise compactification of
f^{*}
, then
C
is commutative and
(\widehat{C},\psi_{*})
is a fibrewise compactification of
f
. The same is true when restricting to perfections.

Proof.

First suppose that
(Z,g)
is a fibrewise compactification of
f
. Let
A\coloneqq C_{0}(Y)
,
B\coloneqq C_{0}(X)
,
C\coloneqq C_{0}(Z)
, and
\varphi\coloneqq f^{*}\in\Mor(A,B)
.
Since
g
is proper,
Lemma

3.5
implies that
\psi\coloneqq g^{*}\in\Morp(A,C)
. Since
\iota_{X}\colon X\to Z
is open,
B
is isomorphic to an ideal of
C
. Let
\alpha_{C}\in\Mor(C,C_{0}(X))
be the induced
*
-homomorphism.
Using
(F1)
at the second equality we have

(\alpha_{C}\circ\psi)(a)(x)=a(g\circ\iota_{X}(x))=a(f(x))=\varphi(a)(x)

for all
a\in A
and
x\in X
.
So,
(NF1)
is satisfied.

For
(NF2)
, note that
C/B\cong C_{0}(Z\setminus\iota_{X}(X))
and

. By

3.5
, we have
\pim(\varphi)=C_{0}(\pr(f))
. For
a\in\pim(\varphi)
we have
\overline{\psi}(a)=(a\circ g)|_{Z\setminus\iota_{X}(X)}
. We use

to see that

restricts to a homeomorphism from

to a closed subset

of

in the subspace topology. Fix a compactly supported function

to a function

with

. That is,

is a fibrewise compactification of

. If

is surjective, then

is injective.

Now suppose that
(C,\psi)
is a fibrewise compactification of

, and

, there is an isomorphism

By

Proposition 3.33
.

Let
A
and
B
be
C^{*}
-algebras, let
\varphi\in\Mor(A,B)
, and let

be a
\varphi
-proper (
\varphi
-perfect)

Then

is a fibrewise compactification (perfection) of

. Moreover, every quotient fibrewise compactification (perfection) of

is isomorphic to

for some
\varphi
-proper (
\varphi
-perfect) ideal

.

Proof.

By

is an ideal in

. Let
\alpha\in\Mor({B}\uplus_{\varphi}^{J}{A},B)
be the induced morphism. Since
\varphi_{J}
is the composition of
{\varphi}_{\uplus}
with the quotient map
{B}\uplus_{\varphi}{A}\twoheadrightarrow{B}\uplus_{\varphi}^{J}{A}
, we have
\varphi_{J}\in\Morp(A,{B}\uplus_{\varphi}^{J}{A})
. For each

{\varphi}_{J}(a)(b,J)=(\varphi(a)b,J)
for all

. Hence,

, and so
({B}\uplus_{\varphi}^{J}{A},\varphi_{J})

For

, so
(0,a+J)\in B_{J}^{\perp}

(\varphi(a)-b,a+J)\in B_{J}^{\perp}

and

\varphi
.
If

\varphi
-perfect and

, then

, so

. Hence,
\varphi_{J}
is injective.

Now suppose that

is a quotient fibrewise compactification of

, with quotient map

. Let

denote the ideal of

be the induced morphism. Let

, so

. By

\varphi
-proper.

. Fix

As

By

. Since

Now, fix

-algebras in the usual sense.

Nontrivial noncommutative examples of generalised directed sequences appear in the theory of
C^{*}
-correspondences.

Example 3.47
.

As in
Example

3.25
, let
(\varphi,X_{A})
be a nondegenerate
A
–
A
-correspondence, and for each
i\geq 0
let
X_{A}^{\ox i}\coloneqq X\ox_{A}X\ox_{A}\cdots\ox_{A}X
be
i
-th tensor power of
X_{A}
, balanced over
A
. Let
\varphi_{0}\coloneqq\varphi
, and for
k\geq 1
let
\varphi_{i}\colon\mathcal{K}_{A}(X_{A}^{\ox i})\to\mathcal{L}_{A}(X_{A}^{\ox i+1})\cong\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{K}_{A}(X_{A}^{\ox i+1}))
be the unique
*
-homomorphism satisfying

\varphi_{i}(T)(x\ox y)=(Tx)\ox y

for all
x\in X_{A}^{\ox i}
and
y\in X_{A}
(see
[
17
, p. 369]
). Then
\varphi_{i}\in\Mor(\mathcal{K}_{A}(X_{A}^{\ox i}),\mathcal{K}_{A}(X_{A}^{\ox i+1}))
, so
(\mathcal{K}_{A}(X_{A}^{\ox i}),\varphi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a generalised directed sequence of
C^{*}
-algebras.

Unlike the case of locally compact Hausdorff spaces, where
\varprojlim(X_{i},f_{i})
makes sense as a topological space even if the
f_{i}
are not proper, there is no good corresponding notion (as far as the author is aware) of a direct limit of a generalised sequence of
C^{*}
-algebras. Instead, we are interested in taking more general types of limits, like we did in the topological setting.

Given a generalised directed sequence
(A_{i},\varphi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
the naive approach is to first extend each
\varphi_{i}\in\Mor(A_{i},A_{i+1})
to a unital
*
-homomorphism
\overline{\varphi_{i}}\colon\mathcal{M}(A_{i})\to\mathcal{M}(A_{i+1})
, and then take the usual direct limit of the resulting directed sequence of
C^{*}
-algebras

A_{0}\overset{\varphi_{0}}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{M}(A_{1})\overset{\overline{\varphi_{1}}}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{M}(A_{2})\overset{\overline{\varphi_{2}}}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{M}(A_{3})\longrightarrow\cdots.

We denote this limit by
\mlima(A_{i},\varphi_{i})
. Since
A_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(A_{i})
, each of the algebras
A_{i}
map into
\mlima(A_{i},\varphi_{i})
, and the limit is compatible with the
\varphi_{i}
. The drawback of this approach is that the limit is typically very large: not only does each
A_{i}
map into
\mlima(A_{i},\varphi_{i})
, but so does
\mathcal{M}(A_{i})
for all
i\geq 1
. For example, if each
A_{i}
is separable, then
\mlima(A_{i},\varphi_{i})
is typically nonseperable.

Instead we seek a smaller notion of limit. More specifically, we insist that the maps from each
A_{i}
into the limit in are proper morphisms.

Lemma 3.48
.

Let
(A_{i},\varphi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a
directed sequence of
C^{*}
-algebras, and let
\mu_{k}\colon A_{k}\to\varinjlim(A_{i},\varphi_{i})
denote the universal
*
-homomorphisms. If each
\varphi_{k}\in\Morp(A_{k},A_{k+1})
, then each
\mu_{k}\in\Morp(A_{k},\varinjlim(A_{i},\varphi_{i}))
.

Proof.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
(a_{\lambda})
be an approximate unit for
A_{k}
with
\|a_{\lambda}\|\leq 1
. Fix
b\in\varinjlim(A_{i},\varphi_{i})
and
\varepsilon>0
. Since
\varinjlim(A_{i},\varphi_{i})=\overline{\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}(A_{i})}
, there exists
n\geq k
and
b_{n}\in A_{n}
such that
\|\mu_{n}(b_{n})-b\|<\varepsilon/3
. Since each
\varphi_{i}
is proper, there exists
\lambda_{0}
such that for
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
we have
\|\varphi_{n-1}\circ\cdots\circ\varphi_{k}(a_{\lambda})b_{n}-b_{n}\|<\varepsilon/3
. Hence, for
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
we have

\displaystyle\|\mu_{k}(a_{\lambda})b-b\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mu_{k}(a_{\lambda})b-\mu_{k}(a_{\lambda})b_{n}\|+\|\mu_{n}(\varphi_{n-1}\circ\cdots\circ\varphi_{k}(a_{\lambda})b_{n})-\mu_{n}(b_{n})\|

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt+\|\mu_{n}(b_{n})-b\|<\varepsilon.

So,
\mu_{k}
is a proper morphism.
∎

Using fibrewise compactifications we can inductively extend a generalised directed sequence of
C^{*}
-algebras to one with proper morphisms, and
Lemma

3.48
implies that the universal maps into the limit will be proper. For the unified algebra construction we get the following notion of a limit.

Definition 3.49
.

Let
(A_{i},\varphi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a generalised directed sequence of
C^{*}
-algebras. Let
\widetilde{A}_{0}\coloneqq A_{0}
. Working inductively, for each
i\geq 1
define

\widetilde{A}_{i}\coloneqq{A_{i}}\uplus_{\overline{\varphi}_{i-1}\circ\alpha_{A_{i-1}}}{\widetilde{A}_{i-1}},

let
\alpha_{i}\in\Mor(\widetilde{A}_{i},A_{i})
be induced by
A_{i}\trianglelefteq\widetilde{A}_{i}
, let
\overline{\varphi}_{i}\colon\mathcal{M}(\widetilde{A}_{i})\to\mathcal{M}(A_{i+1})
be the unique unital map induced by
\varphi_{i}
, and let

\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}\colon\widetilde{A}_{i}\to\widetilde{A}_{i+1}
be given by
\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}(a)\coloneqq{(\overline{\varphi}_{i}\circ\alpha_{A_{i}})}_{\uplus}
.
We call the
C^{*}
-algebra
\unilima(A_{i},\varphi_{i})\coloneqq\varinjlim(\widetilde{A}_{i},\widetilde{\varphi}_{i})
the
unified limit
of
(A_{i},\varphi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
.

By
Proposition

3.20
, the
\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}
are injective and belong to
\Morp(\widetilde{A}_{i},\widetilde{A}_{i+1})
. Explicitly, they satisfy
\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}(a)=(\overline{\varphi}_{i}\circ\alpha_{A_{i}}(a),a)
for all
a\in\widetilde{A}_{i}
. Extending the notation established in
Lemma

2.27
, we write

\widetilde{A}_{i}\eqqcolon{A_{i}}\uplus_{\varphi_{i-1}}{{A_{i-1}}\uplus_{\varphi_{i-2}}{{\cdots}\uplus_{\varphi_{0}}{A_{0}}}}.

By inductively applying
Lemma

3.42
, we may identify
\widetilde{A}_{i}
with

\displaystyle\big\{(a_{i},\ldots,a_{1},a_{0})\in\mathcal{M}(A_{i})\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{M}(A_{1})\oplus A_{0}\bigm|

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\varphi_{0}(a_{0})-a_{1}\in A_{1}\text{ and }\overline{\varphi}_{k}(a_{k})-a_{k+1}\in A_{k+1}\text{ for all }1\leq k<i\big\}.

Under this identification, the map
\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}
satisfies

(23)

\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}(a_{i},\ldots,a_{0})=(\overline{\varphi}_{i}\circ\alpha_{A_{i}}(a_{i},\ldots,a_{0}),(a_{i},\ldots,a_{0}))=(\overline{\varphi}_{i}(a_{i}),a_{i},\ldots,a_{0}).

We record some properties of the unified limit.

Proposition 3.50
.

Let
(A_{i},\varphi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a generalised directed sequence of
C^{*}
-algebras.

(a)

The universal maps
\mu_{k}\colon\widetilde{A}_{k}\to\unilima(A_{i},\varphi_{i})
belong to
\Morp(\widetilde{A}_{k},\unilima(A_{i},\varphi_{i}))
and are injective.

(b)

The maps
\alpha_{A_{i}}\in\Mor(\widetilde{A}_{i},A_{i})
, induced by the ideal
A_{i}\trianglelefteq\widetilde{A}_{i}
, induce a
*
-homomorphism
\unilima(A_{i},\varphi_{i})\to\mlima(A_{i},\varphi_{i})
.

(c)

If
\mathcal{P}
is a property of
C^{*}
-algebras that is closed under extensions and countable direct limits (e.g. separability, nuclearity), and
A_{i}
satisfies
\mathcal{P}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, then
\unilima(A_{i},\varphi_{i})
also satisfies
\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

(a)
That the
\mu_{k}
are injective follows from the
\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}
being injective for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
Lemma

3.48
implies that each
\mu_{k}\in\Morp(\widetilde{A}_{k},\unilima(A_{i},\varphi_{i}))
.

(b)

Using
23
we have
\alpha_{A_{i+1}}\circ\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}=\overline{\varphi}_{i}\circ\alpha_{A_{i}}
for all
i\geq 0
. The existence of a
*
-homomorphism
\unilima(A_{i},\varphi_{i})\to\mlima(A_{i},\varphi_{i})
now follows from the universal property of direct limits (cf.
Lemma

2.30
).

(c)
This follows inductively from the fact that, as a unified algebra, each
\widetilde{A}_{i+1}
is an extension of
\widetilde{A}_{i}
by
A_{i+1}
.
∎

By considering quotient fibrewise compactification of the unified algebra, we can also construct smaller limits of a generalised directed sequence.

Definition 3.51
.

A
regulating sequence
for a generalised directed sequence
(A_{i},\varphi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of
C^{*}
-algebras is a collection
\mathcal{J}=(J_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of ideals
J_{i}\trianglelefteq A_{i}
such that each
J_{i}
is
\varphi_{i}
-proper. If each
J_{i}
is
\varphi_{i}
-perfect, then we call
\mathcal{J}
a
perfect
regulating sequence.

Let
A^{\mathcal{J}}_{0}\coloneqq A_{0}
.
For
i\geq 1
, inductively define

A^{\mathcal{J}}_{i}\coloneqq{A_{i}}\uplus_{\langle{\varphi_{i-1}}\rangle}^{q_{i-1}(J_{i-1}\oplus 0)}{A^{\mathcal{J}}_{i-1}},

where
\langle{\varphi_{i-1}}\rangle\in\Mor(A^{\mathcal{J}}_{i-1},A_{i})
is the map defined in
Lemma

3.43
, and
q_{i}\colon{A_{i}}\uplus_{\langle{\varphi_{i-1}}\rangle}{A_{i-1}^{\mathcal{J}}}\to A^{\mathcal{J}}_{i}
is the quotient map. For
i\geq 0
, let
\varphi^{\mathcal{J}}_{i}\colon A_{i}^{\mathcal{J}}\to A_{i+1}^{\mathcal{J}}
be given by
\varphi^{\mathcal{J}}_{i}\coloneqq\langle{\varphi}\rangle_{q_{i}(J_{i}\oplus 0)}
. We call the
C^{*}
-algebra
\varinjlim^{\mathcal{J}}(A_{i},\varphi_{i})\coloneqq\varinjlim(A^{\mathcal{J}}_{i},\varphi^{\mathcal{J}}_{i})
the
\mathcal{J}
-regulated limit
of
(A_{i},\varphi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
.

By
Proposition

3.33
, the
\varphi^{\mathcal{J}}_{i}
belong to
\Morp(\widetilde{A}_{i},\widetilde{A}_{i+1})
, and are injective if
\mathcal{J}
is perfect.
Extending the notation established in
Lemma

3.43
, we write

A^{\mathcal{J}}_{i}\eqqcolon{A_{i}}\uplus_{\varphi_{i-1}}^{J_{i-1}}{{A_{i-1}}\uplus_{\varphi_{i-2}}^{J_{i-2}}{{\cdots}\uplus_{\varphi_{0}}^{J_{0}}{A_{0}}}}.

By
Lemma

3.43
, we may identify
A_{i}^{\mathcal{J}}
with

\displaystyle\widetilde{A}_{i}/(0\oplus J_{i-1}\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{0})

\displaystyle=\Big\{(a_{i},a_{i-1}+J_{i-1},\ldots,a_{1}+J_{1},a_{0}+J_{0})\in\mathcal{M}(A_{i})\oplus\frac{\mathcal{M}(A_{i-1})}{J_{i-1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\frac{\mathcal{M}(A_{1})}{J_{1}}\oplus\frac{A_{0}}{J_{0}}\Bigm|

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\varphi_{0}(a_{0})-a_{1}\in A_{1}\text{ and }\overline{\varphi}_{k}(a_{k})-a_{k+1}\in A_{k+1}\text{ for all }1\leq k<i\Big\}.

Under this identification, the maps
\varphi^{\mathcal{J}}_{i}
satisfy

(24)

\varphi^{\mathcal{J}}_{i}(a_{i},a_{i-1}+J_{i-1},\ldots,a_{0}+J_{0})=(\overline{\varphi}_{i}(a_{i}),a_{i}+J_{i},\ldots,a_{0}+J_{0}).

We record the following properties of the
\mathcal{J}
-regulated limit.

Corollary 3.52
.

Let
(A_{i},\varphi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a generalised directed sequence of
C^{*}
-algebras with a regulating sequence
\mathcal{J}=(J_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
.

(a)

The universal maps
\mu_{k}\colon A^{\mathcal{J}}_{k}\to\varinjlim^{\mathcal{J}}(A_{i},\varphi_{i})
belong to
\Morp(A^{\mathcal{J}}_{k},\varinjlim^{\mathcal{J}}(A_{i},\varphi_{i}))
, and are injective if
\mathcal{J}
is perfect.

(b)

The maps
\alpha_{A_{i}}\in\Mor(A^{\mathcal{J}}_{i},\mathcal{M}(A_{i}))
, induced by the ideal
A_{i}\trianglelefteq A^{\mathcal{J}}_{i}
, induce a
*
-homomorphism
\varinjlim^{\mathcal{J}}(A_{i},\varphi_{i})\to\mlima(A_{i},\varphi_{i})
.

(c)

If
\mathcal{P}
is a property of
C^{*}
-algebras that is closed under taking quotients, ideals, extensions, and countable direct limits (e.g. separability, nuclearity); and
A_{i}
satisfies
\mathcal{P}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, then
\varprojlim^{\mathcal{J}}(A_{i},\varphi_{i})
satisfies
\mathcal{P}
.

(d)

Let
\mathcal{I}=(I_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be another regulating sequence for
(A_{i},\varphi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
. If
J_{i}\trianglelefteq I_{i}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, then there is a surjection
\varinjlim^{\mathcal{J}}(A_{i},\varphi_{i})\twoheadrightarrow\varinjlim^{\mathcal{I}}(A_{i},\varphi_{i})
.

Proof.

Parts
(a)
,
(b)
, and
(c)
follow from arguments analogous to those found in the proof of
Proposition

3.50
.

For
(d)
, observe that there are quotient maps

\pi_{i}\colon A_{i}^{\mathcal{J}}\cong\frac{\widetilde{A}_{i}}{0\oplus J_{i-1}\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{0}}\to\frac{\widetilde{A}_{i}}{0\oplus I_{i-1}\oplus\cdots\oplus I_{0}}\cong A_{i}^{\mathcal{I}}.

By
24
, we have
\pi_{i+1}\circ\varphi^{\mathcal{J}}_{i}=\pi_{i}\circ\varphi^{\mathcal{J}}_{i+1}
. If
\mu_{k}^{\mathcal{J}}\colon A_{k}^{\mathcal{J}}\to\varinjlim^{\mathcal{J}}(A_{i},\varphi_{i})
and
\mu_{k}^{\mathcal{I}}\colon A_{k}^{\mathcal{J}}\to\varinjlim^{\mathcal{J}}(A_{i},\varphi_{i})
are the universal maps, then the universal property of direct limits gives a
*
-homomorphism
\pi\colon\varinjlim^{\mathcal{J}}(A_{i},\varphi_{i})\to\varinjlim^{\mathcal{I}}(A_{i},\varphi_{i})
such that
\pi\circ\mu_{k}^{\mathcal{J}}=\mu_{k}^{\mathcal{I}}\circ\pi_{k}
for all
k\geq 0
. Since the union of the images of the
\mu_{k}^{\mathcal{I}}
is dense in
\varinjlim^{\mathcal{I}}(A_{i},\varphi_{i})
, and the
\pi_{k}
are surjective,
\pi
is surjective.
∎

We have the following consequence of
Corollary

3.34
.

Corollary 3.53
.

Let
(X_{i},f_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be an inverse sequence of locally compact Hausdorff spaces and continuous maps with a regulating sequence

\mathcal{U}^{*}\coloneqq(C_{0}(U_{i}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a regulating sequence for the generalised directed sequence
(C_{0}(X_{i}),f_{i}^{*})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of

-algebras. Moreover,
C_{0}(\varprojlim^{\mathcal{U}}(X_{i},f_{i}))\cong\varinjlim^{\mathcal{U}^{*}}(C_{0}(X_{i}),f_{i}^{*})
.

We highlight two special cases of regulated limits.

Definition 3.54
.

Let
(A_{i},\varphi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

-algebras.

(a)

If
\mathcal{J}=(\pim(\varphi_{i}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, then we call
\minlima(A_{i},\varphi_{i})\coloneqq\varinjlim^{\mathcal{J}}(A_{i},\varphi_{i})
the

(b)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mlima(A_{i},\varphi_{i})}}

3.6.
Cores of Cuntz–Pimsner algebras as regulated limits

Let
(\varphi,X_{A})
be an
A
–
A
-correspondence as in
Example

3.3
.
In this section, we explicitly compute regulated limits of the generalised directed sequence
(\mathcal{K}_{A}(X_{A}^{\ox i}),\varphi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of

Example

3.47
in terms of relative Cuntz–Pimsner algebras.

Fix a
\varphi
-proper ideal
J\trianglelefteq A
. Let

\tau^{J}=(\tau_{A}^{J},\tau_{X}^{J})
denote a universal
J
-covariant representation of
(\varphi,X_{A})
in the
J
-relative Cuntz–Pimsner algebra
\mathcal{O}_{X,J}
. By the universal property of
\mathcal{O}_{X,J}
, there is a strongly continuous action
\gamma\colon\mathbb{T}\to\Aut(\mathcal{O}_{X,J})
, called the

, satisfying

\gamma_{z}(\tau_{A}^{J}(a))=\tau_{A}^{J}(a)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\gamma_{z}(\tau_{X}^{J}(x))=z\tau_{X}^{J}(x)

for all

and
x\in X_{A}
. The fixed-point algebra
\mathcal{O}_{X,J}^{\gamma}
of

is called the

of
\mathcal{O}_{X,J}
. In
[
17

it is shown that
\mathcal{O}_{X,J}^{\gamma}
can be expressed as a direct limit of

-algebras. In this section, we show that
\mathcal{O}_{X,J}^{\gamma}
is in fact a regulated limit of
(\mathcal{K}_{A}(X_{A}^{\ox i}),\varphi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
.

We first characterise
\pim(\varphi_{i})
and
\kat(\varphi_{i})
. To do this we establish some additional terminology and results about
C^{*}
-correspondences.
Let
X_{A}
be a right Hilbert
A
-module. By
[
18
, Corollary 1.4]
, if
I\trianglelefteq A
, then
XI\coloneqq X\cdot I=\{x\cdot a\mid x\in X,a\in I\}
is an
A
–
A
-correspondence with the operations induced from
X_{A}
.
By
[
10
, Lemma 2.3]
, there is an inclusion
\iota_{I}\colon\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)\to\mathcal{K}_{A}(X)
as an ideal such that
\iota_{I}(\Theta_{x,y}^{XI})=\Theta_{x,y}^{X}
for all
x,y\in XI
. Moreover, for
T\in\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)
the operator
\iota_{I}(T)
is the unique extension of
T
to
\mathcal{L}_{A}(X)
whose range is contained in
XI
. As such we identify
\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)
as an ideal in
\mathcal{K}_{A}(X)
.

The following characterisation of
\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)
is known to experts.

Lemma 3.55
.

Let
X
be a right Hilbert
A
-module and let
I\trianglelefteq A
.
Then

(25)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)&=\overline{\spaan}\{\Theta_{\xi,\eta\cdot a}\mid\xi,\eta\in X,a\in I\}\\
&=\{T\in\mathcal{K}_{A}(X)\mid\langle x|Ty\rangle_{A}\in I\text{ for all }x,y\in X\}.\end{split}

Proof.

For the first equality of
25
, observe that
\Theta_{\xi\cdot a,\eta\cdot b}=\Theta_{\xi,\eta\cdot a^{*}b}
for all
x,y\in X
and
a,b\in I
. So
\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)\subseteq\overline{\spaan}\{\Theta_{\xi,\eta\cdot a}\mid\xi,\eta\in X,a\in I\}
. For the reverse inclusion fix
\xi,\eta\in X
and
a\in A
. Since positive elements span
A
, we can suppose that
a
is positive. Then
\Theta_{\xi,\eta\cdot a}=\Theta_{\xi\cdot a^{1/2},\eta\cdot a^{1/2}}\in\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)
.

For the second equality of
25
, fix
\xi,\eta\in X
and
a\in I
and consider
\Theta_{\xi,\eta\cdot a}\in\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)
. For all
x,y\in X
, we have

\langle x|\Theta_{\xi\cdot,\eta\cdot a}y\rangle_{A}=\langle x|\xi\cdot\langle\eta\cdot a|y\rangle_{A}\rangle_{A}=\langle x|\xi\rangle_{A}a^{*}\langle\eta|y\rangle_{A}\in I.

Since the
\Theta_{\xi,\eta\cdot a}
densely span
\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)
, if
T\in\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)
, then
\langle x|Ty\rangle_{A}\in I
for all
x,y\in X
.

On the other hand, suppose that
T\in\mathcal{K}_{A}(X)
and
\langle x|Ty\rangle_{A}\in I
for all
x,y\in X
.
By
[
18
, Proposition 1.3]
, we have
Ty\in XI
for all
y\in X
.
Choose an increasing approximate unit
(\sum_{(x,y)\in F_{\lambda}}\Theta_{x,y})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
for
\mathcal{K}_{A}(X)
, where for each
\lambda\in\Lambda
,
F_{\lambda}
is a finite subset of
X\times X
. In particular, we have

T=\lim_{\lambda}\sum_{(x,y)\in F_{\lambda}}\Theta_{Tx,y}
in the norm on
\mathcal{K}_{A}(X)
. Since each
Tx\in XI
, by the first equality of
25
, we have
\Theta_{Tx,y}\in\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)
. The ideal
\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)
is closed in
\mathcal{K}_{A}(X)
, so
T\in\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)
.
∎

Let
X_{A}
be a right Hilbert
A
-module. We write
\langle X|X\rangle\coloneqq\overline{\spaan}\{\langle x|y\rangle_{A}\colon x,y\in X\}
. We say
X_{A}
is

if
\langle X\mid X\rangle=A
. If
X_{A}
is not full, then
\langle X|X\rangle
is a proper ideal of

. The module
X_{A}
provides a Morita equivalence between
\mathcal{K}_{A}(X)
and
\langle X|X\rangle
(cf.
[
26
, Proposition 3.8]
). As such, every ideal of
\mathcal{K}_{A}(X)
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)
for some unique ideal
I\trianglelefteq\langle X|X\rangle
. Moreover, the bijection
I\leftrightarrow\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)
is an isomorphism between the ideal lattices of
\langle X|X\rangle
and
\mathcal{K}_{A}(X)

[
26
, Proposition 3.24]
.

For a non-full Hilbert module
X_{A}
we can have
\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)=\mathcal{K}_{A}(XJ)
for distinct ideals
I,J\trianglelefteq A
.

Lemma 3.56
.

Let
X_{A}
be a right Hilbert
A
-module. Then for every ideal
I\trianglelefteq A
we have
\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)=\mathcal{K}_{A}(X\cdot(I\cap\langle X|X\rangle))
and
\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)^{\perp}=\mathcal{K}_{A}(X\cdot(I\cap\langle X|X\rangle)^{\perp})
.

Proof.

Since
I\cap\langle X|X\rangle\trianglelefteq I
, we have
\mathcal{K}_{A}(X\cdot(I\cap\langle X|X\rangle))\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)
. For the reverse inclusion, fix
\xi,\eta\in X
and
a\in I
. By
Lemma

3.55
,
\Theta_{\xi,\eta\cdot a}\in\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)
. For each
y\in X
, we have
\Theta_{\xi,\eta\cdot a}y=\xi\cdot a^{*}\langle\eta|y\rangle_{A}
. Since
a^{*}\langle\eta|y\rangle_{A}\in I\cap\langle X\mid X\rangle
, we have
\langle x|\Theta_{\xi,\eta\cdot a}y\rangle_{A}\in I\cap\langle X|X\rangle
for all
x,y\in X
, so by
Lemma

3.55
, we have
\Theta_{\xi,\eta\cdot a}\in\mathcal{K}_{A}(X\cdot(I\cap\langle X|X\rangle))
. Since the
\Theta_{\xi,\eta\cdot a}
densely span
\mathcal{K}_{A}(X\cdot(I\cap\langle X|X\rangle))
, we have
\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)=\mathcal{K}_{A}(X\cdot(I\cap\langle X|X\rangle))
.

Now suppose that
\xi,\eta\in X
and
a\in(I\cap\langle X|X\rangle)^{\perp}
. Fix
S\in\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)
. Using
Lemma

3.55
, we have

\Theta_{\xi,\eta\cdot a}Sx=\xi\cdot a^{*}\langle\eta|Sx\rangle_{A}=0

for all
x\in X_{A}
. Using
Lemma

3.55
again, for all
x,y\in X_{A}
we have

\langle x|S\Theta_{\xi,\eta\cdot a}y\rangle_{A}=\langle x|S\xi\rangle_{A}a^{*}\langle\eta|y\rangle_{A}=0,

so
S\Theta_{\xi,\eta\cdot a}=0
. Hence,
\Theta_{\xi,\eta\cdot a}\in\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)^{\perp}
. Since such
\Theta_{\xi,\eta\cdot a}
densely span
\mathcal{K}_{A}(X\cdot(I\cap\langle X|X\rangle)^{\perp})
we have
\mathcal{K}_{A}(X\cdot(I\cap\langle X|X\rangle)^{\perp})\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)^{\perp}
.

Now suppose that
T\in\mathcal{K}(XI)^{\perp}
. Fix
a\in I\cap\langle X|X\rangle
. Then there exists
\xi,\eta\in XI
such that
\langle\xi|\eta\rangle_{A}=a
. For each
x,y\in X_{A}
, we have

\langle x|Ty\rangle_{A}a=\langle x|Ty\cdot\langle\xi|\eta\rangle_{A}\rangle_{A}=\langle x|T\Theta_{y,\xi}\eta\rangle_{A}=0

since
\Theta_{y,\xi}\in\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)
, and

a\langle x|Ty\rangle_{A}=\langle x\cdot\langle\eta|\xi\rangle_{A}\mid Ty\rangle_{A}=\langle\Theta_{x,\eta}\xi|Ty\rangle_{A}=\langle\xi|\Theta^{*}_{x,\eta}Ty\rangle_{A}=0

since
\Theta_{x,\eta}\in\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)
. Hence,
\langle x|Ty\rangle_{A}\in(I\cap\langle X|X\rangle)^{\perp}
for all
x,y\in X_{A}
, so
T\in\mathcal{K}_{A}(X\cdot(I\cap\langle X|X\rangle)^{\perp}).

∎

Lemma

3.56
indicates that if a Hilbert module
X_{A}
is not full, then the map taking an ideal
I\trianglelefteq A
to
\mathcal{K}_{A}(XI)
does not behave nicely with respect to annihilators. This makes a description of the Katsura ideals of the maps
\varphi_{i}
from
Example

3.47
difficult. As such, we restrict our attention to full modules.

Proposition 3.57
.

Let
X_{A}
be a full right Hilbert
A
-module and let
(\psi,Y_{B})
be a nondegenerate
A
–
B
-correspondence. Consider the morphism
\lambda\in\Mor(\mathcal{K}_{A}(X),\mathcal{K}_{B}(X\ox Y))
satisfying
\lambda(T)(x\ox y)=(Tx)\ox y
for all
T\in\mathcal{K}_{A}(X)
and
x\ox y\in X\ox_{A}Y
(see
[
25
, p. 190]
). Then

(a)

\pim(\lambda)=\mathcal{K}_{A}(X\cdot\pim(\psi))
;

(b)

\ker(\lambda)=\mathcal{K}_{A}(X\cdot\ker(\psi))
;

(c)

\ker(\lambda)^{\perp}=\mathcal{K}_{A}(X\cdot\ker(\psi)^{\perp})
; and

(d)

\kat(\lambda)=\mathcal{K}_{A}(X\cdot\kat(\psi)).

Moreover, the Morita equivalence between
A
and
\mathcal{K}_{A}(X)
, induced by
X_{A}
, restricts to an isomorphism between the sublattice of
\psi
-proper (
\psi
-perfect) ideals and the sublattice of
\lambda
-proper (
\lambda
-perfect) ideals.

Proof.

(a)
This follows directly from
[
25
, Corollary 3.7]
.

(b)
Observe that
T\in\ker(\lambda)
if and only if

0=\langle x_{1}\ox y_{1}|Tx_{2}\ox y_{2}\rangle_{B}=\langle y_{1}|\psi(\langle x_{1}|Tx_{2}\rangle_{A})y_{2}\rangle_{B}

for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in X_{A}
and
y_{1},y_{2}\in Y_{B}
. This is true if and only if
\langle x_{1}|Tx_{2}\rangle_{A}\in\ker(\psi)
for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in X_{A}
. By
Lemma

3.55
, this is equivalent to
T\in\mathcal{K}_{A}(X\cdot\ker(\psi))
.

(c)
Since
X_{A}
is full, this follows from
(b)
and
Lemma

3.56
.

(d)
Since the Morita equivalence induced by
X_{A}
is an isomorphism of the lattice of ideals, we have

\kat(\lambda)=\mathcal{K}_{A}(X\cdot\pim(\psi))\cap\mathcal{K}_{A}(X\cdot\ker(\psi)^{\perp})=\mathcal{K}_{A}(X\cdot(\pim(\psi)\cap\ker(\psi)^{\perp}))=\mathcal{K}_{A}(X\cdot\kat(\psi)).

The final statement now follows from
(a)
,
(d)
, and
Lemma

3.29
.
