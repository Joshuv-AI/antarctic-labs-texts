Two-stage surgical intervention for a rare case of intersected nasoethmoidal encephalocele and open-lip schizencephaly in an infant: illustrative case
Authors: Silvia Suarez-Monsalve, Santiago Campos-Fajardo, Raul Ramirez-Grueso, Karen Lorena Jacomussi-Alzate, Julie Franco Rodriguez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDSchizencephaly and encephaloceles are rare developmental birth defects, with the former involving abnormal clefts in the cerebral hemispheres connected to the ventricular system and the latter involving a neural tube defect characterized by the protrusion of brain tissue through an abnormal skull opening. These conditions are individually uncommon, and their simultaneous occurrence in a single patient is exceedingly unusual.OBSERVATIONSThis case report explores the intersection of these two rare congenital malformations in a 2-month-old female patient from an indigenous community in a rural area of Colombia. She presented with open-lip schizencephaly accompanied by a nasoethmoidal encephalocele and additional brain abnormalities, including septum pellucidum agenesis, corpus callosum dysgenesis, and a suspected bilateral optic nerve hypoplasia.LESSONSManagement involved a two-step surgical procedure, addressing hydrocephalus with a ventriculoperitoneal shunt and the encephalocele with posterior excision, thereby facilitating optimal cortical gray matter development and enhancing neurological outcomes. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE24259 Keywords: schizencephaly, encephalocele, congenital malformation, pediatric neurosurgeryABBREVIATIONS: CSF = cerebrospinal fluid; MRI = magnetic resonance imaging; STORCH = syphilis, toxoplasmosis, rubella, cytomegalovirus, and herpes simplex virus.

Congenital anomalies of the nervous system always present a distinct clinical and surgical challenge, requiring a comprehensive multidisciplinary approach for a correct diagnosis and management. Schizencephaly, a rare congenital malformation, is characterized by the presence of abnormal clefts lined with cortical gray matter within the cerebral hemispheres that communicate with the ventricular system. Schizencephaly is a result of a disruption and posterior abnormal neuronal migration during early fetal development. It is associated with infection, trauma, teratogens, and genetic mutations including chromosomal aneuploidy and EMX2 mutations. Autosomal recessive inheritance has been proposed, as schizencephaly has been observed in siblings.1, 2 This condition often coexists with other neurological abnormalities, contributing to a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations. Griffiths proposed a classification for schizencephaly, which is as follows: type 1 refers to transmantle heterotopia, type 2 is closed-lip schizencephaly, and type 3 is open-lip schizencephaly.3

Encephaloceles are also rare congenital anomalies representing a neural tube defect, where an abnormal opening of the skull results in the protrusion of brain tissue. This protrusion can occur in various locations. Its severity ranges from a mild skull defect with minimal neural involvement to more complex manifestations that involve craniofacial structures.4 Research suggests an incidence of 1 case of encephalocele in every 10,500 newborns5 in the United States. However, there is a lack of information regarding the prevalence and incidence in South America, particularly in Colombia. The etiology of encephaloceles remains poorly understood, but it is proposed to be multifactorial, often involving an intricate combination of both environmental and genetic factors.6

The intersection of both schizencephaly and encephalocele is a rare occurrence. To our knowledge, few reported cases exist in the medical literature,6–10 adding layers of complexity to the clinical presentation and management of affected individuals. In this case report, we present the scenario of a 2-month-old female with both an open-lip schizencephaly and a nasoethmoidal encephalocele. The case underscores the importance of thoroughly understanding these conditions to guide effective pediatric neurosurgical interventions and optimize outcomes for patients facing these rare challenges.

Illustrative Case

Initial Assessment

A 2-month-old female patient hailed from an indigenous community in a rural area of Colombia. The patient’s mother had no prenatal care, no syphilis, toxoplasmosis, rubella, cytomegalovirus, and herpes simplex virus (STORCH) infection profile, and no ultrasound images before delivery. It is worth noting that the mother experienced two previous spontaneous abortions before this pregnancy. The patient was born at full term with a weight of 2500 g. At the moment of birth, attending physicians noted a soft mass in the right nasal area of approximately 40 × 40 mm, so the patient was referred to the local department hospital for a more specialized assessment. The aforementioned mass was not associated with any signs of infection.

Initial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI; Fig. 1) revealed a right nasoethmoidal mass measuring 24.7 × 25.5 mm and featuring left supratentorial extra-axial communication with displacement of the left cerebral parenchyma and extensive occupation of the left hemicranium. Additionally, there was displacement of the third ventricle, and the septum pellucidum was not identified. Orbits, mastoids, and the posterior cranial base showed no abnormalities. Notably, an anterior skull base defect with paramedian extension and involvement of the cribriform plate with passage into the subcutaneous tissue lateral to the nasal pyramid was evident.

A: Axial T2-weighted MRI showing a left frontal open-lip schizencephaly associated with septum pellucidum agenesis and corpus callosum dysgenesis. B: Axial T2-weighted MRI showing an extended schizencephaly defect as well as bilateral optic nerve hypoplasia. C: Axial T2-weighted MRI of a right-sided nasoethmoidal defect. D: Sagittal T1-weighted MRI of the nasoethmoidal defect connected with the frontal lobe and with ectopic cerebral tissue, compatible with an encephalocele.

These radiological findings led to the diagnosis of a right anterior ethmoidal encephalocele, an open anterior left frontal schizencephaly (type 3), hydrocephalus, probable agenesis of the septum pellucidum, dysgenesis of the corpus callosum, and suspicion of bilateral optic nerve hypoplasia. Given the diagnosis of the described encephalocele and schizencephaly, the patient was referred to our service for surgical management.

Upon arrival, the patient was evaluated by the neurosurgery, pediatric neurology, and maxillofacial surgery teams. At initial examination, the patient was active and reactive, with an isochoric and centrally directed gaze. Facial gesturing was symmetric, no focal motor deficits were observed, and there were no signs of meningeal or cerebellar involvement. Remarkably, the patient had no history of seizures. After ruling out STORCH infections, the patient was considered to have a disorder of neuronal migration. A thorough multidisciplinary assessment was undertaken, and surgical management was decided. Our surgical group determined that it was necessary to conduct a two-step surgery by inserting a ventriculoperitoneal shunt for the management of hydrocephalus and performing posterior excision of the aforementioned encephalocele.

Surgery and Perioperative Process

Cranial Approach for Ventriculoperitoneal Shunt

The patient was prepared for surgery, general anesthesia was administered, and the patient was placed in the supine decubitus position with a left 30° rotation (Fig. 2). Aseptic and antiseptic procedures were followed, including the placement of antimicrobial incise drapes. A ventriculoperitoneal shunt was placed and inserted through the right Kocher point and right abdominal flank without complications. A Codman Hakim pediatric valve was placed and programmed at 90 mm H2O. The ventricular catheter revealed clear cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) exiting distally. A sample was sent for cytochemical analysis. Wound closure was done in layers with Vicryl and Monocryl (Ethicon Inc.), followed by a hydrogen peroxide wash.

Illustration of the surgical incision in our patient. The red line corresponds to a normal Weber-Ferguson incision. The blue dotted line corresponds to our modified approach for excision of the encephalocele.

Nasoethmoidal Encephalocele Resection

Positioning for the second stage of the surgery was in the supine decubitus position with the head placed in a neutral head position. Aseptic and antiseptic measures were repeated. A modified Weber-Ferguson incision (Fig. 2) was performed with a scalpel, achieving posterior hemostasis with monopolar cautery. The ethmoidal encephalocele was exposed and dissected in all planes until visualizing nasal, maxillary, and orbital bony margins.

The encephalocele was ligated at its base and cut. In collaboration with maxillofacial surgery, a plate was placed and secured with screws to cover the preexisting bony defect. This completed the correction of the meningocele through cranioplasty and the correction of cranial asymmetry. Hemostasis was ensured with absorbable hemostatic material and cottonoids. Mild epidural bleeding was observed during closure, and fibrin sealant was used to adhere to an autologous fat graft to minimize the risk of a CSF fistula. The cavity was irrigated, and closure was initiated in layers. Figure 3 features the sequence of events in the operating room.

C: Cosmetic results in the immediate postoperative period with a freshly sutured wound. D: The surgically excised lesion.

Perioperative Management

Immediate postoperative care was managed in the intensive care unit. Antibiotic prophylaxis was considered given the type of lesion and surgical site, so the patient received ampicillin/sulbactam.

The patient required invasive mechanical ventilation for the following 24 hours, with posterior successful extubation. Afterward, she was transferred to general pediatric hospitalization. The facial wound was observed to be in good condition, undergoing reduction of the inflammatory process and adequate scarring (Fig. 4).

Surgical incision during the 1st postoperative week.

An ophthalmology assessment was requested because of the suspected bilateral optic nerve atrophy. A fundoscopy was performed, showing evidence of a diminished diameter of the optic papilla. However, outpatient orbital MRI was requested to further explore said findings and to establish the diagnosis of septo-optic dysplasia. The patient remained under observation in general hospitalization for a further 24 hours without any complications and was subsequently discharged due to the family’s need to return to their community.

After discharge, we received the pathology report for the resected lesion, indicating glial tissue with significant reactive gliosis changes, fibroconnective tissue septa, and the focal presence of respiratory mucosa. No tumoral tissue or other anomalous pathology was observed.

Unfortunately, the patient has not returned for follow-up, and orbital MRI was not conducted. However, a brief assessment was performed via telephone, with the patient’s caregivers reporting a satisfactory evolution 2 months after surgery, including adequate healing and normal growth and development of the child.

Discussion

Observations

The presented case underscores the complexity of developmental disorders, particularly schizencephaly and encephalocele. Schizencephaly, characterized by abnormal neuronal migration, often manifests with various developmental delays, including cognitive and speech impairments, partial or complete paralysis, poor muscle tone, abnormally small head, and seizures.11 However, the patient in this report exhibited no apparent signs of these issues at the time of evaluation, although she is still too young for a comprehensive assessment of speech or intelligence.

It has been proposed that EMX2, a transcription factor–related gene that expresses in the dorsal telencephalon and seems to pattern the functional areas of the neocortex, is involved in the cause of schizencephaly. Nonetheless, genotipification of a schizencephaly case series failed to show that pathological EMX2 is a common cause of schizencephaly.12

On the other hand, encephaloceles are a sac-like protrusion of the brain and meninges through a skull opening, stemming from a neural tube defect occurring during early pregnancy.5 These anomalies are categorized into three main types based on the location and nature of the skull defect: frontoethmoidal, occipital, and cranial vault, as well as the basal type. Encephaloceles occur predominantly in the occipital cranial vault, accounting for over 80% of cases.13 Frontoethmoidal encephaloceles encompass the three subtypes of nasofrontal, nasoethmoidal, and naso-orbital, with the nasoethmoidal subtype being the most frequently reported in the literature. The association between encephaloceles and schizencephaly is uncommon,8, 14 adding a layer of interest to this presentation.

Involvement of the optic nerve and ocular defects associated with the aforementioned conditions has been reported in a syndromic fashion (cerebro-oculo-nasal syndrome), but very few cases have been reported. There is not yet a fully understood mechanism of the morphogenesis of this condition, and genetic causes are also still unclear.15

As mentioned previously, potential causative factors for these abnormalities encompass infections, teratogenic exposure, genetic mutations, and nutritional deficiencies. While the STORCH infection profile ruled out infections postnatally, it should be noted that testing for some infectious and tropical diseases was not possible. Teratogenic exposure was not reported, though the patient lacked prenatal care. Genetic mutations are a plausible consideration, especially given the mother’s history of two spontaneous abortions. Sadly, genetic profiling has not been performed because of the logistical challenges given the patient’s provenance from a rural area. An insufficient diet lacking in folic acid,16 a common risk factor for neural tube closure defects, adds another layer to the potential risk factors in this case, as the mother did not receive any prenatal supplementation.

Lessons

This case report underscores the importance of early intervention in managing multiple birth defects to mitigate their clinical impact. Promptly correcting the encephalocele and addressing hydrocephalus aimed to prevent abnormal head growth and compression of gray matter, recognizing the family’s limited access to advanced medical care in dispersed rural areas of Colombia. The inclusion of early management strategies, acknowledging the positive impact of timely interventions, contributed to an improved prognosis for the patient.

This case report sheds light on the interplay of developmental disorders, exemplified by the coexistence of schizencephaly and encephalocele in a pediatric patient from a rural area of Colombia. The association between these two conditions is uncommon, adding a layer of complexity to the case. Potential causative factors, including infections, teratogenic exposures, gene mutations, and nutritional deficiencies, were considered, as were the challenges in comprehensive testing and follow-up due to the rural provenance of the patient.

Early intervention focused on managing both birth defects aims to mitigate their clinical impact, recognizing the family’s limited access to advanced medical and neurosurgical care. The inclusion of early management strategies not only addresses immediate concerns but also holds the potential to positively influence the long-term prognosis for the patient, emphasizing the significance of timely and pragmatic interventions in resource-constrained settings. Moreover, it is imperative to emphasize the need for further research to unravel the genetic and environmental factors contributing to these rare anomalies. Enhanced understanding in this area is crucial for patient care and outcomes.
