Bleeding risk assessment in coronary artery bypass grafting-related postoperative atrial fibrillation: clinical interpretation and future directions
Authors: Xubo Zhao, Lingfan Sun
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with great interest the proof-of-concept study by Rezk et al.1, which examined whether the four-item PRECISE-DAPT score can stratify post-discharge major bleeding risk in patients with new-onset postoperative atrial fibrillation (POAF) after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) who were not treated with oral anticoagulation (OAC). The study addresses a practical gap between guideline language and bedside decisions: clinicians are asked to balance stroke and bleeding risk, yet have limited guidance on how to quantify bleeding risk in CABG-related POAF. We aim to consider how this score might be used within a coherent clinical pathway, rather than recast it as an anticoagulation rule.

In CABG-related POAF, PRECISE-DAPT is best understood as identifying patients in whom OAC warrants caution, not as a stand-alone rule to start or withhold treatment. In Rezk and colleagues’ study, major bleeding increased progressively across low-, moderate-, and high-risk groups, whereas the 1-year incidence of ischaemic stroke was similar across these categories.1 This dissociation does not lessen the value of bleeding stratification; it shows that high bleeding risk must be interpreted alongside expected thromboembolic benefit. A recent meta-analysis of anticoagulation for POAF after isolated CABG reported increased bleeding without a clear reduction in thromboembolic events.2 The score may therefore be most useful when it raises the threshold for prolonged OAC, particularly when POAF is brief, sinus rhythm has been restored, and stroke risk is not extreme. Its derivation in DAPT-treated percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) patients is relevant, because many CABG patients, especially after acute coronary syndrome, receive antiplatelet therapy or DAPT. This makes PRECISE-DAPT a plausible screening tool, but its comparative value should be tested through CABG-specific calibration and comparison with atrial fibrillation (AF)-oriented scores such as HAS-BLED and ORBIT. HAS-BLED has been examined in cardiac surgery patients,3 and ORBIT was developed for anticoagulated AF populations; PRECISE-DAPT should therefore be demonstrated, not assumed, to add value in POAF. Future analyses should cross-classify bleeding risk with CHA2DS2-VASc score, AF recurrence, antithrombotic exposure, and intended treatment duration, and report absolute risk differences and net clinical benefit.

A second consideration is methodological. The main issue is not simply whether more variables could improve prediction, but whether a score derived in one clinical context can be transported and calibrated reliably in another. PCI and CABG differ in bleeding mechanisms, anatomical sources, and timing: access-site or gastrointestinal bleeding after PCI is not equivalent to bleeding related to surgical wounds, chest tubes, sternal healing, transfusion, and early postoperative renal injury. Rezk et al.1 reported generally satisfactory calibration, but also noted overestimation in patients with predicted bleeding risk above 7%. Because registry bleeding was limited to events requiring hospitalization or causing death, absolute risks should also be interpreted as conservative estimates and contextualized to the surgical course. Miscalibration is clinically consequential: overestimation could make clinicians overly conservative and deny anticoagulation to patients with substantial stroke risk. A CABG-specific approach should separate short-term and long-term decisions. During the first 4–6 weeks, bleeding risk is shaped mainly by surgical recovery, haemoglobin trajectory, chest-tube bleeding, re-exploration, transfusion, acute kidney injury, renal recovery,4,5 and postoperative antithrombotic changes.6 Beyond 3 months, risk is more likely to be driven by age, chronic kidney disease, previous bleeding, frailty, and concomitant antithrombotic therapy. In the early window, a discharge checklist may be more useful than a baseline score; later, baseline characteristics may regain importance. These postoperative variables should be candidates for derivation, recalibration, and external validation, not a ready-to-use alternative score.

Any rhythm-guided pathway should specify decision anchors. Bleeding risk assessment should be paired with rhythm surveillance because the expected benefit of OAC depends on ongoing arrhythmic exposure, not only on a historical in-hospital POAF episode. SEARCH-AF showed that 30-day continuous electrocardiographic monitoring after cardiac surgery increased detection of post-discharge AF compared with usual care.7 More recent long-term monitoring after CABG suggested that new-onset AF may be detected more often than previously recognized, although overall AF burden may be low.8 Detection alone, however, is not a therapeutic threshold. The 2023 ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS AF guideline provides pragmatic thresholds for device-detected atrial high-rate episodes, with different recommendations for episodes lasting at least 24 h, 5 min to 24 h, and less than 5 min.9 These thresholds should not be directly transferred to CABG-related POAF, because AF during postoperative inflammation and adrenergic stress may not have the same prognostic meaning as subclinical AF in non-surgical populations. Even so, they illustrate the burden-based framework that POAF studies should test. A single brief recurrence during early recovery may not carry the same implication as recurrent or sustained AF in a patient with previous stroke. Monitoring duration should also be pragmatic: 30 days may be reasonable for uncertain cases, whereas longer monitoring may be reserved for patients with recurrent symptoms, high thromboembolic risk, or unresolved anticoagulation decisions. A useful pathway should specify who is monitored and what AF-burden result should trigger OAC initiation, continuation, interruption, or specialist review, while accounting for adherence, cost, and follow-up burden.

In summary, Rezk et al. provide an important step towards structured bleeding-risk assessment in CABG-related POAF. The next challenge is to define where PRECISE-DAPT sits within a calibrated, time-specific, and rhythm-guided decision pathway. These proposals remain hypotheses for prospective testing, not validated substitutes for clinical judgement. Pragmatic studies should assess whether recalibrated bleeding tools, separate short-term and long-term decision windows, and pre-specified AF-burden thresholds improve clinically meaningful outcomes compared with uniform anticoagulation or routine non-anticoagulation, while also measuring implementation burden, adherence, and resource use.
