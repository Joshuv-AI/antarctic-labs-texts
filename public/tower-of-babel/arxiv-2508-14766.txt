Algorithmic Collusion is Algorithm Orchestration
Cesare Carissimo, Fryderyk Falniowski, Siavash Rahimi, Heinrich Nax

Abstract
This paper proposes a fresh ‘meta-game’ perspective on the problem of algorithmic collusion in pricing games a la Bertrand. Economists have interpreted the fact that algorithms can learn to price collusively as tacit collusion. We argue instead that the co-parametrization of algorithms – that we show is necessary to obtain algorithmic collusion – requires algorithm designer(s) to engage in explicit collusion by algorithm orchestration. To highlight this, we model a meta-game of algorithm parametrization that is played by algorithm designers, and the relevant strategic analyses at that level reveal new equilibrium and collusion phenomena.



Algorithmic collusion is a growing phenomenon and a major challenge for the law and regulators
(
Schwalbe 2018
)
. Already prior to the advent of AI and the rise of pricing algorithms, because one cannot ‘read the minds of managers,’ collusion was notoriously difficult to detect. And things have now become even more difficult as pricing algorithms operate faster and by themselves. In fact, the law in most countries is such that not all behaviors resulting in collusion are deemed unlawful. In particular, it is not generally unlawful for price setters to sustain high prices through self-enforcing pricing strategies with reward-punishment schemes – where competitors tacitly recognize the reward of supra-competitive prices as well as the threat of punishment via low prices. The current interpretation of antitrust in many countries deems collusion as unlawful when there is
explicit
communication between price setters.
In legal language,
an agreement among competitors to limit competition is unlawful
but
conscious parallelism
is not as long as it is competitively arrived at,
[…] not because such pricing is desirable (it is not), but because it is close to impossible to devise a judicially enforceable remedy for ‘interdependent’ pricing

(
Harrington 2018
)
.

An emerging economic literature studying algorithmic collusion is interpreted as having shown that the conditions for
tacit
collusion, which would not be deemed illegal, are increasingly met as algorithms are able to communicate indirectly through actions without involved competitors ever directly communicating in a traditional sense. We shall discuss this literature in detail in the next section. The point we want to make here now is that our paper takes a stance that is fundamentally quite different from the existing literature.

Our perspective is that

a strategic analysis of the individual algorithms’ designs ought to be conducted, in order to establish whether the interdependent algorithms are designed competitively, that is, as best responses to one another seeking higher payoff unilaterally, or non-competitively, even collusively, so as to increase payoffs jointly.

Our main finding is that

algorithmic collusion can only strive if algorithms are orchestrated, that is, co-parametrized non-competitively in the pursuit of supra-competitive prices and joint payoff improvements. By contrast, algorithms set competitively will end up pricing close to competitively.

We believe our perspective provides a timely alternative to the existing criteria according to which algorithmic collusion is evaluated, especially as internet platforms increasingly distribute their custom pricing algorithms to their market participants. Evidently, there is a massive conflict of interest for the platform as the algorithm orchestrator to not deploy competitive algorithms but to curate the entire landscape jointly.
Indeed, this conflict of interest has been acknowledged in applied legal scholarship which is stuck with the traditional economic framework of tacit versus explicit collusion as a function of communication. As such criteria appear ill-suited for algorithm regulation, and in the absence of more nuanced economic analyses, some legal scholars have gone as far as recommending a general ban of all kinds of algorithms that condition their prices on the history of opponent prices
(
Harrington 2018
)
.

We think such a ban is not realistic and indeed not necessarily welfare-enhancing. Instead, we propose a fresh line of thought and a novel distinction between legal and illegal collusion according to whether the interdependent algorithms are competitively parametrized or the result of algorithm orchestration. Indeed, we show that outruling algorithms altogether may not benefit consumer surplus much, as algorithms that are parametrized as best responses to one another
would behave approximately competitively in the pricing game. By contrast, we find that the kind of highly collusive pricing behaviour as previously identified in the literature would require explicit collusion in parameter setting by the designers of the algorithm.

We obtain our results by embedding the algorithmic interaction of competing algorithms in a ‘meta-game’ of algorithm design that is played by algorithm designers who decide on the parameters of the algorithms that then compete to play the pricing games. You may think of this meta-game as played by algorithm designers who pick the (hyper)parameters of their algorithm, thus affecting both their own and the other parties’ payoffs.

A hyperparameter determines how an algorithm learns. It must be distinguished from the ‘weights’ (sometimes called parameters) which the algorithm learns by interacting.

Importantly, we postulate that the relevant players playing the meta-game (i.e. the algorithm designers) must tune their algorithm against their algorithmic opponents seeking to maximize their payoffs unilaterally. To the best of our knowledge, this kind of analysis is novel in economics, yet deemed relevant in principle by legal scholars who recognize the dynamic improvement process of ‘The Algorithm Game’
(

)
which was a source of inspiration.

The Economics of Algorithmic Collusion

1.1
Q-learning in Bertrand duopoly

A growing body of economic literature studies learning in games of competition (a la Bertrand, Cournot, etc.) and other games (e.g. Prisoner’s dilemma games) as played by interacting algorithms. Several papers identify situations where learning algorithms converge to collusive outcomes that yield supra-competitive payoffs. In this section, with a focus on Q-learning in Bertrand duopoly games, we review the relevant design choices made in the literature and relate them to the kinds of convergent phenomena that have been observed. We organize the existing literature according to the following two dimensions along which existing studies differ:

(i)

the learning algorithms being used—algorithms differ according to how they evaluate payoffs and how they select among available actions. Much of the literature, and indeed the one we shall focus on and contribute to more narrowly is concerned with Q-learning algorithms that vary in terms of their hyperparameters, especially concerning exploration (exp_), being
\epsilon

\epsilon

1.2
Us vis-à-vis the Literature

We have learned from the literature that price collusion between two (and more) learning algorithms is possible. In particular, it is shown to be possible in certain ranges of the parameter spaces of the learning algorithms. What is not known from the literature is whether algorithmic collusion would naturally arise from algorithms being designed by competitors or by allies. This motivates our main research question, which to us seems highly appropriate and timely as regulators strive to adequately regulate algorithmic collusion:

RQ:
Does price collusion require algorithm orchestration?

1.

What pricing dynamics emerge among competitive algorithms?

2.

Instead, what are the pricing dynamics of orchestrated algorithms?

Our focus shifts away from the phenomenon of ‘algorithmic collusion’ to the phenomenon of collusive algorithm parametrization, which we shall refer to as
algorithm orchestration
. To make this perspective explicit, we study the incentives of algorithm designers by formulating a meta-game where the strategies of the players, that is, of the algorithm designers are the hyperparameters that they pick for their algorithms which then compete in an underlying repeated game. We thus distinguish between collusion in the meta-game and price collusion in the underlying repeated game. While we focus on the kinds of algorithms and games that have been studied in the literature, this conceptual framework can be applied to any combination of algorithms and underlying game. We tackle concretely the most popular setting, which is a Bertrand duopoly game played by two
\epsilon
-greedy
Q
-learners.
We find that the meta-game has a unique meta Nash equilibrium that is pure and symmetric. Algorithms parametrized at the equilibrium exhibit pricing behaviors that are only
slightly
supra-competitive and noisy. By contrast, the highly collusive pricing behaviors on the Pareto front of the meta-game are all asymmetric.

2
Algorithm competition versus algorithm orchestration

2.1
Formulating a Meta-Game

In this section we formalize the meta-game. Its players are the algorithm designers, each of which controls one algorithm and sets the hyperparameters of their algorithm. The player’s payoffs are determined by competition in a repeated game, which in this paper we pick as the repeated Bertrand duopoly. For the case of two players, ‘ego’ and ‘alter,’ each player chooses a parameter (or vector of parameters) for their algorithm. We represent the parameter space as
\Theta
and grant both players access to the same space. We denote a player’s choice of parameter as
\theta_{i}\in\Theta
. Ego and alter’s algorithms map past prices (and possibly other relevant information) to the next prices to be set.

The payoffs for ego and alter are determined by evaluating the performance of their algorithms in repeated play of the Bertrand duopoly. The game is repeated for
T
rounds. At each round ego and alter’s algorithms are queried to determine their prices for that round. The demand in each round is a linear decreasing function of the price. The reward for both players in each round is their profit, calculated as their share of the demand multiplied by the price. If they set the same price, demand is split equally. Otherwise, the player with the lowest price is assigned the entire demand.

At the end of the
T
rounds, both players will have played a vector of prices
\vec{p}_{i}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{T})_{i}
and experienced a vector of profits
\vec{r}_{i}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{T})_{i}
. The payoffs for ego and alter are then determined as an aggregation of the profits, for example, the uniformly weighted average of all profits received during repeated play. Hence, we can think of the payoffs as dependent on the parameters of both players as a function
\pi:\Theta\times\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
; a parameter profile
\theta_{1}
,
\theta_{2}
is assigned to a payoff for both players,
\pi(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
.

If alter picks a parameter
\theta_{2}
, the best response parameter
\theta^{*}
of ego is determined with the best response function
br:\Theta\rightarrow\Theta
. Specifically, the best response parameter is
\theta^{*}
such that for all
\theta\in\Theta
,
\pi(\theta^{*},\theta_{2})_{1}>\pi(\theta,\theta_{2})_{1}
. Then, a Nash equilibrium of the meta-game is any parameter profile
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
which is a mutual best response in the parameter picking meta-game:
br(\theta_{1})=\theta_{2}
and
br(\theta_{2})=\theta_{1}
. We refer to this scenario as algorithm competition.

Algorithms can also be orchestrated so that they reach the Pareto frontier of the meta-game, which is the set of all parameter profiles
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
such that there is no
\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
where
\pi(\theta_{1}^{\prime},\theta_{2})_{1}>\pi(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})_{1}
and
\pi(\theta_{1}^{\prime},\theta_{2})_{2}\geq\pi(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})_{2}
.
3
3

3

By symmetry of the meta-game, and the underlying Bertrand Duopoly, this condition expressed solely for one player is sufficient to determine the entire Pareto front.

2.2
The Computational Setup

We set up a two-player meta-game between two algorithm designers parametrizing
Q
-learners. Each player controls the learning hyperparameters of their
Q
-learning algorithm: the learning rate, the exploration rate, and the discount factor. After picking their parameters, the
Q
-learning algorithms play a repeated Bertrand Duopoly. During repeated play, the
Q
-learning algorithms learn and estimate the cumulative sum of discounted future profits for each price level given the price of their opponent. Our setup of the Bertrand Duopoly follows the setups of
Calvano et al. 2020
;
Eschenbaum et al. 2021
;
Asker et al. 2022
.

2.2.1
The Q-Learning Algorithm

Q
-learning is used widely to study learning, and has been center and focus of the algorithmic collusion literature. Optimal in Markov Decision Processes (MDP),
Q
-learning is guaranteed to reach an optimal deterministic policy if exhaustive exploration is allowed
(
Watkins and Dayan 1992
)
. An MDP is defined by a state space
\mathcal{S}
, an action space
\mathcal{A}
, a reward function
R:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and a transition function
\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\rightarrow[0,1]
. In our setup, two players compete in a pricing game. The action space of their pricing game is a set of discrete prices in a range
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1,...,6\}
. They perceive the last price of their opponent and only condition their next action on this information, so the state space is
\mathcal{S}=\{0,1,...,6\}
. Then, they select the next prices simultaneously.

We use a tabular implementation of
Q
-learning so all
q
-values are stored in a table. The Q-table
Q:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
maps states and actions to the cumulative sum of discounted future rewards.

Q
-learning has three parameters that determine the rate of learning
\alpha\in(0,1]
, exploration
\epsilon\in[0,1]
and discounting
\gamma\in[0,1]
. The learning rate governs how quickly
q
-values change given a new transition from state
s
to state
s^{\prime}
with action
a
receiving a reward
r
, following the Bellman update rule:

Q(s,a)\leftarrow Q(s,a)+\alpha(r+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-Q(s,a)).

In the Bellman update appears the discount factor
\gamma
which determines how much actions in the future influence the present. The exploration rate governs the likelihood of picking random actions, exploratory actions, following an
\epsilon
-greedy action policy from a state
s
:

\epsilon\text{-greedy}(s)=\begin{cases}arg\max_{a}Q(s,a)\>\>&w.p.\>1-\epsilon\\
\text{uniform random}\{\mathcal{A}\}\>\>&w.p.\>\epsilon\end{cases}.

2.2.2
The Strategy Space

At the start of the meta-game each player picks a tuple of parameters
(\alpha,\epsilon,\gamma)_{i}=\theta_{i}\in\Theta=[0,1]^{\times 3}
. Each parameter admits values between
0
and
1
. We discretize the parameter space such that each parameter is allowed
10
equidistant values between the following natural specified endpoints:
0.01\leq\alpha\leq 1
;
0\leq\epsilon\leq 0.5
;
0\leq\gamma\leq 1
. We set these bounds in between the reasonable extreme policies. Note that we do not include
\alpha=0
as this would negate all
q
-value updates. Moreover, we do not consider
\epsilon
higher than
0.5
, because when
\epsilon>0.5
the role of learning is overwhelmed by randomness, and we believe that higher values are not realistic choices for the exploration rate of an online
Q
-learning algorithm interested in maximizing the cumulative sum of discounted future rewards, particularly given the focus of the prior literature on decayed exploration.

2.2.3
The Repeated Game Payoffs

As is common for Bertrand Duopolies, consumers are inelastic and always choose the producer with the lowest price. When the prices are equal, they choose one producer randomly. Demand varies linearly based on the price, that is
D_{t}=max_{a}\mathcal{A}-min\{p_{1,t},p_{2,t}\}
. There is no marginal cost of production.

After both algorithm designers pick parameters for their
Q
-learning algorithm
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
, the two algorithms are deployed with their parametrization to learn in a Bertrand Duopoly where they play
T=40000
rounds. The payoff of a parameter profile
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
is expressed as
\pi(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
and is determined by repeated play of the Bertrand Duopoly. In each round
t
player
i
receives a profit
r_{i}^{t}
. Each player accumulates a vector of profits
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{T})
over the course of repeated play of the Bertrand Duopoly. The average of this vector is
\hat{\mathbf{r}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_{t}
. The estimated average profits for a parameter profile
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
are
\pi(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{1},\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{2})
. Finally, the payoffs for each player of the meta-game will be the online performance of their algorithm expressed as the average profit
\hat{\mathbf{r}}
over the learning period
T
.

We simulate every parameter profile
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\in\Theta^{2}
to estimate the payoffs
\pi(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
. For
3
learning parameters that take
10
possible values, we have
1000
parameter choices per player and
1
million parameter profiles. Each parameter profile is evaluated
40
times for statistical averaging
4

4

We choose these parameters as a compromise between computation time and convergence issues. Each parameter profile takes about 10 seconds to simulate. With 1 million combinations, each repeated 40 times, we get a run-time of about 4630 days. This was reduced to 1 week with massive parallel computing.
. The
q
-values of the
q
-tables for each
Q
-learning algorithm are initialized as follows:

Q(s,a_{i})=\frac{\sum_{a_{-i}\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a_{i},a_{-i})}{(1-\gamma)|\mathcal{A}|}.

which is the value they would converge to if their opponent played a uniform random pricing strategy.

3
Designer Incentives in the Meta-Game

In
subsection 2.1
we formulated the meta-game and in
subsection 2.2
we described our computational setup. In the next sections we present our computational results. In this section we focus on the rational play of algorithm designers. In other words, we address the following question for our players:

Given the parameters of the
Q
-learner on the other side, what parameters should one’s own
Q
-learner be equipped with that would generate the highest payoffs for the algorithm designer unilaterally?

We argue that this is the relevant question to ask to determine tacitly colluding algorithm behavior. Any parameter profile that is not mutually consistent with best-responding behavior, which generates higher payoffs for both players, in our view, constitutes explicit collusion.

3.1
The Nash equilibrium of the meta-game is pure, symmetric and unique

We evaluated the payoffs for every
\theta_{1}
for ego and
\theta_{2}
for alter.

Feature 1
.

Every
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
parameter profile of ego and alter with non-zero exploration rates
\epsilon>0
leads to noisy collusive pricing. A consequence of this pricing is that, in time, all price combinations are evaluated.

For each parameter of alter, we determine the best response parameter of ego:
br(\theta_{2})=\theta_{1}^{*}
.

Feature 2
.

For any parameter combination of alter, there exists a pure non-decaying parameter combination of ego that is a best response.

Thus, we can establish the existence of an equilibrium of the meta-game.

Result 1
.

There exists a parameter profile
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
for ego and alter that is a mutual best response, thus constituting a Nash equilibrium of the meta-game, and it turns out that this profile is symmetric, i.e.
br(\theta_{1})=br(\theta_{2})
.

Our main finding of the paper is that the meta-game with online payoffs has a unique pure symmetric Nash equilibrium with parameters
\alpha=0.12
,
\epsilon=0.278
and
\gamma=0.22
. We call this equilibrium the Meta Nash Equilibrium, or Meta Nash for short (MN). Moreover, the Meta Nash parameter profile is a best response to a majority of the parameter space. This is seen in
Figure 1
, where the arrows point from parameters
\theta_{2}
to the best response parameters
br(\theta_{2})=\theta_{1}
, the circles mark the best response parameters, and the sizes of the circles reflect the number of parameters for which
\theta_{1}
is a best response.

3.2.1
Representing best responses in the parameter space

Figure 1

Given the high dimensionality of the parameter space we show the results in a compressed representation which conveys a wealth of information. The figure represents the best response relationship between all parameter profiles in a 3 dimensional space. This figure has three types of plots aligned with the main axes: the cube at the center, the line plots aligned with the interior axes, and the 2D plots aligned with the faces of the cube.

The cube plot at the center of
Figure 1

The line plots are aligned with the main axes of the cube. We take a reference parameter e.g.
\alpha

follow the Meta Nash strategy. Then, we compute the best response reference paramter of ego given alter’s reference parameter, and keeping ego’s the other parameters fixed. We take the average over alter’s other parameters to determine the payoff.

The surface plots are aligned with the faces of the cube. We take two reference parameters e.g.
\alpha,\gamma

\epsilon

These reduced parameter plots can be interpreted as a form of decision making under uncertainty. Given that the reference player only knows the reference parameter of the opponent, we assume the other parameter settings are equiprobable. Then, we assume that the reference player considers the best responses in a reduced space of the parameters, setting their other parameters to a ‘known’ dominant strategy, the Meta Nash values of those parameters. We note that other distributions over the unknown parameters could be considered, and that these may change the reduced equilibria. These face plots should be used to develop intuition about the best response relationships in the entire parameter space.

The arrows in the space start at the parameters of alter and point to the best response parameters of ego. All arrows are colored according to the payoff gain obtained by deviating to the best response, assuming that the starting point was the symmetric parameter profile at the tail of the arrow.

3.2.2
Interpreting best responses

In
Figure 1

In the cube, the parameters which are a best response to at least one other tuple of parameter are marked with blue circles. The size of these circles reflects how many parameters they are best responses to. The arrows between these best response points are highlighted. Best response points that have arrows pointing away from them, are not a symmetric meta equilibrium. There is a sink of these best response arrows which is the Meta Nash. The Meta Nash parameter combination also has most best response arrows pointing to it. It is marked with a star.

All the arrows at the exterior of the parameter space point towards the interior. This is significant in comparison with many results from the literature, where highly collusive results are exalted at the symmetric extremes of the parameter ranges, and in decayed exploration where the exploration rate is varied exponentially from one extreme (1) to the other (0). As we have argued in the previous section, there is further evidence that decayed exploration is not a rational design choice. Our results indicate that the extremes of the parameter space are not best responses to each other.

4
Designer Collusion in the Meta-Game

The literature on algorithmic collusion has focused on price collusion. In this paper we contribute a new kind of perspective on collusion, which we call algorithm orchestration, which represents collusion in the meta-game. Meta-game collusion matters because non-competitive behavior in the meta-game may be what is driving supra-competitive profits. In this section we explain in detail the relationship between price collusion and algorithm orchestration. Given our previous analysis of meta-Nash, we now turn our attention to the Pareto frontier, and present our next result.

Result 2
.

The Bertrand Duopoly meta-game has a Pareto frontier consisting of asymmetric parameter pairs, where alter is a fast learner, frequent explorer and farsighted discounter, while ego is a slow learner, cautious explorer and myopic discounter. Ego achieves higher average profits in these pairings.

Several aspects are worth stressing.
First, as the Pareto frontier is not symmetric, the kind of focal price collusion as identified by
Calvano et al. 2020
is an unlikely result of any type of competition in the online meta-game.
Fully competitive parametrization would result in the unique Nash of the meta-game which leads to noisy competitive pricing. This can be seen in the lower left quadrant of
Figure 2
. Therefore, we argue that focal price collusion is likely to require collusion in the meta-game, and evaluation to be done in the limit.
Second, the Pareto frontier of the meta-game, visualized in
Figure 2
, consists of asymmetric pairs involving at least one very noisy explorer. Indeed, a subset of these parameter profiles set both algorithms to have highly random behavior.
Another subset has highly asymmetric parameter profiles where one
Q
-learner is highly random and the other learns to exploit this randomness.
Already such design choices require collusion in the meta-game, and to obtain anything more collusive and less noisy almost certainly does too.

Third, as concerns Edgeworth cycles, which are another important kind of price behavior resulting from algorithmic collusion (see
Klein 2021
) that are in fact achievable as Markov best responses
(
Maskin and Tirole 1988
)
, we can find on the Pareto frontier some parameter profiles that exhibit some kind of cycling pricing behaviour, see for example the top right quadrant of

Klein 2021
was with symmetric decayed exploration of the
Q

Not all parameter combinations result in supra-competitive payoffs. Indeed, we also find parameter profiles that are not mutual best responses but lead to competitive pricing. For the very low parameter range of learning rate
\alpha

The Nash Equilibrium of the meta-game yields a kind of supra-competitive pricing which is new to the literature. It is neither a focal price, nor an Edgeworth cycle. In fact, it is a noisy pricing behavior, which frequently experiments with different prices. Over time, it achieves a
slightly
higher than competitive average profit. While the pricing dynamics are not a perfect best response to the opponent prices, we establish through the meta game that this design choice is a mutual best response. This is the only pricing behavior we find to lead to approximately competitive pricing while also being a parameter best response. In other words, we find that approximately competitive pricing does not require design orchestration. In the papers by
Calvano et al. 2020
;
Klein 2021
it was shown for the repeated game that competitive equilibrium pricing could be achieved for specific ranges of the parameters. However, both papers relied on a coordinated decay of exploration between
Q
-learners. In our analysis of the meta-game, we find no parameter profiles that lead to perfectly competitive pricing and are a mutual best response.

5
Discussions and Conclusions

5.1
Best responses in the meta-game

We have analyzed the strategic decision making of
Q
-learning algorithm designers. First, we showed that the popular design choice of decaying the exploration rate is suboptimal: it is not a best response in the meta-game. Thus, the collusive outcomes obtained with decayed exploration cannot be considered a form of tacit collusion.

In fact, we showed that the meta-game has a unique pure and symmetric Nash Equilibrium in fixed parameter values, which we call the Meta Nash. The pricing behavior at the Meta Nash leads to only slightly supra-competitive prices, and looks very different from the previously identified collusive behavior in the literature of focal price collusion and Edgeworth cycles, which were obtained under decaying exploration rates. Our study indicates that the supra-competitive pricing behavior, which we would find in a competitive market, is highly noisy. Furthermore, this pricing behavior would be a best response in the meta-game, and therefore could not be considered a form of explicit collusion.

5.3
Limitations

A Bertrand Duopoly played by
Q
-learning is a useful model to reason about price competition, but an unlikely scenario to encounter in the real-world. The main contribution of this paper is the framework by which we judge competitive from collusive design choices for competing algorithms. This may be helpful to define a category of ‘prohibited algorithms’
(
Harrington 2018
)
. However, there are possibly infinitely many classes of algorithms that would need to be evaluated computationally against one another to determine this category
ante facto
. Instead, we advocate for an
ad hoc
evaluation of algorithm design competitiveness. Therefore, the greatest limitation at this present moment is our lack of knowledge of the pricing algorithms that companies are
actually using
.

5.4
Future Work

In future work we are interested to take further steps in the direction of realism. We would begin by introducing noise, in particular to the underlying game. To what extent is algorithmic collusion, which boils down to a coordination problem, disrupted by such noise? Additionally, we encourage future work to evaluate algorithms cumulatively rather than in the limit. This is because evaluation in the limit rests on weak assumptions that exploration is costless. In other words, it assumes that price discovery does not require interaction with a market. This is highly unlikely to be the case, and our results suggest that this assumption yields misleading conclusions. Indeed, it seems that when exploration is costless and learning is evaluated in the limit, the optimal behavior of players should be to learn as much as possible about the opponents and the environment. However, what would a player be learning about? It would learn how the other players are learning, and the other players are equally trying to explore as much as possible and reduce their uncertainty about the others. This setup seems to yield an environment where all players learn to play against players that they will not face during deployment.

5.5
Final remarks

Financial markets, housing markets, smart cities, and platform economies all see increasingly widespread use of pricing algorithms. This may be a major problem, particularly as a growing literature has shown that they are able to learn to collude. We make a difference between algorithm competition – when designers choose competitive parameters – and algorithm orchestration – when designers coordinate their parameters to collusive outcomes.
Our paper indicates that supra-competitive prices between
Q
-learning algorithms can be achieved with algorithm orchestration. Pertinently, we show that the Nash Equilibrium of the meta-game of parameter picking leads to noisy competitive prices. These algorithms generate profits which are slightly supra-competitive, but generally beneficial for the welfare of consumers. If we extend the definition of non-collusion of
Hartline et al. 2024
, that pricing behavior should not be considered collusive if is approximately best responding to the opponent, then we
cannot
consider two players that play the meta-game close to Nash to be colluding with one another. On the other hand, any price pattern similar to steady focal prices or clean Edgeworth cycles may be evidence of collusion by algorithm designers, and regulators should start by out-ruling algorithm designer collusion; i.e. algorithms that are being checked must be shown to be parametrized to maximize payoffs unilaterally, or at least be tuned to that goal.
