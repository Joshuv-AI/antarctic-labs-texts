Advanced imaging of corneal neovascularisation with a novel swept-source AS-OCTA system
Authors: Luca Lucchino, Sabrina Vaccaro, Matteo Airaldi, Eliana Forbice, Antonio Moramarco, Alessandro Lambiase, Luigi Fontana, Francesco Semeraro, Vito Romano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo explore the clinical application of a novel swept-source anterior segment optical coherence tomography angiography (AS-OCTA) system for imaging corneal neovascularisation (CoNV), assessing limbal vasculature and detecting short-term vascular changes following pharmacologic vasoconstriction.MethodsThis cross-sectional observational study included 20 eyes: 10 with clinically diagnosed CoNV of various aetiologies and 10 healthy controls. Each eye underwent AS-OCTA imaging using the BMizar 400kHz Full-Range Swept-Source OCT system at baseline and after administration of 10% phenylephrine (PE). FlowArea (mm2) was automatically calculated across predefined circular regions of interest (ROIs) encompassing the cornea, limbus and episclera. In corneal and limbal ROIs, en face images were segmented into three depth layers: total (epithelium to endothelium), superficial (epithelium to 150 µm) and deep (150 µm to endothelium).ResultsCoNV eyes exhibited significantly higher total and deep corneal FlowArea compared with controls (p=0.0002 and p=0.0005, respectively). Post PE, a significant reduction in FlowArea was observed in the total and deep corneal layers of CoNV eyes (p=0.006 and p=0.009, respectively), and in the limbal region of both groups, with significant reductions observed in CoNV and controls (p=0.006 and p=0.003, respectively).ConclusionsThis AS-OCTA platform enabled high-resolution, depth-resolved visualization of CoNV and revealed vascular changes following topical vasoconstriction. The extended field of view allowed simultaneous assessment of corneal, limbal and episcleral vasculature within a single scan session, supporting its potential role in both diagnostic evaluation and longitudinal monitoring of anterior segment diseases.Keywords: Cornea, Neovascularisation, Conjunctiva, Imaging

Introduction

Under physiological conditions, the human cornea is an avascular tissue, a state actively maintained by the balance between proangiogenic and antiangiogenic factors. This immune-privileged state is essential for corneal transparency and optimal visual function.1 However, a wide range of inflammatory, infectious, degenerative and traumatic conditions can disrupt this balance, resulting in corneal neovascularisation (CoNV).2

The epidemiology of CoNV varies by region and underlying condition. In the USA, it affects an estimated 1.4 million individuals annually.3 CoNV leads to the ingrowth of abnormal blood vessels into the corneal stroma, compromising transparency and contributing to progressive visual impairment. Furthermore, CoNV also disrupts the immune privilege of the cornea by facilitating leucocyte infiltration and antigen presentation, which significantly increases the risk of graft rejection following corneal transplantation. The extent of neovascularisation is directly correlated with graft failure rates, making early detection and monitoring of CoNV a key priority in transplant planning and ocular surface disease management.4 5

The management of CoNV remains challenging due to the variability in vessel depth, activity and distribution, underscoring the need for accurate, reproducible imaging techniques that can guide therapeutic decisions.6 Traditional imaging modalities such as fluorescein angiography and indocyanine green angiography (ICGA) remain the gold standard due to their ability to depict vessel leakage and perfusion dynamics.7 However, their invasive nature, time-consuming workflow and potential side effects have driven the demand for advanced, non-invasive techniques.6 Optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) is a non-invasive imaging technique that allows detailed visualisation of microvascular architecture by detecting phase shifts in reflected low-coherence light caused by moving red blood cells. Through repeated A-scans at the same location, OCTA captures temporal changes in signal caused by blood flow, enabling the reconstruction of en face vascular maps without the need for intravenous dyes.8 While initially developed for retinal and choroidal imaging, recent adaptations have extended its application to the anterior segment, enabling visualisation of corneal and conjunctival microvasculature.9 However, early anterior segment OCTA (AS-OCTA) systems have been limited by field of view restrictions, motion artefacts and challenges in image interpretation compared with dye-based angiography.10 11

Recently, a new swept-source OCT platform has been developed: the BMizar 400kHz Full-Range SS-OCT (TowardPi Medical Technology, China). This system combines high-speed acquisition, extended field of view and advanced image processing software, offering a more comprehensive approach to anterior segment vascular imaging.

We hypothesised that this system would enable high-resolution, depth-resolved and functional assessment of CoNV and limbal vasculature, providing clinically relevant insights beyond traditional AS-OCTA.

Methods

This observational cross-sectional study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Review Board of ASST Spedali Civili di Brescia (protocol number 6210). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment. The study was carried out at the Ophthalmology Unit of ASST Spedali Civili di Brescia, Brescia, Italy, where all subjects were consecutively recruited in April 2025.

Given the exploratory nature of this study and limited availability of patients with stable CoNV, a convenience sample of 10 eyes per group was selected. Future studies with larger cohorts will be needed to confirm these findings and allow for more robust subgroup analysis. In detail, a total of 20 eyes from 20 subjects were included: 10 eyes from patients with clinically diagnosed CoNV of various aetiologies and 10 eyes from healthy age-matched controls (HC). Only one eye per subject was enrolled. In cases of bilateral CoNV, the eye exhibiting more extensive vascularisation on slit-lamp examination was selected.

Inclusion criteria for the CoNV group consisted of the presence of CoNV extending at least 3 mm into the corneal stroma, from the limbus, as estimated by slit-lamp biomicroscopy, with or without corneal scarring, infiltration or oedema.

Exclusion criteria included age under 18 years, inability to maintain stable fixation due to blindness or involuntary eye movements (eg, nystagmus), and poor image quality precluding vascular analysis.

Demographic and clinical data were collected for all participants, including age, sex, diagnosis and the eye imaged. In healthy controls, the right eye was selected for imaging and analysis unless exclusion criteria (eg, motion artefacts or poor scan quality) were met, in which case the left eye was used.

OCTA

AS-OCTA imaging was performed using the BMizar 400kHz Full-Range Swept-Source OCT system (BM-400K, TowardPi Medical Technology, China), equipped with a dedicated anterior segment adaptor. The device employs a swept-source laser centred at 1060 nm, with an A-scan rate of 400 000 scans per second. It provides an axial optical resolution of ≤6 µm and a transverse resolution of 10 µm. Imaging was conducted using the anterior segment scan protocol with a 17×17 mm field of view, enabling wide-field visualisation of the corneal, limbal and adjacent episcleral vasculature.

Participants were aligned using real-time infrared positioning, and fixation was maintained on an internal target. Acquisition time for the 17 × 17 mm scans was approximately 6–7 s, with minor variation depending on steady fixation. Autofocus was manually adjusted to optimise centration and focus. Background illumination was minimised to enhance image quality. Scans with significant motion artefacts (eg, vessel duplication, signal distortions or horizontal banding) that impaired the interpretability of vascular structures, poor centration or suboptimal focus were excluded following quality control review by trained personnel. Additionally, only scans with an image quality score ≥6, as provided by the device software, were included for analysis.

Image processing and quantitative analysis were performed using the BMizar’s integrated anterior segment angiography analysis software. Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined using circular areas centred on the corneal apex: a 5.5 mm radius circle for the cornea and a 6.5 mm radius circle for the limbus, allowing full coverage of the limbal circumference (figure 1, figure 2, online supplemental figure 1). Additionally, two 1 mm radius ROIs were placed over the nasal and temporal sclera at the 0° and 180° meridians, respectively (figure 3). To ensure consistent placement of scleral ROIs between T0 and T1, scan lines and system alignment tools were used to manually position the 1 mm radius ROIs at the 0° and 180° meridians relative to the corneal centre. In the corneal and limbal regions, en face OCTA images were segmented into three predefined depth layers: total (epithelium to endothelium), superficial (epithelium to 150 µm) and deep (150 µm to endothelium) using the system’s integrated analysis software (figure 4, online supplemental figure 2). Corneal boundaries were automatically detected by the device software, while the operator manually defined the depth slab limits within the platform. Scleral regions were analysed as full-thickness scans without depth segmentation.

The primary quantitative metric extracted from each ROI was the vascular FlowArea, which represents the sum of the absolute values of the area occupied by blood vessels in the two-dimensional image, expressed in square millimetres (mm2), and automatically calculated by the system’s built-in software.

Each eye was imaged twice: first at baseline (T0), and then after pharmacologic vasoconstriction (T1).12 Phenylephrine (PE) 10% ophthalmic solution was administered topically in two separate doses: one drop 10 min before the second scan and a second drop 1 min before acquisition. There were no missing data for any of the variables included in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Prism V.9.5.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, California, USA). Descriptive data were summarised using the mean (SD) and median (IQR) where appropriate. The Anderson-Darling test and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests were applied to assess if data were normally distributed. Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U-test was applied to compare variables when appropriate. Intragroup comparisons between pre and post PE measurements were conducted using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, as appropriate. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

This study included 10 eyes from 10 patients, 6 men and 4 women, with CoNV and 10 HC eyes. The mean age of the study group was 59.50±19.55 years (range: 22–64 years). No differences were detected between the two groups with regard to age and sex distribution (p>0.5). Patients with CoNV presented with a range of aetiologies, including infectious keratitis (herpetic, bacterial, fungal and Acanthamoeba) as well as post-keratoplasty neovascularisation (online supplemental table 2).

Study group versus control group

A statistical difference was observed between the study and control groups concerning total cornea FlowArea, 13 944±5487 mm2 and 4609±3004 mm2 (p=0.0002), and deep cornea FlowArea 13 090±5600 mm2 and 4496±3071 mm2 (p=0.0005), respectively. On the contrary, no statistically significant differences were observed for superficial cornea FlowArea, 17 878±7669 mm2 and 13 899±6639 mm2 (p=0.2). With regard to limbus vascularity, a statistical difference was detected for limbus total vascular FlowArea, 29 439±7938 mm2 and 18 278±4556 mm2 (p=0.001), and for limbus deep vascular FlowArea, 28 495±8104 mm2 and 18 130±4665 mm2 (p=0.002). No statistically significant changes were seen in limbus superficial vascular FlowArea, scleral nasal vascular FlowArea and scleral temporal vascular FlowArea, 30 023±8879 mm2 and 26 233±8358 mm2 (p=0.3), 1474±505.9 mm2 and 1643±136.8 mm2 (p=0.3), and 1412 mm2 (IQR: 1301–1586) and 1565 mm2 (IQR: 1242–1768), (p=0.3) respectively (online supplemental table 1).

Study group

Mean values of total cornea FlowArea and deep FlowArea decreased from 13 944±5487 mm2 at T0 to 9734±4370 mm2 at T1 (p=0.006) and from 13 090±5600 mm2 at T0 to 9020±4437 mm2 at T1 (p=0.009), respectively. Conversely, there were no statistically significant differences for superficial FlowArea from 17 878±7669 mm2 at T0 to 19 954±9055 mm2 at T1 (p=0.3). In parallel, the mean value of limbus total vascular FlowArea and deep vascular FlowArea decreased significantly from 29 439±7938 mm2 to 22 536±5009 mm2 (p=0.006) and from 28 495±8104 mm2 to 21 688±5335 mm2 (p=0.007). No statistical differences were observed in limbus superficial vascular FlowArea, scleral nasal vascular FlowArea and scleral temporal vascular FlowArea, 30 023±8879 mm2 and 32 035±10 934 mm2 (p=0.4), 1474±505.9 mm2 and 1280±489.2 mm2 (p=0.08), and from 1412 mm2 (IQR: 1301–1586) to 1608 mm2 (IQR: 1112–1778) (p=0.6), respectively (table 1).

Control group

In the control group, there were no statistically significant differences for all cornea, superficial and deep FlowArea (p=0.1, p=0.9, p=0.08, respectively). Discussion

This study demonstrates the clinical utility of a novel swept-source AS-OCTA system in characterising CoNV and detecting short-term vascular changes following topical vasoconstriction. Our findings support its role in depth-resolved and vascular assessment. Quantitative analysis revealed significantly higher vascular signal in CoNV eyes compared with HCs. Furthermore, a significant reduction in OCTA FlowArea was observed after topical PE administration in both groups.

Previous studies have highlighted the potential of AS-OCTA in detecting CoNV, particularly in eyes with stromal scarring or oedema, where conventional slit-lamp examination may fail to reveal deeper or fine vascular structures.13,16 In these contexts, OCTA offers enhanced sensitivity, allowing visualisation of vascular patterns otherwise obscured by opacity. Moreover, assessing the depth of CoNV is of critical clinical relevance, as superficial and deep vessels may differ in pathophysiology, chronicity and their impact on visual prognosis or graft outcomes.17 Notably, Nanji et al employed spectral-domain and swept-source OCTA to map vessel depth relative to corneal thickness, finding strong correlation with clinical grading.18 Similarly, Binotti et al introduced additional morphometric parameters, such as depth percentage, vascular thickness and volume that correlated with CoNV severity and visual acuity.19 While these studies underscore the potential of AS-OCTA to provide quantitative parameters in the assessment of CoNV, both relied on multistep manual segmentation, external image processing tools and systems with limited scan areas.18 19 Conversely, in the present study, depth evaluation was straightforward and time efficient, facilitated by a swept-source OCTA system with dedicated anterior segment software. Depth localisation was easily verified through en face slabs and corresponding cross-sectional B-scans, without requiring external post-processing or large-format image exports. This efficient workflow is particularly advantageous when imaging extensive neovascular networks, where full corneal coverage is required, and proved effective even in the presence of stromal opacities, likely aided by the enhanced penetration of the 1060 nm swept-source laser.

Motion and projection artefacts are well-known limitations in AS-OCTA, especially when using platforms originally designed for posterior segment imaging.9 11 In earlier studies, such artefacts frequently compromised quantification or required extensive manual correction.14 20 In our experience, the use of a dedicated anterior segment swept-source platform with integrated analysis software substantially reduced these issues, enabling consistent image quality and more stable vascular measurements across scans. Although intra-session repeatability was not formally assessed, the visual consistency observed between baseline and post-phenylephrine scans suggests good scan stability under standardised acquisition conditions. However, artefacts were not entirely eliminated. We observed an unexpectedly elevated FlowArea in the ‘Superficial’ range across all groups. Rather than indicating a true increase in flow signal, this likely reflects residual projection or segmentation-related artefacts affecting FlowArea quantification, despite improvements in resolution and depth specificity. Moreover, analysis of the B-scans revealed occasional vertical streak artefacts or hyperintense columns that corresponded to small circular signals on en face scans and were interpreted by the algorithm as flow, despite the anatomical absence of vasculature. These artefacts are consistent with previously described false-positive OCTA signals caused by axial reflectivity or motion-related noise, possibly enhanced by backscattered light from the scanning beam in combination with the corneal curvature. Similarly, in the control group, slight peripheral overlap of the circular ROI (5.5 mm) with the limbal vascular arcade, where physiological vasculature is normally present, potentially increased signal variability. These findings highlight the ongoing need for refinement of anterior segment-specific acquisition and analysis algorithms to further enhance AS-OCTA applications. Importantly, awareness of potential artefacts is essential when interpreting flow-based metrics to ensure reliable quantification in both research and clinical settings.

Monitoring vascular regression is a key goal in CoNV management. This was already highlighted in expert consensus statements, which emphasised the need for imaging modalities capable of assessing both vascular depth and dynamic changes over time.17 Cai et al showed that AS-OCTA could detect significant reductions in neovascular area following treatment in herpetic keratitis.21 Similarly, Aschauer et al reported post-treatment changes in corneal vasculature using OCTA in high-risk graft candidates, supporting its potential use for longitudinal monitoring.22 Our findings add to this perspective by showing that AS-OCTA is sensitive to short-term haemodynamic changes induced by pharmacologic vasoconstriction. Notably, this response was observed both in eyes with CoNV and in healthy controls, suggesting potential applications for OCTA as a longitudinal tool to assess vascular change and possibly treatment response in clinical settings.

Previous animal studies have demonstrated a high concordance between OCTA and ICGA in detecting CoNV and monitoring regression after anti-VEGF therapy.23 24 OCTA proved capable of capturing subtle vascular changes over time and showed strong correlation with ICGA-derived metrics. In clinical settings, however, results have been more variable. Brunner et al reported that ICGA provided significantly better image quality than AS-OCTA, particularly in eyes with stromal opacities or deep vascularisation, where OCTA showed limitations in vessel detection and segmentation accuracy.11 Similarly, Ang et al found good quantitative agreement between AS-OCTA and ICGA, but their study also highlighted practical limitations, including the need for manual focusing, reduced sensitivity for axial flow, and frequent motion or projection artefacts.10 Nevertheless, some fundamental limitations remain when compared with dye-based angiography. ICGA provides insight into the maturity of corneal neovessels by visualising leakage and differentiating afferent from efferent vessels, features that are currently beyond the reach of OCTA. This distinction is clinically relevant, as vascular maturity determines susceptibility to antiangiogenic therapy and can guide targeted interventions such as fine-needle diathermy.6 7

Although the primary aim of this study was to analyse CoNV, the availability of an extended field of view with the swept-source platform allowed us to explore limbal and episcleral vasculature within the same imaging session. By modifying the ROI to encompass the entire limbal arc, we were able to analyse limbal perfusion patterns and evaluate potential changes in vascular signal following topical PE administration. This response was appreciable in all cases, highlighting the feasibility of using AS-OCTA for functional assessment of limbal circulation. These findings may be particularly relevant in clinical contexts where limbal perfusion is critical for diagnosis and management, such as acute ocular burns, where Dua’s classification relies on accurate detection of ischaemic sectors, or in chronic conditions like atopic keratoconjunctivitis, peripheral ulcerative keratitis and limbal stem cell deficiency.1225,27 While prior studies have investigated limbal vasculature using AS-OCTA,28,30 our ability to capture the entire limbal circumference in a single high-resolution scan represents a meaningful step towards practical, comprehensive evaluation of these complex disorders. Moreover, the wide-field, non-invasive nature of the technique allows for repeated imaging over time without the need for intravenous dyes, enabling longitudinal monitoring of disease activity. This opens the possibility of correlating changes in limbal vascularity with clinical progression or therapeutic response, thus supporting more personalised and timely management strategies.31

Additionally, we preliminarily assessed episcleral and scleral vessels, which were also included in the wider scan dimensions. Although not the primary focus of the present work, this exploratory analysis supports the growing interest in AS-OCTA for inflammatory diseases of the ocular surface, such as episcleritis and scleritis.32,34 Importantly, further optimisation of acquisition protocols, segmentation layers and scan geometry will be necessary to standardise anterior segment vascular imaging across these different anatomical territories.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small and included a heterogeneous group of patients with CoNV secondary to diverse infectious aetiologies as well as post-keratoplasty cases. While this reflects the real-world complexity of CoNV, it also introduces variability that may limit the generalisability of our findings. Additionally, due to the small sample size, inferential statistical results should be interpreted with caution. Second, although the BMizar platform offers significant advantages in acquisition speed, resolution and field of view, certain intrinsic limitations of OCTA remain. Lymphatic vessels, which are critically involved in immune modulation and graft rejection, cannot currently be visualised due to their lack of erythrocyte flow.35 Moreover, motion and projection artefacts although reduced by eye tracking may still affect image consistency in peripheral areas or in patients with poor fixation. Similarly, extremely low-flow vessels may remain undetected, potentially underestimating residual vascular activity.20 Furthermore, interobserver variability in image acquisition and segmentation was not formally assessed, which may impact reproducibility in wider clinical settings.

In conclusion, the system enabled high-resolution, depth-resolved imaging of CoNV across a wide field of view, with quantitative analysis revealing significantly increased vascular signals in diseased eyes and detectable short-term changes following pharmacologic vasoconstriction. Moreover, the extended imaging protocol allowed for the assessment of limbal perfusion and episcleral vasculature, highlighting the potential of this technique beyond the cornea, across a broader spectrum of anterior segment pathologies where vascular status informs prognosis and treatment planning. Continued development of image acquisition protocols, artefact correction strategies, segmentation algorithms and enhanced flow sensitivity will be essential to optimise diagnostic performance in clinical settings.
