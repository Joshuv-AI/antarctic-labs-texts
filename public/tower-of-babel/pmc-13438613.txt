APATCP: programmable multi-controller framework for real-time detection and mitigation of complex TCP flooding attacks in SD-IoT networks
Authors: Ashraf Alyanbaawi, Aya Hassan, Marwa M Khashaba, Osama Elkomy, Ehab Rushdy, Ameer El-Sayed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe exponential growth of the Internet of Things (IoT) has magnified vulnerability to Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDoS) threats, particularly advanced TCP flooding attacks that exploit protocol weaknesses to disrupt services and overwhelm constrained devices. Existing Software-Defined Networking (SDN) defenses often suffer from scalability bottlenecks, high controller overhead, and limited adaptability to evolving traffic dynamics. To overcome these challenges, this paper proposes APATCP, a P4-enabled, multi-controller SD-IoT framework for real-time adaptive detection and mitigation of TCP flooding. APATCP integrates four coordinated modules: Adaptive Collaborative Intrusion Defense (ACID) for decentralized intelligence sharing; Dynamic Traffic Anomaly Mitigation (DTAM) for adaptive thresholding and flow isolation; Dynamic Threat-Adaptive Classifier (DTAC), which leverages 24 P4-extracted features and an adaptive weighted ensemble classifier for complex multi-type attack classification; and FlowGuard-AP, a context-aware mitigation engine with dynamic response strategies. Evaluation across three IoT-centric datasets—CICIoT2024, Edge-IIoTset, and ToN_IoT—demonstrates superior performance, with APATCP achieving 99.3–99.98% accuracy, 98.2–99.5% recall, F1-scores up to 99.2%, and false positive rates as low as 0.4%. In binary classification, it consistently outperformed baselines such as LightGBM (97.5–97.8%) and XGBoost (96.7–97.1%). In multi-class evaluations spanning eight adversarial scenarios—including burst floods, hybrid multi-vector campaigns, and stealthy microbursts—the framework maintained > 98% accuracy while remaining operationally stable. Live emulations confirmed resilience under > 100k packets/s attack rates, keeping CPU load below 30%, packet loss under 2%, and end-to-end latency below 100 ms, while restoring throughput to over 95% of baseline within seconds. These results establish APATCP as a scalable, adaptive, and resource-efficient framework that delivers high detection accuracy, low overhead, and robust mitigation, ensuring reliable defense for next-generation SD-IoT infrastructures against increasingly sophisticated TCP flooding threats.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-62233-8.Keywords: SD-IoT, TCP Flood Detection, Multi-Vector Attacks, Real-Time Mitigation, Feature Extraction, ScalabilitySubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The Internet of Things (IoT) represents one of the most significant technological transformations of the modern era, interconnecting billions of heterogeneous devices across healthcare, transportation, manufacturing, energy systems, and smart cities1. By 2030, it is expected that nearly 29 billion devices will be part of this global ecosystem2. While this connectivity offers unprecedented opportunities for automation, data analytics, and innovative services, it also creates an expanded attack surface that exposes critical systems to evolving cyber threats3.

Many IoT devices are resource-constrained, lack embedded security features, and are often deployed in unattended or physically insecure environments4,5. Once compromised, these devices can be co-opted into massive botnets capable of launching disruptive Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDoS) campaigns6,7. Among these, TCP flooding attacks—particularly SYN floods—pose a serious threat by exploiting the TCP three-way handshake, overwhelming servers and network devices with half-open connections8. This not only exhausts bandwidth and memory resources but also prevents legitimate users from accessing services9. In Software-Defined IoT (SD-IoT) networks, such attacks can also overwhelm the Software-Defined Networking (SDN) controller by flooding it with flow setup requests, filling switch tables, and degrading overall system performance10.

The threat landscape has evolved beyond traditional high-rate floods to multi-vector, low-rate stealth attacks11. Examples include Slowloris-style attacks that maintain long-lived connections under detection thresholds, hybrid floods that combine TCP, UDP, or ICMP traffic, and sequential targeting strategies that cycle across different assets12. These advanced tactics render static threshold-based and signature-driven defenses ineffective13. Furthermore, centralized machine learning (ML) models trained on legacy data often struggle to detect emerging attack patterns, leading to high false positives and significant response delays14,15.

Traditional SDN deployments primarily rely on OpenFlow, which provides limited support for fine-grained state tracking and lacks the flexibility to adapt to fast-changing threats16. In contrast, the P4 programming language enables programmable data planes, allowing real-time packet processing, feature extraction, anomaly detection, and immediate mitigation17. When integrated with ML methods such as decision trees, support vector machines (SVMs), and deep learning, P4 enhances detection accuracy by capturing subtle flow-level behaviors, thereby enabling adaptive responses to modern TCP flooding campaigns18,19. To address these challenges, this paper proposes APATCP—a P4-enabled, multi-controller SD-IoT framework designed for the adaptive detection and mitigation of advanced TCP flooding attacks.

Problem statement and motivation

The growth of IoT has transformed industries from healthcare to transportation, but its reliance on lightweight, often insecure devices makes these networks especially vulnerable to distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks. Among them, TCP flooding remains one of the most disruptive, exploiting protocol weaknesses to overwhelm servers, controllers, and programmable switches. Traditional defenses—reliant on static thresholds, centralized control, or limited visibility—are struggling to keep pace with today’s adaptive and stealthy flooding strategies. This mismatch highlights the urgent need for a scalable, intelligent, and resource-aware defense framework.

Literature review

The integration of SDN into modern infrastructures has enabled flexible, programmable, and centralized network control. However, this paradigm has also introduced new vulnerabilities, especially susceptibility to DDoS attacks such as TCP SYN flooding. As a result, extensive research has focused on developing effective detection and mitigation techniques. An overview of these contributions is provided in Table 1. Kumar et al.23 investigated a protocol-level vulnerability in Multipath TCP (MPTCP) caused by manipulation of the Data Sequence Signal (DSS). They proposed a Data Sequence Map Skipping technique to detect such attacks. While effective in exposing DSS-based attacks, its applicability is limited since it is tailored specifically to MPTCP. Shah et al.18 addressed the Economic Denial of Sustainability (EDoS) problem by introducing EDOS-TSM, a detection model that uses binomial distribution analysis and TTL-based anomaly detection. This method reduces false negatives in spoofed and single-source attacks, although scalability and controller overhead remain unresolved. Das et al.24 employed a Random Forest (RF) model using OpenFlow port statistics, achieving 98% detection accuracy for SYN floods and enabling localized attack identification. However, its dependence on flow statistics introduces potential latency due to collection overhead.

Overview of detection methodologies for TCP flooding attacks in SDN.

Moving to P4-based detection, Musumeci et al.25 implemented machine learning models directly on P4-programmable switches, enabling real-time detection of TCP floods. While offering high precision, the solution is computationally demanding and narrowly focused on specific attack types. Sudar et al.26 proposed ML-TFAD, which combines SYN/ACK proxy validation with a C4.5 decision tree classifier. This dual-layer defense supports early detection and imposes minimal overhead on SDN performance. However, it introduces higher memory complexity at the controller. Sangodoyin et al.27 evaluated multiple classifiers—including CART, Gaussian Naïve Bayes (GNB), Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA), and k-Nearest Neighbor (k-NN)—on an emulated SDN testbed. The CART model achieved up to 98% accuracy with low computational cost. However, the lack of hyperparameter tuning and evaluation on broader datasets limits the robustness of their findings.

Neelavathy Pari et al.28 developed a multi-model AI framework integrated with the Ryu controller, demonstrating real-time detection capabilities. However, the work lacked a detailed comparative analysis across different classifiers, leaving questions about performance optimization. Similarly, Alzahrani and Alenazi29 proposed ML-IDSDN, an intrusion detection system using eight ML algorithms trained on custom datasets. While achieving high throughput and strong F1 scores, its applicability was restricted to specific attack categories and lacked evaluation on datasets. Shalini et al.30 applied a chi-square goodness-of-fit test in the POX controller to mitigate TCP SYN floods early. While effective in certain SDN environments, its applicability is limited, as it primarily addresses SYN floods and depends on specific controller architectures.

Hybrid approaches have also gained attention. Bensaid et al.11 combined Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference Systems (ANFIS) with SDN–fog integration in the FASA framework, achieving high precision and recall in real-time detection. However, the framework was limited to binary classification, restricting its use for multi-class or adaptive attack scenarios. Bosi et al.31 employed Random Forests and Gradient Boosted Machines (GBM) to classify TCP, UDP, and HTTP floods, achieving strong results, although their dataset was limited in diversity. Kapourchali et al.32 leveraged P4-enabled defenses to mitigate HTTP floods, demonstrating reduced latency and response overhead. Nonetheless, the approach increased computational load on P4 devices, raising concerns about scalability. Similarly, Mhamdi et al.33 combined deep autoencoders with Random Forest classifiers in a layered defense framework, which improved detection accuracy across multiple DDoS types but faced computational and scope limitations.

Recent research has explored diverse machine learning and deep learning approaches for intrusion and DDoS detection in SDN and IoT environments. A distributed anomaly detection framework leveraging Apache Hadoop and Spark with GPU-accelerated deep learning was proposed in34, achieving improved detection performance on large-scale log data. At the application layer, SQL injection vulnerabilities in web-based systems were analyzed in35, highlighting risks in database-driven infrastructures and emphasizing the need for robust security mechanisms. While focused on web applications rather than network flooding attacks, the study underscores the broader importance of layered cybersecurity defenses. Hybrid machine learning models have also been widely investigated. Iftikhar et al.36 proposed a SOM-based dimensionality reduction combined with XGBoost classification for intrusion detection on the NSL-KDD dataset, achieving significant improvements in detection metrics, particularly for DoS attacks. Similarly, Ali et al.37 introduced an SDN controller protection framework using PCA for feature reduction and SVM for DDoS detection, reducing computational overhead while improving accuracy.

More recently, Ramya Mall et al.38 introduced ProDetect, a dynamic threshold-based TCP verification mechanism that effectively blocks forged MAC addresses while minimizing collateral impact on legitimate traffic. However, response times increased at the onset of attacks. In parallel, Sinha et al.39 developed DDoSBlocker, which integrates time-based address mapping with ML-driven classification. The system achieved 99.71% accuracy with sub-second mitigation, but its detection scope was limited to host-based attacks, and future work is suggested to handle malicious switches.

In summary, prior research has made notable progress in the detection and mitigation of TCP flooding attacks using SDN. However, existing methods often suffer from one or more limitations, such as narrow attack coverage, computational overhead, reliance on centralized controllers, or limited dataset generalization. These gaps highlight the need for a decentralized, adaptive, and P4-enabled defense framework that can achieve real-time, multi-vector detection and mitigation in heterogeneous IoT environments—motivating the design of APATCP.

The volumetric flood category highlights traditional large-scale attacks that aim to saturate bandwidth and overwhelm system resources. APATCP mitigates these through DTAM’s adaptive anomaly thresholds and FlowGuard-AP’s resource-aware defenses, such as SYN cookies and selective rate limiting.

Stealth floods, such as Slowloris and PUSH floods, represent a more insidious threat by staying below static thresholds. APATCP counters these with DTAC’s stateful ML-driven detection, which extracts granular flow features, combined with DTAM’s baseline adaptation to detect subtle deviations.

In hybrid or multi-vector campaigns, attackers combine TCP floods with UDP or ICMP traffic, or shift between targets to confuse defenses. ACID’s decentralized coordination enables controllers to securely share alerts, enabling cross-domain collaborative detection. FlowGuard-AP then applies multi-layered countermeasures, tailoring mitigation to the evolving attack mix.

Evasion strategies, such as spoofed traffic, fragmentation, or amplification, pose a challenge for signature-based systems. APATCP addresses these through deep packet inspection in FlowGuard-AP, enhanced by DTAC’s feature-rich classifier and ACID’s synchronized intelligence sharing, which prevents adversaries from slipping between isolated domains.

Finally, botnet-driven distributed attacks represent the most serious scenario, where adversaries orchestrate cross-ISP or multi-domain campaigns. APATCP employs Decentralized Warning Conduits (DWCs) to synchronize defenses across distributed controllers, ensuring that large-scale attacks are contained quickly while minimizing service disruption.

By mapping its four integrated modules across these adversary categories, APATCP delivers a comprehensive, multi-layered defense capable of withstanding both conventional floods and modern adaptive TCP flooding strategies in SD-IoT networks.

The adaptive collaborative intrusion defense (ACID) module

The ACID module forms the foundation of the APATCP framework, providing a resilient and flexible defense layer against sophisticated TCP flooding attacks in IoT networks. Unlike conventional centralized approaches that concentrate security responsibilities on a single controller, ACID employs a decentralized, multi-controller architecture to eliminate single points of failure and enhance network robustness. A defining strength of ACID is its adaptive collaboration model, which allows it to seamlessly transition between localized, rapid responses and global, network-wide coordination. This dual capability ensures that the framework can respond quickly to immediate threats while maintaining broader situational awareness across distributed domains. To secure inter-controller communication, ACID integrates three key mechanisms: (i) Secure Communication Channels (SCCs): Encrypted links between controllers to protect sensitive data exchange, (ii) Real-Time Alert Channels (RTACs): Lightweight communication pathways designed for rapid distribution of alerts, and (iii) Secure Data Sharing Channels (SDSCs): Channels for exchanging aggregated statistics, network updates, and collaborative intelligence. The design of ACID is motivated by the limitations of traditional centralized defenses, which often become bottlenecks or single points of failure in large, heterogeneous SD-IoT deployments. By distributing defense responsibilities across multiple controllers, ACID enhances both resilience and scalability. Its adaptive collaboration modes enable flexible responses: in cases of localized anomalies, neighboring controllers collaborate via RTACs for rapid containment, while broader attacks trigger GCI via SDSCs for global coordination.

Lightweight communication protocols such as MQTT ensure that real-time intelligence sharing introduces minimal overhead. At the same time, dynamic key management keeps inter-controller communication secure against evolving threats. ACID also incorporates continuous optimization, enabling it to learn from past incidents and refine thresholds and detection models—making the defense increasingly robust over time.

Nevertheless, the complexity of managing multiple controllers and synchronizing their actions introduces challenges in deployment and requires rigorous validation. Despite this, ACID provides a proactive, adaptive, and scalable defense layer that strengthens the IoT ecosystem against sophisticated TCP flooding campaigns.

The dynamic traffic anomaly mitigation (DTAM) module

The DTAM module delivers real-time, adaptive defense against advanced TCP flooding attacks by combining state-aware traffic analysis with dynamically tuned mitigation strategies. Unlike traditional static systems that rely on fixed thresholds, DTAM continuously recalibrates its detection sensitivity based on evolving traffic conditions and the severity of anomalies. This adaptability enables it to respond effectively to both sudden, high-volume floods and subtle, low-rate intrusions.

At its core, DTAM leverages the Dynamic Anomaly Thresholding System (DATS), enabling traffic monitoring and baseline modeling directly at the switch level. By learning from live traffic patterns, the system adjusts detection thresholds in real time, minimizing false positives and ensuring that evolving attack tactics are quickly recognized.

A key strength of DTAM lies in its ability to enforce precise, targeted mitigation policies. Using P4-based traffic shaping and connection state tracking, it isolates malicious flows without affecting legitimate communication. This fine-grained control is particularly effective against TCP flooding variants that exploit the protocol’s session-oriented design, such as ACK floods, PUSH floods, and Slowloris attacks. Automated response triggers further accelerate mitigation, reducing disruption across the IoT environment and sustaining service availability even under persistent attack pressure.

The design contrasts sharply with static, threshold-driven defenses, which often produce high false alarm rates under fluctuating traffic conditions or miss stealthy attacks that remain below preset thresholds. By continuously updating baselines, DTAM not only strengthens detection accuracy but also ensures mitigation actions are aligned with the real-time behavior of each detected threat. The FlowGuard-AP module introduces a dynamic, decentralized mitigation framework for SD-IoT environments. Operating on P4-enabled switches, it provides real-time detection, isolation, and suppression of malicious traffic flows, ensuring minimal disruption to legitimate communication.

By leveraging Decentralized Warning Conduits (DWC), it rapidly disseminates alerts across IoT controllers and severs compromised devices from the network fabric, thereby reducing the spread and impact of attacks.

FlowGuard-AP begins by inspecting live traffic streams extracted from P4-enabled switches. When a match is found, the suspicious flow is classified as malicious and recorded in the system’s mitigation table. Once malicious traffic is identified, FlowGuard-AP executes targeted mitigation measures. This stage ensures both immediate containment and distributed awareness across the network.

Experimental settings and evaluation criteria

To validate the effectiveness, scalability, and real-time responsiveness of APATCP, we designed a comprehensive SD-IoT experimental environment integrating programmable P4 data-plane processing, distributed SDN control, wireless IoT communication, and heterogeneous attack traffic generation. The evaluation framework was constructed to emulate realistic SD-IoT deployment conditions, including constrained IoT edge communication, dynamic traffic behavior, and multi-controller coordination under adversarial conditions. Multiple IoT-focused datasets and programmable network components were incorporated to ensure reproducibility, a diverse set of attack scenarios, and a realistic assessment of both detection accuracy and mitigation efficiency. In addition to standard classification metrics, the evaluation also considers resource efficiency, latency, and deployment feasibility, which are critical requirements for practical real-time IoT environments.

Experimental settings

The proposed framework was tested and validated in an emulated SD-IoT environment built using Mininet-WiFi, a widely adopted emulator for wireless and IoT network scenarios. The experimental environment was specifically designed to emulate realistic SD-IoT deployment conditions by integrating programmable P4 switches, wireless IoT communication, heterogeneous traffic generation, and distributed SDN control. Mininet-WiFi was selected because it supports wireless station emulation, mobility-aware topologies, access-point configuration, and SDN integration in a controllable, reproducible environment. The adopted topology reflects common SD-IoT architectures, in which multiple IoT domains communicate via programmable edge switches coordinated by distributed controllers. In addition, the use of CICIoT2024, Edge-IIoTset, and ToN_IoT datasets enabled the framework to evaluate diverse IoT traffic behaviors and TCP flooding scenarios representative of practical smart environments, industrial IoT infrastructures, and heterogeneous edge deployments. The experimental topology, illustrated in Fig. 6, consists of six IoT domain sub-networks, each connected to a dedicated P4-enabled software switch (P4-1 to P4-6). These domain switches are aggregated into three higher-level switches, forming a scalable, hierarchical communication structure. Each domain contains multiple IoT devices communicating over wireless access points, while the control plane integrates two SDN controllers—a master controller (C0) and two redundant backups—to ensure fault tolerance and operational continuity.

The blueprint of SDN-Enabled IoT topology within the APATCP framework.

The programmable data plane was implemented using BMv2 (Behavioral Model v2), with P4 programs compiled through the P4C compiler and deployed onto the software switches. Control logic was provided by the Ryu SDN controller, interfacing with P4 switches via the P4 Runtime API to enable dynamic network reconfiguration. Redundant controllers ensured rapid failover in the event of a master failure, maintaining resilience under stress conditions.

For training and evaluation of the intelligent detection engine, we employed three publicly available and widely used IoT-focused datasets: CICIoT2024, Edge-IIoTset, and ToN_IoT. All datasets underwent preprocessing steps, including normalization and feature scaling, to enhance model accuracy and stability. The detection pipeline was designed as a heterogeneous ensemble of classifiers, integrating a set of ML models (DT, SVM, RF, kNN, GBT, NB). These base classifiers were combined using a meta-classifier implemented as a multilayer perceptron (MLP) with two hidden layers of 100 neurons each. The MLP was trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001 and a batch size of 32, and ran for 100 epochs to ensure convergence.

While CICIoT2024, Edge-IIoTset, and ToN_IoT are widely used benchmarks for IoT intrusion detection, it is important to note that they primarily provide packet- and flow-level features and do not include full control-plane telemetry such as switch flow-table updates, controller logs, or northbound/southbound signaling interactions. Therefore, certain controller-level orchestration behaviors in SD-IoT environments may not be explicitly represented in the dataset. Despite these dataset limitations, APATCP was designed to generalize across heterogeneous traffic patterns, and its P4-based in-network feature extraction helps mitigate the absence of explicit control-plane telemetry by capturing fine-grained flow-level behavioral signals.

The evaluation framework employed a comprehensive set of metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, false-positive rate (FPR), true-negative rate (TNR), false-discovery rate (FDR), and false-negative rate (FNR). This multidimensional approach enabled a robust assessment of detection effectiveness while capturing trade-offs between sensitivity and specificity.

The experimental setup further captures several operational characteristics of real SD-IoT environments, including distributed attack surfaces, latency-sensitive communication, and dynamic traffic fluctuations generated by heterogeneous IoT devices. Moreover, the inclusion of multiple TCP flooding variants—such as SYN floods, ACK floods, PUSH floods, and low-rate flooding attacks—provides realistic evaluation conditions for adaptive mitigation mechanisms operating in programmable IoT edge networks.

To mitigate abnormal behavior, we enforced a connection-limiting policy: any host attempting more than 100 incomplete TCP handshakes was automatically blocked from sending further SYN packets. The attack test scenarios

To evaluate the resilience and adaptability of the proposed APATCP framework, eight representative TCP flooding scenarios were carefully designed. These scenarios were constructed to reflect diverse adversarial behaviors observed in real-world IoT networks, ranging from high-rate floods to stealthy, application-layer evasion techniques. Each scenario stresses the framework under different traffic conditions, ensuring that the evaluation covers both conventional and advanced attack strategies. The scenarios (S1–S8) are summarized as follows:

Scenario 1: Focused Burst TCP Flood (S1-FBTF) This scenario emulates short but intense bursts of SYN and ACK packets targeting a single IoT device. The attacker overwhelms the target within narrow time windows, aiming to quickly exhaust connection resources. Such concentrated attack patterns allow us to assess the model’s ability to capture high-speed, burst-oriented anomalies.

Scenario 2: Coordinated ACK Stream Attack (S2-CASA) Here, multiple compromised IoT nodes collaborate to launch a distributed ACK-only flood against backend services. Since the traffic superficially resembles legitimate TCP acknowledgments, traditional stateful mechanisms struggle to detect it. This scenario evaluates APATCP’s capability to recognize stateless anomalies across distributed sources.

Scenario 3: Application Payload Saturation (S3-APS) In this scenario, adversaries inject high-rate TCP packets carrying payloads crafted to resemble legitimate application-layer traffic (e.g., HTTP POST requests, DNS queries). By embedding malicious intent within valid-looking payloads, the attack aims to bypass shallow inspection. This scenario examines how well the system handles sophisticated application-layer floods.

Scenario 4: Gradual Port-Scan Mimicry Flood (S4-GPMF) The attacker performs sequential floods across multiple ports, using irregular timing intervals that mimic stealthy port scans. This low-visibility attack is designed to blend into background traffic, making it challenging for simple threshold-based methods. This test measures APATCP’s temporal sensitivity and capacity to identify evolving multi-port anomalies.

Scenario 5: Multi-Vector Session Hijack Emulation (S5-MVSHE) This hybrid scenario combines TCP flooding with malicious session injection, interleaving crafted GET and DNS requests with port floods on 443 and 80. The complexity of blended attack vectors enables evaluation of the framework’s robustness when traffic heterogeneity is intentionally used to obscure the true intent of malicious flows.

Scenario 6: Sequential Adaptive Channel Flood (S6-SACF) The adversary adapts in real time, switching between TCP SYN floods, HTTP floods, and DNS queries as soon as mitigation responses are applied. This scenario highlights the importance of adaptive detection logic and tests the framework’s resilience against attackers that dynamically change their strategy mid-attack.

Scenario 7: Application-Control Channel Exploit (S7-ACCE) This scenario targets control-plane applications such as MQTT and DNS over TCP, exploiting recursive queries and large subdomain injections to overload service-level processes. The attack demonstrates how adversaries can abuse application-control channels to achieve denial-of-service without relying solely on transport-layer floods.

Scenario 8: Intermittent Microburst Resource Drain (S8-IMRD) This low-and-slow strategy leverages small, high-entropy microbursts of TCP packets injected intermittently to drain resources over time. Traditional detection systems often overlook such subtle anomalies. This scenario tests APATCP’s entropy-aware detection mechanisms to maintain robustness against stealthy, long-duration resource-draining attacks.

Comparative analysis with DL/RL-based DDoS defenses

To address the computational and architectural positioning of APATCP, a comparative evaluation is conducted against representative deep learning (DL), hybrid machine learning, and reinforcement learning (RL)-based DDoS defense frameworks reported in recent SD-IoT and SDN security literature. These methods were selected due to their relevance in either (i) controller-centric DL detection, (ii) hybrid DL-RL mitigation strategies, or (iii) edge/fog-based intelligent intrusion detection systems.

The selected baselines include: (i) DL + DRL hybrid SDN mitigation frameworks41, (ii) deep learning-based SDN detection models42, (iii) CNN/DBN-based intrusion detection systems43, (iv) hybrid DL-Markov edge detection models44, (v) transformer-based SAINT architecture45, (vi) fog-based multiphase entropy-ML frameworks46, (vii) classical ML baselines such as Random Forest and Decision Tree models47, and (viii) ONOS controller-based statistical detection systems48. These methods collectively represent the main design paradigms in modern DDoS defense: centralized intelligence, deep representation learning, reinforcement-driven adaptation, and fog/edge-assisted mitigation.

To ensure fairness, all methods are evaluated under the same SD-IoT environment used for APATCP, using identical P4-extracted feature sets, traffic traces, and preprocessing pipelines. Each model is assessed using consistent binary classification settings for DDoS detection. Where original implementations were unavailable, models were re-implemented following their reported configurations and tuned using the same training/testing split (80/20). Evaluation metrics include detection accuracy, F1-score, FPR, and average response time, which jointly reflect both detection effectiveness and computational efficiency—critical for real-time SD-IoT deployment.

The comparative results in Table 13 and the visualizations in Fig. 12 demonstrate that APATCP consistently outperforms DL, RL, and hybrid architectures in both detection performance and runtime efficiency. While transformer-based models such as SAINT45 achieve strong accuracy, they introduce significant inference latency due to attention-based computations, making them less suitable for real-time SD-IoT environments. Similarly, CNN/DBN architectures43 provide high classification performance but incur substantial computational overhead during continuous inference. Reinforcement learning-based SDN frameworks41 introduce adaptive mitigation policies; however, their training complexity and convergence overhead limit real-time responsiveness, particularly in dynamic IoT traffic conditions. Hybrid fog-based systems46 partially reduce controller burden but introduce inter-node communication delays and synchronization costs that increase overall response time. Edge-based hybrid models44 improve adaptability but remain constrained by partial reliance on statistical approximations, limiting robustness under multi-vector attack scenarios. Classical ML approaches47 and controller-based systems such as ONOS-Flood-Defender48 remain lightweight but lack adaptability to evolving attack strategies and exhibit higher false positive rates.

Resource efficiency evaluation

This section investigates the resource efficiency of the APATCP framework, examining how its P4-enabled data plane and multi-controller architecture reduce overhead compared to traditional OpenFlow-based systems. The evaluation focuses on (i) computational and memory efficiency in the data plane, and (ii) control-plane scalability under high-volume TCP flooding attacks.

(i) P4 vs. OpenFlow Baseline: The first comparison highlights the efficiency gained by migrating feature extraction into the P4 data plane. APATCP’s DTAM and DTAC modules extract 24 traffic features at line rate, forwarding only concise anomaly digests to controllers. In contrast, the OpenFlow baseline must transmit bulk flow statistics for classification.

At a flooding intensity of 100k packets/s (> 75% malicious traffic), APATCP sustained an average detection latency of 92 ms, compared to 181 ms for OpenFlow. CPU utilization at the controller was 28.7% vs. 55.2%, and bandwidth overhead fell by ~ 45% (18.4 Mbps vs. 33–41 Mbps). Table 14 summarizes these results.

By minimizing unnecessary control-plane communication and distributing processing across the data plane and multiple controllers, APATCP reduces sustained processing pressure on centralized controllers, thereby improving scalability and lowering the energy burden on edge infrastructure. Unlike many existing solutions, APATCP adopts an adaptive, architecture-aware design. By integrating P4-enabled programmable switches with distributed SDN controllers, it eliminates the bottlenecks and single points of failure that characterize centralized frameworks. This distributed coordination enhances scalability while supporting real-time traffic analysis across heterogeneous IoT domains.

A key differentiator is APATCP’s dual emphasis on adaptive monitoring and adaptive mitigation. Where most prior works focus primarily on detection, APATCP incorporates dynamic feedback loops that continuously refine mitigation logic based on evolving TCP flooding behaviors. This enables it not only to detect advanced floods but also to tune defenses reactively to minimize disruption while preserving service continuity.

Another strength lies in its dataset strategy. While many earlier studies rely on outdated or narrow datasets (e.g., DARPA, NSL-KDD, or synthetic traffic), APATCP employs CICIoT2024, ToN_IoT, and Edge-IIoTset, combined with P4-extracted live traffic features. This ensures better alignment with contemporary IoT attack surfaces and improves detection reliability against diverse flooding behaviors.

From a performance standpoint, APATCP leverages in-network processing, where detection tasks are executed directly in the data plane via programmable P4 switches. This design reduces latency and control-plane overhead, outperforming traditional frameworks that depend heavily on centralized analysis and slower mitigation mechanisms.

Empirical evaluations highlight APATCP’s superiority, achieving up to 99.98% accuracy across multiple scenarios—surpassing state-of-the-art methods in both precision and adaptability. These gains are attributed to the framework’s synergistic design, combining programmable data planes, ensemble learning, and dynamic controller collaboration.

In summary, APATCP addresses the shortcomings of prior methods by offering a scalable, adaptive, and resource-efficient architecture purpose-built for modern IoT networks. Its ability to dynamically detect and mitigate complex TCP flood variants sets it apart as a robust and forward-looking defense framework. Limitations and future works

The APATCP framework represents a significant advancement in defending IoT networks against advanced TCP flooding attacks, yet—as with any evolving system—it faces several challenges that warrant further investigation. Recognizing these limitations is essential for guiding future refinements and ensuring that the framework remains practical, scalable, and resilient in real-world deployments.

One limitation is reliance on simulation-based validation. Although Mininet-WiFi provided valuable insights into architecture design, programmable SDN orchestration, and system performance, software-based emulation cannot fully reproduce all characteristics of real-world IoT environments. In particular, wireless interference variability, hardware-level timing behavior, constrained battery-powered device operation, and low-power communication technologies such as ZigBee, LoRaWAN, BLE, and NB-IoT are only partially represented within Mininet-WiFi. Furthermore, large-scale emulation involving highly dynamic IoT nodes may introduce timing inaccuracies due to resource limitations on the host machine. Nevertheless, Mininet-WiFi remains widely adopted for SD-IoT experimentation because it enables reproducible evaluation, flexible topology configuration, wireless communication emulation, and programmable SDN integration at low deployment cost.

Another limitation relates to dataset representativeness. Although CICIoT2024 and similar datasets provide rich and diverse IoT traffic patterns, they may not fully capture controller-level telemetry, flow-table dynamics, or inter-controller coordination behaviors inherent in real SD-IoT deployments. In addition, labeling in these datasets is primarily device- or attack-type oriented, which may not explicitly reflect complex cross-device or multi-controller orchestration attacks. Furthermore, as with many benchmark datasets, benign traffic dominates the class distribution, and some attack scenarios are generated from scripted or replayed traffic patterns rather than fully adaptive adversarial behaviors. These factors may introduce distributional bias compared to fully real-world attack environments.

The integration of P4-enabled switches and real IoT traffic datasets in APATCP partially mitigates these limitations by enabling realistic packet-level analysis and programmable data-plane evaluation. Large-scale experiments involving heterogeneous IoT devices, wireless access points, and distributed botnets would provide a deeper understanding of the framework’s robustness under authentic attack conditions. In particular, evaluating APATCP against large-scale botnet-driven volumetric floods and stealthy application-layer attacks would validate its detection and mitigation strategies under stress. Future work will therefore focus on validating APATCP within physical IoT testbeds and low-power wireless environments to assess further performance under real deployment conditions and heterogeneous edge infrastructures.

Conclusion

This paper introduced APATCP, a P4-enabled, multi-controller framework for adaptive detection and mitigation of advanced TCP flooding in SD-IoT environments. Unlike centralized SDN defenses constrained by scalability and response delays, APATCP integrates four synergistic modules—ACID, DTAM, DTAC, and FlowGuard-AP—offering decentralized coordination, adaptive thresholding, ensemble-based multi-class classification, and context-aware mitigation.

Experimental results highlight APATCP’s strengths. On the CICIoT2024, Edge-IIoTset, and ToN_IoT datasets, it achieved up to 99.98% accuracy, 99.5% recall, and false-positive rates below 0.5%, consistently outperforming ensemble baselines such as LightGBM and XGBoost. Binary evaluations showed decisive margins over all baselines, while multi-class classification across eight adversarial scenarios (e.g., coordinated ACK floods, hybrid session hijacks, adaptive channel switching, and microburst drains) confirmed robustness, with accuracy consistently above 98%. Under live attacks exceeding 100k packets/s, APATCP preserved service quality, limiting CPU utilization to < 30%, packet loss to < 2%, and latency to < 100 ms, while restoring throughput to 95–98% of baseline within seconds.

A defining advantage of APATCP is its resilience and adaptability. Through Decentralized Warning Conduits and secure inter-controller collaboration, it eliminates single points of failure and supports synchronized defense across distributed IoT domains. Its adaptive feedback loops enable continuous recalibration of thresholds, classifier weights, and mitigation strategies, ensuring long-term viability against evolving adversaries.

Beyond academic contribution, APATCP provides practical value for IoT operators, critical infrastructure providers, and cloud-edge service ecosystems by minimizing downtime and safeguarding service reliability. While current work focused on TCP floods, future research will extend detection toward application-layer protocols, optimize scalability for ultra-dense IoT networks, and integrate threat intelligence feeds for predictive adaptation.

The low pipeline latency, reduced control-plane overhead, and distributed P4-enabled processing further make APATCP suitable for energy-aware, real-time SD-IoT edge deployments. Although the current evaluation was conducted using Mininet-WiFi-based emulation, future work will extend validation toward physical IoT testbeds and low-power wireless technologies to assess deployment feasibility under real-world operational conditions.

In conclusion, APATCP represents a comprehensive, future-ready defense architecture that unites programmable networking, adaptive machine learning, and decentralized intelligence sharing, significantly strengthening IoT infrastructures against the next generation of DDoS threats.
