Sustained response to guselkumab regardless of baseline characteristics in patients with active psoriatic arthritis and inadequate response to TNF inhibitors: results from the phase 3b COSMOS clinical trial
Authors: Iain B McInnes, Philipp Sewerin, Mohamed Sharaf, Michela Efficace, Frédéric Lavie, Miriam Zimmermann, Laura C Coates
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo prospectively evaluate the effect of guselkumab through 48 weeks across various clinical outcomes in subgroups of patients with psoriatic arthritis (PsA) and inadequate response to tumour necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi-IR) from the phase 3b COSMOS trial. Subgroups were defined by baseline demographics, disease characteristics and prior/ongoing therapies.MethodsPatients with active PsA (tender joint count (TJC) and swollen joint count (SJC) both ≥3) and TNFi-IR were randomised 2:1 to receive guselkumab 100 mg at week 0, week 4, then every 8 weeks through week 44 or to placebo with cross-over to guselkumab 100 mg at week 16 (early escape) or week 24 (planned). Guselkumab effect on joints (American College of Rheumatology (ACR) 20/50/70, enthesitis, dactylitis), skin (Psoriasis Area and Severity Index 90/100, Investigator’s Global Assessment 0/1), patient-reported outcomes (PROs) (Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy–Fatigue, Health Assessment Questionnaire–Disability Index) and composite outcome measures (PsA Disease Activity Score low disease activity, minimal disease activity) were evaluated by baseline patient age, sex, body mass index, SJC, TJC, PsA duration, %body surface area, C reactive protein, pain Visual Analogue Scale, number of prior TNFi and discontinuation reason, and conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drug status. Results are descriptive only.ResultsBaseline characteristics were similar between guselkumab (n=189) and placebo (n=96) groups. The benefit of guselkumab over placebo in achieving ACR 20 (primary endpoint; 50% vs 28%) and ACR 50 (23% vs 8%) response at week 24 was observed within all subgroups. Furthermore, response rates in the guselkumab group increased between week 24 and week 48 within almost all subgroups. Similar response patterns at week 24 and through week 48 were observed across various clinical outcomes.ConclusionsGuselkumab every 8 weeks led to consistent improvements through week 24 in joint, skin, PRO and composite outcomes versus placebo across diverse baseline-defined subgroups of TNFi-IR patients with PsA. Response rates increased or were durable through week 48 within most subgroups.Keywords: arthritis, psoriatic; patient reported outcome measures; fatigue

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Demographic and disease characteristics can impact the achievement of clinical outcomes in patients with psoriatic arthritis (PsA) receiving biological therapy. In the phase 3b COSMOS clinical trial, treatment with guselkumab every 8 weeks resulted in significantly superior efficacy versus placebo in patients with PsA and an inadequate response to one or two prior tumour necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi-IR) across key PsA disease domains in the overall population, including the primary endpoint of American College of Rheumatology (ACR) 20 response at week 24. Furthermore, an initial assessment of the impact of baseline characteristics on guselkumab efficacy showed consistent treatment effect for ACR 20 response within subgroups of patients defined by these characteristics.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Treatment with guselkumab led to consistent improvements at week 24, compared with placebo, in joint, periarticular manifestation, skin and patient-reported outcomes, and in the achievement of low disease activity states as assessed by composite outcome measures within diverse subgroups of patients with PsA and TNFi-IR. Response rates within the subgroups treated with guselkumab further increased or were generally durable through 1 year of treatment regardless of baseline patient demographics, disease characteristics and prior/ongoing therapies.

Introduction

Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a chronic inflammatory rheumatic disorder.1 2 In addition to evidence of psoriasis (including current psoriasis and personal or family history of psoriasis), psoriatic nail involvement, rheumatoid factor negativity, dactylitis and juxta-articular new bone formation,3 patients with PsA may also be affected by swollen, painful and stiff joints, enthesitis and fatigue.1 4 PsA also occurs in conjunction with several comorbidities (eg, cardiovascular disease, obesity and metabolic syndrome) that can each present additional therapeutic challenges.1 Several conventional and biological therapies are approved to treat PsA,5 with variable efficacy depending on baseline demographics and disease characteristics.4 6 Factors associated with worse patient outcomes include female sex,7 pre-existing obesity,8 increasing level of disease activity9 and disease duration9 10 and a history of inadequate response to tumour necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi-IR)11 12 or other advanced therapies.4 13 14 Of particular note, patients with PsA and history of TNFi-IR can be less responsive to and have lower treatment persistence with subsequent tumour necrosis factor inhibitor (TNFi) therapies or other biological therapies.11 13 Patient baseline characteristics can play an important role in guiding physicians’ and patients’ shared clinical decision-making.15

Guselkumab is a human monoclonal antibody that targets the p19 subunit of interleukin (IL)-23 and is approved for the treatment of moderate-to-severe psoriasis and active PsA.16 17 Approval in PsA was based on the phase 3 DISCOVER-118 and DISCOVER-219 clinical trials, which comprised a total population of 89.5% biological-naïve and 10.5% TNFi-experienced patients with PsA, wherein guselkumab treatment resulted in significant improvements in PsA signs and symptoms, compared with placebo, and the safety profile was similar for patients in the guselkumab and placebo groups. These results were corroborated in the phase 3b COSMOS trial, which focused on a population of patients with PsA who previously had inadequate response to one or two TNFi.20 In COSMOS, the primary endpoint of American College of Rheumatology (ACR) 20 response at week 24 was met, demonstrating a significant improvement in the signs and symptoms of PsA in patients with TNFi-IR treated with guselkumab compared with placebo (guselkumab 44% vs placebo 20%).20

A pooled analysis of DISCOVER-1 and DISCOVER-2 evaluated the impact of different baseline characteristics on patient outcomes in a primarily biological-naïve population.4 The treatment effect of guselkumab at week 24 was observed within subgroups defined by patient sex, obesity status, PsA duration or use of conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs) at baseline. Response rates increased or were generally durable through week 52.4 21 22 In these post hoc analyses of the COSMOS trial, we provide a comprehensive evaluation of the effect of guselkumab vs placebo at week 24 within subgroups of patients with TNFi-IR PsA across multiple PsA domains, as well as maintenance of response through 48 weeks. Subgroups were defined by baseline demographics, disease characteristics and prior/ongoing therapies.

Methods

Patients

Eligible adults had a diagnosis of PsA according to the classification criteria for PsA at screening, including active disease (tender joint count (TJC) and swollen joint count (SJC) both ≥3) and active (≥1 psoriatic plaque of ≥2 cm) or documented history of plaque psoriasis or current nail psoriasis, and had demonstrated lack of benefit or intolerance to one or two TNFi. Patients could continue stable baseline use of methotrexate (MTX), sulfasalazine, hydroxychloroquine or leflunomide, oral corticosteroids and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs/other analgesics. Targeted synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs were prohibited before and during study participation. Patients with active tuberculosis were excluded; patients with latent tuberculosis received appropriate prophylaxis.

Trial design

COSMOS was a phase 3b, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial (NCT03796858) conducted at 84 European sites from March 2019 to November 2020.20 The overall COSMOS trial design has been reported previously.20 As an overview, adults were randomised 2:1 to receive guselkumab 100 mg at weeks 0 and 4, then every 8 weeks through week 44 (final assessment at week 48), or to receive placebo at weeks 0, 4, 12 and 20 with crossover to receive guselkumab 100 mg at weeks 24, 28, 36 and 44. Patients with <5% improvement from baseline in both TJC and SJC at week 16 qualified for early escape; those receiving guselkumab continued randomised treatment (including a placebo dose at week 16 to maintain blinding) while those in the placebo group received guselkumab at weeks 16 and 20, then every 8 weeks through week 44. Patients who met early escape criteria could also initiate or increase the dose of one permitted concomitant medication, up to the maximum dose allowed by protocol, at the physician’s discretion.

Assessments

Efficacy assessments and outcome measures of the COSMOS trial have been reported previously.20 In these post hoc analyses, the following efficacy outcomes were evaluated at weeks 24 and 48: (1) ≥20/50/70% improvement in ACR criteria (ACR 20/50/70 response); (2) enthesitis and dactylitis resolution (Leeds Enthesitis Index (LEI)=0 and Dactylitis Severity Score (DSS)=0, respectively) among patients who had an LEI or DSS score of >0 at baseline, respectively; (3) ≥90/100% improvement in Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) score (PASI 90/100 response) in those who had ≥3% body surface area (BSA) and an Investigator’s Global Assessment (IGA) score of ≥2 at baseline; (4) IGA score of 0 (clear) or 1 (minimal) and ≥2 grades reduction from baseline (IGA 0/1) or an IGA 0 response, each among patients who had ≥3% BSA and IGA≥2 at baseline; (5) ≥4 points improvement from baseline in Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy–Fatigue scale (FACIT-Fatigue); (6) ≥0.35 points improvement from baseline in Health Assessment Questionnaire–Disability Index (HAQ-DI) score in those with baseline HAQ-DI score ≥0.35; (7) PsA Disease Activity Score (PASDAS) ≤3.2, indicative of low disease activity (LDA); (8) Disease Activity in Psoriatic Arthritis (DAPSA) score ≤14, indicative of LDA and (9) minimal disease activity (MDA), defined as achievement of 5/7 of the indicated criteria (TJC≤1, SJC≤1, PASI≤1, patient pain on a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS; 0–10 cm) ≤1.5 cm, patient’s global assessment of disease activity (0–10 cm VAS) ≤2.0 cm, HAQ-DI≤0.5 and LEI≤1).

Statistical analysis

Post hoc efficacy analyses were performed on the full analysis set from the COSMOS clinical trial, which included all randomised patients who received ≥1 dose of the study agent. Efficacy outcomes were investigated by the following baseline demographics, disease characteristics and prior/ongoing therapies: age (<60 or ≥60 years), sex (male or female), body mass index (BMI; <30 or ≥30 kg/m2), SJC and TJC (each <10, 10–15 or >15), PsA duration (<2, 2–<6 or ≥6 years), BSA (<3%, 3–<10%, 10–<20% or ≥20%), C reactive protein (CRP) levels (≤0.3, >0.3–1.0 or >1.0 mg/dL), patient pain VAS (<7.5 or ≥7.5 cm), the number of prior TNFi (one or two), reason for discontinuing prior TNFi (safety or efficacy) and csDMARD use (MTX or any other non-biological ongoing at baseline). Response rates at week 24 (comparing guselkumab and placebo) and at week 48 (for the guselkumab group) are presented; patients who discontinued and/or correctly met early escape criteria before week 24 were imputed as non-responders. The likelihood of patients treated with guselkumab, compared with patients treated with placebo, achieving week 24 response overall and for each subgroup was estimated via ORs based on Fisher’s exact test; 95% CIs were also calculated. The study was not powered to detect statistically significant treatment differences within subgroups; thus, p values are not reported. Response rates at week 48, also determined by imputing missing data as no response, were evaluated to determine the sustainability of group-level response rates over time within each patient subgroup.

Results

Patient disposition and baseline characteristics

In total, 285 patients with PsA and TNFi-IR were randomised. Among these, 189 were assigned to guselkumab and 96 were assigned to placebo. At week 16, 39 (21%) patients in the guselkumab arm and 45 (47%) patients in the placebo arm qualified for early escape. The remaining 51 (53%) patients in the placebo arm switched to guselkumab at week 24 as planned. Through week 24, 15 (8%) patients randomised to guselkumab and 8 (8%) patients randomised to placebo discontinued the study agent. Through week 44, 167 (88%) patients who were initially randomised to guselkumab, and 83 (86%) patients who were initially randomised to placebo, completed the study agent. Reasons for patient discontinuation have been reported previously.20

In the overall population, 76% of patients were <60 years old at baseline, 52% were female, 59% had BMI <30 kg/m2, 51% had PsA duration ≥6 years and 88% had one prior TNFi at baseline. Prior TNFi therapies were primarily discontinued for efficacy reasons (84%). Baseline demographics, disease characteristics and prior/ongoing therapies were generally comparable (≤10% difference) between guselkumab and placebo groups, although the guselkumab group included a numerically higher proportion (≥5% difference) of females and of patients with BMI<30 kg/m2, SJC>15, TJC>15, BSA≥20% and pain VAS≥7.5 cm, and a numerically lower proportion of males and of patients with BMI≥30 and ≥35 kg/m2, SJC<10, TJC<10, PsA duration 2–<6 years, BSA<3%, CRP≤0.3 mg/dL and pain VAS 3.5–7.4 cm, compared with the placebo group (table 1).

Post hoc analyses of clinical outcomes by subgroups

Previous findings from COSMOS described clinical outcomes in the overall patient population.20 Here, we further investigated these clinical outcomes within adequately sized subgroups of patients defined by baseline demographics, disease characteristics and prior/ongoing therapies. Subgroup analyses for ACR 70, IGA 0 and DAPSA LDA were not investigated further and are not presented in this manuscript because of the small numbers of patients receiving placebo or patients receiving guselkumab in the overall population who achieved these outcomes at week 24.

Joint outcomes: ACR 20/50 response

Overall, ACR 20 and ACR 50 response rates at week 24 were higher for patients receiving guselkumab compared with placebo (ACR 20, 50% vs 28%, OR 2.6 (95% CI 1.5 to 4.6); ACR 50, 23% vs 8%, OR 3.2 (95% CI 1.4 to 8.3); figure 1, online supplemental table S1).

ACR 20 and ACR 50 response rates at weeks 24 and 48, by baseline patient demographics, disease characteristics and prior/ongoing therapies (NRI). *No patient in the placebo group achieved the response at week 24; therefore, no statistical comparison was possible. ACR, American College of Rheumatology; BMI, body mass index; BSA, body surface area; CRP, C reactive protein; csDMARD, conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drug; MTX, methotrexate; NRI, non-responder imputation; PsA, psoriatic arthritis; SJC, swollen joint count; TJC, tender joint count; TNFi, tumour necrosis factor inhibitor; VAS, Visual Analogue Scale.

The benefit of guselkumab over placebo in achieving the primary endpoint of ACR 20 response at week 24 was observed regardless of patient age (ORs=2.3–3.8), sex (ORs=1.9–3.5) or BMI (ORs=2.4–2.6). Guselkumab was also more effective than placebo within patient subgroups defined by baseline SJC (ORs=2.3–3.4), TJC (ORs=1.5–4.1), PsA duration (ORs=1.8–6.6), %BSA (ORs=1.6–4.2), CRP level (ORs=1.9–4.6) or pain VAS (ORs=2.4–3.1). The response rate for patients treated with guselkumab was also numerically greater than for patients treated with placebo within prior/ongoing therapy subgroups, including those defined by the number of prior TNFi (ORs=2.5–3.8), reason for discontinuing prior TNFi (ORs=2.3–4.7) and ongoing csDMARD use (ORs=2.7–2.8).

Similar patterns were observed for ACR 50 response within baseline subgroups, whereby patients randomised to guselkumab were more likely than those randomised to placebo to achieve this important degree of improvement in PsA symptoms at week 24 regardless of baseline and disease characteristics (ORs=1.3–10.8 for age, sex or BMI; ORs=1.3–8.4 for SJC, TJC, PsA duration, %BSA, CRP level or pain VAS; and ORs=2.2–3.7 within subgroups relating to prior/ongoing therapies and reasons for discontinuing prior TNFi).

Among patients randomised to guselkumab, ACR 20 and ACR 50 response rates increased between weeks 24 and 48 in the overall population and within nearly all subgroups defined by baseline patient demographics, disease characteristics and prior/ongoing therapies.

Periarticular manifestation outcomes: enthesitis and dactylitis resolution

In the overall population, patients treated with guselkumab had higher response rates than patients treated with placebo for the achievement of enthesitis resolution (47% vs 23%, OR=2.9 (95% CI 1.4 to 6.1)) and dactylitis resolution (57% vs 36%, OR=2.3 (95% CI 0.9 to 5.9)) at week 24 (figure 2, online supplemental table S1).

BMI, body mass index; BSA, body surface area; CRP, C reactive protein; csDMARD, conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drug; MTX, methotrexate; NRI, non-responder imputation; PsA, psoriatic arthritis; SJC, swollen joint count; TJC, tender joint count; TNFi, tumour necrosis factor inhibitor; VAS, Visual Analogue Scale.

The response rate of the guselkumab group was numerically greater than the placebo group in achieving enthesitis resolution at week 24 irrespective of baseline patient demographic subgroup (ORs=1.5–6.5 for age, sex or BMI), within most baseline disease subgroups (ORs=1.0–10.5 for SJC, TJC, PsA duration, %BSA, CRP level or pain VAS), and within subgroups relating to prior/ongoing therapies and reasons for discontinuing prior TNFi (ORs=1.4–5.1).

Similarly, the numerical benefit of guselkumab over placebo in achieving dactylitis resolution at week 24 was observed within baseline patient demographic subgroups (ORs=1.2–4.2) and subgroups relating to prior/ongoing therapies and reasons for discontinuing prior TNFi (ORs=1.4–24.0). Patients treated with guselkumab were also more likely to have higher response rates, compared with patients treated with placebo, across most duration categories of disease, numbers of TJC/SJCs, %BSA and irrespective of CRP level or pain VAS (ORs=0.5–10.0).

For patients randomised to guselkumab, enthesitis and dactylitis resolution rates increased or were durable between weeks 24 and 48 in the overall population and within most subgroups defined by baseline patient demographics, disease characteristics and prior/ongoing therapies.

Skin outcomes: PASI 90, PASI 100 and IGA 0/1 response

In the overall population, the response rate for patients treated with guselkumab was higher than for patients treated with placebo in achieving a PASI 90 response (58% vs 11%, OR=10.8 (95% CI 4.2 to 32.6)) and PASI 100 response (35% vs 8%, OR=6.7 (95% CI 2.2 to 26.9)) at week 24 (figure 3, online supplemental table S2).

PASI 90 and PASI 100 response rates at weeks 24 and 48 by baseline patient demographics, disease characteristics and prior/ongoing therapies (NRI). *No patient in the placebo group achieved the response at week 24; therefore, no statistical comparison was possible. As was observed for joint and periarticular manifestation outcomes, guselkumab was more effective than placebo in achieving a PASI 90 response at week 24 regardless of baseline patient demographic subgroup (ORs=6.2–19.4 for age, sex or BMI), baseline disease subgroup (ORs=5.0–25.3 for SJC, TJC, PsA duration, %BSA, CRP level or pain VAS), and within subgroups relating to prior/ongoing therapies and reasons for discontinuing prior TNFi (ORs=9.7–16.0).

The numerical benefit of guselkumab over placebo in achieving a PASI 100 response at week 24 was also observed irrespective of baseline patient demographic subgroup (ORs=4.4–11.2), baseline disease subgroup (ORs=2.2–8.9) and within subgroups relating to prior/ongoing therapies and reasons for discontinuing prior TNFi (ORs=4.0–13.0).

For patients in the guselkumab group, PASI 90 and PASI 100 response rates increased or were durable between weeks 24 and 48 in the overall population and within nearly all subgroups defined by baseline patient demographics, disease characteristics and prior/ongoing therapies.

IGA 0/1 response rates at week 24 were higher for patients randomised to guselkumab than for patients randomised to placebo in the overall population and within the vast majority of subgroups evaluated (online supplemental table S2 and figure S1). Furthermore, IGA 0/1 response rates of patients in the guselkumab group increased or were durable between weeks 24 and 48 within all subgroups (online supplemental figure S1).

Patient-reported outcomes: FACIT-Fatigue and HAQ-DI response

Patient-reported FACIT-Fatigue and HAQ-DI response rates at week 24 were higher for patients treated with guselkumab than for patients treated with placebo (FACIT-Fatigue response, 49% vs 26%, OR=2.8 (95% CI 1.6 to 4.9); HAQ-DI response, 42% vs 24%, OR=2.3 (95% CI 1.2 to 4.3); figure 4, online supplemental table S3).

Patients randomised to guselkumab were also more likely to achieve a FACIT-Fatigue response at week 24, compared with patients randomised to placebo, within all baseline patient demographic subgroups (ORs=1.3–5.0 for age, sex or BMI), most baseline disease subgroups (ORs=1.0–7.7 for SJC, TJC, PsA duration, %BSA, CRP level or pain VAS), and all subgroups relating to prior/ongoing therapies and reasons for discontinuing prior TNFi (ORs=2.4–3.2).

The HAQ-DI response rate at week 24 for patients treated with guselkumab was numerically higher than for patients treated with placebo for the vast majority of baseline patient demographic subgroups (ORs=0.8–4.7), within most baseline disease subgroups (ORs=0.9–5.0), and all subgroups relating to prior/ongoing therapies and reasons for discontinuing prior TNFi (ORs=1.3–3.3).

For patients in the guselkumab group, FACIT-Fatigue response rates increased or were durable from week 24 to week 48 in the overall population and within almost all subgroups evaluated, and HAQ-DI response rates increased within all subgroups.

Composite measures of disease activity: PASDAS LDA and MDA

In the overall population, the week 24 PASDAS LDA and MDA response rates of patients treated with guselkumab were higher than those of patients treated with placebo (PASDAS LDA, 22% vs 7%, OR=3.5 (95% CI 1.5 to 9.7); MDA, 17% vs 4%, OR=4.7 (95% CI 1.6 to 18.7); figure 5, online supplemental table S3).

In accordance with the positive impact of guselkumab on skin, periarticular, joint and patient-reported outcomes, the numerical benefit of guselkumab over placebo in achieving PASDAS LDA and MDA at week 24 was observed within baseline patient demographic subgroups (PASDAS LDA, ORs=2.2–5.5; MDA, ORs=2.3–8.6), baseline disease subgroups (PASDAS LDA, ORs=1.5–10.4; MDA, ORs=1.4–6.7) and within subgroups relating to prior/ongoing therapies and reasons for discontinuing prior TNFi (PASDAS LDA, ORs=3.1–5.8; MDA, ORs=3.0–7.4).

Furthermore, PASDAS LDA response rates of patients randomised to guselkumab increased between weeks 24 and 48 within all subgroups evaluated, and MDA response rates increased or were durable within the vast majority of subgroups.

Discussion

The heterogeneous nature of PsA and the high number of comorbidities associated with the disease results in some patients having lower response rates to clinical outcomes than others.4 These post hoc analyses of the COSMOS trial were conducted to explore the effect of guselkumab versus placebo at week 24, and the maintenance of response through 48 weeks in the guselkumab group, across multiple PsA domains in subgroups of patients with PsA and TNFi-IR. We found that guselkumab treatment resulted in greater response rates, compared with placebo, in this population, regardless of baseline demographics, disease characteristics or prior/ongoing therapies. While trends or differences in response rates were noted within some subgroups, the study was not statistically powered to provide a meaningful evaluation of these differences.

Baseline factors that are known to decrease response to PsA treatment include female sex,7 obesity,8 longer disease duration,1 9 10 more-severe skin involvement,23 lower levels of CRP,24 a history of TNFi-IR11 12 20 and ongoing use of csDMARDs4 at baseline. In these post hoc analyses of patients with TNFi-IR, in the overall population, 24% of patients were ≥60 years old at baseline, 52% were female, 41% had BMI ≥30 kg/m2, 51% had PsA duration ≥6 years and 12% had two prior TNFi at baseline. Furthermore, 63% of patients had SJC<10, whereas 57% had TJC>15, suggesting potential pain sensitisation or fibromyalgia.25 The guselkumab group, compared with the placebo group, had a numerically higher proportion of female patients, patients with SJC and TJC>15, BSA≥20% and pain VAS≥7.5 cm at baseline, each resulting in poorer patient prognosis. Despite this, subcutaneous guselkumab 100 mg every 8 weeks resulted in numerically higher response rates than placebo at week 24 across multiple PsA clinical outcomes, including joint, periarticular manifestation, skin, patient-reported outcomes and composite measures of disease activity. Clinical outcomes were included in further subgroup analyses if sufficient numbers of patients receiving placebo or patients receiving guselkumab in the overall populations achieved the outcome at week 24. In the overall population and within diverse subgroups of patients, guselkumab response rates increased or were generally durable from week 24 to week 48, which is in accordance with previous guselkumab studies.418,20 These favourable results, coupled with the high proportion of patients randomised to guselkumab who completed treatment through week 44 (88%), suggest that guselkumab has a favourable benefit–risk profile for patients with PsA and TNFi-IR. The continued improvement of clinical response over time is particularly notable because of the decreased response rates typically associated with patients with persistent disease.11 12 The phase 3b SOLSTICE study will provide further information on the efficacy of guselkumab in patients with PsA and TNFi-IR by evaluating whether more frequent dosing (every 4 weeks vs every 8 weeks) results in an incremental clinical benefit.26

Patient age does not typically affect PsA treatment outcomes.27 28 However, late-onset PsA may have distinct characteristics (eg, higher baseline pain and fatigue scores, more comorbidities, and increased inflammatory markers), compared with earlier-onset PsA, which may result in different treatment outcomes for older versus younger patients.27 In the PsABIO real-world evidence study, no significant differences in effectiveness, safety or treatment adherence were observed for older (≥60 years) vs younger (<60 years) patients treated with ustekinumab (an IL-12/IL-23 inhibitor).28 Accordingly, in our analyses of the COSMOS trial, guselkumab was numerically more effective than placebo at week 24 across all clinical endpoints for younger patients, and across most clinical endpoints for older patients. Moreover, the response rates in the guselkumab group subsequently increased or were durable through 48 weeks across all clinical endpoints regardless of patient age.

It is well known that patient sex is a factor that impacts response to PsA treatment. Female patients with PsA typically have a longer disease duration, a higher BMI, higher disease severity scores, more-severe fatigue and poorer physical function at baseline than males.4 7 These factors generally lead to a poorer patient prognosis. In contrast, these analyses of COSMOS determined that both male and female patients randomised to guselkumab were more likely to achieve the evaluated clinical outcomes at week 24 than those randomised to placebo, findings that are consistent with those from DISCOVER-1 and DISCOVER-2.4 Furthermore, the response rates of patients in the guselkumab group increased between weeks 24 and 48 across all clinical endpoints irrespective of patient sex.

Another factor known to impact patient response to PsA treatment is obesity.8 Obese patients are more likely to have worse physical function and pain at baseline than non-obese patients.4 29 However, in DISCOVER-1 and DISCOVER-2, the benefit of guselkumab over placebo in achieving clinical outcomes was observed within all BMI subgroups.4 Similarly, across the clinical outcomes in this study, the response rates of patients treated with guselkumab were numerically higher than those of patients treated with placebo at week 24 regardless of baseline BMI (<30 or ≥30 kg/m2). The response rates of patients in the guselkumab group increased through 48 weeks across all clinical outcomes for both BMI subgroups.

In previous analyses, longer disease duration was associated with poorer patient prognosis.1 9 30 In the DISCOVER-1 and DISCOVER-2 clinical trials, guselkumab treatment was effective irrespective of PsA duration.4 Similarly, in the current post hoc analyses of the COSMOS trial, guselkumab was numerically more effective than placebo at week 24 across almost all clinical endpoints. From week 24 to week 48, the response rates of patients treated with guselkumab increased or were durable across the vast majority of clinical outcomes regardless of PsA duration.

It has been observed that patients with PsA and both joint and active skin involvement at baseline have an overall worse clinical state than those without active skin involvement.23 These patients experience more pain, have a higher overall number of PsA symptoms, develop a greater number of concomitant conditions and experience worse clinical outcomes.23 However, it is also known that patients with skin involvement derive greater benefit from guselkumab therapy than TNFi therapy.2 In our study, we observed that patients randomised to guselkumab were more likely to have higher response rates than patients randomised to placebo at week 24 within baseline %BSA subgroups across the vast majority of clinical outcomes. The response rates in the guselkumab group increased or were durable between weeks 24 and 48 irrespective of baseline %BSA across most clinical outcomes, highlighting the benefit of guselkumab treatment for patients with skin involvement.

The effect of baseline pain on clinical outcomes for patients with PsA is currently not well understood31; nonetheless, pain is a dominant and persistent symptom in PsA. Higher levels of pain are associated with decreased health-related quality of life and reduced physical functioning and work productivity and are often an indicator of more-severe PsA.32 In addition, fibromyalgia is often comorbid with severe fatigue in patients with PsA.32 Across most evaluated outcomes in the COSMOS trial, response rates in the guselkumab group were numerically higher than the placebo group at week 24, and over time from week 24 to week 48, response rates in the guselkumab group increased or were durable regardless of patient pain VAS at baseline.

Lower levels of CRP at baseline have been associated with lower retention rates and lower clinical response rates to TNFi therapy compared with higher CRP levels.24 Here, response rates in the guselkumab group were numerically higher than in the placebo group, regardless of CRP levels, across all clinical outcomes. Furthermore, for patients randomised to guselkumab, response rates increased between weeks 24 and 48 for all CRP subgroups across the vast majority of clinical outcomes evaluated.

The COSMOS trial was conducted using a population of patients with TNFi-IR; it is well established that patients with TNFi-IR have decreased response rates to subsequent TNFi11 and other advanced therapies.11 12 20 Importantly, in these post hoc analyses, guselkumab was more likely to be effective than placebo at week 24 irrespective of the number of prior TNFi (one or two) or reason for discontinuing prior TNFi (efficacy or safety) across the assessed clinical outcomes. Additionally, response rates of patients treated with guselkumab continued to increase through week 48 regardless of the number of prior TNFi or the reason for discontinuing prior TNFi. These results are noteworthy given that patients with TNFi-IR represent a population with a high burden of disease.

One potential limitation of these post hoc analyses is that they were not powered to detect statistically significant differences within subgroups, owing to their exploratory nature; OR values were used to determine whether patients randomised to guselkumab, compared with patients randomised to placebo, were more likely to achieve each outcome at week 24. Some subgroups of baseline characteristics had small sample sizes and, therefore, conclusions drawn from these subgroups must be treated with caution. Furthermore, the COSMOS trial only investigated outcomes through 48 weeks. As PsA is a chronic disease, and response to treatment generally increased in all subgroups of patients between weeks 24 and 48, long-term guselkumab therapy may be beneficial for patients with TNFi-IR.

In summary, guselkumab treatment resulted in numerically higher response rates, compared with placebo, across joint (ACR 20, ACR 50), periarticular manifestation (enthesitis/dactylitis resolution), skin (PASI 90, PASI 100, IGA 0/1) and patient-reported (FACIT-F, HAQ-DI) and composite (PASDAS LDA, MDA) outcomes for patients with PsA and TNFi-IR. The results were generally consistent within all patient demographic (age, sex and BMI) and disease characteristic subgroups (SJC, TJC, PsA duration, %BSA, CRP and patient pain) and within subgroups relating to prior/ongoing therapies and reasons for discontinuing prior TNFi. In the guselkumab group, the response rates increased or were durable from week 24 to week 48 across most clinical outcomes and within most subgroups. These results underscore the utility of guselkumab in addressing the unmet need of effective treatments with durable response rates across diverse groups of patients with PsA and TNFi-IR.
