The future of Asian biophysics built through Korea–Japan collaboration
Authors: Chihong Song
Published: 2025

Korea and Japan: Toward a shared research ecosystem

Even when countries, languages, and academic cultures differ, scientific research often forms connections far more naturally than one might expect. My experience conducting research while moving between Korea and Japan for more than two decades has repeatedly affirmed this simple truth. After graduating from Kobe University and beginning my research career at the Korea Basic Science Institute, I moved to the National Institute for Physiological Sciences (NIPS) under the National Institutes of Natural Sciences in Japan, where I spent approximately eight years carrying out Cryo-EM–based structural biology. During this period, I collaborated with Japanese researchers in diverse ways and experienced firsthand how readily scientific networks can develop. Continuous participation in academic activities such as the meetings of the Biophysical Society of Japan (BSJ) and the Japanese Society for Microscopy (JSM) helped these interactions grow gradually over time, and they continue to inform and support my current efforts to operate a Cryo-EM Research Center at Pusan National University School of Medicine and to contribute to the development of Korea’s structural biology and cryo-electron microscopy ecosystem.

Among the insights gained through these experiences, the one that has remained most profound is that differences in research culture between the two countries do not hinder collaboration; rather, they serve as complementary strengths and act as an important driving force for joint progress. For this reason, the announcement that the 2026 Annual Meeting of the BSJ would be held in Busan carried significance far beyond that of a typical academic event. It represents, in my view, a historic turning point—an opportunity for Korea and Japan to co-design a new collaborative model for biophysics in Asia. The meeting will be organized as the International Conference on Biophysics and Biomedical Science 2026 (ICBBS 2026), jointly led by the BSJ and Korean Societies for Biomolecular and Biophysical Sciences. This will mark the first time the two societies share a single international platform and co-develop an academic program, giving the conference added symbolic value. Unlike continental Europe, where geographical continuity fosters long-term regional integration, Japan and Korea are separated by sea and must carefully navigate their shared historical and cultural contexts. In this regard, the decision to co-host the meeting in Busan embodies a meaningful step toward substantive bilateral cooperation.

Japan’s biophysics community has grown steadily on the foundation of world-class research infrastructure, highly trained personnel, and a long-standing academic tradition. Decades of accumulated expertise in structural biology, single-molecule physics, membrane-protein biochemistry, and biomolecular engineering have provided Japan with a particularly strong footing. Representative achievements such as the development of high-speed AFM at Kanazawa University, the extensive membrane-protein structural and dynamics studies conducted at RIKEN, and single-molecule manipulation research at the University of Tokyo, Osaka University, and Kyushu University are widely recognized worldwide. The scale and history of Korea’s biophysics community are more recent, yet the field has expanded at an extraordinary pace in the past decade, making it one of the fastest-growing communities in East Asia. Korea’s rapid adoption of new technologies and agile execution have opened new avenues for collaboration with Japan, while Japan’s systematic and deeply rooted basic-science tradition continues to offer Korean researchers intellectual stimulation and long-term inspiration. Together, these complementary attributes form a solid foundation for sustained, meaningful, and future-oriented scientific cooperation between the two countries.

High-speed AFM (HS-AFM) may serve as an illustrative example. HS-AFM, pioneered by Prof. Toshio Ando’s group at Kanazawa University, was the first technology capable of visualizing dynamic conformational changes of proteins, enzymatic reactions, and membrane-protein motions in real time. It is widely regarded as one of the most iconic achievements in Japanese biophysics. The core components of HS-AFM including ultra-small cantilever design, high-speed scanner development, and active vibration–suppression technologies are the culmination of long-term technological innovation accumulated in Japan. Research in this area continues to advance, driven largely by the Uchihashi and Kodera groups, which are actively pursuing the ultra-fast dynamics of biomolecules. Japan thus maintains a firm global leadership position in HS-AFM. During my time at NIPS, I had the opportunity to collaborate with Prof. Takayuki Uchihashi while conducting Cryo-EM–based research, and through several co-authored publications I experienced firsthand the originality and potential of HS-AFM [1–6]. In contrast, only a limited number of laboratories in Korea specialize in AFM, and access to HS-AFM remains particularly challenging due to the high cost of instrumentation and the substantial technical barriers required for operation. Nevertheless, the demand for HS-AFM has continued to grow in Korea, especially in structural biology, protein dynamics, and single-molecule research. Given this growing need, establishing technical exchange programs between Korea and Japan would enable Japan’s strengths in HS-AFM to substantially contribute to Korea’s advancement in biophysics. Such collaboration could serve as a critical bridge that translates bilateral cooperation into concrete scientific outcomes.

Japan has also long played a leading role in the field of cryo-electron microscopy (Cryo-EM). Although Japanese researchers were not among the recipients of the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for Cryo-EM, Japan’s contributions to instrument development, specimen-preparation methodologies, low-dose imaging strategies, and electron-optical optimization are widely recognized internationally. Japan adopted advanced Cryo-EM technologies at an early stage, and its major research institutions have since developed a highly integrated national ecosystem for Cryo-EM research.

Korea, meanwhile, has rapidly expanded its Cryo-EM capabilities over the past decade, installing more than ten state-of-the-art microscopes nationwide. Single-particle cryo-EM studies of proteins and nucleic acids are now actively performed at major universities and national research institutes, supported by an increasing influx of young researchers and the steady expansion of training programs. In particular, the GSDC supercomputing infrastructure at KISTI has significantly alleviated bottlenecks in Cryo-EM workflows by enabling real-time data transfer, storage, and processing. This cyberinfrastructure has enabled efficient data sharing, reduced disparities in instrument access, and fostered multi-institutional collaboration. It has also enabled individual laboratories—even without dedicated computation clusters—to perform Cryo-EM data analysis, thereby playing a critical role in the explosive growth of Korea’s Cryo-EM ecosystem.

Another major Cryo-EM subfield, Micro-ED, is still in its early stages in Korea but holds strong potential for applications in drug discovery, natural-product structure determination, and small-molecule crystallography. During my time at NIPS, I collaborated with Prof. Haruki Sugiyama at the Institute for Molecular Science (IMS) to establish a Micro-ED workflow on a newly installed 300 kV Cryo-TEM [7]. Through this process, I received valuable technical guidance from researchers at KEK, Osaka University, and the University of Tsukuba, all of whom had extensive operational experience in Micro-ED. This collaboration demonstrated how Japan’s deeply rooted culture of technical sharing significantly enhances the competitiveness of its research institutions.

In situ cellular Cryo-EM including CLEM, Cryo-FIB milling, and Cryo-electron tomography is expected to become one of the most rapidly expanding Cryo-EM subfields. Both Japan and Korea are actively preparing for this growth. In Korea, institutions such as Pusan National University, KAIST, Seoul National University, and POSTECH already operate Cryo-FIB instruments, and additional systems will soon be installed at the Institute for Basic Science (IBS) and the Catholic University of Korea. With this infrastructure in place, regular bilateral technical workshops could establish the largest and most dynamic Cryo-ET research network in Asia. Because in situ Cryo-EM remains a relatively young field worldwide, a jointly developed platform between Japan and Korea has the potential to evolve into an internationally recognized model.

Japan, through the AMED-BINDS program, operates a nationally integrated life-science research platform encompassing Cryo-EM, NMR, X-ray crystallography, mass spectrometry, and computational biology. Established in 2017, AMED-BINDS enables Japanese researchers to access state-of-the-art analytical instruments efficiently, regardless of institutional ownership. Core institutions participate in this unified national platform. Researchers apply through an online portal, undergo project review, and are matched with expert technical support. The platform deploys dedicated technical staff for Cryo-EM, NMR, X-ray, and computational analysis, enabling seamless support from specimen preparation to data processing and structural modeling. Training programs including workshops, specimen-preparation courses, and data-analysis education are also offered regularly for students and researchers.

This integrated Japanese model aligns closely with the goals of Korea’s emerging national-scale infrastructure initiatives such as NEXUS and CELINE. However, unlike Japan, Korea is still in the early stages of developing a national system for rotating technical personnel, shared data-standardization platforms, and centralized support structures. The AMED-BINDS model suggests that Korea must advance beyond equipment-oriented policies to establish a fully integrated national cycle of personnel training, instrument-operation standardization, data-sharing frameworks, and educational programs. This transition is essential not only for securing advanced infrastructure but also for enhancing research productivity, strengthening core technical competencies, and elevating the quality of international collaboration.

Integrated research practices in Japan

During my time at NIPS, I worked closely with the National Institute for Basic Biology (NIBB), the Institute for Molecular Science (IMS), and several university laboratories, which allowed me to engage deeply with Japan’s broad and well-established interdisciplinary biophysics community. The NIPS–NIBB–IMS research complex in Okazaki provides a uniquely integrated environment in which electron microscopy, optical measurement, single-molecule biophysics, computational science, and molecular biology operate in close connection both physically and organizationally. NIPS has long served as an important center for electron microscopy in Japan, having historically housed a high-voltage electron microscope (HVEM) [8] and contributing significantly to the development of phase-plate TEM technology through the pioneering work of Prof. Kuniaki Nagayama [9]. Building upon this foundation, a coordinated joint-use system was established, and under the guidance of Prof. Kazuyoshi Murata, the Cryo-EM program expanded rapidly, accompanied by the introduction of advanced instruments [10] and a marked increase in collaborative research activity. My experience working within Professor Murata’s research group enabled me to take part in many of these projects and provided firsthand exposure to a collaboration-oriented research environment that is not easily found in Korea.

Among the collaborations I took part in, several remain especially memorable. One was a project with Prof. Koichi Kato, using samples from the International Space Station to study how amyloid fibrils form under microgravity [4]. Cryo-EM revealed unique structural features that could not be observed under normal gravity. Another project examined how CAHS proteins in tardigrades self-assemble into protective condensates during desiccation, suggesting a key molecular mechanism for their extreme drought tolerance [2]. This study showed me how scientific curiosity and public interest can naturally come together especially when I later saw our joint work introduced on a Korean science YouTube channel (https://youtu.be/8-bAV1WKpBg?si=lV8WzU8qGqjM8oUB).

I also remember very clearly my collaboration with Prof. Ryota Iino at IMS on ATPase dynamics. This work aimed to analyze the rotational movement and catalytic cycle of membrane ATPases at the single-molecule level, combining Cryo-EM with highly sensitive single-molecule rotation assays [11,12]. Prof. Ryota Iino’s lab is well known worldwide for research on ATPase and other rotary motors, and their techniques can capture fast dynamics with nanosecond-to-millisecond precision. Through this collaboration, I learned how to integrate structural data with single-molecule kinetic measurements, and this experience continues to influence my research today.

In addition, the photosystem research I carried out with Prof. Jun Minagawa and Prof. Ryutaro Tokutsu at NIBB was another meaningful collaboration. This project investigated how the Photosystem II–LHCII supercomplex reorganizes under different light conditions to adjust energy distribution in green algae and plants [13–16]. The Minagawa group is internationally recognized for studies on LHCII remodeling, and pigment–protein interactions. Through this work, I was able to understand how structural changes affect energy-conversion efficiency.

Together, these studies highlighted the strength of Japan’s highly interconnected research environment and profoundly shaped my own scientific perspective and approach.

ICBBS 2026 and the future of Asian biophysics

Japan reaffirmed its international standing as a major nation in basic science with the award of two Nobel Prizes in 2025. This achievement reflects decades of sustained national investment in fundamental research, the systematic development of scientific talent, and a stable infrastructure for supporting long-term inquiry. Collaboration with a country that maintains such a foundation carries strategic value that extends beyond simple technology transfer; it provides an opportunity to enhance the global visibility and influence of life science research within Asia as a whole.

Although the decline in overseas study among younger Japanese researchers has recently become a matter of concern, Korea and Japan are geographically close and share relatively low linguistic and cultural barriers. Increased exchange between young researchers in the two countries has the potential to introduce new diversity and momentum into the Japanese research community. At the same time, Korea has rapidly expanded its research capacity through advances in Cryo-EM, cryogenic specimen-preparation techniques, high-performance computational infrastructure, and rapid technology adoption. These developments, together with the growing participation of younger scientists, are helping Korea establish a more balanced and reciprocal partnership with Japan.

Biophysics, as a discipline that seeks to explain phenomena from the molecular to the tissue level through physical principles, inherently depends on collaboration among researchers across multiple fields. For this reason, cooperation between Japan and Korea represents far more than routine academic exchange; it marks an essential step in establishing a distinct and unified Asian presence within the global biophysics community.

Finally, I sincerely hope that ICBBS 2026, to be held in Busan, will serve as a dynamic venue for new interactions and lasting partnerships, enabling researchers from both countries to jointly shape the future of biophysics in Asia.
