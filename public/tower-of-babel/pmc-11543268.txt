Bilateral Dentate Nuclei Hyperintensities and Response to 4-Aminopyridine in a Patient With Childhood-Onset GAA-FGF14-Related Ataxia
Authors: Pierfrancesco Mitrotti, Elisa Vegezzi, Roberta Zangaglia, Ilaria Palmieri, Marie-Josée Dicaire, Simone Gana, Sayna Rahimi Aghdas, Silvia Nicolosi, Anna Pichiecchio, Cristina Tassorelli, Enza Maria Valente, Micol Avenali
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo report a novel imaging finding of bilateral dentate nuclei hyperintensities in a case of childhood-onset GAA-FGF14–related ataxia (spinocerebellar ataxia 27B, SCA27B) and response to 4-aminopyridine (4-AP).MethodsA 53-year-old woman with unsolved progressive cerebellar ataxia of childhood onset underwent clinical and imaging assessment and extensive genetic investigation.ResultsAfter excluding Friedreich ataxia, most common spinocerebellar ataxia–related expansions, and pathogenic variants in ataxia-related genes through exome sequencing, targeted long-range PCR and repeat-primed PCR analysis revealed a heterozygous pathogenic (GAA)302 expansion in FGF14. Brain MRI showed bilateral dentate nuclei hyperintensities and peridentate white matter degeneration, a feature never reported before in SCA27B. Gait ataxia and frequency of falls improved after starting 4-AP.DiscussionWe confirm that SCA27B, initially considered a late-onset condition, can present with very early onset in childhood and describe a novel imaging feature of this common hereditary ataxia. Previous imaging studies had described a spectrum of findings, variably including cerebellar vermian and hemispheric atrophy, hyperintensities of the superior cerebellar peduncles, cerebral and brainstem atrophy, ventricular enlargement, and corpus callosum thinning. In this case, T2/FLAIR bilateral dentate nuclei hyperintensities and peridentate white matter degeneration expand the neuroradiologic spectrum associated with GAA-FGF14–related ataxia of long duration.

Introduction

The FGF14 gene encodes a protein belonging to the intracellular fibroblast growth factor subfamily, highly expressed in cerebellar granules and Purkinje cells, where it regulates the excitability of voltage-gated sodium channels.1

Pathogenic GAA expansion of the FGF14 gene is one of the most common causes of adult-onset spinocerebellar ataxia (SCA27B; GAA-FGF14–related ataxia), characterized by a slowly progressive pancerebellar syndrome with late onset (median 60 years), prevalent gait involvement, and presence of episodic symptoms.2 In most patients with SCA27B, brain MRI shows cerebellar atrophy, mainly involving the vermis but also cerebellar hemispheres. Additional features include hyperintensities of the superior cerebellar peduncles, cerebral atrophy, ventricular enlargement, corpus callosum thinning, and brainstem atrophy.3

We report a patient with early onset of GAA-FGF14–related ataxia, showing cerebellar and cerebral atrophy associated with bilateral dentate nucleus hyperintensities; we also describe response to 4-AP started after many years from the onset.

Case Report

We report the case of a 53-year-old woman of Italian-Canadian origin. Her family history was negative for neurologic disorders, and her parents were not consanguineous.

She had appropriately achieved the neurodevelopmental milestones and exhibited a slowly progressing gait problem from the age of 11, associated with upper limb incoordination and vertigo that gradually worsened through adolescence. At age 14, she experienced the first fall and complained of moderate fine motor disability. The patient observed an increase in fall frequency, which eventually limited her daily activities (details of clinical history in eAppendix 1).

A first neurologic examination at age 29 showed mild axial and appendicular ataxia, downbeat nystagmus, and normal cognitive functions; however, the patient was still able to walk independently for mid-long distances. Brain MRI performed at the same age revealed significant widening of vermian and hemispherical cerebellar sulci, mild fourth ventricle enlargement, and degeneration of pontine transversal fibers; no other alterations of signal were reported at this stage, no signal alterations have been detected in the FLAIR/T2 sequences.

During her 30s–40s, gait instability and the number of falls gradually worsened; a very modest difficulty with speech articulation appeared at age 45. We first assessed the patient at age 51. She denied paroxysmal symptoms, migraine, nausea, seizures, or visual and hearing disturbances. The patient complained of considerable difficulty in daily life activities due to significant walking instability, required a walker for medium-to-long distances, and experienced multiple falls, sometimes daily. No autonomic dysfunctions were reported. A general physical examination did not reveal telangiectasias or xanthomas. On neurologic evaluation, she exhibited a very mild dysarthric speech, high-frequency head tremor, saccadic smooth pursuit, slowed and dysmetric horizontal saccades, bilateral deficit of vestibulo-ocular reflexes on the bedside head impulse test, and a vertical and left gaze-evoked nystagmus. The head-shaking test resulted negative. No signs of oculomotor apraxia were present. Bilateral upper limbs postural and action tremor with clumsy, staggering movements and a wide-based gait characterized by mild-to-moderate instability were also present. Mild hyperreflexia in the lower limbs was detected, without spasticity or sensory system impairment (FARS stage 3.5, full score 41) (Video).

Neuropsychological testing revealed a mild depressive disorder with anxiety-compulsive traits beginning around the age of 40, with clinical improvement after starting citalopram 20 mg.

Sensory and motor conduction velocities, EMG, and somatosensory-evoked potentials were normal.

Specific laboratory examinations (alpha-fetoprotein, albumin, very long chain fatty acids, copper, cholestanol, vitamin E, HIV, syphilis screening, and autoimmunity) were normal.

A new brain MRI scan was performed that revealed marked cerebellar atrophy mainly involving the superior vermis and hemispheres (Figure, A–C), associated with T2/fluid-attenuated inversion recovery signal hyperintensity in the hilum of dentate nuclei, peridentate, and superior cerebellar peduncle white matter (Figure, A). The bilateral subarachnoid spaces of the parietal regions were mildly enlarged (Figure, D and E).

Genetic testing for the most common hereditary ataxia-related expansions (SCA1, SCA2, SCA3, SCA6, and Friedreich ataxia) and exome sequencing (ES) were all negative. After the identification of FGF14 GAA repeat expansion as the cause of SCA27B,2 targeted long-range PCR and repeat-primed PCR were performed revealing a heterozygous pathogenic expansion (GAA)302.

Given the documented response to 4-AP treatment,4 we started it with a dosage of 10 mg twice daily. 2 months later, the patient reported significant improvement in gait ataxia (Video) and a marked reduction in weekly falls.

Discussion

Our SCA27B case presented 2 main peculiarities: age at onset and the neuroimaging findings.

First, the patient showed onset of ataxia in late childhood (11 years), which is one of the youngest ages at onset reported to date. GAA-FGF14–related ataxia is typically a late-onset disorder, with median onset in the sixth-seventh decade,2 although earlier presentations before age 30 have occasionally been reported.5,6 A single pediatric-onset SCA27B case (6 years) with a GAA-FGF14 expansion of 352 repeats has been reported, without neuroimaging available.5,7 A previous work identified pathogenic GAA-FGF14 expansions in 6 early-onset patients (12-35 years) with permament gait ataxia and dysarthria without episodic symptoms; however, 5 of them exhibited a complex phenotype and carried an additional pathogenic variant in a distinct gene, suggesting alternative diagnoses.7

Expansion of short tandem repeats in the case of maternal transmission was found in SCA27B2; however, further studies are needed to clarify whether and to what extent this phenomenon affects the age at onset. A table summarizing all published cases of early-onset ataxia is available in the supplementary file (eTable 1).

Second, we described the occurrence of bilateral cerebellar dentate nuclei hyperintensities and peridentate white matter degeneration as part of the imaging spectrum of SCA27B. Typical MRI findings of this condition include atrophy of cerebellar vermis and hemispheres or isolated vermian atrophy. In some patients, a reduced brainstem anteroposterior diameter, cerebral atrophy, ventricular enlargement (especially of the fourth ventricle), and superior cerebellar peduncle white matter involvement have also been reported.3

The presence of infratentorial T2 signal abnormalities such as dentate nuclei hyperintensities, in association with global cerebellar atrophy, is a well-known radiologic pattern suggestive of other rare hereditary degenerative ataxias such as cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis, ataxia with oculomotor apraxia 1 and 2, spastic paraplegia type 7, and spinocerebellar ataxia type 10.8 Of note, dentate hyperintensities can also be found in toxic-induced cerebellar dysfunction (e.g., metronidazole) or other rare genetic and sporadic neurologic disorders.9 In this case, however, we did not find any clinical or genetic evidence suggestive of a dual diagnosis because SCA testing and ES failed to disclose any additional pathogenic variants.

Forthcoming extensive whole-genome sequencing analyses in inherited ataxias may uncover cryptic variants, such as structural variations, deep intronic variants, or novel pathogenic repeats, possibly concurring to the wide phenotypic spectrum of these conditions, encompassing atypical cases such as the one presented here.

To date, neuropathologic studies of GAA-FGF14–related ataxia have been performed only in 3 patients, showing cerebellar cortical atrophy, especially of the vermis, a widespread loss of Purkinje cells, and degeneration of granule cell and molecular layers, with sparing of dentate nuclei.2,4 The observation of dentate nuclei hyperintensities in our patient highlights the possibility that alterations of these nuclei could be a hallmark of neurodegeneration (e.g., gliosis and iron deposition) also in SCA27B, as in other inherited SCAs.10 Only one other study reported changes in the dentate nuclei as bilateral hypointensities,11 although the pathologic implications of these findings require further investigation.

We also demonstrated a positive response to 4-AP, even years after onset. The long-term disease course of patients carrying FGF14 expansions still requires extensive exploration. Comprehensive neuroradiologic and pathologic studies on larger cohorts of patients are warranted to elucidate the neuronal damage pattern in SCA27B and the underlying pathogenetic mechanisms.
