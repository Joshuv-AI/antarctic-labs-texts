Power convexity of solutions to complex Monge-Ampère equation in ℂ 2 \mathbb{C}^{2}
Wei Zhang, Qi Zhou

Abstract
. The convexity of solutions to boundary value problems for fully nonlinear elliptic partial differential equations (such as real or complex k k -Hessian equations) is a challenging topic. In this paper, we establish the power convexity of solutions to the Dirichlet problem for the complex Monge-Ampère equation on bounded, smooth, strictly convex domain in ℂ 2 \mathbb{C}^{2} . Our approach is based on the constant rank theorem and the deformation process.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification. Primary 35B50; Secondary 32W20.

Keywords and phrases. Power convexity, complex Monge-Ampère equation, constant rank theorem.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Main result

In 1971, Makar-Limanov
[
35
]
studied the Dirichlet problem for the Laplace equation

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta u=-1&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega,\\
\hskip 8.53581ptu=0&\mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a bounded, strictly convex domain with smooth boundary. He proved that the function
\sqrt{u}
is strictly concave in
\Omega
. Recall that the unique solution
u
of the Dirichlet problem (
1.1
) is commonly referred to as the torsion function of
\Omega
. That is to say, Makar-Limanov
[
35
]
established the
1/2
-concavity of the torsion function of a planar bounded convex domain. Since then, many authors have investigated the convexity of solutions to various kinds of elliptic partial differential equations (PDEs); see Section
1.2
below.

In this work, we will explore the convexity of solutions of the Dirichlet problem for the complex Monge-Ampère equation. The complex Monge-Ampère equation is a fully nonlinear elliptic PDE of the form

\det(u_{i\bar{j}})=f,

where
u
is a plurisubharmonic function, and
(u_{i\bar{j}})
is its complex Hessian in local holomorphic coordinates. This equation plays a fundamental role in complex geometry, Kähler geometry, and fully nonlinear PDE theory.

Let us first recall the solvability of the Dirichlet problem for complex Monge-Ampère equation on bounded domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a bounded strongly pseudoconvex domain with smooth boundary, and that
0<\psi\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})
,
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
. Then Caffarelli, Kohn, Nirenberg and Spruck
[
11
]
proved that the Dirichlet problem

(1.2)

\begin{cases}\det(u_{i\bar{j}})=\psi&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega,\\
\hskip 31.29802ptu=\varphi&\mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega,\end{cases}

admits a unique strictly plurisubharmonic solution
u\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})
.

Our goal in this paper is to investigate the power convexity of the unique solution
u
to problem (
1.2
) when
\psi=1
,
\varphi=0
and
n=2
. Precisely, our main result is as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
be a bounded, strictly convex domain with smooth boundary. Suppose that
u\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})
is the unique solution of Dirichlet problem

(1.3)

\begin{cases}\det(u_{i\bar{j}})=1&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega,\\
\hskip 31.29802ptu=0&\mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Then the function
v=-\sqrt{-u/2}
is strictly convex in
\Omega
.

Remark 1.2
.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first known result addressing the power convexity of solutions to complex Monge-Ampère equations. For
n\geq 3
, whether the unique solution of Dirichlet problem (
1.3
) exhibits the power convexity property remains an open question.

1.2.
Previous works

Convexity has long been a fundamental and intriguing topic in the study of elliptic PDEs. Over the decades, many authors have made significant contributions to this field. Below, we provide a brief overview of the historical developments on the convexity of solutions to elliptic PDEs.

As we mentioned earlier, the
1/2
-concavity of the two-dimensional torsion function was first studied by Makar-Limanov
[
35
]
. His key idea was to introduce the auxiliary function

P=v^{4}\det(\nabla^{2}v),

where
v=\sqrt{u}
and
u
satisfies the Dirichlet problem (
1.1
). He then could show that
P
is superharmonic in
\Omega
. In 1976, Brascamp and Lieb
[
7
]
proved the log-concavity of the first Dirichlet eigenfunction of the Laplacian of a bounded convex domain by parabolic method. Acker, Payne and Philippin
[
1
]
later provided an alternative proof of Brascamp and Lieb’s result along the route of Makar-Limanov
[
35
]
for the two-dimensional case.

In the early 1980s, Korevaar
[
27
,
28
]
made a breakthrough in studying the convexity of solutions to linear and quasilinear elliptic equations. Specifically, for a bounded convex domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a solution
u
, he introduced the convexity test function

\mathcal{C}(x,y,\lambda)=u((1-\lambda)x+\lambda y)-(1-\lambda)u(x)-\lambda u(y)

for
(x,y,\lambda)\in\bar{\Omega}\times\bar{\Omega}\times[0,1]
. Clearly, the nonnegativity of
\mathcal{C}(x,y,\lambda)
is equivalent to the concavity of
u
. This method is commonly known as Korevaar’s concavity maximum principle. Using this, Korevaar reproved the result of Brascamp and Lieb
[
7
]
(see also Caffarelli and Spruck
[
12
]
). Later, Kennington
[
26
]
and Kawohl
[
25
]
presented an improved version that could be used to establish the
1/2
-concavity of the torsion function in high dimensions. For problems involving the
p
-Laplacian or the Finsler Laplacian, due to the lack of regularity in solutions, Korevaar’s concavity maximum principle cannot be applied directly. However, by suitable approximation arguments, power convexity results have been derived in
[
6
,
36
,
38
]
, among others. In 2021, Langford and Scheuer
[
31
]
extended Korevaar’s concavity maximum principle to spherical settings, and proved the convexity of solutions to certain fully nonlinear degenerate elliptic equations under appropriate conditions.

In 1997, Alvarez, Lasry and Lions
[
2
]
developed a new method, the convex envelope method, to establish the convexity of viscosity solution of general fully nonlinear degenerate elliptic equation

F(\nabla^{2}v,\nabla v,v,x)=0

in a bounded convex domain
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In particular, they gave alternative proof of previous results, including the
1/2
-concavity of the torsion function and the log-concavity of the first Dirichlet eigenfunction of the Laplacian. This method was also extend to handle the power convexity of certain elliptic boundary value problems directly (still working on the solution before transformation) as well as the power convexity of solutions of semilinear elliptic systems in
[
5
,
22
]
. Recently, one more application of convex envelope method was ascribed by Crasta and Fragalà
[
16
]
, who proved that the unique viscosity solution
u
to Dirichlet problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta_{\infty}u=-1&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega,\\
\hskip 18.20973ptu=0&\mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega\end{cases}

is
3/4
-concave and in
C^{1}(\Omega)
. Here
\Delta_{\infty}
denotes the usual infinity Laplacian.

Another powerful tool to produce convex solution is the so-called microscopic convexity principle (also called constant rank theorem). This approach was first discovered by Caffarelli and Friedman
[
9
]
for semilinear elliptic equations in two-dimensional case. Combining the deformation process, they established the
1/2
-concavity of the torsion function and the log-concavity of the first Dirichlet eigenfunction of the Laplacian in dimension two. Later, Korevaar and Lewis
[
29
]
proved the analogous results in high dimensions. Similar idea also appeared at the same time in Singer, Wong, Yau and Yau
[
41
]
to deal with the log-concavity of the first Dirichlet eigenfunction of the Laplacian.

For fully nonlinear elliptic equations, the constant rank theorem remains highly effective in dealing with problems related to convexity. One of the earliest results in this direction was obtained by Ma and Xu
[
34
]
, who studied the Dirichlet problem

\begin{cases}\sigma_{2}(\nabla^{2}u)=1&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega,\\
\hskip 34.1433ptu=0&\mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a bounded, convex domain with smooth boundary, and demonstrated that
-\sqrt{-u}
is strictly convex in
\Omega
. In the same way, Liu, Ma and Xu
[
32
]
investigated the
\log
-concavity of the eigenfunction of the
2
-Hessian operator and derived a Brunn-Minkowski inequality for the
2
-Hessian eigenvalue. See Salani
[
39
]
for new proofs of the power convexity results in
[
32
,
34
]
as well as discussions on Brunn-Minkowski inequalities of the
2
-torsion functional. Much more recently, several lower dimensional convexity results of solutions to fully nonlinear elliptic boundary value problems appeared via constant rank theorems, for example
[
13
,
21
,
44
]
. For historical developments on constant rank theorems of convex solutions of fully nonlinear elliptic PDEs and their applications in geometry, one can see
[
3
,
4
,
8
,
10
,
37
,
42
,
43
]
,
[
14
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
, and the references therein.

Similar to the methods of concavity maximum principle, convex envelope and constant rank theorem, Makar-Limanov’s approach has also been extended to high dimensions to deal with
1/2
-concavity of the torsion function and the log-concavity of the first Dirichlet eigenfunction of the Laplacian, see
[
23
,
24
,
33
]
. There are also many other miscellaneous interesting results on convexity of solutions of elliptic PDEs. For example, it is worth mentioning that Kulczycki
[
30
]
drew inspiration from the constant rank theorem and showed the concavity of solution to the Dirichlet problem of
1/2
-Laplacian in dimension two. In Hu
[
20
]
, it was shown that the complex Hessian of the Dirichlet problem for the homogeneous complex Monge-Ampère equation on a ring-shaped domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
has constant rank
n-1
in the weak sense.

1.3.
Remarks on the proof

Let us outline the fundamental framework for proving Theorem
1.1
. We will follow the approach of Caffarelli and Friedman
[
9
]
, and Korevaar and Lewis
[
29
]
, who established the power convexity of the torsion function and the first Dirichlet eigenfunction of the Laplacian by microscopic convexity principle. It is well-known that two key steps are required to implement this approach. The first is to obtain convexity estimates near the boundary, and the second is to establish the constant rank theorem. Recall the Dirichlet problem (
1.3
) for the complex Monge-Ampère equation that we considered in this paper. Letting
v=-\sqrt{-u/2}
, we know that
v
satisfies the Dirichlet problem

\begin{cases}F(\nabla^{2}v,\nabla v,v)=0&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega,\\
\hskip 62.59605ptv=0&\mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle F(\nabla^{2}v,\nabla v,v)=

\displaystyle~v^{2}\left[(v_{11}+v_{33})(v_{22}+v_{44})-(v_{12}+v_{34})(v_{21}+v_{43})-(v_{14}-v_{32})(v_{41}-v_{23})\right]

\displaystyle+v\left[(v_{2}^{2}+v_{4}^{2})(v_{11}+v_{33})+(v_{1}^{2}+v_{3}^{2})(v_{22}+v_{44})\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76219pt\left.-(v_{1}v_{2}+v_{3}v_{4})(v_{12}+v_{34}+v_{21}+v_{43})\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76219pt\left.-(v_{1}v_{4}-v_{2}v_{3})(v_{14}-v_{32}+v_{41}-v_{23})\right]-1.

For more details on the derivation of this equation, we refer to Section
2.3
. Since the domain
\Omega
is strictly convex, we can easily obtain the convexity estimates for
v
near the boundary
\partial\Omega
(see Proposition
4.1
for precise clarification). It suffices to establish the constant rank theorem for the transformed equation

(1.4)

\displaystyle F(\nabla^{2}v,\nabla v,v)=0.

Let us explain why establishing the constant rank theorem for equation (
1.4
) is particularly challenging. Note that equation (
1.4
) is non-homogeneous with respect to the variable
\nabla^{2}v
. In general, it is difficult to verify the crucial inverse convexity structural condition (
2.3
) outlined in Bian and Guan
[
4
]
for non-homogeneous equations. Although Bryan, Ivaki and Scheuer
[
8
]
presents a constant rank theorem for non-homogeneous equations, their conditions do not hold for
k
-Hessian type equations after power transformation. So we have to prove the constant rank theorem directly. This is why, up to now only convexity results in low dimensions
[
13
,
21
,
32
,
34
,
44
]
can be established for fully nonliear elliptic PDEs.

Define

l=\min_{x\in\Omega}\mathrm{rank}(\nabla^{2}v(x)).

By equation (
1.4
), it is clear that
l\geq 2
. Hence to prove the constant rank theorem (Corollary
3.3
), we only need to consider two cases, i.e.,

•

l=2
(Theorem
3.1
);

•

l=3
(Theorem
3.2
).

For the case
l=2
, in order to simplify our calculations, we adopt the quotient type auxiliary function introduced by Bian and Guan
[
4
]
which is defined as follows

\phi(x)=\sigma_{3}(\nabla^{2}v(x))+q(\nabla^{2}v(x)),

where

q(\nabla^{2}v(x))=\begin{cases}\frac{\sigma_{4}(\nabla^{2}v(x))}{\sigma_{3}(\nabla^{2}v(x))}&\mathrm{if}\ \sigma_{3}(\nabla^{2}v(x))>0,\\
0&\mathrm{if}\ \sigma_{3}(\nabla^{2}v(x))=0.\end{cases}

See Section
2.1
for more discussions on the elementary symmetric functions as well as the quotient type auxiliary function. As pointed out by Bian and Guan
[
4
]
, proving the maximum principle for the usual auxiliary function
\sigma_{3}(\nabla^{2}v(x))
is difficult in the context of general fully nonlinear elliptic equations. Choosing
\sigma_{3}(\nabla^{2}v(x))
as the auxiliary function would lead to computational complexity that is unacceptable, even for the specific equation (
1.4
) considered here.

The case
l=3
is significantly challenging. To prove the differential inequality for the auxiliary function
\sigma_{4}(\nabla^{2}v(x))
in this case, we must verify the semi-positivity of a six-variable quadratic form with most of its coefficients being non-zero. This part is highly involved and technical. Our strategy is performing elementary column and row operations cleverly on the corresponding
6\times 6
symmetric matrix. Fortunately, we can diagonalize this
6\times 6
matrix into two
3\times 3
blocks. By carefully computing and arranging the leading principal minors, and utilizing a basic lemma (Lemma
2.5
) from linear algebra, we finally complete the proof of Theorem
3.2
.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we review some preliminary results, including calculations on Bian and Guan’s quotient type auxiliary function, etc. In Section
3
, we analyze two cases and establish differential inequalities in each, leading to the required constant rank theorem (Corollary
3.3
). This constitutes the main part of the paper. Section
4
is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we first review some basic calculations related to Bian and Guan’s quotient type auxiliary function, which plays a crucial role in the proof of Theorem
3.1
(the case when the minimal rank of
\nabla^{2}v
is
2
). Next, we present a useful lemma from linear algebra. Finally, we derive the real form of the two-dimensional complex Monge-Ampère equation. We also give some preliminary computation for convenience in later use.

2.1.
Bian and Guan’s quotient type auxiliary function

We recall the definition of
k
-th elementary symmetric functions. For
1\leq k\leq n
and
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define

\sigma_{k}(\lambda)=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n}\lambda_{i_{1}}\lambda_{i_{2}}\cdots\lambda_{i_{k}}.

We also make the convention that
\sigma_{0}(\lambda)=1
and
\sigma_{-1}(\lambda)=\sigma_{n+1}(\lambda)=0
. The definition of
\sigma_{k}
for vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be extended to
n\times n
symmetric matrices in a natural way by letting
\sigma_{k}(A)=\sigma_{k}(\lambda(A))
, where
\lambda(A)=(\lambda_{1}(A),\lambda_{2}(A),\cdots,\lambda_{n}(A))
are the eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix
A
. We denote by
\sigma_{k-1}(A|i)
the
(k-1)
-th elementary symmetric function with
\lambda_{i}=0
and
\sigma_{k-2}(A|ij)

(i\neq j)
the
(k-2)
-th elementary symmetric function with
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{j}=0
.

The first and second derivatives of the elementary symmetric functions are listed in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that
A=(A_{ij})_{n\times n}
is diagonal. For
k=1,2,\cdots,n
, then we have

\frac{\partial\sigma_{k}(A)}{\partial A_{ij}}=\begin{cases}\sigma_{k-1}(A|i)&\mathrm{if}\ i=j\\
0&\mathrm{if}\ i\neq j,\end{cases}

and

\frac{\partial^{2}\sigma_{k}(A)}{\partial A_{ij}\partial A_{pq}}=\begin{cases}\sigma_{k-2}(A|ip)&\mathrm{if}\ i=j,p=q,i\neq p,\\
-\sigma_{k-2}(A|ip)&\mathrm{if}\ i=q,j=p,i\neq p,\\
0,&\mathrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

As mentioned in Section
1.3
, Bian and Guan
[
4
]
introduced the quotient type auxiliary function

(2.1)

\displaystyle\phi(x)=\sigma_{l+1}(\nabla^{2}v(x))+q(\nabla^{2}v(x)),

where

(2.2)

\displaystyle q(\nabla^{2}v(x))=\begin{cases}\frac{\sigma_{l+2}(\nabla^{2}v(x))}{\sigma_{l+1}(\nabla^{2}v(x))}&\mathrm{if}\ \sigma_{l+1}(\nabla^{2}v(x))>0,\\
0&\mathrm{if}\ \sigma_{l+1}(\nabla^{2}v(x))=0.\end{cases}

Using auxiliary function (
2.1
), they established constant rank theorem for convex solution of general fully nonlinear elliptic equation

F(\nabla^{2}v,\nabla v,v,x)=0\quad\mathrm{in}\ \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

under the structural condition that

(2.3)

\mbox{$F(A^{-1},p,v,x)$ is locally convex in $(A,v,x)$ for each $p$}.

Below we review some notations and lemmas from Bian and Guan
[
4
]
that will be used in Section
3
. Suppose that
\nabla^{2}v(x)
attains its minimal rank
l
(
l=0,1,\cdots,n-1
) at
x_{0}\in\Omega
. We pick an open neighborhood
\mathcal{O}
of
x_{0}
. For any
x\in\mathcal{O}
, let
\lambda_{1}(x)\geq\lambda_{2}(x)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}(x)\geq 0
be the eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}v(x)
. There exists a positive constant
c
independent of
x
, such that
\lambda_{1}(x)\geq\lambda_{2}(x)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{l}(x)\geq c
. Let
G=\{1,2,\cdots,l\}
and
B=\{l+1,l+2,\cdots,n\}
be the “good” and “bad” sets of indices, respectively. If there is no confusion, we also denote the “good” and “bad” eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}v(x)
by
G
and
B
, respectively. Note that for any
\delta>0
, we may choose
\mathcal{O}
small enough such that
\lambda_{i}(x)<\delta
for all
i\in B
and
x\in\mathcal{O}
.

For two functions
h(x)
and
k(x)
defined on
\mathcal{O}
, by
h(x)=O(k(x))
we mean that there exists a positive constant
C
such that
|h(x)|\leq C|k(x)|
. Clearly,
\lambda_{i}(x)=O(\phi)
for each
i\in B
, where
\phi
is the quotient type auxiliary function defined in (
2.1
).

In the rest of this subsection let us review several important lemmas obtained by Bian and Guan in
[
4
]
. For proofs of these lemmas, we refer to their original paper
[
4
]
.

Lemma 2.2

(Lemma 2.4 in
[
4
]
)
.

Let
q(\nabla^{2}v)
be defined as in (
2.2
). Assume that
\nabla^{2}v
is diagonal. Then

\frac{\partial q}{\partial v_{ij}}=\begin{cases}\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}(B|i)-\sigma_{2}(B|i)}{\sigma_{1}^{2}(B)}+O(\phi)&\mathrm{if}\ i=j\in B,\\
O(\phi)&\mathrm{if}\ i=j\in G,\\
0&\mathrm{if}\ i\neq j.\end{cases}

Lemma 2.3

(Proposition 2.1 in
[
4
]
)
.

Let
q(\nabla^{2}v)
be defined as in (
2.2
). Assume that
\nabla^{2}v(x)
is diagonal for
x\in\mathcal{O}
. Then, for any
\alpha,\beta\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i,j,k,m\leq n}\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial v_{ij}\partial v_{km}}v_{ij\alpha}v_{km\beta}=

\displaystyle~O\left(\phi+\sum_{i,j\in B}|\nabla v_{ij}|\right)-2\sum_{i\in B,j\in G}\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}(B|i)-\sigma_{2}(B|i)}{\sigma_{1}^{2}(B)\lambda_{j}}v_{ij\alpha}v_{ji\beta}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}^{3}(B)}\sum_{i\in B}V_{i\alpha}V_{i\beta}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}(B)}\sum_{i,j\in B,i\neq j}v_{ij\alpha}v_{ji\beta},

where

\quad V_{i\alpha}=v_{ii\alpha}\sigma_{1}(B)-v_{ii}\left(\sum_{j\in B}v_{jj\alpha}\right).

Lemma 2.4

(Lemma 3.3 in
[
4
]
)
.

Let
M
be a positive constant such that
0<\lambda_{i}\leq M
and
\frac{1}{M}\leq\gamma_{i}\leq M
for
i=l+1,l+2,\cdots,n
. Assume that
v_{ij\alpha}=v_{ji\alpha}
for

and
\alpha=1,2,\cdots,n
. Then there exists a positive constant
C
depending only on

and

, such that for each
\alpha
, for any
D>0
and any
\delta>0
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{l+1\leq i,j\leq n}|v_{ij\alpha}|\leq

\displaystyle~C\left(1+\frac{2D}{\delta}+D\right)\left(\sigma_{1}(\lambda)+\left|\sum_{l+1\leq i\leq n}\gamma_{i}v_{ii\alpha}\right|\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\delta}{2D}\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}(\lambda)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l+1\leq i,j\leq n\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}|v_{ij\alpha}|^{2}+\frac{C}{D}\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}^{3}(\lambda)}\sum_{l+1\leq i\leq n}V_{i\alpha}^{2},

where

2.2.
A linear algebra lemma

We give a useful lemma from linear algebra, which can be found in standard textbooks on the subject. For completeness, we include a proof here.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
A
be an
n\times n
real symmetric matrix. Assume that
A
has
n-1
nonzero leading principal minors, denoted by
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
,
\cdots
,
P_{n-1}
. Then there exists an
n\times n
invertible matrix
C
such that

C^{T}AC=\mathrm{diag}\left(P_{1},\frac{P_{2}}{P_{1}},\cdots,\frac{P_{n}}{P_{n-1}}\right),

where
P_{n}=\det(A)
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
n
. The result is evident when
n=1
. Assume that the statement holds for
n-1
, and let

A=\begin{pmatrix}A_{n-1}&\alpha\\[5.0pt]
\alpha^{T}&a_{nn}\end{pmatrix},

where
A_{n-1}
is the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
leading principal submatrix of
A
. Since
\det(A_{n-1})=P_{n-1}\neq 0
, we can define an
n\times n
invertible matrix

C_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{n-1}&-A_{n-1}^{-1}\alpha\\[5.0pt]
O&1\end{pmatrix}.

Then we have

C_{1}^{T}AC_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{n-1}&O\\[5.0pt]
O&a_{nn}-\alpha^{T}A_{n-1}^{-1}\alpha\end{pmatrix}.

Note that
\det(C_{1})=1
. It yields that

\det(A)=(a_{nn}-\alpha^{T}A_{n-1}^{-1}\alpha)\det(A_{n-1}),

that is,

a_{nn}-\alpha^{T}A_{n-1}^{-1}\alpha=\frac{P_{n}}{P_{n-1}}.

By the inductive hypothesis, there exists an
(n-1)\times(n-1)
invertible matrix
C_{2}
such that

C_{2}^{T}A_{n-1}C_{2}=\mathrm{diag}\left(P_{1},\frac{P_{2}}{P_{1}},\cdots,\frac{P_{n-1}}{P_{n-2}}\right).

If we let
C_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}C_{2}&O\\
O&1\end{pmatrix}
and
C=C_{1}C_{3}
, then we have

C^{T}AC=\mathrm{diag}\left(P_{1},\frac{P_{2}}{P_{1}},\cdots,\frac{P_{n}}{P_{n-1}}\right).

∎

2.3.
Real form of the complex Monge-Ampère equation

This subsection provides a brief discussion on real form of the complex Monge-Ampère equation on a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Let
z_{1}
,
z_{2}
be complex coordinates in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
,
z_{i}=x_{i}+\sqrt{-1}y_{i}
. If
u
is a smooth real-valued function defined on a domain

Corollary 3.3

(Constant rank theorem)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
be a domain, and let
v\in C^{4}(\Omega)
be a convex solution of (
2.7
). Then the Hessian
\nabla^{2}v
has constant rank throughout
\Omega
.

Proof.

Define

l=\min_{x\in\Omega}\mathrm{rank}(\nabla^{2}v(x)).

As discussed at the end of Section
2.3
,
l
can only be
2
or
3
. Suppose there exists some point
x_{0}\in\Omega
such that
\mathrm{rank}(\nabla^{2}v(x_{0}))=l
. We will show that
\mathrm{rank}(\nabla^{2}v(x))=l
for all
x\in\Omega
. Define the set

\Omega^{\ast}=\{x\in\Omega:\mathrm{rank}(\nabla^{2}v(x))=l\}.

It is clear that
\Omega^{\ast}
is relatively closed in
\Omega
. By the strong maximum principle, Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
imply that
\Omega^{\ast}
is also open. Hence,
\Omega^{\ast}=\Omega
, meaning that
\nabla^{2}v
has constant rank
l
throughout
\Omega
.

This completes the proof of Corollary
3.3
.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.1
by the continuity method. With Corollary
3.3
just established, it is well known that the proof of Theorem
1.1
follows standard arguments; see, for example, Caffarelli and Friedman
[
9
]
, as well as
[
29
,
32
,
34
,
44
]
.

Before proceeding, we require the convexity estimates near the boundary for the function
v=-\sqrt{-u/2}
, where
u
satisfies the Dirichlet problem (
1.3
). In the following proposition, we take
\alpha=1/2
.

Proposition 4.1

(Convexity estimates near the boundary, see Korevaar
[
28
]
, Caffarelli and Friedman
[
9
]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded, smooth, strictly convex domain, and let
u\in C^{2}(\bar{\Omega})
satisfy

u<0\quad\mathrm{in}\ \Omega,\quad u=0\quad\mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega,\quad|\nabla u|>0\quad\mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega.

Then there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that the function
v=-\log(-u)
or
v=-(-u)^{\alpha}

(0<\alpha<1)
is strictly convex in the boundary strip
\Omega\setminus\bar{\Omega}_{\varepsilon}
, where

\Omega_{\varepsilon}=\{x\in\Omega:\mathrm{d}(x,\partial\Omega)>\varepsilon\}.

We now moved on to the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

If
\Omega_{0}=B_{1}
, the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, then the unique solution to the transformed Dirichlet problem (
2.8
) is

v_{0}(x)=-\sqrt{\frac{1-|z_{1}|^{2}-|z_{2}|^{2}}{4}}\quad\mathrm{for}\ x\in B_{1},

where
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})
,
z_{1}=x_{1}+\sqrt{-1}x_{3}
,
z_{2}=x_{2}+\sqrt{-1}x_{4}
,
|z_{1}|^{2}=x_{1}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}
and
|z_{2}|^{2}=x_{2}^{2}+x_{4}^{2}
. Clearly, the function
v_{0}
is strictly convex in
B_{1}
. For an arbitrary bounded, smooth, strictly convex domain
\Omega_{1}=\Omega
, define

\Omega_{t}=(1-t)B_{1}+t\Omega\quad\mathrm{for}\ t\in(0,1).

By the theory of convex bodies, the family
\{\Omega_{t}\}_{t\in(0,1)}
provides a smooth deformation of
B_{1}
into
\Omega
, preserving boundedness, smoothness, and strict convexity. For further details, see Schneider’s excellent book
[
40
]
.

For
t\in(0,1)
, assume that the function
v_{t}
solves the Dirichlet problem

\begin{cases}F(\nabla^{2}v,\nabla v,v)=0&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega_{t},\\
\hskip 61.17325ptv=0&\mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega_{t},\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle F(\nabla^{2}v,\nabla v,v)=

\displaystyle~v^{2}\left[(v_{11}+v_{33})(v_{22}+v_{44})-(v_{12}+v_{34})(v_{21}+v_{43})-(v_{14}-v_{32})(v_{41}-v_{23})\right]

\displaystyle+v\left[(v_{2}^{2}+v_{4}^{2})(v_{11}+v_{33})+(v_{1}^{2}+v_{3}^{2})(v_{22}+v_{44})\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76228pt\left.-(v_{1}v_{2}+v_{3}v_{4})(v_{12}+v_{34}+v_{21}+v_{43})\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76228pt\left.-(v_{1}v_{4}-v_{2}v_{3})(v_{14}-v_{32}+v_{41}-v_{23})\right]-1.

According to the a priori estimates for the Dirichlet problem of complex Monge-Ampère equation established by Caffarelli, Kohn, Nirenberg and Spruck
[
11
]
, we have uniform bounds for
\|v_{t}\|_{C^{3}(\bar{\Omega}_{t})}
, depending only on the geometry of
\Omega
and independent of
t
. On the one hand, since
v_{0}
is strictly convex, it follows that
v_{t}
remains strictly convex for all
t\in(0,\delta)
for some sufficiently small
\delta>0
. On the other hand, if
v_{t}
is strictly convex in
\Omega_{t}
for all
t\in(0,t^{\ast})
with
t^{\ast}\in(0,1)
, then
v_{t^{\ast}}
must be convex in
\Omega_{t^{\ast}}
.

Now, suppose that
v_{1}
is not strictly convex in
\Omega_{1}
. Then there exists some
t_{0}\in(0,1)
at which
v_{t_{0}}
is convex but not strictly convex for the first time. By Corollary
3.3
(Constant rank theorem), the Hessian
\nabla^{2}v_{t_{0}}
is degenerate at every point of
\Omega_{t_{0}}
. However, Proposition
4.1
(Convexity estimates near the boundary) implies that
\nabla^{2}v_{t_{0}}
is of full rank in the boundary strip
\Omega_{t_{0}}\setminus\bar{\Omega}_{t_{0},\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon>0
, which depends only on the geometry of
\partial\Omega_{1}
. This leads to a contradiction. Therefore,
v_{1}
must be strictly convex in
\Omega_{1}
. Thus, we complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

We provide the following example to demonstrate that the convexity exponent
1/2
is sharp. Similar examples can be found in Kennington
[
26
]
, Ma and Xu
[
34
]
, and in the recent work of Zhang and Zhou
[
44
]
, respectively.

Consider the infinite open cone

\Gamma=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:\sqrt{(x_{1})^{2}+(x_{2})^{2}+(x_{3})^{2}}<ax_{4}\},

where
a\in(0,1)
. Let
\Omega\subset\Gamma
be a bounded convex domain, with
y=(0,0,0,0)\in\partial\Omega
and
z\in(0,0,0,1)\in\Omega
. Define the function

w(x)=\frac{A}{2}(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}-a^{2}x_{4}^{2}),

where
A>0
is chosen such that
2A^{2}(1-a^{2})=1
. Thus, the function
w
satisfies

\begin{cases}(w_{11}+w_{33})(w_{22}+w_{44})-(w_{12}+w_{34})(w_{21}+w_{43})-(w_{14}-w_{32})(w_{41}-w_{23})=1&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega,\\
\hskip 352.814ptw\leq 0&\mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Suppose that
u
solves the Dirichlet problem

\begin{cases}(u_{11}+u_{33})(u_{22}+u_{44})-(u_{12}+u_{34})(u_{21}+u_{43})-(u_{14}-u_{32})(u_{41}-u_{23})=1&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega,\\
\hskip 335.7426ptu=0&\mathrm{on}\ \partial\Omega.\end{cases}

By the comparison principle, we have
w\leq u
in
\Omega
. In particular, for
t\in(0,1)
, it follows that
-Aa^{2}t^{2}/2=w(tz)\leq u(tz)\leq 0
. This implies
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0+}t^{-1/\alpha}u(tz)=0
provided
\alpha>1/2
. Consequently, this shows that
-(-u)^{\alpha}
is not convex when
\alpha>1/2
.

In fact, if
-(-u)^{\alpha}
is convex for some
\alpha>1/2
, then we have

-(-u)^{\alpha}((1-t)y+tz)\leq-(1-t)(-u)^{\alpha}(y)-t(-u)^{\alpha}(z)=-t(-u)^{\alpha}(z).

Rearranging this inequality yields
t^{-1/\alpha}u(tz)\leq u(z)<0
, which contradicts the fact that
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0+}t^{-1/\alpha}u(tz)=0
. This confirms the sharpness of the convexity exponent
1/2
.
