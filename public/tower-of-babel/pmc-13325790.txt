Effect of PM2.5 exposure on the risk of incident autoimmune diseases: a prospective cohort study in Taiwan
Authors: Chun-Chieh Wang, Yu-Kai Lin, Yun-Ju Lai, Yu-Yen Chen, Shang-Yih Chan, Wan-Yu Sung, Yi-Chang Chou, Marcelo Chen, Yung-Feng Yen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe interaction between fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and genetic factors is thought to play a significant role in the development of autoimmune diseases. However, the impact of PM2.5 exposure on the onset of these diseases remains unclear. This population-based cohort study investigated the effect of year-to-year variations in PM2.5 exposure levels on incident autoimmune disease risk in Taiwanese adults.MethodsIn this longitudinal study, we followed-up 276,254 adults from the Taiwan MJ cohort (2005–2017) for 9.3 years to identify incident autoimmune diseases cases, ascertained from the patients’ clinical and laboratory reports. Autoimmune diseases were categorized as systemic and organ-specific. Data on residential address-specific annual PM2.5 concentrations were obtained from Taiwan Air Quality-Monitoring sites. A time-dependent Cox regression model, considering death as a competing risk, was used to assess the effect of year-to-year variations in PM2.5 exposure levels on autoimmune disease risk.ResultsDuring the 2,579,362 person-years of follow-up, new-onset autoimmune diseases were observed in 958 (0.35%) individuals, corresponding to an incident density of 37.14 per 100,000 person-years. Participants with high PM2.5 exposure levels (per 5-µg/m3 increase) had a significantly increased risk of incident autoimmune diseases (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 2.97; 95% confidence interval [CI], 2.84–3.11), particularly systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) (aHR, 3.28; 95% CI, 2.99–3.59) and Sjögren’s syndrome (aHR, 2.82; 95% CI, 2.66–2.98).ConclusionsProactive air pollution control strategies are crucial for the prevention of autoimmune diseases, particularly SLE and Sjögren’s syndrome.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-026-01930-2.

Introduction

Autoimmune diseases, affecting nearly 5% of the global population, encompass a broad spectrum of disorders characterized by the body’s immune system attacking its own tissues, leading to chronic inflammation and subsequent tissue damage [1]. The burden and incidence of autoimmune diseases have increased worldwide [2]. Although the exact etiopathogenesis of autoimmune diseases is unclear, factors such as defective immune regulation, genetic susceptibility, and environmental stimuli have been reported to be primarily responsible for triggering autoimmunity [3].

In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward the role of environmental factors in the development of autoimmune diseases [3, 4]. A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis indicated that environmental exposures are important triggers in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases [4], accounting for approximately 40–70% of all cases [5, 6].

Air pollution, the leading environmental risk factor, can cause and exacerbate a range of diseases [7, 8]. Previous reports have indicated that air pollution can trigger systemic inflammatory responses, stimulate T and B cells, and lead to the production of autoantibodies [7, 9]. Furthermore, air pollutants can interact with genetic factors, playing a significant role in the development of autoimmune diseases [3].

Particulate matter with a diameter of less than 2.5 μm (PM2.5) is a major component of air pollution and poses serious health risks, as it can penetrate deep into the lungs and reach peripheral tissues through the bloodstream [10]. Previous reports have shown that exposure to PM2.5 can cause DNA methylation and dysregulated T cell production [7, 11], which may increase the risk of autoimmune diseases. However, research exploring the relationship between PM2.5 exposure and the risk of autoimmune diseases remains limited. A cohort study involving 111,425 female nurses in the U.S. showed no significant association between PM2.5 exposure and the incidence of rheumatoid arthritis [12]. In contrast, an ecological study in Taiwan based on county-level PM2.5 data revealed that a 10.2-µg/m³ increase in PM2.5 exposure level was associated with a 1.12-fold higher risk of developing systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [13]. Another ecological study conducted in the province of Pavia, Italy, showed that municipalities with higher PM2.5 exposure levels had an increased prevalence of multiple sclerosis [14]. Nonetheless, the use of area-level instead of individual-level PM2.5 concentration data to assess the association between PM2.5 exposure and autoimmune disease risk may lead to exposure misclassification [13, 14]. Additionally, using PM2.5 exposure data collected after the onset of autoimmune disease may obscure the temporal relationship between exposure and disease risk [12].

Understanding the effect of PM2.5 exposure on the risk of developing autoimmune diseases is crucial for future prevention programs. Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine how year-to-year variations in PM2.5 exposure levels affect the risk of autoimmune diseases in a large population cohort, using PM2.5 concentration data specific to participant residential address.

Methods

Study setting and participants

The MAJOR (MJ) Health Management Institution has been offering a self-paying health examination program in Taiwan since 1994 [15]. This institution provided our study data, spanning 2005–2017 [16, 17].

The participant selection process is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. In total, 350,954 individuals participated in the MJ health surveillance program between 2005 and 2017. Since autoimmune diseases primarily affect younger to middle-aged adults [18], individuals younger than 18 years (n = 7,365) were excluded. To reduce potential reverse causality, participants with pre-existing autoimmune diseases prior to enrollment (n = 805) were also excluded. In addition, individuals with missing data (n = 66,530) were removed from the analysis (supplementary Table 1). Finally, a total of 276,254 participants were included in the study.

All participants in this cohort were followed up until a new diagnosis of autoimmune diseases, death, or December 31, 2017, whichever was earliest. Verification of participant death was conducted using the Taiwan death certificate database [19]. We linked the MJ dataset to the Registry for Catastrophic Illness of Taiwan to identify participants who received a new autoimmune disease diagnosis [20]. In Taiwan, a specialized committee reviews patient clinical and laboratory reports before documenting an International Classification of Diseases, Ninth or Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9 or ICD-10) code for an autoimmune disease diagnosis in the Registry for Catastrophic Illnesses [20].

Data collection and ethics statement

While participating in the MJ health surveillance program, individuals were instructed to complete a self-administered questionnaire inquiring about their lifestyle and medical history. All participants then underwent standard medical tests, including body measurements and physical examinations.

All participants in the MJ cohort provided signed informed consent, authorizing the MJ Health Management Institution to process data generated from medical screening, with anonymized data used for research and publication. The Research Ethics Committee of Jen-Ai Hospital, Taiwan (No. 202400021B0) approved this study, and the requirement for informed consent was waived. All data used in this study were anonymized and de-identified.

Exposure variable

The Taiwan Air Quality Monitoring Database (TAQMD) was used to determine the annual mean concentrations of PM2.5 corresponding to the geocoded residential addresses of participants [21]. The TAQMD, established by Taiwan’s Environmental Protection Administration and Central Weather Bureau, is a large-scale system designed to document and forecast air quality through 76 nationwide atmospheric monitoring stations [21]. Each station routinely measures hourly concentrations of major air pollutants, including PM2.5.

In our cohort study, we applied an Ordinary Kriging Model to estimate the annual average PM2.5 concentrations (µg/m³) at participants’ residential locations from 2005 to 2017 [22, 23]. The daily average PM2.5 level at each station was calculated when at least 18 h of monitoring data were available. When data were available for fewer than 18 h, the missing values were estimated by multiplying the daily ratio by the mean concentration from other stations on the same day, following the approach proposed by Hoek et al. [24]. The proportion of missing daily PM2.5 data during 2005–2017 was 1.18%.

Daily averages from each monitoring station were subsequently used for spatial interpolation. Each participant’s daily exposure level was estimated by weighting the monitoring data from nearby stations according to distance from their residence. These daily exposure estimates were then averaged to obtain the annual mean exposure.

Spatial interpolation and cross-validation

The ordinary kriging method was performed using ArcGIS Desktop (version 9.3; ESRI Inc., Redlands, CA) with the Geostatistical Analyst tool to interpolate daily average PM2.5 concentrations at the township level. Cross-validation of the daily kriging model was conducted using the leave-one-out method, in which one data point was removed from the fitting process and its concentration was predicted using data from the remaining monitoring stations. The difference between the observed and predicted values was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R²) and the root mean square error (RMSE) [25]. The cross-validation results in our study showed an R² of 0.91 and an RMSE of 7.07, indicating high model accuracy.

Outcome variable

The outcome variable was new-onset autoimmune diseases, determined from the Registry for Catastrophic Illness of Taiwan [20]. In Taiwan, before an ICD-9 or ICD-10 code for autoimmune diseases diagnosis is recorded in the Registry for Catastrophic Illnesses, a clinical evaluation of the patient’s medical records and serological findings is mandatory [20]. Individuals diagnosed with autoimmune diseases by a certified rheumatologist can apply for a catastrophic illness certificate from the National Health Insurance Administration [20]. Holders of this certificate are entitled to exemption from copayment fees associated with healthcare services related to autoimmune diseases [20].

Autoimmune diseases were categorized as systemic and organ-specific [26]. Systemic autoimmune diseases included rheumatoid arthritis (ICD-9 code 714.0; ICD-10 code M05.70-M06.09, M06.20-M06.39, M06.80-M06.89, M06.9), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) (ICD-9 code 7100; ICD-10 code M32.0-M32.9), Sjögren’s syndrome (ICD-9 code 710.2; ICD-10 code M35.00-M35.09), polymyositis (ICD-9 code 710.4; ICD-10 code M33.20-M33.29), and dermatomyositis (ICD-9 code 710.1; ICD-10 code M33.00-M33.19, M33.90-M33.99, M36.0). Organ-specific autoimmune diseases included multiple sclerosis (ICD-9 code 340; ICD-10 code G35), and myasthenia gravis (ICD-9 code 358.0; ICD-10 code G70.00, G70.01), and inflammatory bowel disease (ICD-9 code 555, 556; ICD-10 code K50.00-K50.919, K51.00-K51.919).

Potentially confounding variables

The covariates identified in previous studies as risk factors for autoimmune disease [1, 27, 28] were assessed in our analyses; these included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), education level, urbanization, smoking status, alcohol consumption, fruit and vegetable consumption, leisure time physical activity (LTPA), and occupational physical activity (Supplementary Fig. 2). BMI categories were underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (24–26.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥ 27 kg/m2) [29]. Education level was classified as junior high school or lower, senior high school, or university and above. Urbanization was categorized as urban, suburban, or rural. Smoking status was defined as never, current, or former. Alcohol consumption was classified as none/occasional, once per week, or former. Fruit and vegetable intake was categorized as < 3 servings, 3–4 servings, or ≥ 5 servings per day.

LTPA was categorized as inactive (< 1 MET-h), low (1–7.49 MET-h), moderate (7.5–14.99 MET-h), or high (≥ 15 MET-h) based on activity level [17]. Occupational physical activity was categorized as light (mostly sedentary), moderate (involving repetitive motions while sitting or standing, such as in manufacturing), and heavy (involving heavy lifting, loading, and moving loads).

Statistical analysis

Participant demographic data were analyzed. Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations (SD). The incidence density (ID) for each autoimmune disease was calculated using person-years analysis by dividing the number of observed cases by the total person-years for the risk of that specific disease. The start date for calculating person-years was the date of the first health screening, and the end date was either December 31, 2017, the date of the incident autoimmune disease, or the date of death during follow-up.

Time-dependent Cox regression models [30] were used to assess the impact of cumulative annual PM2.5 exposure on the risk of new-onset autoimmune diseases, adjusting for age, sex, BMI category, education level, urbanization, smoking status, alcohol consumption, fruit and vegetable intake, occupational physical activity, and LTPA, with death considered as a competing risk [31]. In these models, the annual average PM2.5 exposure was included as a time-dependent covariate, whereas other confounders (such as age, sex, BMI, education level, urbanization, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activities) collected at baseline were considered fixed covariates. Adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported to indicate the strength and direction of associations.

To assess the robustness of the main findings, stratified analyses were performed according to age and sex. In addition, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding participants who developed autoimmune diseases within one year after the health examination to evaluate the effect of PM2.5 exposure on the risk of autoimmune diseases. All analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics and incident autoimmune diseases of the study population

This study included 276,254 participants in the analysis with a mean (standard deviation [SD]) age of 39.2 (12.4) years, and 48.8% were men (Table 1). Among all participants, the average (SD) PM2.5 exposure level decreased significantly from 29.8 (6.8) µg/m3 during the initial health screening to 19.1 (4.1) µg/m3 at the final health screening (p < 0.001).

During the 2,579,362 person-years of follow-up, new-onset autoimmune diseases were observed in 958 (0.35%) individuals, corresponding to an incident density of 37.14 per 100,000 person-years. The annual incidence fluctuated over the study period, ranging from 31.94 to 36.71 per 100,000 person-years (supplementary Fig. 3). The most common new-onset systemic autoimmune disease was Sjogren’s syndrome (n = 348; ID = 13.47/100,000 person-years), followed by rheumatoid arthritis (n = 328; ID = 12.70/100,000 person-years) and SLE (n = 134; ID = 5.19/100,000 person-years). In addition, the most common new-onset organ-specific autoimmune disease was myasthenia gravis (n = 75; ID = 2.90/100,000 person-years), followed by inflammatory bowel disease (n = 50; ID = 1.93/100,000 person-years).

Annual mean concentrations of PM2.5 by town from 2005 to 2017 in Taiwan

Figure 1 shows the annual mean PM2.5 concentrations by town in Taiwan from 2005 to 2017. Using 5 µg/m³ increments, we classified the annual average PM2.5 concentrations into the following categories: <10 µg/m³, 10–<15 µg/m³, 15–<20 µg/m³, 20–<25 µg/m³, 25–<30 µg/m³, 30–<35 µg/m³, 35–<40 µg/m³, and ≥ 40 µg/m³. PM2.5 exposure varied substantially across Taiwan, ranging from 14.3 µg/m³ to 44.5 µg/m³ during the study period (5th and 95th percentiles, respectively). While central and southern Taiwan consistently recorded the highest mean PM2.5 concentrations, the national absolute PM2.5 concentration significantly decreased from 32.8 ug/m3 in 2005 to 19.8 ug/m3 in 2017.

Association of PM2.5 exposure with the risk of incident autoimmune diseases

A time-dependent Cox proportional hazards model was used to evaluate the association between PM2.5 exposure and incident autoimmune diseases. Each 5 µg/m³ increase in PM2.5 exposure was associated with a significantly higher risk of incident autoimmune diseases (aHR, 2.97; 95% CI, 2.84–3.11) (Table 2).

Other independent risk factors included age ≥ 50 years (aHR, 1.51; 95% CI, 1.23–1.85), female sex (aHR, 4.15; 95% CI, 3.43–5.02), and residence in suburban (aHR, 1.68; 95% CI, 1.18–2.40) or urban areas (aHR, 2.07; 95% CI, 1.43–2.98) compared with rural areas. Sensitivity analysis of PM2.5 exposure and the risk of autoimmune diseases

To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding participants who developed autoimmune diseases within one year after the health examination. A total of 89 participants with newly diagnosed autoimmune diseases during this period were excluded from the analysis.

The time-dependent Cox proportional hazards model showed that each 5 µg/m³ increase in PM2.5 exposure was significantly associated with a higher risk of incident autoimmune diseases (aHR, 2.89; 95% CI, 2.75–3.04) (supplementary Fig. 4). In addition, higher PM2.5 exposure (per 5 µg/m³ increase) was significantly associated with increased risks of incident SLE (aHR, 3.30; 95% CI, 2.96–3.67) and Sjögren’s syndrome (aHR, 2.81; 95% CI, 2.65–2.98).

Discussion

This is the first longitudinal study conducted to comprehensively evaluate the association between PM2.5 exposure and incident autoimmune diseases. Over a mean follow-up period of 9.3 years, individuals with high PM2.5 exposure levels had a significantly increased risk of incident autoimmune diseases. When the effect of PM2.5 exposure on the risk of specific autoimmune diseases were considered, high PM2.5 exposure levels (per 5-µg/m3 increase) was significantly associated with increased risks of incident SLE and Sjogren’s syndrome.

The interaction between PM2.5 and gene plays a significant role in the development of autoimmune diseases [3]. However, the effect of long-term PM2.5 exposure on the incidence of autoimmune diseases remains unclear. A cohort study conducted in the U.S. revealed that individuals with high PM2.5 exposure levels did not have an increased risk of rheumatoid arthritis [12]. Nonetheless, a cohort study in Taiwan revealed a positive association between PM2.5 exposure and the incidence of SLE [13]. In our study of 276,254 participants with an average follow-up period of 9.3 years, we observed a significant linear dose-response relationship between high PM2.5 exposure levels and an increased risk of developing autoimmune diseases. Specifically, each 5-µg/m³ increase in PM2.5 exposure level was associated with 3.3-fold and 2.8-fold increased risks of incident SLE and Sjogren’s syndrome, respectively. These findings suggest that PM2.5 air pollution contributes to the incidence of autoimmune diseases, particularly SLE and Sjogren’s syndrome.

This study identified a significant association between PM2.5 exposure and an increased risk of incident SLE and Sjögren’s syndrome. PM2.5-induced oxidative stress may explain the elevated risk of SLE among individuals exposed to higher levels of PM2.5. Exposure to PM2.5 can alter the pulmonary microenvironment and provoke oxidative stress, which in turn stimulates T and B lymphocytes and promotes the production of autoantibodies [7]. In an experimental study using normal BALB/c mice, injection of n-hexadecane, a component of PM2.5, induced the production of SLE-specific autoantibodies [32]. Another study reported that elevated PM2.5 levels were significantly associated with increased production of anti-dsDNA autoantibodies in patients with SLE [33].

PM2.5-induced epigenetic modifications may also contribute to the increased risk of incident SLE and Sjögren’s syndrome among individuals exposed to high PM2.5 concentrations. Exposure to PM2.5 has been shown to trigger epigenetic alterations, including DNA methylation [34]. PM2.5-related DNA methylation could lead to T-cell dysregulation and impaired immune tolerance [34, 35], thereby increasing the risk of developing SLE and Sjögren’s syndrome.

PM2.5 exposure is a known risk factor for various health problems, including cardiopulmonary diseases [36]. It has also been linked to DNA methylation [7], which may contribute to an increased risk of autoimmune diseases. Our study adds to this growing concern by demonstrating a link between PM2.5 exposure and increased risk of incident SLE and Sjogren’s syndrome. The observed strong linear dose–response relationship underscores the importance of implementing air quality control measures to reduce the incidence of these diseases. Given that SLE and Sjögren’s syndrome arise from interactions between environmental exposures and genetic susceptibility [37, 38], our findings highlight the urgent need for effective public health policies to improve air quality and mitigate the risk of autoimmune diseases.

This is the first large cohort study conducted to comprehensively investigate the association between PM2.5 exposure and subsequent development of autoimmune diseases. Unlike a previous cross-sectional study [14], our prospective cohort design eliminates issues related to the unclear temporality between PM2.5 exposure and the risk of autoimmune diseases. Furthermore, we utilized participant residential address-specific PM2.5 concentration data, rather than county-level data [13], allowing for a more accurate estimation of the effect of PM2.5 exposure on autoimmune disease risk. Lastly, by linking the MJ cohort data with the death certificate database of Taiwan [19], we were able to comprehensively account for mortality and use a time-dependent Cox regression model with death as a competing risk for precise estimation of the effect of PM2.5 exposure on incident autoimmune diseases [31].

Nonetheless, some limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this large cohort study. First, although the availability of detailed personal information in the MJ cohort allowed us to adjust for a wide range of potential confounders, including physical activity [28], autoimmune disorders are multifactorial diseases in which genetic and environmental factors play dynamic roles in their pathogenesis [1]. However, data on susceptibility genes related to the development of autoimmune diseases were not available. Second, the diagnoses of new-onset autoimmune diseases based on the Registry for Catastrophic Illness of Taiwan may be less accurate than diagnoses made in a prospective clinical setting. However, all autoimmune disease diagnoses in this dataset were confirmed based on the patients’ medical and serological reports, affirming the validity of the Taiwan Catastrophic Illness dataset [20]. Third, this study used participants’ geocoded residential addresses to estimate their annual mean PM2.5 exposure concentrations. However, individual PM2.5 exposure levels may vary dynamically with changes in residential address. Future studies are needed to investigate the effects of PM2.5 exposure on the risk of incident autoimmune diseases while accounting for residential address changes over time. Finally, the generalizability of our findings may be limited, as all participants were enrolled in a private health surveillance program in Taiwan. Although the MJ Health Management Institution is a large health examination program that serves more than one million individuals nationwide [15], further studies are needed to evaluate the applicability of our findings to other populations.

In conclusion, this cohort study revealed that individuals with high PM2.5 exposure levels had a significantly increased risk of developing autoimmune diseases. Specifically, each 5-µg/m³ increase in PM2.5 exposure level was associated with an increased risk of incident SLE and Sjögren’s syndrome. Our findings suggest that proactive air pollution control strategies are crucial for the prevention of autoimmune diseases, particularly SLE and Sjögren’s syndrome.
