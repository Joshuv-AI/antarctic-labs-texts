Effect of growing environment on the antioxidant activity of recultivated pea seedlings (Pisum sativum L.)
Authors: Yuki Tsuruoka, Takahiro Hosoya
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPea seedlings (Pisum sativum L.) are young pea plants with the potential for sustainable cultivation; these plants are cost‐effective and provide high nutritional value. Although numerous studies have explored the nutritional content and functionality of pea seedlings, a few reports exist on the regrowth of pea seedlings after the initial harvest of their edible parts. Hence, this study aimed to elucidate the effect of the growing environment during recultivation on the properties and functionalities of pea seedlings. The non‐edible parts of commercially acquired pea seedlings were recultivated in three different environments: (1) indoors under sunlight during the day, (2) indoors with only artificial fluorescent light, and (3) in a dark room. The edible parts of the recultivated seedlings were extracted with methanol, and the antioxidant activities of the extracts were evaluated using the 2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl radical scavenging assay. Plants grown in environment 1 exhibited the highest antioxidant activity. The antioxidant components of pea seedlings were identified to be kaempferol glycoside (1), quercetin glycoside (3), and their respective p‐coumaroyl acylated glycosides (2 and 4). This determination was made by separating the components via liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry using activity as an indicator. An analysis of these components across the studied environments revealed that the isolated quantities of components 2 and 4 were 2.7‐ and 6.2‐fold greater, respectively, from the plants recultivated in environment 1 than those recultivated in environment 2. This study highlights the importance of the recultivation environment on the production of plant functional components.Keywords: Pisum sativum L., antioxidant, recultivation, acylated flavonoid glycoside

INTRODUCTION

In recent years, the “tertiary functions” of foods have attracted considerable attention owing to increasing global health consciousness. In Japan, the demand for health foods such as tokuho‐labeled products and functional foods is increasing (Hirogaki, 2011). However, these foods are expensive and therefore difficult to consume frequently. Adding such high functionality to the foods regularly consumed would benefit the health of consumers.

One food that has attracted attention is the “microscale” vegetable (Kyriacou et al., 2016). Sprouts and microgreens are fresh, ready‐to‐eat, functional, and nutritious. Sprouts have a short growth cycle and are grown in the dark without media, fertilizers, or pesticides. In contrast, microgreens require light and growth medium and have a long growth cycle (Ebert, 2012). Microgreens are becoming increasingly popular because they are reported to be richer in beneficial components than their mature counterparts (Kyriacou et al., 2016). In Japan, microgreens such as broccoli sprouts, kaiware daikon (Japanese radish), and pea shoots have risen in popularity; furthermore, the pea shoot is known as a vegetable that can be successfully “regrown.”

Pea seedlings are the sprouts of pea beans. In Japan, their roots are cut off, and the stems and leaves are used for cooking. If the remaining seeds and roots are soaked in water, new shoots emerge within a few days; the stems and leaves can then be harvested again. In response to rising vegetable prices in Japan in recent years, an increasing number of people have started inexpensive and simple home gardens through these re‐growing methods, which has further increased the demand for pea seedlings (Nishikawa & Miyata, 2023).

Peas (Pisum sativum) have a high nutritional value in terms of proteins, polysaccharides, and micronutrients (Savage & Deo, 1989); they contain polyphenols and exhibit high antioxidant activity (Amarowicz et al., 2001; Saha et al., 2014). Firmin et al. (1986) reported that flavonoids released from the seeds and roots of legumes act as a signal metabolite for symbiotic nitrogen fixation mechanisms. In addition, Ferreres et al. (1995) showed that flavonols isolated from the shoots of P. sativum (cv. Solara) are acylated derivatives of quercetin and kaempferol containing p‐coumaric, caffeic, ferulic, and sinapic acids.

Although the high nutritional value and various functionalities of pea beans have been elucidated, such properties and trends in pea bean seedlings recultivated in Japanese homes have not been clarified. In this study, we assessed the impact of the growing environment on the antioxidant activity of recultivated pea seedlings.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials and chemicals

Pisum sativum (toh‐myo in Japanese) were purchased from Murakami Farm. n‐Hexane, ethyl acetate, n‐butanol, ethanol, and methanol were purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation. Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC‐MS) grade 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid in water were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific.

Recultivation of P. sativum in various environments

The edible parts of the purchased pea bean plants (the leaves and base of the stem) were cut off. The environments for recultivation were as follows: a room with sunlight during the day (environment 1; illumination during the day: 750–1500 lx, illumination at night: 0 lx), a room with fluorescent lights (environment 2; illumination during the day: 300–750 lx, illumination at night: 0 lx), and a dark room (environment 3; illumination during the day and night: 0 lx). The room temperature (25°C), humidity (∼60%), growth period (10 days in December), and watering frequency (1 time/2 days) were the same for all environments. In each environment, the edible parts of the regrown pea bean seedlings were cut, dried, and ground into a powder.

Antioxidant evaluations using the 2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl radical scavenging assay

Antioxidant activity was evaluated using the 2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging assay. For the assay, 100 mg/mL of each extract was diluted stepwise in ethanol. Next, 100 µL of the stepwise‐diluted solution was transferred to a 96‐well microplate, and 100 µL of 0.4 mM DPPH radical solution was added. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 30 min at 20°C. To determine the percentage of DPPH radicals remaining, sample absorbance at 520 nm was measured using a Synergy HTX microplate reader (BioTek). This result was used to calculate the half‐maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50; µg/mL) value.

DPPH radical scavenging rate (%) = [(Absorbance with sample and DPPH radical solution) − Absorbance with sample)]/(Absorbance of the control) × 100.

Isolation of antioxidant compounds from P. sativum

Powder of the P. sativum leaves and stems (51.0 g) was extracted with methanol (1.0 L). The extract was filtered with a filter paper (pore diameter: 6 µm; ADVANTEC), and the solvent was removed using an evaporator to obtain the methanol extract (26.25 g). The methanol extract was sequentially partitioned with water, n‐hexane, ethyl acetate, and n‐butanol to obtain water (18.22 g), n‐hexane (3.92 g), ethyl acetate (0.42 g), and n‐butanol (3.66 g) extracts, respectively.

The n‐butanol extract, which showed the highest level of antioxidant activity, was further fractionated via flash column chromatography using a 30 g Sfär octadecylsilyl (ODS) C18 column (Biotage) with a water/methanol (9:1–0:1, v/v) solvent system, flow rate of 25 mL/min, and detection wavelengths of 254 and 270 nm. Six fractions were obtained: Bu‐1 (649.1 mg), Bu‐2 (27.1 mg), Bu‐3 (93.3 mg), Bu‐4 (34.9 mg), Bu‐5 (52.0 mg), and Bu‐6 (36.9 mg). Bu‐3, which showed the greatest antioxidant activity, was further separated via high‐performance LC equipped with a Mightysil ODS column (10 × 250 mm; Tokyo, Japan) with a water/methanol (55:45) solvent system, flow rate of 2.5 mL/min, and detection wavelength of 254 nm. Seven fractions were obtained: Bu‐3‐1 (4.7 mg), Bu‐3‐2 (3.5 mg), Bu‐3‐3 (4.4 mg), Bu‐3‐4 (11.3 mg), Bu‐3‐5 (3.3 mg), Bu‐3‐6 (7.0 mg), and Bu‐3‐7 (3.9 mg). Bu‐3‐2, Bu‐3‐3, Bu‐3‐4, and Bu‐3‐6 were completely purified as compounds 3, 1, 4, and 2, respectively. The compounds were then subjected to structural analyses.

Quantitative analysis of antioxidant components by LC‐MS

High resolution‐electrospray ionization‐mass spectrometry (HR‐ESI‐MS) was performed using an Acquity LC system (Waters Corporation) equipped with a Synapt‐G2 MS system (Waters Corporation). The analysis of compounds 1–4 was performed using linear gradient elution. The solvent system comprised solution A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solution B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). A linear gradient from 2% to 100% of solution B was delivered at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min over 10 min. The injection volume was 5 µL of the sample solution. Data were acquired in negative MS scanning mode. The mass tolerance was less than 10 ppm. The operating parameters were as follows: cone gas pressure: 50 L/h, desolvation gas flow: 1000 L/h, source capillary voltage: 2.5 kV for negative mode, source temperature: 120°C, and desolvation temperature: 450°C. Standards for compounds 1–4 were diluted stepwise to 0.1–100 µg/mL in methanol and subjected to LC‐MS to produce calibration curves (area under the curve at the appropriate m/z as a function of concentration). The extract of dried and powdered P. sativum was analyzed by LC‐MS under the same conditions. The area values of compounds 1–4 were calculated, and the final quantification was performed using the calibration curve. To validate the analysis method, the linearity, limit of detection (LOD), and signal‐to‐noise ratios were calculated. The linearity of the calibration curve for each compound was calculated with the correlation coefficients (R 2) ranging from 0.999 to 1.000. The LOD was defined as 0.1 µg/mL, where the area value fell within the standard curve. The sensitivities of this method for each compound were evaluated using signal‐to‐noise ratios; compounds 1–4 were 12.4, 10.4, 7.8, and 3.9. Compounds 1–4 in all samples were quantified within the calibration curves.

Statistics analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the mean ± standard deviation was calculated. In addition, a statistically significant difference was determined at p < 0.01 in the t‐test of the means of a pair of samples.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Effect of recultivation environment on the growth of P. sativum seedlings

The purchased P. sativum plants were cut down to the roots and regrown in three different environments (environment 1: sun, environment 2: fluorescent light, and environment 3: darkness), resulting in changes in appearance (Figure 1). Although the P. sativum recultivated in environment 1 were shorter, the leaves had a darker green color and thicker stems than those of the purchased plants. The P. sativum plants recultivated in environment 2 were in a similar condition and height as the purchased plants after 10 days of growth. The P. sativum recultivated in environment 3 possessed unusually few leaves and elongated, yellow‐green stems. The plants grown in environment 1 had the darkest, most intensely colored leaves and stems; in contrast, the plants grown in environment 3 had the least intense coloring. Furthermore, the plants from environment 1 had the thickest, shortest stems, whereas the plants from environment 3 had the longest, thinnest stems.

Photographs of P. sativum seedlings after recultivation in various environments.

These results indicate that the more sunlight the seedlings are exposed to, the darker their green color and the shorter they are in length. Seedlings grown in the dark become elongated, and their green color fades to yellow in the absence of sunlight. In fact, it is known that green bean seedlings can be harvested in a lit environment, whereas yellow bean seedlings can be harvested in the dark (Xiao et al., 2012). The trend in green color observed in this study is consistent with the fact that light intensity is proportional to chlorophyll content; the same phenomenon has also been observed in bean sprouts and white asparagus (Kim et al., 2024). Furthermore, excessive light is known to inhibit phytomorphogenesis and suppress elongation (Peilin et al., 2022). In this study, elongation inhibition was most prevalent in the plants grown in the sunlight, that is, environment 1.

Evaluation of antioxidant activity using the DPPH radical scavenging assay

The components of the edible parts of P. sativum obtained from the three environments were extracted with methanol to obtain methanol extracts. These extracts were evaluated for antioxidant activity by the DPPH radical scavenging assay and exhibited DPPH radical scavenging activity (Table 1A). The IC50 values for the extracts from environments 1, 2, and 3 were determined to be 509.7 ± 2.7 µg/mL, 1163.9 ± 10.9 µg/mL, and 1349.0 ± 99.1 µg/mL, respectively. Thus, the extracts from the P. sativum recultivated in environment 1 showed the strongest antioxidant activity, whereas the extracts from those grown in environment 3 exhibited the weakest activity. Furthermore, the P. sativum recultivated in environment 2, which were similar in appearance to the purchased plants, produced extracts that showed comparable antioxidant activity to the purchased plants.

Exposure to sunlight causes oxidative stress; the production of antioxidants protects plants from this stress (Salin, 1988). In the present study, we can hypothesize that exposure to sunlight, which was the case for the plants recultivated in environment 1, increased the antioxidant activity of the seedlings to protect them from oxidative stress.

Isolation of antioxidant compounds from P. sativum

To better understand these observed differences in antioxidant activity depending on growth environment, antioxidants were further isolated using activity as an indicator; the antioxidants contained in each P. sativum sample were quantified (Table 1B–D). Liquid–liquid partitioning of the methanol extracts of pea bean seedlings with multiple solvents (H2O, n‐hexane, EtOAc, and n‐butanol) was performed, and antioxidant activity was observed in all solvent extracts. The n‐butanol extract, which displayed both a high yield and high antioxidant activity, underwent further separations using various chromatographic methods. Finally, four compounds (1–4) showing antioxidant activity were isolated from this extract.

Identification of isolated antioxidants by LC‐MS

The isolated compounds were analyzed using HR‐ESI‐MS. Compound 4 showed an ion peak at m/z 933.1548 in HR‐ESI‐MS, and its molecular formula was determined to be C42H46O24. In addition, a comparison with the literature (Ferreres et al., 1995) identified compound 4 as quercetin 3‐O‐(6‐O‐trans‐p‐coumaroyl)‐β‐d‐glucopyranosyl (1→2)‐β‐d‐glucopyranosyl (1→2)‐β‐d‐glucopyranoside, which has previously been isolated from young seedpods of garden peas (Murakami et al., 2001) and pea shoots (Ferreres et al., 1995). Compounds 1–3 were similarly analyzed by MS and compared to the results of a previous study (Ferreres et al., 1995; Murakami et al., 2001). Compound 1 was identified as kaempferol 3‐sophorotrioside, compound 2 as kaempferol 3‐O‐(6‐O‐trans‐p‐coumaroyl)‐β‐d‐glucopyranosyl (1→2)‐β‐d‐glucopyranosyl (1→2)‐β‐d‐glucopyranoside, and compound 3 as quercetin 3‐sophorotrioside. These compounds (Figures 2 and 3) were classified as having sophorotrioside at the 3‐position of quercetin or kaempferol as aglycones. Compounds 2 and 4 both contain an ester‐linked p‐coumaric acid group.

Antioxidant activity evaluation of isolated compounds 1–4

The antioxidant activities of compounds 1–4 were evaluated using the DPPH radical scavenging assay. Compounds 3 and 4, which contain quercetin as an aglycone, showed stronger antioxidant activity in comparison to compounds 1 and 2, which contain kaempferol as an aglycone (Table 2). These results indicate that compounds 3 and 4 are the main antioxidant components in P. sativum.

Quantitative analysis of antioxidant components by LC‐MS

After identifying the main antioxidants in P. sativum, the quantities of these compounds (1–4) in the recultivated plant samples were determined using LC‐MS (Table 3). The plants grown in environment 1 displayed a significantly increased level of quercetin glycosides in comparison to those grown in environments 2 and 3; a 1.8‐fold higher quantity of compound 3 and 6.2‐fold higher quantity of compound 4 were isolated from the P. sativum recultivated in environment 1 in comparison to those recultivated in environment 2, which is the most representative of plants recultivated in a typical household. Kaempferol glycosides were also isolated in higher quantities in the plants of environment 1 in comparison to those of environment 2, but the difference was not as large as that for quercetin glycosides; for instance, compound 2 was isolated in 2.7‐fold higher quantities from the plants recultivated in environment 1 in comparison to those in environment 2.

Although these results indicate that all flavonol glycosides 1–4 contribute to the high antioxidant activity of P. sativum, quercetin glycosides 3 and 4, which were shown to have particularly high antioxidant activity, were notably increased in the recultivated plants grown in environment 1. Interestingly, the p‐coumaric acid acyl‐linked compounds 2 and 4 were also particularly increased in the recultivated plants grown in environment 1.

Matsunaga et al. (2016) reported that the amount of catechins isolated from tea‐leaf shoots increased as the amount of sunlight received by the shoots increased. Seong et al. (2016) reported that more sun‐exposed areas of Chinese cabbages produced higher amounts of antioxidant content. The increase in polyphenols in sun‐exposed Chinese cabbages is consistent with the increase in polyphenols observed for sunlight‐exposed recultivated pea bean seedlings in the current study. However, this outcome contradicts the result that the component specifically bound to p‐coumaric acid increases. Furthermore, flavonoids in legumes have been widely reported as regulators of elongation, differentiation, and defense systems, among other secondary metabolites (Woźniak et al., 2017). The increase in flavonoids observed in the current study during recultivation in environment 1 may be attributable to this metabolic process.

These results show that it is possible to grow pea bean seedlings with higher antioxidative properties by growing them under sunlight. However, the pea bean seedlings grown in environment 1 have a strong, bitter taste; hence, they cannot be eaten raw. In general, polyphenols are known to exhibit bitterness (Soares et al., 2018), and we hypothesize that the increase in polyphenols in the plants recultivated in environment 1 is directly responsible for this stronger, bitter taste. This phenomenon is well‐established in the context of green tea cultivation. Gyokuro tea leaves maintain high concentrations of theanine and amino acids as flavor components and low amounts of catechins; the quality of the sprouts is maintained by limiting their exposure to sunlight. In contrast, typical green tea is characterized by a bitter taste because it is not shaded from sunlight during growth and therefore maintains high concentrations of catechins (Sakaida et al., 2010). The bitterness of pea bean seedlings can likely be controlled by the recultivation environment to obtain pea bean seedlings useful for specific applications.

CONCLUSION

In this study, the antioxidant activity of pea bean seedlings recultivated in different conditions was analyzed. Furthermore, the main antioxidants present in recultivated pea bean seedlings were isolated and quantified. In particular, we observed a significant increase in the amount of p‐coumaric acid bound to quercetin glycosides as the duration of sunlight exposure increased. This was determined to be an important factor in the enhancement of antioxidant activity. However, although these are useful findings, they are limited to Japan and do not guarantee that these interventions will be equally effective in other countries or environments where factors such as water, temperature, and sunlight differ. To achieve the goal of making the world healthier through the consumption of microscale vegetables, these techniques will help analyze microscale vegetables from each region.
