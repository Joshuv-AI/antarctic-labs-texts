Optimization on the Oblique Manifold for Sparse Simplex Constraints via Multiplicative Updates
Flavia Esposito, Andersen Ang

Abstract
Low-rank optimization problems with sparse simplex constraints involve variables that must satisfy nonnegativity, sparsity, and sum-to-1 conditions, making their optimization particularly challenging due to the interplay between low-rank structures and constraints. These problems arise in various applications, including machine learning, signal processing, environmental fields, and computational biology. In this work, we propose a novel manifold optimization approach to efficiently tackle these problems. Our method leverages the geometry of oblique manifolds to reformulate the problem and introduces a new Riemannian optimization method based on Riemannian gradient descent that strictly maintains the simplex constraints. By exploiting the underlying manifold structure, our approach improves optimization efficiency. Experiments on synthetic and real datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method compared to standard Euclidean and Riemannian methods, paving the way for broader applications.

Contribution.

In this work, we introduce a novel Riemannian approach for solving (
NSSls
). The proposed approach consists of reformulating (
NSSls
) as a manifold optimization problem on the oblique manifold and solving the resulting problem using an adaptation of Riemannian Multiplicative Update (RMU), an optimization method introduced in
Esposito and Ang (2025)
. Thus, throughout the paper, RMU denotes the optimization algorithm, whereas the proposed approach refers to the combination of the manifold reformulation and the RMU solver. RMU is based on an approximate Riemannian gradient descent and employs a projection-free strategy that maintains the smoothness requirements on the manifold, namely the differentiability of the retraction and the continuity of the trajectory along the manifold geometry.
The advantage of RMU is computational efficiency in handling the simplex constraint: RMU solves (
NSSls
) by incorporating the constraint directly into the minimization process.
By reinterpreting the constrained problem in
\bm{H}
as a manifold-based optimization, applying an efficient Riemannian solver, and providing robust experimental validation against Euclidean and alternative Riemannian methods, we demonstrate the significant practical benefits of this specific combination.
Moreover, the proposed approach differs from related existing works as follows:

•

in

•

in

Specifically, the considered constraint is the k-capped simplex (

•

in
Guo et al. (2022)
, we have the same problem, but it is solved with a different optimization approach based on the Riemannian conjugate gradient (RCG) algorithm.

We compare RMU with RCG and a variant of
Qian et al. (2011)
, see Section
3

2
Manifold formulation

Constrained problem (
NSSls
) can be viewed as an unconstrained optimization problem on a Riemannian manifold, where the constraints define a smooth search space without boundaries.
In this setting, the optimization is performed directly on the manifold, effectively eliminating the need for explicit constraint handling
Boumal (2023)
.

The literature contains several approaches for handling constraints and penalizations similar to those in (
NSSls
)
Qian et al. (2011)
;
Guo et al. (2022)
.
However, many of these methods rely on Euclidean optimization and enforce the constraints through a post-normalization step, which may affect both efficiency and theoretical consistency.
In contrast, we formulate (
NSSls
) within the framework of Riemannian optimization on the oblique manifold, allowing the constraints to be intrinsically incorporated into the optimization process.
We also note that although the Riemannian Conjugate Gradient (RCG) method
Guo et al. (2022)
uses a similar formulation, it requires more steps and incurs higher per-iteration computational costs than the proposed method, as we will observe in Section
3
.

2.2
Oblique manifold

The oblique manifold
\mathcal{O}\mathcal{B}(r,n)
is the set of matrices whose columns have unit norm.
The set
\mathcal{O}\mathcal{B}(r,n)
is also the product of
r
spheres

\mathcal{S}^{n-1}\!=\!\{\bm{x}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{n}|\,\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\!=\!1\}

\mathcal{O}\mathcal{B}(r,n)
lies on individual spheres.
We refer the reader to
Tu (2010)
;
Boumal (2023)
;
Absil et al. (2008)
for details on the oblique manifold.
Our optimization problem can be seen as unconstrained optimization problem over a curved space, and we make use of Riemannian optimization tools to solve (
L1-quartic-ls
), where each update direction must preserve the column norms, maintaining a smooth but non-linear geometric structure.
To this end, several fundamental concepts from Riemannian optimization are required:

•

the tangent space, which provides a local linear approximation of the manifold;

•

the projection onto the tangent (and normal) spaces, used to compute the Riemannian gradient that satisfies the geometric constraints, unlike the Euclidean gradient, which is generally not tangent to the manifold;

•

and the retraction, which maps an updated point back onto the manifold after each optimization step.

Below, we detail these concepts for the oblique manifold.
The tangent space of
\mathcal{O}\mathcal{B}(r,n)
at a point
\bm{A}
, denoted as
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{A}}\mathcal{O}\mathcal{B}(r,n)

\mathcal{T}_{\bm{A}}\mathcal{O}\mathcal{B}(r,n)=\{\bm{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}\,|\,\textrm{diag}(\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{Z})=\bm{0}\},

with the associated null space
\mathcal{N}_{\bm{A}}\mathcal{O}\mathcal{B}(r,n)\!=\!\{\bm{A}\bm{D}\,|\,\bm{D}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\text{ is diagonal}\}
.
The projectors over these spaces at
\bm{A}
for a generic matrix
\bm{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
are

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}_{\bm{A}}}(\bm{Z})=\bm{Z}-\bm{A}\textrm{diag}(\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{Z}),\quad\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{N}_{\bm{A}}}(\bm{Z})=\bm{A}\textrm{diag}(\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{Z}).

(1)

Lastly, we use metric retraction to move a point back to the manifold from the ambient Euclidean space:

\mathcal{R}_{\bm{A}}(\bm{Z})=(\bm{A}+\bm{Z})\big(\textrm{diag}\big((\bm{A}+\bm{Z})^{\top}(\bm{A}+\bm{Z})\big)^{-1/2}\big).

(2)

Element-wise operations may not preserve the tangent condition

We note that RMU is not strictly a Riemannian gradient descent method, as the element-wise operations, as linear transformations in general, do not preserve the tangent condition of a manifold at a point.
However, we can still perform metric retraction regardless of the point is on the tangent space or not.
This phenomenon may contribute to a slower convergence rate, a tendency that was clearly reflected in the outcomes of our experimental analysis in Section
3

Furthermore, without strict adherence to the tangent space, the rigorous theoretical guarantees of monotonic descent standard in Riemannian gradient methods are not assured, meaning the algorithm relies heavily on the local metric preservation properties of the retraction to achieve convergence in practice.
In the case of the oblique manifold, the convergence of RMU is not affected by the lack of a tangent condition, since the update is mapped back to the manifold through the retraction, and a descent property can still be established locally.
To this aim, we have the following local convergence theorem.

Theorem 2

(Local Convergence)
.

Let
f:\mathcal{O}\mathcal{B}(r,n)\!\to\!\mathbb{R}

be a continuously differentiable function bounded from below in an open neighborhood containing the sequence
\{\bm{A}^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
generated by RMU. Assume that: i) the Riemannian gradient
\textrm{grad}f
is locally Lipschitz continuous; ii) the retraction is the metric retraction

defined in (
2

0<\alpha_{\min}\leq(\bm{\alpha}^{k})_{ij}\leq\alpha_{\max},

iv) either the iterates avoid the nondifferentiable points of the entrywise

Let the iterates be generated by RMU as
\bm{A}^{k+1}=\mathcal{R}_{\bm{A}^{k}}\big(\!-\!\bm{\alpha}^{k}\!\odot\!\textrm{grad}f(\bm{A}^{k})\big).

Hence every accumulation point of the sequence

f(\mathcal{R}_{\bm{A}^{k}}(\bm{\eta}^{k}))=f\big(\bm{A}^{k}+\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}_{\bm{A}^{k}}}(\bm{\eta}^{k})+O(\|\bm{\eta}^{k}\|^{2})\big)
gives:

\begin{array}[]{rcll}f(\mathcal{R}_{\bm{A}^{k}}(\bm{\eta}^{k}))&=&f(\bm{A}^{k})+\langle\nabla f(\bm{A}^{k}),~\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}_{\bm{A}^{k}}}(\bm{\eta}^{k})\rangle+O(\|\bm{\eta}^{k}\|^{2})\\[2.84526pt]
&=&f(\bm{A}^{k})+\langle\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}_{\bm{A}^{k}}}(\nabla f(\bm{A}^{k})),~\bm{\eta}^{k}\rangle+O(\|\bm{\eta}^{k}\|^{2})\\[2.84526pt]
&=&f(\bm{A}^{k})+\langle\textrm{grad}f(\bm{A}^{k}),~\bm{\eta}^{k}\rangle+O(\|\bm{\eta}^{k}\|^{2}),\end{array}

where the last two equalities hold by the properties of the orthogonal projector and the definition of the Riemannian gradient.
Now, since
\langle\textrm{grad}f(\bm{A}^{k}),\bm{\eta}^{k}\rangle\leq-\alpha_{\min}\|\textrm{grad}f(\bm{A}^{k})\|^{2}
and
\|\bm{\eta}^{k}\|^{2}\leq\alpha_{\max}^{2}\|\textrm{grad}f(\bm{A}^{k})\|^{2}
, we have:

f(\bm{A}^{k+1})\leq f(\bm{A}^{k})-c\|\textrm{grad}f(\bm{A}^{k})\|^{2},\qquad c=\alpha_{\min}-S\alpha_{\max}^{2}

where
S
is the existing nonnegative constant such that
\|O(\|\eta\|^{2})\|\!\leq\!S\|\eta\|^{2}
.
For
\alpha_{\max}
sufficiently small we have
c>0
, which guarantees a descent direction.
As
f
is bounded from below, the sequence
\{f(\bm{A}^{k})\}_{k}
is decreasing and convergent, which implies
\|\textrm{grad}f(\bm{A}^{k})\|\!\to\!0
.
∎

We are now ready to describe the details of RMU for solving (
L1-quartic-ls
).

2.4
Algorithm for minimizing (
L1-quartic-ls
) over
\mathcal{O}\mathcal{B}(r,n)

Under the assumptions of Theorem 1, the objective function in (
L1-quartic-ls
) satisfies the hypotheses of RMU.
In fact, although the entry-wise
\ell_{1}
-norm is not differentiable on the
entire ambient space, the RMU iterates remain nonnegative when initialized
with
A^{0}\geq 0
, as established in Theorem 1. Therefore, along the RMU iterates, the
\ell_{1}
-norm reduces to the linear function
\|\bm{A}\|_{1}=\sum_{i,j}{A_{ij}}
, which is continuously differentiable on the nonnegative region.
Thus, Theorem 1 can be applied to this smooth representation of the objective
on the invariant nonnegative region.
Therefore, the update

\bm{A}^{k+1}\!=\!\mathcal{R}_{\bm{A}^{k}}\big(\!-\!\bm{\alpha}^{k}\!\odot\!\textrm{grad}f(\bm{A}^{k})\big)

converges to a stationary point of function (
L1-quartic-ls
) for
\bm{A}\in\mathcal{O}\mathcal{B}(r,n)
.
In this update, function
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
is the retraction (
2
), and
\textrm{grad}f(\cdot)
is the Riemannian gradient computed with the orthogonal projector over the tangent space in (
1
) over the Euclidean (sub-)gradient:

\nabla f(\bm{A})~=~-2\bm{W}^{\top}\big(\bm{X}-\bm{W}(\bm{A}\odot\bm{A})\big)\odot\bm{A}+\lambda^{\prime}\textrm{sign}(\bm{A}),

where
sign
is the element-wise sign function (subgradient of the
\ell_{1}
-norm) with
\textrm{sign}(x)=1
if
x>0
and
\textrm{sign}(x)=-1
if
x<0
and
\textrm{sign}(x)\in[-1,1]
if
x=0
, see for details
(
Beck, 2017
, Example 3.4)
.
The symbol
\lambda^{\prime}
is a rescaled
\lambda
(with respect to the
1/4
in
f
when taking the derivative).

Let
\bm{Q}=\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{W}
and
\bm{P}=\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{X}
, the Riemannian (sub-)gradient is defined as:

\hskip-5.69054pt\begin{array}[]{lll}\textrm{grad}f(\bm{A})=\underbrace{\big(\bm{Q}(\bm{A}\odot\bm{A})\big)\odot\bm{A}+\bm{A}\textrm{diag}\big[\bm{A}^{\top}(\bm{P}\odot\bm{A})\big]+\lambda^{\prime}\textrm{sign}(\bm{A})}_{\textrm{grad}^{+}f}\\
\qquad\qquad~-\underbrace{\biggl\{\bm{P}\odot\bm{A}+\bm{A}\textrm{diag}\big[\bm{A}^{\top}\big(\bm{Q}(\bm{A}\odot\bm{A})\big)\odot\bm{A}\big]+\lambda^{\prime}\bm{A}\textrm{diag}\big[\bm{A}^{\top}\textrm{sign}(\bm{A})\big]\biggr\}}_{\textrm{grad}^{-}f},\end{array}

(4)

where we used a sign-wise splitting

\textrm{grad}f(\bm{A})\!=\!\textrm{grad}^{+}f(\bm{A})-\textrm{grad}^{-}f(\bm{A})
as in (
3
).
Then, from Theorem
1
, considering
\bm{V}^{k}\!=\!-\textrm{grad}f(\bm{A}^{k})
, the sign-wise splitting in (
4
) and the element-wise stepsize
\bm{\alpha}^{k}
, we obtain the RMU update as:

\bm{B}^{k}\!=\!\bm{A}^{k}\odot\textrm{grad}^{-}f(\bm{A}^{k})\oslash\textrm{grad}^{+}f(\bm{A}^{k}),\quad\bm{A}^{k+1}\!=\!\bm{B}^{k}\oslash\textrm{diag}\big[(\bm{B}^{k})^{\top}\bm{B}^{k}\big]^{1/2},

(5)

where
\oslash
is element-wise division and,
\bm{B}^{k}
represents an intermediate Euclidean step taking the element-wise scaled gradient direction before retraction, and
\bm{\alpha}^{k}
is implicitly embedded in the ratio
\bm{A}^{k}\oslash\textrm{grad}^{+}f(\bm{A}^{k})
.
Finally, after
K
iterations, we have
\bm{A}^{K}
, and we compute
\bm{H}\!=\!\bm{A}^{K}\!\odot\!\bm{A}^{K}
.

Remark 4
.

The entry-wise
\ell_{1}
-norm is generally nonsmooth at matrices containing
zero entries. However, RMU is initialized with a nonnegative matrix and
preserves nonnegativity at every iteration. Hence, along the RMU iterates,

\|A\|_{1}=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij},

which is the restriction to the nonnegative orthant of a continuously
differentiable linear function. Its Euclidean gradient is the all-ones
matrix. At an entry such that
A_{ij}=0
, this corresponds to selecting

1\in[-1,1]=\partial|A_{ij}|,

which is a valid element of the subdifferential of the original

\ell_{1}
-norm. Therefore, the gradient employed by RMU is consistent both
with the smooth representation of the objective on the nonnegative region and with the subdifferential of the original objective.
The subgradient of
\|\cdot\|_{1}
, denoted as
\partial\|\bm{A}\|_{1}
, has the form

\partial\|\bm{A}\|_{1}=\textrm{sign}(\bm{A})=\bm{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n},~~\text{where }G_{ij}\in\begin{cases}\{0\}&\text{ if }A_{ij}\neq 0,\\
[-1,1]&\text{ if }A_{ij}=0.\end{cases}

Moreover, we remark that the structure
\textrm{sign}(\bm{A})-\bm{A}\textrm{diag}\big[\bm{A}^{\top}\textrm{sign}(\bm{A})\big]
comes from the projectors in (
1
) applied on
\partial\|\bm{A}\|_{1}
.

Remark 5
.

\bm{A}^{k}\!\oslash\!\textrm{grad}^{+}f(\bm{A}^{k})
in (
5
) is implemented as

\bm{A}^{k}\!\oslash\!\big(\textrm{grad}^{+}f(\bm{A}^{k})+\epsilon\big)
, with
\epsilon\!>\!0
a small positive constant preventing division by zero introduced to avoid division by zero.

Validity of the Retraction of Point Outside the Tangent Space

As mentioned in Section
2.3
the element-wise operations may not preserve the tangent condition, which is generally required for the retraction
\mathcal{R}(\cdot):\mathcal{T}_{\bm{A}}\mathcal{O}\mathcal{B}(r,n)\to\mathcal{O}\mathcal{B}(n,r)
.
We emphasize that because the retraction
\mathcal{R}
is specifically designed to normalize the columns of the input matrix, it maps points back to
\mathcal{O}\mathcal{B}(r,n)
, regardless of whether the input matrix lies in the tangent space.
In fact, the normalization operator defining the retraction can also be applied to points

\bm{Z}\notin\mathcal{T}_{\bm{A}}\mathcal{O}\mathcal{B}(r,n)
.
Furthermore, depending on the location of the point
\bm{Z}
, the metric properties of the retraction
\mathcal{R}(\bm{Z})
are approximately preserved for small
\bm{Z}
, which is sufficient in practical optimization.
This implies that the retraction remains well-defined when applied to directions outside the tangent space and, by its first-order consistency, we can assert that locally the descent property and convergence are guaranteed as stated in Theorem
2
.

Numerical Experiments

In this section, we show numerical results of RMU compared with other methods and tested on different synthetic and real datasets.
Specifically, we first evaluate the proposed approach on synthetic datasets against Euclidean and Riemannian methods.
Second, we present a preliminary experiment on a real dataset to compare the proposed method strictly within the Riemannian optimization framework
4
4

4

The experiments are conducted in MATLAB 2023a on a machine with OS Windows 11 Pro on an AMD Ryzen 7 7700 8-Core Processor CPU 3.80 GHz, and 64GB RAM.
.

Comparative methods.

We compare RMU with three methods: one Riemannian optimization method and two standard Euclidean optimization methods.

•

Riemannian method
:

Guo et al. (2022)
proposed a Riemannian Conjugate Method (RCG) for solving (
NSSls
).
In addition to the Riemannian gradient, RCG also computes: i) a conjugate direction, a hybrid (Dai-Yuan and Hestenes-Stiefel) stepsize using vector transport, iii) update stepsize using Wolfe line search (with at most
5
iterations, following
Gillis (2020)
).

•

Euclidean-based methods.

We consider two Euclidean-based approaches.

–

EMUproj heuristic
:
For Problem (
NSSls
) without the sum-to-1 constraint, the following update formula for
\bm{H}
is proposed by

Qian et al. (2011)

\textstyle\bm{H}_{k+1}^{\text{EMU}}=\bm{H}_{k}\odot\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{M}\oslash\Big(\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{W}\bm{H}_{k}\oplus\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{ij}\sqrt{H_{ij}}\Big),

which is a modified version of the work by Hoyer
Hoyer (2002)
, where
\oplus
is element-wise addition.
To solve Problem (
NSSls
) by EMU, we consider the heuristic

\bm{H}_{k+1}=\textrm{proj}_{\Delta}[\bm{H}_{k+1}^{\text{EMU}}],

where
\textrm{proj}_{\Delta}
is the column-wise projection onto the unit-simplex.
Given a matrix
\bm{X}=[\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}]
with
n
columns, then
\textrm{proj}_{\Delta}(\bm{X})
replaces each
\bm{x}_{i}
by
\bm{x}_{i}/\|\bm{x}_{i}\|_{1}
.

–

SparseMU-proj heuristic
:
The sparse multiplicative update proposed by
Eggert and Korner (2004)
has a form similar to EMU

\bm{H}_{k+1}^{\text{SMUL1}}=\bm{H}_{k}\odot\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{M}\oslash\Big(\bm{W}^{\top}\bm{W}\bm{H}_{k}\oplus\lambda\Big),

which minimizes a different objective function from the one in (
NSSls
), as

\textstyle\argmin_{\bm{H}\geq 0}\tfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{X}-\bm{W}\bm{H}\|_{F}^{2}+\lambda\|\bm{H}\|_{1}.

We note that the difference between EMU and SMUL1 is on the regularization parameter, where the latter uses a constant value of
\lambda
, while the former adopts a dynamically changing
\lambda
scaled by
\sum\sqrt{H_{ij}}
.
To solve Problem (
NSSls
) by SMUL1, we consider the heuristic with the same projection of EMUproj:

\bm{H}_{k+1}=\textrm{proj}_{\Delta}[\bm{H}_{k+1}^{\text{SMUL1}}].

We emphasize that SparseMU-proj is not designed to solve (
NSSls
), however we still plot in our experiment for comparisons due to its proximity to our problem.

Remark 6
.

Note that applying these heuristic projections a posteriori destroys the monotonic decrease guarantees of the original Euclidean multiplicative update rules.
Consequently, these modified Euclidean baselines lack formal convergence guarantees, serving purely as heuristic benchmarks.

Experimental settings.

We generate a matrix
\bm{X}
as the product of two low-rank matrices
\bm{W}_{\text{true}},\bm{H}_{\text{true}}
such as
\bm{X}\!=\!\bm{W}_{\text{true}}\bm{H}_{\text{true}}\!+\!\sigma\bm{E}
.
Both matrices
\bm{W}_{\text{true}},\bm{H}_{\text{true}}
are generated by sampling a uniform distribution, with
\bm{H}_{\text{true}}
explicitly constrained to have exactly
s\%
non-zero entries.
The matrix
\bm{E}
is a noise matrix following a uniform distribution, with
\sigma\!\in\!\mathbb{R}
as scaling factor.
We perform 100 Monte Carlo runs of the algorithms according to the randomness of the dataset generation.
Each of the Monte Carlo runs uses a different random seed to regenerate the synthetic matrices (
\bm{W}_{\text{true}}
and
\bm{H}_{\text{true}}
) from the specified uniform distributions and different levels of noise according to the scaling factor (
\sigma=0,0.1,0.3
).
This has been done to ensure statistical robustness of the experiments.

All the algorithms start with the same initialization and penalty parameter
\lambda
.
We initialize the algorithms by NNSVD
Boutsidis and
Gallopoulos (2008)
.
Given an initial variable
H_{\text{ini}}
, we select the parameter
\lambda
scaled by the ratio

\|\bm{X}-\bm{W}\bm{H}_{\text{ini}}\|_{F}^{2}/\|\bm{H}_{\text{ini}}\|_{1/2}
, which balances the least squares part and the sparsity part at the beginning of the optimization process.
We stop the algorithms when they reach either the maximum number of iterations
k_{\max}
or the maximum runtime
T_{\max}
.
For the experiments, we strictly relied on these maximum iterations and runtime limits as the stopping criteria, rather than a relative tolerance on the objective, to evaluate the performance profile over the entire allocated time budget.

AUC evaluation metrics.

Because the methods have different per-iteration complexities, plotting the function value
F
against the iteration
k
or time
t
alone provides an incomplete and unfair comparison.
See Fig.
2
.

Figure 2
:
A convergence plot of the methods (where
F_{\text{min}}
is the minimum objective value achieved across all compared algorithms for that specific run).
Here, in terms of iterations, the convergence of RCG has the best performance; however, it takes the highest amount of computational time for performing one iteration.

For each method we report the mean and standard deviation of the objective function, a ranking based on the area under the curve (AUC) metric, and a median sparsity measure of
\bm{H}
.
The ranking based on the AUC metric is computed over 100 Monte-Carlo runs.
The
i
th entry of the ranking vector records how many times an algorithm produced the
i
th best solution.
For instance, a ranking tuple
(a,b,c,d)
means the method ranked 1st
a
times, 2nd
b
times, 3rd
c
times, and 4th
d
times out of the 100 runs.
For each run, we execute all algorithms using the same initialization and modeling parameters, and obtain four sequences of the form
\{t_{k},F_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
F_{k}
is the objective value at iteration
k
, and
t_{k}
is the corresponding runtime.
Because different algorithms have different per-iteration costs, we use AUC to compare their performance fairly.
This time-based AUC evaluation metric is a customized metric proposed in this work to explicitly account for the varying per-iteration computational costs of different algorithms.
Over the time interval
[0,T_{\max}]
, we define the AUC as
\int_{0}^{T_{\max}}\!F(t)\text{d}t
,
which represents the total accumulated objective value over time.
For a minimization problem, a smaller AUC indicates that the algorithm kept the objective low for most of the runtime.
Numerically, we treat
F(t)
as a continuous function obtained by interpolating the points
\{t_{k},F_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, and approximate the integral using the trapezoidal rule

\Delta_{k}\!=\!(F_{k-1}+F_{k})(t_{k}-t_{k-1})/2
.
The AUC of a method is the sum of all
\Delta_{k}
.
We use AUC for the following reasons:

•

It ignores the iteration index
k
and evaluates performance in real time
t
, which allows a fair comparison of algorithms with different iteration counts.

•

The per-iteration computational cost is naturally captured in
t_{k}
; expensive iterations are beneficial only if they achieve a sufficiently large decrease in
F
.

•

When algorithms generate objective values at different timestamps
t_{k}
, interpolation creates comparable curves
F(t)
over the common interval
[0,T_{\max}]
.
Thus, AUC enables fair comparison without requiring explicit synchronization of the curves.

•

AUC is robust to early stopping: if an algorithm terminates before
T_{\max}
, interpolation/extrapolation allows evaluation over the entire time window, ensuring consistent comparison across methods.

Sparsity measure
: We also report the percentage sparsity of the output
\bm{H}_{\text{Last}}
as
\text{nnz}(\bm{H}_{\text{Last}}\!>\!10^{-9})/nr\%
.
That is, we take entries in
\bm{H}_{\text{Last}}
below
10^{-9}
as zero, and count the zeros in
\bm{H}_{\text{Last}}
with respect to the total number of entries
rn
.

4
Conclusion

In this work, we have proposed a novel manifold optimization method to solve low-rank problems with sparse simplex constraints by leveraging oblique manifolds.
Our approach ensures that the nonnegativity, sparsity, and sum-to-1 conditions are preserved throughout the optimization process while maintaining the convergence properties of Riemannian gradient descent.

Experiments on synthetic datasets have demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed method compared to standard Euclidean and Riemannian optimization techniques.
Specifically, we obtained comparable, and sometimes superior, solutions faster than the baseline methods.
Moreover, unlike the RCG approach, the proposed RMU does not require computing vector transport or performing Wolfe line searches, making RMU appealing for large-scale problems.
In fact, our approach exhibits improved convergence behavior, numerical stability, and the ability to better exploit the underlying low-rank structure of the problem, particularly for high-dimensional problems.
These advantages make it particularly suitable for applications where structured low-rank solutions with simplex constraints are essential, such as in signal processing, machine learning, and computational biology.

Future work will focus on extending the method to explore adaptive strategies for manifold parameterization and investigating its applicability to real-world datasets in diverse domains.
