Crack-localization in elastomeric self-healing enabled by one supermolecule-analogous molecule
Authors: Xingyi Wang, Shuan Han, Shuchang Luo, Qiuju Li, Jing Li, Wei Li, Lin Gan, Jin Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractResidual invisible cracks make perfect material self-healing difficult to achieve. In principle, cracks could be visualized by luminescent indicators; however, high-energy fluctuations associated with rapid healing kinetics interfere with the electronic transitions of luminophores. Here, grouped secondary bonds are combined in a phenylborate-derived chain extender (BOB-1) in the structural form of supermolecule-analogous dimer, inhibiting conformation perturbation to enhance radiative transition. Asymmetric intramolecular O → B coordination affords a dynamic-exchange activation energy as low as 32.88 kJ·mol−1, enabling second-level healing at room-temperature and minute-level healing at 0 °C. Incorporated into polyurethane elastomers through a facile chain-extension procedure, this dynamic motif produces a distinguishable photoluminescent redshift upon O → B coordination, thereby localizing cracks. The material achieves near-100% mechanical recovery and retains at least 95% strength, over twice that of apparently flaw-free controls. Notably, only a 12.5 mol% BOB-1 fraction is required for chain-extending functionality, the engineering crack-visualization of self-healing integrity verification is expected.Subject terms: Polymers, Mechanical properties, Organic molecules in materials science

Introduction

Self-healing functionality is an effective strategy to address the cracks that inevitably form in polymeric devices during service1,2. Although intrinsic self-healing materials can restore structural continuity after damage, the real-time evaluation of healing completeness remains elusive3–8. Unmonitored self-healing often retains sub-visual-scale residual microdefects9–11. Therefore, recent studies on self-healing materials have mainly focused on the visualization of the healing process and the development of energy-free autonomous healing systems12–15.

On the one hand, spatiotemporally resolved interrogation of how healing proceeds is equally critical16,17. Using laser speckle imaging (LSI) micromechanics, researchers have directly visualized a cooperative sequence near the damaged zone—from delocalized viscoelastic relaxation to interfacial adhesion and chain interpenetration—and mapped the activation frequency window of healing onto the material’s linear relaxation spectrum, establishing a quantitative connection between healing kinetics and material spectroscopy18. On the other hand, recent studies on glassy or high-modulus polyurethane systems show that high-density hydrogen bonds, isocyanate-derived multivalent weak interactions, or hyperbranched molecular topologies can markedly compress the healing timescale at room temperature, achieving ultrafast healing with strength recovery >20 MPa within 1 min19; water-mediated bridging/activation layers have likewise been shown to accelerate interfacial network reconstruction20. These advances indicate that when dynamic bonds at the crack interface complete break–relink–rearrangement on faster secondary relaxations/local segmental motions—ahead of the α-glass relaxation—they can bypass the bottleneck of slow bulk chain diffusion and realize rapid load-bearing recovery at room temperature21,22. Together, these studies suggest that dynamic-bond architectures, such as hydrogen bonding, coordination interactions, and reversible covalent linkages, provide a promising basis for coupling autonomous healing with damage visualization in polymer systems23–29. However, achieving both rapid room-temperature mechanical recovery and reliable optical reporting within a single material system remains a substantial challenge30–34. The ideal system should accomplish interfacial reconstruction at room temperature on a seconds timescale while fluorophores remain capable of reliably reporting the microscopic healing trajectory35–38. Supermolecule self-healing fluorescence offers a promising pathway toward this goal. In supramolecular luminophore systems, the emission originates not from isolated chromophores but from conformationally adaptive aggregates whose electronic states are tightly coupled to dynamic bonding events39,40. Unlike conventional fluorophores that suffer photophysical instability under fluctuating local energy fields, supramolecule-based emissive units leverage coordination-assisted rigidity enhancement, restricted intramolecular motion, and environment-dependent electronic coupling to maintain robust emission even during fast dynamic exchange41–46. Therefore, mechanical damage triggers specific configurational changes—such as bond scission, coordination rearrangement, or dimer-monomer transitions—that translate directly into predictable shifts in both color and intensity47–49. Nevertheless, the energy variations are still excessively drastic, resulting in significantly enhanced nonradiative decay pathways50,51.

Here, we propose a supermolecule-analogous architecture in which low-energy coordination endows the system with a low activation energy for rapid dynamic exchange. In concert with dynamic covalent interactions, this coordination synergistically locks the molecular conformation and suppresses nonradiative decay. Then, we introduced intramolecular O → B coordination around phenylborate to form asymmetric dimers, whose dynamic change is designed to induce photoluminescent shift. In this work, dimer denotes the borate-based paired structural motif generated through coordination-enabled association, which serves as the fundamental switching unit in the present system. We attempted to introduce a minimal loading of the dimer into the polymer network to construct a molecular switch-type self-warning mechanism, in which asymmetric supramolecular units constitute the intrinsic sensing-reporting modules. Density functional theory and molecular dynamics simulations were used to study the stereochemical design requirements necessary to maintain low activation energy. A comparative investigation between naked-eye observation and UV-based monitoring further validates the feasibility of the minimally added functional integration. The advantages and limitations of supermolecule-analog, relative to supramolecular systems, are further investigated, even alongside their potential synergistic effects. Therefore, the proposed material design is expected to realize integrated functionalities of damage perception, optical self-reporting, and autonomous repair, providing valuable guidance for the development of next-generation intelligent polymers and adaptive protective materials in flexible electronics, aerospace, and structural health monitoring fields.

Results

Synthesis and kinetic properties of B–O dimeric molecules

Figure 1 and Supplementary Table 1 summarize a series of synthesized supermolecule-analogous dimer compounds, based on phenolbonate, prepared to investigate how O → B coordination governs dimer stability and dynamic exchange. As shown in Fig. 1a, a reaction between 2-formylbenzeneboronic acid and aliphatic hydrazide is designed to obtain boron–amide heterocycles (Ⅰ), whose experimental NMR spectra are displayed in Supplementary Figs.1–8. The results also reveal that when the number (n) of -CH2- units in the aliphatic group satisfies 1 ≤ n < 3, the dimeric species in the dashed box in Fig. 1a is not formed. By contrast, when n ≥ 3, the molecular conformation is locked by an intramolecular O → B coordination bond. n is counted along the longest-chain direction of R², starting from the carbonyl-adjacent α-carbon and ending at the terminal carbon of R² (or at the carbon bearing the -OH group, where applicable). This value is expressed as an equivalent methylene number, and thus applies to both linear and branched R² substituents. Supplementary Table 2, together with the single-crystal structure of BOB-2 (Fig. 1k and Supplementary Figs. 9, 10 and Supplementary Tables 3–5), confirms the exact dimer structure and the presence of the O → B coordination bond. Meanwhile, UPLC-MS/MS (Ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry) and HRMS (High-resolution mass spectrometry) were used to confirm the rapid exchange within the BOB-X (X represents compounds 1 to 8 in Supplementary Table 1) family (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Table 6). UPLC–MS/MS analysis was performed on an equimolar mixture of BOB-2 and BOB-3, with each component at 2.42 × 10−7 mol/L, immediately after mixing, using a T3 column with water containing 0.1% formic acid and acetonitrile as the mobile phases under ESI+ mode. In the room-temperature mixture, the characteristic peaks of BOB-2 and BOB-3 decreased by ~45.8% and ~45.3%, respectively, and a new peak appeared at an intermediate retention time with the transition m/z 387.0000 → 317.2500 (+). In the same sample, high-resolution MS (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 11) simultaneously resolved three singly charged peaks at m/z 359.1487 (BOB-2), 387.1800 (assigned to BOB-3-2), and 415.2114 (BOB-3), supporting the formation of a new solution species after mixing. The above results illustrate that these dimers have the dynamic exchange ability of covalent bonds, as displayed in Fig. 1b.

Figure 1e and S12 present time-resolved 1H NMR, which demonstrates the exchange kinetics. At 20 °C, a new set of signals assigned to BOB-3-2 appears within 1 min in the 2.70–3.05 ppm window. The intensities of those new peaks reach equilibrium after 10 min, and we use the peak integration to calculate the content of products, as shown in Supplementary Table 7, whose results are consistent with those from UPLC-MS/MS. Temperature-resolved 1H NMR was also employed to verify the dynamic equilibrium (Supplementary Fig. 13). Under a fixed reaction time of 10 min, BOB-3-2 is even detectable at 0 °C, indicating that exchange proceeds at low temperature. Increasing temperature from 10 °C to 30 °C drives the reaction toward equilibrium, as indicated by the integral-based quantification of product formation, as shown in Fig. 1f. Kinetic fits an equimolar reversible second-order model yield k values (Fig. 1g); and Arrhenius analysis gives lnk = –3.95/T + 11.74 and an activation energy Ea = 32.88 kJ mol−1. Such a low Ea, corresponding to molecular motion energy at 19.4 oC (based on calculation in Equation S18 and S19), suggests the dimer is capable of room-temperature (or even low-temperature) fast self-reconstruction.

Figure 1h shows the DFT (Density functional theory)-calculated dynamic exchange route of the dimer, and the obtained overall Gibbs free energy change of −1.1 kcal·mol−1 confirms the thermodynamic spontaneity of this dynamic exchange. Starting from BOB-2 + BOB-3 (0.0 kcal·mol−1), the approach of a water molecule generates a hydrogen-bonded adduct INT1 (12.8 kcal·mol−1), during which the boron atom shifts toward a transient five-coordinate state. In INT1, the scissile B–O bond becomes activated. Upon crossing the rate-determining transition state TS (21.9 kcal·mol−1), the formation of the new B–O bond proceeds concurrently with the cleavage of the old B–O bond. Simultaneously, the water molecules work as a proton shuttle, protonating the leaving group in situ and further lowering the energy barrier. Figure 1i and Supplementary Fig. 14 identify the nucleophilic entry site of water through ESP (electrostatic potential) mapping. Both BOB-2 and BOB-3 display pronounced negative potential minima adjacent to the borate dimer ring (−40.15 and −40.73 kcal·mol−1, respectively), verifying the preferred attack site (blue star) for water and fully supporting the mechanistic pathway proposed in Fig. 1h.

Through dynamic molecular design, this work further compares the dissociation energy of the borate dimer with that of the classical boroxine trimer using DFT calculations (Fig. 1j). This strategy effectively lowers the activation barrier for B–O exchange: the DFT-calculated dissociation energy of the dimer is 221.50 kcal·mol−1, which is less than half of that of the trimer. Considering that conventional boroxine trimers are known to undergo dynamic exchange even at room temperature, the substantially lower dissociation energy of the dimer strongly suggests that the present system should exhibit dynamic exchange capability even at low temperatures. All the DFT-predicted structural features are verified experimentally by single-crystal X-ray diffraction (Fig. 1k). Specifically, both the O → B coordination bond lengths (1.586 Å and 1.561 Å) and B–O bond length (1.406 Å) closely match the DFT-optimized values (1.564 Å for O → B and 1.408 Å for B–O). Taken together, these results validate the supermolecule-analogous structure proposed in Fig. 1a and firmly support the low-temperature, rapid dynamic exchange ability of the designed dimer.

Design of second-scale self-healing elastomers

Figure 2 shows the mechanical properties of a series of polyurethane elastomers denoted as PU-A(y)B(1-y), where PU represents the PTEMG/HMDI prepolymer, A and B correspond to ADH and BOB-1, respectively, and y denotes the molar fraction of ADH, while (1-y) corresponds to the molar fraction of BOB-1 in the total chain-extender content (A + B = 0.01 mol). In Fig. 2a, the mechanical performance of PU-A(0)B(1) is characterized by a tensile strength of 2.2 MPa, a compliance of 0.325 MPa−1, and an elongation at break of 1881%. Another dynamic exchange system of polyurethane whose chain is extended by a supermolecular dimer of ADH (PU-A(1)B(0)) is used for comparison. The compliance (J) and elongation at break (εb) of PU-A(0)B(1) are 3.03-fold and 1.92-fold higher, respectively, when its tensile strength is only 7% of that of the ADH-based system. Motivated by this complementary behavior, we combined the two dynamic chain-extenders to investigate the synergistic effects between hydrogen bonding and the borate dimer. As shown in Fig. 2e, we assume that the dynamic bonds within the dimer can undergo almost complete reconstruction even at low temperatures, whereas hydrogen bonds can only be partially reformed. Upon introducing a small amount of BOB-1, PU-A(0.75)B(0.25) shows pronounced strain hardening and reaches a higher ultimate strength of 54.3 MPa with εb = 1363%. With further increases in BOB-1 content, the modulus and strength decrease slightly, while the fracture strain rises markedly. Among these compositions, the experimental results (Fig. 2b) for PU-A(0.5)B(0.5) indeed demonstrate that when the ratio of ADH (A) to BOB-1 (B) is 1:1, the tensile strength and modulus increase by 18.7-fold and 2.2-fold, respectively, compared with PU-A(0)B(1), while the fracture strain incurs only a minor reduction of 0.16-fold, resulting in a substantial increase in toughness, ADH increases tensile strength σb and modulus E, whereas BOB-1 enhances fracture strain εb and room-temperature self-healing capability. Consequently, when the two chain-extenders are combined (synthetic route and structural characterizations shown in Supplementary Figs. 15–17), the resulting network achieves a synergistic combination of high mechanical strength and dynamic self-healing capability.

Figure 2c shows the strain-recovery efficiency of PU-A(0.5)B(0.5) as a function of healing time at 298 K and 273 K. The strain-recovery efficiency was calculated as εb,healed/εb,original × 100%, where εb,healed is the elongation at break of the healed sample after a given healing time and εb,original is the elongation at break of pristine PU-A(0.5)B(0.5). The value of εb,original used for normalization was 1577%, as determined from the tensile test shown in Fig. 2a. The rapid recovery of elongation at break within short healing periods reflects efficient network reconstruction enabled by reversible bond exchange and dynamic network rearrangement. Therefore, the high strain-recovery efficiency suggests fast dynamic exchange kinetics and network relaxation in the polymer network. Considering that the dynamic exchange of conventional H-bond networks typically requires temperatures above 40 °C and exhibits activation energies much higher than those of reported room-temperature dynamic systems (Fig. 2d), the high level of activity observed here is remarkable17,19,52–60. At −50 °C (Supplementary Figs. 18, 19 and Supplementary Movie 1), PU-A(0.5)B(0.5) can still be stretched stably to 505% under high loads of 80.1 MPa. By contrast, PU-A(1)B(0) and PU-A(0)B(1) fail at ≈200–250% at this temperature with pronounced strength loss. These observations indicate that tuning the ADH/BOB-1 ratio ensures sufficient chain mobility at low temperature and that B–O exchange at BOB-1 sites remains active; together with reversible H-bond reconstruction, PU-A(0.5)B(0.5) exhibits outstanding cold tolerance and anti-brittle toughness. Mechanistically, ADH H-bond clusters furnish load-bearing and energy dissipation, whereas O → B pre-coordination–mediated associative B–O exchange at BOB-1 sites promotes chain sliding and network topological rearrangement under large strains.

Supplementary Figure 20 and Supplementary Movie 2 show that, after introducing BOB-1, the system displays visually discernible, seconds-scale self-healing under ambient conditions. Using PU-A(0.5)B(0.5) as a representative, a specimen healed for only 90 s at 298 K can already be stretched to ~700% of its original length without failure. Supplementary Figure 21a further demonstrates the macroscopic healing kinetics at 298 K. Rejoining occurs rapidly, giving tensile strengths of 7.3 MPa at 90 s and 12.3 MPa at 180 s, and >1000% strain recovery is achieved within 180 s. Supplementary Figure 21b presents the corresponding healing behavior at 273 K. Although healing slows at lower temperatures, PU-A(0.5)B(0.5) presents increasing recovered tensile strengths, reaching over 90% after 2 h. Figure 2c compares healing efficiencies at 298 K and 273 K, together with Supplementary Fig. 22 for the Arrhenius analysis, yielding an apparent macroscopic self-healing activation energy of Ea = 54.23 kJ·mol−1. This value is slightly higher than the molecular B–O site-exchange activation energy (Ea = 32.88 kJ·mol−1), indicating that macroscopic healing involves not only associative boronate exchange but also H-bond reorganization. Consistently, as shown in Supplementary Figs. 24 and 25, SEM-EDS verifies the spatial distribution of BOB: elemental maps of both the surface and the fracture cross-section are uniform at the resolvable scale, and the quantified boron contents in the surface and bulk are essentially identical (3.2 vs. 3.1 wt%). Within experimental resolution and error, no B-related surface segregation or phase separation is detected. Furthermore, the cross-sectional elemental distribution map of PU-A(0.5)B(0.5) in Supplementary Fig. 25 reveals that detectable boron and nitrogen signals are observed near the crack interface. All the above findings demonstrate that the major dynamic exchange, especially for low temperature, originates from the supermolecule-analogous borate dimer.

Cohesive energy density (CED) from molecular-dynamics calculations was used to quantify the overall strength of noncovalent/coordination interactions between chains (Fig. 2f). As the BOB content increased from 0 → 0.5 → 1, the CED rose monotonically from 304.8 → 354.3 → 455.5 MJ·m−3, indicating that O → B pre-coordination and boronate bonding significantly strengthen interchain polar attraction and van der Waals interactions, thereby enhancing network cohesion and interfacial adhesion. Notably, PU-A(0.5)B(0.5) maintains a relatively high CED while exhibiting excellent extensibility. Those findings indicate an optimal balance between cohesion enhancement and chain rearrangement/site exchange, consistent with its high strength–high elongation at room temperature and good stretchability at low temperature (Fig. 2g). To quantify intermolecular interactions during crack rejoining, we calculated nonbonded interaction energies and visualized isosurfaces for representative ADH–ADH and ADH–BOB-1 pairs. The energies are ΔE1 = −84.91 kcal·mol−1 for ADH–ADH and ΔE2 = −36.70 kcal·mol−1 for ADH–BOB-1 (Supplementary Fig. 26). Further analyses based on corresponding RDG–sign(λ₂)ρ show that ADH–ADH contacts display a broader and deeper distribution in the sign(λ₂)ρ < 0 regime, consistent with hydrogen-bond-dominated attraction. By contrast, ADH–BOB-1 exhibits a visibly narrowed negative region, an increased fraction near sign(λ₂)ρ ≈ 0 (van der Waals), and a discernible sign(λ₂)ρ > 0 component (repulsion), reflecting weaker hydrogen bonding and appreciable steric effects. Taken together, these results inform of a two-step healing mechanism, based on synergy between supermolecule-analogous dimers and supermolecular dimers: (1) Immediately after cutting, stronger supermolecular ADH dimers interactions provide higher local attraction and larger work of adhesion at the interface, rapidly restoring load-bearing capability; (2) Rapid exchange of interfacial supermolecule-analogous B–O bonds reconstructs the bulk topology and enables self-healing.

Chain-extender-induced dynamic relaxation and phase-structure regulation

We further quantified the synergistic linkage between BOB-1 and ADH in dynamic mechanics by dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA), stress-relaxation measurements, and microscopic characterization (Fig. 3). In Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 27, the DMA tan δ peak of PU-A(y)B(1-y) shifts from −64.15 °C to −43.06 °C as the content of BOB-1 increases. This finding indicates that BOB-1 brings severer soft-segment constraint than ADH. Meanwhile, the stress-relaxation of PU-A(0.5)B(0.5) (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 28 and Supplementary Table 10) shows that BOB-1-containing samples undergo fast relaxation within a minute at room temperature, and even relax effectively at 0 °C. Arrhenius fitting of τ(T) (Fig. 3b inset and Supplementary Fig. 29) reveals that the activation energy Ea decreases monotonically with BOB-1 content, from 63.48 to 20.62 kJ mol−1. By contrast, ADH-dominant networks exhibit significantly longer relaxation time (τ), consistent with relaxation limited by slower H-bond rearrangement. The systematic Ea reduction together with the shortened τ demonstrates that BOB-1 introduces a low-barrier chemical-relaxation channel for fast healing, while multi-H-bonded ADH primarily contributes to modulus/strength and slower dissipation.

To elucidate how O → B associative exchange of BOB-1 governs macroscopic rheology, we performed temperature-sweep oscillatory tests and time–temperature superposition (TTS) on PU-A(y)B(1-y). In temperature sweeps (Supplementary Fig. 30), all formulations display a crossover from elastic-dominant to viscous-dominant upon heating. Defining the flow-onset temperature Tf at G′ = G″, ADH-dominant PU-A(1)B(0) and PU-A(0.75)B(0.25) give Tf = 155 °C and 172 °C, respectively; with increasing BOB-1, Tf drops rapidly and reaches a minimum of 90 °C. In the tan δ–T curves (Fig. 3d), the viscous regime (tan δ > 1) shifts to lower temperature and amplifies in BOB-1-rich samples, demonstrating that O → B-driven B–O site exchange dominates dissipation and network disentanglement at reduced temperature, conferring low-temperature dynamic reprocess ability; in contrast, ADH-dominant systems retain a broader elastic plateau and higher Tf. Master curves constructed from frequency sweeps with Tref = 150 °C exhibit excellent horizontal shifting. The shift factor aT is well described by the Williams-Landel-Ferry (WLF) equation, and the fitted WLF parameters vary systematically with BOB-1 content, as shown in Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 32. This gradient directly reflects the much higher thermal activation required for reformation of ADH multi-H-bonds relative to the lower barrier for BOB-1-enabled topological rearrangement, consistent with BOB-1-dominated fast exchange.

FTIR spectra are used to investigate the molecular mechanism of the dynamic exchange. Starting from 1D FTIR spectra (Supplementary Fig. 33a–d), the major adsorption peaks related to H-bond are annotated. Upon heating, systematic intensity changes at 1686, 1528, 1240 cm−1 present H-bond rupture and reformation, corresponding to Amide I/II/III, respectively. In Fig. 3g, as the temperature increases from 30 to 150 °C, the H-bonded CONH band at 1639 cm−1 from urea/aminourea progressively decays, whereas two ester carbonyl bands at 1718 and 1755 cm−1 increase concurrently. The 1718 cm−1 band corresponds to H-bonded ester C=O associated with stronger and more ordered hydrogen bonding, while the 1755 cm−1 band corresponds to weakly coordinated or quasi-free ester C=O, characteristic of disordered or weakly H-bonded environments. In the 2D correlation spectra (Fig. 3e, f), the synchronous plot exhibits two principal autopeaks at (1755, 1755) and (1718, 1718), while the asynchronous plot shows three cross peaks (1755, 1718), (1755, 1639), and (1718, 1639). These bands correspond to C=O groups under different H-bond strengths: 1755 cm−1 (ester C=O with weak H-bonding), 1718 cm−1 (ester C=O with strong H-bonding), and 1639 cm−1 (amide C=O with strong H-bonding). According to Noda’s rule, the response sequence upon heating is 1639 → 1755 → 1718: the amide C=O engaged in strong H-bonds ruptures first, followed by ester C=O undergoing rupture and reorganization at different H-bond strengths. To spatially corroborate this sequence, we correlated the 2D results with temperature-dependent micro-FTIR mapping (Fig. 3h). Quantification of the intensity changes reveals a monotonic decrease of the H-bonded CONH band, in contrast to a stepwise increase of the ester COOR bands that resolve into two branches at 1718 cm−1 (H-bonded, indicative of stronger/more ordered interactions) and 1755 cm−1 (weakly coordinated or quasi-free). These trends imply that thermal perturbation initially disrupts the strong, directional urea-type H-bond network, frees carbonyl acceptor sites, and promotes a transition to looser, weakly coordinated states. 1755 cm−1 (disordered ester C=O) and 1718 cm−1 (ordered ester C=O) increase with time/position upon heating, whereas 1639 cm−1 (ordered amide C=O) decreases slowly. Those results reveal that, once segments are thermally activated, the strong amide N–H···O═C H-bond clusters are progressively replaced by a new coordination/H-bond network centered on ester carbonyls. This strong-to-weak site substitution of H-bond at 60 °C is consistent with the microdynamics of ADH inferred from DMA/relaxation and rheology, which is slower than that of BOB-1.

To elucidate how soft–hard cooperation regulates strain-induced structural evolution, we combined in situ tensile XRD (X-ray diffraction), AFM (Atomic force microscopy), and SAXS (Small-angle X-ray scattering) measurements (Fig. 3i–l and Supplementary Figs. 35–37). Under stretching, the amorphous PU-A(y)B(1-y) halo at 2θ = 20.1° gradually shifts toward 23.2°, corresponding to a d-spacing contraction from 4.42 to 3.83 Å. This transition reflects orientation-induced densification of PTEMG soft segments. In PU-A(1)B(0), the 23.2° reflection appears as early as 200% strain, showing that multivalent H-bond clusters act as efficient nucleation sites to promote rapid orientation/crystallization. By contrast, in BOB-1-containing systems (PU-A(0.5)B(0.5), PU-A(0)B(1)), the emergence of the 23.2° peak is delayed until 400–600% strain, demonstrating that dynamic O → B coordination retards premature crystallization and enhances segmental relax ability during stretching. AFM phase imaging (Fig. 3k) corroborates this hard-phase evolution. ADH-rich networks present aggregated, blocky hard domains; adding BOB-1 progressively disperses these into finer nanoscale hard points and eventually into uniformly distributed sparse nodes, reflecting suppression of excessive H-bond clustering by dynamic dimers. This morphological refinement matches the mechanical behavior: ADH-rich samples dissipate energy through large sacrificial hard domains, whereas BOB-1-rich samples dissipate energy through reversible coordination rearrangement. The A/B = 0.5/0.5 formulation achieves an optimized balance of dissipation and structural recovery. In situ tensile AFM and SAXS (Fig. 3j, l) further visualize the dynamic orient–deorient cycle. Under 100–400% strain, the initially isotropic scattering ring becomes elliptically distorted along the tensile axis and the principal peak shifts from 0.06 to 0.05 nm−1, giving reversible domain stretching and soft-phase extension. Upon unloading, both the scattering pattern and q*(scattering vector) return to their original states, confirming full structural reversibility. Mechanistically, H-bond clusters provide immediate load-bearing and orientation drive, while B–O coordination dynamically decoordinates–recoordinates to release strain and erase orientation, enabling nanoscale structural reset. This cooperative mechanism underpins the high-strain energy dissipation, reversible deformation, and delayed crystallization characteristic of the PU-A(0.5)B(0.5) network. To assess network homogeneity and hard-segment distribution, we additionally employed Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (CLSM). The rest discussion detail is provided in Supplementary Information (Supplementary Fig. 38). Taken together, these observations indicate that the soft segments are oriented and that nanoscale hard points are drawn into bundles, while supermolecular H-bond and supermolecule-analogous coordination domains within hard clusters are stretched and realigned along the strain direction.

Visualizing crack rejoining and establishing a fluorescence-based healing qualification mechanism

To further resolve crack rejoining at room and low temperatures, we tracked the damage using super-depth-of-field microscope, polarizing microscope, and microscopic infrared spectroscopy. Super-depth-of-field microscope imaging provides 2D/3D evidence of geometric repair for PU-A(0.5)B(0.5) (Fig. 4a): at 298 K, a well-defined gap at 0 s narrows within 30 s and, by 120 s, the step at the valley disappears and becomes co-planar with the matrix, which indicates geometric closure within a few hundred seconds after being cut. Polarizing microscope (Fig. 4b) verifies the transition from mere contact to optical continuity: at 298 K, scratches visibly heal over 5–60 s (Supplementary Movie 3); at 273 K, the same decay requires 0–30 min, implying that the characteristic times for chain transport and reversible bond exchange are extended at lower temperature yet still support rapid healing. To correlate morphology/optics with molecular reconstruction, microscopic infrared spectroscopy mapping was performed near the crack (Fig. 4c) and spectral perturbations were analyzed at 273 K (Fig. 4d). Upon cutting, intensity heterogeneity appears, accompanied by an increase in free/weakly H-bonded carbonyl (~1725–1700 cm−1) and a decrease in H-bonded carbonyl (~1685–1635 cm−1); C–B–O symmetric/antisymmetric (~1300–1200 cm−1) and B–O–B bending/stretching (~750/700 cm−1) bands diminish concurrently, evidencing disruption of both H-bond clusters and boronate dimers. After healing, the intensity field re-uniformizes, and the perturbed bands largely return to their initial levels and ratios: carbonyls shift from free back to H-bonded, and the C–B–O and B–O–B vibrations reappear. These observations indicate that, following geometric apposition, ADH multipoint H-bonding re-associates (more slowly), while BOB sites undergo O → B-pre-coordination-triggered associative B–O exchange to reconstitute the dimer and restore chemical continuity. The three modalities are temporally self-consistent: at 298 K, the system exhibits seconds-scale geometric closure and rapid chemical network rebuilding; at 273 K, the full process operates on proportionally longer times.

Importantly, during rapid healing, complete restoration cannot be inferred from appearance alone. The fast early-stage recovery is primarily attributed to dynamic reconstruction of BOB-1-associated B–O exchange sites, as supported by the combined molecular kinetic, viscoelastic, and interfacial spectroscopic evidence, as well as the cyclic mechanical results shown in Supplementary Figs. 40 and 41. By contrast, non-BOB-1 regions of the fractured bulk do not fully recover within short ambient-temperature windows (Supplementary Fig. 42a). Multiple cut–heal tensile tests and visualizations (Supplementary Fig. 42b, Supplementary Movie 4) confirm this distinction: even after nine cuts and 10 min of healing, specimens can still be elongated to >1000% without catastrophic failure, indicating that O → B-driven associative B–O exchange rapidly rebuilds a continuous load path at the interface; however, stress–strain curves reveal a progressive reduction in peak stress (Supplementary Fig. 43). Thus, short-time recovery is dominated by interface healing that restores extensibility through B–O exchange, while H-bond clusters (e.g. ADH) are not yet fully rebuilt, leading to a sharp drop in peak strength in the first cycle and further decreases with additional cuts. Quantitative statistics (Supplementary Fig. 44) show that strength and Young’s modulus decline markedly and plateau at lower values after multiple cycles; fracture elongation remains in a highly extensible range (800–1200%) with small fluctuations; and toughness decays rapidly from a high initial value and then stabilizes. These results indicate that rapid healing occurs primarily in the crack-neighboring region and is dominated by B–O exchange within BOB-1, which quickly restores extensibility and geometric continuity. Alternatively, the bulk network not governed by BOB-1 does not fully revert to its initial state on short timescales. Although the cut surface visually closes within minutes, our mechanical tests (Supplementary Figs. 42–45) show that specimens exhibiting no visible scratch under daylight can still suffer noticeable reductions in strength, indicating that macroscopic appearance alone cannot reliably reflect the true healing state. This discrepancy highlights the need for an optical method capable of revealing hidden or partially recovered damage.

To localize invisible cracks in PU-A(0.5)B(0.5) after healing at 298 K and evaluate their residual topographic features, AFM was employed to characterize the surface morphology. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 50a, b, a pronounced height discontinuity is still observed in the crack region. The corresponding height profile (Supplementary Fig. 50c) reveals a vertical difference of approximately 0.9 μm between the two sides of the crack, indicating that macroscopic visual inspection or geometric assessment alone is insufficient to evaluate healing completeness. These residual cracks can still perturb the local stress distribution and electronic environment. Therefore, KPFM (Kelvin probe force microscopy) was further used to map the surface potential around the crack region (Fig. 4e, f). The line profiles extracted from L1 and L2 confirm a clear potential offset across the crack (Fig. 4g, h). The average surface potential of region R1 is −0.357 V, markedly lower than that of region R2 (−0.313 V). By contrast, in the healed state (Supplementary Fig. 51), the average surface-potential difference between regions R3 and R4 decreases to less than 5 mV, indicating that the local electronic environment is essentially restored to continuity after healing. As a BOB-1-free control, PU-A(1)B(0) was examined under identical cutting and ambient healing conditions (Supplementary Figs. 52, 53). Unlike PU-A(0.5)B(0.5), PU-A(1)B(0) retains clear topographic and KPFM potential discontinuities after 1 h, indicating that the hydrogen-bond-dominated network alone is insufficient for rapid interfacial reconstruction. These results provide direct nanoscale evidence that cracks invisible to the naked eye can still be distinguished at the electronic level and further support the key role of the BOB-1-derived borate dimer in restoring interfacial continuity.

Based on the electronic perturbations associated with the presence of cracks, we designed a crack self-warning–healing qualification mechanism. Using PU-A(0.5)B(0.5) as a model system, an in situ monitoring workflow was established, as illustrated in Fig. 4i. A scalpel was used to introduce through-thickness cracks and subsurface cracks into the specimen, which was then allowed to self-heal at room temperature while being simultaneously observed under daylight and 365 nm UV illumination. Under daylight, the scratch becomes nearly invisible within 10 min, easily leading to a misjudgment of healed. However, under UV excitation at the same location, a bright green emission can still be observed, clearly outlining the crack contour and the surrounding crack region, indicating that the interface has not truly healed. After 30 min, the green emission weakens significantly and eventually disappears at the completely healed stage, consistent with the recovery of macroscopic appearance. Supplementary Figure 54 further provides a stepwise visualization of this healing-monitoring process, showing the gradual disappearance of the crack-related optical contrast under UV illumination as healing proceeds. We speculate that this phenomenon is related to geometric re-registration (red box). This on/off optical signal originates from edge emission and changes in the local coordination environment at the crack boundary: on the newly formed surfaces, the lower hydrogen-bond density and the unsaturated three-coordinate open state shift the O → B coordination equilibrium toward the closed state. Such a shift produces a red-shifted (greenish) emission when the network has not yet fully reconstructed. Once dynamic healing and chain interpenetration are complete, the interfacial hydrogen-bond density becomes uniform and the edge emission disappears. Therefore, the synergy of supermolecules and supermolecule-analogs enables visual early warning of cracks and subsurface defects that are invisible to the naked eye, and provides a fluorescence-based criterion to distinguish rejoining of crack faces from true healing. Beyond this crack-warning and healing-qualification function, the same dynamic bond network also endows the material with practical reprocessability and recyclability. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 56–58, PU-A(0.5)B(0.5) can be recycled through solvent-assisted reprocessing or hot pressing, while retaining favorable mechanical performance over multiple recycling cycles, further confirming the reversible nature of the dynamic network.

Optical behavior arising from O → B coordination conformational switching

Figure 5 systematically compares composition, solvation, and molecular-scale conformation to elucidate the physical origin of the surface–cut emission difference. We also observed a color contrast between the film surface and the cut/crack faces after cutting. We attribute this primarily to changes in hydrogen-bond density and coordination density in the polyurethane upon introducing BOB-1. Figure 5a presents 3D fluorescence spectra of the solid elastomers; all PU-A(y)B(1-y) compositions exhibit a main emission at ~482 nm (Supplementary Fig. 48). We hypothesize that the internal color change originates from density-mediated energy migration and therefore propose a coordination-polymorphism model (Fig. 5b). The O → B pre-coordination in the three-coordinate open state corresponds to higher-energy blue emission, whereas closure to a four-coordinate closed state red-shifts the emission to yellow-green.

a 3D excitation-emission fluorescence maps of PU-A(y)B(1-y). b Schematic of the conformational transition of BOB-1. c Emission color changes of BOB-1 under 365 nm excitation at different H2O/THF ratios. d Fluorescence color changes of PU-A(y)B(1-y) dissolved in DMSO under 365 nm excitation. e Emission spectra of BOB-1 measured at different H2O/THF ratios (λex = 365 nm). f Emission spectra of PU-A(y)B(1-y) dissolved in DMSO (λex = 365 nm). g Molecular conformations and MD snapshots of borate coordination in open, intermediate, and closed states. h B–O bond-distance distributions from MD simulations distinguishing closed, intermediate, and open coordination states. i Natural transition orbital (NTO) analysis of the open and closed states. j Schematic of surface–interior emission behavior and energy migration under UV excitation.

To validate the causal relationship between coordination state and chromaticity, we tuned the open/closed equilibrium of the BOB-1 boronate in H2O/THF mixed solvents (Fig. 5c). With increasing THF volume fraction, which favors coordination closure, the solution emission gradually shifts from blue to yellow-green. Frontier molecular orbital analysis (Supplementary Fig. 55) further reveals that this chromatic evolution originates from coordination-induced electronic structure modulation at the molecular level: the open state exhibits a larger energy gap (7.143 eV) than the closed state (6.978 eV), and the coordination-driven transition to the closed state therefore leads to a red-shifted emission. A composition-consistent trend is also observed when dissolving the polyurethane in DMSO (Fig. 5d): as the BOB-1 content increases from 0 to 1, the film’s chromaticity under identical excitation red-shifts, indicating that raising the density of coordination sites effectively locks in the lower-energy emissive state. In the bulk polyurethane, BOB-1 experiences the pull of hydrogen bonding and tends to remain in the three-coordinate open state at the surface; at the freshly cut face, hydrogen bonds no longer compete with O → B coordination, so the local environment favors the four-coordinate closed state, leading to a red-shift.

For the H2O/THF mixed-solvent experiments, the H2O/THF ratios (10:0, 9:1, …, 0:10) refer to volume ratios (v/v), and the solution concentration was fixed at 5.0 × 10−3 mol L−1. Under these conditions, increasing the THF fraction for PU-A(0.5)B(0.5) (Fig. 5e) produces a stepwise redshift and intensity increase in the fluorescence spectra, with the main emission shifting from 431 to 521 nm. This behavior can be understood as the superposition of two effects: changes in polarity/H-bonding shift the O → B equilibrium from the three-coordinate open (blue) to the four-coordinate closed (yellow-green) state, producing a redshift, and rising THF content perturbs the original coordination/H-bond microenvironment, further red-shifting and enhancing the emission. A similar solvent-dependent color change in DMSO across different A/B ratios (Fig. 5f) supports this view: upon dissolving the PU, the initial blue fluorescence of PU-A(1)B(0) arises from the soft/hard segment matrix, and the coordination of dissolved BOB-1 is greatly reduced relative to the solid state because of the altered H-bond density; as the BOB-1 fraction increases in solution, the system shifts toward the closed state, yielding more pronounced redshifts and intensity changes (from 459 nm to 547 nm).

To resolve how local density governs O → B coordination flipping, we constructed three representative microenvironments by MS (Molecular simulation)-level molecular modeling and used the B–O distance as a reaction coordinate for the coordination process. Figure 5g shows snapshots of the three states and their bulk models. In the open state, due to the action of the good solvent water, the carbonyl O remains distant from B, maintaining three coordination in a sparsely packed matrix and emitting blue light. In the intermediate state, local densification and the H-bond network draw the carbonyl O toward B to form pronounced pre-coordination; progressive coordination causes a blue-green emission. In the closed state, a stable O → B coordination bond forms, the B center becomes four-coordinate, chain conformations are more twisted, bulk packing is denser, and the emission becomes yellow-green. The simulated B–O distance distributions (Fig. 5h) distinguish O1 → B1 (upper histograms) and O2 → B2 (lower): 0.15–0.18 nm (O1 → B1) and 0.16–0.19 nm (O2 → B2) correspond to the four-coordinate closed state; 0.30–0.38 nm (O1 → B1) and 0.28–0.37 nm (O2 → B2) mark the intermediate state; and 0.31–0.42 nm (O1 → B1) with 0.31–0.39 nm (O2 → B2) indicate the three-coordinate open state. NTO (Natural transition orbital) analysis indicates that coordination closure modifies the charge-transfer character and stabilizes the emissive excited state, consistent with the reduced HOMO-LUMO gap in the closed state and the observed red-shift upon O → B coordination (Supplementary Figs. 62–71). The above findings demonstrate that the yellow emission on crack comes from the coordination of O → B bonds (Fig. 5j).

Applications of a fluorescence-based crack self-warning system

Figure 6 establishes a wound self-warning system based on reversible coordination and optical response. Figure 6a and Supplementary Movie 5 show side-by-side imaging of PU-A(y)B(1-y) films with varying compositions, first under daylight and then under 365 nm excitation. Under identical camera settings with a 5 s exposure, the gradual color change at the cut is clearly discernible. This resolvable chromaticity–intensity contrast arises from a reversible bias of O → B intramolecular coordination, providing a reference baseline for the subsequent optical criterion of healed or not. Figure 6b and Supplementary Movie 6 depict a strip specimen that is cut and then left to self-heal for 10 min in ambient light. With time, the bright line gradually decays and eventually disappears; only then can true, complete healing be asserted. Figure 6c records the same crack region during load-unload cycling: when the strain increases from 0% to 400%, any incompletely healed wound remains optically visible near the crack, demonstrating real-time, process-level visualization of damage under large deformation.

In Fig. 6d, e, elastomers of PU-A(y)B(1-y) with different ratios were cut into numeric/butterfly patterns and imaged under daylight and 365 nm UV to compare spatial emission after patterning. Under daylight, the samples appear only pale yellow; under UV, strong edge emission outlines the patterns, and incorporation of BOB-1 causes a progressive redshift of the edge emission. As shown in Fig. 6f–h, PU-A(0.5)B(0.5) exhibits rapid, transparent, and robust adhesion across diverse substrates. In Fig. 6f, the elastomer forms a highly transparent and conformal adhesive interface on glass, achieving stable load-bearing adhesion after healing at 298 K within 5 min. Figure 6g, h further demonstrate effective adhesion and repair on polyester and ceramic substrates, respectively, where structural integrity is restored after cutting, and damage states can be clearly visualized under UV irradiation. To demonstrate the applicability of this emission–healing self-warning strategy on device surfaces, PU-A(0.5)B(0.5) was formulated as a DMSO coating solution and dip-coated onto complex curved models (aircraft wing and fuselage; Fig. 6i, bottom). Cross-hatching was then used to introduce cracks. Under daylight, visible scratches are apparent (Visible scratches-Sunlight); under 365 nm, the same regions display brighter green emission stripes (Visible scratches-UV), indicating pronounced edge emission amplified by coordination unsaturation/H-bond enrichment at the crack boundary. Based on the above functions, the PU-A(0.5)B(0.5) can form high-transparency, tough, dense, and conformal functional coatings on complex 3D curvatures, translating microstructural perturbations into readable optical signals.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a chain extender with intrinsic optical crack-localizing capability for self-healing elastomers. This optical function arises from introducing a dynamic borate-ester moiety into an asymmetric semi-flexible dimer. Evidence from VT-NMR, UPLC-MS/MS, DFT calculations, and MS collectively demonstrates that the dimer undergoes multiple transition states during dynamic exchange. The state corresponding variations in the electronic bandgap induce a blue-to-yellow emission red-shift. This mechanism effectively addresses the long-standing challenge that residual cracks after self-healing are indistinguishable to the naked eye. Moreover, the energy barriers between these transition states are extremely low, providing a theoretical foundation for the observed rapid self-healing at low temperatures. Rheology, DMA, 2D-IR spectroscopy, in situ XRD, and AFM phase mapping further elucidate the dynamic mechanical behavior and microstructural evolution of the dimer. The obtained activation-energy analysis indicates that its dynamic exchange rate is approximately three times faster than that of conventional H-bond networks. This accelerated exchange leads to a more homogeneous microphase-separated morphology. Benefiting from its excellent rheological processability, the material can be readily manufactured into casting films, printed structural components, adhesive repairing agents, and soft capsule-type formats. Overall, the asymmetric borate-ester dimer designed in this work offers a promising strategy for addressing the critical challenge of crack-repair monitoring in self-healing polymer materials.

Methods

Reagents and materials

2-Formylbenzeneboronic acid (98%), 4-hydroxybutyric acid hydrazide (95%), acethydrazide (95%), butyric acid hydrazide (95%), isobutyric acid hydrazide (98%), 3-fluoro-2-formylphenylboronic acid (98%), 5-fluoro-2-formylphenylboronic acid (98%), hydroxyacetic acid hydrazide (98%), 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanohydrazide (98%), and adipic dihydrazide (ADH, 99%) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (analytical reagent grade). Dibutyltin dilaurate (98%) and dicyclohexylmethane diisocyanate (HMDI, 99%) were obtained from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Poly(tetramethylene ether glycol) (PTEMG, Mn = 2000) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used as received. All solvents used in the experiments, such as N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), were purchased from Chongqing Chuandong Chemical (Group) Co., Ltd. (analytical reagent grade). Raw materials and reagents were stored in a desiccator under an inert gas atmosphere.

General measurements

General characterization methods, including NMR, FTIR, HRMS, elemental analysis, mechanical testing, DMA, rheological measurements, SAXS, WAXD, DSC, XPS, TGA, AFM, KPFM, SEM-EDS, fluorescence spectroscopy, CLSM, DFT calculations, and MD simulations, are described in detail in the Supplementary Information, and fluorescence decay curves were recorded under excitation at 365 nm with emission monitored at 482 nm.

Synthesis of BOB-X

Detailed synthetic procedures and characterization data for BOB-X are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Synthesis of elastomers

Under a nitrogen atmosphere, weigh poly(tetramethylene ether glycol) with Mn = 2000 (PTEMG-2000, 0.01 mol) into a 250 mL three-necked flask. Using DMF as the solvent, add dicyclohexylmethane diisocyanate (HMDI, 0.0205 mol) via syringe. After adding HMDI and ensuring the mixture is homogeneous, add dibutyltin dilaurate at 0.1 wt% of the total mass of HMDI/PTEMG-2000 as a catalyst. Stir under a nitrogen atmosphere at 80 °C for 3 h to obtain the isocyanate-terminated prepolymer. Cool to approximately 40 °C, then add adipic dihydrazide (ADH, 0–0.01 mol) and heat to 60 °C to continue the reaction for 1 h. Subsequently, add the synthesized BOB-1 (0–0.01 mol), raise the temperature to 80 °C, and continue the reaction for 2 h. Pour the resulting solution into a polytetrafluoroethylene mold and dry at 70 °C to obtain PU-A(y)B(1-y). Here, PU represents the prepolymer of PTEMG and HMDI; A represents ADH; B represents BOB-1; the molar amounts of A and B are in the range of 0–0.01 mol, with the total of A + B being 0.01 mol. y denotes the percentage content in 0.01 mol, for example, PU-A(0.5)B(0.5) means that in the 0.01 mol of AB, 50% is A and 50% is B.

General measurements and synthesis of the model BOB-X are provided in the Supplementary Information files.
