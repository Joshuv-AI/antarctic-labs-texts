Phenotype, subphenotype, and endotype in sepsis and ARDS: a new layer of heterogeneity?
Authors: Liyuan Zhao, Cuixia Zhao, Minmin Wang, Wei Huang
Published: 2026

For decades, sepsis and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) have been defined by syndromic clinical criteria that mask profound biological heterogeneity, which is considered the primary obstacle to therapeutic progress [1]. The proliferation of studies identifying diverse phenotypes, subphenotypes, and endotypes through various approaches promised to resolve this impasse [2]. We argue that current subtyping efforts, despite their inventive cogitation and methodological sophistication, risk merely adding a new layer of heterogeneity that may ultimately impede rather than enable the discarding of the “syndrome” label.

The stated purpose of identifying subphenotypes is to deconstruct heterogeneous clinical syndromes into more biologically homogeneous and clinically treatable subgroups to facilitate precision medicine and optimize individual patient management.

However, current classifications are derived from fundamentally disparate analytical approaches: from high-dimensional clinical variables to multi-omics data and AI-driven deep learning. Existing evidence demonstrates incomplete concordance across subtypes suggesting that different data modalities capture distinct and only loosely correlated biological phenomena [3]. This raises the essential question: which type or subtype, if any, represents “true” disease biology? The multiplicity of classification efforts risks paradoxically exacerbating the very heterogeneity they intend to resolve and generating a fragmented atlas of subtypes while still lacking a unified map of sepsis and ARDS pathobiology.

Moreover, the primary “achievement” of subtyping has been secondary analyses of negative randomized controlled trials, revealing hidden treatment effects obscured in unselected populations. While intellectually fascinating and mechanistically informative, these findings are fundamentally retrospective and hypothesis-generating, but have yet to be incorporated into the standard of care [4]. The path from a promising retrospective p-value for interaction to a bedside clinical decision support tool remains blocked by the absence of prospective validation.

On the other hand, the entire enterprise rests on the assumption that these subgroups reflect distinct expressions of a single underlying disease. Yet the reality may prove far more unsettling. If what we collectively label as sepsis or ARDS actually represents a multitude of distinct diseases, then the subtyping pursuit risks becoming an endeavor built on shifting sands. In such a scenario, the only meaningful utility lies in identifying subgroups that happen to respond to a given therapy, which neither resolves the fundamental definition of the condition nor answers the more elemental question of whether sepsis exists as a coherent disease entity at all.

With the delimitation of phenotypes, subtypes, and endotypes, the field now confronts not only the original complexity of patients but also the methodological and taxonomic complexity of the frameworks designed to make sense of them. We are on the verge of chaos. The field must pivot decisively from descriptive proliferation to causal investigation. It must embrace the uncomfortable possibility that some subtypes may prove to be statistical artifacts rather than biological realities, and be willing to abandon them.
