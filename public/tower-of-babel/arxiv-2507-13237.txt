Robust and efficient estimation of global quantum properties under realistic noise
Qingyue Zhang, Dayue Qin, Zhou You, Feng Xu, Jens Eisert, You Zhou

Abstract
Measuring global quantum properties—such as the fidelity to complex multipartite states—is both an essential and experimentally challenging task. Classical shadow estimation offers favorable sample complexity, but typically relies on many-qubit circuits that are difficult to realize on current platforms. We propose the robust phase shadow scheme, a measurement framework based on random circuits with controlled- Z Z as the unique entangling gate type, tailored to architectures such as trapped ions and neutral atoms. Leveraging tensor diagrammatic reasoning, we rigorously analyze the induced circuit ensemble and show that phase shadows match the performance of full Clifford-based ones. Importantly, our approach supports a noise-robust extension via purely classical post-processing, enabling reliable estimation under realistic, gate-dependent noise where existing techniques often fail. Additionally, by exploiting structural properties of random stabilizer states, we design an efficient post-processing algorithm that resolves a key computational bottleneck in previous shadow protocols. Our results enhance the practicality of shadow-based techniques, providing a robust and scalable route for estimating global properties in noisy quantum systems.

Phase shadow framework.

Equipped with phase circuit ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}
, we can introduce the
phase shadow
(PS)
framework first for the noiseless scenario.
Shadow estimation is a quantum-classical hybrid process. On the quantum side, a random unitary
U\in\mathcal{E}
is applied to an unknown quantum state
\rho\mapsto U\rho U^{\dagger}
and one measures it in the computational basis to get the
classical outcome word
\mathbf{b}

\Pr(\mb{b}|U)=\langle\mathbf{b}|U\rho U^{{\dagger}}|\mathbf{b}\rangle=\tr(\rho\Phi_{U,\mb{b}})
. On the classical side, one ‘prepares’ a quantum snapshot
\Phi_{U,\mathbf{b}}
on the classical computer.
Taking the expectation on both
U
and
\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}(\rho):

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E},\mathbf{b}}\ \Phi_{U,\mathbf{b}}=\sum_{\mathbf{b}}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}}\tr_{1}(\rho\otimes\mathbb{I}\text{ }\Phi_{U,\mathbf{b}}^{\otimes 2})

(3)

\displaystyle=D\tr_{1}(\rho\otimes\mathbb{I}\ \mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(2)}),

where
\tr_{1}
denotes the (partial) trace out the first copy, and in the second line we relate
it to
the predefined moment
function.

At first glance, one might expect that replacing the full Clifford ensemble

Eq.

3

\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}
would introduce considerable bias, since
\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}
is only an approximate design and deviates significantly
from the full Clifford group

.
However, we show that an

is still achievable—not
for the entire quantum state

, but specifically for its
off-diagonal component
,
denoted by
\rho_{f}
.

As illustrated in

(Unbiased and efficient recovery with phase shadow estimation)

Robust estimation under realistic gate noise.

Noise poses a major challenge in implementing any shadow estimation protocol, particularly in Clifford measurement schemes that rely heavily on two-qubit gates. Indeed, gate-dependent noise remains a significant obstacle in notions of quantum learning
Chen et al. 2021
;
Brieger et al. 2025
. We show that the proposed PS can be further enhanced to mitigate such noise in practical scenarios, namely
robust phase shadow
(RPS).

Note that the layer of random
{S}
gates and the final fixed
{H}
gates in

1

Therefore, our focus here is on the noise characteristics of the complex
{CZ}

{CZ}

\widetilde{{CZ}}_{i,j}:={CZ}_{i,j}\times{ZZ}(\theta_{i,j})

{ZZ}(\theta_{i,j})=\mathrm{exp}(-{i}\frac{\theta_{i,j}}{2}{Z}_{i}{Z}_{j})

and each
\theta_{i,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
and
being i.i.d.
Lotshaw et al. 2023

As we show in Appendix
D

noise

\mathcal{D}(\rho)=(1-p_{e})\rho+p_{e}{Z}_{i}{Z}_{j}\rho{Z}_{i}{Z}_{j}

{CZ}
gate is operated. As such, the total noise is gate-dependent on the given layout pattern of the
{CZ}
layer. We additionally consider a more general noise model

{IZ}

;

Discussion.

Our work shows that robust and scalable estimation of global quantum properties can be achieved through hardware-native circuits in realistic quantum platforms and suitable structure-aware design. RPS strikes a natural balance between local random measurements and global Clifford schemes, avoiding exponential overhead while ensuring statistical guarantees under challenging gate-dependent noise. These strengths render RPS a highly promising framework for robust benchmarking, effective error mitigation, and hybrid quantum-classical learning at scale.

Looking ahead, several promising directions arise: generalizing phase-random circuits to other native two-qubit gates like iSWAP and
\sqrt{\text{SWAP}}
to better match other hardware platforms
Gao et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2023
, developing shallow-depth circuit variants to further reduce experimental complexity
Dalzell et al. 2024
;
Schuster et al. 2025
, and exploring applications like cross-platform verification
Hinsche et al. 2025
;
Elben et al. 2020
and the verification of quantum error correction
Gullans et al. 2021
. Such extensions can further deepen the practical impact of RPS and contribute to the co-design of noise-resilient protocols tailored to specific quantum architectures, which seems urgently needed to make further progress in the field.

Appendix B
Ensemble of general diagonal circuits

In this work, we employ circuits with a
{CZ-S-H}
structure, which we refer to as phase circuits, as our main tool for achieving randomized measurements. In this section, we introduce a more general diagonal circuit ensemble, of which the phase circuit ensemble is a special case. A random diagonal circuit incorporates three levels of randomness. The first level corresponds to applying single random diagonal gates. The second level corresponds to applying diagonal gates on all possible qubit subsets, which gives rise to a layer of random diagonal circuits. The third level corresponds to concatenating multiple layers.
Here, we provide a detailed introduction to the structure of the ensemble of general diagonal circuits via the three levels of randomness. At the first level, we uniformly sample
p
-qubit diagonal gates from the ensemble

\mathcal{E}_{p,q,I_{p}}=\{\operatorname{diag}(1,1,1,\dots,e^{i\frac{2\pi}{2^{q}}k})\mid k=0,1,\cdots,2^{q}-1\},

(19)

where
p<n
is the number of qubits,
q\geq 1
is an integer, and
I_{p}
denotes the subset of
[n]
of size
p
.
I_{p}
specifies the qubits on which the gate is being applied. For example, when
p=2,q=1
, the ensemble becomes the
\{\mathbb{I}_{4},{CZ}\}
. When
p=1
and
q=2
, the ensemble becomes
\{\mathbb{I}_{2},{S,S^{2}=Z,S^{3}=S}^{\dagger}\}
.

At the second level, we apply a random gate from the ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{p,q,I_{p}}
to all possible subsets of
[n]
with size
p
. As a result, we obtain a layer of diagonal gates uniformly sampled from the ensemble

\mathcal{E}_{p,q}=\left\{\prod_{I_{p}\in\mathcal{I}_{p}}U_{p,q,I_{p}}\mid U_{p,q,I_{p}}\in\mathcal{E}_{p,q,I_{p}}\right\}.

(20)

For example, the ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{1,2}
means that a single-qubit gate is sampled uniformly and independently from the set
\{\mathbb{I}_{2},{S},{Z},{S}^{\dagger}\}
. Likewise, the ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{2,1}
means that a two-qubit gate is sampled uniformly and independently from the set
\{\mathbb{I}_{2}^{\otimes 2},{CZ}\}
.

At the third level, we concatenate multiple layers of diagonal circuits and an additional layer of Hadamard gates on every qubits to obtain the final circuit
U_{\text{diag}}=U_{p,p}U_{p-1,p}\dots U_{1,p}H^{\otimes n}
, where
U_{p,p}\in\mathcal{E}_{p,p},U_{p-1,p}\in\mathcal{E}_{p-1,p},\dots,U_{1,p}\in\mathcal{E}_{1,p}
. The collection of all
U_{\text{diag}}
constitutes the unitary ensemble of general diagonal circuits given by

\mathcal{E}_{\text{diag}}^{(p)}=\{U_{p,p}U_{p-1,p}\dots U_{1,p}{H}^{\otimes n}|U_{p,p}\in\mathcal{E}_{p,p},U_{p-1,p}\in\mathcal{E}_{p-1,p},\dots,U_{1,p}\in\mathcal{E}_{1,p}\}.

(21)

As a special example, the unitary ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{\text{diag}}^{(2)}
follows the circuit structure
{CS-S-H}
, which is not Clifford due to the
{CS}
gates. Classical shadow tomography, in general, requires that the random circuit can be efficiently simulated classically. Therefore, we replace the
{CS}
gates with the
{CZ}
gates, obtaining the ensemble of phase circuits
\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}
introduced in the main text. As we will demonstrate in
Section

C.2
, this replacement leaves the 2nd and 3rd moments of the
{CS-S-H}
ensemble and
{CZ-S-H}
ensemble unchanged.

Appendix C
Moment functions of diagonal circuit ensembles

The moment functions, particularly,
the 2nd and 3rd moments, are crucial for computing the inverse map of randomized measurements and conducting theoretical performance analysis. In this section, we introduce the definition of the moment function and derive the moment function of the first-level ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{p,q,I_{p}}
.
Denote the
m
-copy twirling channel over a unitary ensemble
\mathcal{E}
as

\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}}^{(m)}(\cdot)\coloneq\mathbb{E}_{U\in\mathcal{E}}U^{\dagger\otimes m}(\cdot)U^{\otimes m},

(22)

the definition of the moment function is as follows.

Definition 1

(Moment functions)
.

For an
n
-qubit system, the
m
-th moment function of
\mathcal{E}
is defined as

\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(m)}\coloneq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\mathbf{b}}\Lambda_{\mathcal{E}}^{(m)}(|\mathbf{b}\rangle\langle\mathbf{b}|^{\otimes m}).

(23)

Introducing
\Phi_{U,\mathbf{b}}\coloneq U^{\dagger}|\mathbf{b}\rangle\langle\mathbf{b}|U
, we can rewrite the
m
-th moment function as

\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(m)}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}}\sum_{\mathbf{b}}\Phi_{U,\mathbf{b}}^{\otimes m}.

(24)

Before we are in the position to explore the moment function
\mathbf{M}_{\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}}^{(m)}
of the phase circuit ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}
, it is necessary to introduce the following lemma.

Lemma 2

(Twirling channels)
.

Let
\mathbf{\hat{x}}
and
\mathbf{\hat{y}}
be two
mn
-bit binary numbers. The twirling channel of the first-level ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{p,q,I_{p}}
, when applied to an
m
-copy
n
-qubit operator
|\mathbf{\hat{x}}\rangle\langle\mathbf{\hat{y}}|
in the computational basis, is given by

\Lambda_{p,q,I_{p}}^{(m)}(|\mathbf{\hat{x}}\rangle\langle\mathbf{\hat{y}}|)=\begin{cases}|\mathbf{\hat{x}}\rangle\langle\mathbf{\hat{y}}|,&\sum\limits_{t=0}^{m-1}\prod\limits_{j\in I_{p}}x_{t,j}\equiv\sum\limits_{t=0}^{m-1}\prod\limits_{j\in I_{p}}y_{t,j}\pmod{2^{q}},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(25)

where
x_{t,j}
and
y_{t,j}
, respectively, label the binary element on the
j
-th qubit of the
t
-th copy of
\mathbf{\hat{x}},\mathbf{\hat{y}}
.

Proof.

Consider a unitary operator
U=\text{diag}(1,1,1,\dots,e^{i\frac{2\pi}{2^{q}}k})
acting on the qubit set
I_{p}
. Its action on
|\mathbf{x}\rangle
is given by

U^{\otimes m}|\mathbf{\hat{x}}\rangle=e^{i\frac{2\pi}{2^{q}}k\sum_{t=0}^{m-1}\prod_{j\in I_{p}}x_{t,j}}|\mathbf{\hat{x}}\rangle.

(26)

Thus, applying the twirling channel on
|\mathbf{\hat{x}}\rangle\langle\mathbf{\hat{y}}|
yields

\displaystyle\Lambda_{p,q,I_{p}}^{(m)}(|\mathbf{\hat{x}}\rangle\langle\mathbf{\hat{y}}|)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U\in\mathcal{E}_{p,q,I_{p}}}U^{\dagger\otimes m}|\mathbf{\hat{x}}\rangle\langle\mathbf{\hat{y}}|U^{\otimes m}

(27)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{q}}\sum_{k=0}^{2^{q}-1}e^{i\frac{2\pi}{2^{q}}k\left(\sum_{t=0}^{m-1}\prod_{j\in I_{p}}y_{t,j}-\sum_{t=0}^{m-1}\prod_{j\in I_{p}}x_{t,j}\right)}|\mathbf{\hat{x}}\rangle\langle\mathbf{\hat{y}}|

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}|\mathbf{\hat{x}}\rangle\langle\mathbf{\hat{y}}|,&\sum\limits_{t=0}^{m-1}\prod\limits_{j\in I_{p}}x_{t,j}\equiv\sum\limits_{t=0}^{m-1}\prod\limits_{j\in I_{p}}y_{t,j}\pmod{2^{q}},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This completes the proof.
∎

Using
Lemma

2
, one can show that

\displaystyle\Lambda_{p,q,I_{p}}^{(m)}\circ\Lambda_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime},I_{p}^{\prime}}^{(m)}(O)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Lambda_{p,q,I_{p}}^{(m)}(O)\cap\Lambda_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime},I_{p}^{\prime}}^{(m)}(O),

(28)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{p,q,I_{p}}^{(m)}(O)\cap\Lambda_{p,q,I_{p}}^{(m)}(O^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\;\forall O\cap O^{\prime}=0,

(29)

for
m
-copy Boolean operators
O
and
O^{\prime}
in the computational basis with the compatible dimension, and we can extend these results to the twirling channel
\Lambda_{p,q}^{(m)}
with the second-level ensemble
\mathcal{E}_{p,q}
in
Eq.

20

\displaystyle\Lambda^{(m)}_{p,q}\circ\Lambda^{(m)}_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}(O)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Lambda^{(m)}_{p,q}(O)\cap\Lambda^{(m)}_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}(O),

(30)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{(m)}_{p,q}(O)\cap\Lambda^{(m)}_{p,q}(O^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\;\forall O\cap O^{\prime}=0.

(31)

Since the intersection operation is commutative, the composition of twirling on the

\widehat{\rho}=N_{f}^{-1}\sum_{i}\rho_{f}^{(i)}+N_{d}^{-1}\sum_{j}\rho_{d}^{(j)}

\widehat{o}=\tr(O\widehat{\rho})
has the variance
\text{Var}(\widehat{o})=N_{f}^{-1}\text{Var}(\widehat{o_{f}})+N_{d}^{-1}\text{Var}(\widehat{o_{d}})
under the total measurement time
N=N_{f}+N_{d}

N_{f}=3N_{d}

\begin{split}\text{Var}(\widehat{o})=N_{f}^{-1}\text{Var}(\widehat{o_{f}})+N_{d}^{-1}\text{Var}(\widehat{o_{d}})\leq N_{f}^{-1}\times 3\|O_{f}\|_{2}^{2}+N_{d}^{-1}\|O_{d}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{4}{3}N^{-1}\times 3\|O_{f}\|_{2}^{2}+4N^{-1}\|O_{d}\|_{2}^{2}=4\|O\|_{2}^{2},\end{split}

(76)

where the last equality holds because

Appendix D
Realistic noise models

In the RPS protocol, we only need to account for the noise on controlled-
{Z}
(
{CZ}
) gates, as other noises on the single qubits at the end of the circuit can be addressed through standard measurement error mitigation techniques
Gluza et al. 2020
.
On realistic noisy quantum computers, the noise on the
{CZ}
gate is the
biased
noise
Roffe et al. 2023
;
Bonilla Ataides et al. 2021
, where certain types of noise are dominant. In the RPS protocol, we model the noise on the
{CZ}
gate as
{ZZ}
-type noise. We have also considered an extended biased noise model, which includes all two-qubit
{Z}
-type Pauli errors, and further provides a robust approach for this extended noise model in
Appendix

I
. We present the mathematical modeling and physical origination of the noise models in the following.
Here, we model a noisy
{CZ}
gate acting on a qubit pair
(i,j)
as

\widetilde{{CZ}}_{i,j}\coloneq{CZ}_{i,j}{ZZ}(\theta_{i,j}),\ {ZZ}(\theta_{i,j})=\exp(-{i} \frac{\theta_{i,j}}{2} {Z}_i {Z}_j),

(78)

where
\theta_{i,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
are
independently and identically distributed
(i.i.d.) random variables. This noise model is realistic, as two-qubit gates are typically implemented using two-qubit Ising couplings, where the rotation angle behaves as a random variable
Maslov and Nam 2018
;
Bravyi et al. 2022
.
The average effect of this random rotation results in a noisy quantum channel

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{i,j}}\widetilde{{CZ}}_{i,j}\rho\widetilde{{CZ}}_{i,j}^{\dagger}&={CZ}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{i,j}}[\exp(-{i} \frac{\theta_{i,j}}{2} {Z}_i {Z}_j)\rho\exp({i} \frac{\theta_{i,j}}{2} {Z}_i {Z}_j)]\cdot{CZ}^{{\dagger}}\\
&={CZ}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{i,j}}[(\cos\frac{\theta_{i,j}}{2}\mathbb{I}_{2}-{i}\sin\frac{\theta_{i,j}}{2}{Z}_{i}{Z}_{j})\rho(\cos\frac{\theta_{i,j}}{2}\mathbb{I}_{2}+{i}\sin\frac{\theta_{i,j}}{2}{Z}_{i}{Z}_{j})]\cdot{CZ}^{{\dagger}}\\
&={CZ}\cdot[\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{i,j}}\cos^{2}\frac{\theta_{i,j}}{2}\rho+\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{i,j}}\sin^{2}\frac{\theta_{i,j}}{2}{Z}_{i}{Z}_{j}\rho{Z}_{i}{Z}_{j}]\cdot{CZ}^{{\dagger}}\\
&={CZ}\cdot\mathcal{D}(\rho)\cdot{CZ}^{{\dagger}},\\
\end{split}

(79)

where
\mathcal{D}(\rho)\coloneq\frac{1}{2}(1+e^{-\sigma^{2}/2})\rho+\frac{1}{2}(1-e^{-\sigma^{2}/2}){Z}_{i}{Z}_{j}\rho{Z}_{i}{Z}_{j}
. In this way, we define the error rate of
{CZ}
gates to be

p_{e}\coloneq\frac{1}{2}(1-e^{-\sigma^{2}/2})\approx\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}.

(80)

Our focus on this type of noise stems from its physical foundations in realistic platforms like trapped ions and Rydberg atoms. Here, we provide a brief description of the noise source in these platforms.

Trapped ion platform.

In trapped ion systems,
{CZ}
gates can be implemented using
global Mølmer–Sørensen
(GMS) gates
Maslov and Nam 2018
. These gates offer native implementation of entangling operations and support efficient execution of Clifford circuits
Bravyi et al. 2022
. Specifically, we consider the implementation of
{CZ}
gates based on GMS interaction
according to
{CZ}_{i,j}={S}_{i}^{{\dagger}}{S}_{j}^{{\dagger}}{H}^{\otimes 2}{GMS}_{i,j}(\frac{\pi}{2}){H}^{\otimes 2}
, and the
{GMS}
gate corresponds to an XX-type interaction of the form

{GMS}_{i,j}(\theta)\coloneq{XX}_{i,j}(\theta)=\exp(-{i}\frac{\theta}{2}{X}_i {X}_j).

(81)

We discuss the noise of the GMS gate
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Martínez-García et al. 2022
;
Manovitz et al. 2017
as follows. Experimental study
Lotshaw et al. 2023
shows that the dominant gate-dependent noise source of the MS gates is the vibrational mode frequency fluctuation. These fluctuations translate into over-rotations, which are effectively modeled by the same noisy
{CZ}
form
\widetilde{{CZ}}={CZ}\times{ZZ}(\theta_{i,j})
, where
\theta_{i,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
and are i.i.d. for every
{CZ}
gate, as mentioned in
Eq.

78
.

Rydberg atom platform.
Rydberg atom arrays are known to exhibit dominant
{Z}
-type errors and leakage
Evered et al. 2023
;
Cong et al. 2022
. Crucially, atom loss can be directly detected and converted into an erasure error
Wu et al. 2022
, which can in turn be converted into
{Z}
-type Pauli errors
Sahay et al. 2023
, especially in alkaline-earth Rydberg atom arrays. For example, it is estimated that for metastable
{}^{171}{\text{Yb}}

Wu et al. 2022
,
98\%
of errors can be converted to erasure errors, resulting in a Pauli-
{Z}
-type error imposing on the
{CZ}
gates.

For two-qubit
{CZ}
gates, the dominant Pauli-
{Z}
-type errors include IZ, ZI, and ZZ errors. Specifically, we denote the noisy two-qubit gates
\widetilde{{CZ}}={CZ}\circ\Lambda
, where

\Lambda(\rho)=(1-\frac{3}{4}p_{e})\rho+\frac{p_{e}}{4}{ZI}\rho{ZI}+\frac{p_{e}}{4}{IZ}\rho{IZ}+\frac{p_{e}}{4}{ZZ}\rho{ZZ}.

(82)

In
Appendix

I
, we provide a detailed discussion of this extended noise model, including its measurement channel, the estimation variance, and the efficient post-processing procedure in phase shadow estimation.

In summary, both the trapped ion platform and the Rydberg atom platform naturally give rise to gate-dependent biased Pauli
{Z}
-type noise, validating the noise model used throughout our analysis and simulation of the RPS protocol.

&=2^{-n}\sum_{I_{k}=I_{n_{3}},I_{i}+I_{j}=I_{n_{1}}+I_{n_{2}}}(1-p_{e})^{{i}\times k}(p_{e})^{j\times k}\tr[\left(\Delta_{2}^{I_{i}}\otimes(\mathbb{I}_{4}-\Delta_{2})^{I_{j}}\otimes(\mathbb{S}_{2}-\Delta_{2})^{I_{k}}\right)\left({P}\otimes{P}\right)]\\

&=\sum_{s=0}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{t:0\leq t\leq n_{2},0\leq s-t\leq n_{1}}(-1)^{t}C_{n_{2}}^{t}C_{n_{1}}^{s-t}(1-p_{e})^{(n_{1}+n_{2}-s)n_{3}}(p_{e})^{sn_{3}},\end{split}

(95)

which completes the proof. The coefficient
\sigma_{P}
can be efficiently calculated using
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
time.

At this point, we add some remarks on the
coefficient

\sigma_{P}
. We now prove that
Eq.

approximately equals to
(1-p_{e})^{n_{3}(n-n_{3})}\approx e^{-p_{e}n_{3}(n-n_{3})}
when
np_{e}\ll 1
.
We find that
Eq.

95
can be
interpreted as a power series in terms of the variable
s
, with each term referred to as the
s
-th order component

\sigma_{P}=\sum_{s=0}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\underbrace{\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq t\leq n_{2}\\
0\leq s-t\leq n_{1}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{t}C_{n_{2}}^{t}C_{n_{1}}^{s-t}\right]}_{\text{$s$-th order coefficient}}(1-p_{e})^{(n_{1}+n_{2})n_{3}}(\frac{p_{e}}{1-p_{e}})^{sn_{3}}.

(96)

For example, when
s=0,1,2
, the
s
-th order terms are respectively
(1-p_{e})^{n_{3}(n-n_{3})}
,
(n_{1}-n_{2})(1-p_{e})^{n_{3}(n-n_{3}-1)}p_{e}^{n_{3}}
and
\frac{(n_{1}-n_{2})^{2}-(n_{1}+n_{2})}{2}(1-p_{e})^{n_{3}(n-n_{3}-2)}p_{e}^{2n_{3}}
(suppose
n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}\geq 2
). Here we discuss the
s
-th order coefficient, which is upper bounded as

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq t\leq n_{2}\\
0\leq s-t\leq n_{1}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{t}C_{n_{2}}^{t}C_{n_{1}}^{s-t}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq t\leq n_{2}\\
0\leq s-t\leq n_{1}\end{subarray}}C_{n_{2}}^{t}C_{n_{1}}^{s-t}\leq sn^{s},

(97)

and the equality holds only when
s=0
. Note that when
np_{e}\ll 1
, the upper bounded of the
s
-th order term is
sn^{s}(p_{e})^{sn_{3}}\leq s(np_{e})^{s}
, which decays exponentially as
s
increases.
Consequently, the series is dominated by the terms with small
s
, and higher-order components contribute negligibly. This implies that, for sufficiently small bit error probabilities

, the dominant contribution to
\sigma_{P}\approx(1-p_{e})^{n_{3}(n-n_{3})}
comes from the lowest-order terms in the series.
As a result, it is often sufficient to retain only the
0
-th order term, which significantly simplifies the analysis and computation.
If we only consider the
0
-th order terms, we can extend the result to the case when error rates of
{CZ}
gates vary across different qubits as

\sigma_{P}\approx(1-p_{e})^{n_{3}(n-n_{3})}=\prod_{s\in I_{n_{1}}+I_{n_{2}},t\in I_{n_{3}}}(1-p_{e}^{(s,t)}),

(98)

where
p_{e}^{(s,t)}
denotes the error rate of
{CZ}
implementing on the
s-
and
t-
th qubit.

Case 1:

\begin{split}&\sum_{\mathbf{B}\in C_{\mathcal{B}}}\tr[{(1-p_{e})^{N^{(1)}_{\mathbf{B}}}(p_{e})^{N^{(2)}_{\mathbf{B}}}}\mathbf{B}\text{ }\rho\otimes\mathring{O_{f}}\otimes\mathring{O_{f}}]\\

where the last inequality holds because, given a class

Case 3:

. In this case, we reorganize the sum over

. Since there are exactly two elements in

\begin{split}&\sum_{\mathbf{B}\in C_{\mathcal{B}}}\tr[{(1-p_{e})^{N^{(1)}_{\mathbf{B}}}(p_{e})^{N^{(2)}_{\mathbf{B}}}}\mathbf{B}\text{ }(\rho\otimes\mathring{O_{f}}\otimes\mathring{O_{f}})]\\

(130)

Since we already know that

(135)

In this way, we plug

to obtain

\begin{split}&\sum_{\mathbf{B}\in C_{\mathcal{B}}}\tr[{(1-p_{e})^{N^{(1)}_{\mathbf{B}}}(p_{e})^{N^{(2)}_{\mathbf{B}}}}\mathbf{B}\text{ }(\rho\otimes\mathring{O_{f}}\otimes\mathring{O_{f}})]\\
&=\sum_{I^{(2)}_{\mathbf{B}}+\sum_{B_{k,r}\in\mathcal{B}(1)}I^{(k,r)}=[n],|I^{(k,r)}|>0}\tr[{(1-p_{e})^{N^{(1)}_{\mathbf{B}}}(p_{e})^{N^{(2)}_{\mathbf{B}}}}\bigotimes_{B_{k,r}\in\mathcal{B}(1)}B_{k,r}^{\otimes I^{(k,r)}}\otimes(\sum_{B_{k,r}\in\mathcal{B}(2)}B_{k,r})^{\otimes I_{\mathbf{B}}^{(2)}}\text{ }(\rho\otimes\mathring{O_{f}}\otimes\mathring{O_{f}})]\\
&\leq\sum_{I^{(2)}_{\mathbf{B}}+\sum_{B_{k,r}\in\mathcal{B}(1)}I^{(k,r)}=[n],|I^{(k,r)}|>0}(p_{e})^{N^{(2)}_{\mathbf{B}}}\tr( \mathring{O_f}^2)\\
&\leq\sum_{I^{(2)}_{\mathbf{B}}+\sum_{B_{k,r}\in\mathcal{B}(1)}I^{(k,r)}=[n],|I^{(k,r)}|>0}(p_{e})^{N_{I,\mathbf{B}}^{(1)}-\frac{1}{2}|\mathcal{B}(1)|}\tr( \mathring{O_f}^2)\\
&\leq\sum_{k=|{\mathcal{B}(1)}|}^{n-1}C_{n}^{k}|{\mathcal{B}(1)}|^{k}(p_{e})^{k-\frac{1}{2}|{\mathcal{B}(1)}|}\tr( \mathring{O_f}^2)=\Theta(1)\tr( \mathring{O_f}^2).\\
\end{split}

(136)

Since there exist only finite numbers of different subsets
C_{\mathcal{B}}
, and the number is independent of the qubit number, the total contribution to the variance of the term
2
is bounded by a constant multiple of
\tr(\mathring{O_f}^2)
. Combine the upper bound of both terms
1
and
2
, we finally obtain

\text{Var}_{\mathcal{E}_{{\text{phase}}},\text{robust}}\left(\widehat{O_{f}}\right)\lesssim[3+\Theta(1)]\tr( \mathring{O_f}^2)\approx[3+\Theta(1)]e^{n^{2}p_{e}/2}\tr(O_f^2).

(137)

We employ the asymptotic notation ‘
\lesssim
’ under the conditions
np_{e}\ll 1
and
n^{2}p_{e}=\Theta(1)
, as discussed in the main text. Under the weak noise regime where
np_{e}\ll 1
, the coefficient
\sigma_{P}
admits the approximation

\sigma_{P}^{-1}\approx(1-p_{e})^{-n_{3}(n-n_{3})}\approx e^{n_{3}(n-n_{3})p_{e}}\leq e^{\frac{n^{2}p_{e}}{4}},

(138)

as derived in
Eq.

98
. This approximation enables quantitative characterization of the estimation variance, with numerical simulations confirming its validity for the parameter regime of interest in

Appendix G
Efficient post-processing of the robust phase shadow

In shadow estimation, the post-processing scheme can be efficiently implemented on classical computers. In this section, we find that although the reversed measurement map is now a combination of exponential terms, we can still efficiently compute the value of the estimator
\widehat{o_{f}}=\tr(\widehat{\rho_f} O)
, where the observable
O
is a Pauli operator or a stabilizer state.

Pauli operators.
Suppose that
Q
denotes an
n
-qubit non-
{Z}
-type Pauli operator

\begin{split}\tr(\widehat{\rho_f} Q)&=\sum_{P\in\mathbf{P}_{n}/\mathcal{Z}_{n}}\sigma_{P}^{-1}\tr( \Phi_{U,\mb{b}}P)\tr(PQ)\\
&=D\sigma_{Q}^{-1}\bra{\mathbf{b}}UQU^{\dagger}\ket{\mathbf{b}},\end{split}

(139)

where we should choose

in the summation. Note that it takes

O

Stabilizer states.

Lemma 5

(

1
in the

)
.

Let

\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}

and there exist a constant

Proof.

Sampling
U

\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}}2^{n_{g}(U,V)}&=\sum_{U\in\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}|}2^{n_{g}(U,V)}\\
&=\frac{|\mathcal{E}_{\text{Cl}}|}{|\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}|}\sum_{U\in\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}_{\text{Cl}}|}2^{n_{g}(U,V)}\\
&<\frac{|\mathcal{E}_{\text{Cl}}|}{|\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}|}\sum_{U\in\mathcal{E}_{\text{Cl}}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}_{\text{Cl}}|}2^{n_{g}(U,V)}=\frac{|\mathcal{E}_{\text{Cl}}|}{|\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}|}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}_{\text{Cl}}}2^{n_{g}(U,V)},\\
\end{split}

(147)

where
\mathcal{E}_{\text{Cl}}
is the the ensemble of
n
-qubit stabilizer circuits. To complete the random phase gates, one should impose
\frac{n(n-1)}{2}
random
{CZ}
gates and
n
random
S
gates. Therefore,
|\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}|=2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}4^{n}=2^{\frac{n(n+3)}{2}}
. Now we determine the value of

. From Ref.
Aaronson and Gottesman 2004
, we obtain that
|\mathcal{E}_{\text{Cl}}|=2^{n}\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(2^{n-k}+1)
. So

\frac{|\mathcal{E}_{\text{Cl}}|}{|\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}|}=\frac{2^{n}\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(2^{n-k}+1)}{2^{\frac{n(n+3)}{2}}}=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(1+\frac{1}{2^{n-k}})<\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\exp({2^{-n+k}})<e,

(148)

which is a constant. This means that from now on, we only need to show that
\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}_{\text{Cl}}}2^{n_{g}(U,V)}=\mathcal{O}(1)
, which requires to figure out the number of
U\in\mathcal{E}_{\text{Cl}}
that satisfy

.

We use a result from Ref.
García et al. 2017
: Given two arbitrary stabilizer states,
\ket{\psi}=U^{\dagger}\ket{\mathbf{0}}
and
\ket{\phi}=V^{\dagger}\ket{\mathbf{0}}
, their inner product satisfies

if and only if 1)
|[\mathbf{S}_{U}]\cap[\mathbf{S}_{V}]|=2^{n_{g}(U,V)}
, and 2)
[\mathbf{S}_{U}\cap\mathbf{S}_{V}]=[\mathbf{S}_{U}]\cap[\mathbf{S}_{V}]
. Also, if the second constraint is violated, then
|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|=0
, which happens when there exists a
P
satisfying
P\in\mathbf{S}_{U}
and
-P\in\mathbf{S}_{V}
.
Therefore, given
V
, the number of
U
satisfying

Theorem 15 of Ref.
García et al. 2017
states that, given a stabilizer state
\ket{\phi}=V^{\dagger}\ket{\mathbf{0}}

\ket{\psi}=U^{\dagger}\ket{\mathbf{0}}

\mathcal{L}_{n}(n-k)=\prod_{j=1}^{n-k}\frac{2}{2^{k}}\frac{4^{n}/2^{j-1}-2^{n}}{2^{n-k}-2^{j-1}}.

(149)

Thus, the number of stabilizer states
\ket{\psi}=U^{\dagger}\ket{\mathbf{0}}
satisfying
n_{g}(U,V)=k
is
2^{k}\mathcal{L}_{n}(n-k)
. Theorem 16 of Ref.
García et al. 2017
gives a limit of

that
\frac{\mathcal{L}_{n}(n-k)}{|\mathcal{E}_{\text{Cl}}|}<\frac{\mathcal{O}(1)}{2^{k(k+3)/2}}
.
Using this result, we conclude that given an arbitrary moment
m
of the random variable
2^{n_{g}(U,V)}
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}}2^{n_{g}(U,V)m}&<e\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}_{\text{Cl}}}2^{n_{g}(U,V)m}\\
&=e\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{2^{k}\mathcal{L}_{n}(n-k)}{|\mathcal{E}_{\text{Cl}}|}2^{km}\\
&<e\sum_{k=0}^{n}2^{k(m+1)}\frac{\mathcal{O}(1)}{2^{k(k+3)/2}}\\
&<e\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\mathcal{O}(1)}{2^{k(k+1-2m)/2}}=\mathcal{O}(1),\end{split}

(150)

when
m
is a constant. Specially, when

145

is also a constant, then we utilize the Markov inequality to obtain that for all

.

\mathbb{P}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}}\left(2^{n_{g}(U,V)}\geq 2^{c}\right)=\mathbb{P}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}}\left(2^{2n_{g}(U,V)}\geq 2^{2c}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}_{\text{phase}}}2^{2n_{g}(U,V)}}{2^{2c}}=\mathcal{O}(2^{-2c}),

(151)

which proves
Eq.

146
.
∎

Therefore, the average total computational complexity is still
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
for all stabilizer states. One may be concerned that the computational complexity will be exponential for a single snapshot when

In the RPS protocol, we model the gate-dependent noise model as the ZZ Pauli error. Such noise naturally arises in realistic physical systems and exhibits a favorable mathematical structure. \Lambda(\rho)=(1-\frac{3}{4}p_{e})\rho+\frac{p_{e}}{4}{ZI}\rho{ZI}+\frac{p_{e}}{4}{IZ}\rho{IZ}+\frac{p_{e}}{4}{ZZ}\rho{ZZ}.

(154)

E

Appendix J
Comment on fermionic analogues

While the scheme of the main text has been concerned with qubits, as they are common in a wealth of physical platforms, it should be
clear that one can also devise fermionic analogues
Wan et al. 2023
;
Helsen et al. 2022b
. The analog of Clifford circuits in this setting are matchgate circuits or fermionic Gaussian unitaries.
For randomly picked such unitaries, one can show that the
first three moments of the
Haar distribution over the continuous group of matchgate circuits are equal to those of the discrete uniform distribution over only the matchgate circuits that are also Clifford unitaries. For this reason, the latter forms what is called a matchgate 3-design
Wan et al. 2023
, useful for fermionic classical shadows. A reasonable analog of the present scheme would
be one that takes the form that first a layer of gates is applied that act as
U(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})^{T}
on the fermionic operators
f_{1},\dots f_{n}
as

U=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{I}+iY)\oplus\mathbb{I},

(158)

where non-trivial unitaries are applied to any pairs of modes
1,\dots,n
and the identity otherwise. This is being followed by a diagonal
unitary
V={\rm diag}(e^{i\theta_{1}},\dots,e^{i\theta_{n}})
for real
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n}
, so that the overall circuit is captured by
UV
. Similarly as above, such restricted fermionic circuits give rise to classical shadows.
