Favorable Response to Immunosuppressive Therapy in Severe Aplastic Anemia With Trisomy 8 and BCOR Mutation: Sustained Hematologic Response Despite Evolving Mutational Profile—A Case Report
Authors: Mengzhu Shen, Yan Wei, Bo Shao, Cao Qin, Xiaofei Jia, Hao Jiang, Jinsong Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSevere aplastic anemia (SAA) is a serious medical condition that is characterized by its abrupt onset, rapid progression of the disease, and alarmingly high mortality rate, making it a significant concern in the field of hematology. Intensive immunosuppressive therapy (IST) is one of the primary therapeutic options; however, some SAA patients do not respond to initial IST treatment. For these patients, salvage treatment remains a significant challenge. Consequently, predicting the efficacy of IST for SAA at initial diagnosis and developing the optimal curative regimen could potentially enhance treatment outcomes. The concurrent presence of trisomy 8 and a BCL6 corepressor (BCOR) mutation in SAA is uncommon and may independently predict the efficacy of IST. In this case report, we presented a SAA patient exhibiting both trisomy 8 and a BCOR mutation at diagnosis. The bone marrow (BM) aspirate showed hypocellular BM and the bone marrow biopsy revealed a severe hypocellular bone marrow (15% cellularity). The patient experienced rapid clinical improvement, evidenced by reduced transfusion requirements and a favorable response to IST. Approximately two year's post‐IST, a follow‐up biopsy indicated remission, with the volume of hematopoietic tissue increasing to 40%. Cytogenetic analysis showed a normal karyotype, and no BCOR mutation was detected by next‐generation sequencing (NGS). Complete blood cell count results also showed improvement. In conclusion, we are the first to report a rare case of SAA characterized by the simultaneous presence of chromosome 8 abnormality and BCOR mutation in detail, which may represent a new distinct entity in SAA.Keywords: BCOR mutation, case report, intensive immunosuppressive therapy, severe aplastic anemia, trisomy 8

Introduction

Severe aplastic anemia (SAA) is a serious medical condition that is characterized by its abrupt onset, rapid progression of the disease, and alarmingly high mortality rate, making it a significant concern in the field of hematology [1, 2]. Intensive immunosuppressive therapy (IST) and allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) are recognized as the primary therapeutic options available for managing this condition, and these approaches have significantly improved the survival prognosis for many patients suffering from SAA [3]. Nevertheless, approximately 30% of patients diagnosed with SAA do not respond favorably to the initial IST treatment, while between 10% and 40% of these individuals may experience a relapse of the disease, and about 20% may exhibit clonal evolution, complicating their clinical management [4, 5, 6]. For these patients, the pursuit of salvage treatment becomes a formidable challenge that requires careful consideration and innovative strategies. Consequently, accurately predicting the efficacy of IST for SAA at the time of initial diagnosis, along with the development of an optimal curative regimen tailored to individual patient needs, may significantly enhance treatment outcomes and improve the overall quality of life for those affected by this debilitating illness.

Numerous studies have meticulously reported a variety of factors that significantly affect the efficacy of IST for SAA patients, and these findings have been thoughtfully summarized by established clinical guidelines [2, 7]. In addition to the basic characteristics that are often considered, such as a young age, less severe disease, an absolute reticulocyte count exceeding > 25 × 109/L, and an absolute lymphocyte count greater than > 1 × 109/L, the occurrence of cytogenetic abnormalities and somatic mutations is particularly noteworthy and warrants further investigation. Specifically, both the presence of trisomy 8 and the BCL6 corepressor/BCL6 corepressor‐like 1 (BCOR/BCORL1) have been identified as independent beneficial predictors for a positive response to IST [8, 9, 10]. However, the implications of their co‐occurrence in the context of treatment response remain shrouded in uncertainty and require additional research to elucidate their potential interactions. To the best of our knowledge, there currently exists no clinically practical and reliable method that can accurately predict the response to IST in patients diagnosed with SAA, highlighting a significant gap in the management of this challenging condition.

In this study, we reported an exceptionally rare case of SAA characterized by the presence of trisomy 8 alongside a BCOR mutation, which is noteworthy due to its infrequency in clinical observations. Furthermore, we undertook a review of the relevant literature, which led us to propose that the coexistence of trisomy 8 and the BCOR mutation may signify the emergence of a distinct pathological entity within the broader spectrum of SAA, warranting further investigation and consideration in future research endeavors.

Case History/Examination

A 56‐year‐old female patient, whose medical history revealed a troubling pattern of persistent fatigue that left her feeling drained, accompanied by a constant ringing in her ears known as tinnitus, and episodes of dyspnea that manifested particularly after any form of physical exercise, had been grappling with these distressing symptoms for a concerning duration of half a month, prompting her to seek medical attention in hopes of finding relief and understanding the underlying causes of her condition. In around November 2023, complete blood cell count (CBC) results showed: white blood cell (WBC) 2.04 × 109/L, absolute neutrophils count (ANC) 0.37 × 109/L, hemoglobin (Hb) 58 g/L, platelets (PLT) 14 × 109/L and absolute reticulocyte count (RET) 46.6 × 109/L. No paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) clone was detected in peripheral blood (PB). The bone marrow (BM) aspirate showed hypocellular BM (Grade V) with a granulocyte/erythroid ratio of 4.09:1. Granulocyte and erythroid accounted for 45.00% and 11.00%, respectively. No blasts were found, and the proportion of lymphocytes was relatively high, accounting for 40%. No megakaryocytes were observed and the level of platelet was less than normal. The morphology is generally normal (Figure 1a). A bone marrow biopsy was performed, revealing a severe hypocellular bone marrow (15% cellularity) without reticular fibers (Figure 1b). Flow cytometry analysis showed that lymphocytes accounted for 32.46%, of which CD5 + T cells accounted for 80.25%. Granulocytes accounted for 58.92%, and the proportion was normal, among which the proportion of CD10+ mature granulocytes was normal. Monocytes accounted for 3.10%, and there were no obvious abnormalities in phenotype. Nucleated red blood cells accounted for 4.46%, with a normal proportion and no obvious abnormalities in phenotype. CD34 + CD117+ immature myeloid cells accounted for 0.01%, and the proportion was reduced. CD19+ B progenitor cells accounted for 56.76% of CD34+ cells, which was a normal proportion. To summarize, hematopoiesis is reduced in the early stage (Figure 1c). Cytogenetic analysis revealed abnormal karyotype 47, XX, +8 [3] / 47, XX, +11 [1] / 46, XX [28] (Figure 1d). Genetic tests of TP53, SF3B1, DNMT3A, WT1, PRAME, MLL‐PTD and EVI1 were all negative. Next‐generation sequencing (NGS) detected a Tier I mutation (strong clinical significance), BCOR mutation (variant allele frequency: 10.9%, October 2023), alongside mutations of uncertain significance (Tier III) in CHEK2 and RUNX1 (Figure 1e). The liver function test, thyroid function test, coagulation panel, and other immune tests were normal.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

The patient was diagnosed with SAA and underwent the treatment of IST, cyclosporine A (CsA), and testosterone undecanoate. The specific regimen meticulously outlined for the patient included antithymocyte globulin (ATG) at a dosage of 3.5 mg per kilogram per day for a duration of five consecutive days, alongside CsA at a dosage of 75 mg every 12 h, administered daily, and testosterone undecanoate at a dosage of 40 mg three times a day. In conjunction with these treatments, methylprednisolone was administered at a daily dosage of 80 mg. Two days after administration, the patient experienced a dramatic increase in body temperature, soaring to a concerning 40°C. Blood culture was taken to detect infectious pathogens, but no positive results were found. The infusion rate of methylprednisolone descended slowly and antibiotics were upgraded to Mepine and linezolid. On the 3rd day, diarrhea occurred, and the stool test was positive for occult blood. No white blood cells were seen in the stool. Fasting was given and the patient improved significantly after intravenous nutrition. On the 6th day, the CBC findings of the peripheral blood were as follows: WBC 5.52 × 109/L, Hb 65 g/L, PLT 43 × 109/L, neutrophils 5.21 × 109/L, and the proportion of lymphocytes 0.7%. The patient experienced a rapid and significant relief from their symptoms, which was notably accompanied by a reduction in the need for transfusions, ultimately leading to the achievement of the IST response (Figure 2).

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Approximately two years after the initiation of IST treatment (Figure 3a–d), a biopsy was performed, which indicated a remission of the condition (Figure 3b). The cellularity of BM reached Grade III, and the volume of hematopoietic tissue had increased to an encouraging 40%, suggesting a positive response to the treatment (Figure 3a). Furthermore, cytogenetic analysis conducted during this period revealed a normal karyotype, specifically 46, XX, indicating no chromosomal abnormalities that could complicate the clinical picture (Figure 3d). The BCOR mutation was no more detected by NGS. Instead, NGS revealed CHEK2, UBA1, RUNX1, RELN, MSH6, FAT1 (Tier III) mutations. The CBC findings were as follows: WBC 2.77 × 109/L, neutrophils 1.33 × 109/L, Hb 112 g/L, PLT 159 × 109/L, and RET 77.4 × 109/L. These results reflected a stable hematological profile, indicative of the patient's overall health status during treatment. The patient was compliant, and the treatment was well tolerated throughout, and importantly, no serious infectious events were reported, as shown in Figure 2. Discussion

The phenomenon of trisomy 8 occurring alongside a BCOR mutation is an exceptionally rare occurrence in the context of SAA, and in our particular case, this coexistence has manifested as a remarkably favorable outcome. Previous studies have documented these cytogenetic abnormalities and somatic mutations in isolation [12], which may inadvertently overlook the significant clinical implications and potential benefits that arise from their combination, suggesting that a more integrated approach to understanding these factors could enhance patient outcomes and treatment strategies.

The effectiveness of IST is intricately contingent upon the presence of residual hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) [13], which play a crucial role in the therapeutic process. Consequently, the presence of gene mutations and chromosomal abnormalities that may hold potential relevance can offer valuable predictive insights into patient outcomes. Among these abnormalities, trisomy 8 stands out as one of the most prevalent cytogenetic alterations observed in myeloid neoplasms [14], occurring in approximately 1.3%–6.7% of cases of AA [9, 15, 16, 17, 18]. Oka et al. conducted a pivotal study demonstrating that hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) harboring trisomy 8 possess the remarkable ability to evade destruction by CD8+ T cells, while their non‐trisomy 8 counterparts are subjected to preferential suppression [19]. This finding suggests a compelling link between the presence of trisomy 8 and the immunobiological mechanisms that underlie the destruction of HSCs. The clinical significance of various cytogenetic abnormalities in the context of AA is progressively being elucidated, with particular attention to the implications of trisomy 8. Notably, this chromosomal abnormality has been associated with a more favorable response to immunosuppressive therapy, leading to improved clinical outcomes, which include enhanced progression‐free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) [8, 10], thereby highlighting its importance in the management of patients with this condition.

However, trisomy 8 is also significantly involved in the most common karyotype that is frequently encountered in cases of myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) that arise secondary to aplastic anemia (AA), with studies indicating that these conditions usually develop in approximately 15% to 26% of patients suffering from AA [20, 21, 22]. This phenomenon may be attributed to the suppression of the target population of hematopoietic cells, which subsequently allows for the expansion of defective non‐target clones, ultimately leading to the emergence of hematological malignancies [19]. Nevertheless, a study conducted by Bai et al. reported that while trisomy 8 reduced the self‐renewal capacity and hindered the differentiation of HSPCs, this genetic alteration alone was found to be insufficient to induce the development of myeloid malignancies in murine models [23]. Therefore, the clinical significance of trisomy 8 in isolation remains a contentious topic, with ongoing debates among researchers regarding its role and implications in hematological disorders.

The advent of NGS technology has revolutionized the field of genomics, enabling the precise identification of specific somatic mutations that serve as critical indicators of clinical outcomes in patients with AA who present with cytogenetic abnormalities [8, 24]. This advanced methodology not only enhances our understanding of the genetic landscape of these patients but also holds the potential to assist healthcare professionals in predicting the response to IST with greater accuracy and confidence. Focusing on the BCOR gene, which is situated at position 11.4 on the short arm of chromosome X and comprises a total of 15 exons [25], previous studies have reported that this gene encodes a transcription factor that plays a pivotal role in the process of hematopoiesis by actively inhibiting differentiation toward the myeloid lineage [25, 26, 27]. The frequency of mutations in the BCOR/BCORL1 within the context of AA has been reported to range from 4.0% to 9.3% [8, 9, 15, 17, 24, 28, 29], indicating a notable prevalence of these genetic alterations among affected individuals. Furthermore, studies have shown that mutations in BCOR and BCORL1 are associated with a more favorable response to immunosuppressive therapy, as well as improvements in both PFS and OS [8, 9]. In addition to these positive correlations, it is noteworthy that the presence of BCOR and BCORL1 mutations is linked to a reduced risk of transformation into more severe conditions such as MDS or AML [22]. Therefore, it can be concluded that the BCOR gene appears to function as a beneficial factor for patients with SAA who are undergoing IST treatment. Moreover, it may serve as a valuable auxiliary indicator, complementing the existing cytogenetic abnormalities to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the patient's prognosis and treatment response.

We are the first to provide a detailed description of a rare case of SAA that presents with both an abnormality in chromosome 8 and a mutation in the BCOR gene. Our review of the existing literature indicates that the coexistence of trisomy 8 alongside the BCOR mutation could signify the emergence of a new and distinct clinical entity within the spectrum of SAA, warranting further investigation and consideration in future studies.
