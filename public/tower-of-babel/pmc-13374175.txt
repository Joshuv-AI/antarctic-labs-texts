Staphylococcus aureus carriage among hemodialysis outpatients: risk factors, antimicrobial resistance, and molecular characterization
Authors: Agnė Kirkliauskienė, Monika Vitkauskaitė, Karolina Vankevičienė, Maksim Bratchikov, Marius Miglinas, Laurynas Rimševičius
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStaphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) carriage is prevalent among hemodialysis outpatients and may be associated with frequent healthcare contact and vascular access use. The aim of the study was to determine the prevalence of S. aureus carriage, factors associated with carriage, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and selected resistance and virulence genes among hemodialysis outpatients.MethodsA cross-sectional observational study was conducted in 2018–2021. Eighty-one hemodialysis outpatients and 81 age-matched adults without kidney disease were included. S. aureus isolates were identified using standard microbiological methods, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed according to EUCAST criteria. Multiplex real-time PCR was used for the detection of resistance genes (ermA, ermC, mecA, tetM, tetK) and the lukF-PV associated virulence gene. Factors associated with carriage were assessed using structured interviews and electronic medical records. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher‘s exact tests, and continuous variables using Mann-Whitney U-test. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Significance level - p < 0.05.ResultsEighty-one hemodialysis outpatients (39.5% female; mean age 62.0 years) were enrolled. S. aureus carriage was detected in 38.3% and was significantly higher than in controls (p = 0.041). In univariable analyses, S. aureus carriage was associated with hemodialysis access type, dialysis vintage, recent hospitalization, antimicrobial use, gastroesophageal reflux disease, atrial fibrillation, anemia, and secondary hyperparathyroidism (all p < 0.05). In exploratory multivariable analysis, only atrial fibrillation remained associated with carriage. Resistance to penicillin was observed in 69.7% of isolates, tetracycline in 6.1%, and erythromycin/clindamycin in 3.0%. No methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) isolates were detected; pvl was detected in 1/33 (3.0%) hemodialysis isolates and in 3/52 (5.8%) isolates overall.ConclusionsCompared to healthy controls, hemodialysis patients were more frequently colonized with S. aureus. The observed resistance and virulence profiles provide local epidemiological data from Lithuania. The selected resistance and virulence gene profiles provide descriptive molecular data but do not allow conclusions regarding lineages or transmission patterns.

Background

Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) is one of the most infamous, versatile commensals and a human pathogen, capable of causing potentially lethal infections [1, 2]. The link between S. aureus nasal carriage and staphylococcal disease was first reported by Danbolt in 1931 [3]. Thenceforward, colonization is considered the prerequisite for developing infections [4]. Successful S. aureus colonization is the outcome of a process determined by various factors, such as bacterial and environmental determinants, and host genetics [5]. Hemodialysis patients are more susceptible to S. aureus infections and/or colonization due to immunodeficiency, frequent attendance in healthcare settings, and repeated antibiotic treatment [6, 7]. Chronic uremia is associated with deficits in cell-mediated immunity, antibody production, and phagocytosis [8].

Although methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) has been extensively studied due to its clinical impact, methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) carriage is also highly relevant, particularly in hemodialysis patients [3, 8–10]. Colonizing strains – whether resistant or susceptible – may act as reservoirs for subsequent infection and transmission within healthcare settings [3, 11, 12]. Therefore, understanding overall S. aureus carriage, rather than focusing exclusively on MRSA, is essential in this population.

Previous studies have reported variable prevalence of S. aureus carriage among hemodialysis patients and have identified potential associated factors, such as vascular access type, duration of dialysis, and healthcare exposure [9, 10, 13, 14]. However, findings are inconsistent, and many studies focus primarily on resistance patterns, particularly MRSA, rather than providing a comprehensive assessment of carriage, associated clinical factors, and molecular characteristics [15–18].

In addition, there is limited information on S. aureus carriage and antimicrobial resistance patterns among hemodialysis patients in Lithuania and similar settings. Such data are essential, as carriage prevalence and resistance profiles may vary substantially across regions due to differences in healthcare exposure, infection control practices, and antibiotic use policies [9, 10, 15–19]. Consequently, locally derived data are necessary to better understand the epidemiology of S. aureus in this high-risk population.

In addition to phenotypic susceptibility testing, targeted molecular analysis can provide supplementary information on the presence of specific resistance and virulence determinants among colonizing strains [5, 20]. In this study, selected resistance genes (ermA, ermC, mecA, tetM, tetK) and the Panton-Valentine leucocidin (lukF-PV) gene were analyzed to characterize the genetic profiles of S. aureus isolates. The selected resistance genes represent the most common genetic mechanisms of resistance to clinically relevant antibiotic classes, including macrolides and lincosamides (ermA, ermC), beta-lactams (mecA), and tetracyclines (tetM, tetK) [20]. The pvl gene was included as a marker associated with increased virulence in certain clinical contexts [21, 22].

The aim of this study was to determine the prevalence of S. aureus carriage among hemodialysis outpatients in Vilnius, Lithuania, to identify associated risk factors, and to characterize antimicrobial resistance patterns and selected resistance and virulence genes. By combining epidemiological, clinical, and microbiological data, this study seeks to provide locally relevant insights into S. aureus carriage in a high-risk population.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted at two major hemodialysis centers in Vilnius, Lithuania: Vilnius University Hospital Santaros Klinikos Centre of Nephrology and Vilnius City Clinical Hospital. The study was designed to determine the prevalence of S. aureus carriage in hemodialysis patients, along with their antibiotic susceptibility patterns, and selected genetic determinants.

Patient population

Consenting participants were enrolled from October 23, 2018, to March 1, 2019, and again from September 9, 2020, to February 2, 2021. Participants were recruited consecutively during routine hemodialysis sessions. Adult (≥ 18 years) patients with chronic kidney disease who were hemodialysis dependent for at least two months and receiving dialysis three times per week were included. The exclusion criteria included acute respiratory infection within three months before enrollment, antibiotic use within the previous two weeks, hospitalization for reasons other than hemodialysis within the past month, or a temporary vascular access for dialysis. At enrollment, all patients completed an initial questionnaire, which consisted of demographics, length of time on hemodialysis, type of dialysis access (fistula or long-term catheter), hospitalization in the past year, use of antibiotics in the past two years, comorbidities, and smoking. Comorbidities were defined using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes. In addition, electronic medical records of dialysis patients were reviewed to obtain more accurate information on antibiotic use and medical history and reduce recall bias. The final sample size was determined by the total number of hemodialysis patients who agreed to participate in the study and provided handwritten consent.

Control group

To compare the frequency of S. aureus carriage and characteristics of antimicrobial resistance between hemodialysis patients and the general population, a control group was created. The control group consisted of 81 healthy adults with no history of kidney disease or dialysis in Vilnius city. The control group was matched from 373 healthy adults based on age using MedCalc version 19.7 (MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium). Control group participants were recruited through random selection of non-medical enterprises. Exclusion criteria included age < 18 years, non-residency in Vilnius city, and hospitalization within the previous 24 months.

Samples collection and swab processing

Nasal and throat specimens were collected using two sterile dry cotton-wool swabs. The anterior nares were sampled by rotating the swab tip in both nostrils, and the throat swab samples were taken from the tonsillar pillars. All swabs were placed into Stuart’s transport medium (Corsham, United Kingdom), transported to the microbiology laboratory, and processed within two hours.

The nasal and throat swabs were inoculated onto two types of culture media: Columbia agar with 5% sheep blood (Bio-Rad, France) and mannitol salt agar (Liofilchem®, Italy) plates. The plates were incubated at 35 ± 2 °C for 24 h. The identification of S. aureus was based on colony morphology on blood agar and mannitol salt agar, catalase, coagulase (Bio-Rad, France), DNase (bioMérieux SA, France), and latex agglutination tests (Bio-Rad, France). The reference strain S. aureus ATCC 29213 was used as the positive quality control strain for comparison with the sample isolates.

To avoid duplicate counting, only non-duplicate S. aureus isolates were included in further analyses. In both study groups, if S. aureus isolates were recovered from both nasal and throat samples of the same participant and showed the same extended antimicrobial susceptibility profile, they were considered duplicate isolates and counted as a single non-duplicate isolate.

Antimicrobial susceptibility tests

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method on Mueller-Hinton agar (Bio-Rad, France). Results were interpreted according to the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) (v. 12.0) standards (2022) [23]. Bacterial suspensions were prepared to match a 0.5 McFarland turbidity standard and inoculated onto the agar surface. Antibiotic discs and E-test strips were applied, followed by incubation at 35 ± 2 °C for 18–24 h. After incubation, inhibition zone diameters were measured and recorded.

The susceptibility of all S. aureus isolates to cefoxitin (30 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), erythromycin (15 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), clindamycin (2 µg), norfloxacin (10 µg), penicillin (1 IU), rifampin (5 µg), tetracycline (30 µg), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (1.25/23.75 µg), and mupirocin (5 µg and 200 µg) was tested using commercial discs (Liofilchem®, Italy). Inducible or constitutive clindamycin resistance was examined by using the double-disc method (D-test). Mupirocin disc diffusion results were interpreted according to previously published criteria [24]. Isolates with a zone diameter of ≥ 14 mm for both 5 µg and 200 µg discs are considered susceptible to mupirocin. Isolates with a zone diameter of < 14 mm in the 5 µg disc but ≥ 14 mm in the 200 µg disc are taken as low-level resistant strains. All isolates with zone diameters < 14 mm for both 5 µg and 200 µg are classified as high-level resistant strains [24].

Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined using the gradient strip method for vancomycin and mupirocin. Susceptibility to vancomycin was determined by using E-test strips (Liofilchem®, Italy). Mupirocin E‑test strips (bioMerieux SA, France) were used with an antibiotic concentration gradient in the range of 0.064 µg/ml to 1024 µg/ml. Isolates with mupirocin MICs ≥ 512 µg/ml were defined as highly resistant, those with MICs 8–256 µg/ml were considered as low resistant, and those with MICs < 4 µg/ml were considered as mupirocin sensitive [25]. For quality control of antimicrobial susceptibility testing, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213 was used as the reference strain in accordance with EUCAST recommendations.

DNA extraction for multiplex real-time polymerase chain reaction

For DNA extraction, S. aureus isolates stored at − 80 °C were subcultured on Brain Heart Infusion agar and incubated aerobically at 35 ± 1 °C for 24 h. Approximately 2–4 colonies were collected and suspended in 500 µL of sterile deionized water. The suspensions were briefly vortexed to obtain homogeneous suspensions, then heated in a Techne Dri-Block® DB-3D heating block at 100 °C for 10 min. After heating, the samples were allowed to cool to room temperature and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The clear supernatant containing bacterial DNA was carefully transferred into sterile storage tubes and stored at − 80 °C until further multiplex real-time PCR analysis.

Detection of selected resistance and virulence associated genes

All S. aureus strains isolated from both the hemodialysis patient group and the control group were subjected to the same molecular testing panel, including detection of mecA, mecC, ermA, ermC, tetK, tetM, lukF-PV gene, and the 16S rRNA coding sequence targets, using our designed multiplex real-time PCR protocols. No selection based on phenotypic antimicrobial resistance was applied. The 16S rRNA target was applied as an internal PCR control. The primers and probes were previously published by Kirkliauskienė et al. [26] (Table 1). The multiplex reactions were divided into mixture I (targeting ermA, lukF-PV, ermC, and 16S rRNA), mixture II (targeting tetK, tetM, mecA, and 16S rRNA), and mixture III (targeting mecC and 16S rRNA) (Table 2). The reactions were carried out in a total volume of 15 µl using 1 µl of S. aureus lysate DNA. The multiplex real-time PCR mixture composition was 7.5 µL of 2x SensiMix™ II Probe (Bioline Reagents, UK), 200 nM of each primer (Biolegio, Netherlands), and 100 nM of each hydrolysis probe (Biolegio, Netherlands). The reactions were performed using a Rotor-Gene Q 5plex HRM thermal cycler (QIAGEN, Germany) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min (1 cycle) followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 20 s and primer annealing and extension at 55 °C for 1 min. Fluorescent signals were acquired at the end of each extension step.

According to the Rotor-Gene Q software analysis, a sample was considered positive for a given target gene (ermA, ermC, mecA, lukF-PV, tetM, or tetK) when the amplification curve crossed the fluorescence threshold under the software’s default normalization conditions, with the threshold set at 0.01, and the corresponding Ct value was < 28. Samples with Ct values ≥ 28 or with no detectable amplification curve were interpreted as negative for the respective gene (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS), version 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY). Percentage and frequencies were used for the categorical variables. Quantitative data were expressed as mean value (± standard deviation) or median (minimal-maximal values). Normality of continuous variables was assessed prior to selecting the appropriate statistical tests. Differences between groups were calculated by the Mann-Whitney U-test as appropriate for quantitative data, and the chi-squared and Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical data. Univariable logistic regression analysis was performed to assess associations between potential risk factors and S. aureus carriage. Variables of clinical relevance were included in a multivariable logistic regression model to evaluate their independent associations. Results were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Given the limited sample size and number of outcome events, the multivariable analysis was considered exploratory and no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 81 hemodialysis patients (HD group) were enrolled in this study and the control group consisted of 81 healthy individuals. The average age of the hemodialysis patients was 62.0 ± 15.4 years; 49 (60.5%) of them were males. The average age of the healthy control group was 57.8 ± 14.7 years; 52 (64.2%) of them were females (Table 3). The mean period on hemodialysis was 43.9 ± 64.0 (range 2–360 months) for 81 hemodialysis patients.

During this study, the carriage of S. aureus was found in 50 participants (30.9%), of which 31 were hemodialysis patients (62.0%) and 19 were controls (38.0%). The difference between HD patients and the control group was statistically significant (P = 0.041); moreover, patients on hemodialysis had approximately 2-fold higher odds of S. aureus carriage (OR 2.023, 95% CI 1.023–4.001). No fully adjusted comparison between hemodialysis patients and controls was performed because of the limited sample size and risk of unstable estimates.

Hemodialysis patients were stratified into five age groups (Fig. 2); no statistically significant differences in carriage were observed between age groups (P = 0.636).

The relationship between the HD duration and isolation rate of S. aureus was assessed, with carriers having longer dialysis duration compared to non-carriers (54.45 ± 68.1 vs. 37.36 ± 61.0, P = 0.038). The risk for S. aureus carriage significantly increased with an increased vintage of dialysis (P = 0.022), with the highest risk in the HD group of 13–24 months (OR 5.25, 95% CI 1.38–19.96). Among patients with AVF, 26/51 (51%) were S. aureus carriers compared with 5/30 (16.7%) with central venous catheters. Patients with arteriovenous fistula (AVF) for vascular access had 5-fold higher odds of being carriers compared with patients with central venous catheters (95% CI 1.72–15.72, P = 0.002).

More than 80% of patients (n = 66) reported a hospitalization in the past year, which was associated with higher S. aureus carriage (P = 0.001), although the duration of hospitalization was not significant (P = 0.237). Antibiotic use was associated with S. aureus carriage (P = 0.004), with patients who had more recently used antibiotics being less likely to be carriers (Table 4).

All patients reported at least one underlying condition, and 66 of them (81.5%) had two or more. In univariable analysis, anemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism, atrial fibrillation, and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) were associated with S. aureus carriage (Table 5). In exploratory multivariable analysis, atrial fibrillation (OR 20.07, 95% CI 2.93–137.50) remained associated with S. aureus carriage, whereas GERD showed a non-significant trend toward association (OR 6.24, 95% CI 0.87–44.99) (Table 5).

Exploratory univariable and multivariable subgroup analyses stratified by age and sex were also performed; however, these analyses were limited by small subgroup sizes and low event numbers. In exploratory subgroup analyses, atrial fibrillation was associated with an increased risk for S. aureus carriage in patients of both genders, while GERD showed an association only among female patients (OR 5.68, 95% CI 1.14–28.38).

In exploratory age-stratified analysis, secondary hyperparathyroidism showed an association with S. aureus carriage among patients younger than 35 years (OR 25.79, 95% CI 1.03–645.70); however, the estimate was highly imprecise, as reflected by the very wide confidence interval.

A total of 43 S. aureus strains were isolated from hemodialysis patients, of which 33 were classified as non-duplicate isolates. In the control group, 21 S. aureus strains were isolated, of which 19 were classified as non-duplicate isolates. The resistance of S. aureus in HD patients and the control group was as follows: penicillin (69.7%, 73.7%, respectively), tetracycline (6.1%, 0%, respectively), clindamycin (3%, 0%, respectively), and erythromycin (3%, 0%, respectively). There was no statistically significant difference in the antibiotic resistance pattern between the two groups (P = 0.436). Phenotypic cefoxitin susceptibility testing and molecular screening for mecA and mecC genes revealed that no MRSA isolates were detected in either the hemodialysis patient group or the control group. Moreover, all strains were susceptible to mupirocin (Table 6).

Discussion

In this observational study, we report the prevalence of S. aureus carriage among hemodialysis patients and risk factors associated with the carriage of the pathogen. We also assessed selected molecular markers, including S. aureus resistance genes and the lukF-PV virulence-associated gene. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this kind of data on hemodialysis patients in Lithuania has not been published yet. Moreover, this is one of the very few studies on this topic conducted worldwide and particularly in Europe.

We found that 38.3% of HD patients were S. aureus carriers, which was close to Scheuch et al. study (40%) conducted in Germany [10] and lower than reported by Price et al. in the UK (49%) [9]. Studies done in the UK, USA, Germany, Taiwan, and Iran reported MRSA colonization rates of 10%, 12%, 11.7%, 28.1%, and 34.9%, respectively [9, 10, 16–18]. Importantly, we did not identify MRSA, representing a key finding in contrast these studies. This difference may reflect local epidemiological conditions, including infection control practices, antibiotic prescribing patterns, and characteristics of the studied population, highlighting the importance of region-specific surveillance.

Although MRSA has received considerable attention due to its association with antimicrobial resistance and more limited treatment options, MSSA remains highly clinically relevant, particularly in hemodialysis populations. MSSA accounts for the majority of S. aureus colonizing strains in many cohorts, often representing up to 80–90% of isolates, and is strongly associated with subsequent infection, including bloodstream infections originating from vascular access sites [9]. While MRSA colonization is associated with a higher risk of difficult-to-treat infections and has been linked to increased morbidity and mortality [27], MSSA accounts for a substantial proportion of invasive S. aureus infections and can result in similarly severe clinical outcomes, including bacteremia and endocarditis, particularly in patients with vascular access devices. Therefore, focusing exclusively on MRSA may underestimate the overall clinical burden of S. aureus in hemodialysis patients. In this population, both MSSA and MRSA carriage should be considered clinically important reservoirs for transmission and subsequent invasive disease [9, 10].

Walsh et al. found that kidney insufficiency itself increases the risk of S. aureus carriage by 2.5-fold [28]. Consistent with this, we observed a higher prevalence of S. aureus carriage among hemodialysis patients compared with the general population. Previous studies have also suggested a relationship between dialysis duration and carriage, with Ghasemian et al. identifying dialysis vintage as a significant factor [13], while Souly et al. reported a similar trend [29]. Our findings are in line with these observations, indicating that longer time on hemodialysis may be associated with increased likelihood of S. aureus carriage. The observed association between more recent antibiotic use and lower S. aureus carriage may reflect temporary suppression of colonization or reverse causation rather than a protective effect of antibiotic exposure itself.

The local healthcare context may also influence these findings. In our setting, the presence of antimicrobial stewardship programs and adherence to institutional antibiotic guidelines may contribute to lower selection pressure for resistant strains, as shown in previous studies demonstrating reductions in antimicrobial resistance following stewardship interventions [8, 12, 30, 31]. In addition, the preference for AVF over central venous catheter (CVC) as first-line vascular access may shape colonization patterns, given the differing risk profiles associated with these modalities. Furthermore, local infection control practices, including hygiene protocols and screening strategies, may influence transmission dynamics within hemodialysis units, as variability in such practices has been associated with differences in S. aureus colonization and infection rates across healthcare settings [32, 33]. Taken together, these system-level factors may contribute to the observed epidemiological patterns and highlight the importance of interpreting carriage prevalence and resistance data within the specific organizational and clinical context of the studied healthcare setting.

According to previous studies [12, 34, 35], both cuffed and uncuffed CVCs are associated with a higher risk of S. aureus bacteremia, however, Scheuch et al. Demonstrated that the S. aureus colonization density in patients with AVF was significantly higher compared to those dialyzed through CVC [10]. Although we did not evaluate colonization density, we observed an association between AVF use and S. aureus carriage. We speculate that the observed association between AVF use and S. aureus carriage may reflect confounding by longer history of kidney disease, longer time on renal replacement therapy, greater cumulative healthcare exposure, and patient selection rather than a direct effect of vascular access type itself.

Previous studies have reported higher S. aureus carriage rates in men and with increasing age [5, 14, 36–40], however, our study did not confirm these associations. Evidence on comorbidities as risk factors for colonization remains inconsistent. While some studies have identified associations with diabetes mellitus and chronic lung disease [14, 18, 41], a meta-analysis demonstrated a significant relationship only for chronic lung disease [15].

In our cohort, anemia and secondary hyperparathyroidism were associated with S. aureus carriage. Anemia has been linked to increased risk of bacteremia and may reflect chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation in hemodialysis patients [42–44], potentially facilitating colonization. Secondary hyperparathyroidism may also influence immune function, as parathyroid hormone can affect T- and B-lymphocyte activity and impair neutrophil migration and chemotaxis, thereby potentially creating conditions favorable for S. aureus colonization [45].

Associations observed for GERD and atrial fibrillation are less clear. These conditions may represent markers of advanced age, higher comorbidity burden, increased healthcare exposure, or medication use rather than direct biological risk factors, as suggested in studies highlighting the role of patient complexity and healthcare contact in S. aureus carriage [5, 15]. It is also possible that condition-specific mechanisms may contribute, such as alterations of the microbiota associated with acid-suppressive therapy in GERD and systemic inflammation in atrial fibrillation, both of which may influence susceptibility to colonization [46–48]. As these potential mechanisms remain speculative, the observed associations should be interpreted cautiously, particularly considering the wide confidence intervals in our analysis.

The resistance profiles of isolated S. aureus in this study demonstrated high penicillin (69.7%) and low tetracycline (6.1%), clindamycin (3%), and erythromycin (3%) levels. A high percentage (81.8–90%) of penicillin resistant S. aureus strains in the hemodialysis population has been observed since 1959 [7, 14, 49]. Resistance to tetracycline, clindamycin and erythromycin was rather low in our study compared to Kozioł-Montewka et al. [50] and Tashakori et al. [51]. (tetracycline resistance: 63.3% and 75%; clindamycin and erythromycin resistance: 18.7% and 25%, respectively). Mupirocin is a cornerstone of S. aureus decolonization regimens; however, increasing resistance has been reported, with approximately 7.6% of clinical S. aureus isolates demonstrating resistance in recent analyses [52]. Hassoun-Kheir et al. reported mupirocin resistance to be 4.2% − 6.7% in hemodialysis patients during different study periods; nevertheless, the number of isolates was particularly low [53]. There were no mupirocin-resistant strains in our study, suggesting preserved susceptibility in this setting.

Targeted molecular analysis provided additional context for the phenotypic susceptibility results. The absence of mecA and mecC genes among tested isolates was consistent with the phenotypic absence of MRSA, supporting the reliability of methicillin susceptibility findings. Among isolates with macrolide/lincosamide resistance, detection of ermC was consistent with an MLSB (Macrolide-Lincosamide-Streptogramin B) resistance mechanism, while tetM was also identified in the same strain despite phenotypic susceptibility to tetracycline. This genotype–phenotype discrepancy may reflect non-expression or insufficient expression of the detected resistance genes, resulting in resistance levels below the phenotypic breakpoint. Overall, the low frequency of detected resistance genes was consistent with the generally low resistance to non- β-lactam antibiotics observed in this cohort. However, because only selected genes were assessed, the analysis does not exclude other resistance mechanisms.

Panton-Valentine leucocidin is a cytotoxic virulence factor associated with severe infections such as necrotizing pneumonia and skin and soft tissue infections [21, 22]. In hemodialysis patients, who are particularly vulnerable, colonization with such strains could have important clinical consequences. In the present study, the pvl gene was detected in a small proportion of isolates (3.0% in hemodialysis patients), indicating that pvl-positivity was uncommon among the isolates tested. This prevalence is lower than reported in studies from Morocco and Iran (8.6%) [54, 55]. In addition, strains harboring both pvl and resistance genes (for example, ermC, tetM) may have a selective advantage, as the combination of enhanced virulence and antimicrobial resistance could promote transmission and sustained colonization, particularly in healthcare settings [20, 56]. However, our study design and targeted molecular approach do not allow assessment of transmission dynamics or clonal relationships. Therefore, the selected resistance and virulence gene profiles should be interpreted as descriptive molecular data only, and do not allow conclusions regarding circulating lineages, strain fitness, or transmission dynamics.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes inference of causality. Second, the modest sample size limits statistical power, and may result in imprecise estimates with wide confidence intervals. In addition, because the number of S. aureus carriers was limited relative to the number of candidate predictors, the multivariable regression model may have been overfitted; therefore, these estimates should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating. Third, selection bias may be present, as participation was voluntary and may not fully represent the broader hemodialysis or control populations. Fourth, residual confounding cannot be excluded, as differences between hemodialysis patients and controls may not be fully accounted for analytically. Finally, the study was conducted in two centers in Lithuania, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings with different clinical practices and epidemiological characteristics. We also did not assess persistence of S. aureus carriage (e.g. persistent vs. intermittent colonization) or evaluate clinical outcomes such as infection or mortality, which limits interpretation of the clinical relevance of colonization. In addition, this study did not include deeper strain typing methods such as spa typing, multilocus sequence typing, or whole-genome sequencing; therefore, clonal relatedness, transmission pathways, and specific circulating lineages could not be determined, and observed differences should be interpreted as gene profile variation rather than evidence of distinct circulating strains.

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths. It focuses on a well-defined, high-risk population of hemodialysis patients, for whom regional data on S. aureus are limited. The use of standardized microbiological methods and antimicrobial susceptibility testing ensures reliable identification of isolates and provides clinically relevant information. In addition, data were collected in routine clinical settings using a uniform protocol across centers, enhancing internal validity and real-world applicability. Finally, the integration of phenotypic and targeted molecular analyses provides complementary insight into resistance and virulence characteristics of circulating strains.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study, approximately 40% of hemodialysis patients were carriers of S. aureus, a prevalence higher than that observed in the general population. No MRSA isolates were identified, all strains remained susceptible to mupirocin, and the prevalence of pvl-positive strains was low with pvl-positivity being uncommon among the isolates tested. The selected resistance and virulence gene profiles provide descriptive molecular data but do not allow conclusions regarding circulating lineages or transmission patterns. These findings provide locally relevant descriptive epidemiological and molecular data that may help inform future surveillance and infection prevention studies in hemodialysis settings. However, prospective studies with longitudinal follow-up and clinical outcome assessment are needed before screening or decolonization strategies can be considered for clinical implementation.
