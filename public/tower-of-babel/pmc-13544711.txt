Bilateral wunderlich syndrome revealing a low-grade oncocytic tumor of the kidney: a case report
Authors: Ernest Junrui Lim, Hoi Pong Nicholas Wong, Zubin Silwal, Yen Seow Benjamin Goh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Wunderlich syndrome is a rare urological emergency characterized by spontaneous, nontraumatic renal hemorrhage, most commonly associated with renal neoplasms. While renal cell carcinoma and angiomyolipoma account for most cases, emerging entities such as low-grade oncocytic tumor (LOT) broaden the differential and may influence management decisions.Case presentation:A 67-year-old woman presented with acute right flank pain, hypotension, severe anemia, and acute kidney injury. Imaging revealed bilateral renal masses with hemorrhage consistent with Wunderlich syndrome. She was stabilized with fluid resuscitation and blood transfusion. Angiography demonstrated no active bleeding, and embolization was not performed. Subsequent magnetic resonance imaging confirmed bilateral multifocal renal masses suspicious for malignancy. Although bilateral nephrectomy was initially considered, a biopsy-guided approach was undertaken. Histology of the left renal lesion revealed an eosinophilic neoplasm consistent with an LOT. The patient was referred for oncologic evaluation and further management.Clinical discussion:This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of spontaneous renal hemorrhage in the presence of bilateral renal masses. Early recognition and stabilization are critical in Wunderlich syndrome. Importantly, tissue diagnosis can significantly alter management, particularly when indolent tumor subtypes such as LOT are identified. Conservative management may be appropriate for hemodynamically stable patients, and angioembolization has limited utility in the absence of focal active bleeding.Conclusion:Bilateral Wunderlich syndrome may occur in association with indolent renal tumors such as LOT. A biopsy-guided, multidisciplinary approach following stabilization can preserve renal function and avoid unnecessary radical surgery.

Introduction

Wunderlich syndrome is characterized by acute nontraumatic hemorrhage into the subcapsular and perirenal spaces, often in the setting of an underlying renal neoplasm[1]. The classic presentation includes acute flank pain, a palpable flank mass, and signs of hypovolemic shock (Lenk’s triad), although the complete triad is not always present[2]. Renal cell carcinoma and angiomyolipoma together account for most reported cases[2], but other etiologies – including rare renal tumors, coagulopathy, and inflammatory conditions – are increasingly recognized, broadening the differential diagnosis of Wunderlich-type presentations[3]. Concurrently, renal pathology has evolved to encompass newly described entities, including low-grade oncocytic tumor (LOT) of the kidney, now recognized by the World Health Organization as a distinct eosinophilic renal neoplasm[4–7],.

Here, we present a novel case of bilateral renal hemorrhage compatible with Wunderlich syndrome, in which a biopsy of the left renal mass confirmed LOT. We describe the clinical presentation, investigations, management considerations, and outcome of this patient. This case underscores both the diagnostic complexity of spontaneous renal hemorrhage and the importance of tissue-based diagnosis in shaping management, particularly when bilateral disease threatens renal function and radical surgery would render the patient anephric. This study was reported in accordance with the SCARE guidelines[8].

Case presentation

A 67-year-old Chinese woman presented with 1 week of exertional dyspnea and 2 days of right flank pain, which markedly worsened on the morning of her presentation. She denied hematuria, dysuria, or other lower urinary tract symptoms.

Past medical history included hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and ductal carcinoma in situ of the right breast treated with wide local excision, adjuvant radiotherapy, and 5 years of adjuvant tamoxifen. Medications included Atorvastatin 10 mg, Losartan 100 mg, and Nifedipine 60 mg twice daily; she was not on antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy. Family history was notable for a sister with breast and lung cancer, and a brother who began hemodialysis in his 50s for nondiabetic renal failure. She was a nonsmoking, nondrinking retiree with no prior urological conditions.

On examination, she was hypotensive and pale, with bilateral palpable flank masses; the right was tender. Point-of-care ultrasound revealed bilateral heterogeneous renal lesions.

Laboratory investigations showed hemoglobin 6.5 g/dL, white cell count 17.97 × 109/L, platelets 308 × 109/L, serum creatinine 306 μmol/L (baseline of 85 μmol/L 2 months prior), lactate 3.4 mmol/L, and normal coagulation parameters. An urgent non-contrast computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis was performed, with contrast withheld because of acute kidney injury.

The CT (Figs 1 and 2) demonstrated a 10.2 × 10.8 × 10.0 cm heterogeneous lower-pole right renal mass with subcapsular and perirenal hemorrhage tracking into the right paracolic gutter and pelvis, suspicious for partial rupture. The left kidney was enlarged with multiple heterogeneous masses, the largest measuring 7.8 × 8.1 × 7.2 cm in the upper pole, with focal hemorrhage and no significant perinephric collection or fat stranding. The patient was resuscitated with intravenous fluids and two units of packed red blood cells, and admitted to the Surgical High Dependency Unit (SHDU). A diagnosis of bilateral Wunderlich syndrome, complicated by hypotension, anemia, and acute kidney injury, was made. Angiography of the right kidney revealed multiple neoplastic lesions but no pseudoaneurysm, arteriovenous fistula, or active contrast extravasation; embolization was therefore not performed. Left-sided embolization was also deferred, as the hemorrhage appeared contained. No further transfusion, fluid resuscitation, or surgical intervention was required.

Examination revealed no family history or stigmata of tuberous sclerosis. She stepped down from the SHDU to the ward on day 3 and was discharged on day 8, with creatinine improving from 306 to 154 μmol/L and her hemoglobin stable at 8.5 g/dL.

Contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging of the kidneys (Fig. 3) on day 5 confirmed bilaterally enlarged kidneys with multiple exophytic, heterogeneous, avidly enhancing masses showing central necrosis and intratumoral hemorrhage, suspicious for renal cell carcinoma. There was no hydronephrosis or definite vascular thrombosis. Staging CT of the chest revealed no pulmonary lesions. Preliminary staging was cT3aN0M0 on the right and cT2aN0M0 on the left. The multidisciplinary tumor board recommended bilateral radical nephrectomy but agreed that biopsy-guided management with systemic therapy was an acceptable alternative, as upfront bilateral nephrectomy would render the patient anephric, requiring lifelong renal replacement therapy. After deliberation with her family, she was discharged on day 8 with outpatient urological follow-up.

At the 2-week review, creatinine had normalized to 85 μmol/L. After further family discussion, she opted for an image-guided biopsy of the left renal mass only, given the high bleeding risk of a right-sided biopsy. Histology of the left-sided lesion revealed monomorphic sheets of bland oncocytic cells with trabecular and focal tubular formations, regular, rounded nuclei, no prominent nucleoli, and no clear cell or papillary architecture, in keeping with an LOT. Immunohistochemistry showed cytokeratin 7 (CK7) strongly and diffusely positive, with CD10, Vimentin, CAIX, and cluster of differentiation 117 (CD117) all negative. Molecular testing was deferred due to financial considerations. The right-sided lesion remains histologically unconfirmed but is radiologically similar to the left-sided LOT.

The patient was referred to medical oncology for systemic therapy and further genetic evaluation, given the bilateral and multifocal nature of her disease.

Discussion

Wunderlich syndrome as a urological emergency

This case highlights Wunderlich syndrome as a true urological emergency requiring prompt recognition and aggressive resuscitation[2]. Lenk’s triad may be incomplete, yet patients can deteriorate rapidly to hemodynamic compromise and acute kidney injury if not treated urgently[2]. Cross-sectional imaging confirms subcapsular and perirenal hematoma and guides further management; however, the immediate priority is stabilization, anemia correction, and renal protection. Early aggressive resuscitation allowed the patient to remain hemodynamically stable long enough for biopsy-guided decision-making, avoiding emergency nephrectomy. Wunderlich syndrome demands the same urgency as other causes of hypovolemic shock and should be managed in close collaboration among urology, critical care, and interventional radiology[9].

Genetic considerations and biopsy-guided decision-making in bilateral renal disease

The bilateral and multifocal renal masses, combined with a family history of multiple malignancies and nondiabetic renal failure, raised the possibility of an underlying genetic condition such as tuberous sclerosis complex or another tuberous sclerosis complex/mechanistic target of rapamycin (TSC/mTOR) pathway disorder[10–12]. LOT is a recently described renal oncocytic neoplasm characterized by solid eosinophilic architecture, diffuse CK7 positivity, CD117 negativity, and recurrent pathogenic mTOR/TSC1/TSC2 variants, conferring mTOR-driven biology and generally indolent clinical behavior[4,13]. Although no clear syndromic features were identified, this pattern supported a biopsy-first approach and further genetic evaluation rather than proceeding directly to bilateral nephrectomy.

Confirming LOT rather than aggressive renal cell carcinoma on the left, shifted management toward surveillance and possible systemic therapy, reserving surgery on the right kidney for clear indications[7]. This case illustrates that integrating histology from at least one lesion with imaging and multidisciplinary input can refine management and prevent unnecessary radical surgery in patients with multifocal renal masses and spontaneous hemorrhage.

Conservative management and the role of tamponade physiology

The right-sided subcapsular and perirenal hematoma, with extension into the right pararenal space and paracolic gutter, was clinically significant but did not require surgical decompression, likely benefiting from physiological self-tamponade within the confined retroperitoneal space[14]. This allowed time to optimize renal function, correct anemia, and plan a biopsy-guided strategy rather than proceeding directly to bilateral nephrectomy – which would have rendered the patient anephric, requiring lifelong renal replacement therapy. The decision was further supported by the indolent LOT histology on the left, which carries a more favorable prognosis than many conventional renal cell carcinomas, underscoring that conservative resuscitation can be sufficient when hemorrhage is contained and the patient remains hemodynamically stable[12].

Challenges and limitations of radiologically guided angioembolization

Angioembolization remains a key management option in Wunderlich syndrome when the bleeding source is focal and identifiable[9,15]. In our case, angiography of the right kidney revealed multiple neoplastic lesions but no pseudoaneurysm, arteriovenous fistula, or active contrast extravasation, indicating the hemorrhage was likely multifocal or already tamponaded. Proceeding with embolization risked non-target ischemia and further loss of renal function without clear benefit in an already renal failure-prone kidney with bilateral tumors and limited functional reserve. The decision to conclude without embolization reflected the balance between the theoretical benefit of vessel occlusion and the risk of renal damage. This case highlights the limitations of embolization in multifocal, nonfocal hemorrhage and reinforces the importance of integrating angiographic findings with clinical and biochemical parameters before intervening.

Conclusion

We describe a 67-year-old woman presenting with bilateral Wunderlich syndrome, in whom an image-guided biopsy of the left renal mass confirmed an LOT.

The case illustrates that Wunderlich-type hemorrhage may occur in the context of indolent LOT-like biology, and that early recognition as a urological emergency, followed by hemodynamic stabilization and a biopsy-guided multidisciplinary strategy, can preserve renal function and avoid bilateral nephrectomy. It further highlights the limitations of angioembolization in multifocal, nonfocal hemorrhage and reinforces tissue-based diagnosis as a key step in managing spontaneous renal hemorrhage with bilateral or multifocal renal masses.
