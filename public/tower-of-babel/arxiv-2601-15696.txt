Learning Functional Graphs with Nonlinear Sufficient Dimension Reduction
Kyongwon Kim kimk@yonsei.ac.kr, Bing Li bxl9@psu.edu

Abstract
Functional graphical models have undergone extensive development during the recent years, leading to a variety models such as the functional Gaussian graphical model, the functional copula Gaussian graphical model, the functional Bayesian graphical model, the nonparametric functional additive graphical model, and the conditional functional graphical model. These models rely either on some parametric form of distributions on random functions, or on additive conditional independence, a criterion that is different from probabilistic conditional independence. In this paper we introduce a nonparametric functional graphical model based on functional sufficient dimension reduction. Our method not only relaxes the Gaussian or copula Gaussian assumptions, but also enhances estimation accuracy by avoiding the “curse of dimensionality”. Moreover, it retains the probabilistic conditional independence as the criterion to determine the absence of edges. By doing simulation study and analysis of the f-MRI dataset, we demonstrate the advantages of our method.

The functional graphical model proposed by

In this paper, we propose a nonparametric functional graphical model based on the recently developed nonlinear sufficient dimension reduction (SDR) for functional data

We then evaluate the conditional independence

Since our method combines the functional graphical model with SDR, we call it the functional sufficient graphical model (f-SGM).

Functional sufficient graphical models

Functional nonlinear sufficient dimension reduction

We use the f-GSIR in

4
Determining conditional independence

In the second step, we determine whether the conditional independence

Let

is a mapping from

and

\displaystyle\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}((f,u),(g,v))=\exp(-\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}\|f-g\|_{\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{\rsfstena H}_{\scriptscriptstyle i}}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}+\gamma_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\|u-v\|^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}),

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle V^{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}V^{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}}=&\,E[(\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}(\cdot,V^{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij})-\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle V^{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}})\otimes(\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}(\cdot,V^{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij})-\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle V^{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}})],\\

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle V^{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}V^{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}}-\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle V^{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}U^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}}\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle U^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}U^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\dagger}\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle U^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}V^{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}}.

, and

5.2
First-level Hilbert spaces

We now construct the first-level Hilbert spaces
\mbox{\rsfsten H}\,_{\scriptscriptstyle 1},\ldots,\mbox{\rsfsten H}\,_{\scriptscriptstyle p}
based on a sample of observations on the multivariate functional data. There are more than one ways to construct these spaces — for example, using spline spaces or RKHS. Here, as in
19
and
31
, we use the RKHS. Let

be an i.i.d. sample of
X
and, for each

be the
p

. In practice, we do not observe the entire function

. Let
\tau:I\times I\to\mathbb{R}
be a positive kernel. We set

\displaystyle\mbox{\rsfsten H}\,_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}=\cdots=\mbox{\rsfsten H}\,_{\scriptscriptstyle p}=\mathrm{span}\{\tau(\cdot,u_{\scriptscriptstyle c}):c=1,\ldots,N\}.

We next represent the functional data
X_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}^{\scriptscriptstyle i},\ldots,X_{\scriptscriptstyle n}^{\scriptscriptstyle i}
as members of
\mbox{\rsfsten H}\,_{\scriptscriptstyle i}
. Let

\mbox{\rsfsten B}\,_{\scriptscriptstyle T}=\{\tau(\cdot,u_{\scriptscriptstyle c}):c=1,\ldots,N\}

\mbox{\rsfsten H}\,_{\scriptscriptstyle i}

To make this clear, let us introduce more notations. For a vector

in
\mathbb{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle N}
and a subset
A

\{1,\ldots,N\}

denote the subvector
\{v_{\scriptscriptstyle c}:c\in A\}
. For a function

, and a vector

represent the vector
(h(w_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}),\ldots,h(w_{\scriptscriptstyle r}))^{\mbox{\tiny{\sf T}}}
. For a function

, and the

just defined, let

represent the
r\times r
matrix
\{k(w_{\scriptscriptstyle a},w_{\scriptscriptstyle b}):a,b=1,\ldots,r\}
, and let
k(\cdot,w)
represent the
r
-dimensional function
(k(\cdot,w_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}),\ldots,k(\cdot,w_{\scriptscriptstyle r}))^{\mbox{\tiny{\sf T}}}
.

Using the functions in
\{\tau(\cdot,u_{\scriptscriptstyle c}):c\in S_{\scriptscriptstyle a}\}
to approximate
X_{\scriptscriptstyle a}^{\scriptscriptstyle i}
amounts to assuming
the subvector
[X_{\scriptscriptstyle a}^{\scriptscriptstyle i}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{\rsfstena B}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}}(S_{\scriptscriptstyle a}^{\scriptscriptstyle c})=0
and only using the subvector
[X_{\scriptscriptstyle a}^{\scriptscriptstyle i}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{\rsfstena B}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}}(S_{\scriptscriptstyle a})
. For any

X_{\scriptscriptstyle a}^{\scriptscriptstyle i}(t)=\{[X_{\scriptscriptstyle a}^{\scriptscriptstyle i}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{\rsfstena B}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}}(S_{\scriptscriptstyle a})\}^{\mbox{\tiny{\sf T}}}\tau(t,u(S_{\scriptscriptstyle a}))

\displaystyle X_{\scriptscriptstyle a}^{\scriptscriptstyle i}(u(S_{\scriptscriptstyle a}))=\begin{pmatrix}\tau(t_{\scriptscriptstyle a1},u(S_{\scriptscriptstyle a}))^{\mbox{\tiny{\sf T}}}\\
\vdots\\
\tau(t_{\scriptscriptstyle am_{\scriptscriptstyle a}},u(S_{\scriptscriptstyle a}))^{\mbox{\tiny{\sf T}}}\end{pmatrix}\,[X_{\scriptscriptstyle a}^{\scriptscriptstyle i}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{\rsfstena B}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}}(S_{\scriptscriptstyle a})=\tau(u(S_{\scriptscriptstyle a}),u(S_{\scriptscriptstyle a}))[X_{\scriptscriptstyle a}^{\scriptscriptstyle i}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{\rsfstena B}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}}(S_{\scriptscriptstyle a}).

Solving the above equation with Tychonoff regularization, we have

\displaystyle[X_{\scriptscriptstyle a}^{\scriptscriptstyle i}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{\rsfstena B}_{\scriptscriptstyle T}}(S_{\scriptscriptstyle a})=\{\tau(u(S_{\scriptscriptstyle a}),u(S_{\scriptscriptstyle a}))+\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle n}I_{\scriptscriptstyle m_{\scriptscriptstyle a}}\}^{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle-1}X_{\scriptscriptstyle a}^{\scriptscriptstyle i}(u(S_{\scriptscriptstyle a})).

(7)

So, the coordinate of

Note that our construction works for both the balanced case, where all the

5.6
Hybrid CCCO and its HS-norm

We estimate the operators in (
6
) by replacing the expectation operator
E
therein with the sample average operator
E_{\scriptscriptstyle n}
, including the expectations in the mean elements. We denote these estimators by putting a hat on the symbols representing the four operators. We then estimate the hybrid CCCO by

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle\ddot{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle i}\ddot{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle j}|U^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}}=\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle V^{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}V^{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}}-\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle V^{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}U^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}}(\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle U^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}U^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}}+\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle n}^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}I)^{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle-1}\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle U^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}V^{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}},

(10)

where
\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle n}^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}>0
is a tuning parameter. We estimate the graph
{\mathsf{G}}
by thresholding the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of the hybrid CCCO for
(i,j)\notin{\mathsf{V}}
.

The coordinate form of the above estimator is derived similarly as we did for f-GSIR. For each
(i,j)\in{\mathsf{V}}
, let
L_{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}
,
L_{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}
, and
L_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}
be the
n\times n
Gram matrices:

\displaystyle\{\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}(V^{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}_{\scriptscriptstyle a},V^{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}_{\scriptscriptstyle b})\}_{\scriptscriptstyle a,b=1}^{\scriptscriptstyle n},\quad\{\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}(V^{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}_{\scriptscriptstyle a},V^{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}_{\scriptscriptstyle b})\}_{\scriptscriptstyle a,b=1}^{\scriptscriptstyle n},\quad\{\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}(U^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}_{\scriptscriptstyle a},U^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}_{\scriptscriptstyle b})\}_{\scriptscriptstyle a,b=1}^{\scriptscriptstyle n},

respectively, and let
H_{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}=Q_{\scriptscriptstyle n}L_{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}Q_{\scriptscriptstyle n}
,
H_{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}=Q_{\scriptscriptstyle n}L_{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}Q_{\scriptscriptstyle n}

H_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}=Q_{\scriptscriptstyle n}L_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}Q_{\scriptscriptstyle n}

{}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle V^{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}V^{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}}=

\displaystyle\,n^{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle-1}H_{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij},\quad_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle V^{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}U^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}}=n^{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle-1}H_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}

{}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle U^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}U^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}}=

\displaystyle\,n^{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle-1}H_{\scriptscriptstyle ij},\quad_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle U^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}V^{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}}=n^{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle-1}H_{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}.

Therefore, by the rules of coordinating mapping described in
31

{}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}}[\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle\ddot{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle i}\ddot{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle j}|U^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}}=H_{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}-H_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}(H_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}+\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle n}^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}I_{\scriptscriptstyle n})^{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle-1}H_{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}.

We assess conditional dependence between
X^{\scriptscriptstyle i}
and
X^{\scriptscriptstyle j}
given
X^{\scriptscriptstyle-(i,j)}
by thresholding the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of
\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle\ddot{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle i}\ddot{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle j}|U^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}}
. The Hilbert Schmidt norm of a finite-rank operator can be represented as the Frobenius norm of a matrix as follows. Let
\|\cdot\|_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{HS}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TR}}
denote the Hilbert-Schmidt norm and trace norm of an operator, respectively, and let
\|\cdot\|_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{F}}
denote Frobenius norm of a matrix. Let
\mathfrak{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1},\mathfrak{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}
be finite-dimensional Hilbert space with spanning systems
\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}
. Let
G_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}
and
G_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}
be the Gram matrices of
\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}
. Let
A:\mathfrak{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}\to\mathfrak{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}
be a linear operator. Then,
\|A\|_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{HS}}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}=\|A^{\scriptscriptstyle*}A\|_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TR}}
, where
A^{\scriptscriptstyle*}
is the adjoint operator of
A
. The eigenvalues of
A^{\scriptscriptstyle*}A
are defined through the maximization problem:

\displaystyle\mbox{maximize}\ \langle f,A^{\scriptscriptstyle*}Af\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}}\quad\mbox{subject to}\ \langle f,f\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}}.

Reexpressed in coordinate representation, the above problem becomes

\displaystyle\mbox{maximize}\ ([f]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}})^{\mbox{\tiny{\sf T}}}G_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}(_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}}[A^{\scriptscriptstyle*}A]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}})([f]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}})\quad\mbox{subject to}\ ([f]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}})^{\mbox{\tiny{\sf T}}}G_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}([f]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}})=1.

Thus we see that the eigenvalues of the operator
A^{\scriptscriptstyle*}A
are the same as the eigenvalues of the matrix
G_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}^{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}(_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}}[A^{\scriptscriptstyle*}A]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}})G_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\dagger 1/2}
, where
G_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\dagger 1/2}
denotes
(G_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\dagger})^{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}
, and
G_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\dagger}
denotes the Moore-Penrose inverse of
G_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\|A^{\scriptscriptstyle*}A\|_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{TR}}=\mathrm{tr}(G_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}^{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}(_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}}[A^{\scriptscriptstyle*}A]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}})G_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\dagger 1/2}).

Furthermore, from
\langle A^{\scriptscriptstyle*}f,g\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}}=\langle f,Ag\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}
for all
g\in\mathfrak{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1},f\in\mathfrak{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}
we see that
(_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}}[A^{\scriptscriptstyle*}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}})^{\mbox{\tiny{\sf T}}}G_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}=G_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}(_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}[A]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}})
, implying
{}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}}[A^{\scriptscriptstyle*}]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}=G_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\dagger}(_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}[A]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}})^{\mbox{\tiny{\sf T}}}G_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}
.
Hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\{G_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}^{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}(_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}}[A^{\scriptscriptstyle*}A]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}})G_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\dagger 1/2}\}=

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{tr}\{G_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\dagger 1/2}(_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}[A]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}})^{\mbox{\tiny{\sf T}}}G_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}(_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}[A]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}})G_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\dagger 1/2})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\|G_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}^{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}(_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}[A]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}})G_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\dagger 1/2})\|_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{F}}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}.

Thus we have proved

\displaystyle\|A\|_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{HS}}=\|G_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}^{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}(_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}[A]_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathfrak{B}_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}})G_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}^{\scriptscriptstyle\dagger 1/2})\|_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{F}}.

(11)

This identity was used in
17
without proof.
By (
11
), we have

\displaystyle\|\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle\ddot{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle i}\ddot{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle j}|U^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}}\|_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{HS}}=\|H_{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}^{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}(H_{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}-H_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}(H_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}+\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle n}^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}I_{\scriptscriptstyle n})^{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle-1}H_{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij})H_{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}^{\scriptscriptstyle\dagger 1/2}\|_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{F}}.

Estimation of the edge set is then based on thresholding this norm; that is,

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathsf{E}}}=\{(i,j)\in{\mathsf{V}}:\|\hat{\Sigma}_{\scriptscriptstyle\ddot{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle i}\ddot{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle j}|U^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}}\|_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{HS}}>\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle n}\}

(12)

for some chosen threshold
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle n}>0
.

5.7
Determination of tuning parameters

There are two types of tuning parameters: those that appears in the kernels and those for the Tychonoff regularization. For the kernels, we have the kernel
\tau
for the first level Hilbert spaces; the kernels
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle(i,j)}
and
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle-(i,j)}
for the second-level Hilbert spaces; the kernels
\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}
,
\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle i,ij}=\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle i}\times\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}
, and
\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle j,ij}=\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle j}\times\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}
for the third-level Hilbert spaces. In this paper we use the Brownian motion kernel for
\tau
; that is,
\tau(u_{\scriptscriptstyle 1},u_{\scriptscriptstyle 2})=\min(u_{\scriptscriptstyle 1},u_{\scriptscriptstyle 2})
for
u_{\scriptscriptstyle 1},u_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\in I
, which contains no tuning parameter. For the second- and third-level Hilbert spaces, we use the Gaussian radial basis function as the kernel. That is, suppose
\kappa:\mbox{\rsfsten H}\,\times\mbox{\rsfsten H}\,\to\mathbb{R}
is a generic positive kernel, where
H
can be a Euclidean space (as is the case for
\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}
) or an RKHS (as is the case for
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle i}
,
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle j}
,
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle(i,j)}
,
\kappa_{\scriptscriptstyle-(i,j)}
). For any
s_{\scriptscriptstyle 1},s_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\in\mbox{\rsfsten H}\,
, the Gaussian RBF is defined as

\displaystyle\kappa(s_{\scriptscriptstyle 1},s_{\scriptscriptstyle 2})=\exp(-\gamma\|s_{\scriptscriptstyle 1}-s_{\scriptscriptstyle 2}\|^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}),

where
\|\cdot\|
is the norm in
H
.
If
S_{\scriptscriptstyle 1},\ldots,S_{\scriptscriptstyle n}
are a sample of observations in
H
, then
\gamma
is chosen by

\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{{n\choose 2}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}{\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle a<b}\|S_{\scriptscriptstyle a}-S_{\scriptscriptstyle b}\|_{\scriptscriptstyle\mbox{\rsfstena H}}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}.

There are three types of Tychonoff regularization parameters:
\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle n}
in (
7
),
\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle n}^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}
in (
9
), and
\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle n}^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}
in (
10
). We use generalized cross validation (GCV) to determine these parameters. For
\eta_{\scriptscriptstyle n}
, we minimize the function

\displaystyle\mathrm{GCV}(\eta)=\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle i=1}^{\scriptscriptstyle p}\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle a=1}^{\scriptscriptstyle n}\frac{\|X_{\scriptscriptstyle a}^{\scriptscriptstyle i}(J_{\scriptscriptstyle a})-K_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(J_{\scriptscriptstyle a},J_{\scriptscriptstyle a})\{K_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(J_{\scriptscriptstyle a},J_{\scriptscriptstyle a})+\eta I_{\scriptscriptstyle m_{\scriptscriptstyle a}})^{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle-1}X_{\scriptscriptstyle a}^{\scriptscriptstyle i}(J_{\scriptscriptstyle a})\|_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{F}}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}{m_{\scriptscriptstyle a}^{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle-1}\mathrm{tr}\{I_{\scriptscriptstyle m_{\scriptscriptstyle a}}-K_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(J_{\scriptscriptstyle a},J_{\scriptscriptstyle a})(K_{\scriptscriptstyle T}(J_{\scriptscriptstyle a},J_{\scriptscriptstyle a})+\eta I_{\scriptscriptstyle m_{\scriptscriptstyle a}})^{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle-1}\}}.

For
\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle n}^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}
, we first reparameterize it as
\epsilon\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\max}(G_{\scriptscriptstyle-(i,j)})
, where
\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\max}
represents the largest eigenvalue. The reason for doing so is to bring the scale of the tuning parameter to the range of eigenvalues of the matrix being regularized. We then minimize the function

\displaystyle\mathrm{GCV}(\epsilon)=\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle i<j}^{\scriptscriptstyle p}\frac{\|G_{\scriptscriptstyle(i,j)}-G_{\scriptscriptstyle-(i,j)}\{G_{\scriptscriptstyle-(i,j)}+\epsilon\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\max}(G_{\scriptscriptstyle-(i,j)})I_{\scriptscriptstyle n}\}^{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle-1}G_{\scriptscriptstyle(i,j)}\|_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{F}}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}{n^{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle-1}\mathrm{tr}\{I_{\scriptscriptstyle n}-G_{\scriptscriptstyle-(i,j)}\{G_{\scriptscriptstyle-(i,j)}+\epsilon\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\max}(G_{\scriptscriptstyle-(i,j)})I_{\scriptscriptstyle n})^{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle-1}\}}.

For
\delta_{\scriptscriptstyle n}^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}
, we first reparameterize it as
\delta\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\max}(G_{\scriptscriptstyle-(i,j)})
, and then minimize the function

\displaystyle\mathrm{GCV}(\delta)=\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle i<j}^{\scriptscriptstyle p}\frac{\|G_{\scriptscriptstyle(i,j)}-H_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}\{H_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}+\delta\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\max}(H_{\scriptscriptstyle ij})I_{\scriptscriptstyle n}\}^{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle-1}G_{\scriptscriptstyle(i,j)}\|_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{F}}^{\scriptscriptstyle 2}}{n^{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle-1}\mathrm{tr}\{I_{\scriptscriptstyle n}-H_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}\{H_{\scriptscriptstyle ij}+\delta\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\max}(H_{\scriptscriptstyle ij})I_{\scriptscriptstyle n}\}^{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle-1}\}}.

The minimizations are carried out over grids of
\eta
,
\epsilon
, and
\delta
.

To determine the threshold
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle n}
in (
12
), similar to
17
and
12
, we use GCV criterion to find an appropriate value of
\rho
which minimizes prediction error. Let
\hat{{\mathsf{E}}}(\rho)
be the estimated edges based on the threshold
\rho
and
\mathcal{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle i}(\rho)=\{j\in{\mathsf{V}}|(i,j)\in\hat{{\mathsf{E}}}(\rho)\}
be the neighborhood of
i
-th node in the graph of
{\mathsf{G}}=({\mathsf{V}},\hat{{\mathsf{E}}}(\rho))
. Then, we decide
\rho
using GCV as

\displaystyle\text{GCV}(\rho)=\sum_{\scriptscriptstyle i=1}^{\scriptscriptstyle p}\frac{\|G_{\scriptscriptstyle X^{\scriptscriptstyle i}}-G_{\scriptscriptstyle C^{\scriptscriptstyle i}(\rho)}^{\mbox{\tiny{\sf T}}}[G_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle i}(\rho)}+\epsilon\hskip 1.42262pt\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\max}(G_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle i}(\rho)})I_{\scriptscriptstyle n}]^{\scriptscriptstyle-1}G_{\scriptscriptstyle X^{\scriptscriptstyle i}}\|_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{F}}}{\frac{1}{n}\text{tr}\{I_{\scriptscriptstyle n}-G_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle i}(\rho)}^{\mbox{\tiny{\sf T}}}[G_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle i}(\rho)}+\epsilon\hskip 1.42262pt\lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\max}(G_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle i}(\rho)})I_{\scriptscriptstyle n}]^{\scriptscriptstyle-1}\}},

(13)

where
G_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{N}^{\scriptscriptstyle i}(\rho)}
indicates a gram matrix from
X^{\scriptscriptstyle N^{\scriptscriptstyle i}(\rho)}
. We can use GCV based on the grid
\rho\in\{k\times 10^{\scriptscriptstyle-2}|k=1,\ldots,7\}
. The idea behind this method is applying GCV to residuals from the regression of the feature of each node
X^{\scriptscriptstyle i}
on the feature of its neighborhood nodes
X^{\scriptscriptstyle N^{\scriptscriptstyle i}(\rho)}
.

6
Simulation comparisons with existing methods

In this section we compare our two-step f-SGM estimator with several existing estimators of the functional graphical model, including the functional Gaussian graphical model (FGGM) of
26
, the nonparametric additive estimator based on the functional additive precision operator (FAPO) proposed in
18

17

For a comprehensive comparison, we include a broad spectrum of models that favor each of the above methods. In particular, Models I and II are nonlinear and additive models that favor FAPO, which is additive in nature. Model III contains heteroscedasticity that cannot be captured by either the Gaussian method or the additive method, but can be captured by our method. Model IV is the Gaussian model that favors FGGM. We then repeat models I, III, and IV to increase the dimension

. We generated undirected functional graphical models by first constructing structural equations, and then removing arrows and joining parents (if any). In all simulations, we use the Brownian motion covariance kernel to construct the first-level Hilbert spaces, and use the Gaussian RBF for the second- and third-level Hilbert spaces. The tuning parameters are determined according to Section

. The grids for
\eta

\epsilon
, and
\delta
for their minimization processes are taken to be
\{3\times 10^{\scriptscriptstyle b}:b=0,\ldots 5\}

7.1
f-MRI dataset

We now apply our method to an f-MRI human brain imaging dataset in the ADHD consortium
(
25
)
, which is released by Neuro Bureau, a neuroscience collaboratory that supports open neuroscience research. The version we used is preprocessed by the Athena pipeline. This dataset is available at

https://www.nitrc.org/frs/?group_id=383.

The dataset consists of observations from
171
children, which are divided into an ADHD group of
73
children and a control group of
98
children. A brain is divided into
116
regions of interest using the anatomical labelling atlas developed by
3
. For each child and each brain region, the f-MRI data are observed at
172
equally spaced time points. The f-MRI data values are extracted by averaging over all voxels within each region at each time point. Our goal of the analysis is to find the difference in the brain network structure between children with and without having ADHD. We applied f-SGM, FAPO, FGGM, and n-SGM to this dataset.

Due to the high computational cost of FGGM for high dimensional networks, we use a thresholding version of FGGM which is described in
18
. First, we calculate a precision matrix based on the functional principal component. Then we determine the connectivity by thresholding the operator norm of
(i,j)
th block of the precision matrix. We choose the threshold so that
7\%
of the pairs of vertices are edges. In particular, the selected threshold is 0.03 for both ADHD and control groups. For the f-SGM and FAPO methods we use the Brownian motion covariance kernel for the first-level spaces and the Gaussian radial basis kernel for the second-level spaces. For the tuning parameters we use the methods that have been discussed in Section
5.7
. We also use the Gaussian radial basis kernel for the first-level spaces and cannot find any significant difference.

8
Discussion

This paper incorporates the recently developed techniques of nonlinear SDR for functional data to construct a flexible nonparametric estimator of the functional graphical model. Our method does not rely on any distribution assumption and, as our simulation experiments indicate, effectively avoids the curse of dimensionality that often hampers a fully nonparametric method.

The current method is an extension of the sufficient graphical model of
17
from the multivariate setting to the multivariate functional setting, which is increasingly common in modern applications, particularly in brain neurological research.
The novelty in this extension lies in the use of the first-level Hilbert spaces that accommodates functional data, and the hybrid conjoined conditional covariance operator, by which we determine absence of edge. In the sufficient graphical model of
17
, the statistical dependence captured through the RKHS defined on the random variables in the vertices; whereas for the current f-SGM the statistical dependence is captured by the RKHS defined on the first-level functional spaces in which the observations on the vertices reside. The RKHS is supported on the first-level Hilbert spaces with its kernel constructed from the inner products of the first-level spaces; nonlinear SDR is then performed on the RKHS (the second-level Hilbert space). After extracting the sufficient predictors
U^{\scriptscriptstyle ij}
using f-GSIR, which is a Euclidean vector, the hybrid CCCO is then introduced to determine conditional independence. This hybrid operator involves two Hilbertian random functions and a Euclidean random vector. In comparison, the CCCO used in the sufficient graphical model of
17
involves to real-valued random variables and a random vector. The implementing algorithms of
17
also need to be modified to adapt to these novel structures.

The algorithms of the f-SGM are reasonably easy to implement. Essentially, we use the function GSIR as a module, and apply it repeatedly to each distinct pair vertices, which each repetition involving an eigendecomposition of an
n
by
n
matrix. The algorithms are easily parallelized for larger networks, as the computation the f-GSIR predictors and the subsequent hybrid CCCO for different pairs of vertices are independent of each other and can be distributed among a large number of different computing units. Thus, it is feasible for computing relatively large networks.

While the current paper focuses on developing the methodology as well as the theoretical structure at the population level, we haven’t touched on the asymptotic developments, such as estimation consistency, convergence rates, optimal tuning, order determination, and statistical inference. We leave these to future research.
