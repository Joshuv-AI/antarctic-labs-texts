Association between serum sodium and the risk of sepsis-related liver injury: a cross-sectional study based on the MIMIC-IV database
Authors: Guiyun Li, Yixu Lin, Di Ren, Yanhong Chen, Sha Wen, Xiaomin Liang, Lin Zhang, Jiang Mei, Yongwen Feng, Shuiqing Gui, Ying Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSepsis-related liver injury (SRLI) is a common complication of sepsis, yet its mechanisms are unclear and the association between serum sodium (SNa) and SRLI has been rarely investigated.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study utilized data from the MIMIC-IV database, focusing on adult intensive care unit (ICU) patients diagnosed with sepsis. The primary outcome was SRLI. SNa levels were categorized into hyponatremia, normonatremia, and hypernatremia groups based on clinical cutoffs for statistical analysis. Binary logistic regression was used to assess the independent association between SNa and SRLI, and the Generalized Additive Model (GAM) was applied to verify potential nonlinear associations. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to ensure the robustness of the results, and subgroup analyses were performed.ResultsAmong 11,809 adult patients with sepsis (57.2% male; mean age 65.8 ± 17.1 years), the prevalence of SRLI was 42.5%. Multivariable binary logistic regression showed that SNa was inversely associated with SRLI (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 0.99; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.98–0.99; P < 0.05). GAM analysis indicated a nonlinear association with a saturation effect between SNa and SRLI, with a threshold at 135 mmol/L. At SNa ≤ 135 mmol/L, SNa was inversely associated with SRLI, whereas the association was not statistically significant at SNa > 135 mmol/L. Subgroup analyses were generally consistent, with a stronger inverse association observed among patients with cirrhosis.ConclusionIn critically ill adults with sepsis, SNa showed a nonlinear inverse association with SRLI, with a threshold around 135 mmol/L. Incorporating sodium into clinical assessment and stratification may be informative; multicenter prospective cohorts are warranted to confirm clinical utility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12330-2.

Background

Sepsis is a life-threatening infection that triggers acute organ dysfunction due to an excessive immune response [1, 2]. It is a major reason for hospital admissions and readmissions to intensive care units, with an estimated 30 million people affected annually, resulting in around 6 million fatalities [3]. Liver injury, a common complication of sepsis, is a component of multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) in these patients [4]. The prevalence of liver dysfunction in sepsis patients ranges from 34% to 46%, according to various reports [5, 6]. Early liver dysfunction, especially if it deteriorates or persists within the first 72 h of severe sepsis, is a robust indicator of adverse outcomes and has been recognized as an independent predictor of poor prognosis [6–8]. Moreover, interventions that mitigate liver damage and enhance liver function have been shown to lower both morbidity and mortality in patients with sepsis, underscoring the liver’s vital role in sepsis outcomes [5, 9].

Despite the acknowledged importance of managing Sepsis-Related Liver Injury (SRLI), standardized diagnostic criteria remain undefined. Common markers for liver damage include elevated levels of bilirubin (TBIL), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT). The thresholds for diagnosis are set at bilirubin levels above 34 µmol/L (2 mg/dL) or ALT and AST levels more than twice the upper limit of normal [6]. These benchmarks, widely accepted in China, were adopted for this study. The underlying mechanisms of SRLI involve inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, microcirculatory and coagulation dysfunction, bacterial translocation, coagulation abnormalities, and apoptosis regulation [1, 8, 10]. Prior research has highlighted the potential of downregulating CUL3 expression to alleviate acute liver damage in sepsis [11] and the protective role of vitamin D-binding protein in reducing oxidative stress and deactivating the JNK signaling pathway [12].

Sodium (Na) plays a critical role in maintaining fluid and electrolyte homeostasis [13, 14]. Sodium metabolism disorders, particularly hypernatremia, are prevalent in intensive care settings, with incidences ranging from 6% to 42% [15, 16]. serum sodium (SNa) levels are also associated with the immune system [17, 18]. Studies have shown a positive correlation between elevated SNa levels and sepsis, highlighting the need to investigate infection sources in cases of hypernatremia upon admission [19]. In elderly emergency patients, abnormal SNa levels are associated with a higher risk of hospitalization and death [20, 21]. Moreover, serum osmolarity disturbances, such as hypernatremia, are associated with adverse health outcomes, including cardiovascular, cerebral, respiratory, renal disorders, and increased mortality [22–24]. Interestingly, high salt intake has been shown to negatively affect fat deposition while offering protective effects in conditions such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [25, 26]. Conversely, chronic mild hyponatremia has been linked to several health issues including gait disturbances [27], cognitive impairments [28], bone demineralization [29], and an increased risk of developing tumors [30, 31]. Hyponatremia is linked to a poor prognosis in patients with a variety of diseases, such as liver, kidney, heart, lung, and tumor diseases [21, 30–36].

However, research on the association between SNa and SRLI remains limited. This retrospective cross-sectional study used the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC)-IV database to investigate the correlation between SNa levels and the risk of SRLI in ICU patients.

Methods

Design and setting

We conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study using de-identified data from the MIMIC-IV database (version 2.2; 2008–2019), curated at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. Access was granted after completion of the required credentialing, and investigator Yixu Lin retrieved the data (Record ID 48,316,675). Because MIMIC-IV is fully de-identified, informed consent was waived and ethics approval was exempted. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. This was not a clinical trial; therefore, a trial registration number does not apply. The study adheres to STROBE guidelines for cross-sectional studies; the participant selection flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1, and the completed STROBE checklist is provided in the Supplement.

Flowchart of study participants

Study population

We identified adult ICU patients (≥ 18 years) with sepsis according to the Sepsis-3 definition (suspected/confirmed infection with an increase in SOFA score ≥ 2 [2]). For patients with multiple ICU admissions, only the first ICU stay was included. We excluded patients with pre-existing liver diseases (active hepatitis, fatty liver, autoimmune liver disease, or liver cancer), acute gallbladder/biliary diseases, or missing key variables (ALT, AST, TBIL, or SNa). Liver cirrhosis was not an exclusion criterion; cirrhosis status was identified from diagnostic codes and clinical documentation to allow subgroup analyses. After screening 22,500 patients, 11,809 met the eligibility criteria for the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Variables

SNa values were extracted from the first 24 h of the index ICU admission.

Outcome measures

SRLI was defined as TBIL ≥ 2 mg/dL or ALT/AST ≥ 80 U/L [6]. This definition follows common practices in China, as no universal standard for SRLI diagnosis exists.

Covariates

Potential confounders were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior evidence: age, gender, ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), blood lactate (Lac), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), neutrophil count (NEUT), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), platelet count (PLT), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR; 2021 CKD-EPI, race-free), infectious-agent category, and cirrhosis. For all enrolled patients, laboratory and physiological covariates were abstracted from ICU day 1 only.

Statistical analysis

Participants were categorized into three groups based on SNa levels: hyponatremia (< 135 mmol/L), normal sodium (135–145 mmol/L), and hypernatremia (> 145 mmol/L). Descriptive statistics for continuous variables (mean, standard deviation [SD], median, and interquartile range [IQR]) were calculated, while categorical variables were presented as percentages. Group differences were assessed using the Chi-square test for categorical variables, one-way ANOVA for normally distributed continuous variables, and the Kruskal-Wallis test for non-normal distributions.

The primary analysis used multivariable binary logistic regression to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between SNa and SRLI, with sequential adjustment for covariates (Model 0: unadjusted; Model I: age and gender; Model II: age, gender, Lac, PLT, LDH, NEUT, BUN, BMI, eGFR, cirrhosis, infectious agent category, and ethnicity). To assess potential nonlinearity on the logit scale, we fitted generalized additive models with penalized splines for SNa. For descriptive purposes, trend tests across SNa categories used category medians.

Hierarchical interaction analysis was used to assess the stability of the results across subgroups. Findings are displayed as Model II stratum-specific adjusted ORs (95% CIs) together with p for interaction in a forest plot. We used median imputation for PLT and BUN due to low missingness (< 1%) and applied missing-indicator methods for other covariates [37] (Additional file 1: Table A1). Statistical analyses were performed using R (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation) and EmpowerStats (http://www.empowerstats.com, X&Y Solutions, Inc, Boston, MA). All tests were two-sided with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics and SRLI prevalence

Table 1 summarizes demographics and clinical characteristics of 11,809 adults (male 57.2%; mean age 65.8 ± 17.1 years; mean SNa 140.3 ± 5.8 mmol/L; mean BMI 29.0 ± 7.7 kg/m²). SRLI prevalence was 42.5% (5,017/11,809). By clinical SNa categories, SRLI prevalence was 52.1% in hyponatremia, 40.4% in normonatremia, and 46.0% in hypernatremia (χ² p < 0.001). Compared with normonatremia, the hyponatremia group was younger with lower PLT, higher BMI, a higher prevalence of cirrhosis, and a greater proportion of White patients; Lac and BUN were higher than in normonatremia but lower than in hypernatremia (all p < 0.05). Consistently, hyponatremia was more frequent in the SRLI group than in the non-SRLI group (Additional file 2 Fig. 1). Univariable associations are provided in Additional file 3 (Table A2) and were directionally consistent with clinical expectations.

Association of SNa with SRLI: primary, categorical, and shape analyses

Using multivariable binary logistic regression, higher SNa was associated with lower odds of SRLI (per 1 mmol/L increase: OR 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98–0.99 in Model II; similar in the crude model and Model I; Table 3). When SNa was analyzed categorically with hyponatremia as reference, adjusted ORs were 0.76 (95% CI 0.67–0.87) for normonatremia and 0.75 (95% CI 0.63–0.89) for hypernatremia; p for trend < 0.05 based on category medians (Table 3). A generalized additive model (GAM) with penalized splines indicated a nonlinear association (Fig. 2). A two-piecewise model with an inflection at 135 mmol/L fit better (LRT p = 0.040): at ≤ 135 mmol/L, each 1 mmol/L higher SNa was associated with lower odds (OR 0.96, 95% CI 0.94–0.99; p = 0.0027), whereas at > 135 mmol/L the association was small and not statistically significant (OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.98–1.00; p = 0.0699) (Table 3).

Stratified and interaction analyses

Stratified and interaction analyses. To assess effect modification of the SNa–SRLI association, we conducted stratified analyses and included multiplicative interaction terms in the fully adjusted model (Model II). Across strata of gender, age, ethnicity, BUN, PLT, eGFR, infectious-agent category, BMI, and lactate, interaction tests were non-significant (all P for interaction ≥ 0.05), and the inverse association was directionally consistent and of similar magnitude. In contrast, among patients with cirrhosis, the inverse association was stronger than in those without (per 1 mmol/L increase in SNa, OR = 0.96, 95% CI, 0.94–0.98; P for interaction < 0.05) (see Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this retrospective cross-sectional study of 11,809 septic patients, we observed a nonlinear association between SNa and SRLI odds, with an inflection at 135 mmol/L. Below this threshold, each 1 mmol/L higher SNa was associated with 4% lower odds of SRLI (OR 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–0.99). Above 135 mmol/L, the association was small and not statistically significant (OR 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98–1.00; p = 0.0699).These patterns suggest that SNa may be a candidate biomarker for SRLI odds stratification in sepsis.

SRLI pathogenesis is multifactorial, involving bile stasis, hepatocellular injury, and systemic inflammatory responses. In the absence of a universally accepted definition, we used bilirubin and liver enzyme criteria and observed an SRLI prevalence of 42.5%, consistent with prior reports [5, 6]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report a nonlinear SNa–SRLI relationship in sepsis, adding nuance to the understanding of sodium homeostasis in hepatic dysfunction.

Above 135 mmol/L, the association was near-null and not statistically significant (see Table 3). This attenuation likely reflects concomitant risk profiles: compared with SNa < 135 mmol/L, patients with SNa ≥ 135 mmol/L were older with higher platelet counts but lower BUN, BMI, and lactate (Table A2, Supplementary File 3). Prior studies implicate BUN, BMI, lactate, age, and thrombocytopenia as independent SRLI determinants [38–46]. Elevated SNa has also been linked to insulin resistance and metabolic dysregulation—impaired glycogen synthesis and reduced lactate clearance—that may burden the liver [47, 48]. Collectively, these factors may overshadow any marginal SNa effect at higher levels, whereas sodium imbalance is more salient in hyponatremia. Subgroup results were broadly consistent across gender, age, renal function, race, and lactate strata; the inverse association appeared stronger in cirrhosis, aligning with reports that lower SNa tracks with worse hepatic function, more complications, and poorer prognosis in cirrhosis [49–55].

Our findings support the potential utility of routine SNa monitoring for prognostic assessment in sepsis and suggest that timely identification of hyponatremia may help flag patients with higher odds of SRLI, particularly in adults with cirrhotic. Given the observational design, these results should be interpreted as associative; interventional studies are needed to determine whether sodium correction improves hepatic outcomes.

Compared with a prior single-center Chinese study that developed an SRLI model without evaluating SNa [39], our analysis complements the literature by pointing to SNa as a candidate risk marker, particularly in the hyponatremic range. The potential links between SNa and SRLI should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than causal. First, immune–inflammatory modulation: preclinical injury models suggest hypertonic saline can reduce inflammatory mediators and oxidative stress [56], and clinical observations associate hypernatremia with lower cytokine levels (e.g., G-CSF, TNF-α) implicated in SRLI pathways [18, 57–59]. Second, hemodynamics: in portal hypertension, hyponatremia correlates with worse liver function and prognosis, consistent with possible synergistic effects on hepatic injury [60]; neurohumoral activation (RAAS) and vasodilation may contribute to fibrosis and dysfunction [61]. Third, metabolic signaling: higher sodium exposure has been linked to mineralocorticoid/aldosterone pathway activation, hepatic lipogenesis, steatosis, and inflammation [26]. These mechanisms derive from heterogeneous evidence (animal and observational, often non-ICU) and warrant targeted mechanistic studies in sepsis populations.

Several strengths of this study enhance its significance. First, the large sample size provides robust statistical power. Second, to our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the nonlinear association between SNa levels and SRLI in septic patients. Third, we employed rigorous statistical adjustments and sensitivity analyses, including variable transformations and subgroup analyses, to validate our findings. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional study design precludes causal inference. Future research could utilize Mendelian randomization or prospective cohort studies to elucidate causality. Second, our findings are derived from ICU patients in the United States, and their generalizability to other populations or non-ICU settings remains uncertain. Expanding data collection to international cohorts could strengthen external validity. Third, while we focused on SRLI prevalence, future studies should investigate the relationship between SNa levels and SRLI severity or prognosis. Finally, despite our efforts to adjust for key confounders, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded, particularly in relation to factors such as pathogen type and inflammatory biomarkers, including C-reactive protein (CRP), neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), ferritin, procalcitonin, and interleukin-6 (IL-6), which were not consistently available in our dataset. Further research is warranted to elucidate the precise mechanisms linking SNa dysregulation to SRLI and to optimize sodium management strategies in septic patients, potentially integrating inflammatory and metabolic markers to refine risk stratification and therapeutic approaches.

Conclusion

In critically ill adults with sepsis, SNa showed a nonlinear inverse association with SRLI, with a threshold around 135 mmol/L. Incorporating sodium into clinical assessment and stratification may be informative; multicenter prospective cohorts are warranted to confirm clinical utility.
