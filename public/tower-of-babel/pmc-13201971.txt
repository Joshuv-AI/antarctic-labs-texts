Occipital transtentorial approach selected based on venous anatomy for a vermian cavernous malformation: illustrative case
Authors: Mizuto Sato, Akimitsu Shirokihara, Natsumi Ogawa, Takaki Ichikawa, Takahiro Miyata, Masashi Nakatsukasa, Joji Inamasu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Cavernous malformations account for approximately 5%–15% of all vascular malformations of the CNS and may occur throughout the brain and spinal cord.1–3 Although infratentorial cavernous malformations are less common than supratentorial lesions, posterior fossa lesions pose distinctive surgical challenges.2–4 Lesions located on the superior surface of the cerebellar vermis immediately beneath the tentorium are particularly demanding because of their deep midline location and their relationship to critical venous structures. In posterior fossa surgery, venous anatomy may be a major determinant of surgical risk. Persistent embryonic venous channels, including the occipital and marginal sinuses, may remain prominent in adults, particularly in the setting of transverse sinus hypoplasia.5–8 Injury to these venous structures during a midline posterior fossa approach can result in severe venous congestion and hemorrhagic complications.5–8 Here, we report a case of hemorrhagic cavernous malformation of the superior cerebellar vermis in which detailed preoperative evaluation of venous anatomy guided selection of an occipital interhemispheric transtentorial approach.

Illustrative Case

A 69-year-old man with a history of hypertension and prostate cancer presented with sudden-onset vertigo, nausea, and truncal ataxia. Noncontrast head CT revealed a hemorrhagic lesion approximately 20 mm in diameter within the cerebellar vermis (Fig. 1A). MRI demonstrated marked blooming on T2*-weighted images and faint capsular enhancement on gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted images (Fig. 1B–D), consistent with a cavernous malformation located immediately beneath the tentorium. Repeated CT scans obtained during the first 5 days after admission consistently demonstrated hematoma enlargement, accompanied by progressive worsening of vertigo, nausea, and truncal ataxia.

Preoperative imaging findings of a cavernous malformation of the superior cerebellar vermis. A: Noncontrast axial CT scan showing a hemorrhagic lesion within the cerebellar vermis. B: Axial T2*-weighted MR image demonstrating marked blooming consistent with hemosiderin deposition. C: Axial gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted MR image showing faint capsular enhancement. D: Sagittal gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted MR image demonstrating a lesion located on the superior surface of the cerebellar vermis immediately beneath the tentorium.

As part of the preoperative assessment for approach selection, CT venography was performed to evaluate the venous anatomy surrounding the lesion, including the occipital bridging veins and major dural venous sinuses. This study demonstrated bilateral hypoplastic transverse sinuses with a dominant occipital sinus and well-developed bilateral marginal sinuses (Fig. 2), indicating that these structures constituted the primary posterior fossa venous outflow pathways. In addition, no major occipital bridging vein was identified along the planned operative corridor. Because of repeated hematoma enlargement, progressive symptom worsening, and the risk of sudden deterioration associated with a posterior fossa lesion, early surgical intervention was considered appropriate. Given the high risk of venous injury associated with a midline posterior fossa approach, an occipital interhemispheric transtentorial approach was selected.

Preoperative CT venograms demonstrating atypical posterior fossa venous anatomy. A: Posterior view of a 3D reconstructed CT venogram showing bilateral hypoplastic transverse sinuses (arrowheads) and a dominant occipital sinus (arrows) with well-developed marginal sinuses. B: Lateral view demonstrating hypoplastic transverse sinuses and prominent venous drainage through the occipital sinus.

Surgery was performed in the prone position with a left occipital craniotomy. Ventricular drainage via left posterior horn puncture was performed to relax the brain and avoid excessive occipital lobe compression during the interhemispheric exposure. Before the tentorial incision was made, intraoperative indocyanine green videoangiography was used to confirm the absence of a major tentorial sinus along the intended incision line (Fig. 3). A limited tentoriotomy then provided direct access to the lesion, allowing evacuation of the hematoma and piecemeal resection of the cavernous malformation (Video 1).

Intraoperative findings during the occipital interhemispheric transtentorial approach. A: Microscopic view of the surgical field demonstrating the planned tentorial incision line. B: Intraoperative indocyanine green videoangiogram confirming the absence of a major tentorial sinus along the planned incision line. C: Operative field after tentorial incision showing a direct surgical corridor to the lesion on the superior surface of the cerebellar vermis with minimal parenchymal disruption.

Postoperative CT and MRI confirmed complete resection without residual hemorrhage or new ischemic lesions (Fig. 4). Formal visual field testing was not performed, but no new visual field deficit was identified on postoperative clinical assessment. The patient’s symptoms improved rapidly, and he was discharged home ambulatory with a modified Rankin Scale score of 1.

Discussion

Observations

This case underscores the importance of detailed venous anatomical assessment when planning surgery for superior vermian cavernous malformations. Preoperative CT venography demonstrated bilateral hypoplastic transverse sinuses with dominant occipital and marginal sinuses, indicating that these persistent embryonic channels served as the primary posterior fossa venous drainage pathways in this patient.5–8 Embryologically, the occipital and marginal sinuses are developmental venous pathways during early posterior fossa maturation; in some patients, particularly those with transverse sinus hypoplasia, these channels may persist as functionally important outflow routes.5–8

In our practice, preoperative CT venography is routinely obtained for lesions in this region when venous anatomy may influence approach selection. The purpose is to evaluate not only the vascular anatomy surrounding the lesion, but also the occipital bridging veins and major dural venous sinuses that may constrain the surgical corridor. In the present case, this routine preoperative assessment revealed a venous configuration that had a direct impact on surgical planning.

Although surgery for cavernous malformations is often deferred to the subacute stage after hemorrhage, earlier intervention was considered justified in this case because repeated imaging demonstrated progressive hematoma enlargement, the patient’s symptoms worsened accordingly, and the posterior fossa location raised concern for abrupt neurological deterioration.9

Alternative routes to the superior vermis include a midline suboccipital exposure, including telovelar corridors, and a suboccipital transtentorial approach. In the present venous configuration, however, a midline posterior fossa route was considered less favorable because it increased the likelihood of encountering functionally dominant occipital or marginal sinuses and their venous connections. Injury to these structures could result in impaired venous outflow, intraoperative venous bleeding, venous congestion, and postoperative venous infarction. In addition, for a centrally located superior vermian lesion, a midline suboccipital route may require greater cerebellar cortical transgression. A suboccipital transtentorial route was also considered less suitable in this case because the working angle was less favorable for obtaining a direct trajectory to the lesion.10–13

By contrast, the occipital interhemispheric transtentorial approach provided the most direct route to the superior vermian surface while avoiding the dominant midline posterior fossa venous drainage pathways.14 This approach is established primarily through the interhemispheric fissure and, in our view, is less constrained by tentorial configuration than suboccipital transtentorial routes.14 In the present case, tentorial configuration was not a limiting factor for exposure.

Several technical points were important. First, preoperative CT venography was used to confirm that no major occipital bridging vein was present along the planned operative corridor. Second, before the tentorial incision was made, intraoperative indocyanine green videoangiography was used to confirm the absence of a major tentorial sinus along the intended incision line, after which a limited tentoriotomy was performed.15 In addition, occipital lobe retraction was minimized by gravity-assisted interhemispheric exposure to avoid occipital lobe symptoms. These measures allowed safe access to the lesion.

Formal visual field testing was not performed in this case. However, no new visual field deficit was identified on postoperative clinical assessment.

Lessons

For superior vermian lesions, patient-specific venous drainage patterns may be a decisive factor in approach selection. When dominant occipital or marginal sinuses are present, these structures should be regarded as critical venous outflow pathways that must be preserved. In such cases, the occipital interhemispheric transtentorial approach can provide a direct and vein-sparing route to the lesion.
