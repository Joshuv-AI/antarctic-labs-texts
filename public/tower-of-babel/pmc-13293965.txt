Dual-system engineered bacteria and dendritic cells enable precise intratumoral IL-12 delivery and potent antitumor immunity
Authors: Yuan Deng, Heng Liang, Arabella H. Wan, Chuwei Liu, Shijia Yan, Jiarui Li, Min Xiao, Qiusheng Zhou, Ruonian Liu, Zitong Zhang, Heteng Zhang, Huiya Cheng, Chengyuan Fu, Qijun Yao, Juan Fang, Silke Neumann, Qiaoping Wang, Jun Huang, Zhi Wang, Guohui Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryInterleukin-12 (IL-12) potently stimulates antitumor immunity, but its clinical use is limited by systemic toxicity and poor spatial control. Here, we develop a dual-engineered delivery system that combines Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) programmed for hypoxia- and quorum-sensing-regulated IL-12 expression with engineered dendritic cells (eDCs) that facilitate bacterial delivery and immune support. In murine colorectal cancer and liver metastasis models, eDC-EcN-IL12-QS preferentially localizes to tumors, increases intratumoral IL-12 and IFN- γ levels, enhances dendritic cell activation and CD8+ T cell infiltration, and suppresses tumor growth and metastatic burden. The system also prolongs survival and remains controllable by antibiotic-mediated clearance. These findings support a programmable microbial-cellular strategy for localized cytokine delivery and immune activation in immunologically cold tumors.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with limited therapeutic options for patients who fail conventional chemotherapy or targeted therapy.1 Although immune checkpoint blockade has achieved durable clinical responses in a subset of cancers, its efficacy in CRC, particularly in microsatellite-stable (MSS) tumors, has been largely disappointing.2,3 The primary barrier is the profoundly immunosuppressive and immunologically “cold” tumor microenvironment (TME), characterized by poor T cell infiltration, defective antigen presentation, and dominant suppressive myeloid programs that collectively constrain effective antitumor immunity.4,5

Interleukin-12 (IL-12) is a central orchestrator of antitumor immunity, capable of bridging innate and adaptive immune responses by promoting dendritic cell (DC) maturation, Th1 polarization, cytotoxic CD8+ T cell activation, and sustained IFN-γ production.6,7,8,9 Preclinical studies have consistently demonstrated the ability of IL-12 to convert immune-desert tumors into inflamed, T cell-rich lesions. However, clinical translation of IL-12 has been severely limited by its narrow therapeutic window. Systemic administration induces dose-limiting toxicities, including hepatotoxicity and cytokine release syndromes, while achieving insufficient and poorly controlled intratumoral exposure.10,11 These challenges underscore a fundamental paradox: IL-12 is immunologically indispensable, yet pharmacologically unmanageable when delivered systemically.

Recent strategies have sought to overcome this limitation through localized cytokine delivery, including intratumoral plasmid electroporation, mRNA-based approaches, and viral vectors encoding IL-12 or related cytokines.12,13 While these methods partially improve spatial confinement, they remain constrained by transient expression, limited penetrance in metastatic disease, and an inability to dynamically adapt cytokine output to tumor-intrinsic cues. Moreover, most current platforms decouple cytokine delivery from real-time immune activation states within the TME, thereby limiting their capacity to sustain productive antitumor responses.

Emerging insights into tumor immunology suggest that durable tumor control requires not only transient immune activation but also temporal coordination between early cytokine signaling and long-lived T cell programs. In particular, IL-12-driven IFN-γ signaling has been implicated in establishing tissue-resident memory T cells (TRMs), a population increasingly recognized as a critical determinant of long-term tumor surveillance and immune persistence.14 In parallel, engineered probiotics and microbial therapeutics have demonstrated a unique capacity to preferentially colonize tumors and reshape local immune landscapes.15,16,17 However, existing microbial delivery systems primarily rely on static payload expression and lack programmable control over cytokine amplitude or timing, limiting both safety and efficacy.

These limitations highlight a central unmet need: a delivery paradigm capable of synchronizing spatially restricted cytokine release with immune activation and persistence, while maintaining systemic safety. To address this challenge, we developed a dual-engineered immunotherapeutic platform that integrates living microbial vectors with genetically modified DCs. In this system, engineered Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) serves as a tumor-localized cytokine amplifier, while engineered DCs (eDCs) provide targeted delivery and costimulatory immune support. By combining density- and hypoxia-responsive bacterial cytokine production18,19 with DC-mediated antigen presentation20 and T cell costimulation,21 this platform enables feedback-controlled, intratumoral IL-12 delivery aligned with the evolving immune context of the tumor.22 Here, we demonstrate that this dual-cellular strategy effectively remodels the immunosuppressive CRC microenvironment, suppresses primary tumor growth and liver metastasis, and elicits robust local and systemic antitumor immunity without inducing systemic toxicity. Together, our findings support a programmable microbial-immune interface for spatially restricted IL-12 delivery and immune activation in immune-desert tumors.

Result

Constructing engineered bacteria EcN-IL-12 enables tumor-restricted IL-12 delivery with minimal systemic toxicity

IL-12 is a heterodimeric cytokine composed of IL12A (p35) and IL12B (p40) subunits, orchestrating Th1-type immune responses, DC activation, and CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity.6 Analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas datasets revealed that low expression of either IL12A or IL12B is significantly correlated with poorer clinical outcomes in CRC, including among patients undergoing immune checkpoint blockade therapy (Figures S1A–S1D). Pan-cancer comparison further demonstrated that IL12A expression is broadly reduced in tumors relative to matched normal tissues (Figure S1E), underscoring its role as a potentially suppressed immunostimulatory cytokine across cancers.

To harness the potent antitumor activity of IL-12 while limiting systemic exposure, we engineered the probiotic strain EcN to conditionally express IL-12 in hypoxic TMEs, aiming to achieve spatially restricted cytokine delivery. To this end, the hypoxia-inducible pvhb promoter was fused to the PelB signal peptide and placed upstream of the IL-12 coding sequence. The pvhb promoter, regulated by the Fnr and ArcAB systems, exhibits maximal activity under low-oxygen conditions (∼1%–2% O2), while remaining minimally active under normoxic or fully anaerobic conditions, thereby confining IL-12 expression to hypoxic tumor regions.19 PelB was included to facilitate periplasmic translocation and oxidative folding, a prerequisite for correct disulfide bond formation and IL-12 bioactivity.23

Using λ-Red recombination, the pvhb-PelB-IL12 cassette was inserted into the lacZ locus of EcN, generating the recombinant strain EcN-IL12 (Figure 1A). Under hypoxic culture conditions, IL-12 protein was predominantly detected in the bacterial pellet fraction, as assessed by Coomassie staining and western blot analysis. Reducing SDS-PAGE revealed a band at approximately 70 kDa, while non-reducing conditions showed a higher-molecular-weight species consistent with disulfide-linked IL-12 complexes (Figures 1B and S2A–S2C). DnaK served as a loading control. To determine whether bacterially produced IL-12 retained biological activity, OT-I CD8+ T cells were co-cultured with OVA-expressing MC38 tumor cells in the presence of either recombinant IL-12 or lysates derived from EcN-IL12. Both conditions resulted in a significant increase in CD8+ T cell-mediated tumor cytotoxicity at 24 and 48 h (Figures 1E and 1F), demonstrating that IL-12 produced by engineered EcN remains functionally in vitro.

We next evaluated the biodistribution, safety profile, and antitumor efficacy of EcN-IL12 in vivo. Following intravenous administration of EcN-IL12 (1 × 106 colony-forming units [CFUs]) into MC38 tumor-bearing mice, bacterial colonization was strongly enriched within tumor tissues, with only transient and low-level recovery observed in the spleen and minimal detection in the liver or kidney (Figures 1G–1I). By day 7 post-injection, bacterial burden was largely restricted to the tumor site, indicating preferential persistence within the TME. Importantly, EcN-IL12-treated mice maintained stable body weight and exhibited no treatment-associated mortality throughout the observation period, in contrast to mice receiving recombinant IL-12 protein, which displayed rapid weight loss and substantial lethality consistent with systemic cytokine toxicity (Figures 1J and 1K). Histopathological examination of major organs, including the heart, lung, liver, spleen, and kidney, revealed no overt tissue damage in the EcN-IL12-treated group (Figure 1O). While mild splenic architectural alternations were observed in IL-12-treated cohorts, no evidence of overt organ toxicity was detected. Consistent with its tumor-restricted activity, EcN-IL-12 treatment significantly reduced tumor growth, volume, and weight compared with EcN control (Figures 1L–1N). Collectively, these results demonstrate that engineered EcN-IL-12 enables localized, biologically active IL-12 delivery within tumors, achieving robust antitumor efficacy, while minimizing systemic toxicity.

Engineered bacteria inhibit growth of immunologically cold tumors and promote local immune activation

Tumor-bearing mice were treated with PBS, wild-type EcN, recombinant IL-12, or EcN-IL12. Tumor growth was monitored longitudinally following treatment initiation. During the early phase of tumor progression, growth kinetics was comparable among treatment groups, reflecting the relatively slow growth rate of CT26 tumors. At later time points, EcN-IL12-treated tumors exhibited a marked reduction in growth compared with PBS and EcN controls, as indicated by tumor growth curves, endpoint tumor volume, and tumor weights (Figures 2C–2E). Recombinant IL-12 also achieved strong tumor suppression, whereas EcN-IL12 produced a comparable antitumor effect without detectable systemic toxicity, highlighting the therapeutic advantage of tumor-restricted cytokine delivery in this immune-refractory setting.

To determine whether tumor control correlated with local immune activation, we next examined immune cell infiltration within tumor tissues. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed a significant increase in intratumoral CD8+ T cell density following EcN-IL12 treatment, reaching levels comparable to those induced by systemic IL-12 administration (Figures 2F and 2G). CD4+ T cell infiltration was also increased, although to a lesser extent than CD8+ T cells (Figures 2H and 2I), consistent with a predominantly cytotoxic, Th1-biased immune response elicited by IL-12.

We next evaluated tumor cell apoptosis by staining for cleaved caspase-3. EcN-IL12 treatment resulted in a significant increase in caspase-3-positive cells within tumor tissues, indicating enhanced tumor cell apoptosis (Figures 2J and 2K). Compared with PBS and EcN controls, both EcN-IL12 and recombinant IL-12 groups showed elevated apoptotic indices, consistent with IL-12-driven cytotoxic immune activity.

To further characterize the cytokine milieu associated with IL-12-mediated tumor control, we first analyzed human CRC transcriptomic datasets. IL12A and IL12B expression positively correlated with IFNG, TNF, and GZMB and negatively with TGFB1, suggesting an association between IL-12 signaling and proinflammatory, cytotoxic immune programs in human CRC (Figures S3A–S3D). Consistent with these observations, ELISA analysis of CT26 tumor lysates demonstrated significantly elevated intratumoral IFN-γ levels in EcN-IL12-treated tumors (Figure 2L). TNF-α levels were increased in both EcN and EcN-IL12 groups (Figure 2M), consistent with innate immune stimulation by the bacterial chassis. As expected, IL-12 concentrations were elevated in tumors from both recombinant IL-12- and EcN-IL12-treated mice (Figure 2N), validating effective cytokine delivery. Notably, EcN-IL12 treatment led to a significant increase in intratumoral granzyme B levels (Figure 2O), whereas TGF-β levels remained largely unchanged across treatment groups (Figure 2P), indicating enhanced cytotoxicity without further amplification of immunosuppressive signaling.

Quorum-sensing-driven circuit amplifies intratumoral IL-12 output and enhances antitumor efficacy

To further improve the spatial control and inducibility of IL-12 expression within tumors, we engineered EcN with an integrated quorum-sensing (QS) module18 together with a synchronized bacterial lysis circuit24,25 (Figure 3A). This LuxI/LuxR module responds to accumulation of acyl-homoserine lactone (AHL) and coordinates density-dependent IL-12 expression across tumor-colonizing bacterial populations, whereas the lysis module enables periodic extracellular release of the cytokine once the population reaches a critical threshold. Western blot analysis confirmed robust IL-12 expression in both the bacterial pellet and supernatant fractions of the QS-engineered strain (EcN-IL12-QS), whereas no detectable signal was observed in wild-type EcN controls (Figure 3B). To compare constitutive versus QS-regulated expression, we cultured EcN-IL12 and EcN-IL12-QS strains under anoxic, hypoxic, and normoxic conditions. Both strains exhibited enhanced bacterial growth under hypoxic conditions, consistent with the activity range of the hypoxia-responsive pvhb promoter, with EcN-IL12-QS showing comparable or slightly accelerated growth kinetics (Figure S4A). ELISA and western blot analyses further demonstrated that under hypoxic conditions, total IL-12 levels released into the culture supernatant were significantly higher in the QS-regulated strain than in the constitutive strain, consistent with synchronized lysis-mediated extracellular release of IL-12 after density-dependent circuit activation (Figures S4B and S4C). To validate the dynamic behavior of the QS circuit, we employed mCherry as an independent reporter, induced by IPTG. Flow cytometry revealed oscillatory fluorescence patterns over time, consistent with density-dependent activation and lysis cycles (Figure 3C). At lower IPTG concentrations (0.5 mM), fluorescence exhibited a characteristic rise-fall-rise pattern, indicative of feedback-controlled population dynamics. Correspondingly, bacterial OD600 measurements showed synchronized oscillations, confirming that mCherry fluorescence reflects bacterial growth and lysis behavior rather than constitutive expression (Figure S4D).

We next evaluated the functional consequence of QS-regulated IL-12 release in vitro. In a triple co-culture system consisting of MC38-OVA tumor cells, OT-1 CD8+ T cells, and engineered bacteria, EcN-IL12-QS induced the highest level of tumor cell killing compared with constitutive EcN-IL12 or control treatments (Figures S4E and S4F). These results demonstrate that QS-mediated, pulsatile IL-12 release enhances CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity in vitro. Given the clinical importance of reversibility and biosafety, we assessed antibiotic sensitivity of the QS strain. In vitro assays showed that ciprofloxacin administration efficiently eliminated EcN-IL12-QS within 24 h, confirming that the engineered strain remains susceptible to standard antibiotics (Figures S4G and S4H).

We next assessed the antitumor efficacy of QS-engineered bacteria in vivo. MC38 tumor-bearing mice were treated intravenously with PBS, recombinant IL-12, EcN, EcN-QS (QS circuit without IL-12), or EcN-IL12-QS on days 7 and 14 post-tumor implantation (Figure 3D). CFU quantification demonstrated preferential and sustained colonization of EcN-IL12-QS within tumors, with minimal bacterial burden detected in the liver, spleen, or kidney (Figures 3E and 3F), indicating that QS engineering does not compromise tumor tropism.

Tumor growth analysis revealed that both EcN-QS and EcN-IL12-QS delayed tumor progression relative to PBS and wild-type EcN, reflecting a modest contribution from bacterial innate immune stimulation (Figures 3G–3I). Importantly, only the EcN-IL12-QS group achieved significant tumor regression, demonstrating that QS-amplified IL-12 expression provides therapeutic benefit beyond vector-mediated effects alone.

Consistent with enhanced tumor control, immunohistochemical analysis showed marked increases in intratumoral CD45+ leukocytes, CD4+ T cells, and CD8+ T cells in the EcN-IL12-QS-treated group, with CD8+ cytotoxic T cells representing the dominant infiltrating population (Figures 3J–3L). His-tagged IL-12 protein was detected exclusively in tumor lysates from EcN-IL12-QS-treated mice, confirming localized cytokine expression in vivo (Figure 3M). ELISA analysis further revealed that intratumoral IL-12 and IFN-γ concentrations were significantly higher in the EcN-IL12-QS group than in all other treatment groups (Figures 3N and 3P). TNF-α levels were modestly elevated (Figure 3O), consistent with bacterial-mediated innate immune activation.

To establish an appropriate benchmark for IL-12 efficacy, we performed a recombinant IL-12 dose-escalation study in the CT26 tumor model (Figure S5A). Low (5 μg), medium (10 μg), and high (20 μg) doses were evaluated. While high-dose IL-12 achieved strong tumor suppression, it was associated with significant body weight loss, whereas low-dose IL-12 showed limited efficacy (Figures S5B–S5E). The medium dose (10 μg) achieved antitumor effects comparable to EcN-IL12-QS without overt toxicity and was, therefore, used as the positive control in subsequent experiments (Figures S5B–S5F).

We further compared constitutive versus QS-regulated IL-12 expression in vivo (Figure S6A). In MC38 tumor-bearing mice, EcN-IL12-QS demonstrated superior tumor suppression compared with constitutive EcN-IL12, achieving approximately 50% tumor regression relative to PBS controls (Figures S6B–S6D). Both engineered strains preferentially accumulated in tumors, whereas unmodified EcN exhibited broader distribution (Figure S6E). Serum IFN-α levels remained within normal range across groups (Figure S6F), indicating preserved systemic safety.

Finally, we explored the potential mechanisms underlying enhanced efficacy. EcN-IL12-QS treatment resulted in the highest intratumoral IL-12 and IFN-γ levels (Figures S6G and S6H). Quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis revealed downregulation of angiogenesis-associated genes (Vegfa and Vegfr2) alongside upregulation of IFN-γ-inducible chemokines Cxcl9 and Cxcl10 (Figure S6I). These changes are consistent with immune-mediated vascular remodeling, although we do not directly assess tumor oxygenation.

In a CT26 liver metastasis model, EcN-IL12-QS treatment significantly reduced metastatic burden and improved survival (Figures S6J–S6M), supporting the conclusion that QS-regulated IL-12 expression enhances therapeutic efficacy, while preserving safety and spatial control.

Dual-engineered system enhances IL-12 delivery and promotes DC-T cell crosstalk

To specifically examine the contribution of CD137-mediated costimulation, we established an antigen-specific DC-T cell co-culture system using CFSE-labeled OT-1 CD8+ T cells. T cells were co-cultured with control DCs, OVA-expressing DCs (DC-OVA), or DCs co-expressing OVA and CD137 (DC-OVA-CD137). Flow cytometric analysis revealed increased CFSE dilution in the DC-OVA-CD137 group, indicating enhanced antigen-driven T cell proliferation (Figure S7A). Consistently, RT-qPCR analysis showed significantly increased expression of Ifn-γ, Tnf-α, and Gzmb transcripts in T cells exposed to CD137 costimulation (Figures S7B–S7D). Elevated secretion of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and granzyme B was further confirmed by ELISA (Figures S7E–S7G), and intracellular cytokine staining corroborated these findings (Figures S7H and S7I). Together, these results demonstrate that CD137 expression on DCs provides a potent costimulatory signal that enhances antigen-specific CD8+ T cell expansion and effector differentiation in vitro.

We next characterized the capacity of eDCs to internalize engineered bacteria. Membrane labeling with DiI and GFP-based bacterial fluorescence confirmed successful genetic modification of eDCs and efficient uptake of EcN-IL12-QS. Flow cytometric analysis showed a dose-dependent increase in GFP+ eDCs with increasing bacterial loading ratios (Figure 4B). Fluorescence microscopy revealed intracellular localization of EcN-IL12-QS within Dil-labeled eDC membranes and DAPI-stained nuclei within 2 h of co-culture (Figure 4C). Transmission electron microscopy further confirmed the presence of intact bacteria enclosed within intracellular vesicular structures (Figure 4D).

Time-course analysis demonstrated that EcN-IL12-QS remained viable within eDCs for several hours, while eDC viability was largely preserved during this period (Figures 4E and 4F). This transient compatibility window supports the feasibility of using eDCs as short-term bacterial carriers without compromising host cell integrity. To assess the functional consequences of this dual-engineered system, eDC-EcN-IL12-QS cells were co-cultured with CD8+ T cells, resulting in significantly increased secretion of IL-12 and IFN-γ compared with control conditions (Figures 4G and 4H). These data indicate that intracellular bacterial loading does not impair, and may enhance, the immunostimulatory capacity of eDCs in vitro.

Finally, we evaluated whether this enhanced DC-T cell interaction translated into improved tumor cell killing. In an antigen-specific cytotoxicity assay using OVA-expressing MC38 tumor cells, co-culture with eDC-EcN-IL12-QS and CFSE-labeled CD8+ T cells resulted in extensive tumor cell loss, as visualized by fluorescence microscopy (Figure 4I). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant reduction in tumor cell viability over 48 h compared with control groups (Figure 4J). These results support a model in which eDC-mediated bacterial delivery and CD137-dependent costimulation act cooperatively to enhance CD8+ T cell-mediated cytotoxicity.

Dual-engineered system suppresses colorectal liver metastasis via localized IL-12 delivery and immune activation

To directly assess the contribution of eDCs as bacterial carriers, CT26 liver metastasis-bearing mice were treated either with eDC-loaded EcN-IL12-QS or with free co-injection of eDCs and EcN-IL12-QS (Figure S8A). Mice receiving eDC-loaded bacteria exhibited a significantly reduced number of metastatic liver nodules compared with the free co-injection group (Figures S8B and S8C). This therapeutic benefit was accompanied by a clear survival advantage, as indicated by a rightward shift in Kaplan-Meier survival curves (Figure S8D).

Immunohistochemical analysis further revealed increased intratumoral bacterial signals and enhanced infiltration of immune cells, particularly CD8+ T cells, in the eDC-loaded group (Figure S8E). Biodistribution analysis performed at early and late time points following treatment demonstrated that bacterial colonization within liver metastases was more than 3-fold higher in the eDC-loaded group, whereas off-target bacterial accumulation in the kidney was significantly reduced (Figure S8F). Consistently, ELISA analysis showed markedly elevated intratumoral IL-12 and IFN-γ levels in the eDC-loaded group (Figures S8G and S8H). Importantly, serum ALT and AST levels remained within normal physiological ranges across treatment groups (Figure S8I), indicating no evidence of overt liver injury associated with bacterial treatment in this model. Together, these data indicate that intracellular loading of EcN-IL12-QS into eDCs enhances tumor localization and immune activation compared with free co-injection.

To further dissect the functional contributions of tumor homing and costimulation, four eDC variants were generated: DC-EcN-IL12-QS (double-negative), DC-CDH17-EcN-IL12-QS (CDH17-only), DC-CD137-EcN-IL12-QS (CD137-only), and dual-positive eDC-EcN-IL12-QS (Figure S9A). In the CT26 liver metastasis model, the dual-positive eDC-EcN-IL12-QS group demonstrated the most pronounced survival benefit, outperforming both single-modification groups and the bacterial-only control (Figure S9C).

Analysis of metastatic burden showed that dual-positive eDC-EcN-IL12-QS treatment resulted in the greatest reduction in metastatic nodules, whereas CDH17-only or CD137-only modifications conferred partial benefit (Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4, Figure 5, Figure 6, Figure 7B, E). Intratumoral IL-12 levels were highest in the dual-positive group, correlating with enhanced bacterial accumulation within tumor tissues (Figure S9F). Notably, CDH17-only eDCs exhibited greater intratumoral bacterial localization than CD137-only eDCs, consistent with a dominant role of CDH17 in tumor homing (Figure S9H). No significant changes in body weight were observed across engineered bacterial treatment groups (Figure S9D), and serum ALT and AST levels remained within normal ranges, indicating preserved hepatic safety (Figures S9I–S9J).

To further characterize immune activation within metastatic lesions, RT-qPCR analysis revealed that tumors from the dual-positive group exhibited the highest expression of DC activation markers and CD8+ T cell-associated genes, consistent with enhanced local immune engagement (Figure S9K). These findings support a model in which tumor homing (CDH17) and costimulatory signaling (CD137) act cooperatively to maximize therapeutic efficacy in metastatic disease.

We next examined functional activation states of immune subsets within metastatic liver lesions. An increased frequency of MHC-II+ CD11c+ DCs was detected in tumors from the dual-treated group (Figures 6D and S11A). In parallel, CD8+CD69+ early-activated T cells (Figures 6E, S10B, and S11B) and CD8+IFN-γ+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (Figures 6F and S11C) were significantly enriched within metastatic liver tissues following dual-engineered treatment. These results indicate coordinated activation of antigen-presenting cells and cytotoxic T cells within the TME, rather than isolated immune subset expansion. In addition to local immune remodeling, systemic immune activation was evident in peripheral blood. Mice treated with EcN-IL12-QS or eDC-EcN-IL12-QS showed significantly increased frequencies of MHC-II+ CD11c+ DCs (Figures 6G and S11D), CD4+ T cells (Figures 6H and S11E), and CD8+ T cells (Figures 6I and S11F) in peripheral blood. These findings suggest that localized IL-12 delivery is associated with broader systemic immune engagement, without evidence of overt cytokine-driven toxicity.

Spatial organization of immune cells within metastatic lesions was further examined by immunofluorescence. CD11c+ and CD86+ activated DCs were found in close proximity within tumor regions, consistent with localized antigen-presenting niches (Figure 6J). In addition, TNF-α+ immune cells were frequently observed adjacent to CD8+ T cells (Figure 6K), supporting the presence of an active inflammatory microenvironment at sites of tumor control.

As a live biotherapeutic strategy, the ability to terminate bacterial activity is critical for clinical safety. To evaluate reversibility, EcN-IL12-QS (free bacteria) and eDC-EcN-IL12-QS (intracellular bacteria) colonization models were established, followed by administration of ciprofloxacin (30 mg/kg) after stable bacterial localization (Figure S12A). In subcutaneous tumor models, antibiotic treatment did not adversely affect body weight or compromise antitumor efficacy, as assessed by tumor volume and tumor weight (Figures S12B–S12E). Quantitative CFU analysis demonstrated that bacterial loads in tumors and major organs declined to below the limit of detection within 24 h of antibiotic administration in both free and eDC-loaded groups (Figure S12F). Consistently, immunohistochemical staining for bacterial O6 antigen confirmed complete clearance of engineered bacteria from tumor tissues following antibiotic treatment (Figure S12G).

Similar findings were observed in the CT26 liver metastasis model. Antibiotic administration did not diminish survival benefit or tumor control, while effectively eliminating bacterial signals from liver tissues (Figures S12H–S12K). Bacterial O6 antigen was readily detectable in tumors prior to antibiotic treatment but became undetectable after ciprofloxacin administration, regardless of whether bacteria were delivered in a free or intracellular form (Figures S12I–S12L). These results demonstrate that engineered EcN strains can be rapidly and effectively cleared using standard antibiotics, providing an essential safety control mechanism for potential clinical translation.

Discussion

Overcoming the spatiotemporal limitations of cytokine delivery remains a key challenge in the treatment of immunologically cold tumors such as MSS-type CRC. IL-12 is a potent immunostimulatory cytokine that bridges innate and adaptive immunity by activating DCs and promoting cytotoxic CD8+ T cell responses. However, its clinical utility has been severely constrained by dose-dependent toxicities, including hepatic inflammation, hematologic disorders, and cytokine storm-like syndromes,6,27 which have limited both dosing flexibility and therapeutic durability.

To address these challenges, recent efforts have explored spatially restricted cytokine delivery strategies, including Salmonella engineered to express IL-2,28 oncolytic viruses encoding GM-CSF,29 and synthetic biomaterials enabling sustained cytokine release.30 While these approaches improve local cytokine exposure, most remain limited by static expression kinetics, incomplete confinement to tumor tissues, or insufficient coordination with evolving immune states in the TME. Moreover, although microbial and cellular vectors exhibit intrinsic tumor tropism,31,32 few platforms dynamically couple cytokine release to bacterial density, tissue context, and immune activation thresholds.

In recent years, IL-12-based immunotherapy has undergone a paradigm shift from systemic protein administration toward gene-engineered, localized delivery systems, as summarized in multiple comprehensive reviews.33,34,35 Within this landscape, viral vector-mediated DC modification has emerged as a major strategy. Adenoviral (Ad) vectors offer high transduction efficiency and, importantly, can activate host antiviral sensors such as TLR9 and cGAS-STING pathways, thereby functioning as potent intrinsic adjuvants that promote Th1 polarization.36 However, clinical translation of Ad-based platforms remains limited by pre-existing anti-vector immunity and rapid induction of neutralizing antibodies, particularly with repeated dosing, which constrains therapeutic durability.37 Lentiviral systems have improved DC transduction efficiency through advances such as Vpx-containing virus-like particles that overcome SAMHD1 restriction,38 yet concerns regarding insertional mutagenesis, regulatory complexity, and good manufacturing practice-grade manufacturing continue to pose barriers to large-scale clinical deployment.

Here, we describe a dual-engineered delivery system that integrates QS-regulated IL-12-secreting EcN with genetically modified DCs (eDCs) expressing CDH17 and CD137, enabling coordinated spatial, temporal, and immunologic control. The engineered EcN preferentially colonizes hypoxic tumor regions and activates IL-12 expression under microaerophilic conditions, while QS-mediated lysis imposes density-dependent, pulsatile cytokine release. Schematic model illustrating how dual-engineered EcN-1917 strains are associated with remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment and suppression of colorectal liver metastasis

Engineered EcN-IL12 strains incorporating hypoxia-responsive expression control, a quorum-sensing module, and a synchronized lysis circuit preferentially accumulate within tumor tissues and release IL-12 in response to local hypoxic and population-density cues. Concurrently, dendritic cells (DCs) genetically modified to express CDH17 and CD137 (eDCs) exhibit enhanced tumor homing and can internalize EcN-IL12 strains, forming a dual-engineered platform (eDC-EcN-IL12-QS). Within the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, this system is associated with enriched intratumoral IL-12 availability and relatively reduced systemic cytokine exposure, leading to increased DC activation, enhanced antigen presentation, and elevated frequencies of activated CD8+ T cells. The resulting increase in IFN-γ and TNF-α production is associated with tumor cell apoptosis and reduced metastatic burden, supporting a coordinated immunotherapeutic strategy for colorectal cancer liver metastases.

The eDC component further enhances this system by providing active tumor homing through CDH17 and sustained T cell activation via CD137.39,40 When combined, these features establish a coordinated “priming-sustaining-remembering” cascade that bridges early innate immune activation with long-term adaptive memory.41,42 This is supported by enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration and IFN-γ production, indicative of TRM formation.43 While our data demonstrate enhanced effector T cell activation and persistence within tumor tissues, the extent to which long-term immunological memory is established warrants further investigation. Notably, the induction of TRMs aligns with emerging concepts of immune hysteresis, wherein early cytokine exposure shapes durable immune phenotypes through IL-10R-dependent feedback.14

This work builds on recent advances in engineering probiotics for cancer immunotherapy but extends the paradigm by embedding programmable immunologic logic within living delivery vehicles.32 The oscillatory behavior conferred by the QS circuit introduces an additional layer of control that may improve the safety-efficacy balance relative to continuous-release designs. From a translational perspective, both components of this platform have favorable clinical precedents. EcN is a well-characterized probiotic with an established safety record in humans,4,5 and DC-based cellular immunotherapies are under active clinical investigation across multiple solid tumors indications.44,45 Nevertheless, practical challenges remain, including host-mediated bacterial clearance, potential off-target colonization, and the logistical complexity of ex vivo DC manipulation.

A key strength of this platform lies in its modularity and extensibility. Additional environmental sensors, such as pH, protease activity, or tumor-specific metabolic cues, could be incorporated to further refine spatial specificity and responsiveness. Moreover, combination with immune checkpoint blockade, cancer vaccines, or adoptive cell therapies may yield synergistic benefits, particularly in immune-deserted tumor contexts. Beyond IL-12, this system could be adapted to deliver other cytokines, chemokines, or immune-engaging biologics in a similarly controlled manner.

In summary, we describe a programmable microbial-immune cell interface that coordinates localized cytokine delivery with intratumoral and systemic immune activation, while maintaining a clinically actionable safety switch through antibiotic-mediated clearance. This dual-engineered strategy addresses long-standing limitations of IL-12 therapy and provides a conceptual framework for logic-controlled, spatially confined immunotherapies designed to reprogram cold tumors.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, although our data support tumor-restricted activity of the engineered EcN platform, we do not directly quantify intratumoral oxygen gradients or bacterial lysis dynamics in vivo at single-lesion resolution. Second, while the dual-engineered system increases effector T cell activation and is associated with features consistent with tissue-resident immune responses, long-term immunological memory is not directly tested through rechallenge experiments. Third, the liver metastasis studies are performed in murine models, and the extent to which bacterial distribution, DC trafficking, and safety profiles will translate to human disease remains to be determined. Finally, although antibiotic-mediated clearance provides an important safety switch, additional pharmacology, biodistribution, and toxicology studies will be required before clinical translation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

HEK293T (human embryonic kidney), HL60 (human promyelocytic leukemia), CT26 (mouse colorectal carcinoma), and MC38 (mouse colon adenocarcinoma) cells are used in this study. HEK293T, HL60, and CT26 cells are obtained from ATCC, and MC38 cells are obtained from Sigma. HEK293T, HL60, and MC38 cells are maintained in DMEM, whereas CT26 cells are maintained in RPMI 1640, each supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and cultured at 37°C in 5% CO2. Cell lines are authenticated by STR profiling and routinely tested negative for mycoplasma contamination.

Primary cells

Primary bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) are isolated from femurs and tibiae of 7- to 8-week-old BALB/c mice and cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, GM-CSF, TNF-α, and LPS, as described below.

Bacterial strains

Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN; DSM 6601) is used as the parental bacterial strain for engineering and is maintained in LB medium at 37°C with shaking.

Animals

Female BALB/c and C57BL/6J mice (5–6 weeks old) are used for subcutaneous tumor studies, and male BALB/c mice (5 weeks old) are used for colorectal liver metastasis studies, as described in the relevant subsections below. OT-1 transgenic mice (C57BL/6-Tg(TcraTcrb)1100Mjb/J) are used as a source of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells. All mice are maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions with ad libitum access to food and water under a 12-h light/dark cycle in temperature- and humidity-controlled rooms. All animal procedures are approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University (SYSU-IACUC-2023-B0755).

Method details

Culture conditions of cell lines and strains

All mammalian cell lines used in this study, including HEK293T, HL60, CT26, and MC38, were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). HEK293T, HL60, and MC38 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Corning), while CT26 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Corning). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Newzerum), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (GIBCO), and cultures were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Cell lines were periodically tested for mycoplasma contamination using a commercial Mycoplasma Stain Assay Kit (C0296, Beyotime) and were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free throughout the study. Cell line identity was validated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling using the GenePrint 10 System (B9510, Promega) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. Primary bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were isolated from the femurs and tibiae of 7- to 8-week-old BALB/c mice. Bone marrow cells were flushed with ice-cold PBS, filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension, and cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, GM-CSF (50 ng/mL), TNF-α (20 ng/mL), LPS (20 ng/mL), and anti-mycoplasma reagent (Yeasen, 40607ES03). Cells were incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 5-7 days to induce DC maturation. The probiotic strain Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) was obtained from our laboratory strain bank and cultured in standard LB medium at 37°C with shaking. Engineered EcN strains expressing fluorescent or luminescent markers (e.g., EcN-CFSE) were constructed via plasmid electroporation and stored in glycerol stocks at −80°C for long-term use.

Plasmid construction

To construct the IL-12 expression system, murine cDNA sequences for pvhb (M30794.1), pelB (amplified from the pET-22b vector, Addgene #84863), il12a (NM_008351.4), and il12b (NM_001303244.1) were PCR-amplified and assembled into a single expression cassette encoding a PelB-IL12A-linker-IL12B-6×His fusion under control of the hypoxia-inducible pvhb promoter. All constructs were sequence-verified by Sanger sequencing and subcloned into appropriate backbone plasmids for bacterial genome integration or protein purification. For chromosomal integration into EcN, the pvhb-PelB-IL12 cassette was inserted at the lacZ locus using a λ-Red recombination system in conjunction with CRISPR-Cas9-mediated selection. Electrocompetent DH5α cells were transformed with the pREDCas9 plasmid and heat-shocked at 42°C for 45 s, followed by recovery in LB medium and plating on selective agar. Subsequently, EcN carrying pREDCas9 was induced with 1 mM IPTG at 30°C to promote Red recombinase expression. The donor plasmid containing the IL-12 expression cassette was PCR-amplified, purified, and co-electroporated (180 V, 450 mA) into competent EcN. Transformed cells were plated onto LB agar containing appropriate antibiotics, IPTG, and X-gal. Colonies exhibiting white phenotype were screened by colony PCR, and validated clones were designated as EcN-IL12. Engineered strains were cultured in sealed LB medium under hypoxic conditions (32°C, 80 rpm) for 24 h prior to downstream applications.

To construct quorum-sensing (QS)-regulated IL-12 strains (EcN-IL12-QS), the T7 RNA polymerase gene flanked by lacZ-homology arms was PCR-amplified from pUC57-T7 and inserted into the EcN genome using blue-white screening. Positive colonies were verified by colony PCR and sequencing. The resultant EcN-T7 strain was then co-electroporated with pTuLys2CMR2 (synchronized bacterial lysis circuit plasmid), which provides a synchronized bacterial lysis module, and pT7-IL12A-linker-IL12B-6×His to reconstitute the QS/lysis-coupled inducible IL-12 expression system. In this design, density-dependent activation of the LuxI/LuxR circuit drives T7-mediated IL-12 expression, whereas the lysis module enables periodic extracellular release of intracellular IL-12 payload. Transformants were selected on LB agar containing chloramphenicol and kanamycin and further validated by PCR.

For overexpression of CDH17 and CD137 in dendritic cells, murine cDNA sequences for cdh17 (NM_019753.4) and cd137 (NM_011612.2) were PCR-amplified and subcloned into lentiviral vectors containing C-terminal 6×His tags. Following sequence verification, plasmids were transformed into E. coli DH5α and cultured on LB agar with ampicillin selection. Endotoxin-free plasmid preparations were obtained using the Endo-free Plasmid Mini Kit I (D6943, Omega) and stored at −20°C until use in lentiviral packaging.

Lentivirus production

Lentiviral vectors encoding CDH17 or CD137 were packaged in HEK293T cells using a third-generation lentiviral system. Briefly, HEK293T cells were seeded at ∼70% confluence and co-transfected with the lentiviral backbone plasmid (CDH17-OE or CD137-OE), psPAX2 (Addgene #12260), and pMD2.G (Addgene #12259) using Lipo8000 transfection reagent (C0533, Beyotime), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Viral supernatants were harvested at 48 and 72 h post-transfection, passed through a 0.45 μm syringe filter to remove cell debris, and concentrated by polyethylene glycol (PEG8000) precipitation to increase viral titer. Concentrated viral particles were either used immediately or stored at −80°C. For dendritic cell transduction, BMDCs were incubated with viral supernatants supplemented with 8 μg/mL polybrene (H8761, Sorabio) for 24 h. Transduced cells were selected using puromycin (540411, Sigma-Aldrich) for three consecutive days at an optimized concentration to establish stable expression. Transgene expression was confirmed by fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry.

Total protein extraction from E. coli

For bacterial protein isolation, overnight E. coli cultures were harvested by centrifugation at 4,000 rpm for 15 min at room temperature. The bacterial pellet was resuspended in 4 mL of Western lysis buffer supplemented with 50 μL of lysozyme (100 mg/mL), and incubated on ice for 15–20 min with gentle agitation every 5 min to facilitate cell wall digestion. Following lysis, the suspension was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C to separate the soluble (supernatant) and insoluble (pellet) protein fractions. Total protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0009, Beyotime), and absorbance at 562 nm was measured with a microplate reader. Concentrations were calculated based on a standard curve generated using serially diluted bovine serum albumin (BSA) standards. All samples were aliquoted and stored at −80°C for downstream applications.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed to detect IL-12 and loading control proteins in bacterial or mammalian cell lysates. Total protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0009, Beyotime), and equal amounts of protein (typically 30 μg per lane) were denatured in SDS loading buffer containing β-mercaptoethanol and boiled at 95°C for 10 min. Proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE (10–12% polyacrylamide) at a constant voltage of 180 V, and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore). Membranes were pre-activated in methanol, rinsed in transfer buffer, and assembled into a transfer sandwich for wet transfer (400 mA, 45 min, room temperature). Following transfer, membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat milk in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20, FD9061, Fdbio) for 15 min at room temperature. Primary antibody incubations were conducted overnight at 4°C in blocking buffer using the following dilutions: anti-IL-12 p70 (30756-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:2000) and anti-DnaK (ab69617, Abcam, 1:5000) as an internal loading control. After three washes in TBST (5 min each), membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse, FDR007/FDM007, Fdbio, 1:5000) for 45 min at room temperature. Blots were developed using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; FD8000, Fdbio) and imaged on a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP system under optimized exposure conditions. Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining was used in parallel to assess total protein loading and gel integrity.

Non-reducing SDS-PAGE for dimeric protein detection

To assess disulfide-linked dimer formation of IL-12, non-reducing SDS-PAGE was performed. Total protein lysates were collected on ice and mixed with SDS loading buffer lacking reducing agents (DTT or β-mercaptoethanol). Samples were incubated at room temperature for 10 min to allow denaturation while preserving disulfide bonds. Proteins (30 μg per lane) were separated on 12% polyacrylamide gels at a constant voltage of 180 V for 60 min. Following electrophoresis, gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 to visualize total protein content. The presence of ∼140 kDa bands under non-reducing conditions was interpreted as evidence of disulfide-linked IL-12 dimer formation.

T cell activation and killing assay

To evaluate the immunostimulatory function of engineered EcN and recombinant IL-12, we conducted antigen-specific T cell activation and cytotoxicity assays using OT-I transgenic mice. Splenocytes were harvested from 8-week-old OT-I mice and passed through a 40 μm cell strainer. Red blood cells were lysed using ammonium chloride buffer (R1010, Sorabio), and CD8+ T cells were isolated via magnetic separation using CD8 MicroBeads and MACS Columns (130-117-044, Miltenyi Biotec). Purified CD8+ T cells were activated overnight in complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 150 U/mL recombinant IL-2 (CM39, NovoProtein), and 1 μg/mL plate-bound anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 antibodies (11453D, Gibco). Activated T cells were co-cultured with OVA-expressing MC38 tumor cells at an effector-to-target (E:T) ratio of 5:1 in the presence of either engineered EcN (1 × 106 CFU/mL), EcN-IL12, eDC-EcN-IL12-QS, or recombinant IL-12 protein (10 ng/mL). Co-cultures were incubated for 24 or 48 h, and bright-field images were acquired to assess tumor cell clearance. Tumor viability was quantified by measuring tumor cell-covered area at each time point using ImageJ software. Cell killing efficiency was expressed as: Tumor viability (%) = 100 × (tumor cell area at time t)/(tumor cell area at 0 h).

Cytokine ELISA in EcN-IL12 strain and tumor environment

To quantify IL-12 and associated cytokines in both bacterial cultures and tumor tissues, ELISA assays were performed following established protocols.

Bacterial cultures

Engineered EcN-IL12 strains were cultured under hypoxic conditions, and bacterial suspensions were centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 30 min at room temperature. The resulting supernatant was concentrated using a 30 kDa molecular weight cutoff ultrafiltration device (UFC803024, Millipore) and washed with PBS to remove low-molecular-weight contaminants. The concentrated protein fraction was resuspended in PBS and used for ELISA.

Tumor tissues

Tumor samples were weighed (∼100 mg) and homogenized in 5 mL of pre-chilled PBS using a mortar and pestle on ice. Homogenates were sonicated using a probe-type ultrasonic disruptor (5 s on, 3 s off, for 30 min), then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. Supernatants were collected and stored on ice prior to quantification.

Cytokine levels including IL-12 (MULTI SCIENCES, EK212), IFN-γ (MULTI SCIENCES, EK280), Granzyme B (GZMB, Proteintech, EK2173), TGF-β (Proteintech, KE10005), and TNF-α (Proteintech, KE10002) were measured using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. For each cytokine, standard curves were generated using serial dilutions of the provided recombinant standards. Absorbance was read at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and concentrations were calculated based on regression-fitted calibration curves.

Quantification of engineered bacterial distribution in tumor-bearing mice

To evaluate the in vivo biodistribution of engineered E. coli strains, colony-forming unit (CFU) assays were performed on tumor and major organ tissues following systemic administration. Tumor-bearing mice were intravenously injected with 1 × 106 CFU of engineered EcN strains. On days 1, 3, and 7 post-injection, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under sterile conditions. Tumors, liver, spleen, and kidneys were aseptically harvested and weighed (∼100 mg per sample). Tissues were homogenized in 1 mL of sterile PBS using a pre-chilled porcelain mortar and pestle. Homogenates were vortexed thoroughly and serially diluted in PBS. Aliquots (200 μL) of each dilution were plated onto LB agar plates supplemented with gentamicin to selectively recover engineered strains. Plates were incubated at 32°C for 16–18 h. Bacterial colonies were photographed against a dark background and enumerated manually. Results were expressed as CFU per gram of tissue. All experiments were performed in biological triplicates. CFU counts were log-transformed prior to statistical analysis. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and intergroup comparisons were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0.

Preparation of EcN-IL12-QS-loaded BMDCs

Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were generated as described above and co-incubated with engineered EcN-IL12-QS strains to generate intracellular bacteria-loaded DCs. BMDCs were seeded in antibiotic-free DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and co-cultured with EcN-IL12-QS at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1:100 (bacteria:cell) at 37°C for indicated durations. After co-incubation, the culture supernatant was aspirated and cells were washed three times with sterile PBS to remove extracellular bacteria. To eliminate remaining extracellular EcN while preserving intracellular viability, cells were incubated in complete DMEM containing 50 μg/mL gentamicin for 60 min at 37°C. This concentration effectively kills extracellular E. coli without compromising intracellular bacterial viability. Following gentamicin treatment, cells were washed three times with PBS and centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 5 min at 4°C. The resulting pellet was resuspended in fresh medium and designated as EcN-IL12-QS-loaded BMDCs for downstream applications.

DC viability assays

To assess the viability of DCs following bacterial loading, a trypan blue exclusion assay was performed. Equal volumes of cell suspensions were mixed 1:1 with 0.4% trypan blue solution (Beyotime, C1313S), and 10 μL of the mixture was loaded into a Countstar automated cell counting slide (Countstar, C0010101). Slides were inserted into the Countstar BioLab cell analyzer to determine the number of viable (unstained) and non-viable (blue-stained) cells. Cell viability was expressed as the percentage of viable cells relative to the total cell count. All measurements were conducted in triplicate.

Murine tumor models

All animal studies were conducted in accordance with the institutional guidelines and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with ad libitum access to food and water, and maintained in temperature- and humidity-controlled environments on a 12-h light/dark cycle. According to the animal care and use committee (IACUC) protocol guidelines, humane endpoints were strictly followed, and animals were euthanized if tumors exceeded 1500 mm3, abdominal distension or if signs of distress were observed.

Subcutaneous tumor models

Five-week-old female BALB/c or C57BL/6J mice were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Sun Yat-sen University and randomly assigned to treatment groups (n = 5 per group). CT26 (BALB/c) or MC38 (C57BL/6J) colorectal cancer cells were cultured to ∼80% confluence, harvested, and washed three times in sterile PBS. A total of 1 × 106 tumor cells suspended in 100 μL PBS were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of each mouse. Treatment Regimens: PBS control; Recombinant IL-12 (10 μg per mouse, i.v.); EcN, EcN-IL12, (1 × 106 CFU per mouse, i.v.) Tumor dimensions were measured every other day using digital calipers. Tumor volume was calculated using the standard formula: Volume (mm3) = 0.5 × length × width2. Body weight was recorded concurrently to monitor treatment-associated toxicity. Treatment with bacterial or dendritic cell formulations was initiated once tumors reached ∼50 mm3 in volume.

Colorectal liver metastasis model

To establish hepatic metastases, CT26 murine colorectal cancer cells were cultured under standard conditions and harvested at 80–90% confluence. Cells were washed three times with ice-cold PBS and resuspended at 1×107 cells/mL. Cell viability (>95%) was confirmed by trypan blue exclusion. Five-week-old male BALB/c mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane in oxygen. Under sterile conditions, a small left flank incision was made to expose the spleen. Using a 29G insulin syringe, 1×106 CT26 cells in 100 μL PBS were injected slowly into the splenic parenchyma. A sterile cotton swab was applied for 1 min post-injection to prevent leakage and bleeding. The abdominal wall and skin were closed using absorbable sutures. Mice were monitored until full recovery and returned to their cages. Treatment Regimens: One week after tumor cell implantation, mice were randomly assigned to the following treatment groups: PBS control; Recombinant IL-12 (10 μg per mouse, i.v.); EcN, EcN-IL12, EcN-QS, or EcN-IL12-QS (1 × 105 CFU per mouse, i.v.); eDC, DC-EcN-IL12-QS, DC-CD137-EcN-IL12-QS, DC-CDH17-EcN-IL12-QS, eDC-EcN, or eDC-EcN-IL12-QS (1 × 106 cells per mouse, i.v.); IL-12 (High: 20 μg; Medium: 10 μg; Low: 5 μg per mouse, i.v.); DC+EcN-IL12-QS (1 × 106 DC cells and 1 × 105 CFU EcN-IL12-QS per mouse); Antibiotic (Ciprofloxacin, 30mg/kg, i.p.). Bacterial suspensions were prepared from overnight LB cultures, washed three times with PBS, and standardized to the desired CFU using OD600 measurements (1 OD ≈ 1 × 108 CFU/mL). Injections were administered via the tail vein once weekly. Control mice received equivalent volumes of PBS. Mice were monitored for tumor burden, weight loss, and signs of distress. At endpoint or when tumors reached ethical limits, mice were euthanized and tumors and major organs were harvested for further analysis.

Immunohistochemistry and hematoxylin & eosin staining of tumor sections

To assess immune cell infiltration, cytokine expression, apoptosis, and tumor cell proliferation, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining were performed on paraffin-embedded tumor sections. Tumor tissues were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 48 h at room temperature, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Serial 4 μm sections were cut using a rotary microtome, mounted on positively charged slides, and dried overnight at 37°C or for 2 h at 60°C. Slides were deparaffinized in xylene (2 × 10 min), rehydrated through descending concentrations of ethanol (100%, 95%, 85%, 70%), and rinsed in distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) or EDTA buffer (pH 8.0) by heating in a pressure cooker or microwave for 20 min, followed by cooling and PBS wash. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. Non-specific binding was blocked using 5% normal goat serum for 30 min at room temperature. Slides were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in antibody diluent: Anti-CD4 (GB15064, Servicebio, 1:200); Anti-CD8 (GB15068, Servicebio, 1:200); Anti-Caspase-3 (GB12532, Servicebio, 1:200); Anti-Ki67 (GB121141, Servicebio, 1:500); Anti-IL-12p70 (30756-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:200); Anti-CD45 (GB113886, Servicebio, 1:200); Anti-O6 (bs-0658R, Bioss, 1:200). After PBS washes (3 × 5 min), slides were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 45 min at room temperature. Signals were developed using DAB substrate solution (Servicebio), and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Adjacent sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin following standard protocols to assess tumor histopathology. Images were acquired using an Olympus BX63 microscope, and quantitative analysis of positively stained cells was performed in at least three randomly selected high-power fields (20×) per sample using ImageJ software.

Flow cytometry analysis of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and blood

To profile immune cell populations in tumors and peripheral blood, multicolor flow cytometry was performed on single-cell suspensions derived from tumor tissues and venous blood.

Tumor dissociation and cell preparation

Excised tumors were minced into ∼1 mm3 fragments and enzymatically digested in tissue dissociation buffer at 37°C for 1 h with shaking (220 rpm). Digested tissues were mechanically disrupted and passed through a 70 μm strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions. Red blood cells were lysed using ammonium chloride lysis buffer (10 min at room temperature), and cells were washed twice with PBS.

Peripheral blood

Whole blood was collected from the retro-orbital venous plexus into anticoagulant-treated tubes. After centrifugation, red blood cells were lysed as described above, and remaining leukocytes were washed with PBS.

Surface and intracellular staining

After Fc receptor blocking, cells were stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies for 30 min at 4°C in the dark: CD45-PerCP (Elabscience, E-AB-F1136J); CD3-PE (E-AB-F1013D); CD4-FITC (E-AB-F1097C); CD8-APC (E-AB-F1104D); MHC II-PE (E-AB-F0990D); CD11c-FITC (E-AB-F0991C); CD86-APC (E-AB-F0994E). For intracellular staining, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 20 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS containing 0.5% BSA, and incubated for 20 min at room temperature. After washing, cells were stained with: Granzyme B-FITC (Biolegend, 372205); IFN-γ-FITC (Biolegend, 505805). Stained cells were analyzed on a flow cytometer equipped with appropriate laser lines. Compensation was applied using single-stain controls. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star).

Flow cytometry analysis of bacterial lysis

To monitor quorum-sensing-induced bacterial lysis and protein expression dynamics, flow cytometry was used to quantify reporter fluorescence in engineered E. coli strains. A 1:100 dilution of overnight bacterial culture was inoculated into 30 mL of fresh LB medium containing appropriate antibiotics. Expression of the mCherry reporter gene was induced with varying concentrations of IPTG (0.5–2 mM). Bacterial samples were collected every hour for up to 5 h post-induction. At each time point, 0.5 mL of bacterial suspension was harvested, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 2 min, and washed three times with sterile PBS. mCherry fluorescence intensity was measured using a flow cytometer equipped with a PE channel (excitation: 561–587 nm). Gating was based on forward/side scatter profiles to exclude debris, and at least 10,000 events were recorded per sample. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software. A threshold of fluorescence intensity was used to determine activation of the quorum-sensing circuit and extent of lysis-associated reporter expression.

Transmission electron microscopy

To visualize intracellular bacteria within dendritic cells and assess ultrastructural features, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed. BMDC-EcN-IL12 co-cultures were collected at ∼80% confluence, gently detached, and immediately fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (prepared in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) at 4°C for 2 h. Cells were washed three times with cold phosphate buffer and post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO4) for 2 h at 4°C to preserve membrane integrity and contrast. Samples were sequentially dehydrated in graded ethanol (30%–100%) and then treated twice with 100% propylene oxide (15 min each). Dehydrated specimens were infiltrated with epoxy resin:propylene oxide (1:1) for 2 h, followed by overnight incubation in pure resin. Polymerization was carried out at 60°C for 48 h. Ultrathin sections (60–70 nm) were cut using an ultramicrotome, mounted on copper grids, and stained with 2% uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Grids were examined under a TEM operated at 80–100 kV, and digital images were acquired using Olympus Viewer software. Subcellular compartments containing intracellular bacteria were identified and documented for morphological assessment.

Immunofluorescence staining and confocal imaging

To evaluate bacterial internalization, DC-bacteria interactions, and immune marker expression, immunofluorescence staining was performed on both live cells and frozen tumor sections, followed by confocal microscopy.

Cell-based imaging

HL60, immature DCs, and mature DCs were cultured to 70–80% confluence, washed with PBS, and stained with 5 μM DiI (Beyotime, C1036) in serum-free RPMI 1640 at 37°C for 30 min in the dark. Labeled cells were washed three times with complete medium and seeded onto glass-bottom confocal dishes for adherence. Engineered EcN-IL12 strains were cultured to mid-log phase, harvested by centrifugation, and resuspended in PBS containing 10 μM CFSE (Invitrogen, C34554). Bacteria were incubated for 20 min at 37°C, quenched with 10% FBS, and washed three times with PBS. Bacterial density was standardized to OD600 ≈ 1 (∼1 × 108 CFU/mL). CFSE-labeled bacteria were added to DiI-labeled cells at a 1:200 ratio and incubated for 30 min at 37°C. Cells were washed thoroughly to remove non-adherent bacteria and treated with 100 μg/mL gentamicin for 30 min to eliminate extracellular bacteria. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, washed, and counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL, 5 min). Images were acquired using an Olympus FV3000 laser-scanning confocal microscope. Fluorophores were excited at the following wavelengths: DiI (549 nm, red), CFSE (488 nm, green), DAPI (405 nm, blue). Images were processed with Olympus Fluoview and ImageJ for fluorescence intensity and colocalization analysis.

Tumor tissue sections

Freshly excised tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, cryoprotected in graded sucrose (10%, 20%, 30%), and embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound. Frozen blocks were sectioned at 8–10 μm thickness and mounted on glass slides. Sections were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min, blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h, and incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies (1:200): CD11c (AiFang Biological, AFRM0294); CD86 (AFRM9023); CD4 (AFRM9419); CD8 (AFRM0004). After PBS washes, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 or 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam, 1:500) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and slides were mounted using antifade medium (Beyotime, P0126). Images were captured using an FV3000 confocal microscope under consistent acquisition settings.

In vitro T cell and DC Co-stimulation assay

DC construction

BMDCs were induced and differentiated from C57bl/6 mouse bone marrow. A lentiviral transduction system was used to construct DCs expressing ovalbumin (OVA) (DC-OVA) or DCs co-expressing OVA and CD137 (DC-OVA-CD137).

T cell sorting and labeling

Naive CD8+ T cells were negatively sorted from the spleens of OT-1 transgenic mice using a magnetic bead sorting kit (Miltenyi Biotec). Cells were labeled with 5 μM CFSE (Thermo Fisher) at 37°C in the dark for 10 min, followed by termination of the reaction with serum-containing medium.

Co-culture

Labeled T cells were co-cultured with eDCs at a 10:1 ratio in 96-well U-bottom plates for 72 h.

Flow cytometry analysis

Cells were harvested. The dilution of CFSE fluorescence intensity in the CD8+ population was detected using a flow cytometer to assess proliferation.

Functional assay

Cells were harvested for further mRNA expression by RT-qPCR detection. After blocking Golgi transport with Brefeldin A and Monensin for 4 h, intracellular staining (IFN-γ, Granzyme B) was performed. Supernatant concentrations of TNF-α, Granzyme B and IFN-γ were detected by ELISA.

IPTG-induced lysis

EcN-IL12-QS was inoculated into LB liquid medium and cultured with shaking at 37°C until OD600 reached 0.2. The bacterial culture was split equally; the experimental group received IPTG at a final concentration of 0.5mM, 1mM and 2Mm, while the control group received an equal volume of solvent. OD600 was measured every 2h using a spectrophotometer f to plot the lysis curve.

Hypoxia, anoxia and normoxia culture

EcN-IL12 and EcN-IL12-QS strains were inoculated into different oxygen condition. The normoxia group was placed in a standard incubator (21% O2, 5% CO2), anoxia group was placed in anaero pack, while the hypoxia group was placed in a hypoxia workstation (Baker Ruskinn) with gas conditions set to 1% O2, 5% CO2, 94% N2. Supernatants were collected after 24 and 48 h of culture.

ALT/AST detection

20μL of serum sample was mixed with 80μL of matrix solution in a 96-well plate. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Following incubation, 20μL of 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine solution was added to each well, and the plate was incubated at 37°C for an additional 20 min. Finally, 200μL of stop solution was added to terminate the reaction. The absorbance was measured at 510 nm using a microplate reader. Activity levels were calculated based on the standard curves generated using sodium pyruvate standards.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells or tissue using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, samples were lysed in TRIzol and mixed with chloroform for phase separation. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C, the aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube. RNA was precipitated with isopropanol and washed with 75% ethanol. The RNA pellet was air-dried and dissolved in RNase-free water. The concentration and purity of the RNA were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The A260/A280 ratio was maintained between 1.8 and 2.0. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Takara, Japan) according to standard procedures. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix on a Bio-Rad CFX96] system. The reaction mixture contained 10μL 2× SYBR Green Master Mix, 0.5μL of forward and reverse primers, 2μL of cDNA template, and RNase-free water. The thermocycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s. The relative expression levels of the target genes were calculated using the 2−δδCt method and normalized to the internal control.

Bioinformatics analysis of gene expression and survival correlation

To investigate the clinical relevance of IL12A and IL12B expression in colorectal cancer (CRC) and other tumor types, publicly available transcriptomic and clinical datasets were analyzed using the following platforms.

GEPIA2

Differential expression of IL12A and IL12B between tumor and adjacent normal tissues was analyzed across TCGA and GTEx datasets. Overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) analyses were performed using the median expression level as a cutoff. The log rank test was used to assess statistical significance (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn).

TIMER2.0

To evaluate immune correlates, the expression of IL12A and IL12B was correlated with markers of immune cell subsets (CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, NK cells) across tumor samples. Spearman’s correlation was used, and p-values <0.05 were considered significant (http://timer.cistrome.org).

Kaplan-Meier plotter

For expanded survival analysis, Kaplan-Meier curves were generated using high vs. low gene expression groups stratified by median values. Hazard ratios (HRs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and log rank p-values were reported. All analyses were performed using default parameters unless otherwise specified (https://kmplot.com).

Quantification and statistical analysis

No statistical methods are used to predetermine sample size. For animal studies, mice are randomly assigned to treatment groups after tumor establishment unless otherwise stated. The value of n represents independent biological replicates, defined as individual mice for in vivo experiments, independent cell cultures for in vitro assays, or independent tumor/tissue samples for biochemical analyses, as specified in the figure legends. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated. Statistical analyses are performed using GraphPad Prism 9.4.1. Comparisons between two groups are performed using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t tests when appropriate. Comparisons among multiple groups are performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test. Tumor growth trajectory analyses are performed using two-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons where indicated. Survival differences are analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method with log rank testing. Correlation analyses for public datasets are performed using Spearman’s correlation. Statistical details, including the test used, exact n, and definition of significance, are provided in the figure legends or indicated in the relevant Results sections. A p value <0.05 is considered statistically significant. Significance is denoted as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗p < 0.0001.
