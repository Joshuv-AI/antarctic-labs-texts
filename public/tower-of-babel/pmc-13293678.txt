Response to the Letter to the Editor Entitled “Interpreting Postdiagnosis Toenail Metal Measurements in Childhood Nephrotic Syndrome”
Authors: Cal H. Robinson, Rulan S. Parekh
Published: 2026

The Author Replies:

We thank Wang et al.1 for their letter. They highlight several of our acknowledged study limitations,2 including temporal relationships between sampling and outcomes, assay detection limits, and incomplete inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry testing for metals other than mercury.

Toenails are a reliable sample for environmental metal testing; levels reflect exposure over the past 6 to 12 months.3,4 We collected toenails cross-sectionally at a median of 2.1 (interquartile range: 0.6–4.5) years from nephrotic syndrome diagnosis. Environmental metal exposure may fluctuate over time owing to changes in diet, residence, or other factors. However, previous studies demonstrate moderate-to-high correlations between longitudinal nail mercury measures 1 to 6 years apart.3 Restricting to 96 children (32%) sampled within 1 year of diagnosis, there were still no significant associations between log mercury concentration and steroid resistance (odds ratio: 0.92, 95% confidence interval: 0.51–1.51) or frequent relapses or steroid dependence (odds ratio: 1.06, 95% confidence interval: 0.73–1.50).

Metal concentrations were below detection limits in 12% (mercury) and 6% to 98% (other metals). Although this reflects low metal exposure, it means imputation had a greater influence on associational estimates. As a “worst-case” scenario, we imputed these concentrations using detection limits. To confirm the robustness of these findings, we repeated the following analyses: (i) imputing them as 0 and (ii) imputing them using nonparametric random forests. There were still no significant associations between mercury and any outcomes using these imputation methods.

Our primary objective was to evaluate associations with mercury, based on published data and patient concerns. Thus, we prioritized analyses for mercury using Direct Mercury Analyzer. Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry methods for mercury are limited, because of mercury loss during acid digestion, adsorption to instruments, and cross-contamination.5,6 After Direct Mercury Analyzer use, only 37% had sufficient samples for inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry. This limits power to detect outcome associations with other metals and may introduce selection bias, because young children with smaller toenail mass were less likely to be analyzed.

High environmental metal exposure was uncommon in children with nephrotic syndrome living in a developed country and no outcome associations were evident. These findings suggest that environmental metal exposure is unlikely to be a primary driver of disease activity. Further studies, with biological sample collection at diagnosis and repeated longitudinally, are warranted to exclude causal associations with nephrotic syndrome pathogenesis and subsequent outcomes.
