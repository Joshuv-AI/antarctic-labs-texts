Study on the climate impacts on the reservoir waterlevel
Authors: Xiaoyu Cui, Lu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate change poses significant challenges to the stability of hydropower systems, and the increased frequency of extreme weather events due to global warming further exacerbates these challenges. To reduce the climate vulnerability of hydropower systems, this study assessed the impact of temperature and precipitation variations on reservoir water level dynamics. Using daily water level data from 17 reservoirs in Sichuan Province, China, this study enhanced a benchmark model by incorporating nonlinear functions that more realistically represent actual conditions than linear functions. Within a linear regression framework, this study incorporated quadratic terms for temperature and precipitation. This constructed a model that accurately quantifies the relationship between climatic factors and the first-order difference of reservoir water level. The results demonstrate that both temperature and precipitation significantly and nonlinearly drive reservoir water level fluctuations. This study successfully characterized the complex relationship between water level fluctuations and climatic variables using a quadratic function and performed an in-depth analysis of the Marginal Rate of Substitution (MRS) between temperature and precipitation. A key finding reveals that the MRS exhibits dynamic dependency, with its numerical values and trends depending on specific temperature and precipitation levels. A turning point in the MRS trend occurs when precipitation reaches 17 centimeters and the temperature is 13.5 °C, which is a critical point for reservoir managers to monitor. Against the backdrop of global climate change threatening water security, this study quantitatively uncovers the dynamic nonlinear coupling mechanisms between climatic factors and reservoir water level. The identified critical tipping points offer deeper insights into climate impacts on reservoir operations, carrying significant theoretical and practical implications for building resilient, sustainable water resource management systems and optimizing hydropower system operations.Keywords: Reservoir water level, Rainfall, Temperature, Non-linearity, Marginal rate of substitution, Managed reservoir systemsSubject terms: Climate change, Hydrology

Introduction

Compared to the temperature trends observed from 1850 to 1900, the global average surface temperature has experienced a substantial increase of approximately 1.5°C by 2023, setting a new record as the warmest year on record1,2. Projections indicate that global average temperatures are likely to continue rising3. Disequilibrium in the climate system has increased the frequency of extreme weather events, with both heatwaves and cold spells exhibiting increased frequency and intensity4. Hydrological systems exhibit high sensitivity to climate fluctuations5. For example, China’s Yellow River basin alternates between droughts and floods6. Meanwhile, seasonal weather greatly affects water flow in the Upper Indus and Danube rivers7–9. Against the backdrop of global warming, the climate’s regulatory influence on water resources has intensified10,11, heightening the risks of global hydrological imbalance. Studies have shown that the increase in greenhouse gas emissions drives global warming, which causes the retreat of glaciers and lakes12–14, and further accelerates the speed of river runoff15,16. These changes critically endanger global water security17–19. Such changes show the deep impact of climate on water systems. Consequently, hydropower infrastructure dependent on these hydrological regimes is becoming increasingly constrained by climate change. Meanwhile, reducing the vulnerability of water systems is an important part of climate governance, and research on the impacts of climate change on reservoirs has grown increasingly critical20,21.

Increasing climate sensitivity of river basins amplifies the vulnerability of hydropower systems to climatic perturbations22. Climate volatility frequently triggers electricity deficits. Illustrative cases include India’s 2022 power crisis induced by drought and extreme heat, which severely compromised the local public welfare23. Concurrently, the Amazon River, which is the world’s largest fluvial system, endured its most severe historical drought in 202224–27. This drought caused critically low water levels that necessitated the decommissioning of a hydroelectric facility. Similar phenomena have been observed in China, as exemplified by the 2022 power rationing event across Sichuan Province. During this incident, persistent extreme high temperatures abruptly caused normally abundant rainfall to dwindle dramatically, ultimately triggering a sharp drop in reservoir water levels and reducing hydroelectric power output by approximately 5%28. These phenomena collectively demonstrate that extreme weather events pose a severe challenge to hydropower development. As we know, power supply serves as the cornerstone of national economies and holds critical significance29, in which hydropower contributes to 16% of global electricity generation30. Energy market perturbations and regional supply crises are fueled by geopolitical conflicts, post-pandemic recovery, and structural pressures, which intensify the pivotal role of renewable resources31. Hence, hydropower’s status as the leading global renewable energy source endows it with strategic necessity32. However, under the expectation that the El Niño phenomenon will continue to intensify climate anomalies, fortifying climate resilience within the global hydropower infrastructure and safeguarding its power generation stability emerge as a pressing priority. Therefore, this study explores how climate-driven factors regulate the power generation of reservoirs and focuses on the synergistic effects generated by temperature and precipitation.

To ensure stable hydropower operations, a profound understanding of climate-reservoir linkage mechanisms is essential. Since the mid-20th century, scholars have developed frameworks for water resource management33,34. Some studies indicate that climate shocks may lead to a declining trend in hydroelectric power generation35–38. However, some predictive models suggest that reservoir runoff will increase over time39,40. Notably, these studies predominantly rely on historical or predictive models but provide limited empirical analysis using actual and real-time data. To address this issue, this study utilizes daily observational records from reservoirs for investigation. Since hydropower plant generation data lacks public availability, scholars often conduct research using hydropower generation potential indicators such as reservoir flow and water level41,42. However, these studies have primarily focused on a single reservoir, which lacks generalizability. Therefore, this study expands the analysis to include reservoir water level data from 17 hydropower plants.

This study selects Sichuan Province, China, as the representative research area. China’s hydropower generation is predominantly concentrated in the southwestern region due to geographical advantages. Sichuan leads nationally in both installed capacity and annual electricity output43. Therefore, daily observational records from 17 large and medium-sized reservoirs in Sichuan are analyzed. These data provide a highly representative sample for integrated research on hydropower systems and climatic factors. Based on such data set, this study employs a dual-track analytical approach combining both linear and nonlinear models. Guided by the classical research logic of “identifying problems—investigating issues—drawing conclusions and insights”, this study has designed and established a research framework. The specific research logic is illustrated in Fig. 1. Beginning with an examination of climate change, this study first investigates the impacts of temperature and precipitation variations on reservoirs. Subsequently, it constructs specific functional relationships between climate factors and reservoir responses (i.e., the changes in reservoir water level of the reservoir system after being affected by the climate factors), followed by an in-depth analysis of the Marginal Rate of Substitution (MRS) between temperature and precipitation. These research efforts not only clarify the intrinsic relationship between climate change and reservoir water level fluctuations but also present computational formulas and value range tables that can be practically integrated into numerical analyses. At last, this study offers more detailed technical support for reservoir operators and provides practical references for reservoir management.

Logical flow of ideas.

Compared with existing literature, this study’s core marginal contributions include the following. First, this study provides empirical evidence for the relationship between climate change and reservoir water level. Due to data availability constraints, existing studies primarily rely on modeling methods to conduct predictive analysis. While useful for projecting future trends, conclusions derived under varying climate scenarios and hydropower models exhibit substantial uncertainty44. Second, it enhances analytical precision by using daily-scale reservoir data. This method addresses the issue that previous studies primarily relied on annual data sets. Third, this study incorporates nonlinear models to explore the association between climate and reservoir water level. By establishing a nonlinear functional relationship between climate factors and reservoir water level, we provide a more precise mathematical description of their relationship. Fourth, this study conducts a detailed analysis of the MRS between temperature and rainfall in influencing the water level. MRS reveals their relative marginal contributions and substitution potential under specific climatic conditions. Fifth, this study not only provides more practical references for reservoir operation and management but also highlights the need for reservoir designers and managers to pay greater attention to reservoir water level changes caused by climate change.

In line with the research objectives, this study is organized as follows. Section 2 outlines the analytical model and describes the data set used. Section 3 presents empirical analysis and key findings. Finally, Sect. 4 concludes the study with a summary of the results.

Methods

Research design

The reservoir water level is affected by both human and natural factors. To clarify the role of climatic factors in reservoir water level fluctuations, a schematic diagram of driving factors was drawn by simplifying the influencing elements, as shown in Fig. 2. Among these, human activities represent a key factor. Reservoir operators regulate water level by adjusting inflow and outflow in response to variables such as reservoir capacity, power generation requirements, and weather patterns. Natural climate emerges as another critical determinant, predominantly influencing water level through evapotranspiration and precipitation45. Therefore, this study selects temperature and precipitation as the principal climatic variables for monitoring46,47, while treating the human factor as a control variable in the regression analysis. Although the influence of evapotranspiration and rainfall on reservoir water level is well-established, the specific magnitude and functional form of their impact remain elusive. To address this gap, we will investigate the quantitative relationship between temperature, rainfall, and the reservoir water level. We aim to derive an equation describing such relationships.

Data

The hydrological data for reservoirs employed in this study were provided by the Sichuan Hydrological and Water Resources Survey Center operating under the Sichuan Provincial Water Resources Department. The detailed data source is available at: http://www.schwr.com:8088/rsvr. This data set is a daily-scale dataset covering key indicators of 17 reservoirs from June 11, 2023, to December 31, 2023. Meanwhile, the daily weather data were collected from the Reliable Prognosis (RP5) weather website, whose specific URL is: https://rp5.ru/. It aggregates global weather station data with comprehensive observation records. The difficulty here is the selection of weather sites and the corresponding data processing. For station selection, we prioritized counties or townships where reservoirs are located or chose the geographically closest stations as corresponding weather stations. Due to significant variations in recording frequencies and data volumes across stations and periods, we applied statistical techniques for data normalization, including averaging, summation, and identifying maximum and minimum values according to indicator attributes. In addition, a primary challenge in studying climate change impacts on the local hydropower system is the unavailability of daily hydropower plant generation data. However, among the accessible data, the reservoir water level is the most important one, since it is a key factor affecting power generation. Since specific generation data are unavailable, we use the daily reservoir water level as a proxy. Moreover, temperature and precipitation are important climate indicators. Precipitation directly affects reservoir water volume, while temperature influences evaporation. Drawing on previous studies, we selected daily rainfall, daily mean temperature, daily maximum temperature, and daily minimum temperature as the main explanatory variables48–51.

As we know, in the reservoir, water is artificially released and stored to regulate power generation. This means operators regulate water flowing into and out of reservoirs. These operations affect reservoir water level and storage capacities52–54. Therefore, when exploring the climate’s effect on the reservoir level, we use outflow and inflow as control variables. This approach removes human influence. We also included horizontal visibility and mean atmospheric pressure as control variables. We aimed to cover more factors affecting the reservoir water level. Horizontal visibility reflects climatic dryness or humidity, which affects water vapor evaporation. Atmospheric pressure directly influences water level changes through physical forces. It also correlates with climate factors such as high temperatures and rainfall. Therefore, atmospheric pressure may indirectly affect the reservoir water level. Thus, these four control variables were included in the empirical analysis.

To mitigate the influence of outliers on the empirical results, both the dependent and independent variables were winsorized at the 1% level in this study. The key variable definitions and summary statistics are also provided in Table 1.

Empirical modeling

To investigate the impact of climate change on reservoir water level in Sichuan Province, China, this study conducts regression analysis using daily panel data of reservoirs and climate. Considering the characteristics of panel data and to improve the accuracy of the analysis results, this study selects the fixed effects model as the core analytical method. In terms of the fixed effects specification, this study incorporates both entity-fixed effects and time-fixed effects into the model and finally adopts the two-way individual-time fixed effects model for regression analysis.

Furthermore, this study incorporates the first-order difference terms of variables into the regression to construct a linear regression model for exploring the relationship between climate change and reservoir water level, with its specific form shown in Eq. (1).

Since the data from the Sichuan Hydrological and Water Resources Survey Center is updated at 8:00 a.m. every day, the reservoir level of the day will also be affected by the water level regulation of the previous day. Thus, the difference between today’s reservoir level and yesterday’s reservoir level, i.e., the first-order difference in reservoir level, is used as the dependent variable in this study.

Precipitation has a significant effect on the reservoir water level. For days with recorded precipitation, the daily total precipitation is used, while for days without precipitation, the value is set to 0. As a result, the rain variable contains excessive zeros. Given that logarithmic transformation requires strictly positive values, this study employs the original rain values rather than their logarithmic counterparts as the independent variable.

Statistical analysis shows that average and minimum temperatures frequently fall below zero. Therefore, logarithmic transformation is not feasible. Formulation of the MRS mathematical expression

Conventional regression analysis estimates only the average marginal effects of temperature and precipitation on reservoir water level. This approach captures limited information from the bivariate quadratic model. To gain deeper and comprehensive insights, this study employs a full quadratic model (i.e., including both interaction and squared terms) of temperature and precipitation to conduct MRS analysis. This method reveals the relative sensitivity of the reservoir level to changes in temperature and precipitation. It also shows their dynamic variations. This multidimensional analysis helps to understand the potential for substitution or complementarity between the two factors. These insights provide reservoir managers with richer information. Managers can adjust strategies, optimize resources, and enhance climate responsiveness under different conditions. Numerical simulation diagram of MRS.

Figure 3 presents a three-dimensional simulation diagram, where the vertical axis represents the calculated value of MRS, and the two horizontal axes denote the values of mean temperature and precipitation, respectively. With reference to the range of temperature and precipitation values in the actual data of this study, the range of mean temperature is set from − 10 °C to 50 °C, and the range of precipitation is set from 0 to 25 cm. The blue curve in the diagram represents the movement curve of MRS as it varies with temperature and precipitation. It can be observed from the blue curve that there is an abrupt change zone, where the positive or negative level of the MRS value changes significantly around the temperature of 13.5°C. Similarly, when the rainfall is 17 centimeters, the positive or negative value of MRS also changes. The turning point shown in Fig. 3 is consistent with that calculated by Eq. (11). These results delineate an evaporation-sensitive zone, which is a region where terrestrial evaporation exhibits a significant response to minor climatic changes. It has weak evaporative stability and is prone to fluctuations of external factors. Notably, the corresponding temperature of this evaporation-sensitive zone identified in this study is approximately 13.5°C. This finding suggests reservoir managers need greater temperature awareness. Measures should be adjusted at 13.5°C or higher, even without extreme heat. In another scenario of 10 centimeters of precipitation and 20°C of temperature, the MRS value of 1.077 indicates that for every 1°C of temperature rise, 1.077 centimeters of compensatory precipitation is needed to maintain water level stability. By extension, the combined data of temperature and rainfall can all be substituted into Eq. (11) to derive the corresponding MRS values. The determination of such a threshold is of crucial importance. When key climatic factors reach this critical value (i.e., the threshold), it may trigger the system’s self-reinforcing feedback mechanism, leading to abrupt and large-scale qualitative changes in the system. Therefore, the conclusions drawn from this study can provide some support for the operation and management of reservoirs.

Conclusions

Main findings

In recent years, the installed capacity of hydropower has been continuously increasing, but a significant gap persists between power supply and demand. The current capacity level remains substantially below the target of 11.2 terawatts (TW) for global renewable energy installed capacity by 2030, as outlined in the International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA) report62. Against the background of the intensifying trend of climate warming, extreme weather events have occurred frequently63, and the uncertainty associated with hydropower development is also growing continuously. Studies have pointed out that the correlation between the increasingly severe hydrological extreme phenomena and climate patterns is continuously strengthening64. This correlation has not only increased the vulnerability of reservoirs but also significantly enhanced the climate sensitivity of key indicators such as reservoir discharge and reservoir water level65.

This study employs regression models, takes daily panel data of 17 reservoirs in Sichuan, China, as the research sample, and, through this analysis, finds that temperature and rainfall have a significant impact on reservoir water level. This finding is not an isolated phenomenon. Reservoirs in other regions of China are similarly impacted by climate change, for example, studies on Danjiangkou (in Hubei Province) show that climate is the main cause of the reduction in its inflow66. However, there is also regional heterogeneity in this impact. For instance, regarding Longyangxia Reservoir in Qinghai Province, the impact of precipitation is dominant in summer and autumn, while in winter and spring, it is mainly affected by temperature67,68. The overall inflow into the Three Gorges Reservoir exhibits a downward trend, with precipitation being the primary factor contributing to this phenomenon69. Nevertheless, this study argues that the impact of temperature is also important, which differs from that study.

Furthermore, this study also conducts a series of in-depth studies. By incorporating quadratic terms of variables into the base model, we find a nonlinear relationship between temperature on rainfall and the first-order difference of the reservoir level. Subsequently, the functional relationship between climate and changes in reservoir water level was derived. Analysis based on the climate-reservoir equation shows that MRS is jointly determined by the levels of temperature and rainfall. Furthermore, the trend in such MRS undergoes significant changes at specific thresholds, notably at a rainfall level of 17 centimeters and a temperature of 13.5°C. These findings fill gaps in the relevant research.

This study establishes a functional relationship that can significantly improve the accuracy of reservoir water level prediction under climate change and provide managers with a better decision-making basis. This study also identifies climate early warning thresholds and develops the MRS trend table (Table 8), which in turn provides support for managers to adjust strategies and avoid risks70. Notably, the 13.5°C temperature threshold is lower than expected, which indicates that micro-level climate changes may cause abnormal water levels and reminds managers to conduct close monitoring and take proactive responses. This study is expected to reduce the climate risks faced by reservoirs and provide scientific support for achieving climate-resilient reservoir management.

Limitations and future studies

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of climate change impacts on first-order differences of reservoir water level. However, due to data constraints, this study is limited to 17 reservoirs in Sichuan Province, China. This limitation restricts broader and more general findings. The incomplete sample limited seasonal comparisons. In addition, the limited daily reservoir data restricted control variable inclusion as well. Regarding methodology, our study omitted the introduction of a spatial analysis model, which can be included in future studies.

In future studies, the scope of analysis can be extended to other regions, and more generalizable work can be conducted using longer-term micro-level data covering wider geographical areas. Seasonal heterogeneity can also be explored, and more data can be collected to reduce omitted variable bias. Additionally, spatial econometric techniques can be leveraged to dissect the spatial spillover effects of climate impacts on adjacent reservoir systems. This approach will enable a more comparative assessment of the variability in outcomes across diverse spatial contexts. Finally, additional non-climate factors can be incorporated into the model as mechanism variables to delve deeper into how these non-climate factors influence the observed effect.
