Colpocephaly and corpus callosum dysgenesis in an adult: A rare case report
Authors: Soheil Mirzaei, Zahra Motaghed, Hooshmand Zarei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionColpocephaly, a midline anomaly, may be associated with agenesis of the corpus callosum. While prenatal diagnosis is possible, this malformation is rarely detected in adults and may be asymptomatic.Case presentationWe present a case of a 54-year-old male with Colpocephaly and dysgenesis of the corpus callosum, incidentally diagnosed during an emergency department visit. Computed tomography imaging revealed bilateral dilation of the posterior horns of the lateral ventricles and the absence of the corpus callosum.Clinical discussionAlthough some adults with Colpocephaly may show clinical symptoms, this anomaly can often be an incidental finding in asymptomatic individuals. Medical imaging plays a crucial role in the early diagnosis of this anomaly. Prenatal ultrasound can also detect midline anomalies, including Colpocephaly.ConclusionAwareness of this anomaly can prevent unnecessary diagnostic and therapeutic interventions.

Introduction

Colpocephaly is a rare congenital brain anomaly characterized by disproportionately enlarged posterior horns of the lateral ventricles. While it primarily manifests with neurological and developmental impairments in infancy, adult diagnosis is exceedingly rare. Indeed, as of 2019, only 8 cases have been reported in the literature [1,2]. The overall incidence of central nervous system (CNS) anomalies is approximately 1 % [3]. The prevalence of agenesis of corpus callosum (ACC) is estimated to be 0.3–0.7 % in the general population, but it is significantly higher, ranging from 2 to 3 %, in individuals with developmental disabilities. Because ACC is often asymptomatic, its exact prevalence is unknown [3,4]. The corpus callosum, the main midline structure composed of approximately 200 million axons [5], is one of the last brain structures to mature. Fully developed between the 18th and 20th weeks of gestation, its growth continues into the third decade of life [3]. Colpocephaly can result from heterogeneous neuroblast disorders in the early stages of development, CNS disorders, perinatal injuries, or intrauterine disturbances. Additionally, maternal exposure to corticosteroids during pregnancy, toxins, and toxoplasmosis infection is believed to lead to chromosomal abnormalities namely (trisomy 8 and 9) and perinatal anoxic-ischemic encephalopathy [6]. Asymptomatic colpocephaly, incidentally discovered in adults, typically does not require specific treatment. Symptomatic cases are managed based on the presenting symptom [1].

Case presentation

A 54-year-old Afghan man residing in Iran with no prior medical history presented to the emergency department due to the consumption of contaminated food. On the initial presentation, the patient had a SpO2 of 92 % and a blood pressure of 130/70 mmHg. The physical examination was notable for epigastric tenderness. Cardiovascular and pulmonary examinations were unremarkable. Routine laboratory investigations were within reference ranges, including a complete blood count and metabolic tests. A computed tomography (CT) scan of the brain revealed a 20 × 29 mm soft tissue mass with punctate calcifications in the left frontal and parietal region of the calvarium, suggestive of a pilar cyst. Ventriculomegaly, characterized by dilation of the third ventricle and posterior horns of the lateral ventricles, was demonstrated. The frontal horns of the lateral ventricles were parallel. ACC was noted. The colpocephalic index, calculated as the ratio of the maximal width of the posterior horns to the frontal horns, was greater than three, confirming the diagnosis of Colpocephaly (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Brain parenchyma demonstrated normal size, anatomy, and density for age. There was no evidence of hydrocephalus or midline shift. The cerebellum and posterior fossa structures appeared normal. A spiral CT scan of the chest revealed centrilobular nodules and ground-glass opacities in the posterior basal segments of the right and left lower lobes, which were consistent with aspiration pneumonia. Clinical discussion

Colpocephaly is a rare congenital brain malformation first described by Benda in 1940. Although Benda used the term ‘vesiculopathy,’ these cases are widely recognized as the first documented cases of colpocephaly. In this anomaly, the posterior horns of the lateral ventricles are disproportionately larger than the frontal horns. The abnormal ventricular enlargement in colpocephaly is believed to be secondary to an interruption of white matter formation during fetal development [2]. Colpocephaly is a nonspecific finding and can be associated with ACC, Chiari malformation, lissencephaly, and microcephaly. The cause may be a disturbance in neuronal migration during early brain development, perinatal injuries, or other CNS disorders [4]. Most cases of colpocephaly reported in the literature are typically diagnosed in infancy. Since the first adult case of colpocephaly was reported by Wunderlich et al. in 1996, a total of 13 cases have been reported in the literature [7]. Recent imaging studies of newborns and pregnant women have shown that ACC occurs in at least 1 in 4000 live births [8]. An X-linked or autosomal dominant inheritance pattern has also been proposed for this anomaly (trisomy 8 and 9) [9].

Radiologically, colpocephaly is characterized by enlargement of the posterior horns of lateral ventricles. It is often associated with complete or partial ACC. Measurement of the lateral ventricle diameters in cases of colpocephaly facilitates diagnosis. The most significant radiological finding in cases of colpocephaly is a posterior horn of the lateral ventricle greater than 20 mm. Another supportive finding in diagnosis is the measurement of other parts of the lateral ventricle less than 7 mm [4,6,10].

A review of the literature suggests that various factors may be associated with colpocephaly, including intrauterine infections, intrauterine growth restriction, perinatal anoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, and maternal drug use (corticosteroids, salbutamol, theophylline). There is also evidence of a genetic inheritance, either X-linked or autosomal dominant. Another possible explanation for the disproportionate enlargement of the posterior horns of the lateral ventricles is periventricular leukomalacia due to the destruction of the white matter of the occipital lobe [4]. The study by Nasrat et al. [11] presents a case of colpocephaly and ACC diagnosed in a 66-year-old African-American woman who did not experience any seizures or cognitive, motor, or sensory impairments. Sangwan et al. [9] reported a case of colpocephaly with partial ACC in a 30-year-old woman with no significant medical history. The patient presented after a syncopal episode with complaints of dizziness, vomiting, and intermittent chronic headaches. This case highlights the clinical and radiological features of colpocephaly in adults. The study by Tam et al. [12] is the first report of obsessive-compulsive personality disorder in a patient with ACC who also had concomitant cavum septum pellucidum and colpocephaly. The study by Güngör Aydın et al. [3] was the first in the literature to encounter ACC during an autopsy of an adult. This study suggests that ACC should be considered in individuals with poor academic performance.

Patients with dysgenesis or ACC may be asymptomatic, and the abnormality may be diagnosed only incidentally [4]. Adult patients often present with nonspecific symptoms such as headache, dizziness, seizures, vertigo, nausea, and motor abnormalities. The most important differential diagnosis for colpocephaly is normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH). Clinically, colpocephaly is associated with nonspecific symptoms, whereas NPH typically presents with a triad of dementia, gait disturbance, and urinary incontinence [9]. Asymptomatic adults in whom colpocephaly is incidentally diagnosed usually do not require treatment [4,6,9]. For symptomatic adult patients, treatment is symptomatic: the most common example is anti-seizure medications for patients presenting with seizures. The prognosis depends on the extent and severity of the abnormalities. There is no possibility of regeneration or degeneration of the corpus callosum [4]. For patients presenting with cognitive or neurological symptoms, a multidisciplinary approach involving neurology, occupational therapy, speech therapy, and physiotherapy can be beneficial [9]. The work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [13].

Conclusion

Currently, dysgenesis of the corpus callosum, colpocephaly, and, generally, holoprosencephaly disorders resulting from the failure of midline structures to separate can be diagnosed by prenatal ultrasounds between 18 and 22 weeks of gestation. However, rare cases may be identified in adulthood, either with or without clinical symptoms. Awareness of these abnormalities helps to avoid unnecessary diagnostic and therapeutic interventions.
