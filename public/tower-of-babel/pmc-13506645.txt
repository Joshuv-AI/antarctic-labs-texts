BroadSCOPE: An Optical Broadband Seismic Sensor for Planetary Exploration
Authors: Angela G Marusiak, Daniella N DellaGiustina, Bradley D Avenson, Donghwan Kim, Ronald-Louis Ballouz, Mehdi Benna, Carina A Bennett, Veronica J Bray, Dathon R Golish, Daniel Hofstadter, Eric Johnson, Ryan Martineau, Steven Meyer, Timothy Neilsen, Eric Reiss, Nicholas C Schmerr, Kevin J Walsh, Renee C Weber, Kris Zacny, S “Hop” Bailey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeismology is crucial for the geologic and interior exploration of planetary bodies. High signal-to-noise (SNR) instruments are required to record low amplitude seismic events such as deep moonquakes. Here we describe the Broadband Seismic Characterization using Optics for Planetary Exploration (“BroadSCOPE”) sensor, a broadband planetary seismometer built by Silicon Audio and the Lunar and Planetary Laboratory at the University of Arizona. Our laser interferometer design offers high sensitivity and a high tilt tolerance, while maintaining low power and mass. Our self-noise floor is targeted at 10−9ms−2/Hz at frequencies of about 1 Hz. We have performed environmental testing to reach a Technology Readiness Level of 6 for lunar conditions. This instrument was specifically developed for the Moon under NASA’s Development and Advancement of Lunar Instrumentation (DALI) program and matured by the Artemis Deployed Instruments opportunity. While designed for the Moon, the sensor could be used to explore other airless bodies such as binary asteroids.Keywords: Seismometer, Moon, Instruments, Lunar seismology

Introduction

Seismometers provide a fundamental means of probing the dynamics and internal structure of planetary bodies, from tectonic processes at the surface to structure deep within the core. Compared to Earth-based deployments, planetary seismology experiments face numerous challenges—primarily smaller, infrequent seismic events and fewer stations. The relative scarcity of available seismic sources requires planetary seismometers to be more sensitive to better record low environmental background noise in addition to seismic events. The constraints of mass, power and downlink for planetary missions, and the lack of human-led deployment, have limited the number of seismometers deployed onto planetary surfaces other than Earth. Fewer stations and the lack of seismic networks make it harder to detect and locate seismic events. Broadband (BB) seismometers are necessary to record low-amplitude, long period signals. However, these instruments must also operate at extreme environmental conditions, while maintaining low mass and low power requirements.

Here we describe the Broadband Seismic Characterization using Optics for Planetary Exploration (“BroadSCOPE”) sensor, a BB device tailored for exploration of the Moon and binary asteroids. A 3-axis seismic instrument could comprise a set of three orthogonal BroadSCOPE sensors in addition to the required electronics. The Moon was first instrumented with seismometers during the Apollo era (Nakamura et al. 1982) but the data quality was poor, in part due to limitations of the instruments. Asteroids, and small bodies in general, have not yet been seismically explored.

Our BroadSCOPE design is tailored for the exploration of the Moon and binary asteroids. The scientific motivation (Sect. 2) drives how we designed our sensor (Table 1) such that it maintains low mass and power requirements while allowing for a high tilt tolerance, and low self-noise floor (10−9ms−2/Hz at ∼1 Hz) over a broad frequency band to detect a wide variety of expected seismicity. We have performed environmental testing (Sect. 3), bringing the technology readiness level (TRL) to 6, allowing for flight-selectable capabilities. We discuss potential deployment strategies (Sect. 4), for the Moon, binary asteroids, and additional planetary bodies. While the sensor can operate on an airless body, it does not require a vacuum to operate, thus expanding our potential deployments to bodies such as Mars and Titan.

Instrument Design and Motivation

The University of Arizona’s Lunar and Planetary Laboratory (UA’s LPL) and Silicon Audio (SiA) partnered in 2016 to produce flight-selectable seismometers (Fig. 1, Table 1). This effort began with development of a short-period “ShortSCOPE” sensor using a commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) Silicon Audio product as the basis (DellaGiustina et al. 2026). Building on that maturation, we subsequently initiated the development of a broadband (BB) instrument, incorporating lessons learned from the ShortSCOPE development. The BroadSCOPE is designed to be more sensitive to longer-period signals, while the ShortSCOPE is designed to be sensitive to high-frequency signals. The BroadSCOPE has a flat response and low noise floor over a frequency band of 0.052–10 Hz recording ground motion in velocity (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21924352). The BroadSCOPE is designed to get the noise floor lower at lower frequencies while flattening the response near 1 Hz and trades higher-frequency noise performance for improved low-frequency performance. In contrast the ShortSCOPE operates as an accelerometer and is designed to have its minimum self-noise at a higher frequency (∼10−9ms−2/Hz at 10 Hz) with a flat response from 0.0095–150 Hz (DellaGiustina et al. 2026). The BroadSCOPE and ShortSCOPE sensors share a common architecture, comprising separate optical and mechanical stages (Fig. 1), described in detail in DellaGiustina et al. (2026). The two instruments can complement each other during planetary deployments by being sensitive across a broader range of frequencies.

(a) CAD drawing of the BroadSCOPE sensor in cross-sectional view. The different colors indicate different components, (b) Photo of sensor in the same orientation as the CAD drawing

The BroadSCOPE represents a significant improvement over mid-period Apollo sensors, especially at frequencies higher than about 1 Hz. While the Apollo Mid-Period (MP) sensors did have a lower noise floor at longer periods, the Apollo sensors were more massive and with lower tilt tolerances. Additionally, the Apollo sensors recorded with poor bit depth (10 bits compared to the 24- and 32-bit capability of BroadSCOPE) and lower sampling rates. Although BroadSCOPE is designed to be sensitive at long periods, its self-noise floor is lower than the self-noise of the Apollo short-period and geophone instruments across all frequency bands.

Science Motivation

The anticipated noise floors of the Moon (Nunn et al. 2020) and asteroids (DellaGiustina et al. 2024; Ballouz et al. 2024) are much lower than that of Earth and require low noise BB instruments. Planetary seismometers must also have low mass, volume, and power, and ideally a high tilt tolerance, to make deployments easier. In the following sections, we show how each target body has motivated our design principles.

The Moon

In recent years, NASA has begun working with commercial partners to send instruments to the surface of the Moon through the Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) and Payloads and Research Investigations on the Surface of the Moon (PRISM) programs. NASA is also sending humans back to the Moon as part of the Artemis program. The Farside Seismic Suite (FSS) (Panning et al. 2021) has been selected for the CLPS/PRISM program, and Lunar Environment Monitoring Station (LEMS) and the South Pole Seismic Station (SPSS) (Panning et al. 2026) have been selected as candidate payloads for Artemis. Lastly, the two most recent planetary decadal surveys (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering and Medicine 2011, 2022) and committee on astrobiology and planetary science (CAPS) (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering and Medicine 2025) also indicated that a Lunar Geophysical Network (LGN) would be a candidate for a New Frontiers–class mission. Building a global seismic network on the Moon through New Frontiers and LGN, or piecemeal through Artemis and CLPS, has been cited as a priority by the lunar science community (Lunar Exploration Roadmap Steering Committee 2016; Noble et al. 2024). Future missions will build off the legacy of the Apollo seismic experiments.

The Apollo-era seismic experiments were successful in achieving several science goals including constraining the modern-day impact rate (Lammlein 1977), understanding the crustal structures beneath landing sites (Hartmann 1973; Kovach and Watkins 1973; Cooper et al. 1974; Lammlein et al. 1974), and confirming that the Moon is differentiated (Lammlein et al. 1974; Goins et al. 1981; Nakamura 1983). However, there are several outstanding science questions due to the limitations of the instruments and by the Apollo stations’ geographic locations (all within 30° of the equator and all on the near side). The location bias has limited our knowledge of the polar and farside regions. Due to the low Apollo Analog-to-Digital (ADC) bit depth, inconsistent sampling rates, and scattering effects of the lunar regolith, the Apollo seismograms appear blocky and are hard to interpret (Fig. 2).

Our instrument offers substantial improvement over the Apollo design and has been developed under several NASA programs, including the Development and Advancement of Lunar Instrumentation (DALI), Maturation of Instruments for Solar System Exploration (MatISSE), and the Artemis III Deployed Instruments opportunity (A3DI). Our instrument can be paired with a 24- or 32-bit ADC, versus the 10-bit Apollo ADC. This higher bit depth makes it possible to better resolve small ground motions and thus more precisely determine the arrival and peak amplitudes of body waves. A maximum sampling rate of 1000 samples per second (sps) could be used, but for most planetary deployments with limited data storage and/or transmission rates, a sampling rate of 25 sps would capture even the highest-frequency events, such as thermal moonquakes (Civilini et al. 2023). Seismograms from our instrument will be easier to read and interpret, translating to improvements in detection, location, arrival time selection, and frequency-content analysis (e.g., surface wave analysis).

One of the most common types of seismic events observed on the Moon are deep moonquakes (DMQs). These seismic events occur deep within the lunar mantle and are linked to the tidal cycle (Goulty 1979; Bulow et al. 2007). DMQs are typically observed on the near side of the Moon. One possible explanation is that the near-side locations of the seismic stations prevented the observation of DMQs originating from the farside. A second possibility is that the crustal dichotomy between the Moon’s near and farside (Jolliff et al. 2000; Nakamura 2005) extends deeper, into the mantle and core, which prevents or reduces the occurrence of DMQs on the farside.

DMQs could also be used to determine whether a partially melted layer exists above a liquid outer core, as theorized by Weber et al. 2011. To detect DMQs, an instrument needs to have a low noise floor, especially at lower frequencies (<0.5 Hz) (Fig. 3). Seismometers sent to the farside, or the polar regions, would make it possible to differentiate between these two hypotheses. The South Pole of the Moon is the target of recently selected lunar missions (e.g., LEMS (Benna et al. 2024), FSS (Panning et al. 2021), SPSS (Panning et al. 2026)) and is of high interest to the lunar science community due to the possibility of icy deposits (Rubanenko et al. 2019; Siegler et al. 2022) and the large impact basin (Spudis et al. 2008). Our BroadSCOPE instrument would be able to detect DMQs, shallow events, impacts, and thermal moonquakes. The recorded events would provide necessary data to build a seismic catalog, investigate local and deep interior structure, assess heterogeneities between regions (polar vs. equatorial, near vs. farside), and quantify seismic hazards (Table 2). Many of these goals have been cited in Science Traceability Matrices (STMs) for the Moon (Marusiak et al. 2023; Neal et al. 2020; Schmerr et al. 2024; Weber et al. 2021).

Asteroids

Data from a seismometer deployed on an asteroid would be able to answer key questions from the most recent decadal survey (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering and Medicine 2022), including (1) how and when did asteroids form, (2) how have planetary bodies evolved, (3) how do impacts disrupt planetary bodies, and (4) how have surface characteristics and compositions of solid bodies been modified.

Improved understanding of asteroid interiors would also fill an important knowledge gap for planetary defense. The NASA Planetary Defense Coordination Office (PDCO) is tasked with planning a response to an asteroid impact threat, which is important due to the hazards that the population of near-Earth asteroids (NEAs) pose to Earth. There are several mitigation strategies, such as deflection with a kinetic impactor, whose outcomes depend on the porosity, strength, and internal structure of the asteroid (Bray et al. 2025). Characterization of asteroid interiors is therefore paramount to properly assess and mitigate the hazard to Earth from NEAs. A seismometer is uniquely poised to reveal interior properties and structure.

Although there have been several missions to study asteroids, none have been able to directly observe the interior of an asteroid. As detailed in DellaGiustina et al. (2024), it is likely asteroids fall within three broad categories for internal structure determined by their porosity-strength phase space: monoliths, fractured shards, and rubble piles (Asphaug and Scheeres 1999; Richardson et al. 2002; Britt and Consolmagno 2003). Monoliths, such as Vesta and Ceres, have high strength and low porosity. Fractured shards have low strength and low porosity and have experienced stresses resulting in large-scale fractures or faults. Examples of proposed fractured shards include Eros, Ida, and Phobos (Thomas et al. 1996; Prockter et al. 2002; Andert et al. 2010). Rubble piles have high porosity and low strength and may have formed from gravitationally re-accumulated materials after a catastrophic collision of a parent body (e.g., Michel et al. 2020; Walsh 2018). Spacecraft observations of the NEAs Itokawa, Ryugu, and Bennu suggest that they are rubble piles (Fujiwara et al. (2006), Watanabe et al. (2019), DellaGiustina et al. (2019)). Small NEAs are most likely to be rubble piles based on their kilometer-scale sizes (Walsh 2018) and canonical theories for NEA binary formation (Margot et al. 2015; Walsh and Jacobson 2015). Recent analysis of NEAR-Shoemaker data may also suggest even larger NEAs may have rubble-pile-like interiors, as well (Ballouz et al. 2025).

To elucidate the internal structure of an asteroid, a seismic experiment would need to either carry an active source or rely on passive seismicity. Seismicity of asteroids has been primarily studied in the context of impact events. A lower-than-expected abundance of small (<200 m) craters has been observed on smaller rubble-pile asteroids, including by the first asteroid rendezvous mission, NEAR (Near Earth Asteroid Rendezvous) Shoemaker (Chapman 2002; Robinson et al. 2002). The small size of NEAs (<20 km diameter) precludes endogenic processes as a means of surface deformation. Therefore, another mechanism must selectively eliminate small craters. Impact-induced surface waves could explain the dearth of small craters, as well as their geographic distribution, on Eros (Ballouz et al. 2025), which suggests that it too may be a rubble pile, contrary to prior inferences. Resolving these contradictory surface observations would allow for a more comprehensive interpretation of surface images of asteroids, which are often used to interpret their complex geologic and dynamical histories.

As on the Moon, tidally induced seismicity could be a potential source on asteroids, such as (99942) Apophis, that have close encounters with Earth (Ballouz et al. 2024). Around 15% of NEAs are expected to be in a binary configuration. Small asteroid binaries (D <10 km) tend to have satellites that are 20–50% the size of the primary bodies, orbit within the distance of 2–5 primary radii (Margot et al. 2015; Walsh and Jacobson 2015), and have orbital periods from a few hours to Earth days. The rotation periods of the primary bodies tend to be fast, around 2–4 hours. Although the masses of the asteroids are much smaller than the Earth-Moon system, the small orbital distance of the secondary means the tidal acceleration can be higher and the resulting ground motion could be detectable by sensitive BB instruments (DellaGiustina et al. 2024), such as the one proposed here (Fig. 3). By studying the seismicity of binaries, we can more precisely measure the tidally induced surface displacements, as a function of material properties and internal structure.

Design Principles

Like the ShortSCOPE sensor, the BroadSCOPE measures displacement of the proof mass inside the sensor as it responds to ground motion. We use a miniaturized laser interferometer and apply a technique similar to the Laser Interferometer Gravity wave Observatory (LIGO) (Martynov et al. 2016) using force-balancing scheme. This is implemented within the BroadSCOPE sensor using an optical and mechanical stage. The optical stage contains the printed circuit board (“optics board”) and mounting structure for the lens–laser assembly and photodiodes, which detect intensity changes from the interferometer. The mechanical stage holds the movable proof mass and the grating–mirror assembly. The common Silicon Audio optical readout, force-feedback architecture, and optical and mechanical stages are described in detail by DellaGiustina et al. (2026); here, we focus on the broadband-specific proof mass, suspension, and response characteristics.

During the sensor design process, we followed three governing equations for force-balanced seismometers (Wielandt and Streckeisen 1982):

Equation (1) states that the seismic acceleration (Δg) is a function of the displacement resolution (Δx) and the free period (T0). Equation (2) governs the resonant frequency (ω0), which is inversely related to the electronic integration constant (τ) and the damping constants (RC). Equation (3) governs the Brownian motion of acceleration (x¨), which is inversely related to the proof mass (M), the free period, and the quality factor (Q) of the suspension system. Note that here, the quality factor refers to intrinsic properties of the sensor. It is not the same quality factor Q that is used in seismology to describe the inverse of attenuation in a medium through which waves travel.

We designed our instrument so that it would have less mass and substantially larger tilt tolerance than other broadband instruments such as InSight’s VBB (Lognonné et al. 2019) (Table 1). Our sensor uses interferometric measurement with a Δx of 10−15m/Hz which is orders of magnitude better than capacitive measurements. Our low Δx allows for a greater trade space of T0 to achieve the desired sensitivity.

The resonant frequency (Equation (2)) is controlled by the proof mass and spring stiffness such that softer springs will lower the resonant frequency, allowing for better detection of long-period signals. We used beryllium copper (BeCu) for leaf springs that shift the resonant frequency while maintaining quality factors and tilt tolerance. A lower resonant frequency further reduces the requirements for detection and electronics noise. Our Brownian noise floor (Equation (3)) is orders of magnitude lower than those of micro-electromechanical (MEMS) devices due to our larger proof mass (∼220 grams). The tilt tolerance of our instrument, 15°, is far greater than that of the InSight VBB (0.25°) (Lognonné et al. 2019) and requires no leveling mechanism, thereby reducing the overall mass. Over a frequency range of 0.05–10 Hz, our sensor has a sensitivity of 10,000V/(ms−1), with a clip level at +/−0.55 mm/s (Fig. 4). This displacement-sensitive interferometer coupled with the coil-magnet actuator affords a large (164 dB without an ADC) dynamic range.

We plot our instrument sensitivity against frequency. Our BroadSCOPE is designed to be most sensitive at frequencies below 10 Hz

To accommodate twisting of the larger proof mass in a dynamic environment (e.g., during launch), we include anti-rotation tabs, akin to the shock stops used in the ShortSCOPE sensor (DellaGiustina et al. 2026). These tabs help prevent the proof mass from being rotated and subsequently placing stress on the leaf springs or causing misalignment. However, there is a tradeoff. The tabs allow the sensor to survive larger dynamic loads but could restrict the lower-limit operating temperature of the instrument due to the potential for contact between the anti-rotation tabs and spring mount. To accommodate the colder thermal environment expected at the lunar south pole, the design can be adapted by unifying the material selection across the assembly—especially for the anti-rotation tabs and inner spring mount—to reduce coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) mismatch and resultant stresses. An alternative approach is to omit the anti-rotation tabs in the build. However, this configuration would likely require soft stowage during launch and transit, similar to the handling requirements for payloads transported to the International Space Station (ISS) (International Space Station Program 2015).

Environmental Testing

To reach a Technology Readiness Level (TRL) of 6 (Hirshorn and Jefferies 2016), we performed dynamic and thermal-vacuum testing. We targeted TRL 6 testing because this level is generally considered suitable for submission to instrument calls such as PRISM or Artemis Deployed Instruments programs. A level of TRL 6 is achieved when the system (or subsystem) prototype is functional in a relevant environment. Our relevant environment includes simulated launch for dynamics testing, a vacuum-like pressure for an airless body environment, and temperatures anticipated in the lunar or asteroid near-surface. We considered the test “passed” if the resonant frequency remained within 5% of the starting value (6.0 Hz). The modulation efficiency was required to remain at or above ∼40% even with a 15° tilt. The quality factor Q was allowed to vary but needed to show proper signal damping, meaning the strength of the signal decreases over time in such a way to avoid either prolonged oscillations or overly constrained oscillations (Fig. 5). The pre- and post-environmental functional checkouts and the GEVS-based dynamic-test approach followed the procedures used for the companion ShortSCOPE qualification campaign (DellaGiustina et al. 2026). Vibration loads were obtained from the NASA Goddard Space Flight Center General Environmental Verification Standard (GSFC 2021) (Fig. 6). This standard specifies random vibration levels in excess of those of the NASA Space Launch System (SLS), the United Launch Alliance (ULA) Vulcan, or the SpaceX Starship. The qualification random profile was applied along each orthogonal axis for a duration of 1 minute per axis. For shock testing, a 50g 1.0 ms half-sine pulse was applied along each orthogonal positive and negative axis for a total of 6 pulses.

Thermal Vacuum

Thermal vacuum (TVAC) testing was conducted at University of Arizona (UA)’s Lunar and Planetary Laboratory (LPL). The environmental conditions mimic those of a lunar environment. We performed two cycles over 360 hours. The BroadSCOPE has a previously known operating temperature range of at least −40 to +50 °C from initial testing and development of sensor components. After the initial TVAC test of the BroadSCOPE sensor was completed, we performed an additional exploratory cold cycle to see the lowest temperatures at which the sensor could operate.

Near the South Pole of the Moon, surface temperatures are expected to range from −223 to +73 °C. At the equator, the mean high and low surface temperatures are −178 to +119.3 °C (Williams et al. 2017). Any equipment sent to the Moon would have some thermal shielding, such as the thermal blankets and heaters used during the Apollo-era (Latham et al. 1970). Burial of the seismometer would also reduce the range of temperatures experienced by our instrument. Heat flow measurements show that by a burial depth of 10 cm, the temperature of the regolith becomes more consistent (Langseth and Keihm 1974; Formisano et al. 2024), and by 0.5 m depth, the diurnal temperature range is reduced to −55–75 °C (Hayne et al. 2017). We use an initial range of −55 to 70 °C because our instruments would likely be buried or at least have thermal shielding. This temperature range coincides with that of other TVAC tests performed to mimic the lunar environment (e.g. Pike et al. 2018; Nunn et al. 2021; Burbridge et al. 2023).

The rate of temperature change is not consistent over the Moon’s diurnal cycle. To mimic the harshest conditions, we use the largest expected change in temperature of ∼130 °C, which occurs on the Moon between local hours 6 and 7 (Williams et al. 2017). This corresponds to roughly a 5 °C change per hour, or ∼ 0.1 °C per minute; thus, the sensor goes from the coldest to the hottest temperatures in about 15 hours. The duration of our TVAC test was designed to mimic a lunar deployment through Artemis. It starts in the lunar day and proceeds through a lunar night before repeating the cycle a second time. By placing multiple thermocouples in our chamber and on our sensor, we found that our sensor reached thermal equilibrium with chamber temperature within an hour. We set our dwell times to 36 hours for both lunar day-night cycles. This time allowed the sensor to reach equilibrium and enabled us to perform several tests over each simulated day-night cycle (Fig. 7).

Temperature profile for TVAC testing started on April 23, 2024. Blue circles indicate points when the sensor’s performance was tested

All tests were performed in vacuum to simulate the lunar environment. We initially baked out our sensor to reduce outgassing and pumped down the TVAC chamber to reach an ambient pressure of ∼1×10−6 torr. While the surface of the Moon is at vacuum, our instrument will be buried in the regolith or covered by thermal blankets; thus, they may not experience true vacuum. For this reason, we find 1×10−6 torr to be sufficient to approximate lunar conditions. We performed several optical and mechanical sweeps throughout the test to ensure that the sensor’s performance was consistent throughout the TVAC testing. The quality factor was also measured, but due to coupling conditions inside the TVAC chamber, it was consistently lower than when tested outside the chamber.

Over two weeks in April 2024, we found that our sensor could withstand temperatures up to 70 °C and down to −55 °C. Even at −55 °C, our sensor’s performance was not degraded by cold temperatures, suggesting that it may be able to survive even colder temperatures than we initially tested (Table 3). After the test was completed, we noted that the optical modulation efficiency was temperature dependent, suggesting that the parallelism between the mirror and grating is reduced as the seismometer temperature increases or decreases from room temperature. Post-TVAC inspection revealed that anti-rotation tabs had broken at some point during environmental testing (e.g., shock or vibration testing) which did not significantly impact the sensor performance since the broken tab was ejected. However, the failure mode would pose a risk, i.e., foreign object debris (FOD), if selected for flight. The failure was addressed by changing the material of the anti-rotation / shock-stop rings to ULTEM 2300, which provides higher impact strength compared to the original material. The anti-rotation tab was also re-designed to avoid stress concentration.

Another test consisting of a single cold plunge was performed with a more gradual descent to colder temperatures, including 30–60-minute pauses allowing for equilibration. During this test, the optics board stayed warmer than initially anticipated. The experiment was briefly stopped so that the thermistor placement could be examined to ensure that the temperatures were being measured correctly and that the instrument was mounted properly. A cold plunge brought the cold plate and housing down to −150 °C and then briefly to −170 °C. The optics board did become colder than in the first two cold plunges but only reached a temperature of −150 °C. It is likely that the internal electronics and laser keep the optics board warmer than the housing. Below temperatures of −130 °C, the power to the laser was boosted to continue optimal functionality. The ability of our sensor to change the current driving the laser—boosting it at low temperatures and turning off the boost at higher ones—allows it to operate at a wider temperature range.

Discussion

Deployment Schemes

Previous studies (Marusiak et al. 2020, 2021a) have shown that instruments can better measure environmental noise floors when they are buried and decoupled from landers. The Artemis program uniquely provides an opportunity for our instruments to be buried in a shallow trench on the Moon. This will limit thermal noise from large temperature fluctuations and reduce the effects of the scattering layer in the near-surface. The crew will be able to hand-pick a suitable spot to deploy the sensor, ensuring that its orientation, tilt, and precise location are known. Risks of a lander malfunctioning (e.g., the Viking seismometer failing to uncage (Anderson et al. 1976)) or toppling over are eliminated.

For robotic deployments on airless bodies such as the Moon or asteroids, lander-mounted or lander-adjacent placements would still be acceptable given that the main source of noise from a lander is often caused by wind (Panning et al. 2020). It may be possible for a robotic lander to bury the seismometer using a pneumatic device (Sanigepalli et al. 2024), or to place the instrument on the surface with a wind and thermal shield, akin to the deployment mechanisms of InSight (Lognonné et al. 2019) and Dragonfly (Barnes et al. 2021). Field tests (Marusiak et al. 2021a) showed that in the absence of wind and with dampened thermal effects, the lander resonance frequency can be measured and filtered out of seismic signals. A lander-mounted deployment would still likely be able to achieve many of the science goals discussed above. The BroadSCOPE could be considered for the seismic payload of the LGN (Neal et al. 2020), future PRISM deployments, or a mission to an asteroid.

Analog-to-Digital Converter Pairing

We developed a low-noise, 32-bit ADC module which would allow us to record low-amplitude signals with higher precision and a wider dynamic range than a 24-bit ADC. The system comprises an FPGA, an ADC board, and a small, custom board to house flash memory. The SNR of the ADC is 124 dB at 1000 sps and can support sampling rates to match our sensor. The ADC module supports sampling rates of 1000, 500, 250, 200, 100, 62.5, 50, and 20 sps, and implements finite-impulse-response filters to prevent aliasing of high-frequency signals into the downsampled data during decimation. These sampling rates would be able to capture even high-frequency events such as thermal moonquakes. High sampling rates coupled with low noise would provide a higher science return. By pairing our BroadSCOPE with the developed digitization package we can offer a complete instrument package. Furthermore, testing at Space Dynamics Lab (SDL) has confirmed the ADC could survive up to 50 krad total ionizing dose and operate between −55 and +125 °C, ranges that would allow for lunar and NEA deployments.

Other Potential Science Targets

Although we focused here on the Moon and asteroids, the BroadSCOPE sensor could achieve science goals on other bodies. The planetary science community would benefit from seismic exploration of icy ocean worlds (Marusiak et al. 2021b). Titan is the target of the confirmed mission Dragonfly (Barnes et al. 2021), which will carry geophones and a short-period sensor. Concept missions to Europa (Hand et al. 2017) and Enceladus (Mackenzie et al. 2021) might carry seismometers as part of the lander payloads. Seismic data would be used to determine the thickness of the ice shells and ocean, including lateral variations, and investigate the deeper rocky interior. The surfaces of these bodies are colder than our TVAC tests, but heaters could be used to keep the seismometers operational. In the case of Europa, our instrument would likely be contained in a vault to protect it from the harsh radiation environment. This would somewhat stabilize the temperature fluctuations expected for instrumentation within a lander vault (Hand et al. 2017), bringing them closer to our already performed environmental testing conditions. Our high tilt tolerance would reduce mission risk as we can still operate and record high-quality data even if the lander becomes tilted on uneven ground. The surface gravity of Europa is similar to the Moon; consequently there are no required changes to the optics stage. Wind shields would not be required for Europa or Enceladus but would be required on Titan, which has a thick atmosphere and can have active winds and storms (Tomasko et al. 2005; Hueso and Sánchez-Lavega 2006; Atreya et al. 2006; Schaller et al. 2009; Hayes et al. 2013). On Titan, the presence of methane clathrates may hinder the detection of some events, as methane clathrates increase attenuation in the ice shell (Marusiak et al. 2022a; Bryant et al. 2024). Icy-ocean worlds likely have tidally driven seismicity, suggesting they may be more active than the Moon (Hurford et al. 2020). While the larger cracking events would be detectable with our sensor, studies have suggested that the environmental noise floor of Europa will be lower than most seismic instrumentation, including our own (Panning et al. 2018). In addition to surface events, bodies like Europa may be capable of generating quakes in the silicate interior, akin to deep moonquakes (Pou et al. 2024). It is worth noting, however, that the presence of a subsurface ocean, or thick ductile crust, would dampen signals, allowing only large magnitude events to be detectable (Marusiak et al. 2022b). These events would have longer period energies, making our instrument well suited for their detection.

Our sensor could also be considered for future Martian missions. Martian seismicity is an order of magnitude larger than the shallow moonquake seismicity of the Moon (Banerdt et al. 2020), providing more chances to record seismic events. Like InSight, our sensor would benefit from a wind and thermal shield to reduce atmospheric noise. Placement on the Martian surface would be required as wind noise would shake a lander-deck and hinder seismic data interpretations (Panning et al. 2020). Unlike InSight, we would not require a leveling mechanism, which would reduce mass and some risks for robotic deployments. In the current configuration, our sensor would not be able to measure the Martian environmental noise floor (Lognonné et al. 2020), but would still be able to detect numerous events. While the sensor is designed for the lunar gravity (1.62 m/s2), the sensor design can adapt for higher gravity, (e.g., Mars 3.721 m/s2) by simply adjusting the distance between the mirror and grating (DellaGiustina et al. 2026). However, the higher gravity will lead to a lower tilt tolerance since the proof mass would be displaced further from its 0°-tilt position at the same tilt angle. Seismic data from a new landing site would allow us to explore regions with higher rates of seismicity like Cerberus Fossae (Ceylan et al. 2023) and further investigate the crustal dichotomy of Mars.

Conclusion

We designed a broadband seismic sensor, the BroadSCOPE, to achieve several science goals for the exploration of our Moon and binary asteroids, such as cataloging seismic events, determining interior structure, and assessing seismic hazards. We show that our sensor passes random vibration testing using the GEVS protocol and survived shocks of 50g. Our instrument is capable of operating in temperatures of −55 to +70 °C and could survive when housing temperatures were down to −170 °C when the anti-rotation tabs are removed. The noise floor of the BroadSCOPE (10−9ms−2/Hz at 1 Hz) is sufficient to detect various lunar seismic events, including deep, shallow, impact-driven, and thermal moonquakes. Our instrument has been developed for the Moon under DALI, adapted for asteroids by MatISSE, and the technology was further matured by the Artemis III Deployed Instruments program. It could eventually be deployed by future landed lunar crews or as part of the Lunar Geophysical Network. The BroadSCOPE would also be able to detect the predicted seismicity of a binary asteroid system and could be suitable for missions to icy ocean worlds and Mars. By maintaining low noise floors, along with low mass and power, and a high tilt tolerance, the BroadSCOPE is an ideal instrument for planetary exploration.
