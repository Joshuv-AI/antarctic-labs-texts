From Ethnic Herbal Medicine to Unani Traditions: Medicinal Plant Knowledge across Southwestern China and Northern Pakistan
Authors: Gao Jian, Xu Ziheng, Qu Qiumei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEthnic herbal medicine originated from the medical knowledge accumulated by ethnic groups in their struggle against disease and harsh environments; the history of each ethnic group’s development through long-term coexistence and competition with nature is, in itself, a history of medicine. In northern Pakistan, herbal plants thrive and are readily available, and medicinal plants exhibit an exceptionally rich biodiversity. Yunnan’s unique natural environment and biodiversity have fostered a wealth of ethnic medicine and cultural resources, making it a treasure trove of global medicine and culture. There is significant potential for cooperation between Yunnan’s border ethnic regions and northern Pakistan in areas such as pharmaceutical research and development, joint construction of pharmaceutical plants, training of medical professionals, and the exchange of medicinal plant cultivation techniques. This topic was chosen to explore the similarities and cultural significance of traditional medicinal plant knowledge between the ethnic herbal medicine systems of Southwestern China and the Unani medical traditions of Northern Pakistan.KEYWORDS: Southwest China, Northern Pakistan, Ethnic Herbal Medicine, The Unani Medical Tradition, Exchange of Medical Knowledge

INTRODUCTION

A total of 6,559 species of natural plant and animal medicinal resources have been identified in Yunnan, with 4,000 to 5,000 species suitable for medicinal use,1 Only 600 to 700 plant species in Pakistan are used for medicinal purposes.2

Yunnan and Pakistan have extensively engaged in exchanges and cooperation across various fields, including traditional medicine technology, medical education, and the training of medical professionals. This not only fosters mutual understanding and trust but also helps Pakistan cultivate talent and enhance its capacity for scientific and technological innovation, thereby strengthening its own development capabilities. Furthermore, it facilitates joint efforts with Pakistan to advance the development of the traditional medicine industry, explore and develop medical resources, and preserve and pass down traditional medical texts, while jointly promoting the construction and development of ethnic regions and maintaining regional harmony and stability. One limitation of this study is that the documentation of ethnobotanical data vary across regions, which may affect the comprehensiveness and comparability of the findings.

METHODOLOGY

This paper employs methods such as literature review, ethnobotanical investigation, and comparative analysis. Although there is an abundance of quantitative study on this issue, there are still few qualitative studies on related topics. As a result, the main source of information for this study is a review of the literature, which includes regional surveys of medicinal plants, ancient medical writings, and ethnobotanical investigations. Ethnobotanical techniques, cross-cultural analytical frameworks, disease classification assessments, and comparisons of medicinal plant applications are all examined using a comparative analytical approach.

The Same Plant Treats Different Diseases:

Ancient Chinese scholars were among the first to provide a comprehensive record of the medicinal uses of willow bark. The *Shennong Bencao Jing* states that the roots, bark, branches, and leaves of the willow tree can all be used medicinally; they have the effects of expelling phlegm, improving vision, clearing heat, detoxifying, promoting urination, and warding off wind. When applied topically, they can treat toothaches. The *Compendium of Materia Medica* states that willow leaves, when decocted, can treat internal bleeding in the chest and abdomen, relieve pain, and cure scabies; willow branches and root bark, when boiled in wine, can be used as a mouthwash for toothaches, and when decocted and taken orally, can treat jaundice and leukorrhea; willow catkins can stop bleeding, treat spleen dampness, and relieve spasms in the limbs. The *Compendium of Chinese Herbal Medicines* describes foxglove as a cardiac stimulant. Its primary effects include stimulating the heart muscle, increasing myocardial contractility, significantly enhancing stroke volume, and improving blood circulation. It also has a diuretic effect in patients with cardiac edema.

A Cross-Cultural Perspective on Traditional Medicine

The Influence of Traditional Ethnic Medicine:

Ethnic medicine is emerging as a vital source and focal point for innovative drug research worldwide.8

Ethnic medicine is an important public welfare undertaking and the most distinctive pillar industry in ethnic regions. For a long time, the Party and the government have attached great importance to the development of healthcare and the ethnic medicine industry in ethnic regions, formulating and implementing a series of policies and regulations that have enabled significant progress in these sectors. The Yunnan Provincial Government has successively issued a series of policies and regulations, including the “Yunnan Provincial Regulations on Traditional Chinese Medicine,” the “Implementation Opinions on the Implementation of the Strategic Plan for the Development of Traditional Chinese Medicine (2016–2030) in Yunnan Province,” and the “13th Five-Year Plan for the Development of Traditional Chinese Medicine.”

The Influence of Unani Medicine and Islamic Medicine:

In Unani medicine, medicines and foods are also classified according to their temperaments, as they have different chemical compositions. The temperament of the human body is determined by the “humors” (that is, the four bodily fluids), which are themselves composed of different elements.9

From a cross-cultural perspective, although traditional ethnic medicine in Southwest China and Unani traditional medicine in northern Pakistan originated from different civilizational systems, they share distinct “common logics” in terms of knowledge structures, conceptions of nature, and health philosophies, while also exhibiting their own unique developmental trajectories.

These common features are manifested in the following ways: both reject the “single-cause theory” of disease and emphasize systemic balance; both stress the vital role of the natural state in health; both belong to “empirical medical traditions” at varying stages of development; and both represent low-cost yet viable forms of healthcare. In addition, traditional medicine of the ethnic groups in Southwest China and Unani traditional medicine in northern Pakistan share distinct similarities in the “integration of spirituality and medicine.”

Of course, structural differences also exist:

Cultural Ties Between Chinese and Pakistani Traditional Medicine-

Traditional medicine plays a unique role as a cultural bridge in the construction of the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor.

First, they can effectively promote cultural exchange and mutual understanding, enhance cultural identity within the “China-Pakistan Economic Corridor,” and create a more harmonious and diverse cultural environment.

Second, they contribute to the development and innovation of traditional medicine itself. The opening up and cooperative exchange of traditional medicine can revitalize the field, creating an interactive platform where traditional medicine from China and Pakistan can learn from, absorb, complement, and refine one another, thereby bringing more opportunities for development and innovation. At the same time, it can effectively promote the intrinsic connection and integration of traditional medicine in both countries, driving the industrialization and internationalization of traditional medicine, and further enhancing its strength and influence.

Third, The peoples of China and Pakistan enjoy a very friendly relationship. On issues such as developing new indigenous medicines, introducing and cultivating valuable medicinal and aromatic plants, establishing quality standards for herbal materials and plant-based preparations as soon as possible, strengthening research on herbal preparations, and better integrating traditional and modern medicine to promote the development of national healthcare and the pharmaceutical industry, Pakistani counterparts look forward to receiving assistance from their Chinese counterparts and engaging in broader and deeper cooperation.

CONCLUSION

This study found that although traditional medicine in Southwest China and Unani traditional medicine in northern Pakistan originated from different civilizational systems and share common characteristics as well as structural differences in certain aspects, there remains significant room for collaboration between the two.

How Pakistan can better integrate its traditional medicine with modern medicine, and engage in broader and deeper cooperation with China, has become a key focus for future medical collaboration between China and Pakistan.
