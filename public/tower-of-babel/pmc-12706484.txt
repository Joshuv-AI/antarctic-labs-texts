Transient neuromonitoring changes and delayed vertebrobasilar infarction following anterior cervical discectomy and fusion: illustrative case
Authors: Jacob M. Chaisson, Jack A. Leoni, Ellery A. Hayden, Gabriel C. Tender, Amit K. Bhandutia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDAnterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is a common spinal procedure with a well-established safety profile. While direct vertebral artery injury is rare and typically recognized intraoperatively, delayed posterior circulation stroke is an even less common but major complication. In patients with risk factors for vascular disease, vascular stress from surgical manipulation may predispose them to thromboembolic events that are not immediately apparent. Transient intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM) changes may represent early, underrecognized indicators of such vascular compromise.OBSERVATIONSThe authors present the case of a 61-year-old male who underwent elective three-level ACDF for degenerative cervical myelopathy. During cage placement, transient right-sided somatosensory evoked potential signal attenuation occurred, while other IONM signaling remained stable. On postoperative day 1, the patient developed dysarthria, hemiparesis, and hoarseness (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score 7). MRI revealed bilateral cerebellar and pontine infarcts, and CT angiography showed right vertebral artery occlusion. He was treated with antiplatelet and anticoagulation therapy, followed by intensive rehabilitation. The patient achieved full motor recovery within 1 year.LESSONSThis case highlights the need for vascular risk stratification in ACDF patients with comorbidities, where preoperative vascular imaging may be beneficial. Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is a widely performed spinal procedure with a well-established safety profile, and perioperative neurological compromise only occurs in 0.4% of cases.1 Although vertebral artery injury is a recognized intraoperative complication—typically identified by immediate hemorrhage or hemodynamic instability—posterior circulation stroke remains exceedingly rare following ACDF.2 Large-scale data confirm that vertebral artery injury itself is uncommon, with an estimated incidence of 0.07% across cervical spine procedures.3 Broader data on perioperative stroke risk in spine surgery are also limited but informative. In a retrospective series of 5029 elective spine surgeries, intraoperative ischemic stroke occurred in 7 (0.15%) cases, 3 of which were vertebrobasilar in nature.4 Existing reports and reviews of perioperative stroke following ACDF primarily involve anterior circulation events, most often related to carotid compression, intraoperative hypotension, or known carotid artery stenosis.5,6 The rare cases of vertebrobasilar stroke that have been reported in the perioperative period have typically involved immediate or early postoperative deficits rather than delayed neurological decline after an initial return to baseline.7

The rarity of posterior circulation infarction in this setting raises important considerations regarding the role of occult vascular vulnerability, such as underlying atherosclerosis, vessel tortuosity, or subclinical intimal injury, particularly in multilevel procedures or patients with known vascular comorbidities.8–10 These subtle risk factors may go unrecognized in routine preoperative planning, as dedicated vertebral artery imaging is not typically performed in standard ACDF protocols.9,10

Among these risk factors, atherosclerotic vertebral arteries are often diagnostically missed and may be especially susceptible to iatrogenic stress during anterior cervical exposure, retraction, or interbody graft placement.11,12 Even minor vascular stress may predispose to delayed thromboembolic events that are not apparent intraoperatively but manifest as ischemic complications in the postoperative period.12 In this context, selective use of preoperative vascular imaging such as CTA or MRA may offer a means of risk stratification in patients with significant vascular comorbidities, helping to identify cervical artery stenosis or disease burden that could alter perioperative management.

IONM, including somatosensory evoked potential (SSEP) and motor evoked potential (MEP), is routinely used to detect evolving neurological injury.13 However, the interpretation of transient, lateralized changes, especially when signals return to baseline, is often ambiguous.14 These changes may reflect early, subclinical vascular compromise rather than mechanical spinal cord compression.15,16 While the majority of transient IONM changes resolve without consequence,14–16 the possibility that such events may represent early vascular instability, particularly in patients with subclinical vascular disease, has not been thoroughly investigated and remains poorly understood.

The purpose of this report is to present a rare case of delayed vertebrobasilar stroke following multilevel ACDF in a patient with known atherosclerotic risk factors and transient SSEP changes. This case highlights the potential relationship between IONM changes and delayed vascular events and underscores the need for more prudent preoperative risk stratification and postoperative vigilance in cervical spine surgery.

Illustrative Case

A 61-year-old male with a history of hypertension and poorly controlled hyperlipidemia presented with a 2-month history of progressive cervical myelopathy, manifesting as bilateral upper extremity paresthesia, impaired fine motor control, and frequent object dropping. Cervical spine MRI demonstrated multilevel degenerative spondylosis from C4 to C7 with severe canal stenosis and associated T2 signal hyperintensity, suggestive of cord myelomalacia (Fig. 1). The preoperative imaging was focused on spinal pathology and did not provide any vascular diagnostic visualization. No dedicated CT angiography or MR angiography was performed to evaluate vascular integrity. After multidisciplinary evaluation, the patient elected to undergo three-level ACDF at C4–5, C5–6, and C6–7. The procedure was performed using a standard anterior cervical approach. Fluoroscopy confirmed the C4–5, C5–6, and C6–7 levels, and self-retaining retractors were placed to optimize exposure. After removal of anterior osteophytes, annulotomies and discectomies were performed in standard fashion. Caspar pins were placed in the adjacent vertebral bodies for distraction and disc space separation. The posterior longitudinal ligament was excised to complete decompression, and bilateral neural foramina were decompressed. Allograft interbody spacers were placed at each level, and retraction was minimized between stages to reduce soft tissue strain. Hemostasis was achieved, and the wound was closed in a standard layered fashion.

At baseline, prior to instrumentation, SSEPs demonstrated symmetric, robust waveforms bilaterally (Fig. 2A and B). Shortly after graft placement at the C4–5 level (first level performed), unilateral attenuation was observed in the right upper extremity (RUE) and right lower extremity (RLE) SSEPs (Fig. 2C and D). MEPs and spontaneous electromyography remained stable throughout. Mean arterial pressure was maintained above 85 mm Hg, and anesthetic depth—measured by minimum alveolar concentration (MAC)—was temporarily reduced from 1.3 to 0.5. No issues with patient positioning were noted by the operative team. SSEP signals gradually returned to baseline over 20 minutes (Fig. 2E and F). Given the full recovery of SSEPs, the procedure was continued without modification; anesthesia was readjusted to 1.3 MAC, and signals remained stable for the remainder of the case. During removal of the posterior longitudinal ligament at C6–7, a small CSF leak was encountered from a pinpoint durotomy near the right uncinate process. The leak was swiftly sealed with fibrin dural sealant without any further complication. This minor event primarily increased operative time but was not associated with hemodynamic instability or neurological change. On conclusion of the case, the patient was extubated uneventfully and transferred to the postanesthesia care unit at a neurological baseline for routine postoperative monitoring.

The immediate postoperative course was unremarkable, and radiographs of the cervical spine showed intact, well-aligned hardware initially postoperatively. A follow-up radiograph at 10 months postoperatively revealed C4–7 fusion and hardware still intact (Fig. 3). Initially, the patient reported improvement of upper extremity paresthesia from preoperative baseline, toleration of oral intake, and ambulation with assistance. However, at 7:00 a m on postoperative day 1 (last reported well at 4:00 am on postoperative day 1 by the nursing staff), he developed an acute right-sided facial droop, dysarthria, hemiparesis, and tongue deviation to the right, prompting stroke activation. Neurological examination revealed a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score of 7. Strength testing demonstrated 2/5 weakness in the RUE and 3/5 weakness in the RLE. The left upper and lower extremities remained intact at 5/5 strength. The tongue deviation raised concern for hypoglossal dysfunction and possible caudal brainstem involvement that was not well visualized on initial imaging. Head CT revealed a right cerebellar hypodensity with mild mass effect on the fourth ventricle but without hydrocephalus. CT angiography demonstrated a thrombus in the V2 segment of the right vertebral artery extending from the C2 to C6 levels (Fig. 4) and a 4-mm aneurysmal lesion at the proximal cervical segment of the right internal carotid artery. CT perfusion showed no mismatch. MRI of the brain confirmed acute infarcts in the right cerebellum and left paramedian pons—findings consistent with embolic vertebrobasilar ischemia (Fig. 5). MRI of the cervical spine showed no evidence of spinal cord infarction, hematoma, or hardware-related complications. Our patient was admitted to the neurocritical care unit for close neurological monitoring. Given the posterior fossa infarct burden and concern for malignant cerebellar edema, he was initiated on 3% hypertonic saline (30 mL/hr continuous infusion for 1 day) and monitored with serial neurological examinations and imaging. Antiplatelet therapy with aspirin (300-mg initial suppository + 81-mg tablets daily for 1 month) and clopidogrel (300-mg initial suppository + 75-mg tablets daily for 1 month) was started due to postoperative thrombolytic contraindication. Given the patient’s inability to protect his airway, he underwent endotracheal intubation followed by tracheostomy. No neurosurgical decompression was required, and the patient remained hemodynamically stable without progression to hydrocephalus.

After stabilization, he was transferred to the floor and eventually discharged to an inpatient rehabilitation facility on postoperative day 14. Over the course of 1 year, the patient demonstrated substantial neurological recovery, including resolution of hemiparesis (full regain of 5/5 function in RUE and RLE) and dysarthria. He ultimately returned to independent living and ambulation without assistance.

Discussion

Observations

This case demonstrates a delayed vertebrobasilar stroke following multilevel ACDF, preceded by transient unilateral SSEP changes during surgery. The signals resolved over the course of 20 minutes and were not accompanied by hemodynamic instability or MEP abnormalities. The remainder of the operation was uneventful apart from a small, controlled durotomy. The patient initially returned to neurological baseline.

Although postoperative CTA also revealed a small proximal right internal carotid artery aneurysmal lesion, the ischemic pattern localized to the posterior circulation, and the concurrent thrombus within the V2 vertebral artery provides a more direct mechanistic explanation. Given the lack of preoperative vascular imaging, it remains unclear whether this aneurysmal lesion was preexisting or a result of iatrogenic stress during surgery. While embolic material from a carotid lesion could theoretically reach the posterior circulation via certain anatomical variants such as a dominant posterior communicating artery, this phenomenon is uncommon.17 Even in such cases, emboli would be expected to localize to the posterior cerebral artery territory rather than the vertebral artery itself.

The interpretation of transient unilateral SSEP changes requires caution, as several intraoperative factors, including anesthetic depth, patient positioning, and blood pressure variability, can cause reversible attenuation. In this case, mean arterial pressure was maintained above 85 mm Hg, making global hypoperfusion less likely.18 Although the signals eventually recovered with a temporary reduction in MAC, anesthetic effects would typically affect both hemispheres rather than cause strictly unilateral changes.19 Positional effects, likewise, usually produce gradual or asymmetric changes confined to a single limb distribution, not simultaneous involvement of both an upper and lower extremity on one side.18,20 By contrast, the abrupt, strictly unilateral attenuation observed here—affecting the RUE and RLE together—aligns more closely with a focal vascular disturbance.21 While causation cannot be proven, the concurrence of graft placement, lateralized SSEP changes, and delayed posterior circulation stroke makes vascular stress a more compelling explanation than solely anesthetic factors.

Mechanical manipulation during multilevel ACDF, particularly wide anterior exposure, interbody distraction, or cage insertion, can subject the vertebral artery to kinking, compression, or subtle intimal injury, especially in vessels with preexisting atherosclerosis or tortuosity.11,12,22,23 Guan et al. highlighted in a systemic review that instrumentation and aggressive retraction are among the most common causes of iatrogenic vertebral artery injury during anterior cervical spine surgery,22 while Dickerman and Zigler described a case of atraumatic vertebral artery dissection after cervical corpectomy due to traction during exposure, resulting in a thromboembolic stroke similar to that presented in our case.24 These findings support the concept that cervical arterial compromise can occur through indirect mechanical stress.

In this case, the simultaneous attenuation of right upper and lower extremity SSEPs suggests a transient disturbance affecting shared subcortical sensory pathways—potentially within the brainstem or thalamus supplied by the vertebrobasilar system—rather than isolated cortical or spinal cord ischemia.25 Although the dorsal column–medial lemniscus pathway decussates in the medulla, unilateral vertebral artery hypoperfusion can still produce ipsilateral SSEP attenuation when vascular dominance or flow asymmetry preferentially compromises perfusion of dorsal column or medullary regions before decussation.25–27 Moreover, given that the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) arises distally from the vertebral artery, right vertebral artery compromise could have reduced both PICA and medullary perfusion, contributing to the lateralized SSEP changes.26,27 This transient attenuation may have reflected subtle intraoperative vascular stress that predisposed to subsequent thrombus formation and embolic vertebrobasilar infarction.26,27

This case underscores that vertebrobasilar infarction after ACDF can occur without catastrophic intraoperative injury. The delayed presentation, absence of hemodynamic instability, and lack of overt intraoperative trauma all point toward a more insidious mechanism, likely arising from subclinical intraoperative vertebral artery stress or intimal injury during cage insertion.

While vertebral artery injuries during ACDF have been reported, they are usually identified intraoperatively due to direct vessel laceration or hemorrhage. In contrast, perioperative anterior circulation strokes are more often linked to carotid compression or preexisting carotid stenosis. To our knowledge, no prior literature has described a delayed posterior circulation infarction associated with lateralized IONM changes. While large studies and isolated cases have quantified cervical artery injury and ischemic stroke risk,3–6 the frequency of vertebrobasilar stroke involving ACDF has not been systemically characterized and remains underrepresented in the literature. The goal of this illustration is to contribute to this underrecognized body of literature.

Neuromonitoring utilization patterns and interpretation thresholds remain highly variable in cervical spine surgery, with no clear evidence-based guidelines regarding isolated SSEP changes.28,29 Experimental and clinical studies have shown that, although rare, SSEPs can be affected by cerebral ischemia or vascular insufficiency, even when MEPs remain stable.30,31 This case suggests that reversible SSEP attenuation, while commonly benign, may in rare instances reflect occult vascular stress in high-risk scenarios.

The implications of this case illustration extend beyond the intraoperative period, highlighting the need for proactive strategies to reduce the risk of vertebrobasilar complications in high-risk patients—both through preoperative risk stratification and heightened postoperative vigilance, particularly if IONM changes are observed. This case also underscores the lack of standardized guidelines for interpreting lateralized SSEP changes, suggesting that the development of evidence-based thresholds and response protocols may be critical for improving intraoperative decision-making and postoperative monitoring.

Lessons

This case illustrates that delayed vertebrobasilar stroke, although rare, can occur following multilevel ACDF in patients with vascular risk factors. Transient, lateralized SSEP changes, particularly when involving both upper and lower extremities and following a presumed inciting event, may indicate vertebrobasilar compromise rather than anesthetic fluctuations, and recovery of signals after anesthetic or positional adjustments should not provide immediate reassurance.

Risk mitigation strategies span the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative phases. Preoperatively, vascular risk assessment is essential in patients with poorly controlled atherosclerotic disease, with selective use of preoperative CTA or MRA in such high-risk individuals as a potential means of identifying vertebral artery stenosis or atherosclerotic burden prior to surgery.

Intraoperatively, maintenance of adequate mean arterial pressure (> 85 mm Hg) is critical to ensure adequate perfusion. Additionally, when possible, minimizing the use of volatile anesthetics is advisable given their potential to falsely suppress IONM signaling. When available, considering more advanced multimodal neuromonitoring, such as brainstem auditory evoked responses, may provide increased sensitivity in detecting vascular compromise. Finally, careful limitation of retraction force may further reduce vascular stress.

Postoperatively, structured neurological examinations and a low threshold for vascular imaging may enable earlier detection of evolving ischemic events.

Finally, this case underscores the need for standardized guidelines to aid in the interpretation of lateralized SSEP changes.

The patient has expressed gratitude for the multidisciplinary care that he received over the course of treatment and is happy to be back to day-to-day living.
