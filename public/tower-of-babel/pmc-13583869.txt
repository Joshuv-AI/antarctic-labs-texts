Impact of real-time respiratory function monitoring on neonatal mask ventilation training: a multicentre simulation-based crossover study
Authors: Latifah alrashed, Eliane Bodanese
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Adequate positive pressure ventilation (PPV) is the most critical intervention in neonatal resuscitation.1 Delivery room studies show that healthcare providers frequently misjudge mask leak during face-mask ventilation of premature infants, highlighting the subjectivity of visual assessment of ventilation effectiveness.2–4

PPV is commonly taught using a manikin, with trainees guided by the resuscitation algorithm and using chest rise as the principal indicator of ventilation effectiveness in the simulated setting—a task that can be difficult even for skilled neonatal practitioners.5 Current newborn resuscitation guidelines recommend a structured sequence of corrective steps when ventilation is ineffective. These include optimising mask seal, repositioning the head and jaw, suctioning the airway, opening the mouth, and increasing inflation pressure, summarised by the ‘MRSOPA’ acronym.6

Respiratory function monitors (RFMs), which provide real-time feedback on tidal volume (Vte), mask leak, airway pressures and ventilation rate during PPV, have been evaluated in manikin-based studies. These studies show that visible RFM feedback enables clinicians to adjust mask fit and peak inspiratory pressure (PIP), achieving recommended tidal volumes and controlling mask leak.7–10

A multicentre randomised controlled trial conducted across 13 Australian hospitals evaluated the effectiveness of a Florian RFM in improving newborn facemask ventilation training.11 In this study, 400 healthcare workers were randomly assigned to either an RFM-visible or RFM-masked group. Training and assessment were performed using a Laerdal Resusci Baby manikin connected to a 50 mL Dräger test lung (simulating a 3 kg term infant) and ventilated with a Neopuff T-piece. At the blinded post-training assessment, participants in the RFM-visible group had a lower median mask leak than those in the RFM-masked group (23% vs 35%) and a higher mean expiratory tidal volume (17.7 mL vs 14.2 mL), while inflating pressures were similar between the groups.

Building on these findings, a multinational before-and-after manikin study investigated whether standardised training using the Monivent NeoTraining RFM system could further enhance mask ventilation skills.9 Involving 412 healthcare professionals, the study used a Laerdal Resusci Baby manikin fitted with a 50 mL test lung (Dräger, Lübeck, Germany) to simulate a term infant. The study demonstrated that RFM-supported training significantly reduced median mask leak (31% to 10%) and increased mean expiratory tidal volume (15.3 to 19.0 mL), with additional improvements observed in ventilation rate, PIP and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP).

Further simulation research evaluated immediate feedback on mask leak and tidal volume using the Juno TrainingMonitor system.10 Clinicians ventilated both term and preterm manikins, and results showed low mask leak and tidal volumes within clinically acceptable ranges across experience levels. Although sequential hidden and visible sessions were used, the focus was usability and clinician satisfaction rather than the effect of feedback visibility on skill acquisition.

More recently, a randomised crossover study evaluated the Juno Training Monitor during mask ventilation of term and preterm manikins using a T-piece resuscitator.12 Involving 49 experienced neonatal clinicians, the study demonstrated significant reductions in excessive mask leak (>60%), from 23.4% to 7.4% in the preterm manikin and from 23.4% to 6.2% in the term manikin, with additional improvements observed in targeted tidal volume delivery.

Beyond simulation settings, clinical evidence also supports the potential benefit of RFMs. A two-phase interventional study comparing hidden versus visible RFM (Neo100) during delivery room resuscitation of infants born between 22 and 36 weeks’ gestation found that visible feedback significantly increased the proportion of ventilations within the target tidal volume range (4–8 mL/kg)13 14 and reduced excessive tidal volumes and mask leak.15

A narrative review including three RCTs and one observational study concluded that RFMs reduce excessive tidal volumes and mask leakage,1 16 but emphasised that widespread adoption remains premature due to limited high-quality RCT evidence, inconsistent findings in a large trial17 and ongoing concerns regarding training requirements, usability and cost-effectiveness.18

These studies suggest that RFMs have the potential to improve ventilation monitoring during neonatal resuscitation, particularly in premature infants. However, the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) concludes that current evidence is still insufficient to support their routine clinical use, highlighting the need for further research.19

Although previous studies demonstrate that RFM-supported training can improve ventilation performance, important methodological gaps remain. Many investigations assessed immediate pre-training and post-training effects within a single session and predominantly used term-sized manikins.9 11 While sequential hidden and visible sessions have been explored,10 the direct impact of feedback visibility on learning processes and short-term retention has not been clearly established.

The present study addresses this gap by employing a multicentre crossover simulation design to evaluate the impact of visible versus hidden RFM feedback on mask ventilation performance during neonatal resuscitation training with a T-piece resuscitator (TPR). Using both term and preterm manikins, this study aims to determine whether real-time visibility of ventilation parameters influences corrective adjustments, ventilation stability and the carry-over effects of feedback on subsequent ventilation performance. The inclusion of a highly realistic premature manikin enabled assessment of ventilation performance in a model designed to replicate extremely low birth weight resuscitation.

Methods

Study design and participants

This multicentre crossover study was conducted between June and December 2024 in neonatal intensive care units of the Royal London Hospital and Whipps Cross University Hospital, UK. It evaluated whether real-time RFM improves mask ventilation performance during neonatal resuscitation simulation.

Neonatal healthcare professionals (consultants, registrars, nurses and student nurses) were recruited. Fifty-seven clinicians were enrolled; eight were excluded due to corrupted recordings from an inappropriate mask during early data collection, leaving 49 for analysis.

Participants were randomised to hidden-first (no visual feedback followed by visible feedback) or visible-first sequences. All participants completed both conditions.

Simulation procedure

Training followed UK Resuscitation Council Newborn Life Support guidelines.14 Participants performed mask ventilation on two high-fidelity neonatal manikins (Lifecast Body Simulation, Elstree Film Studios, London, UK): a preterm model simulating a 23 week gestation neonate with a birth weight of approximately 500 g, and a term model simulating a ≥36 week neonate weighing approximately 2.2 kg. Both manikins incorporated anatomically structured airways to allow delivery and measurement of clinically appropriate tidal volumes. Prior to experimentation, both manikins were tested for internal leaks and calibrated using a VT900 Gas Flow Analyser (Fluke Biomedical, Ohio, USA) to verify airway integrity and respiratory mechanics. Lung compliance measurements confirmed values of 0.2 mL/cm H₂O for the preterm model and 0.7 mL/cm H₂O for the term model.

Ventilation was delivered using a Neopuff T-piece resuscitator (Fisher & Paykel Healthcare, New Zealand) with a Classic T-Piece infant breathing circuit and adjustable PEEP valve. A Europlaz neonatal flow sensor was positioned between the T-piece and a neonatal face mask. The sensor was connected to the RFM via a wired connection. Mask sizes were 35 mm (preterm) and 50 mm (term). The Europlaz RFM sampled flow and pressure at 500 Hz, exported raw data for analysis and displayed real-time expired tidal volume (Vte), ventilation rate and mask leak using numerical values and graphical target indicators.

Gas flow and pressures were standardised (preterm: 8 L/min and PIP 25 cm H₂O; term: 10 L/min and PIP 30 cm H₂O; PEEP was set at 5 cm H₂O for both manikins). Participants ventilated under two conditions: hidden (display not visible) and visible. A 1-min break separated conditions. During the visible condition, the RFM was positioned adjacent to the manikin within the participant’s line of sight. During the hidden condition, the monitor was rotated away from the participant while continuing to record data. Within each condition, participants delivered five inflations, removed the mask, delivered a further five inflations, removed the mask again, and then completed two separate 30-s ventilation periods, with mask removal between periods. Sessions were video recorded (hands and equipment only).

Data processing and outcome measures

Performance was assessed using participant-level measures of mean Vte, median leak, SD of Vte, SD of leak, the proportion of breaths within the target Vte range and the proportion of breaths with leak≤30%. Target Vte ranges were based on UK Resuscitation Council guidance (4–8 mL/kg).13 14

Video recordings were reviewed to identify ventilation adjustments (pressure changes, mask hold or repositioning, airway repositioning and jaw lift). For each identified adjustment, two breaths were selected for analysis: the pre-adjustment breath, defined as the final breath recorded immediately prior to the adjustment, and the post-adjustment breath, defined as the first breath delivered after the adjustment was completed. From each of these breaths, two key ventilation parameters were extracted: expired tidal volume (Vte, mL) and mask leak (%).

Following completion of both study conditions, participants completed a structured questionnaire designed to evaluate perceptions of the RFM and the simulation experience. The questionnaire included questions relating to demographic characteristics, perceived mask leak and chest rise, usefulness of the RFM, confidence during mask ventilation, willingness to use the system in future training, prioritisation of corrective actions during ineffective ventilation and suggestions for improving the display interface. Both closed-ended and open-ended questions were included. Questionnaire responses were analysed descriptively, with qualitative responses grouped into common themes.

Exploratory clustering using affinity propagation (AP) was performed separately for hidden and visible conditions to identify potential behavioural subgroups of participants based on ventilation performance. Clustering was based on standardised mean Vte and median mask leak during the ventilation phase.20

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (V. 29). Visible and hidden conditions were compared using paired analyses restricted to the ventilation phase, as this phase provided the most representative assessment of sustained ventilation performance. Quality filtering excluded breaths with substantial leak (>90%) and breaths associated with mask application or removal.

Mean Vte was analysed using paired t-tests with BCa bootstrap estimates. Percentage and skewed outcomes were analysed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Two-sided p<0.05 was considered significant. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d (paired t-tests) and r (Wilcoxon signed-rank tests), with 95% CIs where applicable.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Health Research Authority (HRA) (reference number: 322155). The HRA determined that Research Ethics Committee (REC) review was not required because the study involved only NHS staff. Local approvals were obtained through Queen Mary University of London Joint Research Management Office (QMUL JRMO) and Barts Health NHS Trust. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Mask leak

In Group 1, median mask leak decreased when feedback was visible (6.79% (0.00–49.60) vs 0.00% (0.00–10.47)), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p=0.053; r=−0.35). In contrast, the proportion of breaths with leak≤30% increased significantly under visible conditions (p=0.006; r=0.45), indicating improved mask seal.

In Group 2, leak values were low in both conditions (median 0.00% visible vs 1.81% hidden), and neither median leak nor the proportion of breaths with leak≤30% differed significantly between conditions (all p>0.05).

Ventilation stability

In Group 1, visibility reduced breath-to-breath variability. During ventilation, the SD of Vte decreased from 1.54 mL/kg to 0.5 mL/kg (p=0.005; r=−0.43), and the SD of leak decreased from 21.25% to 9.15% (p=0.028; r=−0.33). In Group 2, variability did not differ significantly between conditions (all p>0.05). Leak variability was numerically higher when feedback was visible, although this did not reach statistical significance (p=0.058; r=0.29).

Mask leak

Ventilation stability

No statistically significant differences were observed in variability measures between conditions in either group. In Group 1, SD Vte (p=0.903) and SD leak (p=0.149) did not differ significantly. Similarly, in Group 2, neither SD Vte (p=0.338) nor SD leak (p=0.485) differed between conditions.

Across both manikins, most ventilation adjustments occurred under visible conditions, suggesting that real-time feedback prompted corrective action. In the preterm manikin, 24 of 30 adjustments (80%) occurred when the RFM was visible. Similarly, in the term manikin, 40 of 58 adjustments (69%) occurred when the RFM was visible. In the preterm manikin, feedback was primarily associated with regulating excessive tidal volumes through inspiratory pressure reduction, whereas in the term manikin it was mainly associated with correcting mask leak through mask repositioning. Despite these corrective actions, improvements in mask leak were less consistent in the term manikin, suggesting that achieving and maintaining an adequate mask seal remained challenging despite real-time feedback.

Questionnaire findings

Forty-nine participants completed the questionnaire. More than 90% perceived the RFM as helpful, associated it with increased confidence, and viewed it as valuable for future training. Early exposure appeared to improve awareness of mask leak but had a limited influence on the subjective assessment of chest rise. Participants’ prioritisation of corrective actions aligned with established NLS guidance, while qualitative responses most frequently highlighted the need for improved display readability, colour coding, and alert functions, particularly for high mask leak.

Discussion

Principal findings

In this multicentre crossover simulation study using a TPR, visible real-time RFM feedback was associated with more consistent ventilation delivery, particularly with the small preterm manikin. In participants who initially ventilated without feedback, introduction of visible parameters increased the proportion of breaths with lower mask leak and reduced variability in Vte. These findings reflected greater ventilation stability rather than substantial changes in mean delivered volume. Reduced breath-to-breath variability in tidal volume and mask leak are desirable because they facilitate the delivery of safe, consistent ventilation with minimal inspiratory pressures and may reduce the risk of both underventilation and excessive tidal volume delivery.11

Participants who began with visible feedback maintained high performance after the removal of feedback, suggesting a carryover learning effect. In the preterm model, descriptive and inferential analyses showed sustained in-range Vte and controlled leak across conditions. This pattern suggests that recent exposure to visible feedback may influence subsequent ventilation performance even after the feedback is removed and may be conceptually analogous to ‘just-in-time’ simulation training, in which recent practice reinforces procedural and tactile aspects of ventilation performance.

Results with the term manikin were less consistent. In the hidden-first group, visibility was associated with a significant increase in mean Vte during ventilation and entry into the target Vte range, although reductions in mask leak were modest and not statistically significant. In the visible-first group, performance remained descriptively stable across conditions; however, overall leak values remained persistently high. This finding suggests that achieving and maintaining an adequate mask seal remained challenging despite the availability of real-time feedback.

Interpretation in relation to previous studies

These findings align with previous studies showing that visible RFM feedback improves ventilation quality during neonatal resuscitation training.9 11 Similar improvements in ventilation performance and usability have been reported in sequential hidden–visible simulation designs.10 Clinical studies have also shown that real-time RFM feedback can enhance ventilation quality in newborn infants,15 supporting the translational relevance of simulation-based findings. More recently, Carlomagno et al demonstrated that RFM-based training improved tidal volume control and that these gains were maintained for up to 3 months after training, although mask leak remained persistently high despite feedback.21 Together with our findings, these observations suggest that RFM may be particularly effective for improving tidal volume targeting, whereas mask leak appears to be a more challenging skill requiring additional training emphasis.

Differences in RFM interface design and usability should also be considered when comparing studies, as the way ventilation parameters are displayed and presented to users may influence training performance and responsiveness to feedback. For instance, the Juno RFM provides feedback only when leaks exceed 30%, with no corrective indication below that. The Philips NM3 displays neither a leak value nor an alert, requiring users to infer leaks from Vti/Vte values or waveform shape, a difficult task during emergencies.

This study extends the literature by examining ventilation with a very small preterm manikin and addressing previously noted gaps in simulated preterm tidal volume data.10 Second, it also evaluates performance stability and order effects. Third, clustering analysis showed behavioural heterogeneity, with participants grouped by ventilation performance rather than professional role. The visual-RFM experiments redistributed participants toward low-leak, in-range performance profiles in the preterm model, indicating that the benefits of real-time feedback were observed across participants irrespective of role.

The stronger effect observed in the preterm manikin compared with the term manikin suggests that the impact of visible feedback may vary according to the ventilation scenario being simulated. In the preterm model, leak was generally controllable and responsive to corrective actions. In contrast, high leak persisted in the term model despite corrective attempts.

Educational implications

These findings support structured integration of RFM feedback into neonatal resuscitation training. Introducing visible feedback early may help establish performance benchmarks, whereas delayed feedback may encourage reflective correction. A blended approach incorporating both visible and hidden phases may optimise skill acquisition and consolidation.

Questionnaire findings complement the objective ventilation data and support the RFM’s role as a training aid. They also highlight an important educational opportunity: bridging the gap between clinical manoeuvres and displayed ventilation parameters. Linking parameter changes to specific interventions may strengthen clinical reasoning during neonatal ventilation.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include its crossover design, multicentre recruitment, inclusion of diverse professional roles, and integration of objective performance metrics, behavioural clustering and participant-reported outcomes. The focus on a small preterm manikin provides novel data in a clinically challenging simulation context. In addition, the Europlaz RFM’s simplified bar-graph display (figure 3) may have facilitated the rapid interpretation of ventilation parameters compared with the more complex waveform-based interfaces used in some earlier monitors.

Europlaz RFM display during mask ventilation training. The screen shows real-time ventilation parameters, including expired tidal volume (Vte), mask leak (%) and ventilation rate (VR). In this example, Vte is 5.4 mL/kg, mask leak is 23% and VR is 24 breaths per minute. The display includes a central green line representing the target ventilation level, with red lines indicating upper and lower reference boundaries. The simplified bar-graph display facilitates rapid visual assessment of ventilation performance during simulated resuscitation.

Several limitations should be considered. The sample size was modest, limiting power to detect small effects. The study was conducted in a simulation setting, which may not fully replicate clinical conditions. Performance was assessed during brief sessions without long-term follow-up, thereby precluding conclusions about sustained retention. Whether similar longer-term benefits would be observed using the crossover training design employed in this study remains unknown and warrants further investigation. Finally, only selected ventilation parameters were displayed to minimise cognitive load, which may have limited exploration of additional clinically relevant variables.

Several participants perceived the term manikin as less realistic in facial anatomy and tactile characteristics than the preterm model, which participants considered highly realistic in shape and feel. This observation highlights the importance of continued development of neonatal simulation manikins to further enhance realism for ventilation training and research. Future studies comparing different display designs could help clarify how monitor usability influences training outcomes.

Conclusions

This multicentre crossover simulation study using a TPR demonstrated that visible RFM feedback was associated with changes in mask ventilation performance during neonatal training. Effects were particularly evident in mask leak control, ventilation stability and achieving tidal volumes within the target range. Comparison of visible-first and hidden-first conditions suggests that visual feedback may support immediate performance and influence subsequent ventilation behaviour during training.

These findings support integrating RFM feedback into neonatal resuscitation training programmes and highlight its potential as a powerful educational tool. Future research should examine optimal strategies for introducing feedback during training, including timing, delivery format, and integration with clinical decision-making, and determine whether similar long-term benefits can be achieved with crossover training approaches.
