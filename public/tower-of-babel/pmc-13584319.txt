Liposomes can directly modulate serine hydrolase activity and lipid metabolism independent of cargo
Authors: Payton E. Lowrey, Olivia L. Murtagh, Madeline Gunawardena, Rudra Pangeni, Qingguo Xu, Tobias O. Jonson, Ku-Lung Hsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Liposomes are lipid-based nanoparticles that can be used to encapsulate and deliver small molecules, biologics, nucleic acids and other cargo for improved solubility, targeting and controlled release in vivo. These widely studied and clinically validated nanocarriers are often assumed to be biologically inert, yet emerging studies point to important but understudied biological activity. Here, we utilized quantitative chemoproteomics to discover that PEGylated liposomes composed of saturated phospholipids and cholesterol can directly modulate lipase activity independent of their cargo. Macrophages treated with these empty liposomes under inflammatory states showed suppressed TNF-alpha secretion in a phospholipid headgroup- and cholesterol content-dependent manner. These findings reveal unexpected biological effects independent of drug cargo and provide a framework for assessing bioactivity of these common lipid nanocarriers using chemical biology approaches.

Introduction

Liposomes are widely studied and clinically validated nanocarriers in modern biomedicine.1,2 These self-assembled phospholipid bilayer vesicles can be used to encapsulate and deliver small molecules, biologics, and nucleic acids, ultimately improving pharmacokinetics, enhancing stability, and reducing systemic toxicity.3,4 Several approved therapeutics rely on liposomal formulations, highlighting their importance as drug delivery platforms.3–5 In both experimental and translational settings, liposomes lacking encapsulated cargo (described as “empty” or “blank” liposomes) are either used as controls or remain untested under the prevailing assumption that the lipid nanocarrier itself is biologically inert.6–9

The current working model posits that liposomes primarily function as passive vessels that shield encapsulated agents from degradation, modulate biodistribution, and facilitate cellular uptake without significantly perturbing cellular biology.10 While efforts have been devoted to optimizing liposome composition to tune the drug encapsulation efficiency, stability, circulation time, and release kinetics of therapeutic cargo, efforts to study the intrinsic biological activity of the vesicle itself have lagged in comparison.10 These studies are important since liposomes are concentrated assemblies of lipids presented in a defined nanoscale architecture that interface directly with cellular membranes and metabolic components.11

Despite the widespread use of liposomes as drug delivery systems, the intrinsic biological activity of empty liposomes has not been systematically characterized using, for example, unbiased molecular profiling approaches. Most studies evaluate liposomal formulations through functional endpoints or pharmacological readouts, without directly interrogating whether the carrier alone engages intracellular protein targets or alters lipid networks.11 A few recent studies have demonstrated that liposomes have the capacity to directly alter the inflammatory signaling pathways and phenotype of immune cells, yet comprehensive analyses into the intracellular mechanics and dynamics of these changes remain elusive.12–15 Whether exposure to liposomal phospholipid assemblies can directly influence lipid-processing enzymes or alter cellular lipid composition in the absence of encapsulated therapeutics remains poorly defined.

In this study, we investigate the intrinsic bioactivity of empty liposomes in macrophage lipid metabolism and inflammatory signaling using structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis, (chemo)proteomics, and targeted lipidomics. We demonstrate that phospholipid headgroup identity and cholesterol content govern both immunomodulatory activity and direct inhibition of the serine hydrolase alpha/beta-hydrolase domain-containing 6 (ABHD6). Mechanistic studies support a membrane-interface model wherein the transmembrane domain of ABHD6 contributes to liposomal inactivation, with filtration studies demonstrating physical association between the liposome and ABHD6, and pre-incubation kinetics and jump-dilution experiments revealing time-dependent, sustained inactivation inconsistent with nonspecific effects of free lipid components. Collectively, these findings establish that lipid nanocarriers possess measurable, composition-dependent bioactivity independent of drug cargo, and provide a chemical biology framework for evaluating nanocarrier bioactivity.

Results

Empty liposomes suppress LPS-stimulated TNFα secretion in macrophage cells

Empty liposomes composed of natural and pegylated saturated phospholipids (PLs) along with cholesterol were generated following published methods and as described in the SI Methods (Fig. 1A and B).16 Dynamic light scattering (DLS) was used to determine that these formulations produced homogenous vesicles of various sizes depending on the composition. While prior studies demonstrate that conventional empty liposomes can impact inflammatory signaling,12–15 the contribution of lipid structure and composition to this activity within PEGylated liposomes – a common modification employed for liposome-based drug delivery – remains ill defined. Specifically, we tested whether changes to the phospholipid headgroup or cholesterol content impact cellular activity of empty liposomes (Fig. 1C–H). RAW264.7 mouse macrophage cells were pre-treated with empty liposomes (4 or 24 h) followed by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation (5 µg mL−1, 2 h). Macrophage activation was confirmed by significant tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα) secretion upon LPS stimulation as measured by ELISA (∼80% increase upon LPS treatment). The SAR from changes in the phospholipid headgroup (Lip-Blank 1 – choline, Lip-Blank 2 – ethanolamine) was evident. Acute treatment with Lip-Blank 1 resulted in a modest but significant reduction in LPS-stimulated TNFα that was further accentuated after extended treatment time (17% vs. 64% reduction, respectively; Fig. 1C and D). In contrast, Lip-Blank 2 did not show inhibitory activity at either timepoint (Fig. 1E and F). Cholesterol content, interestingly, also influenced cellular activity of empty liposomes. Lip-Blank 3, which had reduced cholesterol content (20%) compared to Lip-Blank 1 (40%), showed prominent blockade of LPS-stimulated TNFα at earlier time points but, unexpectedly, enhanced cytokine production with extended treatment times (43% reduction vs. 51% increase, respectively; Fig. 1G and H). These effects were absent under basal conditions and not due to general cell toxicity (Fig. 1C–H and S1). These data support that liposomes can alter inflammatory signaling independent of their cargo in different ways depending on their lipid chemical composition.

From these SAR studies, Lip-Blank 1 was selected for a more in-depth investigation because of its consistent anti-inflammatory activity across both timepoints and a cholesterol content that is in agreement with stable structures reported in literature.17 Nanoparticle Tracking Analyzer (NTA)-based analysis of Lip-Blank 1 revealed a concentration of 1.1 × 1013 particles (liposomes) per mL and 1.4 × 106 total lipid molecules per liposome. The particle size distribution measured is shown in Fig. S2A. Moreover, cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM) imaging demonstrated that Lip-Blank 1 formed nanosized globular vesicles, with sizes consistent with those measured by the particle size analyzer (Fig. S2B).

Lip-Blank 1 treatment upregulates endo-lysosomal and inflammatory protein expression

We measured global protein expression changes under basal and activated cell states to gain mechanistic insights into Lip-Blank 1 activity in macrophage cells. Endocytic and lysosomal proteins were upregulated with Lip-Blank 1 treatment, which was expected given the known role of the endo-lysosomal pathway in liposome uptake and processing.11 The expression of several enzymes involved in lysosomal storage disorders (LSDs) was affected in basal and inflamed states (Fig. 2A, B and Tables S1–S4).18 Beyond lysosomal functions, the expression of proteins involved in exocytosis was also impacted.

We also detected upregulation of proteins involved in lipid metabolism. These proteins included enzymes involved in cholesterol transport (NPC2),19,20 fatty acid transport (FABPs),21,22 and lipid metabolism (NCEH1, PPT1, ALDH2, COX2; Fig. 2 and Tables S1–S4, SI Dataset 1);23–25 Among the lipid-metabolizing enzymes, the upregulation of COX2 in both basal and inflamed conditions was somewhat unexpected given the TNFα suppression observed with Lip-Blank 1 treatment. COX2 is involved in arachidonic acid (AA) metabolism that leads to production of pro-inflammatory eicosanoids.25 Collectively, this global proteomics data supports a complex cellular response to Lip-Blank 1 treatment involving proteins with functions rooted in lysosomal processing and lipid metabolism.

Fatty acids involved in inflammation initiation and resolution accumulate with Lip-Blank 1 treatment

Based on these changes in fatty acid (FA)-processing enzymes, we performed targeted liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)-based lipidomics to directly determine the impact of Lip-Blank 1 on fatty acid metabolism.26 We detected a Lip-Blank 1-mediated increase in basal secreted AA that was significantly augmented in LPS-stimulated macrophages (∼50 vs. 80% increase, respectively, p < 0.05; Fig. 3A). Previous studies reported that AA in the presence of LPS can increase COX2 expression, offering a potential explanation for the observed COX2 upregulation in response to Lip-Blank 1 treatment (Fig. 2C and D).27 Interestingly, a handful of other poly-(PUFA) and mono-(MUFA) unsaturated fatty acids were also upregulated in both basal and LPS-stimulated cells. Notably, we observed accumulation of the pro-resolving PUFAs, docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, 22 : 6) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA, 20 : 5),28 as well as palmitoleic acid (PoA, 16 : 1) in both basal and inflamed cells (Fig. 3A). PoA has previously been shown to reduce LPS-induced pro-inflammatory cytokine expression including TNFα.29 Lip-Blank 1 treatment increased oleic (OA, 18 : 1) and adrenic acid (AdA, 22 : 4) only under LPS activation (Fig. S3). While changes in MUFAs were observed, the corresponding saturated FA (SFA) species—palmitic (PA, 16 : 0) and stearic acid (SA, 18 : 0)—were not significantly altered by Lip-Blank 1 (SI Dataset 2). We sought to more comprehensively explore liposomal effects on the cellular lipidome given the unexpected increase in unsaturated but not saturated FA pools.

Phospho- and lysophospho-lipid composition is remodeled with Lip-Blank 1 treatment

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis30,31 in global proteomic analyses revealed potential effects of Lip-Blank 1 treatment on plasma membrane repair (Fig. S4 and SI Dataset 3). Considering the influx of saturated phospholipids from Lip-Blank 1 treatment, we expanded our targeted lipidomic analyses to gain a broader perspective of Lip-Blank 1 effects on the cellular lipidome.32 We analyzed diacylglycerols (DAGs), monoacylglycerols (MAGs), glycerophospholipids (PLs) and lysophospholipids (LysoPLs) as well as triacylglycerols (TAGs) and cholesteryl esters (CEs).33

Interestingly, we observed a general upregulation of species across most lipid classes profiled in Lip-Blank 1-compared to vehicle (PBS)-treated, LPS-stimulated macrophages. Specifically, we detected general upregulation (log2(fold change) > 0.5, p < 0.05) of PLs and LysoPLs (25%), DAGs (35%), TAGs (77%) and CEs (61% of upregulated vs. total detected lipids; Fig. 3B, S5 and S6). This broad perturbation to the cellular lipidome is likely from increased exogenous lipid uptake from liposome treatment under LPS stimulation,34 which we confirmed using DiI fluorophore-loaded Lip-Blank 1 (Fig. S7). LPS treatment alone did not alter these lipids (Fig. S8). These broad increases across lipid classes were not observed in Lip-Blank 1-treated cells in the absence of LPS with only minor increases in TAGs and DAGs, and decreases in LysoPLs and saturated MAGs (Fig. 3C, S5, S6 and S9).

Interestingly, the PL components of the liposome, phosphatidylcholine 18 : 0_18 : 0 (DSPC) and phosphatidylethanolamine 18 : 0_18 : 0 (DSPE), were detected but not altered by Lip-Blank 1 treatment (Fig. 3B and C). Many of the accumulated PLs from Lip-Blank 1 treatment in LPS-stimulated cells were composed of mixed stearoyl and unsaturated (either PUFA or MUFA) fatty acyl chains (Fig. 3B). Additionally, a few stearoyl-containing LysoPL species were shown to accumulate with Lip-Blank 1 treatment, including lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LysoPE) 18 : 0, lysophosphatidylglycerol (LysoPG) 18 : 0, and lysophosphatidylserine (LysoPS) 18 : 0 (Fig. 3B).

Lip-Blank 1-mediated lipid metabolism is coupled to alterations in serine lipase activity

Considering the broad lipidomic changes detected, we hypothesized that Lip-Blank 1 treatment could be affecting lipase activity. We addressed this question by performing activity-based protein profiling (ABPP) of serine hydrolases (SHs) using a broad-spectrum, active-site directed fluorophosphonate (FP)-biotin probe. Many SHs are lipases with known roles in inflammation.35–37 We measured changes in SH activity through quantitative ABPP using LC-MS/MS-based chemoproteomics as previously described (Fig. 4A).38 These global ABPP analyses identified a number of SH activities that were altered by cellular Lip-Blank 1 treatment (Fig. 4B and S10).

In agreement with the global proteomic findings, we detected an upregulation in activity of SHs with established roles in the endo-lysosomal pathway. Specifically, we observed an increase in cathepsin A (CTSA), a lysosomal SH which protects lysosomal degrading enzymes from hydrolysis.39 Activity of lysosomal prolylcarboxypeptidase (PRCP), an established lysosomal peptidase, was also increased.40 Additionally, several other soluble lysosomal SHs were upregulated within the LPS-stimulated proteome specifically including palmitoyl-protein thioesterase 2 (PPT2), dipeptidyl-peptidase 2 (DPP2 or DPP7), and sialate O-acetylesterase (SIAE, Fig. 4B and SI Dataset 4).41

We also identified lipases with altered activity upon Lip-Blank 1 treatment including several alpha beta hydrolases (ABHD4, ABHD11, ABHD6) and cytosolic phospholipase A2α (cPLA2α, Fig. 4B). cPLA2α has been shown to hydrolyze AA from phospholipids for subsequent pro-inflammatory eicosanoid production.25 Lip-Blank 1 modulation of cPLA2α activity was cell-state specific. Notably, we observed upregulated cPLA2α activity (insoluble fraction) with Lip-Blank 1 pre-treatment under LPS activated but not basal states, which agrees with the enhanced AA accumulation detected under the same cell treatment conditions (Fig. 3A and 4B).

The observed ABHD6 blockade aligns with the enhanced accumulation of LysoPS and LysoPG species detected in Lip-Blank 1-treated, LPS-stimulated cells (Fig. 3B and 4B). ABHD6 has been shown to hydrolyze diverse lipid substrates including MAG, LysoPS, LysoPG and bis(monoacylglycero)phosphate (BMP).42–44 To directly confirm Lip-Blank 1-mediated ABHD6 inhibition, we performed gel-based competitive ABPP and observed concentration- and time-dependent blockade of covalent activity-based probe labeling in treated cells (Fig. 4C and D). Comparing protein expression and ABPP profiles across ABHD6 and other SHs further confirmed that Lip-Blank 1 modulates protein activity without necessarily altering their protein expression as determined by western blot and proteomic analysis (Fig. 4C, E and F). Taken together, these findings suggest that liposomes can modulate the activity of lipid-metabolizing enzymes including ABHD6 and cPLA2α as measured by ABPP.

Lip-Blank 1 directly blocks ABHD6 biochemical activity

Next, we asked whether the SH inhibitory activity of Lip-Blank 1 detected by ABPP translates to disruption of biochemical activity. ABHD6 biochemical function has previously been measured using commercial and radiolabeling assays that quantify its BMP lipase activity (Fig. 5A).43 Here, we established an LC-MS/MS-based BMP substrate assay that measures BMP hydrolysis activity in lysates derived from Lip-Blank 1-treated, recombinant ABHD6-expressing HEK293T cells (Fig. 5B). Incubation of BMP substrate with ABHD6-HEK293T lysate resulted in significant formation of the LysoPG product compared with mock-transfected control at saturated concentrations of BMP. Treatment of ABHD6-HEK293T cells with Lip-Blank 1 prior to lysis resulted in a significant blockade of ABHD6 biochemical activity in tested lysates (Fig. 5C). Moreover, this in situ inhibitory effect was not due to differences in protein expression (Fig. S11). We note that the decrease in LysoPG production observed here is not contradictory to the lipidomic accumulation of LysoPG in Lip-Blank 1-treated cells (Fig. 3), as the former directly measures ABHD6-catalyzed BMP processing under defined cell-free conditions while the latter reflects steady-state endogenous lipid levels governed by multiple biosynthetic and catabolic pathways in intact cells.

To validate that liposome inhibition of ABHD6 was not a consequence of secondary effects from altered cellular biology, we showed that in vitro treatment with Lip-Blank 1 blocked LysoPG formation in ABHD6-overexpressed HEK293T membrane lysate in the same fashion as KT195, a known covalent inhibitor of ABHD6. At the same time, we demonstrated that the individual lipid components of Lip-Blank 1 did not inactivate recombinant ABHD6 on their own (Fig. 5D). Pre-incubation of ABHD6-overexpressed lysate with Lip-Blank 1 showed evidence of time dependence as determined by a leftward shift in the relative potency (IC50) value (Fig. 5E). We performed a jump dilution study to demonstrate ABHD6 inactivation over time was retained upon a 20X dilution (Fig. 5F).

Lip-Blank inhibitory activity against ABHD6 is dependent on liposome chemical composition

Next, we confirmed that in vitro inactivation with Lip-Blank 1 occurred with endogenous ABHD6 present in RAW264.7 membrane lysate using ABPP. In the same study, we validated that this effect was, again, not a consequence of the individual lipid components of Lip-Blank 1 (Fig. S12A). With in vitro inactivation established, we then demonstrated a SAR between liposome chemical composition and ABHD6 inactivation. In alignment with activity in macrophages, we observed that Lip-Blank 1 and 3 demonstrated higher ABHD6 inhibitory potency than Lip-Blank 2, which did not significantly perturb TNFα expression (Fig. S12B and 1E, F). Additionally, the increased potency of Lip-Blank 3 compared with Lip-Blank 1 aligns with the more drastic TNFα inhibitory activity observed in cells (Fig. S12B and 1G). Taken together, these data reflect how liposome chemical composition can influence enzyme activity as measured by active site-directed probe labeling.

Inhibitory activity of Lip-Blank 1 is dependent on ABHD6 membrane binding

To probe whether Lip-Blank physically associates with ABHD6, we reasoned that Lip-Blank 1(∼100 nm), being orders of magnitude larger than a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) filter pore size, would be retained along with any bound protein cargo below the MWCO. We, therefore, performed centrifugal MWCO filtration (50 kDa) on lysates pre-incubated with Lip-Blank 1 or vehicle, exploiting the size differential between Lip-Blank 1 (>50 kDa) and ABHD6 (∼30 kDa). Enhanced retention of ABHD6 protein in the retentate fraction of Lip-Blank 1- compared to vehicle-treated lysates provided evidence consistent with direct physical association between the liposome and the enzyme. With repeated washing, this effect was observed but reduced compared to samples washed once. Activity-based probe labeling of the retained ABHD6 in Lip-Blank 1-treated lysates by HT-01 in both cases was blocked (Fig. 6A and B).

To investigate the structural basis of Lip-Blank 1-mediated ABHD6 inactivation, we speculated that the interaction could be mediated by the ABHD6 transmembrane domain, given that liposomes interface directly with membrane-embedded proteins.45,46 To test this hypothesis, we obtained a purified transmembrane domain-truncated ABHD6 (pABHD6) with a higher molecular weight (∼64 kDa) as a result of His- and GST-tag addition and observed a near-complete loss of inhibitory activity from Lip-Blank 1 treatment (Fig. 6C).

To confirm that this insensitivity was not an artifact of the purified protein format, we spiked pABHD6 into RAW264.7 membrane lysate and performed an in vitro treatment with Lip-Blank 1. We found that endogenous full length ABHD6 was inhibited while the transmembrane domain null pABHD6 remained insensitive to Lip-Blank 1 treatment (Fig. 6D). To rule out other alternative mechanisms, we confirmed that this inactivation was not due to protein–protein interactions (PPIs), post-translational modification (PTMs) nor internal sequestration of ABHD6 within the liposome via immunopurification mass spectrometry and a proteinase K protection43 assay, respectively (Fig. 6E, S13 and SI Dataset 5). Collectively, these data support a membrane-interface model whereby the transmembrane domain of ABHD6 contributes to sensitivity to Lip-Blank 1-mediated inactivation (Fig. 6F).

Discussion

Liposomes are used to encapsulate, protect and deliver drugs and other cargo for improved solubility, targeting and controlled release in vivo.3,4 These biocompatible delivery vessels are often assumed to be biologically inert, but emerging studies point to unexpected immunomodulatory activity.12–15 Our discovery that PEGylated liposomes modulate protein expression, enzymatic activity, and lipid metabolism independent of therapeutic cargo sheds new light on the biological activity of these nanocarriers.

First, we demonstrated that the lipid headgroup and cholesterol content of liposome formulations produce distinct effects on inflammatory signaling in macrophages (Fig. 1). Taken together with previous studies, our findings suggest that liposomes could potentially be optimized to elicit certain inflammatory responses.12–14 Alongside the altered expression of inflammatory markers like TNFα and COX2, Lip-Blank 1 also increased the expression and activity of proteins involved in exocytosis, endocytosis, and lysosomal degradation. These findings show that LC-MS/MS-based proteomics and chemoproteomics could function as complementary tools to current and often disruptive methods for studying cellular uptake and processing mechanisms of lipid-based nanoparticles.11,47,48

Additionally, lipidomic analysis to understand the cell biological impact of liposomal delivery remains largely unexplored. One potential outcome from liposomal delivery is loading of cells with lipids derived from the nanostructure itself. The Lip-Blank 1 formulation tested here consists principally of phospholipids composed of two saturated fatty acids (SFAs), which, paradoxically, were not affected as measured by targeted lipidomics. Instead, we detected an increase in secreted unsaturated fatty acids involved in promoting (AA27) and resolving (DHA, EPA, POA, OA28,29) inflammation. The increased production of pro- and anti-inflammatory FAs by Lip-Blank 1 could serve as a potential explanation for the paradoxical COX2 upregulation and TNFα reduction observed in Lip-Blank 1-treated, LPS-stimulated cells (Fig. 2 and 3). The observed alteration in FA profiles raises important questions for future investigation, particularly regarding how liposomes composed exclusively of di-saturated phospholipids and cholesterol can increase cellular PUFA and MUFA pools without expanding SFA levels (Fig. 1, SI Dataset 2).

Notably, we detected a general increase in lipid species in response to Lip-Blank 1 treatment under LPS-stimulated but not basal macrophage cell states. LPS activation is reported to increase phagocytic activity of macrophages,34 which could help explain the broad lipidome remodeling from increased liposome uptake (Fig. 3). This claim is supported by the upregulation of several lysosomal SH activities in this stimulated context and enhanced uptake of fluorophore-loaded Lip-Blank 1 by LPS-stimulated macrophages (Fig. 4 and S7). The lipidomic findings presented here provide an initial glimpse of how liposome interactions at the cell surface can alter intracellular lipid metabolism and inflammatory signaling.

Another key finding was that Lip-Blank 1 treatment in cells could directly enhance or block SH activity as measured by quantitative ABPP (Fig. 4). This ABPP analysis was extended to lysates, which showed ABHD6 inhibitory activity, as measured by ABPP and biochemical assay, was dependent on an intact liposome and its chemical constituents (Fig. 5 and S12). Pre-incubation kinetics revealed a time-dependent enhancement of ABHD6 inactivation by Lip-Blank 1, and jump-dilution experiments demonstrated that this inactivation was not rapidly reversed upon dilution below the effective concentration and showed a minimal amount of recovered enzyme activity after increasing timepoints post-dilution. These kinetic features are more consistent with a sustained membrane-interface engagement than with nonspecific effects of free lipid components, suggesting that liposomal architecture plays an active role in mediating enzyme inactivation (Fig. 5).

Centrifugation studies demonstrated that preincubation of ABHD6 with Lip-Blank 1 resulted in enhanced retention of the protein during MWCO filtration, consistent with physical association between full-length ABHD6 and the liposome. Notably, this retention was reduced upon repeated washes and together with the kinetics and jump-dilution data, supports a time-dependent membrane-interface interaction that is partially reversible upon repeated washing but not simple dilution. A transmembrane domain-truncated, purified ABHD6 was insensitive to Lip-Blank 1 inactivation, and this insensitivity was maintained when the truncated protein was spiked back into a membrane lysate alongside inhibitable endogenous full-length ABHD6 (Fig. 6). These data support a membrane-interface model of ABHD6 inactivation whereby the transmembrane form of the enzyme contributes to Lip-Blank 1 sensitivity. To exclude alternative regulatory mechanisms, IP-MS analysis revealed no meaningful changes in ABHD6 PPIs or PTMs upon Lip-Blank 1 treatment (Fig. S7, S12 and SI Dataset 5), and a proteinase K protection assay43 further excluded internal sequestration of ABHD6 within liposomal vesicles (Fig. 6).

Several limitations of the current study point to compelling directions for future work. First, lipid mass balance and turnover kinetic analyses would strengthen the mechanistic interpretation of the global lipidomic changes observed, distinguishing passive lipid loading from active metabolic remodeling. Second, the kinetics and jump-dilution data suggest that Lip-Blank 1 engages ABHD6 through a time-dependent, membrane-interface interaction that is not rapidly reversible under dilution conditions yet can be partially displaced by repeated washing; this binding profile is distinct from nonspecific free lipid effects and warrants further mechanistic exploration. Finally, the membrane-interface model proposed here would benefit from studies using purified full-length ABHD6, which would enable co-flotation assays45 to demonstrate liposome–enzyme association in a system where the transmembrane domain is intact and the interaction is preserved. This would also facilitate chemoproteomics-based mapping of the binding site(s) to define the molecular determinants of liposomal engagement. Together, these studies would provide the mechanistic resolution needed to fully understand how liposomal architecture engages with a membrane-embedded serine hydrolase.

Collectively, our findings reveal the chemical structure and composition of lipids used for liposome formulation can affect inflammatory signaling pathways and intracellular enzyme activity in unexpected ways. The findings also support LC-MS/MS-based (chemo)proteomics as a complementary approach to existing methods for studying cellular processing of nanoparticles.11,47,48 Our studies shed new light on the bioactivity of liposomes outside of their recognized role in drug delivery while depicting a chemical biology-based framework for future exploration.
