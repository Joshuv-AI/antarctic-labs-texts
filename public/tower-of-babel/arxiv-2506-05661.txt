Two dimensional integral representations via branches of the Bruhat-Tits tree
Aguiló-Vidal, Bruno, Arenas-Carmona, Luis, Saavedra-Lagos, Matías

Abstract
We apply the theory of branches in Bruhat-Tits trees, developed in previous works by the second author and others, to the study of two dimensional representations of finite groups over the ring of integers of a number field. We provide a general strategy to perform these computations, and we give explicit formulas for some particular families.



1
Introduction

Representation theory is a branch of mathematics that explores how
algebraic structures can be represented as linear transformations of vector
spaces. It has deep connections to various fields,
including physics, geometry, and number theory.
Since finite group of linear transformations appear naturally in a
variety of contexts, having a classification of the essentially different
ways a finite group can appear as a group of linear transformation
is an important problem. One that has a natural solution through
the study of group algebras, which, at least over fields of characteristic
zero, has an extremely simple structure. This allows us to prove easily,
for instance, the finiteness of the number of irreducible representations,
and often compute their number and dimensions.

Integral representations play an equally significant role in any context
where discrete structures are considered, like in studying the structure of
crystals.
As an example, it is relatively easy to prove that every lattice
\Lambda

with a symmetry
\sigma
of order
2
can be written as the direct sum
of three lattices,
\Lambda_{1}
,
\Lambda_{-1}
and
\Lambda_{i}
,
where
\sigma
acts as the identity on
\Lambda_{1}
, as multiplication by

-1
on
\Lambda_{-1}
, while
\Lambda_{i}
is a sum of rank-
2
lattices
with
\sigma
acting as the matrix
\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right)
on each. This gives
a complete classification of the
\mathbb{Z}
-representations of
the group
C_{2}
with two elements.

More generally, we are interested in classifying maps

\phi:G\rightarrow\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
up to a change of basis,
i.e., up to conjugacy by an invertible matrix in

\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathcal{O}_{K})^{*}
. One possible approach is to imitate
the already successful theory for fields, i.e., study all possible
modules over the group ring
\mathcal{O}_{K}[G]
. An example of this
approach can be found in
[
L
]
. See also
[
HR62
]
,

[
HR63
]
,
[
J
]
and
[
K
]
. This is way harder than
the corresponding problem for fields. For instance, when

R=\mathbb{Z}[C_{2}]
, the finitely generated
torsion free
R
-modules have the structure
that has already been described above, but only lattices of the form

\Lambda=\Lambda_{i}
are actually projective modules, as there are
natural exact sequences of the form

0\rightarrow\Lambda_{\epsilon}\rightarrow\Lambda_{i}\rightarrow\Lambda_{-\epsilon}\rightarrow 0,

for
\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
that fail to split. This tell us that a
complete understanding of the representation theory for these rings
is significantly more involved, and in fact the theory
of
\mathcal{O}_{K}[G]
-modules encodes
abundant arithmetic information, as should be apparent in the
sequel.

Since the representation theory over fields is already well understood,
there is another path to the study of integral representations.
To classify them first under a number field
K
, and then study
the set of integral representation that are conjugate to a given

K
-representation. This set is potentially empty, see

[
CRW
]
or
[
S
]
for an example, or
[
Ho
]
for a more
algorithmic approach. This is our path in this work. To study
integral representation in this context, we use the correspondence
between maximal orders containing a given representation
and integral representations in a given maximal order, as described in
§4 below. Then we study the set of maximal orders containing one given
representation or, equivalently, containing the order it generates.
The cases where this order is maximal have been studied in
[
Ma18
]
.
This is not often the case. However, tools for the study of the set
of maximal orders containing a given suborder, in a more general setting, have already been developed in our previous works. They focus mostly
on two dimensional algebras, and that is the reason we limit
the study to these representation here. However, some of our techniques
can be extended to higher dimensions, and we expect to do so in
future works. Here we focus in adapting the techniques previously
developed to study embeddings of quadratic rings into orders
in quaternion algebras to the precise objective
at hand. This is often simpler when studying representations
of finite groups over their field of definition, since this
theory rely mostly on the arithmetic of cyclotomic fields.
This allows us to obtain some very explicit results,
as those listed below.

.

(Prop.

2
Ideals, extensions, lattices and orders

If
K/\mathbb{Q}
is a finite extension, the ring of integers

\mathcal{O}_{K}\subseteq K
, which is defined as the set of elements
in
K
that are zeroes of monic polynomials with coefficients in

\mathbb{Z}
, is a Dedekind domain. In particular, the non-zero
fractional
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-ideals in
K

form a group under multiplication that we denote by

\mathcal{I}_{K}
. The subset of principal ideals
a\mathcal{O}_{K}
is
a subgroup
\mathcal{P}_{K}
, and the quotient

\mathcal{G}_{K}=\mathcal{I}_{K}/\mathcal{P}_{K}
is called the class group
of
K
. For any finite extension
L/K
there is a multiplicative
norm function

N_{L/K}:\mathcal{I}_{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}_{K}
, and the relative class
group is defined by

\mathcal{G}_{L/K}=N_{L/K}^{-1}(\mathcal{P}_{K})/\mathcal{P}_{L}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{L}.

The order of the group
\mathcal{G}_{K}
is called the class number of
K

and denoted
h_{K}
. Analogously, the order of the group
\mathcal{G}_{L/K}

is called the relative class number of the extension
L/K
and denoted

h_{L/K}
. We also use the group
\mathcal{G}_{K}(n)
which is defined
as the set of classes
\bar{I}\in\mathcal{G}_{K}
that satisfy

\bar{I}^{n}=e_{\mathcal{G}_{K}}=\overline{(1)}
.
Equivalently,
\bar{I}\in\mathcal{G}_{K}(n)
if and only if

I^{n}
is a principal fractional ideal. We also use

h_{K}(n)
for the order of
\mathcal{G}_{K}(n)
.

For every finite place
\nu
of
K
, the completions of
K
and
\mathcal{O}_{K}

are denoted
K_{\nu}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\nu}
, respectively. When recalling the
field
K
is important, we use
\mathcal{O}_{K,\nu}
instead of

\mathcal{O}_{\nu}
. Note that
\mathcal{O}_{K,\nu}=\mathcal{O}_{L,\omega}\cap K_{\nu}

whenever
\omega
is a place of
L
lying over
\nu
.
Similar conventions apply
to the notation
I_{\nu}
for the completion of a fractional ideal
I
.
We also write
K_{\nu}

for infinite (archimedean) places of
K
, although
\mathcal{O}_{\nu}

and
I_{\nu}
fail to be defined in that case.

We usually write
P=P[\nu]
for the maximal ideal satisfying

P_{\nu}=\pi_{\nu}\mathcal{O}_{\nu}
, where
\pi_{\nu}
is a
local uniformizer
at
\nu
, and
P_{\nu^{\prime}}=\mathcal{O}_{\nu^{\prime}}
elsewhere.
Recall that a finite
extension
L/K
is said to be unramified at a place
\nu

if the ideal
P^{\prime}=P[\omega]\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{L}
satisfies

P^{\prime}_{\omega}=\mathcal{O}_{L,\omega}P_{\nu}
for every place
w
of
L
lying
above
\nu
. At unramified places we have

\mathcal{O}_{L,\omega}=\mathcal{O}_{K,\nu}[u_{1},\dots,u_{m}]

for any set of elements whose images
\bar{u}_{1},\dots,\bar{u}_{m}

in the residue field
\mathbb{L}_{\omega}=\mathcal{O}_{L,\omega}/\pi_{\omega}\mathcal{O}_{L,\omega}

satisfy
\mathbb{L}_{\omega}=\mathbb{K}_{\nu}[\bar{u}_{1},\dots,\bar{u}_{m}]
,
where
\mathbb{K}_{\nu}=\mathcal{O}_{K,\nu}/\pi_{\nu}\mathcal{O}_{K,\nu}
.
Similarly,
L/K
is said to be fully ramified
at
\nu
if both of the following conditions hold:

•

There is a unique place
\omega
over
\nu
, and

•

\mathcal{O}_{L,\omega}P_{\nu}=(P^{\prime}_{\omega})^{[L:K]}
.

In this case we have

\mathcal{O}_{L,\omega}=\mathcal{O}_{K,\nu}[\pi_{\omega}]
for any local
uniformizer
\pi_{\omega}\in\mathcal{O}_{L,\omega}
.
This is proved in detail in
[
O
, §16:4]
.

For any finite dimensional
K
-vector space
V
, we define its
completion at
\nu
by
V_{\nu}=K_{\nu}\otimes_{K}V
. If
\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots\mathbf{v}_{n}\}
is a basis of
V
over
K
, any
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-module

\Lambda
satisfying

a\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{O}_{K}\mathbf{v}_{i}\subseteq\Lambda\subseteq b\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{O}_{K}\mathbf{v}_{i},

for some
a,b\in K
,
is called a full lattice, or more precisely, a full

\mathcal{O}_{K}
-lattice on
V
. Note that this definition
is independent of the choice of a basis. A full lattice
in a subspace of
V
is called simply a lattice in
V
.
When
V
is a
K
-algebra, an
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-order in
V

is an
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-lattice that is also a subring.
Full orders are defined analogously. If
K\subseteq L

are two number fields, then
\mathcal{O}_{L}
is a full

\mathcal{O}_{K}
-order in
L
. Every
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-lattice
in
K
is a fractional ideal.

For any lattice
\Lambda
in a vector space
V
, we denote
by
\Lambda_{\nu}\subseteq V_{\nu}
the completion at
\nu
, which is actually
an
\mathcal{O}_{\nu}
-lattice in
V_{\nu}
. A full lattice
\Lambda
on
V

is completely determined by the family
\{\Lambda_{\nu}\}_{\nu}
of
its completions at all finite places. Furthermore, every
such family satisfying a coherence relation determines
a full lattice.
To be precise, if we fix an arbitrary lattice
\Lambda
,
any family

\{\Lambda^{\prime\prime}(\nu)\}_{\nu}
of local lattices, with

\Lambda^{\prime\prime}(\nu)\subseteq V_{\nu}
, is called coherent
with
\Lambda
if

\Lambda^{\prime\prime}(\nu)=\Lambda_{\nu}
for every
v
outside a finite
exceptional set. This concept is important because
it has the following list of properties, to which we refer
as the local global principle for lattices (
LGPL
):

(
\clubsuit
1)

If
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\prime}
are two lattices, then

\Lambda_{\nu}=\Lambda^{\prime}_{\nu}
for every
\nu
outside
a finite set of exceptional non-archimedean places.
In particular, the coherence property is independent of the
choice of a particular lattice
\Lambda
, so we just speak of
coherent families.

(
\clubsuit
2)

If
\Lambda_{\nu}=\Lambda^{\prime}_{\nu}
for every
\nu
,
i.e., the set of
exceptional places is empty, then we have
\Lambda=\Lambda^{\prime}
.

(
\clubsuit
3)

If
\{\Lambda^{\prime\prime}(\nu)\}_{\nu}
is a coherent family of lattices,
then there exists a lattice
\Lambda^{\prime\prime}
satisfying

\Lambda^{\prime\prime}(\nu)=\Lambda^{\prime\prime}_{\nu}
at every non-archimedean place.

The last property above allows us to modify an existing lattice
at a finite set of places to define a new lattice.
See
[
O
, §81:14]
for details.
These properties allows us to study lattices locally, one place at a time.
One important example of this “local modification” is the following:
Let
a
be an adelic linear transformation, i.e., an element

a=(a_{\nu})_{\nu}\in\prod_{\nu}\mathrm{Aut}_{K_{\nu}}(V_{\nu})

for which all coordinates outside a finite set are
in the stabilizer
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathcal{O}_{\nu}}(\Lambda_{\nu})

of
\Lambda_{\nu}
. Then the adelic image
a\Lambda

is defined by modifying the lattice at the remaining places.
This defines an action of adelic matrices on lattices
that is assumed in all that follows.
It can be used for fractional ideals and orders,
in the latter assuming that automorphisms are ring automorphisms.
Since the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{\nu}
is a discrete valuation domain,
and therefore also a principal ideal domain, local study allows us to use
properties of PIDs even when the class group of the global field

K
is not trivial. In fact, every fractional ideal, principal or not,
has an adelic generator.
Lattices that differ at a single place
\nu

are called
\nu
-variants.

Next we require some well known structure results for lattices, whose
proofs are given here in some detail since they are used in
latter sections to explicitly compute examples, and these
computations follow from the proofs given here.

Lemma 2.1
.

(Strong Approximation Theorem).

For every finite set of non-archimedean places
\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n}
,
every family
\{a_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
with
a_{i}\in K_{\nu_{i}}
and
every real number
M
, there exists an element

a\in K
such that
\nu_{i}(a_{i}-a)>M
, for
1\leq i\leq n
,
and
\omega(a)\geq 0
for
\omega\notin\{\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n}\}
.

Proof.

Let
P_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}

be the maximal ideal corresponding to
\nu_{i}
.
Since the class group of
K
is finite, we have
that
P_{i}^{N_{i}}=b_{i}\mathcal{O}_{K}
for some positive
rational integer
N_{i}
and some algebraic integer

b_{i}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Multiplying by a large enough power
of
b_{1}b_{2}\cdots b_{n}
, we can assume that each
a_{i}

is integral, and the result follows from the Chinese
Remainder Theorem.
∎

Recall that, for a matrix
\mathtt{T}\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}(K_{\nu})
,
the valuation
\nu(\mathtt{T})
is defined as the minimum of the
valuations of the coefficients.
Recall also that we use the convention
\nu(0)=\infty
.

Lemma 2.2
.

(Strong Approximation for

For every finite set of non-archimedean places
\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n}
,

\{\mathtt{T}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}

\mathtt{T}_{i}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(K_{\nu_{i}})

, there exists an element

\nu_{i}(\mathtt{T}_{i}-\mathtt{T})>M
, for

This is immediate from the preceding results and the formulas

For any lattice
\Lambda
in an
n

we write

Lemma 2.3
.

Every full

\mathcal{O}_{K}
-lattice
\Lambda
on a two-dimensional vector
space
V
over
K
is isomorphic, as a module, to the
lattice
\mathcal{O}_{K}\times I\subseteq K^{2}
,
for some fractional ideal
I\subseteq K
.
Furthermore, we can find
I
by the formula

\bigwedge^{2}\Lambda=I(\mathbf{e}_{1}\wedge\mathbf{e}_{2})\subseteq\bigwedge^{2}V
, where
\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2}\}

is a basis of
V
.

Proof.

Define
I
by the identity

\bigwedge^{2}\Lambda=I(\mathbf{e}_{1}\wedge\mathbf{e}_{2})
.
Let
\Lambda^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}_{K}\mathbf{e}_{1}\oplus I\mathbf{e}_{2}
.
Then we can find a local matrix
\mathtt{T}_{\nu}\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}(K_{\nu})

such that
\mathtt{T}_{\nu}\Lambda_{\nu}=\Lambda^{\prime}_{\nu}
,
where
\mathtt{T}_{\nu}
is the identity
for almost every place
v
. The fact that

\bigwedge^{2}\Lambda=\bigwedge^{2}\Lambda^{\prime}
implies that the
determinant of
\mathtt{T}_{\nu}
is a unit locally everywhere, and can be
assumed to be one, since it is easy to write down an explicit
matrix fixing
\Lambda^{\prime}
whose determinant is a prescribed unit.
Now the result follows from the preceding lemma, since the stabilizer
of a lattice in
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(K_{\nu})
is open.
∎

Branches and representations

We are often interested, in Number Theory,
in classifying some type of integral structure.
These structures can be often linked to the concept
of lattice or order. Although frequently omitted,
the main tool to relate these concepts is
the following lemma from group actions:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X
and
Y
be
G
-sets with
G
acting transitively on
X
.
Let
Z\subseteq X\times Y

be a
G
-invariant subset. For every element
x\in X
, let
G_{x}=\mathrm{Stab}_{G}(x)

and let
Y_{x}=\{y\in Y|(x,y)\in Z\}
. Then, there is a natural bijective correspondence
\phi

between the orbit sets
G_{x}\backslash Y_{x}
and
G\backslash Z
sending the class
of
y\in Y_{x}
to the class of
(x,y)\in Z
.

Proof.

If
y\in Y_{x}
and
g\in G_{x}
, then

g\cdot(x,y)=(g\cdot x,g\cdot y)=(x,g\cdot y)\in Z
,
hence
Y_{x}
is
G_{x}
-invariant.
Denote by
[y]
the
G_{x}
-orbit of
y\in Y_{x}
,
and by
[x,y]
the
G
-orbit of

(x,y)\in Z
. If
[y]=[y^{\prime}]
, then
y^{\prime}=g\cdot y
for
some
g\in G_{x}
, whence
[x,y^{\prime}]=[x,y]

as before. It follows that
\phi
is well defined.
If
[x,y^{\prime}]=[x,y]
, there must exist

g\in G
satisfying
g\cdot(x,y)=(x,y^{\prime})
,
so in particular
g\cdot x=x
, and
g\in G_{x}
. This
proves that
\phi
is injective. For the surjectivity,
we observe that, for any element

x^{\prime}\in X
, by the transitivity hypotheses,
there exists
g\in G
such that
g\cdot x=x^{\prime}
.
We conclude that any class of the form
[x^{\prime},y^{\prime}]

can be written as
[x^{\prime},y^{\prime}]=[g\cdot x,g\cdot y]=[x,y]
, where
y=g^{-1}\cdot y^{\prime}
.
In particular, since
Z
is
G
-invariant, if
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in Z
, then

(x,y)\in Z
, whence
y\in Y_{x}
. The result follows.
∎

5
Some lemmas on cyclotomic fields

If we consider
V=L
as a vector space, then the completion

V_{\nu}=K_{\nu}\otimes_{K}L

is canonically isomorphic to the product of local fields

L_{\omega_{1}}\times\cdot\times L_{\omega_{r}}
, where

\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{r}
are all places of
L
lying over

\nu
. Furthermore, under this identification,
the global field
L
corresponds to the diagonal in the product.
A set of elements
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\in\mathcal{O}_{L}

are generators, i.e., they satisfy

\mathcal{O}_{L}=\mathcal{O}_{K}[a_{1},\dots,a_{m}]
, provided that
the following local identity is satisfied at every place
\nu
:

\mathcal{O}_{L,\omega_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{O}_{L,\omega_{r}}=\mathcal{O}_{K,\nu}[\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{m}],

(5)

where
\mathbf{a}_{i}=1\otimes a_{i}=\big(\psi_{1}(a_{i}),\dots,\psi_{r}(a_{i})\big)
, for the coordinate
embeddings
\psi_{i}:L\rightarrow L_{\omega_{i}}
. This is a
particular case of the general result for lattices that we
discus in §2. When
L/K
is unramified at
\nu
,
by Nakayama’s Lemma, identity (
5
) is equivalent
to the following condition:

\mathbb{L}_{\omega_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{L}_{\omega_{r}}=\mathbb{K}_{\nu}[\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{1},\dots,\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{m}],

(6)

where
\bar{\mathbf{a}}_{i}
denotes the image of
\mathbf{a}_{i}

in the product, and
\mathbb{L}_{\omega_{j}}
is the residue fields
of
L_{\omega_{j}}
. In this section we prove some
lemmas on cyclotomic integers that
are needed in the sequel. They are well known,
but we include proofs for the sake of
completeness.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\xi
be a root of unity of order
n
. Then
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]

is the full ring of integers of the cyclotomic field

\mathbb{Q}[\xi]
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the local condition
(
5
) for every rational prime
p
.
Let
n
be the order of
\xi
as a root of
unity. Assume first that
p
does not divide
n
. Then
the cyclotomic polynomial has different
roots over the residue field, so the extension
is unramified at this place, and it suffices to check condition (
6
). Since the extension

\mathbb{Q}[\xi]/\mathbb{Q}
is Galois, the residue
fields on the left hand side of the identity are all
isomorphic, and the Galois group of the global extension
acts transitively on the places
\omega_{i}
.
In fact, the fields
\mathbb{L}_{\omega_{1}}

are isomorphic to the field

\mathbb{F}_{p^{s}}
for the smallest value of
s

for which
n
divides
p^{s}-1
, since this is the field
containing the primitive roots of
1
. The coordinates
of the vector
\bar{\mathbf{a}}
, image of

\mathbf{a}=\big(\psi_{1}(\xi),\dots,\psi_{r}(\xi)\big)
,
have, as minimal polynomials, all irreducible factors of
the cyclotomic polynomial, whence the irreducible
polynomial of the vector itself is the cyclotomic polynomial.
Since this polynomial has the same degree as the extension,
both sides of (
6
) are vector spaces of
the same dimension over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
and the result
follows in this case.

In the general case,
we write
n=p^{t}m
with
p
not dividing
m
.
Denote by
\phi_{p^{t}}(x)=\frac{x^{p^{t}}-1}{x^{p^{t-1}}-1}

the cyclotomic polynomial.
Set
K=\mathbb{Q}(\xi)
,
E=\mathbb{Q}(\xi^{m})
and

F=\mathbb{Q}(\xi^{p^{t}})
.
Then
F/\mathbb{Q}
is unramified at
p
,
and therefore the local condition (
5
)
is satisfied at
p
, as before, for this extension.
On the other hand, the extension

E/\mathbb{Q}
is totally ramified at
p
. In fact,

\xi^{m}-1
is a uniformizer of the extension
E

since it is a root of
\phi_{p^{t}}(x+1)
,
which is an Eisenstein polynomial of degree

p^{t-1}(p-1)
. Since
F/\mathbb{Q}
is
unramified at
p
,

\phi_{p^{t}}(x+1)
is still an Eisenstein
polynomial over
F
. More precisely, it is an Eisenstein
polynomial for every local field of the form

F_{\omega_{i}}
where
\omega_{i}
is a place of

F
lying over
p
. In particular, this implies that

K
has a unique place
\omega^{\prime}_{i}
lying
over each place
\omega_{i}
of
F
.
Now we have

\mathcal{O}_{K,\omega^{\prime}_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{O}_{K,\omega^{\prime}_{r}}=\mathcal{O}_{L,\omega_{1}}[\psi_{1}(\xi)^{m}]\times\cdots\times\mathcal{O}_{L,\omega_{r}}[\psi_{r}(\xi)^{m}]

=(\mathcal{O}_{L,\omega_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{O}_{L,\omega_{r}})[\mathbf{a}^{m}]=\mathbb{Z}_{p}[\mathbf{a}^{p^{t}}][\mathbf{a}^{m}]=\mathbb{Z}_{p}[\mathbf{a}].

This concludes the proof. Note that going from the first line to the second we have applied the principle that

(R_{1}\times R_{2})[(x,y)]\cong R_{1}[x]\times R_{2}[y]

since polynomials with coefficients in a product
are in natural correspondence with pairs of polynomials.
The last equality follows from Bezout’s identity.
∎

Example 5.2
.

It is not true in general that
\mathcal{O}_{K}[\xi]

is the full ring of integers of
K(\xi)
for every
number field
K
. For example, if
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})
,
then
\mathcal{O}_{K}=\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]
, but

\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}=\frac{1+i\sqrt{-5}}{2}
is an integer
in
K(i)
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\xi
be a root of unity of order
n
. Then

\xi-1
is a prime in the ring of integers

\mathbb{Z}[\xi]
of
\mathbb{Q}[\xi]

precisely when
n
is a prime power. In any other case

\xi-1
is a unit.

Proof.

If
n=p^{t}
is a
p
-power, then
\phi_{n}(x+1)
is an Eisenstein polynomial
at
p
, so the root
\xi-1
is a uniformizer at the only place of

\mathbb{Q}[\xi]
lying over
p
. At any other place, the primitive

n
-th root of unity cannot be congruent to
1
, since that would force
all
n
-th roots of unity to be trivial in the corresponding residue field,
but this is not the case. The same argument works at every place,
in case
n
is not a prime power.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\xi
be a root of unity of order
n
. Let
m
be an integer
that is relatively prime to
n
. Then the ring
\mathbb{Z}[\xi]

contains a unit that is congruent to

.

Proof.

If
\xi-1
is a unit, there is nothing to prove, so we assume that

is a prime power. The unit in question is

\frac{\xi^{m}-1}{\xi-1}=1+\xi+\cdots+\xi^{m-1}\equiv m\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 1-\xi).

It is a unit because both, the numerator and denominator of the
fraction generate the same ideal, which is the unique maximal ideal
lying over

. The result follows.
∎

Now we consider the field

K=\mathbb{Q}(\xi+\xi^{-1})
, which is the largest totally real field contained in the
cyclotomic extension. It is the invariant
field of the automorphism taking
\xi

to
\xi^{-1}
, whence its ring of integers
is generated by the elements
\xi^{k}+\xi^{-k}

for

. Since

\xi^{k}+\xi^{-k}
is a polynomial in

\xi+\xi^{-1}
, as follows easily from the iteration

\xi^{k+1}+\xi^{-k-1}=(\xi^{k}+\xi^{-k})(\xi+\xi^{-1})-(\xi^{k-1}+\xi^{-k+1}),

we conclude that the ring of integers in

, where

.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let

In this section we consider a decomposable representations

Note that
\chi_{3}=\chi_{2}\chi_{1}^{-1}

Multiplying by

a unit of the ring

It suffices, therefore, to study the set of maximal
orders containing the image of the representation

this section that

It suffices, therefore to study the set of maximal orders
containing

where
\chi_{0}=\chi(g_{0})
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let

\nu(1-\chi_{0})

Recall that
\mathfrak{a}_{s}
is the path from
0
to
\infty
,
and its vertices correspond to balls containing
0
, i.e.,

is in the standard apartment precisely
when

.
Consider a vertex

of the Bruhat-Tits tree that is

from the standard apartment, and corresponds
to a Ball

. The closest point to

in the standard apartment is

, whence

\nu(a)=n-m
. A triplet of visual limits having

as incenter is
\left\{a,a+\pi^{n},\infty\right\}
.
The Moebius transformation associated with the matrix

sends this triplet to the set

.
According to

♠

5)
, the vertex

This will happen if and only if the following conditions are

Since the normalizer in

Lemma 6.2
.

Let

is not a prime power,
then the number of

. The number of such

Proof.

If the order of the representation
\chi

then the element

is a unit in
\mathbb{Z}[\chi_{0}]
,
and therefore also in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
In particular, every maximal order containing

To generalize the previous result to an arbitrary extension,
we need some additional notation. Let
K
be a field containing

\mathbb{Q}[\chi_{0}]
. Let
\tilde{p}\mathcal{O}_{K}=P_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots P_{r}^{e_{r}}
be the prime decomposition of
the ideal
\tilde{p}\mathcal{O}_{K}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Then
the maximal orders
\mathfrak{D}
containing

\mathtt{r}=\mathtt{r}_{\chi}

are those satisfying both of the following conditions:

•

The vertex
v_{\mathfrak{D},Q}
, corresponding to
\mathfrak{D}_{Q}
,
belongs to
\mathfrak{a}_{s}
at every place
Q\notin\{P_{1},\dots,P_{r}\}
,

•

the distance from the vertex
v_{\mathfrak{D},P_{i}}
to

\mathfrak{a}_{s}
, is at most
e_{i}
, for every
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
.

Locally, the set of maximal orders containing
\mathtt{r}

with the same anchor order is a sub-tree that we call the side branch.
It is non-trivial only for the places
P_{1},\dots,P_{r}
,
and its vertices lie at a distance no larger than
e_{i}
from
the stem. Globally, therefore,
the set of orders with a given anchor order is
in correspondence with the vertices in the product of the side
branches corresponding to the places
P_{1},\dots,P_{r}
.
The group of units
\mathcal{O}_{K}^{*}

acts via Moebius transformation as described before,
leaving invariant every ball containing
0
,
i.e., every vertex on the standard apartment, and
therefore act on every side branch. This defines a
coordinate-wise action on the product of side branches.
Locally, this action obeys Lemma

6.1
, which is used in explicit examples below to
count orbit numbers.

Lemma 6.5
.

Side branches corresponding to different anchor orders can be
identified, non canonically, in a way that commutes with the
action of
\mathcal{O}_{K}^{*}
.

Proof.

In the local Bruhat-Tits we can permute
transitively the stem orders, i.e., the vertices in the standard
apartment, multiplying by powers of
a local uniformizer. This is an action by the group

T\subseteq\mathrm{GL}_{2}(K)

diagonal matrices, and therefore it commutes with the action by units
described above. This identification depend on the choice of
a uniformizer, at every place, whence it is non-canonical.
∎

7
Branches and eigenvalues

Before we go on to generalize the results in the previous section to indecomposable abelian representation, we need a way to compute
the part of a branch that is fixed under the action by conjugation
of the group
\mathfrak{L}^{*}
of invertible elements in a two
dimensional algebra
\mathfrak{L}=K[\mathtt{r}]\subseteq\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)
. These results can be found in
[
AB19
]
,
but we include them here for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
\mathtt{r}
be a matrix with eigenvalues
a,b\in K_{\nu}

with the same valuation.
Then conjugation by
\mathtt{r}

fixes precisely the vertices that are at distance at most

\nu\left(1-\frac{a}{b}\right)
of the maximal path whose
visual limits are the fixed points of the Moebius
transformation defined by the matrix
\mathtt{r}
.

Proof.

This is a straightforward
generalization of Lemma
6.1
if we observe that
the action by conjugation of
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(K_{\nu})

is transitive on algebras isomorphic to
K_{\nu}\times K_{\nu}
.
The statement about the visual limits is immediate if
we observe that
\infty
and
0
are both, the visual
limits of the standard apartment, and the invariant points
of the Moebius transformation
\mu(z)=\frac{az}{b}

defined by the diagonal matrix
\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a&0\\
0&b\end{array}\right)
.
∎

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
\mathtt{r}
be a matrix whose eigenvalues
a,b

lie in an unramified quadratic extension
L_{\omega}
of
K_{\nu}
,
and let denote by
\omega
the extension of the valuation

\nu
to this field. Then conjugation by
\mathtt{r}

fixes precisely the vertices that are at distance at most

\omega\left(1-\frac{a}{b}\right)
from the vertex corresponding
to the unique maximal order containing the ring
of integers in
\mathfrak{L}_{\nu}=K_{\nu}[\mathtt{r}]\cong L_{\omega}
.

Proof.

The Bruhat-Tits tree
\mathfrak{t}
for
K_{\nu}
embeds
into the Bruhat-Tits
\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}

tree for the quadratic extension
L_{\omega}
. Now we apply the preceding result. The generator
\sigma
of the Galois group

\mathrm{Gal}(L_{\omega}/K_{\nu})
interchange the visual limits of
the path, whence this path has a unique invariant point.
This can only be the vertex of the path whose distance
from
\mathfrak{t}
is minimal.
Since Moebius transformations of the form
\mu(z)=az+b

with
a\in K_{\nu}^{*}
and
b\in K_{\nu}
act transitively on

L_{\omega}\smallsetminus K_{\nu}
, we can assume that the
visual limits of the path are units whose image
in the residue field generate the extension of
residue fields. Since the visual limits are a Galois orbit,
they have different images on the residue field, whence the
maximal path connecting them passes through the vertex

v_{0}^{[0]}
corresponding to the ball
B_{0}^{[0]}=\{x\in L_{\omega}|\omega(x)\geq 0\}=\mathcal{O}_{L_{\omega}}
,
which is a vertex in
\mathfrak{t}
.
We conclude that the distance from a vertex
in
\mathfrak{t}
to the path is the same as its distance
to the unique vertex of this path that is contained
in
\mathfrak{t}
. Since the ring
of integers in
\mathfrak{L}_{\nu}=K_{\nu}[\mathtt{r}]
is contained
in a unique maximal order in
\mathbb{M}_{2}(K_{\nu})
, but
it must be contained in the maximal order corresponding to every
vertex in the corresponding path, the last statement follows.
∎

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
\mathtt{r}
be a matrix whose eigenvalues
a,b

lie in a ramified quadratic extension
L_{\omega}
of
K_{\nu}
.
Let
\omega
be the (integral valued) extension of
2\nu

to the field
L_{\omega}
, which we identify with the algebra

\mathfrak{L}_{\nu}=K_{\nu}[\mathtt{r}]
.
If
\omega(\mathtt{r})
is odd,
then conjugation by
\mathtt{r}
fixes no vertex
in the Bruhat-Tits tree
\mathfrak{t}
.
Furthermore, there is a constant
\kappa
, such that,
if
\omega(\mathtt{r})
is even,
then conjugation by
\mathtt{r}

fixes precisely the vertices that are at distance at most

d=\frac{1}{2}\big(\omega\left(1-\frac{a}{b}\right)-\kappa-1\big)

from one the vertices
corresponding to the two maximal orders containing the ring
of integers in
\mathfrak{L}_{\nu}=K_{\nu}[\mathtt{r}]
. Furthermore,
such
d
is always an integer when
\omega(\mathtt{r})
is
even.

Proof.

In this case, the graph that naturally embeds into

\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}
is the barycentric subdivision

\mathfrak{t}_{b}
of
\mathfrak{t}
. It is proved
as before that the Galois action has a unique invariant
point in the path connecting the invariant points of
the Moebius transformation corresponding to

\mathtt{r}
, and such point is the vertex
v
that is closest
to
\mathfrak{t}_{b}
. In this case, we can assume that the
visual limits of the path are uniformizing parameters of the
quadratic extensions, whence the vertex
w
in
\mathfrak{t}_{b}

that is closest to the path is a barycenter.
Let
\kappa
be the distance from
v
to
w
.

Let
\mathfrak{H}
be the ring of integers in

\mathfrak{L}_{\nu}=K_{\nu}[\mathtt{r}]\subseteq\mathbb{M}_{2}(K_{\nu})
.
Since
\mathcal{O}_{L_{\omega}}\mathfrak{H}
is an order
in
L_{\omega}[\mathtt{r}]
,
then the maximal orders in
\mathbb{M}_{2}(L_{\omega})
containing

\mathfrak{H}
correspond to a tubular neighborhood of the
path. Therefore, the two maximal orders in
\mathbb{M}_{2}(K_{\nu})

containing it
must corresponds to the endpoints of the edge in

\mathfrak{t}
having
w
as barycenter. It follows
that a vertex, in
\mathfrak{t}
, is at distance

\omega\left(1-\frac{a}{b}\right)
from the path,
if and only if it lies at a distance

\omega\left(1-\frac{a}{b}\right)-\kappa
from
w
, or
equivalently, at a distance

\omega\left(1-\frac{a}{b}\right)-\kappa-1
from one
of the endpoints of the edge in

\mathfrak{t}
having a
w
as barycenter. This is a distance
in
\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}
, so to obtain the corresponding
distance in
\mathfrak{t}
we must divide by two.

Now, fix a uniformizer
\pi_{\omega}\in L_{\omega}
, and
let
\lambda=x+y\pi_{\omega}\in L_{\omega}
, with
x,y\in K_{\nu}

be arbitrary. Note that
x
and
y\pi_{\omega}
have valuations
with different parity. If
y\pi_{\omega}

is dominant, then
x/\lambda

has positive valuation, whence the relation

\frac{\sigma(\pi_{\omega})}{\pi_{\omega}}-\frac{\sigma(\lambda)}{\lambda}=\frac{x}{\lambda}\left(\frac{\sigma(\pi_{\omega})}{\pi_{\omega}}-1\right),

implies that
\frac{\sigma(\lambda)}{\lambda}-1
has the same
valuation as
\frac{\sigma(\pi_{\omega})}{\pi_{\omega}}-1
.
On the other hand, if
x
is dominant, then

\frac{\sigma(\lambda)}{\lambda}-1=\frac{y\pi_{\omega}}{\lambda}\left(\frac{\sigma(\pi_{\omega})}{\pi_{\omega}}-1\right),

has a larger valuation than

\frac{\sigma(\pi_{\omega})}{\pi_{\omega}}-1
.

Finally, if
\mathtt{r}
is a uniformizer in
\mathfrak{L}_{\nu}
,
its determinant, as a matrix, is a uniformizer of
K_{\nu}
,
and therefore the Moebius transformation associated to

\mathtt{r}
cannot leave invariant any vertex of

\mathfrak{t}
. It must, however, leave the path, and
therefore the vertex
w
, invariant. It follows that

\omega\Big(\frac{\sigma(\pi_{\omega})}{\pi_{\omega}}-1\Big)=\kappa.

Now we conclude from the preceding computations that
invariant vertices in
\mathfrak{t}
exists precisely when

\omega(\mathtt{r})
is even, since this coincide with the condition that
\omega\left(1-\frac{a}{b}\right)-\kappa-1

is non-negative.
∎

Now let
L/K
be an extension of global fields.
We define an ideal
I=I[L/K]\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{L}

via local conditions as follows:
If
L/K
is unramified (split or inert) at a place
\omega
of

we define

. If

I_{\omega}=(\pi_{\omega})^{\kappa+1}=\pi_{\omega}\left(\frac{\sigma(\pi_{\omega})}{\pi_{\omega}}-1\right)=\big(\sigma(\pi_{\omega})-\pi_{\omega}\big).

Now we are ready to prove the main result of this section:

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
\mathtt{r}\in\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)
be a matrix whose

lie in a quadratic extension

.
Then there is a maximal order that is invariant under

if and only if
\frac{a}{b}
is a unit and

, where

The fact that

\mathtt{r}

fixes all vertices in a path, at all places splitting

. At inert places, conjugation by
\mathtt{r}

fixes the vertex corresponding to the unique maximal order containing
the maximal order of

subalgebra invariant. Finally, at ramified places, the condition
follows from the congruence, considering the proof of
the preceding lemma.
We conclude that the given condition is equivalent to leaving at least
one vertex of the Bruhat-Tits tree invariant locally at all places.

Since conjugation by
\mathtt{r}
leaves the order

\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})

invariant, at every place at which

\mathtt{r}\in\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathcal{O}_{K})^{*}
,
i.e., at every place outside a finite set, the result is
a consequence of the
correspondence between global lattices and coherent families
described by
(

♣

1)
-
(

♣

3)
, in §2.
∎

8
Indecomposable abelian representations

In this section we consider representations
\rho:G\rightarrow\mathrm{GL}_{2}(K)
, that are not decomposable over the number field

K
, but they are decomposable over an extension
L/K
. Over
L
,
the representation
\rho
must be isomorphic to the direct sum of
two one-dimensional representations,
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
, since

L
is a field of characteristic
0
, and therefore the group
algebra
L[G]
is semisimple. For every element
g\in G
, the
sum
\chi_{1}(g)+\chi_{2}(g)
is the trace of
\rho(g)
, and therefore
it belongs to
K
. For the same reason, the determinant

\chi_{1}(g)\chi_{2}(g)
is in
K
, whence
\chi_{1}(g)
and
\chi_{2}(g)

belong to an extension
L_{g}/K
of degree
1
or
2
. If
L_{g}=K

for all
g
, then
\chi_{1}
is defined over
K
contrary to
the assumption. Assume
L_{g}\neq K
, so that the eigenvalues

\chi_{1}(g)
and
\chi_{2}(g)
of the matrix
\mathtt{r}=\rho(g)

are different, and satisfy the same quadratic equation
f(X)=0
with
coefficients in
K
. We conclude that
K[\mathtt{r}]\cong K[X]/(f)

is both, a field, and a maximal abelian subalgebra of

\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)
. We conclude that
\rho(G)
is contained in a field,
and therefore it is a cyclic group. We can assume therefore that

G=\langle g_{0}\rangle
is cyclic, which we do in the sequel.
Furthermore, the matrix
\mathtt{r}_{0}=\rho(g_{0})
has no eigenvalues
in the field
K
, but they are
contained in the quadratic extension
L=L_{g_{0}}
of
K
.
Since
\mathtt{r}_{0}^{|G|}
is the identity matrix,
the eigenvalues
of
\mathtt{r}_{0}
are roots of unity, and each generates
the extension
L/K
. Moreover, if

\mathrm{Gal}(L/K)=\langle\sigma\rangle
,
then
\sigma(\chi_{1})=\chi_{2}
.

Since
\chi_{1}(g_{0})
and

\chi_{2}(g_{0})
are roots of unity, the trace

\tau=\chi_{1}(g_{0})+\chi_{2}(g_{0})
and the determinant

\delta=\chi_{1}(g_{0})\chi_{2}(g_{0})
belong to the ring of integers

\mathcal{O}_{K}
. It does not hurt, therefore, to assume

\mathtt{r}_{0}=\textnormal{\scriptsize$\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\delta\\
-1&\tau\end{array}\right)$\normalsize}
.
The order
\mathcal{O}_{K}[\mathtt{r}_{0}]\subseteq\mathbb{M}_{2}(K)
is contained in the maximal order

\mathfrak{H}_{\rho}
of the algebra
K[\mathtt{r}_{0}]
. By the general
theory, the localization
\mathfrak{H}_{\rho,\wp}
at every place

\wp
is contained in the maximal order corresponding to
every vertex in a path
\mathfrak{p}(\wp)
.
The path
\mathfrak{p}(\wp)
consists of a unique vertex,
at places
\wp
that are inert for
L/K
,
an edge, at ramified places
\wp
,
or a maximal path, at split places
\wp
. These are called the stem
orders of the representation
\rho
. As in §
6
, for every maximal
order
\mathfrak{D}
containing
\mathcal{O}_{K}[\mathtt{r}_{0}]

its anchor order is defined as
the unique stem order at minimal local distance
at all places. Also as before, the set of maximal orders containing

\mathcal{O}_{K}[\mathtt{r}_{0}]
with the same anchor order is a product
of side branches. The centralizer of the representation
\rho
,
in this case, is the algebra
K[\mathtt{r}_{0}]
. The group

T=K[\mathtt{r}_{0}]^{*}
acts by conjugation on the set of stem orders.
If we choose a vector
\mathbf{v}\in K^{2}
, then

\mathtt{h}\mapsto\mathtt{h}\left(\mathbf{v}\right)
defines an
isomorphism between
K[\mathtt{r}_{0}]
and
K^{2}
which we use to identify
both spaces in the remainder of this section. Then stem orders
have the form
\mathfrak{D}_{\Lambda}
for a lattice
