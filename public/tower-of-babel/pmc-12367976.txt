Small molecules enhance the high-efficiency generation of pancreatic ductal organoids
Authors: Yuwei Liao, Zhifan Lin, Siyu Li, Xiaolei Yin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvancements in three-dimensional (3D) organoid cultures have created more physiologically relevant models for pancreatic disease research, but efficiently generating mature pancreatic ductal cells remains challenging. In this study, we develop a novel protocol to generate pancreatic ductal organoids (PDOs) with high initiation efficiency and an enrichment of pancreatic ductal cells. By utilizing a cocktail of small molecules, we optimize the culture conditions to improve organoid formation. Our findings demonstrate that this protocol facilitates the formation and expansion of PDOs derived from Sox9-positive ductal cells, including heterogeneous ductal cells and acinar cells. These organoid cultures exhibit remarkable stability, supporting long-term expansion. This system provides an efficient model with potential applications in high-throughput drug screening. Moreover, these organoids recapitulate the exocrine cell composition and may reflect the cellular plasticity between ductal and acinar cells, providing a valuable platform for investigating pancreatic diseases such as pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). The model presents a promising tool for future research aimed at understanding disease mechanisms and potentially helping drug development for pancreatic disorders.Keywords: high establishment efficiency, long-term expansion, pancreatic ductal organoids (PDOs), small molecule cocktail

Introduction

The pancreas is a vital organ with dual functionality, consisting of endocrine and exocrine compartments. The endocrine compartment comprises islets containing alpha, beta, delta, epsilon and PP cells, which regulate blood glucose level by secreting hormones such as insulin and glucagon [1]. The exocrine compartment comprises acinar cells that produce digestive enzymes and ductal cells that transport these enzymes to the gastrointestinal tract for nutrient absorption [2]. Studies have shown that pancreatic ducts, which include various ductal cell types, such as large ducts, small ducts, terminal ducts, intrapancreatic ducts, and pancreaticobiliary ducts, are heterogeneous [3]. Additionally, Krt19-, Hnf1β-, and Sox9-positive cells exist in dynamic equilibrium and represent different ductal subsets [4].

Despite its critical role, the pancreas is susceptible to numerous diseases. PDAC, which primarily originates from ductal cells, is one of the most lethal malignancies due to its late diagnosis and rapid progression [5]. Cystic fibrosis affects the pancreas by altering ductal cell function [6], resulting in thick mucus that obstructs enzyme flow [7]. Pancreatitis, often triggered by enzyme blockage within ducts, causes severe inflammation and damage to both exocrine and endocrine tissues [8]. Studying the detailed mechanisms by which diseases affect pancreatic cell types is crucial for developing effective diagnostics and treatments [ 9 , 10], which require model systems that faithfully mimic pancreatic biology.

The emergence of 3D organoid cultures offers a promising new model for studying pancreatic diseases [ 11, 12], particularly those involving ductal cell dysfunction [13]. Pancreatic organoids, engineered to mimic the cellular composition, structure, and function of the pancreas, are derived from pluripotent stem cells, embryonic pancreatic progenitors, and adult pancreatic cells [ 14– 20]. These organoids provide a controlled, physiologically relevant model system that surpasses traditional in vitro and animal models in probing complex disease mechanisms and assessing therapeutic efficacy [21]. During organogenesis, multiple signaling pathways work in concert to regulate cell differentiation and development. Epidermal growth factor (EGF) supports normal pancreatic growth by modulating cell proliferation and development [ 22, 23]. The precise regulation of various signaling pathways ensures proper pancreatic development [ 24, 25]. Small molecule compounds offer targeted intervention by selectively modulating specific pathways [ 26, 27]. Consequently, the combination of small molecule compounds with organoid culture techniques facilitates the precise reconstruction of organ-like structures in vitro, closely replicating in vivo [ 28, 29].

However, current pancreatic organoids face significant challenges that limit their broader application in research and clinical settings [30]. Organoids derived from pluripotent stem cells often exhibit considerable heterogeneity in their cellular composition, complicating reproducibility and reliability due to complex and inefficient differentiation processes [ 14 , 31]. They frequently represent fetal or neonatal exocrine pancreatic ducts and acini rather than adult lineages [20]. The induction process typically takes approximately one month and is difficult to maintain long-term [28]. Additionally, the efficiency of organoid formation under these conditions is low, ranging from 0.24% to 1.7% [32].

In contrast, organoids derived from adult pancreatic ductal cells, despite their greater tissue specificity, are hindered by limited cellular diversity and substantial challenges in initial culture establishment [17]. Research on mouse ductal cell expansion has demonstrated that partial duct ligation (PDL) induces Lgr5 expression, leading to the expansion of Lgr5 precursor cells capable of differentiating into endocrine cells upon in vivo transplantation [33]. Despite the generation of ductal cells in the organoids, their proportion remains low, with notable deficiencies in other cell types.

In this study, we aimed to increase the efficiency of PDO establishment and the proportion of mature ductal cells within it, thereby increasing their utility. Small molecules can regulate cell fate in organoids by modulating signaling pathways or directly affecting epigenetic states [ 34– 36]. By utilizing a cocktail of small molecules, we developed a novel protocol that efficiently generates PDOs enriched with various ductal cells and acinar cells. These PDOs exhibit remarkable stability, allowing for long-term expansion, and offer better mimicry of the pancreatic environment for investigating pancreatic biology and disease.

Materials and Methods

Mice

Adult male C57BL/6J mice (8-10 weeks old; Shanghai Lingchang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) were used for the isolation of pancreatic ductal cells. Sox9-DreER; Rosa26-Ai66 mice were generously provided by Dr. Bin Zhou at the CAS Center for Excellence in Molecular Cell Science. B6. FVB-Tg(Pdx1-cre)6Tuv/ J ( Pdx1-Cre) mice were obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China). Sox9-DreER; Rosa26-Ai66; Pdx1-Cre (PSA) mice were generated by crossing Sox9-DreER ; Rosa26-Ai66 mice with Pdx1-Cre mice. B6. Cg-Tg(Ins2-cre)25Mgn/ J mice, B6. 129-Bhlha15 tm3(cre/ ERT2)Skz / J ( Mist1-CreERT2) mice, Ai14-Rosa26 mice, and B6 . 129P2-Lgr5 tm1(cre/ ERT2)Cle / J ( Lgr5-EGFP-IRES-creERT2) mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Maine, USA). Hyperglycemia was induced in C57BL/6J mice via intraperitoneal injection of 150 mg/kg streptozotocin (STZ; Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, USA) for one week. All the ER-labeled mice were administered with 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT; Sigma-Aldrich) via intraperitoneal injection for two consecutive days, followed by tissue isolation one week post-injection. All experiments involving animal tissues in this study were conducted in accordance with guidelines approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Tongji University (approval no. TJAB04720101).

Isolation of the pancreatic duct and PDO culture

Freshly isolated pancreatic tissues were digested in 10 mL of DMEM (Gibco, Carlsbad, USA) containing 0.13 mg/mL collagenase IV (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.13 mg/mL DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), and 0.01% penicillin‒streptomycin (Gibco) for 1 h. Following digestion, the tissues were cultured in ECM gel (Ceturegel; Yeasen, Shanghai, China) in 48-well plates. The cell culture medium consisted of 2% B27 (Gibco), 1% HEPES (Gibco), 1% GlutaMax (Gibco), 0.5% penicillin-streptomycin, and 0.2% N-acetyl-l-cysteine (NAC; Sigma-Aldrich) in Advanced DMEM/F12 (Gibco). The culture medium was supplemented with 50 ng/mL recombinant murine epidermal growth factor (EGF; Sino Biological, Wayne, USA), 100 ng/mL recombinant human fibroblast growth factor-basic (bFGF; Sino Biological), 10 nM human gastrin I (gastrin; MCE, New Jersey, USA), 2 μM DMH1 (ApexBio, Houston, USA), 10% RSPO1-conditioned media (made in our own lab), 10 μM forskolin (FSK; ApexBio), 10 μM Trolox (Tro; ApexBio), 5 μM ZnSO 4 (Zn; Sinopharm, Shanghai, China), and 1 μM SB590885 (SB; Selleck, Houston, USA), referred to as the 4F condition. The cultures were maintained for 8 days, with passaging every 3–4 days. Organoids were dislodged from the wells via a 200-μL pipette for mechanical dissociation, followed by continued 3D culture without Y27632 (10 μM; TargetMol, Boston, USA). To test other small molecules, prostaglandin E2 (PGE2, 0.2 μM; ApexBio), SQ22536 (10 μM; Selleck), recombinant human FGF-10 (FGF10, 100 ng/mL; PeproTech, Rocky Hill, USA), nicotinamide (Nico, 10 mM; Sigma-Aldrich), TC-G-1008 (5 μM; Selleck), dabrafenib (Dab, 0.5 μM; Selleck), PLX4720 (PLX, 5 μM; Selleck), GDC-0879 (GDC, 0.5 μM; Selleck), catalase (2.5 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich), dimethyl fumarate (DMF, 5 μM; Selleck), oltipraz (5 μM; Selleck), and L-ascorbic acid 2-phosphate (pVc, 100 ng/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) were added to the culture media.

The organoid medium was prepared as previously described [17], which consists of Advanced DMEM/F12 supplemented with B27, 1.25 mM N-acetylcysteine (NAC; Sigma-Aldrich), 10 nM gastrin I (Sigma-Aldrich), and growth factors, including 50 ng/mL EGF (Peprotech), 10% RSPO1-conditioned media, 100 ng/mL Noggin (Peprotech), 100 ng/mL FGF10 (Peprotech), and 10 mM nicotinamide (Nico; Sigma-Aldrich).

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Organoids were harvested, and total RNA was extracted via TRIzol Reagent (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Reverse transcription of 1 μg of total RNA was performed via the PrimeScript First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (TransGen Biotech). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using a Quant Studio 7 Flex system (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, USA) and SYBR Green reaction mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Each reaction was performed at least in triplicate. Ct values were normalized against Hprt values, and fold changes were determined via the ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences utilized were as follows: Krt19 forward, 5′-CAGGTCAGTGTGGAGGTG-3′ and reverse, 5′-TCAATCCGAGCAAGGTAGG-3′; Hprt forward, 5′-TCAGTCAACGGGGGACATAAA-3′ and reverse, 5′-GGGGCTGTACTGCTTAACCAG-3′.

Immunostaining analysis

Organoids were collected in 1.5-mL EP tubes, washed with 1× Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS; Gibco), and plated on slides. The samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Gibco) for 30 min at room temperature, followed by blocking with buffer containing 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich), 0.5% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich), and 0.5% Tween 20 (Sigma-Aldrich) in DPBS for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After the samples were washed three times with DPBS, they were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature and counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; ApexBio).

Frozen sections were prepared by transferring the clones into embedding molds and fixing them with 4% PFA for 20 min at room temperature. The samples were then embedded in tissue freezing medium (Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany), cryosectioned at a thickness of 8 μm, and subjected to blocking for immunostaining. Details of the antibodies used are provided in Table 1.

EdU cell proliferation assay

5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU; 10 μM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) was added to the cell culture media, which was subsequently incubated for 1–2 days before the media were removed. The samples were incubated with Organoid Harvesting Solution (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, USA) for 1 h, followed by wash with DPBS. The samples were then plated on slides and processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

AOPI and PI staining analysis

Organoids were dissociated into single cells via TrypLE (Gibco). After wash with DPBS, an equal volume of AOPI (Count Star, Shanghai, China) was added. The cells were imaged via an IX73 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) or assessed for viability via the Count Star system. For organoid staining, 1 μg/mL propidium iodide (PI; Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the cell medium, and the mixture was incubated for 30 min. After the samples were washed with DPBS, they were photographed with an IX73 microscope (Olympus).

Data analysis

All the experiments were repeated in triplicate. Cytokeratin-19-positive (Krt19 +) ductal cells were quantified via ImageJ software. Immunofluorescence images were captured using an EVOS7000, an Olympus IX73 or a Zeiss LSM880 fluorescence microscope. The quantitative data are presented as the mean ± SD. Statistical significance was assessed via two-tailed Student’s t test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test, and one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Significance levels are indicated as P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed via GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, USA).

Results

Enhanced generation and characterization of pancreatic ductal organoids (PDO) from healthy and hyperglycemic mice

Pancreatic organoid cultures can be initiated from pancreatic ductal fragments isolated from healthy mice via media containing EGF, RSPO1, Noggin, Gastrin, FGF10, and nicotinamide [33] (collectively referred to as the Org condition). Under these conditions, ductal cells form cyst-like organoids with dispersed Krt19 + ductal cells [33]. Our initial goal was to increase culture initiation efficiency and increase the proportion of Krt19 + ductal cells through small molecule screening ( Figure 1A). While the Org condition supports the generation of PDOs, we found that a combination of four small molecules—Forskolin (F), Trolox (T), ZnSO 4 (Z), and SB590885 (S)—termed the 4F condition significantly promoted organoid initiation and increased the colony-forming efficiency by 3-fold appropriately ( Figure 1B,C), generating organoids containing an average of 2000 cells per organoid ( Supplementary Figure S1A). Moreover, the 4F condition greatly increased the percentage of Krt19 + ductal cells ( Figure 1E,F). Notably, nicotinamide, which was originally included in the Org condition, was removed in the 4F condition because its exclusion increased colony formation and cell proliferation ( Supplementary Figure S1B). On the basis of these optimized basal conditions, which included EGF, bFGF, gastrin, DMH1 (a BMP inhibitor that replaces Noggin) and RSPO1, we found that the individual factors FSK, Trolox, ZnSO 4, and SB590885 significantly upregulated Krt19 expression ( Figure 1D). Therefore, we combined these factors into a cocktail of the 4F condition. We then examined the cellular composition of the PDOs under these new conditions. Immunostaining revealed a heterogeneous expression pattern for the pancreatic ductal markers Krt19 and Hnf1β ( Figure 1E). The organoids consisted of various cell populations, including Krt19 +/Hnf1β – cells, Krt19 +/Hnf1β + cells, and Krt19 –/Hnf1β + cells. Krt19 + cells accounted for approximately 30% of the population, whereas Hnf1β + cells accounted for nearly 11% ( Figure 1E,F). Notably, Hnf1β is specifically expressed in trunk progenitors during pancreatic development, as well as in adult ducts [ 4, 37 ]. These results indicate the presence of diverse ductal cell types within the organoids.

Under the 4F condition, the cells exhibited accelerated proliferation, leading to aggregation and transformation into shrunken spheroids within 8 days ( Figure 1B). The transformation process from vesicular to shrunken organoids began around days 6 to 7, with prominent changes occurring between days 7 and 8 ( Supplementary Figure S1C). Cell viability evaluation via AOPI staining on day 8 revealed a viability of approximately 80% ( Supplementary Figure S1D). PI staining was used to compare the distribution of dead cells between vesicular and shrunken clones. The analysis revealed that the shrunken clones contained more dead cells localized in the central region, whereas the vesicular clones presented fewer dead cells ( Supplementary Figure S1 E).

To further assess the cellular composition of PDOs, we performed immunostaining for specific pancreatic cell type markers. We detected the colocalization of mature ductal markers, including EpCAM, Muc1, and Cftr, with Krt19 and Hnf1β ( Figure 1G), indicating that the PDOs contained mature ductal cells. A small population of acinar cells, as indicated by amylase- and Ptf1a-positive staining ( Figure 1H), was also present. Interestingly, some cells coexpressed both acinar and ductal markers ( Figure 1H), suggesting potential transdifferentiation. Endocrine and progenitor cell types were absent in the organoid, as indicated by the lack of markers such as C-peptide, glucagon (Gcg), somatostatin (Sst) and polypeptide (Ppy) for β, α, δ and PP cells, respectively ( Supplementary Figure S1F). Moreover, the PDOs did not express progenitor markers such as Pdx1 and Nkx6.1 ( Figure 1I), confirming that our induced pancreatic ductal organoids primarily consisted of exocrine pancreatic cells.

Furthermore, the 4F condition robustly established PDOs from STZ-induced diabetic mice ( Supplementary Figure S1G,H), which presented similar expression patterns of Krt19 and Hnf1β to those of normal PDOs, although with a slightly lower proportion of Krt19 + cells (18%) ( Supplementary Figure S1 I,J).

These results demonstrate that the optimized 4F culture condition significantly enhance PDO formation, increasing the proportion of ductal cells in diverse states. The coexistence of Krt19 +/Hnf1β – cells, Krt19 +/Hnf1β + cells, Krt19 –/Hnf1β + cells, and acinar cells reflects the dynamic transformation of heterogeneous ductal populations. This cellular heterogeneity suggests that ductal cells may undergo different pathways of differentiation or transdifferentiation during organoid development. Notably, this condition is effective in generating PDOs from hyperglycemic mice, highlighting its potential applicability in disease modeling.

Critical role of small molecules in PDO formation and Krt19 expression

To identify critical factors for PDO generation, we systematically removed individual small molecules from the 4F condition. The removal of SB590885 or forskolin impacted organoid growth, as indicated by the smaller colony sizes observed under conditions without either factor, highlighting their importance in organoid formation and proliferation ( Figure 2A,B). Additionally, clonal formation rate analysis revealed that removing forskolin significantly inhibited ductal organoid formation, with Trolox and SB590885 removal also reducing clonal formation ( Figure 2C).

Further investigation of Krt19 expression levels revealed that the removal of each small molecule from the cocktail markedly decreased Krt19 + cell numbers, with SB590885, ZnSO 4, forskolin, and DMH1 having the most pronounced effects ( Figure 2A,D). These findings indicate that these molecules are crucial for maintaining a high level of Krt19, a key marker for ductal cells.

Forskolin, recognized for its role in cAMP activation [ 38, 39], has crucial importance in PDO formation, cellular proliferation, and Krt19 expression. We compared the effects of other small molecules that modulate cAMP level. The adenylate cyclase inhibitor SQ22536 decreases cellular cAMP level [40], whereas PGE2 increases intracellular cAMP level [41]. We found that SQ22536 significantly suppressed cell proliferation and colony formation, whereas PGE2 formed colonies with less efficacy than did forskolin ( Figure 2E,F). This may be due to the different potencies of forskolin and PGE2, or the role of forskolin in promoting PDO formation might involve additional mechanisms beyond cAMP activation alone.

ZnSO 4 functions as an activator of Zn ion channels, and zinc ions activate the G protein-coupled receptor GPR39 on the cell surface, leading to the regulation of calcium signaling. To elucidate the mechanism of action of ZnSO 4, we employed the GPR39 inhibitor TC-G-1008 to compare its effects on PDOs with those of ZnSO 4. Replacing ZnSO 4 by TC-G-1008 nearly abolished cell proliferation and colony formation, indicating that ZnSO 4 exerts its effects through GPR39 signaling ( Figure 2G,H).

Additionally, we evaluated the effects of Trolox, a vitamin E analog that inhibits ROS, along with pVc, catalase (an enzyme that neutralizes hydrogen peroxide), and the Nrf2 activators dimethyl fumarate (DMF) and oltipraz, which regulate antioxidant proteins [42]. The results revealed that Trolox, pVc, and Oltipraz had similar colony formation rates, whereas catalase and DMF presented lower rates ( Supplementary Figure S2A,B). Analysis of Krt19 expression revealed that pVc and Trolox produced similar proportions of Krt19 + cells, whereas catalase was present at slightly lower percentages. In contrast, the DMF and Oltipraz groups had significantly fewer Krt19 + cells ( Supplementary Figure S2C). These findings suggest that antioxidant effects are driven mainly by direct ROS inhibition rather than Nrf2 activation.

SB590885 is a b-Raf inhibitor. To further explore its mechanism of action, we replaced it by other b-Raf inhibitors. Dabrafenib (Dab), PLX4720 (PLX), and GDC-0879 (GDC) produced effects similar to those of SB590885 in supporting PDO formation and Krt19 expression ( Supplementary Figure S2D–F), suggesting that SB590885 primarily acts through the b-Raf pathway.

Continuous passaging of PDOs

To assess the long-term expansion capacity of PDOs, we passaged them under 4F conditions. We found that PDOs could be maintained for over 30 passages. The expanded PDOs consistently maintained a substantial proportion of Krt19 + cells throughout the passages ( Figure 3A). Despite a gradual decrease in the proportion of Krt19 + cells over time, the overall structure and health of the organoids were preserved, indicating the robustness of these culture conditions for long-term maintenance.

The initiation of PDO cultures from pancreatic ductal cells involves the transformation of ductal cells into heterogeneous populations, including ductal and acinar cells, which may require different conditions than those needed for maintenance. To test this hypothesis and determine the necessity of each small molecule in maintaining Krt19 expression and organoid proliferation, we systematically removed individual components from the 4F condition during serial passaging. Removing SB590885 or Trolox significantly reduced the proportion of Krt19 + cells, although the colony-forming efficiency remained similar ( Figure 3B,C). These findings highlight the specific role of these molecules in sustaining Krt19 expression rather than general organoid proliferation. In contrast, forskolin was essential for both clonal formation and maintaining Krt19 + cells. Its removal led to a significant decrease in the number and size of organoid colonies, indicating its pivotal role in the PDO culture system ( Figure 3 B–E).

Conversely, removing factors from basal organoid culture conditions, including gastrin, DMH1, or bFGF, had little effect on the proportion of Krt19-positive cells. Notably, EGF removal slightly increased the number of Krt19-positive cells during passaging, whereas EGF was crucial for the initiation of PDOs ( Figure 3C). Immunofluorescence analysis after multiple passages without EGF revealed that P30 organoids still maintained a substantial number of Krt19-positive cells ( Figure 3B,C,F). These results indicate that the conditions required for PDO initiation from ductal cells differ from those needed for long-term maintenance of established PDOs, suggesting that different combinations of factors are necessary for reprogramming ductal cells and maintaining PDOs.

Cellular origin and proliferation dynamics in pancreatic ductal organoids

Research has suggested that Lgr5-positive ductal cells induced during pancreatic injury could serve as pancreatic progenitor cells to initiate the formation of pancreatic organoids [33]. Additionally, studies have indicated the presence of progenitors in pancreatic ducts [43]. To elucidate the cellular origin of PDOs under our conditions, we performed lineage tracing analysis ( Figure 4A). We initially utilized Sox9 labeling to investigate the ductal origin of PDOs ( Figure 4A), as Sox9 serves as a marker for pancreatic ductal cells [ 38, 44 ]. Using the Sox9-2A-DreER; Rosa26 Ai66; Pdx1-Cre mouse model (termed PSA, Figure 4A) to label Sox9 + pancreatic ductal cells, we traced the progeny of Sox9-Cre-derived tdTomato cells. Notably, tdTomato + ductal cells demonstrated strong proliferation capacity. Nearly all the colonies expressed tdTomato, indicating that the PDOs originated from Sox9 + ductal cells ( Figure 4B). Immunostaining revealed the colocalization of tdTomato with Krt19 and Hnf1β, confirming the ductal cell origin. Interestingly, the partial colocalization of tdTomato with Amylase indicated a possible transition of Sox9 + ductal cells to acinar cells ( Figure 4C).

To further examine the contribution of Lgr5 + cells to our PDOs, we performed Lgr5 tracing during PDO induction. We found that no cells were originated from Lgr5 in PDOs ( Figure 4D), indicating that Lgr5 cells are not the primary source of our cultured PDOs. Although Lgr5 expression was reported by Lee et al. (2013) [26], our study utilized a novel cocktail of small molecules, which may account for the different origins of PDOs.

During pancreatic cancer development, acinar cells can transition to ductal cells through acinar-to-ductal metaplasia [45]. To investigate whether PDOs are originated from acinar cells, we employed Mist1-tdTomato for lineage tracing. We observed that Mist1-tdTomato-labeled acinar cells, which were initially present in significant numbers, gradually disappeared during the induction process. These findings suggest that acinar cells do not transdifferentiate into ductal cells in our PDO model ( Figure 4E).

To elucidate the dynamics of cell fate during these processes, we monitored cell proliferation via an EdU incorporation assay. Analysis of the colocalization of EdU with Krt19 and Hnf1β ( Figure 4F) revealed that only a small proportion of Krt19 + ductal cells incorporated EdU, whereas a greater proportion of Hnf1β + progenitor cells did so. Additionally, Krt19 + /Hnf1β + ductal cells also demonstrated EdU incorporation ( Figure 4G), suggesting heterogeneity and dynamic equilibrium among distinct ductal cell states.

EdU addition at different time points revealed that cell proliferation primarily occurred from day 5 to day 6 and decreased starting from day 7 ( Figure 4H). Ki67 staining analysis of day 8 pancreatic ductal organoids confirmed a reduced number of proliferating cells at this stage ( Figure 4I). These findings indicate that the early stage of organoid formation involves ductal cell proliferation, whereas the later stage is characterized by the terminal differentiation of ductal cells, transdifferentiation into acinar cells, and cessation of proliferation.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a protocol to generate pancreatic ductal organoids (PDOs) via a combination of small molecules and growth factors: Forskolin, Trolox, ZnSO 4, SB590885, EGF, bFGF, gastrin, RSPO1 and DMH1, referred to as the 4F condition. This protocol significantly enhances the initiation efficiency and proportion of ductal cells. Additionally, the generated PDOs could be stably expanded for more than 30 passages, providing a robust and physiologically relevant model for studying pancreatic diseases and therapeutic development. The major advantage of our system is the rapid generation of PDOs with a high proportion of mature ductal cells. This is achieved by using adult pancreatic ductal cells as the source material for PDO initiation, providing a defined and accessible cell type restricted to the pancreatic lineage. This approach is more efficient than the use of pluripotent stem cells, which require extended differentiation periods of over a month and generate PDOs that cannot be further maintained or passaged. The incorporation of small molecules in our protocol promoted proliferation and further transformation of heterogeneous pancreatic ductal cells and acinar cells, overcoming the problems of limited cellular diversity and low initiation efficiency typically associated with organoid cultures derived from adult tissue cells.

The generation of organoids necessitates a delicate balance between cellular proliferation and differentiation [46]. Our study demonstrated that the initiation and expansion of PDOs involve processes of cell proliferation followed by the differentiation or transdifferentiation of expanded cells into ductal or acinar cells that cease proliferation. This process may recapitulate in vivo pancreatic transformation during the injury response [47]. The coexistence of various ductal cell types and acinar cells within our PDOs enhances their capacity to model cell‒cell interactions, cell plasticity, and cell fate dynamics, which are crucial aspects of pancreatic development and disease.

Overall, the 4F condition effectively modulates these processes through a combination of small molecules, each targeting specific aspects of cell behavior within the organoids. We found that forskolin and SB590885 play the most pronounced roles in promoting organoid formation and proliferation, while all factors contribute to the generation of ductal cells. In this cocktail, each small molecule serves a distinct function: forskolin activates the cAMP pathway and has been used in organoid cultures of liver organoids [ 48, 49]. Zinc is an essential micronutrient for growth and health and has a potential role in regulating intestinal iron absorption and tissue iron mobilization [50]. Additionally, zinc levels are associated with cellular changes in pancreatitis [51] and are related to the pathogenesis of diabetes [52]. Thus, ZnSO 4 is crucial for pancreatic function [53]. The b-Raf inhibitor SB590885 has been shown to activate the ERK pathway [54], which is consistent with the role of bFGF in the 4F condition. Trolox, a water-soluble analog of vitamin E, serves as an antioxidant that supports cell survival and growth [55]. While pancreatic cancer patients are at increased risk of vitamin deficiencies [56]. Together, these small molecules maintain a balance between cell proliferation and transdifferentiation, creating organoid cultures that are not only expandable but also enriched with diverse ductal cells and acinar cells. This balance is critical for generating physiologically relevant models that can be used for extensive research and therapeutic applications.

We demonstrated that PDOs in the 4F condition are generated from Sox9 + ductal cells, with minimal contributions from other cell types, including Lgr5 + cells or Mist1-positive acinar cells. Lgr5 widely serves as a stem cell marker for a variety of epithelial organs, including the small intestine, colon, stomach, and hair follicles, but is absent in the developing and adult pancreas but is activated within the PDL [57]. In addition, although pancreatic acinar cells have been shown to be plastic during PDAC and can be converted into ductal cells [58], we demonstrated that under the 4F conditions, the generated PDOs are not originated from Mist1 + acinar cells. The heterogeneous ductal cells and acinar cells of PDOs were initiated from Sox9 + cells, suggesting a degree of plasticity in the Sox9 + ductal cell population. The culture conditions possibly allow for the differentiation of a subset of progenitor cells into ductal cells and acinar cells.

While promising, certain limitations remain. A comprehensive analysis of the cell types within PDOs is necessary, particularly when single-cell sequencing is used to better evaluate cellular heterogeneity and maturation. Furthermore, the PDO-based drug screening platform, although showing potential, requires further validation in disease models such as PDAC to demonstrate its clinical relevance and therapeutic applicability. In our study, although the organoids were originated from Sox9 + ductal cells, the presence of amylase + acinar cells suggested transdifferentiation or plasticity in vitro. This could reflect cellular behavior similar to that of ductal-to-acinar transdifferentiation, as observed during acinar damage [59]. Acinar cells are also plastic and dedifferentiate into ductal-like cells under specific conditions. Acinar-to-ductal metaplasia (ADM) is a key mechanism in PDAC [ 45, 60]. Under pathological conditions such as chronic inflammation, acinar cells undergo ADM, adopting ductal-like characteristics that can lead to the formation of pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN), a precursor lesion to PDAC. Investigating plasticity and its relevance to ADM or ductal-to-acinar transdifferentiation could provide insights into early pancreatic cancer mechanisms, drug screening and other pancreatic diseases [61].

In summary, the 4F condition significantly increases the efficiency of PDO formation, reducing the time and resources required to generate these models. Improved long-term culture stability allows for extended studies and repeated experiments, enhancing the reliability of research findings. The high proportion of ductal cells provides a more accurate model for studying adult pancreatic diseases, improving the relevance and applicability of research outcomes. This protocol can be adapted for generating PDOs from various sources, including healthy and diseased tissues, facilitating personalized medicine approaches and patient-specific disease modeling.
