Abstract Zip Data
Christopher Lang

Abstract




1
Introduction

An important tool in the analysis of Shimura varieties is the theory of
G
-zips. In
[
PWZ11
]
and
[
PWZ15
]
, a method was developed to compute the underlying topological space of the stack of
G
-zips. For displays we have a very similar formalism, so we want to generalize this method to a more abstract setting.

The idea in the construction is that the stack of
G
-zips of type
\mu
has a description as a quotient stack
[E\backslash G]
, which is complicated to understand, so we do a refinement process to split the problems into smaller, easier problems. To obtain this refinement process, we look at the following datum: The cocharacter
\mu
fixes two parabolic subgroups
P
and
Q
, and we have

E=\left\{\,(x,y)\in P\times Q\ ;\ \varphi(\bar{x})=\bar{y}\,\right\},

where
\bar{x}
is the projection in the Levi quotient. From
E
we have the two projections
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
to
G
, and we call
(E,G,\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
an abstract zip datum. The action of
E
on
G
in the quotient stack
[E\backslash G]
is given by
e.g=\pi_{1}(e)g\pi_{2}(e)^{-1}
. Given such a zip datum, one can refine it by replacing
E
and
G
by certain smaller groups, and restricting the two morphisms.
Then one shows that in order to understand the orbits of
[E\backslash G]
, it is enough to understand the orbits of the refinement plus some double quotient. This double quotient will look something like
P\backslash G/Q
for parabolics
P
and
Q
, and this is understood by the Bruhat decomposition, as the subset
{}^{I}W^{J}
of the Weyl group of
G
. Now the idea is to do this refinement process over and over, until the process becomes stationary at
[E_{n}\backslash G_{n}]
where we only have one orbit, and then we piece together the orbits of
[E\backslash G]
from the double quotients.

We want to generalize such a construction to arbitrary groups
E
and
G
, as well as two group homomorphisms
\tau,\sigma:E\to G
between them, and an action

E\times G\to G,\;(e,g)\mapsto\tau(e)g\sigma(e)^{-1}
.

Definition 1

(Definition
2.1
)
.

A
zip datum
is a quadruple
{\mathcal{Z}}=(E,G,\tau,\sigma)
consisting of two (abstract) groups
E,G
and two group homomorphisms
\tau,\sigma:E\to G
. Given a zip datum, we can look at the conjugated and the refined zip datum

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Z}}^{x}

\displaystyle=(E,G,\tau,{}^{x}\sigma),

\displaystyle x\in G,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}

\displaystyle=(E_{1},G_{1},\tau,\sigma)\coloneqq(\sigma^{-1}(\tau(E)),\tau(E),\tau,\sigma).

Now, instead of looking at the equivalence relation given by the action above, we look at the following coarser equivalence relation. To define it, we need the group
G_{\infty}^{x}\coloneqq\tau(E_{\infty}^{x})
, where
E_{\infty}^{x}
is the larges subgroup satisfying
{}^{x}\sigma(E_{\infty}^{x})\subseteq\tau(E_{\infty}^{x})
. In many cases,
G_{\infty}^{x}
is the intersection of the
G
’s from the repeated refinements of the zip datum
{\mathcal{Z}}^{x}
.

Definition 2

(Definition
2.10
)
.

Given a zip datum
{\mathcal{Z}}=(E,G,\tau,\sigma)
, we define an equivalence relation on
G
by saying
y\sim x
if there are
e\in E
and
g_{x}\in G_{\infty}^{x}
with
y=\tau(e)g_{x}x\sigma(e)^{-1}
.

One can show that in some cases, for example for
G
-zips, the two equivalence relations have the same orbits, but in general this equivalence relation is strictly coarser. But with this equivalence relation we can show the following

Proposition 3

(Proposition
2.14
)
.

Given a zip datum
{\mathcal{Z}}=(E,G,\tau,\sigma)
and
x\in G
. The map

G_{1}^{x}\to\tau(E)x\sigma(E),\quad y\mapsto yx

maps
\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{x}
-equivalence classes in
G_{1}^{x}
bijectively to
\mathcal{Z}
-equivalence classes in
\tau(E)x\sigma(E)
.

Using this proposition one can again see that in order to understand the entire equivalence relation, one needs to understand it on the smaller zip datum
{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}^{x}
and the double quotient
\tau(E)\backslash G/\sigma(E)
. If the refinement process becomes stationary, then one can use this principle to show that the equivalence classes of
{\mathcal{Z}}
can be fully understood with the double quotients of the repeatedly refined zip data. This can be made precise in the following way: A system of representatives of the double quotients of the refined and twisted zip data can be made into a rooted forest, where a node in the
i
-th generation is a representative of a double quotient at the refinement step
i
, see definition
2.18
. If we denote the
i
-th generation of the forest by
R_{i}
and by
R_{i+1}\to R_{i}
the map that sends a node to its parent, then we get the description

G/\sim_{\mathcal{Z}}\,\cong\lim\big(\cdots\to R_{i+1}\to R_{i}\to\cdots\to R_{0}\big),

as long as
R_{i+1}\to R_{i}
is an isomorphism for
i\gg 0
, see theorem
2.19
.

2
Abstract Zip Data

The stack of
G
-zips can be understood as a quotient stack
[E\backslash G]
, where the group
E
acts on
G
by left and right multiplication with two different projections, namely
E
consists of pairs
(p,q)
coming from parabolic subgroups, and this acts on
G
by
(p,q).g=pgq^{-1}
, see
[
PWZ15
, 3.6]
. In
[
PWZ11
]
, the authors constructed a refinement process for this quotient stacks, which yields an explicit description of the underlying topological space, showing that it equals
{}^{I}W
. We now want to generalize this refinement process to the setting of two arbitrary groups
E
and
G
, as well as two group homomorphisms
\tau,\sigma:E\to G
between them, and an action

E\times G\to G,\;(e,g)\mapsto\tau(e)g\sigma(e)^{-1}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
zip datum
is a quadruple
{\mathcal{Z}}=(E,G,\tau,\sigma)
consisting of two (abstract) groups
E,G
and two group homomorphisms
\tau,\sigma:E\to G
. Given a zip datum, we can look at the conjugated and the refined zip datum

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Z}}^{x}

\displaystyle=(E,G,\tau,{}^{x}\sigma),

\displaystyle x\in G,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}

\displaystyle=(E_{1},G_{1},\tau,\sigma)\coloneqq(\sigma^{-1}(\tau(E)),\tau(E),\tau,\sigma),

where
{}^{x}\sigma(e)\coloneqq x\sigma(e)x^{-1}
. Furthermore, we set

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Z}}_{i}

\displaystyle=(E_{i},G_{i},\tau,\sigma)\coloneqq({\mathcal{Z}}_{i-1})_{1},

\displaystyle i>1,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Z}}_{i}^{x}

\displaystyle=(E_{i}^{x},G_{i}^{x},\tau,{}^{x}\sigma)\coloneqq({\mathcal{Z}}^{x})_{i},

\displaystyle x\in G.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that
\tau
and
\sigma
restrict to maps
E_{i}\to G_{i}
, and these are the maps meant in the zip datum
{\mathcal{Z}}_{i}
. When computing
E_{i}=\sigma^{-1}(G_{i-1})
, it does not matter whether we view
\sigma
as a map defined on
E
or on
E_{i-1}
. For example, for
E_{2}
one sees this due to the equation

E_{2}=\sigma^{-1}(\tau(E_{1}))\subseteq\sigma^{-1}(\tau(E))=E_{1}.

In particular, the two sequences
E_{i}
and
G_{i}
are decreasing.
Also note that

\displaystyle({\mathcal{Z}}^{x})_{1}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\sigma^{-1}({}^{x^{-1}}\tau(E)),\tau(E),\tau,{}^{x}\sigma\bigr)

is not the same as

\displaystyle({\mathcal{Z}}_{1})^{x}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\sigma^{-1}(\tau(E)),\tau(E),\tau,{}^{x}\sigma\bigr),

as the first component differs by conjugation under
\sigma(x)^{-1}
.

Definition 2.3
.

Given a zip datum
{\mathcal{Z}}=(E,G,\tau,\sigma)
, we denote by
E_{\infty}=E_{\infty}({\mathcal{Z}})
the largest subgroup of
E
with
\sigma(E_{\infty})\subseteq\tau(E_{\infty})
.
We write

\displaystyle G_{\infty}=G_{\infty}({\mathcal{Z}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\tau(E_{\infty}),

\displaystyle E_{\infty}^{x}

\displaystyle\coloneqq E_{\infty}({\mathcal{Z}}^{x}),

\displaystyle G_{\infty}^{x}

\displaystyle\coloneqq G_{\infty}({\mathcal{Z}}^{x}),\quad x\in G.

Proposition 2.4
.

The groups
E_{\infty}
and
G_{\infty}
are invariant under refinement, i.e. for a zip datum
{\mathcal{Z}}=(E,G,\tau,\sigma)
we have

E_{\infty}({\mathcal{Z}})=E_{\infty}({\mathcal{Z}}_{1})\quad\text{and}\quad G_{\infty}({\mathcal{Z}})=G_{\infty}({\mathcal{Z}}_{1}).

In particular, we have
E_{\infty}\subseteq E_{i}
for all
i\geq 0
.

Proof.

It is enough to show that if we have a subgroup
F\subseteq E
with
\sigma(F)\subseteq\tau(F)
, then we have
F\subseteq E_{1}=\sigma^{-1}(\tau(E))
. But this follows from

\sigma(F)\subseteq\tau(F)\subseteq\tau(E)
.
∎

Example 2.5
.

For a perfect field
k
in characteristic
p
, take
G=\GL_{2}(W(k))
and
E=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
p&*\end{pmatrix}\right\}
with

\displaystyle\tau(e)

\displaystyle=e

\displaystyle\sigma\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
pc&d\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}p\\
&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\varphi(a)&\varphi(b)\\
p\varphi(c)&\varphi(d)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}p^{-1}\\
&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\varphi(a)&p\varphi(b)\\
\varphi(c)&\varphi(d)\end{pmatrix}

Then
G_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
p^{i}&*\end{pmatrix}
and
E_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
p^{i+1}&*\end{pmatrix}
and
G_{\infty}=E_{\infty}=\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
0&*\end{pmatrix}

Now let
x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
. Then we have

{}^{x}\sigma\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
pc&d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\varphi(d)&\varphi(c)\\
p\varphi(b)&\varphi(a)\end{pmatrix}

and hence
G_{i}^{x}=E_{i}^{x}=\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
p&*\end{pmatrix}
for
1\leq i\leq\infty
.

In particular, we see that in these cases the group
E_{\infty}
is the intersection of the
E_{i}
. But note that this does not need to be the case, as the following example shows.

Example 2.6
.

Let us first work in the category of sets. Let
E=[0,1]
,
G=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{[0,1]}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tau\,:\,x\,\mapsto\,(1+x)|\sin(1/x)|}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sigma\,:\,x\,\mapsto\,1+2x}

and
\tau(0)\coloneqq 0
. Then we have

G_{i}=[0,1+a_{i}],\qquad E_{i}=[0,b_{i}]

with
a_{i},b_{i}>0
both monotonously decreasing to
0
, because
a_{i}\leq b_{i-1}
and
b_{i}=\frac{a_{i}}{2}
. But we have
E_{\infty}=\varnothing
.

One can upgrade this example to the category of groups by looking at the free abelian group generated by
E
resp.
G
, i.e. we set
\hat{E}=\mathbb{Z}^{(E)}
and
\hat{G}=\mathbb{Z}^{(G)}
with
\hat{\tau},\hat{\sigma}:\hat{E}\to\hat{G}
such that
\hat{\tau}|_{E}=\tau
and
\hat{\sigma}|_{E}=\sigma
. Then we have

\hat{G}_{i}=\mathbb{Z}^{(G_{i})}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\hat{E}_{i}=\mathbb{Z}^{(E_{i})}.

This follows because

\hat{\tau}(\hat{E}_{i})=\hat{\tau}(\mathbb{Z}^{(E_{i})})=\mathbb{Z}^{(\tau(E_{i}))}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\hat{\sigma}^{-1}(\hat{G}_{i})=\hat{\sigma}^{-1}(\mathbb{Z}^{(G_{i})})=\mathbb{Z}^{(\sigma^{-1}(G_{i}))},

where the last equality holds as
\sigma
is injective. In particular, we have
\bigcap_{i\geq 0}\hat{E}_{i}=\mathbb{Z}^{(\{0\})}
. But one quickly checks
\mathbb{Z}^{(\{1\})}=\hat{\sigma}(\mathbb{Z}^{(\{0\})})\not\subseteq\hat{\tau}(\mathbb{Z}^{(\{0\})})=\mathbb{Z}^{(\{0\})}
, hence
\hat{E}_{\infty}=\{0\}
.

The reason for this failure is that in general we have
\tau(\bigcap_{i}E_{i})\not=\bigcap_{i}\tau(E_{i})
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Given a zip datum
{\mathcal{Z}}=(E,G,\tau,\sigma)
. If we have
\tau(\bigcap_{i}E_{i})=\bigcap_{i}\tau(E_{i})
, then we get

E_{\infty}=\bigcap_{i\geq 0}E_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad G_{\infty}=\bigcap_{i\geq 0}G_{i}

Proof.

By proposition
2.4
we already know ”
\subseteq
” in the first equation. We need to show that
\sigma(\bigcap_{i}E_{i})\subseteq\tau(\bigcap_{i}E_{i})
. But we have

\sigma\left(\bigcap\nolimits_{i}E_{i}\right)\subseteq\sigma(E_{i})=\sigma(\sigma^{-1}(\tau(E_{i-1})))\subseteq\tau(E_{i-1})

and therefore
\sigma(\bigcap_{i}E_{i})\subseteq\bigcap_{i}\tau(E_{i})=\tau(\bigcap_{i}E_{i})
.
For the second equality we have

G_{\infty}=\tau(E_{\infty})=\tau\left(\bigcap\nolimits_{i}E_{i}\right)=\bigcap\nolimits_{i}\tau(E_{i})=\bigcap\nolimits_{i}G_{i+1}.

∎

Remark 2.8
.

This condition is in particular satisfied, if one (equivalently: both) of the sequences
E_{i}
or
G_{i}
becomes stationary. This happens for example if one of these groups is finite, or if we look at group schemes instead of abstract groups and the
E_{i}
or
G_{i}
are closed subgroups inside a Noetherian scheme. This is the reason why the refinement process becomes stationary in
[
PWZ11
]
. But note that this does not hold anymore if we look at displays instead of zips, as example
2.5
shows. However, in this example,
\tau
is injective, so the condition
\tau(\bigcap_{i}E_{i})=\bigcap_{i}\tau(E_{i})
is again satisfied.

Lemma 2.9
.

Given a zip datum
{\mathcal{Z}}=(E,G,\tau,\sigma)
and
x,y\in G
.

1.

If
y\in G_{1}=\tau(E)
, then
{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}^{yx}=({\mathcal{Z}}_{1}^{x})^{y}
and in particular

E_{\infty}^{yx}(\mathcal{Z})=E_{\infty}^{y}(\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{x})\qquad\text{and}\qquad G_{\infty}^{yx}(\mathcal{Z})=G_{\infty}^{y}(\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{x}).

2.

If
x=y
in
\tau(E)\backslash G/\sigma(E)
, i.e.

, then
E_{1}^{y}={}^{\tilde{e}^{-1}}E_{1}^{x}
.

Proof.

1.

The only thing we need to verify is that
E_{1}^{x}=E_{1}^{yx}
, as then both zip data are just the zip datum
(E_{1}^{x},\tau(E),\tau,{}^{yx}\sigma)
. But this follows from

\displaystyle E_{1}^{yx}(\mathcal{Z})=({}^{yx}\sigma)^{-1}(\tau(E))=\sigma^{-1}({}^{x^{-1}y^{-1}}\tau(E))=\sigma^{-1}({}^{x^{-1}\tau(e_{y})^{-1}}\tau(E))

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-1}({}^{x^{-1}}\tau({}^{e_{y}}E))

\displaystyle=({}^{x}\sigma)^{-1}(\tau(E))

\displaystyle=E_{1}^{x},

where
e_{y}\in E
with
\tau(e_{y})=y
.

2.

We have

\displaystyle E_{1}^{y}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-1}\big({}^{y^{-1}}\tau(E)\big)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-1}\big({}^{\sigma(\tilde{e})^{-1}x^{-1}\tau(e)^{-1}}\tau(E)\big)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-1}\big({}^{\sigma(\tilde{e})^{-1}x^{-1}}\tau(E)\big)

\displaystyle={}^{\tilde{e}^{-1}}\sigma^{-1}\big({}^{x^{-1}}\tau(E)\big)

\displaystyle={}^{\tilde{e}^{-1}}E_{1}^{x}.\qed

Definition 2.10
.

Given a zip datum
{\mathcal{Z}}=(E,G,\tau,\sigma)
, we define an equivalence relation on
G
by saying
y\sim x
if there are
e\in E
and
g_{x}\in G_{\infty}^{x}
with
y=\tau(e)g_{x}x\sigma(e)^{-1}
. If the zip datum may be ambiguous, we also write
y\sim_{\mathcal{Z}}x
. We write
o_{\mathcal{Z}}(x)\subseteq G
for the equivalence class of
x\in G
.

Proof.

We show that this indeed defines an equivalence relation.
Reflexivity is clear. For symmetry assume
y=\tau(e)g_{x}x\sigma(e)^{-1}
. Choose
e_{x}\in\tau^{-1}(g_{x})\subseteq E_{\infty}^{x}
and observe

{}^{y}\sigma({}^{e}E_{\infty}^{x})={}^{y\sigma(e)}\sigma(E_{\infty}^{x})={}^{\tau(e)g_{x}x}\sigma(E_{\infty}^{x})\subseteq{}^{\tau(e)g_{x}}\tau(E_{\infty}^{x})=\tau({}^{ee_{x}}E_{\infty}^{x})=\tau({}^{e}E_{\infty}^{x})

and hence
{}^{e}E_{\infty}^{x}\subseteq E_{\infty}^{y}
. Define
e_{y}\coloneqq{}^{e}e_{x}^{-1}\in E_{\infty}^{y}
. With
g_{y}=\tau(e_{y})
and
\tilde{e}=e^{-1}
we have

x=\tau(e_{x})^{-1}\tau(e)^{-1}y\sigma(e)=\tau({}^{e^{-1}}e_{y})\tau(e)^{-1}y\sigma(e)=\tau(e)^{-1}\tau(e_{y})y\sigma(e)=\tau(\tilde{e})g_{y}y\sigma(\tilde{e})^{-1},

hence
x\sim y
. For transitivity assume
y=\tau(e_{1})g_{x}x\sigma(e_{1})^{-1}
and
z=\tau(e_{2})g_{y}y\sigma(e_{2})^{-1}
. Set
e=e_{2}e_{1}
, then

z=\tau(e_{2})g_{y}\tau(e_{1})g_{x}x\sigma(e_{1})^{-1}\sigma(e_{2})^{-1}=\tau(e)\tau({}^{e_{1}^{-1}}e_{y})g_{x}x\sigma(e)^{-1}

and
{}^{e_{1}^{-1}}e_{y}\in{}^{e_{1}^{-1}}E_{\infty}^{y}\subseteq E_{\infty}^{x}
by the same reason as before, so
z\sim x
.
∎

Remark 2.11
.

The proof shows that if
y\sim x
with
y=\tau(e)g_{x}x\sigma(e)^{-1}
, then we have
{}^{e}E_{\infty}^{x}=E_{\infty}^{y}
.

Remark 2.12
.

Given a zip datum
{\mathcal{Z}}=(E,G,\tau,\sigma)
, we can look at the quotient groupoid
[E\backslash G]
given by the action
e.g\coloneqq\tau(e)g\sigma(e)^{-1}
. The equivalence relation
\sim_{\mathcal{Z}}
is coarser than the one given by this group action, so we get a map
|[E\backslash G]|\to G/\sim_{\mathcal{Z}}
. In particular, if we know the equivalence classes of
\sim_{\mathcal{Z}}
, we get a decomposition of
G
by unions of orbits of the group action. In the case of
G
-zips, each equivalence class is even a single orbit, so the map above is bijective, see
[
PWZ11
, 9.18]
.

Example 2.13
.

If
\tau
is surjective, there is only one equivalence class, as in this case
G=G_{\infty}^{x}
for all
x\in G
and we can choose
e
such that
y=\tau(e)
and then
g_{x}\coloneqq\sigma(e)x^{-1}
. This is in particular the case for the zip datum
{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty}^{x}\coloneqq(E_{\infty}^{x},G_{\infty}^{x},\tau,{}^{x}\sigma)

Proposition 2.14
.

Given a zip datum
{\mathcal{Z}}=(E,G,\tau,\sigma)
and
x\in G
. The map

\Psi_{\mathcal{Z}}^{x}:\underbrace{G_{1}^{x}}_{=\tau(E)}\to\tau(E)x\sigma(E),\quad y\mapsto yx

maps
\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{x}
-equivalence classes in
G_{1}^{x}
bijectively to
\mathcal{Z}
-equivalence classes in
\tau(E)x\sigma(E)
. Furthermore, we have

o_{\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{x}}(y)\cdot x=o_{\mathcal{Z}}(yx)\cap G_{1}^{x}\cdot x,\qquad y\in G_{1}^{x}.

Proof.

We first show the equation. An element
z\in G_{1}^{x}=\tau(E)
is in
o_{\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{x}}(y)
if and only if there are
e\in E_{1}^{x}
and
g_{y}\in G_{\infty}^{y}(\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{x})\overset{\ref{E_infty_multiplicative}}{=}G_{\infty}^{yx}
such that
z=\tau(e)g_{y}y({}^{x}\sigma(e))^{-1}
. On the other hand,
zx
is in
o_{\mathcal{Z}}(yx)
if and only if there are
\tilde{e}\in E
and
g_{yx}\in G_{\infty}^{yx}
with
zx=\tau(\tilde{e})g_{yx}yx\sigma(\tilde{e})^{-1}
, or equivalently
z=\tau(\tilde{e})g_{yx}y({}^{x}\sigma(\tilde{e}))^{-1}
. Hence we want to choose
e=\tilde{e}
and
g_{y}=g_{yx}
, and the only thing we need to verify for this is that
\tilde{e}\in E_{1}^{x}=({}^{x}\sigma)^{-1}(\tau(E))
. But after rearranging we have
{}^{x}\sigma(\tilde{e})=z^{-1}\tau(\tilde{e})g_{yx}y\in\tau(E)
, as every factor is in
\tau(E)
, so we are done.

Now we show the first claim. The equation we just showed implies that the map on equivalence classes is well-defined and injective.
But an element of the form
\tau(e)x\sigma(\tilde{e})
is
\mathcal{Z}
-equivalent to

\tau(\tilde{e})\cdot 1\cdot\tau(e)x\sigma(\tilde{e})\sigma(\tilde{e})^{-1}=\tau(\tilde{e}e)x,

which lies in the image of the map, so we are also surjective on equivalence classes.
∎

This proposition suggests that if we change
x
inside one double coset, then the refined zip datum should not change too much. We want to make this precise next.

Lemma 2.15
.

If
G
is a group,
H,K\subseteq G
are subgroups and
x,y\in G
, then

\displaystyle H\backslash G/K

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\sim}{}^{x}H\backslash G/{}^{y}K,

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle\mapsto xgy^{-1}

is a well-defined bijection.

Proof.

We need to check that
g
and
hgk
are mapped to equivalent elements for
h\in H
and
k\in K
. This follows from

xhgky^{-1}=xhx^{-1}xgy^{-1}yky^{-1}=({}^{x}h)xgy^{-1}({}^{y}k)\sim xgy^{-1}.

The inverse is just
g\mapsto x^{-1}gy
.
∎

Lemma 2.16
.

Given groups
G,H
and a
G
-set
X
, an
H
-set
Y
, a group homomorphism
\psi:G\to H
and a map
f:X\to Y
, then we obtain a functor of groupoids
[G\backslash X]\to[H\backslash Y]
if
f(g.x)=\psi(g).f(x)
holds for all
g\in G
and
x\in X
.

Proof.

On objects, the functor maps
x
to
f(x)
, so we need to check that if we have

g\in\Hom(x,x^{\prime})=\left\{\,g\in G\ ;\ g.x=x^{\prime}\,\right\},

then we get

\psi(g)\in\Hom(f(x),f(x^{\prime})),

and for this one needs

\psi(g).f(x)\overset{!}{=}f(x^{\prime})=f(g.x)
. Morphisms are compatible with composition as
\psi
is a group homomorphism, so we indeed get a functor.
∎

.

induces an equivalence of groupoids
[E_{1}^{x}\backslash G_{1}^{x}]\xrightarrow{\sim}[E_{1}^{y}\backslash G_{1}^{y}]
.

Proof.

For the bijection we need to check that

{}^{\tau(\tilde{e})^{-1}}\tau(E_{1}^{x})=\tau(E_{1}^{y})\qquad\text{and}\qquad{}^{\tau(e)}\bigl({}^{x}\sigma(E_{1}^{x})\bigr)={}^{y}\sigma(E_{1}^{y}).

The first one follows directly from the equation
E_{1}^{y}={}^{\tilde{e}^{-1}}E_{1}^{x}
from lemma
2.9
. For the second equation we compute

{}^{\tau(e)}\bigl({}^{x}\sigma(E_{1}^{x})\bigr)={}^{\tau(e)x}\sigma({}^{\tilde{e}}E_{1}^{y})={}^{y\sigma(\tilde{e})^{-1}}\sigma({}^{\tilde{e}}E_{1}^{y})={}^{y}\sigma(E_{1}^{y}).

By lemma
2.16
, such a map
\Psi
induces a map of quotient groupoids if we have
\Psi(\varepsilon.g)=\Psi(\varepsilon).\Psi(g)
. This equality holds as

\displaystyle\Psi(\varepsilon).\Psi(g)

\displaystyle=\tau({}^{\tilde{e}^{-1}}\varepsilon)\tau(\tilde{e})^{-1}g\tau(e)^{-1}\cdot{}^{y}\sigma({}^{\tilde{e}^{-1}}\varepsilon)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\tau(\tilde{e})^{-1}\tau(\varepsilon)gx\sigma(\tilde{e})\sigma({}^{\tilde{e}^{-1}}\varepsilon)^{-1}y^{-1}

\displaystyle=\tau(\tilde{e})^{-1}\tau(\varepsilon)gx\sigma(\varepsilon)^{-1}\sigma(\tilde{e})y^{-1}

and

\displaystyle\Psi(\varepsilon.g)

\displaystyle=\tau(\tilde{e})^{-1}\tau(\varepsilon)g\cdot{}^{x}\sigma(\varepsilon)^{-1}\tau(e)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\tau(\tilde{e})^{-1}\tau(\varepsilon)gx\sigma(\varepsilon)^{-1}x^{-1}\tau(e)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\tau(\tilde{e})^{-1}\tau(\varepsilon)gx\sigma(\varepsilon)^{-1}\sigma(\tilde{e})y^{-1}.\qed

Now we can describe the
{\mathcal{Z}}
-equivalence classes using the refinement process. This description will be helpful, if we understand the double quotients
\tau(E)\backslash G/\sigma(E)
. If
G
is a matrix group, these can often be understood with Bruhat decompositions, and then these double quotients will be subsets of the Weyl group of
G
. The idea now is that if we want to understand the equivalence classes, we need to understand the double quotient, and each point in the double quotient again looks like a zip datum, which we investigate in the same way. So in the end, if we want to specify an equivalence class, we can equivalently give a sequence of elements of certain double quotients. This is the same idea as if you want to specify where you are in the world, you give the continent, then the country, then the state and so on.

In order to make this description formal, we use the language of a
rooted forest
from graph theory, which is a graph without cycles, together with one distinguished node for each connected component. These nodes are called the roots of the forest. We say that a node lives in generation
n
, if the unique path to the root of its connected component has length
n
. In particular, the roots live in generation 0. Every rooted forest
R
has a unique decomposition
R=\coprod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}R_{n}
into its generations.

Definition 2.18
.

A
system of representatives

R=\coprod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}R_{n}
for a zip datum
{\mathcal{Z}}=(E,G,\tau,\sigma)
is a rooted forest with vertices in
G
, such that the roots are a system of representatives of
\tau(E)\backslash G/\sigma(E)
and a vertex
x_{n}\in R_{n}
has as children a system of representatives of
\tau(E_{n+1}^{\tilde{x}_{n}})\backslash G_{n+1}^{\tilde{x}_{n}}/{}^{\tilde{x}_{n}}\sigma(E_{n+1}^{\tilde{x}_{n}})
, where
\tilde{x}_{n}=x_{n}\cdots x_{0}
is the product along the unique path to the root of the tree (
x_{i}
is the parent of
x_{i+1}
).

Theorem 2.19
.

Given a zip datum
{\mathcal{Z}}=(E,G,\tau,\sigma)
with a system of representatives
R
. Assume that
R
becomes stationary in the sense that
R_{N}=R_{N+1}
for
N\gg 0
. Then we have a bijection

G/{\sim_{\mathcal{Z}}}\cong\left\{\,(r_{n})\in\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}R_{n}\ ;\ r_{n}\text{ is the parent of }r_{n+1}\,\right\}=\lim_{n}R_{n},

where the transition maps are given by mapping an element to its parent.

Proof.

The bijection is given by mapping an
x\in G
to the vector
(r_{n})
characterized in the following way: The zeroth entry is the unique
r_{0}\in R_{0}
such that
x=r_{0}
in
\tau(E)\backslash G/\sigma(E)
. The children of
r_{0}
are a system of representatives for the double quotient of the zip datum
{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}^{r_{0}}
, and
r_{1}
is the representative of
xr_{0}^{-1}
in this double quotient, compare proposition
2.14
. In general,
r_{n}
is the representative of
xr_{0}^{-1}\cdots r_{n-1}^{-1}
in the double quotient of
{\mathcal{Z}}_{n}^{r_{n-1}\cdots r_{0}}
. The inverse is given by
(r_{n})\mapsto r_{N}\cdots r_{0}
for
N\gg 0
.
∎

Example 2.20
.

Let’s apply this theorem to the zip datum of example
2.5
, namely
G=\GL_{2}(W(k))
and
E=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
p&*\end{pmatrix}\right\}
. To compute
R_{0}
we must understand

\tau(E)\backslash G/\sigma(E)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
p&*\end{pmatrix}\right\}\backslash\GL_{2}(W(k))/\left\{\begin{pmatrix}*&p\\
*&*\end{pmatrix}\right\}.

This is a special case of the (affine) Bruhat decomposition from
[
KP23
, 5.1]
, namely it has two elements, represented by the identity matrix and
x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
. For these two points we already computed the refined zip data. For
x
we saw that
\tau
is surjective on
{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}^{x}
, so the double quotient is trivial (as well as the double quotients for all the refined ones). For the identity matrix the situation is less clear, as the refinements of the zip datum don’t become stationary. The double quotient of the
i
th refinement is

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
p^{i+1}&*\end{pmatrix}\right\}\scalebox{2}{$\backslash$}\raisebox{5.0pt}{$\left\{\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
p^{i}&*\end{pmatrix}\right\}$}\scalebox{2}{$/$}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}*&p\\
p^{i}&*\end{pmatrix}\right\},

and we claim that all of them only consist of one element. For this it is enough to show that given a matrix
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
p^{i}c&d\end{pmatrix}
, we can find a matrix
\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
p^{i+1}\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}
, such that

\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
p^{i+1}\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
p^{i}c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\left\{\begin{pmatrix}*&p\\
p^{i}&*\end{pmatrix}\right\}.

All we need to do for this is to show that the upper left entry, which equals
\alpha b+\beta d
, is divisible by
p
. Note that
\alpha
must be a unit, but
\beta
can be chosen arbitrary, so we can set
\alpha=\delta=1
,
\gamma=0
and
\beta=-bd^{-1}
. Hence all these double quotients are trivial and we see that
R_{n}=R_{0}
for all
n>0
, so that there are exactly two
\sim_{\mathcal{Z}}
-equivalence classes.

Example 2.21
.

The ”induction step” from

\displaystyle E_{\mu}=\left\{\,(p,q)\in P\times Q\ ;\ \varphi(\bar{p})=\bar{q}\,\right\}

\displaystyle\mapsto p

\displaystyle\sigma:(p,q)

In our language, the group

Let

and

be the Levi quotients. Let

P_{1}^{x}\coloneqq M\cap{}^{\dot{x}^{-1}\dot{y}^{-1}}P\quad\text{and}\quad Q_{1}^{x}\coloneqq\varphi(L\cap{}^{\dot{y}\dot{x}}Q)

We end the discussion of zip data with two results on the structure of the groups
E_{\infty}
and
G_{\infty}
.

Lemma 2.22
.

Given a zip datum
{\mathcal{Z}}=(E,G,\tau,\sigma)
, then we have

E_{\infty}=\left\{\,e\in E\ ;\ \sigma(e)\in G_{\infty}\tau(e)G_{\infty}\,\right\}.

Proof.

If
e\in E_{\infty}
, then
\sigma(e)\in\tau(E_{\infty})=G_{\infty}
, and hence

\sigma(e)=\underbrace{\tau(e^{-1})}_{\in G_{\infty}}\tau(e)\underbrace{\sigma(e)}_{\in G_{\infty}}.

For ”
\supseteq
” suppose that
e\in E
with
\sigma(e)\in G_{\infty}\tau(e)G_{\infty}
. Now look at the group
F
generated by
e
and
E_{\infty}
. We want to show that it satisfies
\sigma(F)\subseteq\tau(F)
, so that
e\in E_{\infty}
. For this it satisfies to show that
\sigma(e)\in\tau(F)
, as we already have
\sigma(E_{\infty})\subseteq\tau(E_{\infty})
and all elements of
F
can be built by
e
,
e^{-1}
and
E_{\infty}
. But

\sigma(e)\in\underbrace{G_{\infty}}_{=\tau(E_{\infty})\subseteq\tau(F)}\quad\underbrace{\tau(e)}_{\in\tau(F)}\quad\underbrace{G_{\infty}}_{=\tau(E_{\infty})\subseteq\tau(F)}\subseteq\tau(F).\qed

Proposition 2.23
.

The group action

\displaystyle E_{\infty}^{x}\times(E\times G_{\infty}^{x})

\displaystyle\to E\times G_{\infty}^{x}

\displaystyle\big(\varepsilon,(e,g)\big)

\displaystyle\mapsto\varepsilon.(e,g)\coloneqq\big(e\varepsilon^{-1},\tau(\varepsilon)g\;{}^{x}\sigma(\varepsilon)^{-1}\big)

makes the map

\displaystyle E\times G_{\infty}^{x}

\displaystyle\twoheadrightarrow o_{\mathcal{Z}}(x)

\displaystyle(e,g)

\displaystyle\mapsto\tau(e)gx\sigma(e)^{-1}

into an
E_{\infty}^{x}
-torsor.

Proof.

We need to show that
(e,g)
and
\varepsilon.(e,g)
have the same image in
o_{\mathcal{Z}}(x)
and that if
(e,g)
and
(\tilde{e},\tilde{g})
have the same image in
o_{\mathcal{Z}}(x)
, then there is a unique
\varepsilon\in E_{\infty}^{x}
with

(\tilde{e},\tilde{g})=\varepsilon.(e,g)=\big(e\varepsilon^{-1},\tau(\varepsilon)g\;{}^{x}\sigma(\varepsilon)^{-1}\big).

The first is just a quick computation, and for the second claim we are forced to define
\varepsilon=\tilde{e}^{-1}e
. Then the equation we need to show is true because by assumption we have

\tau(\tilde{e})\tilde{g}x\sigma(\tilde{e})^{-1}=\tau(e)gx\sigma(e)^{-1}

and hence

\tilde{g}=\tau(\underbrace{\tilde{e}^{-1}e}_{=\varepsilon})gx\sigma(\underbrace{\tilde{e}^{-1}e}_{=\varepsilon})^{-1}x^{-1}.

All that’s left is to verify that we have
\varepsilon\in E_{\infty}^{x}
. But for this we use that we have

E_{\infty}^{x}\overset{\ref{description_E_infty_via_G_infty}}{=}\left\{\,e\in E\ ;\ {}^{x}\sigma(e)\in G_{\infty}^{x}\tau(e)G_{\infty}^{x}\,\right\}

after observing
{}^{x}\sigma(\varepsilon)=\tilde{g}^{-1}\tau(\varepsilon)g
.
∎
