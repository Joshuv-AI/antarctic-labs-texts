PII Jailbreaking in LLMs via Activation Steering Reveals Personal Information Leakage Thanks: Preprint. All the data subject names and LLM responses have been anonymized.
Krishna Kanth Nakka, Xue Jiang, Xuebing Zhou

Abstract
This paper investigates privacy jailbreaking in LLMs via steering , focusing on whether manipulating activations can bypass LLM alignment and alter response behaviors to privacy related queries (e.g., a certain public figure’s sexual orientation ). We begin by identifying attention heads predictive of refusal behavior for private attribute (e.g., sexual orientation ) using lightweight linear probes trained with privacy evaluator labels. Next, we steer the activations of a small subset of these attention heads—guided by the trained probes—to induce the model to generate non-refusal responses. Our experiments show that these steered responses often disclose target attribute details, along with other personal information about data subjects—such as life events, relationships, and personal histories—that the models would typically refuse to produce. Evaluations across four LLMs reveal jailbreaking disclosure rates of at least 95%, with more than 50% on average of these responses revealing true personal information. Our controlled study demonstrates that personal information memorized in LLMs can be extracted through targeted manipulation of internal activations.

1
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have been shown to memorize information from their training data
(
Nasr et al. 2023
;
Carlini et al. 2021
)
, which often includes web-crawled content from a wide range of public and semi-public sources. This raises significant privacy concerns for data subjects whose personal information may be unintentionally retained and revealed. To mitigate these risks, LLMs typically undergo an alignment phase
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
;
Peng et al. 2023
)
, during which they are fine-tuned to adhere to safety and privacy guidelines. Despite these efforts, recent research
(
Liu et al. 2023
;
Chao et al. 2023
;
Mehrotra et al. 2024
)
has demonstrated that LLMs can be jailbroken—i.e., prompted or manipulated to bypass alignment constraints and generate restricted outputs. However, most existing benchmarks
(
Mazeika et al. 2024
;
Souly et al. 2024
)
emphasize harmfulness or copyright violations, without focus on the leakage of personally identifiable information (PII) tied to specific individuals.

In this work, we investigate privacy jailbreaking in LLMs via
steering
. Our attack focuses on
public figures
as data subjects and
sexual orientation
as the private attribute, chosen for its verifiability and sensitivity. We aim to answer two key questions: (1) Can aligned LLMs be steered to produce non-refusal responses to privacy-sensitive prompts that probe PIIs? (2) If so, do the resulting responses disclose factual personal information or merely hallucinate? Our approach first identifies attention heads whose activations are predictive of refusal behavior by training lightweight linear probes. We then intervene on a subset of these heads to steer the model’s output toward disclosure. This setup avoids the use of attacker LLMs and instead directly modifies internal activations of the target model.

Our experiments yield two key findings.
First
, attention head activations can reliably predict binary refusal/disclosure behavior given prompts, and steering the top-
k
heads induces non-refusal responses to sensitive queries.
Second
, we find that these steered responses often align with factual personal information, revealing real-world details such as relationships and personal events that would otherwise be refused. Overall, this study highlights a critical privacy risk: sensitive information memorized during pretraining can be extracted by intervening in internal model representations of the aligned LLMs. This underscores the need for more rigorous privacy testing by LLM providers.

2
Related Work

Privacy Leakage Assessment.

Previous benchmarks for privacy leakage evaluation, such as TrustLLM
(
Sun et al. 2024
)
and Decoding Trust
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
, primarily focus on the leakage of email addresses in the Enron Email dataset
(
Shetty and Adibi 2004
)
, which is part of the PILE corpus
(
Gao et al. 2020
)
. However, email PII is often sanitized using regular expressions during pretraining, and the strict assumption that LLMs are pretrained on Enron subjects makes these benchmarks less effective for assessing real-world leakage. In contrast, our study shifts the focus to the leakage of sensitive sexual orientation information about public figures, who are often included in pretraining data from diverse sources.

LLM Jailbreaking.

Numerous jailbreaking techniques have been proposed, including prompt-based attacks
(
Li et al. 2023
)
using auxiliary LLMs
(
Chao et al. 2023
;
Mehrotra et al. 2024
)
and linguistic perturbations
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
. Among these, LLM steering has emerged as a compelling paradigm for analyzing model behavior from a mechanistic perspective. While prior work has applied steering to elicit unsafe or policy-violating content
(
Cao et al. 2025
;
Cao et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Kirch et al. 2024
)
, our work uniquely focuses on using targeted LLM steering to probe privacy leakage of data subjects.

3.1
Non-Adversarial Privacy Leakage Evaluation

Benchmark Construction.
To perform privacy leakage jailbreaking, we first identify subjects likely to be present in LLM training corpora, along with the ground-truth values for a relevant private attribute. However, pretraining datasets are often regarded as core assets by large model enterprises and are typically not released to the public. Hence, we cannot directly obtain information about datasubjects within these datasets to construct a universal benchmarks. Instead, we focus on a plausible scenario: extracting private information about public figures, under the assumption that these data subjects are frequently included in pretraining data. 3.2
LLM Steering

In this work, we adopt
steering
as our jailbreak strategy due to its controbillity and without any dependence on auxiliary attacker models
(
Chao et al. 2023
;
Mehrotra et al. 2024
)
.

We initiate the jailbreak by directly manipulating the activations of the model’s internal layers in a targeted manner
(
Kim et al. 2025
)
. Specifically, for each input prompt
\mathbf{x}_{d}
(ie., excluding the response
\mathbf{y}_{d}
), we extract the attention activations
\mathbf{A}=\{\mathbf{a}_{d}^{l,h}\}
from the self-attention modules of the target LLM
\mathbf{f}
, across every layer
l
and head
h
.

Probe Training.

Given these set of activations
\{\mathbf{a}_{d}^{l,h}\}
and the corresponding jailbreak labels
y_{d}
obtained from privacy evaluator, we train a linear probe model
w_{d}^{l,h}
independently for each attention head activations
\mathbf{a}_{d}^{l,h}
. Each probe is trained on a balanced dataset: label
\mathbf{1}
is assigned to
refused
prompts, and label
\mathbf{0}
to
disclosed
prompts. For instance, the LLaMA-7B
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
contains
32
layers with
32
heads each, resulting in 1024 total probes. For our experiments, we use a balanced dataset of
112
subjects (
56
per class), with a
50/50
train-validation split.

Intervention.

We select the top-
k
attention heads based on F1-score performance on the validation set. For the remaining evaluation subjects with label other than
declosed
response label (denoted as
D_{test}
), we perform a steering intervention by modifying the activation of the last token at every time-step as follows:

\mathbf{a}_{d}^{l,h}=a_{d}^{l,h}+\alpha w

(1)

where
\alpha
is a tunable scaling coefficient, and
w^{l,h}
is the learned probe weight vector of the corresponding probe model. Finally, as in previous steps, we evaluate the response content after steering with the privacy evaluator and further verify the factuality of the response information using GPT-4
(
OpenAI 2023
)
.

4.1
Probes Predict Privacy Refusal Behavior

As shown in Figure
4

(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
, the best-performing probe achieves AUCROC and F1 scores of 0.89 and 0.83, respectively, on the validation set. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of localizing privacy refusal behavior within LLMs when prompted.

4.2
Steering LLMs to Privacy Jailbreak

Next, we consider the 830 data subjects that constitute
D_{test}
, whose responses
y_{d}
to the given prompt
\mathbf{x}_{d}
, without any steering, exhibit behavior other than
disclosed
, as evaluated by the privacy evaluator.

We then generate steered responses to the same prompt
\mathbf{x}_{d}
by shifting the activation of the top-
k
attention heads with strength
\alpha
, as shown in Equation
1
. In Figure
5
(a), we present a heatmap illustrating the number of data subjects in
D_{test}^{R}
whose responses, when evaluated by the privacy evaluator, are labeled as
disclosed
. We observe that, among the tested hyperparameters,
k=96
and
\alpha=-70
lead to the highest disclosure rate, with 589 subjects disclosed from a total of 830. However, the optimal values for
\alpha
and
k
vary for different models, as shown in Figure
7
in Appendix. Moreover, we find that the probability of disclosing at least once across all 66 steering combinations for each data subject is
97%
. This suggests that steering serves as a effective attack method to induce non-refusal behaviors in LLMs when responding to privacy-probing prompts.

4.3
Do Jailbroken Responses Contain Real Privacy Leakage?

While steering can induce non-refusal behavior in LLMs, our primary goal is to determine whether the responses labeled as
disclosed
by the privacy evaluator actually contain verifiable personal information about the data subjects. To assess factual accuracy, we use GPT-4
(
OpenAI 2023
)
with the prompt provided in Appendix
6
, classifying steered responses into one of four categories:
Factual
,
Hallucination
,
Speculative
, or
Inaccurate
. Notably, the factuality evaluator considers the entire generated response—which often includes information beyond sexual orientation—to make a holistic judgment on whether the generated content is factually accurate. Additionally, the factuality evaluator is also instructed within the prompt to produce a concise reasoning and enumerate the verifiable personal information included in the response.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigate privacy jailbreaking by steering the activations of attribute-discriminative attention heads using lightweight probes. We demonstrate that such steering leads LLMs to generate responses that not only bypass refusal behaviour but also disclose factual, personal information about data subjects. Overall, our findings highlight that privacy-related prompts with targeted steering can serve as a stress test for assessing the extent of memorization of personal information in LLMs.

6
Limitations

Our work has several limitations. First, we rely on automatic privacy evaluation via prompting, but the use of LLM-as-a-Judge
Gu et al. 2024
introduces inherent biases and can lead to labeling errors. Second, the factual accuracy of steered responses is assessed using GPT-4
(
OpenAI 2023
)
, which may also produce evaluation errors. In future work, we aim to address these issues by developing more robust privacy evaluators and leveraging agents with web access to verify factual claims. Finally, our analysis is limited to a single private attribute; extending this framework to other sensitive and verifiable attributes remains an important direction for future research.

7
Broader Impact and Ethics Statement

Our work could potentially be exploited by attackers to probe and extract personal information about data subjects. However, the primary motivation behind this research is to demonstrate a deep understanding of the memorization capabilities within LLMs. By uncovering the memorized information about data subjects, we aim to contribute to efforts that safeguard the rights of these individuals. We curate the data subjects by prompting GPT-4 using prompt in Appendix
4
. Furthermore, for ethical reasons, we anonymize both the data subject names and the LLM responses.

Appendix A
Implementation Details

We use pretrained, instruction-tuned models from HuggingFace
(
Wolf et al. 2019
)
. For response generation, we allow a maximum of 200 tokens and apply greedy decoding for the target LLM. For the privacy evaluator, we employ top-
k
sampling with
k=1
, as we observed that greedy decoding did not consistently produce outputs in the expected structured format.
