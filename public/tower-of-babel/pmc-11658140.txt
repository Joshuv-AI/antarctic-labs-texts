Prevalence and influencing factors of medication-related burden among patients with late-life depression in typical city of eastern China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Dan Liu, Linghe Qiu, Lu Han, Yajing Wang, Fei Wang, Xiaowei Liu, Jianhong Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AimTo evaluate the medication-related burden (MRB) of patients with late-life depression (LLD) and its influencing factors in China using the Living with Medicines Questionnaire-3 (LMQ-3), providing reference for reducing the MRB of those patients.MethodA cross-sectional study was conducted between September 2023 and January 2024 on 588 patients with LLD. LMQ-3 and MRB factors questionnaire were used for data collection. The distribution of variables was assessed using descriptive analysis, while analyses of Mann-Whitney and Kruskal-Wallis were performed to evaluate inter-group differences. To explore the MRB among patients with LLD and influencing factors, multiple linear regression analysis was performed.ResultsThe median (IQR) LMQ-3 score of 588 participants was 102 (18), indicating a moderate MRB level. Regression analysis revealed a significant trend toward higher perceived burden among patients aged 70–79 years old, living in rural areas, receiving more medical insurance settlements, using all cash, taking more than 5 drugs each time, and taking medicine more than 3 times a day (p < 0.05), which were risk factors for higher MRB. Conversely, patients who lived with their children, had an annual household income (including adult children) more than 50,000 Chinese Yuan, and no adverse drug reactions had lower LMQ-3 scores (p < 0.05), which were protective factors. Patients’ concerns about medicine, their lack of autonomy in medicine regimens, and the lack of communication between patients and doctors on treatment regimens were the main causes of the burden.ConclusionsResults of this study provided preliminary evidence of the MRB among patients with LLD. Age, residence, living status, annual household income, type of drug payment, quantity and frequency of medication, and adverse reactions significantly affected the perceived medication burden. It is advisable for health policy makers and health care providers to implement appropriate intervention strategies and burden reduction programs for this vulnerable group.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20939-2.

Introduction

Late-life depression (LLD) stands as one of the most increasingly serious global problem, which usually refers to the depression of people aged 60 years and above [1]. Some studies suggest that LLD affects 1.8 -7.2% of older adults in the community, which represents a common mental health challenge for them [2]. Moreover, LLD seriously affects the quality of life in later life, leading to pain or disability, worsens medical outcomes and increases mortality [3]. Although antidepressants may be effective in treating depression in older adults, they tend to pose greater risk of adverse events and burden due to multiple comorbidities and drug-drug interactions [4]. Previous study of correlation between polypharmacy and LLD had emphasized many factors may increase the prescription behavior of patients with LLD [5].

Research by Janet Krska et al. found that patients who prescribe psychiatric and neurological drugs have a relatively heavy medication-related burden (MRB) [6]. MRB is an important part of the overall treatment burden. Since drugs are the main means of managing most diseases, MRB is the most common part of the treatment burden in qualitative research [7]. Patients with LLD often combined underlying disease, therefore, the main focus of clinical practice guidelines and health care practitioners is to achieve the desired clinical outcomes for each disease, which may lead to better therapy management, but also result in complex medication regimens and prescription cascades [8]. In addition, aging increases the fragility of drug management in older adults [9]. With the aggravation of global aging, the MRB of patients with LLD is facing unprecedented challenges.

A systematic review of 34 articles included in 12 countries revealed that MRB not only seriously affected medication experience and overall health status, but also played a central role in the patient’s medication belief and practice [10]. In addition, the MRB can reflect the medication ability, willingness and attitude of patients to a certain extent [11]. As a result, drug burden measurement may provide an important perspective for identifying and reducing these drug-related obstacles and improving medication compliance.

Most of the published literature interprets MRB as the number of drugs or pills taken by patients every day, or transfers it to the economic aspect of the treatment burden [12]. However, this ignores the key points and subjective feelings of patients [13]. Although some evaluation strategies such as treatment satisfaction questionnaire for medication [14], resisting medicines study [15], cholinergic burden [7], medication life experience [10]and drug management ability [16]are based on patients’ feelings, the dimension is relatively single. Janet Krska et al. developed the Living with Medicines Questionnaire-3 (LMQ-3) from the patient’s perspective, to assess eight dimensions of medication that may impose burdens on patients, including relationships with health professionals, practicalities, indications, effectiveness, side effects, concerns, cost, and autonomy [17]. It quantifies the problems encountered in taking drugs and explores the burden that drugs may bring to people’s daily life with a strategy of subjective structural evaluation. Existing research clearly proved that LMQ-3 has high reliability and effectiveness, and has been verified in some countries [6, 17–19].

However, as far as we know, there is no study on the quantitative evaluation of MRB in patients with LLD using LMQ-3. The existing work using the LMQ-3 scale mainly focuses on the entire elderly population or medication status. For example, Abdelmoneim Awad et al. reported that elderly participants in Kuwait suffered a medication burden of a minimum of 35.4% to a moderate 62.0% [20]. Yongli Wang’s team translated LMQ-3 and confirmed that elderly polypharmacy patients with underlying diseases have a relatively heavy burden of medication in China [21]. For patients with LLD, medication burden evaluation focusing on subjective perception may be more important. Therefore, this study aims to quantitatively analyze the current situation and influencing factors of MRB among patients with LLD in China using the LMQ-3 scale, and to evaluate the relevant sociodemographic characteristics.

Our research holds pivotal importance in the understanding and improving the increasing burden of medication in patients with LLD. Although some patients may benefit from antidepressant therapy, the balance between treatment benefits and drug burden in patients with LLD is being reshaped due to physiological changes in aging that alter the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of drugs in the body, with a significant increase in the risk of adverse events and drug-drug interactions [4]. Seriously, the reduced compliance caused by these potential medication burdens will directly affect the implementation of patients’ medical programs [22]. The issue of population aging in China is becoming increasingly serious, and it is a paramount problem to pay attention to the drug burden of the older adults. Moreover, there is a dearth of research data on many antidepressants in the elderly population, and the safety data in the elderly with chronic physical diseases are extremely lacking [23], while the onset time and consolidation maintenance period required by these patients are often longer. All of these increase the uncertainty of medication among patients with LLD and increase their perceived burden, especially for those with anxiety, agitation, excessive worry, and catastrophic thinking. In addition, depression is a risk factor for suicide in older adults, and the risk factor for LLD is also a risk factor for suicide [24]. The possibility of suicide caused by the aggravation of depressive symptoms in older adults caused by medication burden should be actively excluded. Therefore, gaining an in-depth understanding of the MRB and its influencing factors in patients with LLD is crucial to strengthen the medication management of this vulnerable group.

Materials and methods

Study design and data sources

This cross-sectional study was conducted between September 2023 to January 2024 in Wuxi using a questionnaire survey to patients with LLD based on convenience sampling. Taking into account a standard deviation of 11.3 through literature review [21]and preliminary experiments, and an allowable error of 1, a sample size of 493 was calculated at the two-sided test and a margin of error of 5% (α = 0.05). Considering the loss rate of 20%, the required sample was 617. Finally, 588 samples were recovered, and the overall response rate was about 95.30%. The sample size meets the requirements of reliability and validity of the scale. The inclusion criteria were patients with LLD, defined as those meeting the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for depressive disorders and aged 60 years or older [25]. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients who had hearing impairment, cognitive disorder or inability in communication. (2) Other serious physical diseases and unable to respond properly to the investigation. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Mental Health Center of Jiangnan University (No. WXMHCIRB2023LLky007). All participants signed informed consent before the survey.

Variables and measurements

Dependent variable

The Chinese version of LMQ-3 scale was used to investigate the MRB level [13, 17, 26]. The scale consists of 39 items in 8 dimensions (Tables S1 in supplemental appendix) and 5-point Likert scale was used to assess participants’ attitudes towards these items. Each statement had five answers: ‘Strongly Agree’, ‘Agree’, ‘Neutral’, ‘Disagree’, ‘Strongly Disagreed’. It is recorded as 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 points, respectively. The total score of each interviewee’s attitude was the sum of the scores to each question. Statements of negative wording were reverse-coded to calculate the score. The cumulative scores for each domain are shown in Table 1, with the total cumulative score ranging from 39 to 195 points. (the Chinese version deleted two items during the cultural debugging process, but it was basically consistent with the English version of the LMQ dimension [17]). The higher the score, the greater the perceived burden associated with drug use. Cronbach’s α was examined to assess the internal consistency. LMQ-3 scale also includes a visual analogue scale (VAS) to measure the overall burden from 0 to 10 scores. Permission was obtained from the original developers of the questionnaires.

Independent variables

In this study, several independent variables were considered to analyze the factors associated with MRB. The research team conducted an extensive review of the literature on MRB [20, 21, 27], and then identified three main influencing factors, comprising a total of 20 items. These factors include socio-demographic characteristics, economic status and medical insurance type, physical condition and medical condition. These associated factors were used as independent variables to prepare the ‘Medication-Related Burden and its Influencing Factors Questionnaire’ for data collection (Table S2 in the supplemental appendix). Since the total variable is not normally distributed, median (interquartile range, IQR) was used for analysis.

Data collection

On-site surveys were conducted to collect data at home visits, health lectures, long-term care facilities, and hospitals. The participants were given instructions by the researchers on how to complete the questionnaire and the purpose of the study. Researchers or family members can read the questionnaire to help the participants complete it. For patients who are inconvenient to write, family members can fill in the questionnaire on their behalf after the participants answer the question.

Bias control

This study adopts strict data management methods, such as double entry, cross-examination and controversial data were discussed and backtracked by multiple professionals to ensure data accuracy and reliability. Double entry was conducted by two independent data entry operators, followed by automated comparisons to identify discrepancies. Cross-examination was performed for all inconsistencies, where the original data sources were revisited to confirm and correct potential errors. In cases where ambiguity or disagreements arose, discussions were held within the research team to reach a consensus on the most accurate representation of the data. Backtracking was employed whenever unexpected patterns or outliers were detected, allowing the research team to trace and rectify errors at any stage of data processing.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to evaluate the distribution of variables. Microsoft Excel 2016, PASS 15.0.5 and SPSS 26.0 was used for data collation, sample size calculation, and data analysis, respectively. Percentage, median (IQR), and range were used to describe the demographic characteristics of patients with LLD and LMQ-3 score. Multivariate linear regression analysis was used to explore the MRB level and influencing factors. Independent variable was statistically significant variables in the univariate analysis (p < 0.05). Because the LMQ-3 scores were not normal distribution based on the Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov normality tests, the Mann-Whitney and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to compare the MRB (LMQ-3 domain scores and overall scores) of respondents with different demographic and medical-related characteristics. Multicollinearity and tolerance in linear regression models are evaluated by testing the variance inflation factor (VIF). Statistical significance was accepted as p < 0.05.

Socio-demographic characteristics, economic status and type of medical insurance, physical condition and medical condition of the respondents were considered to be related to MRB. Hence, three models were constructed: model I included social factors such as demographics, economic status and type of medical insurance; model II included individual factors such as physical condition and medical condition; and model III incorporated both social and individual factors. The Durbin-Watson test was used to further evaluate the independence of regression model residuals. The mean and standard deviation of the residual histogram were used to evaluate the normality of regression model residuals. Finally, Model III, which initially included all factors with significant differences, was determined as the best fitting model based on multi-factor evaluation (Table S3 in the supplemental appendix). β value of p < 0.05 was used to identify factors associated with MRB.

Influencing factors related to MRB

Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to assess the factors of MRB in patients with LLD (Table 6). Statistically significant indicators (p < 0.05) from univariate analysis in Table 2 were considered as independent variables, whereas the LMQ-3 score served as the dependent variable. The results demonstrated that age, residence, living conditions, annual household income, types of drug payment, type of each medication, frequency of medicines and adverse drug reactions were significantly related to MRB in patients with LLD. The MRB of patients with LLD aged 70–79 years old, living in rural areas, more medical insurance settlement, medication all at their own expense, taking 5–15 drugs each time, medication more than 3 times a day were heavy, which were risk factors (p < 0.05). Those who lived with their adult children, had an annual household income of more than 50,000 CNY, and did not report adverse reactions had a light MRB, which were protective factors (p < 0.05).

Discussion

China is facing an increasingly serious crisis of population aging, and the series of challenges brought by aging are major public health and social problems. Previous work has confirmed that the MRB of older adults with chronic diseases in China is heavy [21]. As one of the most common mental disorders in the elderly, depression is also one of the mental diseases with the highest incidence. Its drug management strategy is complicated and polypharmacy therapy is more common than non-depressive patients [5]. More importantly, depression is a well-known predictor of poor treatment compliance in patients with chronic diseases [28]. Consequently, it is particularly important to evaluate the MRB of patients with LLD, promote the best drug prescription and improve their health and quality of life.

Our research revealed that the total score of LMQ-3 in patients with LLD was 102.05 ± 14.35 (median 102 (18)), which was at a moderate burden level, slightly lower than the score based on Chinese elderly patients with chronic diseases in Zhengzhou, China (112.9 ± 11.3) [21]. Similarly, a study conducted in Kuwait for elderly patients with chronic diseases showed that the median was 112 (21), which was higher than our findings [20]. There has been no relevant research on MRB specifically focused on patients with LLD. Existing research on adults in the southeast of England [27], people living with HIV [29], and adults in New Zealand [18]have indicated a total LMQ-3 score of 99.7 ± 19.8, 88 (median), and 101.8 ± 21, respectively. Some factors, such as differences in participant population, regional differences, differences in Chinese and English LMQ-3 scores, heterogeneity in age range, economic factors and different cultural variations, can help explain the differences in LMQ-3 levels across these studies.

As indicated from the domain analysis, top three main drivers of MRB in older adults with depression included concern about medicine use, patients’ lack of autonomy in drug regimens, and lack of communication with healthcare professionals. The first two driving factors are the same as the results of Zhengzhou, while another factor in Zhengzhou was the cost-related burden [21]. The possible reason is the difference in the income level of residents between Chinese cities. As a typical city in east China, Wuxi’s Gross Regional Product Per Capita in 2021 was 187,415 CNY [30], while Zhengzhou was 100,092 CNY [31], which was 87.24% lower than Wuxi. Therefore, compared with Zhengzhou, drug-related cost may not be one of the driving factors of MRB among patients in Wuxi. At the same time, the top three drivers showed in Kuwait’s research were concern about medicine use, lack of autonomy in drug regimens, and concerns about side effects [20]. All these studies have emphasized that elderly patients have more concerns about medicine use and lacking autonomy in medication regimens. It is noteworthy that the generally higher score under the dimension of concern about medicine use among patients with LLD when compared with the results in Kuwait. This can be explained by the fact that older adults with depression often have anxiety and agitation, manifested as excessive worry and catastrophic thinking [32], which aggravates anxiety about medication treatment, especially with regard to the potential long-term damage of taking medicine and the drug-drug interaction. In addition, cognitive impairment makes drug management vulnerable for patients with LLD [4], which somewhat heightens their concerns. Based on this, whether to keep close communication with healthcare professionals on doubtful issues such as medicine use, treatment options, and drug management is another key factor affecting patients with LLD. Health professionals can conduct intensive health literacy interventions for those patients to improve their drug cognition. Where possible, give them more autonomy of medicine use, listen to and gain insight into their life experiences with the medication, including any difficulties and concerns in the medication plan. In addition, communication with those patients should be fully strengthened [10], and psychotherapy can be used if necessary, to enhance the positive drug experience, reduce their drug burden and help them to make the best use of drugs.

In this study, age significantly affected MRB. The overall burden perceived by 70–79 years old was more significant when compared with those aged 60–69 years old, which is consistent with the findings of most existing research [20, 33]. A study conducted in New Zealand reported opposite results [18]. One possible reason is that the age group of the study participants differs from that in other studies. In our study, significantly higher scores in two domains related to practical difficulties and side effects may partly explain our results. With age increasing, the cognitive function and self-care ability of patients with LLD gradually decline, which increases their vulnerability in making medication plans, adapting to medication frequency, and completing daily medication [34], resulting in more practical difficulties. Especially among patients aged 80–89 years old, which showed highest score in the dimension of practical difficulty, reflecting that they often need to spend more time every day focusing on drug management, and seriously affects their daily life. However, long-term treatment makes them worried about the efficacy. These were shown in the corresponding dimensions. In addition, patients aged 70–79 are more concerned about side effects, especially in those with chronic diseases. Patients with LLD often have drug combination, and the changes in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics caused by the increasing age amplified the risk of side effects [35]. Therefore, for older patients, health care providers should pay more attention to the practical difficulties of medication and side effects. With the help of pharmacogenomics and other methods, active medication planning and follow-up management should be carried out to reduce the burden.

Our study highlighted that patients living in rural areas have a heavier burden of medication than urban patients. A reasonable explanation is that rural patients have a weak ability to obtain medical resources and information. Large general hospitals built by the government are often located in urban areas, while rural areas are relatively scarce [36]. However, the incidence and mortality of some diseases in rural areas are heavy [37, 38]. This huge contrast intensify the medical anxiety of rural respondents, and this kind of anxiety will amplify those perceived burden [39, 40]. Some studies even pointed out that 75.4% of patients with depression in rural areas did not receive professional treatment, and the incidence of depression was higher than that in urban areas [41]. Inconvenient medical channels occupy most of the time among those patients, affect daily life, and also increase the barriers of communication between patients and doctors, which leads to the lag of adjustment in medical plan, delays the assessment of side effects and efficacy. In addition, studies have confirmed that rural residence is a risk factor for mild cognitive impairment [42], which is a huge challenge for the completion of medication. In terms of medical insurance, rural patients participate in MIUR (formerly named as New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme [43]), which is an inclusive medical insurance for non-urban employee launched by the Chinese government. However, the reimbursement ratio is lower than MIUE [44], which result in a relatively heavy cost burden in rural areas. These factors are similar to those observed in a study from Kuwait. Although the response options were different, their findings indicated that non-Kuwaiti nationals experienced a higher medication burden compared to Kuwaiti nationals [20]. The underlying reason may be that differences in medical resources across regions or nationalities lead to varying levels of medication burden. Therefore, it is necessary to increase the investment in medical health funds and personnel, pay attention to the actual medical treatment of rural patients, and effectively intensify the protection of medical resources in rural areas. At the same time, Internet medical care for patients with chronic diseases can be promoted to break the limitation of long-distance medical treatment and share the results of medical reform with urban residents. It is necessary to intervene in health promotion and disease self-management in rural areas [45]to reduce the impact of the MRB on the areas of daily life and economic expenditure. Meanwhile, policy-maker can further rise the reimbursement ratio of MIUR for chronic diseases and special diseases to reduce the economic burden.

Our study also demonstrated that the significant association between depressed older adults living with adult children and light MRB. Previous studies have indicated that high levels of family support reduce the risk of depressive symptoms in the older adults [46]. Although the relationship between adult children and parents’ happiness in their later years is controversial, the traditional Chinese filial piety culture has a far-reaching impact, and intergenerational cohabitation is still the preferred way of life for most elderly people [47]. Such intergenerational companionship is an important spiritual pillar for most elderly people in China [48], and even a protective factor for depressive symptoms [49, 50]. Hence, for patients with LLD, the intergenerational companionship of children can partially reduce the patient’s medication burden. Older adults usually need certain life support. When living with their parents, on the one hand, adult children can exercise some social functions on behalf of patients, such as communicating with doctors, expressing medical wishes and feedback on curative effects. On the other hand, adult children can provide more support in drug management of their parents, such as medication reminder, medication guidance and adverse reaction identification, to reduce practical difficulties in drug use and improve effect. In addition, adult children can provide a certain degree of economic support for older adults. Interestingly, we found that the pension of participants had no significant difference in the MRB, while the annual household income, especially the income of adult children, had a positive protective effect on the reduction of MRB. The economic security brought by adult children living together may increase well-being [50]and reduce patients’ medication anxiety, including obtaining better drugs to increase efficacy, lower drug cost burden, side effect concerns and impact of drugs on life. Unfortunately, the proportion of depressed elderly people living with their adult children is relatively low. In the future, policies and culture can be tilted to promote children’s intergenerational support for parents.

Not surprisingly, drug payment significantly affected the patient’s burden. Similar with our research, most studies support the relationship between financial factors and MRB, including income [18]and medication costs [21]. In our study, compared with those who enjoy the full reimbursement policy, patients with LLD who are required to cover all or a portion of the medication charge have a heavier MRB. Although China has almost achieved the goal of universal coverage of medical insurance, such as MIUE, MIUR and Supplementary Catastrophic Health Insurance, the economic burden of depression is relatively heavy [51], and there are still some drugs and medical services that need to be paid at own expense, such as brand-name drugs. In addition, China’s basic medical insurance system is affected by many factors such as economic foundation and institutional environment, showing a certain degree of regionalization [52]. Importantly, China is currently conducting a consistency evaluation of generic drugs, which have a higher reimbursement ratio and use priority than the brand-name drugs when passed the evaluation [53, 54]. As a result, in clinical practice, patients with LLD who require long-term antidepressant treatment have to pay a considerable proportion of the drug or at their own expense if they desire to use brand-name drug. Despite benefiting from medical insurance, persistent out-of-pocket medical payments may still be more than they can afford. Furthermore, the majority of hospitals currently prioritize using generic drugs, and patients need to spend a lot of energy to obtain brand-name drugs, including communication with doctors and hospitals, which greatly increases the burden on patients. Some patients who have to turn to generic drugs still harbor misgivings about them and their potential side effects [55]. In turn, it may affect the actual medication experience and efficacy. It can be seen that the drug payment affects almost all dimensions except autonomy, which shows that economic factors actually influence the patient’s MRB. Future policies should actively ameliorate the comprehensive clinical evaluation of generic drugs, promote the reform of medical insurance, increase the proportion of reimbursement, and reduce the medication burden for patients with LLD through medical insurance measures such as chronic disease medical insurance and medical assistance.

Our research also emphasized that medication more than 5 drugs each time and taking more than three times a day significantly increased the MRB among patients with LLD. Studies from New Zealand [18], Bahrain [56], and among HIV patients [29]all reported similar results. Using five or more drugs at the same time is usually the concept of polypharmacy, which is an increasingly serious global problem [57]. Previous studies have manifested that polypharmacy therapy is common among patients with LLD, even more than the increase in the incidence of comorbid physical diseases [58]. However, polypharmacy therapy is a potential reversible risk factor for mild cognitive impairment [59]. Moreover, LLD often coexists with cognitive impairment [4]. Therefore, depressed elderly people with polypharmacy have a greater risk of cognitive impairment, which is easy to miss and take wrong medication in the face of complex treatment schemes, and significantly increases the practical difficulties of medication. When patients use a variety of drugs every day, they need to spend more time, energy, and money on medication practice management, including drug selection, drug reserve, drug-related precautions, drug contraindications, and drug purchase [60], which significantly affect their daily life, especially those who need long-term medication [61]. In fact, the phenomenon of non-compliance among polypharmacy elderly patients is common [62]. Taking more drugs significantly affects their recognition of efficacy, exacerbating concerns about drug interactions and side effects. Furthermore, some scholars believe that unreasonable polypharmacy treatment is associated with poor clinical outcomes, such as increased in adverse drug events [63, 64] and hospitalization rates [65]. Therefore, health care providers should actively pay attention to the prescriptions of those patients, and reduce the prescription cascade through drug reorganization and multidisciplinary consultation when necessary. In addition, tools such as Screening Tool of Older Persons Prescriptions, Beers criteria [66], and Screening Tool to Alert doctors to Right Treatment [67] can be used to avoid potentially inappropriate medications in older people. Meanwhile, pharmaceutical health literacy intervention can be carried out on patients and their families to increase medication compliance.

Our work further confirmed that patients with no adverse reactions after medication have a light burden of medication. Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics alter with age in patients with LLD, along with comorbidities and changes in drug use patterns, potentially raising the risk of adverse events [68], which impact the experience and adherence to therapy [69]. Moreover, serious adverse reactions will result in the switch of drug or increase the frequency of irregular medication, or even interrupt the medication. Our study found that adverse reactions affect patients’ perception of efficacy and the trust in physicians’ treatment options. In addition, adverse reactions affect the daily life of patients, such as social relationships and leisure activities, and even antidepressants with serotonergic activity can cause mild to severe sexual dysfunction, which also affect some depressed elderly people [70]. At the same time, patients with LLD who lack corresponding family care and social support are prone to anxiety, loneliness, and even increase negative emotions such as depression when long-term medication [61], which further aggravates the burden. Therefore, medical staff should fully consider the dosage of drugs and strengthen the monitoring and treatment of adverse drug reactions. Moreover, accurately identify atypical symptoms, select targeted treatment measures for prominent symptoms such as anxiety, insomnia, and somatic symptoms, and adhere to the principle of individualized treatment for patients with LLD are needed. Last but not least, timely psychological support should be provided to patients to better reduce the burden.

Several countries have conducted in-depth studies on the factors influencing MRB, focusing on aspects such as age, gender, economic status, medication costs, polypharmacy, and the presence of medication support. Some findings align with our research, while others differ. Among these studies, gender shows the most variability. Some research has confirmed that females score significantly higher on the LMQ than males [56], including among patients with HIV [71]. This difference may be attributed to variations in psychological nature. However, a study from Kuwait presented opposite results, indicating that males scored higher in the “Relationships/communication with healthcare professionals about medicines” domain [20]. Both our study and a New Zealand study found no significant gender differences [18]. Cultural factors might explain the results in New Zealand, while in our case, it could be due to the comorbidity of anxiety and depression with LLD, which potentially mitigates the psychological burden differences between genders. Employment status shows mixed effects on LMQ-3 scores, particularly across different age groups. For younger participants, unemployment often leads to financial difficulties, resulting in higher LMQ-3 scores [18]. In contrast, for elderly participants, employment can mean the necessity of maintaining a livelihood and enduring an excessive workload, presenting significant challenges in their daily lives, including medication management, which directly translates into higher LMQ-3 scores [56]. Our study is unique in analyzing whether living with adult children impacts patients’ MRB. The findings suggest that family bonds, including medication support, can significantly reduce the medication burden. Similar results were found in a study from Qatar [72]. However, contrasting findings emerged in other researches. A study of the elderly population in Bahrain confirmed that participants requiring medication support experienced a heavier burden [56]. The same result was observed in Kuwait [20], where patients may feel anxious due to fears that medication could strain their relationships with family members [73]. Cultural differences likely play a significant role in these outcomes.

This study is the first to reveal the MRB level and associated factors in patients with LLD. Under the trend of global aging, the number of elderly people with mental illness is increasing. However, few people pay special attention to the level of medication burden of patients with LLD. Our study fills the gap in this sub-domain by assessing various aspects of medication burden in those patients, helping to gain an in-depth understanding of the patients’ daily medication experience and providing policy-makers and health care providers with a more comprehensive understanding of this issue. Given the increasing ageing population in China, it is of paramount importance to prioritize the medication burden of patients with LLD.

Our work should be explained in the context of several limitations. First, a convenience sample of participants with LLD was collected only from Wuxi communities in China, and there may be sampling errors. Therefore, whether and to what degree they can represent the entire data of patients with LLD in China requires thorough investigation. In addition, self-selection bias (e.g., only those willing to participate were included in the study) and sample size may affect the accuracy of the results. Second, the MRB is based on questionnaires, which may lead to reporting bias and under-reporting burden in participants. Additionally, leading questions in the questionnaire may impact the results. Third, this study is cross-sectional and cannot reveal causal relationships. On this basis, follow-up studies can further improve multi-center and large sample studies in other regions of China, providing positive and comprehensive data reference for patients with LLD.

Conclusion

This study analyzed the influencing factors of MRB in patients with LLD during the aging process in China. The key factors include age, residence, living status, annual household income, type of drug payment, quantity and frequency of medication, and adverse reactions. Patients aged 70–79 years old, living in rural areas, partly or all at their own expense, taking more than 5 drugs each time, medication more than 3 times a day, are more likely to show heavy MRB. In contrast, people living with adult children, no adverse reaction and annual household income of more than 50,000 CNY reported low medication burdens. Our study provides preliminary evidence for patients with LLD in mainland China, emphasizing that multiple aspects related to drugs may help alleviate MRB, especially concerns about medicine use, the lack of autonomy over medication regimen, as well as communication between patient and doctor.

This study has important policy implications. Although China is ready to deal with many problems caused by the aging of the population, the burden of expenditure in the medical field is huge, including capital, personnel, drugs and policy pressure. To reduce the MRB of patients with LLD, decision-makers and public health practitioners should carefully consider drug policies and medical resource allocation. Our findings underscore the importance of raising community and family awareness of the MRB and ensuring that the burden among patients with LLD is adequately addressed and appropriately intervened. In addition, cooperation between government and families is crucial for the development of specific policies to provide comprehensive MRB reduction measures for vulnerable community groups, including depressed elderly people, in the context of global aging trends. Chinese medical staff should focus on evaluating such patients and formulating relevant MRB reduction strategies to ensure compliance and daily medication management of patients with LLD.
