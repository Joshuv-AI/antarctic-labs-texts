Myelin Oligodendrocyte Glycoprotein Antibody-Associated Disease Refractory to Sequential Immunotherapy
Authors: Suryadeep B Zala, Shripad S Pujari, Shreejita P Sengupta, Nilesh R Palasdeokar
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disorder (MOGAD) is a distinct inflammatory demyelinating disease of the central nervous system, separate from multiple sclerosis (MS) and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD). MOGAD is characterized by the presence of myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) antibodies and typically presents with optic neuritis, transverse myelitis, or encephalitis. Patients may continue to relapse while undergoing the first-line maintenance immunotherapy, thus requiring switching over to a new agent. The optimal maintenance immunotherapy for preventing relapses remains an area of active investigation, particularly in refractory cases that fail to respond to conventional treatments.[1]

A 22-year-old man first presented in November 2019 at age 16 years with an acute onset of left-sided ataxia. Neurological examination revealed cerebellar signs consistent with left cerebellar dysfunction. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) hyperintense signal changes in the left cerebellum and middle cerebellar peduncle. Laboratory investigations revealed positive anti-MOG antibodies, confirming the diagnosis of MOGAD. The patient received intravenous methylprednisolone (IVMP) with significant clinical improvement. To prevent future relapses, maintenance immunotherapy with azathioprine 50 mg twice daily was initiated. Azathioprine was chosen based on literature demonstrating its effectiveness in MOGAD, with studies showing a significant reduction in annualized relapse rate (ARR) from 1.6 to 0.2.[2] Oral prednisolone was tapered over 3 months [Table 1].

Despite azathioprine maintenance therapy, the patient experienced his first relapse in May 2020, presenting again with left cerebellar ataxia. MRI revealed FLAIR hyperintense lesions with gadolinium enhancement in the left cerebellar and middle cerebellar peduncle regions, with additional involvement of the right temporal white matter [Figure 1]. The patient responded well to IVMP. Due to a breakthrough relapse on azathioprine, maintenance therapy was switched to mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) 1500 mg daily, based on evidence showing a significant effect on relapse rates.[3] Oral prednisolone was tapered over 4 months [Table 1]. Following the switch to MMF, the patient remained symptom-free. A follow-up MRI in August 2021 demonstrated a reduction in both left cerebellar and right temporal lesions [Figure 1]. The patient was continued on MMF 1500 mg daily.

In October 2023, the patient experienced another relapse with subacute left cerebellar ataxia. MRI findings were consistent with previous relapses, showing FLAIR hyperintense and enhancing signals in the left cerebellum [Figure 1]. After improvement with IVMP, the patient was transitioned to rituximab therapy.[4] He received two cycles of rituximab (October 2023 and April 2024), each consisting of 1000 mg of rituximab administered two weeks apart. Oral prednisolone was tapered over 4 months [Table 1].

Despite rituximab treatment, the patient relapsed again in September 2024 with identical clinical and radiological features. MRI demonstrated FLAIR hyperintense signals in the left cerebellum and middle cerebellar peduncle with contrast enhancement. This relapse occurred despite B-cell depletion, highlighting the uncertain efficacy of rituximab in MOGAD compared to NMOSD.[5] Following IVMP treatment, the patient was switched to tocilizumab. Oral prednisolone was tapered over 4 months [Table 1].

The patient received six monthly doses of tocilizumab. Literature supports efficacy of tocilizumab in MOGAD, with case series reporting relapse freedom and corticosteroid discontinuation.[6] However, despite this treatment, the patient experienced another relapse in March 2025 with the characteristic left cerebellar presentation. MRI findings remained consistent with previous episodes [Figure 1].

Following the latest relapse and IVMP response, the patient was initiated on maintenance intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) therapy. This decision was based on emerging evidence supporting IVIG as a promising maintenance therapy for MOGAD, with studies demonstrating a reduction in ARR from 1.4 to 0 when administered at 1 g/kg every four weeks.[7] The patient received two monthly doses of IVIG [Table 1]. After the second dose, due to financial constraints, he was continued on maintenance oral prednisolone at 30 mg per day. The patient has remained relapse-free to date.

This case illustrates the significant therapeutic challenges in managing refractory MOGAD. The patient’s clinical course demonstrates several important features characteristic of MOGAD: a tendency for anatomically consistent relapses (left cerebellar involvement in each episode), a good response to acute corticosteroid treatment (not requiring plasmapheresis or IVIG for acute relapse therapy), and the challenge of achieving sustained remission with conventional immunosuppressive therapies.[8]

The consistent involvement of the left cerebellum and middle cerebellar peduncle in each relapse suggests a predisposition for this anatomical location, which may reflect individual inflammatory patterns or local tissue vulnerability.[9] This anatomical consistency could potentially guide future monitoring strategies and treatment decisions.

The sequential failure of multiple immunotherapeutic agents—azathioprine, MMF, rituximab, and tocilizumab—highlights the heterogeneous nature of MOGAD and the need for individualized treatment approaches. Each therapy has established evidence in MOGAD management, yet this patient experienced breakthrough relapses despite adequate dosing and duration.

The transition to maintenance IVIG represents an emerging therapeutic approach in MOGAD management. Recent multicenter studies have demonstrated promising results with IVIG, particularly when administered at higher doses (1 g/kg every four weeks). Its mechanism of action differs from that of previous immunosuppressive agents, potentially offering efficacy in cases refractory to conventional treatments.[7]

This case report demonstrates the complex therapeutic landscape of MOGAD management, particularly in refractory cases. The sequential failure of multiple established immunotherapies emphasizes the need for personalized treatment approaches and highlights MOGAD’s distinct pathophysiology compared to other demyelinating diseases. The emergence of maintenance IVIG as a therapeutic option provides hope for patients with refractory disease. Long-term follow-up will be essential to evaluate the efficacy of IVIG in preventing future relapses and to guide evidence-based treatment recommendations for similar challenging cases.

Further research into biomarkers predictive of treatment response and the development of novel therapeutic targets will be crucial for improving outcomes in patients with MOGAD who experience multiple treatment failures.
