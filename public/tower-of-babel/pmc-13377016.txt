A national-scale sandy beach dataset for India derived from high-resolution satellite imagery and deep learning
Authors: Giribabu Dandabathula, Subham Roy, Omkar Shashikant Ghatage, Sagar Subhashrao Salunkhe, Durgesh Nandani, Apurba Kumar Bera, Sushil Kumar Srivastav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sandy beaches are important coastal features with ecological, social, and economic significance, yet comprehensive national-scale datasets mapping their extent in India remain scarce. Here, we present a high-resolution, geo-referenced dataset of sandy beach extents along the Indian coastline, derived from IRS ResourceSat-2/2 A LISS-IV multispectral imagery (5.8 m resolution) collected between 2021 and 2024. The dataset was derived from cloud-free imagery acquired under low-tide conditions to ensure consistent delineation of exposed sandy beach extents. Fourteen representative coastal sites were manually annotated to train and validate a U-Net deep learning model, which was subsequently applied to the entire Indian coastline. The model achieved robust performance on independent test tiles, with an intersection-over-union of 0.84, precision of 0.90, recall of 0.94, and F1-score of 0.92. Accuracy was further validated at two independent beaches with low tidal influence, yielding an RMSE of 11.8 m2 (<0.05% relative deviation) between U-Net–derived and manually digitized areas. The final dataset is distributed in Shapefile format to ensure compatibility with standard GIS workflows, supporting applications such as coastal monitoring, habitat assessment, and shoreline change analysis, as well as serving as training data for future machine learning models. An accompanying Google Earth Engine web application is provided for interactive visualization and exploration of the mapped sandy beaches. This dataset represents the first comprehensive, high-resolution mapping of sandy beaches across India, enabling improved coastal management, conservation planning, and research into coastal dynamics.

Background & Summary

Coastal zones have long attracted human settlements because of their abundant resources, strategic importance, and environmental amenity1,2. Remarkably, a majority of the world’s 20 megacities, each with populations exceeding 10 million, are located in coastal regions1. The wide range of ecosystem services provided by coastal areas reinforces their importance in achieving several United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (UN SDGs), which emphasize the conservation and sustainable use of these environments3. Effective coastal management relies on the availability of detailed and spatially explicit datasets that capture the complexity of these areas2. Coastal ecosystems act as natural buffers, protecting communities from storms, erosion, and sea-level rise2,4. They also harbor rich biodiversity and store substantial amounts of carbon, contributing to climate regulation and ecosystem resilience5,6. Furthermore, coastal zones underpin major socio-economic activities, including tourism, fisheries, shipping, port operations, and the extraction of mineral and energy resources7.

Coastal ecosystems form a dynamic transition zone that includes diverse habitats such as sandy beaches and dunes, rocky shores, estuaries, mangroves, seagrass meadows, coral reefs, salt marshes, coastal wetlands, and mudflats, some of which are periodically exposed during low tides8. Among these coastal environments, sandy beaches represent one of the most dynamic coastal landforms, characterized by highly mobile sediment systems and strong interactions between hydrodynamic and geomorphic processes9,10, and form the primary focus of this study. Understanding these complex environments, their interactions with human activities, and the links between terrestrial and marine processes is increasingly reliant on remote sensing technologies11,12. Such approaches enable large-scale, repeatable mapping and classification of coastal ecosystems into distinct land use/land cover (LULC) classes. Common coastal LULC categories include sandy beaches, offshore energy infrastructure, salt pans, aquaculture areas, settlements, agricultural zones, and transportation and port facilities. Mapping these classes supports integrated coastal management, risk assessment, and planning for sustainable coastal development12.

A sandy beach is a coastal landform composed predominantly of unconsolidated sand-sized sediments, formed and maintained by wave-driven processes at the interface between land and sea. The composition and characteristics of sandy beaches depend on sediment sources and local environmental conditions13. Sandy beaches dissipate wave energy through their granular structure and are typically composed of quartz (silica) sand of terrestrial origin or carbonate sand of marine origin14. Terrestrial sand generally results from land erosion and is delivered to the coast by rivers, slope processes, torrents, and gullies, or transported by aeolian processes13,14. In this study, sandy beaches are treated as a geomorphological landform class represented through their sedimentary surface characteristics, and the mapping focuses specifically on exposed sandy beach surfaces along open coasts and island shorelines. Sand-dominated features occurring in non-beach environments, such as tidal flats, river mouths, and deltaic deposits, were not explicitly targeted unless they exhibit clear beach-like geomorphic characteristics.

The formation and evolution of sandy beaches are governed by the interaction of sediment supply, wave climate, and nearshore hydrodynamics10. Sediment availability from terrestrial and marine sources, combined with wave-driven transport processes such as longshore drift and cross-shore exchange, controls beach morphology and spatial extent13–15. Along the Indian coastline, monsoonal wave regimes play a key role in seasonal sediment redistribution, leading to variability in beach width and profile16–18. Coastal settings such as embayed shorelines, deltaic systems, and reef-protected coasts further influence sediment retention and beach stability9. These geomorphic and hydrodynamic controls directly affect the spatial expression of sandy surfaces in satellite imagery and are therefore important for interpreting the mapped beach extents generated in this study.

Given their ecological and socio-economic importance, accurately mapping the extent and condition of sandy beaches is essential for effective coastal management and sustainable development planning. Sandy beaches also support recreation and tourism, contributing substantially to local and national economies15,19. In the context of climate change, spatially explicit data on sandy beaches is essential for assessing the impacts of sea-level rise, shoreline erosion, and the resulting loss of coastal ecosystem services20–23. When available, such datasets enable more effective evaluation of environmental and socio-economic risks and support evidence-based strategies for climate adaptation and coastal resilience planning.

In 2019, the National Hydrographic Organisation (NHO), Government of India, reassessed the Indian coastline using modern geodetic methods and advanced mapping technologies, reporting a total length of 11,098.81 km24,25. Of this, 7,773.33 km corresponds to the mainland states, while 3,325.48 km comprises the islands and President-ruled Union Territories25. Despite global recognition of the ecological and socio-economic importance of coastal zones, comprehensive sandy beach datasets for India’s extensive coastline remain scarce. While several global coastal datasets exist, such as the Global Surface Water dataset26 with a spatial resolution of 30 m and global shoreline products including GSHHG and OpenStreetMap coastlines, these primarily represent water extent or shoreline position rather than explicit sandy beach extents. Similarly, global sandy beach occurrence datasets1 are typically derived at coarser spatial scales and do not delineate precise beach boundaries. In contrast, no publicly available national-scale datasets mapping sandy beach extents across India at comparable spatial resolution currently exist. Owing to these differences in spatial resolution, thematic definition, and methodological approach, direct quantitative comparison is not feasible. Comprehensive assessments of the condition and distribution of sandy beaches along the Indian coastline remain limited1. Previous studies have highlighted several beaches in Goa, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands as notable examples of heavily visited coastal environments27. Many of India’s popular beaches were originally established as holiday destinations during the colonial period or developed around prominent religious sites28,29. In recent years, India has placed strategic emphasis on improving the ecological quality and sustainable management of its coastal landscapes through initiatives such as the Blue Flag certification programme coordinated by the Society of Integrated Coastal Management (SICOM), MoEFCC (https://moef.gov.in/)30,31. As of the latest reports, twelve beaches in India have been awarded Blue Flag certification. The certification requires regular monitoring of beach condition, cleanliness, sediment characteristics, and shoreline stability, all of which depend on reliable and up-to-date sandy beach extent maps. Therefore, standardized high-resolution mapping of sandy beaches provides essential baseline information to support environmental assessment and coastal management.

For developing countries such as India, recognising the economic value of beaches as part of the blue economy is essential26. Accordingly, the dataset presented in this research was generated to support this rationale. The ResourceSat-2/2 A series of Indian Remote Sensing (IRS) satellites hosts the Linear Imaging Self-Scanning Sensor-IV (LISS-IV), a multispectral sensor operating in three spectral bands within the visible and near-infrared regions: green (0.52–0.59 µm), red (0.62–0.68 µm), and near-infrared (0.77–0.86 µm), with a revisit capability of five days enabled by sensor steerability32. LISS-IV imagery has a spatial resolution of 5.8 m and a swath width of 70 km, and imagery acquired between 2021 and 2024 formed the basis of this dataset. The high spatial resolution of LISS-IV provides a significant advantage for mapping sandy beach environments, where features are often narrow and spatially heterogeneous. Compared to medium-resolution sensors (e.g., 10–30 m), the 5.8 m resolution reduces mixed-pixel effects and enables more accurate delineation of beach boundaries, particularly in areas with complex shoreline morphology or adjacent land-cover types such as vegetation and built-up regions. This level of detail is critical for capturing fine-scale coastal features and improving the reliability of segmentation results.

The U-Net deep learning (DL) architecture was employed exclusively to generate the dataset. Conventional machine learning methods, such as Random Forest (RF) and Support Vector Machine (SVM), proved unsuitable for segmenting high-resolution LISS-IV imagery with limited spectral information, as they primarily introduce additional noise and require extensive post-processing. U-Net’s ability to learn complex features enabled accurate delineation of sandy beaches from surrounding land and water. While sandy beaches exhibit distinct spectral signatures detectable by traditional classification methods, such approaches are generally suited to medium- and coarse-resolution imagery1,33. At higher resolutions, small pixel sizes often result in the salt-and-pepper effect, where neighbouring pixels within a single beach feature are misclassified. Such limitations in pixel-based approaches for high-resolution imagery have been well documented by Zou and Greenberg33. Previous studies have demonstrated that shoreline extraction using spectral indices, thresholding, and unsupervised classification approaches can achieve good accuracy under controlled conditions and at local scales34. However, these methods often require site-specific tuning and may exhibit reduced generalization and increased classification uncertainty when applied to heterogeneous coastal environments at national scales. U-Net’s encoder–decoder architecture effectively captures spatial context across pixels, mitigating the salt-and-pepper effect and reducing additional noise introduced by RF and SVM classifiers, particularly when detecting sandy beaches from the limited spectral information in LISS-IV imagery.

Recently, Ni et al.35 used Sentinel-2 imagery and supervised machine learning, including SVM, to produce a 10-meter resolution map of sandy beaches in China. In southeastern Australia, Yong et al.36 applied convolutional neural network–based segmentation to aerial imagery, achieving accurate and rapid prediction of beach outlines. The datasets developed by Ni et al.35 and Yong et al.36, along with the dataset presented here, are expected to enhance research, foster intercontinental collaboration, and integrate social and economic perspectives to support governance and management of coastal zones1,37. These datasets are particularly valuable for assessing the current status of sandy beaches, especially in the context of increasing multidimensional pressures, including environmental, climatic, and anthropogenic factors.

Recent advances in satellite-based coastal research have expanded the capability to monitor and characterize sandy beach systems at regional to global scales. Satellite observations have been widely used to quantify shoreline change, coastal erosion, and intertidal morphology, as well as to derive geomorphic parameters of sandy beach systems38–40. These developments highlight the growing role of multi-sensor satellite observations in advancing quantitative understanding of coastal processes. However, most of these efforts focus on geomorphic characterization or shoreline dynamics rather than providing spatially explicit, high-resolution maps of sandy beach extent as a distinct land-cover class, which is the primary objective of the present study.

The methodology presented here provides a replicable framework for other countries aiming to develop similar datasets to support coastal resource management and blue economy planning. In the Indian context, the dataset offers a foundational geospatial layer that can assist in shoreline monitoring, tourism management, coastal zone planning, and the sustainable development of beach-associated socio-economic activities.

Methods

Data sources

In total, 480 cloud-free ResourceSat-2/2 A LISS-IV scenes covering the Indian coastlines were available for the period 2021–2024. From these, 120 scenes were selected based on two filtering criteria. First, scenes acquired during periods of low atmospheric moisture were prioritized to minimize water vapor effects41. Atmospheric conditions were assessed using MODIS-based atmospheric profiles (MOD07 and MOD05), obtained from NASA’s Level-1 and Atmosphere Archive and Distribution System (LAADS) (https://ladsweb.modaps.eosdis.nasa.gov/). Second, scenes acquired during predicted low-tide conditions were selected using tidal information from the Indian Tide Weather Times portal (https://indiatideweathertimes.in/index.html), with a preference for acquisitions during neap-tide periods (first and third lunar quarters) to reduce variability in tidal range. ResourceSat-2/2 A LISS-IV imagery is typically acquired during morning overpasses (approximately 09:00–11:30 local time). For each scene, acquisition dates were cross-referenced with predicted tidal information from the nearest coastal station, and scenes were selected when acquisition times corresponded closely to low-tide conditions, generally within a ±2–3 hour window of the predicted low tide. This temporal tolerance accounts for uncertainties in matching satellite overpass time with tidal minima. To account for spatial variability in tidal regimes along the Indian coastline, location-specific tidal information was used during scene selection. The selection approach was applied consistently across all coastal regions of India using location-specific tidal information, ensuring methodological consistency despite regional differences in tidal regimes. However, due to regional variability in tidal regimes along the Indian coastline, the extent of intertidal exposure under low-tide conditions, including neap-tide periods, may not be uniform across all locations. Consequently, the mapped beach extent reflects condition-dependent exposure rather than a fully standardized intertidal representation. Low tide imagery exposes a larger portion of the intertidal zone, enabling clearer delineation of sandy areas and reducing misclassification caused by seawater cover during high tide42,43. ResourceSat-2/2 A LISS-IV imagery was obtained from the National Remote Sensing Centre (NRSC) Bhoonidhi data portal (https://bhoonidhi.nrsc.gov.in), where the data are accessible to registered users for research purposes. These selection criteria ensured that both the training datasets and the final mapping inputs contained clearly exposed sandy areas, supporting accurate U-Net segmentation and reliable dataset generation.

It is important to note that the mapped sandy beach extent represents conditions under cloud-free and low-tide acquisition scenarios. As a result, the dataset reflects the exposed sandy surface during these conditions rather than a temporally invariant or fully representative beach extent. This condition-dependent mapping approach introduces a systematic bias, which should be considered when interpreting the dataset, particularly in applications involving dynamic shoreline processes. The LISS-IV images used in this study are provided with standard NRSC preprocessing, including radiometric and geometric corrections. Surface reflectance was subsequently computed to enable consistent analysis across scenes. The imagery, originally available in digital number (DN) format, was processed using the SACRS2 scheme developed by Space Applications Centre (SAC), ISRO44 (https://www.sac.gov.in/). SACRS2, accessed from https://mosdac.gov.in/tools, follows a two-step workflow. First, DN values are converted to top-of-atmosphere (TOA) reflectance using sensor-specific gain, bias, and solar irradiance parameters. Second, atmospheric correction is applied using a radiative transfer approach that accounts for aerosols, water vapour, and ozone, resulting in surface reflectance. This preprocessing ensures radiometric consistency across datasets and improves the comparability of imagery acquired between 2021 and 2024. Accurate detection relies on subtle spectral contrasts between sand, vegetation, and water, which strongly influence classification performance. In the case of LISS-IV, sand is characterized by high reflectance in visible bands (green and red), while water exhibits strong absorption in the near-infrared band, enabling effective separation from surrounding land-cover types. Accurate surface reflectance retrieval is therefore essential, as even small radiometric inconsistencies can diminish the spectral contrast between sand and water, increasing the likelihood of classification errors45.

Datasets such as those generated by Ni et al.35 demonstrated that 10 m Sentinel-2 imagery, with richer spectral coverage, supports supervised classifiers like SVM for sandy-beach mapping. The ResourceSat-2/2 A LISS-IV sensor, by contrast, offers higher spatial resolution (5.8 m) compared with Sentinel-2, which provides 10 m, but only three spectral bands (two visible: green and red, and one near-infrared), resulting in reduced spectral discrimination. Because of this limitation, SVM and RF trials produced pronounced salt-and-pepper noise that required extensive post-processing. This behavior is consistent with literature showing that pixel-based conventional ML on high-resolution imagery often produces fragmented, salt-and-pepper outputs, whereas DL-based segmentation models yield more spatially coherent results46. Accordingly, we adopt a DL approach using the U-Net architecture to delineate sandy beaches from LISS-IV imagery with higher spatial coherence and reliability.

The classification approach is not based solely on spectral properties of sand; rather, the U-Net model leverages both spectral and spatial context learned from annotated training data. During annotation, geomorphological criteria were strictly applied to include only shore-parallel sandy landforms associated with wave action at the land–sea interface, while excluding other sand-dominated environments such as tidal flats, river mouths, deltaic deposits, and sandbars unless they exhibited clear beach-like morphology.

Training dataset preparation

Fourteen representative coastal sites along the Indian coastline were selected to train and validate DL models for sandy beach segmentation. These sites capture a diversity of geomorphic settings, sediment compositions, and shoreline morphologies. The selected sites represent diverse coastal settings across India, including siliciclastic mainland beaches and biogenic carbonate systems of Lakshadweep and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. They span a range of geomorphic and environmental conditions, including urban and tourism-dominated beaches, rural and pristine coasts, river-influenced and deltaic systems, and engineered or erosion-affected shorelines (e.g., Puducherry). The sites also encompass variability in beach width, sediment characteristics, turbidity, and hydrodynamic regimes, ranging from low-energy lagoonal environments to wave-dominated open coasts, ensuring representative diversity for model training, as summarized in Supplementary Table S1. However, certain specialised environments, such as black-sand beaches, were not included in the training dataset. Their geographic locations are shown in Fig. 2. Manual annotation was performed to delineate sandy beach extents within each training site using high-resolution LISS-IV imagery as the base layer, following a consistent labeling protocol to ensure spatial accuracy. During manual annotation, careful consideration was given to the geomorphological context of sandy features.

Only coastal landforms exhibiting characteristic beach morphology, such as continuous shore-parallel sand bodies associated with wave action, were included. Sand accumulations in non-beach environments, including tidal flats, estuarine deposits, and deltaic regions, were excluded unless they clearly represented beach-like systems. This ensured consistency between the conceptual definition of sandy beaches and the training data used for model development. Manual digitization was preferred over automated pre-labeling approaches to obtain precise class boundaries in heterogeneous coastal environments, where spectral confusion between sand and other bright surfaces (such as dry vegetation or built-up areas) frequently leads to misclassification47–49. The resulting binary masks served as ground truth for model training and validation.

In addition to the individual spectral bands (green, red, and near-infrared) and their corresponding masks, spectral indices were incorporated as additional input channels for U-Net training to enhance feature separability. These included the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI = (NIR – Red)/(NIR + Red)) and the Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI = (Green – NIR)/(Green + NIR)), both of which improve discrimination between sand, vegetation, and water. DL models such as U-Net require input images to be of uniform square dimensions (n × n)52. To satisfy this requirement, LISS-IV imagery, the derived spectral indices computed from the corresponding LISS-IV bands (NDVI, NDWI, and composite intensity), and their corresponding manually digitized masks were partitioned into tiles of 128 × 128 pixels, equivalent to ground dimensions of approximately 742 × 742 m at 5.8 m spatial resolution. Because sandy beaches vary in width, orientation, and geomorphic setting, tiling ensured a consistent representation of sandy pixels and prevented class imbalance that would result from using large, irregular image extents. A 40% overlap between adjacent tiles was applied to capture narrow or obliquely oriented sandy beaches, avoid truncation of shoreline features at tile boundaries, and preserve sandy pixel representation. This level of overlap balanced completeness and efficiency by minimizing boundary artifacts and enhancing shoreline delineation, while avoiding redundancy and dataset inflation that would result from higher overlaps. This approach ensured complete coverage of each site while retaining the fine-scale detail of sandy beach features for model training and validation. The tiled dataset was divided into training, validation, and test subsets in a 60:20:20 ratio, ensuring that each subset captured the diversity of shoreline and sediment characteristics. The training set was used to optimize U-Net model parameters, the validation set guided hyperparameter tuning and monitored generalization, and the test set provided an independent evaluation on unseen data. This yielded 652 training samples, 217 validation samples, and 219 test samples from the fourteen coastal sites (refer to Fig. 2).

Although the number of training tiles (652) may appear limited in absolute terms, each tile represents a spatially independent patch derived from high-resolution imagery, capturing substantial intra- and inter-site variability. The dataset was intentionally designed to prioritize environmental diversity over redundancy, with tiles sampled across geographically distributed sites representing a wide range of sediment types, geomorphic settings, and hydrodynamic conditions (Supplementary Table S1). In addition, overlapping tiles and data augmentation further increase the effective variability of the training data, enabling the model to learn robust spatial and spectral features relevant to sandy beach detection.

Dataset partitioning was performed at the tile level rather than the site level, such that tiles from all coastal locations were distributed across the training, validation, and test subsets. This ensured that the full range of environmental conditions is represented during both model training and evaluation, thereby improving generalization across heterogeneous shoreline settings.

The encoder progressively extracts hierarchical features from the input tiles through a series of convolutional and downsampling operations, capturing contextual information at multiple scales. In our implementation, we employed EfficientNet-B0 with six input channels, trained exclusively on LISS-IV–derived datasets. EfficientNet-B0 was selected as the encoder backbone due to its balanced efficiency and accuracy, lightweight architecture, and proven suitability for remote sensing applications53.

The decoder subsequently upsamples these features, reconstructing the spatial resolution while preserving the learned patterns. Skip connections link each encoder layer to its corresponding decoder layer, allowing fine spatial details lost during downsampling to be recovered52, which is crucial for accurately delineating sandy beach boundaries. The final 1 × 1 convolution layer produces a binary segmentation map indicating sandy and non-sandy pixels.

To complement the network design, we employed data augmentation during training to enhance model generalization and mitigate overfitting. Standard transformations such as random rotations, horizontal and vertical flips, and small spatial shifts were applied to expose the model to varied geometric orientations of beaches. In addition, brightness and contrast adjustments were incorporated to simulate different illumination and atmospheric conditions in optical satellite imagery. These augmentations are particularly important for sandy beach environments, where shoreline appearance can vary significantly with tide, season, and sensor acquisition geometry. By synthetically enlarging the training dataset, the model was able to learn more robust representations of sandy beach boundaries, thereby improving segmentation performance across diverse coastal settings.

The model was implemented using the open-source PyTorch deep learning library and trained with a combination of Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) and Tversky loss functions54 (50% each), with parameters α = 0.3 and β = 0.7, to optimize pixelwise segmentation accuracy. This hybrid formulation was adopted to balance pixelwise classification accuracy with sensitivity to class imbalance. While BCE provides stable optimization across all pixels, the Tversky component emphasizes minority sand pixels, which are often under-represented compared to background classes. Together, these losses improve segmentation robustness in imbalanced coastal datasets. The Adam optimizer was employed with a learning rate of 0.0001 and a weight decay of 0.0001 to ensure stable convergence.

The U-Net model architecture, including the EfficientNet-based encoder and decoder pathways, is described above. A detailed layer-wise summary of the implemented model, including input/output shapes, parameters, and memory requirements, is provided as Supplementary Text 1.

Training was performed for 500 epochs with a batch size of 8, balancing computational efficiency and memory usage. The dataset was partitioned into training, validation, and test subsets as described above.

A learning rate scheduler (ReduceLROnPlateau) was employed to adaptively adjust the learning rate during training. The scheduler reduced the learning rate by a factor of 0.5 when the validation loss failed to improve for 30 consecutive epochs. This strategy helped stabilize convergence and avoid premature stagnation of the optimization process.

Training progress was monitored using both BCE and Tversky loss values computed on the training and validation subsets across successive epochs. The resulting curves provided a basis for assessing convergence and evaluating whether the U-Net model was learning effectively and generalizing to unseen data. A steadily decreasing training loss together with stable or decreasing validation loss indicated proper optimization without overfitting, whereas divergence between the two curves was carefully examined to adjust hyperparameters such as learning rate or batch size. All inference images were partitioned into 128 × 128-pixel tiles with 40% overlap, matching the input size used during training. Following prediction, the output tiles were stitched together to reconstruct full-size segmentation masks for each image. These reconstructed masks were then post-processed to remove small spurious regions and smooth boundary artifacts, ensuring spatial consistency. Finally, the predicted masks were exported as a merged Shapefile format, representing the complete delineation of sandy beaches across all sites and forming the final dataset described in this study. Data Records

The dataset generated in this study provides geo-referenced delineations of sandy beach extents along the Indian coastline, derived from LISS-IV multispectral imagery using a U-Net deep learning model. The dataset is available in Shapefile format and is hosted on the Zenodo repository55 (10.5281/zenodo.19440429). Spatial and temporal coverage: The dataset encompasses sandy beach extents along the entire Indian coastline, derived from LISS-IV imagery acquired between 2021 and 2024 at a spatial resolution of 5.8 m.

In addition to the final sandy beach dataset, the training dataset used for model development is also publicly available to support reproducibility. This dataset includes image tiles, manually annotated masks, and derived spectral indices used for training the U-Net model, and is hosted on Zenodo56 (10.5281/zenodo.17365469). This supporting dataset enables reproducibility and transparency of the deep learning workflow and facilitates reuse for related coastal mapping applications.

Technical Validation

The quality of the sandy beach dataset was evaluated using the independent test set and external validation sites. All validation datasets, including independent test sites, were derived from ResourceSat-2/2 A LISS-IV imagery at the same spatial resolution (5.8 m) as the training data, ensuring consistency in model evaluation and avoiding scale-related bias. Predicted masks generated by the trained U-Net model were compared with manually digitized ground truth layers derived from the same LISS-IV imagery to assess spatial accuracy and completeness. Although manual digitization may introduce minor operator-dependent uncertainty, consistent interpretation criteria were followed during delineation. Given the high spatial resolution of the imagery and the generally clear contrast between sandy beaches and surrounding land-cover classes, this uncertainty is expected to be small and does not significantly affect the overall validation results. Standard segmentation metrics, including Intersection over Union (IoU = 0.84), precision (0.90), recall (0.94), and F1-score (0.92), were computed across all test tiles (refer to Table 1), indicating strong and internally consistent model performance across diverse coastal settings. Post-processing steps, including removal of small isolated regions and boundary smoothing, ensured spatial consistency in the final dataset.

The trained U-Net model demonstrated strong generalization, producing coherent segmentation results for all fourteen representative coastal sites, despite variations in shoreline morphology, sediment composition, and beach width. This assessment is based on visual inspection and tile-level evaluation. Visual inspection of predicted masks confirmed that sand features were accurately delineated, with minimal misclassification of adjacent non-sandy surfaces, further supporting dataset quality.

Because the dataset was partitioned at the tile level rather than by site, site-specific performance metrics cannot be computed in a statistically independent manner, which limits rigorous assessment of site-level generalization. Nevertheless, model performance was evaluated across diverse coastal environments through visual inspection of prediction outputs. Minor reductions in segmentation accuracy were observed in complex coastal settings such as rocky or engineered shorelines (e.g., Puducherry) and tidal or mangrove-influenced environments (e.g., Baludera), where spectral ambiguity between sand and adjacent land-cover classes is higher.

These variations are consistent with the diversity of geomorphic, sedimentological, and hydrodynamic conditions represented across the selected training sites (Supplementary Table S1), including differences in sediment type, beach morphology, turbidity, and anthropogenic influence. Environments with atypical sediment characteristics (e.g., dark or mineral-rich sands) may further reduce spectral separability in multispectral imagery, contributing to localized uncertainty in model predictions.

Tidal conditions play an important role in determining the observable extent of sandy beaches in satellite imagery. Variations in tidal stage can significantly influence the exposure of intertidal zones, leading to differences in mapped beach extent. In this study, imagery was consistently selected under low-tide conditions to maximize the visibility of exposed sandy areas and ensure reliable delineation. However, the resulting dataset represents beach extents under these specific tidal conditions, and variations in tidal state may lead to differences in observed shoreline position and beach width in other acquisition scenarios.

In addition, wet sand in intertidal zones may exhibit reduced reflectance compared to dry sand, which can affect spectral separability. However, the use of multi-channel inputs, including spectral indices (NDVI and NDWI) and contextual learning within the U-Net framework, enables the model to identify sandy surfaces based on both spectral and spatial characteristics rather than relying solely on reflectance values.

Nevertheless, localized uncertainty may still occur in regions where wet sand exhibits strong spectral similarity to adjacent land-cover classes. Some uncertainty may persist in transitional coastal environments where distinctions between beaches and other sandy landforms are not sharply defined.

To assess model generalization beyond the training regions, two independent sites, Cuthbert Bay Beach (Rangat Island, Middle Andaman) and Kakana Beach (Nicobar Islands), were selected and evaluated using ResourceSat-2/2 A LISS-IV imagery that was not part of the training datasets. The rationale for selecting these beaches is that they experience relatively low tidal ranges owing to their geographic setting and coastal configuration. Both are situated along sheltered stretches of coastline where open-ocean tidal energy is attenuated by surrounding landforms and nearshore bathymetry. The gentle offshore slope and broad continental shelf in these regions promote gradual dissipation of tidal energy, limiting vertical tidal amplitude at the shoreline. Furthermore, the absence of resonance effects and the beach orientation, often oblique to incoming tidal waves, contribute to their microtidal nature. Consequently, the combination of sheltered morphology, dissipative bathymetry, and non-resonant hydrodynamics results in the low tidal variability observed at these beaches; therefore, they serve as suitable test bed environments for evaluating model generalization.

The differences between the U-Net predictions and reference areas are extremely small (−9 m² for Cuthbert Bay and −14 m² for Kakana), corresponding to relative deviations of less than 0.05% at both sites. It is important to note that the reported area differences are derived from comparisons between vector polygons rather than discrete pixel counts. As such, sub-pixel variations introduced during raster-to-vector conversion and boundary smoothing may result in differences smaller than the nominal pixel area (~33.6 m² for LISS-IV imagery). In addition, manual digitization inherently involves some degree of uncertainty, and repeated delineation of the same feature may yield variations of comparable magnitude. Therefore, these small differences should be interpreted as indicative of very high agreement rather than absolute measurement precision.

While the external validation was performed on two independent sites, these represent a limited subset relative to the full spatial extent of the dataset. However, the model was trained and evaluated using tiles derived from fourteen geographically distributed coastal sites spanning diverse environmental conditions (Supplementary Table S1). The strong performance at independent sites not used during training provides empirical evidence of the model’s ability to generalize beyond the training regions.

Nevertheless, the size of the training dataset remains modest relative to the full extent of the Indian coastline. Although the sampling strategy prioritizes environmental representativeness over redundancy, expanding the number of training sites and image tiles would further improve model robustness and reduce potential bias toward well-represented conditions. Future work will therefore focus on incorporating additional data from underrepresented coastal settings to enhance generalization at the national scale.

While the present validation focuses on area-based agreement and pixel-level segmentation metrics, boundary-based measures such as mean absolute shoreline distance could provide additional insight into positional accuracy and are considered for future work.

Usage Notes

This study presents a geo-referenced dataset delineating sandy beach extents along the Indian coastline, generated from LISS-IV multispectral imagery using a U-Net deep learning model. The dataset is provided in Shapefile format, enabling straightforward integration with geographic information systems (GIS) for visualization, analysis, and further geospatial modeling.

The Shapefile contains polygons representing sandy beach features, which can be overlaid on additional remote sensing products, topographic maps, or in situ measurements to support coastal monitoring, habitat assessment, and shoreline change analysis.

While the dataset captures fine-scale spatial details of sandy beaches, it is important to note that the spatial resolution is limited to 5.8 m, and temporal coverage is restricted to LISS-IV imagery acquired between 2021 and 2024. Small-scale beach features or transient tidal effects may not be fully resolved. Users should account for these limitations when interpreting the data or integrating it with higher-resolution datasets.

The dataset can also serve as a training, validation, and test resource for machine learning and deep learning models targeting coastal feature extraction or shoreline classification from multispectral imagery such as LISS-IV. Researchers and practitioners are encouraged to combine these data with additional high-resolution imagery, environmental variables, or historical datasets to enhance coastal analyses, modeling, and management applications.

To facilitate exploration and practical use of the sandy beach dataset, we have developed a Google Earth Engine (GEE)–based web application, accessible at https://dgiribabu.users.earthengine.app/view/sandy-beaches-of-india. This app allows researchers to visualize the national-scale sandy beach map generated using the U-Net deep learning model applied to IRS ResourceSat-2/2 A LISS-IV imagery. Users can interactively explore shoreline features, examine beach extents across different coastal regions, and gain insights into spatial patterns, supporting applications in coastal monitoring, management, and research.

Limitations

A key limitation of the present dataset is that black-sand beaches were not included in the U-Net training sites, as such areas were absent from the original selection of fourteen representative coastal regions. Consequently, the model did not capture the spectral characteristics unique to black sand, which could lead to misclassification when relying solely on automated segmentation. To address this, black-sand beaches were manually digitized during post-processing to ensure their accurate inclusion in the final dataset. These features represent a relatively limited proportion of the overall mapped coastline, although their exact spatial extent has not been quantified separately. Manual delineation was performed using high-resolution LISS-IV imagery following the same interpretation criteria applied during training data preparation. Although a formal accuracy assessment specific to these areas was not conducted, the distinct spectral characteristics of black sand enable reliable visual identification. Users should note that these areas were not identified through deep learning predictions, and caution is advised when using the dataset for analyses focusing specifically on black-sand or other atypical sediment types.

The mapped sandy beach extent represents conditions under cloud-free and low-tide acquisition scenarios. While this improves the visibility and delineation of exposed sandy areas, it introduces a dependence on observation conditions rather than representing a temporally invariant beach extent. Consequently, the dataset reflects condition-specific shoreline exposure and may not fully capture temporal variability associated with tidal fluctuations, seasonal changes, or dynamic coastal processes.

The selection of low-tide imagery, including neap-tide conditions, was applied consistently across the study area using location-specific tidal information; however, regional variability in tidal regimes may influence the extent of exposed intertidal area. As a result, the mapped sandy beach extent may reflect locally varying tidal conditions rather than a fully standardized intertidal representation, which may introduce uncertainty when comparing beach extents across different coastal regions.

In addition, the number of training sites (fourteen) and independent validation locations (two) is limited relative to the full spatial extent and diversity of the Indian coastline. Although the dataset was designed to capture major environmental gradients, expanding the spatial coverage of training and validation data would further improve model robustness and generalization across diverse coastal settings.
