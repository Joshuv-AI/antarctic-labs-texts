Characterising gut microbiome dysbiosis in diarrhoea calves from multiple farms in Inner Mongolia using 16S and metagenomics
Authors: Junyan Li, Xin Zhang, Xiaochao Zhao, Gaowa Gong, Jian Li, Baolige Dalai, Ziran Mo, Xiaojing Xu, Xiaoqing Jia, Yaning Li, Juan Lai, Penglong Wang, Lei Sun, Yongfeng Liu, Xiaoping Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe pathogenesis of neonatal calf diarrhoea (NCD), a critical disease that contributes to neonatal mortality in calves, remains nebulous.ResultsInner Mongolia, a key region for cattle farming in China, was selected as a study area to provide a comprehensive overview of the epidemiology and treatment of calf diarrhoea. No significant correlation was found between the incidence of diarrhoea and sampling points or medications. The severity of diarrhoea cases was stratified into five levels based on faecal characteristics. To elucidate the pathogenesis of NCD, 16S rRNA gene and metagenomic sequencing analyses were performed across severity levels. Microbial diversity analyses revealed distinct variations in microbial communities at different severity levels. Employing binning and LEfSe methodologies, two potential bacterial pathogens were identified: Escherichia coli (bin.216), leveraging non-canonical virulence mechanisms; and Streptococcus ruminantium (bin.338), an uncharacterised diarrhoeagenic bacterium. Furthermore, the viral agent Escherichia phage VpaE1_ev108 was significantly associated with disease progression. Gene function enrichment analysis revealed a broad spectrum of antibiotic resistance genes even in farms without direct antibiotic treatment, underscoring the pervasive prevalence of drug resistance.ConclusionsThe findings of this study revealed significant gut microbial dysbiosis in calves with severe diarrhoea, through which two putative NCD-associated pathogens were identified: E. coli (bin.216) and S. ruminantium (bin.338). Marked enrichment of Bacteroides spp. and Methanobrevibacter_A sp. 900313645 was observed in healthy cohorts, suggesting their potential protective roles. Therapeutic strategies employing phage-mediated pathogen targeting combined with probiotic transplantation have demonstrated dual benefits, potentially reducing antimicrobial dependency and preserving microbial homeostasis through ecological network reconstruction. Introduction

Neonatal calf diarrhoea (NCD), the predominant type of diarrhoea in calves, is the leading cause of illness and death within the first month of life [1], threatening the health of calves and resulting in significant reductions in farm productivity and economic sustainability [2, 3]. Diarrhoea is responsible for approximately 50% of deaths in unweaned calves in the USA, with a similar rate in South Korea, where 53.4% of calf mortalities have been linked to this ailment. A previous study in Uruguay found that 64.6% of the calves died of diarrhoea before weaning [4]. Statistical analyses in China, from 2003 to 2018, reported an average calf diarrhoea incidence rate of 27.2% [5]. In contemporary calf production in China, calf diarrhoea remains a formidable challenge owing to its multifaceted aetiology. The economic burden of diarrhoea is indeed substantial [6]. For example, in Great Britain, enteric diseases incurred annual losses of 11 million GBP in national herds as early as 2003 [7], while Cryptosporidium, which causes neonatal diarrhoea in calves, generated direct costs per calf of 60.62 EUR in Belgium, 43.83 EUR in France, and 58.24 EUR in the Netherlands during 2018–2021 [8]. Region-specific impacts include 10 million USD in losses from calf diarrhoea in Norway in 2006 [6] and approximately 1 billion JPY attributed to bovine rotavirus outbreaks in Japan (2017) [9]. Direct and indirect economic losses due to calf diarrhoea can reach 10 billion yuan annually in China [10].

Intestinal microbiota are pivotal in the health and susceptibility to diarrhoea in calves [11]; therefore, disturbances in the microbiota can directly affect the production performance of dairy calves, including diminished milk yields, reduced conception rates, and elevated rates of premature culling [6, 12]. Alterations in intestinal microbiota are intricately linked to infectious agents, such as viruses, bacteria, and parasites, and non-infectious factors, such as climatic and environmental shifts and alterations in feed and feeding methods [13, 14]. Recent advances in microbiome research have demonstrated significant microbial dysbiosis in diarrhoeic calves characterised by an increased abundance of Enterococcus, Streptococcus, Ligilactobacillus, Lactobacilus, Gallibacterium, and Escherichia/Shigella. At the species level, diarrhoeic calves exhibit an obvious enrichment of Escherichia coli, Streptococcus gallolyticus, and several Lactobacillus species [15–18]. Regarding diarrhoeal-associated viruses, while bovine rotavirus (BRV) is recognised as the primary viral causative agent worldwide [6], emerging viral metagenomic evidence has expanded our understanding of the enteric virome in diarrheic calves, including bovine coronavirus (BCoV), bovine astrovirus, bovine kobuvirus, and bovine norovirus, diarrhoea virus (BVDV), bovine adenovirus, bovine torovirus, and bovine parvovirus [19–22]. These pathogens may contribute to diarrhoeal pathogenesis either individually or through co-infection.

Moreover, bacteriophages are an indispensable component of the autochthonous gut virome [23]. The associations between gut bacteriophage compositional shifts and microbiome-associated diseases have underscored the therapeutic potential of targeted phage interventions [24, 25]. Archaea play a crucial role in the early colonisation of the neonatal calf gut, with Euryarchaeota (93.73%), Crenarchaeota (5.42%), and Thaumarchaeota (0.58%) dominating the colonic archaeal community within the first 2 days of birth [26, 27]. Methanobrevibacter, Methanosarcina, and Methanocorpusculum are the predominant archaeal genera in the calf gastrointestinal tract, with methane-producing species accounting for 57.96%, suggesting their roles in H2 depletion and carbohydrate degradation [28, 29]. Dynamic interactions among the bacterial, viral, and archaeal communities in the calf gut microbiome are pivotal in intestinal homeostasis, and dysbiosis may contribute to diarrhoeal pathogenesis.

The administration of antibiotics to treat diarrhoea can result in microbial flora imbalance, potentially diminishing the effect of antibiotics against certain bacterial species and exacerbating invasion and colonisation by infectious pathogens [30]. Early changes in the intestinal microbiota can predict the incidence of calf diarrhoea, and a reduction in antibiotic use can enhance calf intestinal health [3, 14]. Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of healthy gut microbiota in reducing the prevalence of calf disease and maintaining the overall health of calves.

The Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, a key livestock production area in northern China, is transitioning from traditional small-scale farming to large-scale dairy operations [31]. This shift, driven by increasing demand for dairy and beef products, has been accompanied by an increase in the incidence of NCD, particularly in large-scale farms with large herd sizes [32, 33]. Multiple pathogens, high stocking density, management practices, and environmental changes are among the factors that contribute to this trend by compromising calf immunity and facilitating pathogen transmission [6]. Despite the growing significance of NCD in this region, previous epidemiological investigations of calf diarrhoea have primarily focused on several well-known enteric pathogens, including BVDV [34], BRV [35], BCoV [36], E. coli [37], and Cryptosporidium parvum [38]. Meanwhile, systematic studies on microbiome-level alterations remain scarce.

This study is the first comprehensive investigation of NCD epidemiology across 12 small- to large-scale cattle farms spanning the diverse ecological regions of Inner Mongolia, China. These farms include high-yield dairy basins, such as Hohhot (annual milk production: 2.54 million tons) and low-output pastoral systems in Alxa (130,000 tons/year), ensuring geographical representativeness and production-scale diversity. Using integrated 16S rRNA gene sequencing and shotgun metagenomics, this study systematically characterised gut microbial dysbiosis in diarrhoeic calves and identified the key microbial players (phages, bacteria, and archaea) and functional pathways associated with NCD pathogenesis. The multi-omics approach used in this study will reveal potential therapeutic targets and probiotic candidates adapted to the Inner Mongolian bovine ecosystem.

Following collection, the samples were divided into two portions: one was used for photographic documentation of sample characteristics and water content test experiments, while the other was designated for next-generation sequencing (NGS) experiments. Samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for short-term cryopreservation without using commercial DNA/RNA stabilisation reagents. Within 24 h post-collection, all specimens were transferred to laboratory-maintained − 80 ℃ ultra-low temperature storage units following dry ice transportation. Then, total genomic DNA was extracted using the CTAB/SDS method [39, 40]. DNA concentration and purity were assessed on 1% agarose gels, and based on the concentration, the DNA was diluted to 1 ng/μl with sterile water. Finally, 78 samples were prepared for constructing the 16S rRNA gene library. Owing to limitations in DNA amount and funding, as well as 16S rRNA gene sequencing outcomes, only 44 of 78 samples were selected for metagenomic library construction. Due to insufficient storage, we could not classify the four samples as NA group (Table S1). Pathogen detection was performed using a commercial calf diarrhoea penta-antigen rapid diagnostic kit (Cat# DIG2411001) (Ahreal Co., Ltd., Qingdao, China) to identify bovine rotavirus, bovine coronavirus, E. coli F5 (K99), C. parvum, and Clostridium perfringens. The assay was conducted following the manufacturer’s protocol and was supplemented with the methodological optimisations described by Lequin et al. [41]; it included pre-analytical sample preparation and post-detection validation steps.

Based on the faecal grading system previously described [42] and considering the actual moisture content of the samples, all specimens were systematically classified into five distinct gradations (L1–L5). The L1 grade closely resembled normal calf faeces in consistency and appearance, while the L4 grade exhibited physical characteristics typical of calves with diarrhoea, particularly demonstrating elevated moisture content and altered viscosity. L5 was characterised by blood-red colouration and exhibited the highest water content. Based on the characteristics and water content of the faeces, we classified this group as displaying the most severe form of diarrhoea.

Samples from the NA group were prioritised for sequencing experiments, resulting in an insufficient number of remaining samples for photographic documentation. Consequently, we could not obtain data to assess the water content and properties of this group; therefore, we categorised these four samples as the NA group.

Bacterial 16S rRNA gene sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

The V4–V5 regions of the 16S rRNA genes were amplified using specific primers 515F and 907R, which included barcodes [43]. All PCR reactions were conducted in 30 μl volumes, each containing 15 μl of Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs, USA), 0.2 μM of each forward and reverse primer, and approximately 10 ng of template DNA. Thermal cycling consisted of an initial denaturation at 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of 98 °C denaturation for 10 s, 50 °C annealing for 30 s, and 72 °C elongation for 60 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The same volume of 1X loading buffer containing SYBR Green was mixed with the PCR products, and electrophoresis was performed using a 2% agarose gel for detection. Samples with bright main bands ranging from 400 to 450 bp were selected for further experiments. PCR products were mixed at equal density ratios and purified using a GeneJET Gel Extraction Kit (Thermo Scientific). Sequencing libraries were generated using the NEBNext® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, USA) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, with index codes added. The library quality was assessed using the Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) and the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent, USA). Finally, the library was sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform, generating 250 bp paired-end reads.

Paired-end sequences were merged using FLASH (v1.2.11) and clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at a 97% similarity threshold using the USEARCH (v7.0.1090) clustering algorithm. OTUs were selected using the UPARSE OTU and UPARSE OTUref algorithms and chimeras were identified using UCHIME. Representative sequences for each OTU were selected, and the RDP classifier (v2.2) [44] was used to annotate taxonomic information for each sequence with a confidence level of 0.7 through the Silva (Release 132) database [45].

For each experiment, the OTU table was rarefied using the same sequence numbers. For each experiment, OTU table was rarefied using an equal number of sequences. Alpha diversity indices, including Chao, Simpson, and Shannon indices, were calculated based on OTU richness and Mothur (v1.41.0). Beta diversity was assessed using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis distances to evaluate the relationships among different groups. Differential bacterial taxa between the groups were identified using the linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe v1.0), with significant difference defined as those exceeding an linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score (log10) threshold of 2.5.

Metagenomic sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Faecal DNA samples were subjected to shotgun metagenomic sequencing, and DNA libraries were prepared using the TruSeq™ DNA Sample Prep Kit, following the manufacturer’s protocol. The libraries were pooled and sequenced on a SURFSeq 5000 platform (GeneMind Biosciences Company Limited), generating 150 bp paired-end reads with an average depth of approximately 34 million reads per sample.

Raw reads were adapter trimmed using cutadapt (version 4.4) and SOAPnuke (v2.1.6) was then applied to filter low-quality reads as follows: (1) reads with ≥ 60 bp with a quality score of < 20; (2) reads with ≥ 1 N base; (3) reads with an overlap of ≥ 8 bp with adapters.

The data were aligned to the Bos taurus genome (GCA_021347905.1 ARS-LIC_NZ_Holstein-Friesian) using Bowtie2 (version 2.4.5, end-to-end) to remove host reads. All clean reads were first assembled into contigs using the “assembly” function of MetaWRAP (v1.3.0) with the parameter “-l 500 -megahit”. For the binning process, the “binning” function of MetaWRAP was employed, utilising three methodologies: CONCOCT (v1.0.0), MaxBin (v2.2.6), and MetaBAT2 (v2.12.1) with parameter “-l 1500”, and then, the “bin_refinement” function was used to consolidate the binning results from these three approaches to recover a single and improved bin set. The quality of the metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) was assessed using CheckM(v1.0.12), with retention criteria set at ≥ 50% completeness and ≤ 10% contamination. The relative abundance (transcripts per million, TPM) of each MAG was obtained via the “quant_bins” function from MetaWRAP, utilising Salmon (v 0.13.1). After improving MAG with reassembly via the “reassemble_bins” function, taxonomic affiliations for each improved MAG were determined using GTDB-Tk (v2.3.2) classify_wf, with the R214 database. Only species-level genome bins (SGBs) were retained and used for the phylogenetic analysis conducted using FastTree (v2.1.10). The phylogenetic tree was visualised using TVBOT. Differential SGBs between the groups were identified using LEfSe (v1.0), with significant difference defined as those exceeding an LDA score (log10) threshold of 2.5.

Functional annotation of SGBs

Open reading frames (ORFs) from all assembled contigs of SGBs were identified with Prodigal (v2.6.3) with the parameter “-p meta”, including amino acid and nucleic acid sequences. CD-HIT (v4.8.1) was used to construct a non-redundant gene catalogue, using the parameters (sequence identity ≥ 95% and sequence coverage ≥ 90%). The TPM and read counts of non-redundant genes were calculated using salmon. Gene differences were identified using DEseq2 (v1.42.1). Antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) were annotated using the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD; v3.2.8) via diamond (v 2.1.8), using the parameters (blastx, –evaluation 1e-5 –very-sensitive –subject-cover 90), and the TPM of ARGs was calculated using Salmon. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway annotation was generated using the evolutionary gene genealogy Non-supervised Orthologous Groups (eggNOG) database (v5.0) and eggNOG-mapper (v 2.1.12) with the parameters (-d bact). Enrichment analysis of the KEGG pathway was conducted using the R package clusterProfiler (v4.10.1). ORFs from SGB, identified as E. coli, were predicted using Prodigal software. The resulting gene set was then aligned against the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB V2025) using DIAMOND with the parameters set to > 80% identity, E-value < 1e-5, very sensitive mode, and > 90% coverage. Redundant hits were removed, and only the best-hit virulence factor was retained for each gene.

Co-occurrence networks analysis

The co-occurrence network was constructed based on the correlations (Pearson correlation coefficient ≥ 0.6, p-value < 0.01) between the SGBs in each group (healthy groups: L1 and L2, L3, L4), utilising the “cormat” function of the WGCNA package (v1.73). Co-occurrence networks were visualised using Cytoscape (v3.10.2).

Analysis of the virus

Viral sequences were identified based on the SOP provided by VirSorter2 (2.2.4). Briefly, VirSorter2 was run with a loose cutoff of 0.5 for maximal sensitivity and a minimal length of 1500 bp. CheckV (v 1.0.1) was then used to control the quality of the VirSorter2 results and trim potential host regions left at the ends of proviruses with the “end_to_end” pipeline. After running VirSorter2 again, all viral sequences were identified based on the non-redundant (NR) protein database from NCBI (data as of 13 November 2023) and Diamond (blastx –evalue 1e-5 –very-sensitive –subject-cover 90), and only the annotated sequences remained. Differential viruses between the groups were identified using LEfSe. Temperate or virulent viruses were identified using BACPHLIP (v0.9.3-alpha).

Statistical analysis

The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to identify differences between groups. For comparisons between two groups, the unpaired Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used. The Kruskal–Wallis and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were performed using the stat_compare_means function of the R package ggpubr (v0.6.0). The versions, references, and citation details for all software tools and databases utilised in this study are comprehensively documented in Table S2.

Results

No significant influence on the incidence of diarrhoea in calves by sampling points and medication

Farm-level observations

Farming scale varies widely, with the distribution of calf numbers ranging from 42 to 2000, and the proportion of calves with diarrhoea differed among the farms (Table 1). For example, five farms in Alxa showed low diarrhoea percentage (1–6%), and Hinggan League farms had a surprisingly high rate of 100%. Several common pathogens associated with NCD were detected, and among the 78 samples, the number of positive cases detected was as follows: Rotavirus (11 cases), E. coli F5 (K99) (8 cases), C. parvum (10 cases), and C. perfringens (33 cases). We also recorded the distance from each farm to the nearest highway to explore the relationship between calf diarrhoea rates and traffic but found no obvious association. Additionally, we observed considerable variation in the management of calf diarrhoea across different farms. Specifically, 66% of the surveyed farms reported not administering medication, whereas 34% (4/12 farms) opted for pharmacological intervention. Notably, a farm in Hohhot adopted a combined therapy of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) and antibiotics to enhance digestive function, but no expected TCM intervention effects were observed. However, our analysis did not reveal a significant correlation between medication use and the prevalence of diarrhoea across the four farms.

Severity-based grouping of diarrhoeal samples

After failing to establish a clear relationship between the location of farms, management practices, and proportion of calves with diarrhoea, we planed to explore the physiological status (Figure S1) of faecal samples from calves with diarrhoea in relation to their gut microbiota. Despite the red hue of the L5 samples, they exhibited an exceptionally high water content. However, our subsequent metagenomic analysis revealed no bovine genomic reads in the L5 samples, leading us to hypothesise that the red discolouration indicates non-bloody diarrhoea. Further investigation revealed that all calves classified under L5 were administered sulphonamides, a known cause of faecal discolouration. Consequently, we merged the L5 samples into the L4 groups for our analysis.

Microbial composition and diversity among diarrhoea calves

Initially, we assessed the alpha diversity indices across the diarrhoea grades, noting that the Chao and Shannon indices peaked in L1, whereas those in L2 were significantly lower (P < 0.05, Fig. 2A). Groups L3 and L4 exhibited similar diversity metrics. These findings demonstrate that calves affected by severe diarrhoea exhibit reduced intestinal microbiota diversity. PCoA confirmed no farm-based spatial clustering, prompting reclassification based on disease severity (Fig. 2B). Subsequent analyses revealed obvious shifts in the microbial composition among the grades (Fig. 2C–E). Notably, Bacteroidales was predominant in L1 and L2, whereas Lactobacillales was more abundant in L3 and L4. Metagenomic binning and disease grades-associated analysis

Bacteria genomes analysis

Owing to the limitations associated with 16S rRNA gene sequencing at the species-level resolution of faecal microbes, we supplemented our analysis with metagenomic sequencing. Samples were selected for metagenomic analysis based on PCoA of 16S rRNA gene sequencing in Fig. 2B, excluding samples with failed library construction, totalling 44 samples across different regions (Table S1). To refine the microbial composition, we initiated metagenomic data binning, yielding 84 bins (> 90% completeness; < 5% contamination) and seven high-quality bins with genome completeness ranging from 93.01 to 97.9%, including Treponema_D porcinum, Prevotella sp002251385, Alistipes shahii, Phocaeicola massiliensis, Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, Parabacteroides, and Streptococcus ruminantium (Table S3). We constructed high-quality phylogenetic trees for the 12 bacterial genera and identified two predominant phyla, including Bacillota and Bacteroidota (Fig. 3A). Class-level distribution showed Clostridia as the predominant lineages. Strikingly, the L4 group exhibited higher relative abundance in the family Lachnospiraceae and the order Lactobacillales. E. coli (bin.216) and virulence genes analysis

E. coli F5 (K99) was detected in more than 10% of calves with diarrhoea (Table S1). 16S rRNA gene sequencing and SGB analysis demonstrated high E. coli prevalence rates (76/78 cases in Table S4 and 42/42 cases in Table S5, respectively), consistent with its role as a commensal organism. However, technical limitations in strain-level resolution preclude definitive identification of pathogenic lineages.

Subsequent virulence gene annotation using the virulence factor database identified 33 virulence-associated genes in the assembled E. coli (bin.216) genome (Table S6). Notably, ten genes were linked to enterobactin/ferrienterobactin biosynthesis and export (entABCDE and fepABCD), whereas six genes encoded E. coli common pilus (ECP) components (ecpABCDER). The relative abundance of virulence genes across the samples was quantified as TPM. Overall, five samples in group H failed to detect all virulence genes, and only four samples in group L4, with the latter exhibiting fewer missing genes (Table S6). Subsequent differential analysis identified nine virulence genes with significant variation in abundance (Figure S3, P < 0.05). Notably, while fimG and cfaA showed higher relative abundance in group H, the remaining seven genes were markedly enriched in groups L3 and L4. Intriguingly, fepA, entC, and entA abundance demonstrated a dose-dependent relationship with the severity of diarrhoea.

Archaea genomes analysis

We also resolved high-quality genomes from the five archaeal species (Fig. 3B) that belong to the families Methanocorpusculaceae and Methanobacteriaceae. Notably, Methanobrevibacter_B boviskoreani exhibited the highest relative abundance in L3, whereas Methanocorpusculum sp. 017387505 and Methanobrevibacter_B wolinii were the most abundant in L2. In addition, Methanobrevibacter sp. 900314635 and Methanobrevibacter_A sp. 900313645 demonstrated higher relative abundances in L1, indicating a disease grade-dependent archaeal distribution.

Gut metabolic shifts in diarrhoea severity

To further elucidate the variations in faecal bacterial and archaeal species compositions across disease grades, we performed LEfSe analysis (Figure S4). Cluster analysis, based on the trends of the average TPM of bacteria (Fig. 4A), identified four distinct patterns corresponding to disease grades (21, 20, 13, and 9 species). A heatmap of bacterial TPM across these patterns highlighted species in pattern four (nine species), with elevated LDA scores in L4. Furthermore, KEGG pathway analysis of genes from these species revealed enrichment in metabolic processes (Fig. 4B), particularly in the metabolism of terpenoids and polyketides, as well as xenobiotic biodegradation and metabolism pathways. Of particular interest was the archaeon MethanobrevibacterA sp900313645, a putative hydrogenotrophic methanogen. Its depletion in L3/L4 calves parallels clinical observations linking Methanobrevibacter smithii deficiency to post-transplant diarrhoea in humans, likely due to impaired H2 disposal and metabolic dysregulation [46]. We hypothesised that reduced methanogenesis in L3/L4 calves might disrupt syntrophic fermentation and exacerbate diarrhoeal pathogenesis. This aligns with the evidence suggesting that microbiota dysbiosis, particularly the depletion of archaea, compromises gut ecosystem resilience, potentially initiating or perpetuating diarrhoea. As key microbiota components, gut-associated phages play a pivotal role in gastrointestinal pathophysiology by modulating intestinal immune responses and bacterial community dynamics. Given the success of phage therapy in managing diarrhoea in calves and piglets, our discovery of S. ruminantium-associated phage depletion in L3/L4 calves presents a potential clinically actionable target [47, 48]. Furthermore, by annotating the assembled sequences of S. ruminantium (bin.338) against the CARD database, only three antibiotic resistance genes were identified: sul3 (sulphonamide antibiotic), cfrE (phenicol antibiotic, oxazolidinone antibiotic, streptogramin antibiotic, lincosamide antibiotic, and pleuromutilin antibiotic), and tetB(P) (tetracycline antibiotic). Notably, the Ulanqab farm had administered sulphamonomethoxine sodium injections as part of its therapeutic regimen (Table 1). This pharmacological intervention potentially contributed to the emergence of sul3 in S. ruminantium (bin.338).

Discussion

Through systematically collecting rectal faecal specimens from 12 representative dairy farms across Inner Mongolia, we established a diarrhoeal severity grading system showing an obvious disruption in microbial community homeostasis in severely affected calves. Metagenomic binning identified two potential bacterial pathogens, E. coli (bin.216), employing non-canonical virulence mechanisms, and S. ruminantium (bin.338), with undefined pathogenic mechanisms.

Our integrated analysis of 16S rRNA gene sequencing and metagenomic SGB data (with only one E. coli strain successfully assembled) revealed consistently high E. coli detection rates (76/78 in Table S4 and 42/44 in Table S5), reinforcing its established role as a core commensal organism in the calf gut microbiome. Intriguingly, profiling of E. coli (bin.216) revealed 33 virulence genes and seven canonical markers (including intimin-eae, shiga toxins-stx1/2, hemolysin-hly, aggregative adherence transporter-aatA, stable toxin-st, and liable toxin-lt), suggesting divergence from classical enterotoxigenic (ETEC) or Shiga toxin-producing (STEC) pathotypes [49]. This atypical profile was reinforced by (i) elevated virulence gene abundance in severe cases (L3/L4), particularly enterobactin/ferrienterobactin biosynthesis and export genes (fepA, entC, and entA) (Figure S3), and (ii) enrichment of Escherichia phage VpaE1_ev108 in these groups (Fig. 5B). Collectively, these findings suggest an emerging, regionally adapted E. coli pathotype in Mongolian herds, possibly leveraging non-canonical virulence mechanisms. This hypothesis warrants longitudinal surveillance and functional validation.

Accumulating evidence supports the therapeutic potential of phage-based interventions against bacterial diarrhoea in ruminants. Probiotic-phage suppositories combining Lactobacillus spp. and Myoviridae phages (φ26, 27, and 29) effectively reduced diarrhoeal duration in neonatal calves infected with E. coli strains Stx and K99 [48]. Similarly, intramuscular administration of E. coli phage Φ IA-38 significantly reduced faecal E. coli K99 counts (P < 0.05) in infected lambs, achieving comparable efficacy to antibiotic treatments [50]. These findings suggest that employing the safe Escherichia phage VpaE1_ev108 to modulate the intestinal microbiota balance, particularly targeting the commensal E. coli (bin.216) strain harbouring atypical virulence genes, is advantageous. To validate this hypothesis, we proposed a phased experimental approach: (1) isolation of the target E. coli strain from faecal samples of calves with diarrhoea; (2) synthesis of phage VpaE1_ev108; (3) in vitro evaluation of VpaE1_ev108 through plaque assays; (4) oral administration trials in murine models to assess safety and efficacy; (5) suppository-based application trials in calves. This systematic review aimed to establish foundational evidence for translating phage therapy into practical agricultural applications.

In our study, we categorised the severity of NCD in calves based on faecal characteristics and identified key pathogenic species associated with varying disease severity using this grading system. Consistent with previous findings [51, 52], our results revealed variations in intestinal microbiota composition among calves with different disease severities, with Bacteroides being prominent in the healthy cohort. Notably, we observed a significant positive correlation between f-Streptococcaceae and disease severity (Fig. 2D, LDA score (log10) > 2.5).

LEfSe analysis revealed a significant enrichment (Fig. 4A, LDA score (log10) > 2.5) of S. ruminantium (bin.338. orig) in group L4, whereas the co-occurrence network analysis demonstrated an inverse abundance trend for Methanobrevibacter_A sp900313645, which was significantly depleted in L3/L4 (Fig. 7B, P < 0.05). As key archaeal constituents of the rumen microbiome, Methanobrevibacter species are substantially depleted in diarrhoeic calves compared to healthy counterparts [53]. Bacterial dysbiosis drives a decline in the acetate-to-propionate ratio and reduces H2 availability, consequently affecting the archaeal community [54]. Depletion of Methanobrevibacter may further disrupt cross-domain microbial interactions, ultimately impairing intestinal barrier integrity. Faecal microbiota transplantation incorporating diverse rumen microorganisms (including bacteria, protozoa, fungi, and archaea) has been demonstrated as an effective approach for treating diarrheic calves [55], highlighting the therapeutic potential of archaea-probiotic combinations [3, 55, 56].

The management of NCD has been a major research focus [57, 58]; however, antibiotic therapy remains the predominant intervention to date [59–61]. Historically, antimicrobials have been widely used for prophylaxis and treatment of calf diarrhoea, despite growing concerns regarding antimicrobial resistance. A retrospective survey of calf diarrhoea and pneumonia revealed that 35.50% of calves received prophylactic antimicrobials, while 35.66% of diseased calves received antimicrobials as therapy [62]. However, the efficacy of antibiotics in preventing or treating calf diarrhoea remains controversial. While some historical reviews reported reduced diarrhoea duration with certain antibiotics, such as chlortetracycline [63], others demonstrated no benefit or increased diarrhoea incidence in antibiotic-treated calves [64–66]. Most alarmingly, recent studies have indicated that prophylactic antibiotic administration failed to reduce the incidence of diarrhoea in calves, with certain regimens paradoxically increasing the risk of diarrhoea compared with untreated controls [67, 68]. Therefore, modern antimicrobial stewardship policies mandate that all basal feedstuffs and milk replacers for dairy operations should remain antibiotic-free in their standard formulations. In our study cohort, although some farms employed antibiotic therapy for diarrhoeal cases, no obvious association was observed between diarrhoea severity and antibiotic administration.

Furthermore, our study revealed a broad spectrum of resistance genes across most farms (Fig. 8B), including those not subjected to pharmaceutical intervention (Table 1). This observation raises alarms regarding antibiotic use and emphasises the pressing necessity of elucidating the precise transmission routes and pathogenesis of NCD to effectively mitigate their occurrence. Two innovative therapeutic strategies show promise for diarrhoeal disease management: (1) phage-mediated pathogenic bacterial targeting and (2) synergistic application of probiotic-phage consortia. These approaches could potentially reduce reliance on conventional antibiotics, thereby contributing to mitigating antimicrobial resistance while maintaining gut microbiota homeostasis [50, 69–71].

The multifactorial pathogenesis of NCD and challenges in its effective control necessitate continued investigation of the contributing factors. Calf management practices, including calving pen design, colostrum management, group housing conditions, bedding material selection, and antibiotic management, significantly influence diarrhoea-related morbidity and mortality [57, 58]. A growing body of evidence highlights the benefits of colostrum feeding in reducing the number of calves with diarrhoea and promoting improved calf growth [72, 73]. The abundance of immune and bioactive factors in colostrum, including immunoglobulins, natural antimicrobial factors, growth factors, anti-inflammatory cytokines, and nutrients, makes it an attractive alternative to antimicrobials for treating neonatal calf diarrhoea [73, 74]. In addition, emerging research has highlighted the potential of probiotic and prebiotic supplementation to enhance gut health and reduce the incidence of diarrhoea in neonatal calves. Common formulations contain live Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Enterococcus, and Bacillus species [75, 76]. Moreover, epidemiological investigations have suggested that potential scale-dependent risks are associated with herd size [58]. Our data revealed no obvious correlation between farm size and prevalence of diarrhoea. These observations reinforce that comprehensive prevention strategies must synergistically address biological determinants and operational variables in contemporary calf production systems.

Although we collected samples from representative cattle farms in Inner Mongolia, the relatively small sample size (n = 78) may not fully reflect the health status of dairy calves across China’s diverse cattle farming industry due to funding constraints. Furthermore, although the approach integrating ELISA-based pathogen detection with NGS techniques (16S rRNA gene sequencing and metagenomic analysis) effectively identified microbial composition shifts, data on the mechanistic underpinnings of gene expression, and metabolite levels remain limited. Future studies should integrate metatranscriptomic and metabolomic analyses to obtain comprehensive functional insights into the pathogenesis of NCDs. Our assessment of calf physiological parameters warrants expansion to incorporate additional rumen microenvironment measurements, particularly pH fluctuations and hydrogen accumulation [77, 78]. These critical environmental factors can potentially modulate gut microbial dynamics.

In summary, our findings fill critical knowledge gaps in livestock microbiome management by demonstrating three pivotal advancements. First, identifying E. coli (bin.216) and S. ruminantium (bin.338) as putative neonatal diarrhoeal pathogens establishes new targets for precision veterinary medicine. Second, the protective consortium formed by Bacteroides spp. and Methanobrevibacter sp. 900313645 provides potential biomarkers for probiotic development. Most importantly, our proposed phage-probiotic synbiotic framework offers a blueprint for global antimicrobial stewardship that can potentially reduce prophylactic antibiotic use in intensive farming systems, while delaying multidrug-resistant pathogen emergence through ecological niche competition.
