Towards real-time additive-free dopamine detection at 10−8 mM with hardware accelerated platform integrated on camera
Authors: Ning Li, Qizhou Wang, Zhao He, Arturo Burguete-Lopez, Fei Xiang, Andrea Fratalocchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTracing physiological neurotransmitters such as dopamine (DA) with detection limits down to 1×10−8 mM is a critical goal in neuroscience for studying brain functions and progressing the understanding of cerebral disease. Addressing this problem requires enhancing the current state-of-the-art additive-free electrochemical workstation methods by over two orders of magnitude. In this work, we implement an ultra-sensitive, additive-free platform exploiting suitably engineered light-scattering membranes and optical accelerators integrated into commercial vision cameras, reporting real-time detection of DA in uric and ascorbic acid below the concentration of 10−8 mM. These performances improve the current best technology by over two orders of magnitude in resolution while providing continuous, real-time detection at video rates. This technology also upgrades the bulk form factor of an electrochemical workstation with an imaging camera’s compact and portable footprint. The optical accelerator implemented in this work is universal and trainable to detect a wide range of biological analytes. This technology’s wide adoption could help enable early disease detection and personalized treatment adjustments while improving the management of neurological, mental, and immune-related conditions.Subject terms: Nanobiotechnology, Sensors and probes, Medical research, Optical spectroscopy, Neuroscience

Introduction

Tracing analyte concentrations at ultra-low quantities is essential across various scientific areas, including security1–3, medicine4–8, food safety9, and environmental protection10,11. In disease diagnosis, ultra-sensitive detection enables tracking subtle changes of biomarker concentration, which indicate early onset or progression of debilitating conditions12. Dopamine (DA) is a fundamental neurotransmitter biomarker whose microscopic change within the 10−7 mM range13–16 reveals various neurological disorders, including Parkinson’s17,18, Alzheimer’s19, depression20, schizophrenia21,22, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder23. Practical strategies for ultra-sensitive detection of DA require identifying the analyte in interference media such as uric acid (UA) and ascorbic acid (AA) without any additive incorporation of enzymes, polymers, aptamers, etc., which introduce impurities and instabilities24,25.

The state-of-the-art in additive-free biosensors that work in physiological conditions reaches the limit of 5 × 10−6 mM with Mn-MoS2/PGS sensor26. These biosensors do not work in real-time and necessitate measurement times from tens of seconds to several minutes27–29, while requiring an enhancement of more than two orders of magnitude to reach the range between 10−8 and 10−6 mM characteristic of a majority of brain disorders30,31. Devising pathways to enhance these performances is challenging due to background noise interference with the signal that, at ultra-low sensitivities, yields variation of measurement results across different manufactured devices32,33, making it difficult to deploy an efficient decision-making strategy that minimizes human bias34,35. Addressing these issues provides a significant opportunity in research, promising early diagnosis and low-cost routine monitoring that could help progress against elusive disorders affecting tens of millions of people worldwide.

This work implements an ultra-sensitive detection platform leveraging hardware artificial intelligence (AI), incorporating data acquisition and feature extraction into a single process. The hardware accelerator integrates with commercially available monochrome cameras, retaining their footprint, portability, and video rate processing speed. In experimental tests, the system reports DA detection at a concentration of 10−8 mM with interference media including UA, AA, phosphate buffer solution (PBS), and artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF).

The proposed integrated hardware AI platform enhanced the detection limit of DA by six orders of magnitude compared to existing AI-based biochemical sensing approaches, which rely solely on software-implemented models such as support vector machine36 and neural networks (NNs)37,38. Furthermore, the proposed platform achieves two orders of magnitude compared to state-of-the-art additive-free DA detection technologies26, while providing a compact, fully integrated form factor that potentially enables point-of-care deployment.

Results

Figure 1 provides a general scheme of the ultra-sensitive detection platform we implement in this work. Input light with spectral intensity distribution Ii(ω) impinges on an analyte with unknown concentration y, placed inside a suitably designed scattering surface (Fig. 1a). When illuminated by a broadband input Ii(ω), the surface outputs a scattering response Io(ω, y) that could leverage different forms of light-matter interaction, including plasmonic resonances in metallic nanostructures39, Mie or Fano resonance in high-refractive-index dielectrics40, and chemical bonding between the analyte and molecules with high scattering cross section26.

Schematics of the general sensing platform.

a Spectral dataset generation using a scattering surface with broadband illumination. b Example of a generated spectral dataset for a varying concentration of analyte. c Encoder-decoder unsupervised training model with explainable-AI (XAI) interface for selecting latent features. d Implementation design of a hardware encoder with sub-pixels of inverse-designed nanoresonators. e Integration of the hardware encoder into a CMOS camera sensor. f Software post-processing network processes the extracted latent features into the analyte concentration. g Each sub-pixel encoder’s trained spectral transmission response implements the multiply and accumulate (MAC) neural network operation required to extract the selected latent space features.

The spectra obtained at different analyte concentrations y compose a training dataset (Fig. 1b). The dataset contains sparse spectral features relating the concentration y with the input beam Ii(ω) spectrum. Instead of characterizing the data with prior-known metrics, the platform uses the spectral dataset to train a hardware encoder E, which extracts spectral features using AI hardware (Fig. 1c). The encoder E follows the methodology of unsupervised dimensionality reduction41, and transforms high-dimensional scattered spectra Io(ω, y) into a latent vector z=E(Io(ω,y)), representing a low-dimensional, refined feature space of the input spectra. In practical tasks such as regression and classification, a machine learning model can leverage the latent vectors to give predictions beyond normal metrics. A decoder model D employs the information contained in the latent vector z to reconstruct the input spectra, providing at the output the set of spectra Io′(ω,y) as close as possible to the input ones. Training of the E- D systems comprises minimizing the mean square error (MSE) loss42 between the input and reconstructed spectra: Lrec=1N∑y∥Io(ω,y)−Io′(ω,y)∥, where N represents the total number of spectra samples. As the training converges, the encoder E provides an information-preserving projection from the spectral scattering responses to the distribution of all possible low-dimensional representations, yielding a latent space encompassing the important sparse features of the input spectra Io(ω, y). The proposed framework consequently applies a supervised feature selection process in the latent space using explainable AI (XAI)43, which optimizes the number of latent features for predicting the analyte concentration y (arrow under Fig. 1c).

We implement the encoder E in optoelectronic hardware using nanostructured encoder elements with engineered transmission responses in the frequency domain Λi(ω) (Fig. 1d) placed over the sensor of a conventional monochrome camera. Figure 2 details the hardware encoder E feature extraction process, using an input spectral dataset X = [Io(ω, y)1, Io(ω, y)2, …, Io(ω, y)N] obtained from DA dissolved in PBS. In this work, we acquire spectral datasets with a commercial Ocean Optics UV-VIS spectrum analyzer equipped with an integrating sphere. The dataset X comprises N = 240 reflection spectra equally measured in 8 concentrations varying from 10−1 to 10−8 mM (Fig. 2a). In this work, we perform dimensionality reduction by principal component analysis (PCA) (Fig. 2b). PCA is a widely used technique in spectral material analysis and classification47–49 that assists in finding the smallest group of uncorrelated and repeated spectral shapes, which linearly combine to reconstruct the dataset X. PCA encodings implement a projection camera process with an identity transfer function, which maximizes the data processing speed. The PCA eigenvector components represent the MAC weights Λ(ω) = [Λ(ω)1, …, λM(ω)] for training the hardware accelerator encoder in Fig. 1f. The first M = 8 components represent the spectral data up to a total of 99.8% explained variance and result in a dataset reconstruction loss Lrec=1.2×10−6. Figure 2b projects the input spectral dataset X along the first three principal components. The projected principal components provide a new set of coordinates to represent the original reflectance spectra, serving as a basis for feature separation and identification. The principal features provide a new set of coordinates to represent the data with successive refinement, serving as a basis for feature separation and identification. The projected points in Fig. 2b show this property quantitatively, separating data distribution patterns in the space where high-concentration and low-concentration data points cluster in different areas.

We identify the most useful principal components for analyte concentration prediction using the XAI integrated gradient (IG) method50. The IG trains an auxiliary regression network F to predict the analyte concentration (green block in Fig. 2c). The network F uses input data comprising the spectral features z = [z1, …, zM], with zi being the projection of the input spectra along the ith principal component ui. The training of F aims to minimize the regression loss Lreg=∣∣y^−y∣∣ between its prediction output y^=F(z) and the ground truth concentration y obtained from measurements. The IG method constructs an integral path from z′ to z by linearly interpolating between them (Fig. 2c). Along this path, it computes the gradient on inputs z′+α(z−z)′ via backpropagation, with α ∈ [0, 1] being the interpolation factor. The integral value in (2) is rescaled by zi−zi′ to ensure that the IS is proportional to the distance between the input and the void sample. The larger the IS deviates from 0, the greater significance zi carries for predicting the analyte concentrations y. Figure 2d plots the absolute values of the calculated IS and the explained variances of each principal component. While consecutive principal components represent the input dataset with accumulating total variance (Fig. 2d dashed line), the IG analysis highlights that the four most informative components for predicting an analyte concentration are in a nonconsecutive order (Fig. 2d, red solid lines). Further reducing the PCA components to the four with the highest IS projects X to a feature space with an explained variance of 83.92% for the input data while carrying a total IS of 86.99%. Figure 4a illustrates the scattering device implemented in this study (details in the “Methods” section). The system integrates a scattering plasmonic system in a microfluidic cell, comprising a central cylindrical cavity with a diameter of 10 mm, capable of accommodating 1 mL of solution. The device includes two input and output analyte channels, each with a diameter of 0.5 mm, positioned on either side of the cavity (Fig. 4a, solid arrows). Figure 4b shows the schematic of the device’s assembly. The bottom scattering layer comprises a nanostructured silicon (Si) wafer decorated with metallic nanoparticles (Fig. 4c). We fabricate nanostructured Si using wet-chemistry self-assembly followed by nanostructuring via reactive ion etching (RIE). The RIE process is separately optimized for each analyte, leveraging the available RIE parameters comprising the gas flow of SF6, CHF3, O2, the set pressure, the power of inductively coupled plasma, the power of radio frequency, and etching time. The optimization process maximizes the separation of features in the latent space generated by the hardware encoder E (details in the “Methods” section). The metal decoration supports strong surface plasmon resonance at the metal-dielectric interface, where the collective oscillation of free electrons generates an intensified local electromagnetic field51,52. This plasmonic field enhancement amplifies subtle refractive index changes near the surface, leading to strong optical signals and high sensitivity53,54. The device’s top layer is a hydrophobic multilayer structure. This system implements a bottom layer of 1H,1H,2H,2H- Perfluorodecanethiol (PFDT, CF3(CF2)7CH2CH2SH), an interlayer of Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), and cover glass treated by Perfluorodecyltrichlorosilane (FDTS, CF3(CF2)5CH2CH2SiCl3). The hydrophobic inner design prevents the analyte from contaminating the scattering substrate, allowing the sensor to be reusable. This property can facilitate the sensor achieving a cleaner baseline, reduced refractive index drift, and enhanced measurement stability, which together lower background noise and improve detection limits. We further validate the overall stability of the scattering device over time. To assess baseline consistency, we measure the reflectance spectra of the device filled with deionized water at multiple time points over 17 days. Figure 4d shows the mean spectra (solid lines) and the corresponding ±3σ interval (shaded area) for each measurement day, where σ represents the standard deviation. The mean intra-day correlation coefficients are 0.9997, 0.9989, 0.9972, 0.9994, and 0.9986 for Day 1 through Day 17, respectively, confirming highly consistent spectral responses across all measurements. Figure 4e further visualizes the inter-day correlation coefficients, showing a gradual decline over time. Nevertheless, the baseline measurements maintain correlation coefficients above 0.994 after 17 days, which reveals subtle variations of the spectra and demonstrates long-term stability of the scattering device.

Supplementary Figs. S2 and S3 use X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to investigate the mechanisms of PFDT layer deposition over the substrate. In Fig. S2, we present the survey spectra of the scatterer and the PFDT-decorated scatterer, and we normalize them to their respective peak maximum to enable a direct comparison between the samples. The unexpected F 1s signal presents in the scatterer, which is due to fluorocarbon polymer formed during the etching process involving reactions of SF6 and CHF3 with Si55. Compared with the pristine scatterer, the Au 4f signal intensity decreases significantly after PFDT modification. The PFDT-decorated scatterer exhibits a stronger F 1s signal intensity than the control sample. Moreover, sulfur peaks appear in the PFDT-decorated scatterer but are absent in the pristine scatterer. These results indicate that the PFDT chains remain on the surface even after rinsing with solvents.

Figure S3 shows the results of the XPS fitting peak for C 1s, Au 4f, and S 2p. Figure S3a shows that the C 1s spectrum contains peaks corresponding to C–C, C–S, –CF2, and –CF356,57, demonstrating the grafting of long fluorocarbon chains onto the surface of the scatterer. Figure S3b shows the XPS spectrum of Au 4f. The Au 4f spectrum shows two main peaks at 83.9 eV (Au 4f7/2) and 87.6 eV (Au 4f5/2), respectively. Fitting the Au 4f peaks reveals two states, Au(0) with Au 4f7/2 binding energy at 83.9 eV, and Au(I) at 84.4 eV58,59. The presence of Au(I) indicates the formation of interfacial covalent bonds between the scatterer and the PFDT ligands, specifically through Au–S bonding. Figure S3c shows the XPS spectrum of S 2p. We deconvoluted the S 2p spectrum into three components, and each component includes a spin-orbit splitting doublet (S 2p1/2 and S 2p3/2, △E ≅ 1.2 eV). The first component represents chemisorbed thiols (regions marked with S 2p1/2B and S 2p3/2B in Fig. S3c), while the second corresponds to weakly bound (physisorbed) thiol species (regions marked with S 2p1/2A and S 2p3/2A in Fig. S3c) that still remain on the surface even after thorough rinsing with ethanol and water60. In addition to these two components, a weak but distinct S 2p doublet (regions marked with oxidized sulfur in Fig. S3c) appears at around 168.3 eV and 169.6 eV. Binding energies in the range of 168.0–170.0 eV are commonly attributed to highly oxidized sulfur species such as sulfonates or sulfates61,62, indicating that a fraction of the thiol groups in the PFDT monolayer underwent oxidation during the assembly or subsequent exposure to air.

Figure 5d employs contact angle (CA) measurements to quantify the hydrophobicity of PFDT-treated scatterers. The CA observed lies above 152.3 ± 0. 6∘ with the relative standard deviation of 0.36% computed from 10 different measurements, showing super-hydrophobic behavior (Fig. S4). It implies that no significant desorption or reorganization of the chemisorbed, physisorbed, or oxidized PFDT species occurs on the timescale of the measurements. Therefore, these surface components do not compromise the stability of the interface or the accuracy of the analyte detection. The CA observed for the FDTS-treated cover glass is 102.2 ± 0. 5∘ (Fig. S5), slightly lower than PFDT but still well above the hydrophobic threshold of 90∘.

From the measured 240 spectra with dopamine concentration across 10−8 to 10−1 mM, we leverage 80% of the samples to train the sensing framework and use 20% of the measurements to calibrate and evaluate. Following the common practice in AI-assisted biochemical detection works, the plot visualizes the distribution of predicted concentrations around true concentration levels, defined by their mean values and the 95% confidence interval (CI). As part of the curve calibration process, we apply linear regression to the predicted and true values, resulting in a fitted slope of 0.949, an intercept of −0.372, and a root mean square error (RMSE) value of 0.3113. Figure 5h summarizes the detection results of the proposed platform and compares them against the state-of-the-art26,63,64. The dynamic range achievable with this technique spans eight orders of magnitude, from 10−8 to 10−1 mM. We further evaluate the model’s generalization ability by calculating the R2 score of the prediction on the test dataset, which yields R2 = 0.9845. To align with common practice in AI-based sensing36,65, we define the limit of detection (LOD) as the lowest concentration that is validated under the calibration framework, which is 10−8 mM. The LOD is comparable to the best integrated and non-integrated state-of-the-art, respectively. It also provides the broadest dynamic range, surpassing the best Mn-MoS2/PGS electrochemical device by two orders of magnitude26.

Discussion

This work implements a hardware-accelerated ultra-sensitive platform technology to detect biochemical analytes in real time. The proposed technology integrates conventional camera sensors and does not require extra additives, avoiding traditional impurity treatment requiring between tens of minutes and several hours82 that make continuous motoring impossible. The entire manufacturing process for a single detection system is within 1000 USD, evaluated with clean room actual costs. This figure represents 1/10 of the price of a state-of-the-art electrochemical workstation, which also requires a significantly larger footprint than a portable integrated camera. By reaching the threshold of DA detection at 10−8 mM, the proposed technology can enable real-time tracking of addiction-related DA dynamics83, and early diagnosis of Parkinson’s disease, facilitating the study of DA’s influence on emotional regulation84,85, and neurotoxic effects86. The universal design of this sensing platform, combining trained optical accelerators with software NNs, can extend this study on a wide range of additional analytes relevant to medical research, including serotonin and norepinephrine for studying depression and anxiety87, glutamate and gamma-aminobutyric acid for understanding neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer’s and epilepsy88,89, and cytokine indicators for cancer and immune system disorders90.

Methods

Materials and reagents

We purchase polystyrene (PS) latex particles (PS200NM, 10% w/v) from MAGSPHERE Inc. We order SylgardTM 184 from Electron Microscopy Sciences. We acquire ethanol, toluene, and sodium dodecyl sulfate solutions (SDS, 10% in H2O), PFDT, PBS, DA, UA/AA from Sigma-Aldrich Inc. We obtain Si wafers from University Wafer Inc. We purchase the artificial cerebrospinal solution (BZ453) from BiochemazoneTM. We use deionized water (Milli-Q® Advantage A10) with a resistance of 18 MΩ cm in all experiments.

Fabrication of the scattering surface

We clean the Si surface with isopropanol, acetone, and ultra-pure water and then dry it with nitrogen gas. Next, we prepare the PS solutions by mixing the ethanol and water with a volume ratio of 1:1 and make these latex particles disperse in the solutions homogeneously by ultrasonic process for 30 min. We then perform self-assembly of PS on the water with the assistance of SDS. After the air-drying process, we etch the sample in the RIE instrument (Oxford Instruments, Plasmalab 100 - ICP 380). We use an oxygen plasma process (JST Manufacturing, Inc., ST0122A0) to remove the residual PS. We finally deposit 80 nm gold film on the structured Si substrate in the sputtering process (Equipment Support Company Ltd, ESCRD4), followed by the annealing process (Qualflow Them/Jipelec, JETFIRST) at 200 °C for 5 min.

Assembly of the scattering device

We perform an oxygen plasma process on the PDMS layer for 1 min. We then assemble it with a PFDT decorated scatterer and an FDTS modified cover glass. After stabilizing the structure for 2 days, the cell is ready for experiments.

Fabrication of the sub-pixel encoders

Using plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition, we deposit amorphous silicon on a fused silica substrate. We consequently spin coat and thermal-process the AR-P 6200.09 (Allresist GmbH) positive electron beam resist and a conductive polymer AR-PC 5090.02 layer on the substrate. We employ a JEOL JBX-6300FS electron-beam lithography system to write the nanostructure pattern, followed by a resist development process. We then deposit a 22 nm layer of chromium on the substrate to form a protective layer in the shape of the patterns to be unetched. We use a RIE system to expose the patterns of silicon. As a final step, we remove the chromium protection mask with perchloric acid and ceric ammonium nitrate solution (TechniEtch Cr01 from MicroChemicals).

Physical characterization

We collect the SEM images on a Carl Zeiss Merlin field-emission SEM with an In-Lens detector. We collect the high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images and STEM-EDS mapping of the FIB-prepared specimen with Titan ST transmission electron microscope (FEI Company) at an operating voltage of 300 kV. We perform the XPS measurements on the Kratos AXIS Supra system. We use a UV-vis spectrometer (Ocean Optics SR-6XR250-10) alongside the OceanView 2.0.18 software to characterize the reflectance of the samples in the 400–800 nm wavelength range.
