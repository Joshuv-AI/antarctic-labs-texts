Water triggers visible-light photochromism in a hydrogen-bonded organic framework
Authors: Haibo Liang, Linjing Tong, Gan Luo, Xiaoxue Kou, Yujian Shen, Yong Shen, Siming Huang, Guosheng Chen, Gangfeng Ouyang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhotochromic materials switched by user-friendly conditions are highly sought after for secure information technologies and adaptive camouflage but remain a formidable challenge. Here, by leveraging dynamic H-bond networks and periodic π-stacked columns in a hydrogen-bonded organic framework (HOF), we report a reversible visible light photochromic system using water as a “trigger” rather than “destroyer”. This behavior arises in a pyrene-based framework, HOF-14, where water adsorption induces a specific lattice contraction via dynamic H-bond network. Such structural change triggers a fundamental redistribution of the framework’s electronic structure, dramatically lowering the exciton binding energy fallen below the ambient thermal energy. This enables spontaneous visible-light-driven electron-hole separation to form pyrene radical cations, which are stabilized by the stacked pyrene columns in HOF. The photochromic transition—a rapid color change from yellow to green within 10 s—is reversible over 50 cycles. We demonstrate high-level information encryption using this HOF ink and its hydrogel formulations, and fabricate adaptive camouflage textiles that dynamically match a foliated environment. These HOF smart textiles are readily recyclable in a closed-loop process, benefiting from the inherent self-repair capability of HOFs—a distinctive feature not found in other classes of porous crystalline materials.Subject terms: Light harvesting, Self-assembly, Self-assembly

Introduction

The escalating demands of the digital age and modern defense have positioned information security and adaptive camouflage as critical global priorities1,2. This has spurred intensive research into stimuli-responsive materials for advanced encryption technologies3–5. Photochromism, which uses spatiotemporally resolved light as a stimulus, is particularly attractive due to its non-contact operation, rapid response, and visual readout6,7. Among these, organic photochromes offer superior structural versatility and ease of modification compared to their inorganic counterparts, enabling unprecedent flexibility for property tailoring. Currently available photochromic systems primarily rely on molecular photoisomerization (e.g., spiropyrans8, diarylethenes9–11, and azobenzenes12,13) or photoinduced electron transfer (e.g., naphthalene diimides14,15, viologens16), which undergo reversible electronic structure changes upon irradiation (Fig. 1a). In most cases, high-energy ultraviolet (UV) irradiation is required to trigger forward switching, despite considerable efforts devoted to red-shifting the absorption spectra of such photoswitches17–19. This reliance on UV light can lead to undesirable photobleaching and potential biological damage, thereby constraining the practical applicability of these systems in real-world scenarios20,21.

Over the past three decades, the development of atomically precise porous reticular frameworks—such as metal–organic frameworks (MOFs)22,23 and covalent organic frameworks (COFs)24,25—has unlocked unprecedented opportunities for functional design. Integrating organic photochromes into these porous scaffolds, either as structural building blocks or guest species, represents a promising paradigm for developing next-generation solid-state photochromic systems (Fig. 1b)26–28, with the following advantages: (1) porous scaffolds shield the embedded chromophores from environmental stresses, enhancing functional durability1,29; (2) it enables precise spatial confinement of chromophores within tailored pore space, preventing aggregation-induced quenching while preserving the molecular freedom necessary for photoisomerization30; (3) the inherent mechanical processability of solid-state materials aligns with real-world settings requirements31,32. Despite these advances, most solid-state organic photochromic systems remain reliant on high-energy and harmful UV irradiation for activation33–35, which not only limits light penetration depth but also contravenes principles of green and sustainable chemistry. Moreover, their forward switching is generally governed by a single stimulus, complicating the realization of high-level information security and adaptive camouflage where multi-stimulus responsiveness is an essential prerequisite36,37. To our knowledge, the development of a solid-state organic photochromic system activated by benign and ubiquitous multi- stimulus remains an unmet challenge.

Hydrogen-bonded organic frameworks (HOFs) represent an emerging class of porous crystalline materials assembled via more flexible intermolecular hydrogen bonds and other supramolecular interactions38,39. They combine the structural precision of reticular frameworks like MOFs and COFs with unique advantages, including environmentally friendly synthesis, facile solution processability, ease of film formation, and an inherent capacity for self-repair40. In particular, HOFs constructed from pyrene-based, carboxylate-functionalized tectons are highly promising for optoelectronics. Their structures are stabilized by strong, face-to-face π-π stacking of the pyrene cores, which not only directs predictable assembly into robust frameworks but also creates highly ordered, one-dimensional π-columns41,42. These columns are known to efficiently stabilize organic radicals through electronic delocalization effect43. We posited that this unique combination of dynamic H-bonded network and electron stabilization within π-stacked columns makes pyrene-based HOFs an ideal, yet largely unexplored, platform for designing next-generation solid-state photochromic materials.

Water is the safest and most ubiquitous solvent on Earth. Although a wide range of photochromic systems—including spiropyran/merocyanine couples and azobenzene derivatives—have been successfully adapted for operation in aqueous environments44–47, water exposure can nevertheless compromise the stability and switching performance of many solid-state photochromic materials based on porous reticular framework31. Accordingly, the exploitation of water as a photochromic trigger in such systems is highly appealing, yet this approach has not been realized to date.

Herein, we report the discovery of a dual-stimulus-responsive solid-state photochromic system within a pyrene-based hydrogen-bonded organic framework (Py-HOF), utilizing visible light and water—both benign, abundant, and sustainable stimuli (Fig. 1c)—as the double-locked photoswitches. We demonstrate that the adsorption-desorption of water molecules within the pores of a Py-HOF (HOF-14) initiates a reversible lattice shrinkage (of 1.3%), driven by the dynamic H-bond network. Crucially, this water-mediated structural contraction shortens the intermolecular H-bond distances of HOF-14, which we reveal leads to a fundamental redistribution of the HOF intralayer electronic structure, substantially reducing the exciton binding energy and dramatically enhancing the efficiency of visible-light-driven electron-hole separation. This process ultimately facilitates the formation of pyrene radical cations within and is consequently stabilized by the discrete π–π stacked columns, while avoiding quenching by water, manifesting as a rapid color change from yellow to green within 10 s. The photochromism is reversible simply by removing the water via either heating or ethanol displacement, with excellent cyclability over more than 50 cycles. Leveraging this unique dual-stimulus (water + visible light) response, we developed highly secure encryption platforms, including invisible inks and smart hydrogels, where concealed information is revealed only upon simultaneous exposure to water and ambient light. Furthermore, we engineered adaptive camouflage textiles that dynamically alter their appearance to blend with a surrounding environment. These textiles are readily recyclable in a closed-loop process, benefiting from the inherent self-repair capability of HOFs—a distinctive feature not found in other classes of porous crystalline materials. This work not only introduces a novel paradigm for environmentally benign photochromism but also establishes HOFs as a premier material class for advanced security and adaptive camouflage technologies.

Results

Photochromic behavior of Py-HOFs

As a proof of concept, we synthesized three isostructural pyrene-based HOFs (Py-HOFs)—HOF-100, PFC-1, and HOF-14—assembled from D2h-symmetric, carboxylic acid-functionalized tectons: 1,3,6,8-tetracarboxy pyrene (H4TCPy)42, 1,3,6,8-tetrakis(p-benzoic acid) pyrene (H4TBAPy)41, and 1,3,6,8-tetra(6-carboxynaphthalen-2-yl) pyrene (H4TNAPy)48, respectively (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 1). The assembly via carboxylic acid dimers and π-π stacking yielded isostructural frameworks with systematically tuned pore sizes from 0.8 × 1.2 nm to 2.5 × 3.0 nm (diagonal dimensions). Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) confirmed their crystallinity, with the experimental patterns of all three Py-HOFs matching well with their simulated counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 2), indicating successful synthesis. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that each Py-HOF crystallizes as nanorods (Supplementary Fig. 3), and Cryo-transmission electron microscopy (Cryo-TEM) further resolved lattice fringes corresponding to the (011) plane of HOF-100 (Supplementary Fig. 4) and PFC-1 (Supplementary Fig. 5) and (110) plane of HOF-14 (Fig. 2b).

The as-synthesized powders of all three Py-HOFs are yellow and exhibit strong UV–vis absorption below 500 nm, attributable to π–π* and n–π* transitions of the highly conjugated pyrene units. Under simulated sunlight within the visible spectrum by a xenon lamp equipped with an AM 1.5 G spectral filter and a 420 nm long-pass cutoff filter (Supplementary Fig. 6; wavelength: λ > 420 nm), neither the dry powders nor the aqueous dispersions of HOF-100 and PFC-1 showed any photochromism (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). To exclude any potential ionic interference, ultrapure water was used as the dispersion medium unless otherwise indicated. Consistently, their UV–vis absorption spectra remained unchanged after illumination. In stark contrast, while dry HOF-14 powder was also photoinert, its aqueous dispersion underwent a distinct color change from yellow to green under identical irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 9). This photochromism was accompanied by the emergence of a new, broad absorption band spanning 530–800 nm. The photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) of pristine HOF-14 was determined to be 2.8% upon excitation at 420 nm. The relatively low PLQY is attributed to aggregation-induced quenching within the π-stacked pyrene columns. Following photochromic conversion, the PLQY of the green photoswitched state decreased markedly to 0.4%, indicative of pronounced electron-hole separation, as discussed below. Notably, simply wetting dry HOF-14 powder with ultrapure water droplets was sufficient to trigger the same yellow-to-green transition upon illumination (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Movie 1). The process is fully reversible; subsequent heating at 95°C and ethanol displacement restored the green sample to its original yellow state, demonstrating excellent reversibility (Supplementary Fig. 10, Supplementary Movies 2 and 3). We also found that, under ambient conditions at room temperature, the green photoswitched state is gradually and completely revert to the original yellow state within 150 min (Supplementary Fig. 11).

To ascertain the specific role of water, we investigated the photochromic behavior under various atmospheric conditions (air, N2, vacuum, CO2, and O2). In all cases, dry HOF-14 powder remained unresponsive to light (Supplementary Fig. 12). Conversely, the aqueous dispersion of HOF-14 consistently exhibited photochromism across these different atmospheres, confirming that water is the pivotal stimulus for the color transition (Supplementary Fig. 13). Furthermore, control experiments established that neither the wetted molecular building block (H4TNAPy) nor its solution exhibited any photochromic response under identical conditions (Supplementary Fig. 14). These results collectively demonstrate that the highly ordered and porous framework of HOF-14 is essential for conferring this unique water-triggered photochromic property.

To evaluate the reversibility and operational durability of photochromic behavior of HOF-14, we subjected the material to repeated irradiation–heating cycles. Each cycle consisted of lightly moistening the HOF-14 powder, irradiating with visible light for 10 s, and thermally recovering at 95 °C for 3 min. The solid-state UV–vis absorption at 631 nm was tracked after each step. Remarkably, the HOF-14 system exhibited no significant degradation in photochromic amplitude after 50 consecutive cycles (retaining over 90% of its initial response), outperforming most of the reported organic photochromes (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Table 1). To directly visualize the photochromic transition in a crystal, we further grew single crystals of HOF-14 and monitored the process in situ using optical microscopy (Fig. 2e). This exceptional cycling stability underscores the robustness of HOF-14 and its high potential for reliable integration into real-world devices and smart materials.

Confirmation of pyrene radical cation formation

We hypothesized that the distinctive photochromism of HOF-14 originates from the generation of stable pyrene radical cations (Py•+)49 via a light-driven mechanism. To validate this, we employed electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy. Control experiments on HOF-100, PFC-1, and their respective monomers revealed no ESR signals under any condition (dry/aqueous, before/after irradiation), confirming their inability to generate photoinduced radicals (Supplementary Fig. 15). Crucially, while dry HOF-14 powder was also ESR-silent, its aqueous dispersion exhibited a pronounced ESR signal at g = 2.003 upon irradiation by visible light, definitively confirming the formation of Py•+ (Fig. 3a). This was further corroborated by zeta potential measurements, which showed a distinct shift in surface charge from –4.53 to +2.91 mV after illumination, consistent with the generation of positively charged species (Supplementary Fig. 16).

The formation of radicals is an intrinsic property of the highly ordered HOF-14 framework and not its molecular building block, as neither the dry H4TNAPy monomer nor its aqueous solution produced any detectable ESR signal (Fig. 3b). The photochromism reversibility of HOF-14 was confirmed by the disappearance of the ESR signal upon heating or ethanol addition, which concurrently restored the original yellow color. Furthermore, the intensity of the Py•+ ESR signal exhibited a strong dependence on the water content. For HOF-14 dispersions in a water/acetonitrile mixed solution, the signal strength at g = 2.003 and the resultant green coloration intensified progressively with increasing water content (50–100%) under fixed irradiation (10 s, Fig. 3c). Similarly, the ESR intensity grew with irradiation time (0–10 s) across all aqueous dispersions of HOF-14 (Supplementary Fig. 17).

These systematic characterizations provide compelling evidence that the observed yellow-to-green photochromism in HOF-14 is directly correlated with the formation and stabilization of pyrene radical cations within its ordered framework (Fig. 3d), dominated by a water-mediated mechanism as discussed below.

Unveiling water-mediated visible light photochromic mechanism

To elucidate the mechanism of the water-mediated photochromism, we first characterized the water adsorption behavior of HOF-14. The water adsorption isotherm revealed a substantial uptake of 291 mg g−1, indicating hydrophilic pore channels (Fig. 4a). Exposing HOF-14 to a high-humidity environment (approximately 100% RH) yielded a water-adsorbed sample (denoted W-HOF-14). The green state of W-HOF-14 after irradiation by visible light was denoted as W-HOF-14-Green. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of W-HOF-14 showed an additional weight loss between 100 and 168 °C, confirming the presence of bound water (Supplementary Fig. 18). Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy displayed a broad band at 3200–3600 cm−1, corresponding to the O–H stretching vibrations of adsorbed water50 (Supplementary Fig. 19). Solid-state 1H NMR further verified this, showing a new resonance at δ 5.36 ppm51 confirms the presence of bound water (Fig. 4b). We noted that that this peak disappeared upon heating, as result of water evaporation. Crucially, the 13C NMR signal for the carboxyl groups (~172 ppm) broadened significantly in the irradiated W-HOF-14 sample, indicating strong hydrogen-bonding interactions between water and the carboxyl groups of the framework that accompany the color change (Fig. 4c). Heating restored the sharp NMR peak of carboxyl groups and the original yellow color, confirming reversibility.

Upon irradiating W-HOF-14 within 10 s, the color changed rapidly from yellow to green (W-HOF-14-Green). This green state then reverted to its original yellow color after water evaporation under ambient conditions without irradiation. Time-dependent PXRD revealed that this transition involves a reversible shrinkage of crystal cell. The diffraction peaks (at 2θ = 3.78°) of W-HOF-14 (Supplementary Fig. 20) and W-HOF-14-Green (Fig. 4d) shifted to higher angles compared to pristine HOF-14, signifying a contraction of the unit cell (ca. 1.3% contraction). This contraction was fully reversed upon water removal after 40 min of ambient aging, restoring the original lattice parameters and color. Notably, the additional diffraction peak at 2θ = 7.10° also exhibits an analogous high-angle shift followed by restoration, although the magnitude of this shift differs. In contrast, the diffraction peak at 2θ = 10.80° shows no discernible movement (Fig. 4d). This observation supports an anisotropic contraction of the framework, which is likely attributable to the interplay of intricate host–guest interactions and the differential structural flexibility along distinct crystallographic axes. The observed variations in relative peak intensities arise from the adsorption of water molecules within the framework pores. Specifically, the presence of these adsorbed species introduces additional scattering of the incident X-ray beam, leading to an attenuation of the principal diffraction peaks located at 2θ = 3.78° for HOF-14. Upon water desorption, this scattering effect diminishes, and the peak intensities are restored. Collectively, these dynamic intensity changes, along with the accompanying peak shifts, further substantiate the critical role of pore-adsorbed water molecules in governing the photochromic behavior of HOF-14. SEM confirmed the crystal morphology of W-HOF-14-Green remained intact before and after ambient aging (Supplementary Fig. 21).

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations illuminated the atomistic details of water adsorption. Water molecules preferentially accumulated around the carboxylate groups, forming stable hydrogen bonds (Supplementary Figs. 22–24). This is in well line with the 13C NMR results (Fig. 4c). For HOF-14, this adsorption shortened the key intramolecular hydrogen-bond length from 2.539 to 2.432 Å and altered its angle from 177.095° to 169.965° (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 25), well supported by the observed lattice contraction in the PXRD test. While water adsorption also shortened hydrogen bonds length in HOF-100 and PFC-1 (Supplementary Figs. 26–28), only HOF-14 exhibited photochromism.

To understand this disparity, we performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations. In the pristine Py-HOFs, the HOMO and LUMO orbitals were largely co-localized on the pyrene-core fragments (Supplementary Fig. 29). Remarkably, water adsorption induced a complete spatial separation of the HOMO and LUMO exclusively in HOF-14, facilitating efficient electron-hole separation under irradiation and favoring Py•+ formation (Fig. 5c). The calculated oscillator strengths for W-HOF-14 exhibit a distinct red-shift relative to the dry framework (Supplementary Fig. 30), aligning precisely with the experimentally observed shift in its UV–vis absorption spectrum. Notably, a pronounced increase in oscillator strength within the 530–800 nm region emerges upon water adsorption, matching well with the color change from yellow to green upon irradiation. In contrast, HOMO-LUMO overlap remained significant in hydrated HOF-100 and PFC-1 (i.e. W-HOF-100 and W-PFC-1, Fig. 5a, b). This fundamental difference in electronic restructuring was corroborated by photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy. The strong fluorescence of solid HOF-14 was substantially quenched in W-HOF-14, due to the water-induced orbital separation that inhibits radiative recombination (Supplementary Fig. 31c). No such quenching occurred in the other hydrated Py-HOFs (Supplementary Fig. 31a, b).

The thermodynamics of exciton dissociation provided further compelling evidence for the favorable electron-hole separation in W-HOF-14. We performed temperature-dependent PL measurements on the dry and hydrated Py-HOFs to extract the activation energy for exciton dissociation (Ea) via Arrhenius Eq. 152:

For the dry powders (HOF-100, PFC-1, and HOF-14), the PL intensity quenched monotonically with increasing temperature (100–300 K), yielding Ea values of 60, 87, and 130 meV, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 32 and 33). Each value substantially exceeds the thermal energy at room temperature (~25 meV)53, confirming that spontaneous exciton dissociation is energetically unfavorable in the dry state (Supplementary Fig. 33d). This behavior persisted for the hydrated forms of HOF-100 and PFC-1 (i.e. W-HOF-100 and W-PFC-1), where PL continued to quench with temperature increase (280–320 K) (Fig. 5d, e, and Supplementary Fig. 34a, b). In striking contrast, W-HOF-14 exhibited an anomalous PL enhancement within the same temperature range (Fig. 5f). This inversion signifies that the energy barrier for exciton dissociation has fallen below the ambient thermal energy, rendering the charge-separated state energetically favorable—and even lower in energy than the exciton itself (Supplementary Fig. 34c, d)54,55. While the experimental temperature window (280–320 K) prevented precise quantification of Ea due to water freezing constraints, the qualitative trend is unambiguous. This spectroscopic signature provides direct evidence that water adsorption uniquely and dramatically reduces the exciton binding and dissociation energy in HOF-14, thereby enabling the efficient, visible-light-driven electron-hole separation that underpins its photochromism.

To elucidate the stabilization mechanism of the photogenerated radicals, we performed spin density isosurface analysis. The calculated distribution of the unpaired electron within a HOF-14 monomer reveals extensive delocalization across the pyrene core, with significant spin density localized on the eight peripheral carbon atoms (Supplementary Fig. 35). A closer examination via localized orbital locator-π (LOL-π) isosurface analysis indicates that, despite intralayer electron localization arising from hydrogen-bond linkages in the XY-plane, the HOF supports extensive π-electron delocalization along the Z-axis. This is facilitated by the face-to-face stacking of the pyrene columns (Fig. 5g). This pronounced spatial delocalization along the columnar axis provides an effective stabilization pathway for the unpaired electron in the Py•+ species, rationalizing its extended lifetime. This conclusion is further corroborated by single-crystal conductivity measurements, which confirm efficient electron migration specifically along the Z-axis, while migration is inhibited within the XY-plane (Fig. 5h).

Collectively, the photochromic mechanism involves a synergistic sequence: (1) water adsorption triggers a specific lattice contraction via strong hydrogen bonding to carboxylic groups in HOF-14. (2) This structural distortion induces a unique redistribution of the frontier orbitals in HOF-14, spatially separating the HOMO and LUMO. (3) This electronic reconfiguration drastically lowers the exciton binding energy, enabling efficient, visible-light-driven electron-hole separation. (4) The resulting pyrene radical cations are stabilized by extensive delocalization within the one-dimensional π-stacked columns, manifesting as the longstanding green coloration.

Information encryption and anti-counterfeiting

Capitalizing on its unique dual-stimulus photochromism, we demonstrate that HOF-14 serves as an exceptional material for advanced information encryption and anti-counterfeiting (Fig. 6a). This system operates on a stringent “AND” logic-gate principle, requiring the simultaneous presence of water and visible light to reveal hidden information. As a proof of concept, an ethanol solution of HOF-14 was used as an invisible ink to print a pattern (e.g., a “Sun Yat-sen University” logo) onto paper (Fig. 6b). Rapid ethanol evaporation left the pattern completely invisible. Decryption is only activated upon the synergistic application of water and visible-light irradiation, which triggers an immediate and vivid color change to green. The encrypted state can be recovered either by applying ethanol or by mild heating, enabling dynamic, multi-cycle information control. This behavior was formalized using logic gate circuitry. The decryption process functions as an “AND” gate, where the output (green color) is “1” only when both inputs, water (“1”) and light (“1”), are present (Fig. 6c). Conversely, the re-encryption process mimics an “OR” gate, where applying either ethanol or heat (inputs “1”) resets the output to the yellow, encrypted state “0” (Supplementary Fig. 36). To illustrate practical utility, we fabricated a high-security quick response (QR) code label (Fig. 6b). The code remains perfectly concealed under ambient conditions but becomes fully visible and smartphone-scannable only upon exposure to both water and light, creating a robust two-factor authentication system.

Furthermore, we extended this photochromic functionality to the microscale by engineering three-dimensional anti-counterfeiting microstructures. Intricate patterns—including line, grid, and lettering—were fabricated via 3D bioprinting using a hydrogel ink composed of HOF-14 and sodium alginate (SA) (Fig. 6d). These hydrogel architectures retain high water content while maintaining excellent structural integrity. This inherent hydration enables reversible color switching upon visible-light irradiation without the need for external water addition (Fig. 6e). The characteristic of water-and-light-gated photochromism, coupled with its straightforward processability into inks and 3D-printable composites, make HOF-14 a versatile and secure platform for dynamic information protection across macroscopic labels and microscopic features.

HOF-14-integrated textiles for adaptive camouflage

The dynamic, stimulus-responsive color change of photochromic materials presents a compelling opportunity for adaptive camouflage. We demonstrate this potential by engineering smart textiles through the direct integration of HOF-14. A uniform, yellow coating was achieved by drop-casting an ethanolic HOF-14 solution onto fabric fibers, followed by solvent evaporation (Fig. 7a). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed the uniform localization of HOF-14 nanorod onto the fabric fibers (Supplementary Fig. 37).

To assess versatility and recyclability, HOF-14 was successfully deposited onto various textiles including polypropylene, terylene, polyamide, and silk. All coated fabrics exhibited robust photochromic behavior, transitioning from yellow to dark green within 10 s of irradiation when pre-misted with water (Fig. 7b). Photostability tests confirmed that the HOF-14-coated cotton maintains its yellow state indefinitely when dry and its green state for over 120 min when moistened and irradiated (Supplementary Figs. 38 and 39). The coatings also demonstrated excellent mechanical robustness (Supplementary Fig. 40), retaining their photochromic function after repeated bending and stretching.

The close-loop recyclability and regeneration of the HOF-14-based smart textile are facilitated by the dynamic hydrogen-bonding network within the HOF. Specifically, HOF-14 nanorods integrated on the cotton fabric can be dissolved in DMF, separated, and rapidly recrystallized upon the addition of ethanol within 5 min (Fig. 7c). The recycled HOF-14 exhibits PXRD patterns and SEM morphologies that are highly consistent with those of the pristine sample, indicating that the framework structure and morphology are well preserved after repeated recycling cycles (Supplementary Figs. 41 and 42). The recovered HOF-14 can then be reused in subsequent rounds of smart-textile fabrication. We further evaluated the recyclability over three consecutive cycles (Fig. 7d). Notably, the HOF-14-coated cotton fabric retained excellent photochromic performance after repeated recycling, demonstrating the robustness and reproducibility of this regenerative smart-textile platform.

We validated the practical camouflage utility in a realistic outdoor scenario. A leaf-shaped HOF-14-functionalized textile, initially yellow against green foliage under dry conditions, transitioned to a matching green hue within 2.5 min of water misting under ambient sunlight (light intensity: 500–1000 W m−2; Sun-Yat-sen University, Guangzhou University Town, January 8, 2025, 8:00–10:00 a.m.). This rapid chromatic response enabled effective concealment amidst vegetation (Fig. 7e, f and Supplementary Fig. 43). While the yellow color was rapidly restored (ca. 30 s) upon spraying with ethanol. Furthermore, we fabricated a functional photochromic glove (Fig. 7g and Supplementary Fig. 44). Upon moistening and exposure to ambient sunlight, the glove underwent a rapid chromatic transition from yellow to green, achieving a dark green hue comparable to that of the surrounding vegetation within 2.5 min. The original yellow coloration was subsequently restored within approximately 1 min upon ethanol spraying, demonstrating a fully reversible and user-controllable camouflage cycle.

Collectively, the HOF-based smart fabrics demonstrate fast and fully reversible color switching, activated solely by the benign stimuli of water and sunlight. This enables on-site visualization of encrypted information without the need for any auxiliary optical device. We believe that this inherent accessibility confers a tangible practical advantage in real-world deployment scenarios.

Discussion

In summary, by harnessing flexible H-bond networks and pyrene-stacked columns in a HOF, we have reported a photochromic system activated by the synergistic combination of water and visible light. This work overcomes a central limitation in photochromic materials—the reliance on single stimuli of high-energy and harmful UV irradiation—by utilizing water and visible light as dual benign and universal triggers. Through a comprehensive mechanistic investigation, we unveiled that water adsorption induces a precise lattice contraction, which in turn reconfigures the intralayer electronic structure. This unique electronic redistribution substantially reduces the exciton binding energy, facilitating efficient visible-light-driven electron-hole separation and the subsequent formation of long-lived pyrene radical cations stabilized by the robust, π-stacked pyrene columns. Beyond introducing a novel paradigm for environmentally benign and user-friendly photochromism, this study fundamentally advances the understanding of stimulus–structure–property relationships in dynamic hydrogen-bonded crystals. Future endeavours will centre on the substituent engineering of pyrene-based monomers to enhance the stability of the corresponding radical species, alongside the development of cost-effective, solvent-free, and scalable synthetic routes to facilitate industrial-scale production. Moreover, the construction of intelligent photochromic systems capable of chameleon-like, multicolour-tunable, and adaptive responses through precise molecular engineering strategies represents a particularly compelling direction for future exploration.

Methods

Synthesis of Py-HOFs microcrystals

Py-HOFs microcrystals were synthesized according to the previously reported method with some modification40,41,47. For HOF-100 and HOF-14, 20 mg of H4TCPy and H4TNAPy were dissolved in 1 and 6 mL of DMF, respectively, and the solutions were heated at 120 °C for 30 min. After cooling to room temperature, 16 mL of acetone and ethanol were added, respectively. The mixtures were further stirred for 12 h, affording yellow precipitates, which were collected, washed three times with ethanol, and finally vacuum-dried at room temperature to yield yellow powders.

For PFC-1, 20 mg of H4TBAPy was dissolved in 3 mL of DMF. Then, 12 mL of methanol was added under stirring. The mixtures were further stirred for 12 h, affording yellow precipitates, which were collected, washed three times with ethanol, and finally vacuum-dried at room temperature to yield yellow powders.

Synthesis of HOF-14 single crystal

H4TNAPy (5 mg) was dissolved in DMF (1 mL) in a vial. This vial was subsequently placed inside a larger vial containing anhydrous ethanol (4 mL). The sealed system was allowed to stand undisturbed in the dark at room temperature for 5 days, yielding yellow HOF-14 single crystals.

Photochromic behavior of HOF-14

To evaluate the photochromic response, HOF-14 was either dispersed in water or wetted with a small amount of water and then irradiated under a sunlight-simulated xenon lamp (λ > 420 nm, 1000 W m−2) equipped with an AM 1.5 G spectral filter and a 420 nm long-pass cutoff filter. The color of the sample changed from yellow to green under light and moisture. Reversibility was assessed by heating the sample at 95 °C for 3 min, upon which the color reverted from green to yellow.

Electron spin resonance (ESR) measurements

For ESR experiments, Py-HOFs was dissolved in water (0.01 mg µL−1) and loaded into 1 mm glass capillaries. The ESR spectra were recorded with a microwave power of 1 mW and a magnetic field range of 333–340 mT. Samples were irradiated under a xenon lamp for 1 min before testing. Measurements for the monomers of H4TCPy, H4TBAPy, and H4TNAPy were carried out under the same conditions.

Information encryption and anti-counterfeiting experiments

To demonstrate anti-counterfeiting applications, HOF-14 was dispersed in anhydrous ethanol at an optimized concentration (5 mg mL−1) to formulate a stable invisible ink. This ink was then applied using custom stamps to fabricate quick response (QR) codes, patterns, and logos onto standard yellow cellulose paper. Upon rapid evaporation of ethanol at ambient conditions, the printed information became visually indiscernible against the yellow background, effectively entering a hidden state.

Decryption was achieved by selectively spraying the area with water (ca. 0.5–1.0 mL) using a spray bottle, followed by light irradiation with a sunlight-simulated xenon lamp (1000 W m−2), which triggered an immediate and vivid color change to green within 10 s, thereby revealing the concealed pattern. The information could be subsequently erased and re-hidden through either spraying ethanol (ca. 0.5–1.0 mL) with a spray bottle or heating (95 °C, 3 min), rendering the original yellow state.

3D print of HOF-14 hydrogel micropattern

HOF-14 hydrogel was prepared by dissolving 0.1 g sodium alginate in 1.8 mL water under stirring, followed by the addition of 200 µL calcium chloride solution (0.25 mg mL−1). After thorough mixing, 50 µL of HOF-14 solution (0.2 g mL−1) was added to the gel mixture. The homogeneous gel was loaded into a syringe of a 3D bioprinter and used for printing micro-patterns with photochromic functionality.

Preparation of photochromic fabrics for adaptive camouflage

Five milliliters of HOF-14 ethanol solution (10 mg mL−1) was drop-cast in batches onto various 3.0 cm × 5.0 cm textile substrates (cotton, polypropylene, terylene, polyamide, and silk). After complete ethanol evaporation (typically within 3 min upon heating at 95 °C), yellow fabrics with photochromic properties were obtained. Their response was evaluated by spraying water (ca. 1.0–2.0 mL) using a spray bottle onto the fabric followed by irradiating with a sunlight-simulated xenon lamp (1000 W m−2, 10 s), with the resulting color change visually observed and recorded. The yellow color was rapidly restored upon spraying with ethanol (ca. 1.0–2.0 mL).

The preparation and color-change procedures for the leaf-shaped HOF-14-functionalized textiles and photochromic glove were identical to those described above, except that ambient sunlight was used as the irradiation source. Outdoor sunlight irradiation was performed at 8:00–10:00 a.m. on January 8, 2025, in Sun-Yat-sen University, Guangzhou University Town (Panyu District, Guangzhou, China) under clear skies (light intensity: 500–1000 W m−2).

Close-loop recycle of HOF-14 photochromic fabrics

To demonstrate the recyclability of the smart textile, HOF-14-nanorod-loaded cotton fabric was immersed in DMF (6 mL), and the HOF-14 nanorod was dissolved via simple ultrasonication. The resulting solution was then mixed with anhydrous ethanol (20 mL) under stirring for 5 min, triggering recrystallization of HOF-14. The regenerated material was collected by centrifugation, washed with ethanol (three times), and reloaded onto cotton fabric to complete a closed-loop fabrication cycle for sustainable smart textiles.

Theoretical calculations

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were first performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)56 to obtain the equilibrium configurations of the Py-HOFs. In addition, the TraPPE force field57 was employed to investigate the equilibrium structures of the three Py-HOFs under different water contents. The optimized structures were subsequently used for further electronic-structure calculations.

All time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) calculations were carried out using the CP2K program58,59. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) was applied60. Geometry optimizations were conducted using the DZVP-MOLOPT-SR-GTH basis set, whereas single-point energy and excited-state calculations were performed with the TZVP-MOLOPT-GTH basis set61. Core electrons were treated using the Goedecker–Teter–Hutter (GTH) pseudopotentials62, and Grimme’s D3 dispersion correction was included to account for van der Waals interactions.

Based on the optimized structures, we further analyzed the frontier molecular orbitals (HOMO and LUMO), oscillator strengths, electron and hole distributions, charge difference density (CDD), and transition density matrix (Transdens). All electron-density visualizations and orbital analyses were conducted using the Multiwfn 3.8 program63,64.
