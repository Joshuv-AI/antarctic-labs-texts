A systematic review and meta-analysis of psychological burden in family caregivers of patients with brain tumors
Authors: Haiyu Chen, Tianxiang Zou, Ziwei Teng, Yue Qin, Yizhi Deng, Renrong Wu, Jing Huang, Qiong Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Family caregivers of brain tumor patients often experience severe psychological problems, with a high prevalence of mental health issues posing a growing challenge in clinical practice. However, the psychological burden borne by these caregivers remains underrecognized. This study aimed to systematically assess the psychological impact on family caregivers of brain tumor patients. Based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-P) 2020 guidelines, the systematic literature search was conducted across multiple databases (PubMed, PsycINFO, Cochrane Library, MEDLINE, and EMBASE) for studies published up to February 2022. Effect sizes were calculated using random-effects models. The prevalence rates of depression, anxiety, and distress among brain tumor family caregivers were estimated by screening scales and analyzed using meta-analysis. This meta-analysis synthesized data from 15 studies encompassing a total of 974 family caregivers. The pooled prevalence of depression among the caregivers was 37.15% (95% confidence interval (CI) 22.00–52.30%). Additionally, the estimated prevalence of anxiety among the overall sample was 49.47% (95% CI 35.81–63.12%), while 64% (95% CI 54–74%) reported experiencing distress burden. Family caregivers of brain tumor patients exhibit a considerable prevalence of depression, anxiety, and distress. These findings highlight the critical need to assess the psychological burden imposed by brain tumors-particularly malignant cases-on both patients and their caregivers. Such assessment is essential for developing targeted interventions to enhance coping mechanisms and improve mental health support. Future research should further elucidate caregivers’ specific psychological challenges to facilitate the implementation of support systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23331-1.

Introduction

More than 65 million American adults serve as family caregivers for loved ones with chronic diseases, disabilities, or age-related conditions, devoting an average of 27 h per week to these duties1. In cases where patients require around-the-clock care, the demands intensify significantly, exacerbating the physical, emotional, and psychological strain on caregivers2. This sustained burden often makes it challenging for caregivers to maintain their own physical and mental health.

Although brain tumors represent a relatively small proportion of overall cancer incidence, they nonetheless account for over 30 million new cases worldwide annually3. Approximately two-thirds of these patients face poor prognoses, often accompanied by severe neurological dysfunction3. The unique characteristics of central nervous system tumors, including their anatomical location, growth patterns, and associated neuroendocrine disturbances, frequently lead to profound and unpredictable alterations in neurological function4. Researches indicate that brain tumors may exacerbate pain perception and emotional instability, potentially through dysregulation of neurobiological pathways involving key neurotransmitter systems5. These effects are frequently exacerbated by neurosurgical interventions, which can lead to lasting changes in personality and behavior6,7. Consequently, individuals with brain tumors often confront a spectrum of complex psychological challenges, ranging from depression and apathy to heightened aggression and even profound personality changes. These neuropsychiatric sequelae significantly impact not only patients’ quality of life but also impose considerable psychological burden on their caregivers8. In Western healthcare systems, where standard oncology practice prioritizes early hospital discharge and outpatient management, the provision of long-term support for brain tumor patients predominantly falls upon informal care networks, particularly family members and friends serving as primary caregivers9. As disease progression leads to worsening clinical manifestations, brain tumor patients experience a gradual decline in functional independence, necessitating increasing reliance on informal caregivers for comprehensive support10. This support extends across multiple domains, including activities of daily living, social interaction, emotional regulation, spiritual sustenance, and financial support. As a result, compared to caregivers of patients with non-neurological malignancies, those caring for brain tumor patients encounter significantly greater psychosocial and physiological challenges, leading to a significant decline in their quality of life11. A study has shown that family caregivers of primary malignant brain tumor patients endure substantial psychological distress, including severe depression, anxiety, and excessive caregiving burdens12. Importantly, family caregivers often exhibit higher stress levels than the patients themselves, and this distress typically follows an escalating pattern over time rather than diminishing7,13,14.

Family caregivers of malignant brain tumor patients are required to provide support and assistance in cancer-related issues, as well as for the neurocognitive and neuropsychological issues faced by the care recipients8. However, the complexity of this caregiving burden often leads caregivers to overlook their own mental well-being, resulting in a dual adverse effect: it compromises their psychological and physical health while simultaneously diminishing their capacity to provide effective care, which may in turn negatively impact the patient’s psychological state10. Existing research demonstrates a significant association between caregivers’ emotional well-being and patients’ clinical outcomes, suggesting that interventions aimed at alleviating caregivers’ psychological distress could enhance care quality and, consequently, improve patients’ overall functioning15. While psychological assessment of patients has become standard practice in clinical settings, routine mental health screening for their family caregivers remains notably absent10. This disparity in clinical focus further undermines the stability of familial caregiving networks. In contrast to the psychological challenges faced by brain tumor patients, the mental health and quality of life of their family caregivers remain understudied and inadequately addressed16. A deeper understanding of this psychological burden and the development of targeted support systems for caregivers are pressing needs in both clinical practice and research17. While several studies have examined the psychological well-being of brain tumor family caregivers, there remains a notable gap in the systematic synthesis of the prevalence of their mental health issues. Such a synthesis could enhance clinical awareness of family caregivers’ psychological needs and facilitate the development of evidence-based interventions to mitigate these challenges.

This study aimed to provide a reliable assessment of the prevalence of depression, anxiety, and psychological distress among informal caregivers of brain tumor patients. Through a systematic review and meta-analysis, we examined 15 observational studies that investigated psychological comorbidities in this population.

Methods

Systematic review protocol

The protocol used for the systematic review and meta-analysis was based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-P) 2020 guidelines (Supplement-PRISMA 2020 Checklist)18. Additionally, this review has been registered in PROSPERO, which is an international and professional database for registering systematic reviews, and the registration ID is CRD42022319411.

Search strategy and inclusion criteria

We searched PubMed, PsycINFO, Cochrane Library, MEDLINE and EMBASE for all related studies published in English, from inception up to February 2022. We also searched the references of the relevant articles to identify additional eligible studies19. Furthermore, reviews and meta-analyses were also checked and searched to identify studies that may have been missed in the former literature searches. Family caregivers in this study were identified as the individual who provided the most care for the care recipients, and also required to be nonprofessional, non-paid carers over 18 years old. The search keywords were derived from the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) database. Detailed search terms are provided in the supplementary materials. In our present study, JH, ZT and TZ performed a systematic review of all potential studies and extracted the primary data. With EndNote, we conducted a thorough screening of the literature, assessing each study based on the established eligibility criteria.

The studies were included if: (1) they reported the prevalence of depression, anxiety, or distress in informal caregivers of patients with brain tumors (primary or metastatic) and (2) screening scales that evaluated depression, anxiety, or distress were used in the analysis. We excluded studies without complete reports; studies using pooled means or other indirect estimates for assessing the prevalence of psychological problems; case reports, reviews, or opinions; and studies that lacked a differentiation of brain tumor data from data on mixed cancer samples.

Data extraction

The following results were extracted from all included studies: first author, year, country, study design, clinical characteristics of the study population (number of caregivers, percentage of female caregivers, mean age of caregivers, education levels, and relationship with the patients), and assessment tools used to evaluate depression, anxiety, or distress along with their respective findings. In cases where the same sample was used in multiple studies, we only included data from the study providing the most detailed information about participants, such as education level, relationship with the patients, and other health problems of caregivers. The methodological quality of the selection, comparability, and outcome of the included studies were assessed by the Newcastle-Ottawa scale20.

Data analysis

The statistical heterogeneity among the included studies was tested using the χ2 test for Cochran’s Q statistic and then by calculating the I2 value (I2 ≦ 25% indicates low heterogeneity; 25%≧I2 ≧ 50% indicates moderate heterogeneity; and ≧ 75% indicates high heterogeneity)21. We used Stata software (version 12.1; Stata Corp, College Station, TX) to analyze all the results22. The prevalence estimates of depression, anxiety, and distress were calculated by random-effects models because of the high heterogeneity observed23. Forest plots were also constructed. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant in all analyses. To assess result stability, we performed sensitivity analyses by systematically excluding individual studies and recalculating pooled prevalence estimates (with 95% CIs) and heterogeneity measures (Tau²/I²) for depression, anxiety, and distress.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The initial queries identified 882 potential articles from the databases included: 192 from PUBMED, 77 from Cochrane Library, 70 from PsycINFO, 103 from MEDLINE, and 440 from EMBASE (Fig. 1). After removing 329 duplicates, we screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining 553 studies and excluded 257 that did not meet the selection criteria. A full-text review of the 300 eligible studies led to the exclusion of 285 records for specific reasons: 141 studies excluded brain tumor patient caregivers, failed to differentiate brain tumor data from mixed cancer samples or the same sample was used in multiple studies24,25; 28 studies focused exclusively on patients rather than caregivers; 62 studies lacked relevant outcome measures for further analysis; 42 studies did not employ valid psychological assessments or report related outcomes;, 3 studies were excluded due to unavailability of full texts; and 9 were reviews or meta-analyses and thus ineligible for inclusion. Finally, the meta-analysis was conducted on data from 15 eligible studies published between 2006 and 2022 that reported the psychological burden affecting family caregivers of brain tumor patients. Among the 15 included studies, the majority were conducted in Europe and North America, with 6 studies from Germany, 3 each from the United States and Australia, 2 from Italy, and 1 from South Korea (Table 1). Methodologically, cross-sectional designs predominated (n = 11), followed by longitudinal (n = 3) and prospective (n = 1) studies.

Characteristics of caregivers of brain tumor patients

The characteristics of family caregivers and care recipients were presented in Table 1. Sample sizes of caregivers of brain tumor patients varied from 20 to 133, with a mean of 65. The pooled mean age of caregivers across studies was 52.60 years (range: 41.72–67.20), calculated based on a sample-size-weighted average of 804 participants from all studies that provided age data. Female caregivers predominated, constituting 71.3% of the total sample. While most caregivers were spouses or partners, the sample also included adult children, parents, and other family members of the patients.

Most studies focused on caregivers of patients with primary brain tumors, including glioblastoma, astrocytoma, oligodendroglioma, and other types of cancer. Across the included research, 97.7% of care recipients had primary brain tumors, while only one study reported a small subset (2.3%) with brain metastases26. Most care recipients had advanced-stage disease or malignant tumors. Across the included studies, caregiver psychological burden was assessed at varying time points, ranging from initial diagnosis to over a decade post-diagnosis. Notably, two of the included studies continued to assess the psychological burden in caregivers after the death of the care-receiving patient27,28.

Depression

Among the studies that assessed depression prevalence, 5 used the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS-D)15,28–31, 3 used the shortened 10-item form of the Center of Epidemiologic Studies-Depression scale (CES-D-S)24,32,33, 1 study used the depression subscale of the Symptom Checklist 90-Revised (SCL-90-R), and 1 used the family caregiver interview questionnaire27. The pooled prevalence of depression among brain tumor caregivers was 37.15% (95% confidence interval (CI) 22.00%–52.30%) in the overall sample (Fig. 2). Significant heterogeneity among the studies was observed in the analysis of depression (I2 = 95.3%, P < 0.01) prevalence. In addition, sensitivity analysis showed stable estimates (31.5–40.3%), with the exclusion study of Alessandra Petruzzi yielding the lowest prevalence (31.5%; 95% CI 20.5–42.6) and reduced heterogeneity (I² = 89.9%) (Table S1). Other exclusions maintained high heterogeneity (I²>94%) with prevalence clustering at 35.4–40.3%, supporting robust central estimates despite persistent heterogeneity.

Anxiety

Studies assessed anxiety prevalence using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS-A)15,28–31or a midpoint score of 8 on the tension-anxiety subscale of the Profile of Mood States (POMS)24,32. The estimated prevalence of anxiety among brain tumor caregivers was 49.47% (95% CI 35.81%–63.12%) (Fig. 3). Significant heterogeneity among the studies was observed in the analysis of anxiety (I2 = 90.2%, P < 0.01) prevalence. The sensitivity analysis revealed anxiety prevalence estimates ranging from 44.9% (95% CI 32.7–57.1) to 55.2% (95% CI 44.4–66.0) (Table S2). Heterogeneity remained substantial (I²=82.6–91.8%). Results demonstrated moderate variability but overall robustness of pooled estimates.

Distress

Six studies assessed distress prevalence using the Distress Thermometer (DT)13,14,26,30,34,35. 64% (95% CI 54%–74%) brain tumor caregivers reported perceiving distress (Fig. 4). Significant heterogeneity among the studies was observed in the analysis of distress (I2 = 79.3%, P < 0.05) prevalence. The sensitivity analysis demonstrated stable distress prevalence estimates ranging from 65.4% (95% CI 56.0–74.8) to 71.0% (95% CI 62.4–79.6), with overlapping confidence intervals across all exclusions. Heterogeneity remained moderate (I²=64.7–73.7%) (Table S3).

Assessment of methodological quality

Based on the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale, all 15 included studies achieved scores ≥ 7 points, indicating satisfactory methodological quality (Table S4).

Discussion

Summary of main findings

Although brain tumors represent only 1.4% of all cancers, according to the cancer statistics of 2018, their high malignancy and poor prognosis make them one of the most devastating cancer types36. Beyond the physical burden of the disease, brain tumor patients often suffer from behavior impairment and cognitive dysfunction, resulting in a greater reliance on caregiver support compared to other cancer populations37. Given these challenges, this analysis aimed to assess psychological problems experienced by caregivers of brain tumor patients. In this meta-analysis, we summarized the prevalence of depression, anxiety, and distress in brain tumor patients’ caregivers and found a high burden of psychological problems in this group.

Our study revealed a significantly higher proportion of female family caregivers, a finding consistent with prior research on informal caregivers for dementia (81.7% female caregivers)38. Accumulating evidence highlights the substantial impact of gender differences on caregivers’ mental health outcomes. For instance, Kim et al.39 observed that male caregivers involved in cancer care tend to exhibit greater self-esteem and lower stress, leading to reduced psychological distress and better overall mental health functioning. Conversely, multiple studies have consistently demonstrated that female caregivers experience a heavier caregiving burden and are at a significantly higher risk of developing moderate-to-severe anxiety and depressive symptoms40–42. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating gender-specific considerations when developing psychosocial support interventions for family caregivers to ensure more tailored and effective assistance.

There were some differences in the prevalence of depression among caregivers compared with other cancer types. A cross-sectional study conducted in northern Taiwan investigating the psychological condition of lung cancer patients’ caregivers prior to initial treatment found that 32.1% of family caregivers were at risk of depression43. Similarly, a longitudinal study focusing on breast cancer patients’ caregivers reported a depression prevalence of 30%44. From these two studies, the prevalence of depression in lung cancer and breast cancer caregivers is slightly lower than that observed among brain tumor caregivers, which may be related to the differences in the prognosis and progression of the cancer. The comparatively higher depression rates among brain tumor caregivers underscore the critical need for targeted mental health support for this specific caregiver population.

Our findings suggest that compared with the general population, caregivers of intracranial tumor patients have a much higher prevalence of depression, anxiety, and distress45–47. Notably, when compared to our previous findings showing depression in 21.7% of brain tumor patients and distress in 38.2%, family caregivers exhibited even greater psychological burden. These results align with existing research documenting the disproportionate mental health impact on neuro-oncology family caregivers7,13,14, highlighting the critical need for targeted psychosocial support interventions for this population. The psychological well-being of family caregivers significantly influences care quality, with excessive burden potentially resulting in suboptimal patient recovery or premature institutionalization, consequently escalating healthcare expenditures. Without adequate support systems, this may precipitate a complete breakdown of informal caregiving networks. To mitigate these risks, a comprehensive support framework encompassing psychological counselling and financial assistance must be implemented. Such measures would serve the dual purpose of safeguarding caregiver mental health while maintaining optimal patient outcomes, ultimately fostering a sustainable care ecosystem.

Our systematic analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity across the included studies. This observed heterogeneity likely stems from several methodological variations. Firstly, the high heterogeneity could partly be explained by the different types of screening tools in different studies. It has been shown that studies using the self-rating Family Caregiver Interview Questionnaire scale reported a much higher prevalence of depression than did studies using clinician-conducted diagnostic interviews with screening methods such as HADS-D or CES-D-SF24,27,29–31. Secondly, the different cut-off scores in the included studies could also greatly affect the results. Studies that used the same screening assessment tool and cut-off scores obtained similar results. Three studies that assessed the distress level in caregivers obtained highly consistent results of 73.1%, 78.1%, and 76%26,30,34. In addition, substantial variability in follow-up durations-ranging from immediate post-diagnosis to more than ten years-emerged as another key source of heterogeneity. Existing literature presents mixed findings regarding symptom progression in these caregivers. One study reports a significant increase in distress levels during disease relapse34, whereas another indicates that symptoms of depression and anxiety remain relatively stable during the first six months following diagnosis14. Notably, emerging evidence indicates a potential gradual amelioration of anxiety symptoms commencing approximately six months post-diagnosis48.

According to our findings, caregivers of brain tumor patients are more likely to develop anxiety than depression, which is consistent with previous findings29,31. Among two similar studies that used HADS-D to assess depression symptoms, the study that included care recipients who were all in an advanced state of the disease reported a much higher prevalence of depression among caregivers than the study in which almost half of all care recipients had benign brain tumors29,30. This could be because caregivers of malignant brain tumor patients report higher physical and psychological burdens, which often parallel the progression of the disease29,37,49. Furthermore, among all the studies assessing depression, the prevalence of depression after bereavement was the highest24,27–31. Taken together, these findings indicate that the psychological problems of caregivers can be influenced by multiple and highly varying individual factors.

Limitations

There are some limitations to the studies included in our analysis. The heterogeneity among the different studies was large, which can partly be explained by the varying tumor types, screening methods, cut-off scores, study design, sample size, country, and publication year among the studies19,50–52. The symptoms and health status of the care recipient play a crucial role in the prevalence of psychological burden among caregivers. However, the studies performed included caregivers of patients with different brain tumor types, and variable clinical features and outcomes, making it difficult to analyze the contribution of a specific tumor type to the prevalence of psychological problems, as the overall results may depend largely on the effect of the most highly prevalent tumor type in the study. Measurement of depression, anxiety and distress by a single screening tool is common in different studies. Group differences and follow-up psychological screening haven’t been reported in most studies. Further, the studies included also differed in the time point of examination relative to the time at which the care recipient received their diagnosis, which may also affect the results. More studies that assess psychological problems during the follow-up period are necessary to obtain more reliable information, which can be helpful for patient prognosis and care. Moreover, detailed caregiver information-such as the relationship with the patient, education level, employment, or comorbidities-is important because physiological and psychosocial factors are known to affect caregivers’ psychological problems; however, this information was missing from some studies53. To overcome these limitations, a multi-dimensional assessment of these factors and findings obtained using different monitoring tools is required, which can provide meaningful information that will enable us to identify the psychological burden in caregivers and to implement interventions for psychological management54–57.

Conclusions

A high prevalence of depression, anxiety, and distress among brain tumor caregivers was found in our study, highlighting the need to understand the psychological impact of brain tumors on patients and caregivers, especially when the patient has a malignant tumor. Healthcare providers should implement comprehensive support measures by conducting regular mental health screenings that account for gender-specific manifestations of distress in family caregivers. These efforts should include targeted professional interventions for high-risk individuals and clear communication about available support resources, such as counselling services. Additionally, family caregiving feasibility assessments should be systematically integrated into treatment planning processes to ensure both patient and caregiver needs are adequately addressed.

Relevance for clinical practice

We conducted a meta-analysis of 15 studies highlighting the key finding of high rates of depression, anxiety, and distress among brain tumor caregivers. The study will help clinical professionals understand and focus on this psychological burden for caregivers of brain tumor patients. It may also link this article to the current state of the field to provide appropriate mental health measures for caregivers and facilitate the development of solutions to quality of life and mental health issues for brain tumor caregivers.
