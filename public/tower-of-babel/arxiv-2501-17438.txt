Unfitted finite element interpolated neural networks
Wei Li 1, Alberto F. Martín 2, Santiago Badia 1,∗

Abstract
. We present a novel approach that integrates unfitted finite element methods and neural networks to approximate partial differential equations on complex geometries. Easy-to-generate background meshes (e.g., a simple Cartesian mesh) that cut the domain boundary (i.e., they do not conform to it) are used to build suitable trial and test finite element spaces. The method seeks a neural network that, when interpolated onto the trial space, minimises a discrete norm of the weak residual functional on the test space associated to the equation. As with unfitted finite elements, essential boundary conditions are weakly imposed by Nitsche’s method. The method is robust to variations in Nitsche coefficient values, and to small cut cells. We experimentally demonstrate the method’s effectiveness in solving both forward and inverse problems across various 2D and 3D complex geometries, including those defined by implicit level-set functions and explicit stereolithography meshes. For forward problems with smooth analytical solutions, the trained neural networks achieve several orders of magnitude smaller H 1 H^{1} errors compared to their interpolation counterparts. These interpolations also maintain expected h h - and p p -convergence rates. Using the same amount of training points, the method is faster than standard PINNs (on both GPU and CPU architectures) while achieving similar or superior accuracy. Moreover, using a discrete dual norm of the residual (achieved by cut cell stabilisation) remarkably accelerates neural network training and further enhances robustness to the choice of Nitsche coefficient values. The experiments also show the method’s high accuracy and reliability in solving inverse problems, even with incomplete observations.



1.
Introduction

\Acp

pde are powerful mathematical tools for modelling a wide range of physical phenomena, including heat transfer, fluid dynamics, and electromagnetics. Since obtaining closed-form analytic solutions to these equations is impractical (if not unfeasible) in most cases, numerical methods like the
fem
are often employed to compute approximate solutions instead. This method makes use of piecewise polynomial spaces on a partition of the computational domain to approximate the solution of the
pde
(
pde
). Its many advantages, such as a solid mathematical foundation
[
1
]
, the capability to handle complex geometries, and the ease of imposing boundary conditions, render
fem
a widely adopted approach for computationally solving
pde
.

Problems posed on complex and/or evolving geometries frequently arise in a wide range of practical applications,
such as those modelled by free-boundary problems.
The use of unstructured mesh generators to generate body-fitted meshes that conform to such geometries is often impractical, challenging, time-consuming, and requiring human intervention in many cases.
Unfitted
fem
, also known as embedded or immersed boundary
fem
, are especially compelling in these scenarios. The differential equation at hand is discretised by embedding the computational domain in an easy-to-generate background mesh (e.g., a simple Cartesian mesh) that does not necessarily conform to its geometrical boundary, thus drastically reducing the geometrical constraints imposed on the meshes to be used for discretisation.
However, unfitted
fem
introduce their own numerical challenges,
such as the small cut cell problem, where arbitrarily
small intersections between the background mesh and the physical domain can lead to numerical instability (i.e., arbitrarily ill-conditioning of the system resulting from discretisation).
We refer the reader to
[
2
]
for a comprehensive and updated review of unfitted
fem
.
Two prominent unfitted
fem
, that we leverage in this work, Cut
fem

[
3
]
and
agfem
(
agfem
)
[
4
]
, address this issue in different ways: Cut
fem
introduces a ghost penalty term
[
5
]
to stabilise the system, while
agfem
eliminates ill-posed cells through aggregation techniques and discrete extension operators. Cut
fem
and
agfem
have been successfully applied to a wide range of
pde
on complex geometries, e.g., interface problems
[
6
]
, fluid-structure interaction
[
7
]
, and linear elasticity
[
8
]
.

Recently,
nn
have rapidly gained popularity in the computational science community for approximating solutions to
pde
. The main idea is that one seeks an approximation to the solution of a
pde
from a trial finite-dimensional
nonlinear manifold
(e.g., a neural network) as opposed to a finite-dimensional linear space as in
fem
.

\Acp
pinn
[
9
]
are a notable approach for their flexibility and ease of implementation. In this method, the
nn
approximates the solution by minimising the strong
pde
residual at randomly sampled collocation points within the computational domain. An additional penalty term in the loss function evaluated at a set of randomly sampled
boundary
collocation points is typically used to weakly impose essential boundary conditions.

\Acp
pinn have demonstrated relative success when applied to a variety of
pde
-constrained problems, including fractional
pde

[
10
]
, fluid mechanics
[
11
]
, and inverse problems
[
12
]
.
Building on a similar concept,
vpinn

[
13
]
, which use the weak
pde
residual as the loss function, have been proposed. With
hp
-refinement capability,
hp
-
vpinn
are often more efficient than
pinn
when
pde
solutions feature singularities and steep gradients (see, e.g.,
[
13
, Figs. 13 and 14]
). One of the main issues in
vpinn
and
pinn
(apart from the imposition of essential boundary conditions) is the difficulties associated to the accurate integration of
nn
and their derivatives.

To overcome the aforementioned difficulties of
vpinn
and
pinn
, the combination of
nn
with
fe
has gained significant attention recently. The authors in
[
14
]
introduced the
ivpinn
(
ivpinn
) method, which interpolates
nn
onto high-order
fe
spaces to address the inexact integration issues found in
vpinn
. When the same evaluation points for
nn
are used,
ivpinn
showed improved accuracy compared to
vpinn
.
Based on the same interpolation idea,
feinn

[
15
]
employ the standard FE offset function to overcome challenges in imposing essential boundary conditions in
pinn
-like methods. Furthermore,
feinn
use a discrete dual norm of the
pde
residual as the loss function, which can be interpreted as a means to precondition the loss to accelerate training convergence and improve accuracy. Similar techniques have been proposed for
vpinn
in
[
16
]
.

\Acp
feinn can easily adapt to inverse problems by adding a data misfit term into the loss function
[
15
]
. Another
fe
-based
nn
approach for inverse problems is discussed in
[
17
]
.
When paired with adaptive mesh refinement,
feinn
can handle
pde
with localised features
[
18
]
. With compatible
fe
, they can accurately solve
pde
with weak solutions in
H(\textbf{curl})
or
H(\textbf{div})
spaces
[
19
]
, covering the full range of spaces in the de Rham complex.
Another notable approach combining
nn
and
fe
is the
hidenn
(
hidenn
)
[
20
]
, where
nn
weights and biases are functions of the nodal coordinates in
fe
spaces. This design enables
hidenn
to accomplish
r
-adaptivity during training and
h
-adaptivity by increasing the number of neurons.
The
fenni
(
fenni
) framework
[
21
]
further improves the efficiency and robustness of
hidenn
training by using a multigrid training strategy. Besides, combining with
pgd
(
pgd
),
fenni
-
pgd

[
22
]
offers a flexible and interpretable solution for real-time surrogate modelling.
In addition to
fem
,
nn
have been combined with other numerical methods, such as finite difference methods
[
23
]
.

Several studies in the literature explore the use of
nn
to solve
pde
on complex geometries. PhyGeoNet
[
24
]
addresses this challenge by mapping the irregular physical domain to a regular reference domain, where classic
cnn
can be trained to approximate the
pde
solution. However, PhyGeoNet lacks the flexibility to handle highly complex domains, as establishing a one-to-one mapping for such geometries is difficult, and direct application of elliptic coordinate transformation is often impossible.
Another popular approach involves training one
nn
in the interior domain and prescribing an offset function that either satisfies or is trained to satisfy the Dirichlet boundary conditions. The final solution is obtained by adding the interior
nn
, multiplied by a distance function that vanishes on the Dirichlet boundary, to the offset function
[
25
,
26
]
. The shortcoming of this method is that neither the offset function nor the distance function is easily obtainable for complex geometries.
The
\Delta
-
pinn
method
[
27
]
enables the use of
pinn
on complex geometries by representing manifolds with eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator. These functions can be approximated numerically for any shape using
fe
. When applied to a steady-state heat transfer problem on a complex sink, the method produces a temperature distribution that is qualitatively closer to the ground truth compared to standard
pinn
. However, the number of eigenfunctions has a significant, non-monotonic impact on the accuracy of the method, and must be carefully tuned for complex geometries.

In this work, we integrate unfitted
fe
into the
feinn
framework to solve
pde
on arbitrarily complex geometries. We coin this novel approach the unfitted
feinn
method.
In a nutshell,
\Acp
nn are interpolated onto high-order unfitted trial
fe
spaces, which are built on a simple background mesh.
The corresponding
linearised
test
fe
space is constructed on a refined mesh. The
nn
are trained to minimise a loss function defined either as the algebraic norm of the residual vector or a discrete dual norm (defined over such test space) of the weak
pde
residual.
The imposition of essential boundary conditions is challenging in
pinn
-like approaches. These methods typically treat the conditions as penalty terms
[
9
]
, use Nitsche’s method
[
28
]
, or rely on distance functions that vanish at the boundary
[
25
,
26
,
29
]
.
Our approach adopts Nitsche’s method, as used in unfitted
fem
, and our experiments demonstrate that unfitted
feinn
offer a broad tolerance for the choice of the Nitsche coefficient.
When an Euclidean norm of the discrete residual is used, the method is an extension of the
ivpinn
with Nitsche’s method in
[
30
]
to unfitted meshes.
However, the use of discrete dual norms proves to be essential for non-smooth solutions. The computation of such norms involves the solution of a Poisson problem discretised using linearised
fe
spaces on unfitted meshes, which is affected by the so-called small cut cell problem
[
2
]
. To solve this issue, both aggregated
fe
spaces
[
4
]
and ghost penalty stabilisation
[
5
]
have been considered.

The unfitted
feinn
method transforms the (non)linear system of equations into a residual minimisation problem. The method is insensitive to small cut cell issues when residual vector-based loss functions (that is, based on algebraic norms applied to the residual vector) are employed. Even with loss functions based on the dual norm of the residual functional, only the computation of the discrete dual norm of the residual, i.e., an approximation of the
H^{1}
inner product for the linearised
fe
space, requires stabilisation; this is a simpler task compared to stabilising the original unfitted problem with a high-order trial space and a linearised test space.
A comprehensive set of experiments on 2D and 3D complex geometries demonstrates the effectiveness of unfitted
feinn
. We evaluate the method on linear and nonlinear forward
pde
, as well as inverse nonlinear problems, to confirm its reliability for solving
pde
with weak
H^{1}
stability.
The geometries considered in the experiments include implicitly defined ones via level-set functions, as well as those defined explicitly by
stl
(
stl
) meshes, which are more representative of real-world applications
[
31
]
.
The method is compared against standard
pinn
on both CPU and GPU architectures. Employing the same training points, unfitted
feinn
reach comparable accuracy using fewer training iterations and reduced computational time.
The trained
nn
significantly outperform
fe
interpolations of the analytic solution in terms of
H^{1}
error, particularly when the solution is smooth. Furthermore, when a discrete dual norm of the residual is applied, we achieve further gains in accuracy, and the number of iterations required for convergence is substantially reduced.

The rest of the article is organised as follows. Sect.
2
introduces the continuous setting, unfitted
fe
, the unfitted
feinn
method, and their associated loss functions. Sect.
3
details the implementation of linearised test
fe
spaces and provides an overview of the
feinn
implementation. Sect.
4
presents numerical experiments with different
pde
on 2D and 3D complex geometries. Finally, Sect.
5
concludes the article and outlines promising directions for future research.

2.
Methodology

2.1.
Continuous setting

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Lipschitz polyhedral domain with boundary
\partial\Omega
and outward pointing unit normal
\boldsymbol{n}
. We focus on
low-dimensional
problems, i.e.,
d\in\{2,3\}
.
The physical domain
\Omega
may have a complex shape represented implicitly by a level set function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\Omega=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\phi(\boldsymbol{x})<0\}
and
\partial\Omega=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\phi(\boldsymbol{x})=0\}
. Alternatively, the boundary
\partial\Omega
can be explicitly described using
stl
surface meshes
[
31
]
.

As a model problem, we consider the Poisson equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions. Its strong form reads: find
u\in U\doteq H^{1}(\Omega)
such that

(1)

-\boldsymbol{\Delta}u=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad u=g\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

where
f\in H^{-1}(\Omega)
is a known source term, and
g\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
is a known Dirichlet boundary data.
Note that Neumann boundary conditions can also be included into the formulation; however, for simplicity and without loss of generality, we focus on the pure Dirichlet case.
The Poisson equation is a representative example of second-order elliptic
pde
with weak
H^{1}
stability, which is the class of
pde
considered in this work. Besides, we adopt a nonlinear
pde
from
[
14
]
that fits in this class.
The strong form of the nonlinear
pde
is as follows:
find
u\in U
such that

(2)

-\boldsymbol{\Delta}u+(\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla})u+\sigma\mathrm{e}^{-u^{2}}=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad u=g\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\sigma
are known coefficients.
We emphasise that the methodology in this work can be readily extended to more general
pde
, as far as suitable unfitted
fe
discretisation schemes are available.

2.2.
Unfitted finite element discretisation

Since
\Omega
has a complex shape and generating a mesh that fits
\Omega
is typically challenging, we use unfitted
fe
to discretise the space. We introduce a background box
\Omega^{\rm box}
that contains
\Omega
(i.e.,
\Omega\subset\Omega^{\rm box}
), and can be easily partitioned by mean of an easy-to-generate mesh, e.g., a simple Cartesian mesh.
Consider a quasi-uniform triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{h}
of
\Omega^{\rm box}
with the characteristic mesh size
h>0
. Following the notation in
[
4
]
, a cell
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
can belong to one of three categories: if
K\cap\Omega=\emptyset
, then
K
is an
external cell
and
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\rm ext}
; if
K\cap\Omega=K
, then
K
is an
interior cell
and
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\rm in}
; otherwise,
K
is a
cut cell
and
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\rm cut}
. The set of active cells is defined as
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\rm act}\doteq\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\rm in}\cup\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\rm cut}
. We then define the standard
fe
space
U_{h}^{\rm std}\subset U
of order
k_{U}
on
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\rm act}
. Note that
U_{h}^{\rm std}
, a conforming Lagrangian
fe
space on interior and cut cells, is exposed to ill-conditioning due to small cut cells
[
3
,
4
]
.

Unfitted
fe
mainly face three challenges: (i) numerical integration over the physical domain
\Omega
; (ii) the imposition of essential boundary conditions; and (iii) stability issues caused by small cut cells.
We give details on how to address (i) in Sect.
3.1
.
To address (ii), Nitsche’s method is widely used to weakly enforce Dirichlet boundary conditions, and we use it in this work as well to build
nn
such that their interpolation onto the trial
fe
space weakly satisfy the essential boundary conditions.
In the following sections, we first introduce Nitsche’s method and then discuss two unfitted
fe
formulations to address (iii).

2.3.
Nitsche’s method

With unfitted space discretisations, strongly enforcing essential boundary conditions
in the approximation space (as it is done with body-fitted
fe
) is challenging. Instead, we use Nitsche’s method to weakly impose these conditions.
We denote the trial and test
fe
spaces as
U_{h}
and
V_{h}
, respectively. Note that
U_{h}\subseteq U
and
V_{h}\subseteq U
can either be the standard
fe
spaces introduced in Sect.
2.2
or the aggregated
fe
spaces introduced in Sect.
2.4
. When necessary, the type of space is indicated with a superscript.
We now define the bilinear form
a:U_{h}\times V_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
and the linear form
\ell:V_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
for the Poisson model problem (
1
) as

\displaystyle a(u_{h},v_{h})

\displaystyle\doteq\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\nabla}u_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}v_{h}\mathrm{d}V+\int_{\partial\Omega}(\frac{\gamma}{h}u_{h}v_{h}-u_{h}\frac{\partial v_{h}}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}}-\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}}v_{h})\mathrm{d}S,

\displaystyle l(v_{h})

\displaystyle\doteq\int_{\Omega}fv_{h}\mathrm{d}V+\int_{\partial\Omega}(\frac{\gamma}{h}gv_{h}-g\frac{\partial v_{h}}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}})\mathrm{d}S,

where
\gamma>0
is the Nitsche coefficient and
\partial/\partial\boldsymbol{n}
denotes derivative in the direction of
\boldsymbol{n}
.
These forms are derived by multiplying the Poisson equation (
1
) by a test function
v_{h}\in V_{h}
, integrating by parts while noting that
v_{h}\neq 0
on
\partial\Omega
, and adding the Nitsche boundary terms.
Then, the Nitsche
fe
formulation reads: find
u_{h}\in U_{h}
such that

(3)

a(u_{h},v_{h})=\ell(v_{h}),\quad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}.

In
fem
, the Nitsche coefficient
\gamma
in
a
and
\ell
is a mesh-dependent parameter that must be sufficiently large to guarantee the coercivity of the bilinear form. Two suggestions to determine
\gamma
are summarised in
[
3
, Sect. 2.1]
.
It is straightforward to verify that Nitsche’s method is consistent with the original problem: when
u_{h}=g
on
\partial\Omega
, the Nitsche boundary terms in
a
and
\ell
vanish, and the formulation reduces to the integration-by-parts form of (
1
).
In regards to the convergence of Nitsche’s method, we refer to
[
32
, Chap. 2]
.

We denote the weak
fe
residual functional as

(4)

\mathcal{R}_{h}(u_{h})\doteq a(u_{h},\cdot)-\ell(\cdot)\in V_{h}^{\prime}.

2.4.
CutFEM and AgFEM

Both Cut
fem
and
agfem
are unfitted methods that use the
fe
formulation (
3
), the difference lies in the way they stabilise the
fe
scheme.
Cut
fem

[
3
]
utilises the standard
fe
spaces
U_{h}^{\rm std}
and
V_{h}^{\rm std}
introduced in Sect.
2.2
. To address the small cut cell problem, the Cut
fem
adds ghost penalty terms
[
5
]
to the left-hand side of (
3
).
The penalty is defined on the skeleton
\mathcal{F}_{g}
, which is the set of interior faces that belong to an element
K
such that
K\cap\partial\Omega\neq\emptyset

[
8
]
. Let us denote the normal to a face
F\in\mathcal{F}_{g}
as
\boldsymbol{n}_{F}
, the ghost penalty terms are defined as

(5)

j(u_{h},v_{h})=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{g}}\sum_{i=1}^{k_{U}}\gamma_{g,i}h^{2i-1}(\lBrack\frac{\partial^{i}u_{h}}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}_{F}}\rBrack,\lBrack\frac{\partial^{i}v_{h}}{\partial\boldsymbol{n}_{F}}\rBrack)_{F},

where
u_{h}\in U_{h}^{\rm std}
,
v_{h}\in V_{h}^{\rm std}
,
k_{U}
is the order of
U_{h}^{\rm std}
,
\gamma_{g,i}>0
are the ghost penalty coefficients,
(\cdot,\cdot)_{F}
is the
L^{2}
inner product over the face
F
,
\partial^{i}/\partial\boldsymbol{n}_{F}
is the
i
-th derivative in the direction of
\boldsymbol{n}_{F}
, and
\lBrack\cdot\rBrack
denotes the jump in a discontinuous function at
F
:

\lBrack w\rBrack\doteq w|_{F_{+}}-w|_{F_{-}}.

Since high-order directional derivative terms are required in the ghost penalty (
5
), Cut
fem
becomes computationally expensive when
k_{U}
is high.

Instead of adding additional terms to the formulation,
agfem

[
4
]
overcomes this issue at the
fe
space level by cell aggregation and a discrete extension operator. Specifically, ill-posed cut cells are
absorbed
by well-posed interior cells to form aggregates, with each ill-posed cut cell belonging to only one aggregate, and each aggregate containing only one well-posed interior cell, i.e., the so-called root cell of the aggregate.
The aggregated
fe
spaces
U_{h}^{\rm ag}
and
V_{h}^{\rm ag}
are then defined on the aggregated mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\rm ag}
. In such
fe
spaces, the
dof
in each aggregate are associated only with the root cell, while the other ill-posed
dof
are linear combinations of those in the root cell (the discrete extension from interior to cut cells). We refer to, e.g.,
[
4
]
, for details of the cell aggregation algorithms, analysis, and implementation details of this method.

2.5.
Neural networks

We focus on fully-connected, feed-forward
nn
composed of affine maps followed by nonlinear activation functions. The network structure is represented by a tuple
(n_{0},\ldots n_{L})\in\mathbb{N}^{L+1}
, where
L
is the number of layers and
n_{k}
is the number of neurons at layer
1\leq k\leq L
. We set
n_{0}=d
for the input layer and
n_{L}=1
for the output layer, and
n
neurons for all hidden layers, i.e.,
n_{1}=n_{2}=...=n_{L-1}=n
.

At each layer
1\leq k\leq L
, the affine map is represented by
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{k-1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
and defined as
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{b}_{k}
, where
\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times n_{k-1}}
is the weight matrix and
\boldsymbol{b}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
is the bias vector. After the application of the affine map corresponding to each layer except the last one, the activation function
\rho:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is applied element-wise.
With these definitions, the network is a parametrisable function
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as:

\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{L}\circ\rho\circ\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{L-1}\circ\ldots\circ\rho\circ\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{1},

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}
represents all the trainable parameters
\boldsymbol{W}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{b}_{k}
. We apply the same fixed activation function at each layer, even though the activations could be different or trainable.
In this work,
\mathcal{N}
denotes the
nn
architecture, and
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
represents a particular realisation of this network.

2.6.
Unfitted finite element interpolated neural networks

The unfitted
feinn
method extends the
feinn
formulation in
[
15
]
to solve elliptic
pde
on complex domains by using unfitted
fe
formulations (
3
). We define a
fe
interpolant
\pi_{h}:\mathcal{C}^{0}\rightarrow U_{h}^{\rm std}
. For the grad-conforming Lagrange (nodal) spaces considered in this work,
\pi_{h}
involves only pointwise evaluations at the free nodes. The unfitted
feinn
method aims to find:

(6)

u_{\mathcal{N}}\in\underset{w_{\mathcal{N}}\in\mathcal{N}}{\mathrm{arg\,min}}\,\mathscr{L}(w_{\mathcal{N}}),\qquad\mathscr{L}(w_{\mathcal{N}})\doteq\left\lVert\mathcal{R}_{h}({\pi}_{h}(w_{\mathcal{N}}))\right\rVert_{X^{\prime}},

where
u_{\mathcal{N}}
is the
nn
approximation to the solution of the problem, and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X^{\prime}}
denotes an appropriate discrete dual norm.

Let us now discuss the choice of the trial and test
fe
spaces. Since the
fe
problem (
3
) is reformulated as a minimisation problem (
6
), and the trial
fe
space is “hidden” in the
pde
residual (
4
), we can choose the trial space to be
U_{h}^{\rm std}
, i.e., the standard unfitted
fe
space
without any stabilisation
.
It is not necessary to apply stabilisation because the discrete minimisation problem is determined by the
nn
parameters, not the ill-posed
dof
in the
fe
space.
In fact, all experiments in Sect.
4
utilise
U_{h}^{\rm std}
as the trial space and yield accurate results.
When
nn
are interpolated onto
U_{h}^{\rm ag}
, they lose control over the cut cells during training because these
dof
are defined as linear combinations of the
dof
in the root cells.
For the test
fe
space in the residual
\mathcal{R}_{h}
(
4
), the choice depends on the specific loss function employed. Options include
V_{h}^{\rm std}
, with or without a ghost penalty, or
V_{h}^{\rm ag}
. Details on the loss functions and their corresponding test space selections are provided in Sect.
2.7
.
Moreover, as shown in
[
15
,
18
]
, the linearisation of test spaces significantly accelerates convergence and improves the accuracy of
nn
solutions. In unfitted
feinn
, we also employ such Petrov-Galerkin
fe
formulation, adopting
linearised

V_{h}^{\rm std}
and
V_{h}^{\rm ag}
. These spaces are constructed by refining the background mesh discretising
\Omega^{\rm box}
and building linear
fe
spaces on the active mesh resulting from intersecting the refined mesh and the domain boundary. Implementation details of these linearised unfitted test spaces are discussed in Sect.
3.1
.

2.7.
Loss functions

The
fe
residual
\mathcal{R}_{h}
(
4
) is isomorphic to the residual vector

[\mathbf{r}_{h}(w_{h})]_{i}=\left<\mathcal{R}_{h}(w_{h}),\varphi^{i}\right>\doteq\mathcal{R}_{h}(w_{h})(\varphi^{i}),

where
\{\varphi^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are the shape functions that span the test
fe
space
V_{h}
and
N
is the dimension of
V_{h}
. We can use the following loss function:

(7)

\mathscr{L}(u_{\mathcal{N}})=\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{h}(\pi_{h}(u_{\mathcal{N}}))\right\rVert_{\chi},

where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\chi}
is an algebraic norm, e.g.,
\ell^{1}
or
\ell^{2}
norm. When using a body-fitted mesh and the
\ell^{2}
norm, the method becomes equivalent to the Nitsche variant of
ivpinn

[
30
]
. However, in contrast to
[
30
]
, in this work we focus on unfitted meshes to handle complex geometries. When the loss (
7
) is employed for unfitted
feinn
, the method is referred to as
\ell^{1}
- or
\ell^{2}
-
feinn
, depending on the algebraic norm chosen.
Although the
\ell^{2}
norm is more common in the literature
[
14
]
, the
\ell^{1}
norm is also effective and can produce remarkable results, as revealed in
[
15
,
18
]
. We indeed use both norms in the experiments of this work.
It is important to note that we always choose
V_{h}^{\rm std}
as the test space when loss (
7
) is used. In this setup, neither the trial nor test spaces are stabilised, nor is there a need for ghost penalty terms to stabilise the discrete variational
fe
formulation. Nevertheless, as demonstrated in the moving domain tests in Sect.
4.1.3
, the unfitted
feinn
method remains robust to small cut cells.
In this setting, the problem is stated as a residual minimisation, where several (local) minima do not disrupt the algorithm. This contrasts sharply with unfitted
fem
, where the solution requires multiplying the inverse of the system matrix and the right-hand side vector, facing ill-conditioning issues, i.e., a lack of uniqueness in the singular limit.

However, as mentioned in
[
15
,
18
]
, the loss (
7
) becomes ill-posed as
h\rightarrow 0
; it amounts to computing the
L^{2}
-norm (up to a constant that depends on the quasi-uniformity of the mesh) of the residual functional, which is only in
H^{-1}(\Omega)
. Besides, using these algebraic norms on the residual vector, optimal convergence rates have not been proven
[
14
]
. It has motivated the use of dual norms in
[
15
]
, i.e.,

(8)

\mathscr{L}(u_{\mathcal{N}})=\left\lVert\mathcal{R}_{h}(\pi_{h}(u_{\mathcal{N}}))\right\rVert_{U^{\prime}}.

In
feinn
, this discrete dual norm can be computed by introducing a discrete Riesz projector
\mathcal{B}_{h}^{-1}:V_{h}^{\prime}\to V_{h}
such that

\mathcal{B}_{h}^{-1}\mathcal{R}_{h}(w_{h})\in V_{h}\ :\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}^{-1}\mathcal{R}_{h}(w_{h}),v_{h}\right)_{U}=\mathcal{R}_{h}(w_{h})(v_{h}),\quad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}.

where
\mathcal{B}_{h}
is (an approximation of) the corresponding Gram matrix in
V_{h}
. Then, we can write the loss
preconditioned
by
\mathcal{B}_{h}
as:

(9)

\mathscr{L}(u_{\mathcal{N}})=\left\lVert\mathcal{B}_{h}^{-1}\mathcal{R}_{h}({\pi}_{h}(u_{\mathcal{N}}))\right\rVert_{X}.

When
X=U
, the loss functions in (
8
) and (
9
) are equivalent, and the method is denoted as
H^{-1}
-
feinn
.
Other choices of
X
are possible, e.g.,
X=L^{2}(\Omega)
.
To reduce computational costs,
\mathcal{B}_{h}
can be substituted with any spectrally equivalent approximation; for example,
gmg
(
gmg
) preconditioners are explored in
[
15
]
.

When utilising the loss function with the discrete dual norm of the residual (
9
), the test
fe
space can be either
V_{h}^{\rm std}
or
V_{h}^{\rm ag}
. However, adding the ghost penalty (
5
) is essential to stabilise the variational problem (
9
) in
V_{h}^{\rm std}
, ensuring its invertibility in inexact arithmetic regardless of the small cuts. In contrast, discretising (
9
) in
V_{h}^{\rm ag}
is inherently stable
[
4
]
. Indeed, in
[
33
]
, it was shown that standard multigrid solvers for body-fitted
fem
reach expected mesh-independent convergence rates and linear
complexity when applied to the Gram matrix in
V_{h}^{\rm ag}
. We compare the performance of these two test spaces in the experiments in Sect.
4.1.5
.

The analysis of
feinn
for the loss function in (
8
) can be found in
[
18
, Sect. 3]
for strong imposition of boundary conditions and coercive systems. The extension to Nitsche’s method is straightforward, with minor modifications. These results can be used in the case of unfitted
feinn
under the assumption of a discrete inf-sup condition between the trial and test spaces (see
[
18
, Sect. 2.2]
) combined with the enhanced stability properties of CutFEM and AgFEM discretisation
[
5
,
4
]
.

2.8.
Extension to inverse problems

Unfitted
feinn
can be extended straightforwardly to solve inverse problems. These include estimating the unknown model parameters of a
pde
from partial and/or noisy observations of the solution and fully reconstructing the solution/state itself.
Let us denote the unknown parameters as
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, which may include physical coefficients, boundary values, or source terms. In addition to the state
nn

u_{\mathcal{N}}
, we introduce another
nn

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{N}}
to approximate the unknown model parameters.
We interpolate
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{N}}
onto suitable
fe
spaces using the interpolants
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{h}
to ensure efficient computation of the loss function and its gradients.
The loss function for the inverse problem is defined as:

(10)

\mathscr{L}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{N}},u_{\mathcal{N}})\doteq\left\lVert\mathbf{d}-\mathcal{D}(u_{\mathcal{N}})\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}}+\alpha\left\lVert\mathcal{R}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{N}}),\pi_{h}(u_{\mathcal{N}}))\right\rVert_{X^{\prime}},

where
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is the observed data and
M\in\mathbb{N}
is the number of observations,
\mathcal{D}:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{M}
is the observation operator, and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the penalty coefficient.
The first term in (
10
) measures the data misfit, while the second term weakly imposes the
pde
constraint.
In the inverse problem experiments in Sect.
4.3
,
\mathcal{D}
is defined by directly evaluating
u_{\mathcal{N}}
at the observation points.
We refer to
[
15
]
for a detailed discussion on
feinn
for inverse problems. The key difference in this work is the use of unfitted
fe
formulations rather than body-fitted
fe
formulations.

3.
Implementation

Unfitted
feinn
are a natural extension of the
feinn
framework, thus allowing one to reuse most of the building blocks of the software implementing the framework. The additional components required by unfitted
feinn
are: (i) the design and implementation of unfitted linearised test
fe
spaces and (ii) the integration over the physical domain with irregular shapes when such domain is cut by a background mesh. We first address (i) and (ii) in Sect.
3.1
, followed by an outline of the Julia implementation of unfitted
feinn
in Sect.
3.2
.

3.1.
Unfitted linearised test spaces

The (body-fitted)
feinn
method in
[
15
]
constructs a trial
fe
space of order
k_{U}
on a coarse mesh. It applies
k_{U}-1
levels of uniform refinement to this coarse mesh and builds a test
fe
space made of linear
polynomials on the refined mesh.
We call such space a
linearised
test
fe
space. It has the same dimension (i.e., number of
dof
) as the trial
fe
space. In practice, such an approach is proven to drastically accelerate training convergence and improve
nn
accuracy
[
15
]
.

In the unfitted case, we can use (and we indeed use) an analogous approach to construct a linearised test space. In particular, we compute a refined background mesh, and then we intersect this mesh and the physical domain. However, as shown below with an example, this can lead to a refined active triangulation that covers only a strict subregion of the coarse active triangulation. As a consequence, a test space made of linear polynomials on this refined active triangulation may result in a reduced number of
dof
compared to those in the trial space. Fortunately, the residual minimisation approach underlying unfitted
feinn
is flexible enough to naturally accommodate such scenarios.

Consider a physical domain
\Omega
within a background box
\Omega^{\rm box}
as displayed in Fig.
1(a)
. Let us assume that we discretise
\Omega^{\rm box}
using a
5\times 4
Cartesian mesh, which we refer to as the coarse mesh.
As shown in Fig.
1(b)
, in this particular case, all cells in the coarse mesh are active cells. Assuming
k_{U}=2
for the trial space
U_{h}^{\rm std}
, the global
dof
of
U_{h}^{\rm std}
are illustrated by black dots in Fig.
1(b)
.
Next, we uniformly refine the coarse mesh once, generating a refined mesh with
10\times 8
cells, as shown in Fig.
1(c)
.
The active cells on this refined mesh, resulting from the intersection of the refined mesh and the domain, are highlighted in orange. A linear test
fe
space
V_{h}^{\rm std}
is then constructed on this refined active cells, with its
dof
represented by blue crosses in Fig.

We immediately observe that

rectangular
discrete system. While this mismatch is problematic for
fem
, as it prevents the application of solvers for algebraic systems of equations, we can still apply the unfitted
feinn
method because such method recasts the problem into a residual minimisation problem, which can be solved using a gradient-based optimiser. Although residual minimisation is also available in
fem

.

Remark 3.1
.

It is actually possible to build a test
fe
space made of linear polynomials with the same number of
dof
as a higher order trial
fe
space. This requires a suitable
fe
on the coarse mesh active cells.
In particular, one can use hierarchical Q
1
-iso-Q
k

fe

[
35
, Sect. 54.4.2]
. These
fe

lead to a well-posed square Petrov-Galerkin discrete system as the basis functions of this
fe

have support across the whole coarse cell.
We indeed tested this
fe
and observed results comparable to those achieved by linear bases on the refined active mesh resulting from cutting the refined background mesh with the domain boundary (i.e., the approach that we finally pursue in this paper).
As linear bases on the refined mesh are significantly easier to implement (i.e., one can reuse verbatim existing unfitted
fe
codes), we chose them in this paper.

In the implementation, we first intersect the refined mesh with
\Omega
to identify the active refined cells, which allows us to construct
V_{h}^{\rm std}
. Next, we determine the active cells on the coarse mesh using the following rule: a coarse cell is active if it has at least one active child cell. This way we do not need to compute the
intersection of the coarse mesh with
\Omega
. The high-order
U_{h}^{\rm std}
is then built on these active coarse cells.
The aggregated test
fe
space
V_{h}^{\rm ag}
, as usual, is constructed from
V_{h}^{\rm std}
. We classify interior cells as well-posed and cut cells as ill-posed, then form aggregates with one well-posed cell as root.
The space
V_{h}^{\rm ag}
is built on these aggregates, with free
dof
determined by the root cells.

Similar to the body-fitted
feinn
method, integration is performed on the
refined
mesh when one uses linearised
fe
spaces.
For interior cells, the integration process is the same as in fitted
fem
. For a cut cell
K
, we discretise the intersection
K\cap\Omega
into a local-to-the-cell submesh of triangles (2D) or tetrahedra (3D) and use quadratures rule for simplices with sufficient degree to integrate exactly over these subcells those polynomials defined on the quadrilateral/hexaedral cells. This is the standard approach pursued in unfitted
fem
to integrate polynomials on
cut cells.

4.
Numerical experiments

We evaluate the accuracy of unfitted
feinn
in solving both linear and nonlinear equations on 2D and 3D complex geometries. The experiments begin with 2D and 3D forward problems, followed by inverse problems in 2D.
The linear
pde
considered is the Poisson equation with a constant diffusion coefficient, as described in (
1
). Its Nitsche
fe
formulation is given in (
3
).
Note that the
feinn
framework can readily handle spatially varying coefficients, as demonstrated in
[
15
]
. In the nonlinear forward and inverse experiments, we focus on the nonlinear equation in strong form (
2
).
We first discuss the settings for forward problems.

We employ the same
nn
structure as in
[
15
]
for all the forward experiments, i.e.,
L=5
layers,
n=50
neurons per hidden layer, and
tanh
activation.
Unless otherwise specified, we use the
BFGS
optimiser from
Optim.jl

[
42
]
to train
nn
, following the settings in
[
15
,
18
]
.
When the number of
dof
is relatively small (e.g., fewer than 1,000),
nn
can easily overfit, so we reduce the number of training iterations (e.g., to a few hundreds). However, with sufficient number of
dof
, longer training does not negatively affect the training results, so we typically set a large number (e.g., 10,000) of iterations to ensure the
nn
training process has converged. We also note that higher trial space orders require more iterations for the training to converge.
Besides, by default, we use
\ell^{2}
-
feinn
(i.e., the Euclidean norm of the residual in loss function (
7
)), which has been proven to be effective for smooth solutions
[
15
,
18
]
. For rough solutions with localised high gradients, we use
H^{-1}
-
feinn
, i.e., loss (
9
) with
X=H^{1}
, which notably improves robustness as shown in Sect.
4.1.5
.

To assess the accuracy of the identified solution
u^{id}
, we compute its
L^{2}
and
H^{1}
errors as:

e_{L^{2}(\Omega)}(u^{id})=\left\lVert u-u^{id}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\qquad e_{H^{1}(\Omega)}(u^{id})=\left\lVert u-u^{id}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)},

where
u
is the true solution,
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\sqrt{\int_{\Omega}|\cdot|^{2}}
, and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=\sqrt{\int_{\Omega}|\cdot|^{2}+|\boldsymbol{\nabla}(\cdot)|^{2}}
.
After training, where the interpolated
nn
(i.e.,
\pi_{h}(u_{\mathcal{N}})
) is used to compute the loss and gradients, we obtain a
nn
solution (i.e.,
u_{\mathcal{N}}
),
u^{id}
can be either
\pi_{h}(u_{\mathcal{N}})
or
u_{\mathcal{N}}
.
For the
h
- and
p
-refinement error convergence tests, we repeat each experiment 10 times with different initial
nn
parameters to account for the sensitivity of achieved results with respect to
nn
initialisation. Since the variations in the
\pi_{h}(u_{\mathcal{N}})
solutions are negligible, we only show the average errors among these 10 runs in the plots.

To establish a baseline to compare the accuracy of the identified solutions, we interpolate the true solution onto the trial
fe
space and compute the
L^{2}
and
H^{1}
errors. These interpolations are denoted as
\pi_{h}(u)
in the figures and their errors are referred to as
\left\lVert u-\pi_{h}(u)\right\rVert
in the text.
There are several reasons that prevent us from using
fem
solutions as a baseline for comparison. Firstly, the
fem
solutions are sensitive to the choice of Nitsche coefficient
\gamma
. Secondly, unfitted
fem
often require additional stabilisation techniques, such as ghost penalties in Cut
fem
, which introduce extra parameters that can affect solution accuracy. Lastly, as mentioned in Sect.
3.1
, the Petrov-Galerkin
fe
formulation proposed in this work leads to a rectangular system that cannot be directly solved using standard sparse direct solvers.
In contrast, computing the
fe
interpolation of the true solution is straightforward and unaffected by these hyperparameters.

For the inverse experiments, we also utilise the
BFGS
optimiser. As recommended in
[
15
]
, lighter
nn
structures are adopted, the activation function is set to
softplus
, and the
\ell^{1}
norm of the residual vector is employed in the second term of the loss function (
10
).
We follow the three-step training strategy proposed in
[
15
, Sect. 4]
to train the
nn
. In step 1, the state
nn

u_{\mathcal{N}}
is trained using only the data misfit error (first term in (
10
)). In step 2, the unknown model parameter
nn

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{N}}
are trained using the
pde
residual (second term in (
10
)) with
u_{\mathcal{N}}
fixed. Finally, in step 3, both
u_{\mathcal{N}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{N}}
are jointly trained using the full loss (
10
).
The first two steps serve as cost-efficient initialisations for the final step, which is the most computationally intensive. Besides, step 3 is divided into several substeps, with the penalty coefficient
\alpha
gradually increased to ensure the convergence of the training.
For more details on the training strategy, refer to
[
15
]
and
[
43
, Chap. 17]
.

4.1.2.
Comparison of

This experiment compares the computational efficiency of unfitted

. The loss function for

[

where

and

, while the second term weakly enforces the Dirichlet boundary condition through penalisation.

We adopt the same disk geometry and smooth analytic solution as in Sect.

. For the
\ell^{2}
-

To ensure a fair comparison, we adopt the strategy in

]

. In particular, we extract the

\Omega
as interior collocation points for

.
Using the level-set function, we then uniformly generate boundary collocation points such that their total number equals to the number the interpolation nodes located outside
\Omega
in
feinn
.
In this setup,
N_{\rm int}=5013
and
N_{\rm b}=868
. This approach ensures both methods are trained on the same interior points and the same amount of total points. However, the computational cost is more favourable to
pinn
, because as opposed to Nitsche’s method in

,
they do not require computing spatial derivatives on the boundary (approximately 15% of the total points).

As suggested in
[
14
]
, convergence in

.
However, convergence curves in
[

For the penalty coefficient

to

and found that

yielded the best results for this problem.

4.1.5.
Discrete dual norms for the Poisson equation

In this experiment, we explore the impact of discrete dual norms in the training of unfitted

-

We use the same disk geometry as in Sect.

. The background box
[0,1]^{2}
is partitioned into
50\times 50
quadrilaterals.
To increase the difficulty of the problem, we use the following analytic solution, which features a sharp gradient around the centre of the disk:

u(x,y)=\sin(3.2x(x-y))(5\mathrm{e}^{-100((x-0.5)^{2}+(y-0.5)^{2})}+1).

Since the solution is rougher than (
11
), the use of a discrete dual norm is instrumental in improving the convergence of the training process.

As described in Sect.
2.4
, we consider two linearised test
fe
spaces: the standard active space
V_{h}^{\rm{std}}
consisting of all internal and cut elements, and the aggregated active space
V_{h}^{\rm{ag}}
.
The discrete dual norm requires to compute the Gram matrix
for the linearised test space with respect to the
H^{1}
-inner product.
However, spectrally equivalent choices can also be considered. For example, in
[
15
, Sect. 6.1.3]
, we demonstrated that a single cycle of
gmg
was an effective approximation of the inverse of the Gram matrix.
Since small cut cells in
V_{h}^{\rm{std}}
may cause the Gram matrix to become singular in the presence
of rounding errors, additional ghost penalty terms (
5
) are required to ensure the Gram matrix is well-posed.
Note that in the context of unfitted
feinn

V_{h}^{\rm{std}}
is always linear. Therefore, a first-order penalty term is sufficient to stabilise the Gram matrix, i.e.,
k_{U}=1
in (
5
).
The implementation of unfitted
feinn
does not actually need to deal with high order derivatives in the case of Cut
fem
stabilisation, nor specialised discrete extensions required for high-order aggregated
fe
spaces
[
46
]
.
Following the recommendation in
[
3
]
, we set
\gamma_{g}=0.1
in this experiment.
On the other hand, the
V_{h}^{\rm{ag}}
space is designed to overcome stability issues by construction, thus no additional penalty terms are needed in the weak formulation.

3

\gamma=10^{-2}

-

[

5.
Conclusions

We combine unfitted
fe
discretisations and the
feinn
framework for the approximation of
pde
on complex geometries using
nn
. In the proposed unfitted
feinn
method, Dirichlet boundary conditions are weakly imposed via Nitsche’s method.
The high-order trial
fe
space onto which the
nn
is interpolated is built on the active cells of a coarse background mesh that is unfitted to the boundary of the geometry. With ease of implementation, and existing unfitted
fem
code reuse in mind, the linearised test space is constructed on the active cells of a uniformly refined version of this coarse background mesh.
Although the trial space has more
dof
than the test space, which leads to a rectangular system, this mismatch
can be naturally accommodated by the
feinn
method as such framework is based on a residual minimisation principle. Indeed, unfitted
feinn
exhibit outstanding convergence and accuracy in practice.

We conduct extensive numerical experiments to evaluate the unfitted
feinn
method on various 2D and 3D complex geometries for different values of numerical parameters, geometries and
pde
.
As opposed to unfitted
fem
, small cut cells have negligible impact on the accuracy of both trained
nn
and their interpolations,
even without using stabilisation techniques
such as Cut
fem
or Ag
fem
to compute the residual (such stabilisations are required, though, for the computation of discrete dual norms).
The
h
- and
p
-convergence tests for the Poisson equation manifest that the trained
nn
outperform the
fe
interpolation of the analytic solution on the same trial space, and their interpolations accomplish the expected convergence rates. Besides, unfitted
feinn
demonstrate robustness to the choice of Nitsche coefficient, which is a another advantage over unfitted
fem
.

In experiments comparing unfitted
feinn
and
pinn
,
feinn
achieve comparable or better accuracy, demonstrate higher robustness to hyperparameter choices, and require significantly less training time on both GPUs and CPUs.
We observe that the use of a discrete dual norm of the residual in the loss function, combined with a robust unfitted discretisation, significantly accelerates
nn
training convergence compared to the Euclidean norm variant and
pinn
. This effect is particularly notable for Poisson problems featuring a sharp gradient in a localised region of the domain, substantially reducing computational cost.
The nonlinear equation experiments verify that unfitted
feinn
are capable of dealing with more challenging
pde
.
The experiments on 3D geometries show that unfitted
feinn
accurately solve
pde
on 3D complex geometries defined implicitly via level-set functions or explicitly through
stl
surface meshes. This makes the method well-suited for real-world
cae
scenarios.

The inverse problem experiments exhibit that unfitted
feinn
can be easily and effectively extended to solve nonlinear inverse problems, delivering highly accurate and robust solutions on complex geometries. This overcomes key limitations of traditional
fem
for inverse settings, which require the use of adjoint solvers, nested loops, and regularisation.

Future research could explore several promising directions.
First, as discussed in
[
18
]
, to exploit the nonlinear approximability properties of
nn
, it is essential to adaptively refine the
fe
spaces. Enhancing unfitted
feinn
with adaptive training techniques
[
18
]
will enable us to tackle problems with multiscale features or singularities on complex geometries.
Another encouraging research avenue is applying unfitted
feinn
to evolutionary
pde
, especially time-dependent problems on moving domains
[
48
]
and fluid-structure interaction problems with evolving geometries
[
7
]
. In time-dependent problems, the
nn
trained at the previous time step serves as a good initial guess for the current step, which can clearly improve the training efficiency of unfitted
feinn
.
