Myxobolus binotati n. sp. (Myxozoa: Myxobolidae) from the ovaries of spotted barb, Barbodes binotatus (Valenciennes, 1842) in Terengganu, Malaysia
Authors: Nadhirah Syafiqah Suhaimi, Csaba Székely, Gábor Cech, Muhammad Hafiz Borkhanuddin, Boglárka Sellyei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To date, only 14 species have been reported worldwide from the ovaries of fish hosts. In the present study, a novel species, Myxobolus binotati n. sp., was described from the ovary of Barbodes binotatus. Spores were oval in frontal view and lemon-shaped in sutural view, measuring 10.7 × 8.3 × 6.2 μm. They possessed two unequal pyriform polar capsules, with larger measuring 4.8 ± 0.4 (4.0–5.8) μm in length, 2.8 ± 0.2 (2.4–3.4) μm in width, while smaller measuring 2.7 ± 0.3 (2.1–3.1) μm in length, 1.6 ± 0.2 (1.3–1.9) μm in width. Polar tubules coiled 7–8 and 4 turns in the larger and smaller capsules, respectively. The spores lacked an intercapsular appendix, iodinophilous vacuole, and mucous envelope, and possessed 8–10 asymmetrically distributed sutural markings. Molecular analysis of 18S rDNA gene revealed that the new species shared the highest sequence similarity (87.3%) with Myxobolus abitus found in the gills of Hypophthalmichthys molitrix. Phylogenetic analyses placed M. binotati n. sp. within a clade comprising species infecting the gills, fins, skin, scales, and ovaries, including several taxa characterized by unequal polar capsules. However, it formed a distinct lineage within this clade. The combined morphological, molecular, and phylogenetic evidence supports the establishment of M. binotati n. sp. as a new species.

Introduction

Myxozoans are the most common microscopic parasites that infect fish in both wild and aquaculture environments, where they can cause disease and significant economic losses through reduced growth, lowered market value, and increased mortality (Bergersen and Anderson, 1997; Feist and Longshaw, 2006; Okamura et al., 2015). Their pathogenic effects vary depending on the infected tissue and parasite species, which may include reduced respiratory capacity due to gill infection (Stilwell et al., 2024), body deformation (Dyková et al., 2002), degenerative cardiomyopathy (Yokoyama et al., 2005), and intestinal necrosis (Branson et al., 1999; Alvarez-Pellitero et al., 2008).

At present, 3043 valid myxozoan species are recorded (Whipps et al., 2025), and about 950 species belong to the genus Myxobolus Bütschli, 1882 (Eiras et al., 2005, 2014, 2021). Of the known Myxobolus species, only around 16 have been described from Malaysia to date, highlighting the limited knowledge of the diversity of this genus in Malaysian freshwater fishes (Molnár et al., 2006; Székely et al., 2009a, 2009b, 2012; Borkhanuddin et al., 2014; Samshuri and Borkhanuddin, 2024; Suhaimi et al., 2025). Myxozoan parasites rarely infect gonadal tissues (ovaries and testes), with only 20 species reported to date, including 12 species belonging to the genus Myxobolus. In freshwater fishes, ovarian infections have been recorded in 10 species belonging to genera Myxobolus (9) and Henneguya (1), whereas testicular infections have been reported less frequently. Barbodes binotatus is a common freshwater fish in Southeast Asia (Kottelat, 2013). This species has a wide distribution includes Malaysia (Ng et al., 2017; Zakaria-Ismail et al., 2019; Kottelat and Lim, 2021), Indonesia (Bariche, 1998; Kottelat, 2000), Brunei (Kottelat and Lim, 1995), Cambodia (Rainboth, 1996), Laos (Kottelat, 2013), Myanmar (Kottelat, 2013), Thailand (Pethiyagoda et al., 2012; Tongnunui and Beamish, 2017), Vietnam (Khoa and Huong, 1993) and the Philippines (Alonzo et al., 2024). It typically occurs in streams, rivers and lakes with clear water and a range of flow conditions from slow to fast. This fish is widely used as a bio-indicator of habitat degradation and freshwater ecosystem health (Isa et al., 2010; Zakeyudin et al., 2012). It is also common in the ornamental fish trade, and in Sabah, this species is consumed as a popular deep-fried delicacy. To date, there have been no records of myxozoan parasites infecting B. binotatus. Therefore, this study presents the first record of a Myxobolus species infecting the ovaries of B. binotatus in Malaysia.

Considering that no myxozoan parasites had been reported from B. binotatus, we conducted a comprehensive survey of this fish. In this study, plasmodia containing myxospores morphologically consistent with Myxobolus were observed in the ovaries. The main goal of this study was to identify and compare the myxobolid species using morphological and molecular data and to assess their phylogenetic relationship within the Myxobolidae.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and microscopic study

A total of thirteen fish specimens of B. binotatus were obtained from a local fish market in Kuala Terengganu, Terengganu, Malaysia. These fish originated from the river of Sungai Nerus in Kuala Nerus, Terengganu. The fish were purchased every two-week intervals over two periods: July to August 2023 and September to November 2024.

Live specimens were transported in oxygenated plastic bags to the Marine Science Biodiversity Laboratory of the Faculty of Science and Marine Environment at Universiti Malaysia Terengganu. Upon arrival, the fish were maintained in aerated tanks until dissection. Taxonomic identification of the fish specimens was performed based on morphological characteristics. In the laboratory, the fish were euthanized by cranial spiking and dissected. Major organs including fins, gills, skin, intestine, stomach, kidney, gallbladder, and muscles were thoroughly examined for the presence of plasmodia of myxosporean parasites. Initial observations were conducted with naked eye, followed by detailed examination using an Olympus SZX7 dissecting microscope (Olympus Corporation, Japan) and a light microscope to confirm the presence of the plasmodia.

Plasmodia were isolated from the ovary of a single individual, ruptured and the released spores were examined on glass slides (wet mount) using an Olympus CX33 biological microscope (Olympus Corporation, Japan). Following microscopic assessment, the remaining ovary tissues with plasmodia were preserved in 90% ethanol for molecular analyses and in 10% neutral buffered formalin for morphological analysis. All fixed specimens were subsequently transported to the Fish Pathology and Parasitology Laboratory at the HUN-REN Veterinary Medical Research Institute (Budapest, Hungary) for further investigation. Photomicrographs were obtained using an Olympus SZX16 stereo-microscope and an Olympus BX53 light microscope equipped with an Olympus DP74 digital camera (Olympus Corporation, Japan). Spore measurements from formalin-fixed specimens were performed in accordance with the guidelines of Lom and Arthur (1989) using ImageJ software. All measurements are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with ranges given in parentheses and expressed in micrometers (μm). Line drawings of Myxobolus spores were prepared from original digital images using Inkscape (Free Software Foundation, Inc., MA, USA).

Molecular analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from spores previously preserved in 90% ethanol. Prior to extraction, the samples were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min, after which the ethanol was carefully removed by pipetting. The resulting spore pellets were rinsed twice with 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.5) to remove any residual ethanol. DNA isolation was carried out using the Geneaid Tissue Genomic DNA Mini Kit (Geneaid Biotech Ltd., Taiwan), following the manufacturer's protocol for animal tissue samples.

The 18S rDNA gene was amplified by direct polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using multiple primer sets, including ACT1F-ACT1R and Myxgen4F-ERIB10 (Table 2). Each PCR was carried out in a final volume of 25 μL, containing 2 μL of template DNA, 1 × DreamTaq buffer (10×; Thermo Scientific), 0.2 mM dNTP mix (10 mM; Thermo Scientific), 1.25 U DreamTaq polymerase (5 U; Thermo Scientific), 12.5 pmol of each primer, and molecular grade water. The amplification conditions were identical for all primer pairs: an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 45 s, annealing at 55 °C for 50 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min with a final elongation step at 72 °C for 10 min. PCR products were visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis. Bands of the expected size were cut from the gel and purified using the DNA Fragment Purification Kit (InViTek GmbH, Berlin, Germany). Purified amplicons were sequenced bidirectionally using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) and sequencing reactions were read on an ABI PRISM 3100 Genetic Analyzer at Delta Bio 2000 Ltd., Szeged, Hungary, using the primers listed in Table 2.

Phylogenetic analyses

The obtained 18S rDNA sequences were visualized, corrected if necessary based on the chromatogram, and assembled using Geneious Prime v11.1 (Bio-matters; Auckland, New Zealand; https://www.geneious.com/) (Kearse et al., 2012). Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using one sequence obtained in this study together with 46 related sequences retrieved from the GenBank database using a threshold of ≥85% identity and ≥80% query coverage, including available sequences of other freshwater ovary-infecting species and Malaysian freshwater myxobolid sequences. The 85% similarity threshold was applied to retain potentially related and phylogenetically informative Myxobolus sequences while excluding highly divergent sequences that were less informative for assessing the phylogenetic position of the new species. Sequences with lower identity (<85%) or poor support were excluded. Chloromyxum cristatum (AY604198) was designated as the outgroup. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using the ClustalW algorithm (Thompson et al., 1994) implemented in MEGA X (Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis, 64-bit version; https://www.megasoftware.net/) (Kumar et al., 2018). Phylogenetic tree was reconstructed using the Maximum Likelihood method with 1000 bootstrap replicates in MEGA X, and Bayesian Inference conducted in MrBayes v3.2.443 (Ronquist et al., 2012). Gaps were treated using partial deletion with 75% site coverage cut-off for ML. The best-fit mucleotide substitution model (GTR + G + I) was selected based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) executed in jModelTest 2.1.10 v2016030344 (Darriba et al., 2012). For Bayesian analysis, Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulations were run for 1,000,000 generations, with the initial 25% discarded as burn-in. A consensus tree was generated from the remaining sampled topologies. Clades were considered well supported when bootstrap values (BS) were ≥ 70% for ML and posterior probabilities (PP) were ≥ 0.90 for BI analysis. The resulting tree topologies were visualized in MEGA X and FigTree v. 1.4.445 (Rambaut, 2018), and further refined graphically using Inkscape software. Pairwise genetic distances were calculated in MEGA X using p-distance model, accounting for both transitions and transversions.

Results

A parasitological examination of B. binotatus revealed no parasites other than myxospores. Among the 13 fish specimens examined, myxospores exhibiting the characteristic morphology of the genus Myxobolus were detected in the ovaries of a single individual. No myxospores or plasmodia were observed in any other tissues or organs, either by visual inspection or under a stereomicroscope. Morphological and molecular analyses confirmed that the detected Myxobolus species represents a novel species.

The assembled nucleotide sequence of the 18 S rDNA region was 1591 bp long. Phylogenetic analysis of multiple-sequence alignment comprising 1788 nucleotide positions, including 807 conserved and 929 variable sites.

Description of novel myxobolus species

Myxobolus binotati n. sp

Plasmodia: Found in the ovaries, oval in shape (Fig. 1A). Spore: Oval in both frontal (Fig. 1B and F), lemon-shaped in sutural view (Fig. 1C and F), 10.7 ± 0.4 (10.0–11.4) μm long, 8.3 ± 0.3 (7.8–8.9) μm wide, 6.2 ± 0.3 (5.9–6.8) μm thick (n = 10). Polar capsule: Two, pyriform, unequal. Larger: 4.8 ± 0.4 (4.0–5.8) μm long, 2.8 ± 0.2 (2.4–3.4) μm wide. Smaller: 2.7 ± 0.3 (2.1–3.1) μm long, 1.6 ± 0.2 (1.3–1.9) μm wide. Intercapsular appendix: Absent. Polar tubule: Coiled seven to eight times in larger capsule, four times in smaller capsule, positioned perpendicularly to capsule axis (Fig. 1D). Sutural ridge: Eight to ten sutural markings, unsymmetrically distributed (Fig. 1E). Sporoplasm: Binucleate, iodinophilous vacuole absent, mucous envelope absent. In comparison with other Myxobolus species infecting the ovaries of fish, M. binotati n. sp. differs markedly in several morphological characteristics. All previously described ovary-infecting Myxobolus species possess equal-sized polar capsules, whereas the polar capsules of M. binotati n. sp. are distinctly unequal. In addition, sutural markings are absent or have not been reported in the other ovary-infecting species (Table 5). Morphometrically, M. binotati n. sp. most closely resembles M. batalhensis from Brazil in terms of spore width, polar capsule width, and the number of polar tubule coils in the larger polar capsule. The number of polar capsule coils is almost similar to M. barakensis and M. batalhensis. Most notably, M. binotati n. sp. differs from other ovary-infecting Myxobolus species in its spore length and thickness, further supporting its morphological distinctiveness (Table 5). Discussion

To date, no species have been reported or described from the ovaries of fish hosts in Malaysia and Southeast Asia. Although the gonads of fish are not among the most common sites of myxosporean infection, several myxobolids have been reported from the ovaries and testes of fish. In this study, a novel Myxobolus species, Myxobolus binotati n. sp. was identified from the ovaries of B. binotatus. This species was characterized based on its morphological features and molecular analysis of the 18 S rDNA gene sequences.

The incidence of M. binotati n. sp. was relatively low in the present study, with the parasite detected in only one of the 13 B. binotatus examined. Although this single observation is sufficient to describe the new species, the small sample size limits the ability to draw reliable conclusions about its prevalence, distribution, or epidemiological significance. Further studies involving larger numbers of B. binotatus from different localities and sampling seasons are needed to determine the prevalence, distribution, and potential epidemiological significance of this parasite.

Myxosporean infections of the gonads can have significant pathological consequences for their hosts. Severe infections have been associated with reduced reproductive success, gonadal regression and even parasitic castration (Gbankoto et al., 2001; Sitjà-Bobadilla, 2009). For example, an unidentified Myxobolus species infecting the ovaries of the African catfish, C. gariepinus (Gbankoto et al., 2015), and M. batalhensis infecting the ovaries of S. hilarii (Vieira et al., 2017) caused deformation and degeneration of oocytes due to extensive spore proliferation, ultimately resulting in impaired reproductive capacity and reduced fertilization success. Similarly, M. dahomeyensis infecting the ovaries of several tilapia species in Benin was reported to destroy oocytes and induce host castration (Grankoto et al., 2001). However, the severity of the pathological effect appears to vary depending on the site and extent of parasite development within the ovary. Infections restricted to the ovarian stroma tend to exhibit lower pathogenicity and are often not associated with inflammatory responses or disruption of oocyte structure (Azevedo et al., 1997; Tajdari et al., 2005; Gbankoto et al., 2015; Xu et al., 2025). Unfortunately, histopathological examination was not possible in the present study because of the limited number of infected specimens available for analysis. Consequently, the exact site of development of M. binotati n. sp. within the ovary could not be determined, and it remains unclear whether the parasite develops within or around the oocytes or within the ovarian stroma. Any potential effects of M. binotati n. sp. on ovarian structure or host reproduction remain speculative at this stage. Further investigations incorporating larger numbers of infected fish and detailed histopathological analyses are required to identify the precise infection site, evaluate the associated pathological effects, and assess the potential impact of M. binotati n. sp. on oocyte development and the reproductive fitness of its host.

Phylogenetic analysis based on 18S rDNA sequences revealed that M. binotati n. sp. formed a distinct lineage within the broader Myxobolus-Thelohanellus assemblage. Notably, this species did not cluster with other ovary-infecting myxobolid species included in the analysis, such as M. algonquinensis, M. batalhensis, M. aculeatus, and H. oviperda, despite sharing the same predilection site. Instead, M. binotati n. sp. was positioned separately from these taxa, indicating that ovarian tissue tropism alone does not necessarily reflect phylogenetic relationships among ovary-infecting myxobolids. A similar discrepancy between infection site and phylogenetic relationships has been observed in myxosporeans, in which species infecting the same tissue are not necessarily closely related. Previous studies have demonstrated that closely related myxobolid species may infect different organs of the same or related hosts, whereas morphologically similar species occupying the same tissue may belong to distantly related lineages (Salim and Desser, 2000; Molnár et al., 2002; Eszterbauer, 2004; Molnár and Eszterbauer, 2015). Therefore, the phylogenetic separation of M. binotati n. sp. from other ovary-infecting species suggests that infection of ovarian tissue may have arisen independently in different Myxobolus lineages or may represent a trait that has been gained or lost during the evolutionary history of the group. The separated phylogenetic placement of M. binotati n. sp. together with its morphological, morphometric, and molecular characteristics, supports its recognition as a distinct species.

The present phylogenetic analysis included sequences focused primarily on taxa with relatively high sequence similarity to M. binotati n. sp. including more divergent Myxobolus species would probably reveal more basal branching patterns and relationships within the genus, however it would be indifferent to direct phylogenetic position of M. binotati n. sp. The new species was consistently placed in a similar position in both ML and BI, supporting the reliability of its inferred phylogenetic placement.

The phylogenetic position of M. binotati n. sp. also appears not to be strongly associated with geographic origin. Although the analysis included numerous Myxobolus and related myxosporean sequences obtained from freshwater fishes in Malaysia, the new species did not cluster closely with these Malaysian taxa. Instead, several Malaysian Myxobolus species were distributed across different parts of the phylogenetic tree, including species infecting the gills, muscles, kidneys, fins, skin and ovaries. This pattern suggests that geographic proximity does not necessarily correspond to close phylogenetic relationships among Malaysian myxobolids. The distribution of Malaysian taxa across multiple lineages may reflect the high diversity of Myxobolus in freshwater fishes and potentially multiple independent evolutionary lineages occurring within the same geographic region. The distinct predilection of M. binotati n. sp., despite its occurrence in the same geographic region as several other sampled myxobolids, further supports its genetic distinctiveness and suggests that neither geographic origin nor predilection site alone can explain its evolutionary relationships. The discovery of M. binotati n. sp. therefore highlights both the diversity of myxosporeans in the Malaysian ichthyofauna and the importance of investigating non-traditional infection sites, such as reproductive organs, to better understand patterns of host specificity, tissue tropism, and parasite evolution.
