Generalized capillary‐rise models: existence and fast solvers in integral Hölder spaces
Josefa Caballero,Łukasz Płociniczak, Kishin Sadarangani

Abstract
We study a class of nonlinear Volterra integral equations that generalize the classical capillary rise models, allowing for nonsmooth kernels and nonlinearities. To accommodate such generalities, we work in two families of function spaces: spaces with prescribed modulus of continuity and integral Hölder spaces. We establish existence results for solutions within the integral Hölder space framework. Furthermore, we analyze the behavior of linear interpolation in these spaces and provide, for the first time, sharp error estimates, demonstrating their optimality. Building on this foundation, we propose a piecewise linear collocation method tailored to solutions in integral Hölder spaces and prove its convergence. For problems admitting smoother solutions, we develop an efficient spectral collocation scheme based on Legendre nodes. Finally, several numerical experiments illustrate the theoretical results and highlight the performance of the proposed methods.



Keywords:
nonlinear Volterra integral equation, capillary rise equation, collocation scheme, integral Hölder spaces, interpolation

The main and new results presented in this paper can be summarized as follows.

•

Existence theorem for (

•

•

•

According to the authors’ knowledge, our findings are the first ones in the literature that concern the above points. Moreover, devising convergent numerical schemes for approximating non-smooth solutions is usually a challenging task. For it, a careful analysis is always required, since one does not have access to the derivatives that simplify the estimation of the error. A key part of our reasoning involves deriving new interpolation error estimates in

For completeness, we also devise a Legendre spectral collocation scheme to solve (

Therefore, we present a concise framework for collocation schemes that can readily be utilized for nonsmooth and smooth solutions of our nonlinear Volterra equation. Numerical computations verify our claims and, actually, behave even better than our theoretical estimates find.

First, we develop the existence theory for (

2
The integral equation

In this section, we develop the theory of treating the main integral equation in a functional analytical setting.

2.1
Function space preliminaries

We start by stating various definitions that will be needed for our results.

Definition 1
.

A function
\rho:\mathbb{R}_{+}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a
modulus of continuity
when it satisfies the following conditions:

i)

\rho(0)=0
and
\rho(\epsilon)>0
for all
\epsilon>0
,

ii)

\rho
is nondecreasing,

iii)

\rho(\epsilon)
is continuous at
\epsilon=0
.

It is also true that a given continuous function induces some modulus of continuity. However, there is a unique minimal modulus defined as follows.

Definition 2
.

Suppose that
x:[0,1]\mapsto\mathbb{R}
. The
minimal modulus of continuity of

\mathbf{x}
is the function
\omega(x,\cdot):[0,1]\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
given by

\omega(x,\sigma):=\sup\left\{|x(t)-x(s)|:\,|t-s|\leq\sigma\right\}\quad\text{for any}\quad 0\leq\sigma\leq 1.

(4)

Of course, a modulus of continuity of a given continuous function is indeed a modulus of continuity according to the first definition. Nonnegativity and continuity at zero are trivial, whereas monotonicity follows from the fact that taking the supremum over a larger set cannot decrease it. Therefore, both of the above definitions are compatible. We can now define the space of functions with a continuity controlled by
\rho

C_{\rho}[0,1]:=\left\{x:[0,1]\mapsto\mathbb{R}\text{ such that for any }t,s\in[0,1]\text{ and }t\neq s\text{ we have }\frac{|x(t)-x(s)|}{\rho(|t-s|)}<\infty\right\}.

(5)

It is known that if we endow the above with the norm

\|x\|_{\rho}:=|x(0)|+\sup_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t,s\in[0,1]}}{{t\neq s}}}\frac{|x(t)-x(s)|}{\rho(|t-s|)},

(6)

then
C_{\rho}[0,1]
becomes a Banach space. Of course, we have
C_{\rho}[0,1]\subseteq C[0,1]
since for any
x\in C_{\rho}[0,1]
we have

|x(t)-x(t)|\leq\|x\|_{\rho}\;\rho(|t-s|).

(7)

The continuity of
x
follows from the fact that
\rho
is continuous at zero.

Consider the following integral equation of the Volterra type

x(t)=\int_{0}^{t}G(t,s)f(s,x(s))ds,\quad t\in[0,1].

(8)

In what follows, we assume the following regularity of the kernel

G\in C([0,1]^{2}),\quad M_{G}:=\max_{s,t\in[0,1]}|G(s,t)|,\quad G(\cdot,s)\in C_{\rho}[0,1],\quad\|G(\cdot,s)\|_{\rho}\leq K_{G},\quad s\in[0,1],

(H1)

for some constants
M_{G},K_{G}>0
. That is, we assume a rather weak regularity of
G
controlled by a given modulus of continuity
\rho
uniformly with respect to the second variable. Note that the existence of
M_{G}
is automatic, as it follows from the continuity of
G
on
[0,1]^{2}
.

The nonlinearity of the equation
f:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is assumed to satisfy

|f(t,x)-f(t,y)|\leq\phi(|x-y|)\quad\text{for any}\quad t\in[0,1]\quad\text{and}\quad M_{f}:=\sup\left\{|f(t,0)|:\,t\in[0,1]\right\}<\infty,

(H2)

where
\phi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
is nondecreasing.

Remark 1
.

Generally, in various boundary value problems that appear in the literature, the kernel
G
is actually the Green function corresponding to an underlying differential equation. It usually happens that
G(\cdot,s)\in H_{\gamma}[0,1]
with
0<\gamma\leq 1
, that is, it is
\gamma
-H”older continuous. For that space, the corresponding modulus of continuity is
\rho(t)=t^{\gamma}
. However, there are
C_{\rho}[0,1]
spaces that do not arise from the Hölder space.

The main space in which we will look for solutions of (
8
) is a certain generalization of the Hölder space. The functions belonging to that space have an integrable modulus of continuity. In
[
32
,
33
]
the authors considered the following definition.

Definition 3
.

Let
0<\alpha\leq 1
and
\alpha\leq\beta<\infty
. For
0<s\leq 1
put

j_{\alpha,\beta}(x,[0,s]):=\int_{0}^{s}\sigma^{-(\beta+1)}\omega(x,\sigma)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}d\sigma.

(9)

The space
J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
of continuous functions satisfying
j_{\alpha,\beta}(x,[0,1])<\infty
is called the
integral-type Hölder space
. In an analogous way, we defined the space
J_{\alpha,\beta}[a,b]
for arbitrary
a<b
.

It can also be proved that if we introduce the following norm

\|x\|_{\alpha,\beta}=|x(0)|+j_{\alpha,\beta}(x,[0,1])^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}},

(10)

we find that
(J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1],\|\cdot\|_{\alpha,\beta})
is a Banach space.

Remark 2
.

If we define the integral-type Hölder space with
\beta=\infty
by

j_{\alpha,\infty}(x,[0,1]):=\sup\left\{\sigma^{-\alpha}\omega(x,\sigma):0<\sigma\leq 1\right\},

(11)

then, as can be shown
[
33
]
(and also see Corollary
1
below), the corresponding space
J_{\alpha,\infty}
is actually equal to
H_{\alpha}[0,1]
. Moreover, we have

\lim\limits_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]=H_{\alpha}[0,1].

(12)

That is, the Hölder space is recovered as a particular limit of
J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
. This also justifies the name of the space considered.

Remark 3
.

The space
J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
with
0<\alpha\leq\beta\leq\infty
is strictly larger than the Hölder space
H_{\gamma}[0,1]
for any
0<\gamma<1
.

We will also work in the usual Lebesgue spaces
L^{p}[0,1]
with
1\leq p\leq\infty
with the norms denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{p}
.

2.2
Existence of solutions

For the study of (
8
) we define the following nonlinear operator

(Tx)(t):=\int_{0}^{t}G(t,s)f(s,x(s))ds,\quad t\in[0,1].

(13)

The next result provides a bound on the modulus of continuity generated by
Tx
.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that
x\in C[0,1]
, then for
0<\sigma\leq 1
we have

\omega(Tx,\sigma)\leq\left(\phi(\|x\|_{\infty})+M_{f}\right)\left(K_{G}\rho(\sigma)+\sigma M_{G}\right)

(14)

Proof.

We would like to estimate
\omega(Tx,\sigma)
for
0<\sigma\leq 1
. Take
t,\tau\in[0,1]
with
|t-\tau|\leq\sigma
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
t>\tau
. Then by (
13
) and subtraction/addition of the integral of
G(\tau,s)f(s,x(s))
, we have

|(Tx)(t)-(Tx)(\tau)|\leq\int_{0}^{\tau}|G(t,s)-G(\tau,s)||f(s,x(s))|ds+\int_{\tau}^{t}|G(t,s)||f(s,x(s))|ds.

(15)

Furthermore, by the regularity assumption on the kernel (
H1
) we further can deduce

\begin{split}|(Tx)(t)-(Tx)(\tau)|&\leq K_{G}\int_{0}^{\tau}\rho(|t-s|)\left(|f(s,x(s))-f(s,0)|+|f(s,0)|\right)ds\\
&+M_{G}\int_{\tau}^{t}\left(|f(s,x(s))-f(s,0)|+|f(s,0)|\right)ds.\end{split}

Since the modulus of continuity is nondecreasing and again using the regularity of
G
, we obtain

\begin{split}|(Tx)(t)-(Tx)(\tau)|&\leq K_{G}\rho(\sigma)\int_{0}^{\tau}\left(|f(s,x(s))-f(s,0)|+|f(s,0)|\right)ds\\
&+M_{G}\int_{\tau}^{t}\left(|f(s,x(s))-f(s,0)|+|f(s,0)|ds\right).\end{split}

Next, we use assumptions on
f
, that is, (
H2
), to obtain

|(Tx)(t)-(Tx)(\tau)|\leq K_{G}\rho(\sigma)\int_{0}^{\tau}\left(\phi(|x(s)|)+M_{f}\right)ds+M_{G}\int_{\tau}^{t}\left(\phi(|x(s)|)+M_{f}\right)ds.

(16)

Finally, since
\phi
is nondecreasing, we have

|(Tx)(t)-(Tx)(\tau)|\leq\left(\phi(\|x\|_{\infty})+M_{f}\right)\left(K_{G}\rho(\sigma)\tau+M_{G}|t-\tau|\right).

(17)

But
0\leq\tau\leq 1
and
|t-\tau|\leq\sigma
, therefore we have proved (
14
).
∎

We can see that
Tx
is a uniformly continuous function with a modulus of continuity being the worse of
\rho(\sigma)
(which is inherited from the kernel) and
\sigma
(Lipschitz continuity). The next result states that
T
maps
J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
to itself.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose that
0<\alpha\leq 1
and
\alpha\leq\beta<\infty
. Assume that

\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{-(\beta+1)}\rho(\sigma)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}d\sigma<\infty.

(18)

Then, for any
x\in J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
we have
Tx\in J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
and

\|Tx\|_{\alpha,\beta}\leq\left(\phi(\|x\|_{\alpha,\beta})+M_{f}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{-(\beta+1)}\left(K_{G}\rho(\sigma)+\sigma M_{G}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}d\sigma\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}.

(19)

Proof.

Let
x\in J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
. Using Lemma
1
we estimate
j_{\alpha,\beta}(Tx,[0,1])
as follows

j_{\alpha,\beta}(Tx,[0,1])\leq\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{-(\beta+1)}\omega(Tx,\sigma)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}d\sigma\leq\left(\phi(\|x\|_{\infty})+M_{f}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{-(\beta+1)}\left(K_{G}\rho(\sigma)+\sigma M_{G}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}d\sigma.

(20)

Therefore, by our assumption and the fact that
\|x\|_{\infty}\leq\|x\|_{\alpha,\beta}
, we have

j_{\alpha,\beta}(Tx,[0,1])\leq\left(\max\{K_{G},M_{G}\}\left(\phi(\|x\|_{\alpha,\beta})+M_{f}\right)\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{-(\beta+1)}\left(\rho(\sigma)+\sigma\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}d\sigma

(21)

Now, due to the Hölder inequality
(a+b)^{\gamma}\leq 2^{\gamma-1}(a^{\gamma}+b^{\gamma})
for
\gamma\geq 1
, we have for
\beta/\alpha\geq 1

\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{-(\beta+1)}\left(\rho(\sigma)+\sigma\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}d\sigma\leq 2^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-1}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{-(\beta+1)}\rho(\sigma)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}d\sigma+\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{-(\beta+1)}\sigma^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}d\sigma\right).

(22)

The second integral is convergent since
\beta(1/\alpha-1)-1>-1
, while the first is finite due to our assumption. Hence,
Tx\in J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
with (
20
) being the optimal estimate. Since
Tx(0)=0
, we prove (
19
).
∎

Notice that due to (
H1
) the condition (
18
) means that
G(\cdot,s)\in J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
for each
s\in[0,1]
.

Remark 4
.

The condition (
18
) is satisfied, for example, by
\rho(\sigma)=C\sigma^{\gamma}
with
0<\gamma<\alpha
as a simple integration shows.

Remark 5
.

Suppose that there exists
r_{0}>0
such that

\left(\phi(r_{0})+M_{f}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{-(\beta+1)}\left(K_{G}\rho(\sigma)+\sigma M_{G}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}d\sigma\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}<r_{0}.

(23)

Then,
T(B_{r_{0}}^{\alpha,\beta})\subseteq B_{r_{0}}^{\alpha,\beta}
, where
B_{r_{0}}^{\alpha,\beta}
is the ball in
J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
centered at zero with radius
r_{0}>0
. This follows from (
19
) and the fact that
\phi
is nondecreasing.

To show the existence of a solution to the equation (
8
) in
J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
, we will use the technique of using the measure of noncompactness.

Definition 4
.

The
Hausdorff measure of noncompactness

\chi
of a bounded set
X
in a Banach space
B
is defined by

\chi(M):=\inf\left\{\epsilon:\text{ X admits a finite }\epsilon-\text{net in }X\right\}.

(24)

Observe that, due to the definition, if
\chi(X)=0
then
X
is relatively compact in the Banach space
B
. Moreover, as can be easily seen, in general it may be very difficult to obtain a explicit formula for the measure of noncompactness. In practice, one usually looks for bounds of
\chi
. The following is very useful in our setting.

Proposition 2

(
[
32
]
, Proposition 6.3)
.

For any bounded set
X
in
J_{\alpha,\beta}
define

c(X)=\limsup\limits_{s\rightarrow 0}\left(\sup_{x\in X}j_{\alpha,\beta}(x,[0,s])\right).

(25)

Then, the Hausdorff measure of noncompactness
\chi(X)
is related with
c(X)
according to

2^{-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\chi(X)\leq c(x)\leq 2^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\chi(X).

(26)

Having these devices, we are ready to find the measure of noncompactness of a ball in
J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
.

Proposition 3
.

Assume (
18
) and suppose that there exists
r_{0}>0
such that (
23
) holds. Then, for any
X\subseteq B_{r_{0}}^{\alpha,\beta}
we have
c(TX)=0
.

Proof.

Let
x\in B_{r_{0}}^{\alpha,\beta}
. By Lemma
1
and the same estimates as in Proposition
1
we have

\begin{split}j_{\alpha,\beta}&(Tx,[0,1])\leq\left(\max\{K_{G},M_{G}\}\left(\phi(\|x\|_{\alpha,\beta})+M_{f}\right)\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{s}\sigma^{-(\beta+1)}\left(\rho(\sigma)+\sigma\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}d\sigma\\
&\leq 2^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-1}\left(\max\{K_{G},M_{G}\}\left(\phi(\|x\|_{\alpha,\beta})+M_{f}\right)\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\sigma^{-(\beta+1)}\rho(\sigma)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}d\sigma+\int_{0}^{s}\sigma^{-(\beta+1)}\sigma^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}d\sigma\right).\end{split}

By assumption (
18
) the first integral is convergent, hence vanishing as
s\rightarrow 0^{+}
. On the other hand, the second one can be computed explicitly

\int_{0}^{s}\sigma^{-(\beta+1)}\sigma^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}d\sigma=\frac{s^{\beta\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}-1\right)}}{\beta\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}-1\right)}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as}\quad s\rightarrow 0^{+}.

(27)

Therefore,

\begin{split}c(TB_{r_{0}}^{\alpha,\beta})&=\limsup\limits_{s\rightarrow 0}\left(\sup_{x\in B_{r_{0}}^{\alpha,\beta}}j_{\alpha,\beta}(Tx,[0,s])\right)\leq 2^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-1}\left(\max\{K_{G},M_{G}\}\left(\phi(\|x\|_{\alpha,\beta})+M_{f}\right)\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\\
&\times\left(\int_{0}^{s}\sigma^{-(\beta+1)}\rho(\sigma)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}d\sigma+\frac{s^{\beta\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}-1\right)}}{\beta\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}-1\right)}\right)\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as}\quad s\rightarrow 0^{+}.\end{split}

Therefore,
c(TX)=0
for any
X\subseteq B_{r_{0}}^{\alpha,\beta}
. The proof is complete.
∎

We can now proceed to the main result regarding the existence.

Theorem 1
.

Assume (
18
) and suppose that there exists
r_{0}>0
satisfying (
23
). Then, the integral equation (
8
) has a solution in
J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
with
0<\alpha<1
and
\alpha\leq\beta<\infty
.

Proof.

The proofs relies on invoking Schauder’s fixed point theorem for the operator
T
defined in (
13
). By our assumption and Remark
5
we know that
T
maps a bounded set
B_{r_{0}}^{\alpha,\beta}
into itself. Moreover, Proposition
3
states that
\chi(TB_{r_{0}}^{\alpha,\beta})=0
, which implies that
TB_{r_{0}}^{\alpha,\beta}
is relatively compact in
J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
. We are left to show that
T:B_{r_{0}}^{\alpha,\beta}\mapsto B_{r_{0}}^{\alpha,\beta}
is continuous. To this end, take a sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n}\subseteq B_{r_{0}}^{\alpha,\beta}
convergent to some
x\in\overline{B_{r_{0}}^{\alpha,\beta}}
in the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\alpha,\beta}
. We will show that
Tx_{n}\rightarrow Tx
in
J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
. Let
t,\tau\in[0,1]
with
\tau<t
and
|t-\tau|\leq\sigma
. By the same estimates as in the proof of Lemma
1
we obtain

\begin{split}|(Tx_{n})(t)-(Tx)(t)&-\left((Tx_{n})(\tau)-(Tx)(\tau)\right)|\leq\int_{0}^{\tau}|G(t,s)-G(\tau,s)||f(s,x_{n}(s))-f(s,x(s))|ds\\
&+\int_{\tau}^{t}|G(t,s)||f(s,x_{n}(s))-f(s,x(s))|=:I_{1}+I_{2}.\end{split}

Taking into account the regularity of
G
and
f
as in (
H1
) and (
H2
) we obtain a bound for the first integral

I_{1}\leq K_{G}\int_{0}^{\tau}\rho(|t-s|)\phi(\|x_{n}-x\|_{\infty})ds\leq K_{G}\rho(\sigma)\phi(\|x_{n}-x\|_{\alpha,\beta})\tau\leq K_{G}\rho(\sigma)\phi(\|x_{n}-x\|_{\alpha,\beta})

(28)

Similarly, the second integral can be estimated as follows

\begin{split}I_{2}&\leq\int_{\tau}^{t}|G(t,s)|\phi(\|x_{n}-x\|_{\alpha,\beta})ds\leq K_{G}\,\phi(\|x_{n}-x\|_{\alpha,\beta})(t-\tau)\\
&\leq M_{G}\phi(\|x_{n}-x\|_{\alpha,\beta})\sigma.\end{split}

Therefore, by the definition of the modulus of continuity, we have

\omega(Tx_{n}-Tx,\sigma)\leq\phi(\|x_{n}-x\|_{\alpha,\beta})\left(K_{G}\rho(\sigma)+M_{G}\sigma\right).

(29)

Now, plugging the above into the definition of the
\|\cdot\|_{\alpha,\beta}
norm we obtain

\|Tx_{n}-Tx\|_{\alpha,\beta}\leq\phi(\|x_{n}-x\|_{\alpha,\beta})\left(\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{-(\beta+1)}\left(K_{G}\rho(\sigma)+M_{G}\sigma\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}d\sigma\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}.

(30)

Using the Hölder inequality
(a+b)^{\gamma}\leq 2^{\gamma-1}(a^{\gamma}+b^{\gamma})
with
\gamma\geq 1
as in the proofs of the above results, we see that our assumption (
18
) implies that

\|Tx_{n}-Tx\|_{\alpha,\beta}\leq C_{G,\rho,\alpha,\beta}\phi(\|x_{n}-x\|_{\alpha,\beta}),

(31)

for some constant
C_{G,\rho,\alpha,\beta}>0
. Therefore, passing to the limit with
n\rightarrow\infty
and using the continuity of
\phi
at zero, we conclude that
Tx_{n}\rightarrow Tx
in
J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
. Hence,
T
is continuous. All hypotheses of Schauder fixed point theorem are then satisfied, therefore, there exists a fixed point of the operator
T
. The integral equation (
8
) then has a solution.
∎

Example.

For the capillary rise equation (
2
) we have

G(t,s)=1-e^{-(t-s)},\quad f(s,x)=1-\sqrt{2x}.

(32)

Therefore, since
G
is Lipschitz while
f
is
1/2
-Hölder continuous, we have

M_{G}=1,\quad K_{G}=1,\quad M_{f}=1,\quad\rho(t)=t,\quad\phi(t)=\sqrt{2t}.

(33)

Hence, after computing an elementary integral, the condition (
23
) becomes

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2r_{0}}}+\frac{1}{r_{0}}<\frac{1}{2}\left(\beta\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}-1\right)\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}.

(34)

Since the left-hand side decreases in
r_{0}
, for any fixed
0<\alpha\leq\beta
there exists a unique
r_{0}>0
such that the above holds (it can explicitly be found by solving a quadratic). ∎

3
Collocation numerical methods

In this section, our objective is to construct an efficient numerical method to solve (
8
). Due to its versatility, our choice is the colocation scheme, which we split into two families. The first is the normal
collocation method
in which we approximate the solution of (
8
) using a piecewise linear polynomial. This approach is very well suited to solve equations with minimal regularity such as those belonging to the space
J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
as in our case. The advantage is that we do not have to deal with the grid-wise pointwise values of the approximation, but rather we insist that the integral equation is satisfied at the previously chosen set of nodes. However, the difficulty is that we have to be able to provide a reliable estimate for the linear interpolation error in the respective function space. Note that in the case of low regularity, it is not desirable to consider higher-order than linear collocations. Since then, the error constant can become very large, leaving the order of convergence unchanged. On the other hand, when sufficient regularity is provided, it is very useful to implement a spectral collocation method, since it is well known that it can achieve exponential accuracy provided the solution is regular enough
[
46
]
. Having that in mind, in what follows, we present these two approaches to devise an efficient and fast numerical scheme to be used in various situations.

Introduce a grid of nodes that partition the interval
[0,1]

0=t_{0}<t_{1}<...<t_{N}=1,

(35)

where
N\in\mathbb{N}
is the total number of subintervals in
[0,1]
. For example, the important uniform grid is defined by

t_{n}=nh,\quad h=\frac{1}{N},\quad 0\leq n\leq N.

(36)

For the collocation scheme, we construct an approximation of
x
requiring that it satisfies the equation and the initial condition at the nodes. Before we consider specific examples of the collocation schemes, we discuss linear interpolation in our integral Hölder space.

3.1
Linear interpolation in the
J_{\alpha,\beta}
space

In this section, we are working with the uniform grid (
36
), but the generalization to a general discretization can be made very easily. Define the interpolation operator (which is a projection on the space of piecewise linear polynomials)

I_{h}x(t)=\frac{1}{h}\left(x(t_{n+1})(t-t_{n})+x(t_{n})(t_{n+1}-t)\right),\quad t\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}],\quad 0\leq n\leq N-1,\quad x\in C[0,1].

(37)

It is well known that for
C^{2}[0,1]
smooth functions we have
\|I_{h}x-x\|_{\infty}\leq Ch^{2}\|x\|_{C^{2}[0,1]}
, where
C>0
is an explicitly known function. Moreover, in
[
47
]
we have shown that for functions of lower regularity, belonging to
H^{k,\gamma}[0,1]
, that is, having
k=0,1
-th derivative
\gamma
-Hölder continuous with
0<\gamma<1
, we have

\|I_{h}x-x\|_{\infty}\leq Ch^{k+\gamma}\|x\|_{H^{k,\gamma}[0,1]}.

(38)

The proof of convergence of the collocation scheme is based on a proper error decomposition such that the interpolation error estimate can be used. Therefore, we have to find its supremum bound for solutions belonging to the space
J_{\alpha,\beta}
. The following lemma is crucial for this result.

Lemma 2
.

Let
x\in C[a,b]
with
a<b
. Then, for any
\gamma,\delta>1
we have

\|x\|_{\infty}\leq C_{\gamma,\delta}(b-a)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\delta}}\left(\int_{a}^{b}\sigma^{-\gamma}\omega(x,\sigma)^{\delta}d\sigma\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}},

(39)

where

C_{\gamma,\delta}=\left(\frac{\gamma-1}{2^{\gamma-1}-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\left(\frac{1}{1-2^{-\frac{\gamma-1}{\delta-1}}}\right)^{\frac{\delta-1}{\delta}}.

(40)

Proof.

Using a simple translation argument, we can assume that
x=x(t)
is defined on
[0,T]
with
T:=b-a
. If the integral in (
39
) is infinite, then the inequality is trivially satisfied. Assume otherwise and fix
t\in[0,T]
. We must relate the point value of
x(t)
to its integrated value. To this end, we will use the dyadic decomposition technique used in harmonic analysis and constructive approximation theory. It is also closely related to Besov spaces (
[
48
]
, Chapter 2 or
[
49
]
, Chapter 2). Any point in
[0,T]
can be uniquely expressed in the binary system as

t=T\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\epsilon_{k}2^{-k}=:\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}t_{k}\quad\text{where}\quad\epsilon_{k}\in\left\{0,1\right\}.

(41)

Therefore, we can express the value of
x(t)
as a telescoping series on exponentially decreasing intervals

x(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(x(t_{k})-x({t_{k-1}})).

(42)

Hence, by the definition of the modulus of continuity, we have

|x(t)|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\omega(x,t_{k}-t_{k-1})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\omega(x,(b-a)2^{-k}).

(43)

Now, since the modulus of continuity is nondecreasing, we immediately have for
\gamma\neq 1

\begin{split}\int_{t2^{-k}}^{t2^{-k+1}}&\sigma^{-\gamma}\omega(x,\sigma)^{\delta}d\sigma\geq\omega(x,t2^{-k})^{\delta}\int_{t2^{-k}}^{t2^{-k+1}}\sigma^{-\gamma}d\sigma\\
&=t^{1-\gamma}2^{(1-k)(1-\gamma)}\frac{2^{\gamma-1}-1}{\gamma-1}\omega(x,t2^{-k})^{\delta},\end{split}

or

\omega(x,t2^{-k})\leq\left(t^{\gamma-1}2^{(1-k)(\gamma-1)}\frac{\gamma-1}{2^{\gamma-1}-1}\int_{t2^{-k}}^{t2^{-k+1}}\sigma^{-\gamma}\omega(x,\sigma)^{\delta}d\sigma\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}.

(44)

Therefore, from (
43
) we obtain

|x(t)|\leq t^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\delta}}\left(\frac{\gamma-1}{2^{\gamma-1}-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{(1-k)\frac{\gamma-1}{\delta}}\left(\int_{t2^{-k}}^{t2^{-k+1}}\sigma^{-\gamma}\omega(x,\sigma)^{\delta}d\sigma\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}.

(45)

Or, using the Hölder inequality with
p=\delta/(\delta-1)
and
q=\delta
, we further have

|x(t)|\leq t^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\delta}}\left(\frac{\gamma-1}{2^{\gamma-1}-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^{(1-k)\frac{\gamma-1}{\delta-1}}\right)^{\frac{\delta-1}{\delta}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{t2^{-k}}^{t2^{-k+1}}\sigma^{-\gamma}\omega(x,\sigma)^{\delta}d\sigma\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}.

(46)

By explicitly summing both series, we can simplify

|x(t)|\leq t^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\delta}}\left(\frac{\gamma-1}{2^{\gamma-1}-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\left(\frac{1}{1-2^{-\frac{\gamma-1}{\delta-1}}}\right)^{\frac{\delta-1}{\delta}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{-\gamma}\omega(x,\sigma)^{\delta}d\sigma\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}.

(47)

Taking the supremum over
t\in[0,T]
finishes the proof.
∎

Immediately, taking
\gamma=\beta+1
and
\delta=\beta/\alpha
for
0<\alpha\leq\beta
we can see that the following result is true.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose that
x\in J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,T]
. Then,

\|x\|_{\infty}\leq\left(\frac{\beta}{2^{\beta}-1}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}\left(1-2^{-\frac{\beta}{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-1}}\right)^{-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{\beta}}T^{\alpha}\|x\|_{\alpha,\beta}.

(48)

Moreover, for
\beta\rightarrow\infty
we obtain

\|x\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{T^{\alpha}}{2^{\alpha}-1}\sup_{0<\sigma\leq T}\frac{\omega(x,\sigma)}{\sigma^{\alpha}}.

(49)

Proof.

We only have to show that in the limit
\beta\rightarrow\infty
the bound becomes (
49
). By simple rules of calculus, the prefactor is simply

\underbrace{\left(\frac{\beta}{2^{\beta}-1}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}}_{\rightarrow 2^{-\alpha}}\underbrace{\left(1-2^{-\frac{\beta}{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-1}}\right)^{-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{\beta}}}_{\rightarrow(1-2^{-\alpha})^{-1}}.

(50)

Now, write the norm as

\|x\|_{\alpha,\beta}=\left(\int_{0}^{T}\sigma^{-1}\left(\frac{\omega(x,\sigma)}{\sigma^{\alpha}}\right)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}d\sigma\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}},

(51)

and set
p:=\beta/\alpha
. Then, the above is the
p
-th norm of
\omega(x,\sigma)/\sigma^{\alpha}
in a weighted Lebesgue space
L^{p}(\mu)
with
d\mu:=\sigma^{-1}d\sigma
. Because
\alpha
is fixed, we have
p\rightarrow\infty
when
\beta\rightarrow\infty
. Hence, due to the well-known results in passing from
L^{p}(\mu)
to
L^{\infty}(\mu)
, we obtain

\|x\|_{\alpha,\beta}=\left\|\frac{\omega(x,\cdot)}{(\cdot)^{\alpha}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mu)}\longrightarrow\left\|\frac{\omega(x,\cdot)}{(\cdot)^{\alpha}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu)}\quad\text{as}\quad p\longrightarrow\infty,

(52)

which is equivalent to (
49
) after taking into account the limit of the prefactor. The proof is complete.
∎

Now, we can formulate our result on the error of interpolation in the
J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
space.

Proposition 4
.

Let
x\in J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
. Then, for its linear interpolation (
37
) and
0<\alpha\leq\beta
we have

\|x-I_{h}x\|_{\infty}\leq\left(\frac{\beta}{2^{\beta}-1}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}\left(1-2^{-\frac{\beta}{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-1}}\right)^{-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{\beta}}h^{\alpha}\left(\int_{0}^{h}\sigma^{-\beta-1}\omega(x,\sigma)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}},

(53)

while for
\beta\rightarrow\infty
we obtain

\|x-I_{h}x\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{h^{\alpha}}{2^{\alpha}-1}\sup_{0<\sigma\leq h}\frac{\omega(x,\sigma)}{\sigma^{\alpha}}.

(54)

Proof.

Take
t\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
for
0\leq n\leq N-1
and notice that since
x(t)=(t-t_{n})x(t)+(t_{n+1}-t)x(t)
from (
37
) we can write

\begin{split}|x(t)-I_{h}x(t)|&=h^{-1}\left|(t-t_{n})(x(t)-x(t_{n+1}))+(t_{n+1}-t)(x(t)-x(t_{n}))\right|\\
&\leq 2\max\{|x(t)-x(t_{n+1})|,|x(t)-x(t_{n})|\}.\end{split}

But the modulus of continuity is not changed when we subtract a constant from the function, hence we can use Corollary
1
to obtain

|x(t)-I_{h}x(t)|\leq\left(\frac{\beta}{2^{\beta}-1}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}\left(1-2^{-\frac{\beta}{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-1}}\right)^{-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{\beta}}h^{\alpha}\left(\int_{0}^{h}\sigma^{-\beta-1}\omega(x,\sigma)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}.

(55)

Since this is true for any subinterval
[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
we conclude that it also holds for the supremum taken on the left-hand side. The proof is complete.
∎

Example.

Take a
\gamma
-Hölder function with
0<\alpha<\gamma<1
. Then
\omega(x,\sigma)=L\sigma^{\gamma}
. By simple computations we have

\begin{split}\|x-I_{h}x\|_{\infty}&\leq L\left(\frac{\beta}{2^{\beta}-1}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}\left(1-2^{-\frac{\beta}{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-1}}\right)^{-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{\beta}}h^{\alpha}\left(\frac{h^{\beta(\gamma/\alpha-1)}}{\beta(\frac{\gamma}{\alpha}-1)}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}\\
&\leq L\left(2^{\beta}-1\right)^{-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}\left(1-2^{-\frac{\beta}{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-1}}\right)^{-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{\beta}}\left(\frac{\gamma}{\alpha}-1\right)^{-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}h^{\gamma}.\end{split}

Which is the correct order for the error of the linear interpolation for Hölder functions (see
[
50
]
). Note also that for
\beta\rightarrow\infty
we can even take
\gamma=\alpha
, which further corresponds to the usual results for linear interpolation. That is to say, having limited smoothness, we cannot get past the order of regularity of the interpolated function.

3.2
Linear collocation scheme

Now, we are ready to set up the linear collocation scheme. According to the definition of the method, we require that the integral equation (
8
) be satisfied at the nodes of our discretization (
35
). By
x_{h}
define the
continuous piecewise linear
approximation to
x
. The collocation conditions are, thus, the following

\begin{cases}x_{h}\text{ is a linear function on each }[t_{n},t_{n+1}],&0\leq n<N,\\
x_{h}(t_{n}^{+})=x_{h}(t_{n+1}^{-}),&0<n<N,\\
x_{h}(t_{n})=(Tx_{h})(t_{n}),&0\leq n\leq N,\\
\end{cases}

(56)

where the operator
T
is defined in (
13
). The above defines
(N-1)+(N+1)=2N
conditions. Since we have
N
subintervals and on each the approximation is linear, we have
N\times 2=2N
coefficients to find. Therefore, the number of unknowns is the same as the number of equations. To devise a convenient form for the implementation, it is natural to introduce the
Lagrange basis
(tent functions)

\begin{split}L_{n}(t)&:=\begin{cases}1-\dfrac{|t-t_{n}|}{h},&t\in[t_{n-1},t_{n+1}],\\
0,&\text{elsewhere},\end{cases}\quad 0<n<N,\\
L_{0}(t)&:=\begin{cases}\dfrac{t_{1}-t}{h},&t\in[t_{0},t_{1}],\\
0,&\text{elsewhere},\end{cases}\quad\quad L_{N}(t):=\begin{cases}\dfrac{t-t_{N-1}}{h},&t\in[t_{N-1},t_{N}],\\
0,&\text{elsewhere}.\end{cases}\end{split}

Of course,
L_{n}(x_{m})=\delta_{nm}
and thus the linear interpolation can conveniently be written as

I_{h}x(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{N}x(t_{n})L_{n}(t).

(57)

In particular, expansion of a constant function
x(t)\equiv 1
gives

\left|\sum_{n=0}^{N}L_{n}(t)\right|=1.

(58)

Moreover, any piecewise linear function (not necessarily the interpolant) can be decomposed into a combination of Lagrange functions. In particular, the collocation approximation
x_{h}
. We thus have,

x_{h}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}x_{h}(t_{n})L_{n}(t),\quad t\in[0,1].

(59)

By this and (
8
) we have
x_{h}(t_{0})=0
and

\begin{split}x_{h}(t_{n})&=\int_{0}^{t_{n}}G(t_{n},s)f(s,x_{h}(s))ds=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}G(t_{n},s)f(s,x_{h}(s))ds\\
&=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}G(t_{n},s)f(s,x(t_{i})L_{i}(s)+x(t_{i+1})L_{i+1}(s))ds,\quad 1\leq n\leq N.\end{split}

Therefore, the collocation approximation can be obtained by solving the following nonlinear system

\textbf{x}_{h}=\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{h}),

(60)

where
\textbf{x}_{h}=(x_{h}(t_{n}))_{n=1}^{N}
and
\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{h})=\left(F_{n}(x_{h}(t_{1}),...,x_{h}(t_{n}))\right)_{n=1}^{N}
, where

F_{n}(x_{h}(t_{1}),...,x_{h}(t_{n}))=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+1}}G(t_{n},s)f(s,x(t_{i})L_{i}(s)+x(t_{i+1})L_{i+1}(s))ds.

(61)

Note that, due to our formulation, the continuity conditions are automatically satisfied. Along with the known initial condition
x_{h}(0)=0
we reduce the number of equations from
2N
to
N
. Of course, in each step, we have to discretize the integral appearing in (
61
) with, for example, trapezoid quadrature since it is exact for piecewise polynomial integrands (and having second order for others). As we can see, to compute a value
x_{h}(t_{n})
we have to know all the previous values of the approximation. Therefore, the system (
60
) can be solved iteratively starting with specifying the starting value
x_{h}(0)=0
.

Having defined the linear collocation scheme, we can proceed to prove that it actually converges to the exact solution of (
8
).

Theorem 2

(Convergence of the linear collocation scheme)
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
1
be satisfied. Suppose that
\phi
from (
H2
) satisfies
\phi(x)\leq Lx
with
L>0
. Then, we have

\|x-x_{h}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{G,f}\left(\frac{\beta}{2^{\beta}-1}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}\left(1-2^{-\frac{\beta}{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}-1}}\right)^{-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{\beta}}h^{\alpha}\left(\int_{0}^{h}\sigma^{-\beta-1}\omega(x,\sigma)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}},

(62)

for some constant
C_{G,f}>0
dependent only on
G
and
f
.

Proof.

By the definition of the collocation scheme (
56
) and the interpolation operator (
37
), since
x_{h}
is a piecewise linear polynomial, we have for any
t\in[0,1]

x_{h}(t)=I_{h}x_{h}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}L_{n}(t)x_{h}(t_{n})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}L_{n}(t)Tx_{h}(t_{n})=I_{h}Tx_{h}(t).

(63)

Let
e_{h}:=x-x_{h}
be the error of the scheme. Then, by the above, we have

e_{h}(t)=x(t)-x_{h}(t)=Tx(t)-I_{h}Tx_{h}(t)=Te_{h}(t)+Tx_{h}(t)-I_{h}Tx_{h}(t).

(64)

Therefore, by the definition of the operator
T
as in (
13
) and the regularity assumptions (
H1
)-(
H1
), we have

|e_{h}(t)|\leq K_{G}\int_{0}^{t}\phi(|e_{h}(s)|)ds+\rho_{h}(t),

(65)

where

\rho_{h}(t):=|Tx_{h}(t)-I_{h}Tx_{h}(t)|.

(66)

But, due to the assumption
\phi(|e_{h}(s)|)\leq L|e_{h}(s)|
, and hence

|e_{h}(t)|\leq K_{G}L\int_{0}^{t}|e_{h}(s)|ds+\rho_{h}(t).

(67)

Now, by the classical Gronwall inequality, for some new constant
D_{G,f}
we obtain

|e_{h}(t)|\leq\rho_{h}(t)+D_{G,f}\int_{0}^{t}\rho_{h}(s)ds\leq(1+D_{G,f})\|\rho_{h}\|_{\infty}.

(68)

Hence, setting
C_{G,f}:=1+D_{G,f}
,

\|e_{h}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{G,f}\|\rho_{h}\|_{\infty}.

(69)

But due to Proposition
1
we have
Tx\in J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
, and therefore from Proposition
4
we can obtain the estimate on the interpolation error
\|\rho_{h}\|_{\infty}
. The proof concludes.
∎

The linear collocation scheme thus converges with an error of the same form as the interpolation error. This corresponds to similar results of convergence in more regular and common spaces (see
[
50
]
).

3.3
Spectral collocation scheme

The linear collocation scheme devised in the previous subsection is a versatile method for solving (
8
) in spaces without much regularity. However, it becomes inefficient when the solution is actually very smooth. On the other end of the spectrum of numerical methods we have spectral collocation schemes that are well-suited for such a case. Here, we describe a version of the spectral method based on Lagrange orthogonal polynomials.

Spectral collocation methods use expansions in the basis of orthogonal polynomials. It is well known that, provided sufficient smoothness, their accuracy can be exponential (so-called
spectral accuracy
). Instead of using a uniform grid, we retain the freedom to approximate the unknown solution
x
of (
8
) by an approximation
x^{N}
that we collocate with the equation on an arbitrarily spaced grid (
35
). In contrast to the linear collocation scheme described above, we now would like to collocate a single (and not piecewise) polynomial of degree
N
. To avoid instability, we have to choose a non-uniform grid to be chosen below. That is, the
spectral collocation approximation
is defined by

x^{N}(t_{n})=(Tx^{N})(t_{n}),\quad x^{N}(0)=0,\quad 0<n\leq N,\quad x^{N}\in\mathbb{P}_{N}[0,1].

(70)

To facilitate convergence analysis, we would like to cast this scheme into a unified framework of functional analysis. More specifically, we want to derive a weak form of the above collocation equations.

Since our initial condition vanishes, we will find a spectral approximation of the solution in the following space

V_{N}:=\left\{v\in\mathbb{P}_{N}[0,1]:v(0)=0\right\}\subseteq L^{2}[0,1].

(71)

Any polynomial from the above space can be expanded in the Lagrange basis, analogously as above. However, now instead of using
N
-linear polynomials, we use one of
N
th degree with collocation points
\left\{t_{n}\right\}_{n=0}^{N}
(not necessarily uniform). That is, the
N
-th degree Lagrange basis
is defined by

L^{N}_{n}(t)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i\leq N\\
i\neq n\end{subarray}}\frac{t-t_{i}}{t_{n}-t_{i}}.

(72)

As can also be checked,
L^{N}_{n}(t_{m})=\delta_{nm}
. Hence, we can define the interpolation operator
I^{N}:C[0,1]\mapsto\mathbb{P}_{N}[0,1]

I^{N}y(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}y(t_{n})L^{N}_{n}(t),\quad y\in C[0,1].

(73)

Now, define the
continuous inner product
on by

(x,y):=\int_{0}^{1}x(t)y(t)dt,\quad x,y\in C[0,1],

(74)

and its
discrete version
by

(x,y)_{N}:=\sum_{n=0}^{N}x(t_{n})y(t_{n})w_{n}.

(75)

We immediately see that
(L^{N}_{i},L^{N}_{j})_{N}=\delta_{ij}w_{i}
so that the Lagrange basis is orthogonal with respect to this inner product.

Furthermore, we would like to choose the nodes
t_{n}
and the weights
w_{n}
such that the discrete inner product would agree with the continuous one when acting on a space of polynomials of maximal degree. This ensures spectral accuracy. As is well-known from numerical analysis, these are Gauss-Legendre nodes and weights scaled to the interval
[0,1]
(see
[
46
]
)

\left\{t_{n}\right\}_{n=0}^{N}:=\text{zeros of }P_{N}(2t-1),\quad\left\{w_{n}\right\}_{n=0}^{N}:=\left\{\frac{2}{(1-t_{n}^{2})P^{\prime}_{N}(2t_{n}-1)}\right\}_{n=0}^{N}.

(76)

For which we have

(x,y)_{N}=(x,y),\quad xy\in\mathbb{P}_{2N+1}.

(77)

Here, Legendre polynomials can be obtained via standard recurrence

(n+1)P_{n+1}(t)=(2n+1)P_{n}-nP_{n}(t),\quad P_{0}(x)=1,\quad P_{1}(x)=x.

(78)

Our scheme (
70
) can now be cast in the weak form by noting that
\left\{L^{N}_{n}\right\}_{n=0}^{N}
spans
V_{N}
. The integral operator
T
in (
70
) can be discretized by the Gaussian quadrature
on each
interval
[0,t_{n}]
by choosing appropriate nodes
\{t_{i}^{(n)}\}_{i=0}^{N}
and weights
\{w_{i}^{(n)}\}_{i=0}^{N}
(they are rescaled values of (
76
))

T^{N}x(t_{n})=\sum_{i=0}^{N}G(t,t_{i}^{(n)})f(t_{i}^{(n)},x(t_{i}^{(n)}))w_{i}^{(n)}.

(79)

Therefore, our fully discretized scheme reads

(x^{N},v)_{N}=(T^{N}x^{N},v)_{N},\quad v\in V_{N}.

(80)

The goal of the spectral collocation scheme is to find
x^{N}(t_{n})
with (
76
) such that the above is satisfied. Also, due to the choice of nonuniform nodes we obtain optimal bounds for the interpolation (see
[
46
]
)

\|x-I^{N}x\|_{2}\leq CN^{-m}\|x^{(m)}\|_{2},\quad\|x-I^{N}x\|_{\infty}\leq CN^{\frac{1}{2}-m}\|x^{(m)}\|_{\infty},\quad x\in C^{m}[0,1],

(81)

for which the spectral accuracy is evident. Moreover, the error of the Gaussian quadrature is of exactly the same form

\|Tx-T^{N}x\|_{2}\leq CN^{-m}\|x^{(m)}\|_{2},\quad\|Tx-T^{N}x\|_{\infty}\leq CN^{\frac{1}{2}-m}\|x^{(m)}\|_{\infty}.

(82)

For the implementation, we observe that on our nodes (
76
) we use the discrete operator
T^{N}
defined in (
79
) and interpolate when the sub-node does not belong to
\{t_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
, hence

x^{N}(t_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}G(t_{n},t_{i}^{(n)})f(t_{i}^{(n)},x^{N}(t_{i}))w_{i}^{(n)},\quad 0<n\leq N.

(83)

The above gives a system of
N\times N
algebraic equations for
\{x_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
that can be solved numerically using Newton or fixed-point iterations.

Having built the framework for the spectral collocation scheme, we can proceed to the convergence proof.

Theorem 3
.

Let
x\in C^{m}[0,1]
be a solution of (
8
) with
m>0
and
x^{N}
its spectral collocation approximation defined in (
80
). Then,

\|x-x^{N}\|_{2}\leq C_{G,f}N^{-m}\|x^{(m)}\|_{2},\quad\|x-x^{N}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{G,f}N^{\frac{1}{2}-m}\|x^{(m)}\|_{\infty},

(84)

for some constant
C_{G,f}>0
dependent only on
G
and
f
.

Proof.

Essentially in the same way as in the proof of Theorem
2
we can obtain the pointwise bound for the error
e^{N}:=x-x^{N}

|e^{N}(t)|\leq K_{G}L\int_{0}^{t}|e^{N}(s)|ds+\rho^{N}(t),

(85)

where

\rho^{N}(t):=|I^{N}Tx^{N}(t)-Tx^{N}(t)|+|Tx^{N}(t)-T^{N}x^{N}(t)|.

(86)

Therefore, by the same Gronwall inequality, we obtain

|e^{N}(t)|\leq\rho^{N}(t)+D_{G,f}\int_{0}^{t}\rho^{N}(s)ds.

(87)

For the
L^{\infty}
estimate we just maximize the above to obtain
\|e^{N}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{G,f}\|\rho^{N}\|_{\infty}
, while for the
L^{2}
norm we use Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

|e^{N}(t)|\leq\rho^{N}(t)+D_{G,f}\|\rho^{N}\|_{2}.

(88)

Therefore, there is a constant such that

\|e^{N}\|_{2}\leq C_{G,f}\|\rho^{N}\|_{2}.

(89)

Since, by the assumption,
Tx^{N}
is sufficiently smooth, we can use (
81
) and (
82
) and conclude the proof.
∎

We have thus devised two spectral collocation schemes to solve (
8
): one designed for solution with limited smoothness and the other for smooth ones. In the following, we practically verify our findings.

5
Conclusion

We have introduced and analyzed a class of non-linear Volterra equations generalizing capillary rise models with only continuity hypotheses on the kernel and non-linearity. Using spaces with prescribed modulus of continuity and integral type Hölder spaces, we proved the existence of solutions in
J_{\alpha,\beta}[0,1]
, derived the first sharp interpolation error bounds in these norms, and established convergence of a piecewise‐linear collocation scheme. For smoother cases, our Legendre-node spectral collocation method achieves exponential convergence. Numerical experiments validate our theoretical findings and demonstrate robustness to low regularity.

Future research includes developing a posteriori estimators and adaptive mesh refinement in the
J_{\alpha,\beta}
setting or in the variable Hölder spaces. Additional directions are the integration of fractional or stochastic formulations and the design of fast hierarchical solvers to handle weak singularities with near‐linear complexity. Also, we would like to consider parallel in time integration to make use of the multithreaded possibilities of modern computers.
