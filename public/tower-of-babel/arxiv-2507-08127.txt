Phonon mode splitting and phonon anomaly in multiband electron systems
K. Ziegler

Abstract
We investigate the topological consequences of coupling chiral fermions to local, dispersionless phonons. This interaction induces a splitting of the phonon spectrum into three bands: a flat band and two bands with linear dispersion, all of which are degenerate at a nodal point located at zero wavevector. The flat band exhibits vanishing Berry curvature, while the linearly dispersing bands carry nontrivial topological features. Their Berry curvature fields assume a hedgehog-like structure in momentum space, analogous to monopole configurations, and reflect the chirality of the underlying fermionic system. Moreover, the effective phonon response reveals a phonon parity anomaly, observable as a discontinuity in the phonon current. This anomaly originates from the singularities of the fermion Green’s function and signals the transfer of topological information from fermions to phonons. Our results demonstrate that phonon currents provide a direct probe of electronic chirality and topological structures. The framework offers a foundation for extending topological concepts to interacting bosonic systems and may guide the engineering of phononic materials with tunable geometric and topological properties. Keywords: phonon transport, topological phonons, chiral fermions, Berry curvature, parity anomaly, Chern-Simons theory



I
Introduction

Phonons, the quantized modes of lattice vibrations, are the primary carriers of thermal energy in insulating and
semiconducting solids. Understanding their transport behavior is essential for describing and engineering thermal
conductivity in materials. Traditional approaches to phonon transport rely on the Boltzmann transport
equation
callaway59
;
hardy63
;
mahan90
, where either harmonic or anharmonic lattice models were
studied.

In thermal transport theory, using the Boltzmann transport equation,
the phonon current density
\mathbf{J}_{\text{phonon}}
can be written in the form
hardy63
:

\mathbf{J}_{\text{phonon}}=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{{\bf k},s}\hbar\omega_{{\bf k},s}\,\mathbf{v}_{{\bf k},s}\,\delta f_{{\bf k},s},

(1)

where

\hbar\omega_{{\bf k},s}
is the energy of a phonon with wave vector
{\bf k}
in branch
s

and
\mathbf{v}_{{\bf k},s}
is the group velocity of that phonon.
\delta f_{{\bf k},s}
represents
the deviation of the phonon distribution from equilibrium, often caused by a temperature gradient or other
perturbations. Finally,
V
is the volume of the material.
This expression quantifies how the non-equilibrium distribution of phonons leads to an energy current
across the material. Note that although phonons are not conserved (they can be created or annihilated
in interactions), the energy they carry is what is crucial for processes like thermal conductivity.

Going beyond the classical Boltzmann theory,
intriguing quantum, geometric and topological features of phonons have attracted recent interest.
One prominent example is the anomalous Hall effect for phonons, which was initially observed in an
experiment
strohm05
. Kagan and Maximov studied this effect, which arises due to
spin-phonon interaction
kagan08
.
Another example for the anomalous Hall effect was observed for an ionic crystal lattice
in the presence of a static magnetic field
zhang10
, based on a model with harmonic coupling
holz71
.
It was demonstrated that phonon modes possess a Berry curvature, indicating a geometric origin for a
transverse thermal response.
Other examples of topological phonon effects in different lattice models and materials have been
studied recently, ranging from magnon-phonon systems in honeycomb ferromagnets
thingstad19
,
to graphene and similar lattice materials
singh18
;
liu20
;
li20
;
ding23
;
xi25
.

These insights, however, are limited to specific lattice systems. Alternatively, we can consider a system, where the
phonons couple to electrons. This is central to the foundation of the BCS theory for
superconductivity
bardeen57
and has many applications in condensed matter physics, such as
the Jahn-Teller effect
jahn37
. The coupling to chiral fermions can lead to the splitting of phonon
modes
ziegler11
;
ziegler11a
, to the renormalization of the effective phonon Green’s function
basko08

as well as to the electronic quantum Hall effect
sinner16
;
sinner19
;
sinner20
.
Inspired by these results, we will investigate in the following the phononic properties that emerge due to the
interaction with fermionic degrees of freedom. The primary goal of this paper is to explore mechanisms by which
Dirac-like or topologically nontrivial phonon modes can arise. Specifically, we examine how an initially isotropic
and disordered phonon field may split into multiple, energetically distinct and dispersive phonon modes through
interactions with
fermions. A central question we seek to answer is: what topological or spectral characteristics do phonons
inherit from the fermions they couple to?

The paper is organized as follows: In Sect.
II
the model for electron-phonon interaction is defined.
Based on this model the phonon modes are treated within a functional-integral representation in
Sect.
II.1
, which provides an effective action resembling a Chern-Simons term for 2+1
dimensions. The phonon current is calculated in Sect.
III
and the phonon eigenbasis is
studied in Sect.
III.1
, including a discussion of the corresponding Berry curvatures.
These results are discussed in Sect.
IV
, deriving a phonon anomaly for chiral fermions
(Sect.
IV.1
) and the absence of the phonon anomaly for non-chiral fermions (Sect.
IV.2
).
Sect.
V
concludes the results of this work and gives an outlook to open questions.

II
Model

The phonon Hamiltonian with three directions of the atomic displacements reads

{\hat{H}}_{\rm ph}=\sum_{\bf r}\sum_{\lambda=1}^{3}b^{\dagger}_{{\bf r},\lambda}\omega_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}b_{{\bf r}^{\prime},\lambda}+h.c.

(2)

with bosonic creation and annihilation operators
b^{\dagger}_{\bf r}
and
b_{\bf r}
for a site
{\bf r}

on a two-dimensional lattice. Three independent directions of atomic displacements are considered, motivated by
two in-plane and one out-off-plane modes in a honeycomb lattice
basko08
;
ziegler11
;
ziegler11a
.
The case of two in-plane modes was studied previously
sinner16
;
sinner19
;
sinner19
, while the extension
to the full
SU(4)
group of formal displacements was discussed in a more recent work
sinner20
.
For simplicity, we assume uncorrelated local phonon fluctuations by choosing

\omega_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}=\omega_{0}\delta_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}
.
The hopping Hamiltonian of chiral electrons, created and annihilated by

c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}\mu}
and
c_{{\bf r}\mu}
, with
N
bands reads

{\hat{H}}_{\rm el}=\sum_{{\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime}{}}\sum_{\lambda=1}^{2}\sum_{\mu,\mu^{\prime}{}=1}^{N}c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}\mu}h_{{\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime},\lambda}\gamma_{\lambda;\mu\mu^{\prime}}c_{{\bf r}^{\prime}\mu^{\prime}}+h.c.

(3)

with generalized Pauli matrices
\{\gamma_{\lambda}\}
.
Here we note that a model with
N
bands would be defined by two
N\times N
matrices
\{\gamma_{\lambda}\}
,
since we restrict the following discussion to two spatial dimensions.
Although we do not specify these matrices explicitly, we can always replace them by Pauli matrices for two bands.
This enables us to interpret this model as a tight-binding Hamiltonian on a honeycomb lattice.
The hopping terms determine the behavior of the Hamiltonian under parity transformation.
We assume that at least some are odd under parity transformation:
h_{{\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime},\lambda}=-h_{{\bf r}^{\prime}-{\bf r},\lambda}
.
Finally, the Hamiltonian of electron-phonon Holstein coupling
holstein59
is

{\hat{H}}_{\rm el-ph}=g\sum_{{\bf r}}\sum_{\lambda=1}^{3}\sum_{\mu,\mu^{\prime}=1}^{N}Q_{{\bf r},\lambda}c^{\dagger}_{{\bf r}\mu}\gamma_{\lambda;\mu\mu^{\prime}}c_{{\bf r}\mu^{\prime}}+h.c.

(4)

with the phonon field
Q_{{\bf r},\lambda}=b^{\dagger}_{{\bf r},\lambda}+b_{{\bf r},\lambda}
.

g
is the electron-phonon coupling strength, where
g=0
decouples phonons from electrons.
A third matrix
\gamma_{3}
appears in the interaction with the displacement
Q_{{\bf r},3}
,
representing an out-of-plane displacement in the case of graphene
ziegler11
.
In the following we consider the electron-phonon system that is described by the Hamiltonian

{\hat{H}}={\hat{H}}_{\rm ph}+{\hat{H}}_{\rm el}+{\hat{H}}_{\rm el-ph}
.
For the subsequent calculations it is convenient to employ the functional-integral representation
of the partition function, which will be discussed in the next section. For instance, the
time-dependent correlation of phonons can be defined as a functional integral.

II.1
Functional-integral representation

We start from the functional integral representation
negele
of the phonon correlator for
{\bf x}=({\bf r},t)

\frac{1}{Z}\int Q_{\bf x}Q_{{\bf x}^{\prime}}\exp(-S){\cal D}[\psi,Q]\ \ {\rm with}\ \ Z=\int\exp(-S){\cal D}[\psi,Q]

(5)

with the covariant action
sinner16
;
sinner19
;
sinner20

S=\int_{\bf x}\left(\omega_{0}Q_{\bf x}^{2}+{\bar{\psi}}_{{\bf x}}i\gamma_{3}\partial_{t}\psi_{{\bf x}}+H_{\rm el-ph}[\psi_{{\bf x}},Q_{{\bf x}}]\right),

(6)

where
{\bar{\psi}}_{\bf x}
is the complex conjugate of the Grassmann field

\psi_{\bf x}
.
H_{\rm el-ph}[\psi_{{\bf x}},Q_{{\bf x}}]
is a functional of the fields, obtained from the
Hamiltonian
{\hat{H}}_{\rm el-ph}
, where the fermion operators
c_{\bf r}
,
c^{\dagger}_{\bf r}
are replaced by the
Grassmann fields
\psi_{\bf x}
,
\psi^{*}_{\bf x}
, respectively and the phonon operator is replaced by
a real field
Q_{\bf x}
.

After integrating over the Grassmann field and rescaling
Q_{\bf x}\to gQ_{\bf x}

we get an effective action for the phonon distribution as

S_{Q}=\frac{\omega_{0}}{g^{2}}\sum_{\mu=1}^{3}\int_{\bf x}Q_{{\bf x},\mu}^{2}+\int_{\bf x}Tr_{N}\log G^{-1}

(7)

with the inverse fermion Green’s function

G^{-1}=(h_{1}+Q_{1})\gamma_{1}+(h_{2}+Q_{2})\gamma_{2}+(i\partial_{t}+Q_{3})\gamma_{3}+im\gamma_{0}
.
The scalar parameter
im
serves as an imaginary energy term that regularizes the Green’s function.
In principle, it can be sent to zero in the end of the calculation. We will see later that it plays a crucial
role in terms of a phonon anomaly.
The structure of
G^{-1}
resembles the Dirac operator with gauge field
Q_{\mu}

for Dirac matrices
\{\gamma_{\nu}\}
and unit matrix
\gamma_{0}

adler69
;
redlich84
;
witten16
.
In particular, for 2+1 dimensional chiral (Weyl) fermions the
\{\gamma_{\nu}\}
are Pauli matrices.
The first integral describes uncorrelated random phonons, the second integral the electron-phonon
coupling.

Next we search for stationary solutions of the saddle-point equation
\delta_{Q_{\nu}}S_{Q}=0
.
An extended discussion of possible solutions was given in Ref.
sinner20
.
We will not repeat the calculation for specific solutions here but discuss the general properties of such solutions.
Assuming that
\gamma_{\nu}\gamma_{\nu^{\prime}}+\gamma_{\nu}\gamma_{\nu^{\prime}}=0
for
\nu^{\prime}\neq\nu
,
there is always a solution
{\bar{Q}}=0
, whose validity is justified by the stability of the fluctuations

q_{\bf x}=Q_{\bf x}-{\bar{Q}}
.
If this solution is unstable, though, we must search for
{\bar{Q}}\neq 0
.
For the following we consider small values of
g
to stay in the stable regime for fluctuations around
{\bar{Q}}_{\nu}=0
.
Once we have found a saddle point
{\bar{Q}}
,
we expand
S_{Q}
around it up to second order in the fluctuating field
q_{\bf x}
:

S_{Q}=S_{{\bar{Q}}}+\frac{\omega_{0}}{g^{2}}\sum_{\mu}\int_{\bf x}q_{{\bf x}\mu}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\bf x}\int_{{\bf x}^{\prime}}Tr_{N}(q_{\bf x}G_{{\bf x}-{\bf x}^{\prime}}q_{{\bf x}^{\prime}}G_{{\bf x}^{\prime}-{\bf x}})+...\ ,

(8)

where the first order term in
q_{\bf x}
vanishes due to the saddle point equation.
Then we expand
q_{\bf x}

in terms of
\{\gamma_{\mu}\}_{\mu=1,2,3}
and the unit matrix
\gamma_{0}
as

q_{\bf x}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{3}q_{{\bf x}\mu}\gamma_{\mu}\ ,\ \ G_{{\bar{\bf x}}}=\sum_{\nu=0}^{3}G_{{\bar{\bf x}}\nu}\gamma_{\nu},

(9)

where only the Green’s function includes
\nu=0
.
After inserting this into
S_{Q}
, the leading term for the long-range behavior
is the linear differential term (cf. App.
A
):

S_{1}=\frac{\omega_{0}}{g^{2}}\sum_{\mu}\int_{\bf x}q_{{\bf x}\mu}^{2}+\sum_{\mu,\mu^{\prime}}D_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}\int_{\bf x}q_{{\bf x}\mu}q_{{\bf x}\mu^{\prime}}+\sum_{\lambda,\mu,\mu^{\prime}=1}^{3}\Gamma_{\lambda;\mu\mu^{\prime}}\int_{\bf x}q_{{\bf x}\mu}\frac{\partial q_{{\bf x}\mu^{\prime}}}{\partial x_{\lambda}}

(10)

with the tensors

D_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}=\frac{\omega_{0}}{g^{2}}\delta_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}=0}^{3}\int_{\bf k}{\tilde{G}}_{{\bf k}\nu}{\tilde{G}}_{{\bf k}\nu^{\prime}}Tr_{N}(\gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{\nu}\gamma_{\mu^{\prime}}\gamma_{\nu^{\prime}})\ ,\ \ \Gamma_{\lambda;\mu\mu^{\prime}}=\sum_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}=0}^{3}C_{\lambda;\nu,\nu^{\prime}}\tau_{\mu\nu\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}},

(11)

where the second tensor consists of (cf. Eq. (
43
))

C_{\lambda;\nu,\nu^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\bf x}x_{\lambda}G_{{{\bf x}}\nu}G_{-{{\bf x}}\nu^{\prime}}=\frac{i}{4}\int_{\bf k}{\tilde{G}}_{{\bf k}\nu}\frac{{\partial{\tilde{G}}_{{\bf k}\nu^{\prime}}}}{\partial k_{\lambda}}\ ,\ \ \tau_{\mu\nu\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}=Tr_{N}(\gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{\nu}\gamma_{\mu^{\prime}}\gamma_{\nu^{\prime}}-\gamma_{\mu^{\prime}}\gamma_{\nu}\gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{\nu^{\prime}}).

(12)

We note that
\tau_{\mu\nu\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}=-\tau_{\mu^{\prime}\nu\mu\nu^{\prime}}
, which implies that
\Gamma_{\lambda;\mu\mu^{\prime}}

is antisymmetric in
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
.
This property removes the boundary terms of the partial integration in Eq. (
10
) due to

\sum_{\mu,\mu^{\prime}=1}^{3}\tau_{\mu\nu\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}q_{{\bf x}\mu^{\prime}}q_{{\bf x}\mu}\Big|_{\rm boundary}=0.

(13)

Moreover, in the spatial integral of
C_{\lambda;\nu\nu^{\prime}}
in Eq. (
12
) the integrand
must have an even parity for a non-vanishing integral, which requires that the product

G_{{{\bf x}}\nu}G_{-{{\bf x}}\nu^{\prime}}
must be odd under parity transformation. Thus, the Green’s function

G
must consist of a sum of terms with odd and even parity.
Finally, we
assume that
D_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}={\bar{D}}\delta_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}
, which is justified for the examples we will
discuss in Sect.
IV
.

The kernel of the quadratic form in Eq. (
10
) can be understood as an
inverse phonon Green’s function

{\cal K}^{-1}_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}=\Omega_{0}\delta_{\mu\mu^{\prime}}+\sum_{\lambda=1}^{3}\Gamma_{\lambda;\mu\mu^{\prime}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\lambda}}\ ,\ \ \Omega_{0}=\frac{\omega_{0}}{g^{2}}-{\bar{D}},

(14)

where the second term resembles an inverse chiral (Weyl) Green’s function, whose Pauli matrices are replaced by the

3\times 3
matrices
\{\Gamma_{\lambda}\}
.
Due to the antisymmetry there are only three independent tensor elements for each value of
\lambda
.
This suggests the linear mapping in coordinate space

\cases{\partial_{x^{\prime}_{1}}=-\sum_{\lambda}\Gamma_{\lambda;23}\partial_{x_{\lambda}}\cr\partial_{x^{\prime}_{2}}=\sum_{\lambda}\Gamma_{\lambda;13}\partial_{x_{\lambda}}\cr\partial_{x^{\prime}_{3}}=-\sum_{\lambda}\Gamma_{\lambda;12}\partial_{x_{\lambda}}\cr}

(15)

such that we get for the second term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
14
)

\sum_{\lambda=1}^{3}\Gamma_{\lambda;\mu\mu^{\prime}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\lambda}}=\sum_{\lambda=1}^{3}\epsilon_{\mu\lambda\mu^{\prime}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}_{\lambda}}.

(16)

This relation indicates that the phonon Green’s function can always be mapped onto a conventional
2+1 dimensional Chern-Simons term
witten16
, where the map itself carries the information about the fermion
system, to which the phonons are coupled. Thus, we have separated the properties of the fermion part
from the phonon part through the map in Eq. (
15
), comprising the parameters

\Gamma_{\lambda;12}
,
\Gamma_{\lambda;13}
and
\Gamma_{\lambda;23}
.
Besides this map, the inverse phonon Green’s function is simply a conventional Chern-Simons term.

III
Phonon currents

Nonequilibrium phonon modes create phonon currents. These currents are calculated,
in analogy with the gauge theory
witten16
,
from the variation of the action in Eq. (
10
)
as the linear response to the gradient of the phonon field:

j_{\mu}=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{q_{{\bf x}\mu}}S_{1}=\Omega_{0}q_{{\bf x}\mu}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{q_{{\bf x}\mu}}\sum_{\lambda,\mu,\mu^{\prime}=1}^{3}\Gamma_{\lambda;\mu\mu^{\prime}}\int_{\bf x}q_{{\bf x}\mu}\frac{\partial q_{{\bf x}\mu^{\prime}}}{\partial x_{\lambda}}=\Omega_{0}q_{{\bf x}\mu}+\sum_{\lambda,\mu^{\prime}=1}^{3}\Gamma_{\lambda;\mu\mu^{\prime}}\frac{\partial q_{{\bf x}\mu^{\prime}}}{\partial x_{\lambda}}

(17)

and after the coordinate transformation

j_{\mu}=\Omega_{0}q_{{\bf x}\mu}+\sum_{\lambda,\mu^{\prime}=1}^{3}\epsilon_{\mu\lambda\mu^{\prime}}\frac{\partial q_{{\bf x}\mu^{\prime}}}{\partial x^{\prime}_{\lambda}}.

(18)

Thus, we have a longitudinal current proportional to
\Omega_{0}
and a universal transverse current, which
is induced by the gradient of the phonon field. Both terms vanish when
q_{{\bf x}\mu}
is a uniform equilibrium
phonon mode, since
q_{{\bf x}\mu}=0
in this case. For
q_{{\bf x}\mu}\neq 0
, on the other hand, we can create
longitudinal as well as transverse currents simultaneously.

Alternatively, we define the phonon current operator through the inverse phonon Green’s function as

{\bf j}_{\lambda}=i[-i{\cal K}^{-1},x_{\lambda}]=\Gamma_{\lambda}.

(19)

The phonon current expectation values with respect to a phonon field
{\vec{\psi}}=(\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\psi_{3})^{T}

and
{\vec{\Gamma}}_{\lambda}=(\Gamma_{\lambda;23},-\Gamma_{\lambda;13},\Gamma_{\lambda;12})^{T}
read

({\vec{\psi}}\cdot{\bf j}_{\lambda}{\vec{\psi}})={\vec{\Gamma}}_{\lambda}\cdot({\vec{\psi}}^{*}\times{\vec{\psi}}).

(20)

The corresponding fermion current operator reads in Fourier representation

{\bf J}_{\lambda}=[{\tilde{H}}_{{\bf k}},\partial_{k_{\lambda}}]=\sum_{\mu=1}^{3}\frac{\partial h_{{\bf k},\mu}}{\partial k_{\lambda}}\gamma_{\mu}\ ,\ \ {\tilde{H}}_{{\bf k}}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{3}{\tilde{h}}_{{\bf k},\mu}\gamma_{\mu}.

(21)

While the fermion current is given by the
N\times N
generalized Pauli matrices
\{\gamma_{\mu}\}
,
the corresponding phonon current is related to the antisymmetric
3\times 3
matrices

\{\Gamma_{\lambda}\}
of Eq. (
11
).

III.1
Phonon Hamiltonian eigenbasis

So far, all vectors and tensors are written in terms of the natural basis, which was introduced in the
Hamiltonians in Eqs. (
2
) – (
4
). Next we study the eigenbasis
of the inverse phonon Green’s function of Eq. (
14
) and its properties.
It is convenient to work in the transformed coordinate space
{\bf x}^{\prime}\equiv(x^{\prime}_{j})
, where we can diagonalize
the expression in Eq. (
16
) after the Fourier transformation
x_{j}^{\prime}\to K_{j}

with the vector

{\vec{K}}=-\sum_{\lambda}k_{\lambda}{\vec{\Gamma}}_{\lambda}
.
Then the eigenvalues of

\pmatrix{\Omega_{0}&-iK_{3}&iK_{2}\cr iK_{3}&\Omega_{0}&-iK_{1}\cr-iK_{2}&iK_{1}&\Omega_{0}\cr}

(22)

are
E_{0}=\Omega_{0}
and
E_{\pm}=\Omega_{0}\pm|{\vec{K}}|

with the corresponding normalized eigenvectors

{\vec{\psi}}_{0}=-\frac{{\vec{K}}}{|{\vec{K}}|}\ ,\ \ \ {\vec{\psi}}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{K_{2}^{2}+K_{3}^{2}}|{\vec{K}}|}\pmatrix{K_{2}^{2}+K_{3}^{2}\cr-K_{1}K_{2}\pm iK_{3}|{\vec{K}}|\cr-K_{1}K_{3}\mp iK_{2}|{\vec{K}}|\cr}.

(23)

Thus, we have a flat band and two bands with a linear dispersion. These three bands are degenerate at
the node
{\vec{K}}=0
(cf. Fig.
1
).
There is no gap as long as the diagonal elements of the inverse phonon Green’s function
are identical, even if the fermion Hamiltonian is gapped.
For these eigenvectors the Berry connections read

-i({\vec{\psi}}_{0}\cdot\nabla_{\vec{K}}{\vec{\psi}}_{0})=0\ ,\ \ -i({\vec{\psi}}_{\pm}\cdot\nabla_{\vec{K}}{\vec{\psi}}_{\pm})=\mp\frac{K_{1}}{|{\vec{K}}|(K_{2}^{2}+K_{3}^{2})}\pmatrix{0\cr K_{3}\cr-K_{2}\cr}.

(24)

While the Berry connection for
{\vec{\psi}}_{0}
vanishes, the Berry connections of
{\vec{\psi}}_{\pm}

represent two opposite vortices in the
K_{2}
–
K_{3}
plane.
The corresponding Berry curvatures of
\psi_{\pm}
are

{\bf B}=-\nabla_{\vec{K}}\times i({\vec{\psi}}_{\pm}\cdot\nabla_{\vec{K}}{\vec{\psi}}_{\pm})=\mp\frac{1}{|{\vec{K}}|^{3}}{{\vec{K}}}=\pm\nabla_{\vec{K}}\frac{1}{|{\vec{K}}|},

(25)

which represent vector fields in the form of hedgehogs for the upper and the lower band with opposite
orientation (Fig.
2
). The divergence
\nabla\cdot{\bf B}=\pm 4\pi\delta^{(3)}({\vec{K}})

reflects that these vector fields represent two Dirac Delta sources with charges
\pm 4\pi

at
{\vec{K}}=0
(Dirac monopoles).
Hedgehog solutions have also been discussed in the context of 2D quantum spin systems
senthil04
;
fradkin13
.
In contrast, the Berry curvature for two-dimensional chiral fermions is
\pi\delta^{(2)}({\bf k})
.
This reflects a mapping from the fermion Berry curvature to the phonon Berry curvature.

Returning to the current operator of Eq. (
20
), the latter is calculated in the eigenbasis of
{\cal K}
.
Since
{\vec{\psi}}_{0}
are real, its current
{\vec{\Gamma}}\cdot({\vec{\psi}}_{0}^{*}\times{\vec{\psi}}_{0})
vanishes.
For the other two eigenvectors we obtain

({\vec{\psi}}_{\pm}\cdot\Gamma_{\lambda}{\vec{\psi}}_{\pm})=\pm i\frac{K_{1}\Gamma_{\lambda;23}-K_{2}\Gamma_{\lambda;13}+K_{3}\Gamma_{\lambda;12}}{|{\vec{K}}|}=\mp\frac{i}{|{\vec{K}}|}\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}}\beta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}k_{\lambda^{\prime}}

(26)

with
\beta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}=\Gamma_{\lambda;12}\Gamma_{\lambda^{\prime};12}+\Gamma_{\lambda;13}\Gamma_{\lambda^{\prime};13}+\Gamma_{\lambda;23}\Gamma_{\lambda^{\prime};23}
.
The quadratic form in terms of the current matrix elements is caused by the specific form of
{\vec{\psi}}_{\pm}

in Eq. (
23
), in contrast to the general linear dependence in Eq. (
20
).

IV
Discussion of the results

The properties of the phonon modes, described by the inverse Green’s function in Eq. (
14
),
are determined by the antisymmetric tensor

\Gamma_{\lambda;\mu\mu^{\prime}}
, defined in Eqs. (
11
) and (
12
).
This raises the question about the very existence of this tensor. And if it exists, what are its properties?
As already mentioned in Sect.
II.1
, its existence is intimately related to the fact that
the fermion Green’s function is neither even nor odd under parity transformation.
We write
G^{-1}=A+imS
, where
A
and
S
are Hermitian, and
A
is odd and
S
is even under parity
transformation.

m
is a real parameter that controls the contribution of the even part and
imS
serves as a regularization
of the Green’s function, which should be sent to 0 at the end of the calculation. After writing

G=(A+imS)^{-1}=[(A-imS)(A+imS)]^{-1}(A-imS)
, we assume that

[A,S]=0
. Then
G
can be split as
G=a-ims
with the odd part
a=(A^{2}+m^{2}S^{2})^{-1}A

and the even part
s=(A^{2}+m^{2}S^{2})^{-1}S
.
The Green’s function is expanded in terms of the matrices
\{\gamma_{\nu}\}

as
G_{\bf x}=\sum_{\nu}G_{{\bf x}\nu}\gamma_{\nu}
.
This gives us with Eq. (
11
) the Hermitian expression

\Gamma_{\lambda;\mu\mu^{\prime}}=-\frac{im}{2}\sum_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}\int_{\bf x}x_{\lambda}(a_{{\bf x}\nu}s_{{\bf x}\nu^{\prime}}+s_{{\bf x}\nu}a_{{\bf x}\nu^{\prime}})\tau_{\mu\nu\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}},

(27)

which switches its sign for
m\to-m
, since the integrand depends only on
m^{2}
.

In the next step we must estimate the
m^{2}
dependence of the integral. This will be studied in terms
of the specific example
a=(H^{2}+m^{2}\gamma_{0})^{-1}H
and
s=(H^{2}+m^{2}\gamma_{0})^{-1}

with the Fourier components
{\tilde{H}}_{{\bf k};\mu\mu^{\prime}}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{3}{\tilde{h}}_{{\bf k},\nu}\gamma_{\nu;\mu\mu^{\prime}}
of the fermion Hamiltonian.
This implies for the integral in Eq. (
12
)

C_{\lambda;\nu\nu^{\prime}}=

\frac{m}{4}\int_{\bf k}\left([(H_{\bf k}^{2}+m^{2}\sigma_{0})^{-1}H_{\bf k}]_{\nu}\partial_{k_{\lambda}}[(H_{\bf k}^{2}+m^{2}\sigma_{0})^{-1}]_{\nu^{\prime}}+[(H_{\bf k}^{2}+m^{2}\sigma_{0})^{-1}]_{\nu}\partial_{k_{\lambda}}[(H_{\bf k}^{2}+m^{2}\sigma_{0})^{-1}H_{\bf k}]_{\nu^{\prime}}\right),

(28)

which reads after a partial integration for the second integral

=\frac{1}{4}m\left\{\int_{\bf k}[(H_{\bf k}^{2}+m^{2}\sigma_{0})^{-1}H_{\bf k}]_{\nu}\partial_{k_{\lambda}}[(H_{\bf k}^{2}+m^{2}\sigma_{0})^{-1}]_{\nu^{\prime}}-(\nu\leftrightarrow\nu^{\prime})\right\}.

(29)

Assuming that
{\tilde{h}}_{{\bf k},\nu}
is linear in
{\bf k}
, there is a single node at
{\bf k}=0
.
Then we absorb the
1/m^{2}
factor by rescaling
{\bf k}\to{\bf k}/|m|=:{\bf p}

V
Conclusion

This work has demonstrated how the interaction between chiral fermions and local, dispersionless phonons
induces nontrivial topological features in the phononic sector. Central to our analysis is a coordinate transformation,
Eq. (
15
), parametrized by the set of coefficients
\Gamma_{\lambda;12}
,
\Gamma_{\lambda;13}
,
and
\Gamma_{\lambda;23}
,
with
\lambda=1,2,3
labeling two spatial and one temporal directions. This transformation enables a clear separation
of fermionic and phononic dynamics, and facilitates the transfer of geometric data—particularly the Berry
curvature—from the electronic to the phononic degrees of freedom.
As a direct outcome of this mapping, the effective phonon Green’s function acquires a Chern-Simons term, provided
the fermion Green’s function breaks parity in a way that renders it neither even nor odd.

\{\Gamma_{\lambda;\mu\mu^{\prime}}\}
encodes topological
characteristics originally present in the fermionic system. On compact manifolds such as the toroidal Brillouin zone,
however, the total Chern-Simons contribution vanishes due to topological cancellation.

The presence of a non-vanishing Chern-Simons term manifests as a phonon parity anomaly—an observable discontinuity
in the phonon current. This anomaly is rooted in the singular structure of the fermionic Green’s function along the real
axis, illustrating a deep connection between fermionic singularities and phononic anomalies.

The phonon spectrum emerging from this interaction consists of three bands: a dispersionless flat band and two bands
with linear dispersion. All three converge at a nodal point at
{\vec{K}}=0
, where the spectrum is degenerate. While the flat
band exhibits vanishing Berry curvature, the upper and lower bands host oppositely signed curvature contributions with
hedgehog-like vector profiles, indicating the formation of effective monopole configurations in momentum space.

These results imply that phonon currents serve as sensitive probes of the chirality and topological features of the
underlying fermionic sector. This phonon-based diagnostic offers a novel route for detecting and characterizing
topological matter in systems where fermionic degrees of freedom are not directly accessible.

Future work could refine this framework by considering specific fermionic Hamiltonians and incorporating phonon
dispersion through spatially dependent couplings
\omega_{{\bf r}{\bf r}^{\prime}}
. Additionally, our results suggest that geometric
and boundary effects—such as edge states and finite-size responses—could play a significant role in shaping observable
phonon transport properties. Such extensions may provide valuable insights for the theoretical model and
the experimental realization of topologically engineered phononic systems.
