The Davenport constant of balls and boxes
Benjamin Girard, Alain Plagne

Abstract
. Given an additively written abelian group G G and a set X ⊆ G X\subseteq G , we let 𝖣 ⁡ ( X ) \mathsf{D}(X) denote the Davenport constant of X X , namely the largest non-negative integer n n for which there exists a sequence x 1 , … , x n x_{1},\dots,x_{n} of elements of X X such that ∑ i = 1 n x i = 0 \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=0 and ∑ i ∈ I x i ≠ 0 \sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\neq 0 for each non-empty proper subset I I of { 1 , … , n } \{1,\ldots,n\} . In this paper, we mainly investigate the case when G G is ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}^{2} and ℤ 3 \mathbb{Z}^{3} , and X X is a discrete Euclidean ball. An application to the classical problem of estimating the Davenport constant of a box – a product of intervals of integers – is then obtained.

1.
Introduction

Let
G
be an additively written abelian group.
Given
X\subseteq G
, we consider the free abelian monoid over
X
.
Its elements will be called sequences.
Let
S=x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
be a sequence of elements of
X
.
We shall say that
the
x_{i}
’s are elements of
S
or, simply, are in
S
(that is, we identify sequences and multisets).
The sequences considered here are unordered.
We call
support
the set of (distinct) elements of
G
appearing in
S
.
We call
n
the
length
of
S
, which we denote by
\|S\|
.
We notice that the support of
S
can be drastically smaller than its length.
Finally a sequence
S=x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
will be called a
zero-sum sequence

whenever
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=0
. A zero-sum sequence will be called
minimal

if
\sum_{i\in I}x_{i}\neq 0
for every non-empty proper subset
I
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

For
G
an abelian group, the study of its minimal zero-sum sequences and their combinatorial properties is part of
what is called
zero-sum theory
, a subfield of additive combinatorics with applications to group theory, graph theory,
Ramsey theory, discrete geometry and factorization theory. Since the 1960s, the theory of these invariants has highly developed in several directions.
The interested reader is referred to the surveys
[
17
,
18
]
or
[
20
]
(see in particular Chapters 5, 6, and 7).

One of the earliest questions in this area, and maybe one of the most important, is concerned with the

Davenport constant
, which has become the prototype of algebraic invariants of combinatorial flavour.
More precisely, the Davenport constant of an abelian group
G
,
denoted by
\mathsf{D}(G)
, is defined as
the maximal length of a minimal zero-sum sequence over
G
.

Assume that our abelian group
G
is finite, and not reduced to
\{0\}
. If we decompose it as a direct sum of cyclic groups

G\simeq C_{n_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n_{r}}
with integers
1<n_{1}\mid\dots\mid n_{r}

(here,
C_{k}
denotes a cyclic group with
k
elements,
r
is the rank of
G
and
n_{r}=\exp G
, the exponent of
G
),
an immediate lower bound for the Davenport constant is

(1)

\mathsf{D}(G)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{r}(n_{i}-1)+1.

This bound cannot be improved in general since it is an equality in the case of
groups of rank at most two and for
p
-groups (with
p
a prime)
[
13
,
27
,
28
]
.
For groups of rank at least four, equality is definitely not the rule, see
[
2
,
13
,
24
]
.
In the case of groups of rank three, it has been conjectured that equality still holds, but this conjecture
remains wide open, see
[
17
]
, and is seemingly difficult.
Concerning upper bounds, the best general result so far is the following:

(2)

\mathsf{D}(G)\leq\left(1+\log\frac{|G|}{\exp G}\right)\exp G,

which is proved in
[
14
]
. We do not know really more than this in general: in spite of so much work related
to the Davenport constant over the years, its actual value has been determined only for a few additional
families of groups beyond the ones for which it was already known by the end of the 1960s.
The general impression is that, although it has a very simple definition,
computing the Davenport constant of a finite abelian group (of rank at least three) is a challenging problem.

The study of certain generalisations of the Davenport constant to subsets of not necessarily finite abelian groups
is of great interest for its applications to factorization theory, where it can be used to describe
the arithmetic of certain monoids, see
[
19
]
and Chapter 3.4 of
[
20
]
.
For instance, in
[
3
]
, Baeth and Geroldinger ask specifically for a study
of the Davenport constant of what we call a box, that is a product of intervals of integers.

Let again
G
be a not necessarily finite abelian group and
X
be any subset of it. We define its Davenport constant,
which we denote by
\mathsf{D}(X)
, as the maximal length of a minimal zero-sum sequence over
X
;
this variant was first introduced by van Emde Boas in
[
13
]
, where it is however denoted by
\mu(G,X)
.
It is trivial but worth remarking that in general, and contrary to the case where
X=G
,
it can happen that
\mathsf{D}(X)
is finite and yet we can build arbitrarily long sequences of elements of
X

with no non-empty zero-sum subsequence.
Also, it is immediate that
\mathsf{D}(X)\leq\mathsf{D}(G)
:
this inequality is in general strict and it is well possible that
\mathsf{D}(X)
is finite while
\mathsf{D}(G)
is not.

Being given
m
and
d
two positive integers, what is called a
box
in what follows is a product of intervals
of the form
\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{d}

(as usual, the notation
\llbracket a,b\rrbracket
(for two real numbers
a\leq b
) stands for
the set, called
interval
, of integers
i
satisfying
a\leq i\leq b
; we shall use this notation or

\{1,\dots,n\}
depending on the context).

The study of the Davenport constant of a box is already documented.
In the case where
d=1
, one has
\mathsf{D}(\llbracket-1,1\rrbracket)=2
and it is almost folklore that

\mathsf{D}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket)=2m-1

for every integer
m\geq 2
(see
[
23
]
for an early proof and
[
11
,
30
,
31
]
for rediscoveries and further development).
When
d=2
, it is known
[
30
]
that
\mathsf{D}(\llbracket-1,1\rrbracket^{2})=4
and that

(3)

(2m-1)^{2}\leq\mathsf{D}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})\leq(2m+1)(4m+1)

for every integer
m\geq 2
,
a slightly better lower bound being obtained in
[
10
]
.
And in general, for
d\geq 3
, the best known bounds
[
30
]
are

(4)

(2m-1)^{d}\leq\mathsf{D}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{d})\leq\left(2\left(d+\frac{1}{d}-1\right)m+1\right)^{d}

for every integer
m\geq 2
.

2.
New results on balls and boxes

The main goal of the present paper is to introduce and study
\mathsf{D}(X)
in the case where

X
is a discrete Euclidean ball (with radius
m
, an integer) of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(
d
being a positive integer),
that is, the following set of integral points:

X=\mathcal{B}^{(d)}_{m}=\{(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:u_{1}^{2}+\cdots+u_{d}^{2}\leq m^{2}\}.

Let
m
be a positive integer. If
m=1
, then
\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{B}^{(d)}_{1})=2
for all
d\geq 1
, since it is easily seen that any minimal zero-sum sequence over
\mathcal{B}^{(d)}_{1}
of length at least
2
must be of the form
u,-u
for some
u\in\mathcal{B}^{(d)}_{1}\setminus\{0\}
. Now, let us assume that
m\geq 2
. In view of the double inclusion

\left\llbracket-\left[\frac{m}{\sqrt{d}}\right],\left[\frac{m}{\sqrt{d}}\right]\right\rrbracket^{d}\subseteq\mathcal{B}^{(d)}_{m}\subseteq\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{d},

(as usual,
[x]
denotes the integral part of a real
x
) we have

\mathsf{D}\left(\left\llbracket-\left[\frac{m}{\sqrt{d}}\right],\left[\frac{m}{\sqrt{d}}\right]\right\rrbracket^{d}\right)\leq\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{B}^{(d)}_{m})\leq\mathsf{D}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{d}),

which, in view of (
4
), gives for
d\geq 3

(5)

\left(2\left[\frac{m}{\sqrt{d}}\right]-1\right)^{d}\leq\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{B}^{(d)}_{m})\leq\left(2\left(d+\frac{1}{d}-1\right)m+1\right)^{d},

which already gives
m^{d}
as the right order of magnitude of
\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{B}^{(d)}_{m})
.
When
d=2
, it follows from (
3
) that

\left(2\left[\frac{m}{\sqrt{2}}\right]-1\right)^{2}\leq\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{B}^{(2)}_{m})\leq(2m+1)(4m+1),

which gives asymptotically, as
m\to+\infty
,

(6)

2\lesssim\frac{\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{B}^{(2)}_{m})}{m^{2}}\lesssim 8.

Here and throughout, whenever two positive sequences
(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(v_{n})_{n\geq 1}

satisfy
\liminf_{n\rightarrow+\infty}v_{n}/u_{n}\geq 1
, we write
u_{n}\lesssim v_{n}
.

We first study more precisely the case of dimension 2 and prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
m\geq 5
be an integer, then

\frac{64}{25}m^{2}-23m+51\leq\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{B}^{(2)}_{m})\leq\frac{5\pi}{4}\left(m+\frac{4}{\sqrt{5}}\right)^{2}.

Moreover, asymptotically as
m\to+\infty
,

\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}m^{2}+O(m^{1.1})\leq\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{B}^{(2)}_{m})\leq\frac{5\pi}{4}m^{2}+O(m).

To have an insight on the quality of these bounds, we compute that
64/25=2.56

and
3\sqrt{3}/2\simeq 2.598\dots
while
5\pi/4\simeq 3.926\dots
which improves on both
sides of (
6
).

For this result, error terms were given. Even though we will not always give them explicitly in the rest of the paper, making the error terms precise will be most of the time possible.

To investigate the Davenport constant, we introduce the quantity

\mathsf{D}^{(k)}(X)

defined in the same way as
\mathsf{D}(X)
, with the additional restriction on the maximum that only sequences
having a support of size
k
are considered.

For the sake of argument, assume the ground set
X\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
to be “sufficiently regular”.
For instance, one could think of
X
as a ball of (a large enough) radius
m
, for some norm over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

On the one hand, it appears likely that a minimal zero-sum sequence of maximal length over
X
should be full-dimensional: for, if
S=(s_{1},0),\dots,(s_{l},0)
is a minimal zero-sum sequence over
X\cap(\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}\times\{0\})
, a small perturbation
\varepsilon_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}
could be found for some
i\in\{1,\dots,l\}
so that
u_{i}=(s_{i}-\varepsilon_{i},1)
and
v_{i}=(\varepsilon_{i},-1)
both belong to
X
. As a result, the new longer sequence over
X
obtained from
S
by deleting
(s_{i},0)
and adding
u_{i}
and
v_{i}
would also be minimal and zero-sum, a contradiction.
We thus expect a minimal zero-sum sequence of maximal length over such an
X
to have a support of size at least
d+1
.
Indeed, seen as vectors of some finite-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{R}
, the elements of a zero-sum sequence
are linearly dependent; this implies that the dimension of the vector space generated by the elements of a zero-sum sequence cannot
be larger than the size of its support minus one.

On the other hand, for statistical reasons, it is likely that the support of a minimal zero-sum sequence over
X
should not be too large.
Indeed, the bigger the support, the bigger the mixing in the sums we can form and
thus the bigger the expected number of distinct sums
(to get a feeling of this philosophy, see for instance related results on subset sums
[
1
,
29
]
).

For these reasons, being able to compute quantities like
\mathsf{D}^{(d+1)}(X)
appears to be interesting.
To the very least, one has

(7)

\mathsf{D}(X)\geq\mathsf{D}^{(d+1)}(X),

this lower bound being hopefully optimal.
For instance, finding a lower bound for
\mathsf{D}^{(3)}(\mathcal{B}^{(2)}_{m})
is precisely how we will obtain the lower bound in Theorem
1
.
In addition, our proof of Theorem
1
can be complemented by proving that our construction of a long minimal zero-sum sequence with
a support of size 3 is asymptotically optimal, so that we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Asymptotically, as
m\to+\infty
,

\mathsf{D}^{(3)}(\mathcal{B}^{(2)}_{m})\sim\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}m^{2}.

An interesting remark here is that (asymptotically) the three vectors forming the support of the sequence leading
to this asymptotic coefficient are the vertices of an equilateral triangle.
This will be discussed later (see Sections
3
and
5
).

Another point of interest of our study of the Davenport constant of discrete Euclidean balls is that
it leads to good upper bounds for the Davenport constant of boxes, for instance in dimension 2,
which was our starting point.

In order to state our first result in this direction, we introduce the following arithmetical function.
For an integer
m\geq 2
, we let
q(m)
denote the smallest prime not dividing
m
.
The following theorem on two-dimensional boxes gives the exact value of
\mathsf{D}^{(3)}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})
(thus improving on (
3
), including the version in
[
10
]
),
and also provides the exact structure of minimal zero-sum sequences over
\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2}
having a support of size
3
and maximum length.
It will be proved in Section
10
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
m\geq 2
be an integer, then

\mathsf{D}^{(3)}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})=4m^{2}-q(m).

Moreover, up to symmetries,
there is only one minimal zero-sum sequence
over
\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2}
having a support of size
3
and maximum length, namely

\begin{pmatrix}m\\
m\end{pmatrix}

repeated
q(m)(m-1)+m
times,

\begin{pmatrix}-m\\
m-1\end{pmatrix}

repeated
2m^{2}-mq(m)
times, and

\begin{pmatrix}m-q(m)\\
-m\end{pmatrix}

repeated
2m^{2}-m
times.

For every integer
m\geq 2
, denote by
S(m)
the sequence in Theorem
3
. We conjecture the following.

Conjecture 1
.

Let
m\geq 2
be an integer, then

\mathsf{D}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})=4m^{2}-q(m).

Moreover, up to symmetries,
S(m)
is the only minimal zero-sum sequence of maximum length over
\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2}
.

As a corollary of Theorem
3
, (
7
) and Lemma
16
on the asymptotic behaviour of
q

(see Section
10
) for the lower bound, and of Theorem
1
together with the inequality

\mathsf{D}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})\leq\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{B}^{(2)}_{\lceil\sqrt{2}m\rceil})
for the upper bound,
we obtain a new general result on the quantity
\mathsf{D}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})
.

Theorem 4
.

For
m\geq 2
, we have

4m^{2}-O(\log m)\leq\mathsf{D}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})\leq\frac{5\pi}{2}m^{2}+O(m).

This result improves the best bounds known so far (
3
), in particular since
5\pi/2<7.854<8
.

We continue our investigations with a result in the three-dimensional case.

Theorem 5
.

Asymptotically as
m\to+\infty
,

\frac{16}{3\sqrt{3}}m^{3}\lesssim\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{B}^{(3)}_{m})\lesssim\frac{1372}{81}\ \pi\ m^{3}.

The constant appearing in the lower bound is equal to
3.079\dots
and
improves on that of (
5
) by a multiplicative factor 2,
while the constant on the right-hand side is about
53.213\dots

which is significantly better than that of (
5
) (namely
101.630\dots
).

Using a similar approach as in Section
5
for the proof of Theorem
2
, we will show
in Section
7
how to adapt our argument in dimension 3 and prove the following result.

Theorem 6
.

Asymptotically, as
m\to+\infty
,

\mathsf{D}^{(4)}(\mathcal{B}^{(3)}_{m})\sim\frac{16}{3\sqrt{3}}m^{3}.

As in dimension 2, the optimal choice in dimension
d=3
for the elements of the support of a minimal zero-sum sequence
with maximal length is close to a regular
(d+1)
-hedron, a tetrahedron.
This geometric fact as well as another will help us coin a general conjecture in any dimension.
See Conjecture
2
below.

Going back to the case of boxes, after having checked (an exhaustive search can be done easily) that

\mathsf{D}^{(4)}(\llbracket-1,1\rrbracket^{3})=10,

we prove the following.

Theorem 7
.

For
m\geq 2
, we have

\mathsf{D}^{(4)}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{3})\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}16m^{3}-16m^{2}+10m-2&{\mathrm{if}}\ m\ \mathrm{is\ odd,}\\
16m^{3}-16m^{2}+8m-1&\mathrm{if}\ m\ \mathrm{is\ even.}\end{array}\right.

In view of (
7
), this improves on inequality (
4
) which, asymptotically, gave only
8m^{3}
as a lower bound.

As a corollary of Theorem
7
and (
7
) on one side, and of Theorem
6
together with the inequality

\mathsf{D}^{(4)}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{3})\leq\mathsf{D}^{(4)}(\mathcal{B}^{(3)}_{\lceil\sqrt{3}m\rceil})
on the other, we obtain the following as a final result.

Theorem 8
.

Asymptotically, as
m\to+\infty
,

\mathsf{D}^{(4)}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{3})\sim 16m^{3}.

As stated above, it is clear that we may extend the questions, some constructions and some geometric interpretations
we have proposed here in any dimension. This will be explained in Section
8
.
Our conjecture is that using an optimal distribution of the
d+1
points on the
(d-1)
-sphere in dimension
d
,
and charging these points adequately leads to the value of
\mathsf{D}^{(d+1)}(\mathcal{B}^{(d)}_{m})
provided that
m
is large enough in terms of
d
.
It also leads to the following conjecture on the Davenport constant of the ball in any dimension.

Conjecture 2
.

Let
d\geq 2
be an integer. Then, for
m\geq\sqrt{d}
, one has

\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{B}^{(d)}_{m})=\mathsf{D}^{(d+1)}(\mathcal{B}^{(d)}_{m})\underset{m\to+\infty}{\sim}\left(\frac{(d+1)^{d+1}}{d^{d}}\right)^{1/2}m^{d}.

The cases
d=1,
2 or 3 of the second part of the conjecture are precisely the cases we treated above.
Also, note that in the case of the box, one could be tempted to conjecture that
\mathsf{D}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{d})/m^{d}
has a limit too when
d
is fixed and
m
tends to
+\infty
(for instance, a natural analogue of this problem in the torsion case is solved, see Theorem
1
in
[
21
]
). However, and contrary to the case of the ball above, there is no guess as to what this limit could be in terms of
d
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1
: lower bounds

As for the lower bound, we start by considering the remainder
r
of
m
in the Euclidean division of
m
by 5.
We denote
m^{\prime}=m-r
which is divisible by 5.

Let

a=\frac{4m^{\prime}}{5}\quad{\rm and}\quad b=\frac{3m^{\prime}}{5}.

Notice that
a^{2}+b^{2}=m^{\prime 2}
thus the three points

\begin{pmatrix}0\\
m^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}-a\\
-(b-1)\end{pmatrix}

and
\begin{pmatrix}a-1\\
-b\end{pmatrix}

all belong to
\mathcal{B}^{(2)}_{m^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}^{(2)}_{m}
.

We consider the sequence
S_{1}
consisting of

\begin{pmatrix}0\\
m^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}

repeated
ab+(a-1)(b-1)=2ab-a-b+1
times,

\begin{pmatrix}-a\\
-(b-1)\end{pmatrix}

repeated
(a-1)m^{\prime}
times and

\begin{pmatrix}a-1\\
-b\end{pmatrix}

repeated
am^{\prime}
times.
It is a zero-sum sequence since

(ab+(a-1)(b-1))\begin{pmatrix}0\\
m^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}+(a-1)m^{\prime}\begin{pmatrix}-a\\
-(b-1)\end{pmatrix}+am^{\prime}\begin{pmatrix}a-1\\
-b\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

It remains to show that
S_{1}
is minimal.
Assume

(8)

k_{1}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
m^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}+k_{2}\begin{pmatrix}-a\\
-(b-1)\end{pmatrix}+k_{3}\begin{pmatrix}a-1\\
-b\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix}

with

(9)

k_{1}\leq 2ab-a-b+1,\quad k_{2}\leq(a-1)m^{\prime}\quad{\rm and}\quad k_{3}\leq am^{\prime}.

Considering the first coordinate in (
8
) shows that
k_{2}a=k_{3}(a-1)
which implies, since
a
and
a-1

are coprime, that there exists an integer
k
such that

k_{2}=(a-1)k\quad{\rm and}\quad k_{3}=ak.

Considering the second coordinate in (
8
) shows that

(10)

k_{1}m^{\prime}=(b-1)k_{2}+bk_{3}=\left((a-1)(b-1)+ab\right)k=(2ab-a-b+1)k.

We observe that
\gcd(m^{\prime},2ab-a-b+1)=1
. Indeed, this assertion is equivalent to

\gcd(25m^{\prime},2\cdot 4m^{\prime}\cdot 3m^{\prime}-5\cdot 4m^{\prime}-5\cdot 3m^{\prime}+25)=\gcd(25m^{\prime},24m^{\prime 2}-35m^{\prime}+25)=25.

Any prime
p
dividing
25m^{\prime}
must divide
m^{\prime}
, since
5
divides
m^{\prime}
.
Therefore such a
p
has to divide
24m^{\prime 2}-35m^{\prime}
and finally
25
: this gives
p=5
.
However, while
25
divides
24m^{\prime 2}-35m^{\prime}+25
,
125
doesn’t due to the fact
that the polynomial
4X^{2}+3X+1
doesn’t vanish mod
5
.
This proves our assertion.

Finally, with this coprimality result, equality (
10
) shows that
(2ab-a-b+1)\mid k_{1}
and
m^{\prime}\mid k
which in view of (
9
) shows that

k_{1}=2ab-a-b+1,\quad k=m^{\prime},\quad k_{2}=(a-1)m^{\prime}\quad{\rm and}\quad k_{3}=am^{\prime}.

This proves our assertion on the minimality of the sequence
S_{1}
.

We finally obtain

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{B}^{(2)}_{m})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{B}^{(2)}_{m^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\|S_{1}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2a(b+m^{\prime})-a-b-m^{\prime}+1

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{64}{25}m^{\prime 2}-\frac{12}{5}m^{\prime}+1.

Since this lower bound is an increasing function of
m^{\prime}
on positive integers and
m^{\prime}=m-r
with
0\leq r\leq 4
we
obtain

\mathsf{D}(\mathcal{B}^{(2)}_{m})\geq\frac{64}{25}(m-4)^{2}-\frac{12}{5}(m-4)+1=\frac{64}{25}m^{2}-\frac{572}{25}m+\frac{1289}{25}

and our first result follows.

We now come to the second assertion in our theorem.
Let
m
be a large enough integer.
We consider
p_{m}
the largest prime
\leq 2\lfloor\sqrt{3}m/2\rfloor-1

and define
a_{m}=(1+p_{m})/2
. We notice immediately that

a_{m}\sim\frac{p_{m}}{2}\sim\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}m

since
p_{m}\sim 2\lfloor\sqrt{3}m/2\rfloor\sim\sqrt{3}m

in view of the known results on the gap between consecutive primes, see for instance
[
4
]
.
More precisely, these results imply that
p_{m}=\sqrt{3}m+O(m^{.55})
which is by far enough
to get a good error term in our estimate below.

We now define the following integer:

b_{m}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{m-1}{2}&{\rm if}\,\,m\,\,{\rm is\,odd},\\
\frac{m}{2}-1&{\rm if}\,\,m\equiv 0\pmod{4},\\
\frac{m}{2}-2&{\rm if}\,\,m\equiv 2\pmod{4}.\end{array}\right.

A simple computation shows that
\gcd(m,b_{m})=1
, whatever the value of
m
.

We consider the sequence
S_{2}

8.
Investigating sequences with support of size
d+1
in dimension
d
: Conjecture
2

In this section, we would like to give some support to Conjecture
2
and explain that an important part
of it is more than conjectural.

In dimension
d=2
or 3, we already observed that the longest minimal zero-sum sequence having a support of size
d+1

is obtained for a support the elements of which are (almost) on the sphere of radius
m

and almost equidistant from one another, that is, as regularly distributed on the sphere as possible.
Then, the length of such a minimal zero-sum sequence is
asymptotically equal to the area of the hexagon of side
m
in dimension 2 and to the volume of the rhombic dodecahedron of side
m
in dimension 3.

Our conjecture is that the same phenomenon should happen in higher dimensions.
Indeed, the two lemmas of the previous section can be adapted to higher dimensions.
The geometric one can be obtained in an analogous – but technically more complicated – way and reads as follows.

Lemma 14
.

Let
d\geq 4
be an integer and
w_{1},\dots,w_{d+1}
be positively dependent vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
L(w_{1},\dots,w_{d+1})

be the convex hull of all the (non-empty)
(2^{d+1}-2)
sums of 1 to
d
vectors among the
w_{i}
’s, namely

\displaystyle L(w_{1},\dots,w_{d+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Conv}(w_{1},\dots,w_{d+1},\,\dots,w_{1}+w_{2},w_{1}+w_{3},\dots,w_{d}+w_{d+1},\dots

\displaystyle\hskip 71.13188pt\dots,w_{1}+w_{2}+\cdots+w_{d-1}+w_{d+1},\ w_{1}+w_{2}+\cdots+w_{d-1}+w_{d}).

Set for
i=1,\dots,d+1
the
d+1
hyperparallelepipeds

\displaystyle L_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\alpha_{1}w_{1}+\alpha_{2}w_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{i-1}w_{i-1}+\alpha_{i+1}w_{i+1}+\cdots+\alpha_{d+1}w_{d+1}\ :

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt0\leq\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{i-1},\alpha_{i+1},\dots,\alpha_{d+1}\leq 1\}

Then,

L(w_{1},\dots,w_{d+1})=\displaystyle\bigcup^{d+1}_{i=1}L_{i}=\displaystyle\bigcup^{d+1}_{i=1}(w_{i}+L_{i}).

From it, one can deduce, as in lower dimensions, an algorithmic lemma.

Corollary 15
.

Let
d\geq 4
be an integer,
n\geq d+1
and
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}
be a minimal zero-sum sequence in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}

having a support of size
d+1
, say
\{w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{d+1}\}
.
Then,
n
is at most the number of points with integer coordinates in
L(w_{1},\dots,w_{d+1})
.

Having this at hand, and approximating the number of integral points of
L(w_{1},\dots,w_{d+1})
using the Principle
of approximation of the volume by the number of integral points, we are led to compute the volume of such an

L(w_{1},\dots,w_{d+1})
where the
w_{i}
’s are the elements of the support of the sequence,
and then maximise it.
We conjecture that the
d+1
vectors
w_{i}
’s leading to the maximum volume
will be obtained as in lower dimensions that is, as the vertices of a regular polytope on
d+1
points regularly spaced
on the unit-sphere of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(up to an homothety), that is with a given fixed distance from one another.
This would generalise in higher dimensions the constructions leading to the equilateral triangle in dimension 2 and
the tetrahedron in dimension 3.
For instance, in dimension
4
, we would obtain a pentachoron.

Then, the idea is that the longest minimal zero-sum sequence with support of size
d+1
will
be obtained by perturbing slightly these
d+1
points so that they become integral and
then move them again so that they have the coprimality conditions which ensures that our
sequence is a minimal zero-sum sequence.

This being said, in order to justify numerically our conjecture, we have to find how to place

d+1
points regularly spaced on the unit-sphere of
\mathbb{R}^{d}

and then compute the volume of the polytope generated by these points.

Define

\sigma_{1}^{(1)}=(-1)\quad{\rm and}\quad\sigma_{2}^{(1)}=(1).

They are one-dimensional points on the unit-sphere
\{-1,1\}
of
\mathbb{R}
being “optimally” far from each other.
The distance between these two points is 2. The length of the segment they define, namely
[-1,1]
, is
V_{1}=2
.

Define

\sigma_{1}^{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}-1/2\\
-\sqrt{3}/2\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma_{2}^{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}-1/2\\
\sqrt{3}/2\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma_{3}^{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

These are two-dimensional points on the unit-sphere (the unit circle) of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
that are “optimally” far from each other and regularly spaced.
The distance between any two of these three points is
\sqrt{3}
.
These are the vertices of an equilateral triangle. The area of the triangle defined by these three points is
3\sqrt{3}/4
.

In dimension 3, we obtain the four vectors:

\sigma_{1}^{(3)}=\begin{pmatrix}-1/3\\
-\sqrt{2}/3\\
-\sqrt{2}/\sqrt{3}\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma_{2}^{(3)}=\begin{pmatrix}-1/3\\
-\sqrt{2}/3\\
\sqrt{2}/\sqrt{3}\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma_{3}^{(3)}=\begin{pmatrix}-1/3\\
2\sqrt{2}/3\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma_{4}^{(3)}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

These are the vertices of a regular tetrahedron. The distance between any two of these four points is
2\sqrt{2}/\sqrt{3}
.
Up to a rotation, these two last sets of points coincide with the supports of the sequences we used above,
in our two- and three-dimensional constructions.

In general, we can always find
d+1
points regularly spaced on the unit-sphere of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
To construct such distributions of points, we may proceed by induction.
Assuming that
(\sigma_{i}^{(d-1)})_{1\leq i\leq d}
is known, we define a finite sequence of
d
-dimensional vectors
as follows:

\sigma_{i}^{(d)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\begin{pmatrix}-1/d\\
\sqrt{1-1/d^{2}}\ \sigma_{i}^{(d-1)}\end{pmatrix}&&{\rm if}\quad i\leq d,\\
&&\\
\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0_{d}\end{pmatrix}&&{\rm if}\quad i=d+1,\\
\end{array}\right.

where
0_{d}
is the all-zero vector of dimension
d
.

The elements of such a sequence are easily shown to belong to the unit-sphere and to be pairwise at distance

\delta_{d}=\sqrt{2\ \frac{d+1}{d}},

a quantity satisfying the immediate induction:
\delta_{d}=\sqrt{1-1/d^{2}}\ \delta_{d-1}
.

Computing
V_{d}
, the volume of the
d
-dimensional simplex with vertices
(\sigma_{i}^{(d)})_{1\leq i\leq d+1}

can be done using the generalisation of Heron’s formula, known as the Cayley-Menger determinant
[
32
]
.
In view of the fact that the distance between any two vertices is fixed, namely equal to
\delta_{d}
, we obtain

V_{d}=\left(\frac{1}{(d!)^{2}\,2^{d}}\ \delta_{d}^{2d}\ |\det U_{d}|\right)^{1/2},

where
U_{d}
is the
d\times d
matrix with ones everywhere except on the diagonal which is filled with twos.
One can check by induction that
\det U_{d}=d+1

[
7
]
.
Finally we obtain:

V_{d}=\left(\frac{1}{(d!)^{2}\ 2^{d}}\ \left(2\ \frac{d+1}{d}\right)^{d}(d+1)\right)^{1/2}=\frac{1}{d!}\left(\frac{(d+1)^{d+1}}{d^{d}}\right)^{1/2}.

To obtain the volume which appears naturally here in our problem, that is the one of

L=L(\sigma_{1}^{(d)},\sigma_{2}^{(d)},\dots,\sigma_{d+1}^{(d)}),

we must multiply
V_{d}
by a certain factor which takes into account the fact that we do not consider
a simplex but a convex hull of the form
L(\sigma_{1}^{(d)},\sigma_{2}^{(d)},\dots,\sigma_{d+1}^{(d)})
.
Indeed we may find this multiplicative factor from the fact that while a unit cube has volume 1,
the standard simplex has a volume

\displaystyle\int_{0\leq x_{i}\leq 1,\ x_{1}+\cdots+x_{d}\leq 1}1\quad{\rm d}x_{1}\cdots{\rm d}x_{d}

\displaystyle\hskip-99.58464pt=

\displaystyle\hskip-42.67912pt\int_{0\leq x_{i}\leq 1,\ x_{1}+\cdots+x_{d-1}\leq 1}\left(\int_{0\leq x_{d}\leq 1-(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{d-1})}1\quad{\rm d}x_{d}\right){\rm d}x_{1}\cdots{\rm d}x_{d-1}

\displaystyle\hskip-99.58464pt=

\displaystyle\hskip-42.67912pt\int_{0\leq x_{i}\leq 1,\ x_{1}+\cdots+x_{d-1}\leq 1}\big(1-(x_{1}+\cdots x_{d-1})\big)\quad{\rm d}x_{1}\cdots{\rm d}x_{d-1}

\displaystyle\hskip-99.58464pt=

\displaystyle\hskip-42.67912pt\frac{1}{2}\int_{0\leq x_{i}\leq 1,\ x_{1}+\cdots+x_{d-2}\leq 1}\big(1-(x_{1}+\cdots x_{d-2})\big)^{2}\quad{\rm d}x_{1}\cdots{\rm d}x_{d-2}

\displaystyle\hskip-99.58464pt=

\displaystyle\hskip-42.67912pt\cdots

\displaystyle\hskip-99.58464pt=

\displaystyle\hskip-42.67912pt\frac{1}{(d-1)!}\int_{0}^{1}(1-x_{1})^{d-1}\quad{\rm d}x_{1}

\displaystyle\hskip-99.58464pt=

\displaystyle\hskip-42.67912pt\frac{1}{d\ !}.

This leads us to
d!\,V_{d}
as the volume of the polyhedron
L(\sigma_{1}^{(d)},\sigma_{2}^{(d)},\dots,\sigma_{d+1}^{(d)})

in dimension
d
(in dimension 2, the area of the regular hexagon is twice the area of the equilateral triangle, while
in dimension 3, the volume of the rhombic dodecahedron is equal to 6 times the one of the tetrahedron).

Conjecture
2
, which generalises our results in dimensions 2 and 3, states that
we have to multiply this by the homothetic factor
m^{d}
to obtain the correct asymptotic value
for
\mathsf{D}^{(d)}(\mathcal{B}^{(d+1)}_{m})
, namely
d!\,V_{d}\ m^{d}
.

9.
Proof of Theorem
3
: lower bound

The integer
m
being given, put
q=q(m)
and define
c=m-q
.
Consider
S
to be the sequence consisting of

\begin{pmatrix}m\\
m\end{pmatrix}

repeated
m^{2}-(m-1)c
times,

\begin{pmatrix}c\\
-m\end{pmatrix}

repeated
2m^{2}-m
times and

\begin{pmatrix}-m\\
m-1\end{pmatrix}
repeated
m^{2}+mc
times.
Note that
S
is a sequence over
\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2}
, the support of which has size 3,
that its length is

m^{2}-(m-1)c+2m^{2}-m+m^{2}+mc=4m^{2}-m+c=4m^{2}-q,

and that
S
sums to

\displaystyle(m^{2}-(m-1)c)\begin{pmatrix}m\\
m\end{pmatrix}+(2m^{2}-m)\begin{pmatrix}c\\
-m\end{pmatrix}+(m^{2}+mc)\begin{pmatrix}-m\\
m-1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}m(m^{2}-(m-1)c)+c(2m^{2}-m)-m(m^{2}+mc)\\
m(m^{2}-(m-1)c)-m(2m^{2}-m))+(m-1)(m^{2}+mc)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Let us now assume that
S
has a zero-sum subsequence
S^{\prime}
, say consisting of

\begin{pmatrix}m\\
m\end{pmatrix}

repeated
u
times,

\begin{pmatrix}c\\
-m\end{pmatrix}

repeated
v
times, and

\begin{pmatrix}-m\\
m-1\end{pmatrix}

repeated
w
times, with
u+v+w>0
and

0\leq u\leq m^{2}-(m-1)c,\quad 0\leq v\leq 2m^{2}-m,\quad 0\leq w\leq m^{2}+mc.

This means that

(26)

um+vc-wm=0\quad\text{ and }\quad um-vm+(m-1)w=0.

In other words

(w-u)m=v(m-q),

and

(v-u)m=w(m-1).

Since both
m
and
m-q
, and
m
and
m-1
respectively are pairs of coprime integers,
we deduce that
m
divides
v
and
w
. Therefore there are two integers
x
and
y
such that

v=mx\quad\text{ and }\quad w=my.

By (
26
), we deduce that

u=my-xc=my-(m-q)x\quad\text{ and }\quad u=mx-(m-1)y,

which implies

(2m-q)x=(2m-1)y.

We now argue that
\gcd(2m-q,2m-1)=1
.
Indeed, assume for a contradiction that
p
is a prime dividing both
2m-q
and
2m-1
.
Then it divides
(2m-1)-(2m-q)=q-1
, thus
p\leq q-1
.
By definition of
q
, and since
p
is prime,
p
divides
m
.
Since it divides
2m-1
also, we obtain
p=1
, a contradiction.

From
\gcd(2m-q,2m-1)=1
we infer that
2m-1
divides
x
and in particular
x\geq 2m-1
,
thus
v=mx\geq(2m-1)m=2m^{2}-m
.
It follows that
v=2m^{2}-m
and
x=2m-1
.
We then find that
y=2m-q
and finally
u=m^{2}-(m-1)c
which means that
S^{\prime}=S
and proves
that
S
is minimal.

This finishes the proof of our lower bound.

10.
Proof of Theorem
3
: the upper bound

Before coming to the very proof of Theorem
3
, we start with a useful lemma
on the asymptotic behaviour of the function
q
, where
q(m)
is defined as the smallest prime not dividing
m
.

Lemma 16
.

For any integer
m\geq 2
,

q(m)\leq 1+4\log m.

Moreover, if
m\geq 3
, then

q(m)<m.

Proof.

Assume that
q(m)\leq 10
. Since it is a prime,
q(m)
can be equal to
2,3,5
or 7.
If
q(m)=2
,
m\geq 3
; if
q(m)=3
,
m\geq 2
; if
q(m)=5
,
m\geq 6
and
if
q(m)=7
,
m\geq 30
. In these four cases, an immediate computation shows that
the inequality is valid.

Let us now assume that
q(m)\geq 11
.
By definition, the product
{\displaystyle\prod_{p<q(m),\ p\,{\rm prime}}p}
divides
m
, thus,
using the classical number-theoretical notation
\vartheta
for the Chebyshev function
[
22
]
, we obtain:

\vartheta(q(m)-1)=\sum_{p<q(m),\ p\,{\rm prime}}\log p\leq\log m.

Dusart proved (this is Theorem 4.2 in
[
12
]
) that

\forall x\geq 2:\quad\quad\vartheta(x)\geq x-3.965\frac{x}{\log^{2}x}.

We therefore have

\frac{1}{4}(q(m)-1)\leq\left(1-\frac{3.965}{\log^{2}10}\right)(q(m)-1)\leq\vartheta(q(m)-1)\leq\log m,

due to the fact that
q(m)
is by definition larger than
10
. The first result follows.

That
q(m)<m
follows from the first inequality for
m\geq 11
and from a direct computation for
3\leq m\leq 10
.

∎

Let us now proceed with the proof of Theorem
3
.

The case
m=2
can be considered independently since an exhaustive computer search is possible. It will
simplify the presentation of the forthcoming argument (mainly due to the fact that the second part of Lemma
16

cannot be applied when
m=2
).
One checks that

\mathsf{D}^{(3)}(\llbracket-2,2\rrbracket^{2})=13=4\times 2^{2}-3,

and that, up to the eight symmetries of the square (generated by
\varphi:(x,y)\mapsto(-x,y)
and
\psi:(x,y)\mapsto(y,x)
), the only minimal zero-sum sequence over
\llbracket-2,2\rrbracket^{2}
having a support of size
3
and length
13
is the one consisting of
\begin{pmatrix}2\\
2\end{pmatrix}
repeated
5
times,
\begin{pmatrix}-2\\
1\end{pmatrix}
repeated
2
times, and
\begin{pmatrix}-1\\
-2\end{pmatrix}

repeated
6
times. Since
q(2)=3
, the result is proved for
m=2
.

From now on, we assume
m\geq 3
.

We start by a reformulation of the problem for which we need some notation.
Let

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}a_{1,1}&a_{1,2}&a_{1,3}\\
a_{2,1}&a_{2,2}&a_{2,3}\\
\end{array}\right)

be a
2\times 3
matrix with coefficients in
\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket
.
For
i=1,2
or
3
, we denote by
A_{i}
the
2\times 2
submatrix of
A
obtained by erasing the
i
-th column
and let

\Delta(A)=\gcd\big(\det(A_{1}),\det(A_{2}),\det(A_{3})\big).

From now on, we define
{\mathcal{C}}
to be the set of
2\times 3
matrices with coefficients in
\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket

such that
\det(A_{1}),\det(A_{2}),

\det(A_{3})\neq 0
and
\Delta(A)=1
.

Finally, we shall say that two
2\times 3
matrices
A
and
B
with coefficients in
\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket
are

analogous
if
B
can be deduced from
A
by exchanging columns and applying one of the eight symmetries of the square
already mentioned (those generated by
\varphi:(x,y)\mapsto(-x,y)
and
\psi:(x,y)\mapsto(y,x)
). Notice that
two analogous matrices
A
and
B
satisfy
\Delta(A)=\Delta(B)
.

Lemma 17
.

Let
m\geq 3
and
S
be a minimal zero-sum sequence with a support of size 3,
\{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\}\subseteq\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2}
,
such that
\mathsf{D}^{(3)}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})=|S|
.
Let
A
be the matrix, the
i
-th column of which is
s_{i}
(
i=1,2,3
), then we have
A\in{\mathcal{C}}
and

\mathsf{D}^{(3)}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{3}|\det A_{i}|.

Proof.

Firstly, we notice that if
A
has rank 1, then the three columns of
A
are collinear.
In this case, the length of our minimal zero-sum sequence
S
with support
\{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\}

would be at most the upper bound obtained for the one-dimensional case, which is
2m-1
.
Yet, it follows from Theorem
3
that

\mathsf{D}^{(3)}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})\geq 4m^{2}-q(m)

which gives a contradiction. We may therefore assume that
A
has rank 2.
Moreover, we must have

(27)

\det(A_{1}),\det(A_{2}),\det(A_{3})\neq 0

for, if say
\det(A_{1})=0
then
s_{2}
and
s_{3}
are proportional but in this case,
in any linear combination of
s_{1},s_{2}
and
s_{3}
equal to zero, the coefficient of
s_{3}
itself must be zero
and we are back again to the one-dimensional case.

By Cramer’s method, it is readily seen that the one-dimensional kernel of
A
is equal to the set of multiples of

r=\frac{1}{\Delta(A)}\begin{pmatrix}\det(A_{1})\\
-\det(A_{2})\\
\det(A_{3})\end{pmatrix}

a vector with integral coefficients, and indeed the one with the smallest integral coefficients.
In particular, all the elements of
(\ker A)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{3}
are integral multiples of
r
.
Denoting by
\ell_{A}
the smallest
\ell_{1}
-norm of a non-zero
integral solution
X
to the system
AX=0
, we find

\ell_{A}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{3}|\det A_{i}|}{\Delta(A)},

and we observe that
\ell_{A}
is the length of our minimal zero-sum sequence
S
.
Thus, by assumption,

(28)

\mathsf{D}^{(3)}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})=\ell_{A}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{3}|\det A_{i}|}{\Delta(A)}.

Again, by the lower bound we obtained in the previous section (see Theorem
3
), we know that

(29)

\ell_{A}\geq 4m^{2}-q(m).

Since for
i=1,2,3
,
|\det A_{i}|\leq 2m^{2}
, we obtain

\ell_{A}\leq\frac{6m^{2}}{\Delta(A)}

and using (
29
), we get

\Delta(A)=\gcd_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}(\det A_{i})\leq\frac{6m^{2}}{4m^{2}-q(m)}<2

in view of
q(m)\leq m<m^{2}
(for
m\geq 3
), by Lemma
16
.
From this we deduce

\Delta(A)=\gcd_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}(\det A_{i})=1.

This, with (
27
), proves that
A\in{\mathcal{C}}
. Moreover, by (
28
),

\mathsf{D}^{(3)}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})=\sum_{i=1,2,3}|\det A_{i}|.

∎

Let us introduce
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
the subset of
{\mathcal{C}}
consisting of matrices
A
satisfying the sign pattern given by

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}+&-&+\\
+&+&-\\
\end{array}\right).

We show how to restrict our research on this subset of
{\mathcal{C}}
.

Lemma 18
.

Let
m\geq 3
and
S
be a minimal zero-sum sequence with a support of size 3,
\{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\}\subseteq\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2}
, such that

\mathsf{D}^{(3)}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})=|S|

Let
A
be the matrix, the
i
-th column of which is
s_{i}
(

).
Then
A
is analogous to a matrix
B
belonging to
{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}
and

\mathsf{D}^{(3)}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{3}|\det B_{i}|.

We notice that two analogous matrices
A
and
B
satisfy
\sum_{i=1}^{3}|\det A_{i}|=\sum_{i=1}^{3}|\det B_{i}|
.
Lemma
18
expresses the fact that in order to compute the maximum of
\sum_{i=1}^{3}|\det A_{i}|
over
{\mathcal{C}}

it is enough to restrict ourselves to matrices belonging to
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
and that every matrix of
{\mathcal{C}}

attaining the value
\mathsf{D}^{(3)}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})
is, up to a reordering of the columns and symmetries,
a matrix of
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}

Proof.

We denote

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}a_{1,1}&a_{1,2}&a_{1,3}\\
a_{2,1}&a_{2,2}&a_{2,3}\\
\end{array}\right).

By permutation of the columns, we may assume that
\max_{i=1,2,3}|\det A_{i}|=|\det A_{3}|
and
by the symmetry of coordinates and permutation of the vectors

a_{1,1},a_{2,1}\geq 0.

Such a symmetry is tantamount to multiplying by
-1
a line or a column,
which does not change the absolute values of the determinants
and is compatible with our assumption that
|\det A_{3}|=\max_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}|\det A_{i}|
.

By the zero-sum sequence property, there is a sum of the three columns of
A
with positive coefficients which is equal to zero.
Thus, we must have
a_{1,2}
or
a_{1,3}\leq 0
.
We may assume without loss of generality that

Since by assumption
\mathsf{D}^{(3)}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})\geq 4m^{2}-q(m)
and
q(m)\leq m-1\leq m^{2}/3

(for any

|\det A_{3}|=\max_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}|\det A_{i}|\geq\frac{\sum_{i=1,2,3}|\det A_{i}|}{3}=\frac{\mathsf{D}^{(3)}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})}{3}\geq\frac{4m^{2}-q(m)}{3}\geq\frac{11}{9}\ m^{2}.

This yields

|a_{1,1}a_{2,2}-a_{2,1}a_{1,2}|\geq\frac{11}{9}\ m^{2}>m^{2}

and the two terms

and
a_{2,1}a_{1,2}
must be of opposite sign.
As
a_{1,2}a_{2,1}\leq 0
and
a_{1,1}\geq 0
, we deduce

a_{2,2}\geq 0.

Considering the second coordinate of the three terms of the support implies, by the zero-sum sequence property,

a_{2,3}\leq 0.

For the matrix
A
, we thus have only two possible combinations of signs

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}+&-&-\\
+&+&-\\
\end{array}\right)

or

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}+&-&+\\
+&+&-\\
\end{array}\right)

depending on the sign of
a_{1,3}
.

In the first case, exchanging the first two vectors and applying a symmetry (the first coordinate is replaced by its opposite),
we arrive in the second case situation. Therefore, without restriction, we may assume that we are in the case

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}+&-&+\\
+&+&-\\
\end{array}\right).

Denoting by
B
the matrix obtained after all the transformations we performed from the beginning, starting from
A
,
we obtain a matrix
B
analogous to
A
such that
B\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
and

\mathsf{D}^{(3)}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{3}|\det A_{i}|=\sum_{i=1}^{3}|\det B_{i}|.

∎

In view of the two previous lemmas, computing
\mathsf{D}^{(3)}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})
consists in
maximising
\sum_{i=1}^{3}|\det A_{i}|
on matrices of
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}

\mathsf{D}^{(3)}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})=\max_{A\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}}\sum_{i=1}^{3}|\det A_{i}|.

Using the sign pattern of
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
, we may reorganise the sum of determinants appearing in this maximum:
the function we have to maximise for

is

(30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|a_{1,1}a_{2,2}-a_{1,2}a_{2,1}|+|a_{1,3}a_{2,1}-a_{2,3}a_{1,1}|+|a_{2,3}a_{1,2}-a_{1,3}a_{2,2}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a_{2,2}-a_{2,3})a_{1,1}+(a_{1,3}-a_{1,2})a_{2,1}+|a_{2,3}a_{1,2}-a_{1,3}a_{2,2}|

We define this quantity as
f(a_{1,1},a_{1,2},a_{1,3},a_{2,1},a_{2,2},a_{2,3})
.

We now fix
M\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
, a matrix attaining the maximum

\max_{A\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}}\sum_{i=1}^{3}|\det A_{i}|=\mathsf{D}^{(3)}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})
and write

(31)

M=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}x_{1}&y_{1}&z_{1}\\
x_{2}&y_{2}&z_{2}\\
\end{array}\right).

We derive some properties on it. For this purpose, we denote by

{\mathcal{M}}^{\ast}_{2,3}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})

the set of all
2\times 3
matrices with coefficients in
\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket
that satisfy the same sign pattern

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}+&-&+\\
+&+&-\\
\end{array}\right)

as the elements in
{{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}}
, but not necessarily the other conditions.

Lemma 19
.

One has
x_{1}=m
.

Proof.

Otherwise,
x_{1}\leq m-1
and

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}^{(3)}(\llbracket-m,m\rrbracket^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1},x_{2},y_{2},z_{2})

\displaystyle=
