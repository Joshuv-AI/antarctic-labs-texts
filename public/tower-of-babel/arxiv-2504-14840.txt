Introduction

The theory of
p
-negative type was originally introduced by Schoenberg
[
8
,
9
]
to study isometric embeddings of finite metric spaces into Euclidean space. Its definition is as follows. The definition of strict
p
-negative type came later and is due to
[

Definition 1.1
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
be a metric space and
p\geq 0
. Then

p
-negative type if
1

1

For
p=0

0^{0}=0
.

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}d_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})^{p}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\leq 0

for all distinct

.

Among other things, Schoenberg

-negative type, which explains its connection to isometric embeddings into Hilbert space. (Again, the results for strict

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
be a metric space and
p\geq 0
.

(1)

X
has
p
-negative type if and only if
(X,d_{X}^{p/2})
embeds isometrically into a Hilbert space.

(2)

X
has strict
p
-negative type if and only if
(X,d_{X}^{p/2})
embeds isometrically into a Hilbert space as an affinely independent set.

(3)

If

-negative type, then
X
also has strict
q
-negative type.

Note that by taking

in the above theorem, one obtains the following characterisation of those metric spaces that embed into a Hilbert space: a metric space
(X,d_{X})
embeds into a Hilbert space if and only if
X
has
2
-negative type. Also, note that the third result in the above theorem implies that the
p
-negative type values of a metric space are all determined by the largest value of

for which
X
has
p
-negative type. If
(X,d_{X})
is a metric space then we define the supremal
p
-negative type of

Since all of the sums that appear in the definition of

-negative type are finite sums a simple limiting argument shows that if
\wp_{X}<\infty
then
X
has
\wp_{X}
-negative type. Consequently, the set of values

such that a metric space has
p
-negative type is always of the form
[0,\wp_{X}]
or
[0,\infty)
.

To quantify the degree of strictness of
p
-negative type that a metric space possesses, the

-negative type gap was introduced by Doust and Weston in

. If
(X,d_{X})
is a metric space and

\frac{\Gamma}{2}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}|\xi_{k}|\bigg)^{2}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}d_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})^{p}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\leq 0

for all distinct
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in X
,
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}=0
and

.

Most importantly, if
X
is a finite metric space and

then
X
has strict

-negative type if and only if

(see
[
5
]
). The usefulness of knowing the value of

when

has strict
p
-negative type is seen in the following result of

has strict

-negative type, then

also has strict
q
-negative type for all

\epsilon=\frac{\ln\big(1+\frac{\Gamma_{X}(p)}{D_{X}^{p}\gamma(n)}\big)}{\ln\mathcal{D}_{X}}

with

,
\mathcal{D}_{X}=D_{X}/\min_{x,y\in X,x\neq y}d_{X}(x,y)

-negative type can be extended to certain values of

.

Doust and Weston in
[
1
]
were able to provide an explicit formula for the
1
-negative type gap of an arbitrary weighted finite metric tree. Using this, they were able to provide non-trivial lower bounds on the supremal
p
-negative type of finite metric trees. Li and Weston in
[
5
]
were able to provide an explicit formula for the
0
-negative type gap of a finite metric space, which led them to conclude non-trivial bounds on the supremal

-negative type of an

[

were able to provide an elegant combinatorial formula for the limit of

as

. In
[
10

, Wolf provided a versatile formula for computing the

-negative type gap of a finite metric space, and among other things, was able to provide a computation of the
1
-negative type gap for the cycle

, when

is odd. Also, in
[

, Wolf showed that the

-negative type gap is closely related to certain eigenvalues of the distance matrix of

Recently, many
p
-negative type properties of finite metric spaces, such as the
p
-negative type gap, have been studied through the lens of the Gramian matrix, whose definition is as follows.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
(X,d_{X})=(\{x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\},d_{X})
be a metric space and
p\geq 0
. The
p
-Gramian of
X
is the matrix
G_{p}(X)=(g_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n}
where

g_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(d_{X}(x_{i},x_{0})^{p}+d_{X}(x_{j},x_{0})^{p}-d_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})^{p})

for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
.

Remark 1.4
.

Note that the Gramian of the metric space depends not only on the metric structure of the space but also on the labelling of the points
x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
.

It follows from Schoenberg’s original arguments
[
8
,
9
]
that a finite metric space has (strict)
p
-negative type if and only if it is (strictly) positive semidefinite. Thus another natural measure of the strictness of
p
-negative type of a finite metric space is the minimum eigenvalue of the Gramian matrix
\lambda_{min}(G_{p})
. In particular, it is obvious that a finite metric space has strict
p
-negative type if and only if
\lambda_{min}(G_{p})>0
. In
[
7
]
the relation between
\lambda_{min}(G_{p})
and the usual
p
-negative type gap as stated above is given. The details of this relation are expanded on in Section
2
.

In
[
7
]
the minimum eigenvalue of the Gramian
\lambda_{min}(G_{p})
was computed for the complete bipartite graphs
K_{n,1}
. Apart from this however, there are no other spaces for which
\lambda_{min}(G_{p})
has been computed. The purpose of this article is to compute
\lambda_{min}(G_{p})
for the class of spaces known as ultrametric spaces, and also to describe the corresponding eigenspace. Most notably here we are able to compute
\lambda_{min}(G_{p})
for all
p\geq 0
, whereas, as mentioned above, only the behaviour of
\Gamma_{X}(p)
as
p\rightarrow\infty
is known.

In Section
2
the relevant background on
\lambda_{min}(G_{p})
and its relation to the usual
p
-negative type gap is given. Then in Section
3
the basic definitions pertaining to ultrametric spaces are covered. In Section

we show that if
X
is a finite ultrametric space with minimum nonzero distance
\alpha_{1}
then
\lambda_{min}(G_{p})=\alpha_{1}^{p}/2
. Then in Section
5

6
we provide an explicit example of these concepts for a specific finite ultrametric space.

2.
Eigenvalues of The Gramian and the
p
-negative Type Gap

In
[
7
]
a more general
p
-negative type gap than that of Doust and Weston in
[
1
,
2
]
was introduced. For this we require the following definitions.

Definition 2.1
.

By a weight vector in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2}
, we mean a vector consisting of nonnegative real numbers

(s,t)=(s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{n},t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2}

such that the following conditions hold.

(1)

\sum_{i=0}^{n}s_{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}
.

(2)

\sum_{i=0}^{n}s_{i}t_{i}=0
(i.e.
s,t
are disjointly supported).

The set of all weight vectors in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+2}
will be denoted by
W_{n}
.

Definition 2.2
.

A nonempty subset
S\subseteq W_{n}
will be called an allowable set if it satisfies the following three properties.

(1)

0\notin S
.

(2)

For all
x\in W_{n}
with
x\neq 0
, there exists some
\lambda>0
such that
\lambda x\in S
.

(3)

S
is compact (as a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+2}
in its usual topology).

We may now define our more general
p
-negative type gap functions.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
(X,d_{X})=(\{x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\},d_{X})
be a metric space and let
S\subseteq W_{n}
be an allowable set. The
p
-negative type gap function (corresponding to
S
and
X
) is the function
\Gamma_{S}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

\Gamma_{S}(p)=\frac{1}{2}\bigg\{\inf_{(s,t)\in S}2\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}s_{i}t_{j}d_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})^{p}-\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}(s_{i}s_{j}+t_{i}t_{j})d_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})^{p}\bigg\}

for all
p\geq 0
.

It was shown in
[
7
]
that for a certain choice of
S
, one actually recovers the minimum eigenvalue of the Gramian matrix.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
(X,d_{X})=(\{x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\},d_{X})
be a metric space,
p\geq 0
and
G=G_{p}(X)
the
p
-Gramian of
X
. Also, set

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\bigg\{(s,t)\in W_{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}^{2}=1\bigg\}.

Then

\Gamma_{S}(p)=\lambda_{min}(G).

3.
Finite Ultrametric Spaces

Ultrametric spaces are an important family of metric spaces that have recently featured in a variety of different applications in the theory of metric embeddings. Let us recall their definition now.

Definition 3.1
.

A metric space
(X,d_{X})
is said to be an ultrametric space if
d_{X}(x,y)\leq\max\{d_{X}(x,z),d_{X}(y,z)\}
for all
x,y,z\in X
.

Remark 3.2
.

To avoid trivial cases, we shall always assume that
|X|\geq 3
.

An important notion in the theory of finite ultrametric spaces is the following one.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
,
|X|>1
, be a finite ultrametric space with minimum nonzero distance
\alpha
. Let
z\in X
be given. The set
B_{z}(\alpha)=\{x\in X:d_{X}(x,z)\leq\alpha\}
is said to be a coterie in
X
if
|B_{z}(\alpha)|>1
.

Some simple facts about coteries are the following. If
(X,d_{X})
is a finite ultrametric space on at least two points then
X
contains at least one coterie. Morever, if
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are coteries then either
B_{1}=B_{2}
or
B_{1}\cap B_{2}=\emptyset
.

Ultrametric spaces are particularly important in the theory of
p
-negative type since they form the class of spaces which have infinite supremal
p
-negative type. That is, it was shown in
[
4
]
that a metric space
(X,d_{X})
has
\wp_{X}=\infty
if and only if
X
is an ultrametric space. As such, if
X
is a finite ultrametric space then
\Gamma_{X}(p)>0
for all
p>0
. Because of this, work has recently been done in the direction of calculating an explicit expression for
\Gamma_{X}(p)
for all
p>0
(see
[
11
,
3
,
4
]
). In particular, a formula for the limit of
\Gamma_{X}(p)
as
p\rightarrow\infty
was given for finite ultrametric spaces in
[
3
]
.

4.
The Minimum Eigenvalue Of The Gramian

Throughout this section we let
(X,d_{X})=(\{x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\},d_{X})
be a finite ultrametric space with nonzero distances
\alpha_{1}<\dots<\alpha_{\ell}
. We also assume that
S
is an allowable set and that
(s,t)\in S
.

For
p>0
let

\gamma(p)=2\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}s_{i}t_{j}d_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})^{p}-\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}(s_{i}s_{j}+t_{i}t_{j})d_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})^{p}.

Then since the non-zero distances in
(X,d_{X})
are
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}
we have that
\gamma(p)=c_{1}\alpha_{1}^{p}+\dots+c_{\ell}\alpha_{\ell}^{p}
for some
c_{1},\dots,c_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

In
[
3
]
an explicit expression was given for the constants
c_{i}
in terms of
s_{i}
and
t_{j}
and the structure of the space
X
. For our purposes we require only the following properties of the constants
c_{i}
, which were proved in
[
3
]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

The constants
c_{i}
satisfy the following properties.

(1)

c_{k}+c_{k+1}+\dots+c_{\ell}\geq 0
for all
1\leq k\leq\ell
.

(2)

c_{1}+\dots+c_{\ell}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}s_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}^{2}
.

Remark 4.2
.

In
[
3
]
it was the case that
2(c_{1}+\dots+c_{\ell})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}s_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}^{2}
. The reason for this difference is that our
\gamma(p)
is actually a factor of
2
times the definition of
\gamma(p)
as given in
[
3
]
.

We may use these properties of the constants
c_{i}
to compute
\lambda_{min}(G_{p})
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let

S=\bigg\{(s,t)\in W_{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}^{2}=1\bigg\}.

Then
\gamma(p)\geq\alpha_{1}^{p}
for all
(s,t)\in S
with equality if and only if
c_{2}=\dots=c_{\ell}=0
and
s_{0}=t_{0}=0
.

Proof.

Here we use the properties of the constants
c_{i}
from Theorem
4.1
and the fact that
0<\alpha_{1}<\dots<\alpha_{\ell}
. So, since
c_{\ell}\geq 0
and
\alpha_{\ell-1}<\alpha_{\ell}
we have that

\displaystyle\gamma(p)

\displaystyle=c_{1}\alpha_{1}^{p}+\dots+c_{\ell}\alpha_{\ell}^{p}

\displaystyle\geq c_{1}\alpha_{1}^{p}+\dots+c_{\ell-1}\alpha_{\ell-1}^{p}+c_{\ell}\alpha_{\ell-1}^{p}

\displaystyle=c_{1}\alpha_{1}^{p}+\dots+(c_{\ell-1}+c_{\ell})\alpha_{\ell-1}^{p}.

Since
c_{\ell-1}+c_{\ell}\geq 0
and
\alpha_{\ell-2}<\alpha_{\ell-1}

\displaystyle c_{1}\alpha_{1}^{p}+\dots+(c_{\ell-1}+c_{\ell})\alpha_{\ell-1}^{p}

\displaystyle\geq c_{1}\alpha_{1}^{p}+\dots+c_{\ell-2}\alpha_{\ell-2}^{p}+(c_{\ell-1}+c_{\ell})\alpha_{\ell-2}^{p}

\displaystyle=c_{1}\alpha_{1}^{p}+\dots+(c_{\ell-2}+c_{\ell-1}+c_{\ell})\alpha_{\ell-2}^{p}.

Continuing in this manner one has that

\gamma(p)\geq(c_{1}+\dots+c_{\ell})\alpha_{1}^{p}=\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{n}s_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{n}t_{i}^{2}\bigg)\alpha_{1}^{p}=(1+s_{0}^{2}+t_{0}^{2})\alpha_{1}^{p}\geq\alpha_{1}^{p}.

By the above string of inequalities, since
\alpha_{1}<\dots<\alpha_{\ell}
it is clear that one has equality if and only if
c_{\ell}=c_{\ell-1}+c_{\ell}=\dots=c_{2}+\dots+c_{\ell}=0
and
s_{0}=t_{0}=0
. But this happens if and only if
c_{2}=\dots=c_{\ell}=0
and
s_{0}=t_{0}=0
and so we are done.
∎

As long as one is careful in choosing
x_{0}
we may now compute the minimum eigenvalue of the Gramian of a finite ultrametric space.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
(X,d_{X})=(\{x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\},d_{X})
be a finite ultrametric space. We will say that
X
is degenerate if there is exactly one coterie
B\subseteq X
and
|B|=2
and
x_{0}\in B
. Otherwise we will say that
X
is nondegenerate.

Remark 4.5
.

Note that since we are assuming that
|X|\geq 3
, the points in
X
may always be reordered so that
X
is nondegenerate.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
(X,d_{X})=(\{x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\},d_{X})
be a nondegenerate finite ultrametric space. Then for any
p>0
one has that
\lambda_{min}(G_{p})=\alpha_{1}^{p}/2
.

Proof.

By Corollary
2.4
, if one sets

S=\bigg\{(s,t)\in W_{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}^{2}=1\bigg\}

then
\lambda_{min}(G_{p})=\Gamma_{S}(p)
. Hence

\lambda_{min}(G_{p})=\Gamma_{S}(p)=\frac{1}{2}\inf_{(s,t)\in S}\gamma(p)\geq\alpha_{1}^{p}/2.

Now, since
|X|\geq 3
and
X
is nondegenerate there exists a coterie
B\subseteq X
and
x_{i},x_{j}\in B
with
1\leq i\neq j\leq n
. Then let
s=(s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{n})
where
s_{i}=1/\sqrt{2}
and
s_{k}=0
otherwise, and let
t=(t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
where
t_{j}=1/\sqrt{2}
and
t_{k}=0
otherwise. Then clearly
(s,t)\in S
. Also, for this choice of
(s,t)
it is straightforward to check that
\gamma(p)=\alpha_{1}^{p}
. Hence
\lambda_{min}(G_{p})=\alpha_{1}^{p}/2
as required.
∎

5.
The Corresponding Eigenspace

In this section we provide a description of the eigenspace of the Gramian matrix corresponding to the minimum eigenvalue
\lambda_{min}(G_{p})
. To do so we require the following characterisation of those
(s,t)\in S
for which
c_{2}=\dots=c_{\ell}=0
(see
[
3
]
).

Denote the distinct coteries of
X
by
B_{1},\dots,B_{r}
and let
X_{0}=X\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}B_{i}
. Also, for
Y\subseteq X
we will let
\mathcal{I}(Y)=\{0\leq i\leq n:x_{i}\in Y\}
.

Also, throughout this section we will assume that
X
is nondegenerate.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(s,t)\in S
. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

c_{2}=\dots=c_{\ell}=0
.

(2)

s_{i}=t_{i}=0
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}(X_{0})
and
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}(B_{j})}s_{i}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}(B_{j})}t_{i}
for all
1\leq j\leq r
.

Let

\Pi_{0}=\{\xi=(\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}:\xi_{0}+\xi_{1}+\dots+\xi_{n}=0\}

and then put

F=\bigg\{\xi\in\Pi_{0}:\xi_{i}=0,\forall i\in\mathcal{I}(X_{0})\cup\{0\}\text{ and }\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}(B_{j})}\xi_{i}=0,\forall 1\leq j\leq r\bigg\}.

Also, define
\pi:\Pi_{0}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
by
\pi(\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
E
be the eigenspace of
G_{p}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{min}(G_{p})=\alpha_{1}^{p}/2
. Then
E=\pi(F)
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda=\lambda_{min}(G_{p})=\alpha_{1}^{p}/2
. First suppose that
\eta\in E
. If
\eta=0
then trivially
\eta\in\pi(F)
. Now suppose that
\eta\neq 0
. Since
\eta\in E
one has that
\langle G_{p}\eta,\eta\rangle=\lambda\|\eta\|_{2}^{2}
. If one puts
\xi=\pi^{-1}(\eta)
then simple manipulation of sums gives that
\langle G_{p}\eta,\eta\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}d_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})^{p}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}
, and hence we have that

\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}d_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})^{p}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}+2\lambda\|\eta\|_{2}^{2}=0.

Now define
s=(s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{n})^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
by
s_{i}=\xi_{i}/\|\eta\|_{2}
if
\xi_{i}\geq 0
and
s_{i}=0
otherwise. Similarly, define
t=(t_{0},t_{1},\dots,t_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
by
t_{i}=-\xi_{i}/\|\eta\|_{2}
if
\xi_{i}\leq 0
and
t_{i}=0
otherwise. Note in particular that
(s,t)\in W_{n}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}^{2}=\|\eta\|_{2}^{2}/\|\eta\|_{2}^{2}=1
. Now, the above equality can be rearranged to read as

\gamma(p)=2\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}s_{i}t_{j}d_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})^{p}-\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}(s_{i}s_{j}+t_{i}t_{j})d_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})^{p}=\alpha_{1}^{p}.

Hence, by Theorem
4.3
one has that
c_{2}=\dots=c_{\ell}=0
and
s_{0}=t_{0}=0
for the weight vector
(s,t)
. By Theorem
5.1
this means that
s_{i}=t_{i}=0
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}(X_{0})
and
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}(B_{j})}s_{i}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}(B_{j})}t_{i}
for all
1\leq j\leq r
. By the definition of
(s,t)
this is clearly equivalent to saying that
\xi\in F
. Hence
\eta=\pi(\xi)\in\pi(F)
.

Conversely, suppose that
\eta\in\pi(F)
. Again, if
\eta=0
then
\eta\in E
trivially. Now suppose that
\eta\neq 0
. For this direction we simply reverse the above argument. So,
\xi=\pi^{-1}(\eta)\in F
. Thus if we define
s,t\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
as above then the definition of
F
implies that the weight vector
(s,t)
has
\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}^{2}=\|\eta\|_{2}^{2}/\|\eta\|_{2}^{2}=1
and
c_{2}=\dots=c_{\ell}=0
and
s_{0}=t_{0}=0
. Hence one has that

\gamma(p)=2\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}s_{i}t_{j}d_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})^{p}-\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}(s_{i}s_{j}+t_{i}t_{j})d_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})^{p}=\alpha_{1}^{p}

which can be rearranged to give

\langle G_{p}\eta,\eta\rangle=\lambda\|\eta\|_{2}^{2}.

Since
\lambda
is the minimum eigenvalue of the symmetric matrix
G_{p}
this is enough to conclude that
\eta
is an eigenvector with eigenvalue
\lambda
. That is,
\eta\in E
and so we are done.
∎

Corollary 5.3
.

If
x_{0}\in X_{0}
then the dimension of the eigenspace
E
is
\sum_{i=1}^{r}|B_{i}|-r
. Otherwise, if
x_{0}\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}B_{i}
then the dimension of the eigenspace
E
is
\sum_{i=1}^{r}|B_{i}|-r-1
.

Proof.

It is a simple matter to write down a basis for
F
. To this end, let
e_{0},e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
be the standard basis for
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
. Also, for each
1\leq j\leq r
let
k_{j}\in\mathcal{I}(B_{j})
with
k_{j}\neq 0
. Then it is not hard to see that the set

\{e_{i}-e_{k_{j}}:i\in\mathcal{I}(B_{j}),i\neq k_{j},i\neq 0,1\leq j\leq r\}

is a basis for
F
. Hence the dimension of
F
is given by
\sum_{i=1}^{r}|B_{i}|-r
if
x_{0}\in X_{0}
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{r}|B_{i}|-r-1
otherwise. Then, noting that
\pi:\Pi_{0}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a linear isomorphism it follows that
E
has the same dimension as
F
.
∎

Let
(X,d_{X})=(\{x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5},x_{6}\},d_{X})

7

It is tedious, yet not difficult, to check that
X

B_{1}=\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
,

x_{0}\in X_{0}

Note that the minimum nonzero distance in

\alpha_{1}=1

G_{p}

\lambda_{min}(G_{p})=1/2
, for all

5.3

k_{1}=1,k_{2}=3,k_{3}=5
. Then a basis for
E
, the eigenspace of
G_{p}
corresponding the the eigenvalue
1/2
, is given by

\{e_{2}-e_{1},e_{4}-e_{3},e_{6}-e_{5}\}=\{(-1,1,0,0,0,0)^{T},(0,0,-1,1,0,0)^{T},(0,0,0,0,-1,1)^{T}\}.
