Incorporating exosomes in tissue engineering scaffolds: pave the way toward clinical application
Authors: Jiahui Zheng, Anqi Wang, Jingzhi Wang, Hui Ma, Caining Wen, Yuanmin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In recent years, the use of endosome-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) in regenerative therapies and tissue repair has attracted much attention. However, local or systemic injection of the free state of extracellular vesicles at the site of action is rapidly cleared and lacks a controlled release mechanism. Incorporation of exosomes into tissue-engineered scaffold materials not only facilitates the prolongation of EVs' retention time at the target site but also enhances their ability to induce cell differentiation, exert anti-inflammatory effects, and modulate immune responses. Thus, in this review, we provide a systematic overview of the biological properties of EVs and discuss the current research progress on specific functional components (nucleic acids, proteins, lipids, and others) of EVs in tissue repair and regeneration. More importantly, we demonstrate the design strategies, therapeutic effects, and mechanisms of action of EV-laden bioscaffolds. We also discuss the challenges and prospects in translating therapies of EV-loaded biomaterials into clinical tissue regeneration and repair.

Introduction

Nowadays, with the rising trend of population aging and the increasing prevalence of chronic diseases, therapies that aim at cell regeneration and restoring the function of damaged tissues have gained worldwide attention. Regenerative medicine (RM) is one of the most advanced fields focused on promoting tissue self-repair and ideally achieving organ regeneration[1]. According to a report by Precedence Research, the size of the global RM market reached as much as USD 43.80 billion in 2025 and is projected to become USD 212.80 billion by 2034[2]. Tissue engineering (TE) is a branch of RM, which utilizes biomaterials to create substitutes for injured and dysfunctional organs. Since its establishment in the late 1980s, TE has made significant progress in vital organ regeneration and material innovation[3]. Since the concept of tissue engineering and regenerative medicine (TERM) was introduced, the entire field has been built on three essential elements: bioactive materials (scaffolds), cellular therapy, and growth factors[4].

Stem cell therapy, considered to be a current mainstay of RM, has demonstrated great potential in experiments and clinical applications[5]. In contrast to traditional drug therapy, which only focuses on a specific disease, stem cell therapy is expected to enhance regenerative capacity and restore normal physiological structure and function. However, stem cell therapy faces controversy and raises concerns due to its tumorigenic features [6], risk of immune response[7] and complex ethical issues. Although the use of stem cells remains debated, enthusiasm persists. Further studies have shown that their repair effects are largely mediated by paracrine signaling via proteins, hormones, and EVs[8]. EVs are a group of membrane-encapsulated particles and, based on their biogenesis, are classified into several subtypes: exosomes/sEVs, microvesicles, and apoptotic bodies[9]. Exosomes, with a lipid bilayer structure, can be secreted by nearly all cells and are found in a wide range of body fluids[10]. Compared with stem cells, exosomes have similar functions and are able to survive in various localized microenvironments with a high degree of stability. There exists a broad consensus that EVs derived from human tissue do not pose higher risks than their tissues or cells of origin[11]. It is also widely accepted that exosomes are easier to store[12], less immunogenic, and proven to be biosafe[13].

Owing to these advantages and their paracrine properties, exosomes have been widely employed in various fields, including disease diagnosis and targeted drug delivery. According to the latest data from ClinicalTrials.gov[14] (as of 3 June 2025), a total of 350 exosome-related clinical studies are registered worldwide, covering a variety of areas such as degenerative diseases, oncology, immune disorders, neuropsychiatric disorders, and COVID-19. However, exosomes suffer from a short half-life and unpredictable local duration. Regardless of the administration method or cellular origin, EVs are always rapidly cleared from the circulation by macrophages and may accumulate in the lungs, spleen, liver, and gastrointestinal tract[15]. Multiple injections or overdose applications used to address the limited maintenance time of local applications can be risky. Therefore, in recent years, to fully exert the therapeutic potential of exosomes, attention has also been paid to solving the problems and challenges posed by their application.

In light of these concerns, researchers are trying to look for solutions using scaffolds. Scaffolds are usually composed of natural or synthetic materials that are intended to mimic the function of the natural extracellular matrix, as well as function as carriers for cells and drugs[16]. Previous studies have found that certain stents, when applied alone, do not provide credible clinical benefits and may lead to stent-related adverse events[17]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the strategy of combining exosomes with scaffolds significantly prolongs their retention and sustains their release[18,19]. Thus, loading biologically active substances, such as exosomes, onto scaffolds represents a promising strategy. As scaffold materials and preparation techniques continue to advance, scaffolds are no longer limited to loading exosomes. Accumulating evidence indicates that exosome-loaded scaffolds can also provide structural support and functional enhancement. They have now gained widespread acceptance and demonstrate practical applications in various fields (Fig. 1). Herein, we commence by describing the biogenesis and methods of exosome isolation. Subsequently, we review the specific mechanisms of exosome tissue repair. Finally, we focus on the research progress of exosome-loaded scaffolds, providing a detailed overview from scaffold cross-linking to scaffold applications in various fields. We conclude with an analysis of current limitations and future perspectives on the application of exosomes and exosomal scaffolds.

Review methodology

The comprehensive search for this narrative review was performed using electronic databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. Articles published between January 2015 and October 2025 were considered, with search terms covering key concepts such as “exosome,” “extracellular vesicles,” “scaffold,” “exosome-loaded,” and “tissue engineering.” Additional relevant publications were identified by examining the reference lists of the retrieved articles.

The inclusion criteria encompassed research or review articles focused on the application of exosomes and exosomal scaffolds in TERM, as well as studies related to exosome isolation, characterization, and functional mechanisms. Exclusions consisted of non-English publications, studies with incomplete methodological details or insufficient raw data, and publications deemed irrelevant or only peripherally related to the topic.

Biogenesis of exosomes

The release of exosomes is defined as the secretion of intracellular vesicles (ILVs) through membrane fusion of multivesicular bodies (MVBs)[20] (Fig. 2). At the beginning, plasma membrane endocytosis occurs in the micro-regions enriched with tetraspanins (e.g., CD9, CD63, CD81) in the cell membrane. Then, early endosomes are formed and may fuse with pre-existing early endosomes[21]. Subsequently, early endosomes mature into late endosomes and later MVBs. During this process, sorting of the cargo is involved, which usually requires proteins, cytokines, and also the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi, which promote the formation of early endosomes[22,23]. With alterations in subcellular localization and membrane composition, the early endosomes develop into late endosomes, which have more ILVs and, in turn, form MVBs, characterized by a multivesicular structure[24]. Ultimately, MVBs follow one of two pathways: degradation by lysosomes or fusion with the plasma membrane to release ILVs as exosomes[25]. Figure 2.(A) The biogenesis of exosomes. The formation of MVBs is primarily regulated by the Endosomal Sorting Complex Required for Transport (ESCRT) complexes, which consist of five protein complexes (ESCRT-0, -I, -II, -III, and Vps4)[26]. ESCRT-0, -I, and -II recruit and aggregate ubiquitinated exosomal cargos, while ESCRT-III mediates membrane scission and vesicle formation. Vps4 facilitates the disassembly and recycling of the ESCRT-III complex[24,26]. Chen et al[27] confirm the critical role of ESCRT in promoting exosome generation. For example, modulation of ESCRT subunits such as HRS and TScG101 can influence exosome production and miRNA content.

Recent studies have also proposed another ESCRT-dependent approach. Larios et al[28] reported an ALIX-mediated exosome biogenesis, which depends on ESCRT-III and lysobisphosphatidic acid to subsequently support ILV genesis.

Other ESCRT-independent mechanisms include the ceramide-dependent pathway[29]. Trajkovic et al[30] proposed that MVB generation of proteolipid protein adopts an ESCRT-independent pathway by inhibiting the ESCRT-related protein or cofactors. They also found that this pathway is dependent on lipid raft structures, in which sphingolipids enriched in exosome-associated structural domains are hydrolyzed by neutral sphingomyelinase (nSMase) to form ceramides, which eventually promote ILV production and release. Tetraspanins, such as CD63, also contribute to cargo sorting and exosome formation independently of ESCRT[31,32]. However, the mechanism of action of the different tetraspanins in different cells remains to be fully described[33]. Therefore, a systematic understanding of exosome biogenesis is essential for revealing the molecular diversity of its subgroups and identifying potential targets for their precise regulation.

EV isolation

Different EV subtypes exhibit distinct biogenesis pathways and compositional profiles. To facilitate the clinical application and commercialization of exosomes, it is necessary to overcome the hurdle of production. Current strategies for enhancing yield involve selecting optimal source cells, optimizing culture conditions, and improving isolation methodologies[34,35]. Multiple techniques have been developed for EV isolation, including ultracentrifugation, size-exclusion chromatography, filtration, polymer-based precipitation, immunoaffinity capture, and microfluidic approaches (Fig. 3). At present, the research on exosome separation methods is gradually moving in two directions: first, improving efficiency and purity through method combination; for instance, integrating polymer precipitation with size-exclusion chromatography effectively reduces soluble protein contamination[43]; second, developing novel approaches using advanced materials, such as DNA-programmed hydrogels that enable specific exosome capture while maintaining structural integrity[44].

Nevertheless, all isolation methods face challenges regarding contaminant removal, including proteins, lipoproteins, and polysaccharides[45]. The biological source significantly influences isolation outcomes, as demonstrated by the incompatibility of certain methods across different bodily fluids[46]. Moreover, EV isolation methods affect the structure and activity[47], function[48], and downstream analyses, e.g., proteomic analysis[49], RNA[50]. In summary, although adopting and attempting to combine different separation strategies, the currently existing exosome isolation methods generally have various defects, and there is no optimal isolation method that can balance purity and efficiency while also achieving mass production. This technical bottleneck not only undermines the credibility of current research but also raises questions regarding the validity of exosome functional findings. To truly reveal the biological essence and therapeutic potential of exosomes, standardization of isolation methods must be advanced in parallel with functional investigation.

EVs in modulating tissue repair

Immunomodulation

Regulating inflammatory cells, especially macrophages and their secreted factors, could be a strategic point for exosomes to elaborate on the function of attenuating injury and promoting tissue repair. Current evidence suggests that different stimuli drive macrophage polarization, leading to their differentiation into distinct M1 and M2 phenotypes. On one hand, M1 macrophages secrete pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, etc., which promote tissue damage and trigger inflammatory responses. On the other hand, M2 macrophages release anti-inflammatory factors such as IL-10 and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), supporting tissue regeneration and repair through their anti-inflammatory functions[51].

RNA shuttling plays a major role in immune regulation, especially in modulating macrophage phenotype switching[52]. Li et al[53] demonstrated in vitro and in vivo that miR-24-3p, carried by bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell (BMMSC)-derived exosomes, can attenuate spinal cord ischemia-reperfusion injury by targeting and downregulating the downstream ERN1 (Endoplasmic Reticulum to Nucleus Signaling-1). Specifically, through negative regulation of ERN1, miR-24-3p can induce and enhance macrophage polarization toward the M2 type, consequently inhibiting apoptosis and attenuating tissue injury. Exosomal lncRNA H19 can also function as a competitive endogenous RNA that binds to and suppresses miR-130b-3p, thereby enhancing downstream PPARγ/STAT3-mediated macrophage M2 polarization[54]. Similar mechanisms are likely applicable to other cargo components within exosomes. In the research of acute lung injury, Xia et al[55] observed that adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell (AdMSC)-derived exosomes could transfer mitochondrial components, especially mitochondrial DNA, into alveolar macrophages. This improved mitochondrial function in damaged macrophages and reduced local inflammation by promoting an anti-inflammatory phenotype and decreasing pro-inflammatory cytokine release. Regarding proteins, CD73, which is stably expressed in MSCs and enriched in MSC-derived exosomes, can also mediate macrophage switching from the M1 to the M2 phenotype and lower the levels of the inflammatory factors IL-1β and TNF-α[56]. Current studies often rely on single-source exosomes and single disease models, leading to considerable variability in the reported regulatory molecules and targets. The lack of systematic cross-study comparisons among key nodes involved in macrophage polarization regulation therefore undermines both the generalizability and reliability of the findings.

Vascular regeneration and extracellular matrix production

Exosomes derived from different stem cell sources can initiate vascular regeneration by transmitting proteins and RNA cargos that activate angiogenesis-related factors and signaling pathways[57]. Xu et al[58] demonstrated that reduced levels of miR-224-3p in BMMSC–derived exosomes can promote angiogenesis through the upregulation of FIP200 (FAK-family interacting protein of 200 kDa). Following internalization by vascular endothelial cells, the reduced miR-224-3p also enhances cellular proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities. Mathiyalagan et al[59] verified that exosomes from human CD34 + stem cells, through direct delivery of miR-126-3p to ischemic sites, induce angiogenic gene expression, while miR-10a and miR-130 inside also exhibit anti-fibrotic properties.

Exosomes derived from human umbilical cord blood promote increased phosphorylation of AKT and ERK1/2 through miR-1-3p, thereby inhibiting PTEN and SPRY1, and eventually enhancing fibroblast function[60]. Extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition is equally important as muscle cell regeneration. However, excessive accumulation of ECM can negatively affect the reconstruction of muscle structure and function. Exosomes derived from myogenic progenitor cells can regulate ECM production. MiR-206, transported by them, can inhibit ribosomal binding protein 1, which is a regulator of collagen, and alleviate the fibrotic process[61]. Using a rat full-thickness skin defect model, Zhang et al[62] demonstrated that exosomes released from human-induced pluripotent stem cell-derived mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) significantly promoted re-epithelialization, collagen synthesis, and neovascularization in injured skin. Mechanistically, specific RNAs encapsulated in the exosomes increased the secretion of type I and III collagen and elastin in a dose-dependent manner. Furthermore, bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMMSCs)–EVs have different levels of upregulation for COL1a1 (encoding type I collagen) and COL3a1 (encoding type III collagen)[63]. By upregulating type I collagen, which is widely present in normal tendons, rather than type III collagen, which is associated with scar formation in injured tendons, the type I/III collagen ratio was optimized to enhance the quality of repair.

Cell proliferation, migration, and differentiation

It is widely recognized that cells can be classified into unstable, stable, and permanent based on their ability to regenerate. Unstable cells undergo continuous division, a property stemming from the self-renewal and multidirectional differentiation potential of stem cells. Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes, in in vitro experiments, were found to activate tendon stem/progenitor cells to promote proliferation, migration, and differentiation into tendons[64]. This reveals that exosomes can activate the regenerative potential of the cells and facilitate endogenous tissue repair.

Stable cells, in contrast to permanent cells, which rarely proliferate, are quiescent at a proliferative level in physiological situations, and most of the cells belong to this category. In nerve regeneration, endothelial cell-derived exosomes can promote proliferation, migration, and enhance the anti-apoptotic capacity of Schwann cells. Moreover, they can simultaneously upregulate growth factors and neurotrophic factors associated with angiogenesis and axonal regeneration, as well as immune-related cytokines that recruit macrophages to phagocytose abnormal blood vessels and myelin sheaths. The mechanism may be related to the upregulation of miR199-5p expression by endothelial cell-derived exosomes and activation of the PI3K/AKT/PTEN signaling pathway[65].

However, the regulation of tissue repair by exosomes is not always positive. Jiao et al[66] investigated the effects of polymorphonuclear neutrophil-derived exosomes in sepsis-related acute lung injury. MiR-30d-5p, enriched in the exosomes, can target and down-regulate suppressor of cytokine signaling and sirtuin 1, while activating the NF-κB signaling pathway. This leads to M1-type macrophage activation and triggers macrophage pyroptosis. This finding serves as an important reminder that the negative effects of exosomes in tissue repair should not be overlooked. Recognizing these dual aspects can help refine the selection of exosomes with genuine therapeutic potential.

In conclusion, through the transfer of their cargo components, exosomes can regulate gene expression levels in recipient cells, modulate signaling pathways, and influence macrophage phenotype. Exosomes primarily promote tissue repair and regeneration by: (1) reducing local inflammatory responses; (2) promoting cell proliferation, migration, regeneration, and inhibiting apoptosis; and (3) promoting vascular regeneration, extracellular matrix synthesis, and fiber repair in localized tissues. Most current studies focus on the positive effects, with insufficient attention given to their potential negative effects under pathological conditions. Moreover, systematic evaluations of exosome distribution, targeting efficacy, and long-term safety remain scarce, which poses a significant barrier to clinical translation.

Exosome-loaded scaffolds

Initially, exosomes were primarily administered through local injection. However, subsequent studies have found that the number of exosomes begins to decline within 24 hours of local injection, resulting in diminished stimulation of surrounding cells over time, which limits their function[67]. In order to maximize the function of exosomes, tissue-engineered scaffolds containing exosomes have emerged as a timely solution. The introduction of scaffolds into the clinical application of exosomes facilitates a significant prolongation of the exosome half-life and enables a sustained release of exosomes and an increase in their retention time in tissue[68].

To develop scaffolds that are adapted to the local microenvironment and meet clinical requirements, researchers seek breakthroughs in scaffold preparation processes. With the progress of tissue engineering, a growing body of cross-linking methods is applied to exosome scaffolds. Cross-linking refers to the technology of joining multiple substances or molecules through intermolecular chemical bonding or physical forces. The published exosome-containing scaffolds can be broadly categorized into two groups based on the cross-linking methods: physical cross-linking and chemical cross-linking (Fig. 4). Physical cross-linking

Physical cross-linking typically relies on non-covalent interactions between molecules to integrate different components of the scaffold. This approach does not require a separate cross-linking agent and has the advantages of a simple operation process, environmental friendliness, and controllable cross-linking. Common types of physical cross-linking include electrostatic interactions, ionic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interactions[69].

Electrostatic interactions

Electrostatic interactions result from the force of interaction between positively and negatively charged groups. Under acidic conditions, the amino group at the C2 position of chitosan (CS) becomes protonated and is charged positively. The phospholipid group on the phospholipid bilayer of exosomes is negatively charged. These electrostatic attractions enable not only exosome isolation but also the construction of exosome-loaded scaffolds. Based on this principle, one study grafted CS onto the scaffold and confirmed that it could mediate the controlled loading and release of EVs according to the change in surface charge and, more importantly, enhanced the half-life of exosomes[70].

Ionic interactions

Unlike electrostatic interactions, ionic interactions arise from the mutual attraction between positive and negative ionic charges. When an aqueous alginate (ALG) solution is mixed with divalent cations such as calcium or barium ions, ionic interactions between the carboxyl groups of ALG and the cations occur, leading to hydrogel formation. Shafei et al[71] designed an ALG-based hydrogel loaded with exosomes originating from ADSCs for promoting tissue regeneration in skin wounds. Degradation experiments showed that the scaffold continuously released exosomes for up to 172 hours and demonstrated excellent pro-healing ability in a rat skin wound model. With the aid of ionic interactions, the scaffolds could be formed without the need for special initiators or cross-linkers. Also, the scaffolds could be formed under mild conditions with effective exosome loading.

Hydrogen bonding

Hydrogen bonding, while not classified as a chemical bond, is a fundamental and widely observed intermolecular and intramolecular interaction. Hydrogen bonds are involved, for example, in base pairing between two polynucleotide chains, in the maintenance of the double helix structure of DNA, and in the formation of the secondary structure of proteins. Silk proteins (SPs) have multiple secondary structures, including α-helix, β-sheet, and cross β-sheet, and their secondary structures directly affect mechanical properties[72]. SPs can form irreversible hydrogels at 37°C after ultrasound without the need for cross-linking agents.

Studies have indicated that exosomes encapsulated within SP hydrogels exhibit improved stability, extended half-life, and sustained release profiles, thereby enhancing the function of miR-675 to prevent aging-induced vascular dysfunction. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis showed that SP solutions were high in random coil and α-helix, while SP hydrogels were rich in β-sheet. This suggests that ultrasound alters the secondary structure of SP, which is maintained by hydrogen bonding, and the β-folded SP has an irreversible hydrogel-forming ability, enhances the strength of the scaffold, and slows down the degradation rate[68].

Hydrophobic interactions

Hydrophobic interactions are interactions in which molecules or groups aggregate with each other by repelling contact with water molecules. In scaffold engineering, the application of hydrophobic interactions is usually observed in the self-assembly process of certain multistage polymers. Swanson et al[73] developed an amphiphilic triblock copolymer, Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid)-Polyethylene glycol- Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid), where the PEG and PLGA segments are hydrophilic and hydrophobic, respectively. This molecular design enables the encapsulation of exosomes within a hierarchical internal structure exhibiting a “sphere-in-sphere” morphology, thereby creating reservoirs that reduce exosome degradation and enhance their biological retention and functional performance.

Chemical cross-linking

Chemical cross-linking is based on monomer polymerization or functional group reactions. It enables strong bonding between materials, greatly enhancing the material's strength, stability, and other aspects of its performance. The polymerization process also allows for the addition of a number of functional groups, which further enhances the functionality of the product obtained from cross-linking[74]. Chemical cross-linking relies on the application of cross-linking agents, which are divided into synthetic cross-linking agents, such as glutaraldehyde, and natural cross-linking agents, such as genipin (GNP)[75]. Natural cross-linking agents exhibit considerably lower cytotoxicity compared to other commonly used chemical cross-linking agents.

Photo-crosslinking

Photo-crosslinking refers to the polymerization of substances containing photosensitive groups under ultraviolet or visible light irradiation, with the assistance of a photo-crosslinking agent. Gelatin methacrylate (GelMA) is a common photo-crosslinkable biomaterial produced by the reaction of gelatin with methacrylic anhydride (MA), resulting in acrylic functional groups. Cheng et al[76] prepared an exosome-loaded GelMA hydrogel by UV irradiation with lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate as the photoinitiator. The hydrogel exhibited a suitable gelation time (15 seconds) and a slow degradation rate in vitro, allowing a sustained release of exosomes for up to 14 days.

Imine bond and other dynamic covalent bonds

The dynamic imine bond is formed through a Schiff base reaction between amino and aldehyde groups. Based on this, Yang et al[77] designed a scaffold in which ALG (ALG) was oxidized to introduce aldehyde groups, and adipic dihydrazide (ADH)-modified hyaluronic acid (HA) was used to provide amino groups. FTIR confirms the presence of imine bonds, which constituted an injectable HA-ALG hydrogel system and enabled controlled delivery of exosomes. Notably, the imine bonds conferred biodegradability and self-healing properties. Additionally, the process of modifying HA with ADH, which involves a grafting reaction/graft copolymerization, allows for the addition of the desired reactive groups to the compound. It has also been found that the Schiff base reaction can be controlled by adjusting the ratio of amino and aldehyde groups to achieve controllable physical properties, including gelation time, swelling rate, degradation properties, and mechanical stretchability[78]. Consequently, by varying the amino ratio using grafting reactions to provide more reactive sites, the physical properties of the scaffold can be modulated.

Similar applications of reversible covalent bond cross-linking methods exist in scaffold preparation, such as disulfide bonds and boronic ester bonds, which can undergo reversible breaking, formation, and reorganization under specific external stimuli[79]. For instance, acylhydrazone bonds are formed by the reaction between hydrazide and aldehyde or ketone groups. They are chemical covalent bonds with temperature- and pH-responsive properties that can break in acidic environments (pH 4–7) or under high-temperature catalytic conditions[69]. In the study by Liu et al[80], a vesicle shuttle was designed to capture exosomes via antibodies and then coupled to magnetic nanoparticles for targeted transport via anti-antibodies and exomagnets. The vesicle shuttle also involved pH-cleavable hydrazone bonds. When delivered to the designated site–in this article, the site of myocardial infarction–acidosis-induced breakage of the stilbene bond allows selective release of exosomes. What’s more, researchers achieved selective release of exosomes through pH-responsive hydrazone bonds that connect the exosome to the exosome delivery system, which would break under a specific pH environment.

Click chemistry

Click chemistry is a chemical reaction method with high specificity and high yield in the construction of biomaterials for tissue engineering. Using the mercapto-epoxy click polymerization reaction, aniline tetramer-modified hyperbranched epoxy polymersthiolated, mercapturized HA, and EXOs and anchoring proteins were assembled to form a conductive and injectable hydrogel. This system adapts to the electrical conduction and mechanical function of the heart, with inhibition of cardiac remodeling[81].

Enzymatic cross-linking

Enzyme-catalyzed reactions are characterized by fast reaction rates, mild reaction conditions, and high specificity. As a result, hydrogels produced by enzyme-catalyzed in situ cross-linking are currently gaining acceptance among researchers. Various cross-linking enzymes have been applied in the preparation of bioactive hydrogels, including transglutaminase, tyrosinase, phosphopantetheinyl transferase, lysyl oxidase, and plasma amine oxidase[82].

Since transglutaminase can catalyze the cross-linking between the γ-acyl group on Glutamine and the ε-amine group on Lysine, Xu et al[83] prepared a recombinant human type III collagen hydrogel that rapidly forms at 37°C. Because the extracellular vesicles also possess γ-acyl groups and ε-amine groups, transglutaminase can achieve stable loading and slow release of the exosomes, eventually accelerating wound healing.

Hybrid cross-linking strategies

Even though physical cross-linking offers several advantages, it also has limitations. On one hand, since physical cross-linking is based on the interaction between non-covalent bonds, the association is usually weak, and the stability of the resulting materials is easily compromised. On the other hand, the physical cross-linking method does not allow for the adjustment of the properties of the combined materials, making it impossible to meet specific needs. At the same time, chemical cross-linking also has some shortcomings. In addition to the widely concerning toxicity of the cross-linking agent, it is also characterized by a complicated production process, the generation of toxic by-products, and high production costs.

Because each cross-linking method has advantages and disadvantages, the recent stent development process or production process fully combines all the methods, and the stent produced by this combination has gained increasing attention, which is well-suited to meet the needs of tissue engineering stents.

Bakadia et al[75] designed a dual-crosslinked hydrogel that undergoes two cross-linking steps in its preparation, involving physical, chemical, and enzymatic cross-linking. In the first step, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) solution with dissolved SP, containing silk sericin (SS) and silk fibroin (SF), was mixed with calcium gluconate and thrombin to form “SP@PRP.” In this process, in the presence of Ca2+, thrombin converts fibrinogen in PRP to fibrin, and fibrin undergoes covalent cross-linking catalyzed by Ca2+-activated factor ⅹIIIa (essentially a transglutaminase present in plasma) to form a reticulated multimer. At the same time, the glutamine and lysine residues of factor ⅹIIIa can also cross-link PRP and SP, which contains glutamine and lysine. Additionally, Ca2+ interacts with the amino group of SP to form an ionic bond that further strengthens the hydrogel network. In the second step, exosomes are mixed with SP@PRP in the presence of the cross-linker GNP, which improves the physical and chemical properties of the hydrogel. The chemical cross-linking agent (GNP) creates covalent bonds and forms physical cross-links, transforming the secondary structure from an irregularly coiled predominant SF to a β-folded conformation. This hydrogel utilizes the advantages of two cross-linking methods – physical cross-linking and chemical cross-linking – with suitable mechanical properties, excellent shear-thinning ability, and remarkable self-repairing ability, making it suitable for a variety of clinical application routes, such as injection and topical application.

TE applications of exosome-loaded scaffolds

As mentioned earlier, exosomes have been considered for incorporation into tissue engineering as a repair material due to their remarkable effects. However, the application of directly injected exosomes is limited by the lack of slow-release properties, rapid in vivo degradation, low utilization rate, and poor targeting ability. These limitations necessitate the development of strategies to enhance their performance, leading to the emergence of exosome-containing tissue-engineered scaffolds. Currently, an ideal exosome delivery system is expected to meet the following requirements: (1) good biocompatibility to allow cell adhesion, proliferation, migration, differentiation, and to assist the exosome in performing its function; (2) a suitable structure and surface area that conforms to the size of the exosome to allow its implantation, release, and the transport of oxygen and nutrients; (3) controllable mechanical properties to provide support for the local environment; (4) biodegradability and biosafety appropriate to the need, ensuring that the scaffold and its degradation products are non-cytotoxic and do not cause inflammatory or immune responses.

Commonly used exosome scaffold materials can be classified into inorganic and organic materials according to their origins (Table 2). Inorganic scaffolds, such as those made from metals and hydroxyapatite (HAP), are widely used for bone tissue regeneration. Organic materials are further categorized into natural polymers and synthetic polymers. Natural polymers have good biocompatibility and bioactivity and include polysaccharides, such as ALG and HA, as well as polymerized proteins, such as gelatin and silk proteins. Synthetic polymers are a class of high molecular weight substances synthesized from small molecule monomers by polymerization reactions, including PEG and polyvinyl alcohol[74]. Since the development of new materials often requires long research cycles, researchers have tried to revolutionize fabrication techniques and introduce new technologies, such as 3D printing and electrostatic spinning, which are now widely used in exosome scaffolds. Wound healing

Wound healing involves multiple processes such as epithelialization, angiogenesis, and granulation tissue formation[113], making it one of the most highly regarded issues in tissue repair. Diabetic wounds present additional challenges due to impaired healing mechanisms, including reduced vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) production[114], dysregulated macrophage phenotype[115], and reduced stability of hypoxia-inducible factor 1-α[113], highlighting the importance of optimizing repair strategies. The most commonly used exosome scaffolding strategy in the field of wound healing in tissue engineering is percutaneous delivery, which allows the active ingredient in the material to act more directly on the site to be repaired with convenience.

Hydrogels with high water content and three-dimensional structure have become the most predominant scaffolds in this field due to many advantages, such as good biocompatibility, ease of preparation, and convenience of loading bio-molecules. CS, obtained by deacetylation of chitin, is a commonly used hydrogel material for wound healing, known for its accessibility, high binding capacity, and degradability[116]. Its applications range from forming exosome-loaded hydrogels alone[84] to serving as a component in composite scaffolds. However, the insolubility of CS in the neutral pH state limits its application[117]. As an alternative option, quaternized CS, modified by quaternary amine groups, has enhanced water solubility and, due to the presence of a large number of cations on the modified polymer chain, not only enhances its electrostatic force with exosomes but also its antimicrobial activity[107]. C.L. Vipin’s research team developed a polysaccharide hydrogel composed primarily of ALG dialdehyde and aminated Pluronic F127, loaded with AdMSC exosomes, specifically for irregular burn wounds. In a rat deep partial-thickness burn model, compared to the control group (mean epidermal thickness 107.3 ± 6.2 μm) and the exosome group (80.7 ± 5.0 μm), it demonstrated a faster re-epithelialization trend (74.3 ± 2.6 μm)[118].

PRP obtained from whole blood via centrifugation and containing a high platelet concentration, is becoming a rising star in RM, especially in the healing of skin injuries. PRP not only can be used to extract exosomes but also has been proven to abound with growth factors far beyond physiological concentrations, including platelet-derived growth factor, TGF, and VEGF, and others[87]. In the study by Wang et al[119], collagen/PRP scaffolds carrying adipose mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes were applied to a full-thickness skin defect mouse model. The treatment effectively promoted the proliferation of keratinocytes and fibroblasts and exhibited superior anti-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic activities. Bakadia et al[75] developed an exosomal scaffold prepared from PRP with silk proteins, induced by calcium gluconate/thrombin. The scaffold showed outstanding properties, including self-healing, shear thinning, swelling capacity, and thermal stability. In in vitro experiments and in the rat diabetic wound model, the scaffold reduced NETosis and oxidative stress as well as induced M2 polarization, which facilitated re-epithelialization and wound healing and avoided scarring.

Nanoscience research and technological applications have spread across various fields, and researchers have also introduced nanomaterials into traditional tissue engineering applications. Mesoporous silica encapsulating the anti-inflammatory drug resveratrol and loaded with platelet-derived extracellular vesicles were incorporated into hydrogel scaffolds synthesized from GelMA and SF glycidyl methacrylate. These constructs showed significant potential in promoting diabetic wound healing in a mouse model[97]. Zhang et al[120] designed a double-loaded hydrogel with electrical conductivity for the treatment of chronic wounds in diabetes mellitus. This hydrogel consists of exosomes, metformin, 4-armed SH-PEG, Ag +, and hydroxylation-modified multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWNTs). MWNTs are multilayered tubular structures formed by the curling of graphene, with low chemical reactivity, mechanical strength, and high electrical conductivity[106]. The modified MWNTs significantly enhanced the electrical conductivity and mechanical strength of the hydrogels and promoted wound healing by facilitating bioelectrical signaling. As previously described, metal–ligand bonding occurring between Ag + and sulfhydryl groups can confer injectability and self-repairing properties to the hydrogels, which can also be strengthened by hydrogen bonding present between sulfhydryl and hydroxyl groups from the modified MWNTs. In both in vivo and in vitro experiments, the double-loaded hydrogels showed good biocompatibility, as well as microvascular protection and pro-healing effects.

Cartilage repair

Cartilage tissue consists of extracellular matrix and chondrocytes. Due to the lack of blood vessels, nerves, and lymphatic tissues, complete regeneration and functional restoration following injury remain challenging[121]. Additional limitations include the dilution of drug concentration by synovial fluid, as well as structural and charge barriers in articular cartilage that prevent drug penetration[122]. Current therapeutic strategies for cartilage defects include microfracture, autologous chondrocyte transplantation, autologous osteochondral grafting, and allogeneic osteochondral transplantation[123]. Cartilage tissue engineering scaffolds with controlled mechanical properties and enhanced bioactivity, when loaded with exosomes, provide new possibilities for cartilage repair.

Natural biomaterials, especially those native to the extracellular mesenchyme, are increasingly used for the delivery of biomolecules due to their excellent accessibility and biocompatibility. These endogenous materials reduce inflammation risks associated with macrophage phagocytosis of degradation products compared to exogenous materials[119]. The decellularized extracellular matrix, which removes original cells causing immune rejection, provides a suitable structural and biochemical environment for the cells and retains many of the components of the natural ECM, such as collagen, glycosaminoglycans, proteoglycans, and growth factors[93]. Using deep-low-temperature freeze-drying and imine bonding, Jiang et al[124] fabricated a scaffold with decellularized articular cartilage ECM, which worked in synergy with exosomes from Wharton’s jelly mesenchymal stem cells to exert a function of outstanding subchondral cartilage repair and bone reconstruction, demonstrating its feasibility in the repair of osteochondral defects. Specifically, this scaffold not only exhibits good compatibility contributed by the materials but also possesses a vertically oriented structure that induced a vertical cellular arrangement similar to that of natural cartilage, compared to the disorganized cell arrangement in the group with exosomes applied alone. Given that decellularized extracellular matrix alone struggles to provide sufficient mechanical strength, Shen et al[125] additionally incorporated nanoclay and GelMA, thereby achieving enhanced mechanical properties and biological functions. Similarly, Li et al[91] combined decellularized ECM with 3D printing technology to precisely prepare multilayered bionic scaffolds with complex spatial structures. A mixture of bone- and cartilage-specific decellularized ECM and exosomes isolated from human adipose-derived stem cells was used to make the bioink, in which GelMA, HA-dopamine, and oxidized HA were added to improve the decellularized ECM’s weak mechanical properties. This dual-network bionic scaffold provides a microenvironment to induce specific differentiation, allowing for the simultaneous regeneration of different tissues in complex regions.

However, there are a number of issues that need to be urgently addressed with regard to endogenous biomaterials, such as the fact that materials of biological organismal origin may often have to contend with low yields or insufficient supply[126].

GelMA, a derivative of gelatin, possesses many functional amino acid motifs associated with cell adhesion and ECM remodeling, giving it ECM-like properties. It is more easily available and less costly compared to the aforementioned ECM. More importantly, the methacryloyl group has photo-crosslinking properties, which means it can be polymerized with light exposure and the assistance of a photoinitiator[127]. Inspired by chondroitin sulfate-based scaffolds promoting cartilage-associated protein synthesis by up-regulating the cartilage-specific gene SOX-9, Guan et al[95] linked aldehyde-functionalized chondroitin sulfate with GelMA via Schiff base, while applying exosomes to enrich the scaffolds with an improved immune microenvironment. The final scaffolds possessed advantages in promoting cartilage production and regulating ECM homeostasis. One more thing, pure GelMA materials exhibit scant mechanical properties and uncontrolled degradation, so doping with different components can better improve scaffold performance[98]. Supporting this viewpoint, Hu et al[128] reported that the strength of GelMA was insufficient for the cartilage repair environment, so laponite nanoclay was introduced into GelMA. The incorporation enabled electrostatic interactions between the components and thus significantly enhanced the hydrogel's mechanical properties. The shear-thinning and UV cross-linking properties allowed the hydrogel to fully fill the cartilage defects. This modified GelMA hydrogel was loaded with and assisted human umbilical cord MSC-derived exosomes. The results demonstrated that the exosome-loaded composite hydrogel can promote cartilage regeneration through activation of the PTEN/AKT signaling pathway.

Hydrogel patch is also an exosome scaffolding strategy. Liu et al[129] produced a tissue patch with o-nitrobenzyl-modified HA and gelatin via imine bonding to form a photo-inducible material. After being mixed with exosomes and shaped by LED light irradiation, the resulting scaffold can chemically connect with the surrounding cartilage matrix to form a seamless chondral integration, and therefore ensure durable and efficient positive modulation of the repair of defective cartilage. By comparing it with direct injection and in vitro preforming, the advantages of in situ formation were demonstrated in a rabbit joint defect model. The idea of combining scaffolds with arthroscopic technology was proposed, which is promising to become the next minimally invasive cartilage injury regeneration treatment breakthrough point.

Bone regeneration

Due to the complexity of the bone structure and its indispensable role in supporting and protecting the organism, bone scaffolds must provide mechanical strength and stability under stress while supporting bone tissue regeneration[130]. The difference with other scaffolds is that, in addition to biocompatibility, bone tissue engineering (BTE) scaffolds are faced with new challenges: the requirements for osteoinductivity and osteoconductivity[131]. Osteoinductivity refers to the ability to recruit host MSCs to promote osteogenesis, and osteoconductivity refers to the structural suitability for the growth of new bone tissue. Since bone tissue is highly vascularized, its insufficiency can affect nutrient transport and metabolic waste removal[132]. Therefore, both the simulation of bone structure and vascularization strategies should be taken into account in BTE.

It is widely accepted that osteoid, together with osteocytes composing bone tissue, constitutes the main component of bone, which includes both organic and inorganic parts. The inorganic component, also known as bone salt, consists mainly of calcium phosphate in the form of HAP. Such calcium phosphate-based biomaterials, also called Ca/P-based ceramics, which constitute important components of bioactive ceramics, include HAP and tricalcium phosphate[108]. These materials demonstrate broad practical prospects due to their excellent biocompatibility, bioactivity, and similarity to bone salt composition[133]. Containing nanoscale HAP synthesized by a hypergravity technique, an ALG-HA composite hydrogel connected by Schiff base bonding had shown outstanding mechanical properties without the requirement of chemical cross-linking agents[77]. With human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells-derived exosomes, it promoted bone defect repair and exhibited enhanced angiogenic capabilities. In addition, a nanocement incorporating calcium sulfate and nano-HAP has been designed to deliver exosomes, which promote bone regeneration by allowing cellular infiltration, vascularization, and matrix deposition[109]. Adopting a scaffold optimization strategy, Wang et al[85] used the biomimetic material coral hydroxyapatite as the main component and further added bifunctionalized polyethylene glycol to provide cross-linking sites, SF, and glycol CS to mimic the extracellular matrix, which together form a hydrogel that combines the advantages of room-temperature cross-linking, rapid self-healing, and excellent mechanical properties to promote bone healing and vascularization. This composite scaffold, which combines the excellent properties of various materials, once again demonstrates the promising prospect of multifunctional composite scaffold development.

Unlike calcium-based materials, metal-based materials have strong mechanical properties that make them particularly suitable for hard tissue replacement, and when modified by exosomes, their lack of bioactivity can be compensated[134]. Considering its light weight, biocompatibility, osteophilicity, and mechanical properties, titanium metal stands out among all the metal biomaterials used in BTE. To improve the bioactivity of titanium scaffolds, exosomes produced by bone morphogenetic protein 2-stimulated macrophages, which have been shown to have a positive effect on osteogenesis, were used to modify titanium nanotubes possessing a three-dimensional structure[110]. This scaffold provided favorable conditions for the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs, such as activation of cellular autophagy and stimulation of cytokine release. Alternatively, the implantation of titanium metal as a scaffold has also caused some problems. Due to its high elastic modulus, stress shielding may occur after implantation, and the degree of deformation under stress is not consistent with the surrounding bone tissue, which can lead to osteoporosis[135]. Researchers are struggling to find solutions to avoid stress shielding. In this context, porous Ti6Al4V scaffolds fabricated via electron beam melting 3D printing technology offer precise matching to bone defect sites; their porous structure helps mitigate stress shielding inherent in titanium alloys due to high elasticity, showing positive effects on bone repair when combined with exosomes from Chevron cells[136].

As polymer science continues to evolve, polymers – synthetic ones in particular – are increasingly being applied as scaffolding materials or additives for exosome delivery due to their convenient production, low cost, and tunable properties compared to the traditional materials mentioned above. Poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL), polyglycolic acid (PGA), and poly(lactic acid) (PLA) scaffolds, along with their copolymers, are the most widely used biodegradable bioscaffolds in clinical applications[112]. Wang et al[100] prepared PCL scaffolds through electrostatic spinning and modified them with polydopamine (PDA), followed by co-incubation with S-nitrosoglutathione (CSNO) to obtain PCL/PDA + CSNO composite scaffolds, in which CSNO acts as a nitric oxide donor to reduce the expression of inflammatory cytokines. In vivo experiments in this study confirmed the exosome scaffold’s role in modulating inflammation and contributing to bone regeneration, but unfortunately, no further in vivo experiments were performed. Further modifications to polymer scaffolds can significantly improve scaffold performance. For instance, polyethyleneimine-modified PCL scaffolds exhibited increased hydrophilicity and suppressed inflammatory responses[137]. Alternatively, silver ions, known for their bactericidal properties and immunomodulatory capacity, were used to modify PCL/exosome scaffolds, enhancing their anti-inflammatory and osteogenic capacities [99]. Another team utilized electrostatic spinning to create PLGA scaffolds modified with gallic acid and magnesium ions (Mg2+). This scaffold, containing hADSCs-derived exosomes, benefited from the anti-inflammatory properties of Mg2+ and gallic acid, which contributed to bone regeneration in terms of osteogenesis and angiogenesis[138]. However, one in vitro experiment discovered that Mg2+ released during the degradation of Mg scaffolds inhibits macrophage activity and angiogenesis and even induces apoptosis. Thus, more in vivo experimental data may be required to clarify the pros and cons of Mg materials in such applications[111]. At the same time, there are polymers with special properties being used in tissue engineering, such as those with temperature responsiveness or electrical conductivity. Poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAAm) hydrogel has unique heat-sensitive properties and can undergo reversible hydrophilic-hydrophobic transformations at specific temperatures. Combining these properties with the photothermal characteristics of black phosphorus (BP), Xu et al[102] developed a PNIPAAm-based hydrogel capable of achieving the efficient and controlled release of exosomes using a near-infrared laser. In this system, BP, when warmed under near-infrared irradiation, caused the hydrogel to contract with water loss, allowing the exosomes to enter the corresponding bone tissues along with water molecules. The exosomes, together with BP, synergistically promoted the formation of new bone.

Inspired by nature, some novel biomimetic materials have been gradually applied in TE. The scaffolds derived from organismal components, composed of decellularized fish scales – whose main components are HAP and collagen fibers – and exosomes derived from osteogenic differentiated BMSCs[94]. This biohybrid scaffold promoted osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, subsequently facilitating bone tissue repair and structural remodeling. However, further studies are needed to elucidate the specific mechanism. Given that comorbidities of osteochondral defects are common in clinical practice and the moist environment where scaffolds for bone-cartilage repair are usually applied, there is an urgent need to develop materials capable of exerting strong adhesion under wet conditions. The mussel foot proteins of marine mussels possess a variety of residues that allow them to adhere tightly to the seafloor. Accordingly, mussel-inspired hydrogels have already been explored, and among the various catechol-based derivatives developed, dopamine is one of the most typical representatives[139]. Injectable hydrogel with ALG-dopamine, chondroitin sulfate, and regenerated SF as main materials can effectively encapsulate BMSC-derived exosomes and maintain high adhesion in the highly humid environment at the site of cartilage defects[96]. Dopamine’s strong adhesion is also reflected in its adherence to other base materials, making it suitable for improved adhesion between exosomes and loaded scaffolds. Hou et al[140] coated the 316 L stainless steel scaffolds with PDA, and the PDA coating showed good exosome loading ability, in which negatively charged PDA could bind positively charged exosomes by electrostatic force. Dopamine-modified gelatin sponge maintains high adhesion and good degradability, while its porous structure provides a larger attachment surface for AdMSCs-derived-exosomes. The scaffold was proven to induce osteogenic differentiation of stem cells to promote bone repair both in in vitro experiments and in a rat femoral defect model[89].

A variety of scaffold fabrication techniques have been put into use in BTE, including solvent casting/particulate leaching, gas foaming, freeze-drying, phase separation, electrospinning, and 3D printing[138]. Diomede et al[112] designed 3D-printed PLA scaffolds and loaded them with polyethyleneimine-modified human gingival mesenchymal stem cell extracellular vesicles. The scaffolds maintained optimal mechanical stability during degradation while enhancing bone mineralization and promoting angiogenesis. Combining desktop-stereolithography 3D-printing with photo-crosslinking technology, Chen et al[141] prepared a bioink containing exosomes, ECM, and GelMA and printed a scaffold with radial channels, whose radial structure was more beneficial to the growth of cells and tissues compared with the random structure. In their study, the delivery of exosomes promoted the restoration of the function of cartilage mitochondria, which provided new insights for the treatment of osteoarthritis.

Currently, microfluidic technology is extensively implemented in clinical applications. Inspired by the elevated levels of matrix metalloproteinase-1 (MMP1) in neovascularized bone, Yang et al[103] developed MMP1-sensitive hydrogel microspheres using a droplet-based microfluidic platform through a mixture of self-assembling peptides, GelMA, and exosomes derived from osteogenically-induced BMSCs. The system exhibited MMP1-sensitive release capability, responding to microenvironmental MMP1 fluctuations during neovascularization, thereby achieving spatiotemporally controlled exosome release and subsequent stem cell recruitment for bone regeneration. Technologies such as 3D printing, electrospinning, and microfluidics have provided diverse approaches for scaffold fabrication, but the lack of direct comparative studies among these techniques makes it difficult to determine their relative advantages.

Nerve regeneration

Progressive loss of neurons underlies the pathogenesis of a group of neurodegenerative diseases, including but not limited to Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, stroke, and spinal cord injury (SCI)[142]. In the context of global aging, neurodegenerative diseases pose a serious threat to population health and quality of life, and place a heavy burden on society. Due to the limited self-repairing ability of the adult brain, researchers have tried to use cell therapy to replace lost neurons, but new problems have arisen, such as low survival rates of transplanted cells and difficulties in integrating them into host tissues[143]. To this end, cell-free therapy has emerged. Exosomes have the unique advantage of breaking the blood-brain barrier[144], together with bio-scaffolds that can provide a suitable microenvironment for nerve regeneration. The combination of exosomes and bio-scaffolds holds out promise for nerve repair and regeneration.

Collagen is a common scaffold material used for exosome delivery for nerve fiber restoration. Zhang et al[88] used a biospecific peptide (BSP), which contains collagen- and exosome-binding domains, as an intermediate to specifically bind a linearly ordered collagen scaffold (LOCS) and paclitaxel (PTX)-containing exosomes of human umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (MExos) to achieve more robust binding under mild conditions. In a rat model of spinal cord transection, LOCS-BSP-MExos-PTX showed excellent results in terms of nerve regeneration, scar reduction, and functional improvement. Their further studies attempted to address the biosafety of BSP and the problem of exosome loading in the scaffold[145]. Through genetic engineering, the CBD-fused Lamp2b protein was overexpressed in cells by taking advantage of the fact that exosomes are enriched with Lamp2b, which can be fused with targeted peptides, in order to directly generate exosomes enriched with collagen-binding structural domains that can bind to collagen-I scaffolds.

In fact, not only are more biocompatible materials being selected for scaffold selection, but some researchers have adopted preparation methods that mimic natural physiological processes. By mimicking the physiological coagulation process, He et al[90] used a mixture of fibrinogen and thrombin solution to prepare fibrin gels (GEL) loaded with rat BMSC-derived exosomes for nerve repair in SCI Scanning electron microscopy confirmed that Gel-Exo possessed a porous structure that facilitates cellular infiltration and migration, while mechanical testing demonstrated that the scaffold has similar mechanical strength to spinal cord tissue. Its advantages lie in good biocompatibility, safety, and degradability, which are manifested in the fact that fibrin can be dissolved by cell-generated fibrinolytic enzymes, and its degradation products have a positive effect on physiological processes such as cell proliferation and adhesion. With the addition of fibrinolytic enzyme inhibitors, the time of its degradation can be controlled, and its porosity and mechanical strength can also be adjusted through the adjustment of the ratio of fibrinogen and thrombin. Besides, in SCI mice models, Gel-Exo enhanced neurogenesis and synaptic function and could deliver the neuropeptide precursor VGF, which is closely related to myelin sheath formation and oligodendrocyte development, to the lesion site, further facilitating the recovery of SCI by inducing oligodendrocyte regeneration.

In addition to the selection of scaffold materials with different properties, a wealth of scaffold preparation techniques has been introduced. Self-assembling peptides are a class of peptides with repetitive peptide sequences that, under certain conditions, are capable of spontaneously forming ordered nanostructures through non-covalent bonding. Common building motifs for self-assembling peptides include EAK16, RADA16, and their dexter-form analogs[104]. Advocating self-assembled peptide nanofiber scaffolds as the optimal choice, Hajinejad et al[146] utilized the self-assembled peptide RADA16 to form 3D nanoscaffolds incorporating stromal-derived factor-1α, which mediates central neurohomeostasis and neuronal cell homing, and loaded them with exosomes derived from human neural stem cells for the treatment of traumatic brain injury (TBI). In adult rat models, the scaffolds successfully ameliorated oxidative stress, inhibited pro-inflammatory effects, and promoted cell proliferation, making them a promising treatment for TBI.

In addition to pursuing innovations in different properties of scaffold materials and scaffold preparation techniques, in response to the characteristics of nerve repair, especially when faced with the difficulty of repairing large nerve defects, the researchers attempted to guide and promote the regeneration of peripheral nerve repair through Nerve Guidance Conduits[147]. Rao et al[86] confirmed that degradable chitin conduits combined with exosomes derived from gingival stem cells can effectively promote nerve fiber regeneration. Wang et al[148] obtained engineered exosomes with miR-218 by transfection of adipose-derived stem cells cultivated in a low CO2 environment, which, together with 3-hydroxybutyric-co-3-hydroxyvaleric acid nanofibrous tubes obtained by electrostatic spinning, promoted myelin sheath formation and nerve fiber repair. The process also brought enlightenment that the exosomes produced by the cells in different environments have different characteristics.

Cardiac regenerative repair

The major obstacles to tissue engineering strategies for cardiac regenerative repair are the lack of suitable cell sources, the lack of biomaterials compatible with myocardial structure and function, the difficulty in vascularizing grafts, and the insufficient maturity of laboratory-cultured myocardium[149]. During the cardiac cycle, cardiomyocytes exhibit excitatory, conductive, and contractile properties, sometimes including autoregulation. Correspondingly, this requires that cardiac scaffold materials also possess dynamic deformability and conductivity to accommodate the electrophysiological properties of the heart as well as its pump function.

HA is an unbranched macromolecular polysaccharide composed of D-glucuronic acid and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, with excellent viscoelasticity, physiological activity, and biocompatibility. Additionally, its biocompatibility and biodegradability can be improved through further modification to accommodate applications in different tissues[150], and it is currently a common biomaterial for cardiac tissue repair. Chen et al[92] modified HA using adamantane and β-cyclodextrin, and the hydrogels, a lyophilized mixture of the two, encapsulated rat EPC-derived EVs, which were confirmed to promote peri-infarct angiogenesis. More importantly, this modification provides the obtained hydrogel with shear-thinning properties, which behaved as an injectable liquid form in the presence of shear stress, and cross-linking could be realized in situ when the shear stress disappeared. Overall, the cardiac repair process is rather slow, and additional stent removal operations are unfavorable. Therefore, in terms of the requirements for the physical properties, the stents need to maintain a balance between cell retention and degradability. In the study by Czosseck et al[101], porous scaffolds made of PLGA and poly(L-lactic acid) were synthesized using electrospinning. These scaffolds exhibited physical stability and a high epitope-to-body ratio, enabling them to avoid host immune attacks, increase exosome-carrying capacity, provide a suitable degradation time window, and improve cell survival at sites of ischemic hypoxia.

In recent years, there has been an increasing number of studies on hydrogels, 3D bioprinting, electrostatic spinning, bioinks, and cardiac patches, with proven potential in cardiac tissue engineering. Cardiac regeneration research is increasingly heading toward engineering functional, complex, and three-dimensional[105], which has led to the introduction of the concept of scaffolds. In addition, it is worth noting that in cardiac injury repair strategies, besides the requirement for materials, researchers and clinicians are more focused on minimizing trauma and operating quickly. One concern for stent implantation is the risk of infection triggered by open-heart surgery. To address this, Yao et al[151] devised a minimally invasive exosome spray to repair myocardial infarction regions. A mixture of exosomes with fibrin solution and thrombin was filled into a dual-chamber syringe separately and, under thoracoscopy, sprayed locally into the infarcted area through a small incision to form an in situ hydrogel. This strategy was effective in improving cardiac proliferation and vascular regeneration while reducing trauma, surgical stress, and inflammatory response compared to invasive cardiac patch methods. Following a similar philosophy, Zhu et al[152] utilized HA in combination with MA to form photocross-linkable MA-HA hydrogels, loading exosomes that were used for cardiac repair after myocardial infarction, as well as temperature-sensitive hydrogels with decellularized extracellular matrix. Both approaches have demonstrated resistance to myocardial remodeling and promoted repair in animal models. The distinguishing features are that the intrapericardial injection works with less invasiveness and requires a shorter time, followed by optimal biodistribution in the heart using the pericardial cavity as a natural mold.

Conclusions and future perspectives

Exosome research has progressed rapidly in recent years. This cell-free technology holds great potential, especially when combined with multifunctional scaffolds. The diversity of exosome structures and cargoes gives them broad potential for clinical applications. Engineered exosomes, noted for their biogenesis and delivery mechanisms, have emerged as promising drug delivery platforms in this field. From a drug development perspective, exosome-based therapeutics carry potential risks, including tumorigenicity and unintended differentiation in target cells. However, short-term animal studies have demonstrated that exosomes effectively promote functional regeneration without causing abnormal tissue hyperplasia or tumor formation within the observed timeframe. Current evidence supports the short-term safety of exosomes. However, long-term effects still need further investigation before clinical translation[153]. In addition to safety considerations, significant challenges remain before clinical translation. These include inherent issues such as exosome heterogeneity, difficulties in standardized isolation/purification, and unclear mechanisms. From a commercialization perspective, the scalable production of high-purity exosomes remains a major challenge. While multiple isolation techniques exist, no single method has proven universally applicable for processing samples from diverse biological sources. This limitation is even more pronounced for industrial-scale production, which must balance large-scale output, high purity, and cost-effectiveness. Beyond these technical hurdles, the clinical translation of exosome-based therapies must also overcome challenges common to most tissue engineering applications – particularly the establishment of clear regulatory guidelines and the resolution of complex ethical considerations that accompany these advanced therapies[154].

However, the unique advantages of exosomes as RM therapy are well supported by current evidence. An exosome-loaded scaffold combines the development of RM EVs-related technology, which greatly addresses the obstacles of exosomes transitioning from the bench to bedside and promotes advancements in biomaterials, clinical treatment, and other fields. Exosome scaffold preparation is still in development, evolving from direct mixing to the current combination of multiple technologies, from synthetic cross-linking agents to the development of natural cross-linking agents, and from a single material to highly simulating the extracellular matrix of bionic materials. It is reasonable to believe that the development of scaffold technology can further advance the clinical application of exosomes. For exosome-loaded scaffolds, future research should focus on enabling progression from in vitro and in vivo studies to clinical trials. Optimizing scaffold-exosome integration strategies to enhance loading efficiency and stability can be a valid route. With continued innovation in exosome engineering and scaffold design, this strategy is anticipated to revolutionize regenerative therapies in the coming decade.
