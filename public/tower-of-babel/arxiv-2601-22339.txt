Quantum-Inspired Reinforcement Learning for Secure and Sustainable AIoT-Driven Supply Chain Systems
Muhammad Bilal Akram Dastagir, Omer Tariq, Shahid Mumtaz, Saif Al-Kuwari, Ahmed Farouk

Abstract
Modern supply chains must balance high-speed logistics with environmental impact and security constraints, prompting a surge of interest in AI-enabled Internet of Things (AIoT) solutions for global commerce. However, conventional supply chain optimization models often overlook crucial sustainability goals and cyber vulnerabilities, leaving systems susceptible to both ecological harm and malicious attacks.To tackle these challenges simultaneously, this work integrates a quantum-inspired reinforcement learning framework that unifies carbon footprint reduction, inventory management, and cryptographic-like security measures. We design a quantum-inspired reinforcement learning framework that couples a controllable spin-chain analogy with real-time AIoT signals and optimizes a multi-objective reward unifying fidelity, security, and carbon costs. The approach learns robust policies with stabilized training via value-based and ensemble updates, supported by window-normalized reward components to ensure commensurate scaling. In simulation, the method exhibits smooth convergence, strong late-episode performance, and graceful degradation under representative noise channels, outperforming standard learned and model-based references, highlighting its robust handling of real-time sustainability and risk demands. These findings reinforce the potential for quantum-inspired AIoT frameworks to drive secure, eco-conscious supply chain operations at scale, laying the groundwork for globally connected infrastructures that responsibly meet both consumer and environmental needs.

Problem Formulation and Objective

We seek to develop a multi-objective control policy for an
N
-spin quantum system subject to AIoT-driven supply chain requirements. Specifically, we aim to optimize a joint objective that includes quantum fidelity, security, and sustainability.

H_{\mathrm{dyn}}(t)=\frac{J}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\!\!\Bigl(\sigma_{n}^{x}\sigma_{n+1}^{x}+\sigma_{n}^{y}\sigma_{n+1}^{y}\Bigr)+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\!B_{n}(t)\,\sigma_{n}^{z},

\widetilde{H}(t)\;=\;H_{\mathrm{dyn}}(t)\;+\;\Delta H_{\mathrm{noise}}(t).

Let

denote the quantum state at time

, evolving according to

\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\psi(t)}=-\,\mathrm{i}\,\widetilde{H}(t)\,\ket{\psi(t)},\quad\ket{\psi(0)}=\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{init}}}.

(3)

A conceptual
N{=}3
example is shown in Fig.

, highlighting the spin sites, nearest-neighbor XY couplings (

), and local

B_{n}(t)
with schematic noise. This diagram maps directly to (

)–(

State and Action Spaces:

To cast this problem as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), define the system

The wavefunction

\displaystyle r_{t}\;=

2
Controllability Lemmas

In quantum control, an essential property is
controllability
, i.e., whether it is possible to reach any state (or at least the target state) from a given initial state. The following lemmas provide the foundation for controlling an
N
-spin chain with a set of available local or global operators.

Lemma 1

(Rank Condition for Controllability)
.

Let

\mathcal{L}\;=\;\mathrm{span}\{\mathrm{i}H_{0},\,\mathrm{i}H_{1},\,\dots,\mathrm{i}H_{K}\},

(8)

be the Lie algebra generated by the drift Hamiltonian
H_{0}
and control Hamiltonians
H_{1},\dots,H_{K}
in

H(t)\;=\;H_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}u_{k}(t)\,H_{k}.

(9)

Let
\mathcal{L}
acts
irreducibly
on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
of dimension
2^{N}
and that

\dim(\mathcal{L})=4^{N}-1
.
Then the system is
fully controllable
, i.e., for any desired unitary
U\in\mathrm{SU}(2^{N})
, one can find a piecewise-constant control sequence
\{u_{k}(t)\}
such that the time-evolution operator
U(T)
approximates
U
arbitrarily closely.

Proof.

The proof follows standard arguments in quantum control based on the
rank
(or
dimension
) condition of the dynamical Lie algebra. Recall:

1.

A quantum system on an
N
-qubit Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\cong(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes N}
has dimension
2^{N}
.

2.

The corresponding Lie algebra of all traceless Hermitian operators (infinitesimal generators of
\mathrm{SU}(2^{N})
) has dimension
4^{N}-1
:

\dim\bigl(\mathrm{su}(2^{N})\bigr)\;=\;(2^{N})^{2}\,-\,1\;=\;4^{N}\,-\,1.

(10)

3.

If the Lie algebra generated by the available Hamiltonians (including the factor
\mathrm{i}
) spans the entire
\mathrm{su}(2^{N})
, then the system is
fully controllable
. In that case, one can construct any desired unitary evolution within
\mathrm{SU}(2^{N})
via appropriate piecewise-constant controls.

Hence, under the assumption that
\dim(\mathcal{L})=4^{N}-1
and
\mathcal{L}
acts irreducibly on
\mathcal{H}
, it follows that
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathrm{su}(2^{N})
. Consequently, one obtains the full set of generators needed to approximate any target operator
U\in\mathrm{SU}(2^{N})
. Standard Trotter or product-formula arguments then show that piecewise-constant controls suffice to reach any neighborhood of
U
. This establishes
full controllability
.
∎

Lemma 2

(Approximate Reachability Under Noise)
.

Consider the noisy Hamiltonian

\widetilde{H}(t)\;=\;H(t)+\Delta(t),

(11)

where
H(t)
is the ideal (noise-free) Hamiltonian from (
9
) and
\Delta(t)
is an unknown noise term, subject to the bound

\|\Delta(t)\|\;\leq\;\delta_{\max}\quad\text{for all }t\in[0,T].

(12)

If the underlying noiseless system is fully controllable, then for sufficiently small
\delta_{\max}
, there exists a piecewise-constant control protocol
\{u_{k}(t)\}
that drives an initial state
\ket{\psi(0)}
to a final state
\ket{\psi(T)}
within
\epsilon
distance of a desired target state
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{target}}}
. Concretely,

\|\ket{\psi(T)}-\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{target}}}\|\;\leq\;\epsilon.

(13)

Proof.

Let
U_{\mathrm{ideal}}(t)
be the time-evolution operator generated by the ideal Hamiltonian
H(t)
, and let
\widetilde{U}(t)
be the corresponding evolution operator in the presence of the noise term
\Delta(t)
. Their dynamics satisfy:

\frac{dU_{\mathrm{ideal}}(t)}{dt}\;=\;-\mathrm{i}H(t)\,U_{\mathrm{ideal}}(t),\quad U_{\mathrm{ideal}}(0)=I,

(14)

\frac{d\widetilde{U}(t)}{dt}\;=\;-\mathrm{i}\bigl(H(t)+\Delta(t)\bigr)\,\widetilde{U}(t),\quad\widetilde{U}(0)=I.

(15)

Then the difference
\widetilde{U}(t)-U_{\mathrm{ideal}}(t)
can be bounded by standard Grönwall
[
13
]
and Dyson-series
[
7
]
arguments. Specifically:

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{U}(t)-U_{\mathrm{ideal}}(t)\|\;\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\|\Delta(\tau)\|\,\|\widetilde{U}(\tau)\|\,d\tau

(16)

\displaystyle\;\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{\max}\,\|\widetilde{U}(\tau)\|\;d\tau.

noting that
\|\widetilde{U}(\tau)\|
is bounded (since it is a unitary or close to unitary evolution). For
\delta_{\max}
sufficiently small, the deviation accumulated by the noise remains arbitrarily small over the finite interval
[0,T]
.

Because the noiseless system is fully controllable, there exists a piecewise-constant protocol that produces the exact final state
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{target}}}
under
H(t)
. Small noise
\Delta(t)
perturbs this evolution only slightly, so we can adjust the control protocol (or accept a small residual error) to ensure

\|\ket{\psi(T)}-\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{target}}}\|\;\leq\;\epsilon.

(17)

Thus,
approximate reachability
holds for sufficiently small bounded noise.
∎

Theorem 1

(Well-Posedness and Ensemble RL Solution)
.

The Markov decision process

\mathcal{M}\;=\;\bigl(\mathcal{S},\,\mathcal{A},\,P,\,r,\,\gamma\bigr)

(18)

A convex combination of two sub-policies (e.g., PPO and DQN) yields a valid policy

that captures both on-policy and off-policy learning.

3.

Solvability:
Bounded rewards and a geometric discount ensure the Bellman equations are well-posed, allowing classical RL convergence results to apply.

Consequently,
\mathcal{M}
is
well-posed
and supports an ensemble RL approach.

Consider the MDP

\mathcal{M}\;=\;\bigl(\mathcal{S},\,\mathcal{A},\,P,\,r,\,\gamma\bigr),

where
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,r,
and

are defined as in

2
,

.

To show that the MDP

\mathcal{M}\;=\;\bigl(\mathcal{S},\,\mathcal{A},\,P,\,r,\,\gamma\bigr)

(20)

is well-posed and admits a valid ensemble RL solution, the following points are verified:

Definition 1

(Well-Defined MDP)
.

A Markov decision process (MDP) is given by the tuple

\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\,\mathcal{A},\,P,\,r,\,\gamma)
,
where:

r(s,a)\;\in\;\bigl[r_{\min},\,r_{\max}\bigr],\qquad 0<\gamma<1.

(21)

In the quantum + AIoT setting,
\mathcal{S}
contains the real and imaginary parts of
\ket{\psi}

\mathcal{A}
comprises discrete or continuous control fields; and

G_{t}\;=\;\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\,r(S_{t+k},A_{t+k})

Definition 2

(Ensemble RL Framework)
.

An

\pi_{\mathrm{PPO}}(a\mid s;\,\theta_{\mathrm{PPO}})
(on-policy, e.g., Proximal Policy Optimization) and

\pi_{\mathrm{DQN}}(a\mid s;\,\theta_{\mathrm{DQN}})
(off-policy, e.g., Deep Q-Network). Their mixture is defined by a convex combination

\displaystyle\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s)\;=

\displaystyle\alpha\,\pi_{\mathrm{PPO}}\bigl(a\mid s;\,\theta_{\mathrm{PPO}}\bigr)

(23)

\displaystyle+\bigl(1-\alpha\bigr)\,\pi_{\mathrm{DQN}}\bigl(a\mid s;\,\theta_{\mathrm{DQN}}\bigr).

where

. Each component
\pi_{\mathrm{PPO}}
and
\pi_{\mathrm{DQN}}
is a valid stochastic policy over the action set
\mathcal{A}
, so
\pi_{\theta}

\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s)\geq 0

\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s)=1

.

Definition 3

(Solvability and RL Convergence)
.

When

V^{\pi}(s)\;=\;\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\,r(S_{t+k},A_{t+k})\,\Bigm|\,S_{t}=s,\;\pi\Bigr]

(24)

remains finite for all

\hat{\pi}
satisfying

\hat{\pi}\;\in\;\arg\max_{\pi}\,V^{\pi}(s),\quad\forall\,s.

(25)

In the ensemble scheme (
23

solvable
MDP.

Definitions

–

\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,r,\gamma)

\pi_{\theta}

(

V^{\pi^{*}}(s)-V^{\pi_{\theta^{(m)}}}(s)\;\leq\;\epsilon,

for all

.

Proof.

By classical off-policy RL theory (e.g., Q-learning), the state-action value function
Q^{(m)}
converges to
Q^{*}
under usual conditions (sufficient exploration, suitable learning rate decay, etc.). Formally,

\lim_{m\to\infty}Q^{(m)}(s,a)\;=\;Q^{*}(s,a)\quad\text{w.p. }(1-\delta_{1}).

(27)

The DQN policy

\pi_{\mathrm{DQN}}^{(m)}(a\mid s)\approx\arg\max_{a}Q^{(m)}(s,a)

then approaches the
\epsilon
-optimal solution for any
\epsilon>0
.

Policy gradient methods (e.g., PPO) optimize a differentiable objective:

J(\theta_{\mathrm{PPO}})\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi},\;a\sim\pi_{\mathrm{PPO}}}\bigl[r(s,a)\bigr],

(28)

where
d^{\pi}
is the (discounted) visitation distribution under policy
\pi
. With diminishing step sizes and stable updates, standard theory implies

\lim_{m\to\infty}\theta_{\mathrm{PPO}}^{(m)}\;=\;\theta_{\mathrm{PPO}}^{*},

(29)

with probability
1-\delta_{2}
, where
\theta_{\mathrm{PPO}}^{*}
corresponds to a locally or globally optimal parameter for the on-policy objective. Consequently,

\pi_{\mathrm{PPO}}^{(m)}
becomes
\epsilon
-optimal in the limit.

Let us define the ensemble policy by

\displaystyle\pi_{\theta^{(m)}}(a\mid s)

\displaystyle=\;\alpha\,\pi_{\mathrm{PPO}}^{(m)}(a\mid s)

(30)

\displaystyle+\;(1-\alpha)\,\pi_{\mathrm{DQN}}^{(m)}(a\mid s),\quad 0\leq\alpha\leq 1.

From (
27
) and (
29
), each sub-policy approaches a (near-)optimal form:

\lim_{m\to\infty}\pi_{\mathrm{DQN}}^{(m)}\;=\;\pi_{\mathrm{DQN}}^{*},\qquad\lim_{m\to\infty}\pi_{\mathrm{PPO}}^{(m)}\;=\;\pi_{\mathrm{PPO}}^{*},

(31)

with probability
1-(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})
. The mixture

\pi_{\theta^{(m)}}
thus converges to

\alpha\,\pi_{\mathrm{PPO}}^{*}(a\mid s)\;+\;(1-\alpha)\,\pi_{\mathrm{DQN}}^{*}(a\mid s).

(32)

If either
\pi_{\mathrm{DQN}}^{*}
or
\pi_{\mathrm{PPO}}^{*}
is sufficiently close to
\pi^{*}
(the globally optimal policy), the mixture cannot deviate significantly from
\pi^{*}
. Formally, let

\Delta_{\mathrm{PPO}}^{(m)}
and

\Delta_{\mathrm{DQN}}^{(m)}

denote the gaps between
\pi_{\mathrm{PPO}}^{(m)},\pi_{\mathrm{DQN}}^{(m)}
and their optimal counterparts. Then, one can show (via standard performance-difference lemmas) that

V^{\pi^{*}}(s)\;-\;V^{\pi_{\theta^{(m)}}}(s)\;\leq\;C\,\bigl(\Delta_{\mathrm{PPO}}^{(m)}+\Delta_{\mathrm{DQN}}^{(m)}\bigr),

(33)

for some constant
C>0
depending on
\alpha
and the MDP’s discount factor
\gamma
. Since each
\Delta_{\mathrm{PPO}}^{(m)}
and
\Delta_{\mathrm{DQN}}^{(m)}
tends to zero with probability
1-\delta
(where
\delta=\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}
), we obtain

\lim_{m\to\infty}\Bigl[V^{\pi^{*}}(s)-V^{\pi_{\theta^{(m)}}}(s)\Bigr]\;=\;0.

(34)

Hence, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
M
such that for all
m>M
,

V^{\pi^{*}}(s)\;-\;V^{\pi_{\theta^{(m)}}}(s)\;\leq\;\epsilon,

(35)

which is precisely the statement of
\epsilon
-optimal convergence in (
26
).

By combining an off-policy DQN sub-policy with an on-policy PPO sub-policy and employing stable training procedures, the ensemble policy
\pi_{\theta^{(m)}}
converges to
\epsilon
-optimality in value. This completes the proof of Theorem
2
.
∎

Theorem 3

(Noise-Resilient Performance Guarantee)
.

Let the quantum system be

\delta_{\max}
-approximately controllable
(cf. Lemma
2
),
and that the ensemble RL framework
converges to an
\epsilon
-optimal policy in terms of
security, emissions, and quantum fidelity from Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
. Then the final spin state

\ket{\psi(T)}

achieves a fidelity

F\!\bigl(\ket{\psi(T)},\,\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{target}}}\bigr)\;\geq\;1-\epsilon^{\prime},

(36)

for some small
\epsilon^{\prime}
, while meeting security constraints and
limiting carbon penalties.

Proof.

By Lemma
2
, for sufficiently small

\delta_{\max}\geq 0
, the noisy Hamiltonian

\widetilde{H}(t)\;=\;H(t)\;+\;\Delta(t),\quad\|\Delta(t)\|\;\leq\;\delta_{\max},

(37)

remains close enough to the ideal Hamiltonian
H(t)
so that there exists
a piecewise-constant protocol (or control sequence)

\{u_{k}(t)\}
driving an initial state
\ket{\psi(0)}
to within

\epsilon_{c}
of a desired target state
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{target}}}
,
i.e.
,

\bigl\|\ket{\psi(T)}-\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{target}}}\bigr\|\;\leq\;\epsilon_{c},

(38)

with
\epsilon_{c}\to 0
as
\delta_{\max}\to 0
.

Next, Theorems
1
and
2

ensure that the learned ensemble policy
\pi_{\theta^{*}}
is

\epsilon
-optimal with respect to a multi-objective reward,
which includes fidelity, security, and green (emission) components.
In particular, let

\displaystyle R\bigl(\psi,\,\mathrm{security},\,\mathrm{emissions}\bigr)\;=

\displaystyle w_{\text{fid}}\,\mathcal{F}\bigl(\ket{\psi},\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{target}}}\bigr)

(39)

\displaystyle+\;w_{\text{sec}}\!\bigl(\text{security}\bigr)

\displaystyle-\;w_{\text{carbon}}\!\bigl(\text{emissions}\bigr).

where
\mathcal{F}
is a fidelity measure, and
w_{\text{fid}},w_{\text{sec}},w_{\text{carbon}}\geq 0
are weights reflecting design priorities. Being
\epsilon
-optimal means that

\pi_{\theta^{*}}
achieves near-maximal reward, which implies it selects
control strategies that maximize fidelity subject to security and
carbon-penalty constraints.

Since the reward includes a term proportional to

\mathcal{F}\bigl(\ket{\psi(T)},\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{target}}}\bigr)
,
the near-optimal policy naturally drives
\ket{\psi(T)}
close to

\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{target}}}
. More formally, combining
(
38
) with the agent’s
\epsilon
-optimal selection
of controls yields:

\bigl\|\ket{\psi(T)}-\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{target}}}\bigr\|\;\leq\;\epsilon_{c}+\epsilon_{\text{RL}},

(40)

where
\epsilon_{\text{RL}}
accounts for the residual suboptimality
due to the RL policy. Hence, the overall deviation from
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{target}}}

is bounded by a small quantity that depends on both
\delta_{\max}

and
\epsilon
.

Recall the fidelity

\displaystyle F(\ket{\phi},\ket{\varphi})=\bigl|\braket{\phi}{\varphi}\bigr|^{2}
.
Using the standard norm-fidelity relationship, one obtains for pure states:

1-F\!\bigl(\ket{\psi(T)},\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{target}}}\bigr)\;\leq\;\bigl\|\ket{\psi(T)}-\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{target}}}\bigr\|^{2}.

(41)

Combining (
41
) with
(
40
) gives

F\!\bigl(\ket{\psi(T)},\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{target}}}\bigr)\;\geq\;1\;-\;\Bigl(\epsilon_{c}+\epsilon_{\text{RL}}\Bigr)^{2}.

(42)

Define
\epsilon^{\prime}\equiv(\epsilon_{c}+\epsilon_{\text{RL}})^{2}
.
Since
\epsilon_{c}\to 0
as
\delta_{\max}\to 0
and

\epsilon_{\text{RL}}\to 0
as the agent’s suboptimality vanishes,
we conclude

F\!\bigl(\ket{\psi(T)},\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{target}}}\bigr)\;\geq\;1-\epsilon^{\prime}.

(43)

Thus, the system achieves high fidelity
\geq 1-\epsilon^{\prime}
, while
the reward structure enforces the desired security constraints
and keeps carbon emission penalties in check.

Under the dual assumptions of
\delta_{\max}
-approximate controllability
and an
\epsilon
-optimal RL policy balancing fidelity, security, and
emissions, the final state remains within fidelity

1-\epsilon^{\prime}
of
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{target}}}
. This completes
the proof of Theorem
3
.
∎

III-C

Proposed Learning Framework

Here, we propose integrating a quantum spin-chain model with classical supply chain metrics and then applying either a DQN or a DQN+PPO ensemble. Our objective is jointly optimizing fidelity, security, and eco-friendly performance under noise, converging to near-optimal solutions as assured by relevant controllability and RL theorems.

III-C
1
Quantum-Inspired Environment for Secure and Green SCS

We model a small
N
-spin chain (e.g.,
N=3
) under an XY Hamiltonian (Lemma
1
) while tracking inventories, a security score, CO
2
emissions, and a quantum metric. The inventories approximate a high-level supply measure, the security score represents protective measures or disruptions, and the CO
2
emissions reflect the energy cost associated with stronger quantum controls. The quantum metric gauges the closeness of the spin-chain state to the target
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{target}}}
. Following Lemma
2
, small bounded noise is introduced into
B_{\mathrm{fields}}
each step, ensuring approximate reachability despite drift. At each step, the environment simulates demand-driven inventory usage, updates the security score if the field is toggled
ON
, and evolves the state
\ket{\psi}
under the noisy Hamiltonian.

III-C
2
Multi-Objective Reward and RL Formulation

To incorporate fidelity, security, and environmental goals, we define the multi-objective reward:

\displaystyle r_{t}\;=

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}\,F\bigl(\ket{\psi(t)},\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{target}}}\bigr)

(44)

\displaystyle+\;\alpha_{2}\,R_{\mathrm{sec}}(\text{security\_score})\;-\;\alpha_{3}\,C_{\mathrm{co2}}(\text{co2\_emission}).

where
F(\cdot,\cdot)
measures the fidelity (squared overlap) from Lemma
2
,
R_{\mathrm{sec}}(\cdot)
rewards higher security, and
C_{\mathrm{co2}}(\cdot)
penalizes emissions. By Theorem
1
, once the quantum chain and supply elements form a valid MDP, standard RL convergence guarantees apply. To render the reward components commensurate, we normalize fidelity
F_{t}
, security score
R^{\mathrm{sec}}_{t}
, and emissions
C^{\mathrm{co2}}_{t}
to
[0,1]
over a sliding window
W
,

\widehat{F}_{t}=\frac{F_{t}-\min_{W}(F)}{\max_{W}(F)-\min_{W}(F)}
,

\widehat{R}^{\mathrm{sec}}_{t}=\frac{R^{\mathrm{sec}}_{t}-\min_{W}(R^{\mathrm{sec}})}{\max_{W}(R^{\mathrm{sec}})-\min_{W}(R^{\mathrm{sec}})}
,

\widehat{C}^{\mathrm{co2}}_{t}=\frac{C^{\mathrm{co2}}_{t}-\min_{W}(C^{\mathrm{co2}})}{\max_{W}(C^{\mathrm{co2}})-\min_{W}(C^{\mathrm{co2}})}
,
and use

r_{t}=\alpha_{1}\,\widehat{F}_{t}+\alpha_{2}\,\widehat{R}^{\mathrm{sec}}_{t}-\alpha_{3}\,\widehat{C}^{\mathrm{co2}}_{t}.

(45)

We adopt
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})=(1.0,1.0,0.5)
, which is best as shown in the Table
III
. Sensitivity impact and tradoff are discussed in the following section
IV-B
.

III-C
3
Proposed Agents and Ensemble Logic

Our first agent,
QuantumEnhancedAIoTAgent
, uses a DQN-based approach with Double DQN updates, a replay buffer for decorrelated sampling, and an epsilon-greedy strategy. According to Theorem
2
, if the environment is Markovian, this DQN agent converges to an
\epsilon
-optimal policy that balances quantum and supply chain objectives.

To improve stability under noise and handle more complex action spaces, we introduce a second agent, PPO, and combine it with DQN in an
EnsembleRLAgent
. By Theorem
3
, if each sub-policy can reach near-optimal performance, then a weighted ensemble of these sub-policies also converges to an
\epsilon
-optimal solution under the multi-objective reward. Specifically,

\displaystyle\pi_{\text{ensemble}}(a\mid s)\;=

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathrm{dqn}}\,\pi_{\mathrm{dqn}}(a\mid s)

(46)

\displaystyle+\;\bigl(1-\omega_{\mathrm{dqn}}\bigr)\,\pi_{\mathrm{ppo}}(a\mid s).

where
\omega_{\mathrm{dqn}}
depends on each agent’s cumulative rewards. This mixture leverages PPO’s exploration and DQN’s Q-value exploitation, aligning with Theorem
1
.

Finally, Lemma
1
and Lemma
2
confirm the controllability of the noisy spin chain, and Theorems
1

3
ensure that an ensemble combining DQN and PPO converges to near-optimal performance. Our approach addresses multiple supply chain objectives by mixing discrete and policy-based methods, and we provide detailed experimental results in the next section comparing single-agent RL, ensemble RL, and other controllers (e.g., GRAPE, MPC) in terms of learning, stability, and outcomes.

V
Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Work

In conclusion, we presented a quantum-inspired reinforcement learning framework that unifies inventory management, carbon-emissions penalization, and security objectives within a single decision model. Leveraging a controllable spin-chain analogy coupled to AIoT signals, the method operationalizes a multi-objective reward and learns robust policies via value-based and ensemble policy updates. Across learning-rate sweeps and ablations, the proposed controller achieved stable convergence and superior peak/late-episode returns relative to strong RL and model-based baselines, with an empirically favorable operating point.

Despite these strengths, several limitations warrant attention. Certain quantum-inspired components rely on idealized assumptions, and our noise analysis is simulation-based; we have not yet implemented or measured these processes on physical AIoT or quantum-analog hardware. In practice, we plan to (i) perform hardware-in-the-loop experiments with realistic sensor/actuator paths, (ii) calibrate channel parameters (bit-/phase-flip, depolarizing, erasure) to device-level error statistics capturing dropout, jitter, and bursty congestion, and (iii) extend to correlated and non-Markovian noise. Additionally, the multi-objective reward can grow complex at scale for interdependent networks. Future work will investigate more rigorous quantum formulations, distributed or hierarchical RL, and closed-loop feedback from decentralized IoT devices. These steps will strengthen the mapping from abstract channels to measured disturbances and further enable secure, sustainable, and efficient next-generation supply chains.
