Cardio‐Oncologic Considerations in Heart Failure With Mildly Reduced Ejection Fraction: Prevalence and Prognostic Impact of Malignancies
Authors: Tobias Schupp, Michelle Goertz, Jonas Dudda, Mohammad Abumayyaleh, Kathrin Weidner, Alexander Schmitt, Felix Lau, Marielen Reinhardt, Noah Abel, Jonas Rusnak, Johannes Lübke, Daniel Duerschmied, Ibrahim Akin, Michael Behnes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with malignancies were commonly excluded from prior heart failure (HF) trials resulting in limited data regarding the outcomes of patients with HF accompanied by concomitant malignancies. The study sought to assess the prevalence and prognostic impact of malignancies in patients with HF and HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction.MethodsConsecutive patients hospitalized with HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction were retrospectively included at 1 institution from 2016 to 2022. The prognosis of patients with malignancies was investigated regarding the primary end point all‐cause mortality at 30 months (median follow‐up). The key secondary end point was rehospitalization for worsening HF.ResultsAmong 2184 patients with HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction, 15.3% (n=335) suffered from concomitant malignancies. In patients with active malignancies (n=257), most patients had solid (n=175) followed by hematological malignancies (n=73) (both: n=9), whereas cancer‐therapy HF was observed in 3.9%. Patients with malignancies had lower rates of ischemic cardiomyopathy and were less commonly treated with beta blockers, renin‐angiotensin aldosterone antagonists, and statins. The presence of malignancies was associated with a higher risk of long‐term all‐cause mortality (59.4% versus 26.2%; P=0.001; adjusted hazard ratio, 2.568 [95% CI, 2.101–3.138]; P=0.001), which was already observed during index hospitalization (8.4% versus 2.5%; P=0.001) and mainly attributed to noncardiac mortality. In contrast, the risk of HF‐related rehospitalization did not significantly differ in both groups.ConclusionsMalignancies were present in 15% of patients with HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction and independently associated with long‐term all‐cause mortality, mainly attributed to an increased risk of in‐hospital noncardiac mortality. The risk of HF‐related rehospitalization was not affected by the presence of concomitant malignancies.Keywords: cancer, comorbidities, heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction, HFmrEF, malignanciesSubject Categories: Cardio-Oncology, Cardiomyopathy, Heart Failure

What Are the Clinical Implications?

The association between malignancies and increased long‐term mortality underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary, guideline‐adherent approach at the intersection of oncology and heart failure care, particularly given the lower rates of pharmacological and interventional heart failure treatment.

The association between malignancies and increased long‐term mortality underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary, guideline‐adherent approach at the intersection of oncology and heart failure care, particularly given the lower rates of pharmacological and interventional heart failure treatment.

Related to ongoing improvements and modern treatment strategies for patients with heart failure (HF), the prognosis of patients with HF has significantly improved and many patients with HF nowadays die from noncardiac causes. 1 , 2 As a consequence, the burden of noncardiovascular comorbidities increases. 3 , 4 Recently, patients with HF and preserved (HFpEF) or mildly reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF, HFmrEF) were demonstrated to be older, accompanied by higher rates of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, valvular heart diseases and arterial hypertension as compared with patients with HF and reduced LVEF (HFrEF). 5 The presence comorbidities—specifically noncardiac comorbidities—was demonstrated to be associated with longer duration of hospitalization and increased in‐hospital mortality rates in patients with HF. 6 Whereas most studies included patients with HFrEF or HFpEF, studies investigating the prognostic impact of patients with HFmrEF remain scarce, contributing to overall limited guideline recommendations for patients with HFmrEF. 7 Therefore, further research is necessary to investigate predictors of prognosis in patients with HFmrEF, which by now represents a distinct phenotype of HF affecting up to 25% of patients with HF. 8 This is of utmost importance, because the risk of all‐cause mortality was only slightly lower in patients with HFmrEF as compared with HFrEF within a registry study of 9134 patients with HF. 9

Many patients with HF suffer from concomitant malignancies, whereas both diseases share similar risk factors and may contribute to the occurrence and progression of each other. 10 Whereas the presence of HF may limit cancer‐related treatment strategies, recent studies suggested HF may further lead to the release of protumorigenic factors and directly contribute to tumor growth. 11 , 12 Prior studies suggested a higher incidence of malignancies in patients with HF 13 , 14 ; however, data regarding the prognostic impact of concomitant malignancies in patients with HF are limited and characterized by heterogenous findings. 15 Given the overall high prevalence of comorbidities in patients with HFmrEF, a largely underrepresented subtype of HF, the authors hypothesize that the presence of malignancies may contribute to a substantial burden of mortality.

Therefore, the present study sought to investigate the prevalence and prognostic impact of concomitant malignancies in patients hospitalized with HFmrEF using a large retrospective registry.

METHODS

Study Patients, Design, and Data Collection

For the present study, all consecutive patients hospitalized with HFmrEF at 1 institution were included from January 2016 to December 2022. 16 Using the electronic hospital information system, all relevant clinical data related to the index event were documented, such as baseline characteristics, vital signs on admission, prior medical history, prior medical treatment, length of index hospital and intensive care unit stay, laboratory values, data derived from all noninvasive or invasive cardiac diagnostics and device therapies, such as echocardiographic data, coronary angiography and data being derived from prior or newly implanted cardiac devices. The present study derived from the HARMER (Heart Failure With Mildly Reduced Ejection Fraction Registry), representing a retrospective single‐center registry including consecutive patients with HFmrEF hospitalized at the University Medical Center Mannheim, Germany (clinicaltrials.gov identifier: NCT05603390). The registry was carried out according to the principles of the declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the medical ethics committee II of the Medical Faculty Mannheim, University of Heidelberg, Germany (ethical approval code: 2022‐818). Related to the retrospective and observational study design, no written informed consent was required. The data sets used or analyzed during the current study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

All consecutive patients ≥18 years of age hospitalized with HFmrEF at 1 institution were included. The diagnosis of HFmrEF was determined according to the 2021 ESC guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of acute and chronic HF. 7 All patients with an LVEF of 41% to 49% during index echocardiography with symptoms or signs of HF were included. For the present study, all echocardiographic examinations and reports were reassessed post hoc by 2 independent cardiologists, whereas important echocardiographic measurements were re‐assessed. In patients with acute decompensated HF (ADHF) or concomitant acute myocardial infarction (AMI) or patients undergoing active cancer therapy, routine echocardiography was performed following hemodynamic stabilization before index hospital discharge. No further exclusion criteria were applied for the present study.

Risk Stratification

For the present study, risk stratification was performed according to the presence or absence of concomitant malignancies. The presence or absence of malignancies was identified by reviewing all patients' reports using the electronic hospital information system. Patients with skin cancers other than malignant melanoma or patients with benign tumors were not included in the malignancy group. Furthermore, the prognosis of patients with active malignancies (ie, new‐diagnosed malignancies, or requiring cancer‐related treatment or progressive or metastatic disease) was compared with patients with prior malignancies (ie, patients who have completed cancer‐related completed treatment with no evidence of recurrent or ongoing malignancies). Thereafter, the prognosis of patients with solid malignancies was compared with patients with hematological malignancies including patients with active malignancies only, as well as the prognosis of different solid malignancies was investigated stratified by the primary affected organ system. Finally, the prognostic impact of the duration of malignancies was investigated with regard to the primary end point.

Study End Points

The primary end point was all‐cause mortality during median follow‐up of 30 months. Secondary end points comprised in‐hospital all‐cause mortality (stratified by cardiac and noncardiac), all‐cause mortality at 12 months, rehospitalization for worsening HF, cardiac rehospitalization, AMI, stroke, coronary revascularization, and major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events at 30 months. HF‐related hospitalization was defined as a rehospitalization due to worsening HF requiring intravenous diuretic therapy. HF‐related rehospitalization comprised patients with hospitalization due to worsening HF as the primary cause or as a result of another cause but associated with worsening HF at the time of admission or as a result of another cause but complicated by worsening HF during its cause. Cardiac rehospitalization was defined as rehospitalization due to a primary cardiac condition, including worsening HF, AMI, coronary revascularization, and symptomatic atrial or ventricular arrhythmias. Major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events was defined as composite of all‐cause mortality, coronary revascularization, nonfatal AMI, and nonfatal stroke.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative data were presented as median and interquartile range. They were compared using the Student's t test for normally distributed data or the Mann–Whitney U test for nonparametric data. Deviations from a Gaussian distribution were tested by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Qualitative data were presented as absolute and relative frequencies and were compared using the chi‐square test or the Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Kaplan–Meier analyses were performed stratified by the presence of active or prior malignancies as compared with patients without malignancies and univariable hazard ratios (HR) were given together with 95% CIs to demonstrate raw group differences that can also reflect real‐world clinical practice. Further Kaplan–Meier analyses were performed comparing the prognosis of patients with solid and hematological malignancies, as well as stratified by the most commonly affected organ systems in patients with solid malignancies, including only patients with active malignancies. Multivariable Cox regression models were performed to investigate an independent association of malignancies, as well as the impact of active versus prior malignancies. Multivariable Cox regression analyses were additionally adjusted for important baseline characteristics (age, sex, body mass index) and comorbidities (prior congestive HF, diabetes, arterial hypertension, chronic kidney disease, AMI, ischemic cardiomyopathy, ADHF, New York Heart Association functional class, LVEF, right ventricular dysfunction, moderate/severe aortic stenosis, mitral regurgitation, anemia, HF pharmacotherapies, coronary angiography), that have yet been demonstrated to affect the prognosis of patients with HF. Right ventricular dysfunction was defined as a tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion <17 mm. 17 Anemia was defined as hemoglobin <12 g/dL in women and <13 g/dL in men. 18

Results of all statistical tests were considered significant for P≤0.05. SPSS (Version 28, IBM, Armonk, NY) was used for statistics.

RESULTS

Study Population

From a total of 2184 patients with HFmrEF included in the HARMER registry, 15% (n=335) had concomitant malignancies (prior: n=78; active: n=257). Among patients with active malignancies, most patients had solid malignancies (n=175) as compared with hematological malignancies (n=73) (both: n=9). The most common types of active solid malignancies comprised urogenital (n=52), followed by gastrointestinal (n=35) and pulmonary (n=35) malignancies, whereas the most common hematological malignancies were lymphoid (n=36) and myeloid (n=28) neoplasms (Figure 1). The distribution of age (median 76 versus 75 years; P=0.178), male sex (66.0% versus 64.3%; P=0.559), as well as the proportion of patients with prior congestive HF (34.0% versus 33.9%; P=0.966), ADHF <12 months (10.7% versus 10.9%; P=0.923), and chronic kidney disease (34.6% versus 30.4%; P=0.128) did not differ among patients with and without malignancies (Table 1). However, patients without malignancies had higher rates of arterial hypertension (79.2% versus 71.0%; P=0.001), hyperlipidemia (31.9% versus 21.8%; P=0.001), and current smoking (19.3% versus 14.6%; P=0.043) compared with patients with malignancies. With regard to comorbidities during index hospitalization, patients without malignancies presented with higher rates of concomitant AMI (21.6% versus 14.9%; P=0.005) and stroke (14.9% versus 6.9%; P=0.001), whereas the proportion of patients with ADHF (22.4% versus 20.9%; P=0.544) and atrial fibrillation (42.4% versus 39.4%; P=0.306) did not significantly differ in both groups.

The median time interval form cancer diagnosis until index hospitalization was 0.9 years (interquartile range [IQR], 0.1–3.4 years) (Table 2). Overall, 16.1% of patients underwent current chemotherapy, whereas anthracyclines were applied in 9.6%, HER2 (human epidermal growth factor receptor 2)‐directed therapies in 1.2% and current immunotherapy was applied in 3.6% of patients with malignancies. The burden of HF‐related symptoms, reflected by New York Heart Association functional class (P=0.365), as well as most echocardiographic characteristics, including the rates of valvular heart diseases did not significantly differ in both groups, except for a slightly higher interventricular septal end diastole in patients without malignancies (median 12 versus 11 mm; P=0.002). A higher proportion of patients without malignancies underwent invasive coronary angiography (43.9% versus 25.1%; P=0.001), whereas the presence and extent of coronary artery disease (3‐vessel: 40.6% versus 42.9%; P=0.962), as well as the rates of percutaneous coronary intervention (54.0% versus 47.6%; P=0.261) did not differ between the 2 groups. With regard to laboratory values, patients with malignancies had lower estimated glomerular filtration rate (median 60 versus 67 mL/min; P=0.004), hemoglobin (median 10.6 versus 12.6 g/dL; P=0.001) and higher C‐reactive protein (median 28 versus 12 mg/L; P=0.001) and NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide) levels (median 3971 versus 2587 pg/mL; P=0.006). Finally, beta blockers (78.7% versus 70.7%; P=0.002), angiotensin‐converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB) (64.8% versus 75.2%; P=0.001), and statins (70.9% versus 53.7%; P=0.001) were more commonly prescribed for patients without malignancies, whereas the prescription rates of aldosterone antagonists (P=0.583) and SGLT2 (sodium glucose linked transporter 2) inhibitors (SGLT2i) (P=0.807) did not significantly differ between the 2 groups.

Prognostic Impact of Malignancies in HFmrEF

During a median follow‐up period of 30 months (interquartile range, 12.1–55.1 months), 59.4% of patients with malignancies and 26.2% of patients without died (Table 3). An increased risk of all‐cause mortality was already observed during index hospitalization (8.4% versus 2.5%; P=0.001) and mainly attributed to higher rates of noncardiac death (6.9% versus 1.5%; odds ratio [OR], 4.794 [95% CI, 2.726–8.431]; P=0.001). Compared with patients without malignancies, the risk of the primary end point all‐cause mortality at 30 months was higher in patients with active (HR, 3.831 [95% CI, 3.212–4.569]; P=0.001) and prior malignancies (HR, 1.586 [95% CI, 1.090–2.307]; P=0.016) (Figure 2; left panel). In contrast, the risk of rehospitalization for HF did not significantly differ in patients with active (HR, 0.902 [95% CI, 0.610–1.333]; P=0.604) and prior (HR, 0.694 [95% CI, 0.327–1.471]; P=0.341) as compared with patients without malignancies (Figure 2; right panel). With regard to further secondary end points, patients with malignancies were less commonly rehospitalized for cardiac causes (HR, 0.664 [95% CI, 0.493–0.895]; P=0.007), whereas the risks of AMI, stroke, and coronary revascularization did not significantly differ between the 2 groups. Furthermore, the risk of major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events at 30 months was higher in patients with malignancies (HR, 2.519 [95% CI, 2.154–2.945]; P=0.001).

Prognostic Impact of the Type of Malignancy in HFmrEF

A total of 248 patients with HFmrEF had either an active solid (n=175) or hematological (n=73) malignancy (both n=9). However, the risk of all‐cause mortality at 30 months did not significantly differ between patients with solid as compared with hematological malignancies (HR, 1.136 [95% CI, 0.823–1.568]; P=0.439) (Figure 3; left panel). In line, there was no significant difference with regard to the risk of HF‐related rehospitalization between the 2 groups (Figure 3; right panel). Furthermore, the prognosis of patients with different types of solid malignancies did not significantly differ in patients with HFmrEF (Figure 4). Finally, the duration of malignancies was not associated with the risk of all‐cause mortality in the present study (P=0.157).

Multivariable Cox Regression Analyses

Even after multivariable adjustments, the presence of malignancies was independently associated with a higher risk of long‐term all‐cause mortality at 30 months (HR, 2.568 [95% CI, 2.101–3.138]; P=0.001) (Table 4; right panel). In addition, higher age (HR, 1.035; P=0.001; per year increase), male sex (HR, 1.237; P=0.027), chronic kidney disease (HR, 1.318; P=0.006), ADHF (HR, 1.592; P=0.001), and the presence of anemia (HR, 1.861; P=0.001) were identified as predictors for a higher risk of long‐term all‐cause mortality, whereas a lower body mass index (HR, 0.960; P=0.001; per 1 kg/m2 increase), as well as treatment with beta blockers (HR, 0.740; P=0.005), ACE inhibitors/ARB (HR, 0.752; P=0.004), coronary angiography (HR, 0.568; P=0.001) were associated with improved survival. Finally, only active (HR, 3.160 [95% CI, 2.551–3.913]; P=0.001) but not prior malignancies (HR, 1.178 [95% CI, 0.739–1.889]; P=0.490) were associated with a higher risk of long‐term all‐cause mortality after multivariable adjustments.

DISCUSSION

The present study sought to investigate the prevalence and prognostic impact of malignancies in patients hospitalized with HFmrEF using a large retrospective data set. From a total of 2184 patients hospitalized with HFmrEF, 15% suffered from concomitant malignancies (prior: 3.6%; active: 11.8%). Patients with HFmrEF and malignancies had lower rates of ischemic cardiomyopathy, less often underwent coronary angiography, and were less commonly treated with beta blockers, ACE inhibitors/ARB, and statins. Overall, 16.1% of patients underwent current chemotherapy, whereas anthracyclines were applied in 9.6%, HER2‐directed therapies in 1.2%, contributing to an overall prevalence of CTHF of only 3.9%. After multivariable adjustments, the presence of malignancies was independently associated with a 2.6‐fold increased risk of all‐cause mortality at 30 months and even a 3.2‐fold increased risk in patients with active malignancies, which was mainly attributed to an increased risk of noncardiac mortality, already during index hospitalization. In contrast, the risk of HF‐related rehospitalization did not significantly differ between patients with or without malignancies. Finally, the prognosis of patients with hematological or solid malignancies did not significantly differ. The study underscores the important prognostic impact of malignancies even in HFmrEF; however, they highlight that this effect may presumably be driven by noncardiac end points in line with an overall low rate of CTHF.

As patients with malignancies were commonly excluded from randomized controlled trials, 19 the proportion of patients with a history of malignancies in HFrEF (4.3%) and HFpEF (8.5%) were generally low in patients included in previous HF trials. 20 The present study found malignancies were present in 15% of patients with HFmrEF, which is in line with registry data, reporting a prevalence of malignancies of approximately 17% among patients with HF. 21 , 22 Whereas most studies investigated to prognostic impact of malignancies with regard to the occurrence of HF, 23 , 24 data regarding the outcomes of patients with HF accompanied by malignancies are limited. Just recently, a substudy of the Swedish HF registry investigated the prognostic impact of a history of malignancies (diagnosed at least 2 years before) in >53 000 patients with HF (HFmrEF in 25%). The authors suggested higher mortality rates in patients with a history of malignancies during a median follow‐up period of 2.4 years, whereas a higher risk of cardiovascular mortality and rehospitalization for HF was specifically found in patients with HFmrEF but not in patients with HFpEF or HFrEF. 21 In line with this, the present study suggested the presence of active malignancies represents the strongest predictor of long‐term all‐cause mortality, which was already observed during index hospitalization, although most baseline characteristics including age, sex, as well as the prevalence of prior HF, coronary artery disease, chronic kidney failure, atrial fibrillation, as well as ADHF <12 months before the index event and during index hospitalization did not differ significantly between patients with or without concomitant malignancies. Of note, malignancies were specifically associated with the risk of noncardiac mortality, already during index hospitalization, whereas the rates of cardiac mortality did not differ significantly during index hospitalization. In line with this, the risk of HF‐related rehospitalization was similar among the 2 groups at 30 months, suggesting the increased risk of long‐term all‐cause mortality of patients with malignancies may not predominantly be driven by cardiac events. Although the levels of NT‐proBNP were higher among patients with malignancies, this may relate to poorer renal function, higher levels of inflammation and overall higher burden of multimorbidity, 25 which was yet demonstrated to be associated with higher NT‐proBNP levels. This may relate to the rather small proportion of patients within the present study suffering from CTHF (ie, 3.9%), in line with rather low rates of current cardiotoxic chemotherapies (ie, anthracyclines: 9.6%, HER2‐directed therapies: 1.2%). From this perspective, the characteristics of patients with HF with and without CTHF were demonstrated to differ significantly with lower cardiovascular risk profile and higher LVEF values among patients with CTHF, whereas the rates of diastolic dysfunction were higher. 26 , 27 However, the prevalence and extent of CTHF may vary significantly depending on the type of cardiotoxic therapy and specifically treatment with anthracyclines, anti‐HER2 targeted therapy and cyclophosphamide may lead to acute decompensated HF, typically with reduced LVEF, whereas checkpoint inhibitor treatment may contribute to the occurrence of myocarditis. 28 From this perspective, the rather low rates of cardiotoxic treatments in patients with HFmrEF may be in line with comparable rates of patients with prior reduced LVEF and an overall low proportion of patients with an acute decrease of LVEF related to cancer‐related therapies.

Although the rates of nonischemic cardiomyopathies were increased in patients with malignancies, CTHF was detected in only 3.9% of patients with malignancies. Whereas this may relate to rather low rates of cardiotoxic therapies, this may also result from an insufficient diagnostic evaluation of HF due to severe comorbid conditions. In line with this, the proportion of patients with unknown HF was significantly higher in patients with malignancies, which may result from challenges in interdisciplinary care, patient preferences, or a presumed lower therapeutic benefit in patients with malignancies. In line, patients with concomitant malignancies were less commonly treated with beta blockers, ACE inhibitors/ARB and statins. The lower prescription rates of HF pharmacotherapies may relate to an increased fear of side effects and interactions, competing treatment priorities, and clinical uncertainty in patients with malignancies and multimorbidity. 29 However, the underuse of HF pharmacotherapies may negatively influence both the outcomes in patients with HF but even cancer‐related outcomes. 30 Specifically beta blockers may decrease the incidence of malignancies and improve the outcomes in patients with malignancies, as the sympathetic nervous system may promote cancer progression by the activation of protein kinase A and exchange protein activated by adenylyl cyclase signaling. 31 Within a meta‐analysis including >76 000 patients with malignancies, the initiation of a beta blocker after the first diagnosis of cancer was associated with improved survival, 32 underlining the need for guideline‐recommended HF therapies, even in patients with malignancies. The rates of SGLT2i treatment were generally low within the present study, because patients were predominantly included before the upgrade of SGLT2i for patients with HFmrEF and HFpEF, 7 and which is in line with contemporary registries. 16 , 33 However, treatment with SGLT2i was yet associated with improved survival in patients with diabetes and concomitant cancer, as demonstrated within a meta‐analysis of 9 studies and >80 000 patients. 34 Further studies are necessary to investigate the effect of an optimal HF pharmacotherapy, specifically including SGLT2i in patients with HFmrEF with concomitant malignancies.

In line with this, the proportion of patients undergoing coronary angiography was much lower in patients with malignancies, although ischemic cardiomyopathy was the leading cause of HF in half of the patients with malignancies with similar extent of coronary artery disease and percutaneous coronary intervention rates in both groups, which may refer to a cautious decision‐making during routine clinical practice in multimorbid patients and in patients with generally impaired long‐term prognosis. The high prevalence of ischemic cardiomyopathy is in line with recent studies, suggesting HFmrEF may be closely linked to HFrEF. 8 , 35 Although data concerning the prognostic impact of coronary angiography in patients with HF and malignancies are scare, a recent study found even higher prevalence of arteriosclerosis, detected by coronary computed tomography angiography, in 312 patients with a history of cancer compared with 624 matched controls without. 36 This is of utmost importance, because ischemic cardiomyopathy was associated with improved prognosis compared with nonischemic causes within the present study, 37 which may be in line with improved revascularization technics and more effectively treatable underlying diseases in patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy. The extent to which patients with multimorbidities or malignancies benefit from innovative treatment options need to be investigated in further studies, particularly in the context of demographic change and the increasing number of patients with multimorbidities.

Taken together, the present study identified the presence of malignancies, especially active malignancies as an independent risk factor for all‐cause mortality and specifically noncardiac mortality in patients with HFmrEF. Although the rates of nonischemic cardiomyopathies were higher in patients with malignancies, the proportion of patients with CTHF was overall low, in line with prior studies suggesting cardiotoxic chemotherapies may mainly lead to HFrEF. 28 In light of the undertreatment of patients with malignancies and HFmrEF with regard to the rates of coronary angiography and HF pharmacotherapies, which may further relate to the recent upgrade of HFmrEF as distinct HF entity, further studies are needed to comprehensively investigate the prognostic impact of malignancies and cancer‐related treatment strategies across the spectrum of LVEF.

Study Limitations

The study has several limitations. Due to the retrospective and single‐center study design, results may be influenced by measured an unmeasured confounding. Because the study primarily focused on the impact of cancer in HFmrEF, the relationship between cancer and HFpEF or HFrEF cannot be inferred and warrants further investigation in future studies. Related to the overall low proportion of patients with CTHF, no further subanalyses regarding treatment with cardiotoxic chemotherapies, immunotherapies, or radiation were performed. Further risk stratification based on the stage of malignancies were not possible related to the various cancer entities with rather low sample numbers. Given the retrospective and observational study design, no timeline of LVEF changes related to cancer‐related treatment was available in the vast majority of patients. Related to the single‐center study design, HF‐related and cardiac rehospitalization were assessed at our institution only. Finally, causes of death beyond index hospitalization were not available for the present study.

CONCLUSIONS

In patients with HFmrEF, 15% of patients suffered from concomitant malignancies. Patients with malignancies less commonly underwent coronary angiography and had lower prescription rates of HF pharmacotherapies including beta blockers and ACE inhibitors/ARBs. The presence of malignancies was identified as independent predictor of long‐term all‐cause mortality—mainly attributed to an increased risk of noncardiac mortality during index hospitalization, whereas the risk of rehospitalization for worsening HF was not affected by the presence or absence of malignancies.
