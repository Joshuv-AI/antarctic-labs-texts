Efficient enzyme-mediated degradation of various emerging pollutants, including 2-mercaptobenzothiazole, paracetamol, and sulfamethoxazole, using HRP@UiO-66-NH2
Authors: Khadega A. Al-Maqdi, Shaikha Alneyadi, Nada Elmerhi, Lamia A. Siddig, Ahmed Alzamly, Iltaf Shah, Syed Salman Ashraf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The increasing contamination of water sources due to industrialization and population growth poses significant environmental and health risks, even at trace pollutant concentrations. Enzymatic bioremediation, particularly using horseradish peroxidase (HRP), has shown great potential in pollutant degradation due to its high selectivity and catalytic efficiency. However, limitations such as poor operational stability and reusability hinder its large-scale application. The degradation of structurally diverse emerging pollutants by UiO-66-NH2-immobilized HRP, including the influence of redox mediation, remains insufficiently investigated. This study addresses this gap by immobilizing HRP onto UiO-66-NH2, a metal–organic framework (MOF) with high stability and surface area. HRP@UiO-66-NH2 was characterized using SEM–EDX, PXRD, FTIR, TGA, and BET analyses, while pollutant degradation was quantified by LC–MS/MS. HRP loading concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 4 mg/mL were evaluated, and 1 mg/mL was selected for subsequent experiments. The immobilized HRP exhibited an optimum H2O2 concentration of 1.08 mM within the tested range of 0–2.43 mM. Both free and immobilized HRP showed maximum activity at pH 4, while the optimum temperature shifted from 30°C for free HRP to 40°C following immobilization. The immobilized enzyme also retained approximately 95% of its initial activity after four reuse cycles. HRP@UiO-66-NH2 was evaluated for the degradation of nine emerging pollutants, including 2-mercaptobenzothiazole, paracetamol, sulfamethoxazole, methylparaben, salicylic acid, lincomycin hydrochloride, atenolol, thiabendazole, and DEET. It degraded 99.7% of 2-mercaptobenzothiazole and 97.4% of paracetamol within 30 min. The addition of 1-hydroxybenzotriazole (HOBT) increased the degradation of sulfamethoxazole, methylparaben, and salicylic acid from 23.3%, 14.5%, and 12.4% to 53.1%, 57.6%, and 54.3%, respectively. The greater thermal and H2O2 tolerance of immobilized HRP suggests a protective effect of the UiO-66-NH2 framework, while the variable response to HOBT indicates pollutant-dependent redox mediation. These findings highlight HRP@UiO-66-NH2 as a promising biocatalyst for sustainable water remediation, warranting further studies to elucidate the underlying degradation mechanisms and optimize real-world applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11356-026-38269-8.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, rapid global population growth and economic expansion have led to various environmental challenges, including water pollution. One critical aspect of water pollution is the presence of high levels of emerging pollutants (EPs) in different water sources (Farré et al. 2008; Peña-Guzmán et al. 2019). Numerous studies have demonstrated the harmful effects of EPs on human health and aquatic ecosystems, even at extremely low concentrations (< µg/L). These pollutants have been detected in water bodies at concentrations ranging from nanograms to several hundred micrograms per liter. For instance, pharmaceuticals such as ibuprofen and metoprolol have been found in groundwater, with concentration ranges of 4.27–510 ng/L and 20–60 ng/L, respectively (Peña-Guzmán et al. 2019). The emerging pollutants examined in this study represent structurally diverse pharmaceuticals, pesticides, personal-care products, and industrial chemicals. These compounds include 2-mercaptobenzothiazole, paracetamol, sulfamethoxazole, methylparaben, salicylic acid, lincomycin hydrochloride, atenolol, thiabendazole, and DEET. Bioremediation, particularly through the use of oxidoreductase enzymes such as horseradish peroxidase (HRP), soybean peroxidase (SBP), and manganese peroxidase (MnP), has been widely explored for the removal of EPs. These enzymes have demonstrated remarkable efficiency due to their high selectivity and catalytic activity (Abushahab et al. 2024; Husain and Husain 2007). However, despite these advantages, enzyme-based remediation faces several limitations, including low operational stability, poor reusability, and challenges in enzyme recovery. To overcome these drawbacks, enzyme immobilization has been employed as a promising approach.

Immobilization enhances enzyme stability under harsh conditions such as extreme temperatures and pH levels, facilitates enzyme recovery, and enables reuse for multiple cycles, thereby reducing the overall cost of enzyme applications in industrial settings (Asgher et al. 2017; Bilal and Asgher 2015). Various immobilization techniques, including adsorption, covalent bonding, entrapment, and crosslinking, have been developed. Each technique offers distinct advantages and limitations. Adsorption, a physical immobilization method, relies on weak interactions such as van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonding, and ionic interactions between the enzyme and the support material. This approach preserves the enzyme’s natural structure, thereby maintaining its activity. Additionally, adsorption-based immobilization is simple, cost-effective, and widely utilized in industrial applications (Hernandez and Fernandez-Lafuente 2011; Rathi and Kumar 2021).

Recent advancements have led to the exploration of hybrid materials such as metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) as novel support materials for enzyme immobilization. MOFs are highly porous, crystalline materials composed of metal ions or clusters coordinated to organic linkers (Rasheed et al. 2020). Their high surface area, structural stability, and tunable porosity make them promising candidates for environmental applications, including EP removal (Ahmad et al. 2020a, b; Gao et al. 2019). Several studies have demonstrated the potential of MOFs as adsorbents for EPs (Abdullah et al. 2026; Khan et al. 2026). For example, the anti-inflammatory drug ketoprofen was effectively adsorbed onto MIL-101/GO, achieving a maximum adsorption capacity (qmax) of 140 mg/g (Sarker et al. 2018). Similarly, glyphosate, a widely used pesticide, exhibited strong adsorption onto UiO-67/GO, with a qmax of 482.69 mg/g (Yang et al. 2017). Beyond their role as adsorbents, MOFs have also gained attention for enzyme immobilization in water remediation applications. For instance, Gao et al. immobilized HRP and chloroperoxidase (CPO) onto H-MOF(Zr), resulting in a 58.2% increase in enzyme activity after incubation at 70 °C for one hour, compared to the free enzyme. Additionally, the hybrid material retained 70.7% of its enzymatic activity after twelve reaction cycles, demonstrating excellent reusability. These composites effectively degraded the pollutants isoproturon and 2,4-dichlorophenol (Gao et al. 2019). Similarly, microperoxidase-8 immobilized on MIL-101(Cr) exhibited 66% retained activity after five consecutive reaction cycles, demonstrating enhanced stability (Gkaniatsou et al. 2018). In another study, Li et al. immobilized HRP on SOM-ZIF-8, achieving 85% enzyme activity retention after five cycles. The HRP@SOM-ZIF-8 composite effectively degraded multiple dyes, including Congo red, methyl orange, rhodamine 6G, and rhodamine B (Li et al. 2020). More recently, laccase immobilized within hierarchically porous ZIF-8 achieved 80% removal of 2,4-dichlorophenol under optimized conditions (Xu et al. 2023). UiO-66-NH2 was selected because its strong Zr–O coordination provides high structural and aqueous stability, while its large accessible surface facilitates enzyme immobilization and subsequent catalyst recovery (Ahmad et al. 2020a, b; Kandiah et al. 2010). The pendant amino groups can promote hydrogen-bonding and electrostatic interactions at the enzyme–MOF interface, supporting enzyme adsorption and enhancing interfacial stability (Ahmad et al. 2020a, b). UiO-66-NH2-based materials have been extensively investigated for environmental adsorption and photocatalytic treatment (Jrad et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2021). Beyond these applications, UiO-66-NH2 has also demonstrated considerable potential as an enzyme immobilization platform. Ahmad et al. immobilized HRP and glucose oxidase (GOx) onto UiO-66-NH2, resulting in a significant increase in enzymatic activity (189 U/mg) compared to the free enzyme (100 U/mg) (Ahmad et al. 2020a, b). Similarly, Kurtuldu et al. utilized UiO-66-NH2 for HRP immobilization to degrade methyl orange (MO) and methylene blue (MB). The immobilized enzyme achieved 60% degradation of MO and 45% degradation of MB under optimal conditions (Kurtuldu et al. 2022). In 2025, HRP@Fe-NU-1003 completely removed 50 µg/mL chloramphenicol within 30 min and achieved 61% mineralization (Ran et al. 2025). Most recently, ZIF-8 nanomotors co-encapsulating HRP and catalase achieved approximately 98% removal of bisphenol A within 2 min, while tannic-acid-modified enzyme@ZIF-8 retained approximately 76% of its initial catalytic activity after 10 reaction cycles (Xu et al. 2026). Although previous studies have demonstrated the degradation of dyes using HRP immobilized on UiO-66-NH2, its performance against mixtures of structurally diverse emerging pollutants remains insufficiently investigated. Furthermore, limited information is available on how redox mediators influence the pollutant-specific performance of this immobilized enzyme system. Therefore, this study aimed to prepare and characterize HRP@UiO-66-NH2, optimize HRP loading, pH, temperature, H2O2 concentration and evaluate its operational reusability. The degradation performance of free and immobilized HRP was compared against a mixture of nine emerging pollutants in a model water system, both in the presence and absence of the redox mediator HOBT.

Materials and methods

Materials

All EPs were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, along with all LC–MS solvents, including LC–MS grade water, LC–MS grade acetonitrile, and hydrogen peroxide. Detailed information on the nine EPs, including supplier, location, catalog number, CAS number, and purity or grade, is provided in Supplementary Table S1. The HRP enzyme, with a specific activity of 130 U/mg, was provided by Sorachim (Switzerland). The buffer used in all experiments was a universal buffer containing 0.2 M potassium phosphate and 0.1 M citric acid.

Preparation of UiO-66-NH2

For the synthesis of UiO-66-NH2, 750 mg of ZrCl4 and 800 mg of 2-aminoterephthalic acid were dissolved in 90 mL of DMF. The mixture was sonicated for 30 min, then heated at 120 °C for 24 h. After cooling, the sample was filtered under vacuum and washed with DMF. It was then soaked in acetone three times, each for 3 h. Finally, the solid was recovered by filtration and dried at 150 °C under vacuum. The crystalline structure of UiO-66-NH2 was confirmed using PXRD (Neyadi et al. 2021).

Preparation of HRP@UiO-66-NH2 composites

The procedure for producing HRP@UiO-66-NH2 composites was previously described (Samui et al. 2020). In brief, 5 mg of UiO-66-NH2 was dispersed in 1 mL of various concentrations of HRP enzyme. The mixtures were subsequently shaken at 150 rpm for 4 h. Following this, the solid materials (HRP@UiO-66-NH2) were collected by centrifugation and washed three times with 0.5 mL of water to remove any unbound HRP. The final HRP@UiO-66-NH2 composites were collected and stored at 4 °C for future experiments.

Characterization

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX)

The surface morphology of the materials was analyzed using scanning electron microscopy. For this analysis, the samples were placed on a specimen holder pre-coated with carbon tape, followed by a thin layer of gold to enhance conductivity. SEM images were captured using a Quattro Environmental Scanning Electron Microscope (ESEM) at accelerating voltages of 5 and 10 kV. Additionally, the SEM system was equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) detector for elemental analysis.

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD)

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) analysis was performed using a Rigaku MiniFlex X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku, Tokyo, Japan). The instrument operated at a voltage of 45 kV and a current of 40 mA. The XRD scans were conducted over a 2θ range of 5° to 50° to analyze the crystallographic structure of the samples.

Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy

Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were collected using a Thermo Nicolet Nexus 470 FT-IR spectrophotometer with the potassium bromide (KBr) pellet procedure. The spectral range extended from 500 to 4000 cm−1, with a resolution of 2 cm−1 and a 512 scans rate. Air was used as the background for all samples, and its spectrum was automatically collected and subtracted from all samples' spectra.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed using a Shimadzu TGA-50 analyzer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The sample was placed in an aluminum pan and analyzed under a nitrogen atmosphere with a flow rate of 10 mL/min. The temperature increased from 25 °C to 600 °C at a heating rate of 10°C/min, allowing for the assessment of thermal stability and decomposition behavior.

Brunauer–Emmett–Teller surface area analysis (BET)

Surface area and pore size measurements were performed using a Quantachrome Autosorb-1 gas sorption analyzer. Approximately 200 mg of each sample was used for analysis. The specific surface area and pore size distribution were determined through nitrogen (N2) physisorption using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method.

Determination of enzyme activity

To measure enzyme activity, 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) was used. For the reaction mixtures, 10 μL of free or immobilized enzyme was added to 190 μL of master mix, which contained both the universal buffer and ABTS. The enzyme activity was measured using a microplate reader, with the reaction carried out at room temperature. The relative activity was calculated as a percentage of the initial activity. In the pH experiments, enzyme activity was tested across a range from pH 2 to pH 9 for both free and immobilized HRP. In the temperature experiments, enzyme activity was examined from 30 °C to 70 °C for both free and immobilized HRP. All experiments used three independent replicates (n = 3); formal randomization was not performed. Results are presented as mean ± SD. Exact p-values were calculated in Microsoft Excel using a one-tailed paired t-test, and figures were prepared using GraphPad Prism. Statistical significance was defined at α = 0.05 (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01).

LC–MS/MS method development

Pollutant degradation was evaluated in a controlled buffered model-water system to minimize matrix interference and enable direct comparison of free and immobilized HRP in the presence and absence of HOBT. LC–MS/MS operated in multiple-reaction-monitoring mode was selected for its sensitivity and selectivity in quantifying the nine structurally diverse pollutants within the mixture. Prior to injection into the LC–MS, the samples were filtered through a cellulose acetate filter. The LC–MS system used a C18 column (ZORBAX Eclipse Plus) with the following specifications: a particle size of 1.8 μm, an inner diameter of 2.1 mm, and a length of 50 mm. The column temperature was maintained at 35°C. The mass spectrometer used for all experiments was an Agilent 6420 Triple Quad detector. Two mobile phases were employed in the method: Mobile phase A consisted of LC–MS grade water with 0.1% formic acid, and Mobile phase B was 100% LC–MS grade acetonitrile. The flow rate was set to 0.4 mL/min. The LC–MS multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) method was as follows: for the first 2.5 min, the mobile phase was 100% A; from 2.5 min to 15 min, a gradient of B was applied, increasing from 0 to 80%; at 15.1 min, the composition shifted to 90% B and 10% A, lasting for 3 min; finally, from 18.1 to 20 min, the composition returned to 95% A and 5% B. Positive polarity mode was used for all LC–MS/MS experiments, and nitrogen gas was employed for the fragmentation process. The instrument settings included a capillary voltage of 4000 V, a gas flow rate of 8 L/min, a gas temperature of 300 °C, and a nebulizer pressure of 45 psi. All experiments used three independent replicates (n = 3); formal randomization was not performed. Results are presented as mean ± SD, and figures were prepared using GraphPad Prism.

Results

Characterization of HRP@UiO-66-NH2

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX)

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis was done to examine the influence of HRP on the morphology and surface features of UiO-66-NH2, shown in Fig. 1a. The synthesized UiO-66-NH2 were found to have crystallized in ball-shaped crystals which is consistent with the previously reported data (Neyadi et al. 2021). Moreover, EDX analysis was done to confirm the elemental composition of UiO-66-NH2 with the presence of carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and oxygen (O) and zirconium (Zr), Fig. 1a. After immobilization, the SEM image of HRP@UiO-66-NH2 showed the same crystals morphology as UiO-66-NH2, indicating that the HRP didn’t affect the morphology of the MOF. In addition, the EDX results confirmed the presence of the same elements and showed an increase in the percentages of nitrogen, carbon, and oxygen after immobilization, indicating the successful immobilization of HRP onto UiO-66-NH2. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD)

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) was used to analyze the crystallinity of UiO-66-NH2 and HRP@UiO-66-NH2. Figure 1b presents the PXRD patterns of simulated UiO-66-NH2, UiO-66-NH2, and HRP@UiO-66-NH2. The main diffraction peaks in the simulated UiO-66-NH2 appear at 2θ = 7.22° and 8.35°, corresponding to the (111) and (200) planes, respectively (Neyadi et al. 2021). These peaks align closely with the diffraction pattern of the synthesized UiO-66-NH2, confirming the successful synthesis of the MOF. Moreover, the diffraction peaks of HRP@UiO-66-NH2 match those of UiO-66-NH2, indicating that the crystalline structure remains intact after incorporating HRP enzyme into the MOF framework, which is consistent with previously published data showing no change in the crystallinity of the MOF after enzyme immobilization such as laccase on Cu-BTC (Zhang et al. 2020), β-lactamase on ZIF-8 (Yang et al. 2021), and meso-MIL-53(Al) (Jia et al. 2019).

Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy

FTIR analysis was performed to confirm the successful immobilization of the HRP enzyme onto the UiO-66-NH2 framework, Fig. 1c. In the HRP spectra, peaks associated with the protein structure were identified. For instance, the amide I absorption band at 1654 cm−1 corresponds to C = O stretching vibrations. Similarly, the amide II band at 1490 cm−1 is attributed to C-N stretching and N–H bending (Chakraborti et al. 2010; Holzbaur et al. 1996; Park et al. 2012). Additionally, the band at 3214 cm−1 is assigned to O–H stretching vibrations (Tavares et al. 2020). For UiO-66-NH2, distinct bands were observed at 3390 cm−1 and 3506 cm−1, corresponding to the symmetric and asymmetric N–H stretching vibrations, respectively (Huang et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2017). Moreover, the chelation of Zr metal with the -COOH group in the linker was indicated by a band at 1659 cm−1. The C-N stretching characteristic of aromatic amines and the N–H scissoring vibrations were observed at 1260 cm−1 and 1450 cm−1, respectively (Huang et al. 2018), confirming the successful synthesis of UiO-66-NH2. After immobilization, the amide I and II peaks of HRP overlapped with the UiO-66-NH2 band s, resulting in broadened signals in the 1500–1700 cm−1 region, implying successful binding of HRP to UiO-66-NH2. The absence of significant shifts or indicators of framework degradation suggests that immobilization does not affect the structural integrity of UiO-66-NH2.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

The thermal analysis of the HRP enzyme, UiO-66-NH2, and HRP@UiO-66-NH2 was conducted using a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA) under N2 gas, within a temperature range of 25 to 600 °C and a flow rate of 10 mL/min. The TGA profiles of UiO-66-NH2 and HRP@UiO-66-NH2, Fig. 1d, indicate three distinct weight loss steps between 25 and 600°C. In the first region, from 25 to 100 °C, the weight loss for UiO-66-NH2 was approximately 7%, while for HRP@UiO-66-NH2, it was about 15%. This significant difference in the TGA curves between UiO-66-NH2 and HRP@UiO-66-NH2 in this temperature range suggests that the smaller weight loss observed for UiO-66-NH2 corresponds to the evaporation of water molecules in its framework. In contrast, the higher weight loss for HRP@UiO-66-NH2 reflects the breakdown of water as well as protein molecules (Gao et al. 2019; Nadar and Rathod 2020). Additionally, the TGA curve of the HRP enzyme shows that its primary decomposition occurs between 75 and 125°C. The second thermal event, characterized by a weight loss of 23% for UiO-66-NH2 and 15% for HRP@UiO-66-NH2, is attributed to the loss of DMF solvent confined within the pores of MOF framework. The final thermal event occurred around 350–400°C for both UiO-66-NH2 and HRP@UiO-66-NH2, corresponding to the decomposition of the MOF structure. These findings confirm the high thermal stability of UiO-66-NH2 and demonstrate that HRP immobilization does not compromise this stability. Furthermore, the results provide additional evidence for the successful immobilization of the HRP enzyme on UiO-66-NH2.

Brunauer–Emmett–Teller Surface Area Analysis (BET)

The N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms of UiO-66-NH₂ and HRP@UiO-66-NH₂ were measured to examine their surface areas and pore size distributions at 77 K Fig. 1e. Both materials exhibited Type I isotherms. A Type I isotherm indicates that UiO-66-NH2 and HRP@UiO-66-NH2 have predominantly microporous characteristics. The surface area of UiO-66-NH2 was determined to be 256.27 m2/g, while that of HRP@UiO-66-NH2 decreased to 207.33 m2/g. This suggests that HRP may have blocked some of the pores within the UiO-66-NH2 framework. This observation aligns with previously reported studies, such as the immobilization of the laccase enzyme on MIL-53(Al), which also resulted in a reduction in surface area after immobilization (Jia et al. 2019). Similar reductions in surface area upon enzyme immobilization have been reported for other MOFs, such as ZIF-8 with β-lactamase (Yang et al. 2021), ZIF-8 with lipase (Karimian et al. 2023), and Fe3O4@MIL-100(Fe) with lipase (Xie and Huang 2019). The mean pore size and pore volume of UiO-66-NH2 were 2.77 nm and 0.17 cm3/g, respectively. In contrast, the mean pore size and pore volume of HRP@UiO-66-NH2 were 2.72 nm and 0.14 cm3/g, respectively. These results suggest that the nanomaterials exhibit predominantly microporous characteristics with minor mesoporosity. Similar behavior has been reported in previous studies in which enzyme was immobilized on MOFs (Yang et al. 2021).

Optimization and recyclability of HRP enzyme immobilized on UiO-66-NH2

Optimization of HRP enzyme loading into UiO-66-NH2

The effect of HRP enzyme concentration on the immobilization process was studied by loading various concentrations of HRP into UiO-66-NH2, as illustrated in Fig. 2a. Four different concentrations of HRP—0.5, 1, 2, and 4 mg/mL—were tested. The results revealed that increasing the enzyme loading concentration from 0.5 to 4 mg/mL led to a rise in the relative activity of the enzymes. However, the relative activity plateaued at 1 mg/mL, and further increasing the concentration to 4 mg/mL had no additional effect. This suggests that UiO-66-NH2 likely reached its maximum uptake capacity at 1 mg/mL. Therefore, 1 mg/mL HRP was selected for all subsequent experiments. To address enzyme leaching, a critical concern for physically immobilized enzymes such as HRP on solid supports, leaching experiments were performed to evaluate the interaction strength between HRP and UiO-66-NH2. Figure 2b depicts the activity of unbound or loosely bound HRP in the washing solution during five consecutive water washing cycles. While minimal enzyme leaching was observed after the first wash, no unbound HRP was detected in the supernatant after the second wash. Optimization of pH, temperature, and H2O2 concentration for HRP immobilized on UiO-66-NH2

pH Optimization

The effect of pH on enzymatic activity was evaluated for HRP immobilized on UiO-66-NH2, with pH values ranging from 2.0 to 9.0. A comparison was also made between free and immobilized HRP enzymes to determine whether immobilization influenced the enzyme’s optimal or operational pH range. The results are presented in Fig. 2c. As shown, both free and immobilized HRP exhibited their highest activity at pH 4.0, indicating that immobilization on UiO-66-NH2 did not alter the enzyme’s optimal pH. The operational pH range for both forms of HRP was narrow, spanning from pH 3.0 to pH 5.0. However, immobilization improved the enzyme's activity at pH 3.0, with a notable ~ 30% enhancement compared to the free enzyme. This suggests that the immobilized enzyme might have improved stability or accessibility to substrates under slightly acidic conditions. At other pH values outside this range, no significant difference in activity was observed between the free and immobilized enzymes, with both losing activity rapidly in highly acidic or basic environments.

Temperature optimization

Enzyme activity is highly influenced by reaction temperature; therefore, experiments were conducted to determine the temperature range in which both free and immobilized enzymes remain active. Additionally, the study aimed to assess whether immobilizing HRP on UiO-66-NH2 extends its operable temperature range. Figure 2d illustrates the effect of reaction temperature (30°C to 70 °C) on the activity of free and immobilized HRP. Free HRP enzyme displayed the highest activity at 30 °C, whereas HRP@UiO-66-NH2 reached its maximum activity at 40°C. Despite the difference in optimal temperature, both free and immobilized HRP exhibited comparable thermal stability trends, with immobilized HRP showing a significant improvement of around 10% or more at 50 and 70°C. After 40 °C for both free and immobilized HRP, activity gradually declined, with substantial drops at 70 °C. A similar result was previously observed for laccase enzyme immobilized on hybrid nanoflowers (Zhu et al. 2019).

H2O2 concentration optimization

Since peroxidase enzymes depend completely on hydrogen peroxide for their activity, the concentration of H2O2 is a determinant factor in optimizing enzyme-mediated processes. An insufficient concentration of hydrogen peroxide can lead to inefficient enzymatic activity, while excessive amount of H2O2 can cause oxidative and permanent damage to the enzymes. Therefore, the relative activity of free and immobilized HRP on UiO-66-NH2 was investigated across a range of H2O2 concentrations from 0 to 2.43 mM. The results, shown in Fig. 2e, clearly demonstrate a trend in HRP activity as H2O2 concentration increases. For the free enzyme, HRP shows an increase in enzyme activity as the H2O2 concentration rises, with maximum activity observed at approximately 0.72 mM. This is in close agreement with previously published data, which reported maximum activity for HRP at around 0.6 mM (Šekuljica et al. 2015) and 0.8 mM (Gholami-Borujeni et al. 2011). However, beyond these concentrations, there is a noticeable decline in HRP activity for the free enzyme, likely due to denaturation and oxidative stress induced by high H2O2 concentrations (Abdulmalek et al. 2012). For immobilized HRP, optimum activity was observed at 1.08 mM. Interestingly, immobilized HRP retained significant relative activity at higher H2O2 concentrations (1.08, 1.62, and 2.43 mM), in contrast to free HRP. This enhanced stability may be attributed to the protective environment provided by UiO-66-NH2 framework, which shields HRP from oxidative damage and helps preserve its structural integrity.

At suitable concentrations, H2O2 activates resting HRP to Compound I, which oxidizes substrates through Compound II before returning to its resting state. Excess H2O2 can instead promote Compound III and irreversibly inactive enzyme species through heme damage, explaining the decline in free-HRP activity above 0.72 mM (Morales-Urrea et al. 2023). The higher optimum of HRP@UiO-66-NH2 at 1.08 mM and its retained activity at 1.62–2.43 mM indicate greater apparent peroxide tolerance following immobilization. Interactions with UiO-66-NH2 may restrict destabilizing conformational changes and help preserve the HRP heme environment.

Recyclability of HRP immobilized on UiO-66-NH2

The purpose of immobilizing different enzymes on MOF support materials is to enable enzyme reusability, which helps reduce procedure costs and increases its practical application in water remediation (Jemli et al. 2016). To assess the reusability of HRP@UiO-66-NH2, it was tested in oxidizing ABTS over four consecutive cycles, as shown in Fig. 2f. It was observed that HRP@UiO-66-NH2 maintained nearly all of its initial activity, with a retention rate of up to 95% after four cycles. A slight decrease in activity was observed with continued use, with its activity dropping to around 60% during the fifth cycle, likely due to enzyme leaching or denaturation (Asgher et al. 2017). Nevertheless, the overall findings suggest that the UiO-66-NH2 support allowed HRP to be reused for up to four cycles without significant loss of activity. Therefore, UiO-66-NH2 support material could be recommended for future large-scale studies.

Although HRP@UiO-66-NH2 retained approximately 95% of its initial enzymatic activity through four reuse cycles, post-remediation PXRD, FTIR, and electron-microscopy analyses were not performed. Retained catalytic activity therefore provides evidence of functional reusability but does not directly demonstrate preservation of the framework’s crystallinity, morphology, or chemical composition. The strong Zr–O coordination of UiO-66-type frameworks and previous reports concerning amino-functionalized Zr-MOF–enzyme composites support the plausibility of structural robustness under aqueous operating conditions (Cavka et al. 2008; Kandiah et al. 2010; Kurtuldu et al. 2022; Zeyadi and Almulaiky 2024). Nevertheless, post-use physicochemical characterization is required to verify this interpretation for the present composite. In addition, the measured pollutant profiles should be regarded as degradation time courses rather than a comprehensive kinetic analysis, because the relative semi-quantitative, multicomponent experimental design was not optimized for kinetic-model discrimination. Future studies should combine post-cycle PXRD, FTIR, SEM–EDX, enzyme-leaching measurements, and sufficiently resolved concentration–time data to determine kinetic parameters and distinguish enzyme deactivation, leaching, mass-transfer limitations, and possible changes in the MOF structure.

Requirement of a redox mediator for the degradation of EPs by HRP@UiO-66-NH2

Previous studies have shown that the presence of a redox mediator (RM) in peroxidase-mediated reactions can have varying effects on the degradation of EPs, either enhancing, inhibiting, or having no impact on degradation (Abushahab et al. 2024). In this study, we investigated the use of 1-hydroxybenzotriazole (HOBT) as a redox mediator in HRP-catalyzed reactions and examined its effect on the degradation of the same nine EPs.

Data analysis (Fig. 3a) shows that the addition of HOBT had no effect on MBT and PCM degradation, with the degradation percentage remaining above 95%. In contrast, HOBT substantially enhanced the degradation of SMX (Fig. 3d), MP, and SA and produced a smaller improvement for DEET, whereas its effects on the other pollutants were limited. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies, where the presence of HOBT as a redox mediator enhanced the degradation of various EPs by different peroxidases (Abushahab et al. 2024; Husain and Husain 2007). However, in some cases, HOBT had no impact on EP degradation, as seen with TBZ, LCM-HCl, and ATL. Prior studies have also reported no enhancement in pollutant degradation despite the presence of a redox mediator, suggesting high resistance to degradation (Almaqdi et al. 2019).

HOBT can be oxidized by HRP to a reactive mediator species that transfers oxidative equivalents to pollutants that interact inefficiently with the enzyme active site (Husain and Husain 2007). The enhanced removal of SMX, MP, and SA is consistent with mediator-assisted oxidation, while HOBT provided little additional benefit for MBT and PCM because their removal by HRP was already nearly complete. The variable responses of the remaining pollutants may reflect differences in molecular structure, redox properties, substrate accessibility, or competition within the mixture. Because transformation products were not identified, these explanations represent plausible mechanisms rather than confirmed degradation pathways.

As shown in Table 2, both free and immobilized HRP completely degraded MBT and PCM, regardless of the presence of HOBT. This similarity in degradation efficiency is not surprising, as the enzyme was physically immobilized onto UiO-66-NH2—an approach known to preserve catalytic activity in certain cases. Physical immobilization is typically less disruptive to enzyme structure compared to other immobilization techniques (Bilal et al. 2017; Mohamad et al. 2015). Conversely, free HRP was unable to degrade the remaining EPs efficiently. However, after the addition of HOBT, degradation improved significantly for several pollutants, particularly SMX, where degradation increased from 15.5% to a remarkable 80.2%. In comparison, the immobilized HRP achieved approximately 30% lower degradation efficiency for SMX. This is a noteworthy result that warrants further investigation. Other pollutants also showed considerable improvement in degradation with HOBT. MP degradation increased from 23.3% to 54.8%, while SA improved from 13.7% to 52.8%. For TBZ and LCM-HCl, the addition of HOBT enhanced degradation from 5.3% to 25.3% and from 8.3% to 23.9%, respectively. However, for HRP@UiO-66-NH2, the degradation percentages for TBZ and LCM-HCl with HOBT were lower, at 15.1% and 13.2%, respectively. This discrepancy may arise from minor conformational changes in the enzyme due to its interaction with the support material, which can influence substrate selectivity and catalytic efficiency (Mohamad et al. 2015; Santos et al. 2015). For ATL, both free and immobilized HRP showed negligible degradation. Interestingly, for DEET, immobilized HRP demonstrated more than a 10% improvement in degradation compared to free HRP. With further optimization, this percentage could potentially increase, which is promising since DEET is considered a recalcitrant pollutant. These results highlight the potential of UiO-66-NH2-immobilized HRP as a promising system for water remediation applications.

MOF-based adsorption removes pollutants through their accumulation on finite surface or pore sites, and continued use therefore depends on adsorbent regeneration (Abdullah et al. 2026; Khan et al. 2026; Rathi and Kumar 2021). In contrast, HRP@UiO-66-NH2 introduces catalytic activity into the porous support: immobilized HRP uses H2O2 to oxidatively transform susceptible pollutants through repeated catalytic cycles rather than relying solely on their accumulation on the MOF. Enzyme immobilization also facilitates catalyst recovery and reuse, while HOBT can extend degradation to pollutants that respond poorly to HRP alone. Therefore, the enzyme–MOF system provides an active and reusable treatment approach beyond the passive sequestration achieved by adsorption alone.

Ecological implications and study limitations

Literature evidence summarized in Supplementary Table S2 indicates that the nine tested EPs are associated with adverse effects in aquatic organisms, including oxidative stress, growth inhibition, and developmental, behavioral, and reproductive effects. Therefore, the high removal of 2- mercaptobenzothiazole and paracetamol by HRP@UiO-66-NH2, together with the HOBT-enhanced removal of sulfamethoxazole, methylparaben, and salicylic acid, demonstrates the potential environmental relevance of this treatment system.

This study was conducted in a controlled model-water system; consequently, performance may differ in wastewater containing organic matter, dissolved ions, and competing contaminants. Furthermore, removal of the pollutants does not confirm complete mineralization or reduced toxicity. Future studies should identify transformation products, evaluate toxicity before and after treatment, examine performance in real wastewater, and larger-scale operation.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the successful immobilization of HRP onto UiO-66-NH2, enhancing the enzyme’s stability, reusability, and catalytic efficiency in pollutant degradation. Characterization techniques confirmed the structural integrity of the MOF post-immobilization, while optimization studies identified favorable pH and temperature conditions for HRP activity. Compared to free HRP, the immobilized enzyme exhibited better thermal stability and can withstand higher H2O2 concentrations as well as retaining over 95% of its activity after multiple cycles, reinforcing its potential for cost-effective water treatment. Notably, HRP@UiO-66-NH2 effectively degraded specific emerging pollutants, particularly in the presence of the redox mediator HOBT, which significantly improved degradation efficiency for certain compounds. These findings highlight the promise of enzyme-MOF composites in water remediation and emphasize the need for further studies to explore pollutant-specific interactions, optimize reaction conditions, and assess real-world scalability. For environmental management, HRP@UiO-66-NH2 could be further evaluated as a targeted polishing treatment for emerging pollutants in water, with redox mediators applied selectively according to their pollutant-specific effects. Future research should identify transformation products and evaluate their toxicity, clarify enzyme–substrate interactions, and assess treatment performance, long-term reusability, and scalability in real wastewater matrices.
