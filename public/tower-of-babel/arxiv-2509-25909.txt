A Reduced Basis Method for the Stochastic Landau-Lifshitz–Gilbert Equation
Andrea Scaglioni, Michael Feischl, Fernando Henríquez

Abstract
In this work, we consider the construction of efficient surrogates for the stochastic version of the Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert (LLG) equation using model order reduction techniques, in particular, the Reduced Basis (RB) method. The Stochastic LLG (SLLG) equation is a widely used phenomenological model for the time evolution of the magnetization field confined to a ferromagnetic body while taking into account the effect of random heat perturbations. This phenomenon is mathematically formulated as a nonlinear parabolic problem, where the stochastic component is represented as a parameter-dependent datum depending on a non-compact and high-dimensional parameter. In an offline phase, we use Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD) on high-fidelity samples of the unbounded parameter space. To that end, we use the so-called tangent plane scheme . For the online phase of the RB method, we again employ the tangent plane scheme in the RB space. This is possible due to our particular construction that reduces both spaces of the magnetization and of its time derivative. Due to the saddle-point nature of this scheme, a stabilization that appropriately enriches the RB space is required. Numerical experiments show a clear advantage over earlier approaches using sparse grid interpolation. In a complementary approach, we test a sparse grid approximation of the reduced coefficients in a purely data-driven method, exhibiting the weaknesses of earlier sparse grid approaches, but benefiting from increased stability.



1
Introduction

The Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert equation (LLG) is a phenomenological model describing the time-evolution of the (normalized)
magnetization

\boldsymbol{m}:[0,T]\times D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
of a magnetic body of sub-micrometer length-scale.
It is of particular relevance for material science and solid state physics, as it models a wide range of microscopic effects, as well as for engineering applications such as magnetic sensors, and
magnetic random access memory
(MRAM) devices among others.
From a mathematical point of view, the LLG equation is a nonlinear PDE of parabolic type with a non-convex constraint, which renders the analysis of the equation and its discretization particularly challenging and often requires non-standard arguments and tools. While there are many numerical schemes available, they often come with certain CFL-type convergence conditions. The first unconditionally convergent method is the midpoint rule
[
12
]
. To remove the necessary solution of a nonlinear system in each time-step, the so-called
tangent plane scheme
(TPS) was introduced in
[
4
]
and extended to high-order in
[
3
]
.

The
Stochastic
LLG equation (SLLG)
[
15
,
27
]
is a stochastic PDE model extending the LLG equation by a multiplicative Wiener noise term that models thermal noise disturbing the magnetization.
This is particularly relevant for engineering applications, where quantifying the effect of thermal noise is vital for the design of reliable
tools such as heat-activated magnetic recording technologies
[
2
]
.

A number of numerical methods have been developed for the space and time discretization of the SLLG equation.
Suitable adaptations of the midpoint rule
[
13
]
and tangent plane scheme
[
5
]
aim to solve the stochastic PDE directly, while
[
20
]
applies the Doss-Sussmann transform
[
17
,
35
]
to the SLLG equation to rewrite it into an LLG equation with a random coefficient.

Particularly for computational design and optimization, fast and efficient surrogate models and the approximation of statistical quantities of interest (e.g. moments and probabilities of events) for the SLLG equation are paramount. However, only few methods are available.
One approach is given in
[
6
]
, where a sparse grid (high dimensional interpolation) method is applied to the SLLG equation.
Here, the SLLG equation is transformed into a parametric PDE by means of the Doss-Sussmann transform (as in
[
20
]
) followed by the Lévy-Ciesielski expansion of the Brownian motion (see e.g.
[
18
, Section 3.3.3]
).
The regularity of the parameter-to-solution map is studied as the application of an abstract framework for more general nonlinear parametric PDEs with Gaussian noise.
Moreover, this work tackles the
curse of dimensionality
in the parameter
space by exploiting the sparsity of the parameter-to-solution map.
The resulting sparse grid approximation achieves an approximation rate that is independent of the number of scalar parameters. However, this can only be shown under severe assumptions on the problem at hand and these restrictions show in numerical experiments. While experiments that do not involve strong forcing work extremely well, even moderate oscillations in time bring the sparse grid method quickly to its limits. This is explainable by the fact that sparse grid interpolation is purely data-driven and does not obey physical energy principles between the interpolation points.
The goal of this work to develop a fast surrogate model that also works in these practically relevant cases.

1.1
Model Order Reduction and the Reduced Basis Method

Model Order Reduction (MOR) techniques seek to construct fast and efficient surrogates for parametric maps while maintaining a certified level of accuracy with respect to the high-fidelity solution.
Among them, the Reduced Basis Method has been successfully applied to
stationary parametric PDEs
[
24
,
30
]
.
Loosely speaking, the RB method follows a two-phases,
offline
and
online
, paradigm. In the former, a basis of reduced dimension
is constructed from a number of expensive high-fidelity snapshots,
whereas in the latter, for a new parametric input, a solution is obtained
as an element of the previously computed reduced space.

The temporal variable introduces additional complexities.
The solution of the time-dependent parametric PDEs is not an element of a discretization space,
but an entire
trajectory
or
sample path
over a period of time.
In addition, and contrary to stationary problems, transport-dominated problems suffer from the so-called Kolmogorov barrier
[
7
,
21
]
, which renders traditional linear MOR techniques computationally expensive.

In the last few years, a variety of approaches have been proposed to tackle
the plethora of challenges posed by time-dependent parametric PDEs (pPDE), we mention non exhaustively:
Combined POD-Greedy sampling techniques
[
22
,
23
]
, structure-preserving approaches for Hamiltonian formulations
[
1
]
,
non-intrusive methods
[
8
]
, and non-linear MOR

[
29
]
.
We refer to
[
25
]
for a comprehensive survey
of these and other MOR techniques for time-dependent problems.

1.2
Contributions

In this work, we explore the construction of efficient surrogates for the
approximation of the stochastic-parametric version of the LLG equation using the RB method. For the construction of the reduced space in the offline phase, we follow a rather standard approach. That is, we define sample points in the unbounded parameter defining the SLLG equation. Then, we numerically compute
the trajectories, sample paths or high-fidelity samples. Using these and the well-known POD method, we extract a basis of reduced dimension which captures the behavior of the solution across the previously computed samples.
Alternatively, for time-dependent problems, usually the POD-Greedy approach is preferred
[
22
,
25
,
34
]
.
Therein, sample paths in time are selected sequentially in a greedy fashion
by exploring the parameter space over a suitably
a priori
selected training set. For practical purposes, this requires, however, the existence of
computable and reliable
a posteriori
error estimators as the ones in
[
24
]
, which for the SLLG are not readily available.

In the online phase, and once the reduced basis is already at hand, we explore two different approaches for the efficient computation of
the reduced solution for a given parametric input.

(i)

Galerkin POD-Tangent Plane Scheme (POD-TPS).

For the computation of the reduced solution, we consider the discretization of the original SLLG equation in the reduced space, as opposed to a Galerkin formulation in the finite element space. As in the computation of the high-fidelity samples, the problem’s evolution in time is handled using the tangent plane scheme.
This discretization results in a saddle point problem at each time step, introducing a Lagrange multiplier to enforce the conditions required by the tangent plane scheme.
As discussed in
[
19
]
, even if the high-fidelity model is inf-sup stable, one cannot guarantee that the reduced problem inherits this property. This may lead to a loss of accuracy of the reduced basis method in the online phase.
Following ideas from
[
19
]
, we propose an approach to enrich the reduced space for the velocity field—namely, the derivative of magnetization—and obtain a provably inf-sup stable pair of reduced spaces for the velocity field and the Lagrange multiplier.

(ii)

Sparse Grid-Reduced Basis Projection (SG-RBP)
.
We consider the parametric maps that consist of the projection of
the parametrically-dependent magnetization field into the reduced space.
Next, we consider the task of approximating these coefficients, which are as many as the chosen dimension of the reduced space, by means of tailored interpolation techniques.
Recently, in
[
6
,
32
]
, the theoretical foundations and
computational algorithms for this sort of sparse grid interpolation
over a high-dimensional, unbounded parameter space are established.
Unlike the previous approach, we are not relying on a Galerkin discretization
in the reduced spaces to compute the reduced solution in the online phase.
Therefore, no unstable behavior in the computation of the reduced solution is encountered.

1.3
Outline

This work is structured as follows.
In Section
2
, we introduce an equivalent random-parametric coefficient LLG equation arising from the discretization of the SLLG equation.
In Section
2.1
, we introduce the tangent plane
scheme, which serves throughout this work as the time-stepping method for the parametric LLG equation.

In Section
3
, we introduce the reduced basis method for the SLLG equation, with a particular focus on the construction of the reduced space using POD in the offline phase and the Galerkin projection-based reduced basis method for the computation of the reduced solution in the online phase.
As previously discussed, one cannot guarantee inf-sup stability of the reduced problem. We propose a stabilization technique, which relies on the construction of a reduced space not only for the velocity field and the Lagrange multiplier, but also for the magnetization field itself.

Section
4
proposes a different approach for the computation of the reduced solution that does not rely on the numerical approximation of a Galerkin problem. Instead of the parameter-to-solution map, that is a map with values in the high-fidelity discretization space, we consider its projection onto the reduced space, which is constructed exactly as in Section
3
. Then, one only needs to approximate as many coefficients as the dimension of the reduced space as opposed to as many as the dimension of the high-fidelity discretization space.

In Section
5
, we present a series of numerical experiments comparing the performance of the reduced basis method during the offline phase and in the online phase, in the latter stage for both the previously described approaches.

2
Model Problem: A Parametric LLG equation

In this section, we introduce a parametric time-dependent PDE arising from the discretization of the SLLG equation (a stochastic PDE).

Let
I\coloneqq[0,T]
denote the time interval of interest with
T>0
,
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
a bounded Lipschitz domain and let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathcal{P})
a probability triple.

The parametric equation is obtained by first applying the Doss-Sussmann transform
[
17
,
35
]
to the stochastic PDE solution. The resulting random field solves an equivalent
random coefficient
LLG equation (see
[
6
]
for more details):

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{cases}\alpha\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m}+\boldsymbol{m}\times\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m}&=(1+\alpha^{2})\left[\mathbf{H}_{\text{eff}}-(\mathbf{H}_{\text{eff}}\cdot\boldsymbol{m})\boldsymbol{m}\right]\qquad\textrm{in }I\times D,\\
\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\boldsymbol{m}&=\mathbf{0}\qquad\textrm{on }I\times\partial D,\\
\boldsymbol{m}(0)&=\boldsymbol{m}^{0}\qquad\textrm{on }D.\end{cases}\end{split}

(2.1)

Here, the constant
\alpha>0
denotes the
Gilbert damping parameter
, which is an empirically derived parameter to fit observational data.
The initial condition
\boldsymbol{m}^{0}:D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
is assumed to be unit-modulus, i.e.
\left|\boldsymbol{m}^{0}\right|_{2}=1
a.e. in
D
with
\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\boldsymbol{m}^{0}=0
on
\partial D
.
The
effective field

\mathbf{H}_{\text{eff}}
is a random field defined as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\text{eff}}(\omega,\boldsymbol{m})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Delta\boldsymbol{m}+\hat{\mathcal{C}}(W(\omega),\boldsymbol{m})

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{C}}(W,\boldsymbol{m})

\displaystyle\coloneqq e^{WG}\Delta e^{-WG}\boldsymbol{m}-\Delta\boldsymbol{m}+e^{-WG}\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{ext}},

where
\omega\in\Omega
,
W=W(\omega,t):\Omega\times I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
denotes the Brownian motion,
G\boldsymbol{u}:=\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{g}
for any
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\boldsymbol{g}:D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a datum representing the spatial noise distribution (we assume it for simplicity to be pointwise unit-modulus in
D
with
\partial_{\boldsymbol{n}}\boldsymbol{g}=0
on
\partial D
),
e^{WG}
denotes the exponential of the linear operator
\boldsymbol{u}\mapsto WG(\boldsymbol{u})
, and

\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{ext}}:I\times D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
represents a given external magnetic field.

In order to derive the
parametric coefficient
LLG equation, we consider the Lévy-Ciesielski construction of the Wiener process, which gives the identity

\displaystyle W(\omega,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}Y_{n}(\omega)\eta_{n}(t)\qquad\text{a.e. }\omega\in\Omega,\quad\forall t\in I,

(2.2)

where
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are i.i.d. standard Normal random variables, and

(\eta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is the
Faber-Schauder basis
over
I
. Here, convergence holds uniformly in
t
and almost surely. See
[
18
, Section 3.3.3]
for more details.

We then substitute the random variables
Y_{n}
with parameters
y_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, to obtain:

\displaystyle W(\boldsymbol{y},t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}y_{n}\eta_{n}(t),

(2.3)

A sufficient condition for (
2.3
) to converge in the Hölder space
C^{\delta/2}(I)
,
0\leq\delta<1
(the time regularity of the Wiener process sample paths), is that the sequence
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\max_{j=1,\dots,\lceil 2^{\ell-1}\rceil}\left|y_{\lfloor 2^{\ell-1}\rfloor+j}\right|2^{-(1-\delta)\ell/2}<\infty.

(2.4)

for any
0\leq\delta<1
.
This condition is obtained by imposing
\left\lVert W(\boldsymbol{y},\cdot)\right\rVert_{C^{\delta}(I)}
to be finite and using the definition of the Faber-Schauder basis (see
[
6
, Section 5]
for further details).

Therefore, we consider the
parameter space

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\left\{\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}:\boldsymbol{y}\text{ satisfies }\eqref{eq:summability_Gamma}\right\}.

Substituting
W(\omega,t)
with
W(\boldsymbol{y},t)
for
\boldsymbol{y}\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
and
t\in I
, Equation (
2.1
) becomes a
parametric coefficient
PDE and its solution
\boldsymbol{m}
is a parameter-dependent function in the sense:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{m}:\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\times I\times D\to\mathbb{R}^{3}.

Scalar multiplication of (
2.1
) with
\boldsymbol{m}
shows, under the assumption of sufficient smoothness, that
\boldsymbol{m}
and
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m}
are orthogonal. Hence, the time evolution
t\mapsto\boldsymbol{m}(\boldsymbol{y},t,\boldsymbol{x})
preserves the modulus of the initial condition, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left|\boldsymbol{m}(\boldsymbol{y},t,\boldsymbol{x})\right|_{2}=\left|\boldsymbol{m}^{0}(\boldsymbol{x})\right|_{2}\qquad\text{for all }t\in I,\boldsymbol{y}\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma},\boldsymbol{x}\in D.

This observation motivates the introduction of the
tangent plane to
\boldsymbol{m}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{m})\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{\phi}\in H^{1}(D)^{3}:\boldsymbol{m}\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}=0\textrm{ a.e. in }D\right\},

(2.5)

and we observe
\left((\mathbf{H}_{\text{eff}}\cdot\boldsymbol{m})\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)_{L^{2}(D)^{3}}=0
for all
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{m})
. Therefore, we may introduce a simplified weak formulation by testing (
2.1
) with
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{m})
and integration by parts: Find
\boldsymbol{m}\in H^{1}(I\times D)^{3}
such that for
t\in I
and a.e.
\boldsymbol{y}\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
holds

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\alpha\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)_{L^{2}(D)^{3}}+\left(\boldsymbol{m}\times\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)_{L^{2}(D)^{3}}+\left(\nabla\boldsymbol{m},\nabla\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)_{L^{2}(D)^{3}}=\left(\hat{\mathcal{C}}(W(\boldsymbol{y},t)\boldsymbol{m}),\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)_{L^{2}(D)^{3}}\end{split}

(2.6)

for all
\boldsymbol{\phi}=\boldsymbol{\psi}\times\boldsymbol{m}
with
\boldsymbol{\psi}\in C^{\infty}(I\times D)
. Note that
\boldsymbol{\phi}(t)\in\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{m}(t))
for all
t\in I
.
Due to the assumptions on
\boldsymbol{g}
, it can be proved that the right-hand side of (
2.6
) admits the following representation:

\displaystyle\left(\hat{\mathcal{C}}(W(\boldsymbol{y},t),\boldsymbol{m}),\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)_{L^{2}(D)^{3}}=

\displaystyle\left(\nabla\boldsymbol{m},\nabla\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)_{L^{2}(D)^{3}}-\left(\nabla\left(e^{WG}\boldsymbol{m}\right),\nabla\left(e^{WG}\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)\right)_{L^{2}(D)^{3}}

\displaystyle+\left(e^{-WG}\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{ext}},\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)_{L^{2}(D)^{3}},

where in turn, for all
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in L^{2}(D)^{3}
, we have (see
[
6
, Equation 4.3]
)

\displaystyle e^{WG}\boldsymbol{\phi}=\boldsymbol{\phi}+\sin(W)G\boldsymbol{\phi}+(1-\cos(W))G^{2}\boldsymbol{\phi}.

Thus, Equation (
2.6
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\alpha\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)_{L^{2}(D)^{3}}+\left(\boldsymbol{m}\times\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)_{L^{2}(D)^{3}}+\left(\nabla\left(e^{WG}\boldsymbol{m}\right),\nabla\left(e^{WG}\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)\right)_{L^{2}(D)^{3}}=\left(e^{-WG}\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{ext}},\boldsymbol{\phi}\right)_{L^{2}(D)^{3}}.\end{split}

(2.7)

The well-posedness of the weak form of this random-parametric coefficient LLG equation has been studied extensively in
[
20
]
.

2.1
High-Fidelity Discretization: Tangent Plane Scheme

The tangent plane scheme is a well-established method for the approximation of the time-evolution of the LLG equations.
Used together with the finite element method, it provides a scheme for the full discretization of the SLLG equation (

).
See
[

, the authors study the convergence of this method and higher-order generalizations applied to the deterministic LLG equation, i.e.
\mathbf{H}_{\text{eff}}=\Delta\boldsymbol{m}
.
For order
k
BDF time stepping and order
p
finite elements, they obtain convergence of order
\mathcal{O}(\tau^{k}+h^{p})
of the
L^{\infty}(I,H^{1}(D)^{3})
error under a mild CFL condition for
k=1,2
.
While they also study
k=3,4,5
, we are not as interested in these higher-order schemes because of the Hölder-
\frac{1}{2}
sample-paths regularity of the Brownian motion, which limits the time regularity of the magnetization, thus also the maximum possible approximation rate in time to
\frac{3}{2}
.

3
The Reduced Basis Method for the Parametric LLG Equation

We are interested in the efficient approximation of the parameter-to-solution map for the magnetization field, i.e.,

\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{m}}:\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\rightarrow L^{2}(I;H^{1}(D)^{3}):\boldsymbol{y}\mapsto\boldsymbol{m}_{h}(\boldsymbol{y})

(3.1)

using MOR, in particular the RB method,
see e.g.
[
24
,
30
]
and

[
25
]
for a survey concerning its application to time-dependent problems.

However, the TPS scheme, as described in Section
2.1
computes, at each time step, an approximation of the velocity field. The magnetization field itself is recovered in a post-processing step, as done in Line
2.11
of Algorithm
1
. Therefore, we focus on the approximation of the velocity parameter-to-solution map, that is

\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{v}}:\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\rightarrow L^{2}(I;H^{1}(D)^{3}):\boldsymbol{y}\mapsto\boldsymbol{v}_{h}(\boldsymbol{y}).

(3.2)

More precisely, we are interested in the construction of a finite-dimensional subspace of
\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
such that each element of the solution manifold

\mathfrak{M}\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}(t,\boldsymbol{y}):\;(t,\boldsymbol{y})\in I\times\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\right\}\subset\boldsymbol{V}_{h}

(3.3)

is efficiently approximated.

In so doing, we encounter the following difficulties.
First, the presence of essential nonlinearities of the form
\boldsymbol{m}\times\Delta\boldsymbol{m}
precludes the rapid convergence of the reduced basis method and, for that matter, of any linear model order reduction technique. The use of the TPS from Section
2.1
, which requires solving
linear
problems only, allows us to circumvent this issue.
However, with the use of the TPS also comes the requirement of using an inf-sup stable pair of spaces to discretize the saddle point formulation in Equation (
2.13
). This cannot be proved for standard choices of reduced spaces for the primal variable and the Lagrange multiplier used in the online Galerkin POD. We therefore apply a stabilization procedure consisting of enriching the primal space with suitable
supremizer
functions.
Finally, the parameter space
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
is of high, and possibly countably infinite, dimension.
The numerical tests from Section
5.3
suggest that the POD generates effective reduced bases robustly with respect to the dimension of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
.

In Section
3.1
we introduce the construction of reduced spaces using POD at a continuous level and shortly discuss rates of convergence.
In Section
3.2
, we present the empirical counterpart of POD, and in Section
3.3
we propose
a tailored stabilization strategy for the online phase of the Galerkin POD method.

3.1
Construction of Reduced Spaces using POD

As discussed, we are interested in the construction of a reduced space
\boldsymbol{V}^{\text{(rb)}}_{J}
of finite, and hopefully small, dimension
J
for the approximation of the parameter-to-solution map (
3.2
) such that

\boldsymbol{V}_{J}^{(\text{rb})}=\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{V}_{J}\subset\boldsymbol{V}_{h}\\
\dim\boldsymbol{V}_{J}\leq J\end{subarray}}\varepsilon(\boldsymbol{V}_{J}),

(3.4)

where, for any
\boldsymbol{V}_{J}\subset\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
, we define

\displaystyle\varepsilon\left(\boldsymbol{V}_{J}\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{h}-\mathsf{P}_{\boldsymbol{V}_{J}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma};L^{2}(I;H^{1}(D)^{3}))}^{2}

(3.5)

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}_{h}(\boldsymbol{y})-\mathsf{P}_{\boldsymbol{V}_{J}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}(\boldsymbol{y})\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(I;H^{1}(D)^{3})}\mu(\text{d}\boldsymbol{y}),

with
\mu
being the tensorized standard Gaussian measure, i.e. the tensor product of standard
Gaussian measures on
\Gamma_{i}=\mathbb{R}
for all
i=1,2,\dots

and
\mathsf{P}_{\boldsymbol{V}_{J}}:H^{1}(D)^{3}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{V}_{J}

is the orthogonal projection operator onto
\boldsymbol{V}_{J}
with respect
to the
H^{1}(\text{D})^{3}
inner product.

The operators defined via

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{h}\colon

\displaystyle L^{2}_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma})\to\boldsymbol{V}_{h},

\displaystyle g\mapsto\mathsf{T}_{h}g

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}(\boldsymbol{y})g(\boldsymbol{y})\mu(\text{d}\boldsymbol{y}),

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}^{\star}_{h}\colon

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}_{h}\to L^{2}_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}),

\displaystyle x\mapsto\mathsf{T}^{\star}_{h}x

\displaystyle=\big(\boldsymbol{v}_{h}(\boldsymbol{y}),x\big)_{H^{1}(D)^{3}}

(3.7)

are of Hilbert-Schmidt type, and thus compact. This makes the integral operator

\displaystyle\mathsf{K}_{h}\colon\boldsymbol{V}_{h}\to\boldsymbol{V}_{h},\qquad x\mapsto\mathsf{K}_{h}x=\mathsf{T}_{h}\mathsf{T}^{\star}_{h}x=\int_{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\boldsymbol{v}_{h}(\boldsymbol{y})\big(u_{h}(\boldsymbol{y}),x\big)_{H^{1}(D)^{3}}\mu(\text{d}\boldsymbol{y})

(3.8)

compact, self-adjoint, and positive definite.
Consequently, it has a countable sequence of eigenpairs

(\zeta_{h,i},\sigma_{h,i}^{2})_{i=1}^{r}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
with
r\in\mathbb{N}
being the rank of the operator
\mathsf{T}_{h}
. Moreover, the eigenvalues accumulate at zero, and we assume that
{\sigma}_{h,1}\geq{\sigma}_{h,2}\geq\cdots\geq{\sigma}_{h,r}\geq 0
.
It is well-known that the span of the eigenfunctions to the
J
largest eigenvalues,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}_{J}^{(\text{rb})}=\operatorname{span}\left\{\zeta_{h,1},\ldots,\zeta_{h,J}\right\}\subset\boldsymbol{V}_{h}

(3.9)

minimizes the projection error (
3.5
)
in the
L^{2}_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma};L^{2}(I;H^{1}(D)^{3}))
-norm
among all subspaces of
\boldsymbol{V}_{h}

of dimension at most
J
.
Furthermore, following
[
30
, Proposition 6.3]
, it holds

\displaystyle\varepsilon\left(\boldsymbol{V}_{J}^{(\text{rb})}\right)=\sum_{i=J+1}^{r}\sigma_{h,i}^{2}.

(3.10)

3.2
Reduced Basis Construction using Empirical POD

Let
\{\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{N_{h}}\}
be a basis of
\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
,
and denote by boldface letters
\mathbf{w}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}}

the coefficients vector of a function
\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
in the
aforementioned basis.
Let
{\bf Q}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}\times N_{h}}
be defined as

\displaystyle\left({\bf Q}_{h}\right)_{i,j}=\left(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i},\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{j}\right)_{H^{1}(D)^{3}},\quad i,j\in\{1,\dots,N_{h}\}.

(3.11)

This one-to-one correspondence yields finite dimensional representations
of the norm and inner product in
\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
, which read

\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{h},\boldsymbol{z}_{h}\right)_{H^{1}(D)^{3}}={\bf w}_{h}^{\star}{\bf Q}_{h}{\bf z}_{h}\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}_{h}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(D)^{3}}=\sqrt{{\bf w}_{h}^{\star}{\bf Q}_{h}{\bf w}_{h}}\eqqcolon\left\lVert{\bf w}_{h}\right\rVert_{{\bf Q}_{h}}

(3.12)

for
\boldsymbol{w}_{h},\boldsymbol{z}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
and their corresponding coefficient vectors
{\bf w}_{h},{\bf z}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}}
.
Observe that
{\bf Q}_{h}
is symmetric and positive definite.

Let
\boldsymbol{V}_{J}
be a subspace of
\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
of dimension
J
spanned
by the orthonormal basis
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{(J)}\}
.
Set
{\bf\Phi}=\left({\bf u}_{h}^{(1)},\dots,{\bf u}_{h}^{(J)}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}\times J}

with coefficient vectors
{\bf u}_{h}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}}
corresponding to the basis

\{\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{N_{h}}\}
of
\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
.

Let
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}(t,\boldsymbol{y})\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
be the velocity field, which depends both on time and the parametric variable
\boldsymbol{y}\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
. Furthermore, let

\{t_{0},\dots,t_{N_{t}}\}\subset I
and
\{\boldsymbol{y}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}^{(N_{S})}\}\subset\boldsymbol{\Gamma}

be sampling points in the time interval
I
and the parameter space
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
,
respectively.
For
(t,\boldsymbol{y})\in I\times\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
,
we consider
{\bf v}_{h}(t,\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}}
to be the representation
of
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}(t,\boldsymbol{y})\in\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
with respect to
\{\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{N_{h}}\}
and define

\displaystyle\varepsilon\left(\boldsymbol{V}_{J}\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{N_{S}N_{t}}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{S}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{t}}\left\lVert{\bf v}_{h}\big(t_{n},\boldsymbol{y}^{(m)}\big)-\sum_{j=1}^{J}\Big(\big({\bf u}^{(j)}_{h}\big)^{\top}{\bf Q}_{h}{\bf v}_{h}\big(t_{n},\boldsymbol{y}^{(m)}\big)\Big){\bf u}^{(j)}_{h}\right\rVert^{2}_{{\bf Q}_{h}}

(3.13)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{S}N_{t}}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{S}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{t}}\left\lVert{\bf v}_{h}\big(t_{n},\boldsymbol{y}^{(m)}\big)-{\bf\Phi}{\bf\Phi}^{\star}{\bf Q}_{h}{\bf v}_{h}\big(t_{n},\boldsymbol{y}^{(m)}\big)\right\rVert^{2}_{{\bf Q}_{h}}.

We are interested in finding the optimal subspace, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{V}^{\text{(rb)}}_{J}=\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{V}_{J}\subset\boldsymbol{V}_{h}\\
\text{dim}(\boldsymbol{V}_{J})\leq J\end{subarray}}\varepsilon\left(\boldsymbol{V}_{J}\right),

(3.14)

which is equivalent to finding
{\bf\Phi}^{\text{(rb)}}_{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}\times J}

such that

{\bf\Phi}^{\text{(rb)}}_{J}=\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf\Phi}_{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}\times J}\\
{\bf\Phi}_{J}\in\mathscr{V}_{J}\end{subarray}}\varepsilon\left({\bf\Phi}_{J}\right).

(3.15)

Here, the notation
\varepsilon({\bf\Phi}_{J})
reflects the dependence
on the subspace
\boldsymbol{V}_{J}
which is encoded in the columns of
{\bf\Phi}_{J}
, and

\mathscr{V}_{J}\coloneqq\left\{{\bf\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}\times J}:{\bf\Phi}^{\top}{\bf{Q}}_{h}{\bf\Phi}={\bf I}_{J}\right\}.

(3.16)

To compute the optimal subspace
\boldsymbol{V}_{J}
, we first sample i.i.d. standard Normal parameter vectors
\boldsymbol{y}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}^{(N_{S})}\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
.
By this we mean that for each
m\in\{1,\dots,N_{S}\}
,
\boldsymbol{y}^{(m)}=(y^{(m)}_{1},\dots,y^{(m)}_{s})
for some
s<\infty
and the components are i.i.d. standard normal.
Then, we assemble the snapshot matrix for the trajectory stemming from the parametric sample
\boldsymbol{y}^{(m)}
as follows

\mathbf{S}_{m}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}{\bf v}_{h}^{0}(\boldsymbol{y}^{(m)}),\dots,{\bf v}_{h}^{N_{t}-1}(\boldsymbol{y}^{(m)})\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}\times N_{t}}

(3.17)

and consider as well the global snapshot matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}\coloneqq\left(\mathbf{S}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{S}_{N_{S}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}\times N},

(3.18)

where
N=N_{t}N_{S}
.
Next, we consider the SVD
\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\dagger}
of
\mathbf{S}=N^{-1/2}{\bf Q}_{h}^{1/2}\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}
,

{\bf U}=\left({\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{N_{h}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}\times N_{h}},\quad{\bf V}=\left({\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{N_{h}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},

(3.19)

are orthogonal matrices, and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\check{\sigma}_{h,1},\ldots,\check{\sigma}_{h,\check{r}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{h}\times N}
with
\check{\sigma}_{h,1}\geq\cdots\geq\check{\sigma}_{h,\check{r}}>0
,
being
\check{r}\in\mathbb{N}
the rank of

\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}
.
Then, the solution of (
3.15
) can be expressed using the
SVD of
\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}
as follows

{\bf\Phi}^{\text{(rb)}}_{J}=\left({\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{\text{(rb)}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}^{\text{(rb)}}_{J}\right)=\left({\bf Q}_{h}^{-1/2}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{1},\dots,{\bf Q}_{h}^{-1/2}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{J}\right)

(3.20)

for
J\leq\check{r}
.

Following
[
30
, Proposition 6.1]
, we have that

\varepsilon\left(\boldsymbol{V}^{\text{(rb)}}_{J}\right)=\sum_{j=J+1}^{\check{r}}\check{\sigma}^{2}_{j}.

(3.21)

Remark 1

(Criterion to select
J
)
.

As per usual, given a tolerance
\epsilon_{\text{POD}}>0
,
we consider the minimum integer
J
such that

\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{J}\check{\sigma}^{2}_{j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{\check{r}}\check{\sigma}^{2}_{j}}\geq 1-\epsilon^{2}_{\text{POD}}.

(3.22)

Remark 2
.

Observe that although the construction of the reduced basis has been thoroughly explained
for the velocity field
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}(t,\boldsymbol{y})
, the exact same procedure can be applied for the construction
of a reduced space for the magnetization field and the Lagrange multiplier involved in the discretization of
(
2.13
).

3.3
Reduced Basis Approximation and Supremizer Stabilization

Following the construction of the reduced basis for the velocity field described in Section
3.2
, in the online phase of the Galerkin POD method, we compute the parametric solution for a given parametric input
\boldsymbol{y}\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
. We do so by seeking the solution of the corresponding variational problem posed in
\boldsymbol{V}^{\text{(rb)}}_{J}
, i.e. the reduced space of dimension
J
for the velocity field, as opposed to in
\boldsymbol{V}_{h}
, namely the
high-fidelity discretization space. In addition, considering that in (
2.13
) we solve a saddle point problem, we also construct a reduced space
W^{\text{(rb)}}_{R}
of dimension
R
for the Lagrange multiplier.

Observe that the dimension of the reduced space for the Lagrange multiplier is not necessarily the same as that of the velocity field.

These considerations yield the following reduced problem to be solved in the online phase of the Galerkin POD method, which

, one has that for each

4
Surrogate Modelling using Sparse Grid Interpolation

Once a reduced basis is available, the Galerkin POD-TPS algorithm from Section
3
provides an efficient reduced order method to approximate the magnetizations
(\boldsymbol{m}_{h}^{n}(\boldsymbol{y}))_{n=0}^{N_{T}}
for a new parameter
\boldsymbol{y}\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
.
However, two issues arise.
Firstly, the online reduced problem
as described in Algorithm
2
lacks inf-sup stability and stabilizing the velocity basis requires an additional computational effort. Secondly, to approximate
M\gg 1
new snapshots corresponding to
\boldsymbol{y}^{\text{(test)}}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}^{\text{(test)}}_{M}\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
, the online algorithm must be used
M
times.

To circumvent these issues, we propose an alternative method based on sparse grid interpolation. Interpolation possibly speeds up the computation of many online samples and circumvents any lack of inf-sup stability.

We begin in Section
4.1
by recalling the sparse grid interpolation notation and determining the relevant method’s parameters. Then, in Section
4.2
, we define a method based on sparse grid interpolation and the projection of the
parameter-to-solution map on a reduced space.
We refer to
[
16
,
28
]
, among others, for a general introduction to sparse grids.

4.1
Sparse Grid Interpolation Notation and Parameters

In this section, we outline the sparse grid notation and relevant parameters.
In the following, we consider:
A
1d nodes family

(\mathcal{Y}_{\nu})_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, which is a sequence of
nodes tuples

\mathcal{Y}_{\nu}=\left\{y_{1},\dots,y_{m(\nu)}\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}
,
i.e. sets of
m(\nu)\in\mathbb{N}
nodes such that
\mathcal{Y}_{0}=\left\{0\right\}
and
\#\mathcal{Y}_{\nu}\leq\#\mathcal{Y}_{\nu+1}
for all
\nu\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
;
A
1d interpolation method

(I_{\nu})_{\nu\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, where
I_{\nu}
interpolates continuous functions of one real variable over the nodes tuple
\mathcal{Y}_{\nu}
;
A
\emph{multi-indexset}

\Lambda
, i.e. a finite subset of
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}
for
s\in\mathbb{N}
.

\Lambda
is called
downward-closed
if for all
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\Lambda
,
\boldsymbol{\nu}-\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\in\Lambda
for all
n=1,\dots,s
such that
\nu_{n}>0
.

Based on the
sparse grid construction

[
16
,
28
]
(which we do not recall here), the parameters above define uniquely a
sparse grid

\mathcal{H}
, i.e. a finite set of nodes in
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
which generalizes tensor products of one-dimensional node tuples.
For
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{H}
, we denote by
L_{\boldsymbol{y}}
the Lagrange interpolation basis function on
\mathcal{H}
, which equals
1
on
\boldsymbol{y}
and 0 on
\mathcal{H}\setminus\left\{\boldsymbol{y}\right\}
.
Finally, the
sparse grid interpolation operator
applied to any continuous function
u
on
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
reads:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}[u](\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{H}}u(\boldsymbol{y})L_{\boldsymbol{y}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}),\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in\boldsymbol{\Gamma}.

We now recall the precise choice of method’s parameters which were found in
[
6
, Section 1.2.3, Section 7]
to give a dimension-independent approximation rate of the pLLG parameter-to-solution map
\boldsymbol{y}\mapsto\boldsymbol{m}(\boldsymbol{y})
:
The 1D nodes family is chosen, for any
\nu>0
, as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{\nu}=\left\{y_{i}\coloneqq\phi\left(-1+\frac{2i}{\left(2^{\nu+1}-1\right)+1}\right)\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,m(\nu)=2^{\nu+1}-1\right\},

where
\phi(x)\coloneqq 2\sqrt{2}{\erf}^{-1}(x)
for
x\in(-1,1)
and
\erf(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-t^{2}}\textrm{d}t
denotes the
error function
. Intuitively,
\phi:(-1,1)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
transforms uniformly distributed nodes into standard normally distributed ones. Using
m(\nu)=2^{\nu+1}-1
nodes guarantees that the nodes family is nested as
\nu\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
increases.
The 1D interpolation operator
I_{\nu}:C^{0}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow C^{0}(\mathbb{R})
is defined as the constant
I_{0}f\equiv f(0)
, if
\nu=0
, and a continuous piecewise-polynomial interpolation if
\nu\geq 1
. In particular, it consists of polynomial interpolation of degree
p\geq 1
in the intervals defined by the 1d nodes, and extension of the nearby polynomial otherwise.
The multi-index set
\Lambda
is defined using a
profit function

\mathcal{P}:\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
In particular,
\Lambda
must contain all multi-indices
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}
with profit above a given threshold
\varepsilon>0
, i.e.

\Lambda=\Lambda(\varepsilon)=\left\{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}:\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}>\varepsilon\right\}
.
The profit function reads:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=\prod_{i:\nu_{i}=1}2^{-\frac{3}{2}\lceil\log_{2}(i)\rceil}\prod_{i:\nu_{i}>1}\left(2^{\nu_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\lceil\log_{2}(i)\rceil}\right)^{-p}\left(\prod_{i:\nu_{i}\geq 1}p2^{\nu_{i}}\right)^{-1}.

This profit function was defined in
[
6
]
based on a regularity analysis of the parameter-to-solution map and guarantees that
\Lambda
is downward-closed for any value of the profit threshold.

4.2
Sparse Grid-Reduced Basis Projection (SG-RBP)

In this section, we describe an alternative surrogate modeling approach for the pLLG equation based on sparse grid interpolation of reduced basis coefficients.
This method leverages the reduced basis constructed in the offline phase and uses sparse grid interpolation with parameters defined in Section
4.1
to efficiently approximate the parametric dependence of the magnetization field.

This method presents some advantages over the Galerkin POD-TPS scheme from Section
3
:
It avoids online Galerkin computations, thus circumventing possible lacks of stability; and, compared to sparse grid interpolation of the high fidelity samples (without reduced basis projection), the dimensionality of the interpolated coordinates vectors is smaller, which results in an enormous speedup.

The convergence of the sparse grid interpolation of the
high-fidelity
LLG samples was studied in
[
6
]
. The result
[
6
, Theorem 7.8]
carries over, as shown in the next proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Consider the parameter-to-solution map
\boldsymbol{y}\mapsto\boldsymbol{m}(\boldsymbol{y})
arising from the pLLG equation (
2.1
) but assuming that

\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{eff}}(W,\boldsymbol{m})=\widehat{\mathcal{C}}(W,\boldsymbol{m})=W\boldsymbol{m}\times\Delta\boldsymbol{g}
.
Additionally, assume that the initial condition is Hölder continuous, i.e.
\boldsymbol{m}^{0}\in C^{2+\delta}(D)^{3}
for some
0<\delta<1
with sufficiently small
C^{2+\delta}(D)^{3}
-seminorm.
Denote by
\mathsf{P}^{\text{(rb)}}_{K}
the orthogonal projection operator onto the magnetization reduced space, as described in Section
3
.
Denote by
\mathcal{I}
the sparse grid interpolation operator defined from the parameters as in Section
4.1
and by
\mathcal{H}\subset\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
the corresponding sparse grid.

Then, for any
\frac{2}{3}<\tau<1
, there exists
C=C(\tau,p)>0
such that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{m}_{K}-\mathcal{I}[\boldsymbol{m}_{K}]\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma},L^{2}(I,H^{1}(D)^{3}))}\leq C\left(\#\mathcal{H}\right)^{1-1/\tau}.

(4.1)

Proof.

In
[
6
, Theorem 7.8]
, the convergence of sparse grid interpolation is proved assuming the regularity of the parameter-to-solution map
\boldsymbol{y}\mapsto\boldsymbol{m}(\boldsymbol{y})
.
More precisely, they assume bounds on the mixed derivatives of the parameter-to-solution map like the ones proved in
[
6
, Proposition 6.5]
. Since here we interpolate the reduced basis projection
\boldsymbol{m}^{n}_{K}(\boldsymbol{y})=\mathsf{P}^{\text{(rb)}}_{K}\boldsymbol{m}^{n}_{h}(\boldsymbol{y})
, a
linear
function of
\boldsymbol{m}^{n}_{h}(\boldsymbol{y})
for all
n=1,\dots,N_{T}
, the same estimates hold.
As a consequence, also the same sparse grid interpolation convergence rates hold.
∎

The previous proposition requires strong assumption on the pLLG problem we are solving. In particular, no external magnetic field can be included in the effective field. Moreover, the simplified expression of
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}(W,\boldsymbol{m})
can only be a good approximation of Equation (
2.1
) in the short time and uniform noise regime, i.e.,
T\ll 1
and
\nabla\boldsymbol{g}\approx\boldsymbol{0}
.

It should be noted that the constant
C=C(p,\tau)
appearing in the convergence estimate depends on problem parameters. The algebraic convergence with respect to the number of sparse grid nodes, with rate at best
\frac{1}{2}
for
\tau=\frac{2}{3}
, may be visible only for large sparse grids in the asymptotic regime.

6
Concluding Remarks

In this work, we considered the SLLG equation, a time-dependent, nonlinear stochastic PDE used to model the evolution
in time of the magnetization field in a ferromagnetic material and subject to random heat variations.

Traditionally, the TPS has been used as a reliable, robust scheme to solve the LLG equation, while additionally preserving the unit modulus of the magnetization.
However, due to its high computational cost, caused by finite element discretizations of large dimensions and small time step sizes, it cannot be used for the repeated computation of large ensembles of the dynamics.

After reformulating the SLLG equation into a parametric PDE, we introduced two reduced order/surrogate modeling numerical schemes to alleviate this computational burden. These are:

•

Galerkin POD-Tangent Plane Scheme (POD-TPS)
:
Firstly, we sample the solution of the parametric LLG equation in a judiciously selected collection of points in the parameter space. Then, by means of a POD as described in Section
3.2
, we extract bases of small dimension for the velocity field and the Lagrange multiplier of the saddle point formulation. We propose a novel stabilization method which is proved to generate an inf-sup stable discretization of the saddle point problem arising in the online phase of the Galerkin POD-TPS method. Numerical experiments indicate that this scheme performs exceptionally well compared to the Galerkin POD-TPS with no stabilization.

•

Sparse Grid-Reduced Basis Projection (SG-RBP)
:
First, we use the high-fidelity TPS to sample the magnetizations corresponding to the sparse grid collocation nodes in the parameter space.
We then compress the magnetizations by projecting onto a reduced basis (computed analogously to the offline phase of the Galerkin POD-TPS scheme).
Finally, in the online phase, we interpolate the compressed coordinates vector with a negligible computational burden. While this method does not require any further stabilization, we observe larger errors than in Galerkin POD-TPS. This is attributed to the
data driven
nature of the interpolation, which does not guarantee energy preservation.

Several challenges remain, particularly in extending these approaches to problems involving moving domain walls. In such cases, classical linear model order reduction methods may be ineffective due to the transport-dominated nature of the magnetization dynamics. Alternatively, one may use non-linear
model order reduction techniques and neural networks to address these issues.

Another possible development is the use of adaptive time-stepping to accurately capture rapid transitions in magnetization dynamics, such as switching events or domain wall motion. Refining the time discretization where sharp variations occur can improve accuracy at only a marginal increase in computational cost.
