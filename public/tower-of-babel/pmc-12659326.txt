The impact of digital inclusive finance on medical and health service expenditures in urban and rural areas of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region
Authors: Chunyang Feng, Ruijiao Lu, Yangyang Wang, Guoxin Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to examine the impact of the development of digital inclusive finance (DIF) at the regional level on the medical and health service expenditures of urban and rural residents in Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region(Xinjiang), so as to help build a new DIF system, and make contributions to better serving the medical security of residents.MethodsIn this study, a large-scale questionnaire survey was used to analyze the cognition of DIF in Xinjiang and the differences in its application in the medical field. With the Xinjiang DIF index in 2022 as the explanatory variable and the total actual medical expenditure of residents as the explained variable. Regression analysis was performed using Stata 18.0 to explore the impact of regional level DIF (total index of DIF, coverage breadth, depth of use, and digitalization degree), individual-level related and regional disparity factors, on the healthcare service expenditures of residents in Xinjiang.ResultsFor every one-unit increase in the total index of DIF, the probability of residents entering the high medical expenditure tier increases by 23.45 times. Among the sub-dimensions, the promoting effects of coverage breadth and digitalization degree are the strongest, both significantly higher than that of depth of use. Gender, age, education background, understanding of DIF, regional division, ethnic composition and other factors all have an impact on residents’ medical and health service expenditures.ConclusionsIn order to promote the development of medical security for urban and rural residents in Xinjiang, it is necessary to continuously enrich the digital application scenarios of DIF for residents and give full play to the role of DIF in promoting people’s livelihood.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13623-7.Keywords: Digital inclusive finance, Medical and health service expenditures, Influencing factors, Andersen’s behavioral model

Introduction

DIF originates from the deep integration of traditional finance with Internet technologies, cloud computing, big data, and blockchain, aiming to provide accessible, efficient, and affordable financial services [1, 2]. Its developmental significance has been reinforced by national policies: the State Council’s “Implementation Opinions on Promoting High-Quality Development of Inclusive Finance” (October 2023) explicitly advocates leveraging digital channels to deliver inclusive financial services in critical livelihood domains, including healthcare [3–5]. The healthcare sector, with its pressing need for equitable and cost-effective service delivery, stands as a primary domain for DIF application. Healthcare, as both a catalyst for economic growth and a cornerstone of common prosperity, is a critical component of livelihood that fundamentally shapes public well-being [6–9]. While economic conditions, demographic shifts, and policy interventions are established drivers of medical and health service expenditures, DIF also plays an important role in bridging the corresponding urban-rural gap. Previous studies indicate that a 1% increase in the DIF index correlates with a decrease of 0.0013 in the Theil index measuring urban-rural disparity in such expenditures. This correlation suggests that the expansion of DIF may reduce inequality in healthcare between urban and rural residents [10–12]. We speculate that DIF transforms latent medical demand into actual expenditure through four synergistic mechanisms. First, DIF services expand healthcare affordability, particularly for rural low-income groups, creating a quasi-income effect that directly enhances medical service accessibility. Second, digital payments break spatiotemporal constraints, drastically lowering transaction friction in medical payments, which is critical for remote rural areas. Third, formal credit mitigates rural credit invisibility, while informal credit alleviates liquidity constraints, thereby jointly unlocking preventive healthcare demand. Fourth, digital health insurance enables seamless enrollment-payment-reimbursement processes, optimizing health risk-sharing efficiency [13]. Despite these potential mechanisms, empirical evidence, particularly from remote and underdeveloped ethnic regions like Xinjiang, remains scarce.

Existing literature has predominantly focused on the macroeconomic effects of DIF or its aggregate impact on medical and health service expenditures, and most studies are situated in economically developed regions, providing insufficient evidence regarding its operation in unique regional contexts characterized by ethnic diversity, geographic remoteness, and digital infrastructure disparities. Crucially, there is a lack of empirical studies that integrate large-scale survey data with quantitative analysis to identify the specific pathways through which DIF influences medical and health service expenditures disparities in multi-ethnic frontier regions. The unique socio-economic and geographic profile of Xinjiang, a frontier multi-ethnic region, presents a critical context in which to examine these underexplored mechanisms. In recent years, Xinjiang’s medical and health security system has improved alongside rapid economic development. However, the persistent urban-rural dual structure has led to substantial disparities in healthcare investments between urban and rural residents [14]. Compared to eastern/central China, ethnic minority regions face greater challenges: sparse populations, economic underdevelopment, and inadequate medical services [15]. Moreover, the urban-rural digital divide has intensified DIF inequality [16]. Given the above context, this study employed a large-scale questionnaire survey to first comprehensively examine urban and rural residents’ understanding of DIF in Xinjiang and the disparities in its application within the medical field. Second, this study extends Andersen’s behavioral model by incorporating regional disparity factors—specifically, regional division and ethnic composition—thereby shifting the research focus from the individual level to a multi-level framework that reflects the urban-rural-ethnic stratification in Xinjiang. The extended model was empirically tested using regression analysis to examine the factors influencing residents’ medical and health service expenditures. Finally, integrating the characteristics of DIF and Xinjiang’s current economic conditions, feasible recommendations were proposed to address these disparities. This study aims to elucidate the contribution of DIF to residents’ medical and health service expenditures within the context of Xinjiang’s urban-rural dual structure.

Methods

Data sources

This study utilizes the Peking University Digital Financial Inclusion Index of China (PKU-DIFIC). The 2022 aggregate index of DIF and its three core dimensions—coverage breadth, depth of use, and digitalization degree—were employed to evaluate DIF development in Xinjiang. Consistent with Peng (2023) and Yu (2023) [17, 18], all DIF indices were divided by 100 to rescale as percentages, facilitating regression interpretation. The 2022 gross domestic product (GDP) data were obtained from the Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region Statistical Yearbook. Data on per capita consumption expenditure of residents (2015–2022) were sourced from the China Health Statistical Yearbook.

Research object

This study employed a random sampling method to conduct a questionnaire survey among urban and rural residents across 14 prefectures, cities, and provincial municipalities in Xinjiang, encompassing 107 county-level administrative units. The questionnaire was developed based on the theoretical framework of Andersen’s behavioral model, with additional dimensions incorporated to assess regional disparities. To enhance its validity and reliability, we conducted a pilot survey. Feedback from the pilot indicated ambiguities in several expressions related to digital financial services, which were subsequently clarified. And remove redundant items based on expert opinions, and add examples where respondents showed confusion (e.g., DIF products now include specific examples like Alipay, WeChat Pay). Finally, the data was collected through the self-designed questionnaire “Survey Questionnaire on Digital Inclusive Finance and Healthcare & Education in Xinjiang Region”, which includes 40 items, this article focuses on 35 of these variables (see supplementary files for details). The survey encompassed respondents’ basic characteristics, economic conditions, and medical circumstances. The following variables were categorized as categorical data: current residential address, gender, household registration, marital status, usage of online financial tools for financial activities, loan application history, participation in medical insurance, presence of long-term illness among family members, regional division and ethnic composition. Additionally, the following variables were treated as ordinal data: age, educational attainment, understanding of DIF products, personal annual income, family annual income, government subsidy funds received, total medical expenses financed through online loans, and self-rated physical health status.

Variable selection

Guided by Andersen’s behavioral model, this study examines the impact of DIF on medical and health service expenditures through three dimensions: predisposing factors, enabling factors, and needs. Critically, we extend Andersen’s behavioral model framework by incorporating regional disparity factors—including regional division and ethnic composition—to capture Xinjiang’s unique socioeconomic stratification. The dependent variable is an ordered categorical variable, while the independent variables include both continuous and categorical types. Detailed descriptions and coding schemes for all variables are provided in Table 1. The total medical expenses are categorized as an ordered discrete variable. Consequently, the multi-level ordered logit model was initially considered. The data underwent both the Brant test and the Wald test, with all resulting P-values being less than 0.000. As a result, the null hypothesis was rejected, indicating that the original data does not satisfy the parallel line assumption. Moreover, since the dependent variable is a 6-level ordinal category, it does not satisfy the continuity assumption of quantile regression. The generalized ordered logit model relaxes the “proportional odds assumption” (which requires the effects of independent variables to be consistent across all ordinal categories) and is suitable for dependent variables that are “partially ordinal”. Given this result, the unordered multinomial logistic regression model was ultimately selected for analysis. Taking into account the level of economic development in the Xinjiang region, we referred to relevant data on residents’ income and consumption expenditure from the Xinjiang Statistical Yearbook. Expenditure levels below 2000 yuan typically cover daily minor medical expenses, the range of 2000 to 10,000 yuan includes treatment costs for more common diseases, while medical expenditures exceeding 10,000 yuan often involve the treatment of major diseases. Thus, during the analysis process, the variables were redefined: expenditure with no relevance or less than 2000 yuan was defined as Level 1, expenditure between 2000 yuan and 10,000 yuan was defined as Level 2, and expenditure exceeding 10,000 yuan was defined as Level 3.

Statistical analysis

Stata 18.0 was utilized to perform descriptive statistics and logistic regression analysis. The regional-level DIF index and its sub-indices, along with individual-level predisposing factors, enabling factors, needs and regional disparity factors, were incorporated into the modeling analysis. A significance level of α = 0.05 was adopted for hypothesis testing.

Results

Basic situation

This study encompassed 14 prefectures, cities, and provincial municipalities in Xinjiang, involving a total of 31,237 participants. The distribution of participants across regions was as follows: 2,297 from Aksu, 1,393 from Altay, 1,150 from Bayingolin Mongolian Autonomous Prefecture, 884 from Bortala Mongolian Autonomous Prefecture, 1,161 from Changji Hui Autonomous Prefecture, 903 from Hami, 3,146 from Hotan, 1,244 from Kashgar, 446 from Karamay, 136 from Kizilsu Kirgiz Autonomous Prefecture, 1,415 from Tacheng, 485 from Turpan, 1,015 from Urumqi, 14,270 from Ili Kazakh Autonomous Prefecture, and 1,292 from provincial municipalities. The demographic characteristics of urban and rural residents in Xinjiang are detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Development trends and current situation of DIF in Xinjiang

As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1A, the total DIF index exhibited a steady increase from 2011 to 2022. Urumqi (the provincial capital) has consistently maintained a leading position, reaching 304.60 in 2022, which is 70.41 higher than Hotan, the city at the bottom of the ranking. Supplementary Fig. 1B illustrates that the total DIF index in Xinjiang rose from 20.34 in 2011 to 342.66 in 2022. However, Xinjiang’s total DIF index consistently remained below the national average across 31 provinces and cities. An analysis of the three indicators—coverage breadth, depth of use, and digitalization degree—across 14 prefectures, cities, and provincial municipalities in Xinjiang is presented in Supplementary Fig. 1C. In 2022, Urumqi and Changji Hui Autonomous Prefecture demonstrated high levels of coverage breadth, whereas Hotan and Kashgar exhibited low levels, reflecting infrastructural gaps in southern Xinjiang. Additionally, significant disparities were observed in the depth of use and coverage breadth across Xinjiang, whereas the digitalization degree showed relatively minor variations. Finally, a comparison of coverage breadth, depth of use, and digitalization degree between Xinjiang and the national average is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1D-F. The coverage breadth index shows a continuous upward trend, while the depth of use index significantly lags behind. In the later stages, the digitalization degree demonstrates an accelerated catch-up trend.

Development trends and current situation of economic and medical in Xinjiang

A comparison of GDP across Xinjiang in 2022 is presented in Supplementary Fig. 2A. The results indicate that Urumqi had the highest GDP, while Changji Hui Autonomous Prefecture exhibited the fastest GDP growth rate. Analysis of per capita consumption expenditure data for national residents from 2015 to 2022 (per capita medical and health service expenditures data in Xinjiang prior to 2015 were incomplete) revealed a rapid increase from 15,712.4 yuan to 24,538.2 yuan, with an average annual growth rate of 6.58%. The proportion of medical and health service expenditures in total per capita consumption remained above 7% during this period. Influenced by factors such as the COVID-19 pandemic, the proportion of medical and health service expenditures declined to 8.69% in 2020 (Supplementary Table 2). Additionally, the financing structure of total national health expenditure from 2015 to 2022 was analyzed. The analysis revealed that the proportion of personal health expenditures in total health expenditures remains relatively stable (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

Awareness of DIF among urban and rural residents in Xinjiang

The questionnaire survey revealed that 10,094 respondents (32.31%) possessed a basic or advanced understanding of DIF products (Fig. 1A), indicating that DIF cognition remains limited among residents. Factors such as gender, household registration, whether to have applied for a loan, whether the family member has a long-term illness and whether to join medical insurance are found to be factors influencing the level of understanding of DIF among different population groups (Supplementary Table 3). 70.20% of respondents reported using online financial tools for financial activities (Fig. 1B). The population of non-agricultural registered residents(61.9%)using online financial tools for financial activities is larger than that of agricultural registered residents (38.1%) (χ² = 69.718, P < 0.001). These activities primarily included transfers and remittances (23.35%), living expenses (19.05%), online shopping (15.93%), and scan-to-pay transactions (15.64%) (Fig. 1C). 73.28% of respondents identified WeChat and Alipay as the primary third-party mobile payment platforms, citing ease of use and time efficiency as key reasons (Fig. 1D-E). These patterns reflect that residents’ DIF usage is concentrated in simple, daily scenarios, with limited involvement in complex services like online investments or insurance, highlighting the shallowness of DIF application. Additionally, 29.80% of respondents reported not using any online financial tools for financial activities, citing a lack of relevant knowledge and interest as primary barriers (Fig. 1F). 50.28% of respondents reported using internet consumer loans (Fig. 1G). With a significant proportion borrowing less than 2,000 yuan annually (Fig. 1H), primarily due to the simple approval process and rapid disbursement of such loans (Fig. 1I). 66.11% of respondents reported applying for loans (Fig. 1J). Factors such as household registration, gender, educational, marital status, whether the family member has a long-term illness, and understanding of DIF products are factors influencing the decision to apply for a loan (Supplementary Table 3). Loans were obtained predominantly through banks (Fig. 1K), with the majority allocated for housing construction and only 10.59% for medical expenses (Fig. 1L). The analysis indicates that while respondents have engaged in DIF’s settlement and credit services, their participation in more advanced financial activities, such as internet investments and savings, remains limited.

Differences in the application of DIF in the field of residential medical care

The survey results indicated that 3.55% of respondents reported poor physical health (Fig. 2A), 26.68% reported having family members with chronic illnesses (Fig. 2B), and hospitals at or above the municipal level were the preferred choice for medical treatment among patients (Fig. 2C). 90.16% of respondents were enrolled in medical insurance (Fig. 2D), with WeChat serving as the primary payment platform for medical insurance (Fig. 2E). 64.90% of respondents had received medical insurance reimbursements (Fig. 2F), and satisfaction rates with the reimbursement ratio, method, and processing time exceeded 90% (Fig. 2G). However, some respondents found the reimbursement process to be complex (Fig. 2H). Over 65% of respondents reported not utilizing online loans for medical expenses (Fig. 2I).

Fixed-effects multinomial logit

The null model yielded an intraclass correlation coefficient of 14.78%, indicating that nearly 15% of the total variation in medical and health service expenditures stems from regional disparities. The forest plot confirmed significant heterogeneity in the DIF effect across different demographic characteristics (Fig. 3). Multi regional analyses substantiate that educational attainment and DIF cognition have a significant positive impact on medical and health service expenditures in most regions. The increase in age is generally accompanied by a rise in medical expenditures, especially in regions with better medical service conditions. Married status is associated with higher medical expenditures in multiple regions. Table 2 presents the results of a multi-level model analysis identifying factors influencing residents’ medical and health service expenditures in Xinjiang. Model 1 included only individual-level variables, while Models 2 to 5 incorporated the regional total DIF index, coverage breadth, depth of use, digitalization degree, and all individual-level variables, respectively. Model fit was assessed using akaike information criterion (AIC), bayesian information criterion (BIC), and pseudo R-squared (R2) revealing that model 2 demonstrated superior fit compared to the single-level model. The total DIF index, coverage breadth, depth of use, and digitalization degree were identified as significant factors influencing medical and health service expenditures. The likelihood ratio test results between the base model and the four extended models (Model 2: LR = 6590.48, P < 0.001; Model 3: LR = 6589.43, P < 0.001; Model 4: LR = 6582.16, P < 0.001; Model 5: LR = 6542.18, P < 0.001) indicate that the newly added total DIF index, coverage breadth, depth of use, and digitalization degree significantly enhance the model’s explanatory power. Model 1 shows that for every one-unit increase in the DIF total index, the probability of residents entering the high medical expenditure level (Level 3) increases by 23.451 times. From a dimensional perspective, the coverage breadth and digitalization degree have the strongest promoting effect on high expenditure, significantly higher than the depth of usage. Additionally, gender, age, education background, household registration, personal annual income, family annual income, whether to use online financial tools for financial activities, government subsidy funds received, whether to join medical insurance, the amount of medical expenses paid by the whole family through online loans, personal physical conditions, whether the family member has a long-term illness, regional division and ethnic composition are the influencing factors of medical and health service expenditure. Table 3 presents robustness checks using sample-splitting approaches. The positive impact of DIF on medical and health service expenditures remains robust across different household-registration types and geographic regions, with digitalization exhibiting the strongest effect among agricultural and Northern Xinjiang populations. Although there are indicator-specific differences in a few subsamples, the core conclusions remain robust across most subgroups, with the pseudo R² values consistently ranging between 0.11 and 0.15.

Discussion

The continuous advancement and deep integration of digital technologies into the finance sector have made the development of DIF an essential driver of high-quality growth in both inclusive finance and the modern economy. As a sustained evolution of traditional inclusive finance, DIF effectively balances commercial viability with social impact. Unlike either indirect or direct financing models, DIF represents a third financing model that closely aligns with the principles of inclusive finance. It thus offers a promising pathway for advancing inclusive finance in the new era [19–21]. Based on research findings, we observe that the development trajectory of the DIF Index in Xinjiang aligns with the national trend. From 2011 to 2013, the growth rate was relatively rapid, primarily due to the initial introduction of the concept of inclusive finance during this period. The definition of inclusive finance remained somewhat ambiguous, and the lack of well-established regulations and supervisory frameworks allowed a surge of enterprises entering the sector, driving rapid development. From 2013 to 2016, the growth rate slowed, largely because the formal introduction of the inclusive finance concept in 2013 clarified the guiding principles, fundamental tenets, and primary objectives for promoting high-quality inclusive finance over the next five years, accompanied by a series of policy measures. After 2016, the growth rate accelerated once again, driven by the introduction of the concept of “DIF” at the G20 Summit, which further delineated the developmental direction of DIF and laid the groundwork for its future high-quality growth [22]. Overall, the current development of DIF in China is progressing favorably, with its inclusive nature being fully realized. However, its development in Xinjiang reveals distinct regional gradients. We posit this stems primarily from language barriers and infrastructure gaps. Such divergence confirms that technological penetration does not equate to inclusive realization—when a “digital-humanities” disconnect occurs, DIF may instead exacerbate regional inequality. This underscores the imperative to establish tri-region coordinated mechanisms for future development.

In recent years, the development of inclusive finance has become deeply integrated with digital technologies, leading to an increasingly expansive reach and a more diverse array of products and services. Beyond its traditional applications in economic development [3], healthcare [23], and education, DIF has extended its influence to areas such as logistics development [24], green agricultural advancement [25, 26], ecological conservation [27], food security [28], carbon emissions reduction [29], and industrial transformation [30]. Notably, China’s mobile payment sector has achieved a relatively leading position globally [31]. A diversified, multi-tiered, and broadly accessible inclusive financial service system has now been largely established, catering to the needs of the entire population. Research indicates that residents across various regions of Xinjiang have begun to engage with the settlement and credit services offered by DIF. However, participation in deeper financial activities such as savings, internet-based investments, and online insurance remains relatively low. This phenomenon may be attributed to the widespread adoption of mobile banking applications by financial institutions, coupled with increasingly stringent government regulations on internet-based lending [32]. These factors have bolstered public trust in online credit services, leading to a gradual rise in the proportion of residents participating in digital credit activities. Nevertheless, challenges persist, including a lack of understanding, concerns about security, and difficulties in navigating digital platforms. Additionally, as Xinjiang is a remote region with underdeveloped digital infrastructure, a significant portion of the population remains excluded from utilizing any financial tools for economic activities. This underscores the considerable distance yet to be traversed in the comprehensive promotion and adoption of DIF.

With the intensification of population aging and the rising level of economic development, the proportion of public health investment continues to expand, and the demand for health and well-being among the population is also increasing [33]. Previous studies have shown that an increase in public health investment significantly enhances residents’ subjective well-being [34]. Therefore, increasing investment in the healthcare sector is a crucial measure to improve public welfare. Research indicates that since the new healthcare reform, the average annual growth rate of medical and health service expenditures in Xinjiang has been 12.25%, yet it lags far behind the national average and even the average of the western regions. In particular, medical and health service expenditures in southern Xinjiang, including areas such as Kashgar and Hotan, falls below the regional average [35]. This disparity can be attributed to Xinjiang’s status as a remote region, with southern Xinjiang experiencing particularly low economic development levels. Coupled with imbalanced regional growth and limited local government fiscal revenues, these factors have resulted in insufficient medical and health service expenditures by local governments. However, the aforementioned findings from this study reveal that residents in Xinjiang have stable medical needs, but the application of DIF in the medical field is currently limited to basic payments and medical insurance processes, failing to effectively address broader medical financing needs. Furthermore, there is still significant room for optimization in the user service experience. This provides a clear direction for promoting the deep integration of DIF and medical services in the future.

Previous studies have identified factors such as population, socio-economic conditions, regional disparities [36], food security [37], urbanization [38], health insurance [39], and disease mortality rates [40] as influential determinants of medical and health service expenditures. Beyond these factors, evidence also suggests that both government and society play active roles in mitigating the financial burden of healthcare on residents [41]. Our research reveals that individual-level factors, including age, education level, household registration status, personal and family annual income, health insurance participation, and self-rated health status, significantly impact residents’ medical and health service expenditures. In addition, this study introduced the variable of regional disparities, including regional divisions and ethnic composition, and found that both variables are factors influencing residents’ medical and health service expenditures. While individual factors directly shape healthcare demand, our key contribution lies in exposing how regional disparity factors reconfigure DIF’s efficacy. Northern Xinjiang is generally more economically developed, with a higher level of urbanization and more advanced digital infrastructure. The relatively high level of economic development results in higher resident incomes, generally better financial literacy, and a concentration of high-quality medical resources. In contrast, Southern Xinjiang is relatively lacking in these aspects. Moreover, different ethnic groups possess unique cultural traditions, consumption habits, diverse languages, and varying levels of education. In ethnic enclaves, DIF must simultaneously overcome spatial segregation, language barriers, and cultural resistance to unleash its effectiveness. Investments in a single dimension are prone to falling into the trap of inefficiency. At the regional level, regression analysis of variables related to the development of DIF also demonstrates its influence on medical and health service expenditures. This is consistent with previous research findings, indicating that DIF generates a strong, positive and robust influence on access to sanitation services in lower and middle-income countries, especially in rural areas [42]. For instance, in rural areas of Ghana, the adoption of mobile money has enhanced healthcare utilization among rural households [43]. Within the DIF index system, these sub-indicators collectively form a comprehensive, multi-dimensional evaluation framework, providing a more accurate reflection of the development status of DIF. Specifically, coverage breadth is measured by user penetration, depth of use by actual utilization, and the digitalization degree by mobility, affordability, credit accessibility, and convenience. The development status of different dimensions varies in their ability to promote medical and health service expenditures. Our empirical results show that the coverage breadth and digitalization degree have the strongest promoting effect on high expenditure, significantly higher than the depth of usage. Firstly, coverage breadth expands the physical and virtual reach of financial services, extending the effective service radius of formal credit, insurance, and digital payments—which helps bridge geographic disparities and ensures access even in remote or sparsely populated areas. By leveling the geographic playing field, coverage breadth raises the ceiling for healthcare expenditures and enhances medical payment capacity, a effect consistent with the Digital Divide Theory which emphasizes reducing spatial and infrastructural barriers to access. Secondly, the strong role of digitalization degree corresponds to Health Equity Theory, emphasizing the reduction of structural barriers and process simplification to ensure equitable access. In regions with high digitalization, the prevalence of mobile payments, online appointment registration, and electronic medical insurance settlement allows patients to “pay with one click” for medical services, reducing time and transportation costs to nearly zero, thereby unleashing previously suppressed medical demand. Digital platforms publicly display doctor ratings, drug prices, and efficacy comparisons, making it easier for patients to discover “cost-effective” or “high-end” services, and actively choose more expensive but higher-quality treatment options. In contrast, depth of use—reflecting repeated or intensive use—may not benefit excluded populations if foundational access and usability are not established, explaining its relatively weaker role in driving medical and health service expenditures.

Based on the above conclusions, we propose the following policy recommendations:

Infrastructure development: It is imperative to recognize the pivotal role of DIF in advancing healthcare services and improving livelihoods, particularly in underdeveloped regions such as Xinjiang. Accelerating the development of digital infrastructure in these western areas is essential. Financial institutions should be actively encouraged to collaborate with healthcare providers to offer services such as online consultations, mobile-based hospitalization settlements, and medical insurance reimbursements, leveraging modern information technologies to enhance operational convenience and efficiency. The government should play a leading role in integrating social resources to address the uneven distribution of infrastructure and socio-technical conditions caused by economic disparities. Special emphasis should be placed on resolving the insufficient coverage of 5G base stations in remote areas. For residents with weaker economic conditions, targeted interventions should be implemented to bridge the urban-rural digital divide, ensuring the rational allocation of information, financial, and technological resources.

Financial literacy: Given that some residents hold misconceptions about financial products and lack adequate financial literacy, it is crucial to focus on individuals who are “financially excluded”—those who have never used online financial tools—as well as families with long-term medical needs and urgent healthcare requirements. From a policy perspective, local governments, healthcare institutions, and community health service centers should actively promote and disseminate knowledge about national DIF policies, helping residents understand this domain and dispel misunderstandings about DIF. Second, building on the varying levels of financial literacy among residents, increased investment in digital skills training for families in underdeveloped regions such as southern Xinjiang is essential. For the ethnic herder communities in Southern Xinjiang, monthly mobile financial literacy classes could be organized. In Northern and Eastern Xinjiang, community digital health assistants (available in multiple languages) could be established. For residents in border areas, regular distribution of bilingual insurance manuals in Chinese-Russian and Chinese-Kazakh can be implemented to enhance the financial literacy of the residents.

Product and service innovation: Financial institutions should be further incentivized to innovate digital financial products tailored to the linguistic, cultural, and economic conditions of minority regions. For instance, mandating that all digital financial products provide interfaces and voice navigation in multiple languages such as Uyghur and Kazakh, it is recommended that manufacturers collaborate with local universities to train AI customer service in ethnic languages, thereby reducing operational errors and trust deficits caused by language barriers. Develop a “Halal Finance” sub-brand, embedding Sharia-compliant investment and lending clauses, and establish financial corners in mosques to enhance the credibility of financial products through religious authority. For families in Southern Xinjiang with an annual per capita income below 2,000 yuan, a “tiered interest rate + fiscal subsidy” combination loan has been introduced. This loan features a 0% interest rate for the first 6 months, with the government covering 100% of the interest. Subsequently, the interest rate will increase annually and be linked to income.

In summary, medical and health service expenditures among residents in Xinjiang is influenced by multiple factors at both individual and regional levels. Notably, the digitalization degree and coverage breadth exert a stronger influence on increasing medical and health service expenditures compared to depth of use. Therefore, strengthening the development of DIF across these dimensions can promote a higher proportion of healthcare spending within overall consumption expenditure, thereby enhancing the contribution of DIF to public welfare. However, this study is subject to several limitations. Although the questionnaire was designed based on the Andersen’s behavioral model and optimized through expert review and pre-survey testing, its measurement validity may remain limited. Furthermore, the reliance on cross-sectional survey data imposes additional limitations. While measures were taken to mitigate these potential biases—including providing clear instructions, incorporating illustrative examples, and refining items through pilot testing—the nature of survey responses inherently involves subjective interpretation and momentary inaccuracy. Nevertheless, the use of a large and demographically diverse sample helps reduce random errors and improves the generalizability of the findings within the study context. For stronger validity, future research could integrate administrative records, financial transaction data, and advanced methods to cross-validate and deepen understanding of DIF’s relationship with medical and health service expenditures. Employing longitudinal designs, panel surveys, or natural experiments would help establish causal relationships and capture the dynamic effects of DIF on medical and health service expenditures over time.

Conclusions

This study provides robust empirical evidence that among the three core dimensions of DIF, coverage breadth and digitalization has the greatest impact on medical and health service expenditures in Xinjiang. Although individual factors directly shape healthcare demand, our primary contribution lies in revealing how regional disparity factors reconfigure the efficacy of DIF. These findings suggest that targeted enhancements to digital infrastructure—particularly multilingual interfaces, culturally attuned financial products, and region-specific subsidies—can effectively unlock suppressed healthcare demand among underserved populations.
