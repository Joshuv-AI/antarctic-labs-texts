Learning mixed quantum states in large-scale experiments
Matteo Votto, Marko Ljubotina, Cécilia Lancien, J. Ignacio Cirac, Peter Zoller, Maksym Serbyn, Lorenzo Piroli, Benoît Vermersch

Abstract
We present and test a protocol to learn the matrix-product operator (MPO) representation of an experimentally prepared quantum state. The protocol takes as an input classical shadows corresponding to local randomized measurements, and outputs the tensors of a MPO which maximizes a suitably-defined fidelity with the experimental state. The tensor optimization is carried out sequentially, similarly to the well-known density matrix renormalization group algorithm. Our approach is provably efficient under certain technical conditions which are expected to be met in short-range correlated states and in typical noisy experimental settings. Under the same conditions, we also provide an efficient scheme to estimate fidelities between the learned and the experimental states. We experimentally demonstrate our protocol by learning entangled quantum states of up to N = 96 N=96 qubits in a superconducting quantum processor. Our method upgrades classical shadows to large-scale quantum computation and simulation experiments.

Learning algorithm—

In order to illustrate and justify all the steps of our algorithm, we make some assumptions on the target state

Application: quantum error mitigation—

We now leverage the access to a MPO description of
\rho
to perform quantum error mitigation.
Quantum error mitigation consists in partially removing the effect of errors in the estimation of properties from a quantum experiment via classical post-processing
Cai et al. 2023
.
Conventional approaches are usually based on assuming specific noise models

, or require additional experiments

;
Guo and Yang 2022
;
Filippov et al. 2023
;
Mangini et al. 2024

Our approach consists instead in quantum principal component analysis (QPCA)

Huang et al. 2022a

\rho=\sum_{a}\Lambda_{a}\ket{\psi_{a}^{\rho}}\bra{\psi_{a}^{\rho}}

\Lambda_{a}>\Lambda_{a+1}

While this idea is powerful, it has been implemented only approximately by virtual distillation

;
Huggins et al. 2021
;

;
Hakoshima et al. 2024
.

Here we perform QPCA by running the DMRG algorithm

H=-\sigma

as a MPS.
Note that this approach does not require additional experiments nor data analysis.

These results experimentally demonstrate QPCA at large scales, and provide a strong case for the application of our learning protocol to quantum error mitigation.

Conclusions and outlook—

Our protocol provides a quantum-to-classical converter
Huang et al. 2022b
, that compresses
N_{u}N_{M}
randomized measurement outcomes from an experimental quantum state
\rho
in a single classical object, the MPO
\sigma
.
Having access to an MPO approximation of
\rho
not only allows us to estimate several physical properties efficiently,
i.e.
without re-processing experimental data each time, but also to apply powerful error-mitigation based on tensor-network algorithms.

Our ability to combine quantum experiments with classical tensor-network methods opens up further possibilities.
For instance, MPO tomography can be associated with MPS preparation algorithms which take as input an MPO/MPS
\sigma
represented classically and devise the quantum circuit that efficiently forms the corresponding state
\rho
in an universal quantum computer
Malz et al. 2024
;
Fomichev et al. 2024
.
This gives us the possibility to interact multiple times with a quantum system, or even connect different experiments to perform quantum computing beyond the possibilities of each individual device.

An interesting use-case consists in quantum circuit cutting:
by saving an intermediate state of a quantum algorithm as a MPO and performing QPCA on it, one could resume the quantum algorithm by reloading the (error-mitigated) state with a MPS preparation circuit.
Another concrete application consists in modular experiments where a fault-tolerant quantum computer is used to analyze the state of a noisy quantum simulator.
For instance, one could
prepare a quantum state of interest in a quantum simulation experiment, and perform MPO tomography; next, one could
use a quantum circuit to prepare its MPS purification on a fault-tolerant quantum computer, and finally apply a quantum algorithm to measure the von Neumann entropy
Gur et al. 2021
;
Luongo and Shao 2024
;
Giovannetti et al. 2025
.

Appendix A
Learning algorithm

In this section we derive the optimization step of the learning algorithm described in the main text.
In particular, given an experimental state
\rho
to be learned and a MPO ansatz
\sigma
, defined as

\sigma=\sum_{\{s_{j}\},\{s^{\prime}_{j}\}}M^{(1)}_{s_{1},s_{1}^{\prime}}M^{(2)}_{s_{2},s_{2}^{\prime}}...~M^{(N)}_{s_{N},s_{N}^{\prime}}\ket{\{s_{j}\}}\bra{\{s_{j}^{\prime}\}}\ ,

(1)

we prove that the optimization step for the tensor
M^{(j)}
proposed in the main text approximately maximizes the geometric-mean fidelity
Liang et al. 2019
;
Elben et al. 2020b

\mathcal{F}_{\text{GM}}(\rho,\sigma)=\frac{\text{tr}[\rho\sigma]}{\sqrt{\text{tr}[\rho^{2}]\text{tr}[\sigma^{2}]}}\ ,

(2)

that is differentiable and can be efficiently computed numerically.
This is proven in two steps.
First, we derive the gradient of
\mathcal{F}_{\text{GM}}(\rho,\sigma)
with respect to a tensor
M^{(j)}
of the MPO
\sigma
, and show that the optimization step can be carried out exactly.
This step is similar to the derivation of the optimization step in the density matrix renormalization group (DMRG) algorithm
Schollwöck 2011
and other sequential tensor network optimization schemes
Stoudenmire and Schwab 2016
;
Han et al. 2018
;
Ayral et al. 2023
.
Then, we show how under the assumptions that (
a
)
\rho
is a MPO with bond dimension
\chi
and (
b
) the states
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}\propto\rho^{2}
have finite correlation lengths, the optimization can be carried out approximately.
This is due to an approximation of the two terms in the gradient, which we prove in detail in App.
B
.
Finally, we explain how to choose the parameters of the learning algorithm to guarantee that the optimization step leads to an accurate solution.

A.1
Exact tensor optimization

We start by deriving the equation satisfied by an individual tensor
M^{(j)}
of the MPO
\sigma
that maximizes
\mathcal{F}_{\text{GM}}(\rho,\sigma)
.
As a first step, we compute the logarithmic gradient of the GM fidelity with respect to a local tensor

G^{(j)}=\frac{\partial_{{M}^{(j)}}\mathcal{F}_{\text{GM}}(\rho,\sigma)}{\mathcal{F}_{\text{GM}}(\rho,\sigma)}=\frac{\text{tr}[\rho\partial_{{M}^{(j)}}\sigma]}{\text{tr}[\rho\sigma]}-\frac{\text{tr}[\sigma\partial_{{M}^{(j)}}\sigma]}{\text{tr}[\sigma^{2}]}\ ,

(3)

where we have used Wirtinger derivative rules
Koor et al. 2023
to obtain that

\partial_{M^{(j)}}\text{tr}[\rho\sigma]=\text{tr}[\rho\partial_{M^{(j)}}\sigma],\ \partial_{M^{(j)}}\text{tr}[\sigma^{2}]=2\text{tr}[\sigma\partial_{M^{(j)}}\sigma]\ .

(4)

Assuming that the GM fidelity has only global maxima as a function of
M^{(j)}
, the optimal
M^{(j)}
satisfies
G^{(j)}=0
.
We show now that it is possible to obtain the local tensor
M^{(j)}
that satisfies this condition exactly by solving a linear system.
Let us define the terms

C^{(j)}_{\rho}=\text{tr}[\rho\partial_{{M}^{(j)}}\sigma],\ \mathcal{C}_{\sigma}^{(j)}M^{(j)}=\text{tr}[\sigma\partial_{{M}^{(j)}}\sigma]

(5)

such that both
C^{(j)}_{\rho}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}^{(j)}
do not depend on
M^{(j)}
(see Fig.
S1
for their diagrammatic notation).
In terms of these objects, the solution to
G^{(j)}=0
is given by

\mathcal{C}^{(j)}_{\sigma}M^{(j)}=\frac{\text{tr}[\sigma^{2}]}{\text{tr}[\rho\sigma]}C^{(j)}_{\rho}\ .

(6)

Using that

\text{tr}[\rho\sigma]=C_{\rho}^{(j)}\cdot M^{(j)}\ ,\ \text{tr}[\sigma^{2}]=\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}^{(j)}M^{(j)}\cdot M^{(j)}

(7)

it is possible to see that Eq. (
6
) is solved by any tensor
M^{(j)}
of the form

M^{(j)}=\lambda\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}^{(j)-1}C_{\rho}^{(j)}

(8)

for any coefficient
\lambda\neq 0
.
We initially choose
\lambda=1
, which corresponds to imposing

\text{tr}[\sigma\partial_{M^{(j)}}\sigma]=\text{tr}[\rho\partial_{M^{(j)}}\sigma]\ ,

(9)

and then normalize the tensor
M^{(j)}
such that
\text{tr}[\sigma]=1
.
We express the resulting update rule for the tensor
M^{(j)}
in the following way

M^{(j)}\leftarrow\mathcal{C}^{(j)-1}_{\sigma}C_{\rho}^{(j)},~M^{(j)}\leftarrow\frac{1}{\text{tr}[\sigma]}M^{(j)}\ .

(10)

where the first update rule consists in solving a linear system of
{4\chi^{\prime}}^{2}
equations, where
\chi^{\prime}
is the bond dimension of the MPO
\sigma
, and the second one normalizes
\sigma
.
This update rule also imposes the Hermiticity of
\sigma
, but not its positivity.
Finally, note that such an optimization scheme would require us to estimate the right-hand side of Eq. (
9
) from experimental data, which requires
O(2^{N})
measurements using classical shadow framework
Huang et al. 2020
.

Approximate tensor optimization

as

\text{tr}[\sigma_{I_{j}}\partial_{M^{(j)}}\sigma_{I_{j}}]=\text{tr}[\rho_{I_{j}}\partial_{M^{(j)}}\sigma_{I_{j}}]

This can be proven rigorously under the assumptions that

1.

\rho
is a translation-invariant matrix-product density operator (MPDO, i.e. a quantum state described by a MPO) with finite bond dimension
\chi
and Rényi-Markov length

2.

the algorithm is close to convergence, so that we can assume that

Note that the condition on the Rényi-Markov length is satisfied by typical MPDOs

Our starting point is the following lemma:
given
\rho
and
\sigma
two translation-invariant MPDOs with bond dimension

of the operator

C_{\rho}^{(j)}=a_{\rho}(C_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}+\varepsilon_{\rho})\ ,

(12)

with

a_{\rho}=\frac{\text{tr}[\rho\tilde{\sigma}]}{\text{tr}[\rho_{I_{j}}\tilde{\sigma}_{I_{j}}]}\ ,

(13)

where

\tilde{\sigma}
is the MPO obtained by substituting the tensor

in

\tilde{M}^{(j)}
such that
\text{tr}[\rho\tilde{\sigma}]\neq 0
, and

||\varepsilon_{\rho}||^{2}_{2}\leq\text{tr}[\rho_{I_{j}}\tilde{\sigma}_{I_{j}}]^{2}K\chi^{2}e^{-\ell/\xi_{\rho\sigma}}\ ,

(14)

where

is a constant.
Analogously, if the Rényi-Markov length
\xi_{\sigma}^{(2)}
is also finite, the same is valid upon the substitution
\rho\rightarrow\sigma
, yielding

\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}^{(j)}\tilde{M}^{(j)}=a_{\sigma}(\mathcal{C}_{\sigma_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}\tilde{M}^{(j)}+\varepsilon_{\sigma})\ ,

(15)

for any tensor
\tilde{M}^{(j)}
such that
\text{tr}[\tilde{\sigma}^{2}]\neq 0

Let us now take the equation satisfied by the optimal tensor
M^{(j)}
[Eq. (
6

\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}^{(j)}M^{(j)}=\frac{M^{(j)}\cdot\mathcal{C}_{\sigma}^{(j)}M^{(j)}}{M^{(j)}\cdot C_{\rho}^{(j)}}C_{\rho}^{(j)}\ ,

(16)

it is now possible to expand it by applying the approximations Eqs. (

\mathcal{C}_{\sigma_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}M^{(j)}+\varepsilon_{\sigma}=\frac{M^{(j)}\cdot\mathcal{C}_{\sigma_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}M^{(j)}+M^{(j)}\cdot\varepsilon_{\sigma}}{M^{(j)}\cdot C_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}+M^{(j)}\cdot\varepsilon_{\rho}}\left(C_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}+\varepsilon_{\rho}\right)\ .

(17)

\mathcal{C}_{\sigma_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}M_{0}^{(j)}=\frac{M_{0}^{(j)}\cdot\mathcal{C}_{\sigma_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}M_{0}^{(j)}}{M_{0}^{(j)}\cdot C_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}}C_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}\ ,

(18)

where

Therefore, the learning algorithm with approximate update is controlled by two parameters

A.3
Convergence guarantees and two-site updates

We now discuss for what choice of
(\ell,\chi^{\prime})
the approximate optimization step described above is guaranteed to converge.
As described in the main text, each tensor
M^{(j)}
is optimized individually by solving Eq. (
11
), which amounts to a linear system in
M^{(j)}
.
This linear system has
4{\chi^{\prime}}^{2}
variables, and at most
4(4^{\ell})^{2}
independent equations.
This can be seen by performing a sequential singular-value decomposition (SVD) of
\sigma_{I_{j}}
, which results in a MPO with maximal bond dimension
4^{\ell}
.
This implies that the linear system solver is guaranteed to converge to a unique solution only if

\chi^{\prime}\leq 4^{\ell}\ .

(20)

When this is not the case, some parameters of the local tensor
M^{(j)}
may not be updated in a way which minimizes the global fidelity, resulting in the propagation of errors.

Another possibility consists in performing two-site updates, where at each step we optimize a tensor
\theta^{(j,j+1)}=M^{(j)}M^{(j+1)}
.
In this way, we do not have to fix the bond dimension of
\sigma
from the beginning, but we can increase it dynamically, similarly to what is done in DMRG
Schollwöck 2011
.
However, even if the bond dimension is initialized to
\chi^{\prime}=1
, it can grow until
\chi^{\prime}=4^{\ell+1}
, as the left and right bond dimensions of
\theta^{(j,j+1)}
can grow up to
4^{\ell}
.
This means that we need to introduce a cut-off to the bond dimension
\chi^{\prime}_{\text{max}}\leq 4^{\ell}
when performing the SVD of
\theta^{(j,j+1)}=M^{(j)}M^{(j+1)}
after a two-site update.
Otherwise, certain local updates might involve solving an under-determined linear system, leading to a possible propagation of uncontrolled errors.

Appendix B
Local approximation of gradient terms

In this section we provide a rigorous proof for the lemma Eq. (
14
) in the case of translation invariant MPDOs, establishing the validity of the local approximate updates with sufficiently large
\ell
.
Before doing so, we define translation-invariant matrix-product density operators (TIMPDOs), and we relate their properties to their transfer operators.
In particular, we rigorously define the correlation length of a MPDO.
The same language will be used in Sec.
D
to prove approximation factorization conditions for the fidelity.

B.1
Translation-invariant matrix-product operators and transfer operators

Figure S3:
Graphical representation of the transfer operators
T_{\rho}^{(n)}
,
T_{\sigma}^{(n)}
and
T_{\rho\sigma}
, defined in Eqs. (
24

A quantum state
\rho
is a translation-invariant MPDO (TIMPDO) if it can be written as in Eq. (
1
) and
M^{(j)}=M
, i.e. when all the tensors are equal.
To have a well-defined translation invariance, we consider MPDOs with periodic boundary conditions, i.e.

\rho=\sum_{\{s_{i}\},\{s^{\prime}_{i}\}}\text{tr}[M^{\prime}_{s_{1},s_{1}^{\prime}}M^{\prime}_{s_{2},s_{2}^{\prime}}...~M^{\prime}_{s_{N},s_{N}^{\prime}}]\ket{s_{1},...~,s_{N}}\bra{s^{\prime}_{1},...~,s^{\prime}_{N}}\ ,

(21)

and similarly for
\sigma

\sigma=\sum_{\{s_{j}\},\{s^{\prime}_{j}\}}\text{tr}[M_{s_{1},s_{1}^{\prime}}M_{s_{2},s_{2}^{\prime}}...~M_{s_{N},s_{N}^{\prime}}]\ket{s_{1},...~,s_{N}}\bra{s^{\prime}_{1},...~,s^{\prime}_{N}}\ .

(22)

For two TIMPDOs
\rho
and
\sigma
we can define the transfer operators (see Fig.
S3
for a graphical representation)

T_{\rho}^{(n)}=\sum_{\{s_{i}^{(k)}\}}M^{\prime}_{s_{i}^{(1)},s_{i}^{(2)}}M^{\prime}_{s_{i}^{(2)},s_{i}^{(3)}}...M^{\prime}_{s_{i}^{(n)},s_{i}^{(1)}},\ T_{\sigma}^{(n)}=\sum_{\{s_{i}^{(k)}\}}M_{s_{i}^{(1)},s_{i}^{(2)}}M_{s_{i}^{(2)},s_{i}^{(3)}}...M_{s_{i}^{(n)},s_{i}^{(1)}}\ ,

(23)

from which we can obtain the moments

\text{tr}\left[\left(T^{(n)}_{\rho}\right)^{N}\right]=\text{tr}[\rho^{n}],~\text{tr}\left[\left(T^{(n)}_{\sigma}\right)^{N}\right]=\text{tr}[\sigma^{n}]\ .

(24)

Additionally, we define the mixed transfer operator

T_{\rho\sigma}=\sum_{s_{i},s_{i}^{\prime}}M^{(i)}_{s_{i},s_{i}^{\prime}}{M^{\prime}}{}^{(i)}_{s_{i}^{\prime},s_{i}}\ ,

(25)

which analogously yields

\text{tr}[\rho\sigma]=\text{tr}\left[T_{\rho\sigma}^{N}\right]\ .

(26)

In what follows, we will focus on the spectral properties of the transfer operators, as obtained from the decompositions

T_{\rho}^{(1)}=\sum_{a}\lambda_{a}\ket{l^{\rho}_{a}}\bra{r^{\rho}_{a}},\ T_{\sigma}^{(1)}=\sum_{a}\mu_{a}\ket{l^{\sigma}_{a}}\bra{r^{\sigma}_{a}},\ T_{\rho\sigma}=\sum_{a}\nu_{a}\ket{l^{\rho\sigma}_{a}}\bra{r^{\rho\sigma}_{a}}\ ,

(27)

where the eigenvalues are ordered in absolute value (e.g.
|\lambda_{i}|\geq|\lambda_{i+1}|
), and we have the orthonormality conditions for left- and right-eigenvectors
\bra{l^{O}_{a}}r_{b}^{O}\rangle=\bra{r^{O}_{a}}l_{b}^{O}\rangle=\delta_{ab}
.
In case the spectrum of the transfer operator
T_{\rho}^{(1)}
is gapped, i.e.
|\lambda_{1}/\lambda_{0}|<1
, the connected two-point correlation functions of
\rho
will decay exponentially with the distance
Cirac et al. 2021
, inducing the following definition of correlation length

|\lambda_{1}/\lambda_{0}|=e^{-1/\xi_{\rho}^{(1)}}\ .

(28)

Additionally, when a higher order transfer operator
T_{\rho}^{(n)}
is gapped, it has been shown that the global entanglement properties (like the Rényi entropies) are approximately determined by subsystems of a finite length
O(\xi^{(n)}_{\rho})
, defined in an analogous way
Vermersch et al. 2024a
.
This is also known as approximate conditional independence in terms of Rényi entropies
Ángela Capel et al. 2024
, and
\xi_{\rho}^{(n)}
is called Rényi-Markov length
Sang and Hsieh 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025
.

In what follows, we assume the correlation lengths
\xi^{(1)}_{\rho}
,
\xi^{(2)}_{\rho}
to be finite.
Additionally, we also assume the mixed transfer
T_{\rho\sigma}
operator to be gapped, i.e.
\xi_{\rho\sigma}=\frac{1}{\text{log}|\nu_{0}/\nu_{1}|}=O(1)
.
While this last condition is much less explored, we believe it to be true for a generic pair of TIMPDOs with finite correlation lengths.
Practically, towards the convergence of the learning algorithm
\rho\sim\sigma
, we expect
\xi_{\rho\sigma}\sim\xi_{\rho}^{(2)}=O(1)
.
We make the further technical assumptions that the complex scalars

\Gamma^{bc}_{a}=\bra{r^{\rho\sigma}_{a}}l_{b}^{\rho}l^{\sigma}_{c}\rangle,\ \tilde{\Gamma}_{bc}^{a}=\bra{r_{b}^{\rho}r^{\sigma}_{c}}l^{\rho\sigma}_{a}\rangle

(29)

are both non-zero, i.e.
\Gamma_{a}^{bc},\tilde{\Gamma}_{bc}^{a}\neq 0
.
We also believe this assumption to be true in the generic case
Vermersch et al. 2024a
.

Appendix C
Statistical errors in the learning algorithm

In this section we study analytically the effect of statistical errors in the learning algorithm due to a finite number of measurements
N_{u}^{L}N_{M}
.
We consider the framework of classical shadows obtained from local randomized measurements, which we briefly review in the first subsection.
Then, we show how to apply it to our learning algorithm, and prove an upper bound on the effect of statistical errors in the update of a single tensor
M^{(j)}
.
For a choice of the parameters
(\ell,\chi^{\prime})
, we prove that the number of measurement bases needed to obtain the optimal tensor
M^{(j)}
up to an error
\varepsilon
scales like
N_{u}^{L}=O(2^{2\ell}{\chi^{\prime}}\varepsilon^{-2})
, independently of the total system size
N
.

C.1
Local randomized measurements and classical shadows

In this section we review the classical shadow framework for local randomized measurements, and highlight results which we use throughout the manuscript.
We refer the reader to Ref.
Elben et al. 2022
for a more comprehensive review.

Let us assume we have prepared a
N-
qubit quantum state
\rho
in an experiment.
Local randomized measurement consists in applying a layer of single-qubit random unitaries
\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}^{(r)}
, before measuring in the computational basis, where
r
is the index associated with the bases, and each
u_{i}^{(r)}
is sampled from the circular unitary ensemble (CUE).
We consider measurements in
N_{u}
different random bases, with
N_{M}
shots per basis, for a total of
M=N_{u}N_{M}
experimental repetitions.
After each experimental shot, we obtain as a result a bitstring
s^{(r,m)}
, with
r\in[1,N_{u}]
,
m\in[1,N_{M}]
.
Such dataset can be then post-processed in various ways to extract properties of
\rho
.
Classical shadows represent a powerful method to post-process such data
Huang et al. 2020
.
For an arbitrary subset of qubits
I
, we can define for the
r-
th measurement basis a classical shadow

\hat{\rho}^{(r)}_{I}=\frac{1}{N_{M}}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{M}}\bigotimes_{j\in I}\left(3\left(u_{j}^{(r)}\right)^{\dagger}\ket{s_{j}^{(r,m)}}\bra{s_{j}^{(r,m)}}u_{j}^{(r)}-\mathbb{I}_{j}\right)\ ,

(57)

where
\mathbb{I}_{j}
is the identity on the
j-
th qubit.
Note that classical shadows only depend on the local unitaries
u_{j}^{(r)}
, which are classically sampled, and on the measurement outcomes
s^{(r,m)}_{j}
.
While for
N_{M}=1
classical shadows are product operators, and therefore numerically efficient, the sum over
N_{M}
can be computed efficiently only for modest subsystem sizes
|I|
.

The main property of classical shadows is that they are unbiased estimators of
\rho
,
i.e.

\rho_{I}=\mathbb{E}[\hat{\rho}_{I}^{(r)}]\ ,

(58)

where
\mathbb{E}
is the quantum mechanical average over all the possible bases and outcomes.
Using this property, it is possible to obtain an estimator for any linear function of
\rho
, e.g. for the expectation value of an observable
O
with support
I

\hat{o}=\frac{1}{N_{u}}\sum_{r}\text{tr}[\hat{\rho}_{I}^{(r)}O]=\text{tr}[\hat{\rho}_{I}O]\ ,

(59)

where we have defined the averaged shadow
\hat{\rho}_{I}=\frac{1}{N_{u}}\sum_{r}\hat{\rho}_{I}^{(r)}
.
The statistical errors of an estimator are related to its variance, which in this case, for
N_{M}=1
, admits the upper bound
Huang et al. 2020
;
Elben et al. 2020a

\text{Var}[\hat{o}]\leq\frac{2^{|I|}}{M}||O||_{2}^{2}\ .

(60)

Analogously, we can write estimators for multi-copy observables

\hat{o}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{n!}\binom{N_{u}}{n}^{-1}\sum_{r_{1}\neq r_{2}\neq...\neq r_{n}}\text{tr}[\hat{\rho}_{I}^{(r_{1})}\hat{\rho}_{I}^{(r_{2})}...\hat{\rho}_{I}^{(r_{n})}O^{(n)}]

(61)

and compute associated bounds on their variance
Rath et al. 2021
, which generally scale exponentially with the support of
O^{(n)}
.

Relevant examples of such multi-copy observables include the moments of the reduced density matrix
\mathcal{P}_{n}(\rho_{I})=\text{tr}[\rho_{I}^{n}]
.
However, it is also possible to estimate these moments in a different way, that does not rely on the knowledge of the unitaries
\{u_{j}^{(r)}\}

Elben et al. 2019
;
Brydges et al. 2019
.
For example, the purity
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\rho_{I})
can be estimated as

\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{2}(\rho_{I})=\frac{2^{|I|}}{N_{u}N_{M}(N_{M}-1)}\sum_{r=1}^{N_{u}}\sum_{m\neq m^{\prime}=1}^{N_{M}}2^{-D[s_{I}^{(r,m)},s_{I}^{(r,m^{\prime})}]}\ ,

(62)

where
D[s,s^{\prime}]=\sum_{j}|s_{j}-s_{j}^{\prime}|
is the Hamming distance between two bitstrings, and
s_{I}^{(r,m)}
is the bitstring obtained from the
m-
th shot in the
r-
th basis restricted to the subsystem
I
.
This estimator also requires
M=O(2^{a|I|})
measurements with
a=O(1)
for finite accuracy
Elben et al. 2019
, but is computationally less expensive than using classical shadows.
Throughout this work, we use Eq. (
62
) to estimate purities, and classical shadows otherwise.

C.2
Statistical errors in the optimization of a single tensor

In this section we study the statistical errors in the update of a local tensor using randomized measurement data.
The update rule Eq. (
11
) requires experimental data to estimate
C_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}=\text{tr}[\rho_{I_{j}}\partial_{M^{(j)}}\sigma_{I_{j}}]
.
Being
C_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}
linear in
\rho
, we can use the estimator Eq. (
59
) for each one of its terms, obtaining

\hat{C}_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}=\frac{1}{N_{u}^{L}}\sum_{r=1}^{N_{u}^{L}}\text{tr}[\hat{\rho}_{I_{j}}^{(r)}\partial_{M^{(j)}}\sigma_{I_{j}}]\ ,

(63)

where
\hat{\rho}_{I_{j}}^{(r)}
are classical shadows on the subsystem
I_{j}=[j-\ell,j+\ell]
, and
N_{u}^{L}
is the number of random measurement bases collected in the learning set.
The statistical errors in this estimator propagate to the tensor
M^{(j)}
, which will be then updated
\hat{M}^{(j)}\propto\mathcal{C}_{\sigma_{I_{j}}}^{(j)-1}\hat{C}_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}
.
Let us define
M^{\prime(j)}=\mathcal{C}_{\sigma_{I_{j}}}^{(j)-1}C_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}\propto M^{(j)}
.
Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain the propagation of errors

||\hat{M}^{\prime(j)}-M^{\prime(j)}||_{2}\leq||\mathcal{C}_{\sigma_{I_{j}}}^{(j)-1}||_{\infty}||\hat{C}_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}-C_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}||_{2}\ ,

(64)

where
||\cdot||_{2}
is the Hilbert-Schmidt norm (see Eq. (
31
)), and
||O||_{\infty}=\text{max}_{v}\frac{||Ov||_{2}}{||v||_{2}}
is the spectral norm.
We expect that the errors in the normalization of
M^{(j)}
will amount to a constant factor.

We now study the statistical errors in norm
-2

||\hat{C}_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}-C_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}||_{2}
.
The statistical errors of the estimator Eq. (
63
) admit an upper bound via the multidimensional Chebyshev inequality
Altomare and Campiti 1994

\text{Pr}\left(||\hat{V}-V||_{2}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[||\hat{V}-V||_{2}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{j}\text{Var}(\hat{V}_{j})\ ,

(65)

with
\mathbb{E}[\hat{V}]=V
, from which we obtain

\text{Pr}\left(||\hat{C}_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}-C_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}||_{2}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}\sum_{l,r=1}^{\chi^{\prime}}\text{Var}\left([\hat{C}_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}]_{lrss^{\prime}}\right)\ ,

(66)

where we have assumed that the bond dimension of
\sigma
is
\chi^{\prime}
for all links.
Assuming that there is
N_{M}=1
shot per random basis, we can apply the upper bound on the variance for estimators of expectation values Eq. (
60
), obtaining

\text{Var}\left([\hat{C}_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}]_{lrss^{\prime}}\right)\leq\frac{2^{2\ell+1}}{N_{u}^{L}}\bra{rr}\mathcal{C}_{\sigma_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}\ket{ll}\ ,

(67)

where
\ket{ll}=\ket{l}\otimes\ket{l}
,
\ket{rr}=\ket{r}\otimes\ket{r}
.
Summing over all the indices results in the upper bound

\sum_{l,r,s,s^{\prime}}\text{Var}\left([\hat{C}_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}]_{lrss^{\prime}}\right)\leq\frac{2^{2\ell+3}\chi^{\prime}}{N_{u}^{L}}\bra{\delta}\mathcal{C}_{\sigma_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}\ket{\delta}\ ,

(68)

where
\ket{\delta}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\chi^{\prime}}}\sum_{l}\ket{ll}
.
We expect that the term
\bra{\delta}\mathcal{C}_{\sigma_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}\ket{\delta}
contributes with a factor proportional to the purity
\text{tr}[\sigma_{I_{j}}^{2}]
.
This can be seen for translation-invariant MPOs using the transfer operator formalism introduced in App.
B
.
At the leading order in the spectrum of
T^{(1)}_{\sigma}
and
T^{(2)}_{\sigma}
, we obtain

\bra{\delta}\mathcal{C}_{\sigma_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}\ket{\delta}\simeq\frac{\bra{r^{\sigma^{2}}_{0}}\delta\rangle\bra{\delta}l^{\sigma^{2}}_{0}\rangle}{\nu_{0}}\mu_{0}^{N-2\ell-1}\nu_{0}^{2\ell+1}\bra{r_{0}^{\sigma^{2}}}l_{0}^{\sigma}l_{0}^{\sigma}\rangle\bra{r_{0}^{\sigma}r_{0}^{\sigma}}l_{0}^{\sigma^{2}}\rangle\ ,

(69)

while for the purity

\text{tr}[\sigma_{I_{j}}^{2}]\simeq\mu_{0}^{N-2\ell-1}\nu_{0}^{2\ell+1}\bra{r_{0}^{\sigma^{2}}}l_{0}^{\sigma}l_{0}^{\sigma}\rangle\bra{r_{0}^{\sigma}r_{0}^{\sigma}}l_{0}^{\sigma^{2}}\rangle\ .

(70)

This implies that, up to corrections of order
O(\chi^{\prime 2}e^{-\ell/\xi_{\sigma}^{(2)}})
, we have

\bra{\delta}\mathcal{C}_{\sigma_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}\ket{\delta}\simeq c~\text{tr}[\sigma_{I_{j}}^{2}]\ ,

(71)

where
c=\frac{\bra{r^{\sigma^{2}}_{0}}\delta\rangle\bra{\delta}l^{\sigma^{2}}_{0}\rangle}{\nu_{0}}=O(1)
is a constant factor.
Under the assumption that
\ell>O\left(\xi_{\sigma}^{(2)}\text{log}(\chi^{\prime 2})\right)
we can absorb the sub-leading terms in a constant

, obtaining the final result

\text{Pr}\left(||\hat{C}_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}-C_{\rho_{I_{j}}}^{(j)}||_{2}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq c^{\prime}\text{tr}[\sigma_{I_{j}}^{2}]\frac{2^{2\ell+3}\chi^{\prime}}{\varepsilon^{2}N_{u}^{L}}\ .

(72)

This implies that the number of measurements needed to achieve a controlled statistical error in norm
-2

||\hat{M}^{(j)}-M^{(j)}||_{2}
scales like
M^{L}=N_{u}^{L}N_{M}=O(2^{2\ell}{\chi^{\prime}}\varepsilon^{-2})
, as reported in the main text, and is independent of the total system size
N
.

Appendix D
Scalable estimation of mixed-state fidelities

In this section we discuss in more detail the AFC estimation of mixed-state fidelities introduced in the main text.
First, leveraging the formalism of transfer operators, we prove approximate factorization conditions (AFC) for the overlap of two TIMPDOs with finite correlation lengths.
Then, we argue that combining them with AFC for purities
Vermersch et al. 2024a
allows us to define the AFC for the fidelities defined in the main text.
This implies that a measurement budget of size polynomial in
N
is sufficient to estimate fidelities in these quantum states.
Lastly, we numerically demonstrate that a modest measurement budget is sufficient to estimate AFC fidelities in thermal states of up to
N=128
qubits.

D.1
Mixed-state fidelities from randomized measurements

First, we comment on the relation between the two mixed-state fidelities introduced in the main text: the GM fidelity (Eq. (
2
)) and the max fidelity, defined as
Liang et al. 2019
;
Elben et al. 2020b

\mathcal{F}_{\text{max}}=\frac{\text{tr}[\rho\sigma]}{\text{max}(\text{tr}[\rho^{2}],\text{tr}[\sigma^{2}])}\ .

(73)

We can see that
\mathcal{F}_{\text{GM}}(\rho,\sigma)\geq\mathcal{F}_{\text{max}}(\rho,\sigma)
, and
\mathcal{F}_{\text{max}}(\rho,\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi})=\bra{\psi}\rho\ket{\psi}
, while the same does not hold for
\mathcal{F}_{\text{GM}}
.
Additionally, the GM fidelity is insensitive to certain noise models such as global depolarization,
e.g.

\mathcal{F}_{\text{GM}}(\rho,(1-p)\rho+p\mathbb{I}/2^{N})\sim 1-O(\text{tr}[\rho^{2}]/2^{N})
.
This makes the max fidelity more suitable to physically distinguish quantum states, while the GM fidelity, being differentiable, is a better-suited cost function for optimization algorithms.

It is possible to evaluate both fidelities from randomized measurements by estimating sequentially the purities
\text{tr}[\rho^{2}]
and
\text{tr}[\sigma^{2}]
and their overlap
\text{tr}[\rho\sigma]
(see Sec.
C.1
).
In the context of our protocol, we are especially interested in estimating the fidelity between an experimentally prepared state
\rho
and a known state
\sigma
; applying Eq. (
60
), we obtain

\text{Var}\left[\text{tr}[\hat{\rho}\sigma]\right]\leq\frac{2^{N}}{M}\text{tr}[\sigma^{2}]\ .

(74)

This implies that the overlap can be estimated up to controlled statistical errors with
M=O(2^{N})
randomized measurements.
Given that estimating purities requires instead
M=O(4^{N})

Rath et al. 2021
, we generally need at least
M=O(4^{N})
measurements to estimate the previously-defined fidelities.

D.2
Approximate factorization conditions for fidelities

We now introduce approximate factorization conditions (AFC) for the above-defined fidelities, and show how they lift the requirement of exponentially-many randomized measurements for fidelity estimation.
First, let us recall AFC for purities, defined in Ref.
Vermersch et al. 2024a

A quantum state
\rho
satisfies AFC for purities if it is possible to approximate its global purity by

\text{tr}[\rho^{2}]\simeq\mathcal{P}^{(k)}_{\text{AFC}}(\rho)=\frac{\text{tr}[\rho_{A_{1}A_{2}}^{2}]\text{tr}[\rho_{A_{2}A_{3}}^{2}]...\text{tr}[\rho_{A_{R-1}A_{R}}^{2}]}{\text{tr}[\rho_{A_{2}}^{2}]\text{tr}[\rho_{A_{3}}^{2}]...\text{tr}[\rho_{A_{R-1}}^{2}]}\ ,

(75)

where
A_{j}
are connected intervals of
|A_{j}|=k
qubits, with relative error

\Bigg|\frac{\mathcal{P}^{(k)}_{2,\text{AFC}}(\rho)}{\mathcal{P}_{2}(\rho)}-1\Bigg|\leq c_{1}\frac{N}{k}e^{-k/\xi}\ .

(76)

For instance, a TIMPDO
\rho
with bond dimension
\chi
and correlation lengths
\xi_{\rho}^{(1)},\xi_{\rho}^{(2)}=O(1)
satisfies AFC for purities, with
\xi=\text{max}(\xi_{\rho}^{(1)},\xi_{\rho}^{(2)})
, and
c_{1}=O(\chi^{2})
.
From this approximation, it has been proven that for
k>\xi
the purity estimation is guaranteed to have a controlled error with
M=O(2^{4k}N^{3})
measurements
Vermersch et al. 2024a
.

In a similar fashion, we now define AFC for the overlap between two quantum states.
We say that two quantum states
\rho
and
\sigma
satisfy AFC for the overlap if the following approximation holds

\text{tr}[\rho\sigma]\simeq\mathcal{O}^{(k)}_{\text{AFC}}(\rho,\sigma)=\frac{\text{tr}[\rho_{A_{1}A_{2}}\sigma_{A_{1}A_{2}}]\text{tr}[\rho_{A_{2}A_{3}}\sigma_{A_{2}A_{3}}]...\text{tr}[\rho_{A_{R-1}A_{R}}\sigma_{A_{R-1}A_{R}}]}{\text{tr}[\rho_{A_{2}}\sigma_{A_{2}}]\text{tr}[\rho_{A_{3}}\sigma_{A_{3}}]...\text{tr}[\rho_{A_{R-1}}\sigma_{A_{R-1}}]}\ ,

(77)

with
A_{j}
connected intervals of
|A_{j}|=k
qubits, where the relative error is bounded by

\Bigg|\frac{\mathcal{O}^{(k)}_{\text{AFC}}(\rho,\sigma)}{\text{tr}[\rho\sigma]}-1\Bigg|\leq c_{2}\frac{N}{k}e^{-k/\xi}

(78)

for some constants
c_{2}
,
\xi
.
When two states
\rho
and
\sigma
satisfy both the AFC for the overlap and, respectively, for purities, we say that they satisfy AFC for the fidelity.
To justify this definition, consider a choice of
k
for a pair of states
\rho
and
\sigma
satisfying AFC such that

\Bigg|\frac{\mathcal{O}^{(k)}_{\text{AFC}}(\rho,\sigma)}{\mathcal{O}(\rho,\sigma)}-1\Bigg|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathcal{O}}~,~\Bigg|\frac{\mathcal{P}^{(k)}_{2,\text{AFC}}(\rho)}{\mathcal{P}_{2}(\rho)}-1\Bigg|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}~,~\Bigg|\frac{\mathcal{P}^{(k)}_{2,\text{AFC}}(\sigma)}{\mathcal{P}_{2}(\sigma)}-1\Bigg|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime\prime}\ ,

(79)

where
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{P}}=(\varepsilon_{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}+\varepsilon_{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime\prime}+\varepsilon_{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime\prime})\leq 1/2
.
Then, it is possible to see that

\Bigg|\frac{\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{\text{AFC}}(\rho,\sigma)}{\mathcal{F}(\rho,\sigma)}-1\Bigg|\leq 2(\varepsilon_{\mathcal{O}}+\varepsilon_{\mathcal{P}})=O\left(\text{poly}\left(\frac{N}{k}e^{-k/\xi}\right)\right)

(80)

for both
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{\text{max}}
and
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{\text{GM}}
, where

\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{\text{max,AFC}}(\rho,\sigma)=\frac{\mathcal{O}^{(k)}_{\text{AFC}}(\rho,\sigma)}{\text{max}(\mathcal{P}^{(k)}_{2,\text{AFC}}(\rho),\mathcal{P}^{(k)}_{2,\text{AFC}}(\sigma))}~,~\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{\text{GM,AFC}}(\rho,\sigma)=\frac{\mathcal{O}^{(k)}_{\text{AFC}}(\rho,\sigma)}{\sqrt{\mathcal{P}^{(k)}_{2,\text{AFC}}(\rho)\mathcal{P}^{(k)}_{2,\text{AFC}}(\sigma)}}\ .

(81)

This means that, for a pair of states
\rho
and
\sigma
satisfying AFC for the fidelity, there exists a value of
k
such that
\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{\text{AFC}}(\rho,\sigma)\simeq\mathcal{F}(\rho,\sigma)
.
Note that this is trivially true for
\rho=\sigma
, as
\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{\text{AFC}}(\rho,\rho)=1
for any
k
.
In App.
D.3
we prove that two translation-invariant MPDOs
\rho
and
\sigma
with finite correlation lengths satisfy the AFC for the overlap (hence for the fidelity), with
c_{2}=O(\chi^{2})
,
\chi
being the largest bond dimension between
\rho
and
\sigma
, and
\xi=\text{max}(\xi_{\rho}^{(1)},\xi_{\sigma}^{(1)},\xi_{\rho\sigma})
(defined in App.
B
).

Finally, let us comment on the statistical errors in fidelity estimation using AFC.
In order to estimate the AFC purity of
N
qubits with controlled errors
M=O(4^{2k}N^{3})
randomized measurements are sufficient
Vermersch et al. 2024a
.
Repeating the same steps of the proof, it is possible to show that
M=O(2^{2k}N^{3})
randomized measurements are sufficient to estimate
\mathcal{O}^{(k)}_{\text{AFC}}(\rho,\sigma)
with controlled errors.
This implies that, asymptotically,
O(4^{2k}N^{3})
measurements are sufficient to estimate AFC fidelities accurately, and we can in principle use this strategy to benchmark the results of the learning algorithm.
In App.
D.4
we demonstrate numerically that the number of measurements needed is in fact within the reach of current experiments.

D.3
Approximate factorization conditions for the overlap: proof for TIMPDOs

In this section we prove that, under technical assumptions similar to the ones defined in Sec.
B
, two TIMPDOs satisfy AFC for the overlap.
In the language of Sec.
B
, we take two TIMPDOs
\rho
and
\sigma
such that

\xi=\text{max}(\xi_{\rho}^{(1)},\xi_{\sigma}^{(1)},\xi_{\rho\sigma})~,~\Gamma_{0}^{bc}\tilde{\Gamma}^{0}_{bc}\neq 0\ .

(82)

From the spectral decompositions of the transfer operators, we obtain the following expression for the overlap

\mathcal{O}(\rho,\sigma)=\frac{\text{tr}[\rho\sigma]}{\text{tr}[\rho]\text{tr}[\sigma]}=\frac{\sum_{a=0}^{\chi^{2}-1}(\nu_{a})^{N}}{\sum_{b,c=0}^{\chi-1}(\lambda_{b}\mu_{c})^{N}}=\left(\frac{\nu_{0}}{\lambda_{0}\mu_{0}}\right)^{N}(1+\delta)\ ,

(83)

where, from the definition

\delta=\frac{(1+\sum_{a=1}^{\chi^{2}-1}(\nu_{a}/\nu_{0})^{N})}{(1+\sum_{b=1}^{\chi-1}(\lambda_{a}/\lambda_{0})^{N})(1+\sum_{c=1}^{\chi-1}(\mu_{a}/\mu_{0})^{N})}-1

(84)

and the ones of the correlation lengths, we obtain the upper bound

|\delta|\leq 4(\chi^{2}e^{-N/\xi_{\rho\sigma}}+\chi e^{-N/\xi_{\rho}^{(1)}}+\chi e^{-N/\xi_{\sigma}^{(1)}}+\chi^{2}e^{-2N/\text{max}(\xi_{\rho}^{(1)},\xi_{\sigma}^{(1)})})\leq 14\chi^{2}e^{-N/\xi}\ ,

(85)

with
\xi=\text{max}(\xi_{\rho}^{(1)},\xi_{\sigma}^{(1)},\xi_{\rho\sigma})
, and under the further assumptions that

\Big|\sum_{b=1}^{\chi-1}(\lambda_{a}/\lambda_{0})^{N}\Big|\leq 1/2~,~\Big|\sum_{b=1}^{\chi-1}(\mu_{a}/\mu_{0})^{N}\Big|\leq 1/2\ ,

(86)

which are satisfied for

N\geq\xi~\text{log}(2\chi)\ .

(87)

Following similar steps, we can also express the overlap on an interval
A_{l}
, with
|A_{l}|=k
, as

\mathcal{O}(\rho_{A_{l}},\sigma_{A_{l}})=\text{tr}[\rho_{A_{l}}\sigma_{A_{l}}]=\sum_{abc}\nu_{a}^{k}\Gamma^{bc}_{a}(\lambda_{b}\mu_{c})^{N-k}\tilde{\Gamma}^{a}_{bc}=\nu_{0}^{k}(\lambda_{0}\mu_{0})^{N-k}\Gamma^{00}_{0}\tilde{\Gamma}_{00}^{0}(1+\delta_{k})\ .

(88)

In this case,
\delta_{k}
admits the upper bound

|\delta_{k}|\leq\beta(\chi^{2}e^{-k/\xi}+2\chi e^{-(N-k)/\xi}+2\chi^{3}e^{-N/\xi}+\chi^{2}e^{-2(N-k)/\xi}+\chi^{4}e^{-(2N-k)/\xi})\leq\frac{7}{2}\beta\chi^{2}e^{-k/\xi}\ ,

(89)

where
\beta=\text{max}_{abc}\left(\Bigg|\frac{\Gamma_{a}^{bc}\tilde{\Gamma}^{a}_{bc}}{\Gamma_{0}^{bc}\tilde{\Gamma}^{0}_{bc}}\Bigg|\right)
is a constant, and we have assumed that

\chi e^{-(N-2k)/\xi}\leq 1/2\rightarrow N\geq 2k+\xi~\text{log}(2\chi)\ .

(90)

Note that we can obtain a similar form for the overlap
\mathcal{O}(\rho_{A_{l}A_{l+1}},\sigma_{A_{l}A_{l+1}})
by simply performing a substitution
k\rightarrow 2k
.
From these two results, we can write the AFC overlap in periodic boundary conditions as

\mathcal{O}^{(k)}_{\text{AFC}}(\rho,\sigma)=\frac{\prod_{l=1}^{R}\mathcal{O}(\rho_{A_{l}A_{l+1}},\sigma_{A_{l}A_{l+1}})}{\prod_{l=1}^{R}\mathcal{O}(\rho_{A_{l}},\sigma_{A_{l}})}=\left(\frac{\nu_{0}}{\lambda_{0}\mu_{0}}\right)^{N}\left(\frac{1+\delta_{2k}}{1+\delta_{k}}\right)^{R}=\left(\frac{\nu_{0}}{\lambda_{0}\mu_{0}}\right)^{N}(1+\delta^{\prime})\ ,

(91)

where
R=N/k
, assuming
N
is a multiple of
k
, and

\delta^{\prime}=\left(\frac{1+\delta_{2k}}{1+\delta_{k}}\right)^{R}-1\ .

(92)

At this point, the errors induced by the AFC form can be expressed as

\Bigg|\frac{\mathcal{O}^{(k)}_{\text{AFC}}(\rho,\sigma)}{\mathcal{O}(\rho,\sigma)}-1\Bigg|=\Bigg|\frac{1+\delta^{\prime}}{1+\delta}-1\Bigg|\ .

(93)

Now, let us assume that
|\delta|\leq 1/2
, which is satisfied when

N\geq\xi~\text{log}(28\chi^{2})\ .

(94)

Under this assumption, we obtain an upper bound on the error induced by the AFC form of the overlap as a function of
|\delta|
and
|\delta^{\prime}|

\Bigg|\frac{\mathcal{O}^{(k)}_{\text{AFC}}(\rho,\sigma)}{\mathcal{O}(\rho,\sigma)}-1\Bigg|\leq 2|\delta^{\prime}-\delta|\leq 2(|\delta|+|\delta^{\prime}|)\ .

(95)

Since we already have an upper bound on
|\delta|
, the last step is to obtain an upper bound on
|\delta^{\prime}|
.
First of all, let us define
\delta^{\prime\prime}\geq\text{max}(|\delta_{k}|,|\delta_{2k}|)
, such that we can write

|\delta^{\prime}|=\Bigg|\left(\frac{1+\delta_{2k}}{1+\delta_{k}}\right)^{R}-1\Bigg|\leq\Bigg|\left(\frac{1+\delta^{\prime\prime}}{1-\delta^{\prime\prime}}\right)^{R}-1\Bigg|\leq|e^{4R\delta^{\prime\prime}}-1|\ .

(96)

As a last assumption, we take

4R\delta^{\prime\prime}\leq 1\ ,

(97)

which is satisfied for

k\geq\xi~\text{log}(14\beta N\chi^{2})\ .

(98)

Under this condition, we can use that
e^{z}-1\leq 2z
for
0\leq z\leq 1
and Eq. (
89
), obtaining

|\delta^{\prime}|\leq|e^{4R\delta^{\prime\prime}}-1|\leq 8R\delta^{\prime\prime}\leq 28\beta\chi^{2}\frac{N}{k}e^{-k/\xi}\ .

(99)

At this point we can combine this bound with the one on
|\delta|
(Eq. (
85
)) to obtain a bound on the AFC error for the overlap Eq. (
95
).
To summarize, assuming

1.

Eq. (
82
)

2.

N\geq\text{max}\left(2k+\xi~\text{log}(2\chi),\xi~\text{log}(28\chi^{2})\right)

3.

k\geq\xi~\text{log}(14\beta N\chi^{2})
,

we finally obtain the upper bound on AFC errors in the overlap

\Bigg|\frac{\mathcal{O}^{(k)}_{\text{AFC}}(\rho,\sigma)}{\mathcal{O}(\rho,\sigma)}-1\Bigg|\leq 28\chi^{2}e^{-k/\xi}\left(e^{-(N-k)\xi}+2\beta N/k\right)\leq 28\left(2\beta+1\right)\chi^{2}\frac{N}{k}e^{-k/\xi}\ ,

(100)

which corresponds to the approximate factorization conditions for the overlap Eq. (
78
), with
c_{2}=28(2\beta+1)\chi^{2}
and
\xi=\text{max}(\xi_{\rho}^{(1)},\xi_{\sigma}^{(1)},\xi_{\rho\sigma})
, with
\beta=\text{max}_{abc}\left(\Bigg|\frac{\Gamma_{a}^{bc}\tilde{\Gamma}^{a}_{bc}}{\Gamma_{0}^{bc}\tilde{\Gamma}^{0}_{bc}}\Bigg|\right)
.

\tau
(also called prior) of
\rho
is available.
In what follows, we review the key elements of common randomized measurements, and explain how to implement them in the learning algorithm.
Then, we describe the prior state used in the experimental demonstration, and compare performances with and without using CRM.

F.1
Common randomized measurements and tensor updates

Let us consider an experimental quantum state
\rho
on which we have collected randomized measurements, which we represent as a set of classical shadows
\{\hat{\rho}^{(r)}\}
.
In parallel, let us assume that we have access to a classical simulation of the experiment, which outputs the quantum state
\tau
.
CRM consist in defining a new set of unbiased estimators
\{\hat{\rho}_{\tau}^{(r)}\}
such that, if
||\rho-\tau||_{2}\ll||\rho||_{2}
, the statistical errors in this new set of estimators is much smaller than conventional classical shadows.
From a classical representation of
\tau
, we can compute its classical shadows on a subsystem
A
for
N_{M}\rightarrow\infty
in the same bases in which we have performed randomized measurements on
\rho
, obtaining

\hat{\tau}^{(r)}_{A}=\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}2^{-D[s,s^{\prime}]}P_{\tau_{A},U}(s)\bigotimes_{j\in A}\left(u_{j}^{(r)\dagger}\ket{s^{\prime}_{j}}\bra{s^{\prime}_{j}}u_{j}^{(r)}\right)\ ,

(104)

where
\ket{s}=\bigotimes_{j\in A}s_{j}
,
\ket{s^{\prime}}=\bigotimes_{j\in A}s^{\prime}_{j}
are computational basis states on the subsystem
A
,
P_{\tau_{A},U}(s)=\bra{s}U^{(r)\dagger}\tau_{A}U^{(r)}\ket{s}
is their statistics for the state
\tau_{A}
in a given random basis, with
U^{(r)}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}^{(r)}
, and
D[s,s^{\prime}]
is the Hamming distance.
At this point, the new set of estimators is defined as

\hat{\rho}_{\tau,A}^{(r)}=\hat{\rho}_{A}^{(r)}-\hat{\tau}_{A}^{(r)}+\tau_{A}\ .

(105)

Note that these new estimators are unbiased, as
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\rho}^{(r)}_{\tau,A}]=\mathbb{E}[\hat{\rho}_{A}^{(r)}]=\rho_{A}
, and
\hat{\rho}^{(r)}_{\tau,A}=\rho_{A}
in the extreme case where
\rho=\tau
and
N_{M}\rightarrow\infty
.
To reach the latter regime, it is sufficient to take at least
N_{M}\gtrsim 2^{|A|}
shots per basis, such that we obtain a good inference on the distribution
P_{\rho_{A},U}(s)
.
More concretely, when estimating the expectation value of a Pauli observable with support on
A
using measurements in
N_{u}
random Pauli bases taking
N_{M}
shots per basis, we obtain the variance
Vermersch et al. 2024b

\text{Var}[\text{tr}[\hat{\rho}_{\tau,A}O]]\leq\frac{3^{|A|}}{N_{u}}\left(\text{tr}[(\rho-\tau)O]^{2}+\frac{1}{N_{M}}\right)\ ,

(106)

where
\hat{\rho}_{\tau,A}=\frac{1}{N_{u}}\sum_{r}(\hat{\rho}_{A}^{(r)}-\hat{\tau}_{A}^{(r)}+\tau_{A})
, which results in a more precise estimation with respect to conventional classical shadows when
|\text{tr}[\rho_{A}O]|<|\text{tr}[(\rho_{A}-\tau_{A})O]|
, given that
N_{M}
is large enough.

We implement CRM in the learning algorithm in a similar way.
First, we perform a MPO simulation of the reduced density matrices
\tau_{I_{j}}
based on a prior knowledge of
\rho
, such as the quantum circuit preparing
\rho
plus some details about the noise, or the Hamiltonian of the system when considering systems at finite temperature.
Then, we modify the update rule in Eq. (
11
) as

M^{(j)}\leftarrow\mathcal{C}^{(j)-1}_{\sigma_{I_{j}}}\left(\hat{C}^{(j)}_{\rho_{I_{j}}}-\hat{C}^{(j)}_{\tau_{I_{j}}}+C^{(j)}_{\tau_{I_{j}}}\right)~,~M^{(j)}\leftarrow\frac{1}{\text{tr}[\sigma]}M^{(j)}\ ,

(107)

where

\hat{C}^{(j)}_{\rho_{I_{j}}}-\hat{C}^{(j)}_{\tau_{I_{j}}}+C^{(j)}_{\tau_{I_{j}}}=\text{tr}[(\hat{\rho}_{I_{j}}-\hat{\tau}_{I_{j}}+\tau)\partial_{M^{(j)}}\sigma]

(108)

is the CRM estimator of
C^{(j)}_{\rho_{I_{j}}}
.
While the analytical bound Eq. (
106
) only works for Pauli observables, we expect the CRM tensor update Eq. (
107
) to be less sensitive to statistical errors when
\tau\sim\rho
.
We study its performances empirically in the next section on experimental data.

Experimental results

We start by describing the prior states
\{\tau_{I_{j}}\}
used for CRM estimation from experimental data in the main text.
We recall that the quantum circuit implemented in the experiment aims to realize the pure state

\ket{\psi}=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{N_{\text{tot}}-1}e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}Z_{j}Z_{j+1}}\prod_{j=1}^{N_{\text{tot}}}e^{-i\frac{\pi}{8}X_{j}}\right)^{D}\ket{0}^{\otimes N}\ ,

(109)

where
D=1,2
is the depth of the circuit,
X_{j}
and
Z_{j}
are local Pauli operators.
The effect of experimental noise effectively result in the preparation of an unknown mixed state
\rho
.
For
D=1,2
such states are exact MPS with bond dimension
\chi=2,4
.

\tau=\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}
is not a useful prior state, since the condition
||\rho-\tau||_{2}\ll||\rho||_{2}
cannot be satisfied for the values of purity
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\rho)
observed.
In particular, the norm
-2
distance is lower bounded by the difference of the purities, i.e.
||\rho-\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}||_{2}\geq|1-\mathcal{P}_{2}(\rho)|\simeq 1-10^{-4}
for
D=1
.

We take instead reduced density matrices
\tau_{I_{j}}^{\prime}=\text{tr}_{\bar{I}_{j}}[\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}]
, and add space-dependent noise using information collected from the experiment.
For each reduced density matrix
\tau_{[i,i+k]}^{\prime}
, we obtain the prior
\tau_{[i,i+k]}
as

\tau_{[i,i+k]}=\left(\prod_{j\in[i,i+k]}\mathcal{E}_{j}(p_{i}^{(k)})\right)[\tau_{[i,i+k]}]\ ,

(110)

where each
\mathcal{E}_{j}(p)[\rho]=(1-p)\rho+p~\text{tr}_{j}[\rho]\otimes\mathbb{I}_{j}
is a local depolarizing channel with strength
p
.
For each interval
[i,i+k]
we consider a homogeneous depolarizing strength
p_{i}^{(k)}
, which we fit by comparing the resulting purity
\text{tr}[\tau_{[i,i+k]}^{2}]
to
\text{tr}[\rho_{[i,i+k]}^{2}]
as estimated from randomized measurement data.
In this way we obtain a coarse-grained noise model for
\tau
.
We plot in Fig.
S7
the observed purities and the corresponding results for the fitting for
D=1,2
and
k=2(\ell+1)
, with the values of
\ell
considered in the main text.

In Fig.

we compare the results of the learning algorithm when analyzing the experimental data with and without CRM.
In all the cases we investigate, we observe that CRM equipped with the noise model described above allow to reach higher fidelities, as expected.
However, apart from the case
D=1
,
\ell=1
, the increase in fidelity is modest,
i.e.
comparable with roughly doubling
N_{u}^{L}
.
A similar consideration can be drawn for purities, for which we observe similar predictions with and without CRM for the cases considered in the main text.
Finally, we study the errors in estimating observables between our learning protocol and conventional randomized measurements.
We define the error in estimating an observable using the learning algorithm as

\delta\langle O\rangle=|\text{tr}[\hat{\rho}O]-\text{tr}[\sigma O]|\ ,

(111)

where
\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{N_{u}^{T}N_{M}}\sum_{r=1}^{N_{u}^{T}N_{M}}\hat{\rho}^{(r)}
is the average classical shadow obtained from the testing set, and
\sigma
is the MPO output by the learning algorithm.
For
N_{u}^{L}=512
,
N_{u}^{T}=1536
, we observe that for
D=1
,
\ell=2
the errors in estimating
\langle X_{j-1}Z_{j}X_{j+1}\rangle
are generally mitigated, with the largest error as a function of
j
being reduced by half.
These results show how CRM can be useful to improve the predictive power of our learning algorithm.
Finally, we remark that a different prior
\tau
can give drastically different results.
For instance, we expect that using a more fine-grained noise model (such as inhomogeneous local depolarization) would result in a better improvement of our results, at the cost of increased computational overhead to fit the model.
