Graph signal aware decomposition of dynamic networks via latent graphs
Bishwadeep Das, Andrei Buciulea, Antonio G. Marques, and Elvin Isufi

Abstract
Dynamics on and of networks refer to changes in topology and node-associated signals, respectively and are pervasive in many socio-technological systems, including social, biological, and infrastructure networks. Due to practical constraints, privacy concerns, or malfunctions, we often observe only a fraction of the topological evolution and associated signal, which not only hinders downstream tasks but also restricts our analysis of network evolution. Such aspects could be mitigated by moving our attention at the underlying latent driving factors of the network evolution, which can be naturally uncovered via low-rank tensor decomposition. Tensor-based methods provide a powerful means of uncovering the underlying factors of network evolution through low-rank decompositions. However, the extracted embeddings typically lack a relational structure and are obtained independently from the node signals. This disconnect reduces the interpretability of the embeddings and overlooks the coupling between topology and signals. To address these limitations, we propose a novel two-way decomposition to represent a dynamic graph topology, where the structural evolution is captured by a linear combination of latent graph adjacency matrices reflecting the overall joint evolution of both the topology and the signal. Using spatio-temporal data, we estimate the latent adjacency matrices and their temporal scaling signatures via alternating minimization, and prove that our approach converges to a stationary point. Numerical results show that the proposed method recovers individually and collectively expressive latent graphs, outperforming both standard tensor-based decompositions and signal-based topology identification methods in reconstructing the missing network—especially when observations are limited.

•

Role of the graph
. They treat the adjacency tensor purely from a tensor perspective, overlooking the graph structure it represents. Consequently, they often yield low-rank decompositions that lack specific graph-related information. This limitation arises partly because the focus is typically on downstream tasks rather than on capturing topological properties. Nonetheless, incorporating graph structure in the decomposition is crucial for uncovering hidden relationships in the embedding, which are typically enforced via priors. The work in
[
36

uses the tensor data to construct a graph for each dimension and use them as a regularizer to obtain latent embeddings. The embeddings themselves do not have any relational structure. Moreover, such approaches do not extend to tensors capturing time-varying graph structures.

•

Leveraging graph signals
. Existing tensor decompositions often overlook the relationship between the graph’s topology and the node-associated signals (data). In many cases, graph signals are not considered at all. Even when they are considered, the data are frequently used as a proxy for the tensor to be learned. By contrast, leveraging graph signals to identify underlying topologies is crucial in several topology identification approaches

, leading to more accurate topological representations. It can also uncover more meaningful latent graphs that jointly shape both topological and signal evolution.

To address some of these limitations, we draw inspiration from the multi-graph perspective of dynamic graphs and their relationship with graph signals, and propose a novel topology-and-signal-aware decomposition of partially observed dynamic networks. At its core, our decomposition relies on a set of latent graphs whose linear combination captures the observed structural evolution. Moreover, we exploit dynamic graph signals to facilitate the recovery of these latent graphs, particularly when the dynamic topology is only partially observed. Consequently, our approach recovers both the latent graphs and their associated temporal signatures that modulate their contributions over time. This framework is characterized by the following features:

1.

It leads to interpretable latent graph decompositions.

2.

It incorporates spatiotemporal nodal signals as priors to enhance the estimation of dynamic networks, especially when the topological observations are limited.

3.

It uses of an alternating optimization algorithm to recover the latent graphs and their corresponding temporal signatures, with convergence guarantees to stationary points.

4.

It demonstrates strong performance in reconstructing unobserved network structures, outperforming low-rank tensor decompositions and other topology-agnostic alternatives.

Consider a dynamic undirected graph

{\mathcal{V}}={1,\ldots,N}
and a time-varying edge set
{\mathcal{E}}_{t}

\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N\times T}
, where the
t
-th frontal slice
\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}_{:,:,t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}

Frequently, we do not observe the complete topology at any given instant. This occurs, for example, in evolving social networks if some users do not reveal their connections for privacy reasons
[
40
]

[
41

[\underline{{\mathbf{M}}}_{:,:t}]_{ij}=[\underline{{\mathbf{M}}}_{:,:t}]_{ji}=1
only if we observe the presence or absence of an edge between nodes
i
and
j
at time
t

\underline{{\mathbf{M}}}\circ\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}
with
\circ
denoting the element-wise Hadamard product. Since observations often include errors, we typically have access to
\underline{{\mathbf{M}}}\circ(\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}+\underline{{\mathbf{E}}})
, where
\underline{{\mathbf{E}}}
represents an error tensor.

A common way to reduce the degrees of freedom in
\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}
and estimate unobserved edges is via low-rank tensor completion. For instance, canonical polyadic or block-term decompositions with very low rank
[
24
,
26
]
make recovery tractable but yield temporal adjacency matrices that are often low rank, which may not align with many real-world graph structures where, for a single time instant, multiple network effects and configurations are taking place jointly
[
37
]
. In contrast, we propose modeling each time-
t
adjacency matrix in

as a weighted sum of
R\!\ll\!T
latent deterministic topologies (i.e., adjacency matrices of size
N\times N
). These latent graphs represent static connections that account for hidden interactions among the nodes. Multiple latent graphs can operate concurrently if certain types of interactions persist over time, or they can switch on and off, as in switching dynamical models
[
42
,
21
]
. Altogether, these latent graphs collectively capture the observed network’s structural evolution, facilitate interpretability and, if eventually required, some (all) of them can be promoted to be low rank as well.

More specifically, let the
r
-th adjacency matrix be
{\mathbf{A}}_{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\!\times\!N}
and consider the vector
{\mathbf{c}}_{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T}
with
[{\mathbf{c}}_{r}]_{t}
denoting the scalar weight of
{\mathbf{A}}_{r}
at time

. We model the time-varying network as

\displaystyle\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}{\mathbf{A}}_{r}\diamond{\mathbf{c}}_{r}.

(1)

In words, we model the
N\!\times\!N\!\times\!T
adjacency tensor
\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}
as the sum of outer products

between the adjacency matrices
{{\mathbf{A}}_{r}}
and their corresponding temporal signature vectors
{{\mathbf{c}}_{r}}
. Clearly, if we focus on a particular time instant

, the adjacency matrix
\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}_{:,:,t}
is

\displaystyle\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}_{:,:,t}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}[{\mathbf{c}}_{r}]_{t}{\mathbf{A}}_{r}

(2)

i.e., a linear combination of the
R
latent adjacency matrices, each scaled by the corresponding elements in
{\mathbf{c}}_{r}
. Figure

[{\mathbf{A}}_{1},{\mathbf{A}}_{2},...]
and their temporal signatures
[{\mathbf{c}}_{1},{\mathbf{c}}_{2},...]
. Similar to low-rank decompositions, the adjacency matrices can also reveal important hidden connectivity factors and be used for subsequent downstream tasks such as temporal link prediction, graph reconstruction, or anomaly detection

. The elements of

are temporal functions that modulate the latent graphs.

Besides the topology, we often have access to time-varying signals at each node. To formalize this, let
\underline{{\mathbf{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times Q\times T}
be the signal tensor associated with the dynamic topology in
\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}
. The signal matrix at time

is
\underline{{\mathbf{X}}}_{:,:,t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\!\times\!Q}

Problem formulation.
Given the observation mask
\underline{{\mathbf{M}}}
, the observed evolution of the network topology
\underline{{\mathbf{M}}}\circ(\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}+\underline{{\mathbf{E}}})
and the spatiotemporal signals
\underline{{\mathbf{X}}}
, the goal is to recover the
R
adjacency matrices
{\mathbf{A}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{A}}_{R}
of the latent graphs along with their temporal signatures
{\mathbf{c}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{c}}_{R}
, respectively.

Let us define the matrices
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{C}}

{\mathbf{A}}=[{\mathbf{A}}_{1},...,{\mathbf{A}}_{R}],~{\mathbf{C}}=[{\mathbf{c}}_{1},...,{\mathbf{c}}_{R}].

(3)

which stack the matrices and their signatures horizontally.
We solve for these by addressing the following general optimization problem

\displaystyle\underset{\{{\mathbf{A}}_{r}\},\{{\mathbf{c}}_{r}\}}{\text{minimize}}~f({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{C}})~=\ell(\underline{{\mathbf{A}}},\underline{{\mathbf{M}}},{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{C}})\!\!+\!\!\sum_{r=1}^{R}\pi_{A}({\mathbf{A}}_{r})\!+\!\pi_{C}({\mathbf{C}})

\displaystyle~\hskip 76.82234pt+g(\underline{{\mathbf{X}}},{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{C}})+h({\mathbf{A}})

(4)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}~{\mathbf{A}}_{r}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{A},~{\mathbf{A}}=[{\mathbf{A}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{A}}_{R}],

\displaystyle~\hskip 39.83368pt{\mathbf{c}}_{r}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{C},~{\mathbf{C}}=[{\mathbf{c}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{c}}_{R}].

The function
\ell()
is the fitting term, measuring how well the latent adjacency matrices
{\mathbf{A}}_{r}
s and their temporal signatures
{\mathbf{c}}_{r}
s approximate the observed part of
\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}
, functions
\pi_{A}()
and
\pi_{C}()
impose structural priors on each of the latent adjacency matrices
{\mathbf{A}}_{r}
s and the temporal signatures in
{\mathbf{C}}
, respectively. The function
g()
accounts for the relationship between the signal tensor
\underline{{\mathbf{X}}}
and the latent adjacency matrices. The function
h()
is a prior on
{\mathbf{A}}
, or more specifically, the relationship across the different
{\mathbf{A}}_{r}
(e.g., penalizing latent matrices with a similar support). The sets
{\mathcal{S}}_{A}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{C}
impose strict structural constraints on the respective variables.

This formulation has a high number of degrees of freedom, with
N^{2}
for each latent graph and
T
for each temporal signature. This leads to a total of
N^{2}R+TR
degrees of freedom. To make the problem tractable, we need to use structural constraints as priors, which we will detail in Section
III
. Moreover, when the number of observations in
\underline{{\mathbf{M}}}\circ\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}
is smaller than
N^{2}R+TR
, the constraints and priors become even more relevant in the context of this under-determined problem.

III
Dynamic Graph Decomposition

Problem (
II
) is generic and can be tailored to diverse contexts. In this section, we refine it by specifying the fitting error loss function, along with the constraints on the latent adjacency matrices in
{\mathbf{A}}
and their temporal signatures in
{\mathbf{C}}
. We also define functions that promote the desired relationships among the latent adjacency matrices
{\mathbf{A}}
and between the signals
\underline{{\mathbf{X}}}
, the adjacency matrices
{\mathbf{A}}
, and the temporal signatures in
{\mathbf{C}}
.

Fitting error.
For the fitting function, we consider a least-squares (LS) cost

\ell(\underline{{\mathbf{A}}},\underline{{\mathbf{M}}},{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{C}})=||\underline{{\mathbf{M}}}\circ(\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}-\sum_{r=1}^{R}{\mathbf{A}}_{r}\diamond{\mathbf{c}}_{r})||_{F}^{2},

(5)

that measures how well the latent graphs predict observed topological evolution. The norm in (
5
) can be adjusted to incorporate prior information about the observation errors. For example, the
\ell_{1}
norm would be well-motivated if errors were sparse and independent across links.

Constraint sets.
We want to estimate latent graphs that are undirected, have positive weights and do not contain self-loops. As a result, we consider
{\mathcal{S}}_{A}=\{{\mathbf{S}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}:\,\text{tr}({\mathbf{S}})=0,\,{\mathbf{S}}={\mathbf{S}}^{\top},\,[{\mathbf{S}}]_{ij}\geq 0\,\text{for all}\,(ij),\}
, representing non-negative symmetric
N\times N
matrices with zero trace. For the temporal signatures, we consider
{\mathcal{S}}_{C}=\{{\mathbf{s}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T}:\,[{\mathbf{s}}]_{i}\geq 0\;\text{for all}\,i\}
. Positive weights in both
{\mathbf{A}}_{r}
and
{\mathbf{c}}_{r}
aid in interpretation, especially when it comes to comparing different time signatures. They also further restrict the degrees of freedom, rendering Problem (
II
) more tractable.

Sparsity of latent graphs.
We consider the latent graphs to be sparse. We impose sparsity on each adjacency matrix via
\pi_{A}({\mathbf{A}}_{r})=||{\mathbf{A}}_{r}||_{1}
. For
{\mathbf{A}}_{r}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{A}
, this means
\pi_{A}({\mathbf{A}}_{r})=\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}}_{r}\mathbf{1}_{N}
, where
\mathbf{1}_{N}
is the column vector of
N
ones.

Signal smoothness.
We assume the graph signals are smooth on the (unobserved) graph
[
43
,
6
]
. Signal smoothness, which implies that nodes connected by edges have similar values, has been commonly used as a prior for identifying topology and performing inference over graphs
[
37
]
. To be more specific, we assume the signals at time
t
,
{\mathbf{X}}_{t}
, to be smooth over the approximated topology at the same time, given by
\sum_{r=1}^{R}[{\mathbf{C}}]_{t,r}{\mathbf{A}}_{r}
. A graph signal is smooth if nodes that share edges have similar signal values. Consider the matrix
{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
with its
(ij)
-th element
[{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}]_{ij}=||[{\mathbf{X}}_{t}]_{i,:}-[{\mathbf{X}}_{t}]_{j,:}||_{2}^{2}
, measuring the squared
\ell_{2}
norm between the signals
[{\mathbf{X}}_{t}]_{i,:}
and
[{\mathbf{X}}_{t}]_{j,:}
at nodes
i
and
j
at time
t
. The smoothness of the graph signal
{\mathbf{X}}_{t}
over the recovered model-driven topology is given by the quadratic variation
\textsf{QV}({\mathbf{X}}_{t})=\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}(\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}_{:,:,t}{\mathbf{Z}}_{t})

[
6
]
. The signal-incorporating prior
g()
is the sum of the signal smoothness function for each time instant, and it is calculated as

\displaystyle\begin{split}g(\underline{{\mathbf{X}}},{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{C}})&=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}({\mathbf{A}}_{:,:,t}{\mathbf{Z}}_{t})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{r=1}^{R}[{\mathbf{C}}]_{t,r}\frac{\text{tr}({\mathbf{A}}_{r}{\mathbf{Z}}_{t})}{2}.\end{split}

(6)

This promotes the creation of new links from the signals in
\underline{{\mathbf{X}}}
that are smooth in the original temporal network when portions of
\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}
are not observed due to the mask. Finally, notice that we do not impose temporal smoothness. However, our model can accommodate it by a straightforward modification of the cost function in (
6
).

Non-overlapping support.
We desire that the supports of the different latent graphs do not overlap so that each component uniquely contributes to the structural evolution and provides distinct insights. One way to promote this non-overlapping support is to penalize the
{\mathbf{A}}_{r}
s that share many common edges. This is achieved by imposing a regularizer on the latent adjacency matrices through the function

\displaystyle h({\mathbf{A}})=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bar{r}=1\ \bar{r}\neq r\end{subarray}}^{R}\text{tr}({\mathbf{A}}_{r}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}}_{\bar{r}}),

(7)

which promotes orthogonality among the latent adjacency matrices. The lowest possible value of
h({\mathbf{A}})
is zero, which is achieved when no edge is shared among the adjacency matrices.

Avoiding trivial solutions.
The formulation in (
II
) can generate trivial solutions for some of the variables. More specifically, suppose we have a mask
\underline{{\mathbf{M}}}
that hides all the information across an edge for all
t
, i.e.,
[\underline{{\mathbf{M}}}_{:,:,t}]_{ij}=[\underline{{\mathbf{M}}}_{:,:,t}]_{ji}=0~\text{for all}~t
. This leads to a trivial solution for the corresponding variables because of the dependence only on the functions
g()
and
h()
. One way to tackle this is to force each node at every time instant to have a positive degree
\zeta>0
. This is expressed through the constraint

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{r=1}^{R}[{\mathbf{C}}]_{t,r}{\mathbf{A}}_{r}\Big)\mathbf{1}_{N}\geq\zeta\mathbf{1}_{N}.

(8)

We extend this constraint for all time instants as

\displaystyle{\mathbf{A}}[{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{N}]\geq\zeta\mathbf{1}_{N\times T},

(9)

where we recall that
{\mathbf{A}}=[{\mathbf{A}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{A}}_{R}]
. This implies that each node in the reconstructed topology at time
t
has a degree of at least
\zeta
.

Smooth temporal signatures.
We consider each temporal signature
{\mathbf{c}}_{r}
to change slowly over time. This helps prevent overfitting to the observed topology
\underline{{\mathbf{M}}}\circ\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}
, especially when fewer observations are present. A gradual change in the temporal factor leads to smaller changes in the latent adjacency matrices.
A gradual change in the temporal signature also helps in predicting an edge if it is not observed over an interval of time, given the true structural evolution is slow. We use the squared Frobenius norm for the matrix formed by the temporal difference of the elements of each
{\mathbf{c}}_{r}
, i.e,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\pi_{C}({\mathbf{C}})=||{\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{C}}||_{F}^{2}=&\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{t=2}^{T}([{\mathbf{C}}]_{t,r}-[{\mathbf{C}}]_{t-1,r})^{2}\\
\end{split}

(10)

where
{\mathbf{D}}
is the
T\!\times\!(T\!-\!1\!)
temporal difference matrix. Additionally, we consider an extra cost function for
{\mathbf{C}}
, namely the squared Frobenius norm. These functions are reasonable from a practical point of view and aid in the convergence analysis of the optimization as we shall elaborate in Section
IV
.

With all these in place, the optimization in (
II
) can now be written as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\underset{\{{\mathbf{A}}_{r}\},\{{\mathbf{c}}_{r}\}}{\text{minimize}}~~f({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{C}})\!=\!\frac{1}{2}\Big\|\underline{{\mathbf{M}}}\circ(\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}-\sum_{r=1}^{R}{\mathbf{A}}_{r}\diamond{\mathbf{c}}_{r})\Big\|_{F}^{2}\!\!\\
&+\gamma\!\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}}_{r}\mathbf{1}\!+\!\delta\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{r=1}^{R}[{\mathbf{C}}]_{t,r}\frac{\text{tr}({\mathbf{A}}_{r}{\mathbf{Z}}_{t})}{2}\!+\!\mu||{\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{C}}||_{F}^{2}\\
&+\!\!\beta\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{\bar{r}=1,\bar{r}\neq r}^{R}\text{tr}({\mathbf{A}}_{r}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}}_{\bar{r}})+\frac{\rho}{2}||{\mathbf{C}}||_{F}^{2}\\
&\text{subject to}\\
&{\mathbf{A}}_{r}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{A}=\{{\mathbf{A}}_{r}:{\mathbf{A}}_{r}\geq{\mathbf{0}}_{N\times N},\text{tr}({\mathbf{A}}_{r})=0,{\mathbf{A}}_{r}={\mathbf{A}}_{r}^{\top}\},\\
&{\mathbf{c}}_{r}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{C}=\{{\mathbf{c}}_{r}:{\mathbf{c}}_{r}\geq\mathbf{0}_{T}\},\\
&[{\mathbf{A}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{A}}_{R}][{\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{N}]\geq\zeta\mathbf{1}_{N\times T},\end{split}

(11)

where
[\gamma,\delta,\beta,\mu,\rho]^{\top}
denote the hyper-parameters.

Remark 1
.

Problem (
11
) is a particular instance of the more general Problem (
II
). The constraints and regularizers can be modified to model diverse scenarios. For example, low-rank latent factors can be promoted via the use of nuclear norms. Additionally, a switching dynamic network can be captured by penalizing the
\ell_{1}
norm of each column in
{\mathbf{C}}
(ensuring that most latent graphs remain inactive at each time instant), the operator
{\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{C}}
(preventing the latent graph from changing too frequently), or combinations of the two.

III-A

Solving the decomposition

Due to the multiple bilinear terms, Problem (
11
) is jointly non-convex in
\{{\mathbf{A}}_{1},...,{\mathbf{A}}_{R},{\mathbf{C}}\}
. However, it is separately convex in each variable when the others are fixed. This makes it easier to solve for one variable at a time via an alternating approach, i.e., we solve a sequence of convex problems in these variables until convergence. We do this by formulating
R+1
optimization problems, where each of the first
R
solves for a latent adjacency matrix
{\mathbf{A}}_{r}
, whereas the last solves for the temporal signatures in
{\mathbf{C}}
. We solve each of them via the alternating direction method of multipliers. Thus, we perform alternating minimization between
R+1
variables.

Updating the
{\mathbf{A}}_{r}
s

Here we outline the update for the
r
-th latent adjacency matrix
{\mathbf{A}}_{r}
s, keeping fixed the other matrices
\{{\mathbf{A}}_{r^{\prime}}\}_{r^{\prime}\neq r}
and the temporal signatures in
{\mathbf{C}}
. Consider the
N^{2}\times 1

{\mathbf{m}}_{t}
formed by vectorizing the mask at time
t

{\mathbf{M}}_{t}
, i.e.,
{\mathbf{m}}_{t}=\text{vec}({\mathbf{M}}_{t})
. Consider also the
N^{2}\times T

{\mathbf{M}}_{0}
that collects the

\mathbf{M_{0}}=[{\mathbf{m}}_{1},{\mathbf{m}}_{2},\ldots,{\mathbf{m}}_{T}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}\times T}.

Let us define the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{r}=\frac{1}{2}[{\mathbf{Z}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{Z}}_{T}]({\mathbf{c}}_{r}\otimes{\mathbf{I}}_{N})

{\mathbf{A}}_{r}
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&f({\mathbf{A}}_{r})=\frac{1}{2}||{\mathbf{A}}_{r}||_{F}^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}[{\mathbf{C}}]_{t,r}^{2}\mathbf{1}_{N^{2}}^{\top}{\mathbf{m}}_{t}\\
&+\text{tr}\bigg({\mathbf{A}}_{r}^{\top}\big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}[{\mathbf{C}}]_{t,r}\mathbf{1}_{N^{2}}^{\top}{\mathbf{m}}_{t}\sum_{\bar{r}=1,\bar{r}\neq r}^{R}[{\mathbf{C}}]_{t,\bar{r}}{\mathbf{A}}_{\bar{r}}\big]\bigg)\\
&-\text{tr}\big({\mathbf{A}}_{r}^{\top}\text{vec}^{-1}\big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}_{:,:,t}[{\mathbf{C}}]_{t,r}\mathbf{1}_{N^{2}}^{\top}{\mathbf{m}}_{t}\big)\big)+\gamma\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}{\mathbf{A}}_{r}\mathbf{1}_{N}\!\\
&+\!\delta\text{tr}({\mathbf{A}}_{r}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{r})+2\beta\text{vec}({\mathbf{A}}_{r})^{\top}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bar{r}=1,\bar{r}\neq r\end{subarray}}^{R}\!\!\text{vec}({\mathbf{A}}_{\bar{r}})\!\!\!.\end{split}

(13)

Note the term
\sum_{t=1}^{T}[{\mathbf{C}}]_{t,r}^{2}\mathbf{1}_{N^{2}}^{\top}{\mathbf{m}}_{t}
that scales the squared Frobenius norm of
{\mathbf{A}}_{r}
. It is the sum across time of the product between the corresponding squared temporal coefficient
[{\mathbf{C}}]_{t,r}
and the number of ones in the mask
\mathbf{1}^{\top}{\mathbf{m}}_{t}
, which is also the number of observations available in
\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}
at that time. Note also that the influence of the mask on updating the
r
-th adjacency matrix is affected by the corresponding temporal signature
{\mathbf{c}}_{r}
. Upon defining the matrices
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{r}={\mathbf{c}}_{r}^{\top}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{N}
and
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{r}=\sum_{\bar{r},\bar{r}\neq r}{\mathbf{A}}_{\bar{r}}({\mathbf{c}}_{\bar{r}}^{\top}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{N})-\zeta\mathbf{1}_{N\times T}
, the associated optimization can be compactly written as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\underset{{\mathbf{A}}_{r}}{\text{minimize}}~f({\mathbf{A}}_{r})\\
&\text{subject to}~{\mathbf{A}}_{r}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{r}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{r}\geq\mathbf{0}_{N\times T},\\
&{\mathbf{A}}_{r}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{A}=\{{\mathbf{S}}:{\mathbf{S}}\geq{\mathbf{0}}_{N\times N},\text{tr}({\mathbf{S}})=0,{\mathbf{S}}={\mathbf{S}}^{\top}\},\end{split}

(14)

where the inequality constraint is the same as in (
8
) but written in terms of
{\mathbf{A}}_{r}
. We solve Problem (
14
) via the Alternating Direction of Method of Multipliers (ADMM)
[
44
]
. The formulation is

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\underset{{\mathbf{A}}_{r},{\mathbf{P}}}{\text{minimize}}~f({\mathbf{A}}_{r})\\
&\text{subject to:}~{\mathbf{A}}_{r}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{A},~{\mathbf{A}}_{r}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{r}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{r}={\mathbf{P}},~{\mathbf{P}}\geq\mathbf{0}_{N\times T},\end{split}

(15)

where the auxiliary variable
{\mathbf{P}}
is introduced to cast the problem in the ADMM format. Then, the augmented Lagrangian of (
15
) is

\displaystyle\begin{split}L({\mathbf{A}}_{r},{\mathbf{P}},\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{r})&=f({\mathbf{A}}_{r})+\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{r}({\mathbf{A}}_{r}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{r}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{r}-{\mathbf{P}}))\\
&+\frac{\lambda}{2}||{\mathbf{A}}_{r}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{r}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{r}-{\mathbf{P}}||_{F}^{2},\end{split}

(16)

where
\lambda>0
. The updates in the primal variables
{\mathbf{A}}_{r}
at iteration
k
are given as

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathbf{A}}_{r}^{k}&={\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{A}}\left({\mathbf{A}}_{r}^{k-1}-\lambda\nabla_{{\mathbf{A}}_{r}}L({\mathbf{A}}_{r}^{k-1})\right),\end{split}

(17)

which is a gradient step followed by a projection onto the set
{\mathcal{S}}_{A}
.
The expression for the gradient
\nabla_{{\mathbf{A}}_{r}}L({\mathbf{A}}_{r})
is detailed in Appendix
C
[cf. (
A
)]. For the auxiliary variable
{\mathbf{P}}
, we note that the Lagrangian is convex w.r.t.
{\mathbf{P}}
and we can readily obtain a closed-form solution, followed by a projection onto the non-negative orthant. The remaining updates for the primal variable
{\mathbf{P}}
and the dual variables
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{r}
are thus

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}^{k}=[\frac{1}{\lambda}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{r}^{k-1\top}+{\mathbf{A}}_{r}^{k}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{r}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{r}]_{+}

(18)

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{r}^{k}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{r}^{k-1}+\lambda({\mathbf{A}}_{r}^{k}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{r}+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{r}-{\mathbf{P}}^{k})^{\top},

(19)

where
[\cdot]_{+}
is an element-wise operator that clips all negative values to zero. The two steps are repeated until a stopping criterion is met.

III-C

Updating
{\mathbf{C}}

In this step, we update
{\mathbf{C}}
keeping
\{{\mathbf{A}}_{r}\}_{r=1}^{R}
fixed. As we did with the adjacency matrices, we first write down the loss function w.r.t.
{\mathbf{C}}
. To do so, we define

{\mathbf{A}}_{\textnormal{vec}}=[\text{vec}(\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}_{:,:,1}),\text{vec}(\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}_{:,:,2}),\ldots,\text{vec}(\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}_{:,:,T})]\in\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}\times T}.

(20)

\mathbf{A_{0}}=[\text{vec}({\mathbf{A}}_{1}),\text{vec}({\mathbf{A}}_{2}),\ldots,\text{vec}({\mathbf{A}}_{R})]\in\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}\times R}.

(21)

The loss in
{\mathbf{C}}
then writes as

\displaystyle\begin{split}f({\mathbf{C}})&=\frac{1}{2}||{\mathbf{M}}_{0}\circ({\mathbf{A}}_{\textnormal{vec}}-{\mathbf{A}}_{0}{\mathbf{C}}^{\top})||_{F}^{2}\!+\!\delta\text{tr}({\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\bar{{\mathbf{Z}}}{\mathbf{A}}_{0})\\
&+\frac{\mu}{2}||{\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{C}}||_{F}^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}||{\mathbf{C}}||_{F}^{2},\\
\end{split}

(22)

where
\text{tr}({\mathbf{C}}^{\top}\bar{{\mathbf{Z}}}{\mathbf{A}}_{0})
is the signal smoothness prior (
6
) re-written to show the dependence on
{\mathbf{C}}
, and
\bar{{\mathbf{Z}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times N^{2}}
is a matrix with row
t
being the vectorized form of the matrix
{\mathbf{Z}}_{t}^{\top}
, i.e.,
[\bar{{\mathbf{Z}}}]_{:,t}=\text{vec}({\mathbf{Z}}_{t}^{\top})^{\top}
. The inequality constraint involving both
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{C}}
in Problem (
11
) translates into
{\mathbf{C}}\Upsilon^{\top}-\zeta\mathbf{1}_{T\times N}\geq\mathbf{0}_{T\times N}
where
\Upsilon=[{\mathbf{A}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{A}}_{R}]({\mathbf{I}}_{R}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{N})
. Thus, the optimization problem for
{\mathbf{C}}
can be compactly written as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\underset{{\mathbf{C}}}{\text{minimize}}\quad&f({\mathbf{C}})\\
\text{subject to: }&{\mathbf{C}}\Upsilon^{\top}-\zeta\mathbf{1}_{T\times N}\geq\mathbf{0}_{T\times N},\\
&{\mathbf{C}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{C}=\{{\mathbf{S}}:{\mathbf{S}}\geq\mathbf{0}_{T\times R}\}.\end{split}

(23)

To deal with the inequality constraint, we formulate another ADMM problem as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\underset{{\mathbf{C}}}{\text{minimize}}\quad&f({\mathbf{C}})\\
\text{subject to: }&{\mathbf{C}}\Upsilon^{\top}-\zeta\mathbf{1}_{T\times N}\!=\!{\mathbf{Q}},\\
&{\mathbf{C}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{C},\;{\mathbf{Q}}\geq\mathbf{0}_{T\times N}.\end{split}

(24)

Then, the augmented Lagrangian becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}L({\mathbf{C}},{\mathbf{Q}},\boldsymbol{\Lambda})=&f({\mathbf{C}})+\text{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}({\mathbf{C}}\Upsilon^{\top}-\zeta\mathbf{1}_{T\times N}-{\mathbf{Q}})\right)\\
&+\frac{\lambda_{c}}{2}||{\mathbf{C}}\Upsilon^{\top}-\zeta\mathbf{1}_{T\times N}-{\mathbf{Q}}||_{F}^{2}.\end{split}

(25)

To update
{\mathbf{C}}
, we take a gradient step followed by a projection onto
{\mathcal{S}}_{C}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathbf{C}}^{k}&={\mathcal{P}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{A}}\left({\mathbf{C}}^{k-1}-\lambda\nabla_{{\mathbf{C}}}L({\mathbf{C}}^{k-1})\right).\end{split}

(26)

The derivative of the augmented Lagrangian w.r.t.
{\mathbf{C}}
is detailed in Equation (
A
) in Appendix
C
. The other two updates are also similar to those made for each
{\mathbf{A}}_{r}
and are respectively

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Q}}^{k}=[\frac{1}{\lambda_{c}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{k-1\top}+({\mathbf{C}}^{k}\boldsymbol{\Upsilon})^{\top}-\zeta\mathbf{1}_{TN})\big]_{+},

(27)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{k}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{k-1}+\lambda_{c}({\mathbf{C}}^{k}\Upsilon^{\top}-\zeta\mathbf{1}_{TN}-{\mathbf{Q}}^{k})^{\top}.

(28)

IV
Complexity and Convergence Analysis

In this section, we analyze the proposed DGD algorithm first in terms of its computational complexity and then in terms of the convergence behavior of the alternating approach.

Parameter complexity.
The parameters to be estimated are
\left(\frac{N-1}{2}+2T\right)NR+(R+2N)T
. These come from two sources: (i)
\left(\frac{N-1}{2}+2T\right)NR
parameters associated with the
R
matrices
{\mathbf{A}}_{r}
, which are symmetric with zero diagonals, and the remaining parameters are related to the additional variables
{\mathbf{P}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{r}
; and (ii)
(R+2N)T
parameters of the
R
temporal signatures
{\mathbf{C}}
and the additional variables
{\mathbf{Q}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
.

The number of parameters of the original problem [cf.(
II
)] is
N^{2}T+TR

Computational complexity.
Recalling that
I
is the number of alternating iterations, the computational complexity of DGD is of order
{\mathcal{O}}((N^{3}R+N^{2}TR+R^{2}N^{2}+T^{2}R+NTR)I)
. For each iteration
i=1,...,I
, the computational complexity is broken down as follows. The computations for estimating
{\mathbf{A}}_{r}
are governed by the gradient
\nabla_{{\mathbf{A}}_{r}}L({\mathbf{A}}_{r})
in (
17
) and the updates of the auxiliary variables
{\mathbf{P}}
in (
18
) and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{r}
in (
19
). We update the
R
matrices
{\mathbf{A}}_{r}
,
{\mathbf{P}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{r}
, leading to a total computational cost of
{\mathcal{O}}((N^{3}+N^{2}T)R)
. In addition, computing
{\mathbf{C}}
has a cost of order
{\mathcal{O}}((R^{2}T^{2}+T^{2}R))
, and each update of the auxiliary variables
{\mathbf{Q}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
has a complexity of order
{\mathcal{O}}(NTR)
.

Remark 2
.

While the updates for
{\mathbf{A}}_{r}
and
{\mathbf{C}}
in (
17
) and (
26
) are carried out via projected gradient descent, one could alternatively derive a closed-form solution from the unconstrained problem and then apply a projection. However, this closed-form solution involves an inverse with complexity of order
{\mathcal{O}}(N^{3})
for each
{\mathbf{A}}_{r}
per ADMM iteration and
{\mathcal{O}}(R^{3}T^{3})
for each
{\mathbf{C}}
update since vectorization is required to obtain the optimal value. Moreover, projecting onto
{\mathcal{S}}_{A}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{C}
afterward may introduce large adjustments, potentially affecting the convergence of Algorithm
1
. By contrast, a gradient step before projection is computationally less demanding, enforces more gradual change (due to the convex nature of the functions), and works effectively in practice.

Comparison

We assess the effectiveness of our approach in reconstructing the unobserved temporal network by comparing its recovery performance with that of other alternatives in various scenarios. Specifically, we compare DGD with the following approaches:

1.

Unconstrained solution (UNC).
This is the unconstrained solution to Problem (
11
), where we drop both the graph and temporal smoothness constraints on the latent adjacency matrices
{\mathbf{A}}_{0}
and the temporal signatures
{\mathbf{C}}

2.

Canonical polyadic decomposition (CPD).
The CPD

3.

The BTD approximates

as a sum of outer products of matrices

No signal dynamic graph decomposition (NSDGD).
This is the proposed approach without incorporating the graph signals, i.e.,
\delta=0

Smooth graph learning (SGL).
For this baseline, we use the observed links, the observed graph signals and the smoothness prior to estimate the topology. Specifically, for each

[\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}]_{:,:,t}

This approach, presented in

For a fair comparison between the different approaches, we ensure that CPD, BTD, NSDGD, and SICA have a similar number of degrees of freedom as DGD. This results in a higher number of terms in the CPD and BTD approaches, which may further complicate the ability to interpret the decomposition.

The results indicate that UNC tends to overfit the observed part of the network and fails to reconstruct the unobserved portions of the temporal network. DGD performs better than the low-rank tensor decomposition-based approaches (CPD and BTD) for all datasets for all percentages of observed topology. The gap in performance varies, from high (USTemp) to low (SwDyn). This corroborates our hypothesis that low-rank tensor decompositions are not very informative or representative of the structural evolution. BTD yields slightly better
RE
scores than CPD. Regarding the results associated with the SICA approach, we observe a slightly improved performance in terms of error compared to low-rank tensor decomposition methods. A notable outcome, as evidenced by the results, is that the SICA approach yields higher errors compared to NSDGD and DGS when the percentage of available data is large, while the opposite trend is observed with a smaller percentage of available data. This effect stems from the assumption in the SICA approach of estimating independent components, which may be overly restrictive for capturing the structure of the network when a substantial amount of information is available but becomes beneficial when data availability is limited.

The DGD and NSDGD demonstrate superior performance compared to other methods. These approaches employ a more sophisticated temporal network structure, by incorporating the latent adjacency matrices and temporal signatures. Notably, DGD achieves the best performance, primarily due to its utilization of additional node signals. This additional information enhances the reconstruction accuracy, particularly in scenarios where a significant portion of the temporal network is unobserved. The gap between DGD and NSDGD narrows as we observe more of the structural evolution, showcasing the need to use the signals as a prior with limited observations. However, we note that SGL outperforms all approaches for SwDyn data when we observe
10
to
40
percent of
\underline{{\mathbf{A}}}
, i.e., just using the signals with signal smoothness prior recovers more of the missing structural evolution. This makes sense, as we generate smooth signals from the topology. This also suggests that a low percentage of observations hinders the recovery of hidden observations when combined with signals. For the other datasets, SGL struggles to recover the structural evolution.

In summary, the proposed approach shows advantages over existing methods by leveraging additional node signal information and employing it to improve the reconstruction error of the temporal network in scenarios with a large percentage of unobserved part of the temporal network.

VI
Conclusion

We introduced a new approach to represent and estimate dynamic networks by modeling their structural evolution as weighted combinations of latent graphs, along with temporal signatures that modulate their importance over time. A key feature of our method is the integration of network observations with partial observations of spatiotemporal signals, assumed to be smooth on the dynamic network. By combining these data sources, we formulated an optimization problem whose solution yields the estimated latent graphs, temporal signatures, and dynamic network. We solved this problem via an alternating minimization scheme and showed that it converges to a block coordinate minimizer. Empirically, each recovered latent graph provides meaningful insights, contributes to representing the overall dynamics, and helps recover missing parts of the network. Numerical results further demonstrated that our method outperforms topology-agnostic low-rank tensor decompositions and approaches relying solely on graph signal smoothness. Future research includes developing an online extension of the decomposition, where the entire sequence of topologies is not available at once and must be updated over time. Another direction is to extend the framework to expanding graphs, accommodating the arrival of new nodes as the graph evolves.
