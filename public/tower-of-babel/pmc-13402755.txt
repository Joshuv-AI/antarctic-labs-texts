Placental dysfunction reflects endocrine impairment and trophoblast alterations in polycystic ovarian syndrome
Authors: Ananya Mahapatra, Gautami Pillai, Remi Rana, Zeel Shah, Laxmipriya Nampoothiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common endocrine disorder that contributes to pregnancy complications like Intra Uterine Growth Restriction (IUGR), leading to compromised foetal outcome. Although maternal metabolic and hormonal imbalances are well-established in PCOS, the specific molecular alterations within the placenta and its outcome remains poorly explored. This study aimed to characterize key molecular signaling alterations in PCOS placentae with respect to steroid hormone receptors, trophoblast lineage specification, along with their structural alterations. To understand the above alterations, PCOS rodent mothers were developed using letrozole treatment for 21days daily orally, following which induction of pregnancy and those pregnant animals were sacrificed at GD18. Tissues were subjected to expression levels of steroidal and placental cell markers using transcriptomic and protein expression, along with morphometric and histological analysis, correlated with hormone profile. Histological analysis of GD 18 PCOS placenta exhibited a reduction in labyrinth zone, with an increased AR expression, along with downregulation of PR, ER ɑ and ER β, indicating an altered steroidal status. Moreover, dysregulation of genes such as Phlda2, Tpbpa, Pcdh12, Prl3b1, CDX2, GCM1, and GATA2 along with reduced expression of SynA, Syn B were observed suggesting an impaired trophoblast differentiation, vascular development, and immune tolerance. Additionally, elevated expressions of Flt4, H2Kd and IFN gamma suggested compensatory mechanisms attempting to offset placental dysfunction. This study clearly indicates maternal PCOS pathophysiology effects placental development by altering the morphology, along with abnormal hormonal homeostasis, contributing to impaired differentiation. Introduction

Polycystic Ovarian syndrome (PCOS) is a prevalent endocrine disorder affecting women of reproductive age, characterized by chronic anovulation, hyperandrogenism along with polycystic ovarian morphology1. It is one of the most common causes of infertility, with an estimated prevalence of 5–10% among women of reproductive age2. While extensive research has focused on the reproductive aspects of hyperandrogenism induced polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS), its impact on pregnancy outcomes, particularly placental function, has received limited attention. The placenta plays a central role in supporting fetal growth and maternal health, and alterations in its structure and function can contribute to pregnancy complications such as preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, and fetal growth restriction3. Women with PCOS are at a significantly higher risk of developing gestational diabetes and preeclampsia4 and in addition to a hostile maternal uterine environment, impaired placental development further contributes to adverse pregnancy outcomes, including fetal growth restriction and preeclampsia5. Placenta is a highly specialized organ essential for foetal development. It facilitates the exchange of gases, nutrients, and waste products between the mother and foetus, thereby ensuring optimal foetal growth and metabolic homeostasis6. Additionally, the placenta produces steroidal hormones such as androgen, estrogens and progesterone that regulate several processes like cellular differentiation, maternal immune tolerance, and maintenance of pregnancy. Disruption in placental structure- function or hormone dysregulation7 could significantly impair biochemical or molecular processes, leading to severe pregnancy complications such as Gestational diabetes, Intra Uterine Growth Restrictions, Preeclampsia. Furthermore, foetal growth restriction (FGR) is often a consequence of placental insufficiency, where insufficient placental nutrient transfer compromises foetal development, increasing the risk of preterm birth, low birth weight, and long-term health consequences for the child5. Such pathological alterations in placental function highlight the organ’s pivotal role in maintaining a healthy pregnancy, with its dysfunction contributing to a spectrum of adverse maternal and fetal outcomes.

Recent studies have indicated that placental dysfunction in PCOS may be linked to impaired trophoblast invasion, abnormal vascular remodeling, and altered cytokine profiles, which can compromise placental efficiency and lead to adverse pregnancy outcomes8,9. Moreover, placental endocrine and metabolic changes in response to altered maternal hormone levels in PCOS may disrupt key signalling pathways that regulate placental growth and function7. Androgen receptors in the placenta have been shown to be upregulated in GDM pregnancies, suggesting a potential link between elevated androgen levels and placental dysfunction10. This upregulation may contribute to increased testosterone and leptin levels, which are associated with placental abnormalities and complications in GDM pregnancies11. Estrogen receptors (ERs), particularly ERα and ERβ, are expressed in the placenta and are involved in angiogenesis and placental vascularization12. Their expression increases with gestational age, suggesting a role in maintaining pregnancy13,14. Progesterone receptors (PRs) are critical for implantation and decidualization, acting through complex paracrine signalling networks5,15. These steroidogenic effectors regulate gene expression necessary for uterine and placental development11,16,17. The balance and interaction between these receptors are essential for normal placental function and the prevention of pregnancy-related disorders. Steroid hormones such as progesterone and androgens play key modulators of trophoblast proliferation, differentiation, and invasion through their interactions with nuclear receptors, and imbalances in placental steroid signaling have been associated with impaired endocrine homeostasis and trophoblast dysfunction in complicated pregnancies18,19. Molecular markers of trophoblast lineage specification, including CDX2, GCM1 in syncytiotrophoblast formation is regulated by PR and AR20,16. ASCL2 in extravillous trophoblast differentiation, and Tpbpa in junctional zone trophoblasts regulated upstream via ER isoforms17,21. Placental steroids majorly PR and ER play a critical role in regulating placental immune environment via regulating vascular development22. These studies confirm that steroid hormones act as key regulators of placental cell fate and differentiation, thereby directly influencing placental structure and function. Understanding the interplay between placental steroid signaling and cell type–specific markers is therefore critical for understanding the mechanisms underlying placental dysfunction and adverse pregnancy outcomes in endocrine disorders such as PCOS.

The objective of this study is to investigate the placental dynamics in a PCOS pregnant rodent model, focusing on structural and functional alterations in the placenta. By using histological and molecular techniques, we aim to explore the reciprocity between the steroidal hormones and the key placental markers at term pregnancy of PCOS mothers. Furthermore, this research will seek to elucidate the underlying mechanisms by which PCOS affects placental development and function and to identify potential biomarkers that could be used to predict adverse pregnancy outcomes in women with PCOS.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Letrozole tablets-2.5 mg, marketed under the brand name Letronat were procured from Natco Pharma Ltd. Ethanol was procured from HiMedia Laboratories Pvt. Ltd. All other reagents of analytical grade were purchased from Sisco Research Laboratories Pvt. Ltd., India. Hormones- testosterone, estradiol, and progesterone were assayed using ELISA kits (Krishgen Biosystems, India). RNAiso Plus was procured from Takara Inc. High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit was procured from TAKARA. SYBR Green (TB Green master mix, TAKARA). Primers used in the study were designed by the primer blast tool of NCBI and synthesized by Europhins.

Animal housing and maintenance

All animal experimental procedures followed the guidelines of the Committee for Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CCSEA), India. Animals procured from registered breeder SyncBio Research Private Limited, Ahmedabad, India and were housed and inbred in a standard controlled Animal Care Facility of Department of Biochemistry, Faculty of Science, The Maharaja Sayajirao University of Baroda, Vadodara, Gujarat, India (CCSEA Registration No. and Date − 938/GO/ReBi/S/06/CCSEA and 26/10/2021) in cages (four mice/cage), and maintained in a temperature-controlled room (22–25 °C, 45% humidity) on a 12: 12-hour dark-light cycle with free access to food and water. Thirty adult virgin 2 to 3 months female Balb/c mice weighing 20–25 g were chosen for the study. The animals were maintained under standard nutritional and environmental conditions throughout the experiment. All the experiments were carried out between 9:00 and 16:00 h, at ambient temperature. Experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethical Committee (IAEC), Department of Biochemistry, The M. S. University of Baroda, Vadodara (Ethical Approval Number - MSU/BIOCHEMISTRY/IAEC/2024/1). Also, experiments were performed in compliance with the ethical standards of the Committee for the Purpose of Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CCSEA), India. Parameters like time required for conception (in days), mating index, fertility index, placental weights, mated females, and pregnant/non-pregnant females were examined for each animal. The mating and fertility index was calculated using the following formula23: Mating index (%) = (No. of females mated /No. of females placed with males) * 100, Fertility index (%) = (No. of Pregnant females / No. of females with confirmed mating) * 100.

Drug administration and experimental design

The animals were categorized into two groups-: Group I (Control group n = 20) received 1% Carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) orally daily for 21 days and served as untreated Control while Group II (PCOS group n = 20) received letrozole (0.5 mg/kg body weight) daily for 21 days and served as PCOS24. After 21 days of the treatment, PCOS validation parameters bodyweight, estrus cycle profile, hormone and cytokine analysis, and ovarian histology were analyzed. Further, female mice from both groups were allowed to mate with age matched male mice of the same strain (2:1). The following morning, females were checked for the presence of a vaginal plug. The appearance of vaginal plug was considered day 1 of pregnancy. On day 18 (at term of the gestation period), animals were sacrificed by cervical dislocation as mentioned in Annexure 6 of Committee for Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CCSEA) guidelines. Blood samples were collected by cardiac puncture. The serum was separated by centrifugation at 1000 g for 10 min and kept in a freezer at − 80 °C for further hormonal analysis. The placenta was excised, trimmed, and appropriate parts were separated for tissue histology, hormonal analysis, RNA isolation, and protein assays.

Hormone estimation

Serum from blood was used as a sample to estimate the testosterone, estradiol, estriol, Insulin and progesterone levels using ELISA kits (Krishgen Biosystems, Mumbai, India). All measurements were taken according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each sample was assayed in triplicates. The sensitivity of the kits was 0.022 ng/mL, 10 pg/mL, 0.47ng/ml, 0.1 ng/mL, 2.65pg/ml and 5 pg/ml for Testosterone, Estradiol, Estriol, Progesterone, Insulin and Interleukin 6 kits respectively. The working range was 0.08 to 16.7 ng/mL, 20 to 3200 pg/mL, 1.23 to 100 ng/ml, 0.3 to 60 ng/mL, 6.17 to 500 pg/ml and 6.25 pg/ml to 400 pg/ml of Testosterone, Estradiol, Estriol, Progesterone, Insulin and Interlukin 6 respectively. The intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) was 8.1 to 9.6%, 5 to 9.3%, 8.2 to 10%, 9.8 to 10.6%, 10.5 to 12% and 9% to 10% for Testosterone, Estradiol, Estriol, Progesterone, Insulin and Interlukin 6 kits respectively. The inter-assay coefficient of variation (CV) was 6.1 to 7.3%, 6.2 to 10.1%, 10 to 12%, 10.2 to 12.6%, 8% to 10%, 10% to 12% for Testosterone, Estradiol, Estriol, Progesterone, Insulin and Interlukin 6 kits.

Oral glucose tolerance test profile

For the Oral Glucose Tolerance Test, animals from the control and treated group were kept 12 h fasting. At 0 min (Fasting) blood glucose was estimated via tail snipping method using Glucometer. 2 g/kg body weight glucose solution was orally administered to each animal and blood glucose was observed in 30 min, 60 min, 90 min, 120 min intervals respectively. Plot the graph by using the above data X-axis =time (minutes); Y-axis = plasma glucose (mg/dL).

Histological examination of the placenta

The whole placenta was excised, trimmed, and washed with 1X PBS and fixed in 10% buffered formalin, processed, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 5-µm-thick sections. The transversed sections were stained with Haematoxylin and Eosin and assessed microscopically. In the ovary, Graafian follicles, peripheral cystic follicles, and corpus luteum were identified. In the placental section, distinctive three zones -decidual, junctional and labyrinth respectively were being identified. Observations and documentation were made on a DM2500 microscope (Leica, Germany) with Leica EZ digital camera under 4X magnification and Nikon Ti2E microscope under 10X magnification. All histological images were analyzed by Image J 1.54 g version software.

Gene expression analysis

Relative quantification of gene expression was carried out using real-time PCR (Applied Biosystems Quant Studio 5) as per previous reported protocol (24). (Primer details in Supplementary File 3)

Protein analysis

Placental tissue samples were homogenized (10% homogenate) in the RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Homogenization was performed on ice using a mechanical tissue homogenizer. The supernatant was collected, and protein concentration was determined using the Lowry protein analysis. Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) on a 10–12% resolving gel, depending on target protein size. Proteins were then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore) using a semi-dry or wet transfer system (Bio-Rad) at 100 V for 90 min. Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat dry milk or 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBS-T) for 1 h at room temperature to reduce non-specific binding. After blocking, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies -Androgen Receptor (D6F11) XP Rabbit mAb#5153, Estrogen Receptor alpha (D8H8) rabbit mAb#8644 from Cell Signalling Technologies (1:2000). After washing with TBS-T, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies, anti-rabbit IgG by Genei (1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. Following further washes, protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Pierce ECL Western Blotting Substrate, Thermo Fisher) and imaged using a chemiluminescence imaging system (e.g., ChemiDoc™, Bio-Rad). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH). Target protein expression was normalized to housekeeping proteins such as β-actin from Cell Signalling Technology #4967. Data were expressed as relative protein expression.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1). Comparisons between control and PCOS groups were conducted using an unpaired (independent-samples) t-test. Data were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variance using Levene’s test prior to analysis. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that maternal hyperandrogenism in a PCOS-like mouse model disrupts placental structure, steroid hormone signaling, and trophoblast lineage differentiation, providing mechanistic insight into impaired fertility and adverse gestational outcomes associated with PCOS. Consistent with previous characterizations of rodent PCOS models, we observed disrupted estrous cyclicity, impaired glucose homeostasis, elevated circulating testosterone, and peripheral ovarian cysts (11, 24). These endocrine and reproductive alterations corresponded with prolonged time to conception, reduced mating and fertility indices, and decreased litter size, collectively indicating compromised reproductive capacity and increased susceptibility to pregnancy complications.

Histopathological assessment revealed disrupted placental zonation and reduced vascular density in PCOS placentae, consistent with impaired placental architecture and function5,25,26. Maternal hyperandrogenism induced widespread placental epigenetic remodeling. Altered DNA methylation and gene expression profiles implicated extracellular matrix pathways—particularly increased collagen deposition—and dysregulated fatty acid metabolism Acsl1 suggesting a shift toward a fibrotic, metabolically stressed placental state that may limit trophoblast motility, vascular plasticity, and nutrient handling27,25,28. These molecular alterations provide a plausible link between steroid dysregulation and the structural abnormalities we observed. Although excessive androgen signaling is generally detrimental, AR also participates in context-dependent regulatory complexes that modulate angiogenesis. In trophoblasts, AR can partner with histone demethylases (KDM1A/KDM4D) to drive VEGFA expression, and prenatal androgenization in sheep increases placental AR and VEGFA expression alongside altered placental morphology, highlighting a conserved AR–angiogenic axis10,26. However, in pathological hyperandrogenic states this signaling appears dysregulated, contributing to aberrant vessel patterning rather than functional angiogenesis. Clinically, conditions such as preeclampsia exhibit two- to threefold higher maternal testosterone levels and increased placental AR expression29; animal models that mimic this androgen rise reproduce maternal hypertension, poor spiral artery remodeling, placental hypoxia, reduced nutrient transport, and fetal growth restriction30. Similarly, GDM placentae overexpresses AR and show reduced aromatase, suggesting impaired conversion of androgens to estrogens and a shift toward high androgenic levels, while hyperandrogenic/PCOS models fed a high-fat diet demonstrate worsened placental inflammation and growth restriction31. Furthermore, human placental studies reveal expression of multiple AR variants, and reduced nuclear AR45—an isoform associated with angiogenesis and considered relatively protective—has been linked to impaired placental vasculature and smaller male fetuses31. This dysregulation of androgen receptor expression indicates a wider disturbance in another crucial placental steroid hormone such as progesterone signaling, essential for pregnancy establishment and maintenance, was also perturbed. Classical nuclear PRs (PR-A/PR-B) regulate trophoblast differentiation, decidualization, and immune tolerance, and alterations in PR isoform ratios have been associated with abnormal placentation32,33,21. Non-genomic progesterone signaling via membrane receptors (PGRMC1/2 and mPRs) plays a complementary role in promoting trophoblast invasion and immune tolerance via HLA-C, HLA-G (mouse counterpart H2Kd), and ELF3 induction; disruption of this axis—either through receptor downregulation or antagonism—compromises these protective pathways (36, 37, 38). Transcription factors including FOXO1, KLF9, RUNX1, and RARA control PR expression and hormone-responsive gene sets, integrating progesterone signaling into trophoblast differentiation and parturition risk. Progesterone’s systemic immunomodulatory role is further underscored by its requirement for expansion and function of FOXP3⁺ regulatory T cells, with PR loss in Tregs leading to fetal loss and decidual CD8⁺ activation, and synthetic progestin treatment capable of restoring tolerance34–36.In our model, reduced expression of placental progesterone receptors (PR) despite unaltered maternal progesterone indicates localized progesterone resistance along with reduced FOXO1 expression and IGFBP1 shows disruption of the PR–FOXO1 axis is a well-established feature of progesterone resistance37–39. Consistent with this, reduced induction of canonical progesterone-responsive genes such as IGFBP1 is widely used as a molecular readout of impaired progesterone signaling37,41,42, (Supplementary File 6). These molecular alterations are further supported by our histological findings, where reduction in the labyrinth zone in PCOS placenta given that proper labyrinth development depends on coordinated trophoblast invasion and vascular remodeling processes regulated by progesterone-dependent pathways, this structural defect provides functional morphological evidence of impaired PR signalling. Estrogen signaling, which complements progesterone action by regulating uteroplacental blood flow, angiogenesis, and spiral artery remodeling, was also altered transcriptionally in PCOS mice placenta. Estrogens act via nuclear receptors and membrane GPER to promote endothelial tube formation and trophoblast invasion through PI3K–Akt–eNOS and YAP–ANGPTL4 pathways43,44. Studies have demonstrated that ERα is predominantly localized in cytotrophoblasts, whereas ERβ is more abundantly expressed in syncytiotrophoblast, where it plays a critical role in trophoblast differentiation and Syntilization45. This differential regulation is biologically significant when considered in the context of cell-type-specific localization and function of estrogen receptors in the placenta. From our study, it has been observed that significant downregulation of ERβ, while ERα expression remained unaltered in the PCOS placenta. Thus, selective downregulation of ERβ may specifically impair syncytiotrophoblast formation and function, consistent with our histological findings of a distorted syncytiotrophoblast layer and reduced expression of syncytialization markers (SynA, SynB and Gcm1). Importantly, similar patterns of dysregulated estrogen receptor signaling have been reported in pathological pregnancies such as preeclampsia where altered ERα/ERβ balance at the maternal–fetal interface has been associated with disrupted steroid hormone signaling46. Clinically, reduced circulating estrogen levels have been linked with placental insufficiency in both preeclampsia and intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), conditions characterized by impaired trophoblast function and vascular development47,48. Furthermore, physiological studies indicate that both ERα and ERβ expression dynamically change during gestation, reflecting adaptive responses to fluctuating estrogen levels49. Genetic disruption of membrane ERα signaling results in defective spiral artery–associated trophoblast giant cell expansion, imbalanced angiogenic factor expression, and embryonic loss, while mass spectrometry studies show that preeclampsia is associated with reduced estradiol and 2-methoxyestradiol50. Altered estrogen-related receptors (ERRγ, ERRβ) link hypoxia and microRNA networks to defective trophoblast turnover in growth-restricted and preeclamptic placentas51. In hyperandrogenic/PCOS placentae, reduced ERβ expression in the absence of significant ERα changes may shift estrogen signaling toward a proliferative, pro-inflammatory state, further impairing trophoblast differentiation and vascular development. Genome-scale regulatory models identify estradiol and dihydrotestosterone as upstream regulators of trophoblast programs, highlighting ER- and AR-driven modulation of cytokine, migration, and invasion pathways52,22. Hyperandrogenic condition in PCOS also gives rise to insulin resistance–driven hyperinsulinemia, a key pathogenic feature of the syndrome53 In the reproductive context, hyperinsulinemia disrupts progesterone-mediated decidualization via altered signaling pathways, leading to impaired stromal differentiation and compromised trophoblast invasion in gestational diabetes milletus like pathophysiology54. Moreover, in hyper insulinemic states, insulin acts synergistically with androgens to induce progesterone resistance, make a steroidogenic shift towards androgens altering progesterone as well as estrogen mediated signalling, thereby contributing to defective implantation and placental dysfunction55.

Steroid hormone signaling converges on core trophoblast transcriptional programs governed by trophoblastic -enhancer–associated factors. CDX2 is widely regarded as a core transcription factor that establishes and maintains trophoblast stem (TS/TSC) identity, while repressing pluripotency programs and promoting trophectoderm fate in early development. Loss of Cdx2 in embryos blocks proper TE specification56. Overexpression of Cdx2 in embryonic stem cells is sufficient to drive trophoblast-like differentiation and TS-like cell formation, establishing Cdx2 as a lineage-defining factor20. Evidence from androgen-excess models supports a direct, androgen receptor (AR)-mediated effect on trophoblast lineage regulators. Continuous DHT exposure has been shown to reduce trophoblast precursor populations and significantly downregulate Cdx2, with its expression correlating with labyrinth trophoblast progenitor abundance57. This is accompanied by reduced Gcm1 and syncytiotrophoblast markers, along with structural defects in the labyrinth layer. Importantly, these abnormalities are rescued by AR antagonism (flutamide), indicating that the effects are AR-dependent rather than secondary consequences. Consistent with this, androgen exposure in human trophoblast organoid models similarly impairs trophoblast differentiation and syncytiotrophoblast formation, supporting a conserved mechanism57.

At the molecular level, our study has also shown that sustained maternal androgen exposure in PCOS condition has exerted direct effects on trophoblast stem and progenitor populations, reflected in reduced expression of Cdx2, indicating disruption in trophoblast stem cell identity thereby hindering trophoblast differentiation. Our findings of reduced Cdx2, Gata2, and Gcm1 expression along with lower expression of Syn A and Syn B, together with a reduced labyrinth zone, indeed suggest impaired trophoblast lineage specification and syncytiotrophoblast differentiation in the PCOS placenta58. Our study positively indicates the effect of hyperandrogenism in PCOS phenotype must play a role in disrupting trophoblast lineage-defining transcriptional regulators via AR signalling, leading to defective establishment of the labyrinth. Other key differentiation genes including GCM1, Prl3b1, ASCL2, and TPBPA. GCM1 serves as a master regulator of syncytiotrophoblast and EVT differentiation via ASCL259–62. Prl3b1 as part of the broader uteroplacental prolactin family involved in hormone-dependent reproductive tissue growth. PRL3B1 (also called placental lactogen II, PL-II) is a mouse placenta-specific prolactin-related hormone. It acts mainly on maternal tissues but is tightly linked to normal late gestation, when fetal growth is maximal63. Functioning as a luteotropic, prolactin-receptor agonist important for progesterone support and pregnancy maintenance63. Overexpression of the placental homeoprotein EGAM1C in trophoblast stem cells upregulates PRL3B1, suggesting PRL3B1 is part of a regulated endocrine program of differentiated trophoblast lineages64. Reduced transcriptional levels of GCM1, Prl3b1 in PCOS placentas suggest defective regulation of the STB–EVT transition, with potential consequences for placental function during late gestation. Tpbpa serves as a definitive marker of spongiotrophoblast and glycogen trophoblast lineages and their progenitors, and its expression delineates the junctional zone critical for trophoblast differentiation and maternal spiral artery remodeling65. Deletion of TFAP2C in TPBPA+ progenitors at E8.5 results in growth arrest of the junctional zone66,67. Our study had shown increased expression of TPBA in PCOS placenta indicating extended trophoblast population towards the labyrinth zone and unregulated invasion linking to placenta; structural imbalance. In addition to differentiation- and growth-promoting factors, a range of regulatory mechanisms operate to control placental growth and function. Imprinted growth-restrictive genes such as Phlda2 showed placental growth and nutrient allocation through interactions with CDX2 and ELF5 networks68,69. Reduced Phlda2 expression in PCOS mouse placentas implies dysregulation of growth-restrictive pathways that normally constrain placental growth, with consequent functional alterations. However, direct regulatory links with AR, PR, ER and core trophoblast factors remain incompletely defined and needs future investigation. These transcriptional alterations compromised syncytiotrophoblast formation and extravillous trophoblast differentiation, culminate into a hypoplastic labyrinth with reduced exchange capacity which might be the preliminary cause for the altered fetal outcome observed in PCOS pregnancy. Such defects are in line with studies showing that continuous androgen exposure suppresses trophoblast precursor proliferation and differentiation and that AR blockade with flutamide prevents these abnormalities, establishing AR signaling as a causal driver of trophoblast lineage disruption70,71.

In the context of PCOS, characterized by a hyperandrogenic and pro-inflammatory milieu, we interpret the elevated IFN-γ expression as a maladaptive and compensatory response. Under physiological conditions, IFN-γ derived from uterine NK cells supports spiral artery remodeling, decidual maintenance, and early angiogenesis (Fig. 6)72,73. However, sustained, or excessive IFN-γ signaling is associated with impaired trophoblast invasion, increased apoptosis, and preeclampsia-like pathology, indicating its context-dependent detrimental effects74,75. While initial upregulation may reflect an attempt to support vascular remodeling and immune homeostasis, its persistence likely contributes to trophoblast dysfunction and defective placentation. Similarly, increased Flt4 (VEGFR3) expression is interpreted within the of angiogenic imbalance. VEGF–VEGFR signaling plays a central role in placental vascular development34 and VEGFR3 contributes to vascular patterning76. Thus, its upregulation may represent an attempt to compensate for impaired placental vascularization, although this response is unlikely to fully restore normal angiogenic function. Along with angiogenic markers, cytokine profile also indicates a distinct immune imbalance at the maternal–fetal interface in PCOS, characterized by reduced placental Interleukin-6 and Interleukin-10 expression alongside elevated Interleukin-1 beta, in the presence of increased maternal circulating IL-6. This pattern suggests a compartmentalized inflammatory response, wherein localized placental inflammation—driven by IL-1β and diminished anti-inflammatory regulation (IL-10)—coexists with systemic maternal inflammation reflected by elevated serum IL-6. In contrast to classical inflammatory pregnancy complications such as preeclampsia and idiopathic preterm birth, where both maternal and placental IL-6 are typically elevated with concomitant reduction in IL-1077,78. Our study has shown elevated levels of IL6, low levels of IL6 and IL10 transcript levels along with elevated levels of TNF α indicates a non-canonical cytokine distribution in PCOS placenta. This divergence highlights the possibility of selective cytokine regulation and placental immune buffering mechanisms in PCOS, rather than uniform activation of inflammatory pathways. Given that a tightly regulated balance between pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β) and anti-inflammatory mediators (IL-10, IL-4) is essential for maintaining maternal–fetal immune homeostasis, disruption of this equilibrium in PCOS may contribute to altered placental function and adverse pregnancy outcomes.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that maternal hyperandrogenism in a PCOS context disrupts the finely balanced interplay between androgen, progesterone, and estrogen signaling in the placenta. This hormonal disequilibrium impairs trophoblast differentiation, vascular remodeling, immune homeostasis, and metabolic adaptation, producing a structurally and functionally compromised placenta. Such disruptions provide mechanistic insight into the increased risk of placental insufficiency, fetal growth restriction, and pregnancy complications observed in PCOS and related hyperandrogenic conditions.

Conclusion

This study highlights the multifactorial nature of placental dysfunction in PCOS pregnancies, characterized by significant alterations in steroid hormone receptor expression, immune modulation, and trophoblast lineage regulation. Increased AR and decreased PR and ERβ expression indicate a hormonally imbalanced and pro-inflammatory placental environment that may impair trophoblast invasion and vascular remodeling. Moreover, aberrant expression of key genes involved in trophoblast differentiation (CDX2, GCM1, GATA2), immune tolerance (H2Kd, Prl3b1), and vascular development (Flt4, Pcdh12) against a proinflammatory state due to elevation of IL1 beta and TNF alpha along with reduction of IL6 and IL10 reveals a complex interplay of compensatory and pathological responses within the PCOS placenta. Notably, changes in Tpbpa and Phlda2 expression suggest both structural and endocrine alterations that may contribute to fetal growth restriction and placental insufficiency in PCOS pregnancy.

Altogether, these findings provide novel insights into the molecular landscape of the PCOS placenta and highlight potential targets for therapeutic intervention. Future research should prioritize mechanistic validation of placental alterations in PCOS and the development of robust placental biomarkers to enhance pregnancy monitoring and improve maternal–fetal outcomes, while also identifying novel markers that can be translated into assisted reproductive technologies for better risk stratification and treatment personalization in this population.

Hyperandrogenism-induced PCOS activates androgen receptor signaling and metabolic stress, triggering inflammasome-mediated IL-1β upregulation and subsequent NF-κB–dependent TNF-α and IFN-γ production. This pro-inflammatory cascade suppresses trophoblast lineage regulators (CDX2, GATA2, GCM1, Syn A, Syn B), impairing syncytiotrophoblast differentiation and promoting a shift toward junctional zone phenotype (ASCL2, TBPA). Concurrently, compensatory upregulation of FLT4 and HLA-G reflects attempted angiogenic and immune adaptations; however, these responses are rendered ineffective due to persistent inflammation and reduced IL-10 and IL-6 signaling. The resulting imbalance leads to defective vascularization, distorted syncytiotrophoblast architecture, and reduced labyrinth zone formation, culminating in placental insufficiency and adverse fetal outcomes.
