Harmonic spectrum of pulsar timing array angular correlations
Bruce Allen, Joseph D. Romano

Abstract
Pulsar timing arrays (PTAs) detect gravitational waves (GWs) via the correlations they create in the arrival times of pulses from different pulsars. The mean correlation, a function of the angle γ \gamma between the directions to two pulsars, was predicted in 1983 by Hellings and Downs (HD). Observation of this angular pattern is crucial evidence that GWs are present, so PTAs “reconstruct the HD curve” by estimating the correlation using pulsar pairs separated by similar angles. The angular pattern may be also expressed as a “harmonic sum” of Legendre polynomials P l ​ ( cos ⁡ γ ) {\rm P}_{l}(\cos\gamma) , with coefficients c l c_{l} . Here, assuming that the GWs and pulsar noise are described by a Gaussian ensemble, we derive optimal estimators for the c l c_{l} and compute their variance. We consider two choices for “optimal”. The first minimizes the variance of each c l c_{l} , independent of the values of the others. The second finds the set of c l c_{l} which minimizes the (squared) deviation of the reconstructed correlation curve from its mean. These are analogous to the so-called “dirty” and “clean” maps of the electromagnetic and (audio-band) GW backgrounds.

Approach 1: Matched Filter –
Our first approach is inspired by
that of
Allen and Romano 2024
. In (AR23) we considered estimators
\hat{\mu}(\gamma)
which are arbitrary linear combinations of the form

\sum_{ab\in\gamma}\sum_{j,k}W_{ab}^{jk}Z_{a}^{j}Z_{b}^{k}\,,

(3)

where
Z_{a}^{j}
is the (complex) Fourier amplitude of the redshift of
pulsar
a
in frequency bin
j
, defined in (AR12), and the
W_{ab}^{jk}
are a set of weights. There, the sum was over a set of
pulsar pairs
ab
in a narrow angular bin at angle
\gamma
, and the
weights satisfied
W_{ab}^{jk*}=W_{ab}^{-j,-k}
, to ensures that the
estimator is real. The weights were selected so that the estimator is
unbiased and minimum variance. (Note that all analysis there and here
may also be done in terms of timing residuals; the results are
identical, see
(
Allen and Romano 2024
, Footnote 19)
.)

In analogy with this, we define an unbiased estimator for
c_{l}
. The
estimator is analogous to (AR28), although we do not know how to
derive it from first principles. For a PTA containing
N_{\rm pul}

pulsars, the estimator is

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{l}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\langle c_{l}\rangle\frac{({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{l}\boldsymbol{\overline{H}})^{t}{\boldsymbol{C^{-1}}}\boldsymbol{ZZ}}{({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{l}\boldsymbol{\overline{H}})^{t}{\boldsymbol{C^{-1}}}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}\boldsymbol{\overline{H}})}

(4)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\langle c_{l}\rangle\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{a<b}\sum_{c<d}\sum_{j,k}\sum_{\ell,m}\mu_{l,ab}{\overline{H}}_{jk}\bigl(C^{-1}\bigr)_{ab,cd}^{jk,\ell m}Z_{c}^{\ell}Z_{d}^{m}\,\,\,\,}{\displaystyle\sum_{a<b}\sum_{c<d}\sum_{j,k}\sum_{\ell,m}\mu_{l,ab}{\overline{H}}_{jk}\bigl(C^{-1}\bigr)_{ab,cd}^{jk,\ell m}\mu_{cd}{\overline{H}}_{\ell m}}\,,

where
\boldsymbol{ZZ}\equiv Z_{a}^{j}Z_{b}^{k}
. Here,

{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{l}\equiv\mu_{{}_{l,ab}}\equiv(1+\delta_{ab}){\rm P}_{l}(\cos\gamma_{ab})\,.

(5)

and
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\sum_{l}\langle c_{l}\rangle{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{l}
are vectors of dimension

N_{\rm pair}=N_{\rm pul}(N_{\rm pul}-1)/2
. In contrast with (
3
),
the sum is
not
confined to a particular angular bin, but
includes
all possible
pulsar-pair cross-correlations
a<b
.

This estimator takes the form

\hat{c}_{l}=\langle c_{l}\rangle\frac{d_{l}}{u_{l}}\,,

(6)

where we have defined

\displaystyle d_{l}

\displaystyle\equiv({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{l}\boldsymbol{\overline{H}})^{t}{\boldsymbol{C^{-1}}}\boldsymbol{ZZ}\,,

(7)

\displaystyle u_{l}

\displaystyle\equiv({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{l}\boldsymbol{\overline{H}})^{t}{\boldsymbol{C^{-1}}}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}\boldsymbol{\overline{H}})\,.

(8)

Expression (
7
) is an
optimal matched filter
for the
angular correlation pattern
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{l}
. The corresponding “filter
template” (
5
) is proportional to the
l
’th term in
(
1
); the
\delta_{ab}
can be dropped since
a<b
.
This is an optimal matched filter because it is obtained by
“whitening the template and the data” via the inverse covariance
matrix
{\boldsymbol{C^{-1}}}
, and then normalizing. Our normalization, placing
u_{l}

in the denominator of (
6
), gives an unbiased estimator of

c_{l}
. To see this, note that
\langle\boldsymbol{ZZ}\rangle={\boldsymbol{\mu}}\boldsymbol{\overline{H}}
. It follows from inspection of (
7
) and (
8
) that

\langle d_{l}\rangle=u_{l}
, thus implying
\langle\hat{c}_{l}\rangle=\langle c_{l}\rangle
.

The variance of the optimal matched filter estimator
(
4
) is easy to calculate. The covariance of
\boldsymbol{ZZ}
is

\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}\equiv\bigl\langle\boldsymbol{ZZ}(\boldsymbol{ZZ})^{\dagger}\bigr\rangle-\bigl\langle\boldsymbol{ZZ}\bigr\rangle\bigl\langle(\boldsymbol{ZZ})^{\dagger}\bigr\rangle\,.

(9)

An explicit formula for

\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}\equiv{\mathcal{C}}_{ab,cd}^{jk,\ell m}

is given in (AR19), in terms of covariance matrices computed from the
gravitational wave background (GWB) and pulsar noise power spectra.
The covariance matrix
{\boldsymbol{C}}
, whose inverse
{\boldsymbol{C^{-1}}}
appears in (
4
),
is the symmetric part of
\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{C}}}
with respect to the frequency
indices (AR20):

{\boldsymbol{C}}\equiv C_{ab,cd}^{jk,\ell m}\equiv{\mathcal{C}}_{ab,cd}^{(jk),(\ell m)}

(10)

From the definition (
7
) of the
d_{l}

it follows that their covariance is

\displaystyle\langle d_{l}d_{l^{\prime}}\rangle-\langle d_{l}\rangle\langle d_{l^{\prime}}\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 14.45377pt=({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{l}\boldsymbol{\overline{H}})^{t}{\boldsymbol{C^{-1}}}\left(\langle({\boldsymbol{Z}}{\boldsymbol{Z}})({\boldsymbol{Z}}{\boldsymbol{Z}})^{\dagger}\rangle-\langle{\boldsymbol{Z}}{\boldsymbol{Z}}\rangle\langle({\boldsymbol{Z}}{\boldsymbol{Z}})^{\dagger}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 86.72377pt\times{\boldsymbol{C^{-1}}}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{l^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\overline{H}})

\displaystyle\hskip 14.45377pt=({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{l}\boldsymbol{\overline{H}})^{t}{\boldsymbol{C^{-1}}}{\boldsymbol{C}}{\boldsymbol{C^{-1}}}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{l^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\overline{H}})

\displaystyle\hskip 14.45377pt=F_{ll^{\prime}}\,,

(11)

where (
9
) was used to get the second equality and we have
defined a matrix

{\boldsymbol{F}}\equiv F_{ll^{\prime}}\equiv({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{l}\boldsymbol{\overline{H}})^{t}{\boldsymbol{C^{-1}}}({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{l^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\overline{H}})\,,

(12)

where
l
and
l^{\prime}
are indices labeling harmonics. Thus, the variance
of the HD correlation estimator is

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\hat{c}_{l}}

\displaystyle\equiv\langle\hat{c}^{2}_{l}\rangle-\langle\hat{c}_{l}\rangle^{2}

(13)

\displaystyle=\langle c_{l}\rangle^{2}\bigl(\langle d_{l}^{2}\rangle-\langle d_{l}\rangle^{2}\bigr)/u_{l}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle c_{l}\rangle^{2}F_{ll}/u_{l}^{2}\,,

where
F_{ll}
are the diagonal elements of
{\boldsymbol{F}}
.

We will write the variance (
13
) as

\sigma^{2}_{\hat{c}_{l}}=\frac{\langle c_{l}\rangle^{2}}{(2L_{\rm eff}+1)N_{\rm freq}}=\frac{\langle c_{l}\rangle^{2}F_{ll}}{u_{l}^{2}}\,,

(14)

where
N_{\rm freq}
and
2L_{\rm eff}+1
are functions of
l
. (This, along with the
formulas given below for
N_{\rm freq}
and
2L_{\rm eff}+1
, is one of the two main
results of this paper.) The division into the two factors is
arbitrary; we break this degeneracy by imposing two requirements: (i)
The effective number of frequency bins
N_{\rm freq}
for which the GWB
dominates pulsar noise equals
N_{\rm cr}
in the crossover frequency
limit. (ii) The effective number of harmonic degrees of freedom
2L_{\rm eff}+1
depends only on the “geometry” of the pulsar timing array,
meaning the pulsar sky directions. Note: the crossover frequency
limit is discussed and defined in
Allen and Romano 2024
between (AR34) and
(AR35).

To find
2L_{\rm eff}+1
and
N_{\rm freq}
, we start with requirement (i), computing

{\boldsymbol{F}}
in the crossover frequency limit, where below “crossover”
frequency
N_{\rm cr}/T
, the GWB dominates the redshifts or timing

this crossover limit,
N_{\rm freq}\to N_{\rm cr}
. The spectrum
H_{jk}
of the GWB
is nonzero only for frequency bins
|j|,|k|\leq N_{\rm cr}
and vanishes if
either
|j|
or
|k|
is larger than
N_{\rm cr}
. The pulsar spectrum

P^{jk}_{a}
has the opposite behavior. For this case, the inverse of
the covariance matrix is given by (AR35). Since
{\boldsymbol{H}}
or
\boldsymbol{\overline{H}}

contracted with
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{a}
vanishes, the same calculation as in (AR37)
shows that

F_{ll^{\prime}}\to 2N_{\rm freq}\,\mathcal{G}_{ll^{\prime}}\,,\qquad u_{l}\to 2N_{\rm freq}\,v_{l}\,,

(15)

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}\equiv\mathcal{G}_{ll^{\prime}}\equiv({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{l})^{t}{\boldsymbol{G^{-1}}}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{l^{\prime}}\,,

(16)

\displaystyle v_{l}\equiv({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{l})^{t}{\boldsymbol{G^{-1}}}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,.

(17)

Substituting these
limiting expressions for
F_{ll}
and

into the rhs of
(
14
) implies that

2L_{\rm eff}+1\equiv\frac{2v_{l}^{2}}{\mathcal{G}_{ll}}\,.

(18)

While (
18
) was obtained in the crossover limit,
requirement (ii) implies that it must always hold.
It then follows from (

) and (

N_{\rm freq}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathcal{G}_{ll}}{F_{ll}}\left(\frac{u_{l}}{v_{l}}\right)^{2}

holds in general (not just in the crossover limit). Note that

depends upon the GWB and pulsar power spectra, as well as upon the

Approach 2: Best Fit –
Our second approach differs from the
first. There, the goal was to estimate a single
c_{l}
“in
isolation”, without taking into consideration the values of the other

c_{l^{\prime}}
(for
l^{\prime}\neq l
). Here, we will instead minimize a “global”
quantity
\chi^{2}
that characterizes the difference between the
observed correlations and the harmonic model (
1
). This
strives to simultaneously find the best estimator for a
set
of

c_{l}
. We denote this set by
\boldsymbol{c}\equiv\{c_{l}\}
, with the understanding
that this denotes some finite subset of
\{c_{0},c_{1},\dots\,\}
. (The remaining
c_{l}
may be set to
zero or other fixed/definite values.)

The starting point of this
\boldsymbol{c}
“reconstruction” is a real
quadratic function
\chi^{2}
of
\boldsymbol{c}
, defined by

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\boldsymbol{c})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{t}(\boldsymbol{c}){\boldsymbol{C^{-1}}}\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})

(27)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{a<b}\sum_{c<d}\sum_{j,k}\sum_{\ell,m}\Delta_{ab}^{jk}(\boldsymbol{c})\bigl(C^{-1}\bigr)_{ab,cd}^{jk,\ell m}\Delta_{cd}^{\ell m}{}^{*}(\boldsymbol{c})\,,

where

\boldsymbol{\Delta}(\boldsymbol{c})\equiv\Delta_{ab}^{jk}(\boldsymbol{c})\equiv Z_{a}^{j}Z_{b}^{k}-{\overline{H}}_{jk}\scalebox{0.85}{\mbox{$\displaystyle\sum_{c_{l}\in\boldsymbol{c}}$}}c_{l}\mu_{l,ab}\,

(28)

and
{\boldsymbol{C^{-1}}}
is the inverse of the covariance matrix given in (
9
).
The
\Delta
’s and the statistic
\chi^{2}
are measures of the difference
between the observed spatial correlations and the model
\sum_{l}c_{l}\mu_{l,ab}
of (
1
), restricted to
c_{l}\in\boldsymbol{c}
.
The value of
\chi^{2}
is a
function of the observed redshifts (or timing residuals), of the GWB
and pulsar noise spectral models, and of a set of real coefficients

\boldsymbol{c}
.

In this approach, model selection or model fitting is carried out by
finding the
\boldsymbol{c}
that minimize
\chi^{2}
, with the other quantities
held fixed. The values of
\boldsymbol{c}
that minimize
\chi^{2}
will be
denoted by
\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}\equiv\{\hat{c}_{l}\}
; they are estimators of the harmonic amplitudes

\boldsymbol{c}
of the spatial correlations, which describe our particular
realization of the Universe, as defined by (
1
). In
contrast with Approach 1, which minimizes the variance of
\hat{c}_{l}

between different realizations of the Universe drawn from the Gaussian
ensemble, this approach minimizes the overall mismatch
\chi^{2}

between the observed correlations in our Universe and the harmonic
decomposition (
1
). (The Gaussian ensemble is employed
to characterize how much mismatch will remain, on the average.)

To accomplish this minimization, take the partial derivative of
(
27
) with respect to a particular
c_{l}\in\boldsymbol{c}
, and set it to zero:

\frac{\partial\chi^{2}(\boldsymbol{c})}{\partial c_{l}}\biggr\rvert_{\boldsymbol{c}=\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}}=0\,.

(29)

For any given value of
l
, this results in a set of linear equations
for the estimators

\sum_{\hat{c}_{l^{\prime}}\in\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}}F_{ll^{\prime}}\hat{c}_{l^{\prime}}=d_{l}\,,

(30)

where
F_{ll^{\prime}}
is defined in (
12
),
and
l
ranges over the set of subscripts that appear in
\boldsymbol{c}
.
To obtain (
30
), we combined terms,
exploiting the fact that
{\boldsymbol{C^{-1}}}
is a symmetric matrix.

Note that the
d_{l}
, which were defined earlier in (
7
), are real,
since
({\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{l}\boldsymbol{\overline{H}})^{t}{\boldsymbol{C^{-1}}}
annihilates the imaginary part of

{\boldsymbol{Z}}{\boldsymbol{Z}}
.
This is a consequence of
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{l}
,
{\overline{H}}
, and
{\boldsymbol{C^{-1}}}
all being real,
which implies that

{\overline{H}}_{jk}\bigl(C^{-1}\bigr)_{ab,cd}^{jk,\ell m}={\overline{H}}_{-j,-k}\bigl(C^{-1}\bigr)_{ab,cd}^{-j,-k,-\ell,-m}\,.

(31)

Thus,

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\sum_{jk}\sum_{\ell m}{\overline{H}}_{jk}\bigl(C^{-1}\bigr)_{ab,cd}^{jk,\ell,m}Z_{c}^{\ell}Z_{d}^{m}

\displaystyle\hskip 7.22743pt=\sum_{jk}\sum_{\ell m}{\overline{H}}_{-j,-k}\bigl(C^{-1}\bigr)_{ab,cd}^{-j,-k,-\ell,-m}Z_{c}^{-\ell}Z_{d}^{-m}

\displaystyle\hskip 7.22743pt=\sum_{jk}\sum_{\ell m}{\overline{H}}_{jk}\bigl(C^{-1}\bigr)_{ab,cd}^{jk,\ell m}Z_{c}^{-\ell}Z_{d}^{-m}

\displaystyle\hskip 7.22743pt=\sum_{jk}\sum_{\ell m}{\overline{H}}_{jk}\bigl(C^{-1}\bigr)_{ab,cd}^{jk,\ell m}\Re(Z_{c}^{\ell}Z_{d}^{m})\,,

(32)

where the first equality follows from changing the sign of the dummy
summation variables
j
,
k
,
\ell
,
m
; the second equality follows from
(
31
); and the last equality follows from the previous two lines and
the definition of the real part of
\boldsymbol{ZZ}
.

The number of linear equations (
30
) is the same as
the number of
c_{l}
in the finite set
\boldsymbol{c}
.
We can choose which ones we wish to
estimate, and are free to arbitrarily set some (e.g.,
\hat{c}_{0}
and/or

\hat{c}_{1}
) to zero (or other values) if desired.
In any case, to arrive at a unique
solution, the number of
\hat{c}_{l}
to be determined must equal the
number of linearly independent equations. Let us use
N_{L}
to
denotes the number of
\hat{c}_{l}
that we wish to obtain, which is the order
of the set
\boldsymbol{c}
.
For example, if
by fiat we constrain
\hat{c}_{0}=\hat{c}_{1}=0
, then we would obtain values for

\hat{c}_{2},\hat{c}_{3},\cdots,\hat{c}_{{}_{N_{L}+1}}
.
The solution for the
\hat{c}_{l}
are then given by

\boldsymbol{\hat{c}}={\boldsymbol{F^{-1}}}\boldsymbol{d}\,,

(33)

where
{\boldsymbol{F^{-1}}}
is the matrix inverse (or pseudoinverse) of
{\boldsymbol{F}}
. Note
that from here forward, we treat
\boldsymbol{c}
as a column vector of dimension

N_{L}
rather than as a set.

The application of
{\boldsymbol{F^{-1}}}
to
\boldsymbol{d}
can be thought of as
“deconvolving” the response of the measurement process
in order to extract the harmonic components
c_{l}
.
In the context of map making,
{\boldsymbol{F}}\equiv F_{ll^{\prime}}
is
called the “Fisher matrix” and the collection
\boldsymbol{d}\equiv d_{l}

is called the “dirty map”, see
(
Thrane et al. 2009
, Sec. III.B)
.
The “clean map”
\boldsymbol{\hat{c}}\equiv\hat{c}_{l}
is the end result of
the deconvolution process.
Indeed, it is easy to see that the “clean map” estimators
\boldsymbol{\hat{c}}
are unbiased.
This follows from

\langle d_{l}\rangle=u_{l}
, which we discussed in the sentences
following (
5
),
together with
u_{l}=\sum_{l^{\prime}}F_{ll^{\prime}}\langle c_{l^{\prime}}\rangle
, which immediately
follows from the definitions (
8
) and (
12
)
of
u_{l}
and
F_{ll^{\prime}}
.

The covariance of the estimators
\hat{c}_{l}
is easily calculated
by first expressing it in terms of the covariance of the
“dirty map” data
d_{l}
:

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{ll^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\equiv\langle\hat{c}_{l}\hat{c}_{l^{\prime}}\rangle-\langle\hat{c}_{l}\rangle\langle\hat{c}_{l^{\prime}}\rangle

(34)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l_{1}}\sum_{l_{2}}(F^{-1})_{ll_{1}}\left(\langle d_{l_{1}}d_{l_{2}}\rangle-\langle d_{l_{1}}\rangle\langle d_{l_{2}}\rangle\right)(F^{-1})_{l_{2}l^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l_{1}}\sum_{l_{2}}(F^{-1})_{ll_{1}}F_{l_{1}l_{2}}(F^{-1})_{l_{2}l^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=(F^{-1})_{ll^{\prime}}\,,

where we used (
11
) to obtain the third equality.
Note that if
F_{ll^{\prime}}
is not invertible,
then
(F^{-1})_{ll^{\prime}}
denotes its pseudoinverse, and the
product
{\boldsymbol{F^{-1}}}{\boldsymbol{F}}
is a rank-
{\boldsymbol{F}}
projector onto the nonnull space.

The variance of a particular
\hat{c}_{l}
estimator is obtained
by setting
l=l^{\prime}
in (
34
):

\sigma^{2}_{\hat{c}_{l}}\equiv\sigma^{2}_{ll}=(F^{-1})_{ll}\,.

(35)

In analogy with Approach 1, we write this variance
in the form

\sigma^{2}_{\hat{c}_{l}}=\frac{\langle c_{l}\rangle^{2}}{(2L_{\rm eff}+1)N_{\rm freq}}=(F^{-1})_{ll}\,,

(36)

where
N_{\rm freq}
and
2L_{\rm eff}+1
are functions of
l
.
As noted for Approach 1, the division into the
two factors is arbitrary.
So we break this degeneracy by imposing the
same two requirements as before.
(See the discussion after (
14
).)

For requirement (i), we again consider the
“crossover” frequency limit, where
N_{\rm freq}\to N_{\rm cr}
.
As before, we find
{\boldsymbol{F}}\to 2N_{\rm freq}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}
.
But this time, we write this relation in terms
of the diagonal components of the inverse
matrices

(F^{-1})_{ll}\to\frac{(\mathcal{G}^{-1})_{ll}}{2N_{\rm freq}}\text{\ \ for }l\geq 2\,,

(37)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}^{-1}}
is the pseudoinverse of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}
.
Substituting this limiting expression for
(F^{-1})_{ll}

into the rhs of (
36
) implies that

2L_{\rm eff}+1\equiv\frac{2\langle c_{l}\rangle^{2}}{\left(\mathcal{G}^{-1}\right)_{ll}}\,.

(38)

As we argued for Approach 1, this relation must always
hold as a consequence of requirement (ii).
It then follows from (
36
) that

N_{\rm freq}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\frac{\left(\mathcal{G}^{-1}\right)_{ll}}{\left(F^{-1}\right)_{ll}}\,.

(39)

As we saw for Approach 1, the effective number of harmonic degrees
of freedom
2L_{\rm eff}+1
simplifies when there are many pulsars uniformly
distributed on the sky. In this limit (
38
) gives

2L_{\rm eff}+1\equiv\frac{2\langle c_{l}\rangle^{2}}{\left(\mathcal{G}^{-1}\right)_{ll}}\to 2\langle c_{l}\rangle^{2}\mathcal{G}_{ll}\to 2l+1\text{\ \ for }l\geq 2\,,

(40)

as a consequence of (
22
).
This is precisely the number of degrees of freedom associated with harmonic index
l
.

1
shows values of
2L_{\rm eff}+1
computed using
(
38
) (with
N_{L}=16
) for several real and fictional PTAs. We note
that when the number of pulsars is large, the values of
2L_{\rm eff}+1
are
similar for both approaches. We expect that
2L_{\rm eff}+1
should satisfy

2L_{\rm eff}+1\leq 2l+1
, but have not been able to prove this. We would
have expected that adding pulsars to an array always increases
2L_{\rm eff}+1
, but we have been unable to prove this and note that numerical
experiments indicate that it is not the case for Approach 1, though there it
appears that the sum over
l
of
2L_{\rm eff}+1
always increases.

Conclusion.—
There is a substantial literature on
the harmonic approach to PTAs (see
Allen 2024
and
references therein). These represent the mean correlations between
pulsars as a sum of Legendre polynomials of the cosine of the
pulsar-pair separation angle. The coefficients in that expansion characterize
the correlations.

Here, we have shown two ways to estimate these coefficients from
measured redshift or timing-residual measurements. The first
approach minimizes the expected variance away from the mean, for a
single coefficient “in isolation”. The second approach minimizes
the square of the mean deviation away from the expected value, where
the measure on the quadratic form is constructed in the same way as
for a conventional
\chi^{2}
statistic. Of greatest interest to us
is not the estimators themselves but rather their variances.

In both cases, the variance is written as a ratio, where the
numerator
\langle c_{l}\rangle^{2}
is the square of the expected
value, and the denominator is an effective number of degrees of
freedom. This denominator, in turn, is the product of an effective
number of angular degrees of freedom and an effective number of
frequency degrees of freedom. Both of these depend upon
l
, but
the effective number of angular degrees of freedom is determined
entirely by the geometry of the PTA, whereas the effective number of
frequency bins depends upon the spectral properties of the GWB and
pulsar noise. The corresponding formulas for first approach are
given in (
14
), (
18
) and (
19
), and for the
second approach in (
36
) (
38
) and (
39
).

The “harmonic-space” analysis done here closely parallels a
corresponding “position-space” analysis done in
Allen and Romano 2024
,
and shares many similar features. Both construct frequency-weighted
estimators of the HD correlation. In comparison with the “white,
zero-lag” estimators of the HD correlation used
in
Allen 2023
;
Allen and Romano 2023
, these permit a further
reduction in the variance. Indeed, by increasing the effective
number of signal-dominated frequency bins, the effects of cosmic
variance can in principle be reduced as much as desired. Such an
increase can be achieved either by adding additional “quiet”
pulsars to the PTA, or alternatively, by increasing the observation
time. Similar conclusions have been reached
in
Pitrou and Cusin 2024
.

We believe that applying these approaches to PTA posteriors will
provide a useful tool to characterize and quantify the HD
correlations and their deviations from the HD curve.
