Connecting cultural wisdom, physical literacy, and physical activity among older adults from diverse Indigenous communities: an exploratory review
Authors: Amanda Froehlich Chow, Heather McWhinney, Tina Alexis, M. Louise Humbert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndigenous older adults across the globe face higher rates of chronic illness and lower life expectancy than non-Indigenous populations, yet little research has examined how physical literacy might support their health and well-being. Traditionally applied to children and youth, physical literacy—understood as one’s relationship with movement and physical activity across the life course—shares with Indigenous worldviews an emphasis on holistic well-being rooted in culture, community, and the environment. This exploratory review examines global literature to identify how physical literacy concepts—such as motivation, confidence, physical competence, and knowledge—connect with ways of knowing, being, and doing among Indigenous older adults, as well as in physical activity programs for this population. Findings reveal strong connections between physical literacy and culturally grounded forms of movement, with successful programs often integrating physical activity with cultural practices such as sharing circles. These initiatives reflect holistic views of health and highlight opportunities for culturally rooted approaches to physical literacy. The findings suggest that meaningful engagement with Indigenous older adults calls for physical literacy frameworks co-developed through community partnerships that honor Indigenous ways of knowing, being, and doing. Implications for policy and practice include the need for participatory program design that prioritizes social connection, intergenerational learning, spiritual health, and community well-being.

Introduction

As populations age worldwide, supporting older adults to remain physically active has become a pressing public health priority—particularly for the estimated 370 million Indigenous Peoples, who experience reduced life expectancy and disproportionate burdens of diabetes, obesity, and other chronic illnesses [1–3].

Regular physical activity is widely recognized to improve cardiometabolic, musculoskeletal, and mental health and to support healthy aging across populations [4–7]. Yet nearly one-third of adults worldwide are insufficiently active, with prevalence highest among older adults [8–10]. Reliable prevalence estimates of physical activity among Indigenous Peoples remain scarce, particularly for older adults. Most available evidence comes from regional or community-based studies in countries such as Australia, Canada, and New Zealand, with few providing comparable or age-specific data. Existing surveillance systems rarely capture land-based or subsistence activities that hold cultural significance, and they often aggregate Indigenous and non-Indigenous participants, obscuring unique patterns of movement and wellness across diverse Indigenous communities [11,12]. For Indigenous communities, physical activity guidelines and programs for older adults may lack cultural relevance, safety, and holistic perspectives, as they often fail to reflect Indigenous worldviews [13–16]. In contrast, the concept of physical literacy positions movement not only as a physical skill but also as a pathway to meaning, identity, and connection across multiple domains of life [17]. This exploratory review examines global literature to consider how physical literacy concepts—such as motivation, confidence, physical competence, and knowledge—intersect with Indigenous ways of knowing, being, and doing in older adulthood.

Colonization and Indigenous Peoples

Mainstream physical activity models often fail to account for the ongoing effects of colonization. While Indigenous Peoples live in diverse contexts across the globe, many have experienced colonization—whether through European settlement, imperial expansion, or internal colonizing pressures from dominant states—with lasting effects on cultural practices and opportunities for movement. Colonization disrupted activities such as hunting, fishing, and gathering that once embedded physical activity in daily life [14,18,19]. These disruptions altered ways of knowing, being, and doing [2,20] and contributed to health inequities that continue to shape the experiences of Indigenous older adults.

Holistic perspectives and physical literacy

Despite these disruptions, Indigenous views of health remain holistic, integrating physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual dimensions with reciprocal relationships among family, kin, community, and land [21,22]. Collective knowledge, culture, and spirituality are woven into daily life and passed down through generations [23,24], influencing how movement is understood and practiced across the lifespan. These perspectives center connection, reciprocity, and well-being, offering a broader understanding of movement than the Western biomedical model, which tends to separate body and mind and emphasize individual responsibility for health [20,25,26]. Much of the research on physical activity and aging reflects these same assumptions, giving limited attention to cultural diversity and to the distinct meanings and motivations that shape movement among Indigenous Peoples [11,13,26]. As Henhawk [14] and Smith [16] argue, Western physical activity frameworks often overlook Indigenous worldviews that place relationality, spirituality, and connection to land at the center of movement. They contend that these frameworks marginalize Indigenous practices and overlook movement as an embodied connection to history, environment, and intergenerational knowledge—integral to identity and survival.

Indigenous perspectives on holism share with physical literacy an emphasis on movement as a source of meaning, identity, and connection across life domains [16]. Physical literacy is understood as a lifelong journey of growth, shaped by diverse movement experiences [27]. The modern concept was formally developed by Margaret Whitehead, a British philosopher of physical education, who described a “robust disposition” toward physical activity that must be “nurtured” if a person is to flourish [28, p. 26]. Rejecting Cartesian dualism, Whitehead emphasizes the “human embodied dimension” through which people experience, interpret, and construct meaning in movement [29, p. 8]. The International Physical Literacy Association [30] defines physical literacy as “the motivation, confidence, physical competence, knowledge and understanding to value and take responsibility for engagement in physical activities for life,” a definition that underpins this paper and reflects the integration of the physical, affective, and cognitive domains [29].

Physical literacy and older adults

Physical literacy research has largely focused on children and adolescents, reflecting its roots in physical education and youth sport, where childhood is viewed as a critical period for motor skill development, habit formation, and lifelong activity [31]. Although its application to older adults remains limited, physical literacy’s emphasis on confidence, motivation, and meaningful engagement in movement could contribute to addressing inactivity, supporting chronic disease prevention, and promoting well-being among this population. Recent review articles [31–35] and models [36] have begun to signal an interest in physical literacy for older adults. Despite these contributions and well-established links between physical activity, health, and successful aging [37,38], the concept remains underused with older adults, underscoring the need for innovative research and practice to support healthy aging.

Aim of this review

Only a handful of studies have examined physical literacy among Indigenous youth [24,39], and even fewer have explored its potential relevance for Indigenous adults, particularly whether it might serve as a useful framework to support increased physical activity in later life [16]. This exploratory review forms part of a larger study that seeks to foster physical literacy among Indigenous older adults through culturally rooted approaches to physical activity. We reviewed global studies to explore links between physical literacy, healthy aging, and Indigenous ways of knowing, being, and doing among older Indigenous adults. Understanding these relationships may help inform physical literacy programming that supports healthier aging amid persistent health inequities.

We use the term Indigenous to refer to the First Peoples of a region and their descendants, who continue to uphold cultural, linguistic, historical, and spiritual connections to their ancestral lands. While we use Indigenous older adult as a general term, we refer to specific populations using the terminology adopted by the original study authors. In describing study findings, we use the term participants to maintain consistency and respect for confidentiality, while recognizing that the individuals represented in these studies are community members, Elders, and Knowledge Seekers whose insights extend beyond the conventional research role of “participants.”

Methods

This exploratory review examines how physical literacy relates to Indigenous ways of knowing, being, and doing in studies of physical activity and healthy aging among Indigenous older adults.

We used Arksey and O'Malley's approach [40] because the topic is under-researched and methods and settings vary. Our aim was to identify primary research that connects physical literacy with these Indigenous frameworks in contexts involving older adults.

Review design and search strategy

Guided by Arksey and O'Malley's framework [40], we followed the stages they identify: determining research questions, identifying relevant studies, selecting studies, charting data, and compiling, summarizing, and reporting results. As Arksey and O’Malley note, the process involved iteration as we revisited stages, continuing to find appropriate articles.

We conducted searches in Scopus, MEDLINE (via PubMed), and Web of Science, consulting a health sciences librarian to refine the strategy and identify appropriate keywords and subject headings. The final search string—(“physical literacy” OR “physical* activ*” OR exercise OR movement) AND (ageing OR aging OR “old age” OR “older adult” OR senior OR elder*) AND (Indigenous OR Aboriginal OR “First Nation” OR “American Indian” OR Amerindian OR Inuit OR Métis OR Māori)—was adapted for each database. Searches were limited to English-language, peer-reviewed articles published between January 1, 2000, and September 10, 2025, that included global studies in which at least 40% of participants were Indigenous and aged 40 years or older, or where the average participant age was 40 or above. We chose 40 as the age cut-off, following protocols applied in other studies, to better capture older adults given the lower life expectancies of many Indigenous Peoples [41]. This date range was selected to include contemporary research from the period during which the concept of physical literacy gained prominence and Indigenous health research expanded substantially in scope and methodological diversity. Books, review articles, and gray literature, such as theses, dissertations, conference abstracts, and policy reports, were excluded.

Entries were cross-checked for duplicates based on title, DOI, and PubMed ID, and records with partial matches (e.g. identical titles but differing DOIs) were manually reviewed for accuracy. Reference lists of included studies and relevant review papers were also screened to identify additional sources.

Because respectful, reciprocal working relationships were a critical goal, included studies had to demonstrate Indigenous or Indigenous-informed methodologies. Eligible studies demonstrated adherence to research approaches grounded in Indigenous worldviews and relational accountability—such as community-based participatory research or Elder- and community-guided designs. Studies that engaged Indigenous communities solely as participants, without evidence of Indigenous involvement in design, data collection, or interpretation, were excluded. However, we included Indigenous-informed studies when they showed cultural grounding through consultation, adaptation of mainstream methods to Indigenous epistemologies, or integration of community values and protocols.

To provide contextual comparison, we retained two studies [42,43] that applied neither Indigenous nor Indigenous-informed methodologies. Both included Indigenous participants and provided insights into cultural understandings of physical activity and aging. In addition, we classified two early ethnographic studies [44,45] as Indigenous-informed rather than fully Indigenous-methodological. Undertaken at a time when scholars were only just beginning to articulate Indigenous research methodologies (e.g. [46,47]), they nonetheless provide valuable historical and cultural perspectives on relational health and aging.

The study selection process is presented in Figure 1. The database search identified articles in Scopus (n = 473), MEDLINE (n = 185), and Web of Science (n = 1699). Selected studies were analyzed for main themes in the findings and for definitional elements of physical literacy, even if the term was not explicitly used. These elements included a holistic approach to health and physical activity, motivation, confidence, physical competence, and knowledge and understanding.

Common reasons for excluding records included the following: lack of a holistic approach; focus solely on older adults or Indigenous Peoples, without specifically addressing Indigenous older adults; minimal references to physical activity or exercise; classification as a systematic or scoping review, a report, conference proceeding, thesis, or book; lack of original research, Indigenous methodology, or adherence to Indigenous protocols; or content that did not fit into any defined category.

Selection and appraisal of studies

Before coding, each eligible study was reviewed to confirm that it met the inclusion criteria and to identify key descriptive features for charting. We noted whether participants were aged 40 years or older, or whether the mean age met that threshold, and whether at least 40% of participants identified as Indigenous. Studies were also classified by primary focus—aging, health promotion, or physical activity—and by the extent to which physical activity was featured and was culturally grounded (e.g. land-based movement, traditional exercise, or community-specific programs). Because a holistic orientation was central to the review framework, studies that lacked reference to holistic concepts of health or movement were excluded. We also noted whether studies reflected one or more defining elements of physical literacy—motivation, confidence, competence, and knowledge and understanding—to guide later coding. The review operationalized the concept of physical literacy by applying these four defining elements, together with holism, as analytic categories to identify how physical literacy was expressed within Indigenous contexts. This approach translated an abstract framework into a structured coding system that captured diverse expressions of movement, health, and learning within Indigenous ways of knowing, being, and doing. In cases of overlap, such as programs addressing both general health and aging, or combining land-based and structured exercise, studies were coded in all relevant categories. This ensured that intersections among aging, culture, and physical literacy were captured rather than collapsed into single classifications.

Data extraction and analysis

We extracted data from each study using a structured charting form that recorded bibliographic information, country or region, aim, population, participant characteristics, sample, approach, methodology, and findings. To organize and interpret findings across diverse cultural and geographic contexts, a two-level coding process was applied. At the first level, descriptive codes captured structural information about each study, including methodological approach (qualitative, mixed methods), type of physical activity or movement focus, and degree of Indigenous involvement (Indigenous, Indigenous-informed, or non-Indigenous methodology). At the second level, conceptual codes were used to analyze how studies represented physical literacy and culturally grounded understandings of movement. These codes—holism, motivation, confidence, competence, and knowledge and understanding—were derived from the International Physical Literacy Association's framework [30] and adapted through Indigenous worldviews emphasizing relationality, spirituality, and collective well-being. Coding was iterative and interpretive: as themes emerged, studies were revisited to refine and confirm categorizations, ensuring that patterns reflected both local meanings and cross-cultural consistencies. This analytic structure allowed for systematic comparison across cultural contexts while remaining grounded in local meanings.

Author roles

TA (an Indigenous scholar) and HM jointly developed the review design. TA conducted the initial database search, focusing on intersections of physical literacy and Indigenous ways of knowing, being, and doing among Indigenous older adults, and led drafting of the first version of the paper. Building on this foundation, HM broadened the search to include literature examining the relationships between healthy aging, physical activity, and Indigenous older adults. This approach was necessary given the paucity of studies directly addressing physical literacy among Indigenous older adults. Together, TA and HM screened more than 2,000 records and assessed 70 full-text articles for inclusion, meeting several times with AFC to discuss and adjudicate studies that required further consideration. HM completed the full-text review and data extraction. TA and HM co-wrote the manuscript, which underwent multiple rounds of revision and refinement by all authors, with AFC and LH contributing methodological and interpretive feedback throughout.

Results

Twenty-five studies met the inclusion criteria (Table 1), providing a sufficiently broad yet focused foundation for exploring how physical literacy relates to Indigenous ways of knowing, being, and doing among older adults. Fourteen studies addressed Indigenous older adults’ general perspectives on healthy aging—including physical activity—with some also discussing programming. Eleven focused on organized programs or interventions for Indigenous older adults. To analyze and present these findings, we organized the results under the core principles of physical literacy: a holistic approach to health and physical activity; motivation; confidence; physical competence; and knowledge and understanding. These principles served as thematic categories to group subthemes identified across studies. The accompanying tables highlight the voices of the Indigenous participants.

A holistic approach to health and physical activity

In all 25 studies, holism was a central theme. The general studies on aging and physical activity emphasized that a balance of physical, emotional, spiritual, cultural, and social health is integral to Indigenous Peoples’ well-being [11,22,42–45,48–53]. The same emphasis appeared in studies on healthy aging and physical activity programs for Indigenous older adults [13,52,54–61]. Participants described holism as central to the collective health and well-being of the community and to relationships among people, nature, and the land. Across studies, participants depicted health and movement as multidimensional, relational, and spiritually grounded—reflecting Indigenous ways of knowing, being, and doing. Subthemes included interconnected well-being, emphasizing that individual, community, and environmental health are inseparable; multidimensional well-being through movement, where physical activity engaged mental, emotional, and spiritual domains; spiritual integration and connection to nature and continuity, which reinforced strength, belonging, and balance; and Elder-led holistic program design, where programs were guided by relational and cultural principles. These subthemes, illustrated by participant quotations in Table 2, show how holistic health is enacted through relationships among people, land, and spirit.

Motivation for being healthy and physically active

Motivation is key to being physically literate. When people are motivated to be active, they are more likely to enhance and refine their movement abilities [17]. Improvement boosts confidence, fueling the desire to remain physically active. In several studies, participants explained why they engage in physical activity and are mindful of healthy aging. Their reasons are captured under two subthemes: social connection and belonging, and cultural and land-based engagement.

Social Connection and Belonging. As shown in Table 3, participants were motivated to engage in physical activity in comfortable and safe social settings where they felt they belonged. Across diverse contexts, movement and exercise were closely tied to family, friendship, and community life, with participants voicing their pleasure in activities that combined movement like dancing or gardening with socializing and relationship building [42,43,63]. Physical activity programs designed for specific cultural and community groups also strengthened motivation, as Indigenous older adults from diverse cultures felt at home with their own people [50,56,64]. Yet several studies observed that in many rural and isolated regions, family outmigration has reduced opportunities for social connection and shared activity, weakening the kinship and community networks that sustain belonging through movement [22,51,53,54].

Cultural and Land-Based Engagement. Participants were motivated to engage in activities that were deeply intertwined with culture and identity—movement and culture were one and the same (e.g. [11,52,63]). Métis older adults, for example, described activity on the land as part of “who they are, or their essence” [52, p. 9], and Inuit participants credited “going out on the land” with delaying the aging process [44, p. 143]. As one participant from New South Wales, Australia, noted, staying healthy helped her “get around the bush” [50, p. 363], illustrating physical literacy’s emphasis on movement across diverse environments [65]. Being on the land, water, or ice reinforced belonging and cultural continuity by providing opportunities to connect or reconnect with places of meaning [22,43,52,63]. For some, returning to ancestral lands evoked emotional and spiritual renewal. Tayan Bnkis (Elders) from Taiwan [60, p. 604] described it as “… a heavy veil [being] lifted … a sense of wholeness, belonging, and continuity.” Alaska Native Elders [11,45,48] likewise emphasized that staying active supported healthy aging and community connection, whether through subsistence activities such as hunting or berry picking, or through intentional exercise like walking.

Participants from diverse communities emphasized the importance of culturally grounded programs that respect Indigenous knowledge and provide culturally safe opportunities for decision-making and participation [55,66]. However, engagement was sometimes limited by transportation challenges or declining mobility, which made it difficult for some to take part in programs or activities (e.g. [22]). Confidence

As a core element of physical literacy, confidence helps sustain lifelong enjoyment in movement. As shown in Table 5, confidence among participants was closely tied to empowerment and autonomy. Indigenous older adults described striving for independence and self-efficacy, emphasizing the value of doing things on their own, such as “old [Inuit] men [who] return from surgery in Yellowknife, go home, pack their sleds, and go hunting or fishing” [44, pp. 141–143]. Physical activity helped them build courage during mental-health crises and heal by participating in community programs described as sources of vitality (e.g. [56,61,66]). Although these examples illustrate how confidence in maintaining active, independent lives is intertwined with purpose, pride, and resilience, it can also be eroded by illness, frailty, and structural barriers such as lack of transportation and culturally insensitive programs [54,60].

Physical competency

A critical dimension of physical literacy, physical competency refers to an individual’s fitness, balance, coordination, and ability to apply movement patterns and skills across diverse activities. As shown in Table 6, many participants maintained mobility through practice and persistence (e.g. [11,45]), describing regular movement—such as walking, stretching, or dancing—as essential to staying mobile. Culturally adapted programs were found to enhance mobility, balance, and strength while reducing the risk of falls [55,56,66]. These studies depict transformation through movement—a process of maintaining and rebuilding physical capacity that enables Indigenous older adults to remain active.

Knowledge and understanding

Knowledge and understanding are critical in developing physical literacy. Understanding movement principles can enhance confidence and competence [28,29]. As shown in Table 7, participants expressed knowledge and understanding through continuous learning, cultural grounding, and intergenerational knowledge sharing. Many participants recognized the importance of remaining physically active [43,45,52]. An older Inuit hunter observed that stopping activity would “cause the body to seize up, much like a neglected snowmobile engine” [44, p. 141]. Older Indigenous adults adapted movement patterns to accommodate age-related physical changes in different contexts [52] and adjusted to diminishing energy [43]. As program participants learned to improve their mobility, balance, and coordination, they became aware of how movement supported their emotional and physical health [55,56,66]. Programs that encouraged reflection and dialogue—for example, those combining exercise with yarning (sharing) circles—enhanced learning and growth by fostering understanding [56]. Participants stressed that programs promoting physical activity and healthy aging should be grounded in Indigenous Knowledge and developed under the guidance of Elders and Knowledge Keepers to ensure community ownership[13,55].

Knowledge and understanding: lifelong learning, cultural grounding, and shared wisdom.

Across studies, knowledge sharing was another vital aspect of understanding, with participants from diverse communities describing teaching and storytelling as both a responsibility and a source of pride. Elders and Knowledge Keepers viewed passing on teachings as essential to sustaining cultural identity and collective well-being [11,44,51]. Many Indigenous older adults engaged in land-based, subsistence, and cultural activities to transmit wisdom and skills to children, grandchildren, and community youth. Sharing stories about how ancestors lived and participating in traditional practices such as berry picking and cutting fish were meaningful ways to link activity with identity and belonging [45]. Indigenous older adults were also strongly motivated to share lessons about the relationship between good health and physical activity with younger generations [44,64]. One participant expressed the desire to “[get] them [young people] under my wing and try and talk to them” [61, p. 10]. The relationship between older and younger generations was reciprocal: Elders passed knowledge and traditions to youth, who in turn engaged with, assisted, and supported them [44,48,51]. Knowledge sharing was thus both instructional and relational, as older adults guided youth while also learning from them [49,60].

Across the studies reviewed, Indigenous older adults described physical activity as a holistic, culturally grounded practice that encompasses far more than physical exercise. The findings affirm that physical literacy, when interpreted through Indigenous ways of knowing, being, and doing, extends beyond skill and competence to include cultural, emotional, and spiritual dimensions of wellness. Although the studies reflect diverse Indigenous communities from many parts of the world, each with its own histories, languages, and traditions, they share an understanding of movement as an embodied expression of identity, belonging, and relationship. Health is experienced through reciprocity and balance—between body and spirit, individual and community, and people and place. Programs that honored these interconnected dimensions were consistently described as more meaningful, effective, and sustainable. These insights suggest that physical literacy, understood as a lifelong, relational, and culturally grounded process, offers a valuable framework for supporting holistic health and well-being among Indigenous older adults.

Discussion

This review examined how physical literacy relates to ways of knowing, being, and doing among diverse Indigenous older adults, drawing on 25 studies of physical activity and aging in daily life, programs, or interventions. The analysis applied key physical literacy themes—holism, motivation, confidence, physical competence, and knowledge and understanding—while identifying conceptual gaps.

Conceptual intersections between physical literacy and Indigenous perspectives

Health and movement were described by study participants as inherently holistic and relational, linking individuals with family, community, land, and spirit. In diverse Indigenous cultures, holism reflects a worldview grounded in relationships and sustained through deep spiritual connections [67]. Motivation to be physically active was generated largely through social, cultural, and land-based engagement, while knowledge and understanding were expressed through learning, adapting to age-related physical changes and shifting circumstances, and transmitting Traditional Knowledge to younger generations. Joy, vitality, and feeling well while moving were sustaining forces, suggesting that positive affect is central to physical activity and engagement. Both the physical literacy framework—based on the International Physical Literacy Association (IPLA) definition—and diverse Indigenous ways of knowing, being, and doing emphasize a holistic approach to physical activity, with motivation, confidence, competence, and knowledge as foundational. Indigenous perspectives place particular importance on spirituality, land, culture, relationality, and intergenerational transmission of knowledge, rooting motivation in community and land, affirming confidence through collective identity, and linking competence to survival and cultural practice. Each stresses lifelong learning and adaptation through movement, suggesting potential conceptual bridges between the two. These intersections suggest that Indigenous ways of knowing, being, and doing could serve as conceptual foundations for extending physical literacy in future models developed collaboratively with Indigenous Knowledge Keepers.

A contested framework

Although conceived as an embodied process [28,29], physical literacy often manifests in practice as individual skill mastery and measurement in children and youth, fragmenting the holistic intent of the original model [39,68,69]. Others argue that physical literacy embeds normative assumptions of capability [70], reinforcing ableist and ageist notions of competence and decline while overlooking movement as relational, spiritual, and communal. Such critiques reflect broader debates that have made physical literacy a conceptually contested construct, with definitions and frameworks that variously emphasize health promotion, motor competence, or embodied experience [71,72].

Recent frameworks have attempted to address these tensions by extending physical literacy beyond childhood and youth, as seen in [17] lifespan model and [36] older-adult model. Cairney et al. [17] situate physical literacy within a behavioral-science and public-health perspective that emphasizes measurable links among physical activity, motivation, and functional independence. Jones et al.’s [36] ecological model embeds individual attributes within interpersonal, organizational, community, and policy contexts, showing how social environments can enable or constrain activity in older age. Yet both frameworks, while representing important progress, remain grounded in Western scientific traditions and give limited attention to spirituality and to the deeper cultural meanings of movement emphasized in Indigenous perspectives.

Reframing physical literacy through Indigenous Knowledge

Addressing these limitations calls for a shift toward inclusive, meaningful participation across the lifespan. Lloyd et al. [31] propose that joyful interaction and relational engagement should guide how physical literacy is conceptualized for aging populations. Smith [16] further argues that when interpreted through Indigenous epistemologies, physical literacy can be re-envisioned as a relational and culturally grounded practice in which movement is a shared, embodied expression of balance and connection rather than an individual pursuit of skill.

By placing Indigenous Knowledge frameworks at the core, physical literacy initiatives can support health and well-being while affirming cultural identity, relationality, and intergenerational learning. In practice, this involves engaging Elders and Knowledge Keepers in program design and delivery so that initiatives reflect local traditions and address community needs. Studies consistently highlight local partnerships, community engagement, and strong leadership [73–75]. Because Indigenous Knowledge systems are dynamic and diverse and shaped by cultural context, they are best understood and conveyed by those immersed in the culture. Practitioners must therefore prioritize ongoing collaboration and adaptability rather than applying one-size-fits-all solutions.

Culturally grounded approaches in practice

Programs grounded in these principles should be co-designed with Indigenous communities to reflect the interconnectedness of culture, spirituality, land, and collective well-being in safe and culturally grounded ways. Participants valued environments that fostered belonging and comfort among peers [50,55], and many attended these programs with family, friends, or community members, strengthening motivation and reinforcing shared responsibility for well-being. Programs like Ironbark, a falls prevention program in Australia that pairs exercise sessions with yarning (sharing) circles, illustrate how integrating movement with storytelling and knowledge sharing can enhance both physical competence and holistic health [56,57,66]. These dual-component programs align strongly with the broader intent of physical literacy. As participants build physical skills, they also deepen understanding of the interconnections between physical activity, health, and cultural knowledge.

Ethical considerations

Adapting physical literacy frameworks for Indigenous older adults is not only a practical challenge but also an ethical responsibility. Applying standardized models without modification risks marginalizing Indigenous practices and reproducing Western dominance in ways that disregard cultural sovereignty. Co-creation with communities, Elders, and Knowledge Keepers is therefore essential to ensure cultural safety and to design programs that affirm local knowledge systems rather than impose external standards. We acknowledge that some communities may choose not to engage with the physical literacy framework at all, and that genuine co-creation with Elders and Knowledge Keepers must include the possibility of reshaping or rejecting the framework entirely. In this way, physical literacy is best understood as a relational and ethical practice—one grounded in dialogue, inclusivity, and respect for diverse ways of knowing, being, and doing.

Limitations

The studies included in this exploratory review primarily used community-based participatory approaches rooted in Indigenous methodologies. Most studies engaged Indigenous older adults to explore their perspectives on aging, health, and physical activity. The community-specific nature of the studies was both a strength and a limitation. Engaging Indigenous older adults helped ensure that the research reflected the community’s knowledge, values, and traditions. However, because all Indigenous communities are unique, and the samples were small, our findings are context-specific rather than generalizable [13,50].

A second limitation was the decision to limit included studies to those reported in English. Research in other languages might have contributed additional insights. Although we aimed for global coverage, most included studies originated from Australia, New Zealand, Canada, or the United States; only two were from other regions. This concentration likely reflects both the predominance of English-language publications in the databases searched and the broader underrepresentation of Indigenous-led health research from other linguistic and geographic contexts. It may also indicate that research infrastructures and Indigenous-focused methodologies are more established in these countries, where such studies are more likely to be indexed in major databases. Future reviews incorporating non-English databases and region-specific sources could provide a more comprehensive global perspective.

Finally, to ensure the use of verifiable, peer-reviewed literature, we excluded gray literature such as theses and policy reports, which may have omitted valuable community-based evaluations or initiatives not yet captured in academic publications.

Conclusion

This exploratory review examined how physical literacy relates to ways of knowing, being, and doing among Indigenous older adults from diverse Indigenous communities, revealing areas of both overlap and tension between the two frameworks. While each emphasizes holistic growth and lifelong movement, physical literacy practice continues to prioritize individual competence and measurement, whereas Indigenous perspectives root activity in relationships, culture, and spirit. Findings from this review suggest that future work should move beyond applying physical literacy unmodified and instead engage in co-creation with Indigenous communities, allowing relational, spiritual, and land-based meanings of movement to guide its development. Future research could investigate how physical literacy might be reframed through participatory and intergenerational approaches, develop evaluation tools grounded in Indigenous epistemologies, and examine whether Indigenous-led models provide culturally resonant ways to describe movement, health, and well-being in later life.
