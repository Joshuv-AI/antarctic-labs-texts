Rao-Blackwellized e-variables
Dante de Roos, Ben Chugg, Peter Grünwald, Aaditya Ramdas

Abstract
We show that for any concave utility, the expected utility of an e-variable can only increase after conditioning on a sufficient statistic. The simplest form of the result has an extremely straightforward proof, which follows from a single application of Jensen’s inequality. Similar statements hold for compound e-variables, asymptotic e-variables, and e-processes. These results echo the Rao-Blackwell theorem, which states that the expected squared error of an estimator can only decrease after conditioning on a sufficient statistic. We provide several applications of this insight, including a simplified derivation of the log-optimal e-variable for linear regression with known variance.

Introduction

The Rao-Blackwell theorem
(
22
;
2
)
is a famous result in statistical decision theory which states that, for any convex loss function, the expected loss of an estimator can only decrease after conditioning on a sufficient statistic. The act of conditioning a random variable on a sufficient statistic has thus come to be known as “Rao-Blackwellization.”

This short article makes the observation that a Rao-Blackwell type result exists for e-variables, e-processes, and related objects. An e-variable
1

1

The realization of an e-variable is typically referred to as an e-value.

for a set of distributions
\mathcal{P}
is a
[0,\infty]
-valued random variable with expectation at most 1 under every
P\in\mathcal{P}
. E-processes,
whose precise definition is given further below,
are their sequential analogues. Together, these “e-statistics”—including their compound and asymptotic relatives—have emerged as useful tools in mathematical statistics and have garnered significant attention over the past five years
(
21
;
6
)
.

In the context of hypothesis testing, one takes
\mathcal{P}
to be the null. Given an e-variable
E
, a level-
\alpha
test is defined by rejecting the null if
E\geq 1/\alpha
, since type-I error control is guaranteed by Markov’s inequality:
P(E\geq 1/\alpha)\leq\mathbb{E}_{P}[E]\alpha\leq\alpha
for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
. The goal is thus to design e-variables for
\mathcal{P}
which are large under the alternative.

In fact, one can often construct e-variables which
tend to become
exponentially large under the alternative. This suggests maximizing this exponent,
via maximizing the expectation of

\log(E)
. This is also known as “Kelly betting,”
(
10

and is equivalent to maximizing the geometric growth rate of
E
. Indeed, e-variables which maximize expected log under the alternative are called
growth-rate optimal
(GRO) e-variables by

(
20

.

Here we prove several results pertaining to the growth of e-statistics under various utility functions:

1.

Theorem
2.1
shows that the expected utility of an e-variable can only increase under the alternative by conditioning the e-variable on a sufficient statistic. Utility can refer to any concave utility function.

2.

Theorem
2.8
strengthens this statement in the case of the log utility, allowing an e-variable
E
and its Rao-Blackwellization to be compared even when
\mathbb{E}[\log(E)]
does not exist or is infinite.

3.

Theorem

extends these results to e-processes by conditioning on a sufficient filtration. Corollaries

and

extend them to compound and asymptotic e-values.

Section
3
then uses these results to recover the the log-optimal e-variable in two scenarios: in fixed-design linear regression and when data are drawn from a Pareto distribution. Furthermore, we demonstrate that even under the very mild assumption that the data are i.i.d. Rao-Blackwellization can be used to improve the expected utility of an e-variable.

We also prove several results which may be of independent interest, such as Lemma
2.5
which gives sufficient conditions under which
\mathbb{E}[\log(E)]
is well-defined, and Lemma

which gives a general version of Jensen’s inequality for nonnegative, potentially nonintegrable, and infinite-valued random variables.

Conditioning on a sufficient statistic has occurred in various places in the e-variable literature.

Recently, for point nulls and alternatives,

Our goal here is to demonstrate that these Rao-Blackwell type results are not specific to particular applications but hold very generally.

2
Rao-Blackwell theorems for e-statistics

We consider a set of probability measures
\{P_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
on a measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
.
We assume
\Theta
is partitioned into the null
\Theta_{0}
and alternative
\Theta_{1}
. Recall that a sufficient statistic for
\Theta
is often (heuristically) defined as a measurable function
S:\Omega\to\mathcal{S}
such that the conditional distribution of the data
X\sim P_{\theta}
given
S
is independent of
\theta
, for all
\theta\in\Theta
. Formally, this means that for every measurable function
h
, there exists some measurable
g_{h}
such that
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[h(X)\mid S]=g_{h}(S)

P_{\theta}
-a.s for all
\theta\in\Theta
, whenever these conditional expectations exist.

When conditioning on a sufficient statistic, we will thus write
\mathbb{E}_{\Theta}[\cdot\mid S]
without referring to any particular parameter
\theta\in\Theta
. Conditioning on
S
should be understood as conditioning on
\sigma(S)
, the
\sigma
-field generated by
S
. More generally, we can also define the notion of a sufficient
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{F}
as a
\sigma
-field such that every conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[h(X)\mid\mathcal{G}]
,
\theta\in\Theta
, has a universal version, which we denote by
\mathbb{E}_{\Theta}[h(X)\mid\mathcal{G}]
. Although sufficient
\sigma
-fields have been the primary objects of study within the mathematical theory of sufficiency as developed by
8
;
1
, we will state and prove most of our results in terms of sufficient statistics to make them appear more gentle for a first reading. It is important to note, however, that our results are also true for sufficient
\sigma
-fields, and we will even briefly need to use this terminology in Section
2.2
when extending our theory to e-processes.

In what follows, the term
random variable
refers to a measurable function defined on
\Omega
taking its values in the extended real line, denoted by
[-\infty,\infty]
. Furthermore, we say that the expectation
\mathbb{E}[X]
of a random variable
X
is
(well-) defined (as an element of
[-\infty,\infty]
)
if
\mathbb{E}[X^{+}]
or
\mathbb{E}[X^{-}]
is finite. Here,
x^{+}:=\max(0,x)
and
x^{-}=\max(0,-x)
respectively denote the positive and negative part of
x
. This condition ensures that
\mathbb{E}[X]
can be unambiguously assigned a value in
[-\infty,\infty]
, and is even sufficient to conclude that conditional expectations
\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]
exists.
Therefore, when
S
is sufficient,
\mathbb{E}_{\Theta}[X\mid S]
is unambiguously defined (and exists) if
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[X]
is defined for all
\theta\in\Theta
. The latter is true in particular when
X
is nonnegative, which is the case when
X
is an e-variable. For details on such generalized (conditional) expectations, we refer to
23
.

Furthermore, whenever we refer to a
concave utility function
, we mean a concave function
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
, which we extend to
f:[0,\infty]\to[-\infty,\infty]
by putting
f(0):=\lim_{x\downarrow 0}f(x)
, and
f(\infty):=\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)
. This construction ensures that the random variable
f(X)
is well-defined for every nonnegative random variable
X
. Lastly, by an e-variable for
\Theta_{0}
we mean an e-variable for
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta_{0}}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
S
be a sufficient statistic for
\Theta
. Let
E
be an e-variable for
\Theta_{0}
and set
G=\mathbb{E}_{\Theta}[E\mid S]
.
Then
G
is an e-variable
and
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(G)]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(E)]
for any concave utility function
f
and any
\theta\in\Theta
, assuming both sides of this inequality are well-defined.

Proof.

By definition of sufficiency,
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[G]=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[E\mid S]]=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[E]
for any
\theta
, implying that
G
is a bonafide e-variable:

\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[G]=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[E]\leq 1.

(1)

The rest of the proof becomes embarrassingly simple after realizing that

\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(E)]=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(E)\mid S]]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[E\mid S])]=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(G)],

follows by the tower property and Jensen’s inequality under suitable integrability assumptions. Such integrability assumptions are completely redundant, however, because we show in Lemma
A.1
in Appendix
A
that the above reasoning also applies under the minimal assumption that
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(E)]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(G)]
are well-defined elements of
[-\infty,\infty]
.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

The random variable
E
need not be an e-variable in Theorem
2.1
for the inequality to hold, provided that both expectations are defined. It need only be nonnegative, as is clear from Lemma
A.1
in Appendix
A
.

Remark 2.3
.

Even though we have formulated Theorem
2.1
in such a manner that it is true for any concave utility function, its applicability (and meaning) relies on the existence of
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(E)]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(G)]
, which may depend on
f
,
\Theta
, and
E
. If, however,
f
is bounded from above or below,
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(E)]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(G)]
always exist, so that Theorem
2.1
is applicable for such utility functions. This class of utility functions, for instance, includes the power utilities
f(x)=x^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)
,
\gamma>1
, which have been studied by
16
, and a bit more generally by
11
. On the other hand, the log-utility is not bounded from above or below, and we will illustrate in Example
2.7
that Theorem
2.1
is not always applicable to this utility.

We make several comments. First, like in the original Rao-Blackwell theorem, it is important that
S
be sufficient so that
G
is computable.
Second, note that the utility improvement is for all
\theta\in\Theta
, not just for
\theta\in\Theta_{1}
. This might make us uncomfortable, but there is no paradox here. The improvement might be different for different parameters, hence there could be no change under the null but a large one under the alternative. Third, we have not assumed the existence of a common dominating measure for the family
\{P_{\theta}\}
. This is unlike the recent statement of
4
, who prove their result using the likelihood ratio between the null and alternative, thus assuming such a measure. Finally, a simple consequence of Theorem
2.1
is that when looking for expected utility optimal, in particular log-optimal (GRO), e-variables, we can restrict our search to those random variables that are functions of a sufficient statistic (if a reasonable one exists). We will come back to this in Section
3.3
.

To make things concrete, let us give a brief example of using Rao-Blackwellization to strictly improve the expected utility of a naive e-variable. A common example in the original Rao-Blackwell setting is to consider an estimator which only uses the first observation. We follow in this tradition.

Example 2.4
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be i.i.d.
\mathrm{Ber}(p)
with parameter
\theta=p\in(0,1)
.
We test the simple null
\Theta_{0}=\{p_{0}\}
for some fixed
p_{0}\in(0,1)

against some alternative
\Theta_{1}
.
A sufficient statistic for
p
is the total number of successes
S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
.
Consider the following naive e-variable which only looks at the first observation:

E:=\exp\bigl\{\lambda X_{1}-\psi(\lambda)\bigr\},

where
\lambda>0
is fixed and
\psi(\lambda):=\log\bigl(p_{0}e^{\lambda}+(1-p_{0})\bigr).

By construction,
\mathbb{E}_{p_{0}}[E]=1
. Define
G(S)=\mathbb{E}_{\Theta}[E\mid S]
, the Rao-Blackwellization of
E
.

Given
S=k
, exchangeability under
p\in(0,1)

implies that the conditional distribution of
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
is uniform over all

\binom{n}{k}
permutations, and therefore
P_{p}(X_{1}=1\mid S=k)=\frac{k}{n}
, and

P_{p}(X_{1}=0\mid S=k)=1-\frac{k}{n}.

Hence

\displaystyle G(S)

\displaystyle=e^{-\psi(\lambda)}\left(\frac{S}{n}e^{\lambda}+\bigg(1-\frac{S}{n}\bigg)\right)=e^{-\psi(\lambda)}\left[1+(e^{\lambda}-1)\frac{S}{n}\right].

Clearly
\mathbb{E}_{p_{0}}[G(S)]=1
.
Consider any strictly concave function
f
on
(0,\infty)
.
We claim that
\mathbb{E}_{p}[f(G)]>\mathbb{E}_{p}[f(E)]
for all
p\in(0,1)
. Note that for any
k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
,
\mathbb{E}_{p}[f(E)\mid S=k]<f(\mathbb{E}_{p}[E\mid S=k])=f(G(k))
, using the strict concavity of
f
. Meanwhile, for
k\in\{0,n\}
,
\mathbb{E}_{p}[f(E)\mid S=k]=f(G(k))
. Since
P_{p}(S\neq 0,n)=1-p^{n}-(1-p)^{n}>0
, it follows that

\mathbb{E}_{p}[f(G)]-\mathbb{E}_{p}[f(E)]>0,

demonstrating strict utility improvement under the alternative.

We emphasize that Rao-Blackwellization is not guaranteed to find the optimal e-variable. For example, for a point null and alternative
\Theta_{0}=\{p_{0}\}
,
\Theta=\{p_{1}\}
, the GRO e-variable for
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\sim\text{Ber}(p)
is the likelihood ratio
L_{n}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{1}(X_{i})/p_{0}(X_{i})
. But the Rao-Blackwellized e-variable
G(S)
in Example
2.4
is not equal to
L_{n}
for any choice of
\lambda
.

2.1
The log-utility

The requirement that
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(E)]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(G)]
are well-defined in Theorem
2.1
is, of course, not always met. (See example
2.7
below.) But Rao-Blackwellization in such cases may still lead to sensible results. This section
focuses on the log-utility in particular, demonstrating how in this case we can
weaken the assumptions on the existence of
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(E)]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(G)]
in Theorem
2.1
and still conclude that
G
is an improvement over
E
.

We begin by giving sufficient conditions under which
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\log(E)]
exists for every e-variable
E
, thereby ensuring that Theorem
2.1
is meaningful. We then present an example where Theorem
2.1
cannot be applied because
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\log(E)]
does not exist. This motivates Theorem
2.8
, a generalization of Theorem
2.1
(in the case of
f=\log
) that allows us to compare the expected log-utility of
E
with that of its Rao–Blackwellization
G
even when
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\log(E)]
or
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\log(G)]
might not be defined.

The following lemma roughly shows that under a weak condition on the KL-divergence
(
13
)
between the alternative distribution and the null all e-variables are comparable in the sense of Theorem
2.1
. Recall that the KL-divergence between two (finite) measures
\nu
and
\mu
is defined as

\displaystyle D_{\text{KL}}(\mu\|\nu):=\begin{cases}\int\log\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}\right)d\nu,&\text{if}\quad\nu\ll\mu,\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\nu\ll\mu
means that
\nu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu
.

D_{\text{KL}}(\cdot\|\cdot)
is always well-defined as an element of
[0,\infty]
. The following lemma also relies on the notion of the
effective null hypothesis
, denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{\text{eff}}

(
16
)
, which is defined as

\mathcal{P}_{\text{eff}}:=\{P\in M_{+}\mid\mathbb{E}_{P}[E]\leq 1\text{ for all }E\in\mathcal{E}\},

(2)

where
M_{+}
denotes the set of (nonnegative) measures on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
, and
\mathcal{E}
the set of e-variables for
\mathcal{P}
. This effective null hypothesis, consisting only of (sub-) probability measures, is the largest extension of the null hypothesis
\mathcal{P}
for which
\mathcal{E}
is still the class of all e-variables. It includes any (
\sigma
-) convex combination of elements of the null, and also all Bayes marginals
P_{W}
with
W
a prior on
\Theta_{0}
, when this notion is defined.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\theta\in\Theta
. If
\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{eff}}}D_{\text{KL}}(P_{\theta}\|P)<\infty
, then
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\log(E)]
is defined for every e-variable
E
.

Proof.

By definition of KL-divergences, because
\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{eff}}}D_{\text{KL}}(P_{\theta}\|P)<\infty
, there is at least one element
P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{eff}}
that dominates
P_{\theta}
. In fact, one can, then, take
P^{\prime}
to be the reverse information projection (RIPr) (as defined in
16
) of
P_{\theta}
onto
\mathcal{P}_{\text{eff}}
, a sub-probability measure which additionally satisfies
P^{\prime}\ll P_{\theta}
when
\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{eff}}}D_{\text{KL}}(P_{\theta}\|P)<\infty
, and achieves the infimum
D_{\text{KL}}(P_{\theta}\|P^{\prime})=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{eff}}}D_{\text{KL}}(P_{\theta}\|P)
. (For details, we refer to
16
.)

Now, let
E
be an
e
-variable, and define the (sub-) probability measure
R
via the relation
dR=EdP^{\prime}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\log(E)]=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\log\frac{dR}{dP^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\log\frac{dP_{\theta}}{dP^{\prime}}+\log\frac{dR}{dP_{\theta}}\right]=D_{\text{KL}}(P_{\theta}\|P^{\prime})+\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\log\frac{dR}{dP_{\theta}}\right].

Since
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[dR/dP_{\theta}]\leq 1
, Jensen’s inequality implies that
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\log(dR/dP_{\theta})]\in[-\infty,0]
. This, combined with the fact that
D_{\text{KL}}(P_{\theta}\|P^{\prime})<\infty
, implies that

-\infty\leq\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\log(E)]\leq D_{\text{KL}}(P_{\theta}\|P^{\prime})<\infty,

and
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\log(E)]
is therefore defined.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

The condition
D_{\text{KL}}(P_{\theta}\|\mathcal{P}_{\text{eff}})<\infty
can easily be verified by constructing a mixture of elements of the null
P^{\prime}
, and checking whether
D_{\text{KL}}(P_{\theta}\|P^{\prime})<\infty
. In most practical situations, this will be the viable strategy, as precisely identifying the effective null is often too difficult. The reason we formulated the above lemma using
\mathcal{P}_{\text{eff}}
is that it is the most general formulation we consider possible, and it may potentially cover some corner cases.

When the conditions of Lemma
2.5
are not met,
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\log(E)]
cannot be guaranteed to exist. Consider the following example.

Example 2.7
.

Consider the simple versus simple testing problem
X\sim P_{\theta_{0}}=N(0,1)
versus
X\sim P_{\theta_{1}}=\Cauchy(0,1)
. As both
P_{\theta_{0}}
and
P_{\theta_{1}}
are symmetric about zero, the statistic
S=|X|
is sufficient for
\Theta=\{\theta_{0},\theta_{1}\}
, and for any
h
we have that
\mathbb{E}_{\Theta}[h(X)\mid S]=(h(X)+h(-X))/2
. The statistic
E=\exp(X-1/2)
is an e-variable, and so is
G=\mathbb{E}_{\Theta}[\exp(X-1/2)\mid S]=(\exp(X-1/2)+\exp(-X-1/2))/2
by Theorem
2.1
. Here,
\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[\log(E)]=\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[X-1/2]
, which is not defined, as
\mathbb{E}[X^{+}]=\mathbb{E}[X^{-}]=\infty
. On the other hand,
\log(G)
is bounded from below, so
\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[\log(G)]
is defined as an element of
(-\infty,\infty]
; in fact,
\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[\log(G)]=\infty
. Therefore,
E
and
G
are not comparable in the sense of Theorem
2.1
.

Faced with Example
2.7
, we next give a stronger result than Theorem
2.1
which enables us to compare
G
and
E
even when
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\log(E)]
does not exist.
Following
14
and
16
, we formulate the improvement of
G
over
E
in terms of their ratio. We use the conventions
0/0=0
and
\infty/\infty=1
.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
E
be an e-variable for
\Theta_{0}
, and
S
a sufficient statistic for
\Theta
. Set
G=\mathbb{E}_{\Theta}[E\mid S]
. Then for all
\theta\in\Theta
,
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[E/G]\leq 1
, and therefore
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\log(E/G)]\leq 0
.

Proof.

Fix
\theta\in\Theta
. Because
E/G
is nonnegative, the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[E/G\mid S]
is defined. Then,
P_{\theta}
-almost surely,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\frac{E}{G}\ \middle|\ S\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\frac{E}{G}\mathbf{1}{\{G<\infty\}}\ \middle|\ S\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\frac{E}{G}\mathbf{1}{\{G=\infty\}}\ \middle|\ S\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\frac{E}{G}\mathbf{1}{\{G<\infty\}}\ \middle|\ S\right]+\mathbf{1}{\{G=\infty\}},

because the conventions
0/0=0
and
\infty/\infty=1
ensure that
E/G\leq 1
on the
\sigma(S)
-measurable set
\{G=\infty\}
. Now, as
G=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[E\mid S]

P_{\theta}
-a.s, it follows that
E=0

P_{\theta}
-a.s on the set
\{G=0\}
. Thus, we may write

\frac{E}{G}\mathbf{1}{\{G<\infty\}}=\left(\frac{\mathbf{1}{\{0<G<\infty\}}}{G}\right)E\quad P_{\theta}\text{-a.s,}

so that the product rule for (general) conditional expectations implies that
P_{\theta}
-a.s,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\frac{E}{G}\mathbf{1}{\{G<\infty\}}\ \middle|\ S\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\mathbf{1}{\{0<G<\infty\}}}{G}\right)\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[E\mid S]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\mathbf{1}{\{0<G<\infty\}}}{G}\right)G=\mathbf{1}{\{0<G<\infty\}}.

Whence,

\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\frac{E}{G}\ \middle|\ S\right]\leq\mathbf{1}{\{0<G<\infty\}}+\mathbf{1}{\{G=\infty\}}\leq 1\quad P_{\theta}\text{-a.s}.

The inequalities
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[E/G\right]\leq 1
and
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\log(E/G)\right]\leq 0

then follow by the tower property and Jensen’s inequality, completing the proof.
∎

We note that Remark
2.2
also applies to Theorem
2.8
. That is,
E
need not be an e-variable; just a nonnegative random variable. Next let us see how the above result overcomes Example
2.7
.

Example 2.9
.

Continuing with Example
2.7
, note that
E/G=2(1+\exp(-2X))^{-1}
. For all
X
,
1+\exp(-2X)\geq 1
so
\log(E/G)\leq\log(2)
. Meanwhile, we also have
1+\exp(-2X)\geq\exp(-2X)
, so
\log(E/G)\leq\log(2)+2X
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[\log(E/G)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[\log(E/G)\mathbf{1}\{X\leq 0\}]+\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[\log(E/G)\mathbf{1}\{X\geq 0\}]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{\log(2)+2x}{\pi(1+x^{2})}\;\textnormal{d}x+\log(2)=-\infty.

That is, Theorem
2.8
still allows
E
and
G
to be compared, even though Theorem
2.1
does not.

2.2
Extension to e-processes

To move to the sequential setting and extend the result to e-processes, we assume that the measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
admits a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. An e-process for
\Theta_{0}
with respect to
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
is a sequence
(E_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of nonnegative random variables such that
E_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable for all
t\geq 0
and
E_{\tau}
is an e-variable for
\Theta_{0}
at every
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-stopping time
\tau
. Recall that
\tau
is a
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-stopping time if the event

.

Let

2.3
Extension to compound and asymptotic e-variables

Next let us note that our results extend easily to compound and asymptotic e-variables
(
9
)
.
Compound e-variables are an important tool in multiple testing; in fact, any procedure which controls the false discovery rate can be recovered (or improved) using the e-BH procedure with some set of compound e-variables
(
9
, Theorem 4.4)
.

Consider
K
(not necessarily disjoint) null hypotheses
\Theta_{0}^{1},\dots,\Theta_{0}^{K}
and set
\Theta_{0}=\cup_{j}\Theta_{0}^{j}
. A
compound
e-variable for
\Theta_{0}^{1},\dots,\Theta_{0}^{K}
is a tuple of nonnegative random variables
(E_{1},\dots,E_{K})
such that

\sum_{k:\theta\in\Theta_{0}^{k}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[E_{k}]\leq K,\quad\text{for all }\theta\in\Theta_{0}.

(3)

For
j=1,\dots,K
, let
S_{j}
be a sufficient statistic for
\Theta_{j}\equiv\Theta_{0}^{j}\cup\Theta_{1}
and define
G_{j}=\mathbb{E}_{\Theta_{j}}[E_{j}\mid S_{j}]
. We again use the notation
\mathbb{E}_{\Theta_{j}}[\cdot\mid S_{j}]
since the quantity is independent of
\theta
by virtue of
S_{j}
being sufficient.
Since
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[G_{j}]=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[E_{j}]
for any
\theta\in\Theta_{j}
, (
3
) holds for
G_{1},\dots,G_{K}
, meaning the collection is a compound e-variable. Further,
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(G_{j})]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(E_{j})]
for all
\theta\in\Theta_{1}\cup\Theta_{0}^{j}
and concave utility functions
f
by Theorem
2.1
and we can likewise apply Theorem
2.8
. We summarize as follows.

Corollary 2.12
.

Let
(E_{1},\dots,E_{K})
be a compound e-variable for the null hypotheses
\Theta_{0}^{1},\dots,\Theta_{0}^{K}
. For
j=1,\dots,K
, let
S_{j}
be a sufficient statistic for
\Theta_{0}^{j}\cup\Theta_{1}
and define
G_{j}=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[E_{j}\mid S_{j}]
. Then
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(G_{j})]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(E_{j})]
for any concave
f
and
\theta\in\Theta_{1}\cup\Theta_{0}^{j}
, and

\sum_{k:\theta\in\Theta_{0}^{k}\cup\Theta_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{\phi}[f(G_{j})]\geq\sum_{k:\theta\in\Theta_{0}^{k}\cup\Theta_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{\phi}[f(E_{j})],\textnormal{~ for all }\theta\in\Theta_{0}\cup\Theta_{1},

(4)

assuming all expectations are well-defined. Moreover,
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\log(E_{j}/G_{j})]\leq 0
for all
j
and
\theta\in\Theta_{0}^{j}\cup\Theta_{1}
.

While this result is reminiscent of the “conditional calibration” approach of
17
for improving e-BH, the target of the conditioning is different. In our case, we Rao–Blackwellize the (compound) e-variables themselves with respect to a sufficient statistic. By contrast,
17
do not condition the e-variables directly. Instead, for each null they condition the per-hypothesis FDR contribution on a (null-)sufficient statistic and then define boosted e-variables indirectly via this conditionally calibrated contribution.

Finally, we turn to asymptotic e-variables, also introduced by
9
.
An asymptotic e-variable is a sequence of nonnegative random variables
(E_{n})
such that
\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{0}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[E_{n}]\leq 1
. A
uniform
asymptotic e-variable is a sequence
(E_{n})
such that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[E_{n}]\leq 1
. The latter condition is much stronger than the former as the limit must hold uniformly over
\Theta_{0}
.

As in Section
2.2
we assume that
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
admits a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
.
Let
(S_{n})
be a sequence of sufficient statistics, where
S_{n}
is sufficient for

\Theta
with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{n}
. Applying Theorem
2.1
and
2.8
to each
E_{n}
(and recalling that the concave
improvement does not require
E_{n}
to be an e-variable; see Remark
2.2
) yields the following result.

Corollary 2.13
.

Let
(S_{n})
be a sequence of sufficient statistics for
\Theta
on
(\Omega,(\mathcal{F}_{n}))
. Let
(E_{n})
be a (uniform) asymptotic e-variable.
For each
n
, set
G_{n}=\mathbb{E}_{\Theta}[E_{n}\mid S_{n}]
. Then (i)
(G_{n})
is a (uniform) asymptotic e-variable and (ii)
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(G_{n})]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[f(E_{n})]
for all concave
f
, assuming both expectations are defined. Moreover,
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\log(E_{n}/G_{n})]\leq 0
for all
n
and
\theta\in\Theta
.

3
Applications

In this section, we present three applications of
Theorems
2.1
and
2.8
. The first two applications illustrate how Rao-Blackwellization can be used to find both GRO and GROW e-variables
(
6
)
. While we have discussed these concepts informally, let us define them here.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the set of e-variables for the null hypothesis
\Theta_{0}
.
For a point alternative
\Theta_{1}=\{\theta_{1}\}
, the GRO e-variable is that which maximizes the expected log-utility under the alternative:

\sup_{E\in\mathcal{E}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[\log(E)].

When the alternative is composite one needs to decide how to compare e-variables under parameters
\theta_{1}\in\Theta_{1}
. One option is to maximize log-utility in the worst case. This leads to the GROW e-variable, which attains the worst case log-utility:

\sup_{E\in\mathcal{E}}\inf_{\theta_{1}\in\Theta_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[\log(E)].

Section
3.1
will use Rao-Blackwellization to recover the GRO e-variable in a linear regression setting, and Section
3.2
will recover the GROW e-variable under heavy-tailed data. Finally, in Section
3.3
, we will show how any e-variable for i.i.d. data can be improved by taking a weighted mean of the e-variable recalculated on permuted data.

3.1
GRO e-variable for linear regression

Consider a standard fixed-design setup in which
Y\sim N(X\theta,\sigma^{2}I_{n})
where
Y=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
and
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
is fixed.
We assume that
\sigma^{2}
is known. Write
\theta=(\theta_{a},\theta_{b})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k-d}
.
Suppose we are interested in testing whether
\theta_{a}=0
, where we treat
\theta_{b}
as nuisance parameters. In particular, our null is whether
\theta=(0,\theta_{b})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k-d}
for some
\theta_{b}
and the alternative is whether
\theta=\theta^{*}
for some fixed
\theta^{*}=(\theta_{a}^{*},\theta_{b}^{*})
with
\theta_{a}^{*}\neq 0
. That is,

\Theta_{0}=\{(0,\theta_{b})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k-d}\},\quad\Theta_{1}=\{\theta^{*}\}.

(5)

Let
\widehat{\theta}=(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}Y
be the MLE. It is well-known that
\widehat{\theta}
is a sufficient statistic for
\Theta_{0}\cup\Theta_{1}
if
X
is full-rank.
Now, under
\Theta_{0}
, we have
\widehat{\theta}\sim N(\theta,\sigma^{2}(X^{\top}X)^{-1})
where
\theta=(0,\theta_{b})
and under
\Theta_{1}
,
\widehat{\theta}\sim N(\theta^{*},\sigma^{2}(X^{\top}X)^{-1})
. In other words, examining the distribution of
\widehat{\theta}
induces a new hypothesis test, namely that of testing a Gaussian location family null against a Gaussian alternative with the same variance. Let us call this the “reduced experiment,” to be contrasted with the “original experiment.”

Let
E=E(Y)
be an e-variable in the original experiment. By Theorem
2.1
, we may assume that
E
is a function of
\widehat{\theta}
. Hence
E(Y)=f(\widehat{\theta}(Y))
for some measurable
f
. That is, there is a bijection between these e-variables in the original experiment with e-variables in the reduced experiment: an e-variable
H(\widehat{\theta})
in the reduced is identified with
E(Y)=H(\widehat{\theta}(Y))
in the original.

Using known results on e-variables for exponential families
(
7
, Section 4.1.1)
, we can conclude that the log-optimal e-variable for the reduced problem is

H(\widehat{\theta})=\frac{\rho(\widehat{\theta};\theta^{*},\sigma^{2}(X^{\top}X)^{-1})}{\rho(\widehat{\theta};\overline{\theta},\sigma^{2}(X^{\top}X)^{-1})},

where
\rho(\cdot;m,\Sigma)
is the Gaussian density with mean
m
and covariance
\Sigma
and
\overline{\theta}
is a particular element of
\Theta_{0}
.
The GRO e-variable for the original problem is therefore

E(Y)=H(\widehat{\theta}(Y)).

For the interested reader, we spell out this example in a bit more detail in Appendix
B.1
.

It is important to note that we have not recovered the results of
7
on linear regression, because we assume that the variance is fixed instead of it being a free parameter.

3.2
GROW e-variable for Pareto data

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be i.i.d.
\Par(m,\alpha)
,
m\in(0,\infty)
,
\alpha>0
. That is,
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
has the joint Lebesgue density
p_{m,\alpha}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, given by

\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\alpha m^{\alpha}}{x_{i}^{\alpha+1}}\mathbf{1}\{x_{i}\geq m\}=\alpha^{n}m^{n\alpha}\exp\left(-(\alpha+1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{x_{i}}{x_{(1)}}\right)\mathbf{1}\{x_{(1)}\geq m\},

where
x_{(1)}:=\min(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. By the Neyman factorization criterion for sufficiency, the statistic
(X_{(1)},\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(X_{i}/X_{(1)}))
is sufficient for
(m,\alpha)
. Let the null
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha_{0}}
be given by
H_{0}:\alpha=\alpha_{0}
, and the alternative
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha_{1}}
by
H_{1}:\alpha=\alpha_{1}
. A straightforward computation shows that for any
P_{m,\alpha_{1}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\alpha_{1}}
,
D_{\text{KL}}(P_{m,\alpha_{1}}\|P_{m,\alpha_{0}})<\infty
, so by Lemma
2.5
we find that the GROW criterion is well-defined for this problem. Furthermore, Theorem
2.1
yields

\sup_{E\in\mathcal{E}}\inf_{m\in(0,\infty)}\mathbb{E}_{m,\alpha_{1}}[\log(E)]=\sup_{E\in\mathcal{E^{\prime}}}\inf_{m\in(0,\infty)}\mathbb{E}_{m,\alpha_{1}}[\log(E)],

where
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
is the set of all e-variables that can be written as a function of the sufficient statistic.
18
shows that

\left(X_{(1)},\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{X_{i}}{X_{(1)}}\right)\sim\Par(m,n\alpha)\otimes\Gamma(n-1,\alpha),

where
\Gamma(n-1,\alpha)
denotes the Gamma distribution with shape parameter
n-1
and rate parameter
\alpha
. Consequently, the statistic
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(X_{i}/X_{(1)})
has the same distribution under all elements of
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha_{0}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha_{1}}
, respectively, and its likelihood ratio is given by

E^{*}:=\frac{p_{\alpha_{1}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{X_{i}}{X_{(1)}}\right)}{p_{\alpha_{0}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{X_{i}}{X_{(1)}}\right)}=\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{0}}\right)^{n-1}\exp\left(-(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{0})\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{X_{i}}{X_{(1)}}\right),

and is an e-variable. This e-variable is indeed an element of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
, and an intuitive candidate for being a GROW e-variable, as the distribution of the entire sufficient statistic depends on the nuisance parameter only via
X_{(1)}
. In fact, it can directly be shown to be GROW under a very weak additional assumption, namely that
n\alpha_{1}>1
. The steps are tedious, and are therefore delegated to Appendix
B.2

3.3
Permutation-based e-variables

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
be a sequence of data that is i.i.d.–or more general, exchangeable–under both the null and alternative. The order statistics
S=(X_{(1)},\ldots,X_{(n)})
can be shown the be sufficient, and

\mathbb{E}_{\Theta}[f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\mid S]=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi}f(X_{\pi(1)},\ldots,X_{\pi(n)}),

where
\pi
runs over the set of permutations on
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Thus, according to Theorems
2.1
and
2.8
, the e-variable
E(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
can be improved in terms of expected concave utility by taking its Rao-Blackwellization

G=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi}E(X_{\pi(1)},\ldots,X_{\pi(n)})

instead.

Whenever
E
is invariant under taking permutations of the data,
G
and
E
will coincide, meaning that nothing can be gained in terms of expected utility. This does, however, indicate that GRO e-variables for testing i.i.d. data are invariant under taking permutations of the data, because GRO e-variables are essentially unique.
Thus, when assessing whether an e-variable is GRO for i.i.d. data, it is almost always wise to verify that it is permutation invariant, if only for the sake of a sanity check.

At first glance, this setting and result might appear to be oddly reminiscent of earlier work on testing for exchangeability by
15
;
12
. However, our situation is different because we assume that the data are i.i.d. (exchangeable) under both the null and alternative hypotheses, while the referenced authors demonstrate how to test this assumption using e-variables. Our result is closer to
21
.

4
Summary

We have investigated how the theory of Rao-Blackwellization applies to e-statistics, proving simple yet powerful results demonstrating how conditioning on a sufficient statistic can improve the expected utility of e-values and e-processes for any concave utility function. We gave several examples demonstrating how our results help recover optimal e-variables, and we hope that they prove useful for e-value methodology in the future.

Appendix A
A general version of Jensen’s inequality

In this section, we present and prove a general version of Jensen’s inequality for nonnegative (and potentially nonintegrable) random variables
X:\Omega\to[0,\infty]
. A similar result for nonnegative concave utility functions has already been established in
24
. However, since we are working with general concave utility functions in this paper, we must generalize this result to its fullest extent.

We consider the concave function
f
to be defined as a function from
(0,\infty)
to
\mathbb{R}
, and we extend the domain of
f
to
[0,\infty]
by defining
f(0):=\lim_{x\downarrow 0}f(x)
and
f(\infty):=\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)
, which exist as elements of
[-\infty,\infty]
. Because
f
is continuous on
(0,\infty)
, this definition ensures that
f
is also continuous as a function from
[0,\infty]
to
[-\infty,\infty]
, provided that we endow
[0,\infty]
and
[-\infty,\infty]
with their respective order topologies. (The latter ensures that we can always take limits inside
f
during the proof of the following lemma, which we will do without further comment.)

Recall that the expectation of a random variable
Y:\Omega\to[-\infty,\infty]
is defined if
\min(\mathbb{E}[Y^{+}],\mathbb{E}[Y^{-}])<\infty
, where
x^{+}=\max(0,x)
, and
x^{-}=\max(0,-x)
respectively denote the positive and negative parts of
x
. This condition ensures that
\mathbb{E}[Y]:=\mathbb{E}[Y^{+}]-\mathbb{E}[Y^{-}]
is well-defined as an element of
[-\infty,\infty]
. Furthermore, conditional expectations can, then, also be defined as
\mathbb{E}[Y\mid\mathcal{G}]:=\mathbb{E}[Y^{+}\mid\mathcal{G}]-\mathbb{E}[Y^{-}\mid\mathcal{G}]
, which almost surely takes its values in
[-\infty,\infty]
. For a full treatment on general (conditional) expectation, we refer to
23
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
X
be a nonnegative random variable, and
f
a concave function as described above. If
\mathbb{E}[f(X)]
is defined, then for any
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{F}
,

\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid\mathcal{G}]\leq f(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}])\quad\text{a.s.}

Additionally, if
\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}])]
is also defined, then
\mathbb{E}[f(X)]\leq\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}])]
.

Proof.

We first assume that
\mathbb{E}[f(X)^{+}]<\infty
. Define, for
0<\varepsilon<\delta

X^{\varepsilon}:=X+\varepsilon,\quad X_{\delta}:=X\wedge\delta,\text{ and}\quad X_{\delta}^{\varepsilon}:=(X+\varepsilon)\wedge\delta,

and note that

X^{\varepsilon}\downarrow X\text{ as $\varepsilon\downarrow 0$,}\quad X_{\delta}\uparrow X\text{ as $\delta\to\infty$,}\quad X_{\delta}^{\varepsilon}\downarrow X_{\delta}\text{ as $\varepsilon\downarrow 0$, and}\quad X_{\delta}^{\varepsilon}\uparrow X^{\varepsilon}\text{ as $\delta\to\infty$.}

The random variable
X_{\delta}^{\varepsilon}
takes its values in the compact set
[\varepsilon,\delta]
, on which
f
has a minimum and a maximum. Thus, both
X_{\delta}^{\varepsilon}
and
f(X_{\delta}^{\varepsilon})
are bounded, and Jensen’s inequality for integrable random variables yields

\mathbb{E}[f(X_{\delta}^{\varepsilon})\mid\mathcal{G}]\leq f(\mathbb{E}[X_{\delta}^{\varepsilon}\mid\mathcal{G}])\quad\text{a.s.}

(6)

The desired result follows by letting
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, and
\delta\to\infty
, in conjunction with appropriate limit theorems for conditional expectations. To that end, note that either one of the following three situations is the case:

1.

\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)\in(-\infty,\infty]
;

2.

\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=-\infty
, and
\lim_{x\downarrow 0}f(x)=-\infty
;

3.

\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=-\infty
, and
\lim_{x\downarrow 0}f(x)\in\mathbb{R}
.

We treat them seperately.

1.

In the first situation, the function
f
is monotonically nondecreasing. Thus,
f(X_{\delta}^{\varepsilon})
and
X_{\delta}^{\varepsilon}
are bounded from below for fixed
\varepsilon
. Since
f(X_{\delta}^{\varepsilon})\uparrow f(X^{\varepsilon})
and
X_{\delta}^{\varepsilon}\uparrow X^{\varepsilon}
as
\delta\to\infty
, the monotone convergence theorem for (general) conditional expectations
(
23
, Chapter 2.7.4; Theorem 2)
yields

\lim_{\delta\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{\delta}^{\varepsilon})\mid\mathcal{G}]=\mathbb{E}[f(X^{\varepsilon})\mid\mathcal{G}],\text{ and}\quad\lim_{\delta\to\infty}f(\mathbb{E}[X_{\delta}^{\varepsilon}\mid\mathcal{G}])=f(\mathbb{E}[X^{\varepsilon}\mid\mathcal{G}])\quad\text{a.s.}

By the monotonicity of
f
, it follows that for
0<\varepsilon<\eta
,
f(x+\varepsilon)\leq f(x+\eta)
, and by the concavity of
f
, there exists a constant
M(\eta)>0
, such that
f(x+\eta)^{+}\leq f(x)^{+}+M(\eta)
for all
x
. Thus,
f(X^{\varepsilon})\leq f(X^{\eta})\leq f(X)^{+}+M(\eta)
, so the family
(f(X^{\varepsilon}))_{0<\varepsilon\leq\eta}
is bounded from above by an integrable random variable. Since
f(X^{\varepsilon})\downarrow f(X)
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, the monotone convergence theorem again yields

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}[f(X^{\varepsilon})\mid\mathcal{G}]=\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid\mathcal{G}]\quad\text{a.s,}

and

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}f(\mathbb{E}[X^{\varepsilon}\mid\mathcal{G}])=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}f(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]+\varepsilon)=f(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}])\quad\text{a.s,}

follows directly. By combining all of these limit results with (
6
), we find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid\mathcal{G}]=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\lim_{\delta\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{\delta}^{\varepsilon})\mid\mathcal{G}]\leq\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\lim_{\delta\to\infty}f(\mathbb{E}[X_{\delta}^{\varepsilon}\mid\mathcal{G}])=f(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}])\quad\text{a.s,}

(7)

as desired.

2.

In the second situation,
f
is bounded from above. Furthermore,
f(x)
decreases eventually as
x\to\infty
, and as
x\downarrow 0
, so we may apply the monotone convergence theorem to (
6
) twice (in any order) to obtain (
7
).

3.

In the third situation,
f
is again bounded from above, but it need not be true anymore that
f(x)
eventually decreases as
x\downarrow 0
. However, because
f(x)
converges to a finite limit as
x\downarrow 0
, it follows that the family
(f(X_{\delta}^{\varepsilon}))_{0<\varepsilon\leq\eta}
is uniformly bounded. This is also true for
(X_{\delta}^{\varepsilon})_{0<\varepsilon\leq\eta}
. Whence, by the dominated convergence theorem
(
23
, Chapter 2.7.4; Theorem 2)
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{\delta}^{\varepsilon})\mid\mathcal{G}]=\mathbb{E}[f(X_{\delta})\mid\mathcal{G}],\text{ and}\quad\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{\delta}^{\varepsilon})\mid\mathcal{G}]=f(\mathbb{E}[X_{\delta}\mid\mathcal{G}])\quad\text{a.s.}

Now, by letting
\delta\to\infty
and applying the monotone convergence theorem in a similar fashion as in the second situation, we again obtain (
7
).

Suppose that
\mathbb{E}[f(X)^{-}]<\infty
. Then, the function
g_{n}(x):=f(x)\wedge n
is bounded from above and concave. In particular,
\mathbb{E}[g_{n}(X)^{+}]<\infty
, so

\mathbb{E}[g_{n}(X)\mid\mathcal{G}]\leq g_{n}(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}])\quad\text{a.s.}

Clearly,
g_{n}(x)\uparrow f(x)
as
n\to\infty
, and because
g_{n}(X)
is bounded from below by the integrable random variable
-f(X)^{-}
, the monotone convergence theorem yields

\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid\mathcal{G}]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[g_{n}(X)\mid\mathcal{G}]\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}g_{n}(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}])=f(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}])\quad\text{a.s,}

which is what we wanted to show.

The final assertion follows from the tower property for general conditional expectations
(
23
, Chapter 2.7.4)
:

\mathbb{E}[f(X)]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[f(X)\mid\mathcal{G}]]\leq\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}])],

provided that the latter expectation is also defined.
∎

B.1

The original experiment can be phrased in terms of null and alternative hypotheses
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
as follows:

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle:Y\sim N(X\theta,\sigma^{2}I_{n}),\quad\theta\in\Theta_{0}=\{(0,\theta_{b})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{k-d}\},

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle:Y\sim N(X\theta,\sigma^{2}I_{n}),\quad\theta\in\Theta_{1}=\{\theta^{*}\}.

If
X
is full-rank, then the MLE
\widehat{\theta}=\widehat{\theta}(Y)=(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}Y
is a sufficient statistic for
\Theta=\Theta_{0}\cup\Theta_{1}
. Examining the distribution of
\widehat{\theta}
under
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
induces a new null and alternative hypothesis
H_{0}^{*}
and
H_{1}^{*}
:

\displaystyle H_{0}^{*}

\displaystyle:\widehat{\theta}\sim N(\theta,\sigma^{2}(X^{\top}X)^{-1}),\quad\theta\in\Theta_{0}

\displaystyle H_{1}^{*}

\displaystyle:\widehat{\theta}\sim N(\theta,\sigma^{2}(X^{\top}X)^{-1}),\quad\theta\in\Theta_{1}.

This is the “reduced” experiment as described in Section
3.1
, which is equivalent to testing a Gaussian location family against a Gaussian distribution with a different mean but the same covariance.
7
show that GRO e-variable for this problem takes the form of a simple versus simple likelihood ratio. In particular, the GRO e-variable for the reduced experiment is

H(\widehat{\theta})=\frac{\rho(\widehat{\theta};\theta^{*},\sigma^{2}(X^{\top}X)^{-1})}{\rho(\widehat{\theta};\overline{\theta},\sigma^{2}(X^{\top}X)^{-1})},

where
\rho(\cdot;m,\Sigma)
is the Gaussian density with mean
m
and covariance
\Sigma
and

\displaystyle\overline{\theta}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta_{0}}D_{\text{KL}}(N(\theta^{*},\sigma^{2}(X^{\top}X)^{-1})\|N(\theta,\sigma^{2}(X^{\top}X)^{-1}))

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta_{0}}(\theta^{*}-\theta)^{\top}\sigma^{-2}X^{\top}X(\theta^{*}-\theta).

Now, Theorem
2.8
implies that when searching for a GRO e-variable
E(Y)
in the original experiment
H_{0}
vs
H_{1}
, we may restrict our attention to those e-variables which are functions of
\widehat{\theta}
. But there is a one-to-one correspondence between such e-variables in the original experiment, and e-variables
H
in the reduced experiment (which are already functions of
\widehat{\theta}
). Indeed, the relationship is simply given by the mapping
E(Y)=H(\widehat{\theta}(Y))
. The optimization problem for the GRO e-variable in both problems is thus identical up to this mapping, and we can conclude
that the GRO e-variable in the original experiment is given by
H(\widehat{\theta}(Y))
.

B.2
Section
3.2

Because the GROW criterion can be taken over the set of e-variables that can be written as a function of the sufficient statistic, the original testing problem

\displaystyle H_{0}:X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\Par(m,\alpha_{0}),\ m>0,\quad H_{1}:X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\Par(m,\alpha_{1}),\ m>0,

can be reduced to

\displaystyle H_{0}^{*}:\left(X_{(1)},\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{X_{i}}{X_{(1)}}\right)\sim\Par(m,n\alpha_{0})\otimes\Gamma(n-1,\alpha_{0}),\ m>0,

\displaystyle\quad H_{1}^{*}:\left(X_{(1)},\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{X_{i}}{X_{(1)}}\right)\sim\Par(m,n\alpha_{1})\otimes\Gamma(n-1,\alpha_{1}),\ m>0,

because the set of e-variables for the latter null hypothesis is precisely the set
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
of e-variables for the former null hypothesis that can be written as a function of the sufficient statistic. Consequently, the GROW e-variable for
H_{0}^{*}
versus
H_{1}^{*}
will be the GROW e-variable for
H_{0}
versus
H_{1}
.

Now, let
P_{m,\alpha}
denote a
\Par(m,\alpha)
distribution, and
Q_{n-1,\alpha}
a
\Gamma(n-1,\alpha)
distribution. Furthermore, for any prior
W
on
m
, we denote the Bayes marginal of
P_{m,\alpha}
relative to
W
by
P_{W,\alpha}
. Then,

\sup_{E\in\mathcal{E}}\inf_{m,\in(0,\infty)}\mathbb{E}_{m,\alpha_{1}}[\log(E)]=\sup_{E\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}\inf_{m,\in(0,\infty)}\mathbb{E}_{m,\alpha_{1}}[\log(E)]=\sup_{E\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}\inf_{W}\mathbb{E}_{W,\alpha_{1}}[\log(E)].

By providing a similar argument as in Lemma
2.5
, we find that for any e-variable
E\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
, and any priors
W_{0}
and
W_{1}
on
m
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{W_{1},\alpha_{1}}[\log(E)]

\displaystyle\leq D_{\text{KL}}(P_{W_{1},n\alpha_{1}}\otimes Q_{n-1,\alpha_{1}}\|P_{W_{0},n\alpha_{0}}\otimes Q_{n-1,\alpha_{0}})

\displaystyle=D_{\text{KL}}(P_{W_{1},n\alpha_{1}}\otimes\|P_{W_{0},n\alpha_{0}})+D_{\text{KL}}(Q_{n-1,\alpha_{1}}\|Q_{n-1,\alpha_{0}}),

so

\sup_{E\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}\inf_{W}\mathbb{E}_{W,\alpha_{1}}[\log(E)]\leq D_{\text{KL}}(Q_{n-1,\alpha_{1}}\|Q_{n-1,\alpha_{0}})+\inf_{W_{0}}\inf_{W_{1}}D_{\text{KL}}(P_{W_{1},n\alpha_{1}}\|P_{W_{0},n\alpha_{0}})

The expected log-utility of
E^{*}
attains the left-summand of this upper bound, so it suffices to show that the right summand is
0
.

To that end, let
W_{u}
denote the
\Gamma(u,u)
-distribution with
u\in(0,\infty)
. It suffices to show that
D_{\text{KL}}(P_{W_{u},n\alpha_{1}}\|P_{W_{u},n\alpha_{0}})
tends to zero as
u\downarrow 0
. The density
f_{W_{u},\alpha}
of
X\sim P_{W_{u},\alpha}
is given by

\displaystyle f_{W_{u},\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{m^{\alpha}a}{x^{\alpha+1}}\frac{u^{u}}{\Gamma(u)m^{u-1}}e^{-um}\mathrm{d}m

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha u^{u}}{x^{\alpha+1}\Gamma(u)}\int_{0}^{x}m^{\alpha+u-1}e^{-um}\mathrm{dm}

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{\alpha+u}\alpha u^{u}}{x^{\alpha+1}\Gamma(u)}\int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha+u-1}e^{-uyx}\mathrm{d}y.

We have that

\displaystyle D_{\text{KL}}(P_{W_{u},n\alpha_{1}}\|P_{W_{u},n\alpha_{0}})=\mathbb{E}_{W_{u},n\alpha_{1}}\left(\log\frac{f_{W_{u},n\alpha_{1}}(X)}{f_{W_{u},n\alpha_{0}}(X)}\right).

Here,

\displaystyle\log\frac{f_{W_{u},n\alpha_{1}}(x)}{f_{W_{u},n\alpha_{0}}(x)}=\log\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}\int_{0}^{1}y^{n\alpha_{1}+u-1}e^{-uyx}\mathrm{d}y}{\alpha_{0}\int_{0}^{1}y^{n\alpha_{0}+u-1}e^{-uyx}\mathrm{d}y}\right)\leq\log\left(\frac{n\alpha_{1}(n\alpha_{0}+u)}{n\alpha_{0}(n\alpha_{1}+u)}\right)+ux,

because

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-ux}}{\alpha+u}\leq\int_{0}^{1}y^{\alpha+u-1}e^{-uyx}\mathrm{d}y\leq\frac{1}{\alpha+u}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle D_{\text{KL}}(P_{W_{u},n\alpha_{1}}\|P_{W_{u},n\alpha_{0}})\leq\log\left(\frac{n\alpha_{1}(n\alpha_{0}+u)}{n\alpha_{0}(n\alpha_{1}+u)}\right)+u\mathbb{E}_{W_{u},n\alpha_{1}}(X).

The first summand tends to
0
as
u\downarrow 0
, and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{W_{u},n\alpha_{1}}(X)=\mathbb{E}_{m\sim W_{u}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{m,n\alpha_{1}}(X)\right)=\mathbb{E}_{m\sim W_{u}}\left(\frac{n\alpha_{1}m}{n\alpha_{1}-1}\right)=\frac{n\alpha_{1}}{n\alpha_{1}-1},

which exists because
n\alpha_{1}>1
by assumption, so the second summand also tends to
0
as
u\downarrow 0
. Thus,

\displaystyle\lim_{u\downarrow 0}D_{\text{KL}}(P_{W_{u},n\alpha_{1}}\|P_{W_{u},n\alpha_{0}})=0,

and therefore

\displaystyle\inf_{W_{0}}\inf_{W_{1}}D_{\text{KL}}(P_{W_{1},n\alpha_{1}}\|P_{W_{0},n\alpha_{0}})=0,

completing the proof. ∎
