Team Time as a wellbeing intervention for NHS staff: a qualitative evaluation of implementation during the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Magdalena Zasada, Suzanne van Even, Jill Maben, Jennifer Oates, Cath Taylor
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSupport for NHS staff was vital during the COVID-19 pandemic and Team Time was developed as an adaptation of Schwartz Rounds™ – a well-evidenced, facilitated group intervention. Building on the existing evidence base for Schwartz Rounds implementation, this study aims to understand the key factors associated with Team Time implementation and sustainability in this context.MethodsTwenty semi-structured interviews were conducted within 10 NHS acute, community and mental health organisations that ran Team Time during the pandemic: six with no prior experience of Schwartz Rounds and four that ran Schwartz Rounds prior to the pandemic. Additionally, three Team Time mentors who provided support to these organisations were also interviewed. Data analysis was underpinned by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR). Data were analysed thematically, and key implementation factors were mapped onto CFIR constructs using the Framework method to explore patterns.ResultsImplementation of Team Time was challenging in organisations that did not run Schwartz Rounds previously. Facilitating factors included having incentives in the outer setting (wider context of the organisation), the adaptability of the intervention, and wide ownership across the organisation. However, barriers to implementation were the perception that the intervention was complex and resource intensive and an unsupportive organisational culture and communication difficulties which hindered engagement. In those organisations which already ran Schwartz Rounds the burden of setting up the organisational infrastructure for Team Time was smaller and the implementation climate more supportive.ConclusionsSimilar to Schwartz Rounds, Team Time needed broad and sustained organisational support for implementation to become embedded in organisations and deliver benefits. Implementing it as an entirely new and stand-alone online intervention during the pandemic was challenging. Team Time should therefore be considered as an addition to Schwartz Rounds and as part of a long-term organisational investment in staff psychological wellbeing, not as a short-term intervention in a period of crisis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-12046-0.Keywords: Staff psychological wellbeing, Organisational intervention, Implementation, COVID-19, Schwartz Rounds, Team Time

Background

Healthcare work is highly demanding, requiring emotional labour and frequent ethical decision-making. In a healthcare system under pressure staff are at risk of high stress, burnout and moral distress [1–4]. This was particularly heightened during the Covid-19 pandemic when staff faced significant care delivery challenges and increased patient deaths [5, 6]. Healthcare provider organisations have a duty of care to their employees and should therefore proactively engage in the prevention of stress and burnout (indeed anticipate and plan for it), as well as alleviating it [1]. Supporting staff wellbeing will also help address other workforce challenges such as staff retention and lowering staff sickness rates [7] and is thus considered of strategic importance. Therefore, effective interventions that would alleviate the psychological and emotional burden of healthcare work are needed.

Schwartz Rounds™ (Rounds) were founded in the USA by The Schwartz Centre for Compassionate Healthcare. They were brought to the UK in 2009 by the charitable organisation Point of Care Foundation (PoCF) which is licenced to support Rounds delivery in the UK and Ireland. Rounds are a structured group intervention led by two facilitators which provides a safe, reflective space for clinical and non-clinical team members from all levels of seniority across the organisation to share work-related experiences associated with providing patient care or working in the organisation more generally, with Rounds titled, for example: “When your best doesn’t feel good enough”, “The day I made a difference”, “I’m human too: when feelings surface”. Every Round starts with up to four previously prepared storytellers sharing their experiences, followed by a facilitated open discussion where other participants share their thoughts and reflections related to the stories. Unlike many other meetings, attendance is optional, open to all employees and there is a particular focus on the emotional, social, and ethical challenges of healthcare work, not on debriefing, problem-solving, decision-making or identifying outcomes.

The implementation of Rounds was evaluated by Maben et al. [8]. This study found that appropriate resourcing and shared ownership are crucial for Rounds’ sustainability, coupled with senior management support to enable all staff groups to participate. Rounds were found to be effective in improving wellbeing, with psychological distress reduced by half for regular attendees compared with a less than 10% reduction in the control group [9], and participants benefiting from increased empathy for self, colleagues and patients [10]. They were theorised as a “counter-cultural space”, providing emotional safety which facilitated open, honest and compassionate conversations and the role-modelling of vulnerability; the benefits were cumulative - the gradual normalising of emotions and improved organisation-wide connections brought about further ripple effects (including changes in practice and new staff support provision) and broader cultural change [8].

Rounds were originally intended to be run face to face and open to all staff within an organisation. This became impossible during the COVID-19 pandemic due to the need for social distancing and for staff having a psychologically safe space to share recent, unprocessed stories with their team. Team Time was therefore developed by the PoCF as an adaptation and replacement of Rounds in organisations that had already implemented them. However, as all healthcare organisations were searching for interventions to support staff at this time, Team Time was subsequently offered to organisations without this prior experience. Team Time differs from Rounds in three key aspects: (a) being run online rather than face to face (originally to mitigate infection control issues during the pandemic), (b) providing a space to share current experiences, rather than emotionally processed past events; and (c) being targeted at teams, rather than being offered organisation-wide to help with psychological safety given the stories were often still raw and unprocessed.

Due to immediate need and pressured circumstances Team Time was implemented at speed at the start of the pandemic in the UK, and thus without a robust process of piloting or evaluation. Building on the evidence base for Rounds, this study therefore aimed to evaluate the implementation of Team Time with the research question ‘What were the key factors associated with Team Time implementation and sustainability in National Health Service (NHS) organisations during the Covid-19 pandemic?’.

Methods

Study design

A qualitative evaluation comprising semi-structured interviews was undertaken in 10 NHS organisations which implemented Team Time. A case study approach, with the organisation as the unit of analysis, supported exploration of organisational contextual factors, due to the known importance of the inner setting context (e.g. resources, leadership, culture) on implementation of interventions [11]. The study was underpinned conceptually by the existing evidence base for Rounds [8] and by the updated Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [11]. The CFIR is an evidence-based framework that assimilates the factors associated with the effective implementation of interventions in five domains:

Intervention: Team Time

As described above, Team Time was an adaptation of Rounds designed to provide a confidential space for teams to share and hear stories of healthcare staff’s current, raw and unprocessed experiences in work in on-line facilitated spaces. Running Team Time requires payment for a licence from the PoCF, a letter of support from the chief executive, the establishment of a steering group (6–12 members from across the organisation), administrative support, and at least 3 facilitators trained by the PoCF. Each Team Time session has a four-stage process akin to Rounds [8]: (1) sourcing stories and panellists, (2) crafting and rehearsing stories in panel preparation, (3) telling stories to trigger reflection and resonance in the Team Time session, (4) post-Team Time ripple effects into the organisation. Implementation and sustainability of Team Time involved an ongoing process of running regular Team Time sessions and steering group meetings, promoting Team Time, identifying teams willing to participate, finding volunteer “storytellers” and supporting them to prepare a story based on their experience. Organisations have a mentor appointed by the PoCF who supports implementation and embedding Team Time.

Sampling and recruitment

Organisations were sampled using two different sampling frames: (1) NHS organisations that had not previously run Rounds and who were funded by NHS England and Improvement to implement Team Time (defined as having at least one member of staff completing at least one of the two days of Team Time training with the PoCF) between 2020 and 2021, N = 17 organisations, (2) NHS organisations that ran Rounds prior to implementing Team Time during the pandemic, N = 9 organisations. The Team Time leads in organisations were initially sent an opt out letter by PoCF, then the research team sent an invitation to all organisations running Team Time (N = 26). Team Time leads then forwarded information about the study to their colleagues who were involved in implementing Team Time, asking for consent to share their emails with the study team. We aimed to recruit a broad range of participants, including Team Time participants and storytellers, however the post-COVID19 workload pressures on staff together with staff turnover changes in the organisations prevented this. Due to our aim of understanding barriers and facilitators to implementation (rather than how/if Team Time works), our sampling frame focused on those most closely involved in implementing/delivering Team Time, who often held organisational support/HR roles.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews aimed to explore how Team Time was implemented and identify the key factors impacting implementation and sustainability through open questions and probing according to existing knowledge. Topic guides were underpinned by the existing evidence base for implementing Rounds [8] and tailored to (1) the Team Time role of interviewees (facilitators, steering group members and mentors), (2) whether the organisation had implemented/not implemented, sustained/not sustained Team Time, and (3) whether Rounds were run prior to Team Time implementation. Interviews were conducted virtually using Microsoft Teams, digitally recorded and took an average of 28 min. All interviews were conducted between December 2022 – March 2023.

Data analysis

The Framework method for thematic analysis [12] was used to integrate and analyse data from interviews, this enabled investigation of patterns within and between case study sites. Analysis took a deductive approach (theory-driven exploration) based on previous research and the CFIR which enabled exploration of known barriers and enablers to implementation of interventions, considering the domain headings of innovation, outer setting, inner setting (organisational factors), individuals and implementation process. Additionally, there were categories developed inductively for key data not captured in the framework, including contextual information, such as whether Rounds were previously run, how/why the decision to implement Team Time was made, who was involved in implementation and the number of Team Time sessions which took place, whether these were with individual teams or organisation-wide and their perceived impact. This was included in the analysis to support understanding of the diverse implementation trajectories in the case study organisations. Ethics

The study obtained a favourable ethical opinion from the University of Surrey Research Ethics committee on 19th August 2022 (ref: FHMS 21–22 233 EGA) followed by approval of an amendment to the study protocol on 15 December 2022 for recruitment of organisations which ran Rounds prior to the pandemic and increase in number of participating organisations.

Consent for research was sought first by email from the main contact for Team Time in each participating site, and then through written consent obtained from each interview participant. Participants’ data was anonymised: sites were allocated pseudonyms (names of trees and shrubs), as were participants, and all identifiable information was removed from the data prior to publication.

Innovation domain: complexity and adaptability

Team Time was designed to provide for the needs of staff in pandemic circumstances, responding to the necessity of social distancing, the need to build strong support networks amongst people who worked together and to address the immediate emotional needs in a crisis. However, the requisites of running Team Time, such as establishing a steering group and training facilitators were perceived as a significant barrier to their implementation, with the process of setting up Team Time described as “too time consuming” and requiring “a massive structure” [Poplar1 – TT lead, no prior SR] and further struggle with “sustaining the scale of delivery” due to availability of facilitators even where Rounds were running prior to the pandemic [Azalea1 – TT Lead, prior SR]. It was noted that organising a Team Time session for a team required a similar level of effort and resources as an online Schwartz Round for the whole organisation but benefitted fewer people and did not have the organisation-wide cultural impact which was associated with Rounds (Birch, Palm); therefore the organisational costs associated with the complexity of Team Time were high and reported as particularly difficult to meet during the pandemic.

Team Time was not intended to be adapted and was envisioned to be distinct from Rounds (e.g. implemented online, with discrete teams and including stories that were current). However, data showed that organisations adapted it, most notably to be delivered organisation-wide, like Rounds, to address the barriers described above. Particularly in organisations with prior experience of Rounds there was a view that they were continuing with their previous activity (including “embedded Rounds” and “pop up Rounds” which were targeted at teams) in a new online context, without necessarily seeing this as a different intervention:

“I don’t know exactly what Team Time is - like my understanding of it is that it is Schwartz Rounds online. But like we didn’t use whatever the Team Time format is specifically, we just did … we translated what we were doing face to face into a virtual setting effectively” [Hibiscus2 – facilitator, prior SR]

In organisations that had previously run Schwartz Rounds the main difference between Rounds and Team Time as described by respondents was the move to delivering the intervention online, with team-based Team Time sessions viewed as providing an additional option when needed. Of the organisations without prior Rounds experience, three sites ran the intervention solely with teams and three delivered predominantly organisation-wide sessions.

Respondents had diverse views about the impact of running the intervention online. On one hand it was reported to facilitate bringing staff together, increasing reach, attendance and inclusion (Hazel, Birch, Azalea, Hibiscus), particularly in organisations that were spread across a wide geographical area:

“we’ve tried on a couple of occasions to move to in-person rounds, and we immediately noticed the decline in participants” [Birch1 – TT Lead, no prior SR]

“digital exists and is kind of okay and makes it really hard [to change to face to face] when somebody brings up the element of being inclusive and accessible” [Hibiscus1 – TT Lead, prior SR]

However, it was also noted that the lack of access to a quiet or confidential space and/or to IT equipment prevented certain staff groups (e.g., ward-based staff) from hosting or attending Team Time:

“people on the wards, nursing staff, they wouldn’t have access to a quiet space even if they wanted to join, if they had time – so that can be quite limiting, especially for staff that are working in very high acuity areas” [Azalea3 – TT facilitator, prior SR]

Respondents described trying to balance between aiming for high attendance and the higher quality of interaction face-to-face:

“you cannot sort of convey camaraderie and care and emotion as well on digital. (…) it also is important that that [accessibility] doesn’t always trump the other value” [Hibiscus1 – TT Lead, prior SR]

At the time of the study (December 2022 – March 2023), some sites had moved (back) to providing Rounds or Team Time face to face with the end of pandemic restrictions, but most still ran some sessions online. Overall, most respondents perceived Team Time as an adaptation of Rounds, not a unique intervention with distinct elements that required a high level of fidelity to a prescribed model in how it is organised. The online aspect and the use of more recent stories (key Team Time features) were seen as a necessary adjustment to meet staff needs in the circumstances of the pandemic.

Outer setting domain: local attitudes, financing and partnerships and connections

The implementation of Team Time occurred during 2020–2021, therefore in the height of the COVID-19 pandemic. These were unprecedented circumstances for healthcare organisations, which presented additional challenges, but also opportunities, in terms of implementing staff support interventions. For organisations who had no prior experience of running Rounds, the increased awareness of the importance of staff’s psychological wellbeing during the pandemic meant that greater prominence was given to staff emotional and psychological support.

“We were really just trying to find ways and means of being able to offer that support to staff whatever that would look like. (…) it was 45 minutes just to focus on them in a structured sort of supportive way really, and we just felt that that [Team Time] would be a really nice offer for teams who would find that helpful.” [Maple2 – facilitator, no prior SR]

NHS England and NHS Improvement (NHSEI) funding was made available to support the implementation of Team Time which was a significant incentive for the organisations which had not run Rounds previously:

“I saw it as a huge opportunity to try and open the door to Schwartz Rounds. And I think that was always the intention was that we would start with Team Time and would move to Schwartz Rounds whenever we could. So yeah, that was the main jumping off point was the free funding through NHS England” [Birch1 – TT Lead, no prior SR]

A key facilitator for organisations which had no prior experience of running Rounds when implementing Team Time, was collaboration with a nearby “buddy” organisation which had this expertise (Hazel, Acacia, Palm):

“We knew about Schwartz. Some of our colleagues in one of our partner Trusts were implementing it, so we spent a little bit of time with them, they shared some knowledge and information, and then we made some enquiries.” [Acacia1 – facilitator, no prior SR]

Acacia and Palm had a particularly close collaboration as they both merged with another organisation which held a Schwartz Rounds licence, therefore benefitted from knowledge sharing and support with setting up the infrastructure, with Acacia sharing a Steering Group with the other organisation.

Inner setting domain: culture and communications

In the organisations that were already running Rounds, they were well embedded, or strongly congruent with their organisational culture of supporting staff psychological wellbeing. Therefore, when it came to implementing Team Time, already there was clarity around its intended aims (Holly) and perceived compatibility with organisational values (Azalea). The significance of having an organisational culture which prioritises staff psychological wellbeing and views such interventions as valuable was highlighted as a facilitator in several sites (Acacia, Buddleia, Hibiscus, Holly). Other organisations reported that staff psychological wellbeing was not taken seriously by senior leadership, with a reactive approach to managing it, and thus Team Time was not viewed as a high enough priority (Poplar, Azalea). In most organisations where there was no prior experience of running Rounds, the circumstances of the pandemic resulted in Team Time being implemented with the intention of meeting immediate psychological support needs, without a long-term commitment or sufficient time and resources dedicated to embedding it and for a culture supportive of staff psychological wellbeing to develop.

“you know new things come along in the health service don’t they, shiny new things, and attention gets taken in different places. (…) And I think as that senior clinical buy-in started to wane it seemed like it just very quickly … we stopped finding teams, we stopped actually delivering the training when I felt that we were starting to build a really good portfolio of how useful and successful it could be. But for whatever reason it just didn’t get capitalised on” [Maple2 – TT facilitator, no prior SR].

Due to this heightened focus, some organisations introduced multiple well-being initiatives (Poplar, Maple, Hazel, Hibiscus), meaning that Team Time had to compete with other initiatives for organisational resources and staff attention. Unprecedented pressures on clinical staff and a high volume of other wellbeing initiatives made it difficult for Team Time to stand out and thus prevented it from fully embedding in these organisations.

Respondents stated that effective communication about Team Time was challenging within organisations (Maple, Hazel, Birch, Poplar, Palm). Routes such as staff e-communications, social media, and information sharing at internal meetings (Acacia, Hazel, Birch) were used, however it was reported that staff were overloaded with communications (Poplar, Holly, Birch), making it more difficult to engage them (Holly).

“I can see it’s just incredible the amount of information you get bombarded with all the time – screensavers whatever, emails whatever … and so you’re competing for information.” [Birch1 – TT Lead, no prior SR]

Informal communication routes were found to be effective, such as having “champions” throughout the organisation who would speak to colleagues:

“One of our steering group members (…) she’s not a qualified member of staff, she’s a support member of staff - she is unbelievable in terms of what she does around engaging with people” [Birch1 – TT Lead, no prior SR]

However, pandemic restrictions and less direct contact between staff hindered informal communication routes.

Individuals domain: implementation leads and innovation recipients

Knowledge and beliefs about Team Time were an important factor in the organisations which did not run Rounds prior to the pandemic. Team Time’s credibility and desirability were supported by prior knowledge about Rounds in several of these organisations. It was highlighted in all organisations that Team Time implementation was initiated, led and/or championed by clinical leaders (e.g. Medical Director or Chief Nurse) or corporate leaders (e.g. members of executive team, Head of Organisational Development) who had good understanding and were passionate about the potential benefits Team Time may bring. However, in several cases (Poplar, Acacia, Hazel) these core individuals left the organisation and it was more difficult to continue without their leadership and support. The benefits of Team Time, although articulated by most respondents as providing an opportunity for connection and reducing isolation (Azalea, Buddleia, Hibiscus, Holly, Hazel, Birch, Poplar, Palm) and supporting staff to manage the emotional aspects of their work (Azalea, Buddleia, Hibiscus, Acacia, Birch, Palm), were reported as difficult to capture and quantify.

As explained above, effective organisation-wide communication about Team Time was in many cases hindered by organisational factors, so the lack of knowledge and understanding amongst wider staff of what Team Time is (and what it is not) and its purpose was a key barrier in these organisations. This was pointed out by study respondents, who said it is not clear from the name what it actually is (Poplar), and its benefits were not being clearly communicated to staff (Birch, Maple).

Interviewees reported difficulties with getting staff members to understand what Team Time is (Hazel, Maple).

“I don’t think people would perceive this as selfcare, that’s the problem, I think people would perceive this as something else that their manager has asked them to do. (…) What I said earlier about it not being described properly in the first instance and people not getting a full understanding of what it is – it’s just, here’s a logo, Team Time, new programme we’ve got funding for….” [Maple2 – facilitator, no prior SR]

Some organisations introduced multiple psychological well-being initiatives (Poplar, Maple, Hazel, Hibiscus) and “it’s almost like the system got saturated with that kind of an intervention during COVID” [Hibiscus2 – facilitator, prior SR]. Introducing multiple new interventions in a relatively short space of time made some staff more cynical about their effectiveness and use - “everyone’s just rolling their eyes” [Hazel1]. Staff not feeling able to prioritise self-care was perceived as commonplace (Maple) and talking about emotions was not ‘normalised’ among staff members (Poplar), with some staff saying that reflective spaces were “not for them” [Birch1].

Implementation process domain: engaging innovation recipients and deliverers

Implementing Team Time is dependent on the engagement of staff as facilitators, steering group members, storytellers and participants/audience members. Respondents described some strategies used for involving staff: getting messages about Team Time out early, asking staff if they felt their team would benefit from it (Holly), finding stories that resonate with the majority of staff (Poplar, Azalea) and managers approaching staff to encourage them to share a story (Hibiscus). Birch introduced CPD points for Team Time attendance and Holly offered refreshments, which onsite participants could pick up, to encourage attendance.

It was however difficult for teams to commit to participating in Team Time sessions due to clinical pressure (Acacia, Maple) and staff members invited to attend sessions reported they were overworked and exhausted:

“You will hear this across every NHS organisation - an hour to attend a Team Time session or a Schwartz Round session is … it feels impossible in the middle of a busy shift.” [Birch1 – TT lead, no previous SR]

Engagement was also reported to be negatively impacted by staff not knowing enough (or at all) about the intervention (Poplar, Birch) and its benefits not immediately obvious in early stages of implementation:

“you need to do enough so that people see the benefit. So, I have done quite a few now, so I see it works … you know if it’s being organised during lunch time, and if I have the time, I will see it worth my while (…) But a lot of people don’t do that because they haven’t been exposed to it enough, and I think there should be the drive to keep on with it and to kind of keep running with it before you think that it may not be something for your organisation” [Palm1 - facilitator, no previous SR]

When Team Time sessions were run within teams, they weren’t necessarily repeated for each team, therefore the same people would not access the intervention more than once, inhibiting the development of a deeper understanding of the intervention and any potential cumulative impact.

“I think very often people were dialling in who’d never been to a session before, didn’t know what to expect, didn’t get it, and probably just didn’t participate either with cameras on or cameras off – they just weren’t engaged enough” [Birch mentor]

Engagement was made more challenging by the intervention being online – which was also a new means of engaging with colleagues. Screentime fatigue set in quickly during the pandemic, or engagement in the meetings was challenging:

“I think initially people were glad to have something, they were glad of some kind of connection … and very quickly it seems like that wore off, like that everyone was sick of looking at a screen all the time. And the value of virtual Schwartz Rounds decreased in currency quickly because it was another thing I had to do online.” [Hibiscus2 – facilitator, prior SR]

Staff suffered from “virtual world fatigue” [Hibiscus2 – facilitator, prior SR]as over time, they disengaged from online meetings, with audience members becoming distracted or seen multi-tasking (e.g., by visibly working on something else while attending the session).

The intense clinical and organisational demand created by the pandemic, when clinical staff were extremely busy and unable to commit time to new projects, hindered their involvement in facilitator and steering group roles, and stalled implementation, possibly limiting sustainability and the reach of Team Time across the organisations.

“I think it was 20 plus [on the steering group] when we first started. I think by the end probably between 5 and 10 (…) people with a bit more of a vested interest. So, facilitators, OD colleagues, HR colleagues (…) The kind of more clinical end, it fizzled out much quicker.” [Maple2 – facilitator, no prior SR]

This meant that leadership of Team Time in organisations that did not previously run Rounds was provided predominantly by corporate teams such as Organisational Development or Staff Support, with little direct engagement from clinical staff. However, it was noted that having steering group members and facilitators who had connections which reached across the organisation (Acacia) and making “a push for organisational ownership not just OD team” (Birch) were a key facilitator to recruiting storytellers and audience members from across the organisation.

“one of the facilitators (…) who is a senior nurse in a teaching role (…) was in contact with lots of nurses out there in the community and you know she could source storytellers that way” [Acacia mentor, no prior SR]

Other facilitators reported difficulties in finding storytellers (Acacia, Birch, Buddleia, Maple, Palm, Holly, Hibiscus) which was attributed to limited staff capacity (Holly), but also, due to pandemic social distancing rules, less face-to-face interaction between staff members when potential storytellers could be recruited.

Discussion

This is the first evaluation of Team Time, an adapted form of Schwartz Rounds, which was developed for healthcare organisations during the COVID-19 pandemic to meet the urgent need for psychological support for healthcare staff. We found that implementing it as an entirely new and stand-alone online intervention in a crisis was challenging, particularly in organisations which could not draw on any prior experience of running Rounds. Using the lens of the CFIR [11], implementation in organisations which didn’t run Rounds was predominantly facilitated by incentives in the organisational outer setting, the perceived adaptability of the intervention itself and broader organisational ownership which enabled engagement. On the other hand, difficulties in implementation were rooted in the perception that the intervention was complex and resource intensive and internal barriers such as communication difficulties and an unsupportive organisational culture impeded implementation and embedding. In those organisations which already ran Rounds pre-pandemic, but also in those which established a close collaboration with others which had implemented Rounds pre-pandemic, the burden of setting up the organisational infrastructure for Team Time was reduced and the implementation climate more supportive.

A hybrid approach to running Team Time/Rounds i.e. online Rounds open to staff across the whole organisation or a combination of Team Time and Rounds was sustained in organisations which had Rounds embedded prior to the pandemic or had a prior commitment to implementing them; five organisations (Azalea, Holly, Acacia, Birch, Poplar) were offering Team Time alongside Rounds at the time of the research. This study confirms findings from Maben et al.’s evaluation of Rounds [8] which indicated that this type of intervention has a cumulative and self-sustaining effect over time, with an established “Schwartz savvy audience” supporting sustainability and increased benefits. The familiarity of Rounds in the organisations which ran them previously meant that staff understood the purpose of Team Time, were confident to contribute to online sessions appropriately and recruitment of Team Time storytellers was also easier. In contrast, in organisations that did not run Rounds, the benefits of Team Time, although articulated by some study participants, could not reach their full potential in the short term, and the cumulative benefits were lost when teams only had “one-off” sessions - this contributed to a loss of motivation to keep running the intervention. Yet it is recognised that culture change in large and complex organisations takes a long time as it entails changes in structures and leadership styles, creating a sense of ownership of the change across the organisation and aligning changes in individual thinking with changes in behaviour [13]. Interventions which, like Team Time, entail new collective, coordinated behaviours, require a positive implementation climate, defined as the extent to which engagement with the intervention is perceived to be expected, supported and rewarded within the organisation [14]. For this reason, aligning staff wellbeing interventions with long-term organisational objectives such as staff retention is argued to be essential [15]. A positive implementation climate requires a shared understanding of organisational expectations, policies and practices associated with Team Time, rooted in elements of organisational culture such as values and beliefs. Without this, implementation is disjointed and dependant on individual staff members’ perceptions and values or a particularly receptive culture in individual teams/departments [14].

Team Time was introduced in a time of crisis, when messages about what it is, its purpose and potential benefits were lost in a deluge of information directed at staff and it was competing for resources and attention with multiple other well-being initiatives which were also being introduced; there was no capacity to establish a supportive organisational culture where there was not one already. The pandemic exacerbated challenges of implementing and sustaining many wellbeing interventions, with heightened demands on staff time and staff not working at the locations where they were run cited as key barriers to implementation [16]. Similar findings were reported for Team Time, where attendance and storyteller recruitment were impacted by the increased clinical workloads and limited direct staff interaction due to remote working. Furthermore, financial and staffing challenges in healthcare organisations, in the NHS and across the world [17–21], bring high managerial scrutiny of the organisational costs of implementing interventions. Organisational leaders and local managers therefore play a key role in implementing organisational wellbeing initiatives – their belief in and understanding of the intervention, support for staff in accessing it and protecting necessary resources, has been well evidenced within and outside of the pandemic context [15, 16, 22, 23].

Routine support for healthcare staff in their challenging emotional work and their preparedness for a time of crisis requires a collective approach and organisational effort, not only exhorting individual staff to look after themselves or be more resilient [24, 25]. Team or organisation-wide interventions are a route to this, promoting positive relationship-building and peer support at all levels of the organisation [1, 26, 27]. This was particularly called for during the pandemic [5, 28], however its impact on staff and healthcare organisations is ongoing and psychological and emotional support, safe, reflective spaces which normalise emotions and improve organisational connections are still needed [29, 30]. Establishing a culture of compassion espoused by leaders at all levels is necessary for improving staff psychological wellbeing, engagement and satisfaction; this requires staff to be attended to, understood, empathised with and helped [1, 31]. Schwartz Rounds and Team Time have been shown to bring such benefits [8, 10, 29, 32, 33] and their adaptability to circumstances and different organisational needs is a particular strength.

This study provides new, significant insights into the key factors which were perceived to support or hinder the implementation of Team Time during the Covid-19 pandemic. The study was limited by the time lapse between implementation and evaluation and recruitment challenges due to work pressures in the post-pandemic NHS environment. The original study design included collecting data on the implementation process through a questionnaire, interviewing a broader range of stakeholders e.g. storytellers and observation of Team Time sessions. However, this was not possible to carry out because key people had left or declined participation due to workload and Team Time was no longer running, or transitioning to Schwartz Rounds. This resulted in not being able to access as many interviewees in some organisations as intended. Our evaluation did however include a good breadth of sites across England, with diverse organisational settings, sizes and previous experience of running Rounds represented in the sample. Further research outside of a pandemic context would be beneficial, as well as capturing the views of a broader group of stakeholders such as storytellers, audience participants and organisational leaders. Finally, research is needed to evaluate how Team Time works for teams when implemented alongside organisation-wide Rounds.

Conclusions

Implementing and sustaining Team Time as a stand-alone online intervention, aimed at teams, was found to be difficult during the COVID-19 pandemic. Implementation was most successful in organisations that had already established Rounds and thereby did not have to ‘learn’ what it was or how it worked. This study highlights that effective implementation and sustainability, particularly in adverse circumstances, require an existing culture of prioritising the wellbeing needs of staff, support from others (e.g. an experienced “buddy” organisation, PoCF community of practice etc.) and growing staff involvement across the organisation through sustained commitment of resources and communication from leaders at all levels. Findings suggest that Team Time is most beneficial when implemented as a supplement to Schwartz Rounds, and as part of a long-term organisational investment in staff psychological wellbeing, not as a short-term intervention in a period of crisis.
