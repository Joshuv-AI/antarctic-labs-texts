Efficacy of periodontal therapy in preventing tooth loss and improving masticatory function in patients with chronic kidney disease: a protocol for a single-centre randomised controlled trial in China
Authors: Yining Zhou, Yujie Wang, Yining He, Jingjing Zhu, Yingli Liu, Zhongchen Song, Huiwen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPeriodontitis and chronic kidney disease (CKD) are inter-related conditions that can significantly impact patient health. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of active non-surgical periodontal therapy (NSPT) combined with supportive periodontal care (SPC) in reducing tooth loss and improving masticatory function in patients with CKD and stage III periodontitis.Methods and analysisThis randomised controlled trial will recruit 86 patients diagnosed with both stage III periodontitis and CKD. Participants will be randomly assigned at a 1:1 ratio to either an experimental group receiving active NSPT supplemented with SPC or a control group receiving oral hygiene instruction with scheduled periodontal monitoring. The intervention will last for 24 months, with assessments conducted at baseline and 3, 6, 12, 18 and 24 months. The primary outcome is the incidence of tooth loss due to periodontitis over the 2-year follow-up period. Secondary outcomes include the number of lost teeth, masticatory function, clinical periodontal parameters and oral health-related quality of life.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol and informed consent form were approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Ninth People’s Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (SH9H-2022-T404-1). Findings will be disseminated to participants and published in peer-reviewed journals.Trial registration numberChiCTR2300068923.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease that affects the supporting tissues of the teeth, resulting in periodontal pocket formation, clinical attachment loss (CAL) and eventual alveolar bone destruction.1 It represents the primary cause of tooth loss in adults. Accumulating evidence indicates that periodontitis compromises periodontal supporting tissues and exacerbates systemic diseases, including diabetes, cardiovascular diseases and neurodegenerative disorders.2 In recent years, growing attention has focused on the relationship between periodontitis and chronic kidney disease (CKD) and an expanding body of epidemiological evidence suggests a complex and significant association between these two conditions.3

CKD is defined by persistent abnormalities in kidney structure or function lasting >3 months and is characterised by the progressive, irreversible loss of functional nephrons.4 It is now recognised as a major global public health concern, alongside cardiovascular diseases, diabetes and cancer, with a worldwide prevalence estimated at approximately 9.1%.4 In the Chinese population, the prevalence is slightly higher (10.8%) and markedly elevated among individuals over 60 years of age compared with the general adult population.5 Patients with CKD frequently experience multiple complications, including disorders of bone metabolism, protein-energy wasting and malnutrition.69 These complications adversely impact patients’ overall health and impose a substantial burden on their families and healthcare systems.

Recent cross-sectional studies have identified a potential link between CKD and alveolar bone loss associated with periodontitis.10 11 Clinical research suggests that patients with CKD exhibit accelerated periodontal tissue destruction and alveolar bone resorption, which may significantly compromise tooth retention, masticatory function and efficiency.12 A reduced number of teeth is associated with diminished masticatory ability, leading to impaired quality-of-life outcomes.13 Similarly, limited masticatory capacity and the consequent reduced consumption of masticable foods may contribute to undernutrition, as patients often prefer softer, easily chewed alternatives.14 Therefore, preserving remaining dentition and enhancing masticatory function are critical components of dietary and nutritional interventions in patients with CKD.

Multiple clinical studies have demonstrated that active non-surgical periodontal therapy (NSPT) combined with supportive periodontal care (SPC) effectively maintains teeth in patients with moderate or advanced periodontitis.1518 It is therefore reasonable to hypothesise that active NSPT supplemented with SPC may reduce tooth loss in patients with CKD and periodontitis,19 potentially improving their masticatory function and overall quality of life. Nevertheless, high-quality clinical trials with long-term follow-up in this population remain scarce.

Accordingly, this single-centre study has been initiated, enrolling patients with CKD diagnosed with stage III periodontitis. Participants will be randomly assigned to one of two groups: an experimental group receiving active NSPT supplemented by SPC and a control group receiving oral hygiene instruction (OHI) with scheduled periodontal monitoring. The aim of this prospective randomised controlled trial is to provide clinical and safety evidence supporting the role of active NSPT as an adjunctive intervention in the personalised management of patients with CKD and periodontitis.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study is designed as a single-centre, parallel-group, randomised controlled trial. The protocol has been prepared in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials guideline for clinical trial protocols. The overview of the study design is shown in figure 1. The intervention used in this study—active NSPT and SPC—follows the internationally recognised S3 guidelines for the treatment of periodontitis.20 Aside from routine visits, no additional follow-up or invasive procedures will be required.

The overview of the study design. CKD, chronic kidney disease; OHIP-14, Oral Health Impact Profile-14.

Patients with CKD and stage III periodontitis will be randomised at a 1:1 ratio into two groups using block randomisation. In the experimental group, patients will receive active NSPT after completing the baseline assessment, followed by a regular SPC (oral hygiene reinforcement and professional mechanical plaque removal (PMPR) if appropriate) programme. In the control group, patients will receive regular OHI with scheduled periodontal monitoring. For ethical reasons, comprehensive NSPT will be provided to the control group at the study end point (after 2 years).

Participants will be followed for 24 months, with follow-up visits scheduled at 3, 6, 12, 18 and 24 months following baseline intervention. The primary outcome is defined as the incidence of tooth loss due to periodontitis over 2 years of follow-up. Secondary outcomes will include parameters such as the number of lost teeth, changes in chewing efficiency (masticatory function), periodontal parameters and oral health-related quality of life (Oral Health Impact Profile-14 (OHIP-14)).

Primary objective of this study

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of active NSPT and SPC compared with routine OHI in reducing the incidence of tooth loss due to periodontitis in patients with CKD with stage III periodontitis.

Participants with CKD and stage III periodontitis treated at the Department of Nephrology, Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine will be screened and invited to participate in this study. The diagnosis of stage III periodontitis will be performed by periodontal specialists. Nephrologists will be responsible for identifying potential participants from the nephrology department and confirming the diagnosis of CKD in these individuals.

Trained physicians will provide oral-related health knowledge to participants during the medical consultation. The recruitment has been initiated since March 2023. The entire experiment is scheduled to be completed by March 2026.

Randomisation and blinding

An independent statistician, who is not involved in any part of the assessments or interventions, will generate the random allocation sequence using statistical software. Subsequently, the randomisation assignments will be concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. A block randomisation method will be used to ensure a balanced allocation of participants between the experimental and control groups at a 1:1 ratio. Stratification will be based on the number of teeth lost due to periodontitis at baseline (≤2 or 3–4). Randomisation will be performed using blocks of varying sizes, with block size permutations implemented to minimise the risk of allocation bias.

Given the nature of the intervention, blinding of investigators and participants is not feasible. However, outcome assessors will remain unaware of group assignments. Specifically, clinicians conducting periodontal examinations and assessing clinical parameters will be blinded to group allocation. Participants’ assignments will be revealed only after completion of the baseline assessment, at which point a separate clinician responsible for delivering the periodontal intervention will open the corresponding envelope.

The clinical treatment records will not be accessible to them.

After completion of the baseline assessments, participants will then proceed to a 2-year intervention period. Both the experimental and control groups will receive the same standard care for the management of CKD. The only difference lies in the specific periodontal treatment approach.

The control group will receive routine OHI and scheduled periodontal monitoring at each follow-up point. The OHI consists of oral hygiene reinforcement and motivation, such as the use of plaque disclosing agents to enhance patients’ motivation for plaque removal, and adjustment or instruction in oral hygiene tools (table 1). The decision to postpone active therapy for the control group was meticulously evaluated for ethical compliance. Notably, participants are recruited from the nephrology department primarily for kidney-related conditions and their periodontal diseases are identified through routine screening rather than self-reported acute complaints. Because their periodontal conditions are asymptomatic and non-urgent, postponing active therapy does not compromise their immediate oral health. Furthermore, multiple measures are implemented to minimise potential risks. Although active therapy is postponed, the control group undergoes scheduled follow-up examinations at 3, 6, 12, 18 and 24 months by a trained periodontist to assess key clinical indicators (probing depth (PD), bleeding on probing and CAL). This allows for the timely detection of any rapid disease progression. If a control participant exhibits acute complications (eg, periodontal abscesses or acute pain), a rescue protocol will be triggered: they will be immediately withdrawn from the control group and provided with comprehensive active periodontal treatment.

In the experimental group, participants will complete their first NSPT within 2 weeks following the baseline assessment. Any tooth diagnosed as hopeless at baseline will be extracted prior to periodontal treatment. All periodontal therapy will be performed in accordance with internationally recognised guidelines20 by experienced periodontists with a minimum of 10 years of clinical experience. The NSPT will include OHI and PMPR (scaling, polishing, prophylaxis and subgingival debridement (if necessary)). The SPC will then be conducted at each follow-up point, focusing on oral hygiene reinforcement, assessment of the patient’s oral hygiene performance, assessment of modifiable risk factors, periodontal charting (if appropriate) and PMPR (table 1).

In cases dictated by clinical necessity (eg, irreversible tooth mobility requiring extraction or acute periodontal abscesses), treatment may deviate from the S3 guidelines. All such deviations will be documented in detail, including the clinical rationale, type of intervention and supporting evidence.

Changes in chewing efficiency (masticatory function)

Masticatory function will be evaluated through the use of both subjective self-report questionnaires and objective two-colour chewing gum tests.

The subjective evaluation is based on a self-report questionnaire (online supplemental file 1) designed to capture participants’ dietary patterns and chewing-related perceptions, including food texture preference (eg, preference for soft vs hard, crunchy or chewy foods), self-reported masticatory weakness and the presence of tooth mobility.

The objective two-colour chewing gum test will be performed according to the methodology described by Schimmel et al.26 During the course of the test, the participants will be seated in an upright position and instructed to chew two pieces of gum of different colours simultaneously. Following 20 cycles of chewing, the participants will be instructed to cease and the gum will be collected by the clinician. The chewed gum will then be flattened using a preprepared 1 mm thick acrylic plate. A flatbed scanner (EPSON Perfection C39, Seiko Epson, Japan) will be employed for the scanning of the flattened chewed gum. A subjective visual assessment will be conducted using Schimmel’s Subjective Assessment scale, followed by an objective colourimetric analysis using the ViewGum software (dHAL Software, Greece). The software provides a quantitative value, known as the variance of hue (VOH), which describes the degree of colour mixture. The VOH value ranges from 0 to 1, with a lower value indicating superior colour mixing and a maximum value of 1 denoting complete colour separation.

Periodontal examination

Periodontal clinical examination includes plaque index (PLI), PD, CAL and full-mouth bleeding score (FMBS).

PLI: PLI assesses the thickness and distribution of dental plaque on tooth surfaces according to the Silness and Löe PLI method.27 This method uses a 4-point scoring system: score 0 indicates no visible plaque; score 1 refers to a thin film of plaque detectable only with a probe; score 2 represents a moderate amount of plaque visible to the naked eye and score 3 indicates a large accumulation of plaque that covers the gingival margin and adjacent tooth surfaces. The scores from all examined tooth surfaces are averaged to yield a final PLI score.

PD: PD is the depth of the gingival sulcus or periodontal pocket surrounding a tooth. PD will be measured at six sites per tooth (mesiobuccal, midbuccal, distobuccal, mesiolingual, midlingual and distolingual) using a calibrated periodontal probe (UNC-15, Hu-Friedy, Chicago, Illinois, USA).28 In healthy gingiva, PD is typically 1–3 mm. A PD >4 mm often indicates the presence of a periodontal pocket, suggesting the onset of gingivitis or periodontitis. Deeper measurements reflect more advanced stages of disease.

CAL: CAL is the distance from the cemento-enamel junction to the base of the periodontal pocket or the junctional epithelium.29 This provides a more accurate understanding of periodontal destruction than PD alone. In a healthy situation, the CAL values should be minimal (close to 0). Conversely, an increased CAL value indicates the progression of periodontal disease.

FMBS: FMBS is a clinical index used to assess the overall health of the gingiva by quantifying the presence of bleeding during periodontal probing. A low FMBS (typically <10%) indicates healthy gingiva with minimal or no inflammation, whereas a higher FMBS indicates increased gingival inflammation.30

FMBS=(number of bleeding sites/total number of sites examined)×100%

Oral Health Impact Profile-14

The OHIP-14 is commonly used in both clinical and research settings to assess the social impact of oral health problems on an individual’s quality of life.31 It is a shortened version of the original 49-item OHIP, focusing on 14 key questions that cover various dimensions of oral health-related quality of life. The patients will answer the OHIP-14 items using a Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (very often), to evaluate both the frequency and intensity of oral health impacts. Total scores indicate the quality-of-life impact. Higher scores indicate greater negative impact.

Data analysis

Sample size

The sample size calculation for this study is based on the following two hypothesis tests: null hypothesis (H0): there is no difference in the incidence of tooth loss due to periodontal inflammation between the experimental group and the control group. Alternative hypothesis (H1): the incidence of tooth loss due to periodontal inflammation in the experimental group is lower than that in the control group.

The sample size calculation is determined by the primary outcome measure of the study, namely the incidence of tooth loss attributable to periodontal inflammation. The following clinically significant differences are used in the calculation: a previous prospective study indicated that the incidence of tooth loss in patients with moderate to severe periodontitis following active NSPT is 6.0%, compared with 17.6% in patients with poor adherence to NSPT.32 Based on our preliminary epidemiological findings, it is estimated that patients with stage III periodontitis and CKD receiving only conventional OHI will experience an approximate 30% incidence of tooth loss over a 2-year period.

To detect a significant difference in tooth loss rate between the two groups at a one-sided significance level of 2.5% with 80% power, a total of 36 participants per group is required, based on calculations using PASS software. Accounting for a 20% attrition rate, a final sample size of 43 participants per group is required. Consequently, the null hypothesis will be rejected if the p value is <0.025.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis will be performed on the intention-to-treat (ITT) population, with a sensitivity analysis conducted on the per-protocol set (PPS).

Descriptive statistical methods will be used to summarise the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables will be expressed as either the mean±SD or the median with IQR, depending on the distribution. Categorical variables will be presented as frequencies and percentages. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test will be applied to assess the normality of clinical indicators at baseline and follow-up. The homogeneity of variances will be examined using the Levene’s test.

For the primary outcome, the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test will be used for comparison between groups. For the secondary outcome (number of lost teeth due to periodontitis), a generalised linear model will be applied. If the data follow a normal distribution, a general linear model will be employed, with adjustments made for CKD stage, smoking, hypertension and diabetes. Depending on the normality and homogeneity of variance, the Mann-Whitney U test or Student’s t-test will be used to compare intergroup differences in periodontal clinical data at baseline and end point. For within-group comparisons, the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test or paired t-test will be applied to assess periodontal clinical data at baseline and end point. Repeated measures outcomes will be analysed using linear mixed-effects models/generalised linear mixed models, including group, time and group-by-time interaction terms, with adjustment for relevant baseline covariates.

To assess potential selection bias arising from participant attrition and replacement, the number of withdrawals, their group allocation and reasons for dropout will be reported in detail. A sensitivity analysis will be conducted to compare outcomes between the PPS and the ITT set.

For the primary outcome (the incidence of tooth loss due to periodontitis), a one-sided test will be used, consistent with the superiority hypothesis and sample size calculation. For all secondary outcomes, two-sided tests will be employed with a significance threshold of p<0.05. Statistical analyses and data visualisation will be performed using SPSS V.25.0 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA) and Prism V.8.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, California, USA).

Data quality and assurance

All investigators underwent a formal training and calibration process before the study commenced, including questionnaire administration, masticatory function measurement, clinical periodontal examinations and NSPT. PMPR and OHI will be delivered by experienced periodontists with substantial periodontics experience. They will deliver high-quality NSPT, including making clinical judgements regarding the extraction of hopeless teeth. Two experienced periodontists will be assigned to this study and will be required to participate in an off-line in-person training programme. The training curriculum will cover a detailed review of the study protocol and an explanation of the operational techniques for periodontal scaling and root planing. A standardised procedural checklist will be distributed to ensure high consistency in every step. This checklist will detail critical parameters, including the specific standards and examination methods for periodontal measurements, specific ultrasonic scaler power settings, correct curette angulation and appropriate scaling force.

All assessors will also undergo calibration exercises to standardise their evaluation methods. The intraclass correlation coefficient will be used to assess reliability, with a minimum required value of 0.75.

Regular monitoring will be conducted by the clinical research coordinator to ensure trial standardisation and data quality, including source documents and records and informed consent documentation. This monitoring will be performed independently of the investigators. Additionally, the study will be overseen by the National Clinical Research Center for Oral Diseases and the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital.

Confidentiality

All trial-related data will be securely stored and retrieved at Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. Following the acquisition of raw patient data, researchers will assign unique subject identification numbers for storage purposes. All CRFs will exclude patient-identifiable information, retaining only the assigned subject identification numbers. These identifiers will be securely stored separately from the original data. No patient names or other personally identifying details will be included in any supplementary research data files.

All documents will be retained in secure storage for a minimum of 5 years, with access restricted exclusively to the research team and authorised personnel. Additionally, a master list of enrolled subjects will be maintained to facilitate linkage with CRFs, clinical data records, chewing efficiency test gum scans and intraoral clinical photographs.

Ethics approval and dissemination

The study protocol and informed consent were approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Ninth People’s Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (SH9H-2022-T404-1) (protocol V.1.0, dated 1 March 2023). Prior to voluntarily signing the consent form, each participant will be informed in detail of specific intervention measures and potential adverse events. All participants, including those in the control group, will receive comprehensive information regarding the study design, including the rationale for postponing therapy, the plan for delayed treatment and the monitoring procedures. They will be explicitly informed that they may withdraw from the study at any time without penalty and will still receive the standard medical care. The Institutional Ethics Committee specifically reviewed the delayed treatment design for the control group and concluded that, given the strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, the provision of standard OHIs and the robust safety monitoring with an immediate rescue protocol, the potential risks to participants are minimal, justified and ethically acceptable. All patient data will be maintained in strict confidence and securely archived throughout all stages of the trial.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were consulted during the trial design stage and their perspectives on key oral health concerns helped refine the research question. The follow-up schedule and study duration were established based on patient feedback, the European Federation of Periodontology (EFP) S3-level clinical practice guideline and overall study feasibility, with the aim of improving participant acceptability and supporting adherence to follow-up. Patients and the public will not be involved in participant recruitment, trial conduct, outcome assessment or data analysis. On study completion, participants will receive a summary of the main study findings in accessible language via email, WeChat or during follow-up visits.

Discussion

Prior research has indicated that individuals with fewer teeth tend to exhibit impaired nutrient intake and compromised nutritional status.14 33 Additionally, poor oral hygiene is well established to impair masticatory ability, which is associated with reduced protein and energy intake34 and may elevate the risk of malnutrition and mortality in patients with CKD. Accordingly, the present study is designed to investigate the effects of NSPT supplemented by SPC on tooth loss and masticatory function and oral health-related quality of life in patients with CKD. Should the intervention prove efficacious, periodontal health may be recognised as a significant factor in the management of patients with CKD.

Despite a substantial body of evidence demonstrating the clinical benefits of NSPT in patients with CKD, the methodological quality of these studies is often suboptimal.35 36 A recent report indicated that 92% of studies assessing the impact of non-surgical periodontal treatment on systemic diseases were assigned ‘low’ confidence ratings.37 Furthermore, these studies exhibited considerable heterogeneity, primarily due to variations in study designs and diagnostic criteria. In contrast, periodontitis diagnosis in the present study strictly adheres to the 2017 World Workshop on the Classification of Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases and Conditions,2 and periodontal intervention is guided by the EFP S3 level clinical practice guidelines.20 Most published clinical trials have follow-up periods of no more than 12 months, which is insufficient to observe actual tooth loss events and long-term changes in masticatory function as a result of periodontal intervention therapy. Therefore, the follow-up period is extended to 24 months in the present study to enable a more reliable evaluation of the efficacy of NSPT supplemented by SPC in preventing tooth loss and preserving masticatory function.

Key strengths of this study include its longitudinal design, standardised treatment protocols and comprehensive follow-up schedule. Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, patient non-adherence and loss to follow-up may diminish the statistical power of the results. Second, the presence of confounding factors, including age, CKD stage and comorbidities such as diabetes, makes it difficult to establish a clear causal relationship between CKD and periodontitis. To ensure the validity of our findings, these confounding factors will be meticulously recorded at baseline to enable rigorous adjustment during data analysis. Additionally, the generalisability of the findings to other ethnic populations may be limited, as the trial cohort consists exclusively of Chinese participants.

Given the frequent neglect of periodontal health in the management of systemic diseases, this study may offer valuable insights for the integrated care of patients with CKD. It highlights the potential role of periodontal therapy as a component of a holistic treatment strategy.
