Key factors influencing the adoption of HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) among commercial sex workers in Nekemte Town, Western Ethiopia, 2023
Authors: Matiyos Lema, Amanuel Lamessa, Edosa Tesfaye Geta, Adisu Tekle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe rate of HIV among female sex workers in Ethiopia ranges from 14% to 28%, significantly surpassing the adult prevalence rate in the country, which stands at only 0.9%. Female sex workers must adopt pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) due to their heightened vulnerability to HIV, attributed to factors such as inconsistent condom use and frequent encounters with multiple sexual partners. PrEP serves as an essential biological prevention strategy that effectively reduces the likelihood of HIV infection when taken consistently. Beyond safeguarding their own health, enhancing the uptake of PrEP among female sex workers is vital for comprehensive HIV prevention initiatives, as it contributes to a decrease in overall HIV transmission within the community.MethodsA facility-based cross-sectional study was done on 422 HIV-negative female sex workers (FSWs) attending HIV negative female sex workers aged 18 years and above at key population friendly clinics in Nekemte town from January 15 to February 28, 2023. Participants were selected using systematic random sampling from client registration lists in two key population-friendly facilities. A pre-tested interviewer-administered questionnaire was utilized to collect data related to the study objectives. Data were entered into Epi Data version 4.1 and exported to SPSS version 24 statistical software for analysis. Binary logistic regression analysis was used to see the association between independent and response variables. P < 0.05 was used to declare the statistical significance.ResultsPrEP uptake among female commercial sex workers in Nekemte town was found to be 43.30% [38.6%, 48.1%]. PrEP use was significantly associated with age greater than 24 years (AOR = 2.03; 95%CI: 1.21, 3.38), awareness of PrEP (AOR = 2.53, 95%CI: 1.61, 3.98), HIV risk perception (AOR = 1.89, 95%CI: 1.20, 2.94), and having flexible working hours for service (AOR = 2.89, 95%CI: 1.85, 4.53). The study indicates a need to increase awareness of PrEP and provide better support for younger female sex workers, improve educational efforts, foster a correct understanding of HIV risk, and offer more flexible service hours. Nevertheless, no notable association were identified between the use of PrEP and factors such as substance abuse, condom usage, religious beliefs, or attitudes toward HIV, which might be attributed to variations in population characteristics and differences in healthcare systems.Keywords: HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis utilization, Female sex workers, Nekemte town

Background

HIV continues to be a significant public health issue, especially affecting key populations like female sex workers (FSWs). In 2023, UNAIDS projected that roughly 39 million individuals were living with HIV worldwide, with 1.3 million new cases diagnosed during the year. The global HIV prevalence rate among adults aged 15–49 was recorded at 0.7%. However, due to various risk factors coupled with discrimination, marginalization, and in some instances, criminalization, there was a higher median HIV prevalence found in certain groups: 2.7% among sex workers, 7.1% among people who inject drugs, 8.5% among transgender individuals, 1.4% among incarcerated individuals, and 7.6% among gay men and other men who have sex with men [1].

Due to factors such as their work environment, poverty, stigma, and discrimination, female sex workers (FSWs) face an increased likelihood of contracting HIV. Their vulnerability is exacerbated by high-risk activities, including drug use, having multiple sexual partners, and inconsistent condom use [1–5]. In 2023, the global median HIV prevalence among sex workers was noted at 3.0% (with a range of 0–62%, across 72 countries reporting). This figure is significantly higher than the estimated global prevalence of 0.7% for the total population aged 15–49 years [6]. In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), there is regional variation, but the estimated HIV prevalence among FSWs is between 10 and 20 times greater than that in the general population [7].

Compared to the general public, sex workers are more likely to contract HIV; this risk has decreased from being 12 times higher in 2010 to 9 times higher in 2022. During the period from 2010 to 2022, the incidence of newly acquired HIV cases in sub-Saharan Africa dropped by 50%. Nonetheless, stigma, violence, harsh laws, and criminalization hinder social justice and equality for sex workers, limiting their access to HIV and reproductive health services [8].

In Ethiopia, female sex workers (FSWs) exhibit a significantly higher HIV prevalence compared to the general population, indicating a concentrated outbreak. A national survey conducted across 16 cities found that the average HIV prevalence among FSWs was 18.7% (95% CI: 17.8–19.7%), with notable differences across regions. The prevalence rates were as follows: Nekemte 19.8%, Adama 16.1%, Gambella 21.6%, Jimma 24.8%, Mizan 15.3%, Hawassa 14.4%, Arba Minch 15.4%, Dilla 16.7%, Dessie 20.3%, Gonder 20%, Bahirdar 28%, Semera 17.5%, Diredawa 23.7%, Harar 23.1%, Addis Ababa 15.4%, and Shashemene 14% [9].

To lower the rate of HIV transmission, barrier methods like the use of condoms have historically been central to global HIV prevention strategies. Recently, innovative protection methods, encompassing biomedical approaches, antiretroviral treatments, and PrEP, have been introduced. Nonetheless, despite these advanced measures, the HIV epidemic remains largely unmanaged [10].

Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) involves the administration of antiretroviral (ARV) medications to prevent HIV infection in individuals who are HIV-negative and at high risk of contracting the virus [11]. It serves as a vital complementary approach to diminish HIV transmission when used alongside condom utilization, counseling, and timely diagnosis and treatment. PrEP is advised for preventing HIV transmission in individuals considered to be at significant risk of contracting HIV, defined by incidence rates equal to or exceeding 3 per 100 person-years in its absence [12].

Pre-exposure prophylaxis is a recommended method for preventing HIV in individuals who are HIV-negative but at significant risk of contracting the virus, with female sex workers being one of the primary groups targeted for PrEP services globally [13]. Research has demonstrated that pre-exposure prophylaxis can effectively decrease the rate of HIV infection in high-risk populations by over 90%. Unlike many other HIV prevention methods, PrEP offers female sex workers a highly effective option that they can use independently, without needing to negotiate, gain commitment, or obtain consent from their partners. Despite these benefits, the adoption of PrEP among female sex workers remains low [14].

As of 2024, 162 nations had integrated WHO recommendations for pre-exposure prophylaxis into their protocols, yet the target for prevention in 2025 remains unattainable. UNAIDS reports that at least half of key populations are not accessing prevention services, funding for HIV prevention efforts is inadequate, cost-effective strategies are seeing limited adoption, and access to prevention resources is still inequitable [15].

In 2023, over 3.5 million individuals accessed PrEP at least one time. More than 75% of this group (2.6 million) was located in the African region. The number of individuals using PrEP grew by 35% from 2022 to 2023, yet these figures remain below the goal of reaching 10 million users by 2025 [16].

Increased and broader access to PrEP has the potential to significantly lower the rates of new HIV infections, particularly among key populations and women in regions with high HIV incidence. The number of individuals utilizing oral PrEP increased from just over 200,000 in 2017 to approximately 3.5 million in 2023; however, this figure still falls considerably short of the global target of 21.2 million by 2025 [17].

Ethiopia has advanced in including PrEP in its strategies for HIV prevention [1, 18]. Although there is increasing interest in HIV prevention approaches such as PrEP, there is still limited information on the actual uptake of PrEP among female sex workers (FSWs) in Ethiopia, and the factors influencing PrEP use among various geographic regions and different subgroups of FSWs are not well understood. This study is sheds light on the adoption of HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis among female commercial sex workers in the area studied, a group that is particularly vulnerable to HIV infection. By pointing critical factors that affect PrEP usage, the study reveals significant shortcomings in existing HIV prevention initiatives. The results provide concrete evidence that can guide the development of more focused, inclusive, and effective strategies to enhance PrEP accessibility and utilization. Additionally, the study aligns with national and regional efforts to achieve HIV prevention objectives and contributes to a greater understanding of how to mitigate HIV transmission and enhance health outcomes for marginalized and underserved communities.

Methods and materials

Study design, study setting, and study period

A cross-sectional study was conducted in Nekemte town, Ethiopia, using a Social Networking Strategy to recruit HIV services for FSWs. Nekemte town is located 331 km to the west of the capital city, Addis Ababa. The town is divided into six administrative divisions and one rural kebele. There are four public health facilities in the town: Nekemte Specialized Hospital (NSH), Wallaga University Referral Hospital (WURH), Nekemte Health Center, and Cheleleki Health Center. Nekemte and Cheleleki Health centers are key population-friendly clinic sites providing HIV care, treatment, and prevention services for FSWs. According to the HIV program report of the town, the number of HIV-negative FSWs recruited through SNS at key population-friendly sites was 1857, with Nekemte HC covering 931 and the remaining 926 covered by Cheleleki HC [19]. The study was conducted from January 15 to February 28, 2023.

Population

The source population consisted of HIV negative commercial sex workers (CSWs) residing and working in Nekemte Town, Western Ethiopia, who were aged 18 years and above and had been actively engaged in sex work for at least the past six months. While HIV-negative female sex workers who visited the key population-friendly clinics of Nekemte town during the data collection period were the study population.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All HIV-negative Female sex workers aged 18 years or above were included in this study while Women sex workers whose HIV status was unknown (either had never undergone testing for HIV or were unaware of their current status) and those who were unable to take part due to severe illness or hospitalization were excluded.

Sample size determination and sampling procedures

The sample size was determined using a single population proportion formula, with a 95% level of significance, a 5% margin of error, a 10% non-response rate, and a 50% probability of HIV-negative FSWs being PrEP usersSince no prior studies were available to provide an estimate of the proportion of the outcome of interest in the target population, a conservative proportion of 50% was chosen. This approach maximizes the sample size and thus ensures sufficient statistical power [20, 21].

by considering 10% non-response rate, the final sample size was 422.

Nekemte Health Center and Cheleleki Health Center were intentionally chosen because they are friendly to key populations and offer comprehensive HIV prevention and care services specifically designed for CSWs and other high-risk groups. Systematic random sampling was utilized to identify study participants. Based on service records, it was anticipated that a total of 858 CSWs would visit the two selected clinics during the study period, with 442 expected from Nekemte Health Center (NHC) and 416 from Cheleleki Health Center. The overall sample size of 422 was proportionately divided between the two facilities: 217 from NHC and 205 from Cheleleki Health Center. The sampling interval (k) was established by dividing the estimated number of individuals at each facility by its designated sample size. The computed sampling interval (k) was 2. At each location, every second eligible participant was chosen after randomly selecting the first respondent from the initial two using a lottery method.

Data collection tools and procedures

The study utilized a pre-tested structured interviewer-administered questionnaire adapted from various literature [22–27] to gather data on socio-demographic, sexual, and behavioral characteristics, knowledge and attitude related to HIV and PrEP, and institutional factors. The questionnaire was developed in English, translated into Afan Oromo, and re-translated to English for consistency. The tool were assessed for its relevance, with certain items chosen and adjusted to align with the local context and study objectives. Modifications involved rewording culturally sensitive inquiries, incorporating locally appropriate response options, and simplifying technical terminology to improve participant comprehension. Expert review by public health professionals with experience in HIV programming and research on key populations was conducted to ensure content validity. The data was gathered by four qualified health professionals: two BSc Nurse Providers who have experience in HIV counseling and PrEP service provision, along with two BSc Case Detection Officers involved in outreach programs for key populations. Each data collector underwent a two-day training session focused on the study’s objectives, the questionnaire content, ethical considerations, and methods for conducting confidential and respectful interviews. Daily supervision of data collection was implemented to ensure quality, consistency, and adherence to the established protocol.

Study variables

Dependent variable

HIV Pre-exposure prophylaxis utilization.

Independent variables

Socio-demographic factors: -Age, Ethnicity, religion (orthodox, protestant, Muslim, others), Marital status (single, married, divorced, widowed, cohabited), Residence, Educational level (no formal education, primary, secondary, tertiary), occupation, income.

Institution-related factors: accessibility, the attitude of providers, flexible working time.

HIV and PrEP-related factors: knowledge of HIV, awareness of PrEP, perceived risk of HIV, attitude towards HIV, HIV-related stigma.

Sexual and behavioral factors: -duration in sex work, condom utilization, substance use (Alcohol, khat, and smoking), sex work-related stigma, having regular partners.

Operational definitions

Pre-exposure Prophylaxis utilization:-is the uptake or use of PrEP as an HIV prevention service(yes/no) which can be current users clients using PrEP during data collection; or Past users clients who had used PrEP previously but were not using at the time of data collection, which could include clients who had discontinued use or those who might be cycling on and off PrEP; or never users-clients who had never used oral PrEP. It was measured as whether a female CSW has ever taken PrEP, with “Yes” indicating ever use coded [1] and “No” indicating never use coded (0) [24, 28].

Accessibility of service: -getting a comprehensive package of HIV services recommended for FSWs at a KP-friendly clinic.

The attitude of providers: Attitude of providers:-was determined by asking the participants’ level of agreement with attitude questions and the response was rated based on a 5-point Likert response (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = Neutral, 4 = agree and 5 = strongly agree). The binary coding for attitudes was determined using the overall mean score as a cutoff point. First, For each participant, a mean score was calculated by averaging their responses across all attitude-related items. Then, the overall mean was calculated by averaging these individual mean scores across all participants. Based on the overall mean, responses were categorized into two groups: those with individual mean scores equal to or above the overall mean were coded as having a positive attitude, while those with scores below the overall mean were coded as having a negative attitude [25].

Flexible working time:- was determined by asking whether the participants can get service outside the normal working hours(yes/no) [24].

HIV related Knowledge- Knowledge of HIV was assessed using (Yes/No) questions each correct responses were coded [1] while incorrect responses were coded (0). The total score for each participant was calculated by summing all correct responses. Participants who scored below 50% of the total possible marks were classified as having poor knowledge, while those scoring 50% and above were classified as having good knowledge [28].

Pre-exposure Prophylaxis awareness: -was assessed with “Pre-exposure prophylaxis is the use of any medication before having sex as protection against HIV infection. Have you heard of PrEP?” (yes/no) [29].

HIV risk perception:- was assessed by asking participants ‘How much do you think you are at risk of getting HIV being an FSW?’; then answers were rated based on a 5-point Likert response (1 = very low, 2 = low, 3 = somewhat, 4 = much and 5 = very much). For analysis, First, the mean score of all participants’ responses on the 5-point Likert scale was calculated. Then, individual scores equal to or above the mean were categorized as high HIV risk perception, while scores below the mean are coded as low HIV risk perception [30].

Attitude towards service related to HIV: the respondents’ attitude was assesed by asking attitude questions towards HIV-related service (5 points Likert scale) and was categorized as negative attitude (scored below the mean value) and positive attitude (scored equal or above the mean value) for data analysis.

HIV/AIDs related prejudicial attitude: participants’ prejudicial attitude toward PLWHA was assessed by asking about the level of agreement with HIV/AIDs-related issues and answers were rated based on a 5-Likert response The mean score of each participant was calculated, and the overall mean of all participants was used as the cutoff. Scores equal to or above the mean indicated a good prejudicial attitude, while scores below indicated a poor prejudicial attitude [31].

Attitude toward PrEP drugs: Respondents’ attitudes regarding PrEP medications were assessed using a 5-Likert scale. The overall mean of all participants served as the cutoff point for “negative attitude,” which was defined as a score below the mean value, and “positive attitude,” which was defined as a score equal to or higher than the mean value.

Duration in sex work was determined by asking the participants how many months they stayed in sex work [24].

Condom utilization: -use of condom correctly and consistently at every act of sex (yes/no).

Substance use: -To assess substance use before sex, participants were asked “The last time you had sex with a sexual client, did you use alcohol/drugs before?” (Yes/no).

Sex work-related stigma/discrimination: participants were asked items that capture aspects of stigma and discrimination related to being a sex worker (yes/no) and categorized as there was sex work-related sigma(yes to all questions) or not [32].

Data quality assurance

Throughout the data collection process, quality checks were conducted to ensure the completeness and accuracy of the data. Two BSc Nurses, serving as data collectors and supervisors, designed and tested the data collection methodology meticulously. The questionnaire was pilot-tested on 5% of HIV-negative FSWs at Gimbi Health Center. Based on the feedback from the pilot test, minor adjustments were made, including simplifying technical terminology, rearranging some questions for logical coherence, and refining the Likert-scale response options to minimize confusion and enhance response accuracy. Any necessary adjustments were addressed. The gathered data was consistently reviewed on a daily basis by supervisors and the principal investigator for completeness, accuracy, and consistency. Supervisors examined the completed questionnaires daily to detect and rectify any missing or inconsistent answers. Any errors or unclear responses were swiftly addressed with the data collectors before moving on to further data collection.

Data processing and analysis

The questionnaires were checked for completeness and consistency, and the data was pre-coded and exported to SPSS version 24 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were performed, and all independent variables with a p-value < 0.25 association with the dependent variable were selected for multivariable analysis model to avoid excluding potentially important factors that might become significant when adjusted for others. The multivariable analysis by enter selection method was used to determine a statistically significant association between independent and dependent variables to include all selected variables at once, allowing for simultaneous control of confounding factors, and the degree of association between explanatory variables and the dependent variable was measured using Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) with 95% CI. The study used the Hosmer and Lemeshow goodness of model fit test to examine the fitness test, with a p-value of 0.89 indicating good model fit, as there was no significant difference between observed and predicted outcomes.

Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with covariates with a VIF value < 5 indicating low multicollinearity and suggesting that the predictor variables did not distort the model estimatesThe VIF was ranged between 1 and 1.2, acceptable for multicollinearity, and no variable was removed due to multi-collinearity.

Additionally, 28.5% had fewer than two children, with the majority having a family size of less than four. Sexual and behavioral characteristics

PrEP was used by 48.1% of individuals with consistent sexual partners, compared to 35.3% among those without such partners. Among individuals involved in sex work for over a year, the rate of PrEP usage was 45.5%, whereas it was 41.1% for those who had been sex workers for less than a year. The data revealed that among female sex workers who saw four or more clients each week, 42.3% utilized PrEP, while the rate was 43.8% for those with fewer than four clients. The usage of PrEP among female commercial sex workers (CSWs) who began engaging in sexual activity before turning 18 was 44.2%, compared to 41.7% for those who started at age 18 or older. For individuals who did not use condoms, PrEP usage was recorded at 50%, while among those who did use condoms, it was 41.1%. The prevalence of PrEP among female CSWs who perceived stigma associated with their profession was 37.5%, compared to 43.4% for those who did not feel such stigma. A notable percentage (60.3%) of the female CSWs in this study reported using substances. Among CSWs with a history of substance use, the PrEP usage rate was 37.3%, while those without a history of substance use reported a rate of 52.45%. Table 2.

HIV& pre-exposure prophylaxis related characteristics

The frequency of PrEP usage is higher among individuals with a strong understanding of HIV (43.5%) in comparison to those who have limited knowledge (42.9%), suggesting a minor link between knowledge and PrEP usage. Those who hold a positive perspective on HIV (48.7%) and possess an increased sense of risk (48.4%) are more likely to utilize PrEP than those lacking these views, indicating that favorable attitudes and perceived risk can influence preventive behaviors. There is a notable relationship between awareness of PrEP and its utilization, as individuals who know about PrEP (52.7%) are more inclined to use it than those who do not (34.4%). Nonetheless, awareness of PrEP in the study group is low (48.6%), highlighting the need to boost awareness through specific educational and outreach initiatives. Access to services and the attitudes of healthcare professionals also play a role in PrEP uptake; individuals with access (44.8%) and those who receive positive reinforcement from providers are more likely to engage in PrEP. Additionally, flexible working hours are associated with higher PrEP usage (55.1%), emphasizing the significance of accommodating workplace conditions. Despite these associations, overall awareness and utilization of PrEP remain at a moderate level, suggesting a necessity for targeted efforts to improve knowledge, attitudes, and access to services for this at-risk population. Factors associated with HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis utilization

The study employed binary logistic regression analysis to identify independent variables associated with PrEP utilization. Factors with a P value less than 0.25, including age, religion, family size, PrEP awareness, HIV-related attitude, risk perception, regular partners, condom utilization, flexible working time, and substance use, were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. Age, PrEP awareness, risk perception, and flexible working time remained significantly associated with PrEP utilization at a 95% confidence level.

The study indicated that individuals aged 24 and older had a higher likelihood of using PrEP compared to those younger than 24 (AOR = 2.03; 95%CI: 1.21, 3.38). Those who were aware about PrEP were 2.53 times more likely to utilize it (AOR = 2.53, 95%CI: 1.61, 3.98). Female sex workers (FSWs) who perceived a higher risk of HIV were 1.88 times more probable to adopt PrEP compared to those with a low-risk perception (AOR = 1.88, 95% CI: 1.20, 2.94). Discussion

This study aimed to assess HIV PrEP utilization and associated factors among FSWs in Nekemte town who visited key population-friendly clinics.The study revealed a 43.30% [95%CI: 0.386 0.481] of Female Sex Workers in Nekemte Town was using PrEP for HIV prevention. This figure is consistent with the same study in Bahir Dar City, North-West Ethiopia (46.2%) [33]. The comparable rates of PrEP utilization might result from the two study environments likely implementing similar HIV prevention approaches, exhibiting similar healthcare systems, and offering analogous access to services for key populations, which includes awareness and provision of PrEP. Given that both studies were conducted in urban areas, the populations may possess similar levels of knowledge, risk awareness, and interaction with healthcare providers, which could explain the consistent PrEP uptake.

However, the PrEP utilization rate found in this study is slightly higher than those reported in Zimbabwe (37.1%) [34], Cameroon (37%) [35], China (35.5%) [29], and rural West Virginia (3.7%) [36]. The Variations may arise from elements such as access to healthcare, awareness levels, and the extent to which PrEP services are integrated into sexual and reproductive health initiatives for these populations. The slightly higher rate observed uptake in the study area might indicate more targeted interventions, enhanced community outreach, or increased backing for PrEP among female sex workers by local health authorities. Furthermore, factors such as stigma, the organization of healthcare systems, and policy initiatives contribute to the variations in PrEP usage in different areas.

In contrast, The PrEP utilization rate observed in this study is somewhat less than that reported in a study from Addis Ababa(67.9%) [37], Tanzania(54.7%) [38], Kenya (62%) [24], Uganda(66.3%) [39], Senegal(82.4%) [40], and Bostwana(72.9%) [41]. The variations in the uptake of PrEP programs may stem from differences in assessment methods used across various studies. The current study examined actual uptake, while previous studies looked at willingness to uptake. The observed differences might also relate to the study environment, outreach strategies, community engagement levels, and health disparities in PrEP programs across different countries or regions.

The study indicates a significant relationship between age and PrEP utilization among female sex workers (FSWs). Specifically, FSWs over the age of 24 and above demonstrated a higher likelihood of using PrEP compared to younger counterparts. Indicating the necessity of Targeted approaches including youth-friendly outreach clinics, peer-led education, and digital campaigns with accessible content can be useful to improve risk perception among younger FSWs or those with low perceived HIV risk. This aligns with findings from previous research, such as a study in Zimbabwe [42], which similarly associated increased age with higher PrEP uptake. The rationale behind this correlation could stem from older individuals being more sexually active with multiple partners, thereby increasing their exposure to HIV and the perceived need for preventive measures. However, contrasting reports from studies in Kenya [24] and Ghana [43], where older FSWs were less inclined to use PrEP, suggest variations in findings influenced by differences in methodologies and sociocultural contexts. These variations may be correlated with other factors like levels of PrEP awareness, and barriers such as stigma, mistrust, or limited access to health services. Cultural beliefs and age-related differences in attitudes toward medical prevention may also affect uptake, underscoring the need for context-specific interpretations of PrEP utilization patterns.

The study also indicates that FSWs who had a greater awareness of HIV risk were nearly twice as inclined to use PrEP compared to those with a lesser perception of risk. This outcome resonates with findings from various global studies in South Africa [28], the United States [44], the UK [45], and Kenya [24], where a diminished perception of HIV risk acted as a deterrent to PrEP adoption. This suggests that accurate risk perception plays a crucial role in motivating individuals to engage in preventive healthcare behaviors such as PrEP uptake. Individuals are more likely to adopt preventive behaviors when they perceive high personal risk, such as FSWs recognizing their vulnerability to HIV [46].

An additional important finding from the study is that flexible working hours have a beneficial effect on PrEP usage among female sex workers (FSWs). The data indicates that FSWs who have flexible work arrangements were almost three times more inclined to use PrEP.This result is in line with previous research in Kenya [24] and Nepal [47], and South Africa [3] emphasizing the importance of convenient clinic opening hours in facilitating PrEP uptake among vulnerable populations. Flexible working hours have a positive effect on PrEP utilization among female sex workers (FSWs), as they enable individuals to attend medical appointments and follow their medication regimen without affecting their earnings or work commitments. This underscores the importance of collaboration among healthcare providers, community organizations, and policymakers to establish supportive conditions by implementing flexible working hours for female sex workers, which can greatly enhance access to and adherence to PrEP. For example, successful initiatives such as Sisters with a Voice in Zimbabwe and TAPS in South Africa demonstrate that community-based service delivery, peer outreach, and multi-month dispensing have been effective in alleviating obstacles and promoting adherence to PrEP. The study provides valuable and timely insights into the utilization of HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis and related factors among female sex workers in Nekemte Town, Ethiopia. By focusing on a vulnerable group that is significantly impacted by HIV, the study addresses a critical gap in regional data and offers essential evidence to inform targeted initiatives. Nonetheless, the study’s generalizability might be limited by its specific inclusion criteria and the fact that participants were mainly recruited from urban locations, potentially neglecting the experiences of sex workers from rural or more marginalized areas. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported data raises the possibility of social desirability bias, particularly regarding sexual behavior and PrEP adherence. Future research should aim to include a more diverse and representative population of female sex workers, especially those not accessing key population-friendly clinics, to improve generalizability. Employing qualitative methods would also provide a deeper understanding of the personal, social, and structural factors influencing PrEP uptake and adherence. It is recommended to carry out longitudinal studies to explore causal relationships and assess changes in behavior and service engagement over time.

Conclusion

The study showed that the uptake of HIV Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) among female sex workers in the region is considerably below the national target of 90%. Various factors influence the acceptance of PrEP, including age, perceptions of HIV risk, knowledge of PrEP, and having adaptable work hours. To enhance the use of PrEP among female sex workers, the Ministry of Health, local health authorities, NGOs, and community organizations should implement specific strategies that address critical factors. Tailored educational and support programs aimed at different age groups can effectively engage diverse populations, while utilizing peer educators to improve risk communication can enhance awareness of HIV susceptibility. Boosting overall awareness through mass media initiatives and community involvement is essential. Furthermore, public health facilities and NGOs should provide flexible service hours and mobile clinics to accommodate the erratic schedules of sex workers. These collaborative efforts will increase the availability and acceptance of PrEP, ultimately promoting its uptake within this high-risk group.
