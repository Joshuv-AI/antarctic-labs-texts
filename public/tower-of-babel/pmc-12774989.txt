Single-port robotic restorative proctectomy with ileal pouch–anal anastomosis for ulcerative colitis: first clinical experience and technical insights
Authors: Gabriele Bislenghi, Albert Wolthuis, André D’Hoore
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRestorative proctocolectomy with ileal pouch–anal anastomosis (IPAA) is the standard surgical option for patients with ulcerative colitis (UC) wishing to avoid a permanent ileostomy. Single-port (SP) robotic surgery offers enhanced dexterity and visualization, potentially overcoming the limitations of single-port laparoscopy in the confined pelvic cavity. Its role in IPAA for UC is unexplored.MethodsPatients with ulcerative colitis undergoing SP robotic proctectomy with IPAA at a tertiary center between October 2024 and June 2025 were reviewed. Procedures followed a standardized three-step approach with transanal transection single stapled (TTSS) anastomosis. Feasibility was defined as completion of the procedure without unplanned conversion to multiport laparoscopy, laparotomy, or transanal minimally invasive surgery (TAMIS). Safety by 30-day postoperative morbidity, readmission, and pouch leak rates was analyzed.ResultsA total of 14 patients (median age 34 years; body mass index (BMI) 26.2 kg/m2) were included. Median operative time was 237 min (range 188–317 min). Hospital stay was 5.8 days (range 3–12 days). No conversions occurred; one case required the unplanned placement of additional 5-mm assistant trocar. Four patients (28.5%) developed postoperative complications. The mean Comprehensive Complication Index was 7.6 ± 12.8 (SD). Three patients (21.4%) required readmission within 30 days postoperatively. All patients underwent ileostomy closure at a median of 64 days after IPAA creation.ConclusionsSP robotic proctectomy with IPAA is feasible and safe in unselected patients with UC, supporting further large prospective evaluation.

Introduction

Restorative proctocolectomy with ileal pouch–anal anastomosis (IPAA) remains the standard surgical treatment for ulcerative colitis (UC) in patients requiring colectomy who wish to avoid a permanent ileostomy [1].

Over the past decades, the technique has undergone substantial evolution and refinement [2]. Recently, single-port (SP) robotic surgery has emerged, combining the benefits of single-port laparoscopy with those of robotic assistance—enhanced dexterity, three-dimensional visualization, tremor filtration, and improved ergonomics—allowing for greater precision and control, in particular during complex pelvic procedures [3].

Although SP robotic surgery has been evaluated in a limited number of colorectal cancer series [4], its role in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), particularly in IPAA for UC, remain unexplored.

The aim of this study is to present our first experience with SP robotic proctectomy with IPAA for UC, focusing on surgical technique, feasibility, and safety.

Methods

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study using data from a prospectively maintained pouch database at the University Hospitals Leuven, Belgium, a tertiary referral center for IBD. All patients undergoing SP robotic proctectomy with IPAA for UC as a second surgical step after previous minimally invasive total colectomy between October 2024 and June 2025 were identified.

The study was conducted within the framework of the Crohn's disease and ulcerative Colitis Advanced REserach (CCARE) registry and approved by the Institutional Review Board (B322201213950/S53684).

Surgical technique

All patients underwent a total colectomy either through a single-port (SP) or a multiport laparoscopic approach. For the second stage of surgery (proctectomy with IPAA construction) patients were positioned in modified Lloyd–Davis and Trendelenburg. All procedures were performed by one colorectal surgeon (GB) using the da Vinci® Single-Port (SP) robotic platform. The robotic port was introduced through a Pfannenstiel incision or the existing ileostomy site, with an additional assistant port placed in either the left or right flank, which served for the placement of a suction drain at the end of the operation (Fig. 1). The procedure started with a complete mobilization of the root of the mesentery with or without high ligation of the ileocolic vessels. Proctectomy proceeded along the total mesorectal excision (TME) plane to the anorectal ring. Transanal minimally invasive surgery (TAMIS) port was used in the first three cases to aid distal dissection, but subsequent cases were completed robotically without TAMIS assistance. Stapled J-pouch was fashioned via the abdominal incision used for the robotic port, with 18-French catheter attached to the anvil of a 29-mm Echelon© circular stapler (©2022 Ethicon US, LLC). Transanal transection single-stapled (TTSS) anastomosis was performed [5]. The anus was exposed using a Lone Star retractor (Cooper Surgical, Trumbull, Connecticut, USA) in combination with a Single Use Anoscope Kit (Frankenman, Hong Kong, China). A purse-string suture was placed 3 cm above the dentate line, followed by a circular incision distal to it to reestablish the plane created abdominally. The specimen was extracted transanally, and the 18-French catheter was also retrieved through the anus. A second purse-string was placed at the rectal cuff, the pouch was advanced, and the anastomosis completed after connecting the anvil to the stapler shaft. In the final six cases, the pouch was constructed with posterior folding and anterior mesenteric orientation to optimize reach and prevent twisting during transanal delivery (Fig. 2). Data collection and outcomes

Demographic and operative data were collected. The primary outcomes were feasibility and safety. Feasibility was defined as completion of the procedure without conversion to multiport laparoscopy, laparotomy, or TAMIS, or unplanned placement of additional assistant trocars. Safety was assessed by 30-day postoperative morbidity and readmission rates, using the Clavien–Dindo classification and the Comprehensive Complication Index (CCI) [6]. Anastomotic leakage was diagnosed radiologically (contrast extravasation or sinus tract on computed tomography (CT) or contrast enema) or endoscopically/examination under anesthesia, either early after pouch creation or before ileostomy closure.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as median (range) or mean (standard deviation (SD)) and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

A total of 14 patients (10 men) with a median age of 34 years (range 20–59 years) were included. The median BMI was 26.2 kg/m2 (range 16.4–37.0 kg/m2). American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score was 2 in 11 patients (78.6%) and 3 in 3 patients (21.4%).

Median operative time was 237 min (range 188–317 min). All but one patient (92.9%) underwent a three-stage approach. No patient required conversion to laparoscopy or laparotomy. In one patient, an additional 5 mm trocar was placed in the right hypochondrium to facilitate optimal pouch orientation before firing the TTSS anastomosis. In the last 11 cases, where TAMIS dissection was not planned preoperatively, no intraoperative conversion to TAMIS was necessary.

Median VAS score ranged from 0 to 10 during the first postoperative days as shown in Fig. 3. Median peak C-reactive protein (CRP) was 55.9 mg/L (range 19.3–199.2 mg/L). Median hospital stay was 5.8 days (range 3–12 days). Postoperative complications occurred in four patients (28.5%), three after discharge. One patient developed a presacral hematoma requiring laparoscopic and percutaneous drainage (Clavien–Dindo IIIb). Two patients experienced mild intraluminal pouch bleeding, successfully managed endoscopically with clips (Clavien–Dindo IIIa). One patient developed a small bowel obstruction due to stoma outlet narrowing, treated conservatively with an endoluminal catheter (Clavien–Dindo II). The mean CCI was 7.6 ± 12.8. Three patients (21.4%) required readmission within 30 days postoperatively. Reasons for readmission were small bowel obstruction, endoluminal bleeding, and fever and pain due to a presacral hematoma. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first reported series of SP robotic IPAA for UC. Previous SP robotic colorectal experience has focused primarily on colorectal cancer, particularly on TME [7].

Ileoanal pouch surgery has undergone substantial evolution over the past two decades [2]. The widespread availability of advanced medical therapies, along with the changing patients’ and disease characteristics, has led to staged approach in most patients with therapy-refractory UC [8]. This trend has been reinforced by the adoption of minimally invasive techniques, which reduce postoperative complications, enable faster recovery, and lower the risk of intraperitoneal adhesions [9].

Single port laparoscopy offers additional benefits, including improved cosmesis, but it is inherently limited by technical challenges such as instrument crowding, lack of triangulation, restricted visibility, and external collisions through a single access site [10]. The SP robotic platform addresses many of these limitations by enhancing dexterity and maneuverability, particularly within the narrow confines of the pelvis [11].

In patients with UC who previously underwent minimally invasive total colectomy, the existing ileostomy site provides an ideal access for SP robotic proctectomy and IPAA as second stage of the surgical strategy. The combination of TME dissection and TTSS anastomosis [5] allows for full exploitation of the SP robot platform’s advantages while avoiding the use of additional costly instruments—particularly relevant given the current unavailability of vessel sealer devices and staplers for the SP robot.

In the present series, the procedure was standardized into three sequential steps: mesenteric mobilization, proctectomy along TME planes, and pouch creation with TTSS anastomosis. In the first three cases, distal rectal dissection was performed using transanal minimally invasive surgery (TAMIS). With growing experience, it became clear that complete dissection down to the anorectal ring could be achieved robotically. No conversions were required, and the series included patients across a wide BMI range, suggesting no selection bias toward technically easier cases.

One technical challenge was maintaining the correct pouch orientation during TTSS anastomosis when only a single assistant port was available. Folding the pouch posteriorly with the mesentery positioned anteriorly improved reach and prevented pouch rotation during the creation of the transanal anastomosis.

Overall morbidity and readmission rate were 28.5% and 21.5% respectively, in line with what already reported in previous studies on minimally invasive IPAA [12]. Importantly, no anastomotic leaks were observed. This may reflect the advantages of the TTSS technique, which for allows rectal transection under direct vision, prevents cuff length disparity, eliminates intersecting staple lines, ensures complete cuff incorporation into the circular anastomosis, and minimizes the risk of injury to adjacent pelvis organs [5]. In a recent cohort study of 130 pouch patients, TTSS was associated with a shorter rectal cuff and reduced postoperative urgency compared with the double-stapled approach [13].

These early results indicate that SP robotic IPAA is technically feasible and safe in unselected patients with UC. While limited by the small sample size, the present study provides a strong rationale for larger prospective studies to define long-term, functional, and quality-of-life outcomes, as well as to establish the role of SP robotics in contemporary pouch surgery.
