Zinc oxide nanoparticle biofortification of lentil seedlings enhances plant growth and zinc bioavailability in rats
Authors: Mona Sorahinobar, Fatemeh Saadati, Sepideh Khaksar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate the potential of zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) in the biofortification of lentil seedlings and subsequently improve the Zn status in rats. In the first phase of the study, the effects of various ZnO NPs concentrations (0, 10, 20, 40, 80, and 160 ppm) on the lentil growth, Zn accumulation, and other physiological parameters were investigated. Subsequently, the rats were fed ZnO NP-biofortified lentil seedlings (20 and 160 ppm) to assess their impact on animal health and Zn status. The results highlighted a concentration-dependent response of lentil seedlings to ZnO NPs, with optimal growth observed at 20 ppm, whereas higher concentrations inhibited lentil growth. Pigment and biochemical analyses revealed a complex interplay between chlorophyll, carotenoids, soluble sugars, and proteins with distinct responses to nanoparticle concentrations. Elevated levels of hydrogen peroxide and malondialdehyde of lentil seedlings at high concentrations of ZnO NPs suggest oxidative stress, countered by the upregulation of antioxidant enzymes and increased phenolic compounds. On the other hand. animal studies have showed that ZnO NP-biofortified lentil seedlings enhance serum zinc and magnesium levels in rats without affecting body weight. While serum triglyceride levels of rats decreased in both treatment groups, an elevation in creatinine and a marked increase in aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels were observed at a higher ZnO NP concentration (160 ppm), indicative of potential kidney and liver stress. Paradoxically, serum iron levels were lower in all groups consuming lentil seedlings than in the control group, suggesting a potential interaction between lentil components and iron metabolism. These findings suggest that ZnO NP-biofortified lentils may be a promising approach to enhance Zn nutrition; however, further investigation is needed to optimize ZnO NPs concentration and assess long-term safety.Keywords: Blood biochemistry, Biomass, Lentil, Nanoparticles, Oxidative stress, Rat, Zinc deficiencySubject terms: Physiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Zinc (Zn), a crucial trace element, plays an indispensable role as a structural, catalytic, and regulatory ion in biological organisms. Delayed wound healing, growth retardation, impaired taste, loss of appetite, hair loss, fertility issues, and increased susceptibility to infection are common manifestations of Zn deficiency in humans1. Zn deficiency is one of the most prevalent deficiencies in essential micronutrients, particularly in underdeveloped countries2. The prevalence of inadequate Zn intake poses a significant concern, with estimates ranging from approximately 17.3–25% of the global population, underscoring the urgent need for targeted interventions and further investigation of this widespread nutritional challenge3,4. In Iran, this problem is more severe in pregnant women (28.0%) and 15–23 months (19.1%)5. In recent years, the spotlight on Zn fortification has intensified, driven by the rapid spread of infectious diseases such as Covid-19, underscoring the essential role of Zn in the immune system. Insufficient intake of Zn from food is the main cause of Zn deficiency, which is related to the low and insufficient amounts of Zn in agricultural soils6. Additionally, the prevalence of Zn deficiency in populations subsisting mainly on cereals may be related to the high phytate content of cereals, which decreases the bioavailability of Zn. Therefore, increasing the Zn content in food and providing solutions to increase its bioavailability, especially in poor and developing countries, are urgently needed.

Biofortification is defined as the process of improving the nutritional value of edible crops through selective conventional breeding, mineral fertilization, or advanced transgenic approaches. In recent years, the efficiency of nutrient-containing nanoparticles in the biofortification of crops has been confirmed7,8. Sprouts due to their relatively low price, widespread use, high production speed, possibility of production in all seasons of the year, and the higher bioavailability of micronutrients compared to the seeds are a valuable biofortification target to confront malnutrition and hidden hunger. Lentils, mung beans, soybeans, and alfalfa are the most commonly used species in sprout production9,10. In our previous study, we confirmed the capability of zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) in to remarkably increase the Zn content of mung bean seedlings8. Despite the promising results of ZnO NPs in biofortifying crops, there is a lack of studies examining the impact of Zn-biofortified sprouts on physiological aspects and their potential for enhancing Zn bioavailability in human, since increasing the Zn content in plants does not necessarily translate to higher bioavailability for human nutrition.

In the current study, due to its wider use in the diet of Iranians and higher cultivation area compared to mung bean, we used lentil seedlings as the target plant. Additionally, researchers showed that lentil sprouts contain high quality proteins, fat, polyphenols and are a rich source of nutrients including Zn and copper11,12. Therefore this study aimed to evaluate the potential of ZnO NPs to enhance the nutritional value of lentil seedlings through zinc biofortification and subsequently improve the zinc status in rats.

Results

The effect of ZnO NPs on lentil seedlings

The presence of ZnO NPs in the culture media significantly enhanced Zn uptake in lentil seedlings, particularly in the roots, indicating a concentration-dependent response. The root Zn content showed a remarkable increase across all ZnO NPs treatments, ranging from 10 to 160 ppm, exhibiting fold increases of 1.72, 2.13, 3.57, 7.69, and 13.53 compared to the control condition. Conversely, the Zn content in shoot samples displayed no significant difference at lower concentrations (10 and 20 ppm), but exhibited a notable elevation at higher concentrations (40 to 160 ppm) of ZnO NPs (Table 1).

In addition to altering the Zn content, ZnO NPs treatment induced discernible changes in the growth biomarkers of L. culinaris seedlings. The fresh and dry weights of seedlings experienced an initial increase up to 20 ppm, with the highest increments of 16.57% and 35% observed at this concentration, respectively, followed by a gradual decline at higher ZnO NPs concentrations. Notably, at 160 ppm ZnO NPs, there was a substantial decrease of 23.67% and 45.78% in the fresh and dry weights, respectively. Similarly, shoot and root lengths exhibited significant enhancement following ZnO NPs treatment up to 20 ppm but displayed a diminishing trend at higher concentrations, indicating a concentration-dependent effect.

The levels of chlorophyll a (Chl a) and its biosynthetic intermediates, including protoporphyrin IX (PPIX), Mg-protoporphyrin IX (Mg-PPIX), and protochlorophyllide (Pchlide), exhibited a positive response to ZnO NP treatment up to 20 ppm, with notable increases (Fig. 1A, B). However, as the ZnO concentration increased beyond this threshold, there was a marked reduction in pigment content. Conversely, ZnO NPs treatment at all concentrations led to a decrease in Chl b content compared with the control samples. Interestingly, the application of ZnO NPs resulted in an increase in carotenoid content across all treatments compared with the control, with no significant differences among the various levels of ZnO NPs treatments.

Discussion

Our results showed that biofortification of lentil seedlings with ZnO NPs significantly increased the Zn concentration in the entire seedling, surpassing a 10-fold increase compared to the control condition. This finding is consistent with our recent report on the potential of ZnO NPs for Zn biofortification in mung bean seedlings8. For most crops, the recommended Zn concentration for proper growth is typically reported to be between 15 and 20 ppm of dry weight (DW) approximately13. According to Marschner14, most plant species naturally contain Zn concentrations between 30 and 100 ppm, while concentrations above 300 ppm DW are considered toxic. Our research findings align with these above-mentioned levels, highlighting the differing tolerance levels of plant organs towards toxicity. Specifically, we observed apparent signs of toxicity, such as reduced root and shoot biomass compared to the control, at Zn internal concentrations exceeding 300 ppm in the roots and 27.20 ppm in the shoot.

On the other hand, our data confirms the significant limitation of root-to-shoot Zn translocation which show the lentils root is more appropriate target for Zn biofortification. The ratio of root Zn concentration to shoot increased from 8.07 in the samples treated with 10 ppm to 24.52 in samples treated with 160 ppm ZnO NPs. The authors argue that this phenomenon is part of the plant’s strategy to sequester Zn into root vacuoles, preventing its accumulation in the highly metabolically active regions15–17. Despite the impressive ability of plants to sequester Zn in the roots, resulting in an imbalanced increase in Zn content in the shoots, the toxic effect of Zn on plant biomass persisted.

Similar to our previous findings in mung bean8; the current results also demonstrate a dose-dependent biphasic trend in the growth of lentil seedlings exposed to ZnO NPs. Specifically, at concentrations of 10 and 20 ppm, ZnO NPs had a beneficial impact on biomass (Table 1). Therefore, the positive effects of Zn on biomass, chlorophyll content, carbohydrates and proteins are probably due to its proven role in various biosynthetic processes from genetic materials to chlorophyll, carbohydrates and auxins and its role in enzyme activity18. Consequently, increased Zn content can impart advantageous effects on the plant, leading to improved growth. The positive effect of Zn application on chlorophyll content and photosynthesis has been reported in other studies conducted in other plants such as tomato19, mung bean8, Flax20 and Cilantro21. Additionally, the vital role of Zn in the formation of Pchlide and its contribution in chloroplast development was determined22.

On the other hand, a toxic effect was observed when the concentration of ZnO NPs exceeded 20 ppm. This was evident as the dry weight of lentil seedlings treated with ZnO NPs decreased, reaching its lowest level of 45.78% compared to the control at 160 ppm (Table 1). Additionally, the higher concentrations of ZnO NPs (40–160 ppm) likewise had a negative effect on the content of chlorophyll a and b. Researchers have demonstrated that excessive Zn in plant cells can cause toxicity by triggering various mechanisms. These include the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) which disrupts cell membrane integration and permeability, leading to the activation of oxidative stress signaling pathways23,24. Metal NPs, such as Zn NPs, have been found to deposit on the cell surface or within subcellular organelles, resulting in cellular oxidative stress25. According to reports, Zn toxicity disrupts the absorption and translocation of essential elements such as Fe and Mg into the chloroplast, affecting the functionality and efficiency of the photosynthetic system in various plant species. This results in the repression of PSII activity26.

MDA and H2O2 content can be considered as bioindicators for the oxidative stress. In current study, the ZnO NPs dependent increase of H2O2 and MDA content in the seedling confirmed oxidative damage (Fig. 3). Plants prevent the accumulation of ROS and subsequent lipid peroxidation by using enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems.

The detoxification of ROS in plants involves several enzymatic reactions. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) plays a crucial role by converting superoxide anions into hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), while peroxidase (POX) further reduces hydrogen peroxide to water (H2O). PPO contributes to ROS detoxification by consuming available oxygen while catalyzing the oxidation of monophenols and/or o-diphenols, which results in the formation of o-quinones. As a byproduct of this reaction, PPO generates superoxide anions, which can be dismutated enzymatically by SOD. This dismutation eventually generates H2O2 as a secondary product, which serves as a substrate for the subsequent enzymatic activity of POX. Collectively, the sequential actions of PPO, SOD, and POX ensure the efficient detoxification of ROS in plants.

SOD plays an important role in counteract damaging effects of ROS by converting superoxide anion to a less toxic form of hydrogen peroxide. Zn is present in the cystolic and chloroplastic Cu/Zn-SOD enzymes, which play a critical role against the oxidative stress. Our findings are consistent with the results of other researchers27–30, who studied the effect of ZnO NPs on antioxidative enzymes in Moringa peregrine, Zea mays, Vigna mungo and Lectuca sativa, respectively.

The results related to the increase in the activity of the antioxidative enzymes such as SOD, POX and PPO in plants treated with ZnO NPs are in agreement with the findings obtained in our previous study, which showed that the treatment with ZnO NPs led to the increase in the activity of PPO and POX enzymes in mungbean plants8.

The differential response of PPO (Fig. 4D), with the highest activity at 40 ppm ZnO NPs, compared to POX (Fig. 4B) and SOD (Fig. 4C), which showed maximum activity at 160 ppm ZnO NPs, in the shoots of treated seedlings may be attributed to their distinct roles in plant defense mechanisms. SOD and POX primarily mitigate oxidative stress by scavenging ROS, whereas PPO is involved in the polymerization of phenolic compounds, which contributes to the strengthening of the cell wall and other defensive structures. The coordinated activity of POX and PPO in the roots of the treated plants may be related to their role in limiting the entry of excessive NPs into the plant by reinforcing the cell wall, a hypothesis that warrants further investigation. The content of H2O2, a product of SOD activity and a substrate for POX, correlates with the activity levels of these enzymes, with high H2O2 levels at 160 ppm signifying intense oxidative stress and triggering maximum SOD and POX activities to mitigate damage. An increase in phenolic, flavonoid and flavonol compounds was observed in the present study under the treatment of ZnO nanoparticles. The role of Zn in carbon allocating to the production of phenolic compounds in the shikimic acid and acetate cycle can be one of the reasons for the increase of these compounds31. The effect of NPs on phenolic compounds as ROS scavengers is related to their important role in the yield and plant adaptation to biotic and abiotic stresses32. The results of this study are consistent with the results of31,33,34 in the potato, Capsicum annuum and rapeseed plant, respectively.

Phenolic and flavonoid compounds are secondary metabolites that play a protective and antioxidant role in plants. Several studies showed that in plants treated with high concentrations of heavy metals, these compounds are active and protect the plants. Flavonols contained in the vacuoles can be used in the flavonol-peroxide cleansing system to scavenge reactive oxygen species, especially H2O2.

Similar to our findings, other studies have reported positive effects of low concentrations of ZnO NPs on protein content and plant growth in4 Solanum lycopersicum35,36 and Vignea radiata8. Intracellular proteins can be oxidized when exposed to free radicals and subsequently degraded by proteolytic enzymes. In the present study, the protein content in plants treated with lower concentrations (10 and 20 ppm) of ZnO NPs increased compared to the control. However, a decrease in protein content was observed in plants treated with higher (80 and 160 ppm) concentrations of ZnO NPs. This suggests that while Zn is essential for maintaining the balance of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and protein stability37, excess Zn can disrupt this balance, leading to reduced protein content, as seen in the present study.

Considering that the recommended daily intake of Zn in the diet is between 8 and 12 mg, with the consumption of about 10 g of 7 days old lentils seedlings, germinated and grown on culture containing 160 ppm of ZnO NPs, this need can be met. Thus, according the tremendous effect of ZnO NPs treatment on the Zn content of seedlings (about 14 times more than that of control), this method could lead to an augmentation that could meet the daily needs of adults.

The administration of ZnO NPs-biofortified lentil seedlings to rats demonstrated an increase in Zn content in blood serum, especially in the T2 group. This supports the bioavailability of Zn from ZnO NPs-treated plants, aligning with the findings of our previous report8. Furthermore, the observed increase in serum magnesium in both T1 and T2 groups suggests a potential positive influence of Zn-biofortified lentil seedlings on magnesium absorption or metabolism.

The decrease in serum iron levels in the control (+) group and a trend of lower iron levels in the T1 and T2 groups hint at a potential negative impact of lentil seedlings on iron absorption. Further investigations are needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Analysis of additional biochemical parameters, including triglycerides, cholesterol, glucose, and creatinine, revealed notable differences between experimental groups. In the T1 group, there was a significant increase in urea levels and a slight reduction in triglyceride levels. In the T2 group, there was a significant reduction in triglyceride levels and a significant increase in creatinine levels. The significant increase in creatinine levels in rats that received Zn-biofortified lentil seedlings raises questions about potential renal effects, warranting further exploration. These findings suggest that ZnO NP biofortification may have potential benefits for managing lipid profiles, but also highlight the need for further investigation into its impact on kidney function.Among the assayed enzymes (ALT, AST, and ALP), the T2 group showed a significant increase in AST activity compared to the control groups. The elevated AST activity in the T2 group indicates a potential impact on liver function. However, the lack of significant changes in ALT and ALP suggests a nuanced response that requires deeper investigation.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study provides valuable insights into the impact of ZnO NPs on lentil seedlings and their subsequent effects on an animal model. The concentration-dependent response in plant growth and biochemical parameters, and the bioavailability of Zn in animal models highlight the need for a nuanced approach in designing biofortification strategies. Further molecular and physiological studies are essential to unravel the intricate mechanisms underlying the observed responses, ensuring the sustainable and safe implementation of ZnO NPs for biofortification purposes.

Materials and methods

The study was divided into two interconnected phases. The first phase involved a plant-based experiment designed to evaluate the influence of varying ZnO NP concentrations on lentil sprouts growth, Zn bioacculmulation, and physiological response. This phase aimed to identify optimal and maximal ZnO NP application rates for subsequent animal studies. The second phase focused on assessing the effects of Zn-biofortified lentil seedlings, produced under the optimized conditions determined in the first phase, on the physiological parameters of experimental animals.

Plant material and culture conditions

The experiment was conducted using a completely randomized design with 10 replications. Each replication consisted of 10 lentil seeds placed in a Petri dish. Petri dishes were randomly assigned to six treatment groups (0, 10, 20, 40, 80, and 160 ppm ZnO NPs). To control for environmental factors, all Petri dishes were placed in the same growth chamber under identical conditions. Lentil (Lens culinaris L.) seeds were first sterilized in 10% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite solution for 10 min, followed by three washes with sterile distilled water. The sterilized seeds of uniform size were placed onto glass Petri dishes, with filter papers at the bottom; then, 10 ml deionized water (control) or different concentrations of ZnO NPs (Nanopowder, Purity: 99+%, Particle Size: 10–30 nm, Crystal Phase: Single crystal, Morphology: Nearly spherical, Specific Surface Area (SSA): 20–60 m²/g, True Density: 5.606 g/cm³) suspensions were added to the dishes. The ZnO NP suspensions were sterilized in an autoclave, and before application, placed for 30 min in an Elmasonic S80(H) Ultrasonic Cleaner with an ultrasonic frequency of 37 kHz and an effective ultrasonic power of 150 W (Elma Schmidbauer GmbH, Singen, Germany) to achieve the proper dispersion of particles. The Petri dishes, after sealing with tape, were kept in a growth chamber at 25 ± 1 °C under a 16 h light photoperiod, RH of 55%, with white fluorescent tubes (56 µmol m− 2 sc− 1). Seedlings obtained after seven days of growth were used for analysis. The mean fresh and dry weights (mg) and lengths (cm) of a minimum of 20 lentil seedlings that were 7 days old were recorded for biometrical analysis, frozen in liquid nitrogen, stored at − 70 °C, and used for biochemical assays. For the physiological and biochemical assays, 25 seedlings were pooled and used as one replicate in triplicate.

Biochemical assays of plant materials

Powdered freeze–dried shoot samples (0.5 g) were placed in a polyethylene tube containing 8 ml nitric acid (65%) for 12 h at room temperature. Then, 2 ml perchloric acid was added to the samples, which were kept at 80 °C for 1 h and 150 °C for 3 h. The solution was filtered through Whatman No. 42 filter paper, diluted to 25 ml with ultrapure water, and stored in the dark before analysis. An ICP–MS (HP–4500, USA) equipped with an Asx–520 auto-sampler was used to measure the Zn concentration.

Chlorophyll a and b contents and carotenoids were measured using a spectrophotometer according to the procedure described by Lichtenthaler and Wellburn (1983)38. Briefly, fresh samples (0.2 g) were homogenized in 80% acetone (Merck, Germany) and the absorbance was recorded spectrophotometrically at 646, 663, and 470 nm. The concentration of photosynthetic pigments was expressed as mg g − 1 fresh weight (FW)38.

The concentrations of protoporphyrin IX (PPIX), Mg-protoporphyrin IX (Mg-PPIX), and protochlorophyllide (Pchlide) were measured according to the method described by Yang et al. (1998)39. The absorbance of the supernatant was recorded at 575, 590, and 628 nm, and their contents were calculated using the following formula: V is the volume of solution and FW is the fresh weight of leaves40.

PPIX (µmol g FW-1) = (0.18016 × A575-0.04036 × A628-0.04515× A590) × V/FW.

Mg-PPIX (µmol g FW-1) = (0.06077 × A590-0.01937 × A575-0.003423× A628) × V/FW.

Pchlide (µmol g FW-1) = (0.03563 × A628 + 0.007225 × A590-0.02955× A575) × V/FW.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was quantified using the spectrophotometric method described by Heath and Packer (1968)41. Fresh seedling samples were extracted with 0.1% TCA, and after centrifugation at 8000 × g for 15 min, the supernatant solution (0.5 ml) was mixed with 1 ml of thiobarbituric acid (0.5%) in TCA (20%). After incubation of the solution at 95 °C for 25 min, the samples were cooled in an ice bath and centrifuged at 8,000 × g for 15 min, and the absorbance of the supernatant was recorded at wavelengths of 532 nm and 600 nm. The MDA content was calculated using an extinction coefficient of 155 mM− 1 cm− 1.

For H2O2 quantification, to 0.5 ml of the samples extract (0.1% TCA), 0.5 ml of potassium phosphate buffer (10 mM, pH 7.0) and 1 ml KI (1 M) was added. H2O2 content was measured at 390 nm using the method described by Velikova et al. (2000)42.

To determine the total protein content, fresh seedling samples (0.2 g) were ground using a mortar and pestle in 1 M Tris–HCl (pH 6.8) at 4 °C. The suspension was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min. The supernatant was separated, collected, and used for determination of protein content and enzyme assays. The protein content was quantified using bovine serum albumin as the standard43.

The superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was assayed as described by Giannopolitis and Ries (1977)44. The reaction solution contained 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer, 13 mM methionine, 75 µM riboflavin, 0.1 mM EDTA, 75 µM NBT, and 100 µl of enzyme extract. The reaction solution was placed in front of the light for 18 min and the absorbance was measured at 560 nm. SOD activity was expressed as units mg− 1 protein.

For estimation of seedling peroxidase (POX) activity, 50 µl of the Tris–HCl extract was added to the reaction mixture containing 0.1 ml benzidine, 0.2 ml H2O2 (3%), and 0.2 M acetate buffer (pH 4.8). POX activity was measured at 530 nm45.

The activity of polyphenol oxidase (PPO) was quantified following the method described by Raymond et al. (1993)46. The reaction mixture consisted of 0.2 ml of 20 mM pyrogallol, 2.5 ml of 200 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.8), and 50 µl of enzyme extract. The enzyme activity was spectrophotometrically measured at 430 nm.

The extraction of phenolic compounds was performed using 0.4 g dried powder of the samples in 80% (v/v) methanol for 48 h. The samples were then transferred to an ultrasonic bath for 20 min at 80 °C. After extraction, samples were centrifuged at 11,000× g for 10 min. The supernatant obtained was then used to determine the content of total phenolic compounds and total flavonoids. For the determination of phenolic compounds, 100 µl of the extract was mixed with 500 µl of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and 400 µl of sodium carbonate. The reaction mixture was then incubated for 30 min at room temperature. The absorbance was read at 630 nm47. The anthocyanin content was extracted using 1% HCl V/V acidified methanol. Fresh samples were homogenized in the extraction solution, centrifuged at 18 000× g at 4 ◦C for 15 min, and stored in the dark for 5 h at 5 °C. The amount of anthocyanins was quantified at 550 nm48. The protocol described by Akkol et al. (2008)49 was used for the measurement of flavonol content. In this procedure, 1.5 ml CH₃COONa (sodium acetate) 5% and 0.5 ml AlCl3 2% were added to 0.5 ml methanolic extract and absorbance was recorded at 445 nm after 2.5 h and Rutin was used to draw a standard curve. Total flavonoids content was determined by aluminum chloride method50. Methanolic extract (0.5 ml) was mixed with 1.5 ml of methanol, 0.1 ml of aluminium chloride (10%), 0.1 ml of potassium acetate (1 M) and 2.8 ml of distilled water and the absorbance was measured at 415 nm after 30 min.

Animals preparation and exprimental design

Twenty male Wistar rats weighing (6–8 weeks old; weighing 210–270 g) were purchased from Shahid Beheshti University and were kept under standard conditions, including constant temperature (22 ± 2 °C), humidity, and a 12 h light/dark cycle. The experimental procedures and animal handling were performed in accordance with the National Institute of Health and Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publications, revised in 2011). All experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of Alzahra University (IR.ALZAHRA.REC.1401.011). An attempt was made to use the minimum number of rats in an ethically responsible manner. It is confirmed that this study was reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Twenty male Wistar rats were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 5/group) using a random number generator. These groups including group I (negative control): rats received saline orally for consecutive five days (n = 5), group II (positive control): rats orally received untreated lentil seedlings at dose of 100 mg/kg for five days (n = 5). Seven-day-old lentil seedlings were dried in an oven at 60 °C for 48 h and then powdered using a mortar and pestle, group III (T1): rats orally received lentil seedlings treated with 20 ppm ZnO NP at dose of 100 mg/kg for five days (n = 5), and.

group IV (T2): rats orally received lentil seedlings treated with 160 ppm 20 ppm ZnO NP at dose of 100 mg/kg for five days (n = 5). Dose of 100 mg/kg was chosen based on the effective dose of lentil extract in an animal model of oxidative stress, as previously reported51. A ZnO NP concentration of 20 ppm was selected to represent an optimal treatment level that showed minimum phytotoxicity while enabling potential zinc biofortification. Conversely, a concentration of 160 ppm ZnO NPs was employed to establish a maximum treatment threshold, allowing for an assessment of its upper limit of efficacy on zinc accumulation and its concomitant impact on plant growth parameters.

24 h after receiving the last gavage, the blood serum of the rats was collected to evaluate biochemical factors. The weights of the animals were measured as physiological parameters before and after treatment (the fifth day).

Assessment of biochemical parameters in blood serum of animal

24 h after the last gavage in different experimental groups, the animals were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of a mixture of anesthetic ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (8 mg/kg). Their blood were collected and were placed inside the test tube for 30 min in the laboratory environment to coagulate. In the next step, the blood samples were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min and their serum were separated52and were kept at -20 ºC. The average serum concentration of triglyceride, cholesterol, glucose52, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP)53, creatinine, and urea were measured using enzymatic kits (Pars Azmoon Company, Iran, according to the instructions of the manufacturer) and a fully-automatic alpha-classic autoanalize54. The concentration of triglyceride, cholesterol, glucose, creatinine, and urea were reported in mg/dl. The concentration of liver enzymes were also reported in U/L. Colorimetric method was used to measure calcium using a spectrophotometric device using Ziestchem. Co. kit (Iran). Atomic absorption method was also used to measure magnesium. Measurement of iron and Zn in blood serum were also done using Ziestchem. Co kit and spectrophotometric method55. The concentration of calcium, magnesium, iron, and Zn were reported in mg/dl.

Statistical analysis

Data obtained from biochemical parameters were analyzed using one-way ANOVA (post hoc Duncan). The data was displayed as Mean ± SD and p-values ˃ 0.05 were not considered statistically significant. The graphs were designed with Graphpad Prism version 6 and Microsoft Excel version 2010.
