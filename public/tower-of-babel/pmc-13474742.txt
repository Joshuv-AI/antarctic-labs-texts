Prognostic predictors in HIV-negative neurosyphilis: a retrospective cohort study with prospective follow-up
Authors: Wei-Yang Cheng, Jian-Feng Qu, Xiao-Wen Liu, Ming-Zi Wang, Run-Xiong Li, Xue-Xin Chen, Yang-Kun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prognosis and its predictors in human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-negative neurosyphilis remain poorly characterized.ObjectiveTo identify clinical, laboratory, and neuroimaging predictors of poor long-term outcomes of HIV-Negative Neurosyphilis.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study with prospective follow-up in 128 newly diagnosed HIV-negative neurosyphilis patients at a single tertiary center. Baseline evaluations included clinical profiles, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, and brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The primary outcome was a composite poor global outcome (modified Rankin Scale[mRS] > 2, Activities of Daily Living [ADL] > 14, or Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly[IQCODE] > 3.19). Multivariable logistic regression identified independent predictors.ResultsAfter a median follow-up of 6.4 years, 40.6% of patients had a poor global outcome and all-cause mortality was 17.2%. Multivariable analysis identified parenchymal neurosyphilis (odds ratio [OR], 19.10; 95% confidence interval [CI], 5.68–64.23), elevated CSF protein (OR, 9.03; 95% CI, 2.31–35.32), and lacunar infarct score (OR, 2.48 per point; 95% CI, 1.44–4.27) as independent predictors of poor outcome. Hypertension predicted mortality (OR, 4.12; 95% CI, 1.50–11.34).ConclusionIn HIV-negative neurosyphilis, parenchymal involvement, elevated CSF protein, and lacunar infarct burden are robust predictors of poor global outcomes, while hypertension predicts mortality. These findings support integrated clinical, CSF, and neuroimaging assessments for prognostic stratification and underscore the need for aggressive vascular risk factor management.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13554-6.Keywords: Neurosyphilis, HIV-negative, Prognosis, Cerebrospinal fluid protein, Lacunar infarct, Magnetic resonance imaging

Introduction

Neurosyphilis, an infectious disease of the central nervous system (CNS) caused by Treponema pallidum, can occur at any stage of syphilis [1, 2]. It can be asymptomatic or symptomatic. Symptomatic neurosyphilis is categorized based on the affected anatomical structures and includes meningeal, meningovascular, and parenchymatous forms (such as general paresis and tabes dorsalis), as well as gummatous neurosyphilis [2, 3]. Mirroring the increased incidence of syphilis [4–6], neurosyphilis is also being reported with growing frequency worldwide. In the United States, the prevalence of neurosyphilis among individuals with early syphilis was 0.84% from 2009 to 2015 [7]; in Australia, the annual incidence was reported to be 2.47 per 100,000 people over the period 2007–2016 [8]; and a similar upward trend was observed in Guangdong Province of China, where the incidence rose from 0.21 to 0.31 per 100,000 people between 2009 and 2014 [9].

Currently, no gold standard exists for diagnosing neurosyphilis. Definitive diagnosis therefore relies on a combination of clinical history, neurological examination, serology, cerebrospinal fluid analysis, and in some instances, imaging assessment [10–12]. The complexity of this process poses considerable difficulty for clinicians, contributing to high rates of missed diagnosis and misdiagnosis [13–15]. As reported by He et al., the misdiagnosis rate was up to 40.68% in symptomatic neurosyphilis [16]. If not treated promptly, neurosyphilis can result in irreversible neurological damage, including paralysis, progressive dementia, or even death [2, 17, 18]. Such outcomes significantly diminish patients’ quality of life and impose a substantial economic burden [19, 20].

Current research on neurosyphilis primarily focuses on risk factors [16, 21, 22], predictive models [23–25], diagnostic biomarkers [26–29], as well as treatment efficacy [30–33]. In contrast, research on long-term follow-up and prognostic prediction remains limited. A pioneering 2018 multicenter retrospective study by Ozturk-Engin et al. (n = 141) was the first to specifically analyze factors influencing poor outcomes in neurosyphilis [34]. While their work identified diplopia as a predictor of unfavorable outcome and headache as a protective factor, its findings are constrained by a moderate sample size, mixed HIV-status cohort, a simplistic qualitative outcome assessment, and non-uniform neuroimaging, with only 73% of patients undergoing either computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Recently, Chen et al.‘s study of 142 HIV-negative patients with symptomatic neurosyphilis linked discharge outcomes to CSF protein and neurosyphilis type but not to symptoms like headache; however, its findings are limited by the retrospective, single-center design, inadequate control for baseline comorbidities and lack of post-discharge follow-up [35]. Another Chinese study (n = 90) published in 2025 found that an elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and higher CSF protein levels were associated with a decline in Activities of Daily Living (ADL) scores and showed no association with specific clinical symptoms [36]. Similarly, this research was a single-center study with a small sample size and lacked both neuroimaging data and longitudinal follow-up.

The scarcity of robust long-term prognostic studies is largely attributable to significant barriers in clinical follow-up, including social stigma [37], patient reluctance toward repeat lumbar punctures [12], and the lack of systematic registries for quantifying neurological outcomes [38]. According to a Chinese meta-analysis of 286 studies, the follow-up rate for neurosyphilis patients dropped sharply from 52% at 3–6 months to 17.3% at one year [39], underscoring the critical need for more prognostic follow-up studies. Furthermore, a Danish nationwide prospective cohort study revealed that, compared to their HIV-positive counterparts, HIV-negative neurosyphilis patients often experience a delayed diagnosis and are consequently at a higher risk of unfavorable outcomes [40]. Consistent with this, earlier research has identified a higher prevalence of late-stage neurosyphilis in HIV-negative individuals [41]. Consequently, the HIV-negative neurosyphilis population, distinct from HIV-positive patients, warrants greater clinical and research attention.

Therefore, this study was designed to identify prognostic predictors in HIV-negative neurosyphilis by retrospectively collecting comprehensive clinical and neuroimaging data, combined with prospective telephone follow-ups utilizing multidimensional scales. A secondary objective is to specifically analyze the prognosis of parenchymal neurosyphilis, given its link to irreversible neurological damage and poor outcomes [35, 42], and to identify factors to inform clinical practice.

Methods

Study design and patient population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study with a prospective follow-up. The study cohort comprised adults newly diagnosed with HIV-negative neurosyphilis between July 2011 and June 2025 at our institution. Patients were identified from the Departments of Neurology and Dermatology by cross-referencing hospital diagnostic coding (ICD-10 code for neurosyphilis) and laboratory records of serum treponemal tests and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) profiles consistent with neurosyphilis. All identified patients subsequently underwent a standardized telephone follow-up assessment in July 2025. The inclusion criteria for neurosyphilis, based on the diagnostic criteria outlined in either the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Sexually Transmitted Infections Treatment Guidelines [12] or the corresponding guidelines from the Chinese CDC [43], were as follows: (1) both Treponema pallidum particle agglutination assay (TPPA) and toluidine red unheated serum test (TRUST) were performed in serum and CSF samples, with a positive serum TPPA required; and (2) positive CSF-TPPA plus either a positive CSF-TRUST, CSF pleocytosis (>5 × 10⁶/L), or elevated CSF protein (>450 mg/L).The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) concomitant HIV infection; (2) prior history of neurosyphilis; (3) incomplete medical records; (4) coexisting CNS disorders or other confounding systemic diseases; (5) absence of MRI data; (6) loss to follow-up.

Definition of parenchymal neurosyphilis

According to contemporary guidelines and expert consensus [1, 2, 12, 44], parenchymal neurosyphilis was defined by meeting all of the following cumulative criteria: (1) fulfillment of the diagnostic criteria for neurosyphilis as previously detailed, encompassing confirmatory serum and cerebrospinal fluid findings; (2) the presence of clinical features indicative of direct parenchymal injury to the brain or spinal cord, manifesting as either the cognitive-dementia profile of general paresis (e.g., memory impairment, executive dysfunction, personality changes, or psychosis) or the sensory-ataxic syndrome of tabes dorsalis (e.g., lancinating pain, sensory ataxia, areflexia, or visceral crises), acknowledging that modern presentations may be attenuated or incomplete; (3) the exclusion of other neurological, psychiatric, or medical disorders that could better explain the clinical presentation upon comprehensive investigation. Notably, patients with a prior history meeting the criteria for parenchymal neurosyphilis were still classified under this category, even when their current admission was for a neurosyphilis-related cerebral infarction (a meningovascular form). Patients who did not meet these criteria were categorized into the non-parenchymal neurosyphilis group.

Data collection and clinical variables

Demographic and clinical variables included age (defined as baseline age at hospital admission for initial neurosyphilis diagnosis), sex, history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, symptomatic status and symptom duration, diagnostic subtype, and syphilis treatment regimen. Additional clinical and vascular risk factors, including smoking history, alcohol consumption, hyperhomocysteinemia, atrial fibrillation, coronary heart disease, tuberculosis history, and hepatitis B history, were also collected for all participants, and these factors were not entered into subsequent multivariable analyses due to the absence of statistically significant associations with the study’s clinical outcomes in preliminary univariate tests (all P > 0.05). Laboratory variables included serum TPPA and TRUST titers along with lumbar puncture findings, namely opening pressure, white blood cell count, protein, glucose, and chloride levels, plus CSF TPPA and TRUST titers. Symptom duration was defined as the time from symptom onset to hospital admission (i.e., before treatment), assessed via retrospective review of electronic medical records, and was set to 0 days for patients with asymptomatic neurosyphilis. Elevated intracranial pressure was defined as an opening CSF pressure > 180 mmH2O.

Neuroimaging acquisition and assessment

All participants underwent brain MRI examinations using 1.5-T (Brilliance, Philips, Amsterdam, Netherlands) or 3.0-T (Sonata, Siemens Medical, Erlangen, Germany) scanners, including T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and contrast-enhanced imaging. All images were independently assessed by two experienced neurologists (Dr. Qu and Dr. Li) blinded to clinical data. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. The following imaging variables were evaluated:

Acute cerebral infarction: Defined as a focal area of hyperintensity on DWI with a corresponding hypointensity on the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) map, indicating restricted diffusion consistent with acute ischemic injury, according to established radiological criteria [45, 46].

Inter-rater reliability tests were performed on 15 randomly selected patients by the two neurologists (Dr. Qu and Dr. Li). The inter-rater agreement of MRI measurements was good to excellent: acute cerebral infarction—inter-rater κ 0.85; encephalitis-like culprit lesions—inter-rater κ 0.86; meningeal thickening—inter-rater κ 0.82; intracranial mass (presence/absence)—inter-rater κ 1.00; enlarged perivascular spaces—inter-rater κ 0.81; lacunar infarct (presence/absence)—inter-rater κ 0.85, lacunar infarct score (ordinal)—intraclass coefficient 0.88; white matter lesions (presence/absence)—inter-rater κ 0.83, white matter lesions (PVH grade)—intraclass coefficient 0.86, white matter lesions (DWMH grade)—intraclass coefficient 0.85; MTLA grade (Scheltens scale)—intraclass coefficient 0.89; GCA (Pasquier scale)—intraclass coefficient 0.87; ventricular enlargement (Evans Index)—intraclass coefficient 0.91.

Outcome measures and follow-up

Follow-up procedures

This prospective follow-up was conducted via telephone in July 2025 by Dr. Cheng. The follow-up protocol comprehensively assessed: (1) symptomatic evolution categorized as complete remission/asymptomatic, partial improvement, no change, clinical deterioration, or death; (2) outcome measures using standardized scales, including the modified Rankin Scale (mRS, with scores > 2 indicating poor functional outcome) [60], a 14-item ADL Scale evaluating both basic and instrumental activities (covering transportation, ambulation, meal preparation, housekeeping, medication management, eating, dressing, grooming, laundry, bathing, shopping, toileting, telephone use, and financial management, scored 1–4 per item with total scores ranging 14–56 where higher scores indicate greater dependency and scores > 14 define impairment) [61], and the 16-item Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly (IQCODE) modified to compare current cognitive status against pre-morbid cognitive state before neurosyphilis onset rather than the standard 10-year reference, using the original 5-point scale (1="much improved” to 5="much worse”) with mean scores > 3.19 indicating cognitive impairment [62, 63]; and (3) precise documentation of the follow-up period.

Definition of outcome measures

The primary outcome was a composite poor global outcome defined as the presence of at least one of the following at follow-up: poor functional outcome (mRS > 2), impaired activities of daily living (ADL score > 14), or cognitive impairment (IQCODE score > 3.19). Secondary outcomes included all-cause death and poor functional outcome (mRS score > 2) separately.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation; non-normally distributed variables as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) TRUST titers were logarithmically transformed (log₂ [1/TRUST titer]) for analysis.

Univariate analyses were conducted using independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, and Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analyses were included as candidate predictors in subsequent multivariable binary logistic regression models.

In the multivariable logistic regression analyses, the primary and secondary outcome measures served as dependent variables. Candidate variables with P < 0.05 from univariate analyses were entered as independent variables, with adjustments for age and follow-up period. When multiple indicators representing the same clinical concept showed statistical significance, quantitative measures were prioritized for inclusion in the multivariate model over qualitative assessments. A backward stepwise selection procedure was employed. To avoid multicollinearity, we ensured a variance inflation factor (VIF) < 5 for all variables; additionally, if the correlation coefficient between any two candidate variables was ≥ 0.40, the variable with the smaller P-value was retained.

A separate logistic regression analysis was performed following the same analytical workflow to identify risk factors associated with parenchymal neurosyphilis, with parenchymal neurosyphilis as the dependent variable, variables showing significant intergroup differences as independent variables, and age and follow-up period as adjusting covariates.

The odds ratios (ORs) represent the likelihood of the outcome for a unit change in the predictor, with all other variables held constant. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.3.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

During the 14-year study period, a total of 214 patients diagnosed with neurosyphilis were consecutively identified. Among them, 86 patients were excluded due to concomitant HIV infection (n = 16), prior history of neurosyphilis (n = 13), incomplete medical records (n = 9), coexisting central nervous system disorders or other confounding systemic diseases (n = 3), absence of MRI data (n = 44), or loss to follow-up (n = 1). A final cohort of 128 HIV-negative patients with new-onset neurosyphilis was therefore included in the prognostic analysis and categorized into parenchymal and non-parenchymal neurosyphilis groups, as illustrated in the flowchart (Fig. 1). As shown in Supplementary Table 1, the 44 excluded patients without MRI were significantly younger (mean age 41.6 vs. 55.6 years, P < 0.001) and had a significantly lower proportion of poor global outcome (11.4% vs. 40.6%, P < 0.001) compared with the included MRI cohort, suggesting less severe disease.

The baseline characteristics of all enrolled patients are summarized in Table 1, including demographic, clinical, laboratory, neuroimaging, and follow-up variables. In this study cohort, 101 patients were male (78.9%), with a mean age of 55.62 ± 12.9 years (range, 18–88years). A cross-sectional follow-up was conducted for all 128 patients. At the time of follow-up, 33.6% of patients were in complete remission or asymptomatic, 29.7% exhibited partial improvement, 11.7% remained unchanged, 7.8% experienced clinical deterioration, and 17.2% had died. Regarding functional and cognitive outcomes, 28.1% of patients presented with an mRS score > 2, 17.9% showed an impaired ADL score, and 23.6% scored ≥ 3.19 on the IQCODE.

Secondary outcome: all-cause death

Univariate analysis revealed that deceased group exhibited a higher prevalence of hypertension compared to survivors. Specifically, on neuroimaging, deceased patients showed a higher frequency of acute cerebral infarction, lacunar infarcts, and a higher lacunar infarct score. A longer follow-up period was also observed in the deceased group (Table 2).

Secondary outcome: poor functional outcome (mRS)

Univariate analysis for poor functional outcome (mRS > 2) identified significant differences in several baseline and neuroimaging characteristics. The poor mRS group had a higher prevalence of hypertension, a longer symptom duration, and a higher proportion of parenchymal neurosyphilis, as well as elevated CSF protein levels. MRI findings more prevalent in this group included lacunar infarcts (and a higher lacunar infarct score), PVH, L-MTLA, and ventricular enlargement. A longer follow-up period was also significantly associated with poor functional outcome (Table 2).

Separate models were constructed for the poor global outcome, all-cause death, and poor functional outcome (mRS > 2), incorporating all variables that were significant in the univariate analyses. Primary outcome: poor global outcome

A stepwise logistic regression analysis with backward elimination was performed. The initial model included the following variables: parenchymal neurosyphilis, elevated CSF protein, the quantitative lacunar infarct score (in preference to the qualitative assessment of lacunar infarct), ventricular enlargement, and follow-up period, with age included as a covariate. The following variables were excluded from the final model due to multicollinearity: PVH, L-MTLA, and GCA (due to correlation with ventricular enlargement; r = 0.430, 0.504, and 0.632, respectively) and symptom duration (due to correlation with parenchymal neurosyphilis; r = 0.550).

The final model demonstrated that parenchymal neurosyphilis (OR = 19.10; 95% CI: 5.68–64.23), elevated CSF protein (OR = 9.03; 95% CI: 2.31–35.32), and lacunar infarct score (OR = 2.48 per point; 95% CI: 1.44–4.27) were independently associated with the poor global outcome (Table 3).

Secondary outcome: all-cause death

A backward stepwise logistic regression model was developed for all-cause death. Hypertension, acute cerebral infarction and follow-up period were entered into the model, with age included as covariates. Lacunar infarct and lacunar infarct score were not included as they were highly correlated with hypertension (r = 0.556 and r = 0.595, respectively). The final model identified that hypertension (OR = 4.12; 95% CI: 1.50-11.34) and follow-up period (OR = 1.00 per day; 95% CI: 1.00–1.01) were significant predictors for all-cause death (Table 3).

Secondary outcome: poor functional outcome (mRS)

A similar backward stepwise logistic regression approach was applied to identify predictors of poor functional outcome (mRS > 2). The model initially incorporated parenchymal neurosyphilis, elevated CSF protein, lacunar infarct score, ventricular enlargement, and follow-up period, with age included as a covariate. The following variables were excluded due to multicollinearity: hypertension and lacunar infarct (with lacunar infarct score; r = 0.595 and 0.952, respectively); symptom duration (with parenchymal neurosyphilis; r = 0.550), and PVH, and L-MTLA (with ventricular enlargement; r = 0.430 and 0.504, respectively).

The final model demonstrated that parenchymal neurosyphilis (OR = 8.84; 95% CI: 2.66–29.41), elevated CSF protein (OR = 8.31; 95% CI: 1.73–39.77), lacunar infarct score (OR = 1.93; 95% CI: 1.17–3.16) and ventricular enlargement (OR = 2.96; 95% CI: 1.09–8.04)were significant independent predictors of poor functional outcome(mRS). Although the follow-up period reached statistical significance (p < 0.05), its effect size was negligible (β ≈ 0, OR = 1.00 ) (Table 3).

Multivariable logistic regression using a backward stepwise method was performed to identify factors independently associated with parenchymal neurosyphilis. Discussion

In our cohort of HIV-negative neurosyphilis patients followed for a median of approximately 6.4 years, we observed a poor global outcome rate of 40.6% and an all-cause mortality of 17.2%. We identified parenchymal neurosyphilis, elevated CSF protein, and lacunar infarct score as independent predictors of the poor global outcome, while hypertension is a significant predictor of all-cause mortality. To our knowledge, this is the first prognostic study specifically focused on HIV-negative neurosyphilis that integrates comprehensive clinical profiles, detailed neuroimaging, and prospective follow-up utilizing multidimensional outcome scales.

Reported rates of poor outcomes in neurosyphilis vary widely (15.9%-88.1%) [34–36, 40], largely attributable to heterogeneous study populations and methodological discrepancies in outcome definitions, assessment tools, and follow-up timing. For instance, a nationwide Danish cohort study reported a relatively low poor outcome rate of 15.9% at six-month [40], likely due to its inclusion of patients with probable, not just definitive, neurosyphilis, thereby diluting the risk estimate. In contrast, a multicenter study by Ozturk-Engin et al., which included only confirmed neurosyphilis cases (both HIV-positive and negative) and applied the same outcome scale, reported a substantially higher rate of 46.1% [34]. This marked difference underscores the impact of study population characteristics on prognostic estimates. Critically, however, previous outcome assessments have often lacked standardized quantitative measures, particularly for the detailed evaluation of cognitive function, a domain frequently impaired in neurosyphilis. Our findings reveal a concerning prognosis for HIV-negative neurosyphilis, characterized by a dual burden of significant morbidity and substantial mortality. The poor global outcome rate of 40.6% indicates that neurological damage often persists despite standard antibiotic therapy, suggesting the involvement of irreversible neurodegenerative mechanisms. Concurrently, the 17.2% all-cause mortality observed in our contemporary cohort is substantially higher than that reported in prior community-based studies with two-year follow-up [64]. These findings underscore the necessity of identifying predictors for these poor outcomes, which remain inadequately defined. Such predictors are essential to stratify patient risk and guide personalized management.

Our study confirms that parenchymal neurosyphilis is an independent predictor of the poor global outcome, consistent with previous literature [35]. This study extends prior knowledge by employing a composite endpoint that incorporates quantified assessments of both daily function and cognition, thereby providing a more objective and comprehensive evaluation. We further demonstrated that parenchymal neurosyphilis was independently associated with poor functional outcome (mRS > 2), but not with all-cause mortality. This dissociation suggests that while standard antibiotic therapy may effectively prevent death in these patients, it fails to reverse the established parenchymal damage, leading to persistent neurological deficits. This persistence of deficits can be attributed to the irreversible structural damage characteristic of parenchymal neurosyphilis, which directly invades brain and spinal cord tissue, leading to neuronal damage and gliosis, progressive cortical atrophy with neuronal loss, and degeneration of the dorsal columns and spinal roots [42, 65, 66]. Therefore, our findings underscore the importance of early diagnosis and treatment to prevent irreversible parenchymal injury. For those already diagnosed, future research should look beyond antibiotics to therapies that target the molecular pathways underlying parenchymal damage, as proposed by Aya Fadel et al. [67]

In our cohort, elevated CSF protein was independently associated with both the poor global outcome and poor functional outcome (mRS > 2). Elevated CSF protein has been well-established in previous research as a biomarker for diagnosing neurosyphilis, assessing its clinical severity, and predicting symptomatic disease [3, 16, 68–70]. Furthermore, its association with poor prognosis has been documented in several prior studies. Tan Yan et al. found in a 41-patient cohort that higher CSF protein predicted lower MMSE scores and poorer daily living function at 3 years [71]. This was replicated by Shi Qiang et al. in 62 patients [72]. Similarly, Qiaoer Chen et al. reported that CSF protein was independently associated with poor discharge outcomes [35], while Xie Caidie et al. demonstrated in 90 patients that CSF protein negatively correlated with admission ADL and predicted poor ADL recovery at discharge [36]. In contrast, a multicenter study by Ozturk-Engin et al. found no correlation between CSF protein and poor outcomes [34]. This discrepancy is likely due to the inclusion of HIV-positive patients in their cohort, as co-infection with HIV significantly alters the levels of CSF protein in neurosyphilis patients [40, 73]. Our longitudinal study of 128 HIV-negative patients confirms the association between CSF protein and poor outcome using a composite endpoint assessed at follow-up. Mechanistically, CSF protein elevation signifies neuroinflammation and blood-brain barrier (BBB) impairment [17, 74], a pathophysiological state that our study confirms is more pronounced in HIV-negative patients with adverse prognoses. This insight positions CSF protein as a key clinical biomarker for identifying patients at high risk for poor outcomes. It further suggests that early anti-inflammatory strategies (e.g., corticosteroids), by stabilizing the BBB and consequently reducing CSF protein levels, could potentially preserve neurological function and improve clinical outcomes. Despite its strong association with poor functional outcomes, elevated CSF protein was not associated with all-cause mortality in our cohort. A possible explanation is that antibiotic therapy effectively eradicates Treponema pallidum and reduces life-threatening complications, but may be limited in reversing established BBB damage and neurological deficits. This could help explain why elevated CSF protein predicts functional decline but not mortality. Future studies with serial CSF sampling and dynamic BBB imaging are needed to clarify this relationship.

Notably, in the present study, the semi-quantitative lacunar infarct score emerged as a robust and independent predictor of both poor global outcome and poor functional outcome (mRS > 2). Previous research has primarily described the neuroimaging spectrum of neurosyphilis [11, 18, 75, 76] and assessed its clinical utility for diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up [16, 77–80]. However, the prognostic significance of these imaging features for clinical outcomes remains unclear. Furthermore, neuroimaging data in prognostic studies are frequently only descriptive [35, 40, 81], or even entirely lacking [36, 71, 72]. For instance, a small series by Kazuhiro Kodama et al. suggested medial temporal lobe atrophy(MTA) might predict poor prognosis in general paresis [82], but the analysis was limited to a qualitative description of outcomes and imaging. Similarly, the negative findings of the multicenter Ozturk-Engin study [34] have been attributed to its pooling of CT with MRI data and qualitative image assessment. Ben Chen et al. later quantified imaging in 35 GP patients, identifying severe MTA as a predictor of poor cognitive outcome [83], though limited by restricted parameters and an exclusive focus on general paresis, a significant constraint given that MRI alterations occur in asymptomatic and myelopathic neurosyphilis [11, 16, 78, 84]. Therefore, our comprehensive semi-quantitative MRI analysis established the lacunar infarct score as a novel predictor for poor global outcome (OR = 2.49; 95% CI: 1.45–4.27) and poor functional outcome (OR = 1.93; 95% CI: 1.17–3.16) in a broader HIV-negative neurosyphilis cohort.

Pathologically, syphilitic vasculitis can affect small arteries [85–87]. MR imaging provides a window into this pathology [11], as evidenced by follow-up studies from Xian-Jin Shang et al. documenting new lacunar infarcts in neurosyphilis patients without atherosclerotic risk factors, thus suggesting the involvement of syphilitic small vessel pathology [84]. Consequently, a high lacunar infarct burden may reflect the severity of underlying small vessel inflammation, thereby contributing to the poor clinical outcomes observed in these patients. In addition, each lacunar infarct, whether of atherosclerotic or other origin and whether silent or symptomatic, may disrupt essential neural circuits [88, 89], thereby diminishing cerebral reserve and ultimately driving the poor prognosis in our cohort by increasing vulnerability to syphilitic injury. This underscores the clinical imperative to assess and manage lacunar infarct burden. Future larger, prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings. Nevertheless, we present the largest semi-quantitative MRI study in neurosyphilis to date. Future research should use multi-parametric MRI to develop fully quantitative biomarkers for prognosis.

Separately, we demonstrated that ventricular enlargement independently predicted poor mRS (OR = 2.96; 95% CI: 1.09–8.04). Previous MRI studies have consistently revealed cerebral atrophy in HIV-negative neurosyphilis patients [16, 18, 82], with multi-parametric MRI further demonstrating reduced gray matter volume in paresis patients [90]. The potential mechanism is cortical neuronal loss and neuropil disruption resulting from inflammation and gliosis [91, 92]. Furthermore, studies have indicated that medial temporal lobe atrophy predicts poor outcomes in GPI [82, 83]. As a sign of cerebral atrophy [54], ventricular enlargement signifies substantial and likely irreversible brain volume loss, and our study directly links ventricular enlargement to poor function outcome on the mRS. Clinically, the observation of ventricular enlargement in patients with neurosyphilis should warrant more aggressive therapeutic strategies. Regarding all-cause mortality, ventricular enlargement was not a predictor; this may be because effective antibiotic therapy controls life-threatening infection, while pre-existing structural brain damage does not directly cause death. Further studies are needed to explore this dissociation. In our study, univariate analysis revealed that left medial temporal lobe atrophy (L-MTLA) was also associated with poor global outcome and poor mRS; however, it was excluded from the multivariate model due to its collinearity with ventricular enlargement. Similarly, global cortical atrophy (GCA) was associated with poor outcomes in univariate analysis but was excluded from the multivariable model due to strong collinearity with ventricular enlargement. Future prospective studies are needed to determine the independent prognostic value of L-MTLA and GCA. Of note, although median MTLA scores did not differ between hemispheres, L-MTLA showed a stronger association with poor outcomes, which may reflect left temporal lobe dominance for cognitive functions. This observation requires validation in larger studies.

In analyzing all-cause mortality, hypertension was identified as a significant independent predictor (OR = 4.13; 95% CI: 1.50–11.34). Previous prognostic studies in neurosyphilis have seldom focused on hypertension [34, 36, 40, 71, 72], and Chen et al. found no association with poor discharge outcomes [35], possibly due to a lack of long-term follow-up. In contrast, our study is the first to identify hypertension as a risk factor for all-cause mortality in HIV-negative neurosyphilis patients during a median follow-up of 6.4 years. Mechanistically, hypertension induces cerebrovascular injury through endothelial dysfunction, vascular remodeling, and blood-brain barrier disruption [93], which may heighten susceptibility to syphilitic vasculitis. Furthermore, chronic hypertension shifts cerebral autoregulation to higher pressures, compromising cerebrovascular reserve and increasing the risk of cerebral hypoperfusion [93, 94], potentially exacerbating the cerebral hypoperfusion implicated in neurosyphilis pathogenesis [95]. We acknowledge that hypertension’s predictive effect on mortality may be partially mediated by underlying cerebral small vessel disease; nevertheless, it remained an independent predictor after adjusting for lacunar infarct burden, supporting the clinical importance of blood pressure control in this cohort. Our results call for increased clinical attention to hypertension management in individuals with HIV-negative neurosyphilis. Notably, hypertension was an independent predictor of all-cause mortality but not of functional outcomes in this study. This observation supports the notion that hypertension may contribute to poor survival independently of neurosyphilis, likely through its well-established role in accelerating atherosclerosis and inducing chronic cerebrovascular disease. These vascular pathologies increase the risk of fatal cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events, which may outweigh any direct interaction between hypertension and syphilitic brain injury. However, a synergistic effect between hypertension and syphilitic vasculitis cannot be entirely excluded. Regardless of the exact mechanisms, our findings strongly endorse rigorous blood pressure control in this population. Larger prospective studies with long-term follow-up and investigations into the pathological mechanisms are needed to confirm this observation.

Parenchymal neurosyphilis accounted for 50.8% of all cases and 57.0% of symptomatic neurosyphilis, consistent with the 56.3% reported by Chen et al. [35] Further analysis identified ventricular enlargement on initial MRI as an independent risk factor for parenchymal neurosyphilis (OR = 2.79, 95% CI: 1.26–6.19), whereas acute cerebral infarction was inversely associated (OR = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.04–0.56). This aligns with the characteristic pathology of parenchymal disease, where direct brain tissue damage leads to progressive cortical atrophy [42, 65], which may progress to ventricular enlargement. Therefore, ventricular enlargement on MRI in patients with neuropsychiatric symptoms should raise clinical suspicion for parenchymal neurosyphilis.

This study possesses several strengths, including the comprehensive collection of detailed clinical data, systematically quantified neuroimaging markers, and prospective multidimensional assessment of functional, cognitive, and daily living outcomes in a well-defined HIV-negative neurosyphilis cohort. These features provide a holistic view of prognosis in this understudied population. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this single-center study with a relatively small sample lacked age- and sex-matched controls, which may limit the generalizability and comparative strength of our findings. Second, variable follow-up duration and unavailable cause-of-death data may introduce heterogeneity in mortality assessment, even though follow-up period was adjusted for in multivariable models. Third, the absence of serial lumbar puncture and neuroimaging data limited our understanding of dynamic biomarker and structural changes over time. Fourth, the extreme imbalance in treatment regimens limited statistical power to evaluate the non-inferiority of oral regimens for neurosyphilis, precluding definitive conclusions on the efficacy of alternative treatments. Fifth, comprehensive socioeconomic data and several potential risk factors were not included in multivariable models, which may restrict the full scope of prognostic evaluation. Sixth, selection bias may have been introduced by excluding patients without confirmatory CSF (due to severe strokes) and those without MRI data; the latter were younger and had better outcomes, suggesting our MRI cohort overrepresents more severe disease. Future multicenter prospective studies with larger sample sizes and serial assessments are warranted to validate and extend our findings.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort study with prospective follow-up of HIV-negative neurosyphilis patients, we identified parenchymal neurosyphilis, elevated CSF protein, and lacunar infarct burden as independent predictors of poor global outcomes, encompassing functional, daily living, and cognitive deficits. Hypertension was a significant risk factor for all-cause mortality. We also suggest that semi-quantitative MRI markers provide independent prognostic value, supporting a stratified approach to neuroimaging (e.g., for symptomatic patients or those with elevated CSF protein). These findings highlight the importance of early diagnosis, aggressive management of vascular risk factors, and the potential utility of neuroimaging and CSF biomarkers in prognostic stratification for this understudied population. Future research should focus on validating these predictors in larger, multicenter cohorts and exploring targeted interventions to improve long-term outcomes.
