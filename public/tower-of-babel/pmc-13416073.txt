EcoImpact: energy conservation using data-driven model predictive control and interpretable machine learning in the buildings sector
Authors: Aseel Hussien, Aref Maksoud, Shouib Nouh Ma’bdeh, Aisha Al-Dahhan, Thar Baker, Ahmed Saleem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents EcoImpact, a novel interpretable predictive-control framework for building energy management that integrates data-driven forecasting, iterative control optimization, and explainable artificial intelligence in a unified workflow. Unlike conventional predictive-control approaches that focus mainly on prediction accuracy, EcoImpact explicitly combines machine-learning-based control with SHAP-based interpretability to identify the operational and environmental factors driving energy-related predictions. This enables not only improved control performance but also transparent decision support for building energy optimization. The proposed framework uses Random Forest and XGBoost models within a custom predictive-control structure to iteratively reduce prediction error and improve energy-management decisions. Experimental results demonstrate that both models achieve substantial error reduction across iterative runs, with final errors of 109.19 for Random Forest and 92.53 for XGBoost, indicating the effectiveness of the proposed framework. In addition, SHAP analysis reveals the most influential features affecting model predictions, providing insight into how the control model prioritizes input variables during decision-making. The main innovation of this study lies in combining predictive control and explainable machine learning into a transparent optimization framework for building energy management. The results show that EcoImpact can improve predictive performance while making model behavior interpretable, supporting more efficient, reliable, and explainable building energy-management strategies.Keywords: Data-Driven Model Predictive Control, Interpretable Machine Learning, SHapley Additive Explanations, Sum of Squared Errors, Machine Learning, Energy ForecastSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The building sector is a major contributor to climate change, accounting for about 30% of global final energy demand1. Energy conservation in this sector is critical, as buildings account for 40% and 39% of energy consumption in Europe and the USA, respectively2. The construction industry is responsible for about one-third of global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, underscoring its critical role in the fight against global warming3. Advanced control strategies, such as Model Predictive Control (MPC), offer promising solutions for optimizing building energy consumption and reducing operational CO₂ emissions4.

Data-Driven Model Predictive Control (DDMPC) is one of the most effective methods for optimizing HVAC system performance in buildings5. DDMPC achieves high efficiency by minimizing energy use while maintaining thermal comfort, accounting for technological constraints, weather forecasts, and dynamic building behavior. In this approach, control inputs are computed by solving an optimization problem at each time step to balance energy consumption and thermal comfort within defined constraints.

Previous research has shown the effectiveness of Model Predictive Control (MPC) and DDMPC in reducing energy consumption in buildings6–8. However, the literature lacks detailed exploration of model interpretability techniques, which are critical for understanding the impact of individual features on predictions. Studies have also shown a tendency to lack detailed exploration of the feature engineering process, as demonstrated by the effort-intensive feature selection and tuning in8. This gap can be addressed by using IML (interpretable machine learning) techniques, which can simplify and automate feature importance evaluation. Furthermore, the limited integration of DDMPC and IML in existing studies may have limited the transferability of their models, resulting in a narrow application scope confined to buildings. Incorporating these techniques in the proposed model enhances its adaptability and potential for broader applications in the energy optimization domain.

This work introduces the EcoImpact framework, which integrates Data-Driven Model Predictive Control (DDMPC) with the explainable artificial intelligence method Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) to support accurate and interpretable building energy management. Interpretable machine learning (IML) encompasses techniques designed to make model outputs more understandable to human users. As ML models become more complex, their decision-making processes often resemble black boxes, leading to challenges in user trust and adoption. IML seeks to address this issue by providing insights into how models arrive at their conclusions, thereby enhancing transparency and accountability. This is particularly relevant in energy management applications, where stakeholders must understand the key factors driving the model’s decisions.

By improving trust and enabling more informed decision-making, IML can significantly support the adoption and impact of ML-based solutions across various fields9 b). In the proposed EcoImpact framework, the roles of DDMPC and SHAP are distinct. DDMPC is responsible for the predictive control process, including forecasting model outputs, reducing prediction errors, and supporting optimized energy management decisions. SHAP, in contrast, does not directly improve prediction accuracy or optimize control actions. Instead, SHAP is used as an interpretability layer to explain the predictions of machine learning models and identify the features that most influence them. Therefore, improvements in prediction accuracy are attributed to the predictive models and the DDMPC process, whereas SHAP contributes to transparency, trust, and diagnostic understanding of model behavior.

The simulation experiments in this study focus on model explanation because interpretability is a central requirement for applying DDMPC in building energy management. Since DDMPC relies on machine learning models to predict control-related outputs, understanding how these models generate predictions is necessary to assess whether the learned relationships are consistent with building-energy behavior. Moreover, reducing prediction error can support energy savings because more accurate predictions enable the controller to estimate better future energy demand, indoor conditions, and system response. This helps reduce unnecessary heating, cooling, or ventilation operations while maintaining comfort constraints. Thus, EcoImpact evaluates both the predictive performance of the DDMPC component and the model’s explainability via SHAP. Recent studies have further advanced receding-horizon and predictive control methods for complex dynamic systems. For example, Hu et al.10, proposed a two-dimensional iterative learning control strategy under infinite-horizon optimization for batch processes with partial actuator failures, demonstrating the value of optimization-based control under faulty operating conditions. Similarly, Shi et al.11, developed a robust predictive tracking fault-tolerant control method for multiphase switched systems with asynchronous switching, based on a Lyapunov–Razumikhin approach. These studies highlight the growing importance of robust, predictive, and optimization-based control strategies in dynamic systems. However, while such methods improve control performance and robustness, their interpretability and transparency remain less explored, particularly in building energy management applications. Applications of MPC and DDMPC in building energy control

MPC has been applied in energy management systems, particularly in optimizing building energy consumption. Advanced control strategies, such as MPC, offer a promising approach to optimizing building energy consumption and reducing operational CO₂ emissions18. However, despite its effectiveness, a significant barrier to widespread MPC implementation is the effort required to create sufficiently accurate system models. This challenge is evident in the building sector because of the heterogeneous nature of the building stock. To address this issue, DDMPC has attracted increasing research attention, as it offers an alternative by using machine learning models rather than traditional physics-based models19. Several studies have integrated machine learning models into various control strategies to improve efficiency and decision-making. For example8, developed a workflow that integrates ANN and linear regression into MPC, enabling efficient energy control. This approach was tested in real-world environments, showing that linear models are suitable for practical applications due to their simplicity and reliability.

Additionally7, used historical building energy data to compare the performance of simple and advanced machine learning models for MPC. It found that a simple linear multi-layer perception model achieves comparable forecast accuracy to state-of-the-art models while being more data efficient. Similarly12, introduced data-driven predictive control (DPC), which uses machine learning models such as regression trees and random forests to develop predictive models for buildings. The study applied DPC to three case studies and found that it achieved comparable performance to traditional MPC, with energy savings of up to 49.2% in real-world applications.

Interpretability challenges in data-driven control

Data-driven models remain largely black boxes, despite their widespread use in research, which makes it difficult for building operators and model developers to understand their decision-making processes20. This lack of interpretability hinders large-scale adoption in practice. Unlike physics-based models, which incorporate domain knowledge, traditional machine learning models often neglect physical principles, limiting their transparency and trustworthiness21. As a result, recent work has focused on enhancing the interpretability of data-driven model implementations. This is particularly important in complex systems like buildings, where transparency and understanding of model behavior are critical for decision-making and regulatory compliance22.

Moreover, decision-makers such as engineers, facility managers, and policymakers increasingly demand not only accurate predictions but also clear explanations for how those predictions are derived. In the absence of interpretability, stakeholders may hesitate to rely on model outputs for operational or strategic decisions. Therefore, achieving transparency in data-driven control systems is essential to foster trust, ensure compliance with building performance standards, and enable broader deployment in real-world applications.

Toward interpretable machine learning in energy control

To overcome the transparency limitations of data-driven models, several studies have focused on enhancing the interpretability of advanced control strategy implementations by leveraging inherently transparent models and integrating domain knowledge. For instance13, introduced Reinforced Predictive Control (RL-MPC), a hybrid approach combining MPC and Reinforcement Learning (RL). This method enhances interpretability by maintaining constraints and optimizing the decisions in the control process while enabling continuous learning. This in turn allows stakeholders to better understand and validate the control actions. Similarly14, developed a DDMPC algorithm using multi-output regression trees. Regression trees are inherently interpretable; this makes them a natural choice for maintaining clarity in decision-making processes. Physically Consistent Deep Learning (PCDL), as proposed by15, integrates physical relationships into the model. This approach enhances interpretability compared to traditional machine learning models like LSTM. Ruddick16, validated TreeC, a metaheuristic algorithm that generates interpretable decision tree control policies, in real-world experiments on residential energy systems. TreeC showed energy management costs comparable to those of other methods and safe grid operations, making it a promising approach for integrating interpretability into MPC.

While the previously mentioned studies enhance the interpretability of data-driven models, an emerging body of work goes further by directly employing IML methods, such as SHAP, within the models’ framework (9 a). Among the most effective IML or XAI techniques, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) has gained prominence for providing readable, consistent explanations of machine learning model predictions23. Compared to other techniques such as LIME or partial dependence plots (PDPs), SHAP offers strong theoretical guarantees grounded in cooperative game theory and delivers both local and global interpretability, making it particularly well-suited for analyzing feature interactions in complex energy models. SHAP is increasingly being used in MPC and DDMPC applications to enhance model transparency and is among the most popular interpretability techniques in recent literature21. For instance6, focuses on improving the interpretability of artificial neural networks (ANNs) used in MPC for building energy systems. By integrating SHAP, the authors address the black-box nature of machine learning models, making it easier to understand how predictions are made. Another approach that leverages SHAP is presented in17, in which the authors explore the lack of trust in Machine Learning Control (MLC) for HVAC systems. They propose an IML framework that combines Shapley values and in-context learning from large language models (LLMs) to enhance transparency. Shapley values show the contributions of the various features, while LLMs explain rule-based parts, packaging the insights into a human-understandable narrative. The framework is demonstrated in a case study involving MPC to improve interpretability and trust in MLC decisions.

Gaps in existing literature and research contribution

While significant strides have been made in creating IML models with MPC for energy management, several gaps remain unaddressed. Many studies, such as Langtry et al.7,, Smarra et al.12,, and Stoffel et al.8,, largely focus on improving predictive accuracy and energy savings, with minimal attention to enhancing the interpretability of these models. For instance, Stoffel et al.8, did not explore methods to explain model behavior or feature contributions, thereby limiting transparency. Similarly12, initially uses regression trees but later switches to Random Forests, trading interpretability for better performance, scalability, and robustness. The study by Langtry et al.7, emphasized data efficiency and forecast accuracy but did not incorporate techniques to interpret predictions or understand feature influence. Although Arroyo et al.13, and Jain et al.14, make valuable contributions to enhancing the interpretability of MPC, both studies rely solely on inherently interpretable models, such as reinforcement learning frameworks and regression trees, respectively. This approach, while ensuring a baseline level of transparency, does not take full advantage of advanced IML techniques that can provide deeper insights into model behavior. Techniques such as SHAP or LIME enable quantifying feature importance and analyzing the influence of individual parameters on predictions, which is particularly useful for understanding and improving model performance. Zhang and Chen17, combine Shapley values with in-context learning from LLMs to improve transparency in MLC. While this approach enhances understanding, it heavily relies on domain expertise to design prompt templates, which can introduce biases and may not scale well in complex scenarios. The proposed approach overcomes this limitation by leveraging a more automated and scalable approach to interpretability, ensuring that the findings are applicable to a broader range of building configurations.

This study addresses these gaps by introducing a novel framework that integrates DDMPC with IML to enhance energy consumption forecasting during the early building design phase. Unlike previous works that emphasize predictive performance while overlooking interpretability and transparency, this approach (the EcoImpact framework) combines random forests and XGBoost models with SHAP values to deliver both high predictive accuracy and useful insights into feature importance. By enabling stakeholders to understand the influence of key building parameters on energy predictions, this work contributes to the advancement of interpretable DDMPC frameworks and supports more informed decision-making in energy-efficient building design.

Methodology

In this work, the predictive accuracy of the random forest and XGBoost models is significantly increased by employing a custom DDMPC algorithm. The SHAP method is used in IML to enhance the model’s interpretability and identify the most influential features driving predictions. This combined approach of data-driven predictive control and interpretability constitutes our EcoImpact framework. To improve reproducibility, additional details on the dataset and hyperparameter settings are provided, including building-related design, environmental, and operational variables. These variables are used to train and evaluate the predictive models within the EcoImpact framework. The variables include envelope and thermal-performance indicators, such as thermal mass and U-value, as well as environmental and energy-related parameters used to represent building energy behavior. The dataset was preprocessed by checking missing values, ensuring consistent feature formatting, and splitting the data into training and testing subsets for model evaluation. The main hyperparameters of the machine learning models were also specified to ensure experimental transparency.

Random forest for predictive modeling

Random Forest is an ensemble learning algorithm that uses both feature randomness and bagging to construct multiple decision trees that form an uncorrelated forest. A random subset of features is generated for each split in a decision tree through feature bagging. This technique helps reduce correlation among decision trees by ensuring that powerful predictors of the target variable do not dominate every tree. Each decision tree in the algorithm is built from a different sample of data drawn from the original training set. In regression, random forests reduce variance by averaging predictions from multiple deep decision trees. In each training subset, one-third of the data is reserved as test data, referred to as the out-of-bag (OOB) sample for validation. This method results in a significant improvement in overall model performance; however, it comes at the cost of slightly increasing bias and reducing interpretability24. For the Random Forest model, the key hyperparameters include the number of trees, maximum tree depth, minimum samples per split, minimum samples per leaf, and random seed. These settings were selected to balance predictive accuracy, model generalization, and computational efficiency. Figure 1.

The random forest training algorithm uses the bagging technique applied to decision trees. For a given training set , the algorithm repeatedly selects random samples from the training set to build individual trees, and it does that for a total of T times. Once the model is done with the training, each decision tree in the forest has learned to make predictions based on the patterns it found in its own subset of the data. When there is a new data point (say, x), each tree in the forest makes its own prediction for x based on what it has learned. Reducing variance without significantly increasing bias is how the bagging process improves model performance. Averaging the predictions of multiple trees offers the benefit of minimizing sensitivity to noise in the training data, provided the trees are not strongly correlated, a virtue that is lacking in predictions from a single tree.

Random forests also provide a straightforward method for ranking variable importance in regression or classification tasks, which can help verify IML results. This feature importance evaluation, known as permutation importance, was originally introduced by25 and can serve as a baseline to verify results from more advanced interpretability methods such as SHAP. A link between energy conservation and the random forest feature selection process can be drawn, as it helps identify the most influential factors affecting building energy consumption26.

Additionally27, has shown that random forest is very reliable in predicting energy consumption levels in buildings, outperforming machine learning models like SVM and BP-ANN. Zekić-Sušac et al.28, further confirms that random forests achieve high accuracy in predicting energy costs for buildings, outperforming models such as ANNs and CARTs.

Extreme gradient boosting (XGBOOST)

XGBoost is an ensemble learning algorithm that operates within the gradient boosting framework. When the predictions of a single model are insufficient for reliable results, ensemble learning combines the strengths of multiple models to produce a more robust and accurate predictive model. XGBoost builds an ensemble of decision trees, where each tree, known as a weak learner or a base learner, is trained sequentially to correct the residual errors of the trees that preceded it. The XGBoost output is the sum of all individual trees, as illustrated in Fig. 2. This iterative approach refines predictions and minimizes a specified loss function via gradient descent.

XGBoost is known for its computational efficiency, making it suitable for large datasets. It also provides valuable insights into feature importance and can effectively handle missing values. These attributes, combined with its ability to create strong predictive models through iterative learning, make XGBoost a powerful and widely used tool in predictive modeling. Interpretable machine learning (SHAP)

The goal of SHAP is to explain the prediction of a single instance by determining the contribution of each feature to the prediction. SHAP calculates these contributions by assigning a value to each feature based on its impact on the model’s output. Figure 3 shows how SHAP values use the training set to establish feature distributions and a baseline prediction, enabling the explanation of each feature’s contribution to the prediction of each instance in the test set by comparing how its features deviate from the typical patterns learned during model training. SHAP also requires access to the model to generate predictions and compute feature contributions by evaluating how predictions change with different feature combinations. Without the model, SHAP cannot determine how features influence the output.

Framework of the SHAP model. The framework shows all the components SHAP needs access to produce the summary plot, those being the dataset and the machine learning model.

Since our example uses the entire test set, the summary plot shows SHAP values across all test instances, not just a single instance, providing a global view of feature importance and impact. Features can be individual values, such as those in tabular data, or groups of values. The dataset being used for the EcoImpact framework is tabular, stored in an Excel file, where each row represents an instance, and each column corresponds to a feature.

SHAP computes Shapley values, providing several key advantages. It ensures that the prediction is fairly distributed among feature values, since it is grounded in a robust theoretical foundation. This results in contrastive explanations, where insight into why a particular prediction was made is provided by contrasting it with an alternative scenario, allowing comparisons between the prediction and the average prediction. Additionally, SHAP includes an efficient implementation for tree-based models, addressing the computational challenges typically associated with Shapley values and enabling their generation for global model interpretations, such as summary plots, without significant computational challenges.

This is particularly relevant in the context of studies such as Ang et al.33,, which explored the explainability and interpretability of data-driven models in urban building energy modeling. In that study, an XGBoost model trained on building energy usage data produced accurate predictions even when input features were modified. However the authors highlighted that predictive accuracy alone is insufficient and the need to consider interpretability in such models, which can be achieved by using tools like SHAP.

This concerns has been echoed more broadly across the literature. For example, the review by34highlights the limited research on interpretability in building energy applications, which continues to hinder adoption and trust in ML solutions. Likewise21, confirms that IML, particularly SHAP and LIME, has become central to improving model transparency. Yet, despite rising interest the field is still maturing and lacking consistency in how these tools are applied. Further reinforcing this35, argue that IML methods are not just helpful, but vital to support the energy transition in buildings, as they enhance trust and ease integration with physics based modeling.

A notable benefit of SHAP is its ability to connect LIME and Shapley values, offering insights into both methods and contributing to a unified framework for IML. By contrast, using separate methods like LIME for local explanations and permutation feature importance or partial dependence plots for global interpretations lacks this unified foundation.

In order to quantify interpretability, both sparsity and fidelity will be used. Sparsity is a measurement that determines how many features significantly drive each prediction. When fewer features dominate the prediction, the model is easier to interpret and understand. Fidelity, on the other hand, measures how closely the SHAP explanations match the actual behavior of your model. After removing the important features, it measures how much the model’s output changes.

To measure sparsity, the number of features in each prediction with a significant SHAP value is counted. The average is then calculated across all predictions using Eq. 3, where TF is the total number of features in the dataset, and UF is the number of features with SHAP values exceeding a defined threshold. In our case the threshold is set to , or 1% of the maximum SHAP value for a given instance i. A high sparsity score that is closer to 1 indicates a fewer number of key features, supporting the model’s interpretability. The 1% threshold was selected as a relative instance-level criterion to account for variation in SHAP-value magnitudes across predictions. This value filters out near-zero SHAP contributions while retaining features with non-negligible influence on the prediction. To assess sensitivity to this threshold, additional sparsity calculations were considered using 0.1% and 5% of the maximum SHAP value. Reducing the threshold to 0.1% would increase the number of features counted as significant, thereby increasing UFand decreasing the sparsity score. In contrast, increasing the threshold to 5% would retain only stronger SHAP contributors, reducing UFand increasing the sparsity score. Therefore, while the absolute sparsity value changes with the selected threshold, the overall interpretation remains consistent: sparsity improves when fewer dominant features explain the prediction.

Fidelity is evaluated by comparing the predictions of the simplified model that does not use the important features to those of the original model. Data-driven model predictive control for process optimization

This section presents the outcomes of the proposed EcoImpact framework, which integrates DDMPC with advanced IML techniques. EcoImpact addresses both optimization and explainability aspects of building energy management through a dual approach: DDMPC minimizes prediction errors by iteratively adjusting control inputs, while the interpretability method SHAP analysis reveals which building parameters most significantly influence model predictions.

MPC is an advanced and sophisticated technique in process control, specifically designed to manage and optimize processes while adhering to predefined constraints. However, its reliance on an accurate model poses many limitations in real-world applications. DDMPC overcomes this limitation by leveraging historical operational data and machine learning techniques to learn system behavior and generate control actions. This approach is valuable for complex industrial processes where developing accurate physical models may be impractical36.

The fundamental innovation of DDMPC lies in its ability to build predictive models directly from operational data. DDMPC implements various machine learning algorithms, such as neural networks, random forests, or gradient boosting methods, to capture the underlying relationships between process variables. These data-driven models learn both the long-term stable behavior of the system (steady-state) and how it responds to changes over time (dynamic characteristics). This allows them to predict future behavior based on past patterns and current conditions, making them effective for control and optimization. Traditional MPC relies on knowledge-based modeling methods, which require extensive prior knowledge and struggle with complex, large-scale systems. Data-driven approaches offer greater adaptability to changing operating conditions, making them valuable in modern applications37.

In terms of operation, DDMPC combines several key inputs: a comprehensive historical database of process measurements, machine learning models trained on historical data, a sophisticated optimization algorithm, and predefined targets and constraints for the process variables. In our system, data simulated using IES-VE software was utilized, with Random Forest and XGBoost serving as the machine learning models, and the gradient-based optimization algorithm Sequential Least Squares Programming (SLSQP) applied for control adjustments.

The control implementation in DDMPC follows an iterative optimization process similar to conventional MPC, but with key differences in how future predictions are generated. At each control interval, the machine learning models use the current process state and proposed control actions to predict system behavior over a finite horizon. The optimization algorithm then determines the sequence of control moves that minimizes a specified objective function while satisfying operational constraints. In our work, DDMPC was not implemented using pre-existing libraries or frameworks. Instead, a custom DDMPC algorithm was developed in Python specifically to use the predictions or outputs from Random Forest and XGBoost to adjust the control variables at each immediate next step, optimizing for both energy conservation and maintaining the desired environmental settings. The framework of our algorithm is shown in Fig. 4. At each step, incremental adjustments are applied to the input x using a fixed step size, and the cumulative control changes applied to x are updated and used as the basis for future adjustments. This feedback loop between predictive modeling and control mechanisms serves as the foundation for reducing energy use within given constraints, improving energy efficiency within the building. This Python-based implementation of DDMPC highlighted its flexibility and applicability beyond traditional use cases, showcasing how custom control algorithms can be integrated with machine learning models to enhance system performance in dynamic and complex environments.

Framework of the custom DDMPC algorithm. With each iteration, the control variable gets adjusted to reduce error.

The performance of the custom DDMPC implementation was evaluated using Sum of Squared Errors (SSE). At each iteration SSE quantifies the total squared difference between each observed value and its predicted value , based on a total of N observations as shown in Eq. 5. This provides a measure of the model’s accuracy and the effectiveness of the control strategy. A lower SSE reflects a closer match between the model’s predictions and the true values, indicating that the DDMPC is effectively optimizing the system’s performance. By using this feedback, the algorithm iteratively adjusts control inputs, progressively minimizing the error.

Algorithm 2

shows how the custom DDMPC algorithm operates. The procedure titled “Predictive control” takes as input the trained model, the test set for the features (XTest), the corresponding actual target values (YTest), and the horizon that defines how many future time steps the model should forecast and optimize.

Since many machine learning models perform better with normalized inputs, the function starts by scaling both feature inputs and target values. The function then enters a loop that runs for the specified horizon; in this case, the horizon is 20. In each iteration, it first makes a prediction using the current scaled X, storing the result as the predicted Y value. At this point, the prediction is purely based on the trained model without any optimization.

Next, the function initializes u with small random values within the range − 0.1 to 0.1 and uses the SLSQP optimization algorithm to find the best u that minimizes the objective function or procedure. The optimization process adjusts u within the defined bounds, balancing the trade-off between reducing prediction error and keeping input adjustments small.

To optimize the input features, the objective function is called, which takes the control variable u that represents small perturbations applied to scaled X. It updates the input features by adding u, making New X in the process, makes a new prediction, New Y, using New X, and calculates the SSE between the predicted (New Y) and actual target values (Scaled Y). Additionally, a regularization term is included to discourage large changes in the scaled X, ensuring that the optimization does not introduce unrealistic modifications to the input features.

After optimization, the function stores the minimized objective value (representing the prediction error after applying control) in Predictions. It then updates Scaled X by adding the optimized u (Result.X), allowing the adjusted inputs to be used in the next iteration of the loop. This iterative process continues for the entire prediction horizon, progressively refining the model’s predictions while ensuring that the adjustments to the input features remain controlled. Finally, when the function is executed, it returns one output, Prediction, which contains the minimized errors over the horizon.

Results and discussion

Before employing DDMPC as a strategy, the performance of both random forest and XGBoost models was evaluated independently using SSE as an error metric, which serves as the cost function in this study. This cost function is central to the optimization process in DDMPC, as it provides a measure of the system’s performance at each iteration. Without any control mechanism, the Random Forest model achieved an SSE of 228.01, and the XGBoost model resulted in an SSE of 7163.09, suggesting a relatively higher discrepancy between the predicted and actual values. To achieve these results, parameters such as the number of iterations, learning rate, and tree depth were fine-tuned using grid search and cross-validation, ensuring that the models were optimally configured for accurate predictions. Having established the baseline performance of both models, enhancing prediction accuracy by applying DDMPC as a control strategy is examined next. This study focuses on building energy consumption prediction, where accurate forecasting is critical for optimizing building energy efficiency and reducing operational costs.

The errors shown in the graphs of Figs. 6 and 7 represent how the SSE values change each iteration for both the Random Forest and XGBoost models; with each iteration, the control vector u gets optimized to minimize the error of the model. u is an array of adjustments applied to the input features (x) at each iteration, it dictates how much x should change at each step. If it is the first iteration, u is initialized randomly within the range (−1, 1), otherwise its set to the cumulative control adjustments, which refers to the sum of all past control changes applied to x from previous iterations. The optimizer adjusts u at each step to find the best set of small changes that reduce the prediction error. The control adjustments are applied to x after being scaled by a step size of 0.1. A smaller step size ensures gradual stable changes to x, which avoids overshooting of the optimal solution.

Prediction errors over iterations using the random forest model.

Prediction errors over iterations using the XGBoost model.

Figure 6 shows the errors that represent the differences between the predicted values and the actual values as computed by the random forest model after employing DDMPC as a control strategy. These errors quantify the cost function or SSE values computed at each iteration. The decreasing trend in these errors indicates that the DDMPC is progressively optimizing the system’s performance by minimizing SSE values. The higher values at the start suggest that the system began far from the optimal state or with suboptimal control inputs. The system starts with an SSE of 117.24 and after 20 iterations gradually decreases to an SSE of 109.19 in the last iteration.

Similarly, Fig. 7 shows the errors as computed by the XGBoost model. A similar trend of decreasing SSE values is observed. The errors stabilize eventually, which indicates the DDMPC has reached a near-optimal solution or a steady state for the given constraints and predictions. The figure shows that the errors start at a higher SSE value of 122.21 and then drastically decrease to 92.53 after 20 iterations.

After implementing DDMPC, both models showed a significant reduction in SSE due to the iterative optimization of the control vector. For the Random Forest model, the SSE decreased from 228.01 to 109.19, reflecting a 52.1% reduction in error. This improvement suggests that DDMPC effectively fine-tuned the model’s predictions by stabilizing input adjustments. Similarly, the XGBoost model saw an even more substantial improvement, with SSE dropping from 7163.09 to 92.53, representing a 98.7% decrease. This reduction indicates that XGBoost, which initially suffered from higher variance, benefited more from the control strategy, as DDMPC helped regulate its predictions and prevent large deviations.

By refining energy consumption forecasts, DDMPC minimizes both overestimation and underestimation of energy needs, which in turn helps prevent unnecessary energy use. To assess practical energy savings, prediction errors before and after DDMPC application were compared. The baseline error, or initial prediction deviation, and post-DDMPC error were calculated. The difference between them highlights how DDMPC improves forecast accuracy, leading to energy savings.

While error reduction demonstrates the technical improvement in model performance, translating these improvements into practical energy savings provides a more meaningful metric for real-world application. Since more precise predictions help optimize energy consumption, particularly in HVAC systems that account for approximately 38% of total energy consumption according to recent studies38, failing to calculate demand accurately can lead to excessive energy waste, this approach provides a solid way to evaluate the model’s impact. The total reduction in error, converted into energy units, was compared against the overall baseline energy consumption to calculate the percentage of energy saved. For the Random Forest model, the total percentage of energy reduction was 5%, while the XGBoost model showed a slightly higher energy reduction of 5.6%.

Although the XGBoost model started with a considerably higher baseline error compared to Random Forest, applying DDMPC enabled it to make large corrective adjustments. Because XGBoost’s initial predictions were less accurate, the control adjustments applied by DDMPC were more substantial, leading to a larger reduction in error. This resulted in a slightly higher percentage of energy savings. Essentially, when a model has a larger error margin, DDMPC can make more significant corrections, thereby enhancing its performance to a greater extent than a model that already has relatively low errors.

Figure 9 shows the summary plot of Shapley values for the XGBoost model. Like Random Forest, the features with the most impact on the model’s predictions are thermal mass, U-value, and DF, as indicated by the wider spread of their Shapley values. The plot shows thermal mass as having negative Shapley values, which indicates that the feature decreases the model’s predicted output compared to the baseline or average. The opposite SHAP directions observed for Thermal Mass in the Random Forest and XGBoost models indicate that the two models learned different marginal relationships between this feature and the predicted output. In the Random Forest model, Thermal Mass has predominantly positive SHAP values, suggesting that higher Thermal Mass generally increases the predicted value relative to the model baseline. In contrast, the XGBoost model assigns predominantly negative SHAP values to Thermal Mass, indicating that higher Thermal Mass tends to reduce the predicted value relative to its baseline. This difference can be explained by the distinct learning mechanisms of the two models. Random Forest builds many independent decision trees using bootstrap samples and averages their predictions. As a result, it tends to capture broader and more averaged feature effects. XGBoost, however, builds trees sequentially, where each new tree corrects the residual errors of the previous trees. Therefore, XGBoost can capture more complex conditional effects and interactions among variables. The negative SHAP direction in XGBoost suggests that when Thermal Mass is considered together with other influential variables such as U-value, DF, and energy-demand-related parameters, its contribution may act as a reducing factor in the prediction.

The SHAP values summary plot of the XGBoost model showing feature importance and effects on predictions.

From a building-energy perspective, this behavior is plausible. Higher Thermal Mass can either increase or decrease predicted energy-related outputs depending on the operating conditions, envelope properties, internal gains, and heat-transfer dynamics. For example, greater Thermal Mass may increase stored heat and delay heat release in some conditions, leading to a positive contribution. Under other conditions, it may stabilize indoor temperature fluctuations and reduce heating or cooling demand, producing a negative contribution. Therefore, the opposite SHAP directions do not necessarily indicate inconsistency, but rather show that the two models represent the effect of Thermal Mass differently because of their different treatment of feature interactions and nonlinear relationships.

This finding highlights the importance of using explainable machine learning not only to rank feature importance but also to compare how different algorithms interpret the same physical variable. While both models identify Thermal Mass as an important predictor, the direction of its SHAP contribution should be interpreted in relation to model structure, feature interactions, and the specific prediction context. Future work may further investigate this behavior using SHAP dependence plots and interaction analysis to determine under which ranges of Thermal Mass and interacting variables the contribution changes direction.

The results shown by IML strongly align with the results of our previous research39 where feature importance was used to determine the influence of each feature on the Random Forest and XGBoost models. Both models were shown to be highly influenced by the features thermal mass, DF, U-value and R-value. While similar, there are a few reasons to use IML techniques like SHAP over traditional feature importance methods. Feature importance only illustrates how much a feature matters without providing insight into the direction or distribution of its impact. On the contrary, the summary plot gives a global view of feature importance, showing not only which features are most important but also the distribution of their impact on the predictions. It visualizes both the magnitude and direction of the feature’s influence, making it clear whether a feature’s effect is positive or negative across the dataset. Besides that, SHAP’s summary plot provides a visual summary of feature importance aided by color coding.

To further evaluate the interpretability of both models, additional metrics, sparsity, and fidelity were examined. Sparsity reflects the summary of the SHAP explanations by measuring the number of features that significantly contribute to each prediction. Fidelity, on the other hand, quantifies how well these explanations capture the behavior of the original model.

For the random forest model, the sparsity value was 0.85, indicating that only a limited subset of features is predominantly responsible for the predictions. The fidelity score was 0.0, which suggests that the SHAP-based explanations reliably capture the model’s behavior. The sparsity level for XGBoost stands at 0.81, and though slightly lower than that of random forest, it still highlights that a similarly compact set of features drives its forecasts. The fidelity score was also 0.0, again demonstrating that the explanations align closely with the model’s behavior.

Conclusion

This paper investigated the integration of DDMPC with the machine learning models, Random Forest and XGBoost, while also employing IML techniques. By leveraging SHAP for interpretability, the research provided insights into the impact of key features on energy consumption predictions. The methodology emphasizes robust model evaluation using Grid Search and cross-validation, to ensure the reliability of the results.

The two machine learning models were evaluated independently with the SSE error metric, after that, DDMPC was used in conjunction with both models and evaluated with the same metric. This integration resulted in a 52.1% and 98.7% decrease in error value for the random forest and XGBoost models, respectively. This translated into a 5% and 5.6% decrease in energy consumption for the models.

The use of IML enabled the identification of the most influential features in the models predictions, which matched the feature importance results from our previous studies. To evaluate interpretability, both sparsity and fidelity were used as metrics. The Random Forest model achieved scores of 0.85 for sparsity and 0.0 for fidelity, while XGBoost achieved scores of 0.81 and 0.0, respectively. These results prove that the SHAP explanations were not only easy to interpret but also accurately reflect the behavior of the machine learning models.

Overall, the results show the potential of combining predictive algorithms with interpretability tools to enhance energy management systems. Future work will focus on refining the models architecture, conducting further comparative analysis, and expanding the scope of applications to validate the broader applicability of this framework. A key aspect of this refinement will be prioritizing high-impact features, such as thermal mass, U-value, and DF, which have the highest SHAP contributions. Ensuring these features are accurately measured and well-represented in the dataset can further improve model reliability and generalizability, leading to better predictions and more effective energy management strategies.
