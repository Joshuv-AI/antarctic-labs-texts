Sex, Drugs, and the Language of Donor Narratives: Associations With Kidney Acceptance
Authors: Ritah R. Chumdermpadetsuk, Yotam Ophir, Jackson Fulk-Logon, John Christopher Polanco-Santana, Thomas H. Feeley, Devin E. Eckhoff, David D. Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background.Despite the shortage of donor kidneys, nonutilization remains common. We hypothesized that social history language in donor narratives influences acceptance. Using Natural Language Processing, we evaluated how language describing high-risk behaviors interacts with objective data (eg, hepatitis B virus [HBV]/hepatitis C virus [HCV] testing) to affect kidney acceptance.Methods.We queried the Standard Transplant Analysis and Research file (2007–2023), analyzing 151 591 donor narratives with Natural Language Processing to identify 40 topics grouped as Templated Administrative Content and Free-Text Clinical Descriptors. Free-Text Clinical Descriptors were further subdivided into 4 communities, including Social History. Modified Poisson regression modeled associations with acceptance across 3 eras (2007–2014, 2015–2020, 2021–2023), including interactions with HBV/HCV serology.Results.Of 151 591 potential donors, 122 106 (80.55%) were accepted. Positive HBV/HCV testing reduced acceptance in era 1 (risk ratio [RR], 0.773; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.744-0.802), with attenuation in era 2 (RR, 0.893; 95% CI, 0.878-0.908), and no effect in era 3 (RR, 1.006; 95% CI, 0.993-1.019). Interactions between HBV/HCV positivity and topics reflecting language related to illicit drug use or infectious risk evaluation were associated with reduced acceptance in era 1 only, with no significant interaction in era 2 or era 3. In era 1, interaction terms indicated reduced acceptance for language describing illicit drug use and/or sex in exchange for drugs (RR, 0.665; 95% CI, 0.549-0.805) and infectious risk evaluation (RR, 0.640; 95% CI, 0.452-0.905).Conclusions.The association between high-risk behavioral language and lower kidney acceptance was present in earlier eras but not in the modern era. These findings suggest that the influence of subjective narrative language on decision-making has diminished over time, consistent with advances in antiviral therapy, policy changes, and increased utilization of higher-risk donors.

INTRODUCTION

Kidney transplantation in the United States is limited by the availability of organs.1 Currently, >800 000 Americans are living with end-stage kidney disease, with >141 000 individuals on the waitlist for a kidney transplant.1 Paradoxically, there is a concerning trend toward increasing nonutilization of kidney allografts, with an all-time high rate of 27.9% in 20231 (compared with 24.6% in 2021 and 17.9% in 2011).2 Patients undergoing hemodialysis experience substantial reductions in quality of life and face increased mortality risk.3 Therefore, understanding and addressing factors contributing to nonutilization is critical.

Organ acceptance decisions are time-sensitive and complex, requiring the integration of medical and logistical considerations.4 One key concern is the risk of disease transmission from donor to recipient. Each potential donor possesses a donor narrative (DN), compiled by Organ Procurement Organizations (OPOs) and often containing demographic, medical, and/or social information designed to help clinicians determine the suitability of the organ for transplantation.4,5 Historically, descriptions of social history have been used to convey the donor’s risk of carrying blood-borne infections, including hepatitis B virus (HBV)/hepatitis C virus (HCV). In recent years, however, new diagnostic technologies, such as nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT), have made laboratory confirmation of HBV/HCV status routinely available while significantly shortening the uncertainty window for false negative testing.6 Furthermore, groundbreaking studies—namely, the THINKER (Transplanting Hepatitis C Kidneys into Negative KidnEy Recipients)7-9 and EXPANDER (Exploring Renal Transplants Using Hepatitis C Infected Donors for HCV Negative Recipients)9,10 trials—have demonstrated the safety of transplanting HCV-infected kidneys; when coupled with a direct-acting antiviral (DAA) medication regimen, HCV infection can be reliably treated with excellent outcomes in naive recipients. Additionally, the detrimental impact of HBV/HCV on kidney allograft quality has not been established.9,11,12 As a result, the transplant community’s perspective on increased risk donation has continued to evolve.13-15

Reflecting these medical advances, the 2020 U.S. Public Health Service (PHS) guidelines eliminated the “increased risk” label to reduce stigma and improve organ utilization.16 Key changes included replacing separate informed consent with recipient notification, shortening the risk-assessment window from 12 mo to 1 mo, and updating screening criteria for HIV, HBV, and HCV. However, a follow-up study 2 y after this update showed no improvement in kidney utilization.17 We hypothesized that despite evolving policies, DNs remain a source of subjective bias that can influence clinical decision-making. Therefore, we sought to investigate how social history language interacts with objective clinical data (eg, HBV/HCV test results) to affect kidney acceptance, and whether modern policy shifts have successfully altered transplant centers’ acceptance patterns over time.

DNs consist of structured as well as unstructured data fields.4,5 Unstructured data fields are nonstandardized, open-ended texts containing objective and subjective elements.4 A rich and large textual data set, it is amenable to analysis via Natural Language Processing (NLP), and particularly Unsupervised Machine Learning techniques such as Topic Modeling (TM).18 TM is a subfield of artificial intelligence that allows for the extraction, organization, and interpretation of relevant themes from open-ended text narratives through computational linguistics and machine learning.18 The use of NLP and TM for clinical research is a new but rapidly growing avenue; only a few exploratory studies within the topic of organ allocation have been conducted, offering promising, if preliminary, insight.4,19-22 This study constitutes one of the first to use Unsupervised Machine Learning to identify the contribution of language in kidney allograft pursuit.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design

This study used data from the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN). The OPTN data system includes data on all donors, waitlisted candidates, and transplant recipients in the United States, submitted by the members of the OPTN. The Health Resources and Services Administration, U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, provides oversight for the activities of the OPTN contractor.

With institutional review board approval (Protocol No. 2022P001007), the Standard Transplant Analysis and Research file was queried to identify 151 591 potential donors with available unstructured DNs. Analysis of DNs was conducted using NLP techniques, specifically the Analysis of Topic Model Networks (ANTMN) approach18 (Supplementary Material [SM]1, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A904). During model selection, 5-fold cross-validation was performed across candidate topic numbers and alpha hyperparameter values, with model performance assessed using held-out perplexity (Figure 1). Perplexity decreased as the number of topics increased, with diminishing improvement at higher topic numbers; therefore, a 40-topic model was selected to balance model performance with clinical interpretability. The model identified 40 topics (SM2, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A904), which were qualitatively labeled by 2 general surgery residents (R.R.C., J.C.P.-S.) and 1 transplant surgeon (D.D.L.), with support from a computational social scientist (Y.O.). Researchers individually reviewed and labeled each topic based on the 200 most representative documents (ie, DNs) and keywords for each topic in the ANTMN model. Subsequently, the topic labels were discussed and iteratively modified until consensus was reached among the 2 residents, transplant surgeon, and computational social scientist (R.R.C., J.C.P.-S., D.D.L., and Y.O.).23,24 We addressed trustworthiness (validity of inferences) by using strategies such as triangulation, member checking, and maintaining an audit trail to ensure the credibility, dependability, and confirmability of our findings.24

Five-fold cross-validation of topic model combinations of number of topics (k) and hyperparameter (alpha).

Topics were manually categorized into 5 groups. First, topics were categorized as Templated Administrative Content (TAC) versus Free-Text Clinical Descriptors (FCDs). TAC topics were templated text elements consistently used across multiple DNs. These elements were not specific to individual potential donors and served to communicate routine, procedural information uniformly (eg, travel instructions, donor hospital contact details, COVID-19 precautions). In contrast, FCD topics were entries unique to each potential donor, offering individualized information. FCD topics were further manually subdivided into 4 thematic clusters (“network communities”): Social History, Medical History, Mechanism of Death, and Donor Workup. These thematic clusters were chosen based on their alignment with key components of the standard History & Physical note. As with topic labeling, the manual categorization of topics was achieved by R.R.C., J.C.P.-S., D.D.L., and Y.O. through a process of iterative review and consensus building.

For the study’s primary analysis, we focused on topics within the Social History network community, relating to sexual behavior, illicit drug use, incarceration, and related lifestyle factors. The ANTMN model estimated the prevalence of each topic in a given DN (whereby the percentages of all 40 topics summed to 100% for each DN). The textual independent variables were percentages associated with NLP-derived topics or the manually clustered communities, treated as continuous variables.

The primary outcome was kidney acceptance (binary), defined as procurement with intent to transplant; nonacceptance was defined as nonprocurement or procurement for purposes other than transplantation. We examined interaction effects with HBV/HCV test results (binary). Negative results were defined as negative HBV surface antigen, HBV NAAT, HCV antibody, and HCV NAAT. Donors with missing values were classified as negative if all available tests were negative. Positive results were defined as any positive HBV surface antigen, HBV NAAT, HCV antibody, or HCV NAAT.

This definition was selected rather than restricting to NAAT positivity alone because serologic results (HBV surface antigen or HCV antibody) may influence acceptance decisions when confirmatory testing is unavailable (~25% of the cohort). Additionally, even with negative NAAT, these findings may affect decision-making because of requirements for recipient disclosure and posttransplant monitoring for potential viral transmission. To assess whether our findings were sensitive to the definition of HBV/HCV positivity, we performed a secondary NAAT-only analysis using HBV or HCV NAAT results alone. Donors without sufficient NAAT data were excluded. Because NAAT testing was incomplete, particularly in earlier eras, these results were interpreted as a sensitivity analysis rather than a replacement for the primary exposure definition.

Subject Selection

Using the Standard Transplant Analysis and Research file (from October 31, 2007, to September 30, 2023), we identified 184 472 potential donors, defined by the United Network for Organ Sharing as any registrant assigned a United Network for Organ Sharing identification number, regardless of whether donation occurred. Potential donors without available DNs (n = 18 508) were omitted before textual modeling. Potential donors aged <18 y (n = 13 708) and with no available HBV or HCV test results (n = 665) were excluded from the statistical analyses. This resulted in a final cohort of 151 591 adult potential donors.

Statistical Analysis

The NLP model was validated against the DNs’ structured data fields (SM3, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A904). Modified Poisson regression with cluster-robust SEs was used to model the impact of different NLP-derived topics and communities on acceptance; the interaction effect of HBV/HCV test results was examined. Models were constructed to evaluate: (1) the role of FCD versus TAC language (ie, the summation of the 4 FCD communities versus the summation of all TAC language), (2) each of the 4 FCD communities (ie, Social History, Medical History, Mechanism of Death, and Donor Workup) separately, and (3) individual topics of interest within the Social History community (Topic 40, “illicit drug use and/or sex in exchange for drugs” and Topic 27, “infectious risk evaluation”; illustrative quotes are provided in SM4 [SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A904]).

To further validate the TM approach, we reviewed the 200 DNs with the highest Topic 40 prevalence to determine whether the model-generated topic corresponded to the intended narrative content. In 199 of 200 cases, the language described affirmative high-risk behaviors, such as illicit drug use, transactional sex, or related exposures, rather than documentation negating such behaviors. These findings support the interpretability of the topic model and suggest that Topic 40 accurately captures donor behaviors associated with infectious risk rather than statements documenting risk absence.

Analyses were stratified into 3 eras selected to reflect major changes in infectious risk terminology, policy, and clinical practice. Era 1 (2007–2014) reflected the period when donors meeting PHS risk criteria were commonly described using “high-risk” terminology, and before widespread use of modern HCV treatment. Era 2 (2015–2020) followed adoption of the “increased risk” framework and coincided with increasing clinical experience with DAAs, including the THINKER and EXPANDER trials supporting transplantation of HCV-positive kidneys into HCV-negative recipients. Era 3 (2021–2023) followed implementation of OPTN policy changes aligned with the 2020 PHS guidelines, which eliminated the specific “increased risk donor” label, shortened the donor risk-assessment window to 1 mo, and removed outdated behavioral criteria.

Our goal was to estimate the effect of unstructured language above and beyond that of variables known to be associated with kidney acceptance.25-29 To account for potential covariates, models were controlled for DN word count, donor age, sex, race, body mass index, ABO blood type, donation after circulatory death, cardiac arrest (before expiration), cause of death, hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, cancer, impaired kidney function (defined as terminal serum creatinine ≥1.5), heavy alcohol use, smoking, and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention increased risk classification (formerly “high-risk”). Cluster-robust SEs were used to account for within-OPO correlation in outcomes. A variance inflation factor threshold of 10 was used to ensure minimal collinearity between covariates; at this threshold, no covariates were removed.30 Observations with missing data were excluded from analysis, and complete case analysis was performed. Statistical significance was assessed using 2-sided P-values with a threshold of 0.05. All statistical tests were conducted using STATA, Version 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

RESULTS

Description of NLP-derived Communities and Topics

The NLP model identified 40 topics, which were qualitatively labeled and manually grouped into TAC (22) versus FCD (18) topics. FCD topics were further manually subdivided into 4 categories: Social History (4), Medical History (3), Mechanism of Death (6), and Donor Workup (5; Figure 2A). General Characteristics of Potential Donors Based on Kidney Acceptance

A total of 151 591 potential donors were studied, of which 122 106 (80.55%) resulted in kidney acceptance. Of 122 106 acceptances, 120 775 (98.91%) were bilateral (ie, both kidneys were accepted), and 1331 (1.09%) were unilateral (ie, only 1 kidney was accepted). The characteristics of potential donors based on acceptance are displayed (Table 1). Accepted donors had a lower mean proportion of FCD language (62.49% versus 65.81%) and were younger (mean age 42.99 versus 51.05 y). Acceptance was less likely among donors with positive HBV and/or HCV test results, hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, cancer, impaired kidney function, smoking, and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention high-risk classification.

Impact of FCD Language Communities and Topics on Kidney Acceptance, With Interaction Effect of HBV/HCV Test Results

FCD language in DNs was not significantly associated with acceptance, although point estimates suggested a lower likelihood of acceptance (Table 2). Among donors with negative HBV/HCV testing, a 1% increase in FCD language was associated with acceptance risk ratios (RRs) of 0.968 (0.894-1.048; era 1), 0.942 (0.877-1.013; era 2), and 0.942 (0.886-1.001; era 3). When interaction effects were examined, positive HBV/HCV testing strengthened this association only in era 2; interaction terms for FCD language × HBV/HCV test results were 0.928 (0.743-1.158; era 1), 0.786 (0.717-0.861; era 2), and 1.002 (0.951-1.056; era 3).

The proportion of FCD language describing social history was not associated with acceptance within the reference group. Among donors with positive HBV/HCV testing, the interaction estimate was directionally lower in era 1 but reached statistical significance only in era 2 (era 1: RR, 0.798 [0.598-1.065] and era 2: RR, 0.837 [0.762-0.919]). No association was observed in the modern era (era 3: RR, 1.004 [0.902-1.119]).

The proportion of DNs dedicated to Topic 40 had point estimates suggesting an increased likelihood of acceptance within the reference group, reaching statistical significance in era 2 only (era 1: RR, 1.094 [0.998-1.199]; era 2: RR, 1.155 [1.086-1.229]; and era 3: RR, 1.102 [0.992-1.224]). Among donors with positive HBV/HCV testing, this relationship reversed in era 1 (RR, 0.665 [0.549-0.805]) but was not observed in subsequent eras (era 2: RR, 0.976 [0.882-1.080] and era 3: RR, 0.982 [0.855-1.127]). Topic 27 was not associated with acceptance within the reference group. Among donors with positive HBV/HCV testing, higher proportions of Topic 27 were associated with reduced acceptance in era 1 (RR, 0.640 [0.452-0.905]), but this effect was not significant in era 2 (RR, 0.831 [0.675-1.024]) or era 3 (RR, 1.041 [0.759-1.428]).

In a NAAT-only sensitivity analysis excluding donors without NAAT data, HBV/HCV positivity was defined using HBV or HCV NAAT results alone (SM6, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A904). The interaction between FCD language and positive HBV/HCV testing remained significant only in era 2, consistent with the primary analysis. However, estimates were less precise because of limited NAAT availability and the smaller sample size in the 2007–2014 era, particularly for less common topics.

DISCUSSION

Our findings suggest that narrative descriptions of high-risk behaviors in DNs amplified the negative effect of HBV/HCV positivity on kidney acceptance in an era-dependent manner. While HBV and HCV testing were associated with substantially reduced acceptance in earlier eras, this effect was attenuated and no longer observed in the modern era. Interaction effects also decreased with time, reflecting a temporal shift in how narrative language influenced decision-making.

In the earliest era (2007–2014), the strongest interaction effects were observed for specific topics related to drug use, transactional sex, and infectious risk evaluation (Topics 40 and 27). During this period, confirmatory NAAT testing was less consistently available, and infectious risk was often inferred from behavioral descriptors rather than objective laboratory confirmation. As a result, narrative descriptions of high-risk behaviors likely amplified uncertainty around true infection status and were associated with lower acceptance of seropositive donors, suggesting that subjective language influenced decision-making beyond available clinical data.

In the intermediate era (2015–2020), broader measures of language, including total FCD and higher-level network communities, demonstrated the strongest interaction effects. Although acceptance of seropositive donors was increasing during this time, additional narrative information related to social history, medical comorbidities, or donor workup was associated with further reductions in utilization among these donors.

In contrast, in the modern era (2021–2023), the combination of FCD with positive HBV/HCV testing was no longer associated with reduced acceptance. This likely reflects a shift toward greater reliance on objective clinical data, particularly confirmatory NAAT testing, with reduced influence from narrative descriptions that do not provide additional risk information beyond laboratory results. Together, these findings suggest that the influence of narrative language is greatest when objective measures of risk are uncertain and diminishes as diagnostic clarity improves. This pattern is also consistent with increasing acceptance of higher-risk donors with the emergence and clinical adoption of DAAs as effective therapy for chronic HCV,31 enabling the safe transplantation of HCV-infected kidneys into naive recipients.7-10

This temporal shift also aligns with the 2020 U.S. PHS guideline updates, which eliminated the “increased risk” designation and reframed risk communication. Although prior work has suggested that terminology changes alone did not immediately improve organ utilization, our results suggest that the broader evolution of clinical practice, diagnostics, and policy likely underlies the reduced influence of narrative framing over time.

From a cognitive perspective, language functions as a framing mechanism that can influence perception and decision-making, particularly under conditions of uncertainty. Terms describing behaviors such as IV drug use or incarceration carry sociocultural connotations that may activate implicit biases or heuristics, potentially leading clinicians to overestimate risk despite available objective data.32 Our findings suggest that such effects were present in earlier eras but may be attenuating as reliance on objective measures increases and clinical experience with higher-risk donors grows.

Despite these improvements, nonutilization of donor kidneys remains high.1 While our models do not establish causality, they identify narrative language as a potentially modifiable component of the acceptance process. Efforts to standardize or refine DNs, such as reducing redundant or potentially biasing language, should be evaluated prospectively to determine whether they can improve utilization. Although language associated with perceived HBV/HCV transmission risk no longer appears to meaningfully reduce donor pursuit in the contemporary era, our findings demonstrate that DN language has the ability to influence transplant decision-making. The magnitude and relevance of language effects may vary across clinical contexts and over time, underscoring the need for ongoing evaluation.

Additionally, the decision to transplant extends beyond initial kidney acceptance and is inherently multifactorial. However, we posit that narrative elements—particularly language describing infectious risk—are more likely to influence whether an organ is pursued rather than whether it is ultimately transplanted. Final utilization decisions often depend on postprocurement factors, including intraoperative assessment, biopsy findings, and machine perfusion characteristics, which are not captured in DNs and therefore fall outside the scope of this analysis. Analyses using transplantation as the outcome yielded findings consistent with those observed for acceptance (data not shown); therefore, to maintain focus on early decision-making, we present acceptance as the primary endpoint.

The use of NLP to study DNs is relatively novel. Prior studies have focused on individual words as predictors of acceptance, limiting interpretability and failing to account for context.4,19 In contrast, our use of TM enabled identification of coherent thematic structures within unstructured text, allowing analysis of language in context. This approach also distinguished between TAC and FCDs, providing insight into how different types of language contribute to decision-making.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design introduces the possibility of misclassification and residual confounding, and causal inferences cannot be established. Additionally, our analysis is limited to information contained within DNs and does not account for verbal communication between transplant centers and OPOs. Variation in acceptance practices across transplant centers may also contribute to these findings and is not fully captured in this analysis. Finally, while TM provides a useful framework for summarizing large text corpora, it cannot fully capture nuanced linguistic context, and interpretation of topics requires subjective judgment.

In conclusion, narrative descriptions of high-risk behaviors in DNs were associated with lower kidney acceptance in earlier eras but not in the modern era. These findings suggest that the influence of narrative language on decision-making may be diminishing in parallel with advances in antiviral therapy, improved diagnostics, and evolving clinical practice. As the transplant community continues to expand the donor pool, understanding the interaction between objective data and narrative framing will be essential to optimizing organ utilization.
