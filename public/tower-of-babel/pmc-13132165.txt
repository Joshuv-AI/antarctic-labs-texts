One- and Two-Year Outcomes of Switching to a Dolutegravir-Based ART Regimen: An Observational Emulation of Sequential Target Trials
Authors: Amy Zheng, Eleanor J Murray, Alana T Brennan, Ross Greener, Emma M Kileel, Jacob Bor, Willem D F Venter, Pedro T Pisa, Matthew P Fox, Mhairi Maskew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical trials evaluating the impact of switching to dolutegravir have excluded individuals with poorly controlled viremia. However, in practice, clinicians may switch individuals to dolutegravir who have such challenges. Utilizing the Target Trial Framework, we estimated the effect of switching to a dolutegravir-based regimen, vs not switching and remaining on an efavirenz-based regimen, on 12- and 24-month retention and viral suppression in Johannesburg, South Africa, from 2019 to 2022.MethodsUsing data from the Themba Lethu HIV Clinical Cohort, individuals were eligible if they were currently on an efavirenz-based regimen, aged 16+, and not pregnant at the start of each trial. We conducted a pooled linear regression model with bootstrapping to estimate the causal risk difference and 95% CI adjusting for patient characteristics via inverse probability treatment weighting.ResultsOf 1493 individuals, 967 (64.7%) switched to a dolutegravir-based regimen over 9 sequential trials, and 526 (35.2%) did not. At 12 months, switch to a dolutegravir-based regimen was associated with a 14 percentage point (95% CI, 0.10 to 0.19) increase in retention and a 2 percentage point (95% CI, −0.04 to 0.08) increase in viral suppression. By 24 months, we observed a 1 percentage point (95% CI, −0.05 to 0.07) increase in retention and an 8 percentage point (95% CI, 0.02 to 0.14) increase in viral suppression.ConclusionsSwitching to dolutegravir was associated with a 14 percentage point increase in retention at 12 months and an 8 percentage point increase in viral suppression at 24 months. Our findings suggest that switching to dolutegravir does not harm and might improve retention and viral suppression over a 24-month period.

With an estimated 8 million people with HIV-1, South Africa has the largest global burden of HIV [1, 2]. As of 2024, 95% of the 8 million people with HIV know their status, 81% of those who know their status are on ART, and of those, 74% are virally suppressed [3].

As part of efforts to achieve the UNAIDS 95-95-95 targets, South Africa routinely updates their antiretroviral therapy (ART) treatment guidelines. In October 2019, South Africa recommended the use of dolutegravir as part of first-line ART instead of efavirenz [4], based on recommendations from the World Health Organization (WHO) and results from randomized clinical trials (RCTs) demonstrating that dolutegravir had better tolerability and fewer adverse drug reactions while maintaining similar efficacy [5–10]. However, RCTs rarely replicate real-world settings and are not suitable for evaluating retention due to intensive follow-up procedures and exclusions of severely ill patients. Furthermore, most trials evaluating the effect of dolutegravir on retention and viral suppression among treatment-experienced patients required patients to be virologically suppressed and/or did not use efavirenz as the sole comparator [9, 11–13]. These requirements do not reflect real-world clinical settings. For example, patients with poor adherence may switch to dolutegravir if clinicians feel it might lead to improved outcomes. The effectiveness of dolutegravir among patients with a history of poor adherence is not well understood. Furthermore, use of a nonefavirenz comparator may not be as informative for clinicians as efavirenz has been recommended and commonly used in first-line ART in low- and middle-income countries [14]. Observational studies evaluating the impact of switching to dolutegravir were limited in their ability to identify causal effects due to residual confounding and selection bias. Novel methods such as the Target Trial Framework can help minimize such biases encountered in observational studies. Applying this novel analytic method within an observational cohort will allow for robust estimation of the impact of switching to dolutegravir on retention in HIV care in a real-world clinical cohort.

Therefore, we sought to leverage a clinical cohort of patients initiating an efavirenz-based regimen in South Africa before 2019 to estimate the effect of switching to dolutegravir-based ART from efavirenz-based regimens compared with initiating and remaining on efavirenz-based ART on 12- and 24-month retention and viral suppression using a Target Trial Emulation Framework.

METHODS

Target Trial of Switching to Dolutegravir From Efavirenz

For this study, we used a target trial framework, which involves 2 key steps: (1) specifying the protocol of the ideal RCT and (2) emulating that trial using observational data. Our target trial of interest would be an RCT whereby individuals who (1) initiated tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/lamivudine/efavirenz or tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/emtricitabine/efavirenz before 2019 and (2) at the time of guideline change in October 2019 remained on an efavirenz-based ART, were aged ≥16, and were not pregnant were randomized to switch to a dolutegravir-based regimen or remain on an efavirenz-based regimen [4]. Individuals would be followed from the time of randomization until 24 months, death, or loss to follow-up. Components of the ideal target trial, as well as the observational emulation, are provided in Table 1.

The target trial would use a sequential randomization approach. At each study visit a patient attended while eligible to switch regimens, they would be randomized to 1 of 2 treatment strategies: (1) remain on efavirenz-based ART or (2) switch to tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/lamivudine/dolutegravir or tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/emtricitabine/dolutegravir. For both groups, the backbone regimen would not change. Once an individual is randomized to switch to a dolutegravir-based regimen, they would no longer be eligible for future randomization but would be followed for assessment of 12- and 24-month outcomes. This trial design is appropriate if the number of eligible individuals is large compared with the probability of being assigned to switch at any given visit and might be used in a real trial if, for example, the availability of the new regimen was low.

The primary outcome would be 12-month retention and viral suppression with a secondary outcome of 24-month retention and viral suppression. After randomization, individuals would be followed until the earliest of 24 months, loss to follow-up, or death. Retention would be defined as attending a study visit at 12 and 24 months +/− 3 months to accommodate flexibility in study visits. Viral suppression would be defined as a viral load ≤50 copies/mL +/− 3 months. The causal contrast would be the intent-to-treat effect of assignment to switching vs not switching (where those assigned to not switch at baseline could switch their treatment regimen at a follow-up visit). This is a single time point treatment intervention.

Observational Emulation of a Target Trial of Switching to Dolutegravir From Efavirenz

We emulated this target trial using data from the Themba Lethu Clinical Cohort. Established in 2004, this is a prospective cohort of patients receiving public sector care at Themba Lethu Clinic in Johannesburg, South Africa [6]. Patients have viral load testing conducted 6 and 12 months after ART initiation and yearly thereafter [15]. The clinic is largely a referral clinic for patients with adherence issues and complex disease.

A sequential trial design was used because few individuals switch to a dolutegravir-based regimen in any week. As patient visits with clinicians do not occur on a strict schedule, emulation of sequential trials is challenging as it is not clear which patients remain on a regimen at each time point. To address this, we defined 9 consecutive 4-month baseline windows starting August 1, 2019. All patients who received clinical care during each baseline window were assessed for eligibility.

The eligibility criteria in the observational emulation were the same as in the target trial (Table 1). For each sequential trial, eligible individuals were those who had not previously switched to dolutegravir, were not pregnant, were aged ≥16 years, and were currently on efavirenz at the start of the baseline window. For sequential trials 2–8, a 4-month baseline window was used to determine each individual's treatment assignment (Figure 1). Sequential trials 1 and 9 used a 5-month baseline window to ensure that a sufficient number of individuals switched in those trials.

Abbreviation: ART, antiretroviral therapy.

Follow-up began the day eligibility criteria were met at the start of the baseline window. Eligible participants were assigned a treatment strategy (switch vs no switch) that was compatible with their data at baseline: if an individual remained on efavirenz for the entire baseline window, they were a nonswitcher for that trial; if they switched to dolutegravir during the baseline window, they were a switcher. Therefore, until an individual switched regimens they could be in more than 1 trial, and individuals who never switched would be in all 9 trials (Supplementary Text 1). We compared outcomes among those who switched vs those who did not to evaluate the effect of “switch now” vs “do not switch now, with the potential to switch later” [16, 17].

The primary outcomes for the emulation were 12-month retention and suppression (≤50 copies/mL) with secondary outcomes at 24 months (Table 1). Retention was defined as attending an appointment (eg, a clinic appointment, medication pickup, or lab appointment) within +/− 6 months of the time point. This extended window was important in 2020–2021 because the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic changed visit scheduling practices and caused disruptions in care, leading to delays in when clinic appointments took place [18]. Viral suppression was evaluated among those who had a viral load at each time point and is therefore conditional upon retention. All analyses evaluating 24-month outcomes were restricted to trials starting in 2019–2021 as participants who switched to dolutegravir in 2022 were not eligible for a full 24 months of follow-up due to database closure on September 1, 2024 (Figure 1).

Statistical Analyses

As switching regimens was at the discretion of patients and their clinicians and not randomized, to emulate randomization in each sequential trial we used inverse probability of treatment weighting of marginal structural models. We conducted multivariable logistic regression to calculate the probability of switching in each baseline window with exposure as the dependent variable and confounders of the relationship between exposure (eg, switch vs do not switch) and outcomes as independent variables [19–21]. Confounders were chosen based on directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) and included the following covariates measured at ART initiation: natal sex, WHO stage, tuberculosis, smoking, alcohol use, and CD4 count; and the following measured at the start of each trial: age, CD4 count conducted (yes/no), tuberculosis, history of ART interruption, prior single-drug substitution; and sequential trial number (Supplementary Figure 1). Education level and employment were included as precision variables. We used unstabilized weights for the probability of switching to avoid introducing bias regarding the distribution of switching times in the weighted pseudo-population [22]. We calculated censoring weights for viral suppression to minimize selection bias due to loss to follow-up [19, 23]. Censoring weights for retention were not calculated as loss to follow-up was part of the outcome. In addition to the covariates used for confounding at 24 months, censoring weights also included whether the individual was retained at 12 months. Stabilized censoring weights were calculated to reduce the variance of effect estimates [19] by using the crude probability of not being censored in the numerator instead of 1 [19].

Inverse probability of treatment and censoring weights were multiplied and truncated at the 99th percentile. We used pooled linear regression models across person-trials under the assumption of no interaction of the effect of interest by calendar time to then estimate the causal risk difference [19]. Standard errors were obtained via 500 nonparametric bootstraps to account for the same individual appearing in multiple trials. A repeated statement in all models was also used to account for clustering by participant.

Secondary Analyses

In addition to the analyses described above, we estimated effects stratified by natal sex and baseline CD4 count. For these analyses, inverse probability of treatment and censoring weights were re-estimated within each stratum, with the stratification variable removed from the weight models.

Sensitivity Analyses

The following sensitivity analyses were conducted: (1) viral suppression was defined as a viral load ≤1000 copies/mL, (2) we restricted analyses of 12-month outcomes to participants eligible for a full 24 months of follow-up, (3) we assumed participants with a missing viral load were virally unsuppressed as having a viral load conducted is conditional upon being retained, (4) weights were truncated at the 95th percentile, (5) unstabilized weights for both treatment and censoring were truncated at the 99th percentile, (6) we changed the baseline window to be a 2-month window, and (7) we changed the baseline window to 1 month.

Ethics Statement

Data from Themba Lethu are collected for routine clinical purposes. The Human Research Ethics Committee of the University of the Witwatersrand (M140201) and the Institutional Review Board of Boston University (H-29768) approved the use of the de-identified data. As such, an informed consent waiver was approved.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

Of the 1493 unique individuals included across 9 sequential trials, 967 (64.7%) switched to a dolutegravir-based regimen and 526 (35.2%) did not. This corresponded to a total of 5213 “person-trials,” of which 967 (18.5%) were switcher and 4246 (81.5%) were nonswitcher “person-trials” (Table 2; Supplementary Table 1). The median age (interquartile range [IQR]) was 44.4 (36.7–52.5) years, with 55.4% (n = 2889) female. Most participants reported completing secondary school or greater and reported no smoking or alcohol use (Table 2). When limited to sequential trials from 2019 to 2021 (eg, Trials 1–7) for the 24-month outcomes of the 1479 unique individuals included, 905 (61.2%) switched to a dolutegravir-based regimen and 574 (38.8%) did not. This corresponded to 4996 “person-trials,” of which 905 (18.1%) were switcher and 4091 (81.9%) were nonswitcher “person-trials” (Supplementary Table 2). Baseline demographics of this subpopulation were similar to those of the overall cohort. Baseline demographic characteristics for each sequential trial and distribution of the weights are presented in Supplementary Tables 3–12.

Effect of Dolutegravir on Retention and Viral Suppression

At 12 months, 95.9% (927 switcher “person-trials”; 99 unique individuals) of 967 switcher “person-trials” were retained. Of 809 switcher “person-trials” with a viral load, 83.2% (673 switcher “person-trials”; 83 unique individuals) were virally suppressed. Comparatively, 77.4% (3288 nonswitcher “person-trials” trials; 1234 unique individuals) of 4246 nonswitcher “person-trials” were retained. Of 2859 nonswitcher “person-trials” with a viral load, 78.0% (2230 nonswitcher “person-trials”; 989 unique individuals) were virally suppressed (Figures 2 and 3). There were 158 (16.3%) switcher “person-trials” and 1387 (32.6%) nonswitcher “person-trials” with a missing viral load. Of the 967 switcher “person-trials,” 27 (2.8%) were lost to follow-up and 13 (1.3%) died by 12 months. Of the 4246 nonswitcher “person-trials,” 891 (20.9%) were lost to follow-up and 67 (1.6%) died. At 12 months, after weighting, we found a 14 percentage point (95% CI, 0.10 to 0.19) increase in retention and a 2 percentage point (95% CI, −0.04 to 0.08) increase in viral suppression among those retained for individuals who switched to a dolutegravir-based ART regimen compared with those who did not across 9 sequential trials (Figures 2 and 3). At 24 months, among 905 switcher “person-trials,” 84.3% (763 switcher “person-trials”; 64 unique individuals) were retained. Of 671 switcher “person-trials” with a viral load, 86.9% (583 switcher “person-trials”; 66 unique individuals) were virally suppressed. Among 4091 nonswitcher “person-trials,” 74.2% (3035 nonswitcher “person-trials”; 1066 unique individuals) were retained. Of 2691 nonswitcher “person-trials,” 81.5% (2192 nonswitcher “person-trials”; 898 unique individuals) were virally suppressed (Figures 2 and 3). There were 234 (25.9%) switcher “person-trials” and 1400 (34.2%) nonswitcher “person-trials” with a missing viral load. Of the 905 switcher “person-trials,” 125 (13.8%) were lost to follow-up and 17 (1.9%) died by 24 months. Of 4091 nonswitcher “person-trials,” 958 (23.4%) were lost to follow-up and 98 (2.4%) died. After weighting, at 24 months we found a 1 percentage point (95% CI, −0.05 to 0.07) increase in retention and, among those retained, an 8 percentage point (95% CI, 0.02 to 0.14) increase in viral suppression (Figures 2 and 3).

When stratified by sex, we found a 17 percentage point (95% CI, 0.13 to 0.21) increase in 12-month retention among females and an 11 percentage point (95% CI, 0.03 to 0.19) increase in retention among males (Figures 2 and 3). However, by 24 months results were similar when stratified by sex (females: risk difference [RD], 0.01; 95% CI, −0.08 to 0.09; males: RD, 0.02; 95% CI, −0.07 to 0.11). Results were similar at 12 months for viral suppression when stratified by sex. At 24 months, we found a 3 percentage point (95% CI, 0.06 to 0.12) increase in viral suppression among females, whereas among males there was a 15 percentage point (95% CI, 0.07 to 0.23) increase comparing those who switched with those who did not.

While effect estimates were less precise when stratified by CD4 count, we found an 11 percentage point (95% CI, 0.02 to 0.19) increase in retention at 24 months compared with those who did not among those with a CD4 ≥350, whereas we saw no difference among those with a CD4 <350 (RD, 0.001; 95% CI, −0.07 to 0.07). Twelve- and 24-month viral suppression estimates were attenuated and less precise for both strata but suggested no difference between switchers and nonswitchers.

Sensitivity Analyses

Estimates from the first 3 sensitivity analyses were less precise but consistent for 12-month viral suppression, with risk differences ranging from 2% to 5% (Supplementary Tables 13 and 14). Risk differences for 24-month suppression were slightly attenuated and less precise, with risk differences being 3% (95% CI, −0.001 to 0.06) when viral suppression was defined as ≤1000 copies/mL and 5% (95% CI, −0.01 to 0.12) when we assumed individuals with a missing viral load were unsuppressed (Supplementary Tables 13 and 14). When we truncated weights at the 95th percentile, 12-month effect estimates for retention and suppression were consistent. At 24 months, the risk difference for retention increased to 8% (95% CI, 0.02 to 0.14), but viral suppression was consistent, with a risk difference of 9% (95% CI, 0.06 to 0.13) (Supplementary Table 15). When we truncated the unstabilized weights for censoring at the 99th percentile, viral suppression effect estimates at 12 and 24 months were the same (Supplementary Table 16). Because 0 time in this sequential trial approach is not aligned, leaving the potential for immortal person-time bias, we evaluated the potential impact by shortening the baseline window to 2- and 1-month windows [24, 25]. The results from these 2 analyses yielded similar results (Supplementary Table 17).

DISCUSSION

Our findings suggest that while switching to dolutegravir is associated with a moderate improvement in 12-month retention, by 24 months the differential effects are attenuated. However, there may be a small improvement in viral suppression comparing those who switch to dolutegravir with those who do not switch at 24 months. These findings contribute to existing literature supporting the use of dolutegravir as the preferred first-line regimen in a real-world clinic setting, with results suggesting that benefits of dolutegravir persist even among individuals with a history of adherence issues. We found no notable differences in retention or suppression when stratified by CD4 count at both time points. When stratified by sex, our results suggest that males who switch to dolutegravir may be slightly more likely to achieve viral suppression by 24 months compared with males who do not switch. This effect was not observed among females, likely because the rates of viral suppression were similar between females who did (89.5%) and did not switch (85.3%) at 24 months, whereas among males 84.3% who did switch achieved viral suppression compared with 76.5% who did not switch over the 9 trials. This large difference may explain why a greater effect was observed among males compared with females and could be why the risk difference of suppression increased at 24 compared with 12 months. If this finding is replicated in future research, it may be beneficial to evaluate whether barriers to switching differ by sex.

The findings from this study are in line with results from clinical trials like SALSA and TANGO that found that switching to a dolutegravir-based regimen is noninferior to the individual's preswitch regimen [9, 11, 12]. Our study population has 3 key differences from these 2 trials. SALSA and TANGO required individuals to be highly adherent and virologically suppressed, with no history of virologic failure [9, 11, 12]. Our study did not have these requirements, with over half of our study population having a history of adherence issues and/or virally unsuppressed at baseline, making the study population more reflective of real-world populations. As a result, given that we found an 8 percentage point improvement in 24-month viral suppression, it may be beneficial to switch individuals to a dolutegravir-based regimen even if they have a prior history of adherence issues/virologic failure. Observational studies like the VICONEL cohort in Lesotho have reported higher rates of suppression after switching, 90% or greater [26–30]. While our suppression rates were slightly smaller than those in other observational studies, this is likely due to differences in comparator groups and other dolutegravir-based regimens that did not consist of either a tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/lamivudine or tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/emtricitabine backbone [26–30].

The results of our study should be interpreted within the context of several limitations. First, our analyses took place during the early COVID-19 pandemic (eg, 2019–2022). During this time, South Africa implemented several lockdowns and travel restrictions that impacted access to the clinic, altered ART dispensing patterns, increased the number of self-transfers (eg, undocumented transfers), and increased morbidity and mortality across South Africa [18]. Prior work has established that these lockdowns and travel restrictions prevented individuals from accessing public HIV clinics [18]. Furthermore, during this time, resources were constrained at the clinic, and as a result sometimes visits and/or corresponding lab results were not recorded. Therefore, a combination of travel restrictions and resource constraints may explain why some individuals had missing viral loads. Second, having a viral load is conditional upon being retained in care. Our primary analysis for viral suppression was among those who were retained, which could bias the results as individuals with a viral load recorded may be more likely to be suppressed than those without a viral load recorded, as individuals who are retained are more likely to remain adherent to ART. However, our sensitivity analysis assuming individuals with a missing viral load were virally unsuppressed did not meaningfully change our conclusions.

Third, we were unable to align time 0. In our study, eligibility criteria were evaluated at the start of the baseline window, but individuals had up to 4 months from the start of that baseline window to switch. This misalignment of time 0 could introduce the potential for immortal person-time bias in 1 of 2 ways [24]. First, if an individual dies during this baseline window before switching, this would mean the individual did not have an opportunity to switch, and it is unknown if they would have switched had they not died. There were 4 individuals who did not switch and died during the baseline window. Therefore, we feel that this likely contributes a negligible source of bias given the small number of individuals to whom this scenario applies. The second way would be if an individual was lost to follow-up before switching. However, once an individual is lost to follow-up, they are no longer eligible to switch ART regimens as they are no longer retained in care; being able to switch is predicated upon attending a clinic appointment and seeing a provider. Thus, it is appropriate to classify individuals lost to follow-up within a baseline window as “nonswitchers” for that trial. To evaluate the potential impact of this bias, we conducted sensitivity analyses with baseline windows shortened to 2 months and 1 month. Effect estimates from these sensitivity analyses were comparable, suggesting that this may not be a large source of bias in our analysis. Despite this, there remains a risk of immortal person-time bias due to the use of baseline windows and would likely bias our estimates towards the null based on our sensitivity analyses [24]. A 4-month baseline window was also preferred over the shorter baseline windows to prevent positivity violations [31].

Fourth, the definitions of retention and viral suppression of the target trial and observational emulation were not aligned. The target trial defined retention and viral suppression at 12 and 24 months with a 3-month window on either side. However, the observational emulation utilized a 6-month window on either side. A 6-month window was chosen to account for the impact the COVID-19 pandemic had on visit scheduling. Fifth, for our observational emulation of 24-month outcomes, we restricted analyses to sequential trials taking place from 2019 to 2021 as the date of database closure did not allow for sequential trials in 2022 to have sufficient 24-month follow-up. Sixth, inverse probability of treatment and censoring weighting relies on untestable assumptions of consistency, exchangeability, and correct model specification [19].

Our findings suggest that while switching to a dolutegravir-based ART regimen was associated with an improvement in retention over the first 12 months, by 24 months there was no notable difference in retention among those who did vs did not switch. However, we observed a moderate but imprecise increase in viral suppression over 24 months among patients who switched to a dolutegravir-based ART regimen. Evaluating whether switching to a dolutegravir-based ART regimen leads to improvements in retention and suppression over the long term is important not only to improve individual quality of life but also to help South Africa achieve the UNAIDS 95-95-95 targets by 2030.
