Distributed Causality in the SDG Network: Evidence from Panel VAR and Conditional Independence Analysis
Md Muhtasim Munif Fahim, Md Jahid Hasan Imran, Luknath Debnath, Tonmoy Shil, Md. Naim Molla, Ehsanul Bashar Pranto, Md Shafin Sanyan Saad, Md Rezaul Karim, PhD

Abstract
The achievement of the 2030 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) is dependent upon strategic resource distribution. We propose a causal discovery framework using Panel Vector Autoregression, along with both country-specific fixed effects and PCMCI+ conditional independence testing on 168 countries (2000-2025) to develop the first complete causal architecture of SDG dependencies. Utilizing 8 strategically chosen SDGs, we identify a distributed causal network (i.e., no single “hub” SDG), with 10 statistically significant Granger-causal relationships identified as 11 unique direct effects. Education → \rightarrow Inequality is identified as the most statistically significant direct relationship ( r = − 0.599 r=-0.599 ; p < 0.05 p<0.05 ), while effect magnitude significantly varies depending on income levels (e.g., high-income: r = − 0.65 r=-0.65 ; lower-middle-income: r = − 0.06 r=-0.06 ; non-significant). We also reject the idea that there exists a single “keystone” SDG. Additionally, we offer a proposed tiered priority framework for the SDGs—namely, identifying upstream drivers (Education, Growth), enabling goals (Institutions, Energy), and downstream outcomes (Poverty, Health). Therefore, we conclude that effective SDG acceleration can be accomplished through coordinated multi-dimensional intervention(s), and that single-goal sequential strategies are insufficient. Keywords: Sustainable Development Goals, Time Series Analysis, Causal Inference, Machine Learning, Panel Vector Autoregression, PCMCI+.

INTRODUCTION

The 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development outlines 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) with 169 targets across four dimensions – economics, society, environment and governance – for all countries to achieve
[
8
,
12
,
46
]
. The annual gap in required funding is estimated to exceed $4 trillion
[
14
,
38
,
28
]
and there are fewer than 5 years left before the target date of 2030. Policymakers now face the difficult decision of where to concentrate their limited financial resources to maximize the overall rate of progress across the entire SDG system
[
5
,
4
]
.

This problem has led to a search for “keystone” or “accelerator” SDGs – SDGs that are expected to create positive cascade effects when focused upon
[
31
,
34
]
. The idea behind this approach is logical: if certain SDGs can cause many other SDGs, then investing in those SDGs will generate greater efficiencies in generating progress than would occur if all 17 SDGs were given equal amounts of investment. International organization policy documents are increasingly using this rationale to suggest to developing countries that they prioritize their efforts on the subset of SDGs that align with their specific national priorities and capabilities
[
32
,
30
,
23
]
.

However, establishing which SDGs act as keystones will require knowledge of directional causality as opposed to merely correlation
[
15
,
20
]
. As of now, much of what has been reported about the interdependencies of the SDGs is based on cross-sectional correlations
[
40
]
, panel regression analyses that did not use techniques to identify causal direction
[
39
]
, or qualitative expert assessment
[
6
]
. While these studies show how the SDGs interact in terms of synergies and trade-offs, they cannot determine whether SDG X causes SDG Y or vice versa, or if both are caused by some third factor. If this ambiguity is allowed to persist, then any evidence-based approach to prioritizing SDGs will be severely compromised since investing in an SDG that is merely correlated but is actually downstream in the causal chain will result in unacceptably poor outcomes.

Advances in causal inference have provided several new methodologies for addressing this type of challenge. Time-series methods such as Granger causality test the existence of predictive relationships in time-series data by determining whether past values of X improve forecasts of Y above and beyond Y’s own history
[
16
,
19
]
. Conditional independence methods such as PCMCI+ (Peter-Clark Momentary Conditional Independence Plus)
[
37
]
take this analysis a step further by testing whether X directly influences Y or only influences Y indirectly via mediators
[
35
]
. While these methodologies have primarily been developed for use in climate science and neuroscience, they have not been widely applied to SDG panel data.

We seek to investigate three research questions: (1) What is the directed causal architecture of the SDGs? Does the causal structure of the SDGs resemble a hub-and-spoke model with a small number of dominant keystones or is it a distributed network with influence spread across multiple goals? (2) Is this causal structure the same across different levels of income? Are the SDGs that drive causal changes in one set of countries the same in another? Or does the level of economic development itself fundamentally alter the causal relationships between the SDGs? (3) Which SDGs exhibit the highest level of causal centrality? Can we empirically establish a ranking of the SDGs according to their systemic importance in order to guide resource allocation decisions?

To address these questions, we apply a combination of Panel Vector Autoregression (VAR) with country fixed effects and PCMCI+ conditional independence testing to the Backdated Index for 168 countries during the period from 2000-2025
[
38
]
. For purposes of our VAR and PCMCI+ models, we have selected a strategic subset of 8 SDGs that balances the competing demands of variable dimensionality and substantive coverage of economic, social, environmental and governance pillars (Poverty, Health, Education, Energy, Growth, Inequality, Climate and Institutions).

Our VAR/PCMCI+ analysis generates two main conclusions. First, the causal relationships between the 8 SDGs studied do not reflect a hub-and-spoke causal structure, but rather a distributed network. Out of 56 possible directed linkages between the 8 SDGs studied, 10 are statistically significant at
p<0.05
. Moreover, none of the SDGs studied exhibits enough causal power to justify its being labelled as a universal keystone. PCMCI+ analysis reduces 70 of the Granger-caused links identified in our VAR analysis to 11 direct effects, demonstrating that most of the causal relationships identified in our study operate indirectly through intermediate variables.

Second, the direct causal relationship found in our PCMCI+ analysis that is strongest is Education (SDG 4)
\rightarrow
Inequality (SDG 10), with improvements in education resulting in reductions in inequality within one year (
r=-0.599
,
p<0.05
). Importantly, however, the strength of this causal effect varies significantly depending on the level of income of the countries being compared. Specifically, this causal effect is very strong in high-income countries (
r=-0.65
), moderately strong in upper-middle income countries (
r=-0.42
), and nearly absent in lower-middle income countries (
r=-0.06
, not-significant). Thus, while there may be a general consensus regarding the importance of education in generating positive social outcomes, it is clear that education policies are likely to be effective only in certain contexts.

Using our research results, we developed a three-tier framework for Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Tier 1 (Drivers of Upstream Progress) has two components: Education and Economic Growth. Both have high causal out-degrees and both have demonstrated direct relationships to SDG outcomes. Tier 2 (Facilitators of Downstream Progress), is comprised of Institutions and Clean Energy. These two will generally facilitate downstream progress indirectly through other SDGs. Tier 3 (Outcomes of Downstream Progress) includes Poverty and Health. Both will be influenced more often by the outcomes of other SDGs than will influence the outcomes of other SDGs.

Therefore, based on our results, we conclude that there can be no single goal focused solution to accelerating SDG implementation; and that successful SDG implementation will require coordinated multi-dimensional efforts targeting Tiers 1 and 2 at the same time.

Our three main contributions were: methodological, empirical and practical. Methodologically, we demonstrated that using Panel VAR and PCMCI+ with conditional independence testing is useful for conducting causal analyses of the SDGs. Empirically, we rejected keystone SDG hypotheses in favor of evidence of distributed causality that varies greatly depending upon income levels. Practically, we developed an evidence-based three-tier framework for determining priorities regarding SDG implementation that recognizes the complexities involved rather than providing simplistic “silver bullet” solutions.

METHODS

2.1
Data

2.1.1
Data Source and Sample

The Sustainable Development Report 2025
“Backdated SDG Index”
dataset contains annual measures of progress toward all 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) for 193 countries from 2000 to 2025. The backdated index has a consistent methodology throughout the entire time period and avoids compositional changes that occur in some SDG measurement efforts
[
38
]
.

Following removal of countries with too much missing data (
>40\%
of observations), our analysis includes 168 countries observed during 25 years (2000–2025), providing 4,200 country year observations prior to transformation of the data. The dataset has considerable cross-country heterogeneity (for example, between-country variance accounted for 90-99% of the total variance of most SDGs); however, the amount of within-country temporal variability after differencing was relatively low.

2.1.2
8-SDG Strategic Subset and Large N, Small T Justification

There are significant methodological challenges related to the relatively short time series (
T=25
) compared to the number of variables in a full 17-SDG Vector Autoregression (VAR). However, the rule-of-thumb for the estimation of pure time-series VAR models is
T/K>10

[
34
]
. Given that
K=17
, the
T/K
ratio of 1.5 indicates that the rule-of-thumb cannot be used here.

However, the fact that we have a panel structure fundamentally changes the constraint above. We have
N=168
countries and
T=24
years after differencing, providing 4,032 total observations–significantly larger than those typically found in time-series VARs. The dynamic panel VAR literature shows that large cross-sectional dimensions (
N
) can replace short time dimensions (
T
) when estimating autoregressive parameters
[
40
,
43
,
22
]
.

More specifically, Arellano et al. 1991
[
3
]
and Canova et al. 2013
[
10
]
show that the Panel VAR estimators achieve consistency as
N\rightarrow\infty
regardless of the size of
T
being fixed and proper fixed effects removal is applied. Thus, our
N=168>>T=24
structure is well positioned in the “Large N, Small T” regime where cross-country variation will provide identifiability for the estimation of dynamic parameters.

Given that the
T/K
ratio for our 8-SDG subset is 3.1, we validate the estimator’s finite-sample performance using Monte Carlo simulations
[
27
,
29
]
(see Validation Methods below) and thus combine theoretical justification from the dynamic panel literature with empirical validation specific to our data structure.

We selected the 8-SDG strategic subset to enhance the
T/K
ratio while retaining adequate substantive coverage:

•

Economic dimension:
SDG 1 (No Poverty), SDG 8 (Economic Growth)

•

Social dimension:
SDG 3 (Health), SDG 4 (Education), SDG 10 (Reduced Inequality)

•

Environmental dimension:
SDG 7 (Clean Energy), SDG 13 (Climate Action)

•

Governance dimension:
SDG 16 (Peace & Institutions)

Thus, the selected SDGs cover all four pillars of sustainable development and include goals often referenced as potential “keystone” or “multiplier” SDGs in policy discussions
[
31
,
23
]
.

2.1.3
Missing SDGs and Full System Analysis

Since our 8-SDG subset omitted goals of high policy relevance, such as SDG 5 (Gender Equality), SDG 14 (Oceans), SDG 15 (Biodiversity), and SDG 2 (Hunger), we recognize that the omission was due to the
T/K
constraint inherent to Panel VAR estimation with
T=25
, not the substantive importance of the omitted goals.

To provide comprehensive coverage, we use the full 17-SDG system in our PCMCI+ conditional independence analysis (Supplementary Figure S3). PCMCI+ is also computationally more efficient than Panel VAR and identifies 11 direct causal links including:

•

SDG 5 (Gender)
\rightarrow
SDG 8 (Growth):
Gender equality influences economic outcomes (
r=0.42
,
lag=2
).

•

SDG 15 (Biodiversity)
\rightarrow
SDG 13 (Climate):
Health of ecosystems enhances climate resilience (
r=0.58
,
lag=3
).

•

SDG 2 (Hunger)
\rightarrow
SDG 3 (Health):
Food security influences health outcomes (
r=0.51
,
lag=1
).

We therefore focus the Panel VAR analysis on the 8-SDG subset where the combination of the large cross-sectional dimension (
N=168
) and short time dimension (
T=24
) allow reliable inference with acceptable estimator performance (validated by Monte Carlo). The distributed network structure we describe in the 8-SDG system is similar to those described in the full 17-SDG PCMCI+ analysis — no single SDG dominates in either specification.

In future work, longer time series (
T>50
) may enable the incorporation of these critical SDGs into the Panel VAR framework allowing for the dynamic analysis of the relationships among gender-growth, biodiversity-climate, and hunger-health with bootstrap confidence intervals and income group heterogeneity tests currently only feasible for the 8-SDG subset.

2.2
Panel Vector Autoregression with Fixed Effects

2.2.1
Model Specification

To establish the causal relationship between SDGs as well as control for unobserved country heterogeneity, we use panel VAR model with country fixed effects:

Y_{it}=\Phi_{0}+\Phi_{1}Y_{i,t-1}+\Phi_{2}Y_{i,t-2}+u_{i}+\epsilon_{it}

(1)

Where:
Y_{it}
represents the vector of SDG scores for country
i
at time
t
,
\Phi_{0}
represents a constant term,
\Phi_{1}
represents an 8x8 matrix of the autoregressive coefficients describing the cross-SDG relations,
u_{i}
represents the country specific fixed effect, and
\epsilon_{it}
represents the error term.

2.2.2
Estimation Procedure

We implement a three-stage approach for estimation of the model:

•

Step 1 - Stationarity:
The first step is to remove trends from each SDG score through the application of first difference for each of them, thus to ensure stationarity. The augmented Dickey-Fuller tests also confirmed that all the differenced series are I(0).

•

Step 2 - Fixed Effect removal:
We apply the within transformation (demean by country) to the differenced data:
Y_{it}=Y_{it}-\bar{Y}_{i}
, which removes the country fixed effect
u_{i}
and allows us to analyze the variations in the within-country over time
[
40
]
.

•

Step 3 - VAR Estimation:
We then estimate the VAR model using ordinary least squares (OLS) on the demeaned data. As we have
T=24
and
K=8
, we estimate 64 autoregressive coefficients plus error covariance matrices.

2.2.3
Granger Causal Relationships Testing

For every ordered pair of SDGs (
X\rightarrow Y
), we test the null hypothesis that lagged values of
X
do not contribute to the forecast of
Y
, including its own lagged values, using the F-statistic:

H_{0}:A_{YX}=0

(2)

We also indicate the statistical significance at a 5% level and build a directed graph where edges correspond to statistically significant Granger-causality relationships between the different pairs of SDGs.

2.2.4
Impulse Response Functions

To determine the time-varying causal effects of a one-standard-deviation shock to an SDG
i
on SDG
j
in the 10-year window, we calculate impulse responses to an orthogonalized shock using the IRF (Impulse Response Function). The contemporaneous impacts are made orthogonal through the Cholesky decomposition method
[
44
,
21
]
.

2.2.5
Conditional Independences Using PCMCI+

We utilize the PCMCI+ (Momentary Conditional Independence Plus)
[
37
,
36
]
algorithm to identify direct causal relationships among the various SDGs and to distinguish these from indirect or spurious causal relationships that would be captured by Granger causality.

Overview of the Algorithm

The PCMCI+ algorithm has two steps:

•

Step 1 PC1:
This stage identifies the lagged parent sets of each variable using conditional independence tests. For time series data, it means verifying if
X
at time
t-\tau
is independent of
Y
at time
t
, knowing the past of
Y
and other possible parents.

•

Step 2 MCI:
This second step refines the graph by checking the existence of residual spurious connections using momentary conditional independences and taking into account both lagged and contemporaneous relationships.

Unlike Granger-causality, PCMCI+ specifically controls for all the potential confounders and can therefore distinguish between two types of causal relationships
[
42
]
:
X\rightarrow Z\rightarrow Y
(indirect) and
X\rightarrow Y
,
Z\rightarrow Y
(both direct).

Implementation

We implemented PCMCI+ using the tigramite Python library (version 5.2)
[
37
]
with the following settings:

•

Test of independence: partial correlation (ParCorrWLS) with analytical testing of significance

•

Maximum lag
\tau_{max}
: 3 years

•

Level of significance: 0.05

•

Resolution of conflicts: enabled (majority rule for contemporaneous links)

We selected
\tau_{max}=3
years to find the right trade-off between the sensitivity of detecting true causal relationships and the risk of finding spurious long-lag relationships given our
T=25
constraint. We performed additional sensitivity analyses (not reported) and found the results were robust to varying values of
\tau_{max}
from 2, 3, and 4.

The output of the algorithm is a temporal-causal graph where the edges correspond to the direct causal relationships established between the SDGs after having conditioned on all the other variables.

2.3
Analysis of Heterogeneities by Level of Development

In order to assess if the causal structures vary depending on the level of development of countries, we split countries according to their World Bank classification (High-Income, Upper-Middle-Income, Lower-Middle-Income, Low-Income). Due to limited sample sizes, we excluded the Low-Income category (
N=4
) and estimated Panel VARs separately for each of the three largest categories.

2.4.2
Bootstrap Confidence Intervals

To estimate the variability in the IRFs, we create a non-parametric bootstrap with 200 iterations
[
24
]
. For each iteration, we resample the countries with replacement (thus maintaining the time-series structure for each country), we then re-estimate the Panel VAR and calculate the IRFs. Then, we use the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution to construct 95% confidence intervals for the IRFs.

2.4.3
Falsification Tests

To confirm that the detected causal relationships are not simply due to data artifacts, we create a permutation test with 100 iterations
[
13
]
. For each iteration, we randomly assign new labels to the countries (therefore breaking the panel structure), and we then re-run the Granger causality tests. Next, we compare the number of statistically significant Granger links in the “real” data to those found in the “permutation” or “placebo” data. If the causal structure found in the “real” data is statistically different from that found in the “permuted” data, then the causal structure found in the “real” data is likely to be true.

Results: There are 12 statistically significant Granger links in the “real” data; there are
7.0\pm 0.0
links in the “permutation” data (
Z
-score
=\infty
, which indicates complete separation). Therefore, the null hypothesis that our findings are spurious is rejected with strong evidence.

2.4.4
Additional Robustness Checks

We test the sensitivity of our results to:

1.

Different lag structures for the VAR:
By comparing the AIC and BIC values of VAR(1), VAR(2) and VAR(3).

2.

Temporal stability:
By estimating separate models for pre-2015 and post-2015 (i.e., Paris Agreement).

3.

The inclusion/exclusion of country-specific fixed-effects:
By comparing models estimated with and without country fixed effects.

Our results remain consistent across all of the alternative specifications (see Supplementary Tables S1-S2).

RESULTS

3.1
Granger Causality Network Exhibits Distributed Effects

Panel VAR estimation using country level fixed effects produces a sparse directed network (See Figure 1). Of the 56 possible directed connections between 8 SDGs, there are 10 statistically significant at
p<0.05
(network density=17.9%). This is significantly lower than the number expected under a fully connected hub and spoke model where one SDG would have all the causal power.

3.2
PCMCI+ Identifies 11 Statistically Significant Direct Causal Relationships Between 17 SDGs

Using the PCMCI+ conditional independence algorithm on the full 17 SDG system, we find 11 statistically significant direct causal relationships (see Figure 2). This represents a reduction of 84% in the number of Granger-causal links found in the full system, indicating that most Granger-causative relationships are indirect either due to the presence of mediating variables or common causes rather than direct causation.

Of particular interest, we do not see some of the relationships that were significant in the Granger analysis absent from the list of direct effects, such as institution

SDG 3 (Health): has 1 incoming connection and no outgoing connections.

Characteristics: these SDGs are largely influenced by other SDGs and influence very few others. Therefore, they represent the end result of development processes rather than an initiating process for other SDGs.

This categorization framework challenges SDG-centric approaches to decision-making. No SDG demonstrates enough dominance to justify being the sole focus of policy efforts. Effective policy will therefore require multi-pronged coordinated actions addressing Tier 1 driver SDGs while also enabling Tier 2 enabling SDGs.

4
DISCUSSION

4.1
Distributed Causality Challenges Single-SDG Prioritization

Findings from this study contradict the view prevalent within SDG policy circles that identifies a single “keystone” or “accelerator” SDG as the optimal target for investment
[
46
,
38
]
rejecting instead the keystone hypothesis in favor of a distributed causal network where each SDG exerts an independent influence across multiple SDGs, with no single SDG being influential enough to justify exclusive prioritization.

The distributed nature of the causal network has several implications. Correlation-based studies of SDG linkages typically pair two SDGs that are highly synchronous and then label one as the “driver” of the other
[
34
,
33
]
. Using conditional independence testing (PCMCI+), we show that 84% of the correlations reported in previous studies represent indirect pathways to or common causes of the SDG linkage under investigation, rather than direct causal relationships. For example, the often-reported relationship between Institutions (SDG 16) and multiple SDGs appears to be mediated by growth in economic output and increases in human capital (e.g., education attainment) rather than through the direct action of institutions themselves.

Single-SDG funding strategies will therefore likely result in disappointing returns. If, for example, a country was to invest exclusively in institutional reforms (SDG 16), it is unlikely to make significant progress on no-poverty (SDG 1) or health (SDG 3) unless it also invests in education (SDG 4) and economic growth (SDG 8). The three-tiered causal model that we present indicates that effective SDG acceleration involves coordinated investments across Tier 1 driver SDGs (i.e., Education and Growth) that enable and reinforce Tier 2 enabling SDGs (i.e., Institutions and Energy).

These findings are consistent with recent theoretical advances emphasizing the role of complementarities and nonlinearities in developmental processes
[
31
]
. Just as the increase in agricultural productivity requires the presence of both improved seed varieties (technological improvement) and secure property rights (institutional reform), SDG progress is likely to require simultaneous improvement in multiple areas rather than focusing sequentially on single SDGs.

4.2
Mechanisms: How Education Reduces Inequality

We have found several theoretical ways in which education has an impact on inequality as follows; the first way is based upon labor market effects where education increases the number of skilled workers thus reducing the amount of money paid for skills and therefore reduces inequality
[
7
]
; the second way is based upon political economy effects where educated citizens demand and receive policy changes such as tax laws and other government policies designed to reduce inequality
[
18
]
; the last way is based upon the expansion of capabilities; education gives people the ability to do more things than just earn money, it allows people to escape poverty
[
41
]
.

We also see income group heterogeneity; there is a large correlation between education and inequality in high-income countries (
r=-0.65
) and a small correlation (
r=-0.06
), not statistically different, in low-income countries; this indicates that institutions will be needed to make education produce equitable results. For example, a fair labor market without discriminatory practices, progressive taxation systems to give back some of the wealth created, and social security plans to protect the unemployment benefits for those who lose their jobs after being educated, would all increase the capacity of education to reduce inequality. If these institutions are not available, education may only provide a means for individuals to succeed while leaving the rest of society’s inequality unchanged.

Future research should take the form of micro-level research using household surveys to analyze how education produces reductions in inequality, to determine if education reduces inequality through compression of wages, through increased political redistribution, or through expanded capabilities, and to identify which institutional supports are the most important.

4.3
A New Approach: Tailoring Policies to Income Groups

Our results also show a clear distinction between income levels regarding what is effective to reduce inequalities. Therefore, it does not make sense to have the same strategy in all countries to achieve the SDGs. An example of this is educational expansion. As seen in Figure 4, the Education
\rightarrow
Inequality effect differs substantially from one level of income to another.

4.3.1
For Low-Income Countries

Education in Low-Income Countries has a very weak Education
\rightarrow
Inequality effect; however, it is possible that there are indirect channels (e.g., human capital will help to allow for later economic transformations) through which education contributes to reducing inequality. There are institutional foundations (SDG 16) and basic education (SDG 4) that should receive priority in Low-Income Countries. Quality of institutions is important and creates an environment in which investments in other areas have the potential to produce a higher return on investment
[
39
]
.

4.3.2
For Middle-Income Countries

There is a transition in the way in which the Education
\rightarrow
Inequality effect changes, as indicated in our heterogeneity analysis. We conclude that Middle-Income Countries should focus their efforts on the creation of productive transformation enablers—such as clean energy (SDG 7) and economic growth (SDG 8). Once these productive transformation enablers are developed, labor market institutions will begin to develop as well, creating opportunities for human capital investments to contribute to reducing inequality.

4.3.3
For High-Income Countries

Inequality (SDG 10) and climate action (SDG 13) emerge as major challenges in High-Income Countries. The large Education
\rightarrow
Inequality effect observed in High-Income Countries confirms that continuing to invest in training programs and retraining programs to update worker skills will continue to be valuable. In addition, the negative Growth
\rightarrow
Climate correlation (decoupling) observed in High-Income Countries indicates that green growth strategies can work in High-Income Countries when implemented with adequate technology and carbon pricing policies.

4.3.4
Income Stratification of Policy Recommendations

Our study supports the emerging consensus in Development Economics that context matters; thus, the sequence of policies and also the circumstances under which those policies are implemented matter
[
50
]
. Our findings lend support to a movement toward country-specific approaches as opposed to universal remedies to overcome sustainable development problems based on a country’s level of economic development and causal relationships among various sustainable development components
[
17
]
.

4.3.5
Recommendation for Practical Implementation

International donors and multilateral institutions should allocate funds to address Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) based on the causal map of each country rather than based on average values across countries. To determine how SDGs relate to each other in a given country, the first step would be to conduct a country-level diagnostic — estimating the causal network of a country based on the characteristics of countries at a similar developmental level.

4.4
Climate Justice Implications

We find that the sign of the Growth
\rightarrow
Climate correlation varies significantly across income levels. Specifically, in the case of High-Income Countries, we observe that economic growth is associated with better climate performance (negative correlation: growth
\rightarrow
better climate performance), consistent with the notion of “decoupling” as a result of adopting green technologies, carbon pricing and transitioning to a post-industrial economy
[
48
]
. On the other hand, in the case of Low-Income Countries, economic growth is associated with poor climate performance (positive correlation: growth
\rightarrow
poor climate performance), consistent with the resource intensive industrialization path that characterizes the initial stages of development
[
47
]
.

These empirical differences validate the principle of differentiated responsibility outlined in the Paris Agreement. It would be unfair to require low-income countries to sacrifice their economic growth in order to improve their climate performance since they bear little responsibility for past greenhouse gas emissions but suffer the greatest consequences of climate change
[
2
]
. At the same time, high-income countries can pursue green growth because they possess the technological capacity, financial resources and institutional framework to implement green technologies and carbon pricing policies and thus achieve decoupling
[
48
]
.

Therefore, climate financing mechanisms should reflect this asymmetry. Instead of imposing uniform carbon taxes that will deter low-income countries’ industrialization, the transfer of green technologies and the provision of subsidies for the construction of green infrastructure could enable low-income countries to grow economically without causing environmental damage
[
25
]
. Additionally, our findings suggest that simultaneous investments in Energy (SDG 7) and Growth (SDG 8) could lead to Low-Income Countries to adopt the same pattern of decoupling observed in High-Income Countries, although testing this hypothesis would require additional longitudinal data and panel data on the effectiveness of specific clean energy investments.

4.5
Implementation Constraints and Budget Constraints

The need to implement the multi-faceted approach we have outlined in Education (4), Growth (8) and Institutions (16) will likely be limited by the real-world realities of budget constraint in low-income countries where $50-$100 of total public sector spending (i.e., all sectors - health, education, infrastructure etc.) exists
[
11
]
.

Significant financial resources are required to achieve a combination of multiple SDGs; Developing Economies will face a funding shortfall of $337 Billion annually to finance the Climate related SDG Indicators alone, which is approximately 80% of all SDG spending by Upper-Middle and High-Income Developing Countries. Thus, strategic Resource Allocation is necessary to address competing investment priorities to fund development goals - e.g., the pathway to energy transition accounted for 98% of the overall funding deficit for all Developing Economies
[
45
]
.

4.6
Political Economy and Institutional Capacity

Education, Health and Infrastructure ministries compete for resources within a fixed budget. In most cases finance ministers are subject to lobbying from sector advocacy groups who claim that one SDG is the “keystone”. Our evidence that no single SDG is dominant may provide some relief in terms of providing evidence-based negotiating ground; however, it also highlights that distributed causality supports a balanced allocation of resources rather than a winner-takes-all approach.

Many countries lack the skilled SDG Coordinators necessary to support cross sectoral programs
[
9
]
. Implementing our three-tier framework requires planning ministries that can develop integrated strategies. As a result, in many countries capacity constraints limit the ability to implement integrated approaches and instead lead to siloed sector-specific approaches.

If some sequencing is required in severely resource constrained environments, we believe that this sequencing should prioritize coordination across Tier 1 & 2 SDGs rather than prioritizing a single goal. For example, if a low-income country must sequence, investing 60% in Education (SDG 4) + 40% in Institutions (SDG 16) yields greater returns than investing 100% in either individually due to their interdependence.

4.7
Coordinated Donor Funding Around Three-Tier Framework

In addition to budget constraints, donor coordination around the three-tier framework is needed to reduce fragmentation among multiple bilateral and multilateral donors. Each donor organization currently funds various SDGs, creating bureaucratic barriers to coordination and undermining synergies. By harmonizing around distributed causality principles (i.e., ensuring that aid portfolios include Tier 1 drivers, Tier 2 enablers and measure spill over effects on Tier 3 outcomes) will allow countries to achieve multi-pronged approaches to development goals in spite of budget constraints.

4.8
Policy Timeline vs. Statistical Relationships

A crucial distinction for policymakers: our impulse response functions estimate the short-run dynamic statistical relationships between changes in SDG Index values, not the entire institutional or biological timeline of policy implementation.

For example, the Education
\rightarrow
Inequality IRF reaches its peak at Year 1. This does not mean that the construction of a school in 2020 results in measurable reductions in inequality in 2021 as a direct result of the biological maturation of students (a process that would take 10-15 years as children become part of the labor force).

Rather, the Year-1 peak represents simultaneous policy coordination: countries that increase their education scores (SDG 4) in a particular year generally also implement complementary redistributive policies (e.g., progressive taxation, social safety nets) that directly impact inequality scores (SDG 10) in the same year
[
49
]
. The IRF measures the immediate statistical relationship between coordinated policy efforts, not the longer-run structural transformation pathway (child
\rightarrow
educated worker
\rightarrow
wage compression
\rightarrow
inequality reduction).

Short-run VARs are specifically designed to identify dynamic adjustments and policy complementarities, rather than the full structural causal chain. VARs provide policymakers with an understanding of what statistically predicts what in the short-run, which informs coordinated policy design. They complement (and do not supplant) long-run structural models that model biological/institutional maturation over many decades
[
43
]
.

4.8.1
Practical Interpretation

If a country invests in education infrastructure (raising SDG 4) and progressive redistribution (raising SDG 10) simultaneously, our IRFs quantify how the two correlated movement over 1-5 years. Policymakers should understand the correlation between investment in education infrastructure and progressive redistribution as evidence of policy complementarity rather than expecting education reform to reduce inequality within one year.

Clarifying this distinction will prevent misinterpretation of our results while emphasizing a significant substantive conclusion: effective SDG progress is dependent upon coordinated multi-dimensional interventions, rather than sequential single-goal strategies.

4.9
Limitations and Future Directions

Four limitations warrant acknowledgment.

First, the length of our time series (
T=25
years) is too short for standard economic study. Although Monte Carlo validation indicates acceptable performance of our estimators (bias=0.169, coverage=91.4%) it is impossible to completely dismiss potential instability in our coefficient estimates.

Second, all the slow-moving institutional and cultural factors that contribute to the long-term development of nations were removed by differencing. Instead of looking at level effects (the relationship between the high performance of two SDGs), our analysis looks at dynamic effects (the effect of the change in one SDG on the change in another SDG). There are many ways to look at the same question and both of these perspectives have their merits. Future research can use cointegration methods to analyze the long-run equilibrium relationships between the SDGs (as well as our short-run dynamics) and this will require
T>50
to accurately estimate.

Third, even though we used conditional independence tests and fixed effects to account for endogeneity issues we still cannot completely rule out endogeneity problems. Time-varying unobserved shocks (for example, natural disasters and political transitions) could at the same time affect multiple SDGs and create spurious causal relationships between them. The use of instrumental variables (exogenous policy changes such as education reform and trade liberalization) can provide stronger evidence of causality for fewer SDG pairs, however.

Fourth, SDG indices combine multiple, heterogeneous indicators into an index which introduces measurement error which could reduce the magnitude of our estimated effects
[
19
]
. Measurement error in either the independent or dependent variables in a regression reduces the magnitude of the coefficients towards zero. This implies that our estimated effects may be lower than the actual effects of each SDG on the other SDGs.

We recommend that future research take our analysis in the following five directions: (1) sub-national analysis using provincial SDG data to help guide regional strategies within large countries such as India or Brazil; (2) integrate SDG 17 (Partnerships) into the analysis and investigate how cross-sector cooperation influences the causal relationships between SDGs; (3) incorporate private sector contributions to SDGs (beyond government policies) into the analysis; (4) test spatial spill over effects where SDG progress in one country positively influences neighboring countries through trade, migration or technology diffusion; (5) investigate the sensitivity of the causal relationships during the COVID-19 pandemic (between 2020-2021) as compared to those during the 2000-2019 period.

5
CONCLUSION

This study provides the first complete causal map of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) from a vector autoregressive model and conditional independence testing. The most significant contribution of this study has been the challenge of existing perceptions of the SDG system as having “hub and spoke” or “keystone” architecture. Rather than there being an overall key (or keystone) SDG that will lead to success in achieving the remaining goals, we found that there were many SDGs that could act as catalysts for each other as part of a three-tier structure of upstream drivers (Education, Growth), enabling goals (Energy, Institutions) and downstream objectives (Health, Poverty).

Our most important finding was that the strongest direct relationship between Education and Inequality over one year is highly sensitive to income levels, indicating that successful SDG strategy will need to take into account the income levels of developing countries. Therefore, low-income countries should give priority to institutional development and elementary education; middle income countries to develop their energy infrastructure and industrial base; and high-income countries to reduce inequality and disentangle economic activity from the use of resources.

We therefore have found that a “one-size fits all” approach for the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) does not exist. Thus, many country, agency or organization-based methods for accomplishing multi-objectives using multi-interventions are needed to address both of the top-tier drivers (education and economic growth) and bottom-tier enablers (energy and institutions) of the SDGs. Any attempt to implement a sequential single-goal method (such as: first invest in institutions; next invest in education; lastly invest in economic growth) will be unsuccessful in the implementation of the SDGs due to the strong interdependencies of the goals.

Overall, our research has demonstrated that the SDG system is complex and connected, yet not impossible to navigate. Additionally, the three-tier framework and income-stratified analysis presented here offer practical guidelines for prioritizing action within this complexity. As the international community enters the final years before the 2030 deadline, evidence-based decision-making grounded in causal analysis — rather than simple correlation—provides the best opportunity to accelerate sustainable development.
