Nourishing the mind: how the EAT-Lancet reference diet (ELD) and MIND diet impact stress, anxiety, and depression
Authors: Farzam Kamrani, Amirhossein Ataei Kachouei, Seyyed Reza Sobhani, Maryam Khosravi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies have suggested a link between diet and mental health. However, there is a lack of evidence regarding the association between emerging diets such as the EAT-Lancet reference diet (ELD) and the Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay (MIND) diet, and mental health in different societies. This study aimed to determine the association between adherence to ELD and MIND diets and the risk of depression, anxiety, and stress.MethodsThis research involved 4579 participants from the PERSIAN Organizational Cohort Study in Mashhad (POCM). To assess dietary intake, a comprehensive 118-item semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) was employed. The Planetary Health Diet Index (PHDI) was used to assess adherence to the ELD. Mental health status was evaluated using the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale-21 items (DASS-21) questionnaire. Binary logistic regression was utilized to examine the relationship between these scores and mental health indicators.ResultsIn the adjusted model, the highest quartile of PHDI showed a 35% reduced risk of depression compared to those in the lowest quartile (OR: 0.653, 95% CI: 0.483–0.883; P = 0.008). However, compared to the reference quartile, participants in the highest quartile of MIND diet exhibited significantly lower risks of depression (OR: 0.611, 95% CI: 0.447–0.836; P = 0.005), anxiety (OR: 0.559, 95% CI: 0.418–0.746; P < 0.001), and stress (OR: 0.629, 95% CI: 0.419–0.944; P = 0.008).ConclusionsThe ELD and MIND diet were both associated with reduced odds of depression. Additionally, MIND diet was associated with decreased likelihood of anxiety and stress. However, no connection was observed between ELD and anxiety or stress. Introduction

Although efforts have been made for the prevention and treatment of mental health disorders, yet they are the leading cause of disability and among the major public health issues globally [1, 2]. Among mental health disorders, depression, anxiety, and stress are highly prevalent by affecting approximately one-third of individuals during the life-span [3]. These conditions result from a combination of biological, environmental, psychological, and in particular lifestyle factors such as dietary pattern [4, 5].

Dietary patterns have been extensively studied as a lifestyle risk factor for mental health disorders [6, 7]. Studies suggest that dietary patterns rich in fruit, vegetables, fish, and whole grains may be associated with a reduced risk of mental health disorders [7, 8]. The Mediterranean and Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diets are two examples of such dietary patterns that have shown beneficial effects on mental health [9, 10]. Limited research exists on the connection between emerging diets like the EAT-Lancet reference diet (ELD) and mental health [11]. Additionally, studies on the relationship between the Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay (MIND) diet and mental health have produced inconclusive results, indicating a need for further investigation [12–14].

Currently, as environmental problems are one of the main challenges faced by humans, one of the concerns are to provide individuals with a healthy but environmentally friendly diet. As an answer to this concern, in 2019, the ELD was proposed by the EAT-Lancet Commission on Healthy Diets from Sustainable Food Systems as a dietary pattern that simultaneously improve human and planetary health [15]. The ELD is designed as a 2500 kcal/day diet that emphasizes a rich variety of fruits and vegetables, whole grains, unsaturated oils, nuts, and legumes. It promotes moderate consumption of seafood, eggs, poultry, and dairy products and limits the intake of added fats, sugar, and red meats [15]. However, it raises the question of whether environmentally protective diets can effectively balance the needs of the environment with those of physical and mental health.

The MIND diet is another plant-based dietary pattern developed to preserve brain health [16]. It promotes ten brain-protective foods, including green leafy vegetables, other vegetables, berries, nuts, beans, whole grains, fish, poultry, olive oil, and wine, while restricting cheese, red meat and its products, fast-fried foods, pastries, sweets, butter, and margarine [16].

Currently, no studies have investigated the association between the ELD and depression, anxiety, and stress. However, several studies have explored the association between the MIND diet and mental health with inconsistent results [13, 17, 18]. For example, Barkhordari et al. have reported a significant inverse association between adherence to the MIND diet and the risk of depression and anxiety, but not for stress [17]. However, Fresán et al. and Salari-Moghaddam et al. have found no significant association between the MIND diet and depression and anxiety [13, 18].

Therefore, as the studies on the association between the MIND diet and the risk of mental health disorders were not conclusive and no studies have examined the relationship between the ELD and these mental health disorders, we aimed to explore the association between the ELD and MIND diet and depression, anxiety, and stress.

Methods

Study design and participants:

This cross-sectional study involved 4579 participants and it was conducted within the framework of the PERSIAN Organizational Cohort Study in Mashhad (POCM). This study enrolled Government organizations employees and primarily aimed to investigate participant’s lifestyles and non-communicable disease risk factors through regular follow-ups. The study protocol has been described in detail elsewhere [19]. In brief, in this study healthy Iranian adults aged 30–70 were recruited, and their baseline characteristics was collected through face-to-face interviews and physical examinations. Sampling commenced in October 2017, and participants are continuously monitored and followed up thereafter. This study utilizes baseline data from the POCM study, which was collected during Phase I (2017 to 2020).

Height and weight were measured based on standard protocols [19]. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight divided by height squared. Before participating, the individuals were informed about the study's objectives and provided written consent.

Human ethics and consent to participate declarations

The study protocol was approved by the Mashhad university of medical sciences ethical committee (IR.MUMS.MEDICAL.REC.1402.414) and was conducted according to the principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in the study. For any participants who were minors or otherwise unable to provide informed consent, written consent was obtained from their parent or legal guardian.

Dietary assessment

Trained nutritionists interviewed participants and guide them to complete a validated 118-item semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) [20] based on their habitual diet during previous year. Each participant was given two questions for each food item: (1) how often they ate that food in the preceding year (how many times per month, week, or day), and (2) how much of that food they typically ate at each sitting (portion size based on the standard serving sizes commonly consumed by Iranians). All reported intakes were converted to g/day using household portion sizes of consumed foods. Food intakes were analyzed using USDA's food database The energy and nutrient intake were determined using Nutritionist IV software (version 7.0; N-Squared Computing, Salem, OR, USA). Ultimately, this study included data from 4579 participants who had available dietary assessment. However, 14 participants were excluded due to missing data for the MIND diet calculation.

PHDI Calculation

The PHDI assesses how well people follow the recommendations of the ELD [15]. It consists of 16 items, categorized into four groups: (1) adequacy components (nuts and peanuts, legumes, fruits, total vegetables, and whole cereals); (2) optimum components (eggs, dairy products, fish and seafood, tubers and potatoes and vegetable oils); (3) ratio components (dark green vegetables/total vegetables and red and orange vegetables/total vegetables) and (4) moderation components (red meat, chickens and substitutes, animal fats and added sugars). Each item earns a score between 0 and 5 or 10 points. The items in the sufficiency, optimal, and moderate categories can receive 10 points, while the ratio items can score a maximum of 5 points, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 150 points [21]. MIND diet calculation

The original MIND diet index included 15 dietary parameters, with 10 being brain-healthy food groups (green leafy vegetables, other vegetables, nuts, berries, beans, whole grains, fish, poultry, olive oil, and wine) and 5 being unhealthy (red meats, butter and stick margarine, cheese, pastries and sweets, and fried/fast food) [16]. The study used 14 food groups to create the MIND diet score. We did not take into account wine consumption because of incomplete data. Participants were classified into tertile based on their adherence to the MIND diet. Individuals with the lowest intake of green leafy vegetables, other vegetables, nuts, berries, beans, whole grains, fish, and poultry were given a score of 0. Those in the middle tertile received a score of 0.5, while those in the highest tertile were given a score of 1. Conversely, for red meats, butter and stick margarine, cheese, pastries and sweets, and fried/fast food intake, individuals in the lowest tertile were given a score of 1, and those in the highest tertile were given a score of 0. Individuals in the middle tertile for these components were assigned a score of 0.5. The overall MIND diet score was calculated by summing up all dietary component scores, resulting in a score between 0 and 14 for each participant.

Depression, anxiety and stress assessment

The Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS) is a comprehensive 21-item self-assessment tool designed to evaluate the levels of depression, anxiety, and stress in individuals. This instrument utilizes seven questions for each condition, with responses rated on a Likert scale ranging from 0 (indicating the absence of the symptom) to 3 (indicating a high level of the symptom). The cumulative score obtained from these questions classifies individuals into categories such as normal, mild, moderate, severe, or very severe based on their reported symptoms. Notably, the DASS has demonstrated robust sensitivity and specificity levels ranging from 75 to 89%, indicating its effectiveness in accurately identifying individuals experiencing depression, anxiety, or stress [22, 23]. Furthermore, this tool has undergone validation specifically for the Iranian population, ensuring its relevance and applicability within this demographic [24, 25].

Statistical analysis

Participants' demographic characteristics, dietary intakes, and mental health status were summarized using descriptive statistics based on the MIND diet and PHDI quartiles. The differences in variables between groups were assessed using the chi-square test for categorical variables and ANOVA for continuous variables. Mean scores of depression, anxiety, and stress were compared using ANOVA across the quartiles of the MIND diet and PHDI. Additionally, a post-hoc test was utilized to identify specific group differences following the significant ANOVA results, allowing for a more detailed understanding of how each quartile of the MIND diet and PHDI influenced mental health outcomes. The cut-offs for defining depression, anxiety, and stress were 9, 7, and 14, respectively [26]. Individuals with scores higher than these thresholds were classified as having the outcomes. Binary logistic regression models were used to investigate the associations between the MIND diet, PHDI, and the risk of depression, anxiety, and stress. Analysis was conducted using both crude and adjusted models. The adjusted models took into account various factors, namely age, sex, BMI, physical activity, education level, smoking status, alcohol and drug use, residence type, ethnicity, family history of psychiatric disorder, and energy intake. All confounders were considered as covariates in the statistical analysis. The results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp. Released 2019. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). A p-value less than 0.05 considered statistically significant, and all tests were two-tailed.

Compared to the first quartile, participants in the highest quartile of PHDI had significantly higher age, proportion of female participants, and BMI (P < 0.001 for all). However, they exhibited significantly lower energy intake and were less likely to be smokers (P < 0.001 for all). Additionally, there were significant differences in physical activity levels across quartiles of PHDI (P = 0.014), but no significant differences were observed regarding educational level (P > 0.05). Furthermore, the highest quartile of MIND diet was significantly associated with higher age, energy intake, BMI (P < 0.001 for all), and proportion of female participants (P = 0.007). Additionally, there were significant differences in physical activity (P = 0.028) among quartiles of MIND diet, but no significant variation in smoking status was observed among quartiles and education level (P > 0.05).

Table 2 shows the mean ± SD of depression, anxiety, and stress levels of the participants across quartiles of PHDI and MIND diet. Participants in the Q3 of PHDI scores exhibited significantly lower mean depression scores (P = 0.003). Participants in the Q1 of the MIND diet showed significantly higher levels of depression compared to those in the Q3 and Q4 (P < 0.05 for all). Similarly, noticeable differences were observed in anxiety levels, with Q1 participants reporting higher anxiety compared to those in Q2, Q3, and Q4 (P < 0.05 for all). A significant contrast was found between Q1 and Q3, indicating that individuals in Q1 experienced greater stress than those in Q3 (P < 0.05 for all). No significant differences were observed in means of anxiety and depression across quartiles of PHDI.

Discussion

This study aims to investigate the association between MIND diet scores (a well-known diet for reducing Alzheimer's disease symptoms) and ELD (an environmentally sustainable diet) with mental health status. The results of this cross-sectional study revealed an inverse association between MIND diet and depression, anxiety, and stress. However, ELD demonstrated an inverse association only with depression and it was not associated with neither anxiety or stress.

Several studies have linked The MIND diet to improved mental health [27, 28]. However, the results of previous studies on the association between MIND diet and mental health in Iran were inconsistent. Several studies have shown that higher adherence to the MIND diet were associated with a reduced risk of depression [17, 18, 29] and anxiety [17, 30], however, other studies in have reported conflicting results, suggesting null association between MIND diet and depression, anxiety, or stress [14, 17, 31]. Variations in findings between studies may result from differences in population characteristics. For instance, while Seifollahi et al. studied 282 female participants and found no significant difference in the severity of depression, anxiety, stress, and MIND diet adherence [14], Rostami et al. found no significant associations between adherence to the MIND diet and stress, anxiety, and depression in their study of 400 male health professionals [31]. Gender and occupation are also factors that could influence mental health outcomes, potentially contributing to the discrepancies in the findings [32, 33]. Furthermore, in contrast to our results, Barkhordari et al. found no significant relationship between the MIND diet and stress [17]. The differing results regarding the relationship between stress and the MIND diet could be due to variations in the target population and the locations where the samples were taken. It is worth considering that Yazd, where the study was conducted, is a smaller city than Mashhad, where our study took place. This difference in population size may have contributed to varying stress levels. A study by Barkhordari et al. found that adherence to the MIND diet was inversely linked to lower stress levels in the second regression model. This model considered age, sex, energy intake, and BMI as cofounders. However, this relationship was not observed in the third regression model, which adjusted the additional confounders, including pregnancy or lactation status, intake of EPA, DHA, and fibers [17]. Adjusting the analysis for these additional confounders could be why Barkhordari et al., unlike Ardekani et al., did not see a relationship between MIND diet adherence and stress [34].

Overall, the MIND diet and ELD are categorized as plant-based diets and similarly, plant-based diets have been found to alleviate depression [35–38]. The ELD emphasizes on plant-based foods, low consumption of red meat and processed foods, and moderate dairy and fish consumption [21]. Currently, limited study has been conducted on the relationship between ELD and mental health. Emerging evidence suggests that adherence to the ELD diet positively influences cognitive function [39]. In line with our findings, one study that utilized different ELD score reported that adherence to the ELD was associated with improved mood and attention [11]. However, this study reported that limited consumption of red meat and poultry was associated with inadequacy of essential nutrient that contribute brain health. This indicates that strict limitation in consumption of animal-based foods may not be the optimal dietary choice for brain health [11]. Despite there are valid reasons for focusing on the relationship between the ELD and depression specifically, its mechanisms have not yet been discovered to the best of our knowledge. So we can't guess why we have not seen any relationship between the ELD and, stress and anxiety. Possibly due to a wider array of factors beyond dietary habits in relationship between ELD and, stress and anxiety, we couldn't discover these relations. So we suggest considering more environmental factors in future studies.

The Mind Diet was initially developed to slow cognitive decline in Alzheimer's patients, focusing on items such as olive oil, nuts, wine, berries, and other specific foods [40]. On the other hand, the ELD was created to establish a globally sustainable and healthy diet, emphasizing eggs, vegetables (particularly dark green and red varieties), and other specific foods [21, 41, 42]. The distinct composition of these two diets regarding micronutrients and macronutrients likely accounts for their differing effects on mental health.

The MIND diet incorporates omega-3 fatty acids, antioxidants, and other nutrients that promote brain health and cognitive function [43]. Meanwhile, ELD prioritizes fiber, vitamin E, potassium, and folate while restricting pyridoxine and calcium intake [44]. While ELD benefits from the abundance of plant-based foods that positively affect mental health, the potential lack of calcium and pyridoxine may counteract these benefits. Pyridoxine (vitamin B6) is essential for producing neurotransmitters such as serotonin, dopamine, and GABA, which regulate the nervous system and mood [45]. A pyridoxine deficiency may adversely impact mental health [46]. Calcium regulates neurotransmitter synthesis and release, crucial for neuronal activation and mood regulation [47, 48]. Studies suggest that higher consumption of dairy and calcium is linked to a protective effect on mental health [49–51]. Conclusively, we have found that the MIND diet has a more significant positive impact on mental health compared to the ELD diet. While previous studies have shown the beneficial effects of the ELD diet on mental health and cognitive function, and in line with them, we also observed the protective effect of the ELD diet on depression symptoms. However, it is crucial to acknowledge and address the nutritional deficiencies associated with the ELD diet to enhance its effectiveness as a dietary pattern for promoting mental health.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this was the first study to evaluate the relationship between ELD and depression, anxiety, and stress. Furthermore, no previous study has compared the MIND diet and ELD regarding their association with mental health. This study benefited from a large sample size, and we adjusted for many potential confounders. However, this study had some limitations. Firstly, because the current study's samples were employees, it may be difficult to generalize our results. Also due to its cross-sectional design, establishing a causal link was not feasible. Therefore, we recommend further research using a cohort design to investigate the impact of ELD and the MIND diet on long-term mental health outcomes. While the PHDI is commonly used to measure adherence to the ELD, future studies should consider using other indices to yield different results. Using the FFQ to assess dietary intake may introduce unavoidable measurement biases, such as recall bias. The DASS-21's reliance on self-reported symptoms may be influenced by factors such as social desirability bias, mood at the time of assessment, and individual differences in symptom perception and reporting. It is also worth noting that the questionnaire does not assess the duration of symptoms, which is essential for distinguishing between acute and chronic mental health issues. Finally, most of the participants in this study were men. Although we adjusted for gender in the regression model, future studies should acknowledge this limitation.

Conclusion

Recently, there has been an increasing focus on environmentally friendly diets. However, the question remains whether these diets also benefit human health. To address this gap in research, we conducted a cross-sectional study to explore the relationship between ELD (a diet with little environmental impact) and the MIND diet, which focuses on mental health factors such as depression, anxiety, and stress. The results of our study indicated that adherence to the MIND diet was inversely associated with depression, anxiety, and stress had a protective effect. Additionally, ELD was found to have a significant inverse relationship with depression, but not with anxiety or stress. Further large-scale cohort studies are required to confirm these results.
