The impact of preoperative sleep disorders on postoperative pain and recovery in patients undergoing gastrectomy: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Wenzhi Zhu, Shuxia Li, Yan Huang, Xiaoyi Wang, Hongyu Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPoor sleep quality is a common comorbidity in surgical patients, but its specific impact on postoperative outcomes in gastric cancer surgery remains underexplored. This study investigated the association of preoperative sleep disorders on pain sensitivity, analgesic consumption, and recovery in this population.MethodsOne hundred patients undergoing elective gastrectomy were prospectively enrolled and stratified into two groups based on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI): a Non-Sleep Disorder group (PSQI < 8, n = 50) and a Sleep Disorder group (PSQI ≥ 8, n = 50). Preoperative assessments included quantitative sensory testing (pressure, cold, heat), anxiety and depression scales, and sleep quality. Postoperative outcomes included pain scores (VAS), sufentanil consumption, rescue analgesia, recovery milestones, and length of hospital stay (LOS).ResultsPreoperatively, the Sleep Disorder group demonstrated a significantly lower heat pain threshold (19.63 ± 14.32 s vs. 33.89 ± 16.78 s, p < 0.001) and a higher heat pain score (3.56 ± 1.76 vs. 2.50 ± 0.89, p < 0.001). Postoperatively, this group reported higher VAS scores at extubation (2.60 ± 0.67 vs. 1.88 ± 0.85, p < 0.001), required more rescue analgesia (38.0% vs. 14.0%, p = 0.011), and consumed more sufentanil at 24 h (111.38 ± 39.24 µg vs. 97.71 ± 25.31 µg, p = 0.042) and 48 h (190.46 ± 61.75 µg vs. 169.39 ± 36.83 µg, p = 0.041). Despite a shorter time to first flatus (4.48 ± 1.92 vs. 5.36 ± 2.30 days, p = 0.040), their overall LOS was significantly longer (8.0 vs. 7.0 days, p = 0.005).ConclusionIn patients undergoing gastric cancer surgery, preoperative sleep disorders are associated with heightened pain sensitivity, increased postoperative opioid requirements, and prolonged hospitalization. These findings highlight the importance of preoperative sleep assessment and tailored perioperative management.

Introduction

Gastric cancer remains a significant global health burden and radical surgery remains the cornerstone of curative treatment (Sung et al. 2021). The postoperative period is often challenging, characterized by pain, gastrointestinal dysfunction, and prolonged recovery (Patel and Ownby 2025). In the pursuit of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS), identifying and modifying preoperative risk factors has become a critical focus of perioperative medicine (Tazreean et al. 2022). Among these factors, sleep quality is increasingly recognized as a pivotal, yet often overlooked, component of a patient’s preoperative physiological and psychological state (Chang et al. 2022).

Perioperative sleep disorders are exceptionally prevalent in the cancer population, with incidence rates reported to be as high as 30–50% (Butris et al. 2023). This high prevalence is driven by a complex interplay of psychological distress (e.g., anxiety and depression), disease-related symptoms such as pain, and the direct pathophysiological impacts of the malignancy itself (Chen et al. 2024). Moreover, the bidirectional relationship between sleep and pain is well-established; sleep disruption can lead to hyperalgesia, while pain further degrades sleep architecture, creating a vicious cycle (Haack et al. 2020). In terms of patients, this cycle can manifest as increased postoperative pain, greater opioid consumption, and delayed recovery (Varallo et al. 2022).

While the negative impact of poor sleep on general surgical outcomes is recognized, its specific effects in the context of major oncologic surgery like gastrectomy are less clear. Radical gastrectomy involves significant physiological stress and alterations to gastrointestinal anatomy, which may interact uniquely with sleep-related vulnerabilities. Furthermore, the effect of preoperative sleep quality on objective measures of pain sensitivity (quantitative sensory testing) in this population is not well-documented.

This prospective cohort study aimed to investigate the association of preoperative sleep disorders on the comprehensive recovery profile of patients following radical gastrectomy.

Methods

Study population

This prospective observational study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University Cancer Hospital(No.2023KT62)and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (No.ChiCTR2400086435). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Patients, aged 18 − 72 years, with American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I-III, scheduled for elective radical gastrectomy for gastric cancer at Peking University Cancer Hospital between October 2024 and October 2025, were enrolled in the study. Exclusion criteria included: multiple primary malignant tumors, pre-existing chronic pain syndromes, psychiatric disorders, long-term use of hypnotics or opioids, cognitive impairment, or repeated gastrectomy and conjoined surgery (Fig. 1).

Group allocation

On the day before surgery, sleep quality of patients was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). A global PSQI score ≥ 8 indicates significant sleep disturbance (Faiz et al. 2024). Based on this cutoff, patients were allocated into two groups: the Non-Sleep Disorder group (PSQI < 8, n = 50) and the Sleep Disorder group (PSQI ≥ 8, n = 50).

Anesthetic and analgesic techniques

General anesthesia was induced with intravenous etomidate (0.3 mg/kg), propofol (2 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.4 µg/kg), and cisatracurium (0.2 mg/kg). Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane inhalation, complemented by continuous intravenous infusions of remifentanil and propofol. The depth of anesthesia was monitored using the Bispectral Index (BIS), which was maintained between 40 and 60. Additionally, a bolus of 10 µg sufentanil was administered intravenously 30 min prior to the end of surgery.

The analgesic pump used sufentanil 5 µg/kg + 0.9% sodium chloride solution+dexmedetomidine 50 ug+tropisetron 10 mg, with a capacity of 120 ml. The initial loading dose was 2 mL, the continuous dose was 0.8 mL/h, the single PCA dose is 2 ml, and the locking time was 15 min. When leaving the recovery room, ensured that the patient’s pain score is ≤ 3 points. If the pain score > 3 points, the patient was administered with sufentanil 0.1 µg/kg, and assessed after 10 min, until the pain score was ≤ 3 points.

Data collection

Baseline data

Demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), education level, operation type, range of gastrectomy and medical history (diabetes, hypertension, smoking, alcohol use, chemotherapy) were recorded.

Preoperative assessments

Quantitative Sensory Testing (QST)

Preoperative pain thresholds were measured using standardized quantitative sensory testing protocols. A mechanical pain meter (Wagner, US) was used to measure the mechanical pain threshold. The probe of the pain meter was placed on the index finger and slowly pressurized. When the subject felt pain, the pressurization was stopped. At that time point, the reading on the display was the subject’s pain threshold. The test was repeated three times, and average values were recorded. The cold pain threshold (CPT) was evaluated by immersing the non-dominant hand in a cold water bath maintained at 5 °C, and the time (in seconds) until the first sensation of pain was recorded, with a maximum cutoff of 60 s. The heat pain threshold (HPT) was measured using a thermostatic metal plate set at 47.5 °C, where patients placed the thenar eminence of the non-dominant hand; the duration (in seconds) until pain-induced withdrawal was recorded, also with a 60-second safety limit.

Psychological assessment

Anxiety and depression were evaluated using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), which consists of 14 items divided into two 7-item subscales for anxiety and depression. A subscale score of 8 or higher was considered indicative of clinically significant symptoms. General dispositional optimism was assessed using the Life Orientation Test-Revised (LOT-R), which includes six items rated on a 5-point Likert scale.

Sleep quality evaluation

The Richards-Campbell Sleep Questionnaire (RCSQ) was administered to subjectively evaluate multiple dimensions of in-hospital sleep quality, including sleep depth, latency, and efficiency.

Intraoperative data

Anesthesia duration, surgery duration, intravenous fluid volumes (crystalloid, colloid), blood loss, and urine output were documented.

Postoperative outcomes

Pain and analgesia

Pain intensity was assessed using a Visual Analog Scale (VAS, 0–10) at extubation, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h postoperatively. All patients received patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) with sufentanil. Total sufentanil consumption at 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h was recorded. The need for rescue analgesic medication (a bolus of non-PCIA opioid) was documented.

Recovery metrics

Time to first flatus, time to first oral intake, and total length of hospital stay (LOS) were recorded.

Adverse events

Incidence of nausea, postoperative fever, re-intubation, and postoperative anxiety was monitored.

Postoperative sleep

RCSQ scores were collected on the first (RCSQ1) and second (RCSQ2) postoperative days.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics (Version 26.0, IBM Corp., USA). Continuous normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using Student’s independent samples *t*-test. Non-normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range) and were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data are presented as numbers (percentages) and were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A two-tailed P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined based on postoperative 24-hour sufentanil consumption as the primary outcome. Pre-test data showed mean consumptions of 95 ± 20 µg in the non-sleep disorder group and 110 ± 30 µg in the sleep disorder group. With a two-sided α of 0.05 and 80% power, 47 patients per group (94 total) were required. Accounting for a 10% dropout rate, a total of 102 patients were enrolled. Ultimately, one patient from each group withdrew, resulting in 100 completed cases (50 per group) for final analysis.

The two groups were well-matched in all baseline demographics and comorbidities, including age, gender, BMI, operation type, range of gastrectomy, anxiety symptoms, depressive symptoms, and medical history (p > 0.05). As per the grouping criteria, the sleep disorder group had a significantly higher PSQI score and a lower preoperative RCSQ score (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Postoperative pain and analgesic consumption

Postoperative pain scores are summarized in Table 4. The Sleep Disorder group reported significantly higher VAS scores immediately after extubation (2.60 ± 0.67 vs. 1.88 ± 0.85, p < 0.001). A significantly higher proportion of patients in the sleep disorder group required rescue analgesic medication (19 [38.0%] vs. 7 [14.0%], p = 0.011). Consequently, total sufentanil consumption was significantly greater in the Sleep Disorder group at both 24 h (111.38 ± 39.24 µg vs. 97.71 ± 25.31 µg, p = 0.042) and 48 h postoperatively (190.46 ± 61.75 µg vs. 169.39 ± 36.83 µg, p = 0.041) (Table 4).

Postoperative recovery and adverse events

The time to first flatus was shorter in the Sleep Disorder group (4.48 ± 1.92 days vs. 5.36 ± 2.30 days, p = 0.040). However, the median length of hospital stay was significantly longer in the sleep disorder group (8.0 days [IQR 7.0, 9.3] vs. 7.0 days [IQR 6.0, 8.0], p = 0.005). There were no significant differences in the time to first oral intake or the incidence of adverse events such as nausea, fever, re-intubation, or postoperative anxiety between the two groups. Postoperative sleep quality, measured by RCSQ, remained significantly worse in the sleep disorder group on both postoperative day 1 and 2 (p < 0.01 for both) (Table 5).

Discussion

Sleep disturbances were increasingly recognized as a significant modifiable risk factor impacting perioperative outcomes. During the perioperative period, sleep architecture is frequently disrupted by a confluence of factors, including the physiological stress of surgery, anesthesia effects, and environmental challenges in the hospital setting. A growing body of evidence links these disturbances to a range of adverse postoperative consequences, such as intensified pain perception, increased analgesic consumption, delayed functional recovery, and heightened risk of complications (Herrero Babiloni et al. 2024, Lawrentschuk et al. 2003, Liu and Brenner 2022). The underlying mechanisms are multifactorial, involving dysregulation of neuroendocrine stress responses, amplification of systemic inflammation, and impairment of central nervous system pain modulatory pathways (Irwin et al. 2016, Roehrs et al. 2006). Against this backdrop, our study investigated the impact of preoperatively identified sleep disorders on a comprehensive set of outcomes in patients undergoing major oncologic surgery.

From the preoperative data, there were the observed reduction in heat pain threshold (19.63 ± 14.32 vs. 33.89 ± 16.78 s, p < 0.001) and elevated heat pain scores (3.56 ± 1.76 vs. 2.50 ± 0.89, p < 0.001) in the sleep disorder group, suggesting altered pain processing pathways, likely mediated through central sensitization mechanisms. The neurobiological underpinnings of these observations warranted careful consideration. Accumulating evidence indicated that sleep disruption activated the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, promoted pro-inflammatory cytokine release (including IL-6 and TNF-α), and impaired descending inhibitory pathways, collectively contributing to a state of heightened pain sensitivity (Cook et al. 2018, Raff et al. 2023, Lange et al. 2022). Our results substantially extended these findings to the surgical context and emphasized the need for tailored analgesic approaches in this vulnerable population.

Furthermore, the clinical implications of these physiological alterations were substantial. In this present study, the significantly higher postoperative pain scores (VAS: 2.60 ± 0.67 vs. 1.88 ± 0.85, p < 0.001), increased sufentanil consumption at both 24 h (111.38 ± 39.24 µg vs. 97.71 ± 25.31 µg, p = 0.042) and 48 h (190.46 ± 61.75 µg vs. 169.39 ± 36.83 µg, p = 0.041), and elevated rescue analgesia rate (38.0% vs. 14.0%, p = 0.011) collectively indicated a substantially greater postoperative pain burden in patients with sleep disorders. This heightened analgesic requirement might reflect underlying neurobiological alterations, including potential µ-opioid receptor downregulation, impaired endogenous opioid function, or disrupted blood-brain barrier permeability (Irwin and Cole 2011, Seiger et al. 2024, Slavich and Irwin 2014, Motivala 2011). These findings strongly supported implementing enhanced multimodal analgesic regimens, potentially incorporating regional anesthesia techniques and non-opioid adjuvants, for this high-risk group to minimize opioid-related complications while ensuring adequate pain control.

However, notably, there was a paradoxical finding that the sleep disorder group had a shorter time to first flatus (4.48 ± 1.92 vs. 5.36 ± 2.30 days, p = 0.040) but a prolonged hospitalization (8.0 vs. 7.0 days, p = 0.005), presenting a fascinating clinical observation. While opioid administration typically delays gastrointestinal recovery through µ-receptor-mediated inhibition of intestinal motility (Xu et al. 2025, Farmer et al. 2018, Paul et al. 2021), the observed accelerated flatus might reflect compensatory neuro-immune mechanisms or differential inflammatory responses counteracting opioid effects. Alternatively, this phenomenon could be attributed to variations in surgical procedures between groups. However, the ultimate prolongation of hospital stay underscores that gastrointestinal function represents just one component of overall recovery, with inadequate pain control, potentially reduced mobility, and other unmeasured factors likely contributing to delayed discharge.

When contextualized within the existing literature, our findings aligned with emerging evidence highlighting sleep as a modifiable risk factor for postoperative outcomes. Nevertheless, our study extended previous work by comprehensively examining multiple pain modalities and demonstrating the interplay between sleep disorders and psychological factors. The association between preoperative sleep quality and postoperative analgesic requirements provided new insights into optimizing pain management strategies for surgical patients.

Therefore, this prospective cohort study adds to the evidence suggesting that preoperative sleep disorders are associated with differences in postoperative recovery in patients undergoing gastric cancer surgery. The findings of this cohort demonstrated that patients with poor preoperative sleep quality exhibited enhanced pain sensitivity, increased postoperative analgesic requirements, and delayed overall recovery, highlighting the critical role of sleep assessment in perioperative management.

Limitations

This study also had several limitations. First, this was a single-center study, so our findings might not fully apply to other institutions or patient populations, even though we followed standardized protocols. Second, we used the PSQI questionnaire to assess sleep quality rather than objective tools like polysomnography. While the PSQI was well-validated and widely used in clinical research, we recognized that subjective reports didn’t capture the full picture of sleep architecture. Future studies with objective sleep monitoring would be valuable. Third, our cutoff of PSQI ≥ 8 to define sleep disorders was higher than the originally recommended cutoff of 5. We chose this threshold to identify patients with more pronounced sleep disturbances in this cancer population, but it did mean our results might not be directly comparable to studies that used lower cutoffs. Fourth, although key variables—including surgical approach (laparoscopic vs. open) and extent of gastrectomy—were well balanced between groups (Table 1), we did not perform multivariable regression analysis. We believed the balanced baseline characteristics reduced the risk of major confounding, but we could not rule out the possibility that unmeasured factors (such as specific surgical reconstruction details or intraoperative fluid management) might have influenced the results. Fifth, the finding that the sleep disorder group had a shorter time to first flatus despite higher opioid use is somewhat counterintuitive. We were not entirely sure what explains this, and it should be interpreted cautiously—it might reflect unmeasured differences in perioperative care rather than a true biological effect. Finally, while our sample size was sufficient for the primary outcomes, it was too small to allow for robust subgroup analyses. Larger, multicenter studies with objective sleep measurements would help confirm and extend our findings.

Conclusion

In this prospective cohort study, preoperative sleep disorders were significantly associated with a postoperative recovery profile characterized by enhanced pain sensitivity, increased analgesic requirements, and prolonged hospitalization in patients undergoing gastrectomy for gastric cancer. These findings suggest that integrating routine sleep assessment into preoperative evaluation and developing targeted interventions for sleep-impaired patients may represent promising strategies for optimizing perioperative outcomes.
