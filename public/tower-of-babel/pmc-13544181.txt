Real-world safety profile of twice a day deferiprone in patients with thalassemia, sickle cell disease, or other anemias
Authors: Sujit Sheth, Ashutosh Lal, Abena Appiah-Kubi, Scott A. Peslak, Noemi Toiber Temin, Mengru Amanda Chen, Thomas Coates
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Twice daily deferiprone was well tolerated in patients with iron overload in clinical practice, with no new safety concerns observed.•All neutropenia and agranulocytosis events recovered, and no fatal outcomes assessed were considered related to deferiprone treatment.

Introduction

Blood transfusions are an important part of treatment regimens for many hematological conditions, including thalassemia, sickle cell disease (SCD), and other anemias.1, 2, 3 Patients with thalassemia or SCD often require either episodic or chronic transfusion therapy, with ∼90% of patients with SCD having had at least 1 transfusion and 35% of patients with thalassemia being transfusion dependent.1,2 Regular transfusions lead to clinically significant iron overload, which is present in ∼90% of patients with transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia.3,4 Iron overload can also occur in patients with nontransfusion-dependent thalassemia because of iron dysregulation from ineffective erythropoiesis.5 In SCD, transfusional iron overload is observed in 13% of patients, of which 70% result from episodic transfusions.6 Iron overload may lead to hepatic, cardiac, and endocrine iron deposition, resulting in organ damage, significant morbidity, and death if left untreated.3,7,8

Iron chelation therapy is the primary treatment for transfusional iron overload and is integral to the treatment paradigm for patients with thalassemia, SCD, and other anemias.3 Deferiprone (DFP) is an established oral iron chelator that was initially approved as a thrice daily formulation by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2011 for the treatment of transfusional iron overload in patients with thalassemia, with subsequent expansion to include patients with SCD and other anemias in 2021.9 The efficacy and safety of DFP thrice daily are well established, with nearly 3 decades of data from randomized controlled trials and real-world studies.10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15

Adherence to chelation therapy is essential for optimizing patients’ long-term outcomes.16, 17, 18 Although adherence to DFP thrice daily in patients with thalassemia has been reported to range from 79% to 98% in randomized controlled trials and cohort studies, real-world adherence is generally lower because of pill burden, particularly the inconvenience of a midday dose.13,16, 17, 18, 19 A twice daily formulation of DFP, with thrice daily-equivalent exposure, was developed to improve adherence.17 The DFP twice daily tablet formulation was approved by the US FDA in 2020 for the treatment of transfusional iron overload in patients aged ≥8 years with thalassemia, and in 2021 for patients with SCD or other anemias.9

There is currently limited evidence on the long-term safety profile of DFP twice daily in patients with thalassemia, SCD, or other anemias. A phase 2 study assessed the safety of DFP twice daily in patients with transfusion-dependent blood disorders and identified no new safety concerns compared with DFP thrice daily.17 However, safety was assessed over only 28 days in 29 patients, all of whom were adults and the most of whom had a primary diagnosis of thalassemia.17 The most common adverse reactions associated with DFP thrice daily in patients with thalassemia syndromes include nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain.9,15 Although less frequently seen, neutropenia and agranulocytosis are of particular interest because they are a possible serious side effect of DFP, and require monitoring according to US prescribing information (USPI).9 Prevalence of neutropenia and agranulocytosis with DFP thrice daily is low, observed in 0.6% and 0.3% of patients with SCD in a real-world analysis,15 and in 7% and 1% of patients with thalassemia syndromes in clinical trials, respectively.9

The objective of this analysis was to assess the safety of DFP twice daily treatment in patients with thalassemia syndromes, SCD, or other anemias in real-world clinical practice in the United States.

Methods

Registry design and objectives

The Ferriprox Total Care Registry is an active drug surveillance program established in the United States in 2011 after FDA approval of DFP thrice daily for the treatment of transfusional iron overload in patients with thalassemia. The registry has been described previously15 and was originally designed to collect and analyze cases of neutropenia, agranulocytosis, and infections that were fatal or led to hospitalization in patients treated with DFP in real-world clinical practice. All patients prescribed DFP thrice daily or twice daily in the United States were enrolled in the registry by a central pharmacy.

Patient population

In this analysis, data were extracted from the registry for all patients based in the United States who were referred for DFP twice daily between 1 July 2020 and 31 August 2023. Patients may have been treated with DFP thrice daily before enrollment. The baseline group included patients with a primary diagnosis classification of thalassemia syndromes, SCD, or other anemias. Of these patients, safety data were assessed for those who received ≥1 shipment of DFP twice daily (safety analysis group). Subgroup analyses were conducted for patients with thalassemia syndromes and SCD. Data for patients with a primary diagnosis classified under other indications were assessed separately. For the treatment of transfusion-dependent iron overload, the recommended DFP twice daily dose is 75 to 99 mg/kg per day (actual body weight) administered in 2 divided doses9 and dosing was determined by the treating physician.

Data collection

The central pharmacy collected patient data at enrollment, including the patient’s sex, age group (<18 or ≥18 years), and primary diagnosis (the primary indication for DFP based on the International Classification of Diseases, tenth edition [ICD-10]). DFP twice daily exposure was defined as time active in the registry (see the supplemental Methods for further details). The central pharmacy also recorded when a patient was discharged and the reason.

With each monthly shipment of DFP twice daily, patients or caregivers were contacted by the central pharmacy to enquire regarding the patient’s well-being. Solicited and unsolicited reports of adverse events (AEs) were collected, regardless of the relationship to DFP treatment, as previously described.15 Healthcare providers were also able to submit reports of treatment-emergent AEs. All relevant patient safety data collected from the registry were deidentified and entered into Chiesi’s global safety database. AEs were adjudicated by the central pharmacy (or reporter when unsolicited) and additionally by the sponsor in accordance with good pharmacovigilance practice to determine potential causality related to DFP, with the most conservative assessment prevailing. Due to the patient data being deidentified, patients’ primary diagnosis was extracted from individual case reports for the analysis of AEs, and was defined as the diagnosis for which DFP twice daily was initially prescribed. Details on AE events coded to neutropenia and agranulocytosis were extracted from individual case reports and each report was considered a unique patient based on birth date and gender.

Analysis of outcomes

The frequencies of AEs, serious AEs (SAEs), AEs of special interest (AESIs), and AEs with a fatal outcome were assessed. An AESI was defined as any AE with a Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities preferred term included in section 6.1 of the USPI.9 The Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities preferred terms “Blood creatinine abnormal,” “Hepatic enzyme increased,” and “Liver function test increased,” were also defined as AESIs based on the authors’ clinical experience.

All cases of neutropenia (including febrile neutropenia) and agranulocytosis were considered SAEs regardless of frequency, severity, or clinical significance. Neutropenia and agranulocytosis were defined based on the physician’s clinical practice. These events were defined as recovered if they were not reported as ongoing and no further follow-up was reported. Further details on the safety analyses are provided in the supplemental Methods.

Statistical analysis

All data were assessed using descriptive statistics and no statistical comparisons were undertaken. Unless otherwise stated, data are reported as mean (standard deviation [SD]).

Ethics statement

This analysis was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, local regulations, and the guidelines on Good Pharmacovigilance Practice, Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practice, and Good Clinical Practice.20, 21, 22 All patients consented to participate in the registry and were encouraged to contact the program with any questions or safety concerns.

Results

Patient population and disposition

Of the 425 patients included in this analysis, 360 had a primary diagnosis classification of thalassemia syndromes, SCD, or other anemias, and comprised the baseline group (Figure 1). The remaining 65 patients had primary diagnoses classified as other indications and were not included in the analysis because of the diversity of patient diagnoses (supplemental Table 1). In total, 123 of 360 patients (34.2%) in the baseline group were discharged from the registry by the data cutoff point (Figure 2). Reported reasons for discharge from the registry included physician direction (9.7%), adverse reaction (5.0%), and switching to deferasirox (DFX; 2.5%). Deferiprone exposure

The safety analysis group comprised 348 patients who received ≥1 DFP twice daily shipment during the data collection period (Figure 1). These patients received DFP twice daily for a mean (SD) of 343.0 (281.9) days. Patients in the thalassemia subgroup (n = 129) received DFP twice daily for longer than those in the SCD subgroup (n = 189), with a mean (SD) exposure of 407.4 (311.1) days and 319.1 (256.5) days, respectively. Total estimated exposure was 327 person-years in the safety analysis group, 144 person-years in the thalassemia subgroup, and 165 person-years in the SCD subgroup. In patients with other indications (n = 64), total estimated exposure was 48 person-years, with a mean (SD) exposure of 272.8 (222.4) days.

In the thalassemia subgroup, nausea was the most commonly reported AE (4.8%, n = 19/396), followed by COVID-19 (3.5%, n = 14/396), and fatigue (2.5%, n = 10/396). In total, 369 SAEs were reported in the safety analysis group, including 92 in the thalassemia subgroup and 238 in the SCD subgroup (Table 2). All sickle cell anemia with crisis events were classified as SAEs, and this was the most commonly reported SAE in both the safety analysis group (14.9%, n = 55/369) and SCD subgroup (22.7%, n = 54/238), followed by ill-defined disorder (4.6%, n = 17/369 and 4.6%, n = 11/238, respectively), and death with no reported cause (4.3%, n = 16/369 and 4.2%, n = 10/238, respectively). Ill-defined disorder (5.4%, n = 5/92), neutropenia (5.4%, n = 5/92), and death with no reported cause (4.3%, n = 4/92) were the most commonly reported SAEs in the thalassemia subgroup. In total, 228 AEs and 67 SAEs were reported in the other indications subgroup, and the most commonly reported events are shown in supplemental Table 2.

AEs of interest

AESIs for the safety analysis group, and thalassemia and SCD subgroups, are shown in Table 3. In the safety analysis group, gastrointestinal disorders such as nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain accounted for only a small proportion of total AEs reported (3.2%, 2.3%, and 1.6%, respectively). A total of 20 AESIs related to increased liver enzymes were reported, including hepatic enzyme increased (1.1%; 6 events in thalassemia, 3 events in SCD, and 2 events in other anemias subgroups), alanine aminotransferase (ALT) increased (0.5%; 3 events in SCD and 2 events in other anemias subgroups), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) increased (0.2%; 2 events in thalassemia subgroup), and liver function test increased (0.2%; 1 event in SCD and 1 event in other anemias subgroups). In total, 4 of the 20 AESIs related to liver function were deemed serious. Only 2 events of increased blood creatinine were reported, occurring in 1 patient with thalassemia and 1 patient with SCD, neither of which were serious. Neutropenia and agranulocytosis

In the safety analysis group, 7 patients reported at least 1 event of neutropenia or agranulocytosis (2.0%, n = 7/348). A total of 7 events of neutropenia were reported in 6 patients (1.7%, n = 6/348), including 4 patients with thalassemia, 1 patient with SCD, and 1 patient with other anemia. All reported cases of neutropenia or agranulocytosis recovered (not reported as ongoing and no further follow-up reported), indicating that none of the reported cases were associated with a fatal outcome. No events of neutropenia or agranulocytosis were reported for patients with other indications.

Discussion

Iron chelation is critical for the management of transfusion-dependent thalassemia, SCD, and other anemias.3,7 Our analysis shows that long-term treatment with DFP twice daily was well tolerated in routine clinical practice in the United States. Neutropenia and agranulocytosis cases were rare, and none were associated with fatal outcomes. The relatively low frequency of AEs we observed may suggest improved tolerability associated with DFP twice daily in real-world clinical practice; however, the limitations of real-world data collection, including the unsolicited nature of reporting and potential missing data, should be considered. A summary of our analysis is shown in the supplemental Video.

The DFP twice daily formulation was developed with the aim to improve real-world treatment adherence by reducing pill burden while maintaining thrice daily-equivalent exposure.17 Our data are consistent with, and expand upon, the safety data from the phase 2 study of DFP twice daily.17 This phase 2 study also indicated a strong patient preference for DFP twice daily over thrice daily, suggesting that DFP twice daily may help improve adherence.17 Improving adherence to iron chelation therapy is key to improving long-term outcomes, including reducing the risk of iron overload-related complications and improving health-related quality of life.16,18 Our data further demonstrate that DFP twice daily has a comparable tolerability to DFP thrice daily and may help support shared decision-making with patients when choosing their preferred formulation and dosing regimen.

The phase 2 study of DFP twice daily was limited by its short duration, clinical setting, small population, and lack of patients with SCD or other anemias.17 The importance and necessity of iron chelation in the management of SCD is often underestimated because of the less frequent use of regular transfusion therapy, despite evidence that iron overload is commonly observed in those patients receiving episodic transfusions.6 Recent insights also suggest ongoing underdiagnosis and undertreatment of iron overload in patients with SCD.24 The fact that blood transfusions are used to treat severe complications of SCD makes it impossible to separate complications attributed to iron overload from those attributed to SCD itself.25 Although it has been shown that chronically transfused iron overloaded patients with SCD have significantly higher risk of poorer outcomes compared with less transfused patients without iron overload, there is no data indicating the contribution of iron itself to poor outcomes.26 Our analysis provides important insights on long-term treatment with DFP twice daily for a substantial number of patients with SCD.

As observed in previous studies, the most commonly reported AEs and SAEs observed in this analysis were predominantly gastrointestinal or related to the underlying disease.12,13,15,27 Sickle cell anemia with crisis was the most frequent AE reported, accounting for 5.4% of all events in the safety analysis group. This is an expected symptom of SCD and is often reported in clinical trials of iron chelators involving patients with SCD.12,28,29 The frequency of arthralgia was lower than that observed in previous studies,27,30 and although previously reported as one of the most common SAEs in patients with thalassemia and SCD who were treated with DFP thrice daily,13,15,27 none of the arthralgia cases in our analysis were deemed serious. Our data suggest a low risk of liver toxicity, consistent with a previous real-world analysis of patients with SCD treated with DFP, which observed an increase in ALT and AST in 1.1% (7 events) and 0.3% of patients (2 events), respectively.15 These levels are lower than those observed in DFP clinical trials, in which abnormal liver function was reported in 5% of pediatric patients with β-thalassemia or other hemoglobinopathies, and increased ALT and AST were each observed in 9.2% of patients with SCD or other anemias.12,13 Renal toxicity was rare in our analysis, with only 1 event of abnormal blood creatinine levels reported in both the thalassemia and SCD subgroups. This is consistent with the low event rate reported in previous studies of 1.6% and <1% for increased blood creatinine and abnormal renal function, respectively.13,15

The overall reported frequencies of neutropenia and agranulocytosis were also relatively low in our analysis (1.7% and 0.6% of patients, respectively) compared with that observed in clinical trials.9,12,14 In pooled clinical trial data from 642 patients with thalassemia syndromes, the incidence of neutropenia and agranulocytosis were 7% and 1%, respectively, compared with 3.1% and 1.6% of patients with thalassemia in our analysis.9 An analysis of >20 years of data from DFP thrice daily studies in patients with thalassemia reported rates of neutropenia and agranulocytosis of 5.5% and 1.5%, respectively, with 61% of agranulocytosis cases occurring within the first 6 months of treatment.14 Our analysis included patients who initiated DFP on the twice daily formulation and those who transitioned from the thrice daily formulation; however, we were unable to assess chelation history before registry enrollment. This inclusion of patients with previous exposure to DFP may therefore contribute to the lower rate of neutropenia we observed in patients with thalassemia.

Compared with the numerous studies assessing patients with thalassemia, fewer clinical studies of DFP have assessed the rates of neutropenia and agranulocytosis in patients with SCD. In the Ferriprox in Patients with Iron Overload in Sickle Cell Disease Trial of DFP thrice daily in patients with SCD or other anemias, incidences of neutropenia and agranulocytosis were 2.6% and 0.7%, respectively.12 Pooled analyses of the Ferriprox in Patients with Iron Overload in Sickle Cell Disease Trial and its 2-year open-label extension reported an agranulocytosis event in 3 patients (1.5%).9,12,27 This was reduced to 1 patient (0.5%) based on the absolute neutrophil count (ANC) threshold recommended in the current DFP USPI.9,12,27,31 In this analysis, we observed that neutropenia and agranulocytosis were particularly rare in the SCD subgroup, with only 1 event of neutropenia (0.5% of patients) and no events of agranulocytosis reported. This is similar to previous real-world analyses of DFP in patients with SCD, which reported incidences of 0.6% and 0.3%, respectively.15 This low prevalence of neutropenia and agranulocytosis is reassuring, as patients with SCD are known to have a higher prevalence of Duffy-null–associated neutrophil count.32,33

Furthermore, in this analysis, all cases of neutropenia or agranulocytosis recovered, and associated infections were reported in only 1 patient with 2 events of neutropenia and 1 event of agranulocytosis, aligning with previous observations of a higher risk of serious infections with lower ANC levels.10

The ANC thresholds for neutropenia and agranulocytosis were updated in the DFP USPI in March 2025 to 0.2 × 109/L ≤ ANC <1.5 × 109/L and ANC < 0.2 × 109/L, respectively.9,31 However, management recommendations for weekly ANC monitoring remain the same.9 The data for this study were collected before this update, and agranulocytosis and neutropenia were therefore assessed according to the previous USPI recommendations and the physician’s assessment. It is important that all physicians who treat patients with thalassemia, SCD, or other anemias are able to correctly identify cases of neutropenia and agranulocytosis based on the updated ANC thresholds, and are able to effectively manage high-risk cases of agranulocytosis and associated infections to avoid fatal outcomes.9,10 In addition, patient education is key to minimizing the risk of associated infections through encouraging early reporting of initial fever symptoms.14

Overall, we observed fatal outcomes in 26 patients (7.5%) with thalassemia, SCD, or other anemias. Of the 13 assessable fatal outcomes, none were considered related to DFP treatment. Although death with no reported cause was the AE most frequently associated with a fatal outcome, case narratives of all fatal outcomes were assessed to ensure no neutropenia or agranulocytosis events had occurred, and all reported cases of neutropenia and agranulocytosis recovered. Given the likelihood of a complete blood count assessment in patients who were hospitalized before death, our data indicates that none of the deaths lacking a reported cause were associated with neutropenia or agranulocytosis. The potential impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on this analysis should be noted, given that COVID-19 was observed to be the third most commonly reported AE. Among patient subgroups, a higher death rate was observed in patients with SCD (8.5% of patients) compared with patients with thalassemia (4.7% of patients). A previous analysis of patients with SCD in this registry from 2011 to 2020 reported a comparable death rate of 7.4%.15 There may be several contributing factors to this higher death rate in patients with SCD. Patients with SCD receiving multiple transfusions, episodic or regular, are likely to have higher SCD-associated morbidity, including end-organ damage in older individuals, which can be exacerbated by iron overload.8,26 The crude mortality rate among patients with SCD in the United States from 2010 to 2020 was 1.6 per 1 million individuals, with an average age at death of 43 years.34 This is comparable to the median (range) age at death we observed of 43 (14-69) years.

Results from the phase 2 study indicate a strong patient preference for DFP twice daily compared with thrice daily, with better convenience and lower pill burden reported,17 suggesting the twice daily formulation could improve patient adherence to this oral iron chelator. Education on the administration of twice daily dosing and the low risk of neutropenia and agranulocytosis in the real-world setting is vital. In the context of the iron chelation therapeutic landscape, although twice daily dosing of DFP may be perceived as less convenient than DFX once-daily dosing, current data suggests twice daily dosing of DFX may be more efficacious and tolerable in some patients than once-daily dosing of equivalent exposure, including in pediatric patients and patients with an inadequate response to once-daily dosing.35, 36, 37, 38, 39 Recent studies and clinical practice also demonstrate that combination chelation therapy may balance the toxicity of iron overload and improve iron chelation coverage to further improve efficacy and compliance.30,40,41 DFP may be prescribed in combination with DFX or deferoxamine, with the double oral combination of DFP and DFX considered to be a preferred regimen compared with combination with parenteral deferoxamine.42, 43, 44, 45, 46 Use of DFP in combination therapy is approved in the European Union,47 and although not specifically approved by the US FDA,9 clinical off-label use of all combinations including DFP is widely practiced in the United States.48

Limitations associated with this analysis of real-world data must be acknowledged, including missing data because of the unsolicited nature of the registry or self-reporting. Caution should therefore be taken when interpreting event incidence because of the nature of real-world evidence. Because of the deidentification of safety data, the primary diagnoses of patients who reported an AE were extracted from case narratives and may not fully align with the ICD-10 classification of primary diagnosis used for patient characteristics data. The ANC thresholds used in the diagnosis of neutropenia and agranulocytosis events, and the frequency and methodology of ANC monitoring, were defined based on the treating physician’s clinical practice and were not always reported, as per the nature of real-world data collection. In addition, the data presented here were collected before the update of recommended ANC thresholds in the USPI and may differ from current recommendations.9 Chelation history data were extracted from case narratives for all patients with an event of neutropenia or agranulocytosis; however, chelation history was not available for all patients. Previous DFP exposure may therefore have affected the observed lower rate of some AEs, including neutropenia and agranulocytosis. In addition, the start date and duration of events were not always reported. Therefore, we could not accurately calculate the time from DFP initiation to the occurrence of reported AEs. Although we extracted the number of patients who reported an event of neutropenia or agranulocytosis from the case narratives, because of the spontaneous nature of AE collection and patient deidentification, it was not possible to report the number of patients who reported other AEs, only the number of events.

In this real-world analysis, DFP twice daily was well tolerated in patients with thalassemia, SCD, and other anemias. No new safety concerns were observed compared with the phase 2 clinical trial of DFP twice daily or the well-established safety profile of DFP thrice daily.9,11, 12, 13,15,17,27 Neutropenia and agranulocytosis, considered as some of the most concerning potential SAEs associated with DFP, were rare, and all reported cases recovered. These data may provide reassurance to both treating physicians and patients on the use of DFP twice daily and support shared decision-making when selecting the preferred dosing regimen to optimize patients’ care.
