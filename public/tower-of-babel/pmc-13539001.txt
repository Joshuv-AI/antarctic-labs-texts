GATES: A Lightweight Tool Automating Pathogenic Variant Discovery From Raw Whole-Exome Sequencing Data
Authors: Nicholas E. Bambach, Julio C. Ricarte-Filho, Erin R. Reichenberger, Aime T. Franco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhole-exome sequencing is a widely used technology to identify pathogenic variants in cancer. Although sequencing itself has become increasingly accessible, downstream analysis remains computationally complex, presenting a challenge for many researchers. Existing pipelines lack integrated support for somatic and germline variant detection and still require significant computational resources.MethodsWe developed GATES (GATK Automated Tool for Exome Sequencing), a lightweight pipeline that automates data preprocessing, variant calling, and variant annotation directly from raw paired-end FASTQ files through a simplified command-line interface. GATES implements the GATK Best Practices for somatic and germline variant detection and leverages Ensembl’s Variant Effect Predictor for functional annotation, outputting the results in a human-readable tab-separated values (TSV) file. We evaluated the pipeline’s performance using the SEQC-II benchmarking dataset and demonstrated its application using a clinical sample harboring known pathogenic germline and somatic variants.ResultsGATES was run on a standard laptop and performed end-to-end variant analysis for each sample within a few hours. In benchmarking with SEQC-II samples, germline and tumor-normal somatic variant calling modes demonstrated high concordance with their respective truth sets. Tumor-only somatic mode showed decreased accuracy, consistent with expected germline contamination. GATES demonstrated high performance across various hardware configurations and compared to the established nf-core/sarek pipeline. GATES further successfully performed somatic and germline analysis of a >100X clinical sample in under 7 hours. Importantly, the pipeline accurately distinguished the known KRAS p.G12V and KEAP1 p.S338L as somatic and germline, respectively.ConclusionBy lowering the technical barriers to exome sequencing analysis, GATES provides a practical solution for pathogenic variant discovery for researchers both with and without computational expertise.

Introduction

Advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies have revolutionized our ability to identify genomic changes that underpin human disease. With techniques such as whole-genome (WGS) and whole-exome sequencing (WES), it is now possible to characterize the genomic landscape of an individual at a single-base resolution. This is particularly important in cancer research, as almost all cancers are caused by genetic alterations. The ability to profile these alterations has allowed researchers to identify mutations that initiate tumor development and promote progression. Some of these variants can be therapeutically targeted, leading to the emergence of precision oncology, where tumors are treated with molecular inhibitors based on their specific mutational profiles.

Although protein-coding exons constitute only 1-2% of the human genome, they harbor the vast majority of pathogenic variants. Indeed, around 85% of disease-causing alterations in monogenic diseases are found in the exome. 1 In cancer, with the notable exception of TERT promoter mutations, nearly all well-validated driver alterations occur within exons. 2 While recent studies have identified candidate non-coding driver variants, they appear relatively infrequent and many still lack functional validation.3-5 Given that pathogenic variants in cancer are almost exclusively protein-altering, WES has become a popular tool in cancer genomics and is often used in lieu of WGS. WES offers several advantages: decreased sequencing cost, smaller output files, less computationally intensive analysis, and most importantly, higher depth of coverage.6-8 Sufficient sequencing depth is of particular importance in cancer genomics as many tumors have significant stromal cell infiltration, which can confound the detection of somatic mutations. Additionally, the higher depth achieved by WES is critical for detecting variants present at low frequencies due to subclonal evolution.9-11 These advantages have contributed to the growing use of WES in cancer research over the past decade.

While the cost of sequencing continues to decrease and streamlined library preparation kits have simplified the generation of high-quality exome data, data analysis remains a substantial challenge for many researchers. Variant analysis involves multi-step workflows, numerous interdependent software tools, and careful parameterization to ensure accuracy and reproducibility. This process is computationally demanding, sensitive to implementation details, and requires consistent adherence to evolving best-practice guidelines. Even in well-resourced environments, managing software dependencies, computational infrastructure, and data provenance can be nontrivial, contributing to delays and inefficiencies. As a result, exome sequencing analysis is often centralized within specialized groups or cores, which may limit flexibility, turnaround time, and accessibility for research teams that use WES intermittently or outside of large-scale production settings.

Although many tools have been developed to combat these challenges, important limitations remain. Pipelines such as SeqMule, 12 Fastq2vcf, 13 and Kuura 14 automate general WES processing, but do not support tumor-normal analysis and the subsequent identification of somatic variants. Conversely, tools developed specifically for tumor sequencing data, such as IMPACT 15 and TOSCA, 16 focus only on somatic variant analysis and lack integrated support for pathogenic germline variant detection, despite the established role of germline variants in both cancer initiation and progression.17-22 These existing WES pipelines further depend on high-performance computing infrastructure and complex software stacks, limiting their portability and increasing the overhead required for setup, maintenance, and execution. Web-based platforms, such as Galaxy, 23 improve accessibility through graphical interfaces, but still require careful tool selection and parameter configuration. In addition, limited storage capacity, prolonged job queues, and constrained data transfer speeds restrict the analysis of large sequencing files. Moreover, sensitive data—such as raw human sequencing reads—are not suitable to be transferred to public servers like Galaxy. Collectively, these limitations highlight the need for a portable, end-to-end WES analysis pipeline that supports both germline and somatic variant discovery while remaining practical to deploy in diverse research environments.

Here, we present GATES (GATK Automated Tool for Exome Sequencing), a package that automates data preprocessing, variant calling, and variant annotation to enable end-to-end WES analysis in a few commands. GATES implements the GATK Best Practices24,25 for both germline and somatic variant discovery and leverages Ensembl’s Variant Effect Predictor 26 to identify potentially pathogenic alterations. Starting from raw FASTQ files, GATES outputs a human-readable tab-separated values (TSV) file containing filtered and annotated variants suitable for downstream analysis and interpretation. The package is designed to be lightweight and portable, allowing analyses to be performed locally on standard macOS and Linux laptop or desktop systems, while remaining compatible with more advanced computational environments. Here, we validate GATES using gold-standard truth sets and demonstrate its application in a clinical sample harboring both germline and somatic pathogenic variants. By reducing operational overhead while preserving established best practices, GATES provides a practical WES analysis solution for researchers across a wide range of computational expertise and resource availability.

Materials and Methods

GATES Pipeline

GATES consists of three main analytical modules: (1) preprocessing, (2) variant calling, and (3) variant annotation. Each module is run through a single terminal command that executes a set of shell scripts to analyze the inputted file. The primary output of one module serves as the input for the next, allowing each analytical module to be executed independently or sequentially for a given sample. The overall GATES pipeline is illustrated in Figure 1, and the following sections describe the components of each module in detail. Specific parameters and software used in each module are further described in Supplementary File S1. All software dependencies required for each module are distributed through the GATES conda environment.(1) Data Preprocessing. This module takes raw paired-end FASTQ files as input and outputs an analysis-ready BAM file. Reads first undergo quality assessment and adapter trimming using fastp. 27 The trimmed reads are mapped to the user-provided reference genome using BWA-MEM, 28 and the outputted SAM file is converted to the BAM format using SAMTools. 29 The aligned BAM file is then coordinate-sorted and duplicate-marked using GATK’s MarkDuplicatesSpark. 30 This tool leverages core parallelism that Picard’s MarkDuplicates and SortSam 31 do not support, speeding up processing time. Individual base quality scores are adjusted using GATK’s BaseRecalibrator 30 and known variant site files that are automatically downloaded from the Broad Institute’s Google Bucket (Supplementary File S1). Additional quality assessment metrics, including average sequencing depth and read alignment rates, are collected using SAMTools 29 and mosdepth 32 and compiled into a comprehensive MultiQC 33 report.(2) Variant Calling. This module takes analysis-ready BAM files as input and outputs an unannotated variant call format (VCF) file. Variant calling can be run in three distinct modes: paired tumor-normal somatic, tumor-only somatic, and germline. Both somatic modes utilize GATK’s Mutect2 30 for variant calling and leverage a pre-compiled panel of normals and common germline site database to increase somatic call specificity (Supplementary File S1). Variant filtering is performed using GATK’s FilterMutectCalls 30 after computing contamination and read-orientation bias estimates. The germline calling mode utilizes GATK’s HaplotypeCaller 30 and applies separate hard-filtering thresholds for single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertion-deletions (INDELs). Default thresholds are based on GATK recommendations and are described in Supplementary File S1.(3) Variant Annotation and Filtering. This module takes unannotated VCF files as input and outputs a table of filtered and annotated variants in the TSV format. Ensembl’s Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) 26 is used to annotate variants with information including gene symbol, variant consequence, amino acid change, Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) notation, SIFT and PolyPhen scores, rsIDs, and ClinVar status. Variant allele frequency and number of reads supporting each allele are also calculated for each variant. To prioritize potentially pathogenic coding alterations, synonymous and intronic variants are filtered out, along with variants that appear in either the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 34 or gnomAD v4.1.0 35 germline variant databases at a minor allele frequency exceeding a user-defined threshold (default = 1%). SEQC-II Reference Sample

Raw sequencing reads generated by the Somatic Working Group of the Sequencing Quality Control Phase II (SEQC-II) Consortium 36 were downloaded using NCBI’s SRA Toolkit 37 from the BioProject accession PRJNA489865. Samples were analyzed with GATES on either a MacBook Pro running macOS (Sequoia 15.5) with an Apple M2 Pro processor (10 cores) and 16 GB of RAM or an HPC cluster running Red Hat Enterprise Linux (v9.6). Samples analyzed on the HPC cluster were allocated 16 cores and 64 GB of RAM. The reference and exome interval files used in the analysis were downloaded from the NCBI FTP site (ftp-trace.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ReferenceSamples/seqc/Somatic_Mutation_WG/). Each replicate was run through the GATES preprocessing module and variants were called in tumor-normal somatic, tumor-only somatic, and germline calling modes. Paired tumor-normal variant calling was also performed with nf-core/sarek (v3.7.1) in the WES mode. 38

VCF output files were compared to germline and somatic truth sets generated by SEQC-II using vcfeval. 39 The number of true positives (TP), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN) were evaluated and used to calculate precision, sensitivity, and F1 score metrics as follows: Precision=TPTP+FP Sensitivity=TPTP+FN F1 score=2 × Precision × SensitivityPrecision+Sensitivity

Whole-Exome Sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from flash-frozen tumor and adjacent-normal tissue using the AllPrep DNA/RNA/miRNA Universal Kit (Qiagen). Adapter ligation and exome enrichment were performed using the Twist Bioscience Human Comprehensive Exome kit. Libraries were subsequently sequenced on Illumina HiSeq or NovaSeq platforms, generating 45M-50M paired-end 2x100 bp reads.

Results

GATES Pipeline for Automated WES Analysis

We developed GATES (GATK Automated Tool for Exome Sequencing), a lightweight package that automates both germline and somatic WES analysis for pathogenic variant discovery. GATES can be run from a standard laptop and includes three analysis modules: (1) preprocessing, (2) variant calling, and (3) variant annotation and filtering (Figure 1). The only user-provided inputs required for complete sample analysis include the raw sequencing FASTQ files, a reference genome FASTA file, an exome capture interval file, and an annotation cache file, which can be downloaded following the instructions provided on the GATES GitHub repository. GATES automatically downloads and organizes all additional files needed for analysis. While the speed of this process is dependent on internet connection, these files only need to be downloaded once, after which GATES can be run completely offline allowing for faster and more portable analysis. The primary output of the pipeline is a filtered and annotated TSV file containing potentially pathogenic variants. GATES conda environment solving times varied between Linux (mean: 445 s, SD: 43.8 s) and macOS (mean: 108 s, SD: 10.3 s) systems. A Docker image is also available for containerized execution. Instructions for installing and running GATES are available at https://github.com/FrancoResearchLab/GATES.

Benchmarking With SEQC-II WES Dataset

To assess performance and determine whether GATES can successfully automate WES analysis with high-quality results, we analyzed sequencing data from a paired tumor-normal reference sample created by the SEQC-II Consortium. This resource was generated by sequencing a triple-negative breast cancer cell line (HCC1395) and matched B lymphocyte normal cell line (HCC1395BL) using various technologies and platforms across different centers. 36 The dataset includes 12 high-coverage WES replicates for both HCC1395 and HCC1395BL.

Raw sequencing reads for each replicate were downloaded and analyzed using GATES. Each replicate was first run through the preprocessing module and variants were subsequently called in tumor-normal somatic, tumor-only somatic, and germline calling modes. Variants identified in each calling mode were compared to the SEQC-II germline and somatic variant truth sets for HCC1395BL and HCC1395, respectively. Precision and sensitivity metrics were calculated for each replicate across all variant calling modes. Variant calls from germline and tumor-normal modes showed high concordance with the SEQC-II truth sets across all replicates (Figure 2A). As expected, the tumor-only somatic mode displayed the lowest levels of precision and sensitivity. With no paired-normal sample to call against, these calls likely contain germline variant contamination. Indeed, approximately one quarter of variants called in tumor-only somatic mode were found in the germline truth set. Across all variant calling modes, analysis time increased linearly with sequencing depth (Figure 2B). Tumor-normal somatic calling consistently required the longest runtime, followed by tumor-only somatic calling, then germline calling. Notably, regardless of which variant calling mode was used, end-to-end sample analysis was completed within a few hours (Figure 2B). We next evaluated the effect of RAM and CPU allocation on workflow runtime. One tumor-normal pair from the SEQC-II dataset, sequenced at a depth of approximately 148X, was analyzed using multiple hardware configurations. Interestingly, while preprocessing time decreased substantially with increasing CPU and RAM allocation, variant calling time across all three modes was largely unaffected (Figure 2C). These trends are likely attributable to the memory-intensive read mapping step performed during sample preprocessing. To compare the performance of GATES with a widely used WES analysis pipeline, we performed somatic variant calling on five SEQC-II tumor-normal pairs using both GATES and nf-core/sarek. 38 Samples were analyzed on an HPC cluster, with each sample allocated 16 CPU cores and 64 GB of RAM, and the resultant VCF files were compared to the HCC1395 truth set. GATES significantly reduced variant calling runtime while maintaining equivalent performance, with no significant difference in F1 score compared with nf-core/sarek (Figure 2D, E). These results demonstrate that GATES provides rapid and automated WES analysis with accuracy comparable to that of an established pipeline.

Analysis of Pediatric Papillary Thyroid Cancer Sample

To further validate GATES and illustrate its utility, we performed WES on tumor and normal samples from a pediatric patient with papillary thyroid carcinoma. This patient harbored a known somatic KRAS p.G12V (c.35G>T) mutation and germline KEAP1 p.S338L (c.1013C>T) mutation, which were both identified by a clinical sequencing panel at the time of surgery. While KRAS p.G12V is known to be pathogenic, we recently showed that the germline KEAP1 p.S338L variant in this patient is also pathogenic. 40

To determine whether these pathogenic variants could be accurately detected by GATES, WES reads from the tumor and adjacent-normal tissue were analyzed through the preprocessing, variant calling, and variant annotation and filtering modules. The tumor and adjacent-normal samples were sequenced at an average depth of 139X and 168X, respectively. Somatic variants were called using both the tumor and adjacent-normal samples analyzed in tumor-normal mode. Germline variants were called using the adjacent-normal sample. The entire analysis process—including preprocessing of both samples, somatic variant calling, germline variant calling, and variant annotation—was run on a standard MacBook Pro and took 6 hours and 51 minutes.

After filtering out variants found in the population at a minor allele frequency >1%, GATES identified 5 somatic and 369 germline non-synonymous variants. Among the somatic variants, GATES correctly identified the known KRAS p.G12V (NM_004985.5:c.35G>T) mutation. Additionally, the KEAP1 p.S338L (NM_203500.2:c.1013C>T) mutation was identified exclusively in the germline variant call set and was absent from the somatic calls, demonstrating the specificity of GATES in distinguishing germline and somatic variants. Consistent with its design for pathogenic variant discovery, GATES correctly detected and classified both known pathogenic alterations in this patient—the somatic KRAS p.G12V and germline KEAP1 p.S338L mutations.

Discussion

Here, we developed GATES (GATK Automated Tool for Exome Sequencing), a computational package that automates WES analysis in a few simple commands. GATES implements the GATK Best Practices,24,25 allowing for simplified and reproducible somatic and germline variant analysis. From raw FASTQ files, GATES produces a human-readable table of filtered and annotated variants, enabling users to easily review and analyze the results.

The package’s modular design allows flexibility for users who may wish to only run certain components of the workflow at a given time or re-run variant analyses in different modes. For example, a user could run just the preprocessing module to generate an aligned BAM file and inspect read alignments in commonly used tools such as the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) before proceeding to variant calling. Once preprocessing is complete, these BAM files can be reused to run variant calling in multiple modes without reprocessing the raw reads, improving workflow efficiency and reducing computational time. Additionally, GATES is built on a set of modular Bash-based scripts, making the workflow transparent and the source code accessible. Users with more advanced computational expertise can modify the scripts to suit their specific research needs—for example, by adjusting filtering thresholds, swapping or adding alternative tools, or customizing annotation parameters to suit specific experimental designs.

Beyond its modular design, GATES is lightweight and can be run from a standard macOS and Linux laptop or desktop systems. This is particularly important for researchers who lack access to high-performance computing resources. Using GATES, we achieved full end-to-end analysis of a >100X WES sample in a matter of hours on a standard MacBook Pro. Importantly, after initial setup, GATES runs entirely on local software and does not require an internet connection. This enables the secure, offline analysis of sequencing data, which is often required when working with sensitive or protected genomic data. In addition, GATES is straightforward to install and only requires the user to have conda installed on their system. All programs used by GATES are automatically installed and managed through a dedicated conda environment, eliminating the need for users to manually install, configure, or resolve individual tools. In addition, a Docker image of GATES is available to facilitate reproducible and platform-independent deployment. This approach simplifies setup, increases portability, and ensures consistent and reproducible analysis across systems.

We validated GATES using both a curated WES benchmarking dataset and a primary clinical sample. With the SEQC-II benchmarking dataset, we analyzed 12 independent replicates and compared the GATES outputs with known somatic and germline variants present in the cell lines. While GATES demonstrated high performance in germline and tumor-normal somatic modes, tumor-only somatic mode suffered from high levels of germline variant contamination. As previously reported, this is an inherent limitation of using a traditional somatic variant caller, like Mutect2, without matched-normal samples.41,42 Therefore, while GATES supports tumor-only somatic variant calling following the GATK Best Practices, this method is not recommended for accurate detection of somatic variants. Many novel computational pipelines, including TOSCA 16 and UNMASC, 43 have recently been developed to limit germline contamination in tumor-only somatic calls and demonstrate high precision. We further compared the performance of GATES with the previously published nf-core/sarek workflow, demonstrating similar accuracy with reduced runtime. 38 Additionally, while nf-core/sarek still requires significant parameterization, GATES provides a more streamlined and automated workflow. Although validation using the SEQC-II dataset confirmed the technical accuracy of GATES, we further tested the pipeline using a clinical sample to both assess its performance with a more complex biological sample and demonstrate its application in a real-world research setting. Using this sample, GATES was able to successfully detect previously identified somatic KRAS and germline KEAP1 mutations. This confirmed GATES’s ability to distinguish somatic and germline variants and emphasized the need to consider both when identifying clinically relevant pathogenic variants.

While GATES provides a simplified framework for automated pathogenic variant discovery, the pipeline is primarily designed to support small-scale exome sequencing analyses. As such, we recognize it is not suited for large cohort studies or population-scale analyses that usually include hundreds of samples and require extensive computing resources, sophisticated quality control, and centralized data management. Additionally, GATES only supports short-variant detection and does not assess copy-number alterations or complex structural variants, which may be clinically relevant. Therefore, we envision GATES as a practical tool for small-scale WES studies, pilot projects, or exploratory analyses, where rapid detection and annotation of simple somatic and germline variants are needed. GATES provides an accessible solution for researchers to perform high-quality WES analysis with or without extensive computational experience and infrastructure. This makes GATES valuable both for laboratories with limited computational support or intermittent WES use and for experienced computational groups, where it can serve as a standardized baseline, rapid prototyping tool, or portable solution for exploratory analyses.

Conclusion

This study presents GATES (GATK Automated Tool for Exome Sequencing), a lightweight package that enables end-to-end automated WES analysis, from raw FASTQ files to filtered and annotated variants. With GATES, WES analysis can be executed on standard laptop or desktop systems. Benchmarking against the SEQC-II reference dataset demonstrated high concordance with established truth sets and performance comparable to an established WES pipeline while reducing analysis time. Application to a clinical pediatric thyroid cancer sample further confirmed the accurate identification and classification of known somatic and germline pathogenic variants. Although designed for small-scale studies and limited to short-variant detection, GATES lowers the technical barriers to WES analysis and provides an accessible, portable solution for pathogenic variant discovery across diverse research environments.
