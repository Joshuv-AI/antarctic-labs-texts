NPC1 trafficking via VPS41-dependent LAMP carriers regulates endosomal cholesterol homeostasis
Authors: Klevis Ndoj, Matteo Tantucci, Paolo Sanza, Kristian Zubak, Giorgia Marodin, Jenina Kingma, Felix Snijder, Tineke Veenendaal, Daniel L. Kober, Noam Zelcer, Judith Klumperman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceNiemann–Pick protein 1 (NPC1) is a lysosomal cholesterol transporter that is critical for cholesterol egress from lysosomes. Mutations in the NPC1 gene results in lysosomal cholesterol accumulation and development of the lysosomal storage disorder Niemann–Pick type C, a progressive neurodegenerative disease. To execute its function, NPC1 must reach the lysosome, yet the cellular trafficking itinerary responsible for this is not fully established. In this study, we identify a route for NPC1 to reach the lysosome directly from the Golgi via small membrane vesicle, so called “Lysosomal Associated Membrane Protein (LAMP) carriers.” This trafficking of NPC1 via LAMP carriers is dependent on Vacuolar Protein Sorting-associated protein 41 (VPS41) and is important for maintaining cellular cholesterol homeostasis.

Mammalian cells acquire exogenous cholesterol via the LDL–receptor pathway (1). LDL is transported to late endosome/lysosome (LE/LY) compartments, where the coordinated action of Niemann-Pick type 1 and 2 (NPC1 and NPC2, respectively) promotes cholesterol egress (2–6). Mutations in these NPC genes are the cause of Niemann–Pick type C (NPC) disease (OMIM# 257220), an autosomal recessive lysosomal cholesterol storage disorder characterized by progressive neurodegeneration (7–9). Mutations in NPC1 account for the majority of cases (~95%), with the remaining ~5% attributed to mutations in NPC2 (8, 10, 11). At large, the mutations identified in NPC1 lead to misfolding and mistrafficking of the encoded protein, often resulting in its early endoplasmic reticulum-associated degradation or mislocalization (9, 12). Nevertheless, many NPC1 mutant proteins retain their function, and delivery of even a small fraction of these mutant proteins to the LE/LY compartment is able to support cholesterol egress and overcome its accumulation (13–15), highlighting the importance of understanding the cellular itinerary that NPC1 follows.

To fulfill their function in cellular cholesterol homeostasis, NPC proteins must reach the LE/LY compartments. While NPC2, a luminal soluble protein, is modified by the addition of a mannose-6-phosphate (M6P) group and relies on the M6P receptor (MPR) to reach LE/LY (16), the cellular itinerary followed by NPC1 is less clear. NPC1 is a 13 transmembrane domain protein that contains a C-terminal di-leucine motif (3, 9, 17). This motif often acts as a sorting signal for targeting of membrane proteins to endocytic compartments (18). However, this signal is not sufficient to route NPC1 to LE/LY (19). A series of studies in yeast and mammalian models, often relying on overexpression studies, have defined several proteins required for NPC1 trafficking to LE/LY. These include an adaptor protein complex 3 (AP-3)–dependent pathway, which is also implicated in trafficking of cargos like LAMP proteins to the lysosomes (20) and more recently adaptor protein 1 (AP-1) (21). Accordingly, deficiency of AP-1 in mouse fibroblasts (MEF) resulted in an NPC-like phenotype, yet the relative contribution of impaired NPC1 and NPC2 trafficking in this setting is not resolved.

Previous studies in our lab have pointed toward a specific role of the Vacuolar Protein Sorting-Associated Protein 41 (VPS41) in delivery of newly synthesized lysosomal membrane proteins to LE/LY (22–24). VPS41, alongside VPS39, is a unique component of the homotypic fusion and vacuole protein sorting (HOPS) tethering complex, which is required for fusion of late endosomes and autophagosomes with lysosomal compartments, and hence for delivery of degradative lysosomal-destined cargo (24–27). We have identified a cellular trafficking route able to deliver newly synthesized biosynthetic cargo directly from the trans Golgi network (TGN) to LE/LY (23). This pathway governs the trafficking of a specialized subset of biosynthetic vesicles, which we referred to as “LAMP carriers” owing to their cargo LAMP1 and LAMP2 proteins. LAMP carriers have a typical diameter of 70 to 200 nm, are positive for LAMP1 and LAMP2, are devoid of a clathrin coat, and are negative for MPR, secretory albumin, and endocytosed BSA (23, 24). Their recruitment to LE/LY relies on the interaction of the GTPase ARL8B present on LAMP carriers and VPS41 present on LE/LY, which subsequently facilitates HOPS-dependent membrane fusion (23).

In the present study, we investigated the endo-lysosomal targeting of endogenous NPC1 in human cells. We identify NPC1 as a cargo of LAMP carriers and show that VPS41 is essential for proper trafficking of NPC1 to LE/LY via this pathway and for subsequent LE/LY cholesterol egress. Our study therefore defines LAMP carriers as a physiologically relevant VPS41-dependent route for delivery of NPC1 to LE/LY.

Results

Development and Characterization of Genome-Engineered HeLa-NPC1mNeon Cells.

Exploring the cellular distribution and trafficking of NPC1 has largely relied on the use of heterologous expression models. To circumvent this requirement and facilitate studying the endogenous trafficking of NPC1, we applied homology-direct-repair (HDR)-based CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome engineering to develop HeLa cells in which the C-terminus of NPC1 was fused in-frame with the bright fluorescent protein mNeonGreen (Fig. 1A) (28, 29). Genomic DNA was isolated from multiple independent engineered clones and integration of mNeonGreen was confirmed by PCR and sequencing (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B). Introducing the C-terminal mNeon fusion did not disrupt production of NPC1, as we were able to detect endogenous NPC1 protein in parental and HeLa-NPC1mNeon cells by immunoblotting against NPC1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Consistent with the fusion with mNeon, NPC1 migrated at a higher molecular weight in HeLa-NPC1mNeon cells. Concordant with the genome engineering, NPC1mNeon was only detected in the engineered cells when immunoblotting against mNeon itself or by FACS analysis, and mNeon-immunoprecipitation enriched NPC1 protein only in the engineered cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D and E).

To rule out that introducing the fusion tag did not alter the gross distribution and function of NPC1mNeon, we initially addressed whether the protein retains its proper membrane distribution. Using a membrane fractionation procedure, we could demonstrate that NPC1mNeon retains its membrane residency in HeLa-NPC1mNeon cells and that this distribution is like that of nonedited NPC1 protein in the parental line (SI Appendix, Fig. S1F). NPC1 trafficking and localization are critical for its function. Previous studies, largely relying on heterologous expression systems, position NPC1 in the late endosomal/lysosomal (LE/LY) compartments. The HeLa-NPC1mNeon cells offer a tractable experimental model to critically evaluate the subcellular localization of endogenous NPC1 protein within the endo-lysosomal system (Fig. 1B). Analysis of colocalization of NPC1mNeon and established markers revealed limited occupancy in early endosomes (~20% colocalization with EEA1 and APPL1) (Fig. 1 C and D). Rather, endogenous NPC1 mainly colocalized with markers of LE (~80% with CD63), and LE/LY (~85% with LAMP1), and slightly lower colocalization with enzymatically active LE/LY (~65% with Sir-Lyso, which marks cathepsin D activity). Collectively, these findings indicate that the distribution of endogenous NPC1mNeon across the endo-lysosomal system aligns with the functional role NPC1 has in cholesterol egress of LDL-derived free cholesterol, as the acidic environment in LE/LY activates acid-dependent lipases that liberate free cholesterol for NPC1-mediated transport (30).

We then asked whether NPC1mNeon retains its function in cholesterol egress from LE/LY. Failure to properly reach this compartment, or loss of cholesterol transport capability would be anticipated to result in lysosomal cholesterol accumulation, as apparent in Niemann–Pick type C disease patients (31). To test whether this was the case, we stained control and genome-engineered cells with filipin, a fluorescent probe that specifically binds free cholesterol, in the presence or absence of the NPC1 inhibitor U18666A (32). In the absence of the inhibitor, filipin staining was predominantly present in the plasma membrane, as previously reported (33), whereas addition of U18666A caused a dramatic accumulation of cholesterol in LAMP1-positive LE/LY of both parental and HeLa-NPC1mNeon cells, as evident from costaining with the lysosomal marker LAMP1 (Fig. 1 E and F). Importantly, the pattern of filipin staining in HeLa-control vs. HeLa-NPC1mNeon cells was indistinguishable, demonstrating that NPC1mNeon protein traffics properly to the LE/LY compartments and that it retains its sensitivity to pharmacological inhibition and role in cholesterol egress. Taken together, these results support the use of the developed HeLa-NPC1mNeon cells for interrogating trafficking and function of endogenous NPC1.

VPS41 Governs Localization, Abundance, and Function of NPC1 in Mammalian Cells.

We recently described VPS41 as a major determinant of a trafficking route from the TGN directly to LE/LY in mammalian cells. This pathway mediates LAMP1/2 trafficking via specialized biosynthetic vesicles termed “LAMP carriers” (23, 24). As NPC1 predominantly localizes to LAMP1-positive compartments, we questioned whether NPC1 may traffic via this route in mammalian cells. To determine whether NPC1 and VPS41 localize to the same membrane compartments and potentially interact, we conducted coimmunoprecipitation assays using overexpressed NPC1 and VPS41. In this assay, we could demonstrate an interaction between NPC1 and VPS41 (Fig. 2A, lane 4), but not with the closely related cholesterol transporter NPC1L1 that traffics to the plasma membrane and not to LE/LY (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). While robust, this assay relies on the use of NPC1 and VPS41 overexpression. Therefore, to test whether this interaction also occurs in a physiological setting, we turned to proximity biotinylation. We developed HeLa-NPC1mTurbo engineered cells, in which endogenous NPC1 is produced as a fusion protein with biotin ligase miniTurbo (NPC1mTurbo), a promiscuous biotin ligase that labels proteins within a radius of ~10 nm (34). Using this model, we found strong biotin-dependent biotinylation of VPS41 by NPC1mTurbo (Fig. 2B, compare lane 2 vs. 5 and lanes 5 vs. 6, respectively). VPS41 biotinylation could not be solely attributed to spatial confinement in membrane vesicles with NPC1mTurbo, as in the same setting other (lysosomal) membrane proteins, among others, e.g., vATPase subunits, CD63, LDLR, SQLE, MCOLN1, and TFRC were not biotinylated (not shown). AlphaFold 3 modeling of the VPS41 and NPC1 interaction did not reveal a confident interaction interface (SI Appendix, Fig. S3); however, all the predicted complexes involved the cytoplasmic domains of NPC1, where mTurboID will be located, and the beta propellor domain of VPS41. These results demonstrate that VPS41 and NPC1 reside in close physical proximity within cellular membranes, suggesting that they colocalize in the same LE/LY compartments and supporting a potential role for VPS41 in NPC1 transport.

To test the functional significance of the VPS41-NPC1 nexus, we used CRISPR/Cas9 to ablate VPS41 in HeLa-NPC1mNeon cells. Depletion of VPS41 in these cells was associated with a marked increase in abundance of both LAMP1 and NPC1 proteins (Fig. 2 C and D), and in cellular NPC1mNeon-associated fluorescence (Fig. 2E). A similar result was obtained in A549VPS41KO cells (Fig. 2 F and G), HeLa-NPC1mTurbo/VPS41KO cells, and in epidermal A431VPS41KO cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B and C and not shown). This suggests that this is a general consequence of VPS41 loss, possibly due to activation of the TFEB/TFE3-dependent CLEAR network that has been proposed to regulate NPC1 and LAMP1 (35–38).

Next, we assessed the functional consequence of VPS41 deletion on NPC1 localization and function. We found that despite having elevated NPC1 abundance, cells lacking VPS41 had marked LE/LY cholesterol accumulation evident by filipin staining (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–C). This could be attributed to failure of endogenous NPC1 to reach LE/LY compartment in cells lacking VPS41. Intriguingly, the distribution of LAMP1 and NPC1 was similarly altered (Fig. 3A), showing a diffuse pattern of small-sized puncta, yet their colocalization remained largely unchanged (Fig. 3 B and C), suggesting that they may follow a similar trafficking route. Moreover, in cells lacking VPS41, the colocalization of NPC1 with Sir-Lyso, a probe for active Cathepsin D and a proxy for active lysosomes, was markedly reduced (Fig. 3 D and E). The same was also observed with a second lysosomal activity-dependent probe, Magic Red B, that stains active Cathepsin B (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B). These findings further support the notion that NPC1 trafficking to LE/LY is impaired when VPS41 is absent, leading to lysosomal cholesterol accumulation.

Lysosomal cholesterol egress is a critical determinant of cellular cholesterol homeostasis. Impaired egress leads not only to lysosomal cholesterol accumulation, as seen in the absence of VPS41, but also to compensatory activation of cholesterol biosynthesis via the Sterol Regulatory Element-Binding Protein 2 (SREBP2)-regulated transcriptional pathway (39). Accordingly, we found that ablation of VPS41 enhances expression of canonical SREBP-regulated target genes such as SQLE, HMGCS, SQS, and HMGCR and the abundance of HMGCS and SQLE protein (Fig. 3 F–H and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 D and E). The expression of NPC1 was also increased, in line with it being an SREBP target gene (40). In aggregate, these results support the notion that VPS41 is a critical determinant of NPC1 trafficking to LE/LY, and that loss of VPS41 results in an NPC1-like cholesterol phenotype.

NPC1 Arrival to LE/LY Occurs via VPS41-Dependent LAMP Carriers.

We have recently demonstrated that LAMP carriers require VPS41 for recruitment to LE/LY (23). This can be experimentally exploited to interrogate whether trafficking of a specific cargo follows this route, by using a VPS41-dependent recruitment assay (schematically depicted in Fig. 4A). In brief, this assay relies on a mitochondrial targeted VPS41 construct (mito-VPS41-HALO), which diverts LAMP carriers from endosomes to mitochondria, and hence the cargo they carry. To test whether NPC1 is recruited by mito-VPS41-HALO, we used HeLa-NPC1mNeon/VPS41KO cells, as this circumvents the confounding effect of endogenous VPS41 expression. Introduction of a control mitochondrial construct lacking VPS41 (mito-HALO) did not alter the cellular distribution of NPC1mNeon (Fig. 4 B and C). In contrast, targeting VPS41 to mitochondria (mito-VPS41-HALO) resulted in marked recruitment of NPC1mNeon to mitochondria, demonstrating the physiological relevance of this trafficking route. To assess whether this recruitment uniquely relies on VPS41, we also mitochondrially targeted VPS3 (mito-VPS3-HALO), a specific subunit of the CORVET complex. NPC1mNeon was not recruited to mitochondria by VPS3 (Fig. 4 D and E). These data are in line with our previous studies showing that recruitment of LAMP carriers is a unique property of the VPS41 subunit of the HOPS complex (23).

To determine the identity of the NPC1-containing membrane compartments recruited by mito-VPS41, we performed immuno-electron microscopy (immuno-EM) on HeLa-NPC1mNeon and HeLa-NPC1mNeon-VPS41KO cells, with or without coexpression of mito-VPS41-V5. Of note, in these experiments, we also cotransfected ARL8b-HA, a necessary adaptor for LAMP carrier trafficking and recruitment by VPS41 (23, 41). Since the mNeonGreen and NPC1 antibodies tested were not able to detect endogenous NPC1mNeon by immuno-EM (not shown), we resorted to using overexpression of NPC1-EGFP. By IF, we confirmed that mito-VPS41-V5 recruited NPC1-EGFP to the mitochondria (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B). Having confirmed intact recruitment, we proceeded to use immuno-EM to analyze the ultrastructural localization of NPC1-EGFP. In wild-type cells, NPC1-EGFP was found in the Golgi region and typical LE/LY compartments. In HeLaVPS41KO cells, NPC1-EGFP was still seen to some extent in LE/LY, which showed an altered, enlarged morphology due to the depletion of VPS41, but predominantly accumulated in small vesicles resembling LAMP carriers around LE/LY. The distribution of NPC1-EGFP in the Golgi region was not altered upon VPS41 ablation, and NPC1 was found in both the Golgi stack and in noncoated vesicles in the TGN (Fig. 5 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 C and D). Coexpression of mito-VPS41-V5 in HeLaVPS41KO cells lead to notable mitochondrial recruitment of LAMP carriers, as previously shown (23). Using the resolution of immuno-EM, we could colocalize NPC1-EGFP with LAMP1 in these 70 to 200 nm uncoated vesicles (Fig. 5 C and D), providing formal proof that NPC1 is a cargo of LAMP carriers. In summary, these findings point toward NPC1 being a specific cargo of the LAMP carrier pathway that is responsible for the direct delivery of lysosomal membrane proteins from the TGN to maturing endo-lysosomes.

Discussion

In this study, we developed genome-engineered cell models that facilitate the interrogation of endogenous NPC1 abundance, subcellular localization, and trafficking. Our most important finding is the identification of NPC1 as a cargo for the so-called LAMP carrier pathway that governs intracellular trafficking of lysosomal membrane proteins directly from the TGN to the LE/LY. As such, this study reveals a previously unrecognized route for delivery of NPC1 to LE/LY that controls cellular cholesterol homeostasis in mammalian cells (Fig. 6).

Graphical abstract depicting the VPS41-dependent trafficking of NPC1 via LAMP-carriers to LE/LY. Scheme depicting NPC1 trafficking to LE/LY via LAMP carriers. The current study identified a VPS41-dependent route for delivery of NPC1 to this compartment via the LAMP carrier pathway.

The multisubunit HOPS complex is a gatekeeper for lysosomal entry, as it is required for fusion of late endosomes and autophagosomes with lysosomal compartments (25–27, 42). Recently, we also unveiled a role for HOPS and its unique subunit VPS41 in biosynthetic transport to LE/LYs through so-called LAMP carriers. In this pathway, endosomal VPS41 recruits TGN-derived LAMP-carriers to LE/LYs, after which HOPS and SNARE VAMP7-dependent fusion can occur (23). The LAMP-carrier pathway is independent of AP1 and MPR, but critically depends on the small GTPase ARL8b, which is present on LAMP carriers and is required for their transport and binding to the N-terminal domain of VPS41 present on endosomes. In a recent study, we introduced expression of ectopic VPS41 as a powerful approach to recruit LAMP1-positive carriers to mitochondria and investigate their protein composition (23). In the current study, we show that upon mitochondrial relocalization of VPS41, NPC1 is redirected to mitochondria in LAMP carriers that by immuno-EM were positive for both LAMP1 and NPC1. This established NPC1 as a cargo of LAMP carriers, which is particularly important given that these carriers enable fast and spatially precise regulation of LE/LY membrane protein composition (27).

Building a functional lysosome is exquisitely dependent on the proper sorting and arrival of lysosomal resident proteins, and when disturbed has a direct impact on lysosomal maturation and activity (36). Accordingly, mistrafficking of lysosomal proteins is intimately linked with development of lysosomal storage diseases, the neurodegenerative NPC disease being a prototypical example. Our study highlights the complexity associated with trafficking of lysosomal membrane proteins toward this compartment. Both LAMP1 and NPC1 can travel to LE/LYs by several routes. They can reach the lysosome indirectly via the plasma membrane, using AP1 or AP3-coated vesicles (20, 21, 24, 43, 44), or directly via LAMP-carriers [(23) and this study]. The relative contributions of either of these pathways remain difficult to determine, as perturbation of one route may lead to compensatory upregulation of one of the others. Yet the clear accumulation of NPC1 in LAMP carriers in VPS41KO cells strongly suggests a critical role for the LAMP pathway in mediating NPC1 transport to LE/LYs. The accumulation of NPC1 in VPS41-deficient cells is likely the synergistic outcome of enhanced NPC1 transcription (Fig. 3 F–H), likely through TFEB and SREBP2 activation upon impaired lysosomal cholesterol egress (37, 38, 40), and decreased lysosomal degradation as a result of the inability of the NPC1-containing LAMP carriers to fuse with the endo-lysosomal compartment.

The redistribution of NPC1 to LAMP carriers in VPS41 knockout cells is accompanied by a marked accumulation of cholesterol in LE/LY, further supporting a critical role for LAMP carriers in delivering NPC1 to these compartments. Notably, depletion of other HOPS components, VPS39 (45) and VPS18 (our unpublished data), also leads to lysosomal cholesterol accumulation, which is likely explained by the HOPS complex being required for fusion of LAMP carriers with LE/LY. Previously, Anderson et al. explained the increase in lysosomal cholesterol in VPS41 and VPS39 KO cells by missorting of NPC2, due to a trafficking defect of the MPR (45). In agreement with this observation, our lab has recently shown that HOPS knockout blocks retrograde trafficking of CI-MPR from endosomes to TGN (26). In contrast to our findings, Anderson et al. reported no significant effect on NPC1 distribution. However, a limitation of this study was the reliance on heterologous expression of NPC proteins and the limited resolution of fluorescence microscopy. Using immuno-EM, we show here that NPC1 and LAMP1 retain their colocalization in LE/LY, but in addition accumulate in LAMP carriers that are in the vicinity of LE/LY, indicative for a block or delay in fusion in the absence of VPS41. Our data are also consistent with earlier studies in Drosophila, in which the homolog of VPS41, Light, is required for targeting of NPC1-YFP to the endo-lysosomal system (22). Hence, with the identification of NPC1 as cargo for LAMP carriers, we now reveal that this pathway is an important determinant of cellular cholesterol homeostasis.

Mutations in VPS41 are associated with the development of rare autosomal recessive Spinocerebellar ataxia (OMIM#619389) (46). Accordingly, neurodegeneration-associated VPS41 mutations impair HOPS function and are associated with diminished lysosomal-originating mTORC-TFEB signaling (38). These features share commonality with those observed in NPC patients, and also, it is therefore tempting to speculate that VPS41 may be a modifier of the NPC phenotype or that mutations in VPS41 may underlie the phenotype in molecularly undefined patients. Zebrafish, fly, and C. elegans models of VPS41 deficiency display altered trafficking of lysosomal membrane proteins (22, 47, 48). These models, alongside our ongoing effort to develop tissue-specific murine Vps41 models, will enable studying the role of the LAMP-carrier pathway in vivo and will facilitate defining the complete protein composition of LAMP carriers. In conclusion, our study establishes a trafficking route for NPC1 from the TGN to LE/LY that is VPS41 depedendent and relies on the recently described LAMP carrier pathway.
