A novel iliofemoral classification for assessing surgical difficulty in direct anterior approach total hip arthroplasty: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Zeshun Chen, Mukun Xiao, Long Chen, Jie Xu, Fenqi Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDirect anterior approach total hip arthroplasty (DAA THA) has become increasingly popular due to its muscle-sparing nature, but it remains technically challenging due to patient-specific anatomical variability. A comprehensive classification system for assessing surgical difficulty in DAA THA is lacking. This study aimed to develop a simple radiograph-based hip anatomic classification to estimate the difficulty of DAA THA.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed primary, unilateral DAA THA performed by a single surgeon from January 2021 to December 2024. On preoperative radiographs, the horizontal offset distance (HOD) and the vertical offset distance (VOD) between the lateral-most iliac crest and the lateral-most greater trochanter were measured. Restricted cubic splines and receiver operating characteristic curves were used to determine HOD and VOD cutoffs for difficult DAA THA and defined four subtypes (Type A: low VOD/low HOD, Type B: low VOD/high HOD, Type C: high VOD/high HOD, Type D: high VOD/low HOD). Perioperative, radiographic, inflammatory, functional, and complication outcomes were compared using one-way analysis of variance, chi-square and other statistical methods. Subgroup analyses were performed to assess generalizability.ResultsHOD and VOD showed weak, nonlinear inverse associations with operative time. Four subtypes were defined using the optimal cutoffs (HOD = 6.25 mm; VOD = 126.95 mm). Type A had the longest operative time and a less favorable perioperative profile, whereas Type C had the shortest operative time, less total blood loss, more frequent cups within Lewinnek safe zones, and better early functional recovery.ConclusionsThis novel iliofemoral classification stratifies DAA THA technical difficulty and early outcomes and may support preoperative risk stratification and surgical planning.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09903-w.

Introduction

In recent years, the direct anterior approach (DAA) for total hip arthroplasty (THA) has gained widespread attention and clinical adoption [1]. Compared with other surgical approaches, the DAA is recognized as a muscle-sparing technique that minimizes soft-tissue trauma, alleviates post-operative pain, facilitates functional recovery, and reduces dislocation risk [2–6]. However, the DAA remains technically demanding with a steep learning curve, because surgical exposure and intraoperative maneuverability are highly dependent on patient-specific anatomy [7–9]. Previous studies have suggested that anatomical factors, including acetabular morphology, femoral geometry, and soft-tissue tension, may influence surgical exposure and operative complexity in DAA THA [10, 11]. In addition, precise anatomical assessment is crucial for optimizing component positioning and offset control [12]. These anatomy-related challenges often prolong operative time (OT).

OT is a key indicator of both safety and efficiency in THA. Prolonged operative duration in primary THA is an independent risk factor for short-term complications, including surgical-site infection and venous thromboembolism [13–15]. Moreover, OT is a major driver of direct procedural costs in arthroplasty [16]. Therefore, preoperative identification of anatomic factors that may complicate DAA THA is clinically important for optimizing surgical planning and controlling healthcare resource utilization.

Emerging evidence indicates that specific features of the pelvis and proximal femur can affect the surgical process and clinical outcomes of DAA THA. For example, the spatial relationship between the greater trochanter (GT) and the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) correlates with OT, blood loss, and complication rates [8]. The femoral neck-shaft angle has also been implicated in the accuracy of femoral stem implantation [17]. In addition, translation patterns such as medialization of the femoral head center relative to its anatomic position can pose distinct technical challenges and require tailored preoperative planning [18]. Nevertheless, there is no anatomical classification system that systematically integrates the spatial relationships of key bony landmarks to enable preoperative assessment of overall surgical difficulty and early to midterm outcomes in DAA THA.

Accordingly, we propose a practical, radiograph-based hip anatomy classification. We hypothesize that this classification stratifies the surgical difficulty of DAA THA and is associated with component positioning accuracy, perioperative complications, and early to midterm functional recovery. Our goal is to provide orthopedic surgeons with an objective and straightforward quantitative tool to support preoperative decision-making in THA.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Fuzhou University Affiliated Provincial Hospital and is reported in accordance with the STROBE statement. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Approval No. K2026-01–010). We assessed 364 consecutive patients for eligibility who underwent primary, unilateral DAA THA performed by a single senior arthroplasty surgeon at our institution between January 2021 and December 2024. Patients were excluded according to the following predefined criteria: (1) history of prior ipsilateral hip surgery (n = 2); (2) severe congenital hip dysplasia (Crowe type 3 or 4), post-infectious deformity, or post-traumatic deformity (n = 13); (3) pathological fractures secondary to tumors or metastatic disease (n = 1); (4) incomplete clinical documentation or non-standardized radiographic imaging, including obvious pelvic rotation, pelvic tilt, or inadequate visualization of the relevant bony landmarks (n = 7); and (5) loss to post-operative follow-up (n = 3). The final study cohort comprised 338 patients included in the analysis.

Data collection

All patients were followed for 12 months. Baseline characteristics collected included age, sex, operative side (OS), body mass index (BMI), underlying diagnosis, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification [19], and Crowe classification for developmental dysplasia of the hip [20]. Perioperative variables recorded were OT, total blood loss calculated using the Gross and Nadler methods [21, 22], length of incision (LOI), transfusion rate, and length of stay (LOS). Peripheral venous blood was obtained preoperatively and on post-operative day 1 to measure C-reactive protein (CRP) and creatine kinase (CK) as markers of systemic inflammation and muscle injury, respectively [23]. Pain at rest and during active motion was assessed using a visual analog scale (VAS) at 6 and 24 h post-operatively. Functional recovery was evaluated with the Harris Hip Score (HHS) [24] preoperatively and at 6 weeks, 6 months, and 12 months post-operatively. Complications within the first post-operative year were recorded, including lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN) dysesthesia, superficial surgical site infection, periprosthetic joint infection, venous thromboembolism (VTE), periprosthetic fracture, dislocation, and revision for any reason.

Radiographic measurements

Preoperative and post-operative radiographic measurements were performed on standardized standing anteroposterior pelvic (AP) radiographs. According to the institutional radiographic protocol, patients were examined in the upright weight-bearing position, with both lower limbs symmetrically aligned in neutral to slight internal rotation and the patellae facing forward. On preoperative AP radiographs, two vertical reference lines were drawn through the lateral-most points of the iliac crest and the GT, respectively; two parallel horizontal lines were also drawn through the same points. The horizontal offset distance (HOD) between the two vertical reference lines and the vertical offset distance (VOD) between the two horizontal reference lines were recorded. HOD was assigned a positive value when the lateral-most point of the GT lay lateral to that of the iliac crest; otherwise, it was negative. On post-operative AP radiographs, cup inclination, cup anteversion, and leg-length discrepancy (LLD) were measured as described by Lewinnek et al. and Ranawat et al. [25, 26]. The absolute value of the coronal stem angle was used to quantify the magnitude of deviation from neutral alignment. The definition of each parameter is shown in Fig. 1. The Lewinnek safe zone (anteversion 15° ± 10°; inclination 40° ± 10°) and a stricter 5° reference zone (anteversion 15° ± 5°; inclination 40° ± 5°) were used as conventional radiographic references to assess the positioning of the acetabular cup across subtypes. All radiographic measurements were performed independently by two observers blinded to clinical outcomes using a picture archiving and communication system (PACS). Each observer repeated all measurements twice. Intra- and interobserver intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were > 0.90. Final values used for analysis were the means of the repeated measurements from both observers.

Surgical technique

Patients underwent DAA THA as previously described [27]. All procedures were performed by a single experienced arthroplasty surgeon, who had surpassed the learning curve [28]. Patients were positioned supine without routine urinary catheterization or traction. A 5–6 cm transverse incision was made using the hip-flexion skin crease as a landmark, with the majority of the incision lateral to the ASIS vertical line to minimize LFCN injury. The Hueter interval was developed, and the anterior capsule was incised and repaired. Acetabular preparation and cup insertion were performed through the main incision. Endoscopic assistance via a small auxiliary portal was used only when additional visualization was considered necessary during selected intraoperative steps. Cup positioning was generally targeted toward approximately 40° of radiographic inclination and 15° of radiographic anteversion, with minor intraoperative adjustment according to stability, bony coverage, and patient-specific anatomy. Femoral elevation, broaching, and stem implantation were completed through the same incision, followed by reduction, periarticular multimodal analgesic injection, layered closure, and no drain placement.

Subtype construction and statistical analysis

Scatterplots were first generated to depict the linear relationships among HOD, VOD, and OT. To explore potential nonlinear associations, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models with four knots were used to visualize the nonlinear relationships of HOD and VOD with OT. In DAA THA, OT is commonly used as an indicator of surgical difficulty [11]. Therefore, in the present study, we defined “DAA surgical difficulty” as an OT greater than the 90th percentile. To assess the robustness of the definition of DAA surgical difficulty, a sensitivity analysis was conducted comparing the surgical difficulty defined by the 90th percentile thresholds for OT and TBL. The association between these two definitions was evaluated using cross-tabulation and Fisher's exact test. Kappa statistic was used to measure the agreement between these definitions. The results showed a strong association between the two definitions p < 0.001) and a moderate agreement (Kappa = 0.37) (Table S1). Based on these findings, we proceeded with defining DAA surgical difficulty as OT in our subsequent analysis and classification. Separate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed for VOD and for HOD to predict surgical difficulty, and the optimal cutoffs a and b were identified by maximizing the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity—1). Based on these cutoffs, four anatomical subtypes were defined: Type A (HOD < b and VOD < a), Type B (HOD > b and VOD < a), Type C (HOD > b and VOD > a), and Type D (HOD < b and VOD > a).

Continuous variables are presented as mean (standard deviation). Between-group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables, and counts (percentages) with the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. When an overall difference was indicated, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted with Bonferroni adjustment of P values. Two subgroup analyses (Type A vs non–Type A; Type C vs non–Type C) were stratified by age, sex, BMI, operative side, ASA classification and Crowe classification, with interaction tests to assess heterogeneity. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.3), and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Radiographic outcomes

Post-operative radiographs showed no between-group differences in cup anteversion. In contrast, cup inclination, coronal stem angle, LLD, and the proportion of cups within the Lewinnek safe zone differed significantly across groups (Table 2). Specifically, Type A exhibited larger LLD and smaller coronal stem angle than the other three Types (P < 0.05). Notably, Type C had the highest proportions of cups within the 10° and 5° Lewinnek safe zones, significantly higher than in Types A and B (P < 0.05).

Inflammatory markers and clinical recovery

Preoperative inflammatory markers did not differ among groups (Table 3). On post-operative day 1, CK levels were significantly higher in Type A than in Type C (P < 0.05). Functional and pain outcomes were summarized in Table 3. Preoperative and 1-year HHS did not differ among groups. During early recovery (6 weeks and 6 months), Type C had significantly higher HHS than all other subtypes (P < 0.001). For pain, Type C had the lowest rest and activity VAS scores at 24 h, significantly lower than in Types A and B (P < 0.05). Discussion

This study develops and validates a novel hip anatomical classification derived from preoperative standing radiographs. We found that this classification effectively stratified the surgical difficulty and early clinical outcomes of DAA THA. Specifically, Type A anatomy was associated with a more difficult procedure and poorer early recovery, whereas Type C anatomy predicted a smoother operation and superior early outcomes.

Although several etiologic and morphologic classifications of hip osteoarthritis have been proposed, few systems explicitly incorporate joint structural deformities together with the technical strategies and challenges required to correct them [18, 29–31]. This issue is particularly salient in the DAA, where complex anatomy can obscure the correct internervous interval, promote unintended medial drift of the approach, and increase complications [32]. Prior work also suggests that the key determinants of DAA THA difficulty extend beyond femoral head medialization alone and include pelvic-sided morphology [33, 34]. Therefore, surgeons should recognize how deviations from native anatomy can increase DAA complexity to optimize preoperative planning.

Our classification centers on quantifying HOD and VOD between the lateral iliac crest and the GT. We observed that smaller VOD and HOD were independently associated with longer OT. Consistent with prior reports, vertically, Sang et al. [8] showed that a reduced coronal-plane vertical distance between the GT and the ASIS correlated with longer OT, greater blood loss, and higher complication rates. A high-riding GT has also been implicated in longer OT and worse Forgotten Joint Score post-operatively [11, 35]. Horizontally, Michael et al. [10] found that a wider pelvis or an ilium overhanging the proximal femur may hinder femoral exposure and increase technical complexity.

The rationale for this classification is supported by operative mechanics and regional anatomy. Femoral exposure is a major challenge in DAA THA [33]. Type A (small HOD and VOD) represents a “high and medial” configuration that medializes the proximal femur, increasing medial soft-tissue tension during traction and external rotation. Moreover, medial soft-tissue impingement can compromise femoral delivery and necessitate additional releases [36, 37], thereby prolonging operative time. In addition, the limited working corridor may increase instrument crowding and soft-tissue trauma [38]. In our cohort, Type A was associated with a higher incidence of wound complications and higher post-operative CK levels than other subtypes.By contrast, Type C (large HOD and VOD) is “low and lateral,” which enlarges the Hueter interval and increases space for retractor placement and instrumentation, facilitating femoral exposure and reducing soft-tissue tension [9]. A relatively inferior greater trochanter aligns the femoral neck axis closer to the incision plane, allowing more direct femoral delivery with less extreme positioning and shorter operative time. This configuration may also shift the working corridor away from vulnerable regional structures described in anatomical studies [39]. In our series, Type C was associated with less perioperative blood loss and reduced tissue trauma.Types B and D represent intermediate morphologies, combining one favorable and one unfavorable feature (vertical position versus medialization). For Type B, limited vertical clearance may mainly impair femoral elevation; in such cases, optimized limb positioning and a more complete capsular release may improve femoral excursion [40]. In Type D, relative femoral medialization may increase medial soft-tissue tension and reduce lateral access; therefore, careful retractor placement and stepwise release of restrictive soft tissues may facilitate safer femoral mobilization and stem insertion [37, 41].

During the learning-curve phase of DAA, complication rates are increased, and for low-volume DAA hip surgeons, adopting a more familiar approach appears more cost-effective [42, 43]. Given that Type A patients are more challenging with respect to exposure, retraction, and component positioning and carry higher perioperative risks, we recommend considering an alternative approach with which the surgeon is more familiar and that affords better exposure. If DAA is selected, careful preoperative imaging assessment and templated planning are recommended [44]. For surgeons early in the DAA learning curve, starting with Type C cases and gradually progressing to more complex morphologies may help reduce complications and revision risk.

This study has some limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective analysis in which all procedures were performed by a single experienced surgeon, which reduced surgeon-related variability but may limit generalizability. The performance of the classification may differ in settings involving multiple surgeons with different levels of experience and technical expertise, particularly for a technically demanding procedure such as DAA THA. Moreover, selective endoscopic assistance was applied when additional visualization was required, which may further limit the direct applicability of the findings to all conventional DAA settings, although the key exposure-related steps were otherwise consistent with standard DAA THA. Second, the classification was derived from two-dimensional radiographs and did not capture three-dimensional hip morphology or spinopelvic–femoral factors that may influence DAA operability. Third, outcomes primarily reflect early to midterm perioperative metrics and recovery; longer follow-up is needed to determine implications for long-term implant survivorship and late complications. Finally, subtype cutoffs were data-driven in a single-center cohort and require external validation in independent multicenter prospective studies.

Conclusion

This practical preoperative X-ray–based classification characterizes patient-specific anatomy and estimates DAA THA technical difficulty. Type A was associated with greater operative complexity and poorer early recovery, whereas Type C showed the most favorable perioperative profile. This novel iliofemoral classification may support preoperative risk stratification and surgical planning.
