Resource-state quantum RAM for fast and error-correctable queries
Authors: Francesco Cesa, Hannes Bernien, Hannes Pichler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Quantum devices can process data in a fundamentally different way than classical computers. To leverage this potential, many algorithms require the aid of a quantum Random Access Memory (QRAM), i.e. a module capable of efficiently loading datasets onto the quantum processor. However, a realisation of this building block is still outstanding due to its formidable resource requirements, which become even more demanding in quantum error-correction schemes. Here we show that the challenge of implementing QRAM can be entirely reduced to a state-preparation problem: since such resource-state is independent on the memory, our approach allows one to prepare it offline, opening the door to new design strategies. As an example, we introduce a heralded ‘QRAM factory’ which enables improved fidelities with high acceptance rate. More broadly, our results introduce the concept of resource-state QRAM: we study its performance in noisy settings, showing that it preserves the noise-resilience of standard QRAM, and discuss how it can be efficiently combined with quantum error-correction. Finally, we propose an implementation with neutral-atom hardware, where our analysis suggests that high-fidelity and low-latency queries can be implemented.

INTRODUCTION

Quantum computation (QC) promises algorithmic speedups for specific tasks1,2, fuelling innovation across numerous modern technologies. Progress has been achieved in developing quantum processing units (QPUs), witnessed by increasing system sizes, improved performances, and the incorporation of quantum error-correction (QEC)3–17. However, critical challenges remain: among these is the development of a quantum Random Access Memory (QRAM)18. This module is analogous to classical RAM, which enables fast import of data from a memory to the central processing unit (CPU). Specifically, when querying a memory location labelled by the address x, the RAM responds by furnishing the corresponding databit Dx. A number of QRAM designs have been proposed, typically envisioning specific hardware18,31–35, and more recently, small-scale prototypes were demonstrated36–38. However, realising Eq. (1) fundamentally requires a space-time circuit volume scaling with N, the memory size, which poses formidable practical challenges. Furthermore, while QRAM benefits of a remarkable resilience against errors18,39, QEC is expected to be necessary for large-scale implementations. Under this perspective, the challenge is exacerbated by the demand for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathcal{O}}}(N)$$\end{document}O(N) non-Clifford gates, which are particularly costly in standard QEC paradigms. Altogether, such practical and theoretical complexity hindered large-scale experimental demonstrations and raised debates on the feasibility of QRAMs, pinning bold question marks on the potential of quantum computers40,41.

Here, we develop a different framework for QRAM design, introducing the concept of resource-state QRAM. Specifically, we show that the complexity of implementing QRAM can be recast into a state-preparation problem. In our approach, fast and robust mid-circuit queries are implemented through simple Clifford operations and single-qubit Pauli measurements (SQPMs): as illustrated in Fig. 1c, this is achieved by consuming a previously assembled data-independent resource-state (RS) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$| \Phi \rangle$$\end{document}∣Φ⟩, which encompasses all the non-Cliffordness required by Eq. (1). Importantly, altogether such a protocol has no additional cost with respect to other designs, and inherits the exponentially enhanced fidelity of the celebrated ‘bucket-brigade’ QRAM design18,39.

Thus, the resource-state QRAM allows one to rethink at data-lookup problems in terms of the high-fidelity assembly of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$| \Phi \rangle$$\end{document}∣Φ⟩ in a QRAM factory, while the query itself can be considered cheap under many metrics (e.g., non-Cliffordness, entanglements cost, connectivity). Crucially, in the QRAM factory, one can now exploit offline operation through state-preparation techniques, opening the door to new design opportunities. Indeed, typically preparing a specific known state is simpler than applying a circuit to an unknown state42: this is a largely exploited concept in QC, with paradigmatic examples being the fault-tolerant injection of magic states42,43, measurement-based quantum computation44,45 or quantum repeater schemes46. As an example, here we develop a high-rate ‘try-until-succeed’ scheme for preparing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$| \Phi \rangle$$\end{document}∣Φ⟩: this allows us to boost the fidelity of queries in a way that would not be possible in the standard paradigm, showcasing how resource-state QRAM unlocks new design strategies and optimisations.

Finally, we propose and analyse an implementation of resource-state QRAM with neutral-atom arrays6–8. We find that dynamical atom rearrangement47 naturally combines with the QRAM factory, and also we note that, in a QEC setting, it would exploit optimally our Clifford query via transversal gates. This motivates our design in Fig. 1d, which complements the ‘zoned’ architecture of modern processors6–8 with a dedicated QRAM zone. The latter is continuously replenished by the parallel QRAM factory and supplies continuous queries. In this example, we show that the query and the factory rate match the clock-time of fault-tolerant operation, indicating that resource-state QRAM could be integrated with negligible latency.

Clifford query

We now show how the complexity of QRAM can be outsourced to the preparation of a resource-state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$| \Phi \rangle$$\end{document}∣Φ⟩, which we define on the way.

Our query construction is inspired by the concept of gate-teleportation (GT), which plays a foundational role in QEC42. While GT is typically only efficient for minimally non-Clifford tasks, we show that it can aid a highly non-Clifford subroutine such as QRAM. This is based on the observation that Eq. For fault-tolerant (FT) queries, in our construction, all operations are fully transversal in many paradigmatic settings, including the surface and CSS codes1. It follows that a FT query proceeds via SQPMs only, by leveraging measurement-based QEC52 e.g., within foliated53 or fusion-based54,55 schemes. Since our protocol is based on destructive SQPMs, we simply measure the physical qubits individually: similarly to Knill QEC56, the results reveal both logical measurement outcomes and syndrome snapshots. Altogether, the query parallelises all the operations we would apply to physical qubits, to logical blocks. As recently demonstrated6,7, this is achieved elegantly with neutral atoms by interfacing the blocks via atom transport.

Latency

While in principle the query runtime is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T}_{{{\rm{query}}}}\simeq 2\tau \log N$$\end{document}Tquery≃2τlogN, the actual latency of resource-state QRAM is also influenced by the interplay between Tquery and the factory rate TΦ. Such interplay, in turn, depends on the design of the QRAM factory; in our proposal [Fig. 4b], the most delicate step (in terms of runtime) is a system reconfiguration, which enables long-range gates. In Methods, we focus on neutral-atom processors6–8, where we show that coherent rearrangement47 allows one to implement such reconfiguration in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathcal{O}}}(1)$$\end{document}O(1) long-range moves. Particularly, this yields 7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T}_{\Phi }\lesssim 3\sqrt{6}T{N}^{1/4}+2\tau \log N , $$\end{document}TΦ≲36TN1/4+2τlogN, where the first term is related to rearrangement, and the second to SQPMs. Specifically, T is the time required for transporting an atom across the minimal trap distance. Considering current experiments47, we set τ ≃ 500 μs and T ≃ 33 μs. With these parameters, TΦ is comparable to Tquery, as the rearrangement time remains negligible up to N ≃ 224 (corresponding to ≃ 2MB of memory), which is much larger than current system sizes of single processors61. For 1 kB of memory, we find Tquery ≃ 13 ms and 1/TΦ ≃ 0.1 kHz, wherein only ≃ 13% of the time is spent on atom transport. Since TΦ ≃ Tquery, we can parallelise a query with the preparation of a new copy of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$| \Phi \rangle$$\end{document}∣Φ⟩ for the next query, resulting in an efficient cycle, not adding substantial runtime. Despite the overheads for syndrome extraction and distillation, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T}_{\Phi } \sim {{\mathcal{O}}}({T}_{{{\rm{query}}}})$$\end{document}TΦ~O(Tquery) also holds in the QEC setting (“Methods”).

Discussion

We now discuss the implications of our results, specially focusing on future directions to be addressed.

We introduced a QRAM design where all the query complexity is reduced to a state-preparation problem. The cost of QRAM can now be tackled independently in an ‘offline factory’ which does not affect the quantum state processed in the QPU, and is memory-independent. The fact that resource-state QRAM offers new optimisation opportunities is exemplified by our heralding procedure, but future directions can now further exploit heralding strategies and potentially design specialised QRAM purification protocols. The remaining online task, which is fast and has zero non-Clifford cost, is specially simplified in the QEC setting, where it can be implemented via transversal operations only. We also note that, while such considerations assume a resource theory where non-Clifford operations represent the dominant operational cost, the benefits of resource-state QRAM would likely persist in other cost models.

At a fundamental level, memory lookups will arguably be among the most costly subroutines of QC. Previous works pointed out that the overhead associated with QRAM requires careful evaluations of its role in quantum advantage40 and of its ‘opportunity-cost’41. In this respect, we note that resource-state QRAM motivates re-evaluations of opportunity costs in an algorithm-specific way. For instance, with resource-state QRAM, the cost can be invested even before the memory is specified, changing the cost allocation options with respect to previous approaches.

Beyond such theoretical aspects, developing QRAM is a formidable technological challenge. By analogy with classical RAM, a sound approach would be to consider QRAM as a separate module, featuring specialised design and hardware components, with high-fidelity interfaces with the QPU. Resource-state QRAM is well matched with such a ‘divide-and-conquer’ strategy, natively incorporating a distinction between the QRAM module (which operates as a factory) and the processor, and allows even for different hardware solutions for QRAM and QPU. As a physical QRAM realisation, we have specifically proposed an implementation with neutral atoms, where simulations suggest that our architecture could be efficiently integrated with low latency. This may be adapted to trapped-ion processors, which have also developed qubit shuttling62, but the general ideas behind our proposal can find application also in other platforms, including superconducting13 or photonic17 systems.

Comments

This protocol only features Clifford operations - the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathcal{O}}}(\log N)$$\end{document}O(logN) parallel BMs. However, the non-stabiliser RS \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$| {\Phi }^{(1)}\rangle$$\end{document}∣Φ(1)⟩ must be provided. The main complexity of V, specially for QEC (see discussion below), is therefore outsourced to the RS preparation. Crucially, this inherits the benefits of RS-based approaches in quantum information, e.g., ‘T − gate injection’42: we no longer need to apply V to an arbitrary input \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$| \psi \rangle$$\end{document}∣ψ⟩ during the algorithm (i.e., online). In contrast, we simply need to prepare an RS, which can be done at any time before the query (offline) in a dedicated ‘factory’ - allowing for efficiently exploiting, e.g., state purification and post-selected protocols.

Typically, GT is limited to relatively simple operations due to its intrinsic randomness: if we wanted to apply exactly V, to cope with Pm we would need to apply the byproduct correction VPmV−1, which is in general almost as complex as V itself. In fact, it is easy to recognise that (by definition of the Clifford hierarchy) there must be at least one outcome m such that VPmV−1 is just one layer below V in the Clifford hierarchy. The crucial insight in the rewriting above, is that when it comes to QRAM, we do not need to correct for the byproduct: to all effects, the random OHE VPm is completely sufficient.

Let the (potentially faulty) output of the QRAM be the density matrix ρD(ψ). Mechanism

At its core, this built-in noise-resilience derives from the strongly constrained error propagation within the circuit decomposition of V in Fig. 5a, where - analogously to the BB - the large majority of faults propagate in such a way to eventually disentangle from the final output. To understand how this occurs in our protocol, recall that our procedure is based on the destructive consumption of the RS \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$| \Phi \rangle$$\end{document}∣Φ⟩: all qubits are measured individually and disentangled from the rest, except for the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\log N+1$$\end{document}logN+1 output qubits. Crucially, by design, most of the entropy generated by faults is left in the measured qubits, and thus removed from the output. While we use feedforward of the (potentially faulty) measurement outcomes to decide the future measurements bases, standard concepts in measurement-based QC44,45,64 show that this is equivalent to the non-suppressed errors in the BB (see the SM).

Similarly to ref. 39, we prove a bound of the form 20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F}_{{{\rm{QRAM}}}}\ge 4{\left[\frac{1}{N}{\sum }_{j}{p}_{j}| {{{\mathcal{G}}}}_{j}| -\frac{1}{2}\right]}^{2} , $$\end{document}FQRAM≥41N∑jpj∣Gj∣−122, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\mathcal{G}}}}_{j}\subseteq {\left\{0 , 1\right\}}^{\log N}$$\end{document}Gj⊆0,1logN is a family of error-free classical addresses defined in the SM, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\{{p}_{j}\right\}$$\end{document}pj is a probability distribution specified by the error model. Crucially, the NOHE inherits from the BB the fact that 21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sum }_{j}{p}_{j}| {{{\mathcal{G}}}}_{j}| \simeq N-C\varepsilon N{\log }^{\alpha }N-{{\mathcal{O}}}({\varepsilon }^{2}) , $$\end{document}∑jpj∣Gj∣≃N−CεNlogαN−O(ε2), where ε is the error-rate, C is a combinatorial term, and α is the dominating exponent. Numerical analysis

Beyond the analytical discussion above, we certify the scaling of FQRAM via numerical simulations, demonstrating the expected behaviour and extracting the dominating exponents α. Despite the dimension of the Hilbert space scaling as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${2}^{{{\mathcal{O}}}(N)}$$\end{document}2O(N), specialised techniques39 allow simulating queries up to N ≃ 104. Our simulations are based on standard quantum Monte Carlo methods, wherein for all parameters we converge to a precision σ ≲ 10−5.

We consider several relevant error models, including standard depolarisation, biased Pauli errors (up to full dephasing), qubit losses, atom heating and phenomenological noise; we confirm Eq. Quantum error-corrected QRAM

Our proposed QRAM module overcomes several QEC challenges, which have long fuelled scepticism on the possibility of error-correcting (and thus scaling-up) a QRAM - and QC more in general40. The first challenge is the conjectured exponential error scaling. The second is the non-Cliffordness of V, demanding unbearable resources for FT implementations, as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathcal{O}}}(N)$$\end{document}O(N) non-Clifford gates need to be applied during the query.

In our QRAM design, errors scale polylogarithmically, and the query is entirely Clifford. Most importantly, we show below that, with standard QEC schemes, any operation can be implemented fault-tolerantly via transversal gates (TGs) only. Since our protocol is entirely based on SQPMs, measurement-based approaches52 to QEC are beneficial. A paradigmatic recipe is to pre-assemble a 3D stabiliser state which foliates the desired QEC code53. Modern approaches grow this RS during the computation, realising a fusion-based scheme54,55. For neutral atom implementations, this can be specially beneficial for correcting atom losses, e.g., from heating.

Fault-tolerant query

The query is entirely transversal on codes where, e.g., the following operations are transversal: (i) Either the CNOT or the CZ gate; (ii) Single-qubit destructive measurements in the X and Z basis. This is the case, e.g., of CSS codes and of the surface code. When these requirements are satisfied, for the query we only need \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\log N$$\end{document}logN parallel destructive BMs (implemented e.g., through CZs and X measurements), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathcal{O}}}(\log N)$$\end{document}O(logN) rounds of SQPMs on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathcal{O}}}(N)$$\end{document}O(N) logical qubits. Thus, there is no T cost associated with the query.

To further understand the advantage of our query scheme, recall that typically one would continuously extract the syndrome via expensive protocols, featuring many ancillas, several rounds of state-verification and repeated measurements1,56,65,66. Importantly, our procedure only contains destructive measurements of the logical qubits in the Pauli basis; combining the physical SQPMs outcomes also provides important syndrome information. This can potentially be leveraged to eliminate the need for redundant syndrome extraction, e.g., through Knill-type teleportation-based QEC56, or by exploiting concepts of ‘algorithmic fault-tolerance’67.

QRAM factory details

Preparing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$| \Phi \rangle$$\end{document}∣Φ⟩ is the most demanding part, both with and without QEC: any remarkable complexity of QRAM is now outsourced here. Our approach has several advantages: (1) The preparation does not directly impact the online query: once the main algorithm is running, any highly complex protocol is already executed, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$| \Phi \rangle$$\end{document}∣Φ⟩ is a resource furnished to the processor. (2) We no longer need to apply UNOHE to an arbitrary input: rather, we prepare a specific, known RS, independent on algorithm and memory. This is a much simpler task, as discussed above regarding the GT implementation of step (i). (3) Heralding procedures based on error-detection and ‘try-until-succeed’ can be leveraged, as discussed below.

Neutral atom blueprint

We now detail our implementation with modern neutral-atom processors, which are described e.g., in refs. 6–8. Here, the physical qubits are single atoms trapped in optical tweezers. Key to this QC platform is the possibility to coherently rearrange the atoms via the tweezers47, allowing for mid-circuit reconfiguration of the system connectivity. Recently, this allowed to probe logical error-corrected QC6,7, by harnessing atom transport for native transversal gates. This strongly motivates our focus on neutral atoms, as it naturally exploits our Clifford query. Moreover, atom shuttling combines surprisingly well with the QRAM factory, allowing for efficient, high-rate preparation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$| \Phi \rangle$$\end{document}∣Φ⟩.

Runtime tradeoff

In Eq. The first two both scale as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathcal{O}}}(\log N)$$\end{document}O(logN), but Tg is negligible, as in neutral-atom experiments47 the typical gate time (τg ~ 100 ns) is much smaller than the typical readout time (τ ~ 500 μs). The most crucial calculation concerns Tr. Following refs. 47,76, the time needed for moving an atom across a distance d scales as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T(d)={T}_{0}\sqrt{d/{d}_{0}}$$\end{document}T(d)=T0d/d0, where T0 ≃ 200 μs is the measured time for moving an atom across a distance of d0 = 110 μm with negligible loss and decoherence. Then, the fact that we only employ \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathcal{O}}}(1)$$\end{document}O(1) total rearrangements, none exceeding a distance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathcal{O}}}(\sqrt{N})$$\end{document}O(N), yields \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T}_{r}\lesssim {{\mathcal{O}}}(T{N}^{1/4})$$\end{document}Tr≲O(TN1/4), where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T={T}_{0}\sqrt{l/{d}_{0}}$$\end{document}T=T0l/d0 and l is the minimal distance between tweezers in the processor. In the SM, we derive the prefactor to be bounded by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3\sqrt{6}$$\end{document}36, resulting in Eq. In the SM, we show how this estimation is modified in a QEC setting, finding that a similar tradeoff holds. Similar overheads impact Tg and Tm. The rearrangement time, however, is only multiplied by a factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathcal{O}}}(\sqrt{d})$$\end{document}O(d): this is because (assuming a surface code implementation), distances incur a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathcal{O}}}(d)$$\end{document}O(d) factor, but the atom motion time only scales with the square root of the distance. It follows that the rearrangement impacts progressively less the time tradeoff between query and factory. Finally, TΦ now is also affected by a time overhead due to T − state factories; in the SM, we show that this only grows as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim \,{{\rm{polylog}}}\,(\log (\log N))$$\end{document}~polylog(log(logN)), and remains small with respect to all the other contributions.
