Molecular dynamics simulations reveal concentration-dependent blockage of graphene quantum dots to water channel protein openings
Authors: Yunbo Du, Yuqi Luo, Zonglin Gu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGraphene quantum dots (GQDs) have attracted significant attention across various scientific research areas due to their exceptional properties. However, studies on the potential toxicity of GQDs have yielded conflicting results. Therefore, a comprehensive evaluation of the toxicity profile of GQDs is essential for a thorough understanding of their biosafety. In this work, employing a molecular dynamics (MD) simulation approach, we investigate the interactions between GQDs and graphene oxide quantum dots (GOQDs) with the AQP1 water channel protein, aiming to explore the potential biological influence of GQDs/GOQDs. Our MD simulation results reveal that GQDs can adsorb to the loop region around the openings of AQP1 water channels, resulting in the blockage of these channels and potential toxicity. Interestingly, this blockage is concentration-dependent, with higher GQD concentrations leading to a greater likelihood of blockage. Additionally, GOQDs show a lower probability of blocking the openings of AQP1 water channels compared to GQDs, due to the hydrophobicity of the loop regions around the openings, which ultimately leads to lower interaction energy. Therefore, these findings provide new insights into the potential adverse impact of GQDs on AQP1 water channels through the blockage of their openings, offering valuable molecular insights into the toxicity profile of GQD nanomaterials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-77592-3.Subject terms: Computational biophysics, Biological physics

Introduction

Since the groundbreaking discoveries of graphene, carbon nanotube and fullerene1–3, carbon nanomaterials have attracted numerous research interests from many fields, e.g., electronics, optics, device, medicine, etc4–11. , due to their unique and outstanding properties12–15. Such extensive applications will possibly cause their exposure to the environment16, yielding potential detrimental influence17–21. In particular, in 2015, scientists have directly observed the existence of anthropogenic carbon nanotubes in the airways of Parisian children, which implies that human may be possibly surrounded by nanomaterials22. This event warns that we should pay enough attention to the environmentally exposed nanomaterial affecting human health.

The possible harmful influence of nanomaterials is commonly elicited by the direct interaction of nanomaterials to biomolecules, e.g., membrane, protein and DNA23–27. Pristine carbon nanomaterials have been widely demonstrated capable of entering into cellular membrane in both experiments and simulations, resulting in the cellular membrane damage (i.e., toxicity)28–32. Also, the direct touch of carbon nanomaterials to proteins and DNA can also cause the structural damage of biomolecules, leading to the possible toxicity33–36. Introduction of graphene nanosheet can inhibit the function of spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 and its variant37. Therefore, understanding the interfacial interaction between nanomaterials and biomolecules is of great important to our comprehension of the molecular insights of nanotoxicity.

Graphene quantum dot (GQD) is an important sub-membrane of carbon nanomaterial, which also gathers many research attentions from various fields. Particularly, the 2023 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded to three scientists for their pioneering discoveries and synthesis of quantum dots. Therefore, the investigation in GQD is expected to be the research hotspot in near future. However, researches regarding the potential toxicity of GQD show reverse consequences. On the one hand, some investigations demonstrate that GQD has low and even no toxicity. For example, Chong et al. used in vitro and in vivo approaches to explore the potential toxicity of GQDs to main organs of mice, and demonstrated that GQD has no obvious toxicity due to its s, even under multi-dosing situation38. Tabish et al. assessed the in vitro and in vivo dark toxicity of GQD and revealed that GQD shows biocompatibility and minimal toxicity39. However, some other investigations report the potential toxicity of GQD. Wang and co-workers explored the Zebrafish Embryo treated by GQD, and their findings confirm that higher concentrations of GQD can cause the remarkable hypoactivity of Zebrafish Embryo in the light-dark test, indicating the potential toxicity40. Deng and colleagues showed that aminated GQDs can strongly perturb the anticoagulant protein C (PC) pathway via activating most genes associated with complement and coagulation system, cell adhesion molecules (CAMs), and MAPK, generating the possible toxicity41. Therefore, a comprehensive evaluation of the toxicity profile of GQDs is crucial for a thorough understanding of its biosafety.

In this work, employing MD simulation approach, we investigate the interaction of GQD/GOQD to an important protein, AQP1 water channel protein, aiming at exploring the potential biological influence of GQD/GOQD. The AQP1 water channel has a significant function allowing the rapid transport of water across cell membranes in response to osmotic gradients, which is widely distributed in many organs, e.g., intestines42. Our MD simulations indicate that the blockage of AQP1 water channel openings by GQDs/GOQDs exhibits a concentration-dependent nature. A higher concentration of GQDs/GOQDs is more likely to result in the blockage of these openings. Furthermore, GQDs have a greater tendency to block the AQP1 water channel openings due to the hydrophobic interactions between the GQDs and the loops around these openings. Our findings reveal the potential toxicity of GQDs/GOQDs to the AQP1 water channel protein through the obstruction of its openings.

Method

The AQP1 water channel protein was showed in Figure S1, wherein this structure comprised AQP1 tetramer. Each AQP1 monomer contained a water channel, allowing the fast transport of water molecules. The AQP1 was initially placed in a POPC membrane. Then, four simulation boxes were constructed as shown in Figure S2, wherein the GQD or GOQD with five and ten numbers were randomly put on the AQP1 in the initial setups, finally yielding four systems, named as 5GQD/AQP1, 10GQD/AQP1, 5GOQD/AQP1 and 10GOQD/AQP1. The initial distance between GQD/GOQD and AQP1 was set to over 2 nm, aiming at avoiding the originally artificial contact. The GQD and GOQD models (including their corresponding force fields) were derived from a previous study43. We selected the size of GQD/GOQD according to the dimensional limit of quantum dot (smaller than 10 nm). In addition, considering the limit of simulation box, we chose GQD/GOQD with a diameter of ~ 1.2 nm as a representation. The combinations were dissolved into the water boxes, and then 0.15 M NaCl was added in each box. The AQP1 was obtained from protein data bank with PDB code of 1J4N44.

We performed the simulations with the GROMACS (version 2018) software package45 using the CHARMM27 force field46. The VMD software package47 was used to analyze and visualize the simulation conformations and trajectories. The TIP3P water model was adopted to describe the water molecules48. The temperature was maintained at 310 K using a v-rescale thermostat49 and pressure was kept at 1 atm using semiisotropic Parrinello-Rahman barostat50 (x + y, z). The long-range electrostatic interactions were treated with the PME method51,52, and the vdW interactions were calculated with a cutoff distance of 1.2 nm. All bonds associated with hydrogen atoms were maintained constant at their equilibrium values with the LINCS algorithm53, and water geometry was also constrained using the SETTLE algorithm54. During the production runs, a time step of 2.0 fs was used, and data were collected every 10 ps. Each system was investigated for 10 independent trajectories with each of 100-ns duration.

Result

The aquaporin 1 (AQP1) water channel protein (cyan ribbon in Figure S1) comprises a water channel highlighted by an orange dashed circle and typically exists as a tetramer44, consisting of four water channels. These channels connect the intracellular and extracellular environments, facilitating the transport of individual water molecules. Subsequently, the AQP1 tetramer is embedded into a POPC membrane, simulating the AQP1-cellular membrane complex. GQDs/GOQDs, in numbers of five and ten, are then randomly positioned above the complex as the initial simulation system, creating four systems: 5GQD/AQP1, 10GQD/AQP1, 5GOQD/AQP1, and 10GOQD/AQP1. Following ten parallel simulations for each system, each lasting 100 ns, we analyzed all trajectories. We have calculated the time-dependent RMSDs of AQP1 in all simulations. The root mean square displacements, RMSDs, (Figure S3) show limited fluctuations at the end of each trajectory, indicating that the simulations are convergent.

Figure 1 presents the final conformations of GQDs binding to AQP1 from ten parallel simulations of the 5GQD/AQP1 system. In all ten conformations, GQDs randomly adsorb onto the AQP1 surface. Notably, in some conformations, GQDs can adsorb at the opening of one AQP1 water channel, as depicted in Fig. 1a and d. Such covering by GQDs can block the permeability of this channel, potentially leading to toxicity, resembling previously reported toxicity of other nanomaterials to channel proteins via adsorbing to the channel entrance55–57. Except those binding to AQP1 surface, some GQDs can also touch the surface under the membrane and one GQD can insert into the membrane bilayer (Figure S4). In particular, the GQDs can also cover the intramembrane opening of AQP1 as shown in Figure S5, wherein GQDs can also generate toxicity via covering the intramembrane opening of AQP1.

(a-j) Final conformations of ten parallel simulations of 5GQD/AQP1 system. The GQDs are showed by gray spheres and the AQP1 tetramer is displayed by cyan ribbons.

Increasing the number of GQDs to ten, the results shown in Fig. 2 reveal a greater likelihood of GQDs adhering to the extracellular openings of AQP1 water channels, as seen in Fig. 2e, g and h, and 2i. GQDs often cluster by aggregating some monomers and then cover the openings of AQP1 water channels, as demonstrated in Fig. 2e and g, and 2i. This aggregation is attributed to the hydrophobic nature of GQDs, which can form clusters in solution. Notably, a higher initial number of GQDs increases the probability of blockage of the water channel openings, indicating GQD concentration dependence. This is reasonable, as more GQDs increase the likelihood of contacting the openings of AQP1 water channels. We also illustrated the local configuration of GQDs binding on the AQP1 opening as shown in Figure S6. Clearly, the GQDs bind to the opening of AQP1 channel, wherein the GQDs locate over the water chain inside the channel.

(a-j) Final conformations of ten parallel simulations of 10GQD/AQP1 system. The GQDs are showed by gray spheres and the AQP1 tetramer is displayed by cyan ribbons.

The contact probability isosurface and corresponding secondary structure of 5 and 10 GQDs binding to the AQP1 tetramer are shown in Fig. 3. It is evident that GQDs have a higher contact probability with the AQP1 extramembrane region in the 10GQD/AQP1 system compared to the 5GQD/AQP1 system, as indicated by the deeper red color and larger area. In addition, the contact probability of each residue by GQDs as shown in Figure S7 also shows the higher contact probability of 10GQD/AQP1 system than 5GQD/AQP1 system. Furthermore, the averaged contact area (Figure S8) also demonstrates that 10 GQDs has larger contact area to AQP1 than 5 GQDs. These confirm our speculation that more GQDs increase the likelihood of contacting the AQP1 surface. Furthermore, the primary contact regions are associated with the loop regions at the top of the AQP1 tetramer, often located around the openings of AQP1 water channels, potentially leading to the blockage of these channels, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

The contact probability surface of AQP1 protein in 5GQD/AQP1 (a) and 10GQD/AQP1 (c) systems. The contact probability secondary structure of AQP1 protein in 5GQD/AQP1 (b) and 10GQD/AQP1 (b) systems. The contact probability of each residue is averaged from all ten parallel simulations.

The dynamic processes of GQD binding to the AQP1 tetramer in two systems are explored by selecting two typical simulations, as shown in Fig. 4. In the 5GQD/AQP1 simulation, the interaction energy (i.e., vdW energy) gradually stabilizes at -617.7 kJ/mol at 34.5 ns, indicating stable GQD-AQP1 binding. In contrast, the 10GQD/AQP1 simulation shows a more complex process, reflecting the successively increased number of GQD adsorbed to AQP1 (Table S1). At around 12.5 ns, the interaction reaches a metastable state, with a vdW energy of -847.3 kJ/mol. At 33.5 ns, the vdW energy reaches − 1153.6 kJ/mol, followed by fluctuating interaction energy. Comparing these processes, we find that a higher number of GQDs leads to a more complex binding process, often resulting in GQD aggregation upon contact with the AQP1 surface.

Interaction energy between GQDs and AQP1 protein in two selected trajectories of 5GQD/AQP1 (a) and 10GQD/AQP1 systems. Some snapshots are plotted to show the binding conformations.

Figure 5 shows the final conformations of 5 GOQDs binding to AQP1 in ten parallel simulations. Interestingly, GOQDs do not adsorb to the openings of AQP1 water channels. Instead, they often adhere to the side surfaces of the AQP1 tetramer at the interface between AQP1 and the membrane. With 10 GOQDs, we observe two conformations where GOQDs adsorb to the water channel openings (Fig. 6). Again, the primary binding site for GOQDs is located at the boundary of AQP1 and the membrane. Thus, a higher concentration of GOQDs increases the likelihood of blocking AQP1 water channel openings, demonstrating GOQD concentration dependence. Comparing the interactions of GQDs and GOQDs with the AQP1 tetramer, we find that GQDs have a greater capacity to block AQP1 water channel openings than GOQDs. Similar to the situation of GQDs binding to AQP1/membrane, GOQDs can also adsorb to the surface under the membrane and insert into the membrane bilayer (as shown in Figure S9).

(a-j) Final conformations of ten parallel simulations of 5GOQD/AQP1 system. The GOQDs are showed by gray (carbon), red (oxygen) and white (hydrogen) spheres and the AQP1 tetramer is displayed by cyan ribbons.

(a-j) Final conformations of ten parallel simulations of 10GOQD/AQP1 system. The GOQDs are showed by gray (carbon), red (oxygen) and white (hydrogen) spheres and the AQP1 tetramer is displayed by cyan ribbons.

To elucidate the factors mediating this interaction difference, we plotted the electrostatic surface of AQP1, as shown in Figure S10. The electrostatic surface indicates that the loops near the water channel openings are predominantly white, indicating hydrophobicity. GQDs, being more hydrophobic than GOQDs due to the hydrophilic oxidation groups on the GOQD surface, are more likely to bind to these loop regions. Specifically, some hydrophobic residues (Table S2), including Phe35, Tyr37, Pro38, Ile 39, Ala47 and Val48, contribute significantly to the interfacial binding of GQDs and AQP1. Although the contact probability of GOQDs to AQP1 also concentrates on the loops near the water channel openings (Fig. 7), this contact mainly involves the GOQDs connecting to the center of the four AQP1 monomers, as shown in Fig. 6b, and does not necessarily block the water channels. Additionally, the contact probability of GOQDs with the AQP1 surface is much lower than that of GQDs, reflected by the lighter red color, which has been also confirmed by the residue dependent contact probability (Figure S7). And the averaged contact areas (Figure S8) between GQDs/GOQDs and AQP1 also confirm the larger contact of GQD to AQP1 than GOQD to AQP1.

The contact probability surface of AQP1 protein in 5GOQD/AQP1 (a) and 10GOQD/AQP1 (c) systems. The contact probability secondary structure of AQP1 protein in 5GOQD/AQP1 (b) and 10GOQD/AQP1 (b) systems. The contact probability of each residue is averaged from all ten parallel simulations.

The dynamic processes of GOQD binding to AQP1 are illustrated using two typical simulations in Fig. 8. Due to the charged atoms on GOQDs, the interaction energy between GOQDs and AQP1 comprises both vdW and Coulomb components. The vdW energy primarily contributes to the GOQD-AQP1 binding compared to the Coulomb energy, indicating that vdW forces mainly mediate the adsorption of GOQDs to AQP1. Furthermore, by comparing the interaction energies of GQDs and GOQDs binding to AQP1, we find that GQDs exhibit stronger interaction energy with AQP1 than GOQDs, suggesting that GQDs are more likely to adsorb to AQP1 and, thus, have a higher likelihood of blocking the water channel openings.

Interaction energy between GOQDs and AQP1 protein in two selected trajectories of 5GOQD/AQP1 (a) and 10GOQD/AQP1 systems. Some snapshots are plotted to show the binding conformations.

To support above conclusion, we further conducted an additional simulation of AQP1 in absence of GQDs/GOQDs (named as control simulation). The initial system (as shown in Figure S11a) is carried out a 100-ns trajectory. The water molecules can enter into the channel inside AQP1 protein (Figure S11b), yielding the water chain. The water density analysis (Figure S11c) also supports the water molecules across the membrane comprising AQP1 protein. Due to the absence of GQDs/GOQDs, the water chain inside the AQP1 links the water chambers at both sides of the membrane. On the contrary, the GQDs/GOQDs can cover the openings of AQP1 water channels, resulting in the blockage of the channels, e.g. as shown in Figure S6.

Although there is no experimental evidence directly demonstrating the blockage of GQDs/GOQDs to AQP1 channels, a similar experimental investigation has revealed that GQDs/GOQDs can cover the openings of some potassium-ion channels, yielding the potential toxicity55, which confirms the possible similar blockage of GQDs/GOQDs to AQP1 water channel protein. Membrane channel protein comprises various types, e.g., other water channels, potassium and sodium ion channels. We plan to extend this work to other channel proteins in near future, aiming at showing a whole profile of the potential toxicity of GQDs/GOQDs to channel protein. Our findings also suggests that oxidized functionalization on GQDs can reduce its possibility to blocking the opening of AQP1. This implies that surface functionalization with oxidation groups on GQD can alleviate the potential toxicity, which is an important approach for safer applications of such nanomaterials in biomedical and environmental applications, and is also demonstrated by the previous study of graphene58.

Conclusion

In summary, we conduct MD simulations to investigate the interaction between GQDs/GOQDs and AQP1 water channel protein, aiming to explore the potential biological effects of GQDs/GOQDs. The MD simulations suggest that GQDs can adhere to the loop structures near the openings of AQP1 water channels, potentially leading to blockage. This blockage can interfere with the normal permeability of AQP1 water channels, potentially causing toxicity. Notably, this blockage is concentration-dependent, with a higher concentration of GQDs increasing the likelihood of blocking the AQP1 water channel openings. Moreover, GOQDs exhibit a lower likelihood of blocking these openings, attributed to the hydrophobicity of the loop regions. Specifically, the loop regions around the AQP1 water channel openings are hydrophobic, making them more attractive to GQDs than GOQDs, as GQDs are more hydrophobic due to the absence of hydrophilic oxidation groups present on GOQDs. Thus, our simulations confirm the potential toxicity of GQDs to AQP1 water channels by blocking the normal channels’ openings.
