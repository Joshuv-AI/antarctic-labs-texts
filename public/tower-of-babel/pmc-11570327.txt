Bacteria vs bacteria; the future of antibiotics?
Authors: Muhammad Sohaib Khan, Kamran Ahmed Khan, Muhammad Jansher
Published: 2024

Respected Editor,

Multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria or superbugs are a well-established alarming problem in the contemporary world. The global fight against the COVID-19 pandemic, while essential, has inadvertently overshadowed and accelerated the proliferation of these superbugs. A notable report from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) underscores the magnitude of this issue, revealing that from March 2020 to October 2020, approximately 80 % of COVID-19 hospitalized patients were administered antibiotics. This spike in antibiotic use likely resulted from difficulties in distinguishing COVID-19 from community-acquired pneumonia when patients first present at a hospital for assessment for respiratory illness [1]. With a constant stream of intense selection pressures in the form of antibiotics, cases of drug-resistant bacteria inevitably surged, and health practitioners found conventional antibiotics less effective against them, with mortality associated with such cases rising [1]. Of particular interest in the realm of antibiotic resistance is MDR gram-negative bacteria, which pose one of the most formidable healthcare challenges today because of their tendency for human-to-human transmission and a limited number of treatment options. Interestingly, however, a potential solution to this specific crisis may paradoxically be hidden within their own ranks; Bdellovibrio Bacteriovorus, a Gram-negative, ubiquitous, obligate aerobic bacteria possessing a unique ability to prey on other Gram-negative bacteria [2].

Despite being discovered in the early 1960s, interest in its use against MDR gram-negative bacteria has been steadily rising. B. Bacteriovorus has already been shown to target the pathogenic and opportunistic bacteria belonging to the genera Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter, Klebsiella, Shigella, and Salmonella, amongst many others [3]. Remarkably, the predators maintain their ability to prey on the host cells despite the MDR status [4]. Now naturally, this approach of deliberately introducing new bacteria as predators in the human body is rightfully questionable and suspicious, concerning the safety of this particular species. However, playing devil's advocate here, there's a case to be made for these bacteria as although human-based trials remain an abstract concept, in vivo extrapolation of this predatory effect has been performed with no adverse effects. Treatment with B.Bacteriovorus against artificially inoculated K. pneumoniae, both the species planted intranasally, has yielded positive results, with the predator bacteria attenuating the pathogen burden in the lungs of rats [5].

As bacteria continue to evolve and drug resistance continues to pose a threat, it's prudent to explore such alternatives to combat this seemingly endless fight. Although there exist significant limitations associated with its use, B. Bacteriovorus, with its promising prospects, might just become a useful ally in combating the potentially disastrous problem of antimicrobial resistance. With superbugs on the rise, sooner or later, it becomes necessary to explore and endorse such alternatives in clinical life, including phage therapy, no matter how eerie and outlandish the concept sounds. This seemingly counterintuitive and unorthodox approach may open new paths in the field of medicine and may potentially be a practicable alternative to traditional antibiotics in our constant fight against bacteria. However, much remains to be seen with how the development of such alternatives proceeds and whether it becomes feasible, efficacious, and cost-effective in the future.
