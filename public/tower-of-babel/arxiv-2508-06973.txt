On Distributional One-Category, diagonal distributional complexity, and related invariants
Ekansh Jauhari, John Oprea

Abstract
. We develop the theory of probabilistic variants of the one-category and diagonal topological complexity, which bound the classical LS-category and topological complexity from below. Unlike any other classical or probabilistic invariants, these invariants are rigid on spaces with finite fundamental group. On Eilenberg–Mac Lane spaces, we identify these new invariants with distributional category and complexity, respectively, and use them to illuminate aspects of the behavior of the latter invariants on aspherical spaces and products of spaces. We also study their properties on covering maps, π 1 \pi_{1} -isomorphisms, H H -spaces, and closed essential manifolds, and consequently, obtain the first examples of closed manifolds beyond the real projective spaces on which the distributional theory disagrees with the classical one.

1.
Introduction

The subject of Lusternik–Schnirelmann (LS) category goes back almost 100 years.
The original idea was to understand the complexity of a space (such as a manifold)
by determining the least number of simple pieces that were required to
assemble it. This number (or nowadays one less than this number) is then
an invariant of (the homotopy type of) the space and serves the usual algebraic
topological function of distinguishing homotopically different spaces. Of course,
Lusternik and Schnirelmann defined category in order to bound the number
of critical points of any smooth function on a manifold from below, and this was essential for their proof of the fact that the topological sphere with any metric
possesses at least 3 closed geodesics. Fox is often credited with bringing
LS-category into the realm of algebraic topology and, in fact, here we will
use a variant of his so-called
one-category
. From the description above,
we see that LS-category is a “molecular theory” in the best reductionist
tradition of classical
19
-th century science — see
[
CLOT03
]
for a modern
approach to LS-category. In the last 20 years or so,
a distinct, but intertwined, theory of
topological complexity
has been
developed by Michael Farber
[
Fa03
]
and (now) a host of others. This theory has its
roots in understanding how difficult the motion planning problem in robotics
is for various configuration spaces. This, too, is a molecular theory. Even from
the vague descriptions we have just given, it should be apparent that
calculating LS-category and topological complexity is a very hard thing
to do for most topological spaces, so any approximating invariants we can find for them prove to be useful (and sometimes essential).

Recently, some new homotopy invariants of Lusternik–Schnirelmann type have been introduced in
[
DJ24
,
KW24
]
.
They are called
distributional category
in
[
DJ24
]
and
analog category
in
[
KW24
]
,
and they provide new lower bounds for LS-category. Both approaches define invariants in terms
of probability measures on spaces, but different topologies are used in the definitions.
In many cases, it has now been shown that the two notions agree. Here, we shall always refer to
distributional category and use the notation
\dcat(X)
for the distributional category of a space
X
, but we will also refer to
[
KW24
]
for various results when they apply.
Distributional category considers probability measures on the based path
space
P_{0}(X)
which lie in a single fiber of the evaluation map to
X
. Of course,
the fiber of this map is the loop space
\Omega X
, and a probability measure here is a relatively complicated thing. Especially, in the original formulation of
distributional category (and topological complexity)
[
DJ24
,
Ja25a
]
, we see a collection of
dynamical states (i.e., paths) with associated probabilities (adding to one, of course)
which all collapse to a designated final state, and without carrying the analogy
too far (we hope), this is reminiscent of the Feynman path-integral approach to
Quantum Mechanics. Certainly, we have gone too far here, but the idea of a kind
of superposition of categorical states is not too outlandish. Furthermore,
at present, there does not seem to be a way of molecularizing the theory by
cutting up a given space into “atoms”. In this sense, the theories of distributional category
and distributional topological complexity are holistic theories rather than reductionist ones.

In this paper, we go a step further by “discretizing” in a well-defined sense.
For a space
X
, we define a new invariant called the
distributional one-category
,
denoted
\dcat_{1}(X)
, that instead considers
probability measures on the universal cover
q\colon\widetilde{X}\to X
that
lie in a single fiber of
q
. Of course, the advantage here is that the fiber of a covering is discrete, and this
entails all sorts of simplifying factors. We prove
that
\dcat_{1}
is a lower bound for
\dcat
and, in various cases, is equal
to
\dcat
. Because we work with
discrete fibers, we can be rather explicit in our proofs, and we hope this
makes the subject more transparent.

As we show in this paper,
\dcat_{1}
turns out to be a very useful tool in understanding other distributional invariants. For instance,
\dcat_{1}
helps us obtain new examples of manifolds (namely, some Lens spaces and projective product spaces) on which the distributional invariants differ from their classical counterparts. Also, it helps disprove a product inequality for the distributional invariants; the question of the existence of such an inequality (whose analogue in the classical theory exists and is very useful for computations) has been open since the inception of these new invariants. Furthermore, owing to its discrete nature,
\dcat_{1}
offers relatively simpler proofs to some key results in the theory of distributional category of aspherical spaces. These features of
\dcat_{1}
provide motivation to develop further the theory of distributional one-category and related complexity-type invariants.

Structure of the paper

This paper is organized as follows. After some background material in Section
2
, we formally define distributional one-category (
\dcat_{1}
) in Section
3
and elucidate its essential
properties. In particular, we show that for a covering map

, we have

\dcat_{1}(Y)\leq\dcat_{1}(X)
. In addition, we show that for a discrete group

\dcat_{1}(\pi)=\dcat(\pi)
, where
\dcat
is the previously defined

of

]

from a simpler viewpoint.

In Section

, we describe cases where

, and look at possibilities for
\dcat_{1}(X)
and
\dcat_{1}(\pi_{1}(X))
to be arbitrarily far apart. Moreover, we extend a result of
[
KW24
]

by showing that if
\pi_{1}(X)
is finite for a space

, then
\dcat_{1}(X)\leq|\pi_{1}(X)|-1
. The powerful influence of the fundamental group on

is also
displayed by the fact that
\dcat_{1}(X)=2
for any space

whose fundamental

In Section

, we use
\dcat_{1}
to revisit results about
\dcat(\pi)
for
\pi
either a finite group or a torsion-free discrete group. In the latter case, we show an Eilenberg–Ganea-type equality
\dcat_{1}(\pi)=\cd(\pi)
in the spirit of
[

]
. While these results are
known, we believe that the
\dcat_{1}

In Section

, we study

]
and explore cases where

for closed manifolds

-manifolds having torsion-free fundamental group. This section recovers similar results on
\dcat
of some essential manifolds from
[
Ja25b
]
.

Section
7
introduces the

of a space

and serves as a lower bound to the

of

]

and analyze the relationship between the diagonal distributional complexity of a space and its discrete fundamental group. As a bonus, we also show that a product inequality

We end this paper in Section

of finite-dimensional CW complexes beyond

,

]

Notations and conventions

All topological spaces considered in this paper are connected CW complexes. We use the term

for continuous functions. The symbol “
\cong
” is used to denote homeomorphisms, isomorphisms, and bijections, and the symbol “
\simeq

2.
Preliminaries

This section contains some facts about (distributional) sectional category.

2.A.
Sectional category

Suppose
f:E\to B
is a (Hurewicz) fibration. For each
n\geq 1
, the iterated join of
n
copies of
E
along
f
is

\ast^{n}E=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}e_{i}\hskip 2.84526pt\middle|\hskip 2.84526pte_{i}\in E,\hskip 1.42262pt\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=1,\hskip 1.42262pt\lambda_{i}\geq 0,\hskip 1.42262ptf(e_{i})=f(e_{j})\right\},

and the iterated fibration
\ast^{n}f:\ast^{n}E\to B
is defined as

\ast^{n}f\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}e_{i}\right)=f(e_{i})

for any
i
with
\lambda_{i}>0
. The smallest integer
n
such that
\ast^{n+1}f
has a section is called the
sectional category
of
f
, denoted
\secat(f)
, see
[
Sc66
]
. It is well-known that
\secat
is a homotopy invariant of fibrations.

2.1 Remark
.

If
f:E\to B
is a fibration that admits a homotopy section, then
f
admits a true section. This follows from the homotopy lifting property of
f
, see, for example,
[
CLOT03
, Exercise 1.22]
. So, in this paper, we will use the term “section” for both a homotopy section and a true section of a given fibration since one can be obtained from the other. Similarly, since a lift of a fibration
f
along a map
g:A\to B
is essentially a section of the pullback fibration
g^{*}f
, we will use the term “lift” for both a homotopy lift as well as a true lift of a given fibration along a given map.

If
E=P_{0}(X)
is a based path space and
f
is the evaluation-at-zero map, defined as
e^{X}(\phi)=\phi(0)
, then the least
n
such that
\ast^{n+1}e^{X}
admits a section is called the
Lusternik–Schnirelmann category
of
X
, denoted
\cat(X)
. More generally, for a map
f:Y\to X
, the least
n
such that
f
has a lift along

is called the
Lusternik–Schnirelmann category
of
f
, denoted
\cat(f)
. Clearly,
\cat(X)=\cat(1_{X})
. Since
\cat
is a homotopy invariant and the
homotopy type of an Eilenberg–Mac Lane space

is determined by
\pi
, we
may use the notation
\cat(K(\pi,1))=\cat(\pi)
.

If
E=P(X)
is the free path space and
f
is the fibration
\text{ev}_{m}^{X}\colon P(X)\to X^{m}
defined as

\text{ev}_{m}^{X}(\phi)=\left(\phi(0),\phi\left(\frac{1}{m-1}\right),\ldots,\phi\left(\frac{m-2}{m-1}\right),\phi(1)\right),

then the least
n
such that
\ast^{n+1}\text{ev}^{X}_{m}
has a section is called the
m
-th sequential topological complexity
of
X
, denoted
\TC_{m}(X)
, see
[
Fa03
,
Ru10
]
for details and the interpretation of this invariant in terms of robot motion planning.

For a discrete group
\Gamma
, its
cohomological dimension
, denoted
\cd(\Gamma)
, is defined to be the largest integer
k
such that
H^{k}(\Gamma,A)\neq 0
for some
{\mathbb{Z}}\Gamma
-module

. The Eilenberg–Ganea theorem (see
[
Br82
, Theorem VIII.7.1]
) implies
\cat(\pi)=\cd(\pi)
, so that the LS-category of discrete groups has an algebraic description. Moreover, for
\cd(\pi)>2
, the smallest dimension of a CW complex that is a
K(\pi,1)
(i.e., the
homotopy dimension
) is
\hdim(K(\pi,1))=\cd(\pi)
.

If

3.
Distributional one-category

In this section, we define a new invariant of distributional type as an analogue to the classical invariant
\cat_{1}

In the notations of Section

of

\cat_{1}(X):=\secat(q^{X})

(see also
[

\cat_{1}(f)=\secat(f^{*}q^{X})

.

If

is the universal covering, then the least integer

such that
q_{n+1}^{X}
has a section is called the
distributional one-category
of
X
, denoted
\dcat_{1}(X)
. More generally, if

f\colon Y\to X
is a map, then the least
n
such that
f
has a lift

in

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{X}_{n+1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q_{n+1}^{X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}

, denoted

3.A.
Basic properties

If
X
is not simply connected, then there cannot be a section of the universal covering map because the composition of the induced maps
on fundamental groups — which must be the identity — factors through the trivial group. Hence,
\cat_{1}(X)=0
if and only if
X
is simply connected. The same
argument says that
\dcat_{1}(X)=0
if and only if
X
is simply connected. Furthermore, we have the following.

3.2 Proposition
.

For any
f:Y\to X
, we have that

\dcat_{1}(f)\leq\cat_{1}(f).

Proof.

The space
\widetilde{X}_{n+1}
is homotopic to a quotient of the
(n+1)
-th iterated fiber join
\ast^{n+1}\widetilde{X}
by
[
KK11
]
(see also
[
Ja25a
, Section 8]
), and
\cat_{1}(f)
is the least
n
such that there is a lift of

along the fibration
\ast^{n+1}\widetilde{X}\to X
. Any such lift then induces a lift of
f
along
q_{n+1}^{X}\colon\widetilde{X}_{n+1}\to X
.
∎

3.3 Proposition
.

For any
f\colon Y\to X
, we have

\dcat_{1}(f)\leq\min\{\dcat(f),\dcat_{1}(Y),\,\dcat_{1}(X)\}.

Proof.

Since
\dcat_{1}(f)=\dsecat(f^{*}q^{X})
, the inequality
\dcat_{1}(f)\leq\dcat(f)
follows from Lemma
2.3
upon taking the covering map as the universal covering
q^{X}
.

Let
\dcat_{1}(Y)=n
. A section
s\colon Y\to\widetilde{Y}_{n+1}
gives a lifting
\widetilde{f}=f_{n+1}\circ s
with

q_{n+1}^{X}\circ\widetilde{f}=q_{n+1}^{X}\circ f_{n+1}\circ s=f\circ q_{n+1}^{Y}\circ s=f

and so
\dcat_{1}(f)\leq n
.

If
\dcat_{1}(X)=n
, then a section
s\colon X\to\widetilde{X}_{n+1}
gives
\widetilde{f}=s\circ f

with

q_{n+1}^{X}\circ\widetilde{f}=q_{n+1}^{X}\circ s\circ f=f

and so
\dcat_{1}(f)\leq n
.
∎

The definition of the universal cover
\widetilde{X}
as fixed endpoint homotopy classes of based paths in

X
gives a map
\omega\colon P_{0}(X)\to\widetilde{X}
with
q^{X}\circ\omega=e^{X}
(
cf
. Section
2.B
), which is a fiber homotopy
equivalence when
X
is an Eilenberg–Mac Lane space
K(\pi,1)
. The map
\omega
induces a map
\omega_{n}\colon P_{0}(X)_{n}\to\widetilde{X}_{n}
for all
n

with
q_{n}^{X}\circ\omega_{n}=e_{n}^{X}
.

3.4 Proposition
.

For any
f\colon Y\to X
with
X=K(\pi,1)
, we have

\dcat_{1}(f)=\dcat(f).

Proof.

In view of Proposition
3.3
, we must only show that
\dcat_{1}(f)\geq\dcat(f)
. If
\dcat_{1}(f)=n
, then there is a lift
\widetilde{f}\colon Y\to\widetilde{X}_{n+1}
with
q_{n+1}^{X}\circ\widetilde{f}=f
. Now,
\omega_{n+1}\colon P_{0}(X)_{n+1}\to\widetilde{X}_{n+1}
is a homotopy equivalence over
X
, so it is a fiber homotopy equivalence as well. Suppose
\omega_{n+1}^{-1}:\widetilde{X}_{n+1}\to P_{0}(X)_{n+1}
is a homotopy inverse of
\omega_{n+1}
. Then we have for the composition
\omega_{n+1}^{-1}\circ\widetilde{f}\colon Y\to P_{0}(X)_{n+1}
that

e_{n+1}^{X}\circ\omega_{n+1}^{-1}\circ\widetilde{f}\simeq q_{n+1}^{X}\circ\widetilde{f}=f.

Now, the map
w_{n+1}^{-1}\circ\widetilde{f}
helps us obtain a lift of
f
(
cf.
Remark
2.1
). This implies the inequality
\dcat(f)\leq n
.
∎

3.5 Proposition
.

Let
\kappa\colon X\to K(\pi,1)
classify the universal cover. For any map
f\colon Y\to X
, we have

\dcat_{1}(f)\leq\dcat(\kappa\circ f)\leq\dcat_{1}(Y).

Proof.

Up to homotopy, we have a pullback diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P_{0}(K)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K,}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q^{X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e^{K}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\kappa}

where
K=K(\pi,1)
. We now have the following homotopy pullback diagram (
cf.
the proof of Lemma
2.2
)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{X}_{n+1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P_{0}(K)_{n+1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q_{n+1}^{X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e_{n+1}^{K}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\kappa}

If
\dcat_{1}(\kappa\circ f)=n
, we have a lift
\widetilde{\kappa f}\colon Y\to P_{0}(K)_{n+1}
with
e_{n+1}^{K}\circ\widetilde{\kappa f}=\kappa\circ f
. Using the universal property of this pullback, we obtain a lift of
f
along
q_{n+1}^{X}
, and therefore,

\dcat_{1}(f)\leq\dcat_{1}(\kappa\circ f)
. By Proposition
3.4
, we have the equality
\dcat_{1}(\kappa\circ f)=\dcat(\kappa\circ f)
, so the first inequality is proven. The second inequality follows from Proposition

3.3
.
∎

For the categorical invariant
\cat_{1}(Y)
, we have that
\cat_{1}(Y)=\cat(\kappa)
, where
\kappa\colon Y\to K(\pi_{1}(Y),1)
classifies the universal cover, see
[
CLOT03
, Section 9.3]
. Here is the analogous result for
\dcat_{1}
.

3.6 Corollary
.

Let
\kappa\colon Y\to K(\pi,1)
classify the universal cover. Then

\dcat_{1}(Y)=\dcat_{1}(\kappa)=\dcat(\kappa).

Proof.

In Proposition
3.5
, let
f=1_{Y}
. We then get

\dcat_{1}(Y)=\dcat_{1}(1_{Y})\leq\dcat(\kappa)\leq\dcat_{1}(Y)

using Proposition
3.4
so that the conclusion follows.
∎

3.7 Proposition
.

For the composition
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle g}}{{\to}}Z
, we have

\dcat_{1}(g\circ f)\leq\min\{\dcat_{1}(f),\,\dcat_{1}(g)\}.

Proof.

Suppose
\dcat_{1}(f)=n
. Then we have a diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{Y}_{n+1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{Z}_{n+1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Z}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{f}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}

which shows that
\dcat_{1}(g\circ f)\leq n
.

Suppose
\dcat_{1}(g)=n
. Then we have a diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{Z}_{n+1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Z}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{g}}

which shows
\dcat(g\circ f)\leq n
.
∎

3.8 Corollary
.

If
f\colon Y\to X
induces an isomorphism of fundamental groups, then

\dcat_{1}(Y)\leq\dcat_{1}(X).

Proof.

We have a diagram

\textstyle{Y\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\kappa_{Y}}
\scriptstyle{f}
\textstyle{K(\pi,1)}
\textstyle{X,\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\kappa_{X}}

where
\kappa_{Y}
and
\kappa_{X}
classify the respective universal covers. By Corollary

3.6
we know that
\dcat_{1}(Y)=\dcat_{1}(\kappa_{Y})
and
\dcat_{1}(X)=\dcat_{1}(\kappa_{X})
. By Proposition
3.7
, we then have

\dcat_{1}(Y)=\dcat_{1}(\kappa_{Y})\leq\min\{\dcat_{1}(f),\,\dcat_{1}(X)\}\leq\dcat_{1}(X).

The above inequality also gives
\dcat_{1}(Y)\leq\dcat_{1}(f)
, which, by Proposition
3.3
implies
\dcat_{1}(f)=\dcat_{1}(Y)
.
∎

In particular, we get the following using Corollary
3.8
and Proposition
3.3
.

3.9 Corollary
.

If
s:X\to L
induces an isomorphism of fundamental groups, then
\dcat_{1}(X)\leq\dcat(L)
.

This corollary can also be obtained from Lemma
2.2
and Proposition
3.3
.

3.10 Remark
.

The (weaker) converse of Corollary
3.9
is not true. Indeed, we have
\dcat_{1}({\mathbb{R}}P^{n})\leq\dcat({\mathbb{R}}P^{n})=1
for each
n
by Proposition
3.3
and
[
DJ24
,
KW24
]
, and if

and

if and only if there exists a map

]
.

3.11 Proposition
.

If
i\colon A\to X
is a homotopy retract, then
\dcat_{1}(A)\leq\dcat_{1}(X)
.

Proof.

Let
r\colon X\to A
be the homotopy retraction with
r\circ i\simeq 1_{A}
. Let

\dcat_{1}(X)=n
with section
s\colon X\to\widetilde{X}_{n+1}
. We then have the following commutative diagram:

\textstyle{\widetilde{X}_{n+1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{q^{X}_{n+1}}
\scriptstyle{r_{n+1}}
\textstyle{\widetilde{A}_{n+1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{q^{A}_{n+1}}
\textstyle{A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{s\circ i}
\scriptstyle{i}
\textstyle{X\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{s}
\scriptstyle{r}
\textstyle{A.}

Therefore,

q^{A}_{n+1}\circ r_{n+1}\circ s\circ i=r\circ q_{n+1}^{X}\circ s\circ i=r\circ i\simeq 1_{A}.

The map
r_{n+1}\circ s\circ i
yields a section of
q^{A}_{n+1}
. Thus,
\dcat_{1}(A)\leq n
holds.
∎

Now, the homotopy invariance of
\dcat_{1}
can be justified.

3.12 Corollary
.

If
f\colon Y\to X
is a homotopy equivalence, then

\dcat_{1}(Y)=\dcat_{1}(X).

Proof.

If
g\colon X\to Y
is a homotopy inverse for
f
, then
f
and
g
are homotopy retracts of each other. So, the inequalities
\dcat_{1}(X)\leq\dcat_{1}(Y)
and
\dcat_{1}(Y)\leq\dcat_{1}(X)
follow from Proposition
3.11
.
∎

Corollary
3.12
can also be proven using Corollary
3.8
instead.

3.B.
Behavior on covering maps

We now aim to generalize Corollary
3.8
from isomorphisms to monomorphisms. To do that, we first look at covering spaces. In this paper, the symbol
\simeq_{*}
means homotopy rel endpoints for paths.

3.13 Lemma
.

Suppose
p\colon Y\to X
is a covering and
\omega
,
\sigma
are two paths in
Y
with
\omega(0)=\sigma(0)
and
p\circ\omega\simeq_{*}p\circ\sigma
. Then
\omega\simeq_{*}\sigma
.

Proof.

Let
H\colon I\times I\to X
be a homotopy with
H_{0}=p\circ\omega
and
H_{1}=p\circ\sigma
such that

H(0,s)=p(\omega(0))=p(\sigma(0))
and
H(1,s)=p(\omega(1))=p(\sigma(1))
for each
s\in I
. Here, we use the convention
H_{s}(t)=H(t,s)
. We have a lift

G
in the commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces I\times\{0\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces I\times I}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\omega}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}

Therefore,
p\circ G=H
with
G_{0}=\omega
and
p\circ G_{1}=H_{1}=p\circ\sigma
. We thus have
p(G(0,s))=H(0,s)=p(\omega(0))=p(\sigma(0))
and
p(G(1,s))=H(1,s)=p(\omega(1))=p(\sigma(1))
. Hence,
G(0,s)\in p^{-1}(p(\omega(0)))

and
G(1,s)\in p^{-1}(p(\omega(1)))
. These fibers are discrete and
G
is continuous, so for all
s\in I
, we must have

G(0,s)=\bar{y}
and
G(1,s)=\widehat{y}
for some points
\bar{y},\widehat{y}\in Y
. Now,
G(0,0)=G_{0}(0)=\omega(0)
, so
\bar{y}=\omega(0)
. Also,
G(1,0)=G_{0}(1)=\omega(1)
, so
\widehat{y}=\omega(1)
. Note that
G_{1}(0)=G(0,1)=\bar{y}=\omega(0)=\sigma(0)
and
p\circ G_{1}=p\circ\sigma
. By the unique path lifting property of
p
, we must have
G_{1}=\sigma
. Therefore,
G
gives
a homotopy
\omega=G_{0}\simeq_{*}G_{1}=\sigma
. Observe that
\sigma(1)=G_{1}(1)=\widehat{y}=\omega(1)
, so
\omega
and
\sigma
have the same terminal points.
∎

3.14 Remark
.

It follows from the technique of the proof of Lemma
3.13
that
\omega\simeq_{*}\sigma
is true again if
\omega(1)=\sigma(1)
and
p\circ\omega\simeq_{*}p\circ\sigma
.

Given a covering map
p:Y\to X
, we use the definition of universal coverings to get

\widetilde{X}=P_{0}(X)/[-]\ \text{ and }\ \widetilde{Y}=P_{0}(Y)/\{-\},

where
[\alpha]=[\beta]
for
\alpha,\beta\in P_{0}(X)
if
\alpha(0)=\beta(0)
and
\alpha\simeq_{*}\beta
, and
\{\sigma\}=\{\tau\}
for
\sigma,\tau\in P_{0}(Y)
if
\sigma(0)=\tau(0)
and
\sigma\simeq_{*}\tau
. We recall that
P_{0}(X)
consists of paths in
X
having terminal point
x_{0}
, and
P_{0}(Y)
consists of paths in
Y
having terminal point
y_{0}\in p^{-1}(x_{0})
. The universal covering maps are given by
q^{X}([\gamma])=\gamma(0)
and
q^{Y}(\{\alpha\})=\alpha(0)
. Of course, we know that
\widetilde{X}\cong\widetilde{Y}
, but we need the exact way this happens.

Define
P\colon\widetilde{Y}\to\widetilde{X}
by
P(\{\sigma\})=[p\circ\sigma]
. Clearly if

\sigma\simeq_{*}\tau
, then
p\circ\sigma\simeq_{*}p\circ\tau
, so
P
is well-defined.
Define
\theta\colon\widetilde{X}\to\widetilde{Y}
as follows. Let
[\gamma]\in\widetilde{X}
. Because
\gamma(1)=x_{0}
, there is a unique lift
\widetilde{\gamma}\colon I\to Y
to
\gamma
ending at

\widetilde{\gamma}(1)=y_{0}
. Define
\theta([\gamma])=\{\widetilde{\gamma}\}
. This map is well-defined
by Remark
3.14
since
\gamma\simeq_{*}\delta
says that the unique lifts
\widetilde{\gamma}

and
\widetilde{\delta}
also have
\widetilde{\gamma}\simeq_{*}\widetilde{\delta}
because
\widetilde{\gamma}(1)=y_{0}=\widetilde{\delta}(1)
. It is easy to check that

P\circ\theta([\gamma])=P(\{\widetilde{\gamma}\})=[p\circ\widetilde{\gamma}]=[\gamma]\ \text{ and }\ \theta\circ P(\{\sigma\})=\theta([p\circ\sigma])=\{\sigma\},

where the last equality comes from the unique path lifting property of
p
. Therefore, we may write

\widetilde{X}_{n+1}=\{\mu\in{\mathcal{B}}_{n+1}(\widetilde{X})\mid\,\text{supp}(\mu)\subset(q^{X})^{-1}(x)\ \text{for\ some\ }x\in X\},\ \text{ and}

\widetilde{Y}_{n+1}=\{\nu\in{\mathcal{B}}_{n+1}(\widetilde{Y})\mid\,\text{supp}(\nu)\subset(q^{Y})^{-1}(y)\ \text{for\ some\ }y\in Y\}.

So,
\mu=\sum_{i}a_{i}[\gamma_{i}]\in\widetilde{X}_{n+1}
means there exists
x\in X
such that
q^{X}(\gamma_{i})=\gamma_{i}(0)=x
is independent of
i
, and
\nu=\sum_{i}b_{i}\{\alpha_{i}\}\in\widetilde{Y}_{n+1}
means there exists
y\in Y
such that
q^{Y}(\alpha_{i})=\alpha_{i}(0)=y
is independent of
i
.

3.15 Theorem
.

If
p\colon Y\to X
is a covering map, then
\dcat_{1}(Y)\leq\dcat_{1}(X)
.

Proof.

Suppose
x_{0}\in X
is a basepoint of
X
. We choose
y_{0}\in p^{-1}(x_{0})
as a basepoint of
Y
. For each
z\in p^{-1}(x_{0})
, we choose and fix a path
\omega_{z}\in P_{0}(Y)
with the property that
\omega_{z}(0)=z
and
\omega_{z}(1)=y_{0}
. Let
\dcat_{1}(X)=n
, and let
s\colon X\to\widetilde{X}_{n+1}
be a section of
q_{n+1}^{X}:\widetilde{X}_{n+1}\to X
. We will now define a map
\tau\colon Y\to\widetilde{Y}_{n+1}
as follows. Suppose

s(x)=\sum_{i}a_{i}[\gamma_{i}]\ \text{ with }\ \gamma_{i}(0)=x\ \text{ for\ all}\ i.

Let
y\in p^{-1}(x)
. Then
\gamma_{i}
lifts to a unique path
\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}\in P(Y)
such that
\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}(0)=y
. If
z_{i}=\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}(1)
, then
\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}\ast\omega_{z_{i}}\in P_{0}(Y)
. We define

\tau(y)=\sum_{i}a_{i}\left\{\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}\ast\omega_{z_{i}}\right\}.

Note that for each
i
, we have
(\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}\ast\omega_{z_{i}})(0)=\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}(0)=y
, so

\text{supp}(\tau(y))\subset(q^{Y})^{-1}(y)
. Suppose
\gamma_{i}\simeq_{*}\alpha
. Then Lemma
3.13
gives
\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}\simeq_{*}\widetilde{\gamma}_{j}
, which entails the equality
z_{i}=\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}(1)=\alpha(1)
. Since we fixed the paths
\omega_{z}
using only their endpoints
\{z,y_{0}\}
, the equality
z_{i}=\alpha(1)
implies
w_{z_{i}}=w_{\alpha(1)}\in P_{0}(Y)
. So, we have

\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}\ast\omega_{z_{i}}\simeq_{*}\alpha\ast\omega_{z_{i}}\simeq_{*}\alpha\ast\omega_{\alpha(1)}.

Therefore,
\tau
is well-defined, and hence, it is a section of
q_{n+1}^{Y}:\widetilde{Y}_{n+1}\to Y
. Thus, we obtain
\dcat_{1}(Y)\leq n=\dcat_{1}(X)
.
∎

By Corollary
3.6
and Theorem
3.15
, for the covering
f\colon Y\to X
and classifying maps
\kappa_{X}\colon X\to K(\pi_{1}(X),1)
and
\kappa_{Y}\colon Y\to K(\pi_{1}(Y),1)
, we have the inequality
\dcat(\kappa_{Y})=\dcat_{1}(\kappa_{Y})\leq\dcat_{1}(\kappa_{X})=\dcat(\kappa_{X})
. This is the analogue of the inequality
\cat_{1}(Y)=\cat(\kappa_{Y})=\cat_{1}(\kappa_{Y})\leq\cat_{1}(\kappa_{X})=\cat(\kappa_{X})=\cat_{1}(X)
from
[
OS12
]
.

3.16 Corollary
.

If
f\colon Y\to X
induces a monomorphism of fundamental groups, then

\dcat_{1}(Y)\leq\dcat_{1}(X).

Proof.

Clearly,
\pi=\operatorname{Im}(f_{*}\colon\pi_{1}(Y)\to\pi_{1}(X))\subset\pi_{1}(X)
since
f_{*}
is a monomorphism. We define a covering
p:\widehat{X}\to X
with
\pi_{1}(\widehat{X})=\pi
. By the lifting criterion, we get a lift
\widehat{f}:Y\to\widehat{X}
of
f
along
p
. Note that
\widehat{f}
induces an isomorphism of fundamental groups. Hence,

\dcat_{1}(Y)\leq\dcat_{1}(\widehat{X})\leq\dcat_{1}(X)

is obtained using Corollary
3.8
and Theorem
3.15
, respectively.
∎

4.
Distributional one-category and the fundamental group

We begin by noting that for any Eilenberg–Mac Lane space
X=K(\pi,1)
, we immediately have
\dcat_{1}(X)\leq\cat_{1}(X)=\cat(X)=\cd(\pi)
. Also, Corollary
3.6
gives
\dcat_{1}(X)=\dcat(X)=\dcat(\pi)
. Moreover, if
\cat(X)=\cd(\pi)
is finite, then
\pi
is torsion-free and so,
\dcat(\pi)=\cd(\pi)
holds due to
[
KW24
]
(see Section
5.B
for a proof from our viewpoint), which means
\dcat_{1}(X)=\cd(\pi)
.

In the case when
X
is
not
an Eilenberg–Mac Lane space and its universal cover
\widetilde{X}
is highly connected (but
not
contractible), we now obtain a similar algebraic description of
\dcat_{1}(X)
and
\cat_{1}(X)
under an additional assumption on
\cd(\pi_{1}(X))
. The following statement can be seen as a partial extension of the above result for Eilenberg–Mac Lane spaces.

4.1 Theorem
.

For
X
with
\pi_{1}(X)=\pi
, suppose that
\widetilde{X}
is
(k-1)
-connected
and that
\cd(\pi)\leq k
. Then

\dcat_{1}(X)=\cat_{1}(X)=\cd(\pi).

Proof.

The Eilenberg–Mac Lane space
K=K(\pi,1)
may be constructed by adding cells
to
X
of dimension greater than or equal to
k+1
. Therefore, we have

\pi_{j}(K,X)=0
for
j\leq k
. If we convert
X\hookrightarrow K
to a
fibration with fiber
F
, then
\pi_{s}(F)=\pi_{s+1}(K,X)
, so that
\pi_{s}(F)=0

for
s\leq k-1
. Since the fiber is
(k-1)
-connected, all possible obstructions to a section of
X\to K
live in
H^{t+1}(K;\pi_{t}(F))=H^{t+1}(\pi;\pi_{t}(F))
for
t\geq k
. In particular, the primary obstruction lives in
H^{k+1}(\pi;\pi_{k}(F))
. But
\cd(\pi)\leq k
implies
H^{t+1}(\pi;\pi_{t}(F))=0
for all
t\geq k
and hence, a section
K\to X
exists. This makes
K
into a homotopy retract of
X
. Then, we have the following inequality:

\cd(\pi)=\dcat(K)=\dcat_{1}(K)\leq\dcat_{1}(X)\leq\dcat_{1}(K)\leq\cd(\pi).

The first equality is true because
\pi
is torsion-free, the second one comes from Corollary
3.6
, the third inequality follows from Proposition
3.11
, the fourth one is by Corollary
3.8
, and the last one is standard for Eilenberg–Mac Lane spaces. Analogous results similarly give
\cd(\pi)=\dcat_{1}(X)\leq\cat_{1}(X)\leq\cd(\pi)
.
∎

4.2 Remark
.

For any
X
with
\pi_{1}(X)=\pi
, Corollary
3.6
implies the inequality
\dcat_{1}(X)\leq\dcat_{1}(\pi)\leq\cd(\pi)
. Therefore, if
X
and
\pi
satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
4.1
, then we have
\dcat_{1}(X)=\dcat_{1}(\pi)
, so the first inequality is sharp.

On the other hand, suppose
X
is such that
\dim(X)<\cd(\pi)
and
\cd(\pi)

Since

4.3 Corollary
.

For

\pi_{1}(X)=\pi
, suppose

\dcat_{1}(X)=\cat_{1}(X)=\cd(\pi)

So, the strict inequalities

4.4 Example
.

If

4.5 Example

If

We now continue with more corollaries of Theorem

.

4.6 Corollary

For

∎

In particular, we have several non-trivial examples of finite coverings for which the inequality in Theorem

In the classical setting,

5.

5.B.
The case of torsion-free groups

In
[

This result has been instrumental in furthering research on distributional category and related invariants, see, for instance,
[

Before we proceed, we recall that
\dcat_{1}(X)\leq\cat_{1}(X)

\cat_{1}(X)
is the least integer

\cat_{1}(\pi)=\cd(\pi)

The exact same proof as in
[

shows that

acts freely on

(by multiplication in
\pi

\pi
is torsion-free. Also, note that the measure space

(

]

If

\dcat(\pi)=\cd(\pi)

Proof.

Since we know that
\dcat_{1}(\pi)\leq\cd(\pi)

\dcat_{1}(\pi)\geq 1

6.

6.A.

cap property
if
\kappa^{*}([M])\frown\mathfrak{a}_{\pi}\neq 0
.

6.4 Remark
.

Since the orientation sheaf
{\mathcal{O}}_{M}
on
M
is determined by the kernel of the first Stiefel–Whitney class
w\colon M\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
, for
M
to take (co)homology with coefficients in
{\mathcal{O}}_{M}
is the same as taking (co)homology with coefficients in the twisted integers
{\mathbb{Z}}_{w}
. Because
\kappa
is a classifying map, we have
\kappa_{*}([M])\in H_{n}(K;{\mathbb{Z}}_{w})
so that
\kappa_{*}([M])\frown\mathfrak{a}_{\pi}\in H_{0}(K;{\mathbb{Z}}_{w}\otimes\mathcal{F})
for the
\pi
-module
\mathcal{F}
as above.

Our main result of this section studies
\dcat_{1}
of connected sums of manifolds that have an essential summand satisfying the cap property.

6.5 Theorem
.

Let
M
be a closed essential
n
-manifold such that
\pi_{1}(M)
is torsion-free. If
M
satisfies the cap property, then
\dcat_{1}(M\#N)=\cat_{1}(M\#N)=n
for each closed
n
-manifold
N
. In particular,
\dcat_{1}(M)=n
.

Proof.

Suppose
\pi=\pi_{1}(M)
and
\kappa\colon M\to K=K(\pi,1)
is a classifying map that does not deform to the
(n-1)
-skeleton. Let
f\colon M\#N\to M
be the degree
1
map that collapses
N
to a point.
The primary obstruction
\mathfrak{a}_{\pi}\in H^{n}(K,\mathcal{F})
to a section of
e_{n}^{K}
gives the primary obstruction
(\kappa\circ f)^{*}(\mathfrak{a}_{\pi})\in H^{n}(M\#N;(\kappa\circ f)^{*}(\mathcal{F}))
to a lift of
\kappa\circ f
along
e^{K}_{n}
. It was shown in
[
Ja25b
, Section 4]
that
(\kappa\circ f)^{*}(\mathfrak{a}_{\pi})\neq 0
. In detail, we have a commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M\#N}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M\cup_{B}N}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M\vee N,}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\kappa}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d}

where
q
is the quotient map
M\cup_{B}N\to(M\cup_{B}N)/B=M\vee N
and
d\colon M\vee N\to M
is the natural collapsing map. Note that
q\circ\iota
induces an isomorphism on the zeroth homology groups with any system of local coefficients. It is not difficult to show that in suitable coefficients,

(ii)

(q\circ\iota)_{*}\left([M\#N]\frown(\kappa\circ f)^{*}(\mathfrak{a}_{\pi}))\right)=([M]\oplus[N])\frown(\kappa^{*}(\mathfrak{a}_{\pi})\oplus\alpha),

where
\alpha
is a class such that
(\kappa\circ d)^{*}(\mathfrak{a}_{\pi})=\kappa^{*}(\mathfrak{a}_{\pi})\oplus\alpha
. The cap property on
M
ensures that
\kappa_{*}([M]\frown\kappa^{*}(\mathfrak{a}_{\pi}))\neq 0
, so that the class in (
ii
) is non-zero, which implies
(\kappa\circ f)^{*}(\mathfrak{a}_{\pi})\neq 0
. This means there is no lift of
\kappa\circ f
along
e^{K}_{n}
since
(\kappa\circ f)^{*}(\mathfrak{a}_{\pi})
is the only obstruction to such a lift. But then
\dcat(\kappa\circ f)\geq n
by definition (
cf.
Section
2.B
). Now, Propositions
3.2
,
3.3
, and
3.4
imply

n\leq\dcat(\kappa\circ f)=\dcat_{1}(\kappa\circ f)\leq\dcat_{1}(M\#N)\leq\cat_{1}(M\#N)\leq n,

where the last inequality is obvious. This completes the proof.
∎

A special case of our interest is as follows.

6.6 Corollary
.

Let
M
be a closed essential
n
-manifold such that
K(\pi_{1}(M),1)
is a closed
n
-manifold. Then
\dcat_{1}(M\#N)=\cat_{1}(M\#N)=n
for each closed
n
-manifold
N
. In particular, this holds if
M
is aspherical.

Proof.

From obstruction theory, it follows that
\kappa_{*}([M])\neq 0
,
[
Ba93
, Theorem 8.2]
. Therefore,
M
satisfies the cap property due to Poincaré duality in
K(\pi_{1}(M),1)
, see
[
Ja25b
, Corollary 3.4]
. Hence, Theorem
6.5
applies.
∎

6.7 Remark
.

One can generalize Corollary
6.6
beyond the realm of manifolds by taking
N
to be a closed (non-manifold) Alexandrov
n
-space
[
BGP92
]
whose curvature is bounded from below. Examples of such spaces include supsensions of
{\mathbb{R}}P^{2}
and
{\mathbb{C}}P^{2}
and their products (and more generally, orbit spaces of isometric compact Lie group actions on complete Riemannian manifolds whose sectional curvatures are uniformly bounded below). In case of such
N
, the connected sum
M\#N
along any
regular point
of
N
(see
[
BGP92
,
FGGGH
]
) still makes sense, and one can obtain the equality
\dcat_{1}(M\#N)=\cat_{1}(M\#N)=n
by proceeding as in Theorem
6.5
and using ideas from
[
Ja25b
, Section 7]
.

The results above provide examples of compact metric spaces whose
\dcat_{1}
values can be as large as desired.

6.8 Remark
.

We emphasize that we only require assumptions on
\pi_{1}(M)
in the above statements; there are no assumptions on
\pi_{1}(M\#N)
. For example, we have
\dcat_{1}(M\#{\mathbb{R}}P^{n})=n
for any aspherical manifold
M
, even though
\dcat_{1}({\mathbb{R}}P^{n})=1
and
K(\pi_{1}(M\#{\mathbb{R}}P^{n}))\simeq M\vee{\mathbb{R}}P^{\infty}
for each
n
.

We have from Corollary
3.6
and Theorem
5.5
that
\dcat_{1}(M)=\dim(M)
if
M
is a closed aspherical manifold. So, Theorem
6.5
and Corollary
6.6
are extensions of this result to some essential manifolds and connected sums having aspherical summands, respectively.

6.9 Remark
.

Since
\dcat_{1}(X)\leq\dcat(X)\leq\dim(X)
is always true, our results recover the corresponding results of
[
Ja25b
]
on distributional category. Concretely, Theorems A, B, C, and F in
[
Ja25b
]
and their corollaries in Sections 3 and 4 there are extended in this section without using those results.

6.B.
Some computations

Let us consider a particular class of smooth, essential manifolds. We say that a closed symplectic manifold
M
is
symplectically aspherical
with respect to a symplectic form
\omega
if for any smooth map
f\colon M\to S^{2}
, one has

\int_{S^{2}}f^{*}\omega=0.

This is equivalent to the algebraic condition that
[\omega]\circ h\colon\pi_{2}(M)\to{\mathbb{R}}
vanishes, where
h\colon\pi_{2}(M)\to H_{2}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})
is the Hurewicz homomorphism and
[\omega]\in H_{\text{dR}}^{2}(M;{\mathbb{R}})
corresponds to
\omega
. It is well-known that symplectically aspherical manifolds are essential (in fact, rationally essential), see
[
RO99
]
. So, we have the following.

6.10 Corollary
.

If
M
is a symplectically aspherical
n
-manifold and
K(\pi_{1}(M),1)
is a closed
n
-manifold, then
\dcat_{1}(M\#N)=\cat_{1}(M\#N)=n
for each
N
.

6.11 Example
.

There are non-trivial symplectically aspherical manifolds with
\pi_{2}\neq 0
due to
[
Go98
]
. Moreover, there are manifolds with
\pi_{2}\neq 0
that satisfy the hypotheses of Corollary
6.10
— we refer the reader to
[
Ja25b
, Section 3.2]
for various such examples in all even dimensions
\geq 4
. We note that for all these closed manifolds, our computation of their distributional one-category is new, in the sense that it does not follow from Corollary
3.6
, or any other known results on the distributional invariants.

We conclude this section by looking at another application of Corollary
6.6
to the distributional one-category of closed
3
-manifolds.

Recall from
[
GLGA92
]
(see also
[
OR02
]
) that if
M
is a closed
3
-manifold, then

\cat(M)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }\pi_{1}(M)=0\\
2&\text{ if }\pi_{1}(M)\neq 0\text{ is free}\\
3&\text{ if }\pi_{1}(M)\text{ is not free}\end{cases}\hskip 11.38109pt\text{and}\hskip 11.38109pt\cat_{1}(M)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\pi_{1}(M)=0\\
1&\text{ if }\pi_{1}(M)\neq 0\text{ is free}\\
3&\text{ if }\pi_{1}(M)\text{ is not free}\end{cases}

Moreover, if
\pi_{1}(M)
is torsion-free, then
\dcat(M)=\cat(M)
, see
[
Ja25b
]
.

6.12 Proposition
.

If
M
is a closed
3
-manifold, then

\dcat_{1}(M)=\cat_{1}(M)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\pi_{1}(M)=0\\
1&\text{ if }\pi_{1}(M)\neq 0\text{ is free}\\
3&\text{ if }\pi_{1}(M)\text{ is not free but torsion-free.}\end{cases}

Proof.

The equality in the first case is trivial. When
\pi_{1}(M)\neq 0
is free, then
\dcat_{1}(M)=\cat_{1}(M)=1
is obtained from Example
4.4
. Let us now assume that
\pi_{1}(M)
is not free but torsion-free. In this case, from the prime decomposition of closed
3
-manifolds and Papakyriakopoulos’s sphere theorem (see
[
He76
]
), it turns out that
M
has a closed aspherical summand (see also
[
Ja25b
, Section 5.1]
for a LS-categorical proof of this fact). Therefore, Corollary
6.6
gives
\dcat_{1}(M)=3
.
∎

The converse of Proposition
6.12
is not true (
cf.
Remark
6.8
).

6.13 Corollary
.

If
M
is a closed
3
-manifold such that
\pi_{1}(M)
is not free, then
\cd(\pi_{1}(M))\geq 3
.

Proof.

First, note that
\cd(\pi_{1}(M))\geq 2
by the Stallings–Swan theorem.
Now, let us suppose
\cd(\pi_{1}(M))=2
. Then we must have
\dcat_{1}(M)=\cat_{1}(M)=2
by Corollary
4.3
. But
\cd(\pi_{1}(M))=2
means
\pi_{1}(M)
is torsion-free, so Proposition
6.12
gives
\dcat_{1}(M)=\cat_{1}(M)=3
, a contradiction. Thus,
\cd(\pi_{1}(M))\neq 2
.
∎

In fact, it follows from the prime decomposition
[
He76
, Theorems 3.15 and 3.21]
that if
\pi_{1}(M)
is not free, then
\cd(\pi_{1}(M))=3
, unless
\pi_{1}(M)
has torsion, in which case
\cd(\pi_{1}(M))
is infinite. However, we obtained this in Corollary
6.13
directly using our invariant
\dcat_{1}
, which is interesting.

6.14 Remark
.

In the case when
\pi_{1}(M)
has non-trivial torsion, finding
\dcat_{1}(M)
may be difficult. In particular, there is no single answer as
\dcat_{1}({\mathbb{R}}P^{3})=1
while
\dcat_{1}(M\#{\mathbb{R}}P^{3})=3
for any aspherical
3
-manifold
M
. If
\pi_{1}(M)={\mathbb{Z}}_{3}
, then
\dcat_{1}(M)\in\{1,2\}
by Corollary
5.2
, but it is unclear if anything more can be said. We do note, however, that if
\dcat_{1}(M)=2
, then
\cat_{1}(M)\geq 2
and hence,
\cat_{1}(M)=3
by
[
GLGA92
]
, so we get examples of closed
3
-manifolds
M\not\simeq{\mathbb{R}}P^{3}
with
\dcat_{1}(M)\neq\cat_{1}(M)
— we will see such examples in Example
8.9
. But we don’t know whether
\dcat_{1}(M)=2
is a possibility for
3
-manifolds. Some manifolds of interest can be
{\mathbb{R}}P^{2}\times S^{1}
and
{\mathbb{R}}P^{3}\#{\mathbb{R}}P^{3}
, whose
\dcat
values are unknown,
[
Ja25b
]
.

7.
Sequential diagonal distributional complexity

In
[
FGLO19a
,
FGLO19b
]
, a new invariant was introduced to provide a lower bound on topological complexity (
cf.
Section
2.A
). This invariant is the diagonal topological complexity
\TC^{\mathcal{D}}
. Soon after, the sequential versions of
\TC^{\mathcal{D}}
, denoted
\TC^{\mathcal{D}}_{m}
for
m\geq 2
, were introduced in
[
FO19
]
and their theory was further developed in
[
PS20
]
. We introduce these invariants here because they are the
\TC
-analogues of
\cat_{1}
.
Indeed, for
m\geq 2
, we always have

(iii)

\cat_{1}(X^{m-1})\leq\TC^{\mathcal{D}}_{m}(X)\leq\cat_{1}(X^{m}),

as well as
\TC_{m}^{\mathcal{D}}(X)\leq\TC_{m}(X)
. The definition of
\TC_{m}^{\mathcal{D}}(X)
can be given as follows. Any path
\gamma\in X^{I}
may
be lifted uniquely to the universal cover
\widetilde{X}
once a starting point is chosen, and then the ending point
is determined by the uniqueness of lifting. In order to do away with choosing starting points, we can take the
(free) diagonal action of
\pi=\pi_{1}(X)
on the product
\widetilde{X}\times\cdots\times\widetilde{X}=\widetilde{X}^{m}
, where the product is taken
m
-times, and form the orbit space

\widetilde{X}\times_{\pi}\cdots\times_{\pi}\widetilde{X}
, which we will denote by
\widetilde{X}^{m}_{\pi}
. This is a covering of
X^{m}
corresponding to the diagonal subgroup
\Delta\subset\pi^{m}
with fiber
\pi^{m-1}
, and there is a commutative diagram

(iv)

\textstyle{P(X)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\phi}
\scriptstyle{\text{ev}_{m}^{X}}
\textstyle{\widetilde{X}^{m}_{\pi}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{r_{m}^{X}}
\textstyle{X^{m},}

where
P(X)
is the free path space of
X
,
\text{ev}^{X}_{m}
is the evaluation fibration defined in Section
2.A
,
r^{X}_{m}
is the covering map, and

\phi(\gamma)=\left[\gamma(0),\gamma\left(\frac{1}{m-1}\right),\ldots,\gamma\left(\frac{m-2}{m-1}\right),\gamma(1)\right]

is well-defined because we quotient by the
\pi
-action. Then we define the
m
-th sequential diagonal topological complexity
as

\TC_{m}^{\mathcal{D}}(X)=\secat(r_{m}^{X}).

In this paper, we define and study the following.

7.1 Definition
.

The
m
-th sequential diagonal distributional complexity
of a space
X
, denoted
\dTC_{m}^{\mathcal{D}}(X)
, is the least integer
k
such that the
fibration

(\widetilde{X}^{m}_{\pi})_{k+1}\xrightarrow{(r_{m}^{X})_{k+1}}X^{m}

admits a section. Here, the fibration
(r_{m}^{X})_{k+1}
has fiber
{\mathcal{B}}_{k+1}(\pi)
.

In other words,
\dTC_{m}^{\mathcal{D}}(X)=\dsecat(r_{m}^{X})
, so that
\dTC_{m}^{\mathcal{D}}(X)\leq\TC_{m}^{\mathcal{D}}(X)
for each
m\geq 2
follows immediately, see Section
2.B
.

7.A.
Standard inequalities

We begin by establishing some properties of
\dTC_{m}^{\mathcal{D}}(X)
in analogy with certain
results for
\TC_{m}^{\mathcal{D}}(X)
discussed in
[
FO19
,
PS20
]
.

7.2 Proposition
.

For each
m\geq 2
, we have that
\dTC_{m}^{\mathcal{D}}(X)\leq\dTC_{m}(X)
. The inequality is saturated if
X=K(\pi,1)
.

Proof.

Suppose
\dTC_{m}(X)=k
. Then the diagram in (
iv
) induces a commutative diagram

\textstyle{P(X)_{k+1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\phi_{k+1}}
\scriptstyle{(\text{ev}_{m}^{X})_{k+1}}
\textstyle{(\widetilde{X}^{m}_{\pi})_{k+1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{(r_{m}^{X})_{k+1}}
\textstyle{X^{m}.}

So, a section
s\colon X^{m}\to P(X)_{k+1}
of
(\text{ev}_{m}^{X})_{k+1}
gives a section
\phi_{k+1}\circ s
of
(r_{m}^{X})_{k+1}
. This implies
\dTC_{m}^{\mathcal{D}}(X)\leq k
. If
X=K(\pi,1)
, then
\phi
is a fiber homotopy equivalence. Therefore,
\phi_{k+1}
is a fiber homotopy equivalence as well that fits in the above diagram and so, any section of
(r_{m}^{X})_{k+1}
determines a section of
(\text{ev}_{m}^{X})_{k+1}
, hence giving the inequality
\dTC_{m}(K(\pi,1))\leq\dTC_{m}^{\mathcal{D}}(K(\pi,1))
.
∎

7.3 Proposition
.

For each
m\geq 2
, we have
\dcat_{1}(X^{m-1})\leq\dTC^{\mathcal{D}}_{m}(X)\leq\dcat_{1}(X^{m})
.

Proof.

Consider the following commutative diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Q}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{X}^{m}_{\pi}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{X}^{m}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{m-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{m}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r_{m}^{X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q^{X^{m}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}

Here,
\iota
is the inclusion of the first
(m-1)
-coordinates, the square on the left is a pullback, and the horizontal arrow on the right is the natural quotient map. First, by the triangle on the right, we get using the proof technique of Proposition
7.2
that

\dTC_{m}^{\mathcal{D}}(X)=\dsecat(r_{m}^{X})\leq\dsecat(q^{X^{m}})=\dcat_{1}(X^{m}).

Now, we look at the pullback space
Q
. At the level of fundamental groups, we have that
\operatorname{Im}(\iota_{\#})=\pi^{m-1}\times 1\subset\pi^{m}
and
\operatorname{Im}((r_{m}^{X})_{\#})=\Delta\subset\pi^{m}
. These subgroups generate all of
\pi^{m}
, so by the exact sequence of homotopy groups, we see that
Q
is a connected covering space of
X^{m-1}
. Furthermore, the fiber of
Q\to X
is
\pi^{m-1}
by the pullback property. But this means
Q\simeq\widetilde{X}^{m-1}
and
\rho\colon Q\to X^{m-1}
is compatible with the universal covering
q^{X^{m-1}}\colon\widetilde{X}^{m-1}\to X^{m-1}
. In other words,
q^{X^{m-1}}
is, up to homotopy, the pullback of
r_{m}^{X}
along
\iota
. Therefore, applying Lemma
2.2
, we get

\dcat_{1}(X^{m-1})=\dsecat(q^{X^{m-1}})\leq\dsecat(r_{m}^{X})=\dTC_{m}^{\mathcal{D}}(X).

This completes the proof.
∎

7.4 Remark
.

Proposition
7.3
shows that the sequence
\{\dTC^{\mathcal{D}}_{m}(X)\}_{m\geq 2}
is non-decreasing for any
X
, so the values
\dTC^{\mathcal{D}}_{m}(X)
can be arbitrarily large for each
m\geq 2
(
cf.
Remark
5.4
and Section
6.A
).

We end this subsection by showing that the lower bound in Proposition
7.3
is attained in the case of CW
H
-spaces (compare with
[
PS20
, Proposition 3.13]
for
\TC^{\mathcal{D}}_{m}
, and with
[
Ja25a
, Theorem 6.3]
for
\dTC_{m}
).

7.5 Proposition
.

If
X
is a CW
H
-space, then for each
m\geq 2
, we have that

\dTC_{m}^{\mathcal{D}}(X)=\dcat_{1}(X^{m-1}).

Proof.

The loop structure of
[X,X]
, due to James, shows that there is a right inverse map

\eta\colon X\to X
with the property that

X\xrightarrow{\Delta}X\times X\xrightarrow{1_{X}\times\eta}X\times X\xrightarrow{m}X

is null-homotopic, where
m
is the multiplication on
X
corresponding to a
H
-space structure. Define
h=m\circ(1_{X}\times\eta)\colon X\times X\to X
. On homology, this map induces
h_{*}(a,b)=a-b
and since, for an
H
-space,
\pi_{1}(X)=H_{1}(X)
, we see that the map of fundamental groups is
h_{\#}(a,b)=a-b
as well. Define
f_{m}\colon X^{m}\to X^{m-1}
such that

f_{m}\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m-1},x_{m}\right)=\left(h\left(x_{1},x_{m}\right),\ldots,h\left(x_{m-1},x_{m}\right)\right).

It follows that the induced map
(f_{m})_{\#}\colon\pi^{m}\to\pi^{m-1}
is such that

(f_{m})_{\#}\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m-1},a_{m}\right)=\left(h_{\#}\left(a_{1},a_{m}\right),\ldots,h_{\#}\left(a_{m-1},a_{m}\right)\right).

Hence,
\Ker((f_{m})_{\#})=\Delta\subset\pi^{m}
. Therefore, we can lift
f_{m}\colon X^{m}\to X^{m-1}
to a map
\widetilde{f_{m}}\colon\widetilde{X}^{m}_{\pi}\to\widetilde{X}^{m-1}
in the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{X}^{m}_{\pi}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{X}^{m-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{m}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{m-1}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{f_{m}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r_{m}^{X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q^{X^{m-1}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{m}}

Note that
\operatorname{Im}((f_{m})_{\#})=\pi^{m}/\Delta\cong\pi^{m-1}
, and this in turn shows that
\widetilde{f_{m}}
restricted to each fiber
\pi^{m}/\Delta\cong\pi^{m-1}
is a bijection. This then implies that the diagram above
is a pullback. But then we get from Lemma
2.2
that

\dTC_{m}^{\mathcal{D}}(X)=\dsecat(r_{m}^{X})\leq\dsecat(q^{X^{m-1}})=\cat_{1}(X^{m-1}).

The reverse inequality follows from Proposition
7.3
.
∎

7.B.
Behavior on
\pi_{1}
-isomorphisms and retracts

Motivated by Corollary
3.8
, we study the behavior of
\dTC^{\mathcal{D}}_{m}
on maps that induce isomorphisms of fundamental groups. For that, we prepare as follows.

7.6 Lemma
.

For a map

which induces an isomorphism of fundamental groups, the following diagram is a pullback:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{X}^{m}_{\pi}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{Y}^{m}_{\pi}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{m}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y^{m}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{f}^{m}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r^{X}_{m}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces r^{Y}_{m}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{m}}

Here,
f^{m}=f\times\cdots\times f
(multiplied
m
-times) and
\widetilde{f}^{m}
denotes the map induced on the
\pi
-quotients by the map on the universal covering spaces.

Proof.

Let
Q=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}])\mid\,f(x_{i})=q^{Y}(\alpha_{i})\text{ for all }i\leq m\}
be the pullback of
r_{m}^{Y}
along
f^{m}
. Define a map
\Theta\colon\widetilde{X}^{m}_{\pi}\to Q
by

\Theta([\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m}])=(q^{X}(\sigma_{1}),\ldots,q^{X}(\sigma_{m}),[\widetilde{f}(\sigma_{1}),\ldots,\widetilde{f}(\sigma_{m})]),

where
\widetilde{f}\colon\widetilde{X}\to\widetilde{Y}
is the natural map on universal covers. Clearly,
\Theta
is well-defined since
f\circ q^{X}=q^{Y}\circ\widetilde{f}
. Since

induces an isomorphism of fundamental groups, we see that

\widetilde{f}
induces bijections of
fibers
(q^{X})^{-1}(x)
and
(q^{Y})^{-1}(f(x))
for all

. Moreover,
the fibers of

and

are both given by

\pi^{m}/\Delta\cong\pi^{m-1}
, where
\Delta
is the diagonal subgroup of
\pi^{m}
. Therefore, we can define a map
\phi\colon Q\to\widetilde{X}^{m}_{\pi}
by

\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}])=[\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m}],

where
q^{X}(\sigma_{i})=x_{i}
and
\widetilde{f}(\sigma_{i})=\alpha_{i}
for each

. If we take the representative
[g\cdot\alpha_{i},\ldots,g\cdot\alpha_{m}]
, we get
[f_{\#}(g)\cdot\sigma_{1},\ldots,f_{\#}(g)\cdot\sigma_{m}]
since
\widetilde{f}
is
f_{\#}
-equivariant. But this element is the same as
[\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m}]
because we quotient by the diagonal action. Hence,
\phi
is well-defined. We now have

\displaystyle\Theta\circ\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}])

\displaystyle=\Theta([\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m}])

\displaystyle=(q^{X}(\sigma_{1}),\ldots,q^{X}(\sigma_{m}),[\widetilde{f}(\sigma_{1}),\ldots,\widetilde{f}(\sigma_{m})])

\displaystyle=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},[\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}])

by the definition of
\phi
. We also have

\displaystyle\phi\circ\Theta\left([\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m}\right])

\displaystyle=\phi(q^{X}(\sigma_{1}),\ldots,q^{X}(\sigma_{m}),[\widetilde{f}(\sigma_{1}),\ldots,\widetilde{f}(\sigma_{m})])

\displaystyle=\left[\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m}\right]

by the definition of

.
∎

7.7 Remark
.

We have shown that the diagram of Lemma

is a pullback
directly, but we could also proceed as follows. The fact that

But since

8.
An upper bound for distributional category

In this section, we turn back to distributional category
(\dcat)
, with the primary aim of obtaining new examples of finite-dimensional CW complexes (in fact, closed manifolds) on which the distributional invariants
do not
coincide with their classical counterparts.

We know that
\dcat_{1}(X)\leq\dcat(X)
for all
X
, with equality holding sometimes. We can also get an upper bound for
\dcat
in terms of
\dcat_{1}
and an important player in the very definition of
\dcat_{1}
, namely the universal cover. The best type of inequality giving a good approximation for
\dcat(X)
from above would be an additive one. However, we know from Example
7.20
that a product inequality of the type
\dcat(X\times Y)\leq\dcat(X)+\dcat(Y)
does not exist. So, the best we can do is obtain a multiplicative inequality that uses two quantities that are definitely less than (or equal to)
\dcat(X)
itself.

8.1 Theorem
.

If
p\colon Y\to X
is a covering map, then

\dcat(X)\leq(\dcat_{1}(X)+1)(\dcat(Y)+1)-1.

Proof.

Let
x_{0}\in X
be the basepoint of
X
and
y_{0}\in p^{-1}(x_{0})
be the basepoint of
Y
. Recall from Section
3.B
that
\widetilde{X}=P_{0}(X)/[-]
and
\widetilde{Y}=P_{0}(Y)/\{-\}
, and
\theta\colon\widetilde{X}\to\widetilde{Y}
is a homeomorphism which maps any
[\gamma]\in\widetilde{X}
to
\{\widetilde{\gamma}\}\in\widetilde{Y}
, where
\widetilde{\gamma}\in P_{0}(Y)
is the lift of
\gamma\in P_{0}(X)
with respect to the point
\gamma(1)=x_{0}
. Recall also that the universal coverings
q^{X}\colon\widetilde{X}\to X
and
q^{Y}\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y
satisfy
q^{X}[\alpha]=\alpha(0)
and
q^{Y}\{\beta\}=\beta(0)
. Suppose
\dcat_{1}(X)=n
and
\dcat(Y)=r
. We then have a section
\sigma\colon X\to\widetilde{X}_{n+1}
of
q^{X}_{n+1}
. For
x\in X
, suppose

\sigma(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}a_{i}\widetilde{x}_{i}\ \text{ with }\ q^{X}(\widetilde{x}_{i})=x.

For each
1\leq i\leq n+1
, we can write
\widetilde{x}_{i}=[\gamma_{i}]
for some
\gamma_{i}\in P_{0}(X)
such that
\gamma_{i}(0)=x
. Then we have for
\theta_{n+1}\circ\sigma\colon X\to\widetilde{Y}_{n+1}
that

\theta_{n+1}\circ\sigma(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}a_{i}\{\widetilde{\gamma}_{i}\}\ \text{ with }\ p(\widetilde{\gamma_{i}}(0))=x.

For simplicity, let us write
\widetilde{\gamma_{i}}(0)=y_{i}\in Y
. Then,

q^{Y}_{n+1}\circ\theta_{n+1}\circ\sigma(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}a_{i}y_{i}\ \text{ with }\ p(y_{i})=x.

Since
\dcat(Y)=r
, there is a section
s\colon Y\to P_{0}(Y)_{r+1}
to the map
e^{Y}_{r+1}
. For each
y_{i}
as above, suppose

s(y_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{r+1}b_{j}\alpha_{ij}\ \text{ with }\ \alpha_{ij}(0)=y_{i}.

Let
k=(n+1)(r+1)
. Suppose
\tau\colon P_{0}(Y)\to P_{0}(X)
is the natural map defined as
\tau(\phi)=p\circ\phi
. We claim that the composition

\psi=\tau_{k}\circ s_{n+1}\circ q^{Y}_{n+1}\circ\theta_{n+1}\circ\sigma\colon X\to P_{0}(X)_{k}

is a section of the fibration
e^{X}_{k}
. This is verified by observing that

\psi(x)=\tau_{k}\circ s_{n+1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}a_{i}y_{i}\right)=\tau_{k}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}a_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{r+1}b_{j}\alpha_{ij}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{r+1}a_{i}b_{j}\left(p\circ\alpha_{ij}\right).

But
(p\circ\alpha_{ij})(0)=p(y_{i})=x
by definition. Hence, we are done.
∎

The case of our particular interest is as follows.

8.2 Corollary
.

If
q\colon\widetilde{X}\to X
denotes the universal covering map,
then

\dcat(X)\leq(\dcat_{1}(X)+1)(\dcat(\widetilde{X})+1)-1.

8.3 Example
.

Suppose
X=K(\pi,1)
. Then
\widetilde{X}\simeq*
, so that
\dcat(\widetilde{X})=0
. Hence, the inequality in Corollary
8.2
reduces to
\dcat(X)\leq\dcat_{1}(X)
and,
together with the standard
\dcat_{1}(X)\leq\dcat(X)
, this recovers our
equality
\dcat_{1}(X)=\dcat(X)
for
X
an Eilenberg–Mac Lane space (
cf.
Corollary
3.6
). For instance, let
X
be a closed surface of genus greater than
1
. Then

\dcat_{1}(X)=\cd(\pi_{1}(X))=2=\dcat(X)
and the inequality is an equality.
Hence, the inequality in Theorem
8.1
is sharp.

8.4 Example
.

If
X
is simply connected, then
\widetilde{X}=X
and
\dcat_{1}(X)=0
so
the inequality reduces to
\dcat(X)\leq\dcat(X)
, a tautology.

Suppose
X=K\times Y
, where
K=K(\pi,1)
and
Y
is simply connected.
Then
K
is a retract of
X
, so
\dcat(K)=\dcat_{1}(K)\leq\dcat_{1}(X)
, while the projection
X\to K
induces an isomorphism of fundamental
groups, so
\dcat_{1}(X)\leq\dcat(K)
. Hence,
\dcat_{1}(X)=\dcat(K)
.
Also, since
Y
is simply connected, we have
\widetilde{X}\simeq Y
. The
inequality then becomes

\dcat(K\times Y)\leq(\dcat(K)+1)(\dcat(Y)+1)-1,

and this is the standard product inequality that is known at present.

8.A.
Closed manifolds with
\dcat\neq\cat

So far, the only known examples of finite-dimensional CW complexes
X
satisfying the inequality
\dcat(X)<\cat(X)
are the real projective spaces,
[
DJ24
,
KW24
]
. As we show in this subsection (and the next one), the crowning achievement of Corollary
8.2
is that it gives us several new examples of well-studied closed manifolds whose distributional category is smaller than their LS-category.

8.5 Example

(Projective product spaces)
.

Let
S=\prod_{i=1}^{r}S^{n_{i}}
be a product of
r
spheres with
n_{1}\leq\cdots\leq n_{r}
. Let
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
act antipodally on each individual sphere
S^{n_{i}}
and diagonally on the product
S
. Denote the quotient by this action
by
P_{\bar{n}}
, where
\bar{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})
. This quotient is
called a
projective product space
and it clearly generalizes the
ordinary real projective space. In
[
FV21
]
, the LS-category of
P_{\bar{n}}

was determined to be

\cat(P_{\bar{n}})=n_{1}+r-1.

Of course, we have
\dcat(P_{\bar{n}})\leq\cat(P_{\bar{n}})
, but in
most cases, we can use the inequality of Corollary
8.2
to
get a better bound. First, note that since
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
acts freely,
S
is the
universal covering space for
P_{\bar{n}}
(when
n_{1}>1
, which we assume).
By our inequality for covering spaces in Theorem
3.15
, we immediately obtain
r\leq\dcat(P_{\bar{n}})
since
\dcat(S)=r
(by using rational cup-length below and category above,
[
DJ24
, Section 6.1]
). But now we recognize that
\pi_{1}(P_{\bar{n}})={\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
, so we know by Corollary
4.9
that

\dcat_{1}(P_{\bar{n}})=1
. Corollary
8.2

then gives

\dcat(P_{\bar{n}})\leq(\dcat_{1}(P_{\bar{n}})+1)(\dcat(S)+1)-1=2r+1.

Hence, we have
r\leq\dcat(P_{\bar{n}})\leq 2r+1
. Therefore, anytime we have

n_{1}>r+2
, the bound given by the inequality of Corollary
8.2
is better and
\dcat(P_{\bar{n}})<\cat(P_{\bar{n}})
. For
instance, if we consider
S=S^{10}\times S^{11}\times S^{12}
, then

\cat(P_{\bar{n}})=12\ \text{ while }\ 3\leq\dcat(P_{\bar{n}})\leq 7.

Also, we note that the rational cup-length of
P_{\bar{n}}
does not help here because

H^{*}(P_{\bar{n}};{\mathbb{Q}})\cong H^{*}(S;{\mathbb{Q}})^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}}.

But the fixed cohomology under the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-action is just
H^{*}(S^{11};{\mathbb{Q}})
since the antipodal map preserves orientation only for odd-dimensional
spheres. So, the projective product spaces
P_{\bar{n}}
generalize real projective spaces
by being closed manifolds with
\dcat(P_{\bar{n}})<\cat(P_{\bar{n}})
. Of
course, by choosing appropriate integers
n_{1}
, this difference can be made arbitrarily large.

8.6 Example

(Lens spaces)
.

Let
p
be a prime,
n\geq 2
be an integer, and
q_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
be integers co-prime to
p
. Suppose
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
acts on
S^{2n-1}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
such that

(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\mapsto\left(e^{2\pi iq_{1}/p}\hskip 0.85358ptz_{1},\ldots,e^{2\pi iq_{n}/p}\hskip 0.85358ptz_{n}\right).

Then the quotient of
S^{2n-1}
by this free
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
action is called a
lens space
, denoted
L^{2n-1}(p;q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})
— we will denote it simply by
L^{2n-1}_{p}
. These spaces also generalize real projective spaces. The universal covering is
q\colon S^{2n-1}\to L^{2n-1}_{p}
and
\pi_{1}(L^{2n-1}_{p})={\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
, irrespective of the choice of
q_{i}
(hence our notation
L^{2n-1}_{p}
). It is known due to
[
Dr
,
KW25
]
that
\dcat(L^{\infty}_{p})=\dcat({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})=p-1
. So, the classifying map
L^{2n-1}_{p}\to K({\mathbb{Z}}_{p},1)
gives us
\dcat_{1}(L^{2n-1}_{p})\leq\dcat_{1}({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})=\dcat({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})=p-1
by Proposition
3.5
and Corollary
3.6
. But now, Corollary
8.2
implies

\dcat(L^{2n-1}_{p})\leq(\dcat_{1}(L^{2n-1}_{p})+1)(\dcat(S^{2n-1})+1)-1\leq 2p-1.

Since
\cat(L^{2n-1}_{p})=2n-1
(see, for instance,
[
CLOT03
, Example 9.29]
), whenever
we have
n>p
, we obtain the strict inequality
\dcat(L^{2n-1}_{p})<\cat(L^{2n-1}_{p})
. Of course, this gap can be made arbitrarily large by taking large
n
. Hence, we have more examples of closed manifolds whose
\dcat
and
\cat
values differ. We also note that the rational cup-length of
L^{2n-1}_{p}
is
1
for each
n,p\geq 2
, so it is not helpful in estimating
\dcat(L^{2n-1}_{p})
.

8.B.
Closed manifolds with
\dTC_{m}\neq\TC_{m}
and
\dTC^{\mathcal{D}}_{m}\neq\TC^{\mathcal{D}}_{m}

Using Examples
8.5
and
8.6
, we also get the first examples of finite-dimensional CW complexes
X
(besides the real projective spaces) for which
\dTC_{m}(X)<\TC_{m}(X)
.

8.7 Example
.

For projective product spaces
P_{\bar{n}}
, where
\bar{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})
with
n_{1}\leq\cdots\leq n_{r}
, we have from Example
8.5
an upper bound

\dTC_{m}(P_{\bar{n}})\leq\dcat(P_{\bar{n}}^{m})\leq(\dcat(P_{\bar{n}})+1)^{m}-1\leq(2r+2)^{m}-1,

see
[
Ja25a
, Section 3]
for the first two inequalities. Note that for any
m\geq 2
, we have
n_{1}+r-1=\cat(P_{\bar{n}})\leq\TC_{m}(P_{\bar{n}})
. So, whenever
n_{1}>(2r+2)^{m}-r
, we see that
\dTC_{m}(P_{\bar{n}})<\TC_{m}(P_{\bar{n}})
. This gives us infinitely many new examples of closed manifolds having arbitrarily far apart
\dTC_{m}
and
\TC_{m}
values for each
m
.

8.8 Example
.

For lens spaces
L^{2n-1}_{p}=L^{2n-1}(p;q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})
, we have a universal upper bound

\dTC_{m}(L^{2n-1}_{p})\leq\dcat((L^{2n-1}_{p})^{m})\leq(\dcat(L^{2n-1}_{p})+1)^{m}-1\leq(2p)^{m}-1

due to Example
8.6
. For any
m\geq 2
, we have
2n-1=\cat(L^{2n-1}_{p})\leq\TC_{m}(L^{2n-1}_{p})
. So, whenever
n>2^{m-1}p^{m}
, we see that
\dTC_{m}(L^{2n-1}_{p})<\TC_{m}(L^{2n-1}_{p})
.

At the cost of some arithmetic, Example
8.8
can be improved by appealing to the lower bounds of
[
Da24
]
.

Our arguments in Examples
8.5
and
8.6
also give the following new examples that show
\dcat_{1}\neq\cat_{1}
and
\dTC^{\mathcal{D}}_{m}\neq\TC^{\mathcal{D}}_{m}
.

8.9 Example
.

Let us take
\dim(S)=3
in the notations of Example
8.5
, so that the only possibilities for
\bar{n}
are
(1,1,1)
,
(1,2)
, and
3
. Since
\pi_{1}(P_{\bar{n}})={\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
, we have from
[
GLGA92
]
(see also Section
6.B
) that
\cat_{1}(P_{\bar{n}})=3
. But then we get

\dcat_{1}(P_{\bar{n}})=1<3=\cat_{1}(P_{\bar{n}})\ \text{ and }\ \dTC_{m}^{\mathcal{D}}(P_{\bar{n}})=1<3\leq\TC_{m}^{\mathcal{D}}(P_{\bar{n}})

for each
m\geq 2
using Corollary
4.9
, Example
7.20
, and (
iii
). Thus,
P_{(1,1,1)}
and
P_{(1,2)}
are closed
3
-manifolds different from
{\mathbb{R}}P^{3}
whose classical and distributional one-categories are different, as well as whose
\dTC_{m}^{\mathcal{D}}
and
\TC_{m}^{\mathcal{D}}
values disagree.

Since the lens spaces
L^{2n-1}_{p}
are essential manifolds, we have
\cat_{1}(L^{2n-1}_{p})=2n-1
(
cf.
Section
6
). Therefore, for any
m\geq 2
, when
n>2^{m-1}p^{m}
, Example
8.6
and Proposition
7.3
give us

\dcat_{1}(L^{2n-1}_{p})\leq 2p-1<2n-1=\cat_{1}(L^{2n-1}_{p}),\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}

\dTC^{\mathcal{D}}_{m}(L^{2n-1}_{p})\leq\dcat_{1}((L^{2n-1}_{p})^{m})\leq(2p)^{m}-1<2n-1\leq\TC^{\mathcal{D}}_{m}(L^{2n-1}_{p}),

where the second inequality is obtained using
\dcat_{1}(X^{m})\leq(\dcat_{1}(X)+1)^{m}-1
, which is easy to show (see, for example,
[
Ja25a
, Section 3]
).
