Bipolar resistive switching in amorphous calcium zirconate memristors
Authors: Vithaldas Raja, Simran Nehra, Anjana Dogra, Ankur Rastogi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report the bipolar resistive switching characteristics of a simple two-terminal memristor based on an amorphous calcium zirconate (a-CaZrO3) thin film. The device, in a Au/a-CaZrO3/FTO configuration, demonstrated reliable and reproducible switching at low operating voltages (± 1 V). The memristor exhibits stable performance metrics, including a reliable endurance over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.2 \times 10^5$$\end{document} cycles, stable data retention for over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5.5 \times 10^3$$\end{document} s, and reliable cycle-to-cycle and device-to-device uniformity. Analysis of the current-voltage characteristics suggests a switching mechanism governed by the formation and rupture of oxygen vacancy-based conductive filaments, a model strongly supported by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy. These findings highlight amorphous CaZrO3 as a promising material for memristor based Boolean logic circuits and data encryption.

Introduction

In the era of data-driven technologies, such as Artificial Intelligence and Internet of things the demand for the development of new memory and parallel computing solutions required for sustainable growth has been at its highest point. The conventional flash memories employed in these technologies today face severe constraints in terms of latency, power consumption and are reaching the fundamental scaling limits of devices. Memristive devices have emerged as a strong alternative to existing memory technology. These devices are governed by different physical mechanisms such as spin-orbit torque1, Mott transition2, charge trapping/detrapping3, ferroelectric polarization4and nano-ionic redox processes5. Because of their high storage density and partial CMOS compatibility, devices based on nano-ionic redox processes have been explored extensively. Resistive Switching (RS) devices store data by changing the electrical resistance of the device under applied external electric field. The change in resistance of the device occurs due to the unique electrochemical processes taking place in the switching material (insulator) sandwiched between two metallic electrodes. Interestingly, these electrochemical processes are defect driven such that in the presence of point defects such as cations, anions or charged vacancies, the device tends to show better switching performance6,7. Depending on the chosen switching layer and electrodes the generation and migration of point defects can either lead to formation/dissolution of a conductive path8 or modulation of the Schottky barrier9. The performance of RS devices is determined by different characteristics such as the ON/OFF ratio, switching voltage, endurance cycles and retention time. Based on their output performance, RS devices can be implemented in many vital applications, such as memory devices10, logic circuits11, and parallel computing architectures12, etc.

It is challenging to observe all the above-mentioned characteristics suitable for all different applications in a single material system. Thus, the material of interest is chosen by considering the area of application. The RS phenomena has been extensively studied in oxide thin films for developing standard CMOS compatible devices. Binary metal oxides such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {TiO}_2$$\end{document}13, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {HfO}_2$$\end{document}14, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {TaO}_x$$\end{document}15,, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {SiO}_2$$\end{document}16 are some of the desired materials for studying resistive switching. However, even though superior resistive switching performance is observed in these materials, there has been recent interest in examining perovskite oxide for developing oxides-based electronics17,18, due to the presence of multi valence elements, coupled with their enhanced structural and chemical stability even in the presence of excess defects19–21. The metal-insulator transition (MIT) property of mixed valence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {La}_{1-x}\hbox {Sr}_x\hbox {MnO}_3$$\end{document} perovskite was effectively utilized by Gonzalez-Rosillo et al.22 in developing a three terminal volume resistive switching device with the insertion of an oxygen migration capping layer. Mladenovi\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\acute{c}$$\end{document} et al.23 have computationally shown the interchange in switching behavior between filamentary and interfacial type resistive switching with change in the termination layer of crystalline \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {SrTiO}_{{3}}$$\end{document} based RS devices. This interchange in switching behavior was reported due to the tuning of the fermi level at the electrode-substrate interface (either \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {TiO}_2$$\end{document} or SrO termination). Chen et al.24 realized stable resistive switching behavior in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {LaCoO}_x$$\end{document} epitaxial thin films, where the switching material undergoes topotactic phase transformations due to oxygen ion migration. A comprehensive study on structure dependent resistive switching performance was presented by Schmitt et al.25. They have shown that by employing amorphous thin films as a counterpart to epitaxial films, the switching can be improved by one order of magnitude because of the higher oxygen vacancy diffusion coefficient in amorphous systems.

Among the different titanates, manganites based perovskites studied for resistive switching, zirconia-based perovskites have also garnered attention in recent times because of their tunable ionic conductivity with defect engineering26. These materials also possess good dielectric properties, which are suitable for developing reliable RS devices27,28. Theoretical investigations have shown that by doping or controlling the A site and B site stoichiometry the concentration of oxygen defects, ions or vacancies, can be tuned effectively26,29,30. This allows for better tuning of the resistive switching performance. By controlling the oxygen pressure during the deposition of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {SrZrO}_3$$\end{document} thin films, Lin et al.31 developed a bilayer RS device with a switching speed of 10 ns. The precise control of oxygen allowed them to grow oxygen-rich and oxygen-deficient layers and optimize the concentration of oxygen defects in the device. Ding et al.32 fabricated a rectifying resistive switching device using pulsed laser deposited \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {BaZrO}_3$$\end{document} thin films, which finds it appropriate use as a selector device in an array of resistive switching devices. The tuning of two switching modes between the bipolar and unipolar switching modes in one single polycrystalline \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {CaZrO}_3$$\end{document} based RS device, by controlling the current compliance of the device, was reported by Asif et al.33. A detailed comparison of resistive switching performance in AZrO3 (A = Ca, Sr, Ba) based devices is shown in supplementary table T1. The one common observation in these single and polycrystalline \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {AZrO}_3$$\end{document} (A = Ca, Sr, Ba) based switching devices is the high operating voltage, (> 5 V). Thus, the controllability of the switching performance in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {AZrO}_3$$\end{document} based RS devices by engineering the oxygen concentrations motivates further tailoring the resistive switching performance in these devices.

In this work, we study the resistive switching behavior of amorphous \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {CaZrO}_3$$\end{document} (a-CZO) thin films grown via the pulsed laser deposition (PLD) technique. The a-CZO thin films were deposited on fluorine doped tin oxide (FTO)-coated glass slides, and FTO served as the bottom electrode for studying the RS behavior. Gold-coated tungsten tips with a diameter of 10 μm were used as the top electrode on the bare surface of the as-deposited thin films. This allows rigorous testing of the switching performance of the material across different locations on the films, before proceeding with complete device fabrication. The use of probe tips also reduces the overall active cell/device area, when compared to top electrodes deposited using shadow masks. The a-CZO thin film-based device showed stable and repeatable bipolar resistive switching behavior at a switching voltage of ± 1 V for 200 I-V sweep cycles at 10 different locations across a film area of 4 × 4 mm2. We observed variability coefficient of less than 4% and 2% for SET and RESET voltages, respectively. Moreover, we found that in defect-rich a-CZO, the endurance cycles can be as good as 1.2 × 10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^5$$\end{document} cycles.

Device characterization

Here, we used a MIM device structure, in which amorphous \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {CaZrO}_3$$\end{document} served as the active material. FTO was used as the bottom electrode and gold (Au) plated tungsten tips with a diameter of 10 μm were used as the top electrode. All current-voltage (I-V) measurements were carried out under ambient conditions at room temperature. The bottom electrode was positively biased and the Au probe was connected to the ground. The voltage was swept in the direction 0 V → + 1.2 V → - 1.2 V → 0 V at a sweep rate of 200 V/s. As the applied voltage reached to a certain threshold value of ∼ 0.8 V (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {V}_{{SET}}$$\end{document}), the switching material underwent a soft breakdown and an abrupt increase in the current was observed. The device switches from its pristine high resistance state (HRS) to a low resistance state (LRS). Oxides based resistive switching devices usually require an electroforming process, to generate some field induced oxygen defects into the switching material which will ease the switching process37. This electroforming step is not essential for the device here, since the concentration of oxygen defects is high in a-CZO, as observed in XPS38. Further sweeping the voltage in the same direction shows an increase in current. As the voltage is swept in the reverse direction, the current traces a linear path, retaining the LRS mode of the device. To analyse the conduction mechanism in this device, we plotted the current voltage sweeps in double logarithmic plots based on different transport models (Space Charge Limited Conduction (SCLC), Poole Frenkel Emission, Schottky Emission, tunnelling). It is generally accepted that, space charge limited conduction is one of the dominant conduction39 mechanisms in filamentary type resistive switching33,39–41. The similar behaviour was observed in our device by utilizing linear curve fitting on data of one of the I-V sweep plotted in double logarithmic scale. The SCLC mechanism is governed by three regions, (i) ohmic conduction at low electric fields, (ii) trap associated conduction at higher electric fields, and (iii) the Child’s law as the traps are filled42. The signature of these regions is identified with change in slope values of the fitted curves, which varies in between 1 and 2. As shown in Fig. 3 (e) and (f), the measured I-V curve was plotted as ln(I) Vs ln(V) corresponding to voltage sweep direction (0 V → + 1.2 V → −1.2 V → 0 V), representing the SET and RESET transitions. As mentioned earlier, at lower applied voltages, Ohmic conduction will take place, and there will be some thermally generated charge carriers that will flow between the electrodes without any obstruction. Thus, in the HRS state from 0 V to ∼ 0.35 V, we observe a linear relation between the current and voltage (I ∝ V) as shown in region-I of Fig. 3(e). Further as the applied voltage is raised to ∼ 0.72 V, the curve slope increases to 1.26 which does not correspond to Ohmic or SCLC conduction mechanism, region-II of Fig. 3(e)41. Thus, upon fitting this region using ln(I) Vs sqrt(V) relation, a linear trend was observed corresponding to thermionic Schottky emission as shown in supplementary figure S1. These excess generated charges would help in overcoming the barrier at the electrode a-CZO film interface. With further increase in the applied voltage, above ∼ 0.72 V, the region-III of Fig. 3(e), injected electrons from the Ohmic electrode will dominate the current flow in the a-CZO film and the current increases quadratically43. After switching, the device follows ohmic conduction in the LRS mode. During the RESET process, from 0 V to ∼ −1.1 V, the device retains its LRS state and retraces a linear current path, as shown in region-A of Fig. 3(f). Furthermore, after a certain threshold RESET voltage, the filament starts to break, and the trapped electrons are detrapped; the injection of charge carriers from the electrode is also be limited, and device follows Ohmic conduction in HRS state(region-B, Fig. 3(f)).

Here, since the SET and RESET transitions occur at opposite voltage polarities, it represents bipolar nonvolatile resistive switching44. The I-V curve observed here resembles a typical filamentary type valence change resistive switching device45. In filamentary type switching devices, formation of conductive paths takes place between the top and bottom electrodes during the SET transition, and it breaks down during the RESET transition. The switching mechanism of this device will be discussed in detail in a later section.

To better understand the physical nature of this conductive path and verify if it exhibits purely metallic transport, we measured the temperature-resistance characteristics (supplementary Fig. S2) and observed decrease in the resistance. This indicates a negative temperature coefficient of resistance (TCR). A standard metallic filament would typically display a positive TCR due to enhanced electron-phonon scattering at higher temperatures. Our observation of a negative TCR infers that the localized filament is actually semiconducting, likely taking the form of a sub-stoichiometric channel dominated by oxygen vacancies. Using Arrhenius relationship, we also estimated a thermal activation energy of approximately 0.27 eV (supplementary Fig. S2). The movement of carriers through this defective filament is trap-driven, which gives rise to the non-linear SCLC or Schottky behaviour as discussed earlier. Temperature increase facilitates the detrapping of carriers from these relatively shallow states. This confirms that the filamentary path retains its semiconducting nature despite the localized nature of the switching.

Furthermore, to evaluate the suitability of the device for real time applications, we performed the endurance and retention characteristics of the device. First, we investigated the endurance of the device by applying a train of voltage pulses. The SET and RESET voltages were chosen from the I–V sweep measurements as + 0.8 V and – 1 V, respectively. These voltage values were selected such that there was no excess generation of field induced defects during the SET cycle and no complete rupture of the filament with the application of the RESET voltage pulse. A read voltage of + 0.2 V with a fixed pulse width of 2.5 ms was applied. Endurance cycles of ∼ 1.3 × 105 were measured, as shown in Fig. 4(a). However, when measuring the switching performance of a memristor device in pulsed voltage cycles, observation of stable resistive switching between the HRS and LRS may require some training cycles in comparison to the I-V sweep cycle. This can be due to both the shorter time of the applied set voltage which may not give enough time for proper redistribution of oxygen vacancies and formation a stable conducting filament, and the inherent stochastic nature of filamentary type resistive switching devices46. Moreover, after a certain number of cycles, once a stable conducting filament is established between the top and bottom electrode through repeated voltage pulsing cycles, the current levels for both HRS and LRS are stabilized. Similar wake-up effects related to oxygen vacancy distribution are often observed in hafnium oxide based ferroelectric switching devices47,48. Even with variation in the current magnitude for HRS and LRS, it is necessary to have a distinguishable gap between these states. Discussion

As mentioned earlier, the SET and RESET processes take place due to the formation and dissolution of conductive filaments between the top Au electrode and bottom FTO electrode. In Fig. 5, we propose a possible mechanism for resistive switching.Fig. 5Energy band structure and switching mechanism schematics of the Au/a-CZO/FTO device. (a) Energy band diagram before contact. Energy band diagram of device after contact and oxygen defect distribution in device (b, c) at zero bias, (d, e) during the SET process, (f, g) during the RESET process.

The work function of FTO and Au is ∼ 4.4 eV and 5.3 eV, respectively50,51. Defect free CaZrO3 is highly insulating, having a bandgap of ∼ 5 eV. However, as observed from XPS it is clear that there are a lot of oxygen defects in the a-CZO film. These defects create an energy state above the intrinsic Fermi energy (∼ 2.5 eV) in CZO. In its oxygen-deficient state, CZO behaves as an n-type semiconductor, because the missing oxygen atoms (oxygen vacancies) provide free electrons for conduction. This semiconducting behavior is strongly supported by the 0.27 eV thermal activation energy and negative TCR extracted from our temperature-dependent measurements. This configuration satisfies the condition lead to the formation of two back-to-back Schottky barriers of unequal height at the interfaces, which contributes to the resistive switching behavior. Here FTO will act as a more ohmic electrode compared to Au because of its higher oxygen affinity and would thus facilitate better oxygen interaction at the CZO/FTO interface. As shown in Fig. 5(a) and (b), under zero external bias, the system is in thermal equilibrium. Under the thermal equilibrium condition, the oxygen ions and vacancies will also be randomly distributed in a-CZO, as shown in Fig. 5(c). The Fermi levels of Au, CZO, and FTO align. Due to the work function mismatch, electrons from the CZO layer flow into the electrodes, creating depletion regions. This causes the conduction and valence bands within the oxide to bend upward at both interfaces, forming two Schottky barriers. The barrier at the Au/CZO interface is higher than that of the FTO/CZO interface. Under the application of a positive bias to the FTO bottom electrode (Fig. 5(d)), rearrangement of the oxygen defects present in terms of vacancies and ions takes place in the switching material. During the SET cycle as the voltage is swept from 0to +1.2 V, the negatively charged oxygen ions are attracted towards the positively biased FTO electrode. The positively charged oxygen vacancies would migrate from the bottom electrode to the top Au electrode, forming a conductive path. Apart from the intrinsic oxygen defects present in the switching material, there would also be the generation of some field-induced defects which would also participate in filament formation40,52. As the applied voltage reaches the VSET, the potential barrier at FTO/CZO interface is further reduced, allowing for a significant flow of electrons via stable filament established between the bottom and top electrodes (Fig. 5(e)). A further increase in voltage would only lead to the strengthening of the filament. Here, since we did not apply any forming voltage to initiate the switching process in the device, and we observed a high current flowing through the device (∼ 8 to 10 mA) at voltages less than 1 V, it can be safely assumed that the amorphous CaZrO3 film is rich in oxygen defects, as supported by XPS.

Upon sweeping voltage in reverse direction from +1.2 V to –1.2 V, the filament will be stable until the VRESET is reached. Furthermore, under the RESET cycle, the oxygen anions are pushed away from the FTO and can recombine with the oxygen vacancies. Thus, as the applied voltage approaches VRESET, the filament begins to rupture gradually, as shown in Fig. 5(f) and (g). As the current levels are high, there will also be secondary thermal effects such as Joule heating which would contribute to the breakdown of the filament. Once the filament breaks, the device switches back to its high resistance state. It should be noted that the filament may not dissolve completely depending on the applied voltage magnitude and speed. The partial rupture of filament can also be attributed to simultaneous thermal and electrochemical processes taking place53.

In summary, this work demonstrates the fabrication and characterization of a memristor using amorphous CaZrO3 as the active switching layer with Au top and FTO bottom electrodes. The devices exhibit stable and reproducible bipolar resistive switching with suitable characteristics for practical applications. Key findings show low operating SET/RESET voltages below ±1 V, the absence of a required electroforming step, endurance of over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.2 \times 10^5$$\end{document} cycles, and reliable data retention lasting for more than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$6 \times 10^3$$\end{document} s. The devices also show notably low device-to-device and cycle-to-cycle variabilities. The conduction mechanism in the High Resistance State (HRS) was found to be governed by the Space Charge Limited Conduction (SCLC) behavior, and the switching behavior is attributed to the formation and rupture of conductive filaments due to oxygen vacancies. This mechanism is also supported by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, which confirmed the presence of a significant concentration of non-lattice oxygen in the as-deposited films. The combination of low-voltage operation and stable performance in a simple, amorphous oxide system makes CaZrO3 a promising material for the development of future low-power, high-density, nonvolatile memory for logic circuits and hardware based security systems.

Methods

A polycrystalline \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {CaZrO}_3$$\end{document} (CZO) target was synthesised by the solid-state reaction method. First, calcium carbonate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {CaCO}_3$$\end{document}) (SRL) and zirconium dioxide (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {ZrO}_2$$\end{document}) (Sigma Aldrich) powders were mixed in stoichiometric amounts (1:1) and hand ground in a mortar – pestle for 60 min. The mixture was then annealed at 1400 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}C for 5 h in a muffle furnace. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) was then added to the sintered powder as a binder to prepare the target. The mixture was again hand ground for 120 min and pressed into a one–inch target using a hydraulic press. The pressed target was again sintered at the same temperature of 1400 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document}C to make it denser. Amorphous thin films of CZO were deposited by the pulsed laser deposition (PLD) technique on commercially available fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)-coated glass substrates (Techinstro). The FTO substrates were sequentially cleaned in acetone and isopropyl alcohol (IPA) for 10 min each in an ultrasonic water bath. A KrF excimer laser (Coherent – COMpexPro 205) of wavelength, λ = 248 nm, was used as the target ablating source. The films were deposited in oxygen environment with oxygen partial pressure of 2.7 × 10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{-4}$$\end{document} mbar. The energy fluence was fixed at 1.05 J/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {cm}^2$$\end{document} with a repetition rate of 2 Hz. To ensure that the films did not crystallize during the deposition process, the substrate temperature was kept at 400 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^o$$\end{document}C. The materials were structurally characterized using a Bruker D8 x-ray diffractometer, and the elemental composition of the films was studied using the Thermofisher Nexsa x–ray photoelectron spectroscopy instrument. The electrical characterization was carried out using a source-measure unit (Keysight B2902B) in a probe station (Everbeing Intn’l Corp) under ambient conditions.
