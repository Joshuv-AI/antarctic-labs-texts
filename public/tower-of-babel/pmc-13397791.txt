HPV genotype distribution and cervical lesions in colposcopy-referred women in Western China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Lai Jiahan, Li Siqi, Mai Keliya, Han Lili
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHPV genotype distribution and sociodemographic patterns of cervical lesion severity remain under-characterized for colposcopy-referred populations in multiethnic western China. This evidence gap impedes precision triage for clinical populations selected through screening thresholds.ObjectivesTo delineate HPV genotype composition and examine sociodemographic profiles associated with biopsy-confirmed cervical lesion grades within a single-center hospital-based colposcopy-referred population in Xinjiang.MethodsCross-sectional analysis of 2,934 patients who underwent colposcopy with complete biopsy data at People’s Hospital of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region (January 2018–June 2025). Associations were evaluated using chi-square tests with standardized residual analysis. Multivariable binary logistic regression identified sociodemographic determinants of high-grade lesions (CIN2+), while restricted cubic splines characterized non-linear age effects. Joint logistic classifiers combining HPV16/18 positivity with ordinal TCT results were constructed, and pairwise AUC comparisons were performed using the DeLong test.ResultsWithin the referral population, high-grade lesions (CIN2+) constituted 44.2% of biopsy specimens. HPV16 accounted for 46.0% of HPV-positive detections, increasing to 70.4% among cervical cancer specimens. HPV52 (9.2%) and HPV58 (8.5%) formed a secondary genotype cluster. For CIN3+, the joint classifier significantly outperformed HPV16/18 or TCT alone (AUC 0.712), whereas for CIN2 + the incremental gain was marginal. Age distribution exhibited a dominant bimodal pattern (peaks at ~ 35 and ~ 51 years), with high-grade precancerous lesions concentrated among patients < 40 years and invasive cancer among those ≥ 50 years. After multivariable adjustment, illiteracy (aOR 2.83, 95% CI 1.58–5.07) and physical-labor occupation (aOR 0.54 vs. mental labor) remained independently associated with CIN2+.ConclusionIn this Xinjiang colposcopy-referred population, HPV16 predominates among high-grade lesions, while HPV52/58 constitute a consequential secondary cluster. These findings support a three-tiered conceptual framework for refining triage: genotype-informed cytology allocation, sociodemographic risk weighting alongside virological data, and intensified surveillance for postmenopausal women with abnormal screening history. Prospective external validation is required before operationalization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16209-6.

Introduction

Cervical cancer remains a global health priority, prompting WHO’s 2020 “Global Strategy to Accelerate the Elimination of Cervical Cancer” with 90–70–90 targets for 2030 [1–3]. The etiology of this malignancy is rooted in persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus genotypes. Notably, HPV-16 and HPV-18 are implicated in over 70% of global cases [4–6]. As the world’s most populous nation, China reported approximately 151,000 new cases and 56,000 deaths in 2022 [7]. The disease burden demonstrates pronounced geographic heterogeneity [8–10], with remote and economically disadvantaged regions exhibiting disproportionately elevated case numbers and mortality [11]. China recently integrated free bivalent HPV vaccination for specified adolescent females into its national immunization program [12], creating unprecedented opportunities for cancer control [13]. However, the concordance between vaccine-targeted HPV 16/18 and circulating genotypes in multi-ethnic populations of Western China remains inadequately characterized [14]. This knowledge gap constrains accurate assessment of vaccine effectiveness and localization of evidence-based strategies, particularly for patient populations undergoing colposcopic biopsy in tertiary care settings.

While community-based screening initiatives generate population-level prevalence data, the clinical and virological profile of the colposcopy-referred population—a high-risk population selected through positive screening thresholds—remains inadequately characterized for hospital-based settings. Notably, the observed genotype distribution in such disease-enriched referral funnels reflects selective enrichment of persistent, high-grade lesions rather than community viral circulation, precluding direct generalization to unscreened general populations.

This challenge is particularly acute in Xinjiang, where vast territory, ethnic diversity, and uneven healthcare distribution limit robust epidemiological data [15]. Optimization of triage protocols within resource-constrained settings constitutes a critical prerequisite for attaining WHO elimination targets. The WHO guideline for screening and treatment of cervical pre-cancer lesions explicitly endorses dual-stain cytology for triaging HPV-positive women [16]. The diagnostic performance of conventional screening modalities across distinct demographic subsets and the role of socioeconomic variables have received limited empirical attention [2, 17]. Furthermore, current evidence predominantly derives from community-based screening initiatives. Consequently, the clinical and virological profile of the colposcopy-referred population—a high-risk population directly informing management pathways—remains inadequately characterized for hospital-based settings [18].

Our team previously described HPV infection patterns among Xinjiang’s general female population, identifying HPV16 as predominant among rural Uyghur women in Hotan with distinctive local risk factors (early age at first marriage, multiple marriages) [19, 20]. providing an initial regional evidence base. However, a substantial evidence deficit persists regarding the colposcopy-referred population—a high-risk population triaged following positive screening [21]. Whether genotype distribution in this clinical population diverges from community-based patterns remains unknown. Moreover, the influence of specific virological and socioeconomic determinants on cervical lesion severity has not been systematically investigated.

Accordingly, this study systematically analyzes biopsy data from this pivotal colposcopy-referred population with two primary objectives: (1) delineate HPV genotype distribution and compare with our general population data; and (2) examine associations between ethnic background, geographical origin, socioeconomic circumstances and high-grade cervical lesions. The resulting evidence will inform integrated control strategies that bridge community screening initiatives with precision clinical management.

Methods

Research design and population

This single-center retrospective cross-sectional study included 6,488 consecutive patients who underwent colposcopy with complete cervical biopsy results at People’s Hospital of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region between January 2018 and June 2025. Of these, 2,934 met eligibility criteria and formed the analytic population. Demographics, HPV testing, and ThinPrep cytology were systematically extracted from electronic records. The institutional Ethics Committee approved the protocol (KY2022080502) and waived informed consent for retrospective use of de-identified data. All records were anonymized prior to analysis. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Referral pathways, eligibility criteria, and population assembly

Regional referral standards and pathways

Colposcopy referral followed China’s national tiered screening program. Women were referred when they met at least one of three thresholds: (i) HPV16/18-positive; (ii) non-16/18 high-risk HPV-positive with TCT ≥ ASC-US; or (iii) TCT ≥ LSIL (any HPV status), or persistent ASC-US with high-risk HPV. Referrals came from community programs, outpatient clinics, and regional hospitals. All women then underwent colposcopy, biopsy, HPV genotyping, and cytology at our center.

Temporal concordance of screening tests

HPV and TCT were required within 90 days before colposcopy. If multiple tests existed, the pair closest to the procedure date was selected. Women with only one test were retained if it alone met a referral threshold (e.g., HPV16/18-positive or TCT ≥ LSIL). Cases with non-16/18 HPV-positive as the sole test but without TCT were excluded, as triage could not be verified.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

(1) Colposcopy with complete cervical biopsy results at People’s Hospital of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region; (2) Complete demographics (age, ethnicity, education, residence, occupation); (3) Documented HPV testing (viral load/genotyping) and/or ThinPrep cytology (TCT).

Exclusion criteria

(1) Acute genital infection or vaginal interference (sexual activity, intravaginal medication, irrigation within 72 h) prior to colposcopy; (2) Prior treatment for cervical precancerous lesions (e.g., CIN) or related hysterectomy; (3) Pregnancy or lactation; (4) Missing key demographic data.

Of the 6,488 women who underwent colposcopy with complete biopsy results during the study period, 3,554 were excluded according to these criteria, yielding a final analytic population of 2,934 women (Fig. 1).

Data collection and variable definition

We extracted data from electronic records. Variables included: (1) Sociodemographics: age, ethnicity (Han, Uyghur, Kazakh, Hui, other), residence region (Northern/Southern Xinjiang), education (primary or below, middle school, high school, university, postgraduate), and occupation (physical labor, mental labor, retired); (2) Laboratory findings: high-risk HPV genotyping (with viral load in some cases) and ThinPrep cytology (TCT) classified as NILM, ASC-US, LSIL, or HSIL+; (3) Histopathology (diagnostic reference): colposcopy-directed biopsy results categorized as normal/inflammation, HPV infection alone, CIN1, CIN2, CIN3, or cervical cancer.

Histopathological assessment

Histopathological diagnoses were extracted from routine clinical reports. While formal inter-observer agreement specific to this retrospective population was not independently calculated, departmental quality-assurance audits for cervical pathology at our institution have documented moderate inter-pathologist concordance (Kappa = 0.59). All cases represent final consensus diagnoses rendered by senior gynecologic pathologists (≥ 5 years of experience), with intradepartmental consultation and selective p16 immunohistochemistry applied to ambiguous cases per institutional standard.

Standardized algorithms were applied to borderline diagnoses: (i) ‘CIN2-3’ (ungradable) was conservatively classified as CIN3 per the WHO 2014 discouragement of this indeterminate category; (ii) CIN1-2 discrepancies utilized p16 immunohistochemistry when available, defaulting to CIN1 if ambiguous; (iii) suspicious microscopic invasion required confirmation via deeper sections or clinical follow-up for cancer classification. Glandular lesions were managed as follows: AIS was merged into the CIN3 category due to comparable clinical management (excisional treatment), while invasive adenocarcinoma (all 29 cases in this referred population) was coded as the highest severity level alongside squamous carcinoma.

TCT and biopsy results were treated as independent variables; pairwise matching was not enforced because referrals were sometimes triggered solely by HPV16/18 positivity without concurrent TCT, lesion progression or sampling variation could occur within the 90-day window, and the two modalities serve distinct clinical purposes (screening vs. diagnosis). Missing TCT or HPV data resulted from incomplete documentation for patients referred directly based on prior external screening results or urgent clinical indications (e.g., visible cervical lesions requiring immediate biopsy without repeat cytology). Consequently, histopathology analysis comprised the full population (n = 2,934), TCT analysis included 2,280 women (77.7%), and HPV genotyping data were available for 2,021 patients (68.9%).

Specimen sampling methods

HPV sampling and detection

Cervical exfoliated cells were collected by a gynecologist who first removed excess mucus with a cotton swab, then used an HPV detection brush rotated five times clockwise at the external cervical os to facilitate mucosal cell adherence. The brush tips were placed separately into vials containing transport medium and stored at 2–8 °C until molecular testing.

HPV Viral Load Quantification. HPV DNA extraction and viral load quantification were performed following manufacturer protocols using a PCR-based assay (QIAGEN Enterprises Management Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

HPV Genotyping. HPV genotyping was performed using the HPV Genotyping Detection Kit (Hybribio Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China). This PCR-based reverse dot-blot hybridization assay detects 21 HPV genotypes, including 15 high-risk types (HPV16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68, 53) and 6 low-risk types (HPV6, 11, 42, 43, 44, 81).

ThinPrep Cytological Test (TCT)

Cellular specimens were collected identically to HPV sampling. Samples were processed by the ThinPrep 2000 system, Papanicolaou-stained, and independently interpreted by two senior cytopathologists blinded to clinical data. Final reports represented consensus diagnoses.

Colposcopy examination and pathological diagnosis

Experienced gynecologists performed colposcopy with targeted biopsies of acetowhite epithelium, iodine-negative zones, or suspicious lesions. Tissue specimens underwent formalin fixation, paraffin embedding, hematoxylin–eosin staining, and microscopic examination for definitive histopathological diagnosis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics and normality assessment

Variables were analyzed by measurement level and distribution. Categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages—composition ratios within the referral population, not population rates. Continuous variables were tested for normality (Shapiro-Wilk test); normally distributed data are expressed as mean ± SD, non-normal data as median(IQR).

Visualization of virological and pathological profiles

Stacked bar charts visualized relationships among HPV genotype, TCT, and histopathology in patients with complete data.

HPV genotype, cytology, and histopathological outcomes

Associations between HPV genotype and histopathology, and between TCT and histopathology, were assessed separately using Pearson’s chi-square test, with Cramer’s V quantifying effect size and standardized residuals identifying disproportionately contributing cells; because this involved multiple cell comparisons, Bonferroni adjustment was applied to the standardized residual P-values to control family-wise error rate. To quantify clinical discrimination for CIN2 + and CIN3+, binary classifiers were constructed using natural thresholds for HPV16/18 positivity, any high-risk HPV positivity, HPV16-only infection, HPV18-only infection, and TCT thresholds. Additionally, joint logistic classifiers combining HPV16/18 positivity with ordinal TCT results were fitted; predicted probabilities were used as continuous test scores, and diagnostic metrics for these joint models were derived at the Youden-index optimal threshold. Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, LR+, and LR− were computed as conditional probabilities within this referred population. AUC was estimated for each predictor, and pairwise AUC comparisons were performed using the DeLong test for correlated ROC curves.

Regression modeling

Ordinal logistic regression was initially considered for the six-tier pathological outcome (1 = normal/inflammation; 2 = HPV infection alone; 3 = CIN1; 4 = CIN2; 5 = CIN3 including AIS reclassified herein; 6 = invasive squamous or adenocarcinoma). However, the proportional odds assumption was severely violated (Brant test χ² = 307.59, df = 65, P < 0.001), driven primarily by age (χ² = 92.92, P < 0.001) and occupation (χ² = 21.36, P < 0.001). Consequently, multivariable binary logistic regression with biopsy-confirmed CIN2 + versus CIN1 or less was adopted as the primary analytic framework. As a supplementary sensitivity analysis, stratified binary logistic regression was performed across three clinically relevant cutpoints: (i) normal/inflammation or HPV infection alone versus CIN1 or worse; (ii) CIN1 or less versus CIN2+; and (iii) CIN2 or less versus CIN3+. These threshold-specific models simultaneously adjusted for age (continuous), ethnicity, region, education, and occupation. Results are reported in Supplementary Tables 8 and interpreted with caution given the exploratory nature of this sensitivity analysis.

Demographic stratification and histopathological distribution

Pearson chi-square tests assessed global associations between demographic variables (age group, ethnicity, education, occupation, and region) and histopathological categories. The chi-square test yields a single global P-value for each demographic–histopathology contingency table; these global P-values were not subjected to multiplicity correction because each test constitutes a single omnibus hypothesis. Where the global association was significant, post-hoc standardized residual analysis was performed to identify cells contributing disproportionately to the chi-square statistic; because this involved multiple pairwise cell comparisons, Bonferroni adjustment was applied to the standardized residual P-values to control family-wise error rate. The number of comparisons equaled the product of demographic categories and histopathology categories within each table. Category-specific deviations were further quantified by odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals.

Age distribution and non-linear risk modeling

To characterize age patterns without assuming unimodality, Hartigans’ dip test was applied as a formal test of unimodality. Exploratory Gaussian mixture modeling (GMM) was subsequently performed with 1–3 components and model selection by Bayesian information criterion (BIC). The dip test rejected unimodality (D = 0.0179, P < 0.001), indicating the distribution departed from a single peak. GMM identified a statistically favored three-component equal-variance model (BIC = − 22,501.6), comprising means at 35.3, 50.8, and 64.2 years. Because the third component carried only 6.9% weight and represented a small postmenopausal tail, we report the two dominant components (combined weight 93.1%) as an exploratory clinical decomposition to aid visual interpretation, while acknowledging that formal model selection supports three components. Age differences across the six histopathological tiers were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test with epsilon-squared effect size, followed by Dunn pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment. Within the primary multivariable framework, restricted cubic splines (RCS) with four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles (ages 27, 39, 49, and 63 years) were fitted in the binary logistic regression model for CIN2 + to avoid arbitrary age categorization; a likelihood-ratio test evaluated non-linearity against a linear age term. Adjusted odds ratios at selected ages were calculated using the population median (44 years) as the reference.

Software and significance level

Analyses were performed in R (v4.3.3). P < 0.05 indicated significance. This descriptive exploratory analysis identifies potential associations without inferring causality or population-level epidemiology.

Results

To check for selection bias, we compared included and excluded women (Supplementary Table 1). Age and ethnicity were similar between groups (P > 0.05).

Basic characteristics of the research subjects

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the final referral population (n = 2,934), selected from 6,488 women who underwent colposcopy at our institution between January 2018 and June 2025. Mean age was 44.11 ± 11.35 years. Histopathology revealed normal tissue or inflammation in 655 women (22.3%) and various cervical lesions in 2,279 (77.7%); these percentages represent composition ratios within this referred population, not population rates. The cohort was predominantly Han (48.3%) and Uyghur (34.1%), with 79.4% residing in Northern Xinjiang. Over half (55.0%) had attained university education or higher. The main occupational categories were mental workers (47.3%) and manual workers (38.2%).

HPV genotype distribution and cervical lesion spectrum in the colposcopy population

Among the 2,021 patients with complete HPV genotyping data, 1,515 (75.0%) had single HPV infection and 506 (25.0%) had multiple HPV infections, yielding 2,249 HPV-positive events. Because of multiple infections, the event count exceeds the number of women. HPV genotype distribution and lesion spectrum are illustrated in Fig. 2. High-grade lesions (CIN2+) comprised 44.2% of biopsies from this colposcopy-referred population (Fig. 2A). HPV 16 predominated (46.0% of HPV-positive cases in the referral population), with HPV 52 (9.2%) and 58 (8.5%) detected more frequently than HPV 18 (8.0%) (Fig. 2B). TCT abnormalities increased with lesion severity: HSIL+ rose from 4.5% in normal/inflammation to 33.3% in carcinoma, while NILM declined from 44.7% to 26.4% (Fig. 2C), HPV 16 proportions increased markedly across histopathological grades, from 34.4% in normal/inflammation to 70.4% in carcinoma; HPV 58 was notably enriched in CIN2–3 (Fig. 2D). Among 29 adenocarcinoma cases, 25 had valid HPV genotyping (4 missing due to external referral). HPV16 was detected in 16 (64.0%) and HPV18 in 9 (36.0%); no case carried HPV16/18 co-infection or non-16/18 genotypes alone (Supplementary Table 12). HPV18 was significantly more common in adenocarcinoma than in squamous cell carcinoma (Fisher exact test: OR 8.14, P < 0.001). These findings are exploratory given the limited sample size.

Association of HPV genotype and TCT findings with histopathological outcomes

In the three-step screening framework, HPV genotype and TCT are primary screening tools and biopsy is definitive. We assessed their predictive value in this referred population.

HPV genotype associated significantly with histopathology (χ² = 305.63, P < 0.001; Cramer’s V = 0.165) (Fig. 3A). By Cohen’s conventions, this indicates small-to-moderate discrimination. Thus, HPV genotype alone has limited ability to predict exact histological grade in this referred population. Standardized residuals showed HPV16 correlated strongly with high-grade lesions. CIN3 (8.11, P < 0.001) and cancer (8.74, P < 0.001) exceeded expected values, while normal/inflammatory tissue showed fewer HPV16 cases than expected (-5.06, P < 0.001). HPV52, 58, and 56 associated mainly with lower-grade lesions (infection, CIN1, CIN2).

As binary classifiers for CIN2+, HPV16/18 positivity (including co-infections) showed AUC 0.640 (95% CI: 0.615–0.664), sensitivity 67.4%, and specificity 60.5% (Table 2). HPV16-only infection had a CIN2 + proportion of 68.3% among positives (455/666), sensitivity 46.5%, and specificity 77.6%. Any HR-HPV positivity showed AUC 0.511 (95% CI: 0.503–0.520), sensitivity 98.3%, and specificity 4.0%. DeLong tests showed no significant difference between HPV16/18 and HPV16-only (P = 0.066), but both outperformed any HR-HPV positivity (both P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 5).

Among HPV16-detected events, CIN2 + accounted for 70.8% (731/1,032); for HPV18 this was 54.2% (97/179), and for HPV52/58 combined it was 54.2% (215/397) (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Table 6). Because of multiple infections, these proportions are composition ratios at the detection-event level, not per-woman rates.

TCT showed a stronger association (χ² = 258.81, P < 0.001; Cramer’s V = 0.242) (Fig. 3B). HSIL correlated with CIN3 (+ 7.45, P < 0.001) and cancer (+ 6.12, P < 0.001). LSIL concentrated in CIN1 (+ 5.83, P < 0.001). ASC-US showed no specific predilection (all residuals < |2.0|).

As an ordinal predictor, TCT achieved AUC 0.630 (95% CI: 0.603–0.656). It did not differ significantly from HPV16/18 (P = 0.602) or HPV16-only (P = 0.630), but outperformed any HR-HPV positivity (P < 0.001) (Table 2, Supplementary Table 5).

At the HSIL+ threshold, TCT showed specificity 97.0%, sensitivity 21.9%, PPV 88.1%, and NPV 54.6%. At the LSIL+ threshold (ASC-US as negative), sensitivity rose to 40.8%, specificity fell to 74.7%, PPV was 62.7%, and NPV was 54.8% (Supplementary Table 4).

To test whether cytology adds value beyond HPV genotyping, we built joint logistic classifiers combining HPV16/18 positivity with ordinal TCT results. For CIN2+, the joint model achieved AUC 0.658 (95% CI: 0.634–0.682), a marginal improvement of 0.018 over HPV16/18 alone (P = 0.048) and a non-significant gain of 0.028 over TCT alone (P = 0.118). At the Youden-index optimal threshold, sensitivity was 65.4%, specificity 63.9%, and PPV 65.3% (Table 2, Supplementary Table 4). For the stricter CIN3 + endpoint (CIN3, squamous cell carcinoma, and adenocarcinoma), the joint model achieved AUC 0.712 (95% CI: 0.689–0.735). It significantly outperformed HPV16/18 alone (ΔAUC = 0.050, P < 0.001) and TCT alone (ΔAUC = 0.034, P = 0.005), with sensitivity 80.2%, specificity 84.3%, and PPV 72.2% (Table 2, Supplementary Tables 4–5).

Association between demographic characteristics and distribution of cervical lesions

We examined how age, education, and occupation related to biopsy-confirmed lesion severity. Details are in Supplementary Table 7.

General demographic characteristics

Age

Age associated significantly with lesion severity (χ² = 239.29, P < 0.001; Cramer’s V = 0.14). Standardized residuals showed a bimodal pattern (Fig. 4A). Women under 40 had more CIN2/3 than expectedresidual > + 2.45 (Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.05). Women aged 50 and above, especially over 60, had far more invasive cancer (residual = + 9.90, Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001). Formal testing rejected a single peak (Hartigans’ dip test D = 0.0179, P < 0.001). Exploratory mixture modeling favored three components (BIC = − 22,501.6) with means at 35.3, 50.8, and 64.2 years. The first two components carried 93.1% of the distribution (49.2% and 43.9%). These represent the dominant bimodal pattern (peaks near 35 and 51 years). The third component (6.9%) is a small elderly tail. The two main peaks are about 2.4 standard deviations apart (SD = 6.58 years), so they overlap substantially. The distribution is better described as a broad bimodal shoulder than as two sharp peaks. Age differed across six histopathological tiers (Kruskal-Wallis H = 215.46, P < 0.001, ε² = 0.074). Invasive carcinoma had the highest median age (53.0 years, IQR 46–60) and was significantly older than all precursor lesions (all P < 0.001). CIN2 had the lowest median age (40.0 years, IQR 33–48). CIN1, CIN2, and CIN3 did not differ significantly (Supplementary Table 9, Supplementary Fig. 2). After adjusting for ethnicity, region, education, and occupation (Table 3), the linear age effect weakened (aOR per 10-year increase 1.04, 95% CI: 0.99–1.10, P = 0.372). This suggests the raw age gradient was partly confounded by social factors. Restricted cubic splines showed a significant non-linear pattern (likelihood-ratio test χ² = 24.82, df = 2, P < 0.001). Risk followed three phases: an early peak near age 35 (aOR 1.20, 95% CI: 1.06–1.34), a midlife low at 45–50 years (aOR 0.86, 95% CI: 0.77–0.96 at age 50), and a steep late rise after 55 (aOR 2.32, 95% CI: 1.48–3.62 at age 70), using the population median as reference (Supplementary Table 11, Supplementary Fig. 3).

Ethnicity and region

After adjusting for age, education, occupation, and region, Uyghur women had lower odds of CIN2 + than Han women (aOR 0.74, 95% CI: 0.61–0.90, P = 0.003). Kazakh women also had lower odds (aOR 0.72, 95% CI: 0.55–0.94, P = 0.017). Regional differences between Northern and Southern Xinjiang were not significant (aOR 1.09, 95% CI: 0.88–1.37, P = 0.427).

Education

Education associated inversely with lesion severity (χ² = 269.75, P < 0.001; Cramer’s V = 0.15). Women with primary education or below had more invasive cancer than expected (residual = + 13.66, Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001). Women with university education or above had less cancer but more precancerous detection (Fig. 4B). In multivariable analysis, lower education remained independently associated with CIN2+: illiterate women had an aOR of 2.83 (95% CI: 1.58–5.07, P < 0.001) and primary school graduates an aOR of 1.62 (95% CI: 1.19–2.20, P = 0.002) versus university-educated women (Table 3).

Occupation

Occupation associated significantly with lesion severity (χ² = 253.99, P < 0.001; Cramer’s V = 0.21). Manual workers had more invasive cancer than expected (residual = + 10.57, Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.001). Mental workers had less cancer but more early-stage lesions (Fig. 4C). In multivariable analysis, mental workers had lower odds of CIN2 + than physical laborers (aOR 0.54, 95% CI: 0.45–0.66, P < 0.001). Retired women also had lower odds (aOR 0.37, 95% CI: 0.28–0.49, P < 0.001) (Table 3).

In a supplementary sensitivity analysis, the protective effect of mental labor appeared to strengthen at higher severity cutpoints (aOR 0.61 for any lesion, 0.54 for CIN2+, and 0.46 for CIN3+; Supplementary Table 8). This pattern is exploratory. The proportional odds assumption was violated, so these models do not establish a statistically significant trend across thresholds.

Discussion

HPV16 predominance in a referral setting: selection bias and viral biology

All findings derive from a single-center colposcopy-referred population. The HPV16 predominance (46.0%) reflects the combined influence of clinical selection bias and viral biological properties. Consequently, generalization to the general community is precluded. These results are hypothesis-generating, and causal validation is not pursued [22–24].

HPV16 proportion in this referred population (46.0%) exceeds community screening benchmarks threefold. This disproportionate representation reflects how colposcopy triage enriches for persistent, progressive lesions. HPV16 integrates into host DNA at a 3–5 fold higher frequency than HPV52/58 within 12 months of infection, with median progression to CIN3 + of 5–8 years [25–27]. In contrast, HPV52/58-related lesions at the population median age (44.1 years) often regress or persist indolently, thereby rarely meeting referral thresholds. This “accelerated triage” mechanism—rather than true prevalence differences—explains why the genotype fell from 23.3% of CIN1 lesions to 8.1% of invasive cancers in this cross-sectional population [28–30]. A key limitation is the absence of viral load data, which prevents assessment of dose-response relationships between HPV16 DNA levels and lesion grade and limits mechanistic interpretation.

Biomarker discrimination in the referral funnel

The modest effect sizes for HPV genotype and TCT in this referred population confirm that both are screening triage tools, not diagnostic substitutes. By standard benchmarks, these associations are small to moderate. Knowing a woman’s HPV genotype or TCT result only marginally improves prediction of her exact biopsy grade. TCT cannot reliably rule out high-grade lesions at either threshold. Any HR-HPV positivity discriminates near-randomly (AUC 0.511), because most women in a colposcopy population already carry detectable high-risk genotypes.

HPV16 enrichment in CIN3 and cancer reflects its known oncogenic dominance. Yet about 30% of HPV16-positive women in this referred population had normal or low-grade tissue. This limits the positive predictive value of genotype-based triage. Conversely, HPV52/58-positive women had appreciable yields of high-grade lesions (46.6% and 62.3%; Supplementary Table 6). Non-HPV16 genotypes therefore co-occur with advanced disease at non-negligible rates. These patterns prevent genotype-only triage without histological confirmation. The high multiple-infection rate also prevents mutually exclusive per-woman risk attribution.

TCT and HPV16/18 showed comparable discrimination (AUC 0.630 versus 0.640; Table 2). Both fall below the threshold typically considered clinically useful for standalone triage. This ceiling is not a test failure but a mathematical consequence of referral enrichment. When the pre-test probability of CIN2 + already exceeds 40%, even well-calibrated biomarkers struggle to reclassify risk meaningfully.

We combined HPV16/18 positivity with ordinal TCT results in joint classifiers. For the broad CIN2 + endpoint, the gain was marginal (ΔAUC = 0.018, P = 0.048). Most HPV16/18-positive referrals already carried cytological abnormality, and most HSIL+ specimens were HPV16/18-positive. Combining two correlated predictors yields diminishing returns for CIN2+, which includes both progressive and regressive lesions.

For the stricter CIN3 + endpoint, the joint model improved substantially (AUC 0.712). Cytology and virology complement each other when the outcome is restricted to lesions requiring excision. Among HPV16/18-positive women with normal or equivocal cytology, the absence of HSIL+ lowers the probability of underlying CIN3+. When both signals align, the positive predictive value for advanced disease rises markedly. Dual-modal integration is therefore most informative for CIN3+.

Even so, the joint model remains constrained by referral enrichment. Its discrimination for CIN2 + stays below the clinically useful threshold. For CIN3 + it approaches but does not exceed this benchmark. The lack of histopathological predilection in the ASC-US category further shows why colposcopic biopsy must remain the diagnostic reference standard.

Sociodemographic determinants of lesion severity in the referral funnel

Education and occupation remained independently associated with CIN2 + after adjustment for age, ethnicity, and region. This suggests that social stratification affects lesion severity through pathways beyond basic demographics. In this referred population, women with no formal schooling or primary education only, and those in physical labor, had higher adjusted odds of high-grade lesions. These effects operate through mechanistic pathways distinctive to Xinjiang’s sociodemographic context: [31–33]

Age and screening access

The bimodal age distribution should not be read as two distinct biological cohorts. It may instead reflect structural filtering by China’s tiered screening system. The first peak near 35 years likely captures women who entered screening at ages 25–30 but faced geographic delays in follow-up [25, 34]. The second peak near 51 years likely reflects perimenopausal women undergoing screening transitions or presenting with bleeding symptoms. The small elderly tail at 64.2 years may represent postmenopausal women who missed earlier screening and reached colposcopy only after long delays.

RCS modeling supported this interpretation with a significant non-linear risk pattern (likelihood-ratio test χ² = 24.82, df = 2, P < 0.001). Risk peaked near age 35, possibly reflecting early marriage patterns and rapid HPV16 progression to CIN2/3 in newly screened women. Risk then fell at 45–50 years, perhaps because screened women with indolent infections had already been treated. After 55, risk rose steeply, likely reflecting women who had dropped out of screening for decades. The age distribution therefore describes when women reach colposcopy, while the risk function describes what lesions they carry. Together, they suggest that age in this setting is shaped by healthcare access over the life course.

Referral pathway reconstruction (Supplementary Table 13) confirmed age heterogeneity in route composition (χ² = 41.78, P < 0.001). Women aged 60 and above were over-represented in the cytology-primary pathway (34.3% versus ~ 20% in younger groups; residual = + 4.19). HPV-directed routes dominated among women under 40. This suggests the bimodal age distribution reflects different screening entry points across the life course, not distinct biological cohorts.

Education as a proxy for linguistic accessibility, not individual competence

Lower education in this setting may reflect language barriers rather than individual health literacy. Ethnic minority women with limited schooling often have restricted Mandarin proficiency, which makes medical communication harder. The shortage of multilingual screening materials and bilingual staff may explain why advanced disease concentrates among the least educated. Health systems should provide more language-concordant services rather than target individual education alone.

Occupation as a marker of mobility-induced discontinuity

The protective effect of mental labor and the higher risk among physical laborers cannot be attributed to occupational carcinogens. Cervical cancer has no known occupational cause. Instead, these patterns may reflect screening disruptions linked to population mobility. Seasonal labor migration and herding lifestyles can interrupt regular screening. Among manual workers, low precancer detection alongside high invasive cancer rates suggests a chain of missed screening, delayed triage, and late symptomatic presentation. This points to gaps in screening continuity for mobile populations, including Kazakh herders and Uyghur agricultural workers.

Geographic and ethnic variations: hypothesis-generating observations

Regional and ethnic stratification showed descriptive differences. HPV16 accounted for 60.3% of cases in southern Xinjiang versus 48.1% in northern Xinjiang. HPV39 was detected in 8.2% of Kazakh women, higher than in other ethnic groups. These figures reflect single-center referral patterns and differential screening access, not true community prevalence. They are descriptive and require further study.

The multivariable model showed lower adjusted odds of CIN2 + among Uyghur women (aOR 0.74, 95% CI: 0.61–0.90) and Kazakh women (aOR 0.72, 95% CI: 0.55–0.94) versus Han women, after adjusting for education, occupation, and region. This likely reflects differential screening access and referral selection rather than biological protection. Women from remote ethnic minority areas may reach colposcopy only after long delays or symptomatic presentation. This can either enrich the denominator with normal or low-grade findings, or concentrate advanced disease in a small subset. Single-center data cannot distinguish these pathways. We caution against interpreting these associations as evidence of ethnic biology or generalizing them to unscreened populations.

Social network or genetic factors such as HLA polymorphisms may contribute to these patterns. However, validation would need viral phylogeography and ethnographic data. Our single-center referral data do not support conclusions about true population-level differences [2, 35].

Methodological constraints and a progressive precision pathway

This study comes from the largest gynecologic referral center in Xinjiang, which handles a substantial share of provincial colposcopy cases. Referral enrichment here exposes systemic gaps rather than serving merely as statistical noise. Several limitations affect interpretation:

Retrospective design and missing data

Electronic records lacked standardized collection. Some excluded women had missing occupation or education data (Supplementary Table 1). We had no information on prior screening history, so we could not distinguish incident from prevalent disease.

Temporal misclassification

TCT and HPV were required within 90 days of colposcopy. Lesions could progress or regress within this window, which may have weakened associations between screening results and biopsy findings.

Unmeasured confounding

We lacked data on smoking, contraception, parity, sexual behavior, HPV vaccination, and prior screening. These may confound sociodemographic associations. For example, lower education may link to lower vaccination coverage.

Pathology interobserver variability

Departmental audits showed moderate agreement (Kappa = 0.59), indicating uncertainty around the CIN2/3 threshold. p16 staining was used for ambiguous cases but not universally, which may have caused some misclassification.

Incident versus persistent infection

Cross-sectional data cannot estimate type-specific progression rates. Our genotype-lesion associations describe conditional distributions in a referred population, not causal risk. Prospective cohorts in Central Asia are needed.

Missing viral load and HLA data

Future studies should include these molecular markers. Without them, factors that modify HPV16 progression cannot be resolved.

Small adenocarcinoma sample

The 29 cases lacked power to confirm the 36% HPV18 proportion. This is consistent with Swedish data (35–52%) [36], but wide confidence intervals preclude firm conclusions.

Policy implications

The high HPV16 enrichment, especially in southern Xinjiang, may inform triage priorities. However, mobile populations, younger married women, and elderly dropouts face distinct barriers that need targeted interventions rather than uniform rollout [37–39]. Single-center data identify local gaps; they do not provide universal solutions.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study describes the virological and sociodemographic patterns among 2,934 colposcopy-referred women in Xinjiang. HPV16 dominates among high-grade lesions and cancer. HPV52 and HPV58 form a secondary cluster with intermediate risk. Cytology adds discrimination mainly for CIN3+, not for the broader CIN2 + endpoint. Age-related risk is non-linear. Lower education and manual labor remain independently associated with advanced disease, suggesting that social factors explain risk beyond virology. These findings support a three-tiered triage concept: (i) genotype-informed cytology allocation for HPV16/18-positive women; (ii) sociodemographic risk weighting alongside virological data for non-16/18 genotypes; and (iii) intensified surveillance for postmenopausal women with abnormal screening history. These hypotheses are exploratory and limited to this single-center referred population. Prospective validation is needed before clinical use. Causal inference and generalizable rates require dedicated cohort studies in this region.
