Antenatal Care Utilization, Maternal Satisfaction, and Access Barriers in Mogadishu, Somalia: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Shafie Abdulkadir Hassan, Muniro Hassan Nor, Fadumo Abdullahi Hirsi, Hamdi Hussein Sabrie, Amina Mohamed Abdullahi, Najma Mohamud Hassan, Ahmed Nor Osman Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntenatal care (ANC) is critical for reducing maternal and neonatal mortality but remains severely compromised in fragile, conflict-affected settings. This study assessed ANC utilization, maternal satisfaction, and access barriers among pregnant women in Mogadishu, Somalia, a setting marked by protracted conflict and among the world’s highest maternal mortality ratios.MethodsA facility-based cross-sectional study using consecutive convenience sampling was conducted between March and May and 2026 at four Maternal and Child Health (MCH) centers in the Hodan and Karaan districts of Mogadishu. Structured, face-to-face interviews were administered to 445 postpartum women, capturing sociodemographic characteristics, ANC attendance, satisfaction, and barriers. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, Pearson’s correlation, and multivariable logistic regression (p < 0.05).ResultsAmong 445 participants (median age 31 years; 80.0% urban), 60.0% initiated ANC by 9 weeks, yet only 6.5% completed the recommended four visits (39.0% attended two). Overall satisfaction was low (36.0%), with marked dissatisfaction regarding interpersonal communication (64.0%), provider competence (60.0%), and waiting times (57.0%). Multivariable analysis identified maternal education and parity as independent predictors of ANC completion, while education was the only independent predictor of satisfaction. Commonly reported barriers included favoritism (n=191), irregular facility hours (n=191), lack of privacy (n=189), restricted movement due to insecurity (n=145), and disrupted medical supplies (n=96).ConclusionDespite promising early ANC initiation, continuous engagement in Mogadishu is associated with profound maternal dissatisfaction, systemic inequities, and a challenging security environment; given the cross-sectional, convenience-sample design, these associations should be interpreted cautiously. Interventions extending beyond infrastructure, including strengthened respectful maternity care, stricter facility governance, and community-based midwifery, may help sustain ANC engagement in this fragile setting.Keywords: antenatal care, maternal health, patient satisfaction, healthcare access barriers, fragile and conflict-affected settings, Somalia

Introduction

Antenatal care (ANC) is a fundamental pillar of maternal and neonatal health, providing a critical window for health promotion, disease prevention, and the early detection of pregnancy-related complications.1 The World Health Organization (WHO) emphasizes the importance of early engagement with healthcare services, recommending that a woman’s first ANC contact occurs within the first 12 weeks of gestation, with a minimum of eight contacts throughout the pregnancy.2 Adequate and timely ANC utilization significantly reduces the risks of maternal mortality, stillbirths, and neonatal deaths by ensuring access to essential services such as nutritional supplementation, immunization, and the management of obstetric conditions.3

However, achieving optimal ANC coverage remains a formidable challenge in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly in fragile and conflict-affected settings. In Sub-Saharan Africa, maternal and neonatal health indicators are disproportionately poor, largely driven by systemic vulnerabilities and the disruption of health services during armed conflicts.4 In these environments, pregnant women face compounding barriers, including the destruction of healthcare infrastructure, severe shortages of medical supplies, and the mass displacement of populations, all of which severely restrict access to life-saving maternal care.5

Somalia exemplifies these profound challenges. Decades of prolonged insecurity, political instability, and recurrent climate shocks have deeply fractured the nation’s health system. Consequently, Somalia has one of the highest maternal mortality ratios globally, estimated at 692 deaths per 100,000 live births, alongside distressingly high neonatal mortality rates.6 The maternal healthcare landscape is characterized by heavily damaged or inadequate infrastructure and a critical shortage of skilled healthcare workers, heavily concentrated in urban centers.7 Furthermore, the ongoing conflict has resulted in millions of internally displaced persons (IDPs) who live in precarious conditions with severely limited access to basic health services.8 For pregnant women in Somalia, accessing ANC is often hindered by formidable transportation difficulties, pervasive financial constraints, and severe security risks restricting safe movement. Additionally, cultural norms, low maternal education, and a lack of awareness regarding the importance of ANC further suppress health-seeking behaviors.9

Beyond access, patient satisfaction is a pivotal determinant of healthcare utilization. Satisfaction reflects a woman’s subjective evaluation of her care, encompassing interpersonal communication, perceived provider competence, waiting times, and privacy.10 In fragile settings like Somalia, where trust in institutions may be eroded, a negative experience during an initial ANC visit can deter a woman from completing the recommended schedule or seeking skilled care during delivery.11 Evaluating satisfaction is therefore just as crucial as measuring access.

Despite the urgency of this public health crisis, empirical evidence regarding maternal healthcare dynamics in Somalia remains sparse. Understanding the specific patterns of ANC utilization, the levels of patient satisfaction, and the context-specific barriers preventing care is essential for guiding evidence-based humanitarian interventions and health policy formulation. Therefore, this study aimed to assess antenatal care utilization, satisfaction, and barriers among pregnant women in Mogadishu, Somalia.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted between March and May 2026 at four Maternal and Child Health (MCH) centers in the Hodan and Karaan districts of Mogadishu, Somalia (two centers per district). The study methodology and findings are reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for cross-sectional research.12 These MCH centers are among the main public facilities providing maternal and child health services within their districts, serving a diverse population including residents and nearby IDP settlements. They provide antenatal, delivery, and postnatal care services to the surrounding community.

Study Population

The study population comprised pregnant women who had successfully completed their pregnancies and delivered at one of the four participating MCH centers during the study period. Selecting women in the immediate postpartum period ensured that participants had fully experienced their pregnancy journey, allowing for a complete retrospective assessment of their ANC utilization and experiences throughout all three trimesters. The explicit inclusion criteria required participants to be postpartum women who accessed the facility for delivery. We excluded critically ill women, those unable to communicate due to severe medical complications, and those who declined to provide informed consent.

The minimum required sample size was determined using the Cochran formula for cross-sectional surveys.13 Assuming an expected ANC utilization prevalence of 50% (due to the lack of updated national registries), a 95% confidence interval, and a 5% margin of error, the calculated sample size was 384 participants. To account for the highly transient nature of the study population which includes many IDPs and women from insecure surrounding districts convenience sampling was employed. Eligible women available in the postnatal wards across the four centers who met the inclusion criteria were approached consecutively until the target sample size was achieved.

Data Collection Tool and Study Variables

Data were collected using a structured, face-to-face, interviewer-administered questionnaire. A face-to-face method was deliberately chosen over self-administered questionnaires due to the high rates of maternal illiteracy in the region, ensuring accurate comprehension and maximizing response rates. Trained bilingual female nurses conducted the interviews in the local Somali language.

The questionnaire was developed following an extensive literature review and adapted from validated tools used in similar regional contexts, specifically an ANC satisfaction questionnaire and an ANC barriers tool previously utilized in Ethiopia.14–16 The tool was contextually modified for the Somali setting and captured a comprehensive overview of the independent and dependent variables across four sections:

Sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics (Independent Variables): This included maternal age, residence (urban vs rural/IDP camp), educational level, socio-economic status, distance to the health facility, occupation, parity, planned status of the pregnancy, and number of living children.

Satisfaction levels: Assessed via 8 items regarding various domains of ANC, including respectful treatment, confidentiality, interpersonal communication, waiting times, provider competence, and counseling on pregnancy complications.

The questionnaire was pre-tested on 20 postnatal women at a different health center in Mogadishu to ensure cultural appropriateness and clarity; these responses were excluded from the final analysis. The internal consistency of the 8-item maternal satisfaction scale was evaluated, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.57. While slightly below the traditional 0.70 threshold, this level of reliability is commonly observed and considered acceptable for binary-response scales and newly adapted tools utilized in exploratory research within fragile, cross-cultural settings.

Outcome Variables

The primary outcome variables were ANC utilization and patient satisfaction. ANC utilization was evaluated based on two parameters: early initiation (defined as the first ANC visit occurring within the first 12 weeks of gestation) and adequate attendance. Adequate attendance was defined as completing a minimum of four ANC visits, aligning with the minimum realistic threshold commonly used in local public health tracking for fragile settings, where the current WHO recommendation of eight contacts remains logistically unfeasible for the vast majority of the population. Antenatal care satisfaction was operationalized as a binary outcome (satisfied vs unsatisfied). Overall satisfaction was determined alongside satisfaction within the specific care domains mentioned above.

Data Analysis and Management

Data cleaning and statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software within the RStudio integrated development environment. Missing data accounted for <1% of total responses; therefore, listwise deletion was employed to handle missingness, exerting a negligible impact on analytical integrity. Descriptive statistics were generated to summarize demographic profiles, utilization rates, and barrier frequencies. Continuous variables with skewed distributions were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Inferential statistics were utilized to explore associations. Cross-tabulations and Chi-square tests were employed to assess relationships between categorical variables, such as residency status and ANC satisfaction. Pearson’s correlation analysis, reported with 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs), was conducted to examine the linear relationships between continuous variables, such as maternal age and the number of ANC visits attended.

Furthermore, to identify independent predictors of ANC completion (≥4 visits) and maternal satisfaction, multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted. The regression models were adjusted for potential confounding variables identified a priori, including maternal age, residence, level of education, parity, occupation, and distance to the health facility. Results from the regression models are presented as Adjusted Odds Ratios (AORs) with their respective 95% CIs. Statistical significance was established at a p-value of < 0.05.

Results

Sociodemographic and Maternal Health Characteristics

A total of 445 pregnant women were included in this study. The median age of the participants was 31.0 years (IQR: 27.0–35.0), and the median age at first birth was 20.0 years (IQR: 18.0–22.0). The majority of the participants resided in urban areas (80.0%, n = 356). More than half of the women had no formal education (56.0%, n = 251), while 20.0% (n = 91) had primary education and 16.0% (n = 73) had secondary education. Most participants (72.0%, n = 320) lived near a health facility.

Regarding maternal characteristics, the median number of pregnancies was 4.0 (IQR: 3.0–6.0). Only 15.0% (n = 66) of the women reported that their current pregnancy was planned. The majority of the participants were housewives (61.0%, n = 273), followed by self-employed women (18.0%, n = 80) and those in private business (15.0%, n = 66) (Table 1).

Utilization of Antenatal Care Services

Antenatal care (ANC) utilization varied among participants. The highest proportion of women attended exactly two ANC visits (39.0%, n = 173), followed by one visit (29.0%, n = 128), and three visits (26.0%, n = 115). Only 6.5% (n = 29) of the women completed the recommended minimum of four visits. Over half of the participants initiated their ANC visits early, with 32.0% (n = 142) starting at 9 weeks and 28.0% (n = 125) starting at 4 weeks (Table 1).

Pearson’s correlation analysis (Table 2) revealed a significant negative correlation between ANC starting time and the total number of ANC visits (r = −0.154, 95% CI: [−0.24, −0.06], p = 0.001). Although statistically significant, this represents a modest effect size, indicating that women who initiated care earlier in their pregnancy attended a slightly higher number of ANC sessions. Additionally, a higher age at first birth was significantly correlated with earlier ANC initiation (r = −0.189, 95% CI: [−0.28, −0.10], p < 0.001), also reflecting a weak but statistically significant linear relationship. Conversely, maternal age (p = 0.530), number of previous pregnancies (p = 0.232), and family size (p = 0.236) showed no significant correlation with the number of ANC visits attended.

Satisfaction with Antenatal Care Services

Overall satisfaction with ANC services was notably low, with only 36.0% (n = 161) of the participants reporting that they were satisfied with the care received. When assessing specific components of care, the highest levels of satisfaction were reported for advice on pregnancy complications (57.0%, n = 253) and advice on birth plans (52.0%, n = 230).

Conversely, high levels of dissatisfaction were observed regarding interpersonal communication skills of the staff (64.0% unsatisfied, n = 287), perceived provider competence (60.0% unsatisfied, n = 267), waiting times (57.0% unsatisfied, n = 254), and appointment availability (56.0% unsatisfied, n = 247) (Table 3). Chi-square testing demonstrated that overall satisfaction with ANC services significantly differed based on the participant’s place of residence (Urban vs Rural) (χ2 = 9.81, p = 0.002).

Predictors of ANC Completion and Maternal Satisfaction

Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of completing the recommended minimum of four ANC visits (Table 4). Maternal age (AOR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.04–1.24, p = 0.004) and formal education were significant positive predictors, with women holding tertiary education exhibiting substantially higher odds of completion (AOR = 7.68, 95% CI: 1.93–34.0) compared to those with no formal education. Conversely, higher parity was independently associated with reduced odds of ANC completion (AOR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.37–0.72, p < 0.001).

Barriers to Accessing Healthcare Services

Participants reported multiple structural, systemic, and security-related barriers to accessing ANC. The most frequently cited systemic barriers were favoritism in the provision of health services (n = 191) and the irregular opening hours or inconvenient service times of health facilities (n = 191). A lack of privacy during ANC follow-ups was also a prominent issue, reported by 189 participants. Chi-square analysis revealed a highly significant association between residence (Urban vs Rural) and dissatisfaction with privacy during ANC visits (χ2 = 18.55, p < 0.001), as well as challenges with appointment availability and adherence (χ2 = 18.42, p < 0.001).

Security-related barriers were also prevalent. A substantial number of women reported restricted movement to operational health facilities due to insecurity (n = 145). Furthermore, disruptions to medical supply availability (n = 96) and facility closures (n = 71) further hindered access to essential maternal care (Table 6). Additional logistical and financial constraints preventing regular ANC attendance were prominent. Specifically, a high proportion of women were deterred by a lack of transport, the absence of night-duty staff, and severe financial limitations (quantitative frequencies detailed in Figure 1).

Discussion

This study assessed antenatal care (ANC) utilization, maternal satisfaction, and barriers to access among pregnant women in a fragile, conflict-affected setting in Somalia. Understanding these distinct but interconnected elements is critically important for improving maternal health services in regions characterized by protracted instability. In contexts where health systems are severely fragmented by ongoing conflict, routine maternal care is often relegated to emergency response, leaving significant gaps in preventive services. Evaluating not only the frequency and timing of ANC visits but also the perceived quality of care and the structural barriers hindering access is essential for designing evidence-based, context-specific interventions. Such comprehensive assessments are necessary to reduce maternal and neonatal morbidity and to build resilient healthcare systems capable of delivering equitable, patient-centered care amid systemic fragility.

In assessing the specific patterns of ANC utilization, our findings revealed a demographic profile characterized by high parity and early childbearing, alongside paradoxical utilization trends. The median maternal age was 31 years (IQR 27–35), with a median age at first birth of 20 years (IQR 18–22) and a median of 4 pregnancies (IQR 3–6). Notably, only 15% of the women reported their pregnancies as planned. Despite this high-risk obstetric profile, ANC completion rates were alarmingly low. While a substantial proportion initiated care early 32% starting at 9 weeks and 28% at 4 weeks only 6.5% completed the previously recommended minimum of four ANC visits, let alone the eight contacts currently endorsed by the WHO ANC recommendations (2016).2 We utilized the four-visit threshold as it remains the most realistic minimum standard commonly used in local public health tracking for fragile settings, where achieving eight contacts is logistically unfeasible for the vast majority of the population. Most women attended exactly two visits (39%), one visit (29%), or three visits (26%). This low completion rate diverges significantly from global targets and aligns with the suboptimal utilization reported by Lateef et al (2024) and Yoseph A et al (2026) in other resource-constrained settings.4,17 However, the early initiation observed here contrasts with the findings of previous studies, which typically characterize low-income settings with pervasive late ANC booking.18,19 The phenomenon of relatively early initiation followed by massive drop-out, also partially reflected in recent study in Somalia, suggests that while awareness of pregnancy confirmation or early care may exist, severe systemic and logistical bottlenecks prevent continuous engagement.9 This “early initiation paradox” may occur because initial visits are primarily motivated by the need for pregnancy confirmation or emergency reassurance rather than a commitment to long-term preventive care. Women in conflict zones may seek initial reassurance but subsequently abandon care due to cost, transportation risks, or the perception that further visits are unnecessary in the absence of acute complications.

The relationship between these utilization patterns and maternal characteristics further elucidates the dynamics of care-seeking behavior. Our analysis demonstrated that earlier ANC initiation was significantly associated with a higher number of total ANC visits (r=−0.154, p=0.001), reinforcing the premise that early booking provides a wider temporal window for women to complete subsequent appointments. Furthermore, a higher age at first birth was significantly associated with earlier ANC initiation (r=−0.189, p<0.001). Crucially, our multivariable logistic regression analysis identified parity and maternal education as strong independent predictors of ANC completion. Higher parity independently predicted lower odds of completing ≥4 visits (AOR=0.53, p<0.001). This likely reflects resource dilution within larger families or “maternal overconfidence” among multiparous women who may perceive subsequent pregnancies as lower risk. Conversely, maternal education strongly predicted both ANC completion and overall satisfaction. Women with tertiary education had substantially higher odds of completing ANC compared to those with no formal education (AOR=7.68, p=0.023). This highlights the critical role of health literacy and reproductive autonomy in navigating fragile health systems, enabling educated women to better overcome access barriers. This finding suggests that women who delay their first pregnancy may possess greater reproductive autonomy, higher health literacy, or better socioeconomic standing, all of which facilitate prompt care-seeking. Conversely, maternal age, total number of pregnancies, and family size showed no significant associations with ANC timing or frequency. This lack of association contradicts some Sub-Saharan African evidence where higher parity often correlates with delayed or reduced ANC due to resource dilution and maternal overconfidence.20 However, it aligns with Miikkulainen et al, suggesting that in deeply fragile settings like Somalia, the overriding constraints of insecurity and systemic collapse may universally suppress utilization across all parity and age groups, leveling out demographic disparities that are more pronounced in stable environments.9

Beyond the frequency and timing of visits, the quality of care as perceived by the mothers was strikingly poor. Only 36% of the participants reported overall satisfaction with the ANC services received. While satisfaction was relatively high regarding the provision of clinical information, specifically advice on pregnancy complications (57%) and birth planning (52%), there was profound dissatisfaction with the structural and interpersonal dimensions of care. The highest levels of dissatisfaction were directed at interpersonal communication (64%), provider competence (60%), waiting times (57%), and appointment availability (56%). These findings echo the systemic deficiencies highlighted by Srivastava et al (2015) and Kruk et al (2018), who emphasize that poor respectful maternity care is a pervasive driver of health system avoidance in low-income countries.10,11 Similarly, Hailu et al (2022) identified poor provider communication and excessive wait times as primary deterrents to ANC continuation.14 Crucially, our findings contrast sharply with a recent study by Ali et al (2025) on the Sudan war, which reported very high maternal satisfaction despite severe ongoing conflict.16 This stark divergence prompts a critical evaluation of how different crisis settings alter patient expectations. In acute, explosive conflict scenarios like the recent Sudan war, patient expectations may drastically lower, potentially leading to a “grateful patient” phenomenon where any access to care however rudimentary is highly appreciated. In contrast, Somalia represents a protracted, chronic crisis. Decades of systemic fragility breed deep-seated frustration with institutional decay, where poor provider attitudes and chronic inefficiencies are no longer viewed as temporary wartime disruptions, but as entrenched systemic failures, which may contribute to the severe dissatisfaction observed in our cohort.

This profound dissatisfaction is inextricably linked to the severe, multifaceted barriers hindering ANC access. Systemic and facility-level barriers were highly prevalent, with a staggering number of women citing favoritism in health services (n=191), irregular facility opening hours (n=191), and a pervasive lack of privacy (n=189). Concurrently, security-related barriers also limited access, including restricted movement due to insecurity (n=145), disruptions to medical supply availability (n=96), and facility closures (n=71). These findings align with Gele et al (2017), who documented the similar disruptions to health infrastructure and supply chains in insecure settings.7 Furthermore, the prominence of favoritism and lack of privacy reflects the systemic collapse of governance and medical ethics noted by Ali et al (2025) in their respective analyses of fragile health systems.16 Urban centers in Somalia host millions of Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs) living in precarious conditions. Systemic barriers such as favoritism likely disproportionately affect these marginalized IDP women compared to settled urbanites, as access often depends on social connections or financial leverage that displaced populations critically lack. While our quantitative design did not capture how favoritism operates at the facility level, prior qualitative work from similarly fragile settings suggests it commonly manifests through informal queue-jumping and preferential allocation of scarce supplies to socially connected patients; targeted qualitative research would help confirm whether these dynamics apply in Mogadishu’s MCH centers. Similarly, although data collection spanned March-May 2026, this study did not systematically record localized security incidents or seasonal/climate events during that window, and we are unable to link specific fluctuations in attendance to discrete security or environmental episodes; future surveillance-linked studies could address this gap.

The manifestation of these barriers and subsequent satisfaction levels was not geographically uniform, as evidenced by significant differences based on maternal residence. Overall satisfaction differed significantly by residence (χ2=9.81, p=0.002), with granular disparities observed in privacy dissatisfaction and appointment challenges. These spatial disparities likely reflect the compounding effects of urbanization and internal displacement. In urban centers of Somalia, rapid population influxes and the proliferation of internally displaced persons (IDP) camps create severe overcrowding in the few functional health facilities, explaining the acute dissatisfaction with privacy and long waiting times. Conversely, rural or peri-urban areas may suffer from absolute resource inequalities, where irregular facility hours and total shutdowns are more common due to logistical isolation and heightened exposure to active conflict. This urban-rural dichotomy is consistent with broader Sub-Saharan African evidence, which routinely demonstrates that while urban women may have physical proximity to facilities, the quality of care is heavily diluted by overwhelming demand and understaffing.

When interpreting these findings, several methodological strengths and limitations must be acknowledged. A primary strength of this study is its large sample size (n=445), which provides robust statistical power in a region where empirical health data is notoriously difficult to collect. Furthermore, it is one of the few contemporary studies to investigate maternal health in conflict-affected Somalia, comprehensively examining utilization, satisfaction, and access barriers simultaneously rather than in isolation, strengthened by multivariable modeling. However, the cross-sectional design inherently precludes the establishment of causal relationships between maternal demographics, barriers, and ANC outcomes; thus, findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causations. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported data introduces the potential for recall bias, particularly concerning the exact gestational age at initial ANC booking and the precise number of previous visits. Self-reporting bias and social desirability may also influence satisfaction scores, though the high rates of reported dissatisfaction suggest women felt reasonably comfortable expressing grievances. The use of convenience sampling across four MCH centers within two urban districts may introduce selection bias, limiting the external validity and generalizability of our findings to highly insecure rural regions where health infrastructure has completely collapsed. Finally, selection bias is an inherent limitation, as the sample only includes women who interacted with the health system to some degree, potentially omitting the most marginalized women who face absolute exclusion from maternal care, and the potential for residual confounding variables cannot be entirely ruled out.

Despite these limitations, the findings hold substantial public health significance and offer clear practical implications for health policy in Somalia and similar fragile settings. To address the steep drop-out rates after initial visits, health authorities and non-governmental organizations must implement strategies to strengthen continuous early ANC engagement, perhaps by unlinking essential preventive care from out-of-pocket costs at the point of service. The glaring dissatisfaction with provider competence and interpersonal communication necessitates urgent, context-tailored communication skills and respectful maternity care training for healthcare workers. At the facility level, administrators must prioritize basic infrastructural adjustments to improve privacy during clinical examinations and establish strict, auditable governance mechanisms to reduce the rampant favoritism that alienates vulnerable women. To circumvent the physical dangers of conflict and irregular facility operations, there is a critical need to increase service accessibility by exploring mobile clinics and community-based ANC models. Deploying trained community midwives who can safely navigate conflict zones and provide continuous care directly within communities may prove to be the most resilient strategy for safeguarding maternal and neonatal health amidst ongoing fragility.

Conclusion

This study highlights a critical gap between maternal health-seeking behavior and healthcare system capacity in conflict-affected Somalia. Although some women initiate antenatal care early, completion of recommended visits remains low, largely associated with dissatisfaction with care quality, poor communication, perceived low provider competence, and inadequate privacy. Systemic barriers, including favoritism, irregular facility operations, insecurity, mobility restrictions, and infrastructure damage, further limit continuity of care. Given the cross-sectional design and convenience sampling, causal and generalizable conclusions should be made cautiously. Strengthening respectful maternity care, provider communication, facility governance, privacy, and decentralized ANC services through community-based midwifery and mobile clinics may improve access and continuity of maternal care in fragile settings.

Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

Ethical approval was granted by the Jamhuriya Research Ethics Committee, Jamhuriya University of Science and Technology in Mogadishu, Somalia, with Approval No. JUREC0159/FHMS373/112025. The study was conducted in strict accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed verbal and written consent were obtained from all participants prior to their involvement. For participants who were illiterate, the information was read aloud to them, and informed consent was obtained using a thumbprint, a procedure formally approved by the ethics committee. To ensure confidentiality and anonymity, all identifying information was removed from the transcripts, and pseudonyms or numerical identifiers were assigned to each participant.
