Response diversity increases functional but decreases compositional stability of grassland communities
Authors: Manuel Pitzer, Vincent Zieschank, Wolfgang Trutschnig, Robert R Junker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary The insurance hypothesis of biodiversity assumes that biodiversity increases ecosystem stability when functionally redundant species exhibit distinct responses to environmental changes, allowing some species to compensate for the loss of others. However, empirical evidence remains scarce.Using a novel approach to quantify the responses of 96 plant species to land use, we tested the hypothesis combining field data with a common garden experiment. By subjecting transplanted grassland plant communities to different land‐use intensities, we quantified response diversity (defined as the variability of responses among species to the same environmental factor), species composition, and functionality from spectral data before and after experimental changes.Our results show that land‐use intensification reduced response diversity of plant communities in the field and the common garden. Moreover, our results indicate that response diversity increases functional stability but decreases stability in terms of species composition (temporal β‐diversity), that is, response‐diverse communities tended to lose species with fewer replacements while response‐depleted communities mainly experienced species turnover.Our work highlights that response diversity underlies multiple dimensions of ecosystem stability as it stabilizes functionality but destabilizes species composition. We further discuss future research directions to adapt the concept of response diversity under global change scenarios. Keywords: biodiversity, compositional stability, ecosystem stability, functional stability, grassland, land use, plant community, response diversity

Introduction

Responses of ecological communities to anthropogenic perturbations define ecosystem stability and thus long‐term functionality (Hautier et al., 2015; Blüthgen et al., 2016; Wagg et al., 2022). It is well established that biodiversity plays a crucial role in enhancing communities' resistance and resilience to global change, thereby supporting the maintenance of ecosystem functions (Tilman et al., 2006; Hautier et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2019). The insurance hypothesis of biodiversity, as one potential mechanism underlying the link between biodiversity, stability, and function (Yachi & Loreau, 1999; Mori et al., 2013), proposes that in biodiverse communities some species may functionally compensate the loss of other species and thus prevent ecosystem degradation (Díaz & Cabido, 2001; Lisner et al., 2023; Bazzichetto et al., 2024). A prerequisite for this compensation is heterogeneous responses (e.g. in abundance or performance) to environmental changes of functionally redundant species within a community (Mori et al., 2013; Nash et al., 2016). Accordingly, ‘response diversity’, defined as interspecific differences in responses to abiotic factors within functionally redundant species, has been recognized as an important component of biodiversity that promotes ecosystem stability (Sasaki et al., 2019; Ross & Sasaki, 2024).

Ecosystem stability is a multidimensional property that quantifies the temporal constancy and resistance of species diversity and composition or of ecosystem functions under fluctuating or shifting environmental conditions over time (Donohue et al., 2013, 2016; Kéfi et al., 2019). Accordingly, effects of environmental changes and disturbances such as land‐use intensification may include alterations in the α‐diversity of communities and changes in species composition due to immigration and extinction of species (known as compositional turnover, β‐diversity; Pimm, 1984; Donohue et al., 2013, 2016). Additionally, environmental changes may also affect the functioning of ecosystems or proxies thereof like spectral indices based on vegetation reflectance (e.g. Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI); see Pettorelli, 2013; Mazzochini et al., 2024; Varouchakis et al., 2025). As diversity components as well as functioning result from the performance of individual species (Elmqvist et al., 2003; Loreau & De Mazancourt, 2013; Laughlin, 2014), response diversity, quantifying the variability in responses to environmental change in functionally redundant species within communities, may represent an underlying mechanism of the biodiversity–stability relationship (Van Ruijven & Berendse, 2010; Bhaskar et al., 2018).

While the potential of response diversity in explaining the relation between biodiversity and ecosystem stability is widely accepted (McCann, 2000; Blüthgen et al., 2016; Schnabel et al., 2021; Polazzo et al., 2024; Nonaka & Sasaki, 2025), the observational, experimental, and statistical methods required to quantify response diversity have not converged to best practices so far (Ross et al., 2023; Ross & Sasaki, 2024). For instance, the use of response trait diversity as proxy for response diversity (Craven et al., 2016; Aquilué et al., 2020) has been criticized for the difficulty in discriminating response from effect traits leading to uncertainties in the interpretation of results (Mori et al., 2013; Ross et al., 2023). More promising may be ‘performance–environment relationships’ (Ross et al., 2023) that directly reflect species responses to environmental changes. Based on performance proxies (such as abundance in studies on pollinators; Winfree & Kremen, 2009; Stavert et al., 2017) or the intrinsic rate of increase in studies on protists and plants (Leary & Petchey, 2009; McCann, 2016; Ross et al., 2023) of several species along the same environmental gradient, previous studies frequently considered the interaction term ‘species × environment’ as indicative of response diversity. Alternatively, direct and pairwise comparison of species' performance curves is a promising approach that reveals more detailed information on species' responses when using abundances as performance proxy along environmental gradients (Fig. 1a; see Ross et al., 2023; see also Stavert et al., 2017; Ruiz‐Moreno et al., 2024).

Ongoing environmental changes such as climate change or land‐use intensification are characterized by unidirectional shifts in mean conditions. Whereas Yachi and Loreau assumed environmental fluctuations (positive and negative deviations from a constant mean) in their original work (Yachi & Loreau, 1999), more recent syntheses now treat the insurance hypothesis and response diversity as intrinsically linked concepts applicable to both stochastic fluctuations and directional environmental shifts (Elmqvist et al., 2003; Mori et al., 2013; Ross & Sasaki, 2024). These unidirectional environmental changes are, however, often also accompanied by changes in the magnitude and frequency of fluctuations, which prevents a clear separation of both effects. Moreover, Yachi and Loreau assumed a closed system, whereas natural communities represent open systems allowing for dynamic changes in species abundances and processes such as immigration from regional species pools and local extinction, which may play a pronounced role in natural communities while the basic underlying theory may still hold true (Yachi & Loreau, 1999). For example, the intensification of land use is among the most severe threats for grassland ecosystems (Foley et al., 2005; Newbold et al., 2015; Díaz et al., 2019) and poses stress on plant communities that negatively respond to mowing and/or fertilization (Wesche et al., 2012; Socher et al., 2013; Allan et al., 2015). In communities consisting of species with divergent responses to land‐use change (high response diversity potentially mediating a high resistance potential to increases and decreases in land‐use intensity), those species that positively respond to land‐use change (i.e. increase in abundance; ‘winners’) will become dominant, whereas species that negatively respond to this change (i.e. decrease in abundance; ‘losers’) may become weak competitors and will eventually be completely outcompeted by other species better adapted to new conditions (Boulangeat et al., 2012; Busch et al., 2019). Consequently, in response‐diverse communities species diversity will likely decrease and species composition will change in a way that the new communities will be composed of the winners of the change only (i.e. a subset of species nested within the species of the previous community; Fig. 1b(i); Gossner et al., 2016). However, such response‐diverse communities may be more resistant and thus more stable in terms of ecosystem functioning when the remaining species compensate for the functions not provided anymore by the lost species (Walker et al., 1999; Chillo et al., 2011; Fig. 1b(i)). As an example, ecosystem productivity may be unaffected despite species decline when remaining plant species compensate the biomass production of the lost species and thus can be considered as functionally redundant to some degree (see Hooper et al., 2005). By contrast, communities consisting of species that collectively respond negatively to land‐use change (low response diversity potentially mediating a low resistance potential) would be expected to decline in functionality, unless the poor performance or even loss of loser species is compensated by immigrating species that thrive under the new conditions (i.e. communities characterized by strong turnover; Stavert et al., 2017; Fig. 1b(ii)). Solely in communities consisting of species that collectively respond positively to land‐use change, both species diversity and composition may be stable and functionality may even increase if species perform better under the new environmental conditions (see Cardinale et al., 2006; Cadotte, 2017; Fig. 1b(iii)). Consequently, we extended the framework of the insurance hypothesis of biodiversity (Yachi & Loreau, 1999) by allowing immigration and local extinctions in this study.

Using a novel, copula‐based approach that accounts for monotonic (constantly positive or negative slopes) and nonmonotonic relationships (with both positive and negative slopes) between plant abundance and environmental gradients (for methodological details, see Supporting Information), which are commonly found in natural systems (Hanusch et al., 2023), we calculated pairwise response dissimilarities rd ij among n = 96 plant species frequently found in the plots of the Biodiversity Exploratories (Fischer et al., 2010). These response dissimilarities rd ij were combined with a common garden experiment, which consisted of grass sods originating from a selection of the same field plots that strongly varied in land‐use intensity (measured as the land‐use intensity index LUI, Blüthgen et al., 2012). LUI ranged from protected areas with minimal usage to intensively managed grasslands. Each grass sod was divided into four subplots that were subjected to one of the four experimental treatments that differed in mowing frequency and amount of applied fertilizer for three growing seasons, which ultimately led to changes in LUI in all subplots. We recorded the abundance of plant species in June 2020 (before treatments started) and July 2022 (after treatment) and measured the NDVI of each community using a 3D‐plant scanner (He et al., 2023; Zieschank & Junker, 2023; Zieschank et al., 2025) in each of the subplots in late June 2020 (before treatments started) and mid‐August in 2022 (after treatment) to track compositional and functional changes due to experimental treatments and infer stability. Based on the assumptions outlined above (Fig. 1), we hypothesize that response diversity increases functional stability (due to compensation) but decreases stability in terms of species diversity and composition under unidirectional land‐use changes. Materials and Methods

Field data

The study was conducted as part of the Biodiversity Exploratories, a large‐scale platform for functional biodiversity research. It comprises three long‐term research sites (‘Exploratories’) at three regions (Schwäbische Alb, Hainich, and Schorfheide‐Chorin) in Germany spanning a north–south gradient with c. 400 km distance between the sites, where biodiversity and land use have been continuously recorded on n = 150 grassland plots since 2006 (Fig. 2a(i,ii); Fischer et al., 2010). Land use includes the amount of applied fertilizer (kg N ha−1 yr−1), grazing intensity (livestock units d−1 ha−1 yr−1), and mowing frequency (number of cuts yr−1), which are commonly applied in combination (Blüthgen et al., 2012). Information about intensities at each plot x is gathered by surveying land owners (Vogt et al., 2019) and integrated into the land‐use intensity index LUI, which quantifies the strength in total land use, has been shown to negatively correlate with plant diversity (Blüthgen et al., 2012) and can be calculated for freely selectable time frames using the LUI calculation tool (Ostrowski et al., 2020) implemented in BExIS (doi: 10.17616/R32P9Q) provided by the Biodiversity Exploratories (see Supporting Information Methods S1, S2 for detailed calculation of LUI following Blüthgen et al., 2012). Grassland plots had a mean LUI of 1.79 (range: 0.58–3.25). In all 150 plots, plant species were identified on a fixed area of 4 × 4 m in May/June from 2008 to 2022 and the abundance of each plant species was estimated (cover in %; Fig. 2a(ii)). The dataset is publicly available on BExIS (https://www.bexis.uni‐jena.de/, ID #31389, v.7, Hinderling & Keller, 2023).

Common garden

Across the Exploratories, we selected n = 13 plots per region that covered a large range of mean land‐use intensities (2006–2019) and collected grass sods with a size of 1 m2 and 10 cm depth in April and May 2020. We split each sod into four subplots of 50 × 50 cm (n = 156, Fig. 2a(iii)) and placed it in a common garden at the Botanical Garden of the Marburg University, Germany, to track the effects of changes in land‐use intensity under reduced environmental heterogeneity (Figs 2b, S1). Each of the four subplots per original plot was assigned to one of the four experimental treatments: ‘00’ (mowing once per year, LUI = 0.99), ‘0M’ (mowing twice per year, LUI = 1.4), ‘F0’ (mowing once per year and fertilizing, LUI = 2.07), and ‘FM’ (mowing twice per year and fertilizing, LUI = 2.3; Fig. 2b). Intensities of treatments of common garden subplots (numbers of cuts (cuts yr−1) and amount of fertilizer (kg N ha−1 yr−1)) were determined based on the 90% quantiles of land‐use management data of all plots in the Biodiversity Exploratories between 2006 and 2016 (latest data which were available at the time of planning). Experimental treatments were initiated in mid‐July 2020. Fertilization (99 kg N ha−1 yr−1) and the first mowing of the respective subplots were conducted in early summer each year, followed by the second mowing in autumn. To avoid spill‐over effects of treatments, subplots were arranged in a semi‐randomized block design with gravel in between subplots and blocks (Fig. 2b, Zieschank & Junker, 2023). Changes in land‐use intensities (∆LUI) were calculated by subtracting the mean LUI values (2008–2019) of the origin plots from the LUI values of common garden subplots (ΔLUI, Fig. 2c). Consequently, positive values indicate land‐use intensification, while negative values indicate decreases in land‐use intensity.

We recorded the taxonomic composition of vascular plant communities in June 2020 (before treatments started) and July 2022 (before second mowing). All species per subplot were identified, and the cover of each species was estimated with a resolution of 1% (Fig. 2d). On the community level, we calculated Shannon diversity for all subplots in 2020 and 2022 (R‐package vegan v.2.8‐8, Oksanen et al., 2024). Changes in Shannon diversity (ΔShannon) were determined by subtracting the Shannon diversity of 2020 from that of 2022 (Fig. 2e). Positive values indicate increases, whereas negative values indicate decreases in species diversity; small changes indicate stability in diversity. As a measure for compositional changes, we calculated temporal β‐diversity of common garden subplots between 2020 and 2022 as the abundance‐based Bray–Curtis dissimilarities (β BRAY, i.e. higher values indicate larger compositional changes; R‐package betapart v.1.6, Baselga et al., 2023). To retrace whether temporal changes resulted from species loss or immigration and replacement, we also calculated temporal β‐diversity as Sørensen dissimilarity (βSOR, based on presence–absence data; Baselga, 2010) and partitioned it into its turnover (βSIM ≙ replacement of species) and nestedness component (βSNE ≙ species losses; Baselga et al., 2023; Fig. 2e). High βSOR values reflect high temporal dissimilarity, whereas higher βSIM values indicate that the temporal β‐diversity results from species replacement and immigration. Conversely, higher βSNE values indicate a stronger species loss without or few replacements.

Apart from vegetation analysis, we gathered community features through digital whole‐community phenotyping (He et al., 2023; Zieschank & Junker, 2023; Zieschank et al., 2025). Specifically, we scanned subplot‐specific plant communities with a customized automated 3D plant scanning system (PlantEye F500; Phenospex, Heerlen, the Netherlands) in June 2020 (before treatments started) and in August in 2022 (before second mowing) roughly matching peak growing season in both years. After processing the scans with the software HortControl (Phenospex), we obtained aggregated, multispectral information on whole‐community level, including the NDVI of each community (Fig. 2d; Zieschank & Junker, 2023). NDVI is a well‐known proxy for community's net primary productivity (NPP) and carbon sequestration potential, two core components of ecosystem functioning, as well as plant physiological health (Pettorelli, 2013; Yang et al., 2022; Varouchakis et al., 2025). Note that NDVI and other vegetation indices derived from spectral reflectance data, such as the Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI), relate nonlinearly to Chl content and green biomass and thus saturate (Xu et al., 2026). Therefore, NDVI is sensitive to a loss of functionality but less sensitive to a gain in functionality when the plateau is already reached. As our study investigates whether response diversity is a mechanism to prevent the loss of functionality, this saturation effect does not affect our primary conclusions. To estimate the functional stability of communities, we calculated subplot‐specific, absolute changes in the NDVI |∆NDVI| between baseline 2020 (before first treatment) and mid‐August 2022. Values close to zero suggest smaller changes in functionality (i.e., more stable), whereas higher values indicate less stability in functionality (Fig. 2e).

Response dissimilarity rd ij

For the calculation of response dissimilarity rd ij , we used abundance–environment curves of plant species growing in communities along a land‐use intensity gradient in n = 150 plots within the Biodiversity Exploratories as species‐specific performance proxies (Fig. 2f(i)). Therefore, we averaged the land‐use intensity index LUI on plot‐level serving as the explanatory variable of the abundance–environment curves (2008–2022) and we calculated the mean abundances per species on plot level as dependent variable across the years 2008–2022. After ordering by LUI ascendingly, we calculated the rolling mean abundances for all plant species that occurred on n ≥ 25 plots (covering n = 96 out of n = 149 plant species found in the common garden) over k = 10 consecutive plots (R‐package runner, Kałędkowski, 2024). Thereby, we increased statistical power and reduced noise and bias via sampling variability by only considering species with sufficient numbers of observations along the gradient.

For each pair of species, we calculated the dissimilarities of the species‐specific abundance–environment curves, which serves as a measure for response dissimilarity rd ij to LUI, using a copula‐based approach as implemented in the R‐package qad (Kasper et al., 2022; Griessenberger et al., 2022a). Therefore, we first calculated the mass distribution of the empirical checkerboard copulas for all given plant species with its rolling mean abundances as dependent variable and the mean LUI as the explanatory variable (both based on field data), resulting in n = 96 empirical copulas with a resolution of 12. As implemented in the R‐package qad, the resolution was defined as the floor function of the square root of the sample size (n = 150 plots), which is the mathematically proven most robust choice for setting the resolution (Junker et al., 2021). Afterward, D1 distances between each pair of empirical copulas were calculated and appropriately normalized with the constant 2 to yield response dissimilarity rd ij ∈0,1, bounded between 0 (identical responses) and 1 (maximally different responses) (Griessenberger et al., 2022a,b; Junker et al., 2021; Trutschnig, 2011, for method validation, see Notes S1 and https://r‐qad.shinyapps.io/quantification_of_dependence/). These values were then compiled into a dissimilarity matrix. To test whether the choice of moving frame size k has a significant effect on the responses, we performed a Mantel test between distance matrices based on moving frame sizes k = 5 or k = 25. Results showed that response diversities are highly correlated (Mantel r = 0.79, P < 0.001), indicating that the shapes of the performance curves are insensitive to choices of k when it is set in a reasonable range.

Subsequently, we established a ‘response space’ based on the response dissimilarity matrix by using nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS; R‐package vegan v.2.8‐8, Oksanen et al., 2024; Fig. 2f(ii)). Within this two‐dimensional response space, distances between species are proportional to the difference in their responses to land‐use intensity (response dissimilarities). We classified species responses to land use into three distinct categories: positively monotonic (species exhibiting an increase in abundances with increasing land‐use intensity), negatively monotonic (species exhibiting a decrease in abundance with increasing abundance), or nonmonotonic (species that do not consistently increase or decrease with increasing land‐use intensity, e.g. unimodal relationships between land‐use intensity and abundance). This response calculation was conducted using the index of monotonicity m as described in Hanusch et al. (2023), which quantifies the deviation of a given performance curve from ideal monotonicity and returning values in the interval [−1,1]. To determine the direction of association, we used Spearman's rank correlation to assign performance curves into three types: negative monotonic associations (m < 0; significant according to Spearman's rank correlation, ρ < 0), positive monotonic associations (m > 0; significant according to Spearman's rank correlation, ρ > 0), or nonmonotonic associations (m ~ 0, not significant according to Spearman's rank correlation), which appropriately reflected species responses to land‐use changes in common garden subplots (Fig. S2).

Response diversity RD

We calculated the response diversity RD of plant communities in the common garden subplots as functional dispersion based on the subplot‐specific abundances of plant species recorded in 2020 or 2022 and the response dissimilarity matrix (Fig. 2g). Functional dispersion (R‐package FD v.1.0.12.1, Laliberté & Legendre, 2010) represents the multivariate dispersion weighted by species abundance, which is insensitive to species richness and not strongly influenced by outliers (Anderson, 2006; Laliberté & Legendre, 2010). Changes in RD (ΔRD) due to experimental treatments were obtained from subtracting RD 2020 from RD 2022. Positive values indicate increases, and negative values indicate decreases in RD. Further validation can be found in Notes S1.

Structural equation model

We used a structural equation model (SEM) to assess and visualize the effects of land‐use intensity on response diversity as well as to analyze whether response diversity mediates the effects of land‐use intensity on ecosystem stability using the lavaan R‐package (Rosseel et al., 2025). SEMs are well‐suited to model and visualize complex hypotheses and mediating effects over multiple levels (McCune & Grace, 2002). We fitted the model using historical LUI and ∆LUI as exogenous variables that directly affect the endogenous variables' functional stability, estimated as the absolute inverse changes in the NDVI (−1*|∆NDVI|), initial response diversity (RD 2020), and stability in diversity (changes in species diversity ΔShannon). We further modeled the direct effects of RD on functional stability and stability in species diversity. In addition, we computed the indirect effects of historical LUI on stability in species diversity and functional stability as the product of the respective standardized path coefficients. Total effects of historical LUI on species stability and functional stability were calculated as the sum of indirect and direct effects. The SEM was fitted using maximum likelihood estimation based on 153 of the 156 subplots (for three plots, NDVI measures are missing).

Linear mixed‐effects models

To confirm the findings of our SEM, we also tested the relationships between RD, land use, and other variables characterizing the plant communities in our subplots by fitting linear mixed‐effects models (Fig. S3; R‐package lmertest v.3.1‐3, Kuznetsova et al., 2020). To account for the fact that each original plot (from the Biodiversity Exploratories) was split into four subplots, we used plot origin as random intercept to account for baseline differences (each model consisted of one fixed and one random factor). To explain RD, we chose a model that only contained the historical LUI as explaining variable. The model explaining Δ RD contained the explanation term ∆LUI only. Both models were tested with subsequent Type I ANOVA (Satterthwaite approximation) using the R‐package lmertest v.3.1‐3 (Kuznetsova et al., 2020). All models aiming for explaining community variables (β‐diversity measures, ΔShannon, and |ΔNDVI|) contained ∆LUI + RD 2020 as fixed explanation terms. Afterward, we performed sequential Type I ANOVA (Satterthwaite approximation) using the same R‐package. All statistical analyses were performed using R: A language and environment for statistical computing (R Core Team, 2023).

Results

Based on the directions of the abundance–environment relationships of n = 96 plant species, 47 species were classified as winners (positive relationships), 39 as losers (negative relationships), and 10 species exhibited nonmonotonic responses to land use (as indicated by the index of monotonicity m). Accordingly, winners, losers, and nonmonotonically responding species clustered in the response space based on response dissimilarities between species pairs (Fig. 3a). The most positively responding species was Lolium perenne (Fig. 3b; m = 0.81), whereas, for example, Chenopodium album showed a clear, nonmonotonic response (Fig. 3c; m < 0.01), and Briza media decreased strongest with increasing land use (Fig. 3d; m = −0.74).

We used a SEM to test and visualize the relationships between land‐use intensity, response diversity, and ecosystem stability. The SEM had a good model fit (Fig. 4: P chi‐square = 0.590; Table S1). Overall, we found clear dependence structures in our SEM (see Table S2 for regression estimates) and all significant paths could also be found in linear mixed‐effects models correcting for plot origin (Fig. S3; Table S3). Before the experimental treatments started, response diversities RD of the initial plant communities in the common garden subplots showed pronounced variations (mean ± SD: 0.28 ± 0.08; range: 0.01–0.42, no significant differences between treatments: ANOVA: F 3,152 = 0.195, P = 0.900). In the SEM, RD was decreased by the historical land‐use intensity index LUI (the land‐use intensity communities experienced in the field before transplantation; Fig. 4; Tables S2, S3). In addition, initial Shannon diversity was also negatively associated with historical LUI, albeit not significantly (Table S3). Historical LUI and changes in land‐use intensity (ΔLUI) decreased the stability in species diversity in the SEM (ΔShannon; Fig. 4; Table S2). However, both exogenous variables did not significantly explain functional stability (inverse |∆NDVI|; Fig. 4; Table S2). RD affected both measures of stability directly, although with different signs. Whereas RD positively influenced functional stability, it directly decreased stability in species diversity (Fig. 4; Table S2). Computing the indirect effects of historical LUI on functional stability revealed a weak but significant negative effect of land‐use intensity via RD (z = −2.599, P < 0.001; Table S2). In addition, RD also mediated the effects of land‐use intensity on the stability in species diversity (z = 3.625, P < 0.001). Our findings also become apparent in the linear mixed‐effects models (Fig. S3; Table S3). At the end of our experiment, the changes in response diversity ΔRD were also linked to ΔLUI matching the negative relationship between initial RD and historical LUI, that is when grass sods were exposed to increased LUI compared with their origin (positive ΔLUI), their RD decreased over the 2 yr in the common garden. The opposite was true when land‐use intensity was decreased in the common garden subplots (Fig. S3d; Table S3).

Structural equation model (SEM) visualizing the relationships between land use, response diversity, and ecosystem stability. Numbers next to arrows represent standardized path estimates and are given for significant paths only (bold arrows). Nonsignificant paths are shown as dashed arrows. Covariances are not shown. Indirect (mediated) effects of historical land‐use intensity via response diversity on functional stability (z = −2.599, P < 0.001) and stability in species diversity (z = 3.625, P < 0.001) are not shown. On the compositional level, abundance‐based temporal β‐diversity βBRAY was positively but not significantly associated with the initial RD (i.e. higher RD led to larger changes in species composition) (Table S3) indicating that a high response diversity rather destabilizes species composition. In this context, species loss (indicated by the nestedness component of Sørensen dissimilarity) increased when initial RD of plant communities was higher (Fig. 5a; Table S3), whereas the turnover component (representing species replacement and immigration) was negatively related to initial RD (Fig. 5b; Table S3). Overall, Sørensen dissimilarity was only decreased by land‐use intensity (Table S3).

Temporal β‐diversity of community composition in response to experimental land‐use changes as a function of initial response diversity RD. Temporal β‐diversity of each subplot was partitioned into a turnover (= high values indicate pronounced replacement of species) and nestedness component (= high values indicate high species losses with fewer or without replacement). β‐diversity was calculated as the Sørensen index considering the presence/absence of species. (a) The nestedness component increased with increasing initial response diversity RD (linear mixed‐effects model; F 1,80.244 = 8.62, P = 0.004, slope = 0.409 (95% confidence intervals: 0.182–0.636)), whereas (b) the turnover component decreased with initial response diversity RD (linear mixed‐effects model; F 1,80.591 = 9.21, P = 0.003, slope = −0.511 (95% confidence intervals: −0.901 to −0.124)).

Discussion

Our common garden experiment reveals both causes and consequences of response diversity among grassland species in the context of anthropogenic land‐use changes. Higher response diversities increased functional stability but destabilized species composition and diversity. Compositional instability in response‐diverse communities was evidenced by higher species losses without replacement compared to less response‐diverse communities. Accordingly, our hypotheses were largely supported: (H1) Overall, responses of common grassland species (estimated by abundance–environment relationships) varied substantially, ranging from negative over nonmonotonic to positive responses to land‐use intensification, which translated into species‐specific positions in the response space. Subplot‐specific compositions of species featuring either similar or dissimilar responses resulted in low or high response diversity depending on the experienced historical land‐use intensities, respectively. (H2) Land‐use intensity reduced response diversity as indicated by our field and common garden results. (H3) We found that plant communities with high response diversities were less stable in terms of species diversity and composition, but more stable in terms of functionality than plant communities with a low response diversity. (H4) Land‐use changes altered species compositions, with stronger shifts in communities that had a high response diversity before experimental treatments started. Those shifts were mainly driven by species loss which resulted in a dominant nestedness component of temporal β‐diversity and indicate that high response diversities may destabilize plant communities in terms of species composition. By contrast, communities with an initially low response diversity also tended to lose species, but those were then replaced by immigrating species, which became apparent in a strong turnover component of temporal β‐diversity.

Yachi & Loreau (1999) identified the asynchronicity of species' responses to environmental fluctuations as determinants for the insurance effects of species‐rich communities. In their model, species‐rich communities featured less variable and on average higher functionality than species‐poor communities in fluctuating environments. In times of global change, environmental conditions often do not only fluctuate but also shift constantly in one direction (Steffen et al., 2015), which has consequences not only for quantitative measures of species' functions but also for their persistence at local and regional scales (Hautier et al., 2015). Whereas extinction and immigration events are not included in Yachi and Loreau's model (Yachi & Loreau, 1999), such events are realistic and important drivers that should also influence response diversity under natural conditions (Elmqvist et al., 2003). Recently, the importance of a stably coexisting species (so‐called core species) for ecosystem functioning has been highlighted (Allan et al., 2025). Despite conceptual differences (i.e. the number of core species is related to increased functionality via complementary effects (Godoy et al., 2020) and response diversity addresses compensatory effects), the sets of stably coexisting species have been linked to differing responses to environmental conditions before (Magurran & Henderson, 2003). In this context, environmental changes such as increased or decreased land‐use intensity may support different sets of stably coexisting species (depending on their responses to the change) ultimately ensuring and stabilizing productivity (Fournier et al., 2017; Snell Taylor et al., 2018). For instance, land‐use intensification leads not only to changes in productivity (Allan et al., 2015; Schils et al., 2022) but also to shifts in species composition and decreased diversity (Weiner et al., 2011; Gossner et al., 2016), which might be even more challenging for ecosystems when resistance and recovery are further reduced by constant pressures in comparison with fluctuating conditions. Aligning with the original hypothesis (Yachi & Loreau, 1999), reductions in land use also caused increases in response diversity over two growing seasons indicating that ecosystems have the potential to recover under decreased stresses not only in diversity and functionality but also in their capacity to functionally tolerate future stresses.

While theoretical and conceptual work on the insurance hypothesis and response diversity usually addresses functional stability (Yachi & Loreau, 1999; Mori et al., 2013), empirical studies considered additional measures for stability reflecting its multidimensionality (Donohue et al., 2016). These measures for stability included the variance of functional diversity based on response traits (Laliberté et al., 2010; Aquilué et al., 2020), the persistence of species and thus species composition and diversity (Ives et al., 1999; Chillo et al., 2011; Baskett et al., 2014), quantitative measures of ecosystem functions (Cariveau et al., 2013; Moore & Olden, 2017; Sasaki et al., 2019), and proxies for ecosystem functions (Mazzochini et al., 2024).

In this context, our results suggest a stabilizing effect of response diversity on ecosystem functions under global change, directly validating Yachi & Loreau's theory (Yachi & Loreau, 1999). By contrast, our findings also indicate that species composition and diversity are dimensions of stability that decreased under high response diversity suggesting that functional compensation may come along with species losses in response‐diverse communities (i.e. winning species replace functionally similar loser species). Latest findings within the Biodiversity Exploratories have also identified functional trait diversity and associated resource‐use strategies as further drivers of community stability (Neyret et al., 2024; Sperandii et al., 2025) showing that multiple mechanisms of diversity can contribute to the relationship between diversity and ecosystem functioning. Sperandii et al. (2025) highlighted the importance of plants' functional strategies – known as the plants' spectrum of form and function (Díaz et al., 2016) – for community stability, whereas functional diversity just played a minor but positive role in grasslands. Thereby, land‐use intensity influenced the acquisitive‐conservative trait spectrum with negative consequences for community stability via decreased species richness and increased population variability. In this context, Busch et al. also linked plant species benefiting from land‐use intensification to more acquisitive trait strategies in the Exploratories before (2019).

Our data confirm previous results separating plant species into those that respond negatively, positively (Busch et al., 2019), or do not show a monotonic response to an environmental gradient (Hanusch et al., 2023), albeit with differing proportions. Whereas the presence of winner and loser species in a community predetermines community responses to land‐use changes that favor the one or the other group of plants (Socher et al., 2013; Gilhaus et al., 2017; Kachler et al., 2023), nonmonotonic responses have been reported to stabilize the diversity and functionality of ecosystems (Zhang et al., 2015; Yan & Zhang, 2018). In this context, response spaces as introduced in this study do not just help to classify groups of species with similar responses but also to identify species that are particularly threatened by global change components. Future studies should validate our findings under more natural conditions with multiple global change components acting simultaneously on vegetation stability, which may alter effects on grasslands (Pompe et al., 2008; de Chazal & Rounsevell, 2009; Bütof et al., 2012).

Our results, along with variable reported net effects of response diversity on ecosystem stability ranging from positive (Sasaki et al., 2019; Aquilué et al., 2020) to no effects (Cariveau et al., 2013; Elsalahy et al., 2020), indicate that the stabilizing effect of response diversity may be system‐specific and depends on how stability is defined. Furthermore, and maybe most importantly, the nature of environmental change (i.e. temporal fluctuating conditions and unidirectional increases in environmental parameters) may strongly affect the relationship between response diversity and ecosystem stability (Donohue et al., 2016; Kéfi et al., 2019). Additionally, as our data demonstrate, the magnitude of change clearly affects stability and the buffering effect of response diversity (Donohue et al., 2016). Finally, most studies, including ours, focus on a single changing environmental factor or stress (Donohue et al., 2016; Kéfi et al., 2019; Ross et al., 2023). Most ecosystems, however, are challenged by changes in multiple factors or multifactorial stress combinations that may act synchronously or sequentially (Zandalinas & Mittler, 2022). It is questionable whether previously stressed communities with consequently reduced response diversities are still sufficiently resistant or resilient to cope with future stresses. In this context, future studies may specifically test whether species that are tolerant to one stressor are also tolerant to another stressor, or whether species feature tolerance to a specific set of stressors while they cannot tolerate others. If species can be divided into those with a universal tolerance and those with rather low thresholds of stress tolerance, communities may quickly loose considerable components of their biodiversity.

We conclude that response diversity is an important determinant for ecosystem stability that mechanistically links functional and compositional stability. Performance–environment relationships turned out to be a powerful tool for assessing pairwise response dissimilarities and response diversity that do not depend on proxies for species responses to environmental changes (see Ross et al., 2023; Ross & Sasaki, 2024). However, the strong context dependency of response diversity–stability relationships requires system‐specific definitions of responses as well as of stability in order to adequately measure and take full advantage of this dimension of diversity. Further insights into the role of multifactorial stress combinations as well as the nature and magnitude of change in affecting response diversity and ecosystem stability are clearly required to develop a robust conceptual framework, and to design conservation strategies that facilitate resilience and resistance and thus maintain ecosystem functioning.
