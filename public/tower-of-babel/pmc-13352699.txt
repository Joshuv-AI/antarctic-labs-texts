Patient-reported health utility in adults with myasthenia gravis: a systematic review and meta-analysis of EQ-5D outcomes
Authors: Venkata Siva Karthik Budumuri, Akshaya Kamalashree G, Hariharan Murugadoss, Vasanth Arun, Aneesh Basheer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMyasthenia gravis (MG) is a chronic autoimmune neuromuscular disorder associated with impaired health-related quality of life. EQ-5D utility and EQ-VAS scores are used in health economic evaluations, but reported estimates vary across MG populations, instruments, tariffs, and study designs.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and CENTRAL from inception to February 2026. Observational studies and randomized controlled trials reporting baseline or pre-intervention EQ-5D utility or EQ-VAS data in adults with MG were included. Random-effects meta-analyses used restricted maximum likelihood estimation. Subgroup, sensitivity, GRADE-informed certainty, and exploratory aggregate-level meta-regression analyses were performed.ResultsTen studies were included. Eight studies (n = 1,514) contributed to EQ-5D utility synthesis, yielding a descriptive pooled mean of 0.74 (95% CI 0.68–0.80), with very high heterogeneity (I² = 95.4%; Cochran Q p < 0.001) and a wide prediction interval (0.52–0.96). Seven studies (n = 2,760) contributed to EQ-VAS synthesis, with a descriptive pooled mean of 66.49 (95% CI 60.99–71.99), very high heterogeneity (I² = 96.3%; Cochran Q p < 0.001), and a wide prediction interval (47.70–85.28). Sensitivity analysis excluding the RCT baseline dataset did not materially change the pooled EQ-5D estimate. In an exploratory analysis based on two studies, utility declined across Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America (MGFA) classes I–IV. Exploratory meta-regression suggested a possible negative association between mean disease duration and utility, but certainty was very low.ConclusionEQ-5D and EQ-VAS estimates indicate impaired patient-reported health status in MG, but pooled values should be interpreted as low-certainty descriptive summaries, not definitive utility inputs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-05033-x.

Introduction

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is a chronic autoimmune neuromuscular disorder characterized by fluctuating skeletal muscle weakness that results from impaired neuromuscular transmission [1, 2]. The global prevalence of the disease is estimated at approximately 12.4 cases per 100,000 people, and it affects patients of all ages, with great heterogeneity in clinical presentation and disease severity [3, 4]. Despite progress in immunotherapy and symptomatic treatment, MG is still linked to high morbidity, functional impairment, and the long-term burden of treatment [5].

MG has a wide clinical spectrum of ocular involvement through generalized muscle weakness with life-threatening respiratory involvement. Disease severity is determined by factors such as antibody status, thymic pathology, response to treatment, and the occurrence of myasthenic crises [6]. Beyond the physical manifestations, MG has significant impacts on psychological wellbeing, social participation, employment, and daily functioning. The unpredictable nature of symptom fluctuation and adverse effects related to treatment also adds to the overall burden of the disease experienced by patients [7].

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) has therefore become an important patient-centered outcome in MG research and clinical practice. Evidence has consistently shown that patients with MG have significantly poorer HRQoL than the general population, with adverse outcomes being observed in patients with greater disease severity, frequent exacerbations, or treatment-related complications [8]. Quantification of HRQoL with preference-based health utility measures is of particular importance to health economic evaluations, as utility measures are used to estimate quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) in cost-utility evaluations and to make decisions on the allocation of healthcare resources.

The EuroQol 5-Dimension (EQ-5D) instrument is the most widely used generic preference-based measure for deriving health utility values and is recommended by major health technology assessment agencies across the world, including the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) [9]. Several observational studies and clinical cohorts have reported EQ-5D utility scores in patients with MG with different disease severities and treatment settings. However, reported estimates differ widely because of variation in study populations, disease classification systems, geographic settings, and EQ-5D instrument versions used. To date, there has been no systematic review and meta-analysis that comprehensively synthesises EQ-5D derived health utility estimates for MG.

A consolidated synthesis of utility values is especially important in the context of emerging therapeutic innovations in MG, such as novel biologics, complement inhibitors, neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) antagonists, and other targeted immunotherapies [10]. Carefully synthesized summary utility estimates, interpreted alongside heterogeneity, prediction intervals, risk of bias, and tariff/value-set differences, may inform health economic models, reimbursement decisions, and policy planning; however, such estimates should not be treated as definitive health-state utilities unless validated in comparable populations. Summarized patient-reported utility data can also help clinicians, researchers, and health economists understand the broader burden of MG beyond traditional clinical endpoints.

Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to synthesize published evidence that reports EQ-5D utility and EQ-5D visual analog scale (VAS) scores in patients with myasthenia gravis. The main objective was to systematically summarize and, where appropriate, pool EQ-5D utility and EQ-VAS estimates in adults with MG. Secondary objectives were to explore differences by disease severity, clinical subgroup, EQ-5D instrument version, and geographical region, while explicitly considering heterogeneity and methodological limitations.

Methods

We conducted a systematic review in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Fig. 1) [11]. The review protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261326180), and the completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided as Supplementary Appendix 1. A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) from database inception to February 18, 2026 (Supplementary Tables 1–4). The search strategy was developed using the PICO framework, with terms focused on the population concept of myasthenia gravis and the outcome concepts of EQ-5D utility and EQ-VAS scores. In PubMed, controlled vocabulary searching used the MeSH term for myasthenia gravis with default explosion to capture indexed MG subtypes and related variants. No date restrictions were applied. Reference lists of included studies were also screened to identify additional eligible publications.

Eligibility criteria

We included observational studies (cross-sectional and cohort studies) and randomised controlled trials (RCTs) that reported EQ-5D utility scores and/or EQ-VAS scores among adults with MG across any Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America (MGFA) stage. For interventional studies, only baseline or pre-intervention EQ-5D data were eligible for extraction, because post-intervention values would reflect treatment effects rather than untreated or usual-care health-state burden. Trial-derived baseline estimates were retained only when they represented pre-randomization or pre-treatment patient-reported health status and were analyzed separately in sensitivity analyses because trial populations may differ from observational cohorts in disease severity, treatment history, eligibility criteria, and comorbidity profile [12]. Adult populations were defined as patients aged ≥ 18 years. We considered studies conducted in any geographic setting and published in the English language as eligible. We excluded case reports, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, book chapters, conference abstracts, editorials, commentaries, and letters to the editor. Studies focusing exclusively on pediatric populations, animal studies, or those lacking EQ-5D utility or EQ-5D VAS outcomes were also excluded. Conference abstracts were excluded because they generally lacked sufficient methodological detail, risk-of-bias information, and extractable baseline EQ-5D mean/dispersion data required for quantitative synthesis. Where conference abstracts appeared to report relevant MG utility or HRQoL outcomes, they were checked against subsequent full-text publications to avoid omitting eligible peer-reviewed data.

Screening and review of studies

Title and abstract screening were performed independently by two reviewers (KB and AK), with disagreements resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (AB), using the Rayyan web-based tool for systematic reviews [13]. Subsequently, at least two authors (AK & HM) independently assessed the full texts of all articles identified during the screening stage. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and mutual consensus among the authors. After removing duplicates, the reviewers came to an agreement on a final list of studies that met the inclusion criteria.

Data extraction

To maintain methodological rigor in the data extraction process, the review team performed a pilot test of the data extraction template using five randomly selected studies before beginning the data extraction process. This pilot phase allowed the template to be refined and consistent in the collection of relevant variables. The final data extraction sheet was created using Microsoft Excel 365 and contained all variables needed as per the study objectives. Data extraction was done systematically by (KB and AK) and then checked by (HM) for accuracy, consistency, and transparency to minimize any errors.

From the included studies, relevant information was extracted to address the objectives of the study, including study characteristics (author, year of publication, country, study design, and study setting), participant characteristics (sample size, age, sex distribution, duration of disease, antibody status where available, and comorbidities), and clinical details related to severity and classification of myasthenia gravis where reported. Disease severity categories, such as those included in the MGFA classification or other definitions of subgroups provided within individual studies, were recorded where available. Only the outcomes obtained from the EQ-5D instrument were extracted. Details regarding the EQ-5D instrument included the version of the EQ-5D instrument (EQ-5D-3 L or EQ-5D-5 L), tariff or value set used, and the reported study subgroups were extracted. Potentially overlapping populations were assessed by comparing author groups, study titles, recruitment periods, countries, data sources, sample sizes, and reported outcomes. When multiple reports appeared to use the same or overlapping source population for the same outcome and time point, only the most complete or directly relevant dataset was retained for that synthesis to avoid double-counting. Related publications reporting different outcomes were included only in outcome-specific analyses. This was particularly relevant for publications derived from or related to the MyRealWorld-MG dataset.

For severity-stratified analyses, we extracted the timing of MGFA classification relative to the EQ-5D assessment where reported. MGFA class was coded as concurrent, baseline/pre-intervention, or unclear/NR. We also extracted the EQ-5D version, country-specific value set or tariff, and whether any mapping or crosswalk algorithm was used. When tariff, mapping, or MGFA timing was not reported, this was coded as NR and treated as a methodological limitation rather than assumed.

The main outcome measures were EQ-5D utility scores, EQ-VAS scores, and measures of central tendency (mean or median) and dispersion (SD, SE, or IQR). For interventional studies, baseline (pre-intervention) values were abstracted to be comparable with observational data. Additional clinical variables were recorded where possible to facilitate subgroup analyses and interpretation of the findings. This structured approach ensured the quality, reliability, and completeness of data to be used for evidence synthesis.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias and key methodological limitations of the included studies were independently evaluated by two reviewers (KB and AK), and any disagreements were resolved by discussion and consensus with a third reviewer (AB). Prior to the formal assessment, piloting of the risk of bias tools was carried out on a subset of randomly selected studies and development of the data extraction form to ensure consistency and clarity in the evaluation.

For cross-sectional studies, methodological quality was evaluated using the Appraisal Tool for Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS). The AXIS tool consists of 20 items that assess the clarity of study objectives, appropriateness of study design, definition and representativeness of the target population, validity and reliability of outcome measures, adequacy of statistical analyses, transparency of reporting, and ethical considerations [14].

For observational cohort studies, risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). NOS is used to assess study quality in three domains: selection of the study groups, comparability of the study cohorts based on study design or analysis, and adequacy of outcome assessment and follow-up. Studies were rated up to a maximum of nine stars, with higher scores representing better methodological quality [15].

For randomized controlled trials, the risk of bias was evaluated using the Revised Cochrane Risk-of-Bias Tool for Randomized Trials (RoB 2), which evaluates the potential for bias in five domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported results [16].

The Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool was used for non-randomized interventional studies. ROBINS-I evaluates bias in seven domains: confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. All quality assessments were conducted independently, and divergences were resolved collaboratively to ensure reliability and transparency [17].

The terms “risk of bias” and “methodological quality” were not used interchangeably. Risk of bias refers to the potential for systematic error affecting the validity of outcome estimates, whereas methodological quality refers more broadly to reporting completeness and design features. Risk-of-bias judgments were used to interpret the certainty and generalizability of pooled estimates.

Data synthesis

A random-effects meta-analysis model was applied a priori across included studies to account for anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity in patient characteristics, disease stages, and study design, using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimator [18]. 95% confidence intervals were calculated for pooled estimates, and 95% prediction intervals were generated where appropriate to reflect the dispersion of true effects across different study settings. Subgroup analyses were performed to investigate variability according to the stages of the disease, geographic region, and EQ-5D instrument version. In addition, separate meta-analyses were conducted according to the MGFA disease classes.

For the meta-analysis, mean EQ-5D utility scores and EQ-VAS scores with corresponding SDs were used. When outcomes were expressed as medians and IQRs, the data were converted into means and SDs by applying the methods described by Wan et al. [19]. However, the primary EQ-5D utility and EQ-VAS syntheses were based on reported means and SDs, or on summary statistics sufficient to derive SDs, rather than on median/IQR conversions. Any converted estimates were interpreted cautiously because skewed distributions may reduce the validity of mean/SD approximations. For studies reporting means with 95% CIs, SDs were derived from the CI width and sample size. When multiple eligible subgroups from the same study contributed to a single synthesis, subgroup means and SDs were combined using weighted methods recommended in the Cochrane Handbook [12, 20].

Statistical heterogeneity between studies was assessed using visual inspection of forest plots, Cochran’s Q test, and the I² statistic. A p-value < 0.10 for Cochran’s Q test was considered indicative of statistically significant heterogeneity. I² values were interpreted according to conventional thresholds, with approximately 25% representing low, 50% moderate, and 75% high heterogeneity. Potential sources of heterogeneity were further investigated using predefined subgroup analyses. A sensitivity analysis was conducted on the overall EQ-5D pooled estimate to test the stability of the results by sequentially excluding individual studies and testing the impact of the results on the pooled effect size.

Exploratory aggregate-level meta-regression analyses were performed to examine the possible influence of study-level mean age and mean disease duration on EQ-5D utility estimates where sufficient data were available. Because only two covariates were examined and the analyses were exploratory, p-values were interpreted cautiously. A post hoc Bonferroni threshold of 0.025 was considered when discussing statistical signals, but no confirmatory inference was made because of the small number of contributing studies and risk of ecological bias.

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and the Egger regression test. All data preparation was done using Microsoft Excel 365, and statistical analyses were carried out using R software.

Certainty of evidence

Certainty in the evidence was assessed using a GRADE-informed approach adapted for single-arm pooled mean outcomes and descriptive domain-level evidence [21]. Certainty judgments considered risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication/reporting bias. Serious inconsistency was assumed when heterogeneity was very high, prediction intervals were wide, or study estimates varied substantially across populations, instruments, tariffs, or clinical contexts. For domain-level EQ-5D-5 L data, certainty was additionally limited by reliance on a single study and absence of quantitative pooling. Certainty ratings were summarized narratively and tabulated for pooled EQ-5D utility, pooled EQ-VAS, and EQ-5D-5 L domain-level outcomes.

Results

The literature search across four electronic databases identified 272 records (PubMed = 17, Embase = 70, Scopus = 158, and Cochrane Library = 27). After removal of 49 duplicates, 223 unique records remained for screening. Following title and abstract review, 194 articles were excluded, and 29 full-text studies were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 10 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in both the systematic review and quantitative meta-analysis. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage included non-eligible publication types, non-MG populations, absence of extractable baseline EQ-5D utility or EQ-VAS data, and potentially overlapping study populations, as detailed in Supplementary Table 5.

Across the included studies, 8 studies contributed data to the EQ-5D utility meta-analysis (total n = 1,514), while seven studies were included in the EQ-VAS analysis (total n = 2,760). The pooled analyses therefore incorporated a substantial number of participants across diverse clinical settings and disease severities. Across studies reporting age, the unweighted mean of study-level mean ages was 54.82 years; the mean of the reported within-study SDs was 15.92 years. Because individual participant data were unavailable, this should be interpreted as an aggregate study-level summary rather than a sample-size-weighted participant-level mean. Regarding study design, the majority were observational in nature, including five cross-sectional studies and three cohort studies, alongside one RCT and one non-randomized interventional study. Overall, the included literature represented a heterogeneous body of evidence encompassing real-world cohorts, registry-based analyses, and clinical trial populations, thereby providing a comprehensive evaluation of health utility outcomes across different clinical contexts. Risk of bias and key methodological limitations were evaluated using design-specific tools, including AXIS for cross-sectional studies, NOS for cohort studies, RoB 2 for the RCT-derived baseline dataset, and ROBINS-I for the non-randomized interventional study.

Five cross-sectional studies [25, 26, 28–30] were assessed with the AXIS tool. Overall, the included studies showed generally acceptable methodological quality, with clear study objectives and appropriate study designs. Most studies used validated outcome measures (EQ-5D) and provided adequate descriptions of statistical methods and results. However, several studies did not explicitly justify the sample size and provided little information about non-responders, which might have raised concerns about selection bias and non-response bias. The reporting of funding sources and conflicts of interest were variable. Ethical approval and consent procedures are usually reported. Overall, the risk of bias between cross-sectional studies was judged to be low to moderate (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Three cohort studies [22, 27, 31] were assessed using the NOS. The total NOS scores ranged from 6 to 8 out of 9 stars, indicating moderate to high methodological quality. Petersson et al. (2025) [31] achieved 8/9 stars, reflecting strong representativeness of the cohort and adequate outcome assessment. Dewilde et al. (2023a) [22] and de Meel et al. (2020) [27] each scored 6/9 stars, with minor concerns related to cohort representativeness, comparability of groups, or follow-up adequacy. Overall, cohort studies were judged to have a moderate risk of bias (Supplementary Fig. 2).

One RCT [23] was evaluated using the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool. The study was assessed to be at low overall risk of bias with no significant concerns for risk of bias identified in all domains, including the process of randomization, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selective reporting (Supplementary Fig. 3).

One non-randomized interventional study [24] was assessed using the ROBINS-I tool. The study was judged to have an overall serious risk of bias, mainly related to issues related to confounding and possible biases of nonrandomized designs. Domain-level assessment showed vulnerability to bias based on confounding and participant selection, but less of a problem in the measurement and reporting domains (Supplementary Fig. 4).

In summary, the evidence base had mixed risk-of-bias concerns. Cross-sectional studies mainly raised concerns regarding sample-size justification and non-response reporting; cohort studies had moderate concerns related to representativeness, comparability, or outcome assessment; the RCT-derived baseline dataset was judged to be at low risk of bias; and the non-randomized interventional study was judged to have serious risk of bias. These limitations were considered when interpreting the pooled estimates and contributed to the very low certainty ratings.

Results of meta-analysis

Overall EQ-5D Utility

Eight studies comprising 1,514 patients with myasthenia gravis were included in the quantitative synthesis of the overall EQ-5D utility scores. Using a random effects model with REML, the pooled mean EQ-5D utility score was 0.74 (95% CI: 0.68–0.80). Individual study estimates varied from 0.63 to 0.89, reflecting the variability in populations and settings of the studies. There was significant statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 95.4%, τ2 = 0.0075, p < 0.0001), indicating substantial true heterogeneity beyond sampling variability. The 95% prediction interval was 0.52 to 0.96, indicating that the true value for the mean utility value in a similar clinical population may be quite variable (Fig. 2). Given the magnitude of heterogeneity, the pooled mean should be interpreted as a summary of observed values rather than a precise population parameter.

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the influence of individual studies on the pooled estimate. Sequential exclusion of each study yielded pooled mean utility values ranging from 0.72 to 0.75, indicating that no single study exerted a disproportionate influence on the overall result. Heterogeneity remained consistently high across all iterations (I² approximately 94–96%), suggesting that the observed variability was not attributable to any individual dataset. These findings indicate that no single study drove the pooled mean; however, heterogeneity remained very high, so the leave-one-out analysis does not overcome concerns about generalizability or justify treating the pooled estimate as a precise population utility value (Supplementary Fig. 5). Given that one non-randomized interventional study was judged to have serious risk of bias using the ROBINS-I tool, an additional sensitivity analysis was conducted excluding this study. Removal of the study did not materially alter the pooled EQ-5D estimate (0.74 versus 0.75 after exclusion), and substantial heterogeneity persisted. Because one RCT-derived baseline dataset was included, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed excluding the RCT baseline estimate from Dewilde et al. 2023b. Exclusion of this dataset did not materially change the pooled EQ-5D utility estimate, which was 0.75 (95% CI: 0.69–0.82), compared with 0.74 (95% CI: 0.68–0.80) in the primary analysis; heterogeneity remained very high (I² = 94.4%). This suggests that the RCT baseline dataset did not drive the pooled estimate, although trial and observational populations may still differ in eligibility criteria, disease severity, treatment history, and comorbidity profile.

Subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Stratification by EQ-5D instrument version produced pooled utility values of 0.70 (95% CI: 0.63–0.76) with EQ-5D-5 L and 0.75 (95% CI: 0.65–0.85) with EQ-5D-3 L with a pooled estimate of 0.77 (95% CI: 0.62–0.92) in studies where the version of the EQ-5D instrument was not reported. The test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (p = 0.5122), indicating that the instrument version did not significantly explain the heterogeneity found (Supplementary Fig. 6). In contrast, geographic stratification demonstrated statistically significant subgroup differences (χ² = 25.16, df = 4, p < 0.0001), indicating that country or regional setting may partially explain variability in reported utility values. Pooled estimates ranged from 0.67 in multinational cohorts to 0.78 in Canadian cohorts, while other country-specific estimates showed wider confidence intervals reflective of smaller sample sizes and within-group heterogeneity. Nevertheless, heterogeneity remained considerable within several subgroups, implying that additional clinical or methodological factors likely contribute to the variability (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 7).

Visual inspection of the funnel plot suggested mild asymmetry; however, Egger’s regression test did not demonstrate statistically significant evidence of small-study effects (t = 1.11, df = 6, p = 0.308), with a bias estimate of 4.86 (SE = 4.37). While these findings do not indicate statistically significant publication bias, the limited number of included studies and the substantial between-study heterogeneity reduce the statistical power of formal asymmetry testing. Accordingly, the absence of statistical significance should be interpreted cautiously. Moreover, in the presence of high heterogeneity, funnel plot asymmetry may reflect underlying clinical or methodological diversity rather than true publication bias, and these alternative explanations cannot be reliably distinguished given the small evidence base (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Overall EQ-VAS score

Seven studies comprising 2,760 patients with MG were included in the quantitative synthesis of EQ-VAS scores. Using a random-effects model with REML estimation, the pooled mean EQ-VAS score was 66.49 (95% CI: 60.99–71.99) on a 0-100 scale. Individual study estimates ranged from 53.20 to 75.40, reflecting variability in patient-reported overall health status across study populations. Substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed (I² = 96.3%, τ² = 51.11, p < 0.0001), indicating considerable variability beyond chance alone. The 95% prediction interval ranged from 47.70 to 85.28, suggesting that the expected mean EQ-VAS score in a comparable clinical setting could vary widely (Fig. 3). Given this high level of heterogeneity, the pooled estimate should be interpreted with caution.

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the influence of individual studies on the pooled estimate. Sequential exclusion of each study yielded pooled mean EQ-VAS values ranging from 65.01 to 68.64, with all corresponding confidence intervals largely overlapping the overall estimate. Heterogeneity remained consistently high across iterations (I² approximately 93%-97%), indicating that no single study disproportionately influenced the pooled effect size and that the observed heterogeneity was not driven by any individual dataset. These findings indicate that no single study drove the pooled EQ-VAS mean; however, heterogeneity remained very high, so the pooled value should still be interpreted as a descriptive summary across heterogeneous settings (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Informally, lower VAS scores appeared more common in earlier and smaller studies, though this observation was not formally tested given the limited number of contributing studies. Planned subgroup analyses for EQ-VAS (e.g., by MGFA class, geographic region, or EQ-5D instrument version) were precluded due to an insufficient number of studies within each subgroup to permit reliable stratified meta-analysis. Consequently, the pooled VAS estimate reflects an aggregate measure across heterogeneous clinical and methodological contexts.

Exploratory analysis stratified by MGFA disease severity

A separate severity-stratified analysis was conducted to examine EQ-5D utility values according to MGFA clinical classification. This analysis was based on data from two studies that reported EQ-5D outcomes stratified by MGFA class, contributing a total of 796 patients across severity categories I to IV. As these severity-stratified estimates were derived from only two studies, they should be interpreted as exploratory and require validation in larger cohorts before they can be used as reliable health state utility inputs in economic models.

A clear gradient in health utility was observed across increasing MGFA classes. Patients with MGFA class I demonstrated the highest pooled mean utility of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.78–0.93), although substantial heterogeneity was present (I² = 92.1%, p = 0.0004). In MGFA class II, the pooled utility was 0.77 (95% CI: 0.75–0.79), with no observed heterogeneity (I² = 0%, p = 0.4769), indicating consistent findings between the contributing studies.

For MGFA class III, the pooled mean utility declined to 0.62 (95% CI: 0.57–0.68), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 66%, p = 0.0864). Patients classified as MGFA class IV had the lowest pooled utility estimate of 0.52 (95% CI: 0.46–0.58), with no significant heterogeneity detected (I² = 0%, p = 0.9106), although the relatively smaller sample size in this category warrants cautious interpretation.

Across all MGFA-stratified groups combined, the pooled mean utility was 0.70 (95% CI: 0.60–0.79), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 98.2%, p < 0.0001). A formal test for differences across severity categories was statistically significant (χ² = 91.98, df = 3, p < 0.0001), suggesting that reported EQ-5D utility differs across MGFA severity categories. However, because these estimates were derived from only two studies, they should be considered exploratory, provisional, and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive health-state utility inputs (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 10).

EQ-5D domain-level proportions

Only one included study [22] reported domain-level proportions for the EQ-5D-5 L instrument. As no other studies provided comparable domain-specific data, quantitative pooling was not feasible, precluding a meta-analysis of domain proportions.

In this study, the proportion of patients reporting any level of problem (ranging from slight to extreme) varied considerably across domains. Problems were most frequently reported in the domains of pain/discomfort (69.7%) and usual activities (68.9%). Anxiety/depression was also commonly affected, with 57.4% of patients reporting at least slight problems. In contrast, 52.3% of patients reported problems with mobility, while self-care was the least affected domain, with 33.6% reporting any degree of difficulty.

Conversely, the proportion of patients reporting no problems was highest for self-care (66.4%), followed by mobility (47.7%) and anxiety/depression (42.6%). Substantially lower proportions of patients reported no problems in usual activities (31.2%) and pain/discomfort (30.3%). Given that these domain-level findings are derived from a single study, they should be interpreted descriptively and with caution. Nevertheless, they provide valuable insight into the multidimensional burden of myasthenia gravis, suggesting that functional activities and symptom-related domains are particularly impacted.

Exploratory meta-regression analyses

Random-effects meta-regression analyses were conducted to examine whether mean age and mean disease duration explained between-study variability in EQ-5D utility estimates. For age (k = 8), a positive but non-statistically significant association was observed (β = 0.0078 per year; 95% CI: -0.0031 to 0.0186; p = 0.1616), suggesting that studies with older mean participant age tended to report slightly higher utility values. However, the confidence interval crossed zero, indicating uncertainty in both the direction and magnitude of the effect. Age explained approximately 14.6% of between-study heterogeneity (R² = 14.63%), but substantial residual heterogeneity remained (I² = 95.58%), and the test of moderators was not statistically significant (QM = 1.96, p = 0.1616) (Supplementary Fig. 11).

In the exploratory disease-duration meta-regression (k = 5), studies with longer mean disease duration tended to report lower EQ-5D utility (β = −0.0335 per year; 95% CI − 0.0539 to − 0.0130; p = 0.0013). Disease duration was extracted as the study-level mean number of years since MG diagnosis or disease onset as reported by each study; definitions and timing were not consistently described across studies. Although the association remained below a conservative Bonferroni threshold for two exploratory covariates, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because it is based on only five studies, uses aggregate study-level data, and is vulnerable to ecological bias. The result should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory (Supplementary Fig. 12).

Given the limited number of contributing studies and the ecological nature of study-level meta-regression, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory rather than confirmatory, pending validation using individual patient-level data.

Certainty of evidence

Using a GRADE-informed approach, the certainty of evidence was rated as very low for the pooled EQ-5D utility estimate, pooled EQ-VAS estimate, and EQ-5D-5 L domain-level findings (Supplementary Table 6). For EQ-5D utility, certainty was downgraded because the evidence was derived mainly from observational studies, heterogeneity was extremely high (I² = 95.4%), the prediction interval was wide (0.52–0.96), and reporting of EQ-5D version, tariff/value set, and mapping procedures was incomplete across studies. For EQ-VAS, certainty was also rated very low because of very high heterogeneity (I² = 96.3%), a wide prediction interval (47.70–85.28), and limited ability to explain between-study variability through subgroup analyses. Domain-level EQ-5D-5 L evidence was rated very low because it was derived from a single MyRealWorld-MG baseline study and could not be pooled across studies. Therefore, the pooled EQ-5D utility and EQ-VAS estimates should be interpreted as low-certainty descriptive summaries, and the domain-level findings should be interpreted as single-study descriptive evidence of multidimensional burden rather than generalizable prevalence estimates.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide, to our knowledge, the first comprehensive synthesis of EQ-5D-derived health utility estimates in adults with myasthenia gravis. Across eight studies (n = 1,514), the pooled mean EQ-5D utility was 0.74 (95% CI: 0.68–0.80), while seven studies (n = 2,760) yielded a pooled EQ-VAS score of 66.49 (95% CI: 60.99–71.99). Together, these findings indicate substantial impairment in patient-reported health status among adults with MG. However, interpretation of these pooled estimates requires caution. Between-study heterogeneity was extremely high for both EQ-5D utility (I² = 95.4%) and EQ-VAS (I² = 96.3%), indicating that most of the observed variability reflects true differences across study populations and contexts rather than sampling error alone. In this setting, the pooled mean should be interpreted as a summary of observed values across heterogeneous clinical environments rather than as a precise estimate of a single underlying population parameter. The wide prediction interval for EQ-5D utility (0.52–0.96) further underscores this variability, suggesting that the expected mean utility in a comparable future clinical population may fall anywhere within this broad range. Consistent with this, the GRADE-informed certainty of evidence was rated very low for the pooled EQ-5D utility and EQ-VAS estimates, reinforcing that these values should be treated as descriptive summaries rather than definitive health-state utility inputs.

The magnitude of heterogeneity likely reflects several sources of clinical and methodological diversity. These include differences in disease severity distribution, healthcare access, treatment exposure, geographic setting, cultural perceptions of health, and variation in EQ-5D instrument versions and country-specific value sets (tariffs). Although subgroup analyses suggested that instrument version (3 L vs. 5 L) did not significantly explain heterogeneity, geographic region was associated with statistically significant differences, consistent with known variability in national valuation algorithms and societal preference weights. Importantly, variation in tariff selection, particularly where crosswalk algorithms or different national value sets were used may contribute meaningfully to between-study differences in utility estimates and cannot be fully disentangled in aggregate-level meta-analysis.

The pooled EQ-5D utility estimate of 0.74 and pooled EQ-VAS score of approximately 66 are consistent with a clinically meaningful patient-reported burden in MG. However, cross-country comparisons with general population utility norms were not formally synthesized in this review. While utility and VAS scores represent distinct constructs, societal preference-weighted health states versus subjective global health ratings, the directional consistency across both metrics supports the overall finding of impaired HRQoL in MG.

A particularly important and clinically coherent finding of this review is the observed gradient in utility across MGFA classes. Based on two studies reporting severity-stratified data, pooled utilities declined progressively from 0.86 in MGFA I to 0.52 in MGFA IV, with statistically significant differences across severity categories. Although derived from limited evidence base and therefore exploratory in nature, this stepwise decline aligns with the clinical trajectory of worsening generalized weakness and functional impairment. Notably, heterogeneity was lower within some severity strata (e.g., MGFA II and IV), suggesting that disease severity may partially account for between-study variability in overall pooled analyses.

These findings have practical implications for health economic modelling. Cost-utility evaluations of emerging MG therapies, including complement inhibitors and FcRn antagonists, require severity-specific health state utilities to model disease progression and treatment transitions. The present synthesis provides empirically derived estimates that may serve as provisional inputs in such models. Nevertheless, given the high residual heterogeneity and the limited number of studies contributing to severity-stratified estimates, these values should be validated in jurisdiction-specific populations before being adopted as definitive model parameters.

Although domain-level proportions were reported in only one study, descriptive findings indicated that pain/discomfort and usual activities were most frequently affected, followed by anxiety/depression. These findings highlight the multidimensional burden of MG beyond neuromuscular weakness alone. However, the lack of consistent domain-level reporting across studies represents an important gap in the literature and limits more granular synthesis.

Exploratory meta-regression analyses demonstrated a statistically significant negative association between disease duration and EQ-5D utility, whereas mean age was not significantly associated. While a negative directional trend was observed for disease duration, statistical power was limited by the small number of contributing studies and persistent residual heterogeneity. Moreover, study-level meta-regression is inherently subject to ecological bias, as associations are based on aggregate data rather than individual-level variation. These findings should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating.

Formal assessment of small-study effects using Egger’s regression test did not demonstrate statistically significant evidence of publication bias. However, the limited number of studies and substantial heterogeneity reduce the reliability and power of asymmetry testing. In highly heterogeneous meta-analyses, funnel plot asymmetry may reflect true clinical diversity rather than selective reporting, and absence of statistical significance should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of absence of bias.

This review has several strengths. It provides the first quantitative synthesis of EQ-5D utility values in adults with MG, incorporates prediction intervals to enhance interpretability, and includes severity-stratified analyses aligned with clinical classification systems. The use of baseline values from interventional studies improves comparability with observational cohorts. By focusing exclusively on preference-based utility measures, the findings are directly relevant to cost-utility analysis and health technology assessment frameworks.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First and foremost, the extreme between-study heterogeneity limits the precision and generalizability of pooled estimates. The very low certainty ratings further reflect the combined impact of predominantly observational evidence, extreme inconsistency, wide prediction intervals, incomplete tariff/value-set reporting, and single-study evidence for domain-level EQ-5D-5 L proportions. Second, the modest number of included studies constrained subgroup and meta-regression analyses. Third, variation in tariff selection, EQ-5D version, and scoring methodology may introduce additional methodological heterogeneity not fully captured in subgroup analyses. Fourth, although the primary EQ-5D utility and EQ-VAS syntheses did not depend on median/IQR-to-mean/SD conversion, any converted estimates in supplementary or extracted data were interpreted cautiously because skewed distributions may reduce the validity of mean/SD approximations. Fifth, reliance on aggregate study-level data precluded adjustment for important clinical covariates such as antibody status, treatment regimen, and comorbidity burden. Although only baseline values from interventional studies were extracted, trial populations may differ systematically from real-world observational cohorts because of eligibility criteria, treatment history, disease severity, refractory status, and exclusion of comorbidities. Sensitivity analysis excluding the RCT-derived baseline dataset did not materially change the pooled EQ-5D estimate, but this does not eliminate potential selection differences between trial and observational populations. Potential overlap among Dewilde/MyRealWorld-MG-related publications was assessed at the study and outcome level; however, because participant-level identifiers were unavailable, residual overlap cannot be completely excluded, particularly for multinational HRQoL datasets. Planned subgroup analyses by responder status, treatment status, and study design were not feasible because these variables were inconsistently reported and several subgroups contained only one or two studies. Finally, severity-stratified estimates were derived from only two studies and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Future research should prioritize standardized reporting of EQ-5D outcomes, including explicit specification of the tariff or value set used, detailed severity stratification, and reporting of domain-level proportions. There is also a clear need for greater representation of Asian and other underrepresented regions, particularly given the increasing prevalence and recognition of myasthenia gravis in these populations [32]. Most currently available evidence originates from Western cohorts, which may limit the generalizability of utility estimates due to differences in healthcare systems, cultural perceptions of health, and country-specific valuation sets. Expanding research in Asian settings would therefore improve the external validity of utility estimates. Harmonized reporting practices and prospective cohort studies incorporating uniform valuation methods would substantially improve the reliability of pooled utility estimation and strengthen inputs for health economic evaluations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides the first quantitative synthesis of EQ-5D health utility values in adults with MG. Although pooled estimates indicate substantial health impairment and a clear severity gradient across MGFA classes, substantial between-study heterogeneity limits the precision and generalizability of pooled estimates. Future research should prioritize standardized reporting of tariff use and severity-stratified outcomes to enable more reliable health state utility estimation for economic modelling.
