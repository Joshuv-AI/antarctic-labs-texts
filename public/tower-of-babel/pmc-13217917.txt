High-throughput small-angle X-ray scattering reveals effective structure factor transitions linked to high-concentration antibody viscosity
Authors: Lateefat Kalejaye, I-En Wu, Jia-Min Chu, Mitali Shah, Maria Monica Castellanos, Shirish Chodankar, Neil Mody, Pin-Kuang Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHigh-concentration monoclonal antibody (mAb) formulations are often constrained by elevated viscosity, largely driven by protein–protein interactions, which complicates manufacturing and limits subcutaneous delivery. Early viscosity risk assessment is essential during discovery, yet traditional measurements require large sample volumes, and lack high-throughput capability. Here, we develop a high-throughput small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) protocol to detect mAb self-association at dilute concentrations, enabling early predictive insights into high-concentration viscosity. Synchrotron SAXS measurements were conducted for 21 mAbs formulated in a histidine buffer at pH 6.0. An initial subset of 10 mAbs analyzed across 1–150 mg/mL revealed that effective structure factor transitions in the low-q region, indicative of interparticle interactions, consistently emerged below 25 mg/mL. Subsequently, 11 additional mAbs were analyzed at 1–25 mg/mL using automated liquid handling and flow cells to enable high-throughput screening. High-viscosity mAbs exhibited detectable low-q upturns at concentrations ≤10 mg/mL, whereas low-viscosity mAbs showed downturns. A classification criterion based on effective structure factor transitions accurately classified all high- and low-viscosity mAbs at 150 mg/mL, offering a scalable, sample-efficient alternative to conventional methods. These results extend recent findings on the concentration-dependent sensitivity of SAXS to short-range attractions, demonstrating that they can emerge at lower concentrations than previously reported. This study presents the most comprehensive and diverse SAXS dataset for mAbs reported to date within a single formulation, providing a valuable resource for developing and validating coarse-grained models that can more accurately capture intermolecular interactions governing high-concentration solution behavior, thereby enabling rational antibody engineering and improved developability.KEYWORDS: Antibody viscosity, High-concentration formulations, monoclonal antibodies, protein-protein interactions, small-angle X-ray scattering

Introduction

High-concentration monoclonal antibody (mAb) formulations are essential for subcutaneous drug delivery,1–3 a more convenient alternative to intravenous administration,4,5 but often encounter developability challenges due to high viscosity,6 which adversely impacts manufacturability, syringeability, and injectability.7–9 These viscosity issues primarily arise from protein–protein interactions (PPIs)10–12 at high concentrations driven by heterogeneous surface properties.10,13,14 These interactions encompass hydrogen bonding, excluded volume, electrostatic, hydrophobic, and van der Waals forces, which manifest as short-range attraction (SRA) and long-range repulsion (LRR) that promote reversible self-association and elevate solution viscosity.13,15,16 To mitigate the risk of high viscosity, it is critical to identify antibody candidates with favorable solution properties early during the discovery and lead optimization stages. Various predictive models have been proposed,17,18 but are often limited by insufficient experimental validation. In parallel, viscosity measurements at high concentrations typically require large sample volumes and substantial laboratory resources, which are rarely available during early development. Consequently, there is a pressing need for efficient approaches that enable the assessment of viscosity-related behavior at high concentrations using minimal sample quantities and that can contribute valuable experimental data for refining computational models.

Biophysical properties such as the diffusion interaction parameter (kD) from dynamic light scattering (DLS)16,19,20 and second virial coefficient (B22), from static light scattering (SLS) measurements21–24 have been widely used to study PPIs under dilute conditions. While kD and B22 provide useful insights into antibody behavior, they are derived from measurements across a series of low concentrations, capturing average interactions rather than resolving how specific interparticle interactions evolve at defined concentrations and have shown only moderate correlations with viscosity at high concentrations. Moreover, they reflect two-body interactions16,21 and do not fully capture the dynamic, concentration-dependent nature of multibody effects, self-association, and crowding phenomena that drive high-concentration viscosity.

Small-angle scattering (SAS) techniques, including small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS)25–27 and small-angle neutron scattering (SANS),28,29 are powerful tools for analyzing biomolecular macromolecules in solutions.30 These techniques provide detailed information on intramolecular structure and are especially sensitive to interparticle interactions that emerge as protein concentration increases, making them well-suited for investigating concentration-dependent PPIs and their impact on solution behavior. Previous SANS studies28,31,32 have revealed anisotropic interactions and reversible cluster formation in concentrated antibody solutions, with these structural features correlating with solution viscosity. Similarly, SAXS has been applied to characterize interparticle interactions in antibody formulations under varying conditions.33–35 Previous studies26,27,36 have correlated SAXS-derived parameters at low concentration to high-concentration mAb viscosity behavior; however, they are constrained by the limited number of mAbs analyzed and have only established correlations at concentrations up to 60 mg/mL.27

Computational modeling approaches complement experimental techniques. All-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are widely used to study protein structure and interactions,37–40 but are computationally expensive and limited in their ability to model antibody–antibody interactions in concentrated environments. Deep-learning surrogate models41,42 have been developed to accelerate all-atom MD simulation approach to obtain viscosity9 and biophysical properties43; however, to reduce computational cost, these models typically, only use variable fragment (Fv) sequence and structure data for training, neglecting Fab−Fc interactions that have been shown to also contribute to solution behavior 15 in addition to Fab–Fab interactions. Coarse-grained (CG) modeling overcomes these limitations by representing antibodies using reduced degrees of freedom, enabling simulations of hundreds to thousands of molecules. Different CG models15,44–47 have been developed to study antibody biophysical properties, but they are typically validated against limited experimental data using single-point observables (B22, SLS S(0), or high-concentration viscosity) for parameterization. These models capture averaged interaction behavior rather than the concentration-dependent evolution of PPIs, restricting model accuracy in predicting repulsive and weakly attractive interactions, while failing to capture strong attractive behavior at high concentrations, where deviations grow as interprotein spacing decreases with concentration. For CG models to be physically realistic, they must retain essential interactions governing solution behavior, minimally including repulsive steric interactions, screened electrostatic repulsions, and attractions, and short-ranged nonelectrostatic attractions. Concentration-dependent SAXS profiles offer a promising solution to this limitation: unlike single-point measurements, SAXS data captures how interparticle interactions evolve across concentration ranges, providing rich experimental constraints for parameterizing and validating CG models that accurately represent both SRA and LRR.

Previous studies have combined SAXS with CG modeling36,48,49 to quantify SRA and LRR from effective structure factor (Seff) obtained from experimental-scattering data at concentrations between 125 and 250 mg/mL, providing mechanistic insights into concentration-dependent interparticle interactions. Most recently, Chowdhury et al.50 further demonstrated that the sensitivity of Seff to viscosity-driving interactions varies with concentration: at dilute conditions, long-range electrostatic repulsions from net charge dominate and suppress low-q upturns, while at very high concentrations (>200 mg/mL), steric repulsion from molecular crowding becomes dominant. They proposed an optimal intermediate concentration (75 mg/mL) for quantifying short-range anisotropic attractions, where mAbs are sufficiently close to attractive patches to align and form contacts without being overwhelmed by packing effects. However, 75 mg/mL was also the lowest concentration at which Seff was evaluated in that study and the analysis was limited to a single mAb. Moreover, measurements at such intermediate concentrations still require substantial sample quantities and may miss earlier signatures of attractive behavior that could inform candidate selection during discovery. We then pose certain questions: Can the optimal concentration for detecting attractive PPIs be reduced below 75 mg/mL? At what concentration do attractive interactions first become detectable in Seff, and at what concentration do LRR begin to offset or dominate SRA? How do this early interaction signatures correlate with high-concentration viscosity across different mAbs?

In this study, we conducted synchrotron SAXS measurements on 21 mAbs, obtaining scattering profiles at multiple concentrations. The first set of 10 mAbs (A1–A10) was measured across a wide concentration range (1–150 mg/mL). We derived the effective structure factor, Seff(q), by normalizing the Porod law-scaled intensity at each concentration to that at a dilute-reference concentration (1–2 mg/mL), where interparticle interactions are negligible, and the scattering approximates the form factor P(q). This approach allowed us to isolate the contribution of interparticle interactions to the scattering signal and visualize how these interactions evolve as a function of concentration. High-viscosity mAbs exhibited a pronounced upturn in Seff(q) profile in the low-q region, indicative of attractive interparticle interactions, even at relatively low concentrations. Although the exact onset of these upturns varied among mAbs, they consistently emerged up to a critical concentration between 5 and 25 mg/mL, well before viscosity becomes problematic, and then start to decrease, possibly due to steric repulsion from molecular crowding becoming dominant. This behavior also suggests that the optimal concentration for detecting attractive PPIs can be lower than previously reported. Based on this observation, we measured the second set of 11 mAbs (B1–B11) over a narrower concentration range (1–25 mg/mL), using high-throughput SAXS with automated liquid handling and flow cells. This captured the critical early signatures of attractive interactions while minimizing sample requirements. Notably, all high-viscosity mAbs across both datasets showed detectable upturns in Seff(q) profile in the low-q region at concentrations below 10 mg/mL, while low-viscosity mAbs exhibited detectable downturns indicative of repulsive interactions. A classification criterion based on the concentration at which Seff(q) transitions occurring at ≤10 mg/mL successfully distinguished all mAbs as high or low viscosity at 150 mg/mL.

Based on these findings, we propose a high-throughput SAXS screening method that uses minimal sample quantities to identify high-viscosity antibody candidates early in development, facilitating the formulation of concentrated mAb therapeutics suitable for subcutaneous administration. Moreover, with 21 structurally diverse mAbs measured across a wide concentration range, this work presents the most comprehensive SAXS dataset for therapeutic mAbs reported to date, serving as a valuable resource for developing and validating coarse-grained models that can more accurately capture the short-range anisotropic attraction and long-range electrostatic repulsions governing high-concentration solution behavior, ultimately enabling mechanistic understanding and rational engineering of antibodies with improved developability profiles.

Results

Experimental viscosity and kD measurements

We measured viscosity at 150 mg/mL and kD at dilute concentrations (2–10 mg/mL) for 21 mAbs, comprising 14 low-viscosity mAbs (A1−A6, B1−B8 ≤20 cP) and 7 high-viscosity mAbs (A7−A10, B9−B11 >20 cP), as shown in Figure 1. We apply a threshold of −8 mL/g to classify kD values.16,21,51–54

(A) viscosity and (B) diffusion interaction parameter (kD) for all 21 mAbs. Viscosity values were measured at 150 mg/mL, while kD values were determined from dilute concentration measurements (2–10 mg/mL). The dashed lines in (A) indicate the viscosity threshold (20 cP), and in (B) the kD threshold (−8 mL/g), commonly associated with the onset of attractive protein–protein interactions. mAbs with viscosity <20 cP and kD >−8 mL/g are color-coded in green, mAbs with viscosity >20 cP in brown, and mAbs with kD <−8 mL/g in yellow.

In general, mAbs with more negative kD values exhibited high viscosity, while those with positive values exhibited low viscosity. This trend is consistent with prior studies reporting that positive (negative) values of kD are qualitatively associated with repulsive (attractive) PPIs. However, notable exceptions were observed; mAbs A8 and B11 have favorable kD values (5.36 and −6.98 mL/g, respectively), but show high viscosity (88.9 cP and 56.93 cP, respectively).

Porod’s law scaling and effective structure factor analysis

To analyze the concentration-dependent interparticle interaction, we applied Porod’s Law Scaling (PLS) normalization to the SAXS intensity data (see Methods). The optimal Porod region for each mAb was determined individually by systematically testing the q-ranges between 0.1 and 0.6 Å−1, selecting the range that yielded R2 > 0.95 for the linear fit of I(q) vs. q−4. Optimal ranges for all 21 mAbs are provided in Table S1. For each mAb, scattering profiles at different concentrations were normalized, and the effective structure factor Seff(q) was calculated by taking the ratio of normalized intensities (I(q) − B/A) at each concentration to that at a dilute reference (1 or 2 mg/mL). At this dilute reference, interparticle interactions are negligible and the normalized intensity approximates the form factor P(q), which represents single-particle scattering. This isolates the concentration-dependent contributions of interparticle interactions, under the decoupling approximation, whereby the intramolecular form factor P(q) is assumed to be concentration-independent. To ensure that the extracted form factors were not contaminated by aggregation or residual intermolecular interactions at the dilute-reference concentration, we evaluated the radius of gyration (Rg) from Guinier analysis and the maximum dimension (Dmax) from pair-distribution functions (p(r)) using BioXTAS RAW.55 The obtained Rg values (46–56 Å) and Dmax values (144–191 Å) across all mAbs (Table S2) are consistent with literature-reported ranges for monomeric IgG1 mAbs,51,56,57 noting that Dmax values toward the upper end of this range are consistent with the intrinsic flexibility of some IgG1 mAbs.56 The corresponding Guinier and p(r) plots for all mAbs are provided in the Supporting Information (SAXS_data_SI folder).

SAXS intensity profiles after buffer subtraction (Figures S4) show increasing intensity with concentration. PLS normalization collapses these curves in the high-q region, enabling clearer visualization of concentration-dependent changes in low-q Seff(q). Figure S5 shows the form factor comparison across all mAbs.

At low concentrations, Seff(q) approaches unity across the entire q-range, indicating minimal interparticle interactions. As concentration increases, Seff(q) deviates from unity in the low-q region: low-viscosity mAbs (A1−A6) exhibit downturns (Seff(q) < 1), indicating repulsive interactions, while high-viscosity mAbs (A7−A10) show upturns (Seff(q) > 1), indicating attractive interactions that contribute to elevated viscosity. As expected, Seff(q) approaches unity in the high-q region at all concentrations as interparticle effects become negligible at small length scales.

Interestingly, Seff(q) for high-viscosity mAbs evolves nonmonotonically with increasing concentration. Initially, Seff(q) exhibits an upturn in the low-q region due to attractive PPIs. However, beyond a certain critical concentration, Seff(q) begins to decrease, likely reflecting a balance between attractive interactions and excluded volume effects or other repulsive contributions that emerge in crowded environments. For example, mAbs A7, A9, and A10 showed upturns from concentrations between 2 and 3 mg/mL, followed by decreases at critical concentrations between 5 and 25 mg/mL. mAbA8 exhibited upturn only at 3 mg/mL and decreased immediately. Although the onset and critical transition concentrations vary among high-viscosity mAbs, Seff(q) deviations generally occur at or below 25 mg/mL. Based on these observations, we measured an additional 11 mAbs (B1−B11), focusing on the 1–25 mg/mL range and using a high-throughput SAXS workflow.

This high-throughput SAXS workflow demonstrates that early signatures of attractive or repulsive interparticle interactions can be detected at low concentrations (≤10 mg/mL) based on the formulation of standard histidine buffer. Early detection, which occurs well before viscosity becomes problematic at high concentrations, enables practical early-stage developability assessment with minimal sample requirements. Moreover, a large dataset of 21 structurally diverse mAbs enabled effective testing of the correlation between low-concentration SAXS signatures and high-concentration viscosity, as detailed in the following section.

Regression correlation of kD and low-q Seff(q) with viscosity

To assess the predictive ability of kD and low-q Seff(q) for high-concentration viscosity, we performed a comparative regression analysis using data from all 21 mAbs. We obtained kD values from the slope of the diffusion coefficient versus concentration curve measured between 2 and 10 mg/mL (Figure S1). Our initial attempt to extrapolate Seff(q = 0) using a two-Yukawa potential model58,59 was constrained by poor fit quality. As a practical alternative,60 we extracted Seff(q) values at q = 0.01 Å−1, where scattering fluctuations are reduced while still retaining sensitivity to interparticle interactions.

Figure 6 presents the correlation between viscosity at 150 mg/mL with kD and Seff (q = 0.01 Å−1). The kD values show a moderate correlation with viscosity (R = −0.78; R2 = 0.60). Seff(q) values extracted at 5, 10, and 15 mg/mL, reveal a concentration-dependent improvement in predictive performance. At 5 mg/mL, Seff(q) exhibits a moderate correlation with viscosity (R = −0.71; R2 = 0.51), as interparticle interactions are not yet sufficiently pronounced. However, at 10 mg/mL, the correlation strengthens substantially (R = 0.94; R2 = 0.89), indicating that attractive or repulsive interactions are clearly detectable for most mAbs at this concentration. At 15 mg/mL, the correlation strength remains similarly strong. Overall, these results demonstrate that interparticle interactions detected at dilute concentrations (10 mg/mL) provide early indicators of the elevated viscosity observed at high concentrations (150 mg/mL).

Regression plots showing correlation between viscosity at 150 mg/mL (A) kD, Seff(q = 0.01 Å−1) at (B) 5 mg/mL, (C) 10 mg/mL, (D) 15 mg/mL. Red dashed lines indicate linear regression fits. Similar plot but excluding the highest viscosity mAbA10 is presented in Figure S10.

Given the wide range of viscosity values in our dataset (5.2–270 cP), we examine whether the highest viscosity mAb (mAbA10, 270 cP) dominates the correlations. Figure S7 shows similar analysis that excluded mAbA10 (n = 20) and the correlations decreased as suspected but remained moderate. While this analysis may be semiquantitative, it can be applied to molecules with a broad range of viscosities. Figure S8 shows Seff (q = 0.01 Å−1) as a function of protein concentration for all mAbs.

Next, we adopted a qualitative classification approach based on whether Seff(q) upturns occur at concentration ≤10 mg/mL, rather than relying on absolute Seff(q) values at a fixed concentration and low-q values for quantitative viscosity prediction. This threshold was selected because Seff(q) transitions are consistently detectable for most mAbs at ~10 mg/mL, highlighting the method’s ability to provide early viscosity risk using minimal sample quantities.

Qualitative classification of viscosity risk using low-q Seff(q) transitions

We evaluated the ability of kD and low-q Seff(q) to classify mAbs into high-viscosity (>20 cP at 150 mg/mL) and low-viscosity (≤20 cP) categories. For the kD-based classification, we applied a threshold of kD = −8 mL/g16,21,51–54 to distinguish mAbs with net attractive interactions from those with net repulsive interactions. For SAXS-based classification, we used a qualitative criterion: mAbs showing Seff(q) upturns in the low-q region at concentrations ≤10 mg/mL were classified as high-viscosity, while those showing only downturns were classified as low-viscosity. Classification results are summarized in Table 1 and shown in Figure 7. Figure 7(A) shows the kD-based classification, which misclassified two high-viscosity mAbs (mAbA8, mAbB11) as low-viscosity, yielding 90% accuracy (19/21 correct). Figure 7(B) shows SAXS-based classification using Seff(q) behavior in the low-q region, which correctly classified all the high- and low-viscosity mAbs, resulting in 100% accuracy.

Overall, while SAXS-derived Seff (q) shows comparable classification performance, it directly probes concentration-dependent interparticle interactions at multiple concentrations rather than deriving a single averaged parameter from a concentration series. Discussion

High-concentration mAb solutions often exhibit elevated viscosity, driven by weak, reversible self-association and clustering.32,61,62 This behavior reflects the interplay between short-range attractive intermolecular interactions and long-range repulsive interactions arising from excluded volume and electrostatic effects, which are further modulated by heterogeneous antibody surface properties.49,60 SAXS provides nanometer-resolution63 insights into the macromolecule structure and interactions in solution, with the ability to probe concentration-dependent interparticle interactions, conformational flexibility, and molecular crowding, making it relevant to biopharmaceutical formulations.64–66

In this study, we developed a high-throughput SAXS screening method to identify high-viscosity risk in antibody candidates at early stages of development using minimal sample quantities. We obtained SAXS data for 21 mAbs formulated in 20 mM histidine/histidine−HCl at pH 6.0. PLS normalization is applied to remove background and scaling effects, and the effective structure factor, Seff(q) was calculated, as the ratio of the normalized intensity at each concentration to that at a dilute reference (1–2 mg/mL), where interparticle interactions are negligible, and the normalized intensity approximates the form factor P(q). This approach isolates interparticle interaction effects, which manifest primarily in the low-q region. At low concentrations, Seff(q) approaches unity across the entire q-range and deviates in the low-q region as concentration increases. Analysis of the first set of mAbs (A1−A10) measured across 1–150 mg/mL revealed early signs of interparticle interactions in the low-q region, especially with high-viscosity mAbs showing upturns typically up to 25 mg/mL. Consequently, the second set of 11 mAbs (B1−B11) was measured across 1–25 mg/mL using an automated liquid-handling system with flow cells, which enabled precise, low-volume dilutions from a 25 mg/mL stock and significantly reduced sample usage. Across both datasets, high-viscosity mAbs exhibited upturns (Seff(q) >1) at concentrations typically ≤10 mg/mL, indicative of net attractive interactions, while low-viscosity mAbs mostly showed downturns (Seff(q) < 1) reflecting net repulsive interactions. Notably, unlike other high-viscosity mAbs, mAbA8 only exhibited an upturn at 3 mg/mL, which was followed by an immediate decrease with increasing concentration.

Our findings build upon recent advances in understanding concentration-dependent PPIs through combined SAXS and CG modeling.36,48,49 Chowdhury et al.50 demonstrated that Seff(q) sensitivity to SRA (primary drivers of high viscosity) varies systematically with concentration, with interaction strength more sensitive at 75 mg/mL compared to 200 mg/mL, where steric repulsion from molecular packing overwhelms SRA signatures, and identified 50–100 mg/mL (optimally 75 mg/mL) as an intermediate window where mAbs are sufficiently close for attractive patches to align without being overwhelmed by crowding. Our study reveals that this intermediate window can extend to lower concentrations: all seven high-viscosity mAbs showed detectable Seff(q) upturns at concentration ≤10 mg/mL that persisted up to 15–25 mg/mL (less than 75 mg/mL previously reported50) before decreasing as crowding effects became more pronounced.

Our initial attempt to extrapolate Seff(q→0) using a two-Yukawa potential model59 to capture competing short-range attraction and long-range repulsion was limited by poor fit quality. As practical alternatives,60 we evaluated experimental Seff(q) values at q = 0.01 Å−1 where scattering fluctuations are reduced while still retaining sensitivity to interparticle interactions. These values show a semiquantitative correlation with high-concentration viscosity, with the correlation strength increasing from moderate at 5 mg/mL to strong at 10 mg/mL (R2 = 0.89). Based on this observation, we adopted a qualitative classification strategy where mAbs exhibiting low-q upturn at concentration ≤10 mg/mL were classified as high viscosity, while those showing only downturns were classified as low viscosity. This 10 mg/mL threshold was selected because all mAbs already showed detectable upturns or downturns at this concentration as observed from the regression correlation. This qualitative approach relies on a fundamental behavioral distinction (the presence or absence of attractive interactions) rather than absolute Seff(q) values and it correctly classifies all mAbs.

This study primarily reports experimental SAXS measurements and observations from initial analysis. All SAXS measurements in this study were performed at a synchrotron source, which provides high-intensity X-rays to detect subtle scattering features even at low concentrations (1–10 mg/mL). Such sensitivity has been demonstrated to be achievable with benchtop SAXS instruments,29 making this approach increasingly accessible for routine developability screening. A critical requirement for accurate structure factor analysis is that the form factor, P(q), used to isolate effective interparticle interactions is not contaminated or influenced by time-dependent aggregation due to prolonged storage or by residual intermolecular interactions at the dilute-reference concentration. To address this, all samples were carefully filtered and hold times were minimized prior to SAXS measurements, and we expect that our analysis was not significantly affected by irreversible aggregates or interactions. To further ensure this, Rg, p(r), and Dmax extracted from the dilute-reference P(q) were analyzed and found to be consistent with reported values for monomeric IgG antibodies,51,56 supporting the absence of significant aggregation or intermolecular interference.

Within our current dataset, SAXS-based classification correctly classified all high- and low-viscosity mAbs, while kD-based classification misclassified two mAbs. However, it is important to emphasize that the goal of this work is not a direct comparison between kD and SAXS classification, particularly given the relatively small dataset size. Notably, one of the misclassified mAbs by kD (mAb B11) exhibited sensitivity to the fitting range: fitting across 2–10 mg/mL yielded a kD value of −6.98 mL/g (classified as repulsive), whereas restricting the fit to lower concentrations (2–6 mg/mL) yielded a kD value of −10.98 mL/g (classified as attractive), consistent with its high-viscosity behavior (Figure S1). This highlights that kD can be sensitive to the selected fitting range in certain cases, reflecting deviations from the assumption of linearity in the diffusion coefficient versus concentration relationship across the full concentration range. All other mAbs show consistent classification with both 3-point (2–6 mg/mL) and 5-point (2–10 mg/mL) fitting, which would reduce the kD-based misclassification to only one mAb (mAbA8). Also, mAbA8 exhibited upturn in Seff(q) only at 3 mg/mL, followed by an immediate decrease with increasing concentration. Although, SAXS classified this mAb as high-viscosity, consistent with its measured viscosity (88.9 cP at 150 mg/mL), this behavior may also represent a potential SAXS misclassification. If so, within this current dataset, SAXS-based classification would be considered comparable to, rather than significantly better than kD.

The primary advantage of SAXS over DLS or SLS is its ability to resolve concentration-dependent changes in interparticle interactions at individual concentrations, rather than relying on average parameters derived from concentration series. High-viscosity mAbs exhibit attractive interactions that emerge at specific concentrations. SAXS captures these transition points through Seff(q) measured independently at each concentration, whereas kD infers interactions indirectly from diffusion measurements and represents an average interaction parameter across the fitted concentration range. Moreover, kD also reflects hydrodynamic contributions in addition to PPIs,51 making complete deconvolution of these effects challenging and potentially complicating interpretations. SAXS, on the other hand, is mostly sensitive to PPIs from SRA and LRR in the low-q region, which directly influence viscosity. An additional advantage is that SAXS-derived interaction profiles can be more readily compared with CG simulation models, enabling integration of experimental and computational frameworks for mechanistic interpretation of high-concentration solution behavior. It is also noteworthy that a previous study9 reported kD-based viscosity classification accuracy of approximately 80% across a larger antibody dataset (~229 mAbs). Accordingly, larger-scale SAXS studies will be necessary to comprehensively evaluate the robustness and generalizability of SAXS-based viscosity prediction across broader and more diverse antibody panels.

While Seff(q) proved to be qualitatively capable of identifying risk in mAbs, future studies will aim to combine the SAXS experimental data with CG modeling and develop solution models to describe the concentration-dependent scattering observed from repulsive to weakly attractive and strongly attractive interactions and to provide deeper mechanistic insights. Importantly, this study focused on mAbs formulated in 20 mM histidine/histidine−HCl at pH 6.0, the 10 mg/mL concentration identified as prominent enough for Seff(q) transitions in the low-q region may be formulation-dependent and should be interpreted in this context. Also, the applicability of this finding to other buffer compositions, pH conditions, ionic strengths, excipients, and salt formulations should also be investigated in future studies to establish its broader utility. Moreover, extending this approach to other modalities, such as bispecific and multispecific antibodies and fusion proteins, should also be considered. The high-throughput screening approach is specifically designed to identify viscosity risk and should be considered part of a comprehensive developability assessment strategy. Antibody interactions can drive other problematic behaviors beyond high viscosity, including liquid–liquid phase separation, opalescence, and irreversible aggregation. While Seff(q) upturns reflect attractive interactions and correlate with high viscosity in our study, their relationship to these other macroscopic behaviors remains unclear.

In conclusion, our SAXS-based screening method presents a high-throughput and scalable strategy for predicting high-concentration viscosity in mAbs using minimal sample requirements at low concentrations (≤10 mg/mL). By detecting early signatures of attractive versus repulsive interparticle interactions through effective structure factor analysis, this approach enables informed candidate selection during early development and supports the formulation of concentrated mAb therapeutics suitable for subcutaneous administration. Additionally, this work presents the largest and most diverse SAXS dataset for therapeutic antibodies to date, opening avenues for developing CG models validated against comprehensive experimental structure factor profiles. Such models can better quantify and deconvolute the effects of short-range attraction and long-range repulsion, advancing mechanistic understanding, and enabling rational antibody engineering.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

All 21 mAbs used in this study were from the IgG1 subclass and manufactured at AstraZeneca (Gaithersburg, MD). Following mAb production, the bulk drug substance solutions were buffer-exchanged into a formulation buffer containing 20 mM histidine/histidine−HCl at pH 6.0 using 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) Slide-A-Lyzer dialysis cassettes (Thermo Scientific). Dialysis was conducted overnight with multiple buffer replacements, maintaining a minimum buffer-to-sample volume ratio of 1000:1 to ensure adequate exchange. Postdialysis, samples were verified to meet the target pH specification. The dialyzed samples were concentrated to reach a final target of 150 mg/mL for the first set of mAbs and 25 mg/mL for the second set of mAbs using 10 kDa MWCO Amicon Ultra-4 centrifugal filters (Millipore, Merck kGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). Final protein concentrations were measured with a microfluidic UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Lunatic, Unchained Labs, Pleasanton, CA), using 2 µL of sample at 280 nm with extinction coefficients and density correction factors previously determined through experimental calibration. Additional quality control testing was performed using high-performance size-exclusion chromatography (HP-SEC) to confirm purity >95%.

Experimental measurements of kD and viscosity

kD: Diffusion coefficient measurements were carried out using Stunner (Unchained Labs) equipped with a 660 nm laser diode for dynamic light scattering. For each antibody, 2 µL at concentrations of 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 mg/mL was dispensed in triplicate into a 96-well Stunner plate (Unchained Labs). Data were collected using five acquisitions of 4 s per well and averaged. Theoretical E1% values were calculated for each antibody sequence and entered for each dilution series to ensure accurate concentration determination. Viscosity correction values of 1.002 cP and 0.846 cP were used for measurements in phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.2) and 20 mM histidine–HCl (pH 6.0), respectively. A refractive index of 1.334 was used for all measurements. Data were exported using Lunatic & Stunner Analysis software (version 8.2.0.259) for further analysis in Microsoft Excel. Viscosity: Viscosity of the mAb solutions was measured using the Honeybun microfluidic viscometer (Unchained Labs; Pleasanton CA). Viscosity of each sample (150 mg/mL) was obtained using the default (35 µL) mode loaded onto the Bun microfluidic chip (cyclic olefin copolymer material of construction; Unchained Labs; Pleasanton CA) and measured in triplicates at 22°C temperature condition. A medical-grade viscosity standard of 10 cP (Paragon Scientific, UK) was tested during each protein sample run.

SAXS experimental measurements

SAXS experiments were conducted at the Life Sciences X-ray Scattering (LiX) beamline (16-ID) at National Synchrotron Light Source II at Brookhaven National Laboratory, using a dual-detector setup—Pilatus 1 M (in air) and Pilatus 900 K (in vacuum)—operating simultaneously to provide a broad, continuous scattering vector (q) range of 0.005 < q < 3.2 Å−1. Data were collected at a wavelength of 0.819 Å, and the momentum transfer, q, was defined as q = 4πsin(θ)/λ. Detector calibration was performed using silver behenate, with its well-defined 5.8 nm lamellar spacing. Two types of sample holders were used depending on sample viscosity. Highly viscous samples were measured in custom flat cells with 100 μm ultrathin Schott glass windows (fixed cells) where radiation damage was mitigated by using a fly scan routine, in which the sample was continuously moved during data collection. Lower-viscosity samples were measured using flow cells, where continuous flow inherently reduces radiation damage. To verify that these protocols prevent radiation damage, standard lysozyme protein was measured prior to experiments using both cell types. The measured lysozyme exhibited no increase in radius of gyration, confirming that both the fly-scan routine (for fixed cells) and continuous flow (for flow cells) effectively prevent radiation damage during SAXS data collection. In both cases, background subtraction was based on measurements from empty cells or matching buffers, and data were processed using the py4xs package in Jupyter Notebooks.

To enable high-throughput SAXS measurements for the second set of mAbs (1–25 mg/mL), we streamlined and automated sample preparation, transfer, and data collection, by adopting a 96-well plate format alongside an Opentrons OT2 liquid-handling robot.67 Only nine wells per row are used to align with the nine-position LiX sample holders, simplifying automated protocol generation. Users submit a sample information spreadsheet that is validated and used to generate a QR-coded plate ID label. Upon arrival, the beamline software automatically creates a transfer protocol and measurement spreadsheet, which the OT2 robot uses to transfer samples into the appropriate holders, facilitating efficient and reproducible SAXS measurements.

The high-throughput setup supports up to 20 sample holders (18 samples each) accommodating 360 unique samples per run. With the ability to perform two automated measurement runs per day, the platform allows for the collection of up to 720 SAXS datasets per day, significantly accelerating data acquisition compared to traditional manual workflows. This automated strategy ensured efficient, reproducible measurements and minimized manual handling while maintaining data quality even at low concentrations.
